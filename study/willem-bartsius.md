## The life

Willem Bartsius was born in Enkhuizen around 1612. No birth record survives: his name appears in neither the birth records of the Dutch Reformed Church nor the marital or burial records of Enkhuizen, and the judicial archive of Enkhuizen is almost completely lost. The c. 1612 date is "generally agreed" (Wurzbach 1906; Thieme-Becker 1908; Benezit 1976; Fredericksen 1976; Bernt 1979) and is anchored by a single hard document: a notarial deed of 8 July 1636, in which Bartsius declares himself to be 24 years old and living in Amsterdam. His father was Paulus Pietersz. Bartsius, stadspensionaris -- legal adviser to the town council -- of Enkhuizen; his mother Frederickje Meijnertsdr. Semeyns came of the wealthy Semeyns herring family. The parents married 31 March 1598. Houbraken calls the father "Paulus Bertius, city secretary" and says the mother was "descended from the House of Egmont"; Van Haute corrects the first from the Enkhuizen archives and the second is Houbraken's embroidery, not archival fact. An elder brother Pieter was baptised 23 February 1599 and probably died before 1630. His sister Aecht, called Aagje or Agatha, married the painter Pieter Simonsz. Potter and was the mother of Paulus Potter.

The family fortune collapsed after the father's death, before September 1626. Frederickje, widowed, sued tenants and debtors in 1626-27; her debts at death in 1635 were f 116-18-0, and both Willem and Pieter Potter refused to be instituted as her heirs (1 December 1635 and 25 May 1636); the estate sale raised f 87. She had left Enkhuizen before May 1633 to live in Amsterdam in her daughter's house. Aagje and Willem retained rights to a house on the Spaensche burchwal in Enkhuizen, for which rent was claimed 30 July 1636.

Nothing is known of his training, and nothing is known of his movements in the late 1620s and early 1630s. Van Haute is explicit on both points. It has been suggested (Fredericksen 1976) that his brother-in-law Pieter Potter, about twelve years older, initiated him into painting while still in Enkhuizen -- but Potter was initially only a glaseschrijver (glass-writer) there and only began painting after settling in Leiden in 1628, and Potter's own teacher is unknown. The suggestion is single-source speculation. The Web Gallery of Art biography says he "might have accompanied his brother-in-law Pieter Potter to Leiden in 1628"; Van Haute shows it is unverifiable, since Leiden had no painters' guild in the first half of the 17th century, and a Bredius note even raises the counter-speculation that Potter joined Bartsius in Alkmaar. A Haarlem stay is not merely undocumented but ruled out: his name appears in neither the Haarlem Guild of St Luke archives nor Goosens's study of painters active in Haarlem, despite perceived affinities with Hendrik Pot and Willem de Poorter. The earliest signed work, 1631, already shows a fully formed history painter at about nineteen -- which implies training somewhere before 1631, but where is unknown.

The first hard facts come from Alkmaar. Bartsius reported to the Alkmaar Guild of St Luke on 17 September 1633 to be registered as a member, and was officially enrolled as a master in 1634. The registration was prompted by a prestigious commission from the city of Alkmaar: the group portrait of the Officers and Standard Bearers of the Oude Schutterij, dated 1634 (W15). In 1634 Abraham Meyndertsz. is recorded as his pupil on the guild's name list -- the only documented pupil, nothing else known about him, possibly a maternal relative; Van Haute suggests Bartsius may have trained him to assist on the large schutterstuk.

He is named in an Amsterdam document of 1 December 1635, and securely situated in Amsterdam only from May 1636. The move may have been precipitated by his mother's death; staying was likely motivated by better financial prospects -- though Amsterdam's history-painting market was effectively monopolised by Rembrandt and his workshop, and Van Haute suggests the competition may explain why he soon gave up painting. The family ties stayed active: on 25 May 1636 Pieter Potter obtained permission to sell Frederickje's estate, the request recording that Pieter "wrote to his brother-in-law Willem Bartsius (presently residing in the city)" about accepting the inheritance; on 30 July 1636 Pieter, as guardian of his children, together with Willem, claimed two years' rent on the Enkhuizen house. The last record of Bartsius is an Amsterdam notarial declaration of 11 April 1639 about the gold-leather factory of Jacob van Ipen; Pieter Potter was one of three participants in that factory on the Weespat and usually ran the shop. After 1639 there is no trace of him in any document -- not even a mention of his death.

He died in or shortly after 1639. NOT 1657. The only evidence for a later life is a drawing, River Landscape with Castle, signed and dated "BARTIVS f. / 1657" (pen and brush in brown, 13 x 18.8 cm, Wallraf-Richartz-Museum, Cologne, inv. 1964/13). Van Haute, following Fred Meijer (RKD), judges the signature and date not fully reliable; Erik Loffler and Meijer suggest that if authentic it is the work of an unknown draughtsman named Bartius, possibly a family member. The near-twenty-year absence of any written or signed evidence makes survival past 1657 unlikely. EN Wikipedia's "1612-1657, Enkhuizen" repeats the 1657 death year from older reference works without the Van Haute/Meijer correction, and the error propagates through the reproduction and print-seller sites, several of which also claim without evidence that he "returned to Enkhuizen" and "continued to work as a painter" after 1639. Treat all of those as low-quality, derivative sources.

## M1. Peripeteia -- the instant a feeling turns into its opposite

The central discovery, and the one Van Haute states most plainly: Bartsius's subject is not the Bible story but the moment a person is moved by powerful, conflicting feelings -- the instant of transition from one mood into its opposite, Dutch staetveranderinge, Greek peripeteia. Death of the Levite's Concubine (1638, W05) is the textbook case: the Levite finds his concubine lying dead before the house door, throws his hands up and recoils in horror, while the old peasant who lodged them sits pensive on the doorstep. In Abraham Pleading with Sarah on Behalf of Hagar (1631, W01), Abraham bends as if about to kneel, holding Sarah, pleading with her to reconsider banishing Hagar and Ishmael; Sarah turns her head away, fist clenched in stern determination; Isaac clings to her leg. In Jacob Shown Joseph's Blood-Stained Cloak (W09), Jacob turns his head away from the gruesome sight, stretches out his left arm as if to push it aside, and clutches his chest theatrically, while Rachel crouches, demonstratively wiping away tears with a cloth. Van Haute connects the idea explicitly to Rembrandt and his pupils, who made peripeteia a central feature -- while noting no direct Rembrandt influence on Bartsius's handling. The mechanism is the hands: the Levite's thrown-up hands, Sarah's clenched fist, Jacob's clutching hand, the raised index finger of Delilah and of the angel in Elijah. The picture is built around one instant of conflicting feeling, and the hands carry it.

**Takeaway:** build one instant of a feeling turning -- a man recoiling with his hands thrown up, a woman turning her head away with a clenched fist, a father clutching his chest at a blood-stained cloak -- and let the hands do the telling. The watchable behaviour is the turn itself: the recoil arriving, the fist tightening, the head turning away, the body caught between two moods and held there.

## M2. The theatrical spotlight -- actors lit, background dissolved

The second mechanism is the staging. Bartsius's figures are "caught in a bright 'spotlight' like actors on a stage": a strong beam of light picks out the protagonists while the surrounding shaded areas lack definition, and background figures are executed in rudimentary shapes and dark tones -- finish is concentrated where the light falls. In Samson and Delilah (1632, W02), the protagonists -- Delilah, Samson, the old hair-cutter, the little boy -- are caught in a strong beam of light, while the shaded background figures dissolve into rudimentary dark shapes. In the Lute Player (1633, W03), the young man stands in a plain interior in a single beam, the dark blue tablecloth and the vanitas paper the only other lit things. Light falls typically from the left, as in St Paul (W04), where it accentuates the roughness of the beard and the furriness of the brown cap with its burning red lining. Sumowski called the 1631 Abraham "a more Rembrandtesque work" for its strong chiaroscuro, though Van Haute finds no direct Rembrandt influence otherwise. The spotlight is not atmosphere; it is a casting decision -- who is lit is who matters, and everyone else is allowed to dissolve.

**Takeaway:** build a stage, not a room -- one beam of light from the left, the protagonists lit like actors in it, the background figures left as rudimentary dark shapes that dissolve at the edges. The watchable behaviour is the light holding its cast: the beam steady, the lit faces bright, the dark figures staying dark, the picture deciding who matters by who is lit.

## M3. Fijnschilderkunst -- the texture catalogue

The defining technical trait is fine painting, not loose painting. Sumowski called Samson and Delilah a work of "elegant fijnschilderkunst and exquisite colouring". Van Haute stresses throughout: minutely rendered anatomical detail; life-like, tactile differentiation of textures -- skin, hair, fabric, stone, metal; "absolute mastery of the brush" in faces and hands; naturalistic rendering of folded drapery; delicate handling of cloth and leather. The Lute Player is the catalogue at its fullest: a black beret decorated with jewels and a feather, a metal-studded gorget, a thin scarf wrapped round the neck, a satin sleeve cut in slits, a cape over the right shoulder finished with a red velvet border and heavy gold embroidery, striped breeches, long leather boots with upturned tips -- every material rendered distinctly in one frame, the face, hair and especially the hands the finest in the oeuvre. The palette is vivid costume colour against dark grounds: red velvet borders with heavy gold embroidery, blue cloaks, yellow cloaks, burning-red linings, dark blue tablecloths, green coats, colourful tassels. He is a fine painter, not a loose or brisk one; his brushwork is more delicate, his colours more vivid, and his figures more elegant than Willem de Poorter's (Van de Watering's comparison). What was NOT found, and must not be invented: no source in this pass gives Bartsius-specific evidence for pigment, ground, underdrawing or imprimatura practice. The study describes fine finish and tactile differentiation, and stops there.

**Takeaway:** build one frame as a texture catalogue -- skin, hair, fur, embroidery, gold, metal, satin, leather, each material rendered distinctly, the hands and the face finished finest of all. The watchable behaviour is the touch: the eye moving from material to material, each one reading as itself, the fine finish never loosening, the costume as much the subject as the man.

## M4. The repertory company -- the same faces and props, re-cast

The fourth mechanism is the reuse. The same faces and the same props migrate across unrelated pictures, so the sixteen paintings read as one small company playing many parts. The knotted shawl or sash ending in tassels appears on Abraham, on the angel in Elijah, on Ceres, on Jacob, and on the Vanitas woman. The metal flask or vessel with a chain sits in the bottom right corner of Abraham, Elijah, Samson and Delilah, and the Vanitas still life. The feathered turban and the parasol recur in Samson and Delilah and in the Choice of Hercules. The faces repeat too: Abraham resembles Elijah resembles Jacob; Isaac resembles the boy left of the hair-cutter in Samson and the boy walking up the platform steps in Jacob; Delilah resembles Ceres resembles Virtue. Frizzy hair and plumed hats, the raised index finger, the twisted moustache of the Amsterdam citizen -- the company carries its wardrobe and its faces from Bible to myth to allegory to portraiture. The attribution history proves the point from the other side: many works were long given to Willem de Poorter, Gerard Dou, Ferdinand Bol, Jacob de Wet, Jan Victors, Gerrit Willemsz. Horst, Nikolaus Knupfer or Govaert Flinck before Sumowski, Van de Watering, Meijer and Van Haute re-attributed them to Bartsius on exactly these recurrences.

**Takeaway:** build one small company and re-cast it -- the same faces, the same knotted tasselled sash, the same chained metal flask, the same feathered turban, playing Abraham one picture and Jacob the next, Delilah one picture and Ceres the next. The watchable behaviour is the recognition: the same prop turning up in a different story, the same face under a different name, the wardrobe migrating while the story changes.

## M5. Direct address -- the finger that turns the viewer into a witness

The fifth mechanism is the look out of the picture. Delilah in Samson and Delilah looks out at the viewer and raises a finger, staging the scene theatrically and calling attention to it. The Lute Player looks straight at the viewer while he plays. The Vanitas woman sits in three-quarter view, staring at the viewer, her hand on a skull. The Gentleman Holding a Tankard (W06) smiles at the viewer and holds up his closed tankard. St Paul looks at the viewer and holds his sword before his chest. The queen in the lost Moses Tramples the Crown of Pharaoh raised her index finger in a listening gesture; the angel in Elijah points his right index finger to heaven. The raised finger and the outward gaze do the same work in every picture: they break the fourth wall of the history scene and turn the viewer from a spectator into a witness, called to attention by the figure inside the frame. It is the one gesture Bartsius shares with Brouwer's turned head in The Smokers -- the picture noticing the viewer -- but where Brouwer's figure turns in surprise, Bartsius's figures point and summon.

**Takeaway:** build a figure who notices the viewer -- a woman looking out and raising a finger, a musician staring straight out while he plays, a saint holding his sword toward the viewer -- and let the look be the picture's hinge. The watchable behaviour is the summons: the finger rising, the gaze holding, the viewer pulled from spectator into witness, the scene staged for the person outside the frame.

## M6. The vanitas inventory -- the still life as a second picture

The sixth mechanism is the floor-level inventory. In the Theorbo-Lute player (W13), on a cloth spread on the floor at the player's feet, a still life of opened and closed books, pieces of paper -- the top sheet titled "Vanitas Vanitatis" -- and a skull places the picture in the allegorical category. In the Lute Player (W03), the table draped with a dark blue cloth holds books, a piece of paper inscribed "Vanitas Vanitatis" and a chain. In the Vanitas (W14), the inventory fills the bottom right corner on the tiled floor: globe, books, playing cards, a fallen glass, violin, flute and lute, a pipe, dice, an overturned hourglass and the metal flask with a chain first seen in the 1631 Abraham; warfare objects are vaguely discernible at left in the background, and a draped table behind the woman holds an opened jewellery box, ornamental silverware, a nautilus cup and the familiar tasselled shawl. The woman herself -- left foot on a book, left hand on a skull on her knee, right hand holding a spoon with a bubble -- personifies Vanitas, surrounded by objects symbolising vanity and brevity. The inventory is a second picture inside the picture: the figure above, the objects below, the moral carried by the things on the floor rather than by the face. A curiosity worth keeping: an intimidated collector had the skull in the Vanitas overpainted with a book between 1929 and 1945, turning the figure into Minerva; the overpaint was later removed.

**Takeaway:** build a picture in two registers -- a figure above, a floor-level inventory below, the still life of books, skull, hourglass, dice, cards, instruments and bubble doing the moral work the face does not. The watchable behaviour is the inventory: the objects laid out one by one on the floor, each a small emblem of brevity, the bubble on the spoon trembling, the hourglass overturned, the picture's meaning sitting on the tiles.

## M7. The doorstep threshold -- interior and exterior in one frame

The seventh mechanism is the architecture. Bartsius stages his biblical scenes on the step before the half-open door of a vine-overgrown house, so that interior and exterior share one frame. In Abraham Pleading (1631), Abraham, Sarah and Isaac stand on a step before the half-open door of a house overgrown with vines, an embroidered carpet cascading from the step. In Death of the Levite's Concubine (1638), the scene is set before a house door overgrown with dense foliage, the concubine lying on the threshold itself, and the painting extends left to reveal ruined buildings. In Jacob Shown Joseph's Blood-Stained Cloak, the figures stand at left before a dilapidated, overgrown building while the right half of the picture opens into an extensive landscape under a clouded sky. The threshold is the device that lets the picture hold two spaces at once: the house behind the door and the world outside it, the private plea and the public road, the dead body on the line between them. The 1631 Abraham and the 1638 Levite are the same doorway seven years apart, and the repetition is the point -- the career begins and ends on a doorstep.

**Takeaway:** build one threshold -- a step before a half-open door of a vine-overgrown house, the figures standing on the line between inside and outside, the dead body or the pleading family on the doorstep itself. The watchable behaviour is the threshold holding: the door half open, the vine overgrowing it, the interior behind and the world outside sharing one frame, the story happening on the step between them.

## M8. The failed crowd -- fourteen sitters and the lesson that stuck

The eighth mechanism is the failure, and it is the most instructive one in the oeuvre. The Alkmaar schutterstuk (1634, W15) is Bartsius's only large multi-figure commission and his least successful composition: fourteen officers of the Oude Schutterij split into two un-unified groups by the standard bearer standing in the middle, six men at right gathered round a table with documents and a map, six men at left posing idly, three standing, three seated. Van Haute is blunt: the composition "lacks balance and unity", several figures are awkwardly proportioned and their heads "do not seem to fit comfortably on their bodies" -- worst the man seated left of the flag bearer, head too large, no left shoulder, unnaturally twisted left arm, severely flattened chest. What succeeds is the small-scale work: individual faces, characterisation, meticulous clothing and accessories, elegantly poised hands holding wineglasses and swords. He was paid f 225 for the painting plus f 90 for the ebony frame, which is still in place -- and the clients were pleased. Van Haute attributes the unevenness to inexperience with complex multi-figure compositions and/or uncoordinated help from his pupil Abraham Meyndertsz. Huys Janssen suggested he looked to Frans Hals's militia meeting of the previous year, but there is no direct evidence he knew it. The lesson of 1634 seems to have stuck: Bartsius never attempted another group portrait, and his later history pictures keep the cast small and the staging frontal. The failure is a mechanism in the negative -- it defines the small cast as a discipline, not a limitation.

**Takeaway:** build the crowd that does not hold -- fourteen sitters split in two by a standard bearer, the heads not sitting on the bodies, the group portrait failing at the scale it was commissioned for -- and then build the same painter's answer, the small cast, three figures on a doorstep, the faces and the hands finished and the ensemble never attempted again. The watchable behaviour is the split: the two groups never joining, the flag bearer standing between them, the small-scale faces succeeding while the whole fails.

## Aesthetic logic

Bartsius's logic is the feeling as the picture, and the small cast as its proper size. He does not build a scene and put an emotion in it; he finds the one instant in which a feeling turns into its opposite -- the recoil, the averted head, the clenched fist, the clutching hand -- and lets it carry the picture, with the Bible story, the costume and the props serving as the trigger. The spotlight is the organising principle: one beam of light from the left, the protagonists lit like actors in it, the background figures allowed to dissolve into rudimentary dark shapes, so the picture decides who matters by who is lit. The fine finish is the discipline: at 17 x 13.5 cm a tankard-holder's smile fills the picture the way a life-size portrait fills a wall, and the tactile differentiation of skin, hair, fur, embroidery, gold, metal, satin and leather makes the costume as much the subject as the man. The repertory company is the economy: the same faces and the same props -- the knotted tasselled sash, the chained metal flask, the feathered turban, the parasol -- re-cast across Bible, myth, allegory and portraiture, so sixteen paintings in eight years read as one small troupe playing many parts. The threshold is the architecture: the step before the vine-overgrown door, interior and exterior in one frame, the story happening on the line between them. And the failed crowd is the limit that proves the rule: fourteen sitters split in two, heads not sitting on bodies, the one commission at scale failing exactly where the small cast succeeds. There is no late loosening of handling to report: the last dated work, 1638, is as finely finished as the first, 1631. The career ends, on Van Haute's reading, not with decline but with interruption -- "cut short prematurely" in or after 1639, plausibly by death, possibly by the impossibility of competing with Rembrandt's workshop in Amsterdam.

## Positions

1. He died in or after 1639, not in 1657. The 1657 date rests on one Cologne drawing whose signature and date Fred Meijer judges not fully reliable; Van Haute's "in or shortly after 1639" is the scholarly consensus of the only monographic study. The study never uses 1657, and never says he returned to Enkhuizen or kept painting after 1639.
2. The Leiden stay of 1628 is hypothesis, not fact. The Web Gallery of Art biography says he "might have accompanied" Pieter Potter to Leiden; Van Haute shows it is unverifiable, and Bredius raises the counter-speculation that Potter joined Bartsius in Alkmaar. A Haarlem stay is ruled out by the guild archives, not merely undocumented.
3. The teacher is unknown. Potter-as-initiator is a suggestion from Fredericksen, single-source, and the study never states it as fact.
4. "Six signed works" is the count, not "six signed history paintings": four histories, one allegory, one tronie. The schutterstuk is unsigned. Four further signed works are known only as lost or missing.
5. The oeuvre count is 6 signed + 10 attributed = 16 extant paintings, 21 Appendix entries including the lost works. Van Haute's concluding "sixteen known works" of history painting conflicts with her own breakdown (7 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 17); the study follows the Appendix list, not the summary number.
6. The Rijksmuseum "A Captain" (SK-A-2214) is not a Bartsius on current evidence. It appears in Wikipedia's references and on aggregation sites, but nowhere in Van Haute's catalogue raisonne. The study does not list it as a work.
7. He is a history painter, not a genre painter. The older literature's genre comparison to Pieter Codde and Anthonie Palamedesz. is corrected by Fredericksen and Van Haute: biblical subjects outnumber genre pieces.
8. No direct Rembrandt influence is discernible in his handling. The peripeteia idea connects him with Rembrandt and his pupils; the chiaroscuro led Sumowski to call the Abraham "a more Rembrandtesque work". The study uses the kinship, not an influence claim.

## Network

The family is the solid core, and it is documented. Sister Aagje married Pieter Simonsz. Potter (Enkhuizen c. 1599 - Amsterdam 1652) and was the mother of Paulus Potter (Enkhuizen 1625 - Amsterdam 1654): Bartsius is Paulus's maternal uncle and Pieter's brother-in-law. Documented joint actions: 1 December 1635, both refused to be instituted as heirs of Frederickje's debt-laden estate; 25 May 1636, Pieter obtained permission to sell the estate, the request recording that Pieter "wrote to his brother-in-law Willem Bartsius (presently residing in the city)" about accepting the inheritance -- direct written contact, and evidence Bartsius was not yet living in Amsterdam at that date; 30 July 1636, Pieter as guardian of his children together with Willem claimed two years' rent on a house on the Spaensche burchwal, Enkhuizen; 11 April 1639, Bartsius requested a declaration about the gold-leather factory of Jacob van Ipen, in which Pieter Potter was a participant and usually ran the shop. The mother's 1635 estate inventory, drawn up by Pieter Potter, included "een landschapje van BAISIUS", valued f 6.

The artistic dialogue with Pieter Potter is the other documented strand. Pieter painted a very similar small-scale lute player, signed and dated 1636, after Bartsius's 1633 Lute Player -- Van Haute: "This confirms the ongoing competition between the two artists after Bartsius joined Potter in Amsterdam in 1636." A link with a representation of the same subject by Pieter Potter, known through an engraving of Pieter Nolpe, supports the attribution of the Interior with Sitting Woman and Standing Officer (W12). Manuth linked the Choice of Hercules (W10) to a lost Pieter Potter picture of the same subject known through a Nolpe engraving. Hofstede de Groot, on the lost Moses Tramples the Crown of Pharaoh (W18): "rather weak art, more or less reminiscent of his brother-in-law Pieter Potter."

The guild contemporaries: Alkmaar Guild of St Luke, registered 17 September 1633, master 1634. Caesar van Everdingen (1616/17 - buried 13 October 1678), born Alkmaar, fellow Alkmaar guildsman and history and portrait painter, painted the same civic-guard body seven years later -- Officers of the Orange Company of the Alkmaar Civic Guard (1641, Stedelijk Museum Alkmaar) -- on the way to the Oranjezaal with Jacob van Campen. No document records a meeting with Bartsius; the link is same city, same guild, same commission type. Emanuel de Witte joined the Alkmaar guild in 1636, the year Bartsius left for Amsterdam: same guild, no overlap in practice.

The closest stylistic kin is Willem de Poorter (Haarlem, active Leiden until 1630, then Haarlem 1630-1645): the central attribution problem. Both painted Samson and Delilah, Moses Tramples the Crown of Pharaoh, and St Paul and St Barnabas at Lystra; Sumowski held Bartsius used De Poorter's Berlin invention as model for his Samson, Van de Watering held De Poorter must have known and followed Bartsius's picture, and Van Haute leaves the question open -- "the circumstances of their relationship are puzzling." Whether they met, in Leiden in the late 1620s or on a Haarlem detour, is unknown. The Haarlem painters Hendrik Pot and Willem de Poorter are "perceived relationships" only, per Van Haute: "there is little chance, let alone evidence, that Bartsius practiced his art in Haarlem."

Frans Hals is a hypothesised model, not a documented one: Huys Janssen argued Bartsius "must have sought inspiration from the militia meeting completed by Frans Hals the previous year" -- Hals's Meeting of Officers and Non-Commissioned Officers of the Cluveniers (1633) -- for the 1634 schutterstuk. Van Haute: "There is, however, no direct evidence that Bartsius actually knew that group portrait." The pre-Rembrandtists, above all Pieter Lastman, popularised the Old Testament subjects Bartsius painted; Karel van Mander's Het Schilder-Boeck is the theoretical background for his attention to proportion, pose, invention, arrangement and emotion. Rembrandt's workshop monopolised the Amsterdam market for biblical histories; that competition may explain why Bartsius soon gave up painting -- or died.

The only documented pupil is Abraham Meyndertsz., recorded on the Alkmaar guild pupil list in 1634, possibly a maternal relative, nothing else known, no surviving work. No source read claims Bartsius taught or influenced his nephew Paulus Potter (Paulus was 11 when Bartsius enrolled in Alkmaar, 14 when Bartsius moved to Amsterdam; Paulus trained with his father). Do not claim it. Posthumous circulation is documented in inventories and wills: a will of 10 March 1658 mentions two drawings by Bartsius ("old tronies in black frames"); a drawing of two women was auctioned 8 March 1824; inventories list Bartsius landscapes in Leiden (1674) and Amsterdam (1661).

The scholarly network: Bernadette Van Haute (the monograph, Oud Holland 2008); Werner Sumowski (Gemalde der Rembrandt-Schuler, 1983-1994 -- the re-attribution engine); Fred Meijer (RKD -- signature authentication, attributions); Willem van de Watering (expertises); Burton Fredericksen (Getty, 1976 -- first modern discussion of the Abraham); Lubomir Slavicek (Prague Choice of Hercules); Paul Dirkse (the St Paul / St Peter pendants).

W02 Samson and Delilah, 1632, oil on panel, 66.4 x 86.7 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Zurich, Private Collection. Signed and dated bottom right "W.P BARCIUS 163(2?)"; the last digit now reads like a 7, but Hofstede de Groot recorded 1632 at the 1824 Loenen sale and Van Haute accepts 1632. Samson asleep, head on Delilah's left knee, while an old manservant cuts his hair; a Philistine with hands raised, a Moor servant and two children stand by; another Philistine beckons an old woman and soldiers entering at right; a high bed canopy at extreme left; flowers, a feathered turban, a stick and a parasol scattered on the tiled floor; a small dog gnawing a bone. Delilah looks out at the viewer and raises a finger. The signature surfaced only in the 1996 cleaning, under a false "G. DOV ... / 163(7?)" painted over it between 1824 and 1905. Sumowski's "masterpiece".
W03 The Lute Player, 1633, oil on canvas, 91.5 x 68 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Museum Wiesbaden, inv. M 805. Signed and dated bottom right "W. BARTSIVS. f. 1633". A young man in fanciful costume stands in a plain interior, left foot forward, looking straight at the viewer, playing the lute; to the left, a table draped with a dark blue cloth holds books, a piece of paper inscribed "Vanitas Vanitatis" and a chain. Costume: black beret decorated with jewels and a feather, metal-studded gorget, thin scarf round the neck, satin sleeve cut in slits, cape over the right shoulder finished with a red velvet border and heavy gold embroidery, striped breeches, long leather boots with upturned tips. W04 St Paul, undated, oil on panel, 69.5 x 59.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Museum Catharijneconvent, Utrecht, inv. StCC s 35. Signed top right "BARTIV F.". Bust, conceived as a pendant with St Peter (W08). Paul, long-bearded, in a blue cloak, turned to the right, looks at the viewer and holds the sword of his execution before his chest. Light falling from the left accentuates the roughness of the beard and the furriness of his brown cap with its burning red lining. Do not use the "1639" found on secondary aggregation sites -- it is unsourced.
W05 Death of the Levite's Concubine, 1638, oil on canvas, 100.5 x 110 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), State Hermitage Museum, St Petersburg, inv. GE-3384. Signed and dated. Judges 19:15-28: the Levite finds his concubine lying dead before the house door, throws his hands up and recoils in horror; the old peasant who lodged them sits pensive on the doorstep; the concubine lies on the threshold. The scene is set before a house door overgrown with dense foliage, extending left to ruined buildings. With Granida (W19), one of the two last dated works.
W06 Gentleman Holding a Tankard, undated, oil on panel, 17 x 13.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Signed "W. BARSIV". A small-scale tronie: the sitter smiles at the viewer and holds up a closed tankard in his right hand; white collar with laced border, a cape draped from the left shoulder over the right arm, a fur-lined hat recalling St Paul. With W16 it could be read as an allegory of Taste, but Van Haute notes the two were "not conceived as part of a series of the five senses" as far as can be told.
W07 Elijah Visited by the Angel, undated, medium and dimensions NOT FOUND -- the Van Haute main-text caption (fig. 3) gives oil on canvas, 112 x 86 cm; note 57 gives oil on panel, 48 x 35 cm. These may be two different versions, or a caption/note error in Van Haute; do not cite either figure without the flag. Location unknown; known only from an old RKD photograph. Elijah sits against a rock, hands folded on his chest in reverence, looking up at the angel hovering just above the ground, who points his right index finger to heaven and extends his left hand toward Elijah's head. The angel wears a loose garment tied by a long shawl wrapped round chest, stomach and hips, knotted in front and ending in tassels. A second version of the subject, once suggested as Bartsius, is "entirely untenable" (Van Haute).
W08 St Peter, undated, oil on panel, 69.5 x 59.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Museum Catharijneconvent, Utrecht, inv. StCC s 36. Unsigned -- only St Paul of the pair is signed. Pendant to W04: Peter, a bearded vigorous old man wrapped in a yellow cloak, turned to the left, looks upward and holds up two keys in a forcefully modelled right hand. The yellow cloak against the dark ground and the upward gaze give Peter the more ecstatic, less confrontational presence of the two.
W09 Jacob Shown Joseph's Blood-Stained Cloak, undated, oil on canvas, 48 x 64 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Collection Earl of Wemyss, Gosford House, inv. G.425. Jacob and his wife Rachel recoil at the blood-smeared cloak of Joseph brought by his brothers. The figures stand at left before a dilapidated, overgrown building, while the right half of the picture opens into an extensive landscape under a clouded sky. Jacob turns his head away, stretches out his left arm as if to push the cloak aside, and clutches his chest theatrically; Rachel crouches, wiping away tears with a cloth. The little boy walking up the platform steps "almost looks like a replica of Isaac". The owner refused permission to publish a reproduction -- no image was available to any leg.
W10 The Choice of Hercules, undated, oil on canvas, 156 x 169 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), National Gallery, Prague, inv. DO 4276. A portrait historie, per Slavicek: members of an Amsterdam family dressed as the story's heroes. The man with the "characteristic twisted moustache of the Amsterdam citizen" plays Hercules, rejecting Voluptas while his wife is cast as Virtue. Voluptas sits beside a "still life of sensual pleasure", pointing at an engraving of the nude Abundantia. The choice of good and bad is also symbolised by the capital letter Y on the rocks between the figures and by a putto crowning both protagonists with laurel wreaths. Authorship was long contested (Jacob de Wet, Jan Victors, Gerrit Willemsz. Horst, Willem de Poorter); Van Haute argues firmly for Bartsius. The tiny lute player in the background derives from the Wiesbaden Lute Player; the still life at bottom right includes the feathered turban and parasol.
W11 Ceres Riding her Snake Chariot, undated, oil on canvas, 80 x 99 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Ovid, Met. V, 642-644: Ceres, searching for her daughter Proserpine, rides her snake chariot holding a torch aloft. Against an "impenetrably dark background" she sits aloof, posture and face devoid of emotion, "merely posing gracefully" in a classicising outfit -- jewels in her hair, a crown of ears of corn, a veil fluttering behind her; wide sleeves strung tight at the wrists recall the angel in Elijah. She resembles Delilah in delicate facial features, carefully poised hands, tactile drapery folds and the shawl knotted round the stomach. The stillness here is the opposite pole to the Levite: a figure posed rather than moved.
W12 Interior with Sitting Woman and Standing Officer, undated, oil on panel, 60 x 48.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. A scene from a play, like Granida: at left a standing officer, tense, pays no attention to the woman seated at right, whose covered head and praying hands mark her as devout; a book lies on the table separating them. Both figures fix their eyes on something happening to the left -- Van Haute suggests the format was originally horizontal and the panel extended left. Previously attributed to Willem de Poorter; Sumowski's and Meijer's attribution to Bartsius "would appear to be correct".
W13 A Young Man in a Plumed Hat Playing a Theorbo-Lute, undated, oil on canvas, 112.5 x 106 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Private Collection. A variation on the Wiesbaden Lute Player: a young man sits playing his theorbo-lute against a dark brown neutral background, wearing a hat topped with two large plumes, a white shirt with wide sleeves tied at the wrists, a green coat, plain brown pants and leather boots. On a cloth spread on the floor at his feet, a still life of opened and closed books, pieces of paper -- the top sheet titled "Vanitas Vanitatis" -- and a skull. This is the work captioned "Lute player with skull and books" on EN Wikipedia and dated "c. 1640" by secondary aggregation sites; that date is unsourced and inconsistent with a death in or after 1639.
W14 Vanitas, undated, oil on canvas, 86.7 x 77.7 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Musee d'Art et d'Histoire, Geneva, inv. Basz. 14. A young woman sits in three-quarter view, staring at the viewer, in an interior before a brown curtain drawn to the right. Her left foot rests on a book; her left hand rests on a skull on her knee; her right hand holds a spoon with a bubble. She wears a loosely fitting white dress tied beneath her breasts with a belt and a heavy gold-coloured cloak, with a crown, strings of pearls and a large brooch. Behind her, a draped table holds an opened jewellery box, ornamental silverware, a nautilus cup and the familiar tasselled shawl. A large still life fills the bottom right corner on the tiled floor: globe, books, playing cards, a fallen glass, violin, flute and lute, a pipe, dice, an overturned hourglass and the metal flask with a chain. Long believed to be Willem de Poorter (since 1950); recognised as Bartsius by Fred Meijer.
W15 Officers and Standard Bearers of the Oude Schutterij of Alkmaar, 1634, oil on canvas, 198.2 x 385.7 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), Stedelijk Museum Alkmaar, inv. 20726. Dated middle right on the map "Ao 1634"; unsigned -- attribution rests on 18th-century historian Simon Eikelenberg's notes and the commission record, not on a signature. Fourteen officers at a meeting, identified as captains Adriaen Cornelisz. Schagen and Willem Willemsz. Kessel/Kissel, lieutenants Pieter Adriaansz. Pauw and Dr Adriaen Bijman, provost Adriaen Cornelisz. Seylemaecker, lieutenant of the provost Cornelis van Molenvliet, standard bearers Jan Cornelisz. Steenhuys and Willem Pietersz. Raaphorst, and midshipmen Jan Pietersz. Bijlevelt, Cornelis Jacobsz. Groet, Meynert van Duyn, Claes Buyk, Antoni Vladeracken and Cornelis Jansz. Bijl. The composition is split in two by the standard bearer standing in the middle: six men at right gather round a table with documents including a map showing a fortress; six men at left pose idly, three standing, three seated. Bartsius was paid f 225 for the painting plus f 90 for the ebony frame, still in place.
W16 Young Man with Flute, undated, oil on panel, 10 x 10 cm, octagon, VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Attributed to Bartsius by Bode (1883). Bust of a smiling young man holding a flute in his left hand, looking straight at the viewer; frizzy-looking hair topped by a plumed hat comparable to the standard bearer's in the Alkmaar schutterstuk. The smallest work in the oeuvre.
W17 The Sacrifice of Manoah, lost, oil on canvas, 60 x 72.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Signed "Baartsius F." on a stone of the altar in the middle. Known from Hofstede de Groot's record of the Christie's London sale 4 July 1927 no. 128 and the Ernst Museum, Budapest, sale 27 Apr 1931 no. 20. Van Haute stresses it is NOT the same picture as Abraham Pleading, despite Benezit and the 1927 sale title conflating them. Subject from Judges 13, in the manner of the pre-Rembrandtist Pieter Lastman. No description of the composition survives in the sources read.
W18 Moses Tramples the Crown of Pharaoh, lost, oil on panel, 54 x 65.5 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Signed and dated on the right "W BARTSIUS 1636". Known only from a Hofstede de Groot fiche dated October 1925, recording a lengthy description: "a long-bearded king in a gold-embroidered garment, a blond boy, a female fortune teller, a man of Jewish appearance entirely in red and with a pointed red cap on his head, a dwarf with a goatee, a soldier, a black man with a parasol, the queen with raised index finger making a listening gesture, two women talking to one another". Hofstede de Groot judged it "rather weak art, more or less reminiscent of his brother-in-law Pieter Potter". Willem de Poorter also painted the subject; the Bartsius version is untraced.
W19 Granida and Daifilo, lost, oil on canvas, height 92, width 113 "duim Ned." (Dutch inches -- Van Haute gives no centimetre conversion; none is offered here) REPORTED, location unknown. Signed and dated "BARTZIUS Ao 1638". Sold E. P. Cremer Jr., Middelburg, 17 May 1847, to Keizer. Known only from Kramm's description (1857): "In the foreground of a rocky wooded landscape is a respectable woman seated on a beautifully bridled white horse; a shepherd offers her a drinking dish..." A scene from the Dutch pastoral play Granida (1605) by Pieter Cornelisz. Hooft -- Daifilo the shepherd offering a dish to the Persian princess Granida. With the Levite's Concubine, one of the two last dated works (1638). Wurzbach thought it Erminia from Tasso; Sumowski recorded it as Granida and Daifilo.
W20 Portrait of a Cavalier, lost, support unknown, 114 x 85 cm VERIFIED (Van Haute), location unknown. Monogrammed and dated "W. B. F. 1634". A full-length portrait, also in 1634. The WB monogram was "often mistaken for that of Willem Buytewech" (Wurzbach, quoted Van Haute). No compositional description survives in the sources read.
W21 Holy Family, lost, support and measurements unknown -- NOT FOUND, location unknown. Recorded by Kramm (1857) and Wurzbach (1906) as executed "in the manner of Rembrandt". No trace remains. The third New Testament entry and the only one with no surviving image or object.

Further titles touched: the four inventory landscapes, known only as titles -- "een landschapje van BAISIUS" (mother's estate inventory, 1635, valued f 6); "een lantschap van Bartsius, bestaende principaelyck in een groote boom" (Paulyntje van Beusecom inventory, Amsterdam, 20 May 1639); "een strandingh van Willem Bartsius" (Willem van Campen inventory, Amsterdam, 10 October 1661); "een groot lantschap door Bartius" (Sara de Witte inventory, Leiden, 1674). Whether his painted landscapes are all lost or unrecognised through wrong attributions is unsolved. The drawing River Landscape with Castle, signed and dated "BARTIVS f. / 1657" (pen and brush in brown, 13 x 18.8 cm, Wallraf-Richartz-Museum, Cologne, inv. 1964/13) is of questionable authorship and is not a painting; it must not be used to extend his life. Rejected attributions, do not catalogue as Bartsius: a second, reversed version of Samson and Delilah; The Finding of Moses in two versions; Esther's Banquet; Esther's Toilette; Jacob's Dream; The Amalekite King Agag Killed by the Prophet Samuel; Kneeling Wailing Woman; a second Vanitas Allegory; An Interior, with a Seated Man Wearing a Turban and Clutching a Skull. NOT used: the Rijksmuseum "A Captain" (SK-A-2214) -- attribution unverified, absent from Van Haute's catalogue raisonne.
