# Brian Easdale: Study Notes

**Site:** Brian Easdale (b. Tuesday 10 August 1909, Manchester, England; d. Monday 30 October 1995, London, England, age 86, cause undisclosed -- he spent his final years in a private room at the Carlton Dene residential care home in Kilburn), British composer of operatic, orchestral, choral and film music; the Archers' house composer after Allan Gray, the man who wrote the music the camera danced to. He was hired to write a two-minute dance and ended up composing the whole of Black Narcissus; he wrote the Red Shoes ballet to 120 drawings before a foot of it was filmed; he won the Academy Award for the Music Score of a Dramatic or Comedy Picture for The Red Shoes (1948), the first British composer to win a film-score Oscar; and he finished, years after he had stopped, by scoring a 23-minute documentary about an island he had never filmed, for the director he had started with. No middle name is given in this study: none appears in any source read. [C33719]

## 1. Manchester, the Temple, the Abbey choir

He was born in Manchester and improvising at the piano from the age of five, producing short pieces over the next five years -- The Pied Piper of Hamlin, The Indian Temple -- the titles already giving away the two poles of his life: the story told in music, and the East as a sound. From the age of ten he was a probationer at the Temple Church in the City of London, on the advice of Sir Henry Walford Davies, and he was educated at Westminster Abbey Choir School and then the Royal College of Music, where he studied composition with Cecil Armstrong Gibbs and orchestration with Gordon Jacob, won the Foli Scholarship for composition and the Cobbett Prize, and had a concert of his own music organised with Herbert Murrill at the Wigmore Hall on 1 July 1931 -- Piano Sonata (1929), String Trio (1931), five pieces to recited texts by his sister, the poet Joan Adeney Easdale. The choirboy is the whole method in miniature: a boy trained to sing words he did not write, in a building built for resonance, learning that music is something a room does, not something a page says. [C33729]

**Takeaway:** Easdale's training was vocal and choral before it was orchestral -- the wordless choir of Black Narcissus, fifteen years later, is a choirboy's instrument, not a film composer's effect.

## 2. The Royal College and the two operas nobody staged

Before he was twenty he had written an opera, Rapunzel (1927), and a Death March conducted by Malcolm Sargent in 1928; a second opera, The Corn King, followed in 1935, with a libretto by Naomi Mitchison, and was not staged until 21 November 1950, in Paddington. The dates matter because they show a concert composer forming first and a film composer second: Six Poems given in Vienna in 1936, Five Pieces for Orchestra, a Piano Concerto broadcast in 1938, a Tone Poem in 1939. He was not a jobbing musician who drifted into film; he was a composer with operas behind him who treated film as a serious compositional form, which is why the Archers eventually gave him structural authority -- the whole soundtrack, the pre-recording, the stopwatch -- that no jobbing film composer of the period held. [C33730]

Two small flags ride on this early life and are carried honestly: the College dates are 1925-1933 in the Chandos booklet and 1927-1933 in AllMusic, unresolved; and Rapunzel's age is seventeen in some sources and eighteen in others, because the opera is dated 1927 and he turned eighteen in August of that year -- so this study states the year and no age. [C33731]

**Takeaway:** a composer who had already written operas before he wrote for film will treat a film as a score with pictures attached, not pictures with music attached.

## 3. The Hampstead bed-sit: MacNeice, Auden, Grierson

In the 1930s he lived in a Hampstead bed-sit, and the man downstairs was the poet Louis MacNeice, who suggested John Grierson's GPO Film Unit, where W. H. Auden was already working alongside the young Benjamin Britten. That staircase is the whole of Easdale's career in one landing: a poet sends a composer to a documentary unit, and the composer meets the other composer he will spend his life being measured against. He wrote documentary scores for the GPO Film Unit -- Big Money (1937), Job in a Million (1937), Men in Danger (1939) -- and Kew Gardens (1936), a four-movement suite that treats a botanical garden as a pastoral tone poem, "luxuriously warm and lambently Baxian", wind solos and cascades, Ravel and Debussy adjacent. No detailed cue-by-cue account of the GPO three survives in the sources read; they matter as the training ground, music that explains, accompanies and paces factual film, written quickly to picture -- the only phase of his career in which music is clearly subordinate to image. [C33732]

**Takeaway:** the documentary years taught him speed and function -- and, in Kew Gardens, the device he never abandoned: place rendered as musical atmosphere rather than illustration of action.

## 4. Britten's shadow: the Group Theatre years

He worked with Britten at the Group Theatre just before the war, orchestrated Britten's On the Frontier (Auden-Isherwood) for the Arts Theatre, Cambridge, in 1939, and arranged and orchestrated early Britten works. Christopher Palmer, in The New Grove, called Easdale's idiom "an eclectic English idiom that owes something to Britten as well as to the Bax-Bridge generations", and Wikipedia's gloss is blunter: "his combination of lush late Romanticism mixed with 'ethnic' colour and more austere Modernism suited the world of film music more than the concert hall." The Chandos review calls the Red Shoes ballet "a sumptuous melange of Ravel, Bax and Copland" and the Black Narcissus suite "half Gaelic with echoes... of Grainger's Jungle Book and Bantock's Gaelic Symphony". The shadow is structural, not personal: Britten is the colleague who became the national composer, and Easdale is the colleague who became the film composer, and the comparison followed him to the end -- his Missa Coventrensis of 1962, written for the consecration of the new Coventry Cathedral, was "inevitably overshadowed by his one-time colleague Britten's War Requiem" at the same event. [C33733]

**Takeaway:** Easdale's idiom was never pure -- late Romanticism, ethnic colour, austere Modernism, all at once -- and that impurity is exactly what made it work on film, where a score must be several things in one scene.

## 5. War: India, Ceylon, and the Godden friendship

He was called up early, served in the Royal Artillery, was assigned to the Crown Film Unit, and was sent on special assignment to Information Films of India, spending much of the war in Ceylon and India working on documentaries for the governments' film units. There he studied Indian music, in particular Nepalese music and instruments, collected the percussion instruments he later brought home, and formed the friendship with Rumer Godden, author of Black Narcissus, that won him the score. The wartime work itself is functionally invisible in the literature -- no titles beyond the GPO three are named in the sources read -- but the India posting is the hinge of his whole career: it gave him the exotic percussion, the Tibetan-horn sound world, and the Godden connection. The percussion instruments brought back from India were used in Black Narcissus, where, in the Guardian obituary's phrase, "enormous bellowing Himalayan trumpets call out at the very beginning of the film". [C33734]

**Takeaway:** a posting, not a plan, made him the Archers' composer -- a friendship in Ceylon and a suitcase of drums did more than any audition.

## 6. The audition piece: Kanchi's drums

He was hired at first only to write a two-minute exotic dance for Jean Simmons, as Kanchi, to perform in Black Narcissus. He made a piece "which almost entirely uses different kinds of drums". Powell and Pressburger were so impressed they gave him the whole score, and that audition piece survives in the film as "Kanchi's Dance". The mechanism is worth naming plainly because it recurs in every creative career and almost never gets written down: a small test, done without compromise, that swallows the job. The drum dance is still visible inside the finished work as a discrete number, and its sound world -- drums, flute, exotic percussion -- recurs as the film's identity. [C33735]

**Takeaway:** the test piece is the score in miniature -- if the two minutes are right, the two hours follow; if they are wrong, no amount of underscore saves it.

## 7. Black Narcissus: four themes and a tone poem

Black Narcissus (1947) is the breakthrough, and its score behaves as a tone poem about place and emotion rather than a theme-per-character system, though Craig Lysy's cue-by-cue analysis names four recurring themes inside it: the Himalayan Theme (woodwinds esotica, strings, nativist percussion, establishing the windswept setting at the top of the world); Kanchi's Theme (dance-like solo flute with exotic percussion accents, the rebellious seductress's identity); the Madness Theme (eerie wordless male and female voices over dissonant string figures, Sister Ruth's desire and madness); and the Flashback Theme (ethereal wordless vocalists and strings, Sister Clodagh's pre-convent past in Ireland). Around them the score is a continuous atmospheric argument. The opening is the statement of intent: massive Tibetan horns over snow-crested mountains, resplendent strings over the credits, then strings into Ruth's idealised Love Theme -- the film announces, in its first two minutes, that the mountains will be heard before they are understood. [C33736]

**Takeaway:** four themes are anchors, not a system -- the score's real work is atmosphere sustained across two hours, not motifs ticked off as characters enter.

## 8. The wordless choir

The same device -- wordless voices -- carries opposite charges, and that is the whole of the mechanism. In flashbacks it is ethereal and nostalgic: women's voices and strings as Clodagh remembers fishing with Con, the emerald necklace, the hunt. In Ruth's madness it is dissonant and otherworldly: mixed male and female voices with "strings sinistri". The choir is not singing words or liturgy; it is the interior state made audible. The MusicWeb review notes the writing is Delian and that the sung wordless syllables are printed in the booklet -- the voices are scored like instruments, with syllables as articulation, not meaning. [C33737]

**Takeaway:** a choir with no words can say two opposite things -- memory and madness -- because the listener supplies the words from the image; the music only supplies the charge.

## 9. Rival sound-worlds: bell against horn

The film's central conflict -- English order against Himalayan sensuality -- is staged as a contest between two musics. Tibetan horns answer the convent bell; nativist drums beat through the night against English hymn-singing; in "The Call to Prayer", Clodagh's bell is answered and contested by blasts of rival Buddhist horns. At Christmas the sisters sing "The First Noel" diegetically, Mr. Dean adding his male voice, and the cue flows into "Lullaby, Mine Liking", which triggers Clodagh's flashback, the song shifting to a solemn orchestral rendering as she extinguishes the altar candles. The conflict is heard before it is seen, and the two sound-worlds never resolve into one. [C33738]

**Takeaway:** when two cultures share a screen, give each a sound and let them argue -- the audience will hear the plot before it understands it.

## 10. Bells and drums as clocks

The bell marks liturgical time -- the 6 a.m. bell in the climax -- and the drums keep the other time, beating through the night and keeping Clodagh awake. In the climax Ruth rings the bell while the Madness Theme swells; a string ostinato tracks her escape; "a horrific accelerando" carries the wordless choir upward; and at 5:07 of the cue there is "a monstrous crescendo of horror" as Ruth pushes Clodagh over the edge and tries to pry her hands off the rope, Ruth falling in the end "supported by bell tolls and drums of death". A descent motif in the strings tracks Ruth running downstairs and down the hill. Time is counted out loud, and the counting is the dread: every toll is a step closer to the edge. [C33739]

**Takeaway:** a bell is a clock the audience cannot ignore -- put the deadline in the soundtrack and the tension keeps itself.

## 11. The composed film: stopwatches on set

Easdale attended rough-cut screenings during the shooting of Black Narcissus, taking notes, and before filming finished he and Powell appeared on set, each holding a stopwatch and the ten-minute score for the sequence being shot, with actors and crew rehearsed to a piano track Easdale had recorded. Powell's verdict, quoted by Kevin Macdonald's biography: "I have never enjoyed myself so much in my life. For the first time I felt I had control of the film with the authority of the music. It was astonishing to everyone, but particularly, of course, to the camera crew that were able to compress or speed up the movement of the action just by saying: 'No, that wasn't fast enough. We've only got seven seconds for that bit of action.'" Macdonald's summary is the definition: "Music was no longer incidental, but formed the emotional core of their films." Easdale was allowed to supervise the entire soundtrack, not just the music, "so that the sound effects became part of the score -- less naturalistic than dramatic". [C33740]

**Takeaway:** the composed film inverts the hierarchy -- the music is recorded first, the film is shot to playback, and time becomes musical time, counted in seconds per shot.

## 12. The handover: Allan Gray's ballet scrapped

Allan Gray was the Archers' first composer -- Blimp, A Canterbury Tale, I Know Where I'm Going!, A Matter of Life and Death -- and he was assigned the Red Shoes ballet first, a ballet "of concert hall quality". Unable to deliver what the producers had in mind, his work was scrapped after a single piano play-through; Easdale, thirty-eight, replaced him. The handover is the hinge between the two composers' runs: Gray 1943-1946, then Easdale 1947-1961, and the Red Shoes ballet is the seam. Gray's unused ballet music survives as an IMDb trivia line; Easdale's replacement, written at breakneck speed to 120 drawings, won the Oscar. The lesson is not that Gray failed but that the job changed underneath him: the Archers no longer wanted a concert-hall ballet fitted to a film, they wanted a film ballet written to the film's design, and Easdale was the man who would write to drawings. [C33741]

**Takeaway:** a replacement is not a punishment -- it is a change of brief; the new man wins by accepting the brief the old man refused, which was to compose to the picture's design, not to the concert hall's.

## 13. One hundred and twenty drawings

The Red Shoes ballet was composed to cartoon drawings and storyboards by Hein Heckroth, approved by choreographer Robert Helpmann, assembled in the correct sequence -- 120 drawings provided to guide him -- and as filming of the ballet progressed, the hand drawings were replaced by the corresponding completed shots. The score was written to "fit the cinematic design", in an unorthodox manner: not to moving images, not to a script, but to a sequence of stills that stood in for the film before the film existed. The ballet's eight short, sharply contrasted movements map onto drawn episodes -- fairground, corridor, ballroom -- rather than onto a dancer's phrasing, and that is why the music feels architectural: it was built to a plan, not to a performance. [C33742]

**Takeaway:** a storyboard is a timing document -- compose to the plan and the film will fit the music, because both were built from the same drawings.

## 14. Beecham, who never met the dancers

Easdale conducted all of the Red Shoes score himself except the ballet, which he insisted should be conducted by Sir Thomas Beecham, who recorded the soundtrack with the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra. Beecham "simply came to the studio to record the music and left it to the dancers and director to make sure that they danced and filmed to HIS account of the music. None of [the] artists ever met Beecham." Helpmann choreographed to the pre-recorded track; Leonide Massine created his own choreography for the Shoemaker; Moira Shearer danced the Girl to a tempo set by a man she never met. One flag rides here and is carried honestly: jennifervyvyan.org asserts flatly that the Red Shoes music "was conducted by no less than Thomas Beecham", while IMDb's Easdale credits list Easdale himself as conductor on the film -- so this study states that Easdale proposed Beecham and that Beecham recorded the ballet with the RPO, and does not present the conducting credit as settled beyond that. [C33743]

**Takeaway:** a recording can be a wall -- the conductor on one side, the dancers on the other, and the tempo the only thing that crosses it.

## 15. The Red Shoes ballet: eight movements

The ballet at the film's centre follows Andersen's story: the Girl (Shearer) puts on the red shoes and cannot stop dancing -- through a fairground, down a long corridor, into a ballroom where she splits into three and is lifted to become a bird and a flower; the Shoemaker (Massine) pursues her; the Boy (Helpmann) turns into a sketch on transparent cellophane as she dances. The John Wilson performing edition (Chandos) gives eight movements: I Allegro vivace (2:37); II Allegro moderato e molto ritmico (1:22); III Poco piu mosso (1:35); IV Lento tranquillo (0:53); V Largo (1:49); VI Presto, quasi cadenza (2:31); VII Allegro assai (2:24); VIII Sostenuto (2:27) -- short sections, one to two and a half minutes each, "a lot of musical activity packed into" them. Shearer's verdict on the music was that it was "exotic strange music -- a quite advanced modernistic score -- but good to dance to -- and written with great rhythmic feeling". [C33744]

The length of the ballet in the film is still conflicted and is carried as a conflict, not settled: 17 minutes per Wikipedia's film page and powell-pressburger.org, 15:39 for the Chandos recorded ballet, 15:56 for the 2009 restoration's standalone ballet, "fifteen minute sequence" in one MusicWeb review, and 18 minutes per the BSC (added in #784) -- five figures, and this study uses 17 minutes as the film-sequence figure because it is sourced twice, while noting the Chandos 15:39 is the recorded concert ballet, not necessarily the film sequence length. [C33745]

**Takeaway:** a ballet inside a film needs short movements and sharp contrasts -- each episode is a room, and the music changes when the dancer changes rooms.

## 16. The ondes martenot enters only when reality breaks

The Red Shoes score was the first British film score to use the ondes martenot, and the instrument is held in reserve: silent for the ballet's opening, then entering with "almost sub-sonic bass notes that add a very worrying air" as the Girl realises it is all going wrong, and a "lovely electronic glissando as the player runs her hand up the keyboard" when she imagines the Shoemaker is first Lermontov, then Julian. It returns in the nightmare Presto and in the Ravel-like triumphant sunrise of the Allegro assai. One unearthly voice equals compulsion: the electronic instrument only speaks when reality breaks, and its silence in the fairground is what makes its entry frightening. The full score was recovered via the Easdale family for the 2007 Proms, where Cynthia Millar played the ondes martenot -- the first public performance from the full score with the instrument in about fifty years. [C33746]

**Takeaway:** hold the strange instrument back -- an electronic voice that speaks all the time is decoration, but one that speaks only when the world breaks is a diagnosis.

## 17. The Oscar: first British composer

At the 21st Academy Awards, ceremony 24 March 1949, Easdale won in the category "Music (Music Score of a Dramatic or Comedy Picture)" for The Red Shoes -- the contemporary category name, not the modern umbrella "Best Original Music Score" that Wikipedia and the pick text use. The nominees were Hamlet (William Walton), Joan of Arc (Hugo Friedhofer), Johnny Belinda (Max Steiner) and The Snake Pit (Alfred Newman); the film took five nominations and two wins, the other for Art Direction, Colour (Heckroth and Lawson). He was the first British composer to win an Academy Award for a film score, corroborated three ways. He also won the Venice Film Festival 1950 International Award for Best Score for Gone to Earth. No other honours were found in any source read -- no BAFTA, no knighthood, no other festival prizes -- and the modern revival of the work is the 2011 Chandos CD The Film Music of Brian Easdale (CHAN 10636, BBC National Orchestra of Wales, Rumon Gamba, recorded March 2010). [C33747]

**Takeaway:** one Oscar and one Venice prize, and then nothing -- the honours record of a man whose work was admired inside the cutting room and filed away outside it.

## 18. The thinning: The Small Back Room

The Small Back Room (1949) is black-and-white, a bomb-disposal drama, and it "features barely any music score" -- a deliberate thinning after the maximalism of Narcissus and Red Shoes. The film is dominated by docu-drama realism -- taped windows, repair-work sounds, feet on pavement above the subterranean back room -- and Easdale's score is correspondingly sparse, "eerie score cuts" surfacing in the mono mix, short sharp interventions rather than continuous underscore. The one expressionist set piece is the whisky-bottle nightmare, in which Sammy, believing Susan has abandoned him, hallucinates the giant bottle growing to towering size and bearing down on him, designed by Heckroth partly to rival Dali's Spellbound dream sequence -- the one place the film lets music take over from realism. Powell noted the Chesil Beach bomb-defusing finale lasts almost exactly the same time as the climactic dance in The Red Shoes, about seventeen minutes: a long, real-time, single-task sequence held together by tension rather than by music, the inverse of the ballet, where music held the sequence together, and here the ticking is literal. [C33748]

**Takeaway:** scarcity is a device -- when the orchestra goes quiet, the one cue that does arrive lands like a shout, and a seventeen-minute sequence held by ticking alone is the ballet with the music taken out.

## 19. The near-musical that wasn't: Elusive Pimpernel

The Elusive Pimpernel (1950) was originally shot as a near-musical, with an extensive score by Easdale -- music carrying scenes, not decorating them -- and was then heavily interfered with by Alexander Korda's Hollywood partners, so the surviving film "cannot really be judged as [an] original work". The mechanism worth noting is the negative one: a score conceived as the film's main expressive system was cut back by producers, leaving a conventional adventure film, and the extent of what was cut, and what the near-musical form actually looked like, is not documented in the sources read. It is the composed film's vulnerability made visible: a method that gives music structural authority also gives producers something structural to cut. [C33749]

**Takeaway:** a score that carries the film can also be cut with the film -- the more the music does, the more the producers can remove by removing the music.

## 20. Gone to Earth: folk material absorbed, Venice

Gone to Earth (1950), Technicolor, Shropshire, 1897, Jennifer Jones as Hazel Woodus, a child of nature with a pet fox cub, won the music award at Venice in 1950. Easdale composed and conducted, and the score absorbs folk material rather than quoting it as background colour: the seven-episode Chandos suite (14:49, performing edition by Philip Lane) runs Titles; The Hunt of the Death Pack; The Prayer; The Shropshire County Fair; Hunter's Spinney; Undern Morning; Finale, and the MusicWeb review calls it "a powerfully gloom-laden, angst-wracked and cloud-hung pastoral gallery" with a "whimsically urbane Shropshire County Fair" as the one light episode -- the score's emotional register is dread and weather, not prettiness, despite the pastoral setting. Easdale included songs in the film score and later extracted them as his Mary Webb Garland for voice and piano, and the film used a real local Methodist church choir from Much Wenlock as extras, whom Powell heard sing and judged "not ragged enough". The American version, The Wild Heart (1952, recut by David O. Selznick, 82 minutes against 111), presumably cut music too; no source read quantifies this. [C33750]

**Takeaway:** folk material works when it is absorbed -- a fair becomes one episode inside a gloom-laden score, not a postcard pinned to it.

## 21. River Plate: Prelude and March

The Battle of the River Plate (1956) is a semi-documentary account of the trapping of the German pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee in Montevideo harbour, commercially very successful, the Archers "not at their best" in the Meares obituary's phrase. The surviving concert form of the score is just two movements -- Prelude (3:19) and March (2:25) -- and the march is the famous, stirring tune, published for orchestra and piano solo in 1957 and widely remembered. The mechanism is the public ceremonial march as narrative engine: naval procedure, ships, guns and flags rendered as a march, with the Prelude carrying the sea and the waiting, and a "Cabalgata de los Gauchos" folded in as local colour. A march is not background here; it is the film's metronome, organising time the way a score organises a ballet. [C33751]

**Takeaway:** a march can carry a whole film -- when the subject is procedure and ceremony, the music should march, not sing.

## 22. Peeping Tom: one piano

Peeping Tom (1960) is the sharpest break in Easdale's method. The score was written by Easdale and performed by the Australian pianist Gordon Watson -- a challenging solo-piano part that "takes up the majority of the sound world" -- and after the lush orchestral Archers scores, one piano, played by a virtuoso, carries a whole horror film. The thinness is expressive: a single instrument for a single, isolated consciousness. The film destroyed Powell's career on release and is now regarded as a masterpiece and a progenitor of the slasher film, and its critical mauling "harmed the careers of composer as well as director". [C33752]

**Takeaway:** reduction is a decision, not a budget -- one piano after a full orchestra says more about a lonely mind than any orchestra could.

## 23. The score that scores someone else's film

Adam Scovell's aural analysis divides Peeping Tom into three modes of viewing, and the score's meaning changes across them: in the regular mode Powell's camera follows Mark; in the irregular mode the view switches to first-person footage from Mark's camera; in the post-modern mode characters watch Mark's recorded footage. The piano score is baroque-tinged and melancholic in the regular mode, but when the film switches to Mark's footage the piano reveals itself as the accompaniment to Mark's films, not Powell's: "Easdale's music no longer scores Powell's film but instead... his music is in fact scoring Mark's films. By doing this, the irregular mode of viewing implicates the audience in Mark's actions... the viewer is the voyeur just as much as Mark is." The same piano that reads as tragic underscore in mode one reads as Mark's own silent-film accompaniment in modes two and three -- and the audience realises it has been watching through his eyes all along. [C33753]

**Takeaway:** a score can change its owner mid-film -- the moment the music stops belonging to the director and starts belonging to the killer, the audience becomes an accomplice.

## 24. The radio that fades out

In the dance sequence with Moira Shearer as Vivian, a diegetic radio blasts period "tropica" music, and as Mark enacts his plan the radio music "inexplicably fades out. There's no explanation given within the visuals of the film; the sound simply zones out. It is however replaced with the piano music that has gradually become a leitmotif for an impending murder which proceeds to get louder and louder." The murder leitmotif is presented as music Mark is scoring in his own head -- if his past haunts him with voices the viewers can hear, the viewer can also hear his cinematic auteurism deciding on his own musical scoring. And Mark's camera never records his victims' voices: "their screams are lost in his memory which is instead pervaded by memories of his father's psychological experiments on him", and the piano fills the space the scream would occupy. [C33754]

**Takeaway:** a diegetic sound that fades for no in-world reason is the sound of a mind taking over -- the radio dies so the killer's own score can be heard.

## 25. The Queen's Guards and the long silence

The Queen's Guards (1961), a military drama about a Grenadier Guards officer preparing for Trooping the Colour, was Easdale's last Archers-era score and the last time he worked with Powell on a feature; he was conductor and musical director as well as composer, and no cue-level description of the score was found in the sources read. Positionally it closes the arc: the film is literally about ceremony, drill and a public march -- the march idiom of River Plate reduced to its ceremonial core. After it he effectively stopped film work. Miracle in Soho (1957, Pressburger solo) is remembered by the people trying to preserve his music only for a love theme; Happy Deathday (1968) is a late, minor credit with no description found; and then a long silence, broken only by the 1978 return. [C33755]

**Takeaway:** the arc ends on ceremony -- the march that began as a naval tune ends as drill, and then the composer stops, because there is nowhere left for the march to go.

## 26. Coventry, the Mass, and Britten's shadow

After the film work ended in the early 1960s he returned to concert music. Missa Coventrensis (1962), for choir, congregation and organ, written for the consecration of the new Coventry Cathedral, was "inevitably overshadowed by his one-time colleague Britten's War Requiem" at the same event. The Sleeping Children (1951), his third opera, had been the first opera commissioned by Britten's English Opera Group, with a libretto by Tyrone Guthrie, "full of dissolving dream images of sex and sadism", alienating most critics at its Cheltenham premiere. A Red Shoes Suite in four movements for concert orchestra followed in 1963, and he was actively involved in its live performance at the 1994 Kenwood Music Festival, conducted by Iain Sutherland. An alcoholic phase in the 1960s is recorded in Wikipedia; then decades in a private room at the Carlton Dene care home in Kilburn, where he continued to work and taught orchestration to a composer and arranger almost to the end. [C33756]

**Takeaway:** the concert works never escaped the film works' shadow -- and the film works never escaped Britten's; a career can be defined by the two shadows it stands between.

## 27. Return to the Edge of the World, 1978

One final Powell collaboration: at sixty-nine, after years away from film, Easdale composed and conducted snippets of incidental music for Return to the Edge of the World (1978), Powell's 22-minute documentary in which Powell, John Laurie and producer Sydney Streeter return to Foula, the Shetland island where Powell shot The Edge of the World in 1937, forty years earlier, and meet the islanders. No description of the music was found in the sources read, but the gesture matters: the composer of the Archers' most artificial, studio-bound fantasies returns, at sixty-nine, to score a plain documentary about a real island and real old men remembering. The arc closes where it opened -- documentary scoring, 1937 to 1978 -- and the man who learned to write music first and fit the film to it ends by writing music last, to a film already shot, about a place already remembered. [C33757]

**Takeaway:** a career that starts in documentary and ends in documentary has come full circle -- the fantasy was the detour, and the island was the home.

## 29. The lost scores

Most of the original scores are reported lost. The 2002 powell-pressburger.org re-recording campaign reports that most original scores "seem to have vanished", with only a few deposited in the British Library, and the Red Shoes full score was recovered via the Easdale family for the 2007 Proms -- the first public performance from the full score with ondes martenot in about fifty years. The Chandos CD of 2011 exists because Philip Lane made performing editions from what survived. The consequence for this study is direct: almost everything written here about cue detail is second-hand, from Lysy's cue-by-cue Black Narcissus analysis, Scovell's Peeping Tom analysis, and the Proms eyewitness account of the ondes martenot entries -- and for The Small Back Room, The Elusive Pimpernel, Outcast of the Islands, Miracle in Soho, The Queen's Guards, Happy Deathday, Return to the Edge of the World and the documentaries, no cue-level sources exist at all. [C33760]

**Takeaway:** a composer whose scores are lost is studied through other people's ears -- the analysis is the archive, and the gaps in it are part of the record.

## 30. The non-film composing

The operas and concert works are the other half of the life and the reason the film method was possible at all: Rapunzel (1927), The Corn King (1935, staged 1950), The Sleeping Children (1951); Death March (1928), Five Pieces for Orchestra, Six Poems (1936), Piano Concerto (broadcast 1938), Tone Poem (1939), Concerto Lirico for piano and orchestra (Cheltenham Festival, 1955, Barbirolli conducting the premiere), Kew Gardens suite (1936), Bengal River, The Phoenix, Adventure On! suite (1957), Cavatina for brass ensemble (1961); Missa Coventrensis (1962), Seelkie, a lyric drama for chorus and chamber orchestra (1954), Leaves of Grass, a Whitman song cycle, the Mary Webb Garland for voice and piano (songs from Gone to Earth), Evening Prelude for organ (1951). The through-line is a concert composer who treated film as a serious compositional form, not a sideline -- which is why the Archers gave him the whole soundtrack and the stopwatch. [C33761]

**Takeaway:** the film scores work because the concert works exist -- a man who can write an opera can write a ballet to drawings, and a man who can write a Mass can write a wordless choir.

## 31. Aesthetic logic: music as authority

Easdale's aesthetic logic is a single inversion, held for fourteen years and then abandoned: music is not the accompaniment to the image but the authority over it. In the documentary years music explained and paced; in the composed-film years (1947-1951) it commanded -- the score was written first, recorded first, and the camera, the dancers and the actors were fitted to it, with a stopwatch as the instrument of command. The devices all serve that inversion: the audition piece that swallows the film, the storyboard as composition brief, the wordless choir as psychology, the rival sound-worlds, the bell and the drum as clocks, the electronic intruder held in reserve. Then the inversion thins: The Small Back Room starves the film of music, Peeping Tom reduces the orchestra to one piano and hands the score to the killer, and the march takes over as the public, outer form of the same authority -- a metronome the whole film can march to. What never changed is the belief that music carries the emotional meaning, and the willingness to let one sound -- a drum, a bell, a piano, an electronic wail -- carry a whole scene. [C33762]

The style that carries the logic is impure by design: lush late Romanticism mixed with ethnic colour and more austere Modernism, Ravel and Bax and Copland in the same bar, a tone poem rather than a theme system, place as atmosphere rather than illustration. The impurity is the point. A pure style would have given the Archers one kind of film; Easdale's mixed idiom gave them a Himalaya built in Surrey, a ballet built from drawings, a horror film scored by one piano, and a naval battle scored by a march -- four different films, one ear. [C33763]

**Takeaway:** give the music authority and the film will obey -- take it away, thin it to one piano, and the film will confess whose music it really is.
