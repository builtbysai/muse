## M1. Open form: the mobile form

The composition is divided into fixed, fully composed modules, events on pages, and the order, the repetition, the tempo and the dynamics of those modules are decided during performance, not in advance, and the score is a set of available forms, not a fixed sequence. Twenty-Five Pages of 1953, written in spring 1953, is the first open-form score, 25 unbound pages in any order, either way up, and Brown's June 1953 note says the basic mobile elements of the piece, page sequence and inversion, clef disposition and time, admit of a considerable number of different presentations. Available Forms I of 1961 is 29 events on six pages, and the conductor begins with any event on any page and builds the work by selecting events and manipulating tempo and dynamics at will. Available Forms II of 1962 is 48 events for two orchestras and two conductors. Novara of 1962 is open form in chamber scale, and Brown's own definition is that the form of the work is unique in each performance but it is always NOVARA because only those composed sound events may be used. Modules I and II of 1966 are pages in any order, chords in any sequence. Brown's own phrase for the whole idea is "a condition of mobility". What you see is a conductor visibly choosing, and the music re-forming itself in real time, and no two performances alike, and each recognizably the same piece. **Takeaway:** compose the parts, not the order, and the piece becomes a mobile. Against the chain: Feldman also divided a piece into fixed units, M1, the graph time-boxes, but his boxes are pinned to the wall in a fixed order, and the freedom is inside the box, and Brown's freedom is the order of the boxes themselves, and the difference is between a fixed frame with free content and a free frame with fixed content.

## M2. Graphic notation: the score as drawing

Musical information is drawn as shapes on a page instead of notes on a staff, lines, rectangles, fields, and the graphic properties, position, length, thickness, direction, stand for musical properties. December 1952 is a single page of horizontal and vertical lines of varying width and thickness, no pitches, no clefs, no meter, and it is a landmark piece in the history of graphic notation. Hodograph I of 1959 sketches the contour and character abstractly, in a graphic style more gestural and calligraphic than the geometric abstraction of December 1952, and the word hodograph is a mathematical term for a curve of velocities, and the piece graphs motion, not notes. From Available Forms I onward, graphic notation is used on the staff in some sections. Brown's claim, with his own qualification, is that Folio holds the first scores which could be called graphic scores in our particular period of contemporary music and the first improvisational scores, and he adds that he would like to know if there were others working with such elements at the time, and he notes that Feldman's 1951 graphs predate his, see C26. What you see is a page that looks like a Mondrian or a technical drawing, and is read as music. **Takeaway:** draw the music, and the drawing becomes the score. Against the chain: Cage also drew music, M10, the graphic notation and transparent overlays, and his drawings are laid over a staff and read by chance operations, and Brown's drawings are the score itself, read by a performer choosing, and the difference is between a drawing that chance reads and a drawing that a person reads.

## M3. Time notation: proportional notation

Rhythms are indicated by the horizontal length and placement of notes relative to each other, to be interpreted flexibly, instead of by metrical values, and pitches and dynamics are exact, and durations are approximate and left to the players. Music for Cello and Piano of 1954 to 1955 is the textbook example, pitches and dynamics in traditional notation, durations left to the players. Twenty-Five Pages of 1953 uses it, and Modules I and II of 1966 use stemless note heads which could be interpreted with even greater flexibility, and Times Five and Novara use it. Feldman's verdict, quoted by Welsh, is that Brown's time notation, in fact, has become part of modern-day compositional usage. What you see is notes spaced across a page like objects in a room, and the player judging the gaps. **Takeaway:** space the notes, do not count them, and the gap becomes the rhythm. Against the chain: Feldman also freed duration, M4, the racecourse design, but his racecourse fixes the pitches and frees only the clock, and Brown's time notation fixes pitches and dynamics and frees the spacing itself, and the difference is between four players drifting apart on a fixed line and one player judging the distance between two notes.

## M4. The conductor as real-time chooser: the placard system

A conducting technique invented for open form. The conductor holds up a placard showing the page number, and the left hand shows which event is to be played, and the right hand gives the downbeat, whose speed and intensity suggest tempo and dynamics. In December 1952 the conductor is a kind of producer, and the left hand at the top of the head means high register, and at the waist means low register, and the conductor chooses timbre and combinations, stops and starts players, changes tempi and color. Available Forms I and II use the placard system, and Cross Sections and Color Fields uses it on the letter F page, and Modules I and II use it. With two conductors, in Available Forms II, the two feed off each other, really like a conversation, and one puts percussion on what the other is doing with brass and strings, and the other answers by taking the brass out and bringing in the woodwinds, and at the final Venice rehearsal Luigi Nono told them, do it that way tonight, and neither could remember what they had done. What you see is two people visibly negotiating a piece in front of an orchestra, by hand signals. **Takeaway:** give the conductor a placard, and the choosing becomes the performance. Against the chain: Cunningham also gave a chooser the evening, M12, chance applied to the whole evening, but his chooser is a die, or a chart, deciding the order before the dancers move, and Brown's chooser is a person, deciding in the moment, in front of the orchestra, and the difference is between a choice made in advance by a system and a choice made live by a hand.

## M5. The Calder mobile as score model: physical against conceptual mobility

Brown's founding analogy. A Calder mobile is an object defined by its movement and having no other existence, and its parts keep the same identity while their relationships change constantly, and Brown distinguishes two mobilities, physical mobility, where the score itself is moved, reordered, turned over, and conceptual mobility, where a fixed graphic is read from different mental directions. December 1952 is a photograph of a certain set of relationships of moving elements, to be considered constantly in movement in all dimensions. November 1952, subtitled Synergy, is read by the eye moving from any point to any other point on the page. Twenty-Five Pages is physical mobility, pages reordered and inverted. The Modules are the conductor re-sequencing fixed chords. Brown says, "My first influence for open form music was Alexander Calder mobiles and not from music", and the impulse came around 1948 to 1949 in Boston, and some of Calder's earliest mobiles were motorized, and December 1952 was conceived as a motorized box on the piano, with horizontal and vertical elements moving at different gearings, never built. What you see is identical elements in endlessly recombining relationships, the same pieces, never the same picture. **Takeaway:** build the piece like a mobile, and the relationships become the music. Against the chain: Cunningham also built from a moving object, M7, the set as an independent performer, but his set is a stage object the dancers move around, and Brown's mobile is the score itself, and the difference is between a room that moves and a page that moves.

## M6. The tape library and the out-takes box: collage of pre-existing material

Composing by selecting and recombining fragments of already-recorded sound, using a program to decide placement, without regard to what the fragments originally were. Octet I of 1953 does it, and the Project's tape library was categorized as city sounds, country sounds, electronic sounds, instrumental sounds, and Brown worked out a program of durational and attack densities from random-sampling tables, then applied it to fragments so brief that their origins were obscured, to have them exist as sheer sound, and as a by-product he used a box of out-takes, the sound content of which was unknown, composing with them in a way that did not depend upon their actual content. What you see is recognizable sounds, speech, singing, orchestral snippets, noise, cut so short and so densely patterned that they stop being quotations and become pure texture. **Takeaway:** cut the sound until its origin is gone, and the fragment becomes a material. Against the chain: Cage also cut tape, M8, the tape cutting and splicing of Williams Mix, but his cutting is a chance operation deciding where each of 600 fragments lands, and Brown's cutting is a density program deciding how many fragments land, and the difference is between a pattern that chance assembles and a density that a program measures.

## M7. The statistical program: random-sampling tables with bias

A manual, pre-computer program. A coordinate grid, abscissa and ordinate, frequency and duration, is laid over the page, and random-sampling tables, something like 10,000 numbers that are cybernetically randomized, supply numbers, and at each intersection the program says whether a line goes left, right, up or down, and how long it is, and how thick, and the composer introduces prejudice, bias potentials, so the result has varying densities rather than uniform distribution, and he stops by taste when the field is full enough. December 1952 places every element by the program, and Octet I runs a density program from the same tables, and Indices of 1954 applies the coordinate technique to some 175 pages of standard notation. Brown says the entire technique of the assemblage was very close to a kind of computer programming in construction, done by hand. What you see is a field that looks random but has clumps and clearings, a designed randomness, denser here, sparser there. **Takeaway:** program the randomness by hand, and bias it, and the field looks designed. Against the chain: Cage also ran a program, M5, charts and geometric selection, and his charts tabulate every parameter and a shape drawn across the table chooses the notes, and Brown's tables choose only where the lines go, and the composer keeps the right to stop, and the difference is between a chart that composes and a table that places.

## M8. Tape as spatial instrument: sound choreography

Loudspeakers are placed around the audience as instruments in their own right, and sound is choreographed to move between them. Octet I is eight separate channels through eight independent sound sources spaced equidistantly, 360 degrees around the audience. Times Five of 1963 is five sound sources in an X at the front of the auditorium, four loudspeakers with the live group at the center point, and the taped material is choreographed to move between and across this space as a kind of kinetic wall of sound. Music for Galerie Stadler of 1964 is four tape loops as an installation. Wikiup of 1979 is six independent playing devices. What you see is sound that travels around the room, so the listener sits inside the instrument. **Takeaway:** put the speakers in a circle, and the room becomes the instrument. Against the chain: Cage also placed sound in space, M7, the radio as an unpredictable instrument, but his space is the air, and the radio brings the outside in, and Brown's space is the room, and the speakers carry the sound around the listener, and the difference is between a window and a circle.

## M9. Closed form with open interiors: the hybrid

The late solution to the open-form problem is a piece with a definite beginning and end, closed form, that contains open sections inside it, open solos, open pages, open cadenzas. Cross Sections and Color Fields of 1972 to 1975 is closed form overall with eight open-form sections, and the letter F page is a fully open page inside an otherwise through-composed piece, and the conductor can call for brass chord II, winds chord I, a percussion cycle with a da capo, strings 1-2-3-4, determining rhythm and tempo. Tracking Pierrot of 1992 features open solos. Corroboree of 1964, String Quartet of 1965 and Windsor Jambs of 1980 all combine both open and closed forms. Brown's analogy is the old concerto, where the soloist was allowed to create cadenzas. What you see is a solid frame with a loose middle, and the piece arrives somewhere definite, but the middle is different every time. **Takeaway:** close the frame and open the middle, and the piece arrives somewhere new each time. Against the chain: Feldman also mixed the fixed and the free, M4, the racecourse, but his mix is fixed pitches with free durations, and the whole piece is the mix, and Brown's mix is a closed frame with open rooms inside it, and the difference is between a piece that is free all the way through and a piece that is free in its rooms.

## M10. The playable object as conductor

A physical sculpture does the conducting. The performers read the moving sculpture and translate its changing configuration into sound, and they also strike the sculpture itself as an instrument. Calder Piece of 1963 to 1966 does it, and Calder made Chef d'orchestre in 1964, a playable standing mobile, and the four percussionists stand equidistant around it, and they imagine the mobile's image superimposed over the score and play the areas of the score corresponding to the mobile's position, and they also play the mobile, including the petals of the mobile itself, and some 100 percussion instruments are employed, and the piece was finished in 1966 and first performed early in 1967 at the Theatre de l'Atelier in Paris, and Brown insisted that the music must never be independent of Chef d'orchestre. What you see is four people watching a slowly turning sculpture and darting between a hundred instruments as it moves, and the sculpture conducts, and the humans obey and answer. **Takeaway:** let the sculpture conduct, and the players become its readers. Against the chain: Cunningham also let an object run the evening, M7, the set as an independent performer, but his set is moved by the dancers, and the dancers are the ones who decide, and Brown's mobile moves on its own, and the players read it, and the difference is between a set the dancers move and a sculpture the players obey.

## M11. Sound objects and chord pillars: the block

Sound is treated as a solid thing that sits in space, a block, a pillar, a field, rather than as a line that goes somewhere. The Modules of 1966 to 1969 do it, and each page is four or five vertical chord structures, sound pillars, each with its own intervallic fingerprint, register and orchestration. Time Spans of 1972 and Cross Sections and Color Fields of 1972 to 1975 are shifting, monumental orchestral blocks with an emphasis on color and harmonic density. The New York Times obituary's phrase is Brown's vision of sound as an almost concrete object, and Brown says, "I'm an abstract sound composer, and I make sound objects which can sit there, and also sound objects which can be varied from performance to performance". What you see is huge, still, colored masses of sound that hang in the air and then shift. **Takeaway:** make the chord a block, and the block becomes the music. Against the chain: Feldman also made sound into an object, M6, sound as a plastic phenomenon, but his object is a sound projected into time, and it floats, and Brown's object is a pillar that sits, and it stands, and the difference is between a sound that floats and a sound that stands.

## M12. Choice, not chance

The deliberate distinction Brown drew against Cage. Cage's chance means a mechanism, coin flips, I Ching, randomizes the result and removes choice, and Brown's open form means informed human choice among real possibilities, made in the moment by people who know the material. The 1991 interview states it at length, "My open form music has choice, multiple choice... Nobody flips coins", and "chance is a technique. You don't go to Las Vegas and play the one-arm bandits by chance. They are chance because they have a mechanism which randomizes the results", and "Cage's music is to some extent created by chance operations. There's no way you can go over to a piano and play one of the eighty-eight notes by chance". And in the 1970 monologue, Cage's coin-flipped continuities were played that way always, even according to a stopwatch, which is a high degree of control and eliminates almost totally the possibility of a performer being flexible. What you see is a performer deciding, visibly, in the moment, not a die being rolled. **Takeaway:** let the player choose, and the choice becomes the music. Against the chain: Cage also let something decide, M6, the I Ching coin-toss chance operations, but his decider is a book, and the book removes the player, and Brown's decider is the player, and the player keeps the choice, and the difference is between a coin that decides and a hand that decides.

## M13. Improvisation as collective conversation: the jazz root

The jazz combo is the model of performance, players listening and answering each other in real time, so the piece is a conversation, not a dictation. December 1952 for more than one player is a collective improvisation, and the String Quartet is the case where the musicians can, in collaboration, make different avenues and possibilities and potentials of realization, and Brown's image is the Jerry Mulligan Quartet, one played a line and then the other one goes an answering line, it is like you and I having a conversation where we exchange ideas, and his stated aim is to introduce the possibility of the musicians not only playing what I write, but interacting like a human family of friendly people. What you see is players watching each other, answering, overlapping, and the music as a roomful of people talking. **Takeaway:** let the players talk, and the talking becomes the piece. Against the chain: Cunningham also let performers answer each other, M6, dancer autonomy, but his autonomy is each dancer deciding their own movement inside a shared clock, and Brown's conversation is players answering each other's sound, and the difference is between a dancer deciding alone and a player answering a friend.

## M14. The score as a photograph of a field: 2D read as 3D

The page is not a timeline but a picture of a space at one instant, and the performer is asked to consider the two-dimensional elements as three-dimensional, moving through space and time, and to move through them. December 1952 is like a photograph of these elements at one moment, but one should consider them constantly in movement in all dimensions, and Brown's own performance note suggests the performer consider this 2D space as 3D and imagine moving through it. The motorized box that December 1952 was meant to be had elements physically moving in front of the pianist, crossing in front of and behind each other, obscuring each other. What you see is a flat page treated as a room you walk through, not a road you follow left to right. **Takeaway:** photograph the field, and let the player walk through it. Against the chain: Duchamp also made a flat thing into a space, M15, the cast shadow and the trace, but his flat thing is a picture that becomes a sculpture, and Brown's flat thing is a score that becomes a room, and the difference is between a picture that leaves the wall and a page that becomes a place.

## M15. The feedback condition: tape ground against live figure, conductor against orchestra

Two layers respond to each other in performance, a fixed layer and a free layer, or a chooser and the chosen, so the piece is made by the exchange itself. Times Five does it, and the tape is an unchanging ground upon which the live material is superimposed, spontaneously and differently in each performance, with a deliberate ambiguity as to origin of sound details between the visible instruments and the tape. Available Forms II does it, and the two conductors are in an immediate and direct feed-back condition of response and forming. Brown on conducting Cross Sections and Color Fields says it is a feedback between the conductor and the orchestra, in a very exciting, stimulating way. The open-form rehearsal rule is that the individual musical events are rehearsed, but the performances are not. What you see is a fixed tape humming underneath while live players improvise against it, or a conductor cueing players who answer back faster than expected. **Takeaway:** fix one layer and free the other, and the exchange becomes the piece. Against the chain: Cage also set two layers against each other, M12, simultaneous independent layers, but his layers are independent, and they meet only by accident, and Brown's layers answer each other, and the meeting is the point, and the difference is between two clocks that happen to agree and two people in conversation.

## M16. Reversal and mirror symmetry: the page turned over

Scores are designed so that orientation is a variable, pages played either way up, graphics read from four sides, two scores that mirror each other. Twenty-Five Pages is notated symmetrically and without clefs, so the top and bottom orientations are reversible. December 1952 is readable from any of the four quadrant positions, right-side up, upside down, sitting on the right margin, or on the left margin. Modules I and II are built so that the union of chords 1 to 4 in one is chord 5 in the other, and the orchestration is swapped between string and brass and woodwind components, mirror images of the same vertical structures, and on page 1 the composite scale of the union of chords 1 to 4 in Module I is identical to chord 5 of Module II, and vice versa. What you see is the same page turned upside down becoming a different piece, and two orchestras playing the same chords in swapped colors. **Takeaway:** turn the page over, and the piece becomes its mirror. Against the chain: Feldman also repeated with a difference, M11, crippled symmetry, but his difference is a deliberate error inside the repetition, and Brown's difference is the whole page turned over, and the difference is between a pattern that is wrong in one place and a page that is right in every direction.

## Aesthetic logic
