## The life: expelled student, LIDL agitator, cafe stage-manager, conductor

Jorg Immendorff was born on June 14, 1945, in Bleckede, Lower Saxony, near Luneburg on the west bank of the Elbe. When he was eleven his father left the family, a break the sources use to explain his later feelings of inadequacy and emotional remoteness. He attended the boarding school Ernst-Kalkuhl Gymnasium, and at sixteen he had his first exhibition in a jazz cellar in Bonn. (C26151, leg 1 C26057-C26059)

In 1963 he began studying at the Art Academy in Dusseldorf, first stage design for three terms under the theatre designer Teo Otto. Otto threw him out of his class for refusing to let one of his paintings serve as stage-set decoration, and Joseph Beuys then accepted him as a student. He switched to Beuys's sculpture class in 1964 and was Beuys's student for the next three years. The academy expelled him in 1969 because of his left-wing political activities and neo-dadaist actions. The expulsion is the hinge of the whole career: the student thrown out of two classes in six years made a lifelong practice out of refusing the institution while painting inside its largest formats. (C26152, leg 1 C26060-C26064)

The first body of work he gave a name to was LIDL, paintings, sculptures, performances and documents executed during 1968-1970. The name was inspired by the sound a child's rattle makes, and much of the work from this period carried the iconography of new beginnings and innocence -- turtles, dogs, goldfish, playhouses, polar bears -- used as working material for a new functional art. LIDL was comparable to Dada but never became an established group; it was a loose set of artists, including James Lee Byars, Marcel Broodthaers, Nam June Paik and Beuys himself, taking part in actions and activities. Art historian Pamela Kort is quoted: LIDL "ridiculed elitist art traditions, the cult of creative 'genius', and the precious aesthetic object". In January 1968 he appeared in front of the West German Parliament in Bonn with a wood block labelled "Lidl" tethered to his ankle and painted in the colours of the German flag; he was arrested for defaming the flag. Ketterer says the LIDL actions of 1968-1970 were developed by Immendorff in cooperation with the artist Chris Reinecke, his first wife; the two founded the LIDL Action Room and rented a room in Pempelfort in 1968 where they organised exhibitions and events. (C26153, leg 1 C26065-C26067; leg 3 C26127)

From 1969 to 1980 -- Ketterer gives the start as 1968 -- Immendorff worked as an art teacher at a public school in Dusseldorf, interrupting his work as an independent painter during the student movement. The schoolteacher decade is the quiet middle of the biography: the agitator who had been expelled from the academy spent eleven years inside a state school, and the didactic paintings of the early 1970s -- school scenes overwritten with text, pictures that ask the viewer a direct political question -- come straight out of that room. (C26154, leg 1 C26068; leg 2 C26095, C26096)

In East Berlin in the fall of 1976, Immendorff met A. R. Penck. Together they formed the "Kollektiv Immendorff-Penck"; the artists from East and West Germany committed themselves to recording political issues in their pictures, campaigned for the abolition of the inner German border and for dissidents, and met again in East Berlin on May 1, 1977, organising several joint actions and exhibitions in the following years, including the joint 1977 Cologne show "Penck mal Immendorff, Immendorff mal Penck". ALBERTINA calls the 1976 encounter a landmark event, resulting in "history paintings" in which the two artist friends expressed their political views in dense and colourful compositions full of symbolism and contemporary allusions, because, according to Immendorff, an artist must not shirk his political responsibility. He began working on Cafe Deutschland in 1977: sixteen large-sized paintings (the count is disputed -- see M1), inspired by Renato Guttuso's Caffe Greco, in which disco-goers symbolise the conflict between East and West Germany. (C26155, leg 1 C26069, C26070; leg 3 C26126)

He took part in documenta in 1972 and 1982 and the Venice Biennale in 1976, according to Ketterer; the Stadel instead says documenta 5 and 7 in 1972 and 1983, and the 37th Venice Biennale in 1972. His works were part of the travelling exhibition "Expressions: New Art from Germany" in 1983-84, visiting New York, Philadelphia, St. Louis, Oslo and Malmo among others. His teaching posts, per Ketterer: guest lecturer at the Stockholm School of Art in 1981; taught at the Hamburg School of Fine Arts a year later, at the same time teaching the Class F+F in Zurich; lectureships in Cologne and at the Munich Academy of Fine Arts followed in 1984; professor at the Stadelschule in Frankfurt in 1989 and a year later at the Dusseldorf Art Academy -- Wikipedia instead dates the Dusseldorf professorship to 1996 and says his master students there included Oda Jaune and Renata Jaworska; in 1997 he lectured at the Academy of Fine Arts in Tianjin in China. He was, in the end, a professor at the same school that had dismissed him decades earlier as a student. (C26156, leg 1 C26071, C26072; leg 3 C26122)

In 1984 Immendorff opened the bar La Paloma near the Reeperbahn in Hamburg St. Pauli and created a large bronze sculpture of Hans Albers there. He created several stage designs, including two for the Salzburg Festival: Elektra (Richard Strauss) in Bremen in 1986 and The Rake's Progress (Igor Stravinsky) at the Salzburg Festival in 1994, the latter also inspiring a series of paintings in which he cast himself as the rake. He contributed to the design of Andre Heller's avant-garde amusement park "Luna, Luna" in 1987, and for three months from 1987 until 1988 he was the foreign artist in residence at the Auckland Art Gallery in New Zealand. One spectacular sculpture is a 25 m tall iron sculpture in the form of an oak tree trunk, Elbquelle, erected in Riesa in 1999. The bar, the stage and the 25-metre oak are the same habit at three scales: Immendorff kept building rooms and sets for other people to walk into. (C26157, leg 1 C26073-C26075)

In 1997 Immendorff won the MARCO prize of the Museum of Contemporary Art in Monterrey, Mexico, described as the world's best endowed art prize at the time; in 1998 he received the merit medal (Bundesverdienstkreuz) of the Federal Republic of Germany, and in 2006 the Goslar Kaiserring. In 2000 he married his former student Oda Jaune; the two had a daughter, Ida, born on August 13, 2001. In August 2003 he was caught in the luxury suite of the Steigenberger Parkhotel in Dusseldorf with seven prostitutes (and four more on their way) and some cocaine. He cooperated with the prosecution, admitted to having taken cocaine since the early 1990s, and at the trial in July 2004 admitted to having organised 27 similar orgies between February 2001 and August 2003. He was sentenced to 11 months on probation and fined 150,000 euros; the mild sentence was justified with his illness and his extensive confession. He had been suspended from his university position but was reinstated after the verdict. (C26158, leg 1 C26076-C26078)

Immendorff was diagnosed with ALS (Lou Gehrig's disease) in 1998. When he could not paint with his left hand any more, he switched to the right; in 2004 he funded a stipend to research the disease. ALBERTINA says the diagnosis decisively changed his pictorial themes: his works until 2003 are characterised by bright, lucid colours and enigmatic motifs, while from 2004 onwards the pictorial themes were once again transformed by his inevitable approaching death, and existential themes dominate. In November 2005 he was treated by emergency physicians and admitted to hospital, where a tracheotomy had to be performed to help him breathe. As of 2006 he used a wheelchair full-time and did not paint any more; instead he directed his assistants to paint following his instructions. His own words, to Die Zeit on March 31, 2005: "my assistants prime canvases, they prepare templates, and I set the notes, I am the composer and the conductor." (C26159, leg 1 C26079-C26081; leg 2 C26105, C26106)

He died in Dusseldorf in 2007, aged 61 -- May 28 per the Wikipedia infobox and lead, Ketterer and the Spencer Museum; May 27 per the Wikipedia body text and Prabook. A CBC report said he "died Monday after he went into cardiac arrest at home in Duesseldorf"; May 28, 2007 was a Monday and May 27 was a Sunday, which supports May 28, but this file prints the split and does not pick a side. His ashes were scattered in the Mediterranean Sea. In his will he left his entire estate, estimated at 15-18 million euros, to his wife; a 12-year-old son from a former relationship who never knew his father went to court over the size of his legitime. A major survey began at the Haus der Kunst in Munich in 2019 and later travelled to the Museo Reina Sofia in Madrid and the Fondazione Querini Stampalia in Venice, curated by Francesco Bonami. (C26160, leg 1 C26083, C26084, C26086)

## M1 -- The stage-room

From Cafe Deutschland I (1978) on, everything happens inside a single fictional interior -- a bar, a cafe, a theatre -- that stays the same while the cast changes. The series is sixteen large paintings by Wikipedia, Ketterer, Luna Luna and artchive, dated 1977-1984; nineteen paintings, all before 1982, by the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts and the Reina Sofia brochure; "hundreds of oils" begun in 1977 and pursued "for nearly a decade" by the Michael Werner Gallery; "around 20 variations" by the Kunstpalast press release; MoMA dates the series 1977-1982 and counts paintings, drawings and prints. This file prints the split and does not pick a number. Numbered entries verified: I, II, III (all 1978), VI/Cafeprobe (1980), X/Parlament I (1981), XIII (1982), XIV/Quadriga (1982), XV/Schwarzer Stern (1982), plus the 1984 closing canvas. The room is a machine for holding opposites together: East and West, Nazi past and Cold War present, high art and disco, the artist and the politician. Evidence: W09 Cafe Deutschland I 1978 (285 x 333 cm per Reina Sofia, 285 x 273 cm per the Expressions catalogue -- dimensions conflict carried; Immendorff reaches his hand through the wall while decadent scenes continue behind him; Brecht hovers above like a glowing UFO; the cafe is a bunker-like refuge); W10 Cafe Deutschland (Style War) 1980 (uninhabited tables with candles burning in red, black, yellow -- the German flag colours -- and a freestanding bookcase painted with the Brandenburg Gate standing in for the Berlin Wall, with small figures scaling it); W11 Cafe Deutschland VI / Cafeprobe 1980 (280 x 350 cm; the cafe explicitly a rehearsal, history staged as a run-through); W14 Cafe Deutschland XIV / Quadriga 1982 (282 x 400 cm; a five-pointed Soviet star as a dominating ice floe, the Quadriga of the Brandenburg Gate as the centre between East and West); W15 Cafe Deutschland 1984 (285 x 330 cm, Wikipedia image caption; the series' closing statement, the crowded colourful disco-stage format at full scale). (C26161, leg 2 C26090-C26094, C26113; leg 3 C26121)

The watchable behaviour is a room that never changes its address while everything inside it is swapped: the same bar, the same tables, the same candles, but a different crowd arguing a different year. The viewer learns the set first and then reads the cast, the way a regular learns a pub and then notices who is sitting in the corner tonight.

**Takeaway:** build one room and re-stage it -- a fixed set with a rotating cast turns every new picture into a new argument in a room the viewer already knows. (C26162)

## M2 -- The mediating self-insert

Immendorff paints himself inside the scene as the go-between who holds the opposites together. In Cafe Deutschland I he reaches his hand through the wall to shake hands with Penck on the other side; in the Cafe de Flore group he paints himself in the centre as a geisha with a fan; in the Rake's Progress paintings he casts himself as the rake; in the last self-portraits he sits at a candle-lit table contemplating the palette in his lifeless hand, and in the 2006 untitled painting he stands at the bar as a skeleton, presumably his own self-portrait. The self-insertion starts early -- W08 Self-Portrait in the Studio 1974 (200 x 150 cm) paints the artist as a figure among figures -- and it never stops; what changes is the role he gives himself: commuter, rake, geisha, corpse. Evidence: W08; W09 (the hand through the wall); W12 Ostjorg 1980 (250 x 200 cm; Immendorff as a divided figure, West-Jorg and East-Jorg, a solidarity portrait tied to Penck's expatriation to the West in 1980); W19 Gyntiana 1992-93 (350 x 700 cm; Immendorff as a geisha in the centre, a fan showing a small figure digging to make the desert fertile); W20 Letztes Selbstportrat I -- Das Bild ruft 1998 (393 x 300 cm; gaudily made-up, at a candle-lit table, engulfed by the silhouette of an eagle, palette in a lifeless hand, made the year of the ALS diagnosis). (C26163, leg 2 C26092, C26097, C26103, C26107, C26108; leg 3 C26126)

The watchable behaviour is the viewer's eye finding the painter first, inside his own picture, and using him as the compass: wherever he stands, that is where the two sides meet. He is never the hero of the scene; he is the hinge it turns on.

**Takeaway:** put yourself in the picture as the hinge, not the hero -- the artist-figure standing between the two sides is what lets the viewer read a crowded scene as one argument. (C26164)

## M3 -- The slogan-picture

The early mechanism is reduction and protest: a political demand painted as the picture itself, so the canvas is a placard, not an illustration. W02 Hort auf zu malen (Stop Painting) 1966, synthetic paint on canvas, 132 x 132 cm: a rallying cry painted as a picture, iconoclastic text inside the canvas, the seed of the whole text-and-image mechanism. Immendorff said it himself: Beuys "rejected painting as an art form", which "inspired my 1966 painting Hort auf zu malen (Stop painting). That painting was born out of hard criticism". W03 Vietnam. Vietnam. Vietnam. 1966 reduces an anti-war protest to a repeated three-word chant as the whole picture content; repetition is the image. W06 Die Meinungssaule (The Opinion Column) 1972, dispersion paint on canvas, 75 x 92 cm: an everyday school scene overwritten and annotated with text, image pattern evoking a Polaroid, text and image standing side by side as equal signifiers of meaning. W07 Wo stehst du mit deiner Kunst, Kollege? (Where Do You Stand With Your Art, Colleague?) 1973, acrylic on canvas in two parts, 130 x 210 cm: the picture asks the viewer -- and the artist -- a direct political question; the title is the mechanism, the work is a demand for a position. The early-1970s didactic paintings consciously reject painterly virtuosity: the function of a picture is to transmit a rallying cry, not an aesthetic message. (C26165, leg 2 C26088, C26095, C26096; leg 3 C26137)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that reads before it is looked at: the words hit first, the image second, and the viewer is put on the spot instead of being shown something. The canvas does not depict a protest; it is one.

**Takeaway:** paint the demand, not a picture of the demand -- when the text is the image, the canvas stops illustrating and starts accusing. (C26166)

## M4 -- The symbolic furniture

Ordinary objects stand in for political structures: a bookcase is the Wall, a candle is the flag, a table is the border, a seam is the wound. W10 Cafe Deutschland (Style War) 1980: a freestanding bookcase painted with the Brandenburg Gate stands in for the Berlin Wall, with small figures scaling it, while candles burn in red, black and yellow on uninhabited tables. W13 Naht (Suture / Seam) 1981, oil on canvas, 180 x 400 cm: the inner-German border rendered as a scar, a single graphic seam across a wide horizontal canvas carrying the whole political content -- the border as a wound, stitched shut. The LIDL action W04 (January 1968) works the same way at body scale: a wood block labelled "Lidl", painted black-red-gold, tethered to the artist's ankle in front of the Bonn Parliament -- an infantile word chained to a state symbol, the work being the action and the arrest rather than an object. Even late, the habit holds: in W18 3. Oktober '90 (1990), reunification day is a domestic still life -- Immendorff sits with Max Ernst at a table with a dish of steamed potatoes, Breton carries a crate of cucumbers, one bearing the date 3 October 1990, and Penck releases an eagle from a cage in the background. (C26167, leg 2 C26089, C26094, C26098, C26102)

The watchable behaviour is a small thing doing a large job: the eye lands on a bookcase or a potato and only then understands it is looking at a wall or a reunification. The symbol is always a household object first and a political structure second, which is why it lands -- the viewer handles it before decoding it.

**Takeaway:** let one ordinary object carry the whole structure -- a bookcase can be a wall and a potato can be a reunification, and the picture is stronger for refusing to paint the structure itself. (C26168)

## M5 -- The crowd as argument

The Cafe Deutschland pictures are dense, jostling crowds of identifiable figures whose crowding IS the political statement: no empty space, no centre, no exit. The nineteen (or sixteen) paintings depict contemporary figures from both German states in shifting configurations, Immendorff often in the mediating position of a cross-border commuter, the border a recurring motif. The crowd is not decoration around a subject; the crowd is the subject -- two Germanys forced into one room with nowhere to stand apart. Evidence: W09 (the crowded disco-stage, decadent scenes continuing behind the artist's reaching hand); W14 (painters handing over paintings made of shards of ice, feathers of the fallen imperial eagle scattered through the cafe -- Cafe Deutschland XIII, 1982, per the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts); W19 Gyntiana (350 x 700 cm panorama of fragmentary scenes where figures are "only triggers, or signs"); W16 Ende gut, alles gut (All's Well That Ends Well) 1983 (his most expensive painting at auction, Sotheby's London, February 7, 2007, $567,272 -- the ironic verdict on the whole divided-Germany cycle, the title itself doing the arguing). The LIDL actions are the same mechanism at street scale: the January 1968 Parliament action puts one body, one block and one flag in front of the state, and the crowd -- parliament, police, press -- completes the work by arresting him. (C26169, leg 2 C26092, C26093, C26099, C26103; leg 1 C26066)

The watchable behaviour is claustrophobia with a pulse: figures hand each other objects, feathers and ice shards drift through the air, nobody has room to pose, and the eye cannot settle on a hero because there is no empty space for one to stand in. The argument is the density itself.

**Takeaway:** crowd the room until the crowding is the point -- when no figure has space to pose, the picture argues by density, not by composition. (C26170)

## M6 -- The repeated set with shifting cast

The same room is re-staged many times, so meaning comes from what changes between versions, not from one image. Cafe Deutschland is the clearest case: one fictional bar interior, restaged sixteen (or nineteen, or around twenty) times between 1977 and 1984, the cast rotating through contemporary figures from both German states while the set -- tables, candles, the Brandenburg Gate bookcase, the carpet carrying the motif of divided Germany -- stays recognisable. W11 Cafe Deutschland VI / Cafeprobe (Cafe-Rehearsal) 1980, synthetic resin on canvas, 280 x 350 cm, makes the mechanism explicit: the cafe is a rehearsal, history staged as a run-through, so the viewer is invited to compare this version against the last one. After 1982 the room stays but the cast changes and the room itself starts to shrink: in W13 Naht the room is reduced to a single seam; in W18 the room shrinks to a dinner table with potatoes and cucumbers; in W19 Gyntiana the room becomes a panorama of fragmentary scenes where the picture can also be read as a landscape. The late works keep the habit: the 2006 untitled painting is still a bar scene, but the room is now dark, the artist is a skeleton, and the cast is the dead -- Duchamp, Beckmann, Hitler. (C26171, leg 2 C26090, C26093, C26098, C26102, C26103, C26108, C26115)

The watchable behaviour is serial recognition: the viewer who has seen one version spots what moved in the next -- a figure gone, a star turned to ice, the candles out. The meaning is in the delta, the way a regular notices the pub has changed its landlord.

**Takeaway:** repeat the set and let the changes do the talking -- when the room stays put, every swapped figure and missing candle is a sentence. (C26172)

## M7 -- The quotation collage

Art-history quotations are pasted into a contemporary scene so that the past argues with the present. The late work multiplies the quotations: Friedrich, Baldung Grien, Durer, the Italian masters, and above all Duchamp. The 2006 untitled painting (Albertina/Essl) quotes Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase (1912) in the centre background, the nude -- possibly the artist's wife -- sitting relaxed on the steps, while in the left half a couple plays chess after the famous photograph of Duchamp and his wife, Max Beckmann watches, and Adolf Hitler sits at the table on the right, accompanied by his dog. W19 Gyntiana shifts the cast to Duchamp, the Expressionists and the Surrealists in the Cafe de Flore group (1987-92). The Rake's Progress series, begun in the 1990s and inspired by Hogarth via the 1994 Salzburg Festival opera staging, casts Immendorff himself as the rake; W04 in that line, "Immendorff's Progress / The Rake's Progress Goes On" 1994 (gouache, watercolor, pencil and crayon on paper, 13 3/4 x 9 7/8 in, MoMA), shows actors seen from behind, standing in a row on the edge of the stage, gazing out at the confused goings-on in the auditorium -- the audience's viewpoint reversed. The quotation is never decoration: the past figure is placed in the room to argue with the present ones. (C26173, leg 2 C26101, C26104, C26108; leg 1 C26074, C26081)

The watchable behaviour is a double-take: the viewer recognises a famous pose inside a contemporary crowd, and the recognition reframes the whole scene -- the nude on the stairs is not a nude, it is Duchamp sitting in on the argument.

**Takeaway:** quote the past as a guest, not as a backdrop -- a recognisable figure dropped into a new room makes the present argue with history instead of merely illustrating it. (C26174)

## M8 -- The depoliticised still life

A historic event is shrunk to tabletop props so that history is handled, not declaimed. W18 3. Oktober '90 (1990) is the purest case: German reunification day, shown as a domestic still life -- Immendorff sits with Max Ernst at a table with a dish of steamed potatoes; Breton carries a crate of cucumbers, one bearing the date 3 October 1990; Penck releases an eagle from a cage in the background. The largest political event of his lifetime is reduced to potatoes, cucumbers and a caged eagle, and the reduction is the point: after fifteen years of shouting the division across 400 cm canvases, the reunion fits on a table. The LIDL works are the same move in reverse at the start of the career: childish motifs -- turtles, dogs, goldfish, playhouses, polar bears -- used as working material for a new functional art, the grand claims of art deflated into toys. W05 Die Lidlstadt nimmt Gestalt an (The Lidl-Town Takes Shape) 1968, chalk on wood, each panel 70 x 90 cm: utopian town planning as drawing, chalk plans on blackboard for cardboard houses, each house a function-specific marker for an "idea space" -- architecture as diagram, a whole city reduced to a blackboard sketch. (C26175, leg 2 C26088, C26102; leg 1 C26065)

The watchable behaviour is scale shock: the viewer expects a history painting and gets a dinner table, and the smallness forces attention onto each prop -- the potato is the event, the cucumber carries the date, the eagle is the only thing allowed to leave the frame.

**Takeaway:** shrink the event to the tabletop and the props do the history -- a potato with a date on it says more about a reunification than a crowd scene can. (C26176)

## M9 -- The conductor method

The artist as composer and conductor, directing other hands while keeping the decisions; authorship separated from execution. ALS was diagnosed in 1998; Immendorff switched from his left hand to his right, then could no longer paint at all. From 2006 he used a wheelchair full-time and directed his assistants to paint following his instructions. He described the new method himself: "my assistants prime canvases, they prepare templates, and I set the notes, I am the composer and the conductor." The pictures change mechanically under this method: motifs blur, art-historical quotations multiply, themes turn to metamorphosis, transience and mortality, and the compositions take on the characteristics of collages without renouncing painting. The narrative tinsel is reduced step by step so that form and colour are at the centre -- a "breakout into freedom" in his own words. The 2006 untitled dark-room painting and the portrait of Gerhard Schroder (2007, oil on canvas, 100 x 130 cm, completed by his assistants, revealed January 2007 -- Schroder in stern heroic pose in the colours of the German flag, painted like an icon, surrounded by little "painter monkeys") are both conductor works: the hand on the canvas is not his, and the picture is still unmistakably his. The painter monkeys are the joke on the method itself -- a recurring motif serving as an ironic commentary on the artist's business. (C26177, leg 2 C26105, C26106, C26109; leg 1 C26079-C26082)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that looks like Immendorff but was not touched by him: the viewer watches for the tell -- a blurred motif, a collage seam, a monkey in the corner -- and finds the authorship in the decisions, not in the brushwork.

**Takeaway:** separate the decisions from the hand -- when one intelligence sets the notes and other hands execute, the work is still the composer's, and the seam between decision and execution becomes part of the picture. (C26178)

## M10 -- The vanitas self-portrait

The artist paints himself as already dead, so the picture is a farewell that keeps working. W20 Letztes Selbstportrat I -- Das Bild ruft (Final Self-Portrait I -- The Picture Calls) 1998, oil on canvas, 393 x 300 cm: Immendorff gaudily made-up, sitting at a table in candle glow, engulfed by the silhouette of an eagle, contemplating the palette in his lifeless hand -- made the year of the ALS diagnosis, the self-portrait become a vanitas and a farewell. The final self-portraits resume the composition of "Bild mit Geduld" (1992): the artist at a table, candle-lit, palette in a lifeless hand. The 2006 untitled painting goes one step further: the skeleton, presumably the artist's self-portrait, stands at the bar next to a large, brightly burning candle that casts long shadows -- the painter as a skeleton serving drinks in the underworld, surrounded by the dead (Duchamp, Beckmann, Hitler) and the living (his wife, on the stairs). ALBERTINA: the works until 2003 are characterised by bright, lucid colours and enigmatic motifs, while from 2004 onwards the pictorial themes were transformed by his inevitable approaching death, and existential themes dominate. In 2006 he selected 25 of his paintings for an illustrated Bible; in the foreword he described his belief in God. (C26179, leg 2 C26107, C26108; leg 1 C26081)

The watchable behaviour is a portrait that refuses to flatter or to mourn: the made-up corpse looks back at the viewer, the palette hangs dead in the hand, and the candle keeps burning beside a skeleton that is still, impossibly, standing at the bar. The picture does not end; it waits.

**Takeaway:** paint yourself already dead and the portrait stops being a likeness -- the candle, the lifeless palette and the skeleton at the bar turn a self-portrait into a working farewell. (C26180)

## The aesthetic logic: the message moves from the placard into the room, then out of the painter's hand

Immendorff's whole career is one movement performed three ways: first the message is ON the picture, then it is IN a room, then the painter's hand is OUT of the picture altogether. The early work (W01, W02, W03, W04, W05, W06, W07) puts the message on the picture: a slogan painted as a canvas, a chant repeated three times as the whole content, a wood block chained to an ankle in front of parliament, chalk town-plans on a blackboard, a school scene overwritten with text, a picture that asks the viewer a direct question. Painting is a tool for agitation and painterly virtuosity is consciously rejected. The middle work (W09, W10, W11, W12, W13, W14, W15, W16, W17, W18, W19) puts the message in a room: one fictional cafe, restaged sixteen or nineteen times, the cast rotating through both German states, the artist himself standing in the middle as the hinge; then the room shrinks -- a seam, a dinner table, a panorama of fragments -- while the habit of staging never leaves. The late work (W20, the 2006 untitled painting, the Schroder portrait) takes the painter's hand out of the picture: ALS, the switch from left hand to right, the wheelchair, the assistants, the conductor's podium -- and the room goes dark, the artist is a skeleton, the cast is the dead. (C26181, leg 2 C26115; leg 1 C26079-C26082)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the picture is always a public act, never a private meditation: a placard, a parliament action, a crowded cafe, a bar he actually opened, a 25-metre oak in a public square. Second, the artist is always inside the work as a figure among figures -- the hinge, the commuter, the rake, the geisha, the skeleton -- never above it as an invisible author. Third, the work is always a procedure carried out in front of witnesses: a chant repeated, a block dragged, a room restaged, a set of notes handed to assistants. That is why Immendorff is the loud pole inside the Dusseldorf cohort and the opposite pole of Palermo even though both were Beuys students expelled from the same class in the same year: Palermo strips the picture down until only a procedure and a rule remain and asks the viewer to walk through what is left, while Immendorff loads the picture up until it is a crowded room full of shouting figures and asks the viewer to find where they themselves stand in it. (C26182, leg 2 C26115; leg 3 C26123, C26124; leg 1 C26062, C26063)

The evolution is the proof. 1966-1976: the exits from painting begin (W01, W02, W03, W04, W05, W06, W07, W08) -- the picture becomes a placard, a chant, an action, a blackboard plan, an interrogation, and the artist starts painting himself into the scene. 1977-1993: the stage-room years (W09, W10, W11, W12, W13, W14, W15, W16, W17, W18, W19) -- one cafe restaged again and again, the crowd as the argument, the border as a seam, reunification as potatoes on a table, the cast shifting from politicians to Duchamp and the Surrealists. 1998-2007: the hand leaves the picture (W20, the 2006 untitled painting, the Schroder portrait) -- the conductor method, the collage character, the vanitas self-portrait, the room gone dark. The late work does not abandon the procedure; it widens the field it is applied to, from one slogan on one canvas to a whole cafe to a whole dark room full of the dead, and the answer in every case is the same -- put the message where people can walk into it, stand in the middle of it yourself, and let the witnesses complete it. (C26183, leg 2 W01-W20; leg 1 C26060-C26086)
