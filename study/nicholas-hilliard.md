# Nicholas Hilliard: Study Notes

**Site:** Nicholas Hilliard (c.1547 - before 7 January 1619; buried 7 January 1619), English goldsmith and limner, best known for his portrait miniatures of Elizabeth I and James I. Eldest son of Richard Hilliard, a leading Exeter goldsmith; a boy in Protestant exile (Wesel, Frankfurt, Geneva, recorded at Geneva on 8 May 1557 aged about ten in the Bodley party); apprenticed in November 1562 to Robert Brandon, the Queen's jeweller; freeman of the Worshipful Company of Goldsmiths on 29 July 1569; in France 1576-1579 in the train of the English ambassador and in the service of Francois, Duke of Anjou; keeper of a shop in Gutter Lane, off Cheapside, from 1579 to 1613, where he broadened the miniature's market from the court to the gentry and, by the end of the century, to well-off city merchants; limner and goldsmith to Elizabeth I for thirty-two years (his first known miniature of the Queen is dated 1572) and to James I, who in 1617 gave him a twelve-year sole licence for royal portraits in engraved form; author of The Art of Limning (c.1600), unpublished in his lifetime; died about 3 January 1619 in debt, buried on 7 January 1619 in St Martin-in-the-Fields in an unmarked grave, his legacies unfunded. His miniatures are watercolour on vellum, usually calfskin, pasted to a playing card, painted in flat open light with almost no shadow, the face laid on a pre-made flesh ground, the jewels and lace built up in thick paint and real gold and silver, the whole small enough to be held in the hand and set in a jewelled locket. Almost 200 miniatures attributed to him survive. The record is strong on the technique and the Elizabethan years, and thin on how he learned limning at all (self-taught by copying Durer, his own claim; taught by Levina Teerlinc, Strong's suggestion, with no evidence the two met), on the goldwork of the great jewelled lockets (the miniature inside each is his; the case is his design or his workshop's, and in the Lyte Jewel probably another goldsmith's), and on the Jacobean years, where the repetitions weaken and his pupil Isaac Oliver takes the fresh work. ## M1. The Carnation Blank
Before the sitter arrives, an opaque flesh-coloured ground ("carnation") is laid over the face and neck area of the vellum; Hilliard kept a stock of pre-made flesh blanks in different shades to save time. Lead white was the basis of the carnation; the treatise also mentions "quicksilver white" as an alternative, the same material women used as face makeup. Pigments were ground to a fine powder and mixed with water in a mussel or oyster shell. Watchable behaviour: the face appears as a flat, warm, matte disc of colour first, and all features are then drawn onto that disc; the portrait is built face-first on a pre-coloured field, never on bare skin. Source: leg 1 (treatise as reported in secondary sources; V and A; Gloriana).

**Takeaway:** the face is painted onto a ready-made patch of skin colour; the ground is prepared before the person arrives.

## M2. Faint Outline, Then Faint Hatching
Features are drawn very faintly with a fine squirrel-hair "pencil" (brush) in brown watercolour, then filled out by faint hatchings. No preliminary drawing on paper survives; the drawing happens directly on the vellum. The outlines of the features are painted very faintly before being filled out by faint hatchings. Watchable behaviour: edges are soft and linear, built from hairline strokes that accumulate rather than from a single confident contour; the face is assembled from strokes, not outlined. Source: leg 1 (treatise as reported; the 1573 "Very Proper Treatise" tradition).

**Takeaway:** the face is not drawn and then coloured; it is hatched into being, stroke by faint stroke.

## M3. Open Light, No Shadow
Deliberate refusal of chiaroscuro: the sitter is lit in flat, open daylight with no cast shadow, and the painting uses almost no modelling shadow. Hilliard's doctrine, written into the treatise: "seeing that best to show oneself needeth no shadow of place but rather the open light", and Elizabeth "chose her place to sit for that purpose in the open alley of a goodly garden, where no tree was near, nor any shadow at all". Watchable behaviour: the face stays evenly lit and legible from every angle, like a lit screen; depth comes from colour and line, never from a dark side. Source: leg 1 (treatise, quoted in Wikipedia citing Strong 1975); leg 2 (the blue ground's open daylight, folded in here only as the light, the ground itself is M9).

**Takeaway:** the portrait refuses shadow on principle; the Queen is shown in open light because shadow would hide her.

## M4. The Stolen Glance
The painter's job is to watch and catch fleeting expressions: "watch, and as it were, catch these lovely graces, witty smilings, and those stolen glances which suddenly like lightning pass and another countenance taketh place". The face is painted live across two or three sittings of several hours (the Gloriana account gives three: a first of two to four hours, a second of four to six, a third to finish), with "discreet talk or reading, quiet mirth and music" and "sweet odours" to keep the sitter natural. Watchable behaviour: the portrait catches a half-smile or a sidelong look mid-change, not a held pose; the likeness is a moment seized, not a mask assembled. Source: leg 1 (treatise, quoted in Wikipedia citing Strong 1975, and in learnantiques).

**Takeaway:** the likeness is a caught moment, not a posed face; the sittings are long so the glance can be stolen.

## M5. The Raised Pearl
A pearl is not painted flat: a raised blob of white is placed with the fine tooth of a ferret or stoat, then a delicate drop of silver is added for lustre. The silver has tarnished over time, so once-shimmering pearls and silver elements now appear blackened. Watchable behaviour: the pearl stands proud of the surface as a tiny dome; it catches real light and throws a real shadow, so it glints when the object is tilted. Source: leg 1 (treatise as reported by the V and A; Gloriana); leg 2 (raised paint for pearls, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the pearl is built, not painted; it is a bump on the vellum that catches real light.

## M6. The Dripped Ruff
Lace and ruffs are made by dripping or dribbling thick white pigment onto the vellum, so the paint stands higher than the rest of the picture (like icing on a cake) and casts a real shadow in directional light that imitates real lace. The lace collar in the c.1595-1600 Elizabeth ("Astraea") miniature was produced this way; the thickness of the dribbled paint means the ruff stands higher than the rest of the painting. Watchable behaviour: the ruff is a relief, not a picture of a ruff; tilt the miniature and the lace throws shadows and the collar reads as solid. Source: leg 1 (V and A; Gloriana); leg 2 (raised paint for lace, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the collar is piped on like icing; its shadow is real, not painted.

## M7. Shell Gold on Ochre
Gold jewellery is imitated by laying an ochre ground and overlaying finely ground gold powder ("shell gold", mixed in a mussel shell with gum), and by painting real gold and silver lines around the edges of the miniature. Hilliard wrote that limning "worketh the metals Gold or Silver with themselves". Watchable behaviour: the gold areas have a granular metallic sparkle distinct from yellow paint; the edge line frames the whole portrait in a thin band of real metal light. Source: leg 1 (treatise as reported; V and A).

**Takeaway:** the gold in the picture is gold, not yellow paint; the metal is worked as metal.

## M8. The Hot-Needle Gem
Jewels are mimicked by trickling a bead of coloured resin from a hot needle onto a drop of gold or silver, building a tiny domed gem. Watchable behaviour: each gem is a small lens of colour over metal, with a highlight that moves as the viewing angle changes. Source: leg 1 (treatise as reported in secondary sources).

**Takeaway:** the gem is a drop of coloured resin over gold; it is a lens, not a flat spot of colour.

## M9. The Blue Field, Then the Red Curtain
The standard ground is a flat, uniform blue that isolates the head; under James I the ground becomes a sumptuous red velvet curtain draped behind the sitter. One early exception (Leicester, c.1571-74) uses a black decorative pattern on a silver ground. Strong dates the abandonment of the solid blue ground to about 1587 (the stormy sky behind Cumberland); by the 1590s the grounds are stormy skies, gardens and flames. Watchable behaviour: the background is a single flat colour panel that switches, once, in the Jacobean work, to a draped curtain; a deliberate background system, not a setting. The sitter's head floats on a sheet of pure colour, and when the miniature is tilted in the hand the flat blue does not change, so the face stays forward while the costume's metallic paint catches light. Source: leg 1 (V and A; Gloriana); leg 2 (the blue ground, M1, folded in here, with the dated examples W1-W6 early, W15a break, W19c Jacobean replacement).

**Takeaway:** the background is a system with two settings, blue then red curtain; the ground changes once in forty years, and that change dates the work.

## M10. The Mask of Youth
For Elizabeth, a fixed, flattering face pattern is repeated across many miniatures (sixteen "Astraea" versions survive), smoothing away age: the face becomes a mask, and all the descriptive energy moves into dress, lace and jewels. The "Mask of Queenship" (c.1584-91) precedes the "Mask of Youth" (c.1592-1603). After the Queen chose Isaac Oliver to produce a new face pattern and was horrified by its blunt realism (wrinkled forehead, auburn wig, sunken eyes, long hooked nose, sketched from life c.1590/92), she returned to Hilliard, who created the soft, flattering, ageless mask. Watchable behaviour: the face stays identical and ageless from portrait to portrait while the costume, ruff and jewels change completely; identity is carried by the clothes, not the face. Place two late Elizabeth miniatures side by side and the faces are identical while the ruffs, necklaces and grounds differ; the Queen does not age between 1592 and 1603. Source: leg 1 (ODNB; V and A); leg 2 (the face pattern and Mask of Youth, M4, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the face is a stencil and the clothes are the portrait; the Queen's age is painted out and her dress painted in.

## M11. The Picture Box
The miniature is made to live inside a jewelled locket: the painting and its enamelled gold case, cameo front, pearls and inscription are one object, worn on the body, opened and closed, held in the hand. The Drake Jewel opens to show Elizabeth and a phoenix; the Heneage Jewel shows a gold bust under rock crystal on the front and the Ark of the English Church on a stormy sea on the back; the Lyte Jewel's openwork cover bears the royal monogram "IR". Watchable behaviour: the portrait is revealed by opening, not by looking; the front of the case (cameo, bust, inscription) and the portrait inside are two faces of one object, and the portrait is private until the case is opened. The setting was part of the work, and Hilliard's goldsmith training is what let him make the painting and its case as one object. Source: leg 1 (V and A Heneage and Drake Jewel records); leg 2 (the jewelled picture-box, M6, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the portrait has a lid; it is shown by opening and hidden by closing, and the jewel is the first face of the portrait.

## M12. The Impresa (Private Device)
A motto or symbol readable only by intimates is set into the miniature: roses for love or the Tudor dynasty; a hand reaching from a cloud clasped by the sitter, with the Latin motto "Attici amoris ergo"; a young man against flames holding a locket to his heart; a reclining earl in a garden, melancholy. The props are few and legible, rose, tree, cloud-hand, flame, locket, glove, and the sitter's gesture (hand on heart, hand clasping the cloud-hand) is the verb of the sentence. Watchable behaviour: the portrait contains a small coded emblem that does not explain itself; the viewer must already know the code to read it. The eye moves from face to prop to motto and back, and the meaning stays private between giver and receiver. Source: leg 1 (V and A; Gloriana); leg 2 (the allegorical impresa miniature, M5, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the miniature is a coded message, not just a likeness; the emblem is the sentence and the face is the signature.

## M13. Gold Inscription in the Ground
The date and the sitter's age are painted in shell gold directly into the blue ground, flanking the head: "Ano Dmi 1577 / AEtatis Suae 30" (self-portrait, 1577), "Attici amoris ergo / Ano. Dni. 1588" (Unknown man clasping a hand from a cloud), "Ano Dmni 1609 / Regni 43" (James I, 1609). The lettering is part of the picture, not a caption added later; it is laid in the same watercolour technique, in gold, at the same scale as the features. Watchable behaviour: the inscription frames the face symmetrically and dates the object from inside itself; in the Drake Jewel a wrongly restored inscription ("1575" for 1586) misdates the whole object, showing the inscription IS the dating evidence. The motto form turns the date-line into a riddle. Source: leg 2 (M2, kept as its own mechanism because leg 1 had none of it; dated instances W4, W13, W20a and the James I 1609 miniature).

**Takeaway:** the miniature dates itself in gold; the inscription is inside the picture, and a wrong inscription misdates the whole object.

## Evolution
Phase 1, Exeter and exile, to 1562. A goldsmith's son, born into the Exeter goldsmiths, a boy in Protestant exile for about four and a half years (Wesel, Frankfurt, Geneva), returning on Elizabeth's accession in 1558. In Frankfurt he saw a drawing by Albrecht Durer for the first time and took Durer as a lasting model; he later used Durer as an example in his treatise. The fluent French he acquired in exile proved useful later. The register is provincial and goldsmith's: plate, not painting.

Phase 2, apprenticeship and the first miniatures, 1562-1576. Seven years under Robert Brandon, the Queen's jeweller, in Cheapside; freeman of the Goldsmiths' Company on 29 July 1569; a workshop with his younger brother John, making and selling gold rings. How he learned limning is not documented; his own claim was that he taught himself by copying Durer. Fourteen miniatures are known from 1571-1576, and even the earliest are unusually accomplished. Small oval format, blue ground, fine linear technique. In 1571 he made "a booke of portraitures" for Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, likely how he became known at court. His first known miniature of Elizabeth is dated 1572, painted from life in open daylight in a garden, with almost no shadow; in 1573 he was granted the reversion of a lease by the Queen for his "good, true and loyal service". The portraiture is still face-first: head and shoulders, minimal costume detail, the sitter's identity carried by the face and the gold inscription.

Phase 3, France, 1576-1579. He left soon after his marriage "with no other intent than to increase his knowledge by this voyage, and upon hope to get a piece of money of the lords and ladies here for his better maintenance in England at his return", as the ambassador Sir Amyas Paulet reported. Employed by Francois, Duke of Anjou (in the duke's papers he appears as "Nicholas Belliart, peintre anglois", receiving a stipend of 200 livres in 1577), made "Valet de Garderobe" to the duke on 600 livres. He stayed with Germain Pilon and George of Ghent, mixed in artistic circles round the French court, and met the poet Ronsard, who is said to have told him "the islands indeed seldom bring forth any cunning man, but when they do it is in high perfection". French influence is visible in the 1577-78 work: the chalk-drawing influence of Francois Clouet on the Alice Brandon miniature, a softer, more fleeting expression than the English work, and the self-confidence of the 1577 self-portrait, painted where painters were gentlemen, not tradesmen. He worked in Paris largely as a goldsmith. He did not make his fortune: in July 1579 he borrowed 70 pounds from Brandon, using his parents' house in Exeter as collateral.

Phase 4, the Gutter Lane shop and the great period, 1579-1590s. The shop opened a revolution, in Strong's word: the clientele for miniatures broadened from the court to the gentry, and by the end of the century to well-off city merchants. A miniature in the mid-1580s cost 3 pounds, a large sum when most people earned less than that in a year. Scale grows in two directions: the cabinet miniature (Cumberland in tilting attire, c.1590; Henry Percy reclining in a garden, c.1590-95) reaches 25-28 cm tall, rectangular, laid on panel; and the allegorical full-length miniature (Young Man Among Roses, c.1587; Hatton full-length, 1588-91) puts a whole standing figure, landscape and props into an oval of about 13 cm. The ground stops being only blue: Strong dates the abandonment of the solid blue ground to about 1587, and by the 1590s the grounds are stormy skies, gardens, flames and landscapes. The face stops being observed and becomes a pattern: from the mid-1580s Hilliard reuses face patterns, the "Mask of Queenship" then the "Mask of Youth", with 16 versions of the latter surviving; no evidence Elizabeth sat for him after the mid-1580s. Allegory and impresa take over the subject matter, and jewellery becomes the real subject: the Drake Jewel and the Heneage Jewel are goldsmiths' objects first and paintings second. Wikipedia's summary is blunt: "His style shows little development after the 1570s, apart from developing some technical refinements". The evolution is in format, ground and subject, not in handling; the hand stays the same, the stage around the face changes.

Phase 5, Jacobean, 1603-1619. The blue ground gives way to the red curtain (Princess Elizabeth, c.1606-09; the James I miniatures of c.1609-15). Repetition replaces observation: the later James I repetitions "are much weaker than his early works"; James did not like sitting and Hilliard probably had few sittings with him. The market changes: James is more generous than Elizabeth with presentation jewels (the Lyte Jewel, 1610; sixteen members of the Earl of Rutland's Danish embassy party received gold chains with the King's picture), and the miniature becomes dynastic propaganda in bulk, the six-portrait family group framed together as a set. The pupil overtakes the master: Isaac Oliver, appointed limner to Anne of Denmark in 1604 (Philip Mould gives 22 June 1605, see C287) and to Henry, Prince of Wales in 1610, takes the young Prince's patronage; Hilliard keeps the old King and the 1617 monopoly on engraved royal portraits, but the fresh work is Oliver's. In his final years Hilliard began to experiment with light and shade, probably to keep abreast of younger rivals. He continued to paint images of Elizabeth after her death, whose popularity increased a few years after 1603 as disaffection with the new regime made the English look back with nostalgia to "Good Queen Bess".

What changed, in one line each. Format: small circular ovals on blue (1570s) to cabinet miniatures and allegorical full-lengths (1580s-90s) to dynastic sets and repetitions (Jacobean). Ground: flat blue (1570s) to stormy skies, gardens and flames (from c.1587) to red curtain (Jacobean). Face: observed from life in open light (1571-1572) to the fixed Mask of Youth (1590s) to weak repetitions of Types I-III of James (1610s). Subject: likeness (1570s) to coded impresa and jewelled object (1580s-90s) to dynastic propaganda in bulk (Jacobean). Market: court (1570s) to gentry and city merchants through the Gutter Lane shop (1580s on) to presentation jewels in bulk (Jacobean). Hand: the handling barely changes across forty years; what changes is the stage around the face.

## Aesthetic logic
Hilliard's aesthetic is the logic of the small precious thing that must be held in the hand to be seen. Everything he makes is a container for a likeness, and the container is always more valuable than the painting: the miniature lives inside a jewelled locket, the locket is worn on the body, the portrait is private until the case is opened, and the setting can be more valuable than the painting. The goldsmith training is not a biographical footnote; it is the technical core. His miniatures are jewellery: metal worked as metal, gems imitated with resin and gold, pearls built as domes, settings made by the same hand. The limner and the goldsmith are one practice, and the work's meaning changes with each opening because the wearer is the meaning. He works in flat open light because the Queen chose it: no shadow, no modelling, the face evenly lit like a lit screen, and the depth comes from colour and line and from the real relief of the paint itself, the raised pearl, the dripped ruff, the shell gold, all catching real light when the object is tilted. The face is the one thing he will not observe twice: for Elizabeth he builds an official face, the Mask of Youth, a political instrument, ageless, unchanging, reproducible, and policed, and all the descriptive energy moves into the dress, the lace and the jewels, so that the clothes become the portrait and the face becomes the stencil. What he avoids is the unique masterpiece; his genius is the system, one face pattern, many costumes, many grounds, and the miniature's meaning changes with each sitter because the sitter is the meaning. He is, in Wikipedia's words, "the central artistic figure of the Elizabethan age", and the centrality is the point: he defined the Queen's image for thirty-two years, and the image outlived her, painted again after her death for a country looking back with nostalgia to "Good Queen Bess". The shadow of the artistic life is the financial life: forty-five years of success and constant debt, imprisonment for surety, pawning a royal miniature for 2 pounds, dying with legacies he could not pay. The court paid in honour and in arrears, and the miniaturist who painted the richest court in Europe died poor, in an unmarked grave.

## Positions
P1. Hilliard is the first native English artist to build a formidable international reputation for his skill, in Italy, France and the Habsburg Empire, and the first great English portraitist. Source: Gloriana; Wikipedia: "the central artistic figure of the Elizabethan age". (Leg 1.)
P2. His art is technically conservative by European standards, flat, linear, almost no chiaroscuro, provincial beside early-Baroque Italy or El Greco, yet superbly executed, fresh and charming. The conservatism is a choice, tied to Elizabeth's taste, not ignorance. Source: leg 1 judgement from the works and the treatise. (Leg 1.)
P3. The goldsmith training is not a biographical footnote; it is the technical core. His miniatures are jewellery: metal worked as metal, gems imitated with resin and gold, pearls built as domes, settings made by the same hand. The limner and the goldsmith are one practice. Source: leg 1, from the technique and the jewel settings. (Leg 1.)
P4. He made limning a gentleman's art and said so in print: "none should meddle with limning but gentlemen alone". This is a status claim, painting as a liberal, clean, discreet art, practised without mess, that a gentleman could leave and return to at will, and it helped raise the artist above the craftsman in England. Source: leg 1, treatise as quoted second-hand. (Leg 1.)
P5. His great subject is not the face but the controlled image. For Elizabeth he built an official face, the Mask of Youth, that was a political instrument: ageless, unchanging, reproducible, and policed (the 1596 Privy Council decree). The face is a state device. Source: leg 1, from the Mask of Youth and the 1596 decree. (Leg 1.)
P6. His best work is early. The 1570s-1580s miniatures have a freshness and psychological penetration his later repetitions of James I and his family lack; his style shows little development after the 1570s except technical refinement. Source: leg 1, from the ODNB and Wikipedia. (Leg 1.)
P7. The allegorical young men of the 1580s (Young Man Among Roses, the cloud-hand, the man against flames) are his most inventive work: private, coded, melancholic, built around the impresa. They show a miniaturist thinking like a poet, not a court recorder. Source: leg 1, from the works catalogue. (Leg 1.)
P8. The three-sitting method is the real engine of his likeness: face painted live, dress and jewels finished in the studio. The portrait is a record of sustained looking, not of a single sitting. Source: leg 1, Gloriana account of the sittings. (Leg 1.)
P9. His influence runs through teaching, not through writing: the treatise was unpublished in his lifetime, but his pupils (Isaac Oliver, Rowland Lockey, Laurence Hilliard) and the workshop carried the practice, and Norgate's later treatise cites "old Mr. Hillyard" as the master. Source: leg 1, from the treatise history and Norgate. (Leg 1.)
P10. Isaac Oliver is both his greatest pupil and the proof of his limits: Oliver travelled, learned perspective, and developed a more modern style; Elizabeth's rejection of Oliver's unflattering face pattern and return to Hilliard shows that what the court wanted from Hilliard was flattery, not truth. Source: leg 1, from the Oliver comparison. (Leg 1.)
P11. His financial life is the shadow of his artistic life: forty-five years of success and constant debt, imprisonment for surety, pawning a royal miniature for 2 pounds, dying with legacies he could not pay. The court paid in honour and in arrears. Source: leg 1, from the biography. (Leg 1.)
P12. The Phoenix and Pelican panel portraits show him working "in great" by 1573, but the evidence points to a studio production to his design rather than his own hand in oil; he is a miniaturist who designed large pictures, not an oil painter. Source: leg 1, from the NPG and dendrochronology evidence. (Leg 1.)

## Network

### Teachers and masters
1. Robert Brandon (d. 30 May 1591) - NEITHER. Hilliard's master: Queen's jeweller and goldsmith, city chamberlain of London 1583-1591, freeman of the Goldsmiths' Company by redemption 1548, Prime Warden 1582-83, one of the Queen's two royal goldsmiths from about 1558 to 1580. Hilliard was admitted into Brandon's household on 13 November 1562, served a seven-year apprenticeship, and was made a freeman of the Goldsmiths' Company on 29 July 1569. Brandon often took his apprentice to court work and presumably presented him to the Queen. He is also Hilliard's father-in-law (entry 6). A maker with a documented career, but no surviving body of signed work was identified in this run, so he is NOT proposed as a candidate; he is a network entry only. Evidence: EN Wikipedia (Nicholas Hilliard; Robert Brandon); ODNB text via Philip Mould; V and A introduction.
2. Levina Teerlinc (1510s - 23 June 1576) - ALREADY CARRIED (alternate in #522). Flemish miniaturist, eldest daughter of Simon Bening. Court painter to Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I, at 40 pounds a year from 1546; the most important miniaturist at the English court between Holbein and Hilliard. Sir Roy Strong and V. J. Murrell suggested she taught Hilliard limning. CAVEAT, flagged hard: there is no evidence she ever met Hilliard, and no surviving work is firmly attributed to her; the V and A's own object text (2015 reassessment by Coombs and Derbyshire) says the teaching link is a nomination, not a documented fact. So the teacher link is a scholarly suggestion, not a fact. Evidence: V and A, Artists of the Tudor Court, 1983; leg 3.

### Pupils
3. Isaac Oliver (c.1565 - buried 2 October 1617) - ALREADY CARRIED (alternate in #522), PROMOTED to top pick for #524. Hilliard's pupil, "by far the most important" of them (Wikipedia) and his "well profiting scholar" (Richard Haydocke, quoted in the DNB). Born Rouen, moved to London 1568 with his Huguenot parents; entered Hilliard's studio as an assistant (not formally apprenticed as a goldsmith, unlike Lockey); earliest known miniature dated 1587; succeeded Hilliard as miniaturist to James I, and was appointed limner to Anne of Denmark in 1604 (Philip Mould gives 22 June 1605, see C287) and to Henry, Prince of Wales, in 1610. He travelled to Italy, developed a more modern, chiaroscuro style, and was Hilliard's rival from the 1590s on. Father of Peter Oliver. Evidence: DNB (Wikisource), Philip Mould, NPG person page (artist associated with 73 portraits), V and A object text, Wikipedia; parent search (225 lines) confirming the pupilage, the appointments and the succession.
4. Laurence Hilliard (baptised 5 March 1582 - 1648) - NEITHER. Son and pupil of Nicholas Hilliard. Took over his father's Gutter Lane workshop in 1613 and carried on the business for many decades; worked out the unexpired time of his father's royal licence after 1619 and became royal limner to James I; presented the Bosworth Jewel miniatures to Charles I; paid 42 pounds from the treasury for five pictures in 1624. His miniatures are rare and were judged a "feeble" version of his father's style (Wikipedia). RECORD WARNING: death date given as 1648 in the Alchetron/Wikipedia line and as "after 1640" in the WebMuseum entry. Verified spare if a proposed candidate fails; see Future candidates.
5. Rowland Lockey (c.1565-1616) - NEITHER. Apprenticed to Hilliard for eight years from Michaelmas 1581, made a freeman or master of the Goldsmiths' Company by 1600. English painter and goldsmith; worked mainly as a copyist of earlier portraits for the long galleries of great houses, including two near life-size copies (c.1592-1594) of Hans Holbein the Younger's lost Family of Sir Thomas More, at Nostell Priory and the NPG; signed portrait miniatures on vellum and the signed title-page engraving for the 1602 Bishops' Bible also survive. Patronised by Bess of Hardwick 1591-1597 and her son Sir William Cavendish 1608-1613. Strong's verdict: of "no significance" beside his fellow-apprentice Isaac Oliver, but his importance lies in carrying Hilliard's aesthetic into the next generation. Verified spare if a proposed candidate fails; see Future candidates.

### Family
6. Alice Brandon (1556-1611) - NEITHER. Wife of Nicholas Hilliard, third surviving child of Robert Brandon (entry 1). Married 15 July 1576 at St Vedast, Foster Lane, London; seven children (Daniel b.1578, Elizabeth b.1579, Francis b.1580, Laurence b.1582, Lettice b.1583, Penelope b.1586, Robert b.1588; a stillborn son Nicholas buried Christmas 1584). Hilliard's miniature of her, painted in France in 1578 while she was pregnant with their first child, pins a head of wheat (fertility) and a carnation (marriage) to her dress; the inscription around the edge recording her as Hilliard's wife was probably added by her son Laurence (entry 4). ### Sitters and patrons
7. Elizabeth I (1533-1603) - NEITHER. Hilliard's defining patron. First known miniature of the Queen dated 1572 (NPG 108); the Pelican and Phoenix panel portraits c.1572-76; the "Mask of Youth" image of the 1590s; annuity of 40 pounds a year from 1599; a gift of 400 pounds in 1591 after he made a second Great Seal; she refused his 1584 monopoly bid (see entry 18) but granted the reversion of a lease in 1573 for "good, true and loyal service". She kept her collection of miniatures locked in a cabinet in her bedroom, wrapped in paper and labelled, the one labelled "My Lord's picture" holding Leicester (entry 17). Typical price for a non-royal miniature was 3 pounds.
8. James I (1566-1625) - NEITHER. Hilliard's second royal patron. Miniatures of James 1603-09 (V and A); a special patent of appointment dated 5 May 1617 granting a sole licence for royal portraits in engraved form for twelve years (the monopoly Elizabeth had refused in 1584); more lavish than Elizabeth in presenting portraits, giving sixteen members of an embassy party gold chains with the King's picture. James disliked sitting, so Hilliard probably had few sittings with him and the later Jacobean repetitions are weaker than the early work. The Lyte Jewel was given by James to the courtier Thomas Lyte in 1610.
9. Anne of Denmark (1574-1619) - NEITHER. Queen consort of James I. Hilliard's London studio produced the group of six Jacobean royal-family miniatures (V and A P.147-152-1910), probably framed together 1625-1650; a Hilliard miniature of Anne is recorded at Christie's and in the V and A and Fitzwilliam collections. CAVEAT: whether Hilliard or Oliver had the sitting that produced the standard Anne type is NOT known (V and A object text for P.148-1910); Oliver was appointed her limner in 1604 while Hilliard remained official court painter to James. The link to Hilliard himself runs through his studio, not a documented sitting.
10. Henry, Prince of Wales (1594-1612) - NEITHER. James I's elder son and heir, a noted patron of artists, died aged 18. He appears in the Hilliard studio family group (entry 9); Oliver was appointed his limner when he established his own household in 1610; Robert Peake was appointed his picture maker in 1604. CAVEAT: no direct sitting with Hilliard himself is evidenced in this run; the link is through the studio group and through Oliver, his appointed limner.
11. Sir Francis Drake (c.1540-1596) - NEITHER. Naval hero and explorer. Hilliard miniature of Drake dated 1581 (Royal Museums Greenwich MNT0023). The Drake Jewel: a gold and enamelled oval pendant locket with a sardonyx cameo on the front and a Hilliard miniature of Elizabeth inside (inscription originally 1586, mis-restored as 1575); family tradition says Elizabeth gave it to Drake, possibly to mark the Armada victory of 1588, but no contemporary document proves the presentation; Drake is shown wearing it in Marcus Gheeraerts the Younger's 1591 portrait (entry 19).
12. Sir Thomas Heneage (d. 1595) - NEITHER. Privy Counsellor and Vice-Chamberlain of the Royal Household. Family tradition says Elizabeth presented him with the Heneage Jewel (also called the Armada Jewel). CAVEAT, carried from #522 (see C279 there and C289 here): the V and A says there is no evidence the jewel was a presentation from Elizabeth, and Strong dates the pendant c.1600, after Heneage's death in 1595, so the first owner may have been his brother Michael (entry 13). The jewel itself is an enamelled gold pendant with a gold profile bust of Elizabeth on the obverse, Hilliard's miniature inside, and the Ark of the English Church on a stormy sea on the reverse; V and A O33883.
13. Michael Heneage (d. 1600) - NEITHER. Brother of Sir Thomas Heneage (entry 12). Per Strong's dating of the Heneage Jewel to c.1600, Michael is the likely first owner if the jewel originated in the Heneage family; his descendant Colonel Godfrey Walker-Heneage sold it at Christie's in 1902.
14. Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex (1565/66-1601) - NEITHER. Queen's favourite, executed for treason 1601. Hilliard's Young Man Among Roses, c.1585-1595 (V and A P.163-1910), is believed to be Essex; Strong calls the identity "almost certainly" Essex. A miniature of Essex dated 1588 is also listed in the gallery.
15. Sir Philip Sidney (1554-1586) - NEITHER. Poet, soldier and courtier. The WebMuseum Hilliard entry lists Sidney among the great Elizabethans who sat for Hilliard, alongside Drake and Raleigh. CAVEAT: no specific dated Sidney miniature by Hilliard surfaced in this run, so the evidence here is thinner than for the other sitters; the link is a listed sitter, not a dated work.
16. Mary, Queen of Scots (1542-1587) - NEITHER. Hilliard's miniature of Mary, 1578-79, watercolour on vellum (V and A P.24-1975; a second version in the Royal Collection with an ultramarine ground, the V and A version in azurite or bice). She sat while a prisoner of Elizabeth; the V and A notes it is possible Mary herself commissioned it rather than Elizabeth, with her jailer the Earl of Shrewsbury sanctioning the sittings. The NPG holds a panel portrait after Hilliard (NPG 429), the "Sheffield portrait" type, inscribed 1578. Hilliard is said to have executed a portrait of her when he was eighteen, though the 1578-79 miniature is the documented work.
17. Robert Dudley, 1st Earl of Leicester (1532/33-1588) - NEITHER. The Queen's favourite and Hilliard's first great court patron. In 1571 Hilliard made him "a booke of portraitures", likely how he became known at court; Hilliard miniature of Leicester c.1571-74 (V and A E.1174-1988), black doublet with gold, black cap studded with gold jewels, black decorative pattern on a silver ground instead of the usual blue. Elizabeth kept his miniature labelled "My Lord's picture" in her bedroom cabinet (entry 7).

### Fellow makers
18. George Gower (c.1540-1596) - ALREADY CARRIED (alternate in #522). English portrait painter, Serjeant Painter to Elizabeth I from 1581 (for life, 5 July 1581). The Plimpton "Sieve" Portrait of Elizabeth I, 1579 (Folger Shakespeare Library); self-portrait 1579, the earliest known independent self-portrait by an English artist. With Hilliard he drafted a patent in 1584 that would have given the two of them a monopoly on all portraits of the Queen; there is no evidence it was ever granted. Gower painted in oils, Hilliard in miniature; the division of labour was the point of the partnership.
19. Marcus Gheeraerts the Younger (c.1561/62 - 19 January 1636) - NEITHER. Flemish artist working at the Tudor court; son of Marcus Gheeraerts the Elder (who is carried as a reserve in #522, the Elder, not the Younger). Brought to England as a child by his father; fashionable portraitist in the last decade of Elizabeth's reign; introduced a new three-dimensional, tonal portraiture against the flat modelling and pure brilliant colour of Hilliard; first to paint on canvas rather than panel in England; the Ditchley Portrait of Elizabeth, 1592 (NPG 2561); favourite portraitist of Anne of Denmark. Direct Hilliard link: his 1591 portrait of Drake (Royal Museums Greenwich BHC2662) shows Drake wearing the Drake Jewel, Hilliard's work (entry 11). Second link: Isaac Oliver married the half-sister of Gheeraerts the Younger. NOT carried in #522 (only his father is); do not confuse the two.
20. Hans Eworth (c.1520-1574) - ALREADY CARRIED (alternate in #522). Flemish painter active in Tudor London; the "Monogrammist HE", identified by Lionel Cust in 1913; about 40 paintings attributed, including Mary I 1554 and Elizabeth I and the Three Goddesses 1569; designer for the Office of the Revels into 1573; de facto court painter to Mary I though he never held official status. Strong called him the most distinguished foreign painter to work in England in the Tudor period after Holbein. He is the generation before Hilliard: in England from c.1549 (per the Artists of the Tudor Court line; #522 gives expelled from Antwerp for heresy 1544 and freeman of the Antwerp guild 1540, FLAG the spread, the two accounts differ on his arrival).
21. Steven van Herwijck (Utrecht c.1530 - London, died before Easter 1567) - ALREADY STUDIED (#522). Netherlandish sculptor and gem engraver, subject of study #522. The Hilliard link is the Heneage one: in 1562 Herwijck made the first portrait medals of private individuals in England, including Anne Heneage, wife of Thomas Heneage (entry 12); Hilliard's miniature went into the Heneage Jewel a generation later. Herwijck's 1565 lead medal of Elizabeth, the only surviving example, is in the NPG (4294). Hilliard took the small royal portrait Herwijck cast in lead and silver and turned it into painted miniatures in gold jewel settings.
22. Germain Pilon (c.1525/28-1590) - ALREADY CARRIED (#521, carried forward in #522). French Renaissance sculptor, the leading French sculptor of his generation after Jean Goujon; tomb of Francis I and tomb of Henry II and Catherine de' Medici at Saint-Denis. Direct Hilliard link: in Paris 1576-78 Hilliard stayed with "Germain Pilon and George of Ghent, respectively the Queen's sculptor and painter" (Wikipedia, Nicholas Hilliard).
23. Hans Holbein the Younger (c.1497/98-1543) - ALREADY CARRIED (main in #522). German-Swiss painter, King's Painter to Henry VIII. Hilliard names him in The Art of Limning as one of his two masters (with Albrecht Durer), "a follower of Holbein's manner of limning"; Lucas Horenbout taught Holbein miniature painting on vellum, per Karel van Mander; Teerlinc became court painter after Holbein's death; Eworth copied Holbein's Henry VIII pattern in the 1560s. The link to Hilliard is through the treatise and the tradition, not a personal meeting (Holbein died before Hilliard was born).

### Scholars and institutions
24. Sir Roy Strong - NEITHER. Art historian, the great scholar of Tudor and Elizabethan portraiture. Author of Nicholas Hilliard (1975), Artists of the Tudor Court: The Portrait Miniature Rediscovered 1520-1620 (V and A exhibition and catalogue, 1983), and Gloriana: The Portraits of Queen Elizabeth I (1987). The parent merged five overlaps: leg 2's blue ground folds into leg 1's blue field (M9); leg 2's raised paint folds into leg 1's raised pearl (M5) and dripped ruff (M6); leg 2's face pattern folds into leg 1's mask of youth (M10); leg 2's allegorical impresa folds into leg 1's impresa (M12); leg 2's jewelled picture-box folds into leg 1's picture box (M11). Leg 2's gold inscription is kept as its own mechanism, M13, because leg 1 had none of it. The study keeps 13 mechanisms.
- C286. The doorway tally. The #522 run recorded the streak at ninety-six holds and nineteen non-holds after its check. This run's check is the one-hundred-and-forty-fourth in a row and is recorded as a HOLD on the substance (ninety-seventh hold) and a FAIL on the letter (twentieth non-hold). The counting note in the Doorway section explains why 97 + 20 does not sum to 144.
- C287. The top pick for #524, verified first-hand by the parent. A parent search (225 lines) confirms Isaac Oliver (c.1565 - buried 2 October 1617) as an English portrait miniature painter, born Rouen, who studied miniature painting under Nicholas Hilliard, developed a naturalistic style influenced by Italian and Flemish art with real chiaroscuro, was appointed miniaturist to Anne of Denmark and to Henry, Prince of Wales, and succeeded Hilliard as the Jacobean court's leading limner; the NPG lists him as artist associated with 73 portraits. The parent also verified him not studied: zero log-header hits, the single whole-log mention being #522's own carried-candidate listing, no local file. Appointment-date spread: leg 3 and Stephen Ongpin give 1604 for the Anne of Denmark appointment, Philip Mould gives 22 June 1605. The study uses 1604 and flags 1605. Birth-year spread carried from #522 C274: c.1565 (Wikipedia) against 1556? (DNB) and c.1558 (V and A).
- C288. Nicholas Hilliard's birth year, parent correction. Wikipedia gives c.1547; the 1911 Britannica gives c.1537. The 1577 self-portrait is inscribed "Aetatis Suae 30" (aged 30), which fits 1547 and NOT 1537; that inscription is the strongest single piece of evidence for 1547, as leg 2 notes. The 1537 date rests on age inscriptions on other miniatures, which Farquhar already flagged in 1908: if Hilliard "was not born until 1547, several important miniatures must be incorrectly dated, or be wrongly attributed to him". The study uses c.1547 as commonly cited and flags the 1537 variant (see C280 in #522).
- C289. The Heneage Jewel presentation and date, confirmed first-hand by the parent. The V and A object record for the Heneage Jewel states: "There is no evidence that the jewel was a presentation from Queen Elizabeth, although it is possible that she gave the miniature by Hilliard to a recipient who had the magnificent case made in tribute to the queen", and Sir Roy Strong dates the pendant c.1600, after Sir Thomas Heneage's death in 1595, so the first owner may have been Michael Heneage, who died in 1600. The doorway's "going to Thomas Heneage" holds as family tradition only. Date spread: the V and A gives "about 1600" and Strong c.1600; leg 1 gives c.1595 in its jewel section, following the older learnantiques-style dating. The study uses c.1600 and flags c.1595. See C279 in #522.
- C290. The "Dangers Averted" medal date. The Fitzwilliam Museum gives the gold medal as "Attributed to Nicholas Hilliard (1537-1619), c.1588"; Christie's lists a 1589 example of the same medal attributed to Hilliard. The doorway's "c.1588/1589" covers both. Note the Fitzwilliam attribution line itself uses the superseded 1537 birth year (see C288); the attribution, not the date, is what the doorway relies on.
- C291. The Drake Jewel inscription, mis-restored. The V and A object text: the miniature's inscription "misleadingly now reads 'Ano Dm 1575 Regni 20', implying that the miniature was painted in 1575, the 20th year of Elizabeth's reign. Close examination has shown that the inscription was incorrectly restored in the past and formerly gave a date of 1586." The 20th regnal year would be 1577-78, so the restored inscription is internally inconsistent; the 28th/29th regnal year fits 1586. Do not cite the jewel as a 1575 miniature.
- C292. The Cumberland cabinet miniature dimensions conflict. Wikipedia gives 25.2 x 17.5 cm; the Nelson-Atkins Starr catalogue gives 28.5 x 17.6 cm for the same object (National Maritime Museum, Greenwich, MNT0193). Neither figure was verified from the Royal Museums Greenwich page in this leg. The study does not state either figure as verified.
- C293. The Pelican and Phoenix panel portraits. Both are attributed, not documented, as Hilliard's own hand; the NPG says they were "likely painted either by or under the direction of Nicholas Hilliard" and notes that "no certainly identified portraits in large by Hilliard have survived". Dendrochronology (2010) proved both were painted in the same year on Eastern Baltic oak from the same two trees, and both share one face pattern in mirror image, traced from a single cartoon; but the Phoenix appears to have been painted by a different artist, "another follower or assistant executed the Phoenix Portrait to his master's design", and its quality, especially hair and lace, is judged lower than the Pelican's. The study keeps both in the separate disputed list, not in the main catalogue.
- C294. The Teerlinc teaching link is unproven. Strong and Murrell suggested Levina Teerlinc taught Hilliard limning, but there is no evidence she ever met Hilliard, and no surviving work is firmly attributed to her; the V and A's own object text (2015 reassessment by Coombs and Derbyshire) says the teaching link is a nomination, not a documented fact, and one source in this run states flatly there is no evidence for the claim and much to disprove it. The study states the suggestion with its provenance, never as fact.
- C295. The treatise was not read directly. No verbatim Hilliard words in this study come from a direct reading of The Art of Limning. The Kinney 1983 and Thornton/Cain editions are printed books with no free full text found; the Walpole Society 1911-12 edition was not found free online. Leg 1 read Norgate's Miniatura (1919 Hardie edition) on archive.org instead, which cites Hilliard repeatedly; all Hilliard quotes (Q1-Q7) are flagged second-hand, and the Norgate and Norman quotes (Q10-Q13) are the only ones read directly.
- C296. Aggregator error on the Young Man Among Roses. Art-aggregator sites (ArtsDot, WahooArt) describe "A Youth Leaning Against a Tree Among Roses" as oil on panel, 1588, 135 x 73 cm, V and A. The V and A's Young Man Among Roses is watercolour on vellum, about 137 x 70 mm; the aggregator figures are the same digits with cm substituted for mm and "oil on panel" invented. Do not cite the aggregator version.
- C297. The Lyte Jewel goldwork. Recent scholarship (2016) points to the King's jeweller George Heriot, not Hilliard, as the likely designer of the gold locket itself; Hilliard's authorship of the miniature inside is not in doubt. The study attributes the miniature to Hilliard and the locket's goldwork to Heriot as the likely designer.
- C298. The death date. Wikipedia: "died on about 3 January 1619 and was buried on 7 January"; the 1911 Britannica says "died on the 7th of January 1619". The burial date, 7 January 1619 at St Martin-in-the-Fields, is the firm fact; the death is "about 3 January". The study uses died about 3 January 1619, buried 7 January 1619.
- C299. Leg 2's works count, parent correction. Leg 2's totals block says "20 entries covering 27 individual works" and lists the multi-work entries as "(W14 x2, W15 x2, W17 disputed, W18 x2, W19 x4, W20 x3)". Counted plainly that is 20 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 3 + 2 = 28 individual works, not 27. The 27 holds only if the disputed W17 (Young Man Against Flames, attribution disputed between Hilliard and Oliver) is excluded. The study uses 28 individual works, of which 27 are undisputed and 1 (W17) is disputed, and states this plainly.
