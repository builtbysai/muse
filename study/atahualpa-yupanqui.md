## M1. The borrowed name

The name is the programme, and the programme is the mechanism. Hector Roberto Chavero is born in Campo de la Cruz, and the born name is Hector, and Hector is the Trojan hero of the Iliad, and the Trojan is the first borrowing, and the borrowing is the mechanism, because the boy who will rename himself after Inca kings is already named after a hero of a foreign epic, and the foreign epic is the first name, and the first name is the programme. The pseudonym of Atahualpa originates in 1913, in a school work in homage to the last Inca sovereign, and years later he adds Yupanqui to the name, and the adding is the programme, and the programme is the mechanism, because the direct meaning of the alias is "he who comes from far lands to tell", from the Quechua Ata "to come", Hu "from far", Alpa "land", and Yupanqui "to tell", and the telling is the name, and the name is the work, and the work is the telling, and the telling is the mechanism. The fullest timeline dates the signature "Yupanqui" to a school monograph on the 12 Incas written at thirteen in 1921, and the critical essay traces the name to his voracious reading of the "Historia general del Peru" of the Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, called the first mestizo of America, and the two dates are carried as compatible variants, not settled, and the carrying is the honesty, and the honesty is the mechanism. The name does its work at once: in 1935 the Buenos Aires daily "Critica" announces him as "the rhapsodist of Indian America", describing him as a descendant of the Incas newly arrived in the port city, and nobody contradicts it, and he himself encourages the misunderstanding, and the encouraging is the programme, and the programme is the mechanism, because the following year the magazine "Sintonia" presents him as "the Peruvian, Indo-American folklorist of Radio El Mundo, Atahualpa Yupanqui", and the Peruvian is the invention, and the invention is the name, and the name is the work. He is a bonaerense, a boy from the pampas, and he becomes the Inca, and the becoming is the mechanism, because the name claims a lineage the history books had left out, and the claiming is the song, and the song is the name, and the name is the telling, and the telling is the far land, and the far land is the pampas, and the pampas are the far land, and the far land is the mechanism. He will live inside the name for the rest of his life, and the living is the programme, and the programme is the name, and the name is the work. **Takeaway:** take the name of the kings the history left out, and let the name be the programme, and let the programme be the telling. Against the chain: Violeta (#369) also renamed herself by gathering, but hers is a woman who drops the boleros and becomes a folklorist by collecting three thousand songs, and Yupanqui's is a boy who takes two kings' names in a schoolroom and becomes the teller from far lands, which is why hers is a conversion and his is an adoption.

## M2. The walk as fieldwork

The walk is the fieldwork, and the fieldwork is the mechanism. Yupanqui travels on foot and on horseback and on muleback through the northwest of Argentina and the Altiplano and the south of Bolivia, and the travelling is the work, and the work is the mechanism, because he learns the music by going to it, and the going is the collecting, and the collecting is the song, and the song is the walk, and the walk is the mechanism. In 1917 the family spends a holiday in Tucuman, and there he discovers a new landscape and a new music, with its own instruments, the bombo and the Indian harp, and its own rhythms, the zamba among them, and the discovering is the walk, and the walk is the fieldwork, and the fieldwork is the mechanism. At nineteen he composes "Camino del indio", inspired by the death of don Anselmo, the Indian of Tafi Viejo who told him old stories in 1917, and the Indian is the teacher, and the teacher is the walk, and the walk is the song, and the song is the Indian, and the Indian is the mechanism. He knows Jujuy and the Calchaqui valleys and the south of Bolivia, and he comes down from Bolivia with his saddlebags loaded with Peruvian yaravi and huayno and baguala and Andean song, and the saddlebags are the fieldwork, and the fieldwork is the walk, and the walk is the mechanism. He walks the Calchaqui valleys for forty days on muleback in 1938 with Luis Huidrobo and Felipe Chocobar, and he returns to the valleys and walks the Jujuy paths on muleback and lives for a time in Cochangasta, and the muleback is the fieldwork, and the fieldwork is the walk, and the walk is the song. He says it himself, in "El canto del viento": "Where I hear a friendly whistle, there I dismount, I give thanks, and I take out the guitar. If they say nothing to me I keep walking, which is perhaps the sign of my destiny: to walk, to walk always", and the whistle is the invitation, and the invitation is the walk, and the walk is the guitar, and the guitar is the mechanism. The notebook is the fieldwork made portable, and the portable is the mechanism, because "El arriero" is born when the muleteer Antonio Fernandez, asked how his life is going in the hills of Anta in Salta, answers "Here you see me. Paying others' faults, driving others' cows", and Yupanqui takes note in his travel notebook and varies the words slightly and composes a "cancion criolla", and the notebook is the walk, and the walk is the song, and the song is the notebook, and the notebook is the mechanism. **Takeaway:** walk to the music on muleback with a notebook, and let the answer to a question become the song. Against the chain: Violeta (#369 M1) also collected, but hers is a gramophone carried into a kitchen to gather three thousand songs from the people, and Yupanqui's is a notebook carried on muleback to take down one muleteer's answer, which is why hers is a harvest and his is a walk.

## M3. The collected form, returned

The old form is the map, and the map is the mechanism. Yupanqui gathers from the same source the most varied musical forms, so different according to the geographical setting, that make up the folklore, and the gathering is the work, and the work is the mechanism, because the milonga is the slow and grave song of the Pampas, and the chacarera and the zamba are dances of lively and joyful rhythm, and the vidala is intimate, and the baguala is the cry or protest of the kolla of the Altiplano, of the Creole muleteer, of the mestizo peon, and the forms are the map, and the map is the geography, and the geography is the form, and the form is the mechanism. He restores all these forms in his compositions with infinite respect, all these forms born of the purest tradition, and the restoring is the work, and the work is the mechanism, because the form is not his invention, and the not-inventing is the respect, and the respect is the song, and the song is the form, and the form is the map, and the map is the mechanism. "Duerme, duerme negrito" is the collected form at its purest: a lullaby he found in the Caribbean border zone between Colombia and Venezuela, sung by a Black woman, and he learned it and loved it and walked it around the world, and his own account, quoted in the academia study, says "These chords belong to an old traditional song that many years ago I found in the Caribbean zone, on the border of Venezuela and Colombia. A woman of colour sang it. I learned it, I loved it, and I walked it around the world. It is mine in what my heart has of sensibility, of receptivity. It is an anonymous, plural, folkloric theme. It belongs to them", and the belonging is the mechanism, because the song belongs to them and he carries it, and the carrying is the collecting, and the collecting is the song, and the song is the form, and the form is the map, and the map is the mechanism. He first records it in 1951, on the BAM 114 disc with "Malambo", and the recording is the returning, and the returning is the mechanism, because the collected song returns to the world as his voice and his guitar, and the returning is the song, and the song is the form, and the form is the map. His method resembles that of Isabel Aretz and, a few years later, Leda Valladares, but with one important difference: between the ethnographer who notes in his notebook the traits of the native and Creole culture and the popular interpreter who records and cuts his creations, Yupanqui ends by leaning toward the second, because he is not a musicologist but a popular interpreter able enough to pour into his virtuoso guitar and his voice without flourishes what he learned by listening and memorising, and the leaning is the mechanism, because the song is a travel diary, and the diary is the form, and the form is the map, and the map is the song. **Takeaway:** take the form where it lives, carry it in the guitar, and give it back to the world as your voice, and let the form stay theirs. Against the chain: Jara (#370 M3) also took the old form as a vessel and refilled it with his own words, but his is a tonada and a cueca taken from the shelf and given new words, and Yupanqui's is a lullaby found on a Caribbean border and carried around the world still belonging to them, which is why his is a refilling and Yupanqui's is a returning.

## M4. The left hand the torturers missed

The left hand is the guitar, and the guitar is the mechanism. Yupanqui plays the guitar with classical technique, and his only heterodoxy is his left-handedness, and the left-handedness is the mechanism, because he is a left-hander forced as a child to write with his less able hand, and the forcing is the school, and the school is the hand, and the hand is the guitar, and the guitar is the mechanism. He studies violin at seven with the priest Ros aenz, who catches him playing a vidala on the violin and ends the lessons, and at eight he studies classical guitar with Bautista Almiron in Junin, his only teacher, riding sixteen kilometres on horseback for the lessons, and with Almiron he discovers Sor and Albeniz and Granados and Tarrega and the guitar transcriptions of Schubert and Liszt and Beethoven and Bach and Schumann, and the discovering is the technique, and the technique is the guitar, and the guitar is the mechanism. The first presentations are more instrumental than vocal, and the instrumental is the proof, and the proof is the guitar, and the guitar is the work, and the work is the mechanism, because the guitar needs no voice, and the no-voice is the technique, and the technique is the left hand, and the left hand is the guitar. The torture misses the hand that matters: under Peron, officers of the Seccion Especial put a typewriter on his right hand and jump on it, trying to break the hand, and he says, "They tried to undo my hand by putting a typewriter on it and jumping on top. But they did not notice a detail: they damaged my right hand, and I, to play the guitar, am left-handed. Even today, several years after that event, there are tones like B minor that cost me to make. I can play them because I use the craft, the knack, but they really cost me", and the missing is the mechanism, because the torturers break the wrong hand, and the wrong hand is the right, and the right is the writing hand, and the writing hand is not the playing hand, and the playing hand is the left, and the left is the guitar, and the guitar is the mechanism. The guitar itself is tuned his way: Carlos Martinez, who recorded his complete guitar work on six CDs for the centenary, reports the open tunings, D minor open for "Danza de la Luna", first string in D, second in A, third in F, fourth in D, fifth in A, sixth in D, so he can play pentatonically without technical difficulty, and the third in A and the sixth in D for major keys, and the fifth in G and the sixth in D for G major and G minor, and the habit of recording with the guitar tuned low, which brings out the harmonics and gives a sweetness and beauty to the sound, and the tuning is the mechanism, because the low tuning is the voice, and the voice is the guitar, and the guitar is the left hand, and the left hand is the tuning, and the tuning is the mechanism. In his arrastres, in all his glissandi, in his way of playing the guitar so romantic and so Creole at once, lies the legacy, and the glissando is the mechanism, and the mechanism is the hand, and the hand is the guitar, and the guitar is the left. **Takeaway:** play with classical technique and a left hand, tune the guitar low and open, and let the torturers break the wrong hand. Against the chain: Jara (#370 M2) also made one guitar the whole band, but his is a stage guitar that holds a room silent before the chorus enters, and Yupanqui's is a classical guitar that carries the whole map of forms in one pair of hands, which is why his is an entrance and Yupanqui's is a language.

## M5. The ungreased axle

The squeak is the company, and the company is the mechanism. "Los ejes de mi carreta" is a milonga on a text by the Uruguayan gaucho poet Romildo Risso, and the text is the mechanism, because the singer says "Because I do not grease the axles they call me abandoned. Because I do not grease the axles they call me abandoned. If I like them to sound, what do I want them greased for? It is too boring to follow and follow the track, to walk and walk the roads with nobody to entertain him. The axles of my cart, I will never grease them", and the refusal is the song, and the song is the refusal, and the refusal is the mechanism. The axle squeaks because it is not greased, and the squeak is the sound of the road, and the road is long, and the long is boring, and the boring is the track, and the track is the same, and the same is the road, and the road is the squeak, and the squeak is the company, and the company is the mechanism, because the man chooses the noise over the silence, and the choosing is the song, and the song is the squeak, and the squeak is the choice, and the choice is the mechanism. The world calls him abandoned for it, and the calling is the judgement, and the judgement is the axle, and the axle is the man, and the man is the cart, and the cart is the road, and the road is the song, and the song is the refusal, and the refusal is the mechanism. The milonga is the slow and grave song of the Pampas, and the slow is the cart, and the cart is the axle, and the axle is the squeak, and the squeak is the milonga, and the milonga is the mechanism, because the form fits the refusal, and the fitting is the song, and the song is the form, and the form is the refusal, and the refusal is the mechanism. **Takeaway:** let the axle squeak, and let the squeak be the company on the long road, and never grease it. Against the chain: Jara (#370 M5) also made a small thing the whole romance, but his is five minutes on a factory clock that ends with a siren, and Yupanqui's is an axle that squeaks for the whole road and never gets greased, which is why his is a clock and Yupanqui's is a refusal.

## M6. The muleteer who pays others' debts

The muleteer is the worker, and the worker is the mechanism. "El arriero" is possibly his greatest creation, and the greatness is the mechanism, because the song is born in the hills of Anta in the province of Salta, where the muleteer Antonio Fernandez, asked how his life is going, answers "Here you see me. Paying others' faults, driving others' cows", and Yupanqui takes note in his travel notebook and varies the words slightly and composes a "cancion criolla" of absolute currency, and the varying is the work, and the work is the mechanism, because the song opens in high poetry, "In the sands dance the whirlwinds, the sun plays in the brightness of the stony ground, and caught in the magic of the roads, the muleteer goes", and the opening is the poetry, and the poetry is the muleteer, and the muleteer is the road, and the road is the song, and the song is the mechanism. The muleteer pays others' faults and drives others' cows, and the paying is the work, and the work is the debt, and the debt is not his, and the not-his is the injustice, and the injustice is the song, and the song is the muleteer, and the muleteer is the mechanism, because the worker carries a debt he did not make, and the carrying is the life, and the life is the road, and the road is the song. The song is recorded in 1944 on Odeon 903 with "A orillas del Yi" on the other side, and the recording is the early work, and the early is the proof, and the proof is that the mechanism is there from the start, and the start is the muleteer, and the muleteer is the song. The authorship is disputed in the record: the popular record calls it his song, and the SADAIC record lists Nenette, as Pablo del Cerro, as versionista of "El Arriero", and the dispute is carried, not smoothed, and the carrying is the honesty, and the honesty is the mechanism. **Takeaway:** let the worker answer one question in his own words, and let his answer be the song, and let the debt be someone else's. Against the chain: Jara (#370 M8) also interviewed the settlement for weeks and let a resident speak inside the track, but his is a microphone carried into a settlement to record the lives the people live, and Yupanqui's is a notebook carried on muleback to take down one man's answer, which is why his is an interview and Yupanqui's is a meeting on the road.

## M7. The lullaby as indictment

The cradle song carries the hardest content, and the carrying is the mechanism. "Duerme, duerme negrito" rocks the Black child to sleep while the mother works, and the rocking is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the indictment, and the indictment is the cradle, and the cradle is the work, and the work is the mother, and the mother is the song. The verses tell the life of a woman who must work in the field, far from her home, during long days, to earn the sustenance of herself and her son, and the telling is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the field, and the field is the mother, and the mother is the child, and the child is the song, and the song is the mechanism, because the softest form carries the hardest fact, and the hardest fact is the mother working, and the working is the coughing, and the coughing is the mourning, and the mourning is the field, and the field is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the mechanism. The lyric promises the child quails and rich fruit and pork, and the promising is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the lie, and the lie is the tenderness, and the tenderness is the song, and the song is the promise, and the promise is the mechanism, because the mother promises what she cannot give, and the cannot-give is the poverty, and the poverty is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the promise, and the promise is the song. And if the Black child does not sleep, the white devil comes and eats his little foot, chacapumba, chacapumba, and the devil is the threat, and the threat is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the white devil, and the white devil is the master, and the master is the field, and the field is the song, and the song is the mechanism, because the lullaby names the devil by his colour, and the naming is the indictment, and the indictment is the cradle, and the cradle is the song. The song is not an innocent cradle song but an accusation against the exploitation of labour in the fields of Latin America, and the accusation is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the mechanism, because the mother leaves her child because she goes to the coffee plantation, and the leaving is the work, and the work is the mother, and the mother is the song. Jara sings it in 1969, and Mercedes Sosa sings it on "El grito de la tierra" in 1970, and Alfredo Zitarrosa sings it, and the singing is the afterlife, and the afterlife is the lullaby, and the lullaby is the indictment, and the indictment is the song. **Takeaway:** sing the lullaby at lullaby tempo over a mother working in the field, and let the white devil be the master, and let the soft form carry the hard fact. Against the chain: Jara (#370 M7) also made the lullaby the protest, but his is a lullaby for the vagrant child and the ragged boy and the baby who died before she had a chance to struggle, and Yupanqui's is a lullaby collected on a Caribbean border and carried around the world, which is why his is a cradle and Yupanqui's is a field.

## M8. The prisoner who writes in coplas

## Network

- **Mercedes Sosa (1935-2010):** the voice who carried him. Evidence: she records his compositions in the 1960s, and she puts "Duerme negrito" on "El grito de la tierra" in 1970, and she records the whole album "Mercedes Sosa interpreta a Atahualpa Yupanqui" in 1977, with "Piedra y camino" and "Guitarra dimelo tu" and "Chacarera de las piedras" and "La viajerita" and "Criollita santiguen~a" among its tracks, and she is named among the younger musicians who make him popular and call him Don Ata.
- **Jorge Cafrune (1937-1978):** the singer who carried him. Evidence: he records Yupanqui's compositions, and he pushes Mercedes Sosa onto the Cosquin stage in 1965, where she sings "Derrumbe indio", and he is named among the continuators of Yupanqui's style, with Suma Paz and Daniel Viglietti and Carlos Di Fulvio.
- **Facundo Cabral (1937-2011):** the younger singer. Evidence: he records Yupanqui's compositions, and he is named among the nueva cancion artists who make Yupanqui popular among younger musicians.
- **Edith Piaf (1915-1963):** the bridge to Paris. Evidence: she hears him in Paris in 1949, and she invites him to perform in Paris on Friday 7 July 1950 at the Theatre de l'Athenes, presenting him with "Voila Yupanqui!", and they meet at Paul Eluard's house, and the invitation leads at once to the Le Chant du Monde contract and the Charles Cros prize.
- **Antoinette Paule Pepin Fitzpatrick, "Nenette", as Pablo del Cerro (1908-1990):** the hidden co-author. Evidence: born 9 April 1908 in Saint Pierre and Miquelon, arrived in Argentina in 1928, met Yupanqui in Tucuman in 1942, corresponded from then, lived with him in Buenos Aires from 1946, a relationship of forty-eight years; classical pianist, co-author of 63 pieces by one count and 65 songs by another, including "El arriero" and "Luna tucumana" and "Chacarera de las piedras" and "El alazan" and "La nadita"; civil marriage in Tlaxcala, Mexico, on Wednesday 1 August 1962, and again on Wednesday 3 January 1979; died Wednesday 14 November 1990 in Buenos Aires.
- **Roberto "Kolla" Chavero (born Wednesday 8 September 1948):** the son and keeper. Evidence: the only son of Yupanqui and Nenette; he sets much of the "Coplas de Juan Prisionero" to music decades later; president of the Fundacion Atahualpa Yupanqui since 2003; his son Emiliano runs the house-museum until his accidental death in 2021 aged 46.
- **Eduardo Falu (1923-2013):** the young guitarist. Evidence: he plays in Yupanqui's Aconquija group in 1941, then aged 18; the critical essay contrasts Falu, who writes songs in collaboration with the poet Jaime Davalos, with Yupanqui, who writes words and music together.
- **Ariel Ramirez (1921-2010) and Felix Luna (1925-2009):** the collaborators contrasted. Evidence: the critical essay contrasts Ramirez, who writes with Luna, with Yupanqui the double author; Luna writes the monograph "Atahualpa Yupanqui" (Ediciones Jucar, 1974); Ramirez and Luna make "Mujeres Argentinas" with Mercedes Sosa.
- **Los Chalchaleros:** the interpreters. Evidence: they record "Luna tucumana" in successive versions, and they share a 1954 Teatro Astral recital with Yupanqui and Santiago Ayala.
- **Santiago Ayala, "El Chucaro" (1918-1994):** the dancer beside him. Evidence: Yupanqui meets him in Salavina in October 1947; his ashes rest beside Yupanqui's under the oak at Cerro Colorado.
- **Daniel Viglietti (1939-2017):** the Uruguayan bridge. Evidence: listed among the interpreters of Yupanqui's compositions; named among those who count Yupanqui among their principal references, with Alfredo Zitarrosa and Silvio Rodriguez and Cecilia Todd; Jara sings two Viglietti songs on "Pongo", so Viglietti is a second bridge between Jara and Yupanqui.
- **Alfredo Zitarrosa (1936-1989):** the singer of the lullaby. Evidence: he performs "Duerme negrito"; he counts Yupanqui among his principal references.
- **Pablo Neruda (1904-1973):** the poet. Evidence: Yupanqui meets him in Chile in 1947 and stays with him some weeks; Yupanqui records "Cancion para Pablo Neruda" in 1974.
- **Isabel Aretz (1909-2005):** the ethnographer. Evidence: Yupanqui makes friends with her in Tucuman in 1940; the critical essay compares his collecting method with hers and with Leda Valladares's, noting he chooses the popular interpreter over the ethnographer.
- **Alfred Metraux (1902-1963):** the ethnologist. Evidence: Yupanqui meets him in Tucuman in the early 1930s and accompanies him through the Northwest to Bolivia.
- **Romildo Risso (1882-1946):** the poet of the axle. Evidence: the Uruguayan gaucho poet whose texts Yupanqui sets, including "Los ejes de mi carreta" and "El aromo"; he finances the first 1936 Odeon Mangruyo discs through Yerba Nectar.
- **Osvaldo Pugliese (1905-1995), Alfredo Varela, Elias Castelnuovo, Jorge Portogalo:** the Communist circle. Evidence: fellow Communist artists and friends in the 1940s, named in the critical essay.
- **Osvaldo Bayer and Julio Cortazar:** the friends in Paris. Evidence: friends with whom he keeps close communication in the Paris years, proof of his human-rights commitment.
- **Angel Parra (1943-2017):** the late companion on stage. Evidence: listed among the interpreters of Yupanqui's compositions; plays concerts with Yupanqui in Berlin and Zurich in late January and February 1992.
- **Pedro Soler and Oscar Caceres:** the three guitars. Evidence: they share the Paris stage with Yupanqui for "3 amigos, 3 guitarras" at the Theatre des Champs-Elysees, 19 to 24 January 1978.
- **Victor Jara (1932-1973):** the repertoire heir. Evidence: Jara sings "Duerme, duerme negrito" on "Pongo en tus manos abiertas" in 1969, and sings Yupanqui's Argentine songs at parties, and is listed among the interpreters of his compositions. Limit carried: no direct Jara to Yupanqui meeting is evidenced in any source read; Radio Universidad de Chile (2026) states plainly that they did not meet in life; the link is repertoire and admiration.
- **Violeta Parra (1917-1967):** the founder. Evidence: listed among the interpreters of Yupanqui's compositions; the "Nueva cancion chilena" article names her as the founder of the movement Yupanqui is listed among.
