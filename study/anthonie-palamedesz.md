# Anthonie Palamedesz.: Study Notes

**Site:** Anthonie Palamedesz. (bapt. 9 November 1602, Leith, Scotland - 27 November 1673, Amsterdam), Delft painter of guardroom scenes, merry companies and portraits; the Delft branch of the kortegaardje, who tamed the guardroom in its second phase -- the soldiers' behaviour refined, the booty gone, middle-class people in the room, and the women mothers instead of courtesans -- and who painted the figures inside other men's churches.

## The life

Anthonie Palamedesz. was baptised on 9 November 1602 in South Leith, Scotland, as Antonius Stevens, the son of Palamedes Willemsz. Stevens and Marie Arsene (Maeijken van Naerssen), who had married in Leith on 6 June 1601. His father was a sculptor and stone-cutter -- a gem-cutter who carved semi-precious stones (jasper, porphyry, agate) into vases and decorative objects -- in service of King James VI of Scotland, likely Flemish by origin, possibly travelling with James to London when James became James I of England. The family left Scotland after the youngest child was born and settled in Delft, where the boys grew up. When Anthonie and his younger brother registered with the Delft Guild they paid the discounted entry fee reserved for city residents, suggesting their father had lived in Delft before Scotland, possibly as a Protestant fugitive from the Spanish Inquisition in his native Flanders -- Houbraken tradition, flagged as speculation (leg 1).

He had two brothers. The elder, Guilliaem (William, 1601-1688), became a tailor. The younger, Palamedes (I) (bapt. 6 August 1605, Leith - buried 26 March 1638, Delft), was the family's battle painter, and was most likely taught by Anthonie himself. The naming trap runs through the whole family and this study states it once, plainly: Palamedes (I) the battle painter is Anthonie's younger brother, not his son; Palamedes (II) (b. 1632/1633 - d. 1705), Anthonie's eldest son, was traditionally believed to have been a painter, but the current view (RKD, per EN Wikipedia) is that this was likely not the case; Palamedes (III) (1631-1683), a painter, was Palamedes (I)'s son -- Anthonie's nephew. There is no painter son named Willem: "Willem" was the elder brother, the tailor. The brief's "sons Palamedes and Willem, both painters" is wrong, and the study does not carry it (legs 1 and 3).

His teachers are traditional, not documented. The sources say he was taught by Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt, the leading Delft portraitist, and by Hans Jordaens (I), the Delft history and genre painter -- not Jacob Jordaens of Antwerp, whom he must not be conflated with. Hendrick Gerritsz. Pot, the Amsterdam painter documented in Delft around 1620, is also named as a possible teacher (Liedtke 2001, p. 318; Sutton, Dictionary of Art 1996). All three attributions are presumed; none is documented, and the study hedges them all (leg 1).

He lived in Haarlem in 1623-1627, where he met Frans Hals -- reported, not fully verified in this leg; the Rijksmuseum catalogue entry says he was "probably in Amsterdam around 1626", having copied sitters from a civic-guard piece by Claes Pietersz. Lastman (d. 1625). He entered the Delft Guild of St Luke on 6 December 1621, per the Guild book (Montias/Kaldenbach chronological list: "1621, 6 Dec. Anthonis Palmedes (1601-1673) genre, from Delft"); some secondary sources say 1636 instead, and EN Wikipedia carries "1621 or 1636". The study follows the Guild book: 1621 is the documented entry, 1636 is best treated as an error. He was deacon (hoofdman) of the Guild in 1635, 1663 and 1672, plus either 1638 or 1658 -- EN Wikipedia says 1658, NL/RU/UK Wikipedias say 1638, and the study does not resolve it (legs 1 and 3).

He married Anna Joosten van Hoorndijck on 16 March 1630 in Delft; six children. Before the marriage he lived in the Vlamingstraat; after it the couple lived on the Burgwal. Anna died in 1651. He remarried on 29 December 1658 to Agata Woodward (Aechgen Woedewart), daughter of an Englishman doing business with Dutch printers; one son, Arthur (1659-1693). He was financially successful early -- he bought a house in Delft in 1638 for 3,400 guilders -- and by 1668 his finances had declined sharply and he received an exceptional subsidy from the Delft city council. Around 1670 he moved to Amsterdam, probably to live with his eldest son at Oudeschans. He died in Amsterdam on 27 November 1673 and was buried on 1 December in the Oude Kerk. (One UK Wikipedia infobox gives "7 November 1673" -- almost certainly a typo for 27 November; every other source gives 27 November.) The old form "born 1601, Delft" still circulates in museum and auction boilerplate; it was corrected by Maja Westhoff's genealogy (*Gens Nostra* 73, 2018, pp. 188-189), which found the Scottish baptismal records. Houbraken's "born in London" is superseded by Westhoff; the study does not use it (leg 1).

He was also a miniaturist, printmaker and draughtsman. WGA lists drawings from the 1630s-40s in black chalk and brush with brown ink: *Seated Cavalier with a Sword and a Raised Glass*, *Walking Musketeer Seen from Behind*, *Couple Playing Trictrac*, *Standing Man*. Like other Delft masters he exhibited in the great hall of the Binnenhof, The Hague (leg 1).

His place in Delft is stated the same way in every source: he "played a major role in the development of genre painting in Delft in the mid 17th century" (Liedtke/Weller, repeated across sources). A 2010 exhibition on the artist was planned at Museum Prinsenhof, Delft (Kolfin/Jansen, per Sotheby's 2006) -- planned, not confirmed held; the study does not assert it happened (leg 1).

## M1. The Delft branch: the ruined hall open at the rear

Palamedesz.'s guardroom has a fixed architecture, and it is his own. The setting is "a large hall of a ruin or a castle, which is open at the rear and usually has a large fireplace" (EN Wikipedia). The 1647 Rijksmuseum picture (W11) is the formula in its dated form: an officer stands between his men issuing orders, a soldier with a banner and drum at right, a man with a breastplate beside him, and at left two men warming themselves by the hearth. The Sotheby's 2011 guardroom (W13), the one work any leg actually saw as an image, is the same formula with the light falling differently: a dark ruined stone hall, open at the rear, dominated by a huge plain fireplace with a black opening at centre-left; pikemen with very long pikes descending a stone staircase at right; card players crouched over a game on the floor; a man smoking at far left; muskets leaning against the wall; a man asleep, slumped, at far right (leg 2).

Against Duck, the contrast is exact. Duck made the room big -- the wide hall, the deserted church, the stable -- and the architecture did the moral work. Palamedesz. makes the room a ruin and opens it at the back: the hall is not a church emptied of worship but a castle emptied of war, its rear wall gone, its fireplace the only thing still working. The room is the subject in both men; in Duck it accuses, in Palamedesz. it shelters. The staircase at right in W13 is the depth device -- figures stacked on different planes, the eye climbing out of the room as well as into it (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** build the room as a ruin open at the rear -- a big fireplace with a black mouth anchoring one side, a staircase climbing out the other, figures stacked on different planes. The watchable behaviour is the room holding the men, not judging them: the eye moves from the hearth to the stairs and back, and nobody is going anywhere.

## M2. The second phase: from c. 1645, the soldiers refine and the booty vanishes

The guardroom has two phases, and Palamedesz. is the painter of the second. "In a second phase, starting roughly around 1645, the behaviour of the soldiers became more refined, thus reflecting the growing civility in Dutch society. In this later phase guardroom scenes were devoid of booty and other signs of war and showed middle-class people interacting with the soldiers" (EN Wikipedia, citing Sotheby's and Rosen 2008). The early phase -- resting soldiers seated, conversing, tending uniforms, arguing over loot, carousing with prostitutes, cards, pipes, morally questionable behaviour -- is the Amsterdam inheritance and Duck's Utrecht version. The second phase is Palamedesz.'s Delft: the loot is gone, the women are not for sale, and the men in the room are, on Borger's reading, not actual soldiers at all but civil guards whose main task was to protect their cities -- middle-class burghers at leisure (leg 3).

The dated anchors make the turn visible. The 1635 Hannover guardroom (W08) is still the early phase: soldiers and women smoking and drinking. The two dated guardrooms of 1647 (W11, Rijksmuseum; W12, Dublin) are the second phase in its settled form: an officer issuing orders, men warming themselves at the hearth, no booty, no transaction. The Sotheby's 2011 picture (W13) is the same composition as the 1647 Rijksmuseum picture, reversed, with the same officer -- the formula fixed, the phase settled (legs 1 and 2).

This is the guardroom's answer to the question the whole chain has been asking since #565: what happens to a genre about idle soldiers when the war ends and the soldiers stay? Codde and Duyster invented the room; Duck widened it; Palamedesz. civilised it. The soldiers do not leave; they become burghers, and the room becomes a club.

**Takeaway:** stage the second phase, not the first -- no booty, no loot, no transaction, the men in the room as civic guards at leisure rather than mercenaries on the make. The watchable behaviour is refinement itself: orders given, men warming their hands, a game played without stakes, the room quiet enough that the fireplace is the loudest thing in it.

## M3. Masculinity pacified: women as mothers, not courtesans

Palamedesz.'s guardrooms are unique in one respect, and the uniqueness is the whole mechanism. "His guardroom scenes are unique, in that he includes women in their role as mothers and not their more conventional role of courtesans" (EN Wikipedia). Rosen's 2008 article gives it its name: *Masculinity Pacified: Women as Mothers in the Guardroom Scenes of Anthonie Palamedes (1602-1673)*. "Palamedesz.' guardroom scenes thus represent the masculine outdoors that has been pacified and tamed by feminine domestic motherhood. In other words, the apparently virile guard room scenes are in fact an embodiment of masculinity pacified, reflecting a broader process in 17th-century Dutch society of growing femininity and civility" (Rosen 2008, via EN Wikipedia). The full Rosen text was not read by any leg; the thesis is taken from the Wikipedia summary, which cites Rosen directly, and is corroborated by a second source with the same claim (leg 3).

The picture is in W13, the one image seen: at lower right sits a woman in a gold dress with a small child in white and a little dog -- the mother-and-child counterweight to the card players, the smokers, the pikemen and the sleeping man. The gold dress is one of only two strong colour accents in a picture of muted browns, greys and black (the other is the officer's blue sash and plume). The mother is the colour in the room. The same figure recurs across the oeuvre: a woman breastfeeding a child is the *Taste* panel of the Louvre *Five Senses* series (W10); a woman with a child appears in the Wikipedia-illustrated trumpeter guardroom (leg 2).

Against Duck, the reversal is complete. Duck's women are courtesans, procuresses, fortune-tellers, sleeping bodies on drums of loot -- the transaction made flesh, the moral in the prop. Palamedesz.'s women are mothers, and the moral is dissolved: there is nothing to accuse, because the woman is not selling anything and the men are not buying. The guardroom is pacified not by removing the women, as Duck does late, but by changing what the women are (leg 3).

**Takeaway:** put a mother and a child in the guardroom, at the edge of the action, and let her carry the only warm colour in the room. The watchable behaviour is the counterweight: the eye travels from the men and their game to the mother and child and back, and the room gets quieter each time it does.

## M4. The fixed centre: the chubby officer in the tall hat

Every Palamedesz. guardroom has the same man at its centre. "The centre [is] almost always taken up by an officer, who is chubby and wearing a tall hat and a small moustache" (EN Wikipedia). In W13, seen as an image: the tall hat with a broad blue feather plume, grey coat, blue sash, pinkish breeches, white stockings, holding a tall halberd or partisan and gesturing as if giving orders. In W11, the 1647 Rijksmuseum picture: the same officer, standing between his men, issuing orders. The two pictures are the same composition reversed -- the officer delivering orders in one, the same officer delivering orders in the other, the room flipped around him (leg 1).

The mechanism is the emblem, not the portrait. Duck has a stock company of figures transferred by drawing from picture to picture -- the boot-pulling man, the standing officer, the same dog in the same pose. Palamedesz. has a single fixed figure, the officer, who recurs across pictures as the genre's centre of gravity. He is not a commander in the Codde sense -- Codde's commander is always legible, the stage's anchor -- and he is not a sitter; he is a type, repeated until he becomes the picture's signature. He signs the pictures too: "Palamedes" on the chimney breast in the Sotheby's 2011 guardroom, "A Palamedes" (AP in ligature) lower left in the Sotheby's 2012 guardroom (leg 1).

The scenes "reach the elegance and monumentality of civic-guard (schutterij) group portraits" (leg 1) -- and the officer is why. A civic-guard piece has its captain at the centre; Palamedesz. gives his guardroom the same centre, in miniature, on a panel a fraction of the size. The guardroom becomes a schutterstuk that fits on a wall at home.

**Takeaway:** fix one figure at the centre and never move him -- a chubby officer, tall hat, small moustache, halberd in hand, giving orders -- and let the room flip around him from picture to picture. The watchable behaviour is recognition: the same man, the same gesture, a different room, and the eye finding him first every time.

## M5. Light from the left, figures crowded to one side

The merry companies and the guardrooms share one lighting system. "The interiors of these scenes are usually lit by a strong light entering from the left from two unknown sources" (EN Wikipedia, after Liedtke). "A strong light falls in from the left and enhances the subtle tension of the scene" (Sotheby's lot 79). The figures are "often typically crowded on one side of the picture" (Liedtke). Essential Vermeer, on the 1632 Mauritshuis merry company (W03), notes the light "diminishes gradually as it travels from left to right" -- the fall-off is the composition (legs 1 and 2).

The mechanism is the crowded edge against the bare wall. The light enters from the left, the figures crowd to one side, and the rest of the room is wall and dark -- a relatively deep space with figures placed in various poses on different planes, but the mass of them on one side and the emptiness on the other. In W13 the staircase at right does the depth work the bare wall does in the merry companies: figures stacked on planes, the eye climbing. The palette stays muted throughout -- browns, greys, black -- with one or two strong accents (the blue sash and plume, the gold dress) doing the colour work (leg 2).

What the literature does not support is a claim about paint handling. No technical-analysis source (cross-sections, pigment studies) was found by any leg. The safe formulation, and the one this study uses, is: small-to-medium oak panels, smooth, unshowy handling, tonal modelling, strong left-hand light. "Fairly summarily painted interiors" (EN Wikipedia, after Liedtke) and "originality in his treatment of light and tone" (Liedtke 2001) are the sourced phrases. The "thin smooth paint" of the Duck study is not sourced for Palamedesz. and is not asserted here (leg 1).

**Takeaway:** light the room from the left and crowd the figures to one side -- let the light die across a bare wall, and put the colour in one or two accents only. The watchable behaviour is the fall-off: the eye starts where the light is strongest and travels right into the dark, and the room reads as deep because the light gives up.

## M6. The merry company without moralising

Palamedesz.'s merry companies are the other half of the practice, and they run on a different rule from everyone else's. The subjects are the same -- music, drinking, smoking, gaming, courtship -- but the tone is not. His elegant, restrained elite "never loose themselves in exuberance and decadency" in contrast to Dirck Hals (Kolfin, via Sotheby's lot 79). He shows "little interest in moralising: he painted the scenes solely for the enjoyment of his patrons" (Liedtke). The best work in this genre is early, the 1630s; the later paintings "appear weaker and more formulaic" (Liedtke, via the Met catalogue) (leg 1).

The mechanism is the emblem held inside the enjoyment. Recurring motifs are re-played across pictures: a page pouring a drink, the emblem of modesty and restraint; a woman at the virginal inscribed *Omnis Spiritus Laudet Dominum* (Ps. 150:6) -- a hymn to music's power, or love in context; the same inscription in the c. 1630/35 family portrait, where it signals family harmony. The moral is in the prop, but it is not a warning: the page pours, the woman plays, and the picture does not punish anyone for it (leg 1).

The anchors are the early 1630s cluster: the Mauritshuis *Merry Company Dining and Making Music* of 1632 (W03, 47.4 x 72.6 cm), the Rijksmuseum *Merry Company in a Room* of 1633 (W05, 55 x 89 cm), the Karlsruhe merry company of 1634 (W06). Small oil-on-panel interiors, shallow rooms, figures crowded to one side against bare walls, hard left light dying across the wall -- the same lighting system as M5, the same restraint as M2, applied to pleasure instead of soldiering (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** stage pleasure without the warning -- music, drink, a game, courtship, all of it elegant and restrained, with one emblem (a page pouring, an inscribed virginal) doing the moral work quietly. The watchable behaviour is the absence of punishment: nobody is caught, nobody is sorry, and the picture is enjoyable because it refuses to scold.

## M7. Staffage for other men's churches

Palamedesz. had a second practice running alongside his own pictures: he painted the people inside other men's architecture. "He further possibly painted the staffage in a few views of church and palace interiors by Dirck van Delen and Anthonie de Lorme" (EN Wikipedia) -- note the hedge, "possibly", which the study keeps. A 1636 document shows he painted figures in a picture by Johannes van Vucht (Jan van Vught); he appears to have done the same for Dirck van Delen and Bartholomeus van Bassen, and possibly Anthonie de Lorme (Rijksmuseum/Wuestman 2007). The direction of the relationship matters: de Lorme painted church interiors, Palamedesz. added the figures -- and Ludolf de Jongh later did the same staffage job for de Lorme, which is part of the Delft chain (leg 1).

The late works are the evidence. The *Great Assembly of 1651* (W15) puts his small figures into a vast architectural interior on copper -- Bartholomeus van Bassen's architecture, Palamedesz.'s staffage, the reverse of his own guardrooms, where the architecture serves the figures. The de Lorme church interior sold at Sotheby's in 2015 (W20) is a Renaissance-style church at night with an elegant couple making an entrance: Sotheby's calls it a late work by Palamedesz., "as it includes elements taken from actual churches, notably the pulpit from Rotterdam's Laurenskerk, while also hearkening back to his early imaginary and fantastical style." A companion piece, de Lorme and Palamedesz.'s *An imaginary church interior illuminated by candlelight* (Christie's), has a Black man in a light purple mantle holding a long candle as the light source -- the figures carrying the light inside the architecture (leg 2).

This is the second church link in the chain after Duck, and it runs the other way. Duck painted the church himself and put his own soldiers in it. Palamedesz. painted the soldiers into someone else's church -- the guardroom painter moonlighting as the figure-painter inside other men's naves, his small elegant figures animating cavernous, candlelit interiors with a recognisable prop (the Laurenskerk pulpit) anchoring the fiction (legs 1 and 2).

**Takeaway:** put small elegant figures inside a vast architecture that someone else built -- a candlelit nave, a great hall, a palace interior -- and let the figures carry the light. The watchable behaviour is the scale inversion: the architecture is enormous, the people are small, and a single candle or a single pulpit is what makes the space legible.

## M8. The late opening: figures shrink, portraits go suave, companies go outdoors

The late career opens up in three directions at once, and all three are the same move: the figure gets smaller and the world gets bigger. First, scale and setting: the Great Assembly (W15, 1651) puts his small figures into a vast architectural interior on copper -- staffage as a service to another painter's architecture. Second, portraiture: the 1652 Bronckhorst pair (W16) and the 1654 portraits (W17, W18) show him absorbing Bartholomeus van der Helst's fashionable Amsterdam portraiture; the early stiffness is gone, the sitters are "suave". Third, the merry company goes outdoors and upmarket: the 1665 Boijmans family group of eight is set on the rolling terrain of a country estate, among animals and dead game, in the manner of Gonzales Coques -- the interior company has become a landed family (legs 1 and 2).

The portraits were always the second pillar of the practice -- the *Portrait of Willem Backer* of 1626 (W01) is the earliest dated work in the list, and the *Family Portrait* of 1635 (W07, Antwerp) recalls Cornelis de Vos's 1631 *Portrait of Anthonie Reyniers and His Family*, its animated group arrangement likely coming from his genre experience. The late portraits keep the genre habits: individuals, couples, families, often with dead game, hunting implements and hounds. The final-year work is an *Elegant Company in an Interior* / *A Wedding Party* of 1673 (Chrysler Museum of Art, 63.15.1) -- the company picture, still being painted in the year he died (leg 2).

He keeps painting guardrooms into the 1660s, but the subject is by then fully domesticated: the room is a ruin, the officer is an emblem, the mother is the colour, the booty is gone, and the fireplace is the loudest thing in the room. Financial decline by 1668 (the Delft city subsidy), the move to Amsterdam around 1670, death on 27 November 1673 (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** end with the opening, not the closing -- figures shrinking into vast architecture, portraits going suave, companies stepping outdoors onto a country estate. The watchable behaviour is the long zoom out: the same small figures, the same officer, the same mother, in a room that keeps getting bigger until it is a landscape.

## The teachers question

The open thread of this study is smaller than Duck's pupillage question but shaped the same way: everything is traditional, nothing is documented. He was "taught by the leading portrait painter Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt and the history and genre painter Hans Jordaens" (EN Wikipedia, Life) -- and the sentence is the whole of the evidence. Hendrick Gerritsz. Pot is named as a possible third teacher (Liedtke 2001, p. 318; Sutton 1996). No document places Palamedesz. in any master's studio, and no document settles which of the three taught him what. The study presents all three as presumed, not documented, and does not write "pupil of Mierevelt" as fact (leg 1).

The forward direction is firmer. Houbraken says Ludolf de Jongh studied under Cornelis Saftleven, then Anthonie Palamedesz. in Delft, then Jan van Bijlert in Utrecht. De Jongh's earliest signed works (from 1642, after his return from France) show Palamedesz.'s influence; his early genre scenes -- dark, monochromatic interiors, peasants and soldiers, bright accents -- resemble Palamedesz.'s; like his master he painted guardroom scenes. De Jongh became the leading genre painter in Rotterdam in the 1650s and influenced Pieter de Hooch. (Some doubt attaches to the van Bijlert leg, not the Palamedesz. leg.) His younger brother Palamedes (I) was most likely taught by Anthonie himself. The sources also credit him with influence on Hendrick van der Burgh and Willem Duyster, and name as followers Adriaen Lucasz. Fonteyn, Laurence Neter and Abraham van Westerveld (legs 1 and 3).

The one hard compositional link in the network is single-sourced and the study says so: the V&A, on *The Trumpeter* (W17), states that "in the 1640s and 1650s Palamedesz. often painted guardroom scenes... adopting compositions recalling designs by Duyster and Jacob Duck but executed in a looser, less refined technique." That is the direct Duck-to-Palamedesz. link, and the evidential backbone of the doorway hop -- one source, V&A catalogue (Kauffmann 1973 / Sutton 1984), flagged as such (leg 3).

## Evolution

Early (c. 1626-1638): the merry company is the engine. Small oil-on-panel interiors (typically 46-55 x 70-89 cm), shallow rooms, figures crowded to one side against bare walls, strong light entering from the left and dying across the wall. Subjects are music, drinking, smoking, gaming, courtship -- elegant rather than moralising, unlike contemporaries. The guardroom appears in parallel but in its early-phase register: soldiers smoking, drinking, arguing over loot, carousing with women (W08, 1635). Portraits begin early too (W01, 1626; W09, 1638) and are already the second pillar. Anchors: W03 (1632, Mauritshuis), W05 (1633, Rijksmuseum), W07 (1635, Antwerp family portrait).

Middle (c. 1640s): the guardroom becomes the signature, and the tone turns. The genre's "second phase, starting roughly around 1645": behaviour refines, booty and signs of war disappear, middle-class people interact with the soldiers, and -- Palamedesz.'s unique move -- women appear as mothers, not courtesans (W13: the mother, child and dog at lower right; the breastfeeding woman in the *Five Senses*). Rosen's reading: masculinity pacified, the masculine outdoors tamed by feminine domestic motherhood. The architecture settles into its formula: a large hall of a ruin or castle, open at the rear, with a large fireplace (W11: two men warming themselves at the hearth; W13: the black fireplace mouth anchoring the left half). The central officer -- chubby, tall hat, small moustache -- becomes a repeated, almost emblematic figure, and compositions take on the monumentality of civic-guard group portraits. The trumpeter becomes a signature motif, repeated across many variants. Anchors: W11 and W12, both dated 1647.

Late (c. 1651-1673): three shifts. (1) Scale and setting open up: the Great Assembly (W15) puts his small figures into a vast architectural interior on copper -- staffage as a service to another painter's architecture, the reverse of his own guardrooms where architecture serves the figures. (2) Portraiture becomes suave and Amsterdam-aware: the 1652 Bronckhorst pair and the 1654 portraits show him absorbing Bartholomeus van der Helst's fashionable portraiture; the early stiffness is gone. (3) The merry company goes outdoors and upmarket: the 1665 Boijmans family group of eight is set on the rolling terrain of a country estate, among animals and dead game, in the manner of Gonzales Coques -- the interior company has become a landed family. Church-interior staffage (W20 and the Christie's candlelight interior) is the late collaborative thread: his small elegant figures animate de Lorme's and van Delen's cavernous, candlelit naves, with the Laurenskerk pulpit as a recognisable prop. He keeps painting guardrooms into the 1660s, but the subject is by then fully domesticated.

What does not change: the muted palette, the strong left-hand light, the figures crowded to one side, the small panel, the officer at the centre, the enjoyment without moralising. What changes is the room (shallow interior to ruined hall to vast hall to country estate), the cast (courtesans out, mothers in, burghers in), and the light (hard left-side daylight on bare walls early, smoky fireplace-and-candlelit gloom late).

## Aesthetic logic

Palamedesz.'s aesthetic is a small room, a strong light from the left, and a fixed man at the centre. The guardroom as Codde and Duyster invented it is a room of men off duty, and the genre's pleasures are abundance, noise, and the textures of loot and drink. Duck kept the room and made it bigger, and let the architecture accuse. Palamedesz. keeps the room and tames it: the ruin open at the rear, the big fireplace, the chubby officer giving orders, the mother and child at the edge with the only warm colour in the picture, the booty gone, the burghers in. His light is one system, not two: a strong light entering from the left, dying gradually as it travels right, the figures crowded to the lit side and the bare wall doing the rest. His palette is muted throughout -- browns, greys, black -- with one or two accents (a blue sash, a gold dress) carrying the colour. His merry companies run on the same system with a different subject: pleasure without the warning, restraint as the style, an emblem (a page pouring, an inscribed virginal) doing the moral work quietly.

The same logic runs through the career in one direction: elegant companies in shallow rooms in the 1630s, the guardroom civilised in the 1640s, the figures shrinking into vast architecture in the 1650s, the company stepping outdoors onto a country estate in the 1660s. The repertoire never quite changes -- the officer, the trumpeter, the card players, the mother, the fireplace, the staircase -- and the refinement is all in the room, the light, and the repetition: the same officer, the same composition reversed, the same cast restaged in a taller ruin.

The failures of the record are part of the logic too. He was born in Scotland and the old books said Delft; his teachers are traditional and undocumented; his sons were called painters and were not; his brother was the battle painter and the battle pictures still circulate under his name; his most-cited article, Rosen 2008, was not read by any leg, only summarised. A painter who spent forty years making the same room quieter and more civilised, and whose own biography is mostly a set of dates other people wrote down -- the baptism, the Guild entry, the marriages, the burial -- is a painter whose career is a long study of one interior, tamed by stages until only the officer, the mother and the fireplace are left.

## Positions

- The dates are bapt. 9 November 1602, South Leith, Scotland (Westhoff 2018, from the baptismal records) to 27 November 1673, Amsterdam (buried 1 December, Oude Kerk). - The Guild entry is 6 December 1621 (Guild book, Montias/Kaldenbach); "1636" is best treated as an error. The deacon years are 1635, 1663, 1672, plus either 1638 or 1658 -- sources disagree, and the study does not resolve it.
- The teachers are presumed, not documented: Mierevelt, Hans Jordaens (I) -- not Jacob Jordaens -- and possibly Hendrick Pot. Do not write "pupil of Mierevelt" as fact.

## Network

The city map in one sentence (Palamedesz. article): "The principal exponents in this genre were Pieter Codde, Willem Duyster and Simon Kick in Amsterdam, Jacob Duck in Utrecht and Anthonie Palamedesz. and Jacob van Velsen in Delft." The chain has been walking the guardroom from city to city -- Amsterdam (#563-#566), Utrecht (#567), now Delft (#568).

The two inventors: Pieter Codde (baptised 11 December 1599, buried 12 October 1678) and Willem Cornelisz. Duyster (1599-1635) "developed and popularised the guardroom scene". Palamedesz. adopted compositions recalling designs by Duyster and Jacob Duck -- the V&A's single-source link, and the backbone of the doorway hop. His own article places him downstream of both inventors and of the northern-Holland contemporaries whose works he "must have known": Dirck Hals, Duyster, Codde and Hendrik Pot (leg 3).

The Delft co-exponent: Jacob Jansz. van Velsen (1597-1656) -- "According to the RKD Jacob influenced Willem Cornelisz Duyster and is known for genre works and military pieces, most notably conversation pieces in the manner of Pieter Codde and Dirck Hals." He is the hinge the other way: the Delft man who shaped the Amsterdam inventor, while Palamedesz. is the Delft man who received the Amsterdam genre back. He is the #569 Reserve (leg 3).

The pupil who carries the line out: Ludolf de Jongh -- Houbraken says he studied under Cornelis Saftleven, then Anthonie Palamedesz. in Delft, then Jan van Bijlert in Utrecht. His earliest signed works (from 1642) show Palamedesz.'s influence; like his master he painted guardroom scenes; he became the leading genre painter in Rotterdam in the 1650s and influenced Pieter de Hooch. The Delft guardroom leaves Delft through him (legs 1 and 3).

The church-interior pair: Dirck van Delen and Anthonie de Lorme -- Palamedesz. "possibly painted the staffage in a few views of church and palace interiors" by them, and in Bartholomeus van Bassen's *Great Assembly of 1651*. De Lorme painted the churches, Palamedesz. added the figures; de Jongh later did the same staffage job for de Lorme. This keeps the Duck church-interior thread alive in the Delft study, running the other way: Duck painted his own churches, Palamedesz. populated other men's (legs 1 and 3).

The family: his father Palamedes Willemsz. Stevens, gem-cutter to James VI; his younger brother Palamedes (I), the battle painter, most likely taught by Anthonie himself; his nephew Palamedes (III), a painter; his eldest son Palamedes (II), traditionally believed a painter, currently thought likely not. The naming trap is the family's own: four men called Palamedes across three generations, and the study keeps them straight (legs 1 and 3).

The teachers: Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt (studied as #527), the Delft portrait spine -- Palamedesz. became one of Delft's leading portraitists after Mierevelt's death in 1641 -- and Hans Jordaens, the Delft history and genre painter, not Jacob Jordaens of Antwerp. Both are traditional, undocumented, and hedged (legs 1 and 3).

## Future candidates
