## The life: fourteen years, three exits from the rectangle

Blinky Palermo was born Peter Schwarze on 2 June 1943 in Leipzig, Germany, with a twin brother, Michael, and the two were adopted as infants by Erika and Wilhelm Heisterkamp, after which he was known as Peter Heisterkamp. The brief for this study said "born Peter Heisterkamp"; every source read says born Peter Schwarze, and Heisterkamp is the adoptive name, so this file prints Schwarze. In 1961 he began his artistic training at the Werkkunstschule in Munster, and in 1962 he began studying at the Kunstakademie in Dusseldorf with Bruno Goller. (C26023, leg 1 C25900-C25903; synthesis C26006)

In 1964 he changed to the group taught by Joseph Beuys, and in the same year he adopted the pseudonym Blinky Palermo, after Frank "Blinky" Palermo, the American mafioso and boxing promoter who managed Sonny Liston. Ketterer gives the reason as his physical similarity to the mafioso; Dia says he adopted the pseudonym in 1964 encouraged by Beuys, his teacher. In 1965 he had his first official exhibition using the name Palermo, in 1966 his first solo show at Galerie Friedrich+Dahlem in Munich, and in 1967 he finished his studies at the academy. The friendships he made during his student days with Sigmar Polke and Gerhard Richter lasted beyond his later move to Monchengladbach in 1969. (C26024, leg 1 C25904-C25909)

His "objects" -- summarily painted canvases wrapped around sculptural stretchers, sometimes arranged in diptychs -- began in 1964. Between late 1966 and 1972 he produced a series of circa 65 Stoffbilder (Fabric Paintings): coloured materials of different widths sewn together along horizontal or vertical seams and attached to stretchers, the colour and material taken ready-made from department-store fabrics and stitched together by others, mostly by his wife Ingrid Kohlhofer on a sewing machine. Beginning in 1968 he realised more than 20 murals and wall drawings at various sites in Europe, including Edinburgh and Brussels, recorded in preparatory sketches and photographic documentation; the originals no longer exist because they were bound to their place of installation. In 1969 he moved to Monchengladbach and set up a studio he shared with Imi Knoebel and Ulrich Ruckriem. (C26025, leg 1 C25910-C25912; leg 2 C25950, C25952)

In 1970, after visiting New York with Gerhard Richter, he and Richter jointly submitted designs for the sports facilities for the 1972 Olympic Games in Munich: for the front of the arena they proposed an array of glass windows in 27 colours, each colour appearing 50 times, with the distribution determined randomly. The proposal was submitted, not built. Four years earlier, when Richter began his Colour Chart paintings in 1966, he had his friend Palermo randomly call out colours, which Richter then adopted for the work; chance thus played its role in the first Colour Chart series only, since by 1971 Richter used mathematical procedures and random permutation without Palermo. He took part in documenta 5 in 1972, and a series of Metallbilder (Metal Pictures) followed in 1972: acrylic paintings on steel or aluminium, usually groupings of four panels, fairly widely separated, each panel bearing a single main acrylic colour area bracketed by bands of one other colour at top and bottom. In December 1973 he moved his practice to New York City. (C26026, leg 1 C25913-C25918; leg 2 C25951; leg 3 C25961, C25963)

Together with Baselitz and Polke he was represented with metal pictures at the XIII Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1975. In 1976 he returned to Dusseldorf, where his metal picture series "To the People of New York City" was created: a 15-part work comprising 40 panels per Dia (Wikipedia says 39; Dia is followed, since Dia holds the work), painted in variations of cadmium red, cadmium yellow and black -- the colours of the West and East German flags. It was shown at the Heiner Friedrich Gallery, New York, in 1977, and at the Dia Art Foundation in 1987. In 1976 his work "Himmelsrichtungen" was shown at the Venice Biennale. After a stay in New York in the early 1970s, he moved into Gerhard Richter's former Dusseldorf studio; no source read in this run dates that move. (C26027, leg 1 C25919-C25922; leg 2 C25949)

Blinky Palermo died in 1977, aged 33, during a trip to the Maldives -- or aged 34, on the other date. Wikipedia gives 18 February 1977, aged 33, at Vihamanaafushi, Maldives; artnet gives 18 February 1977 at Kurumba, Maldives. Ketterer instead gives 17 February 1977, "at the age of 34", of a heart attack while on a holiday trip, and its page header wrongly says "1977 Sri Lanka", contradicting its own body text and every other source. The cause of death is itself split across sources: Ketterer says heart attack and Getty ULAN says heart failure on Kurumba Island, while Wikipedia calls the causes "mysterious" but "widely acknowledged as related to Palermo's drug use", and other summaries call the cause uncertain. Arithmetic favours 33: born 2 June 1943, died February 1977, he had not yet turned 34. Simple Wikipedia's "only 32 years old" is a third figure, almost certainly wrong, and is not used. This file prints the split and does not pick a side. His brother Michael Heisterkamp is the sole heir and owner of the copyright of Palermo's work. (C26028, leg 1 C25923-C25925)

Palermo took part in more than 70 exhibitions in his lifetime, including documenta in 1972 and 1977 and the 13th Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1975; Ketterer puts his whole oeuvre at "just 15 years" of consequent development. MoMA lists him as "Blinky Palermo, German, 1943-1977, 45 works online" and Tate lists him as "Blinky Palermo (Peter Heisterkamp) 1943-1977" with no biographical text on the artist page. Wikipedia names Kazimir Malevich, Barnett Newman and Ellsworth Kelly as painters who inspired him. Posthumously, Dia inaugurated its Chelsea exhibition space in 1987 with a major show of his final work "To the People of New York City" alongside works by Beuys and Knoebel, and in 2010-11 Dia presented "Blinky Palermo: Retrospective 1964-1977" at Dia:Beacon, the first retrospective of his work in the United States. (C26029, leg 1 C25926-C25929; leg 3 C25982)

## M1 -- The seam is the drawing

In the Stoffbilder, lengths of industrially dyed cotton are sewn together along horizontal or vertical seams and stretched on a frame, so the only line in the work is the stitch where two colour fields meet. There is no brushwork and no drawn edge; the joint between two ready-made cloths does the work a drawn line would do. Evidence: W12 Untitled Stoffbild 1970 (2 x 2 m, sewn horizontal seam, MoMA); W07 Untitled Stoffbild 1967-69 (dark blue below, turquoise above, seam the only drawing); W09 Blau/Grun 1968 (one long vertical joint, eye reads up the seam); W05 Landschaft 1966 (blue sky cloth fitted like a puzzle piece against a green land cloth, the horizon a real seam between two beam units). (C26030, leg 2 C25934, C25936, C25938, C25941; synthesis C25993)

The watchable behaviour is a flat field of two colours in which the eye keeps returning to the seam, reading it as a horizon, a fault line, or a fold. The seam never moves, but the two colour masses press against each other along it, so the picture reads as a held tension rather than a painted image.

**Takeaway:** let the joint be the line -- sew two ready-made fields together and the stitch is the whole drawing; never paint the line the seam already makes. (C26031)

## M2 -- Colour taken ready-made, stains and all

Palermo took colour and material quality ready-made from department-store fabrics and had them stitched together by others, mostly by his wife Ingrid Kohlhofer on a sewing machine. The fabric is used exactly as bought, stains and all; the standard manufactured width of the bolt sets the dimensions (most Stoffbilder are about 2 x 2 m because the cloth comes that wide). The artist chooses and joins; he does not mix or apply the colour. Evidence: W08 Rot-Rosa 1966-67 (earliest surviving Stoffbild; stains already present when sewn; the first series was destroyed in 1970-71 apart from this one work); W12 (MoMA text: from banal department-store material, the basic material of West Germany's economic miracle, simple declarations of goods as art); W09 (70 cm width set by the bolt, not by drawing). General claim: circa 65 Stoffbilder, late 1966 to 1972, per leg 2 -- though Wikipedia's own art-market section says "about 56", a conflict inside one article that is carried, not resolved. (C26032, leg 2 C25937, C25938, C25941, C25950; synthesis C25994)

The watchable behaviour is colour fields that look flat and industrial, with small accidents of the shop-bought material (stains, weave, slight unevenness) left visible, so the surface feels like goods rather than painting. The work declares itself as merchandise made into art.

**Takeaway:** buy the colour, do not make it -- take the bolt as it comes, stains included, and let the manufactured width set the size of the picture. (C26033)

## M3 -- The support is the shape

Palermo alters the shape of the conventional canvas so the support itself carries the composition. Summarily painted canvases are wrapped around sculptural stretchers; a totem is a tall narrow strip of wood with cross-pieces; a disk is a circle of cotton on a plywood core; a staff and a disk are two separate portable coloured things whose relation is set by hanging, not by a frame. Evidence: W02 Totem 1964-67 (219 cm tall, 27 cm wide, orange strip with five white cross-pieces carrying blue triangle portions); W03 Winkel Rot-Weiss 1965 (stretcher bars as sculptural vehicle, painting surface moved to the margins); W10 Blaue Scheibe und Stab 1968 (staff 251 cm, disk about 65 cm diameter, sign-like object); W13 Graue Scheibe 1970 (13.1 x 26.5 cm, support itself a disk, pocket scale). (C26034, leg 2 C25931, C25932, C25939, C25942; synthesis C25995)

The watchable behaviour is the painting leaving the rectangle and becoming an object on the wall or in the room. Edges do the compositional work: a red-white angle, a vertical strip interrupted at regular intervals, a flat blue circle beside a near-8-foot pole. The viewer reads the thing first and the colour second.

**Takeaway:** shape the support and the composition follows -- a strip, a disk, a staff are already pictures before any colour is put on them. (C26035)

## M4 -- The wall is the picture

From 1968 Palermo realised more than 20 murals and wall drawings at sites in Europe. Paint is applied directly to the wall, or a stencil is used to remake a motif on a wall elsewhere. The architecture's own frame becomes the composition: the mullions of a glass shopfront traced onto the opposite wall, a whole landing wall coated in flat orange anti-rust primer, a frieze of blue triangles run along a palace wall, a room defined by four billboard-sized panels at its corners. Evidence: W14 Fenster I 1970 Bremerhaven (shopfront mullions traced onto the wall); W15 Documenta 5 staircase wall 1972 (whole landing wall in orange rust-preventive undercoating); W11 Blaues Dreieck 1969 (stencil, brush, paint tube and instructions sold as an edition of 50, so the triangle can be remade elsewhere; Brussels frieze 1970 extends the motif); W19 Venice room 1976 (four panels at 45 degrees to steel girders, black, white, red, yellow). (C26036, leg 2 C25940, C25943, C25944, C25948, C25952; synthesis C25996)

The watchable behaviour is the visitor walking into the work instead of standing before it. A staircase becomes the viewing device; a window is redrawn in line on the wall opposite it; a room is defined by abstract coordinates of colour while keeping the roughness of physical space. The originals no longer exist, being bound to their place; only sketches and photographs record them.

**Takeaway:** paint the room, not a picture of the room -- when the wall is the support, the visitor's walk is the viewing, and the work dies with the building. (C26037)

## M5 -- Serial panels with wall gaps doing the rhythm

The Metal Pictures formula, from 1972 and in earnest from 1974 after the New York move: acrylic on steel or aluminium, usually in groupings of four panels, fairly widely separated, each panel bearing a single main acrylic colour area bracketed by bands of one other colour at top and bottom. The panels are minimally projected from the wall by hidden fasteners, so the colour lifts off the wall and hangs in space; the gaps between panels are resting spaces for the eye and repeat through the room. Evidence: W16 Times of the Day I 1974-75 (four panels, 56.8 x 520 cm overall, gaps repeat through the room); W17 Coney Island II 1975 (four narrow upright panels, fixed colour order, wall gaps doing the rhythm); W18 Osten-Westen II 1976 (two large steel panels, single main colour area bracketed top and bottom); W19 Himmelsrichtungen I 1976 (four small panels naming the cardinal directions). (C26038, leg 2 C25945-C25948, C25951; synthesis C25997)

The watchable behaviour is a row of flat colour signs with even breathing room between them, read left to right like a sentence or a measure of music. The wall between panels is part of the composition, not empty background.

**Takeaway:** hang the panels apart and the wall does the work -- the gap is a rest in the music, and without the rest there is no rhythm. (C26039)

## M6 -- Prescriptive hanging: the gap equals the width

In "To the People of New York City" (1976), Palermo's last work, the hanging itself is a rule. Each of the 15 sections has one to four rectilinear metal panels, with variable space between panels in a grouping, and the distance between panels must equal their respective width (Part VI is the only exception: two panels directly abut to form the illusion of a single panel). The work needs at least 275 feet of wall and is hung low, with rhythmically changing formats that Dia links to the syncopation of jazz. Evidence: W20 (15 parts, 40 panels per Dia, black / cadmium red / cadmium yellow throughout; preparatory felt-pen sketches on parchment record the arrangement as a codex). Panel count discrepancy carried: Dia 40 vs Wikipedia 39; Dia followed. (C26040, leg 2 C25949; synthesis C25998)

The watchable behaviour is the viewer walking a long run of didactic colour signs, decoding red-yellow-black progressions part by part. The spacing rule makes the walk itself the reading: wider panels mean wider pauses, narrow panels quicken the pace. The title comes from the dedication "To the people of N.Y.C." inscribed on the backs of the panels.

**Takeaway:** write the hanging as a rule and the walk becomes the reading -- gap equals width, and the viewer's pace is set by the panels, not by the wall. (C26041)

## M7 -- Chance called out loud

When Richter began his Colour Chart paintings in 1966, he had Palermo randomly call out colours, which Richter then adopted for the work; Palermo visited the studio and, without visual knowledge of the painting, arbitrarily called out sample-card names. The same chance logic governs the joint 1970 Munich Olympics submission: 27 colours, each appearing 50 times, distribution determined randomly. Chance is a spoken, social procedure, not a private dice throw. Precision carried: the Colour Charts call-out is the 1966 first series only; by 1971 Richter used mathematical procedures and random permutation without Palermo. Evidence: leg 3 (both Wikipedia pages, end to end, plus Sotheby's); W04 Flipper 1965 shows the related deadpan grid logic in Palermo's own work (see M8). (C26042, leg 3 C25961, C25963; leg 2 C25933; synthesis C25999)

The watchable behaviour is a grid or array in which colour placement looks arbitrary but is rule-bound: no colour repeats by design, no pattern is composed, and the eye finds its own groupings in a field the artist refused to arrange.

**Takeaway:** call the colours out loud and let chance do the composing -- a spoken procedure, witnessed, is stricter than a hidden one, because the artist cannot quietly improve the result. (C26043)

## M8 -- Found pattern, painted deadpan

Palermo lifts a commercial pattern and paints it flat, without expression. Flipper (1965) takes the red, white and blue grid from the side of the pinball machine at his local cafe; a 1970 MoMA screenprint diptych of the same name reproduces the composition in one panel and removes the blue grid in the other, leaving red squares floating unanchored on white ground. The colour is Pop-adjacent and deadpan, not expressive. The same clarity appears late: Frieze groups the metal pictures as geometrical abstractions where paint becomes surface coating, all depth denied, with the unambiguous clarity of traffic lights. Evidence: W04 Flipper 1965 (89 x 69.5 cm, oil on canvas; MoMA print read end to end); W18 Osten-Westen II 1976 (traffic-light clarity, Frieze); W01 Komposition mit acht roten Rechtecken 1964 (eight equalised red rectangles, reworking Malevich's 1915 "8 Red Rectangles" by removing the diagonal launch, so the group reads chastened and held down). (C26044, leg 2 C25930, C25933, C25947; synthesis C26000)

The watchable behaviour is a grid or sign that holds together only by its pattern, and then, when one element is removed, falls apart into floating fragments. The viewer watches the pattern do the holding, not the painter.

**Takeaway:** take the pattern as found and paint it without comment -- the grid holds the picture together, and removing one element shows that the pattern, not the painter, was doing the work. (C26045)

## M9 -- Sequence as a day: light to dark

"Times of the Day" I-VI (1974-76) is six four-part works in which four thin aluminium panels hung in a row move from light to dark, a day's progression from sunrise to noon to sunset to dusk. Each panel carries contrasting pairs of horizontal bands in acrylic; read left to right the sequence is a day. The smooth metal lets the brush leave slight striations, the only facture in an otherwise flat sign-like surface. Evidence: W16 Times of the Day I 1974-75 (Dia handout read end to end, 170 lines; parts I and II Dia-owned, III-VI private); W17 Coney Island II 1975 (same layout recounted in only three colours, per the contemporary blog account of the retrospective). (C26046, leg 2 C25945, C25946; synthesis C26001)

The watchable behaviour is a slow, legible darkening across four panels, like a time-lapse the viewer reads by walking. The same four-part, same-layout discipline repeats across the six groups with different colour counts, so the series is a system for recounting a day, not six separate pictures.

**Takeaway:** fix the format and let the light do the varying -- four panels, one day, and the sequence is the picture, not the panels. (C26047)

## M10 -- Three colours as a code

The late palette of black, cadmium red and cadmium yellow, held constant across a whole long work while the patterns change. The three colours are the colours of the German flag, and also readable as postwar abstract painting or as Native American visual culture, per Dia. Restricting the palette to three coded colours turns every panel into a sign in a shared language rather than a colour study. Evidence: W20 To the People of New York City 1976 (black / cadmium red / cadmium yellow throughout, 15 parts); W19 Himmelsrichtungen I 1976 and the Venice room (black, white, red, yellow as cardinal coordinates); W18 Osten-Westen II 1976 (the title names the two halves of the divided world Palermo had crossed). (C26048, leg 2 C25947-C25949; synthesis C26002)

The watchable behaviour is a long run of panels in which the viewer stops looking at colour as decoration and starts decoding progressions, like reading flags or signals. The sameness of the palette is what makes the changing arrangements legible.

**Takeaway:** fix three colours and the arrangements become readable -- the code is the palette, and every panel is a word in it. (C26049)

## The aesthetic logic: the picture leaves the rectangle, then the wall, then becomes a rule

Palermo's whole career is one exit performed three ways: first the picture leaves the rectangle, then it leaves the wall, then it stops being a picture at all and becomes a rule-governed arrangement of coloured things. The early works (W01, W02, W03, W04) are still on canvas but already breaking the rectangle: eight red rectangles held down instead of flying, a totem strip, an angle of red and white, a pinball grid lifted flat from a cafe machine. The Stoffbilder (W05, W07, W08, W09, W12) take the next exit: the colour is bought, not mixed, the seam is the drawing, and the bolt decides the size. The middle works (W10, W11, W13, W14, W15) leave the wall as a rectangle altogether: a disk and a staff as two portable things, a triangle that is a stencil procedure, a window traced in line on the opposite wall, a whole staircase landing coated in orange primer. The late works (W16, W17, W18, W19, W20) replace the picture with a system: fixed formats, fixed palettes, rules for spacing, and the viewer walking a sequence. (C26050, leg 2 W01-W20; synthesis C26003)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the material is ready-made: colour and support are taken as found -- department-store cloth, a bolt width, a steel panel, a hardware-store primer -- never mixed, never modelled, never expressive. A description of the materials is already a description of the meaning, and the stains are kept because the goods are the point. Second, the procedure is the picture: the seam, the stencil, the call-out, the spacing rule -- each work is a procedure carried out, and the visible result is the record of it. Third, the viewer walks the work: the wall paintings are entered, the serial panels are read left to right, the fifteen-part code is decoded by walking 275 feet of wall. That is why Palermo is the Minimalist pole inside the Dusseldorf cohort and the opposite pole of Beuys even though Beuys was his teacher: Beuys loads a material into society until the society itself is the sculpture, while Palermo strips the picture down until only a procedure and a rule remain, and then asks the viewer to walk through what is left. (C26051, leg 1 C25910, C25911, C25917; leg 2 C25950-C25952; synthesis C26004)

The evolution is the proof. 1964-67: the exits begin (W01, W02, W03, W04, W05, W08) -- the rectangle is chastened, shaped, angled, gridded, seamed, and the cloth pictures start. 1968-72: the picture is now either a sewn cloth, a wall, or a portable object (W09, W10, W11, W12, W13, W14, W15) -- the rectangle is gone, and the stencil makes the image a procedure that can be remade anywhere. 1973-76: New York, then the Metal Pictures in earnest (W16, W17, W18, W19, W20) -- serial four-panel groupings, the day-sequence, the three-colour code, and the prescriptive hanging of the last work. The late work does not change the procedure; it widens the field it is applied to, from one seam in one cloth to fifteen parts along a whole wall, and the answer in every case is the same -- take it ready-made, join it by rule, hang it by rule, and let the viewer walk it. (C26052, leg 1 C25910-C25922; leg 2 W01-W20; synthesis C26003)
