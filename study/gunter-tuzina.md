Tuzina was born in 1951 in Hamburg, and before art school he trained as an engraver: a 1967-1970 apprenticeship in steel and copper engraving in Hamburg. From 1971 he studied at the Hochschule fuer bildende Kuenste Hamburg, in his final years with Franz-Erhard Walther, until 1977. The inquiry that defines the whole oeuvre began in the early 1970s -- an inquiry into the essence of painting, "a process he continues to this day" -- and from 1975 he worked on the basic principles of painting: lines, squares, rectangles, areas of colour within a relatively set framework. His first exhibitions came in 1976, with wall works and drawings at Galerie Konrad Fischer, Duesseldorf; in 1977 he showed at the Kunstraum Muenchen, invited by Hermann Kern, who had seen an early wall work in Walther's class, and at the Arbeitsgalerie 'Vor Ort' in Hamburg. In 1979 he was invited to "Europa 79" in Stuttgart. Nothing was found about his family, his childhood in Hamburg, or his schooling before the engraving apprenticeship. [C27271]

The museum spine is Dutch-centred and early. In 1978 he had his first solo museum exhibition at the Van Abbemuseum, Eindhoven; in 1983 a solo exhibition at the Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam -- the GalleryViewer CV lists the Stedelijk show under 1984, and this study writes 1983, two narrative sources plus German Wikipedia, with the CV's 1984 carried as the conflicting figure. In 1981 he won the Villa-Romana-Preis in Florence, awarded by the Deutscher Kuenstlerbund as a one-year residence, shared that year with Frank Dornseif, Bruno Erdmann and Dieter Kraemer. In 1985 the Gemeentemuseum Den Haag (now Kunstmuseum Den Haag) gave him a retrospective; in 1987, after it, he painted a mural in a museum stairwell there, which was lost during the building's restoration, and he painted a new one for the 2002 exhibition. Then Kunsthalle Zuerich in 1990, the Museum Fridericianum in Kassel and the Koelnischer Kunstverein in 1991, the Haags Gemeentemuseum in 1992, the Lenbachhaus in Munich in 1993, and the second Den Haag overview, "Between Yellow and Blue" (Tussen geel en blauw), 1 June - 15 September 2002. In 2011 the Kunstmuseum Den Haag built a Projects Gallery show, 20 August - 13 November, around the recently acquired floor relief Zwart, blauw, groen (1991), his largest floor relief. In New York he appeared in two MoMA group shows, "Drawings of the Eighties from the Collection, Part I", 9 November 1989 - 13 February 1990, and "Beuys and After: Contemporary German Drawings from the Collection", 1 February - 14 May 1996; MoMA holds six of his works online. [C27272]

His galleries tell the same two-city story as his residences. He was represented by Galerie Fred Jahn, Munich, 1987-2001, and has worked with Slewe Gallery, Amsterdam, since 2000 -- a 25-year collaboration celebrated with the show "Fruehschnee", 1 March - 5 April 2025, whose title work is the large 2002 painting of the same name. Recent Slewe solos run 2000, 2005, 2009, 2011, 2013 ("Das andere Quadrat und Tondo"), 2016 ("Duo"), 2018 ("New Works"), 2020 ("Und mit Rot"), 2022 ("Darkness and Color"), 2025. His artist books are Uebermalungen 1987 (Verlag Fred Jahn, Munich, 1989) and Uebermalungen 2004-2007 (Salon-Verlag, Cologne, 2013), and the comprehensive catalogue Welt appeared in 2020 (Slewe / Salon Verlag, texts by Rudi Fuchs and Ulli Seegers). Works are held in the MoMA, the Van Abbemuseum, the Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen, the Kunstmuseum Den Haag -- the largest institutional collection of his work in the world -- and the Stedelijk, and in Germany by the Kupferstichkabinett Berlin, the Lenbachhaus and Museum Brandhorst in Munich, the Deutsche Bank collection, the Staatliche Graphische Sammlung in Munich and the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart, with the ERSTE collection in Vienna and the Societe Generale collection in Paris besides. Only one prize was found in any source: the Villa Romana 1981. No other prize, grant or honour appears in the sources read; do not invent further prizes. [C27273]

The positioning is double, and the sources name both halves: his work is assigned to concrete art in the De Stijl tradition and to overpainting (Uebermalung) after Arnulf Rainer. The Kunstmuseum Den Haag places the oeuvre in the abstract tradition from Mondrian and De Stijl culminating in Minimal Art, but recombining colour and line so that "the human scale and the personal touch return". Tuzina states the method himself, in a 2011 interview: "Looking at the painting, one should be able to trace every step, every gesture. I am always interested in providing a density, a kind of high-pressure... like an energy oven or a high-pressure cooker, made visible in this form of painting." And in the Fruehschnee text: "It is important to me that one sees the traces from how something is made." His rectangular paintings are often described as windows; he disagrees -- they are fields where colour and line are brought into balance, or carefully nudged out of it. [C27274]

The evolution is a vocabulary that never changes and a balance inside it that keeps shifting. Early (1978-1980, W01-W02): line first, on the wall and on tall narrow supports -- a 125 x 49 cm vertical canvas in 1978, and in 1980 an oil on transparent paper cut into a geometric shape, the support itself shaped, not just the image. Early 1980s (W03-W07): scale jumps and the support gets rougher -- Bild III (Form mit Tuerkisch-Grab), 1983/85-86, oil on canvas, 215 x 176 cm, worked over three years and tied to Istanbul; oil and wax crayon on canvas and on coarse burlap (Bagno Maria 2, 1984; Santo Spirito, mid-1980s; Triest, 1990/91, 170 x 150 cm); titles naming cities and people (Istanbul, Trieste, Albert Ayler, Santo Spirito). Late 1980s (W10-W13): the work leaves the wall -- the 1987 stairwell mural, the 1989 New York wall drawing, the first floor reliefs, and the Uebermalungen 1987 book, the print as a base to be overpainted by hand. 1990s (W16-W19): the dark triad settles -- Zwart, blauw, groen, the 1991 floor relief; Zoom (Gelb-Gruen-Blau-Selbst I et II), 1992, mixed media, 90.5 x 69.5 cm each, the mature image fixed; words enter the picture, "Blau" written into the 1996 lithograph BG. 2; arcs and transparency appear beside the right angle in the 1998 Ohne Titel. 2000s: the Kunstmuseum's 2002 text states it directly -- "The linearity of his earlier work has to some extent given way to warm shades and areas of colour" -- Den Haag works on hand-coloured yellow paper, the Uebermalungen leftovers repainted 2004-07, small square formats in 2008/09. 2010s-2020s: the other square, the tondo (2013 show; a 40 cm tondo in 2017), "Duo" in 2016 with a live music performance by John Snijders at the opening, and the return -- Fruehschnee 2025 re-reads old lithographs as the basis for new work and reinterprets Friedrich. What does not change across the fifty years is the restricted palette (dark blue, red, green and black, with bright accents), the rectangle as the basic unit, the hand-drawn line that refuses machine straightness, the small-to-medium scale of the paintings, and the habit of working a motif across years and media. [C27275]

## M1 -- The imperfect hand-drawn line

The line is drawn by hand and stays visibly hand-drawn: never perfectly sharp or straight. Slewe's 2011 press release states the contrast with Mondrian directly -- "Like Mondrian, Tuzina constantly seeks the limits of human precision. But where Mondrian endeavoured to produce horizontal and vertical lines with machinelike exactitude, Tuzina plays with the imperfection of the hand-drawn line, never perfectly sharp or straight." The small deviations are what give the work "emotional significance" and a "romantic expression", and the GalleryViewer article of 2025 adds that the lines "are rarely perfectly straight. These small deviations lend the work a tactile charge." The watchable behaviour is the waver caught in passing: the eye follows a line expecting machine straightness and finds the hand in it -- the one place Mondrian hid it. The line is the evidence that a person, not a ruler, was here.

**Takeaway:** draw the line by hand and let the waver show; the deviation is the feeling. [C27276]

## M2 -- The window-like rectangle that is not a window

Rectangular paintings in dark blue, red and green resemble windows -- but Tuzina disagrees. The GalleryViewer article of 2025: "Rather than offering a view, they are fields where colour and line are brought into balance -- or carefully nudged out of it. ... These are not openings to the world beyond, but quiet surfaces that invite you to pause and rest your gaze." The rectangle is a field for balancing colour against line, not a frame for looking through. W01, Untitled, 1978, mixed media on canvas, 125 x 49 cm, already shows the upright strip format; W17, Zoom, 1992, fixes the mature image. The watchable behaviour is the gaze that stops: the viewer approaches expecting a view through, and finds a surface that holds the gaze instead of passing it on.

**Takeaway:** make the rectangle a field, not a window; balance, don't depict. [C27277]

## M3 -- The smaller, deformed inner frame + one diagonal

Inside the canvas sits a second rectangle -- smaller than the canvas, sometimes deformed -- crossed by a single oblique line, "the only distraction for the eye that is otherwise captured by the bold layers of colour" (Societe Generale, on W17, Zoom (Gelb-Gruen-Blau-Selbst I et II), 1992, mixed media, 90.5 x 69.5 cm each, a pair). The inner frame organises the field; the diagonal breaks it once, and only once. W18, the lithograph BG. 2, 1996, colour lithograph with wax crayons on vellum, sheet 42 x 29.6 cm, shows the same device in print: black lines structuring the sheet into squares, with drawings set into two of the fields. W19, Ohne Titel, 1998, oil and pencil on paper, 35 x 28 cm, puts two differently transparent arcs inside right-angled strictness around a deep-dark, slightly transparent core. The watchable behaviour is the single break, counted once: the eye is captured by the colour field, then caught by the diagonal, and the count stops at one.

**Takeaway:** frame the field once inside itself, then break it exactly once. [C27278]

## M4 -- Blackboard palette: dark tones that provide no light

The colours are dark blue, red and green, often mixed to near-black; "the mixed tones of the palette provide no light. The works presented here use the colour of school blackboards" (Societe Generale, on Zoom). Titles name the palette as the work itself: W07, Gruen Blau Schwarz, 1985, acrylic and chalk on canvas, 120 x 75 cm; W16, Zwart, blauw, groen, 1991, the floor relief; the 2020 show "Und mit Rot"; the 2022 show "Darkness and Color". Bright accents appear only as counterweights inside the dark field. The watchable behaviour is absorption: the dark field takes the room's light instead of reflecting it, so the one bright accent inside it reads louder than its size.

**Takeaway:** keep the field dark enough to give no light; let one accent do the talking. [C27279]

## M5 -- Overpainting: the visible trace of making

Lines are "drawn, painted over and traced again" (GalleryViewer, 2025). The mechanism is literal in the Uebermalungen: a serigraph or lithograph is printed, then painted over by hand in oil, chalk or acrylic, so the finished work contains its own earlier state. W12, Uebermalung mit Rot, 1989, is the purest case: acrylic and chalk on a colour serigraph, sheet 31 x 27 cm -- the print underneath supplies the geometry, and the hand-painted red layer partially covers, corrects and re-balances it. W09, Untitled, 1986-87, screenprint and pencil on paper, edition of 160, runs the print to the very edge of the sheet with pencil drawing added over it. The leftover prints of the 1989 book were repainted again in 2004-07, 39 unique copies. The watchable behaviour is the earlier state showing through: the viewer reads the print where the hand changed its mind, and traces every step, every gesture, exactly as Tuzina asks.

**Takeaway:** print the geometry, then paint over it by hand; let the earlier state stay visible. [C27280]

## M6 -- Painting off the wall: wall drawing, mural, floor relief, slanted sculpture

The work leaves the canvas for the room. The early practice was drawing and painting directly on walls. W10 is the 1987 stairwell mural at the Gemeentemuseum Den Haag, lost during the building's restoration and repainted for the 2002 exhibition -- painting made directly on architecture, destroyed by renovation, then remade. W11 is the wall drawing at David Nolan Gallery, New York, 1989 ("Wall Drawing and Works on Paper"): the line drawn at architectural scale on the gallery wall, then presumably painted over after the show. W13, "Form-Grund" no. 2, 1989/90, is a floor piece, sold as such, its title naming the figure-ground relation itself. W16, Zwart, blauw, groen, 1991, is his largest floor relief, the centrepiece of the 2011 Den Haag Projects Gallery show -- a relief that lies on the floor and is walked around, not hung and faced. Even the sculptures keep the hand in: "the edges of his sculptures ... are slanted, contrasting sharply with the perfectly straight angles of industrially produced objects" (Kunstmuseum, 2011). The watchable behaviour is the same vocabulary under different gravity: the viewer walks around a painting instead of facing it.

**Takeaway:** take the painting off the wall; let the floor and the room carry it. [C27281]

## M7 -- Reinterpretation and repetition as method

Old work is the raw material for new work. The 2025 Fruehschnee show hangs old lithographs that form a basis for new work; the 2004-07 Uebermalungen repaint the leftover prints of the 1989 book; W20, Fruehschnee, 2002, a large painting, reinterprets Caspar David Friedrich's painting of the same title -- "revising and reinterpreting Friedrich's romantic, idealistic spirit" -- early snow and landscape feeling inside an abstract rectangle. Nothing is finished once; each work is "part of a continuous exercise in looking, shifting and composing" (GalleryViewer, 2025). The watchable behaviour is recognition across time: the same motif returns in a new medium or a new decade, and the viewer recognises it changed.

**Takeaway:** never finish a motif; re-read it in a new medium. [C27282]

## M8 -- Composition between paintings, not inside one

"According to Klein the composition is not made in the painting it self but occurs in the space, within the relation of other paintings" (Slewe, 2009 press release; the source gives no first name or role for Klein, and none is supplied here). The hang is part of the work: small paintings relate to each other across the wall, and the balance happens between them. This is why the paintings stay small and numerous, and why surveys -- "Darkness and Color", 2022, paintings 1985-2022; "Fruehschnee", 2025 -- read as one composition. The watchable behaviour is the step back: the viewer balances the wall, not the canvas, weighing one small rectangle against the next.

**Takeaway:** compose the hang, not the canvas; the balance lives between paintings. [C27283]

## M9 -- The other square and the tondo: breaking the rectangle

The rectangle is the rule, so the exceptions matter. The 2013 Slewe show is titled "Das andere Quadrat und Tondo" ("The Other Square and Tondo"), and "especially for this exhibition he made also some small tondos, round paintings". The 2017 tondo, 40 cm diameter, acrylic, oil and crayon on wood, puts the same dark field and line vocabulary inside a circle -- no corners for the lines to meet, so the geometry has to resolve differently. Small squares settle in 2008/09, 30 x 30 cm on board and 50 x 40 cm on canvas. The watchable behaviour is the missing corner: the eye looks for the corner that is not there, and the line has to find another way to stop.

**Takeaway:** break the rectangle once, deliberately; the exception proves the rule. [C27284]

## M10 -- Naming: titles and written words as part of the image

Titles do descriptive work: they name colours (W07, W16), places (W03 Istanbul, W15 Triest, W06 Santo Spirito, the Den Haag series), people (W05, Albert Ayler "3", 1984, gouache and pencil on checked paper -- the checked paper itself a ready-made grid the drawing plays against), processes (W12 Uebermalung, W14 Bezeichnen, W13 Form-Grund). And the word enters the picture itself: in W18, the 1996 lithograph BG. 2, the word "Blau" is written several times into the lower-right field. Colour is named as well as shown; the title is a label on the work the way the handwritten label sits "on the central crossing" of W13. The watchable behaviour is the check: the viewer reads the word and then looks for the colour it names -- the label and the field check each other.

**Takeaway:** name the colour in the title or in the picture; let the word and the field check each other. [C27285]

## Aesthetic logic

Tuzina's logic is Mondrian corrected by the hand. The rectangle, the right angle and the restricted palette are all taken from De Stijl and minimalism, and then each is given back its human error: the line wavers, the inner frame deforms, the colour gives no light, the print is painted over. Where Mondrian hid the maker, Tuzina leaves the traces in -- every step, every gesture, the energy of making visible like a pressure cooker. The work is small on purpose: the paintings stay hand-scale so the composition can happen between them on the wall, and the big moves -- the mural, the floor relief -- are the same small vocabulary under different gravity. Against Kiecol's concrete houses, Tuzina's answer is the field itself: no building, no object, just the rectangle balanced against its own line, nudged out of balance once, and left there to hold the gaze. [C27286]
