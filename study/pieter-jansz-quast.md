# Pieter Jansz. Quast: Study Notes

**Site:** Pieter Jansz. Quast (bapt. 17 April 1605, Amsterdam - buried 29 May 1647, Amsterdam), Dutch Golden Age painter of satirical genre scenes, guardroom scenes, merry companies, peasants and beggars; the guardroom's jester -- the man who took the same guardroom van Velsen talked in and turned it into satire, caricature and the Dutch Callot.

D0 "Hop from the van Velsen study" -- HOLDS in substance, HOLDS in the letter. Van Velsen is #570; Quast is the guardroom's jester off him, and the chain is real: van Velsen influenced Duyster, Quast competed with Duyster and Codde in the same guardroom and merry-company idiom, and Dirck Hals's manner links van Velsen's conversation pieces to Quast's merry companies (leg 3).

D1 "while van Velsen kept Delft's soldiers talking in polite conversation" -- HOLDS in substance, FAILS in the letter. Van Velsen is known for conversation pieces "in the manner of Pieter Codde and Dirck Hals" plus military pieces, but the two are separate categories in the sources: his conversation pieces are mostly civilian and musical gatherings (*A Musical Party*, *The Fortune Teller*, both 1631), while his military pieces are a distinct group. The doorway as proposed compresses the two and implies his soldiers were themselves the polite conversationalists. The study uses "kept Delft's companies talking in polite conversation" (leg 3).

D2 "Quast took the same guardroom to Amsterdam and The Hague" -- HOLDS in substance, FAILS in the letter. Quast did paint guardroom scenes with officers and did work in both cities -- born in Amsterdam, The Hague 1634 to about 1641, Amsterdam again to his death -- but the guardroom genre was already Amsterdam-born, "first depicted by Pieter Codde and others in Amsterdam" after the Twelve Years' Truce ended in 1621. Quast did not carry it to Amsterdam from Delft; he worked it in both cities. The study uses "worked the same guardroom in Amsterdam and The Hague" (leg 3).

D3 "Quast turned it into satire, caricature and the Dutch Callot" -- HOLDS in substance, HOLDS in the letter. "His style is characterized by humor, satire and caricature" and "Quast was influenced by the French engraver Jacques Callot and even called the Dutch Callot" (EN Wikipedia). Fully sound (leg 3).

On "the guardroom's jester": no source documents a specific jester figure in a Quast guardroom painting. What is documented is his theatre and commedia types (Callot's *Balli di Sfessania*), his fool and folly subjects (*Extraction of the Stone of Folly*, c. 1630; *The Triumph of Folly*, Mauritshuis), and his general role as the genre's satirical fool. The label is kept as a nickname for Quast himself -- the jester *of* the guardroom -- not as a work title (leg 3).

## The life

Pieter Jansz. Quast was baptised in Amsterdam on 17 April 1605 and buried there on 29 May 1647, both in the Nieuwe Kerk (EN Wikipedia, citing the Amsterdam baptismal register, DTB Dopen, archief 5001, inv. 39, p. 64). The birth year is hedged everywhere good: the baptismal register gives 17 April 1605, but he gave his age as 26 at his marriage banns on 29 June 1632, which puts his birth between mid-1605 and mid-1606, and most museum and vendor sources hedge "1605/06" or "c. 1605/1606". The study uses "baptised 17 April 1605" with the hedge noted. His parents are a single-source claim: EN Wikipedia, citing Ecartico, names Jan Sijmensz, a barge master, and Sybrich Gerritsdr, both from Emden, married 1603; White Rose Fine Art, citing Stanton-Hirst 1982 and Liedtke 2007, says flatly "Nothing is known of his parents or his life before his marriage". The study carries the Ecartico names as unverified. He grew up near the Zeedijk in Amsterdam (EN Wikipedia, Ecartico).

On 29 June 1632 the banns were published at Sloten near Amsterdam, and on 19 December 1632 he married Annetje Splinters (also spelled Splinter), a flower painter from The Hague. She sued him for breaking his marriage vows and forced him to marry her half a year later (EN Wikipedia, citing A. D. de Vries, *Oud-Holland* 3 (1885), p. 309; corroborated by White Rose and Onno van Seggelen). In 1634 he was admitted to the Guild of Saint Luke in The Hague, where he lived until about 1641 (RKD-derived dating, EN Wikipedia) -- Onno van Seggelen says nine years in The Hague and guild membership 1634-1643, a minor conflict the study carries with the RKD-derived form preferred. He was drawing master to the young Prince William II of Orange (1626-1650) according to White Rose and Sotheby's; Nice Art Gallery hedges that "concrete proof remains elusive", and the study treats the tutorship as likely but not proven. The documented 1639 link is his collaboration with Jan Stampioen, mathematics teacher of William II, who was also painted by Quast (EN Wikipedia, citing the 1639 algebra-book frontispiece). Late in 1639 he bought a house on the Paviljoensgracht in The Hague and transferred it to his wife's name early in 1644, possibly to avoid creditors (EN Wikipedia, citing Huiskamp, *Digitaal Vrouwenlexicon*).

The financial decline is the life's second act. He was constantly moving to poorer quarters; there were court cases over debts to a shopkeeper (1640), an innkeeper (1641) and a carpenter (1642); bankruptcy; illegal dice-games (EN Wikipedia, citing Gosselink et al. 2008 and M. J. Bok; White Rose). On 1 June 1643 Annetje entertained a Utrecht clergyman in their house -- the rent collector -- and after she accused him of visiting prostitutes ("Wel, mynheer, ick looff wel dat gy der wel hondert getast en gevoelt hebt..."), he slashed her face with a smashed Roemer glass; Quast rushed in and held the pastor at knifepoint. The attacker is named "Gaspar Roebergen" by White Rose and "Caspar Ruybergen" by Onno van Seggelen -- a spelling variant, carried as such. Sotheby's independently reports the knife-to-the-throat episode from "a Hague notary's report". In February 1644 two men entered the house, damaged a portrait, smeared it with paint and scratched it with knives (White Rose). In 1647 he lived on the Nes in Amsterdam in a house "shabby and uninhabitable due to leaks" per a statement dated 17 May 1647; he refused to pay rent and died two weeks later (EN Wikipedia, citing Roeders 2004). Whether he died in poverty is explicitly questioned in the literature: the old (2016) Wikipedia revision said flatly "He died in poverty, like many artists of the period"; the current article says "It is questionable he died in poverty", citing Leistra and Grove (2003). The evidence cuts both ways -- rent debts, bankruptcy and a leaking house against a house bought in 1639, property transferred to his wife, and a widow able to build a house in The Hague after his death. The study presents the poverty claim as contested, not as fact. Annetje returned to The Hague with their two daughters, had a house built on the Paviljoensgracht which she rented out, remarried the Leiden painter Jacob van Spreeuwen in 1650, and died in 1659 at the Lindengracht in the Jordaan (EN Wikipedia).

## M1. The Dutch Callot -- caricature as commentary

The mechanism the Grove names is caricature with a foreign engine. Quast "was influenced by the French engraver Jacques Callot and even called the Dutch Callot. With his caricatured images on often small panels, Quast provided commentary on the Golden Age" (EN Wikipedia, citing Stanton-Hirst 1982). The link runs through Callot's *Balli di Sfessania* (1621): the figures in Quast's *Merry company / Allegory of seduction* drawing are "directly inspired by Callot's *Balli di Sfessania*" (Onno van Seggelen), and commedia dell'arte was Callot's main source. Quast's live models were the travelling actors -- Hanswurst, Pickleherring -- and the Hague fairs in May and September (Onno van Seggelen, citing Stanton-Hirst). The caricature is not decoration on a genre scene; it is the scene's argument. His subjects are "mostly producing small social genre paintings with humorous, cartoon-like features, ranging from elegant merry companies to guardroom scenes with officers and (most numerous) groups of peasants or beggars" (EN Wikipedia, Grove). The cartoon-like feature -- the exaggerated face, the stooped back, the wooden leg -- is how the picture tells the viewer what it thinks of its sitters. The guardroom's jester is Quast himself: the one man in the chain who stands inside the soldiers' room and laughs at it, where Codde and Duyster recorded it and van Velsen talked in it.

**Takeaway:** build the picture from a caricatured type taken straight off the comic stage -- a commedia figure with an exaggerated face and posture -- and let the exaggeration do the judging. The watchable behaviour is the type holding its pose while the room goes on around it: the buffoon never stops being a buffoon, and the picture's opinion of him never changes.

## M2. Warm brown, strong local colour

The Grove's technical characterisation, quoted in EN Wikipedia (Leistra 2003), is the study's palette rule: Quast's paintings "are heavily and powerfully rendered in warm shades of brown, set off by strong local colouring in the principal figures. His successful peasant scenes are characterized by the use of strong chiaroscuro and a gentle, harmonious palette." The brown is the ground of the picture -- warm, heavy, powerful -- and the local colour is the interruption: a red coat, a yellow shoe, a pink face, placed exactly where the eye must land. The chiaroscuro is strong but the palette stays gentle and harmonious; the brown holds the picture together so the bright notes can be loud without the picture becoming loud. Oil on small wood panels is the dominant painting format (for example *A Party of Merrymakers* 37.5 x 49.5 cm; *Soldiers Gambling with Dice* 36 x 47 cm; *The Foot Operation* 30 x 41 cm). No palette change can be demonstrated across the career: every source describes the same warm-brown tonality, strong chiaroscuro and vivid local colour throughout, and no dated pair of works shows a before and after (leg 2). The brown is there at 23 and at 41.

**Takeaway:** build the room from one warm brown field, heavily rendered, and put the colour only on the principal figures as strong local notes. The watchable behaviour is the brown breathing -- the chiaroscuro shifting slowly while the local colours stay fixed and loud -- so the picture's argument is always where the colour is.

## M3. The guardroom as satire

Quast's guardroom scenes -- *kortegaardjes* -- are soldiers off duty gambling, drinking and smoking, and his version of the room is the satirical one. The Sciences Po seminar (Leonard Pouy, "Painters at War") reads his *Guardroom with officers examining the catch of a looting* as breaking the genre's dichotomy: it places "the soldier back within a discussion over the evaluation of the pillaged good... the officer ennobled himself by taking on the new role of expert, the artist displayed his talent by representing various textures and materials". The mechanism is the loot as a discussion piece: the soldiers are not resting, they are appraising, and the appraisal is the joke -- the officer as connoisseur of stolen goods. His other guardroom pictures keep the same repertory: *Soldiers Gambling with Dice* 1643 (36 x 47 cm, oil on panel, private collection), *Soldiers at an Inn* 1640 (Karlsruhe), *A Soldier and a Laughing Girl in an Interior* (ex-John Wayne collection, given to LACMA 1954) -- a soldier and a young woman facing each other over the final card hand, she hiding a card behind her back and looking out at the viewer, a scoreboard on her lap and a lute at his side. The laughing girl looking out is the mechanism's hinge: the cheat is shown to us, not to the soldier. Quast competed with Pieter Codde and Willem Duyster, "who had a similar style" (EN Wikipedia), in the same genre -- but where Codde and Duyster painted the guardroom straight, Quast painted it as a joke about itself.

**Takeaway:** build a guardroom where the soldiers are appraising something -- dice, cards, loot -- and let one figure in on the joke with the viewer. The watchable behaviour is the appraisal: hands turning the objects over, the loot changing hands, the officer's expertise growing more solemn as the goods get more worthless, and one face turned out to us while the soldiers stay fooled.

## M4. The beggar series -- 'T is al verward-gaeren

The most sustained satirical project of Quast's career is a print series, not a painting: *'T is al verward-gaeren* ("It's all a confused yarn" / "It's all a riddle"), 1634-38, about 26 plates of beggars and peasants, first published as a printed book in 1638. Single beggars and peasant groups: *Beggar with a Bowl* (plate 10, 204 x 160 mm), *Beggars and Peasants Around a Table* (plate 26, one with a wooden leg, 213 x 163 mm), *Tramp with a Sling*, *Marching Beggar Woman with a Basket*, *Lame Beggar Asking for Alms* (Art Institute of Chicago holds three plates; Rijksmuseum prints RP-P-OB-16.366 and others). The series "seems intended to draw attention to the plight of the poor and the social disorder they represented" (Nice Art Gallery -- a weak source, carried with the flag). The mechanism is the single figure as a type: one beggar, one plate, one deformity or prop -- a bowl, a sling, a wooden leg, a basket -- and the type is the whole picture. The plates are small (about 20 x 16 cm), the figures are large in them, and the caricature is the compassion: the exaggeration makes the beggar impossible to look past. The series is firmly Hague (1634-38), the one part of the oeuvre with a secure city and date, and it is the middle of the career holding the satire at its most concentrated. The title-page was reprinted posthumously in 1652 (Met 64.584.9, 19.9 x 16.5 cm).

**Takeaway:** build a series of single figures, one per small plate, each defined by one prop -- a bowl, a sling, a wooden leg, a basket -- and let the figure fill the frame. The watchable behaviour is the procession: one beggar arriving, holding still long enough to be read, and the next taking their place, the yarn staying confused and the figures staying distinct.

## M5. The quack doctor and the stone of folly

The folly subjects are Quast's other satirical strand, and they are present from the start. *Extraction of the Stone of Folly* (also called *Cutting the Stone*), c. 1630, oil on copper, Kunstmuseum St. Gallen, shows a quack "surgeon" cutting the stone of folly from a seated man's head before onlookers -- 19.5 cm high, width not found. *The Foot Operation* (*De voetoperatie*), Rijksmuseum, oil on panel, 30 x 41 cm, shows a barber-surgeon treating a seated man's foot, with a skull on an open book (memento mori) and a woman seated on the floor. *The Triumph of Folly* (Mauritshuis 447) and *The Card Players* (Mauritshuis 658) carry the same strand into the collections. The mechanism is the operation as theatre: a fake cure performed on a willing patient before an audience that knows it is fake and watches anyway. The skull on the book is the picture telling the truth inside the lie -- the operation cures nothing, the folly is the patient, and the onlookers are us. The quack-doctor subjects recur at the edge of the oeuvre in listings the legs could not verify (*A Quack Doctor on a Market Square*, known only from the Nice Art Gallery essay; *A Quack Doctor and Patient in an Interior*, reproduction-site listing only), which is itself evidence of how strongly the motif is associated with his name. The earliest dated works already carry it: the satire is there at 23, not learned later.

**Takeaway:** build an operation -- a stone cut from a head, a foot treated, a cure performed -- with the patient willing, the operator solemn, and the audience watching. The watchable behaviour is the operation proceeding in slow stages while nothing is cured: the knife moving, the onlookers leaning in, the skull on the book staying visible, and the folly remaining exactly where it was.

## M6. The finished drawing on parchment

Quast was a prolific draughtsman, and the finished drawing was a sale object in its own right, not a study for a painting. "He produced finished drawings for sale, often on parchment, and these included landscapes, cityscapes and biblical scenes. Some of his works were engraved, likely by Salomon Savery" (EN Wikipedia, Grove). White Rose: "a prolific draughtsman and made many sheets as finished works of art for early collectors. They were sometimes kept in albums, but also framed and displayed." Onno van Seggelen: "The majority of his drawings executed in pencil and grey wash on vellum and seldomly in red chalk." The known examples are precise about the medium: *Merry Company or the Allegory of Seduction*, c. 1638-45, graphite and grey wash on vellum, 27.8 x 34.7 cm (Ackland Art Museum, Peck Collection 2023.25); *Elegant Company on a Terrace*, 1640, plumbago (graphite) on vellum, 29.85 x 41.28 cm (Nelson-Atkins); *Couples Conversing in the Park of a Country House*, 1638, black chalk on vellum, signed and dated, 213 x 320 mm (Sotheby's); *The Mocking of the Spaniard*, 1641, black chalk and India ink on vellum, 252 x 356 mm (Fondation Custodia); *Two Fighting Women*, 1638, graphite on vellum (Google Arts and Culture); *Head and Shoulders Study of a Man in Profile, wearing a tall hat*, black chalk, monogrammed and dated 'PQ / 1632' (White Rose). The mechanism is the vellum as a finished surface: the grey wash does the modelling, the graphite does the line, and the drawing is complete without ever becoming a painting. The Ackland drawing shows what the medium carries: a rustic gathering outdoors, an old woman with a basket of eggs approaching drinking and smoking men, a principal figure holding a pasglas (a drinking-game beaker), one man passed out on the table, one urinating into a bucket at the left -- the whole merry company, with its passed-out man and its urinating man, rendered in grey wash on animal skin.

**Takeaway:** build a drawing, not a painting -- graphite line and grey wash on a pale vellum ground, the picture complete in two tones. The watchable behaviour is the wash: the grey deepening slowly over the figures while the line stays sharp, the passed-out man staying passed out and the urinating man staying at the bucket, the drawing finishing itself in front of us.

## M7. The merry company and the allegory of seduction

The elegant merry company is Quast's polite strand, and it is satire too -- the satire is just wearing better clothes. *A Party of Merrymakers*, c. 1635-38, oil on wood, 37.5 x 49.5 cm (Met 1973.155.1), is the model: off-key carousing, fancy shoes, frizzy hair, open collars, and a symbolic satyr figure the Met notes contemporary viewers would have read at a glance. The Met's reading is the mechanism: "Viewers of the time would have taken in at a glance not only the off-key carousing but also signs -- the fancy shoes, frizzy hair, open collar, and so on -- that make the symbolic satyr redundant. Quast worked in the cosmopolitan milieus of Amsterdam and The Hague. His paintings and finished drawings reveal a broad knowledge of Dutch, Flemish, and French popular imagery (including Callot's etchings) as well as the comic stage." The signs are the satire: the shoes, the hair and the collar convict the company without a word, and the satyr is redundant because the clothes already say it. The pyramidal composition "appealed to Quast just like his choice of preference for comic theatre types" (Onno van Seggelen) -- the merry company is staged like a theatre scene, the figures arranged in a pyramid with the comic type at its apex. The same strand runs through *Merry Company Making Music*, 1630s, oil on oak, signed "PQ", 49 x 36.5 cm (Budapest, inv. 71.20); *Elegant Company*, Oslo (NG.M.00093); *An Elegant Company in an Interior*, 42.5 x 36.5 cm (Christie's lot 993); and the vellum drawings of 1638-40. The 1641 *Mocking of the Spaniard* adds an explicitly political target -- a single work, not proof of a late intensification -- and the 1638 *Couples Conversing in the Park of a Country House* turns the caricature on upper-class pretension, peacocks included.

**Takeaway:** build a merry company as a theatre scene in a pyramid, the comic type at the apex, and let the clothes do the convicting -- fancy shoes, frizzy hair, open collar. The watchable behaviour is the company carousing off-key while the signs accumulate: each figure's costume detail brightening in turn until the satyr at the edge is redundant, and the pyramid holding its shape while the party slides.

## M8. The sculptor painter

The strangest mechanism in Quast's oeuvre is the one no other painter in the chain has: he made sculpture. Sotheby's 2026 lot essay calls him "one of the few painters of the Golden Age to venture into sculpture". The Rijksmuseum owns a gilded terracotta relief of two fighting men, signed *P. Quast* and dated 1628 on a violin in the foreground -- *A Fight Between Two Street Musicians*, 1628/29, terracotta with gold-dust painting, relief, Rijksmuseum BK-1966-2: two street musicians brawling with clubs, restrained by onlookers, a violin and a triangle on the ground, grotesque caricatured faces. Leeuwenberg (*Rijksmuseum Bulletin* 14, 1966, pp. 60-64) attributed two further *Five Senses* reliefs to Quast (terracotta, Museum de Lakenhal, Leiden, 24 x 32 cm; wood, Israel Museum, Jerusalem, 22.5 x 30 cm); Leeflang (2008) attributed a *Flagellation* terracotta in Museum Catharijneconvent (25 x 23.5 cm). Terracotta "seems to have been the material Pieter Quast preferred for sculpture". The relief is dated 1628 -- before his earliest dated drawing (1632) and at about age 23 -- and the date rests on the inscription on the violin in the relief itself. The mechanism is the caricature in three dimensions: the same grotesque faces and brawling musicians as the paintings, but modelled in clay and gilded, so the joke has weight and casts a shadow. The sculpture is also the oeuvre's earliest dated object: before the paintings, before the drawings, the satire was already a brawl in terracotta. (Flag: Sotheby's 2026 lot essay is sound on the sculpture attributions and unreliable on biographical dates -- it gives birth 1609 and marriage 1638, both flatly wrong against the archival record.)

**Takeaway:** build a small terracotta relief -- two musicians brawling with clubs, a violin and a triangle on the ground, grotesque faces -- gilded, casting a real shadow. The watchable behaviour is the relief's depth: the light travelling across the modelled clay so the brawl's shadows move while the figures stay frozen mid-swing, the violin on the ground catching the gold, and the joke holding still in three dimensions.

## Evolution

There is no "early straight, late satirical" arc in the dated record, and the study says so plainly. The caricature and satire mode is present from the very start: the two earliest signed and dated paintings are both 1628 (*Vertumnus and Pomona*; *Interior of a Horse Stable with an Officer*), and the signed terracotta of 1628/29 (W01) is already a farcical brawl with grotesque faces, read by the Rijksmuseum as a satirical tableau ridiculing objectionable behaviour. The middle of the career (the Hague years) holds the most sustained satirical project, the 26-plate *Beggars and Peasants* series of 1634-38, and the dated drawings of 1638-41 (*Couples Conversing* 1638, *Two Fighting Women* 1638, *Elegant Company on a Terrace* 1640, *The Mocking of the Spaniard* 1641) show the same caricatured types applied to both low-life and high-life targets -- the 1641 drawing adds an explicitly political (anti-Spanish) target, but it is a single work and does not prove a late intensification. The late dated paintings (*Soldiers at an Inn* 1640, *Soldiers Gambling with Dice* 1643) stay inside the same guardroom and gambling repertory with the same small scale and limited palette. The elegant-company strand (W07, W08, W13, W14, W17) clusters in the 1630s across both cities, and the Met dates *A Party of Merrymakers* to the Hague and late-Amsterdam mature style -- but that is a stylistic judgement, not a dated fact. No Amsterdam-versus-The Hague stylistic split can be drawn: the Rijksmuseum dates its two Quast paintings to the whole span 1630-1647, and most paintings are dated only "1630s"; only the print series is firmly Hague (1634-38). The biography itself is contested on one point: the Nice Art Gallery essay (2025, a commercial reproduction site -- weak authority) says he returned to Amsterdam by about 1636, while Wikipedia, the Rijksmuseum and Christie's all place him in The Hague until late 1643 and back in Amsterdam by January 1644; the latter is better sourced and is used here.

## Aesthetic logic

Quast's aesthetic is the joke told small and heavy. Everything is small -- the panels 30 to 50 cm, the etchings about 20 cm, the drawings about 30 cm -- and everything is heavy: warm brown laid on thick, chiaroscuro strong, the local colour loud where it lands. The largeness is in the type: one beggar with a bowl implies a whole city of beggars, one quack operation implies a whole trade of quacks, one merry company in fancy shoes implies a whole class being laughed at. The guardroom taught him the room; Callot taught him the type; the theatre taught him the staging; the Hague fairs gave him the live models. The jester is the chain's conscience: Codde and Duyster painted the soldiers' leisure, van Velsen talked in it, and Quast stood inside it and laughed -- and the laugh is the commentary, because the caricature is the judgement. The brown ground is the world as it is; the strong local colour is the finger pointing at what is wrong with it. The terracotta relief is the same joke with weight: the brawl you can pick up. The poverty question is the honest edge of the life: the record gives debts and a leaking house on one side and a bought house and a widow who could build on the other, and the study presents the question as a question.

## Quotes

Grove Art Online (Leistra 2003), quoted in EN Wikipedia: Quast's paintings "are heavily and powerfully rendered in warm shades of brown, set off by strong local colouring in the principal figures. His successful peasant scenes are characterized by the use of strong chiaroscuro and a gentle, harmonious palette. The caricatural quality of Quast's peasants recalls the work of his fellow-resident of The Hague, Adriaen van de Venne, but Quast's looser style and many of his individual types are closer to the paintings of Adriaen Brouwer, as well as of Adriaen van Ostade, to whom Quast's best work has sometimes been ascribed."

EN Wikipedia, Pieter Quast: "Mostly producing small social genre paintings with humorous, cartoon-like features, ranging from elegant merry companies to guardroom scenes with officers and (most numerous) groups of peasants or beggars." / "His style is characterized by humor, satire and caricature." / "He competed with Pieter Codde and Willem Duyster who had a similar style and was influenced [by] Adriaen Brouwer." / "Quast was influenced by the French engraver Jacques Callot and even called the Dutch Callot. With his caricatured images on often small panels, Quast provided commentary on the Golden Age." / "He produced finished drawings for sale, often on parchment, and these included landscapes, cityscapes and biblical scenes. Some of his works were engraved, likely by Salomon Savery." / "It is questionable he died in poverty."

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, on *A Party of Merrymakers* (1973.155.1): "Viewers of the time would have taken in at a glance not only the off-key carousing but also signs -- the fancy shoes, frizzy hair, open collar, and so on -- that make the symbolic satyr redundant. Quast worked in the cosmopolitan milieus of Amsterdam and The Hague. His paintings and finished drawings reveal a broad knowledge of Dutch, Flemish, and French popular imagery (including Callot's etchings) as well as the comic stage."

Onno van Seggelen, on the *Merry company / Allegory of seduction* drawing: the figures are "directly inspired by Callot's *Balli di Sfessania*"; "Pyramidal shaped composition... appealed to Quast just like his choice of preference for comic theatre types"; "The majority of his drawings executed in pencil and grey wash on vellum and seldomly in red chalk."

Sciences Po seminar (Leonard Pouy, "Painters at War"), on Quast's *Guardroom with officers examining the catch of a looting*: the picture "broke up [the] dichotomy by placing the soldier back within a discussion over the evaluation of the pillaged good... the officer ennobled himself by taking on the new role of expert, the artist displayed his talent by representing various textures and materials."

Hofstede de Groot (1910, vol. 3): Quast's pictures "are of very unequal quality; his best pictures often pass as the works of Brouwer, and probably are still included in this catalogue" (under Brouwer).

Sotheby's 2026 lot essay: Quast was "one of the few painters of the Golden Age to venture into sculpture."

## Network

The defining influence: Jacques Callot (1592-1635), French engraver -- "influenced by the French engraver Jacques Callot and even called the Dutch Callot" (EN Wikipedia); the influence "was so big, Quast was called the 'Dutch Callot'", and his comic theatre figures "were directly inspired by Callot's *Balli di Sfessania* of 1621" (Onno van Seggelen). Callot's commedia dell'arte types are the direct source for Quast's buffoons (leg 3).

The peasant line: Adriaen Brouwer (c. 1605/06 Oudenaarde - January 1638 Antwerp) -- Quast "was influenced [by] Adriaen Brouwer" (EN Wikipedia); Grove: "Quast's looser style and many of his individual types are closer to the paintings of Adriaen Brouwer". A Quast peasant "comes very close to Brouwer's *'The smoker / Allegory on the Taste'* in the Rijksmuseum" (Onno van Seggelen). Brouwer is the shared Hals hinge with van Velsen, who was influenced by Dirck Hals (leg 3).

The competitors: Pieter Codde (Amsterdam 1599-1678) and Willem Duyster (c. 1599-1635) -- "He competed with Pieter Codde and Willem Duyster who had a similar style" (EN Wikipedia). Codde is one of the first guardroom painters -- the genre was "first depicted by Pieter Codde and others in Amsterdam at the conclusion of the Twelve-Year Truce (1621)". The chain runs van Velsen to Duyster (RKD: van Velsen influenced Duyster) to Quast competing with Duyster (leg 3).

The shared hinge: Dirck Hals (Haarlem 1591-1656) -- van Velsen's conversation pieces are "in the manner of Pieter Codde and Dirck Hals", and Dirck Hals was "a prolific specialist in small merry company groups". Kolfin's merry-company study treats Dirck Hals, Codde, Duyster and Quast together, with the guardroom subtype appearing in the 1630s (leg 3).

The pupils: Jan Jansz Buesem and Dirck Cornelis de Hooch -- "His pupils were Jan Jansz Buesem and Dirck Cornelis de Hooch" (EN Wikipedia, citing RKD; White Rose repeats both). Both are thinly documented beyond that one line (leg 3).

The Hague fellow-resident: Adriaen van de Venne (1589-1662) -- Grove: "The caricatural quality of Quast's peasants recalls the work of his fellow-resident of The Hague, Adriaen van de Venne". Van de Venne joined The Hague Guild of St Luke in 1625 and was its dean in 1640; Quast joined the same guild in 1634 -- same city, same guild, overlapping years (leg 3).

The near-double: Adriaen van Ostade (1610-1685) -- Grove: Quast's best work "has sometimes been ascribed" to van Ostade. Ostade was Brouwer's fellow Hals pupil in Haarlem and the great peasant-life painter -- the confusion is itself evidence of how close Quast's peasant manner sits to the Brouwer-Ostade line (leg 3). Van Ostade was studied as #535; he is not a future candidate.

Patrons, collaborators, neighbours: Jan Stampioen, mathematics teacher of William II of Orange -- Quast "collaborated with Jan Stampioen" in 1639 and painted William II; drawing master to the young prince (EN Wikipedia; White Rose). Herman Doomer (furniture maker) and Salomon Savery (engraver) -- collaborators; Savery likely engraved some Quast works. Pieter Nolpe engraved after Quast (and Brouwer). Wife Annetje Splinters, flower painter from The Hague. Hague neighbours on the Groene Burchwal included Jan van Goyen and Paulus Potter (leg 3).

The scholarship: Stanton-Hirst, "Pieter Quast and the Theatre", *Oud Holland* 96 (1982), pp. 213-237 -- the key biography, paywalled, not read; Leeuwenberg, "Beeldhouwwerk van de schilder Pieter Quast", *Rijksmuseum Bulletin* 14 (1966), pp. 60-64 -- the sculpture attributions, not read; Leistra, "Quast, Pieter", *Grove Art Online* 2003 -- paywalled, quoted passage only via Wikipedia; Bredius, "Pieter Jansz. Quast", *Oud-Holland* 20 (1902); Roeders, "Voorts den teekeningen..." (2004 UvA thesis).
