## 1. The Buenos Aires boy who learned piano from Barenboim's father

His full name was Luis Enriquez Bacalov, though Italian Wikipedia's lead gives "Luis Enrique Bacalov", also known as "Luis Enriquez", and he signed his early RCA arrangements and his first film scores as Luis Enriquez. [C31534] He was born on 30 August 1933, a Wednesday, to a family of Bulgarian Jewish origin; his paternal grandparents were Banat Bulgarians who had emigrated to Argentina, and he identified as a Jew but did not practice Judaism -- Italian Wikipedia instead calls him secular and agnostic, from a family of Bulgarian origin with a Jewish religious tradition, a small discrepancy the study carries rather than resolves. [C31535] He began piano study at the age of five with professor Enrique Barenboim, the father of conductor Daniel Barenboim and himself a pupil of Vincenzo Scaramuzza; English Wikipedia and Movie Music UK also name the pianist Berta Sujovolsky as a teacher. [C31536] He completed his studies in Buenos Aires and gave a series of concerts across South America -- a concert pianist first, before anything else, which matters for everything that follows, because his film music is written by a man who could sit at the piano and play it. [C31537]

At twenty he left Argentina and moved to Colombia, where he lived four years and married; Polish Wikipedia adds that he researched folk music in Latin American countries, collaborating in that field with Colombian radio and television. [C31538] From Colombia he moved to Europe: almost a year in Spain, where he suffered under the political climate of Francoism, then Paris, where he did advanced perfection studies, and he arrived in Italy in 1959, where he was offered work and at first stayed six months -- Treccani independently confirms the 1959 arrival. [C31539] No source read names his parents individually, and no source read gives his wife's name; his private family life is essentially undocumented in the sources read, and the study does not invent it. [C31540] Two people sharing his surname appear in his later work, but no source read states any family relationship to him: Daniel Bacalov (percussion) recorded the 2001 album "Tango and Around" with him, and Anna Paola Bacalov did the choreography for "Y Borges cuenta que" in 2008 -- the study names them as collaborators only and does not call either his child. [C31541]

**Takeaway:** The biography is already the mechanism: a Buenos Aires pianist trained by the Barenboim line, four years collecting folk music in Colombia, a year hating Franco's Spain, Paris polish, then Italy at twenty-six -- he arrives in Rome as a man with three musical mother tongues (classical piano, Latin American folk, tango) and no film career yet, and the next forty years are those three tongues taking turns at the front of the orchestra.

## 2. The pop arranger: twenty years inside the Italian song machine

In Italy he quickly established himself as a brilliant arranger, first for the record company Fonit Cetra and then for RCA; at Fonit Cetra he met artists including Claudio Villa, for whom he was also piano accompanist in concert for a year, and Milva. [C31542] Once at RCA, in 1960, Bacalov -- then signing himself Luis Enriquez -- came to attention arranging songs for Nico Fidenco, Sergio Endrigo, Rita Pavone, Umberto Bindi and Neil Sedaka. [C31543] With Endrigo he formed a partnership lasting almost twenty years, documented by songs such as "Io che amo solo te", "Se le cose stanno cosi", "Era d'estate", "Canzone per te", "Lontano dagli occhi", "L'arca di Noe", "Ci vuole un fiore" and "Elisa Elisa". [C31544] He also arranged for Gianni Morandi, and his later connection to the pop world ran both ways: Morricone's own page notes that Rita Pavone's 1962 songs "Come te non c'e' nessuno" and "Pel di carota" were arranged by Bacalov. [C31545] AllMusic says that during his first year in Rome he played piano for a film score composed by Chico Marselli, and that the song "La Ragazza con la Viglia" became a pop hit in Italy and RCA hired him to arrange pop tunes -- single-source, and AllMusic's biography contains other date errors, so the study carries the Marselli detail with care rather than as settled fact. [C31546]

He began composing for the cinema in 1960, under the pseudonym Luis Enriquez; Italian Wikipedia lists his first film score as "La banda del buco", directed by Mario Amendola (1960). [C31547] Two rival first-film dates circulate and neither is corroborated: Movie Music UK's obituary says he scored his first film, a low-budget ghost story called "Questi Fantasmi", in 1954, but Italian Wikipedia lists "Questi fantasmi", directed by Renato Castellani, as a 1967 film; and AllMusic says he became more involved with film scoring in 1958, when he worked as an assistant to Ennio Morricone, which conflicts with the 1960 start given by Italian Wikipedia and Treccani. [C31548] The assistantship itself is the load-bearing fact, and the study uses it with the date as AllMusic gives it, because it is the only source stating it and it is consistent with the late-1950s Rome timeline -- but the study does not use 1958 as a settled start for his film career, which begins on the record in 1960. [C31549]

**Takeaway:** The arranger years are not a detour from the film career; they are the training for it. Twenty years of making other singers' songs work taught him the one skill his film scores run on: a melody that can carry a whole piece, dressed differently each time it returns -- the mechanism that becomes Il Postino thirty years later.

## 3. The westerns: brass, a sung title, and a theme worth stealing

Bacalov's first western score was Django (1966, Sergio Corbucci), and against the contemporary classical style of Morricone's spaghetti-western scores it is more traditional, relying especially on brass and orchestral instrumentation, with Latin and rock elements in several tracks. [C31550] The main title theme, conducted by Bruno Nicolai with lyrics by Franco Migliacci and Robert Mellin, was sung in English by Rocky Roberts; Tarantino reused the song plus "La Corsa (2nd Version)" and other Django cues in Django Unchained (2012). [C31551] The score develops the theme through brass versions, dissonant action music ("Espera y ataque"), manic strings plus trumpet solo ("La Corsa", 2nd version) and mariachi-brass tracks ("Vamonos muchachos"), and the instrumental form carries a Hank Marvin-style guitar solo -- the tune first, the atmosphere second, which is the opposite of the Morricone method and the whole point of the comparison. [C31552]

Sugar Colt (1966, Franco Giraldi), released in Italy 12 October 1966, is built on two recurring themes that clash in tone: a funereal solo trumpet number and a comic piccolo trill; the title song was sung by I Cantori Moderni, and the soundtrack album was reissued on CD by GDM Music in 2003. [C31553] A Bullet for the General (1966, Damiano Damiani), released in Italy 2 December 1966, starred Gian Maria Volonte, Lou Castel and Klaus Kinski in the first Italian western to deal seriously with the Mexican Revolution, per film historian Howard Hughes; the score reuses several themes from Django, was supervised by Ennio Morricone, and carries a stronger Latin influence than most spaghetti-western scores, its action themes at times recalling Leonard Bernstein's West Side Story. [C31554] The reuse is the tell: Bacalov treats a good theme as capital to be spent again, not as a one-film property, and the same habit later lets Tarantino mine him twice. [C31555]

The Price of Power (1969, Tonino Valerii), a spaghetti western released in Italy in December 1969 starring Giuliano Gemma that transposes the JFK assassination onto an 1881 plot to kill President James Garfield, grossed 1,273,858,000 lire domestically; the score uses Nora Orlandi's I 4+4 choir and harmonica player Franco De Gemini, colours that move between elegy, frontier atmosphere and heightened conspiracy. [C31556] His Name Was King (1971, Giancarlo Romitelli), released 18 May 1971, starred Richard Harrison and Klaus Kinski in a revenge western, and its title song "Lo Chiamavano King", sung by Edda Dell'Orso, was reused by Tarantino in Django Unchained as Dr. Schultz's theme. [C31557] The Grand Duel (1972, Giancarlo Santi), Santi's directorial debut -- he had been assistant director on Leone's The Good, the Bad and the Ugly -- starred Lee Van Cleef as a gunfighter defending his values; Bacalov's score is the film's most praised element after Van Cleef, its elegiac main theme ("The Grand Duel -- Parte Prima") rivalling The Great Silence for the genre's most wistful theme, and Tarantino lifted it wholesale for Kill Bill (2003). [C31558] We Still Kill the Old Way (1967, Elio Petri), released in Italy 24 February 1967, based on Leonardo Sciascia's novel To Each His Own and starring Gian Maria Volonte and Irene Papas, won the Best Screenplay award at the 1967 Cannes Film Festival; its soundtrack LP was released in 1968 on United Artists, and a modern reviewer calls it a "moving score" that punctuates the film's backroom conversations and romantic melancholy. [C31559]

**Takeaway:** The western mechanism is melody-first brass: a singable title song, an orchestra that plays tunes rather than textures, and themes recycled across films like a songwriter reusing a chorus. Morricone invented a sound; Bacalov wrote songs inside the same genre, and the songs outlived the genre -- Tarantino keeps coming back to them because a tune survives being lifted in a way a texture does not.

## 4. The sacred collage: the Gospel score

The Gospel According to St. Matthew (1964, Pier Paolo Pasolini) is the strangest brief of Bacalov's career and the one that earned him his first Oscar nomination: the score, arranged by Bacalov, is eclectic -- Johann Sebastian Bach (Mass in B Minor, St Matthew Passion), Mozart, Prokofiev, Odetta ("Sometimes I Feel Like a Motherless Child"), Blind Willie Johnson ("Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground"), and the Congolese Missa Luba, plus several original tracks composed by Bacalov. [C31560] The range runs further in the full account: the Jewish "Kol Nidre" and the "Gloria" from the Congolese Missa Luba sit beside Bach, and Pasolini stated all of the film's music was of a sacred or religious nature from all parts of the world and multiple cultures; Bacalov also composed several original tracks, including "Tre fronde, tre fiori" for Salome's dance, performed on flutes and tambourines by musicians visible in Herod's court. [C31561] Pasolini took personal charge of the film's soundtrack, assisted by Bacalov; the film won the Special Jury Prize at Venice 1964 and was nominated for three Oscars including Best Score, and Bacalov's own nomination came in 1967, when the film was released in the US. [C31562] It earned him that nomination at the 39th Academy Awards, held 10 April 1967, in the category Music (Scoring of Music -- adaptation or treatment); the award went to Ken Thorne for A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum, and the film took three nominations in all. [C31563]

**Takeaway:** The Gospel mechanism is curate, do not invent: Bacalov treats sacred material as found sound to be arranged, and his original contribution is the framing, the cut, and the juxtaposition. It is the same instinct that later truncates the Mass text in Misa Tango -- the composer as editor of the sacred, deciding what stays, what goes, and what sits next to what. A film composer who can make Bach, a blues spiritual and a Congolese Gloria feel like one score has learned the one skill that matters most in his later work: holding unlike things together by the strength of the sequence.

## 5. The rock-classical collision: the 1971-73 triptych

The hinge of Bacalov's career is a three-year collision between classical form and rock bands. Concerto Grosso per i New Trolls (1971) was written as the score for The Designated Victim (1971, Maurizio Lucidi); it was recorded 23-27 March 1971 at Ortophonic Studio, Rome, and Fonit-Cetra, Milan, and the album pairs Bacalov's orchestral concerto grosso (side A) with live New Trolls improvisations (side B), while the film uses the same themes in different arrangements, notably the Adagio ("Shadows") opening the film with lead vocals by actor Tomas Milian. [C31564] Bacalov's idea was a concerto grosso in the Baroque manner in which the solo parts were played by an underground rock group; the 11-minute concerto proper (Allegro / Adagio "Shadows" / Cadenza / "Shadows per Jimi Hendrix") plus a 20-minute live improvisation "Nella Sala Vuota" sold a reported 800,000 copies in Italy per Paolo Barotto's The Return of Italian Pop, and the record is generally regarded as the foundation of Italian progressive rock. [C31565]

Milano calibro 9 (1972, Fernando Di Leo) is the second panel: the score was composed by Bacalov and performed by the Neapolitan progressive rock band Osanna, and the film, released in Italy 15 February 1972, adapts stories by Giorgio Scerbanenco and stars Gastone Moschin as ex-con Ugo Piazza. [C31566] The resulting album, Osanna's Preludio Tema Variazioni e Canzona (Fonit, 1972), is a strict classical-variation form: Bacalov wrote the Preludio and Tema, conducted the orchestra and played piano and harpsichord, while Osanna wrote the seven variations; some of the band's music was recorded live to a screening of the film in Rome and later re-recorded in Milan. [C31567] The record alternates Bacalov's baroque and symphonic writing with Osanna's hard prog, sharp guitar riffs, frantic flute and sax solos, noise inserts, dissonance and early synthesizer use -- Osanna's lineup was Lino Vairetti (lead vocals, acoustic guitar, ARPI synthesizer, keyboards), Danilo Rustici (electric and 12-string guitar) and Elio D'Anna (flute, soprano and tenor sax) -- and after the debut album L'uomo (1971), Osanna released the Milano Calibro 9 soundtrack in 1972 and toured Italy alongside Genesis that same year. [C31568]

The third part of the triptych was Il Rovescio della Medaglia's Contaminazione (1973), which was not a movie soundtrack; the triptych was prompted by the success of the New Trolls record, after which Osanna's label Fonit pushed Osanna and Bacalov to work together. [C31569] The mechanism is one idea applied three times: classical form, rock soloists, orchestra as the other voice. Milano calibro 9 is the version where the collision itself is the subject -- sweetness of strings against power of rock, with no soft compromise -- and the study names the triptych as the hinge because it is the only place in Bacalov's career where the form and the noise are equally the point. [C31570]

**Takeaway:** The triptych mechanism is the Baroque concerto grosso with the solo chair given to a rock band. The form is old (soloists against orchestra), the soloists are new (electric guitar, flute, synthesizer), and the friction between them is the music. Steal the casting, not the sound: put an unlikely soloist in a formal chair and let the form hold them both.

## 6. The crime funk: Di Leo's Milan

Bacalov scored three Di Leo crime films, and the study counts them carefully because the common assumption gets the number wrong. Il Boss (1973), released in Italy 1 February 1973 and starring Henry Silva, Richard Conte and Gianni Garko, is the third part of Di Leo's Milieu Trilogy after Milano Calibro 9 and La mala ordina; its jazzy score sets the mood from the opening shot with a deep bass line and jarring percussion, and reviewers single it out as prog-influenced and among Bacalov's best crime work. [C31571] I padroni della citta (1976), released 3 December 1976, is a lighter-toned Di Leo mafia film starring Jack Palance as mob boss Manzari, aka Scarface, with Harry Baer and Vittorio Caprioli as debt collectors plotting to con him; the opening slow-motion sequence is set to Bacalov's "unsettlingly off-key, almost horror-leaning score", while the film as a whole carries a funky flute-and-guitar-based score. [C31572] The middle film of the Milieu Trilogy, La mala ordina (1972), was scored by Armando Trovajoli, not Bacalov -- so Bacalov's Di Leo crime scores are three, not four, and any account that credits him with the whole trilogy is wrong. [C31573] He also scored a fourth Di Leo film outside the crime set: La Seduzione (1973), an erotic drama shot in Sicily with a soundtrack LP released by Cetra in 1973, counted among his most regarded scores -- a big-orchestra selection with terrific funk beats, mind-melting scats and Brazilian influence, described as destroying all his other soundtrack productions for sheer groove. [C31574]

The crime scores run on jazz-funk, wah-wah guitar and flute, and the same groove spills into the non-Di Leo crime work. The Summertime Killer (1972, Antonio Isasi-Isasmendi), a crime film starring Christopher Mitchum, Olivia Hussey and Karl Malden, with Jeffrey Tambor in his film debut, credits its music to Luis Enriquez Bacalov and Sergio Bardotti; its title theme "Summertime Killer" was used in the Kill Bill, Volume II soundtrack, and the complete original soundtrack was reissued in full stereo by Quartet Records in 2010. [C31575] One Man Against the Organization (1975, Sergio Grieco), released in Italian cinemas 13 September 1975 and starring Howard Ross, Karin Schubert and Stephen Boyd, is about an airport employee who swaps a drug shipment for baking soda and flees to Barcelona; its score mixes cool-jazz languor, period funk, light dissonant experiments and expressive moods, and includes "Carrefour", called by the Four Flies reissue label maybe his funkiest soundtrack piece ever, plus the 7-minute "Fandango", also issued as a 1975 CBS single. [C31576] For Il Boss, Bacalov wrote what one review calls a "driving piano score" that keeps the film at a brisk clip -- the piano, his first instrument, doing the rhythmic work the guitar does in the westerns. [C31577]

The Di Leo overlap runs both ways, which is what makes the doorway hold: Di Leo was uncredited screenwriter on A Fistful of Dollars (1964) and For a Few Dollars More (1965), uncredited screenwriter on Django itself (1966), and credited writer on Sugar Colt (1966) -- which Bacalov also scored -- so Di Leo wrote westerns Bacalov scored, and Bacalov scored crime films Di Leo directed. [C31578] Milano calibro 9 takes its title from Scerbanenco's 1969 short-story collection and is partially based on three of its stories ("Bravi ragazzi bang bang" and "In pineta si uccide meglio" among them); the film's score is the Bacalov/Osanna record, which is the thread that ties this study to #767. [C31579]

**Takeaway:** The crime mechanism is funk as structure: a deep bass line, jarring percussion and a flute that should not belong there, holding a scene together the way the brass held the westerns together. The groove does the narrative work -- it tells you the deal is going wrong before the dialogue does -- and the count matters: three crime scores, a fourth Di Leo film outside the genre, and one trilogy film that belongs to Trovajoli. Getting the count right is part of the mechanism, because the trilogy is not a clean Bacalov set and pretending it is would flatten the very thing that makes his Di Leo work interesting: he scores the beginning and the end of the trilogy, not the middle.

## 7. One theme, dressed many times: Il Postino

Il Postino (1994, Michael Radford), based on Antonio Skarmeta's novel Ardiente Paciencia, premiered in Italy in September 1994 and became a global hit after Miramax picked it up for summer 1995; star Massimo Troisi, gravely ill with a heart condition, died one day after principal photography wrapped. [C31580] Bacalov won the Academy Award for Best Original Dramatic Score at the 68th Academy Awards on 25 March 1996, a ceremony honoring 1995 films; the film had five nominations and one win, and Bacalov beat James Horner, nominated twice in the same category for Apollo 13 and Braveheart; he also won the BAFTA for Best Score in 1996. [C31581] The Il Postino theme later became the subject of a long legal dispute with Sergio Endrigo: a 2001 sentence acquitted Bacalov of plagiarism, a 2003 civil appeal court sentence convicted him of having copied Endrigo, and in 2013 he recognised Endrigo, Riccardo Del Turco and Paolo Margheri as co-authors of the score's theme -- and Italian Wikipedia notes the Oscar statuette was later halved, i.e. the award was split. [C31582] The dispute is part of the mechanism, not gossip about it: the theme was so simple, so much a song, that two men could plausibly claim it, which is exactly what a twenty-year songwriting partnership produces when one partner writes a film theme. [C31583]

The score is dominated by a single main theme in many instrumental variations -- bandoneon (Hector Ulises Passarella), mandolin (Riccardo Pellegrino), Bacalov's solo piano and strings -- with tango and Latin American colouring for Neruda's Chilean identity, plus the vintage 1931 tango "Madreselva" in a Carlos Gardel recording and a new instrumental arrangement. [C31584] The score "has more or less one single theme. Yet arranged for different instrumental groupings (with accordion often playing a central role) and with a varied accompaniment it always seems fresh and new." [C31585] The main theme first appears in the opening "Titoli" carried by bandoneon backed by warm strings, then recurs in tonal and instrumental variations: lightly comedic woodwinds, sunny guitar and undulating piano in "In Bicicletta"; a children's music-box style in "Postino Bambino"; florid solo piano and solo violin in the "Trio Version"; harpsichord and string version; guitar and bandoneon version -- and the only material outside the main theme is the vintage "Madreselva" and a playful theme for Beatrice that is essentially an offshoot of the main theme with more pizzicato strings. [C31586] For Il Postino, Bacalov wanted a bandoneon "a little different than usual" -- what he called a Barlettian sound, with the flavor of a harbor -- and chose Uruguayan bandoneonist Hector Ulises Passarella; the score is described as tango-inflected, using the bandoneon to reflect Pablo Neruda's Chilean identity. [C31587] The Il Postino music is "lyrical, wistful, and intimate", built from "simple melodies arranged with lush orchestrations, creating a sense of nostalgia and quiet longing", with "a gentle Mediterranean warmth in the instrumentation." [C31588]

**Takeaway:** The Il Postino mechanism is economy as a system: one melody, many rooms. It is the opposite of the Morricone method of distinct leitmotifs per character; Bacalov varies the dress, not the tune. The bandoneon is the lead voice because the tango is the home key, and the whole score is what happens when a Buenos Aires pianist who spent twenty years arranging pop songs is asked to write a love theme for a dying postman -- one tune, played until it means everything.

## 8. The tango comes home: Misa Tango and the late concert works

Misa Tango (1997) sets a Spanish-language adaptation of the classic liturgical Mass to the tango rhythms of his native Argentina, and Bacalov truncated the standard Mass text so the work would appeal to all Abrahamic faiths (Christians, Muslims and Jews), deleting all references to Christ except the Agnus Dei and cutting the Credo to most of its first strophe plus part of the second. [C31589] It debuted in Rome in 2000 with Placido Domingo as solo tenor and was recorded by Deutsche Grammophon with Domingo (tenor), Ana Maria Martinez (mezzo-soprano) and Hector Ulises Passarella (bandoneon); its first US performance was 19 May 2002 at Harvard University's Sanders Theatre. [C31590] He was the first to write a triple-concerto for bandoneon, piano, soprano and symphony orchestra: Tango Music with Symphonic Proportions. [C31591] Cantones de Nuestro Tiempos (Psalms for Our Times: The Cambridge Psalms, 2006), text from the Psalms of David for baritone and soprano soloists, orchestra and chorus, had its world premiere in spring 2006 at Sanders Theatre, Cambridge, Massachusetts, by the Cambridge Community Chorus. [C31592] In July 2008 he opened the Settimana Musicale Senese with the opera-ballet "Y Borges cuenta que", with libretto by Carlos Sessano, Alberto Munoz and Bacalov himself. [C31593]

No source found states a personal collaboration, meeting, or working relationship between Bacalov and Astor Piazzolla in Argentina or at any other time -- the study does not assert one. [C31594] The only evidenced Bacalov-Piazzolla link is posthumous and one-directional: Bacalov orchestrated Piazzolla's "Adios Nonino" and "Libertango" for the 2000 Deutsche Grammophon album "Bacalov: Misa Tango; Tangosain / Piazzolla: Adios Nonino; Libertango", credited as "Piazzolla: Adios Nonino (Tango Rapsodia) [Orch. Bacalov]"; Piazzolla died in 1992, so this was an arrangement of a dead composer's work, not a collaboration. [C31595] The late film work keeps the same home key: The Love Letter (1999, Peter Chan) takes the tango as its starting point, weaving variations on the tango through the story, and director Peter Chan's booklet note says the music is "so luxurious, almost sinful" that on hearing it "one can barely walk in a straight line"; Milonga (1999, Emidio Greco) is an Italian thriller in which a Rome inspector, played by Giancarlo Giannini, connects a murder to its killers through a tango heard on the radio, tracking them to a milonga where the tango is danced. [C31596] Assassination Tango (2002, Robert Duvall), premiered at the Toronto International Film Festival 11 September 2002, has Duvall playing an aging Brooklyn hitman sent to Buenos Aires whose delayed contract leaves him free to fall in love with the tango and his teacher Manuela; the soundtrack album, released on RCA Victor in 2003, mixes well-known tangos with Bacalov's new music for the film. [C31597]

**Takeaway:** The late mechanism is the home key made public: after forty years of writing in other people's genres, Bacalov writes the Mass as a tango and the tango as a Mass, and the film scores of the same years do the same thing in miniature -- a murder solved by a dance, a love story that cannot walk straight. The tango was never a flavor for him; it was the rhythmic grid he fell back on whenever he wrote without a genre brief.

## 9. The last twelve years: Taranto and the Orchestra della Magna Grecia

From 2005 until his death he was the principal director of the Orchestra della Magna Grecia in Taranto, southern Italy, and its artistic director; in July 2014 he was publicly calling for more care and attention for the orchestra (ICO della Magna Grecia) in the local press, showing he was actively advocating for it, not only holding the title. [C31598] He held the film-music composition course at the Accademia Musicale Chigiana in Siena and taught at the ACT Multimedia film academy at Cinecitta in Rome. [C31599] He scored Federico Fellini's City of Women in 1980, Fellini's first film after the death of his longtime composer Nino Rota, and he recorded some of the classical piano works of Nino Rota (album Luis Bacalov Plays Nino Rota, 1993) -- the pianist stepping into the dead master's chair and then recording the master's piano music, which is the same arc in miniature: assistant, successor, keeper. [C31600] His last feature-film score was Hidden Moon (2012, Pepe Bojorquez), a romantic drama starring Wes Bentley, Ana Serradilla, Johnathon Schaech and Linda Gray, whose score runs 57:06 on album and stands as his last feature-film score, coming two years after his previous feature and five years before his death; he also scored Elsa and Fred (2014, Michael Radford), an English-language remake of the 2005 Argentine film set and filmed in New Orleans and starring Shirley MacLaine and Christopher Plummer, reuniting him with Radford twenty years after Il Postino. [C31601] In 2015 he received a career prize (Premio alla Carriera) at the Gran Gala di Bergamo, at the Teatro Donizetti. [C31602] Treccani describes him as a classically trained musician and a deep knower of jazz, tango and South American folk traditions, and he was considered a brilliant pianist throughout his life. [C31603]

He died on 15 November 2017 in Rome, at the age of 84. [C31604]

**Takeaway:** The last act completes the arc the study opened with: the pianist becomes the arranger, the arranger becomes the film composer, the film composer becomes the orchestra director -- every chair in the room, occupied in turn, without changing handwriting. The Taranto post is not a retirement; it is the final form of the mechanism, the composer conducting other people's music the way he once arranged other singers' songs.

## 10. Aesthetic logic: the tune is the system

Bacalov's aesthetic logic is that a melody is a system, not a decoration. Six mechanisms carry it, each one a different room the same tune walks through. First, the tango is the home key, not a flavor: tango rhythm and the bandoneon as the structural spine of his concert and late film writing, showing up as a Mass, a concerto and a film theme all built on the same dance pulse. [C31605] Second, one theme in many dressings: a single lyrical melody carried through a whole score in changing instrumental clothes, so the listener hears the same tune as bandoneon solo, music box, trio, harpsichord version and guitar version, each time feeling new. [C31606] Third, melody first, atmosphere second: singable, expansive melody over a traditional orchestral base, with Latin and rock coloring, showing up as a title song you can hum, brass action writing, and mariachi-flavored set pieces -- where Morricone builds atmosphere from sound effects and unusual timbres, Bacalov builds from tune and brass. [C31607] Fourth, the rock-classical collision: a Baroque or classical form with a rock band playing the soloist's role against a symphony orchestra, showing up as hard blues alternating with 18th-century strings, and as a crime-film score that is also a prog-rock album. [C31608] Fifth, sacred collage: assembling already-sacred music from many cultures plus a few original cues, rather than composing a unified score, showing up as Bach next to a Congolese Gloria next to a blues spiritual inside a black-and-white Gospel film. [C31609] Sixth, the career arc itself: a working musician's climb through every chair in the room -- pianist, arranger, assistant, conductor, artistic director -- showing up as a filmography that touches pop songs, westerns, crime funk, Fellini and the concert hall without changing handwriting. [C31610]

The logic holds together because the mechanisms are all versions of the same move: take something that already exists -- a tango rhythm, a single melody, a Baroque form, a Bach chorus, a pop song -- and re-dress it until it fits the room it has to play in. Bacalov rarely invents from nothing; he arranges, varies, collides and reuses, and the originality is in the sequence and the casting, not in the raw material. That is why his themes survive being stolen by Tarantino and reused across his own films: a tune built to be re-dressed is built to travel. [C31611]

**Takeaway:** Steal the system, not the sound: one strong melody, many instrumental rooms; one old form, one unlikely soloist; one found sacred text, one new cut. The piece does not need more material -- it needs the same material, worn differently, until the repetition itself becomes the meaning.

## 11. Network

Fernando Di Leo is the spine of the network: Bacalov scored three Di Leo crime films -- Milano calibro 9 (1972, with Osanna), Il Boss (1973) and Mister Scarface / I padroni della citta (1976) -- and a fourth Di Leo film, La Seduzione (1973), while Di Leo wrote the westerns Bacalov scored, so the two men's careers cross in both directions across a decade. [C31612] Pier Paolo Pasolini gave him his first international recognition: The Gospel According to St. Matthew (1964), with Pasolini personally choosing the sacred collage Bacalov arranged, and the film's three Oscar nominations including Best Score. [C31613] Sergio Corbucci gave him his first western: Django (1966), Bacalov's first western film score, followed months later by Damiano Damiani's A Bullet for the General, which reused several Django themes, and Bacalov also scored the Django sequel Halleluja for Django. [C31614] Michael Radford and Massimo Troisi gave him his Oscar: Il Postino (1994), directed by Radford, co-written by and starring Troisi as Mario, who died of a heart attack on 4 June 1994, twelve hours after principal photography wrapped, having postponed necessary heart surgery to complete filming; the film was nominated for five Academy Awards and won one, Best Score for Bacalov, and won the BAFTA for Best Film Music. [C31615]

Ennio Morricone is the master he apprenticed under and spent his career measured against: Bacalov worked as Morricone's assistant in 1958; in the late 1960s Morricone and three other Italian composers -- Piero Piccioni, Armando Trovajoli and Luis Bacalov -- founded Forum Music Village (Rome), previously the Ortophonic recording studio; the 2007 album Per amore is credited to Ennio Morricone and Luis Bacalov; and Morricone's own page notes Rita Pavone's 1962 songs "Come te non c'e' nessuno" and "Pel di carota" were arranged by Luis Bacalov. [C31616] No source found this run states rivalry, mutual influence in either direction, or a personal friendship -- only the assistantship, the shared studio founding, the Pavone arrangements and the joint album credit -- and the study does not invent one. [C31617] He scored Federico Fellini's City of Women (1980), released in Italy 28 March 1980 and starring Marcello Mastroianni as Snaporaz, Fellini's first film after the death of Nino Rota; the film screened out of competition at Cannes in 1980. [C31618] Two of his songs, "The Grand Duel (Parte Prima)" and "The Summertime Killer", were used in Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill (2003), and Tarantino also used three Bacalov songs from the spaghetti western era in Django Unchained (2012): "Django" and "La Corsa (2nd Version)" from Django (1966) and "Lo Chiamavano King" from His Name Was King. [C31619] Gato Barbieri is the parallel Argentine line: the 1975 album Desbandes is credited to Gato Barbieri and Luis Bacalov (LP, Vista); Barbieri, Argentine tenor saxophonist, moved to Italy in 1962 and later scored Last Tango in Paris -- the Argentine jazz line running parallel to Bacalov's Argentine film-music line. [C31620] The Truce (1997, Francesco Rosi), released 14 February 1997 and based on Primo Levi's memoir of his journey home to Turin after the Red Army liberated Auschwitz, starring John Turturro as Levi, screened in competition at Cannes in 1997 with Bacalov credited for music; Polish Wedding (1998, Theresa Connelly), an American comedy-drama about a Polish-American family in Detroit that premiered at Sundance in January 1998, has its music credited to Luis Bacalov in the AFI Catalog, one of his first American studio assignments after the Il Postino Oscar; and Sea of Dreams (2006, Jose Bojorquez), a Mexican-set drama, has a score described as very dreamy, with pan-pipes and slow melodies in the manner of Morricone, but with Mediterranean instrumental references similar to Il Postino. [C31621]

**Takeaway:** The network is a relay, not a circle: Morricone hands him the assistant's chair, Corbucci hands him the western, Di Leo hands him the crime film, Pasolini hands him the sacred, Radford and Troisi hand him the Oscar -- and at every handoff Bacalov keeps the same handwriting, which is why the chain reads as one composer's arc rather than five different careers.

## 12. What was not heard: the honest gaps

No score audio was listened to in this study and no film was viewed, so every musical description rests on written reviews, track listings and liner notes, not on hearing the scores -- a reader who listens to the Django title theme or the Osanna record will know more about the sound than this study can claim. [C31622] Two scores Bacalov wrote were never used, and the study records them because rejection is part of the mechanism: B. Monkey (1998, Michael Radford) -- Bacalov wrote and recorded a full score in 1998 at Forum Music Village, Rome, conducting it himself, but it was rejected and replaced by Jennie Muskett; and Chocolat (2000) -- Rachel Portman was the first composer, was dropped, and Bacalov was brought in and played some piano ideas, but Portman was brought back before Bacalov recorded any of his score. [C31623] The same fan-run rejected-scores site also reports the Il Postino backstory that an unnamed first composer walked off after a disagreement with the orchestra, Morricone was asked and declined, and his suggestion -- Bacalov -- was used; that anecdote is unverified elsewhere and the study carries it as lore, not fact. [C31624] The full filmography was not enumerated: Wikipedia's "selected filmography" lists about 66 titles while Qobuz credits well over 112 films, so dozens of minor titles (TV films, French and Spanish work, early 1960s comedies) are uncatalogued here, and "The Wind's Fierce" could not be verified as a Bacalov score and is not catalogued, nor is the "Storm Rider" credit in the composers list, which was not traced to a specific film. [C31625]

**Takeaway:** The gaps are the shape of the study: a composer heard through paper, not through speakers. The mechanisms are named from what the sources describe, and the next study that actually listens will either confirm them or correct them -- either outcome is useful, and the study says so plainly rather than pretending the listening happened.
