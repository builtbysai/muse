# Claudio Simonetti: Study Notes

**Site:** Claudio Simonetti (b. Tuesday 19 February 1952, Sao Paulo, Brazil; living), Italian composer and keyboardist, the keyboardist of Goblin and Dario Argento's composer since Profondo rosso (1975) -- the prog-rock keyboardist who was handed a film score with one night to write it, made the theme a record, spent a decade writing horror on synthesisers, and now, at seventy-four, plays his own catalogue back to the films it came from, live, on a stage, with the arrangements unchanged. Claudio Simonetti was born in Sao Paulo on Tuesday 19 February 1952, to an Italian family, and lived in Brazil until he was eleven -- "I was born in Brazil and I lived there until I was 11 years old, so I have the rhythm in my blood", he told Fangoria in November 2026, and the sentence is the whole of his second career in miniature. His father was Enrico Simonetti, composer and entertainer, the man who would bring his son's band to Cinevox in 1974. The family returned to Italy when the boy was eleven -- no source this run gives a reason for the move or an exact year, and the study does not assert one -- and the Brazilian rhythm stayed in the Roman keyboardist, waiting eight years to come out as disco. [C29892]

He studied composition and piano at the Conservatorio di Santa Cecilia in Rome, and named his keyboard influences plainly: Keith Emerson, Brian Auger, Rick Wakeman, Tony Banks. The debut came in 1968, as keyboardist in the beat group Marco & gli Eremiti, recording a 45 for Roman Record Company; then Il Ritratto di Dorian Gray with drummer Walter Martino, playing the Pop di Caracalla festival in 1971; then, in 1972-73, the demo tapes as Oliver, with Massimo Morante on guitar, Fabio Pignatelli on bass and Martino on drums. The band went to London, met Yes producer Eddie Offord, rehearsed for months with drummer Carlo Bordini and English vocalist Clive Haynes, failed to land a deal, ran out of money and came home. The London failure is the hinge of the origin story: the prog band that could not sell itself in England came back to Italy and became a film band instead. [C29893]

Back in Italy they signed to Cinevox -- "thanks to my father Enrico who brought us to the Cinevox Record label in 1974", in Simonetti's own words -- and the label renamed them Cherry Five, "for no apparent reason", as the members had intended to continue as Oliver. The first LP, Cherry Five (Cinevox, 1975), was a straight symphonic-prog record in the Genesis/Yes lineage, six long tracks sung in English by Tony Tartarini, Simonetti and Morante credited as composers of all of them; it sold poorly, and was shelved when Bordini refused to sign with Cinevox, surfacing only in January 1976, after Profondo Rosso had already made Goblin famous. The name was not theirs, the record was not a hit, and the label kept them busy arranging other musicians' compositions -- the Cinevox session work that taught the band soundtrack technique before they ever scored a film of their own. [C29894]

Then Giorgio Gaslini left Profondo rosso. Argento had hired the jazz composer; after three or four days of recording Gaslini left after a conflict with Argento, and Argento gave the band one night to write a score and one day to record it -- ten days for the whole process, three of Gaslini's themes retained, Gaslini wrongly given full composer credit in the original theatrical release. The band changed its name again, to Goblin, to distinguish the new release from the Cherry Five LP about to be marketed. The famous main line is played on harpsichord, not synthesiser -- Simonetti's own correction, in the 2005 Cinevox Collector's Edition liner notes -- over Mellotron, Elka organ, Logan violin, celesta, Fender Rhodes, grand piano and Moog, Gaslini's jazz and blues-funk fused with the band's symphonic-rock Baroque hybrid. The record sold, by Wikipedia's account, over a million copies in its first year and stayed 52 weeks on the Italian charts -- figures the study carries as Wikipedia-only, the sentence carrying a [where?] tag -- and John Carpenter later told Simonetti his Halloween score of 1978 was directly inspired by it: "I stole all your music." [C29895]

Roller (1976) followed, the band's only non-soundtrack studio album of the seventies, with Agostino Marangolo replacing Martino on drums and Maurizio Guarini joining as second keyboardist; the band was unhappy with the result and with Cinevox's promotion, and the discord nearly broke the group up until Argento intervened and kept them together for Suspiria. This time the band had three months, not ten days. Simonetti made demos from the script, Argento played them on set to help the actors, and then Simonetti scrapped the script-based demos -- "when you write the music from the script, it doesn't work" -- and reworked everything to the finished film's mood. The palette explodes beyond prog rock: bouzouki, because Argento had discovered it on a four-day trip to Athens and the coven originates in Greece; Indian tabla drums; celesta and bells; a big Moog, hired because Simonetti idolised Keith Emerson, with Moog expert Felice Fugazza brought in to operate it -- FACT says a rare System 50, FilmInk quotes Simonetti saying System 100, and the study does not resolve the model. The main theme is a repetitive six-note stepwise motif on celesta and bells over a D-minor drone, the sweetness of childhood innocence set against a raspy Sprechstimme voice half-singing, half-retching the motif and periodically shrieking "WITCH!". Argento's brief was that the audience should feel the witches are still there even when they are not on the screen, and the score deliberately avoids sync points -- it fights the film's pacing rather than following it, which is part of its force. [C29896]

Zombi -- Romero's Dawn of the Dead, Argento's European cut -- came in 1978, Minimoog and Roland System 100 and ARP, a warped disco groove for a mall full of the dead, and then the band split. Morante left for a solo career, Simonetti left to become a solo soundtrack composer, and Pignatelli kept the name -- which is why Tenebre (1982) is credited to the three individuals, Simonetti, Morante and Pignatelli, with no drummer at all, only drum machines. "I produced a lot of Italian disco, so I decided to do something disco rock -- electronic with rock music", Simonetti said of Tenebre, and the title theme, vocoder and all, is what he calls one of horror disco's greatest achievements. [C29897]

The disco was not a detour; it was the second face. "Beginning in 1978, Simonetti founded a number of electronic disco projects", and he puts it himself as a split identity: "I have two faces like Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde -- I play horror movies and dance music, maybe because I was born in Brazil and I lived there until I was 11 years old, so I have the rhythm in my blood." With producer Giancarlo Meo he formed the project behind singer Vivien Vee -- "Give Me a Break" became a minor hit in the American dance charts in 1979, "Blue Disease" in 1983 carried the darker Goblin sound -- plus Easy Going ("Baby I Love You" 1978, "Fear" 1979) and, in 1983, the Italo-disco group Crazy Gang. He worked in disco "from '78 to '82 or '83", then stopped -- "the best period for me was the end of the '70s and early '80s. After that, people started doing disco everywhere, but also because of new technology with computers and sequencers" -- and turned back to soundtracks with Phenomena, Demons and Opera. The disco fluency is the direct source of the Tenebre drum-machine sound: the horror composer learned his pulse on the dance floor, and the dance floor learned its dread from him. [C29898]

The solo scores of the eighties are each a different system, and that is the point -- "I always try to do something new every time. I don't like to repeat myself." Conquest (1983), for Lucio Fulci, is all synthesiser except for one flute track: long slow notes, arpeggios, unearthly timbres, a mood piece rather than a theme, the electronics often sounding out of place against the images, which the reviewers note is the point -- Fulci's hazy, dreamlike film gave Simonetti licence to experiment. Demons (1985), for Lamberto Bava and produced by Argento, was the first score he composed essentially alone, few repeated cues, synth-bass throbbing against licensed metal. Phenomena (1985) used only two original Goblin members, Simonetti and Pignatelli, and its title track straddles power metal and trance; Opera (1987) is built around a soprano voice over elegant piano, then pivots to propulsive rock and programmed drums. The Church (1989), for Michele Soavi, and Dracula 3D (2012) are thinly sourced -- no track-level or gear detail was found for either, and the study invents none. Two corrections belong here, because the brief carried them wrong: The Stendhal Syndrome (1996) was scored by Ennio Morricone, not Simonetti, and The Sect (1991) was scored by Pino Donaggio, not Simonetti -- between Opera (1987) and Sleepless (2001) he did no Argento feature score at all, and Inferno (1980), the middle Three Mothers film, was Keith Emerson's. [C29899]

The nineties and after are reunions, splits, and the name. He founded the heavy-metal band Daemonia in 1999, recording updated versions of Goblin favourites; Goblin reformed once, for Argento's Sleepless (2001), and failed to appear at the scheduled Cult-Con show -- Simonetti told fans "old wounds resurfaced during their brief reunion" -- and disbanded again. Morante and Pignatelli reformed Goblin without him as Back to the Goblin (2005); New Goblin formed in March 2010 with Simonetti back in, toured the world in 2011-12, and in the fall of 2013 Simonetti left with Daemonia's rhythm section to form Claudio Simonetti's Goblin, while Pignatelli and Marangolo formed Goblin Rebirth. Since 2013-14 there have been three Goblins, and the study keeps them straight: Simonetti's, on tour now; Goblin Legacy, Guarini's line-up, whose drummer Walter Martino -- the original Goblin drummer, back in September 2024 -- died in Livorno on Saturday 7 March 2026, aged 72, six months into the return; and Goblin Rebirth, inactive for live performance since September 2024. Massimo Morante, the co-founder, died in Rome on Thursday 23 June 2022, aged 69 -- not 70, as NME, Loudwire, Crack and AllMusic say; the dates give 69, and the study uses 69. [C29900]

The re-performer phase began in earnest in 2014, when Claudio Simonetti's Goblin started touring playing complete scores live to screenings of Deep Red, Suspiria and Dawn of the Dead. The re-recordings are not remasters but re-performances: Murder Collection (2015), The Very Best Of volumes (2019, 2021), The Devil Is Back (2019), and for the 45th anniversary of Suspiria (2022) the Suspiria Rock Prog Version, the 1977 score reframed as a prog-rock record and toured live-to-picture in the USA and Canada to sold-out rooms. The live rig is modern -- Roland, digital Mellotron, Moog Voyager, Nord -- and the arrangements are not reworked: "we didn't make new arrangements or change something because people loved this music in this way. And why change?" A technical enabler makes the live score possible: modern plug-ins separate voice from music in the film's audio, leaving clean dialogue and background sounds without the music. Mother of Tears (2007), the close of Argento's Three Mothers trilogy, shows the other late turn: 49 individual cues over four months, a deliberate classical style with Gothic choruses, and an end-title song, "Mater Lacrimarum", recorded with Daemonia and Cradle of Filth's Dani Filth, who recorded his vocals remotely after Simonetti sent a demo imitating the voice he wanted. [C29901]

The present tense is a tour, and it is happening now. The Tenebre soundtrack was newly recorded and reimagined and released on 28 August 2026, ten tracks, thirty-nine minutes, on his own Deep Red label; the North American Tenebre tour runs 21 October-23 November 2026, the score played live to a screening of the film newly remastered in 4K, followed after an intermission by a second set of Goblin greatest hits -- Seattle 7 November, San Francisco 10 November, Atlanta 17 November, Tampa 18-19 November, Nashville tonight, Saturday 21 November, at the Eastside Bowl at 20:00, Greenville 22 November, Asheville 23 November sold out. Then Ravenna on 8 December, Rome's Forum Theatre on 17-18 December for the Suspiria 50th Anniversary Preview, and 2027 dates already booked -- Ghent 13 February, Bologna 18 February, Forli 7 May. His most recent film score, Saturnalia, he told Fangoria he made "in 2024", while English Wikipedia's filmography lists 2025 -- shot 2024, released 2025, most likely, and the study does not resolve it by picking one. He prefers the stage now: "I like to work on films, but I think that now it's not the same... I prefer now to do my live shows. Because films, there are too many... most of the time you don't care about anything that is connected with the music." And on the machines: AI "will destroy music and entertainment for sure." [C29902]

The arc is a single line with two hinges and no reversal: the prog keyboardist of the early seventies, the band scorer of the middle seventies, the solo synth scorer and disco man of the eighties, the working composer of the lean years, the late Argento reunion of the 2000s, and the live re-performer from 2013 on -- the same music, first written in one night, then written alone, then played back to the film it came from, on a stage, with the arrangements unchanged and the instruments new. The place is never decoration: Sao Paulo until eleven, the Conservatorio di Santa Cecilia, the Cinevox offices where the young Frizzi met the young Simonetti, the big church in Rome where the organ was recorded, and now the stage with the film behind him and the audience in front, buying the record the next day the way they did in 1975. ## M1 -- The one night: a deadline as the instrument [C29908]

Gaslini left after three or four days of recording, and Argento gave the band one night to write a score and one day to record it. Ten days for the whole process, three of Gaslini's themes retained, the rest theirs. The mechanism is the deadline as the composing instrument: the theme had to be simple enough to write in a night and memorable enough to carry a film, so it came out repetitive, harpsichord-led, instantly hummable -- a tune that could be written fast because it was built to be repeated. The same gamble repeats at the start of every Argento score that follows: the director fires the safe choice, hands an unknown quantity one night, and the constraint does the composing. Carpenter stole the result for Halloween, which is the mechanism's proof: a theme written in one night became the template for the next decade of horror scoring. [C29909]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one night, one deadline, one theme -- a clock running down while a simple line is written, and the line, once written, refusing to leave the room. [C29910]

## M2 -- The theme that is a record: the score as a hit single [C29911]

Profondo Rosso reached number one in Italy in singles and LP, and the Zombi album outsold the film in several European territories. The mechanism is the score that behaves as a record: the main theme is not laid under the film, it is released from it, a repetitive line with a hook, a groove, a dance-floor pulse, so that people heard the music in the cinema and went the next day to buy the record -- Simonetti's own account of how it worked in the seventies and eighties, and his own account of why it stopped working. The film is the advertisement for the theme, and the theme is the thing that survives the film. [C29912]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a record first and a film cue second -- one theme, repeated until it is a hit, the film playing behind it like a sleeve. [C29913]

## M3 -- The sweet against the violent: celesta, bells, and a voice that retches [C29914]

Suspiria's main theme is a six-note stepwise motif on celesta and bells over a D-minor drone, the sound of childhood innocence and magic -- the Nutcracker association is the point -- set against a Sprechstimme voice half-singing, half-retching the motif and periodically shrieking "WITCH!". Argento's brief was that the audience should feel the witches are still there even when they are not on the screen. The mechanism is the sweet set against the violent, and Simonetti states it as a rule: "I want something sweet, even if the scene is violent" was Argento's direction for Tenebre, so a lullaby plays behind a flashback murder, and in Opera a soprano sings over elegant piano while the violence happens underneath -- "If you listened to this song, you'd never think about horror movies, but it works with the violence scenes." The prettiness is not decoration; it is the threat, because the audience trusts it first. [C29915]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one beautiful thing and one violent thing at the same time -- a music box over a murder, a lullaby over a flashback, a soprano over a killing -- and the beauty must come first, so the violence lands on trust. [C29916]

## M4 -- The invented sound: no samplers, so make the sound [C29917]

In 1977 no samplers existed, so the band recorded sounds they created themselves. The mechanism is invention under constraint: the bouzouki because Argento found it in Athens and the coven comes from Greece; the tabla because the score needed a pulse no drum kit could give; the big Moog, hired because Simonetti idolised Keith Emerson, with Felice Fugazza brought in to operate it; the celesta and bells because the motif needed the sound of a music box and nothing else would do. The score avoids sync points on purpose -- it fights the film's pacing rather than following it -- because a sound invented for the scene does not need to land on the cut; it needs to be in the room before the cut happens. The disagreement in the sources over the Moog model -- System 50 in FACT, System 100 in Simonetti's own words to FilmInk -- is itself evidence of the mechanism: the machine mattered less than the fact that it was big, rare, and had to be played by an expert brought in for the day. [C29918]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one sound nobody has heard before, made from something in the room -- an instrument from the wrong country, a voice used as percussion, a machine too big for the studio -- and the sound should arrive before the image it belongs to. [C29919]

## M5 -- The two faces: horror on one side, disco on the other [C29920]

"I have two faces like Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde -- I play horror movies and dance music." The mechanism is the double life as a technique: from 1978 to 1983 Simonetti ran Italo-disco projects -- Vivien Vee, Easy Going, Crazy Gang -- learning the pulse, the sequencer, the vocoder, the four-on-the-floor, and then he carried the whole apparatus back into horror. Tenebre has no drummer at all, only drum machines -- the personnel list gives Oberheim DMX and Roland TR-808, the Roland interview quotes Simonetti saying Linn, and the study does not resolve the machine -- plus a Roland VP-330 Vocoder Plus, a Roland Jupiter-8, a Roland MC-4 sequencer, and cues deliberately played three to four minutes long so Argento's long takes could breathe. The disco did not dilute the horror; it gave the horror its pulse, a dance-friendly edge under slashing guitars and gothic organ swirls, so that the murder mystery moves like a club record. He stopped the disco when computers and sequencers made it something everybody did -- and kept the machines. [C29921]

**Takeaway:** the piece should have two faces at once -- a dance pulse under a murder, a vocoder over a scream -- and the dance floor should be the thing that makes the horror move. [C29922]

## M6 -- The name kept by the bass player: who owns a band [C29923]

The band split in 1978, and "the bass player kept the name" -- Simonetti's own account, to Fangoria in November 2026. The mechanism is the name as contested property: Pignatelli led the band into the eighties, so Tenebre (1982) is credited to three individuals and not to Goblin at all; Simonetti left New Goblin in the fall of 2013 and formed Claudio Simonetti's Goblin, while Pignatelli and Marangolo formed Goblin Rebirth; Morante, Guarini, Pignatelli and Marangolo released Four of a Kind as "Goblin" in 2014; and since then there have been three Goblins, each with a claim, each with a line-up, each with a different answer to the question of who the band is. The study keeps them straight -- Simonetti's on tour now, Goblin Legacy rebuilding under Guarini, Goblin Rebirth inactive since September 2024 -- because the sources do not: English Wikipedia's infobox still lists a stale composite naming no single real line-up. The music is the same either way; the argument is over whose hands play it. [C29924]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one band with three names -- the same theme played by three different line-ups, each claiming the name, the audience deciding which one is real. [C29925]

## M7 -- The score played to the film, live: the catalogue as repertoire [C29926]

Since February 2014 Claudio Simonetti's Goblin has toured playing complete scores live to screenings -- Deep Red, Suspiria, Dawn of the Dead, Demons in 2023, Tenebre in 2026 -- and the mechanism is the catalogue become repertoire. The three-man, drum-machine original of Tenebre is adapted for six musicians on stage, "we are trying more or less to make the sound remain the same. But maybe with the real drum, it's better" -- and the arrangements are not reworked, because "people loved this music in this way. And why change?" The modern instruments keep the vintage sound -- Roland, digital Mellotron, Moog Voyager, Nord -- and modern plug-ins separate the dialogue from the music in the film's audio, so the band can play over clean dialogue. He prefers the stage now, because in the seventies and eighties people heard the music in the cinema and went the next day to buy the record, and the live show is the last room where that still happens. [C29927]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a film with its score played live in front of it -- the screen behind, the band in front, the same notes, the same arrangement, fifty years later, and the audience hearing the record they bought in 1975 being made again in the room. [C29928]

## M8 -- The first theme is the one: ten themes, choose the first [C29929]

"The most difficult part for me is to choose the right mood for the film, because I might do 10 different themes and then I choose the first one." The mechanism is the first idea as the right idea, and the method is to compose to the finished, edited film, never from the script: "We saw the film finished, and we started playing music over the scenes. I prefer to work this way, because if you work off a script... you never have the real mood." The Suspiria demos prove the rule from the other side -- written from the script, played on set, then scrapped, because a theme written to the script does not know the film's mood yet. And the corollary is never to repeat himself: each film gets a different system -- harpsichord and church organ for Deep Red, celesta and ethnic instruments for Suspiria, vocoder and drum machine for Tenebre, a soprano for Opera, a classical turn with Gothic choruses for Mother of Tears -- so that the first theme is always a new first theme. [C29930]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be ten themes written and nine thrown away -- the first one kept, the film finished before the music starts, and the mood chosen once and never revised. [C29931]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Simonetti is not a composer who writes to a film; he is a keyboardist who writes to a finished film, under a deadline, with a sound he has to invent because the machine he wants does not exist yet -- and then he plays the result back, first as a record, then, fifty years later, live to the picture. Early he is the prog keyboardist, long forms and Mellotron textures, the band that could not sell itself in London; then he is the one-night composer, the theme that is a record, the sweet set against the violent; then he is the two-faced man, disco on one side and horror on the other, the drum machine inside the giallo; late he is the re-performer, the catalogue as repertoire, the same notes on new instruments, the arrangements unchanged because the audience loved them that way. The place is never decoration: Sao Paulo until eleven, the conservatory, the Cinevox offices, the big church in Rome where the organ was recorded, the stage with the film behind him. His own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one he states in the Fangoria interview: do something new every time, do not repeat yourself, compose to the finished film, choose the first theme -- and now, prefer the live show, because the record no longer sells the day after the film. The work is the deadline, the theme, and the replay -- and the replay is the same at twenty-three and at seventy-four, only the room it fills keeps changing. [C29932]
