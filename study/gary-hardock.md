# Gary Hardock: Study Notes

**Site:** Gary Hardock is a Canadian human-computer interaction researcher whose entire published output is three items in three years: a sole-authored Graphics Interface '91 paper, a 1993 M.Sc. thesis at the University of Toronto, and the UIST '93 paper that made his name, "A Marking Based Interface for Collaborative Writing," co-authored with Gordon Kurtenbach and William Buxton. He took a B.A.Sc. in Systems Design Engineering at the University of Waterloo and an M.Sc. in Computer Science at the University of Toronto (1993), working in the Input Research Group in the Computer Systems Research Institute under Buxton's direction; his UIST '93 byline gives the Department of Computer Science, Toronto, and his GI '91 byline gives the Computer Systems Research Institute with the email gary@dgp.toronto.edu. After 1993 he vanishes from the public record: no further publication, no PhD, no later affiliation was found in any source read this run -- his last traces are acknowledgments, thanked for user testing in Kurtenbach, Moran & Buxton, "Contextual animation of gestural commands," Graphics Interface '94, and named in Narine et al., "Building a Media-Space across Wide-Area General Purpose Networks" (DGP, circa 1994). Treat the post-1993 absence as unrecorded, not as a departure from the field; no source says where he went. His birth date and birthplace are not established in any source read; do not assert either. No DBLP author page, Google Scholar profile, or ACM author profile for Hardock was located; the publication list below rests on the Input Research Group bibliography, the Crossref record for DOI 10.1145/168642.168669, and Buxton's papers index, which all agree. [C37177]

**Relevance:** Hardock helps investigate where the expensive method chosen for meaning survives when the cheapest method always wins: his mark is a stroke that costs more than a menu click but carries its own argument -- scope and operation in a single gesture -- and stays visible as a message to a person while remaining executable as a command to the machine. The evidence is MATE's deferred-command mechanism (a mark left visible is an annotation; the same mark tapped later is an edit), the order-independent undo that falls out of it, and the marking-menu rehearsal mechanism by which a novice's slow menu selection becomes an expert's fast mark. [C37178]

**Doorway:** The 458th doorway. Moran asked: when the cheapest method always wins, where does the expensive method survive -- the one chosen for meaning rather than speed -- and who keeps it alive? Hardock answers from the pen side: the expensive method survives as a mark, because a mark is not a method at all but a message that can also be executed -- the proofreader's stroke costs more than a menu command and says more, carrying its scope, its operation, and its author's intent in one visible line, and it stays on the page until someone chooses to act on it. The open question carried forward is: when one mark must speak to both a person and a machine, who decides what it means when the two disagree -- the writer, the reader, or the recogniser? [C37179]

## M1 -- The mark vocabulary: three commands and a comment (1991-1993)

MATE, the Mark-Up Annotator / Text Editor, is deliberately small. As a prototype it supports only three editing commands -- delete, move, and insert -- plus navigation functions; text entry is by keyboard and the stylus only marks. The vocabulary is borrowed from the proofreader's desk and from GEdit, Kurtenbach and Buxton's graphical editor (UIST '91), whose recognition software MATE reuses. Delete has four candidate strokes shown in GI '91 Figure 1 -- cross-out, "pig-tail," stroke-out, and select-and-delete -- with no discussion of which one MATE implements; the UIST '93 figures show a delete as a word struck through with a diagonal cross. Move is the one mark described precisely, in both papers and visible in the figures: circle the text to be moved, then extend the encircling line in one continuous stroke to the destination point. UIST '93 Figure 2 shows the words "from the middle of the paragraph" circled with a tail line dropping to a caret-like insertion point at the end of the paragraph, captioned "(a) A user draws a move command. (b) After lifting the pen, the editing command is performed" -- the command executes on pen-up. Insert is named as a supported command in both papers and shown being executed in Figure 4, but the stroke shape for insert is never specified in the text of either paper; do not invent a caret, however obvious the inference. Copy exists in MATE's vocabulary -- GI '91 says the current version "has borrowed the Move, Copy, and Delete commands from GEdit" -- but UIST '93's own description lists only delete, move, and insert; in GEdit, copy is the move mark plus a small "C" added to the end of the marking. [C37181]

The vocabulary's grammar is scope first, operator second. Circling is the preferred scope mark: a closed loop specifies a collective scope in one continuous motion, and "once the loop is connected both the system and user know that the scoping part of the command is complete." Bracketing (start and end brackets separately) and highlighting (drag-through, warned against because it "could be confused with the Deletion or Underline markings") are the alternatives. GEdit adds the refinements MATE inherits: circle alone with no operator leaves a small square "handle" at the end of the encircling line, and a later command stroke starting inside the handle applies to that scope; nested circles toggle inclusion and exclusion (outermost is inclusion); disjoint circles select disjoint sets, each with its own handle. And alongside the executable marks sit the comment annotations, which are explicitly not commands and need not be recognised: freeform handwritten notes such as "reword this paragraph," visible in the figures as the word "Reword" scrawled diagonally in the margin. "These comments need not be recognized by the computer, as they are only intended to be understood by a collaborator." [C37182]

**Takeaway:** Three commands, one scope grammar, and a deliberate remainder -- the comment that no machine will ever parse. [C37183]

## M2 -- Deferred versus immediate: the annotation is a command not yet run (1991-1993)

The whole system turns on one sentence from the UIST '93 paper, worth quoting because it is the mechanism: "The fact that marking commands to the computer application are visible is the key. If, instead of immediately interpreting a marking command, the computer does not process the mark but simply leaves the mark visible, the mark can be thought of as an annotation. From this point of view, annotations are deferred edit commands. In terms of the different modes of MATE, annotation mode can also be called deferred mode, and edit mode is immediate mode." The framing of the problem is equally direct: "in the first case a person is communicating with another person, whereas in the second case a person is communicating with a computer. The goal then becomes to design a method in which a person can communicate to both another person and to a computer application." [C37184]

The modes are three. Edit, or immediate, mode: a mark is interpreted the moment it is completed and the command executes at once -- the Figure 2 move executes on pen lift. Annotate, or deferred, mode: marks are not interpreted; they stay on the document as annotations. "In this mode a collaborator can specify suggested changes by marking up the document. As with paper documents and Freestyle, markings are not interpreted as commands. They are treated strictly as annotations. These marks do not necessarily have to be editing commands. For example, a marking could be something as vague as 'reword this paragraph'." View/select/edit, or incorporation, mode: two side-by-side views of the same document. The left view is the marked-up document, each reviewer's annotations in a different colour, new annotations addable but the underlying text never changed; the right view is the current clean version, no annotations visible, any mark made there interpreted as a command and executed immediately. The views "work in concert": tap an annotation in the left view and the system performs it, and the result appears in the right view. [C37185]

**Takeaway:** Visibility is a time machine -- a visible command can wait, and waiting is what turns a command into an annotation. [C37186]

## M3 -- Incorporation: tap to execute, tap to undo, in any order (1993)

Everything MATE does for the author follows from the two views plus persistent marks. Do by selection: tap a mark in the annotation view to perform it, with feedback in the thickness of the line -- executed marks become thin, unexecuted marks stay thick ("Annotations that have been 'executed' appear as thin lines (eg., 'ed'). Annotations that have not been executed appear as thick lines. Annotations are colour coded according to who made them"). This works only because "both the user and the computer can understand the editing command identified by the marking." The thinning is a history, but a spatial one rather than a chronological one: "the locations of the annotations immediately tell us what text the commands have been applied to," while "we do not know when or in what order" -- and order does not matter. [C37187]

Undo by selection is the consequence the paper is proudest of: tap an executed mark again to un-incorporate it, in any order. "Such a mechanism for undoing text-editing commands has never been built before." Figure 5 shows it in four panels over the sentence "Note that in the edit view the text is different": (a) initial text, (b) after deletion, (c) after move, (d) after undoing the delete. "It does not matter whether the delete or move command was performed first, either command can be directly undone. Note that the annotations correspond to a specific piece of text, not the position of the annotation." Do plus undo by selection gives previewing: "This allows the user to try 'what if' scenarios... Note that the user need not be the principal author. It could be used by a collaborator wanting to see the results if the annotations were incorporated." The cost of a what-if is a tap. [C37188]

**Takeaway:** When every command stays visible on the page, history is a place, not a timeline -- and undo is pointing at the thing you want back. [C37189]

## M4 -- The broken move: placeholders as visible buffers (1991-1993)

The most original vocabulary item in either paper solves a move that crosses a page boundary. A move across pages is split into two marks joined by a shared symbol. UIST '93 Figure 6 shows it in two panels: top, source text circled with a line arcing to a star/asterisk symbol ("This is source text which is moved to 'star'"); bottom, a line arcing from the star to the destination ("This is the destination to which 'star' is moved to"). The paper then generalizes the star: "Instead [of] using a 'star' symbol to move to and from, we use a marking menu containing various symbols termed placeholders." Reframed as two commands -- move-to-buffer-with-ID and move-from-buffer-with-ID -- this allows multiple text buffers, each with a unique placeholder symbol. "The user chooses which placeholder to move text into, thus providing a strong connection between the placeholder and the text it contains. Also, because the placeholders containing text are visible, it is trivial to determine what text is contained in a placeholder buffer." GI '91 adds that placeholders double as bookmarks -- "a command to go to the page containing a certain placeholder would be useful" -- and likens them to a Macintosh clipboard except that any number can exist. [C37190]

**Takeaway:** A buffer you can see is a buffer you can trust -- the placeholder makes the invisible clipboard into a mark on the page. [C37191]

## M5 -- Marking menus: the menu that teaches itself (1991-1994)

MATE's second mechanism is borrowed whole from Kurtenbach and Buxton, and it is the mechanism that answers Moran's cost question from the other side: a marking menu is a pie menu whose cursor path leaves an ink trail, so selecting a sector draws the mark. Two access paths, one gesture: press-and-wait about a third of a second and the radial menu pops up under the pen tip, stroke toward the item; or draw immediately without waiting and the same directional stroke selects without any menu appearing. The physical movement is identical in both paths, so every novice menu selection rehearses the expert mark -- the mechanism is named by three principles: self-revelation, guidance, rehearsal. The testbed was GEdit, where shorthand strokes create and position squares, circles, and triangles (stroke direction is the object type, stroke start is the centre), and marks also delete, move, and copy. Marks are non-mnemonic, so the menu is what makes them learnable. [C37192]

The numbers are the best-measured in the whole cluster. Pie menus themselves (Callahan, Hopkins, Weiser & Shneiderman, CHI '88, 24 subjects, 8-item menus) were about 15 percent faster than linear menus with a lower error rate, selection time nearly independent of which item was chosen. Kurtenbach, Sellen & Buxton (Human-Computer Interaction 8(1), 1993) found that for menus up to 5 slices, mark selection time and error rate equalled visible pie-menu selection, and with even numbers of slices up to 12 items still gave acceptable performance; odd numbers were slower and more error-prone. The CHI '94 case study is the headline: two ConEd users over 18+ hours and 5,237 selections -- user A used marks for 93.4 percent of 3,013 selections, user B 55 percent of 1,945 -- and the transition is not one-way: after lay-offs both users fell back to the menu to refresh memory, and the dips shrank with experience. Speed: user A mark 0.18 s versus menu 1.097 s (7x; 4.2x even with the 0.33 s pop-up delay subtracted); user B 0.404 s versus 1.543 s (4x; 3.0x excluding delay); the abstract's headline is 3.5x faster on average. Frequency predicts marking: correlation between command frequency and mark-use frequency r-squared 0.81 (A) and 0.88 (B). A third ConEd user made 16,026 selections in 36 hours and finished 3.66 hours sooner by marking -- though the InterCHI '93 hierarchic paper cites the same user as about 15,000 selections saving 1.25 hours, a different calculation basis, reported as printed and not reconciled. Hierarchic marking menus (InterCHI '93, 12 subjects, Momenta pen computer) hold up to breadth 8 and depth 2, or 4-item menus to depth 4, with error under 10 percent; beyond that, off-axis (diagonal) items fail first -- put frequent items on the axes. Marking menus are inappropriate when menu contents change dynamically, because users can never graduate to marks. [C37193]

MATE uses marking menus twice, for operations on marks and for navigation. Figure 7, the menu for operations on annotation markings, is a four-way cross: centre circle, up is Erase, left is GoBack, right is GoTo, down is Hide. The rule: "If the user taps on the mark, it is done or undone. If, however, the user makes a mark starting from an existing mark it is interpreted as a marking menu selection." Figure 8, the navigation menu, is centre circle, left GoBack, right GoTo, up "Move line to here," down "Move line to here" -- the up and down labels are literally identical in the figure as rendered, and the paper does not explain the distinction in text; report it as shown, not as "move up / move down." [C37194]

**Takeaway:** The same stroke is the lesson and the shortcut -- the menu disappears exactly as fast as you stop needing it. [C37195]

## M6 -- Line-driven versus point-and-select: the command on the object (1991)

GI '91 makes the case for the line against the mouse, borrowing three benefits from Wolf & Morrel-Samuels (1987): "Line-markings can specify both a command and its arguments, often in a single motion"; "Temporal ordering of commands and syntactic information can be conveyed by the spatial form of the markings"; "Line-markings allow a more direct form of direct manipulation than the 'point and select' interfaces of mouse-based systems. Instead of first choosing an object and then a command, the command is specified directly on the object." The paper's timed anecdote makes the cost concrete: entering two equations takes "only about 10 seconds to write them out by hand, but over a minute to enter these using MathType and Microsoft Word on an Apple Macintosh," most of it menu and cursor switching, and "the cognitive load of performing these subtasks is the major drawback of the point-and-select method." The figure-ground distinction does the perceptual work: "the type-written text tends to be viewed as background, while the line-markings stand out. This distinction is easily noticed in typewritten documents annotated with a pen. The benefit of this distinction is that the markings are easily distinguished from the text." And a cursor becomes unnecessary: with line-driven input, "positional information is given" by the mark itself, so the editor never has to decide where the insertion point should sit after a navigation jump. The paper is careful about where the line fails: navigation commands and general system commands (file handling, Quit, Undo, Save) are judged inappropriate for line-marking, and the solutions discussed are special symbols for no-argument commands, a special window area, or the non-dominant hand -- buttons for Undo and the like, with the added benefit that "a command can be undone or canceled before it is completed thus taking advantage of the parallelism of two-handed input." The governing rule throughout: "entering commands should not disrupt the user" -- no large hand movements away from the area of interest to reach a menu or button. Two-handed input gets its own section: the touch tablet in the non-dominant hand for page-turning and scrolling (brushing a finger turns pages; left-right turns pages, forward-back scrolls), citing Buxton & Myers (1986) on two-handed performance gains, and the pen-and-paper analogy is invoked deliberately -- the non-dominant hand turns pages, moves paper, holds straight-edges. A terminology footnote is worth carrying: most literature calls these "gestures," but "the common definition of gesture is 'an expressive movement of part of the body'... A gesture may leave a mark, but the mark itself is not a gesture." The usage follows Buxton (1990): "line-driven" interfaces respond to the line left behind, as opposed to "gestural" interfaces which respond to the movement itself. [C37196]

**Takeaway:** Point, then choose, then act -- or draw the act on the thing itself, in one stroke, and skip the choosing. [C37197]

## M7 -- Recognition: what the papers do not say (1991-1993)

The honest headline first: neither Hardock paper describes a recognition algorithm. No classifier, template set, feature list, accuracy figure, or training procedure appears in either paper. The contribution relative to Wang Freestyle is precisely that MATE does recognise -- "With Freestyle, the copy of the document that is distributed is only a 'dumb' snap-shot of the original. Annotations are integrated as a separate layer to the 'snap-shot.' There is no recognition of the markings" -- versus "Our contribution is to incorporate mark recognition into the system..." -- but the recogniser is GEdit's. GI '91 acknowledges it: Hardock thanks "William Buxton and Gordon Kurtenbach who provided many useful insights, ideas, and the recognition software from GEdit." The GEdit paper itself likewise contains no algorithm description (its references point to Rubine's 1990/91 gesture-recognition thesis as related work), and the IRG report's note that HyperMark used "a user trainable gesture recognizer developed by Rubine" is about HyperMark, not MATE; do not attribute Rubine's recogniser to MATE. [C37198]

Disambiguation, where it is discussed at all, is handled by design rather than by a classifier, in three ways. First, the endpoint-in-circle rule for move versus delete: GEdit notes the conflict explicitly -- "a conflict could occur when the amount of movement is slight, resulting in the endpoint of the marking falling within the circle. (The endpoint being within the circle would be interpreted by the system as a delete command.)" -- and presents it as an open problem of marks affecting one another, with no resolution offered. Second, closed-loop completion as agreement: circling gives both user and system a shared signal that scope is complete. Third, deferred mode as disambiguation-by-time: in annotation mode marks are not interpreted at all, so ambiguity simply does not arise there; interpretation happens later, by selection, in the author's hands. The unrecognised-mark problem is flagged as a consistency issue in GI '91: in immediate mode, "what should be done with markings that are not recognized" is unresolved -- dangerous if the user intended a comment annotation to become part of the document -- with the stated consequence that "comment annotations will not be supported in edit mode. However, comments are supported in the view/select/edit mode." [C37199]

**Takeaway:** The papers never say how the machine reads a mark -- only how the design makes misreading survivable. [C37200]

## M8 -- Many hands: colour, clutter, and the 3x3 matrix (1991-1993)

Annotations from several collaborators are overlaid in the annotation view, colour-coded per author -- explicitly compared to "writing on a transparency overlaid on top of a paper document... Then several marked up transparencies can be placed over the paper document." Clutter is fought with Hide Set of Marks, Show Set of Marks, Hide Mark, and Show Hidden Marks. The evidence for the problem is a preliminary transparency study, run twice: GI '91 reports that copies of an earlier draft of the GI paper were given to several proofreaders, each marking it with a coloured marker on transparencies over the paper, and "A preliminary analysis shows that the markings become very cluttered and difficult to see, and that there are many conflicts among the proofreaders' annotations." UIST '93 repeats it: "A preliminary study using transparencies was conducted... The results indicated that the usefulness of this feature depends upon the density of annotations on the page. In some cases a single transparency was cluttered, and overlaying several transparencies made the annotations illegible." No counts of proofreaders or conflicts are given in either paper. [C37201]

The paper's most speculative mechanism is Table 1, a 3x3 matrix of Reader Understands / Misinterprets / Does not Understand against Computer Understands / Misinterprets / Does not Understand, arguing that computer recognition reduces miscommunication between writer and reader: if the reader misinterprets but the computer understands, the computer "may help the reader understand the marking"; if the reader does not understand, they "may accept the computer's interpretation" -- including, honestly, accepting a computer misinterpretation. "The ability of the system to understand the meaning of a mark lessens the possibility of a misunderstanding between the annotation's writer and reader... the computer can give immediate feedback to the writer to verify the writer's expectations about the computer's understanding." The matrix is a proposal, not a result; no experiment tests it. [C37202]

**Takeaway:** Many hands on one page make the page unreadable -- and a machine that understands a mark can arbitrate between two people who do not agree on it. [C37203]

## M9 -- Navigation without scrollbars, and the user test (1991-1993)

MATE has no scrollbars. Hold the middle mouse button or the pen button to enter navigation mode; the page is "grabbed" and pushed -- slow motion drags the page, fast motion flicks it forward or backward like paper. It is implemented as a modified marking menu (Figure 8): "Page flicks are always too fast for the menu to pop up, but the page push commands can also be selected by the menu." The two views are coordinated three ways. Linked navigation: make a move command then, without lifting the pen, draw a line left or right to move both views equally. GoTo Text, applied to a piece of text in either view: the other view aligns to it and the text is highlighted. GoTo Annotation, annotation view only: the mark is interpreted, and if successful the edit view aligns and the affected text is highlighted. The alternatives considered and rejected or deferred: one view per annotation set (magnifies coordination problems); a single view (rejected with Figure 9 -- as the document changes, comment annotations cannot be reflowed: "Note that the placement of 'Poor' is unknown"); a GUI with mouse and keyboard (no "visible audit trail"); speech (good for wordy comments, poor for locations -- "the ideal system would use a combination mark-based speech based interface... left as future work"). Voice annotations were planned but "not central to our current research" and not in the first implementation. The hardware, honestly stated, is a compromise: a Pencept tablet and stylus for input with a separate display, X Window System on a Sun workstation. "A more ideal system would employ a flat-screen display on which the stylus is directly used. The hardware selected... was chosen due to its availability." Parallax is discussed (Tappert et al. 1986: ink-trail misalignment with the stylus tip, resolution, sampling rate, pen-down/up agreement), and the stylus metaphor matters: pen versus pencil versus highlighter versus chalk carry different expectations -- pressure makes a bolder pencil line, little change for a fine-tip marker. [C37204]

The user testing is small and the paper says so. Five users in a mock scenario, playing principal author given a marked-up document; "What they chose to incorporate was entirely up to them." Findings: "users choose to incorporate annotations by selection rather than by manually doing the edit themselves." The history, undo, and preview features were inconclusive because "the users did not use these features." The task "was not complicated enough to bring out the issues regarding the coordination of the two views," and there was "some confusion between the GoTo Text and the GoTo Annotation commands." Navigation testing was confounded by an unreliable stylus: "this made page flicks very difficult to specify. Several users preferred to use the scrollbar, instead of page flicks. Another problem was the response time of the system for page movements, which often confused users about whether it understood their command or not. These results do not conclude that page flicks do not work, but that more fine-tuning will be needed if they are to be useful and usable." No timing, error-rate, or recognition-accuracy numbers are reported for MATE anywhere. [C37205]

**Takeaway:** The flick was beaten by the scrollbar in 1993 -- not because the idea was wrong, but because the stylus lied about where the hand was. [C37206]

## M10 -- The model, the lineage, and the afterlife (1989-2019)

The collaborative-writing scenario is a flow, Figure 1: a principal author creates a document (in MATE's edit mode or any compatible editor); copies are emailed to collaborators; each collaborator reviews in annotation (deferred) mode, marking it up as on paper; marked-up copies return to the principal author, who in view/select/edit mode reviews all reviewers' colour-coded annotations overlaid, taps to incorporate or ignore each, and makes further edits. The model is explicitly grounded in observed paper flow, in Posner (1991), "A Study of Collaborative Writing" (M.Sc. thesis, University of Toronto, cited in both papers), and in Wang Freestyle (Perkins, Blatt, Workman & Ehrlich 1989; Francik & Akagi 1989; Levine & Ehrlich in press) -- introduced late 1988, pen and voice annotation over a faxed or scanned document image, entry about $2,000 and a full system about $12,000, aimed at executives who would not type. Freestyle's distributed copy is a "dumb snapshot" with annotations as a separate layer and no recognition, so the author needs both the annotated copy and the original to revise; MATE's addition is recognition plus the navigation tools enabled by higher-level data structures. The other contemporaries draw the contrast sharply. NoteCards (Trigg, Moran & Halasz, 1984; CHI+GI '87) organises ideas as a network of cards joined by typed links, collaboration by draft-passing and History cards -- asynchronous, link-based, text-typed, not pen-based. ShrEdit (McGuffin & Olson, 1992) is synchronous multi-user editing on networked Macintoshes with selection-level locking -- same-time, where MATE is pass-the-document; ShrEdit edits the shared text directly, with no annotation layer and no deferred commands. Quilt (Leland, Fish & Kraut, CSCW '88) enforces the annotation/comment distinction by social roles and typed annotations -- commentators can only add margin notes -- where MATE enforces it by mode applied to the same freehand stroke. Tivoli on the Xerox LiveBoard (Pedersen, McCall, Moran & Halasz, InterCHI '93) keeps meta-strokes in a separate mode precisely so no ink shape is pre-empted as a gesture -- the opposite of MATE's gamble that one mark can be both. [C37207]

The afterlife is longer than the career. The UIST '93 paper is cited across pen interfaces, annotation systems, and ink patents from 1996 through 2019 -- AcademicInfluence lists 59 citations, one aggregator's count, not Google Scholar's, which was not directly verified. Krishnan & Moriya (1996), "One Stroke Operations," cites it in the marking-as-command lineage. Agrawala & Shilman (2005), "DIZI: A Digital Ink Zooming Interface for Document Annotation," cites it as prior work on digital-ink annotation. "Chronicle" (UIST '10) cites it in the lineage of capturing collaborative activity and annotation history. Leiva et al. (2014), "Context-aware Gestures for Mixed-Initiative Text Editing UIs," cites both Hardock papers, "inspired in part by the Marking Menus technique (Hardock et al., 1993; Kurtenbach and Buxton, ...) and annotation (Hardock, 1991)." Xia et al. (2019), "SpaceInk: Making Space for In-Context Annotations," UIST '19, cites it in a design space of pen-and-touch annotation techniques -- direct continuity 26 years on. Two patents treat MATE as the named prior art for the annotation-versus-changing-content problem: US6687876B1 (Fuji Xerox, 2004), which discusses MATE at length, and US20040194021A1 (2004), which notes MATE "uses color to display annotations in user-controllable layers" but requires reviewing all annotations without differentiating value. And the modern tools split exactly where MATE refused to split. Microsoft Word's Ink Editor (the Action Pen) has nearly MATE's gesture vocabulary thirty years on -- circle to select, line through to delete, curve joining two words to join, vertical line inside a word to split, caret plus handwriting to insert, backwards-L for a new line -- but the disambiguation is the active pen, and ink either edits now or decorates; MATE's deferred reading of the same stroke has no equivalent. Apple Markup ink is always annotation, never an edit, living on a separate layer over the document exactly like Freestyle's snapshot-plus-layer. Google Docs splits the roles MATE fused -- Suggesting mode records proposed edits with accept/reject, Comments attach discussion without touching the text -- and GitHub pull-request review does the same in code, with suggestion blocks the author applies in one click, which is MATE's incorporation step with the human kept in the loop. In all three, the deferred-command idea survives, but the machine no longer recognises a freehand mark. MATE sits in a tight Toronto cluster: Buxton's "line-driven" framing (1990), GEdit (Kurtenbach & Buxton, UIST '91), the marking-menu programme (Kurtenbach, Sellen & Buxton 1991-94; Kurtenbach's PhD thesis 1993, whose acknowledgments say "Gary Hardock utilized my work in his research"), Freestyle as the model, and Posner (1991) for the collaborative-writing model. Funding for the UIST '93 paper: Digital Equipment Corp., Xerox PARC, and NSERC Canada primary; Apple, IBM Canada CAS, and Arnott Design Group additional. [C37208]

## The arc: from command to message and back

Hardock's arc is a single stroke drawn twice. In GI '91 the stroke is a command: the line-driven interface lets one gesture carry its own scope and operation, cheaper in attention than point-and-select, visible where a button click leaves no trace. In UIST '93 the same stroke is a message: left visible and uninterpreted, it is an annotation a collaborator can read, a suggestion the author can accept or ignore, a question the machine has not yet been asked. The two readings meet in the incorporation view, where tapping a mark turns the message back into a command -- and tapping again turns the command back into a message. The marking menu is the same arc at the scale of a gesture: the novice's slow menu selection and the expert's fast mark are the same movement, and the system teaches the fast one by letting the slow one rehearse it. Everything in the work is about a stroke that refuses to be only one thing: scope and operator, message and command, lesson and shortcut, history and undo. The papers are candid about what they do not have -- no recogniser described, no accuracy numbers, five users, a stylus that lied -- and that candour is part of the mechanism: the design makes misreading survivable by leaving the mark visible, so a wrong interpretation can be seen and undone rather than silently executed. [C37210]

## The aesthetic logic

Hardock's aesthetic is the annotated page. The beauty is in a surface that shows its own editing: thick marks not yet acted on, thin marks already executed, colours that say whose hand made each stroke, placeholders that stand in for text held elsewhere. Nothing is hidden in a menu or a history panel; the document is its own interface, its own log, and its own argument. The visual language that follows is ink on paper: a circle that means "this much," a tail that means "goes here," a star that means "held for later," a strikethrough that means "gone, unless you change your mind." The palette is restraint itself -- black ink, one colour per hand, thickness as the only state -- because the mark must be readable by a person and a machine at the same time, and decoration would break the reading for one of them. Slowness is not a mood here; it is the price of meaning, paid in the extra second a handwritten mark costs over a menu click, and the honest piece shows the price rather than hiding it. [C37211]

## Rejections

Four candidate directions were considered and not queued. A literal proofreading-marks piece -- the 41 Chicago marks drawn as a chart -- was rejected: a chart, not a mechanism; the marks are a vocabulary, not a system that runs them. A flick-navigation piece -- grab and flick through a long document, slow push versus fast flick -- was rejected: navigation alone is not a piece, and the 1993 user test itself found users preferred the scrollbar when the stylus was unreliable; the mechanism needs a document worth navigating, not a gesture worth repeating. A recognition-accuracy piece -- a meter showing how well the machine reads the viewer's marks -- was rejected: the papers describe no recogniser, so any accuracy shown would be invented, and a meter measures without making anything. A voice-annotation piece -- spoken comments attached to marks -- was rejected: both papers name voice as future work and neither implements it, so there is no mechanism to build from. HELD, not queued: the 3x3 understanding matrix (a mark the reader misreads but the computer understands, and the other eight cells) is a real mechanism, but it is a second reading of the deferred-mark piece and should be folded into that piece's development rather than built separately; the single-view reflow problem (Figure 9, the "Poor" comment whose placement becomes unknown as the document changes) is a real mechanism too, but it needs its own study of annotation anchoring before it is built.
