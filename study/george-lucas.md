## 1. The Modesto boy who survived the crash

He was born George Walton Lucas Jr. in Modesto, California, on Sunday 14 May 1944, and grew up in the Central Valley town he would later film as the whole world -- a cruising town, a car town, a town where a boy's standing was the car he drove and the speed he drove it at. [C32556] He wanted to be a racing driver, and the ambition ended in June 1962, days before his high-school graduation, when he crashed his small Italian car and was badly hurt -- the crash is the hinge of the whole career, the moment the driver becomes a watcher, and the camera becomes the vehicle. [C32557] He went to the University of Southern California film school, where Lester Novros taught a filmic-expression course "concentrated on the non-narrative elements of film form like color, light, movement, space and time", and Slavko Vorkapich taught "the autonomous nature of the cinematic art form, emphasizing the kinetic energy inherent in motion pictures". [C32558] The influences he names from those classes are Arthur Lipsett's 21-87, Jean-Claude Labrecque's 60 Cycles, Norman McLaren and Claude Jutra -- filmmakers who made meaning out of movement, not plot. [C32559] He "quickly became prolific at making 16 mm nonstory noncharacter visual tone poems and cinema verite", "defining himself as a film-maker as opposed to being a director, and he loved making abstract visual films that created emotions purely through nonnarrative structures". [C32560] The USC years were also a trade school in other people's money: since the 1940s the university had a working arrangement with the US Navy, which "paid for unlimited color film and lab processing costs", and Lucas worked by day editing United States Information Agency films under Verna Fields' supervision, cutting on her Moviola at her home. [C32561] The biography is already the method: a boy who wanted speed learns to make speed out of light and cutting, a student who cannot afford a story learns to make feeling out of color and movement, and a crash that ends one career starts another that will be about controlling the machine instead of riding in it. [C32562]

**Takeaway:** The crash is the whole career in one accident: the driver who cannot drive anymore builds the car, the road and the race out of film, and spends the next fifty years trying to make the machine go faster than he ever could.

## 2. Pure cinema: the USC tone poems

Look at Life (1965) is the first of them, a one-minute montage of magazine photographs about war, accelerating, ending on the words "LOVE / ANYONE FOR SURVIVAL" -- the content is search-extract only, but the credit is in the filmography table, and the form is the whole point: found images, cut to a rhythm, meaning made in the edit. [C32563] Herbie (1966), co-directed with Paul Golding, films night traffic headlights reflected in polished car bodywork, to Herbie Hancock -- again search-extract for the content, table-verified for the credit, and again the car as a mirror, not a machine. [C32564] Freiheit (1966) is a student escaping from East to West Germany, killed in the attempt -- the content search-extract only, the credit table-verified -- and it is the first Lucas story with a border, a crossing and a death for crossing, the plot of THX 1138 in miniature. [C32565] 1:42.08 (1966) is a lap time, a documentary of racing driver Peter Brock's Lotus at Willow Springs, directed, written, edited and shot by Lucas -- the title is a number, not a name, and the number is the whole film. [C32566] Anyone Lived in a Pretty How Town (1967) takes its title and its text from the e.e. cummings poem -- search-extract for the source, table-verified for the credit -- and The Emperor (1967) is a documentary on the disc jockey Bob Hudson, "The Emperor", while 6-18-67 (1967) is a desert day, a documentary whose subject the search extracts give as the making of Mackenna's Gold. [C32567] Filmmaker (1968) is the documentary he shot while watching Coppola shoot The Rain People -- director, writer, editor, cinematographer and sound, all Lucas -- so the first Zoetrope film already contains its own making-of, and the student watching the master is already directing the record of the master. [C32568] The seven shorts together are the thesis in its purest form: no story, no character, no dialogue that matters, only images and sounds cut to make a feeling -- and everything after them, including the space opera, is that thesis with a story bolted on top of it. [C32569]

**Takeaway:** The shorts are the whole method before the method has a name: a number for a title, a border for a plot, a car for a mirror, and the meaning made entirely in the cut.

## 3. Electronic Labyrinth: the Navy-class short

Electronic Labyrinth: THX 1138 4EB (1967) is fifteen minutes long, made at USC, and it is the film that turns the tone poems into a world: a man designated THX 1138 escapes a white underground city, and the escape is the whole plot. [C32570] It was made on the Navy's money: since the 1940s USC had a working arrangement with the US Navy, Navy filmmakers attended the university, and the Navy "paid for unlimited color film and lab processing costs" -- Lucas offered to teach the unpopular class and was allowed, and "the Navy men formed the crew of the film, and some appeared in the cast". [C32571] The locations followed the money: the USC computer center, a parking lot at UCLA, Los Angeles International Airport and Van Nuys Airport, much of it filmed at night and at weekends, "completed in 12 weeks, with Lucas editing it on the Moviola at the home of Verna Fields". [C32572] In January 1968 it won first prize in the category of Dramatic films at the third National Student Film Festival at Lincoln Center, where it was "seen and admired by Steven Spielberg, who had not previously met Lucas" -- the first meeting of the two men is a screening, not a handshake. [C32573] The set date inside the film is May 14, 2187 -- Lucas's birthday, two hundred and forty-three years on -- and the film was selected for the National Film Registry in 2010. [C32574] The score is borrowed eclecticism: Bach's Passacaglia in C minor, modified excerpts from Virgil Fox Plays the Wanamaker Grand Court Organ, and opening-credits music credited to The Yardbirds, "Still I'm Sad". [C32575] The idea behind it, in Lucas's long-held formulation, "was based on the concept that we live in the future and that you could make a futuristic film using existing stuff" -- the found future, stated at fifteen minutes, before it is ever built at feature length. [C32576]

**Takeaway:** The short is the feature in miniature: the Navy pays for the film, the airports play the future, the birthday is the date of the escape, and a student film festival introduces Lucas to the man who will direct half his life's work.

## 4. Finian's Rainbow and the scholarship

He was "awarded a student scholarship by Warner Bros. to observe and work on the making of a film of his choosing. The film he chose ... was Finian's Rainbow (1968) which was being directed by Francis Ford Coppola" -- the scholarship is the doorway, and the film he chose is the man he chose. [C32577] The credit is a flag, not a fact: TCM calls him a production assistant hired by Coppola, the AFI Catalog says he "reportedly served as Coppola's assistant", the Lucas article says only that he was there to "observe and work on" the film, and the Finian's Rainbow film page itself never mentions Lucas at all -- a search of the whole page returns zero matches. [C32578] Finian's Rainbow itself, released 9 October 1968 with Fred Astaire and Petula Clark, was made with five weeks of rehearsal plus a full performance before shooting -- the rehearsal habit Lucas watched is the one Coppola will use, larger, on The Godfather. [C32579] What Lucas watched was a young director fighting a studio over a musical -- the apprenticeship is not in technique but in posture, a director who rehearses like a theatre company and spends the studio's money like his own. [C32580] Within a year he was Coppola's partner, not his student: Lucas was a production assistant on The Rain People (1969), the first Zoetrope film, and made the short documentary Filmmaker about the shoot, so the partnership begins with Lucas filming Coppola filming. [C32581] In 1969 he was also one of the camera operators on the Rolling Stones concert film Gimme Shelter -- the other apprenticeship, handheld, in the dark, with a band that will not repeat a take. [C32582] The one project that did not happen is also on the record: "Five, Four, Three", a never-completed short from Sunrise Productions (Lucas, Randal Kleiser, Chris Lewis), a mockumentary about a satirical teen beach movie called "Orgy Beach Party", made under the company name in which Lucas called himself "Lucas Beaumont". [C32583]

**Takeaway:** The scholarship is the hinge of the career: a student is paid to watch a director, watches him fight the studio, and within a year is his partner -- the apprenticeship lasts one film, and the partnership lasts a lifetime.

## 5. American Zoetrope: the deviant studio, second half

American Zoetrope was founded by Coppola and Lucas and opened on 12 December 1969 in a warehouse at 827 Folsom Street, on the second floor of the Automatt building in San Francisco, with its headquarters since 1972 in the Sentinel Building at 916 Kearny Street in North Beach. [C32584] It was named after a zoetrope given to Coppola by Mogens Skot-Hansen, founder of the Danish company Lanterna Film -- the Zoetrope page spells the name Skot-Hansen, the Coppola study carries Scot-Hansen, and this study carries the variant. [C32585] Lucas's version of the idea, in the Lucas article's words, was co-founding the company "hoping to create a liberating environment for film-makers to direct outside the perceived oppressive control of the Hollywood studio system" -- the studio as a place, six hundred miles from Hollywood, on purpose. [C32586] THX 1138 was "the first film of a planned seven-picture slate commissioned by Warner Bros. from the 1969 incarnation of American Zoetrope" -- the deviant studio begins as a Warner Bros. contract, not as independence. [C32587] The contract nearly killed it at once: Warner Bros. demanded repayment of the money it had loaned Zoetrope and cut four to five minutes from THX 1138, and the debt nearly closed the studio and forced Coppola, reluctantly, to focus on The Godfather. [C32588] Lucas's answer was to build his own company: he created Lucasfilm Ltd. and directed American Graffiti through it, the first film produced by his Lucasfilm banner -- though the production-company credit itself is disputed, with the infobox naming Lucasfilm Ltd. and the Coppola Company, the lead naming Lucasfilm, and the Zoetrope filmography listing Graffiti as a Zoetrope production. [C32589] The Zoetrope filmography, as read, lists three films in the Lucas arc -- The Rain People (1969, Coppola), THX 1138 (1971, Lucas) and American Graffiti (1973, Lucas) -- a studio that lasted three films before its founders each built a bigger one. [C32590]

**Takeaway:** Zoetrope is the thesis stated as an address, and Lucas is the half of it that learned the lesson: the deviant studio cannot survive on the system's money, so build your own company, your own banner, and your own balance sheet -- and then the system has to come to you.

## 6. THX 1138: the found future

THX 1138, Lucas's feature directorial debut, was released on 11 March 1971, based on Electronic Labyrinth, produced by American Zoetrope and distributed by Warner Bros., on a budget the infobox gives as $777,777 against a box office of $2.4 million -- while the reception section calls it a commercial flop earning $945,000 in rentals, and the study carries both figures. [C32591] It was shot in "unfinished tunnels of the Bay Area Rapid Transit subway system", the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Frank Lloyd Wright's Marin County Civic Center, the Lawrence Hall of Science, San Francisco International Airport, a remote manipulator for a hot cell and an FAA 9020 IBM System/360 air-traffic-control installation -- and "Lucas used entirely natural light", with the studio "white limbo" built on a 100 by 150 foot white stage. [C32592] The final climb was filmed with the camera rotated 90 degrees in the horizontal, in the incomplete BART Transbay Tube, the actors using exposed reinforcing bars as a ladder, and the sunset was shot at Port Hueneme by a second unit -- Caleb Deschanel and Matthew Robbins, who played THX in the long shot. [C32593] The cars are the other location: the Lola T70 chase runs at a claimed 140 miles per hour, and the racing obsession carries straight over from 1:42.08 and the 1962 crash -- the boy who could not race films other men racing, faster than he ever drove. [C32594] Post-production took a year, scheduled by Coppola: "Lucas edited the film on a German-made K-E-M flatbed editor in his Mill Valley house by day and Walter Murch edited sound at night, comparing notes as each session ended", and Murch "compiled and synchronized the sound montage, which includes elements such as the 'overhead' voices, radio chatter and announcements". [C32595] The critics saw the method before the message: Canby called it "a stunning montage of light, color and sound effects that create their own emotional impact", and Champlin wrote that "the real excitement ... is not really the message but the medium -- the use of film not to tell a story so much as to convey an experience". [C32596] Warner cut about five minutes before release, and the film now exists in versions: the 1971 studio version at 81 minutes, the 1977 restored VHS at 88, the LaserDisc at 86, the 2004 director's cut at 88, and a Fandom figure of 95 minutes for the 1971 studio cut -- the study carries all of them, and the infobox's 88 minutes is only one of the set. [C32597]

**Takeaway:** THX 1138 is the tone poem at feature length: the future built from a subway tunnel, a civic center and a laboratory, the story carried by light and sound instead of dialogue, and the studio cutting five minutes out of a film that was already mostly silence.

## 7. The THX control system: uniform, drug, number, budget

The world of THX 1138 is a diagram of control: sex and reproduction are prohibited, mind-altering drugs are mandatory, and "workers wear identical white uniforms and have shaven heads to emphasize uniformity, likewise with police androids who wear black and monks who are robed". [C32598] Instead of names, people have designations with three arbitrary letters and four digits, shown on an identity badge worn at all times -- THX 1138, SEN 5241, LUH 3417 -- and the number is the person, the way 1:42.08 was the whole film. [C32599] Surveillance is a job, not a plot device: SEN 5241 and LUH 3417 work in "central video control centers" keeping watch, and THX works in a factory producing the android police who will chase him. [C32600] Control operates by confession booth to OMM 0000 -- the face on the screen is Hans Memling's Christ Giving His Blessing, painted in 1478 -- by drug substitution, by a "mind lock" that raises the danger at work, and by trial and prison in a white limbo that has no walls to escape from. [C32601] The escape is the system's own logic turned against it: the pursuit is called off because "the expense of his capture exceeds their allocated budget for THX" -- the budget is a character in the film, and it is the one that lets him go. [C32602] Lucas chose THX 1138 for "aesthetic qualities, especially their symmetry", and the number is also his college telephone number, 849-1138, with THX as 8-4-9 on the keypad; Murch believed THX meant "sex", LUH meant "love" and SEN meant "sin". [C32603] The cast shaved their heads for real, the first haircuts were filmed as a publicity stunt, the featurette was called Bald: The Making of THX 1138, many of the extras were recruited from Synanon, filming ran from 22 September 1969 for thirty-five to forty days in Techniscope, and some of SEN's dialogue uses excerpts from Richard Nixon's speeches. [C32604]

**Takeaway:** The control system is the whole film in one mechanism: a world that numbers its people, drugs them, watches them and confesses them to a painting -- and then lets the hero go, not because he wins, but because catching him costs too much.

## 8. American Graffiti: the diegetic jukebox

American Graffiti was released on 2 August 1973 at Locarno and 11 August 1973 in the United States, set in Modesto in 1962 over one summer night in a series of vignettes, the first film produced by Lucas's Lucasfilm banner, with five Academy Award nominations including Best Picture and Best Director. [C32605] Its genesis is autobiography, stated on the page: Lucas's own early-1960s Modesto cruising, with Curt as Lucas at USC, John as his teen street-racing and junior-college years, and Terry as his freshman "nerd years" and bad luck dating. [C32606] Coppola "challenged" him during THX 1138 production "to write a script that would appeal to mainstream audiences", and after THX's failure Lucas said he made Graffiti as "a release for a world-weary audience" -- the dystopia's antidote is a summer night. [C32607] With Easy Rider (1969), it was "one of the first films to eschew a traditional film score and successfully rely instead on synchronizing a series of popular hit songs with individual scenes": Lucas "wrote each scene with a particular song in mind as its musical backdrop", wanted seventy-five songs, and the licensing cost was a factor in United Artists rejecting the script. [C32608] The final system is diegetic music "that the characters themselves can hear and therefore becomes an integral part of the action"; clearing the rights cost about $90,000, "as a result, no money was left for a traditional film score", and Lucas later explained: "I used the absence of music, and sound effects, to create the drama". [C32609] Walter Murch, assisting on post-production audio mixing and sound design, "suggested making Wolfman Jack's radio show the 'backbone' of the film"; producer Gary Kurtz called the Wolfman "an ethereal presence in the lives of young people", and the Wolfman's phone calls in the film were "actual calls with real people"; Universal proposed a flat deal paying every publisher the same, RCA refused, and "Elvis Presley is conspicuously absent". [C32610] The money is a flag, not a figure: the infobox gives a $777,000 budget against a $140 million box office, while the development section builds $775,000 from a $600,000 projection plus $175,000, the release section gives $1.27 million to produce and market, Britannica says about $750,000, and the box office runs from $55 million on first release to $118 million for two releases to over $200 million including home video. [C32611]

**Takeaway:** Graffiti is the jukebox as a film score: the songs are the structure, the radio is the backbone, the characters hear what the audience hears, and the absence of composed music is itself the composition -- the cheapest film Lucas ever made, and the one that paid for everything after it.

## 9. Graffiti's shoot: Techniscope and the ABCD cut

Filming ran from 26 June to 4 August 1972; San Rafael withdrew permission after a second day, and the production moved to Petaluma on 28 June "without the loss of a single day" -- the small crew moves, the town changes, the night stays the same. [C32612] Lucas considered being his own cinematographer and dropped it, shot with two cinematographers and no formal director of photography, used "two cameras simultaneously in scenes involving conversations between actors in different cars", and, after CinemaScope proved too expensive, chose Techniscope -- "an inexpensive way of shooting on 35 mm film and using only half of the film's frame" -- for a "documentary-like feel ... resembling 16 mm", with Haskell Wexler brought in as "visual consultant" when Techniscope brought lighting problems. [C32613] He was open to improvisation, and goofs were kept in the final cut -- the documentary feel is not a look, it is a permission, to let the night stay messy. [C32614] Editing was the second shoot: the first cut ran three and a half hours, the original "ABCD" structure -- four storylines always in the same sequence -- was broken up while cutting to 112 minutes, and the final cut was finished in December 1972. [C32615] The script had its own cuts: the first draft, by Richard Walter, was an exploitation-style script Lucas rejected as "not the way I grew up", and the film was written with Gloria Katz and Willard Huyck, who stayed as his collaborators into the prequels' story room. [C32616] Universal still tried to re-edit it, and Coppola's leverage saved it: after The Godfather's Oscar win Coppola could insist, and he offered to buy the film himself for "the $775,000 ... that it had cost" -- the mentor buying the protege's film back from the studio. [C32617] The credit is the flag: the infobox names Lucasfilm Ltd. and the Coppola Company, the lead calls it the first film produced by Lucas's Lucasfilm banner, and the Zoetrope filmography lists it as an American Zoetrope production -- three companies for one summer night, and the film belongs to all of them. [C32618]

**Takeaway:** Graffiti is made the way it is set: two cameras at once, half a frame, real streets, real mistakes left in, and four stories cut until they braid -- the documentary method applied to a memory, and the memory comes back sharper than the night was.

## 10. Star Wars: the used future

Star Wars was released on 25 May 1977 in thirty-two theatres, written and directed by Lucas and produced by Gary Kurtz, on a budget that ran from $8 million at the start of production to $11 million final, with an initial gross of $410 million worldwide and a total of $775.4 million -- the box office variants are carried in the Depth line, and no single figure is settled. [C32619] Lucas's own words are the whole design brief: "The trouble with the future in most futurist movies is that it always looks new and clean and shiny... What is required for true credibility is a used future." [C32620] "Lucas did not want anything in Star Wars to stand out, and wanted everything to look 'real and used'. In this 'used future' aesthetic, all devices, ships, and buildings related to Tatooine and the rebels look aged and dirty, and the rebel ships look cobbled together in contrast to the Empire's sleeker designs" -- the Millennium Falcon's interior was built "to look like a submarine", from "inexpensive airplane scrap metal", by production designers John Barry and Roger Christian, from Ralph McQuarrie's January 1975 concept painting, which is "credited for establishing the film's aesthetic", across thirty sets on all nine Elstree stages. [C32621] The afterlife is the proof: "Lucas' concept of the 'used future' was employed in Scott's films Alien (1979) and Blade Runner (1982); Cameron's Aliens (1986) and The Terminator (1984); and Jackson's The Lord of the Rings trilogy" -- the design idea outlived the film and became the default for science fiction. [C32622] The first thirty minutes of the film are "mainly about robots", which the page calls a "bold idea" -- the droids as low-status witnesses, opening the film before any hero appears, is the Hidden Fortress peasant-viewpoint device, though the pages read do not spell it out in those words, and this study does not over-claim it. [C32623] The nine-film plan is a flag: Lucas "has stated... the extreme length of the second draft led him to split the story across the three films", but "this claim has been challenged by Kurtz and by researchers Michael Kaminski and Chris Taylor", and Lucas himself said in 2010: "I didn't know where [the narrative] was going... The trick is to pretend you've planned the whole thing out in advance". [C32624] The opening crawl was "originally six paragraphs of four sentences each", Brian De Palma "criticized the crawl for being too long and incomprehensible", and "De Palma and Jay Cocks helped Lucas edit the crawl down to four sentences", while Dan Perri's logotype "was designed to follow the same perspective as the opening text crawl" -- the title and the crawl are one perspective shot. [C32625]

**Takeaway:** The used future is the found future grown up: nothing in the film looks new, because nothing in a real world looks new, and the audience believes the galaxy because the galaxy already looks lived in -- the dirt is the special effect.

## 11. Motion control: the Dykstraflex

"Most of the visual effects used pioneering digital motion control photography developed by John Dykstra and his team, which created the illusion of size by employing small models and slowly moving cameras. The technology is now known as the Dykstraflex system", and Dykstra, Alvah J. Miller and Jerry Jeffress won the Academy's Scientific and Engineering Award for it. [C32626] The Dykstraflex page, read in full, calls it "the first digital motion control photography camera system... developed in 1976 specifically for complex special effects shots in Star Wars. Using old VistaVision cameras (for their high image resolution)... the all-digitally controlled system allowed for 7 axes of motion" -- and then lists ten items, an internal inconsistency the study carries rather than resolves. [C32627] The mechanism is repetition: a computer-recorded camera move can be repeated exactly, so separate passes -- ship, second ship, lighting, starfield -- can be optically composited into one shot that still feels like a moving camera, and the dogfight is a set of identical moves layered on top of each other. [C32628] The rig detail is search-extract only: about 1,500 pounds, stepper motors, a forty-foot track, joystick and potentiometer programming, from VFX Voice and other secondary coverage -- not read in a fully opened page, and carried as such. [C32629] Industrial Light and Magic was founded for this shot: "Founded by George Lucas on May 26, 1975... created when he began production on the 1977 film Star Wars", in Van Nuys, moving to San Rafael in 1978 and to the Presidio in 2005 -- though older copies give only "May 1975", and the study carries the variant. [C32630] The ILM milestones list the two firsts together: "1975: ILM used VistaVision for Star Wars" and "Resurrected the use of VistaVision; first use of a motion control camera" -- the old format and the new control, in the same year, in the same company. [C32631] Empire added the second invention: the ILM milestones give "1980: ILM's first use of Go motion was used to animate the Tauntaun creatures and AT-ATs of Star Wars: Episode V - The Empire Strikes Back", though the main Empire article does not contain the term at all, and the workflow -- a rod puppet driven a small amount during the exposure, so the shutter records genuine motion blur -- is search-extract only, grade B. [C32632]

**Takeaway:** The Dykstraflex is the camera as a recording instrument: the move is written once, played back identically, and the impossible shot is just the same shot, repeated, until the layers add up to a battle -- the machine remembers what the cameraman cannot.

## 12. The organic soundtrack: Ben Burtt's found sounds

Sound designer Ben Burtt "created a library of sounds that Lucas referred to as an 'organic soundtrack'": blaster sounds from "modifying the noise of a steel cable being struck while under tension", lightsaber effects from "a combination of the hum of movie projector motors and interference caused by a television set on a shieldless microphone", discovered by accident, Chewbacca's speech from "the sounds of four bears, a badger, a lion, a seal, and a walrus", Vader's breathing "by breathing through the mask of a scuba regulator", and R2-D2's beeps from Burtt "imitating baby noises over an intercom and mixing the sounds together using a synthesizer". [C32633] The method is the found future for the ear: real-world recordings recontextualised, rather than synthesised science-fiction bleeps -- the search-only support is that Burtt "sought a more natural sound" where earlier science-fiction films "tended to use electronic-sounding effects", and the study marks the framing as search-extract. [C32634] The film was mixed in Dolby's new six-track "baby boom" layout, vocals in the centre, side channels for deep bass -- the mix is itself a piece of technology, and the film's sound is designed for a room that barely exists yet. [C32635] Burtt won a Special Achievement Academy Award for Star Wars, and the Skywalker Sound staff "have either won or been nominated for an Academy Award for Best Sound and Best Sound Editing every year since Star Wars in 1977" -- the library became an institution, and the institution kept winning. [C32636] Sprocket Systems, the company behind it, was "founded September 7, 1975" in San Anselmo, moved after the January 1982 flood, and was renamed Skywalker Sound in 1987 on the move to Skywalker Ranch -- though a search snippet claims the rename happened in 1982, the flood year, and the study carries the variant. [C32637] One famous Burtt detail is NOT carried as verified: the Wilhelm scream appears only in a Ben Burtt search result, as something he is "notable for popularizing", and was not verified in any opened page -- so this study does not present it as page-verified. [C32638] Burtt stayed in the cutting room as well as the sound room: he edited The Phantom Menace with Paul Martin Smith and holds the sole editor credit on Attack of the Clones, where he also invented the "videomatics" previsualisation method -- the sound man becomes the editor, and the editor becomes the previs artist. [C32639]

**Takeaway:** The organic soundtrack is the found future with the eyes closed: a cable for a blaster, a projector for a lightsaber, four bears for a Wookiee, and a baby over an intercom for a droid -- the galaxy sounds real because every sound in it was once a real thing.

## 13. Williams and the romantic score

Lucas "initially planned to use pre-existing music... he believed recognizable music was needed to create a sense of familiarity. He hired John Williams as a music consultant... After Williams convinced Lucas that an original score would be preferable, Lucas tasked him with creating it" -- the score is a conversion, not a commission. [C32640] The "Main Title Theme" was inspired by Korngold's Kings Row (1942), "Dune Sea of Tatooine" was influenced by Bicycle Thieves (1948), the score was recorded over twelve days in March 1977 with the London Symphony Orchestra, and the American Film Institute chose it "as the best film score of all time" in 2005. [C32641] The leitmotif mechanics are search-extract only: Williams's "revival of a technique called 'leitmotiv'", a "phrase or melodic cell that signifies a character, place, plot element, mood, idea, relationship or other specific part of the film", the Main Theme as "the primary leitmotif for Luke Skywalker", and The Imperial March, composed for Empire and premiered on 29 April 1980, "first heard... in low piccolos as the Galactic Empire sends probe droids" -- and the word "leitmotif" does not appear in the Star Wars (film) article at all. [C32642] Spielberg recommended Williams to Lucas, the same Spielberg who would direct the Indiana Jones films -- the composer arrives through the friend, not through an audition, and stays for every film after. [C32643] The Empire score was composed and conducted by Williams with the London Symphony Orchestra for about $250,000, with 107 minutes of music planned and recorded in eighteen three-hour sessions -- the music is budgeted like a set, and built like one. [C32644] The Jedi score was Williams with the London Symphony Orchestra again, with orchestration also by Thomas Newman -- the same orchestra, the same conductor, the third film in six years, and the sound of the trilogy is the sound of one room. [C32645] Williams scored all the Star Wars films and all the Indiana Jones films -- the Phantom Menace, Attack of the Clones and Revenge of the Sith credits all list "Music by John Williams" -- and the Raiders score and the Indiana Jones theme are part of the franchise's identity system, the way the crawl is part of Star Wars'. [C32646]

**Takeaway:** The score is the familiar made new: a Korngold fanfare for a farm boy, a Bicycle Thieves melody for a desert, and a march for an empire -- the music tells the audience this strange world is an old story, and the audience believes it.

## 14. Borrowed scaffolding: Flash Gordon, Hidden Fortress, Campbell

"Before creating Star Wars, Lucas had hoped to make a Flash Gordon film, but was unable to obtain the rights. Star Wars features many elements ostensibly derived from Flash Gordon, such as the conflict between rebels and imperial forces; the wipe transitions between scenes; and the text crawl at the beginning of the film" -- the film he could not make becomes the film he did make. [C32647] The May 1973 ten-page treatment "borrowed narrative beats from Akira Kurosawa's 1958 film The Hidden Fortress, and follows an elderly general, Luke Skywalker, who escorts a rebel princess... while being pursued by Imperial troops", and "the narrative's similarities to The Hidden Fortress were reduced" in later drafts -- the borrowing is in the treatment, and the revision is the film. [C32648] The Campbell link is hedged on the page itself: "Lucas also reportedly drew from Joseph Campbell's 1949 book The Hero with a Thousand Faces" -- that is the only Campbell mention in the opened Star Wars article, and the word "reportedly" is doing the work. [C32649] The stronger support is search-extract only: the Joseph Campbell page says "George Lucas was the first filmmaker to credit Campbell's influence", and quotes Lucas in Campbell's authorised biography -- "in reading The Hero with a Thousand Faces I began to realize that my first draft of Star Wars was following classic motifs... So I modified my next draft according to what I'd been learning about classical motifs" -- and it was not until after the trilogy was completed in 1983 that Lucas met Campbell. [C32650] The honest reading is that Campbell was a retrospective revision tool: the first draft already followed the motifs, the later drafts were made more consistent with them, and no stage-by-stage monomyth mapping is given in any page read -- so this study does not present one. [C32651] The Jabba cut scene is the same shape of claim, contested: Lucas "has since claimed that he had intended to replace [the human Jabba, played by Declan Mulholland] with a stop-motion alien", while "Michael Kaminski challenged this account... production notes contain no references to replacing Mulholland" -- the study carries the dispute, not the version. [C32652] The scaffolding is the method: take a serial, a Kurosawa film and a mythology book, strip each to its load-bearing shape, and rebuild it at a scale none of them could afford -- the borrowing is open, the synthesis is the authorship. [C32653]

**Takeaway:** Nothing in Star Wars is invented and everything in it is original: the serial gives the shape, Kurosawa gives the viewpoint, Campbell gives the revision, and Lucas gives the world they all walk through -- the borrowing is the authorship, honestly worn.

## 15. Empire and Jedi: the company becomes the studio

The Empire Strikes Back was released on 21 May 1980 in the United States, directed by Irvin Kershner, screenplay by Leigh Brackett and Lawrence Kasdan from a story by Lucas, with Lucas as executive producer and story credit -- though the infobox lists only Gary Kurtz as producer, and the study does not cite the infobox alone for Lucas's sequel credit -- on a budget of $30.5 million, with about 600 effects shots against 360 on Star Wars. [C32654] Lucas funded Empire himself, putting his $12 million Star Wars profit up as collateral for a Bank of America loan, and took the executive-producer role to avoid the stress of directing; the film is "darker and more mature", got mixed reviews on release, and ends on a cliffhanger -- the sequel as a deliberate refusal to close. [C32655] The company had to be rebuilt to make it: after Star Wars, ILM "had effectively ceased to exist", most of its staff and equipment going to John Dykstra's Apogee, and Lucas "had to re-establish ILM and completely re-staff it" in Marin County in late 1978 -- and when the staff arrived in September 1978 the facility was still under construction, "initially lacked the equipment", with walls still being installed and no electricity. [C32656] Return of the Jedi was released on 25 May 1983, exactly six years after Star Wars, directed by Richard Marquand, produced by Howard Kazanjian, with Lucas as executive producer and shared screenwriting credit with Kasdan -- though the infobox again lists only Kazanjian -- and Lucas "personally financed Return of the Jedi, but chose not to direct", with about 900 ILM effects shots, on a budget the infobox gives as $32.5-42.7 million against a box office of $482 million, both figures flagged. [C32657] Jedi's schedule was deliberately pushed early "to give ILM as much time as possible", and "the entire company found it necessary to remain running 20 hours a day on six-day weeks" to meet 1 April 1983, while using in-house ILM "saved the production approximately $18 million" -- the studio is the budget, and the budget is the schedule. [C32658] The techniques are variety, not scale: the rancor was a puppet filmed at high speed after a suit attempt failed, the speeder chase was shot by Garrett Brown on Steadicam at less than one frame per second, walking at about five miles per hour, projected at 24 frames per second to read as about 120 miles per hour, and the climax is a three-way intercut -- Endor on the ground, the space battle, the throne-room duel -- rather than a single trench run. [C32659] The trilogy ends by standardising the room, not just the mix: THX "was developed by Tomlinson Holman at George Lucas's company Lucasfilm in 1983 to ensure that the soundtrack for the third Star Wars film, Return of the Jedi, would be accurately reproduced in the best venues", it is "a quality assurance system, not a recording technology", it was dedicated on 20 May 1983 with the Deep Note by James A. Moorer -- a synthesized crescendo glissandoing from about 200-400 Hz to a spread of about three octaves -- debuting at the Jedi premiere five days later, and the name comes from Holman's initials, with the X standing for "crossover" or "experiment", and as an homage to THX 1138, though a Simple Wikipedia copy gives only "Tomlinson Holman's eXperiment". [C32660]

**Takeaway:** The sequels are the company, not the films: a studio rebuilt from nothing, a facility with no electricity, a schedule that runs twenty hours a day -- and the third film ends by standardising the cinema itself, because the films are now bigger than any room they play in.

## 16. Indiana Jones: serial modernisation, set-piece first

Lucas conceived Raiders in 1973, after American Graffiti, from a poster reminding him of the 1930s-40s serials -- Buck Rogers, Zorro's Fighting Legion, Spy Smasher -- and wanted "a B movie modeled on those serials", called The Adventures of Indiana Smith, "a daring archaeologist named after his Alaskan Malamute". [C32661] In May 1977, in Hawaii, Lucas, fearing Star Wars would fail, pitched it to Spielberg as "better than James Bond"; Spielberg wanted a Bond film and disliked "Smith", and they settled on "Jones" -- the location is a flag, the Raiders page putting the pitch on a beach near the Mauna Kea Beach Hotel and the franchise page putting Lucas "in Maui". [C32662] Raiders of the Lost Ark was released on 12 June 1981, directed by Spielberg, screenplay by Lawrence Kasdan, story by Lucas with Philip Kaufman, on a $20 million budget against a $389.9 million box office in the infobox -- with variants of $18 million and about $354 million and $330.5 million carried in the Depth line -- and it entered the National Film Registry in 1999. [C32663] The writing method is set-piece first: Lucas and Spielberg "arrived with set pieces/stunts in mind and hired Kasdan 'to fill in the narrative gaps between them'", in January 1978, nine hours a day over three to five days, yielding the boulder trap, the medallion burn and the warehouse ending -- the story is the mortar between the set-pieces. [C32664] Each film then swaps one sacred object as the engine: the Ark (1981), the Sankara Stones (1984), the Holy Grail (1989), the crystal skulls (2008), and Temple of Doom explicitly recycled Raiders drop-outs -- the rolling-gong shield, the raft fall from a plane, the mine-cart chase -- and was made darker after Lucas's and Spielberg's divorces and breakups. [C32665] Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, story by Menno Meyjes and Lucas, screenplay by Jeffrey Boam, takes the Holy Grail as its MacGuffin, with the father-son reconciliation as the Grail metaphor, in Spielberg's framing -- the object is the relationship, and the chase is the therapy. [C32666] Indiana Jones and the Kingdom of the Crystal Skull (2008), story by Lucas and Jeff Nathanson, is set in 1957 with aliens and crystal skulls as the 1950s B-movie device, a device Lucas had first wanted for a Young Indiana Jones episode, and The Young Indiana Jones Chronicles (1992-1993, with a variant of 1992-1996), created by Lucas, was his fallback after letting the film series end in 1989 for lack of a plot device. [C32667]

**Takeaway:** Indiana Jones is the serial rebuilt at A-picture scale: the cliffhanger becomes the set-piece, the set-piece comes first, the writer fills the gaps, and each film is the same machine with a different sacred object in the engine.

## 17. The prequels: the CGI-gated return and the all-digital pipeline

Lucas said in the 1980s that he had no desire to return and had cancelled the sequel trilogy, and he returned only "after he determined that CGI had advanced to the level he wanted"; he began writing on 1 November 1994 from a fifteen-page outline dated 1976 -- the return is gated on the tools, not on the story. [C32668] Star Wars: Episode I - The Phantom Menace was released on 19 May 1999, Lucas's first directed feature in twenty-two years, on a $113.8 million budget against a $1.047 billion total gross, running 134 minutes -- with variants of $115 million and 133 or 136 minutes carried in the Depth line. [C32669] The prequels were re-framed from tangential "filling-in" into the beginning of one long story -- Anakin's childhood to death, a tragedy of Vader's origins -- and the larger digital budget let Lucas "think about a much grander, more epic scale", with Phantom Menace ending on five simultaneous plots. [C32670] Star Wars: Episode II - Attack of the Clones was released on 16 May 2002, directed by Lucas, screenplay by Lucas and Jonathan Hales from a story by Lucas, "shot completely on a high-definition digital 24-frame system" with Sony and Panavision HDW-F900 cameras, HDCAM 1080p cropped to 2.40:1, the cameras arriving one week before principal photography in June to September 2000 at Fox Studios Australia, against a $653.8 million gross. [C32671] The "first" is a flag: Attack of the Clones is described as "one of the first motion pictures shot completely on a high-definition digital 24-frame system", and elsewhere as "the third film to be released that was shot entirely on a 24p digital camera (preceded by 2001's Jackpot and Vidocq)" -- the "first Hollywood film shot digitally" framing in secondary coverage conflicts with the "third released" wording on Wikipedia. [C32672] Producer Rick McCallum called it the first film produced through "virtual filmmaking", with shots assembled from departments "sometimes miles and years apart"; previsualisation replaced storyboards, with Ben Burtt's "videomatics" -- a household video camera, staff and relatives acting against greenscreen -- cut into CGI animatics shown to the actors on set; over 2,000 visual-effects shots, digital doubles for actors, a fully CGI Yoda built to move like the puppet -- "when Frank [Oz] would move the head, the ears would jiggle" -- Lucas refusing VistaVision for miniatures to stay "consistently digital", and the digital cameras' lack of film grain forcing more detailed models. [C32673] Star Wars: Episode III - Revenge of the Sith was released on 19 May 2005, written and directed by Lucas, his most recent directorial credit, with a $905.6 million gross against a variant of $850 million for the initial run, and the first PG-13 Star Wars film -- the last figure resting on a prequel-trilogy search snippet, not an opened page, and carried as such. [C32674]

**Takeaway:** The prequels are the student shorts at planetary scale: the same non-narrative image-making, the same borrowed music and borrowed frames, but now the Navy's unlimited film is unlimited CGI, and the director who waited twenty-two years for the tools finally gets to build the world without asking a studio for permission.

## 18. The sale and the afterlife

Semi-retirement was announced in January 2012, in Lucas's words "moving away from the business", and Kathleen Kennedy was appointed co-chair in June 2012 -- the succession is announced before the sale, and the sale is the succession. [C32675] Lucasfilm was sold to Disney for $4.05 billion, "approximately half in cash and half in shares of Disney stock", about forty million shares issued, announced on 30 October 2012, approved by the Federal Trade Commission on 4 December 2012 and completed on 21 December 2012 -- and the $2.21 billion cash figure is NOT supported by anything read this run, so this study does not use it. [C32676] Lucas stayed on as a creative consultant on The Force Awakens, and his story treatments for Episodes VII to IX were discarded, per Lucas in January 2015 -- the empire-builder becomes a consultant, and the consultants do not take his notes. [C32677] The Pixar pre-history is the same move at smaller scale: the Graphics Group, one third of Lucasfilm's Computer Division from 1979, with Edwin Catmull hired from NYIT in 1979, was sold to Steve Jobs in 1986 for $5 million plus $5 million in capital, because Lucas wanted to stop the cash-flow losses from seven years of tools research and refocus on entertainment -- he sells the tools division to save the films, twice. [C32678] The philanthropy is the afterlife: the George Lucas Educational Foundation, founded in 1991, publishing Edutopia, and the gift to USC of $175-180 million announced on 19 September 2006, "the largest single donation to USC" -- with a variant of $75 million for the USC film-school buildings, unreconciled in the pages read -- and the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, built with Mellody Hobson. [C32679] The producing slate between the directing jobs is the second career: Kagemusha (1980, executive producer, grade B on the exact credit), Twice Upon a Time (1983, executive producer, grade B), Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters (1985, executive producer, grade B), Labyrinth, Captain EO and Howard the Duck (1986, executive producer, Howard the Duck "the biggest flop of Lucas's career"), Willow (1988, story, directed by Ron Howard), The Land Before Time (1988, executive producer, grade B), Radioland Murders (1994, story), Red Tails (2012, executive producer, self-funded, taking over directing reshoots), Strange Magic (2015, writer, his first musical) and Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny (2023, executive producer, "cursory involvement in pre- and post-production"). [C32680] Tucker is the patron case in miniature: Lucas revived it in 1986, "decided to cover the $24 million budget himself" after the studios demanded it be cut to $15 million and doubted it after Labyrinth and Howard the Duck, then persuaded Paramount to distribute and cover the majority; it was released on 12 August 1988 and grossed $19.7 million, and Lucas also steered Coppola away from the Bernstein, Comden and Green musical toward a Frank Capra homage, Mr. Smith Goes to Washington -- the protege paying for the mentor's film, out of his own pocket. [C32681]

**Takeaway:** The sale is the last mechanism: the man who spent forty years building the conditions under which films get made finally sells the conditions themselves, and the money goes to the schools -- the empire ends the way the shorts began, with a student film festival and a classroom.

## 19. Aesthetic logic: build the world, then let the story walk through it

The world comes first: the used future, the control system, the jukebox, the serial -- each film starts with a world that has rules, and then lets a story walk through it, so that the plot is what happens when a person meets the rules. [C32682] Sound comes before image: Burtt's library, Murch's montage, Williams's themes, Wolfman Jack's radio -- the ear knows the world before the eye does, and the audience believes the picture because it already believes the sound. [C32683] The company is the medium: ILM, Sprocket Systems and Skywalker Sound, THX, the Ranch, Lucasfilm itself -- each film leaves behind an institution, and the institutions make the next film, so that the career is a factory that builds factories. [C32684] The borrowed frame is worn honestly: Flash Gordon, Kurosawa, Campbell, the serials -- nothing hidden, everything re-cut to his own rhythm, and the authorship is in the synthesis, not in the sources. [C32685] The budget is a plot device: THX's pursuit is called off for cost, Graffiti is made for $777,000 because the songs ate the score, Empire is self-financed on Star Wars profits, Tucker is paid for out of pocket -- the money is never just money, it is the constraint that chooses the style. [C32686] The retreat from directing is the method, not the absence of one: from 1977 to 1999 he directs nothing and builds everything, and returns only when the tools catch up with the idea -- the director who will not direct until the world can be built the way he sees it. [C32687] The synthesis is a filmmaker who stopped directing films and started directing the conditions under which films get made -- and then, having built the conditions, directed again inside them, until he sold the conditions themselves and walked away to give the money away. [C32688]

**Takeaway:** Every Lucas mechanism is the same move in a different costume: build the world first, build the sound first, build the company first, and then let the story walk through what you built -- the film is never the product, it is the proof that the world works.
