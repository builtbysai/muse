## The life: the supervisor who wrote the letter and then stepped back

Mike Weaver was born in 1937 and died on 24 June 2024. (C11905, C11911, leg 1 Sec. 2) No source found in this run gives his day or month of birth, or his place of birth, and none is stated here. (C11907, C11908, leg 1 Sec. 2) His full forenames were not established; one doctoral CV gives his Oxford supervisor's name as "Mike L.H.L. Weaver", seen only as a search-result snippet, so the expansion of those initials is NOT verified here. (C11910, leg 1 Sec. 2) No place or cause of death was found, and none is stated. (C11913, C11914, leg 1 Sec. 2) His partner was Anne Hammond, photographic historian and his co-editor of History of Photography. (C11915, C11916, leg 1 Sec. 2) His father was Herbert L. Weaver, whose dowsing discoveries were the subject of the first Revealer Cassettes. (C11917, C11918, leg 1 Sec. 2) He is not Mike Ware (born 1939), the Oxford chemistry graduate and alternative-process photographer; not the boxer Michael Dwayne Weaver; not the singer of Big Daddy Weave. (C11901-C11903, leg 1 Sec. 1)

The career runs in six phases, and the hinge is the letter: the work starts as concrete poetry and little magazines, becomes a Fluxus show conceived in correspondence, changes medium to audio tape about fringe knowledge, and then settles into the institutional history of photography, ending as editorship.

(1) Cambridge and concrete poetry, 1963-66. Weaver was a Cambridge-educated academic, working towards his doctorate when he met Stephen Bann and Philip Steadman. (C11919, C11921, leg 1 Sec. 3) In the summer of 1964 he met the concrete poet Ian Hamilton Finlay in Edinburgh, together with Bann. (C11924, leg 1 Sec. 3) He organised the first international exhibition of concrete poetry in Britain, the First International Exhibition of Concrete, Phonetic, and Kinetic Poetry, held in the Rushmore Rooms, St Catharine's College, Cambridge, 28 November - 5 December 1964, with Reg Gadney, Bann and others. (C11926, C11928, C11930, leg 1 Sec. 3) His early critical interests were objectivist and Black Mountain poetry, which he was then studying, and he was engaged on a study of William Carlos Williams and on a book of documents on Black Mountain College. (C11931, C11933, leg 1 Sec. 3) He first corresponded with George Maciunas in 1963, about work for the Cambridge concrete-poetry exhibition. (C11912, leg 1 Sec. 6)

(2) Form, 1966-69. Weaver was a co-editor of Form magazine with Philip Steadman (primary editor) and Stephen Bann: three Cambridge-educated academics running an international magazine focused on concrete poetry and kinetic art, published quarterly from summer 1966 until October 1969. (C11935, C11937, C11939, leg 1 Sec. 4) Weaver served as the magazine's American coeditor. (C11941, leg 1 Sec. 4) From the first issue Form published his "Great Little Magazines" series, excerpts and studies of other international little magazines -- a magazine that mapped magazines. (C11943, leg 1 Sec. 4) Form ceased production in October 1969 because of financial troubles, the largest blow being the withdrawal of an Arts Council of Great Britain grant. (C11945, leg 1 Sec. 4) The issue count is disputed: ten (Princeton finding aid) versus eleven (Greg Thomas, Border Blurs). (C11947-C11949, leg 1 Sec. 4)

(3) Exeter, Fluxus and Fluxshoe, 1967-73. Weaver was a lecturer in American literature at the University of Exeter, and director of the American Arts Documentation Centre there. (C11900, leg 1 Sec. 5; C11903, leg 1 Sec. 5) The SNAC search-result text says he joined Exeter in 1967 as a lecturer in American literature, later becoming Reader in American Arts and Letters, was heavily involved in the American Arts Documentation Centre, centred at the School of English c. 1970-1997, and left the University c. 1978; that page returned a bot-check when opened, so these dates rest on search-result text only. (C11955, leg 2 W04) David Mayor went to Exeter in 1970 for a post-graduate degree under Weaver at the Centre, and at Exeter Mayor was given the task of organising an exhibition of Fluxus by Weaver. (C11905, C11907, leg 1 Sec. 5) Mayor never completed his post-graduate studies, because his research fell by the wayside while he initiated and administered Fluxshoe. (C11909, leg 1 Sec. 5) Fluxshoe was originally conceived by Weaver in a letter written to Ken Friedman on 9 June 1970; Anderson and Afterimage credit Friedman and Weaver jointly, and the conflict is carried unresolved. (C11914, C11916, leg 1 Sec. 6) It was originally to be a modest exercise, mainly photocopies and publications, but grew under organiser Mayor into a larger touring exhibition. (C11919, leg 1 Sec. 6) It opened on 23 October 1972 in Falmouth and toured seven venues -- Falmouth, Exeter, Croydon, Oxford, Nottingham, Blackburn, Hastings -- to August 1973, with over 100 Fluxus and non-Fluxus artists agreeing to appear. (C11921, C11923, leg 1 Sec. 6) The Fluxshoe catalogue (77 pages, illustrated, designed by Felipe Ehrenberg, Beau Geste Press's first publication) was published before the Falmouth opening, edited by Mayor; later scholarship cites it as Friedman, Weaver and Mayor (1972). (C11925, C11927, leg 1 Sec. 6) The supplement Fluxshoe Add End A (1973) was compiled by Ehrenberg, Terry Wright and Mayor; Weaver is not named as an editor of it. (C11929, leg 1 Sec. 6) Weaver's early correspondence with Fluxus figures survives in the Tate Mayor archive: the Fluxshoe correspondence files include "a small amount of early (1964) correspondence to and from Mike Weaver". (C11931, leg 1 Sec. 6) A Tate record holds a 1976 letter from Friedman to Weaver enquiring about material, in which Friedman mentions not hearing from Weaver for six years, that is since 1970 -- the correspondence ran cold after Fluxshoe. (C12015, leg 3)

(4) Revealer Cassettes, from 1976. In 1976, together with Weaver, Mayor began publishing a series of Revealer Cassettes, an audio magazine, named for the dowsing device. (C11933, leg 1 Sec. 7) The first two issues were devoted to the dowsing discoveries of Weaver's father, Herbert L. Weaver. (C11935, leg 1 Sec. 7) Later issues covered radionics, acupuncture and the biodynamic massage methods of Gerda Boyesen. (C11937, leg 1 Sec. 7) A later Journal of Chinese Medicine profile cites "Mayor & Weaver, 1979", seen only as a search-result snippet. (C11939, leg 1 Sec. 7) No complete issue list was found; the number of issues, their dates beyond 1976 and 1979, and their full contents remain unverified. (C11944, C11945, leg 1 Sec. 7)

(5) The photography historian, 1978-89. Weaver worked as chairman of the Photography Advisory Group of the Arts Council of Great Britain from 1978 to 1983. (C11946, leg 1 Sec. 8) He wrote an essay for the Royal Photographic Society centenary catalogue Alvin Langdon Coburn, 1882-1966, Man of Mark (1982), with Margaret Harker. (C11961, leg 2 W09) His book Julia Margaret Cameron, 1815-1879 (Herbert Press, 1984), based on a John Hansard Gallery exhibition catalogue, disputed an earlier analysis of Cameron and elevated her tableaux as sincere religious interpretations. (C11962, C11963, leg 2 W10) In 1986 he published The Photographic Art: Pictorial Traditions in Britain and America (Herbert Press, 144 pages), based on the Scottish Arts Council exhibition of the same name, and Alvin Langdon Coburn: Symbolist Photographer, 1882-1966: Beyond the Craft (Aperture, 80 pages, 62 halftone illustrations), examining the Japanese and Symbolist influence on Coburn's photographs, including the 1917 Vortographs; the same year his Getty essay "A Divine Art of Photography" appeared in Whisper of the Muse: The Overstone Album and Other Photographs, on Cameron's Overstone Album of 109 albumen prints. (C11964, C11966, C11965, leg 2 W11, W12) He edited The Art of Photography 1839-1989 (Yale University Press, 1989), 472 pages with over 80 colour and 400 black-and-white illustrations, the catalogue for an exhibition shown at the Royal Academy of Arts, London, the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, and the Australian National Gallery, Canberra; the obituary notice calls him its author, the catalogue records call him its editor, and the editor role is the catalogue-record version. (C11911, C11915, C11917, leg 1 Sec. 8) He also edited British Photography in the Nineteenth Century: The Fine Art Tradition (Cambridge University Press, 1989), xviii + 304 pages, 22 essays by 19 scholars on seventeen fine-art photographers working in Britain in the nineteenth century. (C11919, leg 1 Sec. 8) He authored William Carlos Williams: The American Background (Cambridge University Press); a contemporary CUP advertisement of December 1971 already advertises it, while a later NNDB entry dates it 1977 -- 1977 cannot be the first-publication year. (C11902, C11904, C11905, leg 1 Sec. 8) His published works, in the obituary notice's phrase, stretched from Talbot, Cameron and Coburn, to Strand and Mapplethorpe. (C11900, leg 1 Sec. 8)

(6) Oxford and the journal, 1991-2000, and after. Weaver was Reader in American Literature at Linacre College, University of Oxford, according to a doctoral CV seen only as a search-result snippet; the SNAC text gives his Reader title differently, as Reader in American Arts and Letters, and the two wordings are carried as an unresolved conflict. (C11930, leg 1 Sec. 9; C11955, leg 2 W04) At Oxford he supervised doctoral students, including Erik Sherman Roraback, whose D.Phil. thesis topic was "Money and Power in Henry James". (C11933, leg 1 Sec. 9) In 1985 the collectors Marjorie and Leonard Vernon travelled to Oxford to study Victorian photography with Anne Hammond and Weaver. (C11939, leg 1 Sec. 9) He co-edited History of Photography with Anne Hammond between 1991 and 2000; John Mraz, interviewed in 2015, said Weaver "came from literary studies" and "did a wonderful job as editor of the journal History of Photography at Oxford University from 1991 to 2000", emphasising learning "to look carefully at a photo" and that "Mike could take a photo and suggest multiple meanings for the image". (C11935, C11937, C11938, leg 1 Sec. 9) His journal essays in this period include "Clement Greenberg and Walker Evans" and "Talbot's Broom and Swift's Broomstick", both in History of Photography 15 (1991). (C11941, leg 1 Sec. 9) He edited Henry Fox Talbot: Selected Texts and Bibliography (Clio Press, 1992), with his introduction "Diogenes with a Camera". (C11970, leg 2 W16) The Edward Steichen special issue of History of Photography, 17:4, Winter 1993, pages 317-402, was edited by Weaver and Hammond. (C11972, leg 2 W18) He contributed a chapter to A New History of Photography, edited by Michel Frizot (Konemann, 1998), whose title was not found. (C11926, leg 1 Sec. 8) No book by Weaver after 1992 was verified in this run, and no source found describes his later life in detail after 2000, beyond his continued editorship to 2000 and his death in 2024. (C11950, leg 2; C11906, C11907, leg 1 Sec. 11) He was a photographer in his own right, although he would claim otherwise, in the obituary notice's words; Hammond wrote about his own photography in "Mike Weaver. The Eye of the Photo Critic", Studies in Photography, Winter 2024, pp. 122-123, which was not located online, and no individual photograph by Weaver, with title and date, was identified in the sources consulted. (C11948, leg 1 Sec. 8; C11900, leg 1 Sec. 10; C11950, leg 2 W20) Geoffrey Batchen's obituary of Weaver was published on the History of Photography website on 10 December 2024; the full text returned HTTP 403 and was not read, so nothing from it is reported here. (C11946, C11948, leg 1 Sec. 10) A Polaroid portrait of Weaver by Mark Haworth-Booth, dated 1978-1983, is in the National Portrait Gallery collection, reference NPG x199233; it is a portrait OF Weaver, not a work BY him. (C11903, leg 1 Sec. 10; C11979, leg 2)

In his own words. No extended interview with Weaver and no book-length autobiography was found, so direct quotation from Weaver himself is limited to short passages, all second-hand or editorial. (C11943, C11944, leg 1 Sec. 10) Batchen, quoted in the obituary notice, said: "Mike Weaver was always a powerful presence in any gathering devoted to the study of photography: learned, single-minded and articulate (and sometimes also irascible) and determined to bring the focus back to pictures and their artistic capacities, where he thought it belonged." Evolution. Early work (1966-76) is editing and organising on subjects other than photography history: a concrete-poetry magazine, an article on abstract film, a Fluxus show conceived in a letter, an audio magazine about dowsing and acupuncture. (C11952-C11954, leg 2) The middle years (1978-89) are institutional and book-form: the Arts Council chairmanship, then the Coburn and Cameron books, the pictorial-traditions survey, and the two big 1989 edited surveys, with subjects running from Talbot, Cameron and Coburn to Strand and Mapplethorpe. (C11950, leg 2; C11964, leg 2 W11) The late years (1991-2000) are editorial and close-reading: a decade co-editing History of Photography, two 1991 journal essays, a Talbot sourcebook, a Steichen special issue; after that the record thins to a chapter title never found and, in 2024, someone else writing about his own photographs. (C11969, leg 2 W15; C11950, leg 2) The arc is from the organiser who starts things in letters and magazines to the historian who reframes a whole field in surveys, and finally to the editor who shapes the field by selecting and arranging other people's work -- and the through-line is that he is almost never the solo author of an object: the magazine has three editors, the show has a delegate, the tape has a co-publisher, the survey has nineteen contributors, the journal has a co-editor. (C12058)

## Mechanism M1: conception by correspondence -- a letter becomes an exhibition

A letter is not a note about a show; it is the show's first draft. Fluxshoe was set going by Weaver's letter to Ken Friedman on 9 June 1970, and from October 1970 material was shipped to Mayor, who was given the task of organising it. (C12007, leg 3) What began as a modest exercise, mainly photocopies and publications, grew under Mayor into a seven-venue tour with over 100 artists. (C11919, leg 1 Sec. 6) The mechanism is the letter as a commissioning document: one correspondent proposes, the other receives, and the exhibition is the correspondence made public, with the gaps in the correspondence -- Friedman not hearing from Weaver for six years after 1970 -- visible as the show's own history. (C12015, leg 3)

**Takeaway:** the letter is the first edit. A piece built on this mechanism must start from a single sent letter and let the replies decide what the show becomes, with unanswered letters visible as gaps, not hidden.

## Mechanism M2: delegation as curatorial method -- hand it away and let it grow

The supervisor gives the task away, and the delegate's additions are the work. Weaver gave Mayor the Fluxus exhibition task at Exeter; Mayor's research "fell by the wayside" while he initiated and administered the tour, and he never completed his post-graduate studies. (C11907, C11909, leg 1 Sec. 5) Anderson's account is explicit about what the delegation did: the show "was originally to be a modest exercise, consisting mainly of photocopies and publications, but as it happened, with the additions and changes that organiser David Mayor allowed, it became a lesson in the living development of art". (C11919, leg 1 Sec. 6) The mechanism is curating by surrender: the originator sets the frame and then does not control the contents, and the show's growth is the delegate's, not the originator's.

**Takeaway:** the hand-off is the method. A piece on this mechanism must let the viewer, or a second agent, change the work after it is set going, and must show the changes as the work -- never present the originator's first version as the finished thing.

## Mechanism M3: the little magazine as network map -- a magazine that maps magazines

Form (1966-69) was an international magazine focused on concrete poetry and kinetic art, edited by three Cambridge academics, and from its first issue it published Weaver's "Great Little Magazines" series, excerpts and studies of other international little magazines. (C11937, C11943, leg 1 Sec. 4) Weaver was the magazine's American coeditor. (C11941, leg 1 Sec. 4) The mechanism is a periodical that treats other periodicals as its subject: the magazine is a map of the network it belongs to, and each issue re-draws the map by excerpting a different node. The archive proves the scale: the Princeton Form papers (C1489, 1965-1974, bought 2014) hold well over a thousand items, including correspondence between the editors, subscribers and contributors. (C11913, C11915, leg 1 Sec. 12)

**Takeaway:** the magazine is a map, and the map is the content. A piece on this mechanism must show a publication whose pages are excerpts from other publications, with the network visible as the table of contents -- and must let the map be re-drawn by choosing which node to excerpt next.

## Mechanism M4: the audio magazine of fringe knowledge -- a magazine you listen to

Revealer Cassettes (from 1976, with Mayor) is a spoken-word cassette magazine named for the dowsing device, and its subjects are the fringe: the first two issues on the dowsing discoveries of Weaver's father, Herbert L. Weaver; later issues on radionics, acupuncture and the biodynamic massage methods of Gerda Boyesen. (C11933, C11935, C11937, leg 1 Sec. 7) The mechanism is a change of medium carrying the same postal, serial logic onto tape: a magazine that arrives by post, is played rather than read, and treats subjects the art world would not print as worth a serial. No complete issue list was found, so the mechanism is carried on the four named subjects and the 1979 citation only, and no issue count is stated. (C11944, leg 1 Sec. 7; C12037, leg 3)

**Takeaway:** the medium changes and the method survives. A piece on this mechanism must carry a serial, postal logic onto a medium that was never meant for it -- a magazine on tape, a catalogue as a recording -- and must treat its fringe subject with the same editorial care as a mainstream one.

## Mechanism M5: close looking as method -- one photograph, many meanings

Weaver's editorial doctrine, as Mraz reports it, was learning "to look carefully at a photo", and "Mike could take a photo and suggest multiple meanings for the image". (C11937, C11938, leg 1 Sec. 9) Batchen's version: Weaver was "determined to bring the focus back to pictures and their artistic capacities, where he thought it belonged". (C11945, leg 1 Sec. 10) The mechanism is the single image held still and read until it gives up more than one meaning: it is what the two 1991 essays do ("Clement Greenberg and Walker Evans", "Talbot's Broom and Swift's Broomstick"), what a decade of co-editing History of Photography does issue by issue, and what Hammond's 2024 piece does for Weaver's own photographs. (C11941, leg 1 Sec. 9; C11950, leg 2 W17) The viewer is not shown many pictures quickly; one picture is held, and the reading accumulates.

**Takeaway:** hold one image until it means several things. A piece on this mechanism must keep a single photograph on screen and let successive readings -- captions, crops, comparisons -- accumulate on it, rather than cycling through many images.

## Mechanism M6: the survey as re-framing -- the argument is the selection

The big surveys do not illustrate a history; they argue one by selecting it. The Photographic Art (1986) argues photography's pictorial traditions in Britain and America; The Art of Photography 1839-1989 (1989, 472 pages, 80+ colour and 400+ black-and-white illustrations) argues the whole medium from 1839 to 1989 through a selection shown at the Royal Academy, Houston and Canberra; British Photography in the Nineteenth Century (1989) argues the fine-art tradition through 22 essays by 19 scholars on seventeen photographers. (C11907, C11911, C11919, leg 1 Sec. 8) The mechanism is selection as argument: the pictures chosen, and the order they are put in, are the thesis, and the catalogue is the thesis in book form. A contemporary review called the 1989 catalogue beautifully printed but criticised its organisation -- which is exactly the point: the organisation IS the argument, so it is the thing a reader argues with. (C11967, leg 2 W13)

**Takeaway:** the selection is the thesis. A piece on this mechanism must make its argument by choosing and ordering -- a survey whose order the viewer can rearrange, and whose argument changes when the order does.

## Mechanism M7: the monograph as close reading -- one photographer, read properly

The single-photographer books re-read one oeuvre until it changes shape. The 1984 Cameron book disputed an earlier analysis and elevated her tableaux as sincere religious interpretations; the 1986 Coburn book examined the Japanese and Symbolist influence on his photographs, including the 1917 Vortographs, a radical series of cubist photographs; the 1986 Getty essay read Cameron's approach through the Overstone Album, a collection of 109 albumen prints. (C11963, leg 2 W10; C11966, leg 2 W12; C11925, leg 1 Sec. 8) The mechanism is the monograph as a sustained act of attention on one body of work, where the re-reading is the contribution, not new material. Weaver's own range, Talbot to Mapplethorpe, is carried the same way: by re-reading, not by discovering. (C11900, leg 1 Sec. 8)

**Takeaway:** one oeuvre, read until it changes. A piece on this mechanism must take a single body of work and let a sustained re-reading change what it means -- the same photographs, a different argument, over time.

## Mechanism M8: editing as authorship -- the field is shaped by arrangement

Weaver shapes photography history mostly by selecting and arranging other people's work: two edited surveys in 1989, the Talbot source volume in 1992 with his introduction "Diogenes with a Camera", a decade co-editing History of Photography with Hammond, and the Steichen special issue of 1993. (C11911, C11919, leg 1 Sec. 8; C11970, leg 2 W16; C11935, leg 1 Sec. 9) The mechanism is authorship without single authorship: the editor's hand is visible in what is included, what is left out, and what is put next to what, and the field remembers the arrangement as the editor's work. It is the same hand as in Form, in the Fluxshoe letter, and in the Revealer tapes -- the hand that starts the system and lets others fill it. (C12058)

**Takeaway:** the arrangement is the authorship. A piece on this mechanism must let the viewer arrange -- select, sequence, juxtapose -- and must treat the resulting arrangement as the work itself, signed by the arranger.

## Aesthetic logic

Weaver has no recognisable style, and that is the point three times over: he is not primarily an author of objects at all, he is the man who starts the system and then steps back. The continuity is not a look but a method, and the method has three moves that repeat across the whole career. First, propose: write the letter, found the magazine, start the tape series, commission the survey -- one document that sets a frame. Second, delegate: give the task to Mayor, let the co-editors fill the magazine, let the contributors fill the survey, let the journal's authors fill the decade -- the work grows through other people's additions, and the originator does not control the contents. Third, arrange: select, sequence and juxtapose, so that the order is the argument and the arrangement is the authorship. The materials are the cheapest communication systems that travel: a letter, a quarterly magazine, a cassette tape, a 77-page catalogue, a 472-page survey, a journal issue. The tone is scholarly and stubborn where Mayor's is funny and domestic: "learned, single-minded and articulate (and sometimes also irascible)", determined to bring the focus back to pictures and their artistic capacities. The scale moves from a single letter to a seven-town tour to a three-venue international survey to a decade of a journal, and the durability moves from a letter that can be lost to books that are still cited twenty years after the last one -- but the hand stays the same: the hand that writes the letter, edits the magazine, and arranges the survey. A piece built from this study should feel proposed, delegated and arranged -- a letter sent, a task handed over, a selection re-ordered -- and should never look like a solo artwork pretending to be a system, or a system pretending to have a single author. (C12058)
