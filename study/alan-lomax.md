# Alan Lomax: Study Notes

**Site:** Alan Lomax, b. Sunday 31 January 1915, Austin, Texas; d. Friday 19 July 2002, Mease Countryside Hospital, Safety Harbor, Florida, age 87. Folklorist, field recordist, broadcaster, and the man who turned other people's songs into an American public inheritance -- first as his father's assistant on the Library of Congress prison-farm trips of 1933, then as Assistant in Charge of the Archive of American Folk Song, then as a BBC presenter, then as the theorist of Cantometrics and the builder of the Global Jukebox. He recorded Lead Belly, Jelly Roll Morton, Woody Guthrie and Muddy Waters for the first time; he carried the repertoire Sharp and Neal had collected in print back out into the field and onto tape; and he spent his last forty years arguing, in print and in code, that every local singing style is a whole social order made audible. [C35083]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Mary Neal study: that study is about the woman who built the revival's first room -- the Esperance Club, the working girls, the dance passed from body to body -- and then lost the institution she built to the man she taught it to. Lomax never met Neal -- she died on Thursday 22 June 1944, six years before he first set foot in England -- and he never met Sharp, who died on Monday 23 June 1924, nine years before Lomax's first field trip. The doorway is not a meeting. Where Neal's study is about keeping people moving, this one is about keeping sound -- and about what keeping sound does to the people who made it. [C35084]

## 1. Austin, 31 January 1915: the folklorist's son

Alan Lomax was born on Sunday 31 January 1915 in Austin, Texas, the third of four children of Bess (Brown) Lomax and John Avery Lomax (1867-1948), the pioneering folklorist whose *Cowboy Songs and Other Frontier Ballads* (1910) had made him the country's best-known collector. His sister Bess Lomax Hawes and his brother John Lomax Jr. also became folklorists; the family trade was collecting, and Alan was raised inside it. Frail health -- childhood asthma, chronic ear infections -- meant mostly homeschooling in elementary school, then Terrill School for Boys in Dallas, then one year at Choate School in Connecticut, where he graduated eighth in his class at fifteen in 1930. The formation that matters is not the schooling but the household: a father who believed the songs of working people were a national literature, and a son who would spend his life arguing the same point louder, with better equipment, and to a much larger audience.

**Takeaway:** He does not discover folk song; he inherits it, the way a printer's son inherits the press. Everything after 1933 is the inheritance being put to work. [C35087]

## 2. Harvard, arrest, and the road, 1930-1933

He matriculated at the University of Texas at Austin instead of Harvard, owing to his mother's declining health; he read Nietzsche, wrote for *The Daily Texan*, and resigned when it refused his birth-control editorial; he collected "race" records and took dates to Black-owned nightclubs at risk of expulsion. His mother died during the spring term of his first UT year -- the exact date is not stated in any source read. Harvard funded his second year (age sixteen): philosophy and physics, a long-distance Plato and Pre-Socratics reading course with UT professor Albert P. Brogan, radical politics, pneumonia, suffering grades. In 1932, aged seventeen, he was arrested in Boston in a demonstration defending Edith Berkman -- the "red flame", threatened with deportation -- and fined $25 for disturbing the peace. He told the FBI in 1942 that he had been fifteen; the record makes him seventeen, and his own statement is the outlier. The politics are not a phase to be edited out of the biography: the FBI file on him ran from 1942 to 1979, some eight hundred pages, and the politics are the reason he left America in 1950.

**Takeaway:** The collector arrives already radicalised. The microphone will be pointed at prisoners and sharecroppers because the politics point it there first. [C35088]

## 3. The prison farms: Lead Belly, 1933-1934

At seventeen he left study to join his father's Library of Congress collecting trips. The Lomaxes began in Texas in June 1933, driving a Ford with a disc recorder in the trunk through the Southern prison farms, on the stated premise that a distinct Black folk music existed which commercial "race" records had not captured. At Angola, Louisiana, in July 1933 they recorded Huddie "Lead Belly" Ledbetter -- the first session on Sunday 16 July 1933, seven songs including the first recording of "Goodnight, Irene". They returned in the summer of 1934. Governor O. K. Allen commuted Lead Belly's sentence on 25 July 1934 and he was released on Wednesday 1 August 1934. The popular telling -- Lomax delivered Lead Belly's petition-song to the governor and sang him out of prison -- is a legend the Lomaxes themselves promoted and the prison records contradict: the release came through built-up good-time credits under Louisiana law, and the state insisted the song had nothing to do with it. The two books that came out of the trips, *American Ballads and Folk Songs* (1934) and *Negro Folk Songs as Sung by Lead Belly* (1936), are the first Lomax father-son anthologies and the template for all the later ones. The discovery that matters here is not only Lead Belly. It is the method: go where the commercial companies never went, record where the song is sung rather than where it is sold, and let the singer's name stand on the label.

**Takeaway:** The prison is the first listening post. The whole career is the same move repeated at larger scale: find the room commerce never entered, and put the microphone in it. [C35089]

## 4. First work alone: Bahamas, Haiti, the degree, 1935-1937

His first field collecting without his father came in the summer of 1935, in Florida and the Bahamas with Zora Neale Hurston and Mary Elizabeth Barnicle -- the earliest field recordings in the Bahamas, spongers' chanteys and anthems from Andros and Cat Island. He returned to UT that fall and was awarded a BA in philosophy, summa cum laude, Phi Beta Kappa, in May 1936. He married Elizabeth Harold Goodman, a UT student, in February 1937 -- the exact day is not found in any source read -- and their daughter Anne, later Anna (Anna Lomax Wood), was born to the marriage. The couple did field research in Haiti on their honeymoon; Elizabeth assisted the recording in Haiti, Alabama, Appalachia and Mississippi. One wrinkle the study does not smooth over: the Grammy-nominated set *Alan Lomax in Haiti* dates its recordings 1936-1937, which does not square cleanly with a 1937 honeymoon -- either the set's date range is loose or there was a 1936 Haiti trip, and it is unresolved. The pattern of the marriage matters more than the dates: Elizabeth was a working partner, not a bystander, and the fieldwork was a household enterprise from the start.

**Takeaway:** By twenty-two he has done the three things that define him: recorded alone, taken the degree, and married a collaborator. The solo career is never really solo. [C35090]

## 5. Assistant in Charge: the Archive, 1937-1942

Alan became the Archive of American Folk Song's first official employee in 1936 (salary $620) and permanent Assistant in Charge in June 1937, serving until late 1942; his father was Honorary Consultant and Curator from 1934. The title matters and the study uses it exactly: he was Assistant in Charge, not "director" unqualified -- Wikipedia's lead calls him director, but the body and the Library's own sources say Assistant in Charge, and the Archive heads list gives Alan Lomax 1937-42. The Archive had been founded in 1928; Robert Winslow Gordon was its first director. Under Lomax the Archive grew by thousands of discs: the 1933-42 Library of Congress field recordings, to which he and his father and numerous collaborators contributed more than ten thousand field recordings in total -- the figure is the Archive's, with collaborators, and must never be quoted as Lomax's personal tally. His Michigan and Wisconsin trip in 1938 (a 1935 Plymouth, a Presto disc recorder, a movie camera) yielded some 250 discs and eight reels of film; more than four hundred recordings survive at the Library. Funding for Library folk-song collecting was terminated by Congress in 1942, which is why his later collecting was independent -- the institution that made him could not keep him, and the rest of the career is the same work done without it.

**Takeaway:** The Archive is the first machine he runs, and the first one that is taken away from him. He spends the next sixty years rebuilding it in other forms. [C35091]

## 6. Jelly Roll Morton: the long interview, 1938

In May 1938 he invited Jelly Roll Morton -- heard playing at the Music Box in Washington, D.C. -- to record at the Library's Coolidge Auditorium. The sessions ran from Monday 23 May to 14 December 1938: eight or more hours of talk and piano, the first recorded oral biography of a jazz musician. The book that came out of it, *Mister Jelly Roll* (1950), is a classic of jazz literature; the 8-CD Rounder set of the complete recordings won two Grammys on Wednesday 8 February 2006. The date of the book is itself a small split -- Folkstreams gives 1949, the standard bibliographic date is 1950, and 1950 is used here as publication, 1949 as disputed. What matters is the form Morton forced on him: the interview as a recording technique, speech as music, a life told to the microphone with songs embedded in the telling. Lomax would use the same form for the rest of his career -- the 1946 *Blues in the Mississippi Night* session (Memphis Slim, Sonny Boy Williamson, Big Bill Broonzy talking blues philosophy), the 1959 field tapes full of spoken introductions and life stories.

**Takeaway:** Morton teaches him that the talk is part of the song. From here on, Lomax records conversations, not just performances. [C35092]

## 7. Muddy Waters and Coahoma County, 1941-1942

With Fisk University -- John Work III, Lewis Jones, Samuel C. Adams Jr. -- Lomax ran the Coahoma County, Mississippi study of 1941-42, the deepest pre-war blues documentation ever made: Son House, David "Honeyboy" Edwards, Willie Brown, and, at Stovall Plantation, McKinley "Muddy Waters" Morganfield. Lomax first recorded Muddy Waters on Thursday 28 August 1941, with John W. Work III present, and returned in July 1942 to record him again; "Country Blues" and "I Be's Troubled" were issued on the Library's Archive of Folk Song series in 1942. The date carries a variant -- some sources give 31 August -- and the study uses 28 August per the National Park Service and flags the other. The session is the career's most famous playback moment: Lomax played the disc back to Waters on the spot, in his house, and the singer heard himself for the first time. He had gone to Clarksdale in 1941 hoping to record Robert Johnson and was told Johnson had died in 1938; he recorded Johnson's contemporaries instead, and the lead of his own Wikipedia article still wrongly credits him with "discovering" Johnson. The Coahoma study is also the clearest statement of the method's limit: the recording is an occasion, not an extraction -- the microphone is in the singer's house, the singer hears the result, and the result changes what happens next.

**Takeaway:** The playback is the hinge. The disc that talks back turns the singer from a source into a collaborator -- and turns the archive from a vault into a mirror. [C35093]

## 8. Radio: the broadcast as a campfire, 1939-1945

Late in 1939 Lomax began hosting *American Folk Song* and *Wellsprings of Music* on CBS's *American School of the Air* -- daily school broadcasts reaching a claimed ten million students in two hundred thousand classrooms, in the United States, Canada, Hawaii and Alaska. In 1940, under his supervision, RCA issued Woody Guthrie's *Dust Bowl Ballads* and Lead Belly's *The Midnight Special and Other Southern Prison Songs* -- biographer Szwed calls them some of the first concept albums. *Back Where I Come From* (CBS, with Nicholas Ray, 1940) ran twenty-one weeks with a racially integrated cast -- Lead Belly, Josh White, Sonny Terry, Brownie McGhee, Burl Ives -- and was cancelled in February 1941 for lack of a sponsor. One *School of the Air* programme co-authored with Guthrie won best Music Education Program of the year, and the series pushed the Music Educators National Conference to adopt American folk song in public-school teaching. On Monday 8 December 1941 he telegrammed fieldworkers in ten localities to collect ordinary Americans' reactions to Pearl Harbor; the "Dear Mr. President" interviews followed in January and February 1942. The radio work is the second machine: the field recording, which was a document of one room, becomes a broadcast to millions of rooms, on a schedule, with a host who introduces the singer and links one song to the next.

**Takeaway:** Radio is the field recording scaled up without changing its shape: one room's song, told weekly, to an audience that gathers, listens, disperses, and returns. [C35094]

## 9. War work and the blacklist, 1941-1950

He spent the remaining war years with the Office of War Information and the U.S. Army Special Services unit, produced and hosted CBS's *Transatlantic Call*, and made the 1944 ballad opera *The Martins and the Coys* for the BBC, with Guthrie, Ives, Seeger and Sonny Terry, alongside the wartime songbook *Freedom Songs of the United Nations* (1943). The postwar years in New York were the busiest of his life as an impresario: Decca folk albums, Town Hall and Carnegie Hall concerts of blues, calypso and flamenco, *Your Ballad Man* (1949, Mutual), *On Top of Old Smokey* (1948, Mutual), and the 1949 V.D. Radio Project ballad dramas -- public-health songs with Roy Acuff, Guthrie, Hank Williams and Sister Rosetta Tharpe, folk stars enlisted against syphilis. He was a founding member of People's Songs and ran campaign music for Henry A. Wallace's 1948 Progressive Party presidential run. The FBI investigation that had begun in 1942 ran until 1979; in June 1942 the Bureau asked Librarian of Congress Archibald MacLeish to fire him, and MacLeish refused, finding no evidence of subversion. In June 1950 *Red Channels* listed him as a Communist sympathiser; with the McCarran Act debated that summer he renewed his passport, cancelled engagements, and sailed on Sunday 24 September 1950 on RMS *Mauretania* for England, telling his agent he hoped to return in January "if things cleared up". He insisted publicly that he was in Britain only for the Columbia world-music project, not as a "victim of witch-hunting" -- the blacklist pressure is the context, and his own denial is part of the record, and the study presents both rather than stating "fled" as unqualified fact.

**Takeaway:** The blacklist is the second thing taken away from him, after the Archive's funding. Exile is what turns an American collector into a transatlantic one. [C35095]

## 10. London: the BBC and the World Library, 1950-1958

Based in London through the 1950s, he edited the eighteen-volume *Columbia World Library of Folk and Primitive Music* -- thirty volumes planned, only eighteen issued -- the LP anthology that funded his European decade and, in effect, invented world music as a commercial category. The two BBC titles must not be swapped: *The Song Hunter* was 1953, six twenty-minute BBC television episodes commissioned by a young David Attenborough; *A Ballad Hunter* was 1957, on the BBC Home Service, radio. Peter Kennedy's memoir calls the June 1953 television programme "A Ballad Hunter in Britain", which conflates the two, and the study does not repeat the error. He formed the skiffle group Alan Lomax and the Ramblers (with Ewan MacColl, Peggy Seeger and Shirley Collins) for British television; his ballad opera *Big Rock Candy Mountain* premiered in December 1955 at Joan Littlewood's Theatre Workshop, featuring Ramblin' Jack Elliott; his film *Oss Oss Wee Oss* -- the Padstow May Day 'Obby 'Oss rite on Kodacolor, directed and scripted by Lomax, produced by Peter Kennedy, camera George Pickow, sound Jean Ritchie -- was shot in 1951 and released in 1953 as a sixteen-minute short, a Margaret Mead classroom favourite. (The sixty-minute figure in some sources likely describes a longer cut or a combined programme; the canonical short is sixteen minutes.) MI5 surveilled him in the early 1950s and concluded, in files released in 2007, that though left-wing there was no evidence he was a Communist.

**Takeaway:** London is where the collector becomes a broadcaster in a second country. The same repertoire, the same microphone, a new audience -- and a new medium, television, that puts the singer's face beside the singer's voice. [C35096]

## 11. Scotland and Ireland, 1951

In the summer of 1951 Lomax made his first song-collecting tour of Scotland, accompanied by Hamish Henderson -- introduced to him by Ewan MacColl -- and Calum and Sorley Maclean: some twenty-five hours recorded, about two hundred and fifty songs, in the north-east and the Western Isles, with a seventy-pound Magnecord -- Jeannie Robertson, Jimmy MacBeath, Davie Stewart. He is credited as the catalyst and inspiration for the School of Scottish Studies, founded the same year, 1951, which acquired the recordings. In Ireland, from January 1951, his guide was Seamus Ennis, the uilleann piper, on a six-week trip with Robin Roberts and Jean Ritchie: Elizabeth Cronin's songs in Irish at Ballyvourney, Margaret Barry, the Traveller singer. The 1951 trips are the doorway in miniature: the repertoire Sharp had collected in print in Appalachia in 1916-18, and the ballads Kennedy-Fraser had collected on wax cylinders in the Hebrides from 1905, are now being sung to Lomax's microphone by living singers in the same traditions. He did not collect these songs; he re-recorded them, in stereo-capable sound, with names attached, and then published them in the ten-album *Folk Songs of Great Britain* (1961) and the *Columbia World Library* volumes.

**Takeaway:** Scotland and Ireland are the proof of the relay. The songs Sharp printed are still being sung; Lomax's job is to catch them while the singers are still alive to sing them. [C35097]

## 12. Spain and Italy, 1952-1955

Spain took seven months and produced some three thousand items plus hundreds of photographs -- the dates are given as 1952-53 in some sources and 1953-54 in others, with the bulk in 1952-53 and further work into 1954, and the study carries the spread rather than choosing. The Seville "Saeta" and the Galician "Alborada de Vigo" he recorded later fed Miles Davis and Gil Evans's *Sketches of Spain* (1960). Italy, in 1953-54 with Diego Carpitella (BBC and the Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia), produced one of the most representative folk-song collections of any single culture Lomax ever made, issued as two *Columbia World Library* volumes, and Lomax called 1954 his happiest year. The photo books that came out of the trips decades later -- *Mirades / Miradas / Glances* (2006, Spain) and *L'anno piu felice della mia vita* (2008, Italy) -- are evidence of something the audio alone does not show: Lomax photographed as he recorded, and his visual eye was part of the field method, not an afterthought. The European trips are also where the Cantometrics idea takes root: listening to Spanish, Italian, Scottish and Irish singing side by side, he begins to ask whether singing style itself is a measurable fingerprint of a society.

**Takeaway:** Europe is where collecting becomes comparison. The single song stops being the unit, and the style -- the way a whole culture sings -- becomes the thing to measure. [C35098]

## 13. Return and the Southern Journey, 1959-1960

He returned to New York in the late 1950s -- TSHA says 1957, Wikipedia says 1959, and the study does not pick a year -- and produced *Folksong '59* at Carnegie Hall in April 1959 (the exact day is not established): Jimmy Driftwood, the Selah Jubilee Singers, Muddy Waters and Memphis Slim, Earl Taylor and the Stoney Mountain Boys, Pete and Mike Seeger, the Cadillacs -- billed as the first time rock 'n' roll and bluegrass played Carnegie Hall, and called by Izzy Young "probably the turning point in American folk music". The Southern Journey, with Shirley Collins -- met at MacColl's party in London in 1954, his assistant and partner -- left New York City in a rented Buick Roadmaster in late August 1959 with a prototype Ampex 600 stereo recorder and German microphones, funded at the last minute by Atlantic's Ertegun brothers. Recording began on Monday 24 August 1959 at Bluefield, Virginia, with Hobart Smith; over two months and more than seventy hours, through Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, Arkansas, Georgia and North Carolina, they recorded fife-and-drum marches, blues, unaccompanied ballads, penitentiary work songs and field hollers, and congregational singing. The discovery of the trip was Mississippi Fred McDowell at Como, Mississippi -- sessions on Monday 21, Tuesday 22 and Friday 25 September 1959, walking out of the woods with a guitar. These were the first stereo field recordings in the Southern United States, issued as the seven-LP Atlantic *Southern Folk Heritage Series* (1960) and twelve more LPs for Prestige, and the singers -- Almeda Riddle, Fred McDowell, Hobart Smith, Ed Young, Bessie Jones and the Georgia Sea Island Singers -- were booked at Newport within a year. Two honest qualifications belong here: the trip's span is given as July-November 1959 in some sources, but the dated itinerary runs late August to late October, and the study uses the itinerary; and Collins's own testimony corrects Lomax's 1993 book, which reduces her to "Shirley Collins was along for the trip", though she made the notes, drafted the contracts and was involved in every part of the recording. He married Antoinette Marchand on Saturday 26 August 1961; they separated the following year and divorced in 1967.

**Takeaway:** The Southern Journey is the 1933 trip done properly: same road, same prisons and porches, but in stereo, with a partner who keeps the books -- and the singers brought to the stage this time, not just to the disc. [C35099]

## 14. Cantometrics: the song as a map, 1959-1968

Lomax first publicly proposed Cantometrics in 1959 and launched a group project at Columbia University, where he was Research Associate in Anthropology from 1961 or 1962 until 1989. With Victor Grauer -- the first co-creator with Lomax of the Cantometrics computer coding system -- plus Conrad Arensberg, Irmgard Bartenieff, Forrestine Paulay, Joan Halifax, Norman Berkowitz and others, he built a system that rated a culture's singing style on thirty-seven factors, each on a one-to-five scale, judged by pairs of listeners: vocal tension, breathiness, rasp, melisma, group cohesion, phrase length, and the rest. The sample grew from 2,527 songs from 233 cultures to 5,776 songs from 1,026 societies. The claim was that singing style correlates with social structure -- sexually restrictive, punitive societies with vocal tension; group singing and polyphony with fewer restrictions on women and more egalitarian organisation -- presented to the American Association for the Advancement of Science in 1966 and published as *Folk Song Style and Culture* (1968), with the boldest form in "The Evolutionary Taxonomy of Culture" in *Science* (1972, with Norman Berkowitz). Sister systems followed: Choreometrics for dance and movement, Parlametrics for speech, Phonotactics for vowel frequency, Minutage for breath management. The reception is part of the story and the study carries both sides: Savage's 2018 comprehensive review calls Cantometrics arguably both the most ambitious and the most controversial undertaking in music and science, finds the song-style to social-structure correlations weakly supported, and notes it was not widely adopted by other researchers for decades -- while the 2022 PLOS ONE Global Jukebox paper presents the cleaned dataset as a live research resource. Both are true: the theory failed to convince the field, and the dataset survived it.

**Takeaway:** Cantometrics is the collector's instinct turned into a measuring instrument. He stops asking what a song says and starts asking what the way it is sung says about the people who sing it -- and the instrument outlives the argument. [C35100]

## 15. The grey-out: cultural equity, 1972-1983

The polemic that gives the late career its name is "An Appeal for Cultural Equity" (1972, *World of Music*): a centralised, commercial communication system is imposing "a few standardized, mass-produced, and cheapened cultures everywhere" -- a "grey-out" that silences local music. The cure he proposes is deliberate: money, airtime and classroom time for local musics, as a matter of human rights. The Association for Cultural Equity, founded in 1983 and moved to Hunter College in 1989, institutionalises the appeal; its mission is to preserve, publish, repatriate and freely disseminate the collections. The same instinct shows in its raw form at Newport in 1965, when he opposed the electrified Butterfield Blues Band set at Dylan's performance -- a defence of the unamplified, local sound against the homogenising star system. TSHA alone adds a fistfight with Dylan's manager; Wikipedia says only that he was strongly opposed, and the study does not present the fistfight as established. The grey-out is the one Lomax idea that needs no technical apparatus to understand: a broadcast signal tries to pull every singer into one grey unison, and the work of a lifetime is to keep the local voices breaking away from it.

**Takeaway:** The grey-out is the enemy the whole career was fighting before it had a name. Every microphone, every broadcast, every archive is a move against it. [C35101]

## 16. Films, Voyager, and the late books, 1970s-1993

The late work is documentary and institutional. He was consultant to the Smithsonian Folklife Festival from 1967; he made the *Rhythms of Earth* dance films (1974-84) from the Choreometrics work; he was consultant to Carl Sagan for the Voyager Golden Record, launched on Monday 5 September 1977, pushing ethnic music onto the record at the expense of Western classical -- Blind Willie Johnson, Louis Armstrong, Chuck Berry, Andean panpipes, Navajo chants, Azerbaijani mugham, Mbuti polyphony, Valya Balkanska's Bulgarian shepherdess song. *The Land Where the Blues Began* -- the film shot in the Delta in 1978, completed 1979, syndicated 1980 (the 1985 date in some sources is likely an award year) -- became the book of 1993, which won the National Book Critics Circle Award for non-fiction; the "National Book Award" in some sources is a conflation and is not used. *American Patchwork*, five films shot 1978-85 from four hundred hours of footage in eight states and Washington, D.C., aired on PBS in 1991 (the 1990 date in TSHA is likely a preview or syndication date). The Alan Lomax Collection debuted on Rounder Records in 1997, more than a hundred CDs planned, produced by his daughter Anna Lomax Wood and Jeffrey Greenberg. Honours accumulated: the National Medal of Arts in 1986, Library of Congress Living Legend in 2000, an honorary doctorate from Tulane in 2001. The books of the period -- *The Folk Songs of North America* (1960, the revival's bible), *The Rainbow Sign* (1959, the portrait of Vera Ward Hall), *Hard-Hitting Songs for Hard-Hit People* (compiled in the 1940s with Guthrie and Seeger, finally published 1967) -- are the fieldwork turned into print, the same relay as ever: tape to book to stage to classroom.

**Takeaway:** The late career is the archive becoming public property. The films, the Golden Record, the Rounder edition -- all of them are the same recordings, handed to larger and larger audiences. [C35102]

## 17. Stroke, retirement, death, 1995-2002

He was felled by strokes in his early eighties -- the sources genuinely disagree, and the study does not force them into agreement: the Association for Cultural Equity's remembrance says he was "felled by not just one but two strokes in 1995"; Encyclopedia.com says "two strokes at the age of 80", which is 1995; the American Studies Association archive review says a "debilitating stroke in 1996"; *Mix* magazine in 1997, quoting his daughter Anna, says she became archive caretaker "after his second stroke at the age of 82", which is 1997 or an age miscounted. The safe wording is that a massive stroke in the mid-1990s forced his retirement in 1996, and he moved to Florida, convalescing at his daughter Anna's home in Tarpon Springs. He died on Friday 19 July 2002, age 87, at Mease Countryside Hospital in Safety Harbor, Florida, survived by his daughter Anna, his stepdaughter Shelley Roitman, his sister Bess Lomax Hawes, and his grandson Odysseus Chairetakis. No obituary read states a cause of death, and the study does not supply one. Posthumous: the Grammy Trustees Award in 2003; in March 2004 the Library of Congress acquired his independent, post-1942 archive, reuniting seventy years of work; the *Jelly Roll Morton: The Complete Library of Congress Recordings* set won two Grammys on Wednesday 8 February 2006; from 2012 some seventeen thousand of his post-1946 recordings were streamed free by the Library and the Association for Cultural Equity. Bob Dylan had publicly thanked him at Wolf Trap on 24 August 1997 as "one of those who unlocked the secrets of this kind of music".

**Takeaway:** He outlives the century he recorded. The archive he built by hand in the 1930s ends up free on the internet in the 2010s -- which is what he was arguing for all along. [C35103]

## 18. The listening machine: the microphone hears the room

Lomax recorded in place -- porches, prison yards, churches, tenement flats -- with one microphone, so the take contains the room, the bystanders, the dogs, the work. The recording is a document of an occasion, not a performance isolated from it. The 1933-34 Ford trip alone covered sixteen thousand miles; the 1959 Southern Journey sessions were made "sitting out with musicians on porches, in booze-soaked tenement flats and at open-air revival meetings". The Lead Belly session notes record the microphone being moved "up and down from mouth to guitar with a resultant uneveness" -- the microphone is visibly present in the sound. The mechanism is the refusal to clean the scene: ambient particles, background figures and incidental noise are part of the composition and are never filtered out, and the frame includes its own edges. A piece built on this mechanism never presents a clean stage; the room is always in the shot, and the room is part of the music.

**Takeaway:** The take is the room. Clean the room and you lose the song -- the song is what happens in that room, with those people in it. [C35104]

## 19. The field kit: weight, wobble, and the eight-minute side

The recording apparatus was a physical imposition: a disc-cutting recorder filling the rear seat and trunk of a Model A Ford, two heavy Edison batteries, a portable generator, an AC motor with no speed governor, under eight minutes per side of an aluminium disc, played back only with a wooden or bamboo needle or a cactus thorn. The machine frequently broke down and was repaired in May 1934 by University of Texas physicist C. P. Boner. By 1959 the kit had shrunk to a rented '51 Buick carrying an Ampex reel-to-reel, German microphones, batteries and cables -- still, in Shirley Collins's phrase, "an unwieldy rig to be lugging around in the late summer humidity". The kit dictated where, how long and how well anything could be recorded, and its limits are audible in the recordings: the slow wobble of the ungoverned motor, the hard cap on session length, the breakdowns that become part of the texture. The sources disagree on the machine's weight -- 300, 315, 350 and 500 pounds are all given for the same 1933 rig, likely different machines and trips conflated -- so no single weight is stated here. What is certain is that the constraint is the aesthetic: the system has visible weight and limits, and the limits shape the sound.

**Takeaway:** The machine is not transparent. Its weight, its wobble and its eight-minute sides are in every recording -- and a piece that hides its own limits is lying about how it was made. [C35105]

## 20. Immediate playback: the disc that talks back

Direct-to-disc machines allowed playback immediately after a recording was made, and Lomax used that fact as a method. The singer heard herself minutes after singing -- often the first time she had ever heard her own voice reproduced. The Association for Cultural Equity archive holds a photograph captioned "Alan Lomax and possibly Raphael Hurtault listening to playback" in La Plaine, Dominica; Lomax's own account of the effect is that when people "would hear their own music recorded and played back to them, they'd realize that their own stuff was as good as anything on radio or TV". The mechanism is a loop that records, then immediately replays itself to its own makers: the figures visibly react to hearing themselves, change their behaviour on the second pass, and the second pass is different from the first. It is the same hinge as the Muddy Waters session in 1941, and it is the reason the archive is a mirror rather than a vault. One honest split belongs here: the systematic return of recordings to communities -- the repatriation ceremonies in Mississippi and Calabria -- is the work of his daughter Anna Lomax Wood and the Association for Cultural Equity after his death, not of Lomax in the field. The study keeps the field-playback half with Lomax and the repatriation half with the Association, and does not merge them into one act.

**Takeaway:** Playback is the moment the subject becomes a collaborator. The piece that plays itself back to its own makers is a different piece on the second hearing. [C35106]

## 21. The neglected corner, and the song that paces the work

Lomax deliberately recorded where commercial record companies did not go -- above all the Southern penitentiaries: all eleven Southern state penitentiaries on the 1933 trip, Angola, Parchman, Cummings, Bellwood. His stated premise was that a distinct Black folk music existed which commercial "race" records had not captured, and in those early days few community members would perform secular, "sinful" songs in the open -- so the prisons were where the secular repertoire survived. The mechanism is attention pointed at the neglected corner of the frame: the figures at the edge, in shadow, doing the work, made the centre. Inside that corner, the music itself is a work system: in the songs Lomax recorded, the leader's call and the group's response is not decoration, it is the mechanism that paces labour. The song coordinates the axe, the hammer, the hoe. Parchman work songs -- "Rosie" and the gang songs -- Mississippi fife-and-drum ensembles, Georgia Sea Island ring shouts, field hollers: one element calls, the many answer, and the answer is what moves the work forward. If the response stops, the work stalls.

**Takeaway:** Find the material where the market never looked, and let the song do the work of pacing it. The call goes out; the many answer; the field advances one beat. [C35107]

## 22. The chain of custody: hand to hand to tape

Lomax's archive is a relay: a song learned from a grandfather, sung to a collector, cut to disc, shelved in the Library of Congress, re-issued on LP, taught to a revival singer, sung again. Each handoff changes the song slightly and preserves it. The 1959 Southern Journey was explicitly a re-recording trip: Lomax obtained an Atlantic contract to re-record, with improved equipment, some of the American musicians first recorded in the 1940s -- Fred McDowell, Almeda Riddle, Hobart Smith, first recorded in the 1940s and re-recorded in stereo in 1959, then brought to Newport. The same chain runs backward to Sharp and Neal: Appalachian ballads Sharp collected in print in 1916-18 were still being sung to Lomax's microphone in the 1930s, 40s and 50s. The mechanism is the telephone chain with the errors kept: a motif visibly passes from figure to figure, each one re-singing it with a small mutation, and the piece is the chain, not any single link. This is also the doorway from study #806 in its purest form: Lomax re-records, re-issues and re-stages the repertoire Sharp and Neal collected in print, and the drift between the printed version and the sung version is the living tradition.

**Takeaway:** The song survives by being passed on, and every pass changes it a little. The errors are not damage; the errors are the style. [C35108]

## 23. The collector inside the take

Lomax was not an invisible observer. He sang, played, prompted, interviewed and argued with his subjects on tape; his voice is in the recordings, asking the next question, joining the chorus, urging the singer on. He was a performer in his own right -- discs for HMV, Nixa, Melodisc and Decca; regular singer on his own radio shows; member of the skiffle group Alan Lomax and the Ramblers on British television -- and his delivery was functional rather than virtuosic: the point was transmission, not display. Lead Belly worked as the Lomaxes' driver on the 1934 trip -- collector and singer sharing a car, a job, a life for months. And the talk was a technique in itself: the 1938 Jelly Roll Morton sessions are eight or more hours of talk as much as music, and the 1959 field tapes are full of spoken introductions and life stories alongside the songs. The mechanism is the absence of a fourth wall: the observer-figure is inside the frame, visibly participating, prompting, joining in, occasionally getting it wrong. A piece built on it has no clean separation between the hand holding the camera and the scene being filmed.

**Takeaway:** There is no outside to the recording. The collector is in the take, and the take is better for it -- the questions are part of the music. [C35109]

## 24. The song as a map, the jukebox as a world

Cantometrics is Lomax's measuring instrument: thirty-seven style factors, rated one to five by pairs of listeners, turning a culture's singing -- vocal tension, breathiness, rasp, melisma, group cohesion, phrase length -- into a profile that can be read at a glance like a weather map. Change one dial and the whole crowd's singing style shifts; the crowd's social arrangement -- tight choir against loose soloists -- follows the dial, not the other way round. The Global Jukebox is the same instrument made navigable: an "intelligent museum", prototyped in the 1980s and realised after his death by the Association for Cultural Equity, in which any song can be placed beside any other song on earth and compared by its measured style, so that a listener can hear the family resemblances between cultures. The working public version launched digitally in January 2012, from the Cantometrics dataset plus seven further datasets, cross-indexed with the D-PLACE database of peoples, languages and cultures; Lomax died in 2002 before it went online and never saw it implemented. The mechanism for a piece is a map first and a performance second: many small song-worlds arranged by kinship rather than geography, where tapping one lights up its relatives across the map, and the listener travels by resemblance.

**Takeaway:** Measure the style and you can map the world by ear. The jukebox is not a shelf of songs; it is a chart of who sings like whom, and why. [C35110]

## 25. The grey-out and the radio hour

Two mechanisms face each other. The grey-out is the central broadcast signal trying to pull every figure into one grey unison -- mass media as a bleaching agent, imposing a few standardised, mass-produced, cheapened cultures everywhere. The radio hour is the counter-move: the broadcast as a campfire, a one-to-many gathering with the same songs and the same voices retold weekly, a host-figure who introduces, a guest who sings, an audience of small figures around the edge that gathers, listens, disperses and returns next time. Lomax ran both sides of this in his own career: three documented radio series between 1939 and 1948, with named shows, named regulars and a measurable institutional effect -- the school broadcasts that pushed folk song into public-school teaching -- and, from 1972, the polemic that named the grey-out and founded an institution to fight it. A piece built on the pair is a visible tension: the grey pull against the local breakaway, and the breakaway is the piece.

**Takeaway:** The broadcast can bleach or it can gather. The difference is whether the signal carries one voice to everyone, or everyone's voice to everyone. [C35111]

## 26. The list, the road, the return

Three mechanisms that are really one habit: keeping the record. The list is the catalogue as a creative act -- the 1940 *Check-list of Recorded Songs* compiled with a young Pete Seeger as assistant, the regional bibliography with Sidney Cowell, the eighteen-volume *Columbia World Library*, the hundred-plus-CD Rounder edition produced by his daughter: every motif that appears is tagged, numbered and filed at the edge of the frame, and the ledger grows as the piece plays. The road is the field trip as a designed system -- a route, a car, a crew of two, a schedule, a sponsor, and a rule: record whoever is singing, where they are singing, in the best sound the technology allows, and bring the best of them back to the stage. The Southern Journey is the cleanest example: New York to Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, Arkansas, the Georgia Sea Islands, North Carolina, stopping at stations, each station adding a new local style to the travelling collection, the collection itself becoming the piece's memory. The return is the archive given back: the repatriation ceremonies in Tate and Panola Counties, Mississippi, in 2013, and in Cinquefrondi, Calabria, in 2011, where the recordings were returned to the communities that made them, with the performers' heirs present and lesson plans for local schools built from the music. The piece that ends by giving itself away closes its loop by opening it: the community re-owns its own song, and sings it differently.

**Takeaway:** The list makes the archive usable, the road makes it grow, and the return makes it honest. A collection that never comes home is a vault; a collection that comes home is a living room. [C35112]

## 27. The ear and the ethics ledger

Lomax's selection was a system with a bias, and the bias is documented from both sides. He sought the uncommercial, the Black, the rural, the "pure" folk; he distrusted hybrids and commercial popular music; he was, in the Texas State Historical Association's words, "a musical conservative who had little use for such hybrid forms as folk-rock". His ear was wide geographically -- Cajun and Creole music, Haitian vodou songs, Italian polyphony, Spanish and Scottish ballads -- but narrow stylistically: it wanted the unmediated, pre-commercial performance. The mechanism is a visible filter: a gate that admits some sounds and refuses others, with the refused sounds pressing against the gate and heard faintly from outside. And at the centre of the legacy sits the unresolved mechanism: who owns a field recording? He recorded other people's songs, paid the singers a fee, often from his own pocket, and offered contracts promising royalties if a song became a commercial success -- and was still accused for decades of claiming copyright over material that was not his. The facts cut both ways: he copyrighted only his books of compilations, which his publishers insisted on, not individual songs; he tried to set up a foundation to channel copyright monies to source singers; when the Weavers' "Goodnight Irene" became a hit, he ploughed the money straight back into the next project. Against that: BMI lists him as "writer" on traditional songs, and "Rosie", a Parchman work song, became the basis for songs by the Animals, Grand Funk Railroad and -- via a sampled riff -- Jay-Z's "Takeover", with Lomax credited as songwriter on a song he did not write. Since 2009 the Association for Cultural Equity has made a systematic effort to locate artists and heirs due royalties and fees. A piece built on this mechanism keeps its credit line visible and moving: a motif appears, and a small tag follows it naming its source; as the motif travels and is reworked, the tag multiplies, is disputed, is corrected. Provenance is not a footnote but a character.

**Takeaway:** Every ear is a filter, and every filter has an edge. The honest piece shows its own gate -- what it lets in, what it keeps out, and who gets paid when the song travels. [C35113]

## 28. The father-son relay, and the voice that sings back

The Lomax enterprise was a two-man system with a built-in argument: John Lomax (cowboy songs, white frontier ballads, the older collector's canon) and Alan (Black music, work songs, blues, the "forgotten people"). They wrote the books together -- *American Ballads and Folk Songs* (1934), *Negro Folk Songs as Sung by Lead Belly* (1936), *Our Singing Country* (1941) -- and took the trips together in the same Ford, and the archive is the product of their collaboration and their friction. Alan was, in one review's phrase, "a boy socialist and less rigid in temperament" who "would nevertheless spend a good part of his life unable to stand up to his father", and whose interests diverged sharply from John's toward Black music and social justice. The mechanism is stereo in the human sense: two collector-figures moving through the same field, hearing different songs -- one bends toward the ballad-singers on the porch, the other toward the work gang in the yard -- sometimes harmonising, sometimes arguing, both recorded. And through it runs Lomax's own plain singing voice: the unadorned, deliberately "folk" delivery on radio, on record, in the field, the simplest line in the piece, sung straight, while more elaborate figures decorate around it. The plain line is the spine; everything else is commentary on it. The revival machine runs on the same relay at larger scale: record in the field, publish in books and LPs, put the singer on radio, book the singer at a festival, and the revival audience learns the song and carries it onward. Three versions of one song, each in its own register, shown in sequence -- the piece is the pipeline.

**Takeaway:** Two ears hear two Americas, and the argument between them is the archive. The plain voice that sings the tune straight is the spine the whole machine hangs from. [C35114]

## Aesthetic logic

Lomax's aesthetic is not a style; it is a set of refusals. Refuse the studio: record where the song is sung. Refuse the clean take: keep the room, the bystanders, the wobble of the motor. Refuse the anonymous source: name the singer, pay the singer, play the disc back to the singer. Refuse the single version: record it again in twenty years, in stereo, and let the two versions argue. Refuse the grey: measure the differences between singing styles instead of smoothing them away, and build a machine -- a jukebox, a catalogue, a broadcast hour -- that makes the differences navigable. The logic is additive rather than selective: the archive grows by addition, the map grows by comparison, the revival grows by relay. Where Neal's aesthetic was the room and the girls -- a social machine for moving bodies -- Lomax's is the microphone and the tape: a listening machine for moving sound. The two aesthetics meet at exactly one point, which is the doorway of this study: the song that was collected in print, sung by a living singer, caught on a disc, played back on the spot, broadcast to millions, and then returned, decades later, to the town it came from. A piece that follows this logic does not decorate a song. It builds the machine that carries the song from the porch to the archive and back again, and lets the viewer watch the carrying.

**Takeaway:** The aesthetic is the refusal. Refuse the studio, the clean take, the anonymous source, the single version, the grey -- and what is left is the song, in its room, with its name on it. [C35115]
