## M1. The jazz and popular-music ear inside a classical frame

The mechanism is not quotation, it is absorption, and Walton takes dance-band rhythm, syncopation, cross-rhythm and a bitter-sweet added-note harmony from 1920s jazz and popular song and uses them as structural material, not as decoration. Facade is the type specimen, and its numbers are literal popular dances pinned down in miniature, Tango-Pasodoble, Fox-Trot "Old Sir Faulk", Valse, Polka, Scotch Rhapsody, Swiss Jodelling Song, Popular Song, Tarantella, and Constant Lambert called them "satiric genre pieces, over in a flash, but unerringly pinning down some aspect of popular music", and the Savoy Orpheans at the Savoy Hotel were a direct listening source, and Edith Sitwell's poems are recited above the music in rhythms Walton determined, so the words and the dance band are two layers with quite separate existences alongside their continued symbiosis. Portsmouth Point runs on the same engine, fast-moving, full of syncopation and cross-rhythm, inspired by Thomas Rowlandson's print of a rumbustious dockside scene, and Kennedy's phrase is that "the sailors of H.M.S. Pinafore have had a night on the tiles", and the piece "for years proved hazardous for conductors and orchestras alike". Belshazzar's Feast presses the same rhythms into a biblical oratorio, "jazzy outbursts" and "edgy rhythms", the metre changing rapidly, 3/4 to 2/4 to 4/4 with 3/8 insertions, and the choir is "treated as an instrument", and the harmony is "essentially conventional in its tonality, although it is scored without a key signature and passes through many keys", so the wrong-note bite, added notes and chromatic clashes inside otherwise diatonic writing, is the jazz ear working inside the classical frame. MusicWeb draws the comparison that decides the mechanism, and Walton "absorbed the freedom jazz rhythm gave his scores rather than aping them as his friend and colleague Constant Lambert did in his Rio Grande", which is why Lambert now sounds like a period piece and Walton does not, and the difference is absorption against imitation, and the absorbed rhythm survives the decade that dates the imitation.

**Takeaway:** do not quote the popular music, absorb it, and let the syncopation and the wrong note become the structure, because a quotation dates the piece to the year of the dance, and an absorbed rhythm outlives it.

## M2. The choral-orchestral blockbuster

The mechanism is scale as argument, and a single large-scale public statement is built by piling forces, double chorus, semi-chorus, baritone, huge orchestra, organ, two antiphonal brass bands, and driving them with rhythmic insistence. Belshazzar's Feast is the type specimen and the only fully successful instance, and nothing later matched its popularity, and the BBC commission of 21 August 1929 asked for a small choir, a soloist and no more than fifteen players, and Walton's music outgrew the brief, and Beecham rescued it for the 1931 Leeds Festival, and his advice was "Well, my boy, as you will probably never hear this work again, you might as well chuck in a couple of brass bands", two extra brass sections of three trumpets, three trombones and tuba each, on hand anyway for Berlioz's Requiem two days later. The premiere was at Leeds Town Hall on Thursday 8 October 1931, Dennis Noble the baritone, the Leeds Festival Chorus, the London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent conducting, and it was an immediate triumph, judged "the most important English choral work since Elgar's The Dream of Gerontius in 1900", and Karajan called it in 1947 "the best choral music that's been written in the last 50 years", and it was banned from the Three Choirs Festival until 2 September 1957, Ivor Atkins objecting to the libretto and H. C. Colles in The Times calling it "stark Judaism from first to last". The mechanism inside the scale is a triptych, lament, Babylonian excess, rejoicing, and each god praised with a different orchestral timbre, see M6, and choral writing so hard the Leeds chorus was rumoured to have revolted, and the rumour is false, see C8, and a finale of antiphonal alleluias. The Coronation Te Deum and Gloria reuse the ceremonial-choral apparatus at smaller voltage, and the mechanism depends on occasion and scale, and Walton never repeated it at Feast size, and the lesson is that the blockbuster is a once-in-a-career form, not a template.

**Takeaway:** do not repeat the big statement, build it once at full voltage and let it stand, because a blockbuster repeated is a formula, and a blockbuster built once is a landmark.

## M3. The slow movement as the emotional centre, the long singing line

The mechanism is inversion, and Walton's lyric gift is placed first, not last, slow, melancholy, wide-interval melody carrying the expressive weight, with fast music as frame or foil. The Viola Concerto opens Andante comodo with a melancholy 9/8 viola theme in A minor, middle register then high register, and the second movement is the fast scherzo, so the conventional fast-slow-fast concerto is inverted, and Kennedy says that in the Viola Concerto "the lyric poet in Walton, who had so far been hidden under a mask of irony, fully emerged". The First Symphony's third movement is marked Andante con malinconia, eleven minutes and twenty-four seconds in the Hyperion recording, and it is the movement critics single out for "sensuous desolation", in Grove's phrase. The Violin Concerto and the Cello Concerto both "open reflectively", and the Cello Concerto adds a ticking rhythm throughout the work suggesting the inexorable passage of time, and the slow-movement theme of Britten's Piano Concerto is what Walton chose to vary in the Improvisations, so he reached for another composer's slow movement as his material, and the choice tells you what he valued. The mechanism is that the singing line is the argument, and the fast music is the weather around it, and the piece is remembered by its slow centre, not by its fireworks.

**Takeaway:** do not save the singing for the slow movement, open with it, and let the fast music be the frame, because a piece remembered by its fireworks is forgotten, and a piece remembered by its line is kept.

## M4. Film music as a second career, and its feedback into the concert style

The mechanism is craft that leaks, and fourteen film scores from 1934 to 1969, written fast to tight deadlines, were treated by Walton as craft rather than art, "Film music is not good film music if it can be used for any other purpose", yet the techniques leaked into the concert works, and the concert style shaped the films in return. The wartime films taught him scene-painting by orchestral colour and the short, sharply characterised cue, and the Spitfire Prelude and Fugue is a film cue that became a concert staple, first concert performance January 1943, Liverpool Philharmonic, Walton conducting, and Henry V mixes Elizabethan pastiche, the Fitzwilliam Virginal Book, the "Agincourt Carol", the fifteenth-century battle-song "Reveillez-vous Piccars", the last two at Vaughan Williams's suggestion, with "wholly characteristic Waltonian music", and Olivier called it "the most wonderful score I've ever heard on a film", and the Agincourt battle sequence is the proof, Kennedy singling out the music making the charge of the French knights "fearfully real". The feedback ran both ways, and Kennedy on the A minor String Quartet says it showed Walton had "thrown off the trammels of his cinema style and rediscovered his true voice", which proves the cinema style had become a real trammel by 1945, and the Quartet is the work where the leak is cauterised. The career ended in public humiliation, and the Battle of Britain score, about twenty minutes of music, was rejected by United Artists, reportedly because it was too short for an LP release, and Ron Goodwin replaced him, and Olivier threatened to remove his name from the credits unless some Walton was retained, and only the "Battle in the Air" sequence survived in the film, and Walton learned of the rejection from the Daily Telegraph, and Lady Walton said "He couldn't sleep for weeks. Nothing like that had ever happened to him", and after this Walton said he would write no more film music, and then Olivier persuaded him to do Three Sisters, and that was the fourteenth and last.

**Takeaway:** do not fence the day job off from the real work, because the craft leaks either way, and the leak is the education, and the work that throws off the trammel is the proof the trammel taught something.

## M5. Englishness as heir of Elgar, not disciple of Vaughan Williams, and the Mediterranean turn

The mechanism is an argued identity, not an assumed one, and Walton's national identity is defined against two predecessors and one climate. The BIS booklet states it plainly, "Though less inherently introspective, Walton was always much more the heir of Elgar than the disciple of Vaughan Williams", and "For Walton, neither the English landscape nor English folk music were primary sources of inspiration", and Britannica places him "between the time of Vaughan Williams and that of Benjamin Britten". Crown Imperial is deliberately "in the Elgarian tradition", and the Viola Concerto's design owes much to Elgar's Cello Concerto, beginning with a slow movement, and he completed exactly two symphonies, like Elgar, and he met Elgar once, at the 1932 Three Choirs performance of the Viola Concerto by Tertis, and Elgar did not share the general enthusiasm. Against Vaughan Williams, The Quest is "oddly reminiscent of Vaughan Williams", in Grove's phrase, and the exception proves the rule, because the resemblance is noted precisely where it is uncharacteristic. The Mediterranean side is the other half of the identity, and the classical-music.com piece says his roots in English tradition coexisted with "an anti-parochial, cosmopolitan streak awakened by that first encounter with the Mediterranean south", the first Italy trip with the Sitwells around 1920, "a new world", and the Amalfi Coast winter of 1928-29 where the Viola Concerto was composed, and the move to Ischia, and the Cello Concerto "mellowed by the Mediterranean sun of his Ischia home", and the late style's "subtler and more finely wrought" tone. Henze's Ischia turn of 1953 repeats Walton's own turn thirty years later, and the doorway's real shared mechanism is the climate changing the line, and the Englishness is not the landscape, it is the line that survives the move.

**Takeaway:** do not inherit the tradition by geography, choose the predecessor and the climate, because an identity taken from the landscape is a postcard, and an identity argued against two masters and one sea is a voice.

## M6. Orchestration as drama, timbre as noun, silence as event

The mechanism is that Walton assigns a distinct orchestral colour to each dramatic idea, and uses silence and shouting as structural devices, so the orchestration is the drama, not the dressing. Belshazzar's Feast is the full demonstration, and each "Praise ye the god of..." is followed by a different interlude, silver by glockenspiel and triangle, iron by anvil strikes, wood by xylophone and wood block, brass by horns, trumpets and trombones, so each god has a sound before it has a meaning, and a whole bar of complete silence follows the Jews' exclamation of horror at the sacrilege, and the silence is the event, and the chorus shouts the word "slain" before the final four words of the soloist's line, and the shouting is the structure, and the writing-on-the-wall music derives its chilling orchestral sounds from Strauss's Salome, in Kennedy's account, and the antiphonal brass bands, two groups of seven placed left and right, turn space into drama. The same instinct works in miniature, and the Five Bagatelles exploit "the guitar's resources to the full", in Kennedy's phrase, and the Viola Concerto opens with muted strings and low clarinet establishing A minor in three bars, so the key is set by colour before it is set by harmony. The mechanism is: give each idea its own timbre, and let silence and shouting do the work harmony cannot, because a colour that only decorates is a garnish, and a colour that names the god is a noun.

**Takeaway:** do not orchestrate the idea after you have written it, give the idea its colour first, and let a bar of silence say what the notes cannot, because orchestration that only dresses the music is decoration, and orchestration that names the thing is drama.

## M7. Words-and-music partnership, the librettist and poet as co-author

The mechanism is that Walton's best-known works are collaborations in which the words are not set but placed alongside the music, or in which the librettist's weakness becomes the work's problem, so the partnership is half the work. Facade is the type specimen, and Sitwell's poems are recited ABOVE the music in rhythms determined by the composer, and the two layers have "quite separate existences... alongside their continued symbiosis", in the OUPblog's phrase, and the work is neither a song cycle nor a melodrama, it is a partnership with a seam showing. Belshazzar's Feast is the same mechanism at choral scale, and Osbert Sitwell assembled the text from the Bible, Daniel, Psalm 137, Revelation, and the work is dedicated to Lord Berners, and the text is an anthology, not a libretto, and the anthology is the partnership. Troilus and Cressida is the mechanism failing, and the failure is substantially a libretto failure, and Hassall, a first-time librettist, "turned out to be unable to grasp the requirements of an opera libretto, especially in dramatic terms", and Walton ended up re-writing Hassall's constant re-draftings by proxy, and the Sitwells each thought one of them should have been asked to write the libretto instead of Hassall, and the old partnership resenting its replacement is the story of the opera. The Bear is the mechanism succeeding, and the success is substantially a libretto success, and Dehn and Walton after Chekhov built a tight vaudeville with three characters, and the tightness is the partnership working. The mechanism is: choose the writer first, and let the writer choose the sound, because the opera is not a composer with a text, it is a partnership with a voice, and the voice is half the music.

**Takeaway:** do not hire a librettist, marry one, and if the marriage fails the opera fails with it, because an opera with a hired text is a setting, and an opera with a partner is a collaboration, and the collaboration is the work.

## M8. The slow worker, long gestation, blockage, revision

The mechanism is scarcity by temperament, and Walton composed slowly, revised continually, "Without an india-rubber I was absolutely sunk", and left long gaps between major works, and the small total output across a sixty-year career is the price and the proof. Symphony No. 1 took 1931/32 to 1935 with an eight-month block on the finale and three burnt finales, "I've burnt about three finales", and the block is usually explained by the break-up with Baroness Imma von Doernberg, to whom the symphony is dedicated, and Constant Lambert suggested the fugal episode that unlocked the finale, so the block was broken by a friend's idea, not by force. Troilus and Cressida took seven years, 1947 to 1954, plus revisions in 1963 and 1976, and the Viola Concerto occupied almost two years, 1928-29, and Facade was revised from 1922 into the early 1940s and not published until 1951, and the A minor String Quartet was set aside in 1939 and finished in 1945-47, and a Third Symphony was attempted repeatedly for Andre Previn and abandoned, and between Facade in 1923 and the Sinfonia Concertante in 1928 he averaged one small piece a year. The consequence is a catalogue in which almost everything is kept, and the revision is not indecision, it is the standard, and the burnt finale is the mechanism working, because a composer who keeps everything has no standard, and a composer who burns three finales has one.

**Takeaway:** do not measure a career by output, measure it by what survived the india-rubber, because a catalogue that keeps everything is a warehouse, and a catalogue that burns three finales is a standard.

## M9. Writing for a specific player

The mechanism is a commissioning economy, and Walton's concertos and chamber works are tailored to a named virtuoso, and the player's refusal or acceptance changes the work's history. The Viola Concerto was written at Sir Thomas Beecham's suggestion for Lionel Tertis, who rejected the manuscript by return of post as too modern, and Tertis later wrote, "With shame and contrition I admit that when the composer offered me the first performance I declined it", and Paul Hindemith stepped in and premiered it at the Queen's Hall Proms on Thursday 3 October 1929, Walton conducting, and Hindemith was himself a composer-violist Walton admired, so the rescue was also a tribute, and Tertis took the work up after the premiere. The Violin Concerto was commissioned by Jascha Heifetz, who held exclusive playing rights for two years, so it was not heard in Britain until 1941, and the exclusivity is part of the work's history. The Cello Concerto was written for Gregor Piatigorsky, who commissioned it and premiered it in Boston in January 1957, and the Violin Sonata was written for Yehudi Menuhin and Louis Kentner, and the Five Bagatelles were written for Julian Bream, who persuaded him to write them, and Anon in Love was written for Peter Pears and Bream, and the Passacaglia for cello of 1980 and Prologo e Fantasia of 1982 were written for Mstislav Rostropovich. The pattern is that the player brings the commission, the prestige, and sometimes the obstruction, and the obstruction is as productive as the commission, because a concerto refused by its dedicatee and rescued by another composer is a better story than a concerto delivered on time, and the story is in the score.

**Takeaway:** do not write for the instrument, write for the player, and let the player's refusal be part of the work, because a concerto written for nobody is an exercise, and a concerto written for a named hand is a relationship.

## M10. Ceremonial and state music

The mechanism is Elgarian pomp filtered through Walton's rhythmic bite, and Walton is the composer the British state calls on for coronations, and the marches and anthems must be grand, singable and instantly public. Crown Imperial of 1937 is the type specimen, and it was composed originally for the coronation of Edward VIII, who abdicated in 1936, and it was used for George VI on the same scheduled day, and then at the 1953 and 2023 coronations, so the same march served three coronations, and the economy is the mechanism, and The Musical Times thought it "unrepresentative" and "unlikely to survive", and it survived eighty-six years. Orb and Sceptre and the Coronation Te Deum followed for the coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953, with Crown Imperial, three Walton pieces at one coronation, and Walton on Orb and Sceptre said it was "goodish, not as good as Crown Imperial, but I did my best", and he was paid 50 pounds by the Arts Council for it, and the self-assessment is the honest price of the commission. The late brass fanfares continue the line, A Queen's Fanfare of 1959, Anniversary Fanfare of 1973, Fanfare for the National of 1974, Roaring Fanfare of 1975, and the mechanism is that the ceremonial piece must work the first time it is heard, in a large room, by people who did not come for the music, and the rhythmic bite is what keeps the pomp from going soft, and Crown Imperial disappointed admirers who wanted the avant-gardist back, and the disappointment is the proof that the ceremonial composer and the modernist were the same man.

**Takeaway:** do not write the ceremonial piece as a lesser work, write it to survive three coronations, because a march that works once is a commission, and a march that works three times is a classic.

## M11. Parody and mask-wearing

The mechanism is a magpie gift for imitation, and Walton wears popular dances, other composers' styles and period pastiche as masks, with the real voice underneath, and the mask is played, not described. Facade is the type specimen, and the classical-music.com piece says "From the start, a phenomenal, magpie-like gift for musical parody and mask-wearing was a Waltonian trademark. This reached a pinnacle in his Chekhov-based chamber opera The Bear". Henry V pastiches Elizabethan music, the Fitzwilliam Virginal Book and Canteloube melodies for the French court, inside a Walton score, and The Wise Virgins is Bach arranged, not Walton composed, eight extracts transcribed for Ashton, and the early String Quartet's "undigested Bartok and Schoenberg" is the mask before the voice, and Walton's own phrase for it is the judgement. The Bear's comedy works because the masks fit, Chekhov and Puccini's Gianni Schicchi, and Troilus fails partly because the mask does not fit, nineteenth-century Italian opera on a post-war audience, and the fit of the mask is the test, because a mask that fits lets the face show through, and a mask that does not fit hides it. The mechanism is: take the style you love, wear it for one piece, and let the voice underneath be heard through it, because a pastiche that only imitates is a copy, and a pastiche that lets the voice through is a portrait.

**Takeaway:** do not hide behind the mask, wear it so the face shows, because a mask that hides the voice is a disguise, and a mask the voice sounds through is a portrait.

## M12. The late style, economy, variation, passacaglia

The mechanism is saying more with less, and after 1945 Walton's music gets shorter, more concentrated, and increasingly built on variation and passacaglia forms, and the forms are a way of paying tribute to other composers by transforming their material. Symphony No. 2 of 1960 is twenty-six to twenty-eight minutes against the First's forty-four, and its finale is a Passacaglia, Theme and Variations, Fugato, Coda (Scherzando), and David Cox called it "more a divertimento than a symphony... highly personal, unmistakably Walton throughout", and Kennedy called it "a superb example of Walton's more mature, concise, and mellow post-1945 style". The Hindemith Variations of 1963 take the theme from the second movement of Hindemith's Cello Concerto of 1940, and the Britten Improvisations of 1969 take the nine-bar slow-movement theme of Britten's Piano Concerto of 1939, and in both, Grove says, "the source material is gradually transformed as Walton's own voice becomes more prominent", so the tribute is a takeover, and the other composer's theme ends as Walton's. MusicWeb says Walton "did fall back on fail-safe compositional formulae, variation and passacaglia form especially", and the late miniatures, the Five Bagatelles of 1970-71, Jubilate Deo of 1971-72, Facade 2 of 1977, are small, polished, and "superb, if smaller-scale", in the classical-music.com piece's phrase, and Kennedy says the style of the Ischia years "did not change, the tone did, into something subtler and more finely wrought", and Tierney says the later works are "if emotionally less direct, more profound". The mechanism is: take a small form, or another man's theme, and make it unmistakably yours by concentration, because a late style that only shrinks is a decline, and a late style that concentrates is a distillation.

**Takeaway:** do not write bigger to prove you are still there, write smaller and more concentrated, because a late work that sprawls is a memory, and a late work that distils is a proof.

## M13. Comic opera succeeds where tragic opera fails

The mechanism is scale and fit, and Walton's one full-length tragic opera is his great failure, and his one-act comedy is his operatic success, and the comedy's small forces, tight plot and single act suit his gifts, brevity, parody, timing, and the tragedy's three acts, weak libretto and heroic-Romantic ambitions exceed them. Troilus and Cressida took seven years, 1947 to 1954, plus revisions in 1963 and 1976, and the premiere at Covent Garden on Friday 3 December 1954, Sargent conducting, George Devine directing, was friendly but the general feeling was of an old-fashioned opera in an outmoded tradition, and it was staged in San Francisco, New York and Milan the next year without success, and it never entered the regular repertory, and Grove calls it "a partially successful attempt to revivify the traditions of nineteenth-century Italian opera in a post-war era wary of heroic Romanticism", and the classical-music.com piece calls it "the major creative disappointment of Walton's life". The Bear took shape in 1965-67, one act, three singers, chamber orchestra, libretto by Paul Dehn and Walton after Chekhov's vaudeville of 1888, commissioned by the Koussevitzky Foundation in 1958, begun at the Aldeburgh Festival's request, premiered at Jubilee Hall, Aldeburgh, on Saturday 3 June 1967, and enthusiastically received, and Andrew Porter called it "one of the strongest and most brilliant things Walton has written", and it has had twenty-three productions worldwide from 2015 to 2025, per Operabase, and Walton worked on it while convalescing from lung-cancer surgery in 1966. The lesson for the study is that Walton's dramatic gift is episodic and witty, not sustained-tragic, and the mechanism is that the right size for the gift is the size of the gift, and a one-act comedy that fits is worth more than a three-act tragedy that does not.

**Takeaway:** do not force the gift into the grand form, give it the form it fits, because a tragedy that exceeds the gift is a disappointment, and a comedy that fits it is a triumph.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Walton's work is that the line survives the mask, and the career is a long education in wearing masks until the face underneath is the line itself. The jazz is a mask, and the mask is absorbed until it is the rhythm, and the parody is a mask, and the mask is worn until the voice sounds through it, and the Elizabethan pastiche is a mask, and the mask is worn until the Agincourt charge is fearfully real, and the ceremonial march is a mask, and the mask is worn until it serves three coronations, and the film cue is a mask, and the mask is worn until the cue becomes a concert staple, and the variation form is a mask, and the mask is another composer's theme worn until it becomes Walton's. The slow movement is the face, and the face is placed first, because the singing line is the argument, and the fast music is the weather around it, and the piece is remembered by its slow centre. The Mediterranean is the instrument, and the climate changes the line, and the Englishness is not the landscape, it is the line that survives the move, and the heir of Elgar is the man who took the Elgarian march and gave it a rhythmic bite, and the disciple of Vaughan Williams he refused to be is the man who never took the folk song, and the refusal is the identity. The slow worker is the standard, and the burnt finale is the mechanism, and the small catalogue is the proof, and almost everything is kept because almost everything was earned. The comedy succeeds where the tragedy fails because the gift is episodic and witty, and the one-act Bear fits the gift, and the three-act Troilus exceeds it, and the fit is the test. The whole oeuvre is one long proof that a composer can start as a modernist, become the establishment, be called old-fashioned, and be revalued in his own lifetime, and the proof is the line, and the line is still singing, and the masks are still on the wall, and the wall is in a garden on a hill in Ischia.
