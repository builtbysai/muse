## The life: the cutter who never photographed the world if she could photograph a photograph of it

Sarah Edwards Charlesworth was born March 29, 1947, in East Orange, New Jersey, the eldest of five children of Roger and Sarah Morgan Charlesworth; her father, a mechanical engineer, was a Western Electric executive whose job moved the family between cities. She was raised in Summit, New Jersey, started high school in Buffalo and finished it in Oklahoma City, where an art teacher encouraged her to become an artist. She attended Bradford Junior College in Haverhill, Massachusetts (1965-67), studying with the Conceptual artist Douglas Huebler, then Barnard College, where she studied painting and earned a B.A. in art history in 1969; her undergraduate thesis was a 50-print, text-free conceptual study of the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum. After her degree she studied briefly under photographer Lisette Model at The New School, then worked as a freelance photographer and became active in downtown Manhattan art circles. In 1969 she saw an early Conceptual Art exhibition organised by Seth Siegelaub (with Huebler, Robert Barry, Lawrence Weiner and Joseph Kosuth) and stopped painting. (C21208, leg 1 C21000-C21002; synthesis C21158)

In 1975 she co-founded The Fox, a magazine dedicated to art theory, with Joseph Kosuth and others -- Wikipedia names Michael Corris, Preston Heller, Andrew Menard and Mel Ramsden; the NYT names Karl Beveridge and Carol Conde as co-founders with Kosuth -- and the magazine lasted three issues, ending in 1976 after internal dissension. She contributed to the feminist art publication Heresies and published the essay "A Declaration of Dependence" in The Fox in 1975. In 1981 she co-founded BOMB magazine with Glenn O'Brien, Betsy Sussler, Liza Bear and Michael McClard, and created the cover art for its very first edition. The Fox founding year is contested -- 1974 per the Guggenheim against 1975 per Wikipedia and the NYT -- and is carried as an open conflict. The magazine work is not a side job; it is the first version of the practice: a page, a text, an image, and an editorial decision about what stays and what goes. (C21209, leg 1 C21003-C21004; synthesis C21171)

The career spine runs in series, one idea worked to its conclusion -- she said in a 1990 BOMB interview that she had not really thought of herself as a photographer: Modern History (1977-79); Stills (1980); In-Photography (1981-82); Objects of Desire (1983-88, 1983-89 per LACMA and Paula Cooper); Renaissance Paintings and Renaissance Drawings (1991); Academy of Secrets (c. 1989-90); Natural Magic (1992-94), the turn from appropriation to photographing objects she arranged herself; Doubleworld (1995); 0+1 (2000); Neverland (2002); Available Light (2012), her final series. The retrospective "Sarah Charlesworth: A Retrospective", organised by SITE Santa Fe and curated by Louis Grachos and Susan Fisher Sterling, opened in 1997 (1998 per the NYT -- carry both) and toured to San Diego, the National Museum of Women in the Arts, Cleveland, and the Rose Art Museum. The posthumous survey "Sarah Charlesworth: Doubleworld", curated by Massimiliano Gioni and Margot Norton, was at the New Museum, June 24-September 20, 2015, with about 70 photographs from 10 bodies of work made between 1977 and 2012, and travelled to LACMA, August 20, 2017-February 4, 2018 (the advisory's original closing was November 26, 2017 -- the show was extended). Group and biennial presence: Whitney Biennials 1985 and 2014; Venice Biennale, Aperto (1986); the Met's "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" (2009); the Walker's "The Last Picture Show" (2004); the Guggenheim's "Speaking with Hands" (2004); the National Gallery's "Shock of the News" (2012). In 1995 she co-curated "Somatogenies" at Artists Space with Cindy Sherman and Laurie Simmons. (C21210, leg 1 C21005, C21010-C21012; synthesis C21158-C21161)

Dealers, by solo exhibitions -- no source read states a formal dated representation contract: Tony Shafrazi Gallery (Stills, 1980), Larry Gagosian (Tabula Rasa, 1982), International With Monument, Jay Gorney Modern Art, Margo Leavin Gallery, Gorney Bravin + Lee, Baldwin Gallery (Aspen), and Susan Inglett Gallery, where "Work in Progress" (2009) and "Available Light" (March 8-April 14, 2012) were shown. The Estate of Sarah Charlesworth is now represented by Paula Cooper Gallery (first exhibition February 23-March 23, 2019; later shows "Modern History" 2021 and "Desire and Seduction" 2025); posthumous solos also at Campoli Presti, Karma, and Krakow Witkin. Teaching: School of Visual Arts (1976 and 1992-2013), Rhode Island School of Design (2000-2008), New York University (1983-85 and 1993), Hartford University (1994, as listed in the CV), Princeton University (2011-2013), appointed Lecturer with the rank of Professor, from September 1, 2012, teaching "Advanced Questions in Photography" and "Introductory Photography" and overhauling the photography classrooms and labs; she was slated to teach a freshman seminar in fall 2013. Awards, CV-corroborated: John Simon Guggenheim Fellowship, Visual Art (1995); National Endowment for the Arts grants in 1976, 1980 and 1983 -- Princeton and the Whitney say "two NEA grants", the CV lists three dated years, carry both; New York State Creative Artists Public Service grant (1977). She married filmmaker Amos Poe in 1983; the marriage ended in divorce (Wikipedia's infobox alone gives 2010 as the end year -- do not assert it without the caveat). They had two children, son Nicholas Tiger Poe and daughter Sarah Lucy Poe. At her death her partner was the playwright Lonnie Carter. She died of a brain aneurysm at 66: June 25, 2013 per Wikipedia, the NYT obituary and Artnet, against June 26 per Princeton and the Brooklyn Rail; place Hartford per Wikipedia and the NYT, against Falls Village per Artnet and the Paula Cooper and Campoli CVs, against "at her home in Connecticut" per Princeton. June 25 is the majority and NYT-consistent date; June 26 may reflect when the death was learned. Do not state a single place without noting the conflict. (C21211, leg 1 C21013-C21018, D1, D2, D5, D12; synthesis C21172)


## M1 -- Unwriting: mask the text and the picture decisions become legible

Photograph a newspaper front page at actual size and mask out all running text, keeping only the masthead, the images, and the captions. The absence is the tool: with the story text gone, image size, placement above or below the fold, and cropping become legible as editorial decisions. In "Movie-Television-News-History, June 21, 1979" (W01) she collected the front pages of 27 American newspapers, all published the same day, all reporting the shooting of ABC journalist Bill Stewart by Nicaraguan National Guard forces, and masked each one the same way; the 27 prints, each about 22 by 14 inches, are the same event told 27 ways by picture decisions alone. Many of the source images are themselves stills photographed from television, some cropped to the shape of a television screen, so the work is a photograph of a newspaper photograph of a television image. In "April 19, 20, 21, 1978" (W02) the selection logic flips: one story, the abduction of Aldo Moro by the Red Brigades, across three consecutive days. The two works state the two selection logics the whole career will reuse: hold the day constant and vary the paper, or hold the story constant and vary the day. The predecessor, "Historical Materialism: Chile Series" (1976-77), only lightened the text around Chile stories; Modern History arrives at masking all of it. MoMA calls the masking "unwriting": an active removal that makes the editorial choices visible because the text that normally carries the story is gone and only the picture decisions remain. (C21212, leg 1 C21006; leg 2 W01-W02; synthesis C21150)

**Takeaway:** remove the words and the picture decisions become the story. (C21213)


## M2 -- Rephotography at altered scale: photograph a photograph, then make it life size

Take a found photograph, tear or crop it to a standard proportion (1:2 in Stills), mount it on board, rephotograph it, and enlarge it far beyond its source size. Charlesworth never photographs the depicted event; she photographs a photograph of it. In "Stills" (W03-W05, 1980) the enlargement is to mural scale, 78 by 42 inches, just over life size, with titles giving only a name (if known) and a place -- "Patricia Cawlings, Los Angeles", "Unidentified Man, Ontani Hotel, Los Angeles", "Unidentified Man, Unidentified Location". The "Unidentified" in the title is part of the work: where the archive gave no name, she supplies none. Seven images were first shown in February 1980 in Tony Shafrazi's East Village apartment; she later printed an eighth from the original source material in 2009 and, as an Art Institute of Chicago commission, six more from unprinted transparencies, bringing the complete series to 14 photographs -- do not say 7 total. One Stills image (W05) is twice removed: Warhol's "Suicide" (1962) silkscreen of a press photograph, rephotographed again. The mechanism is rephotography and serial presentation, NOT the depicted fall: the fall belongs to the source image, and what Charlesworth made is the selection, the tearing, the rephotography, the enlargement, the scale, and the serial hanging. No prompt built from this mechanism may be built around the fall itself. (C21214, leg 1 C21007; leg 2 W03-W05 and GUARD; synthesis C21151)

**Takeaway:** rephotograph the photograph and enlarge it past life size, and the clipping confronts the viewer's body instead of the viewer's eye. (C21215)


## M3 -- Cut-out isolation on a monochrome ground, with a colour code

Excise a single image-object from a book or magazine, rephotograph it centred and floating against a flat field of pure colour, print it as a high-gloss Cibachrome in a matching lacquered wood frame. The levelling is the point: a cheap garment gets the same luxury finish as a religious statue, so the desire attached to it becomes visible as a property of the image, not of the thing. In "White T-Shirt" (W07, 1983) a sculpted, wet-looking, body-shaped garment with no body in it floats on its ground; in "Red Scarf" (W08, 1983) the same treatment is given to a single red scarf. "Harness" (W09, 1983-84) is a studded black leather dominatrix harness isolated against a monochrome lacquered red background, and the viewer supplies the wearer underneath the taut straps. The colour is a code before it is decoration (Collector Daily): red = sexuality, yellow = material desire, blue = metaphysical longing, green = nature, black = the unknown. The lacquered frame is part of the mechanism too: it gives a cut-out magazine image the finish of a luxury object. The transitional work is "Samurai" (W06, 1981): Toshiro Mifune as a samurai, excised and rephotographed as a single monumental figure in a lacquered wood frame, 63 7/8 by 49 7/8 inches -- the cut-out-and-enlarge mechanism already in place, two years before Objects of Desire, and it must not be used to backdate the series to 1981. (C21216, leg 1 C21008; leg 2 W06-W09; synthesis C21152)

**Takeaway:** isolate one thing on one flat colour and finish it like luxury, and the desire shows up as a property of the image. (C21217)


## M4 -- Diptych pairing: the advancing panel and the receding panel

Hang two cut-outs as a pair so each changes the other's meaning: the known, apparent image in the advancing panel, the secondary or hidden image in the receding panel, with no closure in the pairing. In "Figures" (W10, 1983-84) the left panel shows a shiny 1930s-style evening dress, graceful and body-contoured, and the right panel a recumbent bondage figure, which could be male or female; Charlesworth put "the most known, apparent image in the advancing panel and the image with the secondary or more hidden meaning in the receding panel". The extreme form is "Fear of Nothing" (W11, 1988): one panel is an empty black field, the other holds a classical mask and geometric shapes on blue -- the object reduced to almost nothing, the colour field carrying the meaning. MoMA's reading, quoted as description: the black panel could be the representation of nothing itself, a blank space or void, and the mask recoils in fear of the void; or, by the blunt force of the title, the work is about fearlessness itself. The viewer is left to decide which panel is the front and which is the hidden one, whether the black panel is nothing or fearlessness -- the pairing never closes. (C21218, leg 2 W10-W11; synthesis C21153)

**Takeaway:** pair the known image with the hidden one and give the pairing no closure, and the viewer has to finish the work. (C21219)


## M5 -- Serial selection logic: the rule is the work

Each multi-part work states its selection rule in its construction: same day across many papers (W01), same story across consecutive days (W02), same size and same tearing rule across 14 falls (W03-W05), 40 figures under one framing rule ("Figure Drawings", conceived 1988 -- 1998 per one review, carry both -- completed 2008: dark grey figures on white grounds, a Jesus figure bearing a cross, a belly dancer, an East Indian goddess nursing an infant, the Oscar statuette). The rule is the work as much as any single image. Modern History is a thirteen-part opus by this logic, and the 2015 survey counted the series in the same terms: 34 Modern History prints, 14 Stills, 19 Objects of Desire, 40 Figure Drawings, 3 Renaissance Paintings, 5 Doubleworld, 4 0+1, 1 Neverland, 2 Work in Progress, 6 Available Light. A series is one idea worked to its conclusion, and the conclusion is reached when the rule has been run to its end, not when a good picture turns up. (C21220, leg 2 W01-W05 and Beyond the 20; synthesis C21154)

**Takeaway:** state the selection rule in the construction, and the rule is the work as much as any single image. (C21221)


## M6 -- Staged in-camera illusion: the trick is built in the studio, not cut from a magazine

From Natural Magic (1992-94) on, build the trick in the studio and photograph it with analog means -- double exposure, special-effect props, light and scale manipulation with a medium- or large-format camera -- rather than cutting found images. No Photoshop compositing; the camera's own properties produce an image that cannot be true and looks true. In "Trial by Fire" (W15, 1993) two white gloves, suspended in an oval black ground, spontaneously emit flames; the oval recalls nineteenth-century travelling magic-show portals. The Guggenheim: the series "uses photography as medium and subject to explore the image's much vaunted transparency to the real", and the picture "likens photography to a form of charlatanry and conjures the artifice that has always surrounded the medium". This is the watershed both Collector Daily and Maccarone name: the first series staged entirely in the studio and "within the camera". The artist stops cutting up other people's photographs and starts constructing photographs, but the subject stays the same: how a photograph makes belief. (C21222, leg 1 C21005; leg 2 W15; synthesis C21155)

**Takeaway:** stage the impossible in front of the camera and let the camera's own tricks make it look true. (C21223)


## M7 -- Still life of optical instruments: doubling, literal and structural

Assemble antique cameras, stereoscopic viewers, telescopes and daguerreotypes as a Dutch still life and photograph them, so the picture contains both the instrument of seeing and the thing seen. "Doubleworld" (W16, 1995) presents two nineteenth-century stereoscopic viewing devices, each holding a stereophotograph of two women standing side by side -- doubling literal (two viewers, two women, two eyes) and structural (a photograph of a device for looking at photographs). The mahogany frame quotes nineteenth-century photographic presentation. "Still Life with Camera" (W17, 1995) is a diptych divided by a mahogany frame: on the right, a box camera aimed at the group of objects on the left -- a wine bottle, a leather-bound book, a pair of wire glasses, a daguerreotype in an open case -- so the camera and its subject are both inside the same picture. The Dutch still-life tradition (fruit, flowers, skulls; abundance and decay) is transposed onto the instruments of photography itself. The title of the 2015 New Museum survey and the 2017 LACMA exhibition is taken from this work. (C21224, leg 2 W16-W17; synthesis C21156)

**Takeaway:** put the instrument of seeing inside the picture, and the photograph photographs itself. (C21225)


## M8 -- Threshold and light: white on white, and window light as subject

Two late forms of the same subtraction. (a) White on white: photograph a white object on a white ground flooded with light, so only an edge or a shadow distinguishes object from ground. "Seated Buddha" (W18, 2000, in 0+1) is a white Buddha photographed in white light against a white background, 15 by 12 inches -- the same motif that appeared in Objects of Desire, bathed in blue, now shrunk to something the eye must work to find. The series title states the mechanism: 0 and 1, the smallest possible difference between an image and no image. The only difference between the "Seated" Buddha and the "Levitating" one is the barest suggestion of shadow at the base. (b) Available light as subject: photograph refractive objects -- crystal ball, prism, half bowl, hourglass -- in unfiltered studio window light, and hang those images beside documentary images of the studio, so the conditions of making are visible in the same show as the illusions they produce. "Crystal Ball" (W19, 2011) is 32 by 32 inches; "Half Bowl" (W20, 2011) is 41 1/2 by 32 inches -- the ball that inverts the world, and the bowl that catches light and holds it. Work date 2011, series and exhibition date 2012; do not merge them. Cross-cutting device, not a ninth mechanism: minimal titling -- titles give only what the archive gave (a name and a place; a single noun; a concept) and never narrate. (C21226, leg 2 W18-W20 and M10; synthesis C21157)

**Takeaway:** subtract until only an edge or a shadow is left, and the viewer has to work to see anything at all. (C21227)


## The evolution: the source moves from the newspaper to the studio, the mechanism never moves

Early (1977-82): found paper and found body -- the work is done at a table with files, scissors and a camera, not behind a camera pointed at the world. "Historical Materialism: Chile Series" (1976-77) lightens text around Chile stories; Modern History (1977-79) arrives at masking all text; Stills (1980) keeps the rephotographic move and changes scale and subject (front page to single figure, actual size to 78 inches tall); Samurai (1981, W06) carries the cut-out into the single celebrity figure at mural scale, two years before Objects of Desire -- it must not backdate that series. Middle (1983-91): colour, desire, interior turn -- Objects of Desire adds colour, gloss and the lacquered frame to the cut-out; the diptych (W10) and the empty panel (W11) push the isolation until the object nearly disappears. "The Academy of Secrets" ("Subtle Body", W12, 1989) keeps the cut-and-rephotograph toolkit but changes the selection logic from intellectual argument to feeling -- a vertical stack of eight cut-out objects read as a body, ordered by feeling while she was pregnant, at body size (78 by 57 inches), "to confront the body, to contact the emotional body of the viewer". "Renaissance Paintings" and "Renaissance Drawings" (W13, W14, 1991) apply the same cut-out to art history itself, with psychoanalytic rather than journalistic source material. Late (1992-95): behind the camera -- Natural Magic is the watershed, the first series staged entirely in the studio and "within the camera"; Doubleworld continues the studio staging but turns the camera on its own history: antique instruments as still life. Latest (2000-12): threshold and light -- 0+1 (2000) reduces the image to white on white; Neverland (2002) returns colour with studio objects on matching grounds and is the first series with digital manipulation; "Work in Progress" (2009) photographs the camera and its tools through transparent paper; Available Light (2011/2012) makes window light itself the subject. The late work is quieter and more inward than the early work, but the mechanism never changes: isolate one thing, control the ground, control the light, and let the viewer do the reading. (C21228, leg 1 C21005; leg 2 Evolution; synthesis C21158-C21162)

## Aesthetic logic: removal is where the meaning is made

Charlesworth's aesthetic logic is that the image is the argument, not the illustration of one. Every series removes something -- text (unwriting), context (cut-out), the object itself (Fear of Nothing, white on white) -- and the removal is where the meaning is made. The viewer is left to complete the work: which panel is front and which is hidden, whether the black panel is nothing or fearlessness, whether the white Buddha is there at all. Scale is a mechanism, not a format choice: Stills at 78 inches tall make a press clipping just over life size, so a body the newspaper shrank to inches confronts the viewer's body; Subtle Body is built to human-body size expressly to confront the body; the 0+1 Buddha at 15 by 12 inches does the opposite, shrinking the sacred to something the eye must work to find. Colour is a code before it is decoration: in Objects of Desire the monochrome ground labels the desire, and in the late work the ground stops contrasting with the object (white on white, matching grounds in Neverland) and the code collapses into the threshold itself. Photography is the subject at every stage, never the transparent window: Modern History photographs newspapers photographing television; Stills rephotographs press photographs (once, via Warhol); Natural Magic likens photography to charlatanry; Doubleworld photographs the devices for looking at photographs; Available Light photographs what light does in the studio and shows the studio beside it. The career is one sustained inquiry into how a photograph makes belief, carried through five different source regimes. A prompt that takes the cut-out and leaves the ground will make a collage; a prompt that takes the ground and leaves the selection rule will make a colour field; a prompt that takes both, and lets the viewer finish it, will make a Charlesworth. (C21230, synthesis C21163-C21166)
