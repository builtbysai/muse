# Gertrude Stein: Study Notes

**Site:** Gertrude Stein (1874-1946), American writer, poet, playwright and art collector -- born Tuesday 3 February 1874 in Allegheny, Pennsylvania, youngest child of Daniel Stein and Amelia Stein, nee Keyser, German-Jewish parents; the family moved to Vienna and Paris when she was three, then settled in Oakland, California, in 1878, on a 10-acre farm in East Oakland; her mother died in 1888, her father in 1891; Radcliffe College 1893-1897, student of William James, who called her his "most brilliant woman student", coauthor with Leon Mendez Solomons of "Normal Motor Automatism"; Johns Hopkins School of Medicine 1897-1901, left in her fourth year without a degree; Paris 1903 with her brother Leo Stein, the two of them pooling family-trust stipends to buy contemporary art at Ambroise Vollard's gallery, and renting the apartment and atelier at 27 rue de Fleurus where the Saturday-evening salon ran for some forty years; the collection bought Matisse's Woman with a Hat and Picasso's Young Girl with a Flower Basket at the Salon d'Automne of October 1905, and Picasso painted her portrait over the winter of 1905-06 in eighty or ninety sittings, painting the head out in spring 1906 and finishing the face from memory; met Alice B. Toklas on Sunday 8 September 1907, Toklas's first day in Paris, and the two ran the household and the salon together for more than thirty years after Leo left in 1913 or April 1914 and the collection was divided, Gertrude keeping the Picassos and most of the Matisse; author of Q.E.D. (completed Saturday 24 October 1903, published 1950 as Things As They Are), Three Lives (1909), The Making of Americans (written 1906-08 by her account, 1902-1911 by her biographers' evidence, published 1925 in 500 copies), Tender Buttons (1914), Geography and Plays (1922), Four Saints in Three Acts (libretto 1927, music by Virgil Thomson 1928, premiered Wednesday 7 February 1934 at the Wadsworth Atheneum, Hartford), The Autobiography of Alice B. Toklas (1933, her first best-seller), Lectures in America (1935), Everybody's Autobiography (1937), Picasso (1938), The World Is Round (1939), Paris France (1940), Wars I Have Seen (1945) and Brewsie and Willie (1946); drove a Ford named "Auntie" carrying supplies to French hospitals 1916-1918; toured America October 1934 to May 1935, 191 days, 23 states, 37 cities; fled Paris for Bilignin in September 1939 and spent the war there, translating some 27 to 32 speeches by Philippe Petain 1941-1943 at Bernard Fay's suggestion, a project never published in her lifetime; died Saturday 27 July 1946 at the American Hospital in Neuilly-sur-Seine, of stomach cancer, aged 72, and is buried in Pere Lachaise Cemetery, Paris, with Toklas later buried alongside her. ## M1. Insistence, Not Repetition
Say the same small thing again with a different emphasis each time, until the repeat is a new event rather than a copy. Stein's own doctrine, stated in the lecture "Portraits and Repetition" (one of the Lectures in America, delivered 1934-35, published 1935): "there is no such thing as repetition." The full sentence, quoted in two independent secondary sources read in the main leg: "if you like repetition, that is if you like the repeating that is the same thing, but once started expressing this thing, expressing any thing there can be no repetition because the essence is insistence, and if you insist you must each time use emphasis and if you use emphasis it is not possible while anybody is alive that they should use exactly the same emphasis" (Lectures in America, p. 167). The same lecture: "the repetition consists in knowing that that one is a kind of one" and "each sentence is just the difference in emphasis" (p. 198). The distinction is repetition (mere rephrasing) vs insistence (re-experiencing). The binary form of the same mechanism is the shutter: "Shutters shut and open so do queens. Shutters shut and shutters and so shutters shut and shutters and so and so shutters and so shutters shut and so. And so shutters shut and so and also. And also and so and so and also" ("If I Told Him", 1923) -- two states flipping, open/shut, now/not now, until the flip itself is the portrait; the main leg's M19, folded here. In "Melanctha" the defining formula "Melanctha Herbert was a graceful, pale yellow, intelligent, attractive" recurs as a fixed phrase whose meaning shifts with context. What it IS: a motif that loops but never lands identically; each pass changes emphasis by a small delta, and the drift is the story. **Takeaway:** repeat the same beat 3-7 times, shifting one parameter a fraction each time; never copy a beat exactly, because an exact copy is dead.

## M2. The Continuous Present
All events presented simultaneously, with no past or future tense to order them. In "Composition as Explanation" (lecture delivered at Cambridge and Oxford, 1926; published by Hogarth Press 1926): "The time of the composition is the time of the composition... In my beginning it was a continuous present a beginning again and again and again and again, it was a series it was a list it was a similarity and everything different it was a distribution and an equilibrium." And: "A composition of a prolonged present is a natural composition in the world as it has been these thirty years it was more and more a prolonged present. I created then a prolonged present naturally I knew nothing of a continuous present but it came naturally to me to make one." The business of art, per Stein: "to live in the actual present, that is the complete actual present, and to completely express that complete actual present." Mechanically, the present tense is not a tense choice but a tense refusal: Stein removes past-tense narration and future-tense promise, so every sentence happens now. The Jamesian root: William James's "stream of consciousness" chapter in Psychology (1892) -- "ideas recur, although the ideas may be the same, we see them in different relationships" -- and Stein's use of repetition to change a word's implications by re-use, "just as the physical act of repeating a word aloud can alter its meaning." In "Melanctha" the piece opens with the ending (Rose Johnson's baby dies) and relays the same information again almost identically nearly a hundred pages later, because the character is fixed and the events can come in any sequence. What it IS: a scene with no before/after; elements arrive all at once and stay, like a room you walk around rather than a story you follow. **Takeaway:** remove the timeline and let everything be present at once; the beginning is a beginning again and again.

## M3. The Noun Caressed
A single word is repeated until it sheds its associations and becomes a thing in itself. The exact original wording: "Rose is a rose is a rose is a rose." Four roses; the first "Rose" capitalized. It appears in the 1913 poem "Sacred Emily", published in the 1922 book Geography and Plays; in that poem the first "Rose" is the name of a person. The shortened three-rose form "A rose is a rose is a rose" is the famous quotation and is a later variation. Stein's own later account, from Lectures in America: "When I said. 'A rose is a rose is a rose.' And then later made that into a ring I made poetry and what did I do? I caressed completely caressed and addressed a noun." Also from Four in America: "Now listen! I'm no fool. I know that in daily life we don't go around saying 'is a ... is a ... is a ...' Yes, I'm no fool; but I think that in that line the rose is red for the first time in English poetry for a hundred years." Her explanation to an Oxford audience: when the Romantics used the word "rose", it had a direct relationship to an actual rose; for later periods the word also carries the archetypical elements of the romantic era; the sentence expresses the fact that simply using the name of a thing already invokes the imagery and emotions associated with it. Other versions by Stein herself: "Do we suppose that all she knows is that a rose is a rose is a rose is a rose." (Operas and Plays); "... she would carve on the tree Rose is a Rose is a Rose is a Rose is a Rose until it went all the way around." (The World is Round); "A rose tree may be a rose tree may be a rosy rose tree if watered." (Alphabets and Birthdays); "Civilization begins with a rose. A rose is a rose is a rose is a rose. It continues with blooming and it fastens clearly upon excellent examples." (As Fine as Melanctha). "Sacred Emily" itself consists of exactly 367 staccato lines repeating phrases such as "push sea" eight times in one line. What it IS: one word repeated until it turns strange, then familiar again in a new way; the repetition is the caress. **Takeaway:** pick one noun and say it four times, letting each saying change what the word means; never explain the noun, caress it.

## M4. The Cinema Portrait
A subject is built from many near-identical frames, each just that much different from the one before, until the frames fuse into one persistent image. From "Portraits and Repetition": "In a cinema picture no two pictures are exactly alike each one is just that much different than the one before, and so in those early portraits there was ... no repetition." And: by "doing what the cinema was doing" she made "a continuous succession until [she] had not many things but one thing." Also: "any one is of one's period and this our period was undoubtedly the period of the cinema and series production" (Lectures in America, p. 177). Her method, from the same lecture: "I had to find out inside every one what was in them that was intrinsically exciting and I had to find it out not by what they said not by what they did ... but I had to find it out by the intensity of movement that there was inside in any one of them." And: "I must find out what is moving inside them that makes them them, and I must find out how I by the thing moving excitedly inside me can make a portrait of them" (Lectures in America, p. 183). The portrait period (1908-1913) includes "Ada" (Alice B. Toklas), "Two Women" (the Cone sisters), "Miss Furr and Miss Skeene" (Ethel Mars and Maud Hunt Squire), "Men", "Matisse" (1909), "Picasso" (1909), "Portrait of Mabel Dodge at the Villa Curonia" (1911), "Guillaume Apollinaire" (1913). The model on the wall was Picasso's Portrait of Gertrude Stein (1905-06), for which she sat some 80-90 times; Picasso painted out her head in frustration, left for Spain, and on returning finished the face from memory, without her in the room; when people said it did not look like her, Picasso replied, "She will". Stein later said: "I was and still am satisfied with my portrait, for me it is I, and it is the only reproduction of me which is always I, for me." What it IS: a face or figure assembled from flickering near-duplicates that never quite settle, and the settling is the portrait. **Takeaway:** build a subject from twenty near-identical frames, each shifted by one small difference; the person emerges from the flicker, not from any single frame.

## M5. Exact Resemblance Through Permutation
Resemblance is achieved not by likeness but by exhausting the grammatical combinations of a few words. "If I Told Him: A Completed Portrait of Picasso" (1923, written at Antibes that summer; first published in Vanity Fair 1924) was read in full in the main leg. Opening: "If I told him would he like it. Would he like it if I told him. / Would he like it would Napoleon would Napoleon would would he like it." The "exact resemblance" passage, in the manuscript-corrected form: "Exact resemblance. To exact resemblance the exact resemblance as exact as a resemblance, exactly as resembling, exactly resembling, exactly in resemblance exactly a resemblance, exactly and resemblance. For this is so. Because." Note the period after "Exact resemblance" in the manuscript makes "To exact" a verb -- "The verb 'to exact' adds energy to the creative struggle." (Ulla Dydo's note.) The poem then works the words "exactly", "first", "presently", "shutters", "he", "land", "trains" through permutation, and ends: "Let me recite what history teaches. History teaches." Stein defended Cubism as representational all her life: one gets to an exact resemblance "through the distortion, repetition, and altering of the present moment to mimic perception." In her 1938 book Picasso she recounts that in 1909 Picasso showed her photographs that had inspired the Cubist paintings Horta de Ebro and Maison sur la Colline, and "Stein swore that they were no different than the photographs." What it IS: a small word set (if / I / him / told / exactly / first / presently / shutters) permuted until a person emerges from the pattern rather than from description. **Takeaway:** take eight words and permute them until the person appears; resemblance is the pattern, not the likeness.

## M6. The Object on the Table
A domestic object is described without narrative, use, or backstory; only its relations are given. Tender Buttons (1914), written summer 1912 in Spain, published May 1914 by Claire Marie Editions, New York, in 1,000 copies, in three sections: "Objects", "Food", "Rooms". The opening poem, "A Carafe, That is a Blind Glass": "A kind in glass and a cousin, a spectacle and nothing strange a single hurt color and an arrangement in a system to pointing. All this and not ordinary, not unordered in not resembling. The difference is spreading." Per Marjorie Perloff's reading: a carafe is "a kind in glass" (a member of the glass family, with "cousins" bottles, pitchers, jugs, tumblers); "a blind glass" because filled with red wine it cannot be seen through, because it does not mirror the spectator, and because unlike spectacles it does not improve vision; "a single hurt color" is the wine, red as hurt/blood; "an arrangement in a system to pointing" is the Cubist compositional arrangement in which each thing is related to every other thing -- grammar itself. The close: "The difference is spreading" -- no two instances are ever identical, even when the same phrase is repeated. The sections are "not 'about' subjects that are discussed but are their own discrete word objects (verbal constellations). Meaning... is not something to be extracted or deciphered but rather to be responded to." Stein's own claim (1946 "A Transatlantic Interview"): the book was entirely "realistic" in the tradition of Flaubert -- "I used to take objects on a table, like a tumbler or any kind of object and try to get the picture of it clear and separate in my mind and create a word relationship between the word and the things seen." Other named pieces: "A Handkerchief", "A Petticoat", "A Red Stamp", "Roastbeef", "Mutton", "Milk", "Sugar", "Eggs", "Potatoes", "A Box", "A Plate", "A Little Piece of String". The "Rooms" section opens: "Act so that there is no use in a center." What it IS: still-life scenes where objects are named by their kinship ("a kind in glass and a cousin") instead of their function. **Takeaway:** put one object on a table and describe only its relations -- its cousins, its color, its place in the system; never say what it is for.

## M7. The Taboo Game
The expected words for a thing are deliberately avoided; the thing is circled until its sense arrives without being named. In "A Red Stamp", Stein avoids all the words of association (envelope, letter, send, mail, corner) and talks around the subject, asserting "that the sense of an object can be gleaned without typical or expected explanation." The linguistic-relativity reading: "it is possible to indicate an object without stating it outright." The title itself works this way: buttons fasten discrete pieces of fabric (a compositional image); "Tender Buttons" also suggests nipples/clitorises -- "decentered eroticism (meaning disseminated evenly over the body of the text not cathected onto nouns or plot)." What it IS: an object rendered entirely from the words around it, never the word for it; the viewer must supply the missing center. **Takeaway:** forbid the obvious word for the thing and build the thing from everything around it; the absence is the portrait.

## M8. Grammar as Arrangement (The System of Pointing)
Words are placed the way Cubist painters place planes: each thing related to every other thing, with no center and no hierarchy. Grounded in the carafe poem ("an arrangement in a system to pointing") and in the Rooms opening, "Act so that there is no use in a center." The field is flat: Cezanne's lesson, as Stein stated it, was that "in composition one thing was as important as another thing. Each part is as important as the whole." Wikipedia's Literary style section: Cezanne influenced her idea of equality, distinguished from universality: "the whole field of the canvas is important." "Rather than a figure/ground relationship, 'Stein in her work with words used the entire text as a field in which every element mattered as much as any other.'" Stein explained: "[T]he important thing... is that you must have deep down as the deepest thing in you a sense of equality." She owned a portrait of Mme Cezanne painted by her husband and stared at it while working on Three Lives; she credited the Cezanne portrait of Mme Cezanne -- which hung above her desk while she wrote Three Lives -- as the revelatory model: "that the stylistic method of (Three Lives) had been influenced by the Cezanne portrait under which she sat writing. The portrait of Madame Cezanne is one of the monumental examples of the artist's method, each exacting, carefully negotiated plane... having been structured into existence, seeming to fix the subject for all eternity. So it was with Gertrude's repetitive sentences, each one building up, phrase by phrase, the substance of her characters." What it IS: a composition with no focal point; attention moves across a field where every element carries equal weight. **Takeaway:** give every element the same weight and remove the center; the arrangement is the meaning, not any one piece.

## M9. Bottom Nature and the Everyone Typology
People are sorted by an essence that shows in how they say the same thing over and over with infinite variation. The Making of Americans: Being a History of a Family's Progress (written 1906-08 by Stein's account, 1902-1911 by her biographers' evidence; published 1925 by Contact Press, Paris, in a limited run of 500 copies; much-abridged Harcourt Brace edition 1934; full version out of print until Something Else Press 1966; definitive Dalkey Archive edition 1995). Stein set out to write "a history of every one who ever can or is or was or will be living." The ostensible subject is three generations of the fictional Hersland and Dehning families, generalized from her own family; she claimed it was the history of all Americans. The method: the "bottom nature" of men and women -- the essence discernible beneath social dissemblance and habit. From Lectures in America: "I then began again to think about the bottom nature in people, I began to get enormously interested in hearing how everybody said the same thing over and over again with infinite variations but over and over again until finally if you listened with great intensity you could hear it rise and fall and tell all that there was inside them, not so much by the actual words they said or the thoughts they had but the movement of their thoughts and words endlessly the same and endlessly different." The typology in Stein's terms: "dependent" and "independent" types, defined by bottom nature as "independent dependent" (submissive) or "dependent independent" (dominant), making one an "attacking" or "resisting" one; then types of "instrument" (subconsciously receptive to instruction) and "earthy" (having "common natural sense") nature. Britannica: the book "lacks plot, dialogue, and action" and Stein "produced what many readers found to be a repetitious, prolix compilation of vignettes" in fitting her prose "to the sameness or very slight variations she found in human nature." Working from charts and diagrams begun at Radcliffe, she conceived the book in "a space of time that is always filled with moving" -- a conception she considered typically American; "any one is of one's period and this is our period was undoubtedly the period of the cinema and series production." Excerpts were published in the Transatlantic Review in 1924 at Hemingway's urging (Ford Madox Ford agreed). Stein compared it to Joyce's Ulysses and Proust's In Search of Lost Time. What it IS: a crowd of figures each repeating one small gesture; the gesture's rhythm, not its content, tells you who they are. **Takeaway:** give each figure one small repeated gesture and let the rhythm of the repeat sort them; never explain a character, listen to how they repeat.

## M10. The Long Sentence and the Fugal Restart
A sentence that refuses to end, interrupted by fresh beginnings: "To begin then again", "As I was saying ..." The aim, in Stein's lecture "The Gradual Making of the Making of Americans", was to "put down a whole human being felt at one and the same time." "Loving repeating is one way of being" -- and it could take a very long time to achieve "a complete understanding of a character in this way, 'to feel the whole of anyone from the beginning to the ending.'" In "Poetry and Grammar", Stein says the natural way to count "is not that one and one make two" but "to go on counting by one and one": "One and one and one and one and one. That is the natural way to go on." What it IS: a line of text or motion that keeps restarting from the same point, each restart adding one more clause, until the whole person is present at once; a counter that ticks 1, 1, 1, 1... and never reaches 2, a series that grows by adjacency, not by sum. **Takeaway:** begin again from the same point and add one clause each time; the accumulation, not the ending, is the portrait.

## M11. Character as Fixed Type Proved by Identical Description
A character never changes; the proof is that the same sentence describes her at every point of the story. Three Lives (written 1905-06, begun spring 1905, finished 1906; published 1909 by Grafton Press at Stein's own expense; her first critically acclaimed publication). Three stories: "The Good Anna", "Melanctha", "The Gentle Lena". (One low-quality search result, vocal.media, names the third story "Bertha" -- that is WRONG; the third story is "The Gentle Lena", confirmed by Wikipedia and thefreelibrary.com. Do not repeat "Bertha".) Melanctha: the most experimental story, a young African-American woman (Melanctha Herbert) in a turbulent relationship with Jeff Campbell. The repetitive accumulation: Stein repeats the defining formula "Melanctha Herbert was a graceful, pale yellow, intelligent, attractive" continually throughout the piece. The sentence "Anna led an arduous and troubled life" is repeated thrice while describing Anna's occupations, and "You see that Anna led an arduous and troubled life" recurs as a structural refrain linking episodes. Characters' verb tenses and word choice dictate personality: Jeff's consciousness is full of participles ("loving", "being good to everybody", "trying to understand") as he struggles to achieve his emotions rather than simply feeling them, while Melanctha says "I certainly do understand", asserting a definitive nature through verbs. Because the character is fixed, "the events of the story can come in any sequence. Melanctha is the same person throughout the story, unchanged by her environment, which Stein proves by removing the logical progression of time." Word patterns "begin with mere repetition and accumulate meaning by association and juxtaposition, splinter or ramify, sending out subsidiary branch patterns, and discover occasions for recombination or intersection of the various branches in tie-in lines." Short repetitions delimit "narrative bubbles" while long repetitions invest the reader in character development, and repetitions across stories "insist on location" as the interaction of location, yearning and persona. What it IS: a figure who falls in and out of love, lives and dies, while her label never changes; the story is the label's persistence. **Takeaway:** fix the character's label once and never change it; let the events shuffle around the label, and the label's survival is the story.

## M12. The Play as Landscape
A play with no plot, in which scenes are places and the audience's emotion coincides with the scene because nothing is ahead or behind. Four Saints in Three Acts: words by Gertrude Stein, music by Virgil Thomson. Stein gave Thomson the libretto in June 1927; he finished setting it in July 1928. Premiered Wednesday 7 February 1934 (one secondary source gives Thursday 8 February 1934; the 7th is the better attested) at the Wadsworth Atheneum, Hartford, Connecticut, inaugural event of the Avery Memorial Theatre, sets by Florine Stettheimer, choreography by Frederick Ashton, all-black cast; Broadway, 44th Street Theatre, Tuesday 20 February 1934, then the longest-running opera in Broadway history. The opera as landscape: Beinecke dramaturge Timothy Young calls Four Saints "simply a landscape populated by things and pieces of things." Stein's own synopsis: "Act I is scenes from the life of Saint Teresa of Avila. Act II is a garden party in the country near Barcelona. Act III is about Saint Ignatius of Loyola. Act IV is the memory of the other saints and the ending." Note the title lies: it is neither about four saints (about thirty saints appear; the four of the title are Teresa, Ignatius, Saint Settlement -- Teresa's confidante -- and Saint Chavez -- Ignatius's aide) nor in three acts (there are four). "There is an absence of plot in the conventional sense; instead, the author presents a series of seven pictures or tableaux in which the characters move with minimal action. Stein's libretto lacks continuity and movement, emphasizing a circular, repetitive structure." The landscape theory behind it, from the "Plays" lecture (1935): conventional theater makes the viewer "nervous" because the viewer's emotion is always "either behind or ahead of the play". In landscape, spatial relations replace dramatic development: "if the play was exactly like a landscape," the viewer's emotion would coincide with the play "much as a viewer appears to be co-present with landscape that is simply there" (Lectures in America, p. 263). The phases: early plays (A Circular Play, Objects Lie on a Table) are a Cubist phase -- an inclosed space in which objects are present, time virtually eliminated, the "continuous present"; by Four Saints, the play is a landscape -- the inclosed space filled with shifting perspectives depending on the observer's position, "and anything could be introduced into it." After Four Saints, little theoretical development; Listen To Me is "a verbal landscape, a meditation on language, with grammar and syntax for its subject"; The Mother of Us All unites the play-as-landscape with recognizable thematic material and is her most conventional play. What it IS: a stage that is a place, not a story -- the viewer's emotion is co-present with the scene because nothing is ahead or behind. **Takeaway:** build a place, not a plot; let the viewer stand inside it while things happen, and never let the scene run ahead of the viewer's feeling.

## M13. The Saint's Ordinary Day
Sacred figures doing un-sacred chores: mending fish nets, military drill, a garden party, a picnic. In Four Saints: Act One, the saints "sing, they pray, they receive callers, and they take trips to found convents" ("half indoors and half out out of doors"); Act Two, a garden party/picnic -- "light-hearted games" and a vision of the Celestial Mansions; Act Three, Ignatius with his Jesuits near Barcelona -- "mend fish nets, do military drill, and witness a miraculous vision of the Holy Ghost." The famous lines: Saint Ignatius's vision of the Holy Ghost, "Pigeons on the grass alas and a magpie in the sky"; the communion hymn "When this you see remember me"; the end of Act III, "Letting pin in letting let", representing a religious procession. Thomson's music: modern yet reflective of American traditions -- foxtrots, folk dances, Protestant hymns. NOTE: the brief's "let's as operatic language" was NOT verified in any source read in any leg and is not claimed here. What IS verified is the landscape structure, the tableaux, and the quoted lines above. What it IS: holiness shown as routine -- the same small tasks repeated until a vision interrupts them, and the vision is itself just another thing in the landscape. **Takeaway:** give the sacred figure a chore and let her do it the same way every day; the miracle arrives as weather, not as plot.

## M14. The Ventriloquist Memoir
A life told in someone else's voice, so the "I" becomes a "she" and the self is built from outside. The Autobiography of Alice B. Toklas (1933): published 1933; written in six weeks for money; Stein's first best-seller and the book that lifted her "from literary obscurity to almost immediate celebrity in the United States". Seven chapters covering the first three decades of the twentieth century. Written in the voice of Alice B. Toklas, Stein's life partner (met Sunday 8 September 1907, Toklas's first day in Paris). The ventriloquist structure: the title declares Toklas the subject; the book is in fact Stein's life. One essay calls the narrative perspective a ventriloquist act "with Stein as a ventriloquist and Toklas as her dummy", breaking the trinity of author-subject-narrator that defines autobiography. "Wearing Toklas's voice like a mask, Stein produces a brisk, intimate chronicle that doubles as her own life story... The prose speaks in Toklas's cool, declarative tone, doting on a figure invariably called 'Gertrude Stein.'" The book opens with Toklas's California background and arrival in Paris in 1907, then settles into 27 rue de Fleurus. The salon as subject: the Saturday evenings are the book's engine -- "life in Paris was based upon the rue de Fleurus and the Saturday evenings and it was like a kaleidoscope slowly turning. Picasso was there with his high whinnying spanish giggle as were Cezanne and Matisse Hemingway and Fitzgerald. As Toklas put it: The geniuses came and talked to Gertrude Stein and the wives sat with me." The book's famous line on geniuses: "The three geniuses of whom I wish to speak are Gertrude Stein, Pablo Picasso, and Alfred Whitehead." Backlash: Eugene Jolas's pamphlet Testimony against Gertrude Stein, in which Matisse and Braque objected to her portrayal of the Paris community; Braque: "she had entirely misunderstood cubism which she sees simply in terms of personalities." Hemingway called the Autobiography "damned pitiful"; Leo called it a "farrago of lies". What it IS: a portrait gallery where the narrator never says "I" about herself but keeps pointing at the genius in the next chair; the self is built by describing everyone around her. **Takeaway:** tell a life in someone else's voice and never let the narrator say "I" about herself; the subject appears in the space between the other voices.

## M15. Identity vs Entity (The Little Dog)
Identity is what others remember about you; entity is what you are while doing. From the lecture "What Are Master-Pieces and Why Are There So Few of Them" (delivered at Oxford and Cambridge ca. 1935, published 1936): "The thing one gradually comes to find out is that one has no identity that is when one is in the act of doing anything. Identity is recognition, you know who you are because you and others remember anything about yourself but essentially you are not that when you are doing anything. I am I because my little dog knows me but, creatively speaking the little dog knowing that you are you and your recognising that he knows, that is what destroys creation. That is what makes school. Picasso once remarked I do not care who it is that has or does influence me as long as it is not myself." The companion formulation: "There are so few of them because mostly people live in identity and memory that is when they think. They know they are they because their little dog knows them, and so they are not an entity but an identity. And being so memory is necessary to make them exist and so they cannot create master-pieces." And: "a master-piece has nothing to do with human nature or with identity, it has to do with the human mind and the entity that is with a thing in itself and not in relation." Also: "If there was no identity no one could be governed." Note: "I am I because my little dog knows me" is a Mother Goose phrase Stein reused, not her coinage: it opens "Identity: A Poem" (1935); in Everybody's Autobiography she writes "I became worried about identity and remembered the mother goose I am I because my little dog knows me and I was not sure but that that only proved the dog was he and not that I am I"; in How to Write (1931), "Saving the Sentence": "What is a sentence for if I am I then my little dog knows me". What it IS: a figure who exists only while acting, and vanishes into a label the moment anyone (including the figure) recognizes her. **Takeaway:** show the figure only while she is doing; the moment she is recognized, named, or remembered, she stops being the work and becomes the label.

## M16. Counting by One and One
Accumulation without addition: the count never totals, it just continues. From "Poetry and Grammar" (a Lecture in America): the natural way to count "is not that one and one make two" but "to go on counting by one and one": "One and one and one and one and one. That is the natural way to go on." The same refusal of the total runs through the long sentence (M10) and the everyone typology (M9): the list is the work, and the list never closes. In the cinema portrait (M4) the frames accumulate without summing to a single still; in the continuous present (M2) the moments accumulate without summing to a timeline. What it IS: a counter that ticks 1, 1, 1, 1... and never reaches 2; a series that grows by adjacency, not by sum. **Takeaway:** add one more of the same thing and never total them; the growing list is the piece, and a sum would kill it.

## M17. The Disappearing Home ("There Is No There There")
A place is listed in loving detail until the list itself proves the place is gone. The sentence is the tail of a long unpunctuated sentence in Everybody's Autobiography (Random House 1937, p. 289). The full passage, quoted identically in Wikipedia and Quote Investigator (Quote Investigator verified with scans of the 1971 Cooper Square reprint, p. 289): "... we went across the bay on a ferry, that had not changed but Goat Island might just as well not have been there, anyway what was the use of my having come from Oakland it was not natural to have come from there yes write about it if I like or anything if I like but not there, there is no there there." Context: on the 1934-35 American lecture tour, Stein took a ferry to Oakland to visit the farm she grew up on, near what is now 13th Avenue and E. 25th Street. The house had been razed; the farmland developed with new housing. She wrote: "Ah Thirteenth Avenue was the same it was shabby and overgrown.... Not of course the house, the house the big house and the big garden and the eucalyptus trees and the rose hedge naturally were not there any longer existing, what was the use..." The line "meant no insult to the old hometown. It was simply an expression of painful nostalgia." Stein lived in Oakland from age six to 17; the family settled in 1881 at a 10-acre farm in rural East Oakland. Note: Wikiquote glosses the line as referring to "the Oakland section of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania" -- that gloss is WRONG; the passage is about Oakland, California, as every other source confirms. Do not repeat the Wikiquote gloss. Mechanically, the sentence works by accumulation-then-erasure: a long list of things that ARE there (ferry, Goat Island, Thirteenth Avenue) collapses into the repeated "there" that cancels itself. The word "there" appears as adverb, as noun, and as empty pointer in the same breath. What it IS: a childhood street drawn faithfully, item by item, until the drawing is of an absence; each repeated "there" points at nothing. **Takeaway:** list the place item by item, faithfully, until the list itself proves it is gone; the last word must cancel the first.

## M18. The Return to Plain Speech
After decades of hermetic experiment, the same author writes in the ordinary talk of ordinary people -- and the experiment is in the listening, not the language. Brewsie and Willie (1946), her last major work, about the American soldiers she met after the liberation: "a striking stylistic departure": after decades of literary cubism she returned to the ordinary speech of ordinary people. "War demanded realism, she said, and so... she returned to the ordinary speech of ordinary people and gave them the task of speaking and re-imagining the world." The trajectory runs from hermetic difficulty toward accessibility and back toward speech: the Toklas book is deliberately readable, the lectures explain the method in plain sentences, and the final book imitates soldiers' talk. The experimental machinery never disappears; it is re-aimed at plain material. What it IS: two soldiers talking; the poetry is in the cadence of their talk, not in any strange word. **Takeaway:** take the strangest machinery you have built and point it at the plainest talk you can hear; the experiment is in the listening.

## Evolution

EARLY (1893-1903, Harvard/Radcliffe and Johns Hopkins): Stein attended Radcliffe College (then the Harvard Annex), 1893-1897, student of William James; with James's supervision she and Leon Mendez Solomons performed experiments on "normal motor automatism" -- subjects distracted by conversation, observed for unconscious movement. She coauthored "Normal Motor Automatism" (published in Psychological Review) and later "Cultivated Motor Automatism: A Study of Character in its Relation to Attention" (1898). James called her his "most brilliant woman student" and encouraged medical school. She enrolled at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine in 1897 (one of 11 women alongside 52 men) and left in her fourth year without finishing, after failing an important course; she later said medical school bored her. In her 1930s letters she rejected the automatic-writing reading of her work: "there can be automatic movements, but not automatic writing. Writing for the normal person is too complicated an activity to be indulged in automatically." In 1934 B. F. Skinner interpreted Tender Buttons as "normal motor automatism" -- a reading Stein explicitly disowned. Her first novel Q.E.D. was completed Saturday 24 October 1903 (about the lesbian affair triangle at Johns Hopkins, suppressed by the author, published 1950 as Things as They Are). She followed Leo to London in 1902 and to Paris in 1903.

MIDDLE (1903-1932, the salon years, the portrait period): arrival in Paris 1903; 27 rue de Fleurus with Leo until the household dissolved in 1913 or 1914 and the collection was divided; the Picasso portrait 1905-06; Three Lives 1905-06/1909; The Making of Americans 1902-1911/1925; the word portraits 1908-1913; first publication in Camera Work, August 1912; Tender Buttons 1912-13/1914; Geography and Plays 1922 (contains "Sacred Emily"); the landscape breakthrough in Provence 1922 (St.-Remy winter; "Lend A Hand Or Four Religions" 1922, her first landscape play, never produced); "If I Told Him" 1923; "Composition as Explanation" 1926; Four Saints libretto 1927-28; the salon's Saturday evenings throughout. The middle period is the experimental core: hermetic works, portraits, landscape plays, lectures theorizing the method.

LATE (1933-1946, the Toklas autobiography, the war years, Everybody's Autobiography, the American lecture tour 1934-35): The Autobiography of Alice B. Toklas 1933 (best-seller, celebrity); the American tour October 1934-May 1935 -- 191 days, 23 states, 37 cities, audiences capped at 500, lectures read from notes with Q and A after; tea with Eleanor Roosevelt at the White House; visit to Charlie Chaplin in Beverly Hills; interview in Texas by student reporter Walter Cronkite; return to the Oakland childhood home ("there is no there there"); Random House commitment to publish all future work. Lectures in America (1935) collects the lectures, including "Portraits and Repetition", "Poetry and Grammar", "Plays", "The Gradual Making of the Making of Americans". The Geographical History of America (1936) and "What Are Master-Pieces" (1936) theorize human nature vs the human mind, identity vs entity. Picasso (1938), book-length study. The World is Round (1939, children's book), Ida (1941, "an enchanting exercise in pure verbal invention"). Paris France (1940). Wars I Have Seen (1945) -- memoir of the war years in Bilignin/Culoz under Vichy; Stein likened the German army to Keystone Cops. Brewsie and Willie (1946, published the year of her death). Her last words, reported by Toklas: the famous version has her ask "What is the answer?" and answer herself "Then, there is no question!"; Toklas gave two other versions ("In that case, what is the question?" and "What is the question... If there is no question then there is no answer"). What she actually said remains unknown (Janet Malcolm, The New Yorker, June 2005). Died Saturday 27 July 1946, of stomach cancer, aged 72. The Mother of Us All (1947, posthumous), libretto on Susan B. Anthony, music by Thomson, premiered Wednesday 7 May 1947 at Columbia University. Stanzas in Meditation (complete text published posthumously 1946) -- "perhaps Stein's most austere and rigorous experiment in linguistic abstraction."

AFTERLIFE SO FAR: Carl Van Vechten, literary executor, published her unpublished works; Yale University Press issued the posthumous series (Stanzas in Meditation 1956, Patriarchal Poetry 1953, Alphabets and Birthdays 1957, and others). The Library of America canonized her in two volumes, Writings 1903-1932 and Writings 1932-1946 (1998). Her papers are at Yale: the Gertrude Stein and Alice B. Toklas Papers (YCAL MSS 76) and Collection (YCAL MSS 77) at the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, spanning 1837-1961; a further Stein collection (1914-1973) is at the Harry Ransom Center, University of Texas at Austin. Her influence runs through the Language poets, John Ashbery, William H. Gass, and others. The Museum of Modern Art mounted "Four Americans in Paris: The Collections of Gertrude Stein and Her Family" (December 1970 - March 1971), reuniting more than 200 works; "The Steins Collect: Matisse, Picasso, and the Parisian Avant-Garde" reunited about 200 works in 2011-12 (SFMOMA, Grand Palais, Metropolitan Museum of Art). Commemorations: Jo Davidson's 1923 bronze bust (replica in Bryant Park, New York); portrayals by Kathy Bates in Midnight in Paris (2011) and Tracee Chimo in Genius (2018); the opera 27 about Stein and Toklas (premiered Opera Theatre of St. Louis, June 2014); inaugural honoree of San Francisco's Rainbow Honor Walk (2014). The standard biography is by James Mellow, Charmed Circle: Gertrude Stein and Company (1991); other key studies: Richard Bridgman, Janet Hobhouse, Donald Sutherland's Gertrude Stein: A Biography of Her Work (1951), Barbara Will's Unlikely Collaboration (2011/2013), Francesca Wade's Gertrude Stein: An Afterlife (2025), and Roy Morris Jr.'s Gertrude Stein Has Arrived (2019) on the lecture tour.

## Aesthetic Logic

Stein's aesthetic is an aesthetic of equality carried into language. Cezanne taught her that in composition one thing was as important as another thing, and she built a prose in which every element of the text matters as much as any other, with no figure/ground hierarchy, no center, and no plot to rank the parts. The sentence is the unit of composition the way the plane is the unit of a Cubist canvas: it is placed, repeated with a changed emphasis, and placed again, until the accumulation itself is the portrait. Time is refused twice over: the continuous present removes the timeline, and insistence removes the copy -- nothing happens twice in the same way, so nothing can be filed away as "before" or "after". The reader is not told a story but put inside a room: the object on the table, the saint mending a net, the portrait that flickers through near-identical frames. And the whole system is held together by a single ethical demand, stated in "What Are Master-Pieces": identity is what others remember about you, and it destroys creation; entity is what you are while doing, and it is the only thing a work can be. The late turn to plain speech proves the point: the machinery was never the difficulty of the words, it was the discipline of the listening. A piece built on Stein fails when it decorates (adds a rose, adds a salon, adds a French accent) and succeeds when it insists: one thing, said again, with one thing changed, until the thing is present.

C1. The rose is four, not three. "Rose is a rose is a rose is a rose." (Sacred Emily, written 1913, in Geography and Plays, 1922). The shortened "A rose is a rose is a rose" is the popular form. Never present the three-rose form as the poem's wording.

C2. "A rose is a rose is an onion" is Hemingway, not Stein. For Whom the Bell Tolls (1940), Robert Jordan to Agustin (p. 289/294): "A rose is a rose is an onion... An onion is an onion is an onion... a stone is a stein is a rock is a boulder is a pebble." It is a parody of Stein's line, and Hemingway parodied her style from early works ("Mr. and Mrs. Elliot") through A Moveable Feast, and "overtly disparaged Stein" in Green Hills of Africa and A Moveable Feast.

C3. "There is no there there" is about Oakland, California, not Pittsburgh. The passage is about the razed Oakland farm Stein grew up on, near 13th Avenue and E. 25th Street. Wikiquote's gloss ("the Oakland section of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania") is WRONG; do not repeat it.

C4. "Stein taught me to cut" is paraphrase, not a Hemingway verbatim quote. No source in any leg has Hemingway saying it in those words. The evidenced counsel: "Cut out words. Cut everything out, except what you saw, what happened" (reported, not verbatim-verified); Stein told him his work had "too much decoration, too much description" and to compress and concentrate; "from Stein he also learned to omit part of a story to make it stronger" (secondary). Present it as paraphrase/reported counsel, never as a Hemingway verbatim quote.

C5. The "garage owner in Belley" link is UNVERIFIED. The sources place the garage incident after Stein and Hemingway returned from Canada, while they were living at rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs; Belley is where Stein sheltered in World War II. No source read in any leg links the garage to Belley. Do not assert it.

C6. The "bitch" remark is 1935, not 1926, and the break was not a single 1926 event. The remark is from Green Hills of Africa (1935) and Hemingway's 7 September 1935 letter to Maxwell Perkins: "If anyone was ever a bitch that woman was a bitch... What would you like me to put in place of bitch? Fat bitch? Lousy bitch? Old Bitch? Lesbian Bitch?" The cooling in the mid-1920s, the 1933 public rupture (the Autobiography) and the 1935 counter-attack are separate stages; do not compress them into a single 1926 break date. A podcast claim that Hemingway "wanted a sexual relationship with Gertrude Stein [and] Alice... got Stein to cut him out" is a podcast claim only, not verified in print sources read in any leg.

C7. Stein's exact last words are UNKNOWN. Toklas gave three versions (What Is Remembered, 1963; letter to Van Vechten): the famous version, "What is the answer?" answered by herself, "Then, there is no question!"; "In that case, what is the question?"; "What is the question... If there is no question then there is no answer." Janet Malcolm (The New Yorker, June 2005): "What Stein actually said remains unknown." Record all three versions; flag exact wording UNKNOWN.

C8. "I am I because my little dog knows me" is a Mother Goose phrase, not Stein's coinage. It opens "Identity: A Poem" (1935); in Everybody's Autobiography she writes "I became worried about identity and remembered the mother goose I am I because my little dog knows me and I was not sure but that that only proved the dog was he and not that I am I".

C9. "The trouble with Oakland is that there's no there THERE" is Herb Caen (1959), not Stein.

C10. "Looking vs seeing" is NOT a Stein formulation found in any source read in any leg. What IS documented is her claim that she wrote the way Cezanne and Picasso painted -- by putting every element on the same plane, by repetition-as-structure, by refusing hierarchy -- and the equality-of-the-field passage (M8). Do not present "looking vs seeing" as her words.

C11. "Let's" as operatic language in Four Saints was NOT verified in any source read in any leg. Do not assert it. What IS verified is the landscape structure, the tableaux, and the quoted lines in M12 and M13.

C12. The third story of Three Lives is "The Gentle Lena", not "Bertha". One low-quality search result (vocal.media) names it "Bertha"; that is WRONG, confirmed by Wikipedia and thefreelibrary.com. Do not repeat "Bertha".

C13. Four Saints premiere: Wednesday 7 February 1934 is the better attested date (opera article, Nonesuch notes); one academic article gives Thursday 8 February 1934. The libretto date also conflicts: 1927 (opera article, SFMOMA, Thomson sources) vs 1929 (Wikipedia published-works list). Report both; the 1927/7 February dates are the better attested.

C14. The Making of Americans dates: Stein's 1906-08 vs her biographers' evidence of 1902-1911. Report both; do not pick one silently.

C15. Leo's departure from 27 rue de Fleurus: 1913 (Met, Apollo) vs April 1914 (Wikipedia). The collection division is described both ways. Report both.

C16. Number of children: "last of seven children" (current Wikipedia) vs "youngest of a family of five children" (older version and mirrors). Four surviving siblings are named (Michael b. 1865, Simon b. 1868, Bertha b. 1870, Leo b. 1872); the two extra children in the seven count are unnamed in sources read. Report both figures with the conflict flagged.

C19. Move to 5 rue Christine: 1937 (Met essay) vs 1938 (Wikipedia). Report both; the move happened in 1937 or 1938.

C20. Life magazine Berchtesgaden photo: August 6, 1945 issue (Wikipedia article text) vs April 6, 1945 issue (older Wikipedia version). Unresolved.

C21. Death date: 27 July 1946 (nearly all sources) vs 29 July 1946 (one old Wikipedia mirror). The 27th is the attested date.
