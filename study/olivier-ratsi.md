# Olivier Ratsi: Study Notes

**Site:** Olivier Ratsi (b. Paris, 1972), French visual artist, lives and works in Montreuil, just outside Paris; co-founder of the visual label AntiVJ (2007 to 2016, officially) with Yannick Jacquet, Joanie Lemercier and Romain Tardy; since 2013 the author of the Echolyse series, a long investigation of anamorphosis -- geometric structures projected into architectural sites so that an immaterial three-dimensional space appears, but only from one exact point of view. Drawing since childhood; graphic and plastic arts studies (no institution named in any source read); VJ and audiovisual performer in the rave and electro scene from the late 1990s (Emovie); photographic deconstruction (WYSI*not*WYG / Anarchitecture, from 2005; New York Times Style Magazine commission, 2008); building-scale mapping with AntiVJ; since 2015 a turn from projection toward tangible light structures -- tubes, frames, mirrors, fog, water -- and since 2020 permanent public commissions in which light becomes part of the city's infrastructure. In his own three words: "Perception, Perspective, Deconstruction."

## The life: a VJ who learned to take a building apart

Ratsi was born in Paris in 1972 -- on the evidence of two Ville de Paris pages; the French Wikipedia article gives no birth date or place at all, so the dating is thinly attested and is carried as such (C2901). His own account of his training, from the Vanishing Points catalogue interview: "Je dessine depuis mon plus jeune age, j'ai suivi des etudes d'arts graphiques et plastiques, ma pratique a toujours porte sur la perception de l'espace et du temps, d'abord a travers la photographie, puis par le biais de l'image animee lors de performances audiovisuelles et vjing." No school is named and no dates are given in any source read; do not name an institution, and do not call him self-taught either -- the sources simply do not say (C2905). The arc is: drawing from childhood, graphic and plastic arts studies, photography first, then the moving image and VJing. A childhood root is attested only at snippet level, from the French educational book L'art en jeu, and is quoted here as a snippet, not as a verified verbatim source (C2918): as a child he amused himself by modelling the perception he had of the world -- a waterfall or a car's headlights appearing two ways at once, frozen by following their course with the eyes, or blurred and continuous by fixing a point on their trajectory -- and "les exercices pratiques de perception ne m'ont jamais quitte, ils sont pour moi une forme d'hygiene."

He became interested in images in the late 1990s, when he founded Emovie, a collective that made audiovisual performances, clips and VJing for the rave and electro scene. No Emovie dates, members or venues appear in the sources read; do not name other members (C3008). From the 2000s he began "une reflexion autour de la notion de deconstruction spatio-temporelle", which landed in 2005 in the photographic project WYSI*not*WYG (What You See Is NOT What You Get), with the Anarchitecture series -- buildings and urban landscapes photographed in Korea, Japan, Tunisia and France, then destructured with digital tools, with no colour modification, and in part posted in the street as subvertising. The series was commissioned by the New York Times Style Magazine in 2008, and an Anarchitecture photograph was shown at MAAT Lisbon in 2019 in "Fiction and Fabrication. Photography of Architecture After the Digital Turn". The mechanism is the seed of everything after: take a real space, break its perspective rules, put it back together wrong -- first on paper, later in the room itself.

AntiVJ is the hinge, and its founding year is the record's first fault line -- and this time it is a three-way spread, not a two-way one. The label's own site says it "was originally created by Yannick Jacquet, Joanie Lemercier, Olivier Ratsi and Romain Tardy around 2006". Every Ratsi-specific source says 2007: the French Wikipedia ("En 2007, il co-fonde le label visuel AntiVJ"), Chroniques, Signal Festival, STIR, Hopscotch. Study #603 resolved Tardy's year as 2008, on his own words plus Mapping Festival. This study uses 2007 for Ratsi, with the label site's "around 2006" as the informal pre-history -- and does not silently harmonise the three dates (C2903, C3002). What the sources agree on: the four came out of VJing and set out to move the image off screens and onto architecture, and to question the simple mixing of images, giving their performances "a more reflective dimension, in accord with the architecture of the places". The label worked as a label, not a collective -- solo projects, collective projects and external commissions, like a music label. Ratsi was a founder-artist, not a director: the label was co-directed by Lemercier and producer Nicolas Boritch until 2010, then managed by Boritch alone. The "first mapping performances" claim that follows the label around -- Chroniques says AntiVJ initiated "the very first audiovisual performances using the mapping technique" -- is the label's own framing, repeated by its chroniclers; it is carried here as an attributed claim, not as established historical priority (C2914).

His departure is the second fault line, and this run settles it the other way from Tardy's. "En 2016, Olivier Ratsi quitte officiellement le label AntiVJ" (French Wikipedia); "He officially leaves the collective in 2016 to devote more time to personal projects" (Chroniques). Two sources agree, and it is the first dated departure for a founder (C3001). Tardy left at the end of 2013; Lemercier's last label project was 2010. Ratsi stayed on roughly three more years than Tardy, and the Echolyse series -- from 2013 -- was developed while he was still inside the label. There was no clean 2013 break for the whole label, and the study should not present one (C2904).

After several years in Paris he lives and works in Montreuil, just outside Paris -- the 2019-and-later sources converge on Montreuil (Chroniques, the Vanishing Points catalogue, the tunnel Berthier page), while older sources and the 2024 Bigaignon text still say Paris. No move date is given in any source read, so none is assigned here (C2902, C3003).

The solo practice after AntiVJ keeps the mapping and then leaves it behind. The arc runs in phases: late 1990s-2004, VJing and audiovisual performance in the rave and electro scene (Emovie); 2005-2007, photographic deconstruction, WYSI*not*WYG / Anarchitecture, NYT commission 2008; 2007-2016, AntiVJ, building-scale mapping performances in Asia, North America and Europe; 2012-2013, the turn to in situ installation, Echolyse starting in 2013 with Onion Skin, premiered at Proyecta Oaxaca in April 2013; from 2015, a formal turn away from video projection toward tangible structures and sculpture -- RCTNGL at Galerie Denise Rene in May 2015, Perspicere at Galerie Charlot in October 2015, then Shape and Frame Perspective; 2019-2021, institutional scale -- Frame Perspective acquired by the Region Grand Est (June 2019), IIII acquired by the Centre culturel canadien de Paris (March 2020), the Vanishing Points solo in Marseille (December 2019-March 2020, 500 square metres), L'Oeil's December 2020 list of the "50 artists of the 21st century who have shaped the French art scene", and the Gaite Lyrique retrospective solo, "Heureux soient les feles, car ils laisseront passer la lumiere" (19 May-31 October 2021, eleven works); 2022-2024, public commissions and gallery representation -- Spectrum in the tunnel Berthier, Paris (installed from 13 February 2023), Eppur Si Muove at the Friche Babcock, La Courneuve (June-November 2023), the Echolyse solo at Galerie de Rohan, Landerneau (December 2023-January 2024), joining Galerie Bigaignon early in 2024, La Chute d'Icare solo at Bigaignon (July-October 2024); 2025-2026, Rome (Frame Destruct, September 2025-March 2026), Nuit Blanche 2025, Into The Light at the Grande Halle de la Villette (Negative Space, April-August 2025), Fete des Lumieres Lyon (Onion Skin, December 2025), Bassins des Lumieres Bordeaux (L'Echappee des lignes, October-November 2026). Recognition in the sources read is institutional rather than prized: the L'Oeil list, the NYT commission, the two acquisitions, Art Press 2's November 2015 special digital-arts issue illustrated by an Onion Skin image. No awards or prizes appear in any source read (C2920) -- absence noted, not a claim that none exist. Festivals and venues in the sources read span France, Mexico, Brazil, the USA, Canada, the UK, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Slovenia, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Portugal, Spain, Italy, Switzerland, Romania, Lithuania, China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, Kazakhstan and Australia; no single "countries count" is given in any source read, so none is invented.

## The program, in his own words

Three words first: "En trois mots : Perception, Perspective, Deconstruction." Then the standard bio, repeated verbatim across his site, press kits and the Ville de Paris pages: "Le travail d'Olivier Ratsi pose la realite objective, le temps, l'espace et la matiere comme autant de notions d'information intangibles. Se basant sur l'experience de la realite et de ses representations, ainsi que sur la perception de l'espace, il concoit des uvres qui amenent le spectateur a se questionner sur sa propre interpretation du reel."

Light is at once the subject and the means: "La lumiere est a la fois le sujet de mon uvre et un moyen de creation car la lumiere transporte les informations qui nous permettent d'avoir acces a notre environnement. Le numerique est de mon point de vue un outil qui permet de developper mon travail, en utilisant les proprietes de la lumiere de maniere differente. Je ne me suis donc pas dirige vers les arts numeriques, simplement l'outil que j'utilise le plus pour exprimer mes idees est en lien avec la technologie numerique." The correction matters: he refuses the "digital artist" label by redefinition -- digital is the tool, light is the subject. This study does not call him a digital artist without that qualifier.

Architecture is both support and subject: "L'architecture delimite l'espace et cree des frontieres, des obstacles, des formes, des volumes alors qu'a la base, l'espace est infini ; interroger l'espace ne peut donc pas se faire en ignorant l'aspect architectural. De ce fait dans mon travail, j'essaye de creer un dialogue entre l'espace architectural, l'uvre et le spectateur." The dialogue is three-way -- architecture, work, spectator -- and the spectator is a term of the work, not its audience.

The constraint became the foundation: "En projetant sur des surfaces macroscopiques, pour simuler un volume virtuel sur une surface reelle, il faut que les spectateurs soient places idealement dans une zone ou un endroit specifique pour que l'illusion puisse fonctionner parfaitement. Ce qui fut une contrainte pendant les quelques annees ou j'ai pratique la projection mapping sur batiment, a partir de 2007, est finalement devenu le socle de mon travail." What mapping forced on him -- one exact spot where the illusion works -- is now the whole method. From his own Echolyse statement: "The fictitious viewpoint of the artist thus superimposes itself onto that of the viewer, cleverly pushing him or her to become a pedestal for the work, without which it couldn't exist. The surprise effect of anamorphosis tests the viewer's ability to deconstruct/reconstruct what he or she sees in order to better ask: 'What do you really see? Does it match with what you think you see?'"

He lists three ways to experience a work: free movement, living it as an abstraction; standing on the anamorphic viewpoint, superimposing oneself on his viewpoint and imagining the precise geometrical rules; and the surprise effect that tests deconstruction and reconstruction. And the works are a catalyst, not an emotion machine: "Operant une cassure dans la realite objective, ses creations ne se veulent pas tant un declencheur d'emotions ou un moyen de perturber les sens qu'un catalyseur de points de vues, de references culturelles et psychologiques. Ainsi, le spectateur n'est nullement prive de sa capacite subjective de reconstruction/reconstitution de la realite et est invite a prendre position afin de vivre l'uvre en fonction de son propre ressenti." The viewer is never deprived of the capacity to rebuild reality; the work only asks him to take a position. "L'illusion, en s'opposant a l'experience de la realite, a la capacite d'interroger directement le reel. Je l'utilise pour mettre en uvre des dispositifs de deconstruction des reperes spatio-temporels."

Two late glosses complete the program. On design: "Pour moi le design n'est pas une fin en soi, mais un imperatif sur le plan esthetique. Il contribue a rendre les choses plus fluides, afin que le dialogue se fasse 'sans obstacle', et que la lumiere apparaisse comme si elle n'existait sans aucun autre support." On immersion, and the penetrable viewpoint: "Concernant le cote immersif, j'aime multiplier les points de vue dans mon travail. L'anamorphose s'observe en general a l'exterieur de l'uvre, mais je cherche toujours a ce que le spectateur puisse la penetrer afin de multiplier les possibilites de point de vue, un peu comme dans les Penetrables de Jesus-Rafael Soto." The Soto reference is his own. The exhibition path is choreographed the same way: light, sound and slow movement plunge the spectator into a phase of appeasement, reaching its peak at the end of the show with silent and magisterial works, and the placement of the works, the presentation constraints and the physical constraints of the space are all thought so as to create a path between them.

The references he names, or that are named in his presence: Quattrocento perspective and Renaissance anamorphosis; anamorphosis's first artistic traces in Ming China, 14th century (Chroniques); Newton and the prism (Spectrum); Marey and Muybridge chronophotography (Frame Destruct); Jesus-Rafael Soto's Penetrables; Michel Audiard, via the Gaite Lyrique title; Galileo's "Eppur si muove" (Babcock). The Rohan press kit names Georges Rousse and Felice Varini as artists who share his questions about the perception of space -- shared questions, not influences; they are not presented here as his masters.

## The turn: from the photograph to the room, 2005-2013

The early manner is flat and photographic. Discrete Time (research 2005-2007) is digital chronophotography: moving elements of a scene decomposed across planes while fixed elements are kept faithful, so a single still image holds a plurality of moments. Anarchitecture (from 2005) cuts cities apart and reassembles them. Once Upon a Time the Presidents (date unpinned, C2962) glitches the official portraits of past and present US Presidents -- the deconstruction idea applied to the image of power rather than to architecture. The method is established before the medium is: take a built thing apart, put it back together wrong.

The Echolyse years, 2013-2018, make the anamorphosis the whole practice. Onion Skin (2013) states the thesis: two walls at right angles, anamorphic projection, rectangles and triangles -- "peelings" -- projected so that they appear to peel off the walls like onion layers. The piece runs in chapters: first the geometry of the module is revealed, then the geometry of the room, then a third perspective -- two interlocking triangles sampled from the anamorphic point of view. The inspected images show it: two black walls at night, the left carrying nested white rectangles with thin red accents receding into a tunnel, the right mostly black with thin vertical white lines, red-lit tree branches above; and outdoors, two walls meeting at a corner, dense red-and-white horizontal lines forming twin tunnels, a silhouetted man standing at the corner looking in. Duration is contested in the sources -- 14 minutes in 4 parts in the older sources, about 10 minutes with a 15-minute loop on the current site -- and no video was watched to settle it (C2953). DELTA (2014) moves the device into the corner: anamorphosis straddling several walls, a moving tunnel emerging from the form's gravity point, the sound a Shepard tone by Thomas Vaquie -- an infinite ascent. The inspected images show a dark room washed in saturated red, a tall red rectangle at left, skewed red quadrilaterals on the back walls, a red triangle of light on the floor; and DELTA #14 at Marseille, a huge red triangular form projected across a room corner and the floor, nested white outlines forming a tunnel vanishing point. RCTNGL (2015) miniaturises the device into a wall-mounted object: wood-fibre right-angle mounts with a generative projection, a rectangle appearing only from one viewpoint. Perspicere (2015) takes it to its purest form: three 20 cm cubes -- one polycarbonate, one wood, one wood and polycarbonate -- materialising the Quattrocento visual pyramid and the vanishing point. Plane (2015-2016) turns the anamorphosis outward: a white plane form projected by anamorphosis across the architecture of a gallery window, the point of view placed outside the gallery, so the work is seen from the street, on a 1'18" loop. Shape (2015-2020) floats it in the air: thirteen aluminium composite panels hung on nylon threads, a marking on the floor indicating the point of view from which the scattered panels recompose into a single rectangle. Pele-Mele (2016) doubles it: two transparent right-angle modules, vertical and horizontal peelings projected from one module to the other -- a wormhole, a space-time tunnel -- with the viewer free to move around the space and standing between the two modules. XYZ (2016-2021) draws it as pure line: black-and-white lines first follow the breaks of the architecture, then gradually recompose into a rectangle that seems to float in space -- but only from one viewpoint; the title is the Cartesian coordinates, the work a drawing in three dimensions. Fragmented Plane (2016) paints it on a 35-metre wall in Morelia: paint plus projection plus sound, the wall virtually reproduced, deconstructed and painted in 3D from a predefined vanishing point. Frame Perspective (2018) builds it as a walk-through tunnel: a series of frames placed one behind the other, each incorporating a light system, light travelling through the frames as waves -- a breathing effect -- with viewers walking between them. The palette through all of it is red, white and black, the sound is mostly Vaquie, and the viewer is always standing at one exact point.

The walk-in light years, 2019-2021, let the projection give way to built light objects you walk through, and colour arrives. Spectrum (2019) is the break: a site-specific light installation of coloured tubes in a ruined castle at the Electric Castle Festival in Romania -- the premiere is inferred from a support credit and a listing, not stated outright on his site (C2966) -- and the inspected images show a stone tunnel in which nested rectangular frames of glowing tubes in spectrum colours recede into the distance, a man standing inside; a ruined castle interior with diagonal coloured tubes suspended among steel beams; a white corner with nine parallel diagonal light lines in spectrum order crossing it, each line bending at the wall and floor joints. Everything before Spectrum is red, white and black; from here the full spectrum enters, and it never leaves. In Parallel (2019-2020) puts six acrylic tubes, LEDs, cables and a water basin in a room: water as mirror, the suspended light reading as parallel in space from the point of view while the reflection in the water shows a different image of reality. IIII (delivered January 2020) is the first permanent commission: a kinetic installation at the entrance of the Canadian Cultural Centre in Paris, red angular LED lines zigzagging across the four tall entrance bays, redrawing the four openings in light. Negative Space (2020) is the opposite pole: 21 elements in a dark, fog-filled chamber, intermittently lit by glowing white tubes that simulate an infinite architecture, retinal persistence, the visitor passing through luminous doorways that are not there -- the inspected image shows a fog-filled dark chamber, tall white vertical light slits and doorways receding into the haze, three visitors as silhouettes feeling their way through. F(lux) (2020) is Spectrum made into a freestanding object: fourteen elements, long diagonal glowing tubes suspended from a ceiling rig, graded red to orange to green to blue to violet across the room, their reflections on the dark floor. X (2020) is In Parallel at corridor scale and in full red: 24 acrylic tubes above a 17-metre black water channel, the tubes reflected in the water as a dense criss-cross of thin red lines receding into the dark -- the reflection multiplies two tubes into a whole architecture. Plus (2020) and Infinite I (2020) complete the Gaite Lyrique five: Plus is screens and LED, 6.7 by 6.7 metres, with no further description found in the sources read; Infinite I is 24 Dibond double-sided mirror elements, the mirror itself as the material, the reflection as the regress. The Gaite Lyrique show of 2021 gathers all five of the new light sculptures into one exhibition, on a chromatic and thematic path through five cinematographic sequences.

The permanent years, 2020-2024, bring the commissions that stay, and the fall becomes the late theme. The Berthier tunnel (installed from 13 February 2023) is Spectrum as infrastructure: 44 coloured panels above the sidewalks of a Paris tunnel, an homage to Newton's prism, the north corridor running red to violet and the south violet to red, so the pedestrian walks through the spectrum in one direction and back through it in the other. L'Onde Traversante (delivered 2023, Montreuil) is about thirty luminous tubes forming a fluid wave across a hall. Serpentine (delivered September 2024, Maison Madeleine, Place de la Madeleine, Paris) is the largest single permanent piece: a 60-metre light sculpture, an homage to the late Leonardo da Vinci, one flow rising, one descending. And the fall: La Chute d'Icare (prototype 2023, finished version July-October 2024) suspends sixty coloured tubes five metres up and lets some of them lie on the floor -- the inspected image shows a white gallery in which forty to sixty coloured tubes hang suspended at angles from ceiling cables while many more lie on the dark floor in a scattered burst, cables tangled: the fall itself, frozen and collapsed at once. It is the second of a three-part series on the stages of the fall -- before, during, after. Frame Destruct (2023-2024) freezes a square's implosion in eight moments, generated at regular intervals by a 3D simulation -- chronophotography after Marey and Muybridge, but as a sculpture: walking around it reveals the successive stages of the collapse. The inspected images show a dark red room, dozens of thin red light-tubes suspended at angles, arranged left to right from an intact rectangle through increasingly broken and crossed fragments to scattered shards. The circle closes: Discrete Time decomposed movement into a still image in 2005; Frame Destruct decomposes an implosion into a sculpture in 2023. The arc is from light on buildings to light as building -- and from a rectangle that only exists from one point to a square that falls apart in front of you.

## M1 -- The single viewpoint: the form exists only where you stand

The founding gesture, and the one that never leaves the practice: a form that only resolves -- a rectangle, a tunnel, a plane -- from one exact point in space, and falls apart into fragments from everywhere else. Onion Skin's peelings detach themselves from their backdrop only from the chosen viewpoint, generally placed centrally and at eye level. DELTA's tunnel emerges from the corner only from the gravity point. RCTNGL's rectangle snaps into place only when the viewer walks to the point; the inspected image, photographed from an angle, shows the rectangles distorted and open rather than resolved -- which is the mechanism made visible by its failure. Perspicere's three cubes materialise the visual pyramid itself. XYZ's floating rectangle is a drawing in three dimensions that only exists from the mark. Shape's thirteen floating panels recompose into a single rectangle only from the marking on the floor. The mechanism is a refusal with a method: the image is not in the room, it is in the meeting of the room and one body at one spot. Everything else in the room is the price of that meeting. The viewer is not the audience of the form; the viewer is its last component, its pedestal.

**Takeaway:** a form that only exists from one spot makes the viewer's position the medium; a piece that works from everywhere has given up its strongest constraint.

## M2 -- The peeling: layers behind the visible

Echolyse, in his own statement, is an ensemble of works that deal with the perception of space: "Using various devices, geometric structures are projected into different architectural sites with the aim of simulating immaterial three-dimensional spaces. Depending on the project, the resulting immaterial space can provide a virtual extension or a means of perturbing the existing space. The projected structures come to life, decomposing and breaking into fragments over time. Linear and repetitive, they are known as 'Peelings,' a reference to the outer layers of certain vegetables. Hidden behind the first exterior membrane, the infinite number of layers reflects the idea that behind the visible, real world are other hidden aspects, imperceptible at first glance." The peeling is the motion of the whole series: a form that does not sit still but sheds itself, layer after layer, on the line-of-sight axis. Vaquie's score for Onion Skin is written from exactly that: the shape, speed, appearance, interaction and decomposition of each peeling on the line-of-sight axis contribute to a soundtrack that reinforces the impression of volume and space. The peeling is time made visible as depth: what you see now is the layer that has just come off, and the form underneath is already preparing the next one. Frame Perspective is the peeling built as an object -- the graphic language of Onion Skin as a physical tunnel of frames, light travelling through them as waves. Pele-Mele is the peeling doubled -- two modules facing each other, the layers passing between them like a wormhole.

**Takeaway:** a form that sheds itself is a form with a past and a future in the same frame; the layer is the unit of time, not the second.

## M3 -- The photograph taken apart first: deconstruction before construction

Before he ever projected on a building, Ratsi took buildings apart on paper. Anarchitecture photographs a building and then destructures it digitally, with no colour modification -- the city cut up and reassembled, the fragmentation in the architecture itself and in the framing. Discrete Time decomposes a moving body across planes in a single still. Once Upon a Time the Presidents glitches the portraits of power. The operation is identical in all three, and it is the operation that organises everything after: take a real space, break its perspective rules, put it back together wrong. The difference between the photograph and the installation is only where the wrongness lives: in the photograph it lives in the image; in the room it lives in the viewer's body, which has to stand in the one place where the wrongness resolves into a form. The photographic years are not juvenilia; they are the method stated in its cheapest form, before the medium got expensive. And the method returns at the end of the career as a sculpture: Frame Destruct is Discrete Time with the still image replaced by eight moments of a square imploding, laid out in space instead of on paper.

**Takeaway:** the cheapest form of a method is the truest test of it; if the idea does not survive as a photograph, it will not survive as an installation.

## M4 -- The reflection as a second image: water and mirror against the eye

From 2019 the reflection stops being a trick of the projection and becomes a physical element. In Parallel puts six acrylic tubes over a water basin: the suspended light reads as parallel in space from the point of view, but the reflection in the water shows a different image of reality -- the same tubes, a second geometry, contradicting the first. X scales it to a corridor: two huge glowing red tubes crossed above a 17-metre black water channel, and the reflection multiplies the two tubes into a whole architecture of thin red lines receding into the dark -- the reflection is the larger of the two works. Infinite I uses the mirror itself: 24 Dibond double-sided mirror elements, the reflection as the material, an infinite regress of light with no water and no fog. The mechanism is a second viewpoint that no body can occupy: the water sees the work from below, the mirror sees it from inside, and the viewer, standing at the one point where the first image resolves, is shown a second image that refuses to agree with it. Where M1 says "stand here and it resolves", M4 says "stand here and it resolves twice, differently". The question in the Echolyse statement -- "What do you really see? Does it match with what you think you see?" -- is asked here not by a fragment but by a whole second world, lying flat under the first.

**Takeaway:** a reflection is a viewpoint with no viewer in it; put one in the work and the single viewpoint stops being a fact and becomes a question.

## M5 -- The built light object: projection gives way to structure

The second half of the career is a material turn, dated by the sources to 2015: "Depuis 2015, ses uvres sont marquees par une recherche formelle qui s'emploie a se detacher de la video-projection pour s'orienter davantage sur des structures tangibles ou apparentees a de la sculpture." RCTNGL is the first small-format Echolyse work, a wall-mounted object with a generative projection. Frame Perspective is the peel as a walk-through tunnel of frames, each with its own light system, light travelling through them as waves. Spectrum is coloured tubes hung in a ruined castle. F(lux) is fourteen suspended tubes, a spectrum frozen mid-fall. La Chute d'Icare is sixty tubes, some hanging, some on the floor. The light stops being something thrown at a surface and becomes something built in the air -- tubes, frames, mirrors, cables, water. The design imperative he states -- design not as an end but as an aesthetic imperative, making things fluid so the dialogue happens "without obstacle" and light appears "as if it existed without any other support" -- is the program of this turn: the support must disappear, and the only way to make it disappear is to build it so well that the light reads as unsupported. The anamorphic device does not disappear in this turn; it is joined by a second device, the reflection, and by a third, the object itself. The work is no longer an image on a building; it is a thing in a room that light passes through.

**Takeaway:** when the projection becomes the limit, build the light instead of throwing it; the object is the projection's honest form.

## M6 -- The fog that deletes the room: space that exists only for a moment

Negative Space is the opposite pole to the anamorphic works. Instead of a form that only exists from one point, a space that only exists for a moment, in fog. Twenty-one elements -- aluminium, paint, LED, computer, sound, fog -- in a dark chamber 12 by 4.6 by 4 metres: intermittently lit glowing white tubes simulating an infinite architecture, retinal persistence doing the rest, the visitor passing through luminous doorways that are not there. The inspected image shows it exactly: a fog-filled dark chamber, tall white vertical light slits and doorways receding into the haze, three visitors as silhouettes feeling their way through. The fog is the mechanism: it deletes the room's architecture, so the tubes are the only architecture, and then the light switches off and deletes the tubes too, leaving the visitor with the afterimage on the retina -- the architecture that persists after the light is gone. Where the anamorphic works ask the viewer to find the point, Negative Space asks the viewer to keep walking through a room that keeps unmaking itself. The sound design is custom and the sound designer is unnamed in the only source read -- do not attribute it to Vaquie (C3007). The white-tube language here is the same as the spectrum tubes, drained of colour: the same object, with the colour taken out, becomes a different work entirely -- a room that exists only in the interval between one flash and the next.

**Takeaway:** fog is the cheapest architecture there is; use it to delete the room, and the light that remains is the only building left.

## M7 -- The fall, frozen in stages: chronophotography as sculpture

The late theme is the fall, and its mechanism is chronophotography made physical. Frame Destruct (2023-2024) is eight moments of a square imploding, generated at regular intervals by a 3D simulation -- Marey and Muybridge named in the sources -- laid out as a sculpture 815 by 230 by 215 centimetres: walking around it reveals the successive stages of the collapse, the implosion read in space, not in time. La Chute d'Icare is the same idea as a narrative in three stages -- before, during, after -- sixty coloured tubes suspended five metres up, some still hanging, some already on the floor, the fall frozen and collapsed at once. The colour range happens to coincide with the Olympic rings; the sources note the coincidence and nothing more. The mechanism closes the circle that Discrete Time opened in 2005: then, movement decomposed into a still image; now, an implosion decomposed into a sculpture. The viewer does not watch the fall; the viewer walks along it, from the intact rectangle to the scattered shards, and the walking is the playback. Where the Echolyse peelings decompose over time on a wall, the fall decomposes over space on a floor -- the same operation, rotated ninety degrees, from time into distance.

**Takeaway:** a fall does not need to be watched; lay its stages out in space and the viewer's walk becomes the playback.

## M8 -- The permanent spectrum: light as infrastructure

The commissions that stay are the last mechanism, and the most quietly radical: light stops being an event and becomes part of the building. IIII (2020) is a kinetic installation at the entrance of the Canadian Cultural Centre in Paris, red angular LED lines zigzagging across the four tall entrance bays -- the anamorphic line-work of Plane and XYZ become a building's permanent lighting, seen in passing, from the street. The Berthier tunnel (2023) is 44 coloured panels above the sidewalks, an homage to Newton's prism, the spectrum traversed on foot in both directions -- the pedestrian's walk is the interaction, and the work is used every day by people who never chose to see it. L'Onde Traversante (2023) is about thirty luminous tubes forming a fluid wave across a hall in Montreuil. Serpentine (2024) is a 60-metre light sculpture at the Madeleine, one flow rising, one descending -- the rise and the fall in a single gesture, and the largest single permanent piece in the catalogue. The mechanism is a change of tense: the festival work exists for a weekend and is remembered; the permanent work exists every day and is forgotten into the building, becoming the way the place looks. Spectrum leaves the gallery and becomes infrastructure. The anamorphosis, the peeling, the reflection and the fall all end up here -- not as images, but as the light a city walks through.

**Takeaway:** the strongest test of a light work is whether it can survive becoming ordinary; a piece that still works when nobody is looking at it has become architecture.

## The aesthetic logic: the work is finished by where you stand

The whole practice is one question, asked in eight different materials: what do you really see, and does it match what you think you see? The answer is always positional. In the photographs, the position is the camera's. In the Echolyse works, the position is a marked spot on the floor, and the form exists only there. In the reflection works, the position is split -- the eye sees one geometry, the water sees another. In the fog chamber, the position is a walk through a room that keeps unmaking itself. In the fall works, the position is a walk along eight moments. In the permanent works, the position is the daily commute, and the work is finished by people who never stop to look.

Two palettes carry the arc. Red, white and black from 2005 to 2018 -- the Anarchitecture prints, the Onion Skin tunnels, the DELTA rooms, the RCTNGL rectangles -- a palette of diagrams, of lines on black, of the form as a drawing. Then the spectrum from 2019: the castle, the tunnel, the suspended tubes, the fall -- colour as the content itself, the prism as the subject, Newton's spectrum as the work. The break is not decorative; it is the moment the light stops being a line and becomes a material with a temperature.

Three rules govern the work, and they are the rules a piece built from this study must obey. First, the viewpoint is the work: a form that resolves from everywhere is not a Ratsi form. Second, the spectator is a component, not an audience: the work is unfinished until a body stands in the spot, walks the tunnel, or passes through the fog. Third, the mechanism must be honest about its own support: the projection must admit it is a projection, the tube must admit it is a tube, the fog must admit it is fog -- the light appears "as if it existed without any other support" only when the support has been designed out of the way, not when it has been hidden. A piece that decorates a room with light has missed the point; a piece that makes the room disappear, or makes it resolve, or makes it fall apart, has found it.
