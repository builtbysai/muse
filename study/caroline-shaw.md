# Caroline Shaw: Study Notes

**Site:** Caroline Shaw (b. 1 August 1982, Greenville, North Carolina; living), American composer, violinist and singer; a founding member of the vocal ensemble Roomful of Teeth (formed 2009); the youngest-ever winner of the Pulitzer Prize for Music, 2013, for Partita for 8 Voices; five-time Grammy winner, most recently 2025 for Rectangles and Circumstance, with So Percussion. Her practice is the borrowed container filled with sounds it was not built for: a Baroque dance suite sung in speech and throat song, a Haydn minuet played through bow noise and harmonics, a Beethoven quartet compressed to a floor plan, a garden drawn as a ground bass, a hymnal sung over flower pots -- and since 2020, a band that writes songs together with no one person in charge. [C29279]

The two share the ensemble, the hall, the night, and the method: take something that already exists, a system or a form, and let it speak in a voice it did not know it had. [C29280]

Caroline Adelaide Shaw was born on 1 August 1982 in Greenville, North Carolina. Her mother Jon was a violinist and singer, and began teaching her daughter the violin by the Suzuki method when Shaw was two years old, placing her fingers on the strings. Early influences were the choir of the family's local Episcopal church and the organist there who frequently played Bach; she would phone the local classical station to request pieces and record them on cassette from her Sony boombox. Princeton Alumni Weekly adds a striking family detail, single-source: Shaw is the great-great-granddaughter and niece of Chang and Eng Bunker, the 19th-century conjoined twins. She began writing music as a child -- Wikipedia and AllMusic say at ten, mostly imitating the chamber music of Mozart and Brahms, while Princeton Alumni Weekly says her first string quartet came at nine, in G major, with the F-sharps written out individually because she did not yet know key signatures; the two ages disagree by a year, and "around nine or ten" is the safe form. At fourteen she attended a summer programme at the Kinhaven Music School in Vermont, where the calibre and passion of her fellow students sharpened her interest in composition and performance. She trained first as a violinist, aiming at an orchestral or chamber career, and only later turned formally to composition: Bachelor of Music in violin performance from Rice University in 2004, then a Thomas J. Watson Fellowship in 2004/5, a master's degree in violin at Yale University in 2007, and entry to the PhD programme in composition at Princeton University in 2010. Yale awarded her an honorary doctorate in 2022. Her Princeton PhD completion year is not settled from a primary source this run -- the honor-roll page sits behind a CAS login -- only "entered the PhD programme in 2010" and "Princeton University (PhD)" in Wikipedia's infobox are established. [C29283]

In the summer of 2009, at the Massachusetts Museum of Contemporary Art (MASS MoCA), Shaw joined the first rehearsals of Roomful of Teeth, the eight-voice ensemble founded that year by Brad Wells, with Shaw as a founding member. The group set out to mine the expressive potential of the human voice, studying yodelling, Tuvan throat singing, Inuit throat singing, belting, Korean p'ansori, Georgian singing, Sardinian cantu a tenore, and Hindustani and Persian classical singing with masters of each tradition. When Wells needed more repertoire, he invited the singers to contribute; Shaw, a trained violinist and singer but hardly yet a composer, wrote "Passacaglia" in ten days. Over the following summers she added three more movements -- "Allemande", "Sarabande" and "Courante" -- collectively titled Partita for 8 Voices, a Baroque dance suite in all but sound. The piece is built from speech, whispers, sighs, murmurs, wordless melodies and novel vocal effects; Shaw said it was inspired by Sol LeWitt's Wall Drawing 305 and by "our basic desire to draw a line from one point to another", and some lyrics are LeWitt's own drawing instructions. The four movements were written 2009-2011 (the Pulitzer page dates the work 2009-2012). A recording by Roomful of Teeth, including Shaw, was released by New Amsterdam Records on 30 October 2012 on the group's self-titled debut album; the complete work was not premiered live in full until 4 November 2013 at (Le) Poisson Rouge in New York. In April 2013, at thirty, Shaw won the Pulitzer Prize for Music for the Partita, the youngest person ever to do so, and the debut album won the 2014 Grammy for Best Chamber Music/Small Ensemble Performance. Her Grammy record per Grammy.com, read end to end, is 5 wins and 8 nominations: 2014 (Roomful of Teeth debut album, as a member), 2020 (Orange, Attacca Quartet), 2022 (Narrow Sea), 2024 (Rough Magic, Roomful of Teeth), 2025 (Rectangles and Circumstance, with So Percussion). In October 2019 several performers of katajjaq (Inuit throat singing), including Canadian Inuk throat singer Tanya Tagaq, accused Shaw and Roomful of Teeth of cultural appropriation and exoticism for the perceived uncredited quotation of a katajjaq song in the third movement of the Partita. In a joint public statement, Shaw and artistic director Brad Wells acknowledged they had hired and studied with Inuit singers in 2010 and that techniques learned from those studies had been used in the Partita, while maintaining the patterns were "sufficiently distinct from katajjaq". The ensemble subsequently agreed to credit teachers and coaches more prominently, read a source-acknowledgement statement before performances, and explore other ways to support Indigenous musicians. [C29284]

She performs as violinist with the American Contemporary Music Ensemble (ACME) and as vocalist with Roomful of Teeth, and has sung with the Trinity Wall Street Choir, Alarm Will Sound, the Attacca Quartet, So Percussion and the Mark Morris Dance Group Ensemble, among others. She was musician in residence at Dumbarton Oaks in the fall of 2014 and composer in residence with Music on Main in Vancouver through 2016. With the Attacca Quartet she made Orange (2019), which won the 2020 Grammy, and Evergreen (2022). The twenty works inspected run 2009-2024 and trace one practice moving its container steadily outward while the sound inside it stays bodily and found. Phase 1, inside the ensemble, 2009-2012 (W01-W07): Shaw writes as a player-singer inside the groups she belongs to -- Roomful of Teeth, her own Franklin Quartet, So Percussion. The mechanism is the body and the found object -- extended voice (speech, whisper, throat singing, hocket, vocal fry), found percussion (flower pots, cans, bowls), and the string quartet treated as a timbre laboratory (pitchless bow noise, vertical bowing, harmonics, pizzicato as spray). The old form is the container: Baroque dance suite (Partita), minuet and trio (Entr'acte), Bach chorale (Punctum), Chopin mazurka (Gustave Le Gray), a Baroque continuo line (Boris Kerner), an orange (Valencia). She does not develop themes in the classical sense; she saturates a borrowed frame with new sound, then lets it break. Phase 2, commissioned responses, 2015-2019 (W08-W16): the frame widens to institutions and other composers' works -- Dumbarton Oaks gardens (Plan and Elevation, ground bass plus collage), Buxtehude (To the Hands, quotation plus inversion plus data as text), Beethoven (Blueprint as harmonic reduction; Watermark as imagined composing process), Marilynne Robinson and the 2016 election (Three Essays, speech-lilt as material), the Voyager Golden Record (The Listeners, turntable plus re-harmonised nursery song). The orchestra enters (Lo, Watermark, The Listeners, The Observatory 2019), and with it more precise notation -- except where she keeps freedom (the improvised solo in Lo, aleatoric layers in The Evergreen). The subject turns outward: refugees, displacement data, who listens, who is included. Phase 3, the band, 2020-2024 (W17-W20): Shaw becomes a singer-songwriter inside a fixed band, So Percussion as a five-person group with no single leader. Narrow Sea (2017, released 2021) is the hinge -- a composed song cycle with the band as a sonic universe. Let the Soil (2021) is improvised in the studio in three days; Rectangles and Circumstance (2024) is the same method road-tested, with 19th-century poems as the shared expressive mode and instrumental fragments as the starting point. The mechanism shifts from borrowing a form to co-writing a song: text selection as feedback loop, voice plus percussion as the whole band, synthesizers and found percussion alongside strings. Film and TV scoring runs in parallel (Fleishman Is in Trouble, The Sky Is Everywhere, Leonardo da Vinci), reusing the same collaborator pool -- Attacca, So Percussion, Roomful of Teeth -- as a modular ensemble kit. ## M1 -- The borrowed frame: an old form as container

The signature move of her first decade, and the one the whole study turns on. Partita for 8 Voices (2009-2011) takes four Baroque dance forms -- Allemande, Sarabande, Courante, Passacaglia -- and uses them as a frame that is then stretched and broken: the Allemande opens with overlapping square-dance calls and spoken technical directions from Sol LeWitt's Wall Drawing 305, which congeal into a bright angular tune; the Courante hockets breaths between singers and quotes the American folk hymn "Shining Shore"; the Passacaglia builds spoken babble that breaks down into childlike vowels, then vocal fry, then surges into a D-major chord. The same move repeats across the early quartets: Entr'acte (2011) is a minuet-and-trio form riffed on and taken further, triggered by one moment in Haydn's String Quartet Op. 77 No. 2 -- the spare shift to a D-flat major trio in the minuet, a remote-key jump from F major, which Shaw calls going "to the other side of Alice's looking glass"; Punctum (2009, revised 2013) takes a Baroque chorale model, saturates it into the texture while denying its form, and states the full Bach chorale only in the final pages; Gustave Le Gray (2012) builds a frame around a complete performance of Chopin's Mazurka Op. 17 No. 4; Boris Kerner (2012) takes a Baroque continuo line and lets it lean, tilt and jam. The mechanism is saturation without development: the old form is not developed as a theme, it is filled with sounds it was not built for until it breaks, and the break is the piece. The watchable behaviour is a familiar shape -- a dance, a minuet, a chorale -- arriving in an unfamiliar voice, the frame visible throughout and the sound always slightly too big for it. Seen in W02, W03, W01, W04, W07. [C29287]

**Takeaway:** take an old form, fill it with sounds it was not built for, and let it break. [C29287]

## M2 -- Speech, whisper and breath as pitch material

The Partita's sound world is made of things that are not quite singing. The Pulitzer citation names them: "speech, whispers, sighs, murmurs, wordless melodies and novel vocal effects". The mechanism is the voice treated as a continuum from talking to singing, with the in-between states as the material: spoken technical directions from LeWitt's Wall Drawing 305 become the Allemande's opening calls; spoken babble in the Passacaglia breaks down into childlike vowels and then vocal fry before surging into a D-major chord; the Courante passes short breath sounds rapidly between singers in hocket, so the breath itself is the rhythm. The score instruction quoted in sources makes the looseness explicit: each bar in Passacaglia should last about four seconds, cued by conductor or players, pitch spacing approximate, "don't feel too tethered to it." The watchable behaviour is eight singers visibly deciding, bar by bar, how close to speech and how close to song they are -- the audience hears the negotiation, not just the result. Seen in W02. [C29288]

**Takeaway:** let speech, whisper and breath be the notes, and let the singers decide how close to song they sit. [C29288]

## M3 -- The studied voice: eight traditions in one octet

Roomful of Teeth did not invent its extended techniques; it studied them. The ensemble set out to mine the expressive potential of the human voice by studying yodelling, Tuvan throat singing, Inuit throat singing, belting, Korean p'ansori, Georgian singing, Sardinian cantu a tenore, and Hindustani and Persian classical singing with masters of each tradition. The mechanism is apprenticeship as orchestration: each tradition is a timbre the octet can call on, and a piece like the Partita layers them -- Tuvan throat singing, plainsong, Appalachian-style harmony, speech, whisper, sigh, murmur -- the way an orchestra layers instruments. The watchable behaviour is a singer switching traditions mid-phrase, the same throat producing a belted melody and then a throat-sung drone, the octet sounding larger than eight voices because each voice carries several. The limit is on the record too: the 2019 katajjaq controversy, in which Inuk throat singer Tanya Tagaq and others accused Shaw and the ensemble of uncredited quotation, and the ensemble's response -- crediting teachers and coaches more prominently and reading a source-acknowledgement statement before performances -- is part of the mechanism, not an aside: a borrowed voice has to name where it came from. Seen in W02. [C29289]

**Takeaway:** study the tradition before you borrow its voice, and name the teacher when you sing it. [C29289]

## M4 -- Found objects as pitched material

Flower pots are Shaw's second instrument. Taxidermy (2012), her first piece for So Percussion, is scored for percussion quartet on flower pots, vibraphones and marimba: the pots give a muted, bell-like timbre, and the piece layers abrupt tones from cans and blocks against sustained melodic notes from vibraphone and marimba, with steel pan and a hailstorm of chiming attacks that swell and recede; a simple chord progression played by mallet instruments is elaborated by steel pan, a canon of spoken word follows, then the chord progression returns as coda. The same objects recur across the catalogue: Narrow Sea (2017) uses ceramic bowls, flower pots, tin cans, woodblock, hollow pottery bowls, a light bass drum and poured water; Let the Soil (2021) adds steel drums, tuned flower pots, toys and synthesizers; Rectangles and Circumstance (2024) lists pots and pans, shekere, wood block, maracas, claves and sampler alongside drums, piano, Prophet synthesizer, clavinet, glockenspiel, Mellotron, Moog, tuba and steel drum. The mechanism is the domestic object retuned: a pot is a bell with a garden history, a can is a drum with a kitchen history, and the piece is the sound of the household turned into an ensemble. Sources link Taxidermy's sound world to Javanese gamelan, on a single 2015 New Music USA interview line -- a B-grade link, not an A. The watchable behaviour is the eye recognising the object and the ear refusing to: a flower pot that rings like a bell, a can that keeps time like a drum. Seen in W05, W13, W18, W20. [C29290]

**Takeaway:** take the pot off the shelf, and let it ring like a bell. [C29290]

## M5 -- The piano played like a dulcimer

Narrow Sea (2017) treats the piano as a percussion instrument you climb inside. The documented devices: a piano played like a dulcimer by five people at once -- players inside the piano, two mallets each, as if it were a cimbalom -- in Part 3, the most exploratory of the five; Gilbert Kalish muting piano strings with one hand while playing with the other; So Percussion humming a progression that forms a recurring ground bass; a marimba chord progression as the homecoming backdrop; distant electronic oscillations; and poured water. The mechanism is the keyboard instrument un-keyboarded: the piano's strings are struck, muted and bowed by hand rather than hammered by key, so the instrument's familiar voice is replaced by a struck-string shimmer, while the piano itself stays the grounding force of the piece -- the one stable element while the percussion universe changes around it. All five texts come from The Sacred Harp, the 19th-century shape-note hymnal, and all five refer to water (the river Jordan, swelling flood, narrow sea), images of what lies between this world and the next. The watchable behaviour is five people crowded around one piano, mallets in hand, the instrument opened up and played from the inside while the soprano sings over it. Seen in W13. [C29291]

**Takeaway:** open the piano, climb inside it, and play the strings by hand. [C29291]

## M6 -- Harmonic reduction as floor plan

Blueprint (2016) begins as a harmonic reduction -- a kind of floor plan -- of Beethoven's String Quartet Op. 18 No. 6 in B-flat major, and compresses that four-movement quartet into a single movement in which mood and tempo change quickly, even abruptly, with airy textures, terse motifs separated by plentiful rests, and aggressive pizzicato attacks. The mechanism is compression by reduction: take the older work's harmony, strip it to its load-bearing walls, and rebuild it as a one-room house where the doors between moods are left open, so the listener walks from room to room without corridors. The title also puns on the Aizuri Quartet's name (aizuri-e, blue-ink Japanese woodblock printing) and on an architectural blueprint. The Beethoven source is carried as a conflict, not resolved: Shaw's own program note, the Listeners Club, the Hausmann Quartet note and Caramoor all say Op. 18 No. 6, while Strings Magazine (2019) says Op. 18 No. 4, which Shaw played as a teenager; No. 6 is treated here as correct (composer's own note plus three corroborations) and the No. 4 claim is recorded as the outlier. Seen in W10. [C29292]

**Takeaway:** reduce the old quartet to its floor plan, then build one room where the moods change without warning. [C29292]

## M7 -- The garden as score: plan and elevation

Plan and Elevation: The Grounds of Dumbarton Oaks (2015), commissioned for the gardens' 75th anniversary while Shaw was inaugural Early-Career Musician in Residence there, takes its title from the two standard architectural drawings -- plan (bird's-eye view) and elevation (side view with ornamental detail) -- and each of its five movements is based on a simple ground bass line supporting a different musical concept. The Ellipse is infinite repetition, walking around and around a stone path under hornbeams. The Cutting Garden is fragmentation and collage -- cuttings of Ravel, Mozart K. 387, and Shaw's own Entr'acte, Valencia and Punctum, rearranged like cut flowers for display. The Herbaceous Border is spare and strict at first, like the cold geometry of French formal gardens seen from above, building to chaos, and includes slurring portamento Shaw calls "pre-school fingerpaint". The Orangery is slim fractured shadows, light through leaves, strummed cello chords and chirping high pizzicato. The Beech Tree is sustained harmonies, a towering tree as a single sustained presence. The movements flow attacca into one another. The mechanism is place as structure: the garden's layout is the form, the ground bass is the path, and the quotations are the cuttings brought inside and rearranged. The watchable behaviour is a quartet walking a garden in sound -- the same path, five different views, the quotations arriving like flowers cut and set out for display. Seen in W08. [C29293]

**Takeaway:** draw the garden twice -- once from above, once from the side -- and let the path be the bass line. [C29293]

## M8 -- Quotation and inversion, with data as text

To the Hands (2016), for SATB choir and string quintet, is a response to Buxtehude's Membra Jesu Nostri, and its mechanism is to begin inside the 17th-century sound and then expand, colour and break it. The Prelude turns the Ad manus tune into a wordless plainchant on syllables "ah, oh, oo, nn, mm, na'n'n", punctured by an unsettling string pattern. The second movement fragments Buxtehude's setting of "quid sunt plagae istae in medio manuum tuarum" (what are these wounds in the midst of your hands) and inverts the question to "in the midst of our hands". The third movement, without strings, riffs on Emma Lazarus's "The New Colossus" ("Give me your tired, your poor...") and the statue's beacon-hand -- an open, beckoning hand with no wounds. The fifth movement, Litany of the Displaced, passes harmony from one string instrument to another with only brief overlaps while the choir shouts and spoken-sings Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre 2015 figures by country in ascending order, 224 to 7.6 million (Syria); the double bass is absent in this movement. The sixth unfolds "in caverna" into "ever ever will I hold you", quoting Buxtehude's tender refrain. The mechanism is quotation as argument: the old text is quoted, inverted, and then answered with a number -- the displacement figures are not illustrated by the music, they are spoken inside it, and the choir's shouting makes the statistic audible as a crowd. The watchable behaviour is a choir moving from plainchant to shouting, the strings handing a single harmony from player to player like a relay, the numbers climbing until the music has to hold the largest one. Seen in W12. [C29294]

**Takeaway:** quote the old text, invert its question, and answer it with a number spoken aloud. [C29294]

## M9 -- Language as material: speech-lilt, echo chamber, code

The Three Essays (2016-2018) encode the power of language to communicate and miscommunicate. First Essay: Nimrod (2016) takes unrelated sections of material that imitate the lilt and flow of spoken language -- Shaw's markings include "as if Tilda Swinton was reciting the Gettysburg Address" and "with a Midwest lilt" -- and weaves them together with colourful transitions that pick up where the language-like fragments leave off; the Nimrod title alludes to the builder of the Tower of Babel, and a sprightly tune splits into trap doors, lyrical solos, cresting waves, shimmering repose, and a raw slippery chord that slides through one more trap door at the end. Extended effects smooth the joins: sul ponticello and gradually widening and narrowing vibrato. Shaw composed it while listening to Marilynne Robinson's The Givenness of Things and during the 2016 US presidential election, which she said accounted for the "disintegration of elements". Second Essay: Echo (2018) touches on the echo chamber of social media, the "echo" function in the PHP programming language, and the physical effect of an echo, using call-and-response structure to figure self-perpetuating repetition. Third Essay: Ruby (2018) is named for the Ruby programming language as well as the gemstone, and points to the increasing integration of language and technology, bringing the philosophical and musical elements of the first two essays together. The mechanism is the spoken phrase as a melodic shape: the lilt of a sentence becomes the contour of a line, the echo chamber becomes call-and-response, and the code word becomes a title that is also a material. The bar-level mechanism for Echo and Ruby individually was not found in any accessible source and is not asserted here. The watchable behaviour is a quartet that sounds like it is talking -- phrases with accents, pauses and lilts that belong to speech, not to song -- and then like it is arguing with itself, the same phrase coming back changed. Seen in W11, W14. [C29295]

**Takeaway:** let the sentence's lilt be the melody, and let the echo answer itself. [C29295]

## M10 -- The turntable as structural device

The Listeners (2019), Shaw's first oratorio, takes Carl Sagan's Voyager Golden Record (1977) as its conceit and its instrument. The libretto spans five centuries -- Whitman, William Drummond of Hawthornden, Tennyson, Carl Sagan, Yesenia Montilla, Lucille Clifton -- and interleaves sung poems with spoken-word recordings played on a turntable, including Sagan's own words (Pale Blue Dot) and a UN Secretary-General greeting. The turntable is a structural device, not decoration: curated selections are played as found sound against live period instruments, so the recorded voice and the live voice share the stage as equals. The Prologue and Epilogue are a slowed-down, re-harmonised "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" -- the first song Shaw learned as a child -- framing the piece as naive wondering at the stars. The outer movements are built on the single word "Brillas". Shaw's stated turn in the piece: from gazing outward at who might listen out there, to asking who we are on Earth, who is divided from whom, and who is included in the concert hall. The mechanism is the archive played live: the record is not a sample, it is a performer, and the question the piece asks -- who listens -- is asked by the machine that plays the question. The watchable behaviour is a period orchestra and a turntable on the same stage, the needle dropping while the singers wait, Sagan's voice arriving from 1977 into a room full of live instruments. Seen in W16. [C29296]

**Takeaway:** put the record player on stage, and let the archive perform beside the living. [C29296]

## M11 -- Designed freedom: aleatory layers and the improvised solo

Shaw keeps freedom in the score where the occasion earns it, and writes it in rather than leaving it to chance. The Evergreen (2020), her longest quartet at about 16-21 minutes against her usual 7-8, offers the piece to one particular tree found on a walk in an evergreen forest on Swiikw (Galiano Island), British Columbia, in January 2020 -- towering, craggy, warped and knotted, wrapped in soft green moss. Its first movement, Moss, uses aleatoric notation: pizzicato clusters and harmonics layered so the players feel calm, not fitted to a neat timescale -- Shaw's phrase is "design freedom into the music". Stem stretches and grows through gentle textural layering; Water uses extended pizzicato passages evocative of falling and dripping rain; Root stretches and grows, alternating ponticello playing with strong dynamics and dissonance. The same instinct appears earlier in Lo (2016), her first orchestral work: the score is more precisely notated than the Partita, because orchestral rehearsal time is expensive -- except for a violin solo that Shaw largely improvised at the premiere, which sources describe as the expressive centre of the piece. The mechanism is freedom as a designed layer: the aleatory passage and the improvised solo are not gaps in the score, they are the score's load-bearing element, the place where the players' own judgement is the instrument. The watchable behaviour is a quartet visibly not counting together -- clusters landing when they land, the soloist playing what she decides in the moment -- and the piece holding its shape anyway. Seen in W17, W09. [C29297]

**Takeaway:** write the freedom into the score, and let the players decide when the notes land. [C29297]

## M12 -- The band: no one person in charge

Let the Soil Play Its Simple Part (2021) and Rectangles and Circumstance (2024) are not commissions; they are band records. The group -- Shaw on vocals with So Percussion as a band, co-produced with engineer Jonathan Low -- had three days of gratis studio time at Guilford Sound, Vermont, and developed songs from ideas begun during the Narrow Sea sessions, asking "what would we make in the room together... like a band writes in the studio?" Songs start from a fragment -- a chord, a melody, a percussion idea -- and lyrics are sourced or written collaboratively from James Joyce, the Sacred Harp, an Anne Carson poem, the Book of Ruth, "I'll Fly Away", and ABBA ("Lay All Your Love on Me", stripped to a few chorus lines). Documented devices: Shaw's voice synthetically manipulated against mallet filigree and syncopated drumming ("To the Sky"); duets between Shaw and each member of So Percussion in turn; steel drums, tuned flower pots, toys, synthesizers, electronics; word-setting that follows the words literally in places ("Cast the Bells in Sand": ringing pulses submerge into muted drums); repetition of single words ("ago", "until") to track the sway of time. Rectangles and Circumstance is the same method road-tested: most songs started with instrumental pieces or fragments from Jason Treuting or Eric Cha-Beach, and Shaw then set or wrote lyrics to them; the group sourced a set of 19th-century poems that shaped the expressive mode, using verses by Christina Rossetti, Emily Bronte, Emily Dickinson, Gertrude Stein and William Blake; Adam Sliwinski's account of the method is a feedback loop -- he collects poems, sends them to Shaw, watches what catches her interest, then modifies his search based on her feedback. The mechanism is co-writing as a system: no single author, fragments and poems circulating until a song condenses, the band knowing each other's strengths and tendencies intimately after touring together through the pandemic. The watchable behaviour is five people in a room, each holding a fragment, the song arriving from the exchange rather than from any one of them. Seen in W18, W20. [C29298]

**Takeaway:** give the band three days and a fragment each, and let the song condense from the exchange. [C29298]

## M13 -- The old hymn, new melody

Narrow Sea (2017, album 2021) sets five texts from The Sacred Harp, the 19th-century shape-note hymnal first published in 1844, to entirely new melodies, for soprano Dawn Upshaw, pianist Gilbert Kalish and So Percussion. All five texts refer to water -- the river Jordan, swelling flood, narrow sea -- images of what lies between this world and the next, and share joy in looking to heaven (crossing over, going home). The mechanism is the hymn as a text without a tune: keep the words and their water imagery, discard the received melody, and write a new one over a percussion universe that changes from part to part -- ceramic bowls, humming ground bass, the piano-as-dulcimer, flower pots ringing like bells, poured water -- while the piano stays the grounding force. Part 1 opens with a simple marimba chord progression ("I'm only going over Jordan"); Part 3 is the most exploratory, with the piano-as-dulcimer; Part 4 lets flower pots ring like bells. The piece is dedicated to all humans seeking safe refuge. The watchable behaviour is a familiar hymn text arriving on an unfamiliar melody, the water imagery carried by the percussion rather than the words, the homecoming announced by a marimba chord progression that returns like a shore. Seen in W13. [C29299]

**Takeaway:** keep the hymn's words, throw away its tune, and let the water carry it home. [C29299]

## M14 -- The transplant: the Partita palette inside pop, the ensemble as kit

Shaw's extended-vocal technique travels. In October 2015 Kanye West released a remix of "Say You Will" (opening track of 808s and Heartbreak, 2008), co-produced by Shaw, featuring Shaw's vocals in a style similar to her classical compositions; she also features on "Wolves" and contributed vocals to "Father Stretch My Hands Pt. 2", both from The Life of Pablo (2016), and to a leaked version of "Only One" (February 2016). West heard Shaw at a Democratic fundraiser in May 2015, went backstage, shared new material on his phone, and suggested working together. The mechanism is transplant without translation: the Partita palette -- speech, breath, wordless melody -- is dropped into a hip-hop and R-and-B production as backing vocals, and it works because the palette was never about the concert hall, it was about what a voice can do. She is a featured vocalist and producer here, not the composer of the underlying songs, and must not be credited as such. The same modularity runs the other way in her film scoring: the Leonardo da Vinci score (2024), the first entirely original score for a Ken Burns film, is performed by her long-term collaborators Attacca Quartet, So Percussion and Roomful of Teeth, plus bassist John Patitucci, with documented ensemble combinations -- interweaving Attacca lines over a steady So Percussion pulse ("Lady With an Ermine"), wordless Roomful of Teeth vocals soaring over the other ensembles ("Pleasure and Pain"), Patitucci's bass melded with Attacca ("Notebooks"). The mechanism is the collaborator pool as a modular kit: the same three ensembles recombined in different balances for each cue, the piece assembled from trusted parts rather than written from scratch. Seen in W20-context, Leonardo da Vinci score, Kanye West collaboration. [C29300]

**Takeaway:** carry the voice into the pop song unchanged, and build the score from ensembles you already trust. [C29300]

## Aesthetic logic

Shaw's logic is the container and the friction. Against Southworth, whose work knows it is a measurement and shows you the measurement happening, Shaw's work knows it is a borrowing and shows you the borrowing happening: the dance suite, the minuet, the chorale, the mazurka, the hymn, the garden, the quartet, the pop song -- each one is a form with a history, and the piece is what happens when you fill it with sounds it was not built for and refuse to resolve the mismatch. What changes across fifteen years is not the question but the size of the container and the number of hands on it: first her own body and voice inside the octet she helped found, then other composers' works and institutions' gardens, then a band and a screen, each step handing more of the authorship to the room itself. She refuses the blend twice over: the borrowed form keeps its shape against the new sound, and the collaborators keep their voices against each other -- the string quartet does not absorb the flower pots, the hymn does not absorb the new melody, the band has no leader to absorb the others. The meaning is the making, and the making is shared: the LeWitt instructions spoken aloud, the teacher named before the throat song, the displacement figures shouted by the choir, the poems passed back and forth until a song condenses -- because the work is only honest about what it cost when the cost is on view, and only true when the ear can hear the join between the form and its sound. [C29301]
