# Fernando de Szyszlo: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-04. ig-artist-study #334.

**Site:** Fernando de Szyszlo Valdelomar (Lima, 5 July 1925 - Lima, 9 October 2017, age 92), Peruvian painter, sculptor, printmaker and teacher; the man called the father of Peru's abstract art movement and the key figure in advancing abstraction across Latin America from the mid-1950s. Over 100 solo exhibitions; Sao Paulo and Venice biennials; collections at MoMA, the Guggenheim, the Museum of Fine Arts Houston, the Art Museum of the Americas, MALI, and the Blanton. Identity anchors: the imported fire (abstraction given a local accent); the Quechua title as address; the pigment-loaded surface; the emblematic form that cannot be read literally; the scarlet-and-black elegy; the series as excavation; the manifesto as polemic; the Paris conversion; the totemic chamber; the seacoast turn; the definition as engine (painting as the visible encounter of the sacred with the material); the dated political break.

**Doorway:** Hop from the cesar-moro study: from the Peruvian surrealist poet to the Peruvian abstractionist who sat across the dinner table from his memory. Szyszlo's 1970 remark, carried in #333's research, is the hinge: Moro "just stood still and contemplated, unlike the usual chatterboxes in Lima." #333 closed with the exile's double-entry bookkeeping, every move made twice in two languages, never reconciled; #334 opens with the man who inherited the Peruvian side of that table and answered the exile's question in the opposite direction. Where Moro took a French engine into a Spanish life, Szyszlo took a French engine (Hartung's gestural abstraction, Breton's surrealism, met in person at the Cafe de Flore) and made it carry Andean weight. The doorway is literal: Szyszlo's 1963 Apu Inca Atawallpaman series drew its critical reaction from Emilio Adolfo Westphalen, Moro's own closest collaborator, who wrote that what exalted Szyszlo was "not so much the reference to that event as the attitude of the poet before the enigma of death." The same verdict the surrealists gave Moro's poems, handed to the abstractionist's paintings twenty years later, by the same hand.

**Depth:** Eighteen sources read end to end: EN Wikipedia, five ICAA/MFAH document synopses (the 1947 first-show file, the Raygada 1949 review file, the 1951 polemic file, the "polemica de los pintores" file, the Apu Inca 1963 catalog record, the Marta Traba Bogota text), the Google Arts and Culture Puka Wamani page with its full curatorial text in Spanish and English, the Christie's Paclla Pampa lot essay by Abby McEwen (with the 1985 Americas interview quote, the Westphalen quote via Dore Ashton, and two Ashton quotes), the two-part livinginperu interview (his definition of painting; the Camino a Mendieta studio visit; the Inkarri retrospective note; the studio description), the encyclopedia.com entry, the SMU 2023 thesis abstract, the Quart 2024 article passages (the Alvaro de Medina interview distinction; the ArtNexus 1994 Gomez Sicre/Traba quote; the "red hawk" gloss), the johnmoran Estudio No. 3 lot notes, and the artnet, IMMA, Britannica, artsper, prabook, 1stdibs and wikioo biographies. Works inspected: about 20, all via catalog, museum, and auction descriptions (Puka Wamani 1968; Paclla Pampa 1968; Inkarri 1968 at the Blanton; the thirteen Apu Inca Atawallpaman canvases and gouaches via the 1963 catalog record; Mar de Lurin 1989; the Camino a Mendieta etching 2003 and the oil in progress in 2015; Sol Negro 1995 and 2008; the Duino series 1991-94; La Habitacion No. 23 1997; Piedra del Sol 1997; the Intihuatana sculpture; Imago 1971; Estudio No. 3 1974; and the artnet titles Masi Masi 1991, Visitante 1987, Yurak 1986, Mask, Lost City II 1987, Sin titulo 1986), plus the four early shows via contemporary reviews (ICPN 1947, Eielson joint 1948, Galeria de Lima 1949, Sociedad de Arquitectos 1951). NOT accessible this run: no painting viewed as a full-resolution image first-hand (the GAC page carried no usable image URL in text mode); Dore Ashton's 2003 monograph not read end to end; neither memoir (Miradas furtivas 1996; La vida sin Dueno 2016) read; the SMU thesis unread beyond its abstract. Carried unresolved: the Paris dating (1948-55 per EN wiki vs 1949-51 per ICAA); his father's profession (geographer vs physician); the Moro 1970 remark (carried from #333, not re-verified); the Puka Wamani gloss (Christie's "Red God" vs the Quart article's "red hawk"); the "Grupo Norte" founding claim (low-quality source, unverified). ## M1. The imported fire

The founding move, and the one his whole career keeps re-enacting. In Paris around 1949-51, Szyszlo falls under the German Hans Hartung and turns to Abstract Expressionism; he meets Octavio Paz and Andre Breton and sits in the expatriate Latin American circle at the Cafe de Flore, where the table's question is the one that becomes his life's work: how to participate in the international modern movement while preserving a Latin American cultural identity. The mechanism is import-then-ignite: take the European fire (gestural abstraction, surrealist mystery, Old Master chiaroscuro from his Rembrandt/Titian/Tintoretto study) and burn Peruvian fuel in it (pre-Columbian textiles, Quechua poetry, Andean geology). Marta Traba named the result an "art of resistance," the middle path between cultural colonialism and folkloric nationalism. Szyszlo's own formulation, from the 1985 Americas interview: "To create a painting that is original, you have to bring it up from deep inside of you. It has to deal with your circumstance, your tradition, your heritage." Against the chain: Moro (#333 M2) adopted a foreign tongue as his optic and let its faults become texture; Szyszlo adopts a foreign painting language as his optic and lets its faults (the colonialism charge, the folklore charge) become the work's subject. Peret (#332 M2) ran the marvelous with no liquid phase; Szyszlo runs it with pigment, the most liquid solid there is. **Takeaway:** Import the fire, burn local fuel, and let the smoke be the subject. Watch the import: when the European gesture carries Andean weight without illustrating it, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The Quechua address

The titling machine, switched on in the 1960s and never switched off. Szyszlo begins giving his paintings Quechua titles explicitly to distance them from conventionally Western associations (Christie's): Apu Inca Atawallpaman (the colonial elegy for Atahualpa), Puka Wamani ("Red God" per Christie's, "red hawk" per the Quart article; puka = red), Paclla Pampa (paclla = rural/country, pampa = plain: the desolate country), Inkarri (the Andean myth of the returning Inca), Yurak, Masi Masi. The mechanism is an address that refuses translation into reading: the Google Arts and Culture curatorial text states it exactly, "the title evokes the culture of the Andean world, but does not limit the work to a single interpretation." The title is a doorway with no room behind it; it orients without explaining. Against the chain: Moro (#333 M5) translated his friends in consultation so the friendship would continue; Szyszlo translates nothing and lets the untranslated word do the work of the friend, keeping the painting at one remove from the Western viewer. Rahon (#331 M4) inscribed asemic dot-clusters; Szyszlo's asemic layer is a real language the viewer is not expected to read. **Takeaway:** Title in the language the painting is loyal to, and refuse to translate the loyalty. Watch the address: when the title names a mountain god and the canvas shows no mountain, the mechanism is on.

## M3. The loaded pigment

The material engine. The surfaces are built from thick impasto and layered color, worked until they resemble weathered archaeological relics; the GAC text gives the exact mechanics: "the forms are defined through textures loaded with pigment that are fully integrated via an atmosphere created with transparencies and effects of light." He paints in oil on fiberboard (nordex), a hard, unforgiving ground that takes the load and gives nothing back. The palette stays muted, earthy, nocturnal, until the scarlet hits: the mechanism is contrast between the weathered field and the sudden saturated color, the way an excavation finds one painted wall. The academic article notes his brushstrokes recall the feathered cloaks of Paracas, and his forms evoke stone monuments and confined spaces like the Lanzon at Chavin; the painting is a dig site that never finishes digging. Against the chain: Rahon (#331 M1) scratched through matter to find the image; Szyszlo piles matter until the image has to push through from inside. Felguerez (#330) built a machine that generated ideograms; Szyszlo's ideograms are generated by the trowel. **Takeaway:** Load the pigment until the surface is an excavation, then let one color be the find. Watch the dig: when the scarlet arrives like a painted wall in a trench, the mechanism is on.

## M4. The emblematic form

The image-system: claws, fangs, the circle, the stone mass. Puka Wamani's curatorial text describes "the evocations of claws and fangs, or of archetypal images such as the circle, endowed with magical allusions," and then the sentence that is the whole mechanism: "the emblematic character of the forms is highlighted for, although they are defined, cannot be read literally." The forms are legible as emblems and illegible as depictions, both at once; a claw that is not a claw, a circle that is not a sun. In the late 1960s the system grows the totemic form, described by Christie's as "a lone figurative presence, stone-faced and impregnable, the embodiment of both the ravages of the landscape and the long history of the Andean nation." Against the chain: Moro (#333 M4) mounted the beloved on an impossible animal that confessed nothing; Szyszlo's animals confess nothing either, but they are already stone, already monuments. Peret (#332 M3) filed his bestiary with Linnaean paperwork; Szyszlo files his under "magical allusions" and leaves the drawer open. **Takeaway:** Define the form sharply, then refuse every literal reading of it. Watch the emblem: when the claw is unmistakable and belongs to nothing, the mechanism is on.

## M5. The scarlet-and-black elegy

The color engine, and the one with a literary source. The 1963 Apu Inca Atawallpaman series takes its fuel from the sixteenth-century Quechua elegy mourning Atahualpa, in Jose Maria Arguedas's Spanish translation (first edition 1955, with his introduction to Szyszlo's catalog): the death of the last Inca as the founding wound of the Andean world. From it comes the black-and-red scheme Christie's calls "the colors of blood and death," "an ominous black and red" that gives "a strange, numinous beauty to the landscape." The black sun rises in Inkarri (1968, Blanton); the saturated scarlet frames "a shadowy void in the distance" in Paclla Pampa. Dore Ashton traces the scarlets to the feathered textiles of Chimu garments and the circles, dots, and intricate patterns to painted and woven textiles, "translated in his imagination into the rudiments of a pictorial vocabulary." Westphalen's gloss on the whole series is the mechanism's law: what mattered was "not so much the reference to that event as the attitude of the poet before the enigma of death and the bitter loneliness of those condemned to a 'wandering life.'" Against the chain: Moro (#333 M7) listed without punctuation until the breath gave out; Szyszlo elegizes without depicting until the color gives out, which it never does. **Takeaway:** Take one historical wound and render it as a palette, not a scene. Watch the elegy: when the red is blood and the black is the sun at the same time, the mechanism is on.

## M6. The series as excavation

The career's organizing form: not the painting, the series, each one a dig at a named site. Apu Inca Atawallpaman (1963, thirteen paintings on the Quechua elegy); Puka Wamani (1966-69, the red god/hawk years); the seacoast series (Mar de Lurin 1989, Camino a Mendieta, Sol Negro, the Pacific after the Andes); Duino (1991-94, Rilke's elegies as the European counterpart to the Quechua one); La Habitacion No. 23 (1997); Piedra del Sol (1997, Paz's poem as a title, closing the Cafe de Flore circle). The mechanism: each series names its source text or place first, then the paintings work the site for years, returning motifs (the circle, the void, the totem) like strata. The encyclopedia.com survey confirms the period logic: 1970s knives, ceremonial tables, and mummies in sacred gowns; 1980s totemic forms in closed three-dimensional chambers. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M9) looped the bakeress with drift; Szyszlo loops the dig site with drift, and the drift is the career. Felguerez (#330) varied eight shapes inside a field; Szyszlo varies one wound inside a decade. **Takeaway:** Name the site, then dig it for years. Watch the strata: when the same circle returns in a new series carrying a new elegy, the mechanism is on.

## M7. The manifesto as polemic

The public engine, and the one that made him famous before the paintings did. May 1947: he signs the Agrupacion Espacio "Expresion de principios" with Luis Miro Quesada Garland, the symbolic start of Peruvian modernism. June 1951: after the Sociedad de Arquitectos abstract show, his rebuttal to the critics carries the sentence "there are no painters in Peru" (La Prensa, 2 June 1951), detonating the country's first debate on nonfigurative art. The follow-on fight is the instructive one: critic Samuel Perez Barreto defends "telluric abstraction" as the only legitimate Peruvian art, and Szyszlo's own Espacio colleagues reject him, refusing any partisan identification of the national with the Andean. The mechanism: the polemic is not decoration on the work, it is the work's operating permit. He argues for abstraction in public so the paintings can be Andean in private, and he refuses the nationalist label in public so the paintings can carry the nation without illustrating it. Against the chain: Moro (#333 M10) made invective a craft against Huidobro; Szyszlo makes polemic a craft against his own camp, which is harder. Peret (#332 M7) used the pamphlet as a weapon against poets; Szyszlo uses the newspaper against the idea that his art needs a passport. **Takeaway:** Fight in public for the abstraction, so the paintings can be specific in private. Watch the permit: when the artist denounces his own defenders, the mechanism is on.

## M8. The Paris conversion

The biographical engine that powers M1. The ICAA record is precise: almost two years in Paris (1949-51) "proved decisive to a turn towards Abstract Expressionism," largely through Hans Hartung. The Cafe de Flore circle (Paz, Breton, the expatriate Latin Americans) supplies the question; Hartung supplies the gesture; the Old Masters (Rembrandt, Titian, Tintoretto, studied in the Louvre years) supply the chiaroscuro. He returns to Lima in early 1951 and shows the new work within months. The mechanism is conversion as method: go to the center, take its newest tool, and aim it at home. Note the honesty of the record: Raygada's 1949 attack (dependence on models "known only through reproductions") is the before-picture; the Hartung turn is the after. Against the chain: Rahon (#331) formed in exile and never came home to a scene; Szyszlo formed at the center and came home to build the scene, which is why the polemic (M7) was necessary. **Takeaway:** Take the center's newest tool and aim it at home. Watch the conversion: when the Paris gesture starts carrying Quechua weight, the mechanism is on.

## M9. The totemic chamber

The late spatial mechanism. In the 1980s the paintings close in: totemic forms set in closed, three-dimensional spaces that suggest chambers (encyclopedia.com). The open Andean expanses of the 1960s (the desolate country of Paclla Pampa) become interiors; the stone-faced presence gets walls. The mechanism is confinement as intensification: the same emblem (M4), the same elegy (M5), but now in a room, so the viewer is inside the wound rather than looking at the landscape. La Habitacion No. 23 (1997) names it: the room has a number, the way a museum storage room has a number, and the totem is what is stored there. Against the chain: Moro (#333 M3) made the book one love-letter, an interior you read in one breath; Szyszlo makes the painting one chamber, an interior you stand inside. **Takeaway:** Close the space until the emblem has nowhere to stand but facing you. Watch the chamber: when the landscape becomes a room, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The seacoast turn

The late-career rotation of the compass. After decades of the Andes, the named sites move to the Pacific: Mar de Lurin (1989), Camino a Mendieta (the 2003 etching; the oil he was working on in his studio in 2015), Sol Negro (1995, 2008). The livinginperu interview gives the mechanism in his own studio: Camino a Mendieta is "a colourful abstract oil painting inspired on Pisco south beaches," and the conversation turns on "the special sunset colours of Mendieta." The palette lightens; the elegy learns the sunset. The mechanism is the same excavation (M6) pointed at a new site: the sea he lived beside all along, finally admitted as a subject after the mountains were fully worked. Against the chain: Peret (#332 M6) made Mexico his method, the archive as the unconscious; Szyszlo makes the daily coast his late method, the visible as the unconscious. **Takeaway:** When the mountain is fully dug, turn and dig the sea. Watch the rotation: when the scarlet learns the sunset, the mechanism is on.

## M11. The definition as engine

The verbal mechanism, and the most quotable tool in the kit. "I have a very personal definition that the painting is the visible encounter of the sacred with the material" (livinginperu interview). The definition does work: it licenses the whole career (the sacred = the Andean substratum, the material = pigment on fiberboard) and it sets the acceptance test for every canvas (does the encounter happen, or is it just material?). The companion formulation from 1985: "bring it up from deep inside of you... your circumstance, your tradition, your heritage." The mechanism is the artist's sentence as a machine: it generates the work and judges it. Against the chain: Moro (#333 M11) kept an aphorism ledger where the cost of the marvelous life was entered; Szyszlo keeps one definition and spends seventy years paying it off. **Takeaway:** Write the sentence that generates and judges the work, then pay it off for a lifetime. Watch the encounter: when the pigment stops being material and starts being evidence, the mechanism is on.

## M12. The dated break

The political mechanism, and the proof that the "art of resistance" was not a slogan. 1968: the Apu Inca series shows at the Casa de las Americas in Havana, and the Cuban Revolution's leftist intellectuals claim him as progressive. 1971: he publicly breaks with Castro's regime over its treatment of the writer Heberto Padilla. The mechanism is the dated refusal: like the manifesto (M7), the break is public, named, and on the record. It costs him the progressive audience Traba's reading had built, and he pays it. The deeper note is Westphalen's: the work was never about the event but about "the attitude of the poet before the enigma of death," which is why it survives the politics it refused. Against the chain: Moro (#333 M8) broke with Breton in 1944 on two stated levels, formal and moral, and dated it like a legal filing; Szyszlo breaks with Havana in 1971 on one level, moral, and dates it the same way. The student of the surrealists learned the break as a form. **Takeaway:** Date the refusal and pay for it. Watch the break: when the progressive audience is the price of the enigma, the mechanism is on.

## Aesthetic logic

The career is one long demonstration that an international language can be given a local accent without becoming a dialect joke or a folklore poster. The logic runs: import the fire (M8, M1), refuse the literal reading (M4, M2), dig the named site (M6), elegize in scarlet and black (M5), argue the permit in public (M7), refuse the audience when it costs the enigma (M12), and finally turn the compass to the sea (M10) and close the room around the totem (M9). The through-line is the definition (M11): every painting is a place where the sacred becomes visible by means of the material, and the material is always honest about what it is (pigment, fiberboard, the trowel). Nothing is illustrated; everything is encountered. That is why the titles can be in Quechua and the forms can be unreadable and the work still holds: the encounter is the content, and the encounter needs no translation.

## Evolution

1925-1947 formation (Lima; Polish father, Peruvian mother of the Valdelomar family; architecture at the National University of Engineering 1943, abandoned; Fine Arts Academy of the Catholic University 1944) -> 1947-1949 Lima emergence (signs the Agrupacion Espacio "Expresion de principios" May 1947; first solo at the ICPN the same month, Picasso/Tamayo influence, praised by Barreto and Eielson; joint show with Eielson 1948, pre-Columbian elements via the European avant-garde; second solo June 1949, Raygada's attack on secondhand models) -> 1949-1951 Paris (the Hartung conversion to Abstract Expressionism; Paz and Breton at the Cafe de Flore; the international-modernism vs Latin American identity question) -> 1951-1962 the polemic years (Sociedad de Arquitectos show May 1951; "there are no painters in Peru," La Prensa, 2 June 1951; the first debate on nonfigurative art; the telluric-abstraction fight with Perez Barreto; Quechua titles begin in the 1960s) -> 1962-1968 the transnational decade (Cornell professor 1962; Yale visiting lecturer 1965; the Apu Inca Atawallpaman series 1963, thirteen paintings, Arguedas's translation as source, Westphalen's and Traba's readings, the continental tour; the Puka Wamani series 1966-69, the totemic form, the black sun) -> 1968-1971 the political test (Havana Casa de las Americas 1968; the Padilla break 1971) -> 1970s-1980s the chambers (knives, ceremonial tables, mummies in sacred gowns; totemic forms in closed 3D spaces) -> 1989-2010s the seacoast (Mar de Lurin 1989; Camino a Mendieta; Sol Negro; the Duino series 1991-94; La Habitacion No. 23 1997; Piedra del Sol 1997; the MALI 85th-birthday retrospective 2010) -> 2016-2017 the close (memoir La vida sin Dueno, December 2016; death with Lila Yabar in the stair fall, 9 October 2017) -> 2017- rediscovery (the centenary horizon: born 1925, the studies keep coming; the SMU thesis 2023; the works in MoMA, the Guggenheim, Houston, MALI, the Blanton).

## Study-method note

This study was built by the runner's own deep read, notes to disk before composition, continuing the #333 pattern after the #332 sandbox loss: eighteen sources end to end, about twenty works inspected via catalog, museum, and auction descriptions. Load-bearing facts were cross-checked across at least two sources wherever the record allowed; the Paris dating, the father's profession, and the Puka Wamani gloss are carried as conflicts, not resolved by vote. No painting was viewed first-hand at full resolution this run; the Ashton monograph and both memoirs are unread.
