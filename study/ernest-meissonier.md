# Ernest Meissonier: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean-Louis-Ernest Meissonier (1815-1891), French history and military painter -- born Tuesday 21 February 1815 in Lyon, the son of Charles Meissonier, a dye-factory proprietor at Saint-Denis; died Saturday 31 January 1891 in Paris, aged 75, with the unfinished Morning of Castiglione on his easel and his last wax maquette, General Duroc at Castiglione, inscribed by his widow. At seventeen he was placed with a druggist in the Rue des Lombards and obtained leave to become an artist; he entered Leon Cogniet's studio and stayed about four months, painting hired soldiers and horses, and later wrote that he learned little from him -- his real teachers were the seventeenth-century Dutch masters in the Louvre, Ter Borch, Frans van Mieris, Gerard Dow and above all Gabriel Metsu, and he became known as "the French Metsu". He earned his living in the 1830s as a wood-engraving illustrator for the publishers Curmer, Hetzel and Dubochet -- Les Contes Remois, Lamartine's Fall of an Angel, Paul and Virginia, The French Painted by Themselves -- and the engraver's hand is the technical root of his finish. He first exhibited at the Salon in the early 1830s with Dutch Burghers, also called The Visit to the Burgomaster (the sources disagree between 1831 and 1834; see C3), and from 1842 the small "thumbnail" pictures made his reputation: the bonshommes -- goodfellows playing chess, smoking pipes, reading books, making music -- in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century dress, painted in microscopic detail. Salon medals followed, third-class 1840, second-class 1841, first-class 1843 and 1844, grand medals at the Expositions Universelles of 1855 and 1867; he was made a knight of the Legion of Honour in 1846 and rose to the Grand Cross on 29 October 1889, and was elected to the Academie des Beaux-Arts in 1861. In 1846 he bought the Grande Maison at Poissy, eleven miles north-west of Paris on the Seine, with a winter workshop on the top floor and a glass-roofed summer workshop at ground level, stables for eight horses, a photographic workshop, suits of armour, bridles and plumed helmets, and a garden he would trample and sprinkle with flour to imitate melting snow. In 1848, a captain of artillery in the National Guard, he fought for the republican government in the June Days and witnessed the taking of the barricade in the rue de la Mortellerie; the watercolour he made at once was bought by Delacroix, and the oil that followed, 29 by 22 centimetres, is the one modern scene in his work. The Brawl of 1855, shown at the Exposition Universelle, won the Grand Medal of Honor and was bought by Napoleon III for 25,000 francs as a birthday gift for Prince Albert; it remains in the Royal Collection. In the summer of 1859 he attached himself at his own request to Napoleon III's staff in Italy, witnessed Solferino on 24 June 1859, and was commissioned to paint it -- a picture that took more than three years. The Napoleonic cycle followed: a projected five episodes of which only two were completed, 1814, Campagne de France (1860-1864, Musee d'Orsay, 51.5 by 76.5 cm) and 1807, Friedland (c. 1861-1875, Metropolitan Museum of Art, 135.9 by 242.6 cm), the largest and most ambitious painting by an artist renowned for cabinet pictures. Friedland was bought sight unseen by Alexander T. Stewart for $60,000, and 1814 sold in 1890 for 850,000 francs, then the highest price ever paid for a painting by a living artist; a contemporary newspaper reckoned none of his well-known works was to be had for much less than $300 a square inch. In 1863 he led a boycott over Salon rule changes which resulted in the Salon des Refuses (Ross King's account, as reviewed; see C14); in 1868, selected only as an alternate juror, he withdrew his paintings from the Salon in anger. In 1870-71 he was a colonel of a regiment de marche during the Siege of Paris and was among those who recovered the wounded after Buzenval, where the painter Henri Regnault, twenty-seven, was killed; thirteen years later he finished The Siege of Paris (1884), his wife Emma personifying the city in a black veil and a lion skin above the named dead. In 1890 he became the first president of the revived Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts -- the arch-academician presiding over a secession, with Rodin as vice-president -- and showed the unfinished 1806, Jena at its first Salon; he died seven months later. He was married twice, to Emma Steinhel in 1838 (the name is conflicted between sources; see C4) and, after her death in June 1888, to Mlle Bezancon in August 1890; his son Charles (1848-1917) was his pupil. At his death he was, in King's phrase, the most revered painter in Europe; within two generations he was an embarrassment -- in 1964 a statue of him was removed from the Louvre and another melted down for scrap. The scholarly revival came with the Lyon retrospective of 1993, Constance Hungerford's Ernest Meissonier: Master in his Genre (1999), Marc Gotlieb's The Plight of Emulation (1996) and Ross King's The Judgment of Paris (2006), which sets him against Manet, 1863-1874. His works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay (the largest public group, about forty pictures), the Wallace Collection in London (sixteen), the Metropolitan Museum of Art (Friedland, The Card Players, Soldier Playing the Theorbo, A General and His Aide-de-camp), the Louvre (The Barricade), the Conde Museum at Chantilly (1805, Cuirassiers Before the Charge), the Royal Collection (The Brawl) and the Hermitage, the Prado, the National Gallery in London, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Clark, the Walters and the National Gallery of Ireland. ## M1. Microscopic Finish
Every square centimetre resolved to a legible object: cuff, card, button, bridle, paving stone. The Card Players (1863, oil on wood, 35.2 by 26.7 cm, Metropolitan Museum of Art) is the type: four men around a table, every card and cuff resolved, a whole scene inside a panel the size of a book. The Barricade oil (c. 1850-51, 29 by 22 cm, Louvre) carries witnessed horror at the same scale, the paving stones jewel-like, the bodies in red, blue and white smocks. The Catholic Encyclopedia states the rule outright: "Above all he excelled in microscopic canvases, wherein the wonderful reproduction of the minutest details is a perpetual source of astonishment." The 1911 Britannica calls his debut "the first attempt in France in the particular genre which was destined to make Meissonier famous: microscopic painting - miniature in oils." Barbusse puts the smallness first: "But the small size of Meissonier's pictures! That is the one thing that, for the world at large, contemporaries and posterity alike, is the keynote of his talent." Ruskin examined the work under a magnifying glass, "marvelling at Meissonier's manual dexterity and eye for fascinating minutiae." His own rule of scale, recorded by Greard (secondhand here, see C18), runs the other way from what a modern painter would expect: "The smaller the scale of one's picture, the more boldly the relief must be brought out. The larger the scale, the more it must be softened and diminished. This is an absolutely indispensable rule. A life-sized figure treated like one of my small ones, would be unendurable." The watchable behaviour is a picture that does not dissolve as you approach it: the camera zooms in and the picture gains information instead of losing it. **Takeaway:** finish until the zoom finds more, not less; the smallness is the point, not the constraint.

## M2. The Frozen Decisive Instant
A fight, a handshake, a charge, a retreat caught at the single second that explains the whole event. The Brawl (1855) is the type: a seventeenth-century tavern, a fight over a game of cards, the quarrel frozen at the moment the table goes over. Les Deux Amis (1874, 34.6 by 42.9 cm) is the same mechanism gentled: a hussar and a dragoon of the First Empire shaking hands before battle, the handshake as the whole picture. 1807, Friedland is the same mechanism at epic scale: Napoleon on a rearing horse at the centre of a cuirassier charge, the charge caught mid-gallop. 1814, Campagne de France is the same mechanism in defeat: Napoleon on a white horse leading his staff back from Soissons after the Battle of Laon, weary, slouched, hand at his chest, on a muddy, snowy road under a heavy sky. The mechanism has a stated cause in his own doctrine (secondhand, see C18): he never painted a battle, Barbusse reports, because he had never seen one and decided "that he did not really know what a battle was" -- so his war pictures are aftermaths, halts, charges and staff groups, never the clash itself. The watchable behaviour is a picture in which everything leans into one held second and nothing moves, yet the outcome is already decided. **Takeaway:** pick the second that contains the event, then refuse to show anything else.

## M3. The Napoleonic Miniature Epic
A whole campaign, an empire's fall, compressed into a cabinet picture you could hang in a Paris apartment. 1814, Campagne de France (1860-64, oil on wood, 51.5 by 76.5 cm, Musee d'Orsay) is the type: the fall of the Empire as weather -- cold colours, a heavy and menacing sky, Napoleon slumped on a calm white horse, the staff behind him, the road empty ahead. A smaller variant, 1862, oil on panel, 32.4 by 24.2 cm, is at the Walters Art Museum. He conceived a cycle of five key episodes in Napoleon's life and completed two of them: 1814 and 1807, Friedland (c. 1861-75, Metropolitan Museum of Art); The Morning of Castiglione was unfinished at his death, the preparatory oil sketch still on the easel. Friedland is the exception that proves the rule: at 135.9 by 242.6 cm it outgrew the miniature, yet it was painted with cabinet-picture finish, "the epic painted with cabinet-picture finish" in the Met's phrase. The watchable behaviour is a vast historical arc -- retreat, defeat, empire ending -- playing out inside a frame the size of a book, so that the viewer must lean in to read a war. **Takeaway:** shrink the frame, keep the arc; the epic is in the compression, not the acreage.

## M4. The Studio-as-Museum Prop System
Costume, weapon, saddle and uniform treated as research objects, and the correctness as the spectacle. For Friedland the costumes and trappings "were made by tailors, boot-makers, and saddlers" (Cyclopedia of Painters and Paintings); the horses were modelled in wax before painting. He borrowed Napoleon's actual saddle from Prince Napoleon-Jerome Bonaparte for 1814, and had a replica of Napoleon's overcoat made which he wore himself, modelling in front of a mirror. At Poissy he kept a "working library" of breeches, hats, helmets, boots and buckles, bought out second-hand dealers' stocks, and filled both workshops with canvases, brushes, easels, musical instruments, suits of armour, bridles and harnesses and plumed helmets. Barbusse's verdict is that every picture is "a document which may be profitably consulted if one wished to decide a detail of costume or armament." The single figures are the system at its purest: A Musketeer: Time of Louis XIII (1856, Wallace Collection, oil on mahogany panel), A Cavalier: Time of Louis XIV (1856), A Sentinel: Time of Louis XIII (1851) -- a man, a costume, a century, nothing else. The watchable behaviour is a picture in which every prop is correct to the stitch, and the correctness is what you are looking at. **Takeaway:** research the object until the object is the picture; let the stitch do the showing.

## M5. The Wax-Horse Rehearsal
Sculpture as a draft for painting: model the horse in wax, pose it, light it, then paint it. He modelled horses and figures in wax over a wire armature, often "dressed and saddled up" with cloth, leather and metal, as aides to painting; grouping maquettes let him compose multi-figure scenes and avoid perspective errors. Fifteen sculptures were executed in total (Sotheby's); none was exhibited or cast in bronze in his lifetime; eleven waxes owned by his son and daughter were exhibited at the Galerie Georges Petit after his death, and bronze editions by Siot-Decauville followed from 1894 at his family's agreement. The wax studies for Jena run from as early as 1880; the last work of all, General Duroc at Castiglione, December 1890, is a wax maquette inscribed by his widow: "derniere maquette de mon mari Xbre 1890 / mort samedi 31 Janvier 1891 Paris". Named examples held at Grenoble include the Gladiator with Sword, the Trumpet of Murcie (1879), the Dancing Muse (1882) and the Wounded Horse (1884). The watchable behaviour is a horse that freezes mid-gallop, is studied from every angle, and is then released into paint -- the sculpture is the rehearsal, the painting the performance. **Takeaway:** build the thing in three dimensions before you draw it in two; the maquette is the first draft, not an afterthought.

## M6. The Crowd as Texture
Armies rendered as patterned surface -- ranks, plumes, bayonets, horseflesh repeating into a woven field, with one figure as the single readable knot. Friedland is the type: cuirassiers charging across a grain field, hundreds of preparatory studies behind a single charge, the mass resolving into rhythm. 1814 reverses the mechanism: one weary man on a calm white horse against an empty snowy road, the crowd dissolved into a retreating column behind him. Napoleon III at the Battle of Solferino (1863, exhibited Salon 1864, Musee d'Orsay, on deposit at Compiegne) is the mechanism applied to a battle he actually witnessed: the emperor and his staff as the readable knot inside the patterned mass of an army. Even the genre pictures work this way at small scale: Sunday in Poissy (1850, shown Salon 1851 as Le dimanche) puts 26 figures outdoors, each posture a character, the crowd as a catalogue of attitudes. The watchable behaviour is a mass that resolves into rhythm as you look, and then a single white horse or dark coat that pulls the eye out of the pattern. **Takeaway:** weave the crowd, then knot it once; one readable figure is worth a hundred rendered ones.

## M7. The Bonhomme Loop
One man, one absorbing pastime, one small room: chess, smoking, reading, playing the cello, looking at drawings. The Game of Chess (1841) is the type: two men at chess, "the most celebrated picture of the [1841] exhibition" in Muther's phrase, bought by Paul Perier for 2,000 francs, a second-class medal. The Reader first appeared in 1840; Man Reading (1851, Clark Art Institute) and A Reading at Diderot's (Salon 1857) repeat the type across the career. The Smoker, first version Salon 1842, and the Young Man Playing the 'Cello (1842) are the same figure in other pastimes; The Flute-Player (Musee du Louvre) is a single standing musician, foot raised beating time, "admirably life-like". The loop is the mechanism that made his fortune and the one he resented: he claimed, secondhand (see C18), "No words can express my horror of going back to painting genre pictures for a livelihood. Ah! Would that I were independent enough only to do great pictures!" -- the small pictures were undertaken from necessity, he said, and he felt destined for greater things. The watchable behaviour is a figure so absorbed in a small task that time stops; the viewer watches attention itself. **Takeaway:** absorption is the subject; paint a man doing one thing so completely that the room disappears.

## M8. The Game as Drama Engine
Cards and chess as a bloodless duel: the quarrel over the cards, the gamblers' standoff, the piquet players' silent contest. The Brawl (1855) is the type: a fight over a game of cards, the game state readable in the postures, the violence always one move away. The Gamblers (1855) was shown with it at the Exposition Universelle, the paired peak of his genre manner. A Game of Piquet (exhibited 1845, a version 1861) is the two-player card game as a duel of attention; The Card Players (1863) is the late return to the card table, now fully miniaturist, four men around a table, every card and cuff resolved. Innocents and Card Sharpers (1861) and The End of the Game of Cards (1865) extend the same table into the 1860s. The mechanism is that the game gives the picture its plot without giving it a story: two or four faces around a table, the stakes visible in the hands and the eyes, the quarrel always about to happen. The watchable behaviour is a table that holds the whole drama -- the faces change, the cards change, the violence never quite arrives. **Takeaway:** give the figures a game and the picture gets a plot for free; keep the violence one move away, never on the table.

## M9. Eyewitness-to-Allegory Conversion
A scene he saw with his own eyes is first sketched fast, then rebuilt slowly as oil, then finally raised into allegory with named dead. The Barricade is the short form: a watercolour, 1848, 26 by 21 cm, Musee d'Orsay, painted immediately after the June Days and bought by Delacroix, its first owner; then the oil, Rue de la Mortellerie, June 1848, c. 1850-51, 29 by 22 cm, Louvre, exhibited Salon 1850-51 as Memory of Civil War -- an elevated viewpoint down a narrow street, shuttered houses, jewel-like paving stones, bodies in red, blue and white smocks, blood on the stones. His own words on what he saw (quoted in T. J. Clark via the Concordia textbook): "When the barricade in the rue de la Mortellerie was taken, I realized all the horror of such warfare. I saw the defenders shot down, hurled out of windows, the ground strewn with corpses, the earth red with the blood it had not yet drunk." The Siege of Paris is the long form: sketched 1870-71, taken up much later, finished 1884 -- the city personified by his wife Emma, draped in a black veil and a lion skin, rising above a ruined barricade before a tattered tricolore, the spectre of famine and a Prussian eagle in the smoky sky, the dead and dying on the martyr's palms, the named dead among them (see M11), civilians grieving among the corpses. He called it, in his own phrase, "a sort of heroic symphony of France". The watchable behaviour is a quick sketch hardening over years into a memorial: the same street gains a martyr and a flag. **Takeaway:** witness fast, rebuild slow, name the dead last; the allegory is the sketch grown up.

## M10. The Slow Canvas
Execution measured in years, not sessions. Friedland took about a decade and a half, c. 1861-1875 (sources say ten, fourteen and fifteen years; see C8); 1814 took four years, 1860-1864; Solferino took more than three years; Jena, begun 1887, was still unfinished at his death in 1891 and was shown unfinished at the inaugural Salon of the Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in May 1890. The Pantheon mural commission of 1874 was his bid to become leader of the French school and to escape the Salon production cycle; by his death nearly twenty years later he had barely begun it, and the battle cycle of five projected paintings reached only two. The doctrine behind the slowness is recorded in the Art Journal (1883): he refused to part with an unfinished sketch or drawing at any price, and his Poissy garrets held a fortune in studies and unfinished works; Barbusse reports him slashing up a painting everyone pronounced perfect while a buyer waited in the vestibule, crying "I don't know how to paint! I shall never learn my craft." Ten to twelve hours a day at the easel in Poissy, hundreds of preparatory studies for a single picture, "cubic metres" of studies (Barbusse) -- and at the end, a canvas the painter dies before. The watchable behaviour is a painting that visibly accretes, layer on layer, revision on revision, until the painter dies before it does. **Takeaway:** let the picture outlive the session; a canvas that takes fifteen years is a different object from one that takes fifteen days.

## Evolution
SCALE: The early work is microscopic -- cabinet pictures, often oil on panel, a few inches to a foot or two, one or two figures in an interior. The 1850s stay small; Sunday in Poissy is small despite its 26 figures. The Napoleonic turn keeps the small panel for 1814 (51.5 by 76.5 cm) but Friedland breaks the scale entirely (135.9 by 242.6 cm, oil on canvas), his largest and most ambitious picture, and the exception that proves the rule: even his "large" history painting is modest beside Delacroix or Gerome. The late work returns to small panels -- dragoons, gentlemen, Poissy river views -- with the single large exception of The Siege of Paris (53.5 by 70.5 cm, still small for an allegory of a nation). Jena, begun 1887, would have been the second large exception; it stayed unfinished.

SUBJECT: 1830s-40s: Dutch and Flemish pastiche interiors, bonshommes reading, smoking, playing chess and music, musketeers and halberdiers in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century dress; almost entirely men, women and children rare. 1848 is the one rupture into the present: The Barricade. 1850s: the same repertoire made more violent (Les Bravi 1852, The Quarrel 1855) and more crowded (Sunday in Poissy, 26 figures outdoors). 1859-75: contemporary history, Solferino witnessed, then Napoleonic history -- 1814, Friedland, 1805 -- the bonhomme's microscopic finish applied to armies. 1868 Antibes: a plein-air detour -- southern light, landscapes, washerwomen, boules players -- "delightful to sun oneself in the brilliant light of the South instead of wandering about like gnomes in the fog", and a loosening toward "a few salient touches of the brush" (Hungerford via Wikipedia). 1870-71: war witnessed, then memorialised thirteen years later. 1880s: Venice (Saint Mark, the Grand Canal), Poissy river views, portraits of the rich (Stanford, Dumas fils, Hetzel) -- the grand old man painting his own world.

FINISH AND COLOUR: Early: smooth, enamelled, Dutch-brown interiors, grey and black costume with small warm accents. Mid: the same finish, but outdoors -- green grain fields, snow, dust. Late: Antibes and Venice bring a higher, southern key and real landscape light; the Orsay Poissy views of 1889 are his closest approach to contemporary landscape painting, though the finish stays tight. Throughout, the finish never loosens into Impressionist broken touch; the Antibes loosening is relative, a few salient touches against a lifetime of resolution.

CROWDS VS SINGLE FIGURES: Early, one or two figures, the single reader, smoker or chess player as a complete world. 1850s, the crowd as a catalogue of attitudes, 26 figures in Sunday in Poissy, each posture a character. Napoleonic, the crowd as an army: Friedland's cuirassiers are a mass with individual horses distinguishable; 1814 reverses it, one weary man on a calm horse against an empty snowy road, the crowd dissolved into a retreating column. Late, a return to the single figure -- dragoon on vedette, gentleman, sentry -- and the single landscape.

HANDLING: The constant is research before paint: maquette, costume, horse study, staged cavalry charge. What changes is the object of the research -- from a pipe and a chair in a Dutch interior, to a grain field in East Prussia on 14 June, to the gait of a horse, to the faces of named dead men in a siege. The method scales up; the eye does not change. To paint trampled grain in Friedland he paid indemnities to have a squadron of cuirassiers gallop across a ripening grain field near Poissy, then restaged it the following May when the grain was the right size and colour for 14 June in East Prussia. He had a photographic workshop at Poissy; he knew Muybridge's horse-motion sequences, and when Stanford showed him the 1878 instantaneous photographs he realised he had been misrepresenting horses' gaits for decades -- the most exact eye in France corrected by a machine. He was among the first to illustrate the "appui diagonale" gait, diagonal pairs of a horse's legs moving in unison with minimal knee flexion.

FOLDED IN: The named-dead memorial (main-leg M11): in The Siege of Paris the corpses have names, ranks and death dates, painted with the same finish as the living -- Colonel Picot de Dampierre (red scarf, Bagneux), Captain Neverlee (crushed under a wounded horse, Villiers), Colonel Franchetti (30 November 1870), and the painter Henri Regnault, dying at twenty-seven, kneeling against the allegory; in The Barricade the anonymous dead are given the tricolore. The eye moves from face to face in the foreground and each body turns out to be somebody. The Dutch little-master inheritance (main-leg M12): the seventeenth-century cabinet picture (Metsu, Ter Borch, Dow) transplanted into nineteenth-century France -- small scale, domestic interior, costume as period, finish as morality -- a two-hundred-year-old format made to carry modern French life. The price as proof (main-leg M13): record prices as part of the work's meaning -- Friedland $60,000, 1814 at 850,000 francs, The Brawl at 25,000 francs from an emperor, the Cuirassiers bought for 10,000 pounds and resold for 16,000, seven little genre pictures $101,000 in the Secretan sale -- the tiny panel carries a price tag bigger than a palace, value made visible by smallness. The Antibes light break (main-leg M14): in June 1868 he travels to Antibes with his family and two horses and paints plein-air landscapes in southern light, briefly abandoning historical authenticity for eye-catching visual effects by means of a few salient touches of the brush; the miniaturist lets go of the detail for once, and the picture breathes.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic of Meissonier is a logic of paradoxes, and the paradoxes are the point. First, smallness as value: the smaller the picture, the higher the price per inch, until a panel twenty inches high by thirty inches wide is called "the most expensive painting in the world" and a newspaper reckons none of his well-known works is to be had for much less than $300 a square inch. The miniature is not a modest format; it is a luxury object, a cabinet picture for a Paris apartment, an epic you own in private. Second, finish as morality: drawing is absolute truth (P1), the language of truth should be taught everywhere; to sketch a horse from memory would be "an insult to nature" (P3); every picture is a document for costume or armament (P8). The Catholic Encyclopedia's verdict is the other side of the same coin: his eye was "constructed like the lens of a magnifying glass... the eye of a myopic, the eye of a fly, cut like a crystal into millions of facets", a prodigious power of decomposition that "left him incapable of putting anything together again" -- what is wanting is "emotion and life". The finish that made him the most famous painter in Europe is the finish the modernists read as cold-blooded, lacking spirit and imagination. Third, the resentment engine: the genre pictures he claimed to dread -- "No words can express my horror of going back to painting genre pictures for a livelihood" (P7) -- are the pictures that paid for the history he wanted to paint; the market's favourite was the artist's least loved, and the bonhomme loop funded the Napoleonic cycle. Fourth, the battle he never painted: he decided he did not really know what a battle was, because he had never seen one (P4), so the century's great battle pictures from his hand are aftermaths, halts, charges and staff groups -- the epic built from the edges of the event, the charge and the retreat rather than the clash. Fifth, the secession paradox: the arch-academician, the man who led the jury that excluded Courbet in 1872, becomes in 1890 the first president of the breakaway Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts, with Rodin as vice-president and Puvis de Chavannes succeeding him -- the establishment lending its name to the secession, the grand old man as the rebels' figurehead. Sixth, the reversal: Delacroix called him "the incontestable master of our epoch" and said he was "amongst us all, surely... the most likely to survive", and Delacroix was wrong; by his death he was among the most famous painters of the nineteenth century, and within two generations his renown had vanished, his smallness read by modernist critics as proof of a total lack of ambition, his statue removed from the Louvre in 1964 and another melted down for scrap. The revival that followed -- Lyon 1993, Hungerford 1999, Gotlieb 1996, King 2006 -- is scholarship, not fashion: the study of a man who was the best-paid painter of his day and then the century's embarrassment, and whose method (research before paint, the maquette before the canvas, the staged charge before the picture) is now legible as a system rather than a tic. Seventh, the camera's verdict: the man who would not sketch a horse from memory, who staged a squadron across a grain field to get the trample right, learned from Muybridge's 1878 photographs that he had been misrepresenting horses' gaits for decades -- exactitude undone by a better instrument, and the painter who took the correction. The aesthetic, stated plainly, is this: truth is a matter of looking long enough, at small enough scale, with enough preparation -- and the tragedy is that the century stopped believing it. The pieces this study feeds should keep the smallness, the finish, the frozen second and the researched prop, and they should keep the resentment too: the system that pays for itself with pictures it does not love, the epic that fits in a book, the memorial that names its dead.
