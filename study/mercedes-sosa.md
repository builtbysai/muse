# Mercedes Sosa: Study Notes

**Site:** Haydee Mercedes Sosa Giron (born Tuesday 9 July 1935, San Miguel de Tucuman, Argentina, the same day and the same city in which Argentina declared its independence in 1816; died Sunday 4 October 2009, Buenos Aires, at 05:17 by the Spanish Wikipedia and 05:15 by the English Wikipedia, of multiple organ failure after renal failure, aged 74), Argentine cantora, called La Negra Sosa and "la voz de America Latina", the voice of Latin America, and by the English Wikipedia "the voice of the voiceless ones" and "the conscience of Latin America". She recorded forty albums by the English Wikipedia count, about thirty-five studio titles named in the Spanish Wikipedia table, and she won six Latin Grammys (2000, 2003, 2004, 2006, 2009, 2011), including the Latin Grammy Award for Musical Excellence in 2004, the Premio Gardel Album of the Year in 1999 for Al despertar, the Konex de Brillante in 1995 as the best popular artist of the decade, and she was named Doctora honoris causa by the universities of Tucuman in 1997 and Cuyo in 2006, Ciudadana Ilustre of Buenos Aires on 1 April 1992, and a UNESCO Goodwill Ambassador for Latin America and the Caribbean, with the credentials handed over in Mexico in 1999 by the Fundacion account and "desde 2008" by the Spanish Wikipedia infobox. She sang in the Teatro Opera, the Teatro Colon, the Lincoln Center, Carnegie Hall, the Theatre Mogador in Paris, the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam, the Colosseum in Rome and the Sistine Chapel in Vatican City, and she is the singer the whole chain has been walking toward without knowing it, because Pineda fell in love with her as a student, Libertad shared her collaborators, and Manzanero's most travelled song was born from the Peruvian vals of the woman Sosa honoured with a whole album. She is not a singer in the sense of Pineda or Libertad, and that is the point of studying her here: she is the cantora, and the difference between a cantante and a cantora is the whole study, because a cantante is the one who can and a cantora is the one who must, and she must. ## M1. The false name

She did not begin as Mercedes Sosa. She began as Gladys Osorio, a name that did not exist, and the false name is the mechanism. The Spanish Wikipedia gives the day in her own words, and the Fundacion and the CMTV biography agree on it: one day in October 1950, fifteen years old, her parents away on a free train to Buenos Aires for the 17 October Day of Peronist Loyalty, her schoolmates pushing her to a radio contest at LV12 in Tucuman, she hid behind the name Gladys Osorio for fear her father would find out, sang "Triste estoy" by Margarita Palacios, and won a two-month contract. The father did find out, and called her to him with the words she said she still heard: "Gladys Osorio, venga, acerquese... Tengo que felicitarla? Mireme a los ojos". The mechanism has a second false name underneath the first: her parents had agreed to call her Marta Mercedes, but at the civil registry her father changed it to Haydee Mercedes, and her mother and her family never used the legal name, calling her Marta all her life. "En mi casa definitivamente soy la Marta. Para la gente definitivamente soy la Negra." At home I am definitely Marta. For people I am definitely the Negra. So the singer has three names before she has one career: Marta at home, Haydee on the papers, Gladys Osorio on the radio, and then Mercedes Sosa for the world, and the Negra for the people. The mechanism is the name as a hiding place: the girl who is afraid to sing hides behind a name that is not hers, and the woman who sings for a continent keeps the hiding place as a structure, one name for the house and one for the stage, and the stage name is the one that belongs to everyone. **Takeaway:** begin behind a false name. The name that is not yours is the door. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M1) also began in the small rooms, but her small rooms were the school and she sang under her own name; Sosa's small room was a radio contest entered under a name that did not exist, which is why her version is a hiding place and Pineda's is an apprenticeship. Libertad (#356 M12) also left a name as her legacy, the girls named Tania Libertad; Sosa's legacy is a nickname given to her by the people, La Negra, which is why her version is a gift and Libertad's is a christening.

## M2. The manifesto as the career

She signed a manifesto before she was famous, and the manifesto is the mechanism. On Monday 11 February 1963, in the Circulo de Periodistas of Mendoza, Mercedes Sosa, her husband Oscar Matus, the poet and announcer Armando Tejada Gomez, Tito Francia and others launched the Manifiesto del Nuevo Cancionero, and the Spanish Wikipedia quotes it at length: the New Songbook proposes to seek, in the creative richness of Argentine authors and interpreters, the integration of popular music in the diversity of the regional expressions of the country; it wants to apply the national conscience of the people through new and better works; it rejects all closed regionalism; it will purify the musical heritage of traditionalist taboos; it will discard, reject and denounce to the public every crude and subaltern production made for mercantile ends; it affirms that art, like life, must be in permanent transformation. The mechanism is the program stated before the career: the trio that made it, Matus with the music, Tejada Gomez with the words, Sosa with the voice, is named in her own sentence, "En Zamba de la distancia, como en La de los humildes, estan los tres nombres que impulsaron el movimiento: Armando con sus palabras, Matus con la musica y yo con la voz." And the first two albums are the manifesto as titles: La voz de la zafra, the voice of the sugar harvest, the work of her father's province, and Canciones con fundamento, songs with foundation, the title that says the songs must have a reason. The mechanism's proof is its duration: the Spanish Wikipedia says she guided her whole artistic life by the principles of the new songbook, "venciendo a menudo arraigados prejuicios artisticos, culturales e ideologicos", and the last concerts of 2009 were in Guaymallen, Mendoza, the town where the manifesto was written, so the career begins and ends in the same place, under the same document. Watch the manifesto: when the singer who has not yet had a hit signs a document saying the song must have a foundation and must serve the people, and forty-six years later she is still singing by it, and her last concert is in the town where she signed it, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** sign the manifesto before the fame. The foundation is the career. Against the chain: Manzanero (#358 M12) also defended a guild, but his defence was an administration, forty years of meetings in Paris and Mexico City; Sosa's defence was a manifesto signed at twenty-seven, before anyone knew her name, which is why her version is a program and his is a presidency. Pineda (#357 M2) also refused a contract, but her refusal was a single no to a single offer; Sosa's refusal was a document signed with others, which is why her version is a movement and Pineda's is a refusal.

## M3. The interruption at Cosquin

She did not win Cosquin. She interrupted it, and the interruption is the mechanism. In January 1965, at the fifth Festival Nacional de Folklore de Cosquin, the centre of the folklore boom, the singer Jorge Cafrune, already an idol, one metre eighty tall, a deep voice, a wide-brimmed hat, a beard like the Cuban revolutionaries, brought her up from the audience where she was sitting and presented her with words the Spanish Wikipedia preserves: "Yo me voy a atrever, porque es un atrevimiento lo que voy a hacer ahora, y me voy a recibir un tiron de orejas por la Comision... Les voy a ofrecer el canto de una mujer purisima, que no ha tenido oportunidad de darlo... les voy a dejar con ustedes a una tucumana: Mercedes Sosa." I am going to dare, because what I am going to do now is a daring thing, and I am going to get my ears pulled by the Commission... I am going to offer you the song of a most pure woman, who has not had the opportunity to give it... I am going to leave you with a woman from Tucuman: Mercedes Sosa. She sang "Cancion del derrumbe indio" by Fernando Figueredo Iramain, accompanied only by her bombo, her caja, "Cante con una cajita, nomas." I sang with just a little box. The mechanism has a second half that the sources only revealed after her death: she died believing the commission had not wanted to let her sing because she was affiliated with the Communist Party, and she never knew that the interruption had been arranged with the commission in a secret meeting, German Cazenave's idea, Marcelo Simon's complicity, so that Cafrune would present her as his own rebellion and no artist would complain. "Nunca le pudimos contar la verdad a Mercedes, nunca se dio." We could never tell Mercedes the truth, it never happened. So the interruption was both a rebellion and a permission, and she carried the rebellion version for the rest of her life, which is why the mechanism works: the singer believes she was smuggled onto the stage against the rules, and the belief is what makes her sing as if the stage were stolen. The payoff is immediate and stated: Philips signed her, the third album Yo no canto por cantar came out in 1966, and the fame never left her. Watch the interruption: when the famous singer stops his own show, reaches into the audience, and pulls out an unknown woman with a little drum, and the commission that was supposed to stop it had already agreed, and the woman believes for forty-four years that she was smuggled in, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** be pulled from the audience with one drum. The stolen stage is the career. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M3) also broke a ban, but hers was broken by a million and a half people buying a disc; Sosa's ban was broken by one man reaching into the audience, which is why her version is an interruption and Pineda's is a sale. Libertad (#356 M1) also started again from below, but her below was six years out of the media after arriving already famous; Sosa's below was the audience itself, which is why her version is an interruption and Libertad's is a restart.

## M4. The song by another

She wrote almost nothing, and that is the mechanism. Sosa is the interpreter, not the author, and the study must say it plainly, because every other singer in the chain writes or co-writes, and she does not. The catalogue is other people's words sung as if they were hers: "Alfonsina y el mar" and "Juana Azurduy" by Ariel Ramirez and Felix Luna; "Gracias a la vida" by Violeta Parra; "Te recuerdo Amanda" by Victor Jara; "Cancion con todos" by Tejada Gomez and Cesar Isella; "Zamba para no morir" by Hamlet Lima Quintana; "Como la cigarra" by Maria Elena Walsh; "Solo le pido a Dios" by Leon Gieco; "Todo cambia" by Julio Numhauser; "La maza" and "Unicornio" by Silvio Rodriguez; "Yo vengo a ofrecer mi corazon" by Fito Paez; "De mi" by Charly Garcia; "Cuando me acuerdo de mi pais" by Patricio Manns; "Los mareados" by Cobian and Cadicamo; "Tonada del viejo amor" by Jaime Davalos and Eduardo Falu. The mechanism is stated by her own definition of herself: she defined herself as "cantora" before "cantante", a distinction fundamental to the nueva cancion, and Facundo Cabral gave it its sentence, "Cantante es el que puede y cantor el que debe." The singer is the one who can; the cantora is the one who must. So the interpreter is not the lesser figure but the necessary one: the song needs a voice that must sing it, and the voice needs a song that must be sung, and the meeting of the two is the work. The mechanism's proof is the afterlife of the songs: "Cancion con todos" became the unofficial anthem of Latin America, "Gracias a la vida" became the song the whole continent sings, and neither was written by the woman the continent associates with them, which is why the association is the mechanism, the voice taking the song by singing it until it belongs to her. Watch the taking: when the singer who wrote nothing sings a Chilean woman's song, and the continent hears it as her song, and the Chilean woman's daughter says it seemed natural because Violeta would have done the same with Mercedes, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** take the song by singing it. The voice is the authorship. Against the chain: Manzanero (#358 M3) also wrote a song at fifteen and let others carry it, but his songs left him and never came back; Sosa's songs came to her from others and never left, which is why his version is a departure and hers is a taking. Pineda (#357 M11) also took a song by retitling it, "Yolanda" becoming "Te amo"; Sosa took songs without retitling them, by singing them until the continent forgot who wrote them, which is why her version is a voice and Pineda's is a renaming.

## M5. The other country's song

She sang Chile's song, and the other country's song is the mechanism. In the spring of 1969 she made her first presentation in Chile, and at the same time recorded a single with two Chilean authors on it: on side A, "Gracias a la vida" by Violeta Parra, and on side B, "Te recuerdo Amanda" by Victor Jara. Two years later, in 1971, under the government of Salvador Allende, she recorded Homenaje a Violeta Parra, a whole album of the Chilean singer-songwriter's songs, "Gracias a la vida" again, "Volver a los 17", "La carta" with Quilapayun, opening with a recitation of Nicanor Parra's "Defensa de Violeta Parra", and the Spanish Wikipedia calls it one of her best discs and a consecrating interpretation for both the singer and the songs. The mechanism is the tribute as an adoption: she never met Violeta Parra, who died in 1967, two years before the single, and yet the daughter, Isabel Parra, said it always seemed natural that Mercedes sang Violeta, because Violeta would have done the same with Mercedes, they would have loved each other and understood each other. The adoption has a date that the sources state as a coincidence and the study carries as a coincidence: Mercedes Sosa died on 4 October, the birthday of Violeta Parra. The mechanism has a second Chilean song inside it: "Te recuerdo Amanda", the song of Victor Jara, the man who would be tortured and murdered in the Estadio Chile in 1973, the same stadium where Sosa would return in 1992 to honour him. So the single of 1969 contains both Chilean martyrs, the woman who took her own life and the man the dictatorship murdered, and the singer who sang them both became the voice of a country that was not hers. Watch the adoption: when the Argentine singer records a Chilean woman's song she never met, and the Chilean woman's daughter says it was natural, and the Argentine singer dies on the Chilean woman's birthday, and then returns to the stadium where the Chilean man was murdered to sing for him, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** sing the other country's song as if it were yours. The adoption is the career. Against the chain: Libertad (#356 M4) also sang a forbidden song by Chabuca Granda, but hers was forbidden by one teacher and waited thirty-six years; Sosa's Chilean songs were sung at once, in the year they were written, and then banned by two dictatorships, which is why her version is an adoption and Libertad's is a debt. Manzanero (#358 M5) also brought a Peruvian vals home and recorded it as a ballad; Sosa brought a Chilean song home and recorded it as herself, which is why his version is a conversion and hers is an adoption.

## M6. The arrest on stage

She was arrested for singing, on the stage, in front of the audience, and the arrest is the mechanism. In 1978, in a recital in La Plata, in the old Almacen San Jose, she sang songs that were not permitted, and the military came with their weapons, searched her on the stage itself, arrested her and arrested the whole audience with her. The Spanish Wikipedia preserves the account of an admiring woman also called Mercedes, who was in the audience that night and wrote to the official site after Sosa's death: "Te habias entusiasmado y cantado canciones no permitidas, habias abierto las ventanas para que escuchen los que no podian pagar. Estabamos todos euforicos. Pero llegaron ellos con sus armas... Nosotras en fila en el patio, apuntadas, aterradas; vos, tal vez con tu propio miedo, en una oficina donde te hacian escuchar los temas que cantaste, mostrandote tu desobediencia. A las seis de la man~ana, consideraron que ya nos habian dado la leccion y salimos al sol. Sabes que? Valio la pena." You had grown enthusiastic and sung songs that were not permitted, you had opened the windows so those who could not pay could listen. We were all euphoric. But they arrived with their weapons... We in a line in the yard, aimed at, terrified; you, perhaps with your own fear, in an office where they made you listen to the songs you sang, showing you your disobedience. At six in the morning they considered they had given us the lesson and we went out into the sun. You know what? It was worth it. The mechanism is the disobedience as the performance: the windows opened so the people outside could hear, the songs sung that were not permitted, the singer made to listen to her own songs in an office as proof of her disobedience, and the audience that says it was worth it at six in the morning. The year is carried two ways, 1978 by most sources and 1979 by the English Wikipedia, and the number of spectators is CMTV's only, 350, and the musicianguide account says the arrest came because she dared to sing "Cuando tenga la tierra", the call for agrarian reform. The mechanism's consequence is the exile, because after the arrest she could not sing in her own country at all, and the arrest is therefore not an interruption of the career but its hinge. Watch the arrest: when the singer opens the windows so the people outside can hear, and sings the songs that are not permitted, and the soldiers arrest her and the whole audience with her, and make her listen to her own songs as evidence, and the audience walks out at dawn saying it was worth it, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** open the windows and sing the forbidden songs. The arrest is the proof. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M3) also sang a forbidden song, but hers was forbidden by a television program and she was punished by sales; Sosa's was forbidden by a dictatorship and she was punished by arrest, which is why her version is a cell and Pineda's is a market. Libertad (#356 M1) also sang in prisons, but hers were concerts given to prisoners; Sosa's prison was the concert itself, which is why her version is an arrest and Libertad's is a visit.

## M7. The exile that would not sing

She left, and the leaving stopped her voice, and the stopping is the mechanism. In 1979 she went into exile, first in Paris and then in Madrid, and the Spanish Wikipedia states the cost plainly: the exile was very painful for her, her second husband Pocho Mazzitelli had died the year before, in 1978, and she has told that at that moment she came to think of suicide. The musicianguide biography, from the New York Times interview with Larry Rohter, gives the mechanism in her own words: "It was a mental problem, a problem of morale... It wasn't my throat, or anything physical. When you are in exile, you take your suitcase, but there are things that don't fit. There are things in your mind, like colors and smells and childhood attitudes, and there is also the pain and the death you saw. You shouldn't deny those things, because to do so can make you ill." The mechanism is the suitcase that does not fit: the singer takes the suitcase, but the colours and the smells and the childhood attitudes do not fit in it, and the pain and the death she saw do not fit either, and the voice that cannot carry what does not fit stops. She kept recording in exile, A quien doy in 1980 or 1981, with Jose Luis Castin~eira de Dios, "Cuando me acuerdo de mi pais" by Patricio Manns, the Chilean in exile writing about the same pain, "La flor azul", and the first tango she ever recorded, "Los mareados", but the Argentine release censored three of its songs, "Suen~o con serpientes", "Fuego en Anymana" and "Gente humilde", so even the exile's record was censored at home. The Fundacion states the punishment exactly: in theory she could enter and leave the country, she had no judicial case, but she could not sing. "Fue un castigo doble: para ella y para todos los argentinos." It was a double punishment: for her and for all Argentines. Watch the suitcase: when the singer who was arrested on stage leaves for Paris and then Madrid, and her husband has just died, and she thinks of suicide, and she says the problem is not her throat but her morale, and the suitcase does not fit the colours and the smells, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** pack the suitcase and find what does not fit. The morale is the voice. Against the chain: Libertad (#356 M1) also left a country, but hers was a departure from Peru to Mexico, a move toward a new career; Sosa's was a flight from Argentina to Paris, a move away from the only career, which is why her version is an exile and Libertad's is an arrival. Manzanero (#358 M2) also spent ten years behind others, but his ten years were inside the media; Sosa's three years were outside the country, which is why his version is a shadow and hers is an absence.

## M8. The return as a counter-act

She came back and sang thirteen times to full houses under the dictatorship, and the return is the mechanism. On Thursday 18 February 1982, a few months before the dictatorship embarked on the Malvinas War, Mercedes Sosa returned to an Argentine stage in the Teatro Opera in Buenos Aires, and gave thirteen recitals to full houses, and the Spanish Wikipedia calls them a cultural act against the dictatorship and a renewing fact of Argentine popular music, because she included themes and musicians from different currents, folklore, tango and rock nacional. The guests were the litoralen~o Raul Barboza, the pianist Ariel Ramirez, the tanguero Rodolfo Mederos, the rock composer Charly Garcia, the rock singer-songwriter Leon Gieco, the chamamecero Antonio Tarrago Ros, with musical direction by Jose Luis Castin~eira de Dios, the Uruguayan guitarist Omar Espinoza and the percussionist Domingo Cura. The repertoire carried the new songs that would become her emblems: "Como la cigarra" by Maria Elena Walsh, "Tantas veces me mataron, tantas veces me mori, sin embargo estoy aqui resucitando", so many times they killed me, so many times I died, yet here I am, resurrecting; "Solo le pido a Dios" by Leon Gieco; "Maria va" by Tarrago Ros; "Suen~o con serpientes" and "An~os", the first mass diffusion of Silvio Rodriguez and Pablo Milanes in Argentina; and with Charly Garcia, "Cuando ya me empiece a quedar solo", the historic approach of folklore and rock. The mechanism is the audience as the act: "Lo grande fue que estaba toda la gente, mas que amandome a mi, amandose a ellos." The great thing was that all the people were there, more than loving me, loving themselves. And the singer's own discipline: her emotion was so great that to be able to sing she had to ignore the public and not look at it at any moment. The double album Mercedes Sosa en Argentina became an instant best seller, and the facade in ms-08 shows the return as a building, huge neon OPERA letters and a marquee reading MERCEDES SOSA above a crowd. The return had a second half: Admiral Lacoste asked "Quien dio permiso a Mercedes Sosa para estar en mi pais?", and she had to leave again briefly, so the thirteen recitals were a return and a departure at once. Watch the return: when the exiled singer comes back under the dictatorship and sings thirteen times to full houses, and the young rockers share her stage, and she does not look at the audience because the emotion is too great, and the audience is loving itself more than it loves her, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** come back and sing thirteen times. The audience loving itself is the act. Against the chain: Libertad (#356 M11) also came home to sing one song, Schubert's Ave Maria in Zana for a pope; Sosa came home to sing thirteen nights for a dictatorship, which is why her version is a counter-act and Libertad's is a homecoming. Pineda (#357 M12) also celebrated by touring, but hers was a fiftieth anniversary; Sosa's return was a political act under a regime that had banned her, which is why her version is a return and Pineda's is a road.

## M9. The refusal to sing somewhere

She refused to sing in a place until the reason left, and the refusal is the mechanism. Twice she made the same refusal, and both times the refusal is stated as a decision with a condition. After the coup of Tuesday 11 September 1973 in Chile, she swore not to sing in that country while the dictatorship remained in power, and she kept the vow for nineteen years, returning in 1992, after Pinochet had fallen, to sing three times in Vin~a del Mar and Santiago, opening at the Quinta Vergara with "Todavia cantamos" and repeating "Ya cayo, ya cayo!" while the public chanted the chorus of "Todo cambia", and then singing in the Estadio Chile, the stadium where Victor Jara had been tortured and murdered, to honour him. The second refusal is the same shape at home: in 1995 she decided she would not sing again in her native province of Tucuman because the represor Antonio Domingo Bussi had been elected governor, and she kept the vow until Bussi left office on Friday 10 December 1999, returning sixteen days later to say, "Asi como decidi hace muchos an~os no cantar mas en Chile mientras gobernara Pinochet, yo decidi en su momento no volver a cantar mas aqui mientras gobernase Bussi." Just as I decided many years ago not to sing any more in Chile while Pinochet governed, I decided at the time not to sing again here while Bussi governed. The mechanism is the absence as the song: the singer does not sing in the place, and the not-singing is heard, and when she returns the return is the news, "Ya cayo!" and the sixteen days. Watch the refusal: when the singer says she will not sing in Chile while Pinochet governs, and does not sing there for nineteen years, and then opens with "Todavia cantamos" and shouts "Ya cayo!", and says the same about her own province and its governor, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** refuse the place until the reason leaves. The absence is the song. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M2) also refused, but hers was a contract, blonde hair and a miniskirt; Sosa's refusal was a country, which is why her version is a boycott and Pineda's is a contract. Libertad (#356 M4) also waited to sing a forbidden song, but hers waited thirty-six years for a dead teacher; Sosa's waited nineteen years for a fallen dictator, which is why her version is a boycott and Libertad's is a debt.

## M10. The rock that became folklore

She took the rock into the folklore, and the taking is the mechanism. The Spanish Wikipedia gives the confession in her own words, from the documentary Como un pajaro libre: in 1981 she went to see Yellow Submarine in Spain, and she admired it and felt ashamed of herself for having had the prejudice of not seeing it when it premiered, and in the same way she had not listened to Charly Garcia or Nito Mestre, and undoubtedly the same had happened to them with her, and the human being is full of prejudices and preconceptions, and the lack of freedom is felt not only in collective freedom but in the mental freedom of each person. The mechanism is the prejudice confessed and then broken: the folklore singer who had not listened to the rockers invites them onto her stage at the Opera in 1982, Charly Garcia singing "Cuando ya me empiece a quedar solo" with her, and the critic Mariano Blejman says she did more to legitimize rock socially than rock did to interpret the artists of the Nuevo Cancionero, and asks, among today's rockers, who knows who Tejada Gomez was? The taking runs both ways across the career: in 1985 she records Fito Paez's "Yo vengo a ofrecer mi corazon" as the title of her album, in 1993 Fito co-produces Sino, in 1997 she records Alta fidelidad, twelve songs of Charly Garcia, with Alfredo Alcon speaking on "Los sobrevivientes", and on Tuesday 28 January 1997 she closes Cosquin with Charly Garcia, singing "Rezo por vos", "Inconsciente colectivo", "De mi" and his rock version of the Himno Nacional Argentino, to an ovation, and then announces she will not return to Cosquin, tired of the polemics, "Cosquin se acabo para mi. Estoy cansada de las polemicas, y de esta relacion amor-odio con Cosquin." The mechanism's proof is that the rockers came to her and stayed: Garcia was the producer of Alta fidelidad by the enciclopediaiberoamericana account, and the two were frequent guests at each other's concerts and recordings. Watch the taking: when the folklore singer confesses she was ashamed not to have seen Yellow Submarine, and then invites the rocker onto her stage under the dictatorship, and then records a whole album of his songs, and then closes Cosquin with him and says she is finished with Cosquin, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** confess the prejudice and invite the rocker on stage. The rock is part of the songbook. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M4) also refused to be labelled, but her refusal was the voice in every genre; Sosa's refusal was the songbook itself, taking the rock into the folklore, which is why her version is a legitimation and Pineda's is a voice. Manzanero (#358 M11) also refused the label, but his refusal was the catalogue in every rhythm; Sosa's refusal was the prejudice, confessed in public, which is why his version is a catalogue and hers is a confession.

## M11. The three names of the voice

Her voice changed its name as she aged, and the change is the mechanism. The Spanish Wikipedia states it in one paragraph: in her beginnings she had a register close to mezzosoprano, but later it evolved toward contralto. Her vocal register was more than two octaves, and her strong point was the power with which she faced the low notes. Thanks to her dark and warm timbre and to a perfect intonation, she has become one of the most notable voices in the history of Argentine music. The enciclopediaiberoamericana agrees: her initial register was mezzo-soprano, but later it evolved to contralto, always standing out for the power of her voice without neglecting the interpretive nuances. The mechanism is the voice going down, not up: the singer does not climb to the high notes to prove herself, she descends to the low notes and stays there, and the low notes are where the power is. The critic Diego Fischerman, quoted after her death, gives the mechanism its sentence: she cultivated a model of artist in which the mimesis with the public was fundamental, a unique interpreter, with a voice of richness of nuances and exceptional phrasing, and the most finished explication of popular song as a sign of its time. The mechanism has a uniform: the red poncho of the northwest, traditional, worn in ms-04 at Heredia in 2008 and in ms-05 at Managua in 2008, and the black suit with the red scarf, and the gold-yellow shawl of ms-06, and the red and white poncho of the Hasta la victoria cover in ms-03, so the voice that goes down wears the poncho that stays the same, and the Esquire verdict is the mechanism stated as a boast, "Your Spanish may or may not be good, but Mercedes Sosa requires no translation." Watch the descent: when the singer who began as a mezzosoprano is now a contralto, and the low notes are where the power is, and the voice is dark and warm and perfectly in tune, and she wears the same red poncho at seventy-three that she wore at thirty-seven, and the audience needs no translation, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** let the voice go down. The low notes are the power. Against the chain: Libertad (#356 M3) also took the opera, but hers was the high voice as a passport, the soprano climbing; Sosa's was the low voice as a foundation, the contralto descending, which is why her version is a descent and Libertad's is a crossing. Pineda (#357 M4) also had one voice in every genre, but hers was a mezzosoprano, illuminated metal; Sosa's was a contralto, dark and warm, which is why her version is a depth and Pineda's is a light.

## M12. The duet as the continent

She ended with thirty-four duets, and the duet is the mechanism. Cantora, un viaje intimo, her last work, is a double album in which she sings thirty-four songs as duets with outstanding Ibero-American singers, and closes with the Himno Nacional Argentino. Cantora 1 was published in March 2009 and Cantora 2 in June 2009, a little before her death, and the Fundacion gives the making of it: from Thursday 22 May 2008 she began to record, that day the members of the Folkloristas show met in the Estudio Circo Beat to record the version of the Himno Nacional that closes Cantora 2, and alternating with the tours the recording studio became her second home, and so many wanted to be at her side that in the middle of the process it was decided to continue the project with a second CD. The guests are the continent: Joan Manuel Serrat on "Aquellas pequen~as cosas", Luis Alberto Spinetta on "Barro tal vez", Shakira on "La maza", Jorge Drexler on "Sea", Caetano Veloso on "Coracao vagabundo", Soledad on "Agua, fuego, tierra y viento", Leon Gieco on "Himno de mi corazon", Gustavo Cerati on "Zona de promesas", who said he liked that she called him to sing a song together and that it was incredible that she told him she was very nervous to see him, Charly Garcia on "Desarma y sangra", Residente on "Cancion para un nin~o de la calle", Vicentico on "Parao", Lila Downs on "Razon de vivir", and a testimonial account by Tejada Gomez. The mechanism is the duet as a map: each guest is a country and a generation, Spain and Brazil and Chile and Cuba and Mexico and Colombia and Uruguay and Argentina, the young and the old, and the album is the continent singing with one woman, and the hymn at the end is the signature, the whole country after the whole continent. The mechanism's discipline is the body: she was already ill, she sang seated, she could not present Cantora 1 in person at its launch on Tuesday 31 March 2009, and her last recording, in June 2009, was made in a small studio mounted in her house, after a week of study with her singing teacher, "El angel de la bicicleta" and "La soledad". Watch the continent: when the singer who sang other people's songs for fifty years invites thirty-four singers to sing them with her, and the studio becomes her second home, and the project grows from one disc to two because so many want to be at her side, and the last recording is made in her house, and the album closes with the national anthem, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** end with thirty-four duets and the anthem. The continent is the chorus. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M10) also made the duet a family tree, fourteen branches in one album; Sosa's duet is not a tree but a continent, thirty-four branches from every country, which is why her version is a map and Pineda's is a genealogy. Libertad (#356 M5) also made the duet a conversation, trading verses with Manzanero; Sosa's duet is not a conversation but a summons, thirty-four singers coming to her, which is why her version is a continent and Libertad's is a friendship. Manzanero (#358 M9) also made the duet a second career, twenty years of handovers; Sosa's duet is a last work, made in her house while dying, which is why his version is a career and hers is a farewell.

## M13. The women only

She produced a festival of women only, and the producing is the mechanism. In 1988, as producer, she organized one of the most important shows presented in Argentina, Sin Fronteras, which brought together in the Luna Park stadium in Buenos Aires the Argentine Teresa Parodi and Silvina Garre, the Colombian Leonor Gonzalez Mina, the Venezuelan Lilia Vera, the Brazilian Beth Carvalho and the Mexican Amparo Ochoa, besides Mercedes herself, seven women singers from six countries. The enciclopediaiberoamericana calls it the first festival made up only of women artists in Latin America. The mechanism is the producer's chair: the singer who had spent her career being invited, by Cafrune at Cosquin, by Argerich at the Colon, by Pavarotti at Boca Juniors, by the dictatorship's blacklist, by the exile, now invites, and the invitation is the power, seven women on one stage in the biggest indoor stadium in the country. The mechanism has a prehistory in the same year: she and Joan Baez proposed to perform together in Santiago de Chile to support the democratic forces in the campaign of the NO, on the eve of the plebiscite that would decide whether Pinochet kept governing until 1997, and the regime's politicians ordered her entry into Chile to be prohibited, so the women-only festival at home and the banned concert in Chile are the same gesture, women singing together against the men who forbid it. The mechanism's proof is the company: Amparo Ochoa is the same Mexican singer Pineda fell in love with as a student alongside Sosa, so the festival gathers the women the whole chain has been listening to. Watch the producing: when the singer who was always the guest becomes the producer, and puts seven women from six countries on the Luna Park stage, and the same year she and Joan Baez are banned from entering Chile to sing for the NO, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** produce the festival of women only. The invitation is the power. Against the chain: Pineda (#357 M9) also sang in a chord of three, but hers was a trio that lasted ten years and was booked by Sony; Sosa's was a festival she produced herself, one night, seven women, which is why her version is a production and Pineda's is a chord. Libertad (#356 M7) also sang the chord of three; Sosa's chord was seven women from six countries, which is why her version is a festival and Libertad's is a trio.

## M14. The death that was sung

She died, and the country sang her out, and the singing is the mechanism. On Friday 18 September 2009 she entered the Sanatorio de la Trinidad in Palermo, Buenos Aires, for a renal dysfunction that evolved toward cardiorespiratory failure, and her health became critical on Friday 2 October, and she was induced into a pharmacological coma, and she died at 05:17 by the Spanish Wikipedia, 05:15 by the English Wikipedia, on Sunday 4 October 2009, aged 74. The next day, Monday 5 October, her remains were waked in the Congreso Nacional, in the Salon de los Pasos Perdidos, with the president Cristina Fernandez de Kirchner, ministers, religious figures and artists before the coffin, and a multitude of people, and the artists who had to do with her through her life said goodbye to her with one voice singing "Alfonsina y el mar". Three days of national mourning were decreed, and a multitude accompanied the funeral cortege with flowers to the Chacarita cemetery, where her body was cremated as she wished, to scatter her ashes in the three places she loved, Tucuman, Mendoza and Buenos Aires, and Peru and Bolivia put their flags at half-mast, and the president of Brazil, Lula da Silva, sent his minister of culture with a message, "Gracias, Mercedes. Aquella que canto a la vida permanece en los cuatro lados de nuestra America", and the president of Venezuela, Hugo Chavez, published a letter, and the president of Chile, Michelle Bachelet, asked for applause for her. The mechanism is the funeral as a concert: the family published a letter the same day, signed "Familia de Mercedes", saying the great artist was also their grandmother, sister, aunt, mother, and inviting her admirers to say goodbye to her singing, "Lo que mas feliz la hacia a Mercedes era cantar. Y seguramente ella hubiera querido cantarles tambien en este final." What made Mercedes happiest was singing. And surely she would have wanted to sing to you also in this ending. So the wake followed the wish, recordings of her singing transmitted while the people present accompanied them. The mechanism's proof is the prize after death: at the Latin Grammys of 2009 she had three posthumous nominations and won Best Folk Album and Best Recording Package for Cantora 1, and in 2011 Deja la vida volar won again, so the dead singer kept winning. Watch the death: when the singer dies at dawn on a Sunday, and the country wakes her in the Congress, and the artists sing "Alfonsina y el mar" with one voice at the coffin, and the family asks everyone to say goodbye singing, and the ashes go to three places, and the prizes keep coming after death, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** die, and let the country sing you out. The funeral is the concert. Against the chain: Manzanero (#358 M13) also opened his own museum seventeen days before he died, but his was a building that stays open; Sosa's was a singing, a wake in which the people sang, which is why his version is a museum and hers is a concert. Manzanero (#358 M14) also let the song outlive him, the country singing "Esta tarde vi llover" from its windows; Sosa's country sang "Alfonsina y el mar" at her coffin, which is why his version is a window and hers is a wake. Libertad (#356 M12) also left a name, the girls named Tania; Sosa left a voice the whole continent sang back, which is why her version is a chorus and Libertad's is a christening.

## Aesthetic logic

Sosa's aesthetic is the voice as a duty, sung in every genre the song asks for and in every country the song comes from: the zamba, the chacarera, the vidala, the cueca, the tango, the bolero, the rock, the pop, the opera house and the stadium, and the scarcity is the argument, because a singer with no costume, no mask and no character has only the voice to hold the room, and the voice has to be dark enough and low enough and true enough to be believed in every genre and every country. The palette is the one the photographs give: the black and white of ms-01 and ms-07 against the grey studio wall and the piano; the red poncho of ms-02 against the dark stage and the pale drum skin; the red and white poncho of ms-03 against the pale yellow of the record cover; the red poncho of ms-04 against the black stage; the black suit and red scarf of ms-05 against the black of Managua; the gold-yellow shawl of ms-06 against the blue backdrop of the Plaza de Mayo; the black and white zigzag poncho of ms-07 against the grand piano; the blue neon of ms-08 against the night crowd. Where Pineda's logic was one country sung by one voice in every genre, and Libertad's was two countries sung by one voice in one night, and Manzanero's was every country sung by every voice in one song, Sosa's is every country sung by one voice in every song, and the difference is the duty: Pineda carries the song, Libertad carries the country, Manzanero carries nothing because the song carries itself, and Sosa carries the people, because the cantora is the one who must, which is why her version is a duty and theirs are a voice and a set list and a catalogue.
