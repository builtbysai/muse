# Rogelio Polesello: Study Notes

**Site:** Rogelio Polesello, 26 July 1939 - 6 July 2014, Buenos Aires -- Argentine painter, muralist and sculptor, "best known for making Op art (or optical art) known in Latin America" (C8601, C8701). He died aged 74: the English Wikipedia entry says 75, but the Spanish Wikipedia infobox and the Argentine Chamber of Deputies resolution say 74, and the arithmetic agrees (born 26 July 1939, died 6 July 2014, twenty days short of his 75th birthday); this study prints 74 and carries the conflict (see Corrections). He never lived, studied or worked in Paris -- no source read records a Paris move, and the Paris biography in the task brief belongs to Armando Durante (#632), not to him (C8601, C8602, C8603, C8604, C8606). His base was Buenos Aires for his whole life, with solo shows and travel in Washington, Caracas, Mayaguez, Bogota, New York, Mexico City, Sao Paulo and Cordoba (C8602, C8611). His French connection was institutional, not residential: the Prix Georges Braque was sponsored by the French embassy in Argentina, and his 1968 first-prize work was built on the colours of the French flag (C8604). He was the home-front Op painter: while Durante and the GRAV circle worked the same geometry in Paris, Polesello built it in Buenos Aires from 1959, in print-shop methods, and from 1967 in carved acrylic that made the picture into a lens.

Polesello was born in Buenos Aires (C8701), painted there, and is "best known for making Op art (or optical art) known in Latin America" (C8701); his first solo exhibition at Galeria Peuser in 1959 already followed Op Art aesthetics based on Gestalt theories (C8701). Wording note: "brought Op Art home" implies a journey -- carrying it back from abroad -- and no source read shows Polesello travelling to Paris and importing Op Art. His Op work developed in Buenos Aires from 1959 under Vasarely's influence, expressed at Peuser (C8704). "Brought home" is defensible as shorthand for "made known in Latin America" (C8701), but the literal journey is unattested and must not be read into the study. The substance holds; the verb is loose.

D3 "whose Gestalt geometries won the same Prix Georges Braque and filled the Di Tella rooms Durante had left behind" -- NON-HOLD. The clause has two halves. The first half holds; the second fails, and the failure is factual, not stylistic. (a) "Gestalt geometries won the same Prix Georges Braque" -- HOLDS. Polesello's 1959 Peuser paintings were based on Gestalt theories (C8701); he won the First Prize Georges Braque (C8704), listed as the Premio George Braque at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in 1968 (C8702). Durante won the same prize's First Prize in painting in 1966, for his "inestabilidades luminosas" (C8710). Same prize, two years apart, both first prizes. This half is exact. (b) "filled the Di Tella rooms Durante had left behind" -- FAILS. Polesello did hold a solo show at the Centro de Artes Visuales of the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella in 1969 (C8702, C8706). But Durante never occupied Di Tella rooms to leave behind. Durante's only Di Tella connection was as an invitee/participant in the 1965 Premio Nacional -- he did not win it; Carlos Silva did (C8710). Durante was in Paris from 1966 to 1974 (C8710); what he left behind in 1966 was Buenos Aires, not the Di Tella. The rooms Polesello filled in 1969 were Jorge Romero Brest's Centro de Artes Visuales (C8708), not rooms vacated by Durante. The sketch invents a tenancy (Durante holding Di Tella rooms) and a succession (Polesello filling them after him). Neither is attested anywhere read. The 1965 shared prize room is the real link between the two men (C8710), and it is a shared room, not a handed one. ## The life: the advertising boy who made Op Art known in Latin America

Rogelio Polesello was born in Buenos Aires on 26 July 1939 (C8601). No source read gives his parents' names, his childhood address, or any sibling other than a brother named Osvaldo who appears only in a 2014 funeral report (C8606). At fifteen he began working in an advertising agency (C8606), and from an early age he worked in advertising design, "which led him to experiences beyond the art world" (C8602). The graphic-design background runs through his whole method: airbrush, silkscreen, halftone, colour transfer -- the print shop as the painter's studio (C8606, C8676).

He studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Manuel Belgrano and the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon, both in Buenos Aires, and in 1958 received a degree in printmaking, drawing and painting from the Prilidiano Pueyrredon school (C8601, C8602). A second institutional source gives the same 1958 date and the same qualification, described as Professor of printmaking, drawing and illustration (C8610). Davis Publications says he graduated in 1959 (C8608); this study follows 1958, the version given by the two institutional sources, and carries 1959 as unresolved. No source read names a teacher of his.

In 1959 he joined the Asociacion Arte Nuevo, founded by Aldo Pellegrini and Carmelo Arden Quin (C8601), and held his first solo exhibition at the Galeria Peuser, Buenos Aires (C8601, C8602). The paintings in that first solo show followed the aesthetics of Op Art and were based on Gestalt theories: geometric forms, generally black and white, added to or subtracted from the whole by perceptive principles that produced specific optical effects (C8601, C8681). MALBA describes the 1959 Peuser works as geometric paintings with a constructivist influence that explored the possibilities of Op art (C8602), and the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires states that at Peuser his admiration for Victor Vasarely was manifested (C8603). His early solo sequence ran Peuser 1959, Pizarro 1960, Lirolay 1961, Rubbers 1962, Bonino 1966 (C8611). Davis Publications says that at the age of 22 he had his first solo painting exhibition at the Pan American Union in Washington, DC (C8608); that cannot stand beside the 1959 Peuser date, since he turned 22 in July 1961 and the Pan American Union show is dated 1961 in the institutional lists (C8602, C8603). The Pan American Union show was an early solo show, not his first; Peuser 1959 was first.

The 1960s carried him out of Buenos Aires without ever moving him out of it. He took part in the II American Art Biennial, Cordoba, 1964, the Salon Esso de Artistas Plasticos de America Latina at the Pan American Union, Washington, 1965, and the 8th Sao Paulo Biennial, 1965 (C8602, C8611); in 1966 he showed in "The Emergent Decade: Latin American Painters and Painting in the 60s" at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York (C8611), and held solo shows at the Museo de Bellas Artes, Caracas, in 1966 and 1968 (C8602). In 1967 he showed at the Biblioteca Luis Angel Arango of the Banco de la Republica, Bogota (C8602).

The Prix Georges Braque is the one prize in his file that comes with a scene. He won the First Prize in 1968 (C8603, C8604); the prize was a biennial prize established in 1963 by the French Embassy in Argentina (C8613). In June 1968, soon after the events of May in France, a censorship clause in the official announcement triggered a joint reaction from avant-garde groups in Rosario and Buenos Aires (C8604). The awards ceremony was held on 16 July 1968 in the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes (C8604). Protesting artists disrupted the ceremony for twenty minutes, throwing leaflets, rotten eggs and stink bombs at officials and at the first-prize work by Polesello, who had created it based on the colours of the French flag (C8604). Nine people were arrested and later sentenced to thirty days in jail (C8604). The jury included Antonio Berni, Samuel Oliver, Aldo Pellegrini and Jorge Romero Brest, among others (C8604). The protest is not a footnote to the prize; it is the clearest evidence in the file that his geometry was read, in Buenos Aires in 1968, as an official art -- a French-flag painting, hung in the national museum, egged by the avant-garde. His other distinctions are thinner in the record: the Grand Prize of Honor of the National Salon of the Arts and the First Prize ESSO, both with years unattested (C8603); the ESSO year is disputed, 1965 (MALBA) against 1966 (Arte-Online), and 1965 is better supported (C8602, C8609); two Konex Awards, 1982 and 2012, with categories unattested (C8601); Distinguished Personality of the Autonomous City of Buenos Aires, 2013 (C8606).

His solo exhibition at the Centro de Artes Visuales of the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, Buenos Aires, was held in 1969 (C8602, C8603, C8611). The Di Tella tie was personal as well as institutional: "Thanks to his tie to the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella's Center of Visual Arts directed by Jorge Romero Brest, he was able to make his way out into the world at a young age" (C8708). MALBA places him in "the mythical generation of artists that emerged around the Instituto Di Tella in the '60s" (C8707). No source read lists the works in the 1969 Di Tella show, its dates within the year, or its reception.

The 1970s and after are a list, not a story, in the sources read. Solo shows listed by MALBA after 1969: Center for Inter American Relations, New York (1973); Museo de Arte Moderno, Bogota (1973); Museo de Arte Moderno-Bosque de Chapultepec, Mexico City (1973); Palais de Glace, Buenos Aires (1995, "Progresiones"); Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires (2000, anthological); Museo Jose Luis Cuevas, Mexico City (2002); Centro Cultural Recoleta, Buenos Aires (2005) (C8602, C8611). Wikipedia dates the Mexico City show 1974, against MALBA's 1973; unresolved. MALBA notes that he carried out interdisciplinary work related to architecture, public-space intervention, advertising, environmental and textile design (C8603), and his "Eclipse" project (2001-2002) was a commissioned work for Ezeiza International Airport, Buenos Aires (C8603). The same source says his career was guided until his last days by visual research, which he never stopped undertaking (C8603).

He died on 6 July 2014 in Buenos Aires (C8601, C8606). Cause of death is given as a heart attack by both Wikipedia entries and by the El Dia obituary cited in the English entry (C8601); the Chamber of Deputies condolence text says in its reasoning that he died "as a consequence of a bacterial complication" (C8606) -- a parliamentary text, not a medical source, and this study follows heart attack. His wake began 8 July 2014 and the coffin was taken on 10 July from the legislature building to a cemetery in Pilar, Buenos Aires province, attended by his widow Nana, his brother Osvaldo, and his niece Daniela, among family, friends and admirers (C8606).

One year after his death MALBA held the anthological exhibition "Polesello joven 1958-1974", 26 June - 12 October 2015, curator Mercedes Casanegra, 120 works from public and private collections in Argentina and abroad (C8602). Work on the show began alongside the artist more than two years before it opened; a MALBA team began researching and cataloguing the Polesello archive in late 2012, and the archive begins in 1957 (C8602). The show was divided into five sections, from 1958-1959 monotypes and temperas, through early-1960s air-gun and metal-sheet experiments, the 1964-1966 period, carved acrylic from 1967, and large carved acrylic plates, columns and blocks concerned with space and viewer participation (C8602). A bilingual catalogue was published (C8602). The show is anthological and retrospective in substance, but its formal title covers only 1958-1974, not a full life. A posthumous show, "Mas aqui. Rogelio Polesello 1970-2000", curated by Santiago Villanueva, was on view at the Centro Cultural Borges until 21 December 2025 (C8612, C8709).

His work is listed as held by MoMA New York, the Guggenheim New York, the Art Museum of the Americas, the Blanton, the Lowe, the Museo de Arte Moderno de Bogota, the Banco de la Republica, the Museo de Bellas Artes de Caracas, the MNBA, the MAMBA, MALBA and MACBA (C8683), with Wikipedia adding Albright-Knox, La Tertulia, Cisneros Fontanals, Museo Rufino Tamayo and the Bronx Museum (C8611). The one holding verified on a museum's own record in this study is MoMA's: Untitled, 1966, synthetic polymer paint airbrushed on paper, 99.9 x 64.7 cm, object 6.1967, Department of Drawings and Prints, gift of Emilio del Junco (by exchange) (C8605). LACMA holds an Untitled (Sin titulo), c. 1974, purchased with funds from the Bernard and Edith Lewin Collection of Mexican Art Deaccession Fund (C8612, C8675). The Guggenheim holding is listed by MALBA, the Museo Moderno and Wikipedia (C8602, C8603, C8611), but no Guggenheim object record was found in this run, and his 1966 Guggenheim presence was as a participant in "The Emergent Decade" group show, which is exhibition, not acquisition (C8611). Carry the Guggenheim as listed, not verified.

## M1 -- Carved acrylic as a lens: the ring that is not painted on

The one fully attested kinetic-adjacent work, W10, Sin Titulo, 1968, is a square plate of saturated magenta-pink acrylic, 100 x 100 x 5 cm, hung by two thin wires, with rounded corners (C8656). A large concentric carving dominates: a white ring around a pink disc with a white centre spot, like a target or a lens. The carved groove catches light as a bright white annulus; the uncarved pink field is flat and intense. The study author viewed this image directly and confirms the description: the ring is light, not paint (C8754). The mechanism is the whole work: a groove carved into transparent coloured acrylic acts as a concave or convex lens, and light through it reads as a bright white ring that is not painted on. MALBA says his early carved-acrylic works from 1967 were mainly magnifying glasses which function in space by distorting his own paintings (C8654). A review says his mostly transparent engraved-acrylic sculptures with concave and convex ovals produce an optical effect that reflects and alters the perception of the surroundings (C8678). The viewer moves, the ring moves: the effect is parallax-dependent, not fixed in the image plane. W12, Sin titulo, 1970, is the same machine at architectural scale: a tall pink plate, 215 x 103 x 3 cm, standing on the floor, rows of carved white ovals of varying size in loose horizontal bands, each oval with a soft halo where the carving curves, and the plate casting a dotted shadow of light ovals on the floor to its right -- a second, projected version of the pattern (C8657). The ovals are not painted: they are concave/convex carvings that focus and scatter light, so the white centres are refracted daylight. W15, Untitled, c. 1974 (LACMA), is a tall crimson-red freestanding panel with two symmetrical spiral arrangements of circular discs and cut-outs in amber, magenta, yellow and white, where some discs are holes showing white through and others are coloured inlays (C8675). MALBA's fifth section is exactly this: large carved acrylic plates, transparent and coloured, columns and blocks, with multiple concave and convex carvings, tied to experiments in space and viewer participation (C8654). W14, Untitled, 1974, carved acrylic, 219.1 x 104.8 x 1.9 cm, is the same scale class by medium, listing-only (C8666).

**Takeaway:** the lens is the work and the paint is the excuse. A piece that uses Polesello should carve a groove into a coloured transparent field and let light make the ring, never draw one; a drawn ring is a target, and a flat field is a poster. The highlight must move when the viewer moves.

## M2 -- The rotated fan: one fan, turned four times, closing into a tunnel

W11, Untitled, 1970, acrylic on canvas, 115 x 122 cm (C8658), is the clearest flat-painting statement of his Gestalt mechanism, and the study author viewed it directly and confirms leg 2's description (C8754). A green ground holds a rotating square spiral of orange-red bars. Four fans of parallel bars, one per side, each fan rotating about 90 degrees from the last, converge toward a green centre. Bar width decreases toward the centre; spacing tightens as the bars converge. Three thin accent bars interrupt the pattern: one purple, one blue, one dark blue, plus a small red block near the centre and a thin yellow line at the left edge. The mechanism: each fan is a set of parallel bars at a fixed angle; the four fans are the same fan rotated, so the eye reads a pinwheel or a tunnel receding into the centre. The bars get narrower and closer together toward the middle, which reads as perspective depth and as acceleration. The complementary pairing (orange-red bars on green ground) maximises vibration at every edge. The three thin accent bars in unrelated hues break the system once each, which stops the pattern from becoming a pure texture and gives the eye a place to land. The whole -- a vortex -- is in no single bar. It is a complete parametric recipe: fan(angle, bar count, width falloff toward centre, one accent bar per quadrant).

**Takeaway:** one unit, rotated and shrunk, is the whole picture. A piece should take a single fan of parallel bars, rotate it four times around a centre, let the bars narrow as they converge, and break the symmetry exactly once per quadrant with a thin bar in an unrelated hue; a random scatter of bars is not a fan, and a fan with no convergence is a fence.

## M3 -- Concentric arcs clipped by polygons: the circles the eye cannot finish

W16, Magico premonitorio, 1974, acrylic on canvas, 130 x 130 cm (C8660), is a square of interlocking large triangles and pentagons in blues, from near-white to deep indigo, on a dark navy ground. Inside every large shape, fine concentric arcs run in stepped bands of blue, all sharing the same centre points, so the arcs continue across shape boundaries and are cut by them. The study author viewed this image directly and confirms the description (C8754). The mechanism is two grids overlaid and clipped. Grid one is a set of concentric circles (stepped value bands, light to dark blue). Grid two is a set of large straight-edged polygons (triangles, pentagons). The circles are drawn first; the polygons cut them, so each polygon contains a fragment of arc pattern that does not match its neighbours. The eye tries to complete the circles across the cuts and fails, which produces a shimmering, faceted, almost crystalline depth. Value steps are regular (roughly equal bands), so the arcs read as a topographic map of a curved surface that is not there. A supplementary MCMC plate from c. 1974, viewed as the work itself, uses the identical machine in brown, tan and cream instead of blue: same polygon clipping, same concentric arcs, different hue family (C8661). That pair is the strongest evidence of serial variation by palette alone: the geometry is separable from the colour.

**Takeaway:** clip the circles and the eye does the work. A piece should draw concentric arcs in stepped value bands, cut them with large straight-edged polygons, and let each polygon hold an arc fragment that does not continue into its neighbour; arcs that continue cleanly across the cuts are wallpaper, and polygons with no arcs inside are just shards. Swap the whole hue family and keep the geometry identical, and the piece is still the same piece.

## M4 -- The grid with one modulated parameter: a fold made of nothing but width

Sin titulo, 2001, acrylic on canvas, 100 x 100 cm (C8659), viewed as the work itself as a supplementary plate: a black field with a strict grid of white parallelograms that shear progressively -- full parallelograms at the left and right edges, narrowing to thin vertical slivers in the middle columns, then widening again. The grid is regular in row spacing but the cell width is modulated by a diagonal shear, so the white cells appear to fold or to recede down a diagonal valley. The mechanism is the purest grid-modulation in the viewed set: a single grid with one parameter (cell width) varied as a function of column position, producing a moire-like fold from a static print. Nothing else changes. No colour, no rotation, no second grid.

**Takeaway:** change one thing across a strict grid and the grid bends. A piece should lay a regular lattice and vary exactly one parameter across it -- cell width as a smooth valley function of column -- and let the fold appear by itself; two varying parameters is decoration, and a grid with no variation is wallpaper.

## M5 -- The spiral of decreasing discs: a colour cycle that walks inward

W15, Untitled, c. 1974 (LACMA), is a tall crimson-red freestanding panel on two small feet, with two identical spiral clusters of discs, one above the other, each spiralling inward from large discs at the rim to tiny discs at the centre (C8675). Disc colours cycle amber, magenta, yellow and white; some discs are holes showing white through, others are coloured inlays. The spirals are mirror-symmetric top and bottom. The mechanism is two spirals, one rule: disc size decreases and disc spacing tightens as the spiral winds inward, while colour cycles through a fixed four-colour order, holes versus inlays alternate, and the two spirals are stacked vertically and mirror each other, so the panel reads as one symmetric organism rather than two separate motifs. Holes versus inlays alternate, so some discs are light passing through and some are colour sitting in the surface: the panel mixes transmission and reflection in one grid. This is the most complex serial machine in the viewed set, and the closest to a generative algorithm: size = f(angle along spiral), colour = angle mod 4, hole/inlay = alternating step, mirror across the horizontal midline.

**Takeaway:** one spiral, one colour cycle, one mirror. A piece should place discs along an inward spiral, shrink them and tighten their spacing as the angle grows, cycle colour through a fixed four-colour order, alternate holes and inlays, and mirror the whole spiral across the midline; a spiral with random colours is a scatter, and a single spiral with no mirror is half a panel.

## M6 -- Gestalt addition and subtraction: the figure is the residue

The 1959 rule is stated directly: geometric forms, generally in black and white, added to or subtracted from the whole according to perceptive principles that produced specific optical effects (C8681). Marte (W01), 1961, oil on canvas, 100 x 100 cm (C8662), adds dots to a dark whole until a field emerges: a near-black square covered in dense horizontal rows of small raised dots, blue-black on black, with a faint horizontal band across the middle and a thin teal-blue glow along the bottom edge, the dots irregular in spacing and relief, not a machined grid. It is the earliest dated work in the catalogue and the least hard-edged: the optical effect comes from texture and near-threshold value difference, not from flat complementary colour, and a contemporary review calls Marte atypical for its low blue tones and irregular dots (C8678). W20, Untitled, 2007, acrylic on canvas, 100 x 100 cm (C8663), subtracts white shapes from a black whole until a figure emerges: a black ground with large white curved and angular shapes that interlock like cut paper -- a big white wedge top right, a white horizontal bar lower left, white triangles at the bottom -- and small flat colour accents sitting on the black: a red trapezoid, a blue rectangle, a purple square, a green strip at the top edge, a yellow sliver, a pink corner. No grid, no repeated unit, no rotation. The mechanism is now composition by a few large interlocking shapes plus sparse colour accents, closer to cutout collage than to Op pattern, and the Borges 2025 account of 1980s cutouts and fragments (C8679) describes an earlier phase of the same loosening, so W20 looks like the late continuation of that direction, not a sudden break. In both works the figure is the residue of the operation, not a drawn shape.

**Takeaway:** add or subtract, never draw the figure. A piece should start from a whole -- a dark field, a black ground -- and add one unit type or cut one shape type out of it, and let the figure be what is left; a figure drawn on top of the ground is illustration, and a field with nothing added or taken away is empty.

## M7 -- Transparency, multiplication and print layering: one pigment per layer

Faz A (W04), 1964, oil on canvas, 161.6 x 129.9 cm, Lowe Art Museum, object 70.024.015, gift of Esso Inter-America (C8673), is the freest, most painterly work in the catalogue: a large orange field crossed by a diagonal yellow beam; a black disc at top centre; a black-and-white halftone dot grid; a magenta-blue diagonal bar; pink and lilac washes at the edges; a small yellow halftone rectangle near the bottom. Edges are soft and airbrushed, not hard-masked. The mechanism is transparency and multiplication: overlapping translucent colour fields create new hues where they cross, and the halftone dot grids (a print technique) sit on top of painted washes. The diagonal beam reads as light passing through the picture plane. This matches the Google Arts and Culture account of his early method: adapting printing techniques, airbrushing and colour transfer, to enhance optical effects of light and colour, and exploring transparency, multiplication and movement (C8676). In "Arco iris", colours (cool blues, warm reds and yellows) are overlaid in dizzying geometric patterns to create a shimmering, kaleidoscopic effect, and the method is intrinsic to screenprinting, in which each pigment is applied separately, creating a multi-layered surface of variable chromatic densities and saturations (C8676). The date and dimensions of Arco iris are unattested, and no image of it was viewed; carry the method, not a picture of it. W09, Untitled, 1966 (MoMA, object 6.1967), is synthetic polymer paint airbrushed on paper (C8664) -- airbrushed soft edges with no brush mark, which is how a hard geometric pattern can still read as glowing -- but MoMA states "Image not available" for the record, so its composition is not attested.

**Takeaway:** layer translucent pigments, one per layer, and let the overlaps make the new colours. A piece should build its image from translucent shapes with multiply blending, each pigment its own layer, halftone grids sitting on washes; opaque shapes on a ground are not printing, and a gradient is not a layer.

## M8 -- Movement in stillness: the sensation the machines are for

Mercedes Casanegra is quoted as saying his works searched for movement in stillness, applying kinetics to create vibrations to achieve a sort of "lyrical resonance", thus generating a form of movement in the artworks and a change in perception within the viewer (C8677). This is critical language describing the intended effect of M1-M7, not a separate mechanism, and it is recorded here because it names the target sensation for any generative prompt: the picture should feel as if it moves while staying still. The MALBA 2015 show was built, in part, to settle "unfinished business" around his parallel engagement with the new design and art, and with the art-design-industry triad -- "for Polesello, design was at the limit of an industrial art" (C8706). The advertising boy never left the work: the air gun, the metal sheet, the halftone grid, the silkscreen layer, the carved groove are all print-shop tools turned into painting, and the picture they make is a machine for changing what the viewer sees without anything in it moving.

**Takeaway:** stillness that moves is the test, not a style. A piece that uses Polesello should be judged by one question: does the flat image seem to move while nothing in it moves? If the answer needs animation to be yes, the piece has missed him; the movement must be in the geometry, the groove, or the layer, not in the playback.

## The aesthetic logic: the lens, not the depiction, and the home-front Op painter

Polesello's work has one subject and it is not geometry. It is the picture that moves while staying still, and the maker who never left home to find it. The early years are the print shop: dots added to a dark field in Marte, translucent washes and halftone grids in Faz A, numbered series (No. 14 in two media, 31 in 1965) reworking one motif across medium and scale, airbrushed edges where a hard mask would have been easier. The middle years are the break out of the picture plane: from 1967 the carved acrylic, mainly magnifying glasses which function in space by distorting his own paintings (C8654), and two parallel lines that never merge -- flat acrylic paintings with rotating fans and clipped arcs, and freestanding carved plates, columns and blocks up to 219 cm tall, with multiple concave and convex carvings, tied to space and viewer participation (C8654). The late years are the loosening: 1980s cutouts and fragments (C8679), a return to oil in a wide 1:2 format in 1982, and by 2007 a black ground with a few large interlocking white shapes and sparse flat colour accents, the Op machine become a collage machine. What holds the three phases together is the method, not the look: one unit, varied by one rule, and the eye doing the rest. The Gestalt rule of 1959 -- forms added to or subtracted from the whole by perceptive principles -- is still the rule in 2001, when a grid of white parallelograms is folded by varying one parameter, cell width, across its columns. And what holds him apart from Durante, his doorway predecessor, is the geography of the same idea: Durante took the geometry to Paris, built light boxes on Vasarely's principles, quit the movement in disgust at the end of 1973 and spent twenty-three years on something else; Polesello stayed in Buenos Aires, took the same Vasarely admiration at the same 1959 debut, and spent fifty-five years refining the same machine -- the flat picture that vibrates, then the carved plate that refracts, then the black ground that subtracts -- until his last days, guided, as the Museo Moderno puts it, by visual research he never stopped undertaking (C8603). Colour, for him, is a small set turned systematically: orange against green, blue against indigo, crimson against amber, magenta and yellow, black against white. He never needs more colours than the rule holds, because the variation does the work that a bigger palette would do. Subtraction is the design: one fan, one grid, one spiral, one groove, one palette swap, and the patience to let the viewer's eye finish what the artist started -- and the discipline, unlike Durante, never to walk away from the machine (C8755).

## Network

Arte Nuevo -- the founding link: in 1959 Polesello joined the Asociacion Arte Nuevo, founded by Aldo Pellegrini and Carmelo Arden Quin (C8701). Arden Quin's own biography confirms the other side: he returned to Argentina in 1954 and together with Pellegrini founded the Asociacion Arte Nuevo, whose first exhibition was at the Galeria Van Riel in 1955 (C8717). Founders 1954-55, Polesello joins 1959. Arden Quin is proposed for #634 (alternate 2).

Victor Vasarely (#619) -- influence, no meeting attested: Polesello's first solo show at Peuser in 1959 was where "his admiration for Victor Vasarely was manifested" (C8704); his geometry then took references from the New Abstraction "with resources of the optical artists, like the offset of geometric forms, with which it produced a strong effect of instability" (C8705). No source read records a meeting, a letter, or a shared exhibition between the two men; the link is influence, attested by Polesello's own show, not contact. Do not upgrade it to a relationship.
