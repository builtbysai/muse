# Paul Cezanne: Study Notes

**Site:** Paul Cezanne (1839-1906), French painter -- born Saturday 19 January 1839 at 28 rue de l'Opera, Aix-en-Provence, the natural son of Louis-Auguste Cezanne (1798-1886), then a hatter, later a banker, and Anne-Elisabeth-Honorine Aubert (1814-1897), legitimised only when his parents married on Monday 29 January 1844; schooled at the College Bourbon, Aix, where he met Emile Zola in 1852 and the two with Baptistin Baille called themselves "Les Trois Inseparables"; law at his father's wish from 1858, drawing at the free municipal school under Joseph Gibert; Paris in April 1861, rejected by the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, at the free Academie Suisse where he met Camille Pissarro; back to Aix, back to Paris in late 1862, under Courbet and Delacroix; shown in the Salon des Refuses of 1863 and rejected by the Salon every year 1864-1869; the dark period of 1867-1870, palette-knife portraits and violent literary subjects in black, brown and Prussian blue; met Marie-Hortense Fiquet in 1869, son Paul born Thursday 4 January 1872, the liaison hidden from his father for fear of the allowance; Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise 1872-1874 under Pissarro, who told him to paint only with the three primary colours and their deviations, no black, no linear contouring; exhibited with the Impressionists in 1874 and 1877 and never again; the collector Victor Chocquet from 1875; Gardanne 1885-1886 and L'Estaque 1883-1885, the faceted constructive period; married Hortense on Wednesday 28 April 1886 in Aix to legitimise the fourteen-year-old Paul; his father died in October 1886 and left him an income of 25,000 francs, ending the money worry; Zola's L'Oeuvre of March 1886 cooled the forty-year friendship without quite ending it; diabetes from 1890; the Card Players begun 1890-1892 and the Large Bathers begun about 1895; the first solo show at Ambroise Vollard's gallery in November-December 1895, about 50 works shown of nearly 150 Cezanne had sent as a package; the 1904 Salon d'Automne gave him a whole room, 15 October-15 November 1904; Emile Bernard visited for a month in February 1904 and published the letters that became his doctrine; a studio built at Les Lauves in 1901-1902; caught in a storm while painting on Monday 15 October 1906, collapsed on the way home, worked once more in the garden on 16 October on the Portrait of the Gardener Vallier, and died of pneumonia on Monday 22 October 1906, aged 67, at his apartment at 23 rue Boulegon, Aix; buried at the Saint-Pierre Cemetery, Aix; the two posthumous retrospectives of 1907 (Bernheim-Jeune, 79 watercolours, 17-29 June; Salon d'Automne, 49 paintings and 7 watercolours, 5 October-15 November) made him the hinge of Cubism, and Picasso's and Matisse's later words -- "Cezanne is the father of us all" -- made him the father of modern art. Rewald's catalogue counts 954 oil paintings and 645 watercolours, and around 1,400 drawings, most from 18 sketchbooks. ## M1. Modulation, Not Modelling
Volume and depth are built by stepping colour through neighbouring hues -- warm to cool -- instead of by lightening and darkening a single local colour (chiaroscuro) or by outline. The reported wording, via Gasquet and Bernard and quoted by Deleuze, is: one should not say "to model", and even more so one should not say "to mold", one should say "to modulate"; "Contrasts and harmonies of tone -- there you have the secret of drawing and modeling... Modeling, there is only that; one ought not to say model, but to modulate." That wording is SECONDHAND, not a dated letter, and the study does not present it as verbatim. Emile Bernard's account of watching him work is the mechanical evidence: "He began with the shadow parts and with one spot, on which he put a second, larger one, then a third, until all these shades, covering each other, modelled the object with their colouring." What it IS: the object is modelled by laying one coloured patch over another until the patches themselves are the volume -- the shadow is a colour, not a darkening. **Takeaway:** build an apple that turns from red to orange to green-blue across its face with no black shadow anywhere; let the picture read as solid from across the room and as flat colour tiles up close, and let the volume appear only because the hues step.

## M2. The Constructive Stroke
The picture is built from small, regular, roughly parallel patches of paint laid side by side like bricks or dry-stone walling. Julian Bell's image, in the London Review of Books, is that Cezanne "came to treat it more like dry stone walling... Could pigment be correspondingly concrete?" Each stroke is a unit of colour and a unit of structure at once; form emerges from the accumulation, not from drawing first and filling in. Wikipedia's summary is the same: "planes of colour and small brushstrokes that build up to form complex fields", and "Cezanne developed the composition from individual dabs of paint spread across the canvas, from which the form and volume of the object gradually build up." What it IS: the wall is built stroke by stroke, and the drawing is the sum of the strokes, not a line underneath them. **Takeaway:** let a hillside or a tablecloth resolve into a rhythm of repeated diagonal marks; keep the surface visibly worked, and let the picture feel built rather than depicted -- the viewer must be able to see the building.

## M3. The Passage
Contours are left open or shared between object and background, so that one form passes into the next instead of being sealed by outline. In the Sainte-Victoire series "the edges of the houses in the valley often bleed into the surrounding fields, and the mountain edge blends into the sky". In the still lifes, a table edge does not continue cleanly behind the bottle; the cloth hides the centre but cannot make the two sides coincide. What it IS: the edge is a negotiation, not a boundary -- the mountain and the sky share the same blue, separated only by a drawn line, and the eye cannot decide where the object ends and the air begins. **Takeaway:** draw a mountain whose edge is the same blue as the sky beside it, so that only the drawn line separates them; let a table edge fail to meet itself behind a bottle, and let the picture stand up anyway.

## M4. Multiple Viewpoints
A single picture combines views taken from more than one position, as a person moving and looking with two eyes actually sees, instead of the single fixed viewpoint of Renaissance perspective. Wikipedia's account is that Cezanne explored "binocular vision, rendering slightly different, yet simultaneous visual perceptions of the same phenomena", an interest that came from the stereoscopy obsession of his era and from reading Hippolyte Taine's theory of spatial perception. In The Basket of Apples the cookies are seen from the side and slightly from above, the basket from the side, the table from above and in front, all at once; the Art Institute catalogue finds it impossible to match the two sides of the table. What it IS: each object is drawn from the angle that shows it best, and the picture holds together because the angles agree in colour and weight, not because they agree in geometry. **Takeaway:** tilt a table so its left edge cannot meet its right edge, lean the bottle, show the plate from above and the pitcher from the front -- and let the still life stand, because every object was drawn from the angle that shows it best.

## M5. Cylinder, Sphere, Cone
The doctrine, in Cezanne's own dated words to Emile Bernard, 15 April 1904 -- VERIFIED VERBATIM from the Danchev/Rewald editions: "Treat nature by means of the cylinder, the sphere, the cone, everything brought into proper perspective so that each side of an object, of a plane, leads to a central point. Lines parallel to the horizon give breadth... Lines perpendicular to this horizon give depth. But nature for us men is more in depth than on the surface, hence the need to introduce into our light vibrations, represented by the reds and yellows, a sufficient amount of blue tints to give a feeling of air." The letter opens "Allow me to repeat what I said when you were here" -- it restates spoken advice, not a first declaration. Two cautions travel with it: Bernard's published version of the formula differs from the autograph text, and the historyofart biography warns against tracing the formula back to his Aix drawing-school curriculum, for which there is no surviving evidence. And the counterweight, secondhand via Gasquet: "Everything I am telling you about the sphere, the cone, cylinder, concave shadow -- on mornings when I'm tired these notions of mine get me going, they stimulate me, I soon forget them once I start using my eyes." What it IS: geometry as a warm-up and a means of building depth, not a diagram to impose -- a tree trunk reads as a cylinder, an apple as a sphere, a mountain as a cone, and the whole landscape becomes an arrangement of a few solid volumes, forgotten the moment the looking starts. **Takeaway:** build a landscape out of three volumes only -- cylinder, sphere, cone -- and then let the painter forget the geometry the moment his eyes start working; the volumes must survive as structure, not as a lesson.

## M6. Colour as Structure
Depth is made by colour temperature, not by perspective lines: warm colours advance, cool colours recede, and blue in particular carries distance and air. The 15 April 1904 letter says it plainly -- introduce "a sufficient amount of blue tints to give a feeling of air" among the reds and yellows -- and the doctrine runs through the letters: "the main thing in a picture is the meeting of the distance", as reported from the April 1906 visit of Karl Ernst Osthaus, and "atmosphere makes colour sensations grow bluer and dimmer as objects receded". What it IS: the picture's space is a temperature gradient -- warm ochre foreground pushes forward while cool blue mountain and sky fall back, with the same blue appearing in both mountain and sky, and a cool blue-green wall holds warm fruit forward on a table. **Takeaway:** paint a mountain in the same blue as the sky above it and a foreground in warm ochre, and let the distance be nothing but the meeting of the two temperatures; no road, no converging line, may connect them.

## M7. Serial Repetition of a Motif
The same motif is painted again and again -- about 80 Sainte-Victoire paintings and watercolours, five Card Players, four Boy in a Red Waistcoat portraits, three Large Bathers, six 1899 still lifes with the same props, 26 or 27 portraits of Hortense -- changing one variable at a time (angle, light, scale, number of figures, arrangement) so the motif becomes a laboratory rather than a subject. Drawpaintacademy: "Painting the same subject over and over again allows you to explore the way color and light work in different conditions." What it IS: the motif is the constant and the painting is the experiment -- the mountain is grander and more geometric in 1902 than in 1885, the Card Players shrink from five figures to two and the picture gets more monumental, not less. **Takeaway:** paint the same mountain twenty times and let no two be copies; change one thing each time -- the light, the angle, the number of figures -- and let the series, not any single canvas, be the work.

## M8. Still-Life Arrangement as Architecture
The still life is built in the studio like a construction, not found: fruit propped with coins at specific angles, a small block under a tipped basket, cloth folded into ridges that act as structure, objects chosen and placed so that removing one would collapse the balance. "He'd spend hours -- sometimes days -- arranging a few items on a tablecloth. He used coins to prop up the fruit at specific angles." "It is not the objects that should attract attention, but the arrangement of the shapes and colours on the surface." A pear can be oversized "in order to achieve inner balance and an exciting composition". What it IS: the bottle is the hinge between two weights -- in The Basket of Apples the basket tilts forward, held by the bottle and the tablecloth folds, and the picture feels like a building, with the bottle as the hinge. **Takeaway:** spend a day arranging five apples and a bottle, prop one apple on a coin so it sits at the exact angle the composition needs, and let the fruit stay put only because a fold or a bottle holds it; the picture must feel like a building, not a table.

## M9. The Unfinished Edge
Areas of canvas are deliberately left unpainted, and a painting is treated as a process that may never be declared finished. Cezanne "regarded many of his paintings as unfinished, for painting was a never-ending process for him"; the late watercolours and late oils show "empty spaces flanked by colour" that appear complete. The Philadelphia Large Bathers was left with areas of canvas showing through, and the unfinished Sainte-Victoire of 1906 shows the working method: darks and midtones laid in as geometric shapes first, the rest left open. The negative case proves the standard was his own: the Portrait of Gustave Geffroy (1895) was left unfinished because he was dissatisfied with it, after three months of daily sittings. What it IS: white paper or bare linen reads as light, air or distance rather than as a gap -- the picture is caught mid-thought, and the viewer completes it. **Takeaway:** leave a patch of bare canvas in the middle of a finished-looking picture and let it read as air; the picture must feel caught mid-thought, not abandoned, and the viewer must do the last stroke.

## M10. Slow Realisation
The working method in Cezanne's own vocabulary: go out to the motif, receive the sensation (visual impression plus the emotion it triggers), and realise it in paint -- a slow, repeated, cumulative act that he feared would fail to the end. "Painting from nature does not mean copying the object, it means realizing its sensations" (reported by Bernard). Bernard watched the Three Skulls on an Oriental Carpet change colour and form every day during his February 1904 stay although it looked complete from day one, and summed up: "Truly, his way of working was a reflection with a brush in his hand." The letters say the same, VERIFIED VERBATIM: to his son Paul, 15 October 1906, the day of the storm collapse -- "I continue to work with difficulty... Sensations form the foundation of my work, and they are imperishable, I think" -- and to Bernard, 21 September 1906 -- "I have sworn to die painting." Sessions were long: models sat for dozens of sittings, some paintings were worked on for months or years, and on 5 September 1906 he wrote to his son, "I cannot reach the intensity that unfolds before my senses, I do not possess that wonderful richness of colour that animates nature." What it IS: the difficulty is the work, not an obstacle to it -- a skull repainted daily, a mountain returned to for twenty years, a portrait reworked until the sitter's patience outlasted the painter's. **Takeaway:** let a picture that looks simple from a distance reveal, up close, layer upon layer of small adjustments; the piece must show the slowness -- a motif returned to daily, a canvas reworked for months -- and never pretend the result came quickly.

## Evolution

EARLY (1839-1871, Aix, Paris, the dark period): born in Aix, the banker's son who was not yet a banker's son -- his father was still a hatter when he was born, and the family money came later; College Bourbon with Zola, law at his father's wish, drawing under Gibert; Paris in 1861, rejected by the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, at the Academie Suisse with Pissarro; the Salon des Refuses of 1863; the palette-knife period from autumn 1866, a series of still lifes and portraits Cezanne himself later called "une couillarde"; the dark period of 1867-1870, heavy impasto, black, brown and Prussian blue, violent and erotic literary subjects -- The Abduction, The Murder, Women Dressing -- and portraits of his uncle Dominique in the palette-knife manner; the Jas de Bouffan murals of 1860, the Four Seasons signed ironically "Ingres". Lawrence Gowing's verdict, quoted in Wikipedia, is that the palette-knife phase was "the invention of modern expressionism... the idea of art as emotional ejaculation made its first appearance at this moment". The work is about the blow, not the structure. Hortense met in 1869; the war year spent at L'Estaque to avoid conscription, denounced as a deserter in January 1871.

MIDDLE (1872-1894, the apprenticeship and the construction): Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise 1872-1874 under Pissarro, who told him to paint only with the three primary colours and their deviations; the palette lightens, the painting moves outdoors, the loose side-by-side brushstroke appears -- but he never accepts Impressionism's fleeting light, and the programme is already forming: "to make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums" (reported by Gasquet). Exhibited with the Impressionists in 1874 (The Hanged Man's House sold to Count Doria for 300 francs, one of the few sales) and 1877 (16 works, heavy criticism), and never again. Chocquet from 1875, the first consistent buyer. The father learns of Hortense and the son in March 1878 and halves the allowance; in September he relents at 400 francs. The mountain series begins about 1878, age 39, after the view from the train: "What a beautiful motif" (to Zola, 14 April 1878). L'Estaque 1883-1885 and Gardanne 1885-1888, the faceted constructive period, houses as interlocking masses, everything tipped up against the picture plane. The Salon accepts him once, in 1882, because his friend Guillemet lists him as his pupil. Marriage on 28 April 1886; the father's death in October 1886 and the inheritance of 400,000 francs; Zola's L'Oeuvre in March 1886 and the cooling of the friendship -- the 28 November 1887 letter offering a visit shows the break was not clean. Diabetes from 1890; the Card Players begun 1890-1892, models farmhands from the Jas de Bouffan; three works at Les XX in Brussels in 1891; Geffroy's article of 25 March 1894, the first fair critical judgement of his lifetime.

LATE (1895-1906, the recognition and the last decade): the Vollard show of November-December 1895, about 50 works shown of nearly 150 sent, still lifes and landscapes only, no figure pieces; Cezanne stays in Aix with his ill mother and does not attend; the buyers are fellow artists -- Monet first, then Degas, Renoir, Pissarro -- and prices rise a hundredfold over time. The Geffroy portrait of 1895, three months of daily sittings, abandoned unfinished. The second Vollard show 9 May-10 June 1898; the Berlin Nationalgalerie buys The Mill on the Couleuvre in 1897, the second Cezanne in a museum; the Chocquet estate sale of June 1899, 32 Cezannes of the first rank; the Jas de Bouffan sold in November 1899 at his sister's insistence, Vollard buying the contents of the studio; the Portrait of Ambroise Vollard (1899), abandoned after 115 sittings with the words, secondhand, "The front of the shirt is not bad." Land bought at Les Lauves in November 1901, the studio built and moved into by 1903, its long narrow slit of light made for large canvases like The Bathers. Bernard's month-long visit in February 1904 and the letters of 1904-1906 that became the doctrine; the whole room at the Salon d'Automne, 15 October-15 November 1904. The last years are the series carried to the end: the Large Bathers (three versions, the largest 208 x 249 cm, left unfinished), the late Sainte-Victoire paintings bolder and more geometric than the 1880s ones, the skulls, the watercolours. The storm of Monday 15 October 1906, the garden work on 16 October, the death on Monday 22 October.

AFTERLIFE SO FAR: the will of September 1902 excluded Hortense; son Paul, sole heir, sold 29 oil studies and 187 watercolours to Vollard and Bernheim-Jeune jointly for 213,000 and 62,000 francs, the first payment 13 February 1907, then dissipated the inheritance. The two 1907 retrospectives -- Bernheim-Jeune, 79 watercolours, 17-29 June; Salon d'Automne, 49 paintings and 7 watercolours, 5 October-15 November -- drew Picasso, Braque, Leger, Matisse, Derain, Kandinsky, Max Weber, Rilke, Meier-Graefe and Harry Graf Kessler; Picasso painted Les Demoiselles d'Avignon in the summer of 1907, confronting The Large Bathers, and Cubism began from Cezanne's planes and multiple viewpoints, though Gotz Adriani notes the Cubists' reception was "arbitrary" in ignoring Cezanne's motivation from observing nature. The reputation was then made in print: Meier-Graefe's first monograph (1910), Roger Fry's "Manet and the Post-Impressionists" at the Grafton Galleries (1910), coining "Post-Impressionism" and presenting Cezanne as a founder of modern art to the English-speaking world, Fry's Cezanne: A Study of His Development (1927), Clive Bell's Since Cezanne (1922), Gleizes and Metzinger's Du "Cubisme" (1912) placing Cezanne at the start of their way of painting. The Lauves studio, bought from a later owner by an American foundation initiated by James Lord and John Rewald and funded by 114 donors, has been open to the public since 1954; the Jas de Bouffan belongs to the city of Aix, its restored rooms and grounds open since 2025; Aix ran a 30 million euro "Cezanne 2025" campaign with 130 works at the Musee Granet. The market followed the reputation late and then all at once: the Qatar Card Players sold in 2011 for over $250 million, the Philadelphia Mont Sainte-Victoire (1902-04) sold at Christie's on 8 November 2022 for $138 million from the Paul Allen collection.

## Aesthetic Logic

Cezanne's aesthetic is an aesthetic of slowness, and it is the opposite of the Impressionist's. Where the Impressionist catches the fleeting light, Cezanne stays with the motif until the light is made solid; where the Salon painter finishes a picture, Cezanne leaves it unfinished because the sensation has not yet been realised; where the academician draws first and colours in, Cezanne builds the drawing out of coloured patches and lets the contour stay open. The unit of his system is not the picture but the sensation -- the visual impression plus the emotion it triggers -- and the work is the slow, repeated act of realising it: "Painting from nature does not mean copying the object, it means realizing its sensations." The doctrine is practical, not theoretical: "I do not want to be right in theory, but from nature" (letter to Bernard, 25 July 1904, VERIFIED VERBATIM), and the geometry he is famous for is a warm-up he forgets the moment his eyes start working. Tradition is a problem to re-solve outdoors, not a style to imitate: "I want to do Poussin over again, from nature", and the Louvre is "a good book to consult, but it should be only a means. The real, prodigious study to undertake is the diversity of the scene offered by nature" (12 May 1904, VERIFIED VERBATIM). He trusted his eye over theory and over the market alike: he exhibited with the Impressionists twice and never again, he let the Salon reject him every year from 1864 to 1869 and then accepted its single back-door admission in 1882 without gratitude, and he told the world, in the last letter of his life, that he worked with difficulty and could not reach the intensity before his senses. The same eye had limits, and he printed them: he never painted from a nude model for the Bathers, working instead from memory, museum visits and academic studies; he never finished the largest Bathers; he abandoned the Geffroy portrait after three months and the Vollard portrait after 115 sittings. A piece built on Cezanne fails when it decorates (adds an apple, adds a mountain, adds a striped waistcoat) and succeeds when it makes the viewer do the work of looking twice: one motif, one set of coloured patches, one tilted table, and the picture holding together not because the geometry agrees but because the colour does.

## Network

Ambroise Vollard (1866-1939), Gertrude Stein (1874-1946) and Leo Stein (1872-1947) -- the doorway trio, the subjects of #482, #480 and #481. Vollard gave Cezanne his first solo show and bought nearly 150 canvases; the Steins bought Cezannes from Vollard from October 1904, and Leo owned the Philadelphia Large Bathers before Widener bought it in 1937 for $110,000. Full detail in studies #480, #481 and #482; they are not numbered below, so the numbering runs N1-N40 as in the leg.

N1. Emile Zola (1840-1902) -- Novelist. DIRECT. Childhood friend from College Bourbon, Aix (1852); 250+ surviving Cezanne letters, about a third of them to Zola; L'Oeuvre (March 1886, Claude Lantier) cooled the friendship, but the 28 November 1887 letter offering a visit shows contact did not stop permanently in 1886; recent scholarship argues the Dreyfus Affair, on which they were on opposite sides, was a major determinant of the parting. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N2. Camille Pissarro (1830-1903) -- Painter. DIRECT. Met at the Academie Suisse in 1859; worked side by side at Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise 1872-74, Cezanne's Impressionist apprenticeship; Pissarro pushed Cezanne's participation in the first Impressionist exhibition of 1874; Cezanne called him "a father for me. A man to consult and a little like the good Lord", and in a 1906 catalogue listed himself as "Paul Cezanne, pupil of Pissarro"; Pissarro wrote to his daughter-in-law Esther on 13 November 1895 about the Vollard show. Already the top pick for #484; VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N3. Claude Monet (1840-1926) -- Painter. DIRECT. First buyer of a Cezanne painting; called him "the greatest of us all"; introduced Geffroy to Cezanne; Cezanne wrote to Monet on 6 July 1895 about the Geffroy portrait. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N4. Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1841-1919) -- Painter. DIRECT. Lifelong friend; took Chocquet to Tanguy's to see Cezannes; told Vollard to take on Cezanne (per #482); executor of Caillebotte's will; bought Cezannes early. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N5. Edgar Degas (1834-1917) -- Painter. DIRECT. Traded pastels for Cezannes; early buyer after Monet; met Vollard at the 1894 Manet show (per #482). VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N6. Marie-Hortense Fiquet (1850-1922) -- Model, wife. DIRECT. Met 1869 at the Academie Suisse; son Paul born 4 January 1872; the liaison hidden from his father for fear of losing the allowance; married 28 April 1886 in Aix in his parents' presence, to legitimise the fourteen-year-old Paul; 26 or 27 portraits, mostly oil, 1869 to the late 1890s; after his death her settlement from Paul was squandered through gambling. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N7. Paul Cezanne fils (1872-1947) -- Son. DIRECT. Only child; inherited the entire estate; became effectively his father's agent; handled the 1895 Vollard shipment per some accounts, but Vollard's memoir says the canvases came from Cezanne himself (see C1). VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N8. Louis-Auguste Cezanne (1798-1886) -- Father. DIRECT. Hatter turned prosperous banker; opposed the painting, wanted law; paid the allowance Cezanne lived on; died October 1886, leaving the Jas de Bouffan estate -- Cezanne's finances eased. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N9. Marie Cezanne (born 1841; death date NOT established in the sources read) -- Sister. DIRECT. Younger sister, two years his junior; "the only person who could manage the child" (Vollard); walked hand in hand to primary school daily; painted by him (Portrait de Marie Cezanne, soeur de l'artiste, 1865-67 -- sitter identity debated by Rewald). Do not state a death year; none was found. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N10. Ambroise Vollard (1866-1939) -- Dealer. DIRECT. The 1895 first solo show; nearly 150 canvases; portrait sittings 1896-99, abandoned after 115 sittings; made Cezanne's reputation and his own fortune. STUDIED -- subject of #482.

N11. Emile Bernard (1868-1941) -- Painter, writer. DIRECT. First visited Cezanne for a month in February 1904; article in L'Occident, July 1904; nine letters 1904-06 are the best-documented source of Cezanne's ideas; Souvenirs sur Paul Cezanne (1921); also visited in 1906; edited the 1911 Van Gogh letters edition. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED. (NOT Bernard DeVoto #476.)

N12. Joachim Gasquet (1873-1921) -- Writer. DIRECT. Friend from 1896 (his father Henri Gasquet was Cezanne's Saint Joseph school classmate); conversations published as Cezanne (1921); source of the "harmony parallel to nature", "primitive of a new art" and carrot passages -- but later critics warn Gasquet added to the conversations and his dates are uncertain. N13. Maurice Denis (1870-1943) -- Painter. DIRECT. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N15. Julien-Francois Tanguy (1825-1894) -- Paint dealer, "Pere Tanguy". DIRECT. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N17. Thadee Natanson (1868-1951) -- Critic, editor. DIRECT. His 1895 assessment of Cezanne's first retrospective appeared in La Revue Blanche. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N18. Gustave Caillebotte (1848-1894) -- Collector, patron. DIRECT. Bought Cezannes (5 in the bequest); his 1894 bequest of 67 or 68 Impressionist works to the French state (38 accepted for the Luxembourg, February 1897) forced the state to confront Impressionism; Renoir was executor; Cezanne's reported response: "Now Bouguereau can go to hell!" VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N19. Antoine Guillemet (1841-1918) -- Painter. DIRECT. Introduced Cezanne to the Cafe Guerbois (1866); as a Salon jury member in 1882 passed Cezanne off as his pupil to get Portrait de M. L. A. accepted -- Cezanne's first and last Salon acceptance. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N20. Philippe Solari (1840-1906) -- Sculptor. DIRECT. Saint Joseph school classmate; friend of Cezanne and Zola; Thursday soirees at Zola's Paris home 1860-65 with Cezanne and Pissarro; made two sculptures of Cezanne at the end of his life (one from memory, "Cezanne, the dreamer"; one from life in Cezanne's Aix studio); died the same year as Cezanne. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED. (NOT Gioele Solari, the philosopher in #482's carried block.)

N21. Armand Guillaumin (1841-1927) -- Painter. DIRECT. Academie Suisse friend from 1859 with Monet, Pissarro and Cezanne; Vollard's memoir groups them together on his return to Paris. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N22. Alfred Sisley (1839-1899) -- Painter. DIRECT, THIN LINK FLAGGED. Impressionist colleague; exhibited in the group shows; Chocquet owned one Sisley; the Caillebotte bequest included 9 Sisleys. N23. Leo Larguier (1878-1950) -- Writer. DIRECT. Young friend in Cezanne's last years; the "Art is a religion. Its goal is the elevation of thought" remark was made to him. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N24. Charles Camoin (1879-1965) -- Painter. DIRECT. Young Fauve; first met Cezanne in 1902 while on military service in Aix; retained the lesson "art is born from a deep connection to nature". VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N26. Gustave Courbet (1819-1877) -- Painter. SAME-CIRCLE (influence; THIN LINK FLAGGED). Early model with Delacroix; Chocquet hung his first Cezanne "between a Delacroix and a Courbet"; no documented meeting found in this leg. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N27. Edouard Manet (1832-1883) -- Painter. DIRECT, THIN (acquaintance rather than friendship). Cafe Guerbois circle from 1866; Cezanne answered Manet's Olympia (1863) with A Modern Olympia (c. 1873-74, shown at the first Impressionist exhibition 1874, lent by Dr Gachet); Manet declined the 1874 show. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED.

N28. Paul Gauguin (1848-1903) -- Painter. DIRECT. Painted beside Cezanne in 1882 (with Pissarro); owned an estimated 5-8 Cezannes, including Fruit Bowl, Glass and Apples (1879-80), the centrepiece of Denis's Homage; admired Cezanne immensely; Cezanne repaid it with suspicion -- Vollard: Cezanne accused Gauguin of trying to "rob him". N29. Vincent van Gogh (1853-1890) -- Painter. SAME-CIRCLE (THIN LINK FLAGGED).
