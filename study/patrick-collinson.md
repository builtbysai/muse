## M1. "The godly" as a social category

The hotter sort of Protestants called themselves "the godly", not "Puritans", a nickname imposed from outside, probably of Catholic coinage. Godly identity worked through reciprocal recognition: godly people knew each other, kept each other's company, and practised a discourse of separation from the ungodly majority. The category is social, not doctrinal: it describes a lived insiderhood inside a capacious national church, and Collinson "has long abjured the term [Puritan], preferring to adopt the name they often gave themselves, the 'godly'". The piece must name the group by its own word first, and then show the recognition happening, the nod between strangers who know each other by their godliness.

**Takeaway:** name the group by its own word, then trace who recognises whom.

## M2. Puritanism as a movement within the Church, not a sect

The Elizabethan Puritan Movement showed Puritanism as a significant force inside the Elizabethan Anglican Church, not a radical fringe outside it. Non-separating puritans stayed within parish communion even though their position was more exposed than the separatists'. The aversion to separatism is the load-bearing fact: it kept the movement inside the institution it criticised. "Puritanism was not a thing identifiable in itself, but one half of a stressful relationship." The piece must show the reformer staying, not leaving, because the staying is the argument.

**Takeaway:** the most consequential reformers are the ones who refuse to leave.

## M3. Voluntary religion supplementing prayer-book worship

Prophesyings, lectureships, lectures by combination, fasts and spiritual exercises supplemented Prayer Book worship without replacing it. The godly added a voluntary layer of religion on top of the compulsory parish layer. This is how a minority sustains intensity inside a majority institution: it does not secede, it adds. The piece must show two layers in the same building, the compulsory service below and the voluntary meeting above it, both happening, both real.

**Takeaway:** look for the voluntary layer on top of the compulsory one.

## M4. The classical movement and the presbyterian programme

The thesis at the centre of The Elizabethan Puritan Movement: the "Puritan classical movement" of the 1570s-1580s, a network of local classes (presbyteries in embryo) and prophesyings aimed at a "further reformation" of church government. The movement had a membership, a structure and internal contradictions, and it was destroyed, but it was a political and ecclesiastical organism, not a mood. The piece must show the structure, the classes meeting, the programme written down, because a movement is proved by its meetings, not by its feelings.

**Takeaway:** a movement is a structure with members and meetings, not an attitude.

## M5. Prophesyings and lectures by combination

Clerical self-education as an institution: ministers met to expound scripture in turn (prophesyings) and combined to fund lectureships in towns. These were the godly's schools and their publishing network before print did the work. Elizabeth and Whitgift suppressed the prophesyings in 1576-77; the lectureships survived longer. The piece must show the meeting schedule as the infrastructure, the ministers arriving week after week, because the schedule is what made the movement durable.

**Takeaway:** the movement's real infrastructure was its meeting schedule.

## M6. The church within a church

The godly built a parallel set of institutions, classes, exercises, lectureships, fasts, inside the parish system. The result was a church within a church: the same buildings, the same Prayer Book, but a denser, hotter layer of practice for the minority. The national church contained it until Laud. The piece must hold both layers in one frame, the parish church and the hotter church inside it, because the story is the containment, not the separation.

**Takeaway:** parallel institutions inside the formal one are the story, not a footnote.

## M7. Institutional equilibrium and the broad Jacobean church

The Religion of Protestants argues for an "institutional equilibrium": Calvinist theology plus episcopal government, Presbyterianism parried, popery contained, the via media giving a long peace. The Jacobean church was broad enough to absorb moderate puritanism and its voluntary religion. The equilibrium held until the Laudians broke it. Worden: Collinson "has done for early 17th-century religion what Conrad Russell has done for early 17th-century politics. He leaves the Church in the late 1620s where Russell has left Parliament, a very long way from revolution." The piece must show the balance holding, the absorption working, because stability is the achievement, not the default.

**Takeaway:** stability is an achievement of absorption, not of agreement.

## M8. The Grindalian tradition against Laudianism

Grindal's refusal to suppress the prophesyings, and his sequestration, made him the hero of a "Grindalian tradition" of Calvinist, preaching, tolerant episcopacy. Laud is the villain of the story: "the greatest calamity ever visited upon the English Church", the architect of the demise of the Grindalian tradition. Collinson's account of Laud stresses that the difference was largely one of tone, plus the "gratuitously offensive clericalism" of the Durham House divines, and a retreat from Calvinist doctrine. Worden's reservation stands: Laud "may be in danger of becoming too convenient" an explanation. The piece must show the tradition made by its martyr, and the calamity arriving as a change of tone, not as an army.

**Takeaway:** traditions are made by the martyrs of a policy, not by the policy itself.

## M9. The parish as the unit, and its limits

The parish is Collinson's unit of analysis: the place where religion was lived, where churchwardens' accounts record the godly and the ungodly side by side. But he also questions whether parishes were ever cohesive social units, even before the Puritans: "inarticulate and undemonstrative" Anglicanism, the parish church rhythm strengthening its hold, and the godly minority cutting across parish boundaries. The piece must use the parish as the lens and then test it, showing the godly network running across parish lines while the parish bell still rings.

**Takeaway:** use the parish as the lens, then test whether the parish was ever the community.

## M10. The godly against the multitude: saints and sinners

The godly distinguished themselves from the community by dividing saints from sinners, barring the ungodly from the sacraments, and undermining the clergy's role as the parish conciliation service. The challenge was to community, not to hierarchy. Against this, George Herbert's Country Parson stands for reconciliation: the parish priest as the man who keeps neighbours together. The piece must show the exclusion as a social act, the sacrament refused at the altar rail, because the theology is visible only in the refusal.

**Takeaway:** exclusion from the sacrament is a social act before it is a theological one.

## M11. The monarchical republic

"Elizabethan England was a republic which also happened to be a monarchy: or vice versa" (p. 43). The thesis of the 1987 essay: a republican discourse and republican practice existed inside the monarchy, councils, parliaments, bonds, associations, and the regime depended on them. The republic was not a programme to abolish the queen; it was the practice of government by the political nation. The piece must find the republic inside the monarchy first, the council table and the bond, before looking for any republican.

**Takeaway:** find the republic inside the monarchy before looking for republicans.

## M12. Citizens concealed within subjects

"Citizens concealed within subjects" (De Republica Anglorum, p. 19): Elizabethans were subjects in law and citizens in practice, active, Protestant, specifically English citizens who defined themselves by participation. The move is to read the language of citizenship back into a subject's world and to show the practice matching the word. The piece must show the subject acting, voting in the vestry, signing the bond, keeping the parish, because the acting is the citizenship.

**Takeaway:** the subject who acts like a citizen is already a citizen.

## M13. Self-government at the king's command

The Swallowfield Articles of 1596: a Wiltshire village's self-government, recorded in the Huntington Library's Ellesmere MS EL 6162, found during Collinson's 1984 Mellon fellowship, a "scruffy little manuscript" that became the leitmotif of the monarchical-republic thesis. The village governed itself, and did so "at the king's command". Self-government permeated society: lawyers, publicists, preachers, gentry, churchwardens. The piece must let the smallest document carry the largest claim, the village articles in a plain hand, because the grammar of authority is in the document, not in the crown.

**Takeaway:** the smallest document can carry the largest claim, if it is read for its grammar of authority.

## M14. Headless conciliar government: two governments

When Elizabeth would not act, above all on Mary Queen of Scots, the council could proceed without her. Two governments coexisted uneasily: the queen and her council. The council's government was "headless" in the sense that it lacked the monarch's person, but it was still government, and it kept the regime alive. The piece must show the empty chair and the table still working, because the government that runs when the ruler will not rule is the republic in action.

**Takeaway:** look for the government that runs when the ruler will not rule.

## M15. The Bond of Association and the 1585 interregnum bill

The 1584 Bond of Association: signers pledged to pursue anyone harming Elizabeth and to exclude from the throne anyone on whose behalf harm was done, "without reference to any laws or rights of succession", "by no other authority than that residing in the body politic". Burghley's 1585 bill proposed a conciliar and parliamentary interregnum on Elizabeth's death. These are quasi-republican expedients: the body politic acting without the monarch. The piece must show the oath as a constitution in waiting, the signatures accumulating, because the bond is the republic written down before it is declared.

**Takeaway:** an oath can be a constitution in waiting.


## M16. The Elizabethan exclusion crisis

A sustained attempt to prevent Mary Queen of Scots' accession, with five common characteristics with the 1680s exclusion crisis: fear of Catholicism; exclusion of a Catholic successor; fear of arbitrary government; divergence of the political nation's interests from the monarch's; and a Sidney in each, Philip Sidney in the 1580s, Algernon Sidney in the 1680s. The association of 1584 was even found in Shaftesbury's possessions in 1681. The piece must show the same crisis twice, a century apart, because the repetition is the structure, and the structure is the argument.

**Takeaway:** the same crisis twice, a century apart, is a structure, not a coincidence.

## M17. The new political history

"An account of political processes which is also social"; "social history with the politics put back". The programme of De Republica Anglorum: find political life where it was not previously thought to exist, in parishes, in lectureships, in village articles, in bonds and associations, and write the politics of the people who were supposed to have none. The piece must look for politics in the small places, the vestry and the lecture room, because that is where Collinson found it.

**Takeaway:** politics is where people act together, not where the institutions say it is.

## M18. The nation as constructed: Protestantism, language, history

This England argues that "there has never been a British nation" (p. 3): the nation in question is England, not the British Isles, and Englishness was made through language, Protestantism and history. "Elizabethans were indeed constantly defining themselves as active, Protestant, and specifically English, citizens." The nation is a project of self-definition, not a given. The piece must show the nation being made, word by word and sermon by sermon, because a nation that is made can be watched being made.

**Takeaway:** a nation is a verb before it is a noun.

## M19. Prophetic preaching as nation-building and as corrosive

Prophetic preaching brought the nation together as a new Israel, but it was also "corrosive and divisive" (p. 183, This England): it divided the godly from the community and the preachers from the bishops. The same sermon that makes the nation also splits the parish. The piece must hold both effects in one frame, the congregation united by the sermon and divided by it, because the device that builds is the device that breaks.

**Takeaway:** every nation-building device is also a division device.

## M20. Foxe's Acts and Monuments as a living text

Foxe's book was a "living text that recorded performances and invited performance" (p. 235, This England): it was read, performed, added to, and lived by. Collinson weighs truth against fiction in Foxe, rejects Haller's elect-nation thesis, and reads Camden's Latin Annales against their English translations, which lost Camden's "intense, epigrammatic, often ironical style", to show that Camden's great figure was Mary, not Elizabeth, and that the Gloriana myth was not manufactured by Camden. The piece must show the book being performed, not just read, because a performed book is a different kind of evidence.

**Takeaway:** a book that is performed is a different kind of evidence from a book that is read.

## M21. Camden and the making of the Elizabeth myth

Camden's Annales in Latin and in English translation are two different books: the translations flatten the style and shift the emphasis. Collinson's point is that the Elizabeth myth was not a court fabrication but a later, contested construction, and that Camden's real subject was Mary. Read the original language before trusting the translation's story. The piece must show the same page in two languages, the Latin sharp and ironical, the English smoothed into praise, because the myth lives in the translation.

**Takeaway:** translation is interpretation, and the myth may live in the translation.

## M22. Merry England nostalgia

Stow located the golden age in an irrecoverable past; Carew located it in present-day Cornwall; Lambard lamented the lost monastic manuscripts. Nostalgia for "Merry England" came from Catholic sympathisers and from devout Protestants alike. The past was a usable resource for both sides. The piece must show the same lost England claimed by two enemies, because nostalgia is an argument, and the argument is the point.

**Takeaway:** nostalgia is a political argument, not a feeling.

## M23. The Calvinist consensus

In the latter decades of the 16th century and under James I, a "Calvinist consensus" held the church together: a shared Calvinist theology across conformists and moderate puritans. The consensus is what made the "institutional equilibrium" possible, and its collapse under Laud is what ended the peace. The piece must show the consensus as a dated fact, holding for a generation and then ending, because a consensus that can end is a historical event, not a mood.

**Takeaway:** consensus is a historical fact, not a mood, it can be dated and it can end.

## M24. Occasional conformity and religious pluralism

Occasional conformity, taking communion occasionally in the parish church while worshipping elsewhere, masked diversity to facilitate political cooperation. Religious pluralism in Elizabethan England was practised before it was theorised: people conformed enough to cooperate and dissented enough to stay godly. The piece must show the same person in two churches, the parish on Sunday and the conventicle in the week, because the tactic is the belief in action.

**Takeaway:** conformity can be a tactic, not a belief.

## M25. The hunter-and-gatherer historian

Collinson's method as the obituarists describe it: the "hunter and gatherer" historian, averse to theory and sceptical of its usefulness, whose discoveries are "elegantly crafted and placed in the economic, social, political and human conditions of their time and place to tell new stories". The finds: Tobie Matthew's diary (an ill, very old man travelling the Yorkshire dales in bitter weather, preaching and fasting), the Swallowfield Articles, the Thomas Wood and Dering letters. The archive of the godly, diaries, letters, churchwardens' accounts, is the evidence base. The piece must let the find do the work, the diary opened on the desk, because the document is the argument.

**Takeaway:** the find is the argument; the document does the work.

## M26. The biography of a movement, not a man

Cromwell is "not biographable" (LRB, 29 November 2007): after Elton's death, Cromwell was "similarly airbrushed out of history", and Starkey's telling of the monarchy found "no occasion even to mention Cromwell's name". Collinson's books are biographies of movements, the Puritan movement, the Grindalian tradition, the monarchical republic, rather than of men. The posthumous Bancroft book is the exception that proves the rule: it is the anti-Puritan view, the movement seen from the other side. The piece must write the life of the movement, and let the men find their places in it.

**Takeaway:** write the life of the movement and the men will find their places in it.

## M27. The Elizabethan settlement as Protestant, not a via media

The settlement's articles were Protestant in doctrine even where the government was episcopal: the Thirty-Nine Articles, the Prayer Book, the preaching ministry. The "via media" was a political description of the settlement's survival, not a theological description of its content. Collinson's early Elizabethan bishops were heirs of St Ambrose, rebuking emperors, Jewel and Aylmer against Erastianism. The piece must call the settlement what its doctrine says it is, the Protestant articles in plain sight, because the politics needed the softer name and the doctrine did not.

**Takeaway:** call the settlement what its doctrine says it is, not what its politics needed it to be.

## M28. The conventicle and separatist tendency held in check

The conventicle, the godly meeting apart, was the separatist tendency inside the movement, and the aversion to separatism is what held it in check. The godly met separately to pray and to hear sermons, but they returned to the parish for communion. The tension between conventicle and parish is the movement's permanent internal contradiction. The piece must show the line the movement refuses to cross, the meeting apart and the return, because the limit is the movement's shape.

**Takeaway:** the movement's limit is the line it refuses to cross.

## M29. The sermon and the diary as evidence

The godly left diaries and letters: Tobie Matthew's diary, the Thomas Wood letters, the Dering letters. These are not illustrations of the argument; they are the argument. The sermon was the godly's central act, and the diary was the record of a life lived under the sermon. Collinson reads them for practice, not for doctrine. The piece must read the diary for what the diarist did, the miles walked in bitter weather, the fast kept, because the doing is the belief.

**Takeaway:** read the diary for what the diarist did, not for what he believed.

## M30. The long view against revisionist short-termism

From the 1957 thesis to The Birthpangs (1988) and This England (2011), Collinson links the 16th and 17th centuries in one long argument: the Puritan movement of the 1570s, the broad Jacobean church, the Laudian break, the Civil War. Against the revisionist short-termism that made the Civil War an accident of the 1640s, he insists on the longue duree: the causes were laid in the 1580s. "Crudely and flatly, that the Reformation was something which happened in the reigns of Elizabeth and James I. Before that everything was preparative, embryonic." (Birthpangs, p. ix). The piece must take the century when the story needs it, because a cause laid in the 1580s cannot be shown in a decade.

**Takeaway:** if the story needs a century, take the century.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife

Phase 1 (1957-1979): the movement and its archbishop. The 1957 thesis ("The Puritan classical movement in the reign of Elizabeth I", about 1,200 pages, two volumes, half a million words) became The Elizabethan Puritan Movement (1967): puritanism as a movement within the church, with a structure, a membership and a programme. The early work is archival and institutional: the classical movement, the prophesyings, the letters of Thomas Wood (edited 1960) and of Edward Dering (1964). Archbishop Grindal (1979) is the biography of the movement's archbishop, the man who embodied the Grindalian tradition and was broken by it. The Haller correction belongs here too: The Rise of Puritanism (1938) is corrected and given a sociological dimension, and the focus shifts from the future (the Puritan Revolution) to origins and to the Jacobean church as it seemed to contemporaries.

Phase 2 (1980-1994): synthesis, equilibrium, the godly. The Religion of Protestants (1982, Ford Lectures 1979) is the synthesis: the "institutional equilibrium" of the Elizabethan and Jacobean church, Calvinist theology with episcopal government, voluntary religion supplementing the Prayer Book. Godly People (1983) and English Puritanism (1983) collect the essays that define "the godly" as a social category. The Birthpangs of Protestant England (1988, Anstey Lectures 1986) extends the argument into culture: Protestantism becoming respectable, the loss of popular culture, the challenge to community. The two republican texts, "The monarchical republic of Queen Elizabeth I" (1987) and De Republica Anglorum (1989), open the second front: the new political history, politics as participation, citizens concealed within subjects.

Phase 3 (1994-2011): essays, the nation, the general reader. Elizabethan Essays (1994) gathers the republican work. The 2000s turn to the general reader: The Reformation (2003), written "out of my head and off my own shelves" in about 50,000 words, "Better to be wrong than to be boring"; Elizabeth I (2007), a reworking of the ODNB article; Elizabethans (2003); Lady Margaret Beaufort (2003). From Cranmer to Sancroft (2006) returns to the essay form. This England (2011) is the late synthesis of the nation: England, not Britain; Protestantism, language, history; Foxe, Camden, Stow, the exclusion crisis. The History of a History Man (2011) is the memoir, published in the year of his death. Two late reversals are recorded against the work: the 1986 iconophobia thesis "has now been comprehensively refuted" (Hamling and Willis, 2023), and Lake (2015) finds the monarchical-republic theory "not very republican at all".

Posthumous afterlife: Richard Bancroft and Elizabethan Anti-Puritanism (CUP, 2013), finished during terminal illness as "a kind of mental therapy", is the bookend: the anti-Puritan view, the movement seen from the other side, and the preface says Collinson "returns full circle to the territory he traversed in his prodigiously researched and legendary Ph.D. thesis of 1957". Three festschrifts: Religion, Culture and Society in Early Modern Britain (eds Fletcher and Roberts, CUP, 1994, 17 contributors, 14 of them historians); Parergon 14.1 (1996), the Australasian festschrift; Belief and Practice in Reformation England (eds Wabuda and Litzenberger, Ashgate, 1998), the tribute from his students. The response volume The Monarchical Republic of Early Modern England (ed. McDiarmid, Ashgate, 2007) carries the debate forward with an afterword by Collinson. History 100, no. 342 (2015) reassessed four of his books (Lake on The Elizabethan Puritan Movement, Marshall on The Birthpangs, Greengrass on The Reformation, Walsham on The Religion of Protestants) and his relation to literature (Kewes). The "monarchical republic" debate continues through Peltonen, Worden, Guy, Lake, Cust, Hughes and Skinner. Obituaries: Guardian (John Morrill, 1 November 2011), History Today, the Australian Academy of the Humanities (Pesman and Waterhouse, 2012), the Huntington Library (Hindle, 17 October 2011), and the ODNB life by Alexandra Walsham (January 2015).

## Aesthetic logic

The system is held together by one move, repeated at every scale: find the active community inside the formal institution, and show the institution bending around it. At the parish, the godly minority practises voluntary religion inside the Prayer Book church. At the centre, the council governs without the queen inside the monarchy. At the nation, active Protestant citizens define England inside the subject's world. The godly, the council, the citizen, each is the same figure: the participant who makes the institution work by acting inside it and against its letter. The aesthetic is the archive: the diary, the letter, the village articles, the bond, small documents that carry large claims. The tone is the hunter-and-gatherer's: averse to theory, sceptical of its usefulness, convinced that the find, elegantly placed, tells the story. And the arc is tragic in the classical sense: the equilibrium holds for a generation, the godly are absorbed, and then Laud breaks it, the Grindalian tradition is the thing lost, and the loss is the story. The pieces that follow from him should therefore be built on a find and a layer, not on a mood: a diary opened, a village's articles, a bond signed, a church within a church, a republic inside a monarchy. The colour is the colour of paper and ink and parish stone, the motion is the motion of people meeting, the interaction is the inspection, the lifting of the document, the tracing of the network. Nothing in Collinson is decorative, and nothing in a piece from Collinson should be either: if it does not find the active community inside the institution, it does not belong.
