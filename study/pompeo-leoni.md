# Pompeo Leoni: Study Notes

**Site:** Pompeo Leoni (c.1533-1608; birth year and birthplace disputed, see C232), Italian sculptor and medallist, son of Leone Leoni, Philip II of Spain's sculptor, and the man who cut Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks apart and rebound them as the Codex Atlanticus and the Windsor collection. He was born in the early 1530s, in Milan according to the English Wikipedia infobox and the Prado, the Met and the National Gallery of Art, but "probably in Venice, where his father, Leone, and mother, [were living]" according to Kelley Helmstutler Di Dio (2006), whose biographical sketch is the best modern source; Markus Schmidt gives 1530, the Benezit dictionary 1533. His father Leone (c.1509-1590), Arezzo-born imperial sculptor, medallist and goldsmith, had been living in Venice under the protection of Pietro Aretino after 1533, then served as master of the imperial mint at Milan from 20 February 1542, and rebuilt his house there, the Casa degli Omenoni, in 1565-67, named for its heroically scaled herm figures and bearded atlantes. Pompeo "learned the art of sculpture and medal making in the house of his father, Leone Leoni, in Milan, called the Casa degli Omenoni, where he had set up a school" (English Wikipedia, Pompeo Leoni), a house that was simultaneously a foundry, a school, a cast gallery after the Antique -- dominated by a stucco of the equestrian Marcus Aurelius in the courtyard -- and a display of dynastic portraiture. Leone introduced him to court life very early: in March 1548 Pompeo went with his father to Speyer and Brussels, and in 1551 to Augsburg, where he began some reliefs for Cardinal Antoine Perrenot de Granvelle, chief counsellor to Charles V and Philip II, and designed a medal, his first known work; he later travelled to Rome to deliver the medal and the reliefs to Granvelle, and that year also accompanied his father's bronze portrait sculptures of the imperial family to Spain. The royal retinue arrived in Spain on 28 September 1556. In his first years there Pompeo worked on finishing the bronze full-length statues begun by his father -- Philip II, the Empress Isabel, Mary of Hungary, and Charles V and Fury -- plus bronze and marble busts of Charles V, a bronze relief of Isabel, portrait reliefs and full-length marble statues, and medals for members of the Spanish court, on a salary of 30 ducats a month from the regent Juana of Austria. His work was interrupted in 1558 when he was brought before the Inquisition on charges of supporting Lutheranism and sentenced to a year's confinement in a monastery; after his release he resumed work on his father's imperial sculptures. He followed the court from Valladolid to Guadalajara, Toledo and back to Valladolid, and in 1561 settled in Madrid, buying a house and studio facing the Calle de las Tabernillas. By 1570 he was officially established as sculptor to the King, preparing the ephemera for the entry of Philip II's new wife Ana de Austria and receiving a commission for the pedestal for the relics of Saint Eugene for Toledo Cathedral. The 1570s were his first decade of independent authority: a run of tomb monuments, including Juana of Austria at the Descalzas Reales in Madrid, Fernando de Valdes at Salas in Asturias (alabaster, 1576-1582, ensemble finalised by 1586) and Cardinal Diego de Espinosa at Martin Munoz de las Posadas. All his private commissions came to a halt in 1579 when Philip II ordered an elaborate retable for the Capilla Mayor of the church of San Lorenzo in the Escorial. In 1582 Pompeo returned to Milan to collaborate with his father on the fifteen bronze figures required, remained there until 1589, and accompanied the bronzes back to Spain, where they were installed in 1591 -- the English Wikipedia's 1587 assembly date for the mausoleum group conflicts with Di Dio's 1591 installation and is not used in this study, see C233. After 1591 he executed in Madrid the ten gilt-bronze life-size kneeling figures of Charles V and Philip II and their families that flank the high altar, set in place in May 1598, four months before Philip II's death on 13 September 1598. He returned to Milan briefly in 1594 to finish his father's bronze statue of Ferrante Gonzaga. The same years hold the Leonardo acquisition: in 1587-1590 he came into possession of Leonardo's notebooks, which Leonardo had bequeathed to Francesco Melzi, whose son Orazio had kept them in trunks under the eaves of the villa at Vaprio "as useless lumber" (McMurrich), sold some and gave others away, until the tutor Lelio Gavardi abstracted 13 volumes in 1587, the law student Giovanni Ambrogio Mazenta recovered them, and Pompeo induced Orazio, "by the promise of obtaining for him official honours and preferment," to appeal to the Mazenta brothers to restore the volumes so that he might present them to Philip II. No cash price is recorded anywhere in the sources consulted: what he "paid" was political patronage, not money, and there is no evidence he ever gave any Leonardo manuscript to Philip II -- "apparently the collector's instinct proved stronger in Pompeo Leoni than his original intention." What he did with the papers is the famous scissors-and-glue reassembly: he dismembered some of Leonardo's notebooks and sorted the loose leaves by subject into two albums, "Disegni di Machine e delle Arti Secreti et Altre Cose di Leonardo da Vinci Racolti da Pompeo Leoni," the technical and scientific drawings pasted onto large atlas-format sheets -- the Codex Atlanticus, 1,119 paper leaves (2,238 pages) in 12 leather-bound volumes, now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan -- and "Disegni di Leonardo da Vinci restaurati da Pompeo Leoni," the botanical, anatomical and artistic drawings, around 600 sheets mounted on 234 folios, the Windsor album, whose leather binding survives in the Royal Collection. The 1630 account of Giovanni Magenta accused him of being one of the main culprits in the dispersal and of having altered the order of the manuscripts, and the 2026 Leonardotheka 2.0 project, which digitally reunited the Atlanticus sheets with the Windsor pages, calls his work a "disastrous intervention." His Madrid house held a large and prestigious art collection that "became a requisite attraction for illustrious visitors to the Spanish court" (Di Dio) -- two mythological paintings by Correggio, Jupiter and Io and Danae, an Entombment by Taddeo Zuccari, a Madonna and Child attributed to Raphael, a bronze crucifix attributed to Michelangelo, a self-portrait and a Mary Magdalene by Titian, plaster casts after Michelangelo, reliefs from Trajan's Column, wax models for the Escorial royal figures, and four gilded plaster portrait busts of the royal family -- which he bequeathed to his heirs with the plea that it be kept intact in his memory; it was inventoried in Madrid in 1609 and 1613 and dispersed by sale. El Greco painted his portrait (Portrait of a Sculptor, c.1576-1578, 94 x 87 cm, private collection), showing him in black with a white ruff beside a marble bust of Philip II. He died in Madrid in October 1608; the 1610 death date in McMurrich (1930) and the British Museum medal guide (1893) is superseded, see C234. His heir is named Polidoro Calchi by discoveringdavinci.com and Cleodoro Calchi by McMurrich -- unresolved, see C235. ## M1. Sectional Lost-Wax Casting and the Detachable Armour: The Figure as a Kit of Parts
The Leoni foundry cast life-size and larger-than-life bronzes by the indirect lost-wax method: a model (waxed, clay-cored, iron-armatured), invested in refractory mould, wax melted out, molten bronze (about 90 percent copper, 10 percent tin, poured near 800 C) run in through sprues while air and gas escaped through vents, then the mould broken open and the bronze chased and polished. The distinctive Leoni workshop fact is the SCALE and the SECTIONAL casting. The Prado's Charles V and Fury (commissioned March 1549, cast 1551-1555) was cast in Milan in separate pieces: the nude figure of the Emperor and the detachable armour in 1551, the figure of Fury in 1553, the pedestal with trophies and weapons in 1555. It stands 251 cm high and weighs 718.6 kg. The separate bronze elements are attached with large screws, so the armour can be taken off and put on -- the Emperor can be shown nude, as a heroic antique figure, or armoured, as a contemporary general. Vasari marvelled at the two wafer-thin shells of armour that dress and undress the nude figure so easily. The Prado's own words: "The portrait of Charles V is conceived as a dual image: he can be shown either nude, in a heroic pose, or wearing armour of the style of his day, with the Order of the Golden Fleece and the general's sash. The various bronze elements are attached with large screws, making it easy to attach or remove them." The Escorial figures were likewise cast in Milan and shipped to Spain for assembly on site, a production line split across two countries (Milan foundry, Madrid installation). Watchable behaviour: the figure changes state -- hero to king -- without moving; the screws are the hinge of the meaning. Source: leg 1 (Getty technical guidelines; National Portrait Gallery lost-wax process; Prado object text); leg 2 (Prado Charles V and Fury page, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** identity is a removable layer -- the same bronze is a god or a general depending on which screws are in.

## M2. The Cast Portrait Medal: Wax Model, Fine Mould, No Striking
Renaissance portrait medals were NOT struck. The art of making dies in the 15th century would not permit striking anything but very low relief, so medals were cast: a model made in wax, embedded in fine moulding earth or charcoal so fine as to be almost impalpable, then cast in bronze. Leone was regarded as the finest of the Cinquecento medallists, and Pompeo learned the craft in the same house. His first known work, the 1551 Granvelle medal, and his 1554 medal of Ercole II d'Este (obverse portrait, reverse Patience, motto "Diuturna tollerantia," Long endurance; the National Gallery of Art lead example is 6.94 cm in diameter) show the mechanism: a small, portable, repeatable portrait object whose obverse fixes a likeness and whose reverse carries an emblem with a motto. The Patience reverse derives from a Vasari drawing via Michelangelo's selection, reworked by Pompeo into a more sinuous, attenuated figure with sharper waist and neck bend -- the medal as a site where a painted allegory is re-engineered in miniature relief. The same wax-model skill fed the large bronzes: Leone "probably left some finished work in wax (in which many of his sculptures were modelled)." Watchable behaviour: a portrait on one face, a whole allegory with landscape on the other, inside 7 cm of lead -- the medal is a two-sided small monument. Source: leg 1 (British Museum 1893 medal guide; Waldman 1994); leg 2 (National Gallery of Art; Waldman via leg 2).

**Takeaway:** the medal is the school -- compress a portrait and an emblem into a palm-sized object, and the large bronzes are the same compression scaled up.

## M3. Armour as a Picture Surface: Relief Crowding at Monumental Scale
The Philip II full-length bronze (cast 1551, finished 1553, 171 cm, 321.4 kg, Prado) wears its pictures on its body. The breastplate carries a low-relief medallion of the Virgin Mary; the rest of the armour is crowded with classical reliefs: tritons and nereids on the breastplate, the three Graces and Mercury on the left shoulder, Hercules between Vice and Virtue, Mercury, bacchantes, and ram's heads on the hip flaps. The inscription around the pedestal reads PHILIPPVS. ANGLIAE. REX. CAROLI. V. F. The mechanism is the medal's two-sided compression (M2) scaled up onto a body: the armour becomes a portable gallery of emblems. The same crowding governs Charles V and Fury, whose pedestal is crowded with trophies and weapons. Watchable behaviour: the closer you stand, the more the figure dissolves into small scenes; from a distance it reads as a single upright silhouette. Source: leg 2 (Prado Philip II page, read end to end; Prado Charles V and Fury page, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the armour is a gallery -- stand close and the king dissolves into emblems, stand back and the emblems reassemble into a king.

## M4. The Kneeling Rank: Portraiture That Never Stops Praying
The Escorial royal tombs are two facing ranks of five gilt-bronze life-size figures, kneeling at prie-dieus, hands joined, all turned toward the high altar: Charles V with the Empress Isabel and his sisters Eleonora of France and Mary of Hungary, and his daughter Mary, wife of the Emperor Maximilian, on the left; Philip II with his fourth wife Anne of Austria, and behind them Isabella of Valois and Mary of Portugal, his third and first wives, and his son Don Carlos, on the right. The Web Gallery of Art: "The two tombs represent a synthesis of the kneeling figures which occur in many earlier Spanish tombs with the style of bronze portraiture evolved in Milan by Leone Leoni." The mechanism is a dynastic group arranged as an architectural frieze, frozen in a single repeated gesture, so the dead remain permanently present at Mass. The template was the single kneeling figure at prayer in white marble, the tomb of Juana of Austria at the Descalzas Reales, Madrid, of the 1570s. The tombs were set in place in May 1598, four months before Philip II's death. A Polish journal article characterises the figures by "distance, emotional coldness, rigidity and attention to detail." Watchable behaviour: nothing moves, and that is the point -- the stillness is the performance, and the symmetry of the two facing ranks makes the church itself the frame. Source: leg 2 (Web Gallery of Art Escorial tombs page, read end to end; Prado artist page); leg 1 (Polish journal article via snippet).

**Takeaway:** the portrait that prays forever -- five figures a side, one gesture repeated, and the dead never leave the Mass.

## M5. The Scissors-and-Glue Editorial Mechanism: Cut, Sort, Paste, Bind
Leoni's treatment of Leonardo's papers is a mechanical editorial system with four steps: (1) CUT the bound notebooks apart into loose leaves and, where needed, cut leaves into fragments; (2) SORT by subject, not by chronology or original order -- technical-scientific drawings into one pile, artistic/anatomical/botanical drawings into another; (3) PASTE the sorted leaves onto large uniform sheets of atlas format, gluing small and irregular fragments onto a common support so they could be handled as a book; (4) BIND into two albums with title inscriptions on the covers stating the sorting rule. The visible trace of the sort is still legible: on the Codex Atlanticus map of Europe, the missing portion near the Brittany peninsula is now part of the Windsor collection (RL 12444 verso), while the recto of that Windsor sheet carries a profile of a youth -- an "artistic" subject, correctly filed by Leoni's rule into the other album. The mechanism destroys original sequence to create thematic access; five hundred years later the Leonardotheka project reverses it digitally by matching paper, watermarks and dimensions to reconstruct 50 original pages. The irony Leonardo himself supplied: "This will be a collection without order, made up of many sheets which I have copied here, hoping afterwards to arrange them in order in their proper places according to the subjects of which they treat" (Arundel MS 263, via discoveringdavinci.com) -- Leoni did exactly what Leonardo hoped for, by destroying the order Leonardo had. Watchable behaviour: a sheet that is two works at once -- a map on one side, a face on the other -- is split across two books by a decision about which side matters more. Source: leg 1 (discoveringdavinci.com, read end to end; Google Arts and Culture "Ten Key Facts"; Royal Collection / Google Arts "The Leoni binding"); leg 2 (same pages, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** meaning is made by cutting and regrouping, not by preserving sequence -- the archive is a sculpture, and the scissors are the chisel.

## M6. Portrait Likeness as a Reusable Model: One Head, Many Bodies and Media
The Leoni portrait practice worked from a small set of heroic likeness models that were reused, scaled and re-cast across busts, full-length statues, medals and reliefs. The same Charles V model underlies the Prado full-length bronze, the marble Charles V as a Roman ruler, and a family of bronze busts (plaster casts of the bust survive at the Chateau de Chambord and the Royal Museums of Fine Arts Brussels; a bronze version at Gaasbeek). The workshop kept wax and plaster models as masters -- Pompeo's Madrid collection held wax models for the Escorial royal figures and four gilded plaster portrait busts of the royal family. A preparatory terracotta bust from the first Brussels meeting in 1549 served as the model for the Mary of Hungary bronze and also for marble and bronze busts of the same sitter. Consequence, stated by the sources themselves: father and son worked "in a style that is not securely separated," so attributions between Leone and Pompeo, and between the workshop and its assistants, remain insecure. The mechanism is model-reuse as both a production economy and an attribution trap: the likeness is a template, not a sitting. Source: leg 1 (Di Dio 2006 via snippets; Prado object texts); leg 2 (Prado Mary of Hungary page via leg 2; Leone Leoni article).

**Takeaway:** the face is a template -- one head, many bodies, and the last hand to touch the bronze signs for the whole run.

## M7. Gilding and Surface Finish as Meaning: Gilt Bronze, Gilded Plaster, Jewelled Detail
Surface treatment was a separate, deliberate layer of the work, not decoration after the fact. The ten Escorial kneeling figures are GILT bronze, life-size, flanking the high altar: gold surface on bronze bodies, so the royal family reads as both present in prayer and permanently luminous in the basilica. In the Madrid house, four gilded plaster portrait busts of the royal family sat among marble busts on ledges and bureaux -- the same gilding applied to cheap plaster for display copies. The Prado's Habsburg bronzes are noted for "meticulous, jewelry-like detailing" in the armour, and the busts carry the collar of the Order of the Golden Fleece with its dead-sheep badge. The mechanism: material hierarchy (bronze, marble, alabaster, gilt plaster) and surface (gilt, polished, patinated) encode rank and permanence; the same face can be issued in bronze for the altar, marble for the gallery, or gilded plaster for the house. Source: leg 1 (Di Dio 2006 via snippets; Prado object texts); leg 2 (Prado pages, read end to end; Met bust page, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the surface is the rank -- gold on bronze for the altar, gold on plaster for the house, and the same face at two prices.

## M8. The Workshop Relay: Finishing as Authorship
A work passes through several hands across decades and cities -- Milan casting, Madrid finishing, the son's workshop completing -- and the signature records the chain, not a single moment. The Philip II and Mary of Hungary bronzes carry the joint signature LEO. P. POMPE. F. ARET. F. 1564, recording Madrid finishing of Milan castings; the Charles V and Fury group carries a 1551 casting date, a 1553 finish, and a 1564 signature. When Leone died on 22 July 1590, Pompeo "continued the large bronze-casting foundry after his father's death" and took on "the responsibility of completing all his father's unfinished projects" -- including the Escorial retable figures and the Ferrante Gonzaga statue, finished in Milan in 1594. He also moved Leone's works, finished and unfinished, and the Milan art collection to Spain. The San Diego Calvary, commissioned 1605, was possibly made by his officer Millan Vimercato and his son Miguel Angel Leoni over Pompeo's models, ending in 1608, the year of Pompeo's death. The mechanism is the foundry as an inheritable institution: the tools, models, moulds and commissions outlive the master, and the son's career is substantially the completion of the father's order book. This is also why the Escorial bronzes cannot be cleanly divided between father and son. Watchable behaviour: the same figure carries a 1551 casting date, a 1553 finish, and a 1564 signature; authorship is a relay, and the last hand to touch the bronze signs for the whole run. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Leone Leoni, read end to end; Di Dio 2006 via snippets); leg 2 (Prado pages, read end to end; Google Arts and Culture Calvario page, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the signature is a relay baton -- father casts, son finishes, workshop completes, and one name covers three decades.

## M9. The Collector's House as a Second Workshop
Pompeo's Madrid house was not a residence with a collection attached; it was a second workshop, and the collection was its product. Di Dio: "The collection of Pompeo Leoni (c.1533-1608) became a requisite attraction for illustrious visitors to the Spanish court." It held two mythological paintings by Correggio, Jupiter and Io and Danae, sent by Pompeo from Lombardy and preserved in the Leoni household in Milan (Lomazzo, Trattato dell'arte de la pittura, 1584); an Entombment by Taddeo Zuccari, a Madonna and Child attributed to Raphael, a St Anthony by Girolamo Muziano, a bronze crucifix attributed to Michelangelo, a self-portrait and a Mary Magdalene by Titian; reliefs, statuettes and busts including four gilded plaster portrait busts of members of the royal family; plaster casts of parts of Michelangelo's sculptures; reliefs from Trajan's Column; wax models, two of which were models for the Escorial royal-family figures. The collection did three kinds of work at once: it taught (the casts after the Antique in the Casa degli Omenoni had done the same in Milan), it displayed rank (the house of a sculptor holding a Raphael and two Correggios), and it supplied raw material (the wax models were the Escorial figures in an earlier state, and the Correggio Danae was later sold to Emperor Rudolf II). He bequeathed the collection to his heirs with the plea that it be kept intact in his memory; it was inventoried in Madrid in 1609 and 1613 and dispersed by sale. The same instinct governed the Leonardo papers: he collected them, sorted them, bound them, and kept them -- the collector's instinct proving stronger than his stated intention to present them to Philip II. Watchable behaviour: a house you visit to see pictures, and in the same rooms the wax models of the kings kneeling in the Escorial -- the collection and the commission are one continuous display. Source: leg 1 (Di Dio 2006 via snippets; Lomazzo via leg 1); leg 3 (Di Dio 2006 via snippets; Tarugi letter via snippet).

**Takeaway:** the house is the workshop's showroom -- collect like a prince, model like a founder, and let the visitors confuse the two.

## M10. Late Simplification: The Valladolid Turn
The last decade simplifies. The San Diego Calvary and Franciscan saints for the convent of San Diego, Valladolid, commissioned 1605 by the Duke of Lerma, are described by the Museo Nacional de Escultura as "delicate and sober monumental pieces, well arranged but quickly executed," with "a clear formal simplification, with wide pleats that model the body's volumes and elegant stances" -- a direct source of inspiration for the young Gregorio Fernandez and the Valladolid school. The Lerma statues at prayer, for the church of San Pablo in Valladolid, are praised by Google Arts and Culture because the sculptor "dictated the standards to the court school, defined by the magnificence of the works, the elegance in the compositions and the figures' royal solemnity." Against the early bronzes, crowded with tritons and nereids and Graces and ram's heads (M3), the late work drops the ornament and lets the pleat do the modelling: wide folds, elegant stance, sober monumentality. The same simplification is visible in the career arc: from the single standing figure in armour, crowded with relief ornament, to the single kneeling figure at prayer in white marble, to the late Valladolid saints in wide pleats. The mechanism is subtraction under pressure -- the workshop finishing in the year of the master's death, the models handed to officers, the ornament dropped because there is no time and no need for it. Watchable behaviour: a saint whose robe is three wide folds and whose stance is the whole composition -- the pleat replaces the picture gallery. Source: leg 2 (Google Arts and Culture Calvario page and Lerma page, both read end to end; P10).

**Takeaway:** the late work subtracts -- wide pleats instead of crowded relief, and the stance carries what the ornament used to carry.


## Evolution
Three phases, from legs 1 and 2. Phase 1, early Milan, under his father (to 1556): the medal as school, the bronze as inheritance. Pompeo learns sculpture and medal-making in the Casa degli Omenoni, where Leone ran a school, a foundry and a cast gallery after the Antique. His first known autonomous work is a medal, for Granvelle, begun at Augsburg in 1551 at about age 18, and his first securely dated surviving medal is the Ercole II d'Este of 1554, with its Patience reverse borrowed from a Michelangelo/Vasari emblem. The mechanism learned here is compression: a portrait on one face, a whole allegory with landscape on the other, inside 7 cm of lead. At the same time he is the second pair of hands on his father's imperial bronzes: the Charles V and Fury and the Philip II were cast in Milan in 1551-1555, and the joint signature LEO. P. POMPE. F. ARET. already appears on the finished works. The phase ends with the move to Spain: in 1556 he accompanies his father's bronze portrait sculptures of the imperial family to Flanders, Charles V is so pleased he takes father and son to Spain, and the retinue arrives on 28 September 1556. Phase 2, Madrid, finishing and then leading (1556-1579): from finisher to court sculptor. The first years are finishing work: the bronze full-length statues of Philip II, the Empress Isabella, Mary of Hungary, and Charles V and Fury; bronze and marble busts of Charles V; a bronze relief of Isabella; portrait reliefs and full-length marble statues; medals for members of the Spanish court. The work is interrupted in 1558 by the Inquisition trial and a year's confinement, then resumes, and the imperial bronzes are finally completed in 1564 -- the year that appears in the signatures. By 1570 he is officially sculptor to the King. The 1570s are his first decade of independent authority: ephemera for the entry of Philip II's new wife Anne of Austria, the pedestal for the relics of Saint Eugene for Toledo Cathedral, and a run of tomb monuments -- Juana of Austria at the Descalzas Reales, Fernando de Valdes at Salas (1576-1582), Cardinal Espinosa at Martin Munoz de las Posadas. The shift visible across the phase: from the single standing figure in armour, crowded with relief ornament, to the single kneeling figure at prayer in white marble -- the tomb figure as a portrait that behaves, permanently, in a devotional posture. Phase 3, the Escorial and the late years (1579-1608): the system at full scale, then the archive. In 1579 Philip II orders the elaborate retable for the Capilla Mayor, and all private commissions halt. Pompeo returns to Milan in 1582, works with his father (and Adriaen de Vries) on the fifteen gilt-bronze figures, stays until 1589, and installs them in 1591. Then, in Madrid, he executes the ten gilt-bronze kneeling figures of the royal tombs, set in place in May 1598, four months before Philip II's death. The same years hold the Leonardo acquisition: in 1589 he comes into possession of Leonardo's notebooks from Orazio Melzi, and over the following years he cuts the notebooks apart and reassembles them by subject into the Codex Atlanticus and the Windsor album -- a second, paper monument built by the same hands that built the bronze one. The 1590s close with the Ferrante Gonzaga statue finished in Milan in 1594. The last decade is Valladolid and Lerma: the Duke and Duchess at prayer, the San Diego Calvary commissioned in 1605 and finished by his workshop in the year of his death, 1608, and the Antonio de Sotelo tomb completed in 1601. The late work simplifies: wide pleats modelling the body's volumes, elegant stances, delicate and sober monumentality -- the museum's words for the San Diego figures -- where the early bronzes had been crowded with ornament.

Early vs mature vs late, stated plainly. Early Pompeo (to 1556) finishes: medals in Milan, the second pair of hands on his father's imperial bronzes, compression learned at 7 cm, the joint signature already in place. Mature Pompeo (1556-1579) leads: the imperial bronzes completed in Madrid in 1564, sculptor to the King by 1570, the tomb run of the 1570s, the single kneeling figure as the new portrait type. Late Pompeo (1579-1608) monumentalises and archives: fifteen gilt-bronze retable figures and ten kneeling kings at the Escorial, the Leonardo papers cut and rebound into two albums, the Ferrante Gonzaga finished in 1594, the Valladolid simplification of the last decade. What stayed constant across all three phases: (1) the bronze as the medium of state, from the 1551 Granvelle medal to the 1598 kneeling kings; (2) the reusable likeness, one head across busts, statues, medals and reliefs, from the 1549 terracotta of Mary of Hungary to the gilded plaster busts in the Madrid house; (3) the workshop relay, father to son to officers, with the signature recording the chain; (4) the collection as the second workshop, from the casts in the Casa degli Omenoni to the Correggios in the Madrid house to the Leonardo albums. The through-line is not a style change but a scale change: the same medallic compression, the same reusable face, the same sectional casting, applied first to 7 cm of lead, then to a 251 cm emperor, then to a basilica full of kneeling kings, then to 1,119 leaves of another man's notebooks.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is one operation repeated at every scale: take a likeness, cast it in sections, and let the assembly be the work. In bronze the operation is literal -- the medal compressed to 7 cm, the armour crowded with emblems, the emperor unscrewed from hero to king (M1, M2, M3) -- and the same operation governs the tombs, where five figures a side repeat one gesture until the dead are permanently present at Mass (M4); the archive, where another man's notebooks are cut apart and regrouped by subject until they become two books (M5); the likeness, where one head is reused across busts, statues, medals and reliefs until attribution itself becomes insecure (M6); the surface, where gold on bronze and gold on plaster issue the same face at two prices (M7); and the workshop, where father casts, son finishes, and officers complete, and one signature covers three decades (M8). The second constant is the house: wherever Pompeo works, he builds a room that shows the work -- the casts in the Casa degli Omenoni, the Correggios and the wax models in the Madrid house -- and the collection is the second workshop, teaching, displaying rank, and supplying raw material (M9). The third constant is subtraction at the end: the late Valladolid saints drop the crowded relief and let wide pleats do the modelling, the ornament falling away because there is no time and no need for it (M10). Against Melzi the contrast is exact and is the point of the doorway: Melzi's logic is custodianship that ends the observation, the notebooks gathered, ordered, chaptered and then kept like relics; Pompeo's logic is custodianship that cuts the observation apart, the notebooks dismembered, sorted by subject, pasted onto atlas sheets and bound -- the same papers, the same fifty years of guarding, but one man binds them into a book and the other cuts them into two. One leaves the pages blank where he cannot decide; the other decides with scissors and never looks back. The essay voice of the study is this: Pompeo Leoni is not a lesser Leone, and not a failed Leone; he is the condition without which the Escorial would be an empty basilica and Leonardo would be a rumour in a different way -- the hand that could cast a 718 kg emperor in pieces and screw him together, the eye that could see a map and a face on the same sheet and file them in different books, the collector who paid for the greatest archive in art with a promise of preferment and kept it, and the founder whose foundry outlived him by a generation.

## POSITIONS
- P1. Birth c.1533 (Benezit) or 1530 (Schmidt); birthplace Milan (Wikipedia) or probably Venice (Di Dio). Neither date nor place is settled; the study must carry the caveat. Source: leg 1; see C232.
- P2. Pompeo learned sculpture and medal-making in his father's Casa degli Omenoni in Milan, where Leone ran a school, a foundry and a cast gallery after the Antique. Source: EN Wikipedia Pompeo Leoni (read end to end); leg 1.
- P3. He settled in Madrid in 1561, buying a house and studio facing the Calle de las Tabernillas, after following the court through Valladolid, Guadalajara and Toledo. Source: Di Dio 2006 via leg 1; EN Wikipedia Pompeo Leoni.
- P4. In 1558 he was brought before the Inquisition on charges of supporting Lutheranism and sentenced to a year's confinement in a monastery; he resumed work on release. Source: Prado artist page (read end to end by leg 2); Di Dio 2006 via leg 1.
- P5. By 1570 he was officially established as sculptor to Philip II, preparing the entry ephemera for Ana de Austria and the pedestal for the relics of Saint Eugene at Toledo. Source: Di Dio 2006 via leg 1.
- P6. The Escorial retable commission (1579) required fifteen bronze figures; Pompeo worked in Milan 1582-1589 with his father, the bronzes were installed in 1591, and the ten gilt bronze kneeling Habsburg figures flanking the high altar followed. FLAG: EN Wikipedia gives a 1587 assembly date for the mausoleum group, which conflicts with Di Dio's 1591 installation and is unresolved in this leg. Source: Di Dio 2006 via legs 1 and 2; see C233.
- P7. Leoni obtained Leonardo's manuscripts in 1587-1590 not by cash purchase but by promising Orazio Melzi official honours and preferment, recovering seven volumes through the Mazzenta brothers and three more from the Mazzenta heirs; the total number of additional volumes is unknown ("as well as others, how many is unknown" -- McMurrich). Source: McMurrich (1930) and discoveringdavinci.com via leg 1.
- P8. He cut the notebooks apart and rebound them as two subject-sorted albums: the Codex Atlanticus (1,119 leaves / 2,238 pages, 12 volumes, technical-scientific) and the Windsor album (about 600 sheets on 234 folios, artistic/anatomical/botanical). Giovanni Magenta's 1630 account accuses him of being one of the main culprits in the dispersal and of having altered the manuscripts' order. Source: EN Wikipedia Codex Atlanticus (read end to end); Google Arts and Culture "Ten Key Facts"; leg 1.
- P9. He died in Madrid in October 1608; the 1610 death date in McMurrich (1930) and the British Museum medal guide (1893) is superseded. His heir is named Polidoro Calchi by discoveringdavinci and Cleodoro Calchi by McMurrich -- FLAG, unresolved. Source: legs 1 and 2; see C234 and C235.
- P10. He never gave any Leonardo manuscript to Philip II despite that being his stated motive; the collector's instinct proved stronger than the original intention, and the Codex Atlanticus stayed in his possession until his death. Source: discoveringdavinci.com (read end to end by leg 1); Di Dio 2006 via leg 1.
- P11. Pompeo's first known autonomous work is the Granvelle medal, begun at Augsburg in 1551 -- not the Ercole II d'Este medal of 1554, which is his first securely dated surviving medal. Source: Di Dio 2006 biographical sketch via leg 2.
- P12. The Ercole II d'Este reverse, Patience chained to a rock, derives from a Michelangelo/Vasari emblem for Bishop Minerbetti, with the motto supplied by Annibale Caro -- the medal is an act of translation between artists, not an invention from nothing. Source: Waldman 1994 via leg 2.
- P13. The Charles V and Fury group was a joint father-son production in stages: commissioned 1549, nude figure and armour cast 1551, Fury cast 1553, pedestal 1555, finished in Spain 1564. Any single date for it is wrong. Source: Museo del Prado Charles V and Fury page (read end to end by leg 2).
- P14. The Philip II and Mary of Hungary bronzes carry the joint signature LEO. P. POMPE. F. ARET. F. 1564, recording Madrid finishing of Milan castings -- the signature is evidence of the workshop relay, not of a single author or year. Source: Museo del Prado Philip II and Mary of Hungary pages via leg 2.
- P15. The three full-length bronzes of Isabella, Philip II, and Mary of Hungary were commissioned in 1549 for Mary of Hungary's dynastic gallery at Binche, and reached Spain only because Binche was destroyed and the court moved. Their Spanish afterlife is an accident of war, not the original plan. Source: Prado via leg 2; Web Gallery of Art via leg 2.
- P16. Pompeo was tried by the Inquisition in 1558 for Lutheranism and sentenced to a year's confinement in a monastery; his career was interrupted, not ended, and the imperial bronzes were completed in 1564 after his release. Source: Prado artist page (read end to end by leg 2); Di Dio 2006 via leg 1.
- P17. The Escorial high-altar figures (fifteen gilt bronzes, installed 1591) and the royal tomb figures (ten gilt bronzes, set in place May 1598) are two separate commissions, seven years apart; the 1587 date sometimes attached to the tombs is not supported by Di Dio or WGA and should not be used. Source: Di Dio 2006 and Web Gallery of Art via leg 2; see C233.
- P18. The Valdes tomb is dated 1576-1582 in Wikipedia but the Salas guide says the mausoleum was completed 1576-1582 and the parents' tombs finalised in 1586; the ensemble date is best given as 1576-1586, with the discrepancy flagged. Source: Wikipedia and Fundacion Valdes-Salas 2025 via leg 2.
- P19. Leoni obtained Leonardo's papers from Orazio Melzi (seven of thirteen manuscripts retrieved and given to him) and dismembered notebooks to form two subject-sorted volumes; the Codex Atlanticus holds the technical-scientific sheets (1,119 leaves, 2,238 pages, 12 volumes) and the Windsor album the artistic and anatomical sheets (about 600 sheets on 234 folios). Source: EN Wikipedia Codex Atlanticus (read end to end); Google Arts and Culture "The Leoni binding" (read end to end by leg 2).
- P20. Pompeo's late Valladolid work was finished by his workshop: the San Diego Calvary (commissioned 1605) was possibly made by his officer Millan Vimercato and his son Miguel Angel Leoni over his models, ending in 1608; the museum text describes a formal simplification -- wide pleats, elegant stances -- that marks a real stylistic shift from the crowded relief of the 1550s bronzes. Source: Google Arts and Culture Calvario page (read end to end by leg 2).
