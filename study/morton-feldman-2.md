## M1. Graph notation as time-boxes with free pitch choice

The score is a grid of boxes on graph paper, pinned to the wall like a painting, and vertical position means high, middle or low register, and the player chooses the actual pitches inside the register, and each time-box holds four icti at about MM 72, and rectangle length is duration, and placement in the box is the attack moment, and dynamics are very soft throughout. Projection 1 of 1950, for solo cello, written for Seymour Barab, is the first graph piece, and the five Projections were composed December 1950 to January 1951. What you see is a page that looks like a building plan, and a cellist choosing notes inside a box the way a painter chooses a color inside a shape. **Takeaway:** fix the box, free the note, and the page becomes the piece. Against the chain: Cage also used a grid, M5, tabulating every parameter into charts and letting a shape drawn across the table choose, but his grid decides the notes, and Feldman's grid decides only the register, and the difference is between a chart that composes and a box that frames.

## M2. Number-of-notes notation

Arabic numerals in the piano part state HOW MANY pitches to play at once, not which ones, and encircled numerals in the later graph works state the number of successively played single notes. The Projections of 1950 to 1951 and The Straits of Magellan of 1961 use it. What you see is a number where a note should be, and the player supplying the notes, and the count doing the composing. **Takeaway:** notate the count, not the chord, and the number becomes the harmony. Against the chain: Cage also notated by number, M6, the I Ching choosing pitches from a chart, but his numbers are answers from a book, and Feldman's numbers are counts handed to the player, and the difference is between a chance operation and a quantity.

## M3. Piano flageolet and sympathetic resonance

The pianist silently depresses keys with the left hand and plays sounding tones with the right, so the undamped strings ring sympathetically, and in Projection 2 a trumpet is played into the open piano to excite the piano's harmonics, and mutes are used throughout. Projection 2 and Projection 3, both 1951, are the works. What you see is a piano played without being played, the keys going down in silence, and the sound arriving from strings nobody touched. **Takeaway:** press the key without sounding it, and the piano answers from underneath. Against the chain: Cage also changed the piano, M2, preparing it with screws and bolts so one pair of hands becomes an orchestra, but his preparation is hardware in the strings, and Feldman's is a hand on silent keys, and the difference is between a piano rebuilt and a piano overheard.

## M4. Racecourse design: free duration over fixed pitches

The pitches and their succession are fully notated, as stemless note-heads with no meter and no bar-lines, and each player chooses their own durations within a general tempo, so the parts drift out of vertical alignment and re-meet, and each instrument lives out its own individual life in its own individual sound world. The five Durations of 1960 to 1961 do this, and Two Pianos of 1957 is the earlier variant, one score played simultaneously on two pianos, the two pianists gradually diverging. What you see is four players starting together and slowly coming apart, the same notes arriving at different times, and the piece becoming a race nobody wins. **Takeaway:** fix the notes, free the clock, and the ensemble becomes four solitudes. Against the chain: Cunningham also fixed a clock and let the arts meet only at its marks, M15, but his clock binds dancers to music at unit boundaries, and Feldman's clock frees each player inside a shared tempo, and the difference is between a meeting time and a drift.

## M5. Decontrol in sequence, then catastrophe

Give up the controls one parameter at a time, pitch, then rhythm, then dynamics, and as controls are given up the elements lose their initial, inherent identity, and an indeterminate music can lead only to catastrophe, and this catastrophe we allowed to take place, and behind it was sound, which unified everything. Feldman, "Determinate/Indeterminate", 1965. What you see is a composer removing the floorboards one by one and discovering the room still stands, because the sound itself holds it up. **Takeaway:** surrender the parameters in order, and the catastrophe becomes the structure. Against the chain: Cage also surrendered control, M4, but he surrendered it to a system, the I Ching, and kept the system, and Feldman surrendered it to the sound and then took control back, and the difference is between a composer who trusts the book and a composer who trusts the ear.

## M6. Sound as a plastic phenomenon: projection into time

From Varese, sound projection, the projection of sound in space, immobile sonic textures, Varese's music as a floating sculpture, and Feldman's graph music aims at sound as totally plastic, suggesting its own shape, design and poetic metaphor, and his own phrase is that his desire was not to compose but to project sounds into time. What you see is sounds placed in time the way objects are placed in a room, and the piece becoming a sculpture you walk through by listening. **Takeaway:** project the sound into time the way a sculptor places stone in a room. Against the chain: Rauschenberg also made a room out of objects, M9, the working object, but his objects are things you can touch, and Feldman's are sounds you can only hear, and the difference is between a room you enter and a room you listen to.

## M7. Interval fields

Coherence is built not from themes but from the frequent recurrence of certain intervals or interval groups. In Two Pieces for Six Instruments of 1956, major seconds, perfect fourths, fifths and octaves predominate, and Martin Erdmann's term for this is interval fields. What you see is a piece with no melody and no theme that still holds together, because the same small distances keep returning, and the ear learns the field the way a eye learns a room. **Takeaway:** repeat the interval, not the tune, and the field becomes the theme. Against the chain: Johns also built coherence from recurrence, M11, a system that generates the picture, but his system is a device that draws, and Feldman's is a field that repeats, and the difference is between a machine and a climate.

## M8. Thinning and thickening: accents by density, not dynamics

Chords in which most tones stop immediately after entry, leaving one or two sustained tones, and accents are produced by how many tones sound at once, not by louder playing. Two Pieces for Six Instruments of 1956 and Durations 3 of 1961 use it. What you see is a chord that gets louder by getting thicker, and thinner by getting sparser, and the volume never changing at all. **Takeaway:** make the accent out of how many, not how loud, and density becomes dynamics. Against the chain: Cunningham also made emphasis without force, M4, the non-representational step carrying weight by placement, but his emphasis is where the dancer stands, and Feldman's is how many notes sound, and the difference is between a body in space and a chord in time.

## M9. Breaking the horizontal line: the vertical experience

When the horizontal line, time, is broken, the vertical experience, no time, emerges, and time does not shape the sound, the sound shapes time. Feldman on The Straits of Magellan of 1961, where pitch registers are decontrolled, timbre is fanned out, and the density of events is regulated instead of melodic continuity, and the piece is all over like a painting. What you see is a piece with no forward motion and no story, and the listener standing inside a single moment that keeps renewing itself. **Takeaway:** break the line, and the moment becomes the piece. Against the chain: Cage also broke the line, M3, the closed piano and the silence as material, but his break is the absence of sound, and Feldman's is the presence of sound without direction, and the difference is between a silence and a stillness.

## M10. Retranslation: the same thought said another way

The working method of the late period is to state something in a pitch situation, restate it more intervallically, take the suggestions back into another kind of pitchiness, again with another focus, another register, another colour, and the model is Beckett writing in English, translating into French, translating back, and the thought said in another way, and many times another language. Related: Feldman did not let his students use repeat signs, because something might happen right at the end of the measure. What you see is the same small idea returning in a different light, and the piece becoming a conversation with itself in several languages. **Takeaway:** say it again in another register, and the repetition becomes a translation. Against the chain: Duchamp also remade a thing in another medium, M15, the cast shadow and the trace, but his remake is a copy that distorts, and Feldman's is a thought restated, and the difference is between a trace and a translation.

## M11. Crippled symmetry: near-repetition with one small deliberate error

A pattern returns almost the same, but displaced, so the symmetry is crippled, and three different patterns run at different rates of repetition at once, vibraphone fastest, flute slower, celesta slowest, with palindromic mirroring between parts. Crippled Symmetry of 1983 is the work, and the model is the Near and Middle Eastern rugs Feldman collected, and his words are that a growing interest in those rugs made him question notions he previously held on what is symmetrical and what is not. What you see is a pattern you recognize and a pattern that is wrong, and the wrongness is the point, and the eye, or the ear, catches that something is off and has to look again. **Takeaway:** repeat it almost right, and the error becomes the ornament. Against the chain: Johns also repeated with a difference, M9, the duplication that fails, but his failure is a copy that cannot match, and Feldman's is a pattern deliberately crippled, and the difference is between a machine that misses and a hand that chooses to miss.

## M12. Abrash: the same, but not the same

In old rugs dyed in small batches with vegetable dyes, the same colour shifts slightly from batch to batch, a microtonal hue, a shimmer of slight gradation, and Feldman takes this as the model for doubling and for microtonal spelling, double flats and sharps, in String Quartet II, and his words are that he hears a minor 2nd like a minor 3rd almost, it is very, very wide, and deep blue needs re-dyeing over and over, and the weaver's fussiness about timbre is the model. What you see is the same note spelled two ways, and the same colour dyed twice, and the difference so small it can only be felt, not named. **Takeaway:** dye the same colour twice, and the difference becomes the richness. Against the chain: Rauschenberg also doubled a thing, M7, the duplication that fails, but his doubling is two canvases that refuse to match, and Feldman's is one colour dyed twice, and the difference is between a twin and a batch.

## M13. Duration as material: scale, not form

Long works create an overarching, protective space around a vulnerable huddle of sounds, and Feldman's own reason, at Darmstadt in 1984, is that he has the time and the money to write long pieces, economics, like Henry Moore making things big once he could afford the foundry, and the necessity is for increased sophistication in the perception of music as a memory form. String Quartet II of 1983, about six hours in full, For Philip Guston of 1984, four to five hours, and Piano and String Quartet of 1985, about 80 minutes, are the works. What you see is a piece that goes on so long the listener stops waiting for it to end, and starts living inside it, and the length becomes the shelter. **Takeaway:** make it long enough to live in, and the duration becomes the room. Against the chain: Cunningham also made duration the room, M16, Torse fifty-five minutes of torso-driven difficulty, but his duration is the body's endurance, and Feldman's is the memory's endurance, and the difference is between a muscle and a recollection.

## M14. Quiet dynamics as structure

Dynamics are kept very soft throughout, sounds played with a minimum of attack, from the Projections to the last works, and after one too-loud, too-fast performance Feldman barked, "It's too fuckin' loud, and it's too fuckin' fast", and in the late work quiet sounds had become the only ones that interested him. What you see is a piece that never raises its voice, and the listener leaning in, and the leaning in becoming part of the piece. **Takeaway:** keep it quiet enough that the listener has to come to it. Against the chain: Cage also made quiet the material, M3, the silence as the content of 4'33", but his quiet is the room sounding, and Feldman's is the sound almost not sounding, and the difference is between an audience hearing itself and a note barely existing.

## M15. Patterns in a chromatic field

A tiny pitch collection, in String Quartet II essentially the minor 2nd and the major 2nd, those three notes, repeated with slight variation for hours, so the listener loses the sense of measure, and repetitions are recognizable as repetitions, but the patterns of those repetitions are not discernible. Patterns in a Chromatic Field of 1981 and String Quartet II of 1983 are the works. What you see is three notes going on for hours, and the ear losing count, and the pattern becoming a field, the way a rug's pattern becomes a field when you stop counting the knots. **Takeaway:** take three notes and repeat them until the count fails, and the field appears. Against the chain: Twombly also repeated a small mark until it became a field, M9 in his study, but his repetition is a hand writing, and Feldman's is a chord returning, and the difference is between a scribble and a hum.

## M16. Dedication as form: mourning as method

Works titled for friends, the dead, and the living, each carrying the relationship into the music: For Franz Kline of 1962, Piano Piece (to Philip Guston) of 1963, For Frank O'Hara of 1973, an elegy after O'Hara's death in 1966, Rothko Chapel of 1971, for the dead Rothko, For John Cage of 1982, For Philip Guston of 1984, working over the 1968 rupture, For Christian Wolff of 1986, For Samuel Beckett of 1987, and Dohoney's thesis is that friendship and mourning sustained the collective aesthetics of the New York School, and Feldman's music becomes increasingly concerned with what survives, what endures, and what can still be heard, even as it ebbs. What you see is a piece named for a person, and the person gone, and the music going on anyway, and the going on being the memorial. **Takeaway:** name the piece for the friend, and the dedication becomes the form. Against the chain: Cage also dedicated work to friends, M14, reworking found music by chance, but his dedication is a remake, and Feldman's is a mourning, and the difference is between a gift and an elegy.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is the container, and the content is free inside it, and the strictness is never in the notes, it is in the frame, and the frame is the box, the number, the duration, the quiet, the pattern, and the notes are whatever the player chooses, or whatever the chord suggests, or whatever the rug dictates, and the whole practice is a refusal, a refusal of rhetoric, a refusal of the loud gesture, a refusal of the form that announces itself, and the refusal is carried out by making the surface so flat and so quiet that the listener has to come to it, and the coming to it is the piece, and the piece is between categories, between time and space, between painting and music, between the music's construction and its surface, and he is not a clockmaker, and he is interested in getting to time in its unstructured existence, how this wild beast lives in the jungle not in the zoo, and the wild beast is the sound, and the jungle is the duration, and the zoo would be the form, and he never built the zoo, and the rug taught him that a pattern can be wrong in one place and right everywhere else, and the wrong place is where the weaver is, and the weaver is the composer, and the composer is the one who decides where the outer edge is, and the decision as to where the outer edge is, is final, not initial, and he worked until the piece had become its size, the way a painter finds that a canvas is becoming ten by ten, and the size is the piece, and the piece is the size, and the quiet is the piece, and the piece is the quiet, and the mourning is the piece, and the piece is the mourning, and what survives is what can still be heard, even as it ebbs.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. THE "1970 BREAK" THAT DESTROYED THE FRIENDSHIP FAILS. No source read documents a Cage-Feldman break in 1970, or any dated break at all. What the sources document for 1970 is Feldman's music changing radically toward rhythmic precision, Madame Press dying and Feldman writing her elegy, Feldman meeting the violist Karen Philipps, and Rothko dying on Wednesday 25 February 1970. On the friendship itself, Alex Ross says that in later years they did not always think alike, yet the two retained a fraternal bond, and Feldman dedicated For John Cage to Cage in 1982, twelve years after the supposed break, and Cage is quoted talking with Feldman in the 1984 Darmstadt lecture. The doorway conflates the 1970 stylistic turn with a friendship break, and possibly with the Guston rupture, see C2. The study does not present a 1970 Cage-Feldman break as fact. C2. THE DOCUMENTED RUPTURE IS WITH GUSTON, IN 1968, NOT WITH CAGE IN 1970. Per Dohoney via the Gotham review, Feldman and Guston ruptured at the Marlborough Gallery in 1968, when Guston, who had controversially turned away from abstraction and toward figuration, took offense at Feldman's response to one of his paintings, and Feldman remembered, "With that, our friendship was over". The two kept working over the friendship afterward, For Philip Guston of 1984, and Guston's Friend, to M.F., of 1978. The doorway's "friendship the 1970 break destroyed" fits Guston 1968 far better than Cage 1970, but even there the year is wrong, 1968 not 1970, and the friend is wrong, Guston not Cage. C3. THE LOBBY MEETING IS CONFIRMED IN SUBSTANCE, AND THE EXACT DAY IS UNVERIFIED. Early 1950, January 1950 per IRCAM and the Gotham review: Feldman heard the New York Philharmonic play Webern's Symphony op. 21, conducted by Dimitri Mitropoulos, at Carnegie Hall, the next item was Rachmaninoff, both men walked out early, met in the lobby, Feldman asked "Wasn't that beautiful?", and a friendship was born. Nuances: the exact day is not given in any source read, do not invent one; the Gotham review says Cage and Feldman had been acquainted during the 1940s but bonded after the concert, so "met" should be phrased as "bonded" or "met decisively"; the Ruhrtriennale biography dates the meeting to 1949, which conflicts with January 1950 and with the concert's 1949 to 1950 season, treat 1949 as an error unless a program is found; Feldman soon moved into Cage's building on Grand Street. C4. "CO-REALIZATION OF WILLIAMS MIX" IS OVERSTATED, CORRECT TO "ASSISTED". Williams Mix of 1951 to 1953 is Cage's piece, 4 minutes 16 seconds, eight simultaneous tapes, a 193-page graphic score like a dressmaker's pattern, I Ching chance operations, premiered Monday 23 March 1953 at the University of Illinois. Wikipedia says it was created by Cage with the assistance of Earle Brown, Morton Feldman, David Tudor, and Bebe and Louis Barron, but the detailed accounts name the actual splicing team as Cage, Brown and Tudor only, and one account credits Feldman with one specific intervention, getting Cage to include 1,097 sounds into a quarter of an inch of tape, which is a snippet-only fact and is not foregrounded here. Feldman's own contribution to the Project for Music for Magnetic Tape was a separate piece, Intersection for Magnetic Tape of 1953. So Feldman assisted in the realization, he did not co-realize it as an equal. C5. "KEPT CHANCE MUSICAL RATHER THAN THEATRICAL" IS MISLEADING AS PHRASED. The fair version has two halves. True: Feldman refused the direction Cage took toward theater and anarchy, and Ross writes that composers were filling their scores with patterns, pictures and verbal instructions, and matters progressed to the logical extreme of Cage's Theatre Piece of 1960, during which a piano was slapped with a dead fish, but Feldman had no taste for anarchy, and when he realized his notation could lead to a circus atmosphere he turned in another direction. False as worded: Feldman did not keep chance, he abandoned it, and the DRAM notes say that not interested in improvisation and not believing in chance, Feldman was disappointed by the poor performances of his graph scores, and he gave up graph notation because performers improvised cliches. By 1970 he had returned to fully precise notation. The accurate frame is that both men started from indeterminacy, Cage radicalized it into chance operations, silence and theater, and Feldman retreated from it toward exact notation and the sound itself, and the influence ran both ways, Feldman's graph scores pushed Cage toward Music of Changes and 4'33". C6. "BETWEEN CATEGORIES" IS A 1969 PIECE, NOT A DARMSTADT LECTURE. Between Categories of 1969 is a composition for two pianos, two chimes, two violins and two cellos. The Darmstadt lectures were 26 July 1984, the morning after the String Quartet II European premiere, and 1986, a lecture on For Christian Wolff. Feldman also taught at Darmstadt 1984 to 1986. The doorway brief's pairing of "Darmstadt lectures" with "Between Categories" conflates the two. C7. BIRTHPLACE CONFLICT. Wikipedia and Ross say Woodside, Queens, New York City. IRCAM says born in Manhattan. One source says born in Brooklyn. The majority and most specific source says Woodside, Queens, and the study uses it and records the conflict, and does not assert Manhattan or Brooklyn. C8. BUFFALO APPOINTMENT YEAR, 1972 AGAINST 1973. Wikipedia and IRCAM say 1973, age 47, Edgard Varese Professor, a title of his own devising. Ross says that in 1972 he obtained his post at SUNY Buffalo. Unresolved in the sources read, and the 1973 date is the better-sourced one, appointment against joining the faculty may differ, and the study uses 1973. C9. STRING QUARTET II DURATION CONFLICT. Over six hours without a break per Wikipedia, nearly five hours per IRCAM, almost five hours in one review, 6 hours 7 minutes in the Flux Quartet's 2003 recording, originally five-hours-plus and truncated to about four hours for the world premiere on Canadian radio, and Ross heard the Flux Quartet play the six-hour-long String Quartet II in 1999. Safe phrasing, about six hours in full, and the premiere version was cut to about four hours. C10. FOR PHILIP GUSTON DURATION CONFLICT. Around four hours per Wikipedia, five hours long in the 1995 Kotik performance Ross heard. Safe phrasing, four to five hours. C11. COPTIC LIGHT DATE CONFLICT. 1985 per the works list, 1986 per Encyclopaedia Judaica. Unresolved, and the works list is the better source for the catalogue date, and the study uses 1985. C12. SUMMERSPACE DATE, 1958 NOT 1966. Encyclopaedia Judaica dates the Cunningham collaboration 1966. The premiere was Sunday 17 August 1958, American Dance Festival, Connecticut College, and 1966 is the New York City Ballet revival, Thursday 14 April 1966. The 1966 date is the revival, not the creation. C13. ROSS SLIP ON THE FLEXIBLE-DURATION SERIES. Ross writes of a subsequent series of works, which began appearing in 1957, which specified pitches but allowed the performer to decide when and how long they should be played. The series that does this is the Durations, 1960 to 1961. 1957 is Two Pianos, a different device, one score, two pianos, free durations. Ross conflates the two, and the study uses 1960 to 1961 for the Durations. C14. FAMILY BUSINESS EXIT DATE. Ross says he worked in the family business until he was forty-four years old, that is until 1970. Wikipedia says he earned his living there until the 1973 Buffalo appointment. Feldman was dean of the New York Studio School 1969 to 1971, which supports an exit around 1969 to 1970, and Wikipedia's "until then" is loose phrasing. C15. ROTHKO CHAPEL, COMPOSED 1971, PREMIERED 1972. Listings that date the piece 1971 give the composition year. The premiere in the chapel was Sunday 9 April 1972. Rothko himself died Wednesday 25 February 1970 and never saw the chapel, which opened in February 1971. C16. THE "NEW YORK SCHOOL" LABEL IS HASTY AND DOUBLE. IRCAM says Wolff, Brown and Tudor soon formed a group around Cage and Feldman, which was perhaps somewhat hastily dubbed the "New York School", about whom Henry Cowell wrote an article titled "Cage and His Friends" in January 1952 in The Musical Quarterly. The music grouping is Cage, Feldman, Wolff and Brown, with Tudor as the pianist, and Feldman counts four composers, joined by the pianist David Tudor, so Tudor is a member of the circle but not counted among the four composers. The painters' New York School, Pollock, de Kooning, Rothko, Guston, Kline, Newman, is a different, larger grouping that Feldman moved in as a friend and ally, not a member. The study keeps the two apart. C17. GOTHAM REVIEW SLIP, "FELDMAN'S FIRST WORK". The review calls the early-1950s graph pieces "Feldman's first work". He had been composing since the 1940s, First Piano Sonata 1943, Only 1947. The graph pieces are his first mature, first indeterminate works, not his first works, and the study does not repeat the review's phrasing. C18. DARMSTADT LECTURE ANNOTATOR CORRECTION, FELDMAN MISATTRIBUTION. The lecture's own footnotes flag Feldman's errors: "hardening of the categories" is McLuhan's phrase, not Norbert Wiener's as Feldman said, and the Sibelius tune he sang is from the Fifth Symphony's last movement, not the Fourth as he implied. Feldman misremembered details in talk, and the study quotes him with the annotator's corrections where they exist. C19. THE 1,097 SOUNDS DETAIL. Mellors says Morton Feldman got Cage to include 1,097 sounds into a quarter of an inch of tape in Williams Mix. This is the one specific, sourced Feldman intervention in Williams Mix, and it is a snippet-only fact, not read in a full source, and the study records it here and does not foreground it.
