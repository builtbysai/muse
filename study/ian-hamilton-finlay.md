## The life: the poet who left the page, and the garden that replaced it

Ian Hamilton Finlay was born on 28 October 1925 in Nassau, Bahamas, to James Hamilton Finlay and Annie Pettigrew, both of Scots descent; his father bootlegged alcohol from Nassau into the United States until the repeal of prohibition in 1933, then tried orange-growing in Florida and returned to Scotland "in straitened circumstances". (C12500, C12502, leg 1) Finlay was sent back to Scotland at six, boarded at Larchfield School near Helensburgh and then Dollar Academy, and was evacuated at thirteen to Gartmore and then Kirkcudbright at the outbreak of war. (C12502, leg 1) He attended Glasgow School of Art briefly and never completed a degree; his son Alec reports he was expelled for organising a student strike, second-hand, not a primary-source fact. (C12503, leg 1) In 1942 he joined the British Army and served in the Non-Combatant and Service Corps until 1947, spending time in postwar Germany; no source consulted mentions the RAF at all, and the brief's "RAF service" is wrong -- do not repeat it. (C12504, C12505, C12545, leg 1) After the war he worked as a shepherd and labourer in Orkney, then began writing short stories and poems while living on Rousay. (C12506, leg 1) Throughout his life he "suffered severely from agoraphobia", which kept him from travelling to where his work was installed; a stroke in the early 2000s reversed the agoraphobia and let him travel again in his final years. (C12507, leg 1)

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the move from the page to the landscape: the work starts as lyric and dialect poetry, becomes concrete poetry on cards and in booklets, leaves the page for walls and glass, becomes a garden, and ends as war in Arcadia -- tank, carrier, frieze, neon, gun as flute.

(1) Lyric and dialect, to 1962. His first book was The Sea-Bed and Other Stories (1958); his first poetry collection, The Dancers Inherit the Party, was published in 1960 by Migrant Press, with second and third editions in 1962 and 1969. (C12508, leg 1) Glasgow Beasts, an' a Burd (1961) exploited Glaswegian dialect to comic effect and, Sotheran's notes, "alienated Finlay from more traditional Scottish poets and was the start of a long standing antipathy between him and the Scottish art establishment in general". (C12509, leg 1) In a letter of 18 October 1967 he wrote: "Even my attempt to be 'traditional' was a way of being concrete, part of a plainness I wanted". (C12509, leg 1)

(2) Concrete poetry, Wild Hawthorn Press, Poor. Old. Tired. Horse., 1961-1968. Wild Hawthorn Press was founded in Edinburgh in 1961 with Jessie McGuffie, and Sotheran's says it "enabled him to control the design and the quality of the printed works that followed" and published other poets' work as books as well as poem-prints, "a format Finlay pioneered". (C12510, leg 1) Poor. Old. Tired. Horse. (P.O.T.H.), his verbal-visual periodical, ran 1962-1968 in 25 issues, named after a poem by Robert Creeley; its first visual poem appears in no. 5, and the last edition is a collection of one-word poems. (C12511, leg 1) Rapel (1963), his first collection of concrete poetry, was "printed in coloured inks as well as black, and replacing conventional syntax with clusters and geometric patterns", ten loose leaves in a buff folder, with homages to Malevich and Juan Gris. (C12512, leg 1) He reached concrete poetry through Edwin Morgan, who in May or June 1962 alerted him to a letter by E. M. de Melo e Castro in the Times Literary Supplement; that summer he began corresponding with Augusto de Campos, whose address Morgan had passed on, and by spring 1963 he was publishing Augusto's work in P.O.T.H. No. 6. (C12627, leg 3; C12513, leg 1) The Canal Stripe and Ocean Stripe booklets are "kinetic booklet/poems", because "turning the pages enacted the poetry"; Ocean Stripe uses overprinting and pure punctuation to suggest the wake of a boat. (C12514, C12567, legs 1-2) He reduced the monostich to one word, and said "a literal one-word poem would make no sense at all, but it could communicate if it was placed in a garden setting that provided the context" and "the pure concrete poem (is) not for reading, but for contemplation". (C12514, leg 1) His first solo exhibition of concrete poetry was at the Axiom Gallery, London, in 1968; Bann's press contacts helped bring it about. (C12513, leg 1)

(3) Gledfield and Stonypath, 1965-1966. In 1965 Finlay moved to Gledfield Farmhouse, Ardgay, Ross-shire, with his new partner Sue Swan, later Sue Finlay, where he "was able to construct poems in wood and cork, and place them within the landscape around the house"; Acrobats was made of cork letters stuck to a white barn wall, "the perfect example of a concrete poem, where the letters of the word are the 'acrobats' and the reader's eye must partake of the exercise, too". (C12515, C12517, leg 1) In autumn 1966 Ian and Sue Finlay moved to Stonypath, a dilapidated hill farm near Dunsyre in the Pentland Hills, about 25-30 miles south-west of Edinburgh, at about 270-280 m above sea level, "remote", "wild" and "exposed". (C12518, leg 1) The garden's size is disputed: five acres per the National Galleries of Scotland and Wikipedia, seven acres per the Little Sparta Trust. (C12518, leg 1) He renamed the garden "Little Sparta" as a riposte to Edinburgh's nickname, the "Athens of the North"; the year is disputed -- 1978, 1979, 1980 and 1983 all appear in reputable sources -- so it is given here as a range, around 1978-1983. (C12519-C12521, C12641, legs 1 and 3)

(4) The garden, the war, Saint-Just, 1966-1991. The garden was conceived around inter-connected pools, burns and the small loch, Lochan Eck; the first area developed was the cottage garden in front of the house, then a Roman Garden, a Temple Pool Garden and a Wild Garden, with a small English Parkland added in the 1990s. (C12522, C12524, leg 1) Sue Finlay "undertook the majority of the planting and cultivation", and their collaboration at Little Sparta lasted 23 years; she left in 1989. (C12523, leg 1) Finlay came into conflict with Strathclyde Regional Council over rates on a byre in his garden, which the council insisted was commercial premises and Finlay insisted was a garden temple; his propaganda campaign of 1982-1985 became the Little Spartan War, and on 4 February 1983 a sheriff officer attempting to seize works of art in lieu of unpaid rates was confronted by Finlay's supporters, the Saint-Just Vigilantes. (C12525, leg 1) The French Revolution and Saint-Just became the central figure: liberty and terror, virtue and violence, Neo-Classical language with the French Revolution "recurs frequently in the artist's work". (C12526, leg 1) His use of Nazi imagery led to an accusation of neo-Nazi sympathies and antisemitism, the loss of two commissions for the 1989 bicentenary of the French Revolution, and a lawsuit against the Paris magazine Art Press in which he was awarded nominal damages of one franc; Wikipedia states: "The stress of this situation brought about the separation between Finlay and his wife Sue". (C12529, leg 1) He was shortlisted for the Turner Prize in 1985. (C12530, leg 1)

(5) Late works and honours, 1990s-2006. Honorary doctorates from Aberdeen (1987), Heriot-Watt (1993) and Glasgow (2001); the French Communist Party presented him with a bust of Saint-Just in 1991; appointed CBE in the Queen's 2002 New Year Honours; the Scottish Horticultural Medal (2002) and the Scottish Arts Council Creative Scotland Award (2003). (C12531, leg 1) Late works were made largely outside Scotland and often in absentia: Fleur de l'Air in Provence, 1997-2003, worked on for a decade largely by proxy of Pia Maria Simig because he was unwilling to travel; Et In Arcadia Ego (The Hague, 1998), The Present Order (Park Guell, Barcelona, 1999), Cythera (2000), Six Definitions (2001), seven Idylls (Dean Gallery allotments, Edinburgh, 2005). (C12537, leg 1) In December 2004 a Scotland on Sunday poll of fifty artists, gallery directors and arts professionals voted Little Sparta "the most important work of Scottish art"; Sir Roy Strong called it "the only really original garden made in this country since 1945". (C12540, leg 1) Finlay died in Edinburgh on 27 March 2006, aged 80, of cancer per the New York Times obituary; he is buried in Abercorn Churchyard, West Lothian, "buried alone" per Wikipedia, the gravestone referring to his parents and sister, and his epitaph "consists of one word: 'Poet'". (C12501, C12541, C12542, leg 1) He wrote to Robert Nye on 20 January 1970: "I am an 18th century poet, anyway, and not avant-garde in the least. Avant cottage gardener is about my stretch". (C12543, leg 1)

In his own words. No autobiography was found; the closest the run came is the 1966 autobiographical sketch quoted in the LRB review of Selections, the letter to Seamus Cooney of 18 October 1967, the letter to Robert Nye of 20 January 1970, the letter to Ian Gardner of March 1975 ("It is quite hard to tell what is a key work, and there are few works, however humble, which don't have keys"), and the Jacket magazine interview on his disputes with institutions ("What people remembered was that I had caused a lot of trouble to these institutions by asking them to stand up and speak a simple truth"). (C12543, leg 1) One quotation could not be verified and is NOT used as evidence here: "A lot of my work is to do with straightforward affection..." (C12543, leg 1).

Holdings. Tate holds 167 artworks by Finlay; the 2012-13 Tate Britain display in the Duveen Galleries (12 November 2012 - 17 February 2013, curated by Andrew Wilson) comprised 24 works from the Tate collection. (C12535, leg 1) The University of Dundee holds "nearly 300 works by Finlay"; the Getty Research Institute holds the Finlay papers, 1948-1992 (10 linear feet); the Lilly Library holds the Finlay manuscripts 1953-1972 (4,011 items); Princeton purchased a Finlay Archive in 2016 (over 170 books, over 900 cards, over 40 prints). (C12536, leg 1) The Little Sparta Trust was established to preserve the garden; the founding year is disputed -- 1995 per the Trove inventory, ownership passing to the Trust in 2000 per Scottish-Places, established 2005 per the National Galleries of Scotland -- and the Trust now owns the garden and opens it June-September each year. (C12539, leg 1; C12625, leg 3) The garden was designated on the Inventory of Gardens and Designed Landscapes in Scotland on 1 July 1987 (GDL00265). (C12539, leg 1)

Evolution. Phase 1, to 1962: lyric and dialect -- The Sea Bed (1958), Dancers (1960), Glasgow Beasts (1961); the break comes in or around May 1962, when Finlay discovered concrete poetry via a letter to the Times Literary Supplement by E. M. de Melo e Castro, alerted by Edwin Morgan. (C12551, C12554, leg 2) Phase 2, 1963-68: concrete poetry on the page and as object -- Rapel (1963) replaces syntax with clusters and geometric patterns; the poem leaves the page almost at once: Standing Cards (1963-65) for the mantelpiece, Acrobats in glass, cork and screenprint, Wave/Rock in sandblasted glass, Starlit Waters as a netted wooden sculpture. (C12553, C12554, C12556, C12562, leg 2) Phase 3, 1966-76: the garden poem -- the move to Stonypath in 1966 turns the poem into a place; early garden works are still sea poems, but the scale changes: ponds are dug, trees planted, inscriptions set into stone, bridges and lintels; the Homage prints (Vuillard 1971, Malevich 1974) keep the print practice alive while the garden grows. (C12568, C12569, C12571, C12574, leg 2) Phase 4, 1973-83: war, Arcadia and the French Revolution -- the sea gives way to war as the second great subject, without abandoning the first: Arcadia (1973) camouflages a tank in leaves, Et in Arcadia Ego (1976) puts the tank in Poussin's pastoral, Big E (1976/77) and the Midway works (1977) turn aircraft carriers into bird tables, bird baths and stone fragments; the Collaborations show at Kettle's Yard in 1977 makes the collaborative method itself the exhibition subject; the Temple of Apollo turns the garden into a site of real conflict. (C12579, C12580, C12582, C12585, C12588, leg 2) Phase 5, 1982-2006: monumental late works and work outside Scotland -- Five Columns (1982), The World Has Been Empty Since the Romans (1985), A Wartime Garden (1989), Je Vous Salue Marat (1989) and Flute (1991) scale the Saint-Just material up to frieze, neon and gun-sculpture; outside Scotland the same language is installed as public gardens and sculpture. (C12593, C12596, leg 2) The arc is from the lyric poet who wanted plainness, to the concrete poet whose letters performed what they named, to the gardener whose poems had to be walked, to the polemicist whose Arcadia always had a tank in it -- and the through-line is that the poem keeps leaving its medium: page, card, glass, stone, garden, war. (C12660)

## Mechanism M1: the word is the thing -- letters perform what they name

In Acrobats (W03) the letters of the word tumble diagonally across the page and the wall -- the word is the acrobat, the layout is the act: "the individual letters of the word 'acrobats' are screen-printed diagonally across the page, performing visual acrobatics". (C12556, leg 2) In Wave/Rock (W04) the letters of "wave" rise and fall unevenly while "rock" clumps dense at the other end. (C12556, leg 2) The poem is not about the image; the typography is the image. The mechanism is identity between sign and thing: the arrangement of the letters is the motion, the weather, the weight, and the reader's eye must partake of the exercise too -- at Gledfield the cork letters on the barn wall made the reader look up and across the way a spectator watches an act. (C12517, leg 1)

**Takeaway:** the letters must do what the word says. A piece built on this mechanism must make the typography the behaviour -- letters that tumble, rise, fall, clump -- and must never illustrate the word with a picture beside it; the word is the picture.

## Mechanism M2: overlap births a third word

In Wave/Rock, where the letters of "wave" and "rock" meet and superimpose they suggest the third word "wrack" (seaweed), and the thickened stems of the letters suggest, visually, seaweedy rocks; Finlay explained the poem is "about 2 imposing forces, but being a poem presents them in equipoise, resolved". (C12558, leg 2) Two forces held in equipoise resolve into something neither word says alone. The closest verified "sea" diagram is Sea/Land (1967): brown lines = land, blue lines = sea, with a key. (C12597, leg 2) The mechanism is superposition as meaning: two systems overlap, and at the overlap a third term appears that belongs to neither system alone -- wrack is neither wave nor rock, it is what the sea leaves on the rock.

**Takeaway:** two words must meet and make a third. A piece built on this mechanism must show two moving systems colliding and, at the collision, a new word or form appearing that neither system contains on its own -- and the third term must be the point, not a decoration.

## Mechanism M3: the page-turn is the motion -- kinetic booklets

The Canal Stripe and Ocean Stripe booklets (W08) are "kinetic booklet/poems" because "turning the pages enacted the poetry"; Ocean Stripe uses overprinting and pure punctuation to suggest the wake of a boat. (C12567, leg 2) The reader's hand makes the wake. The Standing Cards (W02) must stand up on a mantelpiece to be read as objects, not pages. (C12554, leg 2) The mechanism is the reader as the motor: the poem does nothing until it is handled, and the handling is the motion -- the page-turn is not a way of getting to the next poem, it is the poem happening.

**Takeaway:** the viewer's hand must make the motion. A piece built on this mechanism must do nothing on its own and everything when handled -- the turn, the drag, the flip is the event, and the work must be inert without it.

## Mechanism M4: one word per stone, walked to be read

The Present Order is the Disorder of the Future (W19, with Nicholas Sloan, 1983): each word of Saint-Just's aphorism is inscribed on a single stone block laid out on the ground above Lochan Eck, so the sentence must be walked to be read; it becomes a ruined inscription. (C12589, C12591, leg 2) Sloan "secured the stones, found ones that had the right heft and feel to them, and carved them in this beautiful, monumental way". (C12591, leg 2) The landscape around it becomes part of the poem, and the Cheekwood version, "positioned at the base of a long allee... the words of Saint-Just, spread across eight stones, becomes a fragment, unavoidably recalling ancient ruins or tombstones". (C12590, leg 2) The mechanism is reading as walking: the sentence is scattered across a place, and the reader's body assembles it in order, at walking pace, with the ground between the words doing the work of punctuation.

**Takeaway:** the sentence must be scattered and walked. A piece built on this mechanism must put one word per object across a space and make the viewer cross the space to read -- and the gaps between the words must be part of the meaning, not dead space.

## Mechanism M5: the motto is the function -- sundial as poem

The Malta Sundial (W11, carved by John Andrew, 1979) carries "Dividing the light I disclose the hour" along the dial's edge -- the inscription describes what the object does; without light there is no shadow to disclose the hour. (C12573, leg 2) Evening Will Come (W10) faces west and works as a sundial only in the evening hours, "only when they sew the blue sail"; moss and wear mark time yearly as well as hourly. (C12571, leg 2) The mechanism is a sentence that is also an instruction manual for its own object: the words say what the thing does, and the thing only does it when the words' conditions are met -- light, evening, west. Greg Thomas reads this as a formal shift: the sundial is "a recognizable sculptural form with a poem etched into it. If we removed the poem, that form would remain in place. You'd still have a sundial" -- language is no longer co-extensive with form, as in strict concrete poetry. (C12598, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** the words must describe what the object does, and the object must only work when the words say it does. A piece built on this mechanism must tie its text to its function so tightly that removing the text breaks the function -- and the function must depend on real light, real time, real direction.

## Mechanism M6: homage as compression -- one word, one colour-field, one reference

Homage to Vuillard (W12, 1971, with Michael Harvey): a field of yellow-orange, within it black, within it the single word SINGER -- singer, sewing machine, and the bombed Clydebank Singer factories at once. (C12574, leg 2) Scobie's reading: Vuillard's yellow-orange is "the shade of autumn, fullness verging on decay"; "Singer" means at once the one who sings, the sewing machine (Singer), and -- via the Clydebank Singer factories bombed in March 1941 -- a sign of death. (C12574, leg 2) Homage to Malevich (1974): lines of "lackblockblackb" / "lockblackblockb", vowels shifting a to o while still spelling a word. (C12554, leg 2) The mechanism is the pun as compression: one word carries three meanings, one colour-field carries a season and a death, and the whole of an art-historical reference is folded into a single card. The Homage series runs through the 1970s on postcards and prints, but the individual cards beyond Vuillard, Malevich and Watteau were not verified beyond list lines. (C12575, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** one word must do the work of an essay. A piece built on this mechanism must compress a reference into a single word inside a single colour-field, with the pun doing the work -- and must never explain the pun; the viewer either gets all three meanings at once or gets none of them.

## Mechanism M7: war in Arcadia -- the tank in the pastoral, the warship as bird table

Arcadia (W14, 1973, with George Oliver): a tank camouflaged in a leaf pattern -- idyll and weapon occupy the same pattern. (C12579, leg 2) Et in Arcadia Ego (W15, 1976, with John Andrew): Poussin's motto literalised, death arriving as a tank in the ideal landscape. (C12580, leg 2) Big E (at Midway) (W16, 1976/77): a stone aircraft carrier that is also a bird table and bird bath, so birds landing mimic planes landing and the garden pond becomes Midway. (C12582, leg 2) The mechanism is scale collapse and pattern sharing: the pastoral and the military are not contrasted, they are the same image -- the tank disappears into the leaves, the carrier shrinks to a bird table, the pond becomes a Pacific battle -- and the viewer must decide which is the disguise. Finlay "continually revisited war themes and the concept of the Utopian Arcadia in his work". (C12579, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** the idyll and the weapon must be the same picture. A piece built on this mechanism must put a war machine inside a pastoral so completely that neither can be removed without breaking the other -- and the scale must collapse: the huge made small, the small made the whole battle.

## Mechanism M8: the garden is the work -- the artefact reigns in place

Little Sparta (W09, 1966-2006): "the work would be the garden itself"; each area gets a small artefact "which reigns like a small deity or spirit of place... The work is not an isolated object, but an object with flowers, plants, trees, water and so on". (C12522, leg 1) The Temple of Apollo (W18) turns a farm byre into a temple by inscription and painted Corinthian columns alone -- dedicated "To Apollo. His Music. His Missiles. His Muses" -- and then into a real battlefield in the Little Spartan War. (C12587, C12588, leg 2) The mechanism is context as medium: the artefact does not sit in the garden, the garden is the work, and the artefact reigns over its small area the way a deity reigns over a shrine -- so the poem needs its place the way a one-word poem needs its garden setting to communicate at all. (C12514, leg 1) Historic Environment Scotland calls Little Sparta "considered Hamilton Finlay's most important and best known work and... among the major artistic achievements of the second half of the 20th century". (C12569, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** the place must be the work. A piece built on this mechanism must make the environment the poem -- artefacts placed in a landscape that is itself composed, each one reigning over its small area -- and must never present the object alone on a blank ground; without its place it is not the work.

## The aesthetic logic: plainness, order, and the classical turn

Finlay's aesthetic is plainness enforced by order. The early lyric work wanted plainness -- "Even my attempt to be 'traditional' was a way of being concrete, part of a plainness I wanted" (C12509, leg 1) -- and the concrete work keeps that plainness: one word, one colour-field, one stone, one motto. The poem is "the centre of order and calm and abiding values -- the opposite of an idea of 'confessional poetry'". (C12514, leg 1) But the order is never innocent: the classical language carries the French Revolution, the pastoral carries the tank, the sundial carries the shroud, the garden carries a war over rates. The logic is juxtaposition of apparently opposite ideas -- "Repetition, imitation and tradition lay at the heart of Hamilton's poetry", and he explored "the juxtaposition of apparently opposite ideas". (C12514, leg 1) The garden is the fullest statement of the logic: a place composed like a poem, where every area has its artefact reigning like a small deity, and where the classical order is defended, literally, against the council -- the Little Spartan War is the aesthetic logic acted out, order defended with vigilantes. (C12522, C12525, leg 1) The through-line for a builder is this: Finlay's work is small, plain, and exact, and it is exact about things that do not belong together -- sea and stone, word and weapon, garden and war. A piece that borrows the prettiness without the contradiction has missed him. (C12661)

## The network: the Cambridge triangle, two international poles, and the makers

The strongest chain is Morgan to Finlay to Bann and Weaver to Steadman to Form, framed internationally by Gomringer and Augusto de Campos, and made physically by Sue Finlay and Ian Gardner. (C12645, leg 3) Edwin Morgan introduced Finlay to concrete poetry in May or June 1962 and passed on Augusto de Campos's address; Finlay and Augusto "never met, but they enjoyed an intense correspondence during the 1960s and 70s", and Finlay "first wrote concrete poetry in 1963, guided initially by Augusto". (C12626, C12627, leg 3) Bann and Weaver met Finlay in Edinburgh in the summer of 1964, then carried him into the First International Exhibition of Concrete, Phonetic and Kinetic Poetry at St Catharine's College, Cambridge (28 November - 5 December 1964), co-organised by Weaver and Steadman, and into Image (1964) and Form (1966-69). (C12604, C12612, leg 3) Finlay's correspondence with Bann runs from September 1964 and with Weaver from March 1964, in the Kansas archive MS 48; Bann later edited Finlay's letters to him as Midway (2014) and is listed among the former trustees of the Little Sparta Trust. (C12603, C12610, C12611, C12630, leg 3) Eugen Gomringer is the European pole: correspondence listed in the Lilly Library, 6 Small Pears for Eugen Gomringer (1966), a "Homage to Gomringer", and shared publication in Image (1964), Konkrete Poesie International (1965) and Six Concrete Poems (1967). (C12615-C12618, leg 3) Dom Sylvester Houedard is the British "wider concrete" interlocutor: longstanding correspondence per the Ferris abstract, shared publication in P.O.T.H. No. 10 (1963), Image (1964) and Six Concrete Poems (1967), and co-presence at the Cambridge exhibition of November 1964 -- but no meeting documented, and the muse slug study/dom-sylvester-houedard.md already returns 200, so he is already studied and is not a candidate. (C12618-C12622, C12643, leg 3) Ian Gardner is the clearest "made together" link after Sue Finlay: Tree-Shells (1971/1975), The End (1975-6), Kite-Estuary Model (1971), Two Prospects (1984). (C12623, leg 3) Sue Finlay co-created Little Sparta: the garden was "shaped directly by Sue Finlay's horticultural knowledge and skill", she "undertook the majority of the planting", and their collaboration lasted 23 years. (C12523, leg 1; C12625, leg 3) The Finlay-Steadman link is editorial only -- no personal collaboration was evidenced: no joint work, no letter series, no meeting. (C12614, leg 3) No evidence was found that Finlay met Gomringer, Augusto de Campos or Houedard in person; those links are correspondence plus shared publication. (C12643, leg 3) (C12662)
