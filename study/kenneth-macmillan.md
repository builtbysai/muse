## 1. Dunfermline and Great Yarmouth: the outsider childhood

Born 11 December 1929 in Dunfermline, Fife, the youngest of four surviving children of William MacMillan (1891-1946), a labourer and sometime cook, and Edith MacMillan, nee Shreeve (1888-1942); a fifth child died in infancy. His father had served in the First World War and suffered permanent physical and mental damage. The family moved to Great Yarmouth, Norfolk, in search of work. His mother died in 1942, when he was twelve, and Wikipedia, citing Parry, says this caused him "acute and lasting distress". His father was a distant figure; his only close family relationship was with an elder sister. The Times obituarist, quoted by Wikipedia, suggested that the feeling of being an outsider, displayed in many of MacMillan's ballets, had its roots in this childhood. The biographical fact and the artistic fact are the same shape: a boy from a working-class family with no dance or music background, motherless at twelve, who enters ballet late and spends the rest of his career putting outsiders on stage -- the masked clown in Laiderette, the Adolescent in The Burrow, Anna Anderson in Anastasia, Rudolf in Mayerling, the woman in The Judas Tree. He won a scholarship to Great Yarmouth Grammar School around 1940; the school was evacuated to Retford, Nottinghamshire, during the war, and in Retford the local teacher Jean Thomas introduced him to ballet. He had earlier had Scottish dancing lessons in Dunfermline and tap dancing in Great Yarmouth. He turned to ballet at fourteen. [C34150]

**Takeaway:** the outsider is not a theme he chose, it is the condition he came from -- motherless at twelve, working-class, late to ballet -- and it stays the central figure of his work for forty years. [C34151]

## 2. Sadler's Wells School at fifteen, stage fright at twenty-three

In 1944 the school returned to Great Yarmouth and he found a new ballet teacher, Phyllis Adams. At fifteen he secured admission to the Sadler's Wells Ballet School; the Royal Ballet and Opera says he forged a letter from his disapproving father recommending his son's talents, and de Valois accepted him on merit. He was awarded a scholarship in 1945. In 1946 he appeared in The Sleeping Beauty with which Webster and de Valois reopened the Royal Opera House, first as a non-dancing extra and later in a small dancing role, and in April 1946 he became a founder member of the Sadler's Wells Theatre Ballet, the junior touring company. In October 1946 Frederick Ashton cast him in a leading role in Valses nobles et sentimentales. He was promoted to the senior Covent Garden company at the start of the 1948-49 season, and in October 1949 he danced Florestan in the Act III pas de trois of The Sleeping Beauty at the company's opening gala in New York. Created roles included the Great Admirer in Cranko's Children's Corner (1948), Sherlock Holmes and Professor Moriarty in Margaret Dale's The Great Detective (1953), and Moondog in Cranko's The Lady and the Fool (1954); he appeared in the British film Tread Softly (1950). But he was plagued by severe stage fright and stopped dancing at about twenty-three. His own words, in a BBC interview of 1990: "I stopped dancing when I was about 23 mainly because I had just terrible stage fright and I hated performing. I turned to choreography as a release from dancing and I was lucky enough that the first thing I did everyone liked." De Valois gave him three months' leave; he danced with Cranko's small group at the Kenton Theatre, Henley-on-Thames, and joined de Valois's Choreographers Group, set up in response to Marie Rambert's Ballet Workshops. [C34152]

**Takeaway:** the choreographer exists because the dancer could not bear the stage -- stage fright at twenty-three is the hinge, and choreography is the release from performing, not a promotion out of it. [C34153]

## 3. Somnambulism to Danses concertantes: the first commission

His first ballet, Somnambulism, was made 1 February 1953 for the Choreographers Group, to Stan Kenton jazz, and was well received; MacMillan danced in it himself at short notice. Laiderette followed in 1954, to Frank Martin, introducing the "outsider" character that became a hallmark, and Punch and the Child was made for BBC Television the same year. In January 1955 came Danses concertantes, to Stravinsky, for the Sadler's Wells Theatre Ballet, with designs by Nicholas Georgiadis -- his first professional commission from de Valois and the start of an extensive collaboration with Georgiadis, then still a student at the Slade School. The Times said "it was clear that a powerful choreographic talent had arrived." The ballet is a plotless suite: sharp, spiky neoclassical vocabulary, pointed fingers, angled ports de bras, sudden changes of direction, head wiggles as a motif in the lead ballerina's first solo, head and arms mirroring Stravinsky's notes, structural asymmetry throughout, an opening tableau of lead women seated on the linked arms of the men like a human viaduct, and a finale building into a busy street scene. From 1955 his contract was purely as a choreographer, after a fierce argument with de Valois, who wanted him to continue dancing. His only later Covent Garden appearances as a dancer were two performances as an Ugly Stepsister in Cinderella alongside Ashton in 1956. The same year he was the first of his generation to have an entire evening of his works presented by Sadler's Wells Ballet -- a quadruple bill of Somnambulism, House of Birds, Danses concertantes and Solitaire. [C34154]

**Takeaway:** the first commission is plotless, musical and witty -- the dark psychological narratives that made his name come later, and the plotless pole never leaves him. [C34155]

## 4. The Burrow and the muse: Lynn Seymour

The Burrow, 1958, for the Royal Ballet Touring Company, to Frank Martin, with designs by Georgiadis, was the break into serious dramatic territory: frightened people trapped in oppressive conditions, with menacing echoes of war, oppression and concealment, inspired by Anne Frank's diary and Kafka's story "The Burrow", in a physical style closer to modern dance than ballet. The Times critic said its dramatic impact was strong enough "to make one glad when it ends." It was the first work made on Lynn Seymour, who created the role of the Adolescent and became his muse for many subsequent ballets. (The Arts Desk dates it 1957; the Wikipedia works table, the Seymour article and the Royal Ballet School tribute all give 1958, and 1958 is used.) The muse relationship is the working system of his whole career: Seymour in The Burrow, The Invitation, Romeo and Juliet, Concerto, Anastasia and Mayerling; Marcia Haydee in Stuttgart for Song of the Earth and Requiem; Monica Mason, plucked from the corps for The Rite of Spring; Darcey Bussell and Irek Mukhamedov at the end, for The Prince of the Pagodas, Winter Dreams and The Judas Tree. He did not arrive with counts and fixed steps; he worked on a chosen dancer and built the role out of that dancer's body, plasticity and personality, describing movement in shapes and images rather than in ballet terms. Seymour's "raggedy-doll pliability", in the Arts Desk's phrase, let loose in his mind new expressive possibilities in pas de deux -- the off-balance, spinning, thrown-and-caught partnering that is his signature. [C34156]

**Takeaway:** the muse is the method -- roles are built on specific bodies, and the partnering vocabulary comes out of what one dancer's body can do, not out of a system. [C34157]

## 5. The Invitation: the taboo broken

The Invitation, premiered 10 November 1960 at the New Theatre, Oxford, by the Royal Ballet Touring Company and first given at the Royal Opera House on 30 December 1960, is a one-act sexual drama of almost an hour, to a specially written score by Matyas Seiber -- his last completed work; he was killed in a car crash in September 1960. A young girl, danced by Seymour, is invited to a country house, seduced and then raped by an older married man, the Husband, danced by Desmond Doyle; her Cousin was Christopher Gable, in the first pairing of Seymour and Gable. De Valois originally asked MacMillan to cut the rape scene. The narrative is built in shifting duets -- Girl and Cousin, innocent and flirtatious; Girl and Husband, pursuit, coercion, assault -- with the Wife watching, and a Carnival interlude interrupting the chamber scale with a larger ensemble episode. The rape is staged as dance, not mime alone: the Husband's partnering turns from courtship lifts into forced, floor-bound manipulation of the Girl's body. The Times said: "If the Invitation is MacMillan's ballet, it is also Miss Seymour's." It is the direct predecessor, in casting and in method, of Romeo and Juliet -- Seymour and Gable as the created leads -- and the first full statement of the line that runs through his whole career: sex, power and trauma as ballet subjects, staged literally in the body rather than kept offstage or symbolic. [C34158]

**Takeaway:** the taboo is the breakthrough -- once rape is danced rather than mimed, there is no subject ballet cannot take on, and he spends the next thirty years proving it. [C34159]

## 6. The Rite of Spring: the Chosen Maiden from the corps

The Rite of Spring, premiered 3 May 1962 at the Royal Opera House, to Stravinsky, conducted by Colin Davis, with designs by Sidney Nolan, is MacMillan's mastery of a modernist classic. He had been invited to make a Rite in 1959 and declined; he took it up in 1962. The scenario generally follows the 1913 original, with two plot points brought forward: the selection of the Chosen One, and the start of her sacrificial dance. He cast Monica Mason, then an unknown junior dancer of twenty from the corps de ballet, as the Chosen Maiden; she danced the role in revivals up to 1982 and later supervised revivals. The movement is massed, grounded and percussive -- stomping, crouching, hunched groupings against Stravinsky's jagged rhythms, rather than lifted classical line; Stephanie Jordan's term, quoted in the article, is "a generalised primitivism". The Chosen Maiden is isolated centre-stage while the tribe watches in a ring; her final solo is a solo of exhaustion, repeated jumps and collapses until she falls. John Percival in The Times wrote that "Mr MacMillan's invention can never have been more musical or assured... the choreography, with its blend of primitivism and modern jive, piles climax on climax." Dance and Dancers called it "a singular and signal triumph". John Percival's larger claim, also in The Times, is that since Nijinsky's 1913 original, The Rite had been waiting for a choreographer who could make it work on stage, and MacMillan's was the most successful version to date. [C34160]

**Takeaway:** a corps dancer can carry a whole evening's emotional weight -- the Mason casting is the template for every later muse-casting, and the ritual works because the exhaustion is real, not acted. [C34161]

## 7. Stuttgart and the refused Mahler: Song of the Earth

In 1964 the Royal Opera House board turned down his proposal to make a ballet to Mahler's Das Lied von der Erde -- the second refusal, the first having been in 1959 despite de Valois's support. John Cranko, his friend from Sadler's Wells days and now director of the Stuttgart Ballet, invited him to create it there instead. Song of the Earth premiered 7 November 1965 at the Staatstheater Stuttgart, to Mahler's six songs for two voices and orchestra, sung live, with designs by Georgiadis. The Stuttgart cast was Marcia Haydee as the Woman, Ray Barra as the Man, and Egon Madsen as the Eternal One; the London premiere followed in May 1966 with Haydee, Donald MacLeary and Anthony Dowell. It is one act, about 65 minutes, plotless but with a scenario MacMillan summarised as: "A man and a woman; death takes the man; they both return to her, and at the end of the ballet we find that in death there is the promise of renewal." Six songs, six episodes -- drinking, youth, beauty, loneliness, farewell -- danced while the singers stand and sing, so the audience watches dance and song at once. The Woman is a figure of loneliness, isolated from the movements of her friends; the Man, like his group, is blissfully unaware of his mortality. The Eternal One is not a figure of evil but a gentle, ever-present companion, masked, moving through the ensemble and quietly claiming the Man. The choreography is earthbound, non-classical movement that morphs into modernist curves -- low, weighted, grounded phrasing against Mahler's long lines, the central pas de deux built on slow carries and falls rather than bravura jumps. The closing image of eternity is timed to the elongated repeated word "ewig" (forever): the three figures rising and falling without break as the curtain falls. It was an instant success in Stuttgart, and the Royal Ballet took it up within six months. [C34162]

**Takeaway:** the board's refusal is the mechanism -- the sacred concert score the house would not touch becomes his purest elegy, made in Stuttgart and taken up in London only once it has succeeded. [C34163]

## 8. Romeo and Juliet: the balcony first, the premiere taken

Romeo and Juliet, his first full-length, three-act ballet, premiered Tuesday 9 February 1965 at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, to Prokofiev, with designs by Georgiadis -- large, imposing sets inspired by Italian Quattrocento painting and architecture and by Franco Zeffirelli's 1960 stage production, deliberately dwarfing Juliet to stress her smallness and vulnerability against Veronese society. It was made in five months, and the balcony pas de deux had been created first, for Seymour and Gable to dance on Canadian television in September 1964, in three rehearsals. The ballet was created on Seymour and Gable, but at the insistence of the Royal Opera House management -- David Webster, and Sol Hurok, organiser of the Royal Ballet's American tours -- the gala premiere was danced by Margot Fonteyn as Juliet and Rudolf Nureyev as Romeo, for box-office reasons. MacMillan and his chosen dancers felt betrayed; Seymour and Gable were relegated to second cast, and Gable subsequently gave up dancing altogether. Fonteyn was forty-five at the premiere, not forty-six as one carried figure had it -- born 18 May 1919, she was forty-five in February 1965. The full original cast included David Blair as Mercutio, Desmond Doyle as Tybalt, Anthony Dowell as Benvolio, Michael Somes as Lord Capulet and Georgina Parkinson as Rosaline. The narrative is built as a chain of pas de deux, planned around the characters: Juliet as headstrong, making decisions; Romeo as "swept off his feet by love". Act structure: public Verona (market brawl, rowdy harlots borrowed from Cranko's version, Capulet ball), private love (balcony, bedroom, wedding in Friar Laurence's cell), public violence and death (the Tybalt and Mercutio duels, the tomb). The balcony duet is the hinge: slow, sustained lifts, Juliet carried and folded around Romeo, then the famous bedroom duet with its long, reluctant parting lifts. Crowd scenes use the full corps as a press of citizens, not decoration -- the lovers are repeatedly separated and re-found inside the crowd. There were 43 curtain calls at the premiere, and the safety curtain had to descend to clear the house. A film of the production was made by Paul Czinner in 1966. [C34164]

**Takeaway:** the ballet is made twice -- once on the dancers it was built for, once on the stars it was given to -- and the betrayal is part of the work's history, not a footnote to it. [C34165]

## 9. Berlin: Concerto and the one-act Anastasia

Disillusioned, he left for Berlin, accepting the invitation from the Deutsche Oper to run its ballet company as Director of Ballet, 1966-1969. Parry describes it as an unhappy experience: ballet given distinctly lower priority than opera, he did not speak German, colleagues drifted away, he grew increasingly isolated, smoked and drank heavily, and suffered a minor stroke. Yet the critic Jane Simpson's judgement, quoted by Wikipedia, is that some of MacMillan's finest work was done for Berlin and Stuttgart. He created seven ballets for Berlin, including Valses nobles et sentimentales, Concerto, the one-act Anastasia, The Sleeping Beauty, Olympiad, Cain and Abel and Swan Lake. Concerto, premiered 30 November 1966 at the Deutsche Oper, to Shostakovich's Piano Concerto No. 2, with designs by Jurgen Rose, is one act, plotless, in three movements following the score. The first movement is tightly choreographed ensemble, almost military precision matching Shostakovich's motor rhythms, the corps dancing in unison in sharp formations. The second movement is the famous languid pas de deux, inspired by watching Lynn Seymour warming up at the barre: the man becomes the barre, extending a steady hand while the woman performs movements of focused simplicity, curving and stretching around his support -- slow, sensual, sustained, the opposite of the outer movements. The third movement, intended as a playful pas de deux, had to be remade as a female solo when the male dancer broke his foot before the premiere. The one-act Anastasia, premiered 25 June 1967 at the Deutsche Oper, with Seymour as Anna Anderson, depicted the claimant to the Romanov name in a mental asylum trying to recover her memory, to Martinu and electronic music. It later became Act III of the full-length version. [C34166]

**Takeaway:** the unhappy exile produces the two poles at once -- the purest plotless ballet and the most expressionist one-act -- and the barre duet is the muse method in its simplest form: watch the dancer warm up, and build the ballet on it. [C34167]

## 10. Director of the Royal Ballet: co-director, then sole

In 1970 Ashton retired as artistic director -- de Valois had stepped down in 1963 -- and Webster retired the same year. MacMillan and John Field were secured as co-directors of the ballet. The joint directorship failed: Field left within months to become director of ballet at La Scala, Milan, and MacMillan became sole director three months later, per Britannica. The appointment coincided with a financial crisis and a decision to merge the two ballet companies into one, with job losses; company morale lowered. He was the institution's first home-grown director and choreographer. He brought in outside choreographers -- Robbins, Balanchine, Tetley, Cranko, Van Manen, Neumeier -- as well as choreographing himself, for the New Group and the enlarged resident company. Administration sat badly with him; his creative work of the period split between full-length house epics, fiercely divisive at premiere, and immediately successful shorter works. During this period he met and married Deborah Williams, an Australian painter, later Lady Deborah MacMillan; their daughter Charlotte, a photographer, was born in 1973. The marriage year is split in the sources -- Wikipedia has him married "at the age of 42", which points to 1972, and an infobox gives 1972, while Radio Times, the Yorke Dance Project and filmreference give 1974, and Charlotte was born in 1973, before the 1974 wedding date those sources give; 1972 is the better-supported year but the split is carried, not resolved. John Percival, quoted in Wikipedia, said the marriage "saved him, both physically and mentally [and] gave him stability in his private life and seems to have resolved his confused sexuality." In 1977 he resigned as director after seven years, wishing to concentrate on choreography, and was succeeded by Norman Morrice. [C34168]

**Takeaway:** the directorship is the one job he did not want and did not suit -- seven years of administration that produced the two great full-length epics and the proof that he should be making ballets, not running a company. [C34169]

## 11. Anastasia expanded, Manon: the full-length epics

Anastasia was expanded to a three-act full-length version, premiered 22 July 1971 at the Royal Opera House, with designs by Barry Kay. The structure is a memory: the asylum present frames a recollected past. Acts I and II, added as prequels in conventional classical style to Tchaikovsky, show the imperial family before the revolution -- Act I on the Imperial yacht in summer white, naval officers dancing in uniform and then, incongruously, in striped bathing suits; Act II at the palace, ballroom dancing, Rasputin intervening in the Tsar and Tsarina's duet to make a menage a trois, the ball ending with the arrival of revolutionaries. Act III, the original 1967 one-act, is the opposite: a dark room in a mental hospital, a lone woman in a hospital gown watching newsreel film of the Romanovs on a big screen, then flashbacks in which the family, soldiers, peasants and Bolsheviks fill the stage, Rasputin throws her about, and the murder of the family is recreated. The movement here is expressionist, staccato, more mime than dance, with screams and fragmented, anguished partnering, and phrases from the opening recur as flashbacks of happier times that make her current distress all the more poignant. The two halves do not blend; the contrast is the point. Critics were divided on the 1971 expansion; the one-act was widely acclaimed, above all for Seymour's central performance. Manon, full title L'histoire de Manon, premiered 7 March 1974 at the Royal Opera House, to Massenet -- not from the opera Manon, but a compilation from thirteen other operas, two oratorios, orchestral suites, melodies and piano works, chosen and arranged by Leighton Lucas (one Wikipedia table gives "Leighton Lewis", which looks like a Wikipedia error; Lucas is the widely cited arranger). The source is Abbe Prevost's novel Manon Lescaut (1731). MacMillan left the novel in Antoinette Sibley's dressing room on the last night of the 1973 summer season with a note: it would "come in handy for March 7, '74." Sibley danced Manon, Anthony Dowell was Des Grieux, David Wall was Lescaut, Monica Mason was Lescaut's Mistress, and Derek Rencher was Monsieur G.M. The narrative structure is modelled on Romeo and Juliet: young innocents meet, and love is revealed through a series of pas de deux. Divided opinion at the premiere -- the Guardian on opening night said "Basically, Manon is a slut and Des Grieux is a fool" -- and the audience gave a standing ovation; it is now a repertory classic and one of his best-known works. [C34170]

**Takeaway:** the full-length epic is built backwards or by patchwork -- Anastasia adds a classical past in front of an expressionist present, Manon assembles its score from thirteen operas -- and the divided premiere is the normal reception, not the exception. [C34171]

## 12. Elite Syncopations: the sunny opposite

Elite Syncopations, premiered 7 October 1974 at the Royal Opera House, is the deliberate opposite of Manon, made in the same year. One act, for twelve dancers, to ragtime -- Scott Joplin at the centre, plus Scott Hayden, Joseph F. Lamb, James Scott, Robert Hampton, Paul Pratt, Max Morath and Donald Ashwander -- with a twelve-piece band, two pianos, seated visibly at the back of the stage. Costumes by Ian Spurling were hand-painted body tights. The piece is a suite of short numbers, each a character sketch: a solo or duet per rag, with hip-swinging, easygoing rhythms, loose arms, syncopated footwork and broad humour -- dancers "letting loose" in a way the Royal Ballet rarely allowed. Virtuoso display is the point, not story: turns, jumps and lifts played for charm and timing rather than for drama. The vernacular is bent into classical line: "Flicks, parallel knees and tilted hips are teased into the classical style." Reception was mixed at first -- the Times and the Observer found it vulgar and unstylish, Dance and Dancers called it "cheerfully diverting" -- New York in 1976 praised it enthusiastically, and it has stayed popular ever since. A BBC film was made in 1975, danced by the original cast in Battersea's Big Top. The ragtime revival was at its height after the film The Sting (December 1973); MacMillan had been planning a ragtime ballet before the film. [C34172]

**Takeaway:** the same distortion technique, used for wit instead of dread -- flexed feet and parallel knees are funny here and terrifying in Song of the Earth, and the band sitting on stage is the whole joke: the music is visible, social, part of the scene. [C34173]

## 13. Requiem and Gloria: the elegies

Requiem, premiered 28 November 1976 at the Staatstheater Stuttgart, is to Faure's Requiem, sung live, with designs by Yolanda Sonnabend. The Royal Opera House board had refused the Faure score as inappropriate for ballet -- the same refusal as Song of the Earth -- so MacMillan made it in Stuttgart instead; it was not given at Covent Garden until 1983, Stuttgart holding exclusive rights for six years. In MacMillan's words: "This danced Requiem is dedicated to the memory of my friend and colleague John Cranko, Director of the Stuttgart Ballet 1961-1973." Cranko had died suddenly in 1973. The piece is, in the article's phrase, "a portrait of a ballet company coming to terms with the death of Cranko, their much-loved artistic director." It is an elegiac ensemble work, not a narrative: dancers move in slow, luminous group formations -- processions, lifts that carry a body as if in a funeral cortege, solos that break away from the group and return to it -- while the chorus sings the Requiem text, notably the Pie Jesu. MacMillan had William Blake's blazing heavenly drawings in mind for the imagery. John Percival in The Times called it MacMillan's best ballet to date and criticised the Royal Ballet for failing to secure it. Gloria, premiered 13 March 1980 at the Royal Opera House, is to Poulenc's Gloria, with designs by Andy Klunder -- women's costumes in all-over lycra body tights in whites, blues, greys and silvers. It is a meditation on the First World War, inspired by Vera Brittain's Testament of Youth, televised as a series in 1979. Clement Crisp called it "a meditation upon death's harvest in the First World War." A sung Gloria -- soprano and chorus live -- with dancers in pale, uniform body tights, moving as a collective of young soldiers and mourners rather than as named characters; the design carries much of the meaning, the pale, almost ghostly costumes against Klunder's set making the ensemble read as the dead and the bereaved at once. (The Poulenc Gloria is dated 1959 in ROH Collections and 1961 in the Wikipedia article; the discrepancy is carried, not resolved.) [C34174]

**Takeaway:** the memorial ballet is a form of his own -- dance as a communal act of mourning, using a sacred score without staging a liturgy, and the refusal that sent it to Stuttgart is the same refusal, twice over. [C34175]

## 14. Mayerling: the darkest full-length

Mayerling, premiered 14 February 1978 at the Royal Opera House, is three acts plus prologue and epilogue, to Liszt arranged and orchestrated by John Lanchbery, scenario by Gillian Freeman, designs by Georgiadis, dedicated to Sir Frederick Ashton. The premiere cast was David Wall as Crown Prince Rudolf, Lynn Seymour as Baroness Mary Vetsera, Wendy Ellis as Princess Stephanie, Michael Somes as Emperor Franz Josef, Georgina Parkinson as Empress Elisabeth, and Merle Park as Countess Marie Larisch. It is based on the Mayerling incident of 30 January 1889 -- the apparent murder-suicide of Rudolf, Crown Prince of Austria-Hungary, and his seventeen-year-old mistress Mary Vetsera. The ballet is framed by the cemetery at Heiligenkreuz before dawn, prologue and epilogue the same image, so the whole ballet is a flashback from the grave. Act I: the Hofburg ballroom, the Empress's apartments where Rudolf begs for maternal affection and is coldly rebuffed, Rudolf's apartments where he threatens Stephanie with a revolver and then takes her forcibly. Act II: a notorious tavern -- prostitutes competing for attention, a police raid, Rudolf proposing a suicide pact to Mitzi Kaspar -- the Vetsera house, where Countess Larisch reads Mary's fortune from cards, and the Hofburg birthday celebrations. Act III: a royal shoot, Rudolf firing wildly and killing a courtier, Rudolf's apartments where he asks Mary to die with him, and the hunting lodge at Mayerling -- drink, morphine injection, sex, then he shoots her, reassures his friends, and shoots himself. The partnering is the engine: Rudolf's duets with each woman are different -- formal and cold with Stephanie, tender and desperate with his mother, violent and erotic with Mary -- so the audience reads his psychology through how he handles each partner. Large ensemble scenes alternate with claustrophobic duets. Reception was generally favourable, with reservations: many reviewers found it overly long and the historical background hard to follow. Mary Clarke in the Guardian defended the complexity: "every scene tells something about Rudolf and the Court of Vienna in his time." MacMillan died of a heart attack backstage at Covent Garden on 29 October 1992 during a revival of Mayerling. [C34176]

**Takeaway:** the darkest full-length has no redemption and no hero -- a doomed, drug-using, sexually violent anti-hero at the centre of a classical ballet -- and it is the ballet he died during, which is not a coincidence of programming but the end of the line he had been walking since The Invitation. [C34177]

## 15. The pas de deux as a self-contained story arc

The first mechanism, and the one that runs through everything: MacMillan builds a duet so that it travels through several emotional states inside itself, instead of circling one feeling the way a classical pas de deux does. Judith Mackrell, in the Arts Desk retrospective, states it directly: "unlike a classical pas de deux, which behaves rather like a baroque opera duet by using technical skills to circle expressively around a single emotional moment, a MacMillan duet will have its own story within, moving from flirtation to consummation, or from innocence to violence, or from repression to confession." The watchable behaviour is a change of temperature inside one duet: the holds get closer or rougher, the lifts get higher or more desperate, the speed and the amount of contact change, and the audience can follow a plot inside the duet alone, without mime or programme notes. In Manon the three main duets run at three different temperatures -- first meeting, consummation, death -- and Steven Heathcote's phrase, quoted by the Australian Ballet, is "You couldn't get three more different temperatures." In Romeo and Juliet the balcony duet is the hinge of the whole ballet, and in Mayerling Rudolf's duets with each woman are different by design, so his psychology is read through how he handles each partner. The duets are, in the Australian Ballet's phrase, "the ballet's heart and soul and the fixed points around which the drama revolves". [C34178]

**Takeaway:** the duet is the plot -- a classical pas de deux circles one feeling, a MacMillan duet travels through several, and the story is told by the change in the partnering, not by anything said around it. [C34179]

## 16. Off-balance partnering and the ice-skating glide

The partnering is built deliberately off the vertical axis. The woman is pulled off balance, spun continuously, lifted around the man's neck, thrown and caught, rather than placed in stable, centred poses. The shared centre of gravity sits between the two bodies, not over the man's feet. The watchable behaviour is risk without accident: the woman looks as if she is falling, floating, or being swept away, and the man catches her late or at full stretch. In Manon the first thing Des Grieux does is take Manon's hand, let her pivot on pointe, and gently pull her off balance, then move into floating lifts in which she looks as if she is levitating. Joseph Caley, quoted by the Australian Ballet: "A lot of the moves are off balance and everything is spinning"; Adam Bull, in the same piece, speaks of "lifts around the neck" and "death-defying throws and catches". In Romeo and Juliet, Juliet is "literally swept off her feet" on first meeting Romeo, and Playbill notes the choreography is forgiving should she happen to fall off pointe. A related sub-mechanism, verified second-hand: Lady Deborah MacMillan recalled that MacMillan's "obsession with ice skating was the inspiration behind all the swirling pas de deux" in Manon, and Darcey Bussell concurred that the dancers had "literally to glide along the floor in every slide and every lift" -- reported by Seen and Heard International from a backstage interview, not a direct MacMillan quote, and flagged as such. [C34180]

**Takeaway:** the lift is not a pose, it is a fall caught late -- off-balance is the default state of the partnership, and the glide is what makes the risk look like floating rather than falling. [C34181]

## 17. The repeated duet: the same pas de deux restated as a dying body

MacMillan writes a duet early in a ballet and then repeats its shape near the end with the same partner, but with the woman's body now failing. The choreographic material is recognisably the same; what has changed is weight, control and continuity. In Act I of Manon the lifts are ecstatic and the woman is light; in Act III in the Louisiana swamp she is heavy, constantly falling off balance, with little control over her limbs, "like a rag doll, a dead weight". Des Grieux continuously catches, drags and keeps her close. SeeingDance, on the 2024 Royal Ballet Manon: "In intent, it is a repeat of her and Des Grieux's pas de deux from Act I, but MacMillan cleverly shows us how Manon's body is now broken and failing... she is heavy, constantly falling off balance with little control over her limbs. Des Grieux... continuously catches, drags and keeps her close." The Australian Ballet adds that the Act III duet "should look the least choreographic"; Manon "is exhausted and looks like a rag doll, a dead weight"; Mia Heathcote: "absolute devastation." The repetition makes the decline legible without any mime: the audience measures how far the body has fallen against the earlier version. The same device is noted in outline for Romeo and Juliet, where the lovers' duets recur across the ballet with darkening temperature, but the sources describe the Manon case explicitly. [C34182]

**Takeaway:** repeat the duet and break the body -- the audience does not need to be told she is dying, because they have seen the same lifts danced light, and now they watch them fail. [C34183]

## 18. Dance the drama: no empty moments

Every step carries story or character information; there is no filler virtuosity and no conventional mime interlude. MacMillan removes grand entrances and picturesque poses, brings characters on unnoticed or in semi-darkness, and lets an arabesque or a fall do the work a gesture would do in older narrative ballet. Playbill, on the 50th anniversary of Romeo and Juliet, quotes Leslie Browne: "The movement had to say something: there couldn't be any empty moments in there." The watchable behaviour: Romeo first appears cloaked in semi-darkness, lurking anonymously; Juliet slips into the Capulet ball almost unnoticed. Only Juliet and her ladies are on pointe, which marks her out socially and emotionally from the street fighters and aristocrats. When Romeo lands on an arabesque it is "deliberately softened, slouched and swayed, toward Juliet" -- a gesture aimed at her, not at the audience, so the audience does not applaud, it is "swept up... in the narration." A striking detail: the choreography is "deliberate at falling off pointe, not staying on" -- the slip off pointe is part of the design, not a mistake. The Royal Ballet and Opera puts the general claim as reinventing "the narrative ballet as psychological drama". In Manon the same naturalism holds: the 2024 SeeingDance review says the acting "felt totally unforced and absolutely natural". [C34184]

**Takeaway:** the step is the sentence -- if a movement says nothing about the character, it does not belong, and even falling off pointe is choreographed, because the slip is the acting. [C34185]

## 19. Classical vocabulary bent toward realism and the grotesque

MacMillan keeps the classical base -- pointe work, arabesque, pirouette, lift -- but distorts it: flexed feet, flat-footed parallel positions, torsos bent forward, elbows and wrists held at angles, deep plies, tumbling and lying on the floor. In the "spiritual" ballets he imports whole chunks of Martha Graham technique. The Royal Ballet and Opera: he "forged his own distinctive vocabulary of movement, expanding the conventions of ballet to encompass realistic, even grotesque physical reactions... an Expressionist strand." The watchable behaviour: dancers in pointe shoes moving with flexed feet and parallel legs; men sinking into deep plies and ending on the floor; women with bent arms and angled wrists; bodies that look crumpled, then suddenly expansive. In Song of the Earth the Woman bourrees in parallel "as though tugged by a force outside her control", and falls rigidly into the Man's arms, backwards and then face down, while he holds her elbows behind her "as though she is in a strait jacket". DanceTabs, on the English National Ballet Song of the Earth: "Dancers in pointe shoes have to adapt to flexed feet, flat-footed parallel positions, torsos bent forward, elbows and wrists held at angles. Men sink into deep plies, tumble and lie on the floor." Alastair Macaulay, in his 2003 lecture "Ashton and MacMillan": the three "spiritual" ballets -- Song of the Earth, Requiem, Gloria -- "each contains whole chunks of Graham technique, virtually undigested chunks." The Burrow, in the Arts Desk's phrase, was "much more like modern dance than ballet". [C34186]

**Takeaway:** the classical line is the material, not the law -- bend the foot, flatten the leg, crumple the torso, and the same vocabulary that once meant grace now means dread, grief, or exhaustion. [C34187]

## 20. Creating on a muse: shape-language rehearsal

MacMillan did not arrive with counts and fixed steps. He worked on a chosen dancer -- a muse -- and built the role out of that dancer's body, plasticity and personality. He described movement in shapes and images ("It is kind of something up and around, and then you can bring a leg around") rather than in ballet terms, and he was "more passive" in the studio than choreographers such as Tudor or Balanchine, who "knew exactly what they wanted, knew the counts". The roles fit one dancer's body so closely that later casts inherit a physical signature: Seymour's "raggedy-doll pliability" and "uninhibited dancing", Bussell's line, Mukhamedov's Bolshoi leaps in Winter Dreams. A concrete instance: the central pas de deux of Concerto was made by watching Lynn Seymour warming up at the barre; the man becomes the barre, "extending a steady hand out to his female partner who performs movements of focussed simplicity, curving and stretching". The balcony duet of Romeo and Juliet was made in three rehearsals, for Canadian television, before the full ballet was approved -- Seymour's own phrase, quoted by Playbill, is "Like a man possessed, Kenneth completed the balcony pas-de-deux in three rehearsals". Leslie Browne, in the same piece: "He didn't use ballet terms, but instead described the shape of the movement. It wasn't just pure dance, but dance with intention." Parry, quoted in the Lovereading extract of Different Drummer, says his inspiration "sprang from the music he chose and from the bodies and personalities of the dancers who interested him." [C34188]

**Takeaway:** the dancer is the score's second author -- watch the body warm up, describe the shape not the step, and the role arrives already fitted to the only person who can dance it. [C34189]

## 21. Music first, from pre-existing great scores; the patchwork score

MacMillan usually started from a piece of music he loved -- often a major concert work never intended for ballet -- and let the score's structure and associations generate the dance, rather than commissioning a score to a scenario. Where no single score existed, he assembled one: Manon from Massenet's operas, arranged by Leighton Lucas; Mayerling from Liszt, arranged by John Lanchbery. For the choral ballets he took music with its own life and words -- Mahler, Faure, Poulenc -- and let the lyrics and the score's associations supply the imagery. The ballets' shapes follow the music's movements or songs rather than a plot outline: Song of the Earth follows Mahler's six songs, each song a new scene; Gloria builds crucifix configurations of bodies out of the music's ceremonial, hymn-like character. British Ballet Now and Then, on the choral works: they are set to "iconic choral music that had lives of their own well before MacMillan created his choreography to them", and "in place of a narrative are intensely evocative images that arise from the themes, music score and lyrics". The Arts Desk: for the choral works MacMillan "infused his reaction to the compositions' own narratives, associations and images, rather than impositions of his own devising." This is a working tendency documented across many works, not a stated rule: no interview found in this run has MacMillan stating which comes first, music or drama, and the evidence cuts both ways -- Romeo and Juliet began from a dramatic scene, the balcony duet, made before the full ballet was approved. The refusals are a recurring mechanical fact: the Royal Opera House board twice refused his choice of pre-existing sacred or concert music (Mahler, then Faure) as unsuitable for ballet, so both works premiered in Stuttgart. [C34190]

**Takeaway:** take the music as it is, with its own life, and let the ballet follow it -- and when the house refuses the score, make the ballet somewhere else and bring it back once it has succeeded. [C34191]

## 22. Death and violence staged literally, in the body

MacMillan stages what earlier ballet kept offstage or symbolic -- rape, sexual assault, drug use, murder, suicide -- as danced physical action, with the violence carried in the partnering itself: forcing, dragging, threatening with a prop, injecting, shooting. Death is not a graceful swoon; it is a body going heavy, collapsing, or being dragged. In The Invitation a young girl is raped by an older guest; de Valois originally asked him to cut the scene. In Mayerling Rudolf terrifies his bride Stephanie with a revolver before forcing himself on her, later injects himself with morphine, shoots Mary Vetsera and then himself, with gunshots in each act; the Royal Ballet and Opera cast sheet warns that the production "contains depictions of drug use, sexual violence and suicide. There are gunshots in each act." In The Rite of Spring the Chosen Maiden dances herself to death. In Manon the heroine dies as a dead weight in her lover's arms. In The Judas Tree a woman is destroyed by thirteen men on a Canary Wharf building site: physically abused at several points, raped, and eventually murdered by the gang, while one of her lovers is blamed for her death and betrayed by a kiss from the real culprit, who then hangs himself from the scaffolding. In Different Drummer, Woyzeck's hallucinations are staged as wild expressionist imagery -- Marie held in reverse crucifix form by a strange Christ-like figure in a crown of thorns, kicked aside by Woyzeck, who then hoists Marie up in crucifix form; the shaving scene, Woyzeck wielding a naked blade as he climbs over his superior's body. The Arts Desk calls Mayerling's Rudolf "drug-addicted, death-obsessed". [C34192]

**Takeaway:** violence is not suggested, it is danced -- the partnering does the forcing, the dragging and the killing, and a body going heavy is the most honest death ballet has. [C34193]

## 23. Full-length architecture: pas de deux as fixed points, crowd as contrast

In the three-act ballets MacMillan builds the drama around the pas de deux as fixed structural points -- "the heart and soul and the fixed points around which the drama revolves" -- and sets them against large, busy ensemble scenes: market place, ballroom, tavern, brothel, court. The duets carry the private story; the crowd scenes carry the public world that crushes it. The stage alternates between intimate two-person stillness and teeming ensemble: the Verona market place with its brawls and harlots, the Capulet ball, the Manon inn courtyard, the Mayerling tavern with prostitutes competing for attention and police bursting in. The contrast makes the duets feel stolen and private. Within a single relationship he grades the duets by temperature: in Manon, three different temperatures across first meeting, consummation and death. The Royal Ballet and Opera programme for Romeo and Juliet describes "the vibrant crowd scenes... vividly recreat[ing] the colour and bustle of 16th-century Verona". One candidate phrase was checked and not found: no source in this run describes MacMillan deliberately filling every corner with simultaneous action, and none uses the phrase "something going on in every corner" -- the crowd scenes are verified as bustling and vivid, the every-corner formulation is not, and it is not used here as a mechanism. [C34194]

**Takeaway:** the duet is the private room and the crowd is the public world -- the architecture is the alternation, and the private story only reads because the crowd keeps interrupting it. [C34195]

## 24. The short ballet as laboratory; Anastasia backwards, Winter Dreams as meditation

The Arts Desk states the rule directly: "It was often in his short ballets that he exposed his most adventurous originality and took the greatest risks with his dancers." The Burrow, The Invitation, Las Hermanas, The Rite of Spring, Song of the Earth, Requiem, Gloria, Elite Syncopations, The Judas Tree -- the radical work is one act long, and the full-length form is for sustained psychological narrative. But he also built full-length ballets backwards and by meditation rather than by retelling. Anastasia, built backwards: the one-act of 1967 depicted Anna Anderson in a mental asylum trying to recover her memory; in 1971 MacMillan added two earlier acts as prequels, in conventional classical style, showing the imperial family before the revolution -- so the structure is a memory, the asylum present framing a recollected past, and phrases from the opening recur in Act III as flashbacks of happier times that make her current distress all the more poignant. Winter Dreams, a meditation not a retelling: MacMillan "didn't set out to tell the play's story as a ballet... but to meditate on its character's dilemmas" -- Chekhov's Three Sisters as "an extended pas de deux for Masha and Vershinin, interrupted by vignettes for the other characters", climaxing in a Farewell pas de deux, with Vershinin's big Bolshoi-style leaps as "a pent-up expression of the frustration that dominates the ballet". The final pas de deux had been performed a year earlier, at the Queen Mother's 90th birthday celebrations at the London Palladium, before the full ballet existed. Elite Syncopations is the comic counterpart: ragtime body language teased into classical line, a twelve-piece band seated visibly at the back of the stage, adding to a 1920s party atmosphere. [C34196]

**Takeaway:** the one-act is where he risks, and the full-length is where he remembers -- build the ballet backwards from the asylum, or meditate on a play instead of retelling it, and the structure itself does the psychology. [C34197]

## 25. His own words

All of the following are quoted in a source read end to end; the attribution chain is given each time, and nothing here is paraphrase presented as quotation. "I stopped dancing when I was about 23 mainly because I had just terrible stage fright and I hated performing. I turned to choreography as a release from dancing and I was lucky enough that the first thing I did everyone liked." -- MacMillan, BBC interview 1990, quoted by Wikipedia. On Song of the Earth: "A man and a woman; death takes the man; they both return to her, and at the end of the ballet we find that in death there is the promise of renewal." -- MacMillan, quoted in the Song of the Earth article. On Requiem: "This danced Requiem is dedicated to the memory of my friend and colleague John Cranko, Director of the Stuttgart Ballet 1961-1973." -- MacMillan, quoted in the Requiem article. On La Fin du jour: the ballet "draws its inspiration from the style of the 'thirties; the designs and choreography are inspired by the fashion plates of an era and a way of life shattered forever by the Second World War." -- MacMillan, quoted in the La Fin du jour article. On Winter Dreams: "Although the characters in the ballet are named after those in the play, I have not attempted a balletic reworking of the whole story. Sometimes the choreography reflects the inner life of the characters, at other times the narrative. I have tried to capture the atmosphere and melancholy of Chekhov's masterpiece." -- MacMillan, quoted in the Winter Dreams article. On The Judas Tree: "There are things in me that are untapped and that have come out in this ballet that I find frightening." -- MacMillan, quoted in the Judas Tree article. [C34198]

**Takeaway:** he states the method himself, in the programme notes -- release from dancing, death with a promise of renewal, a memorial for a friend, the atmosphere of a play rather than its story, and things in himself he finds frightening. [C34199]

## 26. Aesthetic logic: the outsider, the duet, the darkness

The aesthetic logic of MacMillan's work runs on three rules, each visible in more than one ballet. First, the outsider is the subject: the central figure is someone who does not belong in the room she is in -- the masked clown, the Adolescent in the burrow, Anna Anderson among her sisters, Rudolf at his own court, the woman on the building site -- and the ballet is built to watch her fail to belong. Second, the duet is the plot: the story is told as a chain of pas de deux, each with its own temperature, and the partnering itself -- who lifts whom, who is carried as dead weight, who is forced to the floor -- is the characterisation. Third, darkness is the default and lightness the exception: the elegies and the ragtime comedy exist, but the line from The Invitation through Manon and Mayerling to The Judas Tree is the spine of the career, and the late return to fairy tale in The Prince of the Pagodas is the exception that proves it. What he never does is the thing a Royal Ballet choreographer of his standing was supposed to do: he never keeps the violence offstage, never lets a classical pas de deux circle one feeling, never commissions a score when a great one already exists, never lets the crowd be decoration, and never lets the ballet end with a redemption it has not earned. The cost of that is visible in the record -- the divided premieres, the board refusals, the unhappy Berlin years, the directorship that did not suit him, the heart attack in 1988 and the death backstage in 1992 -- and the cost is the same as the achievement: a ballet that tells the truth about sex, power and death cannot be a comfortable evening, and he never asked it to be. He is the choreographer who made the narrative ballet a psychological drama, and the study of him is the study of what happens when the pas de deux is given the whole story to carry -- and of what it costs when the man carrying it is also the one who cannot bear to be watched. [C34200]

**Takeaway:** know what the instrument is -- an outsider, a duet, and a darkness -- and the failures are as legible as the successes: a ballet that tells the truth is the same fact as a premiere that divides the house. [C34201]
