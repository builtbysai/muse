## 1. The Friulian boy who learned a language he did not speak

He was born in Bologna on 5 March 1922, a Sunday, the elder son of Susanna Colussi, a Friulian elementary school teacher, and Carlo Alberto Pasolini, a lieutenant in the Royal Italian Army; his parents had married in 1921 and he was named after a paternal uncle. [C31797] The family moved to Conegliano in 1923, then to Belluno in 1925, where their second son, Guidalberto (Guido), was born -- and the birth order matters, because one later source calls Guido the older brother, against the main article's account that Pasolini was the elder son; the study follows the main article and flags the conflict. [C31798] In 1926 his father was arrested for gambling debts, and his mother moved with the children to her family's home in Casarsa della Delizia, in the Friuli region, the village that becomes the first sacred ground of his work. [C31799] He began writing poems at age seven, inspired by the natural beauty of Casarsa, and one of his early influences was the work of Arthur Rimbaud -- the poet of the visionary boy, which is the role Pasolini keeps playing for the rest of his life. [C31800] His father was transferred to Idria in the Julian March (now in Slovenia) in 1931, and in 1933 the family moved again to Cremona in Lombardy, and later to Scandiano and Reggio Emilia; he found it difficult to adapt to all these dislocations, and the dislocations never stop -- Bologna, Friuli, Rome, Matera, Morocco, Yemen, Nepal -- the work is a life of moving and then filming the place he has just arrived in. [C31801] In the Reggio Emilia high school he met his first true friend, Luciano Serra, and in Bologna he spent seven years completing high school, where he cultivated new passions including football -- the game he will still be playing on the Salo set thirty years later. [C31802] In 1939 he graduated and entered the Literature College of the University of Bologna, discovering philology and the aesthetics of figurative arts, and he frequented the local cinema club: the poet is trained as a philologist before he is trained as a filmmaker, and it shows in everything after. [C31803]

In his poems of this period he started to include fragments in Friulan, a minority language he did not speak but learned after he had begun to write poetry in it, calling it "a sort of mystic act of love". [C31804] In 1942 he published at his own expense a collection of poems in Friulan, Poesie a Casarsa, written at the age of eighteen, and it was noted and appreciated by critics including Gianfranco Contini, Alfonso Gatto and Antonio Russi -- though the title itself is flagged, the article's bibliography giving "Poesia a Casarsa" and a mirror giving "Versi a Casarsa". [C31805] He was chief editor of a magazine called Il Setaccio ("The Sieve") but was fired after conflicts with the director, who was aligned with the Fascist regime, and a trip to Germany helped him perceive the "provincial" status of Italian culture, leading him to revise his opinion of Fascist cultural politics and shift gradually to a communist position. [C31806] He founded the Academiuta della lenga furlana (Academy of the Friulan Language) with fellow students -- dated 1943 in one part of the article and six days after Guido's death, 18 February 1945, in another; the study uses the dated 1945 founding and flags the 1943 dating, not settled. [C31807] From May 1944 he and other young enthusiasts of the Friulan language issued a magazine entitled Stroligut di ca da l'aga, wanting to give Casarsa Friulan a status equal to that of Udine, the official regional standard: the first mechanism of his whole career is already here -- take a despised local tongue and treat it as a sacred language. [C31808]

**Takeaway:** The Friuli years are the whole method in miniature. A boy who does not speak Friulan learns it in order to write poetry in it, because the minority language is more real to him than the official one. Everything after is the same move at larger scale: the Roman dialect of the borgate, the southern Italian landscape as the Holy Land, a dead Sade novel as a film about 1944. Find the despised tongue, learn it, and consecrate it.

## 2. War, Guido's death, the scandal, Rome

In the waning years of World War II he was drafted into the Italian Army, and after his regiment was captured by the Germans following Italy's surrender he escaped and fled to Casarsa, where he remained for several years. [C31809] His brother Guido, aged 19, joined the Party of Action and their Brigate Osoppo, taking to the bush near Slovenia, and on Monday 12 February 1945 he was killed in an ambush planted by the Brigate Garibaldi serving in the lines of Josip Broz Tito's Yugoslavian guerrillas, which devastated Pasolini and his mother -- killed not by fascists but by fellow partisans, the wound that never closes in his politics. [C31810] Six days after his brother's death, on Sunday 18 February 1945, Pasolini and others founded the Friulan Language Academy (Academiuta di lenga furlana). [C31811] On account of Guido's death, his father returned to Italy from his detention period in November 1945 and settled in Casarsa, and that same month Pasolini graduated from university after completing a final thesis about the work of Giovanni Pascoli (1855-1912), an Italian poet and classical scholar. [C31812] In 1946 he published a small poetry collection, I Diarii ("The Diaries"), with the Academiuta, travelled to Rome in October, began the Quaderni Rossi (handwritten notebooks with red covers) the following May, completed a drama in Italian, Il Cappellano, and had his poetry collection I Pianti ("The cries") published by the Academiuta. [C31813]

A scandal broke out during a local festival in Ramuscello in September 1949, when he was accused of sexual conduct with three teenagers aged fifteen to sixteen; a judge charged him with "corruption of minors and obscene acts in public places", the trial was scrapped for lack of evidence, and he and the two sixteen-year-old boys were acquitted but forced to pay the court costs. [C31814] He was fired from his teaching job in Valvasone and was expelled from the Italian Communist Party (PCI) by the party's Udine section, which he considered a betrayal -- expelled by the party for the same sexuality the party's enemies would prosecute him for, and he never forgave either side. [C31815] In late 1949 he decided to move to Rome along with his mother, seeking to start a new life; the move is dated January 1950 in the article's detailed account, against an EBSCO extract saying 1945, and the study follows 1950 and flags the conflict. [C31816]

In January 1950 he moved to Rome with his mother Susanna, and he was acquitted of two indecency charges in 1950 and 1952. [C31817] After one year sheltered in a maternal uncle's flat next to Piazza Mattei, he and his 59-year-old mother moved to a run-down suburb called Rebibbia, next to a prison, living there for three years. [C31818] Rebibbia was one of the infamous borgate where poor proletarian immigrants lived, often in horrendous sanitary and social conditions, and Pasolini transferred his Friulan countryside inspiration to this Roman suburb instead of asking other writers for help -- Friuli to Rebibbia is the move that makes him: the sacred countryside becomes the sacred slum. [C31819] He found a job working in the Cinecitta film studios and sold his books in the bancarelle ("sidewalk shops") of Rome, and in 1951, with the help of the Abruzzese-language poet Vittorio Clemente, he found a job as a secondary school teacher in Ciampino, just outside the capital, a long commute involving two train changes for a meagre salary of 27,000 lire. [C31820] In 1954 he left his teaching job, moved to the Monteverde quarter, worked for the literary section of Cinecitta, and published La meglio gioventu, his first important collection of Friulan poems; his ailing, cirrhotic father Carlo Alberto moved in with them and died in 1958. [C31821]

**Takeaway:** Rome does not change him; it gives him a new Friuli. The borgate are the same material as Casarsa -- a poor, despised, pre-modern world living inside the modern city -- and he films it the same way he wrote about Friuli: by learning its language and treating its people as sacred. The scandal and the expulsion are not interruptions of the work; they are the work's subject, the price of loving what both the Church and the Party call unclean.

## 3. The borgate novels

His first novel, Ragazzi di vita (English: Hustlers), which dealt with the Roman lumpenproletariat, was published in 1955 by Garzanti, had great success, but was poorly received by the PCI establishment and by the Italian government. [C31822] Ragazzi di vita initiated a lawsuit for "obscenity" against Pasolini and his publisher Garzanti; although exonerated, he became a target of insinuations, especially in the tabloid press -- the first of what will become, over his life, up to 33 lawsuits, on charges including obscenity and contempt of religion, from which he was always eventually acquitted. [C31823] In 1955, together with Francesco Leonetti, Roberto Roversi and others, he edited and published a poetry magazine called Officina, which closed in 1959 after fourteen issues. [C31824] His second novel, Una vita violenta, was published in 1959 and, unlike his first, was embraced by the Communist cultural sphere. [C31825] He wrote a column titled Dialoghi con Passolini (Passolini in Dialogue) for the PCI magazine Vie Nuove from May 1960 to September 1965, published in book form in 1977 as Le belle bandiere (The Beautiful Flags). [C31826] His poem "The Ashes of Gramsci" dates to 1954 and the collection Le ceneri di Gramsci was published in 1957, voicing tormented tensions between reason and heart and the debates within communism over artistic freedom, socialist realism and commitment -- the Gramsci he loves is the Gramsci the Party will not let him be. [C31827]

**Takeaway:** The novels are the rehearsal for the films. Ragazzi di vita already does what Accattone will do: take the boys of the borgate, give them their own dialect, and refuse to explain or redeem them. The obscenity trial is also the rehearsal: the state will keep trying him for showing what it does not want seen, and he will keep being acquitted, which only proves the trials were the point.

## 4. Screenwriting apprenticeship: Fellini's Roman ear

In 1957, together with Sergio Citti, Pasolini collaborated on Federico Fellini's film Nights of Cabiria, writing dialogue for the Roman dialect sections, and Fellini also asked him to work on dialogue for some episodes of La dolce vita (1960); he made his debut as an actor in The Hunchback of Rome in 1960 and co-wrote Long Night in 1943 (1960), with earlier writing credits including The River Girl (1955), Young Husbands (1958), Bad Girls Don't Cry (1959), Il bell'Antonio (1960), From a Roman Balcony (1960) and Girl in the Window (1961). [C31828] The apprenticeship is a trade: Fellini, the Rimini boy who became Rome's fabulist, hires the Friulian who has learned the borgate's dialect to write the words Fellini's Romans speak -- and Pasolini learns, watching Fellini, that a director can build a whole cinema out of the people the city throws away. [C31829] The two men are the two poles of postwar Italian cinema after this: Fellini the populist-fantastic, Pasolini the sacred-poor, and they need each other to define themselves against. [C31830]

**Takeaway:** He enters cinema through the ear, not the eye. The dialect dialogue is the same work as the Friulan poems: a despised tongue, learned from the outside, treated as literature. When he starts directing, the camera will do what the dialogue did -- take the poor at their own word.

## 5. Accattone (1961): the first film, and the method arrives whole

Pasolini's first film as director and screenwriter was Accattone in 1961, again set among Rome's marginal communities, a story of pimps, prostitutes and thieves that contrasted with Italy's postwar economic recovery, and although he tried to distance himself from neorealism it is considered a type of second neorealism. [C31831] Accattone premiered on Thursday 31 August 1961 at the 22nd Venice International Film Festival, was released in Italy on Monday 25 September 1961, runs 117 minutes, and was produced by Alfredo Bini and Cino Del Duca for Arco Film. [C31832] Franco Citti, a non-actor working as a day laborer at the time, was cast in the title role because Pasolini appreciated his distinctly Roman features, it was Citti's acting debut and he became a regular collaborator, the vast majority of the cast were also non-professional actors, and Bernardo Bertolucci was an assistant director on the film, later describing the experience as his "film school". [C31833] In lieu of an original soundtrack the film is scored with classical music by Johann Sebastian Bach, with Carlo Rustichelli as musical director, and the film's certificate was withdrawn on Wednesday 22 November 1961 following the intercession of the Minister of Tourism and Entertainment, with Federico Fellini publicly protesting the censorship. [C31834] Accattone was critically acclaimed, earning three Nastro d'Argento nominations including Best Director for Pasolini and Best Actor for Citti, a BAFTA nomination for Citti as Best Foreign Actor, and in 2008 it was included in the Italian Ministry of Cultural Heritage's list of 100 Italian films to be saved. [C31835] Whether the film is adapted from his novel Una vita violenta or an original screenplay is flagged, not settled: the article's film table lists it as adapted, the Accattone article treats it as an original screenplay. [C31836]

The mechanism is already complete in the first film. Bach over a pimp's story is not a joke and not a contrast: it is the claim that the sacred and the profane are the same material, and that a pimp's death can be scored like a Passion because, in Pasolini's eye, it is one. [C31837] The non-actor face is the other half: Citti is not playing a pimp, he is a day laborer whose face Pasolini chose because it looked Roman, and the camera holds him frontally, like a painting, until the face stops being a character and becomes an image. [C31838]

**Takeaway:** The first film contains all the later ones. Bach against the borgate, a non-actor held like a saint, the state trying to withdraw the certificate, Fellini defending him: the whole career is Accattone, repeated at larger scale and higher stakes, until Salo repeats it with the sacred removed.

## 6. Mamma Roma (1962): the Mantegna deathbed

Mamma Roma (1962) was written and directed by Pasolini, starring Anna Magnani, Ettore Garofolo and Franco Citti, with screenplay by Pasolini and additional dialogue by Citti and cinematography by Tonino Delli Colli. [C31839] The story follows prostitute Mamma Roma, who, after her pimp Carmine marries, starts a new life as a market seller in Rome to give her 16-year-old son Ettore a better life, but when Carmine forces her back into prostitution and Ettore learns of it, he returns to thieving, is caught during a theft in a hospital, and dies in jail from a fever, leaving his grieving mother behind. [C31840] Pasolini based his screenplay on the true case of Marcello Elisei, who had died in prison, shooting began on 9 April 1962 with a cast that largely consisted of non-professionals, and filming took place in the Parco degli Acquedotti on the ancient Appian Way, where the dome of San Giovanni Bosco in Via Tuscolana is seen prominently in several scenes. [C31841] Mamma Roma premiered on Friday 31 August 1962 at the Venice Film Festival, a police complaint claiming the film was "offensive to good morals" was turned down by a magistrate five days later, at the Rome release in the Quattro Fontane Cinema on Saturday 22 September 1962 Pasolini was confronted with protesting neo-fascists and got involved in a scuffle, its domestic box office was a humble 168 million lire, and it was attacked by both the Right and the Left for opposite reasons -- the position he will occupy for the rest of his life, attacked from both sides at once. [C31842] The deathbed of Ettore reproduces the formal structure of Andrea Mantegna's Lamentation over the Dead Christ, shot from the same extremely low frontal angle from which Mantegna depicts his Christ, so that the boy's strapped body assumes the posture of Christ after the deposition from the cross. [C31843] Magnani is the exception that proves the casting rule: a professional, a star, and the film bends around her -- but the boy who dies is a non-professional, and his death is a painting, not a scene. [C31844]

**Takeaway:** Mamma Roma is where the tableau becomes the argument. The film does not say the poor boy is like Christ; it frames him so that he is Christ, in the exact geometry Mantegna used. The sacred is not a theme laid over the story; it is the composition itself.

## 7. La ricotta (1963): the blasphemy trial

La ricotta (1963), Pasolini's episode in the omnibus film Ro.Go.Pa.G., is a film about a film about the Passion of Jesus, starring Orson Welles as the director, in which the Christ is a starving film extra who gives his box lunch to his hungry family, loses a stolen meal to a visiting movie star's lap dog, and, after stuffing himself with ricotta cheese, dies from indigestion on the cross. [C31845] Soon after its release in 1963 La ricotta was seized "for insulting the religion of the state", Pasolini was put on trial for offence to the Italian state and religion, found guilty and sentenced to prison for four months, a conviction voided by an appeals court the following year, and in the disclaimer added to the film he affirmed that the Passion of Christ was "the greatest [story] that ever took place". [C31846] The joke is the theology: the extra dies on the cross not from the nails but from eating too much, and the film's blasphemy is that it treats the Passion as a labour dispute -- the extra is hungry, the production feeds the dog first, and the crucifixion is just the end of a bad day's work. [C31847]

**Takeaway:** La ricotta is the film that gets him convicted, and it is also the film that states his creed most plainly: the Passion is the greatest story ever told, and the proof is that it keeps happening to the poor on film sets. The state heard blasphemy; he meant it as devotion.

## 8. The Gospel According to St. Matthew (1964): remake by analogy

The Gospel According to St. Matthew (Il Vangelo secondo Matteo, 1964) was written and directed by Pasolini, based on the Gospel of Matthew from the Nativity through the Resurrection, premiered on Friday 4 September 1964 at the 25th Venice International Film Festival where it won the Silver Lion and the OCIC Award, was released in Italy on Friday 2 October 1964, and runs 137 minutes. [C31848] The impetus for the film came in 1962, when Pasolini accepted Pope John XXIII's invitation for a new dialogue with non-Catholic artists and visited Assisi for a seminar at a Franciscan monastery, where the papal visit caused traffic jams that left him confined to his hotel room, there he came across a copy of the New Testament and read all four Gospels straight through, and he claimed that adapting a film from one of them "threw in the shade all the other ideas for work I had in my head". [C31849] He chose Matthew's Gospel because he felt "John was too mystical, Mark too vulgar, and Luke too sentimental", and every word of dialogue in the film is drawn from the Gospel of Matthew, though the film deletes 592 of Matthew's 1070 verses and rearranges scenes at least seventeen times -- the text is sacred and also raw material, to be cut. [C31850] In the neorealist tradition most of the actors were non-professionals: Enrique Irazoqui, a 19-year-old economics student from Spain and a communist activist, played Jesus, Pasolini cast his own mother Susanna as the older Mary, the rest of the cast were mainly locals from Barile, Matera and Massafra, and it also included noted intellectuals such as writers Enzo Siciliano, Alfonso Gatto and Natalia Ginzburg, poet Rodolfo Wilcock and philosopher Giorgio Agamben. [C31851] The film was shot entirely on location in southern Italy, using the real cave dwellings of the Sassi di Matera for Mary and Joseph's working-class home, and Pasolini rejected the Holy Land because he found the locations unsuitable and "commercialized" and Israel's landscape too modern and industrialized to stand in for the time of Christ. [C31852] He described his method as "remake the Gospel by analogy", saying he did not want to reconstruct Christ as he actually was but to make "the history of Christ plus two thousand years of Christian storytelling about the life of Christ", adding "I want to consecrate things again, because that is possible, I want to re-mythologize them", and the film is dedicated to the memory of Pope John XXIII. [C31853]

The collision is the whole film. His Gospel draws on 2,000 years of Christian painting and sculpture, dresses Roman soldiers and Pharisees after Renaissance art and Jesus after Byzantine art and the Expressionist Georges Rouault, scores Bach against Odetta, Blind Willie Johnson, "Kol Nidre" and the Congolese Missa Luba, and sets a first-century story in the present-day poor of southern Italy. [C31854] Irazoqui was picked for his resemblance to El Greco's Christ, and his voice does not belong to him: he is dubbed by Enrico Maria Salerno, so the face on screen and the voice that speaks are two different men, and the film's Christ is a body borrowed by a text. [C31855]

**Takeaway:** The Gospel is the purest statement of the method, and the atheist directing the life of Christ is not a paradox but the point: only a man who does not believe can treat the sacred as a form, and only a man who loves the poor can find the form in Matera. Remake by analogy means the old story is true because it keeps happening, not because it happened once.

## 9. The "cinema of poetry" theory (1965)

Pasolini delivered his lecture "Il cinema di poesia" ("The Cinema of Poetry") in Italian in June 1965 at the first New Cinema Festival at Pesaro, a French translation appeared in Cahiers du Cinema No. 171 in October 1965, it was later collected in his volume of essays Empirismo eretico (1972), and the English version appears in Movies and Methods Vol. 1 (1976), pages 542-558. [C31856] The essay's core claim is that literature works on lin-signs (words), an instrumental language whose primary function is communication, while cinema works on im-signs (images), which have no grammar and no dictionary but nevertheless carry primary meaning because images play a central role in memory and dreams and some images have public, objective meanings, like traffic signs or conventional gestures. [C31857] Pasolini offers two ways of compensating for the primitive semiotic character of im-signs: superimposing a narrative structure derived from literature or oral storytelling, or using the free indirect point-of-view shot (soggettiva libera indiretta), the cinematic adaptation of free indirect discourse, which lets the director slip into the perspective of a neurotic or hypersensitive character. [C31858] Through the free indirect point-of-view shot the filmmaker can create a stylistic substitute for the missing linguistic structure, using obsessive or static framing, reversals in narrow and expansive depth of field, abrupt shifts in the treatment of color, swift changes in angle of view, camera distance from the subject, or the focal lengths of lenses, and certain kinds of sequence shots. [C31859] The essay ends with a twist: Pasolini calls the neurotic, hypersensitive central characters of Antonioni, Bertolucci and Godard "exquisite flowers of the bourgeoisie" and a form of false universalism, resists identifying his own Marxist work with the cinema of poetry that became ascendant after neorealism, and yet regards Antonioni, Bertolucci and Godard as Europe's finest filmmakers -- he writes the theory, exempts himself from it, and praises the men it describes. [C31860]

**Takeaway:** The essay is the instruction manual he then disobeys in the most productive way: his own films use the free indirect shot constantly, but the neurotic character is replaced by a pimp, a prostitute, a peasant Christ. The theory says the camera can think like a character; his practice says the character worth thinking like is the one the bourgeoisie calls unthinkable.

## 10. The Hawks and the Sparrows (1966): the sung credits, and Morricone arrives

The Hawks and the Sparrows was released on 4 May 1966 and entered into the 1966 Cannes Film Festival, where Toto received a Special Mention for his acting. [C31861] It was the last film to star Toto before his death from a heart attack in 1967. [C31862] Toto and his son Ninetto walk through Rome and the countryside, meet a talking crow who presents himself as a left-wing intellectual, hear the crow's tale of two friars told by St. Francis to preach love to hawks (the powerful) and sparrows (the weak), and finally kill and eat the crow because they find him unconscionably boring. [C31863] Pasolini declared The Hawks and the Sparrows his favourite film, the only one that did not disappoint his expectations. [C31864] The film was Morricone's first collaboration with Pasolini, and CAM Sugar calls it the start of the Maestro's creative bond with the director. [C31865] The opening credits are sung: Pasolini wrote lyrics naming each cast and crew member with ironic lines between the names, Morricone wrote the music with a different orchestration for each name, and Domenico Modugno performed it (2 minutes 26 seconds); a 40-second sung coda closes the film. [C31866] Morricone wrote an original score of about 16 minutes for the film, but also yielded to Pasolini's request and adapted Mozart's Die Zauberflote for ocarina and violin, and some of Morricone's original cues were removed by the director. [C31867] The Hawks and the Sparrows score was nominated for the Silver Ribbon Award for Best Score. [C31868]

The crow is the film's joke about the director himself: a left-wing intellectual who talks and talks, telling a parable about hawks and sparrows, until his audience gets bored and eats him. Pasolini kills his own surrogate on screen, and calls the result his favourite film. [C31869] The sung credits are the other mechanism: the credit roll, the most bureaucratic part of a film, becomes a song that names every maker with ironic commentary, so the film begins by telling you it knows it is a film and who made it. [C31870]

**Takeaway:** The Hawks and the Sparrows is the hinge. Before it, the music is found (Bach, the Gospel's collage); after it, Morricone is in the room, and the question becomes how much original music a Pasolini film needs -- and the answer, over the next nine years, will be less and less.

## 11. Oedipus Rex, Porcile, Medea: the films Morricone did not score

Pasolini's episode "La Terra vista dalla Luna" (The Earth Seen from the Moon) appeared in the omnibus film Le Streghe (The Witches, 1967), reuniting Toto, Ninetto Davoli and Silvana Mangano; the Morricone compositions list credits the film's score to Morricone with Piero Piccioni, and maintitles.net counts Le Streghe among the four Pasolini films for which Morricone wrote a full score. [C31871] Oedipus Rex, released at Venice on 3 September 1967, was Pasolini's first feature-length colour film, shot mainly in Morocco at Ouarzazate, Ait-Ben-Haddou and Zagora. [C31872] Oedipus Rex has no Morricone credit: its score is built from Romanian folk music taken from the Electrecord Anthology of Romanian folk music, plus a Japanese gagaku theme, Indonesian kecak, Mozart's String Quartet No. 19 in C major K465 as the mother's theme, and Antonio Fuselli's Marcetta Bandistica as the father's theme. [C31873] Pasolini said of Oedipus Rex, "in Oedipus, I tell the story of my complex of Oedipus. The child in the prologue is me, his father is my father, an infantry officer, and his mother, a teacher, is my mother." [C31874] The film opens and closes in 1920s Italy, which Pasolini called an act of Freudian sublimation; the blind Oedipus ends the film wandering and playing the flute. [C31875]

Porcile (Pigsty), released in Italy on 31 August 1969, runs two parallel stories: a cannibal on the slopes of Etna around 1500, in a tale almost without dialogue until the protagonist's final line, and Julian, a young man in a 1967 Godesberg villa who secretly lies with the pigs and is finally devoured by them. [C31876] Porcile has no Morricone credit: its original music is by Benedetto Ghiglia, and the Horst Wessel Lied is used as music. [C31877] Medea (1969) stars opera singer Maria Callas in her only film role, and she does not sing in the film; it was shot between May and August 1969 in the Goreme open-air museum churches, Pisa and the Citadel of Aleppo. [C31878] Medea's music is credited to Pasolini and Elsa Morante as coordinators, not to Morricone: for the Colchis rituals Pasolini chose Tibetan chant for the elders, Persian santur music for general atmosphere, and Balkan choral music for the women, with Japanese sacred music and Iranian love songs also listed. [C31879] Costume designer Piero Tosi used three colour schemes in Medea: earth tones with blues for Colchis, white and yellow for Iolcus, and red inspired by Pontormo's painting for Corinth. [C31880] Medea was received positively by critics but had no commercial success, and Callas was 46 at the time. [C31881]

The three films prove the rule by breaking it: when Morricone is absent, Pasolini does not hire another composer to write a score -- he curates harder. Romanian folk, Tibetan chant, a Mozart quartet as a mother, a Nazi anthem in a pigsty: the music is assigned by place and class, so the soundtrack is a map of which world a scene belongs to. [C31882]

**Takeaway:** The Morricone-less films are the control experiment. They show that Pasolini's musical method does not need a composer at all -- it needs a record collection and a sense of moral geography. Morricone, when he is there, is working inside a system that already knows how to use found music; his job is to write the few original notes the found music cannot supply.

## 12. Teorema (1968): the Visitor as a theorem

Teorema, Pasolini's sixth feature and his first made mainly with professional actors, premiered at Venice on 4 September 1968 and opened in Italy on 7 September 1968; it runs 98 minutes. [C31883] In Teorema a mysterious Visitor has sex with every member of a Milanese bourgeois household (maid, son, mother, daughter, father) and then leaves; the maid returns to her village, performs miracles and is buried alive in dirt, the daughter falls catatonic, the son paints the Visitor obsessively, the mother picks up young men, and the father gives away his factory, strips naked at a railway station and wanders the slopes of Mount Etna screaming. [C31884] Pasolini expanded Teorema into a novel of the same name in the same year, 1968, written simultaneously with the film's production. [C31885] Pasolini and producer Donato Leoni were charged with obscenity over Teorema but acquitted after only an hour's deliberation; the film had won a special award from the International Catholic Film Office at Venice, which the Vatican had withdrawn after protesting. [C31886] Teorema credits music to Ennio Morricone; MoMA describes a hellishly discordant score of dissonant motifs for string, harpsichord and chorus interwoven with groovy 1960s pop and Mozart's Requiem, and Soundohm says about half of the soundtrack was written by Morricone, the rest being Mozart numbers. [C31887] CAM Sugar calls the Teorema soundtrack a mini-soundtrack, one of Pasolini's most psychedelic and pop moments, with the teenage Milanese beat group Trio Junior performing the vocal track "Fruscio di Foglie Verdi". [C31888]

The film is a theorem in the mathematical sense: posit one unknown (the Visitor, played by Terence Stamp), apply him to every term of a bourgeois household, and read off the results. The maid becomes a saint, the father becomes a naked screamer on Etna -- the same Etna where Porcile's cannibal walks -- and the household, which looked solid, turns out to have been held together by nothing. [C31889]

**Takeaway:** Teorema is the most watchable statement of his politics: the bourgeoisie does not need to be attacked, it needs to be visited. One beautiful stranger, one act of love per member, and the whole structure falls apart by itself. The theorem proves that the sacred is not missing from bourgeois life -- it is the one thing bourgeois life cannot survive.

## 13. The Trilogy of Life (1971-1974): the erotic turn, and the disowning

The Decameron, The Canterbury Tales and Arabian Nights form Pasolini's Trilogy of Life, each adapting a classical story collection. [C31890] The Decameron, first film of the trilogy, premiered at Berlin on 29 June 1971, opened in Italy on 25 August 1971, and won the Silver Bear Extraordinary Jury Prize. [C31891] The Decameron was the third most popular film in Italy in 1971, with 11,167,557 admissions. [C31892] Pasolini moved Boccaccio's tales from Florence to Naples and used Neapolitan dialect, intending not to recreate the medieval world but to criticise the contemporary world, including the economic exploitation of the poorer south by the richer north. [C31893] Pasolini plays Giotto's pupil in The Decameron, and the epilogue has Giotto admire his finished fresco and ask, "Why create a work of art when dreaming about it is so much sweeter?" [C31894] The Decameron soundtrack consists mainly of pre-existing music: in consultation with Morricone, Pasolini chose Italian folk songs from different localities and Latin medieval chants, drawing heavily on Alan Lomax's album Italian Folk Music, Vol. 5: Naples and Campania for Folkways, and the credits read "Music selections by: Pier Paolo Pasolini" and "Musical collaborator: Ennio Morricone", not composer. [C31895] Two Decameron scenes were deleted and are now presumed lost: Girolamo and Salvestra, cut because Pasolini felt it a weaker story, and Rustico and Alibech, cut so the Yemen scenery could be saved as a surprise for Arabian Nights. [C31896]

The Canterbury Tales, second film of the trilogy, premiered at Berlin on 2 July 1972, opened in Italy on 2 September 1972, and won the Golden Bear at the 22nd Berlin International Film Festival. [C31897] The Canterbury Tales adapts eight of Chaucer's 24 tales, and Pasolini himself plays Geoffrey Chaucer; Pasolini considered the film among his most ideological. [C31898] The Canterbury Tales credits music to Ennio Morricone with Carl Hardebeck uncredited, but maintitles.net says the film mostly reworks old music and contains only one original Morricone cue, 32 seconds long; the Criterion credits read "Music selections by: Pier Paolo Pasolini" and "Music collaborator: Ennio Morricone". [C31899] The Canterbury Tales credits roll over the traditional ballad "Ould Piper", and Perkin ends the Cook's Tale arrested and put in the stocks, drunkenly singing the same ballad. [C31900] Arabian Nights, the last film of the trilogy, premiered at Cannes on 20 May 1974, opened in Italy on 20 June 1974, and won the Grand Prix Special at the 1974 Cannes Film Festival; its lost original cut ran 155 minutes against 125 minutes for the release cut, and Pasolini does not appear in it as an actor. [C31901] Arabian Nights is mostly based on the tale of Ali Shar and Zumurrud, drops the Scheherazade frame story, and is built from 16 scenes of stories within stories; Ninetto Davoli plays Aziz, who is castrated by his former lover Budur, and the film's own page reads this as Pasolini's farewell to Davoli, who had married in 1973. [C31902] Most of the Arabian Nights score was composed by Morricone and deliberately keeps away from traditional music, unlike the first two trilogy films; the music is symphonic, to separate the film from reality and give it a dream-like quality, and Mozart's String Quartet No. 15 Andante is also used to contrast the poverty on screen with the richness of the music. [C31903] Arabian Nights was filmed in Iran, north and south Yemen, the deserts of Eritrea and Ethiopia, and Nepal, with some interiors at a studio in Rome; Pasolini had wanted more native actors but Muslim governments did not permit nudity for their citizens, so non-professional native actors mostly play the roles without nudity. [C31904] Pasolini disowned the trilogy in the Abiura della trilogia della vita, written on 15 June 1975 and published posthumously in Corriere della Sera on 9 November 1975, a week after his death. [C31905]

The trilogy's engine is the nested tale: a frame story opens into tales told by characters inside it, and each told tale changes the teller's fate, so the story tree itself is the plot. [C31906] And the disowning is the most Pasolini gesture of all: the three films were his greatest commercial success, 11 million admissions for The Decameron alone, and he repudiated them because success meant the erotic body had been consumed as entertainment instead of liberation -- the sacred poor had become a product, and he would answer it with Salo. [C31907]

**Takeaway:** The Trilogy of Life is the one place where the method seems to fail on its own terms: the folk songs, the dialect, the non-professional bodies are all there, but the audience laughed and came back, and the liberation became a box-office genre. His answer is not to defend the films but to disown them and make a film nobody can enjoy. That is the whole of Pasolini in one move.

## 14. The Morricone collaboration: four full scores, three curations

The director table in the Wikipedia Ennio Morricone article lists "Pier Paolo Pasolini (1922-1975) | 1965-1975 | 7 | mystery/historical | including The Hawks and the Sparrows, Teorema, The Decameron, The Canterbury Tales, Arabian Nights and Salo, or the 120 Days of Sodom". [C31908] The seven films reconcile as: The Hawks and the Sparrows (1966), Le Streghe (1967), Teorema (1968), The Decameron (1971), The Canterbury Tales (1972), Arabian Nights (1974) and Salo (1975) -- the six named in the table plus Le Streghe, which the table omits by name. [C31909] By the maintitles.net account, Morricone wrote a full score for four of the seven (The Hawks and the Sparrows, Le Streghe, Teorema, Arabian Nights) and served as musical advisor or curator for the other three (The Canterbury Tales, The Decameron, Salo), writing next to nothing original for them and mostly adapting existing pieces. [C31910] For Salo Morricone wrote one original four-minute piece and conducted one existing piece, with Arnaldo Graziosi playing the piano; Morricone never saw the full film until its first theatrical viewing in Rome and, as a Roman Catholic, was shocked by it, while remaining grateful that Pasolini had shown him everything but the explicit scenes in the editing room. [C31911] Pasolini began every collaboration with a composer by handing over a written list of requested pieces of music and explaining concretely what he expected; Morricone accepted everything offered early in his career and grew more vocal later, but never declined an offer from Pasolini. [C31912] Morricone called Pasolini by far the humblest, most respectful and friendliest person he collaborated with in his career. [C31913]

The collaboration is the inverse of the Leone one. Leone shot to music Morricone wrote first; Pasolini handed Morricone a list of music that already existed and asked him to arrange, adapt, or fill the gaps. The composer who invented the Dollars sound spent nine years with Pasolini mostly choosing other people's music -- and called him the friendliest collaborator he ever had. [C31914]

**Takeaway:** The Morricone tie is the study's doorway for a reason: it shows two total systems colliding without either one yielding. Morricone's system says the film needs a sound only he can invent; Pasolini's says the film needs the sound the world already has. The seven films are the record of that argument, and the argument ends with Morricone writing four minutes for Salo and being shocked by the film he helped score.

## 15. Theatre and the Corriere della Sera years (1968-1975)

Pasolini's theatre works are listed as Orgia (1968), Porcile (1968), Calderon (1973), Affabulazione (1977), Pilade (1977) and Bestia da stile (1977), the last three published or staged after his death. [C31915] Orgia premiered in Turin in 1968, reportedly not very successfully, and the 1968 stage production is listed with film score by Ennio Morricone in the Istituto Italiano di Cultura record of its 73-minute audio recording, the music remaining unreleased. [C31916] Pasolini began writing for Corriere della Sera in 1973, and his collection of those writings, Scritti corsari, was published by Garzanti in 1975 (first edition May 1975). [C31917] His column "Che cos'e questo golpe?" ("What Is This Coup?"), known as "Io so" ("I know"), was published in Corriere della Sera on 14 November 1974, naming those responsible for the Milan massacre of 12 December 1969 and the Brescia and Bologna massacres of early 1974, and adding "I know. But I don't have any evidence." [C31918] After the Battle of Valle Giulia in 1968 Pasolini wrote the poem "Il PCI ai giovani", siding with the policemen as children of the poor against students he called daddy's boys; over his life he faced up to 33 lawsuits, on charges including obscenity and contempt of religion, and was always eventually acquitted. [C31919]

The columns are the last form of the same work: the poet who learned Friulan to write about peasants now writes in Italy's biggest newspaper that he knows who planted the bombs and has no evidence, and the poem about Valle Giulia sides with the police because the police are the children of the poor and the students are daddy's boys. He is attacked from both sides, again, and he prefers it that way. [C31920]

**Takeaway:** The late prose is the films without images: the same accusations, the same refusal to pick a side that will have him, the same naming of names without evidence. Petrolio will be the novel version, and it will be left unfinished for the same reason the columns stop: he is killed mid-sentence.

## 16. Salo (1975): the film as a machine

Salo is Pasolini's final film, a loose adaptation of the Marquis de Sade's 1785 novel The 120 Days of Sodom (first published in 1904), reset in Italy under the fascist Italian Social Republic. [C31921] It was directed and co-written by Pasolini, with a screenplay by Pasolini and Sergio Citti and uncredited work by Pupi Avati, produced by Alberto Grimaldi, shot by Tonino Delli Colli, scored by Ennio Morricone, and runs 116 minutes. [C31922] Four wealthy libertines -- the Duke, the Bishop, the Magistrate and the President -- agree to marry each other's daughters, then kidnap nine teenage boys and nine teenage girls and hold them for four months in a villa near Marzabotto. [C31923] The film is built in four segments after Dante's Divine Comedy: the Anteinferno, the Circle of Manias, the Circle of Shit, and the Circle of Blood. [C31924] Four middle-aged prostitutes act as narrators, telling stories that set each circle in motion: Signora Vaccari in the Circle of Manias, Signora Maggi in the Circle of Shit, Signora Castelli in the Circle of Blood, while a pianist plays through the days. [C31925] The villa runs on posted rules: the daughters serve naked as servants, the victims almost never speak, and at the end a blue ribbon marks those sentenced to death, while informers -- Graziella, who betrays Eva and Antiniska, and Rino, submissive to the Duke -- are spared. [C31926] Pasolini's stated reading was that the sex is a metaphor for the relation between power and its subjects, the body treated as a commodity and the personality of the Other annulled -- an "anarchy of power" in which sexual acts and abuse stand in for domination. [C31927] The libertines are written as highly educated men who quote Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality, Pound's Cantos and Proust, and the film draws on Barthes, Klossowski and Blanchot on Sade, while the set is dressed with paintings by Severini, Duchamp and Feininger. [C31928] Salo was the first film of a planned Trilogy of Death; the second was to be a biopic of the child murderer Gilles de Rais, never made because Pasolini was murdered before Salo's release. [C31929] The machine is the point: a closed room, a set of spoken rules, narrators who read the next transgression aloud, and each circle escalating the same rules one notch, so the system is watchable as a machine that tightens itself -- and survival is bought by informing on others, the betrayal read aloud in front of everyone, so a private betrayal becomes a public transaction. [C31930]

Shooting took place mainly in spring 1975 at the 16th-century Villa Gonzaga-Zani in Villimpenta, with interiors at Villa Sorra near Castelfranco Emilia, exteriors at Villa Aldini above Bologna, the noble hall and courtyard at Cinecitta, and other scenes at the Grand Hotel Villa Feltrinelli in Gargnano. [C31931] During production some reels were stolen and a ransom was demanded; the scenes were reshot with doubles from a different angle, and at the trial for Pasolini's murder it was hypothesized that he was lured to Ostia Lido by news the reels had been found -- a theory for which there is no concrete evidence. [C31932] Most of the cast were non-professionals, many of them models cast for willingness to appear naked, with nearly 40 actors on set at a time and enormous amounts of footage shot; Ninetto Davoli was first chosen to play the collaborator Claudio but declined over legal problems and was replaced by Claudio Troccoli, Franco Citti was to play one of the studs but did not appear, and Laura Betti was to play Signora Vaccari but declined over legal problems and Novecento commitments. [C31933] Like most Italian films of the time, Salo was shot MOS (without direct sound) and dubbed in post-production; the four masters refused to return to loop their lines and were re-voiced by other actors -- Giancarlo Vigorelli as the Duke, Giorgio Caproni as the Bishop, Aurelio Roncaglia as the Magistrate, Marco Bellocchio as the President -- and Helene Surgere's Vaccari was dubbed by Laura Betti. [C31934] The score mixes Morricone originals played at the piano by Arnaldo Graziosi with Orff's Carmina Burana ("Veris leta facies") and Chopin (Preludes Op. 28 nos. 17 and 4; Valse Op. 34 no. 2). [C31935] Pasolini shot four different endings and chose the "Margherita" ending: two young blackshirts, Bruno and Claudio, dancing a waltz as the tortures continue, Claudio asking the name of Bruno's girlfriend back home. [C31936] The shoot doubled as a reconciliation with Bertolucci: the Salo and Novecento crews played football while Novecento was filmed nearby, Bertolucci coaching from the bench and Pasolini playing on the field; the Salo crew lost 5-2 and Pasolini left before the end shouting at Bertolucci. [C31937]

**Takeaway:** Salo is the same machine as the Gospel with the sacred removed. The tableau, the rules, the narrators, the dubbed voices all remain, but what they frame is power consuming bodies instead of grace touching them. The waltz at the end is the final mechanism: the torturers dance, the torture continues off-screen, and the film refuses to let you look away or to let you watch.

## 17. Salo: release, bans, reception

Salo premiered at the Paris Film Festival on Sunday 23 November 1975, three weeks after Pasolini's murder. [C31938] In Italy the film was first rejected by the censors, approved on 23 December 1975, released on Saturday 10 January 1976, and formally banned in January 1976 after the approval was withdrawn; producer Alberto Grimaldi faced obscenity charges for offending Italians' "sense of modesty". [C31939] In the United States it received a limited release on Monday 3 October 1977 through Zebra Releasing without an MPAA rating, because a submission would have guaranteed an X. [C31940] The British Board of Film Censors rejected it in January 1976; it was first shown uncut at the Old Compton Street Cinema Club in Soho in 1977, the premises were raided by police after a few days, and it was finally passed uncut for theatrical and video distribution in 2000. [C31941] Australia banned it in 1976, passed it uncut R18+ in July 1993 after a 17-year ban, banned it again in February 1998, rejected it again in 2008, and passed it R18+ in 2010 only as a DVD carrying 176 minutes of additional contextual material. [C31942] New Zealand banned it in 1976, upheld the ban in 1993, allowed an uncut festival screening in 1997, and passed the DVD uncut with an R18 rating in 2001. [C31943] In Cincinnati in 1994 an undercover policeman rented the film from a gay bookstore and the owners were arrested for "pandering"; artists including Martin Scorsese and Alec Baldwin signed a brief on its artistic merit, and the Ohio court dismissed the case on Fourth Amendment grounds without ruling on obscenity. [C31944] Critical standing: Rotten Tomatoes reports 74% approval from 42 reviews; the Chicago Film Critics Association named it the 65th-scariest film ever made (2006); a 2000 Village Voice critics poll named it the 89th-greatest film of the 20th century; the Toronto International Film Festival placed it 47th on its Essential 100 list (2010). [C31945] The Criterion Collection released Salo on LaserDisc in 1993 and DVD in 1998, and issued a newly restored Blu-ray and DVD in 2011. [C31946] Its admirers include Michael Haneke, who named it his fourth-favorite film in the 2002 Sight and Sound poll, Rainer Werner Fassbinder, who listed it among his ten favorites, and John Waters, who called it "a beautiful film" about "the pornography of power"; Roland Barthes defended it because it "refuses to allow us to redeem ourselves". [C31947] In May 2006 Time Out's Film Guide named Salo the "Most Controversial Film" of all time. [C31948]

**Takeaway:** The ban history is the film's second release: banned in Italy within weeks, banned in Australia three times, raided in Soho, arrested in Cincinnati, and then, over thirty years, passed uncut everywhere with contextual material attached. The censors kept proving the film's thesis -- that power's first act is to decide what bodies may be shown -- and the film kept outliving them.

## 18. Petrolio: the unfinished denunciation

In 1970 Pasolini bought an old castle near Viterbo and began his last novel, Petrolio, writing it between 1972 and 1975; it was left incomplete at his death. [C31949] Petrolio was published posthumously in 1992 by Einaudi, edited by Maria Careri and Graziella Chiarcossi with a philological note by Aurelio Roncaglia; a fourth edition of 828 pages, edited by Careri and Walter Siti, appeared with Garzanti in 2022. [C31950] The English translation by Ann Goldstein was published by Knopf (Pantheon) in 1997. [C31951] The novel is a fragmentary novel-documentary built from notes (Appunti), some extended polished passages and others cryptic messages from the author to himself; it denounces obscure dealings at the top of government and the corporate world, naming Eni, the CIA and the Mafia. [C31952] At its center is Carlo, an oil executive who splits into two selves: Carlo 1, a super-Machiavellian power monger, and Carlo 2, who lives only to satisfy perverse and insatiable sexual desires. [C31953] Pasolini told Alberto Moravia he hoped Petrolio would eventually appear only in a limited edition, and the book was long held back by his family before its 1992 publication. [C31954]

**Takeaway:** Petrolio is the same machine in prose: a split man, a pile of notes, and a denunciation that never becomes a finished novel because the author was killed mid-sentence. The unfinished state is not an accident of the murder; it is the form the book was always going to take -- notes toward a truth too dangerous to finish.

## 19. The murder at Ostia

Pasolini was murdered on Sunday 2 November 1975 at a beach in Ostia, aged 53; he was savagely beaten, run over several times with his own car, left with multiple broken bones and his testicles crushed by what appeared to have been a metal bar, and his body was partially burned with petrol after death; he was buried in the cemetery of Casarsa della Delizia. [C31955] In the early hours of that morning a Carabinieri patrol stopped his speeding grey Alfa Romeo; inside was 17-year-old Giuseppe "Pino" Pelosi, a hustler known as "Pino the Frog", who had been released from prison for auto theft only two months earlier; he was caught driving Pasolini's car. [C31956] Pelosi confessed to the murder the same day; he was convicted of voluntary homicide on Monday 26 April 1976 and sentenced to nine years, seven months and ten days; the appeal court named him the sole person responsible, and the Court of Cassation confirmed the conviction on Thursday 26 April 1979. [C31957] The first verdict had included the phrase "unknown others", which was later removed from the verdict. [C31958] Twenty-nine years later, on Saturday 7 May 2005, Pelosi retracted his confession on Rai 3, saying it had been made under threat of violence to his family, and claimed three men "with a southern accent" had committed the murder while shouting "dirty communist" at Pasolini. [C31959] His friend Sergio Citti testified that reels of Salo had been stolen and that Pasolini planned to meet the thieves to negotiate their return on 2 November 1975, after a visit to Stockholm; Citti's inquiries turned up a bloody wooden stick and an eyewitness who said he saw a group of men pull Pasolini from the car. [C31960] The Rome police reopened the murder as a cold case after the retraction, but the investigating magistrates found the new elements insufficient to justify a continued inquiry, so no other person has ever been convicted and the murder is still described as unsolved; speculation linking it to the Propaganda Due (P2) masonic lodge emerged in the 1990s after Pasolini's writings were found to link P2 member Eugenio Cefis with violent crimes, and as of 2023 a plea to reopen the case rested on DNA analysis and links to the Banda della Magliana, a criminal organisation tied to P2 and far-right terrorism. [C31961] Pelosi died in Rome in July 2017, still the only person ever convicted of the murder. [C31962] Ninetto Davoli had dined with Pasolini on the night of the murder and went to Ostia the next day, on behalf of the family, to identify the battered body. [C31963]

The sentence length is flagged: LA Weekly (2005) says eight years, English Wikipedia says nine years in prison in 1976, and the detailed account used here says nine years, seven months and ten days. The study uses the detailed figure and does not settle the conflict. [C31964] A November 2026 report that the Rome prosecutor opened a new file on the murder on 2 November 2026, the 51st anniversary, rests on a single low-authority source and is not used as a claim. [C31965]

**Takeaway:** The murder is the last Pasolini text, and it reads like his work: a poor boy, a car, a beach at night, a body treated as a commodity, and a verdict that names one man and then removes the words "unknown others". Whether Pelosi acted alone or not, the case stays officially unsolved, and the unsolved state is itself the point -- the Italy he wrote about in "Io so" is the Italy that killed him and never explained itself.

## 20. The network

Bernardo Bertolucci, born Sunday 16 March 1941 in Parma and died Monday 26 November 2018 in Rome, began as Pasolini's protege: his father Attilio had helped Pasolini publish his first novel, and Pasolini hired the younger Bertolucci as his first assistant on Accattone (1961). [C31966] Bertolucci's directorial debut, La commare secca (1962), used a screenplay by Pasolini co-written with Sergio Citti; he went on to The Conformist, Last Tango in Paris (1972), Novecento (1976) and The Last Emperor. [C31967] Pasolini criticised Last Tango in Paris and failed to defend it against drastic censorship, which soured the two men until their reconciliation on the Salo and Novecento football pitch in spring 1975. [C31968] Sergio Citti, born Tuesday 30 May 1933 in Rome and died Tuesday 11 October 2005 in Rome of a heart attack, met Pasolini at 18 as a working-class house painter and supplied the Roman dialect for Ragazzi di vita; he co-wrote Accattone, Mamma Roma and Salo with Pasolini and was assistant director and dialogue collaborator on Mamma Roma. [C31969] Sergio Citti directed his debut feature Ostia (1970), released Wednesday 11 March 1970, from a screenplay co-written with Pasolini under Pasolini's supervision; he directed about a dozen films in all and was called the only legitimate heir of Pasolini's cinematic style. **Takeaway:** Nobody in the network is hired; everybody is found. ## 21. Aesthetic logic: what makes a Pasolini image a Pasolini image

The sacred tableau: Pasolini said his camera moves over his subjects as if moving over a painting, thinking of the frescoes of Masaccio and Giotto; the BFI's markers are frontal medium close-ups held on faces, and ancient southern Italian landscapes standing in for biblical lands. [C31980] The non-actor face and the long take: faces are chosen as a painter chooses models, non-professionals are filmed in long takes while professionals get shorter scenes so they cannot showboat, and Jesus in the Gospel is played by Enrique Irazoqui, a Spanish student picked for his resemblance to El Greco's Christ. [C31981] The archaic/modern collision and the dubbed voice: Bach sounds over a pimp's story in Accattone and sacred music over profane subjects in the Gospel, while Salo sets an 18th-century Sade text in 1944 with Nietzsche, Pound and Proust quoted aloud; the films are shot MOS and post-synched, Pasolini building his characters through editing and dubbing, so the voice does not belong to the face -- Irazoqui is voiced by Enrico Maria Salerno, and the four masters of Salo are re-voiced by other actors. [C31982] The rule-board circle: a closed room, a set of spoken rules, and a narrator who reads the next transgression aloud, each circle escalating the same rules one notch, so the system is watchable as a machine that tightens itself. [C31983] The betrayal ledger: survival is bought by informing on others, and the ledger is read aloud in front of everyone, so a private betrayal becomes a public transaction. [C31984] The nested-tale engine: a frame story opens into tales told by characters inside it, and each told tale changes the teller's fate, so the story tree itself is the plot. [C31985] The sung credits: the film's credits are sung by a voice that names each maker with ironic commentary, so the credit roll itself becomes a song that summarises the whole work. [C31986] Found-music collage as moral geography: instead of an original score, pre-existing music is assigned by place and class, so Neapolitan folk songs, Latin chants, Romanian folk tunes or Tibetan chants mark which world a scene belongs to. [C31987]

The synthesis is a collision held still. A poor face, front-on and held too long, is treated as a sacred painting; an old text or old music is dropped into a modern slum or a fascist villa without updating it; and the voice is detached from the mouth, so the figure on screen is a body borrowed by a text. The system is not realism and not stylization: it is a document of a body plus a quotation laid over it. [C31988]

**Takeaway:** Every Pasolini mechanism is the same move in a different costume: take something despised -- a dialect, a slum, a pimp, a folk song, a boy from the borgate -- and frame it as sacred, without cleaning it up. The sacred is not added to the poor; it is revealed in them by the act of looking long enough, frontally, and in silence.
