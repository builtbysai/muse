# Rene Carcan: Study Notes

**Site:** Rene Marcel Carcan, born 25 June 1925 at Saint-Josse-ten-Noode, Brussels, died 19 January 1993 at Etterbeek -- Belgian printmaker and watercolourist, also a painter, sculptor, jewellery designer and book illustrator. He studied sculpture under Gustave Jacobs and painting at the Academy of Brussels 1941-1946 under Jacques Maes and Leon Devos, spent 1958-1959 in Italy on a UNESCO grant, and worked in Johnny Friedlander's Paris studio 1960-1965, where he learned colour etching. He co-founded Cap d'Encre in 1963 with Pierre Alechinsky, Gabriel Belgeonne, Francis De Bolle, Lismonde, Marc Laffineur and Gustave Marchoul, and his two catalogues raisonnes hold slightly over 800 prints. The Fondation Rene Carcan was created in his studio in 1990, became the Espace Rene Carcan in 2006, and in 2013 created the Rene Carcan International Prize for Printmaking, first awarded in January 2014 (C4501, C4502, C4503, C4504, C4506, C4508, C4509, C4511, C4514, C4516). The birth day is disputed -- 25 February per the Fondation biography against 25 May per English and German Wikipedia and Annex -- and 25 June is used here as best-supported, on the Nouvelle Biographie Nationale entry read end to end; the civil birth record was not consulted, so the day is not proven (C4511, C4510, C4501, C4632).

## The life: the pupil who became a founder

Carcan is the third man in the chain to arrive by a different road, and the road is the point. Belgeonne abandoned painting for engraving and subtracted toward emptiness; Marchoul learned engraving alone and accumulated techniques for sixty years. Carcan began as a sculptor, then a painter, and only turned to printmaking in his mid-thirties, at the instigation of Philippe Roberts-Jones -- who saw monotypes Carcan had made on the glass used for printing, transposing the angular constructions of his sculpture, and oriented him toward engraving (C4512). The UNESCO grant to Italy in 1958-1959 gave him the Etruscan funerary frescoes, their chromatics and dancing rhythm, and Tuscany and Etruria became a main source of inspiration (C4503). Then the decisive five years: 1960-1965 in Johnny Friedlander's Paris studio, learning colour etching, years the Nouvelle Biographie Nationale calls decisive for his formation (C4512, C4503). He was already exhibiting in Paris from 1962 (C4514); in 1963 he co-founded Cap d'Encre (C4504); in 1964 he installed an atelier in Brussels, rue de l'Automne, and took part in the group's collective volume, which gave pride of place to the engraving/poetry relationship (C4512). The group produced five Portfolios up to 1970 (C4504).

The break came in 1968. Lys des mers, described by the Nouvelle Biographie Nationale as synthesising the efforts of the first period and opening fifteen years of masterpieces, was followed by a press collapsing on his left leg; a long convalescence followed in a modest pavilion in the Brabant countryside, and the episode sealed a break with an abstraction that had never been coldly intellectual for him (C4513). Late in the 1960s he bought a small farmhouse in Huldenberg, Flemish Brabant, and the work turned toward dreamlike, imagined landscapes, half Tuscan, half Breughelian, where the tree becomes a powerful king courted by his vassals, the sunflowers and thistles (C4505). The 1970s were his most prolific decade, exhibiting worldwide -- the United States, Canada, Australia, South Korea, Germany, Norway, Sweden, the Netherlands, Italy, France and Luxembourg (C4507) -- and in the early 1980s he "literally conquered West Germany", holding several successful exhibitions in more than twenty of the main cities (C4507). In 1977 he opened a new atelier at rue Champ du Roi, in a house belonging to his old sculpture teacher Gustave Jacobs, bought out of fidelity (C4513). The last years are those of the Fondation Rene Carcan pour la promotion de la gravure: the non-profit was created in his studio on 1 January 1990, its exhibition space welcoming the biggest names in printmaking of the 20th century, and Friedlander himself opened it in person on 20 October 1989 -- a date that precedes the stated creation by over two months, unresolved (C4508). He died on 19 January 1993, aged 67, his German gallery owner Ricardo De Bernardi becoming sole legatee (C4509, C4511). The association ceased activities in 2006 and became the Espace Rene Carcan (C4509); in 2013, twenty years after his death, it created the international prize that carries his name (C4509, C4516). He had no heirs, and the Fondation was his answer to that (C4508).

## M1 -- Superimposed copper plates: one plate per colour

The first mechanism is the one the sources name outright, and it is the load-bearing technical fact of this study. Andre Sempoux, in the Nouvelle Biographie Nationale: "La superposition des plaques de cuivre est, chez lui, presque aussi philosophique qu'esthetique" -- the superposition of copper plates is, in him, almost as philosophical as it is aesthetic (C4513). Friedlander taught Carcan "the techniques of colour etching" (C4503), and in Carcan's hands that means printing in colour from multiple plates, one plate per colour, printed in register in superposition. The catalogue confirms the practice by its uniformity: 19 of the 20 works are "etching and aquatint", only the two earliest (W01, W02, 1961-1962) are etching alone (C4555). The philosophical half of Sempoux's sentence is the point for a piece: the colours are not mixed on the plate, they are laid one over another, each plate a separate decision, and the finished print is the sum of decisions that can be counted. A piece built on M1 shows its colours arriving plate by plate, each one a transparent layer that changes everything under it, and the viewer can count the plates.

**Takeaway:** print colour as stacked decisions. One plate per colour, each laid over the last, so the finished image is a sum the viewer can count -- and the count is the philosophy.

## M2 -- Structure and feeling: etching draws, aquatint feels

The second mechanism is the division of labour inside the plate, stated by Sempoux in a single sentence: "L'eau-forte impose la structure, l'aquatinte sert au paysage du sentiment" -- etching imposes the structure, aquatint serves the landscape of feeling (C4513). The mechanism is mechanical: the etched needle line bites the copper and holds the ink in grooves, so it draws architecture -- the tree trunk, the pier, the pyramid, the diamond; the aquatint grain, rosin dust melted onto the plate and bitten around, prints as broad areas of tone, so it carries the sky, the water, the light, the feeling. The catalogue shows the split in action: early aquatint is used tonally, a grey grain field under black wedges (W03, La Danse, 1965), and from W04 onward it carries flat colour fields -- the sun disc, the sky, the ground (C4555). The Fondation biography adds that he was able to engrave directly onto the copper plate (C4506): the structure is drawn by hand, not transferred. The mechanism is a clean rule for a piece: line does the drawing, grain does the weather, and the two never swap jobs.

**Takeaway:** give line the structure and grain the feeling, and never let them swap. The tree is etched; the sky is aquatint; the print is the argument between them.

## M3 -- The sun-disc emblem: one sun, eleven times

The third mechanism is the motif that never leaves: the sun as a flat concentric disc in orange and red with a yellow halo, appearing as a disc, ring, burst or crescent-moon counterpart in 11 of the 20 catalogue works -- W04, W06, W09, W10, W11, W14, W15, W17, W18, W19, W20 (C4552). It arrives with the colour: W04 Estacade (1968) is visibly a large yellow-orange sun disc with a red scribbled centre over grey-green water and dark piers, even though the site files it under "Black & White period" (C4555). It is most emblematic in W10 Le feu (1975), one of the four Elements: a concentric orange-red sun disc held in a blue chalice-like tree form, grid lines, three sunflower discs at left, a small red cube with flowers at right (C4552). And it is most distilled at the end: W20 Les bretelles du ciel (1992), the final-year work, is a huge veined sun disc inside a diamond, two dark hanging straps with yellow dots, a perspective grid floor with dotted flowers below (C4559). The sun is the through-line of the whole career -- the celestial torch that polarises the trees and flowers (C4513) -- and its late form is geometry, not weather: a disc inside a diamond on a grid. The biography's palette sentence confirms what the images show: sun-warmed reds and oranges, sometimes sprayed with a spurt of lemon yellow, water greens, azure blues, the earth tones of Sienna, warm colours to glorify a fruitful nature bathed in spring or summer light (C4505, C4568).

**Takeaway:** keep one sun and let it harden into geometry. A disc in 1968, an emblem in 1975, a veined disc in a diamond in 1992 -- the sun is the career, and the career ends when the sun becomes a diagram.

## M4 -- Solar botany: the tree is king, man is timid

The fourth mechanism is the subject that arrives with Huldenberg and never leaves: a dreamlike nature in which the tree is the sovereign. The Fondation biography gives the image in full: dreamlike, imagined landscapes, half Tuscan, half Breughelian, where the tree becomes a powerful king courted by his vassals, the sunflowers and thistles (C4505). The Nouvelle Biographie Nationale describes the same solar botany in the 1970s work: shimmering effects, trees and flowers polarised by the celestial torch, willow branches gnawed by rain, landscapes organised by planes, orange reflections invading the sky -- Marecage (1969), mineralised vegetation and craquelure (C4513). The catalogue shows the court in session: W06 La pierre bleue (1970), a thistle-like plant in flower with an orange dotted sun and a dark blue stone; W14 L'arbre de vie (1979), a Tuscan landscape of cypresses with a central broad tree and a red-and-yellow ringed sun, the whole upper half mirrored in water below; W15 Les feuilles volantes (1980), a close-up of large veined leaves with a small orange sun (C4556, C4558). And the counterweight is the man: he appears only timidly, a single tiny nude figure at the right of W17 Soleil et verticalite vegetale (1982), exactly as the biography describes (C4567). The mechanism is a hierarchy -- tree, sun, flower, water, and man last and smallest -- and a piece built on it keeps the hierarchy: the tree is the largest thing in the frame, the sun the brightest, the man a dot that the viewer has to find.

**Takeaway:** crown the tree and shrink the man. The tree is king, the sunflowers and thistles are the court, and a human appears once, tiny, at the edge -- the hierarchy is the picture.

## M5 -- Serial variation: one vocabulary, six series

The fifth mechanism is the way the fixed vocabulary is deployed: not as single pictures but as series, each one a set of variations on one rule. The catalogue names six: the Elements -- L'air, L'eau, La terre, Le feu, all 1975 (C4552); the Creation series -- eight long-titled works of 1976 beginning "Que...", W12 Que les gaz restes a l'exterieur... among them (C4552); Mes provinces -- eight Belgian-province landscapes of 1977, W13 La Meuse en Ardenne among them (C4552, C4558); the Seasons -- Printemps, Ete, Automne, 1980, with no Hiver in the online catalogue though an auction "Hiver" circa 1982 exists (C4552, C4562); Les jardins d'Huldenberg -- a sun version and a moon version, both 1983, W18 the moon (C4552); the Hommages -- Breughel de Velours, Odilon Redon, James Ensor, Klimt, all 1984 (C4552). The series do the work the single print cannot: the Elements say the vocabulary is a cosmology, the Creation series says it is a genesis, Mes provinces says it is a country, the Seasons say it is a year, the Jardins say it is a garden with a sun and a moon, the Hommages say it is a lineage. The middle career, 1974-1977, is where the vocabulary is fixed -- farmhouse, tree, sun, flower, water -- and arranged as emblems and series (C4557). A piece built on M5 deals its sheets one at a time from a series rule, and the rule is visible in the dealing.

**Takeaway:** deal the vocabulary as a series, not a picture. One rule per series -- four elements, eight provinces, a sun and a moon -- and the set is the statement the single sheet cannot make.

## M6 -- Dotted signals: stars, seeds, signals

The sixth mechanism is the smallest and the most useful for a piece: small saturated dots -- red, blue, yellow -- scattered as stars, seeds or signals, present from W07 through W20 and dominant in the late work (C4552, image evidence). They appear first as flower discs: W07 Incantation II (1972) has small red and blue flower discs in a green netted foreground with an orange band at the foot. They return as seals: W12 (1976) carries four red seal-like discs along the foot. They become a field: W18 Les jardins d'Huldenberg (La lune) (1983) sets dotted stars over a green night ground, and W20 (1992) hangs yellow dots on the dark straps and scatters dotted flowers across the perspective grid floor. No text states the dots' meaning, and none is invented here -- they are observed in the images, not stated in any source, and the study records them as image evidence. Their function is visible anyway: they are the smallest unit of colour in the print, the point at which colour stops being a field and becomes a mark, and in the late work they carry the whole sky. A piece built on M6 works at the dot scale: the image is built from points, and the points are the only things that move.

**Takeaway:** let the dots do the sky. One saturated point at a time, red then blue then yellow, until the field is a signal -- the smallest colour in the print is the one that travels furthest.

## M7 -- Mirrored and symmetrical composition: the sheet as a diagram

The seventh mechanism is the late compositional turn: the landscape stops being a view and becomes a diagram. W14 L'arbre de vie (1979) mirrors its whole upper half in water below -- cypresses, broad tree, ringed sun, all doubled. W19 Le sacre de la vie (1984) is a triptych, three upright panels inscribed Dynamisme, Equilibre, Perspective under blue skies with red suns, ochre grounds, cypresses and thistles -- the widest work in the catalogue at 30 x 96 cm. W20 (1992) builds on a perspective grid floor and a diamond, the veined sun disc centred inside the diamond, the two dark straps hanging symmetrically. The late career, 1979-1992, is the same vocabulary made more distilled and more geometric: symmetrical, mirrored, tripartite, or built on a grid and a diamond (C4559). Against the early abstraction -- interlocking blocks and wedges with no legible landscape (W01-W03) -- the late symmetry is the opposite move: the image is not freed from structure, it is locked into it, and the locking is the meaning. The triptych's three inscribed words are the only place in the catalogue where the print tells the viewer its own rule, and the rule is a balance: dynamism, equilibrium, perspective.

**Takeaway:** lock the late image into symmetry. Mirror it in water, split it into three panels, centre it in a diamond -- the diagram is the destination the landscape was walking toward.

## M8 -- Direct engraving and the monotype origin: the hand on the copper

The eighth mechanism is the origin story, and it is also a boundary: what he did, and what must not be claimed for him. He was able to engrave directly onto the copper plate (C4506) -- the structure drawn by hand into the metal, no transfer, no intermediary. And the turn to printmaking itself came from monotypes on glass: in Italy he transposed onto the glass used for printing the angular constructions he had reached in sculpture, and those monotypes convinced Philippe Roberts-Jones to orient him toward engraving (C4512). A 1990 monograph is titled "Rene Carcan, Monotypes, dessins, gravures, bijoux et sculptures" (C4513). So the hand on the plate has a prehistory in sculpture and in glass: angular constructions first, then the needle. The boundary is equally firm. Burin is not evidenced for Carcan in any source read end to end -- the Fondation biography says only that he could engrave directly onto copper, which could mean burin or drypoint, and the one listing that calls him a figure of "gravure au burin et a l'eau-forte" is demonstrably unreliable (it dates him 1920-1993) (C4519). Drypoint has no evidence at all (C4519). Lithography is weakly evidenced only: German and English Wikipedia claim it, and a single titled lithograph was found, L'Etang, 1974, edition 32/95 (C4515, C4563). The catalogue is 111 works of etching and aquatint against one lithograph. A piece built on M8 shows the hand and the plate, and it does not borrow a tool he never used.

**Takeaway:** draw straight onto the copper and let the sculpture show through. The monotype on glass is the origin, the needle is the method -- and a tool with no evidence stays out of the piece.

## The aesthetic logic: one technique, one sun, one tree

Carcan's logic is the opposite of Marchoul's and the mirror of Belgeonne's, and the three together are the chain. Marchoul mastered a technique per decade and let each set the look; Belgeonne kept one technique and subtracted toward emptiness. Carcan kept one technique -- etching and aquatint, 1965 to 1992, with only the two earliest works in etching alone -- and changed everything inside it: black-and-white abstraction to sun-warmed colour, wedges and blocks to farmhouses and trees, a single sheet to series, a view to a diagram. The variable that moves is not the tool but the sun: it arrives in 1968, becomes an emblem in 1975, a king's companion in 1979, and a veined disc in a diamond in 1992. The constant is the hierarchy -- tree, sun, flower, water, man last and smallest -- and the constant behind the constant is the plate: one plate per colour, structure in the etched line, feeling in the aquatint grain, the hand directly on the copper. The career reads as a single sentence with the sun as its grammar: sculpture taught him the construction, Italy taught him the colour, Friedlander taught him the plates, the press accident taught him the landscape, and the Fondation taught him the ending -- no heirs, so the work itself had to become the heir, and the prize is the heir still working, biennial, twenty years after his death and now in its seventh edition (C4517).

## Future candidates

Top pick for #613 is Johnny Friedlander (1912-1992) -- the tightest hop in the chain: one step further up the same teaching line, from the pupil to the master who taught him colour etching in Paris 1960-1965, who then opened Carcan's foundation in person in 1989.
