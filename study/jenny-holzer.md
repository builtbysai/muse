Jenny Holzer was born on 29 July 1950 in Gallipolis, Ohio. [C28815] She took general art courses at Duke University 1968-70, then studied painting, printmaking and drawing at the University of Chicago, took her BFA at Ohio University in 1972, did summer courses at the Rhode Island School of Design in 1974, entered its MFA programme in 1975 and finished in 1977. She moved to Manhattan in 1976 and joined the Whitney Museum Independent Study Program, where she began her first work with language, installation and public art. Early in her career she worked as a typesetter for Laundry News, a laundromat-industry trade newspaper, to pay the bills, and that work -- making text correct, complete and pretty, and fitted -- influenced her practice; she still compares typesetting to programming. She was an active member of Colab from 1977 to around 1981, taking part in The Times Square Show and other Colab projects. In the 1970s she abandoned abstract painting to make more explicit statements and reach a larger audience than gallery visitors. [C28815]

Her first works with language were the Truisms (1977-79): over 250 short declarative sentences, condensed from the Whitney reading list's complex theoretical texts into brief statements, printed as anonymous broadsheets in black italic capitals on white paper and wheat-pasted to buildings, walls and fences in and around Manhattan. The sentences sound like familiar aphorisms while contradicting one another -- ABUSE OF POWER COMES AS NO SURPRISE, PROTECT ME FROM WHAT I WANT, PRIVATE PROPERTY CREATED CRIME -- and she did not necessarily stand behind all of them: "It was more accurate to offer a wealth of opinion." The Inflammatory Essays (1979-82) were longer and angrier, 100 words in 20 lines on coloured paper, drawing on political, utopian, religious and other manifestos -- Emma Goldman, Lenin, Mao -- pasted around the same streets, a paragraph where the Truisms were a slogan. The Living series began in 1981, printed on aluminium and bronze plaques in the presentation format of medical and government buildings, addressing the necessities of daily life -- eating, breathing, sleeping, human relations -- with paintings by Peter Nadin attached to metal posts alongside. In 1982 the medium of modern computer systems entered her work: she installed her first large electronic sign on the Spectacolor board in Times Square, sponsored by the Public Art Fund, Truisms appearing for seconds at a time between advertisements. The Survival series (1983-85) was the first series written specifically for electronic signs, short pointed urgent instruction legible at a glance. She began working with stone in 1986, and for her exhibition that year at the Barbara Gladstone Gallery she introduced a total environment: a horizontal LED sign and stone benches leading up to an electronic altar. [C28816]

In 1989 she made Laments for the Dia Art Foundation: columns of coloured light and carved marble and granite tops, giving a voice in the first person to thirteen dead individuals -- women, men, children and an infant -- on motherhood, violation, pain, torture and death, with hidden optimism alongside the darkness, at the height of the AIDS epidemic. In December 1989 she installed a 163-metre-long LED sign winding along the parapet of the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum rotunda, a continuous ribbon of scrolling text spiralling up the building, carrying selections from Truisms, Inflammatory Essays, Living, Survival, Under a Rock, Laments and Child Text, with stone benches below holding the same words fixed. In 1990 she became the second female artist chosen to represent the United States at the Venice Biennale -- Diane Arbus was first, shown posthumously in 1972 -- and at the 44th Biennale her LED signboards and marble benches occupied the American Pavilion, with Mother and Child among the texts and posters, hats and T-shirts sold in the streets of Venice; the installation won the Leone d'Oro for best pavilion. After a break she returned in 1993 with Lustmord, from the German for "sex murder", a response to the Bosnian War and the widespread rape and murder of women: three poems retelling sex crimes from the perspectives of victim, observer and perpetrator, in forms ranging from texts written in blue, black and red ink on skin to the Lustmord Table, bones of the body laid on a wooden table with silver bands engraved with the three poems. Text-based light projections have been central since 1996, beginning with Arno on the Arno River in Florence, and running through Xenon for Rio de Janeiro (1999), Xenon for Bordeaux and Paris including the Louvre Pyramid (2001, repeated 2009), For the City (2005) and For the Capitol (2007). [C28817]

She stopped writing her own text in 2001, finding she could not say enough adequately, and went to the texts of others with pleasure -- poems by Wislawa Szymborska, Yehuda Amichai, Henri Cole, Mahmoud Darwish and others, stenographic records of what political parties said at the Reichstag, and since about 2010 declassified government documents concerning Iraq and the Middle East, including excerpts from minutes of interrogations of American soldiers accused of human rights violations at Abu Ghraib. The Redaction Paintings (2008) reproduce declassified memos with much of the text blacked out by censors, the black bars becoming the composition, sometimes with handprints preserved, most at only three times page size so they refer to the actual documents, others very tall so they are physically overwhelming. She works only with material already released, has made no Freedom of Information Act requests of her own, and draws on the National Security Archive collection, old material from the FBI's website, ACLU postings and other places. Permanent works include For 7 World Trade (2006), a 65-foot-wide, 14-foot-high LED wall in the lobby of 7 World Trade Center carrying poems about the joy of being in New York City, and Historical Speeches (1999), a four-sided amber LED sign permanently installed at the Reichstag, Berlin, playing a selection of speeches for 12 days without repeating. In 2024 the Guggenheim re-imagined the 1989 sign as Light Line, climbing all six ramps to the oculus and realising her original vision -- the 1989 version took three revolutions of the rotunda -- with conservators reverse-engineering the 1989 LED hardware and computer programme, and her new AI works WORDS, SO GOOD (2024) and BAD (2023), installed as opposites, one leaning upright and one lying on the gallery floor pulsing. In 2026 she received the Praemium Imperiale in the category Painting, was named to the Time 100 in 2024, and opened "Jenny Holzer -- J'AI VU" at the Musee d'Orsay, Paris, 20 October 2026-21 February 2027, with facade projections, a coloured LED stream through four galleries, nineteen stoneworks and gold-, platinum- and palladium-leafed paintings, fed by more than 750 unique texts from 424 sources and 73 authors. She is living, and lives and works in Hoosick, New York, with a studio in Brooklyn. [C28818]

The twenty works inspected run 1977-2026 and trace one practice moving its sentences onto larger and less holdable surfaces while the voice behind them grows less and less her own. Early, 1977-1985: she writes her own sentences and puts them where they do not belong -- cheap paper on a wall, a bronze plaque that sounds official, an advertising board that sells nothing -- and the power is in the mismatch between voice and surface: Truisms (W01, 1977-79), Inflammatory Essays (W02, 1979-82), Living (W03, 1980-82), Survival (W04, 1983-85) and Texts on the Times Square Spectacolor Board (W05, 1982). ## M1 -- The sentence that contradicts itself: a field of cancelling aphorisms

The Truisms are over 250 short declarative sentences, arranged alphabetically, each sounding like a familiar aphorism while contradicting another one a page -- or a street -- later: A STRONG SENSE OF DUTY IMPRISONS YOU beside FREEDOM IS A LUXURY NOT A NECESSITY; POLITICS IS USED FOR PERSONAL GAIN beside THERE ARE TOO FEW IMMUTABLE TRUTHS TODAY. Nothing adds up, there is no right or wrong statement, and she did not necessarily stand behind all the lines -- "It was more accurate to offer a wealth of opinion." The mechanism is a complete outline of everything she wanted to do, every subject represented and almost every possible point of view, delivered as single sentences a passerby can read in seconds. The watchable behaviour is reading a complete thought, believing it for a moment, and then meeting its opposite a street later and having to decide which one, if either, is yours. Seen in W01, and carried forward wherever Truisms reappear on signs, shirts and stone. [C28820]

**Takeaway:** print every point of view at once and let the passerby choose. [C28820]

## M2 -- The borrowed voice: who is speaking is the work

Holzer often uses the first person, assuming a voice or representing many people in the first person; the many I's and You's in Inflammatory Essays are not her, but many different voices on unmentionable subjects. In Laments she gives a voice in the first person to thirteen dead individuals, so the impression is that a dead person is speaking. In Lustmord the same three poems are told from the perspectives of victim, observer and perpetrator, and the viewer cannot settle who is speaking because the voice, the skin and the bone keep swapping roles. She does not sign her work, because that would diminish its effectiveness -- it would be the work of just one person, easier to toss, and she wants people to concentrate on the content, not "who done it". The mechanism is a voice with no owner: the sentence arrives in the first person, the signature is withheld, and the reader supplies the speaker. The watchable behaviour is the pause before belief -- deciding whether the sentence is a confession, an accusation, a slogan or a lie, and noticing that the sentence works the same whichever it is. Seen in W02, W07 and W10. [C28821]

**Takeaway:** take the signature off and let the reader supply the speaker. [C28821]

## M3 -- Stone against light: two speeds of the same sentence

From the mid-1980s the work splits into two opposed speeds, and the opposition becomes the governing mechanism for the rest of her career. Stone fixes a sentence permanently: benches engraved with poetic phrases, sarcophagi carved with laments, a marble floor etched with epitaphs, a granite picnic table inscribed at UC San Diego. LED lets the same sentence scroll away: a 163-metre ribbon spiralling up the Guggenheim rotunda, nine vertical columns more than 12 metres tall bisecting a Bilbao gallery, red diodes on the front and blue on the back, scrolling a variation of Arno in English, Spanish and Basque. In Under a Rock the two speeds share one room -- a viewer can sit and read the permanent text while the electronic text moves and accuses overhead -- and in Venice the flashing commercial light stands against a floor that must be looked down at and read slowly. The watchable behaviour is the body caught between two reading speeds: sitting still for the stone, craning and walking for the light, and feeling the same words mean something different at each speed. Seen in W06, W08, W09 and W13. [C28822]

**Takeaway:** carve the sentence in stone and scroll it away in light, in the same room. [C28822]

## M4 -- The support is the message: the right surface per series

The sentences had to be sorted onto the right support, and subsequent series had different demands and had to fit in any number of places, so she had to find the right medium, stone or electronics. Truisms belonged on street posters, because the poster is cheap, anonymous and everywhere; Living belonged on bronze and aluminium plaques, because the plaque borrows the authority of medical and government buildings and lends civic weight to directions about eating, sleeping and breathing; Survival belonged on electronic signs, because the sign is built to be legible at a glance to a passerby in a hurry; Laments belonged on sarcophagi, because the tomb is the only honest support for a dead person's voice. The mechanism is a matching exercise: each series is written for the surface that will carry it, and the surface does half the persuading before a word is read. The watchable behaviour is noticing the surface first and the sentence second -- realising the plaque makes the sentence sound official, the poster makes it sound like a rumour, the bench makes it sound like something you are allowed to sit on. Seen in W01, W03, W04 and W07. [C28823]

**Takeaway:** write the sentence for the surface that will carry it, and let the surface persuade first. [C28823]

## M5 -- The commercial surface interrupted: a statement selling nothing

In 1982 Holzer's Truisms appeared for seconds at a time on the Times Square Spectacolor board, between advertisements, sponsored by the Public Art Fund -- her first use of electronic signage, co-opting an advertising surface and its glow, scale and motion. The same sentences that were anonymous paper were now lit, moving and competing with neon, and the sequence was a commercial sequence interrupted by a statement selling nothing: PRIVATE PROPERTY CREATED CRIME, TORTURE IS BARBARIC, ABUSE OF POWER COMES AS NO SURPRISE. The mechanism is insertion, not opposition: the work does not build its own surface, it borrows the most expensive one in the city and spends a few seconds of it on a sentence with no product attached. The watchable behaviour is the double-take in a crowd built to ignore signs -- a shopper reading an ad, then reading a sentence that refuses to sell, then watching it vanish into the next ad. Seen in W05, and in every later LED sign that takes a commercial format and spends it on a sentence. [C28824]

**Takeaway:** borrow the brightest surface in the city and spend a few seconds of it on nothing to sell. [C28824]

## M6 -- The same text on incompatible supports: voice, skin, bone

Lustmord carries the same three poems across three supports that cannot agree with each other: the lines appear on LED boards, written in blue, black and red ink on skin in photographs, and engraved on silver rings worn by human bones laid out on a table as if for scientific examination -- bones purchased from a New York dealer, not from victims, chosen from parts of the body associated with beauty and sensuality, teeth, shoulders, thighs, ribs, back, fingers and pelvis. The mechanism is repetition across incompatibility: the text does not change, the body it is written on does, and each support accuses the others -- the sign is too clean, the skin is too intimate, the bone is too final. The watchable behaviour is a viewer unable to settle who is speaking, because the voice, the skin and the bone keep swapping roles, and the poem reads differently on each. Seen in W10. [C28825]

**Takeaway:** put the same words on a sign, on skin and on bone, and let the supports accuse each other. [C28825]

## M7 -- The projection that will not hold: text on a surface that erases it

Since 1996 Holzer's projections have thrown text onto surfaces that will not hold it: Arno text onto the moving water and riverbank of the Arno in Florence, a memorial that cannot be fixed or photographed cleanly; large text onto waves, beach, mountainside and building facades in Rio de Janeiro, words appearing on a surface that moves, darkens and erases them within seconds; text across the glass Louvre Pyramid, legible only in fragments as the viewer moves around it; poetry and declassified documents onto the facades of the New York Public Library, Rockefeller Center and Bobst Library. The mechanism is a xenon projector casting large letters onto a surface in motion -- water, glass, a crowd's line of sight -- so the text ripples, breaks and reforms with the river or the angle. The watchable behaviour is reading in fragments and losing the sentence mid-way, then catching it again as the water or the light shifts; the page refuses to stay still, and the reading becomes a matter of patience rather than attention. Seen in W12, W14, W15 and W16. [C28826]

**Takeaway:** project the sentence onto water and let the river do the editing. [C28826]

## M8 -- The borrowed text: other people's words at building scale

Since 2001 Holzer has mainly used texts by others, and she stopped writing her own text that year, finding she could not say enough adequately: poems by Szymborska, Amichai, Cole, Darwish and others, stenographic records of what political parties said at the Reichstag, and declassified government documents about Iraq and the Middle East. The pleasure is in reading, not writing, and she is heavily dependent on consultants for the poetry and history projects. The mechanism is selection and scale: the text is found, not made, and then cast at building scale -- a poem on a library, a redacted memo on a university facade, a president's speech thrown from the Kennedy Center onto the Potomac River -- so a city crowd reads on a wall it normally walks past. The watchable behaviour is a crowd stopping to read a poem or a memo in a place built for walking, and the shock is not the size but the source: the words belong to a poet, a parliament or a censor, and the artist's only act was choosing them and making them unavoidable. Seen in W16, W17 and W20. [C28827]

**Takeaway:** stop writing, start choosing, and put the chosen words where they cannot be walked past. [C28827]

## M9 -- The redaction as composition: the black bar is the picture

The Redaction Paintings reproduce declassified memos enlarged to painting scale, with much of the text blacked out by censors, the black bars becoming the composition -- sometimes with handprints or other marks preserved, including postmortem handprints of dead people, most at only three times page size so they refer to the actual documents, others very tall so they are physically overwhelming. Before release the government blackened or excised portions for security or unknowable reasons; researchers have had to stop reading the material at times, and it is a relief to reach wholly blacked-out pages. The mechanism is the censor's mark promoted to the image: the document is reproduced faithfully, the excision is left exactly as the censor left it, and the painting is the shape of what cannot be said. The watchable behaviour is trying to read a document that refuses to be read, and discovering that the refusal -- the black bar, the missing paragraph, the page that is entirely black -- is the statement about war, torture and secrecy after 11 September 2001. Seen in W17. [C28828]

**Takeaway:** enlarge the censored page until the black bar is the composition. [C28828]

## M10 -- The programme as author: pauses, flashes, and a machine that writes

The big installations involve filling the space and programming the electronics, including pauses, flashes, phrasing and more; she likes the programming aspect, compares typesetting to programming, does not know how to program herself and is reliant on others, participates in the decisions, and decisions are often made at the site, not in advance, then fixed when they prove wrong -- boring, wrong for the content, or failing to complete the space. The 2024 Light Line sign divides its text crawls into sequences separated by brief dark pauses, and the new AI works WORDS, SO GOOD (2024) and BAD (2023), are installed as opposites: SO GOOD leaning upright with text prompted about human and ethereal experience, BAD lying on the gallery floor pulsing with AI-generated statements prompted by extremist content from the political alt- and far-right. The mechanism is the programme becoming the author: the text is no longer written by her or borrowed from a poet, but generated by a machine from a prompt she chose, and the programming -- the pause, the pulse, the upright or floor-level placement -- is the voice. The watchable behaviour is comparing two machine voices, one hopeful and upright, one low and pulsing on the floor, and realising the difference between them is the prompt, not the poet. Seen in W19, and in the programmed LED works generally. [C28829]

**Takeaway:** let the programme write, and let the prompt be the only authorship left. [C28829]

## Aesthetic logic

Holzer's logic is the sentence placed where it cannot be avoided, on the surface that makes it hardest to dismiss, with the speaker removed so the sentence has to defend itself. Against Barry, whose word stands alone in a quiet room and asks to be believed, Holzer's sentence arrives in a crowd, in capital letters, for urgency, "to speak a bit loudly", and contradicts itself before the reader has finished agreeing with it. The work is always a mismatch: a truism on a wall that belongs to advertising, a plaque that sounds official but says something nobody official would say, a tombstone for people who were never given a funeral, a government memo enlarged until its censor's black bars are the picture, a poem thrown ten storeys high on a building that normally reflects the sky. What changes across fifty years is not the question but the author: first she writes the sentences herself, then she borrows them from poets and parliaments, then she takes them from censors' files, then she prompts a machine to generate them -- and at each step the sentence stays the same shape, short, declarative, in capitals, while the hand behind it withdraws further. The viewer supplies what the work withholds: the speaker, the verdict, the belief. The work is always the interval -- between the sentence and its opposite, between the stone that fixes it and the light that scrolls it away, between the text that is shown and the text that is blacked out -- and the interval is where the reader has to stand. [C28830]
