# Jean-Eugene Buland: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean-Eugene Buland (1852-1926), French painter -- born on Tuesday 26 October 1852 in Paris, in the former 12th arrondissement, the son of the engraver Jean-Marie Buland (1825-1895) and of Suzanne Wagener, from Luxembourg; his younger brother Jean-Emile Buland (1857-1938) was an engraver who won the Prix de Rome for engraving in 1880. He entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in the atelier of Alexandre Cabanel -- the year of admission is not stated in any source read -- and his earliest work was antique scenes in the academic manner, of which Auguste au tombeau d'Alexandre (Augustus at the Tomb of Alexander, 1872-1878, Musee d'Orsay) is the only dated survivor in the sources read. He took the Second Grand Prix de Rome in painting twice running, in 1878 and 1879 -- second prizes, not failures, and not the first prize, so no Rome residency followed as of right -- and exhibited at the Salon des artistes francais from 1873, the title of his debut work not recorded in the sources read. The turn came in the early 1880s: Alms of a Beggar (1880) replaced gods and saints with a beggar giving his last coin to a well-dressed woman, Le Tripot (1883) put five gamblers in a seedy den facing the viewer, and Mariage innocent (1884) walked a young couple through blossoming fields, the two poles of his mature work side by side. The medals arrived on schedule -- honourable mention 1879, third class 1884, second class 1887, third class at Barcelona 1888, silver at the Exposition Universelle 1889, an award at London 1890 -- and he was made chevalier of the Legion d'honneur in 1894, with no higher grade found in any source read. On Tuesday 27 May 1884 he married Pauline Carpentier at Maisons-Laffitte; they had one son, Jean-Paul, painted reading in 1902. In 1886 he left Paris and settled at Charly-sur-Marne, a village in the Aisne near Chateau-Thierry, and from then on he found his models among the peasants and workers of the Marne valley, venturing to Paris only for the annual Salon and for public commissions: panels for the Salon des Sciences at the Hotel de Ville in 1889, the ceiling of the town hall at Chateau-Thierry in 1894-1897. His wife died in 1915. He died at Charly-sur-Marne on Thursday 18 March 1926, aged 73, and was then nearly forgotten until the first solo retrospective, "Eugene Buland, 1852-1926", ran at the Musee des Beaux-Arts de Carcassonne from Friday 19 October 2007 to Saturday 19 January 2008, touring to Chartres, Charleville-Mezieres, and Quimper from Thursday 30 October 2008 to Sunday 1 February 2009. His works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay (Propagande, 1889, and Auguste au tombeau d'Alexandre), Carcassonne (Mariage innocent, 1884), Caen (La Restitution a la Vierge le lendemain du mariage, 1885, 144 by 209 cm), Bordeaux (Les Heritiers, 1887), the Nationalmuseum in Stockholm (Un Patron, 1888), Quimper (Bretons en priere, 1898, and Visite a la Vierge de Benodet, 1898), Le Havre (Offrande a la Vierge, 1879), Douai (Les Fiances, 1881), Chateau-Thierry (Le Repas du jardinier, 1899), Troyes (Devant les reliques, 1897, a black-chalk drawing), the Chi Mei Museum in Tainan, Taiwan (Un jour d'audience, 1895), Colmar (Une bonne trouvaille, 1898), Saint-Maur-des-Fosses (Procession, 1899), the church of Saint-Martin at Charly-sur-Marne (Jesus chez Marthe et Marie, 1882), and the town hall at Pierrelaye (the municipal council of 1891). ## M1. The Frontal Frieze
Every element pushed to the front of the picture plane, figures standing in a single shallow band with almost no depth, as if in a frieze. Sotheby's on La Lecture (1901): "all of the elements seem to be pushed to the front of the picture plane, as if in a frieze". Alazraki on Le Tripot (1883) and Mariage innocent (1884): the works are large-scale "with the figures in the frontal plane", mirroring Bastien-Lepage. Galerie Ary Jan calls the totally frontal vision of Le repas du jardinier the manner La Lecture abandons, moving toward a representation "still extremely realistic, but softer". The picture reads left to right like a strip of figures; nothing recedes, so the eye cannot escape into depth and must inventory each face, hand and object in turn. **Takeaway:** push everything to the front plane and the picture stops being a window and starts being a strip to read.

## M2. The One Face That Looks Out
In a group of five or more, exactly one figure meets the viewer's eyes while the rest look at the table, the work, or out of the frame. Le Tripot is the type: "Only the subject placed at the center of the painting is looking at the viewer. Three of the gamblers are starring at the gambling table while the last one is looking at something outside the pictorial space." The central elderly man "gazes out at us with an almost blank look", his wooden rake and the large pool of bank money at his side suggesting he may be the croupier. The eye is caught by the single stare, then released to the others, then pulled back; the one looker becomes the anchor and the judge of the whole group. **Takeaway:** let one face look out and the rest look away; the single stare organises the crowd.

## M3. The Reversed Charity
The giver and the receiver swap places, so the picture's moral arrow points backwards. Alms of a Beggar (1880) is the type: a young woman dressed immaculately in white sits outside a church seeking charity, and approaching her, a coin in his right hand, is a man who can only be a beggar himself, clothes patched on patches, faded and filthy, battered old wooden shoes, apparently sightless, about to give her what is probably his last coin. The viewer first reads "rich woman, poor man", then the coin in the man's hand flips the reading; the picture keeps flipping as the eye goes back and forth between the white dress and the patches. The critics ask the questions the picture will not answer: "Why is the well-dressed woman begging? ... Is there such a thing as inward poverty?" **Takeaway:** swap the giver and the receiver and the picture argues with itself; the coin is the argument.

## M4. The Unanswered Question
The picture poses a plain question about who someone is and refuses to answer it, leaving the viewer to supply the story. Le Tripot again: the man looking over the widow's shoulder -- "Is he, perhaps, a son, or a debtor?" The central man -- "Is he just another gambler or is he the croupier?" Alms of a Beggar -- "Why is the well-dressed woman begging?" The viewer invents a biography for the ambiguous figure, then a second one, and the picture holds both; the looking never settles. Hoakley on Le Tripot: "Reading this painting is opening a whole book." The book has no last page. **Takeaway:** ask who the figure is and refuse to say; the viewer's invented biography is the picture's second half.

## M5. The Photographic Collage Assembly
Faces and gestures captured one by one from photographs and assembled into the composition, so each figure is sharply itself and the group feels pasted together rather than posed together. Sotheby's: "Buland used photography extensively to render the faces and gestures of his characters, giving these compositions a graphic impression, as if assembled by collage. This process anticipates the paintings of Norman Rockwell." For Un Patron (1888) he "used photographs quite extensively in the preparatory work... to capture its wealth of detail". Wikipedia, flatly: "He used photographs in order to paint with realism." Each face holds still like a separate portrait; the group never quite breathes as one moment, and that stiffness is the style -- the viewer reads the picture figure by figure, like a contact sheet. **Takeaway:** photograph each face separately and paste the portraits together; the seams are the style.

## M6. The Stilted Rite
Figures going through a formal rite -- wedding, offering, council meeting -- rendered stiff, posed and immobile, as if the ceremony has frozen them. Offering to the Virgin the Day After the Wedding (1885): "a crisp formality in these figures, who appear stilted and posed as they go through the rites and processes of life". The Municipal Council of Pierrelaye (1891) carries "a similar stiff formality to Propaganda Campaign". La Lecture (1901) has "a certain immobility that we find in his portraits from this period, often bringing them closer to photography". The figures hold their poses a beat too long; the rite advances in stills, not flow, and the viewer watches the ceremony as a sequence of held tableaux. **Takeaway:** freeze the rite mid-gesture; the stiffness is the ceremony, not a failure of it.

## M7. The Wedding Sequence
One village rite told across several dated pictures, each catching the next day or the next step. Mariage innocent (1884), the wedding walk through the fields; La Visite lendemain de noces (1884), the visit the day after; Offrande a la Vierge, lendemain de noces (1885), the offering the day after, 144 by 209 cm at Caen. Each picture is one day of a small serial; seen in order the couple moves from field to visit to church, and the viewer follows a story told in annual instalments rather than in one scene. The series is the unit, not the single canvas. **Takeaway:** tell the rite in dated instalments; the story lives between the pictures, not inside one of them.

## M8. The Telling Object
One hand-held or table-top object that carries the whole biography of its owner: a coin, a rake, a cogwheel, a pot, a book. Alms of a Beggar, the coin in the beggar's right hand; Le Tripot, the croupier's wooden rake and the large pool of bank money at his side; Un Patron, the cogwheel the foreman drills while the boy watches; La Lecture, the four hands at the centre, the spools of thread and patchwork textiles; The Tinker (1908), the damaged pots and pans being repaired. The eye lands on the object first, reads the trade or the fate from it, and only then looks up at the face; the object is the caption. **Takeaway:** give each figure one object that says their trade; the object is the label the picture refuses to print.

## M9. The Political Prop in the Hand
A printed thing -- poster, book, coloured print -- carried into a poor interior, so that national politics enters the room as a saleable object. Propagande (1889, Musee d'Orsay, 181.8 by 191.4 cm) is the type: a travelling salesman in the home of a poor family, selling books and coloured prints to the head of the household, a poster of General Boulanger in his left hand, a tricolour rosette in his buttonhole declaring him a canvasser for the General. Motives combine politics with business; the poster is the brightest printed surface in a dim room, and the eye goes to it, reads the face on it, and understands that the salesman is selling a candidate, not a book. The Salon critic's verdict, quoted by Weisberg: "His tableau is not Boulangist. It is also not antiboulangist." **Takeaway:** put the poster in the salesman's hand and let the room argue with it; the politics arrives as merchandise.

## M10. The Ethnographer Inventory
The picture as a field record: every costume, shoe, tool and texture catalogued with the same attention, no detail ranked above another. Galerie Ary Jan: "a meticulous painter who never forgot any details regarding the appearance and the costumes of his persons... Buland adopted the attitude of an ethnographer who showed his observations to the public." The Tinker: varying textures of pots and pans, the damp stone wall glistening with touches of white. Bonheur des parents (1903): vegetables and parings strewn on the floor, worn working shoes, wooden clogs. The eye wanders the inventory -- shoe, clog, paring, pot -- and the accumulation of small true things, not any single gesture, builds the world. The French Wikipedia calls him, for the rigour and minuteness of his technique, a precursor of hyperrealism. **Takeaway:** catalogue everything with the same attention; the world is built by the inventory, not by the gesture.

## Evolution
1872-1879, the academic phase. Buland enters the Ecole in Cabanel's atelier and paints what the atelier prescribes: antique and religious subjects, finished to a high polish. The only dated survivor of this phase with a museum home is Auguste au tombeau d'Alexandre (1872-1878, Musee d'Orsay). The two Second Grand Prix de Rome (1878, 1879) are the peak of this phase -- and its dead end, since the second prize carried no Rome residency as of right, whatever the dealer pages say about a five-year Villa Medici stay. The honourable mention at the Salon of 1879 arrives in the same year as the second Rome prize. The "Symbolist paintings of antique scenes" of his beginnings are otherwise undated and untitled in the sources read, and the Symbolist label itself is dealer and Wikipedia wording, not a scholarly consensus; carry it as antique scenes in the academic manner.

1880-1884, the slow turn. Religious subjects persist (Offrande a Dieu 1880, L'Annonciation 1881, Jesus chez Marthe et Marie 1882) but the treatment is already observational: regional headdress, photographic precision. Alms of a Beggar (1880) is the hinge: a contemporary street scene, painted with academic finish, whose meaning turns on a reversal of roles rather than on a sacred story. The turn is gradual, not a single-year break -- the religious titles run to 1885, with a second L'Annonciation dated 1885 -- and the sources date the abandonment of classical subjects to the return from Rome in 1884, "working closely with Jules Bastien-Lepage". Le Tripot (1883) is the first large-scale secular canvas, a gambling den, followed by the pastoral opposite, Mariage innocent (1884), then the wedding sequence. The medals follow: third class 1884, second class 1887. The mechanism is not a change of technique -- he keeps the academic finish -- but a change of subject, and a change of tool: photographs, used extensively in preparation, notably for Un Patron.

1886-1889, the Charly settlement and the political turn. He settles in Charly-sur-Marne in 1886, models taken from the peasants and workers he frequented, and the subject widens from the village rite to the factory and to national politics. Un Patron (1888) makes an apprentice at a cogwheel into a statement about French industry after the Franco-Prussian War, "an overt politicisation in his work". Propagande (1889) puts a Boulangist canvasser inside a poor family's home, and the Salon critic finds it neither Boulangist nor antiboulangist. The same year he paints several panels of the Salon des Sciences at the Hotel de Ville, and wins the silver medal at the Exposition Universelle. The scale stays large -- Propagande at 181.8 by 191.4 cm -- but the subjects are now institutional and communal rather than sensational, and the white dress in the dark room still does its work: in Alms of a Beggar the bright figure glows first, then the eye notices the dirt around it, and the brightness itself becomes the question.

1891-1900, the civic and devotional middle years. Council portraits (Pierrelaye 1891, 140 by 200 cm, "clearly commissioned by them to record their great deeds"), a courtroom day (Un jour d'audience, 1895), Bretons at prayer (1898), the village church interior (Procession, 1899), the washerwomen's lunch (1900). The committee tableau is the device here: a group of local notables arranged as a formal record, each man posed to be recognised by his neighbours, the picture commissioned by its own subjects, nobody acting, everybody presenting himself, the stiffness the point -- this is a document, not a drama. The gold medal at the Exposition Universelle of 1900 is Alazraki-only and is flagged, not settled; the 1889 silver is the medal carried. He is made chevalier of the Legion d'honneur in 1894 and decorates the ceiling of the town hall at Chateau-Thierry in 1894-1897.

1901-1908, the softer late phase. La Lecture (1901) is the pivot Galerie Ary Jan names: "the painter gradually abandoned the totally frontal vision found in Le repas du jardinier, moving towards a representation that was still extremely realistic, but softer". Bonheur des parents (1903) returns to poor working families in an outhouse, the mother looking down at the breastfeeding baby, "utter tiredness in her eyes, nervousness in the father's expression". Ouvriers se chauffant (1906) is dark and brooding, two workmen on a log lit by a single shaft of daylight through a small high window. Le Tinker (1908) is a cottage-industry portrait. The interrupted task is the late device: work stopped mid-gesture, the hands still holding the tools but the attention gone elsewhere -- the grandmother who has stopped her embroidery to listen to her daughter read, the workmen pausing at the brazier, the washerwomen at their lunch break. The viewer waits for the task to resume, and in that waiting the relationship between the figures becomes the subject. He exhibits regularly at the Salon de la Societe des artistes francais through 1908; no dated work after 1908 was found in sources read, and the last eighteen years of his life (1908-1926) are undocumented in the sources read beyond his wife's death in 1915.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic of Buland is a logic of kept apparatus and changed subject, and then of kept subject and abandoned city. He never abandons the academic technique he learned from Cabanel; the finish stays, the scale stays, the Salon stays -- he exhibits through 1908 and takes the Hotel de Ville commissions -- and what changes is who stands in front of the finish, and then where the painter stands. That is the whole move, and it is why the pictures are legible in a way a looser painting would not be: a beggar painted with the care of a Venus is a claim, not a sketch, and a council painted with the care of a history picture is a document, not a joke. The first logic is the inventory: the frontal frieze, the photographic collage, the ethnographer catalogue -- Buland counts the village the way a naturalist counts specimens, shoe by shoe, pot by pot, and the count is the chronicle. The second logic is the reversal: the beggar who gives, the gambler who is the croupier, the salesman who sells a candidate -- the picture sets up the expected moral arrow and turns it around, and the viewer, given a question and no answer, has to supply the verdict. The third logic is the rite: the wedding in instalments, the offering the day after, the council organising its festival -- provincial life as a sequence of formal occasions, each one frozen a beat too long, the stiffness itself the proof that the occasion mattered. The fourth logic is the withdrawal: after 1886 the painter leaves Paris for a village and stays forty years, and after 1901 the pictures get softer, quieter and more domestic, the frontal plane loosening into intimacy, the figures caught unposed in their own home, the gaze relaxing from confrontation to eavesdropping. The village is the last mechanism: the man who painted the poor for the Salon stops living among the buyers and keeps painting the neighbours anyway, because the chronicle was the point, not the market.

## POSITIONS
P1. History first, then the break. He trained under Cabanel and "with his teacher's influence turned first to history painting"; his earliest works were antique scenes in the academic manner. After the two second places in the Prix de Rome (1878, 1879) he switched to genre scenes of modern life.
P2. The turn was dated 1880 and announced by a reversal. "Alms of a Beggar" (1880) is the first Naturalist work: a young woman in white begging outside a church, approached by a beggar giving her what is probably his last coin. The giver is poorer than the receiver; the picture inverts charity.
P3. The frontal group as the device. "Le Tripot" (1883) and "Mariage innocent" (1884) are large-scale works "with the figures in the frontal plane" mirroring Bastien-Lepage. Five gamblers face the viewer across the table in "Le Tripot"; the frontal stare is the mechanism, as in Pelez, but Buland fills the plane with a crowd rather than an isolated figure.
P4. Photography as the working tool. "He used photographs in order to paint with realism". For "Un Patron" (1888) he "used photographs quite extensively in the preparatory work... to capture its wealth of detail". Photography is a precision instrument for him, not a shortcut: the detail it captures is the point.
P5. Meticulousness as the signature. Galerie Ary Jan: "Eugene Buland was a meticulous painter who never forgot any details regarding the appearance and the costumes of his persons". My Daily Art Display: "a meticulous painter who never overlooked any details with regards to the figures populating his paintings".
P6. The ethnographer stance: observation without judgement. "By representations as objective as possible, Buland tried to give us an account of the everyday life around him... his scenes were always deprived of judgement. Buland adopted the attitude of an ethnographer who showed his observations to the public. His paintings are like an everyday life chronicle, combining portraits and scenes of genre." This is the load-bearing description of his method, and the one that explains Q1: the same indifference that makes "Propaganda Campaign" neither Boulangist nor antiboulangist.
P7. Two registers at once: the sordid and the idyllic. "Le Tripot" (1883) is a seedy gambling den; "Innocent Wedding" (1884) the next year is "deeply romantic" and idyllic, "a far cry from the works above". He does not pick a side between indictment and celebration; he paints both.
P8. The wedding cycle as a serial subject. After "Innocent Wedding" (1884) came "a series of paintings showing events 'the day after the wedding'", including "Offering to the Virgin the Day After the Wedding" (1885), with "a crisp formality in these figures, who appear stilted and posed as they go through the rites and processes of life". The rite, repeated across canvases, is the subject; the stiffness is deliberate.
P9. Politicisation without partisanship, 1888-1889. "Un Patron" (1888) "marks an overt politicisation in his work: the apprentice was part of the nation's efforts to advance in industry and manufacturing after the disaster of the Franco-Prussian War". "Propaganda Campaign" (1889) is "even more political", yet the Salon critic found it neither Boulangist nor antiboulangist. The politics is in the choice of subject, not in a verdict on it.
P10. Provincial municipal painting as a second career. He painted "several panels of the Salon of Sciences at the Hotel de Ville, Paris and decorated the ceiling of the city hall at Chateau-Thierry"; "Municipal Council and Commission of Pierrelaye Organizing a Festival" (1891) is "an example of provincial municipal art... clearly commissioned by them to record their great deeds". The village councillors are painted with the same stiff formality as the wedding parties.
P11. The village as studio: models from the neighbours. "Firmly rooted in the Marne valley, it was in the area around Charly-sur-Marne, where he settled in 1886, that Buland found the models for his paintings, mainly among the peasants and workers he frequented." Late works stay with the same material: "Parental Happiness" (1903), a young labouring couple with a first baby in an agricultural outhouse; "The Tinker" (1908), a cottage repairman; "Workers Warming Themselves" (1906), two workmen at a brazier.
P12. Late softening, not late rupture. "The painter gradually abandoned the totally frontal vision found in 'Le repas du jardinier' (The Gardener's Meal), moving towards a representation that was still extremely realistic, but softer, as in the portrait of his son Jean-Paul in 1902". The frontal plane loosens; the realism does not.
P13. Salon success, unlike Pelez. "His meticulous realism was well-received at the Salon, and after winning a series of medals there, in 1889 he was awarded a silver medal at the Exposition Universelle in Paris." Honourable mention 1879, third-class medal 1884, second-class medal 1887; the gold medal at the Exposition Universelle 1900 is Alazraki-only and is flagged, not settled.
P14. The Zola label, secondhand. His turn is described as "classical Naturalist motifs inspired by the literary Naturalism of Emile Zola in particular". No source read quotes Zola on Buland himself; the Zola connection is the critics' framing, not a documented encounter. The study says "Zola-inspired", not "Zola-approved".
P15. The Bastien-Lepage mould. "From the outset, these were in the mould of Bastien-Lepage's Naturalism"; his large works "mirror the format of those of Jules Bastien Lepage (1848-1884), one of the most widely influential artists of the 19th century". Buland is read as the pupil who carried Bastien-Lepage's format forward after Bastien-Lepage's death in 1884: "Several other Naturalist painters achieved acclaim alongside Jules Bastien-Lepage and sustained the movement after his untimely death in 1884. Jean-Eugene Buland (1852-1926) was among them."
P16. The Meissonier shadow in the early work. "Buland's early small-scale works show the marked influence of Jean Louis Ernest Meissonier (1815-1891), also a favorite of Napoleon III, in their precision and eye for detail." Precision is the through-line from Meissonier's miniatures to the large Naturalist canvases.
P17. The 2007-08 revival. "His first solo retrospective exhibition was held at Carcassonne in 2007-08", the Musee des Beaux-Arts de Carcassonne holding "Mariage Innocent" and publishing Pays's catalogue. Before that he was, in Hoakley's words, one of the artists "considered important, and were very popular, in the late nineteenth century, but who have all but vanished since".
P18. The market verdict: rising from a low base. "His prices at auction are moving steadily upwards. I hope it doesn't prove too late to conserve and document what remains of his work." Auction estimates in the low thousands of euros are cited in the leg; no realised hammer price was established in this study.
P19. The illustrator's second trade. "He also worked as an illustrator for Le Figaro, reflecting his popularity and significant contribution to painting and the development of Naturalism." The engraver's son kept a draughtsman's trade alongside the Salon career.
P20. The forgotten-master frame. Hoakley (2018): "This article is about another, Jean-Eugene Buland (1852-1926), who painted several works which I feel deserve to be unforgotten." Van Nimmen, closing the Weisberg review: "Defying indifference, naturalist pictures once moved hardened critics to tears. Many a reader will wonder why these appealing works are so new to them." The afterlife is a rescue narrative, not a continuous reputation.

## Load-Bearing Corrections
C0. Prix de Rome: second prizes, not failures. The doorway as carried in #493 says Buland turned "after two failed Prix de Rome entries (1878, 1879)"; he took the Second Grand Prix de Rome in both years. The Eclectic Light pupils piece calls the two entries "unsuccessful", which is true only in the sense that he did not win the first prize; "failed" overstates it, and the study uses "second prizes" throughout. D2 is recorded as a HOLD on the turn itself, with this correction named in the Doorway section.
C1. Flagrant delit is 1893, not 1887. Both Wikipedia revisions found give 1893, Museum of Fine Arts, Quimper; 1887 is the year of Les Heritiers. The brief's 1887 date is an error and is not used.
C2. Titles that do not verify and are not used in this study: "Le retour des champs" (a painting by Paul Lazerges, not a Buland); "La Visite au jardinier" (the closest real titles are "La Visite lendemain de noces", 1884, and "Le repas du jardinier"); "Un jour de marche" (the real title is "Un jour d'audience", 1895); "Lecon de catechisme" (not found as a Buland title in 10 searches); "La marchande de fleurs" (not found); "La dispute" (not found); "Scene d'interieur" (not found); the generic "L'offrande" (use the specific titles "Offrande a la Vierge", 1879 and 1885, and "Offrande a Dieu", 1880); the bare "Le dejeuner" (the real title is "Dejeuner des laveuses", 1900); the bare "L'audience" (the real title is "Un jour d'audience", 1895); "Offre a Dieu" is a shortening of "Offrande a Dieu", 1880.
C3. Villa Medici stay: unverified and not asserted. Galerie Ary Jan and Sotheby's say the second prize let him stay at the Villa Medici for five years, returning in 1884; My Daily Art Display says winners were awarded a bursary for three to five years. The Rome residency belonged to first-prize winners, and no source read other than the dealer pages mentions a Rome stay. The study states the second prizes and does not state a Rome stay; the Pays catalogue is the source to check.
C4. Le Tripot location: unknown. The Eclectic Light articles and the French Wikipedia give the location as unknown; the English Wikipedia works list places it at the Museum of Fine Arts, Quimper; Alazraki gives private collections in the USA with Hammer Galleries provenance 1953. C5. Flagrant delit location: not stated. The French Wikipedia lists a private collection in Berlin; the English Wikipedia lists the Museum of Fine Arts, Quimper. Neither is used.
C6. Bretons en priere: 1898, with the conflict flagged. The French Wikipedia dates it 1898, with Joconde backing; the English Wikipedia dates it 1889. One old Wikipedia revision misprints the year as 1989. Use 1898.
C7. Mariage innocent location: Carcassonne, not the Musee du Luxembourg. C8. Death year: 1926, not 1927. He died 18 March 1926, aged 73; Proantic dealer listings giving "1852-1927" are wrong, and ArtsDot's "74 years" is likewise wrong.
C9. Medals: carry the 1889 silver, flag the 1900 gold.
