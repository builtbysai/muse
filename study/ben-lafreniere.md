His own site (benlafreniere.ca) describes him as "a research scientist with expertise in human-computer interaction and human-AI interaction" with "more than 10 years experience working in industry research labs, including Reality Labs Research (Meta), Autodesk Research, and Chatham Labs, an HCI research startup that I co-founded". His ORCID iD is 0000-0002-0546-0466. His stated research topics are user interfaces for generative AI, adaptive and intelligent user interfaces, and interactive help and learning systems, pursued by "designing, prototyping, and evaluating novel interactive systems". [C36665]

**Relevance:** Lafreniere's work asks how many small, locally-instructed contributions assemble into one coherent artifact — the choreography problem: what coordination rules let a hundred hands build one thing instead of a hundred fragments? The evidence is "Crowdsourced Fabrication" (UIST 2016), in which 108 untrained volunteers build a 12-foot bamboo pavilion by following system-generated instructions, and the two papers that frame the same question on screens: "Investigating the Feasibility of Extracting Tool Demonstrations from In-Situ Video Content" (CHI 2014), which cuts the crowd's ordinary work into reusable demonstrations, and "Mixed-Initiative Creative Interfaces for Dynamic Illustration and Generative Design" (MICI@CHI 2017), in which human and system take turns suggesting, producing, and selecting. [C36666]

**Doorway:** The 451st doorway. Zellmer ends on the question of what is left for the picture to do when a system has no model of the viewer at all — can a loop that only ever flips its own bits still surprise? Lafreniere answers the opposite branch: his systems have no model of the viewer either, but they have a precise model of the task and of the crowd, and the picture is what a hundred strangers can build when each of them is told exactly one small thing. The open question carried forward is: when the making is distributed across many hands, what must the system specify so the result is one coherent thing and not a hundred fragments — and where does the system end and the hands begin? [C36667]

## M1 -- The foreman engine ("Crowdsourced Fabrication", 2016)

Subject: 108 untrained volunteers build a 12-foot bamboo pavilion of 224 tensegrity modules — each three bamboo sticks held by tensioned waxed thread — over three days at a design conference, coordinated by a central "foreman engine". The modules sit in 8 rows of 28, the top row attached to a 10-foot-diameter steel ring suspended from the ceiling, two doubly-curved surfaces separated by seams forming a front and a back doorway. The construction space is 30 by 30 feet with four robot stations (one at each corner, each with a 6-axis UR-10 arm) and two material depots; at most four workers build in parallel. The paper's authors number 23, across Autodesk Research Toronto, Autodesk Applied Research Lab San Francisco, The Living New York, the Institute for Computational Design at Stuttgart, and Marcelo Coelho Studio. [C36671]

Visual principle: coherence is visible as convergence. At the start the field is scattered people, loose sticks, and a bare steel ring; at the end it is one doubly-curved surface with a front and back doorway. The in-between state — a half-built lattice with lit nodes marking exactly where the next piece goes — is the picture: the structure is legible at every moment as "done part / next part / not yet", because every unbuilt module has a unique address and every built module is visibly in place. [C36672]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED in the paper, plus leg formalisation labelled as such): each volunteer runs a fixed state machine, driven by a custom Apple Watch app with a colour-coded band: check-in → training station (an LED blinks the worker's colour) → Stick Depot (collect three colour-coded bamboo sticks) → next free robot station (load three sticks one at a time, an LED indicating which stick and its orientation; the robot measures the six stick tips in a lightbox and recomputes winding paths) → tie thread, step back, robot winds → Node Depot (collect 0, 1, or 2 connector nodes, the count depending on the current state of the structure) → pavilion (nodes blink the worker's colour for location, then change colour for orientation) → confirm → done. The foreman engine (Node.js + PostgreSQL, WebSockets) assigns each free worker the lowest unbuilt module whose predecessors (row above) are attached and whose station is free (FORMALISATION of the paper's description), never assigns the same module twice, and re-routes workers automatically when a robot goes offline. The numbers, as stated: mean completion 18 minutes 37 seconds; volunteers 20m 04s against staff 16m 37s (t(188) = 4.55, p < .01); 61 of 108 volunteers answered the survey, 43 Very Positive, 13 the next step down, 5 neutral, 0 negative; 53 of 61 were somewhat or very confident they could have completed the task without staff assistance. Failure is a first-class event, as stated: an integer-truncation error between foreman and robot stations made 15 modules with wrong geometry, the exhibit shut for 90 minutes around noon on Day 2 while staff replaced them, and a second shutdown the same evening followed a structural-integrity check. Detect, quarantine, rebuild. [C36673]

**Takeaway:** The mechanism worth carrying forward is not the pavilion but the coordination pattern: a global model (which module goes where, in which orientation) decomposed into per-worker, just-in-time, location-triggered micro-instructions, with the worker's own colour as the addressing scheme. The foreman is a visible choreographer, not a hidden scheduler — and the fault/repair beat gives the system a second act no static lattice has. [C36674]

## M2 -- The material that lights itself (Crowdsourced Fabrication, instrumented environment)

Subject: 298 individually addressable wireless RGB LED boards — at stations, on robot loading platforms, and embedded in every connector node — plus nine iBeacons for indoor location sensing. The light is an address, not decoration: a node pulses slowly in exactly one worker's colour, and only that worker should go there. [C36675]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): per LED node, state in {off, pulsing(colour c), solid(colour c)}; per worker, identity colour c from the watch band. Four rules: (1) station guidance — the LED at the worker's next station pulses in c; (2) loading guidance — the platform LED lights in the endcap colour of the stick to insert, encoding which stick and its orientation; (3) attachment guidance — pavilion nodes blink in c to mark location, then change colour to encode orientation once the worker confirms location; (4) node gathering — the depot nodes the worker must collect pulse in c. The same node means different things at different phases. Survey evidence, as stated, cuts both ways: LEDs were rated highest for indicating position and orientation of attachment — one participant was "surprised by the seeming intelligence of the [exhibit] in guiding me to install the component... by color-coded lights at the intersecting joints" — and lower for indicating locations across the space, because of occlusion and competing brightness ("There were too many other bright colors on the posters etc. in the area, and the [pavilion] was too big to see the blinking LEDs on the other side"). [C36676]

**Takeaway:** A picture in which light moves as instruction — dark field, scattered points, a travelling pulse that says "you, here, now" — makes addressing itself the visual event. The paper's own failure is a usable rule: guidance must be the brightest thing in the field or it is lost. [C36677]

## M3 -- The clip cutter ("Investigating the Feasibility of Extracting Tool Demonstrations from In-Situ Video Content", 2014)

Subject: 2,439 candidate demonstration clips cut from in-situ screen recordings of real Pixlr users, for six tools (Brush, Clone Stamp, Fill, Gradient, Hue/Saturation, Levels). The paper's premise: short video demonstrations (ToolClips, 10–25 seconds) help users learn tools, but authoring them for hundreds of commands is impractical — AutoCAD shipped ToolClips for only 35 of its 1000+ commands. The question is whether good demonstrations can be mined from recordings of real users doing real work. [C36678]

Visual principle: a long, messy stream of activity becomes watchable only when it is cut at the right joints. The naive cut (one clip per invocation) produces many short, unfollowable fragments; the merged cut (all consecutive invocations) produces clips too long to use. The right cut is legible because each clip contains its own setup: the tool being selected, the setting being adjusted, then the action. [C36679]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED, exact): input is a timestamped log of tool invocations, tool selections, dialog events, undo-stack changes, document snapshots, selection and palette changes. Segmentation: for a given tool, take consecutive sequences where the tool is invoked; start a segment S with the first invocation; append each successive invocation while its start timestamp is under 5 seconds from the start of the first invocation in S (threshold tuned through testing); when an invocation fails the test, report S and start a new segment at that invocation. For modal tools, if the tool was selected under 10 seconds before the first invocation in S, extend the segment boundary back to include the selection. Add 2 seconds of padding to the start and end of every segment. No hard duration cap. Quality model, as stated: 277 sampled clips rated 1–7 by three raters; median score 3.3 (SD 1.7); 31% scored 5 or higher ("good"), 56.3% scored below 4, only 3.6% scored 7; Fleiss' kappa 0.49 before discussion, 0.57 after. The most-mentioned quality features: extent of change to the image; how well the clip shows the tool's dynamics; pace and smoothness; whether settings are adjusted on camera; presence of unrelated or distracting actions. An SVM first-pass filter (precision 0.71, recall 0.44, accuracy 77.4% on 274 clips) is presented as a first pass only, to be refined by collaborative filtering. The data behind it: 21 freelance graphic designers, 25 hours 23 minutes of usage, 11,553 tool invocations of 93 unique tools. [C36680]

**Takeaway:** The crowd's ordinary work, harvested, is itself the teaching material — and the 5-second / 10-second / 2-second constants are directly portable as piece parameters. The paper's honesty about failure modes (wrong layer selected, experimental strokes, padding contamination) is equally useful: a cutter that hides its misses is a black box, and this one shows them. [C36681]

## M4 -- The library that grows (FollowUs, CHI 2013)

Subject: FollowUs, a web tutorial that embeds a fully-featured image editor (application-in-tutorial architecture), captures video of every user performing each tutorial step, and offers later users a library of community demonstrations per step alongside the author's original. [C36682]

Visual principle: one step, many hands. The same instruction performed ten different ways is more informative — and more visually interesting — than one canonical performance. The library is the picture: a step is not a single image but a stack of variants, and the stack grows every time someone completes it. [C36683]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): state is a tutorial of ordered steps; per step, the author's canonical demonstration plus a library L of captured community demonstrations (video plus log), growing monotonically as users complete the step. Capture is automatic and continuous — every user who follows the tutorial contributes their workflow to L without authoring anything. Retrieval: a later user may watch the original or choose any demonstration in L for that step. Evaluation, as stated: 16 participants with little or no experience in the image editor; users perform significantly better with the library of multiple demonstrations than with the author's content alone, and subjectively prefer it. The mechanism's claim is that variation across demonstrations — different pace, different image, different mistakes recovered from — is itself the teaching signal. [C36684]

**Takeaway:** A piece whose image is an accumulating stack of variants of one action — each run adds one more attempt, older attempts fading but never deleted — makes time and community visible in a single frame. The viewer is one more contributor, not the sole author. [C36685]

## M5 -- Seven composable deformation operators (Motion Amplifiers, CHI 2016, via MICI 2017)

Subject: seven motion amplifiers implementing seven of Disney's twelve principles of animation as independent, arbitrarily combinable toggles — squash and stretch, arcs, anticipation, follow through, secondary motion (geometric manipulation); slow-in and slow-out (temporal); staging (clarity and communication). Exaggeration is omitted as too broad to characterise; timing is supported separately via a motion-profile tool. The user sketches a drawing, records a motion by direct manipulation, then taps amplifier icons; each amplifier imposes a deformation on an underlying grid, which in turn updates the strokes. The design goal is modularisation: a complex dynamic broken into "mind-size bites" (Papert, *Mindstorms*, 1980) the user can understand and combine. [C36686]

**Takeaway:** Real mechanism, but ALREADY COVERED: KAZ3 "Seven Dials, One Fall" (PP5706, #817) and GRO2 (PP5697, #816) both prompt this mechanism, with a build-only-one dedupe flag between them. Formalised here for the study's completeness; no new prompt from this study. A third version would need a different substrate, and none is offered. [C36687]

## M6 -- Painted velocity fields (Energy Brushes, UIST 2016, via MICI 2017)

Subject: energy brushes for stylised elemental dynamics — waves, splashes, fire, smoke, explosions. The artist sketches the underlying forces with a brush gesture, creating a stable, repetitive velocity-field pattern by moving flow particles along the gesture; complex dynamics come from combining simple energy patterns. Motion as a field, not a path: the viewer paints weather, and the picture's particles obey the painted wind. [C36688]

**Takeaway:** Real mechanism, but ALREADY COVERED: KAZ4 "Three Winds" (PP5707, #817) and GRO3 (PP5698, #816), with the same build-only-one flag. Formalised here for completeness; no new prompt from this study. [C36689]

## M7 -- The negotiation (generative design, MICI 2017)

Subject: generative design as a mixed-initiative loop, as presented in the MICI 2017 workshop paper (Kazi first author, Lafreniere second, Matejka third — author order settled by direct read of the CEUR PDF, superseding the text-extraction caveat; the paper is a position and survey paper with no user study and no evaluation numbers). The designer states goals and constraints (material, manufacturing method, cost); the system generates hundreds or thousands of alternatives meeting them; the designer selects one or iterates on the constraints for a new population. [C36690]

Visual principle: the picture is a population, not an object. The viewer sees many candidate forms at once and watches the population converge as constraints tighten — selection is the visible act of authorship. [C36691]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): state is a constraint set C; population P = G(C) (hundreds to thousands of alternatives); selection s in P by the human; refined constraints C' = refine(C, s). Initiative state in {human, system, negotiated}: the system may take initiative by proposing; the human takes it back by selecting or re-constraining. Two failure modes are named in the paper: (1) expressing goals and constraints in the system's rigid terms is hard — natural expression of goals and ambiguity is an open problem; (2) generation latency (minutes to hours for 3D shape synthesis) breaks the tight interaction loop. The paper also proposes implicit interest modelling: learn the user's interests from which designs they dwell on, and use it to narrow the population or feed the next iteration. [C36692]

**Takeaway:** A piece in which the viewer never draws — they only constrain and choose — and the picture is the narrowing of a population toward one form. The interaction is earned because selection IS the mechanism, not a way to trigger a new random seed. [C36693]

## M8 -- The strategy map (Workflow Graphs, GI 2020)

Subject: a W-graph encoding how the approaches taken by multiple users performing the same fixed 3D design task converge and diverge. Nodes represent equivalent intermediate task states across users; directed edges represent how a user moved between states, inferred from screen recordings, command logs, and 3D-model content history; edge width represents the number of distinct workflows between two states. The pipeline: an instrumented Tinkercad records screen video, command sequences, and CSG-tree snapshots; a 3D autoencoder (trained on ShapeNet's 55 common object categories) embeds each model snapshot's geometry; DBSCAN clusters snapshots into equivalent states; sequences are compressed and merged into the graph. Case study, as stated: 8 demonstrations for a mug task averaging 26m 24s (SD 10m 46s) and 142 command invocations (SD 101), and 6 for a standing-desk task averaging 23m 23s. [C36694]

**Takeaway:** Many paths through the same task, overlaid, with well-trodden transitions drawn thick and rare ones thin — a map of how a crowd solves one problem. No prompt is built on it here: the mechanism needs a corpus of many users' workflows, and a single piece's one drawing is a timeline, not a strategy graph. Aggregating across runs inside one session would be fake aggregation. (Correction to the working notes: this paper was read at abstract plus section level, not title level — the mechanism above is verified, and the rejection stands on the corpus point, not on unreadness.) [C36695]

## M9 -- The orchestrated room and the bridge (Maestro, DreamRooms, Blocks-to-CAD)

Three systems, three different reading depths — stated plainly so no prompt is built on less than was read. **DreamRooms** (Weingarten et al., GI 2019) was read in full: a VR room-layout prototyping tool in which the user works with a procedural generative process that encodes no a priori objective measure of quality. It starts from a high-entropy state — furniture placed randomly subject only to "inside the room" and "not inside other furniture" — and the user gradually lowers the entropy by setting constraints. The four constraint tools are Lock (freeze a piece), Group (fuse two pieces into one compound object), Guide walls (low blue bars as artificial landmarks), and Edit clearance (drag a handle to set the negative volume a human needs). Deliberately, there is no manual move or place tool. Evaluation: 7 participants, 10.3 designs for the small room and 10.0 for the large room, about 110 and 127 seconds per design. Its mechanism is the same constraint-generation loop as M7, and it is cited as supporting evidence for LAF4 rather than as a separate prompt. **Blocks-to-CAD** (Lafreniere and Grossman, UIST 2018) was read at abstract plus study plus results level: a cross-application bridge from a Minecraft-style voxel game to Tinkercad, with new modelling capabilities introduced as unlockable game features, parallel data representations (blocks in first-person mode, 3D solids in third-person mode), and eight design strategies. Evaluation: 12 children aged 10–14; all completed the transfer tasks; none of the control participants used the workplane tool while all but one experimental participant did. No prompt is built on it: unlocking is a progression system, not a visual system, and a single-canvas piece would reduce the bridge to unlocking tools. **Maestro** (Dziubak et al., UIST 2018) was read at abstract plus grep level only: a workshop orchestration system that visualises each student's progress live from software log data, detects common challenges (extended periods without 3D navigation, multiple consecutive undo or erase actions, inactivity), and pushes recommended interventions to a student's screen; case-study evaluation with one facilitator and 13 children. No prompt is built on it: pacing a workshop has no visual consequence on a single canvas. [C36696]

## The three arcs

Lafreniere's work falls into three arcs, and the middle one is where the transferable mechanisms for generative art live. **Arc one, harvesting the crowd's work (2010–2014):** long-term log analysis of GIMP (GI 2010, over 200 users — vocabularies average only 27 commands of nearly 500 available, about 6 unique commands per session); interviews about FOSS usability culture (CHI 2010, 27 people across 11 projects — the core-user relationship as the engine of quality); mining documentation for interface references ("Then Click OK!", CHI 2012, average F1 0.81); AdaptableGIMP (UIST 2011 poster — borrow a neighbour's interface customisation, task sets with wiki pages); Community Enhanced Tutorials (CHI 2013, M4 above); the comment-section ethnography (ICWSM 2013, over 600 comments — tutorials as in-task help, repertoire expansion, and shadowing an expert); TaggedComments (CHI 2014, 16 participants — significantly improved subjective impressions of comment utility); the CHI 2014 mining paper (M3 above). The mechanism hardens across the arc: capture real use, cut it into reusable units, rank them, serve them back. [C36697]

**Arc one also carries the interface-design companions:** Task-Centric Interfaces (OzCHI 2014 — organise functionality around tasks, users associate tasks with keywords rather than low-level commands); False Feedforward (UIST 2015 — command names, icons, and tooltips mislead users, the running example being GIMP's two identically named "Flip Vertically" commands operating on different objects, with command-disambiguation techniques as the answer); CheatSheet (GI 2015, listing level only — the PDF was unretrievable). [C36698]

**Arc two, collaborating with a generative process (2017–2021):** the MICI paper (2017) names the mixed-initiative loop and the "mind-size bites" decomposition; DreamRooms (2019) is the payoff — a generative process with no objective function, where the human's judgement IS the objective function; PatchProv (CHI 2021) is the craft-world sibling, the same loop with physical fabric instead of virtual rooms — a four-part interface (Piece Bin, Design Wall, Layout Guides, Process Panel), virtual pieces shown semi-opaque against real ones, evaluated with 4 quilters, none of whom got stuck; TwoTorials (GI 2022) lets pairs of remote users complete a tutorial in parallel — 12 participants in pairs, 18.5 against 23 minutes, significantly lower effort, mental demand, and frustration; ElectroTutor (UIST 2018) puts tests inside physical-computing tutorials — 12 participants, 5 of 6 experimental participants completed against 3 of 6 controls, and backtracking fell from 18.8 times to 0.2; the expert-skill thread runs alongside (TOCHI 2017 — the all-or-nothing effect: 5 of 10 participants used the expert technique nearly 100% of the time over 7 days, the other 5 not at all; Rehearsal Hypothesis, GI 2015 — 70% expert use overall; WristTap, CHI 2016 — the 3x3 grid best; HandMark Menus, CHI 2016 — significantly faster than tabs; No-Handed Smartwatch, GI 2017 — foot-based techniques preferred, 40 survey respondents; Peak-End Effects, CHI 2016, Best of CHI honourable mention). [C36699]

**Arc three, managing the errors of autonomous systems (2021–2026):** False Positives vs False Negatives (UIST 2021) quantifies the asymmetry — a bias against false-positive errors equivalent to about 1.5 seconds or more of added recovery time; the gaze papers ask whether the body betrays the error before the user reports it (Peacock, ETRA 2022 — 32 consented, final sample 29 after calibration and code-bug exclusions; Sendhilnathan, UIST 2022 — 19 participants, three classes of input events: intentional actions, recognition errors, user errors); the mediation papers design what the system should do when it knows it erred (Henderson, IMWUT 2022 — text descriptions insufficient on their own to elicit error characteristics; Alghofaili, GI 2024 — mediation dialogs that fade after 2 seconds unless hovered; Wang, CHI 2024 — four visualisation designs for guiding bare-hand gestures, from formative studies with 11 and 6 participants and a controlled experiment with 15); FrameKit (IUI 2024, best paper) generalises adaptation as interpolation between authored keyframe UIs, evaluated with 10 front-end developers; the most recent work moves the same questions into generative AI and smart glasses — Squiggle (UIST 2025, abstract only — an invisible lasso gesture for referencing real-world objects, improving accuracy over gaze-plus-pinch, particularly for compound objects and groups), Aggregated vs Sequential Command Recommendation (GI 2025 — aggregated recommendation can improve task performance and reduced deliberation time), and three listing-level papers (Authoring LLM-Based Assistance, IUI 2025; XAIUI, TIIS — the site lists 2025, the DBLP record gives vol. 16, no. 2, 2026, DOI 10.1145/3774657, unresolved; Benchmarking XR Interfaces, UIST 2026) plus ISMAR 2023, DIS 2024, and ICMI 2024 at listing level. [C36700]

## The aesthetic logic

Lafreniere's logic is decomposition plus harvesting, and its visual consequence is legibility of state. Break a hard creative act into mind-size bites a person can hold; harvest the crowd's ordinary work and cut it into reusable units; coordinate many small contributions through a central model that knows the global state; treat error as a first-class event — detect, quarantine, rebuild, or mediate — rather than as an exception to hide. What the viewer sees, in every system, is a state made legible: done part / next part / not yet (M1), location then orientation (M2), kept segment / cut segment (M3), one attempt among a growing stack (M4), a population narrowing toward one form (M7). The picture is never the finished object alone; it is the record of the coordination that produced it. [C36701]

The failure mode is the mirror image, and DreamRooms states it outright: when the system deliberately encodes no objective measure of quality, the human's judgement becomes the objective function — which only works if the loop is tight, the choices are real, and the differences between iterations are visible. A loose loop with indistinguishable alternatives is not collaboration, it is a slot machine. Steal the legibility, not the apparatus: one global model, many small local instructions, and a picture in which the state of the work can always be read at a glance. [C36702]

## Rejections

Ten candidates were considered and rejected, so the prompts below are not a quota filled by lowering the bar. (1) A literal pavilion clone (12-foot bamboo tensegrity pavilion, same composition, same build sequence): a copy, not a new piece — LAF1 is the computational interpretation of the same idea and supersedes it. (2) A motion-amplifiers piece (seven dials deforming a drawn path): duplicate of KAZ3 (PP5706) and GRO2 (PP5697), already flagged build-only-one. (3) An energy-brushes piece (painted velocity fields, flow particles): duplicate of KAZ4 (PP5707) and GRO3 (PP5698), same flag. (4) A "follow the lights" wayfinding piece (LED guidance with no build): guidance without a structure is navigation — no picture results, no artifact accumulates; the light-as-address rule survives inside LAF1. (5) The Strategy Map (M8): the mechanism needs a corpus of many users' workflows; a single piece's one drawing is a timeline, not a strategy graph. (6) A Maestro piece: pacing a workshop has no visual consequence on a single canvas; DreamRooms folds into LAF4 instead, as supporting evidence for the same constraint-generation loop. (7) A Blocks-to-CAD piece: unlocking is a progression system, not a visual system, and a single canvas would reduce the bridge to unlocking tools. (8) A "daily build" schedule piece (one pavilion module per day, a feed of builds): a schedule, not a mechanism — the same failure as the #830 "daily shader" rejection. (9) A ToolClips help-video piece (short clips demonstrating a tool, shown in a gallery frame): instructional content, not a visual system — the clip mechanism survives as LAF2, where the clips are cut from the piece's own drawing. (10) A tap-to-add-variant sketch (early form of LAF3 in which each tap adds one attempt): tap-as-reseed in disguise — replaced by LAF3 as ranked, where attempts accumulate automatically and the tap is selection, which is the FollowUs mechanism.
