# Jean-Pierre Yvaral: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean-Pierre Yvaral, born Jean-Pierre Vasarhelyi, 25 January 1934 at Paris, France, died 2 August 2002 at Villejuif, France, aged 68 -- French op and kinetic artist, the last of the six GRAV co-founders, and the son who took his father's coded alphabet and turned it into a programme: the man who built pictures by numerical rule, stepped a single hue through fifty values, made a flat surface shimmer by crossing two line systems, and, from 1975, processed the most famous faces in the world until they were abstract compositions that still looked back at you. He trained in graphic art and publicity at the Ecole des Arts Appliques, Paris, in 1950-1953, worked in op art and kinetic art from 1954, co-founded the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel in 1960, and, after the group dissolved at the end of 1968, coined "Numerical Art" in 1975 and used the computer only as a calculating tool, finishing every work by hand in acrylic (C6801, C6814, C6815, C6901, C6903).

## The life: from the father's alphabet to the programmed portrait

Jean-Pierre Yvaral was born Jean-Pierre Vasarhelyi on 25 January 1934 in Paris -- the Norwegian and Belarusian Wikipedia entries place the birth more precisely in the 6th arrondissement -- and died on 2 August 2002 at Villejuif, aged 68 (C6801, C6815, C6819). He was the second son of Victor Vasarely, the pioneer of op art, and Claire (Klara) Spinner, a graphic designer and fellow student of Vasarely's at the Muhely workshop in Budapest; his elder brother Andre was born in 1931 (C6805, C6814, C6818). He was not an only child: the Artsper page that calls him the "fils unique" of Victor Vasarely is wrong, contradicted by the Vasarely Museum biography, which says Klara Spinner gave birth to two sons, Andre (1931) and Jean-Pierre, "called by the pseudonym Yvaral (1934-2002)" (C6805, C6811). The pseudonym is said to come from "Yves Aral", his first pseudonym, described as an anagram of his father's artist name; both sources for that derivation are secondary and undocumented, and no primary or museum source read states it, so it is carried as weak, not as fact (C6820). No source read gives a narrative childhood -- no school before 1950, no wartime experience, no home address -- and the one dealer line about growing up "in a complete artistic ambiance" is dealer copy, not used as fact (C6801, C6819). His mother is Klara Spinner in the museum's Hungarian form and Claire Spinner in the French form; her dates conflict, 1909-1991 from the museum against 1908-1990 from Wikipedia, and neither is settled here (C6805, C6806).

He studied graphic art and publicity at the Ecole des Arts Appliques in Paris between 1950 and 1953, and worked in op art and kinetic art from 1954 onwards (C6801, C6807). Dealer and gallery pages deriving from a single shared text say he began experimenting with geometrical abstract art during his studies and made his first completed works with the movement in 1955; the 1955 date appears only in that repeated dealer text, not in Wikipedia or Tate, and is reported, not independently confirmed (C6808, C6812). From 1959 he systematised his black-and-white research and adopted a more rigorous methodology, with free composition disappearing, according to the Rossille conference text (C6814). The arc the sources attest is abstraction first: he began to experiment with abstract constructions and movement from 1955, and no pre-abstraction figurative painting by Yvaral is named, dated or described in any source used here; the figuration that is attested is late, not early -- from the 1970s he processed instantly recognisable images until they became abstract compositions while the original remained recognisable (C6851, C6866).

In 1960 Yvaral co-founded the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel (GRAV) with Julio Le Parc, Francois Morellet, Francisco Sobrino, Horacio Garcia Rossi and Joel Stein, seeking to develop a coherent abstract visual language composed of simple geometric elements (C6801). The wider circle was larger than the six: in July 1960 twelve artists founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel, and the Centre was renamed GRAV in July 1961, from which point the members were the six named above; the six-name roster is the founder set, not the full membership, and the two lists must not be merged (C6802, C6905). GRAV's aim was to merge the individual identities of the members into a collective and individually anonymous activity, linked to scientific and technological disciplines and based around collective events called Labyrinths, picking up Vasarely's concept that the sole artist was outdated (C6802, C6905). The group was publicly launched in 1963 with the manifesto "Assez de mystifications" at the third Biennale of Paris, held its first Labyrinth there the same year, and won the Gold Medal at the 5th San Marino Biennale in 1963 (C6801, C6802, C6810). On 19 April 1966 GRAV created Une journee dans la rue in Paris, a day-long itinerary of kinetic activities for passers-by, and Yvaral held his first one-man exhibition at the Howard Wise Gallery, New York, in 1966 (C6802, C6808). His role inside GRAV, as far as the sources go, was as a co-founder and continuous member from 1960 to 1968, working on moire effects and a geometric language of forms; no source read singles him out as the group's leader, secretary, theorist or spokesman, and the group presented itself as collective and anonymous (C6802, C6814). GRAV's agreed dissolution was in November 1968, based on the members' recognition that it was impossible to maintain the rigour of a joint programme -- an agreed dissolution, not a split or expulsion (C6802, C6905).

After GRAV, Yvaral turned to colour in 1967-68, and colour had to obey the same systematic constraints as his elementary forms: modulable, combinable, permutable, following systematic laws of distribution (C6814). In the 1970s he built series on themes -- the Cubic Structures series evoking crystallographic networks or molecular arrangements, and the Structural Horizons series staging synthetic planetary landscapes -- and he won Gold Medals at the New Delhi Triennale and the Alexandria Triennale in 1971 (C6801, C6814). Public design work in this period was done with his father, not alone: the facade of the RTL group building at 22 rue Bayard, Paris, created in 1970 in collaboration with Vasarely, and the Renault logo, created in 1972 -- several secondary pages credit Yvaral alone with the Renault diamond, but the Vasarely chronology and the Fondation Vasarely visitor guide both say it was created by Vasarely with his son Yvaral, and the joint creation in 1972 is the version used here (C6814, C6820). In 1975 he coined the phrase "Numerical Art" to describe artwork composed, or programmed, according to numerical rules or algorithms (C6801). The method, step by step: from 1975 he experimented with digital art using an archaic tool -- slides, photoelectric cells and luxmeters -- making his first "digitalised" portraits, landscapes and monuments; from 1985 he used the computer to scan and process his images with algorithms, allowing permutational exploitation (C6814). The qualification matters and is stated by Rossille in so many words: the beauty of Yvaral's digitised images owes nothing to the computer; although ruled by numbers, it is not computer art; the computer was used only as a simple calculating tool, and the artist remained faithful to acrylic on large-format canvas (C6814). English Wikipedia agrees that from 1975 he used computers to digitally process and manipulate images, "although the final images were always hand painted" (C6801). He described himself in the 1970s as feeling closer to a biologist, a physicist and a mathematician than to a traditional painter, working "a little like in a laboratory" and experimenting with visual phenomena; his stated sources of inspiration were largely scientific -- numbers, polygons and polyhedra, molecular chemistry, crystallography, atomic structures, the universe and mathematical beauty in nature (C6814). The Marilyn series is dated 1990-1994, the "Mona Lisa synthetisee" exhibition was prepared in his Faubourg Saint-Antoine studio for the Pavillon des Arts, Paris, in 1984, and he was selected to represent France at Artec 90 in Nagoya in 1990 (C6811, C6812, C6814). He died on 2 August 2002 at Villejuif, survived by his widow Michele Taburno, his wife by a second marriage of 1977, and his only son Pierre Vasarhelyi, according to the 2013 Paris court judgment; the 4 August 2002 Paris version given by Artsper and Plazzart is the outlier and is not used, and no cause of death, burial place or funeral is attested anywhere (C6801, C6811, C6815). The identity of his first wife, the date of his first marriage, and which marriage Pierre was born into are all unattested (C6815).

## M1 -- The progression: a picture written as a rule

A progression in Yvaral's work is a picture built by a stated numerical rule applied step by step across a grid, not by freehand composition (C6851). Yvaral coined "Numerical Art" in 1975 to describe artwork composed, or programmed, according to numerical rules or algorithms, and from that time onwards he used computers to digitally process and manipulate images, although the final images were always hand painted (C6851). Mechanically, the rule operates on three things, each attested in the sources: a series, one motif repeated in an ordered sequence across the field, so that each step is a numbered position in the series; a permutation, the same elements re-ordered by rule, so that the same units produce a different picture when their order changes; and a colour progression, colour or tone stepped by rule from one value to the next across the series, so that the change itself is the subject (C6857). The titles are the programme written on the work: Variation Sur Le Carre (W01, 1959) is one square motif varied step by step by rule; Variation Chromatique Programmee (W06, 1965) is a chromatic variation that is programmed, colour stepped by rule across the panel; Structure en progression positif (W15, 1972) is a structure whose progression is positive, the step growing or brightening in one direction, so that the eye is pulled across the aluminium panel from the smaller or darker end to the larger or lighter end (C6860, C6868, C6857). The numbering is itself the logic: Acceleration Optique Serie 5 No. 32 (W05, 1964) is series, then number within the series, and Ambiguous Structure No.92 (W10, 1969) is a late entry in a long numbered series, the same structural problem re-run with a new parameter set (C6867, C6853). The exact numerical rule -- the formula, step size or permutation table -- for any single named work is unattested in the sources used here; the sources state that the works are composed according to numerical rules, but none publishes the rule for a named work, and none has been invented here (C6851).

**Takeaway:** write the rule first and the picture follows. The title is the programme, the number is the position, and the image is computed, never drawn.

## M2 -- Optical acceleration: the eye reads quickening

The Acceleration works are the progression as a sensation of speed. Acceleration 19, Series B (W02, 1962, plastic cord, plexiglass and wood, 60.5 x 61.8 x 8 cm, MoMA) is cords stretched over plexiglass and wood in a numbered series, so that the spacing of the cords accelerates across the field and the eye reads a speeding-up; "Acceleration" is the artist's own term for this stepped quickening (C6854, C6855). Accelerations Optiques (W04, 1963, metal and vinyl, 53.5 x 50 x 9 cm) is the three-dimensional version: vinyl elements repeated and spaced by rule over a metal ground, so that the eye reads quickening movement across a shallow relief -- viewed as the work itself, the image file present on the opened page (C6858). Kinetic Relief - Optical Acceleration (W03, 1963, Tate) pairs the two GRAV mechanisms in the title: a kinetic relief, a built layered surface, and optical acceleration, the illusion of movement in a still viewer (C6852, C6855). Structural Acceleree C (W13, 1970, screenprint on metal with nylon filament, edition of 150) joins the two mechanisms again -- "Structural", a built grid, and "Acceleree", accelerated -- with the letter C marking the third variant in the series, and the nylon filament standing proud of the metal screenprint so the printed grid and the physical thread interfere as the viewer moves (C6868). By the end of the 1960s he was making many paintings and screenprints with vigorous colour interactions and geometrical compositions, focused on producing optical acceleration effects that suggested movement, projection and recession (C6855). The Alon Zakaim overview states the mechanism plainly: his paintings use rigorous colour interactions and geometrical shapes to achieve optical illusions of spatial depth, and "optical acceleration" gives the artworks the illusion of having movement whilst the viewer is stationary, suggesting projection and recession within the work (C6855).

**Takeaway:** step the spacing and the still picture speeds up. The acceleration is not in the object; it is in the eye counting the steps.

## M3 -- Interference and moire: two systems crossing

Interference avec le cercle 'A' (W08, 1966) is the clearest attested example of moire as a progression, and the best-documented watchable behaviour in the whole catalogue. It is a three-dimensional sculptural relief: taut black string lines attached to a matt blackboard, edged with a simple black frame, numerous strings of black rubber and synthetic polymer rising above the surface, attached to a central black wooden pole (C6866). The source text describes exactly what the viewer sees: black string lines correspond with thin white-painted lines on the baseboard, which fan out from the centre to the edge to create a circle; when viewed from the side, the work coheres into a transparent cone; when seen from the front, the cone disappears and the circle evolves into a dial, revealing a figure-of-eight pattern which moves as the viewer moves; the interference is intensified by the disappearance and reappearance of the white lines as they intersect with the black (C6866). The work involves the spectator, whose physical movement animates the work, though the work itself remains static and has no moveable parts (C6866). Interference B (W09, 1967, kinetic relief, screenprint in white on black wood, metal tube and elasticated string, 62 x 62 x 25.5 cm) repeats the logic in a new material: the printed grid and the physical string interfere, the letter B marking it as the second in the series after the 1966 circle work (C6867). The moire effect, as the University of Sydney states, is a pattern created by superimposing multiple lines to interfere with visual perception (C6866). The same crossing logic runs through the GRAV years in the reliefs and multiples: cords, vinyl threads, plexiglass and screenprint layers that the viewer activates by moving (C6854). The watchable behaviour is in the viewer's eye and body, not in a moving part: the eye follows the stepped repetition and reads it as a slope or swell, moire interference where two grids cross makes a flat surface shimmer or shift, and colour steps read as recession or projection, so a flat square seems to bulge or sink (C6855).

**Takeaway:** lay one system over another and the picture appears in the crossing. Nothing moves except the viewer, and the viewer is the motor.

## M4 -- The ambiguous structure: one grid, two readings

Ambiguous Structure No.92 (W10, 1969, Tate) is the mechanism as a riddle the eye cannot settle: an "ambiguous structure" is a geometric construction that the eye can read two ways at once, so that the same grid flips between readings -- viewed as the work itself, the image file present on the opened Tate artwork page (C6853, C6855). Structure ambigue quadri OS (W16, 1973, acrylic on canvas, 80 x 80 cm) repeats the problem in four quarters: "quadri" marks a four-part construction, so that the same ambiguous grid is run in four quadrants that can be read in more than one way, and the code "OS" is a programme code, as in Polygamme H2 Vi-R (C6859). The number 92 marks W10 as a late entry in a long numbered series of ambiguous structures, meaning the progression here is serial: the same structural problem re-run with a new parameter set, numbered in sequence (C6853). Quadrature Diamant (W14, 1971, acrylic on canvas, 90 x 90 cm, reverse no. 1142) is the same family by another name: "Quadrature" names a square-based geometric construction, and "Diamant" names the rotated square at its centre, so that the progression is a square turning into a diamond by stepped rotation or scaling across the grid -- and its green colourway, Quadrature Diamant Vert (S08, 1972, acrylic on panel, 80.5 x 80.3 cm, reverse no. 1222), shows the colour progression as a variable on the same construction (C6867). Medium is unattested for W10 in the Tate pages viewed, and no colour is claimed for it from direct sight; the flip is in the structure, not in the palette (C6852, C6853).

**Takeaway:** build one grid the eye can read two ways and the picture never settles. The ambiguity is the work; resolving it would end it.

## M5 -- The colour progression: one hue, fifty steps

50 Shades of Blue (W12, 1970, screenprint, sheet 74.9 x 74.9 cm, edition of 300) is the purest colour-progression example in the catalogue: a single hue stepped through 50 values by rule, so that the eye reads depth and movement from tone alone, without a change of motif -- viewed as the work itself, the image file present on the opened page, the blue range claimed only from the title and the source text, not from direct sight (C6862). Polygamme H2 Vi-R (W11, 1969, oil on board, diptych, each board 32 3/4 x 28 1/4 in) is the same idea across a join: a polygamme, a many-ranged colour scale, applied across two boards as a diptych, so that the colour progression runs across the join between the two panels, and the code "H2 Vi-R" is a programme code, not a descriptive title, which fits the numerical-art method of naming by rule (C6870, C6851). The turn to colour itself was systematic, not decorative: in 1967-68 colour had to obey the same constraints as his elementary forms -- modulable, combinable, permutable, following systematic laws of distribution (C6814). Artsper states the same mechanism in plainer terms: his early research explores geometric forms, repetitions, contrasts, and colour interactions to produce effects of vibration and apparent movement, and his works play on illusion, rhythm, depth, and visual displacement (C6857). The colour progression is the mechanism that survives the move from black-and-white systematisation (from 1959) to the vigorous colour interactions of the late 1960s unchanged: the rule is the same, only the variable changes (C6814, C6855).

**Takeaway:** hold the motif still and step the colour, and tone alone makes depth. Fifty blues are not decoration; they are the rule, run on one variable.

## M6 -- The programmed portrait: the face that survives the grid

The late portraits apply the same grid-and-step rule to a face. JFK Lincoln (W17, 1976, lithograph on fine art paper, 81 x 105 cm, edition of 200) is the first portrait in the catalogue in which the progression is applied to a double face: two instantly recognisable images fused by the same grid rule, so that the eye must resolve which face it is seeing, and the resolution shifts with distance -- viewed as the work itself, the image file present on the opened page (C6865, C6857). Face of Dali - Grey (W18, 1977, screenprint, 88.9 x 62.2 cm, edition of 200) reduces Dali's face to a stepped grey grid, so that the portrait is legible at a distance and dissolves into abstract squares up close; the same title exists in a purple colourway (S09), which shows the colour progression as a variable: the same face, the same grid, a different colour rule (C6863). Mona Lisa (W19, c. 1979, colour serigraph on wove paper, 88 x 63 cm, edition of 100) is the most famous face in the catalogue subjected to the numerical treatment: the portrait remains recognisable while its surface becomes a stepped geometric field, and its blue lithograph variant, Mona Lisa bleue / La Joconde (S09, 1979), runs the same face through a different colour rule (C6864). Le Legende ("Marilyn") / Marilyn Numerisee (W20, 1990) is the end point of the arc: Marilyn Monroe's face, processed by the numerical rule until it becomes an abstract composition while the original image remains recognisable, in silkscreen per Artsy and acrylic on canvas per the Invaluable listing -- a medium conflict recorded, not resolved, because the sources may describe two different versions (C6861, C6867). The sources state that Marilyn Monroe's Op Art portraits by Yvaral are arguably his most distinguished and best-known works, and that he created several variations using Monroe's famed visage, each based on different patterns and digital manipulations (C6867). Artsper states the method in full: faces of personalities are subjected to geometric and digital transformations until they become abstract compositions, while retaining enough elements so that the original model remains identifiable -- a dialogue between figuration and abstraction, between familiar image and geometric construction (C6857). The arc is complete and it runs the other way from the usual story: abstraction first, then a programmed return to the recognisable face, not figuration first (C6851).

**Takeaway:** run a famous face through the rule and it survives as a face. The grid does not erase the portrait; it is the test of how little of a face a face needs.

## M7 -- The relief: the progression made physical

The GRAV-period works are progressions you can stand in front of and walk past. Acceleration 19, Series B (W02, 1962) is plastic cord, plexiglass and wood, a numbered series in three dimensions, held at MoMA (C6854). Structure optique en fils vinyliques transparents (S01, 1963-1964, Centre Pompidou) associates serigraph printed wood and nylon threads in a structure designed around optical and kinetic principles (C6857). Acceleration Optique Serie 5 No. 32 (W05, 1964) is a three-dimensional screenprint and collage multiple, published by GRAV itself, so that the printed grid and the built layer interfere (C6867). Structural Acceleree C (W13, 1970) is screenprint on metal with nylon filament, the filament standing proud of the metal so the printed grid and the physical thread interfere as the viewer moves (C6868). The materials are the point: cord, vinyl, plexiglass, nylon thread, metal tube, elasticated string -- the progression leaves the canvas and becomes a shallow relief, and the viewer's movement does what a motor would do in a kinetic sculpture (C6854, C6866). QAGOMA states that GRAV explored the role of the viewer in activating kinetic art through different types of mechanical movements and artificial light, and that the work of GRAV paved the way for interactive art, meaning art that requires the physical participation of the audience to achieve its aims (C6856). The reliefs are the hinge between the flat programmed paintings and the Labyrinths: the same rule, built in depth, activated by a body walking past (C6854, C6907).

**Takeaway:** build the rule in cord and thread and the wall becomes a machine. The depth is shallow and the movement is yours.

## M8 -- The computer as calculator: Numerical Art is not computer art

In 1975 Yvaral coined "Numerical Art" -- in French, art numerique -- to describe artwork composed, or programmed, according to numerical rules or algorithms (C6801, C6810). From 1975 he experimented with digital art using an archaic tool: slides, photoelectric cells and luxmeters, making his first "digitalised" portraits, landscapes and monuments (C6814). From 1985 he used the computer to scan and process his images with algorithms, allowing permutational exploitation, and Art Shortlist gives the same 1985 date for developing a technique with computer tools to structure his works (C6810, C6814). The computer never made the work. Rossille's qualification is the load-bearing sentence of the whole late career: the beauty of Yvaral's digitised images owes nothing to the computer; although ruled by numbers, it is not computer art; the computer was used only as a simple calculating tool, and the artist remained faithful to acrylic on large-format canvas (C6814). Wikipedia agrees that from 1975 he used computers to digitally process and manipulate images, "although the final images were always hand painted", and QAGOMA agrees that from the 1970s he used computer programming to digitally manipulate and process images while continuing to paint and create works by hand (C6801, C6807). Portrait digitalisee (S10, 1984, acrylic on canvas, 150.5 x 100.5 cm, verso no. BM 248) is the method as a single object: a digitised portrait, painted by hand, a metre and a half tall (C6867). He was a forerunner of digital art, working on computer-processed images from 1975 onwards under the name Numerical Art, and he was selected to represent France at Artec 90 in Nagoya in 1990 -- but the forerunner used the machine the way a surveyor uses a theodolite: to calculate, not to draw (C6916).

**Takeaway:** let the machine count and keep the hand for the painting. The number is the rule; the brush is still the author.

## The aesthetic logic: the son who programmed the inheritance

Yvaral's whole career is one argument, made in eight mechanisms: the father's alphabet, run by number. Vasarely patented his method of unites plastiques on 2 March 1959: permutations of geometric forms cut out of a coloured square and rearranged, worked with a strictly defined palette and forms -- three reds, three greens, three blues, two violets, two yellows, black, white, gray; three circles, two squares, two rhomboids, two long rectangles, one triangle, two dissected circles, six ellipses -- later enlarged and numbered, out of which he started serial art, an endless permutation of forms and colours worked out by his assistants; in 1963 he presented the palette to the public under the name Folklore planetaire (C6904). The plastic alphabet is therefore the father's system: a coded, numbered kit of units, not a single grid (C6904). The son's turn on it is dated and named: in 1975 he coined "Numerical Art" for artwork composed according to numerical rules, and from then on he used computers to digitally process and manipulate images, although the final images were always hand painted (C6903). Between the alphabet and the number sits the collective: GRAV's aim was to merge the individual identities of the members into a collective and individually anonymous activity, linked to scientific and technological disciplines and based around Labyrinths, and the group picked up Vasarely's concept that the sole artist was outdated (C6905). The son brought the father's coded kit into the collective that was built on the father's anti-individual creed -- that is the D3 synthesis, and it is synthesis, not quotation (C6903). Each mechanism moves the same inheritance one step further from the hand: the progression writes the rule before the paint (M1); the acceleration lets the eye do the moving (M2); the interference lets the viewer's body do the moving (M3); the ambiguous structure lets the eye do the deciding (M4); the colour progression lets tone do the moving (M5); the portrait lets the rule eat a face and leave it recognisable (M6); the relief lets the rule stand up in cord and thread (M7); the computer lets the rule be calculated and still painted by hand (M8). He described himself in the 1970s as feeling closer to a biologist, a physicist and a mathematician than to a traditional painter, working "a little like in a laboratory" -- and the laboratory is the right image, because the work is an experiment with a stated rule and a visible result, not a composition with a hidden hand (C6814).

The network is the argument's evidence. Vasarely gave him the alphabet (C6904). Stein gave him the cinema: filmmaker Henri-Georges Clouzot gave Stein and Yvaral carte blanche for the hallucinatory special effects of L'Enfer (1964, unfinished) and La Prisonniere (1968), the one documented one-to-one link between Yvaral and another GRAV founder besides the founding itself (C6906). The Denise Rene gallery is the hinge between father and son: Rene was first persuaded to enter the art business after meeting Vasarely at the Cafe de Flore in 1939, the gallery's inaugural show was "Drawings and Compositions by Vasarely", and Yvaral's own solo-exhibit list includes the Denise Rene Gallery, Paris -- though no dated Yvaral solo show there was found (C6910). Le Mouvement at the Galerie Denise Rene, 6-30 April 1955, instigated by Vasarely, is the exhibition context through his father, not through his own presence: Yvaral did not exhibit in Le Mouvement as a named participant in any source found this run (C6909). The Responsive Eye at MoMA, 25 February-25 April 1965, lists Yvaral among the artists to be shown, alongside Vasarely and GRAV -- an announcement listing, not proof of hanging, by the #617 Soto precedent, and no refusal or withdrawal was found (C6908). The adjacent artists map the same field from three sides: Hugo Demarco (1932-1995), fellow founder of the Centre in July 1960 who left before the July 1961 rename, whose optical vibration comes from superimposing complementary colours on a flat surface -- the still, painted cousin of Yvaral's programmed picture, and the top pick for #625 (C6911, C6912); Francois Molnar, Centre co-founder who left in November 1960 to study experimental psychology, the science of how the eye reads a picture -- the reserve for #625, his dates unattested (C6914, C6920); and Vera Molnar, adjacent only, already studied, whose machine imaginaire from 1959 and Molnart software with her husband run parallel to Yvaral's Numerical Art from 1975, not as a documented influence in either direction (C6915). No source found names a specific later artist as Yvaral's pupil or direct follower; what is documented is the forward direction of his own work -- Numerical Art as forerunner of digital art, MoMA and Tate holdings, Artec 90 (C6916). The progression that began as a square variation in 1959 ends as a programmed face in 1990, and nothing dated 1991-2002 was found: the last twelve years of his life leave no titled work in the sources used here (C6861, C6851).

**Takeaway:** the alphabet was the father's; the number was the son's. The picture is a rule, run by hand, and the eye is the last mechanism.
