# Karan Singh: Study Notes

**Site:** Karan Sher Singh is Professor of Computer Science at the University of Toronto and co-director of the Dynamic Graphics Project (DGP) lab. His own home page states his research programme as seeking "mathematical formulations of artistic and perceptual insight, to build tools for creative minds". His publication list spans 1998 to 2026 and names the notable systems: ilovesketch, meshmixer, crossshade, true2form, neobarok, flatfab, cassie. He is not the Australian illustrator of the same name (op-art / mid-century illustration, clients including NASA and Apple) — the two must not be conflated. [C36866]

**Relevance:** Singh's work asks what a single drawn stroke already knows about the solid it describes — which three-dimensional regularities a two-dimensional line implies, and how much of the form a system can infer before the drawer has to say anything more. The evidence is True2Form (SIGGRAPH 2014), which lifts one 2D design sketch into a 3D curve network by detecting and selectively enforcing planar, orthogonal, symmetric, and parallel regularities, and ILoveSketch (UIST 2008), in which a stroke's own timing — ink drying at twice the inter-stroke interval — decides when a curve is finished. [C36867]

**Doorway:** The 452nd doorway. Lafreniere ends on distributed making: when the making is spread across many hands, what must the system specify so the result is one coherent thing and not a hundred fragments — and where does the system end and the hands begin? Singh answers the opposite branch: his systems specify almost nothing and infer the rest. One hand, one stroke, and the system must guess what the stroke meant — the regularities the drawing already implies, the surface the cross-sections already claim, the parameter the silhouette already controls. The open question carried forward is: when the system supplies the consistency and the hand supplies only the sketch, where does the drawing end and the interpretation begin — and what does a stroke already know? [C36868]

**Depth:** Ten papers read in this study's legs — six end to end (Wires, SIGGRAPH 1998; A Suggestive Interface for Image Guided 3D Sketching, CHI 2004; ILoveSketch, UIST 2008; Analytic Drawing of 3D Scaffolds, SIGGRAPH Asia 2009; EverybodyLovesSketch, UIST 2009; True2Form, SIGGRAPH 2014) and four at abstract plus system plus evaluation level (Mid-Air Drawing, TOG 2021; CASSIE, CHI 2021; Piecewise-Smooth Surface Fitting, SIGGRAPH 2022; Squidgets, UIST 2025). The SecondSkin (SIGGRAPH 2015) and Juxtaform (SIGGRAPH 2023) abstracts were read in full by the coordinator from the DGP project page and the SIGGRAPH History Archive; the CrossShade abstract and project page were read in full. Thirty-plus works were inventoried from his publication list (1998–2026). Viewed as pixels: the True2Form teaser, results, and inspiration figures, and ILoveSketch paper pages 1 and 3. No video was watched by any leg; no software was run; no figure was rendered beyond those named; no work was handled in person. Three corrections to the brief are carried in this file: True2Form is SIGGRAPH 2014, not 2010; ILoveSketch is UIST 2008 by Bae, Balakrishnan, and Singh, not CHI 2008 by "Bae, Krikke, Singh"; SmoothSketch (Karpenko and Hughes, SIGGRAPH 2006) is not Singh's work and is not attributed to him. NeoBarok is listed on his home page among his notable systems, but no matching publication was found in the list read — it is dropped here, not described. All weekdays stated in this study were verified with `date -d` in America/New_York. [C36869]

## M1 -- Selective regularization (True2Form, SIGGRAPH 2014)

Subject: A 2D vector sketch — traced on a design drawing, for example Spencer Nugent's airplane — is lifted into a 3D curve network by detecting and selectively enforcing 3D regularity properties. The paper's gallery runs from a toothpaste tube to a camera, a mixer, a guitar, a gamepad, a shoe last, a coffee machine, a vase, and a boot. [C36872]

Visual principle: A drawing is a set of promises about regularity, and the viewer keeps only some of them. In the teaser figure the fuselage tangents look near-parallel in 2D but are not treated as parallel in 3D — a green cross marks the rejected cue. The legible image is the before/after histogram: a spread of angles collapsing into a spike at zero. The blue raw network settling into the black regularized one is the system visibly changing its mind, cue by cue. [C36873]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Two principles govern the reconstruction. Sketch fidelity: the 3D reconstruction should project back onto the input curves, with only small and evenly distributed foreshortening. Shape regularity: viewers imbue line drawings with depth by imposing regularity. The cues, all formulated as properties of the drawn curves: smooth-crossing orthogonality (smoothly crossing curves are perceived as orthogonal in 3D — the one cue the paper says always applies); local tangent parallelism; local symmetry; curve planarity; inter-plane parallelism and orthogonality; curve linearity. The cues are strict, not soft — artists and viewers "either locally expect full regularity or none" — and enforcing all plausible cues at once, or thresholding them all at once, produces distorted results. The algorithm is progressive selective regularization: each cue carries a likelihood in [0, 1] computed from the angle by which it is satisfied in the current 3D network, with a tight inner bound of 5 degrees mapping to likelihood 1 and a loose outer bound of 15 degrees mapping to 0, with a sharp monomial fall-off between them. Regularities enter the energy as soft terms weighted by likelihood (weight wc = 50); after each solver iteration, cues at likelihood 1 become hard constraints and cues at 0, or whose likelihood did not increase, are classified inapplicable; iterate until all cues in a class are classified — four iterations typically suffice per class. Classes resolve in a fixed order: tangent parallelism, then intersection orthogonality, then local symmetry (only at intersections already detected as orthogonal), then curve-level and inter-curve regularities together. The baseline reconstruction minimises fidelity subject to smooth-crossing orthogonality only, bootstrapped by lifting one arbitrary smooth-crossing into 3D and propagating with parallelism weight wp = 5. Fidelity energy weights: projection accuracy wa = 1, shape variation wv = 2, foreshortening wf = 0.001 (wa = 10 in the baseline stage). Reconstruction takes about 3 seconds for small models and just under 30 seconds for the most complex (camera). The only required user annotations are marking occlusions (2D crossings that are not 3D intersections, shown as dots) and optionally flipping the convex/concave interpretation; annotation took under five minutes even for the most complex results. The paper assumes orthographic projection because "the perspective in free-hand sketches is too inexact to meaningfully invert". [C36874]

Artistic opportunity: The regularization process is itself a drama with a visible scoreboard — histograms collapsing, blue curves snapping black, a rejected cue marked with a cross. No gallery piece shows a drawing being corrected by its own implied rules. The correction, not the final model, is the picture. [C36875]

**Takeaway:** Regularity is not imposed on a drawing; it is detected in it, cue by cue, and each detection is a visible decision with a visible cost (fidelity) and a visible rejection (the cross). The solver's timeline — orthogonality first, then parallelism, then symmetry, then the rest — is a ready-made dramatic arc, and the histogram is the scoreboard the gallery has never had. The perception study backs the transfer: the algorithm agreed with the human majority on 46 of 50 questions (n = 31), and none of its 4 disagreements had significant human consistency. [C36876]

## M2 -- Drying ink (ILoveSketch, UIST 2008)

Subject: Multi-stroke pentimenti curve sketching, as professional product designers actually draw: a curve is drawn lightly, repeatedly, darkening as it forms; the system fits each stroke with a cubic Bezier and shows the weighted average curve, finalising it when the ink "dries". One professional designer (12 years' experience, automotive/toy/film) built a spider (140 curves), a jet fighter (74), and a roadster (223 curves, 2.5 hours from scratch) in the evaluation. [C36877]

Visual principle: A curve is not drawn, it settles. In the paper's Figure 4 the estimated curve is pale violet-red while wet and animates to black as it dries — time is visible as colour. The intermediate strokes disappear when the curve finalises, so the picture is always one clean curve with the memory of its attempts baked into its shape. Repair strokes drawn over the overlap of two tangent curves join them smoothly; a new curve starting tangential to an existing one connects automatically — one step further — and a counter-clockwise roll gesture steps back. [C36878]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): State per curve is a set of strokes, each fitted as a cubic Bezier; the estimated curve is the weighted average of the strokes, with passing time reducing the weight of old strokes. Finalisation rule, exact: a lapse of time after a stroke is drawn — 2x the time between the last two strokes — automatically finalises the current curve, the estimated curve animating from pale-violet-red to black over that interval; a tap (period gesture) finalises immediately. Oversketching edits finished curves; strokes drawn over the overlapped part of two tangent curves join them smoothly; if a new settled curve starts tangential to an existing curve the two connect automatically, with a counter-clockwise roll gesture undoing one step. A virtual sketchbook metaphor supports flipping and tearing pages. The designer in the study used the tap to finalise more than the automatic drying, valuing speed over convenience, and disliked the automatic paper rotation — the system's one overreach. [C36879]

Artistic opportunity: Drying is a clock the viewer can read in colour, and the weighted average is a visible negotiation between attempts — the curve is the compromise of its own drafts. A piece that lets the viewer draw and then watches the drawing settle, dry, and join itself makes the act of correction the content. Nothing in the gallery makes a stroke's age visible as colour. [C36880]

**Takeaway:** The 2x rule is the whole mechanism in one number: the time a curve needs to dry is a multiple of the time the hand took to draw it, so the system's patience is calibrated to the drawer's own rhythm. That is the rule to carry — not "ink dries", but "drying time is twice the last interval", with the colour ramp as the visible clock. [C36881]

## M3 -- Sketchability and the page that turns itself (ILoveSketch, UIST 2008)

Subject: A view-dependent scalar, sketchability, that measures how good a given viewing angle is for a given 3D curve sketching method; the system automatically rotates the view to improve it. All 3D curve methods rest on epipolar geometry: a 2D stroke sweeps a stroke-surface of rays from the eye-point, and the 3D curve is the intersection of that surface with a sketch surface. Sketchability is visualised as the opacity of the selected sketch surface — a surface going faint means "bad angle", going solid means "draw now". Two hard numbers: auto-tumble animates the view along the gradient of increasing sketchability until it exceeds 0.8; implicit mode change switches back to the default single-view symmetric method after a view change if the current method's sketchability falls below 0.2. A pause after the first stroke of a multi-stroke curve is read as a cue of an uncomfortable paper orientation, and the sketchpad rotates toward the optimal orientation from the stroke's length, curvature, and orientation. [C36882]

**Takeaway:** Real mechanism, but ALREADY COVERED: RBK5 "The Page That Turns Itself" (PP5692, #815) prompts exactly this — the sketchbook whose page quietly turns itself to the best angle before each stroke lands, from the Balakrishnan study's section on ILoveSketch sketchability. Formalised here for the study's completeness; no new prompt from this study. [C36883]

## M4 -- Cross-section shading (CrossShade, SIGGRAPH 2012)

Subject: Concept sketches shaded in 3D by reading their cross-section curves. Cross-sections — drawn in orange in the paper's Figure 1 — are construction curves artists use to depict man-made objects; the system infers surface normals from them and renders the sketch shaded, in a variety of styles. The paper's image was used for the SIGGRAPH 2012 proceedings back cover. [C36884]

Visual principle: A handful of black lines plus a few orange cross-sections is enough for the eye to see a solid; the system makes that seeing explicit. The cross-hairs — where cross-sections intersect — are the load-bearing points: each one fixes a normal, and the normals propagate along and between curves until the whole drawing has a surface. The visual event is the moment a flat line drawing acquires light: the same lines, first unshaded, then shaded, with nothing added but the light the cross-sections implied. [C36885]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED in the abstract and project page; the paper body was not read): Cross-section curves depict intersections of the imagined 3D surfaces with 3D planes. The paper distills artistic guidelines for drawing cross-sections and insights from perception literature into an explicit mathematical formulation of the relationships between cross-section curves and the geometry they convey. The extracted 3D cross-sections define 3D surface normals at cross-hairs, which are then propagated along and in-between curves to estimate a normal field across the sketch; the normal field enables shading in a variety of rendering styles and setups. The exact propagation rule and normal-field equations were not extracted by this study — abstract level only. [C36886]

Artistic opportunity: The cross-section is the smallest possible claim about a surface — one curve saying "cut here" — and from a few such claims a whole shaded object follows. A piece that draws its own cross-sections and then lets light move across the implied surface makes inference visible as shading. No gallery piece shades a line drawing from curves the drawing itself contains. [C36887]

**Takeaway:** One orange curve is a complete surface hypothesis, and the shading is only the hypothesis made visible. The piece to build is not a shader; it is a drawing that earns its light, cross-section by cross-section, with the claim always preceding the light. [C36888]

## M5 -- Layered skins (SecondSkin, SIGGRAPH 2015)

Subject: Sketch-based construction of layered, shape-interdependent 3D volumes: a closed loop of strokes defines surface patches, and each new layer is built in relation to the layers beneath it. [C36889]

Visual principle: Form as onion, not as solid. A closed loop is a promise of a patch; a patch laid over an existing volume conforms to it, so each layer records the shape of the one below. The picture is the stack: outer skins translucent enough to show the inner forms they depend on, each layer visibly shaped by the one it covers. [C36890]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED in the abstract, read in full by the coordinator; the paper body was not read): The approach is built on three insights from an analysis of representative artist sketches. First, a closed loop of strokes typically defines surface patches that bound volumes in conjunction with underlying surfaces. Second, a significant majority of strokes map to a small set of curve-types that describe the 3D geometric relationship between the stroke and underlying layer geometry. Third, a few simple geometric features allow consistent classification of 2D strokes into that set of 3D curve-types. The algorithm processes strokes as they are drawn, identifies their curve-type, and interprets them as 3D curves on and around underlying 3D geometry, using other connected 3D curves for context. Curve loops are automatically surfaced and turned into volumes bound to the underlying layer. Stroke classification by 15 viewers on ground-truth sketches validates the curve-types and the classification algorithm. The paper is evaluated by a gallery of layered 3D models "that would be tedious to produce using current sketch modelers". [C36891]

Artistic opportunity: Layers that depend on each other are a different kind of drawing from a single surface: the piece can grow outward, skin by skin, each new skin a closed loop the viewer draws around the form so far. Peeling — removing a layer to reveal the one it was shaped by — is a second act no flat drawing has. [C36892]

**Takeaway:** The closed loop is the unit, and the bound volume is the consequence: a loop does not draw a shape, it claims a skin, and the skin is shaped by whatever it covers. The read-before-build flag stands in a milder form than first drafted — the abstract states the loop-to-bound-volume rule explicitly, but the curve-type set and the layer-construction details still need a full paper read before build. [C36893]

## M6 -- The stroke is the widget (Squidgets, UIST 2025)

Subject: Sketched widgets — curves that visually abstract a scene act as handles for direct scene manipulation. The user draws a stroke; it is partially matched against scene curves to select a squidget, and the stroke then drives the associated scene parameter in place. The project page's example: deforming an avocado's shape attributes to match its silhouette abstraction curve. Implemented in Maya; Honorable Mention at UIST 2025. [C36894]

Visual principle: The control is drawn on the thing it controls. There is no panel, no slider, no gizmo floating beside the scene — the curve that describes the avocado is the handle that reshapes it. The match is partial and visible: the user's stroke overlaps the scene curve, the squidget is selected, and the scene deforms as the stroke moves. The picture is a scene wearing its own controls as lines. [C36895]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED in the abstract, introduction, and framework section; the curve-similarity formulas were read at descriptive level, not re-derived): A squidget treats any visually perceived curve in a scene as a manipulation handle, framed as an inverse-rendering problem — compute a minimal change to scene attribute values so that some scene curve visually matches the over-sketched stroke. Three sub-problems: inferring the scene's visual abstraction curves (silhouette, ridge/valley, shading-contrast, and border curves, computed automatically by NPR/toon rendering); matching the stroke to a partial scene curve with a perceptual curve-similarity metric based on corner, spatial, and shape proximity (shape alone suffices when curve shapes are distinct; spatial proximity disambiguates cluttered scenes; nothing is selected if all matches are worse than a threshold); sampling the associated scene attributes around their present values to find the edit whose resulting curve best matches the stroke. Three curve kinds are handled homogeneously: abstraction curves (inferred), rig curves (pre-defined, for example gaze/jaw control curves on a facial rig), and bookmark curves (user-drawn strokes explicitly associated with a configuration of scene attributes; chained bookmarks interpolate continuously with weight w in [0, 1] over both curve and attributes). Evaluation (n = 12, within-subject): select-to-drag was most preferred (7 of 12), the traditional baseline preferred by 5 of 12; one-stroke manipulation was more mentally and physically demanding on hard move tasks. [C36896]

Artistic opportunity: A piece in which every control is a line in the picture itself — draw along a silhouette and the form follows your stroke — makes the interface disappear into the artwork. The interaction is earned because the stroke is the mechanism, not a trigger for it. [C36897]

**Takeaway:** The inversion is the point: instead of the user operating a control that changes a picture, the user draws the picture they want and the system solves for the change. The match threshold matters — "nothing is selected if all matches are worse than a threshold" is the rule that keeps a stray stroke from doing damage, and a piece should show that refusal. [C36898]

## M7 -- Divergence-convergence summary (Juxtaform, SIGGRAPH 2023)

Subject: Interactive visual summarization of large shape collections for conceptual shape design: the exploration, analysis, selection, and refinement of many shapes, integrated into one sketch-stroke-based divergence-convergence workflow. (Pandey, Chevalier, Singh, ACM Transactions on Graphics 42(4), July 2023.) [C36899]

Visual principle (interpretation from the abstract, not verified against the paper): Many candidate forms shown together, summarised so the designer can see the shape of the whole collection at once; a sketch stroke diverges the population toward new variants, a selection converges it. The picture is a population with a visible centre of gravity that the viewer can push around. [C36900]

Computational mechanism (abstract level only — the paper was not read): The abstract states that Juxtaform integrates exploration, analysis, selection, and refinement of large shape collections to support an interactive sketch-stroke-based divergence-convergence shape design workflow, and that it exploits sparse, segmented sketch-stroke visual abstractions, balancing understanding, in-situ juxtaposition, and clutter. No algorithm, parameter, or evaluation number from the paper is asserted here. Any prompt built on this mechanism therefore uses a disclosed procedural surrogate — parameter interpolation and mutation of a population — and must be retired at the gate if the gate requires the paper's algorithm itself. [C36901]

**Takeaway:** The divergence-convergence pair is a real workflow shape — a stroke opens the population, a selection closes it — but this study has not verified the mechanism that implements it. It is carried as the weakest prompt, honestly flagged, not as a proven system. [C36902]

## M8 -- The curve as a handle (Wires, 1998; Suggestive Interface, 2004)

Subject: The earliest form of the question. In Wires (Singh and Fiume, SIGGRAPH 1998) a curve is a deformation armature for existing geometry — the sculptor's armature made computational. In the Suggestive Interface (Tsang, Balakrishnan, Singh, Ranjan, CHI 2004) a scanned concept sketch is placed on orthographic construction planes inside a 3D cuboid, and drawn curves relax into the image under an active-contour energy, while the system offers its next geometry as a suggestion. [C36903]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): A wire is a tuple of a wire curve, a congruent reference copy, a radial scaling factor, a radius of influence, and a density function f(x) = (x squared - 1) squared, at least C1 and monotonically decreasing, with f(0) = 1 and f(x) = 0 for x >= 1. Binding finds the closest point on the reference curve for every object point; deformation scales radially, rotates by the angle between the wire and reference tangents, and translates by the influence-weighted displacement. The paper states explicitly that "projections of the wire curves provide a sketch-like representation of the object" — the curve-as-sketch idea is already present in 1998. In the Suggestive Interface, drawn curves are iteratively moved and resampled to minimise snake energy (internal bending/stretching plus image energy attracting to light/dark areas and sharp edges); gluing (drawing along a curve with the eraser end freezes that part) and pinning (press to set a pin point, drag to set a circular area of influence) let the user paint where the field may and may not act. Suggestions are of two kinds: algorithmic (closure when a curve exceeds a length and is not relatively straight — straightness threshold 70 degrees; extrusion when a curve intersects an existing curve on a different axial plane) and database (curve-signatures — tangent-continuous segments, lengths normalised by model length, stored with mean curvature and relative orientation; up to the top three matches shown in place). Unused suggestions fade after 10 seconds. Only three gestures, distinguished by pen pressure over 800 of 1024 units: stroke, cut (V-shaped), delete (N-shaped). [C36904]

**Takeaway:** The 2004 paper already contains the whole later programme in miniature: the reference as an energy field, the user painting where the field may act, and the suggestion as a ranked, in-place, self-expiring offer — never a mode or a menu. The 10-second fade is the ancestor of every later "the system offers, the hand decides" rule. [C36905]

## M9 -- The drawing declares its own rules (Analytic Drawing, 2009; EverybodyLovesSketch, 2009)

Subject: Two answers to the same question — how does the system infer the 3D the user meant, with the user doing as little mode-switching as possible? In Analytic Drawing of 3D Scaffolds (Schmidt, Khan, Singh, Kurtenbach, SIGGRAPH Asia 2009) the designer draws only lines parallel or perpendicular to existing lines, or connecting known 3D points, and the system infers a 3D scaffold of linear segments; the scaffold then constrains the sketching of 3D curves. In EverybodyLovesSketch (Bae, Balakrishnan, Singh, UIST 2009) the professional's tacit scaffold — tick marks, perspective grids — is imported as automation for novices: 49 high-school students, 47 of 49 creating meaningful 3D shapes. [C36906]

Computational mechanism (AS STATED): Analytic Drawing is a "pure-inference" interface: each candidate 3D line or curve is scored by a fitting function combining a proximity term, constraint priors, and a curvature score that rules out most spurious constraints; curves close to an elliptic arc are snapped to the arc. Right-angle markers give transient feedback — blue for 90-degree angles, red for near-perpendicular connections. A scratch-out gesture temporarily suppresses vertices from constraint collection. Six pilot users (two graphics researchers, two designers, two architects) reached similar proficiency within a few hours; architects were the most comfortable. The paper's own finding, worth carrying: after short training, subjects "could not adequately explain" how the system was interpreting their curves — the inference was opaque to its users, and additional training improved construction significantly. EverybodyLovesSketch adds tick-based plane selection (one tick: horizontal plane; two: rotated vertical plane; three: arbitrary plane), angled flick for arbitrary extrusion, multiple extruded surfaces, copy-and-project, a freeform NURBS sketch surface from a closed loop with an area-fill gesture, and an interactive perspective grid. The paper's own count from Scott Robertson's demo videos: guide lines at about 52% of design curves, tick marks at about 59%, paper adjustments at about 75%. [C36907]

**Takeaway:** Two opposite transfers of the same craft knowledge: Analytic Drawing makes the drawing's own rules the inference (the constraint set is the scaffold, built stroke by stroke from the same marks it will later constrain), while EverybodyLovesSketch imports the professional's habits as automation. The first is the stronger artistic idea — a drawing that declares its own rules as it goes — and the second is a warning: when the system does the tacit work for the user, the user stops understanding what the system is doing, and the piece must show its reasoning or it becomes a black box. [C36908]

## The arc: who does the work

The 27 years read here trace one sustained argument: a drawn line is already a 3D instruction, and the research problem is only ever which half of the instruction does the work. Early work (1998–2004) has the human do it — the curve is a handle or an energy field the user drives. Middle work (2008–2014) negotiates the split: ILoveSketch gives the user an explicit sketch surface plus a scalar that manages view and mode; Analytic Drawing makes the drawing's own rules the inference; True2Form removes the user from inference almost entirely, with the human's perceptual regularities becoming the algorithm. Recent work (2021–2025) asks a different question again: the 3D is already there, and the stroke acts on it — mimicry beats raycasting in mid-air drawing (equi-parameter distance 1.13 mm versus 2.31 mm, p < .001, though slower, 5.29 s versus 4.69 s); CASSIE neatens strokes into a connected armature and surfaces it (lambda = 0.6, tangent threshold pi/6, median 2 subsets tested per stroke); Squidgets treats any visible curve as a handle and the stroke as a desired state to solve for. The stroke has moved from creating geometry, to declaring geometry, to commanding geometry. One thread is constant: the curve is the unit of thought in every paper — tangency, intersection, symmetry, planarity, proximity — never pixels or meshes first. [C36909]

## The aesthetic logic

Singh's systems share a visual grammar that is directly portable: paper white, ink black, and one accent colour that always means the same thing (wet ink pale violet-red, raw curves True2Form blue, cross-sections CrossShade orange, the selected squidget warm). The system always shows its mind: sketchability as opacity, drying as a colour ramp, regularization as collapsing histograms, suggestions that fade after 10 seconds if unused. Nothing is hidden behind a panel; the reasoning is drawn on the drawing. The pieces this study prompts should keep that discipline — the inference visible, the palette restrained to the paper's own colours, the interaction always a stroke, a drag, or a scrub of the system's own timeline, never a menu. [C36910]
