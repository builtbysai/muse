## 1. The boy with the camera: origin

Spielberg was the eldest child and only son of four children, born to Leah Adler (nee Posner, 1920-2017), a concert pianist who ran a kosher dairy restaurant, and Arnold Spielberg (1917-2020), an electrical engineer involved in the development of computers; his paternal grandparents were Ukrainian Jews, and his grandmother in Cincinnati taught English to Holocaust survivors, who in turn taught him numbers from the tattoos on their forearms. [C32712] The family moved in 1952 to Haddon Township, New Jersey, after his father was hired by RCA, and in early 1957 to Phoenix, Arizona, where he was a target of antisemitism in high school ("I got smacked and kicked around. Two bloody noses. It was horrible."); his father lost between 16 and 20 relatives in the Holocaust, and the Holocaust "was always on my mind" at home. [C32713] His first movie experience was Cecil B. DeMille's The Greatest Show on Earth (1952); terrified by the train crash, at age twelve he recreated it with his Lionel trains and filmed it -- his first home movie, and the scene he would restage sixty years later as the centerpiece of The Fabelmans. [C32714] A Boy Scout from 1958, Eagle Scout at thirteen, he made the nine-minute 8mm Western The Last Gunfight for a photography merit badge, and at thirteen the forty-minute war film Escape to Nowhere with classmates, which won first prize in a statewide competition; he made about fifteen to twenty 8mm adventure films in total. [C32715] The films he cites as formative are a curriculum in themselves: Captains Courageous (1937), Disney's Pinocchio and Fantasia (1940), Kurosawa's Rashomon and Seven Samurai, Godzilla, King of the Monsters! (1956), David Lean's Bridge on the River Kwai and Lawrence of Arabia (1962 -- "the film that set me on my journey"), Hitchcock's The Birds (1963), Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove and 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). [C32716] Firelight (1964), a science-fiction feature he wrote and directed as a teenager in Phoenix and funded mainly by his father, was shown in a local theater for one evening and grossed $501 against a $500 budget; scenes from it were later reused, shot for shot by some accounts, in Close Encounters. [C32717] The Universal apprenticeship is the founding myth and it is documented: he jumped off the studio tour bus, hid in a bathroom, met Chuck Silvers, who ran the Universal library; Silvers asked to see his shorts and gave him a three-day pass, and on the fourth day the guard waved him in without a pass, and he spent the next two months at Universal as an unofficial apprentice that summer. [C32718] He applied to USC film school and was turned down because of mediocre grades, enrolled at California State University, Long Beach, and in 1968 Universal gave him the opportunity to write and direct the twenty-six-minute 35mm short Amblin' for theatrical release; studio vice president Sidney Sheinberg was impressed and offered a seven-year directing contract, and a year later he dropped out of college to direct television for Universal, making him the youngest director signed to a long-term plan with a major Hollywood studio. [C32719]

**Takeaway:** The origin is not a film school but a train set: a boy who learned to stage a crash with toys, then spent a summer sneaking into a studio to watch professionals do it, then got hired to do it himself -- the whole career is the same gesture at larger scale.

## 2. Duel: the prototype

Duel (1971) is the directorial debut feature, adapted by Richard Matheson from his own short story (Playboy, April 1971), based on a real 1963 road-rage incident, about a salesman (Dennis Weaver) chased by a psychotic tanker-truck driver across the Mojave Desert; it aired as an ABC Movie of the Week on 13 November 1971, then was extended with extra scenes for international theatrical release, and Spielberg called it "Godzilla vs. Bambi". [C32720] The budget was $450,000 by the Wikipedia infobox ($425,000 and a sixteen-day schedule per TCM; an eleven-day shooting window and a $5,000 director's fee per indepthcine -- the figures conflict and are carried as a conflict), and the film was produced by Universal Television with cinematography by Jack A. Marta and editing by Frank Morriss. [C32721] The truck is personified as a face -- split windshield as eyes, headlights as pupils, front grille as nose, fenders as mouth -- and the driver is never seen except for insert shots of a brawny waving arm and pointed cowboy boots: the monster is a machine with a face, and the man inside it does not exist. [C32722] Spielberg cut roughly fifty percent of the script's dialogue and told the story visually; he blocked the whole film on IBM cards, one per scene with angle and setup count, and used up to five cameras for coverage -- TV-trained economy (shot lists, multi-camera coverage, visual storytelling over dialogue) applied to a feature. [C32723] The professional debut that preceded it was "Eyes" (1969), a Night Gallery segment scripted by Rod Serling and starring Joan Crawford, followed by episodes of Marcus Welby, M.D., The Name of the Game, Columbo (the show's inaugural non-pilot episode, "Murder by the Book"), Owen Marshall, Counselor at Law, and The Psychiatrist; a formative encounter with John Ford taught him about horizon placement in the frame. [C32724] Two more TV films followed Duel -- Something Evil (1972, CBS) and Savage (1973, NBC) -- and the theatrical debut was The Sugarland Express (1974), based on a true story of a married couple on the run to regain custody of their baby, which won Best Screenplay at the 1974 Cannes Film Festival but was not a commercial success; Pauline Kael called him "a born entertainer -- perhaps a new generation's Howard Hawks." [C32725] The Sugarland Express is also the first of many collaborations with composer John Williams, and its signature is sustained moving-car choreography -- a growing convoy of police cars trailing the couple's car across Texas highways, shot as a rolling, ever-expanding procession rather than a chase (search-extract for the convoy description). [C32726]

**Takeaway:** Duel is Jaws in miniature: an unseen menace, a machine given a face, dialogue cut in half, the story told by blocking -- the whole grammar of the blockbuster is already there, at $450,000, on the Mojave.

## 3. Jaws: the broken shark and the invention of withholding

Jaws (1975), based on Peter Benchley's bestseller, was the first film shot on open ocean; the malfunctioning mechanical shark forced Spielberg to suggest the shark's presence indirectly, which he said made a better movie; the schedule overran by 100 days and Universal threatened to cancel, and it set the domestic box-office record, won three Academy Awards (film editing, John Williams's score, sound), and effectively created the summer-blockbuster genre. [C32727] The budget was $9 million by the Wikipedia infobox, originally estimated at $3.5 million for a fifty-five-day shoot, and the shoot at Martha's Vineyard ran May to October 1974, stretching to 159 days; the box office is $495 million by the infobox, against $458 million (EBSCO) and $477.9 million in other tallies, the variance being re-release accounting, and the film was released to more than 450 screens with $700,000 in TV ads, an exceptionally wide release for the time. [C32728] The shark appears for only about four minutes total in the whole film and is barely glimpsed for the first half -- no close-ups, no massive jaws -- yet the fear is everywhere, and the withholding was forced by the three full-scale mechanical "Bruce" models constantly breaking down in salt water, a constraint Spielberg turned into the film's defining technique. [C32729] With no working shark he shot around its absence: underwater point-of-view shots from below, the barrels Quint, Brody and Hooper attach to track the shark without ever showing it in full, and Williams's two-note theme doing the emotional work the creature could not; Spielberg later said he had no choice but to figure out how to tell the story without the shark. [C32730] The first half is full of deliberate fake-outs -- kids with a cardboard fin, a shadow, nothing at all -- and each red herring primes the audience to expect the worst the next time someone goes near the water, cranking anxiety incrementally so the real attack hits harder. [C32731] The dolly zoom on the beach, as Brody realizes a shark attack is happening, keeps Brody the same size in frame while the beach behind him stretches away, externalizing shock and guilt; sources disagree on the direction of the move (dolly in while zooming out, or track away while zooming in), and the effect is consistent across sources -- the technique was invented for Hitchcock's Vertigo (1958) and Spielberg repurposed it from fear of heights to a moment of horrified realization. [C32732] After Brody's "You're gonna need a bigger boat" line the camera stays on Brody's face and never cuts back to the shark -- the reaction is the event, and the spectacle is withheld even at the moment of revelation. [C32733] Quint's USS Indianapolis monologue is delivered with no music and no shark, just a man remembering something horrific; the absence of score is itself the technique, and silence becomes the loudest device in the film. [C32734]

**Takeaway:** The broken shark is the luckiest accident in film history: a machine that would not work forced a director to show nothing, and showing nothing turned out to be scarier than any shark -- withholding is not a budget compromise, it is the method.

## 4. The Spielberg oner: the invisible long take

Tony Zhou (Every Frame a Painting) coined the term for Spielberg's signature: a single unbroken take that contains several different shots inside it (push-in, two matching singles, an insert) through complex blocking, rather than a showy virtuoso move, and the defining rule is that it is supposed to be invisible -- the audience should feel the scene unfolding, not the camera hitting marks. [C32735] His long takes typically run under three minutes, most often in the one-to-two-minute zone -- long enough to cover an entire scene, short enough to keep pace brisk -- and unlike directors who use long takes as a formal signature, Spielberg uses them for emotional effect and disguises them so the audience is immersed in the drama without feeling the camera's kinetic energy. [C32736] The camera never moves unmotivated: in the oner examples (Raiders of the Lost Ark, Minority Report) the camera always follows a motion or an action -- a character walking, turning, reaching -- so the movement reads as caused by the scene rather than by the operator, and the blocking of the actors is fluid while the shot shifts which character it favors mid-take. [C32737] The Raiders example is a single unbroken ninety-second take that combines a push-in, two matching singles, and an insert -- four conventional shots fused into one moving master. [C32738] In the Jaws ferry scene the cameraman barely moves; all the movement is in the actors' blocking and in the choice of location, shooting on a real ferry so the shifting background action keeps the pace snappy and the viewer always has something new to look at. [C32739] Spielberg tracks characters laterally but enriches the frame by placing objects and extras between the camera and the subjects, increasing the visual perception of movement, and a variant is that after tracking, the actors approach the camera and end in a close-up -- in Jaws, the camera tracks Brody and his wife toward the boat. [C32740] In Munich he establishes the spatial relationships among four characters and an entire assassination plan in a single unbroken eighteen-second shot: a wide shot of a phone booth pans to follow a moving vehicle, racks focus to an operative at a newsstand, and tilts up to an apartment window revealing where the explosion will be -- geography established by camera movement and rack focus instead of cutting. [C32741] He was an early adopter of the Steadicam, using it on Close Encounters for the indoor UFO reveal sequence and refining it on Raiders for complex running and gunfight sequences; he and Kaminski stage with foreground, middle ground and background all meaningful, deep focus keeping multiple planes of action sharp, so the audience absorbs the scale of a scene while following individual characters. [C32742]

**Takeaway:** The oner is a master shot that refuses to admit it is a master shot: four shots smuggled inside one, the camera moving only when the scene moves it, the audience never noticing the trick -- invisibility is the virtuosity.

## 5. The Spielberg face and the child's eye

The signature shot is a character looking slightly upward, mouth slightly open, eyes wide, overwhelmed by what they are seeing -- often a low-angle close-up preceded by a slow dolly-in -- and Spielberg builds scenes around the reaction and shoots reaction coverage with the same care as action, holding on the face instead of cutting back to the spectacle. [C32743] The face comes before the reveal: the reaction shot shows the audience the event through its reflection on a human face, the face arrives first and the object of wonder second, so the audience feels the scale of the unseen thing through the person seeing it -- Elliott and E.T. seeing each other for the first time, Grant and Sattler seeing the first living dinosaur. [C32744] In Jurassic Park the dinosaurs appear for roughly fifteen minutes of screen time by one account (about six minutes of CGI dinosaurs by another -- the figures conflict and are carried as a conflict), yet the film works because the characters' reactions are believable and sustained; the awe is measured in reaction shots. [C32745] E.T. was shot mostly from a child's eye level so the audience connects with Elliott and sees the world as a naive child does; adults are frequently shot from the waist down, from knee height, their authority coming entirely from how big they loom next to the kids, and editor Carol Littleton confirms Spielberg and cinematographer Allen Daviau deliberately lowered the camera to a child's point of view, capturing a worldview in which adults exert control or indifference over small people. [C32746] E.T. stays inside what Elliott understands: the alien, the threat and the family's grief all reach the audience through a ten-year-old's head, and when Elliott finds E.T. in the cornfield the audience works it out slowly with him. [C32747] Spielberg shot much of E.T. in chronological order, rare in filmmaking, to create more realistic, emotional reactions from the child actors, and he eschewed storyboards and shot roughly in sequence so the actors' performances would be authentic. [C32748] Over-the-shoulder shots come in two variants: the dramatic variant uses a wide lens so the foreground character looks bigger than the other, conveying dominance, and the claustrophobic variant pushes the shoulder further into frame, shoving the main subject away from center; and he repeatedly composes with physical objects -- mirrors, windows, doors, power lines -- dividing the frame, in Jurassic Park becoming a scale device, a raptor seen through a window, a human point of view framed by everyday objects while a dinosaur intersects it. [C32749]

**Takeaway:** The face is the special effect: show the wonder on a person first and the audience will supply the wonder itself -- the monster is optional, the reaction is not.

## 6. Close Encounters: light as character, five notes as language

Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977), after he declined Jaws 2, was shot in 65mm, cast Francois Truffaut as scientist Claude Lacombe, worked with Douglas Trumbull, and was the first of many collaborations with editor Michael Kahn; it won Oscars for cinematography (Vilmos Zsigmond) and sound effects editing, and Kael called Spielberg "a magician in the age of movies." [C32750] The premise is Indiana lineman Roy Neary (Richard Dreyfuss), single mother Jillian Guiler, and Lacombe converging on Devils Tower, Wyoming, for humanity's first contact; Spielberg holds the sole screenplay credit, with uncredited help from Paul Schrader, John Hill, David Giler, Hal Barwood, Matthew Robbins and Jerry Belson, and the budget was $19.4 million against a $306.9 million box office. [C32751] The mothership was designed by Ralph McQuarrie and built by Greg Jein, its look inspired by an oil refinery Spielberg saw lit at night in India, the emphasis on luminescence rather than Star Wars-style hardware; one UFO model was literally an oxygen mask with lights attached, Dennis Muren hid a tiny R2-D2 (and a pea-sized TIE fighter) on the mothership model, and clouds were made in a tank by layering fresh water over salt water and injecting paint, effects shot in 65mm for compositing quality. [C32752] Humans and aliens communicate using a five-note motif (D-E-C-C-G); Williams and Spielberg reportedly went through about 350 five-note phrases before settling on it, the idea being that it should sound like a greeting, not a melody, and the motif functions both as score and as the in-story communications signal -- humans offer an oboe variation, the mothership answers in deep tuba tones, building a conversational duet between two civilizations. [C32753] The score moves from atonal, eerie textures (Ligeti-like dissonance, disembodied voices) to a glowing finale that borrows "When You Wish Upon a Star", and the title comes from J. Allen Hynek's UFO classification system. [C32754] The Special Edition (1980) added the mothership-interior scene Spielberg later regretted, and the Director's Cut (1998) removed it -- the one time he showed the inside of the wonder and took it back. [C32755] The climax stages first contact as a vast, glowing mothership descending over Devils Tower, with the five-note musical exchange as the greeting -- wonder rendered as scale plus sound, after a film built on small lights in the sky and abductions in the night (the "scale plus sound" reading is leg synthesis of the documented motif and reveal; the reveal itself and the motif are verified). [C32756] This is his first fully authored spectacle -- sole writer-director, a personal obsession (a childhood meteor shower with his father; scenes reused from his lost 1964 feature Firelight) -- and it establishes the Spielberg pattern: ordinary people, wonder, light as a character. [C32757]

**Takeaway:** First contact is not a battle or a speech but a music lesson: five notes, repeated until two civilizations can say hello -- language is the special effect, and light is the set.

## 7. 1941 and Raiders: the humbling, then the discipline

1941 (1979), an action-comedy written by Robert Zemeckis and Bob Gale about Californians preparing for a Japanese invasion after Pearl Harbor, was conceived as an $11 million comedy and ballooned to a $35 million budget, grossing $94.9 million worldwide -- a moderate gross that read as failure after Jaws and Close Encounters -- and was disliked by most critics and studio heads. [C32758] Spielberg later admitted he "did 20 takes on inserts that should have been done by a second unit" and became "so precious and indulgent about getting everything right", and the production ran over schedule and out of control; he called it the greatest lesson of his career: "by the time I did Raiders of the Lost Ark, my next picture, I was humbled. Every single shot was storyboarded. I was 14 days under schedule." [C32759] Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981), screenplay by Lawrence Kasdan based on a story by George Lucas and Philip Kaufman, is a homage to 1930s-40s serials starring Harrison Ford as Indiana Jones; it won five Academy Awards, was the highest-grossing film of 1981, and its budget was $20 million by the Wikipedia infobox against $18 million in several secondary sources (conflict carried, not settled). [C32760] The film was built set-piece-first: Lucas, Kasdan and Spielberg spent three to five days in story sessions generating the set pieces -- the boulder trap in the Peruvian temple, the monkey in Cairo, Toht burning the medallion's imprint into his hand, the Ark crated in a vast warehouse -- and Kasdan then wrote the connective narrative between them. [C32761] The opening idol-swap and boulder sequence is the purest example: a self-contained trap mechanism (idol weight triggers darts, then the boulder) that teaches the hero's character without dialogue, and the film was shot June to September 1980 at Elstree Studios plus La Rochelle, Tunisia, Hawaii and California. [C32762] Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom (1984), darker than its predecessor, led to the creation of the PG-13 rating; Spielberg said he was unhappy with it because it lacked his "personal touches and love", yet it was a blockbuster that won the Oscar for visual effects, and on this project he met his future wife, Kate Capshaw. [C32763] Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade (1989) cast Sean Connery as Jones's father and returned to family-friendly fare after complaints about Temple of Doom's violence, grossing $474 million, his biggest hit since E.T.; Always (1989) followed, a remake of his childhood favorite A Guy Named Joe (1943), and the final film role of Audrey Hepburn. [C32764] The Indiana Jones idea itself was born on a Hawaii beach before the Star Wars premiere in May 1977: building sandcastles, Spielberg said he wanted to direct a James Bond film, and Lucas pitched his long-held archaeologist and treasure-hunter idea; Spielberg introduced Lucas to John Williams, and convinced Lucas Star Wars would be a massive cultural and commercial success. [C32765]

**Takeaway:** Failure is the storyboard: 1941 taught him that indulgence costs schedule, Raiders proved that a film built as a chain of traps, each one a little machine, can run on time and on budget -- discipline is not the enemy of wonder, it is the delivery system.

## 8. E.T.: suburbia, divorce, and the child who stays

E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), written by Melissa Mathison, is a boy, Elliott (Henry Thomas), befriending a stranded alien; Spielberg eschewed storyboards and shot roughly in sequence so the actors' performances would be authentic, and the film grossed $797.3 million worldwide on a $10.5 million budget, won four Academy Awards (score, sound, sound editing, visual effects), and surpassed Star Wars as the highest-grossing film of all time, a record it held eleven years until Jurassic Park. [C32766] The concept grew from an imaginary friend Spielberg invented after his parents' divorce, developed from the unrealized project Night Skies; Columbia rejected it, Universal bought the script for $1 million, and E.T. was the first film character to be a finalist in TIME's Man of the Year sweepstakes. [C32767] The E.T. puppet cost $1.5 million, designed by Carlo Rambaldi, capable of 85 movements and 35 facial expressions, three feet tall, run by two control systems (puppeteers plus an electronic system for mannerisms like wrinkling its nose); cinematographer Allen Daviau shot largely at a child's eye level in soft, glowing light, and the silhouette of Elliott and E.T. flying past the moon became the Amblin Entertainment logo. [C32768] The bicycle against the moon works as pure silhouette: small figures, huge moon, no detail, the E.T. theme playing in full during the chase -- a single iconic image of flight and wonder that has become the film's visual signature. [C32769] Spielberg's parents divorced when he was sixteen and he has returned to the same themes for five decades; the divorce is famously the inspiration behind E.T., and distant fathers are at the center of Close Encounters, Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, Hook, and War of the Worlds -- "Every one of my movies is a personal movie... I don't make films that I don't consider to have something of myself left behind in them." [C32770] In the HBO documentary Spielberg he said: "My main religion was suburbia. Of course, it was all false." -- his suburban kids are comfortable but haunted by the suspicion that they are surrounded by close-minded, reactionary adults, and E.T. was initially not even supposed to feature an alien, just a movie about a lonely, isolated child of divorce. [C32771] Editor Carol Littleton: Spielberg told the crew he had a make-believe buddy who consoled him at that moment, and the film is a portrait of childhood resilience; nearly every one of his films is, in some subterranean way, about his parents' divorce, and many of his most iconic films are haunted by absent fathers, dads abandoning their children for obsession, for pride, for madness, for infidelities large and small. [C32772]

**Takeaway:** The alien is a divorce made visible: a lonely child invents a friend, the friend has to phone home, and the most personal film he ever made outgrossed the spectacle -- smallness, shot at knee height, beats bigness.

## 9. Amblin: the impresario and the house style

In 1980 Spielberg, Kathleen Kennedy and Frank Marshall founded Amblin Productions (the CNN-derived "Fast Facts" timeline says "1984 -- Founds Amblin Entertainment", a discrepancy not resolved from the pages opened; both may be true, 1980 production company versus 1984 formalized company); the first film it produced was Continental Divide (1981), and it went on to produce Gremlins (1984), The Goonies (1985), Back to the Future (1985), Who Framed Roger Rabbit (1988), Twister (1996), Men in Black (1997) and The Mask of Zorro (1998), plus Don Bluth's An American Tail (1986) and The Land Before Time (1988). [C32773] His first film as executive producer was Robert Zemeckis's I Wanna Hold Your Hand (1978); Zemeckis had come to his attention by winning a Student Academy Award at USC for his short A Field of Honor (1973), and Spielberg executive-produced his first two films (1978, 1980) and Back to the Future (1985) after it was rejected by every major studio, and the two later executive-produced Monster House (2006), marking their eighth collaboration. [C32774] Between 1984 and 1990 Spielberg served as producer or executive producer on nineteen films, and in the early 1980s he befriended Warner Communications chief executive Steve Ross, leading him to make films for Warner Bros.; in 1992 he began scaling back producing, saying "Producing has been the least fulfilling aspect of what I've done in the last decade." [C32775] Kathleen Kennedy met Spielberg while working as assistant to John Milius on 1941 (1979); Spielberg hired her as his assistant, she was associate to Spielberg on Raiders (1981) and associate producer on Spielberg-produced Poltergeist (1982), co-founded Amblin Entertainment with Spielberg and Frank Marshall in 1981 (some sources date the formal company to 1982), and her first film as producer was E.T. (1982); she produced or executive produced most Spielberg films for the next three decades and was president of Lucasfilm 2012-2026. [C32776] The television producing runs parallel: Amazing Stories (NBC, 1985, $1 million per episode, creative control, cancelled after two seasons), Animaniacs, Pinky and the Brain, ER (1994), Band of Brothers (2001) and The Pacific (2010) with Tom Hanks. [C32777] The Amblin house style is E.T.'s camera lowered to knee height and its suburbia made strange: the bicycle against the moon became the company logo, and the films it produced are, again and again, children and wonder in a suburb that is comfortable and haunted at once. [C32778] J.J. Abrams is the second-generation case: in 1982 Abrams, fifteen, and Matt Reeves were featured in a Los Angeles Times article about teen Super 8 filmmakers, Kennedy phoned them and hired the pair to repair and restore Spielberg's own teenage 8mm films (Firelight, Escape to Nowhere), and twenty-nine years later Super 8 (2011), written and directed by Abrams and produced by Spielberg, restaged the master's childhood as a summer-1979 valentine to his late-seventies and early-eighties films. [C32779] Zemeckis said Spielberg as a producer would always "respect the filmmaker's vision", and the mentorship is the clearest "Spielberg made a career" case in the network: the mentee who wrote his humbling (1941) and then made the time-travel film every studio rejected, with Spielberg's name on it. [C32780]

**Takeaway:** The studio is the piece: Amblin is E.T.'s camera height turned into a company, a house that makes other directors' wonder -- and the boy who sneaked into Universal ends up hiring the next boy who sneaks in.

## 10. The serious turn: Color Purple, Empire of the Sun, Schindler's List

The Color Purple (1985), an adaptation of Alice Walker's novel and his first film on a dramatic subject, starring Whoopi Goldberg and Oprah Winfrey, was a box-office hit with eleven Academy Award nominations and zero wins, and Spielberg won Best Director from the Directors Guild of America. [C32781] Empire of the Sun (1987), the first American film shot in Shanghai since the 1930s, an adaptation of J. G. Ballard's novel written by Tom Stoppard and starring a young Christian Bale, was a box-office disappointment but was called by David Thomson "the first clear sign that Spielberg the showman was an artist, too"; its budget is $25 million by the Wikipedia infobox against a contemporary $38 million figure (conflict carried), and its box office $66.7 million worldwide. [C32782] Empire of the Sun's scale is built from crowds and aircraft rather than effects: more than 5,000 local extras in the Shanghai street scenes, some old enough to remember the occupation, after a year of negotiations with Shanghai Film Studios and the China Film Co-Production Corporation won a three-week Shanghai shoot, and over 4,000 child actors auditioned for Jim; David Lean was originally to direct, with Spielberg producing, and Lean gave it up as "too similar to a diary" and handed it to Spielberg. [C32783] The 1993 double is the pivot of the career: Jurassic Park combined Stan Winston's practical effects with Industrial Light and Magic CGI and eclipsed E.T. as the highest-grossing film to date, winning three Academy Awards, while Schindler's List, about Oskar Schindler saving 1,100 Jews from the Holocaust, was a project Spielberg waited ten years to make because he did not feel "mature" enough. [C32784] Schindler's List was filmed in Krakow from 1 March 1993 while he supervised Jurassic Park post-production, won seven Academy Awards including Best Picture and his first Best Director, plus seven BAFTAs and three Golden Globes, on a $22 million budget against $322.2 million box office; he used his percentage of the profits to start the Shoah Foundation in 1994, and over 50,000 survivor testimonies were recorded, archives transferred to USC in 2006. [C32785] Schindler's List was shot in black and white, handheld about forty percent of the time, with no storyboards -- a deliberate documentary method influenced by Claude Lanzmann's Shoah (1985) -- and the single color intrusion, the little girl in the red coat during the Krakow ghetto liquidation, seen twice, is the film's only chromatic image, the final scene switching to color for the real Schindlerjuden visiting Schindler's grave. [C32786] Martin Scorsese was attached to direct Schindler's List in 1988 (screenwriter Steven Zaillian already aboard), Spielberg having tried to pass the project to several directors (Polanski, Pollack, Scorsese, Wilder, De Palma); Spielberg offered Scorsese the chance to direct the Cape Fear remake (1991) instead and took Schindler's List himself in 1993, remaining attached to Cape Fear as uncredited executive producer -- and the famous "swap" story is Ovitz's version, publicly denied by both directors in a 2023 conversation (Spielberg: "There was never a trade." Scorsese: "'Cape Fear' was payback"). [C32787] Spielberg has no credit on The Color of Money (1986): the AFI Catalog and Wikipedia list producers Irving Axelrad and Barbara De Fina, Touchstone and Silver Screen Partners II, with no Spielberg or Amblin involvement -- the real producing tie to Scorsese is Cape Fear only. [C32788]

**Takeaway:** The serious turn is a change of camera, not of subject: the same boy who filmed a train crash now films a ghetto liquidation in black and white, handheld, with no storyboards -- and the wonder machine, pointed at history, becomes a witness machine.

## 11. Jurassic Park: the hybrid dinosaur and the grammar of awe

Jurassic Park (1993), based on Michael Crichton's 1990 novel with a screenplay by Crichton and David Koepp, is a theme park of cloned dinosaurs on an island off Costa Rica that opens for inspection and the animals escape; Spielberg acquired the rights pre-publication for $1.5 million plus $500,000 for Crichton to adapt, the budget was $56-63 million, the box office $1.058 billion (over $914 million on the original run, over $1 billion with re-releases), and Hurricane Iniki hit the Kauai location during the shoot. [C32789] The dinosaurs are a deliberate hybrid: only six of the film's fifteen minutes of dinosaur screen time is CGI (52 CGI shots), nine minutes are life-sized animatronics built by Stan Winston's team, including the largest sculpture the studio ever made, the full-scale T. rex, whose rain-soaked shots took six hours per frame to render; Spielberg also invested in creating DTS digital surround sound to carry the dinosaur roars. [C32790] The first living dinosaur is not revealed until about twenty minutes in; before that, dinosaurs exist only as fossils, illustrations, merchandise and decor, and the T. rex is withheld further still, spaced-out encounters, the reveal only when it is suddenly supper time. [C32791] The brachiosaurus reveal follows a fixed grammar: Williams's score starts low and slow, Grant stands in the Jeep, removes his hat, then his sunglasses, as the camera pushes in, Sattler is absorbed by an ancient leaf, Grant turns her head, both stand and look up, off-screen, so high above camera that the scale is felt before anything is seen, and only then an ultra-wide reverse shows the brachiosaurus, so large the camera cannot even contain it, and the theme kicks in. [C32792] In the reveal, editor Michael Kahn does not cut to what Grant sees -- he holds, Grant removes his hat, removes his sunglasses (still no cut), turns Sattler's head, she reacts -- and the filmmakers also withhold the sound of the dinosaur walking until the moment it appears; the brain reaches for the resolution, and withholding it turns the reaching into anticipation. [C32793] Spielberg shot Jurassic Park in 1.85:1, giving twenty-four percent more vertical landscape in each frame than wider ratios, so he could shoot tall dinosaurs and make them look tall; wide shots make humans feel undersized and the world feel huge, and the angle inverts across the film, in act one humans looming over dinosaurs (looking down on their creations), in act two the dinosaurs looming over humans. [C32794] Scale is shown by familiar reference objects: a car window, a rearview mirror ("objects in the mirror are closer than they appear" as the T. rex chases a car), a kitchen, a trailer -- dinosaurs put into spaces built for humans, spaces they are too large to inhabit, so the size is felt physically rather than stated. [C32795] The T. rex attack plays with no music, Spielberg and sound designer Gary Rydstrom deliberately playing the paddock sequence without score, carried by ambient storm sound, the fence breaking, and the T. rex's roar, and the animal is introduced by sound before sight: the approach is first felt as ripples in a glass of water on the dashboard, each footstep a visible pulse, an idea that came to Spielberg from his car rearview mirror vibrating to Earth, Wind and Fire bass, realized by effects supervisor Michael Lantieri running a guitar string under the cup and plucking it from below the dashboard. [C32796]

**Takeaway:** The dinosaur is revealed the way the shark was not: after twenty minutes of fossils and merchandise, a hat comes off, glasses come off, a head turns, and only then does the camera look up -- awe is a sequence of small removals, not a big entrance.

## 12. Saving Private Ryan: the handheld war

Saving Private Ryan (1998), written by Robert Rodat and loosely based on the Niland brothers, is Captain John Miller (Tom Hanks) leading eight men behind enemy lines after D-Day to find Private James Ryan (Matt Damon), whose three brothers have been killed, and bring him home; filmed June to September 1997 almost entirely on location in England and Ireland, it grossed $482.3 million by the infobox ($481.8 million in body text) on a $65-70 million budget, and Spielberg won his second Academy Award for Best Director. [C32797] The Omaha Beach opening cost $12 million, took four weeks, used 1,500 background actors, the cast went through a week-long boot camp with military adviser Dale Dye, and in August 1999 Spielberg and Hanks received the Department of Defense Medal for Distinguished Public Service; Thomson: "Ryan changed war films." [C32798] The D-Day sequence (twenty-four to twenty-five minutes, only seven pages of script) was not storyboarded -- Spielberg invented shots on the day to preserve chaos: "I had to shoot this sequence one step at a time because that's the way the Rangers took the beach: one inch at a time... I was able to make up this whole sequence as I went along." -- and mid-shoot he re-centered the film on Upham as the audience surrogate after seeing dailies. [C32799] Kaminski used a 45-degree shutter angle for stuttering, strobe-like motion, handheld cameras, and bleach-bypass (ENR) processing, skipping the bleaching stage so silver stays in the emulsion, yielding high contrast, desaturated color and gritty grain that evokes 1940s combat photography; Spielberg: "I also drained 60 percent of the colour out of the movie... I didn't want a Hollywood war movie in Technicolor; desaturation was the easiest way of taking the bloom off the rose." [C32800] Kaminski also peeled the protective coating off the lens so light appears harsh and unfiltered, and Spielberg used an "Image Shaker" lens (after trying a Black and Decker drill taped to the camera) to vibrate the image; in the film soldiers are often silhouetted against bright skies in outdoor scenes, a haunting image underscoring vulnerability, with harsh overexposed sunlight alternating with muted overcast shadows. [C32801] Williams's score is withheld entirely during the landing, the sound design (Gary Rydstrom) carrying it, including Miller's temporary deafness after a mortar blast; and the film opens with an old man collapsing at a gravestone, the camera pushing in tight on his eyes, and before the image changes the sound of the ocean arrives, audio from the incoming clip running ahead of the picture, so by the time Omaha Beach appears the audience understands it is inside his memory. [C32802] Hook (1991) is the hinge before this turn: middle-aged Peter Pan (Robin Williams) returns to Neverland, shot almost entirely on sound stages at Sony Pictures Studios, Culver City, across nine stages, a planned seventy-six-day shoot running about forty days over, earning over $300 million worldwide from a $70 million budget despite mostly negative reviews, and Spielberg later said he was out of his depth on it -- the indulgent swing whose mixed reception marks the end of his "kid magician" phase. [C32803] The Lost World: Jurassic Park (1997), budgeted at $73 million, was one of the highest-grossing films of the year, and Amistad (1997) was his first film released under DreamWorks, underperformed, and Spielberg admitted it "became too much of a history lesson." [C32804]

**Takeaway:** The war film is rewritten by refusing the war film: no storyboard, no score on the beach, sixty percent of the color drained, the camera shaken by a drill -- chaos as method, and the audience inside a memory before it knows it is one.

## 13. Kaminski: backlight, bleach bypass, the post-1993 look

Janusz Kaminski, Spielberg's cinematographer since Schindler's List (1993), was discovered in 1991 after Spielberg saw the TV film Wildflower and hired to shoot the Spielberg-produced TV film Class of '61 (1993); he has shot every Spielberg-directed film since, and holds two Best Cinematography Oscars, both for Spielberg (Schindler's List and Saving Private Ryan), with five further nominations, four for Spielberg films. [C32805] Kaminski: "I love backlight not just for the sake of glamorizing [the subject], but because the direction of the light can represent storytelling... if I do backlight, I want to see that backlight. That's my style, and that's the way we've done it in every single movie." -- and he uses heavy diffusion (nets, Pro-Mist filters, stockings over the rear lens element) for a blooming "halation" where light bleeds into shadows. [C32806] Aggressive backlighting through smoke or atmosphere creates strong shafts of light, "God-light" streaming through barn doors in the Raiders warehouse or illuminating the attic in E.T., separating the subject from the background with a bright rim and producing high-contrast silhouettes that read as wonder, spirituality or impending doom. [C32807] Lens flare is used sparingly, Kaminski's diffused light and flares giving films like A.I. a dreamlike quality and creating moments of magic and transcendence, a technique J.J. Abrams later exaggerated to the point of parody; in The Lost World, Dennis Muren encouraged Kaminski to shoot against the sun and introduce dinosaurs as silhouettes or moving against bright light, with sequences full of lens flares. [C32808] Schindler's List was shot in a very crude technical manner by design: "We were kind of aiming toward imperfection, little so-called 'flaws' that might be considered mistakes, such as handheld shots in scenes that would normally be shot on the dolly", and the look drew on Roman Vishniac's pre-war Jewish photography and Robert Capa's wartime imagery; the girl in the red coat was shot in color and painstakingly desaturated frame by frame by rotoscoping (Kaminski's account, used here; some secondary sources describe it differently). [C32809] Kaminski: "You work in metaphors through lights and composition, and the worst thing for me is to see a movie that doesn't have that." -- Minority Report washed in moody blues and grays as homage to film noir, with a bluish side light making two lost people look glamorous yet lonely and alienated; Lincoln's first scene withholds light from Day-Lewis's eyes to preserve mystery and iconography, while a later scene bathes him in hopeful light to signal the 13th Amendment will pass; War Horse's final scene staged against a saturated red sky with silhouette, orange and graduated filters. [C32810] With Kaminski the camera became more handheld as Spielberg's work moved from action to drama, and the early style -- extreme high and low camera angles, long takes, handheld cameras, wide-angle lenses for depth -- gives way after Jaws to a discipline of saving effects scenes until last and excluding media from locations, and by Minority Report to a confidence with elaborate camera movements. [C32811] One exception proves the rule on zooms: Spielberg generally avoids zoom lenses and prefers physical camera movement, but in Munich he and Kaminski leaned heavily on a Cooke 25-250mm zoom for creeping, predatory zooms that signal paranoia and an encroaching threat, a deliberate break from his own rule. [C32812]

**Takeaway:** Light is the argument: backlight you can see, color drained by sixty percent, a lens with its coating peeled off -- the look after 1993 is not prettier, it is more honest, and the honesty is the style.

## 14. Williams: two notes, five notes, and silence

Williams has scored nearly all of Spielberg's features, from The Sugarland Express (1974) through The Fabelmans (2022), about five decades; Spielberg usually does not hear the music until it is recorded, and has often said Williams's scores make his films whole. [C32813] The count is 29 released features per ScreenRant and Variety, against "30 movies together" in Far Out Magazine; the 30th is the reported 2026 UFO film, unreleased and reported by a secondary outlet, so the 29-film figure covers released films through 2022 and the 30th is carried as reported, not confirmed. [C32814] The five Spielberg features Williams did not score are Duel (1971, Billy Goldenberg), The Color Purple (1985, Quincy Jones), Bridge of Spies (2015, Thomas Newman, Williams stepping away for health reasons), Ready Player One (2018, Alan Silvestri, Williams choosing The Post instead), and West Side Story (2021, Bernstein, Williams as music consultant only). [C32815] The Jaws theme is just two alternating notes, E and F, played low in the strings and getting faster as the shark gets closer; because the mechanical shark kept breaking down, the music had to stand in for the creature, the audience feeling the threat through sound before ever seeing the fin, and it won Williams his second Academy Award, his first for an original score. [C32816] Williams builds scores on Wagner-style leitmotifs, recurring themes for characters, places and ideas that transform as the story goes on: the Jurassic Park theme swells at the first dinosaur sighting and returns over the survivors at the end, and the E.T. theme grows from wonder through danger into full flight during the bicycle chase against the moon. [C32817] Rydstrom built the dinosaurs' voices from recombined recordings of living animals, giving each species behavior, size and character through sound, the T. rex roar functioning as a recognizable character voice rather than a reusable effect, and the design goal, per Rydstrom: "sound effects... are really there for the sake of storytelling." [C32818] Spielberg had admired Williams's scores for The Reivers (1969) and The Cowboys (1972) and requested him for his feature debut, and the partnership is also the bridge by which Lucas got Williams for Star Wars -- Spielberg introduced them. [C32819] Williams won three of his five score Oscars on Spielberg films (Jaws, E.T., Schindler's List), and Schindler's List carries Williams's violin theme with Itzhak Perlman, restrained against the black-and-white image -- the score that knows when to be quiet. [C32820]

**Takeaway:** The music is the monster when the monster will not work, the language when the aliens will not speak, and the silence when the beach landing needs no orchestra -- two notes can carry a shark, five notes can carry a civilization, and nothing at all can carry a war.

## 15. Kahn: the cut refused, the smash cut, the J-cut

Michael Kahn has edited Spielberg's films since Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977), winning three Best Editor Oscars (Raiders of the Lost Ark, Schindler's List, Saving Private Ryan) and eight nominations; born 8 December 1930 by Wikipedia, with at least one mirror hedging "c. 1930", he has edited all but two of Spielberg's films since 1977, the exceptions being E.T. (1982, edited by Carol Littleton, Kahn busy on Spielberg-produced Poltergeist) and Disclosure Day (2026, Sarah Broshar), 33 collaborations per Wikipedia's filmography note. [C32821] Kahn: "What is editing? Manipulation." -- and the manipulation is timed to emotion above all, Walter Murch's Rule of Six putting emotion at fifty-one percent, above story, rhythm, everything. [C32822] The smash cut for a joke, "the Brody cut": in Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, Indy sells the Nazis a confident portrait of Marcus Brody as an untouchable operator who speaks a dozen languages, then a hard cut to Brody hopelessly lost, asking whether anyone speaks English -- the joke lands because the picture in the viewer's head is demolished half a second after it is built. [C32823] The J-cut into a flashback opens Saving Private Ryan: an old man collapsing at a gravestone, the camera pushing in tight on his eyes, and before the image changes, the sound of the ocean arrives, audio from the incoming clip running ahead of the picture, so by the time Omaha Beach appears the audience understands it is inside his memory -- the technique controls when the audience sees and hears something, not just what. [C32824] The cut Spielberg refuses to make is the Jurassic Park reveal: Kahn withholds the reverse shot entirely, no cut to what Grant sees, no cut to the dinosaur's footsteps, because the brain automatically chops experience into discrete events and reaches for resolution, and withholding the close makes the reaching itself the anticipation -- Spielberg learned this on Jaws, where the broken shark forced him to show less and it got scarier. [C32825] The refusal to cut again in The Post: Spielberg shot a lot of coverage for the first breakfast scene between Hanks and Streep, then told Kahn and Sarah Broshar: "Let's not cut." -- the master plays from beginning to end, putting the audience at the table and letting them choose who to look at and when. [C32826] Kahn uses parallel editing to contrast worlds, in Schindler's List cutting between the life of the Jews in the camp and the lifestyles of Schindler and the Nazis, and shifting points of view, in Tintin moving point of view between characters as a visitor arrives; in Jurassic Park he uses slower cuts in the build-up to the T. rex's first on-screen appearance, while in Saving Private Ryan a four-minute sequence has about fifty cuts all synchronized to the sound of bullets -- the rhythm is set by the material, not a house tempo. [C32827] No distinct Spielberg dissolve or wipe grammar was found in any source consulted: the documented transition devices are the J-cut into flashback, the smash cut, crossfades (in Munich, Kahn crossfades to a blood-red sky reflected in an airplane window after a flashback), and the withheld reverse -- do not claim a wipe or dissolve signature. [C32828]

**Takeaway:** The editor's best cut is the one he refuses: hold the face, hold the master, let the sound arrive early -- the audience's brain will do the cutting for you, and it will cut toward the feeling.

## 16. The 2000s: design, dread, and the Kushner histories

A.I. Artificial Intelligence (2001) was Kubrick's project since the early 1970s -- he acquired the rights, cycled through writers, and held it because CGI was not advanced enough for the David character; he handed it to Spielberg in 1995, and it only gained momentum after Kubrick's death in 1999, with Spielberg directing it himself trying to stay faithful to Kubrick's vision, grossing $236 million on a $90-100 million budget. [C32829] Minority Report (2002), from Philip K. Dick's 1956 story, grossed over $358 million on a $102 million budget and was the first Spielberg-Cruise pairing; Anderton conducts the precogs' visions with conductor-like haptic hand gestures, scrubbing, rewinding and fast-forwarding images across a wall of opaque glass, and the future was designed by production designer Alex McDowell with a fifteen-person think tank of scientists, urban planners and futurists convened by Spielberg for three days, plus MIT Media Lab science adviser John Underkoffler, yielding retinal scanners, personalized advertising and gesture interfaces that have since become real. [C32830] Catch Me If You Can (2002), based on Frank Abagnale's autobiography and starring Leonardo DiCaprio, was filmed in 147 locations in only 52 days on a $52 million budget, grossing $352.1 million; it was the light-footed palate cleanser, a breezy actor-driven caper between two heavy science-fiction films, and proof he could do pure pleasure with no effects at all, and he completed his B.A. in Film and Electronic Media from Cal State Long Beach in 2002, presenting Schindler's List as his final project. [C32831] War of the Worlds (2005), based on H. G. Wells, grossed over $600 million on a $132 million budget, shot in 73 days with production beginning before the script was finalized; the tripods are kept largely obscured, emerging from underground after lightning strikes and an EMP, fear of the unseen, the Jaws technique at blockbuster scale, and the film is the post-9/11 film, an invasion story told from the ground, from a failing father's point of view, with ash-covered survivors fleeing a city. [C32832] Munich (2005), about Israel's retaliation after the 1972 Munich massacre, received five Oscar nominations and is one of his most controversial films; its budget is $70 million by Wikipedia against $75 million (the-numbers) and $77 million (namu.wiki), its box office $131 million with $47.4 million domestic, one of his lowest domestic grosses, and its structure is a revenge procedural that keeps cutting back to the Munich hostage crisis itself, Avner's memories and flashbacks of the massacre intruding on the assassinations, so the violence is always shadowed by its origin (interpretive framing of the structure; the Budapest and Malta shooting and consultation facts are sourced). [C32833] Tony Kushner is the late-career writing partner: four Spielberg screenplays, his only Spielberg collaborations and nearly his whole filmography -- Munich (2005, co-written with Eric Roth), Lincoln (2012), West Side Story (2021), The Fabelmans (2022, co-written with Spielberg) -- with four Oscar nominations from the partnership. [C32834] In the mid-2000s he scaled down directing and became more selective, sold DreamWorks to Viacom in December 2005, planned Interstellar but abandoned it (eventually directed by Christopher Nolan), and Indiana Jones and the Kingdom of the Crystal Skull (2008), released nineteen years after Last Crusade, grossed $790 million worldwide. [C32835] DreamWorks itself was set up in 1994 with Jeffrey Katzenberg and David Geffen, officially founded 12 October 1994, the three partners each putting in $33 million plus $500 million from Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen and $300 million from CJ Group heiress Miky Lee, the founders comparing themselves to the founders of United Artists in 1919; sold to Viacom and Paramount in December 2005 for $1.6 billion, separated from Paramount in 2008, and Amblin Partners formed 16 December 2015 with DreamWorks as a subsidiary brand. [C32836]

**Takeaway:** The 2000s split the career in two directions at once: the designed tomorrow (a think tank building 2054) and the moral dread (a hit team haunted by the massacre it avenges) -- and the same director made both in the same year.

## 17. Late Spielberg: Lincoln, West Side Story, The Fabelmans

Lincoln (2012), Daniel Day-Lewis as Abraham Lincoln, written by Tony Kushner from Doris Kearns Goodwin's Team of Rivals and depicting the final four months of Lincoln's life, earned over $250 million on a $65 million budget and was nominated for twelve Academy Awards, winning Best Actor and Best Production Design. [C32837] At Day-Lewis's insistence, enforced by Spielberg, the set was kept period-pure: no modern items allowed, no T-shirts with logos, no paper coffee cups, no sneakers, no iPhones, and Spielberg himself wore a blazer and button-down every day to direct; Day-Lewis stayed in character throughout, and Kaminski's candlelit, smoky interiors make the film play like a stage piece, talk, not battle, as the action. [C32838] Bridge of Spies (2015), a Cold War thriller about James B. Donovan's negotiations for Gary Powers, won Mark Rylance Best Supporting Actor on a $40 million budget against $165.5 million box office, with a score by Thomas Newman, a rare Spielberg film without John Williams; The BFG (2016) was his first Disney-branded film and the last film written by E.T. screenwriter Melissa Mathison, and The Post (2017) took on the Washington Post and the Pentagon Papers with Tom Hanks and Meryl Streep. [C32839] Ready Player One (2018), adapted from Ernest Cline's novel, premiered at SXSW 2018 on a $155-175 million budget against $608 million box office; ILM delivered about 900 VFX shots for the OASIS sequences, the showpiece Overlook Hotel sequence a digital recreation of Kubrick's The Shining built from high-quality telecine of the original film with Kubrick's blueprints consulted, and the film is essentially two visual systems, drab live-action 2045 Columbus versus the saturated, reference-dense OASIS. [C32840] West Side Story (2021), filmed 2019 and written by Tony Kushner, took seven Oscar nominations on a $100 million budget against a $76 million box office, a bomb against an estimated $300 million break-even, though acclaimed (Ariana DeBose won Best Supporting Actress); Rita Moreno, Anita in 1961, served as executive producer and played Valentina, and Justin Peck choreographed original dances rather than replicating Jerome Robbins. [C32841] The opening of "The Dance at the Gym" is three separate shots stitched to read as one continuous take, beginning as Steadicam and becoming a cable-cam shot, and the neighborhood itself is shot as a demolition zone, the Sharks and Jets fighting over streets already half-torn down for Lincoln Center, making the romance play out in rubble. [C32842] The Fabelmans (2022), a self-portrait of the director as a young man co-written with Kushner, Gabriel LaBelle as Sammy Fabelman and Michelle Williams as his mother Mitzi, won the TIFF People's Choice Award and took seven Oscar nominations including Best Picture, Best Director and Best Original Screenplay, on a $40 million budget against $45.6 million box office; conceived as early as 1999 (sister Anne's draft titled I'll Be Home), the screenplay completed late 2020, filmed July to September 2021 in Los Angeles over 59 days during COVID, and dedicated to his parents Leah Adler (d. 2017) and Arnold Spielberg (d. 2020). [C32843] The film restages his own origin scenes, the boy's first cinema trip (Cecil B. DeMille's The Greatest Show on Earth, 1952) and Sammy filming a train crash with his toy set, the camera as the instrument that lets him see the truth about his family; Seth Rogen said Spielberg "was crying a lot on set", "pretty much everything that happens in the movie is something that happened to him", and it is Spielberg's first screenplay credit since A.I. (2001). [C32844]

**Takeaway:** The late films are a return with interest: a musical at seventy-four, a history film that is all talk, and then the childhood itself, filmed as the origin of the camera -- the boy who filmed the train crash finally films himself filming it.

## 18. Aesthetic logic: wonder and horror are one machine

The same grammar serves both: the withheld reveal plus the reaction face produces awe in Jurassic Park and dread in Jaws, the child point of view produces wonder in E.T. and terror in the Jurassic Park kitchen scene (raptors stalking children, shot from inside the space), and the T. rex reveal and the shark reveal are the same machine run in opposite emotional directions (interpretation, the shared-mechanism reading is leg synthesis; the individual techniques are verified above). [C32845] Recurrent themes, per the main article: "ordinary people in extraordinary circumstances" who become heroes, childlike wonder and faith, "the goodness in humanity will prevail", childhood, loss of innocence, need for parental figures, usually a flawed or irresponsible father figure personally resonant with his childhood, and extraterrestrial life -- he described himself as like an "alien" during childhood, and said E.T. was a springboard to purge the pain of his parents' split. [C32846] World War II is a lifelong subject: his father Arnold was of the "World War II ethic", distant, practical, preoccupied, and Spielberg has returned to WWII across Saving Private Ryan, Schindler's List, Band of Brothers, The Pacific and Masters of the Air, blending gritty realism with male sentimentality in what critics call "dad history". [C32847] He says he chooses projects by whether a story speaks to him and he would be proud to add it to his body of work even if collaborators doubt it, and he says he was "self-taught", "my film school was really the cultural heritage of Hollywood and international filmmaking", renting a John Ford film before starting every movie ("I try to rent a John Ford film... before I start every movie"). [C32848] His method on set is improvisation inside discipline: "I just sort of come in, look at the set, sometimes I do it the night before, sometimes I get in early before the crew even shows up, and I walk the set with a work light on, and I figure out my day, or I figure out the first shot." -- and he used no storyboards on Bridge of Spies, Amistad, Lincoln and others: "Every movie I begin, it's just scary... because I get to make the movie in real time." [C32849] On Jaws and Close Encounters he would find the emotional theme of each scene, have actors improvise around it with a tape recorder running, then rewrite the scene overnight using the strongest lines: "The actors helped me shake out the fat and get right down to what the scene was about." -- and Jurassic Park's ending was rewritten on set, the climax originally built around the velociraptors until mid-shoot Spielberg decided the T. rex should return for the final bow, with no storyboards and no advance prep. [C32850] The box-office relay is the proof of scale: Coppola's The Godfather (1972) was the highest-grossing film ever until Spielberg's Jaws, whose record was broken two years later by Lucas's Star Wars, surpassed five years later by Spielberg's E.T.; his films have earned almost $11 billion worldwide (Wikipedia's lead figure as of the page crawl, not cross-checked against Box Office Mojo), making him the highest-grossing film director of all time, and seven of his films are in the National Film Registry: Jaws, Close Encounters, Raiders, E.T., Jurassic Park, Schindler's List, and Saving Private Ryan. [C32851]

**Takeaway:** Wonder and horror are not two subjects but one mechanism with the polarity reversed: withhold the thing, hold the face, let the sound arrive first -- and the audience will feel awe or dread depending only on which way you turn the dial.

## 19. The movie brats: Lucas, Coppola, and the friendship that made the blockbuster

Lucas first met Spielberg in 1967, when twenty-year-old Spielberg came to a USC screening that included Lucas's student short Electronic Labyrinth: THX 1138 4EB, and Spielberg met Coppola that same year, 1967, when Coppola had him over for a general meeting; Coppola met Lucas in 1968 when Lucas shadowed him on Finian's Rainbow, and the two co-founded American Zoetrope in 1969. [C32852] The three became close friends who "spoke the same creative language" and shared a love of computers and special effects, and Spielberg is described as a major figure of the New Hollywood era and pioneer of the modern blockbuster; Coppola, Lucas and Spielberg were the three most successful filmmakers in American cinema history by one collective-biography account, and "almost single-handedly (triple-handedly?)... resuscitated their industry."
