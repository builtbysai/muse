**Site:** Cindy Sherman -- born January 19, 1954, in Glen Ridge, New Jersey; lives and works in New York -- American artist of the Pictures Generation who photographs herself as someone else, again and again, until the self disappears into a serial system. ## The life: the face that is never the same twice

Cynthia Morris Sherman was born January 19, 1954, in Glen Ridge, New Jersey, the youngest of five children; the family moved soon after to Huntington, Long Island. She enrolled at Buffalo State in 1972, first painting, then photography after a failed freshman class repeated with Barbara Jo Revelle; in 1974 she co-founded Hallwalls in Buffalo with Robert Longo, Charles Clough, and Nancy Dwyer. She graduated with a BA in 1976 per Wikipedia and the Metro Pictures CV; the graduation year is contested (one secondary source says 1977; Moderna Museet says she started in 1974, not 1972) and no source read end to end gave a BFA title, so no BA-versus-BFA conflict was established. She moved to New York City, commonly dated 1977, living with Longo 1974-1980. (C20308, leg 1 C20100-C20105; synthesis C20250-C20251)

The Untitled Film Stills (1977-1980) brought international recognition; MoMA bought the series for an estimated $1 million in 1995. Her first solo show in New York was at The Kitchen in 1980, and Metro Pictures opened later that year with her photographs as its first show; the Metro CV also lists a 1980 solo at the Contemporary Arts Museum, Houston, so which 1980 solo came first overall is unsettled. (C20309, leg 1 C20106-C20107; synthesis C20252)

Later series run in order, and each one changes the apparatus rather than the face: Rear Screen Projections (1980, the switch to colour and larger formats); Centerfolds, also called Horizontals (1981, commissioned but not used by Artforum); Pink Robes (1982, Untitled #97-#100); Fairy Tales (1985) and Disasters (1986-1989, first prostheses and mannequins); fashion work including a 1983 Dianne B. commission, a 1993 Harper's Bazaar editorial, and the 1994 Comme des Garcons series; History Portraits (1989-1990, thirty-five large colour photographs restaging European portraiture); Sex Pictures (mannequins and prostheses, Sherman absent); head shots (2000-2002, return as model); Clowns (2003-2004, digital backdrops); Society Portraits (2008, wealthy older women, digital manipulation); the 2010 photographic mural (five 18-foot figures); Chanel (2010-2012); The Imitation of Life (2016, aging actress-like women); the 2017 Instagram selfie project with W Magazine; tapestries from 2019. She directed Office Killer (1997). (C20310, leg 1 C20108-C20113; synthesis C20253)

Major retrospectives: Rotterdam (1996); MOCA Los Angeles and MoMA (1997, sponsored by Madonna); Kunsthaus Bregenz, Louisiana, and Jeu de Paume (2006-2007); MoMA, February 26-June 11, 2012 (more than 170 photographs, travelling to SFMOMA, the Walker, and Dallas); National Portrait Gallery, London (2019). She took part in the Venice Biennale in 1982 and 1995, five Whitney Biennials, and the Met's The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984 (2009). Awards include the MacArthur Fellowship (1995), Hasselblad Award (1999), Praemium Imperiale (2016), and Wolf Prize (2020). Represented by Metro Pictures for forty years, then Sprueth Magers, then Hauser and Wirth from 2021. Auction records: Untitled #153 sold for $2.7 million (2010); a print of Untitled #96 fetched $3.89 million at Christie's (2011). (C20311, leg 1 C20114-C20118; synthesis C20254)


## M1 -- Self-portraiture as serial system: the series is the portrait

Photograph the same body again and again, changing one element per frame, so identity is built by accumulation across a series rather than by a single likeness. The earliest proof predates any film reference: "Untitled #479" (1975, W04) is twenty-three hand-coloured prints in which one element changes per frame until a plain student becomes a vamp -- the serial engine, before the camera ever borrowed a genre. The Untitled Film Stills (W01, W02, W03) run the same engine inside a borrowed frame: sixty-nine stills, one woman, a different role in each, and the viewer assembles the person from the run, not from any one image. The Centerfolds (W06) do it again in a horizontal format. No single Sherman photograph is a portrait of Sherman; the portrait is the stack. That is why the work survives being copied image by image and fails when it is shown as one picture: the system needs the run.

**Takeaway:** repeat the same performer with one variable changed per frame and the series becomes the portrait. (C20312, leg 2 C20171; synthesis C20256)

## M2 -- The same face re-cast: recognition is the effect

Keep the face constant and change the casting, so the viewer recognises the actor and still believes each new role. The same face is a librarian in one still and a starlet in the next (W01, W02); a fashion victim with false teeth and exaggerated makeup (W07); a would-be actress smiling too hard (W15); a quieter headshot straight to camera (W16). The mechanism depends on the face not changing: if the performer were different each time, the series would be a casting call; because it is the same face, the viewer holds two facts at once -- that is Sherman, and that is not Sherman -- and the tension between them is the work. The recognition is not a flaw in the disguise; it is the disguise working. A stranger in the same wig would be a photograph of a stranger; Sherman in the wig is a photograph of acting.

**Takeaway:** keep the face constant and change the casting, and recognition itself becomes the effect. (C20313, leg 2 C20172; synthesis C20257)

## M3 -- The set as the subject, the frame borrowed from film

Borrow a mass-media frame whole -- film still, rear projection, centerfold spread, fashion advertisement -- and fill it with a body that does not fit its promise, so the genre is exposed by its own shape. A woman mid-look toward something off-frame (W01) hands the viewer a film that does not exist, and the viewer invents it. A studio-lit woman stands against a projected landscape and the seam shows (W05). A centerfold shape is filled with anxiety instead of invitation (W06). A fashion figure wears false teeth inside fashion's own pose (W07). In every case the frame is borrowed intact and the body inside it refuses the frame: the still promises a story and delivers only a look; the centerfold promises seduction and delivers dread; the fashion shot promises desire and delivers a grimace. The genre does the work; Sherman only has to stand inside it wrong.

**Takeaway:** borrow a mass-media frame whole, then let the body inside it refuse the frame. (C20314, leg 2 C20173; synthesis C20258)

## M4 -- The camera as mirror, the gaze returned

Direct the figure's gaze at the viewer and the portrait turns into an accusation. The city woman looks back over her shoulder (W02) and the plot is the look itself. The centerfold figure (W06) does not invite the viewer in; she watches the viewer watching. The headshot actress (W15) smiles too hard straight at the lens, and the smile is aimed at whoever is looking. The quieter headshot (W16) looks straight to camera with nothing to sell. Sherman's figures rarely look away into the middle distance the way a painted portrait does; they catch the viewer mid-look. The mechanism is a direction, not a costume: the same wig and makeup with the gaze averted would be a character study; with the gaze returned it is a confrontation. The viewer came to look at a woman and finds the woman looking back.

**Takeaway:** direct the gaze at the viewer and the portrait turns into an accusation. (C20315, leg 2 C20174; synthesis C20259)

## M5 -- The wig and prosthetic as the character: show the seam

Build the character out of visible artifice -- wigs, false breasts, moulded torsos, corpse makeup, clown paint -- and leave the artifice visible at the edges, so the character is assembled in front of the viewer. The mud-caked corpse on debris (W09) is a fairy tale after the story ends, and the makeup is the story. The History Portraits (W10, W11, W12) copy the Old Masters with moulded breastplates worn like armour and prosthetic noses that do not quite sit right; the copy and the unmasking happen in one gesture. The Clowns (W17) wear garish paint over digital backdrops, and the paint is the character. The seam is never hidden because the seam is the subject: Sherman is not pretending to be a countess or a clown; she is showing you the kit she used to become one, and the kit is the point. Hide the prosthetic and you get a costume photograph; show it and you get a photograph about pretending.

**Takeaway:** show the seam of the prosthetic and the disguise becomes the subject. (C20316, leg 2 C20175; synthesis C20260)

## M6 -- The body as doll, the self removed

Step out of the frame and let mannequins, dolls, and prosthetic parts take the performer's place, so the image keeps its erotic or grotesque charge with no person in it. The Sex Pictures (W13, W14) arrange dismembered mannequin parts in sexual poses; the fused prosthetic genitalia in close-up are louder than the pose. The Comme des Garcons mannequins (W08) carry the image while the costume does the acting. Even the corpse (W09) belongs here: the body is present as a thing, not as a person. The mechanism is subtraction: remove the performer and the genre is left with nothing to hide behind. A sex picture with a real body in it can always be read as a photograph of a person; a sex picture with only parts in it can only be read as a photograph of the genre itself. The dolls do not pretend; that is why they are more disturbing than the disguise.

**Takeaway:** remove the performer and let the props perform, and the genre is left with nothing to hide behind. (C20317, leg 2 C20176; synthesis C20261)

## M7 -- The backdrop as a lie: plant one wrong detail

Build the world as a flat backdrop -- a projected slide, a digital gradient, a green-screen Cloisters arch, a drawn Central Park -- and plant one small mismatch that breaks the place. The Rear Screen Projection (W05) puts a studio-lit woman against a projected landscape and lets the seam show. The Clowns (W17) stand before garish digital backdrops painted in software, and the background is now the costume. The grand dame (W19) poses before a digital Cloisters arch, betrayed by cheap pink plastic slippers. The mural figures (W20) stand eighteen feet tall against a drawn park. In each case the backdrop is a lie the photograph admits: the light does not match, the slippers are wrong, the scale is impossible. The mismatch is the mechanism, not a mistake -- without it the backdrop would be a set, and with it the backdrop is a confession. One wrong detail is enough; two would be a joke, and none would be a lie told straight.

**Takeaway:** build the world as a flat backdrop and plant one wrong detail that breaks it. (C20318, leg 2 C20177; synthesis C20262)

## M8 -- Scale and ageing as the system

Let size and age carry the meaning costume once carried. The small 8 x 10 in. stills grow into 8-foot society portraits and an 18-foot mural, while the characters age from ingenue to clown to grand dame. The headshot actress (W15) is young and trying too hard; the clown (W17) is older and louder; the grand dame (W19) is older still, grand in a blue caftan and betrayed by her slippers; the mural figures (W20) are eighteen feet tall and the self-portrait has left the frame and become architecture. The face is the same; what changes is how big it is printed and how old the character is allowed to be. Scale is not presentation here, it is content: a film still at 8 x 10 in. is a private fantasy, and the same face at eighteen feet is a public monument to a person who never existed. Age is the same: the ingenue and the grand dame are the same performer at different points in a life that is not hers.

**Takeaway:** enlarge the print and age the character, and the same face tells a different story. (C20319, leg 2 C20178; synthesis C20263)


## The evolution: the apparatus changes, the face does not

Three movements, and the face never changes -- only the apparatus around it. First, 1975-1980, the serial engine is built: twenty-three hand-coloured prints that turn a student into a vamp one variable at a time (W04), then sixty-nine film stills that turn one woman into a whole cast (W01-W03). Second, 1980-1990, the borrowed frame widens: rear projections, centerfolds, fashion, fairy tales, disasters, history portraits. The body is still Sherman's, but the frame is now a genre with a promise -- the centerfold, the fashion ad, the Old Master -- and the body inside it refuses the promise (W05-W12). Third, 1992 onward, the self leaves: mannequins and prosthetic parts take the roles (W13, W14), then Sherman returns as an older model -- headshots, clowns, society portraits -- while the backdrop becomes digital and the print becomes architecture (W15-W20). The operation never changes: one face, one variable at a time, the series as the portrait. What changes is what the variable is allowed to be: first a wig, then a genre, then a body that is not a body, then a scale and an age. The 2010 mural is the same operation at its largest: five figures, eighteen feet tall, the self-portrait become a building. (C20320, leg 2 C20150-C20170; synthesis C20255)

## Aesthetic logic: the portrait is a system, and the system has no original

Sherman's aesthetic logic is that a portrait is never a likeness of a person -- it is a system for producing likenesses, and the system has no original. There is no real Sherman underneath the characters, because the work never shows her; there is only the face, reused, and the variables stacked on it. That is why the photographs are untitled and numbered: a title would name a character, and the character is not the point; the number says where this frame sits in the run. It is why the disguise is always visible at the edges: the work is not about becoming someone else, it is about the kit that lets anyone become someone else. And it is why the genres are borrowed whole: the film still, the centerfold, the fashion ad, the Old Master portrait are all systems for producing women, and Sherman's system sits on top of them and shows their workings. The humour is dry and the dread is real -- the centerfold that frightens, the fashion victim with false teeth, the corpse after the fairy tale, the grand dame in plastic slippers -- but the joke and the dread are both methods: they are how the system shows you it is a system. What she refuses, consistently, is the portrait as a window onto a soul: there is no soul on offer, only a face, a wig, a backdrop, and a number. (C20321, leg 1 C20100-C20119; leg 2 C20171-C20178; synthesis C20256-C20263)
