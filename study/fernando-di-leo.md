## 1. The lawyer's son who learned to wait

Fernando Di Leo was born 11 January 1932 in San Ferdinando di Puglia, then in the province of Foggia, the son and grandson of lawyers; his father was a criminal lawyer active at the Naples bar — a circumstance that later let Di Leo shoot undisturbed on Naples sets, "protected" by his father's former clients. [C31064] His sister is the historian Rita Di Leo, who still tells his story. He lived in San Ferdinando until he was eleven, a boy with a histrionic streak who staged makeshift film sets and threw chairs and tables from the village bar with other children — chaos he later blamed on the wind, "Ha stat u' vind" — images and sounds that resurfaced in his films. [C31065] He studied in Foggia, where he graduated in law and became best friends with Renzo Arbore (Di Leo was "the ringleader"), going to the cinema twice a day. [C31066] The law degree was completed and then set aside: he chose not to practise and went to Rome's Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia, where, with fellow students Marco Bellocchio, Franz Weisz and Enzo Dell'Aquila, he formed a militant cinephile group attentive to the French Nouvelle Vague and British Free Cinema. [C31067] Before directing he wrote for the magazine *Sipario* and, through his friendship with Duccio Tessari, began as a screenwriter. [C31068]

He discovered noir fiction as a teenager: André Gide's admiration for Dashiell Hammett led him to *Red Harvest*. Film noir came later, with the American movies that flooded Italy after the war. [C31069] The order matters — the books first, the films second — because everything Di Leo made afterwards reads like a man who learned the genre from Hammett's sentences before he learned it from anybody's camera. His directorial debut came in 1963, the episode *Un posto in paradiso* ("A Place in Heaven"), co-directed with Enzo Dell'Aquila, in the omnibus comedy *Gli eroi di ieri, oggi, domani*; its anticlerical satire drew censorship, and Giulio Andreotti, then undersecretary to the Prime Minister's Office in charge of Italian film production, stopped its distribution and ordered cuts. [C31070] The first film is therefore a film about power telling a joke about power, and power answering with scissors — the whole career in miniature, except that afterwards Di Leo learned to make the joke inside the genre, where the censors could not find it.

The western years, 1964–1968, are the apprenticeship: a script mill in which he wrote, mostly uncredited at first, for Tessari's Ringo films, *Kiss Kiss... Bang Bang*, *Massacre Time* (Fulci), *Seven Guns for the MacGregors*, *Johnny Yuma*, *Navajo Joe*, *Sugar Colt*, *Long Days of Vengeance* and many more — about twenty-five western credits in five years. [C31071] Two of those credits carry the career's most famous dispute. Di Leo claimed — and critics including Curti and McDonagh accept — that he worked uncredited on the treatments and scripts of Leone's *A Fistful of Dollars* (1964) and *For a Few Dollars More* (1965) alongside Tessari; Leone himself claimed sole authorship from Tessari's treatment, Bolzoni's account credits Tessari with the majority of the script, and the credited writers are Leone, Tessari and Vincenzoni. [C31072] The claim is carried here as a contested claim, not a fact, and the modesty with which Di Leo held it is itself evidence of something: he never asked for the credit, saying "those films were all thanks to Sergio Leone", and that "il genio viene dopo la fase di scrittura" — genius comes after the writing phase. [C31073] The Django anecdote is cleaner and better documented: Corbucci, unconvinced by the *Django* script, asked Di Leo to "take a look" the day before leaving for Almería; Di Leo revised the script in a single night and delivered it to the director at the airport in time for departure, his name never appearing in the credits. [C31074] He thought the western genre "infantile" except for *A Fistful of Dollars*, and when offered a western to direct he turned it down. [C31075]

His first solo feature as director was the war film *Code Name: Red Roses* (*Rose rosse per il führer*, 1968), *Dirty Dozen*-inspired, which had the misfortune to open against the Italian release of *The Dirty Dozen* itself. [C31076] Then the erotic films of 1969 — *Brucia ragazzo, brucia* (*A Woman on Fire*), which made him known through a scandal over its treatment of female sexuality, and *Amarsi male* (*A Wrong Way to Love*) the same year — and the turn that matters: *I ragazzi del massacro* (*Naked Violence*, 1969), his first adaptation of Giorgio Scerbanenco, Italy's great crime novelist. [C31077] The film reduces the novel to a bare skeleton and is almost a *kammerspiel*: the first half is set entirely in Inspector Lamberti's office as he interrogates eleven teenagers who raped and murdered their evening-school teacher, the opening gang-rape shot in obsessive wide-angle handheld with no direct physical contact shown. [C31078] *La bestia uccide a sangue freddo* (*Slaughter Hotel*, 1971), his only horror film, starring Klaus Kinski and Margaret Lee, is a cult movie and a precursor of the American pulp vogue — and it is also the film that proves the rule: Di Leo's one excursion into horror is still, at bottom, a film about a closed room and the people trapped in it with each other. [C31079]

**Takeaway:** Di Leo learned the genre twice — once from Hammett's sentences as a teenager, once from the western script mill as a young professional — and the second education taught him what the first could not: how to write inside a commercial machine without letting the machine write him. The overnight *Django* rewrite is the whole apprenticeship in one night: a script fixed in the dark, delivered at the airport, no credit taken.

## 2. The Milieu Trilogy as moral geometry

The Trilogia del milieu — *Milano calibro 9* (*Caliber 9*, released Italy 15 February 1972), *La mala ordina* (*The Italian Connection*, 2 September 1972) and *Il Boss* (1 February 1973) — is three variations on one geometry, not a continuing story: no character and no plot carries across the three films, and Tarantino, who modelled *Pulp Fiction*'s three-variations-on-a-theme structure on the trilogy, understood the point exactly. [C31080] All three were produced by Armando Novelli for Cineproduzioni Daunia 70, shot by Franco Villa and cut by Amedeo Giomini; all three did serious business in Italy (₤754,443,000, ₤852,404,000 and ₤774,172,000 respectively); all three refuse catharsis, ending in loss, ambiguity, or no ending at all. [C31081]

The geometry has four poles. The first is **the professional against the amateur**. In *Caliber 9* the contrast is staged as a pair: Ugo Piazza (Gastone Moschin), stone-faced, silent, hands in pockets, "a monolith of a man: cold, with undecipherable icy eyes", against Rocco Musco (Mario Adorf), "vulgarly elegant, with greasy hair and an Errol Flynn moustache", hammy, performing his power in front of his men. [C31082] The professional's virtue is patience and silence; the amateur's vice is noise, display and appetite — and the casting is the argument: Moschin, a comic actor known for simpletons, reinvented as a Milanese version of Lino Ventura in Melville's films, his hands in the pockets of his Marine jacket, his eyes fixed on the ground, walking fast along the canals of Milan. [C31083] In *The Italian Connection* the professionals are the imported hitmen and the amateur is the local apparatus that frames an innocent man and then cannot control the consequences; in *Il Boss* the professional is Lanzetta, whose "expression barely changes throughout", against bosses and climbers who cannot stop talking, lying and manoeuvring. [C31084]

The second pole is **the avenger**, and each film's avenger is different. Chino avenges the blind Don Vincenzo out of loyalty to the old order, and dies doing it — and in doing so becomes Piazza's unwitting instrument, realising "You finally got me to kill The American". [C31085] Canali avenges his wife and daughter out of grief, and his revenge is the only one that is unambiguously his own. [C31086] Cocchi in *Il Boss* avenges his Don, but it is not righteousness: Cocchi wants the Attardi markets for himself — revenge as a cover story for a power grab. [C31087] The third pole is **who kills whom and why**, and the pattern across all three is exact: nobody kills for the reason they claim. [C31088] Rocco kills the couriers because they touched the money, therefore expendable; Rocco kills Don Vincenzo because the old order is in the way; Chino kills the Americano for revenge; Piazza kills Nelly for betrayal with a single punch while dying himself; Rocco kills Luca for disrespecting Piazza's stature — the killer becoming the avenger of his enemy. [C31089] Don Vito kills Canali's wife and daughter as bait; Canali kills Don Vito's gang and Don Vito for revenge; Canali kills both imported hitmen in self-defence, at the cost of his own life, probably. [C31090] Lanzetta kills the Attardi family on orders; Cocchi kidnaps Rina for leverage; the bosses kill each other by proxy for the rest of the film, for business. [C31091] The stated reason — theft, orders, honour — is always a cover for money, fear or advancement.

The fourth pole is **the old code against the new gangs**, and it is the one the films keep testing to destruction. Don Vincenzo's line, quoted in three sources: "They call it the Mafia, but really they're just gangs fighting each other. The real Mafia doesn't exist anymore" — in the fuller version, "When drug dealers want to invest their earnings, they build apartment blocks. So the construction gangs kill them. Where's the Mafia in that? The real mafia is gone." [C31092] The code is "a hoax dreamed up by the bosses to keep the soldiers in line", and the geometry is therefore not good against evil but old lie against new lie: honour, respect and "rules" (Chino: "I'll do whatever you want, but don't ask me to break the rules") against the Americano's "Do unto others what they would do to you, before they do it" and Corrasco's "You can count on my word" — both mottos falsified by the films themselves. [C31093] The cruel irony the critics identify is that the police commissioner in *Caliber 9* is *right* about Piazza — he did steal the money, he is a manipulator — which makes the film's moral geometry self-contradictory by design: "Smartness becomes foolishness, love turns into treason, hostility is replaced by respect." [C31094]

The endings complete the geometry by removing closure itself. *Caliber 9* ends with a twist and a dead hero: Piazza retrieves the blue bag with the $300,000 from an abandoned church — he stole it years ago and orchestrated everything to get the Americano killed — and is shot by Luca, Nelly's lover, after which Rocco beats Luca to death with his bare hands "in respect of the man who was once his enemy", shouting "You can't backstab a man like Ugo Piazza!" [C31095] The final shot is a cigarette slowly burning down on the edge of a table. [C31096] *The Italian Connection* ends with an ambiguous collapse in a scrapyard, Canali severely wounded, the film leaving it open whether he survives. [C31097] *Il Boss* ends with no ending at all — the on-screen sign "continua", a sequel that never materialised. [C31098]

**Takeaway:** The trilogy is a moral geometry in the strict sense: four poles — professional/amateur, avenger, stated reason against real reason, old code against new gangs — and three films that test the same geometry with different weights until closure itself is removed. The mechanism to steal is not the violence but the test: put a motto in a man's mouth, then let the plot falsify it.

## 3. The imported killer as plot engine

The mechanism appears in all three films, in escalating form, and it is the most portable thing Di Leo ever built. [C31099] In *Caliber 9* the Americano himself is the import — an American launderer ruling Milan from behind a curtain, "something like the Wizard of Oz … a puppeteer who doesn't reveal his presence until it is absolutely necessary", an unseen godhead for most of the film whose power works at a distance, through Rocco. [C31100] The engine: a foreign boss whose money and whose rules are imposed on a local milieu that no longer believes in them. In *The Italian Connection* the import is literal and central — two New York hitmen flown to Milan to kill a man for a crime he did not commit, Dave Catania (Henry Silva) and Frank Webster (Woody Strode), sent by New York boss Corso to find and kill Luca Canali, a small-time pimp accused of stealing a $6 million heroin shipment. [C31101] Di Leo's own title for the film was *Ordini da un altro mondo* — "Orders From Another World" — and the "other world" is the American Mafia that subjugates the Italian one, a "transversal, a class view of the underworld". [C31102] The hitmen are the plot engine in the mechanical sense: their arrival forces every local actor to move — Don Vito must cooperate, Canali must run, Canali's friends must choose sides — and their professionalism is also the film's visual joke and its danger: Silva "unusually expressive, flirting with hookers and as much out to have a good time as murder Canali", Strode "the intense, brooding one". [C31103] The engine breaks when the target refuses the role of victim: the imported killers are killed by the man they were sent to kill, in a scrapyard, by a pimp. [C31104] The Silva/Strode duo is cited by Tarantino as the model for Vincent Vega and Jules Winnfield in *Pulp Fiction* — one white, one black, one loose, one brooding — which is the mechanism's afterlife in plain sight. [C31105]

In *Il Boss* the import is inverted: Lanzetta is a home-grown killer (Palermo, orphan, Mafia-raised, adopted at fifteen with all records erased, indoctrinated in a code of honour "as if they're the dogma of a secret cult"), but the *order* is imported — Corrasco, the Sicilian boss, answers to "the bosses in Rome", who forbid inter-family warfare because it is "bad for business". [C31106] The engine here is not a man flown in but a command flown in — and the command is disobeyed, because the local actors' appetites override it. [C31107] The imported killer mechanism has curdled into its opposite: the killer is loyal, the bosses are the ones who break the rules. [C31108] Why it works as an engine is the same in all three: the outsider has no local ties, so he can be given a simple, clean job ("find him, kill him"); his simplicity exposes the local order's complexity and rot; and because he is a professional, his presence raises the stakes for everyone — the locals cannot match him on his own terms, so they cheat, frame and betray, which is what actually drives the plot. [C31109] The failure mode is identical in all three films: the imported instrument develops a will of its own, or the target refuses to be a target, and the local order collapses into the open. [C31110]

**Takeaway:** The imported killer is a plot engine because he is a clean input into a dirty system: give a professional a simple job in a city that runs on betrayal, and the betrayal has to surface to stop him. The engine's three states — godhead, hired hands, disobeyed memo — are the trilogy's real arc.

## 4. The literate gangster picture: Scerbanenco, Hammett, Melville

The Scerbanenco root is the first fact and the deepest. Giorgio Scerbanenco (1911–1969), Ukrainian-born Italian crime writer, "the father of Italian noir", wrote fiction set almost entirely in Milan that "frequently portray[s] the social tensions brought by rapid consumerism and urbanisation in Italy from the 1960s onward, using Milan and its working-class inhabitants as the primary backdrop" — his criminals are products of conditions, poverty, inequality and exploitation, not puzzles to be solved. [C31111] Di Leo adapted him twice before the trilogy — *Naked Violence* (1969), from the third Duca Lamberti novel — and then built the trilogy on him: *Caliber 9* from the 1969 short-story collection *Milano calibro 9*, *The Italian Connection* from a story in the same collection, uncredited. [C31112] "When I read his books, I realized that we shared the same vision of the world. He was a realist, he wrote about petty crooks and small-time criminals", Di Leo said — and the crucial qualification is that *Caliber 9* "preserves the spirit rather than the letter of Scerbanenco's pages": only the central courier-exchange sequence (two simultaneous bomb blasts) comes from the story "Stazione centrale ammazzare subito"; the rest is Di Leo's invention. [C31113] Scerbanenco died in October 1969, months after *Naked Violence* — he never saw the trilogy his books made possible. [C31114]

The Hammett line runs through Gide: Di Leo discovered Hammett as a teenager through André Gide's admiration for him, "intrigued by the fact that Gide cited him as an author on a par with Hemingway or Steinbeck", then watched "old Warner and RKO noir flicks" and set out to find "his own way to Italian film noir — one that would be different from both the sociological approach of Lizzani's films and the 'American' approach". [C31115] *Red Harvest* is the specific Hammett title named, and the inheritance is visible in the films: taciturn protagonists, plots built on a chain of betrayals rather than a mystery, and a world in which institutions are rotten and the only honest men are criminals who keep their word. [C31116] The Melville line is the third strand: the Monthly Film Bulletin's 1974 review already spotted "the Melvillian respect shared by the two professionals Ugo and Chino", and Di Leo "greatly admired" Melville and hoped to collaborate with him someday. [C31117] In the trilogy the Melville inheritance is visible in the professional's ritual (Lanzetta's attaché case, the rifle assembly, the silent approach), the code of honour treated as a religion, and the casting of Moschin as a Milanese Lino Ventura. [C31118] *Gangsters '70* (1968, co-written with Mino Guerrini) turns its nameless gangsters — "Mezzo Miliardo", "Sempresì", "il Viaggiatore" — "into Italian counterparts of the existential protagonists of Melville's *Le deuxième souffle* or *Le cercle rouge*", with themes of "defeat, betrayal, dignity and self-respect" and "no room for hope". [C31119]

What makes the writing literate, against the genre norm, is stated directly by Curti: "di Leo's characters talk in a peculiar, literate manner, even when they are murdering, torturing or making love, while the use of dialectal terms both as a euphonic device and as an anthropological connotation achieves extremely complex effects." [C31120] The instances are concrete. Dialogue as Socratic debate: the Wolff/Pistilli police scenes in *Caliber 9* are "something approaching 'Socratic dialogues'" in which Mercuri — "the 'voice' of di Leo" — argues the police "should act on a larger scale … Let's get the ones who evict people and who beat up students and workers", and that "The Americano is an effect, not a cause [….] The mass of Southerners who come to live up North do the most menial jobs … No wonder they turn to crime", while Wolff answers "Stop being a subversive [….] You read too much left-wing material." [C31121] The same scenes allude to the Red Brigades, then "beginning to hit the news" in 1972 — and Di Leo later regretted keeping them, feeling they hampered pacing and pulled focus from the criminals, which is itself a measure of how much the literate impulse cost him in genre terms. [C31122] The unsaid as language: in *Il Boss*, "death orders come in vague, allusive formulas (corpses become 'complications' or 'difficulties'), where what is left unsaid has more importance than the words that are spent, in a sort of secret code known only to the affiliates", and murder is discussed on the telephone in financial jargon — transactions, payments — as if it were bookkeeping. [C31123] The aphorism as thesis: "You have to accept it"; "Do unto others … before they do it"; "You can count on my word"; "We are all Vietnamized!" — each film is built around one or two such sentences, and the plot is the sentence being tested to destruction. [C31124] *Caliber 9* "is in many ways a rejection of the ideas to be found in a movie such as *Dirty Harry*" — the rogue-cop fantasy is answered by showing the cops as cynical and the criminals as the ones with a code. [C31125] Film Comment's summary of the sensibility: Di Leo approached the genre "with a moralist's sensibility", and "the hallmark of Di Leo's films is a clear-eyed consideration of moral choices and their consequences." [C31126]

**Takeaway:** The literate gangster picture is not a crime film with good dialogue pasted on; it is a crime film whose dialogue IS the mechanism — Socratic debates between cops, coded euphemisms between killers, and one aphorism per film that the plot exists to falsify. Steal the aphorism, not the accent.

## 5. Black Milan: the visual system

The look is a place before it is a style. Offscreen: "Milan, the so called 'black Milan' described in Scerbanenco's books, has never been so impressive: foggy canals, decaying popular houses, sleazy nightclubs, empty parking lots. Di Leo doesn't go for sociological notations, but films Italy's biggest Northern city with a realism unknown to genre cinema." [C31127] The visual argument is made by contrast: "The stark contrast between ultramodern, stylish apartments and squalid hotel rooms echoes the hiatus between the luxurious headquarters of the big boss, the 'Americano' … and the poor, minuscule apartment where the old and blind mafia godfather … lives." [C31128] *The Italian Connection* was shot on location in Milan and at Rome's Dear Studios; *Il Boss* is set in Palermo, shot as "a nocturnal, indefinite, depopulated town". [C31129] The set pieces are staged in recognisable Milanese spaces: the bowling alley exchange, the hotel room trashed by Rocco, the abandoned church outside Milan where the money is hidden, the piazza Duomo courier relay. [C31130]

The violence is staged direct, unlingering and economic. Fandor: "Di Leo has none of the baroque style of the *giallo* nor the operatic bloodbaths of the spaghetti westerns. The violence of these films is extreme but direct and his shoots the same way, with a camera that constantly brings us into the middle of every conflict and an almost garish display of blunt color." [C31131] The signature example is the kidnapping of Rina in *Il Boss*: "the entire kidnap sequence, from the kick to the boyfriend's balls to the wide shot of the car exiting the frame in the distance occupies a mere 18 waste-free seconds of screen time", including "a single breathless four-second shot" in which Melende tapes Rina's mouth and "the camera whip-tilts down to the rear wheel of the car just in time for it to speed out of frame". [C31132] The opening of the same film is an exercise in ritual: Lanzetta crosses the road carrying a large, ominous case, opens it, assembles the rifle and telescopic sight, "rarely will he show even a flicker of emotion" — the hitman ritual as a genre grammar, shot in "beautifully composed" frames that "exploit the stark noir shadows of a city at night". [C31133] The editing rhythm is the cut as a mood change: "In the blink of an edit, the mood instantly changes" — from the morgue, weeping and autopsy slabs, to two students "walk[ing] cheerily arm-in-arm through the streets of Palermo serenaded by a score that just oozes happy contentment", and seconds later the kidnapping. The abruptness is the point: contentment and violence are adjacent, not separated. [C31134]

Music is identity, not accompaniment. Bacalov's scores for *Caliber 9* (with Osanna's prog-rock) and *Il Boss* are repeatedly singled out — "The jazzy score by prolific composer Luis Bacalov sets the mood with its deep bass line and jarring percussion" — and "The importance of music in the identity of 1970s Italian genre cinema cannot be overstated". [C31135] *The Italian Connection* is the exception in the trilogy, scored by Armando Trovajoli rather than Bacalov. [C31136] Performance is the last part of the visual system: the trilogy's look depends on casting against type and on faces — Moschin's stillness against Adorf's Rocco "deformed with twitches and scares", Silva's "chiselled features, laser stare and stoic expression" and the "clinically precise performance … with the minimum of perfectly timed cues". [C31137] The first Adorf–Moschin scene in Rocco's car is "a small masterpiece in its own, with Rocco alternating enticements and allusive treats while Ugo stays silent". [C31138] And the period surface is meaning: "Di Leo's films are supremely of the Seventies, from the clothes and accessories … to the liquor of choice, which is, of course, J&B scotch, official drink of Italian exploitation, its red-and-yellow label radiating cosmopolitan chic." [C31139]

**Takeaway:** Black Milan is a visual system built from three contrasts — ultramodern against squalid, stillness against twitch, contentment against violence in a single cut — and the violence is economic because the contrast does the work. A kidnapping in eighteen seconds needs no lingering: the cut before it was a love song.

## 6. The criminals' side: the post-trilogy run

The principle is stated by Pulici and it governs everything after the trilogy: "He has almost always told stories from the other side, that is, from the side of the criminals." [C31140] It is wrong to call his films "police" films — and the proof is that the one time the police appear in a title, the image is negative. *Il poliziotto è marcio* (*Shoot First, Die Later*, 1974), his only true poliziottesco in the cop-protagonist sense, opens with Inspector Domenico Malacarne (Luc Merenda) proudly announcing his corruption: "Yes sir, I'm corrupt! … I got 60 million in my bank account…" [C31141] The mechanism is identification built and then deliberately broken: Di Leo builds the first half-hour as a heroic identification machine — a car chase "among the most exhilarating action sequences ever seen in an Italian film" — and then pulls the rug out, because the hero is the villain. [C31142] The title alone was a provocation: posters were ripped up, anonymous phone threats ("You have to stop with this crap!"), and the film was unreleased theatrically in America, surfacing on home video decades later. [C31143] It is based on William P. McGivern's 1956 novel *Rogue Cop*, and Merenda — "the Alain Delon to Di Leo's Melville", in TCM's phrase — became Di Leo's post-trilogy leading man, the corrupt cop in 1974, the poor father in *Kidnap Syndicate* (1975), the con-artist in *Nick the Sting* (1976). [C31144]

The post-trilogy run is a study in shrinking autonomy, and the films record it. *Colpo in canna* (*Loaded Guns*, 1975; Offscreen dates it 1974) is "divertissement in two acts", a sunny Neapolitan crime farce in which Ursula Andress plays a hostess who "behaves just like a man" — beats and is beaten, cheats, deceives, beds whom she wants — and the script originally made her bisexual, dropped as too "strong" for Italian audiences; the mechanism is the noir macho attitude turned upside down, men as brainless studs, the woman's body as her weapon. [C31145] *La città sconvolta: caccia spietata ai rapitori* (*Kidnap Syndicate*, 1975) is work for hire, co-scripted with producer Galliano Juso: a gang kidnaps two boys, one the son of a rich engineer (James Mason, who refuses to pay) and one the son of a poor mechanic (Luc Merenda, who would give everything) — the class contrast makes Mason the real villain — and the first half owes much to Kurosawa's *High and Low*, while the second half is nearly a remake of *La mala ordina*, "without the power and inspiration of the model". [C31146] *Gli amici di Nick Hezard* (*Nick the Sting*, 1976) is work for hire from a pre-existing script by Alberto Silvestri, a *Sting* rip-off in which the shoot went over budget, Merenda is unconvincing as a master con-artist, and expensive scenes were cut — Di Leo: "It was absurd — just as if you refuse caviar and go for sardines…" — and the mechanism on offer is a good script visibly falling apart under budget, the film as a record of its own mutilation. [C31147] *I padroni della città* (*Rulers of the City* / *Mister Scarface*, 1976), the last film produced by Daunia 70, is the ironic, picaresque Roman underworld of small-time crooks, Jack Palance as "Mr. Scarface", an homage to Pasolini's *Accattone* a year after Pasolini's death; the Harry Baer street pursuit through Rome is the sequence Tarantino kept in mind for the Steve Buscemi flashbacks in *Reservoir Dogs*. [C31148] The scripts for other directors in the same years — Guerrieri's *Liberi armati pericolosi* (*Young, Violent, Dangerous*, 1976) and Deodato's *Uomini si nasce poliziotti si muore* (*Live Like a Cop, Die Like a Man*, 1976), a hyperviolent, parodic buddy-cop film with a crime-predicting computer à la Philip K. Dick's "The Minority Report" — show the same mind working at one remove, and the Deodato film is a "jaw-dropping evisceration of buddy-cop tropes". [C31149]

Three misattributions are struck here and must not be carried into any later study: *The Violent Professionals* (1973) is Sergio Martino's, written by Ernesto Gastaldi; *Milano odia* / *Almost Human* (1974) is Umberto Lenzi's, also Gastaldi-written, with Milian and Silva; *The Big Racket* (1976) is Enzo G. Castellari's. [C31150] All three belong to the same chain and the same years, which is exactly why they are so often filed under Di Leo's name — and the confusion is itself evidence of how completely he owned the form in the public mind. [C31151]

**Takeaway:** The post-trilogy run is the same moralist working with less room: the identification machine that breaks, the class contrast that makes the rich man the villain, the farce that inverts the macho code, the good script visibly mutilated by budget. The principle never changes — the story is told from the criminals' side — only the budget does.

## 7. The long fade and the rediscovery

*Diamanti sporchi di sangue* (*Blood and Diamonds*, 1978) was Di Leo's last truly successful film, and it opened in Rome on 17 March 1978, the day after the Red Brigades kidnapped Aldo Moro; the city at night was deserted, cinemas included. [C31152] The script was originally called *Roma calibro 9*: cat burglar Guido Mauri (Claudio Cassinelli) does five years in jail, is released, and seeks revenge on the boss he believes betrayed him — a reversal of *Caliber 9* in which the hero does everything wrong and ends by surrendering to be killed under the old rules, only for the boss to shoot him in the shoulder instead: "there is no catharsis this time". [C31153] *Avere vent'anni* (*To Be Twenty*, 1978), Gloria Guida and Lilli Carati as two hitchhikers in one of the last hippie communes, is a controversial, ambitious pro-feminist parable whose abrupt, cruel, downbeat ending caused the film to be hastily re-edited, its message "not just softened but completely overturned" — the re-edit as a second author, the released film arguing the opposite of the shot film. [C31154] *Vacanze per un massacro – Madness* (*Madness*, 1980; Offscreen dates it 1979) is an erotic *kammerspiel*: Joe Dallesandro as an escaped convict who becomes the uninvited observer of a seedy love triangle in a desolate country house. [C31155] The television series *L'assassino ha le ore contate* (1981) — six episodes, Agatha Christie in vein, drawing on his Scerbanenco experience — was intended for RAI Uno and never aired; it remains unreleased. [C31156] *Killer contro killers* (*Killer vs. Killers*, 1985), his last film, was originally conceived as a remake of John Huston's *The Asphalt Jungle*; Henry Silva leads a commando sent to steal a formula from a military base, the heist happens halfway through and matters less than the betrayal after it, and the final showdown is in a zoo, Silva with a bazooka among escaped animals — never released theatrically in Italy, surfacing on DVD twenty years later. [C31157] The cause of the end is structural, not personal: "the inexorable crisis of Italian cinema pushed him into a forced retirement", and between 1980 and 1985 Italian cinema admissions fell from 241.8 to 123.1 million per year, theatres closing or becoming adult-only venues. [C31158] The unmade projects measure what was lost with him: *Bazooka*, an Italian *Rambo* that never happened; *Il pederasta* (later *Uno di quelli*), the story of a homosexual man that would have been the first film to tackle male homosexuality without taboo; *Il dio Kurt*, from Alberto Moravia's play, with Henry Fonda and Charlotte Rampling attached and a budget of one billion lire, blocked by distributors over the subject. [C31159]

The rediscovery came from the margin, as it usually does. The cult magazine *Nocturno Cinema* reintroduced Di Leo to a new generation from the mid-1990s — first a "massive, groundbreaking interview" in September–October 1996, at his home, when he was "almost forgotten in Italy", then features on his films, a monograph by Davide Pulici (2000), and a whole monographic issue, Dossier Nocturno n.14, *Calibro 9. Il cinema di Fernando Di Leo* (September 2003), a few months before his death. [C31160] The 61st Venice International Film Festival (1–11 September 2004) launched *The Secret History of Italian Cinema*, a new official retrospective section aimed at restoring sides of Italian cinema "forgotten, invisible, unknown or misunderstood", and its first edition, "Italian Kings of the B's", included five Di Leo films — *Naked Violence* (1969), *Caliber 9* (1972), *The Italian Connection* (1972), *The Boss* (1973), *Rulers of the City* (1976) — with Quentin Tarantino and Joe Dante as godfathers; Tarantino presented a special homage to Di Leo. [C31161] Offscreen: "The 2004 Venice retrospective … marked the definitive critical recognition of Fernando di Leo as one of the most important Italian directors of the 1970s, and a true innovator of the crime and noir genre." [C31162] Tarantino's championing is specific and verifiable: watching *Mister Scarface* as a teenager working in a Santa Monica video store "was what flipped the switch" and led him to watch all of Di Leo's films; he calls Di Leo "the master" of the Italian gangster movie — "I am a huge fan of Italian gangster movies, I've seen them all and Fernando Di Leo is, without a doubt, the master of this genre" — and called *La mala ordina* "a masterpiece of Italian noir" and *Caliber 9* "the greatest Italian noir". [C31163] The afterlife continues: Raro Video's *Fernando Di Leo Crime Collection* Blu-rays (2012) restored from 35mm negatives in collaboration with the Venice Film Festival; the Metrograph retrospective "Fernando Di Leo: Pulp Maestro" in New York (2024); the Fernando Di Leo Short Film Festival in his birthplace, San Ferdinando di Puglia, founded after Tarantino's praise. [C31164] And the newest book-length study, Curti & Monetti's *Fernando Di Leo: Master of Italian Film Noir* (McFarland, 2026), is the first English-language monograph — the rediscovery, twenty years on, still arriving. [C31165]

**Takeaway:** Di Leo's career is a parable about the distance between the work and its recognition: the films were made in five years, 1969–1976, and recognised thirty years later, by a cult magazine, a video-store clerk turned director, and a festival retrospective. The work did not change in the interval. The audience did.

## 8. Aesthetic logic: the moralist in the genre machine

Di Leo's aesthetic logic is a single sentence with three clauses. [C31166] First: **the genre is the alibi, not the subject.** He made westerns he thought infantile, erotic films the censors watched, crime films the politicians sued over — and in each case the genre was the cover under which a moral essay could be smuggled past the people who paid for it. The anticlerical episode of 1963 was censored because the joke was on the surface; the trilogy was not, because the joke was the structure. [C31167] Second: **the mechanism is the morality.** He does not preach; he builds a machine — a suspended accusation, an imported killer, a motto — and lets it run until it falsifies itself. The moral judgement is not in the dialogue, it is in what the machine does to the people inside it: Piazza's patience is a weapon, Canali's grief is a conversion, Lanzetta's loyalty is a trap. [C31168] Third: **the professional is the only honest figure, and honesty is fatal.** In a world where bosses lie, cops are corrupt, lovers betray and the code is a hoax, the man who keeps his word is the one the system must destroy — and the films destroy him, every time, without sentimentality: Piazza shot by his girlfriend's lover, Canali collapsing in a scrapyard, Lanzetta left standing in a film with no ending because there is nothing left to end with. [C31169]

The visual corollary follows: stillness against noise. Moschin's hands in his pockets against Adorf's twitches; Silva's unchanging expression against bosses who cannot stop talking; a cigarette burning down on a table against a city that never stops moving. [C31170] The cut is the second corollary: contentment and violence adjacent, not separated, because in Di Leo's Milan they are adjacent — the morgue and the students arm-in-arm, the love song and the kidnapping eighteen seconds later. [C31171] The third corollary is the refusal of catharsis, which is not a pose but a position: a film that ends "continua" is a film that says the machine is still running, and the audience leaves the cinema inside it. [C31172] The failure mode to avoid, in any piece built from this study, is the operatic one: Di Leo has "none of the baroque style of the *giallo* nor the operatic bloodbaths of the spaghetti westerns", and a piece that makes his violence spectacular, his dialogue purple, or his Milan picturesque has missed the point — the point is the directness, the literate flatness, the economy. [C31173] What survives him is not a style but a test: put a sentence in a man's mouth, build a machine around it, and watch the machine prove the sentence false. [C31174]
