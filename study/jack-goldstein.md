## The life: the artist who removed himself

Jack Goldstein was born September 27, 1945, in Montreal, Quebec, Canada, to a Jewish family, and moved as a boy to Los Angeles, where he attended high school in the 1960s and first took an interest in art. He was a Canadian-born, California- and New York-based performance and conceptual artist who became a post-conceptual painter in the 1980s. (C22508, leg 1 C22300-C22302; synthesis C22456)

He earned a B.F.A. from the Chouinard Art Institute, Los Angeles, in 1969, and an M.F.A. from the California Institute of the Arts, Valencia, in 1972; his own CV gives the same spans as 1966-1969 (B.F.A.) and 1970-1972 (M.F.A.). One year is UNRESOLVED and must be carried, not settled: Chrissie Iles's Artforum obituary says his Chouinard training ran "from 1966 to 1970" against the CV and Nasher giving 1966-1969. He was a member of the inaugural class of CalArts, where he worked in post-studio art under John Baldessari, and he was Baldessari's teaching assistant -- in his own words, "I took a class with John and liked him immediately. The next year I became a grad student and in one class was John's teaching assistant." In his first CalArts year he was also Nam June Paik's assistant (Paik was new to Los Angeles and did not know how to drive; Goldstein was tasked with teaching him), and at one point teaching assistant to sculpture professor Wolfgang Stoerchle. (C22509, leg 1 C22303-C22306; synthesis C22457)

While still a student at CalArts in 1972, Goldstein buried himself alive as his thesis performance: with a stethoscope attached to his chest, he breathed air from plastic tubes while a red light above ground flashed to the rhythm of his beating heart. The early films are the same body enduring for the camera -- in "A Nail" (W01, 1971) he hammers a nail into wood and pries it loose with his teeth; in "A Glass of Milk" (W03, 1972) he pounds on a card table until the milk spills. Helene Winer -- then director of Pomona College Art Gallery, later a co-founder of Metro Pictures -- gave him his first solo show, in 1971, encouraged his move to New York, and played a central role in his life. The rival first-solo claim (Eugenia Butler Gallery, Los Angeles, 1972) comes from the same unreliable mirror that printed the wrong death date and is not independently confirmed or refuted; Iles is preferred. He moved to New York in 1974, dividing his time between Los Angeles and New York, and during this period shared a studio building with James Welling. By the mid-1970s he had stopped appearing in his own films and performances and instead hired actors, stuntmen, and light and sound technicians from the film industry. The body goes underground in 1972 and never comes back. (C22510, leg 1 C22307, C22309-C22313; leg 2 W01-W03; synthesis C22459)

He became one of the linchpins of the Pictures Group, which gained its first recognition at Artists Space in New York in the fall of 1977. "Pictures", organised by critic Douglas Crimp, ran September 24-October 29, 1977 (opening reception September 23) with five artists: Troy Brauntuch, Jack Goldstein, Sherrie Levine, Robert Longo, and Philip Smith. Goldstein's contribution was coloured phonograph records, short films, and photographs. Always print the five-name Artists Space list: the Met's 2009 press release mis-states the 1977 roster as four names, omitting Philip Smith. One source says Crimp deleted Smith and added Cindy Sherman in the spring 1979 "October" expansion of his catalogue essay -- that swap is single-sourced and is NOT printed here as fact; re-verify before any later study prints it. Metro Pictures was founded in 1980 by Helene Winer and Janelle Reiring; its inaugural exhibitions featured Sherman, Longo, Brauntuch, Goldstein, Lawler, Levine, Welling, Prince, and Walter Robinson. Correction to the #688-#690 legs, carried here: Louise Lawler's "Arranged by Louise Lawler" opened her first Metro Pictures solo in 1982, NOT 1981 -- her 1981 solo was at Jancar/Kuhlenschmidt, Los Angeles -- and the entry wall was an arrangement of works the gallery had on hand by Longo, Sherman, Goldstein, Simmons, and Welling. That is Goldstein's strongest evidenced joint-show tie: his work physically hung, arranged by Lawler, in her Metro solo. He was courted by gallerist Mary Boone (Kraus 2004 review, single low-reliability source -- do NOT print Boone as his dealer). (C22511, leg 1 C22310-C22311; leg 3 C22403-C22405, C22409, C22417-C22419; synthesis C22462, C22465)

The records are films with the picture removed. In 1976 he issued "A Suite of Nine 7-Inch Records with Sound Effects" (W12): nine 45 rpm records in coloured vinyl, edition of 100, each disc one event only -- "one object, one action, one event" -- a forest burning (red-and-white marbled vinyl, beginning mid-event and ending abruptly with no fade), a tornado you hear but never see (purple vinyl that spins like one), a German shepherd barking (red vinyl, with minor exceptions identical to the soundtrack of "Shane" the film). He wanted a tornado film, could not film a real tornado, so he made a sound recording instead: "the record gives you the same experience." "The Six-Minute Drown" (W13, 1977) is six minutes of wildly agitated water interspersed with human screams -- a film climax stretched until its suspense curve collapses and the sound detaches from any imagined film. The correction to the brief, carried: "The Burning Forest" is a sound work, not a painting -- one record in this suite. And there are no "aphorism paintings with text over found images": the aphorisms are text works on paper (W20) and the paintings are found-photograph works with no text; the two lines never combine. (C22512, leg 2 W12-W13, W20; synthesis C22468, C22471)

He began to concentrate on painting in the late 1970s and increasingly after 1980. The paintings were based on found photographs of natural phenomena, science and technology, recording what he called "the spectacular instant" -- streaking fighter jets, lightning storms, exploding nebulae, city skylines lit by fireworks or bombing raids. He hired painters to execute his canvases -- among the first contemporary painters to do so -- and at one point had seven assistants working with him, building the immaculate airbrushed surfaces up from layers of sanded gesso with spray paint applied at the end; he directed the work, shifting the emphasis from "making" to "producing." The paintings fall into series -- war spectacles 1980-83, nature 1983-86, technology 1986-90 -- and individual untitled canvases should not be given a subject beyond their series. As the 1980s wore on his paintings sold less well; reluctant to teach rather than practise full-time, he left New York in 1992 ("In 1992, Jack left New York and disappeared", Artforum) and lived the decade in relative isolation in California. (C22513, leg 1 C22314-C22316; leg 2 W17-W19; synthesis C22459)

The last works dissolve the author altogether. The "Aphorisms" (1982-85) are one sentence per 8.5 x 11 sheet -- "My door is on fire", "Tomorrow never comes", later turning cinematic: "The hero died in slow motion". "Totems: Selected Writings" (1988-90) is a box of 100 pages of computer-generated texts in vertically oriented totem-pole forms, with readymade clip art inserted, demanding a top-to-bottom rather than left-to-right reading. "Selected Writings, 1992-2002" is eighteen bound volumes, each page three sentences taken from literary and philosophical sources (Plato, Kant, Freud, Kafka among them), the first quote in capitals, the second lowercase, the third bold -- Goldstein read Kafka, Hegel, Nietzsche backwards to break their narrative, lifted phrases, and collaged three to a page with enormous margins. Language treated exactly like a found photograph: appropriated, cropped, enlarged, left to float in empty space. One of his last projects was the memoir "Jack Goldstein and the CalArts Mafia", written with Richard Hertz, published by Minneola Press in 2003, around the time of his death. (C22514, leg 1 C22317-C22318; leg 2 W20; synthesis C22459)

Goldstein died March 14, 2003, in San Bernardino, California, aged 57, by suicide by hanging. This date is RESOLVED this run, closing the #696 guard: the rival "November 14, 2003, New York City, age 58" is WRONG and must not be printed -- its only source, isitaholidaytoday.com, contradicts itself on the same page (body: "passed away on March 14, 2003"; Timeline box: "November 14, 2003, in New York City"), and the March date is corroborated by the contemporaneous record -- NYT obituary March 19, 2003 (Roberta Smith), LA Times obituary March 20, 2003 (Suzanne Muchnic), Artforum's May 2003 obituary ("committed suicide in San Bernardino in March at the age of fifty-seven"), Artnet, and the michele didier dossier ("committed suicide on 14 March 2003"). The Believer essay adds, secondhand and single-sourced, that he hanged himself from a tree in front of the trailer he had lived in for more than a decade -- the hanging is multiply attested, the tree/trailer detail is not, and it is carried as such. (C22515, leg 1 C22319-C22322; synthesis C22458)

The return came just before the end, and the recognition just after. The Whitney presented the solo show "Jack Goldstein: Films and Performance", June 27-September 22, 2002, including a restaging of his 1979 performance "Two Boxers" -- his return to New York for the first time in a decade. Less than a year after his death he was featured in the 2004 Whitney Biennial as a major film influence alongside Stan Brakhage. "Jack Goldstein x 10,000", the first American retrospective, was organised by the Orange County Museum of Art (shown 2012, guest curator Philipp Kaiser) and presented at The Jewish Museum, New York, May 10-September 29, 2013, with nearly 40 works including 17 films. His group exhibitions include "Pictures" (1977), Documenta 7 (1982), Documenta 8 (1987), and "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984", Metropolitan Museum of Art, April 21-August 2, 2009 (use April 21 per the Met's own release, not Wikipedia's April 29) -- 160+ works by 30 artists, organised by Douglas Eklund, the release calling Goldstein Baldessari's teaching assistant, "the ringleader of this group that came to be known in New York, and ... one of the most important innovators of the 'Pictures' artists." His work is held by the Whitney, MoMA, Tate, the Met, MOCA Los Angeles, The Broad, MMK Frankfurt, the Orange County Museum of Art, the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago, MACBA Barcelona, and the Vancouver Art Gallery. Tate specifically holds "A Glass of Milk" (1972), "A Spotlight" (1972), "Shane" (1975), and "Under Water Sea Fantasy" (1983). Two holdings limits: no Art Institute of Chicago collection holding could be confirmed (site search returned nothing; AIC appears only as the 1975 "Avant-Garde Film" exhibition venue), and of MoMA's holdings only "Untitled", 1981 (object 237.2015) was confirmed as a specific accession record -- treat other specific MoMA works as unconfirmed. (C22516, leg 1 C22323-C22329; leg 3 C22406-C22408, C22424; synthesis C22460-C22461, C22463)


## M1 -- The looped fragment

A few seconds of found or staged footage is cut out of its source, its beginning and end joined so it repeats without resolution. The MGM lion roars, blinks, roars again (W06, "Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer", 1975, 16mm colour sound loop, c. 2-3 min -- the duration is disputed, 2 min per Whitney/Kantor/Wikipedia against 3 min per Border Crossings and the Jewish Museum caption, so no single figure is quoted as settled); the diver jumps, twists, vanishes into black, jumps again (W15, "The Jump", 1978, 16mm colour silent, 26 sec loop); the dog barks on cue, over and over (W05, "Shane", 1975, 16mm colour sound, 3 min -- one 1301PE news item dates it 1973, every museum/gallery source says 1975). The viewer waits for the story to continue; it never does. Kantor adds that the MGM picture "moves partly in reverse" -- the loop is not a clean repeat. The loop turns a logo into a neurotic tic (Kantor's word) and a dive into an eternal present. (C22517, leg 2 W05, W06, W15 and M1; synthesis C22469)

The same fragment returns at every scale of the career. The records are loops you cannot see: "A German Shepherd" (W12) is the bark without the dog, pressed in red vinyl. The paintings are loops you cannot move: a lightning bolt shearing a sky that fills a 72 x 96 in canvas (W19), a burning city lost in a huge black field (W18) -- the spectacular instant, frozen at the moment before anything changes, and then held there forever. A prompt built from this mechanism must make the repeat visible -- a seam, a blink, a jump that restarts -- and let the viewer feel the story refusing to continue. (C22518, leg 2 W12, W18, W19 and M1; synthesis C22469)

**Takeaway:** cut one short fragment out of a familiar moving image, join its end to its beginning, and let it repeat without resolution -- the viewer waits for the story to continue and it never does. (C22519)


## M2 -- The single dramatic event, isolated from narrative

One action -- a bark, a spill, a knife filling with light, a tree falling, a forest burning -- is lifted out of any before/after and presented alone, usually against a monochrome field. In "A Glass of Milk" (W03, 1972) pounding continues until the milk spills and the film can end; in "The Knife" (W09, 1975) reflected light slowly fills the sleek steel weapon like a liquid -- first in green, then in red, blue and yellow -- and nothing else happens, a distillation of Hitchcock/de Palma suspense with no victim, no hand, no story, only light filling a blade; in the records (W12) each disc is one event only. The event is the whole work; there is no plot to dilute it. (C22520, leg 2 W03, W09, W12 and M2; synthesis C22470)

The same isolation runs the whole career. "A Nail" (W01) is one pointless manual task; "Some Butterflies" (W07, 1975) is one hand waggling paper butterflies on its fingertips against an electric blue field; "Bone China" (W11, 1976) is a painted bird flapping frantically around a china plate to the sound of beating wings, getting nowhere; the late paintings are one event -- a lightning bolt, a volcanic eruption, a burning city -- blown up until it fills the frame. The generative rule follows: pick one event, remove everything before and after it, and let the event be the entire duration of the piece. (C22521, leg 2 W01, W07, W11, W19 and M2; synthesis C22470)

**Takeaway:** isolate one dramatic event -- a spill, a bark, a blade filling with light -- strip away all before and after, and present it alone against a flat field; the event is the whole work. (C22522)


## M3 -- The sound-image split

The soundtrack is detached from its picture and issued as a separate object (a vinyl record), or the picture is shown silent while the sound lives elsewhere. "A German Shepherd" the record (W12) is, with minor exceptions, identical to the soundtrack of "Shane" the film (W05) -- same bark, no dog visible. "The Tornado" (W12) is a tornado you hear but never see, on purple vinyl that spins like one. "The Jump" (W15) is silent, its spectacle purely visual. The split forces the missing half to be supplied mentally -- the listener "sees" a film that was never made. Goldstein's own account: he wanted a tornado film, could not film a real tornado, so he made a sound recording instead -- "the record gives you the same experience." (C22523, leg 2 W05, W12, W15 and M3; synthesis C22471)

The split is a machine for making the viewer do the work. Play "The Burning Forest" (W12) and the listener pictures a forest fire from crackle and roar; the record begins mid-event and ends abruptly with no fade, so there is no cue for when the imagined film starts or stops. In the gallery the split becomes spatial: at OCMA the MGM lion was projected on its own wall next to "Shane", so lion and dog answered each other across the room -- two halves of a film that was never shot, barking and roaring at each other. A prompt built from this mechanism must separate the sound from the picture and let the missing half be supplied by whoever is watching. (C22524, leg 2 W06, W12 and M3; synthesis C22471)

**Takeaway:** detach the soundtrack from its picture -- issue the sound as its own object, or show the picture silent -- and let the viewer supply the missing half in their head. (C22525)


## M4 -- The mental image (sound or text as a prompt for an inner picture)

The work supplies only a title, a sound, or a sentence, and the image is completed in the viewer's head. Playing "The Burning Forest" (W12) the listener pictures a forest fire from crackle and roar. Reading "My door is on fire" (W20) the reader pictures a domestic disaster. "Two Boxers" (W16, 1979) ends on an empty stage and "the picture [is] now purely mental." The Buchholz exhibition premise states it directly: the text and sound works "prompt an invocation of imagery in the mind." Nothing is shown; everything is pictured, by the audience. (C22526, leg 2 W12, W16, W20 and M4; synthesis C22472)

The mechanism scales from a sentence to a whole performance. The aphorisms are one line per sheet, pinned up in the studio and photographed by James Welling -- "Driving 5 miles to put gas in the car when there's a gas station around the corner", "Tomorrow never comes", "The hero died in slow motion" -- each a film that exists only as a caption. "Totems" (W20) stacks computer-generated texts in totem-pole columns with readymade clip art, demanding a top-to-bottom rather than left-to-right reading, more margin than text: the emptiness around the words is part of the prompt. A prompt built from this mechanism must give the viewer a sound or a sentence and withhold the picture entirely -- the image happens in the head or not at all. (C22527, leg 2 W16, W20 and M4; synthesis C22472)

**Takeaway:** give only a sound, a title, or a sentence, and withhold the picture -- the image is completed in the viewer's head, or it does not happen at all. (C22528)


## M5 -- Appropriation with the signature stripped

A mass-media image (MGM logo, Riefenstahl diver, van Gogh painting, geology-textbook volcano, thriller soundtrack) is taken, its identifying marks removed or its context cut away, and re-presented as if it were the artist's own image. The MGM lion without the company name (W06); the "Olympia" diver with the stadium, the crowd, and the water blacked out, leaving only sparkling light (W15); van Gogh traced through paper so the hand only mimics a reproduction (W04, "The Portrait of Pere Tanguy", 1974 -- the hinge work, Grade C: filmography listing plus Kantor's paragraph only, no medium, duration, or description confirmed beyond "film, 1974"); a volcano from a textbook blown up until its origin is unreadable (W19). The viewer recognises the image and simultaneously cannot place it. (C22529, leg 2 W04, W06, W15, W19 and M5; synthesis C22473)

The stripping is the point, and it is what separates Goldstein from his teacher's students who borrow openly. Salle repaints the borrowing by hand and leaves the tell of the translation visible; Goldstein removes the signature and lets the image stand as if it had no author at all. In "The Jump" the rotoscoping was done by paid technicians, so even the hand that transformed the diver is not his. The mechanism is subtraction: take a famous image, cut away everything that names it, and what remains is an icon with no owner. A prompt built from this mechanism must start from a recognisable image and remove its identifying marks until the viewer knows it and cannot place it. (C22530, leg 2 W04, W15 and M5; synthesis C22473)

**Takeaway:** take a famous image, strip its identifying marks and context, and re-present it as if it had no author -- the viewer recognises it and cannot place it at the same time. (C22531)


## M6 -- Professional finish, amateur/pointless content (Hollywood means, anti-Hollywood ends)

Goldstein rents Hollywood equipment, hires technicians, stuntmen, trained animals and painters, and applies commercial production values to an action with no commercial purpose. A trained German shepherd barking on cue like a session musician (W05); rotoscoping by paid technicians for a 26-second diver (W15); airbrushed canvases with no brushstroke, made by assistants (W18-W19). The slickness is the content: the work looks like a product and behaves like a joke on products. His line, quoted in Frieze: the art world is "a microcosm of Hollywood... only there is less money going around." (C22532, leg 2 W05, W15, W18-W19 and M6; synthesis C22474)

The finish is doing the meaning. In "Shane" the dog is a Hollywood-trained animal and the trainer is never seen -- only the command's effect, a bark laid down rhythmically "as if he were laying down some beats". In the paintings the finish is literally industrial: layers of sanded gesso, spray paint applied last, boxlike stretchers standing six or more inches off the wall, no brushstroke anywhere. The work arrives looking finished in the commercial sense -- and then does nothing a commercial object is supposed to do. A prompt built from this mechanism must spend its craft budget on polish and its content budget on nothing: immaculate surface, pointless event. (C22533, leg 2 W05, W18-W19 and M6; synthesis C22474)

**Takeaway:** apply full commercial production values -- trained animals, paid technicians, airbrushed finish -- to an action with no commercial purpose; the slickness is the joke. (C22534)


## M7 -- The monochrome field as a stage

The event is set against a single flat colour -- red for MGM (W06), black for "The Jump" (W15), electric blue for "Some Butterflies" (W07), deep blue for "The Chair" (W10), black for the 1980s paintings (W18-W19). The field removes all context, so the figure reads as an icon, not a scene. A hand against an electric blue background waggles tiny paper butterflies on each fingertip (W07); a rain of multi-hued costume feathers slowly covers a black wooden seat until the chair itself nearly disappears into the deep blue background (W10); a diver becomes a figure of red-golden light against a black void (W15). The field is doing the framing that a narrative used to do. (C22535, leg 2 W06, W07, W10, W15, W18-W19 and M7; synthesis C22475)

The field is also installable as architecture. "The Jump" was projected onto a red wall in a space barely illuminated by two fluorescent sticks, and in Kantor's account onto a fuchsia-coloured wall illuminated by black lights -- the wall itself becomes part of the work, the room tinted by the field. The paintings do the same thing at canvas scale: a tiny burning city lost in a huge black field (W18) makes the black the subject and the city the accident. A prompt built from this mechanism must pick one flat colour, fill the whole frame with it, and put exactly one event on it -- no ground, no horizon, no second colour to hide behind. (C22536, leg 2 W07, W10, W15, W18 and M7; synthesis C22475)

**Takeaway:** set one event against one flat colour filling the whole frame -- no ground, no horizon -- so the figure reads as an icon and the field does the framing. (C22537)


## M8 -- The performed picture that collapses into a still (score to freeze to empty stage)

A live performance is written as a score/poster ("Portfolio of Performances") in discrete "pictures" that get simpler as they proceed, ending in stillness or absence. "Two Boxers" (W16, 1979, c. 5 min) calls for a stage, red and white lights, professional boxers, and "heroic" music, and runs in three pictures that ultimately collapse into one: Prussian marching music in darkness; boxers fight in silence under a white strobe; boxers freeze into a still image in red light; lastly the audience contemplates an empty stage, the picture now purely mental. "Two Fencers" (1977) works the same way with fencers and cloak-and-dagger film music. The performance is a film being unmade in front of the audience, frame by frame, until only the mental image is left. (C22538, leg 2 W16 and M8; synthesis C22476)

The collapse is the mechanism, and the score is what makes it repeatable: because it is written down, anyone can perform it, and the artist does not need to be there at all -- which is the point. The first performance was at the Groningen Arts Festival in 1979; the Whitney restaged it in 2002, a pair of real fighters duking it out under a strobe light, and Nottingham Contemporary restaged it later. Each restaging proves the same thing: the work is a set of instructions for an image that ends in its own absence. A prompt built from this mechanism must run a short sequence of discrete pictures that get simpler with each step and end on an empty frame. (C22539, leg 2 W16 and M8; synthesis C22476)

**Takeaway:** write a performance as a score of discrete pictures that get simpler at each step -- music, fight, freeze, empty stage -- until the image is gone and only the mental picture remains. (C22540)


## M9 -- The spectacular instant, enlarged to near-abstraction

A found photograph of a violent natural or technological event (bombing, lightning, volcanic eruption, a parachutist from a jet, a burning building) is cropped tight, blown up to monumental scale, and painted in flat airbrushed acrylic. A tiny burning city lost in a huge black field ("Untitled", W18, 1981, acrylic on canvas, 82 3/4 x 130 in; "Untitled (#61 Burning City)", 1981, is the named example of the war-spectacles series); a lightning bolt shearing a sky that fills a 72 x 96 in canvas ("Untitled (Lightning)", W19, 1984); tiny astronauts with cut tethers in a void ("Untitled", W17, 1979, acrylic on three photographs, 84 3/8 x 126 1/2 in overall). The sublime is delivered at billboard scale with the reportage caption removed -- the viewer feels awe first and only later, if at all, asks what the picture was of. (C22541, leg 2 W17-W19 and M9; synthesis C22477)

The enlargement is what turns a photograph into a painting and a news image into an icon. The source is always a found photograph -- a geology textbook volcano, a bombing-raid skyline, a lightning storm -- and the painting keeps the photograph's composition while removing its context: no caption, no date, no place, no brushstroke to say a hand was here. In "Under Water Sea Fantasy" (begun 1983, finished 2003, 16mm colour sound, 6 min 30 sec) the same instinct returns to film with no body in it at all -- only archival footage of volcanic eruptions, a lunar eclipse, marine life montaged together. A prompt built from this mechanism must take one violent natural or technological instant, crop it tight, blow it up until its origin is unreadable, and render it flat. (C22542, leg 2 W17-W19 and M9; synthesis C22477)

**Takeaway:** take one spectacular instant -- lightning, eruption, bombing -- crop it tight, blow it up to monumental scale, and paint it flat with no caption; awe first, identification later if at all. (C22543)


## M10 -- Self-effacement as a technique (the disappearing author)

At each phase the artist removes one more trace of his own hand -- buried body (W02) to traced copy (W04) to hired performers (W05-W16) to hired painters (W18-W19) to other authors' words (W20). The work never shows Goldstein after 1974; the paintings bear no brushstroke; the "Totems" pages are set in readymade fonts with clip art; "Selected Writings" is literally other people's sentences. The disappearance is not biography (though the 1990s retreat and the 2003 suicide are the biographical echo); it is the mechanism that lets a generic image stand in for a personal one. Crimp's corrected "Ballet Shoe" reading is the purest instance: in "A Ballet Shoe" (W08, 1975, 16mm colour, 19 sec) male hands, Goldstein's, already inside the frame, untie ornamental ribbons that were never holding the shoe -- the dancer descends from pointe by skill, not because the ribbon was released. Even the apparent cause-and-effect in the image is not the artist's doing. (C22544, leg 2 W02, W04, W08, W20 and M10; synthesis C22478)

The arc is one direction, six phases: studio action 1971-72 (own body enduring, camera records); copying/appropriation 1974-76 (hired hands, trained animals, found footage; sound separated); sound alone 1976-77 (no body; the listener supplies the image); installation/score 1977-79 (professional athletes, no artist; performers follow a score); painting 1979-84 (no body; tiny figures dwarfed or absent; hired painters, airbrush, no brushstroke); text 1982-2000 (author dissolved into other authors' sentences read backwards). What stays constant is the unit: one event, isolated, repeated or frozen -- a bark, a roar, a jump, a drowning, a lightning bolt. What changes is the carrier: performance to film to record to installation to painting to text. A prompt built from this mechanism must remove the author one step at a time -- first the body, then the hand, then the signature -- until only the event remains. (C22545, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C22467)

**Takeaway:** remove the author in stages -- body, hand, signature -- until the work is only an event with no one left in it to claim it. (C22546)


## The aesthetic logic: a picture with nobody in it

Goldstein's career is one system run for thirty years: take one event from the world -- a bark, a roar, a jump, a spill, a drowning, a lightning bolt -- strip away its before and after, set it against a flat colour or a black field, have someone else perform it or paint it, and repeat it until it stops being a story and starts being an icon. The event is always generic, always borrowed, always slightly famous: a corporate logo, a Riefenstahl diver, a textbook volcano, a thriller soundtrack, a sentence from Kafka. The artist's contribution is not the image; it is the cut, the loop, the silence where the sound should be, and the absence where the author should be. (C22547, leg 2 mechanisms M1-M10; synthesis C22467-C22469)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the event is the whole work: one action, isolated, with no plot to dilute it -- and the shorter the event, the longer it lasts, because a 26-second diver or a 19-second shoe can loop forever while a story would have to end. Second, the missing half is the work: sound without picture, picture without sound, sentence without image, performance without performer -- the viewer completes what the artist withheld, and the completion is the experience. Third, the author disappears on purpose: the body goes underground in 1972, the hand is replaced by hired technicians and painters, the signature is stripped from the image, the words are other people's sentences read backwards. The disappearance is a technique, not a tragedy -- though the 1990s retreat and the 2003 death are its biographical echo, and the work must not be read backwards from the death. (C22548, leg 2 M2, M3, M4, M10; synthesis C22467)

The evolution is the proof. 1971-72: the body endures for the camera -- a nail pried loose with teeth, a table pounded until the milk spills, a man buried alive with only a blinking red light above ground. 1974-76: the body is replaced -- hired hands, a trained dog, a diver on found footage, a lion that belongs to a studio. 1976-77: the body is gone and the sound remains -- nine coloured records, six minutes of drowning. 1977-79: the score replaces the performer -- boxers fight, freeze, vanish, and the stage is empty. 1979-84: the painting replaces the film -- found photographs of spectacular instants blown up to monumental scale, painted by other hands, no brushstroke. 1982-2000: the author replaces himself with other authors -- sentences read backwards, three to a page, enormous margins. The teacher burnt his paintings and replaced the hand with a rule; the classmate Baldessari made his teaching assistant took the rule further than anyone: he removed the hand, then the body, then the name, until the work was only an event happening to nobody. (C22549, leg 2 W01-W20 evolution; synthesis C22467)
