## The life: Asmara, Santos, Sao Paulo, and the wall

Alex Vallauri was born Alessandro Vallauri on 9 October 1949 in Asmara, Ethiopia (today Eritrea), to an Italian family. All sources agree on the date and place. On origins the sources split in wording, not in substance: the English Wikipedia calls him "a Brazilian artist of Italian origin"; the Portuguese Wikipedia calls him "an artist of Ethiopian origin settled in Brazil" and lists his nationality as Italian; the Sao Paulo state legislature bill text calls him "son of Italians"; the Bienal Foundation calls him "born in Ethiopia, of Italian nationality". The coherent reading is: born in Asmara to an Italian family, later naturalised Brazilian (naturalisation is stated only by Spinelli). No source read names his parents, gives their names, or explains why the family was in Asmara; do not invent either. (C16708, leg 1 C16500, C16503; synthesis C16651)

Curator Joao Spinelli says Vallauri began art studies at 14 at the Asociacion Estimulo de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires, "where the family lived until moving to Santos in 1964", and that he "assumed Brazil as his country, naturalising as Brazilian". No other source read mentions Buenos Aires at all. Treat the Buenos Aires years as single-source (Spinelli) until corroborated. The family moved to Santos in 1964 (Spinelli, Urbanario) or 1965 (Itau, both Wikipedias): two sources each, unresolved; print "1964/65". In Santos he took up woodcut, depicting people of the port -- dockworkers and prostitutes -- and won an award at the Salao de Arte Jovem in 1968 (Itau only for the award). When exactly he moved from Santos to the city of Sao Paulo is not dated in any source read; Itau says only "later". Do not date the move. (C16709, leg 1 C16501, C16503, C16507; synthesis C16652)

He entered the Visual Communication course at FAAP (Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado) in 1968, where he met Spinelli; in 1969 he was a monitor at the Sao Paulo Biennial; in 1971, aged 22, he was already in the Biennial selection, in a print room alongside established names. Itau says he graduated in visual communication from FAAP in 1971 and two years later (1973) began teaching observational drawing and free expression there. The 1968 entry plus 1971 graduation sequence is internally coherent. The English Wikipedia instead says "In 1965 he graduated in Visual Communication from FAAP". That is impossible on Spinelli's dates (he would have graduated the year he arrived, aged 15/16, before entering in 1968) and is plainly a garbled sentence: 1965 is the arrival year in the same source family. Print 1971; the 1965 graduation is an error, not a rival date. (C16710, leg 1 C16501, C16503, C16505; synthesis C16652)

His first solo exhibition was in 1970, at the Associacao dos Amigos do Museu de Arte Moderna de Sao Paulo (MAM-SP), old Rua 7 de Abril address, confirmed by both Itau and the Bienal Foundation, which reproduces the invitation from its archive dossier. In the mid-1970s he photographed 1950s tile panels on Sao Paulo restaurant walls (kitsch); he collected some 400 rubber stamps from a factory with 1950s designs and used them in his work. In 1975-76 he went to Europe to improve his technique and specialised in lithography at the Litho Art Center, Stockholm. Back in Brazil in 1977, he continued graffiti in public spaces, "this time on the walls of Sao Paulo" (Itau) -- and in 1977 he showed at the 14th Bienal with the audiovisual projection "Ao alcance de todos", built from images of urban art he collected. (C16711, leg 1 C16501-C16504; synthesis C16653)

The street turn is dated by the journalist Jotabe Medeiros (O Estado de S. Paulo, 27/03/1988, quoted by the Bienal Foundation): "Alex Vallauri began his graffiti in 78, when there appeared on the walls of Sao Paulo an enigmatic long boot. The boot soon got a leg, black gloves, and ended up becoming the main character of his work, A Rainha do Frango Assado." The practice, as described across sources: anonymous at first -- "anonymous performance", the public seeing only "the marks left on the walls" (his own words, quoted by the Bienal Foundation); cardboard cut-out stencil masks receiving spray paint (Itau note); images of immediate comprehension, because in the chaos of the city they had to be read at once (PT Wikipedia); characters from comics and pin-ups, appropriations such as Lee Falk's Mandrake (Itau); the high-heeled black boot (Bota Preta) as the recurring stencil. Beyond walls he stamped shirts, badges and stickers; graffiti for him was the form of communication closest to his ideal of "art for all" (both Wikipedias). From 1981 he made larger works combining several small stencils joined by freehand spray lines. Where exactly in Sao Paulo he painted is poorly documented: no source read names a specific wall, street or neighbourhood for the 1978-81 work, except the 1982 MAM entrance panel and, from a 2025 ALESP article, an anecdote of a boot graffitied on the door of a studio near USP (Arthur Lara's atelier, via Carlos Matuck). Do not invent wall locations. (C16712, leg 1 C16501, C16502, C16504, C16508; synthesis C16653)

Between 1982 and 1983 he lived in New York and studied graphic arts at the Pratt Institute (Itau; both Wikipedias; Spinelli). Before leaving, on 31 March 1982 (Jornal da Tarde), he painted a panel with Carlos Matuck and Waldemar Zeidler Junior at the entrance of MAM in Ibirapuera Park (Bienal Foundation dossier). In New York, according to Spinelli (single source, via Canal Contemporaneo and cited by the English Wikipedia to the Folha 2013 article): he met Andy Warhol, Jean-Michel Basquiat and Keith Haring; Warhol praised his graffiti on gift-wrap; he became friends with Haring and Basquiat, "with whom he graffitied the streets of NY". Urbanario adds, also single-source, that he executed two murals on local-government commission, one in Tompkins Square Park and one near the State Courthouse, the first surviving until 2002. No New York mural is confirmed by any second source read. Treat the Warhol/Basquiat/Haring friendships and the two murals as Spinelli/Urbanario-only claims, not as settled biography. After returning to Brazil he taught again at FAAP (both Wikipedias; Itau implies the same). (C16713, leg 1 C16501-C16503, C16507; synthesis C16654)

In 1985 he presented the installation "A Festa da Rainha do Frango Assado" (The Roast Chicken Queen's Party) at the 18th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo -- described by Itau as graffiti brought together with objects, simulating the rooms of a house full of consumer-society icons, "a space of frivolities built on an utterly makeshift set". Urbanario gives its size as 88 square metres: a 1950s-aesthetic house with furniture and inhabitants both physical and stencil-painted. In a letter to curator Sheila Leirner, 5 May 1985, he described the proposal as gathering his graffiti scattered around the city into one big celebration (Bienal Foundation). The character was played by his friend, actress Claudia Raia, then 18 and starring in the soap "Roque Santeiro", for the installation's official video; she called the setting "so crazy, tacky and joyful at the same time" (Jornal da Tarde, 3/12/1985, via the Bienal Foundation). The Rainha "became choreography in 1987" (Medeiros, OESP 27/03/1988). (C16714, leg 1 C16501, C16502, C16507; synthesis C16653)

He died of AIDS in Sao Paulo on 27 March 1987, aged 37. Date, place, cause and age agree across every source read (both Wikipedias, Itau-derived biography, Bienal Foundation, ALESP, Urbanario, Invaluable). No source read gives a hospital, a day-by-day final illness, or any detail beyond the cause. Do not invent any. Note the Urbanario error here: it says he died "on his birthday" -- wrong; his birthday was 9 October. That error is flagged, not carried. He is repeatedly grouped with the Geracao 80 generation: the Bienal Foundation calls him "one of the exponents of the so-called Geracao 80". (C16715, leg 1 C16500, C16502, C16504, C16507; synthesis C16653)

Posthumous shows followed: "A trama do gosto" (1987) and "Bienal Brasil seculo XX" (1994) included his work posthumously -- these are posthumous inclusions, not lifetime participations, and must not be counted as a fifth or sixth Biennial. The retrospective "Viva Vallauri" at the Museu da Imagem e do Som (MIS), Sao Paulo, is dated 1988 by Itau alone and 1998 by both Wikipedias; the title is real (Guta Marques audio special "Especial Viva Vallauri", MIS, 1999). The major retrospective "Alex Vallauri: Sao Paulo e Nova York como suporte" ran at MAM-SP, 17 April - 23 June 2013, curated by Joao Spinelli, about 170 works, staged as a reverse retrospective, from the final works back to the earliest. Graffiti Day, 27 March, the date of his death, is celebrated as Dia do Grafite in Sao Paulo city (Law 13,903/2004) and in Sao Paulo state (Law 17,565/2022), and in Rio de Janeiro. No gallery representation was verified: no Simoes de Assis or Almeida e Dale artist page for Vallauri was found in search; do not claim representation by either. No public collection holding his work was verified in any source read. (C16716, leg 1 C16501, C16503-C16505, C16508, C16509; synthesis C16655)

## The mechanisms

### M1 -- One-icon stencil campaign: repeat a single sharp silhouette until the city learns it

Mechanically: cut one motif -- the Bota, a striped tie, a corded telephone, a hand with a cigarette -- as a cardboard stencil, spray it slightly larger than life, and repeat it across the city at night, anonymously, for months. The image is synthetic: a single outline, flat black, no shading, readable at a glance from a moving bus. The evidencing works are W05 (Bota, 1978-79, the campaign itself), W06 (tie), W09 (telephone) and W12 (cigarette hand). (C16719, leg 2 C16550, C16551, C16554; synthesis C16660)

Watchable behaviour: the same shape pops up in a new place each night -- on a bench, a door, a hoarding -- until the viewer starts hunting for it; recognition, not contemplation, is the payoff. A piece built on this mechanism should introduce ONE silhouette, stamp it in a new spot at irregular intervals, and let the viewer feel the city filling up with it.

**Takeaway:** one shape, stamped everywhere, becomes a signature louder than a name. (C16720, leg 2 C16550)

### M2 -- Modular assembly: small stencils plus freehand spray make a large figure

Mechanically: keep a kit of small stencils (boot, glove, sunglasses, telephone, heart, star) and combine them on the wall, joining the parts with freehand spray lines drawn on the spot. From 1981 the compositions grow: several small stencils united by hand-drawn spray strokes. The Rainha is the textbook case: boot stencil plus glove stencils plus sunglasses stencil, then a woman's body drawn freehand around them. Urbanario states this stencil-plus-freehand combination is characteristic of Vallauri and was apparently used by no other artist of the eighties or today; the academic paper says the same of his New York work, calling it modular Pop Art stencilling. The evidencing works are W10 (Rainha with telephone), W15 (Rainha born live in the Tompkins mural), W19 and W20 (the Queen as a full character and a house) and W14 (collaborative panel). (C16721, leg 2 C16551, C16554, C16557; synthesis C16661)

Watchable behaviour: separate flat parts appear one by one, then a loose spray line stitches them into a single figure that is visibly made of pieces -- the seams stay visible, and that is the charm. A piece built on this mechanism should assemble its figure live from separate stamps, then draw the connecting body in one loose pass.

**Takeaway:** the figure is a kit, not a drawing. Stamp the parts, then draw the body that holds them together. (C16722, leg 2 C16551)

### M3 -- Site-matched icon: the image must rhyme with the place it lands on

Mechanically: choose the wall by what the building sells or is. A lion goes on the poultry shop (the predator eyeing the chickens), a tiger on the butcher's shop, a striped tie where executives walk, a telephone where people wait, a boot in a ladies' toilet (a colleague), in a men's toilet (a prostitute), on a park bench (company), in a gay sauna (a transvestite). Vallauri's pupil Julio Barreto states the rule explicitly: not too high, not too low, a composition with the wall, and a relation of meaning. The evidencing works are W11 (lion/tiger), W06 (tie personified by passers-by into executives), W05 (the boot's shifting meaning by location, per Rota-Rossi) and W09 (telephone where people wait to be answered). (C16723, leg 2 C16554; synthesis C16662)

Watchable behaviour: the viewer gets the joke only by reading image and place together; move the same stencil to a different shop and the meaning flips. A piece built on this mechanism should place each icon against a backdrop that changes what it means, and let the backdrop do half the work.

**Takeaway:** the wall is half the picture. Put the lion where the chickens are. (C16724, leg 2 C16554)

### M4 -- Appropriation and recontextualisation: steal a famous image, put it where it does not belong

Mechanically: take an image everybody already knows -- the Last Supper, Mandrake the magician, a 1950s restaurant tile panel, a pin-up, a comic-strip hero -- and reprint it in a new medium (woodcut, T-shirt, stencil, xerox) in a new place (a wall, a shirt, a postcard). The recognition is instant; the displacement is the joke and the critique. The 1950s mass-culture iconography runs through the whole career, from the tile photographs of 1974-77 to the 1950s house of W20. The evidencing works are W01 (Santa Ceia woodcuts), W07 (Santa Ceia T-shirts), W08 (Mandrake), W04 (tile panels as video) and W02 (the pinned mouth as a stolen political emblem). (C16725, leg 2 C16550, C16552, C16553, C16558; synthesis C16663)

Watchable behaviour: the viewer recognises the image in half a second, then notices it is in the wrong place, in the wrong medium, at the wrong size -- and the double-take is the work. A piece built on this mechanism should open with a familiar image and then misplace it.

**Takeaway:** recognition first, displacement second. The famous image is only the bait. (C16726, leg 2 C16550)

### M5 -- The open work: the public finishes the picture

Mechanically: make the image simple, legible and provocative enough that a passer-by can answer it -- with a sprayed reply, a chalk speech balloon, a drawn profile, a phrase. Vallauri said he wanted people to participate, "adding or taking away details", and called the activity "anonymous performance": the public sees only the marks left on the walls. The evidencing works are W06 (three men's profiles and "te amo gracinha" added to the tie), W09 (the overnight telephone dialogue: "he never answers!" / "no, I was busy!"), W08 (Guta Marques stopping at night to talk to the Mandrake graffiti) and W15 (neighbours in the East Village asking for the Queen to be reworked as a dark-skinned woman, and getting it). (C16727, leg 2 C16552, C16554; synthesis C16664)

Watchable behaviour: the image is never finished; each night something is added, and the work grows a conversation around itself. A piece built on this mechanism should leave a visible gap -- a blank speech area, an unanswered phone -- and let new marks arrive over time.

**Takeaway:** the picture is a question. Leave room for the street to answer it. (C16728, leg 2 C16554)

### M6 -- Same image, every support: wall, shirt, postcard, PVC, paper, xerox

Mechanically: never let an image live on one surface. The Bota goes from wall to T-shirt to photographic postcard; the PVC plaques (W17, W18) solve the collector's problem -- cut the graffiti to the shape of the drawing so it can hang indoors without being painted on a wall; the same images enter mail art, artist's books, signed colour xerox editions, stamps (a 400-stamp collection of 1950s factory designs), gift wrapping, and photographs of the street work itself. The evidencing works are W05 (wall plus T-shirt plus postcard), W17/W18 (PVC), W13 (street work shown as a gallery retrospective plus video), W14 (street panel at a museum entrance) and W04 (photographs become video become Bienal work). (C16729, leg 2 C16551, C16553, C16555; synthesis C16665)

Watchable behaviour: one motif keeps changing material -- spray on brick, ink on cotton, cut PVC, xerox on paper -- and each material makes the same shape feel different. A piece built on this mechanism should restamp its hero image across at least three different surfaces in sequence.

**Takeaway:** the image is not the wall. Stamp it on everything until the city and the gallery hold the same picture. (C16730, leg 2 C16551)

### M7 -- The kitsch consumer house as a stage

Mechanically: gather the scattered street icons into a single enclosed 1950s interior -- a house, a picnic, a room -- and fill it with real objects that have themselves been graffitied (chairs, televisions, a hairdryer, a bidet, a dustbin). The irony, per Itau Cultural, is a space of frivolities built on an utterly makeshift set, pointing to the disposable nature of modernity; Claudia Raia's verdict on the spot was "crazy, tacky and joyful at the same time". The evidencing works are W20 (the 88 square metre Festa house, 1985), W19 (the Queen at a picnic) and W04 (the tile-panel / roadside-restaurant world that feeds it). (C16731, leg 2 C16550, C16552, C16553; synthesis C16666)

Watchable behaviour: the viewer walks from open street into a closed, crowded, cheerful room where every object is both furniture and picture -- the graffiti stops being a mark on a wall and becomes the wallpaper of a life. A piece built on this mechanism should close the open street into one cluttered room and let the icons become furniture.

**Takeaway:** bring the street indoors and it becomes a house -- tacky, joyful, and full of things to buy. (C16732, leg 2 C16552)

### M8 -- Printmaker's hand: high contrast, flat colour, body fragments

Mechanically: everything the street work does, the woodcut did first. Large-format woodcuts in high contrast and flat, vivid colour; the favoured subjects are objects and fragments of the human body -- lips, legs, a mouth, a boot. The stencil is the woodcut's child: same bold cut-out silhouette, same flat fill, same instant legibility, but executed in spray on a wall instead of ink on paper. The early Santos work (dockworkers, prostitutes, port characters) already works in high-contrast graphic black and white. The evidencing works are W01, W02, W03 (the print sequence) and W05 (the boot as a cut-out silhouette, the direct descendant of the stiletto-leg woodcuts). (C16733, leg 2 C16550, C16558; synthesis C16667)

Watchable behaviour: no shading, no modelling, no grey -- a shape cut out of black or flat colour, landing whole. A piece built on this mechanism should keep every form a flat cut-out; if a shape needs shading, it is the wrong shape.

**Takeaway:** cut the shape out, fill it flat, and let the silhouette do all the talking. (C16734, leg 2 C16550)


## The aesthetic logic: the city as a printing press

Vallauri's logic is a press, not a canvas. The woodcut taught him the move -- cut the shape out, fill it flat, let the silhouette do the talking (M8) -- and the street gave him the machine: a cardboard stencil, a can of spray, and a city full of walls that already meant something (M1, M3). The image has to be read at a glance from a moving bus, so it is one outline, flat black, no shading. The wall is half the picture, so the lion goes where the chickens are and the boot changes its meaning with the toilet it lands in. The famous image is only bait (M4): the Last Supper on a T-shirt, Mandrake on a wall, a 1950s tile panel in a Biennial video -- recognition first, displacement second, and the double-take is the work.

The watchability follows from that press. One silhouette stamped everywhere (M1) makes the viewer hunt for it; the city fills up and recognition, not contemplation, is the payoff. Small stencils joined by a freehand spray line (M2) make a figure that is visibly made of pieces, the seams left showing. The picture is never finished (M5): a passer-by adds three profiles to a tie, another hand answers a telephone that never gets answered, and the neighbours in the East Village ask for the Queen to be reworked and get her reworked. The same image keeps changing material (M6) -- spray on brick, ink on cotton, cut PVC, xerox on paper -- until the city and the gallery hold the same picture. And at the end the street comes indoors (M7): the scattered icons are gathered into one crowded 1950s house where every object is both furniture and picture, tacky and joyful at once.

What he is not: not a painter (the oil canvas never enters his practice; the woodcut and the stencil are the whole technique); not a gallery artist who happens to paint walls (the street came first and the gallery came to the street -- the 1981 Pinacoteca show was a retrospective of three years of street work, while the work itself stayed on the wall); not a tagger (the image is a legible icon, not a signature -- the signature is the icon, and the icon is louder than a name); not a member of 3Nos3 (the collective tie runs through Hudinilson Jr. only); not a 1984 Parque Lage participant (the archival list omits him); not a late-style artist (there is no thinning, no slowing -- the last major work is his biggest, loudest and most crowded, the exact opposite of Guinle, whose last works thinned to washes as his body failed). The closest kin in the studied corpus is Guinle, who also lets a material do the picturing -- but Guinle's paint thins until it is light, while Vallauri's stencil multiplies until it is a house. His operation is his own: cut one sharp shape, stamp it everywhere, let the wall change what it means, let the street answer it, and in the end bring the whole scattered city indoors and throw it a party.


## Works inspected (W01-W20)

Grades: A = seen/described in a source read end to end; B = described in a reliable secondary source; C = thin/uncertain, do not build prompts on C works alone. 20 works verified, 0 invented, 0 C-grade: 16 A, 4 B.

- W01 Santa Ceia (Last Supper) woodcut series, 1970-1972, large-format woodcut, high contrast, flat colour -- B. A series reinterpreting the Last Supper in high contrast and flat colour, made before Warhol's version, never exhibited by the artist (thesis quoting Spinelli 2010, p.160). The series is the print origin of the later Santa Ceia T-shirts (W07). The "before Warhol" claim is Spinelli's, not this run's; Warhol's own Last Supper series is 1986, which makes the chronology plausible. (M4, M8)
- W02 Boca com Alfinete (Mouth with Pin), 1973, large-format woodcut -- A. High-contrast graphic work with evident political content: a mouth pinned shut, a reference to censorship under the military dictatorship. His best-documented early print. (M4, M8)
- W03 Telefones and stiletto-leg woodcuts, early 1970s, large-format woodcut -- B. A sequence of large woodcuts in vivid colour stamping objects and details of the human body: telephones, pins piercing and closing large red lips (a direct dictatorship reference), and cinematic well-drawn female legs in stiletto heels that never left his production afterwards (thesis quoting Spinelli). The telephone and the stiletto leg both return as street stencils (W09, W05). (M4, M8)
- W04 Arte Para Todos (Art for All), video, 1977, shown at the 14th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo -- A. Photographic records of 1950s tile panels on Sao Paulo restaurant walls, edited into a video; the Bienal's own account names the proposal Ao alcance de todos (Within Everyone's Reach) and quotes his motive: roadside-restaurant images, arcade art, bar and bakery decoration -- the aesthetic information of a city whose majority never enters a gallery. (M4, M6)
- W05 Bota Preta (Black Boot), 1978-1979, stencil + spray paint on Sao Paulo walls, park benches, doors -- A. A synthetic silhouette of a high-heeled boot, taken from sadomasochistic imagery, slightly larger than life size, repeated in an intense propagation campaign, anonymously at first; also reproduced on T-shirts and on photographic postcards of the city. Photographed 1979 next to the artist on a park bench. The boot later grows a leg, black gloves, and becomes the Rainha. (M1, M2, M6)
- W06 Gravatas Listradas (Striped Ties), 1979-1981, stencil + spray paint on a Sao Paulo wall -- A. A striped tie stencilled on a wall; anonymous passers-by added the profile of three men to it, personifying the accessory into the "tie-wearing professionals" of the city, and added the phrase "te amo gracinha". The clearest single case of the public finishing his image. (M1, M3, M5)
- W07 Santa Ceia T-shirts, late 1970s (date not stated in the source; placed by the MAM narrative at the start of the move from prints to walls), stencil/screen print on T-shirts -- A. Prints of the Last Supper on T-shirts, the step from the W01 woodcuts to wearable street images; the MAM account lists them as the start of the sequence "from Santa Ceia prints on T-shirts to the boots, gloves and Mandrake". (M4, M6)
- W08 Magico Mandrake e Abra-me Cadabra-me (Mandrake the Magician and "Open-me Cadabra-me"), 1980s, stencil + spray paint on Sao Paulo walls -- A. Lee Falk's comic-strip magician Mandrake, stencilled, paired with the motto "Abra-me cadabra-me". The motto's author, sociologist Guta Marques, describes stopping at night to "talk" to the graffiti before knowing whose it was. (M4, M5)
- W09 Telefone com Fio (Corded Telephone), 1982, stencil + spray paint, New York -- A. A corded old-style telephone stencilled in New York. Passers-by wrote on the wall around it at night: "ah, ele nunca atende!" ("he never answers!"), answered by another hand: "nao, e que eu estava ocupado!" ("no, I was busy!") -- the telephone as a prop for a public dialogue. (M1, M3, M5)
- W10 Rainha do Frango Assado com Telefone (Roast Chicken Queen with Telephone), 1980s, stencil + spray paint, Sao Paulo -- A. The Rainha figure shown with the telephone motif. Evidence that the Queen was not a fixed portrait but a kit of parts recombined with other stencils. (M2, M3)
- W11 Leao na avicola / Tigre no acougue (Lion at the poultry shop / Tiger at the butcher's), 1980s, stencil + spray paint on shop walls, Sao Paulo -- B. Julio Barreto, who learned stencil from Vallauri, reports the rule: a poultry slaughterhouse got a lion on its wall, a meat shop got a tiger -- the image must rhyme with the trade. No single photograph with a firm date was found for either animal; the pairing is evidenced by testimony, not by a dated image, hence B. (M3)
- W12 Mao segurando um cigarro (Hand holding a cigarette), 1980s, stencil + spray paint, Sao Paulo -- B. A hand holding a cigarette, cited as the artist's deliberate invitation to the passer-by to visualise a suspension of time, idle time, a moment of release. The motif also survives on a T-shirt. (M1, M3)
- W13 Muros de Sao Paulo, solo show, 1981, Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo -- A. A retrospective of three years of street work (1978-1981); the audiovisual documentation Interferencia Urbana-Grafite records the three-year campaign shown there. The show won him the 1981 Arte Comunicacao prize from the Sao Paulo art critics' association. This is the moment the anonymous street campaign becomes a gallery retrospective -- while the work itself stays on the wall. (M6)
- W14 Painel na entrada do MAM (Panel at the MAM entrance), 1982, graffiti panel, Museu de Arte Moderna, Parque Ibirapuera, Sao Paulo -- A. Painted with Carlos Matuck and Waldemar Zeidler Junior just before Vallauri left for New York; documented by a Jornal da Tarde clipping, 31 March 1982, in the Bienal archive. Collaborative, dated, placed at an institution's door -- the street entering the museum literally at its entrance. (M2, M6)
- W15 Tompkins Square Park mural, 1982-1983, New York -- A. A mural commissioned by the local government in Tompkins Square Park, centre of East Village life; it was here, working in front of neighbours, that the Rainha was born: a 1950s-advertising white woman reworked at the largely Latino neighbours' request into a dark-skinned woman with thick lips and full forms. The first New York work survived until 2002. Thin description (one passage), but in a source read end to end; single-source (Urbanario). (M2, M5)
- W16 State Courthouse mural, 1982-1983, New York -- A. A second mural commissioned by the local government, near the State Courthouse. One-line mention in the same end-to-end source as W15; no image or description of its subject was found this run; single-source (Urbanario). (M2)
- W17 Panterete (Little Panther), date not stated, graffiti on PVC plaque cut to the shape of the drawing -- A. Collectors wanted his work but did not want it fixed to their walls, so he cut graffiti on PVC sheets to the outline of the drawing; Panterete is named among the examples in the MAM 2013 show. The street image made portable and collectible without becoming a canvas painting. (M6)
- W18 Caranguejo (Crab), date not stated, graffiti on PVC plaque cut to the shape of the drawing -- A. Named alongside Panterete and a Coracao in the MAM 2013 show. A crab: another single, instantly readable animal icon, same portable-PVC solution as W17. (M1, M6)
- W19 A Rainha do Frango Assado em Pic-nic no Glicerio (The Roast Chicken Queen at a Picnic in Glicerio), c.1985, medium not stated in the source read -- A. A detail photograph by Kenji Ota reproduced by the Bienal; one of his most famous works, made for the 18th Biennial. The Queen taken out of the wall and into a picnic scene -- the figure as a character with a life, not a logo. (M2, M7)
- W20 A Festa na Casa da Rainha do Frango Assado (The Party at the Roast Chicken Queen's House), 1985, installation, 88 square metres, 18th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo -- A. A 1950s-style house with furniture and inhabitants both physical and stencil-painted, plus graffitied objects -- chairs, televisions, a hairdryer, a bidet, a dustbin -- gathering his scattered street graffiti into one celebration. Actress Claudia Raia, then 18, played the Queen for the installation's official video and called the setting "crazy, tacky and joyful at the same time", defining the Queen as "a consumerist person, without criteria". (M2, M7)

Early vs late arc: Early (1965-1977, print and collection): Santos port characters in high-contrast graphic work; award at the Salao de Arte Jovem 1968; solo show at the Associacao Amigos do MAM-SP 1970; Bienal 1971 with prints; the Santa Ceia woodcuts 1970-72 and the political Boca com Alfinete 1973; study in Stockholm 1975-76; then a turn to collecting rather than making -- the photographs of 1950s tile panels in restaurants, confectionery wrappings, a 400-stamp collection -- shown as the video Arte Para Todos at the 1977 Biennial. Colour: black-and-white contrast, then flat vivid woodcut colour. Scale: paper, print size. Surface: paper, ink. Street turn (1978-1981): the same graphic language leaves the paper. From 1978 the Bota campaign repeats one stencil across Sao Paulo, anonymous; small-scale images from popular imagery and art history; then from 1981 larger works joining several small stencils with freehand spray. The 1981 Pinacoteca show Muros de Sao Paulo retrospectively frames three years of street work, and the critics' prize follows. Scale jumps from a single boot to a wall; surface jumps from paper to brick, bench, door, hoarding. New York (1982-1983): Pratt Institute study; two government-commissioned murals (Tompkins Square Park, State Courthouse); the modular compositions reach their largest and most structured form; the Rainha is born live in front of neighbours and is reworked to their faces. The street work is now collaborative and public in the making, not only in the viewing. Late / gallery (1984-1985, he dies 27 March 1987): the street images are gathered, not abandoned. PVC plaques make them collectible (Panterete, Caranguejo, Coracao); signed colour xerox artist's books, mail art and stamps carry them on paper; and the 1985 Bienal installation Festa na Casa da Rainha do Frango Assado (88 sq m) reassembles the whole scattered vocabulary -- boots, gloves, telephones, chairs, televisions -- into one 1950s house. Colour stays flat, bright, pop and kitsch throughout; what changes is scale (single boot to a house), surface (paper to wall to PVC to room), and authorship (anonymous night work to a public, participatory, named practice). There is no late thinning or late style: the last major work is his biggest, loudest and most crowded. (C16736; synthesis C16657)
