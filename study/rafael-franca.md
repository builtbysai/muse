## The life: the boy who decided at nine, the night worker, the man who filmed his own death

Rafael Franca was born in Porto Alegre, Rio Grande do Sul, in 1957, and died in Chicago in May 1991 of AIDS-related illness, before completing 34 years of age. No source read gives an exact day or month of birth, and none gives an exact day of death. At the age of nine he told his parents of his decision to devote his life to painting, and as a teenager he studied drawing, painting and lithography in Porto Alegre; no teacher names are given in the sources read. That nine-year-old's decision is the whole career in one sentence: he never did anything else, and he never had time to do it slowly. (C13908, leg 1 C13737, C13738, C13739)

In 1978 he moved from Porto Alegre to Sao Paulo to study at the Escola de Comunicacoes e Artes of the Universidade de Sao Paulo (ECA-USP). He had chosen the USP programme largely because of Regina Silveira, whose work he had encountered in Porto Alegre; at ECA-USP from 1978 he studied under Silveira, who encouraged experimentation with new media and conceptual approaches. At USP he developed intense research in engraving and printmaking, and, in Silveira's account, what he really wanted on moving to Sao Paulo was to get close to the new media of art and specifically to learn photographic printing procedures. No source read states that he completed a degree at ECA-USP, and none gives a USP graduation year; do not print one. (C13909, leg 1 C13740, C13748, C13749, C13750)

In 1979 he co-founded the collective 3nos3 with Hudinilson Jr. and Mario Ramiro -- all three born in 1957, Franca and Ramiro fellow USP students who met Hudinilson Jr. at a joint underground exhibition -- and devoted the year largely to urban interventions with the group. The name is glossed as both "Three Knots" and "We Three" in Portuguese (nos = knots; nos = we). In 1979/80 he was working in Sao Paulo at the Centro de Estudos ASTER with Silveira in off-set printing, and it was at ASTER that catalogues of video-installation work by Buky Schwartz, Taka Iimura and Nam June Paik reached him and pushed him toward video installation. His own account: "My work with video began in a more or less unexpected way. In 1979/80 I was working in Sao Paulo, at the Centro de Estudos ASTER with Regina Silveira in off-set. I then received some catalogues of video-installation works by artists such as Buck Schwarz, Taka Limura and Nam June Paik. These projects had a strong impact on me and led me to think about some problems for video installation." (The "Buck Schwarz" is transcription noise for Buky Schwartz; see Corrections 8.) (C13910, leg 1 C13741, C13752, C13753, C13754, C13761, C13773)

The 3nos3 actions ran 1979-1982, mostly at night, in cheap ephemeral materials -- black and blue trash bags, masking tape, rolls of coloured plastic and cellophane -- staged to be photographed and to circulate second-hand through newspapers, television and mail art. Their own term, from 1980 onward, was "interversao" (interversion), a neologism fusing intervention and inversion, defined by the group as "the act of inverting; changing the natural or habitual order". The group worked under Brazil's military dictatorship (1964-1985) during the abertura, seeking autonomy from the traditional art circuit and treating the city as a surface for inscription and the media as a second site of the work. The actions, in order: Ensacamento (27 April 1979), hooding some sixty public statues with trash bags tied like a noose, starting at the Monument to Independence; X-Galeria (night of 2 June 1979), sealing 27 galleries plus MASP with a masking-tape X; Triptico (24-26 August 1979), three 2.20 x 1.40 m panels in a triangular ensemble on the sidewalk in front of the Theatro Municipal, the group's longest action at three days, ripped apart and removed by the Sanitation Department after being read as advertising; Interdicao (21 September 1979), coloured cellophane bands across Avenida Paulista near MASP, the members standing motionless like street vendors while motorists decided whether to cross; Interversao VI (night of 15 June 1980), a 100 m x 4 m red plastic roll across the two buracos linking Avenida Paulista and Avenida Consolacao, removed within hours by military police, fire brigade and transport department; Conecao (13 May 1981, daylight), a 25 m roll of red polyethylene erupting from a ventilation shaft at Avenida Reboucas / Avenida Dr. Arnaldo; Arco 10 (night of 7 December 1981), a 100 m roll of yellow cellophane suspended over Avenida Sumare; 27.4/3 (27 April 1982), two red plastic bands on the lawns beside Avenida 23 de Maio, continued as 27.4/3 - II on the Pinacoteca facade, where visitors entered only by lowering their bodies. (C13911, leg 1 C13755, C13756, C13757; leg 2 C13782-C13787, C13791)

In parallel he made his first video installations: "Television Sets" (1980) at the Cooperativa de Artistas Plasticos, six monitors and seven painted panels in closed circuit, and "Poligonos Regulares" (1981) at the Pinacoteca do Estado, 18 monitors in four polygons, a camera filming a black square drawn on the wall and feeding it live to every screen. In 1982 the group disbanded after its Pinacoteca show "3NOS3/3ANOS" (19 May-6 June 1982) and a final mock-performative farewell, "Acabou" (It's Over), at the newly inaugurated SESC Pompeia; the same year Franca co-organised Artemicro at MAC-USP with Silveira, and moved to Chicago on a scholarship to the School of the Art Institute of Chicago. He graduated from SAIC with a master's degree in 1984 -- the Art Institute of Chicago describes it as an MFA in art and technology -- and the technical facilities and intellectual atmosphere of SAIC were, in Machado's account, fundamental to the development of his style. (C13912, leg 1 C13742, C13743, C13744, C13751, C13758, C13762)

He lived in Chicago for the rest of his life, making videos and teaching there through the 1980s until his death. Machado records that he taught, wrote for newspapers and art journals, and handled the curatorship of video-art exhibitions; he was, in Machado's words, both a producer and "a researcher of electronic media". A SAIC catalogue (undated in the excerpt found) lists him among its Assistant Professors; the catalogue year was not established this run, so the rank and dates must not be printed without that check. The Chicago videos turned inward: single-channel experimental fiction, the artist himself as protagonist facing his doppelganger, literary adaptations of Yourcenar, Poe and Graciliano Ramos, no sound-image synchrony and no linear story. Machado: "What Rafael sought was to create a fiction, but without any of those resources of seduction that cinema or television use to capture the spectator. So Rafael Franca's videos are totally desdramatized videos, it is a Brechtian resource in the manner of video, and not in the manner of theatre." And as programme: "The main characters in Franca's videos are almost always himself, whether figured as a protagonist, or making himself projected in another. Rafael found in video a suitable way to meditate and speculate on his own inner conflicts, especially his greatest obsession: the fatality of death. His work, of a very personal nature, was also centered on the issue of homosexuality." (C13913, leg 1 C13745, C13763, C13764, C13765, C13776; leg 2 C13802)

From 1987 the documentary pole took over: "Without Fear of Vertigo" mixes documentary interviews about suicide and facing death as AIDS emerges, and his last video, "Preludio de uma Morte Anunciada", was finished only a few days before his death -- the Leiden thesis abstract says it was concluded two days prior -- and features Franca himself with his then-partner Geraldo Rivello, with the first names of friends lost to AIDS appearing on screen. Silveira, on their last meeting in Chicago, assured Franca that he had produced a complete oeuvre; Franca went as far as disclosing his desire to have video art transmitted over television to be watched "with the lights on". The closing line of his last video is "Above all they had no fear of vertigo". Silveira's verdict on the whole production: "His production was quite vertiginous. In fact, he knew how to sew together all these media: the interventions in the street, the prints, the books, the xerox, the video. I think Rafael was an artist with this capacity." (C13914, leg 1 C13746, C13775, C13777; leg 2 C13800)

His estate has been held by MAC-USP since his death in 1991: serigraphs, photographs, signed photocopies, master video tapes and extensive paper documentation, including technical plans, photographs and explanatory texts for four video installations from the 1980s. The installation objects themselves were NOT formally donated -- only the documentation -- which is why remounts depend on those documents; "Poligonos Regulares" was remounted at the Pinacoteca Octogono in 2011, when old CRT sets exploded in testing and on opening night. His brother Hugo Franca was a crucial collaborator on the posthumous documentary "Rafael Franca: Obra como Testamento" (2001) and now regulates the artist's copyright. Retrospectives followed: "Sem Medo da Vertigem" at Paco das Artes in 1997, "Preludio" at Galeria Jaqueline Martins in 2014, "Requiem and vertigo" at the same gallery in 2021, and "Rafael Franca" at the Art Institute of Chicago in 2024. (C13915, leg 1 C13747, C13766, C13767, C13772; leg 2 C13788, C13811)

His own framing of the video installation, for the study's voice: "In video installations, time is conjugated in the present and in the past. We find ourselves surrounded by events that are happening right now and events that occurred earlier... They give artists the capacity to create an environment that presents to the spectator an artificial world, where the basic questions of time, space and flow of information are determined by the artist and his work." And on television and spectacle: "Technological development reflects the current cultural and social moment, in which the idea of spectacle invades the everyday... in which the electronic image is invariably 'better' than the real, in which spectacle permeates all everyday actions and in which the spectator wants more and more to feel enveloped by something that really does not exist." (C13916, leg 1 C13774)

The through-line of the life is not a medium but a mechanism: a simple system -- a bag, a band, a closed circuit, a cut -- that makes a body, a space or an image unstable, and then lets the instability carry the meaning. The nine-year-old who decided to devote his life to painting became the twenty-two-year-old who hooded the city's statues overnight, the twenty-four-year-old who fed a black square back to eighteen monitors live, and the thirty-three-year-old who put his own face and his dead friends' first names on screen days before dying. The scale changes; the system does not. (C13917, leg 2 C13803)

## M1 -- The hooded monument: a trash bag turns a public statue blind overnight

A black trash bag tied like a noose over a statue's head turns a public monument blind and anonymous overnight. "Ensacamento" (1979, W02): some sixty statues -- 68 on the Gygax/Kilchmann count -- hooded between midnight and the early morning hours of 27 April 1979, on a route from the Monument to Independence via Marechal Deodoro, Monumento as Bandeiras, the Kennedy and Verdi busts and Carlos Gomes to Anhanguera, the press tipped off in advance so the photographs, not the bags, are what lasts; the bags were removed within hours. The bag is the whole sculpture: no carving, no casting, no plinth, just a cheap black skin over a bronze head that the city walks past every day without seeing. Watchable: a familiar bronze head goes black; the city wakes to its own heroes blindfolded; by noon the evidence is a newspaper photograph, and the photograph is the work's second site. (C13920, leg 2 C13782; leg 1 C13757)

**Takeaway:** a bag earns its monument when the cheapest possible skin changes what the statue means. If the bag only covers the head, it is a prank; if the city wakes to its heroes blindfolded and the photograph carries the act into the papers, the bag is the sculpture and the press is the gallery.


## M2 -- The tape X that seals the gallery: the show moves outside

A single X of masking tape across a door closes the art world out of itself. "X-Galeria" (1979, W05): the entrances of 27 commercial galleries plus MASP sealed in one night, 2 June 1979, with a mimeographed leaflet reading "O que esta dentro fica / O que esta fora se expande" (What is inside stays / What is outside expands). The tape is the cheapest possible lock, and it does the work of a manifesto: a door you could push open but do not, because the tape says the show is outside now. Watchable: a door with a tape X across it, a leaflet on the pavement, and the whole commercial circuit locked out of its own rooms by a material that costs nothing and holds nothing. (C13921, leg 2 C13785; leg 1 C13757)

**Takeaway:** a tape X earns its seal when the lock is weaker than the door and still holds. If the tape only marks the door, it is a sign; if twenty-eight rooms stay shut because a strip of tape says the art is outside, the tape is the exhibition and the street is the gallery.


## M3 -- The plastic band that closes the avenue: the driver decides, not the barrier

A roll of coloured plastic stretched across a road is a wall the motorist must decide about. "Interdicao" (1979, W04): bands of blue, yellow, brown and red cellophane across Avenida Paulista near MASP from 4pm on 21 September 1979, the members standing motionless like street vendors while drivers decided whether to cross; Ramiro telling drivers "You, sir, are the one who knows the answer". "Interversao VI" (1980, W07): a 100 m long, 4 m wide roll of red plastic drawn across the two large oval cavities (buracos) linking Avenida Paulista and Avenida Consolacao, lasting a few hours before military police, fire brigade and transport department restored order. The plastic could not stop a bicycle; the rule it announced was only a colour. Watchable: a car slows at a line of plastic, the driver weighs a material that could not stop a bicycle against a rule that is only a colour; the decision, not the barrier, is the work. (C13922, leg 2 C13784, C13787; leg 1 C13757)

**Takeaway:** a plastic band earns its closure when the barrier is too weak to work and the decision still happens. If the plastic only decorates the road, it is bunting; if a driver stops at a coloured line that could not hold him, the line is the law and the hesitation is the work.


## M4 -- The closed circuit: geometries in time

A black square drawn on the wall is filmed live and fed back to a bank of monitors, so the room watches itself watching. "Television Sets" (1980, W06): six TV monitors showing a static black square, interspersed with seven white panels each bearing a painted black square; a camera at the end of the sequence captured the final painted square and fed it back to all monitors live in closed circuit -- the first of Franca's video installations. "Poligonos Regulares" (1981, W08): 18 monitors grouped to form four different polygons, each screen a face; a video camera filmed a black square drawn on the gallery wall and transmitted it live in closed circuit to all monitors. "Third Commentary" (1980-1981, W09) is the third of the three, the same closed-circuit black geometry, venue and monitor count unstated. Silveira's phrase, quoted in the MODOS article, calls these "geometries in time": the xerox experiments extended into four-dimensional space. The 1983 SAIC lecture text quoted in MODOS states the programme plainly: television as a source of light, cameras and monitors as devices for experiments in time and space, and the closed circuit as a way to confuse reality and simulation. Watchable: the square on the wall and the square on every screen are the same square at the same instant; walk in front of the camera and you are inside the geometry; the room, the picture and the viewer are one loop, live. (C13923, leg 2 C13786, C13788, C13789, C13801, C13802; leg 1 C13774)

**Takeaway:** a closed circuit earns its loop when the picture and the room are the same thing at the same instant. If the monitors only play a recording, it is television; if a square drawn on the wall is live on eighteen screens and the visitor walking in is inside it, the circuit is the drawing and time is the geometry.


## M5 -- The xerox glitch as a drawing: the machine draws, the artist only feeds it

The copy machine's distortion is not a defect to correct but the image itself. "Sequencias" (c. 1979, W01): Franca's early xerox and offset-print experiments with the distortions and glitches of reproductive technologies, the groundwork for the later video work. The Visual AIDS essay dates the exploration to his USP years and names Sequencias as the example of his fascination with the distortions and glitches of reproductive technologies, laying the groundwork for the video work; no dimensions, edition, venue or surviving-sheet count is stated in sources read. The move is the same one the closed circuit will make with video: take a machine built to reproduce faithfully, and let its failure to do so become the picture. Watchable: a copied image that degrades, stretches and doubles with each pass until the error is the picture; the machine draws, the artist only feeds it. (C13924, leg 2 C13781; leg 1 C13749)

**Takeaway:** a glitch earns its drawing when the error replaces the image instead of spoiling it. If the copy is only a bad copy, it is a mistake; if each pass degrades the picture until the distortion is what you look at, the machine is the draughtsman and the failure is the work.


## M6 -- The doppelganger on video: desdramatized fiction

The artist faces his own double on screen, with no synchrony and no linear story, and the cut does the arguing. "Fighting the Invisible Enemy" (1983, W12): 2 min 45 sec of tuned-out TV screen, radio feedback, static noise and a seated figure suggesting an interrogation, a zapping-style chaotic paste-up of images and sounds. "Combat in Vain" (1984, W13): a free adaptation of Yourcenar, a man and a woman walking toward each other on a bridge through fast montage and cacophony, modernity's objects -- skyscrapers, cars, monitors -- turning threatening. "Reencontro" (1984, W14): the first of a narrative trilogy, a lonely man confronted by his own traumatic past and mortality, read as a modern William Wilson (Poe) under the dictatorship with explicit references to torture methods; Franca himself as protagonist facing his doppelganger, no sound-image synchrony, no linear story. The trilogy continues with "Getting Out" (1985, W15), a woman locked at home in a building that catches fire, and "As If Exiled in Paradise" (1986, W16), a writer at his desk whose reality is gradually dominated by hallucinations, terror and paranoia. Machado: the videos are "totally desdramatized", a Brechtian resource in the manner of video. Watchable: a face that is the artist and also his accuser; sound and image refuse to agree; the story never resolves, it only repeats, and the repetition is the argument. (C13925, leg 2 C13792, C13793, C13794, C13795, C13796; leg 1 C13776)

**Takeaway:** a double earns its fiction when the same face is both protagonist and accuser. If the double only mirrors the actor, it is a trick; if sound and image refuse to agree and the story repeats instead of resolving, the cut is the argument and the desdramatization is the drama.


## M7 -- The documentary turn: death named on screen

Fiction drops away and the video becomes a roll of the dead. "Without Fear of Vertigo" (1987, W17): 11 min 47 sec mixing documentary interviews with experimental form -- Franca and Brazilian and North-American friends discuss suicide and facing death as AIDS emerges -- ending with the police questioning of Peter Whitehall, jailed in the US for helping his companion Yann Bondy die. "Preludio de uma Morte Anunciada" (1991, W20): the final work, finished days before his death, Franca and his partner Geraldo Rivello exchanging caresses in fragmentary close-ups -- torsos, faces, hands -- to Bidu Saiao's 1943 recording of "Addio, del passato" from La Traviata, while from one minute in, the first names of male friends dead of AIDS fade in one at a time, ending with Franca's full face after the text "Above all they had no fear of vertigo". Machado and Videobrasil call Preludio one of the rare quasi-documentary moments in his video work; with the exception of these two, the videos are inventions of new narrative forms. Watchable: a name appears, holds, fades; another name; the list is the work, and the bodies on screen are the last two names not yet listed. (C13926, leg 2 C13797, C13800, C13803; leg 1 C13746)

**Takeaway:** a list of names earns its documentary when the names replace the fiction instead of illustrating it. If the names only caption the footage, they are credits; if one name fades in, holds, fades, and the next takes its place while two bodies hold each other on screen, the list is the work and the living are the last entries.


## M8 -- The low band you must duck under: the threshold is a gesture

A plastic band hung at body height turns entering a museum into a bow. "27.4/3 - II" (1982, W11): a horizontal band of red polyethylene stretched window to window across the Pinacoteca facade for the 3NOS3/3ANOS show (19 May-6 June 1982), at a height that let visitors enter only by lowering their bodies; the second act of the 27 April 1982 action laying two red plastic bands on the lawns beside Avenida 23 de Maio. Inside the show Franca showed the video "Carta 23"; outside, every visitor performed the work on the way in. Watchable: every visitor ducks the same red line on the way in; the threshold is a gesture, repeated hundreds of times a day, and the building itself is wearing the work. (C13927, leg 2 C13791; leg 1 C13758)

**Takeaway:** a band earns its threshold when the building makes every visitor perform it. If the band only hangs on the facade, it is decoration; if a hundred people a day bow to the same red line to get inside, the gesture is the work and the museum is wearing it.


## The aesthetic logic: a cheap system, a second site, a body made unstable

Franca's programme has three terms, and they never change, only scale down to the cheapest material that can carry them. First, the system is always cheap and legible: a trash bag, a strip of tape, a roll of plastic, a black square, a photocopier, a cut between two shots. Nothing in the work costs anything, and nothing in it is hidden -- the mechanism is visible in the same glance as the effect, which is why a passerby, a motorist or a gallery visitor can read it without a wall text. Second, the work always happens in a second site: the newspaper photograph of the hooded statues, the leaflet left by the taped gallery, the live monitor showing the square on the wall, the list of names fading in over the bodies. The first site -- the street at night, the wall, the screen -- is only the trigger; the meaning lands where the work is seen second-hand, after the fact, on paper or on a monitor. Third, the body is always the subject: the statue's head under the bag, the motorist deciding at the plastic line, the visitor ducking the red band, Franca's own face confronting its double, two bodies in close-up while the names of the dead fade in. The system is a way of putting a body in an unstable position and watching what it does.

That is why the same moves survive twelve years of changing supports, from the street to the gallery to the video monitor: take the cheapest legible system, aim it at a body or a space that thinks it is stable, and let the instability do the arguing -- the statue is blind, the gallery is shut, the driver must choose, the visitor must bow, the face is its own accuser, the list of names is the whole film. The medium is chosen per problem, which is why the oeuvre runs from trash bags to closed-circuit video without ever changing its question: what is the smallest system that makes a body, a space or an image unstable, and where does the work land when the system is gone? (C13928)
