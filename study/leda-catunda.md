## The life: the girl who learned to sew from her grandmother and made softness a doctorate

Leda Catunda Serra was born in Sao Paulo in 1961 -- the full date, 23 June 1961, is given only by the Wikipedia family, and no gallery, museum or interview source read gives a day or month, so the day and month are single-source and provisional (see Corrections 1) -- and lives and works in Sao Paulo. She is a painter first and a sculptor second only in the sense that her paintings keep leaving the wall: painting-objects, stuffed, sewn, padded, hung and suspended, made from the printed surfaces of everyday Brazilian life. (C14608, leg 1 C14420, C14421)

Her mother, Vera Catunda Serra, was an architect and landscaper, and her father, Geraldo Serra Gomes, was an architect and a professor at the Faculty of Architecture and Urbanism of the University of Sao Paulo (FAU-USP). She is described, in the Portuguese Wikipedia only (citing a Cultura FM radio programme), as the great-niece of the mathematician Omar Catunda and the composer Eunice Catunda -- single-source, not corroborated (see Corrections 7). Her childhood fed the work directly: she has said she learned to sew from her grandmother, who had a children's clothing factory in Campinas, and that as a child she sewed buttons and made clothes for her dolls; in a 2025 interview she separately credits "my Portuguese grandmother" who "sewed endlessly" for her sensibility toward handmade labour -- whether the two grandmothers are the same person is not stated anywhere, so the accounts are reported separately, not merged (see Corrections 9). She originally planned to be a rock musician before turning to visual art -- English Wikipedia only, citing an archived MAC-USP page that was not opened, single-source (see Corrections 6). (C14609, leg 1 C14422-C14426)

In 1980 Catunda enrolled in the visual arts programme of the Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado (FAAP) in Sao Paulo, and graduated in 1984 -- the degree described in several Portuguese sources as a licenciatura (teaching degree) in plastic arts. Her teachers included Regina Silveira, Julio Plaza, Nelson Leirner and Walter Zanini, through whom she had her first contacts with conceptual art, which showed in her first lithographs. She worked as a class monitor in a course given by Regina Silveira and formed close relationships with her teachers; she calls Silveira "my friend and teacher" and names Tarsila do Amaral, Georgia O'Keeffe and Eva Hesse among the artists who incite her.

VERDICT: Catunda's FAAP graduation in 1984 is corroborated on all sides -- Itau gives the 1980-1984 attendance, the English Wikipedia states the 1984 graduation, and a 1986 Veja magazine profile quoted in a secondary source already calls her "only two years out of FAAP". There is no contradiction to resolve here, which is the exact opposite of Zerbini's case in #670, where the gallery-CV line "graduated FAAP 1982" was shown false. Do NOT print a 1982 graduation for Zerbini; DO print 1984 for Catunda. (C14610, leg 1 C14427-C14430)

She first exhibited at the Young Art Salon in Santos in 1981 -- the Itau entry and a gallery profile call it the 9th (IX) Salao de Arte Jovem de Santos; the Google Arts and Culture asset text calls it the 11th (XI). The numeral is contradictory; IX is the majority reading and is used here with the flag (see Corrections 2). In 1982 she made lithographs appropriating television images, a procedure familiar from her teachers' generation, and at the same time painted realist pictures; her earliest mixed-media work was a composition based on photographs of TV images, used as the starting point for lithographs, paintings and video texts. In 1983 she took part in "Pintura como meio" (Painting as a Medium) at MAC-USP, curated by Aracy Amaral, alongside fellow newcomers Sergio Romagnolo, Ana Tavares, Ciro Cozzolino and Sergio Niculitcheff. The Portuguese Wikipedia states she was married to Romagnolo "for a period"; a secondary source says she "came to marry" him. No source read gives dates for the marriage, so no dates are given here (see Corrections 8). Also in 1983 she showed at the 17th Sao Paulo International Biennial, presenting a videotext. (C14611, leg 1 C14431-C14434)

In 1984 she took part in "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" (How Are You, 80s Generation?) at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage, Rio de Janeiro, the generation-defining show -- the English Wikipedia says it featured over 100 artists, the Zerbini study records 123 practitioners; either way it was a large group show, and her participation in it is the agreed turning point of her career. She gained national prominence after the show, appearing on magazine and newspaper covers, with critics noting her work as a challenge to the conceptual art of the 1970s. A 1986 Veja magazine cover story in Sao Paulo called her "A Garota das Artes" (The Girl of the Arts) and "a princesinha das artes da Geracao 80" (the little princess of the Geracao 80 arts), noting that only two years after graduating from FAAP she was already in the collections of the country's collectors, including Gilberto Chateaubriand. In the same year, 1984, she showed with Sergio Romagnolo, Ciro Cozzolino and Leonilson in a four-artist exhibition at Galeria Luisa Strina, Sao Paulo; the e-flux history of the gallery names Leonilson, Catunda, Caetano de Almeida and Edgard de Souza as the FAAP-attending generation that marked an important transition for Luisa Strina. Also in 1984 she took part in the 1st Havana Biennial in Cuba and in ARCO/84 in Madrid. Note: the Catalan and Spanish Wikipedias state that in 1984 she "directed" the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage. No other source read supports this; she was a participant in the show held there, not its director, and the claim is treated as a garbled Wikipedia line, not a fact (see Corrections 3). (C14612, leg 1 C14435-C14438)

Her first solo exhibition, as listed by both the Itau entry and her own gallery CV, was in 1985 at Thomas Cohn Arte Contemporanea, Rio de Janeiro. No source read calls any earlier show her first solo, and the 1985 Thomas Cohn listing is consistent across the two independent listings, so it is used without a caveat -- contrast Zerbini, whose listed 1982 Madrid "first solo" was contradicted by the artist himself. Further early solos followed: 1986, Espaco de Investigacao at MARGS (Museu de Arte do Rio Grande do Sul), Porto Alegre; 1987, Galeria Luisa Strina, Sao Paulo; 1988, Thomas Cohn, Rio de Janeiro. She showed at the 18th Sao Paulo International Biennial in 1985, and in 1987 in "Modernidade: 20th-Century Brazilian Art" at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris. Other 1980s group shows include "Today's Art of Brazil" at the Hara Museum, Tokyo (1985), "Brazilian Painting" at Snug Harbor, New York (1986), and "U-ABC" at the Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam (1989), from which the Stedelijk acquired her painting/collage "Sozinha no quarto" (1985-1986). (C14613, leg 1 C14439-C14442)

In 1986 she began teaching graphic arts at FAAP and in her own studio, continuing until the mid-1990s, and from the late 1980s she gave workshops and open courses at cultural institutions in Brazil and occasionally abroad. Between 1998 and 2005 she taught painting and drawing in the Visual Arts programme at the Faculdade Santa Marcelina (FASM) in Sao Paulo. In 1990 she received the Premio Brasilia de Artes Plasticas / Distrito Federal in the acquisition category -- the only award listed in the Itau entry and the only award in her gallery CV, which records it as "Aquisicao, Premio Brasilia de Artes Plasticas, Brasilia". No other award was found in any source read. (C14614, leg 1 C14443-C14445)

Her early 1980s work explored the limits between painting and object, with a descriptive, cartoonish, narrative character, using printed fabrics and industrial materials finished by hand. She began the "Vedacoes" (Closures) series in the early 1980s: printed images on soft objects -- blankets, towels, curtains -- partially covered with paint, leaving only fragments of the figures exposed and recreating the image through subtraction. From 1989 she tried to resize her production, paying closer attention to its visual and plastic aspects and freeing herself from the anecdotal, strongly narrative character of the earlier work, without abandoning the figure. In the 1990s she eliminated narratives in favour of geometric compositions, in a production "cleaner" in colour, figuration and texture, and deepened her interest in the materials themselves: tulle, velvet, plastic, quilting, canvas, leather and Formica, superimposed into pictorial surfaces. Her "soft paintings" -- paintings over towels, bedclothes, leather, velvet and silk -- became her signature, and by the 1990s the stuffed, padded, sewn volumes made the work tend from two dimensions toward three. (C14615, leg 1 C14446-C14450)

In her own words, "collage is the best word to describe my creative process", and her motto is "I like to enjoy the things other people like" -- an interest in the taste of others, in the images people surround themselves with, from upholstery fabric to rock-band T-shirts. Everything is made by hand: "no one is allowed to sew any of my painting-objects but me", though she has three assistants, one handling emails and images and two helping build the works, with heavy wood works built in a carpenter's workshop. In recent years the work has taken on a baroque intensity: pleated drapes, lush ornamentation, proliferating flaps and tongue-shaped forms, which she links partly to the pandemic years working alone in the studio, "responding to a kind of restlessness -- an urge to keep adding something more". (C14616, leg 1 C14451, C14452)

She obtained her doctorate in Arts at the Escola de Comunicacoes e Artes of the University of Sao Paulo (ECA/USP), with the dissertation "Poetica da Maciez: Pinturas e Objetos Poeticos" (Poetics of Softness: Paintings and Poetic Objects), supervised by Julio Plaza. The YEAR is contradictory across sources: the Itau entry and the Portuguese Wikipedia give 2003; a scholarly article on her work cites the thesis as "USP/ECA, Sao Paulo, 2002"; the English, Spanish and Catalan Wikipedias give 2001, apparently deriving from an old MAC-USP page. The 2003 date (Itau) is used here as the primary reading, with 2001 and 2002 flagged as unresolved alternatives (see Corrections 4). No masters degree is mentioned in any source read; do not assert one. The doctorate fed directly back into the practice: her 2003 solo show at the Centro Universitario Maria Antonia, Sao Paulo, was titled "Poetics of Softness: Paintings and Objects", the same title as the thesis. (C14617, leg 1 C14453, C14454)

She showed at the 22nd Sao Paulo International Biennial in 1994, in the "Mostra do Descobrimento" (Rediscovery Exhibition) in 2000, and at the 3rd Bienal do Mercosul in Porto Alegre in 2001. The first book on her work, "Leda Catunda" by Tadeu Chiarelli, was published by Cosac & Naify in 1998. Later monographs and catalogues listed in her own bibliography include "Leda Catunda 1983-2008", Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo, curated by Ivo Mesquita (2009); "Leda Catunda - Pinturas Recentes", Museu Oscar Niemeyer, Curitiba (2013); "Leda Catunda: I Love You Baby", Instituto Tomie Ohtake, curated by Paulo Miyada (2017); and "Leda Catunda - Tempo circular", with an interview by Fernanda Brenner and text by Paulo Miyada (2019). Her first large retrospective, "Leda Catunda 1983-2008", was held at the Estacao Pinacoteca, Sao Paulo, in 2009, bringing together approximately 70 works -- paintings, watercolours and collages -- developed from 1983 to 2008, described by her gallery as "the most complete exhibition ever held of Catunda's work". A survey of recent paintings, "Pinturas Recentes", was shown at the Museu Oscar Niemeyer, Curitiba, and at MAM Rio, Rio de Janeiro; her CV lists it under 2013 at both venues and the Artsy/FDAG account agrees on 2013, while the Americas Society page says 2014 and places the Niemeyer museum in Rio de Janeiro (it is in Curitiba) -- the CV's 2013 dating is used here (see Corrections 5). (C14618, leg 1 C14455-C14458)

"I Love You Baby", Instituto Tomie Ohtake, Sao Paulo, 2016-2017, curated by Paulo Miyada, was an 88-piece solo exhibition of works from 2013 to 2016, centred on the selfie and on what the artist calls "emotional consumerism". She took part in the 33rd Sao Paulo Biennial in 2018 ("Afinidades Afetivas"), her fourth Sao Paulo Biennial appearance after 1983, 1985 and 1994 -- the four-edition count is given by the Americas Society, by Bortolami, and by the Google Arts and Culture asset text, and is consistent with her CV's 2018 Biennial entry (see Corrections 10). Further solos: "Projeto Parede | Paisagem moderna", MASP - Museu de Arte de Sao Paulo, 2019; "Leda Catunda & Alejandra Seeber", MALBA, Buenos Aires, 2021; "Leda Catunda: Geography", Bortolami, New York, 2022; "Leda Catunda: EUFORIA", ICA Milano (Fondazione ICA), Milan, 2023; "Paisagem Selvagem", Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel | Carpintaria, Rio de Janeiro, 2024, marking her return to Rio a decade after her last exhibition at MAM Rio. In 2025 she had two further solos: "I like to like what others are liking" at the Sharjah Art Foundation, Sharjah, United Arab Emirates, 26 September 2025 to 8 February 2026, curated by Hoor Al Qasimi with Meera Madhu, described as the most comprehensive solo exhibition of her work outside Brazil to date, navigating four decades of practice; and "Favorita" at Emanuela Campoli, Paris, her first solo exhibition in Paris. A 2015 survey, "Leda Catunda 1985-2015", was held at the Centro Cultural Banco do Nordeste, Fortaleza. A MAM Sao Paulo survey is listed by the Americas Society as 2019; her CV does not show a 2019 MAM Sao Paulo solo, and the 2019 museum show in her CV is the MASP Projeto Parede, so the Americas Society's "MAM, Sao Paulo (2019)" is treated as a likely conflation of MAM and MASP and is not repeated as fact (see Corrections 5). (C14619, leg 1 C14459-C14463)

Her early gallery history runs through Galeria Luisa Strina (four-artist show 1984, solo 1987, and a place in the gallery's 40th-anniversary history show in 2014-2015) and Thomas Cohn Arte Contemporanea, Rio de Janeiro (solos 1985, 1988, 1993). From the mid-1990s she showed with Galeria Camargo Vilaca, later Galeria Fortes Vilaca (solos 1996, 1998, 2002, 2004), the gallery now known as Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel, which represents her in Brazil. She is also represented by Bortolami in New York, which lists her among its represented artists and showed "Geography" in 2022. She has also had solos with Galeria Ramis Barquet, New York (2001, 2004), Galeria 111, Lisbon (2008), Galeria Ruth Benzacar, Buenos Aires (2012), and Kubik Gallery, Porto (2017). Her work is held in public collections including the Centre Pompidou, Paris; MALBA, Buenos Aires; MOCA, Los Angeles; Instituto Inhotim, Brumadinho; Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo; Fundacao de Serralves, Porto; Fundacion ARCO, Santiago de Compostela; Stedelijk Museum Amsterdam; Sharjah Art Foundation; The Cleveland Museum of Art; MASP, Sao Paulo; MAM Sao Paulo; MAM Rio de Janeiro; the Blanton Museum of Art, Austin; MAC-USP, Sao Paulo; and MAM Bahia, Salvador. (C14620, leg 1 C14464-C14467)

Evolution: 1983-1990, flat but already soft. The Vedacoes series works by subtraction on printed blankets, towels and curtains; "Jaguar" (1984) keeps the found print legible. Figuration is borrowed, not drawn: cartoons, hearts, suns, flowers, animals and geometric shapes, plus handmade and mass-produced materials (fabric, towels, blankets, artificial fur, plastic, leather, even puzzle pieces). Volume arrives as an object glued onto the picture: plastic roofs in "Paisagem da Estrada" (1987), fake fur and Formica in "Figado" (1990). Critics of the time note the supports themselves: beach tent, leather of dismantled armchairs, bath towels, comforters, heavy lace, wigs, blender-cover frills turned into little-house roofs with lights. Contrast with Zerbini (#670): his large lush canvases stay flat and painterly; her "canvas" is already a household object with a previous life. 1991-1993, the turn inward. By 1991 the recognisable patterns and motifs had almost completely disappeared, giving way to more abstract compositions, while material appropriation stayed central; bellies, tongues, insects and hybrid semi-abstract symbols replaced floral and animal patterns. "Barriga" (1993) is the pivot: object-like paintings become painting-like objects, autonomous forms occupying space. The body replaces the picture: bulge, sag, weight. 1993-2010, soft painting as system. Large-scale painting-objects and installations ("Paisagem com Onca", "Duas Arvores", "Rio Comprido") put the work on the floor and let visitors walk around it. The 2006 painting-objects are tears and organic crescents made by collage-like superimposing, intertwining, cutting out and painting of supple, richly textured, intensely coloured materials; the result is voluminous and padded, transcending the pictorial plane. The 2004 large paper collages use superposition, intertwining, juxtaposition and transparency, with an amoeba-like principal form and contained images from patterned paper or fabric, including original brain designs printed on voile. Personal images enter by silk-screen: "Todo o Pessoal" (2006); "Mundo Macio" (2007) strings nature-printed scraps on orange fabric. 2013-2019, the selfie and accumulation. "I Love You Baby" (2016-2017, 88 works, 2013-2016) turns Facebook selfies and rock-band T-shirts, sarongs, pareos and beach towels into painting-objects; the main operation is collage followed by a paint finish, and only Catunda herself sews the painting-objects. "Morumbi" (2014) and "Crowd" (2016) scale this up to 260 x 372 cm and 245 x 520 cm. By 2019 excess itself becomes the construction principle: "Selva" hides images under layers of paint and fabric; "Pretzel" (2018) and the ongoing "Coisas para Comprar" inventory product labels. 2021-2025, baroque, suspended, bodily. Tongues, bellies, flaps and protrusions proliferate: "Duas linguas" (2021), "Gotas transparentes" (2021), the Escamosa series (2021-2023) and "Gomos" (2023). Gravity is now a tool: foam accumulates at the bottom of stuffed wall works, and "Figado", "Siamesas" and "Duas Arvores" occupy wall and floor at once, while suspended works invite viewers to walk around them. The 2024 "Paisagem Selvagem" works push accumulation to a fever pitch: "Paisagem Selvagem II", "Caprichosa", "Cinema" and "Sao Tomas". "Favorita" (2025) closes the arc by returning to real garments as the image itself, and "Caminho com saias" (2025) is mainly pleated drapes with protuberances and unfolding flaps. The circle is explicit in the Sharjah essay: domestic patterns from the early pieces re-emerge, now coexisting with the abstract belly/tongue forms. (C14622, leg 2 C14470-C14489, C14490-C14506)


## M1 -- Subtraction painting: the picture is made by hiding, not by adding

Take a printed soft object -- a blanket, a towel, a curtain, a bedspread -- and paint over most of its image, leaving fragments exposed; the painting is an edit of a found picture. "Vedacao Rosa" (1983, W01): acrylic on fabric, 220 x 140 cm, in the MAM Rio / Chateaubriand collection; Catunda looked for printed images on soft objects and partially covered them, leaving only fragments of the figures exposed, recreating the image through subtraction. "Jaguar" (1984, W02) inverts the move: instead of covering the printed image, her painting highlights it -- she joins cut-out fabrics, sews them together and superimposes elements unusual in painting, and the works lie between painting and object. Watchable: a printed cloth image half-erased by paint; the image flickers between legible and lost, and the eye keeps trying to complete a picture the paint refuses to finish. (C14623, leg 2 C14470, C14471, C14490)

**Takeaway:** a picture earns its subtraction when the hiding is the drawing. If the paint only covers, it is a whitewash; if a printed blanket is edited down to fragments that the eye must complete, the subtraction is the composition and the found image is the material.


## M2 -- Stuff and sag: gravity is a tool, not a flaw

Canvas or fabric is stretched, stuffed with foam and fixed to the wall; the stuffing settles, foam accumulates at the bottom, and the form slumps forward off the wall. "Barriga" (1993, W05): stretched and stuffed canvas, a literal three-dimensional belly that slumps forward off the wall. The Sharjah essay, read end to end: "This is not a painting of a belly; Barriga is unapologetically physical, as if declaring: 'I'm here, I'm heavy, I'm sagging, I'm real.'" It marks the break between her earlier and later work and the start of her "digestion" process. "Duas Barrigas" (1993, W06) doubles the motif; two bulges side by side read as a body with company, not a single lump. "Figado" (1990, W04) works the same contrast theatrically: soft fake fur against hard Formica, an organ name attached to a lumpy fake-fur mass, the material joke (fur = flesh, Formica = hard shell) doing the figuration. Watchable: a slow, permanent droop; the painting looks heavy, tired and bodily, advancing toward whoever approaches it. (C14624, leg 2 C14473, C14474, C14475, C14495)

**Takeaway:** a painting earns its sag when gravity does the modelling. If the stuffing only bulges, it is a cushion; if the foam settles to the bottom and the belly slumps forward off the wall, heavy and real, the sag is the form and the wall is only where it started.


## M3 -- Sewing as drawing: the seam is the line

Cut-out fabrics are joined by sewing, by hand and by machine; only Catunda sews her own painting-objects, and the seam is the line that joins unlike images. "Jaguar" (1984, W02): sewn cut-out fabrics, the seam joining what paint alone could not. "Morumbi" (2014, W12): acrylic on canvas and fabric, 260 x 372 cm, a large painting-object in which lines cross to unite different images borrowed from her studio and neighbourhood -- the fabric bands stitch separate images into one neighbourhood map. "Crowd" (2016, W14): acrylic on canvas and fabric, 245 x 520 cm, from "I Love You Baby" (88 works, 2013-2016), the show about the selfie and emotional consumerism; the same interview describes the show's "freeform, brazen machine-sewn large eye-drop curves in pink and other kitschy color mixes... stitched with garish mass-media references". Watchable: seams, puckers and stitched curves read as drawn lines, but with a physical ridge and a slight pull in the cloth -- the drawing has a thickness a pencil never had. (C14625, leg 2 C14471, C14481, C14483, C14501)

**Takeaway:** a seam earns its line when the joining is the drawing. If the sewing only holds the cloth together, it is craft; if the stitched curve is the contour and the pucker is the shading, the needle is the pencil and the ridge is the mark.


## M4 -- Domestic textile as support: the bedroom is the canvas

Towels, bedclothes, sheets, velvet, silk, voile, leather, T-shirts and jeans replace canvas; each material arrives with its own print, pile, sheen and history. "Vedacao Rosa" (1983, W01) is acrylic on fabric -- a blanket, not a canvas. "Mar Linda" (2016, W13): acrylic on fabric and voile, 174 x 174 cm, based on the posts and selfies of a well-known Brazilian social media teenager who lent Catunda her Facebook photos; the teenager performs a fictitious character dubbed "Mar Linda" (in Portuguese slang, "the most beautiful"); shown in "I Love You Baby", Instituto Tomie Ohtake, 2016-2017. "Favorita" (2025, W20): the title work of her first Paris solo show, clustered motifs including geometric shapes and real apparel charged with emotional investment -- a row of skirts, teamwear, bags and jeans; the brand, cut, wash and wear of the jeans signal a "tribe", and signs of wear become emotional traces imprinted with the body's shape. The Bienal de Sao Paulo logo (Aloisio Magalhaes, 1965) appears top right. Watchable: the support is already patterned and already touched; paint sits differently on terry cloth, velvet and voile, and the viewer reads bedroom and wardrobe before reading art. (C14626, leg 2 C14470, C14482, C14489, C14501)

**Takeaway:** a textile earns its support when the cloth arrives with a life. If the fabric is only a surface, it is a substitute canvas; if the towel was printed before the painter touched it and the jeans were worn before they were hung, the support is the subject and the paint is the second layer.


## M5 -- Layer, hide, partly disclose: accumulation is the organising principle

Images are stacked under layers of paint and fabric so they are never fully disclosed; accumulation is the organising principle, not a side effect. "Selva" (2019, W15): with "Linguas Douradas II" (2019), it embodies "the logic of accumulation, where images are not always fully disclosed, but hidden and partly covered in multiple layers of paint and fabric". "Escamosa II" (2023, W18): acrylic and enamel with fabric and voile, 260 x 74 cm, a tall, narrow scaly column; the surface reads as skin, armour and fabric at once. "Caprichosa" (2024, W19): appropriated images, such as a heavy-metal skull T-shirt and the camel figures from a Moroccan pencil holder, brought together "within an intensely baroque landscape"; FDAG calls it the fever pitch of curved, winding, wrinkled, rolled, folded and accumulated forms in a tableau of fragmentary scenes. Watchable: the eye hunts for half-buried pictures; each look finds a different fragment, and the work never settles into one image. (C14627, leg 2 C14484, C14487, C14488)

**Takeaway:** a layer earns its hiding when the accumulation is the structure. If the layers only overlap, it is clutter; if the images are deliberately never fully disclosed and the pile itself is the organising principle, the hiding is the composition and the next look finds a different picture.


## M6 -- Print transfer, silk-screened memory: a face becomes upholstery

Personal or mass-media photographs are silk-screened or printed onto fabric and voile, then cut, sewn and painted over; the print is a memory made into cloth. "Todo o Pessoal" (2006, W11): in the early 2000s Catunda began silk-screening personal images onto the fabric she worked with, using images of friends and family; "Todo o Pessoal" (2006) and "Todo o Pessoal II" (2008) are the named examples. "Mar Linda" (2016, W13): borrowed Facebook selfies silk-screened and painted onto fabric and voile. "Mundo Macio" (2007, W10): fabric printed with nature images cut into organic shapes and connected by strings of orange fabric -- the "morulas" moment of the early 2000s, works made of many layers of fabric. Watchable: a face or a landscape appears as a textile pattern; intimacy is flattened into decoration and then re-inflated by stuffing. (C14628, leg 2 C14479, C14480, C14482, C14498)

**Takeaway:** a print earns its transfer when the memory becomes the material. If the photo is only printed on cloth, it is a souvenir; if the friend's face is silk-screened, cut, sewn and painted over until portraiture becomes upholstery, the memory is the pattern and the cloth is the photograph.


## M7 -- Wall-to-floor installation: the room is the support

Works are built at human scale to occupy wall and floor at once, or hang suspended so viewers walk around them; rivers run two metres into the room and trees stand on the floor. "Rio Comprido" (W09): a great blue plastic-and-canvas structure with several scissures (slits) that expose the wall it is attached to; it forms a large river that flows to the ground and runs two metres into the room. Visitors can walk around it and establish physical contact with it. "Duas Arvores" (W08): two trees made of fabric entwine, with four small blue plastic lakes on the ground; the work occupies part of the floor and visitors can walk around it. "Paisagem com Onca" (W07): a landscape composed of hundreds of pieces of printed fabric -- images of plants, flowers, skies, jaguars -- overlaid, cut and printed, human-scale, voluminous, inflated. Watchable: the painting stops being something faced and becomes something entered; the viewer's body, walking around it, completes the composition. (C14629, leg 2 C14476, C14477, C14478, C14495)

**Takeaway:** a painting earns the floor when the room becomes its support. If the work only hangs low, it is a big picture; if the river leaves the wall, crosses two metres of floor and lets the viewer walk around it and touch it, the room is the canvas and the walk is the viewing.


## M8 -- Light through cloth: the painting leaks colour into the room

Transparent and translucent textiles (voile, stacked fabrics) are layered so light passes through them and throws coloured shadows into the surrounding space. "Gotas transparentes" (2021, W17): "Light filters through stacked textiles, casting coloured shadows out into the surrounding space." The Sharjah essay adds that in this work (and "Besouro", 2021) floral, geometric and animal patterns are applied to fabrics cut into ovoid shapes that morph into tongues, swell into bellies and slip back into tongues. "Mar Linda" (2016, W13) uses voile as a painting layer; "Escamosa II" (2023, W18) layers fabric and voile in a tall scaly column. Watchable: the work has a second, projected image on the wall and floor that moves as the light changes; the painting leaks colour into the room. (C14630, leg 2 C14482, C14486, C14487)

**Takeaway:** cloth earns its light when the shadow is the second painting. If the voile is only translucent, it is a veil; if stacked textiles throw coloured shadows onto the wall and floor that move with the light, the room is the second canvas and the sun is the second painter.


## The aesthetic logic: softness, excess, and the body that will not stay flat

Catunda's programme has three terms, and they never change, only grow more bodily. First, softness: the support is already soft, already printed, already touched -- a towel, a bedspread, a T-shirt, a pair of jeans -- and the painting is what happens to it next: covered, cut, sewn, stuffed. The doctorate is titled "Poetica da Maciez" for a reason: softness is not the subject, it is the method. Second, excess: accumulation is the organising principle, from the hundreds of printed scraps in a landscape to the baroque pile of a skull T-shirt and souvenir camels to the real clothes of "Favorita" -- and her own motto explains the appetite, "I like to enjoy the things other people like". The work takes in what other people surround themselves with and keeps adding, until the pile is the picture. Third, the body: bellies, tongues, flaps, protrusions -- the painting bulges, sags, slumps and oozes, and gravity is a tool, not a flaw. The foam settles to the bottom and the belly advances toward the viewer.

That is why the same moves survive forty years of changing supports, from printed blankets edited by subtraction to stuffed bellies to silk-screened selfies to real garments hung as painting, without ever changing their question: what does a painting become when its support is a thing somebody already lived with? The answer, at the end, is a wardrobe: a row of skirts, teamwear, bags and jeans, their wear marks the biography, hung as a painting in Paris -- the garment is the image, and the body that wore it is the drawing. (C14631)


## The network: the closest friend, the classmates, the teachers, the group

1. **Leonilson (1957-1993)** -- one of his two closest friends of the early-1980s period, with Sergio Romagnolo; doorway itself, already studied (#669). Documented from both sides: Newcity Brazil's "Keeping Leonilson Alive" (2017) states, of Leonilson's early-1980s pop-figurative phase, "It bears the mark of a group of artists, especially Leda Catunda and Sergio Romagnolo, his closest friends at the period"; asked about Leonilson as "your close friend", Catunda answers, "We were very very close friends. I guess what impacted me most about Leonilson was the fact he was a downright full-time artist, he felt and fed on art twenty-four hours a day." The local #669 study records that they met in 1983. Shared exhibition: the four-artist show "Leda Catunda, Sergio Romagnolo, Ciro Cozzolino and Leonilson" at Galeria Luisa Strina, Sao Paulo, 1984; a 1984 photograph of the four together (left to right: Leonilson, Romagnolo, Cozzolino, Catunda) illustrates the Newcity interview. No joint work evidenced; the link is the friendship, the shared show, and the shared generation. (C14632, leg 3 C14520, C14521)

2. **Luiz Zerbini (b. 1959)** -- Geracao 80 peer, shared 1984 show, doorway itself, already studied (#670). Both took part in "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage, Rio de Janeiro, 1984. Catunda herself names Zerbini first among her generation friends: "I am inevitably connected to the production of my artist friends from my generation, Luiz Zerbini, Daniel Senise, Barrao, Ana Tavares, Jac Leirner, Paulo Pasta and others." Contrast pole: Zerbini's painting is large-scale and lush -- Fondation Cartier: "His canvases are of impressive dimensions"; the six-metre-wide "Happiness Beyond Paradise" is the extreme case -- against Catunda's sewn soft supports; the contrast is material and handling, not size alone. No joint work by Catunda and Zerbini is evidenced; the link is the shared show, the shared generation, and the shared gallery (both show with Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel). He appears with Catunda in both 1984 anchor shows: the Parque Lage Geracao 80 show and the four-artist Luisa Strina show (with Cozzolino and Leonilson). The ICAA Documents Project discusses him alongside Catunda and Milhazes as Geracao 80 painters. No local study file exists for Romagnolo. Doorway for a later study: through the shared superlative and the shared 1984 shows. (C14634, leg 3 C14524)

4. **Ciro Cozzolino (b. 1959)** -- fourth member of the 1984 group. The fourth name in the 1984 Luisa Strina show and the third figure from the left in the 1984 photograph with Leonilson, Romagnolo and Catunda.
