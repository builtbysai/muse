# Wilhelm von Kobell: Study Notes

**Site:** Wilhelm von Kobell (6 April 1766, Mannheim - 15 July 1853, Munich; as commonly cited, not verified from a primary source -- the 1855 death year persists in the 1905 New International Encyclopaedia and the auction databases), German painter, printmaker and teacher; the German who learned the Dutch cattle piece from a dead Haarlem copyist and taught it to Munich. The only named artist the RKD says Dirck van Bergen influenced: son of Ferdinand Kobell, nephew of Franz Kobell, first lessons from father and uncle, engraving under Franz Anton von Leydendorf and Egid Verhelst at the Mannheim Zeichnungsakademie; Rome 1778 (he was twelve -- see D3); court painter at Mannheim, followed his father to Munich, professor at the Munich Academy (1808 in Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia, 1814 in the Lenbachhaus account -- see D4); supported by Charles Theodore, Elector of Bavaria, 500 florins a year 1792-1799; Vienna 1809 and Paris 1810 for battle-piece studies. ## The life

Wilhelm von Kobell was born in Mannheim on 6 April 1766, the son of Ferdinand Kobell (1740-1799), landscape painter and etcher, and Maria Anna Lederer (1744-1820), daughter of a Court Councilor; Ferdinand and Maria Anna had seven children. His first lessons were from his father and his uncle Franz Kobell (1749-1822), landscape and architectural painter. He then trained in engraving under Franz Anton von Leydendorf and Egid Verhelst at the Zeichnungsakademie in Mannheim. He studied and copied works in the galleries of Mannheim and Dusseldorf, especially those of Philips Wouwerman, and practised various styles, including 17th-century Dutch painting and 18th-century English art. The sources say he went to Rome in 1778; he was twelve, and no source read explains the circumstances (see D3).

He became court painter at Mannheim after his return, then followed his father to Munich when the Elector's court moved there. Charles Theodore, Elector of Bavaria, supported him with 500 florins a year from 1792 until Theodore's death in 1799. He went to Vienna in 1809 and Paris in 1810 to make studies for his battle pieces. He became professor of landscape painting at the Munich Academy -- in 1808 by the Wikipedia and 1905 encyclopaedia account, in 1814 by the Lenbachhaus account, succeeding Johann Georg von Dillis; the chair was abolished in 1826 at the urging of the history painter Peter von Cornelius (see D4). He was made an honorary member of the Vienna Academy in 1808 (Invaluable, search result only). He was court painter to Crown Prince Ludwig in Munich and depicted numerous horses from the royal stable. He was ennobled -- personal nobility in 1815 by one account, 1833 by another, hereditary nobility in 1833 or 1837 (Invaluable, search result only, contradicting itself). He died in Munich on 15 July 1853 by the Wikipedia, Wikidata and Met account, or in 1855 by the 1905 encyclopaedia and the auction databases (see D1 of the disputes: the death-year dispute is carried as a flag, not settled here).

His early paintings were landscapes, rural genre scenes and animal pieces, of which the galleries of Bamberg, Weimar, Mannheim, Karlsruhe, Berlin, Darmstadt, Frankfort and Wiesbaden contain fine specimens. He became most widely known through his etchings and aquatint engravings after Wouverman, Berchem, Roos, Ruysdael and others, in which he reproduced the peculiar style of each master with uncommon success. Two cycles of Napoleonic battle paintings were made for Marshal Berthier, Napoleon's Chief of Staff, and for Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria, held by the Neue Pinakothek, many on permanent loan to the Bayerische Armeemuseum in Ingolstadt; the two cycles were first reunited in Munich in over thirty years in the 2014 exhibition "B for Battle Paintings". The Siege of Kosel (1808), 202 x 305.5 cm, was the first of a commissioned series of 12 large canvases recording the allies' victorious engagements. He travelled to England, France and Italy throughout his life but ultimately based his style on Dutch art. The catalogue raisonne is Siegfried Wichmann, "Wilhelm von Kobell. Monographie und kritisches Verzeichnis der Werke", Munich 1970, cited in auction listings, not read in this study. The earliest monograph cited is a Baron Stengel biography, Nuremberg 1822.

He left no named pupil in any source read. He was professor of landscape painting at the Munich Academy and is described as a teacher, but no pupil of his is named anywhere in the sources read -- a real gap in the record, not an absence the legs could prove. His son Wolfgang Franz von Kobell (1803-1882) was a mineralogist and writer of Bavarian-dialect poems and stories, not a painter. The family chronicler Luise von Kobell wrote "Unter den vier Koenigen Bayerns" (Munich, 1894), cited as a source on Wilhelm.

## M1. Style-copying etching: the pastiche after a named master
Kobell's reproductive etchings and aquatints after a specific Dutch master deliberately reproduce that master's peculiar style rather than imposing his own hand. A set of prints after Wouverman, Berchem, Roos, Ruysdael and others, each visibly in a different handling; the prints were his widest fame -- "He became most widely known through his etchings and aquatint engravings... in which he reproduced the peculiar style of each master with uncommon success" (Wikipedia; New International Encyclopaedia). Leg 2's aquatint-as-translation finding belongs here: the aquatints are not original inventions but translations of named Dutch (and one family) pictures into a tonal print medium -- take a Dutch oil, re-draw it in etching, and use aquatint resin grain to rebuild its tonal masses (sky, water, foliage) as printable tone. The dated sequence runs Berchem 1791, van Bergen 1792, Wouwerman 1794, Franz Kobell 1797, Wijck 1801, Romeyn 1819. **Takeaway:** a style dial -- one scene re-rendered in several distinct master-handlings, with the "after" credit carried on the museum record itself.

## M2. Aquatint as tonal wash
Aquatint, in which the outline is first etched and the shadows are then laid in by an acid wash, producing an effect resembling a drawing in Indian ink. The Met objects Les Occupations du Soir au Camp (1794, after Wouwerman, sheet 34.1 x 43.2 cm) and Landscape with Shepherds and Cattle (1797, after his uncle Franz Kobell, 25.6 x 33.7 cm) are both catalogued as aquatint. The process description comes from Fagan's 1876 British Museum Handbook, returned in search only, not read end to end -- treat the process description as search-sourced. **Takeaway:** tonal washes, granular grain, soft tonal masses instead of line; the print as a wash drawing that can be repeated.

## M3. The horse as anatomical subject
Horses painted with precise, observed anatomy -- musculature, wet coats, gait, posture -- as the central subject rather than staffage. Riders at the Tegernsee (1832) is described in search results as rendering "the gleam of wet coats" and "the muscular forms of the horses"; the RISD checklist notes he depicted numerous horses from the royal stable for Crown Prince Ludwig; his reputation rested on "clever work on battle scenes and horses". Leg 2's horse-anatomy finding belongs here: the Met's Three studies on a man on a saddle (black and white chalk on blue paper, three attempts at one seated rider) is direct evidence that the rider was drawn repeatedly as a study before it was painted -- the horse is not a prop, it is the unit of the picture, and the rider's seat is the variable Kobell tested; the white horse in Riders at the Tegernsee and in the c. 1852 Emming rider is the same device, a light-coloured horse used as the picture's brightest mass. **Takeaway:** horse gaits as a motion study -- walk, trot, rear, fall -- with the rider's seat as the tested variable.

## M4. Battle as strategic map: the chessboard composition
Battle groups placed like pieces on a chessboard across a readable terrain, so the painting can be read like a strategic map. The Web Gallery of Art on The Siege of Kosel: he "placed his groups of horses like figures on a chess board, and the painting can be read like a strategic map"; he "evaded the influence of the theatrical battle scenes in vogue" in Paris. Leg 2's battle-construction finding belongs here: the Siege of Kosel is built as a landscape with a battle in it, not a battle with a landscape behind it -- foreground, a stationary assembly of commanders in morning sun; middle distance, the town; background valley, the fighting, in shade. The mechanism is a three-band depth stack with light reserved for the observers and shade for the action, which lets a landscape painter paint a battle without abandoning his own compositional grammar. **Takeaway:** top-down legibility inside a perspectival scene; units as tokens on terrain.

## M5. The commanders-on-the-rise viewpoint
A two-plane battle composition -- a stationary assembly of commanders, lit by early morning sun on a rise in the foreground, looking out over the fighting in a shaded valley below, with the distant town beyond. The Siege of Kosel (1808): "a stationary assembly of commanders, illuminated by the early morning sun, looking out over the distant town and the fighting in the shaded valley below. Later works in the series recorded actual battle scenes." (Wikipedia, The Siege of Kosel). **Takeaway:** a fixed spectator group framing a distant moving battle -- the viewer watches watchers.

## M6. Portrait-in-battle
Battle pieces that contain many portraits of famous contemporaries -- identifiable individuals inside a mass scene. Stated in both Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia: "His battle pieces contain many portraits of famous contemporaries." **Takeaway:** a crowd scene where a few faces are specific, named, portrait-accurate -- the mass as a container for portraits.

## M7. Wide-sky Munich landscape with colourful figure groups
The distinctive Munich landscape formula -- a broad sky over a low Bavarian plain, animated by small, colourful groups of rural or bourgeois figures. Lenbachhaus: "Setting colorful groups of rural or bourgeois figures in scenes beneath wide skies, painters like Wilhelm von Kobell established a distinctive style of landscape painting associated with Munich." **Takeaway:** sky as the dominant field (two-thirds of the frame), figures as colour accents.

## M8. En plein air sketch, studio finish
Going out of the city to find motifs in the Bavarian pre-Alpine countryside and capturing impressions in sketches made outdoors, instead of composing ideal landscapes after Claude Lorrain or Jacob van Ruisdael. Lenbachhaus on the first Munich generation (Dillis, Kobell, Wagenbauer, Dorner the Younger, Warnberger): "The impressions of nature they captured in sketches created en plein air ushered in a new conception of the landscape free from the constraints of academic convention." Note: this passage is about the generation as a whole, not Kobell alone -- do not over-attribute. **Takeaway:** sketch-to-finish pipeline; a scene that starts as a quick outdoor note and ends as a minute, still, finished oil.

## M9. The encounter picture (Begegnungsbild)
A landscape organised around a meeting between people of different stations on a road or riverbank -- a mounted huntsman speaking to a peasant woman, with a view of Munich behind. A Huntsman and a Peasant Woman by the Isar River with a View of Munich (1823, Met, oil on wood, 25.1 x 20.3 cm) and Gentleman on Horseback and Country Girl on the Banks of the Isar near Munich (1831, 237 x 306 cm as given by a reproduction site -- reported-but-unverified and suspicious, see Disputes). CAUTION: the term "Begegnungsbilder" itself comes only from artsdot/wahooart search snippets, NOT from any end-to-end read; the works and their meeting structure are documented in the Met listing and search results. Leg 2's encounter finding belongs here: one mounted figure plus one or two standing figures plus one or two animals, placed on a road, riverbank or lakeshore, with a named place behind (Munich skyline, Tegernsee Abbey, Wallberg). The machine takes social rank as its input (mounted = higher, standing = lower) and produces a meeting as its output. It appears in print in 1791-1799 (W2, W6), in oil from 1802 (W8), and matures in 1823-1832 (W18, W20). It is repeatable: the same formula yields the 1829, 1832 and 1838 Tegernsee versions. **Takeaway:** two figures meeting, a social exchange frozen mid-gesture, with the place named behind them.

## M10. Serial commission structure: the 12-canvas cycles
History painting delivered as a numbered series -- 12 large canvases for one patron, a second cycle for another -- rather than single pictures. The Kosel series of 12; two full cycles (Berthier and Ludwig) reunited in 2014. **Takeaway:** a sequence that progresses from assembly to action -- "first canvas featured a stationary assembly... Later works in the series recorded actual battle scenes".

## M11. Engraving discipline under painting
A painter whose hand was formed first by engraving -- line control, tonal gradation, patience with small scale -- which then shows up as meticulous, minute detail in oil. Trained in engraving at the Zeichnungsakademie before his painting reputation; small-format oils (25 x 20 cm Huntsman; 28 x 26 cm Riders at the Tegernsee) carrying high detail; "minutely painted and magically still" Bavarian landscapes (Shepherd Gallery catalogue, search result only). **Takeaway:** fine line work and stillness as a deliberate register.

## M12. Seasonal serial landscape -- UNVERIFIED
The same five landscapes painted through the cycle of the four seasons, 12 paintings in total. This series is attested ONLY in Design+Encyclopedia entries (search results) -- a low-quality, repetitive source that also claims the pictures appear "on stamps, coins and currency" without evidence. No museum or scholarly source in this study confirms the series, and leg 2 found no corroboration; the number 12 also matches the 12-canvas battle commission, which suggests confusion. Treat as UNVERIFIED; do not build a prompt on it. **Takeaway:** none until verified -- one motif, four seasons, twelve variations, if the series exists at all.

## M13. Versioning as a working method
Kobell reworked a successful composition with small changes in staffage. The Tegernsee rider exists in at least three versions (Berlin 1832, private 1832, 1838); the Neureuth view exists in at least two (Munich 1833, c. 1832/33 private); the cattle-drive motif exists in at least two sheets (W9 and Wichmann no. 745). The changes are in staffage -- children and sheep against rider, woman and boy in the Neureuth pair -- while the viewpoint stays fixed. (Leg 2 finding L2-M5.) **Takeaway:** same viewpoint, different staffage as a prompt variable; a composition as a template with interchangeable figures.

## M14. Small-format finish: compression
The mature oils are 25-38 cm, oil on wood or panel, described by the Met as "small, jewel-like". The mechanism is compression: a battle canvas's figure count and a Dutch landscape's depth, fitted into a panel the size of a book. Detail is therefore the point, not an accessory: costume, dog breed and mountain silhouette are all legible at that size because they are painted as identifiers, not as texture. (Leg 2 finding L2-M6.) **Takeaway:** a wide world in a book-sized panel; every element must read as an identifier.

### W1. Genre Scenes (Genreszenen), 1787 and 1788
Two etchings with aquatint, each inscribed at the bottom; 18 x 15 cm each, reported-but-unverified (auction listing via search snippet; no museum or auction page read end to end). Sold at auction (Kunstauktionhaus Georg Rehm, July 2026 listing in search results); present location NOT VERIFIED. Kobell's earliest dated graphic work found in this study, made when he was 21-22; the scenes themselves are NOT VERIFIED beyond the title "Genre Scenes". It fixes the start of his printmaking career before the better-documented Met aquatints of 1791-1797, and shows he was already working in the etching-plus-aquatint combination that would become his signature graphic technique.

### W2. Landscape with Peasants Receiving Alms from a Horseman, 1791
Aquatint, after Nicolaes Berchem. Sheet 9 3/8 x 12 1/8 in. (23.8 x 30.8 cm), VERIFIED (Met page). Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Object Number 26.43.6. An aquatint translation of a Berchem composition: peasants receiving alms from a mounted horseman in a landscape. The earliest dated Met aquatint in this study, and direct evidence of the Dutch-model thread at its start -- Kobell is not copying Berchem's subject loosely, he is reproducing a named Dutch master's picture in aquatint, with the "after Berchem" credit carried on the museum record itself. The charity encounter between a rider and peasants on foot is also the social geometry -- mounted giver, standing receivers -- that his own later encounter pictures would reuse with Bavarian figures instead of Dutch ones.

### W3. Peasant Woman Nursing her Child beside Two Cows and Three Sheep, 1792
Aquatint, after Dirck van Bergen. Sheet 9 3/4 x 8 5/16 in. (24.8 x 21.1 cm), VERIFIED (Met page). Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Object Number 26.43.8. A peasant woman nursing her child, seated outdoors beside two cows and three sheep. It shows the aquatint-after-Dutch-master program applied to an intimate, unheroic subject -- no rider, no alms, no camp -- and because the animal count in the title (two cows, three sheep) shows how precisely Kobell's animal pieces were inventoried: the livestock are not background, they are the co-subjects, counted like figures. This is the documented instance behind the van Bergen influence claim (see D1): Kobell reproduced a van Bergen picture in aquatint in 1792.

### W4. Les Occupations du Soir au Camp (The Evening Occupations in Camp), 1794
Aquatint, after Philips Wouwerman. Sheet 13 7/16 x 17 in. (34.1 x 43.2 cm), VERIFIED (Met page). Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Object Number 26.43.5. An aquatint after Wouwerman showing evening occupations in a military camp. Wouwerman was the Dutch master Kobell was "especially attracted by" and copied most, so this is the central Dutch model in the series; and it is a camp scene -- soldiers at rest, horses, evening routines -- made fifteen years before Kobell visited Vienna and Paris to study battle pieces, so the military subject enters his work first as a borrowed Dutch image translated into aquatint, and only later as observed contemporary warfare. It is also the largest of the Met aquatints in this study, at 34.1 x 43.2 cm.

### W5. Landscape with Shepherds and Cattle, 1797
Aquatint, after Franz Kobell, his uncle. Sheet 10 1/16 x 13 1/4 in. (25.6 x 33.7 cm), VERIFIED (Met page). Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Object Number 26.43.9. Shepherds with cattle in a landscape, engraved in aquatint after a work by his uncle Franz Kobell rather than a Dutch master. It breaks the Dutch-only pattern of W2-W4 and shows the family thread: Wilhelm learned from his father Ferdinand and his uncle Franz, and here he translates a family member's landscape into print, treating Franz's composition with the same aquatint care he gave Berchem and Wouwerman. It is the last of the 1790s Met aquatint group in this study, closing the first print phase before the battle years.

### W6. Peasants from Tegernsee Meet a Company of Infantry from the Regiment of Hohenhausen, 1799
Date reported-but-unverified (Artsy past-auction listing in search results). Medium, dimensions and location NOT VERIFIED. Peasants from Tegernsee meet a company of infantry from the Hohenhausen regiment. It is the earliest dated Tegernsee subject found in this study, predating the famous Tegernsee riders of 1832-1833 by more than thirty years, and it already stages the encounter Kobell would repeat for the rest of his career: local civilians meeting uniformed men on a road, neither group dominating, the meeting itself the picture. The Tegernsee is therefore not a late discovery but a lifelong location, and the soldier-peasant encounter predates his formal battle-painting studies of 1809-1810.

### W7. Self-Portrait with Hat, c. 1800
Date reported-but-unverified (Wikipedia caption). Medium, dimensions and location NOT VERIFIED. A self-portrait of the artist as a young man in a hat, used as the lead image on his Wikipedia article. A second, fuller description from a print retailer (Gallerix, search snippet) describes a three-quarter view against a dark background, black top hat, dark coat with wide lapels, white cravat loosely knotted -- but that description is reported-but-unverified and may describe a different self-portrait impression. It matters as the visual anchor for Kobell's own face at about age 34, just before the battle-painting decade.

### W8. Figures on Horseback and Walking Meeting on a Promenade near Munich, 1802
Date reported-but-unverified (Artsy past-auction listing). Medium, dimensions and location NOT VERIFIED. Figures on horseback and figures walking meet on a promenade near Munich. The title is almost a definition of the encounter picture in its plainest form -- mounted figures and walking figures meeting on a named road near a named city -- and it dates that formula to 1802, two decades before the small jewel-like Isar encounter pictures of the 1820s. The promenade setting also shows the encounter moving out of the pastoral field and onto the city's edge.

### W9. Morning Drive of Cows at an Upper Bavarian Lake, 1803
Pencil, pen and watercolour on paper; 24 x 38 cm, reported-but-unverified. Private collection (acquired by the present owner's family at Karl and Faber, Munich, 2-3 October 1951, Lot 1622; earlier collection of Senator Dr. Gwinner, Frankfurt). A morning cattle drive at an Upper Bavarian lake, drawn in pencil, pen and watercolour, signed and dated lower right. It is a dated, signed work on paper from the year before the battle period, showing the working method behind the oils: a light, precise drawing medium used to fix a moving herd at a specific time of day (morning) and place (a named lake region). The catalogue raisonne cross-reference (Wichmann 1970, no. 745, a closely related "Shepherdess with cattle and goat before a wide river landscape") shows Kobell reworking the same cattle-drive motif across sheets.

### W10. The Siege of Kosel (Die Belagerung von Cosel), 1808
Oil on canvas; 202 x 305.5 cm, reported-but-unverified (stated on the Wikipedia page read end to end; no museum or auction page read end to end in this study states them). Neue Pinakothek, Munich. The first of a commissioned series of 12 large canvases recording the allies' victorious engagements in the Napoleonic Wars. The incident shown took place on 18 March 1806: Bavarian allies under Maximilian I Joseph of Bavaria, with Napoleon's youngest brother Jerome Bonaparte mopping up fortified Silesian towns, repulsing a Prussian sortie from Kosel. Unlike the later battle canvases, this first picture is a stationary assembly of commanders in early morning sun looking out over the distant town and the fighting in the shaded valley below. It is the hinge of Kobell's career: the moment a landscape and animal painter is scaled up, by royal commission, to history painting at over 2 by 3 metres, and its calm, observational construction shows his landscape eye surviving inside the battle genre. Patron uncertain within the source itself: "Maximilian (or otherwise Crown Prince Ludwig) later commissioned Kobell to produce a series of 12 large canvases". Spelling: "Kosel" (Wikipedia) against "Cosel" (Web Gallery of Art) for the same painting.

### W11. Group of Riders in a Stormy Tegernsee Landscape, 1808
Date reported-but-unverified (Artsy past-auction listing, Sotheby's 7 December 1993). Medium, dimensions and location NOT VERIFIED. A group of riders in a stormy landscape at the Tegernsee. It pairs the Tegernsee rider motif (see W6) with weather -- storm, not the calm sunshine of the later Tegernsee pictures -- in the same year as the Siege of Kosel, showing that in 1808 Kobell was working the same rider group in two opposite registers at once: the large, calm, commissioned battle canvas and the small, turbulent, private landscape.

### W12. The Third Day of the Battle of Hanau, 1808 (date as given; see Disputes)
Date 1808 as stated on Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia; this date conflicts with the battle itself, fought 30-31 October 1813. Treat the 1808 date as reported-but-unverified and probably wrong. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Neue Pinakothek, Munich (per Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia). A battle painting of the Battle of Hanau, in which Karl Philipp von Wrede's Austro-Bavarian corps tried to block Napoleon's retreat after Leipzig, and Napoleon broke through to the Rhine. The "Third Day" in the title is itself unexplained in the sources read: the battle lasted two days (30-31 October 1813). It is the single most-cited Kobell battle picture in the reference literature -- named in both the 1905 encyclopaedia and the modern Wikipedia article as the Neue Pinakothek's holding -- and the date conflict around it is a warning that the battle-cycle dates in secondary sources cannot be trusted without the Bavarian State Painting Collections catalogue.

### W13. The Surrender of Brieg, date NOT VERIFIED
No date found in the sources searched; the event depicted, the surrender of Brieg in Silesia, belongs to the 1806-1807 campaign. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Banquet Hall of the Konigsbau, Munich (per Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia; the Konigsbau is the Munich Residenz wing built for Ludwig I). The surrender of the Silesian fortress of Brieg, one of the large battle-cycle canvases in the Konigsbau Banquet Hall alongside the Cavalry Skirmish at Arnhofen and the Battles of Eckmuhl and Wagram. It matters as the ceremonial end of a siege rather than a combat scene -- a surrender is a formal, static, portrait-like event -- which fits Kobell's strength (assembled commanders, horses at rest, observed detail) better than a melee.

### W14. The Battle of Eckmuhl, date NOT VERIFIED (battle 21-22 April 1809)
Painting date NOT VERIFIED; the battle it depicts was fought 21-22 April 1809 in the War of the Fifth Coalition, when Napoleon, Davout and Lefebvre's Bavarians defeated Archduke Charles's Austrians at Eggmuhl in Bavaria. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Banquet Hall of the Konigsbau, Munich. A large battle canvas of Eckmuhl, the turning point of the 1809 campaign, in which Bavarian troops under Vandamme stormed the bridge at Eckmuhl and took the town's chateau. It is the most Bavarian of the cycle's battles -- Bavarian troops in the vanguard, a Bavarian victory claimed as such -- and the bridge-storming subject is a problem of composition Kobell had to solve as a landscape painter: a narrow crossing point, two banks, and a mass of men funnelling through it, a landscape problem (a river, a bridge, a road) as much as a battle problem. Its companion in the same hall, the Battle of Wagram (5-6 July 1809), is listed in Beyond the 20.

### W15. Cattle Market before a Large City on a Lake, 1820
Oil (medium as given by reproduction sources; support NOT VERIFIED). Dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Albertina, Vienna, inventory number 14766, reported-but-unverified (from a reproduction-site record). A lively cattle market in the foreground, with people, cows, horses and a dog, set against a sprawling city rising beside a vast lake. It is the grandest statement of the animal piece as a public scene: the market gathers the livestock, the traders, the horses and the city into one wide composition, and it is the work Wikipedia's editors chose to lead Kobell's selected paintings. It also shows the scale shift after the battle years -- back to civilian life, but keeping the battle canvas's ambition to fill a wide field with many figures and animals, each individually observed.

### W16. A Country Lane, 1821
Medium, dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Hamburger Kunsthalle, Hamburg (per Wikipedia selected paintings list). A country lane -- no further subject description was found in the sources searched in this study, and the Hamburger Kunsthalle object page was not read end to end. It matters despite the thin record because its museum placement is specific and checkable (Hamburger Kunsthalle, 1821), and because the title marks the lane itself -- the road as a place, not a route between places -- as the subject, the purest form of Kobell's mature landscape idea: a narrow, walkable strip of ground with figures on it, the whole picture built around a path receding into distance.

### W17. Self-Portrait of the Artist as a Hunter on his Estate at Tegernsee, 1821
Oil on paper, laid on card; 23.6 x 31.5 cm, reported-but-unverified. Private collection, Saxony/Rhineland (provenance: Amalie Emma von Passavant-Gontard, Frankfurt, to 1931; Hugo Helbing, Frankfurt, 1-3 December 1931; Ludwigs Galerie, Munich, 1931 to at least 1932); exhibited Berlin 1932, "Deutsche Kunst im Zeitalter Goethes", Galerie Paul Cassirer, cat. no. 123. Monogrammed and dated "WK 1821" lower right; an old label on the verso in brown ink reads "Wilhelm Kobell as a hunter on his estate at Tegernsee, by himself". It is self-mythology with an address: the Tegernsee was not just a motif, it was his property, and the hunter's role -- the mounted or walking gentleman with dogs and a gun -- is the role he gives himself in the picture, the same role his huntsmen play in the Isar encounter pictures.

### W18. A Huntsman and a Peasant Woman by the Isar River with a View of Munich, 1823
Oil on wood; 9 7/8 x 8 in. (25.1 x 20.3 cm), VERIFIED (Met page). Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Object Number 2019.141.15; Bequest of Mrs. Charles Wrightsman, 2019. The Met's own text calls it a small, jewel-like encounter picture: a hunter and his dog (a Riesenbracke) appear alongside a small boy and a young peasant woman in the traditional costume of the region around Munich, with a sweeping view across the banks of the Isar toward the city's skyline behind them. Kobell made a companion painting to this one, now in the Cleveland Museum of Art. It is the clearest surviving definition of the mature Kobell system in one small panel: two social ranks meeting on a riverbank, a named dog breed, a named regional costume, a real city skyline used as a backdrop, and a companion-piece structure that turns a single encounter into a pair. Reproduction sites that call it oil on canvas, 25 x 20 cm, are wrong on the support; the Met's oil on wood stands.

### W19. A Horseman and Two Peasant Girls, 1829
Oil on canvas; 27.5 x 30.2 cm, reported-but-unverified (stated on the Google Arts and Culture page read end to end; Google Arts and Culture is an aggregator, not the holding museum's object page). Kunsthalle Karlsruhe. A horseman with two peasant girls, a landscape painting on canvas; no fuller description was given on the page read (the English description was listed as "will follow shortly"). It is the 1829 link between the 1823 Isar huntsman and the 1832 Tegernsee riders: the same encounter formula -- one mounted man, two standing country girls -- restated six years later, slightly larger and squarer in format (27.5 x 30.2 cm, nearly square), showing the formula had become a repeatable, collectible type rather than a one-off invention. Note: Google Arts and Culture spells the creator "Wilhelm von Korbell", a transcription error; the work is Kobell's.

### W20. Riders before the Tegernsee (Reiter am Tegernsee), 1832
Oil on panel; 28 by 25.6 cm (11 by 10 in.), VERIFIED (Sotheby's page, for the private-collection version, Wichmann no. 1505). Private collection (Fritz Nathan, Zurich, by 1970; thence by descent; sold Sotheby's London, 12 December 2018, Lot 1). A second version of the same title and year is in the Alte Nationalgalerie, Berlin (Wichmann no. 1504); a third version dated 1838 was sold at Christie's London, 20 May 1993. Signed and dated "Wilhelm. Kobell 1832" lower right. The view is south across Lake Tegernsee in Bavaria; the long building in the distance on the left is Tegernsee Abbey, dominated by the distinctive silhouette of the Wallberg mountain beyond (Sotheby's catalogue note). In the foreground a man on a white horse in a yellow coat sits facing forward, with two horses standing by, dogs at his feet, and a sandy path leading toward the distant shore. It is the most fully documented small Kobell in this study -- signed, dated, catalogued (Wichmann no. 1505), exhibited three times (Munich 1966, Jegenstorf 1970, Zurich 1973), with a named viewpoint and a named mountain -- and the existence of three versions proves the Tegernsee rider was a deliberate, repeatable composition, not a single sketch. The three versions must not be conflated into one dimension: WahooArt gives the Berlin version as oil on canvas, 28 x 26 cm, reported-but-unverified and a different support; the Christie's 1838 version is oil on panel, 27.94 x 25.4 cm, reported-but-unverified.

## Evolution

### Phase 1 - Training and Dutch copying: c. 1787-1802
Small etchings and aquatints, rural genre, animal pieces. Works: W1 (1787-88), W2-W5 (1791-1797), W6 (1799), W7-W8 (c. 1800-1802). Subjects are borrowed (Berchem, Wouwerman, van Bergen, his uncle Franz) or local and small (Tegernsee peasants, a Munich promenade). Scale is small: sheets of about 18-34 cm. Technique is print-first: etching plus aquatint, the aquatint giving tonal wash to a line image. Palette in the prints is monochrome by definition; in the watercolours (W9, just after this phase) it is light and precise. What changes inside the phase: the "after" credit moves from Dutch masters to a family member (Franz Kobell, W5), and the encounter motif (rider meets peasants, W2 and W6) appears in print before it appears in oil.

### Phase 2 - Battle years: c. 1803-1815 (commissioned, large, public)
The morning cattle drive of 1803 (W9) is the last small civilian work before the scale jump. Then the royal commission: 12 large canvases for Maximilian I Joseph / Crown Prince Ludwig, beginning with the Siege of Kosel (W10, 1808, over 2 by 3 metres), plus the Konigsbau cycle (W13-W14 and Wagram, Arnhofen). Studies for the battle pieces were made in Vienna in 1809 and Paris in 1810. Subjects change completely: sieges, surrenders, bridge-stormings, named commanders, many portraits of famous contemporaries inserted into the ranks. Scale changes by an order of magnitude: from 25 cm sheets to 305 cm canvases. Technique changes from aquatint to large oil, built as landscape first (commanders watching from a height, W10) with fighting in the valley below. What stays constant: the landscape eye -- the battle is staged as a view, with a foreground group, a middle distance, and a named town or river -- and the horse, which moves from pastoral animal to the centre of the composition. The small rider pictures do not stop during this phase (W11, stormy Tegernsee riders, 1808), so the two registers run in parallel.

### Phase 3 - Mature encounter pictures: c. 1818-1833
Return to civilian subjects, but with the battle canvas's ambition compressed into small panels. Works: W15 (1820), W16-W17 (1821), W18 (1823), W19 (1829), W20 (1832), and the 1833 Neureuth view in Beyond the 20. Subjects are meetings on named ground: the Isar with Munich behind (W18), the Tegernsee with the Abbey and the Wallberg behind (W20), a country lane (W16), a cattle market before a lakeside city (W15). Scale settles small and jewel-like: panels of about 25-38 cm, oil on wood or panel, a few on paper laid on card. Palette, as far as the texts allow (no painting was inspected at usable resolution in this study), is described in secondary sources as clear, sunlit and precise, with local colour in costume (yellow coat, green hunting jacket, white bonnet) used as the accent against a blue-green landscape. Technique is now oil, small and finished, with the aquatint habit surviving as a way of seeing: tonal gradation, fine detail, and a clean separation of foreground group from distant view. What changes from Phase 2: the crowd thins to two or three figures plus animals; the event is a meeting, not a battle; the viewpoint is low and near, not a commander's height. What stays constant from Phase 1: the Dutch composition (low horizon, a road or riverbank leading in, a group at the side), the counted animals, and the Tegernsee/Isar geography.

### Phase 4 - Late repetition and return: c. 1833-1852
Evidence is thinner here, because the late works in this study are mostly auction listings. The pattern is repetition of the Phase 3 motifs in new versions: a second and third Riders at the Tegernsee (1832 private version, 1838 version), the Neureuth view (1833), the young shepherdess before Tegernsee Monastery (1838), Soldiers in Winter (1848), and the last rider at Emming on the Ammersee (c. 1852, Wichmann no. 1691). Subjects do not change -- the same lakes, the same rider, the same shepherdess. Scale stays small (15-36 cm in the dated late works found). What changes: the new motif is winter (Soldiers in Winter, 1848; Stagecoach in Winter, undated), and the rider pictures become more private and less topographical -- the 1852 Emming rider is a single man, a white horse and a dog before a pond-house, with no city or abbey behind. What stays constant to the end: the horse as the centre of the picture, the small format, and the Tegernsee/Isar/Ammersee lake country as the only landscape that matters.

### The Dutch-model thread
Who he copied or etched after, and when: Nicolaes Berchem (W2, 1791); Dirck van Bergen (W3, 1792); Philips Wouwerman (W4, 1794 -- the central model, "especially attracted by Wouverman" per the sources); Franz Kobell, his uncle (W5, 1797 -- the family exception); Thomas Wijck (La Caravane en Repos, 1801); Willem Romeyn (The Shepherd's Rest, 1819 -- a late return to the program, after a picture in the Bavarian King's collection). The canonical list in the reference literature adds Roos and Ruysdael (Ruysdael = Jacob van Ruisdael); no dated Kobell aquatint after Roos or Ruisdael was found with a verifiable date in this study, so their place in the sequence is NOT VERIFIED here. When it runs: the dated aquatints cluster 1791-1797, with a tail in 1801 and a single late return in 1819. The program therefore belongs to Phase 1, not to the mature oil years: Kobell learned the Dutch landscape by translating it into aquatint, then stopped translating and started composing his own Bavarian versions of the same scenes. How it shows: the "after" credit is carried on the museum record; the Dutch composition (low horizon, side group, receding road or water) survives into the encounter pictures; the animal counts and the rider-peasant encounter are Dutch subjects restaged in Bavarian costume.

## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

- Death year 1853 vs 1855. For 1853: EN Wikipedia ("6 April 1766 - 15 July 1853"), Wikidata (15 July 1853), Met object records ("Mannheim 1766-1853 Munich"), Shepherd Gallery catalogue. For 1855: New International Encyclopaedia 1905 ("Wilhelm von Kobell (1766-1855)"), Invaluable ("1766 Mannheim - 1855 Munchen"), Artprice, isitaholidaytoday ("passed away on July 15, 1855"). Both sides give the same day and month (15 July). UNRESOLVED.
- Professor date, four versions. 1808: EN Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia ("became professor at the Academy in 1808"). 1813: Ketterer Kunst biography of Dillis ("He was therefore replaced by Wilhelm von Kobell in 1813"), search result only. 1814: Lenbachhaus ("succeeded by Kobell in 1814") and Invaluable ("1814-1826 war Kobell als Nachfolger von Johann Georg von Dillis Professor der Landschaftsmalerei"), search result only for Invaluable. 1815: isitaholidaytoday timeline ("1815 Becomes Professor in Munich"), search result only, low quality. The Lenbachhaus version is the most circumstantial: Dillis first held the chair (Academy founded 1808), Kobell succeeded him 1814, chair abolished 1826. The 1808 version likely confuses the Academy's founding and Dillis's appointment with Kobell's. UNRESOLVED.
- Rome 1778, age 12. Wikipedia and the 1905 encyclopaedia both say "In 1778, he went to Rome". Born 6 April 1766, he would have been 12. A solo study trip at 12 is implausible; the trip may have been with his father, or the year may be a repeated error (1788 would make him 22). No source read explains the circumstances. FLAG -- do not state the Rome trip as a solo study journey.
- Nobility dates. Invaluable snippet (search result only) says in one place "1833 erlangte er den personlichen und 1837 schliesslich den erblichen Adel" and in its artist-info line "1815 Erhebung in den personlichen und 1833 in den erblichen Adelsstand". Personal nobility is given as both 1815 and 1833, hereditary as both 1833 and 1837. UNRESOLVED.
- Van Bergen influence, single-source claim, now with one documented instance. ## Aesthetic logic

Kobell's aesthetic is the logic of the faithful copyist who never stops copying but changes what he copies from. He begins by translating other men's pictures into aquatint -- Berchem, Wouwerman, van Bergen, his uncle Franz -- and the translation is the training: the hand learns the Dutch composition (low horizon, side group, receding road or water) by rebuilding it in tone. Then the translation stops and the composition stays: the Bavarian encounter pictures are Dutch pictures with Bavarian figures, Bavarian costume, Bavarian lakes. The battle years are the same logic at the largest scale: a landscape painter is asked to paint war, and he paints it as a landscape with a battle in it -- commanders watching from a height, fighting in the valley below, the town beyond. Scale is the genre switch: 25 cm panels for peace, 305 cm canvases for war, and the small panels after the war keep the battle canvas's ambition compressed into a book-sized frame. The horse is the unit throughout -- in the aquatint, in the camp, in the battle, on the road, at the watering place -- and the rider's seat is the variable he tested in chalk before he painted it. The sky does the emotional work: two-thirds of the frame in the Munich landscapes, wide and high over a low plain, with small colourful figure groups as accents. Nothing in Kobell is sublime in the Friedrich sense; everything is observed, counted and placed -- two cows and three sheep, a named dog breed, a named mountain, a named abbey. The picture is a report before it is a commemoration, and the report is built from identifiers.

## Positions

P1. Kobell is a copyist who became an originator: his whole method starts in imitation (copying Wouverman in the gallery, reproducing each Dutch master's style in aquatint) and only later hardens into a Munich manner of his own. The copying is the technique, not a phase to outgrow.
P2. His Dutchness is a lifelong operating system, not an early influence: "throughout his life Kobell traveled to England, France and Italy but ultimately based his style on Dutch art" (Wikipedia). Travel is intake; Dutch art is the filter.
P3. He is a painter of the Bavarian plain, not of the sublime Alps: wide skies, low horizons, riverbanks (Isar, Tegernsee), cattle markets, country lanes. The Lenbachhaus frames him as a founder of a specifically Munich landscape, not a German Romantic in the Friedrich sense.
P4. His battle painting is anti-theatrical by design: he went to Paris in 1810, saw the theatrical battle scenes in vogue there, and evaded them -- choosing the chessboard/map reading over the heroic close-up (Web Gallery of Art).
P5. His battles are reports before they are commemorations: the 2014 Neue Pinakothek framing says the 19th-century intention was to combine glorification with "the character of realistic reports", and that "this ambitious endeavour and reality soon went their separate ways" (artdaily). Kobell sits exactly on that fault line.
P6. The small picture is his natural scale for peace, the huge canvas for war: cabinet oils of 25 x 20 cm against 202 x 305.5 cm battle canvases. Scale itself marks the genre switch.
P7. The horse is his unit of composition: in battle, in the hunt, at the watering place, on the road -- groups of horses are placed like figures on a chessboard, and the landscape is arranged around them.
P8. He is a court artist in the full sense: stipend from the Elector (500 florins, 1792-1799), court painter at Mannheim, then Munich, then professor, then ennobled -- a career built inside patronage, with the battle cycles as state commissions for two rival patrons (Berthier and Ludwig).
P9. His fame inverted over time: in his lifetime he was "most widely known through his etchings and aquatint engravings" (1905 encyclopaedia); today he is remembered, if at all, for the big battle canvases and the Munich landscapes. The prints were the reputation; the oils are the afterlife.
P10. He is a hinge figure between the 18th-century gallery copyist and the 19th-century plein-air painter: trained on old masters in the gallery, he belongs to the first Munich generation that went out to sketch nature directly -- but his finish stays minute, still and detailed rather than sketchy.
