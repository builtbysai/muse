# Antonio Asis: Study Notes

**Site:** Antonio Asis, 1932 - 30 March 2019 -- Argentine kinetic and Op artist, born in Buenos Aires, living in Paris from the spring of 1956 until his death, 63 years; his dealer Sicardi Gallery calls him one of the leading figures of kinetic art and Op Art in the second half of the twentieth century, above all for perforated metal plates and grilles laid over painted geometric surfaces that seem to change and move as the viewer changes position (C8001). He began with hand-drawn geometric abstraction in Buenos Aires, arrived in Paris at twenty-four and produced his first vibrating relief within months, and then spent sixty years running the same few systems -- the grille, the overlapping rings, the colour grid, the floating disc, the spring-mounted object -- with exceptional constancy, abandoning stretched canvas for paper, cardboard and wood, and drawing with compass and ruling pen until his death (C8007). With Hugo Demarco and Horacio Garcia Rossi he co-founded Groupe Position in 1971, the short-lived group of Argentine artists in Paris dedicated to optical-kinetic art. He was never a member of GRAV and never had a solo show at the Galerie Denise Rene, though he moved in that circle for the whole of his Paris life.

## The life: the plate that shivers

Antonio Asis was born in Buenos Aires, Argentina, in 1932, and died in Paris on 30 March 2019 (C8003). His dealer, the Sicardi Gallery in Houston, announced his death on 30 March 2019 and says it worked with him for the twelve years before that date (C8003). No source consulted gives an exact birth date, only the year 1932, so no birth day or month is stated here. He is remembered as one of the leading figures of kinetic art and Op Art in the second half of the twentieth century, above all for perforated metal plates and grilles laid over painted geometric surfaces that seem to change and move as the viewer changes position (C8001, C8006). He came from one of the many Lebanese families that settled in Argentina (C8009, C8010), and he later became a French citizen -- the citizenship is stated by the Freijo Gallery alone and is flagged below (C8009).

Asis began studying art at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires at the age of fourteen, which puts the start of his training in 1946 (C8001, C8003). The English Wikipedia dates his attendance there from 1946 to 1950 (C8002), and a Buenos Aires gallery page found in search agrees on 1946 to 1950 (C8012). He then continued at the Escuela de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon, where he studied composition with Hector Cartier, who encouraged him to explore the principles of design as a foundation for his work (C8001). Cartier built the Vision chair on the new parameters of Gestalt theory, and Asis took from him the key concept of the relativity of color and of the gaze, which runs through all his later work (C8004). The Poirier essay on Artsy adds that Cartier introduced Asis to the geometric art of Constructivism and the Bauhaus, to the perceptual logic of Gestalt theory, and to kineticism as revealed in the 1955 exhibition "Le Mouvement" at Galerie Denise Rene in Paris (C8007).

Throughout the 1940s Asis explored abstraction and non-representational art, and with the publication of Arturo magazine in 1944 and the creation of the Asociacion Arte Concreto-Invencion (AACI), Buenos Aires was an important site for post-war abstraction, and Asis was an active member of this creative community (C8001, C8003). The Geometricae obituary places his formation in the same years and names the groups that followed Arturo, MADI and the Movimiento Arte Concreto Invencion (C8004). The MUNTREF retrospective catalogue says he graduated from the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes and attended from his student years the emergence and polemics of the concrete art movement in Argentina (C8010). No source consulted names a specific AACI exhibition, work, or office held by Asis, so "active member of this creative community" is the full extent of what can be said; do not upgrade it to a founding role or a named AACI show. The same AACI is the root Vardanega joined in 1946 (C8109), so the two men share a Buenos Aires institutional root a decade before either reached Paris, though no source puts them in the same AACI room at the same time.

In the spring of 1956 Asis moved to Paris at the age of twenty-four, and he lived and worked there for the remaining sixty-three years of his life (C8001, C8003). Soon after arriving he met and married Lydwine D'Andigne, established his studio, and worked in Paris until shortly before his death (C8003, C8004). The Geometricae obituary says he met Lydwine very soon after arriving in Paris in 1956 and remained with her for the rest of his life, that his early Paris life was hard and he painted walls to eat, and that his studio was on the Rue Saint-Jacques (C8004). No source consulted gives a marriage date.

In Paris he soon became part of an international circuit of kinetic artists, and the Sicardi biography says: "Among others, he befriended Yaacov Agam, Nicolas Schoffer, Jesus Rafael Soto, Jean Tinguely, and Victor Vasarely" (C8001). The Freijo Gallery repeats the same five names in the same order (C8009). The evidence is not equal for all five, and the differences matter. Soto is the strongest case: the RGR Art biography says Asis worked closely with Soto and Vasarely in Paris (C8005), and Geometricae says Soto encouraged Asis to come to Carboneras, Spain, in 1964, that Asis spent a few days there, fell in love with the town, and soon after bought a house in Los Cocones, keeping a lasting bond with the place and spending summers there with Lydwine for years (C8004). The English Wikipedia says he befriended the Denise Rene group of artists "such as Jesus Rafael Soto, Pol Bury, and Yaacov Agam" on arrival in Paris (C8002). JCMC adds an independent leg for Soto: "Influenced by Jesus Soto, he produced his first vibrant relief, Lineaire (1956)" (C8102). Agam is well attested as a named associate: he appears in the Sicardi list (C8001), the Freijo list (C8009), the ARTnews obituary, which names Agam, Schoffer, and Soto as the kinetic artists Asis befriended after moving to Paris (C8006), the Geometricae account, which says Asis later associated with the Galerie Denise Rene and with artists including Soto, Vasarely, and Agam (C8004), and the English Wikipedia (C8002) -- but no source consulted records a specific joint exhibition or project between Asis and Agam. Vasarely is attested as a named associate but thin: he appears in the Sicardi list (C8001), the Freijo list (C8009), and the Geometricae account (C8004), and RGR Art says Asis worked closely with Soto and Vasarely (C8005), but no source records a specific meeting, joint show, or project between Asis and Vasarely. Schoffer is attested by three sources but only as a name: Sicardi (C8001), Freijo (C8009), and ARTnews (C8006) all name him, but none records a meeting, a joint show, or a project, and he is not named in the Geometricae account (C8004) or the English Wikipedia (C8002). Tinguely is the weakest of the five: he appears only in the Sicardi list (C8001) and in the Freijo text, which repeats the Sicardi wording (C8009); no other source consulted names Tinguely in connection with Asis at all. Treat the Asis-Tinguely friendship as unattested beyond a single dealer biography copied once, and say "associated with the Denise Rene circle that included Tinguely," not "befriended Tinguely."

The Denise Rene association itself is asserted rather than documented in detail. Geometricae says Asis "later associated himself with the Galerie Denise Rene" (C8004). The RGR Art exhibition list records one Asis appearance in a Denise Rene context: "Von Konstruktivism zur Kinetik, Galerie Denise Rene / Hans Mayer, Krefeld, Germany" in 1967 (C8005). No source consulted records a solo exhibition by Asis at the Galerie Denise Rene, so do not claim one. The gallery's roster at the time did include Schoffer, Agam, Tinguely, Soto, and Vasarely (C8011), which is the concrete basis for the shared-circle claim.

His signature work is the perforated metal plate superimposed over a painted surface. The combination of the metal grille and the geometric compositions of circles and squares creates a surface that seems to change and move as the viewer changes position (C8001). Geometricae describes the same mechanism in two lines of work: the "interferencias," reflections and visual effects on photosensitive paper, and the "esculturas vibratorias," in which a grille or grid is superimposed on a surface painted with geometric patterns, creating a sensation of visual movement as the spectator walks past the piece (C8004). The MUNTREF catalogue says the same thing in plainer terms: a few seconds of moving past the work are enough to notice the vibrations produced by the superposition of the painted weave and the metal grilles placed between the painting and the viewer's eye (C8010). The English Wikipedia names the two signature forms as the "Interferences concentriques" paintings and the "Vibrations" sculptures with a metal grille (C8002). The Poirier essay adds that the concentric-circle targets, alone or arranged in a grid, are both visual and anti-visual, and that in the Interferences the concentric rings overlap like the waves produced by raindrops falling on water; it also says the key-form made of fine colored concentric circles was produced by Asis from 1968 until his death (C8007). A Christie's lot found in search, an untitled oil on wood with metal screen executed in 1972 and sold for $25,000 in 2007, is a dated object that shows the grille construction in use in the early 1970s (C8013).

RGR Art adds a less-known side of the work: among his most innovative contributions were tactile fixtures of objects, usually spheres, fixed on the canvas, oriented toward interaction, producing types of movement that did not presuppose an equivalence between sight and perception but alluded to a wider conception of kinetic art that included the sense of touch (C8005). The Poirier essay frames the whole practice as programmed art: between 1956 and 1960 Asis developed a singular system that radically excluded easel painting, abandoning stretched canvas for modest supports such as paper, cardboard, or sometimes wood, suited to the high precision of line drawings made with compass or ruling pen, and he applied the method with exceptional constancy until his death in 2019 (C8007).

In 1971 Asis co-founded the artist group Groupe Position, which included Armando Durante, Hugo Demarco, and Horacio Garcia Rossi, all Argentine artists living in Paris and all interested in movement and the vibrations of light (C8001, C8002). RGR Art confirms he became a founding member of Groupe Position in 1971, describing it as a group of Argentine artists living in Paris offering different approaches to kinetic problems, from mechanical and electrical intervention to the pure development of color vibrations, with Asis midway with his sculptural lattices (C8005). ARTnews says he cofounded Position, a group of Paris-based Argentine artists, in 1971 (C8006). The fourth co-founder is Leopoldo Torres Aguero: JCMC gives the roster as "Leopoldo Torres Aguero, Armando Durante, Hugo Demarco and Horacio Garcia-Rossi" (C8102), and Arte al Dia says the group was "created in April, 1971, and was dissolved at the end of 1972" and showed in "Brussels, Paris, Brescia, Madrid, Seville, Bilbao, Bergamo and Zurich" (C8111). The group was therefore short-lived, about twenty months, but genuinely exhibition-active, not a paper grouping; it organized a show at the Gallery Les Ambassadeurs in Zurich in 1972 (C8102). The two names the brief asked for, Hugo Demarco and Horacio Garcia Rossi, are confirmed by both Sicardi and Freijo (C8001, C8009). The roster spelling is handled in the Corrections section: Sicardi and Demarco's article print "Carlos Aguero," which this study treats as a garble of Leopoldo Torres Aguero (C8151).

## M1 -- The perforated plate: a grille over paint, the viewer is the motor

The work that defines him is not a painting and not a sculpture but a sandwich. A metal plate pierced with holes or slots is mounted at a fixed stand-off in front of a painted geometric composition of bands, circles or squares (C8053). The plate is fixed; the painting is fixed; what moves is the viewer, walking past. At each viewpoint a different set of painted marks shows through a different set of holes, so the pattern appears to shift and shiver (C8001). JCMC describes the first statement, Lineaire of 1956, as the start of "a series of analogous works constructed from perforated metallic plates, superimposed on geometric compositions of bands, circles or squares that, when the observer moved, became powerfully active and created an endless number of optical and kinetic effects" (C8102). Fifty years later the same construction is still his mechanism: Vibrations sur carres bleus et noire No. 2179 of 2005 is acrylic on wood with a metal grid, 66 x 66 x 16 cm, so the stand-off is real depth, not a surface effect (C8070). The clearest view of the hardware is the unnumbered Vibration 4 cercles noir et blanc: a black slatted grille of vertical bars over four large black-and-white circles, so the circles are visibly sliced into strips by the bars. The unnumbered Vibrations sur carres No. 1590 shows the perforated variant: a black plate drilled with a dense grid of round holes, with darker concentric zones emerging from the hole pattern itself. The mechanism needs no motor, no bulb, no program: the stand-off depth plus the viewer's own walking does all the work, and the work is dead -- a flat painted panel behind a flat pierced sheet -- until a body passes in front of it.

**Takeaway:** the plate is the whole system in one object. A piece that uses Asis should build a real layered surface -- paint behind, pierced plate in front, a genuine gap between them -- and let the viewer's own movement do the shivering. If the pattern moves without the viewer moving, the mechanism has been replaced, not used.

## M2 -- Overlapping rings: ripples from many centres at once

The flat counterpart to the grille is the interference painting. Two or more systems of concentric rings, drawn in flat black and white and later in colour, have their centres scattered so the systems overlap (C8054, C8068). Nothing in the object moves. The eye cannot settle on one centre, because every ring belongs to two systems at once; at each crossing a new lens or petal shape appears, and the surface seems to expand and contract like ripples. Esquisse noire of 1958 shows it already formed: broken white rings on black, their centres scattered across a wide strip so the rings collide and overlap (C8054). Interferences en noir et blanc of 1972 shows it matured: full black-and-white ring systems filling the whole surface, many centres of different sizes crowded edge to edge, the rings cutting across each other so that lens and petal shapes appear at every crossing (C8068). Christie's describes the polychrome version as "circular waves radiating from multiple centers" that "give the impression of simultaneous, concurrent geneses", each epicentre "struggl[ing] against its neighbors". The Poirier essay says the concentric rings overlap like the waves produced by raindrops falling on water (C8007). The unnumbered JCMC image shows the polychrome form: black-and-white and blue-and-white ring systems colliding across a wide field.

**Takeaway:** the ripple is the mechanism, not the decoration. A piece should start from two or more ring centres and let the crossings make the shapes; a single ring system is a target, not an interference, and a drawn wave is not a wave made by overlapping centres.

## M3 -- The colour grid: strict squares that flicker

A grid of small flat-tint squares, each colour placed by rule, with stronger colours forming vertical and horizontal bands (C8055, C8056, C8059, C8060, C8069). Nothing moves. Adjacent hues produce simultaneous contrast, and the retina generates afterimages at the boundaries, so the flat grid seems to flicker and oscillate. Carres rythmiques of 1960 is the earliest dated work in the strand: the JCMC page states Asis's first Espaces couleurs carres date from 1959, and that "Labyrinthe polychrome and Carres rythmiques belong to this series", a plane of small coloured squares ordered by strict rules whose surface "sparkles and vibrates" (C8055). The quiet pastel form is the untitled gouache of 1960, a dense grid of roughly 30 by 30 small squares in pale blue, green, pink and lavender, with stronger yellow squares scattered through it at irregular intervals (C8056). The saturated form is the two Chromatisme quadrille polychrome gouaches of 1964 and 1965, dense grids in red, orange, blue, teal, magenta, black and grey, with vertical and horizontal bands of stronger colour running through the field (C8059, C8060). The Drawing Room calls the rhythm "precise and syncopated", oscillating across the ground. Twenty years on, the Labyrinthe Polychrome of 1985 shows the same system with larger cells and a softer palette, yellow, pale blue, pink, grey-green and lilac on a white board with a wide white margin (C8069). The labyrinth is the grid itself; there is no path drawn, only the eye's path across the cells.

**Takeaway:** the grid must be strict before it can shimmer. A piece should place its colours by rule, let the bands be the rhythm and the rest the noise it plays against, and trust the retina to do the flickering; a random scatter of bright squares is a mosaic, not a vibration.

## M4 -- Black and white in progression: the figure-ground flip

The colourless extreme of the grid is a single work, the untitled gouache of 1962 from the series Cercles noirs et blanc en progression: a strict 13 by 13 grid of alternating black and white discs, each disc sitting on a black square, so the white discs read as holes and the black discs as solid (C8058). Nothing moves. The eye flips between reading black as the figure and white as the figure, because the two are exactly balanced; the discs seem to advance toward the viewer and fall back in turn. The series title says the mechanism: "Cercles noirs et blanc en progression" -- black and white circles in progression, the progression being the alternation itself. There is no colour, no overlap, no variation except the alternation. It is the purest case in this study of a system with one rule and one effect, and it proves the vibration does not need colour at all.

**Takeaway:** balance is the mechanism. A piece should hold black and white in exact equilibrium and let the eye do the flipping; the moment one colour dominates, the flip stops and the grid goes flat.

## M5 -- Floating discs: depth made of size alone

Isolated discs and rings of varied size, ring thickness and saturation, scattered on an empty white ground with no horizon and no overlap (C8057, C8061, C8062). Nothing moves. The eye assigns depth by size and saturation alone -- large and saturated reads near, small and pale reads far -- so flat discs seem suspended at different depths in mid-air. Geometria Libre of 1962 shows it in full colour: about a hundred discs and rings of many sizes scattered without a grid, solid discs and hollow rings in green, purple, blue, yellow, pink and black, some concentric, some single, overlapping nothing (C8057). Geometria Libre of 1966 shows the same scattered-disc format four years later, now almost monochrome: discs and rings in blues, greys and black on white, with only the faintest pale blue wash (C8061). The near-monochrome version proves the depth effect does not need colour: size, ring thickness and tonal value alone separate near discs from far ones. Geometria libre (4508) of 1967 is described in prose as concentric circles and colour spheres "floating as if in mid-air among broad expanses of white" (C8062). The Drawing Room's 2015 release adds the mechanism's late form: rings of "dilating electric colors" that "swell and narrow within the tension of the square field".

**Takeaway:** the void is part of the work. A piece should give its discs empty white to float in, with no grid and no overlap to anchor them; crowd the field and the depth collapses into pattern.

## M6 -- The spring-mounted object: touch as the motor

A solid object -- a cube, a spiral, a cork ball -- mounted on a spring, sometimes in front of a mirror or a geometric painting that multiplies its movement (C8064, C8065). The object is fixed until a hand displaces it; the spring returns it with a damped oscillation. The eye watches a composition that is re-made by each touch and never repeats exactly. Cube No. 1 of 1969 is a glossy red cube, 9 7/16 inches on a side, aluminum, wood and springs, photographed against black with a small metal spring coil just visible under its base (C8064). Cube No. 5 of 1969 is the same construction scaled up to furniture size, 23 5/8 inches on a side, a large plain aluminium cube with a spring visible under its lower left corner (C8065). In a still photograph both look like plain blank boxes; the mechanism is almost invisible, which is the point Sicardi's text makes: "His series of Cubes, made with aluminum, wood, and springs, invite interaction: the slightest touch animates them." JCMC describes the earlier series -- Spirales, Reflexes and Boules tactiles -- as metallic spirals, springs or cork spheres that "vibrated, responding to the viewer's manipulation, thus generating a composition of movable elements", with movements "multiplied in some cases by positioning mirrors or geometric paintings behind them". The unnumbered Asistype 16 shows the form: a white cork ball mounted at the centre of concentric coloured rings. The unnumbered Boules tactiles sur fond rouge shows 25 white balls on springs in five rows on a red panel, the spring coils visible at the left of each row. RGR Art frames this as the wider conception of kinetic art: tactile fixtures oriented toward interaction, producing types of movement that did not presuppose an equivalence between sight and perception but alluded to a wider conception that included the sense of touch (C8005).

**Takeaway:** the spring is the work and the cube is what the spring moves. A piece should make the touch displace something real and let the spring answer with its own damped return; a cube that only wobbles on a timer has no hand in it, and a button that restarts the motion is not a touch.

## M7 -- The touch-activated room: the viewer as the switch

Chambre-Ballon of 1968 scales M6 from a tabletop cube to a room (C8063). Five giant vinyl spheres mounted on springs, wired to light, were presented at the Maison de la Culture de Grenoble in 1968, in "Cinetisme, Spectacle, Environnement". The spheres "vibrated and became illuminated when touched." The viewer is inside the work, and touching is the only way to switch it on. This is the only work in this study where the viewer is a necessary part of the circuit rather than an optional one: without a hand there is no vibration and no light. No image of the installation was found, so it stays prose-only, but the mechanism is stated plainly in the JCMC account and the Grenoble showing is confirmed in the Sicardi exhibition list (C8001). It is also the largest-scale work in this study, and the clearest proof that Asis's kinetic art was never only optical: the hand replaces the eye as the motor, and the room replaces the panel as the surface.

**Takeaway:** the room is the piece and the touch is the switch. A piece that uses this mechanism should make the viewer enter it and make the light depend on contact; a room that lights itself on a timer has turned the visitor back into a spectator.

## M8 -- The target: one centre, rings that swell and narrow

A single system of concentric rings, their widths varying from ring to ring, in electric colours, inside a square field (C8066). Nothing moves. The eye is pulled to the centre and held there, while the varying ring widths make the target seem to swell and narrow. Cercles concentriques (1433) of 1969 is a small gouache on paperboard, 5.5 by 5.5 inches, the earliest dated work in the strand as the Drawing Room frames it: the 2015 show covers "the artist's 1961-2011 Cercles Concentriques, small gouache compositions of concentric rings of dilating electric colors", with "the reduced target format of varying widths of circles vibrating within one another". The Poirier essay says the key-form made of fine colored concentric circles was produced by Asis from 1968 until his death (C8007). The unnumbered Cercles Concentriques of 2008 shows the late form: a single ring system in blue and gold on black, signed 2008. The target is the reduction of M2 to one centre: where the interference painting needs many centres to keep the eye unsettled, the target needs one centre to hold it still, and the varying ring widths do the moving instead.

**Takeaway:** one centre is enough if the rings do the work. A piece should vary the ring widths and let the target swell and narrow inside its square; equal rings make a bullseye, not a vibration.

## The aesthetic logic: the surface is the machine, the viewer is the power

Asis's work has one material and it is not metal or paint. It is the interval between two surfaces -- the gap between the pierced plate and the painted ground, the gap between two ring systems, the gap between two colours in a grid, the gap between a disc and the white void behind it. Everything he builds is a way of holding two flat things apart at a fixed distance and letting a third thing -- the viewer's body, the viewer's hand, the viewer's eye -- cross the gap. That is why his forms are so few -- the circle, the square, the disc, the grid, the cube, the ball -- and why they repeat for fifty years: the form is a housing for the interval, and the housing does not need to change when the interval does the work. The grille of 1956 is the grille of 2005, because a plate with holes in it over a painting does not age; the colour grid of 1960 is the colour grid of 1985, because a strict square order does not go out of date. Colour, for him, is not a property of a surface but a relation between two surfaces: a colour seen through a hole is a different colour from the same colour seen plain, a disc read against white is a different disc from the same disc read against a grid. The viewer is part of the circuit in three different ways across the career, and a piece that uses him should choose one and honour it: the viewer who walks past the plate and shivers it by moving; the viewer who touches the cube and the ball and is answered by a spring; the viewer who stands still and lets the eye do the moving, flipping figure and ground, chasing rings that never settle. What he never does is decorate. There is no ornament in the plates, no pattern for its own sake, no colour that is not produced by the interval. The plate exists so the painting behind it changes; the spring exists so the cube answers the hand; the white ground exists so the discs float in it. Subtraction is the design: one plate, one grid, one spring, one interval, and the patience to let the viewer cross it.
