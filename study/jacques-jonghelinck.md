# Jacques Jonghelinck: Study Notes

**Site:** Jacques Jonghelinck (Antwerp, 21 October 1530 - Antwerp, 31 May 1606), Flemish sculptor, medallist, seal-engraver and bronze-founder, the Brussels court medallist who carried the Habsburg medal into the Dutch Revolt and worked both sides of it. He was the younger brother of Nicolaes Jonghelinck (1517-1570), merchant banker and art collector of Antwerp, who owned sixteen pictures by Pieter Bruegel the Elder by 1565, including the Tower of Babel and the Months cycle; Jacques designed a fortified country house for Nicolaes in 1547 and sold it to him in 1554, and the Bruegels were probably commissioned for it. His father is not named in any source consulted, and his training, master and apprenticeship are undocumented; a Wikimedia authority file lists a work location of Milan in 1552, snippet only, unverified, and no source states he trained under Leone Leoni, Jacopo da Trezzo or any other Italian, though all three were in Brussels in the 1550s. As early as 1556 he was entrusted with cutting seals for various chanceries of Philip II's government, and in the same years he made a smaller marriage medal modelled after Jacopo da Trezzo's gold marriage medal of Mary Tudor (CNG eAuction 397, lot 856: "a smaller marriage medal modeled after this was contemporaneously done by Jacques Jonghelink, who would serve as a seal-cutter in the early court of Philip II"). He moved from Antwerp to Brussels in 1562 to set up a workshop in a building of the palace complex, and was appointed court sculptor in 1563. In Brussels he specialised in funeral monuments for an aristocratic clientele and was also a successful merchant and financier. He belonged to the immediate entourage of Antoine Perrenot de Granvelle, diplomat and President of the Council of State 1556-1564. His first large commission was the gilt-bronze and black-marble memorial of Charles the Bold (died 1477) in the Church of Our Lady, Bruges, made with the sculptor Joos Aerts, ordered by Philip II in 1559 and completed 1563 (the tomb plaque gives 1558-1562; the dates conflict, see C253). In the spring of 1566, from that palace workshop, he made a mould for a small medal and cast the Geuzen medals in lead, tin, copper, silver and gold: about 25 mm across, Philip II's bust on the obverse with "1566" cut into the truncation and the French legend "EN TOVT FIDELLES AV ROY", a beggar's sack and clasped hands on the reverse with "IVSQVES A PORTER LA BESACE", worn on the breast on a ribbon. The letters between Granvelle in Madrid and his secretary Maximilian Morillon in Brussels record the making: on 15 June 1566 Morillon sent Granvelle a lead specimen, and on 7 July he wrote that he got angry at Jonghelinck "because he had broken his first Geuzen medal", probably the mould, which Jonghelinck remade for small profit. The mature portrait medals followed in the same years: Antonius van Straelen, 1565, silver, 5.23 cm, whose reverse, Fortune sailing in a shell, the Victoria and Albert Museum calls "perhaps the most successful effort attributed to Jonghelinck"; Margaret of Parma, 1567, bronze relief, Justice trampling monsters on the reverse; Alberik, Count of Lodron, 1567, silver, 2.8 cm; Viglius van Aytta, 1568, silver, 5.3 cm. In 1569 he cast his masterwork, a full-length over-life-size bronze of Fernando Alvarez de Toledo, 3rd Duke of Alba, from the gunmetal of the sixteen cannon captured at Jemmingen, erected in the citadel of Antwerp, Alba standing in armour trampling a prostrate figure with two heads, four arms and one body, the pedestal inscribed with his pacification of the Netherlands and the work itself inscribed "ex aere captivo", from captured bronze. It stood about three years and was taken down on 9 June 1574, on the evidence of a contemporary medal inscription, then stored and melted down to make new artillery (see C248). The same portrait was worked back down in the same year: a bronze bust of Alba dated 1571, now in the Frick Collection, also cast from the captured metal, and a 3.95 cm silver Alba medal dated 1571. In parallel the Spanish court pulled him toward mythology and the garden: Silenus astride a Cask, bronze, 1570, crowning a fountain at Aranjuez; eight life-size bronzes of the planetary deities, c. 1570, before 1586, in the Royal Palace, Madrid, of which Mercury and Jupiter are confirmed by name and the other six are not verified in this study, re-attributed to Jonghelinck only in Bert Meijer's 1979 Oud Holland article; a bronze bust of Philip II in the Prado. In 1572 he was appointed head ("waardijn", director or warden) of the Antwerp mint, and Forrer says he held the title of Sculptor, Metalfounder and Sealengraver to the King, an appointment he preserved under the Archdukes Albert and Isabella. The mint years turned his output administrative and reproductive: a silver commemorative medal for the Pacification of Ghent, 1577, cast in large numbers and deliberately thin, many specimens holed by the thin casting; a medal of Abraham Ortelius, 1578, silver, 3.49 cm, reverse a serpent crowned with orb and cross among books, Greek legend translated "Knowledge through Faith"; and, attributed tentatively by Charles Avery, six bronze roundels of the Spanish Fury, c. 1578-80, 17.8 cm, at the Victoria and Albert Museum, showing the sack of Antwerp of 4-6 November 1576 and the liberation of the city in August 1577, compositions following Marten de Vos's drawings, with an empty rectangle in one scene marking the former site of the Alba statue. His latest dated work found in this study is a silver double-portrait medal of Philip II and Alessandro Farnese, 1587, 33 mm, British Museum M.224. His son Gaspar Jonghelinck was Mintwarden at Antwerp in 1601, so the mint office stayed in the family a generation on. ## M1. One Mould, Many Metals: The Graded Cast
A single medal mould yields casts in lead, tin, copper, silver and gold; the cheap metal runs in the largest numbers and the precious metals in small ones. The Geuzen medal of spring 1566 was cast successively in all five metals from one mould, with more cast in lead than in any other metal, and Morillon sneered to Granvelle that it was "a medal for poor people". The same logic governs da Trezzo's marriage medals, gold for royal hands, silver for Granvelle. The design never changes; only the metal does, so the object's value tracks the recipient's rank, not the design. It is a production system, not a style choice: one model, a hierarchy of materials, and distribution as the point of the object. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals; CoinsWeekly on the da Trezzo gold medal).

**Takeaway:** the mould is the design and the metal is the rank; one model makes a whole social ladder of objects.

## M2. The Broken Mould as Remake
When the first Geuzen mould broke in July 1566, Jonghelinck remade it; the design survived its tool. Morillon wrote to Granvelle on 7 July 1566 that he got angry at Jonghelinck "because he had broken his first Geuzen medal", which the article glosses as probably the mould breaking, and Jonghelinck could reproduce the mould, though he made only a small profit on the first version. The break is not a failure of the work but a workshop event inside a mass-production run, and the remake is what lets the medal go on being cast in five metals. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end; Morillon letter of 7 July 1566, second-hand, via article).

**Takeaway:** the mould is expendable and the design is not; breaking it is part of making many, not the end of making one.

## M3. Thin Casting as Economy
Deliberately minimising silver in the 1577 Pacification of Ghent medal leaves small holes in the cast surface; thrift is visible as pitting. Jonghelinck "tried to maximise his profit by minimising silver in the casting process, and many medals have small holes due to thin casting". The holes are not damage and not a flaw in the mould; they are the production choice showing through the object, a cost-cutting decision that every surviving specimen carries. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals).

**Takeaway:** when the metal is the budget, the economy shows in the skin of the object; thinness is a signature, not an accident.

## M4. The Eyelet as Wear Point
Medals were cast with an eyelet for a ribbon or chain; the eyelet is the part that breaks, and later collectors removed it. The Geuzen medal was worn on the breast on a ribbon around the neck, sometimes against armour, and about half of known specimens have the eyelet broken off, a result of late 17th- and 18th-century collectors removing them as unfashionable in a cabinet. The 1577 Pacification medal likewise carries an eyelet or its broken scar. Wearability shaped the form, small, light, 25 mm, eyelet at the top, and later collecting reshaped the survivors. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals).

**Takeaway:** the eyelet is the hinge between the object and the body; its absence on half the survivors is the record of how the medal was worn and then un-worn.

## M5. Cannon into Statue: Captured Bronze as Message
Captured gunmetal, sixteen cannon from Jemmingen, is melted and recast as a colossal portrait statue; the material is the message. The Alba statue was made in 1569 from the gunmetal of the cannon captured at Jemmingen, and the fact was inscribed on the work itself, "ex aere captivo", from captured bronze, on the Galle engraving "Ivngelingi Opvs ex aere captivo". The metal was the trophy and the message: rebel guns remade as the governor's body, standing in armour trampling a prostrate figure with two heads, four arms and one body. The cycle then closed violently: the statue stood about three years, was taken down on 9 June 1574, stored, and melted down to make new artillery, bronze returning to bronze. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacques Jonghelinck, read end to end; Motley via search snippet); leg 2 (Bass, The Monument's End, via Princeton University Press page; Harvard Art Museums 2004.190; British Museum print 1877,0811.789).

**Takeaway:** the statue's material was its argument, and the argument was answered in the same material; a monument made of captured guns is a gun again when it falls.

## M6. Gilt Bronze on Black Marble: The Two-Tone Tomb
The Charles the Bold memorial pairs gilt-bronze figures with black marble architecture for a two-tone tomb. The effigy of Charles in full armour, crowned, wearing the Order of the Golden Fleece, lies in repose on a polished black marble slab beside the older Gothic tomb of his daughter Mary of Burgundy (1490-1502), and the design deliberately echoes Mary's tomb while inserting Renaissance forms. The contrast does the work: bright metal figure against dark stone ground, the dead duke lit against the dark slab. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacques Jonghelinck, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Jacques Jonghelinck and Charles the Bold).

**Takeaway:** the tomb is a picture made of two materials; the gold needs the black marble the way a portrait needs a ground.

## M7. The Medal as Wearable Badge: A Portrait Becomes a Uniform
A 25 mm cast medal, worn on the breast on a ribbon, turns a portrait into a political uniform; copies add miniature beggar's bowls and flasks to the same ribbon. The Geuzen medal's obverse still shows the king, loyalist on the face, rebel on the reverse, and it was worn by the Compromise nobles and, the Holland Society history says, by William of Orange at the time of his assassination. The medal is small, cheap, wearable and mass-cast in base metal with precious-metal versions for the elite, and it is plausibly the first political medal in the modern sense. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end); leg 3 (Holland Society history, via search).

**Takeaway:** at 25 mm on a ribbon the portrait stops being a keepsake and starts being a side; the wearer is the frame.

## M8. Engraving as Distribution: The Work Travels as Print
Designs travel as prints: Philips Galle engraved Jonghelinck's Roman-god statues in 1586, a series of which the Metropolitan Museum holds Plate 7, Diana; the Wierix brothers engraved the Spanish Fury compositions after designs by Marten de Vos in 1578-79, which the plaquettes then follow, though Jeremy Warren argued the plaquettes derive from de Vos's drawings, not the prints, and were very likely made before the prints. The statues themselves are known through the prints, and the plaquettes' compositions through the engravings; the engraving is the distribution system for bronze that cannot travel. Source: leg 1 (Met/Wikimedia snippet; V and A plaquette records); leg 2 (V and A The Oath of the Victors, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the bronze stays put and the print goes everywhere; for a sculptor of heavy objects, engraving is the only way the work circulates.

## M9. The Seal as Miniature Sculpture
Seal cutting for Philip II's chanceries from 1556 put Jonghelinck's modelling into administrative metal; his court title joined Sculptor, Metalfounder and Sealengraver. He was entrusted with cutting seals for various chanceries of Philip II's government as early as 1556, and Forrer says he held the title of Sculptor, Metalfounder and Sealengraver to the King, an appointment he preserved under the Archdukes Albert and Isabella. The seal is the smallest sculpture he made and the most used: every document it stamps carries his modelling, unsigned, into the administration. Source: leg 1 (Forrer, Biographical Dictionary of Medallists, snippet only).

**Takeaway:** the seal is a portrait that works for a living; the court sculptor's smallest object is the one that touches the most paper.

## M10. The Fountain Figure as Replacement
Silenus astride a Cask (1570) was made to take the place of Giambologna's Samson and a Philistine at Aranjuez; a bronze can be swapped into an existing water system. The figure crowns a fountain in the gardens of Aranjuez, the drunken Silenus riding astride a wine cask, water presumably issuing from the cask or the figure, and it replaced the Giambologna, which had been given to Charles, Prince of Wales in 1623. The commission is not a new fountain but a new top for an old one: the plinth, the pipes and the garden stay, only the figure changes. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacques Jonghelinck, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Jacques Jonghelinck).

**Takeaway:** a fountain figure is a plug-in object; the garden keeps its plumbing and changes its god.

## M11. The Dated Truncation and the Rim Legend: A Compact Information System
In the mature medals the portrait bust is cut at the chest and the date is inscribed on the cut itself, "1566" on the truncation of Philip's bust in the Geuzen medal, "1571" on the truncation of the Alba medal. The legend runs around the rim in abbreviated Latin, naming person and office, and the reverse is left free for allegory. The uniface Margaret of Parma medal in the Hood Museum shows the system reduced to face-plus-legend alone. Face, name, office and date all fit inside a 3-5 cm disc, and the date sits where the bust is cut, as if the year were part of the body. Source: leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end; Burlington Magazine Frick acquisitions 2007; Hood Museum Margaret of Parma page, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the medal is a label as well as a likeness; the date cut into the chest makes the year part of the portrait.

## M12. Reverse Allegory as Argument: Obverse Is Who, Reverse Is What They Stand For
The reverse is never decoration; it states the sitter's claim. Van Straelen: Fortune sailing in a shell, "by virtue and constancy", a burgomaster's luck and steadiness. Margaret of Parma: Justice trampling monsters in the Low Countries, a governor's programme. Viglius: an hourglass, a lit torch and an open book on a table, "life of mortals is a vigil", a councillor's vigilance. Ortelius: a serpent crowned with orb and cross among books, "Knowledge through Faith" in Greek, a scholar's creed. Geuzen: a beggar's sack and clasped hands, a political identity. The mechanism is consistent across thirty years: obverse is who, reverse is what they stand for, in a single emblematic scene that must read at 3-5 cm. Source: leg 2 (V and A van Straelen and Ortelius pages, read end to end; Artera Margaret of Parma and Viglius pages; de Coster jeton catalogue; EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals).

**Takeaway:** the back of the medal is the argument and the front is the name; a 5 cm disc carries a whole political programme if the emblem is exact.

## Evolution
Phase 1, learning the Italian idiom, c. 1554-1562. The earliest securely discussed work is a reduction: a smaller copy of Jacopo da Trezzo's marriage medal. Jonghelinck begins as a seal-cutter in Philip II's court, working inside an Italian's design, not his own. The Hanau-Munzenberg contract (1562) and the Charles the Bold memorial (1558-1563) then show the first scale jump: from a 3-6 cm medal to a life-size gilt-bronze effigy on black marble, executed in collaboration with Joos Aerts. The memorial is deliberately retrospective, copying the Gothic form of Mary of Burgundy's tomb of 1490-1502 while inserting Renaissance detail. Portrait here is still funerary and dynastic, not personal.

Phase 2, the mature portrait medal, 1565-1568. Four portrait medals in four years, all of men and one woman of the Brussels governing circle: van Straelen, Margaret of Parma, Lodron, Viglius. The format settles: a bust in low relief, cut at the chest, ringed by a Latin legend naming office as well as person; the reverse carries the sitter's argument as allegory rather than as a second portrait. Sizes cluster small, 2.8 cm Lodron, 5.23 cm van Straelen, 5.3 cm Viglius, objects for the hand and the cabinet, not for display. The van Straelen Fortune, sailing in her shell, is the high point of this phase: the V and A judges it his most successful medal, and the modelling of a nude figure in motion inside a 5 cm disc is a technical step beyond the stiff profile busts of phase 1.

Phase 3, the political medal and the mass object, 1566-1568. The Geuzen medal breaks the phase-2 pattern in every way at once. Same workshop, same hand, same year as the court portraits, but now the medal is a badge, not a keepsake: cast in lead for the poor and in gold for the rich from one mould, fitted with an eyelet, worn on a ribbon on the breast, and lettered in French so its slogan can be read aloud in the street. The obverse still shows the king, loyalist on the face, rebel on the reverse. This is the first political medal in the modern sense, and its production logic, one mould, graded metals, wearable, cheap enough to copy, is the opposite of the unique court medal. Morillon's letters show Jonghelinck breaking his first mould in July 1566 and remaking it for small profit: mass production as a workshop problem, not a court commission.

Phase 4, monumental bronze and the Spanish court, 1569-1571. The scale jump is extreme: from 2.8 cm medals to an over-life-size public statue cast from captured cannon. The Alba colossus takes the medal's logic, portrait plus allegorical reverse plus inscribed legend, and blows it up to civic scale, with the metal itself as the message, "from captured bronze". The same portrait is then worked back down: the Frick bronze bust (1571) and the 3.95 cm silver Alba medal (1571) repeat the sitter in the same year at two smaller scales. In parallel, the Spanish court commissions pull him toward mythology and the garden: Silenus astride a Cask (1570) for Aranjuez, the eight life-size planetary deities (c. 1570, before 1586) for the Royal Palace in Madrid, a bronze bust of Philip II for the Prado. The portrait medalist becomes a bronze sculptor of full figures in the round, the first time the human body, not just the head, is his subject.

Phase 5, the mint and the late work, 1572-1587. Appointed head of the Antwerp mint in 1572, Jonghelinck's output turns administrative and reproductive. The 1577 Pacification medal is cast in large numbers, deliberately thin, silver saved, holes left in the casting, profit taken from the process. The late portrait medals shrink again: Ortelius 1578 at 3.49 cm, Philip II and Farnese 1587 at 33 mm. The reverse allegory also changes character: from political Fortune and Justice to a humanist emblem, the Ortelius serpent with orb and cross among books, Greek legend "Knowledge through Faith". The Spanish Fury plaquettes (c. 1578-80, 17.8 cm roundels) are the exception to the shrinkage, large, multi-figure, narrative reliefs, but their attribution is tentative, and if they are his, they show him working against his own employer, memorialising the Spanish Fury and the liberation of Antwerp. The career ends where it began, small silver portrait discs, but the sitters have changed from kings and governors to a cartographer, and the reverse from dynastic allegory to a personal emblem.

What changed, in one line each. Portrait style: copied Italian profile (1554) to refined low-relief bust with the date cut into the truncation (1571) to small, intimate humanist portrait (1578, 1587). Reverse allegory: second portrait (da Trezzo model) to single-figure allegory as argument (Fortune, Justice, Vigilance) to slogan in the vernacular (Geuzen) to personal emblem (Ortelius serpent). Scale: 3-6 cm medal to life-size tomb effigy to 2.8-5.3 cm cabinet medals to over-life-size public colossus to life-size garden and palace bronzes to 3.3-3.5 cm late medals, with one 17.8 cm plaquette exception. Production: unique court object to graded-metal casting from one mould to mass distribution with cost-cutting (thin silver, holes) to mint output.

## Aesthetic logic
Jonghelinck's aesthetic is the logic of the small thing that must carry a large claim. Everything he makes is a container for an argument, and the container is always smaller than the argument: a 25 mm disc carries a rebellion, a 3.95 cm disc carries a governor, a seal carries a government. The portrait is the anchor and the reverse is the proof, and the two faces together make a complete sentence, who on the front, what they stand for on the back, with the date cut into the body of the bust so the sentence is dated. Scale is not a property of the work but a register he plays across: the same Alba portrait exists as a colossus, a bust and a 3.95 cm medal in the same three years, and the same Geuzen design exists in lead for the poor and gold for the rich, so the work is never one object but a family of objects at different sizes and prices, all struck from the same model. Material is never neutral in his hands: lead is the poor man's medal, gold the king's, captured cannon the governor's body, thin silver the mint's profit, and the holes in the 1577 medal are the economy showing through the skin. He works both sides of the Revolt not out of indecision but because the medal is a technology of allegiance, and allegiance is a market: the court pays for the king's face and the rebels pay for the beggar's sack, and the same mould serves both, because the mould does not choose sides. The beauty in his best work, the van Straelen Fortune sailing in her shell, is the beauty of a whole figure moving inside a space too small for it, a nude woman, a shell, a sail and waves in 5.23 cm, the figure doing in relief what the allegory does in words: carrying the sitter's claim on her back. What he avoids is the unique masterpiece; his genius is the system, one model, many metals, many sizes, many wearers, and the work's meaning changes with each recasting.


## Positions
P1. Jonghelinck was a court artist who worked both sides of the Dutch Revolt: he made the Geuzen (rebel) medals of 1566 and the Alba (Spanish) colossus of 1569, and the Spanish Fury plaquettes are attributed to him although he was the Spanish mint-master. Evidence: Geuzen medals article; V and A plaquette records (attribution tentative). (Leg 1.)
P2. His workshop sat inside the Brussels palace complex, placing medal-making at the centre of government, not in a craftsman's quarter. Evidence: Geuzen medals article (Morillon/Granvelle letters). (Leg 1.)
P3. He was a merchant and financier as well as a sculptor, and his brother Nicolaes was a merchant banker; the family combined art, money and collecting. Evidence: Wikipedia Jonghelinck article; Wikipedia Nicolaes article (88 lines). (Leg 1.)
P4. His career pivots on 1562-1563: move to Brussels, court sculptor, and the Charles the Bold monument; the medals follow from the court position. Evidence: Wikipedia Jonghelinck article. (Leg 1.)
P5. The Geuzen medal is plausibly the first political medal: small, cheap, wearable, mass-cast in base metal with precious-metal versions for the elite. Evidence: Geuzen medals article ("Probable first political medal"). (Leg 1.)
P6. His most praised medal is not a royal portrait but the 1565 van Straelen medal, whose reverse (Fortune on a shell) the V and A calls graceful and beautifully modelled. Evidence: V and A record O349450 (87 lines, read end to end). (Leg 1.)
P7. The Alba statue's destruction is part of the work's history: made from captured cannon, destroyed by the regime that ordered it, then commemorated in medals of its own fall (Harvard medal 1574-1577). Evidence: Wikipedia; Harvard Art Museums record (snippet). (Leg 1.)
P8. His large bronzes were re-attributed, not rediscovered: eight life-size bronzes in Madrid were recognised as his only in Meijer's 1979 Oud Holland article. Evidence: Warburg Institute records citing Meijer 1979 (snippet). (Leg 1.)
P9. His mint post (1572) made him both a maker of state coin and a maker of rebel imagery; the two roles overlapped for the rest of his career. Evidence: V and A plaquette records; Wikipedia. (Leg 1.)
P10. He designed architecture too: the fortified country house for his brother Nicolaes (designed 1547, sold 1554), visible on a 1582 map of Antwerp. Evidence: Wikipedia Nicolaes article. (Leg 1.)
P11. He worked the same portrait across three scales in one year: the over-life-size Alba statue (1569, or installed 1571), the bronze Alba bust (1571) and the 3.95 cm silver Alba medal (1571) are one sitter at civic, domestic and pocket scale, and the bust and the medal are the same year. Evidence: leg 2 catalogue entries 10, 13 and 14 (Bass 2024; Burlington Magazine 2007). (Parent, from leg 2.)
P12. The reverse allegory changes character with the sitter: from dynastic Fortune and Justice for governors to a humanist personal emblem, the Ortelius serpent with orb and cross among books, for a cartographer. Evidence: leg 2 catalogue entries 5, 7 and 17 (V and A pages, read end to end). (Parent, from leg 2.)
P13. The 1577 Pacification medal's thin casting is a deliberate economy, not a defect: silver was minimised to maximise profit, and the holes in surviving specimens are the visible record of that choice. Evidence: leg 2 catalogue entry 16 (EN Wikipedia Geuzen medals, read end to end). (Parent, from leg 2.)
P14. The Alba statue survives only as an engraving and a destruction medal: Philips Galle's engraving after Jonghelinck and the Harvard medal of its fall are the visual record, and the Frick bust, privately commissioned and also cast from the captured metal, is the best surviving source for the lost statue's surface. Evidence: leg 2 catalogue entries 10, 13 and the related Harvard medal (Bass 2024; British Museum print 1877,0811.789). (Parent, from leg 2.)
P15. The Spanish Fury plaquettes, if his, put the Spanish mint-master on the rebel side of the story: the attribution is tentative precisely because a Habsburg mint-master making anti-Spanish subjects is a contradiction, and the V and A says so in terms. Evidence: leg 2 catalogue entry 18 (V and A The Oath of the Victors, read end to end; V and A, updated March 2025). (Parent, from leg 2.)

### Patrons, rulers and sitters
1. Jacopo da Trezzo (studied #520) - the original. Da Trezzo's c.1554 gold marriage medal of Mary Tudor (67-67.7 mm) is the model Jonghelinck's smaller contemporary marriage medal was made after (CNG eAuction 397 lot 856). The two men are the Milanese and the Brussels ends of the same Habsburg medallic court; da Trezzo worked in the Low Countries 1555-1559 while Jonghelinck was cutting seals for Philip II's government from 1556 (Forrer).
2. Pompeo Leoni (studied #519) - same Habsburg medallic world, one generation on. No documented direct contact with Jonghelinck was found; the bridge is da Trezzo, with whom Pompeo collaborated in Madrid, and whose marriage medal Jonghelinck copied in Brussels.
3. Leone Leoni (already carried #516) - the Milanese master of the Habsburg medal. No documented meeting with Jonghelinck was found. Circumstantial only: the Commons authority file lists Jonghelinck's work location as Milan in 1552, when Leone was master of the Milan mint; both men served the same Habsburg patrons. Do not present as a master-pupil link.
4. Philip II of Spain (already carried #514) - the patron who defines Jonghelinck's career. Jonghelinck was entrusted with cutting seals for various chanceries of Philip II's government as early as 1556 (Forrer); court sculptor 1563; head of the Antwerp mint 1572; he kept the title "Sculptor, Metalfounder and Sealengraver to the King" under the Archdukes Albert and Isabella (Forrer). Sitters on his medals include Philip II himself (Geuzen medal obverse, 1566; 1587 medal with Alessandro Farnese, British Museum M.224).
5. Fernando Alvarez de Toledo, 3rd Duke of Alba (neither) - sitter and commissioner of the over-life-size bronze of 1569, cast from the gunmetal of the 16 cannon captured at Jemmingen, erected in the Antwerp citadel. Alba was governor of the Netherlands 1567-1573. A bronze bust of Alba dated 1571 is in the Frick Collection.
6. Margaret of Parma (neither) - regent/governor-general of the Netherlands 1559-1567, daughter of Charles V, mother of Alessandro Farnese. Jonghelinck made her portrait medal: a uniface Margaret of Parma medal is catalogued as Smolderen No. 52 (Hood Museum, Dartmouth, object 2016.64.126), and a bronze relief of Margaret with her son Alexander Farnese, reverse Justice trampling monsters, dated 1567, is attributed to Jonghelinck (artera.ae, weak, AI-curated source; treat the 1567 relief attribution as tentative). She is also the political context of the Geuzen medals: the Compromise petition was presented to her on 5 April 1566, and she resigned in 1567 on Alba's arrival.
7. Antoine Perrenot de Granvelle, Cardinal Granvelle (neither) - diplomat, President of the Council of State 1556-1564. Jonghelinck "belonged to the immediate entourage" of Granvelle (Wikipedia). The Granvelle-Morillon letters are the documentary source for the Geuzen medals. Jonghelinck made Granvelle's portrait medal (post-1561, illustrated in the Wikipedia article) and a portrait medal for Jean Scheyfve's "literary opponent Granvelle" (see entry 16).
8. Maximilian Morillon (neither) - Granvelle's secretary in Brussels. His letters to Granvelle in Madrid document the Geuzen medal mould (spring 1566), the sending of a lead specimen on 15 June 1566, and his anger at Jonghelinck on 7 July 1566 for breaking his first Geuzen medal. He is the paper trail behind the most famous object of Jonghelinck's career.
9. Anthonis Mor (already carried #519) - court portraitist of the same Brussels circle. No documented collaboration with Jonghelinck was found; the link is shared sitters and a shared court. Mor painted Mary Tudor in 1554 (the portrait long assumed to be da Trezzo's model for the marriage medal; CoinsWeekly now thinks both men sketched the queen at the same sitting), Margaret of Parma (1562, Berlin; and the Prado diptych after 1565), Granvelle (1549, Vienna), and Steven van Herwijck (1564, Mauritshuis).
10. Mary Tudor, Mary I of England (neither) - sitter of the marriage medal. Da Trezzo's gold original c.1554 (67.7 mm, only two gold specimens known); Jonghelinck's smaller contemporary version after it (CNG; CoinWorld: a 1555 Antwerp medal "likely produced in Antwerp in 1555 by Jacques Jonghelinck").
11. Alessandro Farnese, Duke of Parma (neither) - sitter. Silver medal of 1587 pairing Alessandro Farnese with Philip II, designed by Jonghelinck, 33 mm (British Museum M.224; Smolderen 1996, no. 101, p.358). Son of Margaret of Parma (entry 6).
12. Don John of Austria (neither) - after the Pacification of Ghent, accepted by John of Austria, Jonghelinck "produced a silver commemorative medal for casting in large numbers" (vL.I 243/230, 1577) (Geuzen medals article).
13. Luis de Requesens (neither) - Alba's successor as governor. Per Motley, he threw down and demolished the Alba statue; per Wikipedia it was destroyed after Alba's death on Philip II's orders. Flagged conflict; see D3 and C248.
14. Archdukes Albert and Isabella (neither) - joint rulers of the Spanish Netherlands from 1599. Jonghelinck preserved his appointment as Sculptor, Metalfounder and Sealengraver to the King under them (Forrer), carrying his Habsburg service into a third reign.
15. Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor (already carried #514) - dynastic context only: father of Philip II and of Margaret of Parma, grandfather of Don John. No Jonghelinck work for Charles V himself was verified.
16. Philipp III von Hanau-Munzenberg (neither) - commission. A contract for his funeral monument (died 1561) dated 1562 is listed among Jonghelinck's references (Wikipedia reference list). Jonghelinck specialised in funeral monuments for an aristocratic clientele.

### Family and household

20. Gaspar Jonghelinck (neither) - son. "Son of Jacques Jonghelinck; Mintwarden at Antwerp, 1601" (Forrer, Biographical Dictionary of Medallists). The mint office stayed in the family a generation on.

### Collaborators, workshop and the Antwerp mint circle
22. Joos Aerts (neither) - collaborator. Jonghelinck "collaborated as sculptor and bronze-founder with the sculptor Joos Aerts in the gilt-bronze and black marble memorial of Charles the Bold (died 1477)" in the Onze Lieve Vrouwekerk, Bruges, completed 1563 (Wikipedia).
23. Charles the Bold, Duke of Burgundy (neither) - subject of the Bruges tomb (died 1477). A dead duke, not a living contact: the commission that put Jonghelinck and Aerts together.
24. Jean Scheyfve (neither) - sitter and commissioner. Chancellor of Brabant 1557-1579; "In 1575 he commissioned the sculptor Jacques Jonghelinck to make his portrait medallion, which shows him wearing a cuirass and mantle with the motto 'danger.' Jonghelinck also made a portrait medal for his literary opponent Granvelle" (Wikipedia, Jean Scheyfve).
25. Philippe de Montmorency, Count of Horn, and Walburge de Nuenar (neither) - sitters. Silver medal designed by Jonghelinck, 1565-1566, 62 mm: Horn in profile with the Golden Fleece, his wife Walburge on the reverse (British Museum M.2921; Smolderen 1996, no. 47, p.272; van Loon vol. I, p.75).
26. Marten de Vos (neither) - designer behind the Spanish Fury roundels. The six bronze plaquettes attributed to Jonghelinck (V and A A.33-1975 to A.38-1975, c.1578-80) derive from drawings by Marten de Vos, later engraved by the Wierix brothers; Jeremy Warren argues the plaquettes derive from the drawings, not the engravings, and were very likely made before the prints. Five de Vos drawings survive (four Ashmolean, one Met).
27. The Wierix brothers (neither) - engravers. Their seven engravings after Marten de Vos, published Antwerp 1578-79, are the printed parallel to the Spanish Fury roundels; five of the subjects (including the Demolition of the Citadel) derive from them.
28. Philips Galle (neither) - engraver after Jonghelinck. The Met's "Statues of Roman Gods" series (1586) is engraved by Philips Galle after Jacques Jonghelinck (e.g. Plate 7, Diana, MET 37.68.57). Jonghelinck's statues circulated as prints.
29. Steven van Herwijck (neither; also a #522 candidate, see below) - adjacent medallist. Worked in Antwerp 1559 (nine medals survive, including Jacobus Fabius); the Web Gallery of Art biography states "He must also have known the work of Jacques Jonghelinck". Both men were in Antwerp in 1559-1564, and both were painted by Anthonis Mor (Mor's 1564 Portrait of a Goldsmith is identified as Herwijck).

### The other side: the Geuzen and the Revolt
30. William I of Orange (neither) - the Geuzen leader. The Geuzen medal was worn by William of Orange at the time of his assassination (Holland Society history), and the medal type Jonghelinck cast became the badge of the opposition. He is the sitter Jonghelinck never made and the cause his medal served.
31. Hendrik van Brederode (neither) - Geuzen leader. With Louis of Nassau he led the nobles who signed the Compromise; he presented the Request to Margaret of Parma on 5 April 1566, and at the Culemburg dinner of 6 April 1566 the confederates took the name "gueux". The Geuzen medals Jonghelinck cast are the material trace of that week.
32. The Geuzen / Compromise of the Nobles (collective, neither) - the party whose badge Jonghelinck's medals became: about 400 nobles united in Brussels in early April 1566, adopting beggar's bowl and wallet as emblems, with the motto "EN TOVT FIDELLES AV ROY / IVSQVES A PORTER LA BESACE" ("In everything loyal to the king, even to carrying the beggar's bag").

## Corrections
- C248. The Alba statue's destruction date. The task brief's "destroyed 1577" is wrong, and so is the Wikipedia account that it was destroyed after Alba's death (1582) on Philip II's orders. The dated evidence is a contemporary medal: the Harvard Art Museums medal commemorating the destruction is inscribed with the fall on 9 June 1574, the V and A plaquette text says the statue "had been taken down in 1574", and Motley and Bass (2024) agree it was thrown down by Alba's successor Requesens, stood only about three years, was stored, then melted down to make new artillery. The study uses 9 June 1574 throughout.
- C249. The Alba statue's pose. The statue was a standing figure trampling rebels, per Motley, Bass (2024) and Philips Galle's engraving after Jonghelinck. One modern summary (warhistory.org) describes Alba on horseback trampling the Dutch under his horse's hooves, after an equestrian St James the Moorslayer model; no equestrian version is supported by the engraving or by Motley, and the study treats the equestrian claim as erroneous.
- C250. The doorway tally. The #520 run recorded the streak at ninety-four holds and seventeen non-holds after its check. This run's check is the one-hundred-and-forty-second in a row and is recorded as a HOLD on the substance (ninety-fifth hold) and a FAIL on the letter (eighteenth non-hold). The counting note in the Doorway section explains why 95 + 18 does not sum to 142.
- C251. The mechanism merge. Leg 1 supplied 10 mechanisms and leg 2 supplied 5. The parent merged three overlaps: leg 2's one-mould-five-metals folds into leg 1's one-mould-many-metals (M1); leg 2's worn-object mechanism folds into leg 1's eyelet (M4) and wearable-badge (M7) mechanisms; leg 2's captured-bronze mechanism folds into leg 1's cannon-into-statue (M5). Leg 2's dated-truncation and reverse-allegory mechanisms are kept as their own mechanisms, M11 and M12, because leg 1 had neither. The study keeps 12 mechanisms.
- C252. The top pick for #522, verified first-hand by the parent. A parent search (302 lines) confirms Steven van Herwijck (Utrecht c.1530 - London 1565/67) as a Netherlandish sculptor and gem engraver: Antwerp 1559, nine medals surviving; England 1562, first portrait medals of private individuals made in England; Anthonis Mor's Portrait of a Goldsmith, identified as Herwijck, dated 1564; Elizabeth I medal 1565, National Portrait Gallery, the only surviving example, found in a ploughed field in 1962.
- C253. The Charles the Bold memorial dates are unresolved. The tomb plaque gives 1558-1562, Wikipedia gives "completed in 1563" and says Philip II ordered the monument in 1559, and Forrer says Jonghelinck executed the Bruges monument in 1559. The study uses the range 1559-1563 and does not merge the dates. The Alba statue's making (1569) versus erection (1571, per Bass 2024) is likewise unresolved; the study uses the range 1569-1571.
- C254. The marriage-medal evidence. The D1 claim, that Jonghelinck made a smaller marriage medal after da Trezzo's original, rests on leg 3's CNG eAuction 397 lot 856 research note, on a mid-18th-century reissue after Jonghelinck, corroborated by CoinWorld on the 1555 Antwerp medal. Leg 1 found no Jonghelinck connection to the marriage medal independently and flagged the claim as not assertable from its own sources. The study states the claim with its provenance, as an auction-house research note, not as a museum object record, and the doorway records the letter-fail on that basis.
- C255. The British Museum M.224 page. The British Museum object page for medal M.224 returned only site footer (46 lines) because the record is JavaScript-rendered. The M.224 facts used in this study (33 mm, 12.51 g, Smolderen 101) come from the British Museum search-result text for the same object URL and are flagged as such wherever they appear.
- C256. The Geuzen medal's earliest type is only probably Jonghelinck's. The Granvelle-Morillon letters prove Jonghelinck made a mould and cast Geuzen medals in spring 1566, but the surviving silver-gilt type vL.I 85/84.5 is attributed to him on that letter evidence, and the Geuzen medals article states "there is no absolute proof that this is the earliest type of Geuzen medal and was produced by Jacques Jonghelinck". A second early type, vL.I 85/84.4, is by an unknown maker. The study does not merge the two types.
- C257. The 1560 Viglius jeton is not Jonghelinck's. The de Coster jeton catalogue describes a 1560 Viglius type in detail (obverse "VIGLIVS ZUICHEMUS PRAESES"; reverse "VITA MORTALIVM VIGILIA", a table with hourglass, lit torch and open book), but that 1560 jeton is NOT confirmed as Jonghelinck's work; only the 1568 Rijksmuseum medal is attributed to him. The study does not conflate the two, and no Viglius reverse description is quoted as Jonghelinck's own work.
- C258. The Silenus footnote trap. Wikipedia says Silenus astride a Cask (1570) "replaced Giambologna's Samson and a Philistine" at Aranjuez, and the footnote "Now at the Victoria and Albert Museum, London" attaches to the Samson, NOT to the Silenus. The Silenus's current location is NOT VERIFIED in this study, and the V and A holding is not claimed for it.
