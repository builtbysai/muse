Rueckriem was born on 30 September 1938 in Duesseldorf, and his training is a stonemason's before it is an artist's: a stonemason apprenticeship in Dueren 1957-1959, then work at the Cologne Dombauhuette, the cathedral workshop of Cologne Cathedral -- one source dates his becoming a journeyman there to 1959, the National Galleries of Scotland dates the assistantship to 1960-1961, and the two datings are carried side by side -- and study at the Cologne Werkschulen under Ludwig Gies. At the same time as his early freelance work he was an art teacher at the Gymnasium am Wirteltor in Dueren; from 1963 he worked as a free-lance artist, and from 1963 to 1971 he lived and worked in Noervenich, with a studio in the Gymnicher Burg. In 1966/1967 he first showed sculptures made from wooden beams, and from 1968 he made stone sculptures; in 1980 he stopped using steel and wood in order to concentrate on stone, and more recently on granite. [C27578]

Through his close association with Gallery Konrad Fischer in Duesseldorf he came into contact with Carl Andre, Richard Long, Sol LeWitt and Royden Rabinowitch, and from 1969 he worked in a shared studio with Blinky Palermo in Moenchengladbach -- Imi Knoebel was the third sharer of that studio. The teaching posts run in sequence: professor at the Hochschule fuer bildende Kuenste Hamburg in 1974, at the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf in 1984, and at the Staedelschule in Frankfurt am Main in 1988. The Duesseldorf academy, where he later taught, had rejected him as a student. His Hamburg post overlapped Tuzina's last three study years (1974-1977) and Kiecol's study from 1975, and he was Kiecol's teacher -- but not Tuzina's, whose named final teacher was Walther. [C27579]

He had taken part in international group exhibitions since 1966. He took part in documenta 5 in Kassel in 1972, in the section Individuelle Mythologien Prozesse, and in documenta 7 in 1982, documenta 8 in 1987 and documenta IX in 1992; participation in documenta 6 (1977) is not established -- the participant R-list contains no Rueckriem, and the auction-house line claiming every documenta from 5 to 9 is wrong on that point. In 1978 he took part in the Venice Biennale together with Dieter Krieg as a representative of the Federal Republic of Germany, showing four split dolomite stones. His first retrospective took place in 1973 at the Kunsthalle Tuebingen. His awards run Foerderpreis des Landes Nordrhein-Westfalen 1973, Konrad-von-Soest-Preis 1984, Deutscher Kritikerpreis for visual art 1984, Arnold Bode Award of the city of Kassel 1985, Piepenbrock Preis fuer Skulptur in Berlin 1998, Grosser Kulturpreis der Sparkassen-Kulturstiftung Rheinland 2001, and Kulturpreis des Kreises Dueren 2015. [C27580]

His working method is stated as four processes in order: a drawing of the sculpture seen in the block, then split, cut, ground and polished, with everything done to the stone left readable in sequence, first, second, third. He works from mostly cuboid blanks selected in the quarry, fixes a simple basic form such as a stele, cube, disc, wall or floor relief, draws a division of the blank under strict architectural rules, and reassembles the parts to the original shape, with hammer, iron wedge and circular saw traces left visible at open interfaces and thin cracks; he treats the quarry as his studio and the place as part of the work. In 1994 the Skulpturen-Hallen Ulrich Rueckriem opened in Rommerskirchen-Sinsteden, presenting about 100 sculptures in two halls and an outdoor area of 2000 square metres. For the south courtyard of the Reichstag in Berlin he made two floor sculptures from Normandy granite, each cut from a separated piece into five slabs and laid in their original positions aligned with the courtyard paving; the middle slab of each was mill-cut, ground and polished to a blue-grey reflecting surface, set against the rusty-grey surrounding slabs. In 2008, on his 70th birthday, he gave his archive to the art-history collections of the Ruhr-Universitaet Bochum. [C27581]

The twenty works inspected run 1968-2014 and trace one operation at growing scale. Early, 1968-1979: from iron (Rote Plastik, 1968) to small split dolomite opened with hammer and wedges (Dolomit, gespalten, 1968-69, 95 x 42 x 42 cm), then museum dolomite and a first large public cut, the long low ground slab at Muenster (Dolomit zugeschnitten, 1977, 3.3 x 7.2 x 1.2 m), and low floor pieces and a changed diamond of four rough triangles (Untitled, 1979, MoMA; Untitled (diamond - ruit), 1979, Groningen). Middle, 1982-1989: the work goes monumental and public, granite replacing dolomite as the main stone -- the documenta 7 granite block at Kassel (1982), tall upright Normandy granite slabs in Montreal and Berlin (1984, 1985), the four-part wall reliefs Vier Jahreszeiten (1986), three split Finnish granite monoliths at Muenster (1987), a 579 cm low granite beam at Chicago (1987), eight identical split limestones at Dublin (1988), and a Normandy granite block at Bellingham whose removed centre exposes a polished cavity (1989). Late, 1995-2014: the operation leaves the single block behind and becomes landscape and architecture -- twenty steles in a combinatory grid in the fields near Abiego (Siglo XX, 1995), forty granite floor cuboids answering Mies's grid in Berlin (40 Bodenreliefs, 1998), ten split stones re-marking a former colliery along the Irwell Trail (1999), two floor reliefs set into the Reichstag courtyard paving (date not given in the source used), and a seven-section split stone altar, gilded inside, for Hildesheim Cathedral (2014). Grades: A 11, B 8, C 1 -- the sole C is the Reichstag floor reliefs, date and dimensions unverified in the Bundestag source. [C27582]

## M1 -- Split and reassemble to the original shape

The quarried block is divided into parts -- split with hammer and iron wedges, cut with a circular saw, drilled -- and the parts are put back in their original positions, so the finished sculpture has the outline of the block it came from. The watchable behaviour is the eye reading a whole block first and only then finding the seams: the work is a solid that has been opened and closed again, and the seams are the evidence. Seen in Dolomit, gespalten (hammer and wedges, 1968-69), Vier Jahreszeiten (six or seven parts per panel, 1986), Finnischer Granit gespalten (seven and nine fragments, 1987), Limestone (eight limestones split and reassembled, 1988), the Bellingham Untitled (drilled, cut, split, put together again, 1989) and the Rueckriem-Altar (seven sections, 2014).

**Takeaway:** divide the block and put it back exactly; the seams are the sculpture. [C27583]

## M2 -- The split face left as the record of the work

The rough break is not smoothed away: hammer, wedge, saw and drill traces stay visible at open interfaces and thin cracks, and the sequence of operations -- split, cut, ground, polished -- stays readable in order. The watchable behaviour is the surface telling the making in sequence, first, second, third; boreholes run like a row of beads along a hairline crack in the Muenster granite (1987), and drill holes from the quarry extraction stay visible at the edges of the Kassel block (1982) and the Reichstag floor reliefs.

**Takeaway:** leave the break visible; the process is the ornament. [C27584]

## M3 -- Cut against split: sawn precision versus wedge fracture

Two different divisions are kept apart in the work: the saw makes a precise, calculated cut, while the wedge makes a rough, seldom-straight fracture. The watchable behaviour is sawn faces reading as flat, exact planes and split faces reading as torn, granular breaks; in Vier Jahreszeiten the two meet in one panel, circular-saw traces beside wedge-opened cracks, and in Dolomit zugeschnitten the cut alone governs, a long low slab with sawn edges laid flat in the landscape.

**Takeaway:** keep the saw and the wedge apart; precision against fracture is the contrast. [C27585]

## M4 -- The removed centre: a worked void inside the block

A section of the block is taken out and the interior is exposed: in the Bellingham Untitled the centre section was removed to reveal a polished granite cavity inside an otherwise rough, quarry-left block, and in the Rueckriem-Altar the interior of the altar base was gilded. The watchable behaviour is the block reading as a sentinel with a smooth hollow at its core, rough outside against polished inside; the void is the one place the hand has finished the stone, and it draws the eye into the mass instead of across it.

**Takeaway:** take the centre out and finish only the hollow; the void is the work. [C27586]

## M5 -- The repeated identical unit, each split differently

The same cut size is repeated across many blocks -- eight limestones each 150 x 50 x 50 cm (1988), twenty steles each 3.50 x 1 x 1 m (1995), forty cuboids each 120 x 120 cm in plan (1998) -- and each unit carries its own split seams. The watchable behaviour is sameness of size making the small differences of each split visible; the row or field reads as one system built from near-identical parts, and the eye compares unit to unit instead of taking in a single mass.

**Takeaway:** repeat the size exactly and let each split differ; the comparison is the composition. [C27587]

## M6 -- The ground plane: stone laid flat and walked on

The work drops to floor level: a low wide slab only about 11 cm high against a 178 cm square plan (Untitled, 1979, MoMA), a 579 cm low granite beam (Chicago, 1987), a long cut slab laid in the landscape (Muenster, 1977), forty floor cuboids (Berlin, 1998), and two floor reliefs set into the Reichstag courtyard paving. The watchable behaviour is the viewer walking beside, over and around the work rather than facing it; the stone is underfoot, read from above, and its weight is spread across the floor instead of raised on a base.

**Takeaway:** lay the stone flat and let the viewer walk on the sculpture. [C27588]

## M7 -- The stele and the upright block as sentinel and axis

Single upright blocks stand as markers: the documenta 7 granite block beside the Neue Galerie making a visual axis (1982), the 475 cm tall Montreal slab (1984), the three split monoliths closing a gap in a hedgerow at Muenster (1987), the twenty steles in the fields near Abiego (1995), and the seven tall slabs marking a former railway line at Outwood (1999). The watchable behaviour is the upright reading as a marker in space, a point that organises the ground around it; from a distance the Abiego group closes into a continuous wall, and as the viewer approaches the columns open and move apart.

**Takeaway:** stand one block up and let it organise the ground around it. [C27589]

## M8 -- The wall relief as a flat elevation drawing

Four approximately square panels of shallow depth (Vier Jahreszeiten, 1986), each divided from an upwardly tapering wedge form into six or seven parts and reassembled, hang as wall reliefs. The watchable behaviour is the panels reading at once as massive volumes and as flat elevation drawings; the linear incisions make a rhythm across the rust-brown, spotted surface, a grid of openings over a heavy body, so the wall piece is both drawing and weight.

**Takeaway:** hang the split block on the wall; let it read as drawing and as weight at once. [C27590]

## M9 -- The combinatory field: a grid law, not a composition

The placement of many units follows a stated rule rather than a composed arrangement: the twenty steles of Siglo XX are ordered by a combinatory law of infinite possibilities, analogous to the eight queens puzzle, that prevents the crossing of their axes; the forty cuboids of 40 Bodenreliefs answer the strict 120 x 120 cm grid of Mies van der Rohe's floor panels in a loose chessboard form. The watchable behaviour is entering the group turning the space into an interior (Abiego), and the rough unhewn tops of the cuboids breaking the grid rhythm and making the viewer suddenly aware of the right angles in the floor, glass walls and ceiling (Berlin).

**Takeaway:** state the placement rule and let the rule compose; the grid is the author. [C27591]

## M10 -- Place as part of the work: the quarry as studio

Rueckriem works from mostly cuboid blanks selected in the quarry, treats the quarry as his studio and the place as part of the work, and sets each piece against its site: the Muenster slab as counterpoint to the Petrikirche (1977), the Kassel block as counterpoint to the classicist pavilion (1982), the Muenster blocks along an arbitrarily determined vista (1987), the Abiego steles beside a cattle trail in front of an almond forest (1995), the Outwood stones re-marking a derelict colliery (1999), and the Hildesheim altar fixed above a saint's shrine, unmovable elsewhere (2014). The watchable behaviour is the work that cannot be read without its ground; the stone answers the building, the hedgerow, the track or the shrine it stands beside.

**Takeaway:** choose the site first; the stone only means something against its ground. [C27592]

## Aesthetic logic

Rueckriem's logic is subtraction by division: nothing is added to the stone and nothing is carved away into a picture; the sculpture is the block divided and returned to itself, with the division left legible. The quarry is the studio, the block is the drawing, and the finished work is a record of its own making -- saw lines, wedge breaks and boreholes are the ornament, and the only polish is a chosen contrast, the blue-grey reflecting middle slab against rusty-grey granite in the Reichstag courtyard or the polished cavity against rough outer stone at Bellingham. Scale is the variable, not the method: the same split-cut-reassemble operation runs from a 95 cm dolomite block to a 7.6 m stele and a 20 m field of steles, and the meaning shifts with the site, from quarry fragment to city square to cathedral altar. Against Walther's fabric that holds nothing until two people pull it taut between them, Rueckriem's answer is a mass that holds everything by itself and asks nothing of the viewer but to walk around it and read the seams.
