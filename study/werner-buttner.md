Buttner is the painter who arrived at painting sideways and then made the sideways arrival his subject. Born in Jena in 1954, he emigrated from the German Democratic Republic to West Germany with his parents shortly before the Berlin Wall was built in 1961, and grew up in Munich and Berlin. He moved to Berlin in 1968, studied law at the Freie Universitat Berlin from 1973 to 1977 -- he later said he did only three semesters, and the discrepancy is unresolved -- and after law worked as a social worker at Berlin-Tegel Prison. He never attended art school; he is a self-taught painter who began to paint at Albert Oehlen's insistence. The prison, the law books and the lack of academy training are not biography decoration: they are the reason his pictures read like verdicts handed down by a man who never joined the court. [C26462]

He met Albert Oehlen in Berlin in the mid-1970s, in the shared flat where Oehlen lived, and the two talked about art for about three years before starting to work together. In 1977 they moved to Hamburg together, where Oehlen had been accepted to study with Sigmar Polke at the Academy -- though another account has Oehlen moving to Berlin in 1977 and working as a waiter and decorator with Buttner, and the two versions are not reconciled in the sources read. Together with Oehlen he founded the Liga zur Bekampfung des widerspruchlichen Verhaltens (League Against Contradictory Behavior) in 1976, per ZKM, though German Wikipedia ties its founding to a 1978 mural for the Hamburg bookshop "Welt" and a charge over pornographic depictions. He met Martin Kippenberger in Hamburg after the move, and in 1979 exhibited in Kippenberger's Buro project as part of the group show "Elend" in Berlin. A third account, the artnet first-person version, has the three meeting in Berlin around 1979 and singing a German miner's hymn in wool suits at a small Cologne gallery in 1983 -- the trio's meeting place and date are genuinely unsettled, and the "Hamburg trio" and "Cologne trio" names are both loose, since the men met in Berlin and Hamburg and were active together in Cologne. [C26463]

What bound the three was not simply a shared figurative style but a shared stance: a systematic inversion of 1968 slogans that had become empty phrases, expressed through cynical irony, corny jokes and philosophical depth, and a defiant return to painting after conceptual art and minimalism had declared painting dead. Their approach became known as "Bad Painting": deliberately clumsy handling, unpretentious materials and everyday narratives, used as a critique of German postwar complacency rather than as mere neo-expressionist release. The ZKM account stresses that the three did not really belong to the New Wild painters, who simply wanted to let rip free of conceptualist theory; Buttner, Oehlen and Kippenberger kept an ironic, literary and conceptual edge that set them apart from the wider neo-expressionist flood. The provocations followed the stance: in 1980 Buttner started a Samenbank fur DDR-Fluchtlinge (Sperm Bank for Refugees from the GDR), an invented institution, and in 1981 he founded the Kirche der Ununterschiedlichkeit (Church of Non-Difference / Church of Indifference) with Albert and Markus Oehlen. Key joint landmarks were the Martin-Gropius-Bau survey "Zeitgeist" in Berlin in 1982 and "Wahrheit ist Arbeit" (Truth Is Work) at the Museum Folkwang, Essen, in 1984, a book and exhibition the trio treated as a manifesto; a later trio survey, "Buttner, Kippenberger and Oehlen: Trio Infernal", was shown at Thomas Ammann Fine Art, Zurich, in 2020. His first solo exhibition was with Galerie Max Hetzler in 1981, and his 1980-81 self-portrait masturbating in a cinema became his early scandal and calling card. [C26464]

The teaching career is the long second half of the story. Buttner was appointed professor of painting at the Hochschule fur bildende Kunste Hamburg in 1989, and retired in autumn 2021 after more than thirty years of teaching painting there; the Hamburger Kunsthalle marked the retirement with his solo exhibition "Last Lecture Show", conceived in close collaboration with the artist and the university. His best-documented pupil is Daniel Richter, listed as his pupil by the Stadel Museum and recorded as studying with Buttner in Hamburg from 1992 to 1996, while also working as Albert Oehlen's assistant. Jonathan Meese is repeatedly described in Buttner profiles as a former student Buttner helped steer, but Meese's own gallery biographies say he studied under Franz Erhard Walther in Hamburg in the late 1990s, so the Meese link is not corroborated here and is treated as unverified. [C26465]

The work's evolution runs from mud to light. In the 1980s Buttner painted gesturally in brown hues, in a muddy palette of greys and browns, working alla prima (wet-on-wet) in rapid, impasto brushstrokes, with isolated splashes, drips and threads of paint and a heavy use of black as a recognisable signature. Since the 1990s, predominantly concrete forms and bright colours have determined his pictorial language, and in the later works the fast early technique gives way to a more precise painterly style with greater intellectual and visual subtlety. His subjects stayed wide throughout: still lifes, self-portraits, animal pictures, seascapes, history painting and religious subjects, reworked with linguistic elements into ironic commentaries on society and the human condition. He calls himself a text-image strategist: he begins each day with a morning newspaper, and the trenchant, often ironic titles are essential to the work, as in the long drawing series "Schrecken der Demokratie" (Disasters of Democracy), begun in 1979 and ended in 1999. Since about 2000 he has also worked extensively in collage, including neo-Dadaist collages and interventions into found paintings bought at flea markets. The most comprehensive retrospective to date was "Werner Buttner: Gemeine Wahrheiten" (Common/Cruel Truths) at ZKM Karlsruhe, 6 April to 22 September 2013, with around 300 paintings, drawings, collages and sculptures, which then travelled to the Weserburg Museum of Contemporary Art, Bremen, in 2014. Recent solo exhibitions after retirement include "Malerei 1981-2022" at Galerie Max Hetzler, Berlin, 23 June to 19 August 2023, his tenth solo show with that gallery, and two 2025 shows in China: "Fool, Leaving the Shelter of Seclusion" at Yuz Museum, Shanghai, 22 March to 22 June 2025, and "He, Who Does Not Thrash Painting, Hates Painting!" at Triumph Gallery, Beijing, 2025. His work is held by, among others, the Museum of Modern Art, New York; the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York; the Stadel Museum, Frankfurt; the Hamburger Kunsthalle; ZKM Karlsruhe; the Museum Moderner Kunst Stiftung Ludwig, Vienna; the Cincinnati Art Museum; and Harvard Art Museums. In 2011 he received the Hans-Platschek-Preis fur Kunst und Schrift, and in 2019 he founded the Stiftung Storer des Stumpfsinns (Foundation for Disturbers of the Whole Shit) to offer residencies in his house and studio in Geesthacht near Hamburg, where he lives and works. With solo exhibitions in 2025 and no obituary or death notice found, he is living as of November 2026. [C26466]

## M1 -- The title as the picture's second half

Buttner calls himself a text-image strategist, and the title is not a label stuck on afterwards; it is half the mechanism. "Die Kuh, die den blauen Pullover gefressen hat" (The Cow That Ate the Blue Pullover, 1983) tells you the joke before you see the cow; "Die Probleme des Minigolfs in der europaischen Malerei" (The Problems of Minigolf in European Painting, 1982/83) turns a garden game into an art-historical problem; "Ist Humor ein Konzept der Natur?" (Is Humour a Concept of Nature?, 1992) asks a philosophy question the canvas then has to answer. The titles are trenchant and often ironic, drawn from the morning newspaper he reads before painting, and they do the work a caption would do in a cartoon: they set the frame, and the picture plays inside it. The long drawing series "Schrecken der Demokratie" (Disasters of Democracy, 1979-1999) runs the same mechanism for twenty years -- the title is a standing headline, and each drawing is one day's evidence under it.

**Takeaway:** a title that argues is a second picture; give the piece its headline first, then let the image prosecute it. [C26467]

## M2 -- Bad Painting: deliberate clumsiness as critique

The early handling is the mechanism stated in paint: alla prima, wet-on-wet, rapid impasto brushstrokes in a muddy palette of greys and browns, with isolated splashes, drips and threads of paint and a heavy use of black as a recognisable signature. The clumsiness is deliberate -- unpretentious materials, everyday narratives, a refusal of finish -- and it is aimed at German postwar complacency, not at the viewer. The point is that a painting can be badly made on purpose and still be exact: the rough brushwork in the 1980 self-portrait (ochre, grey and black, rough visible strokes, a man in a cinema seat staring out) is the joke landing, not a failure of craft. The ironic, literary, conceptual edge is what separates this from the wider New Wild letting-rip; the badness is a position, argued in paint.

**Takeaway:** clumsiness is a tool only when it is chosen; make the roughness do the arguing, and keep it legible enough that the joke lands. [C26468]

## M3 -- The absent figure

Buttner's sharpest device is to paint the person by painting what is left of them. In "Badende Russen II" (Bathing Russians II, 1982, oil on canvas, 150 x 190 cm) no soldiers are visible at all: two pairs of dark red boots stand upright on an ochre-brown beach beside folded dark uniforms with white belts and caps, under a black stormy sky with white breaking waves. Buttner explains the soldiers have gone swimming, and the German phrase "baden gehen" -- to go swimming, and to fail -- points at the Soviet Union's coming collapse. The same metonymy runs "Stalin" (1985), a history painting in which, per Hetzler, "only the dictator's supposed boots are depicted". The figure is absent; the boots, the uniform, the folded cap carry the whole charge, and the viewer's mind supplies the body the painter refused to draw.

**Takeaway:** leave the figure out and paint its leavings; absence makes the viewer do the work, and the work is the point. [C26469]

## M4 -- Muddy palette to bright concrete forms

The colour story is a two-act arc, and the hinge is the 1990s. The 1980s pictures sit in ochre, grey and brown with black as signature; the later works are predominantly concrete forms in bright colours, painted more precisely, with greater intellectual and visual subtlety. The viewed reproductions show the hinge: "Singing Men" (2005, oil on canvas, 190 x 190 cm) is four long-haired men in flat golden-orange against a black-and-grey cloudy ground with a red strip along the bottom; "Herodotus and Thucydides, Storytellers" (2018, oil on canvas, 190 x 150 cm) sets pinkish bearded heads and small dark-red petroglyph figures on a pale yellow ground under a mottled olive-green mass; "On Thrownness and Entanglement" (2017, oil on canvas, 190 x 240 cm) runs a bright blue sky band with birds across the top and an olive-green ground band along the bottom. The palette did not just brighten; the forms hardened into flat, legible shapes, so the later pictures read like posters for jokes the early pictures mumbled.

**Takeaway:** let the palette change with the argument -- mud for the grumble, flat bright colour for the verdict. [C26470]

## M5 -- Found-painting intervention and collage

Since about 2000 Buttner has worked extensively in collage, including neo-Dadaist collages and interventions into found paintings bought at flea markets. The mechanism is visible whole in "Grande Toilette with Little Snake" (2007, oil on found painting and frame, 71 x 59 cm): a woman with long brown hair and a red top, arms raised to her hair, stands at a blue tabletop; a vase of pink roses sits to her left and a small orange-and-black banded snake lies across the table in the foreground; the found painting sits in a wide pale wooden frame with a small brass title plate. The artist's modification is laid over somebody else's finished picture, and the frame and the title plate -- the apparatus of respectability -- are part of the joke. The found painting supplies the earnestness; Buttner supplies the snake.

**Takeaway:** start from somebody else's finished picture and add one wrong thing; the respectability of the frame makes the intrusion funnier. [C26471]

## M6 -- The self-portrait as provocation

Buttner introduces himself the way he introduces everything else: with a joke the audience cannot unsee. "Self-Portrait Masturbating at the Cinema" (1980, oil on canvas, 150 x 115 cm) shows a seated man in a dark blue shirt and grey trousers staring out at the viewer from a cinema seat, his right hand in his lap inside a yellow paper bag, a large black shadow figure looming behind him to the left, in ochre, grey and black with rough visible brushwork. It became his early scandal and calling card, and it sets the template: the self-portrait is not a likeness but a confession staged as a gag. The template repeats in three dimensions in "Smoking Brain (Sort of Self-Portrait)" (1983, wood, brass, paper, wire, 54 x 34 x 34.5 cm): a dark-stained wooden cage with chicken-wire mesh panels, and inside, on the cage floor, a dark charred-looking rock or lump from which a small brass tube rises holding a crumpled white paper form. The brain smokes; the man is a caged lump with a tube.

**Takeaway:** introduce yourself with the joke you cannot take back; a self-portrait that embarrasses is one the viewer remembers. [C26472]

## M7 -- History painting by metonymy

Buttner paints history the way he paints people: by the objects history leaves behind. "Stalin" (1985) is a history painting of boots only. "Kantine Bautzen" (Bautzen Canteen, 1993, oil on canvas, 150 x 120 cm) takes its title from the town of the GDR's most notorious political prison and lets the canteen stand for the institution. "Herodotus and Thucydides, Storytellers" (2018) puts two large back-to-back bearded heads in pinkish flesh tones above a dark red strip, with small dark-red petroglyph-style figures -- hunters, horsemen, spear carriers -- scattered on a pale yellow ground and three dark-red boats with stick figures on a mottled olive-green mass above: the fathers of history rendered as cave painting, history as a story told in stick figures. The mechanism is the same as M3 at a larger scale: the event is never shown, only its emblems, and the emblems are asked to carry the weight.

**Takeaway:** never paint the event; paint the boots, the canteen, the boats -- the emblem is the history, and it is funnier for being small. [C26473]

## M8 -- The sculpture as a joke object

The sign system was never only paint, and the sculptures are jokes built from scrap. "Smoking Brain" (1983) is wood, brass, paper and wire: a cage, a charred lump, a brass tube, a crumpled paper form -- a brain that smokes, built like a school science project. The materials are common and unpretentious, the construction is simple and spontaneous, and the humour comes from the literalness: a smoking brain is a cage with something burning in it. The ZKM retrospective's 300 works included sculptures alongside paintings, drawings and collages, so the object-making runs through the whole career rather than sitting at its edge.

**Takeaway:** build the joke as an object and the joke gets heavier; a literal sculpture of a metaphor is funnier than the metaphor alone. [C26474]

## M9 -- The singing group and the choir portrait

Buttner paints groups as choirs, and the choir is a self-portrait of the trio. "Singing Men" (2005) shows four long-haired men standing in a row, full-length, in matching high-collared suits, the third with arms crossed, the fourth pointing toward the viewer -- a group portrait staged as a performance. The motif reaches back to the trio itself: in a small Cologne gallery in 1983, Buttner, Oehlen and Kippenberger, in wool suits, sang a German miner's hymn to alienate the room. The singing is the mechanism: a group of men, dressed alike, performing in unison, and the performance is both sincere and a wind-up. The same row-of-men construction returns in the petroglyph hunters of "Herodotus and Thucydides" and in the folded uniforms of "Bathing Russians II" -- men as a repeated unit, a chorus line of history.

**Takeaway:** a row of matching men is a chorus; dress them alike, line them up, and let one of them break the unison. [C26475]

## M10 -- Late return to the hometown

The late work turns back to Jena, and the return is literal: a postcard blown up to the size of a painting. "On Thrownness and Entanglement" (2017, oil on canvas, 190 x 240 cm) shows, at right, a small child riding a black pony; the centre is an enlarged old engraved townscape of Jena, labelled "Jena", on an ochre ground; a bare blue-grey tree stands at left; a bright blue sky band with birds runs across the top and an olive-green ground band along the bottom. Buttner says the picture shows him aged two on a pony against a blown-up postcard of his hometown Jena. After forty years of ironic distance -- the slogans inverted, the dictators reduced to boots, the found paintings invaded -- the late picture puts the child back in the town he left before the Wall went up, and the irony softens into something close to tenderness, without ever dropping the joke: the town is a postcard, the child is two, and the title is Heidegger.

**Takeaway:** the last move of an ironist is the sincere one; let the late picture go home, and let the hometown arrive as a postcard. [C26476]

## Aesthetic logic

Buttner's logic is a headline before it is a picture. He begins each day with a morning newspaper, and the picture is the day's news processed through a painter who never went to art school, studied law, worked in a prison, and learned to paint from a friend who insisted on it. The title argues, the muddy early palette grumbles, the bright late palette delivers the verdict, and the figure is usually absent -- boots, uniforms, a cage, a postcard -- because the emblem is funnier and truer than the body. The Bad Painting clumsiness is not a lack of skill but a refusal of the polished surface that German postwar painting used to pretend nothing had happened; the joke is the critique, and the critique is aimed at complacency, at slogans gone empty, at dictators and at art itself. The trio is the human hinge of the whole story: three painters in wool suits singing a miner's hymn to alienate a room, founding leagues and churches that never existed, treating "Truth Is Work" as a manifesto. The early work codifies the stance under the mud, the middle work lets the joke harden into bright concrete forms, and the late work proves the stance by going home -- a child on a pony in front of a postcard of Jena, with a philosophy title overhead. [C26477]
