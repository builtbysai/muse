# Maurice-Quentin de La Tour: Study Notes

**Site:** Maurice-Quentin de La Tour (Friday 5 September 1704 - Sunday 17 February 1788), French pastellist -- born at Saint-Quentin, in Picardy, the third son of Francois de La Tour, musician and cantor at the collegiate church of Saint-Quentin; apprenticed to Claude Dupouch in Paris from October 1719; made his Salon debut in 1737 with the laughing self-portrait and the portrait of Madame Boucher; was agree by the Academie royale on Saturday 25 May 1737, received as a member on Saturday 24 September 1746 with a portrait of Restout, named conseiller on Saturday 27 March 1751, and held a logement in the Louvre from Wednesday 10 March 1745; was portraitist to the king from 1750 until 1773; exhibited about 120 pastels at the Paris Salons between 1737 and 1773, more pastels and more portraits at the official Salons than any other eighteenth-century artist; retired to Saint-Quentin in 1784, was interdicted for mental incapacity on Saturday 15 January 1785, and died intestate aged 83, his wills revoked. He worked in pastel and in nothing else -- no oil, no miniature, no prints -- and in portraiture and in nothing else. The signature works are the Autoportrait a l'index of Salon 1737 (Louvre), the President de Rieux of 1739-41 (Getty, 200.7 x 149.9 cm, sixteen sheets), Marie Leczinska of Salon 1748 (Louvre), the Self-Portrait au jabot of c.1750-51 (Musee de Picardie, Amiens), Mlle Ferrand meditating on Newton of 1752 (Munich), the Rousseau of 1753 (Geneva), the d'Alembert of 1753 (Louvre), and the full-length Madame de Pompadour of 1752-55 (Louvre). The modern record is Neil Jeffares's Dictionary of Pastellists Before 1800 entry and online catalogue raisonne (2022, updated 11 November 2026), the authority used throughout; Xavier Salmon's monographs (2004, 2024); and the Musee Antoine-Lecuyer at Saint-Quentin, which holds the studio fund his brother bequeathed to the town. ## M1. The Preparation -- The Face First, the Body Later, the Study Often Better

The preparation is the core of La Tour's practice, and it is a mechanism, not a sketch. A preparation is a rapid pastel study, generally in a series, made to find the framing and the lighting that best flatter the subject: the head alone, in black chalk and pastel on bare buff paper, floating with no body, no background, no context. He made only one version of each stage of his preparations, and he called them etudes himself; the word "preparation" in this sense was first used by the Goncourts, anticipated by Champfleury in 1855. Most preparations are smaller than the finished portrait; a handful are full-scale or unfinished portraits in their own right. The sitting that produces them is fast and conversational: Belle de Zuylen came each morning to Groenesteyn for a three-hour session, La Tour keeping her attention with lively, witty talk, and after a month he was still struggling with the eyes, abandoned the first version and made another. The first preparation for her, which only came to light in 2015, shows bold horizontal and vertical folds, suggesting he juxtaposed several preparations to combine the successful features of each. The Saint-Quentin collection -- his studio fund, bequeathed to the town by his brother -- holds the largest group, and their legendary status comes largely from this survival: fewer than 300 of the roughly 1,900 catalogue numbers are universally accepted as fully autograph, and that includes numerous preparations. The watchable behaviour is the head arriving before the picture: a face caught in conversation, still warm from the sitting, with the rest of the portrait not yet decided -- and the study is often more alive than the finished portrait, because the finished portrait has to cool down into a likeness while the preparation only has to stay hot. **Takeaway:** a portrait that begins as a face alone on bare paper, found by talking, and the study is the work -- the finish is the cooling of it.

## M2. The Yellow-Ochre Ground -- A Wash That Kills the Blue and Starves the Mould

La Tour's paper was usually blue -- a light blue-grey from indigo -- and blue paper is a problem: it shows through, and covering it opaquely takes so much pastel that the salt in the pastel material attracts mould. His answer, described in his letter to Belle de Charriere of Saturday 14 April 1770, was a light wash of yellow ochre in water, well mixed with a little egg yolk, brushed over the blue paper before he began: "mettre avec une brosse une legere teinture d'ocre jaune a l'eau simple, bien delayee ensemble avec un peu de jaune d'oeuf sur du papier bleu; cela empeche le lourd qu'il est difficile d'eviter par la quantite de couleurs necessaires pour couvrir le bleu du papier." One stroke does three jobs: it kills the show-through of the blue, it cuts the amount of pastel needed to cover the paper opaquely, and by cutting the pastel it starves the mould-attracting salt. The preparation with yellow ochre and egg yolk has been found in several works, including the Belle de Charriere preparation, the finished princesse de Rohan and the Laideguive. He also used the same wash to remove salt traces from chalks, using a knife and even a hot iron passed close to the pastel. The ground is economy, chemistry and conservation in one brushstroke, and it is invisible in the finished work -- which is the point: the mechanism that makes the portrait possible is the one the viewer never sees. The watchable behaviour is the paper warming under the pigment: a blue ground turned to a warm ochre field, so the flesh sits on warmth instead of fighting cold blue, and the portrait stays fresh because the ground was thrifty with salt. **Takeaway:** a ground that is a wash, not a layer -- yellow ochre and egg yolk over blue paper, killing show-through, cutting the pastel, and starving the mould, all before the first stroke of the face.

## M3. The Pieced Monument -- Oil Scale from Sheets the Size of a Book

Pastel was supposed to be small and fragile, and La Tour made it monumental by refusing to accept the size of a sheet of paper. The largest readily available sheet was about 70 x 50 cm, so he glued sheets edge to edge, hiding the joins along drapery and furniture lines, and built portraits nearly two metres high. The President de Rieux (1739-41, Salon 1741) is pastel on sixteen sheets of paper mounted on canvas, 200.7 x 149.9 cm -- the Getty calls it the largest pastel made in the eighteenth century -- a full-length seated magistrate in his red robe with a whole room of possessions around him: library, globe turned to West Africa, imported porcelain, fine furniture. The Madame de Pompadour (1752-55) is pastel on seven sheets of blue paper mounted on canvas, nearly two metres high, sealed under a glass sheet of unprecedented size. He made a particular practice of working the head on a smaller sheet for convenience, then pasting it in -- in the Pompadour, several layers of paper were applied over a hole in the canvas -- and the joins were disguised by a small overlap and kept off the face. The piecing is a manifesto: pastel can rival oil at oil's own scale, in oil's own rooms, and the fragility everyone cites is a property of the handling, not of the medium. The watchable behaviour is the seam: a portrait so large it cannot be one sheet, whose joins run along the lines of the composition like the seams of a garment, and whose head was drawn separately and pasted in, so the face is literally a piece of paper laid onto the picture. **Takeaway:** scale as a manifesto -- a portrait built from book-sized sheets glued along the composition's own lines, proving a "fragile" medium can stand two metres tall.

## M4. The Rotated Sheet -- Turning the Paper to Defeat Its Own Lines

All paper was handmade on frames, leaving chain and laid lines, and La Tour rubbed them down, leaving an irregular network of loose fibres excellent for holding pastel -- but the horizontal chain lines remained, a mechanical grid running through every portrait. His answer, in at least eighteen autograph examples, was to rotate the sheet: wedge-shaped additions to the strainer turn the paper 3 to 10 degrees, so the chain lines run diagonally and the eye stops reading them as a grid. Villars was rotated clockwise about 3 degrees, Madame Rouille the opposite way, Marie Fel about 10 degrees clockwise (the chair back is no longer horizontal), an attributed inconnue known as Madame de Stael about 7 degrees. Jeffares argues this was deliberate: La Tour felt the normally horizontal paper chain lines were a visual distraction, and the rotation was his way of defeating them -- the same motive as the yellow-ochre coat against show-through. The support itself becomes a compositional decision: the picture is tilted on purpose, a few degrees off level, so that the material stops announcing itself. The watchable behaviour is the almost-level: a portrait that looks straight until you notice the chair back is not horizontal, the horizon is not level, and the whole image is leaning a few degrees -- the paper turned so its own lines cannot be seen. **Takeaway:** a portrait tilted 3 to 10 degrees on purpose, so the paper's own chain lines stop reading as a grid -- the support turned into a compositional decision.

## M5. The Secret Fixative and the Glass Sandwich -- A Varnish That Spoils, a Skin of Glass

Pastel falls off. La Tour spent his career trying to fix it, and the trying is the mechanism. As early as 1745 the Mercure critic reported he had "eu le bonheur de trouver un vernis qui sans alterer en rien la fraicheur et la fleur de son Pastel, le fixe de facon que l'ebranlement le plus violent ne le peut deranger" -- found a varnish that fixes the pastel without altering its freshness, so that the most violent shaking cannot disturb it. Petit de Bachaumont, 1750, gives the other side: "[La Tour] s'est entete d'un vernis qu'il croit avoir invente, et qui tres-souvent luy gaste tout ce qu'il a fait" -- he is infatuated with a varnish he believes he invented, which very often spoils everything he has made. Dossie, 1764, notes the methods "are at present kept as close secrets". The visual evidence is on the works themselves: water and tide marks on the autoportrait a la toque d'atelier and other Saint-Quentin works indicate local fixing, and microscopic examination of the Garnier d'Isle shows intermediate layers of fixative used as a working tool, to isolate parts of the drawing as he proceeded. Investigation of the princesse de Rohan revealed sturgeon glue, suggesting he had discovered the essential ingredient of Loriot's fixing technique far earlier than thought -- unless the fixative was added in a later restoration, which is not resolved. When varnish failed, he resorted to sandwiching his pastels between two sheets of glass, sealed together, the backing sheet thick (about 1.5 cm) and extremely heavy: at least two works use or used the system, Jean Monnet (1756) and Lord Coventry (1752), both originally on paper maroufled onto strained canvas, the frame removed and the flattened supports placed between two sheets of glass with enough convexity to avoid contact between pastel and front glass. Gautier-Dagoty, on the Pompadour in 1755: "c'est, dit-on, la glace qui a cet avantage; elle met tout d'accord" -- it is, they say, the glass that has this advantage; it brings everything into agreement. The watchable behaviour is the sheen: a pastel that looks too even, too agreed, because there is glass between you and it -- and the glass itself is the fixative, the medium's second skin. **Takeaway:** a medium that will not stay put, fixed by a secret varnish that often spoils the work, and then by glass itself -- the picture sealed between two sheets, the glass doing the agreeing the pastel cannot do.

## M6. The Laughing Self-Portrait -- The Index Point That Goes Sideways

The 1737 Autoportrait a l'index is the first self-portrait La Tour ever exhibited, and it is a joke told in the language of portraiture. He shows himself laughing, leaning on a window -- a la croisee -- with his index finger pointing to the left, toward something outside the frame that the viewer cannot see. The name derives from the pointing finger, and the portrait was exhibited at the Salon of 1737 beside the portrait of Madame Boucher, his public debut pair. It is lit from the right, suggesting a mirror was used, while almost all his portraits of other sitters are lit from the left. The Democritus reading -- the laughing philosopher, the counterpart to Heraclitus weeping -- was not published until ten years after the portrait was exhibited, and the finger has been read a dozen ways since: Diderot claimed it was a practical joke against the rival Perronneau, but that fails on dates, since Perronneau's first Salon was 1746; the Toledo reading has him pointing toward the studio door, after the anecdote of La Tour locking his studio door on a visiting abbe and watching from the window, grinning. What matters is the direction: the point goes sideways, to one side, an elegant aside -- not at the viewer. Sixteen or more versions of the composition exist, and he repeated the gesture into old age (a late repetition of c.1770, Louvre). The watchable behaviour is the aside: a laughing man who points at something you cannot see, so you keep looking where he points and finding nothing -- and the portrait stays open, because the thing pointed at is never in the picture. Ducreux, a generation later, takes the same finger and turns it straight at the viewer, and that turn is the whole difference between the master and the pupil who may never have been his pupil. **Takeaway:** a self-portrait that laughs and points sideways, at something outside the frame -- the gesture that stays open because what it points at is never shown, and that Ducreux later turns straight at the viewer.

## M7. The Close Sitting -- Two Feet Away, Talking, While the Face Forgets Itself

La Tour worked a couple of feet from his sitters -- his letter of Sunday 1 August 1762 to Marigny says so, presumably because of myopia -- and that distance is the method. Three-hour morning sessions, as with Belle de Zuylen, and the talk is the instrument: "tandis qu'il ne semble occupe qu'a saisir la ressemblance de ses modeles, sa conversation vive, animee, spirituelle, charme l'ennui de l'attitude, et l'ame est peinte sur la toile avec autant d'energie que les traits du visage" -- while he seems occupied only with catching the likeness, his lively, animated, witty conversation charms away the boredom of the pose, and the soul is painted with as much energy as the features. Diderot, who watched him work (Salon de 1767): "J'ai vu peindre La Tour, il est tranquille et froid; il ne se tourmente point; il ne souffre point, il ne se halete point... il reste froid, et cependant son imitation est chaude" -- I have seen La Tour paint, he is calm and cold; he does not torment himself, does not suffer, does not pant... he stays cold, and yet his imitation is warm. The closeness had a cost: it caused him perspective problems, and the disturbing effect that sitters' eyes no longer seemed to look in the same direction. The clothes and accessories were added later in the studio, after the sitter left, often to formula -- which is why the same leaning composition recurs (Ferrand, Madame de Mondonville, Marie Fel) while the faces stay individual. The watchable behaviour is the face caught while the talk distracts: a sitter who has forgotten to pose, because the painter is two feet away and talking, and the portrait is the face at the moment it stops performing. **Takeaway:** a sitting done at two feet, in conversation, so the sitter forgets to pose -- the face caught warm while the painter stays cold, and the body added later in the studio.

## M8. The Open-Eyed Smile -- Defying de Piles, Changing with Distance

Roger de Piles had ruled that when the sitter puts on a smiling air, the eyes close. La Tour defied him: his smiles keep the eyes open, and Kenneth Clark called the result the "smile of reason". The mechanism is optical as well as expressive. In the Duval de l'Epinoy (1745), two top front teeth are virtually invisible in reproduction, and the expression changes with distance: at about one metre it shifts from wry and quizzical to pure pleasure. The smile is the hardest transient expression to catch, and he catches it by working fast, close up, and in conversation -- the same method as M7, aimed at the one expression a posed sitter cannot hold. His portrait of Chardin shows extreme bold hatching a decade before Chardin's own pastels, and the finish varies by sitter, from smooth "caressee" (duc de Villars) to bold hatching. The watchable behaviour is the smile that will not sit still: a face whose expression changes as you step closer, whose teeth appear and disappear with distance, and whose eyes stay open against the rule that says they must close. **Takeaway:** a smile that breaks the rule -- eyes open, teeth that appear with distance, an expression that changes as the viewer steps closer, because it was caught fast and close, not posed.

## M9. The Robin Hood Fee Scale -- The Rich Pay for the Poor

La Tour's fees were a system, and the system was a politics. The standard tariff, in Tournehem's letter of 13 May 1747: a bust 1,500 livres, knee-length 2,500, full-length grand portraits 4,000. Prince Charles Edward Stuart, 1748: 1,200 livres. The future Louis XVI, 1762: estimated 2,400, settled at 3,000 in 1765. For the second Pompadour pastel he demanded the unprecedented 48,000 livres, and about 24,000 was paid. And the same hand charged 200 livres for Laideguive, a concession, and charged Madame de Mondonville double the 25 louis she said she could pay -- 1,200 livres against her 600. Marie Fel's phrase for it: "les riches devoit payer pour les pauvres" -- the rich had to pay for the poor. He could afford the scale: a pension of 1,000 livres a year from the Batiments du roi from 1752 to 1779, two annuities of 6,300 livres a year on capital of 63,000 purchased in 1765 alone, an income of 19,750 livres listed in his 1768 will, inheritances from his brother Charles and from the abbe Huber, and property speculation with Pierre Salles that ended in losses and lawsuits. The watchable behaviour is the price as a portrait of the sitter: the same hand, the same medium, and a fee that says what the court thought the face was worth -- 48,000 livres for a mistress becoming a political figure, 200 for a friend. **Takeaway:** a fee scale that is a politics -- the rich pay the price that lets the poor sit for almost nothing, and the price list is itself a record of who mattered.

## M10. The Overworking Fault -- Tormenting the Pastel Until It Spoils

Pastel does not want to be tormented, and La Tour tormented it anyway. Petit de Bachaumont, 1750: "il ne sait pas s'arreter a propos; il cherche toujours a faire mieux qu'il n'a fait, d'ou il arrive qu'a force de travailler et de tourmenter son ouvrage souvent il le gate... le pastel ne veut pas etre tourmente, trop de travail lui ote sa fleur" -- he does not know when to stop; he always seeks to do better than he has done, so that by dint of working and tormenting his work he often spoils it... pastel does not want to be tormented, too much work takes away its bloom. Diderot, 1782: "Un peintre celebre de nos jours emploie les dernieres annees de sa vie a gater les chefs-d'oeuvre qu'il a produits dans la vigueur de son age" -- a celebrated painter of our day spends the last years of his life spoiling the masterpieces he produced in the vigour of his age. La Tour took it to heart: writing to Belle de Zuylen on 5 March 1770 about repainting Restout, he said he had learned "de ne pas tourmenter les teintes quand elles sont justes" -- not to torment the tones when they are right. He did not always manage it: in old age he ruined his own Restout and Dumont le Romain pastels by retouching them, and the revolutionary inventory of 9 December 1793 notes "Ces deux tableaux sont perdus par l'auteur meme qui, trop vieux, voulut les retoucher" -- these two pictures are lost by the author himself, who, too old, wanted to retouch them. His motto, as reported: "Il faut semer un tableau d'effets et non pas l'en paver" -- sow a picture with effects, do not pave it with them. The watchable behaviour is the bloom going: a surface that was fresh when the sitting ended and dull where the hand went back, the portrait spoiled by the same care that made it, and the artist's own rule -- sow, do not pave -- broken by his own hand in old age. **Takeaway:** a medium that spoils under the hand that loves it -- the bloom lost by retouching, the masterpiece ruined by its own maker, and the rule he stated and broke: sow effects, do not pave them.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Early Saint-Quentin and Paris, to 1737. Born Friday 5 September 1704 at Saint-Quentin, third son of a musician; apprenticed to Dupouch from October 1719 (12 October per Marandet 2002, 16 October per fr.wikipedia -- see C5); turned down by Delaunay and Vernansal in Paris, encouraged by Louis de Boullongne, who told him "Vous ne savez encore ni peindre ni dessiner mais vous possedez un talent qui peut vous mener loin"; his real master in effect was Jean Restout, who taught him "a faire tourner une tete et a faire circuler l'air entre la figure et le fond". Almost nothing survives from his twenties: the earliest known portrait, Voltaire, exists only as a 1731 Langlois engraving, and Jeffares dates the sittings to April 1735 (see C20). The technique is already pastel-only, already fast, already face-first. The 1737 Salon debut is a double statement: a laughing self-portrait that points sideways and a society wife, Madame Boucher. Scale is small, head and shoulders; sitters are himself and the artistic circle.

Phase 2 -- Salon breakthrough, 1737 to the 1750s. The breakthrough is scale and sitter range, not medium. In 1738 Madame Rouille shows the reflected-colour observation (red cloak in white skin) that becomes his signature of close looking. Then the 1739-41 President de Rieux explodes the format: 200.7 x 149.9 cm, sixteen sheets, a full-length seated magistrate with a whole room of possessions -- proof that pastel could match oil at monumental scale. The 1740s add the royal series: Duval de l'Epinoy (1745) at his desk taking snuff, the Dauphin, Louis XV, the Queen, the Marechal de Saxe -- fifteen pastels at the 1748 Salon alone (fourteen per the arthive figure -- see C6). He is agree in 1737, given a Louvre logement in 1745, received into the Academie in 1746, made portraitist to the king in 1750. Sitter type shifts from artists and their wives to the royal family, the noblesse de robe, and the nouveaux riches financiers. Handling: the face is worked fast, close up, talking, while clothes and accessories are added later in the studio, often to formula. The preparations -- small face-only studies in black chalk and pastel on buff paper, the head turned left, right and front -- are produced in series.

Phase 3 -- Maturity, 1750s to 1760s. The 1753 Salon is the peak: an unprecedented eighteen portraits, including Rousseau, d'Alembert, Louis de Silvestre, Nivelle de La Chaussee, Pietro Manelli, and Mlle Ferrand meditating on Newton. The sitter range is now the whole Enlightenment -- philosophes, scientists, singers, salon women -- alongside the court. The Pompadour (1752-55, Salon 1755) is the technical summit: seven sheets, nearly two metres, the largest glass sheet of its day, a full-length portrait as a piece of statecraft for a mistress becoming a political figure. But the mature works also show the formula hardening: Ferrand (c.1752), Madame de Mondonville (c.1752) leaning on her harpsichord, and Marie Fel (c.1757) all use essentially the same leaning three-quarter composition, with the face doing the individual work and the body working to a formula. Prices peak with the work: 48,000 livres demanded for the second Pompadour, about 24,000 paid. After the deaths of the Dauphin (1765) and his widow (1767), royal work ends and output falls off through the 1760s; the 1766 and 1771 Charriere portraits show the late sitter type -- a foreign woman of letters, painted for herself, not for the court.

Phase 4 -- Retirement to Saint-Quentin, 1773-1788. La Tour is last exhibited at the Salon in 1773. From 1776 philanthropy dominates his life: foundations for women in confinement and for old artisans in Saint-Quentin, prizes for the Academie, and a free drawing school in Saint-Quentin -- settled with 6,000 livres in 1778 and perpetual annuities purchased on Thursday 23 January 1783, the school's legal footing dating to 1783, re-established by petition in 1805 after falling into desuetude during the Revolution (see C12). He returns to Saint-Quentin with his half-brother Jean-Francois in 1784, aged 80, is confined to his home under his brother's care, and dies intestate on Sunday 17 February 1788, aged 83. No securely dated work from 1784-88 was found in any source read; the late self-portrait repetitions (a l'index, c.1770, Louvre) are the last datable statements, the 1737 gesture repeated by an old man. His studio contents -- 93 pastels, sketches and finished works, plus the preparations -- passed to the city and form the nucleus of the Musee Antoine-Lecuyer in Saint-Quentin. The Goncourt brothers' 1860s visit to Saint-Quentin revived his reputation, praising exactly the preparations: "The head comes towards you... it comes out of its frame."

## Aesthetic Logic

The whole system is a machine for catching a face before it has time to pose. He starts from the opposite problem to his pupil's: not a miniaturist who found one unit and let the sitters change around it, nor a portraitist who found one transaction and let it change hands, but a pastellist who found one sitting -- a face, two feet away, talked into forgetting itself -- and let everything else be built around it afterwards. The preparation is the thesis: a face alone on bare paper, found by conversation, often better than the finished portrait because the finish has to cool down into a likeness. The ground is the second thesis: a wash of yellow ochre and egg yolk over blue paper, killing show-through and starving the mould, invisible in the finished work and the reason the work can exist at all. The scale is the third: sheets glued along the composition's own lines until a fragile medium stands two metres tall, with the head drawn separately and pasted in. And the smile is the fourth: the hardest transient expression, caught by working fast and close, eyes open against the rule, changing with the viewer's distance. What makes him usable for generative work is not the royal subject -- that is the biography -- but the system underneath it: a portrait that begins as a face alone and gets its body later; a ground that does three invisible jobs; a scale built from book-sized sheets; a gesture that points sideways at something never shown; a fee scale that is a politics; and a medium that spoils under the hand that loves it, so the artist's own rule -- sow effects, do not pave them -- is the warning the work carries with it. And the refusal is the point twice over: he refused to teach, so the "only pupil" is a phrase invented after his death; and he refused to finish, in the sense that the preparation is the finish -- the face caught warm, the rest added later, the picture left open where the finger points.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Pastel and nothing else. The oeuvre is almost entirely pastel portraits plus preparations in chalk, occasionally with some pastel. He did not work in oil or miniature, drew only in chalk, and made no prints. Evidence: Jeffares essay II/III.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] The Salon machine. Debut 1737 with Madame Boucher and the laughing self-portrait; first of about 120 pastels exhibited at the Salons 1737-1773 by Jeffares's count (the "150 portraits" figure is the Britannica/Wikipedia commonplace -- see C9); he exhibited more pastels and more portraits at the official Salons than any other eighteenth-century artist, yet was far less productive than some pastellists; fewer than 300 of about 1,900 catalogue numbers are universally accepted as fully autograph. Evidence: Jeffares essay; en.wikipedia.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] The open-eyed smile. He defied Roger de Piles ("when the sitter puts on a smiling air, the eyes close") -- La Tour leaves the eyes open; Kenneth Clark called it the "smile of reason". The Duval de l'Epinoy trick: two top front teeth are virtually invisible in reproduction, and the expression changes at about 1 metre from wry and quizzical to pure pleasure. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] The agonised preparation. He agonised over the etudes to capture the soul of the sitter and worked decades on unsatisfactory portraits, often to their detriment. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] The paper limit forced the scale. Paper size about 60 x 50 cm forced joined sheets: Marie Fel on five sheets; President de Rieux on 16 sheets (200.7 x 149.9 cm). Evidence: Jeffares essay; Getty/Rieux essay.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The Robin Hood fee scale. Standard bust 1,500 livres (Tournehem letter 13 May 1747: grands 4,000, knee-length 2,500, bust 1,500); Prince Charles Edward Stuart 1748, 1,200 livres; future Louis XVI 1762, 2,400 livres (settled 1765 at 3,000); second Pompadour pastel demanded 48,000 livres, about 24,000 paid; concessionary 200 livres (Laideguive); Madame de Mondonville charged double the 25 louis she could pay (1,200 livres). Marie Fel's phrase for it: "les riches devoit payer pour les pauvres". Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] The court career, then the stop. Agree 25 May 1737; received into the Academie 1746 (reception piece: Restout); conseiller 1751; portraitist to the king 1750, held until 1773; apartment in the Palais du Louvre 1745; pension 1,000 livres a year from the Batiments du roi from 1752 until 1 January 1779. Royal portraiture ceased in the late 1760s. Evidence: Jeffares essay; en.wikipedia.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] The philanthropist. Founded the free drawing school (Ecole Royale Gratuite de Dessin) in Saint-Quentin; donations to impoverished women in confinement and disabled and ageing artisans; advisor and benefactor to the Academie royale and the Academy of Sciences and Belles Lettres of Amiens; donations exceeded 90,000 livres. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] No pupil on paper. Jeffares I.18: "Numerous claims to have been his 'pupil' require sceptical evaluation (and possibly indicate no more than having been granted the privilege of watching the master at work)... not a single document evidencing apprentissage or allouage has been found in the Minutier central or any other archive... No pupil is listed as his student in the manuscript Listes des eleves de l'Academie royale." Evidence: Jeffares essay I.18.
- P10. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The "only pupil" is a nineteenth-century construction. The livret of 1796 calls Ducreux "eleve de Latour" eight years after La Tour's death; the earliest secondary reference to "le seul eleve de Latour" is Arnault 1822; Dinaux 1852 adds Liotard as a second principal pupil; La Tour's second will names virtually every artist he had met but not Ducreux. Inference: the phrase records a studio visit or a borrowed name, not an apprenticeship. Evidence: Jeffares Ducreux entry and essay I.18; #506.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] The finger points sideways. Self-Portrait with Index Finger, Salon 1737: the finger points to the left, toward the studio door, not at the viewer (Christie's; Toledo Museum; Apollo February 2025). Diderot's explanation -- a practical joke against Perronneau -- fails on dates, since Perronneau's first Salon was 1746. Evidence: Jeffares essay; Apollo; Christie's; Toledo; see D2.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] Books, scores, no pets. At least 20 pastels include books or scores (Pompadour, Mondonville, Ferrand with Newton, Marie Fel with her brother's cantatille "Les yeux de l'Amour"); never pet animals apart from dogs. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] The finish varies by sitter. Finish ranged from smooth "caressee" (duc de Villars) to bold hatching; his portrait of Chardin shows extreme bold hatching a decade before Chardin's own pastels; lead white used for brilliance (1757 face highlights, Tronchin). Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P14. [ABSENCE -- stated plainly] No statement on his art survives. No published statement, no treatise, no Encyclopedie article: he promised Diderot an article on painting for the Encyclopedie and never delivered it. His own words survive only in letters and in remarks reported by others. Evidence: Jeffares essay; see Q1.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] Breakdown, retirement, intestacy. Nervous breakdown and mental illness; retired aged 80 to Saint-Quentin (1784), confined to the care of his half-brother Jean-Francois; died intestate 17 February 1788. Evidence: Jeffares essay; en.wikipedia.
- P16. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The laugh is the brand. Sixteen or more versions of the a l'index composition exist; Laura Cumming: "more glad-faced self-portraits than any other artist"; copies by Montjoye, Voiriot and others spread the laughing face. Inference: the self-portrait is not a study but a repeatable device -- the recognisable face of the practice, issued the way a shop issues a sign. Evidence: Jeffares essay; Toledo Museum; Cumming.
- P17. [RECEPTION] The prices climbed for a century. Mlle Salle Fr18,000 (Madame Denain sale 1893); Madame Rouille Fr31,550 (1897), Fr365,000 (Bardac 1920), Fr1 million (1926); Duval de l'Epinoy Fr5,210 (1903), then Fr120,000 (Doucet), Fr600,000 (Doucet sale 1912 -- then the highest price ever for a pastel), then higher still to Gulbenkian. The 50-franc banknote 1976-1992 carried his portrait. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P18. [RECEPTION] The modernists claimed him. Gauguin, Matisse, Mary Cassatt, Maurice Barres and Anatole France noted his pastels; Degas copied La Tour; Jacques Doucet was inspired to collect eighteenth-century pastels by seeing La Tour heads at Degas's. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P19. [RECEPTION] The Perronneau split is a later construction. Rosenberg (Dictionnaire amoureuse du Louvre, 2007) and Blanche prefer Perronneau; Fleury 1911: "Perronneau n'a jamais ete le rival du pastelliste saint-quentinois" (Perronneau was never the rival of the Saint-Quentin pastellist). The rivalry story -- Diderot's joke, the side-by-side Salon 1750/1751 -- is largely a critical device for sorting two pastellists, not a documented feud. Evidence: Jeffares essay.
- P20. [FROM RECORD] The first portrait survives only as a print. The earliest known portrait, Voltaire, is attested only by Langlois's engraving of 1731; no pastel original is known. The career begins, in the record, as a reproduction. Evidence: Jeffares essay; en.wikipedia.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C1. THE FINGER POINTS LEFT, NOT AT THE VIEWER -- AND #506'S TOP-PICK LINE IS WRONG. The brief's wording ("La Tour's self-portrait pointing at the viewer") and #506's top-pick line ("whose self-portrait pointing at the viewer Ducreux's Moqueur answers") are both wrong about La Tour's gesture. Christie's: the Autoportrait a l'index "shows La Tour laughing, leaning on a window and pointing with his index to the left"; Apollo: he "directs our attention to something we cannot immediately identify"; the Toledo reading has him pointing toward the studio door. The works leg's table and Phase 1 repeat the same error ("index finger pointing out at the viewer"), and the parent corrects it here: the point goes sideways, to one side, an elegant aside. What points at the viewer is Ducreux's Moqueur (Spencer Museum: "pointing a mocking finger at the viewer"). VERDICT: La Tour points left, to one side; Ducreux turns the point straight at the viewer. The doorway holds in substance and fails in the letter, which is how the check is recorded.
- C2. "ONLY PUPIL" -- HOLDS AS A PHRASE, REJECTED AS A FACT. Jeffares, in the La Tour essay (I.18): "although the suggestion that Ducreux was his 'only pupil' cannot be correct. The evidence of their association is thin." The claim rests on Ducreux being described as "eleve de Latour" in the livret of the 1796 salon, long after La Tour's death; the earliest secondary reference to "le seul eleve de Latour" is Arnault 1822; Dinaux 1852 names Ducreux and Liotard as two principal pupils; no apprenticeship document exists anywhere; no pupil is listed in the Academie Listes des eleves; La Tour's second will names virtually every artist he had met but not Ducreux; fr.wikipedia adds that his character did not lead him to pass on his knowledge at all. VERDICT: say "widely referred to as La Tour's only pupil; the evidence is thin" and give the documented teaching case -- Jacques Neilson, taught pastel by memorandum of 10 August 1749 -- the weight instead.
- C3. THE AMIENS JABOT SELF-PORTRAIT MAY BE A MONTJOYE COPY. The famous Autoportrait au jabot (Musee de Picardie, Amiens, 64.5 x 53.5 cm, frame inscribed "SUPER OMNES DOCENTES SE INTELLEXIT") is dated 1750 by utpictura18 (Salon 1750) and c.1751 by Wikipedia and print sources; Jeffares's 2019 essay argues the Amiens version may be a copy by Jean-Gabriel Montjoye, not autograph, and that the 1751 note on the back belongs to a different version sold in 1867. The Diderot story -- La Tour secretly painting his own portrait while Perronneau painted his, the two shown side by side -- is secure as an anecdote; which physical pastel is the Salon one is not. VERDICT: use c.1750-51 with the attribution disputed, and do not call the Amiens version autograph without the flag.
- C4. DEATH DATE -- 17 FEBRUARY, WITH JEFFARES'S SLASH NOTED. Jeffares's essay header gives the death date as "16/17.II.1788", the only source in this study to admit the 16th; every other source (en.wikipedia, fr.wikipedia, Britannica) gives 17 February. The 17th is the received date; Jeffares's slash marks genuine documentary uncertainty. VERDICT: Sunday 17 February 1788, with Jeffares's "16/17" noted.
- C5. APPRENTICESHIP DATE -- 12 OR 16 OCTOBER 1719. Marandet 2002 dates the Dupouch contract from 12 October 1719 (en.wikipedia); fr.wikipedia gives 16 October 1719. Unresolved; this study uses October 1719 without the day.
- C6. SALON 1748 COUNT -- 15 OR 14. Fr.wikipedia says 15 portraits including king, queen, dauphin and Marechal de Saxe; arthive and artdaily search extracts say 14 pastels sent in 1748, eight now in the Louvre. Unresolved; this study uses 15 with the 14 flagged.
- C7. POMPADOUR SIZE -- DISPUTED. Fr.wikipedia gives 175 x 128 cm; Jeffares (p. 36) gives 177.5 x 136 cm; secondary listings give 175 x 127 cm and 175 x 150 cm, and one gives an evidently erroneous 2136 x 178 cm. Unresolved; this study says "nearly two metres high" and does not state a single dimension.
- C8. DE RIEUX SHEET COUNT -- 16 OR 12. Jeffares and the Getty's own online exhibit "Pastels in Pieces" give sixteen sheets; the Getty's 2018 press release (via artdaily) and Hyperallergic say twelve. This study uses sixteen, Jeffares's and the Getty exhibit's figure, with the press figure noted.
- C9. "150 PORTRAITS" IS THE COMMONPLACE; JEFFARES COUNTS ABOUT 120. Britannica and Wikipedia say 150 portraits exhibited at the Salons over 36 or 37 years; Jeffares's counted figure is about 120 pastels in all, 1737-1773, some three-fifths known today. VERDICT: use about 120 as the counted figure; the 150 is the repeated commonplace. (Parent correction to the legs.)
- C10. LONDON TRIP 1725 -- TRADITIONAL, NOT ESTABLISHED. The traditional biographies (en.wikipedia, fr.wikipedia, Britannica) state a 1725 London trip, invited by Horace Walpole after the Cambrai congress portraits, as fact. Jeffares finds the evidence thin, Bury's exhaustive search for his 1971 monograph found no evidence of La Tour in London, and the Cambrai pastel series is now attributed to Birochon. VERDICT: treat the London trip as traditional, not established.
- C11. COBBE CONFLATES TWO SELF-PORTRAITS. The Cobbe Collection page describes its self-portrait as "L'autoportrait a l'index or L'autoportrait a l'oeil de boeuf" -- these are two distinct works in Jeffares (J.46.1001 a l'index, 1737; the a l'oeil de boeuf is the roundel self-portrait, also c.1737, Louvre, pastel on blue paper 59 x 49 cm per fr.wikipedia). Do not conflate them.
- C12. SCHOOL FOUNDATION -- 1782, 1778, OR 1783. Fr.wikipedia says "En 1782, il fonde une ecole de dessin"; Jeffares's documents show the 1778 settlement of 6,000 livres for the ecole de dessin at Saint-Quentin and the annuity purchase of 23 January 1783, with the school re-established by petition in 1805 after falling into desuetude during the Revolution. VERDICT: the school's legal footing dates to 1783; "founded 1782" is the fr.wikipedia shorthand.
- C13. DAUPHIN PORTRAIT DATE -- 1745 OR 1748. Wikipedia's gallery captions the Louvre Dauphin 1745; the Dauphin article (Marigny letter) says La Tour made four portraits of the Dauphin, of which the only known survivor, at the Louvre, is dated 1748. Both dates are recorded; neither is resolved.
- C14. AGE AT DEATH -- 83 OR 84. Unanimous sources say 83 (born 5 September 1704, died 17 February 1788); NiceArtGallery says 84. VERDICT: 83.
- C15. CARRIERA BIRTH YEAR -- 1673 OR 1675. The top pick for #508, Rosalba Carriera, is given as born 7 October 1673 by most sources and 1675 by some; the discrepancy is flagged, not resolved here, and the #508 leg should settle it.
- C16. BOQUET DATES -- DISPUTED. Jeanne-Angelique Boquet is given as 1731 - 4 February 1804 by Wikipedia and Paris 1740-1786 by Jeffares; the conflict is flagged, not resolved, and the leg that takes her up should settle it before proposing her higher.
- C17. LARGILLIERRE BIRTH -- 10 OCTOBER OR 2 OCTOBER 1656. Baptised 10 October 1656 per most sources; some give 2 October 1656 as the birth date. Flagged, not resolved.
- C18. PERRONNEAU SIDE-BY-SIDE -- SALON 1750 OR 1751. Uptictura18 says the two self-portraits were shown side by side at the Salon of 1751; Wikipedia (Perronneau) says 1750. Flagged, not resolved.
- C19. THE FATHER'S DISAPPROVAL -- POPULAR, UNSUPPORTED. En.wikipedia notes La Tour "is popularly said to have disapproved of his son taking up the arts, but there is nothing to support this." Do not repeat the disapproval as fact.
- C20. VOLTAIRE -- 1731 ENGRAVING VS APRIL 1735 SITTINGS. The earliest known portrait is attested only by a 1731 Langlois engraving, per the older sources; Jeffares dates the Voltaire sittings to April 1735. Read as an earlier portrait known only through the engraving, with the documented sittings in April 1735; the two are not necessarily the same portrait.
