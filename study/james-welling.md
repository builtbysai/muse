## The life: the photographer who never studied photography

James Welling was born in 1951 in Hartford, Connecticut, and grew up in nearby Simsbury. He attended Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh in 1969-1971, where he studied drawing with Gandy Brodie, and the University of Pittsburgh in 1970-1971, where he took modern dance classes. In 1971 he transferred to the California Institute of the Arts in Valencia. At CalArts he worked primarily in film and video. He received a B.F.A. in 1972 and an M.F.A. in 1974. His CalArts teachers named in the sources read end to end were John Baldessari, Wolfgang Stoerchle and Jack Goldstein, and he was Baldessari's teaching assistant in 1973-1974. (C23108, leg 1 C22900-C22910; synthesis C23052)

He never formally studied photography. He taught himself developing and printing after setting up a darkroom in August 1976, and began to make photographs in 1976 with a 4x5 view camera. The camera came late and second-hand, which is the whole career in miniature: he approaches photography as an outsider with a film-maker's eye, and every series is a question about the apparatus rather than a picture of a place. (C23109, leg 1 C22911-C22912; synthesis C23052)

In 1977 he began Diary/Landscape, pairing details of his ancestors' diaries with Connecticut landscapes -- W01, A269, Autumn 1979, a gelatin silver print 8.8 x 7.1 cm, the only early work that takes its subject from the world rather than building it. In 1978 he moved to New York and began the abstract studio series: Aluminum Foil (W02-W04, 1980-81), Drapes (velvet scattered with pastry dough), Gelatin Photographs (ink-infused gelatin), and Tile Photographs (W06, 1985, gelatin silver print mounted to board, 20 x 16 in.), shown at Metro Pictures in the early 1980s. The foil pictures are small, black, contact-printed, with no horizon and no orientation -- a photograph that refuses to say what it is of. The diary generation (great- vs great-great-grandparents) and the 1840-41 date are unresolved between Wikipedia and UbuWeb, so only the year, title and pairing are printed; the third Metro Pictures year (1984 vs 1985) is likewise unresolved. (C23110, leg 1 C22913-C22915; leg 2 W01-W06; synthesis C23053)

The middle years oscillate between the found and the built. Degrades (W07), colour photograms begun in 1986, are the first colour in the work -- shadows and graded colour fields, no camera. Railroad Photographs (W08), begun 1987/1988, and the H.H. Richardson architectural photographs return to found, documentary landscape and building facades. Light Sources (W09, Meriden), begun in the early 1990s (1991 per Wikipedia, 1992 per UbuWeb, unresolved), photographs things that transmit light -- sun through branches, ceiling lights -- and is his first use of digital technology, scanned prints and large Iris prints. New Abstractions (W10, 1998) returns to abstraction as enlarged photograms made blind in darkness, strips of Bristol board dropped at random where the maker cannot see what he is doing. Connecticut Landscape, begun 1998, revisits places from his teens with an 8x10 view camera. The through-line is the apparatus: enlarger light, film, darkroom darkness, and now the scanner. (C23111, leg 1 C22915; leg 2 W07-W10; synthesis C23053, C23058)

The colour turn runs 2004-2010. Flowers (W11, 012, 2006, chromogenic print 118.1 x 94.6 cm at the Met) is the first sustained colour work: photograms printed in Newton's seven colours, then multi-colour gels, then from 2014 Photoshop RGB channels -- increasingly saturated and psychedelic. The Flowers start date is unresolved (2004 per the Zwirner Metamorphosis release, 2006 per the artist's interview), so no single start year is printed. Hexachromes (W12, 2005/2006) dissect additive colour vision with six sequential filter exposures of an agave. Quadrilaterals (W13, 2008, photo engravings with aquatint, plate 13 5/8 x 10 13/16 in., Met portfolio of 11 plates) rebuilds abstraction inside the 3D program Maya. Glass House (W14 0696, 2006; W15 5912, 2008, inkjet prints) takes the Flowers gels out of the darkroom and holds them in front of the lens at Philip Johnson's house: a real building, flooded with filter colour, becomes a laboratory for transparency, reflectivity and trichromatic vision. The Glass House dates are best attested as 2006-2009; Wikipedia's 2005 start is unverified and is not printed. Maison de Verre (W16, MDV 9205, 2009, inkjet print, dimensions explicitly uncertain) is the contrast case: the same glass-house idea, but the colour and glow are added digitally afterwards instead of in-camera. (C23112, leg 2 W11-W16; synthesis C23058)

The recent work reprocesses rather than photographs. Chemical (W18, 2010-) returns to the darkroom without a camera or a negative: chemistry on paper in room light, thirty years after Aluminum Foil, the same abstraction on the edge of representation. Wyeth (2009/10-2015) uses digital collage and subtle chromatic alteration to record the places Andrew Wyeth painted. Choreograph (W17, 4776, 2015, inkjet print on rag paper, 106.7 x 160 cm) returns to the modern dance Welling studied in his early twenties: dancers layered with architecture and landscape in the RGB channels, figuration competing with itself. Cento (W19, Julia Mamaea, 2018, collotype-based colour prints from a handmade gelatin/potassium dichromate emulsion with dye) photographs Greek and Roman sculpture in museums worldwide, applies highly unnatural digital colour, then hand-inks the print with oil paint -- a single negative generating 30+ prints. Thought Objects (W20, Table, 2021/2023, UV-curable ink on Dibond, 42 x 63 in.) reprocesses old negatives with simple digital tools used wrong on purpose: strip collage, over-sharpening into bas-relief, positive/negative overlay, chance newspaper offset prints. The arc: from building a subject to photograph, to photographing a subject to rebuild it, to rebuilding old photographs themselves. (C23113, leg 2 W17-W20; synthesis C23058)

The standing follows the work. In 1995 he moved to Los Angeles to head the photography area at UCLA, Professor and Area Head of Photography 1995-2016; since 2012 he has been a Lecturer with the rank of Professor in the Visual Art Program at Princeton. He has been represented by David Zwirner since 2005; Thought Objects (January 2024) was his tenth solo show there. In 2024 he was Photographer in Residence at the American Academy in Rome. His work was in The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984 at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, April 21-August 2, 2009, organized by Douglas Eklund, more than 160 works by 30 artists; in documenta 9, Kassel, 1992; and in the Whitney Biennial 2008. His first museum exhibition was Kunsthalle Bern, 1990. Major surveys: Wexner Center 2000 (travelled to Baltimore and MOCA Los Angeles), Monograph at Cincinnati Art Museum 2013 (travelled to the Hammer), and Metamorphosis (S.M.A.K. Ghent and Kunstforum Wien, 2017). Awards: DG BANK-Forderpreis Fotografie 1999; Infinity Award (ICP) 2014. Collections include the Met, MoMA, Whitney, Guggenheim, Centre Pompidou, Tate, LACMA, Hammer, MOCA Los Angeles, MFA Boston, the Art Institute of Chicago and the Wadsworth Atheneum. He lives and works in New York. (C23114, leg 1 C22916-C22929; synthesis C23054-C23055)

## M1 -- Build the subject, don't find it

Welling's early abstract work is made, not found. He builds a small tabletop subject in the studio -- crumpled aluminium foil, draped velvet scattered with pastry dough, ink-infused gelatin, plastic tiles -- lights it dramatically, and photographs it close up, rather than going out to find a picture in the world. The foil pictures (W02 The Wayfarer, 1980; W03 B41, 1980-81, 14 x 10.8 cm at the Met; W04 Spiral (1-8-80), 1980) and the Tile Photographs (W06, Tile Photograph 7, 1985) are all built this way, and so are the Drapes and Gelatin Photographs behind W05 (C40, 1981, gelatin silver print, 9 5/16 x 7 11/16 in. at MoMA -- no series attribution verified, so it is left unattributed). The subject is a prop; the photograph is what the prop becomes under light. (C23116, leg 2 M1; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is a thing becoming a place. A plain material is crumpled, folded or scattered on screen; the camera pushes in until the material fills the frame; the same material re-reads as landscape, starfield, water, strata as light moves across it. The viewer watches the transformation happen, and the transformation is the picture. A prompt built from this mechanism must start with the material visibly plain -- foil, cloth, tile -- and let the close-up and the light do the work, never by cutting to a landscape. (C23117, leg 2 M1, W02-W04, W06; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** build a small tabletop subject, light it hard, push in close -- and let a plain material become a landscape, a starfield, water, strata. (C23118)


## M2 -- The dark contact print: over-expose twice, print very dark

The early prints are made by over-exposing twice. Over-expose in camera to secure density of detail in the shadow, then over-expose again when printing, so the print is very dark and the highlights are febrile points in expanses of black. Print at contact size -- the same size as the negative -- for what the CAG Vancouver text calls a "facsimile effect": no enlargement between object and image. The foil pictures (W02-W04), the small Diary/Landscape prints (W01, 8.8 x 7.1 cm), and C40 (W05) are all made this way. (C23119, leg 2 M2; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is emergence from black. The image starts nearly black; pinpricks and ridges of light slowly emerge as the "exposure" is pushed; the field stays claustrophobic with no horizon and no orienting reference, and the viewer must decide which way is up. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the frame almost black and let the light arrive slowly -- never start bright and dim down, never add a horizon to help. (C23120, leg 2 M2, W01-W05; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** over-expose twice and print very dark -- let pinpricks of light emerge from black, with no horizon and no way up. (C23121)


## M3 -- The photogram: a picture without a camera, in darkness

The photogram removes the camera entirely. Place objects -- hands, flowers, strips of Bristol board -- or shadows directly on photographic paper or film and expose it, with no lens and no camera. In New Abstractions (W10, 1998) the strips are dropped at random in total darkness, so the maker cannot see what he is doing; the composition is the record of a chance drop. The same operation runs the colour work: Degrades (W07, begun 1986, colour photograms made with shadows, 10 x 8 in. to 40 x 30 in.) and Flowers (W11, photograms of flowers) are photograms first and photographs second. (C23122, leg 2 M3; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is a drop in the dark. Shapes fall or are laid onto a light-sensitive field in darkness; when the "light" comes on, their shadows resolve into hard-edged black-and-white forms or coloured flower silhouettes. A prompt built from this mechanism must show the drop -- the shapes falling, the field dark, the reveal -- and must let chance place them, never compose them by hand. (C23123, leg 2 M3, W07, W10, W11; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** drop the shapes in darkness and let the light reveal them -- the picture is the record of a chance drop, not a composed view. (C23124)


## M4 -- Colour separation: one scene, three (then six) pictures

Colour enters Welling's work as a separation, not a tint. Expose the same film or the same scene sequentially through red, green and blue separation filters -- the trichromatic process -- or through six filters (red, green, blue, cyan, magenta, yellow) in the Hexachromes (W12, 2005/2006, an agave). In Photoshop the same operation is done by putting different images into the R, G and B channels, as in Choreograph (W17) and the late Flowers (W11, from 2014). Because the exposures are sequential, anything that moves between them -- a shadow, a dancer, a branch -- is recorded as a coloured fringe or swath. The Glass House pictures (W14, W15) use filter combinations the same way. (C23125, leg 2 M4; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is a picture splitting into ghosts. A stable subject -- agave, house, dancer -- holds still while its shadow or its double splits into red, green and blue ghosts that drift apart and recombine; moving edges leave rainbow trails and the image flickers between one picture and three. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the subject still and let the channels move -- the colour is the motion, not a filter laid over it. (C23126, leg 2 M4, W11, W12, W14, W15, W17; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** split one scene into red, green and blue and let the ghosts drift apart -- anything that moves between exposures comes back as a coloured fringe. (C23127)


## M5 -- Shoot through the filter

The filter goes in front of the lens, between lens and subject, at the moment of exposure. Welling holds coloured plastic filters, clear or fogged plastic, pieces of clear, uneven or tinted glass, and finally a diffraction grating in front of the lens. The filter is both noun and verb: it lets some wavelengths through and some kinds of information seep in. The Glass House pictures (W14 0696, 2006; W15 5912, 2008) are the full statement -- the house flooded panel by panel with unnatural colour, grass turning red, sun flares splattering into spectra -- and the early Flowers (W11) layered gels above the negative in the colour darkroom the same way. Maison de Verre (W16) is the contrast case that proves the rule: there the colour and glow are added digitally afterwards, not in-camera, and the picture is a different kind of thing. (C23128, leg 2 M5; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is watching the filter, not the subject. A recognisable building or room is flooded, panel by panel, with unnatural colour; the same view cycles through filter after filter, and the viewer watches the colour arrive rather than the scene change. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the subject fixed and change only the filter in front of it -- never move the camera to fake the effect. (C23129, leg 2 M5, W11, W14, W15, W16; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** hold the filter in front of the lens and keep the subject still -- let the viewer watch the colour arrive, panel by panel. (C23130)


## M6 -- The building as a lens

Photograph a glass building so that its transparent and reflective skin does the picturing. Philip Johnson's Glass House (1949) in W14 and W15, and Chareau's Maison de Verre (1928-32) in W16, reflect the landscape behind the camera, refract the interior, and layer interior and exterior until they cannot be separated -- and the photographer himself vanishes from the reflections. The building is not the subject; it is the lens, and the picture is what the lens does to the world around it. (C23131, leg 2 M6; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is interior and exterior trading places. A glass box sits in a landscape; trees, sky and interior furniture reflect and refract across its faces in shifting layers; no photographer is visible anywhere in the reflections; interior and exterior trade places as light and season change. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the camera outside the glass and let the reflections do the picturing -- never cut inside to show the room plainly. (C23132, leg 2 M6, W14, W15, W16; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** let the glass building do the picturing -- reflections and refractions layer until interior and exterior cannot be separated, and the photographer vanishes. (C23133)


## M7 -- One motif, a hundred variants

Welling returns to one motif across dozens of sessions and prints each variant, often titled only by a date or a printer-queue number: 1-8-80, 2-25-80, 0696, 5912, 4776, 012, B41, C40. One roll of foil, one coastline, one house, one flower negative, one sculpture bust -- Julia Mamaea (W19) was printed 30+ times by different processes. Meaning accumulates across the series, not inside a single frame, and a single frame is legible only as one frame of a long run. (C23134, leg 2 M7; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is learning by variation. The same motif repeats with one parameter changed each time -- light angle, filter, colour, crop -- while a counter or date stamp ticks forward, and the viewer learns the motif by its variations. A prompt built from this mechanism must repeat the motif visibly, with one change per repeat and a ticking stamp, and must never let one frame pretend to be the whole work. (C23135, leg 2 M7, W02-W04, W11, W14, W15, W17, W19; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** return to one motif and change one thing per frame -- the series is the work, and the viewer learns it by its variations. (C23136)


## M8 -- The photograph as a flat field

Push the image until the subject drops out and only a field remains: graded colour in the Degrades (W07), chemical bloom on paper in Chemical (W18, 2010-), or a flower negative printed in a single saturated Newton colour in Flowers (W11, or in rust monochrome). The picture is a surface to look AT, not a window to look THROUGH. (C23137, leg 2 M8; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is a stain spreading. Colour floods the frame as a gradient or a stain; the stain spreads, blooms and reticulating like developer on paper; figuration appears only at the edge of the field, as a reading the viewer supplies, then dissolves again. A prompt built from this mechanism must let the colour field fill the frame and must let the figure dissolve -- never hold the subject in focus while the field decorates behind it. (C23138, leg 2 M8, W07, W11, W18; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** push until the subject drops out and only a field of colour and light remains -- the picture is a surface, not a window. (C23139)


## M9 -- Reprocess the old negative

Take photographs made years earlier and rework them with simple digital tools used against their purpose. Superimpose dance, architecture and landscape in the RGB channels (Choreograph, W17); cut thin strips from different photographs of the same place and realign them into a coherent but disoriented image (Thought Objects, W20, including Pier 24, The Battery, the Salk Institute, the Orange County Government Center); combine morning and afternoon exposures of one building so it reflects itself; over-sharpen until the picture reads as a bas-relief (Paeonia, Canna Lily, Oak in W20); overlay chance offset prints from newspaper used to clean rollers. Cento (W19) applies digital colour to sculpture photographs the same way. The Thought Objects dual dates are the signature: Table is 2021/2023 -- the first date the source photograph, the second the processing and printing -- though no source seen states the convention explicitly, so it is flagged, not asserted as certain. (C23140, leg 2 M9; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is an old photograph coming apart and back together. Layers of unrelated images fade in and out of each other; a skyline is sliced into vertical strips that slide and re-register; an old photograph is reprinted until its texture lifts off the surface like relief; the same source image returns in a new colour and a new arrangement. A prompt built from this mechanism must start from an old, recognisable photograph and show the reworking -- never start from a blank canvas. (C23141, leg 2 M9, W17, W19, W20; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** take an old photograph and rework it with simple tools used wrong on purpose -- slice it, over-sharpen it, layer it -- until it becomes a new picture. (C23142)


## M10 -- Handwork on the print: paint, ink and chance marks on the surface

After printing, work the surface by hand. Roll oil paint over a laser print on a polyester lithographic plate with a brayer, brush on additional paint and powdered pigment, remove selective areas, let scratches, drips and rivulets happen -- the Cento process (W19, MACS description). Or spread liquid and powdered developer across photo paper with tools -- Chemical (W18). Each print becomes unique, as much a painting as a photograph. (C23143, leg 2 M10; synthesis C23059)

The watchable behaviour is the glaze. A printed image is glazed with a translucent veil of paint or developer; the veil drips, scratches and pools; colour desaturates under the glaze in some places and flares in others; the finished surface carries visible hand marks that no two prints share. A prompt built from this mechanism must show the hand arriving after the print -- the brayer, the brush, the drip -- and must let the marks differ every time. (C23144, leg 2 M10, W18, W19, W20; synthesis C23059)

**Takeaway:** glaze the finished print by hand -- paint, developer, drips and scratches -- so no two prints ever share the same marks. (C23145)


## The aesthetic logic: the apparatus is the subject

Welling's career is one question asked fifty ways: what is a photograph? He never answers it with a picture of the world. He answers it by changing what the apparatus does -- building the subject, removing the camera, splitting the colour, filtering the lens, reprocessing the negative, painting the print -- and watching what survives. The photograph is never a window; it is a record of what the machine was made to do. (C23146, leg 2 mechanisms M1-M10; synthesis C23059)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, build it before you photograph it: foil, drapes, gelatin, tiles, flowers laid on paper, a glass house chosen because its skin pictures the world -- the subject is always constructed, found only in the one early exception, Diary/Landscape. Second, let the apparatus decide: over-exposure, darkness, sequential filters, diffraction, chance drops, RGB channels -- the mechanism makes the picture, and the artist's job is to set the mechanism running and watch. Third, return and rework: one motif across dozens of sessions, one negative printed thirty times, old photographs sliced and over-sharpened into new ones -- nothing is finished, everything is a source for the next version. The teacher Baldessari replaced the hand with a rule; the student took the rule into the darkroom and made the rule the picture. (C23147, leg 2 M1, M3, M7, M9; synthesis C23059)

The evolution is the proof. 1975-86: small, dark, black-and-white, constructed -- Polaroids, Diary/Landscape (the found exception), foil, drapes, gelatin, tiles, all contact-printed with no horizon. 1986-2005: colour enters through the darkroom (Degrades), landscape returns (Railroad, Light Sources, Connecticut Landscape), the scanner arrives, and New Abstractions are made blind in darkness. 2004-10: trichromatic colour becomes the subject -- Flowers, Hexachromes, Glass House through gels, Maison de Verre as the digital contrast case. 2010-24: digital collage, the body, antiquity, reprocessing -- Chemical without a camera, Choreograph layering dancers in RGB, Cento hand-inking antiquity, Thought Objects rebuilding old negatives. What stays constant is the question: the apparatus, not the world. What changes is the scale: 8.8 x 7.1 cm prints in 1979 (W01) become 106.7 x 160 cm inkjets in 2015 (W17) and 42 x 63 in. UV panels in 2023 (W20). Colour, which entered as a darkroom gel, ends as the whole picture: liberated from the subject and its history. (C23148, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C23058)
