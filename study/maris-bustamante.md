# Maris Bustamante: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #341.

**Site:** Maris Bustamante (Mexico City, 20 November 1949), Mexican artist, co-founder of the No-grupo (1977-1983) and of Polvo de Gallina Negra (1983-1993), the first feminist art group in Mexico. Painter turned happening-maker turned performance artist turned researcher and teacher, author of La patente del taco (1979), of El pene como instrumento de trabajo (1982), of the MADRES! project (1987), and of a thirty-year run of masks, aprons, recipes, and television performances made from cardboard, photocopy, styrofoam, and the cheapest materials at hand. Identity anchors: the mask you wear becomes you; the object handed to the audience; the symbolic kidnapping; the legal paper as the artwork; the erotic slogan as instruction; the lucha-libre poster as invitation; the pregnant belly as prop; the recipe as the artwork; television as the stage; the authority face swap; the anti-homage; the individual inside the group; the cheap material; the photograph as the performance; the domestic object turned stage; humor as the delivery system.

**Doorway:** Hop from the hersua study: from Hersua, whose house hosted the meetings where the No-grupo formed in 1977, to Bustamante, the No-grupo's central figure, who took the group's anti-object stance into performance, costume, and the body itself. #340 closed with the man who built a ring of 64 prisms in a lava field and called it the pyramid of today, a work you complete by walking through it. #341 opens with the woman who met in his house and then refused the object altogether: no ring, no monument, no wall, only a cardboard face handed to a stranger, a taco declared her legal property, a belly made of styrofoam and worn on live television. Where Hersua makes geometry stand still and wait for you to walk into it, Bustamante makes the artwork walk away from you, into the audience's hands, onto the television, into the kitchen. The doorway is the house itself: the meetings happened at Hersua's table, and what came out of them was the decision that the work would not be a thing.

## M1. The mask you wear becomes you

A cardboard face cut from her own photograph, the eye holes cut out, worn by a stranger: the wearer lends their body and takes her face. The earliest instance is the 10th Paris Biennale, 1977: cardboard masks made from photographs of the four members' faces, sent to the Centre Georges Pompidou for the public to wear, the group not attending physically. Her own mask, viewed first-hand, is a die-cut cardboard head, her photographed face with wavy shoulder-length hair and round wire glasses, the eye holes cut out as white ovals, the mouth slightly open, no phallus, no tag visible, the cut following the outline of her hair and shoulders: the plainest face in the set, a face with the eyes removed, meant to be worn by a stranger in Paris. The group's own formulation, quoted in the MoMA post: "We want to be there without being there physically; we want the viewer to be the one who takes us there; we want to inhabit the visage of each person who puts on our masks; we want to be the spectator." The mechanism reaches its full form at Caliente-Caliente in 1982, where the audience wears her face with the phallus nose, photographed in the crowd, the critic Raquel Tibol holding the mask up over her own face, other audience members behind her also holding or wearing masks and reading the printed programs. The mask is a photograph you put on like a hat, and the wearer's own eyes look out of someone else's printed eyes. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M8) pinned words in a grid and let the reader recombine them, the poem as a sculpture you read; Bustamante pins a face on a stranger and lets the stranger complete it, the portrait as a sculpture you wear, and the reader is replaced by the wearer. Sebastian (#338 M4) built the letter large enough to walk into; Bustamante builds the face small enough to put on, and the walking-into is replaced by the putting-on. **Takeaway:** Cut a face from a photograph and hand it to a stranger; the wearer is the work, and the portrait is finished only when someone else's eyes look out of it. Watch the eyes: when a printed face is worn and the wearer's real eyes show through the cut holes, and the mask is the whole work, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The object handed to the audience

The work is not on the wall; it is distributed. At Caliente-Caliente, 300 die-cut masks of her own face were handed out to the audience, who wore them during the performance; the distribution is photographed, the audience becoming the display surface. Small heterogeneous objects were given to the public at every Montaje: pigments in small plastic bags in the colours of a Gerzso painting (Trece planos molidos de Gerzso, 1978), a portable painting for the recipient; sachets of potion at the first Polvo de Gallina Negra performance in 1983, brewed in a cauldron before a thousand spectators and handed out with the recipe printed. The object is cheap, light, and made to leave the room in someone's pocket. The museum keeps the photograph of the distribution; the work itself is gone, worn home. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M9) made the sculpture something a body stands inside, the body completing it while present; Bustamante makes the sculpture something a body carries away, the body completing it after leaving, and the completion happens in the street, not in the gallery. Silva (#339 M15) gave the work away as a gift and let the dialogue keep it alive; Bustamante gives the work away as a handout and lets the pocket keep it alive, and the gift is worn, not kept. **Takeaway:** Make the work small, cheap, and hand it out; the audience's hands are the gallery, and the piece survives as something carried, not something hung. Watch the hands: when the crowd is holding the work up, putting it on, and reading the program at the same time, and nothing is left on the wall, the mechanism is on.

## M3. The symbolic kidnapping

Take a revered thing out of its place and declare it stolen. The second No-grupo action, 1978, was Presencia-Ambiente a Gunther Gerzso: the group declared the sexagenarian master painter kidnapped, then agreed to murder him and offer him as a sacrifice, and Gerzso replied in delight, "OIGANME BIEN TODOS: NO QUIERO SER RESCATADO," I do not want to be rescued. The victim agrees to stay kidnapped, which is the joke's second turn: the kidnapping is a tribute the victim enjoys. The same engine drives La patente del taco (1979), described as an "obra-abandono": the taco is kidnapped from daily life, extracted from its context, and abandoned in the Museo de Arte Moderno, where it becomes art by being out of place. The ransom note is the work: a declaration in the press, a patent application, a manifesto. Against the chain: Escobedo (#335) engineered failure, boats designed to sink, and the sinking was the work; Bustamante engineers theft, a master designed to be stolen, and the victim's refusal to be rescued is the work, which is failure's comic twin. Hersua (#340 M14) staged a performance that steals itself, children carrying crowns away from the university steps; Bustamante stages a kidnapping that keeps itself, the victim staying put, and the theft is the tribute. **Takeaway:** Remove a revered thing, declare it stolen in public, and let the victim refuse rescue; the removal is the work and the refusal is the punchline. Watch the ransom note: when an ordinary or sacred thing is gone and a declaration stands in its place, and the victim is enjoying it, the mechanism is on.

## M4. The legal paper as the artwork

The patent, the manifesto, the certificate: bureaucracy is the medium. La patente del taco (1979) runs in three moments, as the Museo Amparo object page records them: research, the taco traced to the Codigo Sierra of 1551 in the library of the Museo Nacional de Antropologia; the attempt to patent it at the Direccion General de Derechos de Autor, where after "varias vicisitudes" the director approved the request; and the materialization as documents, a Manifiesto de reconocimiento al taco, a visual statement (a black-and-white photograph of a taco by Alfredo Nunez), a literary statement ("el taco es un acto que toca"), and three capsules concentrating the taco's symbolism as cultural penetration, its kidnapping, and the patent itself. The work exists as paperwork that a state office stamped. A woman declaring a primordial element of Mexican identity legally hers, giving it erotic, phallic characteristics, claiming it as an object of pleasure under her control alone: the legal form is what makes the claim real, and the claim is what makes the form art. No photograph of the documents themselves was viewed this run; the account rests on the Museo Amparo object page and the Artishock essay, and the study does not describe the documents from an image it never saw. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M12) decided the work as a maquette and let the site scale it, the model as the real work; Bustamante decides the work as a form filed at a government office and lets the stamp scale it, the paperwork as the real work, and the scaling is legal, not spatial. Gurria (#336) kept time with a hammer and let the signal be the work; Bustamante keeps time with a filing and lets the approval be the work, and the approval is the lightest material she ever used. **Takeaway:** File the form, get the stamp, and let the paperwork be the sculpture; the state office is the foundry, and the signature is the casting. Watch the stamp: when a food becomes property by signature, and the documents are the whole work, the mechanism is on.

## M5. The erotic slogan as instruction

A command that is also a joke and also a dare: "Atrevase a cometer un acto erotico! Un taco comase!" Dare to commit an erotic act, eat a taco. The slogan is the work's operating instruction: eat, and the eating is the erotic act. It ran on television, in the press, and in the museum, and it works because it is addressed to the viewer's mouth. The taco is phallic, the eating is the act, and the instruction collapses the distance between the symbol and the body: you do not contemplate the erotic object, you put it in your mouth. The same direct address runs through the whole practice: the mask tells the audience to wear her face, the recipe tells the public to brew the potion, the poster tells the crowd to come and watch the fight. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M8) wrote words on paper slips and let the reader recombine them, the order the reader's; Bustamante writes one sentence and lets the reader obey it, the order the sentence's, and the reading is replaced by the eating. Silva (#339 M12) built the gate as a triangular void that is the point; Bustamante builds the slogan as a mouth that is the point, and the void is replaced by the bite. **Takeaway:** Write one sentence that tells the viewer to do something with their body, and let the doing be the work; the slogan is the instruction and the mouth is the gallery. Watch the mouth: when the words tell the viewer to eat and the eating is the erotic act, and the sentence is the whole work, the mechanism is on.

## M6. The lucha-libre poster as invitation

Announcements in the visual language of popular urban events: the Caliente-Caliente poster, viewed first-hand, stages an art performance as a wrestling match. A lucha-libre style flyer, black on cream: the big title "CALIENTE CALIENTE", "Sensacional mano a mano", "POR UNICA VEZ EN MEXICO", "Por Venezuela CARLOS ZERPA y por Mexico NO-GRUPO", a small oval portrait of Zerpa at left and a group photo of four people at right, the city names NEW YORK, PARIS, LONDRES with little drawings (the Statue of Liberty, the Eiffel Tower, Big Ben), a box reading "un espectaculo concebido como la vida, tiene como una digresion humano como el arte", the footer "CARLOS ZERPA - MARIS BUSTAMANTE - MELQUIADES HERRERA - ALFREDO NUNEZ - RUBEN VALENCIA" and "montaje de momentos plasticos", and two dates in small print, Tuesday 19 January 1982 at 1 p.m. in the traditional Escuela de San Carlos and Thursday 21 January 1982 at 9:30 p.m. in the Museo de Arte Moderno, both weekdays verified against the 1982 calendar. The publicity IS conceptual: the group said so, and the poster is listed among the works. The poster promises a fight and delivers a performance, and the audience arrives for a spectacle, which is exactly what the performance gives them, only the fight is between a Venezuelan painter and a Mexican group, and the weapons are cardboard masks. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M10) built a ring with no front and let the walk be the work; Bustamante builds a poster with a fight on it and lets the arrival be the work, and the ring is replaced by the flyer. Cabrera (#337) laid one body down in sequence until it was a step; Bustamante lays one poster up in a street until it is a crowd, and the sequence is replaced by the turnout. **Takeaway:** Announce the performance as a wrestling match, in the poster's own language, and let the poster be a work; the crowd that arrives for the fight is the audience the performance needs. Watch the poster: when the flyer promises a mano a mano and the event delivers masks instead of punches, and the poster is kept as a work, the mechanism is on.

## M7. The pregnant belly as prop

The body, padded and performed: styrofoam bellies and aprons in MADRES! (1987); the host Guillermo Ochoa given a belly on live television, crowned Reina del hogar, taking nausea pills; Maris sawing Mayer's styrofoam belly the day before her daughter Neus was born; the apron as a sewing pattern in Agenda Colombia (1982), viewed first-hand, a dashed cut-line apron with two pockets over a faint landscape sketch, the figure's head a photographic portrait of Karl Marx, the speech bubble "MUJERes del mundo: unios!", the hands folded demurely at the belly. Pregnancy as something you can try on, take off, and saw open. The belly is a costume, and the costume is the argument: motherhood is a role the culture hands women, so the artists hand the role to a male television host and watch him wear it. The 1987 television footage was not watched this run; the account rests on Monica Mayer's first-person account and the Polvo de Gallina Negra history, and the study does not describe the broadcast from footage it never saw. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M9) put the artist's own body inside a stack of wire rings, the body completing the sculpture while present; Bustamante puts a false body on a television host, the false body completing the argument while broadcast, and the standing-inside is replaced by the putting-on. Silva (#339 M7) geometrized the figure and kept it; Bustamante pads the figure and gives it away, and the figure is gone, only the belly remains. **Takeaway:** Make the belly from styrofoam, put it on a man on live television, and let the costume do the arguing; pregnancy you can try on is motherhood you can question. Watch the belly: when a padded belly is worn, crowned, and sawed open, and the wearing is the point, the mechanism is on.

## M8. The recipe as the artwork

A set of instructions you take home and perform in your kitchen: the evil-eye recipe for rapists, brewed in a cauldron at the Hemiciclo a Juarez on 7 October 1983 before about a thousand spectators at a feminist march against rape, Mayer, Bustamante, and Herminia Dosal in witches' costumes around the cauldron, brewing a potion to give rapists the evil eye, handing out the brew in sachets, the recipe published in FEM magazine and a feminist agenda in 1984; "Las Recetas del Grupo Polvo de Gallina Negra", read at the closing of La fiesta de quince anos at the Escuela de San Carlos in 1984; and the group's very name is a protective powder bought in the market, polvo de gallina negra, black hen powder sold against the evil eye. The recipe is the work's portable form: it fits in a sachet, it is performed in a kitchen, it needs no museum. The cauldron is the stage and the sachet is the souvenir, and the souvenir is also the instruction. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M8) pinned words in a grid and let the reader recombine them; Bustamante prints a recipe in a magazine and lets the reader brew it, and the recombination is replaced by the cooking. Escobedo (#335 M3) repeated one frame and let one parameter drift until a snake emerged; Bustamante repeats one recipe and lets one cook drift until a potion emerges, and the drift is the work. **Takeaway:** Write the recipe, brew it in public, hand it out in sachets; the kitchen is the gallery and the instructions are the sculpture. Watch the sachet: when a potion is mixed in a cauldron and pocketed by the crowd, and the printed recipe outlives the performance, the mechanism is on.

## M9. Television as the stage

The performance made for the broadcast, not the museum: "social performances" for the non-arts public, as the English Wikipedia puts it; Madre por un dia on Nuestro Mundo (Televisa, Canal 2), 28 August 1987, both artists in styrofoam pregnant bellies and aprons, a ventriloquist doll as Catalina Creel from the telenovela Cuna de lobos, the host crowned and dosed; Instantanea con Frida (1991, Mexican television), title, year, and medium only, no description found; the 1984 television lecture-performances in the Tres Madres project, including "Contra el arquetipo de la madre" on Canal 7. The audience is whoever happens to have the set on. The work reaches people who would never enter a museum, and it does so by speaking television's own language: the host, the crown, the doll, the nausea pills, the nine-month joke (nine months later someone phoned Ochoa to ask if it had been a girl or a boy). The widely repeated "around 200 million viewers" estimate for the 1987 broadcast is not carried as fact here: it exceeds Mexico's 1987 population and is implausible as a Mexican audience, likely a garbled or cumulative estimate. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M10) built a ring in a lava field and let the visitor be the center; Bustamante builds a performance in a television studio and lets the viewer at home be the center, and the lava field is replaced by the living room. Goeritz (#321) built the Ruta de la Amistad as a corridor experienced from a car; Bustamante builds the performance as a broadcast experienced from a couch, and the car is replaced by the set. **Takeaway:** Make the performance for the television, not the museum, and let the broadcast be the venue; the audience that never enters a gallery is the audience the work is for. Watch the set: when a pregnant artist crowns a news anchor on live television and the channel does not change, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The authority face swap

Put the wrong head on the body: her head replaced by Karl Marx's in Agenda Colombia (1982), viewed first-hand, the bearded portrait over a sewing-pattern apron, the speech bubble "MUJERes del mundo: unios!", the slogan in the bubble contradicting the face; her own face given a phallus nose to answer Freud in El pene como instrumento de trabajo (1982), viewed first-hand, the pale fabric phallus stitched in place of the nose with the seam visible down the middle, the black eyeglasses separate from it, not integrated as one museum description has it; the Mona Lisa pose with Groucho glasses, MoMA's simile for the posed documentation photograph, quoted here as MoMA's, not as a description of what the photo shows (what the photo shows is the glasses, the phallus nose, a pink lipstick kiss print, and a pink "maris bustamante" stamp). The swap is the argument: the authority's face, on a woman's body, saying a woman's slogan. Marx says "women of the world unite"; Freud's theory gets a nose it can smell itself with. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M4) built the letter large enough to walk into; Bustamante builds the head small enough to swap, and the walking-into is replaced by the swapping. Hersua (#340 M7) repeated one disc and let the viewer's angle drift until a circle became a bar; Bustamante repeats one face and lets the head drift until the authority becomes the joke, and the drift is the work. **Takeaway:** Take a familiar portrait, change the head, and let the new head say the slogan; the swap is the argument and the face is the evidence. Watch the head: when Marx's beard sits over a sewing-pattern apron and the slogan contradicts the face, the mechanism is on.

## M11. The anti-homage

Honour the master by mocking him: the Primer inventario de las formas PIAS en Mexico de 1922 a 1992 includes (anti) homages to Pablo Picasso, Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz, and Marcel Duchamp; the Gerzso and Cuevas actions (1978, 1979) are homages staged as attacks, a kidnapping and a fake-legitimation system for paintings found to be fake. The master's colours are carried away in plastic bags (Trece planos molidos de Gerzso: pigments in the colours of one of his paintings, in small plastic bags, a portable painting for the recipient); the prodigal son is told he is a defeated son who had to return to be the same, and his father is also a loser. The attack is the tribute: only a master worth stealing is worth kidnapping, only a star system worth attacking is worth a postscript to its catalogue. The group called itself a workshop of critique, of attitudes and positions, not a theatre, and the critique is aimed upward, at the masters, never down. Against the chain: Felguerez (#330 M8) used titles as triggers that the paintings then neutralized; Bustamante uses homages as triggers that the actions then detonate, and the neutralization is replaced by the prank. Hersua (#340 M11) built the pyramid of today from the sensation of standing at Monte Alban; Bustamante builds the anti-homage of today from the sensation of standing in front of a master, and the pyramid is replaced by the kidnapping. **Takeaway:** Pay tribute by staging an attack, and let the attack be the honour; the master's colours in a plastic bag are worth more than a wreath. Watch the tribute: when a homage turns into a prank and the prank is aimed at a master, and the victim enjoys it, the mechanism is on.

## M12. The individual inside the group

No-grupo's rule: each member makes their own action on a shared theme, keeping autonomy, and the sum is the Montaje. Bustamante's taco patent is signed by her alone inside the group; her El pene is her personal contribution to the shared Caliente-Caliente; the Agenda Colombia page is hers alone inside a day-planner made by more than forty artists. The group is not a forced and fraudulent collectivism, in its own words, which frees responsibility in comfortable anonymity; it is a workshop of critique, of attitudes and positions. Four separate actions happen under one title, and one of them is visibly signed by a single hand. The mechanism is the group's answer to the problem of collectivity: the group exists so the individual can be bolder, not so the individual can hide. Her own vocabulary, mask, apron, recipe, was already formed by 1982, a year before Polvo de Gallina Negra, inside the group and signed by her. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M10) built a ring of 64 identical modules around an empty center and let the visitor be the center; Bustamante builds a group of four autonomous actions around a shared theme and lets the signature be the center, and the ring is replaced by the roster. Silva (#339) proposed the Espacio Escultorico and Hersua's maquette won it, a collective authorship where attribution to any single member is futile; Bustamante's group is the opposite collective, where every action carries its author's name, and the attribution is the point. **Takeaway:** Build the group as four separate actions under one title, and let each one carry its author's name; the collective is the frame and the signature is the work. Watch the signature: when four actions share a theme and one of them is visibly signed by a single hand, the mechanism is on.

## M13. The cheap material

Cardboard, photocopy, plastic bags, bricks, styrofoam, a kitchen cauldron: "poor" materials against grandiloquence. The mask is die-cut cardboard, viewed first-hand, the photograph pasted on card, the eye holes cut out with a blade; the books are bricks, Artists Books I II III (1982), publications made of bricks; the belly is styrofoam; the pigments travel in plastic bags; the potion is brewed in a cauldron. Expensive ideas carried by the cheapest possible stuff; a brick presented as a book. The cheapness is not a budget, it is a position: against the oil painting, the bronze, the marble, the whole apparatus of the masterpiece, and for the materials of the street, the market, the kitchen, the school. The group were pioneers of popular urban aesthetics and low-cost "poor" materials, and the poverty of the material is what lets the work be handed out, worn, eaten, and taken home. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M13) used rough material, honest matter, stone and iron left to speak; Bustamante uses cheap material, dishonest matter, cardboard pretending to be a face, styrofoam pretending to be a belly, and the pretence is the point. Cabrera (#337 M2) cut one T into stone and let the incision carry the body; Bustamante cuts one face into cardboard and lets the cut carry the body, and the stone is replaced by the card. **Takeaway:** Make the work from the cheapest material that will hold the idea, and let the cheapness be the argument; a brick that is a book beats a book that is a brick. Watch the material: when cardboard, styrofoam, and plastic bags carry the whole idea, and the work could be handed out on the street, the mechanism is on.

## M14. The photograph as the performance

For the camera, not the room: the Malba work is "a sort of performance for the photographic camera"; the taco exists as a black-and-white photograph by Alfredo Nunez; the posed El pene documentation with the kiss print and the pink stamp, viewed first-hand, is a performance staged for the still image, Bustamante standing against a plain wall, wearing the glasses-and-phallus-nose, one hand on her hip, the other resting on her belly, a black t-shirt with a white squiggle line and a small pouch-like object attached at the chest, a pink lipstick kiss print and a pink stamp reading "maris bustamante" with a pink star at upper right. A gesture held still for the shutter; the photograph, not the event, becoming the work that survives. The 1977 Paris masks were never seen by the group in Paris at all: they were seen by the public who wore them, and then by the camera. The performance that survives is the one the photograph holds, and the photograph is the work the museum acquires: Malba acquired the photograph and the mask in 2020, the period copy signed by the artist with the No Grupo stamp. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M14) staged a performance that steals itself, children carrying crowns away; Bustamante stages a performance that photographs itself, the camera carrying the work away, and the theft is replaced by the shutter. Escobedo (#335) engineered failure, boats designed to sink; Bustamante engineers the still image, a gesture designed to hold, and the holding is the work. **Takeaway:** Stage the gesture for the camera and let the photograph be the performance; the shutter is the venue and the print is the work that survives. Watch the shutter: when a pose is held for the still image and the photograph, not the event, is what the museum acquires, the mechanism is on.

## M15. The domestic object turned stage

The apron, the cauldron, the bed, the kitchen: women's work made into the set. The apron as a sewing pattern in Agenda Colombia (1982), viewed first-hand, dashed cut-lines, two pockets, the head replaced by Marx; the apron as a uniform in MADRES! (1987) and in the 1991 abortion march, viewed first-hand, two women in aprons walking together, one in a floral apron over a visibly rounded belly, holding a hand-painted placard ("Si a la MATERNIDAD Voluntaria NO a la GUERRA / Polvo de Gallina Negra") with a drawing of a hen, the Catalina Creel ventriloquist doll in a checkered dress standing among the marchers; beds for the Foro de la Mujer installation; Corazon sangriento (2004) performed in her own kitchen, title, year, and venue only, no description found. A kitchen object moved onto a stage and used as if it had always belonged there. The domestic is not the background of the work, it is the set, the costume, and the subject at once: the apron is the uniform of the role, the cauldron is the laboratory of the potion, the kitchen is the theatre. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M5) gave the outside one hot color and the inside another, the color doing the work of the door; Bustamante gives the outside an apron and the inside a belly, the costume doing the work of the argument, and the color is replaced by the cloth. Silva (#339 M12) built the altar and the gate as a triangular void that is the point; Bustamante builds the kitchen as the stage that is the point, and the void is replaced by the apron. **Takeaway:** Take the apron, the pot, the bed out of the house and put them on the stage; the domestic object is the set, and the set is the argument. Watch the apron: when a sewing-pattern apron is worn as a uniform in a march and the kitchen has become the theatre, the mechanism is on.

## M16. Humor as the delivery system

The joke lands before the argument: "humor riesgo", caustic to absurd, in her own account; satire as the working method; the laugh that lets a feminist argument be heard at all. Monica Mayer's formulation, quoted in the Polvo de Gallina Negra history: if it does not use humour, people do not hear you or they attack you. The mechanism is the order of operations: first the laugh, then the point. The taco is funny before it is a patent; the phallus nose is funny before it is a reply to Freud; the kidnapped master is funny before it is a critique of the star system; the crowned news anchor is funny before it is a performance about motherhood. The humor was dismissed at the time as insufficient for political discourse, which is exactly why it worked: the joke gets past the guard that the argument cannot. The group's performances were criticized and even violently heckled, and the heckling is part of the record: the joke provokes the reaction the argument needs. Against the chain: Hersua (#340 M16) made silence and solitude the material, the quiet at the center of the ring; Bustamante makes laughter and noise the material, the joke at the center of the performance, and the silence is replaced by the laugh. Gurria (#336) kept time with a hammer; Bustamante keeps time with a punchline, and the signal is replaced by the joke. **Takeaway:** Tell the joke first and let the argument ride in behind it; the laugh opens the ear the lecture cannot. Watch the laugh: when the audience laughs and then goes quiet as the joke's target becomes clear, the mechanism is on.

## Evolution

1949-1970: formation. Born 20 November 1949, Mexico City, to a Mexican mother and a Catalan father, a refugee of the Spanish Civil War. Painting since age 11. Studies at La Esmeralda 1968-1973; teaches there 1974-1976; helps found the INBA CEDART art-education centres 1976-1980. No painting from these years is named or photographed in any source read; the early work is asserted, not shown. The first happening, 1971, in the lobby facing the MUCA at the Facultad de Arquitectura, UNAM, is the first datable work and already non-objectual: a lobby action inviting the public in, not an object on a wall.

1977-1983: No-grupo. The core years, ages 27 to 33. The sequence: 1977, the Paris Biennale masks, absent presence; 1978, the Gerzso kidnapping, satire aimed at a master painter; 1979, the Cuevas attack and the Taco Patent, her first fully individual signature inside the group; 1980, the Radio-novela, the museum itself as the joke's target; 1982, Caliente-Caliente and El pene, the feminist turn inside the group, the mask as a distributable object, and the brick books and Agenda Colombia, the group as publisher and as political ally; 1983, La muerte del performance at MAM and the split. Eleven Montajes de momentos plasticos in 1979-1983. The arc inside the group: from sending her face away (Paris, 1977) to putting her face on 300 strangers (1982); from mocking male masters (Gerzso, Cuevas) to mocking Freud and machismo directly; from the group as a workshop to her own feminist vocabulary, mask, apron, recipe, already formed by 1982, a year before Polvo de Gallina Negra.

1983-1993: Polvo de Gallina Negra. The feminist decade. 1983, the witches' cauldron and the evil-eye recipe; 1984, domestic labour as subject and the 36 lecture-performances in state schools, plus the first feminist manifesto; 1987, MADRES!, the most ambitious project, which moves the work onto national television and makes her own pregnant body the material; 1990, Tres Madres para un Desmadre; 1993, the group formally ends. The shift: the target moves from the art world (Gerzso, Cuevas, the museum) to the institutions of everyday life (the home, the school, the television studio, the street march); the prop moves from the mask to the apron and the styrofoam belly; the audience moves from the museum crowd to the television public and the school hall.

1990-2004: solo, thinly documented. Obsenikus (1990, a bar), Instantanea con Frida (1991, television), Yo Tarzan, Tu America (1992, Austin), Corazon sangriento (2004, her kitchen). The titles alone tell a story: from the bar to the kitchen, from the public stage to her own house, from the collective to the solitary body. But that story is an inference from four titles; no descriptions, reviews, or photographs of any of the four were found, and no study section is built on them beyond what the Hammer Museum's selected list gives.

2001 onward: researcher and teacher. Rockefeller grant 2001 for the history of Mexican non-objectual art 1969-1979 (a different project from the Primer inventario de las formas PIAS 1922-1992, and the two must not be merged); director of the Centro de Artes, Humanidades y Ciencias Transdisciplinarias since 2005; Fulbright visiting professor, San Francisco State University, 2008-2009; UAM Azcapotzalco professor for more than thirty years, now professor emerita; writings in the major anthologies (Corpus Delecti 2000, Arte [no es] Vida 2008, Asco: Elite of the Obscure 2011). Retrospectives and anthologies after 2010 (No-Grupo at MAM 2010-11; Perder la forma humana, Reina Sofia 2012-13; Polvo de Gallina Negra anthologies 2022-23) reframe her as a historical figure. The evolution ends where it began: the work becomes the archive, and the archive (Fondo No-Grupo, Arkheia, MUAC) becomes the work's afterlife.

## Aesthetic logic

The work is a joke told in the cheapest possible materials, aimed at the most powerful possible target, and handed to the audience to finish. The logic has four moves, always in the same order. First, take something revered, a master painter, a national food, a psychoanalytic theory, a television host, and remove it from its place. Second, replace it with something cheap and bodily, a cardboard mask, a patent form, a styrofoam belly, a recipe in a sachet. Third, hand the replacement to the audience, the public, the viewer at home, and let them wear it, eat it, brew it. Fourth, let the laugh do the work the lecture cannot: the argument arrives after the joke, and by then the audience is already wearing it. Nothing is illustrated and nothing is monumental; everything is reduced to a mechanism a body can wear, eat, or brew. The signature move is the distribution: the work is finished not when it is made but when it leaves the room in someone's hands. The anti-object stance is literal: the object is refused, and what remains is the paper, the photograph, the recipe, the broadcast, the laugh.

## Network

Hersua (Manuel Hernandez Suarez, b. 1940; study #340): the doorway. The No-grupo arose from meetings at the sculptor Hersua's house, and Hersua is named among the early participants; he was NOT one of the four permanent members. His house is the literal meeting place; his Espacio Escultorico generation is the older circle Bustamante's group reacted against and learned from. Melquiades Herrera (b. 24 May 1949, d. 18 October 2003; exact dates single-sourced): No-grupo co-founder, San Carlos graduate, professor of geometry, visual education, and design at San Carlos and ENAP, "winner of the Nobel prize in art" by the group's nickname, a performance pioneer in his own right. Alfredo Nunez (b. 1949): No-grupo co-founder, San Carlos graduate, "proprietor of abandons" by the group's nickname; took the black-and-white taco photograph for La patente del taco. Ruben Valencia (b. 1949, d. 1990): No-grupo co-founder, San Carlos graduate, "ex-geometric artist" by the group's nickname; Bustamante's husband, father of her daughters, including Neus Valencia Garcia de Bustamante, born 1988. Juan Acha (1916-1995): Peruvian-born critic and theorist, the group's acknowledged influence; coined "arte no-objetual" and "No-Objetualismos"; directed the Primer Coloquio de Arte No Objetual, Medellin 1981, where No-grupo participated; the theoretical bridge from Hersua's generation (he wrote two books on Hersua) to Bustamante's. Monica Mayer (b. 1954): Polvo de Gallina Negra co-founder (1983-1993); her 1978 El Tendedero at MAM's Salon 77-78 Nuevas Tendencias is what first moved Bustamante toward feminist art, in Bustamante's own account; they met properly when Mayer and Victor Lerma interviewed No-grupo in Europe in 1980; ten years of joint work. Carlos Zerpa (Venezuelan artist): No-grupo's guest opponent in Caliente-Caliente (1982); known primarily as a painter, also active as a performance artist in the early 1980s and close to No-grupo. Gunther Gerzso (1915-2000): established painter, target of the 1978 kidnapping action; he played along. Jose Luis Cuevas (1934-2017): established artist, target of Atentado al hijo prodigo (1979) at his MAM retrospective. Herminia Dosal (photographer): third founder of Polvo de Gallina Negra (21 June 1983), left soon after over aesthetic differences; present at the first performance. Victor Lerma: with Mayer, interviewed No-grupo in Europe in 1980; the interview is where Bustamante and Mayer became friends. Carla Stellweg: worked at MAM at the time; familiarized the group with the term "performance", which they rejected in favour of Bustamante's Spanish "Montajes de Momentos Plasticos". Raquel Tibol (1923-2015): art critic, photographed wearing a mask in the Caliente-Caliente audience. Ana Victoria Jimenez (photographer, feminist archive): took the 1991 Polvo de Gallina Negra demonstration photograph; also a participant in the 1984 Se solicita esposa lectures. Andres Bustamante (brother): comedian (El Guiri Guiri); Maris designed sets and props for his television work and invited him into the El Cuervo contraespectaculo of the mid-1980s. Institutions: Museo de Arte Moderno (the group's main stage and the 2010-11 retrospective); Museo Universitario del Chopo; MUAC-UNAM Centro de Documentacion Arkheia (Fondo No-Grupo, holding the masks, posters, and photographs); Museo Amparo (2022 Polvo de Gallina Negra anthology); Museo Reina Sofia (Perder la forma humana 2012-13); Hammer Museum, Radical Women (2017); MALBA (holding El pene, acquired 2020); UAM Azcapotzalco (her teaching base); La Esmeralda and the Academia de San Carlos (the two schools the four members came from).

## Study-method note

First attempt at #341, Julio Cesar Chara, was abandoned after a full research leg: one collective work, zero solo works, no dates, no training, no exhibition, no interview, no museum holding, no Wikipedia article, no auction record after 18 distinct search queries, and the leg's notes are kept at hidden_files/study341/research-notes.md. The Chara leg also corrected two items the chain had carried: the phrase "los inseparables" for the 1969 Arte Otro trio (Hersua, Chara, Sebastian) has no locatable source and is not repeated here, and "Julio Cesar Schara" (poet, critic, academic) is a different person from "Julio Cesar Chara" and must not be merged, though whether "Chara" is a misspelling remains an open archival question. A parallel collision found this run: Nao Bustamante (Chicana performance artist, b. 1969; Indig/urrito, 1992, a burrito strapped to her hips, white men invited to eat it) is a different artist from Maris Bustamante and must not be merged; the shared surname and the shared 1992 burrito-adjacent performance year make the confusion easy. "Contra los superheroes gringos" (1979), listed only in the English Wikipedia infobox, returned zero corroborating hits and is not used as a grounded work anywhere in this study. The Frieze claim of a "65-metre-long taco" has no second source and is not repeated as fact. The "around 200 million viewers" estimate for the 1987 broadcast is not repeated as fact. The runner spot-checked two photographs first-hand: the El pene mask object (die-cut cardboard head, black eyeglasses with white oval eye holes, pale fabric phallus stitched in place of the nose with the seam visible, tag reading "maris bustamante / DEL NO GRUPO" with a star), confirming the leg's viewing and the correction to MALBA's "lentes con pene integrado" wording (the glasses and the phallus nose are two separate pieces); and the Caliente-Caliente poster (lucha-libre flyer, "Sensacional mano a mano", Zerpa portrait left, group photo right, NEW YORK, PARIS, LONDRES with drawings, both dates in small print), confirming the leg's transcription.
