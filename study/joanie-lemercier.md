# Joanie Lemercier: Study Notes

**Site:** Joanie Lemercier (b. 1982), French visual artist and environmental activist, based in Brussels; co-director, with curator Juliette Bibasse, of Studio Lemercier; co-founder of the visual label AntiVJ; the practice is light projected in space -- "Light as a medium, space as a canvas." Born in Brittany (his own words: "born and raised in Brittany"); Fundacion Telefonica gives Rennes, 1982, while the AntiVJ chronology gives Pithiviers, 21 June 1982 -- the birthplace is unresolved (C2521). No formal art-school degree or university training appears in any source read; the training on the record is his mother, an art teacher who taught pattern design for fabrics, and two decades of self-taught coding and projection work (C2421). The arc runs 2005-2026: club-night projections in Bristol, the AntiVJ label, architectural mapping and stage design, the turn to gallery installations in 2010, the noise-function volcanoes and the water-screen works, the Hambach coal mine in 2019, Feral File's +GRAPH in November 2023, and the Solar Lab's wind-and-sun machines from 2024. His own account of the method: "I can use light to modify the perception of the viewer." His stated program, on the future: "My vision of the future goes more toward degrowth and reducing our impact. Low-tech -- I think there's enormous potential in using the energy and light from the sun."

## The life: patterns at five, projectors at twenty

The origin story Lemercier tells in every interview starts with his mother. She was an art teacher who also taught graphic design at a fashion school, where students used computers to draw patterns for fabric. His school was across the street from where she taught; from the age of five he crossed over twice a week to sit at a computer and draw patterns for textiles. "There's a deep connection between how I started using a computer and what I do now; my love for patterns comes from this background" (CLOT, 2020). That early education, his site says, "grounded his interest in physical structures: geometry, patterns, and minimalist forms."

He started his career as a visual artist at about twenty, moved to Bristol in the UK, and began projecting almost by accident: a friend asked him to do projections for a club night, "and I started using projectors to make art, and I've never stopped" (Contemporary Fashion, 2021). His own site dates his work with projected light to 2005; Sotheby's, artnet and Berliner Festspiele say 2006 (C2412). In 2006 -- or 2008, depending on the source (C2411, C2501) -- he co-founded the visual label AntiVJ with Yannick Jacquet, Romain Tardy and Olivier Ratsi. The label's own site reconciles the spread in substance: "In 2006, I started using the name AntiVJ as a nickname for my solo VJing performances" (his blog), while the four-artist label dates to 2008. AntiVJ was built around projected light -- video-mapping, tracking and stereoscopy, stage design for festivals such as MUTEK (Montreal, Mexico), architectural projections worldwide -- and through it he worked with musicians including Flying Lotus (a special show at the Roundhouse, London), Portishead's Adrian Utley (London 2012 Cultural Olympiad), Murcof, Thomas Vaquie and Before Tigers. He later stepped out of the label he had created; a CMU course page summarises the parting as 2013, but the year is secondary-sourced (C2512). The Jay-Z name appears on his site with no detail found in any source read (C2417); the Barneys New York commission of 2013, where creative direction was shared with Shawn "Jay-Z" Carter, is the documented instance.

In 2010 he turned his focus from stage and club work toward installations and gallery work, exhibiting at the China Museum of Digital Art in Beijing, Art Basel Miami and the Sundance Film Festival; his site says he has been represented by a New York gallery since 2010 (a 2013 press release names Muriel Guepin Gallery -- current representation unverified, C2416). In 2013 he founded a creative studio in New York; since 2015 the studio has been based in Brussels, co-directed with independent curator Juliette Bibasse (b. 1984), with whom he has collaborated since 2013. Bibasse curates the studio's shows, directs the (Un)Holy Light walk in Leuven, and co-founded SALOON Brussels.

## The turn: from the club to the sublime, 2010-2018

His work has always run on two parallel tracks, in his own account: geometric patterns -- rooted in the Op Art of Victor Vasarely and the repetitive-pattern research of the 1970s -- and landscape. The landscape track began as a reaction against living inside the computer: after a decade of dark rooms and laptops he started hiking on every work trip, and discovered the Romantic sublime -- Caspar David Friedrich, William Turner, landscapes "so huge and so immense that you can't really grasp them." He set out to explore the same sublime "through the lens of technology, computers, and algorithms": volcanoes, mountain chains, oceans, rendered as wireframe topographies and algorithm-distorted grids.

The turn has a date, a residency, and a method. *Eyjafjallajokull* (2010), developed during a onedotzero residency at EMPAC, New York, is a wireframe topography of the Icelandic volcano drawn across two perpendicular walls, with a layer of projected light laid over the painting as a "virtual layer" that adds colour, animation, motion and depth. The working method, in his own account: a 100 by 100 grid in x-y-z space, points moved only on the Y axis, then Perlin noise, simplex noise, turbulence and Voronoi applied -- scale, offset, contrast -- to turn the grid into landscape, ocean, dunes or mountains. "With this noise, with this texture, you can recreate a cloud, the mountain, the surface of the ocean... Even if you don't have a degree in mathematics, with a single function, you can create a very, really wide variety of realistic landscapes." The noise inventor is described by Lemercier only as "an American guy working in VFX in the film industry... worked on Tron in 1982"; he does not name him, and the study does not assert the identification (C2418).

The same two-layer method -- a drawn or printed base, plus a projected light layer -- carries the next eight years: *FUJI* (2014), a large hand drawing of Mount Fuji intensified by projected light, retelling the 10th-century Tale of the Bamboo Cutter (Princess Kaguya) with light as the storyteller; *La Montagne, cent quatorze mille polygones* (2016-2018), an imaginary valley built from a single algorithm-distorted grid of 114,000 polygons, the polygon count itself the title; *Pantheism* (2016), straight lines and repetitive patterns standing in for landscapes, rocks, planets and caves, after Sol LeWitt's murals and Spinoza; *Nimbes* (2014, with James Ginzburg of Emptyset), a dome cosmogony of forests, cathedrals, deserts and volcanoes; *Cosmos Express* (2017) and *Nebulae* (2019, Sonar+D), the cosmos strand at train-window and dome scale. Alongside them, the surface itself starts to disappear: *Brume* (2017) and *Constellations* (2018) project light onto atomised water mist, so the image hangs in the air with no screen -- the technique he calls the No-logram, "not a hologram": "I've used various techniques to explore similar aesthetics: peppers ghost... semi transparent screens, mirrors and lenses, which are often referred to as 'holograms' but are in fact cheap tricks and just 2d projections. To avoid misleading use of the word hologram, I use the term No-logram (not a hologram)."

## The practice: the cloud problem, the plotter, the mine

Three load-bearing ideas organise everything he makes, all in his own words or in the words of the people who write about him.

First, the cloud as the hard problem. For years, Lemercier says, clouds resisted every digital tool: "the inner structure of clouds being one of the most challenging to recreate with software, many of his attempts to represent skies with digital tools fell flat." He photographed hundreds of real clouds while travelling. It took him a decade to find his way through them: pointillism. In the *Point Cloud* series (silkscreen prints from 2016; developed 2019-2021) he transforms cloud photographs into monochrome fields of dots whose positions and density are computed by a "weighted Voronoi" algorithm -- dot density standing in for depth, shadow, gradient and brightness. One later work, *Pointcloud (Trang)*, is a single towering cumulus photographed above Trang Airport in Indonesia on 8 January 2018, computationally abstracted into a dense cloud of data points (C2470).

Second, the plotter as an extension of the hand. *Paysages Possibles* / *Possible Landscapes* (2016-2021) is a plotter machine that acts as an extension of his hand, drawing unique landscapes from simple code, after Vera Molnar. In 2023 he collaborated directly with Molnar herself -- then nearly 100 -- on *Compositions Lumineuses* for LLUM Barcelona (a generative laser, video and sound machine producing minimal geometric compositions at facade scale) and *Carre Magique d'un trait lumineux*, animating her magic-square compositions after Durer's *Melencolia* (1514). His stated position, from that project's text: the art lies "in an arrangement between what is determined by the artist (rules, protocol) and what remains undetermined (accidents, chance)"; the machine "only implements what it would be unable to imagine on its own." The LeWitt sentence he quotes there -- "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art" -- is the hinge of the whole practice.

Third, the mine as the new landscape. In 2019 he visited the Hambach open-pit lignite mine in Germany, about two hours from Brussels -- Europe's largest single source of CO2, a pit 8 km by 5 km that had erased some forty villages and all but 10 percent of a 12,000-year-old forest, emitting about 270,000 tons of CO2 a day. "It turned me into a climate activist overnight." He attended the last mass of a church built in 1893 in Immerath before its demolition, watched a police officer strike an 18-year-old protester in the face with a metal beam, and joined Extinction Rebellion, Ende Gelande, Les Soulevements de la Terre and Fridays for Future. He began taking lasers and power banks to protests, projecting slogans and the Extinction Rebellion hourglass logo onto Buckingham Palace, the Houses of Parliament and other buildings -- light as graffiti that leaves no mark -- and running online workshops to train other activists to do the same. He is careful about the label: "I don't necessarily use the term 'artivism'... I like being an artist and an activist at the same time. I believe those two things are very different" (objkt, 2025). The activism fed back into the gallery work: his first major solo show, *Paisajes de Luz* / *Lightscapes* at Fundacion Telefonica, Madrid (5 February - 26 September 2021, curated by Bibasse, seven installations), is built in two halves -- the first a poetic tour of volcanoes and computer-generated mountains, the second a confrontation with the Hambach mine: drone footage, the bucket-wheel excavator Bagger 293 scraping the largest man-made hole in Europe, the *Slow Violence* installation on the forest's destruction, and *With Action, Comes Hope*, footage of the activism itself. The show drew about 86,000 visitors in Madrid by his own approximate count (C2424) and has since travelled to Mexico City, Lima, Arequipa and Le Havre. His *Point Cloud* series was featured in the New York Times (13 April 2021) as the illustration of the ethical cost of energy-intensive Ethereum NFTs; he left Ethereum for two years, advocated #CleanNFT, and now works primarily on the energy-efficient Tezos chain (C2419).

In November 2023 he joined Feral File's *+GRAPH* -- curated by Casey Reas, the sequel to 2021's *-GRAPH* -- as the one new artist alongside the five returnees (Iskra Velitchkova, Licia He, Aleksandra Jovanic, Julien Gachadoat, James Merrill), each building generative software that makes limitless drawings and selecting 30 to render as physical plotter works. He was NOT in *-GRAPH* 2021; the sixth artist there was Tyler Hobbs (C1904, re-verified from the toshareproject roster this run; Hobbs himself is already studied, #220, C2502). Reas's sentence on Lemercier, verbatim: "Joanie is focusing on climate science and the changes to his environment through rendering fragments of clouds." The +GRAPH work's title is the study's one open question (C2520): Arshake captions the illustrated work "Fine Particles" (image file "Joanie-Lemercier-Fine-Particles.jpg"), while the physical plotter drawings in the Bre Pettis collection are catalogued as "Nuages Possibles" #30, #31, #33 and #34, 2023, white ink on black paper, 27.9 by 27.9 cm. Both titles attach to the same cloud-fragment plotter series in the sources read; the study uses "Nuages Possibles" for the drawings and records "Fine Particles" as the Arshake caption, without asserting which is canonical.

Since 2024 the newest strand is the Solar Lab, co-founded with Bibasse: "a playground for experiments and thoughts about low tech, energy and resources all in connection with the sun and the wind." Its prototypes are low-tech kinetic devices activated by sun, wind and water -- solar reflectors shared as open-source technology, shown at the second Solar Biennale *SOLEIL.S* at mudac, Lausanne (March-September 2025), and a first kinetic piece with no motor and no electricity, *Wind flow*, a grid of 3D-printed modules balanced on nails that self-align with ambient airflow, after Vera Molnar's *Inclinaisons* (1971). His most recent drop, *Incomplete Landscapes* (objkt.one, 4 December 2025), is a slow, ever-changing browser-based projection of vectorised mountain landscapes -- HTML, JavaScript and WebGPU, built with his assistant and technical developer Martin Pirson -- whose collectors can export a frame and receive a plotter drawing of it. He has been coding for more than fifteen years, he says, and is "fluent with basic coding" but leans on Pirson for high-end work.

## M1: The two-layer method -- a drawn base, a projected light

The method that carries the whole middle career: make the picture twice. First a hand-drawn or printed base -- a wireframe volcano painted on a wall (Eyjafjallajokull), a mountain drawn in horizontal lines behind bamboo (FUJI), a valley printed as a mesh (La Montagne), a Fuji drawn as lines (Edges). Then a projected light layer laid over it that adds colour, animation, motion and depth. The two layers are independent: the drawing is still, the light moves; the drawing is the place, the light is the weather. The Barneys windows are the commercial extreme of the same method -- paper-and-glass sculpture by day, generative projection by night -- and *Paper and Light* is its cheapest form, folded A4 pyramids catching projected light at different angles. The mechanism is visible in the work: you can always tell where the drawing stops and the light begins, and the pleasure is watching the light rewrite the drawing without touching it.

**Takeaway:** a piece that is two pictures at once -- one fixed, one projected -- where the projection is the animation and the drawing is the anchor; the seam between them is the work.

## M2: The noise mountain -- one function, any landscape

A 100 by 100 grid in x-y-z space; points moved only on the Y axis; Perlin noise, simplex noise, turbulence and Voronoi applied -- scale, offset, contrast -- and the grid becomes landscape, ocean, dunes or mountains. That is the whole engine, and it runs for a decade: Eyjafjallajokull's volcano, La Montagne's 114,000-polygon valley, the wireframe terrains of Nebulae. The polygon count is sometimes the title, because the count is the work: a mountain you can count. The noise function is doing the job a degree in mathematics would do -- Lemercier has none, and says so: "Even if you don't have a degree in mathematics, with a single function, you can create a very, really wide variety of realistic landscapes." The sublime, in this version, is a parameter.

**Takeaway:** terrain as a grid plus a noise function, where the parameters (scale, offset, contrast) are the visible controls and the mountain is what the function happens to say.

## M3: The screen that disappears -- mist, water, open air

After a decade of projecting onto walls, paper and sculpture, the surface itself starts to disappear. Brume (2017) projects onto a screen of fine water droplets; Constellations (2018) puts the water screen on lakes and rivers, the image hanging in the air over real water; Lightfall (2024) fills a dark room with a curtain of falling mist that is invisible when unlit, so panes and rings of laser light seemingly solidify in mid-air and the visitor walks through them, body temperature dropping as they enter the water curtain. The technical problem is the invisibility: the droplets must be fine enough to vanish unlit and dense enough to catch light -- "super fine particles of water, high pressure gaz and custom nozzles" (Pylon Hub). He refuses the word hologram for it: the No-logram is a 2d projection onto a 3d medium, and naming it honestly is part of the work. The evolution, stated as a dematerialisation: wall, then paper sculpture, then mist, then open air, then -- in the Solar Lab works -- no electricity at all.

**Takeaway:** the projection surface as the variable -- make the screen thinner and thinner until the image floats, and the viewer's body entering the image is the interaction.

## M4: The weighted-Voronoi cloud -- dots standing in for depth

Clouds resisted every digital tool for a decade, so Lemercier stopped simulating them and started sampling them. Photograph hundreds of real clouds; run a weighted-Voronoi algorithm over the photograph; the output is a monochrome field of dots whose density stands in for depth, shadow, gradient and brightness. The reduced number of dots gives "a sense of volume, transparency and particles density that our computer models would fail to preserve." Point Cloud (2016) does it on paper as silkscreen; *Pointcloud (Trang)* does it to a single cumulus photographed 8 January 2018; *Nuages Possibles* / *Fine Particles* (2023, +GRAPH) does it generatively, the software producing limitless cloud-fragment compositions and 30 selected as white-ink-on-black-paper plotter drawings. The photograph is the data source, the algorithm is the translator, the dot field is the cloud. A cloud is, after all, already a field of particles.

**Takeaway:** a photograph reduced to dots by a density rule, where the dot field is the image -- density is depth, and the cloud is what the dots agree on.

## M5: The plotter as hand -- code that draws like a person

The plotter strand runs through the whole career and is the bridge to this study. *Paysages Possibles* (2016-2021) is a plotter machine that acts as an extension of his hand, drawing unique landscapes from simple code, after Vera Molnar. The +GRAPH drawings (2023) are the same idea at exhibition scale: generative software, 30 selected variations, physical 1/1 drawings in white ink on black paper. *Incomplete Landscapes* (2025) closes the loop: the browser projection is the piece, the collector exports a frame, and Lemercier plots that frame -- the buyer finishes the work by choosing the moment. With Molnar herself in 2023, the position is stated outright: the art lies "in an arrangement between what is determined by the artist (rules, protocol) and what remains undetermined (accidents, chance)". The machine draws; the artist decides what the machine is allowed to decide.

**Takeaway:** a generative system whose output is a physical drawing, where the selection -- 30 of limitless, one frame of an endless animation -- is the artistic act.

## M6: The documented sublime -- the mine replaces the volcano

The pivot of the whole career. The simulated sublime (volcanoes, mountain chains, oceans rendered as wireframe) is replaced by a documented, real sublime of destruction: the Hambach surface mine, 50 sq km, the largest open-pit lignite mine in Europe, emitting about 270,000 tons of CO2 a day, erasing a 12,000-year-old forest and several villages. He filmed it by drone, swooping to the bottom of the crater nearly 1,000 feet below sea level (RWE bars photographers from the pit). The five chapters of *The Hambach Forest and the Technological Sublime* move from sun-dappled remnants of the old forest to the colossal machines chewing biologically dead soil, to eco-activists alighting on the mine; chapter five pans across empty plots where people once lived. Part of the piece is "extremely contemplative, almost peaceful, like a suspended moment, the beauty of this Technological Sublime." His own account: "my work about landscapes -- the landscape is now the coal mine... the loop is closing." The title's frame is Rob Nixon's slow violence: destruction whose 40-year CO2 lag makes it hard to comprehend at human scale. From here on, the work's energy budget becomes part of the work -- carbon footprints published, flights refused, NFTs moved to Tezos, and finally the projector itself questioned.

**Takeaway:** a real damaged place, documented by drone, presented as the sublime -- the landscape is now the wound, and the beauty is the trap.

## M7: Light as protest graffiti -- a projection that leaves no mark

The same equipment, pointed at power. Lasers and power banks and solar panels, carried to protests, projecting slogans and the Extinction Rebellion hourglass logo onto Buckingham Palace, the Houses of Parliament, a bucket-wheel excavator, the pit edge -- "stop this madness" written in light along the edge of the mine. The mechanism is the point: a projection leaves no mark, needs no permission, and vanishes when the power is cut; it is graffiti with an off switch. He runs online workshops training other activists to do the same, from up to 500 m away. The work is careful about its own category -- artist and activist at the same time, two different things -- and the gallery work absorbs the method: *Slow Violence* and *With Action, Comes Hope* put the activism itself on the wall in Madrid. The architectural mapping of 2007 (Light Up Bristol) returns, twelve years later, as a political instrument.

**Takeaway:** projected light as temporary writing on buildings -- the message appears, the building is unchanged, and the disappearance is part of the statement.

## M8: The machine that does nothing -- wind, sun, mirrors

The late turn is away from the projector entirely. The Solar Lab (co-founded 2024 with Bibasse) is "a playground for experiments and thoughts about low tech, energy and resources all in connection with the sun and the wind." *Wind flow* is a grid of delicate 3D-printed modules, each balanced on a nail, reacting to ambient airflow -- no motor, no electricity, the first kinetic piece, formally after Molnar's *Inclinaisons* (1971): grids animated by subtle angular variations, between order and chance. The compositions are generative, shaped by the living environment, never repeating, and the piece is designed to be assembled anywhere from simple instructions, avoiding travel. *The Color Machine* (2026) is a motorised sculpture of 19 mirrors and multiple coloured light sources, slowly shifting reflections across the space -- a tribute to Charles and Ray Eames' Do-Nothing Machine, "a machine set in motion simply to create an experience, rather than to produce anything." The work that began by adding light to surfaces now removes the machine and lets the environment do the drawing. The degrowth sentence behind it: "My vision of the future goes more toward degrowth and reducing our impact."

**Takeaway:** a machine with no motor and no electricity, where the wind or the sun is the animator -- the environment draws the picture, and the artist builds the frame that lets it.

## The aesthetic logic: the screen that disappears

Lemercier's aesthetic is a twenty-year dematerialisation, and it has three terms. First, the surface thins: wall, then paper sculpture, then mist, then open air, then no machine at all -- each step removes something between the viewer and the light, and the image gets closer to being nothing but light. Second, the subject stays put: the grid, the line, the dot, and the belief that light can change what a surface is -- Op-Art pattern and Romantic landscape, the two tracks he named himself, never merge and never leave. Third, the energy becomes the ethics: the carbon footprint of a show, the flights refused, the NFT chain switched, the projector questioned, the wind doing the work for free. The work is at its best when all three terms are present at once -- a real place, a thinning screen, an honest energy budget -- as in Lightfall, where the mist is the screen and the sensors switch it off when nobody is there, or in the +GRAPH drawings, where the cloud is a dot field and the plotter is the hand. It is at its thinnest when the technique runs ahead of the subject, as in the early club mappings, which are precise and cold, or the cosmos pieces, which are spectacular and unheld. The evolution, stated as one sentence: he spent twenty years learning to put light on things, then spent the last years learning to take the things away -- until only the light, the wind, and the damaged ground are left.
