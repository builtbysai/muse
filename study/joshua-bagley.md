# Joshua Bagley: Study Notes

**Site:** Joshua Bagley, generative artist, Instagram and Twitter @gengeomergence; joshua-bagley.com. He lives in western Massachusetts and studied aerospace engineering at the University of New Hampshire; in 2021 he left a job in engineering to follow generative art full time. No birth date appears in any source read for this study, and none is stated here. [C39427]

**Doorway:** The 470th doorway. Takes up the open question from the Ivan Sutherland study (#849), quoted verbatim: "when the hand, the instrument, and even the clock are gone, what does the gesture still mean -- and who is it for?" Bagley answers it by removing the hand altogether. In his work there is no hand, no instrument, and no clock the viewer can touch: a seed curve is set down, and the system grows it by its own rules. The gesture is the seed -- a small closed shape, chosen once -- and what it means is whatever the system makes of it over a life the viewer only watches. The hand never touches at all, and the meaning is the life record the growth leaves behind. [C39428]

## M1 -- The three forces: a curve that grows itself

**Subject:** A closed polyline that folds itself into an organism.

**Visual principle:** Complexity from three local forces only. Nothing is placed by a hand; the eye reads the folds as a living thing precisely because no part was drawn. Density reads as age, open loops read as youth.

**Computational mechanism:** The Spaghetti Bones project description states the algorithm verbatim: "You start with a string of nodes connected to one another to form a shape. Each node feels 3 forces: A repulsion force from all other nodes; An attraction force towards its immediate neighbors; An attraction force towards the midpoint between its neighbors. As you add more nodes into the shape, these 3 rules cause the shape to curve into itself, growing into a complex form of layered undulating lines." The goal, in his words: "to create a shape that fills as much space as possible while maintaining a rigid form and avoiding overlapping itself. By focusing specifically on where and when you add more nodes, and how you interpret those nodes into a shape, there is much variety to be found in this simple algorithm." That last sentence is the whole design space: node-insertion policy plus node-to-form interpretation, on top of three fixed forces. The biological model is explicit: "The term is used in biology to describe how certain systems in plant life work, i.e. how matter grows, fills space, and maintains its structure. An obvious example in nature is a cabbage." He learned the technique in December 2020 from Anders Hoff's description of a differential line, and it has been "a continuous focus of mine ever since." The 2023 interview says he first learned "this technique" in "late 2022" instead; the December 2020 dating from his own site is used here, and the discrepancy is unresolved. A piece built on this keeps the forces fixed and lets insertion policy and interpretation vary: edges longer than a maximum subdivide (a node is inserted), edges shorter than a minimum collapse, a spatial hash keeps repulsion near linear in the node count, and the node count is capped (about 1200) so the piece can run live. Note the honest boundary: the force-ablation idea in BAG8 -- removing repulsion on a schedule and watching the organism knot -- is a derived experiment on his stated rules, not a Bagley work; he never published a force-removal study, and the prompt must not claim he did.

**Artistic opportunity:** The growth IS the motion and the drawing at once. The piece is a system being watched, not a picture being made, and its temperament -- fast, sluggish, trembling -- is set by three strengths and one insertion policy.

**Takeaway:** Three forces and an insertion policy are the whole organism. Everything else -- the folds, the density, the cabbage -- is what the rules do when they are left alone. [C39431]

## M2 -- Tracing and occlusion: the life record

**Subject:** Not the growth, but the history the growth leaves behind.

**Visual principle:** Each stage of the organism's life is stamped as one outline, like rings on a tree. The finished image is a biography, not a snapshot; age reads as color, density reads as history.

**Computational mechanism:** Every output of Spaghetti Bones is a long-form simulation with a life arc: "Each output of 'Spaghetti Bones' starts with a simple shape as a seed for the differential growth algorithm. Throughout the rendering process, the shape is fed more nodes, and grows until it cannot find any empty space to fill. As it grows, a form is interpreted from the nodes of the growth and drawn onto the screen, creating a snapshot of the growth at that particular moment. There are many rules that govern how the shape grows, and how its form is interpreted, and these rules often evolve throughout the rendering process. When the render is complete, the resulting image is a full catalog of the history of the shape's life." The move that turned the experiment into a project was tracing plus occlusion, described in the 2023 interview: "At first, I was tracing an outline of the shape as it grew continuously on the canvas, isolating different steps of the growing process using color. I decided I wanted to minimize the amount each outline could overlap one another on the canvas, which ultimately was the idea that changed the algorithm from an experiment to inspiration for my next project. By isolating each outline on the canvas, and creating line breaks in areas where outlines overlapped, complex patterns started to emerge." The tree-ring image recurs in both texts: the traces mark "each stage of its life, like rings on a tree stump." In a piece, this becomes a stamping rule: every K ticks, stamp the current polyline as a permanent ring, colored by life stage; any stamped segment that would fall within 2px of an already-stamped segment is broken with a gap of about 6px, so no two rings overlap and each outline keeps its own space. The live organism is drawn faintly beneath its own history until space exhaustion stops it.

**Artistic opportunity:** Time becomes the composition. This is the doorway piece's mechanism: the seed is the gesture, and the rings are what the system made of it -- a meaning written by growth, not by a hand.

**Takeaway:** Bagley's real invention is not the growth but the record: isolate each outline, break it where it would overlap, and the life of the organism becomes the picture. [C39432]

## M3 -- The rules themselves age

**Subject:** A growth whose rules change as it lives.

**Visual principle:** The organism has a youth, a maturity, and an old age, and each looks different because the rules, not just the shape, have changed. The piece has a biography built into its rules.

**Computational mechanism:** "In total, there are 59 variables that work together to create each output of Spaghetti Bones. The bulk of these variables go towards defining rules for how the life-form grows/ages, and how to trace a path through the nodes that make up the life-form. But living things evolve, and in Spaghetti Bones, many of these variables also evolve. Aspects like the overall size, growth rules, and even how the path is traced, often are allowed to change throughout the rendering process." The parameters are not fixed per output; they drift during the render. That is the key mechanical difference from a static differential-growth sketch. A piece takes the 59-variable idea down to an age schedule: repulsion radius, subdivision threshold, curl strength, trace width, and trace color are all functions of age, in three fixed stages (youth / maturity / old age), after which node feeding stops and the organism dies. The schedule is the score; the growth is the performance. One irreversible life, no regrow loop.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece with a beginning, a middle, and an end that are all visible as different looks -- the same organism, three rule regimes, one death.

**Takeaway:** In Spaghetti Bones the variables are not set; they age. A fixed schedule of changing rules is a stronger piece than a fixed set of rules, because the life has an arc. [C39433]

## M4 -- Light through the growth: Spotlight's photon optics

**Subject:** Growth shapes you can only see by the light that has passed through them.

**Visual principle:** The shapes are faint outlines; the picture is the light. Thousands of photon dots accumulate along reflected and refracted paths, and the structure of the shapes is revealed slowly, like an exposure developing.

**Computational mechanism:** Spotlight (Art Blocks Playground, released Tuesday 22 February 2022) began from watching light refract through a glass cup. "Spotlight begins by arranging a series of shapes on the screen. Each shape has its own color and boundaries. Shapes range from perfect polygons with equal sides and angles, to differential growth blobs with endless folds and curves... These shapes are not drawn, but you may display their outlines at any time by pressing 'd' in the live view. Next, 3000 photons spawn and begin traveling across the canvas with set velocity and heading. Once a photon collides with a shapes edge, it has a two options: reflect or refract. Reflection causes the photon to bounce off of the edge of the shape, while refraction allows the photon to pass through. Both events change the photons heading using real world mathematical equations of reflection and refraction." The drawing is the history of the light: "As a photon moves, it traces its path by periodically placing small circles. The color of these circles depends on the color of the shape the photon last interacted with." Texture is built from a single mark: "Spotlight has a strong focus on creating texture using only one drawn element: small circles. There are no hard edges or solid fills defining shapes, only the history of light as it moves through the canvas." There are "checks that prevent circles from being placed directly on top of other circles (in most circumstances)," and "Between 3 and 4 million circles is an average number to consider an output fully rendered." Differential growth returns here as shape material: "Watch for Spotlight mints with 'Form: Differential Growth' which include these beautiful shapes." Technically the project uses a quad-tree, learned from Daniel Shiffman's videos, and a pseudo-random number generator by Piter Pasma. Palettes mix three carried over from earlier projects (Asteroid, Fog, Kyra) with seven new and one generative ('Chroma Leak', a grayscale palette sampling one color from the others); 'Wingtip Sunrise' was sampled from a photo Bagley took during a 5 a.m. flight home from the Art Blocks meetup in Marfa, Texas.

**Artistic opportunity:** Bagley's second system layered on his first. The growth is the lens; the light is the drawing. It is the only mechanism in this study where the growth itself is never drawn at all.

**Takeaway:** Spotlight proves the growth engine is reusable as material: the same blobs, lit instead of inked, become a completely different picture. [C39434]

## M5 -- The seed is destiny: topology as the only choice

**Subject:** The same rules, a different starting shape -- and a different organism.

**Visual principle:** Initial conditions are destiny. The viewer can see the seed inside the adult, because the topology of the start constrains everything that follows.

**Computational mechanism:** The growth core (M1) run from different seeds: a circle, a rounded triangle, a crescent, an open string with two free ends. For the open string, end nodes have only one neighbor, so they wander under a doubled curl term and coil into spirals; ends can never join, because there is no closure rule. Growth concentrates where the topology allows it -- at the free ends for the open string, everywhere for the closed curve. When total length exceeds its cap, the oldest middle segments stop subdividing, so growth concentrates further at the ends. Bagley's released projects all close the curve; the open string is the unexamined variant of his own starting point ("You start with a string of nodes connected to one another to form a shape"). The seed is the only thing the artist chooses; everything else is the system's answer.

**Artistic opportunity:** A controlled experiment made visible. One rule set, several seeds, several organisms -- the piece is about how little choice the artist needs to make for the system to make something new.

**Takeaway:** The seed is the gesture in Bagley's work: a single small choice, and the system does the rest. [C39435]

## M6 -- Two growths, one space

**Subject:** Two growths that want the same space.

**Visual principle:** The contact front between two organisms is the event. One eats the other, node by node, and the front is the only thing that moves with intent.

**Computational mechanism:** Two closed growths (M1 core each) seeded at opposite edges, same three forces each. The stopping rule from Spaghetti Bones -- each output "grows until it cannot find any empty space to fill" -- is here turned into a contest: when a node of one organism comes within a capture radius (6px) of a node of the other, the organism with fewer nodes at that moment loses the node -- it is removed from the loser and inserted into the winner's polyline at the nearest edge -- at most one capture per 3 frames. Below 12 nodes the loser dissolves. A spatial hash finds contacts. The winner keeps growing until space exhaustion, holds, then a new pair seeds. The mechanism is staged, not observed in Bagley's work: his growths compete with the field, never with each other. The capture rule is this study's, not his; the growth core is his.

**Artistic opportunity:** Growth as contest. The piece has a plot -- a beginning, a front, a death -- generated by one local rule, and the winner varies by seed, so the plot is never fixed.

**Takeaway:** One local rule (the smaller loses a node on contact) is enough to give a growth a story. [C39436]

## Dreams: unequal subdivision

Bagley's first Art Blocks release, Dreams (Art Blocks Curated, 2021, 700 editions), is "a study of unequal subdivision," emphasizing how uniform and crooked lines interact and the intricate patterns that can develop from simple rules. Bagley himself: "Fun colors and funky shapes. I wanted to make something that was simple yet contained a lot of hidden complexity. I especially wanted to create static compositions that felt alive." The system is a trait machine for emergence, fully documented on his Features page: hand-picked palettes inspired by old sci-fi art, comics, and posters (the palette 'Kyra' was sampled from a photo of his 13-year-old leopard gecko); Structures Stripes (60.29%, 422/700), Complex (29.57%), Maze (10.14%); Stories Chaotic, Sporadic, Repetitive; continuous Reality (Locked to Lost) and Anchor (Shallows to Floor); rare traits Paralysis (47), Flying (14), Corrupt (7), Disconnection (5), Clarity (11), Pure Minimal (4). And he is explicit that the defined traits are not the whole story: "There are going to be many emergent styles and traits to find, beyond the ones that are defined." Outputs inspected: Dreams #246 (flat teal-and-cream warped stripes crossed by chunky pink, green, ochre, and black blocks); Dreams #629 (violent yellow-and-black zigzag stripes sliced by a diagonal band of red, blue, and white blocks -- Bagley calls it the output closest to his earliest tests, "like someone took a knife and sliced through the center of the canvas, spilling out color"); Dreams #268 (hot pink, mint green, yellow, and navy warped blocks, "very distorted buildings" that give vertigo). The visual constant: flat color, hard edges, warped stripe fields interrupted by blocky intrusions. His own criterion for a good output is two-ended: #629 is the reminder of the initial idea ("I find that it can sometimes be easy to lose track of your initial idea when developing and expanding upon a project, so when that one came out, it was a nice reminder that I didn't change things too drastically from what made me love it initially"), and #268 is genuine surprise ("When I saw it, I didn't fully understand exactly how it came to be"). The standing criterion for Dreams: "Look for the way the color and structure combine to create a story in each output." [C39437]

## Ecumenopolis: the city the algorithm saw

Ecumenopolis (2021) was created shortly after Dreams: "While I was making Dreams, some of the patterns I was seeing in the test outputs reminded me of strange, crooked, wobbly cityscapes. With that inspiration, I took the Dreams algorithm as a starting point and expanded it to create otherworldly planet-wide cities." His site confirms the lineage: "Dreams and Ecumenopolis were two very connected projects. Both used similar fundamental building blocks to create, and the idea for Ecumenopolis was born while developing Dreams. The focus of both projects was to create complexity from simple rules." The Features page documents a building-block city system: every building is made of blocks; Height determines how many blocks make up each building, Scale the size of each block; Lean (None to Max), Complication (None, Wind, Unsure, Kinked) -- Wind carries an honest, unresolved bug: "Wind always blows from right to left, causing the buildings to lean to the left. This was unintentional, and I am still unsure what the cause of this is"; Vertigo and Warping govern thickness change; Stacked puts buildings on buildings; Blueprint (Chaos/Structure); Architect and Painter (Single/Multiple); Takeover's two special cases -- Robot City ("fragmented patterns on the faces of the buildings... a city where robotic life has taken over, creating buildings with circuit-board-like patterns") and Overgrowth ("patterns that continue from one building to the next, ignoring the boundaries of each building... an abandoned city where plant life has taken over"); Lights (On/Off/Party, only for monochromatic palettes); Atmosphere (Clear to Heavy fog); Themes Atmospheric (monochrome, palettes named after gases), Typical (multi-color, mostly Dreams palettes), Anomaly (one color per building, brightness varied). Outputs inspected as images (paired feature illustrations from his own page): #116 and #95 Atmospheric, two monochrome cities, one blood red, one acid green; #530 and #538 Typical, striped pastel towers; #289 and #605 Anomaly, buildings dissolved into flowing ribbons; #430 and #344 Lights: Party; #205 and #305 Overgrowth; #382 and #310 Robot City; #57 and #227 Complication: Wind, towers bent like combed hair. ## Isoplanet: the loop becomes sand

The most recent work inspected is also the furthest from differential growth as line work. Isoplanet (Art Blocks Studio, 2025) is 300 unique lifeless worlds. Each edition ships with Sandcast Imaging software and a data file from one of 300 planets, gathered by fictional AFAR probes; the software renders the landscape with a falling-sand simulation in which each pixel is a simulated sand particle with mass, color, and sideways stability, poured by up to 4 sand generators following a closed-loop path. The framing is deliberate: Ecumenopolis studied the most densely populated places; Isoplanet "shines a spotlight" on the lifeless ones, never witnessed until the owner renders them. Output inspected: Isoplanet #31, diagonal striations in hot pink, red, teal, and ochre with a visibly pixelated sand-grain texture, reading as an aerial view of eroded strata. The growth logic is still there -- the closed-loop generator path -- but the rendering is granular and geological. The arc is: line on paper, then line inside a system, then the system's own history as the picture, then the system rendered as matter. [C39439]

## The arc

He taught himself Python in spring 2020 for his engineering job, got hooked on the problem solving, and by late summer 2020 was making cellular automata for fun; then Processing, "the program I use today." He lost his engineering job during the pandemic, found far more satisfaction making and sharing art, and came to Art Blocks as "the golden ticket to turning my hobby into a career" -- pragmatic as well as artistic: "the monetary potential of NFTs was enticing," and it was "an amazing opportunity to finally get my artwork out there on someplace that wasn't social media." Early (2020-2021, Instagram era): differential growth drawn as line or wash on paper-like grounds -- ink-black organisms, pastel mineral washes, confined rectangular patches; the organism is the whole picture, and the ground is paper. Middle (2021-2022, Art Blocks): the same growth engine embedded inside larger systems -- Dreams turns it into flat, hard-edged, high-chroma compositions about subdivision; Ecumenopolis reuses the Dreams algorithm for wobbly cities; Spotlight replaces line with photon-traced stipple and real refraction physics. Color moves from ink-and-paper restraint to full flat palettes sampled from life (a gecko, a 5 a.m. flight). Recent (2023-2025): Spaghetti Bones returns to differential growth as the sole subject but at full color and density, with the growth's own history traced as the image; Isoplanet drops the line entirely for falling sand, keeping only the loop-path generator. His method, verbatim from the Dreams interview: "My favorite method of creating art is to take a core idea or technique, learn how to do it the right way, and then break it apart." He leans, in Spaghetti Bones, into "adding ominous undertones to otherwise playful artworks." The mint is where the unknown enters, and he accepts it: "no matter how much you've iterated the algorithm in advance, you have no idea what will comprise the works in the series until the random element is introduced through the minting process." Even after "thousands of outputs" in development, release still surprises him -- and release re-opens the work: "when a project finally gets released after staring at it for so long during development, it takes on a new light. It's like I get to re-discover the project for the first time." [C39440]

## Aesthetic logic

Bagley's beauty is the beauty of a system that fills space without ever overlapping itself. One rule set (three forces, an insertion policy) generates the cabbage, the city, the photon field, and the sand planet, and every project is the same engine with one thing changed: the interpretation of the nodes. In Dreams the nodes become flat blocks; in Ecumenopolis they become buildings; in Spotlight they become the shapes light passes through; in Spaghetti Bones they become the life record itself. Nothing decorative is present that is not also a rule: the palettes are sampled from life (a gecko, a dawn flight), the ominous undertone is a color choice inside the system, and even the project's name came from looking at an output and naming what it resembled ("Spaghetti Bones" -- his partner Nicole's name for an early output, kept "to highlight the playfulness of each output, to encourage imagination when viewing this abstract art"). A piece built from Bagley should look like a life being lived on the canvas -- spare in its rules, dense in its result -- and it should be willing to let the system, not the hand, decide what the picture is. That is the doorway's answer in visual form: the gesture is the seed, and the system writes the biography. [C39441]
