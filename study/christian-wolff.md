## M1. Restricted pitch reservoir: three notes that feel like twelve

A tiny fixed set of absolute pitches, never transposed, with fixed registration, and the whole piece is the recombination of that pool. Duo for Violins of 1950 uses 3 pitches, the Trio of 1951 uses 4, For Piano I of 1952 uses 9, and Cage's assignment was to do a rhythmic structure monophonically with five notes, and Wolff's gloss is that with two instruments and three pitches there are twelve distinct sounds once combinations and releases and overlaps are counted, and Oteri calls the result "a serial minimalism, a generation before minimalism happened". What you see is a small pool of sounds recombined until the pool feels large, and the scarcity is the material, not a limitation on it. **Takeaway:** fix the pitches and free everything else, and three notes become a world. Against the chain: Feldman also restricted, M1, the graph time-boxes, but his boxes count sounds inside a register, and Wolff's reservoir fixes the exact pitches and frees the order, and the difference is between counting inside a frame and recombining a fixed handful.

## M2. Square-root rhythmic structure: the same ratio at two scales

A Cage-taught scheme in which the time proportions of the smaller structural units are the same as those of the larger total unit, and Wolff adjusted the total beat count to get a number with a square root, and the scheme stayed in use for fifty years, and Long Piano of 2004-05 uses it in patches 57 to 67, eleven larger sections, each with eleven subdivisions in the same proportions. What you see is self-similar time, the same ratio at two scales, and a piece that rhymes with itself without repeating itself. **Takeaway:** make the small time shaped like the big time, and the structure hears itself. Against the chain: Brown also freed time, M3, time notation, but his time notation spaces notes freely and lets the player judge the gaps, and Wolff's square-root structure fixes the proportions at both scales and frees only the content, and the difference is between a clock the player judges and a clock the composer proportions.

## M3. Aural cue coordination: play only when you hear it

Players play only in response to sounds they hear from other players, instead of following a beat or a conductor, and from 1957 Wolff introduced into his works various degrees of free choice, sometimes requiring the players to react to the musical activities of their partners according to spontaneous and unanticipated cues, and Duo for Pianists I of 1957 is the first fully cue-based work, and in For 1, 2 or 3 People of 1964 the relationships between sounds, within a part and between players, are what is notated, and pitch is secondary. What you see is musicians visibly listening and waiting, then answering, and the ensemble is a conversation with no chairman. **Takeaway:** notate the listening, not the beat, and the players become the clock. Against the chain: Brown also gave the choosing to a person, M4, the conductor as real-time chooser, but his chooser stands in front with placards and signals, and Wolff's choosers are the players themselves, cued by each other's sounds, and the difference is between one person choosing for everyone and everyone choosing by listening.

## M4. Invented and graphic notation: the page as a map

Scores that abandon the stave for symbols and lines and spatial layouts, and For Pianist of 1959 and Edges of 1968 are the landmarks, and Edges is "bits of information scattered over a page", "very loud" and "play dirty" and "play in the middle", which the player plays around or in relationship to, not literally, and Edges is the nearest Wolff came to a purely improvisational piece. What you see is a page that looks like a map, read spatially rather than left to right, and the notation is a set of landmarks the player navigates. **Takeaway:** scatter the instructions on the page, and the player reads the terrain. Against the chain: Cage also drew music, M10, graphic notation and transparent overlays, and his drawings are laid over a staff and read by chance operations, and Brown also drew music, M2, and his drawings are the score itself, read by a performer choosing, and Wolff's fragments are neither chance-read nor score, they are provocations the player plays around, and the difference is between a drawing that chance reads, a drawing that a person reads, and a drawing that a person answers.

## M5. Prose scores: the score as plain language

The score is plain-language instructions, and Stones, from the Prose Collection of 1968-71, says make sounds with stones, with a few general suggestions and one prohibition, do not break anything, and it runs 10 seconds to a couple of hours, for one to twenty people, and Wolff says the music "exists entirely in performance" and the score is a necessary but not sufficient condition, and the score for Stones is performable by people who have never played an instrument. The Prose Collection also holds Play and Song and For Jill and Sticks and Groundspace and Fits and Starts and You Blew It and Crazy Mad Love and Looking North and Double Song and Pit Music. What you see is ordinary objects and ordinary people becoming the ensemble, and the barrier between player and audience dissolved by a sentence. **Takeaway:** write the piece in sentences, and anyone can play it. Against the chain: Brown's scores are pictures, M2, and Feldman retreated to exact notation when he left indeterminacy behind, and Wolff went the other way, toward language anyone can read, and the difference is between a score that needs a specialist to decode it and a score that needs only a reader.

## M6. Counting and number games: structure by counting, not by melody

Structure by counting rather than by melody, and For 1, 2 or 3 People is ten pieces, any selection, any number of players, and dynamics-only passages specify "make 2 loud sounds within the next 1 second, then 1 very soft, one soft and one very loud sound within a next space of 2 seconds" with nothing else specified, and the counting is the form, and the sounds land on counted slots. What you see is performers counting aloud in their heads, sounds landing on counted slots, and a piece whose shape is arithmetic. **Takeaway:** count the slots and leave the sounds free, and the numbers become the melody. Against the chain: Feldman also counted, M1, the graph time-boxes, but his boxes count how many sounds happen in a fixed time, and Wolff's counting fixes when a sound lands and frees what it is, and the difference is between counting sounds and counting time.

## M7. Open instrumentation and variable player count: the piece re-forms with whoever shows up

Burdocks of 1970-71 is one or more groups of five or more players, any instruments, 10 to 55 minutes, and the Exercises are two or more players, free instrumentation, and For 1, 2 or 3 People specifies no instrumentation at all, and the same piece re-forms with whoever shows up, and the instrumentation is a variable of the performance, not a property of the work. What you see is the same piece arriving in a different body each time, and the ensemble is whoever is in the room. **Takeaway:** leave the forces open, and the piece fits any room. Against the chain: Brown's Available Forms also free the order, M1, but his forms fix the forces and free the sequence, and Wolff frees the forces themselves, and the difference is between a mobile with fixed parts and a piece that rebuilds itself from whoever arrives.

## M8. Heterophony: one tune, many near-unisons

Everyone reads the same single line, in treble or bass clef, so parallel lines drift apart by enharmonic differences into "constant parallel, slightly asymmetrical parallel sixths", and Wolff learned it from Dixieland jazz, Friday and Saturday nights on 8th Street as a schoolboy, and from gospel and Ornette Coleman, and the Exercises are "entirely based on that principle". What you see is one tune, many near-unisons, shimmering apart and back together, and the unison is never quite a unison. **Takeaway:** give everyone the same line, and the line splits into a shimmer. Against the chain: Brown also learned from jazz, M13, improvisation as collective conversation, but his conversation is players answering each other with different material, and Wolff's heterophony is everyone playing the same material and drifting apart, and the difference is between a conversation and a chorus that cannot quite agree.

## M9. Tablature and parameter-only notation: fix one thing exactly, leave the rest open

Specify one parameter precisely and leave the rest open, and Long Piano begins with a peace march in tablature, fingering and rhythm prescribed, pitches not, and its optional prelude specifies rhythmically when each of the player's ten fingers plays, but not what pitches, "a kind of tablature for counterpoint for up to ten voices". What you see is hands moving in exact rhythms while the notes stay free, and the precision is in the body, not in the pitch. **Takeaway:** notate the hands and free the notes, and the rhythm carries the piece. Against the chain: Brown's time notation, M3, fixes pitches and dynamics and frees durations, and Wolff's tablature is the mirror image, fixing rhythm and fingering and freeing pitches, and the difference is between a free clock on fixed notes and a fixed clock on free notes.

## M10. Found sound and everyday objects: the instrument is whatever is in the room

Stones, and Drinks of 1969 for glasses and drinks, and Burdocks played with "various sound producing objects" in the Rauschenberg tribute For Bob of 2006, with Takehisa Kosugi, and the score for Stones is performable by people who have never played an instrument, and the instrument is whatever is in the room, and the piece is a use for objects, not a demand for instruments. What you see is a table of stones and glasses becoming an orchestra, and the everyday made musical by attention. **Takeaway:** score the objects in the room, and the room becomes the instrument. Against the chain: Cage also used found sound, M8, the tape cutting and splicing of Williams Mix, but his cutting is a chance operation deciding where each of 600 recorded fragments lands, and Wolff hands the stones to the players and lets them choose, and the difference is between found sound cut by chance and found sound played by hand.

## M11. Tune as material: variation, not arrangement

From the mid-1970s Wolff works from folk and political songs, but does not arrange them, and his model is variations, "but subject to my way of writing, for instance, discontinuously", and in Bread and Roses of 1976 the violinist plays the tune straight, then the variations come, and in Wobbly Music of 1975-76 the chorus sings three early American labour songs, then Wolff does "my thing with that material", and in other pieces the tune is buried and unrecognisable to the listener, and the tune is shared property, not a quotation to admire. What you see is a familiar tune surfacing, then dissolving into fragments, and the recognition is the hook and the dissolution is the piece. **Takeaway:** play the tune straight once, then let it dissolve, and the tune becomes material. Against the chain: Brown's tape library, M6, cuts fragments until their origin is gone, and Wolff keeps the origin audible first and dissolves it after, and the difference is between a fragment whose past is erased and a tune whose past is remembered and then let go.

## M12. Discontinuity and patchwork form: jump cuts that add up

Material is assembled in discontinuous blocks that still add up, and Long Piano is 94 numbered patches, a few blank, meaning silence, modelled on Jennifer Bartlett's Rhapsody, 988 one-foot-square plates, 154 feet, first shown in 1976, and Incidental Music of 2003-04 is about 100 numbered pieces, and Wolff says "The procedures may vary... They make smaller continuities; the overall procedure is more about dis-continuity; but so that a lot of discontinuous material somehow adds up to a piece that works as a piece". What you see is jump cuts between short scenes that cohere by accumulation, and the whole is a mosaic, not a line. **Takeaway:** cut the piece into numbered patches, and the jumps become the form. Against the chain: Brown's open form, M1, also assembles blocks, but his blocks are reordered live by a conductor, and Wolff's patches are fixed in order and discontinuous in content, and the difference is between a reshuffle and a jump cut.

## M13. The worst-case test: freedom inside a fence, not freedom without one

Wolff's own compositional quality control for open notation, and he imagines the most outrageous thing a performer could do under the instructions, and if that worst case is acceptable, the notation stands, and if he can imagine something he really does not want, he changes it, and he also tests on the piano and crosses out what does not sound right, sometimes reusing crossed-out material later. What you see is a rule set with visible edges, and the freedom is bounded by a test the composer ran in his head before anyone played a note. **Takeaway:** imagine the worst a player could do, and if you can live with it, the notation is done. Against the chain: Brown also fenced freedom, M12, choice not chance, but his fence is the composed event itself, only those sounds may be used, and Wolff's fence is a test applied to the instructions, and the difference is between fencing the material and fencing the behaviour.

## M14. Decentred control: no conductor, or many

In orchestral writing Wolff tries to arrange that "the conductor does not control what is happening in the music" and that back-bench players get solos, and Ordinary Matter of 2001, three orchestras, one to three conductors, eighty parts, gives every member a solo, and the performance can begin with three minutes of music and nobody conducting at all, and authority is distributed across the stage, and the centre of the piece keeps moving. What you see is an orchestra with no centre, and the music starting before anyone is in charge. **Takeaway:** take the conductor out, or multiply them, and the orchestra governs itself. Against the chain: Brown's placard conductor, M4, chooses live in front of the orchestra, and Wolff removes the chooser or triples them until choosing is nobody's job, and the difference is between a visible chooser and a room that chooses together.

## M15. Silence and the admitted environment: the room itself plays

Early pieces are mostly silence with isolated sounds, and in those silences environmental sounds become listenable, and Cage's anecdote, retold by Tilbury, has someone asking to close a window during a Wolff performance, and Wolff saying it was not necessary, the sounds of the environment were no disturbance, and Wolff's own line is that extraneous noise "is going to be part of the acoustic experience... so why not accept it". What you see is long stillnesses in which the room itself plays, and the silence is not empty, it is open. **Takeaway:** leave the window open, and the street becomes part of the piece. Against the chain: Cage framed silence as the piece itself, and Wolff's silences are structural gaps inside pieces, and the difference is between silence as the subject and silence as the room the sounds live in.

## M16. Music and dance, independent and simultaneous: two streams, one clock

With Merce Cunningham from 1953, music and dance occur at the same time yet somewhat independently, revolutionary when they began it, and Wolff calls Cunningham's choreography the greatest influence on his music over the years, and for Cunningham Events he improvised in the pit, unable to see the dancers, agreeing with fellow players to stay silent for stretches, with Takehisa Kosugi and William Winant and Steve Lacy and Keith Rowe, and his music was used by the Cunningham Dance Company from 1953 onward. What you see is two independent streams sharing a clock but not a script, and the dance and the music meet only by accident, and the accidents are the piece. **Takeaway:** share the clock, not the script, and two arts can happen at once. Against the chain: Cunningham is shared with Brown and Cage, and Brown's version puts the set on stage as an independent performer, and Wolff's version puts the musicians in a pit where they cannot see the dancers, and the difference is between independence you can watch and independence you cannot see.

## Evolution

PHASE 1, EARLY, 1950 to 1956, restriction and structure. Very small pitch reservoirs, 3 to 9 pitches, square-root rhythmic structures learned from Cage, much silence, Webern as model, transparency and hocketed lines and harmonic stasis, and piano music dominates because David Tudor was the only available resource, and Wolff says "starting in '51, I think, all I did was write piano music because our resources were seriously limited. There was no money", and his self-assessment is "my first real pieces, I think of those early pieces with very small numbers of notes", and the phase runs from Duo for Violins of 1950 through Suite (I) of 1954, and the pieces are small, exact, and mostly quiet.

PHASE 2, MIDDLE, 1957 to 1971, coordination and openness. The aural-cue system in Duo for Pianists I of 1957, invented notation in For Pianist of 1959, the coordination pieces For 1, 2 or 3 People of 1964 and Pairs and Edges of 1968, prose scores in the Prose Collection of 1968-71, and a return to strict systems in Tilbury 1 to 4 and Snowdrop of 1969-70, and Wolff calls For 1, 2 or 3 People "the first piece I did that was more indeterminate than anything I'd done before", and the social idea is present but not yet political, and the pieces model a social relationship on stage without intending politics, and Burdocks of 1970-71 closes the phase with the first tune of Wolff's own.

PHASE 3, LATE, 1972 onward, politics, tunes, consolidation. The turn is dated to the late 1960s, civil rights and Vietnam, and Wolff asks "what are we doing with this esoteric kind of music when all of this stuff is going on in the world?", and the first explicitly political pieces are Accompaniments of 1972 and Changing the System of 1973-74, and the material shifts to folk and political songs, Bread and Roses and Wobbly Music and the Exercises, and rhythm shifts from durational to accentual, and notation looks more conventional but stays open, and the Tilbury pieces onward are the "fragile" ones, because they look determinate while remaining open, and from the mid-1970s the politics recede, and later work is more personal and memorial, For Morty of 1987 for Feldman, the Long Piano patch for Jackson Mac Low, who died during composition, and orchestral, Ordinary Matter of 2001 and Rhapsody of 2009, and Wolff's own summary is that he has "not really" changed since the mid-1970s, "I keep hoping that it might", and the early ideas return combined with the 1970s music, and the series continues to Exercise 39 of 2020 and Resistance 2 of 2024 and Solo Cellist with 5 Players of 2025, the 290th work on the list.

## Aesthetic logic

The score is a social contract, not an object, and that is the logic that runs through all sixteen mechanisms, and the piece is a situation between people, and the notation is the terms of the situation, and listening is the instrument, and the worst-case test is the ethics, and a rule is good when the worst thing it allows is still music. The early restriction is the same logic in miniature, a handful of pitches shared by two players who must listen to each other to know when to play, and the middle coordination pieces make the logic explicit, the score says when to listen and what to listen for, and the late political turn makes it public, the tune is shared property and the orchestra is a workplace where the back bench gets a solo and the conductor does not control what happens, and Cardew's line, quoted by Wolff, that the orchestra is "the ultimate image of alienated work" is the problem the late pieces answer by redesigning the work. Discontinuity is the same logic in time, the patches do not pretend to be one continuous argument, they add up the way a meeting adds up, and silence is the same logic in space, the room is admitted because the room is where the people are, and the window stays open because closing it would pretend the piece is sealed off from the world. The giver who will not take credit is the same logic as a person, and Wolff deflects the I Ching gift the way his scores deflect authority, and the baby of the New York School outlasted the circle by turning its tools toward other people, and the music is not about him, it is about what happens between the players, and between the players and the room, and between the tune and the people who already know it.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. FELDMAN QUOTE, HOLDS, with exact wording. The brief's paraphrase, "a symbol for me of the way I really would have wanted to have been myself", is accurate. Exact text in BOMB: Cage says "One of the most useful things that could happen for the musical life now is a concert that would let people experience Christian Wolff's music." Feldman replies, "Christian is becoming a symbol for me [of] the way . . . I really would have wanted to have been myself." Note the ellipsis and "becoming", and that the exchange was a 1966 radio broadcast, Cage prompting. The study uses the quoted form, not the paraphrase.

C2. I CHING GIFT, HOLDS, with Wolff's own qualification. The Bollingen edition had just been published by his parents' Pantheon Books, and Wolff gave Cage the copy, verified in three sources. But Wolff himself refuses sole causation: "that's not me, that's the I Ching" and "He was headed in that direction", adding that Feldman's graph pieces were already moving toward indeterminacy. The study presents the gift as catalyst, not as single cause.

C3. CHANGING THE SYSTEM DATE, CORRECT TO 1973-74. The brief says 1972-73. The composer's own works list says 1973-4, and Tilbury also says 1973-4 and names Accompaniments of 1972 as the first political piece. Encyclopedia.com says 1972-73 and Wikipedia says 1973. The study uses 1973-74, citing the composer's list.

C4. MUSIC OF CHANGES, 1951, NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED in the full pages read. No full page read here dates Music of Changes. The gift is verified and the chance period follows, but the study phrases it as "Cage's chance period, beginning with Music of Changes" without a year, and does not present 1951 as verified from this leg.

C5. OCTET I PREMIERE, HOLDS IN SUBSTANCE, date detail corrected. The brief says Octet I premiered at the same Illinois festival, March 1953, as tape works by Cage and Wolff. Ziegler confirms all four tape works were premiered at the Festival of Contemporary Arts, University of Illinois, in March 1953, and Williams Mix was premiered Monday 23 March 1953, weekday verified. BUT the Earle Brown Foundation's own page lists a performance on Saturday 28 February 1953 at the same festival while its program notes say "first performance... in March 1953", so the page contradicts itself, and the #401 study recorded a 22 March 1953 chronology date from the Foundation, so there are now three dates in play for the same premiere, 28 February, 22 March and March 1953.
