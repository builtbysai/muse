## The life: forty years of code, fifteen years of two lives

The biography is unusually well documented because Zancan tells it himself, at length, in two interviews: Waiting To Be Signed #076, "Beautiful Utopias" (transcript, ~2024), and Irish Tech News (6 August 2025, by Selva Ozelli). The study does not fill the gaps those interviews leave. The evidenced record: Michael Zancan, born in France in 1976 (Rifugio Digitale; bre.art collection cards -- secondary-sourced, no primary source, see C1724); "originally from the south of France" (Irish Tech News); lives and works in Bordeaux. No birth day or month, no school or university name for the electronics engineering degree, no name for the development studio he founded with a friend -- none of these exists in any source read, and the study invents none of them (C1734).

He learned to code at eight, from reference books, writing programs on checkered sheets of paper, able to type and test them only every two months or so, at his mother's workplace during school holidays. "I started coding at age eight and never stopped. It's been about 40 years now, across so many languages and computers, and here I am doing generative art in JavaScript." At fifteen he was in the demo scene, coding in assembler on an Atari: "It would have been pretentious to call it 'art' back then, but in hindsight, it was an early form of generative art, though immature and raw." The demo scene matters twice in this story -- it is the origin, and twenty years later its code comes back, replicated, inside the Bright Moments Finale (see M6 and W16).

Alongside the code ran a lifelong passion for drawing, "more serious -- and rebellious -- when I started graffiti", and then oil painting, "the most intimidating" medium, which he chose to confront. He wanted art school; his parents said, "as long as we're paying for your studies, you finish them, then you're free to do whatever you want." He graduated as an electronics engineer, spent one year, 2000, expatriated to Texas working at Texas Instruments, then quit to become a painter and taught himself oil painting. "It took me almost a year to finish my first painting, and that's when I understood I wasn't going to make a living from painting -- but programming was too important to me to give up either. So I went back to programming at the same time and kept doing both for years." For more than fifteen years he lived a dual life: "passionate oil painter on one side, creative coding studio director on the other." The studio, founded with a friend, ran for twenty years doing interactive work -- touch tables, video projections for museums, always real-time graphics and interactivity, "pretty demanding... it felt like mercenary work a lot of the time." That experience is the reason for the most consequential aesthetic decision in the whole practice: "That experience with interactive, real-time animation is actually the reason I decided generative art, for me, would only be still images."

He is a maker in the physical sense too: "I come from a painting background, and beyond that, I'm a maker -- I like to make things, I need to make things... I need to have made something physical in a day to call it a day, to feel satisfied. Doesn't matter if I'm building a wall, doing plumbing, or making a piece of art." He spent years building his house and studio. He is a single father of two daughters, as he stated it himself (WTBS, ~2024; Please Respond, 2026). He calls himself "a French artist working with code and pens" and his style "figurative-generative art" -- nature as the subject, code as the means, the painter's eye kept.

## The turn: Beeple, the plotter, and the right soil

He first heard about NFTs in March 2021, the day of Beeple's $69 million sale -- "It was in the news; I wasn't on Twitter, wasn't following anything, but I heard about it in the office that day." A coworker mentioned it; he spent the same evening researching and setting up a wallet. But the plotter came first. His studio partner had been doing plotter art for about two years; during lockdown they got funding to drive robots to draw things, and Zancan started building software to drive robots and plotters, eventually a big CNC machine they wanted to convert into a plotting device. He used his friend's generative art as test material, then "started generating my own SVG files just to test things, and I got hooked... I was practicing every day, making generative art every night to try on the plotter. I developed the art and the plotter software side by side." The company project was abandoned; he kept the software, and it is still the only software he uses -- nowadays it does embroidery too. His first plotted sketches were two simple ones: a tree, and grass. Those two seeds became trees.js and grass.js, the two algorithms everything else is built from.

The first NFT chronology is ambiguous and the study keeps it ambiguous (C1735). In April 2021 he minted a discreet test NFT on Tezos (Hic et Nunc), never listed it, eventually burned it. In September 2021 he released his first NFT properly: a digital version of the oak tree, split into 18 separate pieces on Foundation. A week later the first two Lushtemples minted on Tezos and sold out in seconds. The WTBS remark that "fx(hash) came along two or three months after my first NFT" fits September, not April, so the study treats September 2021 as the first NFT and April as the unlisted experiment. What changed everything was Tezos: his first Ethereum mint "felt emotionally and socially quite neutral. Most of the collectors remained anonymous. By contrast, arriving on Tezos just a week later was incredibly rich in emotion and connection... From then on, I began minting a new piece every Friday." "NFTs changed everything. They became the 'right soil' for my art to bloom. I had never entered the art world -- but NFTs created a new kind of art world that welcomed people like me."

## The practice: image first, algorithm second

The method is stated plainly in the Bright Moments interview and it inverts the usual generative-art story: "I visualize an image in my mind first and then explore which algorithm could help me achieve that vision, rather than starting with an algorithm and attempting to create graphics from it. The latter often leads to more mathematical and abstract visuals." WTBS, same point: "I worked the way I did when painting: I had an image in mind and built my code to reach that specific image... There was almost no randomness involved" -- early on. The painter's eye is the driver; the code is the tool. "For me, code is merely a tool to realize my visions."

Nature is the subject because code solves a painter's problem: "When attempting to depict a scene from nature, such as a grass field or a tree with many leaves, I always sought precision and detail... With code, I can now recreate a scene by repeating elements millions of times and introducing variations through randomness." And nature is the model for the system itself: "Nature is built from DNA, which contains the blueprint... However, accidents and struggles during growth cause each individual to be unique... The process in generative art is quite similar... by introducing randomness, you can achieve a variety of results." The Verse statement for Rapture Captured says it most directly: "In my works, nature is not the main subject, but it is a means of depicting, among other things, the vital force, the resilience of the living... Algorithmic art opened up this possibility for me to render nature, in its movement, its density, its wild abundance." On the yellow mesh in that work: pure yellow #ffff00 "immediately places it in the digital realm"; the holes in the net "symbolize the impossibility of capturing the true nature"; the flowers "twisted to the extreme under netting, are there to evoke the sense of hypocrisy in using technology to create art that evokes nature."

Two sentences carry the whole practice. On the work: "I never wanted to keep doing the same type of art over and over... every new release has something genuinely new, different from the last. Sometimes I rewrote the code entirely just for that one release... I just cannot do low-effort work. It's not in my genes." On the values: "I hope that at some point the original idea behind blockchain and NFTs resurfaces -- that it's not just about money, but about the beauty of openness, accessibility, decentralization. Those are beautiful utopias, and I really adhered to those values."

## The evolution: from oil to thread

The arc is a migration of one vocabulary across media, not a widening of it. Oil painting (c. 2000-2020, figure and lush nature, first painting almost a year) gives him colour, composition, and symbolism. Lockdown plotter work (2020-21) gives him the machine: art and plotter software developed side by side, SVG test files becoming the art. Spring 2021, the oak-tree research and the grass research split into trees.js and grass.js, two seed algorithms that later merge into one modular framework -- "a Photoshop for generative art" with many entry points, rewritten or extended for every release. September 2021, the first NFTs. December 2021, Garden, Monoliths turns the single-image practice into a 255-output system and becomes the platform's blue-chip standard. 2022 is collaborations and platform experiments: (kinder)Garden, Monuments with Yazid, 8BIDOU's randomized sale, A Bugged Forest's kept glitch, the Verse open edition that runs away with itself. 2023 is the physical return: robot-painted canvases with Artmatr, a coded landscape stretched across sixteen monitors inside a former WWII air-raid shelter in Florence. 2024 is thread: Aux Arbres, 76,208 iterations, built to look embroidered -- and then, after meeting Samer Dabra in Paris and Matt Jacobson at Plotterfest in New York, real embroidery, a half-ton industrial machine craned into the Bordeaux studio, and Tree 138, six months of uninterrupted work, 83 embroideries forming one tree. 2026 is code as shared material: Please Respond, inheriting another artist's program and revealing the model underneath it instead of writing from a blank page.

Through it all, one decision never moves: still images only, by choice, because his studio career was real-time and interactive. The motion lives in the first render -- the garden growing before your eyes, the tree assembling itself -- and then the image stops, and stays stopped.

**Takeaway:** the evolution is a migration, not an expansion. The same grass, the same tree, the same monolith, carried from oil to code to plotter to robot to thread -- and each medium is asked to do what only it can do with them.

## M1 -- The window that reveals the whole

The founding mechanism, and the one that explains the fragmentation everywhere else. The Oak Tree (spring 2021, first NFT September 2021): a vector-tree algorithm computes an entire oak -- trunk, bark, dense foliage -- but each drawing shows only a small window of it, a tight, immersive framing with the trunk mass slightly off-centre and dense leaves scattered around. Zancan discovered the code was calculating the whole tree while showing only a window, then patiently computed and plotted sample after sample -- hundreds of hours of plotting, each drawing 4-11 hours of pen work -- until all 18 framed A4 drawings, hung together, revealed the oak in its entirety. The fragmentation is the point: a visual metaphor for decentralisation, offered as 18 separate NFTs, each a 4200x5940 high-resolution PNG, each addressed by coordinates relative to part #0,0 (part #-1,+3, and so on). The system knows the whole; the viewer is given a window; the whole is recovered only by assembly.

**Takeaway:** the window is not a crop. The code computes everything and shows a part, and the part is addressed like a map tile. A piece that shows the whole tree would be a picture; a piece that shows one window of a computed whole is a system with a viewpoint.

## M2 -- Grass against the monolith

The signature image, and the mechanism that made the reputation. Garden, Monoliths (9 December 2021, fxhash, 255 unique editions, 18 tez): one algorithm, seeded per mint, grows a garden scene -- dense grasses, leaves, and flowers rendered as thousands of individual line strokes -- around one or more monoliths: stark geometric slabs and portals that read as ancient architecture. The algorithm and variables setup come directly from Monoliths #000, the 1/1 plotter drawing (50 hours of plotting, AxiDraw A1, Rotring Isographs down to 0.1 mm, Arches 300 g/m2) that is the common ancestor of every monolith in the series. The contrast is the mechanism: organic density against geometric stillness, growth against stone, and the monoliths "convey the process of aging to eternity" -- the garden is alive and temporary, the slab is dead and permanent, and the picture holds both at once. It sold out in under a minute; Zancan barely minted one himself, then sold his own copy the next day for 18 tez to a collector who missed out. Secondary volume reached about 1.2M tez on his own site's figure ("nearly $1 million" in the WTBS host's dollars -- different units, different dates, convert neither, see C1722). Kaloh's September 2022 analysis has it #1 by volume among fxhash's top ten at 545.3k tez, with the highest average price paid (2,102 tez) and the then-highest fxhash sale (75k tez).

**Takeaway:** the monolith is not a shape in the garden; it is the garden's opposite, and the picture is the tension between them. Take the slab out and you have wallpaper; take the grass out and you have a diagram.

## M3 -- The bug as co-author

The mechanism that proves the control was real by surrendering it. A Bugged Forest (2022, fxhash, 1,000 unique trees): a programming bug in the tree routine was kept, not fixed. The algorithm produces trees that are visibly "wrong" -- bent, broken, asymmetrical, each with unique character and flaws -- and the bug becomes co-author. The system is the same Tree.js/Grass.js core, shared functions for generative and plottable output, with the glitch left in as a feature. Released from Hellfest, Clisson -- "a hot, earthbound, hostile to some, friendly to others location" -- and the setting is part of the meaning: embracing uniqueness (even the freaks), releasing control through art, collective suffering in the face of climate change. Electric Chronicles, on Tree_Line, puts it as a principle: "code not as perfect design but as a field of contingency... control and surrender can interplay."

**Takeaway:** the bug is not a defect the artist tolerates; it is a second author he hires. A system that can only do what it was told is a printer; a system with a kept bug is a collaborator.

## M4 -- Two systems, one contract

The collaboration as a mechanism, seen from Zancan's side of the frame -- the mirror of the Yazid study's M6, and the doorway's hinge. (kinder)Garden, Monuments (with Yazid Azahari; fxhash, released on the opening morning of fxhash 1.0, 16 April 2022; 309 unique iterations, Vanilla JS, outputs in SVG): two codes fused in one frame. Zancan's grass.js garden grows first -- "we witness the growth of a peaceful and inviting garden. Foliage and flowers steadily grow before our eyes" -- while Yazid's building blocks (cubes, triangles, spheres, prisms, from his Hashed Cities) stack vertically inside the garden in playful colours, reimagined in three dimensions, "bursting through the serene natural garden". Where Garden, Monoliths' structures convey "aging to eternity, the small towers here suggest ever-repeating cycles of youth" -- kindergarten building blocks, childhood play, a garden as playground. The work is also a protocol first: "the first project on fxhash to allow collaboration on the smart contract level" -- multiple artists releasing together and sharing revenue and royalties in perpetuity. Because the outputs are SVG, collectors were offered the code to plot or print themselves, plus a print shop for signed, numbered prints. One iteration, #96, was offered at Sotheby's Digital Art Day 2024.

**Takeaway:** the collaboration is a mechanism because the two systems stay visible inside one frame -- the garden grows, the blocks stack, and neither absorbs the other. The smart contract is the frame that lets them share the wall.

## M5 -- The sale as an output

The joke that is also a system. 8BIDOU (2022, during the fxhash downtime before 1.0): an algorithm computes the arrangement of pixels for an 8-bit rendition of Garden, Monoliths -- pixel art stored immutably on-chain as 8x8 pixel data. The translation is deliberately ridiculous ("absolutely nuts, didn't make sense at all"), made because the 8-bit style was trendy and the vision was clear overnight. But the joke is structural, not visual: not just the image but the sale parameters are randomised outputs of the same generative act -- the name, the edition count, and the price were themselves randomly chosen by the algorithm, so a 1/1 could cost 2 tez, or one of 32 editions at 64 tez each. "I didn't expect it to sell... but people liked it." The market is not outside the work; the market is one more variable the system rolls.

**Takeaway:** when the price is a random output, the sale stops being the frame around the art and becomes part of the art. The mechanism is the willingness to let the system price itself.

## M6 -- The medium migrates

The mechanism that carries the whole career: the same vocabulary, a new body, every time. The grass/tree/line system migrates across media while the output stays a still image by deliberate choice. Plotter drawings are the work itself (Monoliths #000, the Oak Tree's 18 parts, 4-11 hours each, visible artefacts kept -- a pencilled cross marking start-zero, two straight ink lines from paper relief and a too-low pen height early in the run). Then ORGANIC MATR (2023, with Artmatr): the generative process designs the image and Artmatr's robots translate it into physical painting -- not a print, a robot-painted canvas, charcoal the primary medium, "the rawest tool touched by the human hand", binding technology to the oldest drawing medium. Then Sheltered Landscapes (Florence, September-October 2023): the two years of plotting algorithms culminate as one continuous coded landscape across 16 monitors inside a former WWII air-raid shelter -- nature rendered in code inside a place built to shelter people from bombs; the garden as shelter. Then thread: Aux Arbres is built to look embroidered, and then becomes embroidery -- a sample from an Aux Arbres output on Matt Jacobson's machine in New York, "I fell in love with the process instantly", a half-ton industrial embroidery machine craned into the Bordeaux studio, and Tree 138, 83 embroideries forming one tree. The Bright Moments Finale (Venice, 2024) is the migration in the other direction, back into code: 9,548 bytes of handwritten JavaScript, no minimiser, a complete 3D engine with vector/matrix maths, a triangle rasteriser, a generative alphabet, a line-occlusion algorithm, noise backgrounds, real-time animation, full SVG export -- built from scratch because Art Blocks demanded small on-chain code, reviving his 2002 demoscene code (real-time 3D cubes and pyramids, before the canvas element existed, then simulated with divs changing background colour). Kumono Shingou (5 May 2023, 100 editions) is the same pressure from the other side: his framework was 300-400 kB and Art Blocks demanded small code, so he shipped a self-extracting archive, paying $16,000 in gas at mint because Pepe-coin congestion spiked fees -- and disliked hiding the code, because "there's so much art in the code itself."

**Takeaway:** the medium is not the work's container; it is the work's next body. The same tree plotted, robot-painted, embroidered, and compressed to 9.5 kB is four different arguments about what the tree is.

## M7 -- The forest as meta-collection

The mechanism that turns the edition into a fragment. Aux Arbres ("To the Trees", 2024, Highlight/Bright Moments, Coinbase France launch, Hotel de Crillon, Paris): 76,208 unique iterations across more than 43,000 collectors -- "the largest generative art collection ever at the time", his dated claim from August 2025, not a standing fact (C1704). Each minted piece is attached to a given tree (Arbre); as pieces mint, new trees are created at about one tree per 80 pieces; tree shapes and features are driven by randomness, so each piece and each tree is unique. The result is a meta-collection of 965 distinct trees sitting above the 76,208 pieces -- the individual artwork is a fragment; the forest is the work. "A creative, collective effort to build a forest... Each piece is unique; each one belongs to a larger whole." The second layer was designed from the start as material for future exploration: it feeds Tree 138 (W19), where one tree from the meta-collection, selected "through an empirical process of social randomization", is rebuilt physically over six months as 83 embroideries. The pitch needed something "very French" -- textile -- and the code was worked to look embroidered before any real embroidery existed.

**Takeaway:** the edition is not the work; the forest is. A system that makes 76,208 pictures is a factory; a system that makes 76,208 pictures which assemble into 965 trees is an ecology.

## M8 -- Inheriting code

The latest mechanism, and the inversion of everything before it: instead of writing from a blank page, take someone else's program and reveal what is inside it. Please Respond (15 May 2026, Shape; concept by Tyler Hobbs; ten artists, one algorithm passed sequentially, each spending two weeks with the prior works; Zancan Day 10 of 10, edition 50, 0.07 ETH, sold out): Zancan inherited a fully functional long-form P5.js program from Aleksandra Jovanic, having never worked with P5 before -- her comments in the code let him grasp its architecture. He copied her sketch, fed it her final piece's hash, and displayed the 3D model object in it -- revealing the low-poly human model underneath rather than abstracting it away. The inherited external asset was a .OBJ low-poly man (from Juan Rodriguez Garcia's earlier piece); he hacked out the part centred on the vertex that "says, this is where it hurts" into a compact JavaScript object, avoiding the external dependency. His own framework -- five years of accumulated algorithms, "a big toolbox... like a Photoshop for generative art" -- supplied a 3D-mesh class plus hand-coded hole-cutting, brute-force sorting and curve smoothing for the line (echoing Jovanic's ribbon connecting the model's vertices), his signature single-line organic thread connecting graphical elements and guiding the eye, a hand-coded font (every character designed by typing numbers from his head in JavaScript), and his years-old "Cloud Background" script, borrowed from Tyler Hobbs's piece, of which he had only a PNG -- he borrowed Hobbs's palette. Spontaneous French text written while sleepy during testing stayed, half-hidden in the grass "like a secret meaning". The sun, moon, and planet colours were changed to Jovanic's palette at his daughters' approval. "Please Respond was like a collaboration but exempt from ego-collision issues."

**Takeaway:** the mechanism is inheritance without erasure. He does not rewrite her program; he feeds it her own hash, cuts a hole where it hurts, and lets the model underneath show. Code as shared material -- the next artist starts where the last one stopped, and the work is what the handoff reveals.

## Aesthetic logic: the painter's eye, the machine's hand

Zancan's aesthetic is a wager in the opposite direction from Yazid's: that a larger vocabulary, run through a painter's eye, is more beautiful than a smaller one -- provided the vocabulary is one vocabulary, carried everywhere. Grass, trees, monoliths, lines: the same four things, plotted, robot-painted, embroidered, compressed to 9.5 kB, inherited and re-cut. The restraint is not in the vocabulary but in the output: still images only, by choice, because a career of real-time interactivity taught him what motion costs. The motion is spent once, in the first render -- the garden growing, the tree assembling -- and then the image stops, and the stopping is the point: a still image that was once moving is a different object from a still image that never moved.

The palette is the painter's, not the programmer's: lush, figurative, symbolic -- pink backgrounds with #C0FFEE coffee-ring stains, pure yellow #ffff00 to place a work "in the digital realm", sepia Lushtemples, charcoal as "the rawest tool touched by the human hand". The code is the programmer's, and he cares about it as code: "The beauty of code matters to him as much as the images it produces." The audience's responses track exactly this double claim -- a record Tezos sale, a Sotheby's lot, a Basel installation, a bitforms solo show -- for work that refuses to choose between the painting and the program. And his own account of it, the sentence he gave, is the aesthetic stated plainly: NFTs as "the 'right soil' for my art to bloom", the place where "the painter's eye meets math and algorithms". The work is not about nature; the work IS nature, rendered by a machine that was taught to paint by a man who spent fifteen years painting in oils and forty years writing code.

W16 -- Bright Moments Finale (Venice, 2024; Grand Finale; tour started in Venice Beach, travelled 9 major cities over 3 years).
