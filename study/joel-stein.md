# Joel Stein: Study Notes

**Site:** Joel Stein, born 25 May 1926 at Saint-Martin-Boulogne, France, died 24 July 2012 at Foucarville, France, aged 86 -- French kinetic artist, GRAV co-founder, the group's principal theoretician of the spectator as homo ludens: the man who programmed pictures on mathematical bases from 1956, overlaid grids until moire appeared, built reliefs the spectator's hand could rearrange, walk-through labyrinths the spectator had to play, Polascopes that made colour out of polarised light, an abacus that turned counting into a game of chance, and, from 1968, laser figures drawn in smoke-filled air. He enrolled at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, Paris, in 1946, frequented Fernand Leger's atelier from 1949, met Francois Morellet and turned to programmed abstraction in 1956, co-founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel in 1960 and GRAV in 1961, and kept working the same toolkit -- light, mirror, movement, spectator -- until his death (C6601, C6602, C6603, C6605, C6612, C6614, C6615).

## The life: from the Leger atelier to the laser in smoke

Joel Stein was born 25 May 1926 at Saint-Martin-Boulogne (Pas-de-Calais) and died 24 July 2012 at Foucarville (Manche), aged 86 (C6601). The dates are confirmed by the INSEE death file (acte number 4) and agree exactly with the French and German Wikipedias (C6601). He is not the living American journalist Joel Stein, born 23 July 1971 in Edison, New Jersey, who holds the English Wikipedia article under the same name; there is no English Wikipedia article on the artist, which is a standing confusion risk (C6620). No source read gives family background, childhood, wartime experience or private life; the record begins with his art schooling.

He enrolled in 1946 at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, Paris, and frequented the atelier of Fernand Leger, the JCMC page dating the Leger attendance more precisely as beginning in 1949, absorbing Leger's social humanism and commitment to working in a variety of media including film (C6602). His early prints were drypoints and monotypes, learned in the atelier of Georges Visat, and he also frequented Braque and Matta (C6651). The decisive turn came in 1956. After meeting Francois Morellet, Stein adopted "all the principles of abstraction (as the most controlled and homogenous organization of a surface) through the systematic operations of repetition, progression, and permutation, to affect at once form and color" (C6603). The French Wikipedia puts it more briefly: from 1956, his first geometric pictures programmed on mathematical bases (C6603). His models were Max Bill and concrete art, and Victor Vasarely and what Vasarely already called "kinetic" (C6603). The South American artists newly arrived in Paris and Stein shared "the pictorial inheritance from the same source: Max Bill and concrete art as well as Victor Vasarely and the presage of what he already called 'kinetic'" (C6706). Vasarely is the group's acknowledged source, never a member or co-founder -- the rule carried from #619 onward (C6301, C6706).

From 1958 he developed the idea of the labyrinth, still two-dimensional, and in 1959 he made his first manipulable reliefs (C6604). In 1960 Morellet, Stein, Hector Garcia Miranda and Yvaral exhibited together under the name Motus at Galerie Azimut, Milan, then Padua and Brussels; the Motus members renounced signing their works, attributing them to the group, and declared a plastic research with no commercial objective (C6605). A few months later Horacio Garcia Rossi, Julio Le Parc, Morellet, Francisco Sobrino, Stein, Yvaral, Vera Molnar, Francois Molnar, Hector Garcia Miranda and Hugo Demarco formed the CRAV (Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel) (C6605). Disagreements over anonymity and over the shared premises at rue Beautreillis led Vera and Francois Molnar, Garcia Miranda and Demarco to leave; in 1961 the CRAV became the GRAV around six artists: Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein and Yvaral (C6605). The GRAV held two fundamental principles: the figure of the artist must disappear, and the work is finished only when the public takes hold of it (C6606). In January 1961 the group drew up its first proposals; its 1963 manifesto appealed to the direct participation of the public, with an influence on the public's behaviour, notably through interactive labyrinths (C6606). The group sought a social function for art: the viewer became active and the art interactive; rejecting aestheticism, the artists believed the work should avoid all subjectivity so as to let viewers use their own senses, and instability became the rule (C6606).

After GRAV formed, Stein's research followed the common programme: retinal stimulation and visual instability (C6607). He superimposed grids and weaves and explored the moire effect, gathering part of the results in the album Jeux de trames (1962), prefaced by Pierre Schaeffer (C6607). In the same year his research on the chromatic polarisation of light produced the first luminous boxes, the Polascopes -- rotating discs and mirrors creating kaleidoscopes of colour (C6607). Through Schaeffer he was in contact with the Groupe de Recherche Musicale of the ORTF, for which he made television image sequences and credit sequences (C6607). Stein then moved to real and interactive movement of the object: Tourne-disques (turntables), Triedres, kaleidoscopes, and, for the first Labyrinth of 1963, manipulable lamps; the playful aspect was then accentuated by creations such as the Bouliers (abacuses, e.g. Boulier, 1965), Spirales, and manipulable boxes (C6608). The mirror allowed ephemeral compositions made of real movement and unstable structures; the triedres, in which balls in reflection are set in motion by the spectator, and the truncated-pyramid prisms that recreate a virtual sphere, date from the GRAV years (C6608).

The first Labyrinth was presented in 1963 at the third Paris Biennale: a space inside the museum hall where the museum's rules did not apply, entered under the sign "Entrez, cassez" (Enter, break), a parcours of twenty installations -- wall reliefs, luminous devices -- which won the first prize for team work (C6608). The interplay of audience and artwork reached a high level of sophistication in GRAV's Maze II at Nouvelles Tendances (Paris, 1964) and Maze III, exhibited in New York in 1965 (C6609). Stein also took part, as a member of GRAV, in documenta III, Kassel, 1964, in the section Licht und Bewegung (Light and Movement) (C6609). On 19 April 1966 GRAV created Une journee dans la rue (A Day in the Street) in Paris: from 8 am, gifts handed to passers-by at the Chatelet metro entrance; at 10 am, the public invited to assemble and disassemble evolving structures on the Champs-Elysees; at noon, habitable kinetic objects could be manipulated; at 2 pm, a giant kaleidoscope was made available and balloons floated in a fountain; at 6 pm, passers-by were invited to walk on mobile slabs at Montparnasse (C6610). The public was to engage in kinetic activities, one of them wearing distorting glasses (C6610). The group's reasoning: "We think that the sum total of these routine gestures can lead to total passivity, and create a general need for reaction" (C6610).

Parallel to GRAV, filmmaker Henri-Georges Clouzot gave Stein and Yvaral carte blanche for the hallucinatory special effects of L'Enfer (1964, unfinished) and La Prisonniere (1968) (C6611). L'Enfer was abandoned in 1964 after Clouzot suffered a heart attack during production; the surviving footage was reconstructed by Serge Bromberg and Ruxandra Medrea in the 2009 documentary Henri-Georges Clouzot's Inferno, which included an interview with Stein (C6611). The JCMC claim that the film was unfinished "when Clouzot died" in 1964 is wrong -- Clouzot died in 1977 -- and is not repeated (C6611).

GRAV's last proposal took place in May 1968 at the Maison de la Culture, Grenoble; the exhibition closed after a week because of the May 1968 political movement (C6613). In June 1968 GRAV expelled Julio Le Parc, who had turned back to painting; the group dissolved itself at the end of 1968 (C6613). The agreed reasons differ in emphasis: the recognition that it was impossible to maintain the rigour of a joint programme and to ensure artistic anonymity among the members, and (Xippas) the assessment that the revolution the artists had sought had been accomplished (C6613).

Inspired by new technologies, Stein introduced the laser in 1968 and continued his research on colour (C6614). JCMC dates it to "mid-1968" and adds the strong claim that he "was the first French artist to use the laser for artistic purposes" (C6614). That "first" claim appears in no other source read; the French Wikipedia and Xippas say only that he introduced or went on to experiment with the laser, so it is flagged as single-source and unverified, not stated as fact (C6614, C6753).

Evolution: the work moves from retinal effect (eye) to manual interaction (hand) to whole-body parcours (street, 1966) (C6604, C6607, C6608, C6610). Late and post-GRAV (1969-2012), Stein continued to produce Op art works -- some straightforward Op art paintings and prints, others more complex kinetic devices (C6615). The triedres and truncated-pyramid prisms of the GRAV years were followed, "more recently", by sundial-type installations that reconstruct shadow and light in movement or destabilise the path of a ray of light (C6615). Xippas (2016) says he "went on to experiment with a laser and returned to his research on the chromatic interactions -- darkness and light took a variety of shapes" (C6615). No source read documents a sharp late break in style; the late career reads as a continuation and refinement of the GRAV toolkit (light, mirror, movement, spectator) rather than a repudiation of it (C6615). The 2010 retrospective tour framed the career as continuous: Caen, Abbaye aux Dames and Abbaye aux Hommes, February-April 2010; Acri (MACA), July-September 2010; Senigallia (MUSINF), October 2010; Vicenza, Basilica Palladiana (LAMeC), December 2010-January 2011 -- all "Joel Stein, retrospective 1964-2010" (C6616). In 2010 the Musee d'art et d'histoire de Cholet reproduced the 1963 labyrinth, in part a replica; some devices date from 1963 (C6616). Posthumous: Joel Stein et le GRAV, Galerie Xippas, Paris, 26 November 2016-21 January 2017, curator Arnauld Pierre, with works by Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein and Yvaral (C6618).

Exhibition spine in brief: Motus shows 1960 (Milan, Padua, Brussels); Assez de mystifications 1, second Paris Biennale, 1961; Labyrinthe I, third Paris Biennale, 1963 (first prize, team work); documenta III, Kassel, 1964; Labyrinthe II, fourth Paris Biennale, 1965; Labyrinthe III, New York, 1965; Kunst Licht Kunst, Eindhoven, 1966; Proposition pour une salle de jeu, Indica, London, 1966; Une journee dans la rue, Paris, 19 April 1966; Lumiere et mouvement, Musee d'art moderne de la Ville, Paris, 1967; Participation, Museum am Ostwall, Dortmund, 1968; Cinetisme environnement spectacle, Grenoble, May 1968 (C6605, C6606, C6608, C6609, C6610, C6613). Solo and late: Valmore retrospective, 24 November 2006-10 February 2007, "personale retrospettiva dal 1960 ad oggi" (C6617). The French Wikipedia's exhibition section is headed "recent solo exhibitions" but mixes in group shows and fair stands; it is not cited as a solo-show list (C6617). Artsy says "This artist has no solo shows yet" while French Wikipedia and Valmore document solo retrospectives; Artsy's CV is incomplete for this artist and is used only for the posthumous group-show tail, 2021-2025 (C6616, C6618).

Collections: no verified public-collection list was found; do not invent one. What is documented: the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection (JCMC, Miami) holds Cercles rouges et oranges combinables, a diptych, oil on wood, 80 x 160 cm (C6619); Galerie Valmore (2006) states only, in general terms, that his works "are found in numerous museums, galleries and Italian and foreign collections" -- no institution named (C6619); a Centre Pompidou 2010 donation brochure reproduces on its cover Stein's Neuf triedres, ou Triedre a neuf cellules (1963) alongside a Vasarely -- evidence of association with the Pompidou, not proof of a holding (C6619). The Denise Rene link is collection and homage level only: Sotheby's sale "Denise Rene, Militante de l'abstraction" (Paris, 27 November-4 December 2025) lists lot 322, Joel Stein, "Acceleration optique, variation sur un theme 1964", a Stein work held in the Galerie Denise Rene collection; no solo Stein show at Galerie Denise Rene was found, and none is claimed (C6707).

## M1 -- The programmed repetition: the rule makes the picture

The founding mechanism is the programmed picture: the image is built from a pre-set mathematical rule before paint touches surface -- repetition, progression and permutation applied to form and colour at once (C6603, C6652). The first geometric paintings programmed on mathematical bases date from 1956 (C6652). The painting itself does not move; the "movement" is virtual, produced in the viewer's eye by the programmed regularity (C6652). Cercles rouges et oranges combinables (S01, undated, diptych, oil on wood, 80 x 160 cm, JCMC) is the rule made re-arrangeable: two panels of overlapping red, orange and white circles whose order and pairing can be changed, so the composition is re-combinable by arrangement (C6652). Viewed as the work: the left panel carries a large white ring and white crescent over orange and red discs; the right panel is the same vocabulary mirrored and permuted; the two panels visibly belong to one system (C6652). Variation chromatique (W14, 1966) applies the same rule to hue instead of shape: graded colour progressions across the surface, so the eye reads a slow shift rather than a flat field; it sold at auction in Brno in 2021 for 38,565 EUR, Stein's auction record at the time of that source (C6664). Medium and dimensions for W14 are not stated in the source that names the work; do not assume acrylic on canvas (C6664).

**Takeaway:** write the rule first and the picture follows. Repetition, progression, permutation -- one small rule set generates the whole series, and the hand never has to decide twice.

## M2 -- Moire interference: the pattern in the overlap

Two regular patterns laid over each other make a third pattern that belongs to neither layer alone. Stein concentrates on moire effects from about 1961 (C6655): superimposed grids and weaves, where the two periodic structures interfere and moire fringes appear that shift when the viewer moves, or when one layer is displaced against the other (C6655). Jeux de trames (W06, 1962) gathers the results: a portfolio of 10 reliefs in colour, signed, text by Pierre Schaeffer, published by Editions Georges Visat et Cie, Paris, edition of 103 copies, 46 x 39 cm (C6656). Each relief superimposes grids and weaves so the eye reads a moire and vibration that is not drawn anywhere; the relief surface adds a real shadow-shift to the printed interference (C6656). Viewed as the work: one relief from the portfolio shows a dense blue field of small repeated white and black dot-and-ring motifs on cream paper, signed lower right; the repetition is visibly systematic, and the moire shimmer is weak in a still photograph -- which is itself the point, since the effect needs the moving eye (C6656). Through Schaeffer, Stein was in contact with the Groupe de Recherche Musicale of the ORTF, for which he made television image sequences and credit sequences -- the visual researcher borrowing the sound researcher's ear (C6607, C6709).

**Takeaway:** overlay two grids and the work is the interference, not the marks. The pattern lives between the sheets, and it only wakes up when the eye moves.

## M3 -- The manipulable relief: the hand finishes the picture

The first break with the fixed picture: raised elements on a panel that the spectator's hand can move, shift or recombine (C6654). The first manipulable reliefs date from 1959 (C6654). The composition is not finished until the viewer handles it, and it can be left in a different state from the one it was found in (C6654). Neuf triedres, ou Triedre a neuf cellules (W08, 1963) is the mechanism as a mirror machine: nine trihedral (three-mirrored-corner) cells, in which balls in reflection are set in motion by the spectator, and truncated-pyramid prisms recreate a virtual sphere (C6658). The mirror corners multiply and fragment the reflected balls, so a small real movement makes a large, unstable reflected composition (C6658). The nine-cell arrangement itself is attested only by title and date on the Pompidou brochure cover; its exact cell layout and materials were not found (C6658). The kaleidoscopes and mirror landscapes of the GRAV years (W11, c. 1963-1965) work the same way at room scale: mirrors multiply hanging globes into a field of reflections, and a small hand movement re-composes the whole reflected landscape -- the work is the reflected field, which exists only while someone is looking into it and moving it (C6661). No individual kaleidoscope title, date or dimensions were found; the entry records the type as described (C6661). The later manipulable boxes and spirals (S11, post-1963) continue the same turn: a box the hand opens, turns or rearranges; a spiral form the eye follows inward or that rotates (C6660).

**Takeaway:** put the composition in the spectator's hands and the work never finishes. A touch is a decision, and the piece keeps the last decision it was given.

## M4 -- The labyrinth as playable field: the route is the work

The labyrinth began as a painted path-structure in 1958 -- branching maze geometry on a flat surface, the viewer's eye made to travel a route with choices, dead ends and returns, still flat and still, a diagram of a route rather than a space to walk through (C6653). The first Labyrinth of 1963 turned the diagram into a space: a walk-through environment in the entrance hall of the Musee d'art moderne de la Ville de Paris at the third Paris Biennale, entered under the sign "Entrez, cassez", a parcours of twenty installations -- wall reliefs, luminous devices -- which won the first prize for team work (C6608, C6659). Stein's contribution was manipulable lamps and Disques a manipuler (C6659). His lamps are hand-manipulated light sources: the visitor aims and moves the light, changing what the next visitor sees (C6659). The labyrinth is deliberately built to eliminate the distance between spectator and work (C6659). Maze II (Paris, 1964) and Maze III (New York, 1965) carried the idea further, and Stein's separate contribution to each is not attested (C6659). Une journee dans la rue (S08, 19 April 1966) took the same idea out of the gallery and into the street: passers-by invited to try kinetic experiences across sites in Paris, including springing seats and distorting glasses, and to answer a questionnaire (C6663). Stein's individual contribution to the day is not separately attested; the event is collective, not a Stein-titled work (C6663).

**Takeaway:** draw the maze, then build it big enough to walk in. The work stops being a picture and becomes a route -- and a route only exists while somebody is walking it.

## M5 -- The Polascope and the turntable: colour and pattern by rotation

The Polascope (W07, first light boxes, 1962) is an instrument for chromatic polarisation, not a picture of light: light passes through polarising filters and rotating discs, reflected by mirrors, and turning a disc (or the viewer's position) changes the polarisation angle, so colours appear, shift and vanish inside the box (C6657). Later versions are described as boxes that diffuse light like kaleidoscopes (C6657). No usable image of any Polascope was found; the entry is prose-only (C6657). The Tourne-disques (W10, c. 1963) is the same rotation logic without the box: a disc or set of discs mounted to spin by hand, the printed or relief pattern only complete in rotation -- at rest a static design, spun, the pattern blurs, re-forms and produces flicker and moire in time (C6660). The spectator controls speed and direction, so the image is a function of the hand (C6660). No single dated Tourne-disque example is attested by title (C6660).

**Takeaway:** spin it and the picture is not the disc but the rotation. Speed is a material: the same marks are a different image at every speed.

## M6 -- Anamorphosis: the correct view is earned

Anamorphose (W15, 1967, metal and painted metal, mirror, wood, 61 x 74 x 31 cm) is the mechanism in a box: a distorted, twisted band sits inside a mirrored box, and the band alone is unreadable; its reflection in the mirrors deforms and re-completes it, so the "correct" form exists only as a reflection, from a particular viewpoint (C6665). The spectator must move to find the viewpoint where the anamorphosis resolves (C6665). Viewed as the work: a white-framed mirrored box, inside it a black-and-white striped band twisting into a loop like a Mobius strip, its stripes warping in the mirror walls; the photograph confirms the mechanism -- the striped band is the object, the mirrors do the deforming (C6665). It was exhibited at the Caen, Acri, Senigallia and Vicenza retrospectives of 2010-2011 and listed at Sotheby's Contemporary Discoveries, February 2026, estimate 5,000-7,000 EUR (C6665). Anamorphoses II (W16, 1967) is the companion in spirit but flat and drawn: a Mobius ring -- a surface with one side and one edge -- drawn and coloured so the eye follows the strip around and returns on the "other" side without ever crossing an edge; the twist makes inside and outside continuous, and the painted stripes make the continuity visible (C6666). Only the title, date and Mobius-ring description are attested for W16; medium, dimensions and holding are not stated (C6666). The truncated-pyramid prism (S12, GRAV years) is the same idea without the band: a mirror and prism object in which reflections assemble a sphere that is not physically there -- the sphere exists only in reflection (C6658).

**Takeaway:** distort the object and let the mirror repair it. The true form is never on the object; it is in the reflection, and only from the one place where it lines up.

## M7 -- The abacus: counting as a game of chance

The Grand boulier geant-moyen no. 2 bis (W13, 1966, metal, wood, rubber, aluminium, 5 x 7 ft, approx. 152 x 213 cm) is Stein's most literal homo ludens machine: coloured balls are launched down a chute fitted with hazards -- holes and gates -- and the path a ball takes, and which hazards it hits, is varied by the force of propulsion the spectator gives it (C6663). It is a chance machine with a skill input: same chute, different throw, different route and different visual and audible result; the Studio International reviewer calls it "fun to play" (C6663). It was shown at the Indica Gallery, London, 1966, reviewed in Studio International, August 1966, p. 40 (C6663). Viewed as the work, partially: the photograph is a small black-and-white image at the top left of the magazine page, the sloped board and chute structure identifiable, but the balls, holes and gates cannot be read from the reproduction (C6663). Xippas dates "the Boulier" to 1965 while Studio International captions no. 2 bis as 1966; these are probably two versions or two showings, and the 1966 no. 2 bis is the one documented here (C6663). The Boulier is named among the participatory objects of the Labyrinthes (C6705).

**Takeaway:** give the spectator a ball and a slope and the work becomes a game. The throw is the input, the chute is the rule, and no two plays come out the same.

## M8 -- Light as material: the beam you can steer

From 1968 Stein worked with the laser, and his own description of the Sigma work (Bordeaux, 1969) is the clearest "light as a tangible object" statement in the catalogue: "A beam passes through a 2 m long hollow prism made of polished steel and strikes a corner mirror which is mounted on ball bearings to permit it to be given an oscillatory motion. The laser beam is reflected by the mirror back through the smoke-filled hollow prism as points and intersecting lines of light" (C6668). The system was a 5 mW helium-neon laser, the hollow prism 2 m long, the corner mirror on ball bearings, the prism smoke-filled; mechanical mirrors and metal prisms concentrated, divided, multiplied and reflected the beam, and audience movement controlled and modified the figures in space (C6668). A laser system for a ballet at the Opera Comique, Paris, c. 1968, preceded the Sigma showing (C6668). The same light-as-material logic runs in the late sundial-type installations (S10): a fixed structure casts a shadow that moves with the day, or a mirror train deflects a sunbeam along a path the visitor can interrupt (C6670). No individual sundial title, date, medium or dimensions are attested (C6670). The film effects for L'Enfer (W12, 1964, unfinished) and La Prisonniere (W17, 1967/1968) are the same devices fixed on film: moire, polarised light and rotating patterns transferred to the cinema frame, so the movement is in the projected image rather than in an object in the room, and the spectator cannot operate them -- the movement is authored and replayed identically at every screening (C6662, C6667). La Prisonniere is dated 1967 by one source and 1968 by another; the film was released in 1968, so the effects work is 1967 with a 1968 film date the likelier split (C6667).

**Takeaway:** make light solid and the room becomes the canvas. A beam through smoke is a line you can draw in the air -- and when the audience moves, the drawing moves with them.

## The aesthetic logic: the spectator is the last mechanism

Stein's whole career is one argument, made in eight mechanisms: the picture is not finished until somebody else touches it. The 1956 programmed paintings already hand the decision to the rule; the moire hands it to the moving eye; the reliefs, triedres and kaleidoscopes hand it to the hand; the labyrinth hands it to the walking body; the Boulier hands it to the throw; the laser hands it to the audience's movement in the room. Each step moves the agency one layer outward -- from the artist's rule, to the eye, to the hand, to the body, to the crowd. That is what "principal theoretician of this new homo ludens" means in practice: not a slogan about play but a sequence of devices, each one removing one more decision from the artist and giving it to the spectator (C6612, C6705). The political goal was explicit -- "the advent of a new democratic public in the communicative euphoria of game and play" (C6612) -- and Stein himself later acknowledged the risk: audience interaction with GRAV installations could become "a kind of entertainment, a spectacle in which the audience itself becomes one of the components of the work" (C6612). The late work does not retreat from that risk; the sundial installations and the laser simply hand the agency to the sun and the room, the last spectators left (C6615, C6670).

The network is the argument's evidence. Morellet gave him the programme in 1956 (C6703); Leger gave him the social humanism and the multi-media habit (C6602); Schaeffer gave him the preface and the ORTF contact (C6707, C6709); Vasarely gave the whole group its source and its anti-individual creed (C6706). The adjacent artists map the same field from three sides: Hugo Demarco (1932-1995), GRAV manifesto signatory and fellow CRAV founder, first solo at Galerie Denise Rene in 1961, whose optical vibration comes from superimposing complementary colours on a flat surface -- the still, painted cousin of Stein's moire (C6711); Gerhard von Graevenitz (1934-1983), the German kinetic artist who lived in Paris in 1961 in contact with GRAV and shared a studio with Le Parc, then co-founded Nouvelle Tendance in 1962 -- the hop across the border, not a GRAV member (C6712); Pierre Schaeffer (1910-1995), the composer who prefaced Jeux de trames and whose Groupe de Recherche Musicale gave Stein television work -- the cross-disciplinary hop, sound research to visual research (C6709). And Clouzot, with Yvaral, gave Stein the cinema: the hallucinatory effects of L'Enfer and La Prisonniere, where the spectator's hand is finally taken away again and the movement is fixed on film (C6609, C6662, C6667). Delta (W19, 1969, acrylic on canvas, 80 x 100 cm) shows what the programme looks like when the hand is taken out: concentric bands in graded blue-to-green run in three lobes that weave over and under each other like a knot, the eye following the bands through the crossings while the colour grade does the moving (C6669). Viewed as the work: black ground, three interlaced lobes, the over-under logic clearly readable (C6669). Plus blanc que blanc (W20, 1996, acrylic on canvas, 50 x 50 cm) is the late joke in two colours only: a blue square frame with a notch holds a blue disc overlapping a white disc, and which disc is "in front" flips as the eye re-reads the notches; the white is made whiter by the blue that frames it, and the white areas are the active shapes, not the background (C6670).

**Takeaway:** the work is a machine for handing decisions away. Rule, eye, hand, body, crowd, sun -- each new mechanism gives the next decision to somebody who is not the artist.

## Corrections and open questions

- GRAV founding: the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel was founded in 1960 and became GRAV in 1961; Xippas and the English Wikipedia say founded or founding member in 1960, JCMC says "GRAV (Paris, 1961-1968)", the French GRAV article's infobox lists both July 1960 and July 1961. This study words it as co-founder of the milieu in 1960 (Motus/CRAV) and of the six-member GRAV in 1961 (C6605, C6606, C6720).
- GRAV size: six vs eleven. The six are Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein, Yvaral; the eleven counts the wider CRAV circle (Vera and Francois Molnar, Hector Garcia Miranda, Hugo Demarco). Never merge the lists (C6605, C6710).
- Dissolution: November vs December 1968 (English Wikipedia and JCMC say November; the French GRAV article says December; Xippas says "the end of 1968"; the German GRAV article is the outlier with 1969). This study uses "end of 1968" (C6613).
- JCMC is wrong on Clouzot: L'Enfer was not "unfinished in 1964 when Clouzot died" -- Clouzot died in 1977, made La Prisonniere in 1968 after L'Enfer, and L'Enfer was abandoned in 1964 after his heart attack. The JCMC sentence is not repeated (C6611).
- "First French artist to use the laser" is JCMC-only and unverified elsewhere; French Wikipedia and Xippas say only that he introduced or experimented with the laser in 1968. Not stated as fact (C6614, C6753).
- Boulier date: 1965 (Xippas) vs no. 2 bis 1966 (Studio International) -- probably two versions; the 1966 no. 2 bis is the one documented (C6663).
- La Prisonniere: 1967 vs 1968 across sources; the film was released in 1968, so the effects work is 1967 with a 1968 film date the likelier split (C6667).
- Sotheby's lot pages carry impossible metadata -- "Joel Stein 1971-2002" on the Anamorphose page -- and are not used for Stein's dates; JCMC (25 May 1926 - 24 July 2012) is the date source (C6719).
- "Boite a lumiere instable" (1966) is Garcia Rossi's, not Stein's; it is excluded from the catalogue (C6754).
- The Denise Rene link is collection and homage level only; no solo Stein show at Galerie Denise Rene was found, and none is claimed (C6707).
- "Morellet's friend since 1949" (#620) was not re-sourced this run; Xippas confirms only that the meeting preceded the 1956 turn (C6708).
- Gaps carried honestly: no obituary, cause of death, burial place or family record; no named public-collection list; no 1969-1999 exhibition record; no usable image of any Polascope, triedre, kaleidoscope, laser work or labyrinth installation; the French Wikipedia exhibition list mixes solo and group shows under a "solo" heading; Artsy's "no solo shows" contradicts documented retrospectives and is not relied on (C6616, C6617, C6619, C6755).
- The doorway tally arithmetic gap of 5 (C863) is carried, not fixed (C6715).
