# Louise Lawler: Study Notes

**Site:** Louise Lawler -- born 1947 in Bronxville, New York; lives and works in Brooklyn, New York -- American artist of the Pictures Generation who photographs other artists' work where it actually lives: in collectors' homes, museums, galleries, auction houses, and storage, and lets the room, the label, or the wall do the work. She refuses the label photographer -- "I don't exactly think I am a photographer... I'm just trying to point things out" -- and her practice runs from audio (Birdcalls, 1972/81) through photographs, text works, paperweights, tracings, adjusted-to-fit vinyls, distorted versions, swiped blurs, and one billboard, all of it a single operation: point the camera at art in its place and let the place argue. Born 1947 in Bronxville in every source read this run; the exact day is not established (only the MoMA 1987 catalogue PDF gives "February 3, 1947", search summary only, and it is not printed as fact). Degree contested: BFA, Cornell, 1969 (Wikipedia, TheArtStory, Dia, Sprueth Magers, Sotheby's, artnet) vs "holds an MFA from Cornell" (ICP, read end to end, no year) -- whether the MFA is a second degree or an ICP error is not established. ## The life: the art in its room

Lawler was born in 1947 in Bronxville, New York. At Cornell she earned her BFA in 1969 (the degree title is contested -- see the Site line) and helped organise the Andrew Dickson White Museum's 1969 exhibition "Earth Art". She moved to Manhattan in 1969 (1970 per Dia; unresolved) and took a job at the Castelli Gallery, where she met Janelle Reiring, later co-founder of Metro Pictures (1980). Her earliest known work grew out of the Castelli years: in the winter of 1970, assisting on Willoughby Sharp's "Projects: Pier 18", she and Martha Kite sang loudly off-key on the walk home and mimicked bird-like sounds chanting "Willoughby Willoughby"; from that parody she composed a list of 28 famous male artists of the 1960s and turned their names into birdcalls -- "Birdcalls", conceived 1972, recorded 1981, Lawler alone reading her own list. The 1972 origin was Lawler and Martha Kite; the 1981 recording is Lawler alone. Sherrie Levine had nothing to do with Birdcalls, and the brief's framing of it as a Lawler-Levine audio work is refuted this run. (C20008, leg 1 C19800-C19801; leg 2 C19850; synthesis C19950, C19973)

Her first gallery appearance was in the 1978 Artists Space group show organised by Janelle Reiring (Lawler, Adrian Piper, Cindy Sherman), where she borrowed a small 1883 portrait of a horse from Aqueduct Racetrack and mounted it on an otherwise empty wall. In 1979 she presented "A Movie Will Be Shown Without the Picture" at the Aero Theater, Santa Monica: the soundtrack of "The Misfits" played to a blank screen. Her first solo show at Metro Pictures is dated 1981 by TheArtStory and 1982 (20 November-18 December 1982) by Wikipedia and Dia, and TheArtStory is internally inconsistent on the first-solo question -- its biography section calls the 1979 Aero Theater show her first solo exhibition, while its "Mature Period" section gives Metro Pictures 1981. No single first-solo year is printed here as settled. The breakthrough came in 1984, when she was given full access to the New York City and Connecticut homes of collectors Burton and Emily Hall Tremaine: "Pollock and Tureen" (1984), "Monogram" (1984). From 1984 her signature mode is fixed: photographs of other artists' works as arranged and lived with in collectors' homes, museums, galleries, auction houses, art fairs, and storage, with titles crediting the arranger ("Arranged by ...") rather than the maker. (C20009, leg 1 C19802-C19803; leg 2 C19851, C19854-C19856; synthesis C19951)

Career phases after 1984 run through museum surveys rather than a change of subject: MoMA "Projects 9: enough" (19 September-10 November 1987); Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (1990); Hirshhorn "Monochrome" (1997); Portikus, Frankfurt (2003); Museum fur Gegenwartskunst, Basel (2004, "Louise Lawler and Others"); Dia:Beacon (2005, "In and Out of Place: Louise Lawler and Andy Warhol"); Wexner Center (2006, "Twice Untitled and Other Pictures (looking back)"); Museum Ludwig, Cologne (2013, "Adjusted"); MoMA "Louise Lawler: WHY PICTURES NOW" (30 April-30 July 2017), the first New York museum survey, organised by Roxana Marcoci with Kelly Sidley; "She's Here", Sammlung Verbund, Vienna (2018); "Andy in Chicago", Art Institute of Chicago (2019-2021); "Grand Arles Express", Collection Lambert, Avignon (2023). Whitney Biennials 1991, 2000, 2008; documenta 12 (2007); documenta 13 (2012, Sprueth Magers only, not confirmed end to end); 59th Venice Biennale (2022). From the 2000s on she restages her own archive as much as other artists' work: "adjusted to fit" images (start year unresolved -- "from 2016" per the Sprueth Magers biography, "In 2011, Lawler began making her adjusted to fit works" per the Logan Center note; neither is printed as settled), "tracings" with illustrator Jon Buller (first shown in the 2013 Ludwig survey), "swiped" images (long exposures, swift camera movements), paperweight multiples, and ephemera throughout. Her one billboard work is "Triangle (adjusted to fit)" (2008/2009/2011), the 15th in the High Line's series of 25-by-75-foot billboards. Dealers: Metro Pictures from her first solo there until the gallery closed in 2021 (last show September-October 2021); Sprueth Magers since 1987; Yvon Lambert, Paris. Her work is held in some 60 public collections including MoMA, the Whitney, the Guggenheim, the Met, the Art Institute of Chicago, LACMA, Tate, Centre Pompidou and Kunstmuseum Basel. (C20010, leg 1 C19804-C19808; leg 2 C19865, C19869; synthesis C19952)

Collaborations are a through-line: with Sherrie Levine, "A Picture Is No Substitute for Anything" (1981-82); with Barbara Kruger and Levine, the Flue centerfold (December 1980) -- title corrected this run, see the corrections section; with Allan McCollum, "Ideal Settings: For Presentation and Display" (1984, single-source) and a New Observations magazine page (primary-voice, Lawler's own email); with Liam Gillick, a site-specific vinyl wall sticker at Casey Kaplan Gallery (2013). Personal life is thinly documented and Lawler refuses it: she answers requests for a portrait with a picture of a parrot ("Portrait", 1982). TheArtStory alone reports she was married to art historian Benjamin Buchloh between 1981 and 1995 (single-source, not printed as more). She was made a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 2019 (search summary only). (C20011, leg 1 C19806, C19809; leg 3 C19906, C19916; synthesis C19953)


## M1 -- Re-presentation: photograph the art where it lives, and the room is the work

Photograph someone else's artwork in the place it actually sits -- a collector's dining room, bedroom or living room, a museum gallery, an auction house, a gallery being installed -- and present that photograph as the work. The camera never touches the artwork; the framing, the cropping and the neighbours do all the work. Pollock's "Frieze" is cropped to wallpaper behind a tureen ("Pollock and Tureen", 1984, W06); Johns's "White Flag" rhymes with a monogrammed bedsheet ("Monogram", 1984, W07); Koons's "Rabbit" stands next to a refrigerator in a Chicago apartment ("Foreground", 1994, W12); Gonzalez-Torres's bulbs lie unlit on the floor before installation ("Bulbs", 2005/2006, W14); Judd's sculptures become black shapes in a darkened MoMA ("LIGHTS OFF, AFTER HOURS, IN THE DARK", 2021, W19). The operation never changes across forty years; only the target widens, from a borrowed horse portrait and a blank screen (1978-79) through collectors' bedrooms and auction rooms (1982-94) to the museum after hours (2021) and the camera itself moving across the masterpiece (2023). The artwork is never shown as the museum would show it -- frontal, lit, alone. It is shown as it is lived with: half-hidden, cropped by a doorway, demoted by a tureen, rhymed by a bedsheet.

**Takeaway:** point the camera at the art in its room and let the room argue. (C20012, leg 2 C19870; synthesis C19960)

## M2 -- The arranger as author: credit the collector, not the artist

Give the work a title that names whoever arranged the scene -- "Arranged by Barbara and Eugene Schwartz", "Arranged by Mr. and Mrs. Burton Tremaine" -- and let that person's taste be the composition. The title on the wall names the collectors while the artists inside the photograph (Sherman, Pollock, Johns) go unnamed. At Metro Pictures in 1982 Lawler did the same to her own dealers, showing gallery stock as "Arranged by Louise Lawler" and pricing it at the sum of its parts plus 10 percent (it did not sell). The question of who made the picture -- the artist, the collector, the dealer, Lawler -- is left open on purpose. The mechanism is a title, not a picture: the same photograph with a different "Arranged by" line is a different work about a different author. Watch for it in the works: Sherman's "Untitled No. 88" and "Untitled No. 100" seen through a doorway, credited to the Schwartzes (W05); the Tremaine dining room and bedroom, credited to the Tremaines (W06, W07).

**Takeaway:** title the picture after the person who hung it. (C20013, leg 2 C19871; synthesis C19961)

## M3 -- The text does the work: matchbooks, cards, labels and wall texts as the picture

Put the words where the picture should be, or make the label the work. A matchbook printed WHY PICTURES NOW propped in an ashtray ("Why Pictures Now", 1981, W03) -- a three-by-six-inch object that became the title work of the 2017 MoMA survey. A powder-blue announcement card as the only artwork in a Los Angeles show ("A Picture Is No Substitute for Anything", 1981-82, W04) -- the invitation replaces the exhibition, and the Turcotte mailer ("His gesture moved us to tears.") was itself the only artwork in that show. A Plexiglas label in gold capitals asking DOES ANDY WARHOL MAKE YOU CRY? next to a gold Marilyn at auction ("Does Andy Warhol Make You Cry?", 1988, W09). Six text panels of the Helms Amendment standing among 88 photographs of plastic cups ("Helms Amendment (963)", 1989, W10). A "No Drones" slogan on a photograph of a war painting and on drinking glasses sold at the show (W17). The text is never illustration: it is the work, or it reframes the image beside it. The political ephemera (matchbooks, glasses, "WAR IS TERROR" 2001/2003) belong here too, as does the collaborative ad-hoc gallery (W04), which is text-as-work at the scale of an exhibition.

**Takeaway:** print the question on the matchbook and photograph that. (C20014, leg 2 C19872; synthesis C19962)

## M4 -- Shoot the back, the label, the side: the frame that is not there

Refuse the frontal, well-lit museum view and photograph what the institution hides -- the wall label, the back of the picture, the stretcher, the deinstalled canvas on its side, the dark gallery. A Lorrain landscape reduced to a fragment beside its label ("Fragment/Frame/Text (#163)", 1984, W08). Richter's "Mustang Squadron" shot obliquely so its hanging device shows ("No Drones", 2010/2011, W17). Two framed pictures photographed face to the wall, labels unreadable ("Twice Untitled and Other Pictures (looking back)", Wexner Center, 2006, W15 -- the entry photograph was shown in the exhibition as "Please Pay Attention"; do not call the entry image itself "Twice Untitled" without the retitling note). Judd's objects as silhouettes in an unlit museum (W19). The artwork is present but withheld, and the withholding is the point: the viewer is made to read the label, the edge, the shadow, instead of the picture. Do not claim from the title alone that the Hirshhorn "Monochrome" show (1997, W13) was all black-and-white photographs -- only its dates and the illustrated work were verified end to end.

**Takeaway:** photograph the label, the back, or the dark -- never the front. (C20015, leg 2 C19873; synthesis C19963)

## M5 -- Re-present the re-presentation: one photograph, many afterlives

Take an earlier Lawler photograph and run it through another format -- paperweight, tracing, adjusted-to-fit vinyl, distorted version, swiped blur, gif -- so the same image returns changed each time. "Salon Hodler" as a colour photograph, then as a crystal-dome paperweight ("Untitled (Salon Hodler)", paperweight, 1992-93, W11), then as a projection, a tracing and a gif -- the Swiss collector's salon with two Hodlers shrunk to a desk toy. "Pollock and Tureen" as a photograph, then as a wall-size black-and-white outline tracing the viewer must fill in from memory. "Pollyanna" as a photograph, then stretched to a wall, then digitally swirled (W18). "Formica" (2011/2012) existing as a silver dye bleach print, a tracing and an adjusted, distorted "slippery slope 2" version. Nothing is a final state; every showing is a new version. The late career turns the archive on itself: the same photograph returns as paperweight, tracing, adjusted vinyl, distortion, and swipe, so no showing is ever final. The "tracings" as ghosts (black-and-white line versions that remove colour and detail, made with Jon Buller) belong here.

**Takeaway:** never show the same picture twice the same way. (C20016, leg 2 C19874; synthesis C19964)

## M6 -- Let the wall decide the picture: adjusted to fit

Stretch, squeeze or re-scale a photograph to match the exact proportions of the wall it hangs on, so the exhibition space -- not the artist -- determines the image's shape. The Sotheby's room pulled like taffy across a 25-by-75-foot billboard ("Triangle (adjusted to fit)", 2008/2009/2011; High Line billboard, New York, 2014, W16 -- do not date the billboard 2008, and do not date the work 2014). "Pollyanna" and "Triangle" as floor-to-ceiling vinyl murals at MoMA, each wall a different stretch (W18). "Hair (adjusted to fit)" filling four walls and a freestanding wall at the Venice Biennale (dates vary by installation: 2005/2019/2021 in the Biennale catalogue text; 2002/2003/2016/2022 in the Maharam release -- do not merge the two date strings into one). The instruction that every reproduction of an adjusted-to-fit work must itself be stretched to the space given. The distortion is not a defect; it is the work accepting the room's terms. The start year is unresolved ("from 2016" per the Sprueth Magers biography vs "In 2011, Lawler began making her adjusted to fit works" per the Logan Center note) and neither is printed as settled.

**Takeaway:** give the wall the file and let the wall stretch it. (C20017, leg 2 C19875; synthesis C19965)

## M7 -- Distort for the times / swipe: the present bends the image

When the political weather changes, bend the picture with it -- digitally twist an old image, or move the camera during a long exposure so the masterpiece blurs. "Pollyanna" swirled in 2017 as a reaction to fake news and "alternative facts" (W18 -- base photograph 2007/2008, adjusted to fit 2012, distorted for the times 2017; do not present the swirl as original to 2007). Pollock and Still double-exposed into one smeared image by a moving camera; Giacometti's "Palace at 4 a.m." as a dark blur ("Swiped" works, "GOING THROUGH THE MOTIONS", Sprueth Magers, Los Angeles, 10 November 2023-10 February 2024, W20 -- the most recent solo show verified this run; no 2025 solo show was verified end to end). "No Drones" turning a 1964 war painting into a 2011 anti-drone statement (W17 -- the 2010/2011 chromogenic print of Richter's "Mustang Squadron" is a different thing from the "No Drones" exhibitions, London 2011 and Metro Pictures 2014, both tracings shows; do not conflate them). The blur and the twist are dated: they belong to the year they were made, not the year the source was photographed.

**Takeaway:** when the times twist, twist the picture. (C20018, leg 2 C19876; synthesis C19966)

## M8 -- Sound and absence: the work you hear but cannot see

Remove the image entirely and leave only sound, or only the announcement of an image. Twenty-eight male artists' names chirped as birdcalls for 7 minutes and 1 second, with the list of names printed on a panel nearby ("Birdcalls", 1972/81, W01 -- conceived 1972 with Martha Kite, recorded 1981 by Lawler alone; the 1971 origin variant and the 1972/2008 work dating are flagged, use 1972/81). A full-length film soundtrack -- "The Misfits", "The Hustler", "Saturday Night Fever" -- played to a blank screen while the audience sits in the dark and supplies the missing pictures from memory ("A Movie Will Be Shown Without the Picture", 1979, W02). An invitation card to "Swan Lake" that admits nobody, since tickets must be bought at the box office. The audience does the seeing that the work refuses to do. This is the mechanism that makes Lawler more than a photographer of photographs: the first thing she ever made was sound, and the blank screen is as much her work as any print.

**Takeaway:** play the sound, blank the screen, and let the room imagine the picture. (C20019, leg 2 C19877; synthesis C19967)


## The evolution: the operation stays, the picture comes apart

Three movements, and the operation never changes -- point the camera at art where it actually lives and let the room, the label, or the wall do the work -- but its target widens and its picture comes apart. First, 1978-79, the work is absence and borrowing: a horse portrait borrowed from a racetrack on an empty wall, a film soundtrack with no film. Then 1981-84, the signature mode locks in: the announcement card as the exhibition (W04), the collector's home as the gallery (W05-W07), the label beside the fragment (W08). Then 1984-94, the mode runs through its full range -- auction rooms, bedrooms, installations in progress, political ephemera -- while the titles keep crediting the arranger. Second, the picture itself is progressively unmade: first the artwork is cropped, rhymed, or demoted by its neighbours; then the label replaces the painting; then the image is shrunk to a paperweight, stretched to a wall, swirled for the news, and finally smeared by a moving camera. Third, the late career turns the archive on itself: the same photograph returns as paperweight, tracing, adjusted vinyl, distortion, and swipe, so no showing is ever a final state. The 2021 MoMA-after-hours photographs (W19) and the 2023 swiped works (W20) are the same operation at its two ends: in one, the museum is dark and the art is a silhouette; in the other, the camera moves and the art is a blur. Both refuse the frontal view that museums exist to provide. (C20020, leg 2 C19850-C19869; synthesis C19959)

## Aesthetic logic: the picture is a place, and the place has an author

Lawler's aesthetic logic is that a picture is never just a picture -- it is a picture in a place, and the place has an author. The museum, the collector, the dealer, the auction house, the wall: each of them composes the artwork a second time, and Lawler photographs that second composition. That is why the titles name arrangers instead of artists, why the label is photographed instead of the painting, why the billboard is stretched to fit the wall rather than printed to fit the photograph. The work is funny -- a tureen upstaging a Pollock, a refrigerator next to a Rabbit, birdcalls for 28 famous men -- but the joke is a method: humour is how she points at power without making a speech about it. The political works (the Helms cups, the No Drones slogan, WAR IS TERROR) use the same method at a different temperature: the ephemera carries the statement, the photograph carries the ephemera. And the late distortions carry the same logic into the present: when the room is a billboard, the picture stretches; when the times are fake news, the picture swirls; when the camera moves, the masterpiece smears. The picture always accepts the terms of where it is. What she refuses, consistently, is the frontal, well-lit, decontextualised museum view -- the one view that pretends a picture has no place and no author but its maker. Her one-line method, in her own words: "I'm just trying to point things out." (C20021, leg 1 C19818; leg 2 C19870-C19877; synthesis C19960-C19967)
