## M1. Post-Webernian serialism with "false octaves": the homage is the analysis

The Quintette a la memoire d'Anton Webern of 1955, for clarinet, bass clarinet, violin, cello and piano, takes the tone-row from Webern's Quartet op. 22 and builds on major sevenths and minor ninths, which Pousseur called "false octaves", in IRCAM's phrase, and Koenig called "indistinct octaves". The wide intervals sound like mistuned octaves, and the mistuning is the point: the homage does not copy Webern's row, it reworks it through intervals that bend the octave out of shape, and the analysis is audible, because the listener hears Webern's material arriving wrong, stretched by a semitone, and the wrongness is the tribute. The early serial apprenticeship runs through the Sept Versets des Psaumes de la Penitence of 1950, mixed choir, drawn "quite closely" on Webern in IRCAM's phrase, and the Trois Chants sacres of 1951, soprano and string trio, written after meeting Boulez at Royaumont in 1951, and the Symphonies a 15 Solistes of 1954-55, fifteen solo instruments in seven brief sections "in the spirit of Pierrot lunaire", with a density of harmonic groups inside a tightly organized timeline under pervasive chromaticism. Pousseur was unusually interested in Webern's FREE-ATONAL works rather than the serial ones, and argued against "insisting on a complete and continual exposition of the chromatic total", in his 1955 and 1956 Webern essays, and the row is a tool for bending pitch, not a law for filling it. The mechanism is: take a master's row, stretch its intervals by a semitone, and let the homage be heard as a deliberate mistuning, because a tribute that sounds exactly like its model is a copy, and a tribute that sounds like its model bent is an analysis.

**Takeaway:** do not copy the row you admire; stretch it by a semitone and let the stretch be the tribute, because a homage that sounds wrong in exactly the right way is an analysis, and a homage that sounds right is a copy.

## M2. Generalized series and the network: one field that can sound tonal or serial

The "Apotheose de Rameau" essay of 1968 supplies the theory, and La Seconde Apotheose de Rameau of 1981, chamber orchestra of 21 instruments, premiered in Paris on Monday 9 November 1981, is the compositional demonstration of it. Rameau's "unipolar" harmony stands against Webern's "multipolar" conception, and the network is a "distribution of notes along several axes, each constituted as a chain of one and the same interval", so that transposing one harmonic field onto another yields fields that can sound tonal or serial, and one piece can hold both languages at once. Couleurs croisees of 1967, large orchestra, commissioned by the Koussevitzky Foundation at the Library of Congress, premiered by Brussels Radio on Friday 20 December 1968, is the demonstration in the large: a series of variations on the civil-rights song "We Shall Overcome", rigorously derived from that song as a central matrix through the network technique, in six sections moving from chromatic periods and Expressionist borrowings to a symbolic ending in diatonicism. The same network drives the historical parody of Die Erprobung des Petrus Hebraicus of 1974, where the musical matrix is borrowed from the B-minor fugue of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier Book I, all twelve tones, and Act I pays homage to Bach's forms, cantata, ricercare, passacaglia, and Act II moves into expressionism close to Erwartung, and Act III ends in free music with electroacoustics, and it drives the Romantic paraphrase of Dichterliebesreigentraum of 1992-93, a grand paraphrase of Schumann's Dichterliebe, paired with Pousseur's book Schumann le Poete. The mechanism is: build one harmonic field from a borrowed tune or a borrowed fugue, distribute its notes along interval-chains, and transpose the field until it sounds tonal in one place and serial in another, because the network is a machine for letting irreconcilable styles live in one piece, and the irreconcilability is the subject.

**Takeaway:** do not choose between tonal and serial; build one network that can be transposed into both, and let the borrowed tune be the matrix, because a field that can sound like two languages is truer than a piece that speaks only one.

## M3. Open form and the work in movement, in tape: thirty-two loops and two rules

Scambi of 1957 is the first open-form work of electronic music, and Umberto Eco cited it in The Open Work as the exemplar of the "work in movement". Realized at the Studio di Fonologia in Milan, with the RAI, and performed at Incontri Musicali in Milan in May 1957, it is thirty-two loops of filtered white noise, each 30 or 42 seconds, made with Alfredo Lietti's filter and the technician Marino Zuccheri, and the thirty-two loops are sixteen pairs of segments, "layers", assembled in many ways under two linking rules, maximal conformity between the end of one segment and the start of the next, and successive dominance of characters. Five versions were realized at first: two by Berio, one by Marc Wilkinson, two by Pousseur, a long one of about six and a half minutes and a short one of about four. The piece is complete when the material exists, and every assembly is a valid version, and the listener, or the performer, assembles the piece, and no assembly is the definitive one. Boulez hated it, and wrote to Stockhausen on 25 September 1957 that it was "absolutely zero" and "abominable", and the hatred is part of the record, because the hostile witness proves the piece mattered. The mechanism is: make the material once, carefully, and then refuse to fix its order, and give the assembly two rules instead of one score, so that the piece is a set of possibilities, not a sequence, and every performance is a different valid version of the same work. The tape does not play a piece, it offers a field, and the field is assembled in the hearing.

**Takeaway:** do not fix the order of a finished material; give it two linking rules and let every assembly be a valid version, because a piece that can only be played one way is a recording, and a piece that can be assembled is a work in movement.

## M4. Mobile form for live players: the leader and the follower swap

Mobile of 1957-58, for two pianos, is the live-players version of Scambi's idea, and it is a two-player game where Boulez's Third Piano Sonata and Stockhausen's Klavierstuck XI are single-player mobiles. Ten continuous sections, three interchangeable parts per piano, and two performers as "leader" and "follower", and the roles swap, and the leading groups are fully specified but only "in approximate, broad, qualitative fashion", in Pousseur's 1959 phrase quoted by IRCAM, and time units, dynamics, attacks and pedalling are variable, and only an approximate total duration is indicated. The follower watches the leader, and the leader leads only approximately, and the piece is the negotiation between the two, and the audience watches two people decide, in public, what happens next. The mechanism is: write the material fully but the leadership only approximately, and let two players take turns leading and following, because the music is the relationship between them, and the relationship is visible, and the piece changes every time the roles swap. The mobile is not a score with choices in it, it is a game with two players in it, and the game is the piece.

**Takeaway:** do not write a solo mobile and call it open; write it for two players who take turns leading, and let the leadership be approximate, because a game with one player is a puzzle, and a game with two is a relationship, and the relationship is what the audience watches.

## M5. Aleatory by game rules: the lottery and the checkerboard

Repons pour sept musiciens of 1960, for seven musicians, revised with an actor as Repons avec son paysage in 1965, is partly determined by lottery and by the players' free choice based on moves on a checkerboard, in Britannica's account. Chance is a game with rules, not a surrender, and the lottery draws the material, and the checkerboard governs the choice, and the players move like pieces on a board, and the piece is the game played out loud. Pousseur noticed the problem himself: "While the play could prove exciting for performers well-versed in the instructions, to the audience it came off as esoteric", in IRCAM's quotation, and the 1965 version adds a text by Michel Butor "designed both to assist the musicians in their playing... as well as to inform the public by means of an imaginary poetic representation", so that the audience can follow the game it is watching. The mechanism is: let chance enter through a game with visible rules, a lottery draw, a move on a board, so that the audience can see the choice being made, and if the audience cannot follow the game, the game is not finished, and the composer must add the text, the image, the narration that lets the public in. The aleatory is not the point, the legibility of the aleatory is the point, and a game the audience cannot read is a private joke.

**Takeaway:** do not hide chance inside the score; put it on a board the audience can see, and if they cannot follow the game, add the words that let them in, because a game with rules nobody can read is a secret, and a piece is not a secret.

## M6. The audience as co-author: an opera the audience votes in

Votre Faust of 1960-68, "fantaisie variable genre opera", for five actors, four singers, twelve instrumentalists and tape, libretto by Michel Butor, is about seven hours of performable material in a thousand-page score, and any one version runs three to three-and-a-half hours, and the audience votes, interrupts, and in part three is invited to drown out the performers with protests. The structure is five locations, each with a colour and a musical style, blue room and French, green cabaret and French plus German, yellow street and plus English, red fair and plus Italian, violet port and plus Spanish, and five languages introduced progressively, and five epilogues, six routes to each, thirty possible resolutions, and the audience votes, Maggy or Greta to the fair, cruise or stay, and the Theatre Director mediates and at one point calls on an audience member to choose. The concert version was given in Buffalo on Sunday 17 March 1968, and the staged premiere was at the Piccola Scala in Milan on Wednesday 15 January 1969, and the premiere was a fiasco: audiences shouted "basta!", threw coins, cried "Bravo Mozart!" at the Don Giovanni quotation, and planted actors, a claque, voted in French at appointed times, and Pousseur called it a "creation-naufrage", a premiere-shipwreck. Berio loved the score and blamed Butor's text and the staging, and Boulez dismissed the quotation theatre as schoolboy mimicry, and only two further stagings came before 2000, Gelsenkirchen on Saturday 13 March 1982 and Bonn in 1999, then Berlin and Basel in 2013. The mechanism is: give the audience a real ballot with real consequences, thirty resolutions, and let the vote be the form, because the audience is not a witness to the opera, it is a character in it, and the opera is not finished until the audience finishes it, and the fiasco is part of the work, because a vote that cannot go wrong is not a vote. The audience votes, and the vote is the piece.

**Takeaway:** do not invite the audience to watch a choice; give them a ballot with thirty resolutions and let the vote be the form, because an audience that can only applaud is a crowd, and an audience that can vote is a co-author, and a vote that cannot go wrong is not a vote.

## M7. Quotation and collage as structure: the fairground sorts history by nation

Votre Faust sorts history by nation and style in its fairground booths, and the sorting is the structure. The French booth runs Gounod, Berlioz, Bizet and Massenet into Milhaud, and the German booth runs Weber, Lortzing and Wagner into Schoenberg, and the Italian booth runs Rossini to Puccini into Stravinsky, and the English booth runs Handel, Gounod, Mendelssohn, Chopin and Franck into Hindemith, and the Prologue in the sky is a "re-overture" of one hundred celestial notes derived from nine twelve-tone rows quoted from Boulez, Schoenberg, Stockhausen, Stravinsky, Webern and Pousseur himself. Die Erprobung des Petrus Hebraicus of 1974, chamber opera in three acts, libretto by Leo Wintgens after Michel Butor, for Schoenberg's centenary, does the same work with Bach, Schoenberg and Erwartung: the young schoolteacher P. H., Pousseur Henri, evokes Schoenberg in Act I, and the musical matrix is borrowed from the B-minor fugue of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier Book I, all twelve tones, and Act I pays homage to Bach's forms, cantata, ricercare, passacaglia, and Act II moves into expressionism close to Erwartung, and Act III ends in free music with electroacoustics. Boulez's objection, that quotation theatre is schoolboy mimicry, is part of the record, and the objection is answered by the structure itself, because the quotations are not decorations, they are the load-bearing walls, and the booths are the architecture. The mechanism is: sort the past by nation and style, put each nation's music in its own booth, and let the booths be the form, because quotation is not a reference pasted onto a piece, it is a structure the piece is built from, and a quotation that can be removed without damage was never structural.

**Takeaway:** do not quote a tune as a wink; sort the past into booths and build the piece out of the booths, because a quotation that can be cut without damage was decoration, and a quotation that holds the roof up is structure.

## M8. Filtered-noise tape composition: white noise through a filter, narrowed to bands

Scambi and Seismogrammes are built from white noise passed through a filter, narrowed to half-octave bands, selected by amplitude threshold, and the material is statistical, with directed motion. Seismogrammes I-II of 1954, electronic music on tape, was Pousseur's first electronic work, performed in October 1954 on the first concert of full-scale compositions from the WDR Studio for Electronic Music in Cologne, and Pousseur could only work intermittently, a schoolteacher in Malmedy, and Stockhausen helped carry out the technical realization. Scambi's thirty-two sequences are four basic structural types, high or low against fast or slow, against discontinuous or continuous, blended into sixteen intermodulated types, against retrogrades, and the result is thirty-two sequences, and the motion is directed, a spiral density change, a rising or falling pitch tendency disturbed by small contrary deviations. The mechanism is: take the most neutral material there is, white noise, and filter it until it has a character, and then let the character move, because the filter is the composer, and the noise is the instrument, and the piece is the sound of a filter being played. The tape studio is not a recorder, it is an instrument, and the instrument is a filter with a threshold.

**Takeaway:** do not start from a note; start from white noise and filter it until it has a character, and let the character move, because the filter is the composer, and a piece built from filtered noise has no notes to hide behind.

## M9. Musique mixte synchronization: three notation systems in one score

Rimes pour differentes sources sonores of 1958-59, orchestra and tape, premiered at Donaueschingen on Saturday 17 October 1959, is early musique mixte, and the coordination of live and recorded sound is guaranteed by transcribing the recorded sections partly in standard notation, partly in approximate harmonic and rhythmic notation, and partly through graphical forms, in Minetti's account, so that three notation systems live in one score. The orchestra and the tape are two kinds of time, one human and one fixed, and the score must hold them together without pretending they are the same kind of time, and the three notations are the admission that one notation cannot do the job. The mechanism is: when two media meet, do not force one notation onto both, and write the live parts in the notation live players read, and the taped parts in the notation taped sound needs, and the meeting points in pictures, because a score that pretends tape and orchestra are the same thing will lie about both. The mixed piece is a treaty between two times, and the treaty is written in three languages.

**Takeaway:** do not force one notation onto two media; write the live in notes, the taped in approximations, and the meeting in pictures, because a score that pretends tape and orchestra are the same thing lies about both.

## M10. Colour-mapped form: blue tones, red and yellow, and five rooms in five colours

Trois Visages de Liege of 1961, electronic music produced in Brussels and commissioned for a sound and light show, is three movements "almost in the classical sense": a long introductory Andante, "mostly limited to blue tones and fleeting forms", and a Scherzo in red and yellow, "with clear and sharp articulations", and a Finale "in the vein of the chorale", in Pousseur's own account, "La musique electronique, art figuratif?", quoted by IRCAM, and the duration is about twenty minutes and fifty seconds. Colour is structure, not decoration, and the blue movement is fleeting because blue is fleeting, and the red and yellow movement is sharp because red and yellow are sharp, and the chorale finale is a chorale because the piece has earned a chorale. Votre Faust extends the mapping to the whole opera: five locations, five lighting colours, five languages introduced progressively, blue room, green cabaret, yellow street, red fair, violet port, and the colour tells the audience where it is before the music tells it what to hear. The mechanism is: map a colour onto a movement, and a movement onto a colour, and let the colour do structural work, because the ear hears the form faster when the eye has already seen it, and a colour that only decorates is a costume, and a colour that carries the form is a load-bearing wall.

**Takeaway:** do not colour a finished piece; map the colour onto the form before the form is finished, and let the colour tell the audience where it is, because a colour that only decorates is a costume, and a colour that carries the form is a wall.

## M11. Political text-setting and the protest-song matrix: "We Shall Overcome" as a serial matrix

Couleurs croisees of 1967 derives everything from "We Shall Overcome", and the civil-rights song is the central matrix, and the whole piece is rigorously derived from it through the network technique, and Pousseur wanted a voice "modeled on the manner of a Black church minister and powerful enough to hold its own against the orchestra". The politics are in the matrix, not in a slogan pasted onto the music, and the song is the serial row, and the row is a song the audience already knows, and the piece is a demonstration that a protest song can be a rigorous compositional system. Croisees des couleurs croisees of 1970, amplified female voice, preferably Black, two to five pianos, six sound sources, two radios, two tape recorders, two turntables, about twenty-five minutes, premiered in New York on Sunday 29 November 1970 by Pousseur conducting with mezzo-soprano Joy Blackett, intensifies the original: the texts excerpt Chief Seattle's speech and William Lloyd Garrison's open letter "To the Public", and the piece is dedicated in memoriam to Martin Luther King Jr., and the pianos inherit much of Couleurs croisees' harmonic and rhythmic material, and the radios are dialled aleatorily, and Alain Feron called it an indictment of racism "in which the emotional force of the message is matched by its sound organization". The mechanism is: take a borrowed song or a borrowed speech, make it the matrix, derive the whole piece from it, and let the radios bring in the present moment by chance, because politics enters as borrowed song and borrowed speech, not as slogan, and a protest song used as a serial matrix is a political act that cannot be removed from the music, since the music IS the song, transformed.

**Takeaway:** do not set a political text on top of neutral music; make the borrowed song the matrix and derive everything from it, because a slogan pasted onto music can be peeled off, and a song that IS the row cannot.

## M12. Phoneme decomposition: the voice reduced to its smallest sounds

Phonemes pour Cathy of 1966, mezzo-soprano solo, Madrigal V, is written for Cathy Berberian, and the voice is reduced to phonemes, and language is broken down to its smallest sounds, and the voice is sound material rather than text carrier. Votre Faust breaks texts down to phonemes, recited, sung or shouted in five European languages, and the five languages are introduced progressively across the five locations, and the phoneme is the unit that survives the translation between them. The madrigal series runs through the whole career as the laboratory for this work: Madrigal I of 1958 for solo clarinet, Madrigal II of 1961 for flute, violin, viola da gamba and harpsichord, four early instruments, the old-instrument pole of the series, set against Madrigal I's solo modern clarinet, and Madrigal III of 1962, Madrigal IV of 1965 for oboe, and Madrigal V as Phonemes pour Cathy, and the series tests vocal and instrumental utterance across old and new instruments, and the phoneme is the common ground, because a phoneme is older than any language and newer than any instrument. The mechanism is: break the word down to the phoneme, and the phoneme down to the sound, and let the sound be the music, because a word that is only spoken is a message, and a word broken into phonemes is a material, and the material can be composed.

**Takeaway:** do not set the word; break it into phonemes and compose the phonemes, because a word that stays whole is a message, and a word broken into its smallest sounds is a material, and a material can be music.

## M13. Satellite works and work-clusters: a big work is a solar system

Votre Faust generated satellites for over thirty years, Miroir de Votre Faust of 1964-65, Jeu de Miroirs de Votre Faust of 1964-65, Echos de Votre Faust of 1961-69, Les Ruines de Jeruzona, La Passion selon Guignol, Parade, Il sogno di Leporello of 2005, Aiguillages au carrefour des immortels of 2002, and Die Erprobung generated the Berlin chronicles, Chroniques berlinoises, Ballade berlinoise, Canines, Chroniques canines, and the Icare cycle and Aquarius-Memorial, in memoriam Karel Goeyvaerts, 1993-98, form further clusters. A big work is a solar system, not a single object, and the satellites are not leftovers, they are the work continuing in smaller bodies, and the catalogue is organized as clusters, not as a shelf of separate pieces. The mechanism is: when a work is too big to finish, do not finish it, and let it generate satellites, and each satellite is a complete piece that carries a fragment of the big work's DNA, because a large idea does not fit in one piece, and the pieces around it are the idea's real shape. The opera is the sun, and the satellites are the system, and the system is the work.

**Takeaway:** do not finish a big idea in one piece; let it throw off satellites for thirty years, and treat each satellite as a complete piece, because a large idea does not fit in one object, and the cluster around it is the idea's real shape.

## M14. Ethno-electroacoustic planetary time: the whole world as a sample library, organized by the clock

Seize Paysages planetaires of 2000 is ethno-electroacoustic music, a three-CD synthesis, commissioned by the Brussels architect Philippe Samyn for a business complex at Nivelles, an electronic carillon sounding variations every hour from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. Pousseur mapped the 16 working hours onto the 24 hours of a terrestrial revolution and divided the globe into eight north/south zones, and over a background of evolving noises, sea, fire, city, swamp, industry, forest, sit ethno-musical samples from the regions involved, digitally transformed until barely recognizable as traditional music, and the landscape titles contract several regions, "Alaskamazonie", "Gamelan Celtibere", and Michel Butor wrote the accompanying prose-verse, and the sixty-page booklet carries two long Pousseur essays, and the work was realized in the studio of his son Denis. Voix et Vues planetaires of 2003-04 extends the late planetary project into image, "multimedia" work combining electroacoustic music with digital images and texts by Butor. The mechanism is: take the whole world as a sample library, organize it by the clock, and let the hour of the day choose which part of the world sounds, because planetary time is the only form big enough for a whole world, and the carillon is the clock made audible, and the listener hears the earth turn. The late synthesis is the electronic and the ethnographic in one system, and the system is a clock, and the clock is the piece.

**Takeaway:** do not sample the world at random; map the hours onto the turning earth and let the clock choose what sounds, because a sample library with no clock is a drawer, and a sample library organized by planetary time is a world.

## Evolution

P1. Post-Webernian serialism, about 1949-1955. Tone-rows in a post-Webernian spirit, aphoristic choral and chamber pieces, and the Webern essays, "Le chromatisme organique d'Anton Webern" of 1955 and "De Schoenberg a Webern: une mutation" of 1956, and Pousseur was unusually interested in Webern's free-atonal works rather than the serial ones, and argued against insisting on a complete and continual exposition of the chromatic total. The works are the Sept Versets of 1950, the Trois Chants sacres of 1951, Prospection of 1952-53 for three pianos tuned in sixths of a tone, microtonal detuning as a serial parameter, pitch space itself serialized by retuning, and the Symphonies a 15 Solistes of 1954-55, and the Quintette a la memoire d'Anton Webern of 1955, and the early electronics begin at the end of the phase, Seismogrammes of 1954, with Stockhausen helping the schoolteacher from Malmedy carry out the technical realization in Cologne.

P2. Mobility and the open work, 1956-1959. Scambi, Mobile, Rimes, and the mobile becomes the signature, first in tape, then in live players, then in mixed media, and Pousseur lectures at Darmstadt from 1957 to 1967. The period is the breakthrough, and the breakthrough is a refusal: the work will no longer be fixed, and the tape loops will be assembled, and the two pianos will take turns leading, and the orchestra and the tape will be held together by three notations, and the piece is complete when the material exists, not when the order is fixed.

P3. The Butor turn and Votre Faust, 1960-1969. The 1960 watershed, in Universal Edition's phrase, is "refusing the refusal" of historical experience, and the break with Darmstadt orthodoxy is a fulfilment of Webern, not a renunciation. The collaboration with Michel Butor begins with the letter of 29 September 1960, and twenty-odd works follow over forty-plus years, and music becomes "realist", in Butor's 1960 Esprit article "La musique, art realiste", representational and theatrical, and the audience is invited inside the work, in Repons avec son paysage of 1965 and in Votre Faust, and the three-year residency at SUNY Buffalo from 1966 to 1969 closes the decade. The period is the turn from the mobile to the theatre, and the theatre is the mobile with people in it.

P4. Generalized series and the network, after 1970. The "Apotheose de Rameau" essay of 1968 supplies the theory, and the network is a distribution of notes along interval-chains, letting one harmonic field transpose into many, so that tonal, serial, jazz and popular-song languages can live in one piece. Couleurs croisees is the demonstration, and Die Erprobung is the historical parody, and Dichterliebesreigentraum is the Romantic paraphrase, and La Seconde Apotheose de Rameau is the theory made audible. Satellite-work clusters become the working method, the Faust satellites, the Icare cycle, Aquarius-Memorial, and teaching dominates the 1980s, director of the Liege Conservatory from 1975, Institut de Pedagogie musicale in Paris from 1983 to 1987, the magazine Marsyas, and the late turn is ethno-electroacoustic planetary music, 2000-04, and then the unfinished memorial to Froidebise in 2009, closing the circle opened in 1947-52, when the Variations group first showed him Webern.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Pousseur's work is that nothing is finished until someone else finishes it, and the work is a field of possibilities, and the possibilities are the work. The mobile is not a trick, it is a belief: a piece that can only be played one way is a recording, and a piece that can be assembled, voted in, led by either of two players, dialled on a radio, is a work, because the work is what happens when someone meets the material, and the meeting is the music. The quotation is not a reference, it is a structure, and the fairground booths sort history by nation because history IS sorted by nation, and the booths are honest about it, and the Prologue quotes nine twelve-tone rows because the sky is made of other people's rows, and the opera is a sky full of borrowed stars. The network is not a system, it is a permission: one harmonic field that can sound tonal or serial gives the composer permission to use both languages, and the permission is the point, because the avant-garde's refusal of history was itself a refusal, and Pousseur refused the refusal, and the refusal of the refusal is the whole career, from the Webern essays that argued against the complete exposition of the chromatic total, through the mobile that refused to fix its order, to the opera that refused to fix its ending, to the planetary music that refused to fix its samples. The politics are not in a party card, they are in the form, and the form is democratic, and the democracy is literal: the audience votes, and the vote counts, and the protest song is the matrix, and the matrix is rigorous, and the rigour is the politics, because a protest song derived with serial rigour is a proof that rigour and feeling are not enemies. The colour is not decoration, it is the map, and the blue movement is fleeting because blue is fleeting, and the five rooms are five colours because the audience needs to know where it is before it can hear what is happening. The satellite is not a leftover, it is the work continuing, and the big idea does not fit in one piece, so it throws off pieces for thirty years, and the pieces are the idea. The whole oeuvre is one long education in openness, from the thirty-two tape loops of Scambi that any assembler can assemble, through the two pianos that take turns leading, through the checkerboard that lets the players choose, through the opera that lets the audience vote, through the radios that let the present moment in, to the planetary carillon that lets the hour choose which part of the world sounds, and the lesson is that a work is not a finished object, it is a field, and the field is finished by whoever enters it.
