## M1. Glass as membrane (the pane that keeps both sides)

Aquariums, hotel windows, car windshields, glass doors, condensation-blurred panes: the device repeats across forty years. Veracruz, Mexico, 2003 (f10): a woman leans at an aquarium tank in turquoise water-light, fish gliding above her, her face doubled by the glass. Beluga Whale, 1998 (f16): through aquarium glass, the white whale glides while ghost-visitors and sky reflect across the same pane. After the Rain, Rush County, 2015 (f08): through a rain-specked windshield, a towering white cumulus over a NO HUNTING sign. Eleanor, 2019 (f09): an elderly woman dozing in a lamplit Victorian interior, shot through a glass door that layers reflected sunlit trees over the room. Four Days Before, Trieste, 2022 (f14): through a hotel window, the curtain edge, the ceiling lamp blooming as a golden double orb, red rooftops and a rising moon in blue dusk. Winter Storm, 2016 (f18): a snow-whiteout park shot through condensed glass, soft fog-blobs on the pane itself. The mechanism differs from Alex's mirrors: his mirrors are frames within frames, compositional insets with their own light; her glass is a membrane, inside and outside co-present, neither resolving. She was named the "visual poet of thresholds" for exactly this. The watchable behavior is the double exposure you cannot separate: the glass refuses to tell you which side is the picture.

**Takeaway:** put a pane between the viewer and the scene and keep both sides (aquarium, windshield, hotel window, glass door); let reflections lay a second world over the first; never let the viewer resolve which side is the picture (the membrane is the subject, not the window).

## M2. Words last, pairing at the end (photographs first, except once)

"I think in images, whether I have a camera in my hand or a pencil. That means that the photographs tend to come first, the words later on... Only near the project's end, did I settle on image and text pairings" (photo-eye). The mechanism is temporal, not compositional: the images are made for years, the text is chosen at the book's end, and the pairing is a decision, not an illustration. The goal is not description but expansion: "suggesting the more elusive metaphysical geography that lies outside the frame." Her signature IG format encodes it weekly: a short poem (often by someone else, Glück, Berger, Walcott, Cole, Sze, Hirshfield, Graham, Adnan, Rankine, Carson, Li Qingzhao, Long Soldier, Peterson...) set against one of her photographs, the poem never describing the photo. It rhymes slantwise or not at all. The doctrine was reversed exactly once: A Difficulty Is a Light (Chose Commune, 2024), her first hybrid poetry book, where the tercets arose first (January 2023, about 30 by spring) and the photographs followed, "acting like a rest in music, lengthening the silence between the ten poetic movements, a silence often tinged with blue and gold." The reversal proves the rule: the method is a two-stage process with a deliberate gap between the stages.

**Takeaway:** generate the images first and choose the text last (a two-stage process with a deliberate gap); never let the words describe the image (expansion, not illustration); allow one reversal per career, and name it as the reversal.

## M3. Poetry lives in the space between (the blind-spot doctrine)

"Perhaps this hybrid book's subtle poetry exists in the space between its words and images, another kind of blind spot, a rich emptiness that seems filled, at times, with the faintest echoings" (her 2017 photo-eye best-books piece, on Teju Cole's Blind Spot). The foundation is biographical: at 15 she handwrote Dickinson lines into a spiral sketchbook collaged with family snapshots; "I was creating a kind of slantwise conversation between the two." Dickinson's "Tell all the Truth but tell it slant" is the named doctrine, and she says "I blame becoming a poet on reading too much Emily Dickinson at too early an age. Her 'certain slant of light' taught me not only how to see in a landscape, but also how to feel its emotional resonance." Poetic forms become book armatures: villanelles in My Dakota, epistolary poems in Night Calls ("Dad became the 'you'"; letter poems "meant to be overheard by a third person, a future reader"), tercets in A Difficulty Is a Light. Wright Morris is her photo-text creed: "I don't give up the camera eye when I write, merely the camera," and "Wright Morris was a pioneer of what he called 'photo-texts'." The watchable behavior is the empty middle: the photograph and the text face each other across a silence, and the meaning lives in the silence.

**Takeaway:** pair image and text so they never touch (the meaning lives in the space between); let the text come from elsewhere (her poem, someone else's poem, a found line) and never let it describe the picture; name the slant: tell it slant, and let the blind spot be the composition's center.

## M4. Confusion as method (foreground and background refuse to separate)

My Dakota's visual rule, stated in her own captions: "an image where it's difficult to distinguish the foreground from the background." Abandoned Farmhouse (f05): looking through a shroud-like curtain at a ruined farm interior, the veil doubling the scene so inside and outside are undecidable. "I felt confused while photographing in South Dakota, but I also felt confused when I returned... to edit," and Eugene Richards's counsel during the edit: "Becky, sometimes confusion is good." Alex's gloss on her process: "Whereas I tend to dive into a project, Rebecca often wades into it, slowly finding her way to the metaphors that guide her work." The mechanism is wading: she does not arrive at the metaphor and then photograph it; she photographs until the metaphor finds her, and then the edit is where she learns what she was confused about. The confusion is not a defect of the seeing; it is the seeing. For generative work this is a focus model: a scene that refuses to resolve depth, a foreground and background that swap places, the veil as a permanent lens.

**Takeaway:** make the foreground and background undecidable (a veil, a curtain, a focus plane that refuses to commit); let confusion be the method, not the failure (photograph first, learn what you were confused about in the edit); wade, do not dive.

## M5. The not-finding photograph (the bird never appears)

The Painted Bunting (f11): a sepia pond reflecting bare branches, a white graffiti tag on the stone rim, one drifting leaf. The bird itself never appears. The post pairs her poem: "I look and I look and I look / until I, too, flit / like a bird blown off course, / acclimatizing to not finding / what I'm looking for." The mechanism is inverted search: weeks of looking for a bird that never shows, and the photograph shows the pond instead. The failure to find IS the subject. This is the direct inverse of Alex's 99.9% failure doctrine (#269, M6): his doctrine says keep walking until the frame arrives; hers says the non-arrival is also a frame, and it gets the book. The watchable behavior is an empty theater: the stage is set, the spotlight is on, and the actor never comes, and you keep watching because the emptiness is where the poem lives.

**Takeaway:** photograph the non-arrival (the bird that never shows, the pond instead); let the empty frame stand as a finished picture; pair it with text that names the failure, not the subject.

## M6. Birds as grief instruments (the flock is the elegy)

Terns (f01, Violet Isle, Cuba), murmurations over bare branches (f12 Aftershock, Oaxaca 2018; f13 Murmuration II, A Difficulty Is a Light), the mute swan on black water (f17), pelicans, geese, owls, waxwings: birds recur across decades as the figures that carry elegy. The context is explicit: My Dakota is an elegy for her brother Dave (d. 2006); A Difficulty Is a Light is an elegy for her remaining brother (d. by suicide, fall 2022, the identical twin of the first); Night Calls is haunted by her father (d. 2025). "What does an aftershock look like? The birds knew before we knew." Murmuration I is the last word of A Difficulty Is a Light: "the dark flock appears to be rising skyward... since grief often ends with a letting go." On the swan: "For the past two months, I feel a curious need to be in the company of swans... why are they calling to me? Perhaps it's because I know too much about them: those hauntingly beautiful swan songs before death are nothing but sheer myth." The mechanism is a transfer function: grief goes in, birds come out. They are never decorative. They are the only figures allowed to move.

**Takeaway:** let birds carry the elegy (the flock is the transfer function from grief to motion); never decorate with them (they are the only figures allowed to move); end the work with the flock rising.

## M7. Night driving as doctrine (the windshield is the viewfinder)

Night Calls, six years of driving Rush County, Indiana, at night, Radius 2020: "presented at night, which is when I often photographed, echoing the rhythm of Dad's house calls." The car's headlights, the windshield, the fogged road: After the Rain (f08) is the windshield as viewfinder. "Driving in the dark, even time seems to break open." The spur is a question, not a place: W. Eugene Smith's "Country Doctor" Life essay seen at ICP, "How would a woman tell this story, especially if she happened to be the doctor's daughter?" The epistolary text addresses the father as "you." The sycamore quartet punctuates the book: "to punctuate the book's meandering sequence, I used four vertical photographs of sycamores... this quartet of sycamores became a metaphor for my father." The mechanism is a moving darkroom: the car is the only studio, the headlights are the only light, and the road keeps the frame from settling.

**Takeaway:** make the car the studio (night, headlights, windshield as viewfinder); let the road keep the frame from settling; punctuate the sequence with a repeating motif that stands in for the person the work is really about.

## M8. Time as the silent collaborator (the wall of small prints)

The sequencing method, from the Slant Rhymes interviews: "when we first spread our small Cuba prints on the floor of our hotel room in Cadiz, Spain, we began to see that our photographs talk to one another in surprising ways." The protocol: small prints taped to the studio wall in the considered sequence for WEEKS OR MONTHS; "Time, we find, is the best editor"; "time has become our silent collaborator" (R.N.W.). "Whatever disagreements we may have are usually resolved by time. The longer those little prints stay up on the wall, the greater the likelihood that we'll come to an agreement on the sequence" (A.W.). The pairing rules (A.W.): "Sometimes it's a shared palette or geometry that links our photographs, other times a shared geography or subject or tone." Her maximal-difference principle (R.N.W.): "the more distance between the two things being compared, the greater the leap of imagination needed to bridge the gap," like surprising poetic metaphors; the pairs "rhyme at a slant, more akin to what's called a slant rhyme in poetry, a pair of words that don't rhyme exactly, such as 'blue' and 'moon.'" And the My Dakota handwriting discipline, from David Chickey at Radius: "handwrite my spare text in pencil (the way I write in my notebooks) and to pull each interrelated poem through the book, page by page, line by line, like the centerline of those back roads." The mechanism is duration as an editing instrument: nothing is decided until the wall has lived with it.

**Takeaway:** sequence on the wall, small, for weeks or months (time is the silent collaborator); pair by maximal difference (the greater the leap, the better the metaphor); pull the text through the book by hand, line by line, like the centerline of a back road.

## Aesthetic logic

Rebecca Norris Webb's practice is an argument that a photograph is half a sentence, and that the other half should come from somewhere the camera cannot go. Alex photographs the world to see what is there; she photographs the world to see what is missing: the bird that never shows, the brother who died, the father whose house calls she echoes, the poem that belongs to someone else. Her instruments are all membranes: glass that keeps both sides, curtains that refuse to separate foreground from background, the silence between an image and a text that never describe each other. Where Alex's engine is additive (more planes, more color, more books), hers is subtractive (confusion kept, not resolved; the flock rising instead of the subject arriving; the words arriving years after the pictures). The doorway matters: #269's teacher taught her nothing; she taught the teacher how to sequence. Dante Sisofo (#268) took Alex's sequencing lesson and built an archive out of it; the archive's real author was the woman taping small prints to the wall and letting time edit. Two directions from one partnership: the frame that fills with the world, and the frame that keeps what the world took away. This study is the second direction, stated as eight mechanisms with tunable parameters.
