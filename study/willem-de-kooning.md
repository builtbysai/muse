## M1. The scrape-and-repaint cycle (paint on, paint off, paint on again)

De Kooning's central procedure is not painting but re-painting, and the painting is what survives the scraping. The paint is applied, judged, and scraped off, with a palette knife, a rag, or the edge of a tool, down to a ghost image, and then painted over again, and on Woman I the cycle ran for nearly two years, per MoMA, with "many preliminary studies, numerous painting sessions, the scraping down and re-painting of entire sections", and "hundreds of revisions, several abandonments and restarts", per Hess via The Art Story, and Elaine estimated about 200 scraped-away versions on that one canvas, an estimate attributed to her, not a counted fact, and on Excavation the Art Institute of Chicago states the same procedure, "intensive building up of the surface and scraping down of its paint layers, often for months, until the desired effect was achieved", and the dated timeline for Woman I supports the scale and forbids more precision, begun in the summer of 1950, work ceased in January-February 1952, Meyer Schapiro encouraged a revisit in June, finished mid-June 1952, more revisions in December, and the watchable behaviour is a surface that never settles, pentimenti and ghost limbs showing through the final image, forms that look arrived-at rather than drawn, a painting that visibly remembers its own earlier states, and the mechanism is the opposite of the single inspired gesture, because the gesture is rehearsed hundreds of times on the same canvas and the canvas keeps the rehearsal. **Takeaway:** paint the picture, scrape it down to a ghost, and paint it again, hundreds of times, until the surface remembers its own earlier states, so the finished painting is the survivor of its own destruction. Against the chain: Gorky (#386 M4) also built his paintings in hundreds and hundreds of layers, and his is an accumulation that makes the canvas heavy, and de Kooning's is an accumulation that is then removed, which is why Gorky's picture is a weight and de Kooning's picture is a ghost, and the ghost is the scraping, and the scraping is the memory, and the memory is the surface.

## M2. The Woman as armature (one figure to hang the paint on)

## M6. The glimpse (the "slipping glimpser")

De Kooning's own theory of content comes from the 1960 Sylvester interview, "Content Is A Glimpse", recorded for BBC radio in March 1960 and first published in print in 1963: "Content, if you want to say, is a glimpse of something, an encounter, you know, like a flash, it is very tiny, very tiny, content." ## M8. Arm-span scale (the canvas sized to a body)
