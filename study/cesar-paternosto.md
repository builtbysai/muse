# Cesar Paternosto: Study Notes

**Site:** Cesar Paternosto, born La Plata, Argentina, 1931 (the day is contested, 29 November against 27 April -- see Corrections 1), Argentine painter, sculptor and theorist, the platense who pushed his painting to its edges instead of its centre: first "geometria sensible", bands of atonal, highly saturated colour that left room for the painter's touch against the detached, uniform look of the first Argentine concrete generation; then, from February 1969, the oblique (lateral) vision, painting only on the deep side edges of the stretcher and leaving the frontal surface blank, so the viewer must walk around the work to see it at all; then, from 1977, the Andean turn, square canvases halved vertically, marble powder, earth and grey pigments, Quechua titles, and the thesis that abstraction was born in the stone and textiles of the Andes centuries before modernism, set out in "Piedra abstracta" (1989) and "The Stone and the Thread" (1996); then the Porticoes, the "Marginality and Displacements" perimeter bands, the water threads, and late painted objects on aluminium that are neither sculpture nor painting but both (C9601, C9602, C9603, C9604). He is living, active in Segovia, Spain, through 2026, aged 94-95, with retrospectives in La Plata and Mexico City and an honorary doctorate from the Universidad Nacional de La Plata in 2026 (C9601, C9603).

## The life: law abandoned, the front abandoned, Europe abandoned for the Andes

Cesar Paternosto was born in La Plata, Argentina, in 1931 (C9602, C9618, C9611). His father Pedro was a chemistry professor at the National University; his mother Maria Esther Nethol was a piano teacher and homemaker; the paternal grandparents came from Calabria, the maternal grandfather from Navarre (C9602). A sister, Graciela, was born in 1935 and died in 1984 (C9602). La Plata itself matters to the work: a city founded in 1882, the first modern city designed on a drawing board, its plan a square grid of blocks criss-crossed by diagonals at 45 degrees, with a mandala-like centre, the Plaza Moreno flanked by City Hall and the neo-gothic cathedral; and the seat of the Museo de Ciencias Naturales, whose pre-Columbian collection from the Andean region he would revisit in 1961 and again, decisively, in the 1970s (C9602). At eleven he taught himself to paint in oils, copying landscapes from magazine reproductions; at school he drew and made carbon copies from comic books (C9602). He entered law school at the National University in 1951, grew disenchanted, worked in a meat packing plant, tried acting, and in 1956 returned to painting, taking private lessons with Jorge R. Mieri, the start of a lifelong friendship; Mieri introduced him to modernist principles and to the flatness of the pictorial surface (C9601, C9602). In 1957-1960 he attended Hector Cartier's lectures on design and colour theory at the School of Fine Arts, a "mini-Bauhaus" wedged inside a strictly academic curriculum; Cartier's teaching had a cardinal influence on him (C9601, C9602). He graduated Master in Law in June 1958, was appointed staff lawyer at the Attorney General's office of the Province of Buenos Aires, married Maria Rosa Marino in March 1959, took a post as legal counselor to the Mines Department in Rio Negro, Patagonia, resigned after six months and returned to La Plata to paint (C9602). In 1961 he attended Emilio Estiu's aesthetics course at the Institute of Philosophy (C9601, C9602).

His first group show was the Salon Arte Nuevo in 1961, with a work strongly influenced by Antoni Tapies' informel painting (C9601, C9602). In 1960 he had seen the Grupo Si exhibition in La Plata and, through his friend Alejandro Puente, was invited to join the group, which practised informalist painting in the line of Tapies, Millares, Munoz and Saura and the American action painters (C9602, C9603). With Grupo Si he showed in Lima and at the Museo Provincial de Bellas Artes in La Plata, and in 1962 at the Museo de Arte Moderno in Buenos Aires, which acquired one of his works; in November 1962 he showed at Galeria Rubbers in Buenos Aires, sharing the space with Nelson Blanco, another Grupo Si member (C9602). Meanwhile he was building his studio-house in City Bell, on the semi-rural outskirts of La Plata, designed by the architect Vicente Krause, and learning cement casting, a technique he would return to thirty years later in sculpture (C9602). His daughter Maria Andrea was born in January 1962 (C9602).

In 1962 he revisited the Museo de Ciencias Naturales and "discovered" the pre-Columbian ceramics of northwest Argentina; he studied Klee's and Miro's primitivism and gradually broke away from Tapies; by 1962-63 he was scratching out a sort of pictographic archaic geometry inspired by Amerindian art, in heavy impastos in dark earth tones (C9602). In 1963 he moved into the City Bell house, sent paintings to San Juan, Puerto Rico, left the archaic symbolism behind, made imaginary figurations in oils and ink drawings inspired by Paul Klee, then studied Klee's banded or "architectural" watercolors of the Bauhaus period and painted "soft edged" geometric abstractions (C9602). In 1964 he met Aldo Pellegrini, the surrealist poet and critic, who in a lecture at the Di Tella Institute named Puente and Paternosto the spearheads of a "new abstraction" in Argentina, different from the established Concrete art tradition affiliated with Max Bill's aesthetics; the same year Clement Greenberg, visiting Buenos Aires, singled out his work, Puente's and Honorio Morales' as the only things that interested him there (C9602). In October 1964 he showed the "Magic Balloons" series at Galeria Lirolay, sharing the space with Puente; seeing Josef Albers' "Homage to the Square" in a travelling show at the Di Tella encouraged him to use colour directly from the tube, and to intensify its saturation he juxtaposed strips of metallic paper glued to the canvas (C9602). Pellegrini wrote the preface for that catalogue, coining the name "geometria sensible" (sensitive geometry) to designate the abstraction developed by Paternosto and Puente (C9602). In 1965 he stopped using metallic paper and started painting bands exploring the "atonality" of colour: "strange chords, such as a brown next to a pink, and the like", in his own words; soon the bands became waving and concentrically arranged; he also began using thicker stretchers, prolonging the bands of colour onto them (C9602, C9606). "Staccato" (1965), with its concentric curvilinear bands of atonal colour, is the emblematic work of this moment; it was acquired by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, in 2008 after being auctioned at Christie's the preceding year (C9601, C9604, C9606).

In 1966 he was selected for the annual Di Tella National Prize exhibition -- participation, not a win; the RGR CV lists it under group exhibitions, and he must not be called a Di Tella prize winner (C9603) -- and Jorge Romero Brest invited him and Puente to show at Galeria Bonino, writing the catalogue introduction (C9602). He was invited to the III Bienal Americana de Arte in Cordoba, where the jury was presided by Alfred H. Barr Jr., founding director of MoMA; Carlos Cruz-Diez took the Great Honor Prize and Paternosto won First Prize for his entry, three shaped two-panel pieces (C9601, C9602, C9603). One of them, the diptych "Duino" (1966), was bought by Barr for MoMA's collection through the Inter-American Fund; MoMA records it as oil on canvas in two parts, object 450.1967.a-b, acquired 1967 (C9602, C9607). The shaped canvases of 1966 were composed of several parts hung at predetermined distances, works that only acquired their entity once installed, a modality he would return to for decades (C9602). In 1966 the collector William H. Weintraub became an enthusiastic patron, instrumental in sales including three works bought by Joseph H. Hirshhorn, now in the Hirshhorn Museum (C9602).

His second daughter Ana Maria was born in January 1967; in April 1967 he travelled to New York with his family to explore settling there, and in November 1967 returned to settle (C9602). The move was propelled by the growing appreciation of his work in New York and, in the background, by the 1966 military coup in Argentina, the Revolucion Argentina, which was closing in on freedom of expression while the Di Tella Institute came under attack (C9601, C9610). He rented a studio in Little Italy, worked as a waiter at the Figaro Cafe in Greenwich Village and as an arts and crafts instructor for a summer program on the Lower East Side; his third daughter Maria Jose was born in September 1968 (C9602). In September 1968 he opened "Symmetrical/Asymmetrical" at the AM Sachs Gallery on 57th Street, four multi-part pieces of the same size exploring the inter-relationships among areas of colour in outer semi-circular shaped canvases and wooden volumes placed at the centre, hung at precise intervals; Time magazine's September 1968 notice called it an important debut: "Singing colors, good craftsmanship, and a deft hand at playing off symmetrical and asymmetrical forms... each is composed of at least seven individual units, some of canvas, others of wood, which are mounted on the wall to form echoing arrangements of color, pattern, and mathematical dimensions" (C9610). He shared a loft at 248 Lafayette Street with Fernando Maza and Luis Wells (C9602).

The breakthrough came in February 1969: he decided to leave the frontal surface of the painting blank, using only the sides to paint on, aware he had hit a first, "literally pushing the medium to the limits" (C9602). His own site puts it in his own words: he "brought the pictorial notations to the side edges of the picture leaving the frontal surface blank, an iconoclastic silence that implied the rejection of today's surrounding visual noise", while "emphasizing the object nature of painting" (C9601). The stimulus was the reductivist climate created by the minimalist sculptors, and at the same time a fierce rejection of the minimalist theorists' belief, chiefly Donald Judd's, that "sculpture was more powerful than painting": instead he was critically returning to easel painting, "that cultural artifact exclusive to the West", yet inviting the viewer to read it in a non-frontal, sideways and ambulatory manner, walking from one side of the painting to the other to absorb its totality (C9601). In January 1970 he opened the second AM Sachs show, titled "The Oblique Vision": the canvases looked "boxy" because the sides were deep, 4 1/2 or 5 inches, crowded with series of small squares and bands, making for an exceedingly complex reading since the viewer had to move from one side to the other to apprehend the totality; some titles came from Borges's fiction, "Tlon, Uqbar"; he later destroyed most of these works, the pale pastel frontal tones having proved unsatisfactory (C9601, C9602). The show was positively reviewed by Kay Kline in Art News ("carefully proportioned... balanced with the regular irregularity and poetry of Mondrian. All is achieved through indirect suggestion; a faint halation on the surrounding wall indicates the implicit presence of colors which cannot be explicitly seen, while corners and edges appear to gently disintegrate"); Lucy R. Lippard, Sol LeWitt, Richard Bellamy and John Weber saw it, and LeWitt's comment was "It's a very good idea" (C9602, C9610). Carter Ratcliff, in Art International's "New York Letter" (March 1970), was hostile, calling Paternosto "an eccentric because he has chosen such an odd and fruitless place to stop in the transition from painting to sculpture" (C9602, C9610). The same season Jo Baer showed her "wraparound" paintings a few blocks away at the Noah Goldowsky Gallery; Artforum's March 1970 review of Baer, by a reviewer who had briefly visited Paternosto's show, did not mention him at all, not even in a footnote, a slight the chronology reads as the New York establishment's refusal to see a South American artist at the cutting edge (C9602, C9610). In April 1970 he separated from his wife, who returned to Argentina with their daughters; he moved into the studio (C9602). He rejected an invitation to the Sao Paulo Biennial because of the Brazilian military regime's repression and torture of political prisoners (C9602). In 1971 he took part in the meetings of Latin American artists in New York that produced the "Museo Latinoamericano" and the "Contra-Bienal" catalogue against the Sao Paulo Biennial under the dictatorship, alongside Arnold Belkin, Luis Camnitzer, Leonel Gongora, Leandro Katz, Luis Molinari Flores, Liliana Porter, Alejandro Puente, Rolando Pena, Carla Stellweg and Luis Wells; Gordon Matta-Clark later joined the meetings (C9602). Back in New York he saw Mondrian's centennial exhibition at the Guggenheim and realised that, from the 1920s on, Mondrian tended to leave the centre of his paintings open while the colour rectangles and black bars were pushed to the edges and often prolonged around the lip of the support; he wrote in his Notes that by leaving the frontal surface blank his approach implied taking Mondrian's structuring of space to its last consequences (C9602, C9609). In 1971 Jesus Rafael Soto visited his studio, brought by Luis Molinari Flores, and bought "Discurrimiento" (1970), a five-foot square painting, for the museum of modern art he was planning in Ciudad Bolivar, Venezuela (C9602, C9616). In 1971-72 the frontal surfaces went white again while the areas of colour on the sides were greatly simplified (C9602).

In 1972 he worked on three- and four-panel pieces, returning systematically to the multi-part idea first developed in 1966: each piece composed of vertical rectangles of different widths determined by a module and hung invariably 8 inches apart, white fronts, lateral views showing sequential series of contrasting bands of colour; all the decisions, width of the module, the 8-inch separation, the depth of the sides, were dictated by intuition, by visual judgments, "the rationalization comes afterward" (C9602). "The Hidden Order" (1972) is from this period; it entered MoMA's collection as part of the Patricia Phelps de Cisneros donation, acquired 2017 (MoMA provenance) / 2018 (own site) (C9601, C9608). He met Denise Rene at a MoMA cocktail party; she took small pieces on consignment, and her partner Hans Mayer bought a group of paintings outright for a Dusseldorf show in May 1972 (C9602). He travelled to Europe for the first time, following a "Piero della Francesca trail" suggested by his friend Miguel Ocampo, and on returning to New York learned he had been awarded a Guggenheim Fellowship for Painting (C9602, C9603). Seymour Knox bought the four-panel "Sequential" (1972) for the Albright-Knox Art Gallery, Buffalo (C9602). In January 1973 he opened at Galerie Denise Rene in New York, publishing excerpts from his "Notes, 1969-1972", edited by Kynaston McShyne; he signed a contract with the gallery and received a monthly stipend for a year (C9602). He found a loft at 135 Hudson Street, where he lived until 2004 (C9602). In 1975 the "modernist white" of the frontal surfaces gave way to sandy greys or pale terracottas, while some of the elements painted on the sides re-entered the frontal surface (C9602). He attended meetings of the Artists Meeting for Cultural Change (AMCC) at Lucy Lippard's and Rudolf Baranik's lofts, and worked as art editor of the magazine "Point of Contact / Punto de Contacto" (C9602).

In 1977 he travelled to Buenos Aires and pondered returning to Argentina, but the military junta's "dirty war" was in full swing, and he understood he was better off in New York (C9602, C9610). After a show at Galeria Artemultiple in Buenos Aires that July, at the urging of Dr. Kamala di Tella, who was doing anthropological work in the Andes, he set out on a long trip through northern Argentina, Bolivia and Peru, visiting Tiwanaku in Bolivia and Ollantaytambo and Machu Picchu in Peru (C9601, C9602). The encounter with the sculptural works of the Inca period, of an unexpected "abstract" persuasion, had a great influence on his work; the tectonics of the Temple of the Sun at Ollantaytambo became a decisive inspiration (C9601). Strengthened by his discovery of the symbolic sense colour acquired in ancient cultures, he restricted himself to a monochromatic range of earth and grey pigments, "the sandy grays of the landscape", consistently used a square canvas format "of mandala lineage", vertically halving it and adding marble powder to one of the areas, a practice that, though strictly pictorial, created a metaphorical reference to the spellbinding subtlety with which the massive monoliths of the Temple were adjoined (C9601). He began titling paintings with the Quechua name of the month in which they were finished, and with names of monuments or sites (C9602). One of these paintings, "Tastil II" (1978), named after an archaeological site in northern Argentina, was acquired by the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires (C9602, C9614). In 1979 he returned to Peru with funding from his friend Miguel Ocampo, carrying out an intensive photographic survey around Cuzco, Ollantaytambo, Machu Picchu and Nazca (C9602). He lectured on "Abstract Sculpture of the Incas" at NYU and Columbia in 1980, married Cecilia Vicuna in June 1981, and in September 1981 opened the retrospective "Paintings: 1969-1980" at the Center for Interamerican Relations (today Americas Society), with Lippard's essay "The Abstraction of Memory"; Betty Parsons wrote in the visitors' book "You are a great painter", though it got no reviews (C9602). In January-February 1982 he showed at the Fuji Television Gallery in Tokyo, lectured on Inca stone sculpture at the Bridgestone Museum, visited Kyoto and the Ryoan-ji Zen rock garden, where he had "the revelatory understanding of when emptiness, the sparseness, becomes fullness", met Isamu Noguchi, recently returned from Peru, and they discussed each other's photographs of the Inca works (C9602). In 1983 his research expanded into textiles: the Andean weaving traditions, "arguably the most sophisticated ever", influenced his painting, which began reflecting symbolic-structural forms such as the "hour glass" ("X"), "a sort of Andean ying-yang, that is repeated all-over the surface inscribed in a grid" (C9602). In 1984 Mary-Anne Martin showed the new work in New York with Sam Hunter's essay "Cesar Paternosto and the Return of the Enchantment", Grace Glueck reviewing it for the New York Times; he was invited to the first Havana Biennial and donated "Wari" (1983) to the Centro Wifredo Lam (C9602). In 1989 "Piedra abstracta, la escultura inca: una vision contemporanea" came out, illustrated with his own photographs and ink drawings, launched at the CAYC with a dialogue with Fermin Fevre; George Kubler called it "a book of deep learning" (C9601, C9602).

In 1990 he worked on the Portico and Facade series, stopped using oils and started preparing colours with dry pigments, returning to "an iconic monochromatism of the late seventies"; the new structured canvases have openings referring to the Andean or Greek "sun gates" or to the symbolic architecture of antiquity, like the Egyptian mastaba (C9602). In 1991 he spent a month as artist in residence at the Rockefeller Foundation's Bellagio Study Center with Cecilia Vicuna, working on wood models for sculptures; he was awarded a Gottlieb Foundation grant in March 1991, having received a Pollock-Krasner grant in December 1990 (C9601, C9602, C9603). In 1993 he made his first sculptures in pigmented cement, based on the Bellagio wood models, casting cement, a technique learned long ago, now more feasible than carving stone, incorporating dry pigments into the mix, and introducing openings in these "Impossible Architectures", as Vicuna called them; doors or windows, as in the Porticoes, are metaphors for the transition from sacred to profane space (C9602). His show "Abstraction as Meaning" opened at Exit Art in New York in June 1993, featuring almost the complete Portico and Facade series and the pigmented cement sculptures (C9602, C9603). In 1994 he returned to the square format with no openings, the Post-Portico series, spare arrangements of shapes within a monochromatic field relating to the profiles of the Portico structures (C9602). In 1995 he showed paintings, cement sculptures and works on paper at Cecilia de Torres Ltd. (C9602, C9603). In 1996 the English version of "Piedra abstracta" came out from the University of Texas Press as "The Stone and the Thread: Andean Roots of Abstract Art", translated by Esther Allen, 272 pages; the same year he returned to a technique last used in 1971, tracing lines with watercolour pencils on paper or canvas and going over them with a wet brush, which Vicuna named "Hilos de agua" (Water Threads), a return to the chromatism of the sixties (C9601, C9602). In 1997 he spaced the lines of the Hilos de agua rhythmically, "as if creating equivalents of 'notes' and 'silences'", the "score" developed within a conspicuously pencilled grid, "a residual effect of his long involvement with ancient textiles" (C9602). In 1998 he began the "Confluence" paintings, bringing the two paths together: rhythmically spaced lines within a grid set on monochromatic earth-red or ochre fields; he curated "North and South Connected: An Abstraction of the Americas" at Cecilia de Torres Ltd., featuring ancient Andean textiles alongside Torres-Garcia, the Alberses, Gottlieb, Nevelson, Jensen, Tawney, Fonseca, Matto, Ramirez Villamizar, Puente, Vicuna and himself, with a main essay arguing for an abstraction of the Americas born in open exchange with Amerindian sources in the 1930s and 40s; Holland Cotter in the New York Times called it "eye opening" (C9602). In 1999 he started the "Anabasis" series, "emphasizing the grid as the zero degree of abstraction" (C9602). In 2000 he co-founded MACLA (Museo de Arte Contemporaneo Latinoamericano) in La Plata, donating two works, "Portico #6" (1992) and "Post-Portico Buenos Aires II" (1994) (C9602, C9603). In 2001 he curated "Abstraction: The Amerindian Paradigm" at the Palais des Beaux-Arts, Brussels, which travelled to the IVAM in Valencia, and opened "White/Red" at Art in General, New York, eight pieces, four from the "white" period (1969-1971) and four from the "red" period (1980-2001); the show closed early because of the 11 September attacks (C9602, C9603). The same year the Museo de Arte Moderno in Buenos Aires reconstructed his 1971 Carmen Waugh show as "La vision oblicua: exposicion en Carmen Waugh, Buenos Aires 1971/2001": most of the 1971 works had been lost, so they were repainted from colour photographs (C9602, C9603). In 2002 he began the "Marginality and Displacements" series and, with Cecilia Vicuna, opened "Dis solving: Threads of Water and Light" at the Drawing Center, New York (C9601, C9602, C9603).

In February 2004 he took the decision to leave New York after 37 years and settle in Spain, choosing Segovia, "propitiated, to a great extent, by the manifest recognition his work is getting in that country" (C9602, C9604). He moved in May 2005 into a residence in Plaza San Esteban, Segovia, with his companion Inmaculada (Inma) Gonzalez Chao, conservator of the Esteban Vicente museum (C9602). In January 2006 he opened his first solo show in Madrid, "Marginalidades, desplazamientos y ritmos" at Galeria Guillermo de Osma, and in November 2007 was awarded the Premio Villa de Madrid, Pintura, Francisco de Goya, for that exhibition (C9602, C9603). In 2008 Norman Foster bought two major works at ARCO, the four-panel "Cuarteto: Naranja, Azul, Negro" and "Senal y Desplazamientos", both 2007 (C9602). In 2010 he opened "Pintura: La vision integral" at Guillermo de Osma, thirteen paintings and nine works on paper, and in December finished "La llegada" (The Arrival), a pictorial intervention in the arrivals hall of the AVE high-speed train at Atocha station, Madrid, commissioned by the architect Rafael Moneo with associate Pedro Elcuaz: the structure is 51.83 metres wide, 7 metres high and 1.28 metres deep, and it was precisely for that "depth", the lateral planes of the two rows of diamonds composing the structure, that he conceived the red, blue and black planes of colour, rhythmically arranged to reflect the transit patterns of arriving travellers (C9601, C9602, C9603, C9604). In 2012 he opened "Painting as Object: The Lateral Expansion" at Cecilia de Torres Ltd., New York, with a 10-panel piece conceived for the space (C9603, C9623). In 2017 he opened "Hacia una pintura objetual" (Towards Painting as Object) in the Balcony Gallery of the Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, Madrid, choosing works from the museum collection to dialogue with his own, a drawing by Juan Gris, a Cubist Picasso, three Mondrians, and a cut-out wood piece by Joaquin Torres-Garcia, with his own works from the lateral-vision period of the 1970s, multi-panel works from 1972 and 1974, and "Hilos de agua. Intervals (Grid 2)" (1997), lent by the Baroness Thyssen-Bornemisza (C9601, C9604). In 2019 the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes opened "Cesar Paternosto: la mirada excentrica" (The Eccentric Gaze), curated by its director Andres Duprat, about 38-40 works from the 1960s, 1970s and recent production, several never publicly exhibited, plus the site-specific "Deconstruccion pictorica" (Pictorial Deconstruction), a cubic gallery space articulated by 4-inch-thick partitions of differing heights, painted white on white, over which the colour notations of his painting are developed, bands and planes of yellow, cadmium red light and black, "a deconstruction of a painting which, by opening up in space invites to be walked around, 'lived in' for a moment" (C9601, C9605, C9614). In 2020 his book "La irrupcion del otro: la abstraccion en la modernidad tardia" came out from Ediciones La Bahia, and his interview with Hans Ulrich Obrist was published by Manuella Editions with an introduction by Lucy Lippard (C9601, C9603). In 2022 he conceived the first models of works on aluminium, atop structures of industrially painted white panels, coloured quadrangular forms that extend his pictorial language: "Strictly speaking, they are neither sculpture or painting because they are both at the same time" (C9601). In March 2026 MoMA opened "Hard Edges, Expanded Fields" (gallery 415), including "Duino" alongside Ellsworth Kelly, Kenneth Noland, Frank Stella, Anthony Caro, Anne Truitt, Fanny Sanin, Rasheed Araeen and Mohamed Melehi (C9601, C9624). In 2026 the Universidad Nacional de La Plata presented "La imaginacion geometrica" (The Geometric Imagination), his first retrospective in his birth city, curated by Natalia Giglietti at the Centro de Arte and the Museo de Ciencias Naturales, with works from his beginnings in 1962 through 2026; the university's School of Arts unanimously conferred an honorary doctorate on him (C9601, C9603). Also in 2026, Galeria RGR, Mexico City, presented "Piedra Abstracta" (Abstract Stone), curated by Gabriela Rangel, opening 3 September 2026, thirteen works made between 1969 and 2021, his first solo exhibition in Mexico since 1986 (C9603, C9615, C9616, C9622).

**Takeaway:** the biography is a single problem worked for sixty years, painting as an object. Law abandoned for painting, Tapies abandoned for geometry, the front of the canvas abandoned for its edges, Europe abandoned for the Andes as the origin of abstraction, New York abandoned for Segovia. Each abandonment is a move to the margin, and the margin is where the work happens.

## M1 -- "Geometria sensible": atonal bands, the painter's touch

Bands of highly saturated colour, waving and concentrically arranged, in "strange chords, such as a brown next to a pink", exploring the atonality of colour the way serial music explores atonality, with a lightly varied application of paint and subtle diversity of tone within each colour plane (C9602, C9606). The mechanism is the chord, not the shape: two colours that should not sit together are put together, and the band that carries them waves instead of running straight, so the geometry is felt rather than computed. Against the first Argentine concrete generation, whose uniform, detached look left no room for the painter's hand, Paternosto's geometry keeps the touch visible: the paint is applied with slight variation, the edges are soft, the bands are hand-drawn curves, not ruled lines (C9606). The title "Staccato" names the musical model directly, jazz and tango, detached notes, and the work is described as a pictorial symphony of sinuous, undulating colour ribbons (C9604, C9606). Seen in W01 "Staccato" (1965): a horizontal canvas, red across the top, orange across the bottom and filling a large disc at lower right, over which run thick waving concentric bands in a fixed repeating order -- pale sky blue, olive green, dark brown, bright blue, red -- looping around two centres, a tight loop cut off at the left edge and a large loop enclosing the orange disc at right, with a third band-arc rising from the bottom centre; the bands are flat, hard-edged, evenly wide, and the paint inside each band is slightly uneven in tone, hand-painted rather than sprayed (C9655). The study author viewed W01 directly: the two looping centres, the fixed band order, and the hand-painted unevenness inside the flat bands are exactly as leg 2 describes (C9751). The contrast with Silva (#637) is exact and is the doorway of this study: Silva counts identical dots on a ruled grid from a centre; Paternosto waves bands of clashing colour by hand and lets the painter's touch show. One geometry is arithmetic, the other is sensitive (C9621).

**Takeaway:** the band is the unit, the chord is the rule, the touch is the proof. A piece that draws straight hard-edged stripes in flat colour has Paternosto's outline and none of his mechanism; the bands must wave, the colours must clash like wrong notes, and the hand must be visible in the paint.

## M2 -- Shaped canvases and multi-panel wall installations

From 1966, paintings composed of several parts hung at predetermined distances, works that only acquire their entity once installed: the three shaped two-panel pieces of the Cordoba entry, "Duino" (1966, oil on canvas in two parts, 6 ft 6 3/4 in. high), "Maldoror" (1966, 180 x 170 cm), and the 1968 "Symmetrical/Asymmetrical" pieces, each at least seven units, some canvas, some wood, mounted on the wall in echoing arrangements (C9602, C9607, C9610). The 1972 system is stricter and is stated as a rule: vertical rectangles of different widths determined by a module, hung invariably 8 inches apart, white fronts, lateral views showing sequential series of contrasting bands of colour; the width of the module, the 8-inch separation and the depth of the sides were all "dictated by intuition, by visual judgments. The rationalization comes afterward" (C9602). Later the system loosens: 1974 "complex units" of three panels, two vertical and one horizontal, spaced 3 1/2 or 4 inches apart, the thickness of the stretchers; the 2012 show built a 10-panel piece conceived for the gallery space; the "Trio" and "Cuarteto" works of the 2000s are three- and four-panel pieces (C9602, C9603). Seen in W17 "Increasing-Decreasing" (2012): six tall white panels standing in a row on a gallery wall, increasing in width from the left and then decreasing again toward the right, each panel carrying thin vertical edge stripes in orange and red on its sides, visible as coloured lines between the white fronts, the fronts themselves blank white -- the increasing-decreasing rhythm of W10's eleven bars become the widths of six separate paintings hung as one work (C9656, C9664). The identification of W17 is probable, not proven (see Corrections 9). The watchable behaviour: the wall between the panels is part of the picture, and the intervals between panels, like the intervals between notes, carry the rhythm. The Art Miami text makes the same point about the later installations: the paintings, freed from frames and placed at intervals on the gallery wall, "behave as a single work of art to which architectural space and light are integrated as constitutive elements" (C9604).

**Takeaway:** the gap is a note. A piece that hangs one rectangle on a wall has shown a painting; a Paternosto piece hangs several rectangles at set intervals, and the wall between them does the work of the silence between notes.

## M3 -- The oblique (lateral) vision: painting on the edge

The central mechanism, and the one this study exists to record. From February 1969: the frontal surface of the painting is left blank, white or pale, and the pictorial notations are painted only on the deep side edges of the stretcher, 4 1/2 or 5 inches deep in the 1970 works (C9601, C9602). The canvas is emptied of imagery and asserts itself in space as an object; the viewer must move, walk from one side of the painting to the other, "circumambulate" it, to absorb its totality, reading it in a non-frontal, sideways and ambulatory manner (C9601, C9610). The front is not empty by accident: it is "an iconoclastic silence that implied the rejection of today's surrounding visual noise", and a silence "that runs against the visual noise of consumer society" (C9601, C9609). The colour on the edges is not decoration on a box; it is the whole picture, displaced to the margin, and its presence is felt frontally only as a faint halation on the surrounding wall, "the implicit presence of colors which cannot be explicitly seen, while corners and edges appear to gently disintegrate" (C9610). Seen in W04 "Tlon" (1969): a square canvas photographed at a slight angle so its left edge is visible; the front is blank white, entirely empty; the left edge is painted, a pale peach/salmon vertical band running the full height, broken midway by two small pale blocks, one cream and one pale yellow; that is the whole picture from the front, white plus a coloured edge glimpsed from the side (C9658, C9657, C9665). The study author viewed W04 directly in the paired Tlon-Uqbar reproduction: the blank front and the salmon edge band are exactly as leg 2 describes, and the companion canvas at right (Uqbar, W07) shows the same blank front with taupe blocks on both side edges at different heights (C9752). Seen again in W06 "Sagitario" (1972): a square canvas photographed at an angle against white, the front a blank white square, the left edge carrying two stacked vertical bars, red on the upper half, blue on the lower half, each edged with a thin magenta-pink line; nothing painted on the front at all -- the cleanest single demonstration of the oblique vision in the catalogue (C9652). The mechanism has a genealogy he stated himself: Mondrian, who from the 1920s left the centre of his paintings open and pushed colour rectangles and black bars to the edges, often prolonging them around the lip of the support; Paternosto's blank front takes Mondrian's structuring of space "to its last consequences" (C9602, C9609). It also has a rival he acknowledged: Jo Baer's wraparound paintings of the same months, where an elongated rectangle of dark colour grasps the side of the painting and about an inch of the front (C9602). And it has a limit he discovered himself: the 1970 works were too "boxy", their sides crowded with small squares and bands, "an exceedingly complex reading", and the pastel frontal tones unsatisfactory, so he destroyed most of them and simplified the sides to white fronts with a few bands (C9602). The 2001 MAMBA reconstruction had to repaint the 1971 Carmen Waugh works from colour photographs because most were lost (C9602).

**Takeaway:** the picture is on the side. A piece that paints a pattern on the front of a canvas and calls it Paternosto has missed the mechanism entirely; the front must be blank, the colour must live on the edges, and the viewer must have to move to see it. The blank is the picture, the edge is the painting.

## M4 -- Silence, white, and the rejection of visual noise

White is not a ground in Paternosto, it is a position. The blank frontal surface is an "iconoclastic silence" against "today's surrounding visual noise" (C9601); the 1978 essay "The Forms of Silence" (Martin-Crosa) names the Andean-period work by it; the 2012 "White/Red" show paired the white period (1969-1971) against the red period (1980-2001) as the two poles of the work (C9602). The silence is also a theory of contemplation: a silent, contemplative space in painting "is a way to counteract the overwhelming visual noise of today's mass media", and the mandala-like square format belongs to the same idea, a space for concentrated visualisation (C9602). The mechanism is audible in the titles: "Staccato", "Adagio", "Paradox", "Sequential", "Trio", "Cuarteto", "Acorde Minimo" (Minimal Chord), "Contrapunto", "Hilos de agua" (Water Threads) with notes and silences spaced rhythmically (C9602, C9604). The Art Miami text describes the later installations as unfolding "like a ribbon, across space into an evolving symphony where light, color, and rhythm propose a distilled enjoyment of the visual event integrated into the surrounding space" (C9604). The viewer is not asked to look, but to listen with the eyes: intervals, silences, chords, and the faint halation of unseen colour on the wall (C9610).

**Takeaway:** white is the loudest decision in the picture. A piece that fills the front has not added to Paternosto, it has removed the silence that is the work; the blank must be deliberate, and the colour must be heard from the side, not seen head-on.

## M5 -- The Andean turn: square, halved, marble powder, earth

From 1977-78, a complete change of palette and format, driven by the Andes rather than by New York. The square canvas, "of mandala lineage", vertically halved, one half mixed with marble powder to obtain a textural contrast, the partition a metaphor for the subtlety with which the Inca builders joined the gigantic blocks of the Temple of the Sun at Ollantaytambo (C9601, C9602). The palette is restricted to a monochromatic range of natural pigments, terracotta and sandy greys, "the sandy grays of the landscape"; colour is no longer atonal clash but symbolic, red above all, "a sacred color in many cultures, as well as a symbol of the connection with the earth" (C9602). The titles change register: Quechua names of the months in which the paintings were finished, names of monuments and sites, "Tastil II", "Pawqar", "Inti", "Trilce", "Yantra No. 3", "T'oqapu", "Saywite" (C9602). In 1981 short horizontal bands appear at the very centre of the square canvas, traversing the vertical partition at the middle, encoding references to the symbolic significance numbers had in Andean culture (C9602). From 1983 the weaving traditions of the Andes enter the painting: symbolic-structural forms such as the "hour glass" ("X"), "a sort of Andean ying-yang, that is repeated all-over the surface inscribed in a grid" (C9602). Seen in W08 "Pawqar" (1978): a square canvas in near-monochrome terracotta rust, matte and sandy in texture from the marble powder; the front divided by a single faint vertical seam just right of centre, where two slightly different rust tones meet; on the left edge, low down, a thin dark vertical slit; that is all, no bands, no blocks, no second colour on the front -- the colour is the stone, not a chord (C9652). Seen in W09 "Ayamarka 2" (1980): a pale grey-green square, mottled like old plaster, with a faint darker border frame just inside the edge; slightly below centre a small pale square outline, hand-drawn and uneven, in off-white with brownish flecks, with a short horizontal bar inside it; the mark is so faint it nearly disappears into the ground, and at a glance the sheet looks blank; the small square reads as a doorway, a window, or a ground-plan (C9652). Seen in W10 "Archetypal Geometry Series I" (1982): a sandy beige square, mottled, with a faint darker border; down the centre a vertical column of 11 short horizontal bars, evenly spaced from near the top to near the bottom, longest at the top and bottom and shortest at the middle, an hourglass rhythm; each bar is itself two-toned, dark mottled brown on its left half, pale cream on its right half, split at the centre line of the sheet (C9652). The thesis behind all of it, published in "Piedra abstracta" (1989) and "The Stone and the Thread" (1996), is that the roots of abstract art lie in the stone and textiles of the Andes, carved and woven centuries before modernism, and that geometric abstraction is a synthesis he names "the irruption of the other": abstract geometric forms evolved in arts foreign to the Western tradition and long antedated the modern version, which is "a late appearance in the global history of art" (C9601, C9603). The textile paradigm is central: the orthogonal geometric matrix inherent to the weaving structure, an art practice "virtually born in the mind and hands of women" (C9601). The MNBA text states the same doctrine as a demand: his geometry "demands a passage to the hidden order of things" (C9614).

**Takeaway:** the Andes are not a subject, they are the origin story. A piece that paints Andean-looking patterns on a rectangle has illustrated Paternosto; a piece that halves a square, textures one half, restricts itself to earth and red, and titles itself in Quechua has used his mechanism.

## M6 -- Porticoes and openings: the sun gate

From 1990, the structured canvas with a rectangular opening, the Portico series, executed through the 1990s, with the Facade series beside it: openings that refer to the "sun gates" from the Andes as well as the ones from ancient Greece, and to the symbolic architecture of antiquity like the Egyptian mastaba, "suggesting the passage from the sacred to the profane space" (C9601, C9602). The openings are also the mechanism by which the painting stops being a screen: a hole in the frontal plane is the most literal way to refuse the frontal image, and it descends directly from the oblique vision, the same refusal carried one step further, from the edge of the canvas to a cut through it (C9601). The 1993 pigmented cement sculptures, the "Impossible Architectures", carry the same openings as doors or windows, "metaphors for the transition from the sacred to the profane space" (C9602). The 1994 Post-Portico series returns to the square format with no openings, the spare arrangement of shapes within a monochromatic field still relating to the profiles of the Portico structures, so the opening survives as a shape even when it is no longer cut (C9602). From 2014 the "Paper Constructions" and the apertures on the pictorial plane continue the line: planes that project as flaps, "not only bestowed an ambiguous dimension to it", with the persistent colour notes anchoring these hybrid objects to the painting experience (C9601). The 2019 MNBA "Pictorial Deconstruction" is the Portico idea at architectural scale: a cubic gallery articulated by white-on-white partitions, walked through rather than looked at (C9601, C9605). Seen in W11 "Tambo Colorado II" (1991): a rust-brown canvas whose top edge is stepped, a central block rising above the two side blocks, like a temple silhouette or a stepped pyramid in profile; cut into it are three tall white vertical slots, real openings through the painted surface to the white wall behind, the centre slot the tallest, running most of the height, the two flanking slots shorter and lower; the brown paint mottled and matte, two slightly lighter brown rectangles framing the side slots (C9652). Seen in W12 "Cruz del Sur" (1992): a dark brown stepped canvas, darker and rougher than W11, its top block narrower and taller; three white vertical slots, one small slot high in the top block, and two tall slots below, left and right, separated by a thick dark pier; the arrangement is the Southern Cross as a constellation of openings, one star above, two below, plus the dark mass as the sky (C9652). The two works together show the Portico device is a system with variables -- number of openings, their heights, the step of the top -- not a single image.

**Takeaway:** the opening is a door, not a hole. A piece that cuts a rectangle out of a canvas for the look of it has the shape and none of the meaning; the opening must be a passage, sacred to profane, and the viewer must be able to pass it, or walk through what it implies.

## M7 -- Marginality and Displacements: the perimeter band

From early 2002, the synthesis of everything before it: black stripes on a white background, thin bands that stick to the perimeter of the canvas, occasionally breaking off and reappearing on the sides (C9601, C9602). The description on his own site is the clearest statement of the late mechanism: "another way to materialize his original ambition that the painting was read as a whole object", something that later presented as the "integral vision of painting"; when he used to empty the frontal surface, he forced the viewer to search for the depicted matter only on the side edges, but now "there is a (minimal) frontal image; yet the beholder that overcomes the conventional frontal stance will be gratified: what is painted on the sides amplifies the image" (C9601). The chronology adds the same point: "The bands follow the perimeter of the canvas, but occasionally break up and reappear on the side, thus synthesizing his long-standing aspiration of painting being read as a whole object. There is a frontal image, yet what is painted on the sides extends the image, completes it" (C9602). Red bands are soon added to the black-on-white scheme, and the resemblance to Mondrian or Malevich is "immediate, though superficial"; the triad black, white and red has a more significant connotation, the oldest and most universal set of colour categories, light and dark followed almost universally by a term for red, as John Gage points out in "Color and Meaning" (C9601, C9602). And the title carries a second reading, "charged with metaphorical allusions to the existential condition of Latin Americans in general, in other words, marginality within the West and displacement, the intellectual and artistic diaspora" (C9602). Seen in W16 "Contrapunto II (Desert Light)" (2003): an ochre-tan square, mottled, with only five short lines, all pushed to the edges -- a white vertical near the top left, a red horizontal at mid-left, a white horizontal along the bottom left edge, a red vertical near the bottom right, a dark brown vertical at the right edge; the centre is entirely empty; the title's counterpoint is literal, a white line answering a red line across the empty middle, top left against bottom right (C9652). Seen in W20 "Post-Deconstruct (New York)" (2016): an off-white square photographed at an angle so the right edge and the canvas depth are visible; on the front a yellow horizontal bar at mid-left, a small black square just right of centre, a tall white vertical bar (barely lighter than the ground) right of centre, and a tall black vertical bar near the right edge; a tiny red rectangle sits on the right edge itself, half on the front, half on the side; the white vertical bar is almost invisible against the ground, a deliberate near-disappearance, the "silence" the artist talks about, painted rather than left blank (C9652). The mechanism is the perimeter itself: the band hugs the edge, breaks, continues around the side, and the painting can only be read whole by a viewer who moves. It is the oblique vision domesticated into a frontal image that still refuses to stay frontal.

**Takeaway:** the edge is the image now. A piece that draws a black border on a white canvas has the look; a Paternosto piece lets the border break and continue around the side, in black, white and red, so the front is only half the picture and the margin is the subject.

## M8 -- Grid, line, water threads: the score

The grid is the zero degree of abstraction, and the line is its music. From 1996, the "Hilos de agua" (Water Threads): lines traced with watercolour pencils on paper or canvas, gone over with a wet brush, a return to the chromatism of the sixties (C9602). From 1997 the lines are spaced rhythmically, "as if creating equivalents of 'notes' and 'silences'", the "score" developed within a conspicuously pencilled grid, "a residual effect of his long involvement with ancient textiles" (C9602). From 1998, "Confluence" sets the rhythmically spaced lines within a grid on monochromatic earth-red or ochre fields, replacing the off-white backgrounds (C9602). From 1999, "Anabasis" continues "emphasizing the grid as the zero degree of abstraction" (C9602). The 2009 "Conjuntos/Progresiones" are folded-paper works, tempera on folded paper, and the "De-construcciones" are works on flat sheets (C9602). Seen in W13 "Hilos de Agua, Intervals (Vertical I)" (1997): a white square, nearly empty; hairline vertical threads run top to bottom in grey, ochre and brown, two thin lines in the upper half, and in the lower half a loose cluster of five or six threads toward the centre-right plus single threads at both side edges; the threads are uneven, hand-drawn, some stopping short of the edge; the interval -- the gap between threads -- is the subject, as in the Webern silences the Cecilia de Torres text cites for the early work (C9652). Seen in W14 "Confluence 9" (1998): a deep maroon-burgundy square, matte, with faint horizontal and vertical seams dividing it into a barely visible grid; across the middle band, seven short vertical dashes in red, magenta and dark blue, at irregular intervals and two slightly different heights, like notes on a stave or stitches; the dashes are thin, short, and sparse, most of the field empty maroon; the title's "9" is a series number, not a count visible in the picture (C9652). Seen in W15 "Mojacar No. 1" (2000): a deep red paper square, its edges rough and deckled; seven thin vertical dashes scattered across it, each a different colour and length, not aligned into rows or a grid, sitting at different heights like marks made at different moments (C9652). The grid here is the opposite of Silva's (#637): Silva's grid is a rule that generates dots by counting; Paternosto's grid is a loom, a textile matrix on which lines are spaced like notes, and the rhythm is the content (C9621). The same grid logic governs the installations: works hung at intervals, panels spaced by a module, the wall as the silence between notes (C9604). His own 2004 text is titled "La reticula y las formas geometricas arquetipicas" (The grid and archetypal geometric forms) (C9602).

**Takeaway:** the grid is a loom and a score, not a ruler. A piece that draws a grid and fills it has drawn a chart; a Paternosto piece spaces lines on the grid the way notes are spaced on a staff, with silences between them, and the spacing is the picture.

## Evolution: bands, edges, stone, gate, margin

Early (1961-1966, La Plata and Buenos Aires): Tapies' informel, Grupo Si, then archaic pictographic geometry from the La Plata museum's pre-Columbian ceramics, then Klee, then the "Magic Balloons" and the metallic-paper strips, then "geometria sensible" proper, waving bands of atonal, highly saturated colour, "Staccato" (1965) the emblem, shaped canvases and multi-part wall installations, "Duino" (1966) the prize work (C9601, C9602). The picture is all front, all colour, and the bands are the system: atonal chords, hand-painted, no straight edge.

Middle (1967-1977, New York): the multi-part pieces continue, then the February 1969 break, the blank front and the painted edges, "The Oblique Vision" (1970), the white fronts and simplified side bands, the module-and-8-inch multi-panel system of 1972, "The Hidden Order" and "Sequential", the Denise Rene years, the sandy greys and pale terracottas of 1975, the elements from the sides re-entering the front (C9601, C9602). The front is emptied to blank white and the painting moves to the side edges, first as thin bars (Sagitario), then as blocks at different heights on different sides (Uqbar). The viewer must walk.

Andean (1977-1989): the square halved vertically, marble powder, earth and grey pigments, Quechua titles, the hour-glass "X" in a grid, the "Portico" openings of 1990-93, pigmented cement sculpture, the book "Piedra abstracta" (1989) (C9601, C9602). Colour leaves except as stone; the mark is reduced to a seam, a faint square, a counted row of bars.

Late (1990-2026): Post-Portico squares, "Hilos de agua" and "Confluence" and "Anabasis" grids, the curatorial work ("North and South Connected" 1998, "Abstraction: The Amerindian Paradigm" 2001), "White/Red" (2001), "Marginality and Displacements" (2002- ), cor-ten steel "Tectonic Investigations", "La llegada" at Atocha (2010), "Painting as Object: The Lateral Expansion" (2012), the Thyssen show (2017), the MNBA "Eccentric Gaze" and its walk-through "Pictorial Deconstruction" (2019), the aluminium painted objects (2022- ), and the 2026 retrospectives in La Plata and Mexico City (C9601, C9602, C9603).

What does NOT change across sixty years: the picture is never only on the front. The shaped canvas of 1966, the painted edge of 1969, the halved square of 1978, the Portico opening of 1990, the perimeter band of 2002, the aluminium object of 2022 are all the same move, the centre evacuated and the meaning pushed to the margin, and the viewer made to move. The palette changes, saturated to white to earth to black-white-red, and the support changes, canvas to cement to steel to aluminium, but the displacement does not. 19 of 20 catalogued works are square or near-square (exception: the tall Orange, 1965).

## The network: the platense, the critic, the dealer, the museum

Alejandro Puente: the closest peer, the friend who invited him into Grupo Si, who shared the Lirolay show (1964), the Centro por la Libertad de la Cultura show (1965) and the Bonino show (1966), and with whom he shares the coinage of "geometria sensible"; Pellegrini named the two together as the spearheads of a "new abstraction" in 1964, and Greenberg singled out the two together the same year (C9602). In 1964, Galeria Lirolay, Buenos Aires: "Nueva geometria: Paternosto y Puente", catalogue prologue by Aldo Pellegrini, who named their work "geometria sensible" -- productions preserving the trace of the maker, the qualities of the material, and frayed contours, against the net forms and flat colours of the concrete artists (C9706). In 1966, Galeria Bonino, Buenos Aires: joint exhibition Paternosto-Puente, where "Naranja, magenta y azul" was first shown; Romero Brest's prologue said the two had perhaps "without consciousness" created a new genre of piece, "that seems a picture and is not a picture, that seems an object and is not an object", which he called "portable mural" (C9706). Puente is the co-author of the doctrine, not a follower, and the top pick for #639.

Carlos Silva (#637): the doorway contrast, and one evidenced shared room, "Bright Geometry: Abstract Geometric Paintings and Sculptures by Artists from Argentina and Uruguay, 1950-1980" at Cecilia de Torres Ltd., New York, featuring Maria Freire, Antonio Llorens, Cesar Paternosto, Rogelio Polesello, Carlos Silva, and Horacio Torres; no date for the show was established this run, and it is a gallery survey, not a 1960s room, and it is the only Paternosto-Silva shared roster found (C9710). Negative evidence, stated honestly: Paternosto was NOT in the 1973 Paris show "Projection et dynamisme" (six: Brizzi, Espinosa, MacEntyre, Ocampo, Vidal, Silva); the Di Tella prizes fall in different years (Silva 1965; Paternosto/Puente 1966); no shared Di Tella room exists, and none is claimed (C9712, C9713). Same Buenos Aires geometric generation, same Di Tella circuit, same Romero Brest, opposite doctrine: Silva counts identical dots outward from a centre on a ruled grid; Paternosto waves bands by hand and then evacuates the centre altogether, putting the picture on the edge. Silva's picture is in the middle of the field; Paternosto's is in the side of the stretcher (C9621).

Aldo Pellegrini: the critic who named the doctrine, writing the 1964 Lirolay catalogue preface that coined "geometria sensible", and who sat on the 1966 Cordoba jury that gave Paternosto first prize (C9602, C9603).

Jorge Romero Brest: the institutional anchor, director of the Di Tella's Centro de Artes Visuales, who invited Paternosto and Puente to Bonino in 1966 and wrote the introduction; the same Romero Brest who convened Silva's 1963 "Ocho artistas constructivos" and sat on Silva's 1965 Di Tella jury, so the two studies share their institutional anchor while their mechanisms oppose (C9602, C9621, C9718).

Alfred H. Barr Jr.: the judge who mattered most, president of the 1966 Cordoba jury, buyer of "Duino" for MoMA through the Inter-American Fund, and the man whose purchase carried Paternosto into the museum the next year (C9602, C9607).

Denise Rene and Hans Mayer: the dealers who carried the oblique vision into Europe, Dusseldorf 1972, New York 1973 and 1976, Paris 1974, and again Paris 2015, buying the unsold Paris show outright (C9602, C9603).

Lucy R. Lippard: the critic and ally, who wrote "The Abstraction of Memory" for the 1981 retrospective, whose "Overlay" (1983) appraised his work within an international movement oriented towards ancient symbolic meanings, who encouraged the Inca manuscript, and who wrote the introduction to the 2020 Obrist interview; the AMCC meetings at her loft are part of his New York political life (C9602).

Cecilia Vicuna: Chilean poet and artist, met in New York in May 1980, married June 1981, collaborator on "Dis solving" at the Drawing Center (2002) and on the Bellagio residencies, who named the "Hilos de agua" and the "Impossible Architectures"; the marriage and the collaboration run through the Andean decades (C9602).

## Aesthetic logic

Paternosto's aesthetic is the logic of the margin. Pick one place where a painting is not supposed to happen, the side of the stretcher, the edge of the canvas, the wall between two panels, the opening cut through the plane, the perimeter band, the lateral face of a station beam, and put the picture there. The front is left blank not because there is nothing to say but because the front is the place where painting has always been looked at, and therefore the place where it has stopped being seen. The geometry is sensitive because it is made by a hand that waves a band, spaces a line like a note, halves a square and textures one half with marble powder, and lets intuition decide the module and the gap before any rationalisation. The colour is atonal because the chords are strange, brown next to pink, and then it is symbolic because red is sacred and earth is the ground, and then it is black, white and red because those are the first colours any language names. The Andes are the proof that none of this is European invention: the stone was carved and the thread was woven centuries before Mondrian, and the textile grid is the original matrix of geometric art, made by women's hands. That is why the work keeps returning to the same move in new materials, canvas, cement, cor-ten steel, aluminium: the support changes, the displacement does not. A Paternosto that paints a pattern on the front of a canvas has not simplified him; it has put the picture back where he spent sixty years removing it from. The viewer must move, the front must be silent, and the edge must carry the picture.

The contrast with Silva is the study's spine, and it is a contrast inside one city circuit, between two men of the same generation who shared Romero Brest and the Di Tella rooms. Silva's generator is a counted grid of dots expanding from a centre; Paternosto's is a band pushed to the edge, then a blank front, then a perimeter. Silva's depth comes from a size rule along the rows; Paternosto's presence comes from a displacement, the picture moved to where the eye does not expect it. Silva painted on a table and removed gravity; Paternosto hung his paintings as objects and removed frontality. Both asked how a flat painting moves, and Silva answered by counting, Paternosto by moving the viewer. That is why the two studies belong together, and why Paternosto is the opposite, not the follower: same generation, same city, same rooms, opposite mechanism.
