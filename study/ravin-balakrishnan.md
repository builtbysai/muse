## 1. The brief: the second hand does the framing

Balakrishnan's whole programme can be stated in one sentence: one hand does the work, and a second channel -- the other hand, a tilt, a pressure, a hover, a foot puck, the camera's own tracking -- carries the framing, the scale, or the command, so the first hand never has to stop. The question behind every device and every paper is the same one, asked in a different room each time: what should the hands actually hold, and what should the second hand be allowed to do while the first hand works? The answer is never a menu. A menu is a stop: you put the pen down, you look away, you pick, you resume. Balakrishnan's systems are built so that never happens. The non-dominant hand sets the frame and the dominant hand works inside it; the pressure of the pen sets the zoom while the stroke sets the value; the tilt of the phone picks the letter while the key is pressed; the hover of the pen summons the command while the ink waits. The second channel is always cheaper than the first -- coarser, slower, quieter -- and that is the point: the expensive channel, the precise drawing hand, is never interrupted by the cheap one. Everything in this study follows from that one sentence, and every mechanism below is a different way of giving the second hand a job. [C35434]

**Takeaway:** The job is never interrupted: one hand works, the second channel frames, and the piece is the continuity between them.

## 2. Alias and Toronto: the lab that made the hands

The biography is short because the lab is the biography. He arrives at Toronto for the MSc in 1993, is advised by Bill Buxton, and from August 1996 to July 2001 is a researcher at Alias|wavefront -- the Toronto 3D-graphics company -- while doing the PhD part-time under Buxton. The Alias years are where the work happens: the Rockin'Mouse (1997), the PadMouse (1998), digital tape drawing and the bend-and-twist strip (1999), the bimanual camera work (1999), the volumetric-display paper (2001). The co-authors are the same room over and over: Gordon Kurtenbach and George Fitzmaurice at Alias, Buxton at Toronto, Ken Hinckley at Microsoft Research. Fitzmaurice's thesis acknowledgments name Balakrishnan among the friends before they are co-authors; Balakrishnan's PhD committee is Chignell, Fiume and MacKenzie, and his MSc thesis is "The Evolution and Evaluation of a 3D Input Device" -- the device question stated before the career starts. He joins the Toronto faculty in 2001, the year the PhD lands, is tenured in 2006, full professor in 2011, department chair 2015-2019 -- during which he hires 29 new tenure-stream professors in four years, and is, the award citation notes, one of the first HCI professors to chair a computer science department at a leading institution. He co-directs the Dynamic Graphics Project, the lab Buxton's generation built, and mentors more than 50 graduate students and postdocs; the award citation calls the mentorship "perhaps his greatest contribution to the HCI community." The visiting positions trace the same network outward: MERL 2005-2007, Paris-Sud and INRIA 2006, Microsoft Research's four labs on sabbatical 2007-2008, HP Labs Bangalore 2009. The through-line is unbroken from 1994 to 2023: the first paper is an empirical study of reaching into a fishtank VR display (with Colin Ware, Graphics Interface 1994), and the latest is a camera robot that watches an instructor and frames the shot itself (Stargazer, CHI 2023). Thirty years, one question: what do the hands hold, and what does the second channel carry. [C35435]

**Takeaway:** The lab is the biography: thirty years, one room, one question -- what do the hands hold.

## 3. The thesis: two hands, one job (M1)

The rule: the two hands are two motors in a serial linkage, and they have different jobs. Guiard's Kinematic Chain model, which Balakrishnan tests rather than assumes: the non-dominant hand sets the frame of reference relative to which the dominant hand works; the non-dominant hand moves coarsely and slowly, the dominant hand finely and fast; the non-dominant hand acts first, the dominant hand second. The test is in "The Role of Kinesthetic Reference Frames in Two-Handed Input Performance" (UIST 1999, with Ken Hinckley): does the model survive when the hands' kinesthetic reference frames are disjoint -- two separate mice, no shared surface -- and when visual feedback is removed? Finding: the reference principle is robust to frame mismatch as long as appropriate visual feedback is present, and with no visual feedback at all, body-relative kinesthetic cues alone still guide the two-handed manipulation. The hands know where they are relative to the body even when the eyes do not. The companion work is "Exploring Bimanual Camera Control and Object Manipulation in 3D Graphics Interfaces" (CHI 1999, with Kurtenbach): one hand drives the camera, the other manipulates the object, so the user never has to stop modelling to re-aim the view -- camera and object moving at the same time, no mode switching. "Symmetric Bimanual Interaction" (CHI 2000, with Hinckley) is the theory paper: both hands doing symmetric work. The PhD thesis (2001, "Issues in Bimanual Interaction for Computer Graphics") is the formal statement. The point for a generative practice is the role split, not the symmetry: two agents with visibly different jobs -- one big, slow hand that lays down a frame, rotates or holds a sheet, and one small, fast hand that draws inside it. The piece reads as a duet, not two equal pointers: the coarse hand leads, the fine hand follows, and the fine hand's motion is visibly relative to the coarse hand's frame. [C35436]

**Takeaway:** Two hands, two jobs: the coarse hand frames, the fine hand works, and the frame comes first.

## 4. The Rockin'Mouse: tilt is a dimension (M5)

The rule: a mouse with a rounded bottom can be rocked while it slides, so one hand controls four degrees of freedom at once with no mode switch. Sliding gives the usual two degrees of freedom; rocking gives two more, tilt about two axes. A regular mouse can only work two dimensions at a time in 3D and forces dimension-switching; the Rockin'Mouse controls all of them at once. The paper is "The Rockin'Mouse: Integral 3D Manipulation on a Plane" (CHI 1997, with Baudel, Kurtenbach and Fitzmaurice), and the experiment is the claim: 30% faster than a standard mouse on a typical 3D task. The design rationale is stated explicitly and is worth quoting in spirit: keep everything that makes the mouse successful -- any grip, a flat surface, no special posture -- and add tilt. It is the earliest statement of the programme's method: do not invent a new device, add one cheap channel to the device everyone already holds. The patent is US #6115028 (2000), "Three-dimensional input system using tilt". The watchable behaviour is a single object that slides AND rocks: sliding moves it across the plane, rocking tips and rolls it in depth -- two motions fused into one gesture, never sequenced. [C35437]

**Takeaway:** Add one channel to the device everyone already holds: tilt is a dimension, not a new machine.

## 5. The PadMouse: the other hand gets a voice (M2)

The rule: the non-dominant hand's device should not be a second mouse, it should be a positioner that is also a command surface. The PadMouse (CHI 1998, with Pranay Patel) is a regular mouse base with a 2-DOF touchpad mounted on top of it, designed for the non-dominant hand. The hand still slides the mouse for positioning, while the index and middle finger on the touchpad fire commands via "Marking Keys" -- directional flick gestures on the pad -- up to 32 modifiers and commands, quickly and accurately, in the experiment. Before it, the non-dominant hand in two-handed interfaces was limited to coarse positioning; the PadMouse lets it also select tools, modes and modifiers. The mechanism is the Guiard frame made into hardware: the left hand slides and flicks to switch tools and modes, the right hand draws and positions precisely with a stylus, and the drawing hand never stops to pick a tool. The watchable behaviour is one side of the canvas as a "command hand": a slow-moving pad whose taps and flicks switch the other hand's tool, colour or mode, the change visible as a ripple from the pad, never as a menu. [C35438]

**Takeaway:** Give the other hand a voice: a positioner that is also a command surface, so the drawing hand never stops.

## 6. Tape drawing: the line is a physical object (M3)

The rule: a strip of tape, laid down by two hands under tension, is a better drawing instrument than a pencil at full scale -- and the digital version should keep the hands, not just the line. Tape drawing is the automotive-design craft of sketching full-size car profiles on upright walls using strips of black photographic tape instead of pencil, and "Digital Tape Drawing" (UIST 1999, with Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach and Buxton) is the flagship work of the whole corpus. The paper was built from observation and videotape of tape artists at Renault in France and the General Motors Design Center in Detroit, and it analyses why the craft works: the tape's slight elasticity and its physical continuity make perfectly straight lines and smooth continuous curves easy at a scale where freehand drawing fails, while staying freehand -- no ruler, no French curve. The digital system recreates it on an 8 x 6 ft back-projected screen with a 6-DOF tracker in each hand, and the mechanism is the tension between the two hands: the right hand holds the roll of tape, the left hand slides along the tape pressing it down. Hold the right hand still while the left fastens and you get a perfectly straight line; move the right hand in an arc while the left fastens and you get a smooth curve; the unfastened span between the hands is a live preview of the line. Undo is peeling the tape off; editing is re-laying or tearing off strips. The digital version preserves all of this and adds what tape cannot do: saving, retrieval, and combining front and side views into 3D curves -- the sequel, "Creating Principal 3D Curves with Digital Tape Drawing" (CHI 2002, with Grossman, Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Khan and Buxton), fuses tape curves drawn on two flat views into a single non-planar 3D curve, the principal curve that defines a vehicle's form. The paper notes the implication for bimanual theory outright: a naturally two-handed technique whose skill is on a par with any other artistic medium. The patent is US #6642927 (2003). The watchable behaviour is a black strip unspooling across the canvas, laid down by two cooperating agents -- one feeds, one presses. The line is visibly a physical object being placed, not a stroke being painted: it can be lifted, peeled back, re-laid, cut. Curves sag and straighten with the tape's elasticity, and a car-silhouette-like profile emerges from successive strips. [C35439]

**Takeaway:** The line is a thing, not a mark: tension between two hands draws it, and peeling is undo.

## 7. ShapeTape: bend the strip, bend the curve (M4)

The rule: a physical strip whose deformation IS the input -- bend it and the curve bends, twist it and the curve twists. ShapeTape ("Exploring Interactive Curve and Surface Manipulation Using a Bend and Twist Sensitive Input Strip", I3DG 1999, with Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach and Singh) is a 48 x 1 x 0.1 cm rubber tape with a spring-steel core and fibre-optic sensors that senses its own bend and twist continuously along its length. Held in both hands -- at times all ten fingers -- bending and twisting it directly bends and twists a virtual curve in Maya. A 6-DOF tracker on the tape's start point positions and orients the curve in 3D space, and a foot-operated 2-DOF puck gives camera control -- because both hands are busy holding the tape, secondary functions were deliberately moved off the hands; the paper says relying on mouse and keyboard would force the user to release the tape. That foot puck is the second channel in its purest form: the hands are fully occupied, so the framing goes to the feet. The CHI 2003 form of the work (Grossman, Balakrishnan and Singh, "An interface for creating and manipulating curves using a high degree-of-freedom input device") carried it into the curve-creation literature, and the patent is US #7289121 (2008). The watchable behaviour is a single flexible ribbon on screen that bends, twists, coils and springs back as if held by invisible hands; its curvature IS the drawing. Two ends pulled apart straighten it; twisting one end corkscrews the whole line. The camera orbits on its own -- the foot-puck role -- so the viewer watches the ribbon from a slowly drifting viewpoint. [C35440]

**Takeaway:** The prop is the input: bend the strip and the curve bends -- there is nothing to translate.

## 8. The volume you can walk around (M6)

The rule: a volumetric display shows imagery in true 3D space inside an enclosure, so the image is a volume you walk around and reach into, not a picture you look at. "User Interfaces for Volumetric Displays" (IEEE Computer, March 2001, with Fitzmaurice and Kurtenbach, a cover feature) is the first serious treatment of interaction design for true volumetric displays -- displays that illuminate actual points in 3D space, swept-volume (the dome-shaped "Felix" display) and static-volume, so a 3D image floats in a volume you can walk around, with all depth cues, no glasses, no head tracking. The paper uses physical mockups to explore the new problems: how do you point at something inside a volume? How do widgets work when they can be read from any side? How do you rotate the display enclosure to see the back? The programme continues in "Multi-Finger Gestural Interaction with 3D Volumetric Displays" (UIST 2004, Grossman, Wigdor and Balakrishnan, Best Paper): the user reaches into the volume with several fingers and manipulates the floating 3D image directly. Selection is its own problem, solved in the UIST 2006 work (Grossman and Balakrishnan): a ray cursor beats a 3D point cursor for single targets, and in dense scenes four new ray techniques disambiguate multiple intersected targets -- the "depth ray" performed best, cutting movement time, error rate and input footprint. "Collaborative Interaction with Volumetric Displays" (CHI 2008, Grossman and Balakrishnan) extends it to two people around one volume, and CHI 2007 work measured orientation and text-readability effects in the volume. The watchable behaviour is objects floating in a glass dome that the viewer can walk around -- in a piece, the viewpoint orbits on its own or on drag -- while fingers, or proxy hands, reach in from different sides and pluck, rotate and stack objects in true depth. Selection is a ray from a hand, not a point: the ray visibly pierces several objects and the depth ray resolves the ambiguity by sliding focus along the ray. Two hands from opposite sides can work the same volume at once. [C35441]

**Takeaway:** The image is a volume: walk around it, reach into it, and select with a ray, not a point.

## 9. The camera as a window, the camera on rails (M7)

The rule: the viewpoint is either a physical window you push through space, or a rail the camera is authored to travel on -- and the two poles belong together. The first pole is Boom Chameleon (UIST 2002): a flat-panel display on a tracked mechanical boom, a physical window into a 3D world with a 1:1 mapping between real and virtual space -- move the display and the viewpoint moves, the virtual model staying fixed in physical space while the user walks around it. Zoom, pan and tumble are replaced by physical manipulation of the display, and touchscreen, microphone and speaker capture viewpoint, voice and gesture annotations simultaneously and continuously, with no mode switching between annotation types. **Boom Chameleon is not a Balakrishnan paper** -- the authors are Tsang, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach, Khan and Buxton -- and this study discusses it only as lab-adjacent work in the same Alias/DGP programme, built on Fitzmaurice's "window in hand" metaphor and on Balakrishnan's 2001 volumetric-displays paper. The second pole is his: StyleCam (UIST 2002, Burtnyk lead, with Khan, Fitzmaurice, Balakrishnan and Kurtenbach), the opposite pole -- authorial control over the view. Camera surfaces constrain where the viewpoint can travel; animation clips give authored transitions (camera moves, slates, fades, wipes, edits); one unified interaction lets the user move seamlessly and continuously between spatial control (moving on a camera surface) and temporal control (scrubbing an animated transition). The viewer's focus stays on content, never on widgets, and the experience approaches the pacing of a TV commercial or film while staying interactive. The demo was an interactive car advertisement. The watchable behaviour has two faces: a framed window that drifts through space while the world inside it shifts in exact counterpoint -- walk the frame left, see the object's left side; push it closer, zoom in -- and a camera that glides along invisible rails and, at set points, dissolves into a scripted sweep, which the viewer can linger on or scrub, but never falls off the rails into free-form tumbling. [C35442]

**Takeaway:** The camera is either a window you push or a rail you ride: free movement and authored pacing are two poles of the same question.

## 10. The sketchbook that turns its own page (M8)

The rule: a 3D sketching system should keep the pen-and-paper feel and infer what you mean, so there are no menus and no mode switches anywhere. ILoveSketch (UIST 2008, Bae, Balakrishnan and Singh) is a 3D curve-sketching system for professional product designers with three novel contributions: automatic view rotation to improve curve sketchability -- the view rotates itself so the curve you are about to draw is always drawn on a well-oriented plane; an axis widget for sketch-surface selection; and implicitly inferred changes between sketching techniques -- the system infers which technique you mean from the stroke, instead of making you switch modes. Around that: a virtual sketchbook (tear, peel, pan, zoom, rotate pages), multi-stroke NURBS curve creation, and oversketching to edit finished curves. It was evaluated by a professional designer, and 100 models were created in 4 days at a SIGGRAPH 2009 eTech demo. EverybodyLovesSketch (UIST 2009, same authors) reworks it for novices: tick-based sketch-plane selection, single-view definition of extrusion vectors, a perspective grid, surfacing for projective sketching -- tested with 49 high-school students over 11 days. The watchable behaviour is a sketchbook whose page quietly turns itself to the best angle before each stroke lands; strokes that the system reads as intent -- a scribble over an old curve edits it rather than adding a new one; a 3D wireframe that grows stroke by stroke, each new curve landing on the plane the last one implied. No menus, no mode buttons: the mode is inferred and the inference is visible -- the page turning, the axis widget swinging into place. [C35443]

**Takeaway:** Infer, do not ask: the page turns itself, the mode is read from the stroke, and no menu ever appears.

## 11. The cursor that breathes (M9)

The rule: the cursor's activation area should always contain exactly one target -- the nearest one -- so you never have to steer onto a tiny target, only get closer to it than to any other. The Bubble Cursor (CHI 2005, Grossman and Balakrishnan, Best Paper) is an area cursor whose activation area dynamically resizes with the proximity of surrounding targets: radius equals distance to the nearest target. Formally it expands each target to its Voronoi region -- the set of points closer to that target than to any other. Two controlled experiments showed it significantly outperforms the point cursor and object pointing, and its performance is accurately modelled by Fitts' law. The visual is a bubble that swells in empty space and shrinks as it nears a target, morphing to encompass the target it has claimed. The watchable behaviour is the purest in the whole corpus: a soft circle drifting through a field of dots -- it inflates in the gaps, deflates as it closes on a dot, and visibly "claims" the nearest dot by wrapping it. Selection is by proximity, not by precise pointing -- the piece is about the bubble's breathing, the targets lighting as they are claimed, one at a time, never two. [C35444]

**Takeaway:** Proximity is selection: the bubble breathes, claims exactly one target, and aiming becomes unnecessary.

## 12. Direction is the command (M10)

The rule: a flick in a direction IS the command, and the menu never needs to appear. Marking menus are Kurtenbach's invention; the Balakrishnan contribution is the 2004 refinement (Zhao and Balakrishnan, UIST 2004): the traditional hierarchical version uses one "zig-zag" compound mark through submenu levels, and its space requirement grows quadratically with depth. The replacement is a series of inflection-free simple marks, one per level, all overlapping in the same small space: faster and more accurate overall, dramatically so in large menus, and needing about one-sixteenth the space for a 4-level menu -- suited to small pen devices. The expert works eyes-free from muscle memory; the novice sees the radial menu, the expert flicks straight through it. Two relatives complete the family. Pressure Marks (Ramos and Balakrishnan, CHI 2007) use pen pressure as the mark's channel. Hover Widgets (Grossman, Hinckley, Baudisch, Agrawala and Balakrishnan, CHI 2006) are short gestures made while the pen HOVERS above the surface, in tracking state, then pen-down to activate -- a backwards-'L' summons a marking menu -- creating a command layer clearly distinct from the ink layer, and faster with fewer divided-attention errors than toolbar activation. The watchable behaviour is radial menus that never appear: a flick up, a flick left -- each flick is a command, and the command's effect blooms from the flick's endpoint. In expert mode the flicks chain into quick staccato sequences, each overlapping the last in the same small patch of canvas. A hovering pen leaves a faint ghost trail; drop the pen and the ghost becomes the command. [C35445]

**Takeaway:** Direction is the command: flick, and the menu never appears; the expert works eyes-free.

## 13. Pressure is zoom (M11)

The rule: pen pressure controls the SCALE of the parameter space while sliding controls the value within it -- press lightly and values fly past in big steps, press hard and the space magnifies so the same slide nudges by ones. Zliding (UIST 2005, Ramos and Balakrishnan) is "zoom sliding": pressure is the zoom channel, x-y sliding is the value channel, coupled in one gesture. The Zlider widget instantiates it, and experiments marginally favoured a pressure-sensing stylus tip over bimanual split-control versions -- the second channel works best when it rides on the same hand as the first. The same family: Pressure Widgets (Ramos, Boulos and Balakrishnan, CHI 2004) and pen rolling (Bi, Moscovich, Ramos, Balakrishnan and Hinckley, UIST 2008) -- rolling the pen barrel between the fingers as an extra input channel during a stroke. The watchable behaviour is a slider or knob whose sensitivity visibly breathes with pressure, and a pen stroke that thickens and slows as pressure rises, the line itself recording the zoom level. [C35446]

**Takeaway:** Pressure is the scale: press lightly to fly, press hard to nudge -- one gesture, two channels.

## 14. Tilt and the vacuum: the target comes to you (M12)

The rule: when the target is out of reach, move the world or pull the target near -- do not make the hand travel. Two compact mechanisms, grouped because both solve the same problem. TiltText (UIST 2003, Wigdor and Balakrishnan): a 12-key phone keypad maps 26 letters onto keys 2-9 ambiguously; tilting the phone in one of four directions (left, forward, right, back) while pressing the key picks which letter on that key. 23% faster than MultiTap by the end of a 5-hour, 640-phrase experiment; language-independent, usable without looking. The Vacuum (CHI 2005, Bezerianos and Balakrishnan): on a large display, distant objects are hard to reach without walking. The vacuum is a circular widget with a user-controllable arc of influence from its centre to the display edges; proxies of all objects inside the arc are pulled close to the centre, within arm's reach, and manipulated in lieu of the originals. A within-arm's-reach buffer zone exempts nearby objects. Best when selecting multiple targets in sequence. Related reach mechanisms: the Satellite Cursor (CHI 2010) -- multiple cursors achieving MAGIC pointing without gaze tracking -- and HybridPointing (UIST 2006), fluid switching between absolute and relative pointing. The watchable behaviour has two faces: a field that leans -- tilt the whole scene one of four ways and the ambiguous choice resolves, four lean directions, four outcomes, the lean visible as the whole canvas tipping -- and a wedge or arc that sweeps out from a point while distant objects slide along it toward the centre as ghost proxies, are nudged, and snap back to their true places. [C35447]

**Takeaway:** Bring the target to the hand: lean to disambiguate, pull the distant near, and never make the hand travel.

## 15. BumpTop: the desk that obeys physics

The escape from the lab is BumpTop -- "Keepin' It Real: Pushing the Desktop Metaphor with Physics, Piles and the Pen" (CHI 2006, Agarawala and Balakrishnan). Files are 3D boxes on a virtual desk with real physics, on the NovodeX engine: icons have mass and friction, can be tossed, slid like hockey pucks, bounced, stacked into piles instead of filed in folders; piles can be fanned, flipped through like a book, sorted, or have corners pulled out to mark important files; icons can be pinned to the walls like paper on a corkboard, creased and folded like paper, and a window turned on end can be used as a shovel to push icons into a corner. It was built for pen input on tablet PCs, it began as Agarawala's master's thesis, and a demo video posted for DemoCamp in 2006 caused a minor online sensation and crashed the University of Toronto server. The company, Bump Technologies Inc. (bumptop.com), was co-founded on it and acquired by Google in May 2010. **The name collision must be kept straight:** there is a different company also called Bump Technologies -- the maker of the Bump phone-to-phone contact-sharing app, founded in the US, acquired by Google in 2013. ## 16. The occlusion problem: the hand hides the work

The rule: with direct pen input, the hand itself is the obstacle -- so the interface must know what the hand is hiding and move the content out from under it. "Occlusion-Aware Interfaces" (CHI 2010, Vogel and Balakrishnan, Best Paper) starts from a measurement: with direct pen input, the hand and forearm can occlude up to 47% of a 12-inch display, and status messages and previews hide under the hand. The companion CHI 2009 paper ("Hand Occlusion with Tablet-Sized Direct Pen Input", Vogel and colleagues) confirms the 47% figure. The system fits a five-parameter geometric model of the occlusion silhouette per user, knows what area is currently hidden, and counteracts it: the Occlusion-Aware Viewer lifts important content out from under the hand and shows it in a bubble-like callout in clear space. It cut task time by up to 23% in a monitoring task. The mechanism is the second channel turned inward: the interface watches the hand the way the hand watches the work, and the hidden thing is shown beside the hand rather than beneath it. It is the same move as the Bubble Cursor -- the interface does the aiming so the hand does not have to -- applied to the hand's own shadow. [C35449]

**Takeaway:** The hand hides the work, so the interface must watch the hand: lift what is hidden into clear space.

## 17. The late turn: the camera watches back

The output slows after 2010 -- 9 papers in 2011, 5 in 2012, one to four a year after 2015 -- and the venue mix shifts: ICT-for-development work (camera phones and pico projectors in rural India, MobileHCI 2011, with Mathur, Ramachandran and Cutrell), visualization (KronoMiner 2011, TrailMap 2013), then from about 2016 a turn to embodied remote presence. StarHopper (Graphics Interface 2020, Li, Balakrishnan and Grossman) is a touch interface for remote object-centric drone navigation: tap an object in the drone's video and the drone flies to look at it. ASTEROIDS (CHI 2022, Li, Sousa, Li, Liu, Chen, Balakrishnan and Grossman) is a swarm of small tabletop robots, each carrying a camera and screen, acting as the physical embodiment of a remote instructor demonstrating a hands-on skill at a workbench -- the audience controls the swarm, changes camera positions, and switches between robots' viewpoints, so the remote teacher becomes several small moving bodies on your desk instead of one flat video. Stargazer (CHI 2023, Li, Sousa, Mahadevan, Wang, Aoyagui, Yu, Yang, Balakrishnan, Tang and Grossman -- DGP paper #148, the latest refereed paper on the DGP list) is a 7-DOF robot-arm camera that films how-to videos by autonomously tracking regions of interest and reading subtle instructor cues -- pointing gestures, speech -- to reframe; three shot types (action, object, instructor) identified from 50 how-to videos. Route Tapestries (UIST 2021, Li and colleagues) navigates 360-degree virtual-tour videos with slit-scan visualizations -- a whole route compressed into one tapestry strip. The arc closes: from the user moving the camera (Boom Chameleon, 2002) to the camera moving itself by watching the user (Stargazer, 2023). The through-line survives: the interface is still a physical thing -- a robot, a drone, a camera -- moving in space, and the user's body is still the controller; but the "hand" is now remote, and the "display" is a robot on a workbench. [C35450]

**Takeaway:** The camera learned to watch back: from pushing the window to a robot that frames the shot itself.

## 18. Aesthetic logic: the second channel

Put the mechanisms together and the aesthetic is one sentence: the primary gesture is never interrupted. Every choice serves that sentence. The two-handed split makes the coarse hand carry the frame so the fine hand never stops to aim. The tape makes the line a physical object under tension, so drawing is placing, not painting. The strip makes the bend the input, so there is nothing to translate. The volumetric display makes the image a volume, so selection is a ray through space rather than a point on a plane. The camera on rails makes pacing the authored thing, so the viewer never falls off into free-form tumbling. The sketchbook turns its own page, so the stroke is never broken by a mode switch. The bubble breathes so the target comes to the cursor, and the vacuum pulls the distant near, so the hand never travels. The flick is the command, so the menu never appears. Pressure is the zoom, so the slider never needs a second hand. The occlusion viewer lifts the hidden content out from under the hand, so the hand never has to move to see. And the late camera watches the instructor, so the shot frames itself. The work is not about devices; it is about continuity. A menu is a stop, a mode switch is a stop, a walk across the room is a stop, and Balakrishnan spent thirty years removing stops one cheap channel at a time -- the other hand, the tilt, the pressure, the hover, the foot, the camera's own eye. For a generative practice the warning is direct: a piece that makes the viewer stop to pick, to switch, to aim precisely, has broken the one rule this whole programme obeys. [C35451]

**Takeaway:** Continuity is the aesthetic: a menu is a stop, a mode switch is a stop, and thirty years went into removing stops.
