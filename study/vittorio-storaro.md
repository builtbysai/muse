## 1. The projectionist's son from Rome

He was born in Rome on Monday 24 June 1940, the son of a film projectionist, and Film Reference names his parents Renato Storaro and Teodolinda Laparelli, a grade B detail not stated in the main Wikipedia article. [C32249] He began studying photography at age 11 and went on to formal cinematography studies at the Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia at age 18, having first enrolled at C.I.A.C., the Italian Cinematographic Training Centre, and he was employed as an assistant cameraman at age 20 and promoted to camera operator within a year, according to IMDb-derived biography, grade B. [C32250] His short-film work listed in the filmographies runs from Etruscologia in 1961 through L'urlo in 1965-1966, Sortilegio, Il labirinto and Sirtaki in 1966, to Rapporto segreto in 1967, six shorts in six years before his first feature. [C32251] Wikipedia's filmography lists Youth March (Giovinezza, giovinezza), directed by Franco Rossi, in 1969 as his earliest feature entry in that table, though the IMDb-derived biography dates the same credit to 1968, and the study uses 1969 with the flag carried. [C32252] He was on the crew when Bernardo Bertolucci directed Before the Revolution, with British Cinematographer dating that crew work to 1963 and the film itself dated 1964 in other listings, and he told the ASC roundtable that from the first time he met Bernardo in the early 1960s, Bertolucci made clear that camera positioning and movement were part of the director's grammar. [C32253] He later said of his schooling, in the Film Comment interview, "My God, I only have one leg," describing his discovery that nine years of film school had taught him technology but nothing about art. [C32254] Francis Ford Coppola once noted, as quoted in the Wikipedia article, "Vittorio is the only man I ever knew that could fall off a ladder in a white suit, into the mud, and not get dirty." [C32255] The arc of the career runs from those 1961 shorts to Coup de chance in 2023, more than sixty years behind the camera, with three Academy Awards for Best Cinematography, a Primetime Emmy, a BAFTA, a Cannes Technical Grand Prize and a lifetime of honours in between -- and the through-line is not the prizes but the method, worked out in the 1970s with Bertolucci and never abandoned. [C32256]

**Takeaway:** He starts in the projection booth, the son of the man who throws the light onto the screen, and spends nine years in film school learning the machinery before discovering the machinery is only one leg. Everything after that is the search for the other leg: a theory of what light and colour mean, not just how they are made. The biography is the method in miniature -- the technician who goes looking for the artist, and finds him in Goethe.

## 2. Goethe's theory of colours as a working method

His philosophy is largely inspired by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's theory of colours, which focuses in part on the psychological effects that different colours have and the way colours influence our perceptions of situations. [C32257] The books that carry the method are the Writing with Light series: Writing with Light, Volume 1: The Light was published by Aperture in 2002 as the first of his philosophy books, and the trilogy's volumes are In Journey with Light, on the emotional quality of lighting, Chromatic Emotion, on the power and meaning of colour, and The Equilibrium of Elements, on the natural elements of life. [C32258] A fourth volume, The Muses, is listed as continuing the Writing with Light series, grade B from a speaker-biography listing only, and a fifth book, Storaro -- Writing with Light, Colours, Elements, was reported by Film and Digital Times in October 2011 with more than 400 images, while the documentary Writing with Light: Vittorio Storaro, directed by David M. Thompson, dates to 1992, and Writer of Light: The Cinematography of Vittorio Storaro, compiled and edited by Ray Zone from American Cinematographer, was first published by ASC Press in 2000. [C32259] "All great films are a resolution of a conflict between darkness and light," Storaro says, as quoted in the ICG profile "Maestro of Light" and on IMDb. [C32260] "We are writing stories with light and darkness, motion and colors," he says in the same profile, and the sentence is the whole programme: light and darkness are the nouns, motion and colour the verbs, and the story is what happens when they are put in conflict. [C32261] Colour and light act on the body, not only the eyes, since strong light raises metabolism and blood pressure while penumbra lowers blood pressure, as he told the AnOther Cafe Society interview of 23 December 2016. [C32262] "Every single color relies on a very specific wavelength of energy," he says in the Film and Digital Times Wonder Wheel interview of 2 December 2017, which is why the choice of a colour is, for him, a physical decision before it is an aesthetic one: each wavelength does something different to the person watching. [C32263]

**Takeaway:** Goethe is not a reference for Storaro, he is the operating system. A colour is not chosen because it looks right; it is chosen because a specific wavelength of energy does a specific thing to a human body, and the film is a sequence of those bodily effects arranged until two opposites resolve. That is what makes the method portable across sixty years, five directors and two technologies: the physics does not change when the camera does.

## 3. The spectrum as a life

Storaro maps the colour spectrum onto a human life: black is the unconscious, red is blood and birth, orange is family warmth, yellow is consciousness, green is knowledge, blue is intelligence and freedom, indigo is material power, and violet is the last stage of life. [C32264] White is the sum of all the colours and means balance and equilibrium, the unity of the life stages, so a film that reaches white has resolved the conflicts its colours were carrying. [C32265] His core principle is equilibrium between opposite elements, so that a film resolves the conflict between darkness and light, cold and warmth, and blue and orange, and the resolution is the story, not the decoration on it. [C32266] In The Last Emperor he charted Pu Yi's life across the spectrum, from the yellow of childhood innocence to the violet of life's twilight, so the emperor's biography is legible as a slow walk down the spectrum. [C32267] In Tango he ran the spectrum backwards, from violet for aged dancers through indigo, blue, green and yellow to orange for repression and red for violent death, so the dance film tells its story by walking the spectrum in reverse, from the end of life back to its blood. [C32268] In Wonder Wheel he gave the two rival women opposing physiological colours, orange for Ginny, who leaves work at sunset, against azure for Caroline, who goes to school in the evening, so the rivalry is visible as two temperatures that cannot occupy the same hour. [C32269] In Dick Tracy he locked the film to seven colours only -- cyan, magenta and yellow, red, green and blue, plus black as anchor -- and assigned colours by character, with Tracy in yellow as light, day and sun, Tess in orange, the Kid in red, and Big Boy, Breathless and Pruneface in blue, indigo and violet. [C32270] He described the Tracy-Breathless story as an impossible communion between the sun and moon, day and night, good and evil, which is the equilibrium principle stated as a love story: two opposite temperatures that can never resolve. [C32271] In Apocalypse Now black is the central colour, representing the unconscious, with Colonel Kurtz as the dark side of the United States, so the whole film is a journey toward the black at the end of the river. [C32272] In Cafe Society he opposed a low-tonality, penumbra-heavy, lunar Bronx against a solar, summer Hollywood, using period photographers, Georgia O'Keeffe, Ben Shahn, Otto Dix, Tamara de Lempicka and Edward Hopper as references, so the two coasts are two moons and suns. [C32273] In Flamenco he told the story without a plot as a sunset-to-moonrise-to-dawn cycle, moving the painted panels from Plato's square through triangle to circle, so the geometry of the stage walks the same path as the light. [C32274] In 1900 a secondary review describes childhood as drenched summer colour, early fascism under heavy clouds and torrential rain, the wedding as an icy winter picture, and liberation bringing spring's acid primary tints with increasingly bright and celebratory lighting, grade B, so the fifty years of the film are a calendar written in colour temperature. [C32275]

**Takeaway:** The spectrum is a clock, a biography and a moral argument at once. Black to violet is a life; violet to red is a life run backwards; orange against azure is a rivalry; yellow against blue is a city. Once the mapping is fixed, the cinematographer does not have to decide what a scene looks like -- the scene's place in the life, or the day, or the argument, decides it for him. That is the whole trick: the colour system does the directing.

## 4. Writing with light

Cinematography is literally writing with light and motion, from the Greek, and Storaro calls the cinematographer a writer with light, using light, darkness, motion and colour as a language. [C32276] He has used a lighting console on all his films since One from the Heart, shot in 1980, so he can change light, shadow and colour during a shot itself, which turns the lighting from a setup into a performance: the light moves while the camera rolls. [C32277] Rosco made him a custom set of saturated gels, marketed as the Storaro Selection, built on the Newton spectrum from red through violet, so the palette of his films is a physical object, a box of gels, before it is an image. [C32278] He enhanced the Apocalypse Now look by flashing the negative, and on Wonder Wheel colourist Anthony Raffaele recreated that film flashing as Baselight layers, so Storaro could ask on set for ten percent more flash positive or twenty percent more flash negative, the old photochemical trick surviving as a number in a digital grade. [C32279] Reds used the ENR silver-retention process, named for Ernesto Novelli-Raimond, which Storaro said he could not apply to the first release prints of Apocalypse Now but later used on Reds, so the silver stays in the print and the blacks go ink-like. [C32280] On Dick Tracy he used layered lighting with one light per element, tiny apertures for deep focus, split diopters, retained silver for ink-like blacks, and no haze or smoke, a discipline of separation: every element in the frame gets its own light, and nothing is allowed to bleed. [C32281] Dick Tracy used the sodium-vapour process to blend actors into 57 hand-painted matte backgrounds, and it was the last major Hollywood film to use that process, so the most artificial-looking film of his career is also the most hand-made. [C32282]

**Takeaway:** The tools are the philosophy made physical: a console so the light can change mid-shot, a box of gels that is the spectrum itself, a flashing process that lifts the shadows, a silver process that keeps the blacks. He does not light a scene and then shoot it; he writes the scene in light, and the writing happens while the camera is running. The console is the pen.

## 5. The Conformist (1970): the frame as a cage

The Conformist is a 1970 film directed by Bernardo Bertolucci, based on the 1951 novel by Alberto Moravia, with cinematography by Storaro and a Wikipedia infobox runtime of 108 minutes, though Kino Lorber and the Annali credits give 111 minutes, and the study carries both. [C32283] Storaro said of it: "I felt that there should be no middle ground between Marcello Clerici and his surroundings; I wanted to create a kind of visual cage around him, using very sharp light and shadows to create a claustrophobic feeling." [C32284] For the Rome sequences he wanted that cage almost monochromatic, used venetian blinds and tracing paper, and said they should never see any reality beyond the windows, so the fascist capital is a room with no outside. [C32285] The cage opens on the train journey from Rome to Ventimiglia and Paris, progressing from morning to an orange afternoon to a blue evening, with Paris then saturated in blue because Paris represented freedom for Italians of that era, so the journey out of fascism is a journey through the spectrum. [C32286] The striped-dress dance match happened purely by accident, when production designer Ferdinando Scarfiotti arrived with the striped dress after Storaro had already planned stripes of light from venetian blinds, and the swinging lamp in the Chinese restaurant returns Clerici to the cage of darkness and light. [C32287] Storaro said of the film: "In a way, The Conformist was my passport to Apocalypse Now, and all of the films I've done since then." [C32288] The film won the National Society of Film Critics Awards for Best Director and Best Cinematography, and it was the film that launched Storaro's international career, the cage becoming his calling card. [C32289]

**Takeaway:** The cage is the first complete statement of the method: a psychological condition, conformism, rendered as a lighting condition, sharp light and shadow with no middle ground. The venetian blind is the whole career in one object -- a piece of furniture that turns light into bars. From here on, every Storaro film is a room whose light is its argument.

## 6. Plato's cave: light as identity

Marcello's unfinished senior thesis was on Plato's Allegory of the Cave, and in Quadri's study he recalls it, while Quadri picks the thread up and turns it into an attack on Marcello's perception, describing the near-sightless prisoners reacting to shadows cast by their unseen captors' flames. [C32290] In the scene Storaro used two windows and an arc light erected in the neighbour's garden, and when Quadri reopens a window Clerici has shut, the light obliterates Clerici's shadow image, so he becomes a man without an identity -- the thesis discussion becomes a lighting event, and the protagonist's own shadow is thrown on the wall while his teacher tells him he mistakes shadows for reality. [C32291] Using shadows, bars, and the camera as a surveillance device, Bertolucci and Storaro turn the cinematic canvas into a prison: the film consistently frames characters within bars, in the form of horizontal and vertical shadows as well as barred windows. [C32292] The EUR district of Rome -- commissioned by Mussolini as a model city, rationalist architecture -- is a key location, alongside the Palazzo dei Congressi, the Museum of the Ara Pacis, Sant' Angelo Bridge, Santa Marinella, the Theatre of Marcellus and the Colosseum, with studio scenes at Cinecitta, so the fascist city itself is the set, and its rationalist geometry is the cage built in stone. [C32293]

**Takeaway:** Plato's cave is not quoted in The Conformist, it is staged: a window opens, an arc light fires, and a man's shadow is erased from the wall in front of him. That is the purest form of Storaro's method -- an idea from philosophy turned into a lighting cue, and the lighting cue doing the philosophical work. Identity, in this film, is a shadow, and light can take it away.

## 7. Last Tango in Paris (1972): what can come after azure

Last Tango in Paris is a 1972 film directed by Bertolucci, starring Marlon Brando and Maria Schneider, with cinematography by Storaro and a Wikipedia runtime of 129 minutes. [C32294] Storaro told a reporter: "after The Conformist I had a moment of crisis; I was asking myself: what can come after azure?... I did not have the slightest idea that an orange film could be born. We needed another kind of emotion... It was the case of Last Tango." [C32295] Bertolucci and Storaro took the Francis Bacon paintings as inspiration for rich oranges, cool greys, icy whites and occasional reds, Storaro presented Paris as a city of light, and Brando's character was visualised always with orange colour, like a sun in its sunset time. [C32296] In the apartment Storaro never used light inside it, prepared all lighting outside the window on a very little balcony, used Bausch and Lomb lenses for softness, and shot on Kodak 3200 degrees K negative, so the sealed room is lit entirely from the outside, like a cell with one window. [C32297] Storaro told the ASC on Last Tango: "It is Bernardo who usually 'writes the story with the camera.' It is me who usually 'writes the story with the light.'" [C32298]

**Takeaway:** The crisis is the method working as designed: if each film must have its own colour, then after azure there has to be a next colour, and the next colour has to mean a different emotion. Orange is not a prettier azure, it is a different temperature, a different time of day, a different stage of life -- the sun setting instead of the sky at noon. The film is orange because the man in it is setting.

## 8. 1900 (1976) and La Luna (1979): seasons and shadows

1900, in Italian Novecento, is a 1976 epic directed by Bertolucci, photographed by Storaro, with an original runtime of 317 minutes, following landowner Alfredo Berlinghieri and peasant Olmo Dalco, both born in 1901, in Emilia. [C32299] On La Luna, a 1979 film directed by Bertolucci and photographed by Storaro, Storaro told the ASC that Bertolucci pushed him to see completely into the shadows and shoot in a more conscious, straightforward way, which Storaro said he often fought, so the fallow film is a fight about how dark a shadow is allowed to be. [C32300] La Luna's release date is flagged on its own page: the infobox gives 30 September 1979 while the lead text says the film was released on 29 August 1979, and the page does not resolve them. [C32301]

**Takeaway:** 1900 is scale, La Luna is shadow. The epic stretches the colour calendar across fifty years and five hours; the smaller film is the argument about the shadows inside that calendar, and Storaro fights it because his whole method depends on the shadow being readable, not empty. The director wants to see into the dark; the cinematographer wants the dark to stay dark enough to mean something.

## 9. The Last Emperor (1987): colour as a life's arc

The Last Emperor is a 1987 film directed by Bertolucci and photographed by Storaro, with a Wikipedia runtime of 163 minutes, and it was the first Western feature film authorised by the People's Republic of China to film in the Forbidden City. [C32302] Storaro explained in Visions of Light, as reported second-hand, that red represents birth and the blood that starts the flashback, orange is the warm colour of family and the Forbidden City, yellow is the emperor's identity and the sun, and green, the tutor's bike and hat, represents knowledge. [C32303] A different secondary summary extends that scheme to green and grey for Puyi's later life as a citizen, so the exact endpoint of the colour progression is not uniformly stated across the sources read: one account ends the life in violet, the twilight, and another ends it in green and grey, the citizen, and the study carries both. [C32304] The film won Storaro his third Academy Award for Best Cinematography at the 1988 ceremony, along with the David di Donatello for Best Cinematography in 1988 and the British Society of Cinematographers Best Cinematography award in 1988, with a BAFTA nomination as well. [C32305]

**Takeaway:** The Last Emperor is the spectrum-as-a-life played at full scale, in the largest rooms Storaro ever lit, with a whole empire as the set. The flashback structure is the mechanism: the present is a prison cell, the past is a palace, and the colour tells you which act of the life you are in before a word is spoken. It is the Conformist cage grown into a palace, and the Oscar is the industry recognising that the colour system had become a language.

## 10. The Sheltering Sky (1990) and Little Buddha (1993): desert and two formats

The Sheltering Sky is a 1990 film directed by Bertolucci and photographed by Storaro, with a runtime of 138 minutes, and Storaro won the BAFTA for Best Cinematography for it in 1991. [C32306] For The Sheltering Sky, secondary reviews describe a shift from warm, intimate lighting in Tangier to the harsh, bleaching white of the deep desert, making landscape into character, grade B and not Storaro's own wording in the pages read. [C32307] Little Buddha is a 1993 film directed by Bertolucci and photographed by Storaro, with a runtime of 140 minutes, whose Buddha flashback scenes were photographed in 65 mm Todd-AO while the rest was filmed in 35 mm anamorphic Technovision. [C32308] No specific colour or light mechanism for Little Buddha was established from the Wikipedia pages read, beyond that large-format split, and the study says so plainly rather than inventing one. [C32309] Little Buddha's budget is flagged: the infobox gives $35 million while the box-office text gives $30 million, against $48 million worldwide, and its year is flagged too, 1993 in Storaro's filmography against 1994 in one FilmReference credit list. [C32310]

**Takeaway:** The desert bleaches and the format splits. In The Sheltering Sky the light itself is the antagonist, washing the colour out of the travellers until there is nothing left to photograph; in Little Buddha the format does the work the colour used to do, 65 mm for the sacred past and 35 mm for the washed-out present. The run ends here -- Stealing Beauty was shot by Darius Khondji, and Besieged, The Dreamers and Me and You by Fabio Cianchetti -- and the light changes with the cinematographer, which is the proof that the light was his.

## 11. Apocalypse Now (1979): artificial light over natural light

After a gruelling 15-month shoot in the Philippines, Apocalypse Now was released on 15 August 1979. [C32311] It was Storaro's American film debut and his first collaboration with Coppola, and it earned him his first Academy Award. [C32312] Storaro initially refused the film because he did not want to interfere in Coppola's relationship with Gordon Willis, and he accepted only after Willis assured him he was not part of the project. [C32313] Coppola told him to read Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, and Storaro concluded that the darkness in the title belonged not to the jungle culture but to the supposedly civilised culture making its way up the river. [C32314] His visual strategy was a conflict between artificial light and natural light, with black as a central colour representing the unconscious and Colonel Kurtz as the dark side of the United States. [C32315] He wanted the jungle in very aggressive, saturated, non-naturalistic colour, guided by Burne Hogarth's Tarzan drawings and by Henri Rousseau's jungle paintings and silhouettes. [C32316] Coppola wanted the helicopter attack to be a magnificent show rather than realism, creating a conflict between beauty and horror, which is why Kilgore is enchanted by the beauty of napalm while people die. [C32317] For the Do Lung Bridge sequence he used towers with arc lights directed by walkie-talkie, and for the Playboy show he put Photofloods inside the stage so artificial light intruded into the jungle, working with only one thousand-amp generator. [C32318] He shot with Mitchell reflex cameras modified by Technovision to accept Cooke anamorphic lenses, and he enhanced the look by flashing the negative. [C32319] The Kurtz compound explosion took nine nights and about ten cameras, used a real-stone temple and real dynamite, and no one was hurt. [C32320] The Oscar was verified directly on oscars.org this run: the 52nd Academy Awards were held on Monday 14 April 1980, and Storaro is listed as the Apocalypse Now cinematography winner. [C32321]

**Takeaway:** The jungle film is the Conformist cage turned inside out: instead of bars of shadow falling on a man in a room, it is artificial light falling on a jungle, one culture superimposing itself on another by superimposing its light. The napalm is beautiful because Coppola wanted it beautiful, and Storaro's job was to make the beauty legible as horror -- the saturated colour is the lie the film tells about itself, and the black at the end of the river is the truth underneath it.

## 12. Reds (1981) and Dick Tracy (1990): the colour systems

Reds follows journalist John Reed, played by Beatty, and Louise Bryant, played by Diane Keaton, and it was nominated for twelve Oscars and won three, including Best Director and Best Cinematography. [C32322] Harvard Film Archive describes Reds as photographed in rich shades of sepia and amber. [C32323] Reds used the ENR silver-retention process, named for Ernesto Novelli-Raimond, which Storaro said he could not apply to the first release prints of Apocalypse Now but later used on Reds. [C32324] Dick Tracy was Oscar-nominated for Best Cinematography in 1991 but did not win, while the Reds win recognised the epic period palette and ENR silver-retention look. [C32325] Beatty is a three-picture collaborator by Storaro's own count, namely Reds, Dick Tracy and Bulworth, in the Live Design Tango interview. [C32326] Bulworth is a 1998 political satire directed by and starring Warren Beatty, with cinematography by Storaro and a release date of 15 May 1998, and one review describes its visual split as almost colourless political offices and corporate meetings against vibrant blue, orange and yellow filters in the predominantly Black neighbourhood Bulworth enters. [C32327] Bulworth is dated 1997 in one FilmReference credit list but 1998, with a 15 May 1998 release, in its film page, so 1998 is the preferred date. [C32328]

**Takeaway:** Reds is the past in sepia and amber, Dick Tracy is the present in seven flat colours, Bulworth is the split between a colourless establishment and a vivid street. Three films with one director, three different answers to the same question -- what colour is this world? -- and the answer is always a system, never a mood. The system is what the Oscar recognised: not a pretty image, but a complete colour world with rules.

## 13. The Saura dance films (1995-2021)

Flamenco, a 1995 documentary directed by Carlos Saura, is listed with Storaro as cinematographer, and it is the first of seven Saura collaborations. [C32329] Taxi, a 1996 film directed by Saura, lists Storaro as cinematographer. [C32330] Tango, a 1998 film directed by Saura, lists Storaro as cinematographer and won the Cannes Technical Grand Prize for Storaro in 1998. [C32331] Goya in Bordeaux, a 1999 Saura film shot by Storaro, won Best Cinematography at the European Film Awards in 2000 and the Goya Award for Best Cinematography in 2000, and Storaro was Goya-nominated for Flamenco in 1996 and for Tango in 1999. ## 14. Univisium 2.00:1

Univisium, from macaronic Latin for unity of images, is a proposed universal format created by Vittorio Storaro and his son Fabrizio to unify theatrical and television films at 2.00:1. [C32337] Storaro announced it in 1998 in an interview with International Photographer, originally calling it Univision. [C32338] The 2.00:1 ratio is the mathematical average of 65 mm at 2.20:1 and HD at 1.78:1. [C32339] His argument was that a film has a short life on the big screen and a much longer life on an electronic screen, so one common composition would end the nightmare of composing for two different aspect ratios. [C32340] The 35 mm proposal used a Super 35-sized negative with three perforations per frame and a 24 mm by 12 mm aperture, using twenty-five percent less film and giving about thirty-three percent more shooting time per magazine. [C32341] It also proposed spherical lenses, which are cheaper, faster and give more depth of field than anamorphic lenses, plus shooting at twenty-five frames per second. [C32342] Storaro's first Univisium films listed are Tango (1998) and Decadence (1998), followed by Goya in Bordeaux (1999). [C32343] He has since used Univisium for nearly all of his films, including Frank Herbert's Dune (2000), Cafe Society (2016) and Wonder Wheel (2017). [C32344] He also reframed Apocalypse Now, Reds and The Last Emperor to 2.00:1 for DVD, which proved controversial, and the Blu-ray and 4K UHD releases restored their original aspect ratios. [C32345] As of 2023 the unification had not happened, although 2:1 later became common in streaming work such as House of Cards, Stranger Things and Jurassic World. [C32346] The first-use date is flagged: the ASC Dune article dates Univisium's first use on Tango to 1997, against the film's 1998 release year. [C32347]

**Takeaway:** Univisium is the method applied to the frame itself: if colour is a system with rules, the shape of the image should be a system with rules too, one shape for every screen, so the composition never has to be re-thought for television. The reframing controversy is the proof of how seriously he takes it -- he was willing to re-cut his own masterpieces to fit the system, and the audience, which preferred the originals, was the check on the system. The format did not win, but the streaming frame quietly became 2:1 anyway.

## 15. The Allen late period (2016-2023): digital light

Cafe Society was Storaro's first project shot digitally, and it was also Allen's first digital feature. [C32348] It was shot on Sony CineAlta F65 cameras, with the lighter PMW-F55 for Steadicam, and Cooke S4 lenses, and Storaro said he did not change his approach or lighting setup for digital, choosing the F65 because it was close in personality to the Arriflex 535B he had loved for almost twenty years. [C32349] He gave Cafe Society four looks: a desaturated, monochromatic Bronx inspired by Alfred Stieglitz and Georgia O'Keeffe, a warm Hollywood inspired by Edward Steichen and German Expressionism, a brighter and more sophisticated Art Deco New York inspired by Tamara de Lempicka, and a final Los Angeles inspired by Edward Hopper. [C32350] The film used a three-strip Technicolor look created in Baselight with ACES colour, in a 4K 16-bit workflow. [C32351] Wonder Wheel was Allen's fiftieth film as director, was released generally on his eighty-second birthday, and was set in 1950s Coney Island. [C32352] Storaro's vision for Wonder Wheel was a bright, saturated view of long hot summer days, inspired by painters Reginald Marsh and Norman Rockwell. [C32353] It was shot on Sony F65 cameras with Cooke and Angenieux lenses in 4K high dynamic range, with on-set Prelight grades, Baselight Linked Grades, ACES throughout, and a Saturday hotel-room viewing session Storaro called the weeklies. [C32354] Colourist Anthony Raffaele recreated Storaro's film flashing as Baselight layers, so Storaro could ask on set for ten percent more flash positive or twenty percent more flash negative. [C32355] Storaro shot five Allen features in all: Cafe Society (2016), Wonder Wheel (2017), A Rainy Day in New York (2019), Rifkin's Festival (2020) and Coup de chance (2023). [C32356]

**Takeaway:** The digital turn changes nothing and proves everything. He picks the camera that behaves like the film camera he loved, lights exactly as he always lit, and asks for his old flashing trick by percentage in the grade -- the method survives the technology change intact, because the method was never about the stock. The four looks of Cafe Society are the colour-as-temperature system applied to two cities and two decades, and the camera is just the newest pen.

## 16. Evolution: early vs recent

The early period, 1961-1972, is the apprenticeship in public: six shorts, a first feature in 1969, the Bertolucci crew work, and then the Conformist cage and the azure crisis that followed it, all on film stock, all with Bertolucci, the method being invented shot by shot. [C32357] The middle period, 1976-1999, is the epic and the system at full power: 1900, the Apocalypse Now debut, three Oscars, the ENR silver process on Reds, the seven-colour Dick Tracy, the seven Saura dance films, and Univisium proposed in 1998 as the format to hold it all. [C32358] The late period, 2000-2023, is the digital turn and the long partnerships: the Dune miniseries and its Emmy, the digital Allen films from Cafe Society onward, and Saura until The King of All the World in 2021, with the method unchanged and the tools renamed. [C32359] Fabrizio Storaro co-created Univisium with his father, and he did the visualisation for the Univision brochure. [C32360] He founded Proxima in 1991, an Italian digital effects facility, and for Frank Herbert's Dune (2000) he helped create the show's otherworldly TransLites with his father, while Proxima's effects for the miniseries won an Emmy. [C32361] For Goya in Bordeaux (1999) he shared a Goya Award nomination for Best Special Effects with Reyes Abades. [C32362] For Dominion: Prequel to the Exorcist (2005) he shared visual-effects supervision with Paolo Zeccara, producing 120 effects shots, about seventy percent of them at 4K resolution. [C32363] He founded Ox Art in 2002 to create digital cinema sets, called Translite, in Univisium 2:1, seen most recently at that time in Goya and Dune. [C32364]

**Takeaway:** The evolution is not a change of mind, it is a change of tools around a fixed mind. The azure crisis of 1972 and the digital choice of 2016 are the same decision made twice: pick the colour, or the camera, whose personality matches the emotion, and then let the system do the work. The son enters the story as the keeper of the format, the father as the keeper of the light, and the two halves of the career -- the image and its shape -- end up in one family.

## 17. The network

Bertolucci is the root: Storaro names eight pictures with him, namely The Spider's Stratagem, The Conformist, Last Tango in Paris, 1900, La Luna, The Last Emperor, The Sheltering Sky and Little Buddha, in the Live Design Tango interview. [C32365] Coppola is the American door: Storaro names four pictures with him, namely Apocalypse Now, One from the Heart, Tucker and New York Stories, in the Live Design Tango interview, with Captain EO (1986) as an additional short collaboration. [C32366] Beatty is the three-picture run: Storaro names three pictures with him, namely Reds, Dick Tracy and Bulworth, in the Live Design Tango interview. [C32367] Allen is the late door: Storaro shot five Allen features, Cafe Society (2016), Wonder Wheel (2017), A Rainy Day in New York (2019), Rifkin's Festival (2020) and Coup de chance (2023). [C32371] The Academy Museum calls The Conformist the first feature film collaboration between Bertolucci, Storaro and art director Ferdinando Scarfiotti, and the distinction between The Spider's Stratagem as a made-for-television work shot in summer 1969 and The Conformist as the first feature collaboration is a plausible reconciliation, but it is NOT settled here and needs a first-film check. **Takeaway:** The network is a set of doors, and each door is a director who lets the cinematographer bring his whole system with him: Bertolucci gives him the room and the cage, Coppola gives him the river and the jungle, Beatty gives him the epic and the comic strip, Saura gives him the empty stage, Allen gives him the digital camera. He never adapts to a director by abandoning the method; he adapts by finding which colour the director's world already is. That is why the network holds for fifty years: the system is portable, and every new director is a new temperature to measure.

## 18. Aesthetic logic: what makes a Storaro image a Storaro image

Colour as temperature and as biography: every image sits somewhere on the spectrum from black to violet, and that position tells you the time of day, the stage of the life, the temperature of the world and the emotional state of the person in it, before a word is spoken. [C32375] Light as writing and equilibrium as the plot: the film is a conflict between opposites -- darkness and light, cold and warmth, blue and orange, artificial and natural -- and the story is the resolution of that conflict, written with a lighting console, a box of gels and a flashing process. [C32376] The bounded space whose light is its law: the Conformist cage, the Last Tango apartment lit only from outside, the Forbidden City, the desert that bleaches, the dance stage that walks from sunset to dawn -- each film seals its characters in one space and lets the light of that space do the moral work. [C32377] Camera movement as the director's grammar, light as the cinematographer's: Storaro told the ASC that from the first time he met Bernardo in the early 1960s, Bertolucci made clear that camera positioning and movement were part of the director's grammar, and Storaro's own division of labour, stated on Last Tango, is that Bertolucci writes the story with the camera and he writes the story with the light. [C32378] The format as argument: Univisium says the shape of the image is part of the system, one ratio for the big screen and the small one, so the composition is composed once and survives every screen it will ever meet. [C32379] The synthesis is a room lit by a theory: a bounded space, a colour that names its temperature and its place in a life, a light that can change while the camera rolls, and two opposites held until they resolve into white, the sum of all the colours and the balance of the whole. [C32380]

**Takeaway:** Every Storaro mechanism is the same move in a different costume: fix the colour system first, then let the system decide what the image looks like. The cage, the orange apartment, the saturated jungle, the sepia revolution, the seven-colour comic strip, the dance floor at dawn -- all of them are the spectrum doing the directing, and the cinematographer is the man who reads the spectrum and writes it down in light. He does not decorate films; he translates them, from story into wavelength, and back again.
