## M1. The object lexicon (the alphabet, then the landscape)

A fixed, small vocabulary of things, light bulb, shoe, clock, book, brick, cigarette, bottle, ladder, nail, garbage-can lid, hood, bean-head, learned one object at a time in ink drawings, the 1968 to 1969 pure drawings, then multiplied and assembled into large paintings, and the watchable behaviour is a viewer who learns the alphabet across paintings and then watches the same dozen things recombine into new sentences, and the mechanism is the alphabet first and the landscape second, the drawing doing the learning and the painting doing the assembling, and the alphabet is the landscape, and the landscape is the alphabet. Evidence: Musa Mayer in Tate Etc, he begins work on a series of very spare abstract ink drawings, and at that time he starts making drawings and little paintings of things, objects, books, bricks, shoes, and from these works a sort of alphabet of images emerges, and he begins to work on a larger scale, and the things begin to multiply, and suddenly he is creating these huge canvases where he is assembling whole landscapes and narratives and interiors; EBSCO's iconography list is the same lexicon. **Takeaway:** draw one object at a time in ink until an alphabet exists, then let the objects multiply and assemble themselves into a landscape, so the viewer learns the dozen things and watches them recombine into new sentences. Against the chain: Hartigan (#390) also used icons, and hers are autobiographical emblems, a star, a motorcycle, a billboard, doing the identifying work, and Rivers (#391) also reused images, and his come from reproductions, a schoolbook, a cigar box, a magazine photograph, and Guston's is a drawn alphabet, learned object by object in ink before any large painting is attempted, which is why her picture is an emblem and his is a reproduction and Guston's is a sentence, and the sentence is the alphabet, and the alphabet is the landscape.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is the image that will not stay dissolved. He finds what to paint, the mural years, persecution, war, the Klan, then finds how to paint, the abstract years, the stroke, the field, the hatch-mark cloud, then the two converge, the late years, the what returns inside the how. Mayer's three-phase reading in Tate Etc is the cleanest statement: the first period of my father's career was all about finding what to paint, and then the abstract period was about finding how to paint, and then those two elements converged in the late work, it is that convergence I think that sustains the interest in his work over time. The late paintings are therefore not a rejection of abstraction but its fulfilment: the abstract stroke, taught to depict a shoe, a hood, a light bulb. And the moral logic is the self under the hood: the painter puts himself behind the mask and asks what kind of man he is, the question that became the Met's 2023 show title, What Kind of Man Am I?, taken from his 1977 interview line, what kind of man am I, sitting at home, reading magazines, going into a frustrated fury about everything, and then going into my studio to adjust a red to a blue? The lexicon is the discipline: twelve things, recombined for a decade, the constraint doing the work the invention cannot do, and the change is the technique: each manner abandoned the moment it is mastered, and the abandonment is the picture, and the picture is the convergence, and the convergence is the hood, and the hood is the painter, and the painter is the question.
