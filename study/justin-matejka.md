## The question: prediction as a number

Matejka's career is one long answer to a single question, asked at three scales. At the scale of the picture, the visualization work asks: what should the rendering do when the data gets dense -- and the answer is a number, not a style: the mean opacity of the pixels actually used stays at 40%, and the point opacity is whatever solves for that. At the scale of the interface, the learnability work asks: what should the software do when it knows what you are about to need -- and the answer is again a number: a similarity score, a coverage level, a relevance that decays linearly to zero in five minutes. At the scale of the record, the video and history work asks: what should the past do when the present arrives -- and the answer is a mapping: one dot per event, one heatmap over the live window, one trip scored by inverse frequency against the last five commands. [C36480]

The second question is what the prediction costs. Every one of these systems trades something for its foresight: Dynamic Opacity trades the user's control of a slider for a computed default; CommunityCommands trades the privacy of 40 million command tuples for a palette of suggestions; Patina trades a clean interface for a coloured record of where everybody has clicked; Ambient Help trades screen space for resources the user did not ask for. The study therefore has to do two things at once: take the prediction rules seriously as executable mechanisms, and refuse to pretend the trade is free -- each prompt below names what the system gives up, because the giving-up is part of the system. [C36481]

**Takeaway:** Do not build "a Matejka" as a scatter plot with a slider on it. Build the prediction rule -- one stream the user already produces, one score computed from it, one visible consequence -- and build the decay that removes the consequence when it is no longer earned. [C36482]

## M1 -- The forty-percent invariant (Dynamic Opacity Optimization, CHI 2015)

Subject: scatter plots at extreme over-plotting -- so many points per pixel that the distribution disappears into a solid blob. Visual principle: the over-plotting factor is (# of points x area of each point) / area of the chart; at 4x, four times as many pixels are needed to represent the data as the chart has. The standard mitigations (smaller points, unfilled glyphs, jitter, sampling) fail at extreme over-plotting or are undesirable; reducing point opacity is the remaining option, but graphing packages default to 100% and offer no automatic selection. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the authors crowd-sourced the answer first, then built the model from it. Study 1: 46 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers recruited, 15 removed for gaming the task, leaving 31 valid participants, paid $2 for ~10 minutes; each trial showed a scatter plot and the participant moved a slider to set the opacity they thought gave the best overall legibility in both light and dark areas. Charts were 80x80 px with 2x2 px square points; point counts took 27 values, the cubes 1^3 through 27^3 (1 to 19,683 points), giving over-plotting factors from 0.0006x to 12.3x; three Gaussian distributions (wide/medium/narrow; sigma = 0.7, 0.2, 0.1, all centred at 0.5, bound 0-1 per axis); 81 distribution x point-count combinations x 3 blocks = 243 trials per participant. Distribution was a significant factor (F(2,25) = 31.9, p < .0001): wide distributions got the highest opacity settings, narrow the lowest. [C36486]

The discovered invariant is the Mean Opacity of Utilized Pixels (MOUP): the sum of the final opacities of every pixel in the plot, divided by the number of pixels whose opacity is greater than zero -- only pixels actually covered by at least one point. Except where the over-plotting factor is very low, MOUP stays relatively constant at around 40% and is not affected by distribution type: users create scatter plots with a fixed MOUP of 40% in over-plotting scenarios. The model therefore solves for alpha_MOUP_0.4, the individual point opacity that yields MOUP = 0.4, by binary search over the opacity space to a precision of three decimal places; the un-optimized implementation "can compute at 30fps for graphs up to 250x250 pixels in size". [C36487]

The formulas, as printed -- with two flags. The final opacity of a pixel with l overlapping layers at point opacity alpha follows the recursion O_f(l, alpha) = alpha + O_f(l-1, alpha) . alpha for l > 1, and alpha for l = 1; MOUP(P, alpha) is the sum over pixels of O_f(l_p, alpha) divided by the count of pixels with l_p > 0. FLAG ONE: as printed, that recursion is not the standard Porter-Duff "over" operator the paper cites -- the standard form multiplies the accumulated lower layers by (1 - alpha), giving 1 - (1 - alpha)^l. The printed form exceeds 1 when alpha is large and l >= 2, but within the study's parameter range (alpha small where overlap is deep, overlap shallow where alpha is large) it stays bounded, and the fitted results stand; the patent (US 2016/0358351 A1, Eq. 2) prints the identical form, so this is the authors' text, not a one-off extraction error. Any piece reusing the mechanism should use the standard bounded operator. The Low Density Multiplier is printed as LDM_opf = min{1, 1 - 0.15 x log(opf / 0.75)}, verified visually against the printed page and against the patent (Eq. 5), with alpha_optimal = LDM_opf x alpha_MOUP_0.4. FLAG TWO: as printed, the bracket exceeds 1 exactly below opf = 0.75, so min{1, .} holds it at 1 there and the multiplier only bites above ~0.75 -- the opposite of the paper's own prose, which says the term acts only below ~0.8x and evaluates to 1 otherwise. The printed formula and the prose claim do not reconcile as written; the study reports the formula as printed and does not "fix" it. [C36488]

Fit to user data (OBSERVED): Study 1, the algorithm's opacities against the user-generated levels correlate at R^2 = .9904 overall (per-distribution R^2 .9921-.9994; the medium-distribution fit is y = 0.52 + 1.034x, R^2 = .9994). Study 2, real-world data: 55 workers signed up, 12 removed for gaming, 43 valid; four distributions (D1-D4) derived from the physical properties of baseball pitching data (PITCHf/x, Fast 2010) instead of Gaussians; three grid/dot configurations (C1 = 80x80 px, 2x2 px squares; C2 = 250x250 px, 2x2 px squares; C3 = 250x250 px, 4 px-diameter circles); five point-count levels (250, 1,000, 4,000, 16,000, 48,000), over-plotting factors from 0.038x to 30x; 4 blocks x 60 conditions = 240 trials per participant. Result: R^2 = 0.9899 against user choices, with every predicted value inside the interquartile range of the user data. The technique was patented as US 2016/0358351 A1, filed Friday 5 June 2015, published Thursday 8 December 2016, inventors Justin Frank Matejka, Fraser Anderson, George Fitzmaurice; the patent's predetermined MOUP value is "between 35%-45%, such as 40%". [C36489]

**Takeaway:** A plot stays legible not because someone chose a good opacity but because a measured quantity is held constant while the data grows. Build the invariant, not the slider. [C36490]

## M2 -- Same stats, different graphs (CHI 2017)

Subject: thirteen datasets of 142 points each with identical summary statistics to two decimal places -- mean of x 54.26, sample variance of x 16.76, mean of y 47.83, sample variance of y 26.93, correlation -0.06, linear regression y = 53 - 0.1x, R^2 0.004 -- and thirteen completely different shapes: a dinosaur, a ring, a bullseye, a star, a cross, lines, clouds. Visual principle: the summary table is the same and the picture is not; the statistic is blind and the eye is not. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the dinosaur dataset was made by Alberto Cairo in 2016; the other twelve were created by Matejka and Fitzmaurice at Autodesk using simulated annealing -- small, random, biased changes to each point toward the desired shape, each shape taking 200,000 iterations of perturbations. The paper (CHI 2017, pp. 1290-1294, published Tuesday 2 May 2017, DOI 10.1145/3025453.3025912) is the "Datasaurus dozen", and the Wikipedia image of it was viewed as rendered pixels this run: a small statistics table at the top with near-identical values across rows, a curved arrow leading down to a grid of thirteen small scatterplots, the Dino plot a clearly recognisable Tyrannosaurus silhouette made of dots. Artistic opportunity: annealing is a general machine for holding one thing fixed while another changes -- freeze the statistics, free the shape. A generative analogue needs only this: a target shape, a set of numbers that must not move, and a perturbation rule that improves the shape while the numbers are re-checked after every step. Most generated work varies everything at once; the Datasaurus shows how freezing one quantity makes the variation of the other legible. [C36491]

**Takeaway:** Hold the numbers still and let the shape move. Build the constraint, not the shape. [C36492]

## M3 -- Predict the next new command (CommunityCommands, UIST 2009; TOCHI 2011)

Subject: feature-rich software (AutoCAD) in which most users never find most commands. Visual principle: the signal is each user's command-frequency table, collected from Autodesk's Customer Involvement Program: 40 million {User, Command, Time} tuples from 16,000 AutoCAD users over 6 months. There is no explicit rating; a user's implicit "rating" for a command is its usage frequency. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): to stop the few commands everybody uses from dominating similarity, frequencies are weighted by command-frequency x inverse-user-frequency (cf-iuf, the tf-idf analogue): cf = n_ij / sum_k n_kj, iuf = log(|S| / |{u_j : c_i in u_j}|), cf-iuf = alpha . cf . iuf, with alpha a tuning parameter. Similarity between users (or between commands) is the cosine of the angle between their cf-iuf vectors. Two algorithms: user-based -- find the n most similar users, take a weighted average of their command frequencies as the active user's expected frequency table, delete every command the user has already used, return the top 10; item-based -- build a command-to-command cosine similarity matrix M, take the active user's used commands as the "active list" L, score each unused command by the mean of its similarity to all commands in L, return the top 10. On top of both sit 21 hand-written domain rules from ~2 hours with a domain expert (4 "upgrades", e.g. MouseWheelPan supersedes Pan; 17 "equivalencies", e.g. HATCH vs BHATCH). [C36493]

Evaluation used a new k-tail method: split a user's command sequence into a training set and a test set containing k commands never seen in training (k = 1 measures predicting the next new command). Offline, on 4,033 users with >= 2,000 observed commands, item-based predicted the next new command correctly for 850 users at k = 1, about 240 more than Linton's OWL baseline. Online, 17 professional AutoCAD users rated top-8 lists from each algorithm: item-based produced 30.9% "good" recommendations (unfamiliar + would use) vs 14.7% for Linton and 27.2% for user-based, and 22.1% "poor" (would not use) vs 41.9% for Linton and 32.4% for user-based -- the paper's headline "2.1 times as many good suggestions as existing techniques". How the prediction is surfaced: a peripheral palette docked in the unused space beside the AutoCAD command line (bottom right), showing a ranked list of command buttons; clicking executes the command, hovering shows a tooltip; a toggle switches between "Long Term" (whole history) and "Short Term" (last 15 minutes of commands); each button has a close box (removes the command and blocks it from future suggestion) and a pin (keeps it); a command "notebook" holds user/manager tips per command. Once a command is used it is never recommended again -- a limitation the paper names. [C36494]

The journal version (Li, Matejka, Grossman, Konstan, Fitzmaurice, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 18(2), Article 6, June 2011, 35 pp., DOI 10.1145/1970378.1970380; Matejka second author) adds a design space with two axes -- the active user's input history (current session vs entire history) and the community database's organisation (session-based vs user-based command correlations) -- giving four quadrants, and a 6-week field study (32 AutoCAD users completing, 52 recruited; 4 weeks of silent logging, then 2 weeks with the palette showing exactly 5 recommendations, counterbalanced short-term vs long-term): the short-term list changed far faster (~120 list events/hour vs ~13 for long-term); users adopted on average 5.2 commands suggested by the short-term algorithm vs 2.8 by the long-term (difference not significant, p = 0.113); 56% of users preferred short-term, 16% long-term; only 9% found the list reordering distracting. New-command use jumped in the first recommender week (17.5 new commands vs a model-predicted 6.1 without the recommender, a 187% increase), and most of the extra commands had never appeared in the recommendation list -- users discovered them while investigating recommended ones. [C36495]

Deployment (OBSERVED, abstract and introduction read): the plug-in was publicly downloadable for AutoCAD and, over a one-year period, was used by more than 1,100 AutoCAD users, issuing over 55,000 command recommendations, with the item-by-item similarity matrix pushed to each user's machine so recommendations compute locally (Li, Matejka, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, "Deploying CommunityCommands", AI Magazine 36(3), 2015, DOI 10.1609/aimag.v36i3.2600). The patent counterpart is US 9,063,925 B2, "System and method for recommending next commands when using a software application", inventors Fitzmaurice and Justin Frank Matejka, priority 28 October 2008, granted Tuesday 23 June 2015. [C36496]

**Takeaway:** The community's past is a prediction about your next move, and the prediction is a cosine. Build the similarity, not the list. [C36497]

## M4 -- Paint the past over the present (Patina, CHI 2013)

Subject: a heatmap of how software is actually used, drawn over the live interface. Visual principle: clicks are not plotted at raw screen coordinates. Each click is assigned to the smallest control region (accessibility region, else control window) that contains it, matched to the same region in the current view by Name/Role/Description, and re-mapped by relative position inside the region, so the heatmap survives window resizing and ribbon reflow. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the system collects usage data with no instrumentation of the target application, using only Windows window metrics and Microsoft Active Accessibility data, plus a low-level mouse hook for clicks. The intensity map draws a semi-transparent circle (radius 20 px, base opacity 40%) at each mapped click, darkening where they overlap; clicks in regions larger than 64x64 px (4,096 px^2) are dimmed, down to 3% for regions over 90,000 px^2, so canvas clicks do not drown out widget clicks. Colour encodes two populations at once: the active user's usage in blue, the community's in orange, with a combined view taking per-pixel intensity = max of the two and colour = a blend by relative proportion. The overlay is shown on demand (F2 "You", F3 "Others", both = combined), fading in over 0.3 s at 50% opacity; a transient variant appears automatically when new UI components become visible, showing only those controls, fading in and out over 5 seconds. The paper also re-implements Scented Widgets, USHER-style outlier warnings, and a CommunityCommands-style recommendation list (commands the community uses a lot that the active user never uses, click to highlight the element) on top of the same collected data. Figure 1, viewed as rendered pixels this run, shows the Microsoft Word ribbon with a translucent heatmap overlaid -- glowing red/orange/yellow blobs over frequently used controls and cooler blue/green patches elsewhere, side by side with the plain interface. [C36498]

Deployment (OBSERVED): 8 internal users for 1 week, Microsoft Word only, 8,742 clicks recorded, overlay activated 285 times (92 personal, 130 community, 63 combined); 80% of clicks fell in regions over 90,000 px^2. Artistic opportunity: a heatmap is a picture of a history, and the two-colour rule (you vs everyone) turns any accumulated trace into a comparison. The dimming rule for large regions is the portable idea: weight a mark by the size of the region it lands in, so the big empty canvas cannot shout down the small crowded controls. And the transient variant is a small, complete rule for novelty: when something new appears, show only the new thing, for five seconds, then let it go. [C36499]

**Takeaway:** The interface remembers where hands have been, and the memory is a picture. Build the heat, not the map. [C36500]

## M5 -- Help that decays (Ambient Help, CHI 2011; IP-QAT, UIST 2011)

Subject: learning resources that arrive without being asked for, and leave without being dismissed. Visual principle: Ambient Help presents multiple videos and textual help resources ambiently on a secondary display while the user works, chosen by application context -- the commands currently being used, the contents of the document being modified, open dialogue windows, the user's expertise level -- plus each resource's metadata (keywords, commands it references, target expertise level, community quality ratings). Computational mechanism (from the PATENT, not the paper -- the ACM DL full text returned HTTP 403, abstract only read): a relevance score per resource, computed from that context; the highest-scoring resources are selected and displayed in a dedicated help space, sized and positioned by relevance. Two decay/anti-repeat rules are specified: a newly displayed video's score is multiplied by a time-weighting factor starting above 1 (e.g. 1.2) and decreasing linearly to 0 after a set interval (e.g. five minutes), so it is guaranteed to be replaced; and any resource among the 50 most recently displayed may be excluded from relevance calculations until the user issues a new command, which ends the exclusion. User selections (clicks, rewinds, replays, bookmarks) raise that resource's future relevance score. The patent is US 2012/0259845 A1 (Matejka, Grossman, Fitzmaurice; published Thursday 11 October 2012), read in full. The paper (CHI 2011, Vancouver, pp. 2751-2760, DOI 10.1145/1978942.1979349) reports an experiment showing it supports finding more helpful information without harming productivity versus a traditional help condition -- abstract-level only, not verified against the full text. [C36501]

IP-QAT: In-Product Questions, Answers, & Tips (UIST 2011, Santa Barbara, pp. 175-184, DOI 10.1145/2047196.2047218; Matejka first author) is the same lineage from the other side: a community question-and-answer system integrated into the software application itself, context-aware so it shows relevant posts based on the user's recent activity, designed for minimal transaction costs so users post easily, with annotated images, file attachments, and tagging posts with relevant UI components; a cloud-based implementation released to 37 users for a 2-week field study, which increased user contributions and was rated more useful and easier to use than the existing commercial discussion board. Abstract-level only (ACM DL PDF also 403); the corresponding patent is US 9,141,253, granted Tuesday 22 September 2015. Artistic opportunity: the decay rule is the mechanism -- a displayed thing carries a score that falls linearly to zero over five minutes, and the fifty most recent things are barred from return. That is a complete, executable rule for attention: nothing is allowed to linger, and nothing is allowed to repeat until the user acts. [C36502]

**Takeaway:** Attention is a resource with a half-life, and the system should spend it on purpose. Build the decay, not the shelf. [C36503]

## M6 -- Trial and error in four phases (Supercharging Trial-and-Error, CHI 2022)

Subject: learning complex software by trying things, and the cost of trying. Visual principle: the paper's conceptual model splits a trial-and-error episode into four repeating phases -- Exploration ("Where do I need to go to do X?"), Execution, Assessment ("Is it what I want?"), Recovery ("Revert to a clean state") -- and names four challenges: C1 cannot find commands, C2 cannot operate commands, C3 suboptimal solutions, C4 costly recovery. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): three techniques, each an executable rule, implemented in Fusion 360. ToolTrack: every command carries a coverage level -- 0 never used, 1 tooltip opened at least once, 2 executed at least once -- and at level 2 the percentage of the command's parameters modified at least once; the interface marks level-0 commands with a large top-left yellow corner, level-1 with a small one, level-2 with nothing (a progress bar on hover). ToolTrip: a workflow is a titled sequence of commands with a description of its end goal; hovering a command shows up to three ToolTrips containing it, and following one pins a view listing its commands, each badged with its position number in the sequence. ToolTrips are generated automatically from 1,936 community screencasts (filtered from 73,573 public Fusion 360 screencasts: excluding bug reports, troubleshooting, untitled, older than 6 months, under 5 or over 30 commands), and suggested by a score: filter to trips containing the command under the cursor, then add the inverse frequency of each trip command that also appears in the user's last 5 executed commands (frequencies computed over the ToolTrip database), with a second variant adding the inverse frequency of trip commands the user has never explored, to surface novelty. ToolTaste: right-click any command -- even a disabled one -- and choose "Test on copy" (an in-memory copy of the current document) or "Test on example" (a curated example document), opening a green-tinted "[TEST]" tab where the user experiments freely, resets, and then merges the changes into the main document or discards them. (Masson, Vermeulen, Fitzmaurice, Matejka, CHI 2022, New Orleans, 13 pp., DOI 10.1145/3491102.3501895; Matejka last author.) [C36504]

Artistic opportunity: three portable rules. Coverage as a visible mark (a corner that shrinks as a thing is used) turns any collection into a record of exploration. Trips scored by inverse frequency against the last five actions turn a history into a suggestion for the next five. And "test on copy" is a sandbox with a merge -- the freedom to try without the cost of breaking the original, which is what makes trial-and-error affordable at all. A piece that charges for its experiments will be used cautiously; a piece that makes experiments free will be used boldly, and the boldness is the picture. [C36505]

**Takeaway:** Mark what is unused, suggest the next five steps, and make trying free. Build the sandbox, not the tutorial. [C36506]

## M7 -- One dot, one event (Video Lens, UIST 2014)

Subject: large video collections -- hundreds of clips, thousands of events -- explored as data, not watched as television. Visual principle: individual events (not videos) are the primary entity, with a constant "1 dot = 1 event" mapping across all views and a red dot for the event currently playing. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): each metadata attribute gets a Single-Attribute Controller (SAC, 20 px tall): dots positioned by attribute value, opacity dynamically lowered as point count rises; hovering a SAC highlights those events in every other view while fading the rest. A Multi-Attribute Grid maps attributes to x, y, colour and size by dragging SAC labels onto its dimensions; video timelines place events by timestamp, one per video. Playback uses two overlaid players: while Player 1 plays clip C_n, Player 2 pre-loads C_{n+1} so the next clip starts with no perceptible delay; auto-advance plays each event for its own duration, or 1 s / 3 s from its start. Evaluation: 12 baseball-fanatic participants, 18 tasks on a 42-inch display; 7 of 18 tasks completed in a median under 30 s, 16 of 18 in under 1 minute; all 12 found the system enjoyable, 11 of 12 said they would use it if available. (Matejka, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2014, Honolulu, DOI 10.1145/2642918.2647366.) Artistic opportunity: the dot is the unit of the archive, and the cross-view highlight is the query -- hover one strip and the same events light up everywhere else. Any piece that accumulates events can be read this way: the dots are the history, the strips are the attributes, and the highlight is the question. [C36507]

**Takeaway:** One dot per event, and one hover to ask the same question of every view. Build the dots, not the player. [C36508]

## M8 -- The record that follows the body (Chronicle, UIST 2010; Waken, UIST 2012; Swifter, CHI 2013)

Subject: the user's own past, made navigable. Visual principle: Chronicle captures document workflow histories so the user can scrub and replay their own past actions; Waken reverse-engineers usage information and interface structure from software videos, recognising UI components and activities from screen-captured video, with a player that lets the user interact directly with the UI components shown in the video; Swifter hands recorded video back to the hand as a thumbnail grid for direct frame selection, outperforming the earlier Swift technique by a factor of up to 48% in a scene-locating task (abstract claim). Computational mechanism (ABSTRACT/TITLE LEVEL ONLY -- none of the three was read end to end this run; no mechanism detail beyond the abstracts is asserted here): the shared rule is that a recording stops being a playback and becomes a surface -- scrubbed, clicked, queried -- so the past follows the present instead of the present following the past. The doorway's third branch lives here: where YouMove's guidance fades on a schedule, these systems hand the record back as a thing to operate. (Chronicle: Grossman, Matejka, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2010, DOI 10.1145/1866029.1866054. Waken: Banovic, Grossman, Matejka, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2012, DOI 10.1145/2380116.2380129. Swifter: Matejka, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2013, Paris, DOI 10.1145/2470654.2466149.) [C36509]

**Takeaway:** A recording is not something you watch; it is something you operate. Build the scrubber, not the replay. [C36510]

## Cross-cutting mechanisms

Four rules recur across the whole catalogue, each stated as an executable rule rather than a theme: (1) The prediction is always a number. MOUP = 40%, a cosine similarity, an inverse frequency, a worst-joint error in metres, a coverage level 0/1/2, a relevance score decaying linearly to zero -- never an atmosphere, never a vibe. Executable form: `score = f(stream)`, stated in one line, with the threshold named. (2) Surface ambiently, never interrupt. The palette sits in unused space beside the command line; the heatmap appears on demand at 50% opacity; the help lives on a secondary display; the trip appears on hover. Executable form: the prediction is visible in the periphery and costs nothing to ignore. (3) Decay on purpose. Recommendations vanish once used; videos decay to zero in five minutes; heatmaps fade in five seconds; guidance withdraws on a five-stage schedule. Executable form: every displayed prediction carries a lifetime, and the lifetime is part of the rule. (4) The record follows the user. Command histories, clicks, screencasts, workflow histories -- the training set is always the user's own past, and the system re-reads it after every action. ## Aesthetic logic

Matejka's systems are never about the device. The scatter plot is a field of dots; the interesting object is the 40% that stays constant while the dots multiply. The palette is a list of buttons; the interesting object is the cosine that ranks them. The heatmap is a coloured overlay; the interesting object is the rule that dims a click by the size of the region it lands in. The video shelf is a set of thumbnails; the interesting object is the score that falls to zero in five minutes so the next thing can arrive. In every case the artefact is a container for a rule, and the rule is small enough to state in one sentence: hold MOUP at 40%; score by cosine similarity to the commands you have used; dim by region size; decay to zero in five minutes; commit on five events or one second. The visual language follows from the rule rather than preceding it: dots that thin as they multiply, corners that shrink as tools are used, a shelf that empties itself on a clock, a heatmap that is blue where you have been and orange where everyone has been. Nothing is decorated; everything that is visible is the state of a prediction. That is the aesthetic to carry into the pieces: the picture should be the prediction's current state, and nothing else. [C36512]
