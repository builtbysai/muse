# Thomas P. Moran: Study Notes

**Site:** Thomas P. Moran is an American human-computer interaction researcher whose fifty-year arc runs from the founding theory of HCI at Xerox PARC, through hypermedia and tailorability systems, to media spaces and activity-centred collaboration at IBM. He took a B.Architecture at the University of Detroit and a Ph.D. in computer science at Carnegie Mellon (CMU lists him among its 1974 SCS alumni; he joined PARC in 1974), and with Stuart Card and Allen Newell formed the Applied Information-Processing Psychology Project at PARC in 1974. He was Principal Scientist and manager of the User Interface and the Collaborative Systems Areas at Xerox PARC, 1974-2001; founding Director of Xerox EuroPARC, Cambridge, England, 1986-90 (the lab was announced in November 1986, established in 1987, and dedicated in June 1988, per Moran's own CHI '89 paper -- do not state a single founding year flatly); and later Distinguished Engineer at IBM's Almaden Research Center, San Jose, California, where he ran the Unified Activity Management programme. With Card and Newell he wrote *The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction* (1983), the book that introduced GOMS, the Model Human Processor, and the Keystroke-Level Model. He founded the journal *Human-Computer Interaction* in 1984 and edited it for 30 years. He is an ACM Fellow (2003; the year rests on the Wikipedia fellows list cross-checked against his article, the ACM Digital Library profile excerpt read this run not displaying Fellow status), a member of the CHI Academy (inducted 2001, first cohort), and recipient of the ACM SIGCHI Lifetime Achievement Award (2004). An AAAS Fellowship in 2008 is reported by Wikipedia only -- no second source was found, so do not assert it without one. His systems work includes NoteCards, Buttons, the RAVE media space, the Tivoli electronic whiteboard, and the ActivityExplorer. His birth date and birthplace are not established in any source read; do not assert either -- searches return only the painter Thomas Moran (1837-1926) and the businessman Thomas J. Moran (1952-2018), both different people. [C37131]

**Relevance:** Moran helps investigate the cost-of-a-method question: given a task, what is the cheapest sequence of methods to perform it, and can each method's cost be predicted from its elementary operators before it is executed? The evidence is the 1983 book's machinery -- the Model Human Processor with published cycle times, the Keystroke-Level Model with six priced operators, and GOMS selection rules that choose among methods by predicted cost -- together with three systems in which methods are explicit objects: NoteCards' typed links, Buttons' executable marks, and RAVE's five graded degrees of presence. [C37132]

**Doorway:** The 457th doorway. Baudel asked: when the instrument itself decides what counts as a command, who holds the instrument -- and what happens when both hands, or both parties, hold one? Moran answers from the model side: a command has a price in operator time, methods are ranked by predicted cost, and a selection rule picks the cheapest -- the instrument is held by whoever writes the rule. The open question carried forward is: when the cheapest method always wins, where does the expensive method survive -- the one chosen for meaning rather than speed -- and who keeps it alive? [C37133]

**Depth:** Three research legs. Read end to end: Card, Moran & Newell, "The Keystroke-Level Model for User Performance Time with Interactive Systems," *Communications of the ACM* 23(7), July 1980, pp. 396-410 (full text via the CMU Newell archive scan); MacLean, Carter, Lovstrand & Moran, "User-Tailorable Systems: Pressing the Issues with Buttons," CHI '90, pp. 175-182 (full text, 780 lines, scanned proceedings); Gaver, Moran et al., "Realizing a Video Environment: EuroPARC's RAVE System," CHI '92, pp. 27-35 (full text, 819 lines). ## M1 -- The Model Human Processor: three clocks and three memories (1983)

The Model Human Processor (MHP) is the book's account of the user as hardware. Three processors -- perceptual, cognitive, motor -- each with a cycle time; three memory stores -- visual image store, auditory store, working memory -- plus long-term memory; each memory with a capacity and a decay half-life. The published nominal values, with the book's ranges in brackets: perceptual processor cycle Tp 100 ms [50-200]; cognitive processor cycle Tc 70 ms [25-170]; motor processor cycle Tm 70 ms [30-100]; eye movement 230 ms [70-700]. The visual image store holds 17 letters [7-17] with a decay half-life of 200 ms [90-1000]; the auditory store holds 5 letters [4.4-6.2] with a half-life of 1500 ms [900-3500]; working memory holds 7 chunks effectively [5-9], 3 chunks purely [2.5-4.2], with a decay half-life of 7 s for 3 chunks [5-34] and 73 s for 1 chunk [73-226]. Two principles keep the numbers honest: the perceptual rate varies inversely with stimulus intensity, and the cognitive rate shortens with effort and practice. And the book refuses to quote a point prediction: it runs three versions of the model -- Slowman (all parameters at worst performance), Fastman (best), Middleman (nominal) -- so a prediction is properly a range, not a number. The worked examples in Chapter 2 are the model's party tricks: apparent motion needs a frame rate above 1/Tp, 10 frames per second nominal, 20 for Fastman -- "in general accord with the frame rates commonly employed for motion picture cameras, 18 fps silent, 24 fps sound"; simple reaction time is Tp + Tc + Tm = 100 + 70 + 70 = 240 ms; a choice reaction-time example totals 310 ms; clicks at 10 per second are heard separately, at 30 per second three clicks inside one 100 ms cycle fuse into a single percept. One discrepancy is flagged, not smoothed over: the MIT 6.813 course page lists the Tc and Tm ranges swapped relative to the book (Tc [30-100], Tm [25-170]); the book and Wikipedia agree with each other, the MIT page is the outlier, and its swapped ranges must not be used. [C37135]

The MHP is not a theory of what people do. It is a parts list with tolerances, and the tolerances are the point: a system built for Middleman fails Slowman, and the book insists you say so. The three versions are a discipline against the single-number lie -- every prediction carries its own error bars, and the error bars are part of the prediction. [C37136]

**Takeaway:** A user is three clocks and three leaky stores, and an honest prediction is a range between Slowman and Fastman. [C37137]

## M2 -- The Keystroke-Level Model: six operators, one sum (1980)

The Keystroke-Level Model (KLM) is the book's most audacious simplification, published three years before the book in *Communications of the ACM* 23(7), July 1980, pp. 396-410, and read end to end this run. Its core idea, in the paper's own words: "the time for an expert to do a task on an interactive system is simply the time it takes to do the keystrokes" -- then generalized to six operators. Execution time Texecute = TK + TP + TH + TD + TM + TR. The operator times, from the paper's Figure 1: K, keystroke or button press, by typing skill -- 0.08 s for a 135 wpm best typist, 0.12 for a 90 wpm good typist, 0.20 for a 55 wpm average skilled typist, 0.28 for a 40 wpm average non-secretary, 0.50 for random letters, 0.75 for complex codes, 1.20 for the worst typist on an unfamiliar keyboard; P, point with the mouse, 1.10 s constant (Fitts's law form tP = 0.8 + 0.1 x log2(d/s + 0.5), range 0.8-1.5 s; the button press after pointing is a separate K of 0.2 s); H, home the hands on keyboard or device, 0.40 s; D, draw nD segments of total length lD cm, 0.9 x nD + 0.16 x lD (restricted to the mouse, cursor constrained to a 0.56 cm grid); M, mentally prepare, 1.35 s; R(t), system response, t seconds, counted only when the user must wait, overlapping with a following M by up to 1.35 s (only the non-overlapping part counts). Note the trap: some secondary sources use M = 1.2 s; the 1980 paper's published value is 1.35 s, and the Wikipedia KLM page explicitly notes the difference -- use 1.35 as the Card-Moran-Newell value. [C37138]

The scope is stated bluntly in the paper and must be carried with the model: it predicts execution time of unit tasks only, not acquisition time, for expert users, routine tasks, error-free execution. A unit task has two parts -- acquisition (building a mental representation), which takes about 2-3 seconds when reading from a marked-up manuscript and 5-30 seconds when generated in the mind for routine design, longer for creative composition; and execution, which "rarely takes over 20 seconds." The M-placement heuristics (Figure 2, read in full) are the model's real machinery: Rule 0, insert M before all Ks not part of argument strings and before all Ps that select commands; Rule 1, delete M if the following operator is fully anticipated (PMK becomes PK); Rule 2, if a string of MKs is one cognitive unit, delete all Ms but the first; Rule 3, delete M before a redundant terminator K; Rule 4, delete M before a K terminating a constant string, keep it before a K terminating a variable string. The optimality assumption (section 3.2) is the seed of everything that follows: "expert users will, in general, use the most efficient method, i.e., the method taking the least time" -- so the model predicts the fastest method will be used, and experts "can quickly (in about a second) choose the appropriate method." Validation: tested against data from 28 users, on 10 systems, over 14 task types, with a root-mean-square prediction error of 21% for individual tasks and "much better for collections of tasks." The worked example: replacing a 5-letter word, the POET line-editor method predicted 8.4 seconds against the DISPED display-editor method's 6.2 seconds, a difference of about 2 seconds. The paper also admits what the model ignores: up to a fourth of an expert's time can be spent correcting errors. [C37139]

**Takeaway:** Six priced operators and five placement rules turn any routine task into a sum -- and the cheapest sum is the method the expert will use. [C37140]

## M3 -- GOMS: goals, methods, and the rule that chooses (1983)

GOMS is the book's full model, and the KLM is its simplest member. The definition, as the book states it: a set of Goals, a set of Operators, a set of Methods for achieving the goals, and a set of Selection rules for choosing among competing methods for goals. Goals are hierarchical symbolic structures, running from WRITE-RESEARCH-PAPER down to DELETE-WORD; operators are atomic perceptual, motor, or cognitive acts with assigned execution times; methods are learned procedures, sequences of subgoals and operators; selection rules are conditionals -- the textbook example is copy-and-paste: if the hands are on the keyboard, use the keyboard method, else use the mouse method. The book's canonical example, Chapter 5, is GOAL EDIT-MANUSCRIPT, decomposed into EDIT-UNIT-TASK repeated, then ACQUIRE-UNIT-TASK and EXECUTE-UNIT-TASK, then MODIFY-TEXT with a selection between methods -- Use-QS-Method versus Use-LF-Method for locating a line, Use-S-Command versus Use-M-Command for modifying text -- then VERIFY-EDIT. Five variants grew from the original: KLM (no goals, no selection rules, M more aggregated at 1.35 s); CMN-GOMS (the original); NGOMSL (adds learning-time prediction); CPM-GOMS (processors run in parallel, modelling overlapping actions); SGOMS (planning units, interruptions). GOMS was used to evaluate the Xerox Star interface. [C37141]

What matters for this project is not the acronym but the shape of the reasoning: a task is a tree of goals, each goal has more than one method, each method has a price, and a rule -- written down, inspectable, arguable -- picks one. The selection rule is where the human lives in the model: it is the only place a preference can enter, and it enters as a condition on the world, not as a feeling. [C37142]

**Takeaway:** A task is a tree of goals, each with priced methods, and a written rule picks the cheapest. [C37143]

## M4 -- Command Language Grammar and the mapping problem (1981, 1983)

Before the book, Moran's solo work asked what a command language is made of. Command Language Grammar (CLG), *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies* 15(1), 1981, pp. 3-50, describes an interface in three components and six levels: Conceptual (task level, semantic level), Communication (syntactic level, interaction level), Physical (spatial level, device level). The psychological view treats CLG as a description of the user's mental model, and the emphasis is on the whole: "it is the effect of the user interface as a whole (all levels combined) which constitutes the user's model." Two years later, "Getting into a system" (CHI '83, pp. 45-49) posed the external-internal task mapping problem: tasks in the external world must be reformulated into the system's internal concepts before the system can be used, and the analysis is a mapping between an external task space and an internal task space. The mismatch between the two spaces drives learnability, and an efficiency metric falls out of it -- essentially the number of device actions required to achieve one domain-level goal. [C37144]

CLG and ETIT are the same question from two sides: the grammar asks what the system is made of, the mapping asks what it costs to get from what you want to what the system understands. The mapping's efficiency metric -- device actions per domain goal -- is the KLM's cost idea applied to the gap between intention and mechanism, and it is the most portable number in Moran's whole body of work: count how many machine acts one human goal requires, and you have measured the interface. [C37145]

**Takeaway:** One human goal costs as many machine acts as the mapping demands -- count them, and you have measured the interface. [C37146]

## M5 -- NoteCards: two primitives (1983-1988)

NoteCards is the first system in this study, and the most radical in its restraint. Developed at Xerox PARC by Randall Trigg, Frank Halasz, and Thomas Moran; the first successful prototype was completed by Halasz in November 1983 (Wikipedia dates the system's development to 1984 -- both are consistent if 1983 is the prototype and 1984 the system, so do not state a single "created in" year without that qualification). The paper is Halasz, Moran & Trigg, "NoteCards in a nutshell," CHI+GI '87, Toronto, April 5-9, 1987, pp. 45-52; its full text is paywalled at ACM, but its substance is restated in Halasz's "Reflections on NoteCards" (*Communications of the ACM* 31(7), July 1988, pp. 836-852), read to line 1225 of 2320 this run, and in the Venue NoteCards User's Guide, Release 2.0, August 1991. The system has exactly two primitive constructs, and everything else is built from them: notecards and links. A notecard is the electronic generalization of the 3x5 paper card, containing an arbitrary amount of one editable "substance" -- text, structured drawing, bitmap image, with new card types including animation cards supported; every card has a title, is displayed in its own window, and can be edited, but its contents cannot scroll. A link is a typed, directional connection between a source card and a destination card, the type a user-chosen label naming the relationship. The anchoring is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is the design: a link is anchored by an icon at a specific location inside the substance of its source card, but anchored to its destination card as a whole. Clicking the link icon traverses it -- retrieves the destination card and displays it. In the paper's worked example, cards typically carried "See" or "Unspecified" links placed at the end of the card text, preceded by the word "See." [C37147]

On top of the two primitives sit browsers and fileboxes. A browser is a notecard containing a structural diagram of a network of notecards, computed for the user by the system: cards appear as boxed titles, links as edges, and different dashing styles distinguish different link types; the boxed titles are themselves traversable link icons. Browsers support two levels of editing: edit the underlying network structure through operations on nodes and edges in the browser, or add and delete nodes and edges in the diagram without changing the underlying structures. A browser is itself a card and can be linked to and filed. A filebox is a specialized card in which other cards -- including other fileboxes -- can be filed, forming a hierarchy that is a directed acyclic graph; every notecard, including fileboxes, must be filed in one or more fileboxes. Navigation (following links card to card) is the primary means of access, overview browsers the second, a limited search facility (match a string in title or text) the third. The system was fully integrated into the Xerox Lisp environment, with a programmer's interface of over 100 Lisp functions for creating new card types, writing programs that monitor or process a network, and integrating Lisp programs into NoteCards. By 1988 it had over 70 registered users within Xerox, plus users at universities, government, and industry. The worked example is a history graduate student researching and writing a 25-page paper, who kept a browser of his filebox hierarchy on screen at all times: 40 fileboxes containing 268 non-filebox cards, cards averaging about 100 words, about half with links embedded. [C37148]

Halasz's "Reflections" is honest about the three places the system strains, and the strains are the interesting part. Issue 1, search and query: queries could serve as a filtering mechanism, displaying only the aspects of the network that match -- but "the NoteCards browser currently operates in this manner, but only with respect to a very limited set of structure queries," so the general filter was proposed, not implemented. Issue 2, composites: users improvised composition with "head cards" -- a legal "CaseCluster" of 10 cards (8 content cards, 1 browser, 1 empty head card gathering the other nine, links to the case pointing at the head card) -- but browsers cannot display a CaseCluster as a single entity inheriting its components' links; they show either only the head card (hiding component links) or all ten cards (hiding the grouping). Fileboxes have a related flaw: filing is implemented with ordinary reference links, but users think of it as an inclusion (part/whole) relation, causing confusion. Issue 3, premature organization: the system forces users to segment ideas into cards, title them, and file them up front, before they understand the material; experienced users worked around it by keeping the whole idea stream in one text card, or by filing everything in one filebox and using a sketch card to organize cards into piles by similarity, rearranging the piles freely until stable. Issues 4-7 (lines 1225-2320 of the text) were not read this run; they concern versioning, collaborative work, and further issues. [C37149]

**Takeaway:** Two primitives -- a card and a typed link -- and the whole system is what you can build, filter, and file from them. [C37150]

## M6 -- Buttons: the gentle slope (1990)

Buttons is the second system, and the one that treats the user as a tailor rather than a programmer. MacLean, Carter, Lovstrand & Moran, "User-Tailorable Systems: Pressing the Issues with Buttons," CHI '90, Seattle, April 1-5, 1990, pp. 175-182, read end to end this run (full text via a scanned-proceedings mirror; the ACM full text is paywalled). A button is a screen object in Xerox Lisp that "looks pressable" and, when pressed with a mouse click, carries out an action. Buttons can live on the desktop or inside documents, can be sent by email, and can be moved freely around the screen; they can be used with no understanding of the encapsulated action. The architecture is a simple object-oriented framework with an Active Property Engine (in the spirit of LOOPS' Active Values): properties may have agents -- procedures activated when operations are performed on the property, setting or getting its value, editing or copying it -- handling computed values, side effects, dependencies, indirection, lazy evaluation, class abstraction, and delegation. Buttons were originally built into Xerox Lisp as part of the Rooms system (Henderson & Card); Austin Henderson wrote the original Buttons software, and the CHI '90 architecture is a considerable extension, upwards compatible with Rooms buttons. A correction to the brief: the follow-up "EmbeddedButtons" paper (*ACM Transactions on Information Systems* 10(4), October 1992, pp. 381-407) is by Bier & Goodisman, not Moran -- it supports turning existing document objects into buttons, so buttons inherit all the attributes of normal document objects. [C37151]

The paper's argument is the "gentle slope" of tailoring techniques, ordered by the skill required, and it is the most humane ladder in the HCI literature. First, move and place buttons: a remarkable amount of tailoring is done simply by "begging, stealing or borrowing" appropriate buttons and placing them in strategic places. Second, situated creation: capture relevant aspects of the system state into a button for later re-use, without programming -- the user does the task once manually, then encapsulates the relevant parts. Examples: a button "containing" a long phrase that must be repeated in a report, kept for a few hours then discarded; the window system modified with a "buttonize" option, where selecting it produces a button that recreates that window (a text window's button recreates the window without knowing the file's location; a file-browser window's button re-examines a directory without re-entering the file pattern and viewing parameters). Unlike programming-by-example, the route the user took is not preserved -- the computer regenerates the state in the most efficient way. Third, copy and specialize: instead of class inheritance, copy the whole button and change a few details -- a prototype approach that makes buttons independent, conceptually simple objects, emailable without the recipient needing a class hierarchy. Fourth, tailoring-oriented attributes and parameters: each button exposes its own parameters, edited with the editor appropriate to the type -- a file-browser button has parameters for file pattern, search depth, and info shown, and copying it and changing the pattern to "CHI", depth to "1", and shown info to file size yields a new button. Fifth, modify the Lisp code inside a button: the code is a small encapsulated fragment, so a "tinkerer" with limited Lisp can change it -- one non-Lisp-programmer researcher received audio-visual connection buttons by email, modified the internal Lisp to make connections relevant to himself, and passed them on. Sixth, arbitrary Lisp, for the experienced programmer. The paper frames the population as workers on the plains (no expectation of control), tinkerers in the foothills, programmers on the peaks, and adds a "handyman" role -- a team member working alongside users, seeding their environments with buttons and bridging to programmers. The four "workers" in the study were EuroPARC administrative staff with no tailoring experience; one admin's weekly calendar distribution (email to some people, nearest printer for others, hard copies for others) collapsed into a single-click button, and all admin staff shared a button to add an item to the weekly meeting agenda. Users organized buttons by graphic image and spatial location ("I like my buttons to look different so I don't have to read them") and treated them as "screen furnishings" or "wallpaper"; early on they were "not my personal buttons," "sewn to the screen," and later "I don't know what I'd do without my Buttons," "Buttons are my friends, always there..." One user whose system was accidentally modified compared it to her house being ransacked by burglars. The paper draws its distinctions sharply: Xerox Viewpoint's CUSP buttons exist only inside documents and require the CUSP language; HyperCard buttons are for building information structures and cannot reach into other applications; Buttons live anywhere and, because every Xerox Lisp application shares Lisp as its underlying language, can modify the behavior of other applications. [C37152]

**Takeaway:** A button is a captured state you can press, copy, and pass on -- tailoring as a slope, not a cliff. [C37153]

## M7 -- RAVE: five degrees of presence (1992)

RAVE is the third system, and the one that makes presence a costed choice. Gaver, Moran, MacLean, Lovstrand, Dourish, Carter & Buxton, "Realizing a Video Environment: EuroPARC's RAVE System," CHI '92, 3-7 May 1992, pp. 27-35, DOI 10.1145/142750.142754, read in full this run. RAVE is the Ravenscroft Audio Video Environment at EuroPARC, Ravenscroft House, 61 Regent Street, Cambridge -- about 30 staff, 27 rooms and 5 open areas on 4 floors, every room wired with audio and video cables to a central switch plus digital networks, each room with an audio-video "node" (camera, monitor, microphone, speakers) users could move and switch on or off, all connections completely computer-controlled. A media space, the paper defines it, is "a computer-controlled network of audio-video equipment used to support collaboration." The design framework has two dimensions of shared work -- degree of engagement (how much shared focus) against amount of planning (spontaneous versus planned) -- and four landmarks: general awareness (pervasive knowing who is around, what they are doing, how busy); serendipitous communication (unplanned interaction leading to exchange); division of labour (split task, coordinated separately); focussed collaboration (planned, close work on a shared task). The design goal was fluid movement between these, not just support for focussed work. The lineage runs back to the first media space at PARC in the mid-1980s, built by Bob Stults and Steve Harrison (office-to-office always-on audio and video, growing into a switched network Palo Alto-Portland). [C37154]

The system itself is five generic RAVE Buttons -- tailorable on-screen buttons, which the paper notes "evolved together" with the account of collaboration. Background: the default view of a public area on your monitor (for example the largest public space), like having the common area outside your door without the noise, maintaining general awareness. Sweep: short (about 1 second) one-way connections to various nodes, customizable to all or a subset, used to find out who is around and what they are doing. Glance: a single 3-second one-way connection to a selected node, used to check if a person is in and whether they are busy -- explicitly likened to "walking by somebody's door and glancing in." Vphone: two-way audio and video, the recipient must explicitly accept, closest to a telephone call, for short focussed conversations. Office share: functionally identical to vphone (the same two-way connection) but intended to last hours, days, or even months -- "sharing an office," with audio volume controllable and a small video image, so the other's presence "allows but does not demand social engagement." The paper stresses that vphone and office share differ solely in the intention with which the connection is made -- "a good example of interface tools which emerged to control our system in terms of users' tasks, rather than technological functionality." [C37155]

Privacy in RAVE is four separable facets, and the trade-off between them is the paper's sharpest argument: control over who can see or hear you; knowledge of when somebody is seeing or hearing you; knowledge of the intention behind the connection; and avoiding connections being intrusive on your work. Explicit permission for every connection would give control, but the requests themselves would be intrusive -- so the initial protection was social convention and trust ("willingly naive"), plus physical control (turn the camera to the wall, microphone off). Godard (Dourish, 1991, EuroPARC Tech. Report EPC-91-134; the report itself was not read this run, the account here is the RAVE paper's) is a software layer over the iiif patchbay that mediates all connection requests, so control operates at the service level (glance versus vphone) rather than the plug level: users set per-person, per-service permissions via control panels, Godard records pre-existing connections so they can be restored, and it generates the connection events that feed notifications. The privacy feedback is auditory, and the sound design is exact: when a glance is made, Godard plays a sound at the target's location several seconds before the connection -- by default a door opening, a door closing on break; a knock or telephone bell means a vphone request, door sounds mean glances, footsteps mean sweeps, a camera whir means framegrabber access; sounds are shaped to rise slowly in loudness to avoid startling. The rationale given: sound informs without requiring spatial attention or symmetry, and caricatures of natural sounds are intuitive and memorable. Khronika (Lovstrand, 1991) is the event notification service built on three entities -- events (class, start time, duration, hierarchically classified, for example "professional," "electronic," "entertainment"), daemons (user-created sets of constraints watching for event types), and notifications (email, on-screen message, synthesized speech, or non-speech audio); a daemon watching for seminar events in the conference room containing "RAVE" notifies 5 minutes before, and about 50 sounds a day were requested from Khronika. Polyscope distributed digitized still images within the building every 5 minutes, 200x150 pixels, no grey scale, with a simple animation facility looping a few successive images (jerky; one researcher "periodically transmogrify[ied] into Elvis Presley"); clicking ("buttoning") an image opened a pop-up menu to initiate a glance or vphone. Portholes (Dourish & Bly, CHI '92) ran between EuroPARC and PARC -- about 6,000 miles -- so researchers who had never been co-present "speak of 'knowing' one another." Both present several remote locations simultaneously, giving passive awareness without setting up links. Related systems named in the paper: iiif (the integrated interactive intermedia facility, the patchbay, Buxton & Moran 1990), the ShrEdit collaborative text editor, and export experiments including an audio-video link in a London architecture firm and the WAVE project (Welwyn-Venray, England-Netherlands). [C37156]

**Takeaway:** Presence is a ladder with five rungs, each with a price in duration, direction, and permission -- and the same wire means something different by intention. [C37157]

## M8 -- Design rationale and the workaday world (1989-1991)

Two later pieces of Moran's PARC work generalize the programme beyond single systems. Design rationale, with MacLean, Young, Bellotti and Moran: "Questions, Options, and Criteria: Elements of Design Space Analysis," *Human-Computer Interaction* 6(3-4), 1991, pp. 201-250 (abstract read; full text behind paywalls), preceded by MacLean, Young & Moran, "Design rationale: The argument behind the artifact," CHI '89, pp. 247-252. QOC is a semiformal notation for representing the design space around an artifact: Questions identify key design issues, Options provide possible answers, Criteria assess and compare the options, plus justifications (consistency, models and analogies, data and theory). The Workaday World, with Anderson: Moran & Anderson, "The Workaday World as a Paradigm for CSCW Design," CSCW '90, pp. 381-393 (secondary summaries read; full text not read this run). The paradigm treats technology, sociality, and work practice as three inseparable, dialectically related aspects of everyday working life; technology is not central but must be put "in proper perspective," and design should support, enhance, and sustain existing work practices rather than center the tool. It grew out of the EuroPARC media-space work -- RAVE was the laboratory, the workaday world the lesson drawn from it. [C37158]

QOC is the book's selection rule turned into a notation: the options are the methods, the criteria are the prices, and the argument is the record of why one was chosen. The workaday world is the same move in the other direction: the model stops pretending the task is the whole world, and admits the sociality and the practice the task sits inside. [C37159]

**Takeaway:** Record the argument, not just the choice -- and design for the workday the tool sits inside, not the tool. [C37160]

## M9 -- Activity as the unit: the IBM years (2005-2012)

Moran's IBM programme, Unified Activity Management (UAM), argues that collaborative-work support should be organized around an explicit computational representation of activity -- "personal objectives or social agreements to collaborate" -- sitting between concrete actions (email, call, edit) and preplanned processes. The position paper is "Unified Activity Management: Explicitly Representing Activity in Work-Support Systems," ECSCW 2005 workshop, Paris, 20 September 2005, read in full this run; the representation is meant to be generic enough to unify the different tools people use. The programme's publications, verified via IBM Research pages and the ACM Digital Library profile: "Business activity patterns: A new model for collaborative business applications," *IBM Systems Journal*, 8 December 2006 (abstract read: the vision of UAM, activities, activity-centred computing, activity patterns); "Collaborative activity management: Organizing documents for collective action," SIGDOC 2007 (abstract read: the ActivityExplorer project -- shared-document collections, fine-grained access control, persistent communications, real-time status); U.S. patent application US20060168550A1, filed 21 January 2005, published 27 July 2006, inventors including Michael Muller and Thomas Moran (status: abandoned). Later publications through 2012, per the ACM profile (88 publications, 1980-2012, 5,211 citations): "Fitting an activity-centric system into an ecology of workplace tools" (CHI '10, with Balakrishnan & Matthews); "Collaboration personas" (CHI '11, with Matthews, Whittaker & Yuen); "Topika: integrating collaborative sharing with email" (CHI '11); "Smarter social collaboration at IBM research" (CSCW '11); and his latest listed publication, "Productive Interrelationships between Collaborative Groups Ease the Challenges of Dynamic and Multi-Teaming" (*CSCW* journal, October 2012, with Matthews, Whittaker, Helsley & Judge). No retirement date was found, and no publications after 2012 appear in the ACM record seen this run -- do not assert a retirement year. [C37161]

UAM is the MHP's question asked at the scale of a working life: what is the unit, and what does it cost to hold it? The answer is activity -- bigger than an action, smaller than a process, the thing a person would actually name if you asked what they are doing. Forty years after the book, Moran is still counting the same thing: the price of getting from intention to effect, now measured across tools instead of inside one. [C37162]

**Takeaway:** The unit is the activity -- between the action and the process -- and the system should hold it explicitly. [C37163]

## The arc: from price to intention

Moran's arc is a single question asked at four scales. In the book, the question is what a command costs, and the answer is a sum of operator times. In NoteCards, the question is what a connection means, and the answer is the type written on the link. In Buttons, the question is what a user can do without programming, and the answer is a slope from borrowing to writing. In RAVE, the question is what presence costs, and the answer is a ladder of durations and permissions -- where the same wire, vphone or office share, means two different things by intention alone. The models price commands; the systems show that price is not the whole story. A link type, a button's captured state, a glance's knock before it arrives: these are costs the ledger does not capture, and they are the ones that decide whether the system is livable. [C37164]

## The aesthetic logic

Moran's aesthetic is accounting. Every act has a price, every price is a choice, and the beauty is in the ledger made visible: a drawing that shows its own operator trace, a triptych that shows the spread between Slowman and Fastman instead of pretending to one true number, a field that fuses into motion only above a threshold you can feel. Slowness is not a mood here; it is a cost, measured in milliseconds and seconds, and the honest piece shows the cost rather than hiding it. The visual language that follows is spare: marks that arrive at a rate, gaps where the mind prepares, links whose dashing says what kind of relationship they carry, buttons that are plainly pressable because they are plainly actions. Nothing decorative, because decoration is a cost with no method behind it. [C37165]

## Rejections

Three candidate directions were considered and not queued. A literal GOMS diagram piece -- the goal tree drawn as a tree -- was rejected: a viewpoint, not a mechanism; the tree is a picture of the model, not a system that runs it. A KLM stopwatch piece -- a meter that counts the predicted seconds of the viewer's own actions -- was rejected: a meter, not a system; it measures without making anything. The browser-as-live-filter idea (one graph, two renderers, a filter predicate over link type) was HELD, not queued: it is a real mechanism, but it is a second view of the typed-link traversal piece and should be folded into that piece's development rather than built separately. The pile-sorting idea (premature organization: a stream segmented into piles by similarity, piles rearranged as new material arrives) was HELD for the same reason -- it is a second mechanism from the same NoteCards issue and deserves its own study of the filing problem before it is built. The Khronika awareness idea (low-resolution portholes refreshed every five minutes, daemons firing notifications on matching events) was HELD: the deliberate poverty of the signal is a real aesthetic, but its mechanism overlaps the presence-ladder piece and it needs a distinct computational question of its own.
