# Sebastien Bourdon: Study Notes

**Site:** Sebastien Bourdon (2 February 1616, Montpellier -- 8 May 1671, Paris), French painter and etcher; son of a Montpellier glass painter, apprentice in Paris at seven, bambocciante in Rome, painter of the 1643 Notre-Dame May, co-founder of the Academie Royale in 1648, and from 1652 first court painter to Queen Christina of Sweden in Stockholm -- the Frenchman who displaced the Dutchmen at her court, fixed her public image in two portraits, and talked her out of giving away the Correggios. He returned to Paris in 1653/54, became rector of the Academie, painted the Fall of Simon Magus for his native Montpellier, lectured at the Academie in his last years, and died in Paris at 55 with the Deification of Hercules unfinished on the easel.

## The life

Sebastien Bourdon was born in Montpellier on 2 February 1616, baptised on 10 February, the son of Marin Bourdon, a painter and glazier, and Jeanne Gaultiere, daughter of a master goldsmith. The family was of modest means and Reformed Protestant -- not Lutheran, as one modern page has it (see C1242). Montpellier in 1622 resisted a royal siege under the Prince de Conde and surrendered to Louis XIII's army when Bourdon was six. At seven he was sent to Paris as an apprentice painter, 1623-1630, and left at about fourteen. The master's identity is not firmly established: one modern page names "Jean Barthelemy" ("often identified as"), and Chalmers calls him only "a very indifferent painter" (see C1243). From about 1630 to 1634 he worked in Bordeaux and Toulouse, details scarce; Chalmers adds a short army enlistment in Paris, ended when a captain recognised his talent, discharged him and funded his travel to Rome.

Rome is the first disputed date. Older sources give arrival in 1634 at eighteen, staying three years; Bosquet's archival research (cited by Oberlin) documents Bourdon in France until 1636 and in Rome later that year, fleeing in 1638 (see C1235). The study uses 1636-38. In Rome he did not join the French circle around Poussin. He fell in with the Bamboccianti, followers of Pieter van Laer ("Il Bamboccio") and Jan Miel, and painted small-scale bambocciate -- military bivouacs, travellers at rest beside Italianate ruins. Only about a dozen bambocciate by Bourdon are known. He also worked for a dealer making copies from the old masters, and studied Poussin, Claude Lorrain and Caravaggio. The Encampment (W01, c. 1636-38, Oberlin, round, 56.6 cm diameter) is the surviving document of the Roman years: two men playing mora before a tent, a urinating horse copied directly from van Laer's etching Pissing Horse. He was forced to flee Rome in 1638, fearing denunciation to the Inquisition for his Reformed faith after a quarrel with a fellow painter -- the Musee protestant names Louis de Boullogne the elder as the man who threatened him and pressed him to renounce his faith; another page says only "allegedly by a fellow French artist, possibly Louis de Boullogne the Elder". Via Venice (Venetian colourism; Correggio and the Carracci in Emilia) and northern Italy, he was back in Paris by the late 1630s.

The first Paris period (1638/37-1652) is where the career is made. He became friendly with Louis du Guernier, a Protestant painter, and married du Guernier's sister Suzanne, widow of the engineer Nicolas Colsonnet, on 13 January 1641 at Charenton. Through the marriage he entered the Paris Protestant circle of artists, goldsmiths and jewellers. In 1643, at twenty-seven, the Goldsmiths' Corporation chose him to paint the annual "May" altarpiece for Notre-Dame -- The Crucifixion of St Peter (W07), his chef d'oeuvre, still in Notre-Dame (see C1239 for the Stadel misnaming). His son Abraham was born in 1648, baptised at Charenton, godfather Abraham Bosse. In 1648 he was a co-founder of the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture and one of the original twelve elders (anciens) running it -- not one of twelve founders, a misconception the study corrects (see C1246): there were twenty-two founding members, and twelve anciens elected in February 1648. The Proces-verbaux record Bourdon settling the Academy's accounts for April 1648, its first month, and again in April 1649 -- he was its treasurer-like officer in the founding months. After about 1640 Poussin's influence becomes pronounced: geometric compositions, clearly defined planes, brighter palette. Before that he was largely insensible to Poussin -- Christie's notes he "does not seem to have encountered Poussin's art in Rome". Soldiers Playing Cards (1643, Caen) is his only dated bambocciata, painted after his return to Paris, Italianate motifs adapted to a French vocabulary.

Stockholm, 1652 to 1653/54, is the hinge. The end date splits the sources: the Musee protestant and the Wikimedia timeline say 1652-1653, the National Galleries of Scotland, the Stadel and NiceArtGallery say 1652-1654, Britannica says he returned to Paris in 1653, and the Prado dates the equestrian portrait 1653-1654, straddling both (see C1233). The study writes "1652 to 1653/54" and does not resolve it. He was invited to Stockholm and made first court painter to Queen Christina -- the title neither Beck nor Danckerts held (see C1247). His principal duty was portraits of the queen and her court, and his Christina portraits defined her public image as a ruler. The seated Queen Christina (W12, c. 1653, Prado P002402, 98 x 105 cm) -- black silk dress, white puffed-sleeved undershirt tied at the neck with a thin black ribbon, sceptre in her left hand, seated on a chair upholstered in red velvet trimmed with gold braid -- is the formal state image; the equestrian Queen Christina of Sweden, on Horseback (W11, 1653-54, Prado P001503, 340.5 x 303 cm) -- the queen in grey on a rearing horse in levade, a falconer with a hooded hawk and hounds beside her -- is the theatrical one, sent by Christina from Stockholm to Philip IV of Spain in 1653 as a diplomatic signal of her reaffirmation on the Catholic side after the Thirty Years' War. He also painted Countess Ebba Sparre (W14, 1652/53, NGA Washington), the queen's closest companion, in a sapphire-blue satin gown with a pearl choker, holding a wreath of flowers. The Correggios anecdote belongs to these years (see M3 and C1234). Descartes, who died in Stockholm in February 1650, cannot have sat to Bourdon, who arrived in 1652 (see C1241).

Back in Paris in 1653/54, after the Fronde, commissions flowed. Chalmers lists a Dead Christ and a Woman Taken in Adultery as his first works on return; Britannica corroborates a Dead Christ for Saint-Benoit, 1653. He was named rector of the Academie in 1654 (Oberlin) -- NiceArtGallery says 1655, and a director appointment is dated 1665 elsewhere (see C1236); the study prefers 1654 with the split flagged, noting he was still returning from Stockholm that year. In 1657-58 he was in Montpellier to paint The Fall of Simon Magus (W17) for the high altar of Saint-Pierre Cathedral, where it still hangs, and painted many portraits of local notables during the stay. His wife Suzanne died in September 1658; he remarried Marguerite Jumeau, daughter of a Tours merchant, the wedding at Charenton presided over by Pastor Drelincourt -- the Musee protestant implies 1658/59, NiceArtGallery says 1663 (see C1238). His daughter Anne, born 1653, was by 1687 the only surviving child, having fled France after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685. He delivered annual lectures (conferences) at the Academie Royale in 1667-70; the topics were not established in this run (see C1228 in the legs). Late commissions included work for the city of Cologne and the Abbey of St George (Britannica, undated). The masterpiece of his later years was the gallery of the Hotel de Bretonvilliers in Paris -- now destroyed. His final work, The Deification of Hercules for the Tuileries Palace, was interrupted by his death. He died in Paris on 8 May 1671, aged 55, and was buried in the Saints-Peres churchyard, the Reformed burial ground in the Faubourg Saint-Germain.

For two centuries he was dismissed as a pasticheur, an imitator, partly because he spent much of his career outside France -- an overstatement the study does not repeat (see C1249): by the working timeline he was abroad for roughly five of forty-eight working years. A comprehensive exhibition in 2000 at the Musee Fabre, Montpellier (also shown in Strasbourg, 7 November 2000 - 4 February 2001), with Jacques Thuillier's catalogue raisonne (Sebastien Bourdon 1616-1671, RMN, 2000), partly rectified the judgement. The Musee Fabre holds a fine Lamentation from his last years.

## The evolution: four phases, one method

The catalogue opens in Rome, not in France. Phase 1, Rome 1636-38 (W01-W04 in embryo), is the bambocciata: small or mid-scale, low-life, anecdotal, observational -- camps, kilns, games, brawls. But the ambition is visible even here: A Roman Lime Kiln (W02, 1637, Alte Pinakothek, 245.5 x 172.5 cm REPORTED only) is a bambocciata at history-painting scale, and the Death of Dido (W04, c. 1637-40, Hermitage, 158.5 x 136.5 cm) already attempts classical tragedy at near life-size. The constants are set early: a taste for the rarely-painted subject, a crowded, clearly staged group, and architecture or ruins as a framing device.

Phase 2, first Paris 1637/38-1652 (W05-W10), widens rather than breaks. The genre mode continues but brightens for Parisian taste -- The Beggars (W05, c. 1637-38, Louvre) and Interior of an Inn (W10, c. 1637/43, National Galleries of Scotland, oil on copper), the latter with the Scottish gallery's note that after his return he "continued to paint in the 'Bamboccianti' style, but adapted his work to Parisian tastes by using brighter colours". At the same time the religious and history commissions arrive and dominate: the 1643 Notre-Dame May (W07) at twenty-seven, then the competitive Poussinesque histories of the 1640s -- the Massacre of the Innocents (W08, 1640s, Hermitage, 126 x 177 cm), which the Hermitage calls "one of the most complex in terms of composition to be found among Bourdon's works" and most likely a response to Charles Le Brun's 1647 Massacre, with the figure modelling showing the influence of Poussin's Gathering of the Manna and Rape of the Sabine Women. What changes is scale and self-consciousness: the Massacre is explicitly a reply to Le Brun and a homage to Poussin. He kept the bambocciata as a commercial line into the 1640s -- the only dated one is 1643 -- while building the academic reputation that made him a founder of the Academie in 1648.

Phase 3, Stockholm 1652-54 (W11-W14, W15 begun), is a portrait interlude and the most concentrated stylistic shift of his career: two portrait modes at once. The public mode is Rubensian and theatrical (W11, the 3.4 m equestrian portrait with falconer and hounds); the private mode is intimate, bust- or half-length, the sitter isolated against a plain dark ground (W13, the Nationalmuseum bust Christina, 72 x 58 cm REPORTED only; W14, Ebba Sparre). The second mode is the historically consequential one -- Wikipedia's claim that it "set a formula for middle-class portraiture for the rest of the century" is the standard art-historical judgement. Colour also shifts: the black dress, white sleeve and red velvet of W12 is a restrained, almost graphic palette, very different from the bright bambocciata colour of the 1630s.

Phase 4, late Paris 1654-71 (W16-W20, plus the Seven Acts of Mercy), is the return to history, more classical, more restrained, and more Protestant in its scriptural literalism than the early work. Moses and the Brazen Serpent (W16, 1653-54 by the conventional dating, Prado 1650-1660; 151 x 113 cm) is the bridge between the court portraits and the late religious histories: the same clear Poussinesque grouping, the same dramatic chiaroscuro, now applied to a scene of collective suffering and salvation. The Finding of Moses (W19, c. 1655/1660 per the NGA, not c. 1650 per Wikipedia; 119.6 x 172.8 cm) is the late religious history at its most classical: a frieze-like, clearly articulated multi-figure group, the landscape subordinate to the narrative, the colour clear and cool. The Return of the Ark (W20, 1659, National Gallery London, 105 x 135 cm REPORTED only) is a late landscape-history: the sacred object small within a wide, Dughet-like landscape, the procession and the terrain the subject rather than any single heroic figure. The Seven Acts of Mercy (1665-71, dedicated to Colbert) are the culminating late work: seven large paintings, each c. 122 x 175 cm REPORTED, each matched to an Old Testament episode, etched by his own hand -- deliberately pitched against Poussin's Seven Sacraments in format (122 x 175 cm against Poussin's 117 x 178 cm). The paintings are now in the Ringling Museum of Art, Sarasota, in poor condition; the copper plates are in the Louvre Chalcographie; the etchings are considered one of the masterpieces of French printmaking. The late work never resolves into a single "Bourdon style" -- which is exactly why he was dismissed as a pasticheur for so long and why the 2000 Musee Fabre retrospective mattered.

What stayed constant: (1) eclecticism as a method -- he borrows a mode per subject (van Laer for genre, Poussin for history, Rubens for state portraiture, Dughet for landscape) rather than imposing one manner; (2) the crowded, legible multi-figure group organised around a clear narrative beat; (3) architecture and ruins as stage furniture, from the Roman kiln to the temple colonnades of the Massacre; (4) a preference for erudite, uncommon subjects -- Laban's idols, the brazen serpent, Simon Magus, the return of the Ark -- over the standard repertoire; (5) colour as a structural device rather than decoration, clearest in the black/white/red of the Christina portraits and the cool clarity of the late histories. What changed: scale (from 56 cm tondi to 3.4 m state portraits to the May altarpiece); genre range (genre, history, portraiture, landscape-history); the handling of light (Caravaggesque drama in the 1640s giving way to a cooler, more even, classical light after Stockholm); and the degree of self-consciousness about models -- the early borrowing is opportunistic, the late borrowing (the Seven Acts against Poussin's Sacraments) is programmatic.

## M1 -- Imitation as the method: paint in the manner of the subject

Bourdon's first mechanism is the one his reputation was built on and then punished for: he could paint convincingly in the manner of Poussin, Claude Lorrain, Caravaggio, Rubens, Gaspard Dughet, the Bamboccianti (van Laer, Miel), Castiglione, Correggio, the Carracci, and the Venetian colourists (Titian, Veronese, Tintoretto) -- switching manner by subject. The imitation was literal at times: the horse in The Encampment is copied directly from van Laer's etching Pissing Horse. Contemporary and later criticism called him a "brilliant imitator" who "lacked a truly original, distinctive personal style" and "lacked both the consistency and intelligence of his model [Poussin]" (Stadel). The counter-view, and the study's: he synthesised influences into works with their own integrity, and his decorative colour sense made him an "effective mediator of the classical style". The mechanism generalises: the range gave art historians "exercise in tracing his adaptation of his models", while "the lack of an immediately recognizable 'Bourdon style' has somewhat dampened public appreciation" (Wikipedia). The 18th-century verdicts in Chalmers sharpen the picture: "uncommon readiness of hand, though frequently incorrect, particularly in the extremities of his figures"; "in one day he painted twelve portraits after life, as large as nature"; "his touch is extremely light, his colouring good, his attitudes are full of variety, and sometimes graceful, and his expression is lively and animated"; "his conceptions were often extravagant, nor would many of his compositions abide a critical examination"; "his pictures are seldom finished, and those which appear most so, are not always his best". The point for the study is that eclecticism was not a failure to find a style -- it was the system, and the 2000 retrospective is the modern rehabilitation of it.

**Takeaway:** let the subject choose the manner -- one painter, many masters, the mode changing with the picture, and the imitation itself as the visible system rather than a flaw to hide.

## M2 -- The image of the queen: two portraits, one ruler

Bourdon's second mechanism is the one the doorway names: he fixed Christina's public image in two modes at once. The public mode is the equestrian portrait (W11): the queen in grey on a rearing black horse in levade, a falconer with a hooded hawk and hounds beside her, looking directly at the viewer, over 3.4 m high, modelled on Rubens's equestrian portraits -- state portraiture as theatre, sent by Christina herself from Stockholm to Philip IV of Spain in 1653 as a diplomatic signal of her reaffirmation on the Catholic side after the Thirty Years' War, in the Alcazar by 1666 in a pre-eminent position. The private mode is the seated portrait (W12) and the bust (W13): the queen in black silk against a white undershirt, sceptre in hand, red velvet behind her, isolated against a plain dark ground -- the intimate, sympathetic portrait that "set a formula for middle-class portraiture for the rest of the century". The mechanism is the pair: the same sitter, the same years, two opposite registers -- the ruler on horseback for a foreign king, the person in black silk for the room. Compare Beck (#584, M1): Beck's portraits travelled as diplomatic tokens too, but his travelling half survives only as engravings and copies, while Bourdon's two Christinas survive as the images Europe kept -- the Prado equestrian and the seated queen are the pictures the scholarship is about. The qualification travels with the mechanism: the Prado seated canvas is a workshop replica of a presumed lost original (see C1244), so the image is Bourdon's and the canvas is the workshop's -- and the study says so.

**Takeaway:** build the portrait in two registers at once -- the ruler on horseback for the foreign king, the person in black silk for the room -- and let the pair, not either picture alone, be the image of the queen.

## M3 -- The gift refused: the painter who talks the queen out of giving away the pictures

The third mechanism is the Correggios anecdote, and the study carries it exactly as what it is: a story from the 19th-century biographical tradition, single-sourced to Chalmers (citing D'Angerville, Pilkington, Strutt), with no scholarly corroboration found in this run (see C1234). As told: Christina showed Bourdon pictures her father had found when he took Prague, which had remained unpacked, and asked him to examine them. He reported favourably, particularly of some by Correggio, and she offered them to him as a present. Bourdon declined with "liberality and disinterestedness", saying they were among the finest paintings in Europe and a crowned head ought never to part with them. She kept them, took them to Rome on abdication; after her death the heirs of Don Livio Odescalchi, who had bought them, sold them to the Regent, duc d'Orleans, and they became part of the Orleans Collection. The outer chain -- Prague sack 1648, Christina's collection, Rome, Odescalchi heirs, Regent d'Orleans, Orleans Collection -- is consistent with known collection history; the Bourdon-refusal episode itself is anecdote. The mechanism is the painter as the keeper of the collection rather than its beneficiary: the court painter who refuses the present because the pictures belong to the crown, and whose refusal is what keeps the collection intact for Rome. It is the mirror image of Beck's golden chains (#584, M8): Beck was paid in honour, nine chains from the sovereigns he painted; Bourdon was offered pictures and refused them, and the refusal is the honour. The anecdote's loose detail -- the pictures found by "the king her father" at Prague, when Gustavus Adolphus died in 1632 and the 1648 sack was by Swedish troops under Konigsmarck -- is carried openly rather than smoothed away.

**Takeaway:** let the painter refuse the present -- one queen offering the finest pictures in Europe, one painter saying a crowned head ought never to part with them, and the collection kept intact by the refusal.

## M4 -- The May: the guild commission that makes the reputation

The fourth mechanism is the commission as the making of a career. In 1643, at twenty-seven, the Paris Goldsmiths' Corporation chose Bourdon to paint the annual "May" altarpiece for Notre-Dame -- The Crucifixion of St Peter (W07), St Peter fastened head-down to the cross at his own request, raised by two executioners while a woman with an infant watches in the foreground and angels above hold the martyr's crown. The commission made his Parisian reputation, won him lodgings in the Louvre (1641) and a place among the founders of the Academie Royale in 1648. The mechanism is the public, dated, guild-chosen altarpiece as the hinge: one picture, chosen by a corporation for the cathedral, that turns a returned Roman genre painter into a Parisian history painter. The May was an annual, competitive, highly visible commission -- the painter who got it was the painter Paris was watching -- and Bourdon got it at twenty-seven, five years after his return from Rome. The study notes the small related version in the Musee Carnavalet (41 x 55 cm, REPORTED) and the later independent version in the Spencer Museum of Art (c. 1644-45, 81.4 x 62.4 cm), long thought to be the modello and now regarded as an independent later work. The title trap travels with it: the Stadel calls it "The Beheading of Peter" and puts it in the Louvre (see C1239); the picture is the Crucifixion, in Notre-Dame.

**Takeaway:** build the one commission that makes the career -- a guild altarpiece for the cathedral, painted at twenty-seven, and the reputation that follows from a single public picture rather than from a lifetime of small ones.

## M5 -- The academy: the institution as the work

The fifth mechanism is the Academie itself. In 1648 Bourdon was a co-founder of the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture and one of the twelve anciens elected in February 1648 to run it in turn, one per calendar month -- alongside Charles Le Brun, Charles Errard, Francois Perrier, Juste d'Egmont, Michel I Corneille, Henri Beaubrun, Laurent de La Hyre, Eustache Le Sueur, and the sculptors Simon Guillain, Jacques Sarazin and Gerard van Opstal, with Martin de Charmois as Chef. There were twenty-two founding members, not twelve (see C1246), and the study does not repeat the "twelve founders" error. The Proces-verbaux record Bourdon settling the Academy's accounts for April 1648, its first month, and again in April 1649 -- the treasurer-like officer of the founding months. He was named rector in 1654 (or 1655, or director in 1665 -- see C1236) and delivered annual lectures at the Academie in 1667-70, the topics unestablished in this run. The mechanism is the institution as a second oeuvre: the painter who helps found the body that will judge painting, keeps its accounts in its first month, and lectures in it in his last years -- and whose pupil Nicolas-Pierre Loir then enters the same Academie in 1663 with the support of Le Brun and Louis XIV, the door Bourdon helped build admitting his own pupil (see the Future candidates). The contrast with Beck is the point: Beck's career is a single travelling commission for one queen; Bourdon's is a Parisian institution built to outlast any single patron, and the lectures of 1667-70 are the painter turning teacher at the end of the same career that began with an apprenticeship at seven.

**Takeaway:** build the institution beside the pictures -- a painter settling the Academy's accounts in its first month, lecturing in it in his last years, and the door he built admitting his own pupil fifteen years later.

## M6 -- The bambocciata: low life at history scale

The sixth mechanism is the one Bourdon carried out of Rome and never quite dropped: the bambocciata, the low-life genre scene, staged with the care of a history painting. The Encampment (W01) is small and circular, 56.6 cm across -- two men playing mora before a tent, travellers camped in the shadow of ancient ruins. A Roman Lime Kiln (W02) is the same subject at history-painting scale, 245.5 x 172.5 cm REPORTED only, a bustling lime-burning works with labourers, beggars and onlookers -- Sotheby's calls it Bourdon's most famous painting of the bambocciata type, and the later Attack by Brigands shows "no longer signs of Van Laer's influence" by comparison. The Beggars (W05) and Interior of an Inn (W10) carry the mode into Paris, brightened for Parisian taste. The mechanism is the hinge: the low-life subject staged monumentally, the kiln as big as an altarpiece, the beggars composed like a biblical group -- and the mode kept alive in Paris into the 1640s alongside the May altarpiece and the Poussinesque histories, the only dated bambocciata being 1643, the same year as the Crucifixion of St Peter. The study does not treat the bambocciata as a phase he outgrew; it is a commercial line and a compositional school he kept, and the crowded, legible multi-figure group of the late histories is the bambocciata grown up.

**Takeaway:** stage the low life large -- a lime kiln as big as an altarpiece, beggars composed like a biblical group, and the genre scene kept alive beside the history painting rather than abandoned for it.

## M7 -- Faith as the frame: the Protestant in a Catholic court

The seventh mechanism is the one that runs under the whole career and explains its shape: Bourdon was a Reformed Protestant -- Huguenot -- in a Catholic century, and the faith is a constraint before it is a subject. It drove him out of Rome in 1638, fearing denunciation to the Inquisition after a quarrel with a fellow painter. It shaped his Paris life: marriage at Charenton in 1641, his son baptised at Charenton in 1648, his second marriage at Charenton, his children buried in the Saints-Peres churchyard, the Reformed burial ground. It shaped his circle: the Protestant artists, goldsmiths and jewellers of Paris, du Guernier his brother-in-law, Bosse godfather to his son, Henri Testelin's son named Sebastien for him. It shaped his subjects: Laban Searching for his Household Gods (W03) and its Hermitage sequel Jacob Burying Laban's Images, where the Hermitage notes the choice of the rare subject "is possibly linked to the artist's faith" -- the burying of idols carrying confessional resonance for a Protestant painter. And it is the reason the Stockholm years could not last: he served a queen who was secretly converting to Catholicism and would abdicate in June 1654, and the Frenchman who took the title at her court left before the abdication was complete. The friendship with Louis de Boullogne the elder -- the Catholic friend who pressed him to renounce his faith without success, and stayed his friend anyway -- is the mechanism in miniature: the confessional line crossed by friendship, not by conversion. The study does not claim the faith explains the pictures; it claims the faith explains the movements -- Rome 1638, Charenton 1641, Stockholm 1652, the return to Paris, the burial at Saints-Peres.

**Takeaway:** let the faith frame the movements -- a Protestant painter fleeing Rome, marrying at Charenton, serving a queen about to turn Catholic, and burying idols on canvas while his own children are buried in the Reformed churchyard.

## M8 -- The lost late work: the gallery that survives only in description

The eighth mechanism is absence at the end of the career, the mirror of Beck's seven-year oeuvre (#584, M7). The masterpiece of Bourdon's later years was the gallery of the Hotel de Bretonvilliers in Paris -- now destroyed. His final work, The Deification of Hercules for the Tuileries Palace, was interrupted by his death. The Seven Acts of Mercy (1665-71) survive as paintings in poor condition in Sarasota, and as etchings -- his own hand, the copper plates in the Louvre Chalcographie -- considered one of the masterpieces of French printmaking. The mechanism is the late work that survives in the second medium: the gallery gone, the Hercules unfinished, the Mercy paintings decayed, and the etchings carrying the late manner -- the same logic as Beck's Falck engravings (#584, M6), but for Bourdon the engravings are his own, not another man's after him. The study does not invent the Bretonvilliers gallery from description; it states the loss and lets the etchings do the work. The patron of the Bretonvilliers gallery was not confirmed in this run (see leg 3, section 1.5), and the study does not name one; Nicolas Fouquet is not an evidenced Bourdon patron (see C1245) and must not be presented as one -- Vaux-le-Vicomte was Le Vau, Le Brun and Le Notre, with Le Brun as Fouquet's painter.

**Takeaway:** show the gallery that is gone -- a destroyed masterpiece, an unfinished Hercules, and the etchings carrying the late work in the painter's own hand, the second medium doing what the first could not keep.

## The aesthetic logic

Bourdon's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the logic of a man who painted in the manner of the subject rather than in a manner of his own. Let the subject choose the manner: van Laer for the camp, Poussin for the history, Rubens for the state portrait, Dughet for the landscape -- the mode is a tool, picked up and put down, and the imitation is the system, not the failure of one. Paint the pair, not the picture: the queen on horseback for the foreign king and the queen in black silk for the room; the bambocciata beside the altarpiece; the gallery and the etching of the gallery -- Bourdon works in complementary states, and the meaning sits between them. Paint the group, legibly: the crowded multi-figure scene organised around a clear narrative beat, the architecture as stage furniture, the colour as structure -- black, white and red for the queen, cool clarity for the late histories -- and the erudite subject preferred over the standard one, because the rarely-painted episode is where the painter's reading shows. And let the second medium carry the first: the etching is the record of the travelling half of Beck's oeuvre and of the destroyed half of Bourdon's own, the copy is the famous portrait, and the original is lost without the system collapsing -- the mechanism survives its pictures.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. Bourdon was a glass painter's son from Montpellier who became the favourite of a queen by being quick, charming, and willing to imitate anybody -- Chalmers stresses his "uncommon readiness of hand" and the twelve portraits painted in a day, and the refusal of the Correggios is the receipts of a man who was paid in honour and gave it back. He never becomes a Poussin, his nominal model, and the contact was second-hand at best -- he was largely insensible to Poussin until the 1640s, and the late borrowing is programmatic rather than absorbed. He never becomes a Le Brun, his fellow ancien, and the Vaux commission went to Le Brun while Bourdon was in Montpellier. He remains a gifted eclectic working inside a queen's court and a Parisian academy, and the thinness of his surviving reputation -- one verified seated portrait that is itself a workshop replica, one famous equestrian, a destroyed gallery, an unfinished Hercules -- is the price of that position: the pictures were made to travel and to decorate, and travelling pictures get lost and decorated rooms get demolished. The speed that made him famous is also what the record keeps: a remark about twelve portraits in a day, a refusal of a present, and a gallery that survives in print.
