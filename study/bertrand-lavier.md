# Bertrand Lavier: Study Notes

**Site:** Bertrand Lavier -- born 1949 in Chatillon-sur-Seine; lives and works between Paris and Aignay-le-Duc, Burgundy -- French conceptual artist, painter and sculptor of the post-readymade era, known for the "Lavier touch": covering an everyday object in acrylic paint in the exact colour of the surface underneath, in thick visible brushstrokes, so the object stays itself and becomes a painted image of itself at once. ## The life: horticulture, paint, selection

Lavier was born in 1949 in Chatillon-sur-Seine and trained as a horticultural engineer at Versailles, not as an artist -- Crown Point Press states the lack of art training directly, and presents the horticulture studies as the origin of his lasting interest in hybridization. He worked as a landscapist in 1971 and took part in group exhibitions the same year; his 1971 debut in the art world used photography to play on appearances allowed by technical reproducibility, and he took part in the VII Biennale de Paris in 1971. His first listed solo exhibition was at Galerie Lara Vincy, Paris, in 1973, and his first museum exhibition was in 1975 in Paris at the Centre National d'Art Contemporain. (C19408, leg 1 C19200-C19206; synthesis C19350-C19351)

The first work, in 1969, was white paint on the leaves of a climbing plant in the garden of his house in Burgundy -- two intersecting diagonal lines on the leaves, read by Verzotti as an ironic farewell to his horticultural background and an announcement of his decision to become an artist. The early 1970s work stays with language and photography: language-based pieces such as Dessin (1974) and the paint-sample monochromes, Rouge geranium par Duco et Ripolin, first executed on a wall in 1974. The painted-object turn is dated 1980 by KEWENIG and 1981 by Verzotti, both recorded, unresolved: from then on Lavier covers objects entirely with a layer of acrylic paint in thick brushstrokes, matching the colour of the surface underneath. The first object chosen was a transistor radio in perfect working order, and the first exhibition devoted to this kind of work included five elements: a file cabinet, a grand piano, two wardrobes, and a refrigerator. The superimposed-object works date to the mid-1980s -- a refrigerator on top of a safe, the safe as plinth, the title a sum of the two brand names. (C19409, leg 1 C19204-C19205, C19209-C19210; leg 2 C19251-C19253, C19257; synthesis C19350, C19357)

Venice 1976 is the early career's public peak: the French pavilion list names eight artists for 1976 -- Herve Fisher, Fred Forest, Raymond Hains, Alain Jacquet, Bertrand Lavier, Jean-Pierre Raynaud, Jean-Michel Sanejouand, and Jean-Paul Thenot -- with Pierre Restany as commissioner. The work he showed there included Arcades des Palais des Doges 1 x 8 - 8 x 1 (1975), sixteen arches of the Doges Palace that prove on closer look to be eight arches with one arch repeated eight times. Guard: sources disagree on the Biennale numbering -- 37th (EN Wikipedia) vs 38th (KEWENIG, Xavier Hufkens) -- both recorded, unresolved. He took part in Documenta in 1982 and 1987, and showed at MoMA New York in "An International Survey of Recent Painting and Sculpture" in 1984. The two Centre Pompidou solos anchor the middle career: 1991, and the retrospective "Bertrand Lavier, depuis 1969" in 2012; between them a solo at Castello di Rivoli in 1996-1997 and the retrospective "Expositions 1976-2001" at MAMCO Geneva in 2001. (C19410, leg 1 C19207-C19208, C19211-C19212; synthesis C19351)

From the 1990s on he also exhibited damaged and found vehicles as themselves, without painting over them: the accident-damaged Alfa Romeo Giulietta (1993) and the deflated balloon Dolly (1993) are the early examples, and the same line continues to the wrecked Fiat 500 painted Lamborghini red in the 2026 Xavier Hufkens show, which the gallery traces back to Giulietta. The Walt Disney Productions cycle began with his intervention in 1984, after he found a Walt Disney comic in which Mickey and Minnie visit a museum of contemporary art; he enlarged the fictitious abstract paintings from the comic into Cibachrome prints and later into three-dimensional polyester sculptures. Recent solo exhibitions include the Fosun Foundation shows in Shanghai and Chengdu in 2022, "Allegoria" at Mennour, Paris, in 2023, and "Morceaux Choisis" at Xavier Hufkens, Brussels, 15 January-21 March 2026, which introduced two new bodies of work -- Inclusions, found flea-market paintings encased in blocks of acrylic resin, and Brushstrokes, literal brushstrokes fabricated from painted steel -- alongside painted vitrines, mirrors, Fujichromes, and juxtaposed-colour paintings. His work is held in public collections including the Centre Pompidou, MOCA Los Angeles, the National Museum of Modern Art Tokyo, the Stedelijk Museum, the National Gallery of Victoria, Castello di Rivoli, MUMOK, the Kroller-Muller Museum, and the Pinault Collection. A late public work, "Quelque chose de...", a blue Harley-Davidson fixed to a mast like a guitar neck, was installed in 2021 as a tribute to Johnny Hallyday. (C19411, leg 1 C19213-C19219; synthesis C19351-C19352)


## The works: one operation, widening targets

The catalogue runs W01-W20, 1969 to the 2026 Fiat 500. Nine grades A exist; the grades below are B where a good secondary description was read end to end or the description was thin, C where only a bibliographic or auction record exists (one in this set). The evolution is the argument: the core operation never changes -- coat a thing in paint in its own colour so it becomes a painted image of itself -- but its target widens from a plant and a radio to windows, pianos, monuments, and other art, while paint itself becomes optional. (C19412, leg 2 C19250, C19273-C19275; synthesis C19354-C19357)

**W01, painted climbing-plant leaves, 1969** -- A. White paint on the leaves of a climbing plant in the garden of his house in Burgundy; untitled in the pages read. The Xavier Hufkens page says he was already using paint to reflect reality by literally covering the plant, under a visible contradictory principle later repeated in his most significant works. Mechanism: paint-to-match by covering -- the object is hidden by a coat that pictures it, so representation happens only when the original disappears under paint. (C19413, leg 2 C19251)

**W02, "Rouge geranium par Duco et Ripolin" (Geranium Red by Duco and Ripolin), 1974** -- B. Industrial paint, in later versions paint on Plexiglas or oil on canvas; first executed directly on a wall in 1974, in several later versions. Lavier exhibited only two paint samples, covering two long halves of barely touching surfaces, sometimes walls, sometimes canvases; the two makers both sell a colour named geranium red, yet the two reds differ. Mechanism: name against matter -- two paints with the identical colour name are placed side by side so language declares them the same while the eye sees them different; the work asks who defines a colour, the maker, the name, or the eye. (C19414, leg 2 C19252)

**W03, "Solid State", 1980** -- B. Acrylic paint on a portable transistor radio in perfect working order, covered in paint so as to imitate the object itself. The first exhibition devoted to this sort of work also included a file cabinet, a grand piano, two wardrobes, and a refrigerator. Mechanism: homochromatic overpainting -- paint in the exact colour of the surface it conceals, applied in thick Van Gogh-style brushstrokes, so the object stays functional in theory and becomes a painted image of itself. (C19415, leg 2 C19253)

**W04, "Ventomatic", 1982** -- A. Acrylic paint on a fan; Castello di Rivoli, Turin. A fan covered with brushstrokes of acrylic paint that reiterate the colour and details of the object; the fan has a sound function, blowing air, which the artistic operation intentionally denies by coating it. Mechanism: same-colour overpainting applied to a working machine -- the paint copies the object exactly and at the same time cancels its use, turning a tool into a picture of a tool. (C19416, leg 2 C19254)

**W05, "Fenetre" (Window), 1982-1996** -- A. Acrylic paint on a window, including transparent acrylic on the glass panes; created on a window of the Castello di Rivoli, Turin, in conjunction with his solo exhibition there. Each detail of the window is painted the same colour as the object itself, including the glass panes, which are covered with transparent acrylic paint; the window keeps its shape and frame while its transparency, its whole function, is painted over. Mechanism: overpainting extended from movable objects to architecture -- the gallery itself becomes the painted object, and a surface meant to be seen through becomes a surface to be looked at. (C19417, leg 2 C19255)

**W06, "Ignis", 1983** -- B. Glycerophthalic acrylic on a refrigerator, 122 x 54 x 52 cm; exhibited Milan, Galerie Massimo Minini, 1983; provenance Galerie Massimo Minini, purchased from the gallery in 1984. The auction record gives the medium and dimensions but no colour or brushwork description beyond the painted-refrigerator fact, so the visual account here is thin. Mechanism: the painted appliance -- a domestic machine, still refrigerator-shaped, turned into a monochrome painted volume, the standard early-1980s Lavier move at household scale. (C19418, leg 2 C19256)

**W07, "Brandt / Fichet-Bauche", 1984-1985** -- B. A painted refrigerator placed on top of a painted safe; the two brand names form the title. A fridge hauled up onto a safe, so the safe serves as the plinth and the fridge sits too high to use; the title is written as a sum of the two brands, food against money. Guard: the date is 1984 in the Almine Rech / Bloomberg Space text and 1985 in Wikipedia -- do not print one year without naming the conflict. A related pairing, Miele / Fichet-Bauche, 1984, is listed in exhibition records. Mechanism: superimposition, also called grafting -- two unmodified objects are stacked so that 1 + 1 = 1, a new single sculpture in which the lower object changes function, from safe to pedestal, and the upper object loses its function. Lavier's own quoted words in the Pecci press text: a freezer on top of a safe is no longer an ordinary freezer plus an ordinary safe, but a low sculpture on a plinth, a curious bissociation in which something is represented and at the same time turns into something completely new. (C19419, leg 2 C19257-C19258)

**W08, "Steinway and Sons", 1987** -- A. Acrylic paint on an actual Steinway and Sons grand piano; Centre Pompidou collection; shown in the 1991 Centre Pompidou exhibition and in the 2012 retrospective. A real grand piano, deprived of its functionality, covered by broad brushstrokes of acrylic colour reproducing the ideal pictorial touch of the modern canon, from Van Gogh to Neo-Expressionism; the paint is pure coating that replicates what it is covering. Mechanism: tautological overpainting at monumental scale -- the most prestigious instrument of concert music is reduced to a painted image of itself, and the grand expressive gesture is drained of theatricality because it only copies what is underneath. (C19420, leg 2 C19259)

**W09, "Integrales / Triangle", 1989** -- B. A set of petanque boules placed on top of a loudspeaker; the title joins the brand names of the boules (Integrales) and the loudspeaker (Triangle); in the catalogue of the 1991 Centre Pompidou retrospective. The boules rest on the speaker, an absurd pairing of a game and an audio device; the juxtaposition creates a virtual sound impression, the typical clinking of petanque boules imagined as coming out of the speaker. Mechanism: superimposition by brand-name pun -- two objects are joined by their names rather than by their uses, and the sculpture exists in the sound the viewer imagines, not in any sound it makes. (C19421, leg 2 C19260)

**W10, "Rouge-Bordeaux par Novemail et Ripolin", 1990** -- B. Paint on panels, two side-by-side monochrome panels. Two panels of Burgundy red paint, literally out of the can, both named Rouge-Bordeaux, one from Novemail and one from Ripolin, yet the two burgundy reds are quite different in tone. A related show, Rouge Bordeaux par Ripolin et Renaulac, was held at Chateau du Marquis d'Alesme, Margaux, in 1987. Mechanism: the colour-name test in its purest form -- no object is painted at all, only two industrial paints are placed side by side, so the gap between the name and the matter is the whole work. (C19422, leg 2 C19261)

**W11, "Chambord", 1992** -- C. Acrylic on mirror, framed, 85 x 73 x 8 cm; private collection, sold at auction 1 October 2024; no museum holding found in the pages read. No visual description beyond the medium and dimensions is given in the record read -- listed as acrylic on mirror, signed, titled, and dated on the reverse, with no account of the brushwork, colour, or how much of the mirror remains reflective. Mechanism, inferred only: the painted mirror -- paint laid over a reflecting surface, so the work is inferred to blur or block reflection, but this cannot be verified from the record read and is not claimed here. (C19423, leg 2 C19262)

**W12, "Giulietta", 1993** -- B. A wrecked Alfa Romeo Giulietta car, presented unpainted on a broad white base; shown at Galerie Liliane et Michel Durand-Dessert, Paris, 1993. The car is placed on a white pedestal like a precious object of contemplation, but it is a wreck bearing the spectacular scars of an accident -- shattered windshield and windows, dented fender, smashed body and doors, crushed bumpers, blinded headlights; still recognisable as a car, but a car that will never run again, the corpse of a car. The Artforum reading adds that Giulietta carries two kinds of form at once, form born of good Italian design and form acquired accidentally by violent impact, and that Lavier's move is addition rather than subtraction, synthesis rather than rupture, play and pleasure of forms rather than conceptualist reduction. Mechanism: the ready-destroy -- no formal intervention by the artist, only selection and display; the damage is found, not made, and the wreck becomes a contemporary memento mori, a recumbent figure on a tomb. (C19424, leg 2 C19263-C19264)

**W13, "Mobymatic", 1993** -- A. A damaged moped, presented without formal intervention; Castello di Rivoli, Turin; also shown in Cinq pieces faciles with a damaged motorcycle, a cut threshing machine, and an electric guitar on an iron base. A damaged moped presented as a sculptural object, the flashy damage referring to a dramatic story able to stir emotions, while the title simply repeats the brand name and legitimises no precise narrative. Mechanism: the ready-destroy in its smallest vehicle form -- Duchamp's readymade re-proposed with objects that connote possibly tragic implications, so the found accident does the expressive work the artist refuses to do. (C19425, leg 2 C19265)

**W14, "Walt Disney Productions 1947-1994", 1994** -- A. The series began in 1984; this version is a black-and-white photograph, virtual image, 74 x 80 cm framed (Minini listing); other versions are paintings, sculptures, and Cibachrome prints. The source is a 1977 Disney comic strip, Traits Tres Abstraits, in Journal de Mickey issue 1279, in which Mickey and Minnie visit a museum of modern art; the cartoonist Sergio Asteriti filled the comic museum with imaginary paintings and sculptures in modernist idioms, and Lavier enlarged those background vignettes to life size and remade them as real paintings and sculptures. The series was shown at Kunsthalle Bern in 1984, Guggenheim SoHo in 1998, Museum of Contemporary Art San Diego in 1999, and kamel mennour London in 2018. Mechanism: fiction-to-reality transposition -- an invented artwork, drawn as mere decor, is built as a real object, flipping figuration and abstraction, since a figurative object is made from an imaginary approximation of abstraction. (C19426, leg 2 C19266)

**W15, "Guzzini", 1996** -- A. Lamps mounted onto their fixtures; Castello di Rivoli, Turin. Lamps mounted onto their fixtures, with the brand name as title; the material refers to the gallery or museum context, turning technical equipment that usually surrounds the work into the work itself, and exhibiting an empty space in which the light emitted by the lamps illuminates nothing. Mechanism: institutional readymade -- the support system of display is put on display, so the lighting that normally serves art becomes the art, lighting an empty space. (C19427, leg 2 C19267)

**W16, "La Bocca sur Zanker", 2005** -- B. Sofa on a freezer, 174 x 215 x 81.5 cm; Fondation Louis Vuitton collection; shown in Medley, Espace Louis Vuitton Tokyo, 19 April to 4 November 2018. The red mouth-shaped Bocca sofa, designed by Salvador Dali and originally produced in 1970, balanced on a white chest freezer; a variant, La Bocca / Haier, 2005, puts the same sofa on a reclining white Haier freezer, and another, La Bocca / Bosch, on a Bosch freezer. Mechanism: superimposition as visual equation -- hot lips against cold storage, curve against right angle, Surrealist design icon against anonymous white goods, with the freezer acting as plinth and the sofa as statue. (C19428, leg 2 C19268)

**W17, "Atomium, detail No. 10", 2007** -- B. Acrylic on aluminium, 210 x 247 cm; Fondation Louis Vuitton collection; shown in Medley, Espace Louis Vuitton Tokyo, 2018, and in Atomiums, Xavier Hufkens, Brussels, 8 November to 15 December 2007, which showed eleven such plates. An industrial plate from the Atomium in Brussels, a 1958 World's Fair landmark, discarded during restoration work, painted with broad brushstrokes of shiny silver acrylic paint, the original colour of the building; the segment can no longer be recognised as part of the Atomium, with no indication of its original functional or visual context. Lavier says he has not painted over the segment but repainted it, with the pictorial subject as support. Mechanism: repainting, not overpainting -- a fragment of a monument becomes a painting whose support is the monument itself. (C19429, leg 2 C19269)

**W18, "Ibo", 2008** -- B. Nickel-plated bronze and metal, 98 x 23 x 14 cm; signed on the base, numbered, with the foundry stamp Bronze Romain et fils, edition ea 3/4; Fondation Louis Vuitton collection; shown in Medley, Espace Louis Vuitton Tokyo, 2018; sold at Christie's, Collection Daniel Lebard, November 2021, for 30,000 EUR. A chrome-plated sculpture, described by the Fondation as a chrome-plated sculpture and by the auction record as nickel-plated bronze and metal; no figurative description of its shape is given in the records read. Mechanism: translation into a prestige material -- a form, whose source shape is not described in the records read, is cast and plated so that surface shine, not painted brushwork, carries the work. (C19430, leg 2 C19270)

**W19, "Fountain", 2009 / 2014-2015** -- A. Garden hoses, nozzles, and water, installed outdoors; first installed in the grounds of Versailles in 2009; the Serpentine version ran 14 October 2014 to 4 October 2015, commissioned by the Serpentine as part of its ongoing outdoor commissions in Kensington Gardens. Instead of a classical sculpture of a figure or natural form, the jets of water emanate from an apparently unruly mass of garden hoses; the bright colours and mass-produced forms of the hoses and nozzles stand in stark contrast to the neoclassical facade of the Serpentine North Gallery and the smooth lines of the Zaha Hadid Magazine restaurant. Mechanism: the readymade fountain -- Duchamp's Fountain is answered with a working fountain made of the cheapest garden equipment, so a traditional garden feature is rebuilt from unsightly mass-produced objects that actually spurt water. (C19431, leg 2 C19271)

**W20, "Fiat 500", latest Xavier Hufkens show** -- A. Wrecked Fiat 500 car, crushed body painted in glossy red; Xavier Hufkens, Brussels, standing in isolation at the end of the gallery on a platform that functions more like a stage than a plinth; the latest exhibition, Morceaux Choisis. The wreckage of a Fiat 500, one of the icons of the last century's golden era of the automobile, its crushed body painted in a glossy, enticing red; the dissonance is in the naming, since this is not simply red but Lamborghini red, a reminder that naming itself produces meaning. Guard: no date is given on the page read, so no year is stated here. Mechanism: the hybrid late work -- part Duchampian readymade, part painted object, the damage found rather than fabricated, and the paint colour borrowed from another brand, so the car is reborn as a singular ruin, seductive and disturbing, pristine and damaged, and the question is left open: sculpture, painting, or both. (C19432, leg 2 C19272)

The evolution falls into three moves. First, the core operation stays fixed while its target widens: from a living plant in 1969 and a radio in 1980, through fans, windows, pianos, and fridges, to a fragment of a national monument in 2007, the move is always to cover a thing in paint in its own colour so that it becomes a painted image of itself. Second, the paint itself becomes optional over time: the 1974 colour-name panels need no object at all, the 1993 ready-destroy works need no paint at all, the Disney series replaces painting an object with building a fiction, and the latest show replaces making with selecting, with a found wreck, found flea-market canvases encased in resin as Inclusions, and literal Brushstrokes fabricated in painted steel. Third, the second half of the career turns the same logic outward onto art itself: mirrors are painted so they stop reflecting the viewer, other artists are translated into new media such as a Frank Stella painting remade in neon tubes and a Calder sculpture set on a radiator of the same brand, a Paleolithic Venus is enlarged by 3D modelling to near two metres in La Venus d'Amiens, 2016, and a map of France is repainted region by region in Jeu de Carte, 2022 -- so the painted object ends as a method for re-authoring anything, not only for coating things. (C19433, leg 2 C19273-C19275; synthesis C19357)


## Mechanisms: eight ways to make a thing into its image

**M1 -- Homochromatic overpainting.** Coat an object entirely in acrylic paint in the exact colour of the surface underneath, in thick visible brushstrokes, so the object stays itself and becomes a painted image of itself at once. Watchable behavior: brushstrokes accumulate over a recognizable thing -- a radio, a fan, a piano -- and the thing turns flat and pictorial while keeping its shape; the paint copies what it covers and cancels its use in the same gesture. (W01, W03, W04, W08) **Takeaway:** paint the thing its own colour until the thing becomes a picture of itself. (C19434, synthesis C19358)

**M2 -- The colour-name test.** Place two industrial paints side by side that share one colour name from different makers, so language declares them identical while the eye sees them differ. Watchable behavior: two long panels or wall halves, both "geranium red" or both "Rouge-Bordeaux", and the viewer hunts the difference the name denies; no object is painted at all, the gap between name and matter is the whole work. (W02, W10) **Takeaway:** let the name lie and let the eye prove it. (C19435, synthesis C19359)

**M3 -- Superimposition, 1 + 1 = 1.** Stack two unmodified objects so the lower one changes function into a pedestal and the upper one loses its function, and title the result as a sum of the two brand names. Watchable behavior: a fridge hauled onto a safe, boules resting on a speaker, a mouth sofa balanced on a freezer -- each pairing sits too high or too wrong to use, and the brand-name title (Brandt / Fichet-Bauche, La Bocca sur Zanker) reads as an equation that adds up to one new sculpture. (W07, W09, W16) **Takeaway:** stack two things until one becomes the plinth and both become one. (C19436, synthesis C19360)

**M4 -- The ready-destroy.** Take a found damaged object -- a wrecked car, a damaged moped -- and show it as itself with no formal intervention, only selection and a plinth. Watchable behavior: the wreck sits on a white base or a stage like a precious object, its scars readable -- shattered windshield, dented fender, crushed bumpers -- while the artist refuses to invent the story the damage implies; the damage is found, not made, and the object is a contemporary memento mori. (W12, W13, W20) **Takeaway:** show the wreck as found and refuse the story. (C19437, synthesis C19361)

**M5 -- Fiction made real.** Take an invented artwork drawn as mere decor in a comic -- imaginary abstract paintings hanging in a cartoon museum -- enlarge it to life size and build it as a real painting or sculpture. Watchable behavior: a background vignette from a Mickey comic grows into a full-scale object, flipping figuration and abstraction, since a figurative object is made from an imaginary approximation of abstraction. (W14) **Takeaway:** build the background of the cartoon at life size. (C19438, synthesis C19362)

**M6 -- The support becomes the work.** Put the equipment that normally serves display on display -- gallery lamps, a window -- so the thing that frames art becomes the art. Watchable behavior: lamps mounted on their fixtures light an empty space and illuminate nothing; a window is painted over pane by pane until a surface meant to be seen through becomes a surface to be looked at. (W05, W15) **Takeaway:** exhibit the equipment and let it light nothing. (C19439, synthesis C19363)

**M7 -- Re-authoring monuments and other art.** Take a fragment of a landmark or another artist's work and repaint or translate it, so the support is the monument or the artwork itself. Watchable behavior: a discarded Atomium plate, repainted in its own silver, can no longer be recognized as part of the building; a Frank Stella painting is remade in neon, a Calder set on a radiator of the same brand, a Paleolithic Venus enlarged by 3D modelling, a map of France repainted region by region -- each piece is a new author signing over an old one. (W17, W18) **Takeaway:** sign over the monument in its own colour. (C19440, synthesis C19364)

**M8 -- The working readymade.** Rebuild a traditional garden feature from the cheapest mass-produced equipment so that it actually works, answering Duchamp's Fountain with a fountain that spurts. Watchable behavior: an apparently unruly mass of bright garden hoses and nozzles stands against a neoclassical facade and really sprays water; the unsightly object performs the function the sculpture it replaces only symbolized. (W19) **Takeaway:** answer the readymade with a readymade that works. (C19441, synthesis C19365)

Held out of the eight, both strong seeds: **M9, the painted mirror** -- paint laid over a reflecting surface so the work blurs or blocks the viewer's own reflection; held out because the evidence is thin, Chambord (1992) being the sole mirror work given a W number and grade C, known only from an auction record, with the named mirror paintings Volvic, Wattwiller, and Chateldon noted but not inspected. **M10, the imagined sound** -- two objects joined by their brand names so the sculpture exists in a sound the viewer imagines rather than hears, petanque boules on a loudspeaker, the clink of the game heard in the head, not from the speaker; held out as the subtlest of the superimpositions and the most dependent on the viewer supplying the missing sense. (C19442, synthesis C19366-C19367)


## The network: a meeting, a failed federation, a lineage

The network is dominated by one room in the summer of 1974 and one lineage that runs away from it. The room: Journiac's apartment on Ile Saint-Louis, where Lavier sat at the first meeting alongside Pane, Rabascall, Agullo, Herve, Selz, Fischer, Thenot and Forest, and where Pluchart tried and failed to federate them into a "groupe socio-corporel". Lavier took no recorded side in the naming split that broke the meetings -- Pane defending art corporel "a la fonction jouissante" against Fischer's objective sociological practice -- and his bio pages never mention the debate, the meetings, Pane, Journiac or Pluchart at all; the whole doorway tie rests on the sociological-art sources, not on his own biography. Pierre Restany is the hinge between Lavier and the Collective without being a tie to it: Restany created Nouveau Realisme in 1960, one of the two named influences on Lavier, and invited the Collective to Venice in 1976, where Lavier was also present as a French Pavilion artist -- a parallel presence, never a joint show. (C19443, leg 3 C19302-C19309, C19316; synthesis C19353)

The lineage runs Duchamp to Raymond Hains, not through the body-art circle. EN Wikipedia names the Duchampian legacy and Nouveau Realisme as his inspirations; the Fondation Pernod Ricard calls Lavier an "augmenter", a term borrowed from Raymond Hains, who borrowed it from Roger Caillois; Lavier paid Hains a direct tribute with "Merci Raymond" at the Musee de la Monnaie de Paris in 2016. His gallery and institutional network after 1974 is dense and well documented, and none of it runs through the sociological-art circle: early solos at Galerie Eric Fabre, Paris (1976, 1977, 1979); Le Consortium, Dijon; ARC/Musee d'Art Moderne de Paris (1985); Centre Pompidou retrospectives in 1991 and 2012; Documenta 1982 and 1987; long gallery relationships with Yvon Lambert, kamel mennour, Xavier Hufkens, Kewenig and Massimo de Carlo. Two-person shows give the forward picks: with Sherrie Levine, "Affinites selectives", Palais des Beaux-Arts, Brussels, 1990; with Francois Morellet, Rijksmuseum Kroller-Muller, 1987 (Morellet already studied, #620, so excluded); with Niele Toroni, "Bonjour Monsieur Lavier, Bonjour Monsieur Toroni", Galerie Media, Neuchatel, 1982 (a single show title, a thin link). Three-person: "Apres" with Christian Boltanski and Angelika Markul, KEWENIG Palma, 2018; Boltanski also curated Lavier into "Take Me (I'm Yours)", Monnaie de Paris, 2015. (C19444, leg 3 C19310-C19315; synthesis C19368-C19371)


## Aesthetic logic: the tautology as a machine

Lavier's aesthetic is an aesthetic of the tautology made visible, and the tautology is a machine with three settings. The first setting is the copy: paint a thing in its own colour and the painting is the thing, so the oldest question in painting -- what does the painting represent? -- gets the shortest possible answer, itself. The brushstroke is the giveaway and the joke at once: the Van Gogh touch, the most expressive gesture in the modern canon, is spent copying a radio, a fan, a fridge, and the gesture is drained of theatricality because it only replicates what is underneath. The second setting is the name: two paints share a colour name and differ, a wreck is red but the red is Lamborghini red, a fridge on a safe is titled as a sum of brands -- and in each case the name is doing the work the paint refuses to do, declaring identity where the eye sees difference, or borrowing prestige where the object has none. The third setting is selection: from 1993 on, paint itself becomes optional, and the artist's hand moves from the brush to the plinth, the stage, the resin block -- the wreck is not made, it is found; the fiction is not painted, it is enlarged; the monument is not built, it is repainted in its own silver.

That is why nothing in Lavier is ever finished in the usual sense. The series are chantiers, building sites, open-ended and still running: the painted objects, the superimpositions, the colour-name panels, the ready-destroys, the Disney fictions -- each is a procedure that can take any new object, any new name, any new wreck, and run it through the same machine. The work is not the object; the work is the operation, and the operation is portable. A generative piece in his manner should therefore never render a painted object and stop; it should run the operation -- coat a thing in its own colour, test two names against each other, stack two things until one becomes a plinth, show a wreck and refuse its story -- and let the operation take whatever the viewer brings to it. **Takeaway:** run the tautology as a machine, and let the operation be the work. (C19445, synthesis C19357-C19365)
