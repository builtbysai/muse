## M1. The stepwise arch

What it is: a melody that climbs by step, passed from section to section, until it peaks and falls back. In the Adagio for Strings of 1936, the opening B-flat in the first violins is answered two beats later by the lower strings, creating "an uneasy, shifting suspension as the melody begins a stepwise motion, like the hesitant climbing of stairs", in Johanna Keller's words in the New York Times, and the melody ascends and then descends in arch form, and the violas take it a fifth down, darker and woodier, and the cellos take it up, and the whole orchestra cries out in fortissimo, and four climactic chords, and a long pause, and the theme returns, and the piece fades on an unresolved dominant chord, and the pulse is subtly manipulated by shifting the primary 4/2 meter into isolated measures of 5/2, 6/2 and 3/2, and the music theorist Matthew BaileyShea hears "a deliberately archaic sound, with Renaissance-like polyphony and simple tertian harmonies" under a "chant-like melody", in B-flat minor "with some modal inflections", and the piece is about eight minutes long, and it was composed in Rome in the summer of 1936 with Menotti, inspired by Virgil's Georgics, and premiered Saturday 5 November 1938 by Arturo Toscanini and the NBC Symphony Orchestra, Studio 8H, in a radio broadcast, with the First Essay on the same program, and Toscanini had returned the score without comment in January 1938, and word came through Menotti that he had already memorized it, and he reportedly did not look at the music again until the day before the premiere, and after the first rehearsal he said "Semplice e bella", simple and beautiful, and the piece became "America's semi-official music for mourning", broadcast at Franklin Roosevelt's death in 1945, Einstein's funeral in 1955, John F. Kennedy's funeral in 1963, Princess Grace's funeral in 1982, and at the 2001 Proms after 9/11, and voted the "saddest classical" work by BBC Radio Today listeners in 2004, and entered in the National Recording Registry in 2005, and Barber himself grew tired of its ubiquity, "They always play that piece. **Takeaway:** the climb must be stepwise and handed on, and a melody that leaps to its climax has not used the arch.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Samuel Barber's work is that a composer who could sing, play and conduct refused to give up melody, and the stepwise arch is the refusal made audible, one step at a time, because a melody that climbs by step cannot be accused of showing off, and the extracted movement is the refusal made practical, because a good movement deserves a second room, and the singer's line is the refusal made physical, because a throat knows what a system does not, and the perpetual-motion finale is the refusal made stubborn, because the commissioner asked for a different ending and the composer kept his own, and the narrow serialism is the refusal made polite, because a row can visit a tonal house without moving in, and the American idiom is the refusal made plain, because a boogie played straight needs no apology, and the word painting is the refusal made literal, because the text tells the music what to do and the music obeys, and the small-poem cycle is the refusal made small, because ten little rooms hold more than one big hall, and the chant opening is the refusal made ancient, because a bare male chant needs no orchestra to be complete, and the opera of waiting is the refusal made domestic, because the same room and the same gesture, repeated by the next woman, is the whole tragedy, and the oversized failure is the refusal made public, because the spectacle ate the opera and the composer spent ten years quietly rebuilding it anyway, and the performer-tailored concerto is the refusal made personal, because a part written for one pair of hands cannot be played by any hands, and the suppressed work is the refusal made violent, because the composer tore up his own symphony rather than let it stand, and the Joyce thread is the refusal made lifelong, because one author followed him for forty years, and the late dissonant song is the refusal made lonely, because the line stays beautiful while the harmony will not resolve, and the unfinished last movement is the refusal made final, because the career ends mid-concerto, and the proof is that the Adagio is played at every funeral in America while the rest of the catalogue waits to be heard, and the composer who wrote it wished they would play his other pieces, and the other pieces are the ones that explain why the Adagio works.
