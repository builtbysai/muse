## M1. Sound production as material: the manner of making is the music

The doctrine is stated by Lachenmann himself and quoted in Wikipedia and in Paul Griffiths's Steinway essay: sound events are chosen and organized so that the manner in which they are generated is at least as important as the acoustic qualities of the result, and the listener hears materials, energies and resistance encountered. The instrument stops being a machine for producing notes and becomes a sounding body with a grain, and the performer stops being an executant and becomes a worker at a material. From temA (1968) on, in principle everything he writes is built this way. The cello in Pression (1969-70) is squeezed, pressed, jerked, slid, hit and stroked, and the bow is shoved below the strings into the box between fingerboard, bridge and body, and the tailpiece and the body are bowed, and behind the bridge, and extreme bow pressure is applied to the string wrappings near the tailpiece, and the saltando, the springing bow, is a structural device, and most sounds sit near the threshold of audibility, and late in the piece one normal tone appears in a middle register, on a string tuned down a semitone, with top and bottom strings also detuned for sympathetic resonance and the echo of a major chord, so that the ordinary note arrives as a shock after ten minutes of noise. The piano in Guero (1970) plays not a single normal note: fingers are drawn across the keys, the tuning pegs or the near ends of the strings, hands are slipped audibly across the key surface, brisk tapping replaces striking, and what is heard is mostly soft sound with a faint aura of the piano's reverberant body. The string quartet in Gran Torso (1971-72) treats its sixteen strings as one resonating body, scratching, bowing, striking, sliding fingers, playing on body, ribs and tuning pegs, at quintuple pianissimo, with copious performance notes on tuning and retuning, fingering, damping and bow actions. The mechanism is: make the production audible, and the production becomes the composition, so that a listener who has never seen the instrument still hears the hand, the pressure, the friction, the resistance.

**Takeaway:** do not paint the sound, paint the making of the sound; the gesture is the material, and the note is only what is left over.

## M2. Action notation: the score writes gestures, not results

The score prescribes physical actions to be executed, not the sounding result, and the result cannot be anticipated without the performer's experimentation. Pression is written this way, and the player must discover what the gestures sound like by doing them, and the piece is different in every pair of hands. Salut fur Caudwell (1977) is written in a two-system score: the right hand as tablature for six strings with rhythm and dynamics, the left hand fingering only, non-transposing, the second guitar scordatura a semitone low, with a detailed legend of new techniques, pick plucking, barre stops with fingers and bottleneck, left-hand pressure variation, contact-point changes, damping, palm rubbing of strings in geometric figures, rasgueo-like gestures. Guero carries tablature-like elements for the piano. The mechanism is a transfer of authority from the page to the body: the composer writes what to do, the performer finds out what it sounds like, and the audience hears a sound that no one, including the composer, could have fully imagined in advance, because the sound is the product of a physical encounter, not a mental image.

**Takeaway:** write the action and let the sound surprise you; a score that prescribes gestures instead of pitches makes the performer a researcher, not a reproducer.

## M3. Bow pressure and bow placement as continuous controls

Bow pressure is a continuous control from near-silence to crushing noise, and bow placement is a continuous control from the normal sounding point to the tailpiece, the body, the bridge, behind the bridge, the string wrappings. In Pression the loud noise section is extreme bow pressure on the string wrappings near the tailpiece, and the quiet sections are bow pressure so light the sound barely exists, and the piece is a journey across that gradient. In Gran Torso the excessive bow pressure produces grain, the German "granum", the sound of the bow hair grinding against the string, and the grain is the title, the torso, the body of the quartet. In Reigen seliger Geister (1989), the second string quartet, the timbral opposite poles are evanescent whisperings and staggered shadowy flautandi against a landscape of pizzicati, and the piece ends in a wild scordatura from randomly twisting the tuning pegs. The mechanism is: take the two variables a string player controls without thinking, pressure and placement, and make them the composition, so that the whole expressive range of the piece is a map of where and how hard the bow touches the string.

**Takeaway:** find the two knobs the instrument already has and compose with them; the technique is already the music, it only needs to be made audible.

## M4. The forbidden sound, the child's sound: reclaiming the accident

The sounds a beginner makes by accident, knocking the bridge, bowing the body, scraping, are the sounds the trained player is taught to suppress, and Lachenmann reclaims them as material. The Arts Desk names this as the nub of the music. Ein Kinderspiel (1980, some sources 1981), seven little pieces for piano written for his son David, makes it explicit: a child's untutored attack is recovered, silent clusters, silently depressed keys releasing strings to reverberate, extreme treble with extreme-bass resonance, sostenuto-pedal filtering, the "Filter-Schaukel" in which fingers hold notes and filter them out of struck clusters, folk-song material, "Hanschen klein", five-note groups for "Akiko", his daughter. The mechanism is a reversal of training: the mistake is the technique, and the piece is built from the sounds the instrument makes when it is mishandled, which are also the sounds it makes when it is handled honestly, without the varnish of virtuosity.

**Takeaway:** the accident is a technique that has not been named yet; build from the sounds the instrument makes when nobody is showing off.

## M5. Breath and voice as noise material

Breath is thematised as material, and the voice is used as noise, not as song. temA (1968) puns on the German "Atem", breath, and breathy vocal sounds meet extended flute and cello techniques, and from here the overall sound of his work fundamentally changed. Consolation I (1967) and Consolation II (1968) pass syllables and phonetic particles between voices, the same device Nono used in Cori di Didone, and the text is Ernst Toller's Masse-Mensch in the first and the medieval Wessobrun Prayer in the second. Salut fur Caudwell (1977) delivers spoken text from Christopher Caudwell's "Illusions and Reality" (1937), in German translation, bars 55-171, neutrally at mezzo-forte with strict rhythm and IPA transcription, words used as sound, not rhetoric. "...zwei Gefuhle...", Musik mit Leonardo (1991-92), delivers Leonardo da Vinci's text in German translation in a completely staccato, fragmented manner, recognisable only in spots. The opera, Das Madchen mit den Schwefelholzern (1990-96), uses shivering, gasping voices, breathing and vocal techniques, swooshing, hissing, grating strings, rubbing, clapping, knocking, clicking and clanging, and the performers speak, play, sing, act and move, and there are no arias, no melodies, no characters in the usual sense, and the plot is dissolved into music. The mechanism is: the breath is the oldest instrument and the voice is the most familiar noise, and when the breath is made audible as breath, and the word is broken into phonemes, the body of the singer becomes as concrete as the body of the cello.

**Takeaway:** the voice is a wind instrument that happens to carry words; break the words and the breath is what is left, and the breath is enough.

## M6. The meta-musical fermata: silence that forces reflection

Long stretches of near-silence and minimised activity, tied to a key portion of the text, force self-reflection in the listener, and the fermata is meta-musical because it is not a pause in the music but a pause in the music's claim on the listener. Consolation I introduces it, and Consolation II repeats it, and Leipert traces it through the later output, and the two Consolations were decisive for his large-scale formal planning and localised sound-gesture organisation. The mechanism is a deliberate withdrawal: the music stops insisting, the listener is left alone with what has just been heard, and the silence becomes the loudest event in the piece, because it is the moment the listener hears themselves listening.

**Takeaway:** stop the music at the point that matters and let the silence do the arguing; a fermata that lasts long enough becomes a mirror.

## M7. The structure-net: an empty container filled with sound objects

Time is first fragmented into sections, an empty container after Cage, and each section is filled or not filled with sound objects, and each sound object has its own vital space in which it is born, develops and dies. Notturno (1966/67) is one of the earliest pieces to use structure-nets in its sketches, and Trio fluido (1966/68) is an early structure-net piece, and Salut fur Caudwell is built as empty-container fragmentation into sections, each dominated by one sound object, a "polyphony of ordered juxtapositions". The mechanism is a two-stage composition: first draw the net, the empty grid of time, then decide which cells live and which stay empty, so that the form is a field of presences and absences, and the silence between objects is as composed as the objects themselves.

**Takeaway:** draw the empty grid first and then decide what lives in it; the empty cells are part of the form, not holes in it.

## M8. The five sound types: a taxonomy with its own time

The essay "Klangtypen der neuen Musik" (1966, revised 1993), read here in Peter Ivan Edwards's full English translation, builds a taxonomy of five sound types, each with its own Eigenzeit, its own time. Kadenzklang, cadence sound, is a process with its own slope, and its Eigenzeit is its whole duration, with subtypes Impulsklang, Einschwingklang and Ausschwingklang. Farbklang, colour sound, is a stationary spectrum with a tiny Eigenzeit. Fluktuationsklang is periodic internal movement that is still a state. Texturklang is a non-repeating statistical texture whose Eigenzeit is effectively endless. Strukturklang is a sound whose inner construction is form, and its Eigenzeit is its whole duration, and "structure is a polyphony of orderings". The essay is built on Schaeffer's sound object and on Stockhausen's Klanggerausch and Zeitgerausch, and Stockhausen's Gruppen is Lachenmann's example of a growing Texturklang, and Boulez's Structures Ia is his example of a serially controlled Strukturklang. The mechanism is a clock for each sound: a sound is not just a colour or a pitch, it is a duration with a shape, and the composer chooses sounds by the shape of their time as much as by the shape of their spectrum.

**Takeaway:** every sound has its own clock; compose the clocks, not just the colours, and the piece will have a time of its own.

## M9. Familiar material as ruin and shadow

A quoted or half-remembered tradition is stripped of its linguistic context and re-ordered, and it appears as a damaged remnant inside noise. Accanto (1975-76) plays a tape of Mozart's Clarinet Concerto K.622 in its entirety, in minimal alienated fragments, against a solo clarinet that recalls a classical concerto and an orchestra of noise, friction and unorthodox sound generation, and the beloved concerto is transformed into an unpleasant noisy state, and in one passage it is combined with a coarse vocal action, and "accanto" means "in addition" or "beside". Tanzsuite mit Deutschlandlied (1979-80) plays with traditional forms around Haydn's Emperor Quartet and the German national anthem. Salut fur Caudwell lets familiar materials return as ruin or shadow: Castelnuovo-Tedesco arpeggios in bars 239-240, a flamenco Phrygian cadence shadow from bar 465 to the end. Staub (1985-87) is based on material from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony and is intended to be programmed with that work. Ausklang (1984-85) recalls virtuoso piano concertos in rapid repeated notes, glissandi, chromatic runs and arpeggios, alluding to Charlemagne Palestine's repeated notes. Reigen seliger Geister takes its title from Gluck's Orfeo ed Euridice. Schreiben (2002-03) is said, with the author's own caveat, to be based on the opening contrabass E-flat of Wagner's Das Rheingold, the same pitch Strauss used to open Eine Alpensymphonie. Marche fatale (2016-17) takes the march itself, the most familiar form of all, as its material. The mechanism is quotation without collage: the familiar thing is not pasted in for recognition, it is ground down until only its shadow is left, and the shadow is the piece's memory of a music it can no longer play straight.

**Takeaway:** do not quote the tradition, ruin it gently; the shadow of a familiar tune inside noise is more moving than the tune itself.

## M10. Tape, radio and recorded voice inside the orchestra

A recording is placed inside live sound as an alien object from another space. Kontrakadenz (1970-71) opens with falling ping-pong balls, coins and other percussive rustlings, and two ad-hoc players switch on a radio and tune in a speaking voice, the composer announcing the performance itself, and a sustained Hammond organ figure commented on by electric guitar and percussion leads to a crescendo tam-tam strike, then roaring brass and strings, and the climax passage repeats with variations, and La Folia calls it his finest work. Fassade (1973) runs a tape of radio static, white noise and slivers of voice through a large orchestra in a secret march with forward propulsion across about 22 minutes, and the noise factor is very high, which makes the subdued moments prominent. Accanto plays the Mozart tape. Scenario (1965), his only published tape piece, realised April-May 1965 at IPEM in Ghent and first broadcast Sunday 20 June 1965 on Belgian Radio BRT3, is built from four material types, concrete elements, sounds, textures and noise, each in six to seven segments recurring in progressively shorter intervals, with text against texture, and draws partly on Introversion I (1963), and after it he abandoned electroacoustics for purely instrumental music, because only performed sound has the energy and presence he wants. The mechanism is the foreign body: the recording comes from another room, another time, another medium, and its presence inside the live sound makes the live sound audible as live, by contrast.

**Takeaway:** put a recording inside the live sound and the live sound becomes audible as live; the alien object is the proof that the rest is happening now.

## M11. Spatial deployment and the ensemble as one instrument

Players are spread through the hall, and the whole ensemble is treated as a single resonating body. Interieur I (1966) places a solo percussionist in a horseshoe-shaped setup of unpitched instruments, cymbals, gongs and metallic objects, and the composer calls it his breakthrough piece, his unofficial Opus One. Schwankungen am Rand (1974-75), "Fluctuations at the Edge", the edge between instruments and between noise and silence, deploys strings and brass through the concert space, mostly quiet and almost static, punctuated by brass outbursts, pianos leading with a steady cluster pulse, and it was premiered at Donaueschingen in October 1975 by the SWF Sinfonieorchester conducted by Ernest Bour. Gran Torso treats the quartet as one sixteen-string body. The opera's original staging idea was the audience in a complete circle with instrumentalists and singers around them, and the 2016 Spoleto staging put the orchestra on a ledge surrounding the audience. The mechanism is the room as a body: the ensemble is not a group of soloists, it is one instrument with many hands, and the listener sits inside it.

**Takeaway:** spread the players through the room and the room becomes the instrument; the listener's seat is a parameter, not an accident.

## M12. Pedal and resonance engineering: the piano's afterlife

Silently depressed keys, sostenuto-pedal capture, sympathetic resonance, microphones inside the piano. Ein Kinderspiel uses silently depressed keys releasing strings to reverberate, and the sostenuto-pedal filtering of the "Filter-Schaukel". Serynade (1997-98, revised 2000), written for his wife Yukiko Sugawara, the "y" in the title her initial, is a duo for piano and pedals, an almost ungodly web of pedal requirements, isolated notes and chords suspended in time by the pedals, reverberations changing, depressed keys modulating the sostenuto, and an interfacial microphone, a PZM or Crown, placed inside the Steinway D near the third sound hole, and the incomplete version was premiered at Akiyoshidai on Thursday 27 August 1998 and the complete version at Stuttgart, at the Eclat festival, on Sunday 13 February 2000. Ausklang outwits the piano's inevitable decay, its "Ausklang", with stopping, pedal, resonance, echo and grasping techniques that prolong the sound beyond its natural limit and let it continue sounding in the orchestra. Guero lets the piano's reverberant body be the sound. The mechanism is the afterlife of the note: the piano's sound is mostly what happens after the hammer falls, and the pedals and the silently held strings are the instruments that play that afterlife.

**Takeaway:** the note ends when the hammer falls, and the music begins; compose the resonance, not the attack.

## M13. Self-quotation as dialogue

Late works stage fragments of earlier works against each other. Concertini (2005), commissioned by Ensemble Modern with support from the patron Betty Freeman, is the composer "giving a concert" with himself: fragments from earlier works, Souvenir (1959), Schwankungen am Rand, Mouvement and Grido most clearly, enter a complex dialogue across about 35 minutes, and Ensemble Modern played it in 2025 in four major German halls for his 90th birthday. Double (Grido II) (2004) transcribes the third string quartet Grido (2000-01) for string orchestra, adding richness and bass weight, with a moment of spectral harmony about a fifth of the way through. The mechanism is the archive as material: the composer's own past becomes the familiar material, and the late work is a room in which his earlier selves argue with each other, and the argument is the form.

**Takeaway:** quote yourself the way you quote Mozart, as a ruin and a shadow; the dialogue with your own past is the late style.

## M14. The late turn to the known

After decades of pushing into the unknown, the late works push into the known: melody, march, Romantic orchestral sound, presented without nostalgia and without returning to orthodoxy. Marche fatale (2016-17), piano solo with an orchestral version of 2017, is a cheerful, almost melodious march, and the scandal is not that it sounds weird but that it sounds so unashamedly catchy. My Melodies (2012-18, composed 2016-18 per BR Klassik), eight horns and orchestra, takes its impetus from a rehearsal of the opera in Madrid in 2008, hearing eight horns as one homogeneous yet complex instrument, and the premiere came ten years later in 2018, and the work was extended by 77 bars in the 2023 musica viva Munich version, premiered Friday 23 June 2023 at the Herkulessaal in Munich by the Bavarian Radio Symphony Orchestra conducted by Matthias Hermann, and with it he "conquered Romantic orchestral sound in his own inimitable fashion". Serynade returns to orthodox piano sound. Mes Adieux (2021-22), a string trio, his most recent work, bids farewell "with consummate concentration and serenity both to itself and to its repertoire of gestures". The mechanism is the system applied to familiar material instead of against it: the noise system is not abandoned, it is turned on the march, the horn, the melody, and the familiar thing comes out strange and new, because it has been made by the same hands that made the noise.

**Takeaway:** after sixty years of the unknown, the known is the strangest material of all; play the march as if you had never heard one, and it will sound like the future.

## Evolution

P1. Apprenticeship and serialism, 1955-1962. Stuttgart study with Johann Nepomuk David and Jurgen Uhde, 1955-58; Darmstadt 1957; Nono in Venice 1958-60. Works governed by abstract ideas of order, and sound as the outcome of serial systems. Funf Variationen (1956), the only surviving evidence of his earliest style, Schubert's German Dance in C-sharp minor, D643, taken apart by classical variation technique and reassembled in an almost Beethoven-like style; Rondo (1957) for two pianos, dedicated to David; Souvenir (1959) for 41 instruments; Funf Strophen (1961, withdrawn), nine instruments, first performed 1962 at the Venice Biennale. Echo Andante (1961-62) is the hinge: still guided by Nono's sound-classification system, but erratic sound movements are smoothed and major and minor colours shine between dissonant harmonies, and it ends with a pure C major chord in triple forte, and the mode of attack, tender, harsh, distorted, is already a compositional means, and the note is heard as energy, not pitch, and his own 1962 introductory text says that structure was not the means to expressive ends, and that expressivity, already inherent in the means, became the point of departure.

P2. The electronic detour, 1963-1965. Cologne meetings with Stockhausen, Pousseur, Rzewski and Kontarsky, 1963-64; contact with the percussionists Christoph Caskel and Michael W. Ranta, whose playing fed directly into Interieur I. Introversion I (1963, withdrawn) and Scenario (1965), the tape piece from Ghent. He learns from tape how sounds can seem to make their own courses, and then he abandons electronics, because only performed sound has the energy and presence he wants.

P3. Invention of musique concrete instrumentale, 1966-1971. The essay "Klangtypen der neuen Musik" (1966). Interieur I (1966), the breakthrough, the unofficial Opus One; Notturno (1966/67); Trio fluido (1966/68); the Consolations (1967-68); temA (1968); Air (1968-69), with its cadenza toward the middle, not near the climax, entirely free for the performer, lasting a few minutes, and its croaking toy frogs, electric doorbells operated by ad-hoc players, Japanese temple gong, brass filled with water, and its electric guitar part, the first in his work. Then the three solo studies, Pression, Dal niente and Guero (1969-70), and the orchestral Kontrakadenz (1970-71), and the special effects stop being special and become the whole discourse.

P4. Large-scale noise orchestra, 1971-1984. Gran Torso (1971-72); Klangschatten - mein Saitenspiel (1972), three concert grand pianos and a string ensemble of 48 strings, pianos blurring the barrier between percussion and string instruments; Fassade (1973); Schwankungen am Rand (1974-75); Accanto (1975-76); Salut fur Caudwell (1977); Tanzsuite mit Deutschlandlied (1979-80); Ein Kinderspiel (1980); Mouvement (1982-84); Ausklang (1984-85). The noise system is now married to large form, to quotation-as-ruin, and to the concert hall as a spatial field. The scandals belong here: orchestras abandoning performances mid-concert in Frankfurt 1969, Munich 1971, Warsaw 1976, Donaueschingen 1980, and in 1986 the SWF Symphony Orchestra refused to give the commissioned premiere of Staub.

P5. History, theatre and the long line, 1985-2005. Staub (1985-87), based on Beethoven's Ninth; Allegro sostenuto (1986-88), a prolonged 30-minute allegro, tranquil and sombre rather than fast; Reigen seliger Geister (1989); "...zwei Gefuhle..." (1991-92), later incorporated into the opera, where it forms a crucial point in the middle; the opera itself, Das Madchen mit den Schwefelholzern (1990-96), the unexpected popular success, all performances sold out, the Ernst von Siemens Music Prize following in 1997; Serynade (1997-98); NUN (1997-99), "the attempt to create a kind of presence, not music that continues, no discursive text, music as a situation", quoting the Japanese philosopher Kitaro Nishida; Grido (2000-01), mostly whisper or faint song, a slow melody unfolding across the whole 25-minute piece like a rope, ending with a Mozartian coda, a dance-like final phase rattling off into nowhere; Schreiben (2002-03), a rapid susurration running from section to section like scribbling, slow brass-led waves of clamour, thin textures leaving tense suspense; Concertini (2005). Sounds are now allowed to be notes again, but only as sounds, and a slow melody can run through a whole quartet, and the composer quotes himself.

P6. Late concentration, 2008-2022. A decade of near-silence after ...got lost... (2007-08), a scena for soprano and piano, an offshoot from the opera, a vocal meditation with far less original or extended technique than elsewhere, and Steinway's Listen Magazine, written before 2016, calls it his latest work, "no new work since 2008". Then My Melodies (2012-18, revised to 2023), Marche fatale (2016-17) and Mes Adieux (2021-22). The turn into the known: melody, march, Romantic horn sound, farewell. The system is not abandoned; it is applied to familiar material instead of against it.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Lachenmann's work is that the sound is the trace of the action that made it, and the action is the thought, and the thought is the listening. The instrument is not a neutral carrier of pitches, it is a body with a grain, and the grain is the music. The bow pressed too hard is not a mistake, it is a fact about the string, and the fact is audible, and the audible fact is the composition. The noise is not an effect applied to the note, it is the note stripped of its varnish, and the note that survives the stripping, the one normal tone in Pression, the C major chord at the end of Echo Andante, the march in Marche fatale, is more moving for having been earned through the noise. The quotation is not a reference, it is a ruin, and the ruin is more honest than the original, because the original pretended the past was still whole. The silence is not an absence, it is the listener hearing themselves listen, and the fermata is the moment the music hands the work over to the ear. The late turn is not a reconciliation with tradition, it is the same system turned on the tradition itself, and the march played by the hands that made Gran Torso is a different march from any march ever written, because it has been made by a man who knows what a bow sounds like when it is pressed too hard. The whole oeuvre is one long education of the hand, from the serial exercises of the 1950s, through the tape studio in Ghent, through the solo studies that taught the instruments to speak their own language, through the orchestras that became single resonating bodies, to the late works in which the hand finally plays the familiar things, and the familiar things come out strange, because they have been touched by the same hand.

## Network

N1. Luigi Nono (1924-1990). Teacher. Met at the Darmstadt summer course in 1957, and first private pupil in Venice 1958-60, staying in Nono's home. Nono taught that music is an attitude to the world, politically awake and ethically responsible, and told him "Out with these melodic expressions!". Nono's influence is the social and political reflection, the unconventional techniques and noises, and the syllable-passing vocal writing of Cori di Didone (1958), the model for Consolation I, and the Darmstadt serial debate, which broke apart while Lachenmann was Nono's pupil. Lachenmann's "...zwei Gefuhle..." is subtitled "Musik mit Leonardo" and his opera material includes a memorial text for Nono, and the work list gives it as "...zwei Gefuhle..., Musik mit Leonardo (in memoriam Luigi Nono)". Nono was studied as #409, so he is NOT re-proposed.

N2. Karlheinz Stockhausen (1928-2007). Darmstadt colleague and interlocutor. Met at Darmstadt 1957, and Lachenmann attended Stockhausen's Cologne courses 1963-64. Stockhausen's "Klanggerausch" and "...how time passes..." (1956) is an explicit source for the Klangtypen essay, and Gruppen is Lachenmann's example of a growing Texturklang, and Scenario suggests he had listened productively to Stockhausen's Kontakte. The Nono-Stockhausen aesthetic dispute of 1958-60 marked the effective dissolution of the Darmstadt School, and it happened while Lachenmann was Nono's pupil in Venice. Stockhausen already stands as an alternate from #402 and #408, so he is NOT re-proposed as new.

N3. Pierre Boulez (1925-2016). Darmstadt colleague. Met at Darmstadt 1957. Boulez's Structures Ia for two pianos is Lachenmann's example of a serially controlled Strukturklang, and Nono's 1957 lecture implicitly opposed Boulez's theories since 1951. Boulez already stands as an alternate from #402 and #408, so he is NOT re-proposed as new.

N4. Pierre Schaeffer (1910-1995). The origin of the term. Musique concrete (1948) is the model Lachenmann transfers to instruments, and the BBVA page calls concrete instrumental music "a step beyond" Schaeffer, and the Klangtypen essay builds on Schaeffer's sound object. N5. John Cage (1912-1992). Structural influence, admitted late. Met at Darmstadt 1958. Cage was studied as #398, so he is NOT re-proposed.

N6. Olivier Messiaen (1908-1992). Taught in Darmstadt in the mid-1960s and inspired Lachenmann then. No work-level mechanism was traced in this set.
