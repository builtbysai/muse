# David Salle: Study Notes

**Site:** David Salle (surname pronounced "Sally") -- born 1952 in Norman, Oklahoma, raised in Wichita, Kansas -- American Postmodern painter, printmaker, photographer, and stage designer who spent forty-five years stacking borrowed images, grisaille panels, furniture outlines, and real objects onto single canvases that refuse to add up. He is the student who kept painting: where his teacher John Baldessari burnt his paintings in 1970 and replaced the hand with a rule, Salle took the rule -- borrow the image, arrange it, let the viewer do the work -- and put the hand back in, repainting every borrowing by hand at mural scale. ## The life: the student who kept painting

David Salle was born in Norman, Oklahoma, in 1952 -- the day is UNRESOLVED and must be carried, not settled: most sources give September 28, 1952 (Wikipedia, Whitney, National Gallery of Australia), while Galerie Bruno Bischofberger gives November 28, 1952. The year and the place are not disputed. He grew up in Wichita, Kansas, the only child of Russian-Jewish immigrant parents who worked at the same dress store: his mother a saleswoman, his father a buyer, window dresser, and advertising-layout man, and a Sunday painter. He took art classes from the age of eight at the Wichita Art Association, and in high school attended outside art classes three days a week. (C22209, leg 1 C22000-C22006; synthesis C22150)

In 1970 he was part of the foundational class at the newly established California Institute of the Arts in Valencia, where he studied with John Baldessari, earned his BFA in 1973 in three years and his MFA in 1975 in two. Baldessari recalled him as "certainly one of the best" of his students, and Salle credits Baldessari with showing him a path to his artistry. The CalArts window carries one variant: Britannica says he "attended" 1973 to 1975, against the two end-to-end sources and the studio CV giving 1970 entry, BFA 1973, MFA 1975. After graduating in 1975 he moved to New York, worked for Vito Acconci and in the art department of a pornography magazine, taught drawing at Ocean County Community College in Toms River, New Jersey, was Assistant Professor at Hartford Art School from 1977 to 1980, and taught at the School of Visual Arts in 1981. His current base is date-dependent and both attested: Wikipedia says East Hampton, New York; Skarstedt, the National Gallery of Australia, and other pages say Brooklyn. Do not print one as settled. (C22210, leg 1 C22007-C22011; synthesis C22151)

His first show was a small show of paintings in Larry Gagosian's loft on West Broadway in 1979, in a loft shared with Annina Nosei. Bruno Bischofberger bought his first Salle paintings in early 1979 and began showing him in 1980; in 1981 he became jointly affiliated with Mary Boone Gallery and Leo Castelli Gallery. Later dealers included Lehmann Maupin, Gagosian, Thaddaeus Ropac, Skarstedt, and Gladstone. His work came to public attention in New York in the early 1980s, and by the end of that decade it was in the collections of the Tate, the Pompidou, the Whitney, MoMA, and MOCA Los Angeles. The exhibition spine: Documenta 7 (1982), Venice Biennale 1982 and 1993, Whitney Biennial 1983, 1985, 1991, Paris Biennale and Carnegie International 1985; first solo museum show, Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen, Rotterdam, 1983; Stedelijk travelling retrospective 1999-2000 (Vienna, Turin, Guggenheim Bilbao, January 18-May 14, 2000) and a separate retrospective at the Museo de Arte Contemporaneo de Monterrey in 2000; the Met's "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" in 2009; later solos at the Arts Club of Chicago (Ghost Paintings, 2013), Dallas Contemporary (2015), and Malaga (2016). (C22211, leg 1 C22012-C22021; synthesis C22152, C22153)

Beyond painting he designed sets and costumes for Kathy Acker's "Birth of the Poet" -- the date is UNRESOLVED, 1981 (Hopper House summary) vs 1985 / December 1985 (NGA, Encyclopedia.com) -- and for ballets by choreographer Karole Armitage, his life partner of many years, including "The Mollino Room" for American Ballet Theatre in 1986. In 1995 he made his Hollywood directorial debut with the feature film "Search and Destroy," produced by Martin Scorsese; the cast short lists differ across sources (Walken and Griffin Dunne vs Hopper, Hawke, and Walken), so no short list is printed here as the complete cast. His collection of critical essays, "How to See: Looking, Talking, and Thinking About Art," was published by W. W. Norton in 2016, and he contributes regularly to Town & Country and the New York Review of Books. Awards: CAPS grant 1979; Guggenheim Fellowship for Theater Design 1986. The auction record is dated, not absolute: as reported in 2001 the record was "Tennyson" (1983), $550,000 at Christie's, November 7, 1989; later reported sales were higher -- "Mingus in Mexico" (1990), 608,750 pounds at Christie's London, October 2017; "A Bigger Rack" (1998), 515,250 pounds, March 2019; "Nadar's Grey" (1990), $495,000 hammer at Phillips New York, November 2018. (C22212, leg 1 C22022-C22028; synthesis C22154, C22155)

The late work keeps the same system and changes the tools. During the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020-21 he painted the Tree of Life series -- Adam and Eve, the Garden of Eden, Peter Arno cartoons -- canvases alternating black-and-white and polychrome palettes, with a stylised tree vertically bisecting the picture plane and a long narrow "subterranean" panel of roots below. In 2023 he collaborated with computer scientists on a generative-AI program trained on his paintings, used for the 2025 "Some Versions of Pastoral" backgrounds, which he prints on linen and overpaints, describing his algorithm as a "joystick." The borrowing machine that started with a magazine reproduction of a tapestry now runs on a model trained on his own past work -- the self-quotation mechanism, automated. (C22213, leg 1 C22029; leg 2 W20; synthesis C22156)




## M1 -- Jump the interval

Salle builds pictures as panels with a gap the eye must cross. In "Muscular Paper" (W04, 1985) the triptych is three parts with distinct surfaces, textures, and luminosity -- oil, synthetic polymer paint, and charcoal on canvas and fabric, with painted wood, 8 ft 2 1/8 in. by 15 ft 7 1/8 in. overall, MoMA -- and Salle's own gloss is the mechanism stated plainly: "it is the intervals that your eye confronts and has to jump over that creates the specific tone of the painting." The left panel shows a Brassai photograph of Picasso's 1931 sculpture "Bather"; the centre doubles a head from Ribera's "The Club-Footed Boy" (1642); the right replicates a bridge from a Max Beckmann print -- and on top of all three sit unrelated images and patterns: doubled Batmanesque cartoon heads from Ribera affixed onto the buttocks of two rope-skipping nudes, a stencil-like Beckmann copy over cheap plaid fabric, the Picasso photograph turned into a haphazard pegboard, a wooden lemon squeezer in the middle orange panel. Nothing blends; everything abuts. (C22214, leg 2 W04 and M1; synthesis C22158)

The same hard interval returns at every scale. "Sextant in Dogtown" (W07, 1987) subdivides one canvas into rectangular compartments. The Ghost Paintings (W11, 1992) are three contiguous horizontal linen panels. The After Michelangelo cycle (W15-W17, 2005-2006) is two panels each, about 8 ft high and 15 ft wide. The interval is not a seam to hide; it is the instrument. A prompt built from this mechanism must make the gap visible -- a black joint, a change of ground, a panel that slides -- and let the eye do the work of jumping it. (C22215, leg 2 W07, W11, W15-W17 and M1; synthesis C22158)

**Takeaway:** build the frame as two or three hard-edged panels with different surfaces and palettes, and animate by sliding one panel or swapping its content while the others hold -- the jump between panels is a visible beat, never a blend. (C22216)


## M2 -- Repaint the borrowed image

Nothing in a Salle is pasted. Everything borrowed is repainted by hand, so the borrowing reads as translation, not collage cut. In "Muscular Paper" (W04) the Brassai photograph, the Ribera head, and the Beckmann bridge are all rendered in paint, each in a different handling -- one as a pegboard, one doubled and cartooned, one stencilled over plaid. In the Tapestry Paintings (W08, W10, 1989-1991) the source is twice removed: narrative scenes from historical tapestries, some of them reproductions of famous works, others by an anonymous Russian tapestry-maker Salle found reproduced in a magazine and copied onto canvas at monumental scale. The borrowing is visible as a re-making: the viewer can feel the image has been translated, because the brushwork, the colour shift, and the crop all say so. (C22217, leg 2 W04, W08, W10 and M2; synthesis C22159)

The same translation runs the whole career. "Mingus in Mexico" (W09, 1990) takes grisaille figures probably culled from a Renaissance fresco or tapestry. The After Michelangelo cycle (W15-W17) repaints the Sistine Chapel -- the god figure from "The Creation of Adam" in W15, the deluge in W16, the altar-wall figures in W17 -- at 8 by 15 feet. Late, "Self-Ironing Pants" (W19, 2019) copies Peter Arno's New Yorker cartoons of pear-shaped fuddy-duddies from the 1920s to the 1960s, one of them in striped pajamas contemplating a bathtub under an explosion of bubblegum packs. The rule never changes: take one source image per zone and re-render it in a different style, keeping a tell of the translation. (C22218, leg 2 W09, W15-W17, W19 and M2; synthesis C22159)

**Takeaway:** borrow one recognisable image per zone -- a photograph, a fresco, a cartoon -- and repaint it in a different style with a visible tell (brushwork, colour shift, crop), so the viewer feels the image was re-made, not placed. (C22219)


## M3 -- Grisaille against colour

Black-and-white passages sit directly against saturated colour passages on the same canvas, so value contrast, not hue, organises the picture. The Skarstedt Tree of Life text states it as a career fact: Salle took "the world of the printed page -- the black, white and grey tones -- as a subject," and the orchestration of darks and lights has characterised his painting since the early 1980s. In "Sextant in Dogtown" (W07) black-and-white panels counterpoise brightly coloured ones, and three inky grisaille renderings -- based on photographs Salle took himself, of a seminude woman holding a garment in one instance and a Noguchi lamp in another -- sit below acrid-colour clown panels. In "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) the background is grisaille figures from a Renaissance fresco or tapestry with touches of crimson and yellow. In "Dean Martin in 'Some Came Running'" (W10) the tapestry background of women and men in feathered caps drinking and playing backgammon is transposed to grisaille against dripping peach smears and fiery colour. (C22220, leg 2 W07, W09, W10 and M3; synthesis C22160)

The Ghost Paintings (W11) push the same contrast into the ground itself: photographic grey under fields of intense colour painted over it. The Tree of Life series (2020-21) alternates whole canvases between a black-and-white palette and a polychrome one. The mechanism is a switch, not a blend: assign each zone a value mode -- full colour, grisaille, or duotone -- and let the picture read as a value pattern first and a scene second. (C22221, leg 2 W11 and M3; leg 1 C22029; synthesis C22160)

**Takeaway:** give each image zone a value mode -- colour, grisaille, or duotone -- and let zones flip mode on a timer, so the picture reads as a value pattern first and a scene second. (C22222)


## M4 -- Cut the head off

Bodies in Salle are truncated, headless, or covered, so the figure becomes form rather than a person. In "Sextant in Dogtown" (W07) the woman's head is truncated above the neck in two frames and cloaked in a third, while the figure at top right operates an old oceanic navigation device, a sextant, and jester-like harlequin characters stand adjacent to scantily clad female figures. The Whitney text makes the act of seeing and not-seeing the theme: the viewer looks at the woman, is looked at by the clowns, and watches the cartographer look. In "Comedy" (W13, 1995) the right half shows a domestic scene turned on its side, like a midcentury furniture advertisement, partially covered by collage-like images: colourful butterflies framing a headless mannequin in a voluminous dress, and below it a ruffled harlequin collar. In "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) an ambiguous figure draped in a sheet hovers in the all-over field; in "Brother Animal" (W02, 1983) a disembodied organ -- perhaps a brain, liver, or kidney -- floats on coarse brown upholstery fabric beside the couple. (C22223, leg 2 W07, W13, W09, W02 and M4; synthesis C22161)

Headlessness is a switch, not a style. The same figure with and without a head reads as two different objects, and the crop is what does the work -- by the frame edge, by an overlapping panel, by a cloak. A prompt built from this mechanism must crop at the neck, waist, or knees and let the head slide out of frame and back, so the viewer notices the moment a person becomes a shape. (C22224, leg 2 M4; synthesis C22161)

**Takeaway:** crop figures at the neck, waist, or knees by the frame edge or an overlapping panel, and let a head slide out of frame and back -- headlessness is a switch, and the same figure with and without a head reads as two different objects. (C22225)


## M5 -- Say the name, say nothing

Words appear as painted shapes that refuse to explain the picture -- a name, a caption in another language, or a bubble with nothing in it. In "Tennyson" (W03, 1983 -- date variant 1981 carried, see Depth) a sensual nude lies on a sienna plain like a sun-soaked beach, her back coyly turned, and the name of the Victorian poet spans the centre in block letters, of which only the first two are coloured: "te". A rust-coloured mark bisects the top half of the buttocks; swatches of turquoise and pink sit in the upper left, framing a three-dimensional ear carved of wood and affixed to the canvas. The word is a graphic shape first and a meaning second. (C22226, leg 2 W03 and M5; synthesis C22162)

In "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) a pink/salmon empty cartoon speech bubble sits in the all-over field and denies the viewer any narrative explanation. In "Dean Martin in 'Some Came Running'" (W10) a Cyrillic caption is visible at the lower right corner of the tapestry ground -- legible to almost no one looking at it, and that is the point. The text is never a caption in the ordinary sense; it is a painted object that withholds. Animate it by letting the bubble fill with nothing, or the word lose letters one at a time, and the emptiness stays the point. (C22227, leg 2 W09, W10 and M5; synthesis C22162)

**Takeaway:** place one large word or one empty speech bubble over the image field, and let the word lose letters or the bubble fill with nothing -- the text is a graphic shape first, and its emptiness is the point. (C22228)


## M6 -- Furniture with no floor

Chairs, tables, lamps, and beds are drawn as thin outlines that hover over figures and grounds, weightless and out of scale, like ghosts of interiors. In "Brother Animal" (W02) two legless chairs are layered over the couple in the bedroom, along with a pink sketch of what appears to be an apartment complex and a pair of clowns in the white wedge toward the centre. In "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) seven pieces of rustic "Willow" furniture -- rocking chairs, benches, a side table -- are rendered in sketchy blue outline ringing the perimeter, ghosts of chairs hovering in outline form. In "Dean Martin in 'Some Came Running'" (W10) ghostly outlines of chairs and tables in khaki and pink drift over the grisaille tapestry ground. In "Comedy" (W13) the furniture-ad scene is turned on its side. (C22229, leg 2 W02, W09, W10, W13 and M6; synthesis C22163)

The outlines obey no perspective and cast no shadow. They pass in front of figures without touching them, and they scale without permission -- a chair can be bigger than the person beside it. That is the generative behaviour: draw furniture as outline-only line art drifting slowly across the frame, in front of figures, out of scale, never casting a shadow, never landing on a floor. (C22230, leg 2 M6; synthesis C22163)

**Takeaway:** draw furniture as outline-only line art drifting across the frame in front of figures, out of scale and shadowless -- a chair bigger than the person beside it is the mechanism working, not a mistake. (C22231)


## M7 -- Bolt something real to the canvas

The painting breaks its own flatness with a physical thing bolted on. In "Brother Animal" (W02) the medium itself lists wooden chairs: oil and acrylic on canvas and fabric, with wooden chairs. In "Tennyson" (W03) a three-dimensional ear carved of wood is affixed to the canvas beside the turquoise and pink swatches. In "Diabolical Life Restoring Machines" (W06, date not stated in the source) three white ceramic electrical sockets containing blue light bulbs sit below the photo-montaged section, on a work of three united canvases that also holds a small painting of a brown-and-white spaniel, a seated Madonna and child, a yellow-orange cantaloupe with one wedge removed, a glued-on photographic sheet of two women and a man in a boat-like amusement-park vessel, contour-drawn women with splayed legs, and an orange rectangle with three green gesturing hands. In "Sailor" (W18, 2007) a wooden shelf with a small model boat is affixed to the canvas near the woman's right collarbone. (C22232, leg 2 W02, W03, W06, W18 and M7; synthesis C22164)

In a generative piece the attached object cannot be a real thing, so it must be simulated as the one layer with real depth: a shelf, a bulb, a chair back that casts a shadow across the painted surface and occludes whatever drifts behind it, and never moves with the image field. The object is the anchor; everything painted is allowed to move except it. (C22233, leg 2 M7; synthesis C22164)

**Takeaway:** give the piece one attached object -- shelf, bulb, chair back -- with real depth and a real shadow, occluding what drifts behind it, and never let it move with the image field. (C22234)


## M8 -- Print the photograph, then paint over it

The painting starts from a photograph printed or transferred onto the support, then is painted over, so photo and paint occupy the same surface at different depths. The Ghost Paintings (W11, 1992) are ink on photosensitized linen: large photographic images printed on three contiguous linen panels -- a woman creating improvised movements under a large piece of fabric, staged live for the camera with longtime model Beverly Eaby -- then painted over with fields of intense colour. In "Dean Martin in 'Some Came Running'" (W10) one inset panel is a photograph from a series Salle shot in the studio with model Beverly Eaby posing behind a bedsheet, made from a negative exposed directly onto photosensitized linen. "Picture Builder" (W12, 1993) is based on a small-scale collage and retains the cut-and-paste feel: flashes of erotic imagery intertwined with an inverted Texaco logo, a half-hidden crowd, a fireplace, and recurring motifs. (C22235, leg 2 W11, W10, W12 and M8; synthesis C22165)

Late, the same ground goes digital. "Suspenders" (W20, 2025) is oil, acrylic, Flashe, and charcoal on an archival UV print on linen: the AI backgrounds from "Some Versions of Pastoral" are printed on linen as backdrops, then overpainted with overlapping, dramatised groupings of vividly pigmented everyday objects -- fragmented figures in bathing suits or sun dresses, plaid skirts and bikinis. The photographic layer shows through in patches and fades in and out, as if the print is developing and un-developing. (C22236, leg 2 W20 and M8; synthesis C22165)

**Takeaway:** begin each scene from a photographic layer -- grainy, grey, slightly misregistered -- then paint opaque colour fields and line drawings over it, and let the photo show through in patches, fading in and out like a print developing. (C22237)


## M9 -- The same props keep coming back

The same image or object returns in painting after painting, so the oeuvre behaves like a cast of recurring characters and props. The Met's text on "Picture Builder" (W12) lists motifs recurring throughout Salle's practice: a crumpled handkerchief, blueberries, a hat, a draped sheet. The girl drinking from a cup in "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) is an image he repeats in a number of other paintings, and the drinking woman reappears in the left inset of "Dean Martin in 'Some Came Running'" (W10) -- a chiaroscuro painting, based on a photo Salle took, of a woman in antiquated dress drinking from a glass. The ruffled harlequin collar in "Comedy" (W13) looks back toward earlier paintings including "Sextant in Dogtown" (W07). The draped-sheet figure from W06 and W10 returns in the Ghost series photographs. (C22238, leg 2 W12, W09, W10, W13 and M9; synthesis C22166)

The generative version is a small prop library: handkerchief, hat, sheet, chair outline, drinking glass. The props recur across generated scenes with small changes, and the viewer learns them and starts tracking them instead of looking for a story. The props are the continuity; the scenes are disposable. (C22239, leg 2 M9; synthesis C22166)

**Takeaway:** keep a small prop library -- handkerchief, hat, draped sheet, drinking glass, chair outline -- that recurs across scenes with small changes, so the viewer tracks the props instead of looking for a story. (C22240)


## M10 -- No centre, no hierarchy

Motifs knit into a non-hierarchical fabric with no single focal point, or a representational image is twisted into a swirling vortex. Salle called "Mingus in Mexico" (W09) an attempt at "all-over" composition, recalling Pollock: the background is filled with grisaille figures from a Renaissance fresco or tapestry, superimposed with blue-outlined Willow furniture ringing the perimeter, a pink empty speech bubble, a cartoon black Mammy figure, and two smaller canvases inset into spaces cut from the larger surface -- one a monochrome painting copied from a photograph of a woman drinking from a glass, the other a photographic print of drapery. TheArtStory calls his pastiche element a "Vortex," a visual maelstrom left open to interpretation, and notes the vortex motif recurs from 2004 onward. In "Sailor" (W18) the Sailor Moon figure -- recognisable by her buns-and-pigtails hairstyle and sailor suit -- is distorted into a whirling vortex shape beside a representational portrait of a woman in warm grey tones. (C22241, leg 2 W09, W18 and M10; synthesis C22167)

In "Lampwick's Dilemma" (W08, 1989) multi-layered images are instilled with rhythm and velocity, which the eye distills as it zooms in and out of focus over the surface. In "Muscular Paper" (W04) superimposed images compete for prominence and suggest multiple and shifting meanings. The generative rule follows: fill the frame edge to edge with motifs of equal weight, animate by slow rotation, drift, or a vortex pull toward one point that never resolves, and never let one element dominate for long -- when one grows, another grows to compete with it. (C22242, leg 2 W08, W04 and M10; synthesis C22167)

**Takeaway:** fill the frame edge to edge with motifs of equal weight and pull them toward a vortex that never resolves -- when one element grows, grow another to compete with it. (C22243)


## The aesthetic logic: a picture that refuses to add up

Salle's career is one system run for forty-five years: take images from everywhere -- a Brassai photograph, a Ribera head, a Beckmann print, a magazine tapestry, a Peter Arno cartoon, a Michelangelo ceiling, a photograph of a model under a sheet -- repaint each one by hand in a different style, set them side by side across hard intervals, in grisaille against colour, with heads cut off and furniture floating and a word that explains nothing, and bolt a real object to the canvas so the flatness itself is in question. The picture refuses to add up, and that refusal is the work. The viewer is not given a story; the viewer is given an editing table and told to cut. (C22244, leg 2 mechanisms M1-M10; synthesis C22158-C22167)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, borrowing is translation: nothing is pasted, everything is repainted, and the tell of the translation -- the brushwork, the colour shift, the crop -- is what makes the borrowing visible instead of hidden. Second, the interval is the instrument: the gap between panels, the value jump between grisaille and colour, the head cut off at the neck, the empty bubble -- every mechanism is a break, and the breaks are where the picture happens. Third, the props outlive the scenes: the handkerchief, the blueberries, the hat, the draped sheet, the drinking woman, the chair outline return for decades, so the oeuvre has a cast even though no single painting has a plot. The late AI work is the same system with a new tool: the model trained on his own paintings generates the backgrounds, he prints them on linen and overpaints them, and the algorithm is a "joystick" -- the hand still steering, the borrowing still translated, the picture still refusing to add up. (C22245, leg 1 C22029; leg 2 M2, M9; synthesis C22156, C22159, C22166)

The evolution is the proof. 1980-1983: split frames, attached chairs and a carved ear, a name in block letters -- the system arrives whole in "Brother Animal" and "Tennyson." 1985-1987: the system scales up -- the "Muscular Paper" triptych at 8 by 15 feet, the compartmented "Sextant in Dogtown." 1989-1993: the ground goes photographic -- Tapestry Paintings, Ghost Paintings, "Picture Builder." 1995-2007: the system turns on itself -- self-quotation, the harlequin collar looking back, Michelangelo repainted, Sailor Moon in a vortex. 2019-2025: the borrowing goes to cartoons and then to the artist's own archive, via a model he calls a joystick. The teacher burnt his paintings and replaced the hand with a rule; the student kept the hand and spent his career running the rule through it. (C22246, leg 2 W01-W20 evolution; synthesis C22157)
