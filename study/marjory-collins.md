# Marjory Collins: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-03. ig-artist-study #298.

**Site:** Marjory Collins has no personal Instagram presence: verified absence, not a gap. She died in 1985, and the living archive is the federal one: more than 3,000 of her 1942-43 negatives and color transparencies in the Farm Security Administration / Office of War Information Collection at the Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division, public domain as United States government work, plus her personal papers ("Papers of Marjory Collins, 1904-1985") at the Schlesinger Library, Radcliffe Institute, Harvard University. Identity anchors: Marjory Collins, born March 15, 1912, New York City, to Elizabeth Everts Paine and the writer and editor Frederick Lewis Collins (the brief's "born Marjory Hall" and its 1905 date are both wrong; the 1905 fiction would have made her 37 on Stryker's payroll, and PetaPixel's contemporary arithmetic, "then 30-year-old" in 1942, confirms 1912); grew up in Scarsdale, graduated the elite Brearley School; started at Sweet Briar College, made her social debut, married Yale student John "Jack" I. H. Baur in 1933 (not Frederick Collins, who was her father; the brief inverted the relationship), spent a year in Europe including the University of Munich, divorced in 1935. Determined to reject her patrician roots, she moved to Greenwich Village in 1935, studied informally with the avant-garde photographer Ralph Steiner through 1940, attended Photo League lectures and exhibitions, sold her wedding silver to buy a camera, and became a documentary photographer. Her name appeared on the masthead of PM magazine, where Steiner was photo editor; she also worked at U.S. Camera; Black Star, the Associated Press, PIX, and Time, Inc. all represented her work, at a time when relatively few women were full-time magazine photographers. An August 1941 story about Hoboken, New Jersey, got her hired at the New York office of the Foreign Service of the United States Office of War Information; by January 1942 she had transferred to Washington, D.C., to join Roy Stryker's photographic team. Over the next eighteen months she completed approximately fifty assignments and three thousand photographs (January 1942 to June 1943), was praised in Stryker's April 13, 1943 Gossip Sheet ("Marjory is in Buffalo, working on women in industry. This is a special story on women workers for the London Overseas Office"), and then left the collection: her Grimm family photographs are among the last Collins images in the FSA/OWI files. In 1944 she went to Alaska as a freelance photographer for a construction company, then worked in Africa and Europe (Egypt, Ireland, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Italy) for United States government agencies and the commercial press; married and divorced again by 1945; a third marriage failed between 1948 and 1950, and the husband destroyed the bulk of her prints and negatives. For the next thirty years she worked as editor and writer as well as photographer: founded and edited the vanguard publication Peace Concerns (begun 1962), associated with the Center for the Study of Democratic Institutions, worked for the American Public Health Association and edited the Journal of Public Health in the late 1960s. In 1971 she founded Prime Time, "for the liberation of women in the prime of life," the first magazine for older women, published in New York 1971-76 to a print run of 3,000, after finding herself out of a job and on welfare for an operation: "Ageism and sexism hit me hard." Associate of the Women's Institute for Freedom of the Press, 1977. Moved to San Francisco in the 1980s, took an M.A. in American Studies at Antioch College West in 1984 for her study of older women's role in society, and died of cancer in 1985 at seventy-three, researching a pictorial exhibition on women's history. Work at the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston. She called herself "a rebel looking for a cause."

## M1. The named-worker portrait: the caption is a biography

What it is: the LOC caption names the worker with specifics no portrait could supply: age, former trade, new trade, pay. "Gladys Chase, thirty-five, was a beautician for thirteen years before becoming a scarfer." "Emma Dougherty who does a man's work for a man's pay, cleaning out her end-grinding machine." "Mrs. Grimm, a twenty-six year old widow with six children." The caption does the sociology; the portrait does the handshake. The naming is the mechanism, not decoration: a named worker cannot be a type, and the OWI's own brief wanted types ("representative types actually at work"). Collins answered the brief's noun with a proper name.

What it IS, watchably: f10. Gladys Chase, Black, goggles pushed up onto her cap, round glasses, the smallest confident smile, flash-lit out of factory blackness into a plain dark ground. She poses. The pose is dignified, not glamorous, and the caption behind it says beautician, thirteen years, now a scarfer. f12: Nan Hannegan, the door-to-door recruiting badge pinned to her novelty-print blouse, grinning straight into the lens on a Buffalo street. The badge and the grin are the portrait; the caption supplies the mission.

Against the chain: Bubley (#295) named every worker in the caption and fired the flash inches from the face while the room forgot she was there; Collins names her and lets her pose for it, the difference between the census and the introduction. Mark (#292) stayed thirty-two years to earn the name; Collins earns it in one assignment by writing the biography into the caption, which is the magazine worker's shortcut and she does not apologize for it. Nobody else in the chain made the caption do half the portraiture.

**Takeaway:** name the worker in the caption with trade history and pay, then let her pose: the biography is the portrait's other half.

## M2. The unposed shift: motion and company instead of the posed cutie

What it is: Stryker's written brief for the Buffalo women-in-industry story, quoted by Brannan: the photographs should portray "representative types actually at work rather than posed 'cuties,'" showing "the very important contribution made towards final victory and how they have adapted themselves to wartime conditions." Collins took the brief's own words, "actually at work," and used them against the brief's glamour objective, the Rosie-the-Riveter concept the OWI wanted. Halpern's verdict on the result: "Quite different than the posed shots that make up most of the FSA images of industrial labor, these shop floor photographs are valuable for the way they depict motion, worker interactivity (including across lines of race), and the collective nature of work itself." The mechanism's proof of work is the complaint it drew: fellow OWI photographer Alfred Palmer said her pictures showed "the seamy side of life." Palmer's news group wanted the photographs cleaned up for publication; Stryker's photographers wanted to show how deeply Americans sacrificed. She showed the sacrifice; the Grimm images are among the last of hers the collection kept.

What it IS, watchably: f11. Two women painting snubbers at Symington-Gould, caught mid-stroke, barrels stacked around them, the flash freezing the work rather than the worker; one bends to the part, the other watches her own hand. f18: nine women of Republic Steel's processing department eating lunch on stacked metal at night, the whole collective at rest, nobody performing for the lens, the plant's dark swallowing the background. f10: the between-tasks smile, goggles up, the work paused but not posed.

Against the chain: Bubley (#295) worked the brief faithfully until it broke, leaving the uncroppable sign in the frame; Collins worked the brief's own wording until the brief's objective broke, and the collection stopped taking her pictures. Weegee (#281) fixed the rig at f/16 and ten feet; Collins carries the flash into the plant and lets the workers arrange themselves, which is the opposite discipline with the same tool. The chain's other labor photographers pose the worker; Collins photographs the work.

**Takeaway:** shoot the work as motion and company, never as a pose: the interactivity is the image, and the complaint it draws is the proof.

## M3. The day-in-the-life: one family, at work, at play, at home

What it is: the picture-magazine format carried over from PM, where she had learned the "day-in-the-life" story. Brannan's example: the Winn family, Czech, arrived in New York about 1939, "appeared to be thriving" in October 1942, portrayed "at work, at play, and at home" for publications dropped behind enemy lines. The Grimm family got the same treatment in Buffalo: the widow crane operator, the six children under twelve, the older ones keeping house, the younger daughters in a foster home Monday through Friday. The Atkins family at Greenbelt: supper, the bedtime story, the bath. The mechanism converts the assignment from a subject into a duration: one household followed through its day, and the propaganda brief becomes a domestic narrative that outlasts its purpose.

What it IS, watchably: f09. The Grimm Sunday dinner: the mother leaning across the crowded table to serve, six children eating, the radio on the dresser behind them, nobody looking at the camera. The poverty is in the frame (Brannan: "Some images reveal the family's poverty and their struggle to maintain nutrition and housekeeping ideals") and so is the Sunday. f13: the Czech schoolroom, the children crowded around the teacher's desk, one girl looking up mid-lesson with her whole face open. f04: the Army Air Corps major and his wife on the couch, laughing, hands joined, the farewell to Libya folded inside the joke.

Against the chain: Parks (#296) made the return the unit across decades, the dossier accruing time until it accused him; Collins makes the day the unit inside one assignment, the magazine's format doing the work the essay would do. Lange (#293) arrived on the payroll and kept the seeing for herself; Collins arrives on the payroll and gives the day to the family. Maier (#290) never showed the practice; Collins's day-in-the-life exists to be shown, in the magazine, on schedule.

**Takeaway:** take one household through its whole day, at work, at play, at home: the sequence is the story, and the camera is a guest who stays for dinner.

## M4. The hyphenated assembly: gather the group, let belonging argue

What it is: the OWI multiculturalism brief, sharpened by Executive Order 8802 and the overseas leaflet program: show the Chinese-, Czech-, German-, Irish-, Italian-, Jewish-, and Turkish-Americans thriving, to reassure people in Axis countries. Collins's version of the mechanism: assemble the ethnic group in its own setting and photograph the belonging, not the difference. No caption needs to explain it; the assembly is the argument. The upbeat surface is the assignment; the warmth is hers.

What it IS, watchably: f14. Mott Street in the rain: the women with hands over their hearts, faces upturned toward the flag raising, the bunting sagging wet over the doorway at 285, the brick wall holding the whole composition up. f13: the Czech classroom, the children leaning into the lesson together. f17: the Old Mennonite of Lititz, close, smiling under the plain hat, the beard and the glasses doing all the identifying that any caption could.

Against the chain: Parks (#296) restaged the icon with the cleaning woman in its armature, the institution's own language turned; Collins assembles the actual people and lets them be unremarkable, which is the whole point. Bubley (#295) photographed the assignment as a census; Collins photographs it as a gathering. The difference is temperature: the census counts, the gathering includes. Wolcott (#294) drove the payroll's miles alone through the rural South; Collins works the city blocks group by group, and nobody runs her out of town.

**Takeaway:** gather the group in its own place and photograph the belonging: the unremarkable togetherness is the whole argument.

## M5. Kodachrome for the ordinary: the color of groceries and naps

What it is: she carried color film interchangeably with black and white; Brannan's account of the color program is explicit that the photographers carried "cameras loaded with color film, not just black and white" and "would use these interchangeably." The Collins 8c run, about 65 items found on Commons, is not the spectacular but the routine: the Giant Food rooftop ramp, the carrot display, the trailer camp, the tourist home, the park sleepers, the municipal pool. Against the OWI's glamour objective, color here is not excitement but evidence of normal life continuing. The wartime everyday, in Kodachrome, is the propaganda: life chugging along, in color, while the war is elsewhere.

What it IS, watchably: f01. The ramp descending from the roof parking lot, the brick parapet, the parked cars, the couple walking down toward the camera on a July day. f02: the carrot tops mounded high, the handwritten sign, "California Sugar Sweet Carrots 2 lbs 17c," the shopper's hand in the pile. f06: the sleepers stretched out on the Ellipse grass on a hot Sunday, shoes off, the city napping. (Viewed as grayscale derivatives; the color is established by the 8c item series and the curator's program, stated plainly in the Depth line.)

Against the chain: Parks (#296) shot Jim Crow in Kodachrome as evidence, the sign's paint prosecuting; Wolcott (#294) ran the color experiment inside Stryker's unit and refused the pity reading of poverty. Collins is the third grammar: color as the normal, the color of groceries and naps, which is its own wartime claim. The chain's later colorists use color as palette; Collins uses it as routine, a decade before Parks's Life color and three decades before the color decade.

**Takeaway:** point the color camera at the routine, not the remarkable: the carrot sign and the parking ramp, in color, are the argument that life continues.

## M6. The maker in the merchandise: the self-portrait as inventory

What it is: the self-portrait at a public sale (f19): Collins reflected in the arched mirror of a dresser set out on the sidewalk, camera in her hands at chest height, chairs in the foreground, the brick wall behind. She photographs herself as one more item at the sale, filed between the furniture, priced with the chairs. Beside it stands the FSA-OWI staff portrait (f20), the payroll's own headshot. Read against Halpern's record, that she "put up with abuse and even sabotage from male colleagues who felt women had no place in the dark room or out on assignment," the self-portrait is the quiet filing of a counterclaim: she was there, with the camera, on the assignment, and here is the negative to prove it.

What it IS, watchably: f19. The small figure centered in the mirror's arch, the camera held squarely, the ornate dresser doing the framing, the empty chairs in front like an audience. She does not center herself; the merchandise does, and she is inside it.

Against the chain: Parks (#296) filed the maker first, the Speed Graphic self-portrait as the dossier's cover page, the credential as medium. Maier (#290) framed herself in mirrors across a lifetime and let the world develop it. Collins's version is humbler and funnier than either: the maker as inventory at a public sale. Nobody else in the chain put the photographer up for sale with the chairs.

**Takeaway:** put the maker in the frame with the tool, filed among the subjects like one more item: the quietest possible claim of presence, and therefore the hardest to dismiss.

## Evolution

The 1930s: the PM years, the day-in-the-life format learned, the sabotage of male colleagues endured, the wedding silver sold for the camera. January 1942: Washington, the home front in color and black and white, the Giant Food summer, the trailer camps, the rationing lines, the municipal pool. The everyday as propaganda, shot interchangeably in Kodachrome. Fall 1942: New York, Pennsylvania Station's waiting soldiers, the single-day New York Times pipeline (82 images, dispatches to distribution), the Central Park wander, Mott Street in the rain. 1943: Buffalo's women in industry, the seamy side, Palmer's complaint, the last frames in the collection; Baltimore's shipyards; Stockton's braceros; Lititz and the mirror. June 1943: the payroll ends. 1944 to 1950: Alaska, Africa, Europe, then the destroyed archive, the hole in the middle of the career where the negatives were. 1962 onward: the camera becomes the editing desk. Peace Concerns, the Journal of Public Health, Prime Time, the Antioch M.A. The through-line is not a style but a conversion: the assignment photographer becomes the maker of room for other people's pictures, and the rebel looking for a cause finds the cause in other women's prime of life.

## The aesthetic logic: the upbeat surface, the seamy negative

Brannan's two sentences hold the whole logic. The LOC photographs are "upbeat" and "harmonious," reflecting "the OWI editorial requests for visual stories about the ideal American way of life," and Collins herself called the Grimm images her best because they showed "life as it really is." Every mechanism serves both readings at once, which is why the work survived the propaganda office and outlived it. The named worker smiles and the caption carries the pay stub (M1). The shift is unposed and the complaint it drew is the proof (M2). The day-in-the-life is harmonious and the widow works the crane while her children keep house (M3). The hyphenated assembly celebrates and it was shot for leaflets dropped behind enemy lines (M4). The color is cheerful and it documents rationing (M5). The maker files herself among the merchandise, quietly, where the colleagues who sabotaged her cannot unfile her (M6). The seamy side is the negative of the upbeat print, and Collins printed both. Palmer wanted one; Stryker's Gossip Sheet praised the other; she kept making both until the collection stopped taking hers. That is the signature: the propaganda assignment, worked so faithfully in its own words that it broke into documentary.

## Network (evidenced, with direction labels)

- Roy Stryker: one-way, employer. Hired her into the OWI photographic unit after the Hoboken piece; moved her to Washington by January 1942; praised the Buffalo women-in-industry story in the April 13, 1943 Gossip Sheet ("for the London Overseas Office"); his "pictures of life as it is" encouragement is the license she used against the glamour brief.
- Gordon Parks (#296): lateral. Same January 1942 payroll; Brannan: Collins "worked closely with OWI colleagues John Vachon and Gordon Parks and contributed to a substantial photographic study of African Americans."
- John Vachon: lateral. The third hand in that African-American study; same unit, same months.
- Alfred Palmer: one-way, antagonist. Fellow OWI photographer; complained her photographs showed "the seamy side of life"; his news group wanted the pictures cleaned up, her group wanted the sacrifice shown.
- Ralph Steiner: one-way, mentor. Informal study 1935-40; PM's photo editor; her masthead credit; the day-in-the-life format's likely source.
- Esther Bubley (#295): lateral, successor. Arrived fall 1942 as an OWI darkroom assistant while Collins was in the field; the two women overlapped on the wartime payroll, the invisible census and the named introduction.
- Marion Post Wolcott (#294): lateral. Fellow of Stryker's four women; fellow government Kodachrome shooter (her roughly 120 color slides against Collins's 8c run); Wolcott resigned in May 1942 as Collins's eighteen months built.
- Beverly W. Brannan: one-way, archivist. LOC Prints and Photographs curator; wrote the 2009 biographical essay this study leans on; narrates Collins inside the C-SPAN color program.
- Lisette Model and Weegee: one-way, critical comparison. Halpern sets her swing-shift dance series beside their best work and finds it compares favorably.
