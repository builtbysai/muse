# Simon Vouet: Study Notes

**Site:** Simon Vouet (9 January 1590, Paris -- 30 June 1649, Paris), French Baroque painter; the child-prodigy portraitist who spent fourteen years in Italy, was elected president of the Accademia di San Luca in 1624, was summoned home by Louis XIII in 1627 as Premier peintre du Roi, and ran the largest atelier in Paris -- the master who taught Le Brun, Mignard and Le Sueur in the same studio, imported the Italian Baroque into France and then unlearned its darkness for Venetian light, and died a year after his pupils founded the Academie Royale without him.

D2 "who brought the Italian Baroque to Paris" -- HOLDS in substance, HOLDS in the letter. The substance: summoned back by Louis XIII in 1627 after fourteen years in Italy, he "brought with him an Italian idiom hitherto unknown in France that revitalized French painting" (Grove, Barbara Brejon de Lavergnee). The letter: "brought" claims the import, not the invention -- he carried a Roman manner home, and the Paris work that follows is the evidence.

D3 "taught Le Brun, Mignard and Le Sueur in the same studio" -- HOLDS in substance, HOLDS in the letter. The substance: Le Brun was placed in Vouet's studio at eleven (c. 1630, by Chancellor Seguier); Le Sueur entered in 1632 (Sotheby's); Mignard entered in 1633 (Dayton, British Museum, NGA biographies). All three were in the Paris atelier in the early 1630s, the same room, the same years. The letter: "the same studio" claims one atelier, not three separate tutelages -- and the atelier is the point, the largest studio in Paris, run as a school.

D4 "and died outside the Academy his pupils founded" -- HOLDS in substance, HOLDS in the letter, with the reservation carried. The substance: the Academie Royale was founded in 1648 by twenty-two founding members who elected twelve anciens -- Le Brun, Errard, Perrier, Juste d'Egmont, Michel I Corneille, Henri Beaubrun, Laurent de La Hyre, Sebastien Bourdon, Eustache Le Sueur, Simon Guillain, Jacques Sarazin, Gerard van Opstal -- and Vouet's name appears in neither list, nor among the signatories of the 5 September 1648 declaration (see C1348). Le Brun and Le Sueur, his pupils, were among the founders; Vouet died 30 June 1649, a year after the founding, outside the institution. The letter: "his pupils founded" claims the founders included his pupils, which they did, not that all the founders were his pupils. The reservation: Matthiesen Gallery alone says he "helped found" the Academy -- a claim with no documentary support in anything read, reported in the study and not adopted.

## The life

Simon Vouet was born in Paris on 9 January 1590 -- the day is split by one in the literature, 9 January against the Dayton/Grove 8 January, and the study uses 9 January on EN/FR Wikipedia, Encyclopedia.com and Lempertz (see C1373) -- and died in Paris on 30 June 1649, aged fifty-nine, buried in the church of Saint-Jean-en-Greve, now destroyed. His father Laurent Vouet was a Parisian painter "often employed in the service of the Crown" (Grove: born c. 1553-8) who taught Simon the rudiments; his younger brother Aubin Vouet (1595-1641) was also a painter. The family was a painting dynasty by the next generation: his wife Virginia da Vezzo, his son Louis-Rene Vouet, his sons-in-law Michel Dorigny and Francois Tortebat, and his grandson Ludovico Dorigny were all painters (EN Wikipedia). Encyclopedia.com alone calls him grandson of the Master of the King's Falcons -- single-sourced, not corroborated (see C1391).

He was a child prodigy. At fourteen he travelled to England to paint a commissioned portrait -- "a Lady of quality" per Felibien as quoted by Grove (FR Wikipedia dates the trip c. 1604-1606, see C1375). In 1611-12 he went to Constantinople in the entourage of the Baron de Sancy, French ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, to paint portraits; Chalmers says he drew the Grand Signor's picture "by the strength of memory, and from a view of him at the ambassador's audience", and it "proved a great likeness". FR Wikipedia says he painted the portrait of Mustafa I there -- chronologically impossible, since Mustafa I reigned 1617-18 and 1622-23 and the sultan in 1611-12 was Ahmed I; the study says "a Grand Seigneur", unidentified (see C1376). From Constantinople he went to Venice in 1612-13 and was in Rome by 1614; the Italian sojourn is conventionally dated 1613-1627.

Rome made him. He received a pension from the King of France (increased from 1617) and worked for the Barberini (Cardinal Francesco Barberini; Maffeo Barberini became Urban VIII in 1623), Cassiano dal Pozzo, Paolo Giordano Orsini and Vincenzo Giustiniani. In Genoa in 1620-22, working for the Doria princes, he painted Marcantonio Doria (Louvre, 1621) and Gian Carlo Doria, saw Rubens's Circumcision (1606) in Sant'Ambrogio, and met the northern Caravaggists (Gerrit van Honthorst) and Italians (Lanfranco, Manfredi, Orazio Borgianni, Bernardo Strozzi, Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi). He befriended Artemisia Gentileschi in Rome and painted her portrait in 1623. He was elected president (principe) of the Accademia di San Luca in 1624 -- Encyclopedia.com alone adds "the first non-Italian to be elected director, or prince", which the study does not adopt without corroboration (see C1388); how long he held the presidency is unclear (see C1379). His most prominent official Italian commission was an altarpiece for St Peter's, Rome, 1625-26: first planned as St Peter Healing the Sick (drawings survive; a wash study is at Princeton), then abandoned in 1625 in favour of the Adoration of the Cross, with St Francis, St Anthony of Padua and St John Chrysostom, for the Canon Choir chapel behind Michelangelo's Pieta. The painting was destroyed during the 18th century, "though fragments remain" -- which fragments, and where, was not established this run (see C1408); two modelli survive at LACMA. In 1626 he married the Italian painter Virginia da Vezzo (da Vezzi, born c. 1607); she modelled as the Madonna and female saints in his religious pictures. They had five children. Virginia died in France in October 1638, leaving five children aged eleven to a few months old; he remarried in June 1640 to the French widow Radegonde Beranger/Berenger (spelling varies, see C1382), with whom he had three more children.

In 1626 he was offered a brevet du roi with a lucrative pension and housing in the Louvre for himself, his family and his atelier, and he returned in response to a royal summons, pressed by the Duc de Bethune (EN Wikipedia) / on the "pressing recommendations" of Maximilien de Bethune, duc de Sully (FR Wikipedia). He reached Paris in late November 1627, stopping in Venice on the way back -- Encyclopedia.com alone says a four-month stop (see C1380) -- and the Venetian stay is credited with lightening his palette before the Paris years. On his return Louis XIII made him Premier peintre du Roi; Matthiesen notes it was "not yet an official post but a notable distinction". EN Wikipedia's summary judgement, quoting Donald Posner reviewing Crelly: "Vouet was indisputably the leading artist in Paris". Grove: "When he was summoned back to France by Louis XIII in 1627 he thus brought with him an Italian idiom hitherto unknown in France that revitalized French painting."

The Paris commissions came in the order the patrons did. Royal work: portraits, tapestry cartoons and paintings for the Palais du Louvre, the Palais du Luxembourg and the Chateau de Saint-Germain-en-Laye. Lodgings in the Grande Galerie of the Louvre; his posthumous inventory of the Louvre lodging is dated 1639 and 1640. The Chateau Neuf de Saint-Germain-en-Laye work included the Four Cardinal Virtues (1638), now in the Salon de Mars, Versailles. For Cardinal Richelieu in 1632 he worked at the Palais-Royal (then Palais-Cardinal) and at Richelieu's chateau at Rueil -- EN Wikipedia and Lempertz say Chateau de Malmaison, FR Wikipedia says "chateau du Val de Ruel"; whether these are the same building or two Richelieu properties at Rueil is unresolved (see C1381). For the Palais-Royal he painted with Philippe de Champaigne the Galerie des Hommes Illustres (c. 1630-31; Grove calls the results "more uneven", with visible studio contribution). For Richelieu's Jesuit church of Saint-Paul-Saint-Louis he painted the Presentation of Jesus in the Temple (c. 1640-1641, Louvre; 393 x 250 cm REPORTED, FR Wikipedia -- no museum object page was opened, see C1386), with its upper panel The Apotheosis of St Louis now at Rouen; Brejon de Lavergnee calls it "among the greatest masterpieces of seventeenth-century monumental painting". The Palais-Royal murals for Anne of Austria (1644-45, for the dowager Queen Regent after Richelieu's death) are partially supported -- Matthiesen and a Dorigny engraving caption -- but the pictures are lost and undescribed, so the patronage stays flagged, not asserted (see C1349, softened by C1420). Marie de' Medici is NOT a Vouet patron: the Luxembourg cycle was Rubens's, and Vouet's Luxembourg work was for Louis XIII (see C1419). No specific surviving Vouet work for the Luxembourg was identified in any source read (see C1400).

Private hotels and chateaux, 1631-32 and after: the chateau of the president de Fourcy at Chessy (Rinaldo and Armida suite, 1631); the hotel Bullion (24 scenes from the Story of Ulysses for the upper gallery; only eight scenes known, from tapestries at Cheverny); the chateau of Marshal d'Effiat at Chilly (14 mythological subjects on the gallery ceiling, c. 1630-31; Meeting of the Gods lost, Sunrise and Moonrise known from Dorigny engravings); the hotel of the Duc d'Aumont; the chapelle Seguier for Chancellor Pierre Seguier (Adoration of the Magi, destroyed, engraved by Dorigny 1638; a Crucifixion c. 1636-37, Lyon, over the altar with 22 compositions in the panelling; library decor engraved 1640; lower gallery of 12 mythological compositions engraved 1651, alluding to events of Louis XIII's reign); the gallery of the Chateau de Wideville (frescoes in the nymphaeum, Parnassus and Nymph and River God, in situ); Fontainebleau (The Four Elements, lost, engraved by Michel Dorigny 1644). Only two major decorative schemes survive in substance, those for the chateaux of Colombes and Chessy (EN Wikipedia, quoting Brejon de Lavergnee; not independently verified, see C1410); the rest are known from engravings by Michel Dorigny, Francois Tortebat, Claude Mellan, Michel Lasne and Pierre Daret. "Well aware of his posterity, Vouet owned his own printing press and was granted a royal privilege to replicate his designs" (Encyclopedia.com) -- the engraving is not a copy but the publication system: the design travels while the painting rots or burns.

The Hotel Lambert is NOT a Vouet decoration, and the study says so plainly because the brief listed it and two earlier studies' candidate blocks named it in passing. Every source checked says the interiors were decorated by Charles Le Brun (Galerie d'Hercule), Francois Perrier and Eustache Le Sueur (Cabinet de l'Amour, 1646-47; Cabinet des Muses) for Nicolas Lambert de Thorigny (see C1372/C1399/C1417). Vouet's connection is indirect: Le Brun and Le Sueur were his pupils, and the Lambert commission belongs to the pupils' generation. Any text that puts Vouet at the Hotel Lambert is wrong.

He also worked in tapestry cartoons (mostly untraced): an Old Testament series of eight tapestries (including Moses Saved from the Waters, c. 1630, Louvre; six scenes engraved by Tortebat 1665); twelve tapestries after Tasso's Jerusalem Delivered (including Rinaldo in the Arms of Armida); six from the Story of Theagenes and Chariclea (1634-35, Legion of Honor, San Francisco); eight after the Odyssey; twenty-three Loves of the Gods (including Neptune and Ceres, Aurora and Cephalus, Hotel de Sully). The tapestries were "highly coloured" with "unusual wide, handsome borders embellished with fruits, flowers and putti" (Grove).

At the king's express request, in the king's presence, he made pastel portraits of the ladies and gentlemen of the court -- some 60 examples found, divided among French collections (Grove). His posthumous inventory records a gilt-calf book, arms of the king, containing 64 pastel portraits (item 293) and a second small book with 15 heads (item 294); about thirty survive, dated 1632-34 (Jeffares, Dictionary of Pastellists). In these he used pastels of different colours, "an entirely new version of the typical contemporary court portrait executed in three colours" by the Dumonstier family (Grove). He also taught Louis XIII to draw -- attested in Chalmers, Jeffares and Christie's lot notes, but no primary document was cited in anything read; well-attested tradition, not documentarily proven (see C1390).

He "quickly organized a large studio" (Grove/FR Wikipedia), "the largest and most significant studio in Paris" (Matthiesen). Pupils and assistants included, per the sources: Charles Le Brun, Pierre Mignard, Eustache Le Sueur (the three studied as #585-587), Nicolas-Pierre Loir (#586), Valentin de Boulogne (claimed, chronologically tight -- see C1412), Francois Perrier (born the same year as Vouet; "pupil" is strained, see C1425), Nicolas Chaperon, Michel Corneille the elder, Charles Poerson, Pierre Daret, Charles-Alphonse du Fresnoy (confirmed independently -- Mignard's "comrade at Vouet's", Catholic Encyclopedia), Claude Mellan (pupil vs collaborator unresolved, see C1429), Abraham Willaerts (single-sourced, weak, see C1423), Michel Dorigny, Francois Tortebat, Noel Quillerier, Francois Bellin, Pierre Patel the elder, Thomas Blanchet, Claude Francois, and Andre Le Notre, the garden designer of Versailles, "was a student of Vouet" (EN Wikipedia alone -- single-source, see C1422). Virginia da Vezzo taught a drawing course for women in the studio (Grove). Adam Frans van der Meulen was NOT a pupil: he was Pieter Snayers's pupil in Brussels (registered 5 March 1651) and reached Paris only in 1664, fifteen years after Vouet's death -- resolved NO this run. Laurent de La Hyre was in the studio's orbit, not a pupil: standard biography makes him a pupil of Georges Lallemant (see C1421). Poussin was NOT his pupil: Lempertz lists "Philippe de Champaigne, Nicolas Poussin and, in particular, Charles le Brun" as his students, and both the Poussin and the Champaigne claims are wrong (see C1366, C1414); FR Wikipedia records only that Vouet was "en relation avec Nicolas Poussin" in Rome -- acquaintance, not pupillage.

The Poussin overlap is the one rivalry with a dated record. Poussin arrived in Paris on 17 December 1640 to great pomp; Louis XIII embraced him at Saint-Germain-en-Laye and is said to have exclaimed "Voila Vouet bien attrape" -- "there's Vouet caught out" (Blaney thesis, McGill 1999). By 1642 the climate had turned: Poussin was "tired, dejected and somewhat dismayed by the hostility of his critics -- the architect Lemercier, and painters Simon Vouet and Fouquieres" -- and left Paris in July 1642. FR Wikipedia: "Sa gloire souffre du sejour a Paris de Poussin, de 1640 a 1642" -- stated without evidence of personal conflict, and the friendship/jealousy tradition stays tradition only (see C1350, C1384). Claude Lorrain: no evidenced personal link to Vouet was found this run (see C1418). Artemisia Gentileschi is the one Italian contemporary with a documented personal link: Vouet befriended her in Rome and painted her portrait in 1623. Claude Vignon was a friend from the Italian years.

The Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture was founded in 1648: petition presented 20 January 1648 to the nine-year-old Louis XIV, Anne of Austria and the Royal Council; statutes of 13 articles approved in February, published 9 March 1648; twenty-two founding members who in February 1648 elected twelve anciens. Vouet's name appears in neither list, nor among the signatories of the 5 September 1648 declaration (Guillain, De la Hire, Le Brun, Errard, Sarazin, Van Opstal, Corneille, Perrier, Le Sueur, Duguernier, Bourdon, Testelin). Side A (exclusion / no role): EN Wikipedia, quoting Brejon de Lavergnee: Vouet "played no role in the foundation of the Academie Royale", and "his most influential pupil was Charles le Brun, who ... jealously excluded Vouet from the Academie Royale in 1648". Side B (helped found): Matthiesen Gallery alone: "In 1648, shortly before his death, he helped found the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture"; Sartle repeats "In 1648, Vouet founded the French Academy". A third variant (Lempertz): Le Brun quarrelled with Vouet and Vouet was "forced to leave the Academie Royale" -- which presupposes membership, contradicting both the founder/ancien lists and Side A; single-sourced dealer copy, treated as embellishment (see C1383). The documentary lists support Side A, and this study reports both sides and asserts neither as settled, though it notes Side A is the evidenced one (see C1348).

His last years produced, among others, Saint Francis of Paola Resuscitating a Child (1648, Levis, Quebec), which Brejon de Lavergnee calls "the apogee of French seventeenth-century painting", and the Presentation in the Temple (c. 1640-41). "During his lifetime ... Vouet's stature increased continually, his paintings becoming ever more beautiful, particularly in the last decade" (Brejon de Lavergnee via EN Wikipedia). He died 30 June 1649 in Paris. The Russian Wikipedia's claim that he died "in besieged Paris of the Fronde" is single-sourced (see C1387); Paris was blockaded January-March 1649 during the Fronde parlementaire, so the setting is plausible, but the claim that his death is connected to the siege is not evidenced. His posthumous inventory ran 3 July-21 August 1649.

Afterlife: neglected by biographers 1660-1690 ("only Poussin and Rubens were taken seriously"), rehabilitated by Crelly's 1962 monograph and the Grand Palais retrospective 1990-91. A new catalogue raisonne by Arnauld and Barbara Brejon de Lavergnee is "forthcoming" (EN Wikipedia). Current context: the Vouet Season 2026-2028 (FRAME) -- Rennes Musee des Beaux-Arts, Studio Secrets, 20 November 2026-21 March 2027; Musee Fabre, Montpellier, a Herodias investigation, 18 December 2026-18 April 2027; then a major retrospective, the first in the US and in France in over 35 years -- Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco (Legion of Honor) 2027, Petit Palais Paris 2028 -- marking the 400th anniversary of his 1627 return to Paris.

## The evolution: Rome darkness to Paris brightness

Three phases, all evidenced by the works. The through-line is an unlearning: a painter who arrives in Paris as the best Caravaggist in France and spends twenty-two years spending the darkness, until the late pictures are lit by an unreal modelling light and the last one is pure gravity.

**1. Rome, 1614-c. 1622 -- the Caravaggesque years.** Life-size half-length figures on a plain dark ground: a fortune-teller, a guitarist, a David, a Judith, a Jerome -- single figures or tight groups, no landscape, no architecture, no decorative layout. Mary Magdalene (W01, 1614-15, Quirinal Palace) is the earliest surviving dated painting, a half-length devotional female saint of the kind he painted repeatedly in Rome; no reliable visual description was found for it, and the study describes none. The Fortune Teller (W02, 1617, Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Rome; 95 x 135 cm REPORTED) is the first documented Roman picture: commissioned in 1617 by Cassiano dal Pozzo, inscribed on the back "The Egyptian woman, (called) commonly 'the gypsy, fortuneteller of the foolish artisan', painted from life by Simon Vouet 1617". Only three half-figures, in the Caravaggesque manner: a young gypsy reads a young man's palm, the two exchanging an intense gaze in a shadowy, spotlit space, while an old gypsy at the right picks the man's pocket with her left hand and makes a vulgar gesture with her right (thumb stuck between the first two fingers, a Tuscan gesture Dante mentions in Inferno XX, 2). Unlike Caravaggio's two-figure treatment of the same theme, Vouet adds the old woman, and the treatment is burlesque -- "a fine illustration of a Caravaggesque genre scene but its burlesque treatment is quite alien to the Caravaggesque spirit" (Grove). The paint is rich and surprisingly intact, brushwork agile; the vivacious chromatic range is carried by the young gypsy's red sleeve. A second Fortune-Teller (W04, c. 1620, National Gallery of Canada, Ottawa; 120 x 170 cm REPORTED) treats the same subject at nearly twice the area; whether it repeats the Rome composition was not verified, and the study does not transfer W02's description to it. The brief's "Fortune Teller c. 1614-15, Ottawa" is wrong on both the date and the version (see C1392); there are at least two Fortune Tellers plus a Florence copy, and secondary sources routinely conflate them (see C1393). Woman Playing a Guitar (W03, c. 1618, Met, 106.5 x 75.8 cm REPORTED) is a satin-garbed woman at three-quarter length, playing a guitar, gazing off into space -- the Met describes her as "lost in reverie", lit against a dark background, the picture carried by costume, light and a held expression. David with the Head of Goliath (W05, 1620-22, Palazzo Bianco, Genoa; 121 x 94 cm VERIFIED) is the peak of the phase: David half-length, right arm, shoulder and chest bare, holding the giant's huge head in his left hand and glancing over his right shoulder, mouth just slightly open -- "a sophisticated mixture of apprehension and assurance" (Sotheby's). Light comes from outside the frame at the left and bathes almost the whole figure; the Genoa museum calls it "la plus caravagesque de toutes ses oeuvres connues", the most Caravaggesque of all his known works. Judith with the Head of Holofernes (W06, c. 1620-25, Alte Pinakothek, Munich; 97 x 73.5 cm REPORTED) is the paired type: finely dressed, sword in her right hand, the head displayed at her left. Saint Jerome and the Angel (W08, c. 1622-25, NGA, Washington; dimensions 179.8 x 144.8 cm REPORTED from an AI-generated site only, see C1397) is an elderly Jerome at a table laden with writing implements -- globe, scrolls, inkwells, a partly unfurled manuscript -- interrupted by an angel holding a trumpet aloft: the Last Judgement trumpet breaking into the scholar's work, in dramatic chiaroscuro, browns, reds and touches of gold. The Circumcision (W07, 1622) and Saint Agatha's Vision of Saint Peter in Prison (W09, c. 1625, Palazzo Abatellis, Palermo) have no verified visual description and no verified dimensions, and the study describes neither; the Circumcision's location is split between the church of Sant'Angelo a Segno and the Museo di Capodimonte, Naples (see C1403). But even here Vouet is not Caravaggio: the Fortune Teller's burlesque pickpocket joke is alien to the Caravaggesque spirit, and the Birth of the Virgin (c. 1620, San Francesco a Ripa) already shows "the linked series of gestures, the fullness of the draperies, the delicacy of the colour harmony and the care taken to achieve an elegant effect" -- lyricism inside the darkness.

**2. Rome, 1622-1627 -- the turn towards Venice.** The palette grows lighter and the compositions open up. The Clothing of St Francis (1624, San Lorenzo in Lucina) "represents another stage": psychological reactions still Caravaggesque, but the narrative attention, the decorative arrangement and the lighting contrasts recall Bologna and Lanfranco -- and Vouet "subsequently often adopted the decorative option seen here, setting his scene on a staircase with a background of columns". Portraits stay broad and from life, but the figure types grow more elegant and the colour more Venetian. The Self-Portrait (W10, c. 1626-27, Musee des Beaux-Arts de Lyon; 45 x 36.5 cm REPORTED) is the hinge picture as a person: a small, frontal bust, bearded, with a forelock of hair, painted in Rome on the eve of the return -- possibly a marriage gift to Virginia da Vezzo, whom he married in 1626 ("may have been" -- keep the qualifier; see C1405). Time Defeated by Love, Beauty and Hope (W11, 1627, Prado; 107 x 142 cm VERIFIED) is the hinge in a single canvas: signed and dated on the front, lower left corner, "SIMON VOUET, ROME. 1627". Saturn, who represents Time, has fallen beside his attributes, a scythe and an hourglass. Beauty -- possibly a portrait of Virginia da Vezzo -- holds him by the hair, brandishing a lance over him, while Hope threatens him with a hook (her iconographic symbol; other accounts call it her anchor), and three cupids pluck the feathers from his wings. Time is defied by Love -- a reversal of the traditional roles, in which Time devours everything. Grove calls it the picture that "foreshadowed his new interest in Venice and the privileged role of colour, moving figures, wind-blown draperies, light colouring and landscape background" -- the Roman tenebrism giving way to Venetian brightness in a single canvas. Allegory of Peace (W12, 1627, Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Rome) is the least-verified work in this study: date, location and the 350 x 250 cm dimensions all rest on a single mirror family, no museum page or scholarly source was found, and the brief's "(Louvre)" is wrong (see C1398) -- the study keeps the title and drops the rest. Institutionally this is also the peak: president of the Accademia di San Luca in 1624, pensioned by the French crown, patronised by the Barberini, Cassiano dal Pozzo and Vincenzo Giustiniani, commissioned for St Peter's.

**3. Paris, late 1627-1649 -- the decorator.** Vouet reaches Paris in late November 1627 as Premier peintre du Roi and becomes, in Wikipedia's phrase, "indisputably the leading artist in Paris". The easel pictures change scale and temperature. The Toilette of Venus (W13, 1629, Cincinnati Art Museum; 183 x 153 cm REPORTED, orientation unsettled, see C1396) is signed and dated 1629 -- the brief's "1640, Carnegie Museum of Art, Pittsburgh" is wrong, and no Carnegie source was found (see C1395). Because it is signed and dated, it is a benchmark in the archaeometry programme establishing a pigment chronology across Vouet's career; its arrangement of figures, mirror, drapery and palette were not described in any source read, and the study does not invent them. Lot and His Daughters (W14, 1633, Musee des Beaux-Arts de Strasbourg; 160 x 130 cm REPORTED) is the canonical Paris-period Old Testament history piece: after the destruction of Sodom, Lot and his daughters take refuge in a cave; the daughters, believing no men remain, get their father drunk and lie with him, one each night -- the subject, judged shameful in the Middle Ages, depicted frequently from the Renaissance because it offered an erotic subject, and the erotic charge carried by the daughters' bodies is the reason the subject was painted at all. Madonna and Child (W15, 1633, NGA; 109 x 89 cm REPORTED) is "the earliest and the only signed and dated version of his great series devoted to the Virgin and Child" (NGA 2016 Annual Report); Vouet "painted a dozen compositions of the Virgin and her son at half-length, many of which were reproduced as prints" -- the Madonna type Virginia da Vezzo modelled for, now a repeatable half-length product of the Paris studio. The Muses Urania and Calliope (W16, c. 1634, NGA; 79.8 x 125 cm VERIFIED, oil on wood, "Simon Vouet and Studio") are two female figures seated on the ground before a classical podium: Urania, muse of astronomy, diadem of six stars, a celestial globe on which she rests, in blue and white; Calliope, muse of epic poetry, in red and yellow, a book in her lap. Serene and structured rather than dramatic -- one panel of a decorative Muse cycle for a Paris interior, and the studio's hand visible in the attribution. Compare the Parnassus, or Apollo and the Muses (c. 1640, Museum of Fine Arts, Budapest; 88 x 221 cm REPORTED): the same Muse material as a full nine-figure frieze, Apollo Musagetes at the centre playing a lyre, instead of a two-figure panel. Allegory of Virtue (W17, c. 1634, Louvre; 210 x 113 cm REPORTED) is a tall, narrow allegorical single figure whose title history is the verified substance -- Virtue, once Victory, now catalogued in the Louvre's orbit as an allegory of good government -- and must not be merged with the different Louvre allegory "Allegorie du Bon Gouvernement" (1644-45, RF 1961 12) (see C1402). The Crucifixion (W18, 1636-37, Musee des Beaux-Arts de Lyon; 216 x 146 cm VERIFIED) was commissioned by Chancellor Pierre Seguier for the private chapel of his town house, placed over the main altar, and is "characterized by an economy of means" (MBA Lyon): Christ, leaning his head to one side, dies at the precise moment the sky darkens at midday; a beam of light outlines his body, which follows a classical ideal, its whiteness contrasting with the stormy background, reinforced by a low-angle view. At the foot of the cross, two holy women and Saint John assist the Virgin Mary, who has fainted; Saint Mary Magdalene, isolated at the left, seems seized with dread -- seen from behind, startled, arms outstretched in recoil. The group at the foot is bathed in an unreal light "which recalls Vouet's training in the Rome of Caravaggio"; it models ample drapery, accenting vivid colours and illuminating pastel tones. The faces and the light "reinforce all the theatricality and solemnity of the scene", and Vouet "renders the range of emotions through an exchange of gestures and significant glances. Curves and counter-curves reveal the compassion and spiritual intimacy of the three main figures." Allegory of Wealth (W19, c. 1640, Louvre; 170 x 124 cm REPORTED) is a woman, symbol of Fortune and Abundance, in a yellow robe, surrounded by putti and jewels, with gold and silver plate -- but the title flipped without the paint changing: the traditional Allegory of Wealth (La Richesse, named by Frederic Villot in the mid-19th century) is now, on Milovanovic's 2015 re-reading, the Allegory of Contempt for Wealth, and the Louvre now entitles it Allegory of Faith and of Contempt for Wealth (see C1401). The Presentation of Christ in the Temple (W20, c. 1640-41, Louvre) is the monumental, crowded altarpiece -- "one of the most Baroque of all Vouet's compositions" (WGA) -- Simeon receiving the Child in a tall, multi-figure architectural setting, its main influences "the cool order of Domenichino and the much softer, more sensual approach of Guido Reni", in which Vouet "reached the level of accomplishment found in the work of Orazio Gentileschi". Venus and Adonis (1642, Getty) is the late Paris palette in one sentence: "Adonis wears a yellow-gold robe over a fuchsia-colored tunic and lavender sandals, and Venus is partially clad in a white undergarment and a subdued violet-blue robe. Her creamy white skin contrasts with the darker, smooth skin of Adonis" (Google Arts & Culture) -- Venus and Adonis meeting each other's eyes, "their bodies still half entwined", the poignant moment from Ovid in which the mortal Adonis, captivated by hunting, rejects Venus's advances, foreshadowing his death. The Four Cardinal Virtues (1638, Salon de Mars, Versailles) are four separate allegories -- Temperance, Force, Prudence, Justice -- for a royal interior, the allegory as room decoration rather than easel picture. Saint Francis of Paola Resuscitating a Child (1648, Levis, Quebec) is the late-style endpoint: the aged, bearded saint in a brown robe, kneeling and holding a staff, resurrecting a child in an architectural space with columns and arches -- and Brejon de Lavergnee's verdict, that in the works of the 1640s "one can see Vouet concluding his career with pictures of an immense gravity informed by an intense spiritual energy. Images of the greatest force, these paintings constitute the apogee of French seventeenth-century painting."

What changed, in one line per register. Light: single-source tenebrism on a dark ground (W02-W08) to even, modelling, "unreal" light that picks out white flesh against stormy skies (W18) and bright, decorative illumination (W16, Venus and Adonis). Palette: blacks, browns, whites (Roman church pictures) to Venetian clear colour (W11) to the late Paris palette of fuchsia, lavender, violet-blue, yellow-gold, creamy white. Figure: half-length, from-life, mouth open, turned to the spectator (W03, W05, W10) to full-length, multi-figure, di sotto in su, figures "linked like great splashes of colour" in architectural space (W18, W20, the ceilings). Scale: 95-121 cm cabinet pictures (W02, W05) to 160-216 cm history pieces and altarpieces (W14, W18, W20) to whole rooms and galleries (Seguier, Chilly, Bullion) of which only fragments and engravings survive. Authorship: autograph Roman pictures, often signed by circumstance (the 1617 inscription, the 1627 signature on W11, the signed and dated 1629 Toilette and 1633 Madonna) to Paris studio production, with the Muses (W16) explicitly "Vouet and Studio" and the allegories circulating in studio versions. Grove's critical judgement, which the study carries rather than smoothing over: his religious paintings "showed no further stylistic development during the last two decades of his career", his vocabulary "restricted" and his secular and religious worlds "interchangeable" -- while conceding the audacious colour combinations, rich brushwork and mastery of space, perspective and large surfaces, and insisting that "a great decorator" was, in the first half of the 17th century, an accolade, not an insult. The late 1640s then reverse the charge: the Presentation, the Crucifixion and above all Saint Francis of Paola show decoration giving way, at the very end, to gravity. He dies in June 1649, a year after the Academie Royale was founded without him.

## M1 -- The chiaroscuro unlearned: tenebrism deliberately abandoned

Vouet's career is a two-phase light system, and the second phase is a decision, not a drift. Italian period: Caravaggesque -- life-size figures on plain dark grounds, dramatic light contrasts, restricted palette of blacks, browns and whites (Temptation of St Francis, Birth of the Virgin; Grove). Paris period: "knowing that Caravaggio and his followers were never really appreciated in the French capital, Vouet gradually altered his manner" (Encyclopedia.com); during the Venice stop "he modified his heavy chiaroscuro with the grace, fluidity, and color of northern Italian painting" (Encyclopedia.com). Lempertz: "whilst strongly influenced by chiaroscuro and Caravaggio at the beginning, he later chose fresher colours and subtler shadowing." Time Defeated (W11, 1627) is the hinge in one canvas: painted in Rome, signed and dated there, it already has the moving figures, wind-blown draperies, light colouring and landscape background of the Paris work. The mechanism is a light system swapped mid-career: hard single-source tenebrism early, soft diffused light with bright local colour late. Watchable behaviour: the same saint or goddess first carved out of black, later floating in pale light -- and the swap is visible as a sequence, not a single style.

**Takeaway:** unlearn the darkness on purpose -- paint the same figure twice, once carved out of black by a single hard light, once floating in pale diffused light with bright local colour, and let the swap be the subject: the light system itself changes, and the picture is the record of the change.

## M2 -- Di sotto in su: the ceiling as a stage seen from below

Veronese's foreshortened perspective, absorbed in Italy. Grove: the Paris schemes "introduced a new type of illusionistic decoration with steep perspective"; ceilings in the Italian style, "figures are shown di sotto in su with striking foreshortening" (Chilly); religious works show "realistic and powerful figures shown in movement and da sotto in su". The Presentation uses steps "represented di sotto in su, de bas en haut" (FR Wikipedia). The mechanism: figures foreshortened along a steep vertical axis so a flat ceiling reads as open sky; the viewer must look up and the picture completes itself from below. The staging that goes with it is theatrical: FR Wikipedia's analysis of the Presentation reads a diagonal ascent "d'en bas a droite au haut a gauche", depth accentuated by repoussoir figures in the foreground, and "le vide central" -- two unequal pyramidal groups with the swaddled child at the centre "au-dessus d'un vide audacieux"; the spectator is invited to climb the steps, di sotto in su, like Simeon. The picture is staged like theatre -- diagonal thrust, foreground figures as a proscenium, a deliberate empty centre that the eye must cross.

**Takeaway:** build the picture from below -- figures foreshortened up a steep vertical axis so the flat surface reads as open sky, a diagonal climbing from lower right to upper left, repoussoir figures as a proscenium, and a deliberate empty centre the eye must cross to reach the child.

## M3 -- Drawing first: black chalk, white heightening, sweeping contour

"Without doubt one of the outstanding seventeenth-century draughtsmen, equal to Annibale Carracci and Lanfranco" (Pierre Rosenberg via EN Wikipedia). His advice to Mellan: "to draw and to set this study above all others" (Grove). Paris drawings are "mainly executed in black chalk highlighted with white" and show debt to Annibale Carracci and Domenichino (Grove); the drawings are "elegant figure studies that reveal his use of firm, sweeping contours that effortlessly render the human form in motion" (Encyclopedia.com). FR Wikipedia on a Beaux-Arts sheet: "pierre noire tres grasse, technique chere a l'artiste", thick hatching rendering modelling, "arcades et pommettes saillantes, nez vigoureux, orbites creusees par l'ombre". Few Italian-period drawings survive; the Louvre holds the main corpus (Barbara Brejon de Lavergnee inventory, 1987). The mechanism: contour first, modelling by hatching, white chalk as light -- the drawing is the design, the painting the colouring-in. Young painters "eagerly approached" him "wishing to extend their training and study drawing" (Grove); his wife taught a drawing course for women; the atelier "utilized, trained, and influenced more than a generation of painters and printmakers" (Encyclopedia.com). The studio was a transmission engine: one man's manner becomes a national school by teaching, not by painting alone.

**Takeaway:** draw the contour before the colour -- firm, sweeping black-chalk outlines, modelling by thick hatching, white chalk as the only light, the figure existing first as a line in motion and only second as a painted body, and let the finished picture carry the drawing in it.

## M4 -- The modello and the workshop: the small sketch that runs the big room

Two modelli for the St Peter's altarpiece survive (LACMA, 1625); Grove notes a wash-over-black-chalk study (St Peter Healing the Sick, Princeton) "of particular interest as it appears to refer to a prestigious commission". The Paris atelier ran on his designs executed by pupils (Chalmers: pupils "from his instructions ... well knew how to execute his designs"). The mechanism: a small-scale oil sketch fixes composition, light and colour; the large canvas is a scaled-up execution, often by other hands -- which is why "few of his canvases are signed and dated" and attribution is hard (Grove). The authorship register proves it: autograph Roman pictures, often signed by circumstance, give way to Paris studio production, with the Muses (W16) explicitly "Vouet and Studio" and the allegories circulating in studio versions. The same mechanism is what makes the lost decorations recoverable: the design survives in the modello and the engraving while the wall is gone. The picture is a contract: the sketch is the promise, the room is the delivery, and other hands do the delivering.

**Takeaway:** fix the picture small and let other hands build it big -- one oil sketch that settles the composition, the light and the colour, then the same design scaled up across a wall by the workshop, the sketch and the wall shown side by side so the viewer can see the promise and the delivery at once.

## M5 -- Pastel as a new court portrait: likeness as a live performance

At the king's express request, in the king's presence, he made pastel portraits of the ladies and gentlemen of the court -- some 60 examples found, divided among French collections (Grove). His posthumous inventory records a gilt-calf book, arms of the king, containing 64 pastel portraits (item 293) and a second small book with 15 heads (item 294); about thirty survive, dated 1632-34 (Jeffares). In these he used pastels of different colours, "an entirely new version of the typical contemporary court portrait executed in three colours" by the Dumonstier family (Grove): "simple, sober but lively images, in which the model seems vividly present" (Grove). Also the Portrait of Angelique Vouet (1635-38, pastel, Louvre) and the Portrait of Cardinal Mazarin (c. 1642, pastel, Louvre). The mechanism: dry colour sticks used for quick, present-tense likeness in the king's presence -- portraiture as live performance, not studio construction. The sitter is caught, not composed: the chalk is fast, the sitting is short, the likeness is a record of a moment in a room rather than a monument built over weeks. The book is the system: sixty-four heads in a gilt-calf volume, the court collected as a set, each head the same size, the same speed, the same present tense -- a portrait gallery that fits in a book.

**Takeaway:** catch the likeness live -- pastel sticks, a short sitting, the model vividly present because the drawing was made while they were there, and collect the heads in a book: sixty-four portraits the same size, the same speed, the court as a set of quick, present-tense faces.

## M6 -- The anamorphic elephant: the picture that only exists in a mirror

Vouet's red-chalk drawing Eight Satyrs Admiring the Anamorphosis of an Elephant (1624-27, Hessisches Landesmuseum, Darmstadt; engraved by Hans Troschel, Met 45.97(78)) shows satyrs staring at a cylindrical mirror in which a distorted, unreadable drawing resolves into an elephant. The design served as frontispiece to the second edition of Jean-Francois Niceron's La Perspective curieuse (Paris, 1652) -- the most famous treatise on anamorphosis (Met). Gomez (2016) treats it as the meeting of emblem tradition and optics. The mechanism: a drawing deliberately distorted so that only its reflection in a cylinder is legible -- the image is a function of the viewpoint, not of the surface. This is the most directly generative mechanism in Vouet's oeuvre: the picture is computed by optics. The satyrs are the audience inside the picture, doing what the viewer must do: they look at the wrong thing (the stretched smear on the paper) and the right thing (the elephant in the mirror) at the same time, and the joke is that the smear is the picture and the elephant is the reflection. The drawing's date (1624-27) rests on the Mahon essay; Troschel died in 1628 in Rome, so the engraving predates 1628; the frontispiece use (Niceron, 2nd ed., 1652) is posthumous (see C1389).

**Takeaway:** draw the picture so it cannot be seen -- stretch the elephant across the surface until it is an unreadable smear, and let it resolve only in a cylindrical mirror, the image a function of the viewpoint: the viewer must move to the one place where the picture exists, and the satyrs inside the frame show them how.

## M7 -- The pearly palette: pale flesh against saturated drapery

Grove: "embellished by a pearly treatment of the flesh". Venus and Adonis (1642, Getty): "Adonis wears a yellow-gold robe over a fuchsia-colored tunic and lavender sandals, and Venus is partially clad in a white undergarment and a subdued violet-blue robe. Her creamy white skin contrasts with the darker, smooth skin of Adonis" (Google Arts & Culture). Encyclopedia.com: "extravagant color, lively facture, and dazzling light of sixteenth-century Venetian artists" blended with Fontainebleau mannerism, Carracci classicism and Caravaggio naturalism. FR Wikipedia: "couleurs brillantes", "attitudes declamatoires, des tetes d'expression, les poses recherchees". The mechanism: near-white flesh as the light source of the picture, ringed by one or two saturated drapery colours (crimson, ultramarine, gold) -- colour as theatre lighting. The Crucifixion (W18) is the same mechanism in a religious register: the beam of light outlines Christ's classical body, its whiteness contrasting with the stormy background, while the women's robes -- yellows, oranges, blues, pinks -- contrast with the sombre browns and greys behind them (that colour list comes from an AI-generated description; treat as REPORTED, not museum prose). The linked-gesture chain belongs here too: Grove on the Birth of the Virgin (c. 1620, San Francesco a Ripa) praises "the linked series of gestures" that binds the composition -- figures connected by a relay of hands, gazes and drapery folds, the composition a circuit and the eye travelling it like current. And on the Clothing of St Francis, "the presentation of the characters' psychological reactions" with "the decorative arrangement". The flesh is the lamp; the drapery is the coloured glass around it; the gestures are the wiring.

**Takeaway:** light the picture from the skin -- near-white flesh as the lamp, one or two saturated drapery colours (crimson, ultramarine, gold) as the glass around it, the figures linked by a relay of hands and gazes so the eye travels the composition like current, and let the colour be theatre lighting, not description.

## M8 -- The decorative machine: the room as the picture

The Paris schemes "extended to and occupied the ceilings, in the Italian style" (Grove); the Hotel Seguier chapel used "powerful trompe l'oeil effects to suggest architectural elements and space" (Grove); the Bullion Ulysses gallery and the Chilly ceiling are multi-canvas narrative machines; tapestry borders carry "fruits, flowers and putti" (Grove). The mechanism: painting, architecture and textile designed together -- the room is the picture, and the picture's job is to dissolve the room. The work is now mostly decoration -- ceilings in steep di sotto in su perspective, galleries of mythology and allegory, tapestry cartoons, pastels of the court -- executed through a large studio. The figure type stabilises: realistic, powerful figures in movement, "linked like great splashes of colour" (Grove), pearly flesh, multiplied draperies and splashes of colour, drapery studied from life and embellished. The scale register tells the story: 95-121 cm cabinet pictures (W02, W05) to 160-216 cm history pieces and altarpieces (W14, W18, W20) to whole rooms and galleries (Seguier, Chilly, Bullion) of which only fragments and engravings survive. The secular and religious worlds are interchangeable by design -- the same figure type, the same drapery, the same light, whether the subject is Venus or the Crucifixion -- and Grove's criticism ("no further stylistic development during the last two decades", "restricted" vocabulary) is the same fact stated as a fault: the machine, once built, kept running. "A great decorator" was, in the first half of the 17th century, an accolade, not an insult.

**Takeaway:** dissolve the room -- painting, architecture and textile designed together, the ceiling opening to sky, the gallery telling one story across many canvases, the tapestry border carrying fruit, flowers and putti, and let the picture's job be the room's disappearance: the viewer stands inside the painting, not in front of it.

## The aesthetic logic

Vouet's aesthetic is import, unlearning, and decoration -- in that order. The construction is Italian: Caravaggesque tenebrism learned in Rome (life-size figures on plain dark grounds, dramatic light, restricted palette), Bolognese classicism from the Carracci, Domenichino and Reni (the cool order, the staircase with a background of columns), Venetian colour from Veronese and Titian (clear colouring, large-scale composition, di sotto in su ceilings), and Fontainebleau mannerism carried over from the French court tradition (Encyclopedia.com's "astute blend of sixteenth-century mannerist French court art at Fontainebleau, the Romano-Bolognese classicism of the Carracci, the naturalism of Caravaggio, and the extravagant color, lively facture, and dazzling light of sixteenth-century Venetian artists"). The unlearning is Parisian: knowing Caravaggio was never really appreciated in the French capital, he swaps the hard single-source light for soft diffused light and bright local colour, and the palette goes from blacks, browns and whites to fuchsia, lavender, violet-blue, yellow-gold, creamy white. The decoration is the destination: "slightly elongated monumental figures with swirling draperies slowly float across the surfaces of his large canvases" (Encyclopedia.com); "ample compositions, theatrical perspectives, declamatory attitudes, expressive heads, sought-after poses and brilliant colours" (FR Wikipedia, citing Thuillier). He works by drawing (black chalk heightened with white, firm sweeping contours), by modello (the small sketch that runs the big room), and by transmission (the atelier as a school, the engraving as the publication system, the pastel book as the court in a volume). The through-line is the room: a painter who starts with a half-length figure on a dark ground and ends with whole galleries, whose secular and religious worlds are interchangeable because both are decoration, and whose late pictures -- the Presentation, the Crucifixion, Saint Francis of Paola -- turn the decoration back toward gravity, "an immense gravity informed by an intense spiritual energy". The reputation curve runs with it: dominant in his lifetime ("indisputably the leading artist in Paris"), threatened only by Poussin's Paris stay of 1640-42, neglected by biographers 1660-1690 ("only Poussin and Rubens were taken seriously"), rehabilitated by Crelly in 1962 and the Grand Palais retrospective of 1990-91, and now, four hundred years after his return, given a season of his own -- Rennes, Montpellier, San Francisco, the Petit Palais. The 18th-century view is itself evidence of how total his takeover was: Chalmers concedes he "destroy[ed] the insipid and barbarous manner which then reigned" while denying him "genius for grand compositions" and knowledge of "the rules of perspective" -- the hostile verdict of a generation that had already been remade in his image. The study's duty is to keep the two facts together: he was the master who made French painting Italian, and then made it French again by unlearning the darkness.

## The network

**Teachers:** Laurent Vouet (father), the only formal master -- taught Simon the rudiments in Paris. **The models, in Rome:** Caravaggio (dramatic lighting, learned through the Roman Caravaggisti); the Carracci academy in Bologna (classicism, drawing); Veronese and Titian (Venetian colour, di sotto in su); Guercino, Lanfranco, Guido Reni, Domenichino (the Bolognese tempering); Rubens (seen in Genoa -- the Circumcision of 1606 in Sant'Ambrogio). **The Roman circle:** Artemisia Gentileschi (friend; portrait 1623 -- the one Italian contemporary with a documented personal link); Orazio Gentileschi; Valentin de Boulogne (fellow Frenchman in Rome, Vouet the senior figure; formal pupilage chronologically tight, see C1412); Claude Vignon (friend from the Italian years); Jacques de Letin; Nicolas Poussin (acquaintance in Rome -- NOT a pupil, see C1366); Claude Mellan (engraver, pupil vs collaborator unresolved, see C1429); Cassiano dal Pozzo, Paolo Giordano Orsini, Vincenzo Giustiniani and the Barberini (patron-collectors). **Patrons:** Louis XIII (royal pension from 1617/18; summoned him home in 1627 as Premier peintre du Roi; commissioned the Louvre, Luxembourg and Saint-Germain work; also his pupil in drawing, see C1390); Cardinal Richelieu (Palais-Royal and the Rueil chateau, 1632; the Saint-Paul-Saint-Louis Presentation); Anne of Austria (Palais-Royal murals 1644-45 for the dowager Queen Regent -- partially supported, pictures lost, see C1349/C1420); Chancellor Pierre Seguier (the Hotel Seguier chapel, the most ambitious decorative scheme; also the Academie's protecteur); the Doria princes of Genoa (Marcantonio and Gian Carlo Doria); president de Fourcy (Chessy), Claude de Bullion (Hotel Bullion), Marshal d'Effiat (Chilly), the Duc d'Aumont, the Chateau de Wideville. Marie de' Medici is NOT a patron (see C1419). **Pupils and the line forward:** Charles Le Brun (placed at eleven by Seguier; later organised all interior decorative painting at Versailles and dictated the official style under Louis XIV -- and jealously excluded Vouet from the Academie Royale in 1648); Pierre Mignard (entered 1633, left for Rome late 1635; Le Brun's great rival, later Director of the Academie after Le Brun's death); Eustache Le Sueur (#587, entered 1632, quarrel reported 1641); Nicolas-Pierre Loir (#586, pupil of Bourdon and of Vouet, then disciple of Poussin); Charles-Alphonse du Fresnoy (confirmed via Mignard's "comrade at Vouet's"); Valentin de Boulogne (claimed, see C1412); Francois Perrier (Roman associate, not a pupil in any strict sense, see C1425); Nicolas Chaperon, Michel Corneille the elder, Charles Poerson, Pierre Daret, Abraham Willaerts (Wikipedia list only this run -- the study does not lean on them without a second source); Michel Dorigny and Francois Tortebat (sons-in-law and engravers of the lost decorations); Andre Le Notre (Wikipedia only, see C1422). **The family workshop:** Virginia da Vezzo (first wife, painter, model for the Madonnas, taught a drawing course for women); son Louis-Rene Vouet (Paris, 4 December 1637-1675, verified this run via Jeffares); daughter Jeanne-Angelique Vouet (1630-1674, married Michel Dorigny in 1648); grandson Ludovico Dorigny, painter. **The engravers as transmitters:** Perrier, Dorigny, Tortebat, Mellan, Lasne, Daret -- the print afterlife by which a painter whose decorations are mostly lost stayed visible, and the reason Vouet owned his own press and held a royal privilege. **Rivals:** Poussin (the Roman rivalry, then the Paris overlap 1640-42, "Voila Vouet bien attrape"); Claude Lorrain (no evidenced personal link, see C1418); Jacques Blanchard and Claude Vignon (contemporaries; Blunt pairs them in one chapter, "Simon Vouet's Career in Paris, and his Contemporaries Blanchard and Vignon"). **No documented contact** in the sources read between Vouet and Loir beyond the pupilage itself, or between Vouet and Bourdon beyond the Academy lists. The link is institutional and genealogical, not personal: Vouet taught the men who founded the Academy he never joined.

**Adjacent artists (the map):** Francois Perrier (Roman associate who carried Vouet's decorative language into the Hotel Lambert, painting there alongside Le Brun and Le Sueur); Artemisia Gentileschi (the documented Roman friendship, portrait 1623); Claude Vignon (friend from the Italian years, fellow Frenchman in Rome, later a Paris contemporary).
