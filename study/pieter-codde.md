## The life

Pieter Jacobsz. Codde was baptised on 11 December 1599 in the Oude Kerk, Amsterdam, the fourth of nine children of Jacob Pietersz Codde -- a paalknecht, a minor city official who collected port fees, registered vessels and delivered mail to shippers -- and Maria Jans. The family lived in the Paalhuis near the river IJ on the Nieuwe Brug, by virtue of the father's office (leg 1 F1).

His training is unknown, and the Amsterdam Guild of Saint Luke records that could settle it are lost. He was long said to have studied with Frans Hals; the modern position (Leiden Collection 2024; National Gallery) is that this is unproven, and that his master was more likely the portrait painter, inn-keeper, actor and art-dealer Barent van Someren (1572-1632) or possibly Cornelis van der Voort (1576-1624). Van Someren is also the host of the 1625 brawl below, which makes him the likeliest teacher on two counts at once: the man who may have taught Codde is the man in whose garden house Codde struck Duyster (leg 1 C9).

In 1623 Codde married the eighteen-year-old Marritje Arents -- the marriage documents already list his occupation as "painter" -- and their daughter Clara was baptised in April 1624. Clara died in 1635. By 1628/1630 Codde was living on the Sint Anthoniesbreestraat, the artists' quarter; his neighbours included Rembrandt and Pieter Potter. In 1631 his capital was estimated at only 1,000 guilders; by the separation inventory of 2 May 1636 he owned an extensive collection of paintings (many unframed, by Haarlem-connected artists including Frans Hals, Pieter Claesz and Jan van Goyen), porcelain, silver and silk -- evidence he was also an art dealer. In 1657 he bought Keizersgracht 385 for 5,000 guilders and lived there the rest of his life (leg 1).

The brawl is the first document of his adult life, and it is also the first document of the guardroom's inventors together in one room. The notarial testimony (Amsterdam City Archives, Notary W. Cluijt, 1 July 1625; witnesses Louwijs du Pree, about 34, lieutenant in Venetian service, and Michael van de Sande, about 42; full transcription and translation in the Leiden Collection 2024, Appendix 1, taken from Playter 1972) records that on Whit Monday, 19 May 1625 -- the second day of Pentecost -- at a gathering at the estate "Meerhuysen", where Barent van Someren was tenant, talk turned to a certain Pieter Raes, with whom Duyster had earlier quarrelled. Codde said "At that time, I was still your friend"; Duyster replied "You were not my friend; I can prove otherwise." Codde: "All I said was that you were too know-it-all." Duyster, trying to defuse it: "Let's leave it at that... We can settle this issue next time." Codde: "If you wish." Duyster then challenged him: "If you are an honorable man, come tomorrow if you dare, with a suitable sword, and if you don't come, I'll consider you a scoundrel." Codde stood up and struck Duyster in the face with an earthenware jug, then hit him in the face with a pewter tankard, so that blood spattered over Duyster's clothes; Duyster grew dizzy and Codde was thrown out of the house by bystanders. It was one-sided: Codde hit Duyster; Duyster did not hit Codde. The other man is Willem Cornelisz. Duyster -- EN Wikipedia writes "Cornelis Duyster", a painter who does not exist, and its "the two hit each other in the face with jars" is wrong on both counts (leg 1; leg 3 K18, K20).

The marriage broke down in 1635-36. On the eve of Twelfth Night, 6 January 1636, after a drinking evening with another couple, Codde allegedly raped the twenty-two-year-old maidservant Aefge Jans in the kitchen; three city midwives examined her in the torture chamber and found no evidence of rape; Codde said he was too drunk to recall. EN Wikipedia says "the couple divorced" in 1636. The Leiden Collection says instead that Codde separated from his wife and remained married: in 1651 he asked witnesses to report on his wife's relationships with other men -- Sijmon Hop, with whom she lived about three years as husband and wife and by whom she was twice pregnant; Willem Blau, about six months; and Pieter Potter, found in bed with her by a neighbour, Cristina Anseel, who then ate kandeel (egg liqueur) with them. The 1651 testimony is transcribed in full in the Leiden Collection, Appendix 2. The study uses "separated, not divorced" (Leiden) and the name Marritje Arents -- NL Wikipedia's "Anna" appears in no other source and is likely an error (leg 1 C6; leg 3 N10, K23, K24).

He rarely signed or dated paintings after 1645, but remained active as a painter into the 1650s; in 1672 he was among the professionals the dealer Gerrit Uylenburgh asked to certify a number of Italian paintings. He was also a poet and a lute player: in 1627 the playwright Elias Herckmans dedicated his tragedy *Tyrus* to Codde, inspired by Codde's now-lost painting of the subject; in 1633 Codde's own pastoral love poem "Waerom vlucht ghy Millibe" appeared in *Hollands Nachtegaelken*; his 1636 household inventory lists "een oude luijt" (an old lute) and a viol. His final will, 8 October 1669, left most of his property -- including the Keizersgracht house -- to his housekeeper Barentje Willems, who sold it in 1679 for 2,500 guilders. He never remarried; Leiden and musicksmonument both read the bequest as evidence of a deeper relationship than master and servant. He died in 1678 and was buried at the Oude Kerk on 12 October 1678, aged 78 (leg 1).

## M1. The invention of the kortegaardje, the Amsterdam half: a stage, a spotlight, a legible commander

The guardroom scene -- kortegaardje, a corruption of the French corps de garde -- is a genre painting depicting an interior of a temporary nature hosting officers, soldiers and their camp followers, passing their leisure time surrounded by military items and booty. That is Jochai Rosen's definition ("The Dutch Guardroom Scene of the Golden Age: A Definition", Artibus et Historiae 27, no. 53, 2006, p. 171), and Rosen's fuller account adds the mechanism's purpose: the scene "is free of any violent action, and it usually appears respectable and in some cases even merry"; it "usually does not revolve around a clear subject matter, but is a collection of motifs, anecdotes, and notions"; it "gave the middle-class man, nesting safely at home, a restrained drama devoid of violence while touching his social sensitivity."

Codde was, per Rosen, "the most dominant and innovative of the group" that formulated it -- Codde, Duyster, Pieter Quast, Pieter Potter and Jacob Duck -- and "the most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s" (Rosen 2020, pp. 2, 5, 85, 208, via Steigrad). The Eighty Years' War resumed in 1621, putting soldiers in Amsterdam streets and romanticising mercenary life; the first guardrooms appear in the latter half of the 1620s, and the genre peaks in the 1630s-40s. The earliest dated guardroom in this catalogue is Dresden, 1628 (W08); Rosen's peak for Codde is 1628-1638, "at the height of his creativity devoting his talents to the modernization of Merry Company scenes as well as the formulation of... the guardroom scene" (leg 1; leg 2; leg 3 N1).

The mechanism, per Rosen via Steigrad, has three parts. First, the stage-like setting with simultaneous vignettes: a series of small scenes occur at once across the room -- gambling, drinking, sleeping, carousing, tussling -- with spot lighting to heighten the drama. Second, the commander is always legible: by superior dress, attitude and prominent placement; class distinctions between officers and soldiers are maintained, so the viewer can read rank at a glance. Third, the scene is devoid of violence: off-duty hours only, the fantasy a controlled theatre for a bourgeois buyer, "geared toward the taste of young bourgeois men wishing to impress their comrades with military spectacles". Small size, relatively inexpensive, very popular; many copies after his compositions testify to commercial success (leg 1; leg 3 N1).

The props are loot: sacks of booty spilling in the foreground, weapons, armour, cards, pipes. A woman smoking a pipe is a common allusion to sexual intercourse; a low neckline marks the prostitute; a soldier wedged between an officer's back and a saint's effigy gazes out at the viewer with a knowing grin. Where Duyster's guardroom quiets the men into silence (M5 of #565), Codde's guardroom stages them: the room is a theatre with several plays running at once, and the spotlights tell the viewer which one to watch (leg 1; leg 3 N1).

**Takeaway:** build the room as a stage with several small scenes running at once, each under its own spot of light, and make the commander legible at a glance by dress, attitude and placement. The watchable behaviour is the simultaneity -- the eye moving from vignette to vignette while nothing violent happens -- and the room must stay a theatre, not a report.

## M2. The silvery-gray tonality: a restricted palette, invisible brushwork

The single most repeated technical claim about Codde is the palette: small-scale paintings in silvery-gray tonalities (EN/NL Wikipedia), a restricted palette of silvery grays and browns (Leiden 2024), "shades of silvery grey and ochres" (WGA on *Young Scholar*), "subtle silvery-gray tonalities" (WGA biography). Sotheby's, on a musical company: "renowned for his meticulous technique and practically invisible brushwork... reveling in the depiction of texture, light, and color" (leg 1).

The tonality is not there at the start. Christie's notes that the clear, bright tonality of the early work precedes "an increasingly muted color scheme of greys and black" -- the famous silvery-gray is a development, not a birthright. The Rijksmuseum entry on the earliest genre paintings says they recall the broad, narrative scenes of Pieter Lastman; by the early 1630s the grey has taken over, and the Sphinx dossier calls the early portraits' palette "cool silver tones" (leg 2, Evolution).

The tonality is also the mechanism of the theatre (M1): the spot lighting works because the ground is grey, and the commander's superior dress reads because the room is restrained enough for one bright thing to carry. WGA's critical aside on the larger genre compositions is the counterweight: Codde "gives way to his preference for rather coarse and plump types, over-glossy textures, and exaggerated highlights. The rooms Codde represents are always of less interest to him than the people he placed in them." The grey is a ground for people, not a subject in itself (leg 1).

The exception points the way the palette would later go. *A Guardroom Scene in a Church* (c. 1631-1635, M5) is executed in a brown tonality with brushwork of an "immediacy and sketchiness not found in his other subject matter" (Steigrad, citing Rosen). And the late work warms: the c. 1645 *Portrait of a Young Man as Bacchus* "has begun to incorporate golds and browns into his palette, presumably under the influence of Rembrandt" (Sphinx). The grey is the middle of the career; the brown is the late turn (leg 1; leg 2).

**Takeaway:** hold the palette to silvery grays and browns and let one bright thing -- a sash, a collar, a lit face -- carry the picture. The brushwork must be practically invisible, the texture meticulous, and the light must do its work against a restrained ground; if the room gets bright or colourful, the tonality is gone.

## M3. Music as love: the musical merry company

Codde's first known genre work is a music lesson -- *The Dancing Lesson* (Louvre), 1627 -- and music runs through the whole career: *Company Making Music* (1625, Nationalmuseum), *Interior with Musicians Seated around a Table* (c. 1627), *An Elegant Company* (1632), *Merry Company* (1633, Vienna), *Company of Music-Makers* (c. 1633, Städel), *Musical Company* (1639, private collection). Elegant, spare interiors; young, well-dressed ladies and gentlemen; music-making as a metaphor for love and courtship (Sotheby's; Bijl-Van-Urk). An amorous overtone is never far away (leg 1; leg 2).

The mechanism is the metaphor made visible. In *Interior with Musicians* (W05), a male singer at left follows sheet music held by the woman beside him, mouth open mid-song; a lute player at the far end of the table -- the lute as the serenading lover's instrument; music as a metaphor for love. In *The Galant Company* (W13, 1633), two men enter with a shot hare and two partridges; hunting is the men's "love-hunt" of the women, underscored by a four-poster bed in the corner. In *An Elegant Company* (W12, 1632), at least thirteen figures hold wine glasses and converse in a banquet-hall-like interior, the household drinkware brought out, toasts to bride and groom with successively smaller glasses -- possibly a wedding game (leg 1; leg 2).

The music is also the social glue of the whole oeuvre: it is what the figures do together when they are not gambling or drinking, and it is the one activity that makes a company elegant rather than rowdy. The guardroom's card game and the merry company's song are the same device in two registers -- a shared action that lets the figures be together without speaking to one another. Codde was himself a lute player and a published love poet, which makes the metaphor biographical as well as pictorial: the man who wrote "Waerom vlucht ghy Millibe" painted rooms full of people making the music he made (leg 1).

**Takeaway:** make the music the courtship. The piece should show a small company making music together -- a singer following the sheet music a woman holds, a lute player at the far end of the table -- and the watchable behaviour is the playing itself, the song as the thing that holds the room together; the love is in the music, never stated outright.

## M4. The hidden monogram and the industry of copies: a composition re-issued, not a picture painted once

Codde rarely signed on the surface. He tucked his monogram into an object inside the fiction: on the pages of a music book, lower right, in *Interior with Musicians* (W05, c. 1627, "PCodde f. / 162*(?)"); on a mirror on the table in *The Galant Company* (W13, 1633, "PC"); in ligature on the cradle in *The Adoration of the Shepherds* (W20, 1645). The date goes the same way: 1633 inscribed on the pediment over the door in W13, an earlier reading of 1635 still circulating in old catalogues. The signature habit is already present in the earliest dated genre work and runs to the end of the career (leg 2).

The second half of the mechanism is repetition. Codde "often reproduced his most successful paintings" (Sotheby's). The Schwerin elegant-company composition exists in at least four versions -- the Schwerin panel the prime version, c. 1630, per Rosen; a second in Warsaw; a third in private ownership; a weaker version with a basket under the chimney circulated on the Amsterdam market; a copy attributed to Palamedesz. was with W. Wheeler, London, in 1950. The Karlsruhe guardroom exists in at least five versions or imitations, with differing details (the middle card-player with or without a beard). The *Dancing Lesson* and *Interior with Musicians* compositions exist in multiple versions and copies. The oeuvre is a small set of successful compositions re-issued with variations, not a stream of one-offs -- and Rosen's 2017 essay "Pieter Codde and the Industry of Copies in 17th-Century Dutch Painting" makes the copies themselves a subject (leg 1; leg 2 C7, C8).

The figure stock repeats too. The V&A's note on *Backgammon Players* records that the chin-on-hand figure in *The Dancing Lesson* reappears in several Codde works -- a reused figure, carried from composition to composition like a prop. The tric-trac group, the lute player, the singer with the sheet music: the same actors, restaged (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** hide the signature inside the picture, on an object in the scene -- a music book, a mirror, a cradle -- and then re-issue the same composition with variations, the same figures restaged in a new room. The piece should feel like one of several versions, not a one-off, and the maker's mark should be something the viewer has to find in the fiction.

## M5. The church guardroom, once: the backdrop he would not repeat

Deserted churches as guardroom backdrops were common for Duck, Quast and Potter. Codde has only one known example: *A Guardroom Scene in a Church*, c. 1631-1635, Rosen catalogue no. 112 (listed as "whereabouts unknown" at publication; now with Lawrence Steigrad Fine Arts, ex-private collection NRW; Van Ham, Cologne, 18 November 2011, lot 83). Left to right: shadowy figures gambling, drinking, sleeping; a spotlighted prostitute; a sack of booty spilling in the foreground; a couple tussling centre-midground; a card game with a camp follower and an officer looming at right; a woman smoking a pipe (the period allusion to sex); a soldier wedged between the officer's back and a saint's effigy, gazing out at the viewer with a knowing grin (leg 1; leg 2).

The mechanism is the single exception that proves the rule. The church guardroom is executed in a brown tonality with brushwork of an "immediacy and sketchiness not found in his other subject matter" (Steigrad, citing Rosen). The grey tonality of M2 is suspended here, and the handling loosens -- the one picture where Codde paints like the subject demands rather than like his palette allows. Against Duck's wide and tall hallways and church interiors, Quast's and Potter's many deserted churches, Codde's single church guardroom reads as a deliberate refusal: he tried the backdrop once, in a different tonality and a sketchier hand, and did not go back (leg 1; leg 3 N1).

It is also the picture where the guardroom's theatre is most explicit: the soldier wedged between the officer's back and the saint's effigy grins straight at the viewer, breaking the fourth wall of the stage. The sacred backdrop makes the profane vignettes louder -- gambling, drinking, sleeping, tussling, the spotlighted prostitute -- and the grin tells the viewer the picture knows it (leg 1; leg 2).

**Takeaway:** do the church guardroom once, in brown, sketchier than anything else in the room, and then stop. The piece should put the off-duty vignettes in a deserted church, the booty sack in the foreground, and one figure grinning out at the viewer -- and the singularity is the point: a backdrop tried once and not repeated.

## M6. Finishing Hals: the ventriloquist act at forty times the scale

The Meagre Company -- *Militia Company of District XI under the Command of Captain Reynier Reael*, known as the Meagre Company -- is the one rupture in the oeuvre. Sixteen officers, full-length, oil on canvas, 209 x 429 cm (support, Rijksmuseum's own page; WGA gives 207.3 x 427.5 cm), commissioned from Frans Hals in 1633 for the Amsterdam St George Civic Guard, the only militia group portrait Hals painted outside Haarlem. Hals was unhappy commuting to Amsterdam; in 1636 he was called to Amsterdam to finish it, refused, and offered to receive the sitters in Haarlem; the offer was refused and Codde was hired. Codde finished it in 1637. It is roughly forty times the area of a typical Codde panel, and Codde had never made a civic-guard piece before and "usually painted very delicately and in a small format"; for this commission he imitated Hals's loose style as closely as possible (Frans Hals Museum, via leg 2). The nickname comes from later Rijksmuseum viewers noting how slim these men look beside Rembrandt's *Night Watch* figures, painted over a decade later. Van Gogh admired it on his 1885 Rijksmuseum visit ("the heads -- some twenty -- sparkling with spirit and life") (leg 1; leg 2 W18).

The old view of the division of labour -- Hals completed only the left half, plus the third, fifth and seventh faces from the right; the rest is Codde; "Codde's work is recognizably smoother" -- is now superseded. The 2024 technical study (Springer, open-access chapter "Case Study 4: Frans Hals & Co", MA-XRF scans and infrared reflectography, 2017) calls the left/right split "too simplistic": Hals sketched the entire design alla prima on the canvas, leaving figures in various states of completion; Codde made revisions on the left as well, and Hals-painted passages survive on the right (the fifth and seventh figures from the right are "much more animated in their expressions; it looks as if they were barely touched by Codde"). Faces were more worked up than costumes and poses when Codde took over; the right-hand figures' poses were only summarily indicated, which explains their awkwardness. Hofstede de Groot already warned in 1910 that "attempts to assign definite portions of the picture to Hals or to Codde have not resulted in any agreement among authorities" (leg 1 C4; leg 3 D6).

Codde's tells, per the 2024 study, are catalogued in detail: figures on the right lean backwards with lopsided centres of gravity; feet fairly wide apart; "typical Codde shoes" (less pronounced shape, different colour application); twisted wrists and contrived hand gestures; hands crowded together causing *haspeling* (confusion of limbs) and *sparteling* (sprawl); tonal contrasts that give individual objects (shoes, armour, collars) convincing three-dimensionality but scatter attention across the composition; sashes in the middle and right painted with soft transitions and dull, repetitive touches, the orange-red having turned a dull pinkish red, disrupting the overall *houding* (balance of colours and tones); faces on the right more wooden in expression. By contrast Hals's left side shows *koppeling en sprong* (joining and spatial leaps), *eigentlick* (natural, befitting) postures -- the captain leaning casually on the sides of his shoes, hand on heart; the lieutenant wrapping one lower leg around the other -- and the *oogenblikkige beweeging* (instantaneous movement) "snapshot effect". Codde used no copper at all in the skin tones of his c. 1635 guard scene, while Hals regularly used azurite in skin tones in the late 1620s-30s -- a pigment fingerprint (leg 1).

The mechanism is ventriloquism at scale: a small-format, smooth, linear painter hired to finish another man's large-format, loose picture, succeeding well enough that WGA says he "succeeded so well in capturing Hals's spirit and the touch of his brush that experts still disagree where the work of the one ends and the other begins" -- and failing in exactly the places the technical study can now name: the shoes, the wrists, the feet, the sashes. The tells are the mechanism's visible residue (leg 1; leg 3 N3).

**Takeaway:** take a small smooth hand and make it finish a large loose picture, and let the tells show -- the shoes a little wrong, the wrists twisted, the feet too wide apart, the sashes gone dull pink. The piece should be a collaboration across hands, the seam visible to anyone who looks at the feet, and the scale itself the point: forty times the normal area, one painter ventriloquising another.

## M7. The long life and the thin tail: forty-three years past the co-inventor

Codde outlived Duyster by 43 years and carried the genre through its whole first arc -- 1628-1638 at his height -- and then kept going for another forty years. The career is the opposite shape of Duyster's ten-year fuse: a long life, a short peak, and a thin tail. The Rijksmuseum biography states it plainly: "There are few dated works after 1645." Only four dated works follow 1645 in this catalogue: *Diana and her Nymphs* 1651 (date also read 1653 and 1655), *Portrait of a Family* 1661, *Landscape with a Lake and Castle* 1666, and *The Judgement of Midas* 1670 (leg 2).

After 1639 the genre tap stops: the *Musical Company* (1639, canvas, private collection) is the last big genre date. Christie's lot essay: "after [the late 1620s and 1630s] the artist increasingly turned his attention to portraits and, to a lesser extent, historical subjects." Portraits: small-scale, in the manner of Thomas de Keyser; family groups as musical-company hybrids (1640 National Gallery family; 1642 Rijksmuseum *Family Group* with books and a globe; 1661 Stockholm *Portrait of a Family*). History: *Judgement of Paris* 1635 (already mid-career), *Adoration of the Shepherds* 1645 (Rembrandt-facing, per the Rijksmuseum), *Sacrifice of Polyxena* c. 1640-50, *Diana and her Nymphs* 1651, *Continence of Scipio*, *Granida and Daifilo*, *Judgement of Midas* 1670. Playter's claim that history concentration began around 1640 is partly contradicted by the 1627 Herckmans eulogy on the lost *Fall of Tyre* -- history was there from the 1620s, it just becomes a larger share late (leg 2, Evolution).

The subjects darken at the end. The 1645 soldier-raid pictures -- *Soldiers Breaking into a Peasant's Cottage* (Statens Museum for Kunst, Copenhagen) and *Men raiding a peasant couple* (Landesmuseum Hannover) -- are violent where the 1630s guardrooms were leisurely: the Eighty Years' War's home-front cost, painted in the war's final years. The guardroom's founding rule -- devoid of violence, off-duty hours only -- is broken by the same hand, twenty years on, when the war comes home (leg 2).

He bought a house on the Keizersgracht in 1657 and was still consulted as an expert valuer in 1672. The long late career is mostly undocumented by dated pictures -- a painter who lived to 78, peaked at 29-39, and then painted quietly for four decades while the genre he invented went on without him into its second phase, its church interiors, its gentrified Palamedesz. halls, and ter Borch's afterlife (leg 1; leg 2; leg 3 N6, N8).

**Takeaway:** a career with a short peak and a forty-year tail. The piece should show the guardroom at its height and then keep going past it -- the same painter, the same small panels, the dates thinning out, the subjects darkening into the 1645 raid pictures -- and the long quiet after the invention is the point, not a decline to be hidden.

## M8. The notarial life: a painter documented by lawsuits, not by guild records

Codde's life is documented by notaries, not by the guild -- because the Amsterdam Guild of Saint Luke records are lost, no guild office is documented for him, and whether he was formally a guild master at all is undocumented (presumed, since he sold paintings and took civic commissions). What survives is paper of another kind: the 1 July 1625 testimony about the brawl, and the 30 October 1651 testimony about his wife (leg 1 C11).

The brawl (M8's first document) is pinned to a day and a pair of vessels: Whit Monday, 19 May 1625, at Meerhuysen, tenant Barent van Someren, witnesses Louwijs du Pree and Michael van de Sande, trigger an argument about Pieter Raes -- Codde striking Duyster with an earthenware jug, then a pewter tankard, blood spattering over Duyster's clothes, Codde thrown out by bystanders. The 1651 testimony (M8's second document) is the separated husband asking neighbours to report on his wife: Sijmon Hop, with whom she lived about three years as husband and wife and by whom she was twice pregnant; Willem Blau, about six months; and Pieter Potter, found in bed with her by Cristina Anseel, who then ate kandeel with them. The painter's domestic life enters the record because he asked for it to be written down (leg 1; leg 3 N2, N10).

The mechanism is the archive itself: a painter whose biography is two notarial accounts, a separation inventory, and a will -- the 1636 inventory listing the paintings, porcelain, silver and silk that prove he dealt in art; the 1669 will leaving the Keizersgracht house to his housekeeper Barentje Willems, who sold it in 1679 for 2,500 guilders, half what he paid for it in 1657. No guild record, no pupil register, no studio inventory of his own making. The life is what other people wrote down when something went wrong (leg 1).

It is also what separates Codde's afterlife from Duyster's. Duyster's oeuvre had to be recovered from monograms on drums and carpets, because his pictures kept losing his name. Codde's name was never lost -- he lived too long, sold too much, and finished too famous a picture -- but his life was never written by the institutions that write painters' lives. The record is the brawl, the separation, and the will: a painter known by his lawsuits (leg 1; leg 3 N12).

**Takeaway:** document the painter by his paperwork, not his pictures. The piece should be built from the notarial record -- the jug and the tankard, the witnesses' names, the inventory's porcelain and silk, the will's housekeeper -- and the biography should feel like paper, not paint: a life assembled from the documents filed when something went wrong.

## Evolution

The career is long -- the earliest dated work is 1625 (W01, W03) and the last dated is 1670 (Judgement of Midas) -- and almost everything after 1645 is dated by style and costume rather than signature, because so few late works are dated. What the sources do support:

Early (1625-1628): the small panel, the mirror, the music table. The earliest dated work is a portrait-genre hybrid (woman and mirror, 1625), not a company scene; the first genre work is the Dancing Lesson (1627). Subjects: single figures and small groups in domestic interiors; music from the very start (W03, 1625). Format: small oak panels, roughly 30-40 x 35-55 cm. Style: the earliest genre paintings "recall the broad, narrative scenes of Pieter Lastman" (Rijksmuseum); Christie's says the clear, bright tonality of the early work precedes "an increasingly muted color scheme of greys and black" -- the famous silvery-grey tonality is NOT there at the start; it develops. The signature habit is already present: the monogram hidden on the music book (W05).

Mid / height (1628-1638): the guardroom decade. 1628 is the hinge year: Tric-Trac Players, A Conversation, and the Dresden guardroom are all dated 1628. Rosen (via Steigrad): "From 1628-1638 Codde was at the height of his creativity devoting his talents to the modernization of Merry Company scenes as well as the formulation of... the guardroom scene." The guardroom is invented here, with Duyster as co-inventor: off-duty soldiers gambling, drinking, sleeping, smoking, carousing with camp followers in requisitioned quarters; class legible at a glance; stage-like simultaneous episodes with spot lighting; mostly WITHOUT violence -- the violence comes later (1645 raid pictures). Codde is "the most dominant and innovative" of the group and "the most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s". Merry companies modernised in parallel: Renaissance-style wainscoting and door-frames become the standard 1630s interior; figures make music, dance, converse; masked dancers (1636) and tric-trac players recur. Palette: the silvery-grey tonality now dominant; "practically invisible brushwork" and meticulous texture (Sotheby's); compressed figure groupings. Scale: still small -- the modal work is 30-55 x 40-70 cm; the widest genre pieces are An Elegant Company (58.8 x 92.7) and the Vienna Merry Company (49 x 77). The one rupture is the Meagre Company (1637, 209 x 429 cm canvas), where he ventriloquises Hals's loose handling at 40x his normal area. Repetition as a system: the Schwerin elegant-company composition in at least four versions; the Karlsruhe guardroom in at least five; the Dancing Lesson and Interior with Musicians compositions in multiple versions and copies.

Late (1639-1678): portraits, history, and a long quiet tail. After 1639 the genre tap stops: the Musical Company (1639, canvas, private) is the last big genre date. Portraits: small-scale, in the manner of Thomas de Keyser; family groups as musical-company hybrids (1640 NG family; 1642 Rijksmuseum Family Group with books and globe; 1661 Stockholm Portrait of a Family). History: Judgement of Paris 1635, Adoration of the Shepherds 1645, Sacrifice of Polyxena c. 1640-50, Diana and her Nymphs 1651, Continence of Scipio, Granida and Daifilo, Judgement of Midas 1670. Palette shift: the c. 1645 Bacchus portrait keeps the grey tones but "has begun to incorporate golds and browns into his palette, presumably under the influence of Rembrandt" (Sphinx); the church guardroom's brown tonality and sketchy brushwork points the same way: looser, warmer. Subjects darken: the 1645 soldier-raid pictures are violent where the 1630s guardrooms were leisurely. Output thins drastically: few dated works after 1645; a landscape in 1666 and a history picture in 1670 are the last dated traces. He bought a house on the Keizersgracht in 1657 and was still consulted as an expert valuer in 1672; he died 1678.

What did NOT change: Amsterdam throughout (born, worked, died there); small scale (panel, ~30-60 cm) except the Meagre Company; interiors as the default stage; music as the default social glue; the hidden monogram (on a mirror, a music book, a cradle); the reuse of successful compositions and figure stock (chin-on-hand man, the tric-trac group, the lute player); the silvery-grey tonal core from the early 1630s onward.

## Aesthetic logic

Codde's aesthetic is a small grey room, a spotlight, and a song. The guardroom scene as he and Duyster invented it is a room of men off duty, and the genre's pleasures are abundance, noise, and the textures of loot and drink. Codde keeps the room and stages the men: several small scenes at once, each under its own spot of light, the commander always legible by dress and placement, the violence kept out. His palette is the palette of the grey the light falls on -- silvery grays and browns, practically invisible brushwork, meticulous texture -- and his signature is hidden inside the fiction, on a music book or a mirror or a cradle, because the picture is a stage and the author's name is a prop.

The same logic runs through the career in one direction: bright Lastman-like beginnings, the grey taking over in the early 1630s, the guardroom decade at its height, then the long quiet tail -- portraits and history, golds and browns warming the grey, the 1645 raid pictures breaking the no-violence rule twenty years late. The repertoire never quite changes -- music, gambling, drinking, the tric-trac board, the officer at the centre -- and the refinement is all in the light, the restraint, and the repetition: the same compositions re-issued, the same figures restaged, the same monogram tucked into a new object.

The failures of the record are part of the logic too. His training is unknown because the guild records are lost; his life is documented by two notarial accounts -- a brawl and a broken marriage -- because those are the papers that survive when a painter leaves no guild record; his most famous picture is half another man's, and the experts still disagree where the one ends and the other begins. A painter who lived to 78, peaked at 29-39, and then painted quietly for forty years while the genre he invented went on without him is a painter whose career is a long tail after a short invention -- and the tail is mostly undocumented, because dated pictures stop and the house on the Keizersgracht is the last thing the record can prove.

## Positions

- The dates are baptised 11 December 1599 and buried 12 October 1678, Amsterdam. The "11 December 1599 - 12 October 1678" form is used as birth-death in most secondary pages; the study carries the baptism/burial caveat.
- "Co-inventor of the guardroom scene with Willem Duyster" is the NGI's and EN Wikipedia's wording, and the study uses it. Rosen's "most dominant and innovative" and "most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s" are Rosen's, via Steigrad's citations of the 2020 catalogue raisonné -- the book itself was not read by any leg.
- The pupillage of Duyster under Codde is stated flatly in EN Wikipedia and the 1913 Valentiner/Johnson catalogue, and hedged by the National Gallery ("may have trained with"). The study carries it as stated-by, with the modern doubt visible (NL Wikipedia: now considered unlikely because Duyster is regarded as the better painter). Both men were born in 1599, which already makes a master-pupil relation odd.
- The brawl is dated Whit Monday, 19 May 1625, at Meerhuysen, tenant Barent van Someren, one-sided: Codde struck Duyster with an earthenware jug, then a pewter tankard. "Tin pitchers" plural and "summer of 1625" are not used. EN Wikipedia's "Cornelis Duyster" is a typo -- there is no painter Cornelis Duyster.
- "Simon Kick was their brother-in-law" (EN Wikipedia Codde article) is loose: Simon was Duyster's brother-in-law only (double wedding 5 September 1631). No marriage link between Codde and the Kicks exists in any source read. The study does not call Simon Codde's brother-in-law.

- The Meagre Company division of labour is disputed, not settled. The old left/right split is superseded by the 2024 Springer technical study (Hals sketched the whole design; Codde revised on the left too; Hals passages survive on the right). The finish in 1637 is not disputed.
- "Oval / copper supports" for Codde is NOT confirmed: no source read describes his portraits as oval or on copper; the works inspected are oil on panel or canvas, small rectangular. ## Quotes

These would be depicted passing their leisure time surrounded by military items and booty." / The scene "is free of any violent action, and it usually appears respectable and in some cases even merry"; it "usually does not revolve around a clear subject matter, but is a collection of motifs, anecdotes, and notions"; it "gave the middle-class man, nesting safely at home, a restrained drama devoid of violence while touching his social sensitivity."

## Network

The family web first. Codde married Marritje Arents in 1623; their daughter Clara was baptised in April 1624 and died in 1635. No marriage link between Codde and the Kick family exists: Simon Kick was Duyster's brother-in-law, not Codde's -- Duyster married Simon's sister Margrieta Kick and Simon married Duyster's sister, in a double wedding on 5 September 1631. The Codde-Kick link runs through Duyster only. Duyster's death of plague in 1635 is the event Rosen's 2021 synopsis says drove Simon, 32, to take up painting. Simon's son Cornelis (#563) was trained under his father from c. 1650 in genre, portraits, schuttersstukken and guardrooms, then turned to flowers.

The inventors: Pieter Codde (baptised 11 December 1599, buried 12 October 1678) and Willem Cornelisz. Duyster (1599-1635) "developed and popularised the guardroom scene" (NGI). Codde was, per Rosen via Steigrad, "the most dominant and innovative" of the group that formulated it, and "the most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s". Codde's pupil was Duyster, "who was about the same age" -- pupil and exact contemporary at once, stated flatly in EN Wikipedia and the 1913 catalogue, hedged by the National Gallery, doubted by modern scholarship -- and the two came to blows at Van Someren's Meerhuysen party on Whit Monday, 19 May 1625: an earthenware jug, then a pewter tankard, one-sided. Codde outlived Duyster by 43 years and carried the genre through its whole first arc. In 1637 Codde finished Hals's unfinished schutterstuk, The Meagre Company -- Hals painted the design and the left, Codde finished it, smoother than Hals, and the 2024 technical study says the split was never as clean as the old left/right story.

The circle, three cities in one sentence (Palamedesz. article): Codde, Duyster and Kick in Amsterdam; Jacob Duck in Utrecht; Palamedesz. and Jacob van Velsen in Delft. Duck (c. 1600-1667), the most successful exponent, painted guardrooms from 1628 to 1655, often wide and tall hallways or church interiors; he modelled his style on both Codde and Duyster (Salomon via Sotheby's), and was influenced by Codde, not his pupil -- hedged three ways (Artnet "pupil", Sotheby's "probably learnt", Wikipedia "influenced"). Palamedesz. (bapt. 9 November 1602 - 27 November 1673), the Delft branch, painted guardrooms into the 1660s -- a large hall of a ruin or castle, open at the rear, a large fireplace, a chubby officer in a tall hat at the centre -- and his guardrooms are unique in including women as mothers, not courtesans: "masculinity pacified". The Rijksmuseum says "Codde painted merry companies related in style to those by Anthonie Palamedesz." -- a direct Codde-Palamedesz. style link. Van Velsen (1597-1656) is the one documented influence running into Duyster: the RKD says he influenced Duyster, and he is known for genre works and military pieces "in the manner of Pieter Codde and Dirck Hals". Quast (bapt. 1605-1647) is the caricature and satire branch, the Dutch Callot, who competed with Codde and Duyster in the same market and shared the deserted-church guardroom with Duck and Potter. Pieter Symonsz Potter (1597-1652), father of Paulus Potter, painted a Guardroom Interior (Schwerin) and -- in primary-source form, the 1651 notarial account -- was found in bed with Codde's separated wife by a neighbour; he also, snippet-level (WGA biography), "served as a witness for the baptism of de Heem's son Cornelis in Leiden in 1631", touching the de Heem line a generation before Cornelis Kick does. Gerard ter Borch (1617-1681) is the afterlife: his early-1640s guardrooms, painted after visits to Amsterdam in the early 1630s where he may have seen Duyster's and Codde's, carried the genre's lessons -- small groups, full-length figures, upright format, subtle psychological interplay -- into the rarefied domestic interiors of the 1650s and 1660s.

The named forerunners of Codde, per the Leiden Collection: Willem Buytewech (1591/92-1624) and Dirck Hals (1591-1656). The named influences on Duyster, per the National Gallery: Dirck Hals, Anthonie Palamedesz., Jacob van Velsen and Thomas de Keyser. The host of the 1625 brawl, Barent van Someren (1572-1632), is also Codde's likeliest teacher (with Cornelis van der Voort). Codde's other recorded pupil, besides Duyster, is a painter named Albert Jansz (Rijksmuseum, Gallant Company catalogue entry) -- identity otherwise unknown. Bartholomeus van der Helst (1613-1670) was "probably a pupil of either Codde or Hals" and went on to paint several more militia group portrait commissions where "Codde was never given another militia group portrait commission" -- the man who won the schutterstuk market Codde lost. Gerrit Uylenburgh asked Codde to certify Italian paintings in 1672; Hendrick Uylenburgh's Sint Anthoniesbreestraat studio is the probable shared workplace of Hals and Codde on the Meagre Company, where Rembrandt was also active in the early 1630s.
