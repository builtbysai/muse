## M1. Additive rhythm and cross-rhythm

What it is: the regular pulse replaced by a series of irregular rhythmic metres, "a kind of rhythm the effect of which is determined by an accumulation of irregular, unpredictable accents", and it is first used in the String Quartet No. 1 of 1934-35, and it is derived from Bartok and Stravinsky and from the English madrigalists, and it is the signature of the "individual voice", and it runs through the Concerto for Double String Orchestra, where two orchestras play in imitation and syncopation, and through the Piano Sonata No. 1, whose finale has innovative jazz syncopations, and the watchable behaviour is a beat you cannot tap along to, that keeps regrouping itself, and the music trips and recovers, trips and recovers, and the rhythm is the argument, not the decoration.

**Takeaway:** the beat that will not sit still is the whole early style, and a piece built on additive rhythm must keep regrouping its accents, or it is only a syncopation.

## M2. The spiritual as chorale

What it is: borrowed songs of oppression given the structural job Bach gave to chorales, punctuation, repose, moral focus, and it is the innovation of A Child of Our Time, where five African-American spirituals, "Steal Away", "Nobody Knows the Trouble I See, Lord", "Go Down, Moses", "O, By and By" and "Deep River", stand in place of Bach's chorales, one for every key situation, and "Deep River" closes the work, hope after a long dark winter, and Tippett heard "Steal Away" on the radio and recognised songs of oppression as universal, and the score instructs that the spirituals "should not be thought of as congregational hymns, but as integral parts of the Oratorio; nor should they be sentimentalised but sung with a strong underlying beat and slightly 'swung'", and there are no proper names anywhere, Paris is "a great city" and Grynszpan is "the boy", and the watchable behaviour is the same tune returning at the end of each act, each time carrying more weight, and a community song that is also the argument.

**Takeaway:** the borrowed song carries the moral weight of the whole structure, and it must return heavier each time, or it is only a quotation.

## M3. The madrigal and early-music synthesis

**Takeaway:** the old music is the material, not the reference, and a piece that borrows a form must make it do new work, or it is only a period costume.

## M4. Shadow and light

What it is: the Jungian dichotomy, learned in analysis with John Layard in 1938-39, made into a compositional principle, and every work stages the divided psyche accepting its dark half, and A Child of Our Time closes with "I would know my shadow and my light, so shall I at last be whole", and The Midsummer Marriage stages anima and animus, the warm soft young man rebuffed by the cold hard young woman, and King Priam answers Priam's "Yes...yes" with the unseen chorus "No...no", and The Knot Garden is the maze as the unconscious, and the watchable behaviour is two opposed forces on stage at once, neither winning, and the piece ending in their reconciliation rather than one's victory.

**Takeaway:** the piece must end with both halves present and reconciled, and a victory for one side breaks the mechanism.

## M5. Opera as ritual

What it is: the opera as a ceremony, not a story, and dances, processions and symbolic actions carry the meaning while the plot stays deliberately thin, and The Midsummer Marriage has four Ritual Dances, hound and hare, otter and fish, hawk and broken-winged bird, then the fire ritual that turns carnal love into divine love, and King Priam has the commentator trio and the gods doubled by the human characters, Athene with Hecuba, Hera with Andromache, Aphrodite with Helen, and The Knot Garden has Mangus staging The Tempest as therapy, Mel and Dov as Caliban and Ariel, Faber as Ferdinand, Flora as Miranda, Mangus as Prospero, and the watchable behaviour is a dance that happens instead of a conversation, and a ritual repeated until it changes the people performing it.

**Takeaway:** the dance must do the symbolic work the words cannot, and a ritual that changes nothing is only a ballet.

## M6. The great divide

What it is: the deliberate stylistic rupture at King Priam, and the warm, lyrical, key-centred first period is broken open into dissonance, atonality, bitonality and abrupt juxtaposition, and the works are King Priam of 1962, the Piano Sonata No. 2 of 1962, the Concerto for Orchestra of 1963, The Vision of Saint Augustine of 1965 and The Knot Garden of 1970, and Iain Stannard calls it a "great divide" between the works before and after King Priam, and Milner calls Priam "a complete break", and the music marks it plainly, "very little of the music is in a definite key", and the counterpoint largely drops away, and the watchable behaviour is the same composer suddenly speaking in a harder voice, and the orchestra getting sparer, and the harmonies getting stranger, and the juxtapositions getting sharper.

**Takeaway:** the rupture must be audible as a break, not a drift, and a piece that only gets a little harsher has not crossed the divide.

## M7. The borrowed theme, transformed

**Takeaway:** the borrowed theme must return changed by its new home, and a quotation that only decorates is a programme note, not a mechanism.

## M8. The blues as ground bass

**Takeaway:** the blues must be the structure, not the flavour, and a blues that only colours the surface is a costume change.

## M9. The opera of the present day

## M10. Writing for a specific performer

What it is: music composed for one named person's sound, so the piece is inseparable from the player, and the works are the Piano Sonata No. 1 and the Fantasia on a Theme of Handel for Phyllis Sellick, Boyhood's End and The Heart's Assurance for Peter Pears with Britten at the piano, Songs for Achilles for Pears and Julian Bream at Aldeburgh in 1961, The Vision of Saint Augustine for Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau, The Blue Guitar for Julian Bream, the Concerto for Orchestra for Britten's 50th birthday, the Symphony No. 3 soprano part for Heather Harper, and Byzantium for Faye Robinson, and the watchable behaviour is a part that fits one voice like a glove, and the singer is the subject, not the vehicle.

**Takeaway:** the part must fit one named throat and one named pair of hands, and a part written for nobody is an exercise.

## M11. Time-layering and the birth-to-death cycle

What it is: a work that stages time itself, a life, a day, the history of the universe, as a single span, and the Symphony No. 4 is one movement, a birth-to-death cycle beginning and ending with the sounds of breathing, first attempted with a wind machine, and the Mask of Time is cosmic history from the Big Bang to the present, after Jacob Bronowski's The Ascent of Man, and The Rose Lake is dawn to dusk, the lake waking, singing, and singing itself to sleep, and the watchable behaviour is a piece that starts with a breath and ends with a breath, and a day passing in half an hour.

**Takeaway:** the span must be audible as one arc, and a piece that only lists its sections is a catalogue, not a cycle.

## M12. The Mediterranean and gamelan turn

What it is: non-Western sound-worlds absorbed late and used as colour and structure, and gamelan is heard on recordings in youth and briefly in the Piano Sonata No. 1 of 1938, and the Java and Bali journeys of 1976-77 bring it back, and the Triple Concerto finale is inspired by gamelan, and The Rose Lake has glittering percussion, 36 rototoms, and the Senegal visit behind The Rose Lake turns the whitish-green water of Lake Retba, coloured by dunaliella salina algae, "a marvellous translucent pink" at midday, into a singing orchestral form, and the watchable behaviour is tuned percussion taking over from the strings as the centre of the sound, and the orchestra shimmering instead of singing.

**Takeaway:** the foreign sound must become the centre, not the garnish, and a gamelan that only colours the edges is a travel postcard.

## M13. Pacifism in the work

What it is: the composer's politics made into musical form, and refusal, imprisonment and reconciliation staged rather than preached, and A Child of Our Time is the oratorio of the oppressed, written as war broke out, composing begun Tuesday 3 September 1939, the day Britain declared war, and the prison sentence is June 1943, three months, two served in Wormwood Scrubs, and the Symphony No. 1 is conceived in prison, and the Peace Pledge Union is joined in November 1940, president from 1959, and the 1977 attack on Carter's neutron bomb plans, and the cost is real, the BBC banned his music from the air until his release, and the watchable behaviour is a work that refuses to take sides in the usual way, and the persecutors and the persecuted both get a chorus, and the ending is reconciliation, not triumph.

**Takeaway:** the refusal must be staged, not announced, and a piece that preaches its politics is a pamphlet, not an oratorio.

## M14. The self-written libretto

What it is: Tippett wrote his own words for every opera and oratorio, after Eliot advised him to write his own libretto for A Child of Our Time, "his own superior poetry would either distract attention from the music, or otherwise be swallowed up by it", and the practice made the operas wholly his, and wholly exposed in the late operas, and the libretto of The Midsummer Marriage was called "one of the worst in the 350-year history of opera", and the vernacular of The Ice Break and New Year was widely mocked, and Berkeley's verdict is that Eliot "perhaps did Tippett a great disservice" in encouraging him, and the watchable behaviour is words and music by the same hand, and the libretto's weaknesses are the music's weaknesses, and its strangeness is the music's strangeness.

**Takeaway:** the words and the music must stand or fall together, and a libretto that only serves the music is a translation, not a voice.

## Aesthetic logic
