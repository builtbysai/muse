# Robert Mangold: Study Notes

**Site:** Robert Mangold -- American geometric painter; born Tuesday 12 October 1937, North Tonawanda, New York; living, in Washingtonville, New York, and still making new work at 89 -- his Pace Gallery show *Pentagons and Folded Space* (Friday 9 May - Friday 15 August 2025) presented paintings and works on paper made between 2022 and 2024, when he was 84 to 87. Cleveland Institute of Art 1956-1959; Yale School of Art and Architecture, BFA 1961, MFA June 1963; first solo show, Thibaut Gallery, New York, 1964; *Walls and Areas*, Fischbach Gallery, 1965; *Systematic Painting* at the Guggenheim and *Primary Structures* at the Jewish Museum, both 1966; represented by Pace Gallery since 1991 (first Pace exhibition 1992) and by Lisson Gallery, London, since 1973. He prefers "Geometric Painter" to "Minimalist". [C35623]

**Relevance:** Mangold investigates what "imperfect" means as a rule -- a circle perturbed by how much, in which direction, and when does the failure to close become the picture? His evidence is a sixty-year practice, not a single work: the imperfect circles and distorted circles of 1972-1975, the shaped canvases whose edges complete or contradict the drawn figure, the X and + and Frame paintings of the 1980s, the Columns of 2002-2007, the Rings of 2008-2011, the Pentagons of 2022-2024. In every series one geometric ideal is tested against hand-drawn deviation and against the rectangle -- or the shaped edge -- of the support, and the deviation is the whole content: an inscribed square whose two corners overlap the painting's edge, a circle that turns into a polygon where it meets the canvas, a ring whose centre is empty wall. Those are computable questions with visible answers: an offset of 1-4% of radius, a tilt of a few degrees, a figure that overshoots its container by a sliver, a drawn line that hands over to a physical edge. That is the question a generative practice needs from the other side of Wigdor's: not what the hand knows before the picture answers, but what the hand draws when it refuses to draw the ideal -- and how little failure it takes for the failure to be the picture. [C35624]

**Doorway:** The 440th doorway. Hop from the #819 Daniel Wigdor study, which ends on the open question of what the hand already knows, and whether the picture can answer before the finger lands -- his answer is measured: the tap predicted 128 ms before touchdown, the drag answered in 1 ms, the contact read from its shape. Mangold is the hand that draws the circle that fails to close -- the deviation from the ideal is the picture. The doorway is from the hand that knows to the hand that misses, and the turn is this: Wigdor asked how fast the system can hear the hand; Mangold asks how little the hand has to miss by for the miss to be the work. [C35625]

## The question: what does "imperfect" mean as a rule?

Mangold's imperfection is not noise, not wobble, not hand-tremor texture. Three distinct, testable kinds of imperfection recur across sixty years, and they must not be conflated. First, displacement: the ideal figure is drawn correctly but in the wrong place -- off-centre, tilted, oversized for its container. The circle in *Distorted Circle* is "drawn ever so slightly off-centre, barely touching or just slipping off the edges of the ever so slightly tilted, rhombus canvas" (Sotheby's essay): the circle itself is fine; its registration against the support fails. Second, distortion: the figure is deformed to fit a container that is not its own shape -- in *Circle In and Out of a Polygon 2* (1973), "what appears to be a perfect circle or square... is partially contorted in order to fit within the confines of the shaped canvases." Third, substitution: a drawn line is replaced by a physical edge -- in the same 1973 work, "half of the circle is outlined on the acrylic surface, while the other half continues as the curved edge on the painting's right," and the interior graphite line "becomes interchangeable with the top, left, and bottom borders of the support." The figure is complete only if the viewer counts the canvas edge as part of the drawing. [C35627]

When does the failure to close become the picture? When the gap is small enough to be read as a near-miss and large enough to be unmistakable: the figure must be recognisable as a circle, square, or ellipse -- at least 85-92% of its perimeter present, in drawn or edge form -- while the missing or displaced remainder is exactly one thing: one overshoot, one substitution, one tilt, never a scatter of errors. One clean failure, legible at a glance, is the picture; two or more unrelated failures read as sloppiness, not as Mangold. That single-failure constraint is itself a rule, and every mechanism in this study carries it. The hand enters the same way: Mangold's line is hand-drawn at arm scale -- "The drawn line [in my paintings], while not an action line [as] in a Pollock, is nevertheless connected to arm movement and human size" -- and the hand is one smooth low-frequency drift over the arc, a slow deviation, never random per-point jitter. Formalised for a builder: deviation is a single sinusoid of one to two cycles per full turn, amplitude 0.5-2% of radius. Per-point jitter is a rejected stand-in, not his hand. [C35628]

**Takeaway:** "Imperfect" is a parameter, not a mood. Name the failure mode (offset, tilt, overscale, deformation-by-container, substitution-by-edge), name its magnitude (1-5% of the figure's size), and enforce exactly one failure per picture. A prompt that cannot name those three things is not a Mangold prompt. [C35629]

## A life in geometry: North Tonawanda to the Bowery

Robert Peter Mangold was born Tuesday 12 October 1937 in North Tonawanda, New York, "a small town outside of Buffalo," between Buffalo and Niagara Falls, to Aloysius Mangold, a cabinet finisher who sprayed finishes on organ cabinets at the Wurlitzer factory, and Blanche Mangold, a department-store buyer who played piano and organ. The family lived in the country along the Erie Canal; he had one older brother, seven years older, and "kind of grew up as an only child." He did very well at school, took "just about all they had to offer" in high school -- ceramics, mechanical drawing -- and planned to be a commercial artist, Norman Rockwell the model in his mind; he had no sense contemporary painters existed as a career. With no money from his parents for education, he worked for a year after high school (c. 1955) to save up, wrote to many schools, and Cleveland was the only one that replied with a friendly letter inviting him to visit. [C35630]

At the Cleveland Institute of Art (1956-1959) he enrolled in illustration, found it "seemed like factory work... which was what I was running away from," and transferred to fine art. The turning point was a student trip to the Carnegie International in Pittsburgh, where he saw New York School painting in the flesh for the first time -- de Kooning, Kline, Gottlieb, Pollock, plus Burri, Tapies, Picasso -- and "it wasn't an abstraction of nature, and it wasn't like design. It was like something that confronted you and had this physical and emotional presence, suddenly." The year of that trip is unstable across sources -- Crown Point says 1957, he said "'56 or '57" (MoMA 1999) and "'58... beginning/middle of my third year" (AAA 1994); 1957 is the best-supported year and the exact year is treated here as unresolved. In summer 1959 he went to the Yale Summer School of Music and Art at Norfolk, Connecticut, on a fellowship, met Bernard Chaet, and applied to Yale; he entered the Yale School of Art and Architecture in fall 1960, took the BFA in 1961 and the MFA in June 1963 (two years in residence, a third year out of residence in New York, returning to New Haven to show work before a jury). TheArtStory's claim of an MFA in 1962 is wrong, and Sotheby's claim that he "completed his BFA at the Cleveland Institute of Art in 1961" is wrong -- the BFA was Yale's. His Yale cohort was extraordinary: Nancy Graves, Brice Marden, Richard Serra, Chuck Close, and Sylvia Plimack, whom he married in New Haven in 1961. Sylvia Plimack Mangold (born Sunday 18 September 1938, New York City; Cooper Union 1956-1959; Yale BFA 1961) became a painter of intensely observed trees and landscape, working from nature -- the opposite pole from his abstraction. They have two sons: James Mangold, the film director, and Andrew Mangold, a musician. [C35631]

He moved to New York in 1962, while still enrolled at Yale, and worked at the Museum of Modern Art: a guard for three months in the summer of 1962 (June, July, August), then a library page from fall 1962 to late 1963 (MoMA's records put the library employment through 1964). The guard job mattered more than the library job: through it he met Sol LeWitt, who worked the night desk -- "Sol's been a friend my whole life" -- and Lucy Lippard, then doing research in the library, who dubbed LeWitt, Robert Ryman, and Mangold "the Bowery Boys" for their cheap Bowery flats. He and Sylvia took a loft in Ryman's Bowery building, three floors above Ryman. He left MoMA for teaching (School of Visual Arts, 1963-1970, and Hunter College), and the teaching paid better for fewer hours -- "a step up." What struck him when he first moved to New York was that "so much of what we see, we see in fragments. We see part of a truck going by, or part of a building. We never see anything in completeness" -- skyscrapers, bridges, plazas, "pieces of architecture that are both solid and atmospheric," and the gritty scale and colour of industry fed the early work directly. [C35632]

**Takeaway:** Mangold's biography is a straight line from a factory town to a factory floor of painting: Wurlitzer organ cabinets, sprayed finishes, plywood walls, roller-applied acrylic. The industrial means are not a phase he outgrew; they are the practice. A piece built on Mangold should feel made, not rendered -- flat, rolled, cut, joined. [C35633]

## Walls and areas: painting as object (1964-1968)

The first mature work is not a picture of a wall but a piece of one. *Walls and Areas* (Fischbach Gallery, 1965) showed sections of walls made from wall materials -- sheet wood, plywood, sheetrock -- spray-painted in wall colours (brick red, institutional two-tone), some with a notch cut in a corner revealing the board behind. *Yellow Wall (Section I + II)* (1964, National Gallery of Art) is two notched panels built from standard 4 x 8 ft plywood sheets, 244 x 244 cm overall, flat yellow, no drawing at all. *Red Wall* (1964; Tate lists 1965 -- the conflict is flagged, not resolved) is a flat rust-red irregular rectangle with a small rectangular notch cut out of the top-left corner and a shallow notch in the bottom edge; at thumbnail size no drawn line is visible, and the shaped edge, not any image on the surface, is the content. *Manila - Neutral Area* (1965-66; the Pace conversation gives 1965-67) grades gently from buff to ivory across two panels; a notch in the left panel evokes a roof overhang, the division between the panels suggests the borderline between two adjacent buildings. "Each work is a totality, but it implies that much more could be there." [C35634]

The means are the message, and the means are industrial. Early 1960s work is oil, applied by brush and then sprayed on Masonite or plywood -- flat, no hand, but fragile; shellac brushed on as a sealer under the paint, whose brush marks are visible in some early works (the marks are the shellac, not the paint). In 1968 he switched to acrylic, applied with a roller -- the W Series was the first acrylic and roller work -- because he could not spray acrylic (the water base dried in the gun), and the roller was "a very practical way of applying the paint without seeming sentimental or romantic about it"; he also hated waiting for oil to dry. Spraying was chosen to avoid the artist's touch. The colour came from the industrial world around him: manila folder, stapler green, file cabinets. "Shape is the first element in my work... everything starts there." [C35635]

**Takeaway:** Before the line, there is the wall. The first Mangold mechanism is not a drawing but a cut: a rectangle with a notch removed, a panel that is a fragment of a larger wall. Any piece that starts with a drawn circle has skipped the first ten years. [C35636]

## The near-miss inscription: a perfect figure that does not fit (M1)

The circle years begin when the shaped canvas returns and the drawn line arrives to test it. From 1970 he made shaped canvases, and from about 1970-71 he returned to stretched canvas -- the hard supports were heavy, got damaged in moving, and "fell off of people's walls" -- but he kept the roller through the 1970s, and the hand-drawn graphite line became the signature device: lines that are often imperfect, slightly trembling against the geometry. The emblem is the imperfect circle. *Circle Painting #4* (1973) is a circular canvas four feet in diameter, muted purple, with a pencil drawing of an inscribed square that "doesn't quite fit -- two corners overlap the painting's edges, setting up a strangely ethereal tension" (New Yorker, quoted by Mnuchin). *Distorted Circle within a Polygon I* (1972, MoMA, 6 ft 8 in. x 7 ft 5 in.) is a circle drawn inside -- and distorted by -- a polygonal canvas. *Distorted Square Within a Circle 1* (1973, Smithsonian, screenprint) inverts the pair. *Untitled (from Seven Aquatints)* (1973) is the same device at print scale: on a white sheet, a flat olive-green square, inside it a thin dark circle that overflows the square on all four sides, and a thin white square outline, slightly tilted, inscribed inside the circle -- three geometries that almost, but do not quite, fit each other. [C35637]

The mechanism is a near-miss inscription, and its parameters are small and testable. The ideal figure F (circle radius R, square side S, ellipse axes a, b) is inscribed in a support polygon P (rhombus, distorted polygon, circle); the centre offset is 1-4% of radius; the tilt applied to the support (not to the figure) is 0-4 degrees; the scale excess is 1-5%, so the figure overshoots the edge by a visible sliver. The constraints are what make it Mangold and not a mistake: exactly one or two crossings of figure and boundary (three or more reads as a different figure, not a near-miss); total overshoot arc at most 8% of the perimeter; exactly ONE failure mode active per picture -- offset OR tilt OR overscale, never two combined; and at least 92% of the figure's perimeter inside the support, so the figure stays recognisable. The magnitudes are a formalisation from descriptions ("ever so slightly," "barely touching"), not measurements from the works -- a builder should verify them against reproductions of *Circle Painting #4* and *Distorted Circle within a Polygon I* and adjust. In an animated version the offset drifts slowly (period 20-40 seconds) so crossings appear and disappear one at a time; the picture's state is the current crossing count and which segment is out. The eye flicks between the one or two places where the line meets the edge; the overshoot sliver is the only high-information region in an otherwise empty field. [C35638]

**Takeaway:** The cheapest possible drama -- one circle, one edge, one mistake. A generative piece can search the offset-tilt-scale space live and freeze only when the crossing count is one or two and the overshoot is under 8%; every other state is visibly "not yet the picture," which makes the search itself watchable. [C35639]

## Container-dictated distortion: the support bends the figure (M2)

*Circle In and Out of a Polygon 2* (1973, Guggenheim, 182 x 183 cm) is the second kind of imperfection, and it must not be confused with the first. What is visible is a pale, almost white-blue square-ish field whose right edge is cut on a diagonal, so the whole shape reads as an irregular polygon, and a single thin dark hand-drawn line running around it: on the left a smooth circular arc, on the right it straightens into the polygon's edges -- one continuous line that is a circle on one side and a polygon on the other, slipping in and out of the canvas edge. Almost no colour: the palest blue-grey wash against white. The line is the whole event. The rule is that the ideal circle is subjected to an affine or piecewise deformation whose parameters are dictated by the container's outline -- the container is the cause, the distortion is the effect. Where the boundary is straight (a polygon side), the drawn radius flattens; where the boundary is curved, it follows. The deformation must be derivable: given the drawing, the viewer can reconstruct the container, and if the drawing cannot be reverse-engineered to the container, the mechanism has failed. [C35640]

**Takeaway:** In M1 the figure is wrong for the support; in M2 the support is the reason the figure is wrong. A piece where the support itself is the variable -- tap to cycle the side count, drag to change the sector angle -- and the circle re-fits in real time is a live demonstration that form follows container, not the other way round. [C35641]

## Edge-as-line substitution: closure completed by the canvas edge (M3)

The third kind of imperfection is the most Mangoldian, and the one that travels best into code. In *Circle In and Out of a Polygon 2* (1973), half of the circle is outlined on the acrylic surface while the other half continues as the curved edge of the painting's right side, and the interior graphite line becomes interchangeable with the top, left, and bottom borders of the support. In *A Rectangle and a Circle within a Square* (1975, Honolulu Museum of Art), overlapping shapes have "contours formed by combinations of canvas edges and both drawn and implied lines." The figure exists only if the viewer accepts the edge of the object as a line in the drawing. Two ontologies -- image and object -- share one curve. [C35642]

The mechanism partitions a closed curve's perimeter into drawn arcs and edge arcs, where each edge arc coincides exactly with a segment of the support's boundary. A contiguous arc of 25-60% of the perimeter has its drawn stroke deleted and the support boundary moved to coincide with it -- or equivalently, the support is chosen so its boundary already coincides with that arc. The constraints: the delegated arc must coincide with the edge to within 0.5% of the radius (a visible gap kills the substitution); the drawn arcs must meet the edge arcs tangentially, with no kink at the handoff, or the kink must fall exactly at a support vertex; and at least one drawn arc and one edge arc must exist -- all-drawn or all-edge is not this mechanism. As the delegated fraction grows from 0 to 60%, the drawn line retreats and the edge takes over segment by segment, and the figure stays "the same circle" while its ontology flips. The eye traces the circle, reaches the handoff, and hesitates -- is the line still there? The hesitation is the picture. [C35643]

**Takeaway:** The canvas edge is a first-class citizen, not a boundary condition. A circle whose stroke is progressively replaced by the frame -- drag to move the handoff point, and the picture asks at every position, "is this still a circle?" -- is the single most transferable Mangold mechanism to code. [C35644]

## The shaped support as a cut, and the W/V/X decomposition (M4, M5)

The support can also be a fragment of a larger ideal shape, with the missing part present by implication. *1/3 Gray-Green Curved Area* (1966) is two joined panels comprising a third of a full circle. *Pink Area* (1965) is a square picture of two vertical panels "with a rectangle removed from its bottom right-hand corner." *Curved Plane/Figure VIII, Study* (1995) holds ellipses within a half-circle "cut off a canvas truncated on its right edge." The rule: the support is the ideal parent shape minus a cut region, and the cut must be legible as a cut from a known whole -- the sector angle a simple fraction (one-half, one-third, one-quarter) or the notch rectangular and corner-anchored; an arbitrary freeform cut reads as a blob, not a fragment. The seam between panels is always a chord or radius of the parent shape, never arbitrary. Step the sector angle through simple fractions and the support visibly becomes a smaller fragment of the same implied whole; the absent two-thirds of the circle is felt -- the eye draws it in the empty wall space. [C35645]

The W, V, X series (1968) is the same fragment logic turned into a system: a sequence of paintings in which he "deconstructed a semicircular form into fragmented shapes, systematically exploring the effects of varying combinations of outline, interior, and color." *1/2 W, V, X Series (Orange, Green, Blue)* (1968) is three half-circle canvases, each 12 x 24 in. The mechanism is exhaustive permutation of a tiny alphabet: the semicircle never changes shape or size -- it is the control -- while binary switches flip outline present or absent, interior filled or unfilled, interior chord pattern (none, single diameter, V-notch, W, X), colour from a small set of muted generics, panel count one to three. Each combination is one picture; the series is the enumeration. Step through the enumeration in Gray-code order, so each step changes exactly one switch, and the viewer sees which switch caused which change. Two cautions: the W/V/X interior patterns as chord patterns are a formalisation of "deconstructed a semicircular form" and should be checked against reproductions before a prompt fixes the chord rules; and the semicircle is the control in every member, or the series is not a series. [C35646]

**Takeaway:** A fragment is a ratio -- one-third present, two-thirds implied -- and a series is an enumeration with a control. Both are numbers a piece can vary, and both are more Mangold than any single picture. [C35647]

## Seam as line, and the frame paintings: the void as the centre (M6, M7)

Multi-panel paintings give Mangold a second line-system: the physical seam between canvases functions as a drawn line, echoing, interrupting, or continuing the interior composition. In *A Triangle Within Two Rectangles (Red)* (1977), "the physical lines formed by the edges of adjoining canvases both echo and interrupt the drawn lines of the interior composition." In *Red/White Zone Painting II* (1996), three panels with a curved top edge, "Mangold paints over the canvas joins to create an incongruity between the painted image and the structure of the support." The rule: the drawn figure's lines are computed as if the support were one surface, and the seams are overlaid as physical discontinuities; a drawn line crossing a seam either continues exactly, jogs by a small offset (0.5-2% of canvas width, so the panels are mis-registered), or stops at the seam. At most one seam per picture is a jog or a stop -- the rest continue -- so the interruption is a single event, the same single-failure constraint as M1. [C35648]

The Frame paintings, begun 1983, push the seam logic to its limit: four canvases joined into a rectangular frame with an empty centre, circled by a drawn line. *Model for Four Color Frame Painting #1* (1983) is "four rectangular panels together constitute a frame, then circled with a drawn black line"; *Four Color Frame Painting Number 11* (1985, MoMA) is four canvases, synthetic polymer paint and pencil; *Four-Color Frame Painting #4* (1984) is 10 x 7 feet. The centre is empty -- a void, not a shape -- and the drawn ellipse is the only thing that acknowledges the hole. The rule: the frame band width is constant on all four sides (12-30% of the outer width); the ellipse is drawn across the joins as if the frame were one surface, and must cross at least two panel joins, so the drawing is visibly continuous across separate objects; the void is a true hole -- nothing drawn inside it except the ellipse's arc passing through. As the band fattens and the void shrinks, the ellipse is progressively swallowed by the frame or freed into the void, and there is an exact ratio at which it stops reading as a closed curve and starts reading as four separate arcs. That threshold is a number, and a generative piece can find it live. [C35649]

**Takeaway:** The seam is a line with a different status from the pencil line -- it is real, cast-shadow real -- yet it participates in the same geometry. A piece about registration, where two panels almost align and dragging one by a few pixels breaks or heals the drawn figure at the seam, is a mechanism no single-canvas piece can have. [C35650]

## The ring: bending a column into a circle (M8, M9)

The Ring paintings (2008-2011) are the imperfect circle returned thirty-five years later as an object with a hole in it. Two-panel canvases are joined to form a ring with an empty centre -- "for all the enclosure a circle signifies, the central area is empty, a void. The painted image takes place in the band of color, circling the empty center" -- explicitly linked by Mangold to the 1980s Frame paintings. Faint pencil lines segment each panel; heavier curving lines press against the ring's boundaries. He arrived at the idea by "conceptually bending two columns and bringing them together to form a ring." Before this, his only round paintings were the single-panel *Circle* paintings of 1973-75; the circular motif -- fragmented and whole, regular and distorted -- runs through the whole career. Curator Marla Prather, on the first Ring painting (2008), noted that he invests the circle with no symbolic or spiritual value, while being "deeply aware of the signifying capacity of abstract form." *Ring Image C* (2008) is a perfect annulus, 96 in. in diameter, flat turquoise-teal, with a circular hole in the middle through which the white wall shows; thin dark pencil lines undulate around the ring, an ellipse-like curve crossing the ring's inner and outer edges, plus faint straight seams where the two joined panels meet. *Ring A (Yellow)* (2010) is smaller and looser: a yellow annulus on a white sheet, with a single thin dark grey circle drawn over it, off-centre -- it starts outside the ring at the top, crosses the yellow band, and runs inside the hole at the bottom, never closing neatly. The drawn circle and the painted ring disagree about centre and radius. The imperfection is the point -- the 1973 imperfect circle, returned as a ring. Exhibited: *New Paintings and Works on Paper -- The Ring Series*, Lisson, London, 9 September - 3 October 2009; *Ring Paintings*, Pace, New York, Friday 18 March - 23 April 2011. [C35651]

The mechanism is a bend, and the bend is honest. Start from a column: a vertical band of width w and height L = 2 x pi x R-mean. Bend it by mapping vertical coordinate y to angle theta = y / R-mean. The column's horizontal subdivision lines become the ring's radial division lines; the column's long curving line becomes the heavy arc wrapping the ring; the two ends meet at the seam. The bend is isometric in the mapping: arc length is preserved, so the ring's band length equals the column's height, and the transformation is honest, not a squash. Exactly one seam (two halves) -- Mangold's rings are two-piece. The heavy curve must be tangent to at least one boundary -- a free-floating curve inside the band is not "pressing against" anything. Animate the bend parameter t from 0 to 1: t = 0 is the straight column, t = 1 is the closed ring; the seam closes last, and the moment the two ends meet is the event; the division lines rotate from horizontal to radial as t goes from 0 to 1. A column curling into a ring is a transformation the eye can follow end to end -- the most cinematic mechanism in Mangold, and the one he himself describes as the origin of the series. The pressing curve is its own sub-mechanism: a single arc of one radius, solved from its tangency points -- choose the tangency points, the curve follows -- with at least one tangency, or the curve is decoration, not pressure. [C35652]

**Takeaway:** The strongest candidate for a generative piece: a bend parameter the user controls (drag to curl and uncurl), with the seam, the divisions, and the heavy curve all re-solving from the bend. The piece shows its own derivation -- column becomes ring -- which is exactly Mangold's own account of the idea. [C35653]

## The column: trunk, appendages, subdivision (M10)

The Column paintings (from 2002) narrow the vocabulary to four elements: the ring shape, the curled line, the column, and the column structures. *Column Structure I-XII* (2006/07) each have "a central vertical trunk measuring 10 feet high and 2 feet wide that is subdivided by straight, horizontal lines and appended with squares or triangles that jut from the sides, usually near the top." *Column Structure VIII* (2006, Metropolitan Museum of Art, 10 ft x 60 in.) is described by the Met as "an extended series of narrow columnar compositions animated with elegant hand-drawn curves," in which "the artist disrupts the symmetry of the column by adding geometric appendages... curving ones unify the overall composition as they intersect with the grid and with the painting's edges at critical junctures." *Column Structure IX* (2006) is a bright lemon-yellow shape like a squared-off T rotated -- a tall vertical trunk with a wide crossbar at the top and a rectangular bite taken out of the upper right -- with two thin dark curves sweeping across it, one big arc from the top-left corner down to the bottom tip and a second curve crossing it, forming a long pointed leaf down the trunk, over faint pencil grid lines dividing the yellow into squares. The mechanism is a vertical stack with a rhythm: regular horizontal subdivisions (the grid) broken once or twice by an asymmetric appendage (the event), then re-unified by a curve that crosses everything. Order, disruption, reconciliation -- in that order. The constraints: appendages at most three and all in the top 40% (more, or lower, and the column stops reading as a column); the trunk's outline is the support outline -- the canvas is the column shape, not a rectangle containing a column; and the unifying curve must intersect the trunk's edges at critical junctures, a subdivision line or an appendage corner, not at an arbitrary point. [C35654]

**Takeaway:** A vertical-scroll, tall-format piece -- the natural shape for a phone screen. The user adds or removes appendages (tap a side near the top) and the unifying curve re-threads itself through them. The column is a stack with a state, and its state space is small enough to enumerate. [C35655]

## Ellipse across a colour break, and the faint division (M11, M13)

The Zone paintings of the 1990s make perception, not geometry, the subject. *Red/White Zone Painting II* (1996, 90 x 198 1/4 in.) is three panels with a curved top edge forming one long shallow dome: outer panels rust-red, each with thin dark ellipses drawn on them, the central panel plain white. Mangold "paints over the canvas joins" and "draws sweeping intersecting ellipses over the textured red panels. The ovals perfectly align but are separated by the painting's central white panel" -- the viewer must mentally connect the lines across the gap. *Yellow/Black Zone Painting IV* (1996) is the same construction in yellow and black: two bright yellow outer panels with curved top edges, each carrying thin leaning ellipses, flanking a wide flat black central panel, and the ellipses stop dead at the black -- the line is interrupted by colour, not by a join. *Curved Plane/Figure VIII* (1995) is a long low triptych with a curved top edge, two outer panels in bright orange carrying tall thin hand-drawn ellipses that lean and overlap, a wide central panel in flat warm grey, empty -- the ellipses are drawn as if continuing behind the grey centre, and the viewer completes them mentally. The mechanism: one ellipse drawn across two or three panels of different colours, so the same line reads differently against each ground; the ellipse is computed in the support's coordinate system, independent of the colour bands, and where it crosses a band boundary nothing changes in the line -- the ground changes under it. The ellipse must cross at least one colour boundary, and its line weight and colour are constant across bands -- the line does not adapt to its ground. In the Zone variant the central panel is white and the ellipse segment over it is drawn in white or omitted, so the ovals perfectly align but are separated. Slide the colour boundary horizontally while the ellipse stays fixed, and the reading of the figure (one ellipse? two arcs?) flips at the point where a band boundary passes through the ellipse's extreme point. [C35656]

Under the heavy line sits a second, quieter system. In the Ring paintings, "faint pencil lines, visible through monochromatic washes of acrylic paint, segment each panel into even proportions"; in *Column Structure VIII*, "straight lines in pencil subdivide each section." The mechanism is a faint division: the panel divided into two to six exactly even parts by faint lines, with the heavy figure drawn over them, independent of the divisions. Two line-weights, two jobs: the faint lines are structure (the grid), the heavy line is the figure. The faint lines must be visible on close inspection and invisible at a glance -- in code, opacity roughly 0.15-0.30 over the ground -- and the heavy figure must cross at least one faint line. Step the division count upward and the faint grid re-divides while the heavy figure stays put, so its relationship to the grid changes at each step. The piece rewards sustained looking, which is Mangold's own stated effect: the work invites intense scrutiny. [C35657]

**Takeaway:** One line and one moving edge is a whole piece: drag the colour boundary through a fixed ellipse and watch the figure survive or split. And a piece with two viewing distances built in -- at a glance one heavy curve, on attention a faint equal grid -- is the quietest mechanism here, and the easiest to rush past. [C35658]

## Serial exhaustion: one motif, worked until it is used up (M12)

Mangold "almost always works in extensive series, often carried through both paintings and works on paper," in groups he described in 1994 as usually 10-12 works taking one to three years, "distinct bundles of work that have a beginning and an ending," worked intuitively with no pre-defined plan; after finishing a group comes "a panicky period" until the next idea suggests itself. In 1999 he said he does dozens of studies for major paintings. The series are the practice: Circle Paintings (1973-75), Column Structures I-XII (2006/07, continuing past XII to XIII A and XV), the Ring series (2008-12), the Pentagons (2022-24). The mechanism is serial exhaustion: enumerate the variation space of a motif systematically (not randomly) -- colour from a small set of muted generics, size or scale, and one structural parameter of the motif -- keep a visited set, and end the series when a new variation produces no perceptible difference from an existing member: a perceptual distance to its nearest neighbour below a threshold (a colour difference below just-noticeable, or a structural parameter change that moves no line by more than 0.5% of canvas). The stopping rule is explicit and checkable: the piece must be able to say "this motif is exhausted" and stop, and the termination itself is displayed -- the grid fills, the last cell is marked. The motif itself never changes mid-series. [C35659]

**Takeaway:** A meta-mechanism: a piece that runs a series -- it generates Mangold-like members (one motif, colour, size, and parameter steps) and stops, visibly, when the motif is used up. The user picks the motif; the machine does the exhausting. This is the only honest way to make "a Mangold" generatively: not one picture, but a finite, terminating series. [C35660]

## The tilted support, and figures that share one edge (M14, M15)

Two quieter mechanisms complete the set. The tilted support: a canvas that is a square or rectangle tilted by a few degrees -- a rhombus -- so that the wrongness is in the support, not the drawing. The circle in *Distorted Circle* sits in "the ever so slightly tilted, rhombus canvas." The figure is computed in the support's local coordinates -- perfect in the canvas, wrong in the room -- and the support is then rotated by 1-5 degrees about its centre. Larger reads as a deliberate diamond, not a tilt; the figure is not additionally distorted, or the tilt is not the single failure. The screen edge is the giveaway: the canvas is never parallel to it. Let the tilt oscillate slowly between minus and plus the maximum (period 30-60 seconds) and the figure rocks almost imperceptibly; the viewer notices the tilt only when it reverses. [C35661]

Overlapping figures that share one edge: in *A Rectangle and a Circle within a Square* (1975), overlapping shapes have contours formed by combinations of canvas edges and drawn and implied lines; in *Four Triangles within a Circle, #1* (1974, Art Institute of Chicago, 36 in. diameter), four triangles are inscribed in a circle, sharing the circle's boundary and each other's chords; in *1/4 circle, a square and a triangle within a rectangle (cream)* (1975, Yale University Art Gallery), three figures share one rectangle's discipline. The rule: each figure is inscribed so that at least one of its vertices or arcs lies on the container's boundary, not inside it; where two figures meet, they share a vertex or a chord, and the shared element is drawn once, in one weight -- double-drawing the shared line is an error; and each figure must retain at least 50% of its own perimeter unshared, or the figures collapse into one blob. Rotate one figure inside its circumscribed circle while keeping its boundary contacts, and the shared chords re-solve continuously: the figures interlock and re-interlock, and the shared line is the hinge. [C35662]

**Takeaway:** The subtlest mechanism here is the tilt -- 2 degrees each way over a minute, and the interaction is noticing. The shared edge is the busiest: one line doing two jobs, and the container is the law the figures live under. [C35663]

## Aesthetic logic

Mangold's aesthetic logic is a discipline of addition by subtraction. "It seemed to me when I began that the only way to make a painting was to start at zero and add one thing at a time" (quoted by Michael Klein, 2001 interview). "As a painter, I control what I do in my studio. If I'm working on something that really engages me, I'm satisfied. I work intuitively around an idea and continue with it until it runs out for me." The constants across sixty years are few and strict: one or two flat colours; one thin hand-drawn line, when present, always slightly imperfect; the shaped or joined canvas doing half the work; the wall treated as part of the painting (voids, gaps between panels, the ring's hole); scale that swings between intimate (a 17-in. masonite panel, a 10-in. print) and architectural (10-ft columns, 5-metre zones). Colour is a parameter, not a subject: muted monochrome through the 1970s, then brighter, saturated colour in the early-mid 1980s (pumpkin orange, fiery red, jungle green) for the X, +, and Frame paintings, then the deep ochres of the Attic Series (1990-92, a square canvas cut away on one side into a gentle arc, two pencilled triangles meeting at the centre, the shape, colour, and line deriving from Greek vases -- Attic means Attica), then the rust-red, white, yellow, and black of the Zones, then the pentagons of 2022-24 in orange-red, green, dark grey-black, pale yellow, deep green, beige. [C35664]

The later work closes the circle back to the beginning. The Plane Structures (2019-22) are diagonally oriented, shapeshifting structures that "seem to be in motion, propelling themselves up the gallery wall" (*Plane Structure 7* and *8*, 2021), and since 2018 Mangold has made works free of drawing, breaking from his longstanding practice of incorporating line -- a return to the drawing-free early Walls. The Pentagons (2022-24) are broad planes of colour across pentagonal and folded multi-panel canvases: *Four Pentagons* (2022, Art Institute of Chicago, on loan to Pace for the 2025 show, "one of the largest works that Mangold has produced in decades"), four large monochrome panels varying in shape, size, and colour, each with a ninety-degree angle in the bottom right corner; *Three Pentagons* (2023); *Double Pentagon Oxide 4* (2024), a single wide pentagonal canvas split vertically into deep green and pale beige, no drawn line at all; *Divided Image* (2024), a slightly irregular quadrilateral split vertically into olive and lemon-yellow, one seam, no drawn line. The Brooklyn Rail's reviewer (July 2025) reads the Pentagons between Kelly (monochrome planar form) and Stella (suggested spatiality): "though the shapes of the canvases remain legible, they nevertheless seem to move through space, warping, bending, and folding." The 1960s Walls returned with sixty years of restraint: one shape, two tones, one division. [C35665]
