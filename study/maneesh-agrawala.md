# Maneesh Agrawala: Study Notes

**Site:** Maneesh Agrawala is the Forest Baskett Professor of Computer Science at Stanford University and Director of the Brown Institute for Media Innovation, a post he has held since returning to Stanford in 2015 after a decade (2005-2015) as Professor of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science at UC Berkeley and three years at Microsoft Research. He took his B.S. in Mathematics (1994) and his Ph.D. in Computer Science (2002, dissertation "Visualizing Route Maps", advisor Pat Hanrahan) both at Stanford. His stated program, in his own words on his Stanford page, is to investigate "how cognitive design principles can be used to improve the effectiveness of audio/visual media" -- to discover the design principles and then instantiate them in interactive and automated design tools. He is a MacArthur Fellow (2009, $500,000, announced Tuesday 22 September 2009), a SIGGRAPH Significant New Researcher (2008), a Sloan Fellow (2007), an NSF CAREER awardee (2007), an Allen Distinguished Investigator (2014), a member of the SIGCHI Academy (2021), and an ACM Fellow (2022, announced Wednesday 18 January 2023). He is credited as a Software Engineer (Rendering Software Development) on Pixar's *A Bug's Life* (1998) in four independent credit listings; no Pixar-primary source was read. [C36979]

**Relevance:** Agrawala's work asks how a system decides what to show, in what order, and from which viewpoint, so that a flat drawing can be built from or understood -- the drawing as a program that reveals a thing step by step rather than a picture of it. The evidence is "Designing Effective Step-by-Step Assembly Instructions" (SIGGRAPH 2003), which derives viewpoint-selection and step-segmentation rules from cognitive-psychology experiments and implements them as an algorithm that generates assembly diagrams, and "Artistic Multiprojection Rendering" (2000), which renders one scene from several viewpoints at once under a single master camera that keeps the occlusion honest. [C36980]

**Doorway:** The 454th doorway. Hinckley asked where intention lives when the system reads the hand before it acts -- in the hand that approaches, in the sensor that watches, or in the rule that decides what the approach meant? Agrawala answers the third branch, the rule: his systems read the requirements of a task (navigate, assemble, inspect) and pre-compute the depiction those requirements demand, before the viewer looks. LineDrive decides what a navigator needs to know before laying out a map; the assembly planner decides what a builder needs to see before rendering a diagram; the explosion graph decides what must move, in what order, to expose a chosen part. The open question carried forward is: if the rule pre-decides what you need to see, who corrects the rule when it hides the thing you actually wanted -- and what does the correction look like, a nudge, an override, or a new rule entirely? [C36981]

## M1 -- The explosion graph (Automated Generation of Interactive 3D Exploded View Diagrams, SIGGRAPH 2008)

**Subject:** A complex manufactured object -- turbine, iPod, transmission, disk brake, carburetor, human arm -- taken apart along straight lines so every hidden part becomes visible while the whole stays readable. The paper's examples run from 18 parts (disk brake) to 55 (transmission), and the system is demonstrated on models up to roughly 50 parts. [C36983]

**Visual principle:** Occlusion resolved by separation rather than by removal. Parts keep their relative order and orientation, so the viewer can mentally reassemble the object; compactness (parts move as little as possible) trades against visibility (every part of interest must be seen). The tension between the two is a visual tension the viewer can feel. [C36984]

**Computational mechanism:** The input is a 3D model whose parts are separate solid objects, optionally grouped into a sub-assembly hierarchy; default explosion directions are the six axis directions of the model's coordinate frame. Three low-level relations are computed first: contact (shortest distance between a part pair is exactly 0), blocking (local translational blocking cones per contacting pair, combined into a directional blocking graph per direction), and containment -- the paper's own definition is explicitly *approximate*: part p1 contains p2 if the convex hull of p2 lies completely inside the convex hull of p1 AND p1 blocks p2 in every explosion direction. The explosion graph is a directed acyclic graph built iteratively: all parts start in the active set S; at each iteration, find parts unblocked in at least one direction by any other active part; for each, compute the minimum distance it must move in an unblocked direction to escape the bounding box of the active parts in contact with it; the part with the minimum escape distance is added to the graph, with an edge from every active part that touches it and its minimum-escape direction stored as its explosion direction; then it is removed from S. For each sub-assembly the same algorithm runs with its direct children treated as atomic parts, giving nested graphs that expand and collapse independently. Interlock is handled in two classes: split an interlocked sub-assembly into the largest removable partial sub-assembly, and among candidates remove the smallest; or split a container part into two segments with a cutting plane through the bounding box centre of the container, normal parallel to the chosen explosion direction, choosing the direction that requires the smallest separation for the two segments to fully disocclude and escape the bounding box of the contained parts. Nested containers get an extra constraint: prefer directions where nested container bounding boxes stay inside the exploded bounding box of the outer container, else take the direction minimizing total overflow volume. Explosion graphs are precomputed from 26 viewpoints (faces, edges, corners of an axis-aligned cube centred on the model bounding-box centre); at viewing time the system switches to the graph closest to the current viewpoint. Animation runs in reverse topological order -- outermost to innermost, larger sub-assemblies before smaller ones -- and collapse runs in the opposite order, so blocking constraints are never violated mid-animation. The hierarchical target-exposure iteration increases a sub-assembly's offset by 1 percent of the whole-model bounding box diagonal per iteration. Precomputation on a 2.6 GHz Core 2 Duo: the transmission (55 parts) took 1,778 s total (885 s contacts, 835 s blocking, 2.5 s graph); the disk brake (18 parts) 635 s; the iPod (19 parts) 235 s. [C36985]

**Takeaway:** Order, direction, and distance are all computed from contact, never authored. A piece built on the explosion graph is not an animation of an explosion; it is a system that decides, for any assembly, what can move, in which order, and how far -- and the viewer can feel the constraints when dragging against them. [C36986]

## M2 -- Target exposure: explode only what hides the target (SIGGRAPH 2008, section 5.4)

**Subject:** The same exploded object, but the viewer names one part (or sub-assembly) and the system exposes only that part, leaving the rest assembled. Labels mark the target in red. [C36987]

**Visual principle:** Selective revelation. Most of the object stays whole, so context survives; only the occluders of the chosen target move, and only as far as needed. The reveal is a negotiation: the smallest possible disturbance that makes the chosen thing visible. [C36988]

**Computational mechanism:** Given a target set T, the system visits parts in topological order of the explosion graph and moves part p only as far along its explosion direction as needed to satisfy four conditions: (1) p does not occlude any previously visited target; (2) if p is in T, p is not occluded by any visited part; (3) p is not occluded by any visited target that touches p; (4) if p is in T, p does not occlude any visited part that touches p. Each move is the minimum distance along p's explosion direction that eliminates the unwanted occlusion, and if p is moved, its explosion-graph descendants move with it so no blocking constraint is violated. The hierarchical version processes the highest-level sub-assembly first, recurses inside any sub-assembly containing targets, then checks blocking and occlusion against visited parts and iteratively increases the sub-assembly offset by 1 percent of the bounding-box diagonal until the constraints hold. The failure case is stated in the paper: if a part's explosion direction is exactly parallel to the viewing direction, the target cannot be exposed from that viewpoint, and the system reports this; in practice such failures rarely arise. A cutaway variant, for models instrumented as in Li et al. 2007, first cuts a hole large enough for the target to escape through, choosing explosion direction d that escapes the bounding box fastest subject to d.v < 0 (v the viewing direction), then explodes the sub-assembly through the hole and internally. [C36989]

**Takeaway:** The reveal as a computation, not a flourish: the machine knows what hides what and moves exactly the minimum. Hover-riffle and blocking-checked dragging (P1 sections 5.2-5.3) belong here too -- hover over a part and it explodes away from adjacent portions and returns when the cursor leaves, click pins it, and distortion is restricted to the computed explosion directions, unlike a fisheye; a drag slides a part only along its stored explosion direction, propagates through explosion ancestors, and halts at blocking descendants until a click moves them out of the way. [C36990]

## M3 -- Assembly planning as visibility search (Designing Effective Step-by-Step Assembly Instructions, SIGGRAPH 2003)

**Subject:** Step-by-step instructions for assembling an everyday object -- TV stand (9 non-fastener parts, 6 steps), bookcase (9 parts, 10 steps), LEGO car (61 parts, 8 steps), case27 (25 parts, a synthetic test object, 6 steps), 80/20 table (13 parts, 15 steps) -- generated from part geometry alone, plus a default camera viewpoint and optional grouping labels and ordering constraints. [C36991]

**Visual principle:** One significant part per diagram; new parts fully visible; earlier parts partly visible for context; future parts never fully hidden by the current step. Repetition is boring, so repeated operations are shown twice and then skipped. The paper's own hierarchy: people conceive attachment as combine subassemblies, then join significant individual parts, then attach smaller parts and fasteners; for most build-at-home objects a two-level hierarchy (significant parts plus less important parts and fasteners) suffices. Action diagrams, which spatially separate the parts to be attached and use guidelines to show where they attach, are superior to structural diagrams for the TV stand task, because they contain all the structural information plus explicitly depict the operations; the exception is LEGO, whose parts fasten the same way, so showing the operation matters less. [C36992]

**Computational mechanism:** The planner searches the space of feasible assembly sequences; the presenter renders each step. Low-level computations: Contacts(P), parts at shortest distance exactly 0.0 from some part in P, with contact faces stored; blocking via a local translational blocking (LTB) cone per pair of contacting parts, combined into a blocking graph that partitions the sphere of translational directions, with a directed graph per region (edge pi -> pj means pi is blocked by pj for all directions in that region); for many assemblies only the 6 principal motion directions are stored. Visibility Vis(P, Q) is a measured quantity, not an analytic one: a two-pass pixel-count render -- render P in green, count green pixels for Area(P); without clearing the framebuffer render Q in red, count remaining green pixels for Area(P, Q); Vis(P, Q) = Area(P, Q) / Area(P). The planner's state is (Ui unattached parts, Ai attached parts); at each step it considers subsets R of Ui. Hard constraints, any violation rejecting R: interference (every unattached part and its fasteners must be removable from Ai union R, i.e. RemoveDirs non-empty); attachment (each part in R must fasten onto, or at least touch, some already-attached part; fastener-free parts need only contact); ordering (user constraints "attach pk before pl" respected); grouping (all parts in a symmetric group added together). The only objective function is visibility, three scores summed: current parts Score(R) = min over rj in R of Vis(rj, R - rj); previous parts Score(Ai) = Vis(Ai, R); future parts Score(Ui - R) = min over uj in Ui - R of Vis(uj, R). The paper's headline numbers, empirical: "visibility scores at or above 50% for minimum current part visibility, 10% for previous part visibility, and 25% for minimum future part visibility produce good results." The naive search space is 2^N; two accelerations: cull parts that individually fail interference, attachment, or ordering into a smaller set Ui', then process subsets of Ui' in breadth-first order, largest first, and exit as soon as an entire level yields a subset with visibility score greater than an empirically determined threshold -- the paper never states the numeric value of that early-exit threshold, and this file quotes none. The result: the LEGO car planner scored only 99 subsets out of an astronomically large exhaustive space; the TV stand 12 of 882; the bookcase 13 of 885. Sequence determination then splits a chosen subset with more than one significant part into one-significant-part steps, ordered least visible first, ties broken by adding the part furthest from the camera first; similar-action groups are shown in full detail only the first two times by default, then omitted; fasteners are reinserted from Contacts(P) intersected with the fastener set before presentation. Reorientation has two triggers, a preferred orientation for a significant part or low visibility: if the maximum visibility of any part in the current step is below a 35% threshold, the system searches a small set of alternative viewpoints -- above, at oblique angles, left or right, following Blanz et al. 1999's finding that people prefer viewing most objects from above and at oblique angles -- and picks the one giving the largest minimum visibility over all parts in the step; an object reorientation, once applied, is maintained for all subsequent diagrams. [C36993]

**Takeaway:** "Understandable" is a computable score, and the planner searches for the most understandable next step. A piece can show that weighing happening -- what is new, what is context, what is still to come -- not just its result. The human experiments behind the principles (three experiments on a TV stand, Heiser and Tversky 2002) report only qualitative outcomes in this paper -- highly rated instructions took less time and fewer errors, with no participant counts, no effect sizes, no p-values; the numbers live in the companion paper: Heiser et al., AVI 2004, pp. 311-319, DOI 10.1145/989863.989917, whose abstract reports the computer-generated instructions reduced assembly time by an average of 35% and errors by 50% against factory-provided and highly rated hand-designed instructions. [C36994]

## M4 -- Stacks and contact-face guidelines (SIGGRAPH 2003, section 5.2)

**Subject:** A single assembly step drawn so the viewer can see exactly where the new part goes. The paper's worked example is a TV-stand wheel: a guideline from the wheel's bounding-box centre misses the stem, while one from the contact faces passes through it (Fig. 9, viewed as pixels). [C36995]

**Visual principle:** Separate to show, connect to explain. New parts are pulled away from the assembly along a free direction; thin guidelines reconnect each part to its true attachment point, so alignment is never ambiguous. [C36996]

**Computational mechanism:** The separation direction for each new part pi must be interference-free -- one of RemoveDirs(pi, Aj) -- and among those is the direction that lets pi escape the bounding box of the earlier parts Aj as fast as possible (shortest distance from the contact-faces' bounding-box centre to the contact parts' bounding box, over the feasible removal directions); ties break toward the camera viewpoint. A fixed translation per part fails when new parts stack on one another, so the presenter builds stacks: a sequence of parts where (1) all parts except the first share the same separation direction, (2) the parts share a stabbing line in the separation direction, (3) each part is in contact with the next part in the sequence. Stacks are built greedily from the directional blocking graph -- start from a root part, form a stabbing line through its centre, take the maximal sequence meeting the stack conditions in both directions, the furthest part in the negative separation direction becoming the base part -- and each part added to a stack is removed from the group and the blocking graph updated until the group is empty. Given a stack expansion distance d, start at the base and translate each subsequent part by d from the previous part along the separation direction, so total translation accumulates down the stack; if a non-base part is itself a base part for another stack, that whole stack translates by the same amount, recursively. The already-attached parts Aj are included as a single grouped part with no separation directions, providing a base part for all stacks. The same machinery generates full exploded views by building stacks from all parts (Fig. 12: the four rods at top no longer align with the parts they slide into after expansion, so no guidelines are generated for them). Guideline endpoints are computed from the contact faces, not part centres: look up the contact faces between pi and the next part in the stack, compute the centre of their bounding box, shoot a ray in the positive and negative separation directions, set endpoints to the intersections of those rays with the contact faces' bounding box. Style rules: if either endpoint contains a fastener, the guideline is drawn dotted; if the part at the end of a guideline is a significant part, it is drawn as an arrow in the negative separation direction, indicating how the part should be brought in. Rendering style follows technical-illustration conventions (Martin 1989): Gouraud shading, part edges outlined in contrasting colours -- black lines for lighter parts, white lines for darker parts -- to differentiate same-coloured parts. What the system does not automate is noted in the paper: the circular insets for small fasteners in Fig. 11 steps 1-3 were added manually. [C36997]

**Takeaway:** The guideline rule is a small, exact piece of visual intelligence: a line that finds where two things actually touch, not where their centres are. In a generative piece the guidelines become the drawing itself -- thin threads that know the contact geometry -- and the dotted-versus-arrow coding (fastener versus significant part) is a ready-made visual language for "small thing that holds" versus "big thing that moves." [C36998]

## M5 -- Multiprojection: one scene, many cameras (Artistic Multiprojection Rendering, Rendering Techniques 2000)

**Subject:** A hand-drawn-looking image of a 3D scene in which each object is drawn from its own best viewpoint: de Chirico's street, Cezanne's still life, a city from above, a row of columns. The paper's three stated functions for multiple projections in art are expression (de Chirico's incongruous spatial system creates "the melancholy aura"), representation (best views; reducing wide-angle distortion -- Raphael's *School of Athens* corrects the foreground humans so a sphere reads circular instead of elliptical), and visualization (artificial perspective via multiple parallel projections, where objects do not diminish with distance and one face keeps its true shape, allowing size and area comparisons). [C36999]

**Visual principle:** A single perspective is a restriction. Different objects want different projections, and the picture stays coherent anyway: "the overall impression of a three-dimensional space remains intact despite large disparities between projections." Inconsistencies create tension between planes, flattening some regions while enhancing depth in others. [C37000]

**Computational mechanism:** The input is a set of camera groups (each associating a collection of geometric objects with one local camera), plus a master camera and optional user-specified occlusion constraints. Stage 1 renders each camera group into a separate image layer; stage 2 detects occlusion constraints; stage 3 composites the layers. The hard problem is visibility: in a multiprojection image there is no uniquely defined visibility ordering, because the fibre at a pixel is the union of several lines from different cameras. The naive fix -- comparing local depth values, or folding the local projection into each object's modelling transform and using a standard z-buffer -- produces objectionable occlusion artifacts: a vase on a table would intersect the table, because the vase's local projection moves it in 3D. The paper's key move: the master camera imposes visibility ordering; local cameras affect only shape distortion. Depth-based compositing transforms every pixel of every layer back into world space using its pixel coordinates and z-value, applies the master camera projection, and runs a standard z-buffer test to find the frontmost point at each master-camera pixel -- but per-pixel depth alone is not enough (Fig. 2, viewed as pixels: a column point and a floor point coincide in the multiprojection image, the floor point is closer to the master camera, so depth compositing lets the floor wrongly occlude the column, yet in the master view the column occludes the floor). The fix is object-level occlusion constraints, computed automatically with respect to the master camera after Snyder and Lengyel 1998: render objects with the master camera into a buffer storing (camera group ID, depth) per pixel; sort objects per pixel by depth; for each pair, if a conflicting occlusion constraint already appears, mark the pair non-ordered, else add the constraint "A occludes B" -- A occludes B only if every point of A is in front of B in the master view. Compositing then uses the constraint where one exists and falls back to depth compositing otherwise; user-specified constraints override computed ones, conflicts resolved in favour of the user, conflicting computed constraints removed. Two ambiguities fall back to depth: convex hulls intersecting (A occludes B in some regions, B occludes A in others -- a binary occlusion cycle, marked non-ordered) and layers overlapping where the master view shows no occlusion at all. A 3-object cycle (A occludes B, B occludes C, C occludes A) is handled pixel by pixel and produces the desired image regardless of order, unless a single pixel contains all three, which requires an arbitrary choice of which object is visible; the paper states this rarely occurs in practice and the user can choose. Performance on an SGI RealityEngine: the de Chirico scene, 5 camera groups, renders at 10-12 fps at 402x491; the Cezanne still life, 10 camera groups, at 3-5 fps at 333x256. Adjusting the 5 de Chirico cameras interactively took about an hour and a half, about 20 minutes of it matching the painting and the rest animating the van; the 10 still-life cameras took about an hour. All occlusion relationships in every example were computed by the algorithm -- no manual occlusion constraints were needed for the shown results. [C37001]

**Takeaway:** Perspective itself becomes a per-object parameter, and occlusion is negotiated by a master camera rather than by physics. A piece can let each object see the world from its own angle while the picture holds together -- exactly the Cubist and Cezanne move, made executable. [C37002]

## M6 -- Camera constraints: dolly, fixed view, fixed position, direct orientation (Rendering Techniques 2000, section 5)

**Subject:** Controlling a local camera so a chosen visual property stays put while everything else changes. Four constraints, each a system of equations over camera parameters P (centre of projection), v (view unit vector), w (image-plane unit normal), u (world up), n (distance to image plane along v). Object size is characterized by two points x1, x2 on the object; in practice x is the centre of the object's bounding sphere and x1, x2 are the extremal points of the bounding sphere in a plane parallel to the image plane. [C37003]

**Visual principle:** Hold one thing constant (size, view, position, orientation) and let perspective do the talking. The held constant is what makes the distortion readable instead of chaotic. [C37004]

**Computational mechanism:** (1) Object-size constraint -- the Vertigo dolly-zoom: keep the projected distance between two points on the object constant while the camera dollies toward or away from it. Simplest implementation: move the camera toward the object centre, P' = P + (x - P) delta t, keep v and w fixed, recompute the near distance n' from the ratio of projected distances before and after. Perspective convergence changes while size and position stay fixed; in the film *Vertigo*, Hitchcock uses this to give the audience the sense of vertigo. Pushed to the limit (camera at infinity, view vector parallel to the line from camera to object centre) it yields an oblique projection; applied per object, with receding parallels aimed at one vanishing point, it builds artificial perspective (Plate 3). (2) Fixed-view constraint -- the sprite that stays 3D: translate an object parallel to the image plane while keeping its view, size, and image position fixed -- the direction from object centre to camera centre preserved, image position and projected size preserved -- so the object translates "much like a 2D sprite," but unlike a sprite it "continues to move in 3D and can therefore rotate and deform as a 3D body." (3) Fixed-position constraint -- off-axis pan: the object stays fixed in the image plane as it translates in world space, so different sides of it are exposed; P and w stay fixed, v and n change, the image-plane centre shifted by d1.up + d2.(up x w) for the image displacement d of the object centre. (4) Direct-orientation constraint -- per-object "look at me" cameras: point each object's local camera directly at it (image-plane normal parallel to the direction to the object), preserving image position and approximately preserving size, with the distance from camera to the object's image kept fixed as a simpler substitute for the size equation. Fig. 5 (viewed as pixels): a row of columns under a wide-angle camera -- (a) single projection stretches the edge columns; (b) one shared parallel projection corrects the distortion but the columns look flat and the lighting is wrong (every column lit identically); (c) direct orientation per column corrects the distortion, "we see exactly the same parts of each column as in the original view and the lighting is correct because the visibility is unchanged" -- because all local cameras share the same centre of projection. [C37005]

**Takeaway:** Four small equations, four distinct visual feelings: the Vertigo squeeze, the flat cartoon slide, the pinned object that turns in place, the column that refuses to stretch. Each is a complete interactive instrument: one dragged parameter, one held invariant, one visible distortion. Animation caveat from the paper: motion parallax may conflict with the perspective and occlusion cues and disconcert the viewer; animating objects while holding the background fixed works well, moving the whole scene works only for relatively small translations and rotations -- except when all local cameras share the same centre of projection, where moving the whole scene causes no unexpected effects. [C37006]

## M7 -- Route generalization: distort to be understood (Rendering Effective Route Maps, SIGGRAPH 2001 -- secondary sources only)

**Subject:** A driving-directions map that looks hand-drawn: short roads drawn long, long roads drawn short, bends straightened, everything off the route deleted. This mechanism was NOT read end to end; everything below is transcribed from a slide-deck summary, the dissertation abstract, and the SIGGRAPH History Archives page, and the build leg must read the SIGGRAPH 2001 paper (pp. 241-249, DOI 10.1145/383259.383286) before building on any of these numbers. [C37007]

**Visual principle:** Communicate turning points, not geography. Correct lengths, correct angles, and correct shapes are explicitly NOT the goal (a cognitive-psychology finding cited in the paper). Space is distributed by importance, not by physical length. [C37008]

**Computational mechanism:** The pipeline, as reported: shape simplification, road layout, label layout, context layout, decoration. Shape simplification under three hard constraints -- no missing intersection, no false intersection, no wrong turn direction -- with more conservative simplification where shape matters (curvy highway ramps). Road layout as simulated annealing over all road-layout configurations, with a layout scoring function and a perturbation function: a perturbation modifies a road's length by up to 20 percent or its orientation by 5 degrees (slide-deck figure). Scoring penalizes short roads, changes in the ordering of road lengths, changes in orientation from the original, missing and false intersections, and bad shape (start and end orientations differing, or endpoints too close). Label layout tries deterministic placement first, then resorts to search for conflict resolution; context adds back cross-streets and landmarks; decoration adds a global orientation arrow and explicit turn points. Output sizing (slide-deck figure): 200 pixels for routes with 10 steps plus 10 pixels per additional step. Evaluation, from the dissertation abstract: feedback from over 2,200 users, "just over 99 percent" of whom believed LineDrive maps preferable to standard computer-generated route maps; the slide deck separately says more than 95 percent said they would use the system -- the two figures measure different things (preference versus would-use) and must not be conflated. [C37009]

**Takeaway:** Generalization is the mapmaker's version of the explosion graph's compactness rule: lie about geometry in exactly the ways that help the eye. It keeps intersections and turn directions true while everything else bends -- and it is honest about its dishonesty, which is why the mechanism can be a piece rather than a trick. [C37010]

## The arc: the rule that decides what you see

Thirty years of work, one sustained argument: a drawing should be computed from what its reader needs, not traced from what is there. The early work (1995-2002, Stanford) asks how a picture can be less accurate and more useful -- 3D painting on scanned surfaces, soft shadows, then LineDrive, which throws away most of a map and reshapes what remains so a driver can follow it. The middle work (2003-2008, Microsoft Research and Berkeley) is the systematic version: take the design principles out of hand-made exemplars and cognitive-psychology experiments, write them as rules, and let the machine draw. Assembly instructions, exploded views, cutaways, tourist maps -- each is the same three-stage method the 2011 Communications of the ACM paper states outright: identify principles from hand-designed exemplars and perception research, instantiate them in algorithms, evaluate. The later work (2010-2025, Stanford) widens the media -- mechanical assemblies that explain themselves in motion, interview video cut on dialogue, talking-head video edited by editing its transcript, quilts proved sewable before a stitch, visual beats time-warped onto musical beats -- and then hands the same "draw what I mean, not what I said" control to generative models: ControlNet (2023, shared Marr Prize, ICCV 2023) conditions a frozen diffusion model on edges, pose, depth, or scribbles through zero convolutions that start at zero so training cannot damage the base model, and FramePack (2025) packs frame context by importance so a next-frame video model can hold far more frames. The through-line is not a medium but a stance: the system decides what the viewer needs before the viewer looks, and the decision is a rule you can read, not a taste you must guess. [C37011]

## The aesthetic logic

Agrawala's systems share a visual grammar that is directly portable: one global ordering plus many local freedoms. The master camera orders occlusion while local cameras distort shape; the visibility score ranks steps while each step's diagram is free; the explosion graph fixes order and direction while the viewer drags within them. The drawing is always a negotiation between a rule and a freedom, and the rule is always legible in the picture: guidelines that find contact faces, dotted lines for fasteners and arrows for significant parts, a vanishing point you can drag, a viewpoint that changes only when visibility falls below 35 percent. Nothing is hidden behind a black box; the computation shows its work. That is why his mechanisms transfer to generative pieces so cleanly: each one is already a small machine for deciding what to show, and the piece is that machine running on new material. [C37012]
