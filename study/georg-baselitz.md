## The life: born into a destroyed order, the picture turned over

Georg Baselitz was born Hans-Georg Bruno Kern on January 23, 1938, in Deutschbaselitz, in Upper Lusatia, Saxony, Germany, the son of an elementary school teacher; the family lived in the local school building and moved to Kamenz in 1950. He grew up in the desolation and ruin of post-war East Germany, and in later life recalled seeing Dresden, about 20 miles from home, burning in the distance after the Allied firebombing in 1945, summing up his starting point as: "I was born into a destroyed order, a destroyed landscape, a destroyed people." A reproduction of Louis-Ferdinand von Rayski's Wermsdorfer Wald (Wermsdorf Wood, 1859) hung in the assembly hall of his school in Kamenz and had a formative and lasting influence on him; by age 15 he had already painted portraits, religious subjects, still lifes and landscapes. (C25528, leg 1 C25400, C25401, C25402; synthesis C25490)

In 1955 he applied to the Kunstakademie in Dresden and was rejected; in 1956 he enrolled at the Hochschule fuer Bildende und Angewandte Kunst in East Berlin, studying painting under Walter Womacka and Herbert Behrens-Hangler, and befriending Peter Graf and Ralf Winkler (later A. R. Penck). After two semesters he was expelled for "sociopolitical immaturity" because he did not comply with the socialist ideas of the GDR -- in practice, painting in a manner indebted to Picasso rather than in the officially approved Socialist Realist style. In 1957 he resumed his studies at the Hochschule der Kuenste in West Berlin, where he met his future wife Johanna Elke Kretzschmar, studied under Hann Trier, and immersed himself in Ernst Wilhelm Nay, Wassily Kandinsky, Kasimir Malevich, Jackson Pollock and Philip Guston. He attended Hann Trier's master class in 1961, completed his studies in 1962, and in 1961 adopted the name Georg Baselitz, after his birthplace. The name-change year carries a variant: TheArtStory gives 1958, against Wikipedia, Guggenheim Bilbao, Britannica, artnet and White Cube for 1961; 1961 is used here. He married Kretzschmar in 1962; their sons Daniel Blau and Anton Kern both became gallerists. (C25529, leg 1 C25403, C25404, C25405, C25406; synthesis C25490)

At the turn of 1959 to 1960 he began producing his first original works in a distinct style of his own, among them the Rayski-Head series, and in 1961 he and Eugen Schoenebeck staged an exhibition in an abandoned house in Berlin and published the "1. Pandaemonisches Manifest" (Pandemonium Manifesto), a second manifesto following in 1962. His first solo exhibition in West Berlin, at Galerie Werner & Katz in 1963, caused a public scandal: two pictures, The Big Night Down the Drain (Die grosse Nacht im Eimer, 1962/63) and The Naked Man (Der Nackte Mann, 1962), were seized two days after the opening by the public prosecutor on grounds of lewd content, and the court case did not end until October 1965, when the paintings were returned. The scandal, amplified by press coverage, jump-started his career in the West. In spring 1964 he produced his first etchings at Schloss Wolfsburg, and in 1965 he won a six-month scholarship to the Villa Romana in Florence, where he studied Mannerist graphics and produced the Animal Piece (Tierstueck) pictures. (C25530, leg 1 C25407, C25408; synthesis C25490)

After returning from Florence he created the Heroes (Helden, also called Neue Typen / New Types) series between 1965 and 1966, including the large-format composition The Great Friends (Die grossen Freunde, Museum Ludwig, Cologne): solitary, ragged, broken figures with small heads on well-built bodies, standing alone in a barren landscape with naked arms and legs and hands opened in a summoning gesture, widely read as personifications of the battered German spirit after the war. Through early 1969 he produced further large-format pictures such as Woodsmen (Waldarbeiter) as part of the Fracture Pictures (Frakturbilder). (C25531, leg 1 C25409, C25410; synthesis C25490)

On the basis of his Fractures, Baselitz used Rayski's Wermsdorf Wood -- the painting from his childhood school hall -- as a model and painted his first picture with an inverted motif: The Wood on Its Head (Der Wald auf dem Kopf, 1969). It was followed by a series of upside-down portraits titled Friends (Freunde), depicting his wife Elke, friends and art-world figures. His own account of why: to overcome the representational, content-driven character of his earlier work and to stress the artifice of painting; "I must take everything which has been an object of painting -- landscape, the portrait, and the nude, for example -- and paint it upside-down. That is the way to liberate representation from content"; "painting is not a mirror of reality... one of the best ways to destroy this myth is by painting the image upside down." The early inverted works were based on Polaroid snapshots turned upside down, painted in a controlled, realistic style with a degree of naturalism that later disappeared. The first exhibition of works with inverted motifs was at the Galeriehaus in Cologne in 1970. In 2020 the Baselitz family donated six of the 1969 inverted paintings to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, including his first painting of Elke; the Met showed them in 2021 as Georg Baselitz: Pivotal Turn. The inversion then stayed with him for the rest of his career: he continued to paint and display his subjects upside down, and it became his defining feature. One dissenting summary -- the Minneapolis Institute of Art collection note says he "later abandoned this approach" -- contradicts every other source read and is not relied on here. (C25532, leg 1 C25411, C25412, C25413; synthesis C25490)

During the 1970s he regularly exhibited at Galerie Heiner Friedrich in Munich, took part in documenta 5 in Kassel in 1972, where his increasingly abstract upside-down paintings drew harsh criticism, and began using a fingerpainting technique that year. In 1971 the family moved to Forst an der Weinstrasse, using the old village school as a studio, and he began painting bird motifs; in 1975 the family moved to Derneburg, near Hildesheim (Schloss Derneburg, bought 1975, his home for more than 30 years). He began teaching at the Staatliche Akademie der Bildenden Kuenste in Karlsruhe in 1977 and was appointed professor there in 1978; from 1978 to 1980 he worked on diptychs in tempera, multipart pictures and large-format single works such as The Gleaner, Rubble Woman, Eagle and Boy Reading. In 1980 he showed a sculpture for the first time at the Venice Biennale, representing West Germany with Anselm Kiefer. (C25533, leg 1 C25414, C25415; synthesis C25490)

In 1981 his work was exhibited in New York for the first time (Xavier Fourcade) and included in A New Spirit in Painting at the Royal Academy, London; by 1982 he was devoting more time to sculpture, and in 1983 he began using Christian motifs and completed Dinner in Dresden (Nachtessen in Dresden). In 1986 he received the Goslarer Kaiserring; in 1989 he was named Chevalier dans l'Ordre des Arts et des Lettres. The first major exhibition of his work in East Germany was at the Nationalgalerie im Alten Museum, Berlin, in 1990. His first major retrospective in the United States was at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York, in 1995, touring to Los Angeles County Museum of Art, the Hirshhorn Museum and the Nationalgalerie, Berlin. In 2005 he began the Remix paintings, returning to key phases of his own art history and making new, reimagined versions of important earlier images, with a fluid, transparent paint quality, partly inspired by Edvard Munch, who also repeatedly repainted his own motifs. His work was shown at the Royal Academy, London, in late 2007, and a comprehensive survey ran November 2009 to March 2010 across the Museum Frieder Burda and the Staatliche Kunsthalle Baden-Baden. (C25534, leg 1 C25419, C25420, C25422; synthesis C25490)

From 2014 he worked on a series of quiet portraits of himself and Elke in dark washes of blue and black, described as a meditation on mortality and ageing, and the Avignon paintings from 2014 onward were singled out in Robert Isaf's obituary as the point from which his vision "reached its full potential". In 2019 a retrospective curated by Kosme de Baranano was held at the Gallerie dell'Accademia in Venice for the 58th Venice Biennale -- the first exhibition by a living artist in that museum -- and Devotion (Gagosian New York, 2019) showed paintings and works on paper inspired by self-portraits of artists he admired. From 2013 he and Elke lived together in Salzburg, Austria, and both obtained Austrian citizenship in 2015. His final years were physically frail: in a 2023 interview he said he worked every day for a very limited amount of time, using walkers, carts, walking canes, chairs, extended brushes and extended palette knives; the final White Cube show Back Again (2026) brought together recurring motifs -- eagles, hero figures, portraits of Elke -- painted from a wheeled chair with a brush attached to a pole. His final painting series, Eroi d'Oro (Heroes of Gold, 2025-26), monumental self-portraits and portraits of Elke against grounds of luminous gold, was due to open at the Fondazione Giorgio Cini, Venice, on May 6, 2026, six days after his death. His final sculpture, Stravinsky, was a giant, emaciated wooden doll inspired by puppets he designed for the Salzburg Marionette Theatre's 2025 production of Stravinsky's Soldier's Tale, cast in bronze by the Noack foundry. Georg Baselitz died on April 30, 2026, at the age of 88, peacefully, survived by Elke and his sons Daniel Blau and Anton Kern. No cause of death was published in any source read. (C25535, leg 1 C25422, C25423, C25426, C25427, C25428, C25429; synthesis C25490)

## M1 -- The 180-degree inversion: the picture reads as abstraction until it flips

The motif is painted and displayed upside down, first in Der Wald auf dem Kopf (W07, 1969) and then as a lifelong rule for figures, landscapes, portraits and still lifes (W08, W09, W18). Baselitz's own account: an upside-down object is "suitable for painting because it is unsuitable as an object"; inversion empties narrative and forces attention onto colour and form organisation. The intended effect, in Gagosian's words, is that inverted portraits and landscapes "seem at first glance to be complete abstractions"; White Cube adds that inversion "enabled him to emphasise the abstract qualities of his compositions while retaining the psychological charge of their figurative subject matter." The method in the early years was mechanical: Polaroid snapshots turned upside down and painted in a controlled, realistic style, so the inversion is a procedure applied to a photograph, not a free gesture. Works: W07, W08, W09, W18. (C25536, leg 2 M1, W07, W08, W09, W18; synthesis M1)

The watchable behaviour is recognition flickering on and off: a figure that reads as an abstract tangle of limbs and colour until the whole frame rotates 180 degrees, at which point a face and a body snap into recognition, then rotate back and dissolve again. The flip itself is the event. The danger for any imitator is the obvious one: an inversion that is only a trick is a screensaver; Baselitz's inversion works because the picture underneath is a real portrait, with a real sitter, a real dog, a real pair of figures seated side by side, and the flip is what makes the viewer look at the paint instead of the story. (C25537, leg 2 M1; synthesis M1)

**Takeaway:** the inversion must be a procedure applied to a real motif, not a filter; a picture with nothing underneath to recognise has nothing to flip. (C25538)

## M2 -- Fracture segmentation: the body arrives in slices

Subjects -- woodsmen, animals, forests, the painter himself -- cut into horizontal bands or irregular fragments, with the pieces offset, tilted or separated by gaps (W04, W05, W06; 1967-69). The Art Institute text on Woodman (W06) describes the figure as segmented into "horizontal bands or irregular fragments", and the same text says the work heralded the inverted figures that followed soon after: fracture is the mechanism that leads directly into inversion, the body broken apart before it is turned over. In Drei Streifen (W04, 1966), the painter figure is split by vertical stripes and bands. Works: W04, W05, W06. (C25539, leg 2 M2, W04, W05, W06; synthesis M2)

The watchable behaviour is a body that never fully coheres: it arrives as stacked slices that slide sideways out of alignment, then re-stack; a head detaches and drifts a few centimetres from its neck before snapping back. The seams stay visible, and the viewer keeps checking whether the pieces belong to one body or several. In motion the fracture is a rhythm, not a wound: slice, slide, re-stack, slice again. (C25540, leg 2 M2; synthesis M2)

**Takeaway:** the fragments must belong to one body that the viewer can still assemble; slices that never re-stack are confetti, not fracture. (C25541)

## M3 -- The monumental wounded antihero: one giant, too big for the frame

A single oversized figure filling a 162 x 130 cm or larger canvas, bloated, blocky, bleeding, limping, in tattered rags, set in a ruined landscape (W02, W03; Heroes 1965-66). Proportions are distorted, anatomy exaggerated, entrails sometimes visible through a near-transparent body. Rebel (W03, 1965, 162.7 x 130.2 cm, grade A) is the wounded, bleeding, limping antihero in a destroyed landscape; The Great Friends (W02, 1965) is the monumental grotesque pair. Baselitz identified himself with the Heroes and said they bore attributes of his own rural childhood; they have been widely read as personifications of the battered German spirit after the war, and as a metaphorical image of a man who throws the illusory, megalomaniacal ideals of the Third Reich and East Germany overboard. Works: W02, W03. (C25542, leg 2 M3, W02, W03; synthesis M3)

The watchable behaviour is a giant that sways and lurches across the frame, too big for it, limbs too long, torso heaving; each step lands heavy and the body lists to one side, never upright, never heroic in gait. The size is the point: the figure is monumental and broken at the same time, so the viewer reads power and damage in one body. (C25543, leg 2 M3; synthesis M3)

**Takeaway:** the hero must be wounded and monumental at once; a big figure that is whole is a statue, and a broken figure that is small is a casualty. (C25544)

## M4 -- Aggressive loaded brushwork, and the finger that replaces the brush

Fast, broad, heavily loaded strokes that stay visible as physical ridges of paint; bright, dissonant colour laid over dark or white ground in a single pass (W14 Adieu; W17 Remix; W20 late eagles). Calvocoressi calls Adieu's idiom "aggressive brushwork and bright colours"; the Remix works are "rapidly painted with swathes of bright, transparent hue" and "explosive, meandering lines" -- the 1962-63 Big Night re-painted from photographs, enlarged, in hours where the original took months. From 1972 Baselitz also painted with his fingers instead of a brush, a technique he called a "nonstyle" (W10, W11): the Stedelijk text on Fingermalerei-Akt says "the substance of the paint, manipulated by the artist's fingers instead of a brush, is emphasized." The two devices share one refusal: the hand in the paint, not the brush as a polite intermediary. In the final years the same refusal returned as a brush attached to a pole, worked from a wheeled chair, leaving visible tracks across canvases placed on the floor. Works: W14, W17, W20, W10, W11. (C25545, leg 2 M4, M5, W14, W17, W20, W10, W11; synthesis M4, M5)

The watchable behaviour is paint arriving in real time: strokes appearing as sweeping arm-wide arcs, paint dragging and skipping over the surface, leaving dry-brush trails; colour fields colliding wet-on-wet and bleeding at the edges; and where the finger takes over, fingertip smears and dragged ridges instead of brush marks, an eagle's wing built from five-finger rakes, the paint furrowed in parallel fingertip grooves that widen where the hand presses harder. (C25546, leg 2 M4, M5; synthesis M4, M5)

**Takeaway:** the mark must be the hand's, not the tool's; a smooth surface with a story about aggression is illustration, not this mechanism. (C25547)

## M5 -- Chainsaw and axe carving in painted wood: the tree stays a tree

Sculptures hacked from limewood blocks with chainsaws and axes, knots, cracks and chopping marks retained, then partly painted in primary colours (W13, W15). Baselitz began making large-scale wooden sculptures in 1979; his first sculpture, Model for a Sculpture (Modell fuer eine Skulptur, 1979-80), was exhibited in the West German Pavilion at the Venice Biennale in 1980, where the raised right arm of the prostrate male figure was read by some as a Nazi salute -- an interpretation Baselitz disavowed, saying the gesture referenced carvings by the West African Lobi people, for whom it symbolised surrender. Gagosian (2004) records more than fifty wood sculptures made since 1980, each hewn from a single tree trunk with axe and chainsaw, with paint suggesting clothing or facial features, wavering between figuration and abstraction; the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris notes the heads retain knots, cracks and crevices from chopping and hacking, and the sculptures conjure both trees and the tribal totems Baselitz collected. His own rule: "I want to avoid all manual dexterity, all artistic elegance." Works: W13, W15. (C25548, leg 2 M6, W13, W15; synthesis M6)

The watchable behaviour is a block that is struck and split, chips flying, a limb emerging from the block with each blow; the surface stays faceted and scarred, paint catching only on the high ridges of the cuts. The sculpture never forgets it was a tree: the trunk's grain, knots and cracks are the figure's anatomy, and the paint is a highlight laid over the wound, not a skin. (C25549, leg 2 M6; synthesis M6)

**Takeaway:** the carving must keep the tree visible in the figure; a smooth painted statue is a different art, not this one. (C25550)

## M6 -- Woodcut: black line block plus colour blocks, misregistered on purpose

Large woodcuts built from a black line block with differently shaded colour blocks, in the manner of 16th-century chiaroscuro woodcuts (Grosser Kopf, 1960s, Christie's; Eagle portfolio 1974-75, MoMA; Head and Bottle 1982, 1000 x 485 mm). The cut is bold, the line massive, the colour flat and offset. Printmaking was intrinsic to his practice from the start, not a sideline: his first etchings were made at Schloss Wolfsburg in spring 1964, he worked in etching, woodcut and linocut, plus large-format linocuts from 1977, and the first retrospective of his prints was at Leverkusen in 1974. One linocut, Woman at the Window (Frau am Fenster), state I, 1979, was reworked in stages until the figural composition became almost completely abstract -- the print as a machine for draining the figure. Works: Grosser Kopf, Eagle portfolio, Head and Bottle. (C25551, leg 2 M7; synthesis M7)

The watchable behaviour is a head that prints itself in layers: first the black key-block slams down, then each colour block lands slightly misregistered, so the image shudders between aligned and offset states. The misregistration is the event, not the error: the picture is never quite in register, and the eye keeps correcting it. (C25552, leg 2 M7; synthesis M7)

**Takeaway:** the layers must misregister visibly and on purpose; a cleanly registered print is a reproduction, not this mechanism. (C25553)

## M7 -- The repeated motif and the ground that flips: one idea, sixteen canvases

The same composition painted again and again with small variations: 18 shouting women in Strassenbild (W12), 16 identical seated pairs in Mrs Lenin and the Nightingale (W18), five titmice in one canvas (W16), the Remix re-paintings of Big Night (W17). The variation is the content; the repetition is the structure. Mrs Lenin (W18, 2008, sixteen paintings, 300 x 250 cm each, grade A) makes the mechanism explicit: the same composition repeated 16 times -- two upside-down male figures seated side by side (Lenin as "Mrs Lenin" in skirt and heels; Stalin as "the nightingale"), after Otto Dix, The Artist's Parents II (1924) -- eight in white and grey on a black ground, eight polychromatic on a white ground. The ground polarity is the second half of the same device: the same motif set alternately on a black ground (white and grey paint) and on a white ground (polychromatic paint), so the figure reads alternately as light cut out of dark and dark cut out of light. Works: W12, W16, W17, W18. (C25554, leg 2 M8, M9, W12, W16, W17, W18; synthesis M8, M9)

The watchable behaviour is comparison as the experience: a grid or row of near-identical figures in which one element changes per frame -- ground flips black to white, a figure's colour inverts, a head turns -- so the eye scans across the series like frames of a film, and the figure strobes between positive and negative while holding its pose. A single Mrs Lenin canvas is a joke about two dictators; sixteen of them, half on black and half on white, is a machine for watching the same joke change its charge. (C25555, leg 2 M8, M9; synthesis M8, M9)

**Takeaway:** the copies must differ enough to compare and agree enough to be the same idea; identical prints are an edition, and unrelated pictures are a group show. (C25556)

## M8 -- The Remix: repaint your own past, faster and brighter

From 2005 Baselitz returned to key phases of his own art history and made new, reimagined versions of important earlier images, with a fluid, transparent paint quality, partly inspired by Edvard Munch, who also repeatedly repainted his own motifs. Grosse Nacht (Remix) IV (W17, 2008, 250 x 200 cm, grade A) re-paints the 1962-63 Big Night from photographs, enlarged, rapidly painted in bright transparent hues on a white ground, with a skull added; the original took months, the Remix took hours. The mechanism is self-appropriation as a system: the artist's own early work becomes the canonical image to restage, the way Kippenberger restaged Picasso and Gericault -- except here the source is himself, thirty or forty years younger. Works: W17, and the Remix series from 2005 onward. (C25557, leg 2 M8, W17; synthesis M8)

The watchable behaviour is a painting repainting itself: the old composition arrives in ghostly white, the new paint floods over it in transparent swathes, a skull appears where none was, and the picture that took months is finished in hours before the viewer's eyes. The speed is legible in the surface -- the paint has not had time to settle, and that unsettledness is the point. (C25558, leg 2 M8, W17; synthesis M8)

**Takeaway:** the remix must change the picture's charge, not just its palette; a recolour is a reprint, not a remix. (C25559)

## M9 -- Attached objects and transfer: stockings, fabric, spatula, collage eyes

Late practice (2020-24) glues real pairs of women's nylon stockings and fabric onto the canvas and paints over and around them (W19); fabric transfer of Elke's figure; eagles painted with spatulas rather than brushes, some with cut-out plastic circles glued on as eyes (W20, Ropac "adler barfuss" text). Spaziergang am Meer (A Walk by the Sea, W19, 2021, 300 x 400 cm, grade A) is three figures made of three pairs of black stockings hovering above pillow-like squares of off-white fabric -- the first collage in his practice, stockings glued on, painted over and around. The 2024 Sigmund eagle painting (W20, 300 x 450 cm, grade B) shows monumental paired birds in flight, wings spread almost three metres wide, rendered in gestural strokes with spatulas, not brushes, some with cut-out plastic circles as eyes. The mechanism is the object refusing to become paint: the stocking stays a stocking, the plastic disc stays a disc, and the painting has to work around them. Works: W19, W20. (C25560, leg 2 M10, W19, W20; synthesis M10)

The watchable behaviour is a pair of stocking-legs dangling and swinging from the top of the frame while paint splatters up from below like a skirt; a spatula scraping a wing into being in one flat pass; two plastic-disc eyes snapping onto a bird's head and staring, unblinking, as the body churns beneath them. The attached thing moves like a thing, not like paint, and the picture is the negotiation between the two. (C25561, leg 2 M10; synthesis M10)

**Takeaway:** the attached object must stay visibly itself; an object fully absorbed into paint is a texture, not this mechanism. (C25562)

## M10 -- The eagle, the dog, the tree: a motif that outlives its meaning

Across sixty years Baselitz returns to the same small set of motifs -- Rayski's forest, heroes and new types, eagles, dogs, trees, Elke, self-portraits, Munch echoes, rubble women and Dresden -- and paints them again in every technique he owns. The eagle runs from the 1974 portfolio through Fingermalerei-Adler (W10, 1971/72, 250 x 180 cm, grade A), an enormous eagle with wings outstretched painted upside down with the fingers, to the 2024 Sigmund eagle series, monumental paired birds rendered larger than life. The dog appears in the 1969 inverted portraits (a tan dog unreadable when inverted, in Working Man from Dresden, W09) and in the final White Cube show Back Again (2026). Elke is the most constant motif of all: the first painting of her in 1969 (Portrait of Elke I, W08, 162 x 130 cm, grade A), the Friends portraits, the 2014 dark-wash portraits, the final gold-ground naked portraits of Elke in Eroi d'Oro. The motif is not a subject that gets used up; it is a test piece the artist returns to with a new tool each time, so the career reads as one motif tested against sixty years of technique. Works: W08, W09, W10, W19, W20. (C25563, leg 2 M10, W08, W09, W10, W19, W20; synthesis M10)

The watchable behaviour is a motif that keeps coming back changed: the eagle arrives as a woodcut, then as a finger painting, then as a spatula painting with plastic eyes; Elke arrives as a controlled 1969 portrait, then as a dark wash, then as a gold-ground nude. The viewer recognises the motif first and the technique second, and the gap between the two is the picture's history. (C25564, leg 2 M10; synthesis M10)

**Takeaway:** the motif must return often enough to be recognised and change enough to be worth returning to; a motif painted once is a subject, not a system. (C25565)

## The aesthetic logic: the picture is the procedure, the motif is the test piece

Baselitz's whole career is one procedure performed sixty ways: take a recognisable motif -- portrait, nude, landscape, eagle, tree, hero figure, dog, Elke -- and put it through a device that drains its content, so that colour and brushwork have to carry the picture. The inversion drains narrative. The fracture drains wholeness. The finger drains the brush. The chainsaw drains elegance. The woodcut drains registration. The series drains uniqueness. The stocking drains paint itself. In each case the artist sets up a procedure -- a flip, a cut, a repetition, a glued object -- and then lets the procedure carry what his hand alone could not. That is why his work is the opposite pole of Kippenberger's even though the two men are now tied by an upside-down body: Kippenberger sets up a persona and performs what it demands, Baselitz sets up a procedure and obeys what it returns. (C25566, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25490, C25501-C25510)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the motif is the constant and the device is the variable: the eagle, the hero, Elke return in every decade, and what changes is the procedure -- inverted, fractured, finger-painted, chainsawed, printed, repeated, glued -- which is why the career reads as one test piece run through sixty years of tools. Second, the picture refuses both poles at once: the refusal of Socialist Realism and of Western abstraction is the same refusal, and the inversion is the device that lets him embrace elements of both -- figuration that reads as abstraction at first glance, abstraction that turns out to be a portrait on a second look. Third, the material is the argument: thick impasto, crude and deliberately unrefined rendering, heightened palette, fingerpainting, dark blue-black washes in the late portraits, gold ground in the final series, wood gouged with axe and chainsaw -- the paint and the wood do the thinking the motif is no longer allowed to do. (C25567, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25490)

The evolution is the proof. 1962-64: the scandal paintings (W01), the obscenity seizure, the first etchings -- a young painter fighting the state with a phallus and a manifesto. 1965-69: the Heroes (W02, W03) and the Fractures (W04, W05, W06), the body broken apart, and then the turn: Der Wald auf dem Kopf (W07, 1969), the first inversion, after Rayski's forest from the school hall. 1969-72: the inverted Friends portraits (W08, W09), the finger-painted eagle and nude (W10, W11) -- the procedure set, the motif fixed. 1979-84: the street picture in eighteen parts (W12), the first sculpture (W13), the raised arm (W15), Adieu (W14) -- the picture leaves the canvas and becomes an object. 1988-2008: five titmice (W16), sixteen Mrs Lenins (W18), the Remix (W17) -- the series as a machine, the artist repainting himself. 2021-26: stockings and fabric (W19), spatula eagles with plastic eyes (W20), gold-ground portraits of Elke, the final Stravinsky doll -- the procedure pushed to its most literal: the object glued on, the eye snapped on, the portrait painted from a wheeled chair with a brush on a pole. The late work does not soften the procedure; it strips it to its bones, and the answer in both cases is the same -- turn the picture over, and paint what is left. (C25568, leg 2 evolution; leg 1 C25407-C25427; synthesis C25490)
