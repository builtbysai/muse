## The life: the girl who grew up inside a collection and learned to count what other people throw away

Jacqueline "Jac" Leirner was born in Sao Paulo in 1961 -- the day and month, 4 July, appear only in the German and French Wikipedias, both drawing on the same Wikidata entry, and no gallery, museum or interview source read gives a day or month, so the day and month are single-source and provisional (see Corrections 1) -- and lives and works in Sao Paulo. She is a sculptor first and a painter only in the loosest sense: her pictures are made of things, counted and ordered, and her programme has barely changed in forty years -- collect one identical everyday object for years, sort it, then re-order it by a strict formal rule, line, grid, cord, stack, so that repetition, not depiction, makes the form. (C14908, leg 1 C14701, C14702; leg 2 C14774)

She grew up inside a collection. Her parents, Fulvia (nee Bornstein) Leirner, a practicing psychologist, and Adolfo Leirner (also spelled Adolpho in several sources), a textile engineer born in Sao Paulo in 1935 to Polish Jewish immigrants who arrived in Brazil in the 1930s, were prominent collectors of postwar Brazilian abstraction; Adolfo went to England in 1953 to study textile engineering and design, bought the first work of his Brazilian Constructive collection in 1961 -- the year Jac was born -- and the collection of nearly 100 objects was acquired by the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston between 2005 and 2007. Together her parents built a Brazilian Art Deco collection of 47 pieces donated to MAC USP in 2020. The wider family map -- sculptor Felicia Leirner as Adolfo's aunt, Isai Leirner as his uncle and creator of the Leirner Award, installation artist Jeanete Musatti as Adolfo's sister, video artist Betty Leirner as Jac's sister -- rests on a single Newcity profile of Adolfo and is reported as one source's map, not as multiply attested fact. (C14909, leg 1 C14703-C14705, C14707)

One correction before anything else, because it is printed wrong in a place people copy from: Jac Leirner is NOT the daughter of the artist Nelson Leirner. The Portuguese Wikipedia opens "Filha do renomado artista Nelson Leirner" and its Nelson Leirner article repeats the claim; every other source read says her parents are the collectors Adolfo and Fulvia, MoMA's post calls Nelson "the artist's uncle", and Newcity calls Nelson Adolfo's first cousin. Do not print the Portuguese Wikipedia filiation. (C14910, leg 1 C14706)

In 1979 she enrolled in the visual arts programme of the Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado (FAAP) in Sao Paulo and graduated in 1984 -- corroborated on all sides (Itau mirror, both Wikipedias, AWARE, MoMA, gallery profiles), exactly as Catunda's 1984 graduation was in #671, and printable without caveat. She taught at FAAP between 1987 and 1989. What no source read gives, and what must not be printed, is a Catunda-style teacher list for her: MoMA's post states that "in the 1970s and early 1980s, Nelson Leirner (the artist's uncle), Julio Plaza, Regina Silveira, and Walter Zanini reformed FAAP's curriculum" and that "her professors were renowned artists, educators, and thinkers" -- but it does not list the four reformers one by one as her individual teachers. The safe wording is that she studied at FAAP while those four were reforming its curriculum. MoMA's post does name her classmates, "including Leda Catunda and Jose Leonilson", who "would go on to be among the most visible artists of their generation" -- the direct FAAP link to #671 and #669. In the 1980s she also played bass in the punk band U.K.C.T.; her own account, via interview, frames the punk years as feeding her attention to repeating cultural symbols. (C14911, leg 1 C14708-C14712)

Her work evolved from drawing and painting around 1981. Her first exhibition took place in Sao Paulo in 1982, and her first solo, "Objetos Brancos e Imagens Objetuais" (White Objects and Object Images), was at Galeria Tenda, Sao Paulo, in 1982; her earliest listed group show is "Xerox -- Projeto Intersemiotico" at FAAP, 1980. The title of that first solo already names the programme: object-images. She took part in the 17th Sao Paulo International Biennial in 1983 -- her first Biennial, not 1989, whatever the Newcity Adolfo profile and the French Wikipedia say (see Corrections 3). (C14912, leg 1 C14713-C14715)

From the mid-1980s the accumulation programme arrives in two linked series. Os Cem (The Hundreds), 1985-1987, collects thousands of devalued 100-cruzeiro banknotes from 1985, pierced and strung into lines, strips, wheels and floor spreads; Pulmao (Lung), 1987, is made from 1,200 Marlboro soft packs she smoked over three years, each taken apart -- pull strips, price tags, laminated paper, cellophane, tax stamps -- and each part becoming a distinct sculpture by knotting, sewing, gluing, folding, puncturing, overlapping and stacking. The banknote work is explicitly about hyperinflation: the notes lost purchasing power within weeks, making visible the fragility of the monetary system and the distance between inscribed value and effective value. Solos followed at once: Os Cem at Petite Galerie, Rio de Janeiro, 1987; Pulmao at Galeria Millan, Sao Paulo, 1987. (C14913, leg 1 C14716; leg 2 C14752-C14757, C14774)

In 1989 she showed "Nomes" (Names) at Galeria Millan and the museum-bags room "Names" at the 20th Sao Paulo Biennial, where the English curator Guy Brett saw her work -- Brett had seen the money wheel at her parents' home and took works to a group show in England in 1989. In 1990 she took part in Aperto 90 at the Venice Biennale, and Gill Hedley of the British Council then brought her to Oxford for six months as a fellow at University College and artist in residence at the Museum of Modern Art, Oxford, with tutorials at the Ruskin School. In 1991 she was artist in residence at the Walker Art Center, Minneapolis, and at MoMA Oxford, with three institutional solos that year -- "Currents" at the Institute of Contemporary Art, Boston; "Viewpoints" at the Walker; and a solo at MoMA Oxford -- and MoMA acquired two Pulmao works. In 1992 she took part in Documenta IX, Kassel, and showed "Directions" at the Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden, Washington D.C. In 1994 she was one of seven artists in "Sense and Sensibility: Women Artists and Minimalism in the Nineties" at MoMA, New York, 16 June-11 September, organised by Lynn Zelevansky. (C14914, leg 1 C14718-C14723)

In 1997 she represented Brazil at the 47th Venice Biennale, with "Nice to Meet You" at Galerie Lelong, New York, and "Foi um prazer" at Galeria Camargo Vilaca, Sao Paulo, the same year. In 1998 she was a visiting artist at the Rijksakademie van beeldende Kunsten, Amsterdam. In 2001 she received a grant from the John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation. She showed at the 7th Havana Biennial in 2000 -- not the "9th in 2005" of the Esther Schipper profile, which is wrong on both number and year (see Corrections 5) -- and at the 5th Mercosur Biennial, Porto Alegre, 2005. The 22nd Sao Paulo Biennial in 1994 is claimed by the Esther Schipper profile and by Museum Ludwig but absent from the Itau-mirror exhibition list: probable but unsettled, and not printed here as fact (see Corrections 4). (C14915, leg 1 C14724-C14727, C14729, C14742)

The later career is a long institutional consolidation with the material turning, in phases, from paper and plastic to hardware and light. "Ad Infinitum" at CCBB, Rio de Janeiro and Sao Paulo, 2002; "Adhesive 44" at the Miami Art Museum, 2004; "Projeto Parede" at MAM/SP, 1999. In 2011 she had a major retrospective at the Estacao Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo, which won the APCA prize for best exhibition of the year, awarded 2012. In 2012 she was artist in residence at the Yale University School of Art, where she developed the "Hardware Silk" works -- precision levels, steel cables, hardware, cigarette rolling papers -- shown at Yale in 2012 and at White Cube, London, in 2013. Solos followed at the Museo Tamayo, Mexico City, and CAAM, Las Palmas, both 2014; "metrica minima" at Galpao Fortes Vilaca, Sao Paulo, 2015-2016, after the Sudoku series debuted at the Sharjah Biennial in 2015; "Junkie" at White Cube, London, 2016, prints of sculptures carved from cocaine crystals during four binges in 2010; "Add It Up" at the Fruitmarket Gallery, Edinburgh, and "Institutional Ghost" at IMMA, Dublin, both 2017. (C14916, leg 1 C14728-C14734)

In 2019 the Gesellschaft fur Moderne Kunst am Museum Ludwig recognised her with the Wolfgang Hahn Prize -- the first time a South American artist received it -- with an exhibition at Museum Ludwig, Cologne, and acquisition of "Museum Bags" (1985/2018) for the collection. (The German Wikipedia dates the ceremony to 9 April 2019; single-source, flagged.) In 2022 she showed "Us Horizon" at Esther Schipper, Berlin. In 2023 the Swiss Institute, New York, gave her a solo, 10 May-27 August, described by the institute as her first major institutional solo exhibition in New York -- the qualifier does the work, since she had New York gallery solos earlier (Galerie Lelong 1994 and 1997, Bohen Foundation 1998). Her work is held by, among others, MoMA, New York; Tate, London; the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum; the Hirshhorn; MAM Sao Paulo; the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo; the Walker Art Center; the Art Gallery of Ontario; the Blanton Museum of Art; MOCA Los Angeles; and the Carnegie Museum of Art. (C14917, leg 1 C14735-C14738, C14746)

Her gallery history in Brazil runs Millan (solos 1987, 1989) to Camargo Vilaca (solos 1993, 1997, 2000), the gallery that became Fortes Vilaca and then Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel, which represents her in Brazil -- stated verbatim on the gallery's own exhibition page. Abroad: Galerie Lelong, New York, her first gallery in the US, joined in 1991 (solos 1994, 1997); White Cube, London ("Junkie", 2016 -- the French Wikipedia infobox lists her as "Representee par White Cube", which alone does not establish current representation); Esther Schipper, Berlin ("Us Horizon", 2022). Luisa Strina is NOT a gallerist link: one group show in 2008-09 only. Nara Roesler hosts her portfolio but no source states representation -- do not print it flat. On degrees: the gallery profiles word her 1984 FAAP qualification as a "BFA in Fine Arts"; Arte Al Dia's "obtained her MFA" is unsupported by every other source and inconsistent with a 1984 first degree -- treat it as an error. No source read mentions any postgraduate degree for Leirner; do not assert one. (C14918, leg 1 C14737, C14741, C14748; leg 3 C14817-C14819)

In her own words, via the 2016 Newcity interview, she says "Nice to Meet You" was ready "by 1987 for the Brazilian pavilion of the Venice Biennale". No exhibition list read shows a 1987 Venice appearance -- Venice years are even, and her Venice showings are 1990 and 1997. Treat it as a likely misremembered year in interview; do not print it as fact. (C14919, leg 1 C14743, Contradictions 9)

Evolution: 1982-1987, early systems to accumulation. Printmaking and watercolour systems of tonal control and ordering give way to the fixed driver -- collect one identical everyday object for years, sort it, re-order it by a strict formal rule (line, grid, cord, stack), so repetition, not depiction, makes the form; Os Cem (banknotes, hyperinflation making visible the gap between inscribed and effective value) and Pulmao (1,200 Marlboro packs smoked over three years, disassembled into every part) are the twin founding series. 1989-1997, circulation systems: the material shifts from things consumed by her own body to things circulating through institutions -- museum bags (Nomes), museum correspondence (To and From), airline loot (Corpus Delicti), business cards (Nice to Meet You) -- same operation, wider subject: the art world's own mail, travel and contacts. A side move in this phase is Furos (1989): instead of accumulating objects, she perforates the newspaper that reports the news, turning the medium of circulation itself into the content. 1997-2013, adhesive, tape and light: flatness returns as grid and line -- Hip Hop tape lines across walls, doors, corners and windows; Adesivos stickers on glass plates, with a construction level already embedded in Adesivo 18 (2001); Little Light kilometres of copper wire as spatial drawing, the artist's only piece with a metaphorical resonance, about journey and illumination; Skin rolling papers gummed in grids. 2012-2023, hardware, smoking residue, final bag format: Hardware Silk (ropes, clips, chains, levels, rulers), High 'n' Low and The End (cigarette butts on taut cables), Leveled Spirit (38 levels), closing with Us Horizon (1985-2022, the bag series in its "new and final format") and July 4th (2023, the pile form applied to a founding document). MoMA's post names five series together as her best-known work -- Os cem (1986-87), Pulmao (1987), Nomes (1989), Corpus Delicti (1992-93) and Nice to Meet You (1997) -- and that grouping is the spine of the arc. (C14921, leg 2 C14774-C14777)


## M1 -- Serial accumulation: one identical object, collected for years, re-ordered by a strict rule

The programme itself, stated by AWARE: her work "has relied on the process of accumulating everyday objects. These objects are amassed for long periods of time then organised and assembled into varied spatial configurations." The object is always identical and always mass-produced -- thousands of 100-cruzeiro notes pierced and strung (W02, W03), 1,200 identical Marlboro packs disassembled and sorted by part (W04, W05), business cards sorted by typography, colour, size and graphic design (W12), 200-plus shopping bags sorted by shape, colour and font (W19). The rule is strict and formal -- line, grid, cord, stack -- and the form emerges from repetition alone. Nothing is depicted; the count is the picture. Contrast with #671 M5, which shares the word "accumulation": Catunda accumulates by layering, hiding and sewing printed cloth, so the image is half-buried; Leirner accumulates by sorting and counting identical units, so every unit stays legible. The guard is the phrasing: serial ordering of identical units, never layered concealment. (C14922, leg 2 C14752-C14757, C14765, C14772; leg 3 C14804, C14827)

**Takeaway:** an accumulation earns its count when every unit stays legible. If the units only pile up, it is a heap; if thousands of identical objects are sorted by one strict rule until the line or grid is the form, the counting is the composition and the repetition is the image.

## M2 -- Money as line: devalued banknotes pierced and threaded

The banknote is emptied of value and kept as material. Os Cem pierces 100-cruzeiro bills and strings them into long strips, wheels and floor spreads (W02, W03); Little Blue Phase threads thousands of 100-cruzado notes on a cord (W09). The notes lost purchasing power within weeks -- hyperinflation -- so the work makes visible the fragility of the monetary system: money turned into a line that no longer buys anything, swaying on its thread. The series dating carries a small variance (1985-1987 per AWARE and the portfolio, 1986-87 per MoMA, 1987 per Escritorio/Itau); individual works use their own object-record dates. (C14923, leg 2 C14752-C14754, C14761, C14774)

**Takeaway:** money earns its line when the value is gone and the paper remains. If the bills only hang, it is a curtain of cash; if each note is pierced, threaded and stretched until the currency is a thread, the line is the argument and the devaluation is the subject.

## M3 -- The pack taken apart: one consumed object disassembled into all its parts

Pulmao (W04, W05): 1,200 Marlboro soft packs smoked over three years, each taken apart -- pull strips, price tags, laminated paper, cellophane, tax stamps -- and each part becoming a distinct sculpture by knotting, sewing, gluing, folding, puncturing, overlapping and stacking. MoMA's post gives the canonical record: eight sculptures and a multiple from the disassembled parts, the flattened perforated packs suspended on a steel cord 440-480 cm wide against the small plexiglass box multiple holding ten cellophane wrappers still flecked with tobacco. The heavy cord and the weightless box are the same habit, weighed two ways. (C14924, leg 2 C14755-C14757)

**Takeaway:** a habit earns its anatomy when every part is kept. If the packs only pile up, it is litter; if each pack is taken apart and every part -- strip, stamp, cellophane -- becomes its own sculpture, the disassembly is the drawing and the smoking is the clock.

## M4 -- Institutional envelopes sorted by format, material and colour

To and From (W08): envelopes from museum correspondence, kept and lent by the institutions, supported by steel cables and arranged linearly by format, material and colour, foregrounding stamps, folds and trajectories. The series began in 1991 during her residency at MoMA Oxford at the invitation of the British Council, made from envelopes used in correspondence between the museum and other institutions; AWARE corroborates that during the 1991 residencies at MoMA Oxford and the Walker she collected the museums' correspondence, organised it by various criteria, and assembled it into pieces such as To and From. The mail system of the art world, made visible as a line. (C14925, leg 2 C14760)

**Takeaway:** mail earns its line when the envelope is the evidence. If the envelopes only hang, it is a washing line; if each one is sorted by format, material and colour until the stamps and folds read as a route map, the correspondence is the institution and the line is its diary.

## M5 -- Airline loot as evidence: stolen and negotiated objects re-ordered

Corpus Delicti (W10, W11): ashtrays, boarding passes, tickets, cutlery, blankets and cups appropriated from air travel, reorganised in multiple configurations; the title is the legal term for material evidence of a crime, with the ironic dimension of small thefts negotiated with flight attendants. The 1992 floor piece, shown at Documenta IX, layers bubble wrap, glass planes, chained ashtrays and boarding passes under glass; the 1993 piece is polyurethane cutlery and rope, 317.8 cm long -- a distinct configuration, not a duplicate. Dating carries a variance (1985/1993 per Escritorio, 1992-93 per MoMA); the two dated instances in the 20 use their own object-record dates. (C14926, leg 2 C14763, C14764)

**Takeaway:** loot earns its evidence when the theft is the label. If the ashtrays only sit in a row, it is a souvenir shelf; if the boarding passes lie under glass and the ashtrays are chained, the journey is the exhibit and the small crime is the catalogue.

## M6 -- Adhesive as drawing: tape and stickers as line and grid

Hip Hop (W13), begun 1997, installation 2001: adhesive tapes of different widths, origins and tonalities laid in long horizontal lines across walls, doors, corners and windows -- a continuous field of colour and rhythm, related by critics to Mondrian's Broadway Boogie Woogie, emphasising cadence and rhythm from variations in tape colour and width and a "fortuitous, circumstantial order" from differences in width, texture and tonality. Adesivos (W14): stickers collected over decades from museums, galleries, airports, magazines and packaging, organised by chromatic, graphic and typological criteria and applied to translucent glass plates as linear and modular constructions -- and Adesivo 18 already embeds a construction level in the work, hardware entering the sticker phase. The room itself becomes the drawing surface. (C14927, leg 2 C14766, C14767)

**Takeaway:** tape earns its line when it turns the corner. If the tape only stripes a wall, it is decoration; if the lines cross doors and windows and keep going, the room is the paper and the tape is the pencil.

## M7 -- Hardware as colour system: levels, rulers and cables as measuring drawing

Leveled Spirit (W17): 38 spirit levels of varied lengths in a rainbow corner array. High 'n' Low (W18): steel cables, tensioners and precision levels spanning a gallery, carrying lines of pot cigarette butts -- "the minimum degree of matter, synthesized to its tinier element", per the FDAG text; wire ropes support the butts and the sculpture "is defined by lines that tighten the space". Little Light 5 (W15): kilometres of copper wire running along walls to connect small bulbs, turning energy conduction into spatial drawing. Skin (W16): 680 translucent rolling papers gummed in a grid that flutters as the viewer walks past. Measuring tools do the work of colour and line; taut cables divide space. (C14928, leg 2 C14768-C14771)

**Takeaway:** hardware earns its colour when the tool stops measuring and starts drawing. If the levels only hang, it is a toolbox; if 38 levels make a rainbow and taut cables carry lines of butts, the measurement is the composition and the tension is the line.

## M8 -- Bags as cloth: plastic shopping bags sewn into fabric

Nomes (W07 Azuis, W19 Us Horizon): hundreds of museum and bookshop bags sewn onto interfacing to form large surfaces covering rooms and walls -- bags "as if forming a fabric". Azuis (1990) is a blue plastic-bag surface 150 x 420 cm. Us Horizon (1985-2022) gives the series its "new and final format": a double row of over 200 vintage bags at eye level across three walls, top row right side up, lower row upside down, organised by shape, colour and fonts, collecting begun in 1985. A wall of carrier bags that reads as cloth, each logo a small printed memory. Guard: the fabric image is real but the operation is joining bags, not sewing printed cloth -- it is not Catunda's M4. (C14929, leg 2 C14759, C14772; leg 3 C14827)

**Takeaway:** a bag earns its cloth when the logo becomes the weave. If the bags only hang, it is a shop window; if hundreds are sewn into a surface that covers a wall, the carrier is the textile and the printed brand is the pattern.


## The aesthetic logic: counting, sorting, and the object that stays legible

Leirner's programme has three terms, and they never change, only change material. First, counting: the work begins with a number -- 1,200 packs, thousands of notes, 680 rolling papers, 38 levels, 200 bags -- and the number is the first thing the viewer feels. The object is collected for years before it is touched, and the collecting is the work; the studio is a warehouse of identical things waiting for their rule. Second, sorting: the rule is always formal and always strict -- pierce and thread, sort by typography and colour, arrange by format and material, span on a cable -- and the rule is visible in the finished work, not hidden behind it. Third, legibility: the unit is never destroyed, never painted over, never buried. A banknote is pierced but still a banknote; a cigarette pack is flattened but still a Marlboro; a bag is sewn but still a museum bag. The object keeps its name, its logo, its stamp, its fold -- and that is the point. The work is not about the thing, it is about what the thing has been through: money that lost its value, a pack that was smoked, a letter that travelled, a flight that was taken, a card that was handed over. The material is evidence, and the sorting is the verdict.

That is why the same operation survives forty years of changing materials, from banknotes to envelopes to tape to spirit levels, without ever changing its question: what does an object become when you keep it, count it, and put it in a line? The answer, at the end, is a horizon: a double row of 200 vintage shopping bags at eye level, top row up, bottom row down, the whole history of carrying things hung as a cloth -- the bag is the fabric, and the count is the drawing. (C14930)
