## The life: the dark ground, then the light inside it

Ross Bleckner was born May 12, 1949, in Brooklyn, New York, grew up Jewish, and in 1961 his family moved to Hewlett Harbor, Long Island, where he attended George W. Hewlett High School. In 1965 he saw his first art exhibition, The Responsive Eye (Op art) at the Museum of Modern Art; it had a huge impact on him and was the moment he decided to become an artist. He was preoccupied with death from childhood -- an aunt's lingering death from pancreatic cancer, and an early ambition to be the New York Times obituary writer. He studied at New York University, where he studied alongside Sol LeWitt and Chuck Close, worked in an art supply store and drove a taxi during college, and received his B.A. in 1971. The B.A. (not B.F.A.) is confirmed by five dealer/gallery sources against Wikipedia alone; use B.A. 1971. (C24308, leg 1 C24100-C24102, C24126; synthesis C24255-C24256)

The CalArts point carries the same correction #702 recorded for Fischl. Bleckner earned his M.F.A. at the California Institute of the Arts, Valencia, in 1973. He was in CalArts' painting division under Allan Hacklin and Paul Brach. He was NOT a Baldessari student: Baldessari himself, in Hertz, is explicit: "I never worked with Ross Bleckner nor with Eric Fischl, who were both painting with Allan Hacklin and Paul Brach." At CalArts he met David Salle and became friends with him; he also met Eric Fischl there. Painting was officially dead at CalArts in those years -- the school's prestige lay in conceptual art, photography, video, and installation -- and Bleckner, Fischl, and Salle were the painters who pushed back. Fischl, asked in 2016 what united the three, answered: "We were painters." Bleckner is named in Hertz's foreword sentence listing the 1970-74 CalArts artists who went to New York; he is NOT in Hertz's "nucleus of five" -- never upgrade him into it. He is also in the CalArts blog alumni list for "Where Art Might Happen: The Early Years of CalArts" (Kestner Gesellschaft, Aug. 30-Nov. 10, 2019; Kunsthaus Graz, Mar. 13-Jun. 7, 2020) and in that show itself. (C24309, leg 1 C24103-C24104; leg 3 C24209, C24212-C24213; synthesis C24250)

Bleckner moved back to New York in 1974 and moved into a Tribeca loft building on White Street, which his parents helped him buy that year. Painter Julian Schnabel rented three floors; from 1977 to 1983 the Mudd Club, a nightclub frequented by musicians and artists, occupied space in the same building. Bleckner sold the building in 2004. He first shared a loft with filmmaker Lizzie Borden on arrival in 1973. His first solo exhibition was in 1975 at Cunningham Ward Gallery, New York. In 1979 he began his long association with Mary Boone Gallery, New York; his first Boone solo show was in 1983. Vanity Fair (Feb. 1990) calls him "the third member of Mary Boone's original gang (after Schnabel and Salle)." Bleckner introduced Salle to Boone in 1981, and Salle brought Fischl into the gallery in 1982 -- the introducer chain runs Bleckner to Salle to Fischl, and all three were Boone artists. Boone represented Bleckner for almost four decades; his 16th solo exhibition at Mary Boone was in 2016, and he began showing with Petzel Gallery in 2019, before Boone closed that year. He also shows with Capitain Petzel (Berlin), Maruani Mercier (Brussels/Paris), and Vielmetter (Los Angeles). (C24310, leg 1 C24105-C24106, C24114; leg 3 C24216; synthesis C24264)

The early 1980s are optical first. Bleckner's early Stripe paintings pay homage to Bridget Riley and were not particularly well received by critics. His atmospheric Weather series followed in 1983. In 1984 his art attracted a burst of attention when a single large painting was on view at Nature Morte in the East Village. In 1981 he met Thomas Ammann, the influential Swiss dealer who went on to collect his work. Per Milazzo's monograph, the 1976-83 work comprises Constructivist and Post-Constructivist still lifes and landscapes, largely overlooked at the time. Around 1984-86 he was painting canvases he viewed as memorials, in which candelabras, vases, chandeliers, and rococo motifs seem to float against dark grounds -- in part a response to the AIDS crisis. 8,122+ as of January 1986 (1986) bears in its title the number of people who had died of AIDS to date, the digits painted in red in the four corners; other memorial works are dedicated to individuals, and dot patterns suggest the lesions of AIDS-related sarcomas. (C24311, leg 1 C24107-C24108; leg 2 W01, W02, W04; synthesis C24258)

Bleckner commenced his Constellation paintings (1987-93), suggestive of night skies, and the Architecture of the Sky canvases (1988-93), which call to mind domed interiors. His first solo museum exhibition was organized by the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art in 1988. In the early 1990s he made his first Cell paintings, referring to diseased human cells: multicoloured volumetric circles or "cells," smoothly layered against a darker grey ground, looking like droplets of blood or molecules under a microscope. He continued painting aspects of the body at microscopic level, including forms related to DNA and cancer cells -- the latter in response to his father's unsuccessful battle with cancer. Small Count (1990) paints white dots over a dark canvas, bright white and fading, for white blood cells being destroyed by AIDS. Throbbing Hearts (1994) has red splotch-like patterns over a dark canvas resembling Kaposi's sarcoma marks. Bleckner created a series of bird paintings (1995-2003) and experimented with varied surfaces and the airbrush. He has painted flowers since 1985 -- not from life but pictures of flowers, his default source the book The Last Flowers of Manet (1986); in April 2025 he showed flower paintings at Petzel based on Cy Twombly's photographs of flowers (Twombly a friend; homage and remembrance). (C24312, leg 1 C24109-C24111; leg 2 W03, W09-W17; synthesis C24258)

The Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York, held a mid-career retrospective of Bleckner's work in 1995, when he was 45. Gallery and artist bios call him the youngest artist to receive a mid-career retrospective at the museum; artnet (2025) corrects this to the SECOND youngest -- Brice Marden was 37 when honoured in 1975. The safe form is "at 45, one of the youngest artists ever given a mid-career retrospective at the Guggenheim (the second youngest, after Brice Marden, 37 in 1975)." The show held 268 paintings and drawings; by then Bleckner had already had 10 other solo museum exhibitions. Bleckner himself, interviewed in 2025, said the meaningful part was that his parents were still alive to see it; afterwards he felt his work had to grow. He was in the Whitney Biennial in 1975, 1987, and 1989, the Biennale of Sydney (1988) and the Carnegie International (1988). Per Milazzo's monograph the new-millennium work includes the Specific and Anonymous, Inheritance, Protein, and Meditation paintings, and the Time (or Clock) paintings (2006-10). Recent work: Petzel solos Pharmaceutria (2019), Heart in the corner of the room (2021), Mashber (2024), Octopus (2026); the April 2025 Petzel show was eight domestically sized flower paintings after Twombly's flower photographs, each priced $40,000, most sold; It Used to Be, Capitain Petzel, Berlin (2025); upcoming ROSS BLECKNER: NEVER THE LESS, Guild Hall, East Hampton (2026). (C24313, leg 1 C24112-C24113, C24115-C24116; synthesis C24257)

The trio is the bridge from #702 and the strongest documented peer tie in Bleckner's network. Bleckner, Eric Fischl, and David Salle "met in the early 1970s, while they were students at CalArts, and have been friends ever since" (W Magazine, "Three Kings," July 27, 2016). The Parrish Art Museum's Unfinished Business: Paintings from the 1970s and 1980s by Ross Bleckner, Eric Fischl, and David Salle (August 7-October 16, 2016, Parrish Art Museum, Water Mill) was the first exhibition to focus on their early work and their decades-long friendship; the three stayed close geographically -- for 20 years Salle lived on one end of White Street and Bleckner on the other, and in the 1990s both had houses on Daniels Lane, Sagaponack. The Parrish press release frames the show around the friendship: "decades-long friendship that began at [CalArts], matured in New York City, and continued on the East End of Long Island." (C24314, leg 1 C24117; leg 3 C24209-C24211; synthesis C24251-C24252)

Alongside the painting runs a civic life. Bleckner is currently a Clinical Professor of Studio Art at New York University's Steinhardt School of Culture, Education, and Human Development; gallery bios say he "has taught at many of the nation's most prestigious universities," but no source read this run names the other institutions or dates -- do not list any. He is on the board of the AIDS Community Research Initiative of America (ACRIA). In May 2009 he was awarded the title of Goodwill Ambassador by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) -- the first fine artist to receive the position. Later in 2009 he travelled to Gulu, Uganda, to work with former child soldiers and abductees. In 2009 he published Examined Life: Writings, 1972-2007 (Edgewise Press); the documentary Ross Bleckner: Remember Me appeared in 2000; Richard Milazzo's The Paintings of Ross Bleckner (2007) is the first monograph, covering 1976-2006. In 1993 Bleckner bought Truman Capote's modern beach house on a five-acre property in Sagaponack, New York, for $800,000; he has lived in New York's West Village since 2004. In 2018 his former assistant Cody Gilman filed a lawsuit accusing the artist of sexual harassment; Bleckner denied the allegations, and in 2020 the lawsuit was settled out of court for an undisclosed amount and dismissed with prejudice. His first painting at auction, One Wish (1986), fetched $187,000 at Christie's in 1988; a 1988 abstract canvas set a record of $277,000 in the 2022 evening sale of the Thomas and Doris Ammann collection. His works are held in public collections worldwide, including MoMA and the Whitney (New York), SFMOMA, the National Gallery of Art, the Carnegie Museum of Art, the Walker Art Center, Tate Modern, and the Museo Reina Sofia. No art prize or fellowship was found in any source read this run beyond the 2009 UN title -- do not invent one. (C24315, leg 1 C24118-C24124; synthesis C24270, C24272)

The evolution is the proof. 1976-83: Constructivist/Suprematist still lifes and landscapes, then Riley-homage Stripes and the atmospheric Weather series -- dark grounds, optical shimmer, cool and unfashionable. 1984-86: the turn to memorial imagery -- chandeliers, candelabras, rococo motifs floating on dark grounds, and the AIDS death-count title -- where the dark ground becomes a subject (loss) rather than a backdrop. 1987-93: Constellations and Architecture of the Sky -- night skies and domed interiors, light as structure. Early 1990s: Cells -- the microscopic body, blood droplets and molecules on dark grey. 1995-2003: Birds; flowers throughout since 1985, becoming the dominant late motif (Manet's last flowers, then Twombly's flower photographs). 2000s-2020s: the same motifs redeployed and combined -- Inheritance, Protein, Meditation, Time (Clock) paintings -- with the flower as the vehicle of transformation and the dark ground giving way, in the recent flower paintings, to lighter, more lyrical surfaces. What never changes: a repeated motif on a dark field, light emerging from darkness, and the painting as a meditation on impermanence. (C24316, leg 1 C24125; leg 2 evolution; synthesis C24258)


## M1 -- Inscribe, then submerge

A radiant motif -- chandelier, urn, trophy, number -- is painted onto the canvas and then buried under successive layers of paint. Gary Indiana's 1986 description is the mechanical record: motifs "inscribed onto the canvas and then submerged in layers of paint, which obscure but fail to extinguish their smoldering presence". The objects seem "to be crumbling apart; specks of brilliant luminosity suggest their imminent extinction". The layering is not glazing for depth; it is a burial that leaves the buried thing still glowing through. (C24317, leg 2 M1, W04, W08; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a slow emergence-and-sinking. Start with the motif bright and legible, then let veils of dark paint wash over it in passes until only its brightest points survive -- then pull one veil back and let it flare again. The motif should never be fully sharp or fully gone; hold it at the threshold where the eye keeps losing it and finding it. A number or a chandelier that flickers in and out of the dark reads as a memory, not a picture. (C24318, leg 2 M1; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** paint the motif bright, then bury it under dark veils until only its brightest points survive -- hold it at the edge of vanishing. (C24319)


## M2 -- The dark ground as the active element

The ground is never a background. In the 1980s it is a dark blue or black field; in the cell paintings a darker gray; in the recent work a monochrome field. Bleckner says his paintings work in "polarities; light and dark, deep and shallow space", and that when AIDS arrived "the light in my painting took on a darkness because the world changes under your feet sometimes". The dark is what does the work: it absorbs, it threatens, it makes the luminous motif read as something surviving inside it rather than sitting on it. Britannica's Growing Grass (W02, 1987, oil on linen, 108 x 72 in.) states the geometry plainly -- a dark blue field behind equally spaced vertical lines -- and the effect is the field pressing forward, not receding. (C24320, leg 2 M2, W02; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is the ground breathing. Let the dark field slowly deepen and lighten in a long cycle -- never to black, never to gray -- while the motif's brightness stays fixed. When the ground deepens, the motif should look further away and more endangered; when it lightens, the motif should look closer and more present. The viewer should feel the ground as weather, not wallpaper. (C24321, leg 2 M2; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** drive the dark ground, not the motif -- let it deepen and lighten so the bright thing looks endangered, then present, then endangered again. (C24322)


## M3 -- The cell as a volumetric droplet

The cell paintings are built from smooth, multicoloured, volumetric circles -- "droplets of blood or molecules viewed under a microscope" (Petzel) -- laid in smooth layers on a dark gray ground. Mechanically: a circle is not drawn, it is modelled, with a highlight, a shaded volume and a soft edge, so each cell reads as a wet, three-dimensional drop floating in front of the ground rather than a flat disc on it. In Molecular Basis (W16, 1998, oil on canvas, 213 x 152 cm) each abutting cell is itself composed of "countless identical spherical particles"; in Bonds and Proteins (W17, 1999, oil on linen, 60 x 60 in.) cells bind into dense organic chains that intertwine and mutate toward the viewer. The same unit repeats at two scales at once: the particle inside the cell, the cell inside the field. (C24323, leg 2 M3, W16, W17; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is slow drift and swell. Cells float, jostle, touch and separate; each one pulses -- swelling slightly, then easing -- on its own slow clock, out of phase with its neighbours. Occasionally two cells touch and merge into one larger droplet, or one splits. Nothing moves fast; the menace is in the slowness, the sense of something growing while you watch. A cell that swells and dims reads as a white blood cell fading (Small Count, W10). (C24324, leg 2 M3; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** build each cell as a wet, lit droplet that drifts, swells and merges on its own slow clock -- never a flat disc, never in unison. (C24325)


## M4 -- Repetition with difference

Bleckner's surfaces are built from repeated units that are never identical: equally spaced vertical stripes in shifting colours (Growing Grass, W02); fields of dots, some bright white, some fading (Small Count, W10); constellations of points; cells in rows. The title Repetitious Visitation (W03, 1987, oil on canvas, 9 ft 5 1/4 in. x 7 ft, MoMA 520.1987) names the rule: the same thing comes back, but each visit is different. Motifs are "repeated from painting to painting" and he is "in the habit of redeploying and combining old motifs". Within a single painting the repetition is a grid or a field; across paintings it is a series -- Stripes, Constellations, Cells, Birds, Flowers -- each worked until exhausted. (C24326, leg 2 M4, W02, W03, W10; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a field that refuses to settle into pattern. Run a grid of stripes or dots and let each unit vary -- one brighter, one fading, one slightly off-spacing, one missing -- on a slow, unsynchronised cycle. The eye should keep trying to read a regular pattern and keep failing by a little. That failure is the optical shimmer and the emotional point at once: the same thing, returning, changed. (C24327, leg 2 M4; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** repeat the unit across the whole field but vary each one -- brightness, fade, spacing -- so the pattern never quite locks. (C24328)


## M5 -- Constellation placement

The Constellation paintings (1987-93) place small points of light across a dark field the way stars sit in a night sky: sparse, uneven, with large empty stretches between clusters. Unlike the stripes (even spacing) or the cells (abutting, packed), the constellation is a logic of gaps -- the emptiness is most of the painting, and the points are rare enough that each one matters. The points are not connected by lines; the viewer connects them. The series title is doing mechanical work: a constellation is a pattern the viewer completes, not one the painter draws. No named Constellation painting with verified dimensions was found in the searches run; the mechanism rests on the series description and on Repetitious Visitation's scale, not on a single titled constellation work. (C24329, leg 2 M5, W03; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is slow ignition. Points fade in one at a time, hold, and fade out, in no fixed order, so the pattern the viewer thinks they see keeps dissolving and reforming. A cluster should gather, seem to mean something -- a shape, a figure -- then lose a point and fall apart. Never draw the connecting lines; let the viewer's eye draw them, then betray them. (C24330, leg 2 M5; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** place a few bright points in a mostly empty dark field and let them ignite one at a time -- the viewer connects them, and the connection keeps failing. (C24331)


## M6 -- The dome: architecture of the sky

The Architecture of the Sky canvases (1988-93, and again 2014/2016) are domed interiors: a circular, enclosing form that puts the viewer inside a vault rather than in front of a picture. Dome (W09, 1990-1991, oil and resin on canvas, 96 x 96 in.) is a perfect square with a black centre Christie's reads as a black hole -- "darkness is light that has not yet reached a destiny" -- and a "network of tiny cell shaped paint pools" dragging cosmology into anatomy. The dome is a container for the sky: the biggest thing (the cosmos) rendered as an interior (a room). The 2016 domes repeat the form at 84 x 84 in. and 120 x 78 in., and the motif survives into Mausoleum (2016) -- the dome as tomb. (C24332, leg 2 M6, W09, W19; synthesis C24259, C24267)

The watchable behaviour is enclosure. Build a circular form that slowly opens and closes -- a ring of light widening, a dark centre deepening -- so the viewer feels inside it rather than looking at it. The centre should pull: points of light drift slowly inward toward the dark middle and vanish, or drift outward from it and ignite. The motion is radial, never lateral; the painting is a room with a hole in its ceiling. (C24333, leg 2 M6; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** build a circular vault whose dark centre pulls light inward -- the viewer is inside the sky, not in front of it. (C24334)


## M7 -- Optical shimmer

The Stripe paintings are an homage to Bridget Riley, and the mechanism is Riley's: high-contrast, evenly spaced verticals that make the surface vibrate. Britannica on Growing Grass (W02): equally spaced vertical lines in green, brown, yellow and blue on a dark blue field "produce an illusion of movement, like that of blades of grass waving in a field". Nothing in the painting moves; the movement is in the viewer's visual system. The Petzel bio names the result for the whole career: the paintings "elicit a powerful hypnotic and dizzying effect". Bleckner himself frames the 1980s optical paintings as being about "looking and not looking". (C24335, leg 2 M7, W02; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a shimmer that needs the viewer's eye, not the clock. Hold vertical stripes steady and let their colours shift almost imperceptibly -- one stripe warming, its neighbour cooling -- so the field seems to ripple without anything moving. Or let the stripe spacing breathe by a few pixels. The effect should be strongest at the edges of vision and collapse if you stare at one stripe: looking directly should kill it, glancing should revive it. (C24336, leg 2 M7; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** hold the stripes still and shift their colours by a hair -- the movement must live in the viewer's eye, not in the picture. (C24337)


## M8 -- The placeholder image

Bleckner's own formulation, from the New American Paintings interview: "The imagery is a place holder for the chemistry." A chandelier, a bird, a flower, a dome is not the subject; it is a stand-in for a state -- loss, memory, change -- that cannot be painted directly. The painting works in "polarities; light and dark, deep and shallow space", and he tries "not to do one thing in my paintings" but "to change the mood or the tone through these processes". The motif is interchangeable; the chemistry -- the layering, the light, the dark -- is the constant. That is why the same motif can be a memorial in 1986, a cell in 1998, a flower in 2025, and why he redeploys and combines old motifs across decades. (C24338, leg 2 M8; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a slow substitution. Hold the chemistry fixed -- same dark ground, same layering, same light -- and let the motif change underneath it: a chandelier dissolves into a cluster of cells, the cells dissolve into a flower, the flower into a dome, each one melting into the next without the ground or the light ever changing. The viewer should feel that the picture is about the same thing all along, and that the thing is not any of the images. (C24339, leg 2 M8; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** keep the dark ground and the light fixed and let the motif dissolve into another motif -- the image is a placeholder, the chemistry is the subject. (C24340)


## M9 -- Erasure and the ghost of form

The recent work adds a second operation to the layering of M1: erasure. Capitain Petzel (2025): "He layers paint, erases, and builds surfaces that retain the ghost of form yet evoke something spectral, symbolic, and deeply emotional." The flower paintings work the same way: plants "dissolve into luminous bodies or patterned repetitions, their physical specificity replaced by an atmospheric presence"; rain "tends to take on the form of vertical drips, scattered droplets, or hazy veils of paint". The Mashber show (2024) names "modes of erasure" alongside floral motifs and brain-scan landscapes; the title is the Hebrew for "crisis", originally "birthing chair" -- "the seat between life and death". The mechanism is subtractive: paint is removed as well as added, and the removed thing leaves a ghost that is more present than a painted thing would be. (C24341, leg 2 M9, W18, W20; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a form that is half-erased as you watch. Let a flower or a cell be painted in, then wiped -- not fully, but to a smear, a halo, a stain -- and then partially repainted, so the image accumulates as a palimpsest of its own erasures. Rain should fall as vertical drips that smear downward and fade before reaching the bottom. The painting should look like something remembered, not something seen. (C24342, leg 2 M9; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** paint the form in, wipe it to a ghost, repaint it -- let the erasure be the image, not the accident. (C24343)


## M10 -- Scale as immersion: monumental and intimate in one practice

Bleckner works at both extremes and the extremes do different work. The monumental canvases -- Repetitious Visitation (W03) at 9 ft 5 in. x 7 ft, Memorial II (W12, 1994, oil on linen, 8 x 10 ft, MoMA 278.1994) at 8 x 10 ft, Oceans (W01, 1984, oil on canvas, 120 x 144 in.) at 10 x 12 ft, Unend (W20, 2025, oil on canvas, 120 x 84 in.) at 10 x 7 ft -- are immersive fields: Capitain Petzel calls the canvas "a space for meditation, allowing the viewer to immerse themselves in luminous fields and reflective surfaces", and the paintings "shimmer and dissolve, much like experience itself". The intimate works -- Sunflower (W15, 1996, watercolor on paper, 22 x 29 3/4 in.), Color Cells (1997) at 30 x 30 in., the 2024 untitled works at 30 x 30 in. -- are the same motifs held close, like a specimen. The recent Sunset paintings (2025, 60 x 60 in.) reduce the composition to "a single body [that] hovers on a monochrome background". Scale is a dial, not a default. (C24344, leg 2 M10, W01, W03, W12, W15, W20; synthesis C24259)

The watchable behaviour is a zoom between the two scales. Start intimate -- a single cell, a single flower, small in the frame -- and slowly pull back until the same motif is a field filling the whole view; then push in again. Or run the same motif at two scales at once: a large, dim field behind, a small, bright specimen in front. The shift should feel like moving between looking at the night sky and looking through a microscope -- which, for Bleckner, are the same act. (C24345, leg 2 M10; synthesis C24259)

**Takeaway:** run the same motif at two scales at once -- a dim monumental field behind, a small bright specimen in front -- and let the viewer zoom between sky and microscope. (C24346)


## The aesthetic logic: light that survives inside the dark

Bleckner's career is one act performed fifty ways: a bright motif laid on a dark field, the dark doing the work, the light surviving inside it. He never illustrates the subject. A death count becomes four red digits in the corners of a dark canvas; diseased cells become wet, lit droplets on dark grey; a loss becomes a chandelier glowing through buried layers of paint; a crisis becomes a flower wiped to a ghost. The painting is finished when the motif is at the edge of vanishing and still visible -- the threshold where the eye keeps losing it and finding it. (C24347, leg 1 C24125; leg 2 M1-M10; synthesis C24258-C24259)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the ground is the subject: the dark field deepens and lightens, absorbs and threatens, and the motif's job is to survive inside it, not to sit on it. Second, the motif is a placeholder: chandelier, cell, bird, flower, dome -- interchangeable stand-ins for a chemistry of layering, light and dark that never changes, which is why the same motif can be a memorial in 1986, a cell in 1998, a flower in 2025. Third, repetition with difference: the unit comes back -- stripe, dot, cell, point of light -- but each visit is different, so the pattern never locks and the eye never settles. Baldessari replaced the hand with a rule and the picture with a photograph; his school's painters -- Fischl, Bleckner, Salle -- kept the hand and the body, and Bleckner kept the hand on the darkest ground of the three, painting the thing that cannot be shown by painting the light that survives it. (C24348, leg 2 M2, M4, M8; synthesis C24259)

The evolution is the proof. 1976-83: the optical dark field, stripes after Riley, weather after atmosphere. 1984-86: the dark ground becomes loss itself -- memorials, the count, the lesions. 1987-93: the motif lifts into the sky -- constellations, domes, light as structure. Early 1990s: the motif shrinks into the body -- cells, droplets, molecules under glass. 1995-2003: the motif takes wing -- birds, whole and airborne. 1996 onward: the motif blooms -- flowers, first from Manet's last bouquets, then from Twombly's photographs, the ground lightening as the motif dissolves into glow. 2014-25: return, reduction, erasure -- the whole vocabulary restaged at once, then reduced to a single body hovering on monochrome, the image "disembodied, almost vaporized, into this continuous pulsating glow", in his own words. What stays constant is the question the painting asks without answering: what is still glowing in the dark, and for how long? (C24349, leg 1 C24125; leg 2 evolution; synthesis C24258)
