# Leonilson: Study Notes

**Site:** Jose Leonilson Bezerra Dias -- known, in his lifetime and since, by the single name Leonilson -- born 1 March 1957 in Fortaleza, Ceara, died 28 May 1993 in Sao Paulo of AIDS, aged 36; a Brazilian painter, draughtsman and embroiderer whose work is almost exclusively autobiographical, built as a diary in fragments, and whose last two years, after an HIV diagnosis in August 1991, shrank the diary from large bright canvases to tiny stitched cloths until the stitch was the only mark the body could still make. (C14125)

## The life: the boy who drew on anything, the stitcher of his own body

Jose Leonilson Bezerra Dias was born in Fortaleza, Ceara, on 1 March 1957 -- the day and month rest on the Portuguese Wikipedia (citing the Itau Cultural Enciclopedia) and are corroborated by the German, Swedish and Esperanto editions; the Itau Enciclopedia entry itself gives only the year, so the 1 March date is well-corroborated but not primary-sourced (see Corrections 2) -- and died in Sao Paulo on 28 May 1993, of AIDS, aged 36. He is known, in his lifetime and since, by the single name Leonilson. (C14128, leg 1 C13955, C13992)

He was the fourth child in a family of five from Fortaleza. His father was in the retail business -- a textile merchant, described in one source as a well-off cloth dealer -- and his mother was skilled in handmade embroidery, a common practice for women in the northeast of Brazil. The family was very Catholic. As a child he always had a pencil in hand, drawing on any surface he could find, and he loved perusing the pages of Barsa, the Brazilian version of the Encyclopedia Britannica. His father wished he would take on the family business in Porto Velho, a city in the Amazon region; nobody in the family criticised his drive toward art, but nobody encouraged it either. In 1961 the family moved from Fortaleza to Sao Paulo, where Leonilson lived for the rest of his life. He was about four years old at the move. The family trade is the whole later career in miniature: a textile merchant's son, raised watching his mother embroider, who ends by making the body out of cloth, buttons and thread instead of paint. (C14129, leg 1 C13956, C13957)

His earliest surviving work is an untitled acrylic painting of a fish, "Peixe" (Fish), dated 1971, made when he was fourteen. He made his first assemblage in 1972, in his mid-teens; "Mirro" (Mirror, 1975), a fragment of a pair of jeans fixed to a shallow wooden box with felt pieces embroidered in a cruciform and the word MIRRO stitched upside-down, is described as a close approximation of that first work. The two categories are different -- first known work vs first assemblage -- and do not conflict once separated. (C14130, leg 1 C13958; leg 2 C14045)

In his teens in Sao Paulo he enrolled at the Escola Pan-Americana de Arte, and then in 1977 entered the art course at the Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado (FAAP), where he studied with Julio Plaza, Nelson Leirner and Regina Silveira. He left FAAP unfinished: the Itau Cultural Enciclopedia gives his FAAP years as 1977-1980, the Portuguese Wikipedia dates the abandonment of the course to 1980, and curator Ricardo Resende says the decision to become an artist in 1979 led him to drop out of FAAP for the second and last time -- the two accounts do not agree on the year, and the earlier dropout is not dated in any source read (see Corrections 3). Do not print a FAAP graduation: none is evidenced; he left unfinished on every account. From 1978 to 1981 he attended the Centro de Estudos ASTER in Sao Paulo (also called the Escola de Artes Aster / Escola de Artes Visuais Aster), taking watercolour classes with Dudi Maia Rosa. His ASTER teachers were Regina Silveira, Julio Plaza and the Italian-born Donato Ferrari, all artists of conceptual extraction, and he also studied art history there with Walter Zanini. ASTER itself had been founded in 1978 by Silveira, Plaza, Donato Ferrari and Zanini -- the same school where Franca was working in off-set printing with Silveira in 1979/80. (C14131, leg 1 C13959, C13960, C13961)

Among his ASTER classmates were the future artists Luiz Zerbini, Jac Leirner, Ana Tavares and Rafael Franca, and the future gallerist Eduardo Brandao (Galeria Vermelho). In 1979 he shared a studio with Zerbini, and in that same year, by his own later account in interviews including one with Adriano Pedrosa, he made the decision to devote his life to art. No source read records any joint work between Leonilson and Franca: they were classmates in the same cohort, not collaborators. (C14132, leg 1 C13962)

Leonilson never studied at ECA-USP, the Escola de Comunicacoes e Artes of the Universidade de Sao Paulo. That was Franca's school: Franca moved from Porto Alegre to Sao Paulo in 1978 to study at ECA-USP under Silveira. Leonilson's link to Silveira runs through FAAP and ASTER instead. Any study text that puts Leonilson at ECA-USP is wrong and must not be repeated. (C14133, leg 1 C13963, C13996)

In 1979 he took part in his first group exhibition, "Desenho Jovem" (Young Drawing) at MAC-USP, the Museu de Arte Contemporanea da Universidade de Sao Paulo. In 1980 he showed in the Panorama da Arte Atual Brasileira / Desenho e Gravura at MAM/SP, the Museu de Arte Moderna de Sao Paulo, and in a two-person show with Luiz Zerbini at the Teatro Lira Paulistana. His first trip abroad came in 1981: in Madrid he held his first solo exhibition, at the Galeria Casa do Brasil, visited other European cities, and took part in "Giovane Arte Internazionale" at the Galleria Giuli in Lecce, Italy. In 1982 he returned to Europe, travelling in Italy, Germany and Portugal, exhibited solo at the Galleria Pellegrino in Bologna, and in Milan met Antonio Dias, who introduced him to the art critic Achille Bonito Oliva, linked to the Italian Transavanguardia. He returned to Brazil in 1982. In 1983 he held two solo exhibitions in Brazil, at the Galeria Luisa Strina in Sao Paulo and the Galeria Thomas Cohn in Rio de Janeiro, plus a solo at the Galeria Tina Presser in Porto Alegre, and he met Leda Catunda, who with Sergio Romagnolo became one of his closest friends of the period. (C14134, leg 1 C13964, C13965, C13966)

Leonilson is counted as one of the main names of Geracao 80 (the 80s Generation), the loosely affiliated group of artists associated with the return to painting -- the "volta a pintura" -- after the conceptual 1970s, and with an expressive, pop-inflected visual language. The generation-defining exhibition was "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" (How Are You, Generation 80?) at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage, Rio de Janeiro, in 1984, in which he took part. He resisted being grouped with the generation even while associated closely with its success: the Americas Society notes that he "resisted being grouped with the so-called 80s Generation in Brazil, despite having been associated closely with its meteoric success." (C14135, leg 1 C13967)

He was NOT a member of Casa 7. Casa 7 was five painters -- Rodrigo Andrade, Carlito Carvalhosa, Fabio Miguez, Nuno Ramos and Paulo Monteiro (Antonio Malta was involved initially) -- who from 1982/83 shared a rented house, number 7, in Pinheiros, Sao Paulo, as a collective studio and showed together at the 18th Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1985. Leonilson exhibited alongside some of them but no source read places him in the group, and the group's own members name only the five. The link is generational -- same Sao Paulo painting revival, same Geracao 80 moment -- not membership. (C14136, leg 1 C13968; leg 3 C14077)

In 1985 he took part in the 18th Sao Paulo International Biennial at the Fundacao Bienal, and in the Paris Biennale -- the 13th Paris Biennale, dated 1984 in the Escritorio de Arte exhibition list and 1985 by Almeida & Dale and the Portuguese Wikipedia (see Corrections 4). 1985 was also the year Brazil's twenty-one-year military dictatorship ended, opening the new phase of creative freedom in which his generation worked. Through the mid-1980s he travelled constantly: in 1986 he made "Boa Viagem" (Safe Trip), a sand, fire and stone installation for the 1st International Exhibition of Ephemeral Sculptures in Fortaleza, and in 1987 he showed "Moving Mountains" at the Kunstforum in Munich. The Lisson press release states that beginning in 1987 he went to New York once a year until his death; that annual rhythm rests on Lisson alone in the sources read (see Corrections 10). (C14137, leg 1 C13969, C13970)

In 1989 his work changed decisively. He exhibited "Anotacoes de Viagem" (Travel Notes) at the Galeria Luisa Strina in Sao Paulo, pieces made with buttons, semi-precious stones and embroidery, introducing sewing as a new and fundamental procedure. The same year he encountered the embroidered work of Arthur Bispo do Rosario (1911-1989) in a landmark Rio de Janeiro exhibition, an artist he admired. Sewing was familiar ground: the son of a textile merchant, he had watched his mother embroider at home every day, and late in life he spoke of that as a formative influence. (C14138, leg 1 C13971)

In 1990 he travelled to London (twice), New York, Paris, Venice and Amsterdam, received the Brasilia Prize for Visual Arts at the Salao Nacional, showed solo at the Pulitzer Art Gallery in Amsterdam, and began recording audio tapes -- ideas, emotions, daily life -- with a view to a book his friend Ricardo Ferreira would realise as he wished. Those tapes later became the basis of Carlos Nader's film "A Paixao de JL" (2015), described as a posthumous partnership between artist and director. (C14139, leg 1 C13972)

From March 1991 until May 1993 he drew illustrations for the weekly column by journalist Barbara Gancia in the newspaper Folha de S. Paulo -- about a hundred drawings in all. In August 1991 a test revealed that he was HIV-positive; he was 34. From mid-1991 the personal and autobiographical character of the work intensified, and living with the disease dominated it completely: blank spaces, tiny formats, and a language of silence in the drawings and engravings. The seven-drawing series "O Perigoso" (The Dangerous, 1992) treats his condition with irony -- the first drawing carries a drop of his own HIV-positive blood -- and "34 with Scars" (1991), now at MoMA, stitches his age and his wounds into a small voile panel. He kept drawing and painting in 1991-93 -- the Gancia illustrations ran to May 1993 -- so the English Wikipedia's claim that after the diagnosis he "fully pivoted to solely use textiles and embroidery" overstates the turn: embroidery became dominant and the scale shrank; he did not stop drawing. (C14140, leg 1 C13973, C13974, C14001)

In 1992 he organised the exhibition "Um Olhar sobre o Figurativo" for the Galeria Casa Triangulo in Sao Paulo, travelled to Amsterdam, Munich, Paris and New York, and showed in the group exhibitions "X Mostra de Gravura Cidade de Curitiba / Mostra America" in Curitiba and "Pintura Brasil Decada 80", organised by the Itau Galeria. In 1993 he held solo exhibitions at the Galeria Sao Paulo in Sao Paulo and the Galeria Thomas Cohn in Rio de Janeiro, and took part in the travelling group show "Cartographies" in Winnipeg, Ottawa and Caracas. His last work was "Instalacao sobre duas figuras" (Installation on Two Figures), an installation conceived for the Capela do Morumbi in Sao Paulo in 1993: light white fabrics expressing the fragility of life, soft shirts covering the chairs, embroideries reading "da falsa moral" (of false morality) and "do bom coracao" (of the good heart), and hope in "Lazaro". He did not see it mounted, because he was already very ill; friends and family mounted it. He died in Sao Paulo on 28 May 1993, of AIDS, aged 36. For that Capela do Morumbi installation and the other solo exhibition of the same year he received, in 1994, a posthumous tribute and the prize of the Associacao Paulista de Criticos de Arte (APCA). At the 24th Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1998 he was honoured with a special room, and an image of one of his sculptures was the emblem of the event. (C14141, leg 1 C13975, C13976, C13977)

In the year of his death, family and friends founded the Projeto Leonilson (Leonilson Project), with the aim of organising the artist's archives and researching, cataloguing and publicising his works. It was founded informally in 1993 and is directed by his sister Ana Lenice Dias, known as Nicinha; Leonilson left his mother as sole heir to his legacy, and the Projeto was created with the works and personal archive he left her -- books, letters, photos, works by his artist friends. The Projeto is based in a house in Vila Mariana, in the south of Sao Paulo, that belonged to his mother, and holds more than 400 exhibitions catalogued, plus a growing personal archive of correspondence and diaries. A separate body, the Sociedade dos Amigos do Projeto Leonilson, was founded in 1995 -- do not merge the two dates. After twenty-four years of work the Projeto published the catalogue raisonne in June 2017, in three volumes (1971-1980, 1981-1989, 1990-1993), edited by Ana Lenice Dias and Gabriela Dias Clemente with Ricardo Resende. The counts differ by source: the raisonne itself is described as 3,400 records at launch, Newcity Brazil calls it 3,800 entries, and the Galeria Marilia Razuk page speaks of approximately 4,000 artworks left at his death (see Corrections 8). Visual AIDS calls it the first catalogue raisonne by a deceased contemporary artist to be published in Brazil. (C14142, leg 1 C13978, C13979, C14003)

The posthumous exhibition history runs: "Leonilson: sao tantas as verdades" (So Many Truths), curated by Lisette Lagnado at the Galeria de Arte do Sesi, Sao Paulo, in 1995-96, his first retrospective, which travelled to Rio and Belo Horizonte; "Projects 53: Oliver Herring and Jose Leonilson" at MoMA, New York, 18 January-12 March 1996; "Sob o Peso dos Meus Amores" (Under the Heavy Weight of My Loves) at Itau Cultural, Sao Paulo, in 2011; "Truth Fiction" at the Pinacoteca de Sao Paulo in 2014, curated by Adriano Pedrosa; "Jose Leonilson: Empty Man" at the Americas Society, New York, 27 September 2017-3 February 2018, his first solo exhibition in the United States, with about 50 works; "Leonilson: Drawn 1975-1993", first retrospective in Europe, at the KW Institute for Contemporary Art, Berlin, in 2020-21, travelling to Malmo Konsthall and the Museu de Arte Contemporanea de Serralves, Porto, in 2021-22; and "Leonilson: agora e as oportunidades" (Now and Opportunities), curated by Adriano Pedrosa with Teo Teotonio, at MASP, Sao Paulo, 23 August-17 November 2024, more than 300 works from the last five years of his life, 1989-1993. In his lifetime he showed at the 18th Bienal de Sao Paulo (1985) and the 13th Paris Biennale (1984 or 1985). Posthumously he was included in the Istanbul Biennale (1997, 2011) and the 52nd Venice Biennale (2007), in Robert Storr's central international exhibition "Think with the Senses -- Feel with the Mind". There was no Venice Biennale showing in 1991, in his lifetime or otherwise. (C14143, leg 1 C13980, C13981)

His works are held, across the sources read, by: the Centre Georges Pompidou, Paris; Tate Modern, London; the Museu de Arte de Sao Paulo Assis Chateaubriand (MASP); the Museu de Arte Moderna de Sao Paulo (MAM/SP); the Museu de Arte Moderna do Rio de Janeiro (MAM/RJ); the Museu de Arte Contemporanea da Universidade de Sao Paulo (MAC-USP); the Pinacoteca de Sao Paulo; the Museu d'Art Contemporani de Barcelona (MACBA); the Museu de Arte do Rio Grande do Sul Ado Malagoli (MARGS), Porto Alegre; the Museu de Arte Contemporanea de Niteroi (MAC-Niteroi, Joao Sattamini collection); the Museu da Gravura, Curitiba; the Museu de Arte de Brasilia; the Museum of Modern Art, New York (MoMA); the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA); the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston; the Coleccion Patricia Phelps de Cisneros; the Stadtische Galerie im Lenbachhaus, Munich; the Museu de Arte Contemporanea de Serralves, Porto; the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires; the Museo de Arte Latinoamericano de Buenos Aires (MALBA); the Instituto Moreira Salles, Rio de Janeiro; and Inhotim. MoMA holds at least seven of his works, including "34 with Scars" (1991, acrylic, embroidery thread and plastic tacks on voile, 41 x 31 cm, object number 1144.2001, gift of Carmen Bezerra Dias in memory of Theodorino Torquato Dias), "The Japanese Woman" (1988), "To Make Your Soul Close to Me" (1989), "Sunset, Earthquake, Loneliness" (1990) and "I Am Your Man" (1992). Tate holds "The Penelope" (1993), ten panels of diaphanous fabrics patched into a draping banner, 222 x 83 cm (Tate object T07768) -- the Tate artwork page fetched this run returned only 28 lines and no artwork text, so the medium, dimensions and panel count rest on the English Wikipedia's Tate-cited entry, not on a Tate page read end to end. MASP holds "Agora e as oportunidades" (Now and Opportunities, 1991), the painting that gives the 2024 exhibition its title; the MASP collection record gives it as a 2020 donation from Fernanda Feitosa and Heitor Martins, inventory number MASP.11149 -- read only as a search excerpt, not opened end to end. (C14144, leg 1 C13982, C13983, C13984, C13985)

Between 30 October and 10 December 1992, already in failing health, he gave Lisette Lagnado the long interview "A dimensao da fala" (The Dimension of Speech), published in "Leonilson: sao tantas as verdades" (1995/1996). In it he calls himself not so much an artist as a "curious" person and a wanderer ("andarilho"), "ambiguous, completely", and says his works "don't deliver a truth directly, but they show an open vision." Lagnado, who first wrote about him early and later directed the Projeto's catalogue raisonne work, reads each piece as a letter to an intimate diary. His personal lexicon of recurring signs, built from 1984 onward and stable to the end, runs: the open book, the tower, the radar, the atom, the heart, the spiral, the clock, the compass, the hourglass, the mountain, the volcano, the ocean, the staircase, the globe and the mathematical symbol for infinity, with fire and water superimposed -- the vocabulary through which a private diary became a public, legible language. His early work drew on Antonio Dias, Blinky Palermo and Paul Klee, with later reference points including Eva Hesse, Joseph Beuys, Robert Ryman, Keith Haring, Jean-Michel Basquiat and Kenny Scharf, and the decisive influence of Helio Oiticica, Lygia Clark and Mira Schendel's generation before him. Two further biographical threads, each resting on a single source read and flagged as such: the Projeto's house in Vila Mariana has a Sala do Teatro with material from the period when he made costumes for the theatre group Asdrubal Trouxe o Trombone, and his mother embroidered at home every day. (C14145, leg 1 C13986, C13987, C13988, C13989)

The through-line of the life, for the study's use: a boy from Fortaleza who drew on anything, moved to Sao Paulo at four, learned his craft from the conceptual generation at FAAP and ASTER, found his public in the return to painting, and then -- after the HIV diagnosis of August 1991 -- spent his last two years stitching his own body, age, weight and fear into voile and felt, at smaller and smaller scale, until a white installation in a chapel said what painting no longer could. The scale collapses; the diary does not. (C14146, leg 1 C13990)

## M1 -- The stitch as a scar: the needle draws the wound

The mark is sewn, so the mark is also a wound and a repair. "34 with Scars" (1991, W14): a small voile panel, 41 x 31 cm, the empty white fabric standing in for a body, black scars stitched into it and the age 34 stitched beside them, made soon after the August 1991 HIV diagnosis. "Empty Man" (1991, W13): thread on a found embroidered linen picturing the tortoise and the hare, stitched beneath with "salt. blood. salive" and a man's torso ringed by the broken phrase "empty man / lone / ready". "O Perigoso" (1992, W17): seven drawings, the first carrying a drop of his own HIV-positive blood. The stitch replaces the brush: a scar, a tally, a word is literally sewn, and the cloth is the skin of the work. Watchable: a white cloth with a black stitched scar across it; the scar does not illustrate the illness, it is the illness made visible in thread. (C14149, leg 2 C14018, C14022, C14026; leg 1 C13973, C13974)

**Takeaway:** a stitch earns its scar when the thread is the wound instead of a picture of one. If the stitch only decorates the cloth, it is embroidery; if the scar is sewn into the fabric that stands in for the body, the needle is the diagnosis and the cloth is the patient.


## M2 -- Text as confession: one word carries the whole work

A single word, sewn or painted, carries the emotional charge and the work is a letter to an intimate diary. "Ninguem" (1992, W16): the word "nobody" sewn on the edge of a pink pillow, the lover addressed as no one. "Empty Man" (1991, W13): "empty man / lone / ready" broken across a torso. "Agora e as oportunidades" (1991, W12): a totemic figure split in two, "I am one man, I am two", "I am almost 2 meters tall and I have been alone for several nights", six glasses inscribed "the Blacks, the homosexuals, the Jews, the women, the cripples, the communists" -- a roll call of minorities naming his own exclusion. Supporting works outside the 20 repeat the move: "Traidor" (1991), the word traitor sewn above a sea of crystals; "O mentiroso" (1992), the liar sewn on a cloth bag; "Jose" (1991), the first name embroidered alone on transparent voile. Watchable: one word on a soft object, the rest of the cloth blank; the silence around the word is the illness, and the word is the confession. (C14150, leg 2 C14021, C14022, C14025, C14031; leg 1 C13986)

**Takeaway:** a word earns its confession when the blank cloth around it is part of the sentence. If the word only labels the picture, it is a caption; if one word on a pillow names an absence and the empty fabric holds the silence, the word is the work and the cloth is the diary page.


## M3 -- Small scale as intimacy: the viewer must lean in

The tiny format forces the viewer close, like reading over someone's shoulder. "El Puerto" (1992, W18): 16 x 23 cm, a scrap of the artist's own striped shirt covering a cheap street-market mirror. "34 with Scars" (1991, W14): 41 x 31 cm. "Men with Their Own Attentions" (1989, W10): watercolor and ink on paper, 32 x 24 cm. The late works are described in sources as "minute poignant testimonials", "stamp-size watercolors lost in the lonely void of the white paper or voile fabric", "haiku-like works". The scale is the opposite of the 1980s canvases -- "O Ventilador" c. 1983 is 177 x 185 cm, "O Peao" 1987 is 196 x 108 cm -- and the shrink is the story: the same diary, the same symbols, written smaller and smaller until the stitch is the only mark the body can still make. Watchable: a work the size of a postcard that you have to step up to and bend toward; the intimacy is enforced by the format, not suggested by the subject. (C14151, leg 2 C14019, C14023, C14027, C14032; leg 3 C14061, C14062)

**Takeaway:** a small work earns its intimacy when the format makes the viewer lean in. If the work is only small, it is a miniature; if the tiny cloth and the blank space around one word force you to read over the artist's shoulder, the scale is the confession and the closeness is the work.


## M4 -- The body mapped on cloth: rivers are veins

Organs, veins, rivers and streets are the same drawing, the body as a geography with the mouth, the heart or the wound as its destination. "Todos os rios levam a sua boca" (c. 1989, W09): acrylic on canvas, 212 x 100 cm, rivers as veins, arteries and roots, the mouth as the destination of every route. "O Pescador de Palavras" (1986, W05): a white fisherman by a tree holding a rod, a spiral whirlpool of small black marks, the German words "rababa", "Haus" and "Brucke" scattered on the cloth -- fishing for words, the bridge as an "in between" of places and tongues. "Agora e as oportunidades" (1991, W12): the divided body as a political body. Supporting works: "As ruas da cidade" (c. 1988), human organs with words flowing through them; "O que posso fazer dentro de mim" (1991), organ-like forms with "golden interior", "what there is of you in me", "jewels". Watchable: a map whose rivers end at a mouth, whose streets carry words instead of cars; the body is the territory and the drawing is the atlas. (C14152, leg 2 C14014, C14018, C14021, C14031, C14033)

**Takeaway:** a body map earns its geography when the route has a destination. If the organs only decorate the cloth, they are anatomy; if every river on the canvas leads to a mouth and every street carries a word, the body is the map and the destination is the meaning.


## M5 -- Found cloth as the body by metonymy: the shirt stands in for the man

A shirt scrap, a found linen, a pink pillow or a cheap mirror is the body by metonymy, the object standing in for the person who wore or used it. "Mirro" (c. 1975, W02): a fragment of a pair of jeans fixed to a shallow wooden box, buttons for eyes -- an early self-portrait in which found cloth and buttons stand in for a face, the body already made of clothing. "Empty Man" (1991, W13): a found linen picturing the tortoise and the hare, stitched over. "El Puerto" (1992, W18): a scrap of the artist's own shirt, striped like pyjamas, covering a mirror. "Ninguem" (1992, W16): a pink pillow naming absence. The mechanism runs from the first assemblage (1972) to the last works: the son of a textile merchant, raised watching his mother embroider, makes the body out of the materials of the family trade -- cloth, buttons, thread -- instead of paint. Watchable: a shirt you recognise as a garment before you read it as a work; the person is absent and the clothing is the only evidence he was there. (C14153, leg 2 C14011, C14022, C14025, C14027, C14033; leg 1 C13956, C13971)

**Takeaway:** a found cloth earns its body when the object is the person, not a picture of one. If the shirt only hangs on the work, it is a prop; if the scrap of pyjama-striped shirt is the only self-portrait left and the mirror behind it is covered, the cloth is the man and the absence is the portrait.


## M6 -- The mirror that refuses to reflect: identity as data

The mirror is covered, so the self cannot be seen, and embroidered biographical data is the only identity left. "El Puerto" (1992, W18): the shirt scrap covers the mirror, embroidered in blue thread with "Leo", age "35", weight "60", height "1.79 m" (one source gives 1.77 m), then the title; the artist said "I don't lift the little curtain. Hardly ever. I'm glad the mirror is covered". The curtain is the work: a cheap mirror that refuses to reflect, a name and three numbers standing in for a face. Supporting: "Jose" (1991), the first name embroidered alone on transparent voile stretched on a chassis -- the name without the body. Watchable: a mirror you cannot look into, a curtain of shirt-cloth with a name and numbers stitched on it; the refusal to show the face is the portrait, and the data is all that is left of the person. (C14154, leg 2 C14027, C14031; leg 1 C13973)

**Takeaway:** a covered mirror earns its portrait when the refusal is the likeness. If the cloth only hides the mirror, it is a cover; if the name, age, weight and height stitched on the curtain are the only identity the work offers, the data is the face and the covered mirror is the self-portrait.


## M7 -- Gender inversion: the masculine article on a feminine myth

The masculine article "O" forced onto feminine nouns makes waiting and weaving a male position and unfixes the gender of both maker and myth. "O Penelope, O Recruta, O Aranha" (1991, W15): embroidery on voile and canvas, 50 x 18 cm, black thread spelling "voce q espero imenso e nao sei quem e" (you whom I wait for immensely and do not know who you are) and the number 34, with the three titles stitched down the left edge -- Penelope, the recruit, the spider, all given the masculine "O". "O Penelope" (1993, W19): ten panels of diaphanous off-white and pale-blue fabrics patched into a draping banner, 222 x 83 cm, large imperfect hand-sewn stitches along the edge, an empty chair stitched into the margins -- the shroud as a waiting device, Penelope weaving by day and unpicking by night, the masculine "O" on a feminine myth, sewing as the only thing holding a dying man together. Watchable: a banner of pale cloth with a chair stitched empty into its margin; the waiting is the work, and the man doing the waiting has taken the woman's myth and her article with it. (C14155, leg 2 C14024, C14028, C14033)

**Takeaway:** an inverted article earns its gender when the grammar changes who waits. If "O Penelope" were only a title, it would be a typo; if the masculine article makes weaving and waiting a male position and the empty chair is stitched into a dying man's banner, the grammar is the work and the myth has changed hands.


## M8 -- The chapel as a sickroom: the body dissolves into fabric

The last work dissolves the body into fabric and a single word of resurrection. "Instalacao sobre duas figuras" (1993, W20): his last work, an installation for the Capela do Morumbi, Sao Paulo -- light white fabrics expressing the fragility of life, soft shirts covering the chairs, embroideries reading "falsa moral" (of false morality) and "bom coracao" (of the good heart), and hope in "Lazaro". He never saw it mounted; friends and family mounted it while he was already very ill, and he died weeks later. The clothing is the congregation: shirts where bodies should sit, words where faces should be. It is the end of the shrink that runs through the whole oeuvre -- from 196 x 108 cm canvases to a 16 x 23 cm mirror to a chapel full of empty shirts -- and the diary's last entry is not a picture but a room. Watchable: a chapel whose chairs wear soft shirts, each shirt carrying one embroidered word; the congregation is clothing, the body has become fabric, and the only living word in the room is Lazarus. (C14156, leg 2 C14029; leg 1 C13976, C13990)

**Takeaway:** a chapel of shirts earns its sickroom when the clothing is the congregation. If the shirts only decorate the chairs, they are laundry; if every chair wears a shirt and the only word of hope in the room is Lazaro, the body has dissolved into fabric and the resurrection is the last word.


## The aesthetic logic: the diary that shrinks, the stitch that replaces the brush, the cloth that stands in for the body

Leonilson's programme has three terms, and they never change, only shrink to the smallest material that can carry them. First, the diary is the whole oeuvre: every work is a dated letter to an intimate diary, in Lagnado's reading, and the same symbols -- volcano, tower, heart, clock, hourglass, atom, book, mountain, spiral, compass -- run from 1984 to the end without changing, only getting smaller. The diary does not change its subject; it changes its size. Second, the scale collapses, and the collapse is the story: from 196 x 108 cm canvases to a 16 x 23 cm mirror to a chapel full of empty shirts, the shrink tracks the body's own shrinking, and the intimacy is enforced by the format -- the viewer must lean in, read over the artist's shoulder, bend toward a postcard-sized cloth. Third, the body is made of cloth: a shirt scrap, a found linen, a pink pillow, a piece of voile stand in for the person, and the stitch is the only mark the body can still make -- a scar, a tally, a word, sewn into the fabric that stands in for the skin. The mirror is covered, the name is stitched, the word is the last evidence.

That is why the same moves survive ten years of changing supports, from large bright painting to tiny embroidery without ever changing their question: what is the smallest system that can hold a life, a body and a fear, and where does the work land when the body is gone? The answer, at the end, is a room: a chapel whose chairs wear soft shirts, each shirt carrying one embroidered word, and the only living word in the room is Lazarus. (C14157)
