# George Butterworth: Study Notes

**Site:** George Sainton Kaye Butterworth, MC, b. Sunday 12 July 1885, 16 Westbourne Square, Paddington, London, England; d. ## 1. Paddington, York, and the railway father

George Sainton Kaye Butterworth was born on Sunday 12 July 1885 at 16 Westbourne Square, Paddington, London, the only child of Alexander Kaye Butterworth (1854-1946), solicitor for the Great Western Railway and later solicitor and then general manager of the North Eastern Railway, and Julia Marguerite Wigan (1849-1911), daughter of George Wigan MD of Portishead, Somerset, a professional soprano before marriage who gave up her career and poured her musical energy into her son. The names are a small genealogy in themselves: George after both grandfathers, Kaye for his great-grandfather John Kaye, Bishop of Lincoln, Sainton after his mother's singing teacher Charlotte Sainton-Dolby. He was baptised on 30 August 1885 by his grandfather the Reverend George Butterworth, vicar of Deerhurst, Gloucestershire. In 1891, when George was six, the family moved to York on his father's North Eastern Railway appointment -- the York Civic Trust and Cecil Sharp's People date the move to 1891, against Wikipedia's loose "soon after his birth", and 1891 is used here. The family home, "Riseholme" on Driffield Terrace, later became part of the Mount School, where a blue plaque was unveiled in the centenary year 2016. His first music lessons came from his mother, then private tutors and piano lessons with the Leipzig-trained Christian Padel in York; he began composing early, simple hymns at prep school, and took dance lessons as a child -- a detail that matters later, because the dancer never left the composer.

**Takeaway:** the childhood is a railway manager's son with a soprano mother, a York upbringing, and music lessons from the woman who had given up singing to raise him -- the folk collector and the morris dancer both start here, in a house where music was a mother's second career. [C34749]

## 2. Eton: King's Scholar, cricket, and the lost Barcarolle

From spring 1896 Butterworth was at Aysgarth Preparatory School in the Yorkshire Dales, where he played the chapel organ and wrote his first hymns. He went to Eton College as a King's Scholar -- fourth on the list -- in 1899 (the Dictionary of National Biography 1927 and York Civic Trust agree on 1899; one war-epitaph source says 1898, unresolved by a year). At Eton he studied music with T. F. Dunhill, was a popular figure and an impressive cricketer who won "a serious quantity of sports trophies", and carried musical sketchbooks, jotting ideas by the cricket pitch. He wrote a violin sonata, a string quartet and a Barcarolle for orchestra, performed in April 1903 according to the Eton College Chronicle -- and all three are now lost, destroyed, the first entries in a catalogue defined as much by what he threw away as by what he kept. His father pressed him toward law, his own profession; George went to Oxford intending the Bar. The law never happened.

**Takeaway:** Eton gives him the pattern of the whole life -- the sportsman and the musician in the same body, the sketchbook carried everywhere, and the first works already destroyed by the man who wrote them. [C34750]

## 3. Oxford, 1904-1908: Greats, the Musical Club, and the folk conversion

Butterworth went up to Trinity College, Oxford, in October 1904, reading Greats -- literae humaniores, classics, ancient history, philosophy -- and graduated in June 1908 with a Third Class degree, the music having crowded out the degree. He was president of the Oxford University Musical Club from October 1906 to March 1907 (the Dictionary of National Biography dates the presidency precisely; other sources give no dates), sang in the Oxford Bach Choir under Hugh Allen, and made the friendships that shaped everything after: Ralph Vaughan Williams, Cecil Sharp, Hugh Allen, Adrian Boult, R. O. Morris, Francis "Timmy" Jekyll, Reginald Tiddy. The decisive Oxford event is not a composition but a conversion. He joined the Folk-Song Society in 1906, met Vaughan Williams that year, and was already in touch with Lucy Broadwood about Sussex songs he had collected. In April 1907 he saw an Esperance Club concert at the Queen's Hall and called it "a most delicious entertainment". In the summer of 1907 he collected in Sussex -- including "The Banks of Green Willow" from Mrs Cranstone of Billingshurst and four versions of "Green Bushes". The folk work began as a parallel life to the composing; within five years it would be inside it.

**Takeaway:** Oxford is where the collector is born, not the composer -- the degree is a Third, the presidency is a footnote, and the real event is a summer in Sussex with a notebook, meeting the singers whose tunes would become his orchestral works. [C34751]

## 4. Collecting: Sussex 1907, Jekyll, and the phonograph

Butterworth collected mostly in Sussex, often with Francis Jekyll -- Butterworth took the melodies, Jekyll the words -- and sometimes with Vaughan Williams, including a trip to East Anglia in October 1910, and in Oxfordshire and Bicester in April 1912, and the rapper and longsword dances of the North East in autumn 1912. How much he collected is genuinely unresolved, and this study does not settle it: Wikipedia and the York Civic Trust say "over 450", Cecil Sharp's People and the British Music Collection say "over 300 folk songs", and the English Folk Dance and Song Society catalogue, cited by the war-epitaph research, gives almost 300 folk songs, 134 dances and 29 dance tunes -- about 463 items in all. The likeliest reading is that ~300 counts songs and ~450 counts songs plus dances plus tunes, but no source states that explicitly, so both numbers are carried with their scopes. He sometimes used a phonograph and wax cylinders -- a variant of the title tune was phonographed from David Clements in a Basingstoke workhouse in 1909 -- but the claim, found in one source, that he was the first to record folk song on the phonograph is wrong: Percy Grainger was recording Joseph Taylor on wax cylinder in 1907, and Vaughan Williams recorded Clements himself in 1909. Butterworth was an early adopter, not the first. The manuscripts survive with words divorced from tunes, reunited only by a later Leeds M.Phil. edition; the collection itself went to the English Folk Dance and Song Society and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library, where it is digitised in the Take 6 / Full English archive.

**Takeaway:** the collecting is fieldwork, not library work -- Sussex villages, a workhouse, a phonograph, a friend taking the words while he took the tunes -- and the honest count is "about three hundred songs, nearer four hundred and fifty items", not one clean number. [C34752]

## 5. Morris dancing: the professional dancer

Butterworth was not only a collector but a practitioner, and a good one: an expert dancer, a member of Cecil Sharp's demonstration team for three years, employed for a while as a professional morris dancer by the Folk-Dance Society, and, by several accounts, possibly the best folk dancer of his generation. A Kinora silent film shot at Kelmscott Manor in June 1912 shows him dancing jigs -- "Molly Oxford" and "I'll go and enlist for a sailor". He was a founder member of the English Folk Dance Society when it formed in December 1911 and a committee member soon after -- and here the study corrects a widely repeated error: Wikipedia's claim that he was a founder member "in 1906" of the English Folk Dance and Song Society conflates two bodies. He joined the Folk-Song Society in 1906; the Folk-Dance Society was formed in 1911; the merged English Folk Dance and Song Society dates from 1932. In March 1913 he wrote to Sharp that "dancing was killing his music" and decided to give it up -- though he kept dancing for another eighteen months, including the Folk-Dance Society team's Paris trip in June 1913. The dance practice left its mark on the music not as quoted steps -- no source ties a specific morris step-pattern to a specific bar of his concert music, and that claim must not be made -- but as a bodily, stepping pulse: the lilting quality repeatedly noted in the songs, the dance-like ritornello between verses, and a collecting practice done on foot, in company, by ear and by body.

**Takeaway:** he danced the material before he wrote it -- three years in Sharp's demonstration team, a professional morris dancer, filmed in 1912 -- and the step is in the music as pulse, not as quotation. [C34753]

## 6. The Times, Radley, and the RCM: three jobs that did not hold

He left Oxford in July 1908 and spent about a year as a music critic for The Times, renting rooms in Bloomsbury, and contributing articles on living composers to Grove's Dictionary of Music and Musicians; he grew disillusioned with criticism. One source alone claims he wrote for The Times "under the name J. A. Fuller Maitland" -- Maitland was the paper's senior chief critic, and the claim as phrased is implausible and unsupported elsewhere; it is not repeated here. From September 1909 to July 1910 he was assistant music master at Radley College, Abingdon, in charge of piano tuition, founding a choir, helping with sport; he left after one year. From September 1910 he spent about a year at the Royal College of Music, studying organ with Sir Walter Parratt (first study), piano with Herbert Sharpe, and harmony and theory with Charles Wood -- Wikipedia adds that he "worked with Hubert Parry among others", though Parry was the College's director and whether he taught Butterworth directly is unclear; the named tutors are Parratt, Sharpe and Wood. He found the RCM limiting, frustrating, "far too dominated by Germanic disciplines", and left dissatisfied, depressed and lost. One source alone says he considered a BMus at Oxford in 1910 and passed the first of three required exams; no other source mentions it, and it is carried as single-sourced and unverified. By 1911 the day jobs had all fallen away; the centre of gravity became fieldwork, dance demonstration and composition, funded precariously.

**Takeaway:** three institutions in three years, and none of them held him -- the critic, the schoolmaster and the conservatoire student all gave way to the collector, the dancer and the composer, working outside every institution that tried to employ him. [C34754]

## 7. The mother's death and the creative burst

Julia Butterworth died in January 1911, aged 61, of Hodgkin's disease -- the disease is stated only by the York Civic Trust, and the exact day in January is not established in any source read. Her death, the biographers agree, plunged him into a very creative period: the eleven Housman songs were written in 1910-11, the Rhapsody completed in 1911, the Two English Idylls in 1910-11, the Sussex arrangements prefaced in June 1912, Love Blows as the Wind Blows in 1911-12. The grief is not a theory imposed from outside; it is the chronology. Requiescat, his March 1911 setting of Oscar Wilde's epitaph for a young girl, was written two months after her death, in F minor, with bars that modulate to remote sharp keys at "I vex my heart alone, She is at rest" -- a song Barlow, after Pirie, calls almost Finzi-like in its word-setting. The burst lasted three years, 1911 to 1914, and produced everything he is remembered by.

**Takeaway:** the mother dies in January 1911 and the work pours out -- the Housman songs, the idylls, the Rhapsody, all of it in three years, and then the war ends it. [C34755]

## 8. The eleven Housman songs: one cycle, two books, no settled order

Between 1909 and 1911 Butterworth set eleven of the sixty-three poems in A. E. Housman's A Shropshire Lad (1896), intending one narrative cycle, and then split them for publication into Six Songs from A Shropshire Lad (Augener, 1911) and Bredon Hill and Other Songs (Augener, 1912). He never settled a preferred order: the Eton manuscript order, the 16 May 1911 premiere order and the published order are all different. The songs are dedicated to Victor Annesley Barrington-Kennett, an Eton and Oxford friend who also died in France in 1916. He used no known folk tunes in the songs -- with one possible exception: "When I Was One and Twenty" was said to be based on a folk tune that has defied identification, and no source tune has ever been named; "Through Moorfields" has been claimed, not established. The rest are his own invention in a folk-like idiom, which is exactly the point: the melodies sound like folk song because he had absorbed the idiom from the field, not because he quoted it. Three of the eleven songs have no accidental at all -- "Is My Team Ploughing?" among them -- and the piano is at times tacet, at other times just the odd note to keep the sense of form. The songs run about thirteen to fifteen minutes a set, and they are among the most frequently performed English art songs a century later.

**Takeaway:** eleven poems from one book, split into two sets, in no settled order -- a cycle that was never published as a cycle, written in a folk idiom with almost no folk tunes in it, and spare to the point where the piano sometimes stops altogether. [C34756]

## 9. 16 May 1911: nine songs, Boult, and the Oxford premiere

Nine of the songs -- not all eleven -- were first performed on Tuesday 16 May 1911 at the Oxford University Musical Club, sung by the baritone James Campbell McInnes with Butterworth at the piano, the concert organised by Adrian Boult. "The Lads in Their Hundreds" was not performed that night, and "On the Idle Hill of Summer" was not yet finished (it was completed later at Cheyne Gardens, London). Shortly after, Boult sang several of the songs himself at a private function. The London premiere of the Six Songs followed on 20 June 1911 at the Aeolian Hall, McInnes with Hamilton Harty, where "The Lads in Their Hundreds" was encored -- presumably its first performance. The encyclopedia.com works list gives "London, June 20, 1911" for the Six Songs without distinguishing the events; the two are separate performances, Oxford first and London second, and the Oxford date is the premiere. The doorway is in this room: the twenty-two-year-old Boult, who had entered Christ Church in 1908, Butterworth's final year, organising the first hearing of his friend's songs -- three years before his own professional debut would premiere Butterworth's orchestral idyll.

**Takeaway:** the first hearing is nine songs, not eleven, in Oxford, organised by Boult -- the doorway tie is a concert, a date, and a piano, not a vague friendship. [C34757]

## 10. Two English Idylls: the first orchestral works

Butterworth's first orchestral works were the Two English Idylls of 1910-11, premiered at the Oxford University Musical Club on Thursday 8 February 1912, conducted by Hugh Allen. Number 1, Allegro scherzando, uses "Dabbling in the Dew" (opening oboe), "Just as the Tide was Flowing" and "Henry Martin" -- all collected in 1907; Number 2, Adagio non troppo, uses "Phoebe and her dark-eyed sailor" and is more serious in conception. Each lasts about four and a half minutes. John France, reviewing the BIS disc, calls them "one work and not two". The second Idyll's tune also appears, in Jekyll's version, in "Roving in the Dew" -- the collecting and the composing cross-referencing each other. Carlos Kleiber programmed Idyll Number 1 with the Chicago Symphony Orchestra in 1978, and a circulating recording of it is dated 2 June 1983; the appearance and the recording are different events and must not be merged. The idylls already show the shape that governs all three of his orchestral idylls: founded on Sussex folk melodies collected in 1907, an arch shape, four and a half to six minutes long.

**Takeaway:** the first orchestral works are already the whole method -- collected tunes as building blocks, a short arch, and a premiere in Oxford under Hugh Allen. [C34758]

## 11. A Shropshire Lad, Rhapsody: the epilogue that contains the whole

The Rhapsody, A Shropshire Lad, was begun in 1909 or 1910 and completed in 1911 -- not 1912, as IMSLP and some disc notes have it; 1911 is the better-attested completion date. Butterworth called it an "orchestral epilogue" to the eleven songs, to "express the home-thoughts of the exiled Shropshire Lad". It was first called The Land of Lost Content, then The Cherry Tree, before its final title. It is in A minor, about ten minutes, for a full orchestra -- two flutes, two oboes, cor anglais, two clarinets, bass clarinet, two bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones, tuba, timpani, harp, strings -- and it was first performed at the Leeds Festival on Thursday 2 October 1913, the London Symphony Orchestra under Arthur Nikisch, with Butterworth and Vaughan Williams present and Elgar possibly in the audience. Its material is radically economical: "Loveliest of Trees, the cherry now" -- the first of the eleven songs -- provides the melodic material for the entire piece, except for a plaintive quote from "With Rue My Heart Is Laden" (the last of the eleven) in the flute at the conclusion, and a final allusion back to the opening of "Loveliest of Trees", as if the cycles of life, love and death have come full circle and are poised to begin again. Though it has a decided flavour of English folk song, no folk melodies were actually used. Andrew Clements's verdict, quoted in the BIS booklet, calls it "his masterpiece, and one of the greatest of all English orchestral works"; the Dictionary of National Biography of 1927 agrees it is his masterpiece. It was influential on Vaughan Williams (A Pastoral Symphony), Gerald Finzi (A Severn Rhapsody) and Ernest Moeran (First Rhapsody) -- influence running forward from Butterworth, not into him. The London premiere was at the Queen's Hall on 20 March 1914, conducted by Geoffrey Toye -- the same date as the London premiere of The Banks of Green Willow, and the two get conflated in some disc notes.

**Takeaway:** a ten-minute orchestral work built from the first song, closed by the last song, and then glancing back at the first -- the whole cycle folded into a single epilogue, with no folk tune in it at all. [C34759]

## 12. The Banks of Green Willow: the ballad, the tunes, the idyll

The Banks of Green Willow, an idyll for orchestra composed in 1913, is the most played and most recorded of his orchestral works: A major, about six minutes, for a small orchestra of two flutes, two oboes, two clarinets, two bassoons, two horns, one trumpet, harp and strings, published by Stainer & Bell in 1914. Butterworth called it a "musical illustration to the ballad of the same name". The ballad itself is brutal: a farmer's daughter, pregnant by a sea-captain, gives birth aboard ship, asks to be thrown overboard with her baby, and dies; the Cranstones' version is the milder one. The piece is built from just three melodies -- the title tune, taken from Mr and Mrs Cranstone of Billingshurst in June 1907; "Green Bushes", noted in versions from at least ten different singers, the closest to Ned Harding of Lower Beeding in June 1907, though the tune as it appears in the idyll is not any of them; and one original theme of Butterworth's own, a horn theme. A variant of the title tune heard a few bars from the end comes from yet another singer, David Clements, recorded on phonograph in a Basingstoke workhouse in 1909. The shape is the arch: solo clarinet and strings set the pastoral scene with the title theme, a short development and restatement, then a sombre, agitated turn as Butterworth's own horn theme enters, an animated motif driving to a main climax "surprisingly passionate for such a short work", then the music subsides, "Green Bushes" enters hesitantly on oboe, is repeated gently on flute with harp, and the piece ends tranquilly with snatches of the variant title theme on solo violin, horn and oboe. It was premiered at West Kirby Public Hall on Friday 27 February 1914, combined Halle and Liverpool Philharmonic players, conducted by the twenty-four-year-old Adrian Boult -- Boult's first professional concert, on a programme that also included the British premiere of Wolf's Italian Serenade. The London premiere followed three weeks later, the Queen's Hall Orchestra under Geoffrey Toye -- the last occasion Butterworth heard his own music. Some disc notes give "first performed at the Queen's Hall, London on March 20th, 1914"; that is the London premiere, not the premiere.

**Takeaway:** three melodies -- two collected, one invented -- and a drowning ballad under a tranquil surface: the piece is a composite, not a quotation, and its premiere is the doorway of this whole study. [C34760]

## 13. Love Blows as the Wind Blows: Henley and the last music

Love Blows as the Wind Blows sets four poems by W. E. Henley from the Book of Verses (1888): "In the Year That's Come and Gone", "Life in Her Creaking Shoes", "Fill a Glass with Golden Wine", and "On the Way to Kew". The original version, voice with piano or string quartet, dates from 1911-12; the orchestral version of 1914 -- the only song cycle Butterworth orchestrated himself -- has only three songs (the third was never orchestrated) and a much-altered vocal line in the opening song, and it was the last music he worked on before France. It was not performed in his lifetime: the first known performance was at the Royal College of Music memorial concert on 3 June 1918, Frederick Grisewood (a student, later the BBC's first announcer) with the English String Quartet. It was published in 1921, Vaughan Williams having made the piano reductions; the single published vocal score awkwardly combines both versions. The orchestral version shows Vaughan Williams's style and a familiarity with Wagner, Elgar and Debussy, and a delicacy that brings to mind Debussy or Ibert. The opening song begins with a solitary horn call fading into clarinet ripples; the last closes with an oboe remembering Richmond and Kew.

**Takeaway:** the last music he worked on is a Henley cycle nobody heard while he lived -- orchestrated in 1914, first performed in 1918 at a memorial concert, and the only cycle he orchestrated himself. [C34761]

## 14. Folk Songs from Sussex and the Sharp publications

In 1912 Butterworth prefaced Folk Songs from Sussex -- eleven Sussex folk songs with piano accompaniment, the tunes "printed exactly as they were sung, without 'improvement'", the words edited as little as possible -- and it was published by Augener in 1913. The preface is dated 30 June 1912, written from 19 Cheyne Gardens; the arrangements themselves mostly date from 1906-09, and Francis Jekyll noted four of the eleven. It is not Vaughan Williams's Folk Songs from Sussex (Novello, 1912, fifteen songs collected by W. Percy Merrick) -- same title, same year, different set, and the two must not be confused. With Cecil Sharp he published The Country Dance Book parts 3 (1912) and 4 (1916) -- the fourth part issued after his death, a posthumous publication, not a 1916 collaboration in person -- Morris Dance Tunes sets 9 and 10 (1914), not "Books 8 & 9" as Wikipedia's list has it, and The Morris Book part 5 (1913). The dance books are the one part of his field collection published in full in his lifetime; the songs survived as field notes, words divorced from tunes, fitted back together only by a later editor.

**Takeaway:** the fieldwork reached print through Sharp -- dance books in his lifetime, the Sussex songs prefaced in 1912 and published in 1913, and the rest left as notebooks for someone else to reunite. [C34762]

## 15. The Vaughan Williams hinge: "You ought to write a symphony"

The closest tie in Butterworth's life is Ralph Vaughan Williams. They were friends from 1906, collected together, and after 1910 were neighbours in Chelsea. Vaughan Williams had a symphonic poem in progress; Butterworth told him, in his "gruff, abrupt manner", "You know, you ought to write a symphony" -- the prompting that turned it into A London Symphony, in Vaughan Williams's own recollection, quoted in Wikipedia and the York Civic Trust page. Vaughan Williams showed him the sketches "bit by bit" and praised his "wonderful power of criticism of other men's work". The influence ran both ways, and this study does not make Butterworth the pupil: the Rhapsody was influential on Vaughan Williams's Pastoral Symphony, and Vaughan Williams dedicated the printed edition of A London Symphony (1920) to Butterworth's memory. Butterworth left almost all his manuscripts to Vaughan Williams, who kept them until his own death; after Ursula Vaughan Williams's death the original works went to the Bodleian Library, Oxford, and the folk-song collection to the English Folk Dance and Song Society and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library. The two sources as consulted do not resolve the manuscript locations cleanly -- one page puts the remaining manuscripts in the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library -- but the Bodleian location for the compositions is the better-attested one, and the split is carried.

**Takeaway:** the hinge of both men's music is a single blunt sentence -- "You know, you ought to write a symphony" -- and the critic who said it became the dedicatee of the symphony it made. [C34763]

## 16. The London Symphony reconstruction

When the manuscript of A London Symphony was lost -- sent to Germany just before the war, for engraving or to the conductor Fritz Busch -- Butterworth, with Geoffrey Toye and the critic Edward J. Dent, helped Vaughan Williams reconstruct it. Toye had enlisted with Butterworth in August 1914 as a private in the Duke of Cornwall's Light Infantry, and Dent's part in the reconstruction is the one dated joint act between him and Butterworth; no wider friendship is evidenced in the sources read. The reconstruction is a small, exact image of what Butterworth was to Vaughan Williams: not a co-composer, but the man with the ear and the memory who could put the lost thing back together, bit by bit, as he had criticised it bit by bit. The printed edition of 1920 was dedicated to his memory, and the first performance had been in 1914.

**Takeaway:** he rebuilt his friend's symphony from memory and sketches after its manuscript was lost -- the critic as restorer, and the last joint work before the war took him. [C34764]

## 17. What he destroyed -- and what survived

Before leaving for France, Butterworth destroyed many works he did not care for, in case he did not return and have the chance to revise them. The timing, the scale and the motive are stated differently by different sources, and no source read quotes Butterworth himself giving a reason -- so this study does not present a single clean version. Wikipedia, after Barlow, says "before and during the war"; Military History Matters says before the outbreak of the war; the war-epitaph research, after Murphy, says in August 1915, before he went to the front, he destroyed "virtually every piece of his unpublished work", on embarkation leave; the BIS booklet instead places the destruction "when home on leave", during service. The honest account is that he destroyed manuscripts on at least one occasion before or during service, most plausibly on embarkation leave in August 1915, of works he judged inferior or unfinished. Two different destructions are probably being conflated: the Eton works -- violin sonata, string quartet, Barcarolle -- may have been destroyed much earlier, as routine self-criticism, while the 1915 purge is the dated event. What is certainly lost: the Eton sonata, quartet and Barcarolle, the Fantasia short score (if it ever existed as a complete document), and an unknown number of unpublished works whose titles we do not know -- which is precisely why the loss matters and why its scale cannot be counted. What survived is in fact substantial: the complete extant list in this study, plus the Fantasia fragment, plus the folk-song field collection. A destruction of "virtually everything unpublished" sits uneasily with the survival of the Suite for String Quartet (about 1910, unpublished in his lifetime), the Fantasia fragment and numerous songs, unless "unpublished" is doing heavy lifting; "many" is preferred, with the certainly-lost named.

**Takeaway:** he destroyed his own work on at least one occasion before France, most plausibly August 1915 -- and the catalogue we have is a survivor's list, selected first by the composer and then by Vaughan Williams. [C34765]

## 18. Enlistment: the private who declined a safe commission

On the outbreak of war on 4 August 1914 Butterworth was at the Stratford-upon-Avon Festival with the English Folk Dance Society demonstration team. He immediately tried to join up, was turned away amid the rush of volunteers, went to London, tried again, and by 2 September 1914 was in the Army. He declined a safe commission offered by a family friend, not wanting to use his social position unfairly, and enlisted as a private in the Duke of Cornwall's Light Infantry, alongside friends including Geoffrey Toye and R. O. Morris. He was commissioned in the first week of October 1914 as a subaltern in the Durham Light Infantry, later temporary lieutenant, serving in the Army as "G. S. Kaye-Butterworth". Which battalion is unresolved: Wikipedia gives the 13th Battalion, Durham Light Infantry, in the 23rd Division; the war-epitaph research gives the 17th Battalion arriving in France on 26 August 1915. No battalion number is asserted here. He trained at Aldershot through the first half of 1915, and his battalion arrived in France on Thursday 26 August 1915. His diary's first twenty-four hours in the trenches are dated 11 September 1915; the first casualties came on 22 October. He collected some folk songs from the Durham miners under his command -- the collecting did not stop at the front, even as the composing did. There is reasonable evidence, in Wikipedia's words after Barlow, that he had put composition behind him by the time he went to France, and it is by no means certain he would have resumed it had he returned -- but that is a qualified scholarly judgement, not a documented decision, and his diary's silence on music is suggestive, not conclusive. The counterweight is the Fantasia fragment, which shows he was still sketching in the summer of 1914.

**Takeaway:** he refused the safe commission and enlisted as a private -- the same fastidiousness that destroyed his manuscripts, applied to his own life, refusing to use his position unfairly. [C34766]

## 19. France: the miners, the diary, the silence on music

His platoon and company were largely County Durham miners, and his letters are full of admiration for them; he came to have enormous respect for them, and the class levelling of the trenches is a recurring theme in all the war sources. He was at first in a quiet sector. He was mentioned in dispatches early in July 1916 and recommended for the Military Cross for Bailiff Wood on 9 July, which was not awarded. A small shell fragment hit him in the back in late July 1916 -- "a slight scratch, which I had dressed", reported in his last letter home on 29 July -- not, as Wikipedia's account of the mid-July action implies, wounded on 16-17 July; the Military Cross citation's wounding refers to his captain, not to him. He was home on leave in January 1916 (his father's wedding) and again in early June 1916 (a signalling course). The diary makes no mention of music at all in the trenches. On 1 August his company dug a forward trench toward Munster Alley, within two hundred yards of the German front line; the men named it Butterworth Trench in his honour, and it appeared on all the official maps.

**Takeaway:** in France he collected songs from his miners and wrote nothing -- the collector kept working and the composer went silent, and the trench his men dug was named for him before he died in it. [C34767]

## 20. The Military Cross: once, not twice

Butterworth was awarded the Military Cross for commanding his company "with great ability and coolness" after his captain had been wounded, in continuous action in the Pozieres fighting of mid-July 1916 -- Wikipedia dates the action 16-17 July, the war histories 17-19 July, and the exact days are not settled here; the citation itself, in the London Gazette of 25 August 1916, gives no dates. The citation reads: "For conspicuous gallantry in action. After his captain had been wounded, Butterworth commanded his company with great ability and coolness, and with his energy and utter disregard of danger he set a fine example on the front line. His name had previously been brought to notice for good and gallant work." The award was gazetted on Friday 25 August 1916 -- after his death; he never received it. It has been incorrectly stated that he won the Military Cross twice: the Military Cross was not awarded posthumously at the time, so he could never have been awarded it twice. The error comes from Brigadier Page Croft telling his father that Butterworth had "won" the medal again on the night he died -- a recommendation, not an award -- and it is repeated in Phasmid's list of citations and in The Gazette's own article, which compounds it by calling the award a Military Medal for bravery on 5 August 1916. Both are wrong: the award was the Military Cross, an officers' decoration, for the mid-July action, not for the day he died.

**Takeaway:** one Military Cross, gazetted after his death, for the mid-July fighting -- the "second MC" is a recommendation mistaken for an award, and the Military Medal version is wrong on both the medal and the date. [C34768]

## 21. Pozieres: Munster Alley, 4-5 August 1916

On 4 August 1916 the Durham Light Infantry seized a hundred yards of Munster Alley, the German communications trench; the 23rd Division had been ordered to attack it, and in desperate fighting during the night of 4-5 August, despite friendly fire from Australian artillery, Butterworth and his miners captured and held it, with heavy losses. At about 04:00 on Saturday 5 August he escorted Brigadier-General Henry Page Croft, commander of the 68th Infantry Brigade, on a tour of the newly captured trench; at about 04:45 -- or, in Croft's own letter, within a minute of Croft leaving him -- he was shot through the head by a German sniper. He was thirty-one. Croft's letter to Sir Alexander called him "a brilliant musician in times of peace, and an equally brilliant soldier in times of stress"; Croft had not known Butterworth was a composer of note until after his death. Hugh Allen's verdict: "When George Butterworth was killed in action on August 5 1916 English music was robbed of one of its most promising composers." Parry railed against the "indiscriminate carnage"; E. J. Dent wrote: "His death is the greatest loss that our modern music has suffered." Vaughan Williams wrote to Holst in October 1916: "I sometimes dread coming back to normal life with so many gaps, especially, of course, George Butterworth."

**Takeaway:** he held the trench his men had named for him, walked it with his brigadier at four in the morning, and was shot by a sniper a minute after the brigadier left him. [C34769]

## 22. The body that was never found: Thiepval and the Pozieres Memorial

Butterworth's body was never recovered. He was buried hastily by his men in the side of the trench, where he fell, about thirty yards behind the front line; an exact map reference was given to his father, but by the end of the war no trace of the grave or body remained. His name is on the Thiepval Memorial to the Missing, which carries 72,195 names. Wikipedia and the York Civic Trust add that his unidentified remains "may well lie" or "might have been interred" at the nearby Pozieres Memorial, a Commonwealth War Graves Commission cemetery -- a different place from Thiepval, and the two must not be conflated: Thiepval is where his name is recorded; the Pozieres Memorial cemetery is where his unidentified remains may lie. Sir Alexander erected a plaque at St Mary's Priory Church, Deerhurst, Gloucestershire, for George and his nephew Hugh Butterworth (killed at Loos in 1915); the composer's grandfather, the Reverend George Butterworth, had been vicar there. Sir Alexander privately printed the George Butterworth Memorial Volume in 1918. Butterworth's name is one of thirty-eight on the Royal College of Music war memorial. A lane at Pozieres is the Chemin George Butterworth; there is a Butterworth Close in Newport and a Butterworth Road in Tiruchirappalli, India. A blue plaque was unveiled at the York family home in 2016.

**Takeaway:** no grave, only a name on the Thiepval Memorial and a possible unidentified burial at Pozieres -- the man who destroyed his own manuscripts left no body either, only the work that survived him. [C34770]

## 23. What he did not do, and what is not his

Four corrections belong in the study itself, because the errors are the ones a careless account repeats. First, he did not meet A. E. Housman: the eleven poems shaped his best-known work, but no source records a meeting, a letter or a conversation between them. Housman made it a principle never to refuse permission for settings, and Butterworth's permission route ran through the publisher Grant Richards -- a surviving letter, Butterworth to Grant Richards, 25 April 1911, asks permission to publish poems from A Shropshire Lad. Housman disliked listening to settings of his poems and found performances embarrassing, and forbade reproduction of texts in programmes. The debt is one-way, and the study does not imply a friendship. Second, he had no documented personal tie to Gustav Holst, Arnold Bax or Henry Balfour Gardiner: same generation, same movement, shared friends and teachers, but nothing personal found in any source read -- and Balfour Gardiner's self-financed Queen's Hall concerts of 1912-13, which promoted Bax, Holst, Grainger, Quilter, Cyril Scott and Norman O'Neill, did not include Butterworth at all. Third, the Orchestral Fantasia is not a lost completed work, and neither completion is his: begun in the summer of 1914 and left incomplete at enlistment, it survives as ninety-two or ninety-three bars of full score, about three and a half minutes, headed "see short score" -- the short score is lost -- and the two modern completions, Martin Yates's at about sixteen and a half minutes and Kriss Russman's at about eight and a half, disagree with each other by a factor of two in length. Neither may stand for the piece, and neither duration may be cited as "the piece". Fourth, he has no opus numbers: the "IGB" numbers on IMSLP are IMSLP internal references, not opus numbers, and "Op." must never be cited for him. The orchestrations of the Six Songs by Lance Baker and Kriss Russman, and of all eleven songs by Phillip Brookes, are arrangements, not Butterworth's works.

**Takeaway:** the corrections are the study -- no meeting with Housman, no tie to Holst or Bax or Balfour Gardiner, no completed Fantasia, no opus numbers -- and a careful account strips these away rather than repeating them. [C34771]

## 24. Mechanism: variant synthesis -- a tune that is none of its sources

Butterworth did not quote folk tunes verbatim. He collected many versions of the same tune and then made a new one out of their shared features. Versions of "Green Bushes" were noted from at least ten different singers, "though the tune as it appears in the Idyll is not any of them. Each use of each tune varies slightly, and it is likely that Butterworth created new variants based on features of all the various versions he collected." The "Green Bushes" in the piece most closely resembles Ned Harding's, from Lower Beeding, Sussex, in June 1907 -- but is not his either. The Banks of Green Willow is built from just three melodies, and every return of each tune is slightly different. The watchable translation is a single melodic line, a drawn curve, that is never drawn twice the same: keep eight to twelve stored variants of one contour, each a small perturbation of the same arch, and each cycle draw a new variant blended from several stored ones -- the rise from variant A, the peak from variant B, the fall from variant C. The viewer should feel "same tune, never the same drawing": the contour is recognisable, the exact path is not. Keep one hue family across variants so the identity reads as shape, not colour. Watch-only; the variation is the system.

**Takeaway:** the tune is a composite of ten singers and none of them -- the mechanism is synthesis, not quotation, and the piece must vary the same contour every time without ever repeating it exactly. [C34772]

## 25. Mechanism: the arch -- calm, swell, one crest, subsidence

All three orchestral idylls share the same shape: founded on folk melodies Butterworth collected in Sussex in 1907, each has a similar "arch" shape, and each lasts between four and a half and six minutes. The Banks of Green Willow opens in pastoral calm, swells through a sombre, agitated turn, reaches a single main climax "surprisingly passionate for such a short work", subsides as "Green Bushes" enters hesitantly on oboe, and ends tranquilly with fragments on solo violin, horn and oboe. The Rhapsody follows the same arch in a larger frame: it opens Moderato, molto tranquillo e senza rigore in A minor, moves to a full-orchestra passage in E-flat major, and returns to the A-minor theme with a new solo-flute subject. Rise, crest, return -- never a straight line, never a second crest. The watchable translation is one arch per run: forms start sparse and low, a single line, pale, near the bottom, thicken and rise toward a single dense, saturated crest at about two-thirds, then thin, slow and settle -- the last minute is fragments only, drifting down. The crest must be genuinely the loudest and densest moment; the piece must not re-peak afterwards. Muted greens and greys rising to one warm flare, then cooling again. Watch-only.

**Takeaway:** one arch, one crest, no second peak -- the whole piece is a hill climbed once, and the ending is fragments drifting down. [C34773]

## 26. Mechanism: economy of means -- the superfluous note removed

The defining critical consensus on Butterworth is spareness. Hyperion, on the songs: "A characteristic of the songs is their economy of means", shown in "The Lads in Their Hundreds" by "its lilting melody, piano ritornello between verses derived from it and spare harmony." A. V. Butcher, in Music & Letters in 1948: "A most noticeable characteristic of Butterworth's writing is his simplicity and his restraint. There are few accidentals, few extravagant chords; indeed, three of the eleven songs have no accidental at all." Adrian Boult, on the Rhapsody, "said that its perfection eliminated every superfluous note." In "Loveliest of Trees" the piano is at times tacet, "at other times just the odd note to keep the sense of form". The orchestral equivalent is the small Banks orchestra -- two flutes, two oboes, two clarinets, two bassoons, two horns, one trumpet, harp, strings -- and, in the Rhapsody, orchestration "for the most part, very understated, beginning with just hints at the main subject, suggested by violas and clarinets". The watchable translation is a subtraction piece: start with the full field and remove until only the load-bearing elements remain -- one line, one ground, one accent. Nothing decorative may survive a second look. Motion is slow and unhurried because there is so little to move; when something does move, it is the whole event. Two or three colours at most, large areas of empty ground. This is a discipline mechanism for the prompt-writer as much as for the piece: if a proposed element cannot be justified as the melody, the ground, or the single accent, cut it. Watch-only.

**Takeaway:** the perfection is in what was removed -- Boult's phrase, "eliminated every superfluous note", is the whole method, and the piece must be built by subtraction. [C34774]

## 27. Mechanism: the tune handed from solo voice to solo voice

Butterworth's orchestration is a relay, not a tutti. In The Banks of Green Willow a solo clarinet and strings create the pastoral scene with the title theme; "Green Bushes" enters hesitantly on oboe, is repeated gently on flute accompanied by harp, and the piece ends tranquilly with snatches of the variant title theme on violin solo, horn and oboe. The Rhapsody ends the same way: "a solo violin ascending skywards, strings whispering and a flute adding a gentle farewell". One instrument speaks at a time; the rest shadow, whisper, or fall silent; the melody is never doubled by the whole orchestra at once except at the single climax. The watchable translation is one drawn line at a time, passed between distinct voices -- a thin pale line (clarinet), then the same contour redrawn as a slightly thicker, warmer line (oboe), then a fine bright line (flute), then a single trembling thread (solo violin). Each voice keeps the same contour but changes weight, colour and texture. The ground stays almost empty while a voice speaks; other voices only shadow it faintly. Motion: slow, unhurried hand-offs; the eye follows the single moving line. Watch-only.

**Takeaway:** the melody is passed hand to hand, never shouted by everyone at once -- one voice speaks, the rest whisper, and the eye follows the single moving line. [C34775]

## 28. Mechanism: two voices in one frame -- the dialogue of the quick and the dead

"Is My Team Ploughing?" stages a conversation between a dead man's ghost and his living friend, and Butterworth gives each a different musical identity. Hyperion: "a conversation between the quick and the dead with melody and harmony that are heart-rending in effect... The poignant falling sequence of bare chords uncannily suggests the cold of the grave; by comparison the chords underpinning his friend's answers course with life. After the chilling last response... the chords of the ghost fade to end the song in utter bleakness." The mechanics, from the Hoflich preface: the dead man's verses are always pianissimo and supported by long, descending secondary chords, apparently taken from Grieg's String Quartet; the living friend's replies are a lower vocal line, quicker and louder than before, with long common chords. Butcher adds that the same tune serves four times over, "except that the last line of all, 'Never ask me whose', by its mere difference of one note seems to leave the dead man's question unanswered." The song has no accidentals at all. The watchable translation is two forms sharing one contour but living in opposite states, alternating: one pale, slow, descending, barely there -- the ghost, thin, cold blue-grey, falling; one lower, quicker, warmer and denser -- the living, dark, earthy, pulsing. They take turns occupying the frame; they never overlap. At the end, the living form answers -- and one small element is wrong by a single step, a note, colour or position shifted by one increment, and the ghost form fades without resolving. The power is in the alternation and the single final difference, not in complexity. Watch-only; the dialogue is the motion.

**Takeaway:** two voices, one tune, opposite states -- and the whole song turns on a single changed note at the very end. [C34776]

## 29. Mechanism: strophic return with a tiny difference

Butterworth repeats the same strophe and changes almost nothing -- and the almost-nothing is the point. In "When I Was One-and-Twenty", the young man's bitter realisation "is brilliantly emphasised by a mere one-bar extension of the tune at the conclusion of the song". In "Is My Team Ploughing?", the same tune four times over, changed by one note at the very end. In "The Lads in Their Hundreds", a piano ritornello between verses is derived from the lilting melody -- the same material returning as a frame between verses. In The Banks of Green Willow, each use of each tune varies slightly. The watchable translation is a loop that is almost a loop: a form repeats its path three or four times with near-identity -- same route, same speed, same colour -- and on the final pass one thing changes: the path extends by one small segment, one vertex moves by a single step, one colour shifts by one shade. The piece's whole drama is that single difference, visible only because the repetitions trained the eye. This is the strongest Butterworth mechanism for a generative loop piece, and it directly forbids the "random every time" default: variation must be counted -- one change, in one place, at the end -- not sprayed. Watch-only.

**Takeaway:** repeat, repeat, repeat -- then one bar longer, one note different: the change is visible only because everything else stayed the same. [C34777]

## 30. Mechanism: bells -- oscillation that turns into a toll

In "Bredon Hill", one sound-image unifies the whole song and changes its meaning halfway through. Hyperion: "Musical images of bells, from joyous pealing to funereal tolling, permeate and unify the setting of 'Bredon Hill'. The clangour is set in motion by the oscillating chords at the beginning, and the scales in the accompaniment... for the first four verses radiate happiness. However, at the beginning of the fifth verse the change is stark and wintry, the doleful chords like a single bell tolling the coffin to the grave. At the end, there is a recall of the main bell motif, but now tainted with the experience of death and loss." The same oscillating figure, first as pealing (many, bright, fast) and then as tolling (one, slow, low): the material does not change; its state changes. The watchable translation is an oscillating field -- a set of forms swinging or pulsing in phase, bright and quick -- that, at a single marked moment, collapses to a single slow toll: one form, striking once, low and heavy, with a long decay. Then a faint recall of the oscillation, but slower and dimmer, "tainted" -- the same motion at half speed, in a colder colour. The mechanism is the phase change of one image, not two different images. Motion: oscillation, then a single pulse, then a dimmed echo. Colour: bright gold and white peal cooling to iron grey. Watch-only.

**Takeaway:** the same bells, first pealing and then tolling -- the material never changes, only its state, and the piece turns on that single phase change. [C34778]

## 31. Mechanism: transience counted -- the petal that falls, the springs that run out

"Loveliest of Trees" is a counting song: the lad is twenty, threescore years and ten are allotted, twenty will not come again, fifty springs remain -- "and since to look at things in bloom / Fifty springs are little room, / About the woodlands I will go / To see the cherry hung with snow." Hyperion: the song's opening is "a brief, magical descending phrase for piano, which seems to encapsulate both the delicacy and transience of the blossom and, by extension, of life itself." The vocal line "starts on a high E, hanging like a petal and then gently falling to the ground." The Rhapsody carries the same counting into orchestral terms: the solo violin "ascending skywards" at the end is the same transience, looked at from below. The watchable translation is a finite-count piece: a fixed number of petals, blossoms or white marks -- fifty is the evidenced number, use it -- that fall, one by one, from a branch-line across the top of the frame, each drifting down at its own speed and fading where it lands. The piece ends when the count runs out -- visibly, the branch is bare. The falling must be gentle, unhurried, and unrepeatable within a run: no petal is re-used. Colour: white blossom on a pale green-grey ground, the ground slowly accumulating the fallen white. Watch-only; the count is the structure, and the ending is the point.

**Takeaway:** fifty springs, counted down one petal at a time -- the piece ends when the count runs out, and the ending is the point. [C34779]

## 32. Mechanism: bright surface, dark undertow -- the pastoral that is not innocent

The loveliest surfaces in Butterworth carry the darkest stories. The Banks of Green Willow is his own "musical illustration to the ballad of the same name": a farmer's daughter falls in love with a sea-captain, becomes pregnant, runs away with him having stolen money from her parents, and, in the collected versions, drowns or is thrown overboard, to be buried "on the banks of green willow". The piece depicts, in the usual reading, her resting place after the events. The Housman settings work the same way: "Think No More, Lad" is "a fine piece of musical irony with a superficially carefree manner that masks the darker undertones of the poem"; "The Lads in Their Hundreds" is at its simplest and most folk-like precisely where it sings of young men going off to die. Housman's Shropshire is an imagined "land of lost content", shot through with irony and loss. Nothing in the music announces the darkness: the tune stays lilting, the harmony stays spare, the close stays tranquil. The undertow is in the subject, and in the listener's knowledge -- which is why the post-war reception could hear these pre-war pieces as elegies without changing a note. That reception must be handled honestly: the songs (1909-11) and the Rhapsody (1911, performed 1913) predate the First World War, and Housman's poems were written in the shadow of the Second Boer War. The parallel between the poems' death-obsession and Butterworth's death is regularly made, and the Rhapsody "has come to be seen as an elegy before the fact" -- but that is post-war reception, not compositional intent. Do not prompt the pieces as about the Somme. The honest form is: written before the war, heard after it as an elegy. The watchable translation is a serene, pastoral field -- soft greens, slow drifting forms, a tranquil surface -- with one element that does not belong to the idyll and is never explained: a dark form moving slowly beneath the surface, a single cold colour in the palette, a line that sinks instead of rising. Do not illustrate the ballad -- no ship, no drowning figure; the mechanism is the tension between a calm surface and a dark undertow that the viewer senses but cannot name. Watch-only; the viewer should sit with the tension, not resolve it.

**Takeaway:** the surface is tranquil and the undertow is dark, and the piece never explains the tension -- it was written before the war and heard after it as an elegy, and that order matters. [C34780]

## 33. Aesthetic logic: fastidiousness, and the pastoral that is not his word

Butterworth's aesthetic is fastidiousness carried to the point of self-destruction: a composer who revised habitually, who destroyed the works he judged inferior, who wrote eleven songs and four short orchestral pieces and stopped, who left no symphony, no opera, no oratorio, and whose largest orchestral work lasts ten minutes. Vaughan Williams said of him: "no composer whose music expressed his character more exactly... the determination to be and to say exactly what he meant and no other." Hugh Allen's character sketch -- "great shyness with great power, and roughness with a very tender heart" -- is the same portrait in other words: gruff, abrupt, taciturn, plain-spoken, impatient of humbug and pretension, "utterly unable and unwilling to pretend". The music is the character: short, exact, unadorned, saying what it means and no other. Two labels must be handled with care. "Pastoral" is a reception label, not his word: Butterworth called The Banks of Green Willow an "Idyll" and a "musical illustration to the ballad", and the Rhapsody an "orchestral epilogue". "English pastoral style" is how later writing classifies the Rhapsody, and the label is further compromised by the "Cowpat School" mockery of the 1950s and by the fact that the pastoral surface covers a drowning ballad and death-obsessed poems. Use "idyll" and "epilogue" -- his words -- and treat "pastoral" as a label to handle with irony, never as a neutral description. Likewise, no harmonic "system" is evidenced for him: what is evidenced is few accidentals, spare harmony, and modal colour absorbed from collected tunes -- the Dorian mode is evidenced for the single "When I Was One-and-Twenty" tune -- with influences "detected" from Elgar, Sibelius, Vaughan Williams, Debussy and Wagner. "Detected" is a critic's word, not a system. And the influence ran both ways with Vaughan Williams: Butterworth catalysed the London Symphony, and the Rhapsody influenced the Pastoral Symphony. The prompt-writer's rule from this study is: short, exact, and no more than the subject needs; one tune, one arch, one change; the rest is silence.

**Takeaway:** fastidiousness is the aesthetic -- short, exact, saying what it means and no other -- and the words to use are his ("idyll", "epilogue"), not the labels later writing stuck on him. [C34781]
