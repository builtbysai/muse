## 1. The brief: the sketch is the specification

Kazi's whole programme can be stated in one sentence: the user sketches intent in the most direct form available -- a patch, a gesture, a notation, a spoken word -- and the system does the tedious synthesis -- replication, deformation, simulation, code -- while the user stays in control through direct manipulation. What changes across fifteen years is what does the synthesis: hand-built geometry (2014-2016), optimisation and tracking (2017-2021), vision-language models and generative AI (2024-2026). What does not change is the input: a coarse, ambiguous, human mark, never a parameter panel. The design study behind Draco states the grievance plainly: professional animators must "convert their artistic vision into physical parameters" and plan the whole shot before starting, and the tedium blocks rapid prototyping. Every Kazi system is an answer to that grievance in a different room: Vignette lets an illustrator draw a fraction of a texture and gesture-fill the rest; Draco lets a drawer sketch three fish and get a school; SKUID lets a drawer record one motion and get seven kinds of exaggeration; DreamSketch lets a designer sketch a bracket loosely and get sixty-four candidate brackets. The sketch is never a picture of the result. It is the instruction for making it. [C35504]

**Takeaway:** The sketch is the specification: a coarse human mark in, a finished system out, and the user never touches a parameter panel.

## 2. Craft first: sand, ink, whiteboard

The PhD years (2011-2013, NUS, advisor Shengdong Zhao) set the pattern by starting from traditional craft, not from software. SandCanvas (CHI 2011, 7-12 May 2011, Vancouver; Honourable Mention; with Chua, Zhao, Davis and Low) is a digital multi-touch medium inspired by sand animation, the performance art of telling stories by manipulating sand with bare hands: it analyses common sand-animation hand gestures, captures the whole contact region between hand and surface rather than a single point, and then goes beyond physical sand with colour and textured backgrounds, undo, recording of performances, and mixing sand animation with video. Vignette (CHI 2012, 5-10 May 2012, Austin, pp. 1727-1736; with Igarashi, Zhao and Davis; designed during the summer 2011 JST ERATO visit) is a sketch-based tool for pen-and-ink illustration with built-in texture synthesis: the user draws a fraction of a texture -- a few example strokes, leaves, scales, hatching -- and uses a freeform gesture to fill a region with that texture automatically, in 1D and 2D with stitching, preserving the illustrator's own style where Photoshop pattern brushes and copy-paste lose control of density, variation and deformation. SketchStory (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, Vol. 19, No. 12, December 2013, pp. 2416-2425; Lee lead, with Kazi and Greg Smith of Microsoft Research) is a data-enabled digital whiteboard for telling stories with data in the style of whiteboard animation: the presenter sketches an example icon and a chart axis, the system recognises a small set of sketch gestures ('L' invokes an x-y chart, a circle invokes a pie chart) and completes the chart by synthesising visuals from the presenter's icon and binding them to the underlying data. Vignette's "draw a fraction, gesture-fill the rest" is the direct ancestor of Draco's patch; SketchStory's gesture-invoked chart is the ancestor of Kitty's and DrawTalking's sketch-as-command. [C35505]

**Takeaway:** Start from the craft, not the software: the practitioner's workflow stays, and the computation fills in only what the hand does not want to repeat.

## 3. Kinetic textures: the picture that breathes (Draco, CHI 2014)

The most generative-ready mechanism in the whole corpus, and the core of this study. Draco (Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Zhao, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2014, 26 April - 1 May 2014, Toronto, pp. 351-360; DOI 10.1145/2556288.2556987; Honourable Mention top 5 percent, Best Talk, Best Video; also SIGGRAPH 2014 Posters, Article 5, DOI 10.1145/2614217.2614221) is a sketch-based interface, implemented in Java for tablet and tablet PC, that adds continuous, looping animation to still illustrations -- a medium "between static illustration and videos". A kinetic texture is one animation component that encodes a whole collection of similar objects AND their motion together, and it consists of exactly two things: a patch, a small number of representative objects drawn by the user as the source example, and a set of motion properties at two scales -- global motion, the overall shape and direction of the whole collection, and granular motion, the variation of each individual element. Two types. Emitting textures: objects continuously emanate from a sketched emitter -- a line, emitting perpendicular to it, or a point, emitting in all directions -- and follow a global trajectory guided by a motion-vector field computed from sketched motion paths; each path point contributes a unit motion vector weighted by distance, and three sliders control emission velocity, emission frequency, and cohesion, the magnetism of objects toward the paths (low cohesion gives a uniform spread between paths; high cohesion makes objects cling to the paths, with obstacle-avoidance-like effects). A texture outline removes objects that cross it; masks hide objects inside regions; a motion-profile widget lets the user sketch a curve for scale or velocity along the trajectory, so bubbles grow and decelerate as they leave the emitter. Oscillating textures: a finite collection built by replicating the patch along a sketched brush skeleton (as in Vignette), then sketching a second, target skeleton, with the texture oscillating between the two by sinusoidal interpolation of position and orientation, at a speed set by a slider; granular motion can also be added with no oscillating skeleton at all, for local motion only. Granular motion itself has two kinds only, translation along a small 2D path and pivot rotation about a pivot point, authored by direct manipulation in a patch widget -- drag or rotate one example object and the transformation is recorded and applied to every repeated object -- with velocity (frequency) and phase (synchronisation; at minimum phase all objects move in lockstep, at higher phase they desynchronise). The final trajectory of each object is the factorisation of global motion and granular motion. The user evaluation (8 participants, 4 professional animators) reproduced 5 textures in a mean 7 minutes 40 seconds, all unaided, ease-of-use 4.63 out of 5; the freeform stage produced rain, waterfalls, flocking, ripples, trees, hair, clothes, fire, a scuba diver's legs, and camera-movement effects. The paper's stated design goal is animation that "provide continuous animation effects while preserving the unique timeless nature of still illustrations" -- the picture stays a picture, but it breathes. It transferred: Draco shipped as Autodesk SketchBook Motion, Apple's 2016 iPad App of the Year. [C35506]

**Takeaway:** One drawn thing plus a path becomes a living field, at two time-scales at once -- slow drift of the group, fast jitter of each member -- with visible birth and death in the emitting case and visible phase in the oscillating case.

## 4. The graph that makes things cause things (Kitty, UIST 2014)

Kitty (Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2014, 5-8 October 2014, Honolulu, pp. 395-405; DOI 10.1145/2642918.2647375) builds directly on Draco and extends it in the one direction Draco lacks: interactivity and causality. A dynamic illustration in Kitty consists of visual entities (objects and kinetic textures), parameters (the visual, spatial, temporal and quantitative attributes of those entities), a relational graph defining functional relationships between entities, and interactivity. Nodes are the entities, colour-coded by type (objects, emitting textures, oscillating textures); each node carries named scalar parameters -- for objects, translation, rotation, scale, opacity and ambient speed; for emitting textures, opacity, emission frequency and emission speed; for oscillating textures, opacity and skeleton length; granular velocity is the single local parameter shared by both texture types. Edges are directed and encode a functional relationship between ONE parameter of a source node and ONE parameter of a target node, each edge carrying a one-dimensional function tp = f(sp), default identity, overwritten by directly sketching a new curve in a mapping widget (source on the x-axis, target on the y-axis), with an exploration cursor dragged to preview values live; a mapping can also be loaded from a CSV file. Chain effects combine in sequence: the paper's motivating example is a cook holding an egg over a pot -- dragging the egg down into the pot triggers soup splashes and the cat's eyelids closing, while turning the stove knob increases the fire and the steam -- and the verified chain is knob rotation to fire scale to steam emission frequency, while a second chain, egg translation to splash emission frequency, uses a peaked curve so emission is highest exactly when the egg hits the soup surface. Interactivity is defined at node level: each node can have ONE of its parameters assigned for input control, spatial parameters by direct manipulation (drag the egg, rotate the windmill), non-spatial parameters by a swipe toward or away from the entity. On any input change the graph is traversed breadth-first from the touched node, applying each edge's function, and any edge that would close a cycle is not traversed, which prevents infinite loops while still allowing bi-directional relationships. Note on vocabulary: the paper does NOT use the words "trigger" or "response" as named grammar terms; the verified vocabulary is relational graph, directed edge, functional mapping, chain effect, interactivity. Do not present "trigger/response" as a quotation from the paper. [C35507]

**Takeaway:** Causality made visible and touchable: one drag propagates through a visible chain, and the shape of each link in the chain -- the sketched curve -- is itself the behaviour.

## 5. Seven ways to fall (SKUID, CHI 2016)

SKUID (Kazi, Grossman, Umetani, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2016, 7-12 May 2016, San Jose, pp. 4599-4609; DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858386; also SIGGRAPH 2016 Talks) is the direct sequel to Draco and fixes its admitted limitation: Draco animates collections but its objects are rigid, while classical 2D animation gets its expressiveness from exaggeration. SKUID takes Disney's twelve principles of animation (Thomas and Johnston, "The Illusion of Life") and turns seven of them into independent, composable "motion amplifiers", each a high-level, sketchable control, not a physics simulation: squash and stretch, arcs, anticipation, follow through, secondary motion (the five geometric principles), slow-in and slow-out (temporal), and staging (clarity and communication). Explicitly NOT implemented: solid drawing and appeal (visual design, "not directly associated with motion design"), exaggeration (omitted as "a broad concept that is challenging to characterize"), and straight-ahead and pose-to-pose (handled instead by performance-driven recording); timing has no amplifier of its own and is supported through the motion-profile tool. The workflow: sketch a picture, lasso strokes to make an object, record a translation or rotation motion by directly dragging the object through a radial menu, and the seven amplifier icons appear; tapping one applies it immediately, and amplifiers can be arbitrarily mixed. The shared substrate is purely geometric: the object's bounding box is discretised into a square uniform grid, every amplifier except staging controls the movement and deformation of that grid, and the strokes inside are updated by bilinear interpolation, with shape matching for stability and the grid stiffened near inversion. Each amplifier is mechanically distinct: slow-in and slow-out reads velocity from a sketched bell-shaped profile curve; squash and stretch scales the grid axes volume-preservingly (hs = 1 + vss, vs = 1/hs, vss clamped to [-0.5, 0.5]) from a profile, with a stated limitation that roughly diagonal motion produces almost no squash-and-stretch because the x and y projections are nearly equal; follow-through lets the user sketch rigid and stationary regions, leaving the rest passive to catch up by shape-matched spring-back, with a stiffness slider; arcs moves the rigid points on the object's line of action along the motion path while the rest follows passively; secondary motion parents a child object to the nearest grid point of a parent (a fox's tail lagging and whipping as the fox rotates); anticipation linearly interpolates the line of action toward a sketched target pose before the main motion, volume preserved; staging emits randomised motion trails from trailing-edge grid points when speed exceeds a threshold, with threshold, density and length sliders. A bouncing ball is faked, not simulated: collisions are not handled, and the bounce is set by choosing the squash-and-stretch and slow-in and slow-out profiles appropriately. Two professional animators with 25+ years' experience were interviewed to guide the design; the user study (6 participants, 2 professional animators) rated ease of use 4.16 out of 5. [C35508]

**Takeaway:** Seven named, separable deformation rules, each with an instantly readable visual signature, and a built-in composition rule -- mix at will -- so a piece can let one amplifier dominate and then rotate through the others.

## 6. Wind you can paint (Energy-Brushes, UIST 2016)

Energy-Brushes (Xing, Kazi, Grossman, Wei, Stam, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2016, 16-19 October 2016, Tokyo, pp. 755-766; DOI 10.1145/2984511.2984585; Kazi is second author, first author Jun Xing) is positioned explicitly against Draco: in Draco the strokes are rigid and the velocity field is invariant over time; Energy-Brushes adds time-varying dynamics, where the underlying velocity field changes over time and deforms the neighbouring strokes. It is also positioned against Motion Amplifiers: amplifiers are for active, primary character animation; energy brushes are for passive, secondary effects -- fire, smoke, water, hair, clothes -- governed by external forces. The device it copies is the classical hand-drawn animator's energy stroke (Gilland, "Elemental Magic"): artists sketch gestures that coarsely define underlying forces -- heat ascension, air expansion, water drift -- and the detail emerges from those forces. The framework has three layers. Strokes: the drawn content, each stroke a group of points sampled at a constant 6-pixel distance, each sample carrying position, velocity, appearance and constraint parameters; filled regions are triangulated. Flow particles: the detail layer, each particle a position, a velocity, and a local velocity field with two tunable parameters, radius and strength; design (the shape of the field) is separated from control (radius and strength). Energy brushes: the coarse control layer, a stroke that defines the coarse direction of the forces and continuously emits flow particles that traverse the trajectory from one end to the other; four parameters per brush -- interval, speed, strength, size -- each variable spatially along the brush and temporally over a particle's life, with per-parameter randomness. Each frame, every stroke sample's velocity is the sum of influences from all particles within radius, position advances by tg times v (tg = 0.1 in the implementation), then constraints run and free segments are resampled. Three built-in particle types are verified: wind, a uniform upward field fading outward, f = (1 - r) times up; swirl, a rotational field fading outward, f = (1 - r) times tangential over r, with a direction flip; smoke, a Gaussian-derived up-field built from the gradient, Laplacian and Hessian of a Gaussian, divergence-free like swirl, so no collisions. Custom particles can be designed by sketching directly in a velocity-field canvas, and time-varying particles are keyframed layers with per-keyframe lifetimes -- the paper's example is an explosion particle with three layers specifying different emit directions, whose emit direction changes abruptly as the particle moves, an effect the paper says would be very challenging with static particles. Three stroke constraints define material behaviour: anchor (fixes a sample's position), stretch (pulls adjacent samples toward rest length), rigidity (as-rigid-as-possible deformation, with a strength parameter interpolating between pre- and post-rigid positions). Weak constraints give smoke; strong stretch gives a waving flag. Brushes compose: one brush gives a simple, perfectly repetitive pattern; adding further brushes adds randomness and detail, and their interaction produces emergent phenomena. [C35509]

**Takeaway:** An invisible field, made visible only by what it deforms -- the brush stroke is the weather, and the drawn matter is the leaves that prove it.

## 7. The trace becomes the thing (ChronoFab, CHI 2016)

ChronoFab (Kazi, Grossman, Mogk, Schmidt, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2016, pp. 908-918; DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858138) takes Draco's stylised motion depiction into the physical world: a 3D modelling tool that crafts "motion sculptures", tangible, 3D-printable representations of a 3D animated model, visualising the object's motion with static, transient, ephemeral visuals left behind. It was built by first surveying the art form: the authors catalogued 79 motion sculptures by 47 artists and entities, from art magazines, art history books, film production documentaries and toy figurines, and consulted motion-sculpture artists; historical anchors cited are Etienne-Jules Marey's bronze flying gull, Umberto Boccioni's "Unique Forms of Continuity in Space" (1913), Dennis Hoyt, Olga Ziemska's "Stillness in Motion", and Raphael Perret's "BodyCloud". Four depiction techniques, all from the same inputs -- a trajectory, a time interval, a chosen component (vertex, edge, or whole model): motion lines, geometry sculpted along a sampled trajectory (22 catalogued artifacts by 15 artists; five variations: Cylinder, Tapered, Turbulent, Streak, Rugged; radius, start-bound, end-bound and turbulence-strength sliders); multiple stroboscopic stamps, discrete frozen poses overlapped into one artifact (22 by 12 artists; Plane unscaled and Scaled, with a minimum-scale slider and an interval slider); sweeps, geometry sampled from a 2D curve attached to the object's surface over a time range (19 by 11 artists; Plane, Tapered, Turbulent; the 2D analogue is a long-exposure blur); and particle systems, smoke and fluid trails along the trajectory (8 by 4 artists; particle velocity, size, emission frequency and turbulence strength). Artists routinely combine techniques -- Perret's "BodyCloud" uses stamps plus sweep -- and the paper's own fabricated examples include a creature throwing a tire, a flying plane, a bouncing ball, a fighting action figure, moths around a light, a dancing ballerina, a lamp shade from a moth's flight, and a snake bracelet. Two rules from the survey carry directly into a piece: traces get thinner or more turbulent as they age, and in 16 catalogued examples the motion geometry itself doubles as the support structure. A structural-stability check visualises weak regions for thickening. In user sessions ChronoFab cut authoring time 79 percent versus traditional 3D modelling (4 minutes 25 seconds versus 21 minutes 10 seconds), ease of use 4.5 out of 5. [C35510]

**Takeaway:** Motion does not have to be shown by moving; it can be shown by what the moving thing leaves behind, until the leftover is the whole image.

## 8. The sketch parents a family (DreamSketch, UIST 2017)

DreamSketch (Kazi, Grossman, Cheong, Hashemi, Fitzmaurice, UIST 2017, 22-25 October 2017, Quebec City, pp. 401-414; DOI 10.1145/3126594.3126662) is the fabrication-era counterpart to Draco: where Draco adds continuous ambient motion to a still drawing, DreamSketch adds generative multiplicity to a rough sketch. The user coarsely sketches the design context -- a chair's surroundings, a bracket's load points, a manikin for scale -- then a generative-design algorithm (topology optimisation) produces multiple 3D solutions augmented into the sketched scene. The framing quote is Buxton: "If you want to get the most out of a sketch, you need to leave big enough holes. Ambiguity creates the holes." The problem definition is the mechanism: the user draws on a planar 2D canvas that can be positioned and rotated in 3D, extrudes the longest stroke into proxy geometry, and declares objects as interface objects (defining load cases, integrated into the solution) or obstacles (empty volumes the solution must not occupy); design variables are recorded by dragging an object along the canvas as a polygon path in 3D, at most three variables per problem, each discretised into exactly 4 segments, so a solution is generated for every combination (3 variables = 4 to the 3rd = 64 solutions); constraints are authored by sketching an edge from a source object to a constrained object, the mapping a 1D function tp = F(sp) shown in a widget with an exploration cursor; a manikin registers its hand to the nearest object and updates pose by 2D inverse kinematics so the designer can judge ergonomics; load cases are force vectors sketched with the force tool, stroke length proportional to force magnitude. Generation runs topology optimisation minimising compliance (inverse stiffness under load) and volume, with a weighting parameter set by a proprietary heuristic; execution is offline and slow, minutes to hours per problem set, and the paper is explicit that DreamSketch is not real-time. Navigation is the piece-able half: the generated alternatives are presented back inside the sketched context with quantitative attributes per solution (volume, weight, stress), and the user navigates by directly dragging the objects through their design variables, the system finding and displaying the closest corresponding solution for each configuration. What the viewer sees: a loose pencil sketch of a room, and inside it a dozen ghostly chair-variants blooming, morphing and swapping as the solver runs -- the same sketch parenting a family of forms, each one a different answer to the same loose question. Examples in the paper: a dragon-shaped lamp holder, a bicycle frame, a wine-bottle holder, a bike with manikin rider. [C35511]

**Takeaway:** Ambiguity in, multiplicity out -- one loose sketch, a family of forms, and the design is the act of choosing between siblings.

## 9. One curve, four jobs: the sketched function

The cross-cutting device that appears in four of Kazi's systems under the same widget, and the single most portable mechanism in his corpus after the kinetic texture itself: a one-dimensional function, authored by sketching a curve, that maps one quantity onto another. In Draco it is the motion profile: the user sketches a curve for scale or velocity along a trajectory, so bubbles grow and decelerate as they move away from the emitter, with profile height at a point on the path setting the scale or velocity of elements at that point. In SKUID it is the motion profile again: slow-in and slow-out reads velocity from a sketched bell-shaped curve, squash-and-stretch reads its strength from a sketched profile against a neutral line, the user can inject extra breakpoints by tapping directly on the motion path, and a yellow control point shown on both the path and the profile lets the user coordinate effect with position. In Kitty it is the functional mapping on a graph edge: tp = f(sp), source parameter on the x-axis, target parameter on the y-axis, default identity, overwritten by sketching a new curve, with an exploration cursor dragged in the mapping space to preview the corresponding values live -- and the shape of the curve IS the behaviour, a peaked curve meaning "most intense at the moment of impact", a steep curve meaning "a little turn goes a long way". In DreamSketch it is the constraint function: a condition under which one object's geometric parameter depends on another object's position, authored by sketching an edge from source to constrained object, the mapping tp = F(sp) shown in the same widget with the same exploration cursor -- a translation constraint keeping rotors symmetric about a rider, a scale constraint tying wheel size to an obstacle's position, a rotation constraint re-orienting a wine bottle as its holder bases move. Four systems, one widget, one rule: the function is drawn, not typed, and its shape is visible in the picture it governs. A generative piece that lets the viewer redraw one curve and watch a whole behaviour change is faithful to Kazi in a way that borrowing his rain or his fire is not. [C35512]

**Takeaway:** The function is drawn, not typed: one sketched curve, four jobs, and the shape of the curve is the behaviour.

## 10. The picture that animates itself (Mixed-Initiative, UIST 2018)

"A Mixed-Initiative Interface for Animating Static Pictures" (Willett, Kazi, Chen, Fitzmaurice, Finkelstein, Grossman, UIST 2018, 14-17 October 2018, Berlin, pp. 649-661; DOI 10.1145/3242587.3242612; Willett lead) closes the loop back to Draco: instead of authoring kinetic textures from scratch, start from a static picture, a raster image. With a few user-drawn scribbles as input -- sample objects scribbled in green, overall motion direction in red -- the interface extracts repetitive texture elements in the image (find all the balloons or boats), separates them into their own layers, inpaints the background, and builds multiple layers to enhance depth cues; after segmentation analysis, animation processes automatically generate kinetic textures that are spatio-temporally coherent with the source image, producing a seamlessly looping animation. The system synthesises existing image-processing algorithms and new animation optimisation algorithms into one sketch-based system, and builds on Autodesk SketchBook Motion. User evaluation feedback is reported as showing illustrators and animators can add life to still images across a broad range of visual styles. The mechanism detail beyond this summary was not verified in this run -- the paper was read at abstract and introduction level only -- so no prompt in this study is built on it, and the photo-based idea the mechanisms leg considered was deliberately not proposed: the paper was not read in full, and a piece that needs an input photograph does not fit the gallery's self-contained format. [C35513]

**Takeaway:** The loop closes: the picture supplies its own patch, the scribble supplies the direction, and the kinetic texture writes itself.

## 11. The sketch in the world (SymbiosisSketch, VR, RealitySketch)

The 3D and AR turn, 2017-2020, takes the sketch off the page and into space. "Experimental Evaluation of Sketching on Surfaces in VR" (Arora, Kazi, Anderson, Grossman, Singh, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2017, 6-11 May 2017, Denver, pp. 5643-5654) is the empirical groundwork, cited here at title, authors and venue level only; mechanism and findings were not verified in this pass and nothing is quoted from it. SymbiosisSketch (Arora, Kazi, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, Singh, CHI 2018, 21-26 April 2018, Montreal, Paper 185, pp. 1-15; DOI 10.1145/3173574.3173759; Arora lead) is a hybrid sketching system combining drawing in air (3D) and drawing on a surface (2D) to create detailed 3D designs of arbitrary scale in AR: 3D is immersive, unconstrained and life-sized, 2D is precise, constrained and ergonomic, and users draw interchangeably on a 2D tablet or in 3D within an ergonomically comfortable canonical volume, mapped to arbitrary scale in AR on a Microsoft HoloLens. The motivation is stated by Arora in the press coverage: in Tilt Brush "it was extremely hard to draw in the air". RealitySketch (Suzuki, Kazi, Wei, DiVerdi, Li, Leithinger, UIST 2020, 20-23 October 2020, virtual, pp. 166-181; DOI 10.1145/3379337.3415892; Best Paper Honourable Mention and Best Demo Honourable Mention) is an AR interface for sketching interactive graphics and visualisations, built on Apple ARKit: prior AR sketching tools produce static sketches floating in mid-air, but in RealitySketch the user draws graphical elements on a mobile AR screen and binds them to physical objects in real time, so sketched elements dynamically move with the corresponding physical motion, integrating object tracking with AR-based visualisation; evaluated with teachers and students at the University of Calgary, with use cases in physics teaching, video creation, education, storytelling and AR prototyping. The through-line from Draco is unbroken: the sketch is still the specification, but the canvas is now the room, and the moving thing is a physical object the drawing is tied to. [C35514]

**Takeaway:** The canvas becomes the room: a sketch bound to a moving physical object, so the drawing follows the world instead of the page.

## 12. The live stream and the prototype (StreamSketch, VisPoll, Pronto, Rapido)

The Adobe period, 2020-2021, turns the sketch into a social and prototyping instrument. Pronto (Leiva, Nguyen, Kazi, Asente, CHI 2020, 25-30 April 2020, Honolulu, pp. 1-13; DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376160; Leiva lead) is a tablet-based video prototyping system combining 2D video with 3D manipulation for early-stage mobile AR design, supporting four intertwined activities -- capturing 3D spatial information alongside a video scenario, positioning and sketching 2D drawings in a 3D world, and enacting animations with physical interactions. Rapido (Leiva, Gronbaek, Klokmose, Nguyen, Kazi, Asente, UIST 2021, 10-14 October 2021, virtual, pp. 626-637; DOI 10.1145/3472749.3474774; Leiva lead) turns non-interactive video prototypes into fully interactive mobile AR prototypes using programming by demonstration: designers record a video prototype, sketch assets, and demonstrate interactions -- touch inputs, animation paths, rules such as having a sketch follow the focus area of the device -- while a live website visualises an editable overview of the demonstrated examples in a timeline and infers a state machine of the user flow, which drives an interactive prototype testable in the AR interface. StreamSketch (Lu, Kazi, Wei, Dontcheva, Karahalios, Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, Vol. 5, CSCW1, April 2021, Article 58; DOI 10.1145/3449132; Lu lead) lets viewers and streamers interact during creative live streams using multiple modalities including freeform sketches and text, where interaction had been text-only chat; evaluated by two professional artist streamers and their viewers over six streaming sessions, it increased engagement, with efficiency, moderation and tool integration as continued challenges. Its companion, "Beyond Show of Hands" (Chung, Shin, Xia, Wei, Kazi, CHI 2021, 8-13 May 2021, Yokohama, 14 pages; DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445419; Chung lead), addresses scale with a Visual Input Management Framework: viewer visual inputs are treated as a combination of visual attributes (shape, size, colour, rotation, position), and streamers can specify, aggregate and visualise many visual inputs by those attributes, instantiated as VisPoll. These four are catalogued at abstract level; no prompt in this study is built on them, because their mechanisms are about other people's interaction with a stream or a prototype, not about a watchable system on a canvas. [C35515]

**Takeaway:** The sketch leaves the studio: it becomes a chat message, a poll, and a prototype -- the same direct mark, now addressed to someone else.

## 13. The layered turn: stylisation in 3D (CHI 2022)

"A Layered Authoring Tool for Stylized 3D Animations" (Ma, Wei, Kazi, CHI 2022, 29 April - 5 May 2022, New Orleans, Article 383, 14 pages; DOI 10.1145/3491102.3501894; Best Paper Honourable Mention, top 5 percent; Ma lead) moves SKUID's principles from 2D to 3D. Stylisation is core to 2D animation but hard in 3D because of the extra spatial dimension and responsive actions like contact and collision. The system has a layered authoring interface to balance ease of use and expressiveness: at the surface level, a timeline sequencer where users add preset stylisation effects -- squash and stretch, follow through, arcs, staging -- as additional channels for objects, individually or as groups, and adjust spatial and temporal parameters; at the second level, a node graph where edits propagate and users can build custom stylisations once comfortable. The system also stylises interactions among multiple objects (force, energy, collision). A pilot user study found the fluid layered UI better than existing tools for both ease of use and expressiveness. This is the SKUID idea grown up: the same seven amplifiers, now as channels on a timeline and nodes in a graph, with the same promise -- exaggeration as a set of independent, combinable controls, never as a physics simulation the artist must tune. [C35516]

**Takeaway:** The amplifiers grow a second floor: presets on a timeline for the beginner, a node graph for the expert, the same seven deformations underneath.

## 14. The talking sketch (DrawTalking, UIST 2024)

DrawTalking (Rosenberg, Kazi, Wei, Xia, Perlin, UIST 2024, 13-16 October 2024, Pittsburgh, 25 pages; DOI 10.1145/3654777.3676334; Rosenberg lead; short version CHI EA 2024, Article 113) is the return of the Kitty idea, now with speech. Users build interactive worlds by sketching and speaking while telling stories, on a multi-touch iPad app with a manipulable infinite canvas. Speech serves a dual purpose: it is the narrative, and it is input -- the user names sketches with nouns, adjectives and adverbs, giving them semantics and behaviours ("this is a dog", "it is bouncy"), and narrates desired rules as if explaining to an audience. The prototype gives programming-like capability without requiring code, needs no preparation or staging step, and supports changing content and behaviour at any time. An early open-ended study found the mechanics resonate across many creative-exploratory use cases. The paper explicitly cites Draco and Kitty as prior work in interactive sketching, and its framing reference is Bret Victor's "Inventing on Principles" (2014). The through-line from 2014 is exact: in Kitty the sketched curve is the function; in DrawTalking the spoken word is the semantics. The sketch is still the specification, and now the voice is too. Per Kazi's site, DrawTalking is now open sourced. [C35517]

**Takeaway:** The sketch learns to listen: say what a drawing is, and it becomes that thing -- the word is the second specification.

## 15. The textbook that moves (Augmented Physics, UIST 2024)

Augmented Physics (Gunturu, Wen, Zhang, Thundathil, Kazi, Suzuki, UIST 2024, 13-16 October 2024, Pittsburgh, 12 pages; DOI 10.1145/3654777.3676392; Best Paper; Gunturu lead) is the mixed-initiative idea from 2018 rebuilt with modern machine learning. A machine-learning-integrated authoring tool turns static physics textbook diagrams into embedded interactive simulations: the user photographs or scans a diagram with an iPad; Segment Anything segments objects (skier, ski jump); the user assigns roles; multi-modal large language models help generate the simulation; basic physics (gravity and the rest) makes objects move. It covers optics (a prism refracting sunlight), circuits (currents along wires), kinematics (skier, pendulum), and looping animation. Four augmentation strategies come from an elicitation study with 7 physics instructors: augmented experiments, animated diagrams, bi-directional binding, and parameter visualisation -- for example, adjust snow friction and the skier speeds up or slows down. Technical evaluation on 200 diagrams across 6 physics textbooks, no-modification pipeline success: kinematics 64 percent, optics 44 percent, circuits 40 percent, animation 66 percent (higher with small adjustments). Evaluated with a usability study (N=12) and expert interviews (N=12). The mechanism that matters for a generative practice is the bi-directional binding: a static diagram and a running simulation that stay linked, so changing a number in one changes the motion in the other -- the same binding Kitty drew as a graph edge in 2014, now between a page and a physics engine. [C35518]

**Takeaway:** The page becomes a simulation: photograph a diagram, and the drawing starts obeying physics -- while the numbers stay visible beside it.

## 16. The logo that writes its own code (LogoMotion, CHI 2025)

LogoMotion (Liu, Kazi, Wei, Fisher, Langlois, Walker, Chilton, CHI 2025, 26 April - 1 May 2025, Yokohama, 16 pages; DOI 10.1145/3706598.3714155; Liu lead) is the first Kazi work to use vision-language models for animation. Input: a static layered logo PDF. The pipeline has three stages: preprocessing, where the PDF is converted to an HTML canvas representation, augmented with hierarchy, groupings, and per-element vision-language-model captions (each layer isolated against a plain background, captioned by GPT-4V, the caption set as alt text); visually-grounded code synthesis, where a vision-language model is given the HTML plus the logo image, suggests a design concept, then implements JavaScript animation code for it; and visually-grounded program repair, where the last frame of the animation is compared to the target image and the model self-refines if there are visual errors on any layer. Novices edit via code-connected AI editing widgets -- GUI controls for motion, grouping and timing -- instead of editing code. A comparison study on 276 animations found LogoMotion more content-aware than an industry-leading tool; a user evaluation (n=16) against a prompt-only baseline found the widgets helped users edit with control, iteration and creative expression. Limitations stated in the paper: path motion, morphing and physics-based animation were out of scope. The shift from 2014 is total and the through-line is intact: in Draco the synthesis was hand-built geometry; in LogoMotion the synthesis is a model writing code -- but in both cases the user sketches or selects intent in the most direct form available, and the system does the tedious synthesis while the user stays in control through direct manipulation. [C35519]

**Takeaway:** The synthesiser changes -- a model writing code instead of a grid deforming strokes -- but the contract does not: intent in, tedium out, control stays with the hand.

## 17. Blocks of narrative motion (Narrative Motion Blocks, DIS 2025; Notational Animating, CHI 2026)

The two most recent steps in the Draco lineage, both read at abstract and summary level only. Narrative Motion Blocks (Bourgault, Wei, Jacobs, Kazi, DIS 2025, 5-9 July 2025, Madeira; Best Paper; DOI 10.1145/3715336.3735766; Bourgault lead; work done during a summer 2024 internship at Adobe Research; page numbers not confirmed) builds aniMate, a hand-drawn animation system integrating direct manipulation and natural language interaction: its central component is narrative motion blocks, clip-like components on a timeline that let animators specify animated behaviours with a combination of textual and manual input, bridging the gap between direct-manipulation animation (precise but laborious) and pure language prompting (expressive but imprecise). Notational Animating (Shi, Wei, Zhao, Zhao, Kazi, CHI 2026, 13-17 April 2026, Barcelona; Best Paper Honourable Mention; DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790707; Shi lead; page numbers not confirmed) is almost a return to Draco's first principles: users sketch high-level notations over static drawings to indicate intended motions, and automatic methods (including generative-AI models) interpret the notations and generate animation keyframes; the notation representation is source, path, and target. The paper frames the trade-off explicitly: notation ambiguity shifts the burden of interpretation to the system (gulf of execution) and widens the gulf of evaluation, since users must understand how the system interpreted their notations to refine the output. Its related-work discussion cites Draco, Kitty, Energy Brushes and Motion Doodles as systems that "map sketched notations to specific animation effects". Kazi's site also lists a second CHI 2026 honourable mention, MoSound, for which authorship and details were not verified in this pass; it is named here only, not catalogued as a work. [C35520]

**Takeaway:** Twelve years on, the input is still a sketch -- now a notation, source-path-target -- and the open question is still who interprets the ambiguity, the hand or the system.

## 18. Shipped: SketchBook Motion, Fresco, Character Animator

[C35521]

**Takeaway:** The proof is the shelf: the living picture left the lab and became an app of the year, and the research reads differently once you know it shipped.

## 19. Your paper is dead: the animated figure

"Your Paper is Dead!: Bringing Life to Research Articles with Animated Figures" (Grossman, Chevalier, Kazi, CHI EA 2015, 18-23 April 2015, Seoul; DOI 10.1145/2702613.2732501; Grossman lead) is a position and reflection piece, not a tool, and it is the moment the medium itself becomes the message. It proposes the animated figure: a short, autonomous, continuously looping video embedded in a paper that enhances what would otherwise be a static figure, while minimising disruption to reading. The Draco CHI 2014 paper was the first paper in the ACM Digital Library to embed an animated figure. The piece gives a history of publication formats, use cases for animated figures, a how-to for embedding them in PDFs (with an online appendix), and recommendations and caveats for the research community; it was adapted into "Bringing research articles to life with animated figures", ACM Interactions 23(4), June 2016, pp. 52-57. Page split carried, not settled: Chevalier's publications page lists the CHI EA paper at pp. 461-470, while researchr and the ACM Interactions adaptation cite pp. 461-475. The animated figure is the kinetic texture applied to the paper itself: the figure stays a figure, but it breathes -- and the argument is that a research community that studies motion should be allowed to show motion, not merely describe it. [C35522]

**Takeaway:** The paper learns from its own subject: a figure that moves is not a gimmick, it is the argument made visible.

## 20. What not to build from

Four corrections, stated plainly so no later study repeats them. First, RealityCanvas (UIST 2023, Xia, Monteiro, Van, Suzuki) is sometimes listed alongside Kazi's AR work, but Kazi is NOT an author; it is not his work and nothing should be built from it under his name. Second, "trigger/response" is NOT Kitty's vocabulary: the paper's terms are relational graph, directed edge, functional mapping tp = f(sp), chain effect, and interactivity; "trigger/response" is a fair paraphrase, not a quotation, and must not be presented as the paper's grammar. Third, Kazi has no visual style and no images of his own: his demo material is plain academic figures -- black strokes on white, flat UI screenshots -- and the prompts in this study translate his tools' real demo motion, which is observable in the papers, into invented imagery; the imagery is the coordinator's, the rule his. Do not present any prompt's imagery as "Kazi's artwork" or "in the style of Kazi", and do not invent a "Kazi palette". Fourth, DreamSketch's topology optimiser cannot run live in a piece: it takes minutes to hours per problem set, and its weighting heuristic is proprietary and unpublished. The DreamSketch prompt in this study keeps only the verified, piece-able half -- discretised variable-space navigation over a precomputed family, with a disclosed procedural surrogate for the optimiser -- and says so in the brief; if the curatorial gate requires the optimisation itself, that prompt should be rejected, not fudged. The same honesty applies to the Energy-Brushes smoke field, whose matrix entries were extracted with OCR noise: the structural claim (a Gaussian-derived, divergence-free up-field) is verified, the exact entries should be re-checked against the PDF before implementation. [C35523]

**Takeaway:** The rule is his; the picture is ours. Say which is which in every prompt, and never dress a rule up as his art.

## 21. The aesthetic logic

Kazi's aesthetic, insofar as a researcher has one, is the aesthetic of the sketch that refuses to become a simulation. Nothing in his work is decorated; everything is a gesture that becomes a rule. The visual language of his papers is the language of the lab: a few fish, a few leaves, a few bubbles; a black stroke on white; a grid over a blob; a curve sketched in a widget. What makes it generative material is not how it looks but how it BEHAVES: every system has a rule you can state in one sentence, a state you can watch change, and a visible cause for every motion (the emitter is drawn, the path is drawn, the knob is drawn, the curve is drawn). The pieces this study feeds should therefore be built as instruments, not pictures: a patch with a path, a brush with a field, a knob with a chain, a curve with a function. The palette is the coordinator's choice and should be justified on its own terms -- the study's prompts use ink-on-paper black and white with one warm accent, because that is what his demo figures actually look like, and because a single accent makes the moving element legible against the still one. The motion should be ambient and endless where the mechanism is ambient (Draco, Energy-Brushes), and staged and scheduled where the mechanism is staged (Kitty's chain, ChronoFab's accumulation, DreamSketch's navigation). Interaction belongs only where the system earns it: the Draco piece earns a drag that redraws a path, the Kitty piece earns a drag that turns a knob, the SKUID piece earns toggles that switch a live deformation -- and click-to-reload is never the interaction, because none of his systems reload. The fifth-study rule is honoured here by the branch itself: the last four studies ran through the Toronto HCI lineage, with Mullen's painted volumes in between, and Kazi is the different-visual-language branch the rule calls for -- the sketch-as-system, the living picture, the drawn thing that moves by its own rules. [C35524]

## 22. Evolution: four eras

Era 1, the craft years, 2011-2013 (NUS; PhD 2014, advisor Zhao): the question is how computation can serve a traditional practice without breaking it -- sand animation, pen-and-ink illustration, whiteboard storytelling. The move is to preserve the practitioner's workflow and make the computer disappear into it, and the collaborators are the NUS lab (Zhao, Low, Davis, Chua) plus Igarashi in Japan and Lee and Smith at Microsoft Research. The artefacts are thesis chapters. Era 2, Autodesk Research, 2014-2018 (Toronto; intern during the PhD, then researcher): the question becomes how a still drawing can live and cause things. Draco and Kitty (2014), the animated-figure argument (2015), SKUID, ChronoFab and Energy-Brushes (2016), DreamSketch, the VR sketching evaluation and SymbiosisSketch (2017-2018). The collaborator centre of gravity is Grossman and Fitzmaurice, with Chevalier, Zhao, Umetani, Mogk, Schmidt, Xing, Wei and Stam. And this is the era of transfer: Draco as SketchBook Motion, Apple's iPad App of the Year 2016. Era 3, Adobe Research, 2019-2021 (Seattle; Senior Research Scientist): the question becomes how sketches live in the world and in front of other people -- AR video prototyping (Pronto, Rapido), sketches bound to moving physical objects (RealitySketch), sketches as a live-stream interaction modality (StreamSketch, VisPoll), and the layered 3D stylisation tool (2022). The collaborator centre shifts to Li-Yi Wei and a new cohort (Leiva, Suzuki, Lu, Ma). The products ship: Fresco motion features (2021), Character Animator contributions (Emmy 2019). Era 4, the AI turn, 2022-2026: the question becomes how sketches, speech and language models author motion together -- DrawTalking (2024), Augmented Physics (2024, Best Paper), LogoMotion (2025), Narrative Motion Blocks (2025, Best Paper), Notational Animating (2026, Honourable Mention). The collaborator centre shifts again to his own students and junior collaborators (Rosenberg, Gunturu, Liu, Bourgault, Shi) and to Ken Perlin. The through-line across all four eras is the sketch as specification: the user sketches intent in the most direct form available, the system does the tedious synthesis, and the user stays in control through direct manipulation. The instrument changes; the move -- coarse sketch in, continuous looping system out, with the artist controlling motion at two scales at once -- does not. [C35525]
