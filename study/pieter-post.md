# Pieter Post: Study Notes

**Site:** Pieter Jansz. Post (baptised 1 May 1608, Haarlem -- buried 8 May 1669, The Hague), Dutch Golden Age painter-architect; the executor who carried Jacob van Campen's classicism from paper into stone -- the Mauritshuis, the Noordeinde alterations, Huis ten Bosch -- and then, as court architect and government architect, built his own classicism across the republic: Maastricht's town hall, the Leiden and Gouda weigh houses, the Delft powder magazine, and the water-board house at Halfweg. He began as a painter of dunes and bleaching fields, painted only thirteen pictures, and spent the rest of his life building the rooms other painters filled.

## The life

Pieter Jansz. Post was baptised in Haarlem on 1 May 1608, the son of Jan Jansz. Post (c. 1575-1614), a stained-glass painter from Leiden, and Francijntje Pietersdr. Verbraken (1581-1656) of Haarlem; the parents had married in Haarlem on 19 June 1604, and his father died when Pieter was about six. His younger brother Frans Jansz. Post (17 November 1612 -- buried 17 February 1680) became the landscape painter of Dutch Brazil, the first European artist to paint the Americas; Houbraken says Pieter introduced Frans to Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen while working on the Mauritshuis plans, and Frans sailed for Recife on 25 October 1636. The 1612 birth year belongs to Frans, not Pieter -- the study uses 1608-1669 throughout, and any source giving 1612 for Pieter is confusing the brothers (see C1097).

Nothing is known about Pieter's training as a painter. The Terwen & Ottenheym summary is blunt: it is not known from whom he learned to paint. His landscapes show affinities with the Haarlem painter Cornelis Vroom (1590/91-1661), "who may have been his teacher" -- conjecture only, and Post's datable dune painting of c. 1631 "seems to predate similar works by Vroom". No source read documents formal architectural training, and none documents a formal pupillage under Jacob van Campen: what is documented is that he "came into contact with architecture from the mid-1630s when he assisted Jacob van Campen with the construction of the Mauritshuis". Popular and tourist sources call him Van Campen's "pupil" or "apprentice"; treat that as unverified popular wording, not scholarly fact. He is mentioned by Ampzing in his Haarlem city chronicle of 1628, alongside other Haarlem painters. The RKD gives his Haarlem Guild of St Luke membership as 1623 -- doubted in the Terwen-derived text, since he "would have been only 15 years old at that time and could hardly [have] completed his training".

In 1640 he was put in charge of building the Oude Hof, Noordeinde, The Hague, after Van Campen's 1639 designs. In 1645 he was appointed architect and painter to Stadholder Frederik Hendrik (WGA, Britannica, RKD); the Terwen-derived text dates the formal appointment as "Architect-in-Ordinary to the Stadholder" to 1646, when he settled in The Hague -- possibly two different appointments, a 1645 role and a 1646 formal title, though no source says so explicitly. After Frederik Hendrik's death (1647) and into the stadholderless period after 1650, "Post continued to work for the government. He designed a wide variety of buildings including town houses, country houses, weigh-houses and locks". He died in The Hague in 1669, aged 61, and was buried on 8 May 1669. No source read names a wife or a marriage date; do not invent one.

His children carried the name on. Maurits Post (baptised c. 10 December 1645 -- 6 June 1677) became an architect, "probably his father's assistant", took over his father's projects on Pieter's death in 1669, and was architect to Stadholder William III from 1670 or 1672 (the sources disagree on the year); his works include Castle Amerongen, Soestdijk and the Prinzenhof at Kleve. Johan Post became a painter. A daughter married the anatomist and collector Frederik Ruysch; her daughter Rachel Ruysch became the famous flower painter -- Pieter Post is her grandfather.

## The painter first: thirteen pictures, then no more

Post's painting career is short enough to count. Only thirteen paintings survive, from about 1630 to 1633: mainly landscapes, plus depictions of soldiers plundering a city and four combat scenes. W01, View of the bleaching fields near Haarlem (1631, oil on panel, signed and dated, 43.7 x 61.5 cm VERIFIED, Lugt Collection, Paris), is the small signed prototype. W02, A Panoramic View of the Dunes near Haarlem (c. 1631, oil on canvas, 75 x 99.5 cm VERIFIED), is his largest and most ambitious painting, "nearly a metre in width", a large-scale variation on W01. W03, Landscape with a Haystack (1633, Mauritshuis), survives without verified dimensions in the sources read. W04, a plundering scene illustrated on the Kunsthandel P. de Boer page (soldiers plundering a village with horse-drawn wagons, a draw-well in the foreground, a burning church on the left), has no verified title, date, location or dimensions in any source read -- do not cite it as a titled work. W05, the remaining landscapes and four combat scenes, are an unnamed group; no individual titles were found.

WGA is blunt about the paintings: his 1630s landscapes and battle scenes "are not significant" as painting, while his architecture "is one of the high-spots of classicism in Holland". The same sources still credit the landscapes as "an original contribution to the early development of the Dutch realistic landscape" that "helped shape the landscape style of his younger brother Frans". The painter stops at about 1633 and the builder begins; the thirteen pictures are the whole easel oeuvre, and everything after them is stone, paper and print. That is mechanism M7 below, and it is also the career's hinge: the painter's eye for perspective and measured drawing becomes the builder's skill of turning another architect's paper design into stone, on site, with masons.

## M1 -- The executor-translator: turning another man's design into stone

Post's core skill was not inventing the classicist idea -- that was Van Campen's -- but realising it. He assisted Van Campen with the Mauritshuis (W06, built 1633-44; construction dates conflict, see C1101), supervised the Noordeinde alterations (W07, 1640) from Van Campen's 1639 designs, lengthening the main building and adding wings either side to create the characteristic H-form, and executed the late-phase alterations at Huis Honselaarsdijk (W09, from 1646) on the basis of a 1628 French ground-plan proposal. At the Amsterdam Town Hall (W08, 1648-55) he is credited only by Encyclopedia.com with assisting Van Campen; the construction supervision belongs to Daniel Stalpaert, and the Post work list omits the building -- do not over-claim. The mechanism is measured drawings, on-site direction of master builders (Cornelis Pesser made changes on site at Maastricht), and the print record as instruction. The executor is the man who knows what a drawing costs in stone: which pilaster rhythm a mason can actually cut, where a wing can be added without breaking the symmetry, how a design survives contact with a building site.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is not the building; the building is the drawing after it has been argued with, measured, and cut -- and the man who does that arguing is the one who decides what the style actually looks like at full size.

## M2 -- The classicist facade as a disciplined kit

The Mauritshuis (W06) is a two-storey, strictly symmetrical Dutch Classicist block: giant pilasters, a pediment, a pale stone facade, four apartments and a great hall inside, the cupola and much of the interior lost to the fire of 1704. The Leiden Waag (W17, designed c. 1657, opened 1659) is the same kit at working scale: a Tuscan order on a rusticated base, the facade sculpture of the scales by Rombout Verhulst announcing the function. The dealer summary calls it "clarity of design, balanced compositions, and elegant restraint, avoiding excessive ornamentation while still conveying grandeur and order". The kit scales from palace to weigh house without changing grammar -- that scalability is the mechanism. Symmetry, pilasters, pediment, rusticated base: the same four pieces, recombined, whether the client is a count in Brazil or a cheese-weigher in Leiden.

**Takeaway:** a facade that is a kit, not a composition -- four parts, recombined to any scale, so the same language speaks for a palace and a weigh house and neither has to shout.

## M3 -- The cruciform hall as a dynastic machine

Huis ten Bosch (W10, groundbreaking 2 September 1645; completion dates 1645-47 to 1645-52, see C1102) is a compact centralised villa suburbana: a cross-shaped central hall -- the Oranjezaal -- with five-room apartments on either side, ordered public to private, so the plan stages access to the resident. The Oranjezaal cycle was coordinated by Van Campen and painted by about twelve artists; Jordaens's Triumph of Frederik Hendrik is 19 metres tall, possibly the largest 17th-century Dutch canvas. After Frederik Hendrik's death in 1647 Amalia rededicated the house as his memorial -- the "New Artemisia" -- and her own portrait sits at the apex of the dome, the keystone of the house. The plan is a social machine first and a memorial second: it stages who may approach the prince, and after his death it stages mourning. The influence is named as Palladio's Villa Capra at Vicenza, and the plan's discipline is the same as Post's landscapes: order the ground, then let the eye walk it.

**Takeaway:** a plan that is a machine for access -- public to private, room to room, the crossing as the middle of the world -- and when the patron dies the same machine becomes a mausoleum without changing a wall.

## M4 -- The town hall planted in the square

Maastricht City Hall (W18, commissioned 1655, construction 1659-64, tower completed 1684) is Post's most accomplished work, and its mechanism is siting. The hall stands in the centre of the Markt, five storeys of Namur stone with a slate roof, a 25-metre tower rising to 47 metres in total, the roof at 23.5 metres -- and the site was imposed against the wishes of the Prince-Bishopric of Liege: two houses, the cloth hall, the belfry and part of the city wall were demolished to make room, and the Markt "gained an almost squared shape" around it. Maastricht was a condominium of Brabant and the Prince-Bishopric; choosing the North-Holland-born Post "can be seen as sign of the increasing influence of the States General". The north half of the building represents Liege, the south Brabant. Contrast with the Amsterdam Town Hall, where Post only assisted: there the hall dominates by sheer mass on 13,659 piles; at Maastricht it dominates by freestanding centrality. The building reorganises the square around itself, and the square reorganises the city around the building.

**Takeaway:** siting as politics -- put the hall in the middle of the square, clear the ground for it, and the building does not need to shout; the empty space around it is the argument.

## M5 -- The working building: function legible in the facade

The Leiden Waag (W17) and the Gouda Kaaswaag (W21, 1668) are weigh houses, and they say so. At Leiden the Tuscan order sits on a rusticated base and Verhulst's marble relief of the scales sits in the facade; at Gouda the bas-relief by Bartholomeus Eggers, likely after a design by Post, shows cheese being weighed, flanked by the arms of the four 1668 mayors, and the upper floor served the militia as an armoury from 1668 to 1907. The Leiden Waag stayed a weigh house until 1972 -- the last unit of cheese was sold that year. The Delft Kruithuis (W19, 1659-62), a gunpowder depot on the Schie outside the city after the Delft explosion of 1654, is the same device in a military key: a slim gate tower, a main warehouse, outbuildings with stepped gables, function stated plainly. The ornament is an operating instruction: the relief shows citizens how the weighing works, and the plain robust classicism says the weighing is honest.

**Takeaway:** when the building's job is written on its face -- scales, cheese, powder -- the facade stops being decoration and becomes a sign, and the sign is what keeps the building in use for three hundred years.

## M6 -- Painted architecture: the architect as staffage painter

The one place Post's two careers visibly overlap is inside another man's painting. W24, Count William II of Holland Granting Privileges to the Dike Wardens of Spaarndam in 1255 (1654, by Caesar van Everdingen with Pieter Post; 218 x 212 cm on retail sources only, not museum-verified; dated 1655 by the same retail sources), was commissioned as decoration for the meeting hall of the Gemeenlandshuis Zwanenburg (W11, Halfweg, 1645-48), the water-board house Post designed. The division of labour is stated on the Post Wikipedia page: Everdingen painted the figures, Post painted the architectural elements. A 1651 portrait of Post by Pieter Nolpe is a detail of this larger work. The water board's foundation myth -- a 1255 privilege -- is staged inside architecture painted by the architect who built the room the picture hung in. Perspective construction learned as a landscape painter, deployed as architectural propaganda for a board that manages dikes.

**Takeaway:** the architect's hand inside the history painting -- the building drawn by the man who builds buildings, so the painted architecture is not scenery but a signature, and the room and the picture vouch for each other.

## M7 -- The landscape as survey: the high viewpoint and the working countryside

W01 and W02 are views from slightly raised ground: dunes giving way to flat coastal plain, hedgerows, pastures, grazing animals, farm buildings, the bleaching fields of the linen industry laid out like a map. The catalogue language is precise: "simple, unpretentious composition and subtle aerial perspective", "a delicate palette in multiple shades of green, together with the effects of sunlight filtered through high cloud". The mechanism is recession in bands -- a foreground track with figures, a middle plain, a hazy coast -- plus aerial perspective, giving depth without drama. The bleaching fields are not decoration; they are the linen industry, observed topographically. This is the same spatial thinking as his plans: order the ground, then let the eye walk it. The painter who surveys a landscape from a dune is already the architect who surveys a square from a scaffold.

**Takeaway:** a landscape read as a survey -- bands of ground receding to a hazy coast, the working countryside laid out like a plan -- and the depth comes from order, not from drama.

## M8 -- The print as publication: architecture as a reproducible paper system

Post published his buildings the way a painter publishes prints. W25, Begraeffenisse van syne hoogheyt Frederick Henrick (1651), the funeral book for Frederik Hendrik, engraved by Pieter Nolpe and sold by the author in The Hague. W26, the collected Alle de wercken van architectuure, gemaeckt door P. Post (Amsterdam, Frederik de Wit, not before 1664), reissued at Leiden by Pieter van der Aa in 1715 as Les Ouvrages d'Architecture de Pierre Post -- eight parts in one volume, 73 copperplates of plans, elevations, sections and perspectives, engraved mostly by Jan Mathijs and Pieter Nolpe after Post's designs, folio 43 x 29 x 9 cm. The series inside it -- De sael van Orange, Swanenburch, Het stadhuys van Maastrigt, Perspective uytbeeldinge van Vreden-burch, and the chimney-piece designs Afbeeldingen, van weinighe schoorsteen-wercken -- extend the system from the town hall down to the fireplace. The engraved section and floorplan are the building's portable double: Huis ten Bosch circulated to architects who never saw it, and the influence reached English architects, notably Hugh May. The building's afterlife is on paper.

**Takeaway:** publish the building as a paper system -- plan, elevation, section, perspective, down to the chimney-piece -- and the architecture outlives the architect by travelling further than he ever did.

## The aesthetic logic

Post's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the same rules in paint and in stone. Order the ground first: the landscape in bands, the plan in rooms public to private, the square cleared around the hall. Use a kit, not a composition: symmetry, pilasters, pediment, rusticated base, recombined from palace to weigh house, so the language never has to be reinvented for a new client. Let the function show: scales on the Waag, cheese on the Kaaswaag, powder behind the Kruithuis gate, the weighing and the storing written on the face. Publish the system: the engraved plan and section are part of the building, not an afterthought, and the building is finished when it can be rebuilt from paper.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. The Mauritshuis was built for Johan Maurits while he was governor-general of Dutch Brazil (1636-1644); his Brazilian income came from sugar and enslaved labour, and the house was called the "Sugar Palace". The Maastricht hall was imposed on a city that did not want it there, by a state asserting its influence. The classicism is not neutral: it is the visual language a new republic and a grieving widow chose to say who they were, and Post was the man who could build it at the scale of a city -- after Van Campen had drawn it, and after the painter in him had learned to survey the ground.

## The network

Jacob van Campen (1596/1595-1657): senior partner and collaborator -- Mauritshuis, Noordeinde, Huis ten Bosch together; Post assisted him from the mid-1630s and carried his designs into stone. No formal pupillage is documented; do not call Post his pupil without qualification. Frans Post (1612-1680): younger brother, landscape painter of Brazil; Pieter introduced him to Johan Maurits while working on the Mauritshuis plans, and Frans sailed in 1636. Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen (1604-1679): Mauritshuis patron, the man who took Frans to Brazil; Post "may have designed the town-plan and several buildings at Mauritsstad" -- attribution only, not fact, and he did not go to Brazil himself. Frederik Hendrik (1584-1647) and Amalia van Solms (1602-1675): court patrons from 1645; Amalia commissioned Huis ten Bosch and rededicated it as her husband's memorial. Constantijn Huygens (1596-1687): the contact runs through Van Campen -- Post assisted on the Huygenshuis, and Huygens supervised the Oranjezaal in Post's building; no direct Huygens-Post letter or meeting is documented, and any claim of a personal relationship is unproven. Caesar van Everdingen (1616/17-1678): collaborator on the Rijnland picture (W24) -- figures Caesar, architecture Post -- painted for Post's Zwanenburg hall. Daniel Stalpaert (c. 1615-1676): worked on the same Amsterdam Town Hall project in the same years, supervising construction after Van Campen's 1654 walkout; no direct Post-Stalpaert document was found, and the link is project-mediated, not personal. Maurits Post (c. 1645-1677) and Johan Post: sons -- Maurits the architect who took over his father's projects and became architect to William III, Johan the painter; Maurits is the only assistantship attested ("probably his father's assistant"). Rachel Ruysch: granddaughter, the flower painter. Pieter Nolpe and Jan Mathijs/Mathysz: the engravers of his published designs; Frederik de Wit and Pieter van der Aa: the publishers. Salomon de Bray (1597-1664) and Pieter de Grebber (c. 1600-1652/53): Haarlem classicists beside Post in the same court and guild milieu, De Bray with his Architectura Moderna (1631) and Scamozzi translation (1658), De Grebber with his Regulen (1649). Cornelis Vroom (1590/91-1661): the possible painting teacher, conjecture only. Palladio, Serlio and Scamozzi: book sources, not personal contacts -- no evidence Post read Scamozzi directly or travelled to Italy; the Palladian link is second-hand, through Huis ten Bosch's debt to the Villa Capra. No pupils of Pieter Post are documented; do not invent any.

Top pick for #580 is Pieter de Grebber (c. 1600 -- between 24 September 1652 and 29 January 1653). The painterly half of Haarlem Classicism: eldest son of Frans Pietersz de Grebber, trained by his father and Hendrick Goltzius, Guild of St Luke member 1632, and -- with Salomon de Bray -- "forerunner and first peak of the 'Haarlem classicism' school", painting with "well-organised clarity and light tints". He chains to Post three ways: same court patrons (he decorated Huis Honselaarsdijk and Paleis Noordeinde, buildings Post worked on); same Haarlem guild milieu; and he wrote the 1649 treatise Regulen welcke by een goet Schilder en Teyckenaar geobserveert en achtervolght moeten werden, a rules-of-the-craft text that pairs with Post's engraved Ouvrages. His pupils included Nicolaes Berchem, Gerbrand Ban, Egbert van Heemskerck and Dirck Helmbreeker, giving the study a forward network. Doorway sketch: "Hop from the Post study: the court architect who built the rooms hands the brush to the Haarlem painter who filled them -- Pieter de Grebber, who with Salomon de Bray turned Van Campen and Post's classicism into paint at Honselaarsdijk, Noordeinde and Huis ten Bosch, and wrote down the rules for doing it in 1649."
