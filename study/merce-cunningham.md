## M1. Common time: the separation of elements

Music, dance, decor and costume are composed independently, sharing only a duration and a stage, and they meet for the first time at the performance, and the elements are "independent and interdependent, sharing a common time". Root of an Unfocus of 1944 already works this way, six years before the company and seven years before chance: the piece is divided into time units, and dance and music come together only at the beginning and the end of each unit, independent in between. Summerspace of 1958 is the classic case: the three elements were made separately in three places and met on opening night, and Feldman compared it to a wedding dress arriving the morning of the wedding. What you see is a big movement with no sound at all, then an enormous prepared-piano sound arriving after it, and a dance that starts, continues for twenty minutes and stops, with no regard for the music's shape, and the audience composes the relationship. **Takeaway:** give each art only the clock and the stage, let them meet at the curtain, and the audience will compose the relationship. Against the chain: Cage (#398) also fixed only the duration and let the room compose the rest, M9, but his layers are sounds in a room, and Cunningham's are whole arts on a stage, and the difference is between a room that plays and a stage where three arts arrive as strangers.

## M2. Chance operations: the coin, the card, the die

Coin tosses, playing cards, dice, and the I Ching, 64 hexagrams, decide the order of movements, the combinations of phrases, the pairing of body parts, even which version of a whole evening is performed, and charts are drawn up first, space, time, positions, movement, and then chance fills them in. Sixteen Dances for Soloist and Company of Three of 1951 is the first time Cunningham used chance in choreography: the dance explores the nine permanent emotions, rasas, of Indian aesthetics, anger, humour, sorrow, the heroic, the odious, the wondrous, fear, the erotic, tranquility, and chance dictated the order of appearance of the emotions and the order of discrete moves, and seven of the nine are solos, the erotic a duet, tranquility a quartet. Suite by Chance of 1953 is the first work made entirely through chance procedures, and Cunningham's own account of the Untitled Solo behind it is movements "written on scraps of paper for the legs and the arms, the head, all different", done "not to the music but with the music of Christian Wolff". Torse of 1976 is the most literal I Ching structure in his work: sixty-four distinct phrases, and the floor divided into sixty-four squares, one per hexagram, with separate space charts and movement charts, coin tosses determining the combinations. What you see is a dance whose sequence the dancers themselves do not know until the night of the performance, and a die cast onstage before the audience in Split Sides, and 64 phrases on a 64-square floor in Torse. **Takeaway:** draw the chart first, let the coin fill it in, and the body will do what no taste would have chosen. Against the chain: Cage also asked the same book the same questions with the same coins, M6, but his answer was a score and Cunningham's was a body, and the difference is between a note chosen by a coin and a step chosen by a coin.

## M3. Indeterminacy: the sequence is the variable

Sections are rehearsed so they can be put in any order and done at any time, and the changing element per show is the sequence itself. Sixteen Dances reordered nightly, Story's eighteen sections reshuffled, and Field Dances of 1963 giving each dancer a sequence to do as they pleased, entering and exiting at will, in any order, as many times as desired. What you see is the same material returning in a different order every night, so no two performances of the same dance are the same dance. **Takeaway:** rehearse the parts so they can go in any order, and the sequence becomes the piece. Against the chain: Cage also left the realization open, M10, the performer reading overlays and choosing, but his performer chooses how to play, and Cunningham's dancer knows how to dance and does not know in what order, and the difference is between a score that stays open and a sequence that stays open.

## M4. Non-representational movement: the step about itself

Movement represents no narrative, emotion or idea, and the dance is about itself, and everyday actions, walking, potting a plant, wrapping a towel, changing tights, sit beside virtuoso ballet technique with no hierarchy between them. Variations V of 1965 inserts Duchampian non-dance activities into the dance: Cunningham potted a plant, Carolyn Brown smashed the pot, another dancer wrapped a towel around her hair, and pot and towel were wired to microphones, and Mark Franko's reading is that "bodies in ordinary motion are dance readymades". Walkaround Time ends with the dancers milling around behind the assembled decor and seating themselves in a line on the floor, and that sitting down is the ending of a 48-minute work. What you see is a dancer smashing a pot onstage as a musical event, and a striptease as a Duchamp reference, and dancers sitting down as a finale. **Takeaway:** put the everyday act beside the virtuoso leap with no hierarchy, and the movement will mean nothing but itself. Against the chain: Cage also amplified an everyday action into a composition, M11, one sentence, a disciplined action, but his action IS the piece, and Cunningham's action is one step among steps, and the difference is between a command to a body and a body that commands itself.

## M5. Decentred space: no front, no center

There is no front and center and no privileged spot, and dance can happen anywhere on the stage and be viewed from any angle. Cunningham told Rauschenberg only that Summerspace had no center, and the design follows: no focal point, the stage a spreading subsection of a larger unity continuing out of sight, and Rauschenberg's pointillist backdrop and dotted leotards, sprayed with thousands of colored dots, make the dancers dissolve into the landscape like fauna. Audiences sat in an L-shape in the studio, and the viewer's focus is never directed to one spot, forced to choose among many centers of activity. What you see is birds in a field of dots, leaping and turning, appearing and vanishing into the painted air, and no place on the stage that matters more than any other. **Takeaway:** remove the center and the viewer must choose where to look, and the choosing is the viewing. Against the chain: Cage also removed the front, M12, his audience sitting in the middle in four triangular sections, facing each other, unable to see everything, but his front is removed from the room, and Cunningham's center is removed from the stage, and the difference is between an audience that cannot see it all and a stage that has no it to see.

## M6. Dancer autonomy: the performer decides

The choreographer gives the dancer a sequence, a pile of clothes, or a set of choices, and the dancer decides. In Story of 1963 the dancers chose their costumes from a pile of second-hand clothes before each performance, and Rauschenberg designed the sets from found objects in whatever theater the company was in. In Field Dances the dancers entered and exited at will. In eyeSpace of 2006 the autonomy passes to the audience: each listener borrows an iPod Shuffle and hears the score in shuffle mode, a random order per listener, over a live environmental soundscape. What you see is a dance that is never the same twice because the dancers dressed themselves, and a concert whose music is different for every seat in the house. **Takeaway:** hand the dancer the choice and the piece will choose itself differently every night. Against the chain: Cage also gave the performer a tool and a choice, M10, the transparencies to overlay and read, but his choice is musical, and Cunningham's is bodily, a costume, an entrance, a shuffle, and the difference is between a reader of overlays and a dancer who dresses herself.

## M7. The set as an independent performer: weather and furniture that moves

The decor is an object with its own behavior, not a backdrop: it floats, drifts, moves, blocks sightlines, and must be navigated. Warhol's Silver Clouds in RainForest of 1968 are helium-filled Mylar pillows floating freely around the stage, some drifting unpredictably, and the stage itself breathes. Johns's seven vinyl boxes in Walkaround Time are a maze the dancers must pass in front of, around and behind, and then reassemble at the end. Robert Morris's Canfield set of 1969 is a 25-foot column with airplane runway lights moving across the stage, sweeping the dance into darkness while the dancers wear reflective leotards. What you see is silver pillows drifting like weather through dancers in torn skin-colored tights, and a painting taken apart and walked through, and a column of light deciding when the dance can be seen. **Takeaway:** make the set behave, and the dancers must negotiate with it like a partner. Against the chain: Cage also changed the object by adding hardware, M2, the prepared piano, but his hardware makes new sounds, and Cunningham's set makes new weather, and the difference is between an instrument that takes on new jobs and a room that will not sit still.

## M8. The dance for camera: the frame as collaborator

Choreography is composed for the film or video frame rather than the proscenium, with the camera as a collaborator, not a recorder. Westbeth of 1974 and Locale of 1979 were made for videotape, and Fractions I of 1978 begins in black and white, flashes of color growing more frequent until only brief flashes back to black and white remain, with four television sets standing inside the camera frame, and the dance changes medium as you watch it. Points in Space of 1986 was choreographed for the camera as a coproduction of the Cunningham Dance Foundation and the BBC, later transferred to the stage, and the program is a 25-minute documentary on the making, then the 33-minute dance, broadcast on A and E on Wednesday 29 October 1987. What you see is a dance that changes medium as you watch it, the image itself fractioning from monochrome into color, televisions watching the dancers back, and weather as a design brief, the camera as a collaborator rather than a recorder. **Takeaway:** compose for the frame, not for the proscenium, and the camera becomes a dancer. Against the chain: Cage also cut his medium to a drawn pattern, M8, the tape laid on the dressmaker's score, but his medium is the cut, and Cunningham's is the camera, and the difference is between a score you can lay tape on and a frame you must dance inside.

## M9. LifeForms and DanceForms: the screen figure as sketchbook

A computer program showing a human figure in three-dimensional space is used as a choreographic sketchbook. Cunningham, arthritic and no longer able to demonstrate, composed movement on screen: impossible coordinations of arms and legs, positions a body could hold but a choreographer would never invent by moving his own body. He began experimenting with LifeForms in 1989, and after 1991, when it was redesigned and relaunched as DanceForms, the program became his regular tool, and every work after 1991 used computer-generated movement. BIPED of 1999 was choreographed as 70 phrases with two dancers working in the program. What you see is the disjointed, independent arm-and-leg motions of the 1990s dances, which dancers found agonizing, and a choreographer in a chair inventing steps his own body could no longer show. **Takeaway:** draw the body on a screen, and it will invent coordinations no body would volunteer. Against the chain: Cage also read his notes from a chart he did not draw by hand, M13, the stars under a transparent strip giving pitches, but his source is the sky, and Cunningham's is a digital body, and the difference is between a keyboard made of stars and a dancer made of numbers.

## M10. Motion capture and projection: the body and its data-shadow

A dancer's movement is recorded as data, sensors on the body, then animated and projected as a second, virtual dancer sharing the stage. Hand-drawn Spaces of 1998, a three-screen animation commissioned by SIGGRAPH, was the study for BIPED, and in BIPED the dancers' phrases were recorded by motion capture at the Modern Uprising Motion Capture Studios in New York on 6 February 1999 and animated by Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar into chalk-like virtual figures, projected onto a gauzy scrim at the front of the stage, behind which the live dancers move through darkness, lines, dots, clusters, then ghostly figures performing the same phrases as the company, floating in front of and above them. Loops, choreography for the hands, was released as motion-capture data under a Creative Commons license in 2008. What you see is the dead and the living dancing the same phrase at once, a body and its own data-shadow. **Takeaway:** record the body as data, project the data back beside it, and the dance doubles itself. Against the chain: Cage also overlaid one thing on another and read the result, M10, the transparent sheets of points and lines, but his overlay is a score, and Cunningham's is a second dancer, and the difference is between a reading and a haunting.

## M11. The Event: the repertory as a deck of cards

Excerpts from old or new dances, sometimes two or more simultaneously, are recombined for the space at hand: a museum, a gymnasium, an outdoor stage, a gallery of Serra or Flavin. Events began in 1964 with Museum Event No. 1 in Vienna, and in 1974 Cunningham abandoned his company's repertory evenings for Events, and the Walkaround Time boxes were reused for Events for years after 1968. What you see is a Cunningham evening with no fixed program, the repertory treated as a deck of cards cut to fit the room. **Takeaway:** cut the repertory to fit the room, and every room gets a different evening. Against the chain: Cage also staged an evening with no program, M12, Theatre Piece No. 1, each participant given two time segments to fill as they chose, but his event has no program because nobody agreed on one, and Cunningham's has no fixed program because the room decides it, and the difference is between a clock with unrelated acts and a deck cut to fit the space.

## M12. Chance applied to the whole evening: the die onstage

Not just the movement but the entire production, dance, music, set, costume, light, is made in two versions, and the combination is chosen by a public throw. Split Sides of 2003 has two dance halves, A and B, two scores, Radiohead and Sigur Ros, both without vocals or beats, random sounds fed through electronics, two sets, Robert Heishman and Catherine Yass, two costume schemes, black and white and color, James Hall, and two light plots, the 200 and 300 series, James F. Ingalls, and on the day of performance a die is cast onstage, in front of the audience, to select which version of each element goes first, 32 possible combinations, and on opening night Rauschenberg and Johns rolled the die. What you see is the whole evening decided by a dice throw in public, the audience watching chance happen instead of hearing about it afterwards. **Takeaway:** make everything twice, throw the die where everyone can see it, and the evening becomes an event. Against the chain: Cage also chanced a whole piece, M6, but his chance happened before the concert and the result was fixed forever after, and Cunningham's happens onstage in front of the audience, and the difference is between a coin tossed in private and a die thrown in public.

## M13. The choreographic readymade: a training exercise, lifted whole

A found movement vocabulary, a training exercise, an everyday action, is lifted whole into a dance and treated as material. The opening of Walkaround Time was built from Cunningham's own training technique, "a choreographic readymade", and the non-dance activities of Variations V, potting a plant, smashing a pot, wrapping a towel, are everyday acts made into composition, and the RainForest costumes came from Cunningham's own ripped tights, shown to Johns as the model. What you see is a dancer's warm-up become the opening of a major work, and a pair of torn tights become the costume. **Takeaway:** lift the exercise whole, and the habit becomes the dance. Against the chain: Cage also reworked a found thing, M14, keeping Satie's rhythm and chancing the notes, but his remake is a whole piece rebuilt note by note, and Cunningham's is a habit lifted whole, and the difference is between remaking a piece and reusing a warm-up.

## M14. Collaboration by separation: the wedding-dress method

The collaborators work apart, knowing only the stage dimensions, the number and physical details of the dancers, and the duration, and the pieces meet on opening night. Summerspace was made in three places at once, and Johns did not see the Walkaround Time props with the dancers until dress rehearsal, and Cunningham gave collaborators a single image, "think of the night as if it were day" for Winterbranch, "think of weather" for Points in Space, "think misty" for Inlets, and nothing else. What you see is a wedding dress arriving the morning of the wedding, and three strangers meeting on a stage for the first time in front of an audience. **Takeaway:** tell each collaborator one image and the size of the room, and the meeting will be the work. Against the chain: Cage also separated his collaborators, M5, tabulating every parameter into charts and letting a shape drawn across the table choose, but his separation is a chart, and Cunningham's is a single sentence, and the difference is between a clerk of tables and a giver of images.

## M15. Time units as structure: the clock that binds

The dance is divided into clock units, and dance and music meet only at the unit boundaries. Root of an Unfocus of 1944, the first mature work, was built this way before chance existed in Cunningham's practice, and the same clock discipline runs through sixty years of work, from time brackets to shuffle mode. What you see is a solo dancer working through fear as pure movement, meeting the music only at fixed clock marks, and sixty years later the same discipline holding a dance together. **Takeaway:** fix the clock, let the arts meet only at its marks, and the separation will hold for a lifetime. Against the chain: Cage also fixed a frame first, M1, the proportions 4, 3, 2, 3, 4, each unit subdivided by the same proportions, but his frame is a ratio, and Cunningham's is a clock, and the difference is between a measurement and a duration.

## M16. The body as its own instrument: the technique is the content

The technique itself, the high leg extensions, the light jumps, the torso arches with sudden head-tilts, the intricate footwork, is the content, and there is no story for the body to carry. Torse of 1976 is fifty-five minutes of torso-driven difficulty, ten dancers, and Arlene Croce called the result "monomaniacally urgent", and Ellen Cornfield said her calves were "in contraction for about three years". Roaratorio of 1983 mixes modern and vernacular vocabularies, injecting Cunningham technique with buoyant jigs and reels, leaps, triplets, big-bird arabesques and torso arches with sudden head-tilts. What you see is ten dancers driven through footwork and legwork of unprecedented difficulty, facing every which way at different points, and an Irish village assembling itself out of jigs and found sound. **Takeaway:** make the technique the content, and the body needs no story to carry. Against the chain: Cage also changed the instrument so one pair of hands becomes an orchestra, M2, but his instrument is prepared with screws and bolts, and Cunningham's body is the preparation, and the difference is between a piano that became a drum and a dancer who became the piece.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is the separation, and the dance is not a picture OF anything but a body in time, and everything is relational, the clock against the step, the coin against the sequence, the music against the dance, the decor against the floor, the front against the field, the training exercise against the stage, the screen figure against the live body, the data-shadow against the dancer, the die against the evening, and the relations are what carry the meaning, and he is the choreographer who walked out of expression by the front door, who took the story and replaced it with a step, and the step is the point, because without the step the body would be an illustration and without the body the step would be a word, and with both the two are a single argument, and the argument is that a coin can choreograph a dance, and a dance can be a clock, and a clock can be a stage, and a stage can be a field, and a field can be weather, and weather can be a painting, and a painting can be taken apart and walked through, and a walk can be a dance, and a dance can be data, and data can be a ghost, and a ghost can dance beside the living, and the living can throw a die and let the evening decide itself, and the evening is whatever happens while the clock runs.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. THE OBJECT COUNT IS WRONG: seven, not twelve. The brief says "seven inflatable vinyl rectangles and five clear plastic boxes", which reads as twelve objects. The sources agree the set is SEVEN elements in total: the artron piece says "seven inflatable vinyl rectangles... imprinted with fantastical mechanistic imagery drawn from The Large Glass", and the Ohio State account says the two "bride" sections are hung upstage and the five sections from the "bachelor" portion are distributed around the stage. The five bachelor boxes ARE five of the seven. The study says: seven semi-transparent vinyl boxes, two bride sections hung upstage, five bachelor sections on the floor, all carrying enlarged Large Glass imagery. "Inflatable" is artron's word; the Ohio State account calls them "large semi-transparent vinyl boxes" and the Paris Opera calls them "movable, transparent plastic blocks". The study uses "semi-transparent vinyl boxes" as the neutral description and attributes "inflatable" to the artron account if it is used. C2. THE PREMIERE DATE CONFLICTS. Paris Opera and Festival d'Automne both say Sunday 10 March 1968, State University College, Buffalo, 2nd Festival of the Arts Today. The Ohio State account says the premiere was Tuesday 19 March 1968, in Buffalo, and its author says he attended it. The preponderance, two institutional programmes against one personal recollection, favors 10 March 1968, but the study gives 10 March 1968 and notes the 19 March claim, and does not assert the 19th as settled. Duchamp attended the premiere on either date; he died on Wednesday 2 October 1968, seven months after the 10 March date. C3. JOHNS'S ADVISORSHIP START DATE. The brief's 1967 to 1980 is right; the Ohio State account says Johns "has been the Cunningham company's artistic advisor since 1966". That is the only source giving 1966; Wikipedia, Macaulay, artnet and the Bilbao show all give 1967. The study uses 1967 to 1980 and does not cite the 1966 figure. C4. "THE CHOREOGRAPHER WHO DANCED THE GLASS" NEEDS GLOSSING. Cunningham did perform in Walkaround Time: Macaulay's Design Meets Dance piece says that in Johns's Walkaround Time designs Cunningham "had to change tights onstage while continuing a constantly rhythmic tread of the legs". Macaulay also attributes to the piece "his striptease solo (a reference to Nude Descending a Staircase)" and "the serene solo for Carolyn Brown with its use of astonishing stillness"; the "his" in the striptease reference is Macaulay's reading, and Johns did not confirm or deny it. The study says Cunningham danced inside Johns's Glass and changed costume onstage; it does not present the striptease as certainly Cunningham's own solo without attribution to Macaulay. C5. THE ART STORY MISDATES THE CAGE MEETING. It says Cunningham met Cage "in December of that same year [1939]" at the Cornish School. That is impossible: Cunningham moved to New York in September 1939 to join Graham. The Common Time catalogue essay says that in 1938 Cunningham was a second-year student at Cornish and Cage was the new dance accompanist. The study uses 1938. Note also that Cage was accompanist for Bonnie Bird's dance classes. C6. RAUSCHENBERG'S TENURE DATES CONFLICT. Wikipedia gives resident designer 1954 to 1964; Macaulay says Rauschenberg "worked closely with the Cunningham troupe as its main designer from 1953 to 1964". The company was formed in the summer of 1953, so 1953 is plausible as the start of informal work and 1954 as the formal residency. The study uses 1954 to 1964 and notes Macaulay says 1953. Separately, the Rauschenberg Foundation's Antic Meet listing prints a "revised version: Brooklyn Academy of Music, New York, Jan. 12, 1957", a date before the Thursday 14 August 1958 premiere, which cannot be right as printed; it is presumably a typo for 1967. The study does not cite the 1957 date. C7. GRAHAM TENURE END DATE. Wikipedia and Britannica give 1939 to 1945, six years, leaving Graham's company in 1945. The Teak history says Cunningham danced in Graham's major choreographies "until 1946". The study uses 1939 to 1945; the 1946 figure is a single-source outlier. C8. FRACTIONS DATE. MoMA catalogues Fractions I as 1978, video, 32:59. Macaulay calls it "the 1977 videotape of Fractions" and the Novelsummary essay says Atlas Films "produced Fractions I in 1977". The film was shot and produced in 1977; the work is catalogued 1978. The study dates the work 1978 and notes the film was made in 1977. C9. LIFEFORMS START DATE. Kennedy Center, The Art Story and the Novelsummary essay all say Cunningham began working with LifeForms in 1989. Wikipedia says he "after 1991 choreographed using the computer program LifeForms". Kennedy Center also says "every work Cunningham made after 1991 utilized movement generated on Dance Forms". The honest reading: he began experimenting in 1989 and the program became his regular tool after 1991, when it was redesigned and relaunched as DanceForms. The study uses 1989 for the first experiments, with the 1991 regular-use date noted. C10. WORK COUNTS CONFLICT. Wikipedia: "more than 200 dances and over 800 Events". The Trust's own biography: "180 dances" and "nearly 800 Events". A Screenrant piece citing the Trust says "190 repertory dances and more than 700 events". The study uses the Trust's figures, 180 dances, nearly 800 Events, and does not assert "more than 200" as the Trust's number. C11. BIRTH ORDER CONFLICTS. Wikipedia says Cunningham was "the second of three sons"; The Art Story agrees in substance, "older and younger brothers". The New York Times obituary says he was "the third of four children of Clifford Cunningham, a lawyer, and the former Mayme Joach". Unresolved in the sources read. The study says "one of three sons" per Wikipedia and The Art Story, and avoids the ordinal. Note also the mother's name: Wikipedia gives no maiden name in the text read; the NYT gives "Mayme Joach", The Art Story gives "Mayme Joach Cunningham", encyclopedia.com gives "Marion Cunningham" for the mother, a further conflict on the mother's first name, Mayme against Marion. The study does not assert a maiden name or a first name for the mother. C12. THE ART STORY'S "1953" FOR THEATRE PIECE NO. 1 IS WRONG IN A QUOTED EXCERPT. The Ben Davis artnet piece quoted in the research leg says "the Cage-orchestrated Theater Piece #1 (Black Mountain Piece) of 1953 is sometimes called the 'first happening'." Theatre Piece No. 1 was August 1952, see #398 notes C11. The Davis piece is a secondary excerpt, not a source read end to end; the study does not repeat its 1953 date. C13. CAROLYN BROWN'S BIRTH DATE CONFLICTS INSIDE WIKIPEDIA. The infobox gives 26 September 1927; the body text gives 27 September 1927. Both the Boston Globe obituary and the Dance Magazine obituary use 26 September. The study uses 26 September 1927. Her death, 7 January 2025, aged 97, is agreed. C14. THE LARGE GLASS DATE. The artron piece calls it "Duchamp's 1923 work The Large Glass". The standard dating is 1915 to 1923; 1923 is the year Duchamp declared it definitively unfinished. The study dates the Glass 1915 to 1923, not 1923 alone. C15. EVENTS: 1964 AND 1974 ARE BOTH TRUE. Britannica says "In 1974 Cunningham abandoned his company's repertory... for what he called Events." The Trust biography says Events began in 1964 with Museum Event No. 1. The two dates describe different things: the first Event in 1964 and the abandonment of repertory evenings in favor of Events in 1974. The study states both. C16. "CUNNINGHAM DANCED IN EVERY PERFORMANCE UNTIL 1989" NEEDS ITS SECOND HALF. The NYT obituary says "Until 1989, when he reached age 70, he appeared in every single performance given by his ensemble." Wikipedia says he "performed as a dancer in his company into the early 1990s", and he danced a duet with Baryshnikov in 1999 at 80. Both are true: every performance until 1989, then selected appearances into the 1990s. The study does not write that he stopped dancing in 1989. C17. THE SEASONS CHOREOGRAPHY IS LOST. The ballet premiered on Saturday 17 May 1947 with Cunningham's choreography, but that choreography is now lost. The study does not describe its movement as if it survives; only the score, the Noguchi decor, the nine-section structure and the Indian cosmology are documented. C18. SUITE BY CHANCE'S "FIRST ELECTRONIC SCORE" CLAIM. Britannica says it was "the first modern dance performed to an electronic score, which was commissioned from... Christian Wolff." That is Britannica's claim, single-sourced in this leg. The study attributes it to Britannica rather than stating it as settled fact. C19. SECOND HAND'S SOCRATES. Cunningham saw himself as Socrates in Second Hand, but he never told the dancers: Carolyn Brown learned it only after the premiere, from Cage. The study presents the Socrates reading as Cunningham's private idea, revealed afterwards, not as something the audience could have known. C20. DUCHAMP AND CUNNINGHAM: NO FRIENDSHIP IS EVIDENCED. The Walker mnartists piece says "Duchamp was also a pal of Cunningham and his partner, composer John Cage." That is the only source in this leg calling Duchamp Cunningham's pal, and it is a passing remark in an exhibition review. The documented contact is: one February 1968 dinner, a permission, an attendance at the premiere, and a reported suggestion about the reassembly. The study does not upgrade that into a friendship; the Cage-Duchamp chess friendship is the documented one, see #398 notes.
