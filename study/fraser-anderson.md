## The question: the recorded movement, the gap, and the fading guide

Anderson's career is one long answer to a single question, asked at three scales. At the scale of the body, the Alberta work asks: how does a person acquire a physical skill, and what feedback helps rather than hurts? The surgical rig fuses motion, force, and torque into a skill score; the rehab tabletop records how a stroke patient reaches; the prosthesis trainer turns muscle activation into visible effect. At the scale of the guide, YouMove and the gesture-guides paper ask: how much help is too much, and when must it be withdrawn? The answer, measured, is that help which stays visible becomes a crutch -- performance with the guide and learning without it are anti-correlated. At the scale of the room and the story, the Autodesk work asks: how does a system turn what people do into something reusable -- a three-minute help session archived and searchable, a played scene distilled into a typed narrative frame, a recorded motion edited in space and time? The through-line is the recorded gesture: a rehab exercise in 2009, a dance move in a mirror in 2013, a motion clip in 2022, a character's performance in 2026. [C36344]

The second question is what the following costs. Every one of these systems trades something for its guidance: YouMove trades free movement for a scored comparison against one recorded body; the adaptive gesture guide trades visibility for learning; MicroMentor trades unlimited help for a three-minute slot that strangers will actually accept; PlayWrite trades precise authorial control for an improvising partner (its own study scores Control 3.92, the clear outlier against Expressiveness 6.35). The study therefore has to do two things at once: take the guidance rules seriously as executable mechanisms, and refuse to pretend the trade is free -- each prompt below names what the system gives up, because the giving-up is part of the system. [C36345]

**Takeaway:** Do not build "an Anderson" as a mirror with a skeleton on it. Build the gap rule -- one recorded movement, one live body, one stated comparison -- and build the fading schedule that withdraws the help. [C36346]

## The work in three phases

In 2009-2014 the work is about the body, measured and trained: a multi-touch tabletop for upper-extremity rehabilitation (OzCHI 2009, 12 activities, installed at Glenrose Rehabilitation Hospital, Edmonton); Virtual Wiihab (2010), a Wii-based rehab system that adds measurement and customisation to commercial games; a robotic hexapod reproducing recorded horse motion for equine-assisted therapy (IEEE VR 2010 poster); a laparoscopic-surgery rig fusing optical tracking with force/torque sensing to compute total mechanical work, which correlates negatively with skill (Computer Aided Surgery, 2012); the gesture-guides experiment (CHI 2013); YouMove (UIST 2013); and an AR system for myoelectric prosthesis training (ICDVRAT 2012 conference paper; journal version 2014 -- cite as 2012, not 2014 alone). [C36351]

In 2015-2019 the unit of analysis moves from the learner's body to the thing being made and the room it is made in: subtlety versus candour as two papers answering each other in the same year (Supporting Subtlety, CHI 2015, Best Paper Honorable Mention and Best Talk Award; Candid Interaction, UIST 2015, seven prototypes including the Fog Hat and the Status Wristband); RetroFab (CHI 2016, Best Paper Honorable Mention), retrofitting legacy appliances with proxy interfaces; Crowdsourced Fabrication (UIST 2016), a hundred volunteers choreographed by a foreman engine into building a 12-foot bamboo pavilion; Trigger-Action-Circuits (UIST 2017), behaviour descriptions compiled into circuits; Pineal (CHI 2017), a phone as a smart object's heart; Loki (UIST 2019), bi-directional mixed-reality telepresence for teaching physical tasks at a distance. [C36352]

In 2020-2026 the work turns to knowledge, then to generative creative practice: MicroMentor (CHI 2020) and Think-Aloud Computing (CHI 2021) capture help and intent as archivable fragments; AvatAR (CHI 2022) replays measured motion so an analyst can stand inside the data; VideoPoseVR (2022) and TimeTunnel (CHI 2024) turn recorded motion into editable material, laying time out as an axis in space; WorldSmith (UIST 2023), Paratrouper (CHI 2025), PrevizWhiz (CHI 2026), and PlayWrite (CHI 2026) hand authoring to generative models while keeping the human's hands -- sketching, staging, puppeteering, playing -- on the controls. [C36353]

**Takeaway:** The arc is a widening of the unit that is also a narrowing of the question: the body, then the artefact, then the story -- but the mechanism that survives as a piece is always the same shape, a recorded gesture and a stated rule for what follows it. [C36354]

## M1 -- The recorded body as a trainable reference (YouMove, UIST 2013)

Subject: a trainer performs a full-body movement once before a Kinect; the system captures video, audio, and 3D skeleton data, and the trainer annotates it. Visual principle: the movement is stored not as video alone but as a time series of 20-joint 3D skeletons at 30 Hz plus a sparse set of author-chosen keyframes -- postures that matter -- each naming its important joints (the author clicks joints directly on the frame) and optionally carrying a short audiovisual annotation. The author also sets global parameters: timing, smoothness, precision, stability. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): capture is mp4 video + wav audio + plain-text files of timestamped skeleton locations and keyframe metadata, synchronised by timestamps. The author's skeleton is aligned to each trainee by hierarchical bone-rescaling -- hips aligned first, then each bone resized to match the trainer's corresponding bone, propagating down the hierarchy. Uniform scaling is explicitly rejected; orientation is not aligned (trainer and trainee assumed to face the Kinect the same way). Artistic opportunity: the recording is a function `posture(t) -> 20 joint positions` plus a list of `(t_k, {important joints}, annotation?)`. A generative analogue needs only this: a reference trajectory, a sparse set of moments that matter, and a per-moment subset of joints that matter. Most generated motion is continuous and undifferentiated; YouMove shows how selecting a few moments and a few joints turns a stream into something judgeable. [C36355]

**Takeaway:** A movement becomes teachable when it is reduced to the moments that matter and the joints that matter. Build the keyframes, not the stream. [C36356]

## M2 -- The mirror that cross-fades self and guide (YouMove)

Subject: a 3.2 m x 1.8 m half-silvered mirror with a rear projector, a Kinect below it, and a servo-driven room-light dimmer. Visual principle: the mirror film transmits 16% of visible light and reflects 58%, so the trainee's reflection and the projected image can be visible at once. The balance is controlled by ambient light: bright room + dark projection = reflection only; dark room + bright projection = projection only; moderate light = both. The system multiplexes attention between the self and the guide by dimming the room. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the mirror corners and the user's head position define an asymmetric projection matrix, so projected content renders in the mirror's coordinate space, aligned to the reflection; the only calibration is the mirror's location in Kinect space. Interaction is by reflection selection -- the user's reflected hand dwells over a button to activate it; during dwell the button expands, increasing the activation area in case the hand drifts. Global menu buttons sit on the left (never triggered accidentally during training) and their vertical position adapts to the user's height; contextual buttons appear near the head, body-relative. Virtual cursors appear if the system has waited more than 5 seconds for a selection. Artistic opportunity: two images share one surface, and a single scalar (ambient light) cross-fades between them -- `visibility(reflection) ∝ ambient`, `visibility(projection) ∝ 1 - ambient`. A generative work could cross-fade between "what you are doing" and "what the system wants" with one continuous control, the blend itself being the state of the piece. The growing dwell target is a smaller but real idea: a control that becomes more forgiving as you hesitate. [C36357]

**Takeaway:** One scalar can hold two pictures on one surface. Build the cross-fade, not the two pictures. [C36358]

## M3 -- Score the worst joint, forgive the phase (YouMove)

Subject: after each stage the trainee is scored against the trainer's movement; a gold star (score >= 7.5) unlocks the next stage, and repeating a stage twice also unlocks it. Visual principle: error is shown as red circles overlaid on the trainee's joints in the mirror, circle radius proportional to joint error; if the largest error is in the depth (z) axis, a call-out window opens showing a side view of the trainee's body superimposed on the trainer's. In post-stage feedback, error is shown as two skeletons -- the trainee's average skeleton across repetitions in blue, the trainer's in green -- with the same red circles; the trainee can walk left or right to rotate the 3D view. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): each keyframe is scored on the joint with the maximum error (Euclidean distance, metres), over only the author-specified important joints. To allow small timing errors, a window of +/-0.5 s around the target frame is searched for the best-matching posture; if the author flagged timing as important, the window shrinks to +/-0.25 s. Maximum Euclidean distance maps linearly to a score: 0 error = 10; 0.15 m error = 7.5 (the gold-star threshold). If precision is flagged, the mapping tightens so 0.10 m = 7.5. Values were determined by experimentation. The Posture Guide pauses at each keyframe until the trainee holds a stable position for 1 s or 5 s elapse; a progress bar shows accumulated stable time. If stability is flagged, the threshold drops from 5 cm to 3 cm per frame. The same skeleton comparison powers query by example -- the user holds a representative posture, the system snapshots it and compares it against every keyframe in the library, returning the most similar movements in a grid. Evaluation: YouMove improved pre-to-post-test scores 44% vs 20% for video (F(1,7) = 9.98, p = 0.02), measured as RMSE over neck, hands, elbows, knees, feet. Artistic opportunity: `score = 10 - (50/3) * min over |dt| <= w of max over important joints of ||p_trainee - p_trainer||`. Three design choices do the work: (1) score by the worst joint, not the average -- one bad limb fails you; (2) a time window that forgives phase error but not shape error; (3) a single linear map with a named threshold, so the number is always legible. A piece that judges by the single largest deviation produces a different drama from one that averages: attention snaps to the one red circle, the one limb that is wrong -- a spotlight mechanism, not a grade. [C36359]

**Takeaway:** Judge by the worst, forgive the timing. Build the one red circle. [C36360]

## M4 -- Guidance that fades on a schedule (YouMove's five stages; Gesture Guides, CHI 2013)

Subject: training proceeds through five stages -- Demonstration, Posture Guide, Movement Guide, Mirror, On Your Own -- in order (locked until unlocked), each presenting the same movement with less guidance. Visual principle: each stage removes one layer of support: video only -> frozen keyframes with error circles -> full-speed skeleton with cue ribbons -> bare reflection with audio counting -> nothing but a starting cue ("And") and a white screen that hides the reflection entirely. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): Demonstration plays the recorded video; a voice counts "And ... One, Two ..." at each keyframe (numbers, not a metronome, to help memorise the sequence); counting is present in all stages except On Your Own. Posture Guide pauses at each keyframe, the trainer's skeleton renders green and is re-aligned to the trainee every frame, repeats twice. Movement Guide runs the skeleton at full speed without pausing, re-aligned to the trainee's starting location each repetition, plus cue ribbons: 3D trajectories of the hands and feet 300 ms ahead of the current frame, opacity increasing with how far ahead they are, shown only if the joint will exceed 75 cm/s within the next 300 ms. If smoothness is flagged, ribbons extend to 500 ms ahead. (The paper prints "500m/s", an evident typo for 500 ms.) Mirror projects a black screen, ambient light up, no visual cues, audio counting only. On Your Own projects white, ambient light off (no reflection visible), only the word "And" as a start cue. Post-stage feedback aggregates across repetitions: average skeleton per keyframe, average score, and a summary video per keyframe -- the trainer as a static image, the trainee as one image per repetition shown for 0.5 s each, so variation across repetitions is visible as flicker. The gesture-guides paper (Anderson & Bischof, CHI 2013, Paris, 27 April-2 May 2013, pp. 1109-1118, DOI 10.1145/2470654.2466143) supplies the same rule in miniature: four guides compared under a retention-and-transfer paradigm (tested 15 min and 24 h later, with no guide). The adaptive guide is a full-scale template the user traces that disappears part-way through the stroke, earlier and earlier as training proceeds: first trial it vanishes when stroke length equals target length (effectively never); midway, at half the target length; by the final trial it never appears. During training, error order was Static < Dynamic < Adaptive < Crib; at retention the order inverted -- Crib and Adaptive had significantly lower error than either traceable guide. The dynamic guide was worst on both axes, because every update pulled the guide away from the template, so tracing it meant deviating from the target. Artistic opportunity: the stage sequence is a fading schedule over a guidance vector `[video, frozen keyframes, error circles, ribbons, reflection, audio]`, one component removed per stage. The cue ribbon rule is executable as stated -- `show ribbon for joint j iff max velocity of j over next 300 ms > 75 cm/s; opacity ∝ time-ahead` -- a visual rule for anticipation applicable to any trajectory. The flickering summary video is a way to show a distribution rather than a single outcome. Most interactive pieces keep the same interface forever; YouMove argues the interface should decay on purpose. And the dynamic-guide failure is a warning: a guide that updates to match you teaches you the wrong thing. Any piece that adapts its guidance should adapt by withdrawing, not by conforming. [C36361]

**Takeaway:** The interface should delete itself on a schedule. Build the withdrawal, not the help. [C36362]

## M5 -- The three-minute clock (MicroMentor, CHI 2020)

Subject: peer-to-peer software help: a novice asks a question, an expert answers over video, the session is capped at 3 minutes and recorded into a searchable archive. Visual principle: the bound itself is the design: a countdown timer in the meeting toolbar turns red at 30 s left, flashes at 10 s, and ends the call at 0 s. The asker knows the cost before asking; the helper knows the commitment before accepting. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): formative study, 6 participants, Fusion 360, in-person: 37 questions; 1-minute sessions answered 39%, 3-minute sessions 83%, no-limit 92%. Mean session 136 s under a 3-minute cap, 228 s with no limit; questions answered under no limit took 166 s on average. Stress: 3 minutes "not stressful"; 1 minute stressful for complex questions; no limit produced guilt about using the helper's time. Askers took 16 s on average to state the question. Evaluation, 12 participants, MicroMentor vs web resources: mean time from posting to a helper accepting = 28 s; asker joins ~5 s later; mean session 131 s; total average request 164 s. 85% of requests rated 4-5 stars (23/27); helpers gave 5 stars 78% of the time. Artistic opportunity: a hard cap `T = 180 s` with two warning thresholds (`T-30 s` colour change, `T-10 s` flashing) plus automatic termination. The empirical claim is that `T = 180` sits at the knee of the curve: doubling the cap from 60 s to 180 s more than doubles success (39% -> 83%), while removing the cap gains only 9 points at the cost of unbounded time and guilt. The mechanism is not "short sessions" but a known, short, fixed duration. A piece could run in 3-minute movements with the same warning thresholds, so the audience feels the same pressure: enough time to do something real, not enough to ramble. [C36363]

**Takeaway:** A known bound is a kindness. Build the clock, not the session. [C36364]

## M6 -- The request that carries its own context (MicroMentor)

Subject: one button starts a screen + audio recording that auto-stops after 16 s; the system transcribes the audio, captures a screenshot, and attaches the user's last 5 commands. Visual principle: the request card shows topic + supplementary content (screenshot or recording) + the asker's recent command history. The helper decides from the card alone, without a meeting first. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): two submission paths -- a small button bottom-right of the screen, or the "Ask" panel. Recording auto-ends at 16 s or on "Done"; typing is also allowed (for users unwilling to share screen/voice). A screenshot is captured automatically if no video. Last 5 commands are attached automatically; the number of active users is shown on the "Help Request" button. Command histories are extracted from the OS accessibility API: clicked GUI elements expose names ("Pivot Table"), which become a human-readable command log. Matching: the system ranks all available helpers by a match score from seven factors -- (1) command-history overlap with the asker's most recent commands; (2) expertise level (novice/intermediate/advanced, self-set), helpers at or above the asker's level preferred; (3) time since last help session, to avoid overload; (4) previous interactions with this asker; (5) average star rating; (6) additional self-declared skills; (7) relevance of those skills to the request topic. Triage notifies one distinct user every 10 s in sorted order until the request is accepted; helpers see their own match score and a pink "Top Match" badge if they are best-ranked. Accepting auto-starts a video conference; the asker is auto-added after 5 s. After the session both parties rate out of 5 stars; if the asker rates <= 3, the system offers to resubmit -- to all other helpers (the low-rated helper gets no push notification). Sessions are auto-recorded and archived: {video, speech transcript, command log, duration, satisfaction flag, participants}; search indexes topic, application, commands, and speech, and the transcript is synchronised to the video so commands and utterances are navigation points. Two honest gaps: the match-score weights and combination function are not specified in the paper, and the evaluation disabled mentor matching entirely ("left for future work"), so the matching mechanism is described but unvalidated -- no weights are invented here. Artistic opportunity: `request = {topic (typed or transcribed), media (16 s max recording or auto screenshot), context (last 5 commands)}`. The 16 s cap on the question mirrors the 180 s cap on the answer: both ends of the exchange are bounded, and the bound on the question is what makes the bound on the answer achievable -- the helper has already seen the problem before the clock starts. The deeper transfer: the system silently records what the user was doing just before they asked, and hands that to the responder. A piece could keep a rolling window of the visitor's recent actions and show it back as the context for whatever happens next. And the resubmission rule is a small, complete feedback loop: failure routes around the failure, automatically. [C36365]

**Takeaway:** What you did just before you asked is the question. Build the rolling window, not the form. [C36366]

## M7 -- Play as input, intent frames as the unit (PlayWrite, CHI 2026)

Subject: in passthrough XR (Unity 6, Apple Vision Pro, PolySpatial), the user stages a story on a bounded platform with virtual characters and props, speaking dialogue in-role and moving figures by hand. Visual principle: each character has two mutually exclusive interaction areas: grab the body to talk, grab the transform handle near the character to move it -- so the system never has to guess whether a grab means "speak" or "move". Props attach to a character's "hand zones" when brought within a proximity threshold -- giving, holding, dropping as a spatial act. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): Grab to Talk -- grabbing a character's body starts voice input, triggers an idle-to-talking animation, activates the speech subsystem, and hides the transform handle to prevent gesture conflict; a short delay ensures the animation starts before voice capture begins. Drag to Move -- dragging the transform handle computes a target destination and updates pose and animation state in real time; collision bounds on the platform restrict movement to allowable regions; movement stops when the target is reached or the drag ends. Attach a Prop -- objects brought near a hand zone within a proximity threshold auto-attach to that hand. The dialogue history panel logs recognised speech with timestamp and the character name the user is speaking as. The dialogue loop has three modes: reactive speech -- when the user finishes voicing a character, the system triggers a context-aware response from the corresponding AI character (addressee resolution: first check whether the user's line names another character; if no name, use spatial orientation -- whichever character the speaker is facing); proactive speech -- if no input (speech, grab, or movement) is detected for 10 s, the system treats it as inactivity and lets a character speak autonomously; override -- the user can grab an AI-controlled character at any time and voice its line, replacing the previous AI response ("the way to correct the AI and put your own thing in is to pick up the character", P6). Each character has a configurable token budget (typically 512-2048 tokens) and a unique system prompt; GPT-4-turbo; latency under 2 s per turn. Study, N=13 writers: action mix differed by task -- Aladdin (closed-ended): user speech 48%, movement ~20%, AI reactive 17%, AI proactive 9%; Robin Hood (open-ended): movement 32%, user speech 29%, AI reactive 21%, AI proactive 10%; object grabs low in both (6-10%). CSI: Expressiveness 6.35, Enjoyment 6.15, Immersion 5.85, Results Worth Effort 5.85, Exploration 5.38, Control 3.92 -- control the clear outlier; the paper is explicit that preserving precise intent is the unsolved tension. Artistic opportunity: a three-mode dialogue state machine -- `reactive (on user utterance end)`, `proactive (on 10 s silence)`, `override (on grab + speak)` -- with addressee resolution as a two-step rule (name -> facing). It needs no language model to be useful: any generative piece with autonomous agents can use the same three modes -- respond when the visitor acts, act after 10 s of stillness, always let the visitor seize an agent mid-sentence. The addressee rule is embodied logic worth stealing: who you face is who you address. And proximity as commitment -- bring a thing close enough to a hand and it belongs to that hand; no button, no menu; distance is the verb. [C36367]

**Takeaway:** Give each agent two handles, one per verb, and never let the system guess which verb a grab means. Build the two handles, not the dialogue. [C36368]

## M8 -- First time counts: novelty with a cool-down, commit on count-or-timeout (PlayWrite)

Subject: a multi-agent pipeline watches the play and distils it into Intent Frames -- structured narrative beats, each visualised as a rearrangeable story marble on a timeline. The marbles, in the user's order, are passed to an LLM that writes a synopsis or screenplay. Visual principle: a marble is a small, self-contained, tangible unit -- "interactive tangible bits rather than compiled code blocks" -- with a card showing a description and a snapshot of the event. Picking one up replays the 3D scene and dialogue with characters restored to their marks at that moment. Marbles can be picked up, grouped, discarded, re-sequenced; the timeline order is the story order. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): three observer agents run in parallel and asynchronously, emitting semantically tagged observations (IntentFeatures: actor, target, location, timestamp, semanticLabel, confidence). Environment Agent polls Zones of Interest, Movement Trails, and Prop Proximity. Observations are consumed selectively by Environmental Salience S_e: salience rises with significant movement (displacement beyond a threshold m_MovementThreshold), with proximity events, and with zone entry. Social Agent tracks character-prop relationships, character-character interactions, and behavioural patterns, prioritised by Social Novelty S_s: highest for first-time interactions; a repeat between the same characters within a 5-second cool-down is heavily down-weighted, so only sustained or newly re-initiated encounters count. Narrator Agent is the memory backbone: full dialogue history, character arcs (emotional states, evolving goals, unresolved tensions), role consistency, referencing a StoryRoleConfiguration file as the blueprint for coherence. The Intent Frame Agent fuses the three streams -- temporal filtering (suppress jittery movements, repeated low-salience cues), contextual prioritisation (weight by relevance to active arcs, spatial transitions, user-driven patterns), cross-agent fusion (co-occurring observations merge -- e.g. Environment reports movement + Social detects a relation shift -> "approach with anger"). Each frame is ranked by `R = w_e*E + w_s*S + w_n*N`, where E = Environmental Salience (e.g. object displacement > 0.5 m), S = Social Novelty, N = Narrative Progression Likelihood from the Narrator's logs. Weights adjust dynamically at runtime from recent event history. Frames sit in a priority queue; a commit happens once a threshold is reached (e.g. N = 5 events) or after a timeout (T = 1 s). A committed Intent Frame is a C# object with a ranked list of merged actions, characters, time, a tension score (1 = calm to 10 = extreme conflict, LLM-computed from actions and dialogue), and a resolved intentType -- a classification by narrative function in a three-act structure (e.g. IncitingIncident, RisingAction, Climax), grounded in McKee. Story assembly treats the marble order as canonical; the paper is candid that extreme reordering can produce continuity glitches (objects before introduction, characters re-entering after exit) -- read as jump-cut or flashback. Honest gaps: the paper does not publish the salience weights, the displacement threshold beyond the 0.5 m example, or the dynamic weight-adjustment rule in numeric form; tension and intentType are LLM-judged, not rule-computed. Artistic opportunity: the pipeline is `raw events -> per-agent salience/novelty scores -> weighted fusion R -> threshold/timeout commit -> typed frame (tension 1-10, act-function label) -> tangible token -> ordered sequence -> rendered narrative`. The two most portable rules: first-time counts, repeats within 5 s don't (a novelty decay with a cool-down), and commit on count-or-timeout (N = 5 or T = 1 s -- a buffer that flushes on either fullness or age). This transfers without any AI: a piece could watch its own scene with the same three eyes -- where things are, who is near whom, what has happened before -- commit the winners as tangible tokens, and let the audience reorder the tokens to re-sequence the piece. And the novelty rule is a general antidote to the most common failure of generative art: repeating its best trick until it is noise. [C36369]

**Takeaway:** Commit the event when it is full or when it is old, and never let a repeat count as a first time. Build the marble, not the story. [C36370]

## Cross-cutting mechanisms

Four rules recur across all four papers, each stated as an executable rule rather than a theme: (1) Guidance must fade, on a schedule the system controls. YouMove's five stages, the adaptive guide's vanishing trace, the gesture paper's retention paradigm: help that stays visible becomes a crutch, and the system is designed around its own withdrawal. Executable form: a guidance level `g` that decreases as a function of repetitions or stage, never increasing. (2) Score the worst, not the average. YouMove scores a keyframe by its maximum joint error; PlayWrite's salience picks the single most important event; MicroMentor's matching ranks by the best helper. In each case one extremal value drives the decision. Executable form: `score = max(error)` or `pick = argmax(salience)`, never a mean. (3) Forgive phase, judge shape. YouMove's +/-0.5 s search window, the gesture paper's RMSE over resampled (phase-normalised) trajectories, PlayWrite's temporal filtering of jitter: all three separate when something happened from what it was. Executable form: align first (window search, resampling), then compare. (4) First time counts; repeats decay. PlayWrite's Social Novelty (first contact high, repeats within 5 s down-weighted), MicroMentor's "time since last session" factor and duplicate-question routing, YouMove's stage unlock on repetition (twice = pass, so repetition is a floor, not a reward). Executable form: a novelty score with a cool-down window, so a repeated event contributes less than a new one. [C36371]

## Aesthetic logic

Anderson's systems are never about the device. The mirror is a 3.2 m sheet of half-silvered glass with a projector behind it; the interesting object is the blend, one scalar deciding how much of the self and how much of the guide is visible. The gesture guide is a line that disappears; the interesting object is the schedule of its disappearance. The three-minute session is a clock; the interesting object is the knee of the curve at 180 seconds, where doubling the time more than doubles the success and removing the bound gains almost nothing. The story marble is a token; the interesting object is the rule that decides when an event is worth keeping. In every case the artefact is a container for a rule, and the rule is small enough to state in one sentence: score the worst joint; fade the guide by repetition; cap the exchange at three minutes; commit on five events or one second; first time counts, repeats decay. The visual language follows from the rule rather than preceding it: red circles sized by error, a ribbon that appears only when a joint is about to move fast, a timer that turns red at thirty seconds, a marble that exists only because an event crossed a threshold. Nothing is decorated; everything that is visible is the state of a rule. That is the aesthetic to carry into the pieces: the picture should be the rule's current state, and nothing else. [C36372]
