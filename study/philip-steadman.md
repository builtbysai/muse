## The life: the architect who never practised, and measured everything instead

Philip Steadman was born in 1942 in Northwich, Cheshire, to Dorothy and Fred Steadman; his father was a chemical engineer at ICI, at a factory making caustic soda. (C12303, leg 1) His mother read English literature at Oxford and his father read chemistry there; his mother died when he was sixteen, his father resigned from ICI in his fifties and became a Church of England vicar, and Steadman describes himself as an atheist. (C12305, C12307, leg 1) He decided he wanted to be an architect at about ten, made model buildings from childhood, loved drawing, visited old churches and drew architectural subjects. (C12308, leg 1) He was a boarder at the Dragon School, Oxford, from age eight, then won a scholarship to Winchester College, where he specialised in sciences and mathematics, failed O-level chemistry, and started a printing society with Alex Reid on an old press left to the school. (C12309, C12310, leg 1) It was at Winchester that he met Stephen Bann as a schoolboy. (C12419, leg 3; = #659 C12104)

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is the move from editing the avant-garde to measuring the built world: the work starts as concrete poetry and kinetic art, becomes the mathematics of plans, turns to the optics of old paintings, and ends as the modelling of a whole country's buildings.

(1) Cambridge and Form, 1960-1969. Steadman studied architecture at Cambridge, 1960-1965, at Trinity Hall: a 2:2 after three years, then a resit of the fourth- and fifth-year diploma. (C12311-C12313, leg 1; Cambridge start 1960 per his own site, 1959 per his Buildings and Cities reflection -- unresolved, see C12463) He met his wife Ruth Brandon at Cambridge, through the Society of Arts. (C12314, leg 1) His teachers included Leslie Martin, Professor and Head of Department, C. A. St John Wilson, Colin Rowe, and the young Peter Eisenman. (C12316, leg 1) In 1963 he heard Ivan Sutherland talk about "Sketchpad" at the Cambridge Mathematical Laboratory, the beginning of his interest in computer-aided design. (C12317, leg 1) At Cambridge he became interested in concrete poetry and kinetic art, and with friends organised an exhibition at St Catharine's College, the International Exhibition of Kinetic and Concrete Poetry. (C12315, leg 1) With Mike Weaver and Stephen Bann he founded Form magazine: ten issues, 1966-1969, Steadman primary editor throughout. (C12318-C12321, leg 1) He also edited Image: Kinetic Art: Concrete Poetry (November 1964), a 64-page magazine issue, and co-authored Kinetic Art: Four Essays with Bann, Gadney and Popper in 1966. (C12352, C12353, leg 2)

(2) The Martin Centre, the PhD that never was, and the first CAD company, 1965-1977. After graduating he joined the newly formed Centre for Land Use and Built Form Studies at Cambridge, later the Martin Centre, working on university planning and people's use of time in towns. (C12323, leg 1) As a graduate student he wrote a PhD on biological analogy in architecture, with Leslie Martin officially his supervisor -- though Steadman says Martin never gave him advice and he never met Martin once for a supervision. (C12324, leg 1) He never got the PhD: three examiners died during examination, including Lancelot Law Whyte; three vivas and four committees over several years. (C12326, leg 1) He withdrew, and later got an ScD on published work, including the book that came out of the thesis. (C12327, leg 1; ScD year unknown) The thesis was published as The Evolution of Designs: Biological Analogy in Architecture and the Applied Arts (Cambridge University Press, 1979), built on D'Arcy Thompson's work on form. (C12328, leg 1) In 1967 he and Richard Stibbs produced a primitive interactive graphical system for laying out rooms along corridors and giving them sizes, drawing on a cathode ray tube with a light pen. (C12330, leg 1) In 1969 he and colleagues set up Applied Research of Cambridge, one of the world's first architectural CAD companies, twenty people from the department each putting in 100 pounds; it grew fast, built big tailor-made systems mainly for building hospitals, and ended in 1984 after a McDonnell Douglas buyout. (C12331, C12332, leg 1) He was a visiting research fellow at Princeton University in 1972, and a consultant in the Middle East, including at Pahlavi University at Shiraz in Iran, on a new campus, before the fall of the Shah. (C12329, C12333, leg 1)

(3) The Open University, 1977-1998. Steadman joined the Open University in 1977, at the Centre for Configurational Studies, a group brought together by Lionel March; he was Director until 1998. (C12334, C12335, leg 1) He chose the Open University lectureship over an offer of Assistant Director of Research at Cambridge. (C12336, leg 1) At the Open University he taught history of art as well as technology, and did early work on mathematical methods for representing and enumerating small rectangular plans, leading to studies of the British housing stock and non-domestic buildings. (C12337, leg 1) The books of this period are The Geometry of Environment with Lionel March (1971), Energy, Environment and Building (1975), The Evolution of Designs (1979), Doomsday: Britain After Nuclear Attack with Openshaw and Greene (1983), and Architectural Morphology (1983). (C12342, leg 1)

(4) UCL and the camera books, 1999-2026. He joined UCL in 1999. (C12338, leg 1) From the early 1990s he worked for the UK government, with large teams, on modelling the building stock of England and Wales and its energy use; with colleagues he developed the 3DStock method. (C12339, leg 1) The London Building Stock Model was delivered to the Greater London Authority in 2020, and in 2026 his UCL group delivered the National Buildings Database to the UK Department of Energy Security and Net Zero, covering all buildings in Great Britain and their energy use; the project ran 2023-2026, led by Professor Paul Ruyssevelt. (C12340, C12341, leg 1) In parallel came the camera books: Vermeer's Camera (Oxford University Press, 2001), Building Types and Built Forms (2014), Why Are Most Buildings Rectangular? (2017), Renaissance Fun: The Machines Behind the Scenes (UCL Press, 2021), and Canaletto's Camera (UCL Press, June 2025), supported by a Leverhulme Trust Emeritus Fellowship awarded in 2021. (C12343, C12344, leg 1) He also wrote books on energy and buildings, American cities, and the effects of nuclear attack on Britain, and helped produce four computer-animated films on Leonardo da Vinci for a London exhibition in 1989. (C12345, leg 1) He married Ruth Brandon in 1972; she is a British journalist, historian and author, born 1943. (C12346, leg 1) They have one daughter, Lily, born 1982, a museum curator in Leamington Spa, married to Tom Crowther, a philosopher at Warwick, with two children, Isaac and Beatrice. (C12347, leg 1) He is a member of the Labour Party, was involved in the CND campaign against nuclear weapons, still draws by hand -- doing architectural illustrations for papers and books, and saying drawing is an opportunity for thinking -- and loved skating from his Dragon School days, though by the 2020 interview he no longer skated for fear of falling and breaking something. (C12348, C12349, leg 1) His current titles are Emeritus Professor of Urban and Built Form Studies at the Bartlett, Senior Research Associate at the UCL Energy Institute, and Principal Research Fellow in the UCL staff directory. No honours beyond the Leverhulme Emeritus Fellowship (2021) and the ScD were found; none are assumed. (Leg 1 Sec. 12)

## Mechanism M1: graph-theoretic plan generation -- the plan assembles itself from a graph

A house plan is a graph: rooms are points, adjacency or connectivity between rooms are lines. Enumerate the graphs that satisfy a minimum standard, then generate the plans that fit them. Automatic Generation of Minimum-Standard House Plans (Working Paper 23, Cambridge, 1970) is the mechanism in print, and the 1967 system Steadman built with Richard Stibbs is the mechanism in action: a primitive interactive graphical system for laying out rooms along corridors and giving them sizes, drawing on a cathode ray tube with a light pen. (C12358, C12359, leg 2 W04; C12330, leg 1) The mechanism is the plan as a solved constraint: the rooms are not drawn and then connected, they are connected and then drawn, and the corridor snaps into place when the graph is satisfied.

**Takeaway:** the graph is the plan before the plan is drawn. A piece built on this mechanism must show a plan assembling itself room by room as a graph is satisfied -- corridors snapping into place, rooms resizing as the connections demand -- and must let the viewer see the graph and the plan as one thing, not two.

## Mechanism M2: rectangular dissection and plan enumeration -- every possible split, counted

A rectangle can be cut into smaller rectangles by every possible dissection, and the whole space of possible plans can be listed and counted. Steadman's Open University work developed mathematical methods for representing and enumerating small rectangular plans, and Architectural Morphology (1983) is the book of the method; The Geometry of Environment (1971, with Lionel March) is its first statement. (C12368, leg 2 W09; C12337, leg 1; C12360, leg 2 W05) The mechanism is exhaustion as a design tool: instead of inventing one plan, enumerate all of them, and the design problem becomes choosing among a counted set rather than guessing at an uncounted one.

**Takeaway:** the rectangle contains all its plans. A piece on this mechanism must show a rectangle splitting and re-splitting through every possible dissection, with the count of possible plans ticking up -- and must let the viewer feel the space of possibilities as a real, countable thing, not a metaphor.

## Mechanism M3: morphospace mapping of building types -- real types as points among the never-built

Lay building forms out in a "morphospace", a coordinate space of possible forms, so real building types sit as points among the forms that are possible but never built. Building Types and Built Forms (2014), with more than 400 illustrations, lays forms out in morphospaces, and Why Are Most Buildings Rectangular? (2017) asks the question the empty regions pose: if the rectangle is only one point in the space, why does almost everything sit on it? (C12379, C12380, leg 2 W15; C12381, C12382, leg 2 W16) The mechanism is the map of what could be: the built world is a small lit region inside a much larger dark space of forms, and the dark space is the interesting part, because it shows what was never tried.

**Takeaway:** the empty regions are the argument. A piece on this mechanism must show a cloud of possible forms with real types lighting up inside it -- and must make the unlit regions visible, because what was never built is the question the piece asks.

## Mechanism M4: biological analogy and copying drift -- designs evolve by being copied badly

Designs evolve like organisms: repeated copying with small variations, so forms drift over generations. The Evolution of Designs (1979, revised 2008) is the thesis that came out of the PhD that never was, built on D'Arcy Thompson's work on form; Steadman's copying experiments showed that drawings copied again and again tend over time to become more diagrammatic. (C12364, C12365, leg 2 W07; C12328, leg 1; C12399, leg 2) The mechanism is drift by hand: each copyist simplifies a little, drops a detail, straightens a curve, and after enough generations the drawing is a diagram of itself -- evolution without a designer, only a chain of copyists.

**Takeaway:** copying is the engine, not the error. A piece on this mechanism must show a drawing copied again and again, each generation simplifying toward a diagram -- and must let the viewer watch a family tree of forms branch by variation, with no generation drawn from imagination, only from the last copy.

## Mechanism M5: inverse-perspective reconstruction -- the flat painting unfolds into a room

From the perspective geometry of a painting, work backwards to reconstruct the three-dimensional room and the viewpoint it was painted from: measure the vanishing points and the orthogonals, and rebuild the space in 3D. Vermeer's Camera (2001) is the mechanism applied at full length: inverse perspective reconstructs ten Vermeer interiors, room by room, viewpoint by viewpoint. (C12375, C12376, leg 2 W13; C12432, leg 3) The mechanism is the painting as a measurement: the flat image contains the room, encoded in its perspective, and the reconstruction is a decoding, not an interpretation.

**Takeaway:** the painting is the room, encoded. A piece on this mechanism must take a flat interior and unfold it into a measurable 3D room -- walls and furniture sliding into their true positions as the vanishing points are found -- and must let the viewer see the flat image and the rebuilt room as the same object in two states.

## Mechanism M6: camera-obscura booth projection -- the image is the size of the canvas

A closed booth or cubicle, with the back wall as projection screen: light enters through a lens and throws an image onto the back wall, and the projected image is the same size as the canvas, so the painter can trace it directly. Vermeer's Camera (2001) turns on this finding: the projected images at the back wall of at least six Vermeer interiors are the same sizes as the respective canvases. (C12376, leg 2 W13) Canaletto's Camera (2025) closes the line: Canaletto's 1730s sketchbook of 120 pages was drawn with a camera obscura, and Steadman's colleague Adam Azmy reconstructed an eighteenth-century camera to prove it. (C12387, C12388, leg 2 W19; C12435, leg 3) The mechanism is the dark room as a drawing machine: the painter does not invent the view, he traces the light, and the trace is the painting.

**Takeaway:** the room is the camera. A piece on this mechanism must show light entering a dark booth and throwing a life-size image onto the back wall, with a hand tracing its outlines -- and must let the viewer feel the moment the projected image and the canvas become the same size, because that equality is the whole claim.

## Mechanism M7: 3DStock building-stock modelling -- a city rises as counted blocks

Model a whole building stock -- a city, then a country -- as 3D forms with energy use attached to every building, so the stock can be counted, mapped and steered to net zero. The London Building Stock Model (2020) covers every building in the 33 London Boroughs; the National Buildings Database (2023-2026) covers all buildings in Great Britain; the 3DStock method behind both was developed with colleagues from the early 1990s. (C12383, C12384, leg 2 W17; C12389, C12390, leg 2 W20; C12339, leg 1) The mechanism is the city as a database with a shape: each building is a block with a number attached, and the numbers add up to a country's energy use.

**Takeaway:** the city is a count before it is a skyline. A piece on this mechanism must show a city rising as a field of extruded blocks coloured by energy use, zooming out from one street to the whole country -- and must let the viewer see the count grow as the view widens, because the total is the work, not the view.

## Mechanism M8: reconstructing hidden machines -- a still engraving comes alive

Take a Renaissance machine described in Vitruvius or Hero of Alexandria, or a Leonardo device, and rebuild it as a working computer animation or model so its hidden mechanism can be seen moving. The four computer-animated Leonardo films for the Hayward Gallery exhibition (1989) are the first form; Renaissance Fun: The Machines Behind the Scenes (2021) is the book form, centred on Vitruvius and Hero, running through stage machinery, gardens, fountains, automata and self-playing instruments, and ending at Pratolino and Mercury and Mars in Parma in 1628. (C12369, C12370, leg 2 W10; C12385, C12386, leg 2 W18) The mechanism is reconstruction as revelation: the engraving shows the machine at rest, the animation shows it working, and the working is the argument that the machine was real.

**Takeaway:** the machine is the motion, not the picture of it. A piece on this mechanism must take a still engraving of a machine and bring it alive -- gears turning, fountains playing, automata moving -- and must let the viewer see the hidden mechanism, because the hidden part is the piece.

## Aesthetic logic

Steadman has no recognisable style, and the reason is the opposite of Bann's: where Bann arranges other people's work, Steadman measures other people's work -- and the measurement is the style. The continuity is a single move repeated across sixty years: take a finished thing, work backwards to the rules that made it, then run the rules forward to see what else they could make. A house plan becomes a graph, and the graph generates every plan that fits the rules. A rectangle becomes a dissection, and the dissection enumerates every plan the rectangle allows. A building type becomes a point in a morphospace, and the morphospace shows the types that were never built. A drawing copied again and again becomes a diagram, and the diagram shows how designs evolve. A Vermeer becomes a set of vanishing points, and the vanishing points rebuild the room and the booth the painter stood in. A city becomes a field of extruded blocks, and the blocks carry the energy of every building in Britain. The materials are the cheapest instruments that measure: a graph, a rectangle, a vanishing point, a light pen, a dark booth, a database. The tone is patient and empirical where Bann's is patient and literary: Steadman's books demystify without diminishing -- Vermeer used a camera, and the paintings are no smaller for it; most buildings are rectangular, and the reason is geometry, not failure. The scale moves from a single room laid out with a light pen to a magazine of ten issues to a company of twenty people to a database of every building in Great Britain, and the durability moves from a stapled magazine that can be lost to a national database that a government department now runs -- but the hand stays the same: the hand that still draws by hand, because drawing is an opportunity for thinking. A piece built from this study should feel measured, enumerated and reconstructed -- graphs that grow into plans, paintings that unfold into rooms, cities that rise as counted blocks -- and should never look like a finished building pretending to be a system, or a mystery pretending it cannot be measured. (C12461)
