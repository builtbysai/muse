## The life: the boy who painted at four, the looker who found his landscape in Rio

Luiz Pierre Zerbini was born in Sao Paulo in 1959 -- the full date, 28 April 1959, is given only by the Portuguese Wikipedia, and no gallery, museum or interview source read gives a day or month, so the day and month are single-source and provisional (see Corrections 4) -- and lives and works in Rio de Janeiro, where he moved in 1982. He is a painter first and a multimedia artist second: sculpture, video, drawing, photography and installation all feed back into the painting, and the painting is the centre that holds them. (C14366, leg 1 C14187)

As a child he drew and painted every day at a table behind a sofa in his parents' house, a habit he describes as changing "very gradual[ly]" into being an artist: "I didn't notice when I became an artist." At the age of four he began painting lessons with Jose Antonio Van Acker (1931-2000), a Sao Paulo painter whose house he later visited to watch him paint; he also studied photography with Carlos Moreira and watercolour with Dudi Maia Rosa. Conceptual Fine Arts dates the Van Acker apprenticeship to 1973, which fits a start at age four only loosely (he would have been 13 or 14 in 1973), so the "age four" and "1973" dates do not sit cleanly together (see Corrections 3). As a teenager he lived for a year in Laguna, in the southern state of Santa Catarina, where he spent most of his time surfing; the experience fixed his lifelong fascination with waves. (C14367, leg 1 C14188)

In 1978 Zerbini enrolled in the Fine Arts programme of the Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado (FAAP) in Sao Paulo. The Itau Cultural entry (via Escritorio de Arte) states he attended the FAAP visual arts course "between 1978 and 1980", and the Portuguese Wikipedia repeats 1978-1980, adding that he had his first contact with printmaking there. Conceptual Fine Arts states plainly that his FAAP studies "were often interrupted by long trips, and were eventually left unfinished". Zerbini himself, in the 2024 arte!brasileiros interview, says he entered FAAP "in the 1980s" (against the 1978 enrolment date), took entrance exams for Law and History before ending up in Fine Arts, and "came and went several times", calling college "a difficult place for me, difficult to adapt to" because he was already figurative while the school favoured abstract, concrete and neoconcrete artists. He also says his mother later sent him a box containing his final FAAP work, a modular three-dimensional sculpture of cubes and squares, which he reworked as "Artist's DNA". (C14368, leg 1 C14189)

VERDICT: Zerbini attended FAAP 1978-1980 and left unfinished. Do NOT print a 1982 FAAP graduation. The "graduated in Fine Arts from FAAP in 1982" line appears only in gallery CV boilerplate (Stephen Friedman CV, Sikkema Jenkins, Ocula, ArtDaily) and is contradicted by the Itau entry, by Conceptual Fine Arts, and by the artist's own account. The 1982 date is best read as a garbled CV line (1982 is the year he moved to Rio and held the Madrid show), not a graduation. (C14369, leg 1 C14190, C14210)

Among Leonilson's ASTER classmates were the future artists Luiz Zerbini, Jac Leirner, Ana Tavares and Rafael Franca, and the future gallerist Eduardo Brandao (Newcity Brazil). In 1979 Leonilson shared a studio with Zerbini, and in that same year, by his own later account, he made the decision to devote his life to art. No Zerbini biography read -- Itau, Wikipedia, any gallery page, or either interview with Zerbini himself -- mentions ASTER enrolment by name, and Zerbini's watercolour teacher Dudi Maia Rosa was also Leonilson's watercolour teacher at ASTER, which is consistent with, but does not independently prove, shared ASTER attendance. Treat "ASTER classmate" as established by the Newcity classmate list, but flag that it is single-sourced on Zerbini's side (see Corrections 5). The two men showed together twice, early: a two-person show, "Leonilson and Luiz Zerbini", at the Teatro Lira Paulistana, Sao Paulo, in 1980, and again at the Galeria de Arte da Casa do Brasil, Madrid, in 1981. No source read records any joint work by the two -- they were classmates, studio-sharers and two-person exhibitors, not collaborators. (C14370, leg 1 C14191, C14192)

In 1982 Zerbini moved from Sao Paulo to Rio de Janeiro; every gallery biography gives the year as 1982, and Zerbini himself says: "In 1982, I moved from Sao Paulo, where I was born, to Rio. And here I found the landscape I was looking for without knowing." In Rio he worked as a set designer for the theatre group Asdrubal Trouxe o Trombone and gave performances in Rio bars together with the actress Regina Case. He was married to Regina Case, with whom he had a daughter; the marriage is stated in the Portuguese Wikipedia (citing a 2011 ISTOE profile) and in Google Arts and Culture, but no source read gives dates for the marriage or the daughter's name, and the ISTOE article itself was not opened this run. (C14371, leg 1 C14193)

His first solo exhibition is listed by Itau and by the Stephen Friedman CV as 1982, at the Casa do Brasil (Galeria Casa do Brasil) in Madrid, Spain. Zerbini himself contradicts this: in the 2024 interview he says he "spent five years without doing anything" and "it wasn't until 85 that I had my first solo exhibition at the Subdistrito gallery" in Sao Paulo, adding that he sold all the works from that first exhibition but then had no production left to live on. The 1982 Madrid listing is therefore suspect -- the same venue hosted the two-person Leonilson/Zerbini show in 1981, and the 1982 entry may mislabel a two-person or group showing as a solo. Do not call Madrid 1982 his first solo without a caveat (see Corrections 2). In 1985 he occupied part of the Salao Nacional de Artes Plasticas in Rio de Janeiro and received a Special Mention from the jury (Itau; the Portuguese Wikipedia calls it the "Referencia Especial do Juri" of the Salao Nacional, 1985). He also worked at the Subdistrito Comercial de Arte, Joao Sattamini's gallery in Sao Paulo, where he recalls: "Many artists worked there. I remember Casa Sete, for example." He was NOT a member of Casa 7 -- the group was five painters (Rodrigo Andrade, Carlito Carvalhosa, Fabio Miguez, Nuno Ramos, Paulo Monteiro) sharing a house in Pinheiros -- and Zerbini's remark is a memory of the scene, not a membership claim. (C14372, leg 1 C14194, C14195)

Zerbini is counted as one of the main names of Geracao 80 (the 80s Generation), the loosely affiliated group associated with the return to painting after the conceptual 1970s. The generation-defining exhibition was "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" (How Are You, 80s Generation?) at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage, Rio de Janeiro, in 1984, in which he took part; Conceptual Fine Arts notes the show presented 123 practitioners, including Beatriz Milhazes and Leda Catunda. His 1980s work was figurative painting with surrealist inspiration -- Itau cites "A Tragedia E um Acumulo de Mal-Entendidos" (Tragedy Is an Accumulation of Misunderstandings), 1987, and "Zoo", 1985, and "Piscina das Criancas" (Children's Pool), 1985 -- using photography in collages as studies for large canvases, with a sober palette that slowly gave way to brighter, highly colourful work, exotic plants of Rio's Atlantic forest becoming protagonists and friends appearing as portrait-like figures. In 1987 he took part in the 19th Sao Paulo International Biennial. His other biennial appearances, per the gallery CVs, are the Bienal Internacional de Cuenca (1996), the 7th Bienal de La Habana (2000), the 2nd Bienal do Mercosul, Porto Alegre (2001), and the 29th Bienal de Sao Paulo (2010). (C14373, leg 1 C14196, C14197)

In 1995 Zerbini received the critics' grand prize in the visual arts category from the Associacao Paulista de Criticos de Arte (APCA) -- the one award listed in every biography and the only award in the Stephen Friedman CV. That same year, together with the artist Barrao (b. 1959), the video and film editor Sergio Mekler (b. 1963) and the music producer Chico Neves, he founded the group Chelpa Ferro (an archaic expression meaning money), which works with sculpture, technological installations and electronic music, exploring electronic and industrial sounds, new uses for old electronic equipment, and noises made by everyday objects such as printers, cash registers and ping-pong balls. At the 26th Sao Paulo International Biennial in 2004 the group showed an installation in which dry tree branches and trunks were set in motion by electric pedals operated by the public, producing a soft sound like wind. Chelpa Ferro took part in the 2005 Venice Biennale and performed at the 15th Videobrasil Festival in 2005. Note: some later accounts list Chelpa Ferro as Zerbini, Barrao and Mekler only, dropping Chico Neves; the four-founder version is the Itau one (see Corrections 6). (C14374, leg 1 C14198)

Towards the late 1990s his colourful canvases became increasingly abstract and, above all, geometric, in part inspired by the rigid modernist architecture of Brazil's metropolises. His 1999 exhibition at Galeria Camargo Vilaca (now Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel) in Sao Paulo, "Pedra Nao E Gente Ainda" (Stone Is Not People Yet), presented paintings made with an ancient Chinese marbling technique -- paper dyed underwater in a solution of paint and algae -- and was the first time he exhibited an entirely abstract body of work. Zerbini himself says abstraction "has always accompanied me, since the beginning", citing the modular FAAP sculpture his mother returned to him. His path towards abstraction peaked in 2007 with "My Last Painting", 270 x 420 cm, a canvas painted with a metallic, shiny material that reflects whatever stands in front of it like a distorted mirror. His book "Rasura" (2005; Cosac Naify edition listed 2006), a book of images of other artists' works, fragments and his own annotations with almost no text, is described by Zerbini as a watershed that made him rethink his whole oeuvre. (C14375, leg 1 C14199)

In 2008 Zerbini was invited to take part in an exhibition about Domenico Vandelli, an 18th-century Italian naturalist who with Dom Joao VI organised the scientific/artistic expedition to Brazil; the show, "O Gabinete de Curiosidades de Domenico Vandelli", opened at the Botanical Garden's Environment Museum in Rio in 2008 and toured to Inhotim in 2009. For it he made his first installation using real plants and leaves, after a four-month residency at the Botanical Gardens of Rio de Janeiro (per The Fourdrinier, search excerpt). He had lived for years beside Rio's botanical garden, going almost every day, and in August 2016 moved to a much bigger studio in the Gavea neighbourhood with a garden of his favourite plants behind it. (C14376, leg 1 C14200)

In 2016 he began making monotypes with the printer Joao Sanchez, using real leaves, fruits, bark, seeds and feathers as printing matrices: the two drove a truck carrying a printing press from Rio to Inhotim, harvested plants in the botanical garden there, and printed directly from them. The first results appeared in his artist's book "Minhas Impressoes" (UQ! Editions, 2016) and in the exhibition "Monotipias" at Galpao Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel, Sao Paulo, in 2017 -- his first prints in a long career. The Fondation Cartier published the resulting body of work as "Botanica: Monotypes 2016-2020" (2021), more than 300 monotypes. Zerbini says the monotypes changed his painting: the flat colour seen in his later work "started to appear in the paintings" after monotype, and "there is a contamination, I'm constantly taking things that I'm learning from other techniques and mixing them". (C14377, leg 1 C14201)

A run of mid-career surveys followed: "Amor" at MAM Rio de Janeiro (2012); "amor lugar comum" at Instituto Inhotim, Brumadinho (opened 2013, long-running, listed 2013-2018); "Pinturas" at Casa Daros, Rio de Janeiro (2014); "Natureza Espiritual da Realidade" (Spiritual Nature of Reality) at Galpao Fortes Vilaca, Sao Paulo (2015). The book "Amor Lugar Comum" was published by Cosac Naify in 2013, and the Arte Bra monograph "Luiz Zerbini" (Editora Aeroplano, texts by Agnaldo Farias, Hermano Vianna and Sergio Romagnolo) appeared in 2010. (C14378, leg 1 C14202)

Zerbini's three galleries are Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel (Sao Paulo), Sikkema Jenkins & Co (New York; first show 2016, "Perhappiness") and Stephen Friedman Gallery (London; first show 2017). His first solo exhibition in a UK public institution, "Intuitive Ratio", opened at the South London Gallery on 8 June 2018 and ran to 19 August 2018. In 2019 he had a major solo presentation in the group show "Nous les Arbres" (Trees) at the Fondation Cartier, Paris, converting the main gallery into a large-scale herbarium set around a living fig tree, juxtaposing hyperreal paintings of the rainforest with symbols of Brazilian modernity; the installation toured to the Power Station of Art, Shanghai (2021) and the Triennale Milano (2023). His second Stephen Friedman show, "Fire", opened online 26 January 2021 and physically 13 April-15 May 2021, including the 300 x 600 cm triptych "Happiness Beyond Paradise" (2020), painted after a first trip to the Pantanal. He told Studio International he caught the virus in March (2020, by context) and that immunity "made my life simpler" because he could keep going to the studio. (C14379, leg 1 C14203)

"Luiz Zerbini: The Same Story Is Never the Same" (A mesma historia nunca e a mesma) at MASP, Sao Paulo, 2 May-5 June 2022, curated by Adriano Pedrosa and Guilherme Giufrida, was his first solo show at a museum in Sao Paulo and brought together around 50 works. The curatorial conception grew from his painting "A Primeira Missa" (First Mass, 2014), which reimagines Victor Meirelles's canonical 1861 painting of the first mass in Brazil from the point of view of the people who lived there; for the show MASP commissioned four new large-scale history paintings, on the Canudos War (1896-97), the Haximu Massacre (1993, Yanomami land), illegal mining, and the historical cycles of monoculture. The show also included 29 monotypes from the "Macunaima" series (2017), made for an edition of Mario de Andrade's modernist classic, and two installations in Lina Bo Bardi's glass vitrines, one with roots from his studio garden. Zerbini discussed the Haximu painting at length in 2024, saying he did not want to show the Yanomami as victims and only agreed to make it after Bruce Albert consulted Davi Kopenawa, who said it had to be done. (C14380, leg 1 C14204)

"Paisagens Ruminadas" (Ruminated Landscapes), curated by Clarissa Diniz, at the Centro Cultural Banco do Brasil, Rio de Janeiro, 19 June-2 September 2024, then touring to CCBB Brasilia in September 2024, was the first retrospective Zerbini had done. It included a work from 1976, made when he was 16 -- "a little painting", made before he considered himself an artist -- so the show covered about 40 years by his count (Diniz called it 50 years of career; he disputes that). His own gloss on the title: "ruminating, rethinking... I don't actually swallow, I just ruminate, that thing that stays in my mouth, without transformation." Recent exhibitions since include "Afinidades III - Cochichos" (Whispering), Museu Oscar Niemeyer, Curitiba, November 2024 (more than 44 paintings, watercolours and monotypes from his travel notebooks, in dialogue with Parana landscape painters from the MON collection); "Saudade do Mundo Pequeno", A.Galeria Passeio Cultural Primavera, Florianopolis (2025); "Vagarosa Luminescencia Voadora" (Leisurely Floating Luminescence), Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel, Sao Paulo (2025); "Observations: Luiz Zerbini in Conversation with Frank Walter", Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel | Jardins, Sao Paulo, 25 November 2025-7 February 2026; and "Estrelas Escolhidas", Instituto Tomie Ohtake, Sao Paulo (2026, per FDAG). In 2017, with Barrao and Albano Afonso, he made the show "Zerbini, Barrao, Albano" at Santander Cultural, Porto Alegre, gathering 43 works of painting, printmaking, sculpture, installation and photography. His travel notebooks, begun in 2014 and kept more steadily during the pandemic (trips to Buzios, Paraty, Bocaina), were published as "Sabados, domingos e feriados" (Saturdays, Sundays and Holidays), Cobogo, 2023, edited by Tiago Mesquita. (C14381, leg 1 C14205, C14206)

Zerbini works in acrylic, which dries much faster than oil, building paintings in many layers, adding elements and covering others; he paints from life where possible ("there is a particular connection, a unique tempo"), and also from memory and photographs. His large paintings are made at 1:1 scale -- "a bottle was the size of a bottle" -- because his old studio was small and he could not step back from the canvas. The grid runs through the work, visible or invisible: "all the world, all the things and thoughts are floating in front of a grid... Science and xamas [shamans] have agreed, for different reasons, that reality seen through a powerful lens is pure geometry." He treats a painting as an oracle: after months looking at a painting without knowing what to do, "I understood that paintings really talk... I treat a painting as an oracle. The painting gives you everything you need to keep going." On politics: "Art, as everything, is political." On method: "I'm a curious guy, always in need of new stuff... I like to create relationships, creating thoughts that complement each other. And I use everything I have as material. The material is the world." He collects objects picked up on beaches throughout his life, catalogued in his studio, each carrying "an emotional memory of where I was when I picked them up". He also says psychoanalysis, as a tool, "saved me and still saves me today... from myself". (C14382, leg 1 C14207)

His works are held, across the sources read, by: Instituto Inhotim, Brumadinho; Instituto Itau Cultural, Sao Paulo; MAM Rio de Janeiro; MAM Sao Paulo; MASP, Sao Paulo; Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo; Fondation Cartier pour l'art contemporain, Paris; LACMA, Los Angeles; Fondazione Sandretto Re Rebaudengo, Turin; Kistefos Museum, Jevnaker, Norway; and the Pizzuti Collection of the Columbus Museum of Art, Ohio. Note the Stephen Friedman CV's public-collections list is shorter (Inhotim, Itau Cultural, MAM Rio, MAM Sao Paulo, Fondazione Sandretto Re Rebaudengo) than the FDAG/Sikkema lists; the longer list above combines the gallery pages, which agree with each other. (C14383, leg 1 C14208)

The through-line of the life, for the study's use: a Sao Paulo boy who learned to paint at four, surfed in Laguna, left FAAP unfinished, shared a studio with Leonilson in 1979, moved to Rio in 1982 and found his landscape there, broke through with Geracao 80 in 1984, spent the 1990s turning figuration into geometry, then spent the 2000s-2010s letting plants, grids and real leaves back into the painting -- first as installations, then as monotypes printed from the plants themselves -- until by 2022 he was repainting Brazilian history itself, from the colonisers' first mass to the Yanomami massacre, at 1:1 scale. The through-line is looking: "I understand all things through my eyes. And I enjoy to be surprised." (C14384, leg 1 C14209)

Evolution: large, colourful, figurative canvases of odd quasi-theatrical scenes -- "Zoo" and "Piscina das Criancas" in 1985, "A Tragedia" in 1987 -- built from photographic collages used as studies, with pop-art framing and dreamlike high chroma; self-portraiture already present, turned skeletal by 1997 ("Eu e a brisa"); the first technical swerve in 1999, when "Pinturas Dentro D'agua" lets water and algae dye the paper, the medium doing part of the drawing. Middle (2002-2015): the grid arrives and never leaves. In 2002 the paintings already run three ways at once -- big figurative accumulation ("Eu e Mini"), striped veils over windows ("O Sol, Ar, Sombra"), painted wood and books ("Documentos Politicos", "Arte e Tecnologia") -- and the six-piece polyptych "Historia em Quadrinhos" mixes grids, stripes, monochromes and the organic bookish pattern. By 2011-2014 the weave is the method: photographic image plus abstract overlay, figure and background blended non-hierarchically, city surfaces (signs, tiles, cables) inside the same canvas as plants. The table installations ("Natureza Espiritual da Realidade" from 2012) are the same weave in three dimensions: found objects sorted into squares under marked glass. Recent (2016-2025): two technical discoveries rewire the painting. First the monotype: from the 2016 Inhotim residency with Joao Sanchez, real leaves become the matrix -- painted, pressed, lifted -- and the leaf's veins, texture and outline emerge through veils of colour; the artist says the plant becomes a collaborator, not a subject, and the flat colour of the monotypes then contaminates the paintings. Second the roller: paint on a foam roller, used as a printing tool on the canvas, producing textile-like repetition ("Diagrama de um tempo insano III"). At the same time the subject turns historical and ecological: "A Primeira Missa" (2014) reframes colonisation from the Indigenous side, then "Massacre de Haximu", "Rio das mortes" and finally "Os Comedores de Terra" (2025) -- a 360-degree room in which a five-metre painting of burning, gasoline-filmed forest is backed by sculptural ore. The scale never shrinks: the artist says his large paintings are always one-to-one, a bottle the size of a bottle, because his old studio was small and he worked close to the canvas. The through-line, in the artist's own words (Brooklyn Rail, 2026): he does not separate abstraction, geometry and figuration into distinct phases; he works on all of them simultaneously, everything exists at the same time, and the monotypes and paintings constantly influence each other. The distinctions are the viewers', not his: one vocabulary, different visual expressions, the same questions -- observation, transformation, nature, history, systems of knowledge, ways of seeing. (C14386, leg 2 C14262-C14267)


## M1 -- The grid that holds the jungle: geometry is the trellis, not the cage

The grid is the one constant from the 2000s to the present, and it never wins over the plants -- it holds them up. "Happiness Beyond Paradise" (2020, W15): acrylic on canvas 300 x 600 cm, a six-metre panoramic rainforest, an orthogonal grid tempered with dynamic interlocking curves of colour and the flourishes and arabesques of palms, cycads and other tropical vegetation -- the grid holds the rainforest, the curves break it. "Concrete Jungle" (2011, W08): natural and man-made forms -- plants, trees and animals alongside loudspeakers and cables -- set against a geometric, richly coloured backdrop, and behind the objects a system of tiny rectangles individually filled with blocks of colour, a geometric kaleidoscope whose hues echo the rest of the canvas. "Dry River" (2022, W19): the grid as the structure of an imagined landscape of plants, trees, leaves and textural fields, in a multi-colour palette across verdant tonality against the neutral tones of built systems. Supporting: "Optocinetico" (2020), 32 squares in a four-by-eight grid, 160 x 320 cm; "Arvore do Viajante" (2020), the traveller's tree standing in a rigid grid of cellular and corrugated patterns. The artist's own rule: "all the world, all the things and thoughts are floating in front of a grid... Science and xamas [shamans] have agreed, for different reasons, that reality seen through a powerful lens is pure geometry." Watchable: a grid of squares, each one filled with a different green, and the leaves growing across the lines that were meant to contain them; the geometry is visible, the jungle is louder. (C14387, leg 2 C14244, C14250, C14252, C14255; leg 1 C14207)

**Takeaway:** a grid earns its jungle when the plants are allowed to break it. If the grid only organises the picture, it is a layout; if the squares hold the rainforest and the palms push through the lines, the geometry is the trellis and the growth is the work.


## M2 -- The plant as collaborator: the leaf prints itself

The leaf is not drawn, it is pressed, and the print is the leaf's own doing. "Brumadinho" (2016, W13): a series of ten monotypes, oil on paper, 106 x 80 cm, conceived from an immersive process in the gardens of Inhotim, where the artist collected the botanical species used in the engravings; a week-long residency with printer Joao Sanchez, a truck carrying a press that takes 107 x 80 cm sheets, leaves and flowers gathered with the gardeners and put through the press; at the IDB Cultural Center the works are mounted on a table with movable faces that the public can move. The "Dry River" monotypes (2022, W19): leaves, flowers and branches laid on a painted metal plate, then pressed onto Kozo paper, so leafy veins, spindly roots and curling petals take form against patterned backgrounds. "Macunaima" monotypes (2017, W14): 29 monotypes made for an edition of Mario de Andrade's 1928 modernist classic. The artist's own account: the plant becomes a collaborator, not a subject; the flat colour of the monotypes then contaminates the paintings -- "there is a contamination, I'm constantly taking things that I'm learning from other techniques and mixing them". Watchable: a real leaf laid on a plate, inked, pressed, lifted -- and the veins left behind in colour are the drawing; the plant did the drawing and the artist did the choosing. (C14388, leg 2 C14248, C14249, C14255; leg 1 C14201)

**Takeaway:** a leaf earns its print when the plant does the drawing. If the leaf is only painted, it is a still life; if the real leaf is pressed and its veins emerge through veils of colour, the plant is the collaborator and the print is the evidence.


## M3 -- The roller as a press inside the painting: repetition without a machine

Paint goes on a foam roller, the roller goes on the canvas, and the painting acquires a printer's repetition without ever leaving the studio. "Diagrama de um tempo insano III" (2020, W16): acrylic on canvas, 200 x 200 cm; paint applied to rollers to mark the squared-up canvas with a harmonious array of loosely linear patterns reminiscent of textile design. The discovery, in the artist's own words (Brooklyn Rail, 2026): he painted a canvas, used a foam roller to spread the paint, the wet paint transferred the image elsewhere, and he then began painting directly onto the roller and using it as a tool. The roller is the mechanism: a printmaker's tool inside a painting, producing repetition with small differences each pass -- textile-like, but never identical, because the hand drives the roller. Watchable: a roller crossing a squared canvas and leaving a band of loose linear pattern behind it; each pass repeats the last and misses it slightly, and the misses are the painting. (C14389, leg 2 C14251)

**Takeaway:** a roller earns its pattern when the repetition is handmade. If the pattern only repeats, it is wallpaper; if a foam roller carries the paint and each pass lands slightly differently, the repetition is the rhythm and the misses are the drawing.


## M4 -- History repainted from the other side: the viewpoint moves to the beach

A canonical scene is repainted with the camera on the other side: the people who were already there become the viewers, and the arrivals become the subject. "A Primeira Missa" (2014, W11): acrylic on canvas, 200 x 300 cm (dimensions rest on a designboom caption, search excerpt only); Zerbini recreated one of the most classic scenes in Brazilian historical painting, proposing a new representation of the 1500 first mass, a symbol of Portuguese colonisation -- and the starting point was a 1980s drawing imagining the Europeans' arrival from the viewpoint of those already living in Brazil. "Massacre de Haximu" (2020, W17): the 1993 massacre, when miners invaded Yanomami land and killed the village, the death count disputed (16 by some accounts, 80 by others); he consulted Bruce Albert, co-author with Davi Kopenawa of "The Falling Sky", who told him the painting had to be done; while painting it he read in the newspaper that the man who carried out the massacre had been released after 18 years in prison and had returned to Yanomami land with large rafts, mining the river again. "Rio das mortes" (2021, W18): illegal gold mining on Yanomami land, the pollution of the region's major river systems shown in kaleidoscopic streams of colour winding down the canvas and turning a muddled, sickly tint, a work tent erected over the river referencing exploited poor labourers. "Os Comedores de Terra" (2025, W20): built from documents on illegal gold mines in the Amazon -- in a pit lies an oozing rainbow-coloured mass of gasoline forming a shimmering film over water, columns of fire rise among felled trees, lush foliage and flowers in the foreground against ghostly devastation behind; the title alludes to the Yanomami name for miners and loggers, who dig the Earth's vital substances out of its entrails. Watchable: a history painting where the viewpoint has crossed to the other side of the beach, and the familiar scene is suddenly unfamiliar because you are standing where the invaded stood. (C14390, leg 2 C14247, C14253, C14254, C14258; leg 1 C14204)

**Takeaway:** a history painting earns its retelling when the viewpoint moves. If the scene is only repainted, it is a copy; if the first mass is seen from the shore where the people already lived, and the massacre is painted only after the people it happened to said it had to be done, the viewpoint is the work and the history is the subject.


## M5 -- The table as inventory: the museum in miniature, sorted into squares

The painting leaves the wall and becomes a table of small treasures, sorted into squares under marked glass. "Natureza Espiritual da Realidade" (2012, W09): a sculpture of wooden tables configured like museum display cases; first form 2012 in "Amor" at MAM Rio, new composition 2014 at Casa Daros, shown again as the title work in 2015; at each showing the arrangement changes. Divided into squares, the tables hold curious objects collected on trips or for personal reasons -- seashells, stones, bricks, ceramic tiles, tree trunks, plants, fishing nets, even a R$10 bill -- arranged sometimes as still lifes, sometimes as abstract compositions. The artist also draws graphic marks on the glass tabletop and adds coloured gelatin, changing how colour and light fall on the objects. The gallery reads it both as an inventory of the references in the paintings and as a three-dimensional translation of his pictorial process. Supporting: "Jewels of the forest" (2021), a table installation displaying seeds, shells, rocks and leaves as reclaimed treasures in jewellery cases, the table below painted with a gold-patterned skeleton symbolising the death toll of illegal mining. Watchable: a table divided into squares, each square holding one found thing, the glass above it marked and tinted; the inventory is the painting, laid flat, and the squares are the grid with the wall taken away. (C14391, leg 2 C14245, C14272)

**Takeaway:** a table earns its inventory when the squares are the painting. If the objects only sit on a table, it is a collection; if each square holds one found thing under marked, tinted glass and the arrangement changes every showing, the table is the canvas laid flat and the sorting is the composition.


## M6 -- The photograph as scaffold, then the weave: figure and background trade places

A photograph starts the painting and then disappears into it; figure and background are arranged non-hierarchically, one image following another until the composition is complete. "Brasil Colonia" (1993, W04): a 20 ft wide canvas recording the artist's routine on a trip to Art Cologne in Germany -- long walks, museum visits -- rebuilt from the photos he took, with daily-routine details gaining a historical bias; the title is a pun on his feelings at his first international art fair. "Eu e Mini no universo, sob os efeitos narcoticos do amor" (2002, W07): at the bottom two figures, apparently the artist and his wife Mini, lie in the sun in a setting half patio, half beach, bamboos making vertical lines behind them, plants, translucent curtains, coconuts casting shadows; around them a tape recorder, record player, Walkman, loudspeaker, telephone, sandal, seashell and a lava lamp ejaculating Day-Glo green goo, all joined by cables, adaptors, plugs and extension leads; above, a marquee-like structure covered with a tiled multicoloured grid; behind, a seascape with psychedelic undulating sea-lines and mountains. "Ilha da Mare" (2015, W12): the weave of photographic image plus abstract overlay includes a precarious stage with a chicken, loudspeakers, crabs, bricks, and finally the sea -- the mechanism stated in the same text: he begins with photographic images and overlays abstract elements in a complex weave where figure and background are blended, arranged non-hierarchically, one image following another until the composition is complete. Watchable: a photo that slowly stops being a photo -- the patio becomes a beach, the cables become the drawing, the grid marquee becomes the sky -- and no element is more important than any other. (C14392, leg 2 C14240, C14242, C14246)

**Takeaway:** a photograph earns its painting when it stops being a photograph. If the photo only underlies the canvas, it is a study; if the rebuilt scene lets figure and background trade places until nothing is the subject and everything is, the weave is the work and the photo was only the scaffold.


## M7 -- Water does the drawing: the paper is dyed, not painted

The paper goes underwater and the water makes the painting; the artist immerses the sheet and the medium produces the image. "Pinturas Dentro D'agua" (Underwater Paintings, 1999, W06): an ancient Chinese marbling technique in which paper is dyed underwater in a solution of paint and algae; the water does the work -- the artist immerses the sheet and the aqueous medium produces abstract paintings in luminous colours that reflect the fluidity of the medium. The source notes the paintings look easy and seductive at first sight but carry questions of popular and conceptual art. The 1999 show "Pedra Nao E Gente Ainda" (Stone Is Not People Yet) at Galeria Camargo Vilaca was the first time he exhibited an entirely abstract body of work, and the underwater paintings were its core. Supporting: "Sertao" (2009), a film of dry landscapes shown only through their reflections in water, with a digital glitch used as a visual effect (film NOT viewed). Watchable: a sheet of paper sinking into coloured water, pigment blooming and spreading on its own, and the sheet lifted out carrying a luminous abstraction no brush touched; the water drew it and the artist chose when to stop it. (C14393, leg 2 C14239, C14266; leg 1 C14199)

**Takeaway:** water earns its drawing when the medium does the work. If the water only wets the paper, it is a wash; if the sheet is dyed underwater and the pigment blooms on its own until the artist lifts it out, the water is the draughtsman and the lifting is the decision.


## M8 -- Scale as immersion, one to one: the painting is the size of the world it shows

The large paintings are made at 1:1 scale because the studio was small and the artist could not step back -- and the closeness became the method. "Happiness Beyond Paradise" (2020, W15): 300 x 600 cm, a six-metre panoramic triptych; a bottle was the size of a bottle. "Os Comedores de Terra" (2025, W20): a 360-degree installation joining a five-metre painting and vibrantly coloured sculptures emulating precious stones and ore, 375 x 500 x 130 cm -- the viewer stands inside the painting, not in front of it. "My Last Painting" (2007), supporting: 270 x 420 cm, a canvas painted with a metallic, shiny material that reflects whatever stands in front of it like a distorted mirror -- the viewer is literally in the painting. The scale never shrinks: from the 1980s figurative canvases to the 2025 installation, the work grows with its subject, and the small studio that forced the 1:1 scale is the reason the paintings read at arm's length even at six metres wide. This is the exact opposite of Leonilson's shrink -- Leonilson's diary got smaller as his body did; Zerbini's painting got bigger as his subject did, from a garden to a rainforest to a whole history. Watchable: a painting wider than the room's wall, painted so close to the canvas that every leaf is life-size; you step back and it is a landscape, you step close and it is a garden at your feet. (C14394, leg 2 C14250, C14258, C14262; leg 1 C14199, C14207)

**Takeaway:** scale earns its immersion when the painting is the size of what it shows. If the canvas is only big, it is a mural; if a six-metre rainforest is painted leaf by leaf at life size because the studio never let the painter step back, the scale is the closeness and the immersion is the work.


## The aesthetic logic: the grid, the collaborator, and the scale that never shrinks

Zerbini's programme has three terms, and they never change, only grow to the size of the subject. First, the grid: visible or invisible, it runs through everything -- the squares of the table installations, the tiled marquee over a beach, the tiny rectangles behind "Concrete Jungle", the orthogonal grid of a six-metre rainforest -- and it is a trellis, not a cage: the plants are allowed to break it, and the breaking is the painting. Second, the collaborator: the plant prints itself, the water draws itself, the roller repeats itself with small misses, the people a history happened to say whether the painting may be made -- the artist sets up the system and lets something else do part of the work, then chooses when to stop it. Third, the scale: one to one, always, from a bottle the size of a bottle to a rainforest six metres wide to a room you stand inside, because the old studio was small and he worked close to the canvas, and the closeness stayed even when the studio grew.

That is why the same moves survive forty years of changing supports, from pop-figurative canvases to underwater dyeing to monotypes pressed from real leaves to history painting without ever changing their question: what does the world look like when you let it draw itself, inside a grid, at the size it actually is? The answer, at the end, is a room: a five-metre painting of a burning forest with gasoline filming the water and columns of fire among felled trees, lush foliage in the foreground against ghostly devastation behind, and sculptural stones and ore standing in for what was dug out of the earth. The grid holds it, the documents made it, and the scale makes you stand inside it. (C14395)
