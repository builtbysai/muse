# Marie Bashkirtseff: Study Notes

**Site:** Marie Bashkirtseff (1858-1884), Russian-born painter, pastellist, sculptor and diarist -- born Maria Konstantinovna Bashkirtseva on Wednesday 24 November 1858 (12 November Old Style) at Gavrontsi, near Poltava in the Russian Empire (now Ukraine), the daughter of Konstantin Pavlovich Bashkirtsev, a local marshal of nobility, and of Maria Stepanovna Babanina; her younger brother Paul was born in 1859. Her parents separated in her early childhood (1859 in the fullest biographical accounts, 1862 in Encyclopedia.com; Wikipedia's "when she was 12" is rejected as a confusion with the 1870 departure from Russia), and in May 1870 her mother took the children out of Russia for good, travelling Europe with her grandfather, her aunt and the family physician until the family settled at the Villa Acqua-Viva, Nice, in the autumn of 1872. She began her Journal in January 1873, aged 12, and kept it for 106 notebooks until Monday 20 October 1884, six days before her death; chronic laryngitis destroyed her singing voice in 1877 and she resolved instead to become an artist, joining the women's atelier of the Academie Julian, Paris, in October 1877, under Rodolphe Julian and Tony Robert-Fleury. Her Salon debut came in 1880 with Question du Divorce, signed "Russ" so the judges would not know she was a woman; In the Studio followed at the Salon of 1881, signed "Andrey"; Jean et Jacques at the Salon of 1883; and The Meeting, her largest and best-known work, at the Salon of 1884. She died in Paris on Friday 31 October 1884, of tuberculosis, aged 25, forty days before Jules Bastien-Lepage died on Wednesday 10 December 1884. She is buried in the Cimetiere de Passy, Paris, in a mausoleum designed by Emile Bastien-Lepage as a full-sized artist's studio, complete with her easels, palettes and stool and the unfinished Holy Women inside, now declared a historic monument. Her Journal was first published in 1887, in two volumes edited by Andre Theuriet, heavily abridged and bowdlerised -- the family removed the Romanoff trial, her descriptions of her body, and two years of her age, so that she appeared born in 1860 -- and it was an immediate sensation; the true 1858 birth date was established only by the 16-volume unabridged French edition of 2005. Her output in 1877-1884 is counted at about 229 works (100 paintings, 6 pastels, 118 drawings and 5 sculptures, Parker and Pollock's count), of which at least 60 survive, the rest destroyed or looted in the Second World War. Her works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay, Paris (The Meeting, Portrait of Madame X, La Douleur de Nausicaa), the State Russian Museum, St Petersburg (Autumn, The Umbrella, Spring), the Dnipro State Art Museum, Dnipro, Ukraine (In the Studio), the Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam (The Artist's Sister), the Musee des Beaux-Arts Jules Cheret, Nice (Self-portrait with Palette and the portrait of her brother Paul), the Musee des Beaux-Arts de la ville de Paris, Petit Palais (La Parisienne, portrait of Irma), the Kharkiv Art Museum (At a Book) and the Osterreichische Galerie Belvedere, Vienna (In the Mist). ## M1. The Hidden Object (A Circle of Attention Around Something the Viewer Cannot See)
The composition is built as a ring of gazes converging on a single point that is deliberately withheld from the viewer. The tallest figure holds the object, but the object itself is never shown; the picture's energy is the attention, not the thing. In The Meeting (1884), six boys "stand in a circle fascinated with what the tallest boy has in his hand, although it is not visible to us" (My Daily Art Display); Google Arts and Culture: "Six young boys, their expressions and attitudes captured with precision, stand in a circle around an object that is difficult to identify, but which is certainly the reason behind their discussion". Her own first sketch, April 1883: "Six street children grouped, their heads close to one another, half length only. The eldest is about twelve, and the youngest six. The tallest, seen almost from the back, holds a nest, and the others are looking on, in various and suitable attitudes". ChasingART reads the same gesture differently: the older youth "holds what appears to be a bent branch, drawn taut with a string. Another boy watches closely, a length of string held between his lips... a small but telling gesture that suggests demonstration rather than exchange". The object is unidentified and the study does not assert one reading over the other. The mechanism is watchable because each boy's face is a different reaction to the same unseen thing: "Each boy occupies a distinct psychological position. One stands with his hands clasped behind his back, watchful and self-contained. Another leans forward, openly curious. A third hangs back, measuring the exchange". **Takeaway:** hide the object, paint the attention; a ring of different faces looking at one unseen point is a whole drama with no event in it.

## M2. The Turned Back as Anchor (The Biggest Figure Faces Away)
The tallest, most important figure is painted from behind, so the viewer is put in the crowd looking over his shoulder instead of facing the scene. The back becomes the picture's spine. In The Meeting the tallest boy is "seen almost from the back" and holds the object the others want to see; his back is what the other five boys and the viewer all face. The same device recurs at the edge of the same canvas: the little girl is "seen from the back, her head raised and her arms crossed". In the studio painting the model is a boy on a dais seen by sixteen women, and the central painter herself is turned three-quarters away from us toward him. The device is the opposite of the frontal portrait stare she uses elsewhere (M4): here the turned back creates a viewpoint inside the group rather than a confrontation with it. **Takeaway:** a back turned to the viewer is not a missing face, it is a doorway; put the tallest figure's back in the middle and the viewer joins the circle.

## M3. The Walking-Away Figure (One Small Figure Exits at the Edge)
While the main group is locked in a circle, one small figure -- always a girl -- walks away from the action at the picture's edge, alone, seen from behind. She is the picture's second sentence. In The Meeting, "In the distance 2 or 3 little girls walking away"; the first sketch counts one: "The sixth is a little girl of four, seen from the back, her head raised and her arms crossed". Google Arts and Culture reads her as the feminist charge of the painting: "it is interesting to speculate on the title of the painting and the presence, on the right, of a little girl walking away. As someone who was committed to the feminist struggles of her time, perhaps Marie Bashkirtseff is here denouncing a misogynist society; the debate remains a male issue and the woman is kept at a distance". My Daily Art Display reports the same feminist reading. The mechanism is mechanical: the group is a closed circle; the girl is the only open line, the only figure with somewhere else to go, and the eye follows her out of the frame. **Takeaway:** one figure walking away at the edge breaks the closed circle and gives the picture its argument; the group is the scene, the departure is the meaning.

## M4. Two-Speed Painting (One Face Finished to the Last Pore, Everything Else Simplified to Flat Shapes)
The face is rendered with full detail while the clothing, umbrella and background are reduced to simple colour shapes. The contrast, not the detail alone, is what makes the face command attention. This is the Bastien-Lepage compromise she inherited: "Bastien's composite of detailed realism blended with more painterly passages shows in one of her best portraits, The Umbrella (1883)". In The Umbrella, "Simplification plays an essential role in this painting. Notice the pleasant contrast between the detailed face and the rest of the painting... The umbrella, clothing, and background are nothing more than simple color shapes. They fall back, allowing the girl's face to command your attention". The same economy governs the hair: "Bashkirtseff didn't try to paint every strand of hair. Rather, she painted the hair as clusters". The value structure does the same work: "The painting is built upon a strong value structure... Pay close attention to the use of small, dark accents around the eyes, nose, and lips. They help these delicate features stand out from the rest of the face". Draw Paint Academy on the sister-in-law portrait: "notice the simplified values, especially around the face. She didn't paint every subtle change in value". The mechanism is also visible in reverse in The Meeting, where every boy's face gets the detailed treatment and the fence, posters and clothes are the simplified ground. **Takeaway:** detail is a budget; spend it all on the face and pay for it with flat shapes everywhere else, and the face will hold the whole picture.

## M5. Edge Variance as Steering (Soft and Lost Edges Merge, Hard Edges Accuse)
Edges are not uniform. Soft and lost edges dissolve one shape into another; hard edges snap a feature into focus. The viewer is steered around the picture by edge quality, not by outline. In The Umbrella, "This painting is a masterclass in edge variance. Edges are what allow your eyes to transition throughout a painting... Soft and lost edges help merge the dark clothing and umbrella into one shape, even though they are distinct objects. Most of the face is painted with soft edges, giving a soft, youthful, and feminine appearance. However, there are some hard edges used around the eyes, lips, and mouth to help accentuate these features. Soft edges allow the girl's hair to flow gently into the darkness of the umbrella... Hard edges keep the abstract shapes (umbrella, clothing, and background) sharp and distinct". A second device inside the same mechanism: "the umbrella and clothing form a solid dark shape. This is simplification using value -- taking two distinct objects and merging them into one shape. We know where the umbrella and clothing stop and start; we don't need to be shown". And the asymmetry: "Notice the asymmetry of the design, despite the girl being positioned directly in the center. The background and umbrella play an important role in disrupting the symmetry of the girl". **Takeaway:** decide the edge before you decide the shape; a lost edge merges two objects into one mass, a hard edge on an eye stops the whole room.

## M6. The Dull Palette with One Saturated Accent
The picture is built from grays, blacks and weak skin tones, and then a single small patch of saturated colour -- lips, cheeks, a blue dress -- is allowed to be the loudest thing in the room. Restraint makes the accent possible. In The Umbrella, "In terms of color, it's a dull painting, with mostly grays, blacks, and weak skin tones. This suits the moody atmosphere. As Bashkirtseff wrote about this painting, 'I work outside and it rains constantly.' You can feel that through the dull colors... Attention is again drawn towards the face with relatively more color being used... Pay close attention to the subtle changes in color on the girl's face. Each change in color marks a significant change in structure. And the girl's rosy cheeks, lips, and nose are suggestive of her youth. Her lips being the most saturated color in the painting". The same economy in In the Studio: sixteen students in muted tones, and the eye goes first to "the woman seated at the centre of the work. She wears a bright blue dress" -- one saturated garment in a room of browns and ochres. Wahooart's summary of In the Studio agrees: "The color palette is dominated by warm tones, specifically browns, ochres, and reds, though these are tempered by a significant use of muted colors". In the Mist works the same way in reverse: a grey foggy street with "a bright plume of flame from a fire in the centre of her canvas". **Takeaway:** keep the whole picture dull and the weather in the colour, then spend one saturated note -- lips, a blue dress, one flame -- and it will carry the painting.

## M7. The Crowded Room as a System (Sixteen People, Sixteen Different Jobs)
A large group scene in which every figure is doing something different -- painting, watching, talking, posing -- so the picture reads as a working system rather than a posed tableau. The crowd is the subject. In the Studio is "a fascinating work featuring sixteen students all taking part in a life-drawing session... It is a hubbub of activity. Some of the females are concentrating intently on their canvases whilst others partake in chit-chat. The two females in the foreground, one seated, one standing, engage in conversation". The room is small and the figures are crammed in: "The studio looks well organised, although small in size, but that maybe due to the number of people crammed into the room". The system includes the tools of the trade as props: the central figure holds "a small brush and a maulstick" and works on the nude model "holding a staff whilst standing on the raised dais so that he can be seen clearly by all the female students"; the school skeleton is in the painting too. One colleague "staring out at us. Maybe someone is entering the room" -- a figure whose job is to notice the viewer. The mechanism's proof is the commission itself: Julian asked for a painting of his academy because "A woman's studio has never been painted", and her diary records the practical fight it took: she had to take down a partition to open up the studio, and Julian first made her pay for the construction. **Takeaway:** fill the room with different kinds of attention -- one paints, one talks, one stares out -- and the crowd becomes a machine you can watch working.

## M8. Self-Insertion at the Centre (The Painter Paints Herself Painting)
She puts herself in the middle of the picture, in the act of painting, as the central figure. The self-portrait is not a separate genre; it is smuggled into the group scene as its anchor. In the Studio, "Marie Bashkirtseff is the central figure seated in the foreground". The same instinct drives Self-portrait with Palette (1880), painted the year of her first Salon acceptance, and the journal's account of the nude-model fight: she was "the first person to insist that women be allowed to paint from the nude model", and she records the men's verdict with pride: "M Julian and the others said at the men's studio that I had neither the touch, nor the manner, nor the capabilities of a woman... so much talent and vigor, nay even brutality, in drawing". The mechanism is also a claim about the room: a woman's studio had never been painted, and she painted it with herself at its centre. **Takeaway:** put yourself in the middle doing the work, not posing beside it; the self-portrait earns its place by being the picture's engine.

## M9. The Signature as a Device (A Man's Name on a Woman's Painting)
She signs early Salon pictures with male or neutral names so the jury reads the painting before it reads the sex of the painter. The signature is a small machine for getting past a gate. Question du Divorce (1880) was signed "Russ" (for Russian), "so that the judges would not know she was a woman". In the Studio was signed "Andrey" "because it was a name that wasn't female in French or Russian and it begins with an A, which would place her at the beginning of the judges list, making her fate known more quickly". The same impulse runs through her writing life: she wrote for Hubertine Auclert's feminist newspaper La Citoyenne in 1881 under the pen name "Pauline Orrel" -- though Eclectic Light notes this "appears to be unsupported by the original edited versions of her diaries", so the Orrel claim is flagged as contested, not established (see P14 and the Load-Bearing Corrections). She also began her Maupassant correspondence under an assumed name. The mechanism matters because the paintings that carried male signatures are the ones that got her in: the 1880 Salon acceptance, the 1881 studio picture. **Takeaway:** when the gate reads the name before the work, change the name; a signature is a tool, and "Andrey" got the painting hung.

## M10. The Empty City (People Removed, Weather and Rubbish Left In)
A cityscape with the people taken out, so that fog, flame, fallen leaves and a fallen bench become the subject. The absence of figures is the point, not a gap. In the Mist (1882) shows "almost deserted city streets on a foggy day, with a bright plume of flame from a fire in the centre of her canvas"; Draw Paint Academy: "It's atmospheric and moody. There's a sense of unity throughout the scene, with everything basked in the same light and mist". Autumn (1883) is "unusual in being devoid of people. The leaf litter, occasional rubbish, and fallen bench strengthen its feeling of desolation in the midst of the bustling city". My Daily Art Display's inventory of Autumn is a list of debris, not people: "The fallen bench straddles the pavement and the road. The crumbling stonework of the pavement is clearly visible and which is now home for the fallen, windswept leaves and what looks like an abandoned newspaper lies in the gutter close to the fallen bench". The writer adds the reason the mechanism works: "The painting is devoid of people and this fact alone means we are not distracted from the artist's detailed depiction of the area". This is the one place where she breaks her own rule -- everywhere else the figure is the subject; here the street itself, emptied, is the portrait of Paris. **Takeaway:** take the people out and let the fog, the fire, the fallen bench and the newspaper in the gutter do the talking; an empty street is a portrait of the city.

## Evolution
1877-1880, arrival and the first Salon. She settles in Paris in 1877, studies at the Robert-Fleury studio and the Academie Julian, and works "from 8 to noon and from 2 to 5" daily by May 1879. Her first works are conventional portraits: Self-portrait with Palette (1880) is "skilful if conventional" in the same way The Artist's Sister (1881) is. First Salon acceptance 1880 with Question du Divorce, signed "Russ". She is still painting the world she came from: a woman reading, a self-portrait with a palette. The journal of this period is about fame as a goal in itself: "At twenty-two I shall be either famous or dead".

1880-1881, the studio picture and the claim to the room. On Christmas Eve 1880 Julian commissions the studio painting; she fights him over a partition, signs it "Andrey", and it hangs at the Salon of 1881 to great acclaim. This is the high point of the academic phase: a large multi-figure interior, sixteen students, a nude model, a velvet drape painted to show off. The mechanism is M7 (the crowded room) and M8 (herself at the centre). In autumn 1881 her health breaks: deafness increases, Dr. Fauvel finds her lungs infected.

1882-1883, the turn to the streets. She visits Bastien-Lepage's home studio on Saturday 21 January 1882 -- he gives her two brushes -- and meets him again in Nice later in 1882. "He persuaded her to look beyond her wealthy lifestyle and observe and depict in her paintings those who were less financially fortunate than herself. She listened to Bastien-Lepage and soon the subjects of her work changed. Her works soon depicted the lower classes and street scenes". The orphanage becomes her studio: she arranges with a local orphanage to paint the children, visits on 23 August 1882 handing out sweets, and paints The Umbrella outdoors in the rain, In the Mist (1882), Jean et Jacques (February 1883, thrown away and restarted in two hours). Brushwork loosens; the two-speed painting of M4 and the dull palette of M6 arrive. She also paints her one true cityscape without people (In the Mist) and her one peopled landscape without a story (Autumn, 1883). Salon 1883: Jean et Jacques, La Parisienne and the Dina Babanine pastel accepted, with an honourable mention -- which work took it is unresolved (see the Load-Bearing Corrections).

1883-1884, the last year and the big canvas. By Easter 1883 she has decided on six life-size boys on a street corner. Spring is begun May 1883 as a decorative panel, her one rural Bastien-Lepage pastiche, painted out of doors at Sevres in March-June 1884. The Meeting is finished for the Salon of 1884 and is a public success but takes no medal; the state buys it. She paints the pastel Portrait of Madame X for the same Salon. In May 1884 she writes the journal preface that plans for both outcomes: fame in life, or the journal published after death. By July 1884 she and Jules see each other three times a week; by August she knows she is dying. The Holy Women, her intended masterpiece, is left unfinished. Last journal entry Monday 20 October 1884; death Friday 31 October 1884; Bastien-Lepage dies Wednesday 10 December 1884, forty days later.

Afterlife: the Journal is published in 1887 (only the second diary by a woman published in France to that date); Gladstone calls it "a book without a parallel"; it becomes the model for the confessional diaries of Anais Nin, Katherine Mansfield, Pierre Louys and Mary MacLane. The mausoleum in Cimetiere de Passy, designed by Emile Bastien-Lepage as a full-sized studio, is declared a historic monument. About 230 works made 1877-1884; at least 60 survive, the rest destroyed or looted in the Second World War.

## Aesthetic Logic
Bashkirtseff's logic is a transfer of ownership. Bastien-Lepage owned the fields -- her own sentence, widely circulated: "I say nothing of the fields because Bastien-Lepage reigns over them as a sovereign; but the streets, however, have not yet had their... Bastien" (translation, secondhand; not found in the Serrano 1889 text read this leg -- see the Doorway and the Load-Bearing Corrections). She takes his system -- life-size figures painted outdoors, faces finished to photographic precision, everything else simplified -- and moves it from the village to the Paris street, from peasants to orphanage children and schoolboys. The transfer is literal in the pictures: the same square format and the same peasant under a tree appear in Spring, her one rural canvas, and it reads as his; the street pictures read as hers because the subject changed and the system stayed.

Her second logic is the diary as the other half of the work. The paintings are small in number -- about 230 works, 60 surviving -- and the journal is thousands of pages. The journal does three jobs the paintings cannot: it records the making (the partition fight, the rain, the thrown-away canvas, the street lamp Julian made her remove); it states the ambition out loud ("I am resolved to be a great artist and I will be one"); and it plans its own publication after her death. The paintings observe the world as it forms; the diary insists on a place inside it. That is why the two famous works are one group scene and one written record: The Meeting watches power being negotiated among boys, and the Journal watches fame being negotiated by a girl.

Her third logic is refusal as a technique. She refuses the drapery Robert-Fleury wants in The Meeting ("not finding drapery fitting to her modern street boys"); she refuses the medal system by being indignant at it and then outliving it in print; she refuses the women's atelier as a separate, lesser room by painting it as the largest and most ambitious picture of her student years, with herself at its centre. The refusals are visible in the pictures as absences: no drapery, no object in the boys' hands, no people in Autumn, no finished masterpiece. What she leaves out is the argument.

The through-line for prompts: a system of attention, not a style. Rings of gazes around a hidden point; one face finished and the rest simplified; dull weather-colour with one saturated note; a crowd where every figure has a different job; a girl walking away at the edge; a city emptied of people. All of it is watchable because it is all about looking -- who looks at whom, who looks away, and who is allowed to look at all.

## POSITIONS
Status key: OWN = her own stated view or practice, evidenced from her Journal/letters as printed in a source read this leg; SECONDHAND = her view as reported/quoted by a contemporary or biographer; RECEPTION = what others said or did about her. All her Journal quotes are translations from French.

P1. OWN (via Blind's quotation): her chief object as a painter was to seize life -- to seize the flying impression as she happened to see it, and render it with unflinching faithfulness to nature, without any attempt at arrangement, composition, or beauty of treatment. Source: Blind 1892 study. Status: SECONDHAND quotation, her stated aim as Blind presents it.
P2. OWN (TRANSLATION, via Wikipedia citing Serrano 1889): she ceded the fields to Bastien-Lepage and claimed the streets for herself -- "I say nothing of the fields because Bastien-Lepage reigns over them as a sovereign; but the streets, however, have not yet had their... Bastien." CAVEAT: this sentence does NOT occur in the Serrano 1889 text as read this leg (browser.find for "I say nothing of the fields" and for "reigns" found no such passage; the two "reigns" hits are unrelated). It evidently comes from another translation (Blind 1890 or Hall/Heckel 1890) or the unexpurgated manuscript, and Wikipedia's citation to Serrano is at best imprecise. Treat as her view, TRANSLATION, not verified in Serrano.
P3. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Serrano 1889 preface): fame was the plan from the start, with the diary as the fallback route -- "If I do not die young I hope to live as a great artist; but if I die young, I intend to have my journal, which cannot fail to be interesting, published." Written May 1884, five months before her death.
P4. OWN (via Blind's quotation, 1892 study): art became her only passion -- "Outside of my art, which I commenced from caprice and ambition, which I continued out of vanity, and which I now worship; outside of this passion -- for it is a passion -- there is nothing." Status: SECONDHAND quotation.
P5. OWN (via Blind's quotation, 1892 study): work demands total self-throw -- "Work is a fatiguing process, dreaded yet loved by fine and powerful natures, who frequently succumb to it. For if the artist does not fling himself into his work as unhesitatingly as Curtius did into the chasm at his feet..." Status: SECONDHAND quotation (the page renders "fine" as "line", an evident transcription error; quoted here as "fine").
P6. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Kernberger excerpts): women must have the nude model -- "I would like horribly to pose in the gentlemen's studio -- nude. People are ashamed to be nude because they are afraid they aren't perfect." Lapham's Quarterly reports she was the first person to insist women be allowed to paint from the nude model at the Academie Julian.
P7. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Kernberger excerpts): women's unfreedom is the central injustice -- "How short life is; how sad to live so little! How much women are to be pitied! At least men are free. They have absolute freedom in ordinary life... Having liberty is half the battle in developing talent." She adds that a young pretty woman who emancipates herself "blacklists herself", so "there's nothing to do but regret my sex".
P8. OWN (via Blind's quotation, 1892 study): the poor and the street are where movement and truth live -- asked why she does not paint the elegances around her, she replies, "Where, then, shall I find any movement, any of that savage and primitive liberty, any true expression?" Status: SECONDHAND quotation.
P9. OWN (via Blind, 1892 study, and Journal account): Spain in autumn 1881 woke her to colour -- Velasquez and Goya "opened her eyes", and she "raised herself on tiptoe" to master their method; on return to Paris she painted The Umbrella (1883), "a great leap forward". Status: SECONDHAND report of a Journal passage.
P10. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Serrano 1889, March 1876): ambition and vanity are the engine, confessed without apology -- "I am ambitious -- that is my greatest fault!" and (5 April 1876) "Vanity! Vanity! Vanity! The beginning and the end of all things, and the eternal and sole cause of all things."
P11. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Serrano 1889 preface): the diary must be exact truth or nothing -- "If this book is not the exact, the absolute, the strict truth, it has no raison d'etre. Not only do I always write what I think, but I have not even dreamed, for a single instant, of disguising anything that was to my disadvantage." From October 1875 she placed every notebook under the motto "Gloriae cupiditate".
P12. OWN (via Blind, 1892 study): observation is the whole of genius -- she "loved to recall Balzac's questionable definition that the genius of observation is almost the whole of human genius", and her portraits succeed because "All her likenesses are singularly individual, and we realize their character at a glance." Status: SECONDHAND.
P13. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM in substance, Kernberger excerpts; Blind 1892 study): her great unpainted ambition was a religious picture done as realism -- "I made a sketch for a subject that attracts me: Mary Magdalene and the other Mary at Christ's sepulcher. I won't use conventions or traditional effects, but show it as it's supposed to have been, to let people feel it." The two Maries were to be forlorn outcasts, "the Louise Michels of their time"; only sketches survive, plus the unfinished canvas in her Passy tomb.
P14. OWN, CONTESTED: feminist journalism under the pen name "Pauline Orrel" (also spelled Orell). Wikipedia states she wrote several articles for Hubertine Auclert's feminist newspaper La Citoyenne in 1881 as "Pauline Orrel". Eclectic Light says this claim "appears to be unsupported by the original edited versions of her diaries". The unexpurgated journal (Kernberger excerpts) DOES record her visiting Auclert's office, giving the name "Pauline Orell", joining the organisation and paying dues -- so the Auclert connection and the pen name are real; the newspaper column itself is unverified. Status: PARTIAL / CONTESTED.
P15. RECEPTION: Gladstone's verdict made the diary a European event. W. E. Gladstone reviewed the Journal in The Nineteenth Century (1889) and called it "a book without a parallel" for its insight into "human nature" and its sustained "self-record". Within a year the first English translation appeared in the US (Serrano 1889); Blind's followed in 1890.
P16. RECEPTION: the critics made her Bastien-Lepage's pupil, and it annoyed her. Blind (1892 study): "Many of the French critics called her the pupil of Bastien. But she had of course never been his actual pupil, having been trained in quite a different school, and it always gave her much annoyance to be called so."
P17. RECEPTION: The Meeting (Salon 1884) was a public and press success but won no medal -- only an honourable mention (for the pastel portrait of her cousin shown the same year). Blind calls it "a triumph of realism" that "secured a wide recognition for Marie Bashkirtseff, not only in artistic circles, but from the general public". Clara Erskine Clement Waters (1904) reports it was suspected she had been aided in the work, which may explain the missing medal.
P18. RECEPTION: the diary became a cult within three years. By 1890 her name was, as one English reviewer put it, "a household word". Pilgrimages to the Passy tomb (designed by Emile Bastien-Lepage) and her mother's conducted tours of the studio became part of "the Bashkirtseff cult". Her mother corresponded with readers and ran what one Ukrainian source calls "Madam Bashkirtseff's Salon" at the tomb.
P19. RECEPTION: the moral panic was immediate and gendered. Ferdinand Brunetiere demanded what right "this petite peintresse" had to impose herself on the public; reviewers called her "overstrung and overbalanced" and her frankness "an abdication of womanhood"; William T. Stead allowed her genius but not womanliness; George Bernard Shaw took Stead to task in 1913. Anatole France gave the Journal a chapter in La Vie litteraire, emerging "somewhat exhausted" by "a troubled soul"; Maurice Barres sanctified her as "Notre Dame du Sleeping Car" and urged French youth to do the same.
P20. RECEPTION / AFTERLIFE: she founded a diary-writing lineage. Mary MacLane named Byron and Bashkirtseff as the two minds most resembling her own and was dubbed "the Butte Bashkirtseff" by H. L. Mencken; Anais Nin (aged 18) and Katherine Mansfield (just 19) wrote entranced in their diaries of reading hers; Pierre Louys, aged 16, conceded in 1887 that the idea of keeping a diary came to him on reading Bashkirtseff; W. N. P. Barbellion declared her "the very spit of [him]" and published The Journal of a Disappointed Man (1919); Paula Modersohn-Becker, briefly a Julian student herself, sighed in her diary at Bashkirtseff's tenacity. Oscar Wilde put the joke on stage in 1895: Gwendolen never travels without her diary, "one should always have something sensational to read in the train".

## Load-Bearing Corrections
1. Birth date: 24 November 1858 (New Style) / 12 November 1858 (Old Style) is now established; the Old Style day is a Wednesday, the same physical day as 24 November New Style. Legs 1 and 2 give it as Friday, by running date -d on the literal date 12 November 1858 -- that is the wrong computation for an Old Style date, and the study carries Wednesday. Older sources give 11 November 1860 (her own falsified preface), 23 November 1860 (1911 Britannica), January 1859 / 24 January 1859 (Encyclopedia.com). Do not use 1860 or 1859 as her birth year. Age at death is 25.
2. First meeting with Bastien-Lepage: the main leg's Evolution draft places it "in Nice, January 1882". The bio leg shows the 21 January 1882 studio visit was in Paris (with Madame Cartwright; he gave her two brushes) and the Nice meeting came later in 1882, month not pinned. The study carries the corrected version.
3. Sorrow of Nausicaa date: the main leg carries 1882 with 1884 flagged; the bio leg shows the signature "MB 84" and bashkirtseff.org support 1884, and the journal entry of Tuesday 8 January 1884 has the sculpture being mounted, which fits a work modelled in 1882-83 and cast or mounted in January 1884. The study carries 1884 with the 1882 figure flagged as the possible modelling date.
4. Salon participation: Wikipedia says she exhibited "every year thereafter until her death (except 1883)". This is wrong on the sources read: the 1883 Salon catalogue lists Jean et Jacques (no. 125), and multiple sources record the 1883 Salon acceptance and honourable mention. Do not repeat the "except 1883" claim.
5. Which 1883 work took the honourable mention: musings says Jean et Jacques, awarded by Lefebvre, Bouguereau and Robert-Fleury; 1911 Britannica says the pastel portrait of her cousin took the mention honorable; Eclectic Light ties the 1883 mention to The Umbrella's paragraph; Wilson says by May 1884 she had "secured only one medal at the Salon and this -- to her disappointment -- for a pastel". Unresolved; the study says she received an honourable mention in 1883 and flags the disagreement, and does not assign it to a single work.
6. The Meeting medal 1884: mydailyartdisplay and Rollins say the jury passed her over for a medal and the public complained; Wikipedia says she received an honourable mention in 1884 for the pastel portrait of her cousin. Unresolved; the study carries both accounts as reported.
7. In the Studio dimensions: 188 x 154 cm (Eclectic Light) vs 154 x 186 cm (Rollins) vs 154 x 188 cm (wahooart). Location: Dnipro State Art Museum (Eclectic Light; Lapham's Dnipropetrovsk) vs National Art Museum of Ukraine, Kyiv (wahooart). The study carries Dnipro with the conflict flagged; the Dnipro attribution is the majority and the more specific one.
8. The Meeting object: a nest (her first journal sketch) vs a bent branch drawn taut with a string (ChasingART). The object is not visible in the painting; the study carries it as unidentified, with both readings noted, and does not assert one.
9. The Meeting scale: ChasingART calls it "modest in scale"; the Orsay dimensions are 193 x 177 cm. The "modest" claim is not sustainable; do not repeat it.
10. "Three months before him": Eclectic Light says she died "just three months before" Bastien-Lepage. The gap is 40 days: 31 October to 10 December 1884. Use forty days, not three months.
11. "First woman artist presented in the Louvre" (wpower headline) is garbled: The Meeting was bought by the state and hung in the Luxembourg, not the Louvre. Do not repeat the Louvre claim.
12. The Pauline Orrel / La Citoyenne claim: Wikipedia states it as fact; Eclectic Light says it "appears to be unsupported by the original edited versions of her diaries". The Auclert visit as "Pauline Orell" IS verified in the Kernberger excerpts. Flag the newspaper column as contested.
13. Jean et Jacques date: 1883 is the majority (Salon catalogue 1883, diary sources); 1911 Britannica dates it 1882. Carry 1883.
14. Parents' separation: 1859 (Rollins/Cahuet), age 2 (musings), 1862 (Encyclopedia.com), age 12 (Wikipedia -- reject as confusion with the 1870 departure). Safest statement: the marriage broke down in her early childhood; the family left Russia in May 1870.
15. Age when the journal began: 12 (journal itself), about 13 (Wikipedia), 14 (Wilson), 15 (musings). The 12 figure follows the true birth date.
16. Output totals: 229 works (100 paintings + 6 pastels + 118 drawings + 5 sculptures, Parker and Pollock via Zimmerman) vs about 230 (Prabook) vs 200 works of art (Rollins) vs 100 oils in the 1885 catalogue (Sotheby's). Notebook counts: 106 (Wilson/Rollins) vs 104 exercise books (Zimmerman).
17. Newberry Library, Chicago as holder of Jean et Jacques (Encyclopedia.com): no corroboration in sources read; do not state as a current holding.
18. Eclectic Light's 2016 "Brief Candles" piece and the 2026 "Naturalists" piece are near-identical in text (the 2026 piece reuses the 2016 captions and paragraphs); they count as one source voice, not two independent corroborations. Only the 2026 piece was read end to end in the main leg.
19. The UCL blog dates the deafness entries 1880 in its introduction and 1882 in its later paragraph; the 16 November "I am growing deaf" entry belongs to the 1882 deafness crisis per the blog's own later text.
20. Death cause: tuberculosis / consumption (all sources agree). The #495 study file says she died "not of the same disease" as Bastien-Lepage -- consistent: he died of testicular cancer, she of tuberculosis. No conflict.
