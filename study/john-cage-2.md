**Site:** John Milton Cage Jr., born Thursday 5 September 1912 in Los Angeles, California, the son of John Milton Cage Sr., an inventor, and Lucretia Harvey Cage, and he studied with Henry Cowell in 1933, who recommended Arnold Schoenberg, with whom he studied from 1933 to 1935 free of charge on the condition that he devote his life to music, and he answered that he would devote his life to beating his head against that wall, and Schoenberg later called him an inventor, of genius, not a composer, and he taught at the Cornish School in Seattle, Mills College, and Black Mountain College, and he wrote dance music for Merce Cunningham from the early 1940s, and they became partners for life after his divorce in 1945, living together in Stony Point, New York, from 1954, and he devised the prepared piano for Bacchanale in 1940, Cage sometimes said 1938, see C5, and he adopted chance operations in 1951 after his pupil Christian Wolff gave him the I Ching, see C8, and he wrote Music of Changes in 1951, 4'33" in 1952, and the Variations series from 1958 to 1967, and he played chess weekly with Marcel Duchamp from about 1965 until Duchamp's death, and he made HPSCHD with Lejaren Hiller, premiered Friday 16 May 1969, and the Europeras from 1987 to 1991, and the Number Pieces from 1987 to 1992, and his only film, One11, a film of light, completed weeks before his death, and he died on Wednesday 12 August 1992 in New York, aged 79, 29,196 days, less than a month short of 80, and his ashes were scattered in the Ramapo Mountains near Stony Point with his parents', and he is the composer who replaced the note with the question, the score with the chart, the concert with the room, and the composer with the coin toss.

## M1. Nested proportions: the frame before the sound

The rhythmic structure of a piece is fixed as a set of proportions, 4, 3, 2, 3, 4, and each unit is subdivided by the same proportions, like a fractal, and content is then poured into the frame. Imaginary Landscape No. 1 of 1939 has four large sections of 16, 17, 18, 19 bars, each divided into four subsections, the first three of 5 bars, and it is the first use of nested proportions and the first live electronics: two variable-speed turntables playing frequency recordings, muted piano, cymbal. First Construction (in Metal) of 1939 has five sections of 4, 3, 2, 3, 4 units, each unit 16 bars divided 4, 3, 2, and the content is built from sixteen motives, played on brake drums, tin cans and gongs. What you hear is clanging metal percussion in strict, almost architectural rhythmic blocks, and sliding record pitches locked into an audible arithmetic grid, music that feels architectural and balanced even when the sounds are strange, the ear sensing halves and quarters rather than melodies. **Takeaway:** fix the proportions first, subdivide them by themselves, then pour any sound into the frame, and the balance will survive the strangeness. Against the chain: Duchamp (#397) also fixed a frame first, the standard of the dropped thread, but his frame was a chance object kept as a ruler, and Cage's frame is a ratio kept as a clock, and the difference is between a measurement and a duration.

## M2. The prepared piano: one piano, a percussion orchestra

Screws, bolts, nuts, rubber, plastic, felt weather stripping and wood are wedged between or on the strings of a grand piano, changing each prepared note into a percussion sound, gong, drum, marimba, muted thud, and the performer often determines exact positions by experiment. Bacchanale of 1940 was written for Syvilla Fort's dance at the Cornish School in Seattle, where the room had no space for a percussion ensemble, only a grand piano: Cage tried a metal pie plate on the strings, right sound, bounced around, then nails, slipped through, then settled on screws, bolts and weather stripping. Twelve notes were prepared, mostly weather stripping, and the score tells the player to "determine position and size of mutes by experiment" in 11 of 12 cases, and it was finished in three days, per Cage in 1982. The idea originated with Henry Cowell and his "string piano", plucking and scraping inside the piano, and Cage coined the term and made it famous, he did not invent it ex nihilo, see C5. Sonatas and Interludes of 1946 to 1948 is the prepared piano become a concert instrument: sixteen sonatas and four interludes, built to express the eight "permanent emotions" of the Indian rasa tradition, white, humor, wonder, erotic, heroic, black, anger, fear, disgust, sorrow, with nested proportions taken to non-integer ratios, Sonata I, 1 1/4, 3/4, 1 1/4, 3/4, 1 1/2, 1 1/2, and it was premiered in full by Maro Ajemian at Carnegie Hall in 1949, and the performance won Cage a Guggenheim Fellowship, and it is widely called his first masterpiece and his finest pre-chance work. What you hear is a gamelan-like piano, melodies that sound almost tonal, structures you feel as balanced halves rather than hear as tunes, and what you see is an instrument that looks normal from the outside and is a thicket of hardware inside. **Takeaway:** change the instrument, not the player, and one pair of hands becomes an orchestra, and the preparation is the composition. Against the chain: Duchamp also changed the object by adding hardware, the bicycle wheel on a stool, but his hardware was a joke about use, and Cage's hardware is a working percussion kit, and the difference is between an object that refuses its job and an instrument that takes on new ones.

## M3. The closed piano and silence as material

The piano lid stays shut and the instrument is struck, scraped or knocked, or silence is written into the structure as a sound of equal value. The Wonderful Widow of Eighteen Springs of 1942 is voice and closed piano: the pianist plays only the closed lid, knocks and scrapes, and the singer sings a Joyce text, and it was performed by Cathy Berberian and Luciano Berio. The Perilous Night of 1943 to 1944 is the first large-scale work for prepared piano, six movements, a narrative of danger and a fall, per Cage, with low gong-like sonorities and long silences between events. Waiting of 1952 frames a single ostinato with long silences. The line ends at 4'33", a whole piece of framed non-playing, see M9. What you hear is a voice floating over soft percussive taps on a piano that is never opened, and then the room itself, wind, rain, coughs, chairs, your own listening. **Takeaway:** close the lid and the piano becomes a drum, and write the silence into the structure and the room becomes the instrument. Against the chain: Duchamp also worked with what is not there, the delay in the Glass, but his absence is a machine that does not work, and Cage's absence is a room that does, and the difference is between a picture of nothing and a duration full of something.

## M4. Gamuts: harmony that hovers instead of travelling

A chord with fixed instrumentation is composed first, and the piece moves from gamut to gamut only when the melody needs a note the next gamut contains, and the other notes in the chord are bystanders, so no chord pulls toward a resolution. The String Quartet in Four Parts of 1949 to 1950 is Cage's break with harmony: he composed "gamuts", chords with fixed instrumentation, and moved from gamut to gamut only because the next gamut contained a note the melody needed, and the remaining notes form no directional harmony. What you hear is quiet, floating chords with no pull toward a home key, stasis instead of argument. **Takeaway:** fix the chord first and let the melody drag the piece from chord to chord, and the harmony will hover instead of travelling. Against the chain: Duchamp also broke a travelling form, the painting that goes nowhere in the Glass, but his break is a machine stalled in glass, and Cage's is a chord refusing to resolve, and the difference is between a picture that will not move and a sound that will not arrive.

## M5. Charts and geometric selection: the composer as clerk

Before composing, Cage tabulates every parameter, sounds, durations, dynamics, tempi, densities, into charts, then picks from them by a pattern, a geometric figure traced across the chart. The Concerto for Prepared Piano and Chamber Orchestra of 1950 to 1951 is built from charts of durations, dynamics and melodies, chosen by simple geometric patterns, and its last movement is the step toward chance: Cage used the I Ching there for the first time in a composed work. What you hear is a prepared piano soloist against an orchestra that seems to answer from a different set of rules, the final movement loosening into unpredictability, and a music of evenly distributed variety, no parameter favored, the composer acting as a clerk of his own tables. **Takeaway:** tabulate everything first, then let a shape drawn across the table choose, and taste never gets a vote. Against the chain: Duchamp also tabulated, the Green Box notes, but his tables are notes toward a machine, and Cage's are the machine itself, and the difference is between a box of instructions and a score made of them.

## M6. I Ching coin-toss chance operations: the question, not the answer

Three coin tosses make a line, six lines make one of 64 hexagrams, and the hexagram number selects a cell from an 8-by-8 chart. Cage asked the book questions, "which mode?", "which transposition?", "which note?". The gift came from his pupil Christian Wolff in early 1951: the first complete English translation, published by Wolff's father Kurt Wolff at Pantheon Books in 1950. Music of Changes of 1951 is the method made audible: for solo piano, written for David Tudor, four books completed Wednesday 16 May, 2 August, 18 October and 13 December 1951, large 8-by-8 charts of sounds, durations, dynamics, tempi and densities, coin tosses producing a hexagram, the hexagram picking a cell, the cell giving the event. Book I was played in public by Tudor on Thursday 5 July 1951, and the complete premiere was by Tudor on Tuesday 1 January 1952 at the Cherry Lane Theater, New York, and Tudor recorded it complete in 1956. The score is fully notated and fixed from performance to performance; only the composition was chanced. Cage was so poor while writing it that he left Tudor instructions for finishing it if he died. What you hear is extreme piano music, dense chordal clusters alternating with single notes and long silences, tempos that accelerate and slow by spatial notation, a pianist working from a stopwatch, a fully notated score that no taste could have written. **Takeaway:** ask a book a question with coins, and the answer will be a piece no one would have chosen, and then write it down exactly. Against the chain: Duchamp also asked a question with a mechanism, the numbered balls dropped through a funnel into a toy train, see C1, but his answer was 85 notes sung once, and Cage's was a system for asking forever, and the difference is between one chance event and a chance practice.

## M7. The radio as an unpredictable instrument

A broadcast receiver is played like an instrument: tuning, volume and tone are the performance, and the content, music, speech, static, dead air, is whatever is on the air at that moment in that place. Imaginary Landscape No. 4 (March No. 2) of 1951 is for 24 performers on 12 radios plus conductor, and it is Cage's first composition based fully on chance operations: each radio has two players, one tunes, one works volume and tone, following chance-generated notations that say how far to turn the knobs. It was premiered in May 1951, the exact day is disputed, 2 May per Wikipedia, 10 May per Revill, see C13, at McMillin Theater, Columbia University, Cage conducting, and the performers included Judith Malina, Julian Beck, Lou Harrison and Remy Charlip. The concert ran late, most stations were off the air, and the premiere was mostly silence and static. What you hear is fragments of broadcast, music, talk, static, surfacing and vanishing on a strict clock, the room's own radio weather, a piece that cannot be rehearsed into sameness. **Takeaway:** play the receiver, not the recording, and the piece will be different in every town and every hour, because the town is the instrument. Against the chain: Duchamp also let the room decide, the readymade chosen from what the shop had, but his choice was once and for all, and Cage's is made again every second the radio is on, and the difference is between a found object and a found broadcast.

## M8. Tape cutting and splicing as composition

Recorded sounds are sorted into categories, selected by chance, and cut and spliced to a full-size drawn score that shows exactly where the tape shall be cut; the tape is laid on the score. Williams Mix of 1951 to 1953 is for eight simultaneously played quarter-inch magnetic tapes, 4 minutes 15 seconds, some sources say 4'16", the first octophonic piece, and the third of five pieces in the Project for Music for Magnetic Tape of 1951 to 1954, funded by architect Paul Williams Jr. Sounds recorded by Bebe and Louis Barron were sorted into six categories, A city, B country, C electronic, D manually produced, E wind, F "small", and a deck of 1024 cards and I Ching operations selected them. The 193-page score is a full-size drawing of tape fragments, "a dressmaker's pattern: it literally shows where the tape shall be cut, and you lay the tape on the score itself." About 600 recordings were needed, some 350 distinct sounds, 2,128 iterations. It was realized with Earle Brown, Morton Feldman, David Tudor and the Barrons, the score was completed in October 1952, the tapes finished 16 January 1953, and it was premiered in March 1953, the 23rd per Wikipedia, the 22nd per the Cage database, see C12, at the University of Illinois Festival of Contemporary Arts. What you hear is sound jumping cut to cut, city noise into electronic buzz into a voice fragment, up to sixteen layers at once, circling the room from eight speakers, a collage with a dressmaker's precision. **Takeaway:** draw the cut first, lay the tape on the drawing, and the score becomes a recipe anyone can remake with other sounds. Against the chain: Duchamp also cut and pasted, the Boite-en-valise as a portable museum of miniatures, but his box preserves the past, and Cage's pattern remakes it, and the difference is between a museum and a recipe.

## M9. Duration as the only composed element: the frame

The composer fixes only time lengths, three movements of 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, 1 minute 40 seconds, a single sentence, a time bracket, and leaves the sound content to the room, the performer, or chance. 4'33" of 1952 is for any instrument or combination: the performer plays nothing, and the content is the ambient sound of the room. It was conceived in 1947 to 1948 as "Silent Prayer", a piece of uninterrupted silence to sell to the Muzak Co., three or four and a half minutes long. The direct triggers were the 1951 visit to Harvard's anechoic chamber, Cage heard two sounds, his nervous system high, his blood circulation low, and concluded silence does not exist, and Rauschenberg's White Paintings of 1951: "The white paintings came first; my silent piece came later." It was premiered by David Tudor on Friday 29 August 1952 at Maverick Concert Hall, Woodstock, New York, as part of a contemporary piano recital: Tudor closed and opened the keyboard lid to mark movements and timed them with a stopwatch, though whether he closed to begin or opened to begin is disputed, see C6. Wind sounded in the first movement, rain on the roof in the second, audience talking and walking out in the third. The 1961 published score gives different lengths, 33 seconds, 2 minutes 40 seconds, 1 minute 20 seconds, both totals 4'33", the digits rearranged. The original manuscript is lost; Tudor reconstructed it from memory twice in 1989, differently. Cage was not the first to compose silence, and he said he did not know the precedents: Alphonse Allais, Funeral March for the Obsequies of a Deaf Man, 1897, 24 empty measures; Erwin Schulhoff, In Futurum, 1919, a movement of rests; Yves Klein, Monotone-Silence Symphony, 1949, two twenty-minute silent movements, see C7. The famous 273-seconds-equals-absolute-zero theory is false: the duration was decided by chance, see C4. What you hear is the room itself, and the listening itself is the event, and the audience becomes the performer. **Takeaway:** compose only the duration, and the room will compose the rest, differently every time. Against the chain: Duchamp also composed a duration, the delay of the Glass, eight years of work declared unfinished, but his duration is a wait for a picture, and Cage's is a frame for a room, and the difference is between waiting for something and listening to everything.

## M10. Graphic notation and transparent overlays: the score as a tool

The score is a set of transparent sheets, points and lines, or a book of graphic shapes; the performer overlays them and reads the result as a coordinate system for sound qualities, or chooses an order and a realization. The Concert for Piano and Orchestra of 1957 to 1958 is the piece that made Tudor's role central: the solo piano part is a separate work, the Solo for Piano, 84 pages of graphic notations, letters A to Z and more, and the orchestra has 63 parts, each a single page of notation to be played in any order, at any time, with a conductor using arm movements to indicate time. It was premiered 15 May 1958 at Town Hall, New York, Tudor soloist. Variations I of 1958 has six transparent squares, one with points of various sizes, five with five intersecting lines; the performer overlays them and reads lines and points as a coordinate system in which the lines are axes of sound characteristics, lowest frequency, simplest overtone structure, and so on. No number of performers or instruments is specified, and it was composed as a tribute to David Tudor. Later Variations are increasingly text-only: Variations III of 1962 is instructions with no reference to music, instruments or sounds. What you see is performers measuring transparencies with rulers and then making sounds, and the same score yields wholly different concerts. **Takeaway:** print the score on transparent sheets, and the performer becomes a reader of overlays, and the piece becomes a tool, not a text. Against the chain: Duchamp also made a score that is not a score, the Green Box notes, but his notes describe a machine that was never built, and Cage's transparencies are the machine, and the difference is between notes about a work and a work made of notes.

## M11. Text scores and mesostics: one sentence is enough

The score is a sentence, "In a situation provided with maximum amplification, perform a disciplined action", or a poem built as a mesostic, a vertical spine word running down the middle of horizontal lines. 0'00" of 1962, also called 4'33" No. 2, has that one sentence as its whole score, and its first performance was Cage writing that sentence. What you see is an amplified everyday action, writing, shaving, eating, made enormous by the microphones, and the discipline is the composition. Song Books of 1970 has ninety solos for voice, using texts, mesostics, and the I Ching, and Solos 67 and 91 are the "36 Mesostics re. and not re. Marcel Duchamp", written for Shigeko Kubota's book Marcel Duchamp and John Cage of 1970. What you hear is a singer choosing among solos, speaking, singing, shouting, whispering, using the voice as an instrument with no fixed order, and a text that is also a picture. **Takeaway:** write one sentence, amplify the action it names, and the discipline is the piece. Against the chain: Duchamp also wrote one sentence that is a work, the signature R. Mutt 1917, but his sentence claims an object, and Cage's commands an action, and the difference is between a name on a thing and an instruction to a body.

## M12. Simultaneous independent layers: the event and the circus

Several performers or groups do unrelated things in the same space during assigned time intervals; nothing is synchronized except the clock. Theatre Piece No. 1 of 1952, the Black Mountain "Untitled Event", was organized by Cage in August 1952, best guess early August, perhaps the 16th, in the dining hall of Black Mountain College, North Carolina, the same day he conceived it. The audience sat in the middle in four triangular sections, facing each other, unable to see everything. Cage stood on a ladder reading his "Juilliard Lecture", with silences; M. C. Richards and Charles Olson read poetry from a second ladder; Merce Cunningham danced through the audience chased by a barking dog; David Tudor played prepared piano; Rauschenberg played Edith Piaf records on an Edison-horn gramophone; four of Rauschenberg's White Paintings were suspended from the ceiling; slides and a film by Nicholas Cernovitch played; coffee was served by four boys in white. Forty-five minutes; each participant had two time segments to fill as they chose. Thirty-five to fifty people attended. It was untitled at the time, and it was later called the first Happening, though the term "happening" was coined by Allan Kaprow in the late 1950s, see C11. Musicircus of 1967 assembles performers to play together in one space, a mass superimposition of many musics. HPSCHD of 1967 to 1969 is the same idea at the largest scale, see W18 in the notes. What you see is several unrelated events happening at once in all directions, no front, no plot, and the spectator composes the piece by choosing where to look and listen. **Takeaway:** give everyone a time slot and no instructions to agree, and the audience will compose the piece by choosing what to attend to. Against the chain: Duchamp also staged unrelated things at once, the 1938 Surrealist exhibition with its coal sacks and flashlights, but his simultaneity was a room to walk through, and Cage's is a clock to sit inside, and the difference is between an exhibition and an event.

## M13. Star charts as pitch source: the night sky as a keyboard

A transparent strip is laid on an astronomical chart, Atlas Eclipticalis, Atlas Australis; the stars under the strip give pitches; the I Ching decides which pitches stay single and which become aggregates chosen from a table of about 550. Atlas Eclipticalis of 1961 to 1962 is fully notated, its pitches from star charts, and with Winter Music it is the source of the later etudes method. Etudes Australes of 1974 to 1975 are 32 etudes for piano, dedicated to Grete Sultan, pitches from the Atlas Australis star charts read through a transparent strip, aggregates chosen from a table of about 550, and they are extremely difficult by design, "a performance would show that the impossible is not impossible". Freeman Etudes of 1977 to 1990 are 32 etudes for violin, for Paul Zukofsky, also star-chart derived. What you hear is music of extreme difficulty and sparseness, the night sky read as a keyboard. **Takeaway:** lay a strip across a star chart, and the sky will give you notes no hand would choose. Against the chain: Duchamp also read a sky, the Milky Way in the Glass, but his sky is a picture of desire, and Cage's is a table of pitches, and the difference is between a symbol and a source.

## M14. Reworking found music by chance: the familiar ghost

Take an existing piece, keep its rhythmic structure, and replace its pitches, or some of them, with chance-determined ones. Cheap Imitation of 1969 is Cage's first fully notated work in years, a chance-controlled reworking of Erik Satie's Socrate of 1919: Cage kept Satie's rhythmic structure and chose pitches by asking the I Ching three questions per phrase, which of seven white-note modes, which of twelve chromatic transpositions, which note of the seven imitates Satie's note. It was written because Cage and Cunningham wanted to extend a 1947 Satie transcription and could not get the rights. It is the last work Cage performed in public himself; arthritis ended his performing in the 1970s. What you hear is Satie's shapes, gentle and familiar, with the notes displaced into an unfamiliar sweetness, a familiar ghost with the notes moved. **Takeaway:** keep the rhythm of a beloved piece, chance the notes, and the ghost will sing in a voice no one has heard. Against the chain: Duchamp also reworked a beloved picture, the moustache on the Mona Lisa, but his rework is a single gesture on a postcard, and Cage's is a whole piece rebuilt note by note, and the difference is between a joke on a reproduction and a method for remaking.

## M15. Tracing as notation: draw around the stones

Draw around real objects, the fifteen stones of the Ryoanji garden, placed at I Ching-determined points on a grid, and use the tracings as graphic notation; the curves are read as glissandi within a pitch range, the empty sand as a percussion part of one unspecified sound in unison. Ryoanji of 1983 to 1985 is solos for oboe, flute, voice, trombone and double bass with percussion or orchestral obbligato, its glissandi traced from the perimeters of the fifteen stones from the Kyoto rock garden. The same stones generate drawings, etchings and music. What you hear is a solo line that slides like a finger around a stone. **Takeaway:** trace a real thing, and the tracing is a score no hand could have invented. Against the chain: Duchamp also traced a real thing, the torn square traced and shadow-cast in According to What, but Johns did the tracing, not Duchamp, and Cage traced the stones himself, and the difference is between a trace remembered by a friend and a trace made by the composer.

## M16. Time brackets: the piece that breathes

The score is a set of short fragments, each with a window in which it may start and a window in which it must end, for example start anywhere between 1 minute 15 seconds and 1 minute 45 seconds, end anywhere from 2 minutes to 2 minutes 30 seconds, and pitches are chosen from a range by chance. The Number Pieces of 1987 to 1992 are about forty, The Art Story says 52, see C14, each named for the number of performers, and they were written as arthritis ended Cage's performing. What you hear is a quiet, drifting ensemble texture, notes overlapping or standing alone differently every time, the piece breathing rather than marching. **Takeaway:** fix only the windows, and the notes will arrive when they arrive, and the piece will breathe. Against the chain: Duchamp also fixed only windows, the two peepholes of Etant donnes, but his windows frame a secret you may look at, and Cage's frame a sound you may hear, and the difference is between a door you cannot open and a time you cannot hold.

## Evolution

Four phases. The through-line: from the hand, to the preparation, to the question, to the window. 1) Apprenticeship and percussion, 1933 to 1940: twelve-tone and 25-note rows under Schoenberg from 1933 to 1935, then rhythm replaces harmony as the organizing principle, percussion ensembles, dance accompaniment at UCLA, Mills and Cornish, found objects, brake drums, tin cans, as instruments, nested proportions, and the phase ends with the prepared piano, a practical solution to a small room. 2) The prepared piano and the Eastern turn, 1940 to 1950: dance commissions dominate, the prepared piano becomes a concert instrument, The Perilous Night, Sonatas and Interludes, and in the mid-1940s a crisis, disillusion with music as communication, Gita Sarabhai teaches Indian music and philosophy from early 1946, D. T. Suzuki's Zen lectures in the late 1940s, Coomaraswamy, and Sarabhai's stated goal, music "to sober and quiet the mind, thus rendering it susceptible to divine influences", and gamuts break harmony, and silence grows inside the pieces. 3) Chance, indeterminacy, the event, 1951 to 1969: the I Ching arrives in early 1951, Wolff's gift, Music of Changes and Imaginary Landscape No. 4 are the first fully chance-based works, Williams Mix puts chance on tape, 4'33" puts it in the room, Theatre Piece No. 1 puts it on a stage with dancers, poets and a dog, the New York School forms, Cage, Feldman, Brown, Wolff, Tudor, graphic scores, text scores, tape and radio, the New School classes of 1956 to 1961 seed Fluxus and Happenings, and the phase ends with HPSCHD, the largest work, and Cheap Imitation, the turn back to fully notated music. 4) Return to notation, the late systems, 1970 to 1992: Song Books, improvisation admitted on Cage's terms, plants and conch shells as chance instruments, Child of Tree 1975, Branches 1976, Inlets 1977, water-filled conch shells tipped until a bubble forms and sounds, by pure chance, the star-chart etudes, Etudes Australes, Freeman Etudes, Ryoanji tracings, the Europeras, chance applied to all of opera at once, the Number Pieces, about forty in five years, written as arthritis ended his performing, the visual work, prints at Crown Point Press from 1978, watercolors, the Ryoanji drawings, becomes a parallel practice, and One11, a film of light, is the last work.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is the removal, and the music is not a picture OF the world but a room in time, and everything is relational, the proportion against the sound, the preparation against the string, the silence against the note, the gamut against the resolution, the chart against the taste, the coin against the decision, the radio against the broadcast, the cut against the tape, the duration against the room, the overlay against the reading, the sentence against the action, the event against the audience, the star against the pitch, the ghost against the note, the tracing against the stone, the window against the arrival, and the relations are what carry the meaning, and he is the composer who walked out of composing by the front door, who took the decision and replaced it with a question, and the question is the point, because without the question the note would be a choice and without the note the question would be a word, and with both the two are a single argument, and the argument is that a coin can compose a sonata, and a sonata can be a silence, and a silence can be a room, and a room can be a radio, and a radio can be a chessboard, and a chessboard can be a concert, and a concert can be a game, and a game can be a piece, and the piece is whatever happens while the clock runs.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. "Erratum Musical (1913)" is singular in the brief; there were TWO pieces called Erratum Musical, both 1913, plus a third related note. The first is the vocal chance piece: Duchamp with his sisters Yvonne and Magdeleine drew notes from a hat, 75 notes for the 75 syllables of the dictionary definition of "imprimer", and sang them in the order drawn. The second, subtitled La Mariee mise a nu par ses celibataires, meme, is a system, not a score: numbered balls dropped through a funnel into the moving cars of a toy train, 85 balls per The Kitchen, 85 notes of the keyboard per Lotringer. A third note, "Musical Sculpture", "Sounds lasting and leaving from different places and forming a sounding sculpture that lasts", exists only as a Green Box note. The study says which Erratum it means. C2. Reunion, four corrections to the brief's one sentence. (a) It was not only "the two of them": Cage played Duchamp first, then Cage played Teeny Duchamp; the musicians were David Behrman, Gordon Mumma, David Tudor and Lowell Cross. (b) The board did not "make" music and there was no computer and no laser: the four musicians' sound systems ran continuously, and the board gated them on and off. Cross, who built the board, explicitly debunks the Slonimsky and Baker's version, computer plus laser rays, as fiction. (c) The mechanism was 64 photoresistors, one per square, covered and uncovered by the pieces, plus 9 contact microphones so the audience heard the physical moves; 16 inputs, 8 outputs to loudspeakers. (d) Cage "conceived (but did not actually 'compose')" Reunion, per Cross; the concert ran 8:30 pm Tuesday 5 March 1968 to about 1:00 am Wednesday 6 March, and it was Duchamp's last public appearance. C3. "The silence after the Glass" misplaces the trigger. The Large Glass was 1915 to 1923, but Cage's own account names Rauschenberg's White Paintings of 1951 as what pushed him to write 4'33": "The white paintings came first; my silent piece came later." The Glass is a legitimate doorway image, Duchamp's delay, his silence, his chess, but the study does not present it as the cause of 4'33". The honest chain is: Glass, delay, silence, chess, to White Paintings, the empty surface that changes with the room, to 4'33", the empty duration that changes with the room. C4. 4'33" is not silence, and "the silence" in the doorway must be glossed on first use. The content of the piece is the ambient sound of the room; Cage's point, after the anechoic chamber, is that absolute silence does not exist. Calling it "four minutes and 33 seconds of silence" repeats the error Cage spent forty years correcting. Also, the famous 273-seconds-equals-absolute-zero theory is false: the duration was decided by chance. C5. "Cage invented the prepared piano" needs two qualifications. (a) The idea originated with Henry Cowell and his "string piano", plucking and scraping inside the piano, for example The Banshee; Cage devised the prepared piano for Bacchanale, inspired by Cowell. "Coined the term and made it famous" is accurate; "invented ex nihilo" is not. (b) The date is disputed: Cage said in 1974 and 1982 interviews that Bacchanale was composed in 1938; the manuscript used for the Edition Peters edition says 1940, and most scholars use 1940. The study dates it "1940 (Cage sometimes said 1938)" and does not assert a single year as settled. C6. 4'33" movement lengths differ between the premiere and the published score. Premiere, Friday 29 August 1952: 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, 1 minute 40 seconds. The 1961 published score: 33 seconds, 2 minutes 40 seconds, 1 minute 20 seconds. Both total 4'33"; the digits are the same, rearranged. The original manuscript is lost; Tudor reconstructed it from memory twice in 1989 and the two reconstructions differ. Also disputed: whether Tudor closed the lid to begin a movement and opened it to end it, Tudor's account, or opened it to begin and closed it to end it, Carolyn Brown and others present. The study does not describe the lid choreography as settled fact. C7. Cage was not the first to compose silence, and he said he did not know the precedents. Alphonse Allais, Funeral March for the Obsequies of a Deaf Man, 1897, 24 empty measures; Erwin Schulhoff, In Futurum, 1919, a movement of rests; Yves Klein, Monotone-Silence Symphony, 1949, two twenty-minute silent movements. Cage wrote that he was not aware of Allais at the time. The study does not call 4'33" the first silent piece. C8. "Cage discovered chance in 1950", as some summaries have it, is a year early. Chance operations began in 1951: Wolff gave Cage the I Ching in early 1951; the third movement of the Concerto for Prepared Piano, 1950 to 1951, is the first use, and Music of Changes and Imaginary Landscape No. 4 are the first fully chance-based works. Also note the I Ching gift: the translation was published by Wolff's father Kurt Wolff at Pantheon Books in 1950; Wolff gave it to Cage in early 1951. C9. "Cage met Tudor through Feldman" is the standard account and is now doubtful. Wikipedia note 3: recent research, Kyle Gann, 2008, shows Cage may have met Tudor almost a decade earlier, in 1942, through Jean Erdman. The study says "Tudor, whom Cage knew from the early 1940s" or attributes the Feldman version. C10. Lotringer's date for Dreams That Money Can Buy is loose. He writes "(1946)"; the film was produced 1944 to 1946, released September 1947, world premiere New York 23 April 1948. Also, the doorway names the piece Cage wrote for it: Music for Marcel Duchamp, for prepared piano, for the "Discs" sequence. C11. Theatre Piece No. 1 was not called that at the time and was not certainly the "first happening". It was untitled; the name came later. The date is uncertain, early August 1952, best guess the 16th; only 35 to 50 people attended; accounts disagree on details, whether the White Paintings were projection screens, how long it lasted, whether a Franz Kline painting was present. The term "happening" was coined by Allan Kaprow in the late 1950s. The study calls it "later called the first Happening" and gives the August 1952 date without a day. C12. Williams Mix premiere date conflicts between sources. Wikipedia infobox: 23 March 1953. The Cage database of compositions, via Podbean transcript: 22 March 1953. Duration likewise: 4 minutes 15 seconds in most sources, 4 minutes 16 seconds in the Wikipedia infobox. The study uses "March 1953" and does not assert the 23rd as settled. C13. Imaginary Landscape No. 4 premiere date conflicts. Wikipedia: first performance 2 May 1951, McMillin Theater, Columbia. Revill, via the locusonus timeline: 10 May 1951. The Wikipedia infobox also lists "Performed: 1 March 1951, New York", which agrees with neither. The study uses "May 1951" and does not assert a day. C14. The Number Pieces count conflicts. Wikipedia: "some forty". The Art Story: 52. The study says "about forty" and does not assert 52. Similarly The Art Story dates them to "the final six years", 1987 to 1992 is five years and some months; Wikipedia says "the last five years". C15. HPSCHD numbers vary by source. Audience: 6,000, Wikipedia and Sousa Archives, against about 8,000, Hiller biography. Tapes: 52 computer-generated tapes, played as 208 tape copies on 52 machines, Wikipedia; Britannica says "51 tape machines". Slides: 6,400, 5,000 from NASA. The study uses the Wikipedia and Sousa figures and flags the audience discrepancy. C16. The Art Story misdates the Indian influence on Sonatas and Interludes. It says the cycle was "heavily influenced by Cage's studies of Indian music and philosophy in the early 1950s", but the work is 1946 to 1948 and the Sarabhai study began in early 1946. The influence is late-1940s, not early 1950s. Also The Art Story's claim that the work was "dedicated to Maro Ajemian" is single-sourced in this leg; Ajemian premiered it at Carnegie Hall in 1949, which is agreed, but the dedication is not asserted here. C17. "Duchamp gave up art for chess" is lore, not fact, and the doorway does not repeat it flatly. Duchamp's studio was empty because he worked in a second, secret space on Etant donnes, 1946 to 1966, his last project. Cage knew this. The honest phrasing: he withdrew from public art-making and played chess in public while building Etant donnes in secret. C18. Cage's birth and Erratum Musical are a year apart, not the same moment. Cage was born Thursday 5 September 1912; Erratum Musical is 1913, first performed, per one source, at the family home in Rouen at New Year, 1913, which would put its performance days after Cage turned four months old. Cage's question, "How is it that you used chance operations when I was just being born?", is his own loose, poetic phrasing, not a chronology; it is quoted as a quotation, not as a date. Note also the tension in the sources: "devised in 1913" against "first performed at New Year, 1913" implies composition in 1912; the study treats 1913 as the conventional date and flags the performance claim as single-sourced. C19. Cage did not meet Duchamp in 1942 and become close at once. They met in the 1940s, Wikipedia: New York, 1942, via Ernst and Guggenheim, but became close only twenty years later, when Cage sought Duchamp's company as his health failed. The study does not compress the 1942 meeting and the 1965 to 1968 chess friendship into one encounter. C20. The I Ching method is not "randomization". Cage's procedures varied by piece and were usually complex: charts, questions, tables of aggregates, geometric patterns. Cheap Imitation alone required three distinct questions per phrase. The study describes the mechanism, M6, and does not call it "random". C21. Two small dating slips to avoid repeating: (a) Britannica's FAQ says Cage "began to experiment" with the prepared piano "starting about 1939", but 1939 is not supported by the other sources, 1938 or 1940; (b) one visual-arts source says Cage "invented" the prepared piano "in 1938" and that he began using chance "in 1950", both are the early side of disputed dates, see C5 and C8.
