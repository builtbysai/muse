# Giovanna Fratellini: Study Notes

**Site:** Giovanna Marmocchini Cortesi Fratellini (Wednesday 27 October 1666 - Wednesday 18 April 1731), Florentine pastellist -- born in Florence, daughter of Giovanni Marmocchini Cortesi and Petronilla Beccatelli; presented as a girl by her uncle to the court of Vittoria della Rovere, Grand Duchess of Tuscany, where she was educated and served as lady-in-waiting; trained in miniature with the Capuchin Ippolito Galantini (1627-1706), in drawing and oil with Anton Domenico Gabbiani, and in pastel with Domenico Tempesti (c.1655-1737); married Giuliano Fratellini in 1684, aged 18; accepted into the Accademia del disegno in 1706 and made a full member in 1710; named official portraitist to Violante Beatrice of Bavaria, Grand Princess of Tuscany and governor of Siena, in 1717, completing over twenty pastels and miniatures for her between 1717 and 1722; sent by Violante to Bologna in 1719 to portray the exiled James Francis Edward Stuart and his wife Maria Klementyna Sobieska, then to Venice, where she met Rosalba Carriera; commissioned again by James in Rome in 1728-29 for oils and pastel repetitions of the Stuart family; taught her son Lorenzo Maria Fratellini (1690-1729) and her pupils Violante Beatrice Siries and Maria Maddalena Baldacci; died in Florence on Wednesday 18 April 1731, aged 64, and was buried in the church of Ognissanti, near her son. Neil Jeffares's Dictionary of Pastellists entry (J.326, updated 6 September 2026) lists roughly 100 works, most of them pastels on paper in the Uffizi portrait collection (inv. 1890) and at Villa La Petraia; the signature works are the Self-Portrait of 1720 (pastel, 72 x 57 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 2064), the Violante of Bavaria of 1720 (Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 2588), the Clemens August of 1718 (12 scudi, from life) and the Johann Theodor of 1722 (15 scudi, from life, with hands), the Cecilia Pazzi child-lady portrait, and the Faustina Bordoni of c.1723. The modern record is Jeffares's catalogue, Jane Fortune's Invisible Women (2009) and the scholarship of Mario Monteverdi (1981) and Silvia Meloni Trkulja (1989). This file is plain ASCII by house rule, so the Italian words in it are written without their accents here. The birth day, 27 October, rests on the Italian Wikipedia and Davis Publications; Jeffares and the English Wikipedia give the year only (see C4).

## M1. The Miniature First -- Small Before Large, Ivory Before Paper

She learned pastel second, and the first medium set the terms of the second. The training order is the same in every source that gives it: miniature painting with the Capuchin Ippolito Galantini (1627-1706), court miniaturist; drawing and oil with Anton Domenico Gabbiani; then refinement in pastel with Domenico Tempesti (c.1655-1737). The miniature years are the phase with no surviving dated object -- no firmly dated work before 1713 was verified in any source read -- but the biography is explicit about what the phase produced: miniatures, enamels and small portraits set with precious stones or enclosed in medallions, given as gifts to court ladies and illustrious guests. The one candidate object is the enamelled portrait that Marvin Chauncey Ross wrote up in the Burlington Magazine in November 1947, known only from that citation, its subject and present location unverified. What the miniature taught her, and what the pastel kept, is the small scale as a discipline: a face the size of a thumb, worked with the same care as a state portrait, and a support -- ivory, enamel -- that does part of the work. Her pastel portraits never get large; the biggest, the Self-Portrait of 1720, is 72 x 57 cm, and the court-lady standard is about 55 x 43 cm. The watchable behaviour is the hand that learned on ivory and never unlearned it: a large pastel that still behaves like a miniature, the face finished to the same small tolerance, the picture small enough to be held and given. **Takeaway:** miniature first, pastel second -- the enamel and ivory years are the training, the pastel is the same hand at a slightly larger scale, and the smallness is the signature, not a limitation.

## M2. Pastel on Paper, the Court-Lady Format -- One Size, Repeated for a Decade

Her mature work is a format, and the format is the product. Jeffares's catalogue notation is the same for entry after entry: "pstl/ppr", pastel on paper, bust length, about 55 x 43 cm, often oval, with Uffizi inventory numbers in the 1890 series and Villa Lappeggi or Villa La Petraia provenance. The court-lady series -- Marchesa Bentivogli Tempi (1720, 65 x 51 cm), Contessa Bolognetti (1718, 55 x 43 cm), Marchesa Corsini (c.1720, 54 x 43.5 cm), Eleonora Luisa Gonzaga (1715, 63 x 52 cm), and two dozen more of the same shape -- is one portrait repeated with different names attached: the same bust, the same soft high-key colour, the same idealised face, the same fluttering ribbons. The variation is in the sitter's name and the dress, not in the picture. The larger exceptions prove the rule: the Self-Portrait at 72 x 57 cm, the Clemens August at 63 x 52 cm, the Johann Theodor at 63 x 50 cm -- all of them men, or herself, and all of them priced differently. No binder, ground colour, fixative or working order is documented anywhere in the sources read, and this study claims none; the honest record is medium plus support plus size. The watchable behaviour is the series as a system: a row of near-identical ovals, each one a different Florentine noblewoman, each one finished to the same soft standard, so the court itself becomes a set of matching pictures. **Takeaway:** pastel on paper, about 55 x 43 cm, repeated for a decade -- the court-lady portrait is a format, and the format, not any single sitter, is the work.

## M3. The Invoice -- Paid by the Portrait, at Set Rates, in Her Own Name

She was a professional in the literal sense: she billed, and she billed in her own name. The invoices survive in Jeffares and in the Stuart papers, and they give a price list, not a mystery. Twelve scudi for a Violante commission (6 October 1720). Twelve scudi for the Bishop of Munster portrait, from life, in pastel (20 June 1718). Fifteen scudi for the Bishop of Ratisbon portrait, from life, with hands (10 November 1722) -- the hands cost extra, which is how the 15-scudi figure should be read: the top rate, for more work. Eight luigi each for the five Bologna Stuart portraits. Twenty pistoles each for the Stuart oils of 1728. And the itemised bill of 11 February 1729, for a month and a half's work in Bologna: five crystals at a luigi each (paoli 130), five black pine cases (paoli 35), five fine canvases (paoli 15), packing (paoli 4.6), total paoli 239 -- the crystals and the cases and the packing on the same bill as the pictures, because the picture does not travel without them. The Stuart papers add the sting: James III was disappointed with both the oils and the pastels, and still ordered the repetitions paid "at the rate of original ones" (letter of 19 February 1729) -- a dissatisfied royal patron paying full price because the invoice was the contract. Fortune's line, quoted in the search results, puts her with Siries and Maria Hadfield Cosway as "three women artists who issued invoices in their own names and were accepted as members of the city's historic drawing academy". No regular court salary figure was found; do not invent one. The watchable behaviour is the bill itself: a price per face, a surcharge for hands, crystals and pine cases itemised beside the portraits, and a king paying the full rate for work he did not like -- the portrait as a priced unit, and the woman as the one who sets the price. **Takeaway:** up to 15 scudi a pastel, 20 pistoles an oil, 8 luigi a Stuart portrait, crystals and packing on the bill -- she invoiced by the portrait in her own name, and even a disappointed king paid the rate.

## M4. The Self-Portrait as the Exception -- Flattered Sitters, a Plain Self

She flattered everyone except herself, and the exception is the picture that matters. Davis states the rule plainly: "as the official portrait painter of the Medici court in Florence, she was obliged to flatter the members of the nobility to acquire commissions" -- and the court-lady portraits are idealised accordingly, soft high-key faces with the flaws removed. The Self-Portrait of 1720 (pastel on paper, 72 x 57 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 2064) breaks the rule in both directions at once. It is unidealised: "Fratellini's plain features and thin lips are a far cry from the extreme idealization... of the upper class Italians whom she painted" (Davis), and the portraits were "realistic, but not naturalistic" while the self-portraits were "unflinchingly naturalistic". And it is a deliberate display of range inside a single image: she shows herself at work holding oil paints and painting a miniature portrait -- said to be her son Lorenzo -- while the work itself is pastel. The pastellist presents herself as an oil painter and a miniaturist as well, in a medium that is neither. The self-portrait was itself a repeatable court commodity: an autograph replica, signed on the verso "Giovanna Fratellini faceva l'anno 1720", 63 x 50 cm, now in a Swiss private collection, sold at Bern in 2012 and 2018. The Italian Wikipedia says she made several self-portraits, but no other autograph one is identified in Jeffares, and the "Autoritratto, Florence 1737" in Jeffares (J.326.106) is dated six years after her death and is a later copy, not hers. The watchable behaviour is the double portrait in one frame: the plain face at work, and the tiny miniature inside it -- the flattered world outside the picture, the honest face inside it, and the medium pretending to be two other media at once. **Takeaway:** the sitters are flattered, the self is plain -- the 1720 self-portrait is the exception that proves the court rule, a pastel showing its maker holding oil paints and painting a miniature.

## M5. The Ribbon -- Fluttering Ribbons as the Signature

In a body of work bound by court likeness, one motif marks the hand. The Web Gallery of Art, on the Self-Portrait, notes the "fluttering ribbons she is jauntily wearing"; the English Wikipedia calls them her "signature elements, fluttering ribbons, spryly worn". They recur across the court-lady portraits -- a ribbon at the throat, at the shoulder, in the hair -- a small, lively, uncommissioned flourish inside pictures that are otherwise formulaic. Nothing in the record explains them: no sitter asked for ribbons, no patron paid for them, no treatise names them. They are hers the way a painter's handwriting is hers, the one place in the standardised format where the painter, not the court, decides. The watchable behaviour is the ribbon moving while the face stays still: the sitter held in the formal oval, the ribbon fluttering at the edge of it, as if a draught had got into the portrait room -- the liveliness the court portrait is not supposed to have, smuggled in as a piece of dress. **Takeaway:** fluttering ribbons, spryly worn, in picture after picture -- the one motif the court did not commission, and the signature that tells the hand in a body of matching ovals.

## M6. The Child Lady -- Children Painted as Small Adults

Her most disquieting series paints children as courtiers, not as children. The type-piece is Cecilia Pazzi, a young girl holding a cat, pastel, 55 x 42 cm, Villa di Lappeggi (Jeffares J.326.179) -- dated 1720 by Fortune via the English Wikipedia, though Jeffares gives no date, so the date carries that caveat. The girl is shown in an adult pose with adult features, in adult dress, holding the cat the way a grown sitter holds an attribute. The English Wikipedia, citing Fortune, names the series: portraits of young noble girls in adult poses and with adult features. The format is the same as the court ladies -- the same 55 x 43 cm bust, the same soft high-key pastel -- so the child enters the series on the same terms as the marchesa, and the picture does not acknowledge the difference. The watchable behaviour is the mismatch inside the sameness: a small face in a grown woman's pose, the cat as the only concession to childhood, the portrait treating a girl as if she were already a lady of the court -- because at the Medici court, in the commission's terms, she already was. **Takeaway:** the child lady -- a girl in an adult pose with adult features, in the court-lady format, the cat the only sign she is a child.

## M7. The Copy and the Repetition -- Pastel as the Second Version

She copied, and she repeated, and the repetition was a paid service, not a failure of invention. The documented copy after a master is the Holy Family after Annibale Carracci, seen by Edward Wright at Pistoia in 1720-22 and praised in print in 1730 (Jeffares J.326.305) -- the copy is what brought Wright's judgement, that she "comes nearest to Rosa Alba of Venice for Miniature, and I think does at least equal her for Crayons in large". She copied after Gabbiani's paintings too, her teacher's work repeated in her hand. The Stuart commissions make the mechanism commercial: in 1728 James III had her paint himself and his wife first in oils at 20 pistoles each, was disappointed, and commissioned repetitions in pastel plus pastels of his sons -- the pastel as the second version of the oil, at the same rate. The self-portrait was repeated the same way: the autograph 1720 replica, signed and dated on the verso, sold twice at Bern. And the copies outlived her: a posthumous copy after the self-portrait by I. Hugford, Florence 1767 (Jeffares J.326.272), and the 1737 "Autoritratto" that is not hers at all. The watchable behaviour is the second version arriving after the first: the oil first, the pastel after it, the same face done again at full price -- the portrait as a repeatable object, and the copy as the business. **Takeaway:** oil first, pastel as the repetition; a master's Holy Family copied, a self-portrait signed twice -- the copy and the repeat are the paid work, not the sketch for it.

## M8. The Lost History -- Baccanals and a Lucretia That Do Not Survive

She began with history, and the history is gone. Before 1713, Prince Ferdinando de' Medici commissioned history and mythology in pastel: the Two Baccanali, the Death of Lucretia, the Judgement of Paris, Several Venuses, the Magdalene in the desert, heads of Bacchantes, other stories and fables (Jeffares J.326.273-279). These were large, multi-figure, ambitious subjects -- pastel used as a history-painting medium, not just for heads. All are untraced. What survives of them is a single sentence of praise from her first biographer, Francesco Moucke (1762), who says the two Baccanali were "tenuti in gran pregio" -- held in great esteem. The religious paintings for Cosimo III are recorded the same way, by Moucke, with no individual work named in any source read. After 1717 the history subjects stop: the catalogue becomes portraits, almost exclusively, for fourteen years. The ambition did not fail in public; it simply left no object behind, and the portraiture paid. The watchable behaviour is the absence itself: a catalogue of a hundred entries with a hole at the start where the history was, the most praised pictures the ones nobody can see -- the career remembered for the portraits because the histories did not survive to argue back. **Takeaway:** the Baccanali and the Lucretia, praised by Moucke and untraced -- history in pastel, attempted before 1713 and then abandoned, surviving only as a biographer's sentence.

## M9. The School -- Women Teaching Women, in a Male Academy World

Her afterlife is a teaching chain, and the chain is female. Jeffares: "She taught Violante Beatrice Siries, as well as her son Lorenzo." Siries (1709-1783), named after the patroness Violante, succeeded Fratellini as official court portraitist after her death -- Paris 1726 under Rigaud and Boucher, Rome 1734, then the court post, then teacher in her turn of Anna Bacherini Piattoli and Maria Hadfield Cosway. Maria Maddalena Gozzi Baldacci (1718-1782) began at Fratellini's school, then moved to Campiglia's workshop, and painted miniatures in pastel and watercolour in soft, delicate colours. Fortune's line, quoted in the search results, names the succession plainly: "teacher-student succession of Giovanna Fratellini, Violante Siries Cerroti, and Anna Bacherini Piattoli" -- and "The trio represents a unique example of women artists teaching or being taught by other women." The teaching happened inside the court and the household, not in the academy: she taught with her son Lorenzo beside her, the mother and son and pupil working in the same room, and the self-portrait shows her painting Lorenzo's miniature. The Villa La Petraia fancy heads -- peasant man, peasant girl, girl with flowers -- are attributed "Fratellini or Piattoli" throughout, so the workshop idiom outlived her and the attributions blur, which is itself the evidence: the school kept painting in her manner after she was gone. The watchable behaviour is the chain, not the single picture: a pupil taking the court post, teaching the next pupil, the manner passing from hand to hand while the academy admits the women one at a time -- the school as the work that outlives the painter. **Takeaway:** Fratellini to Siries to Piattoli to Cosway -- a female teaching chain inside a male academy world, the court post handed down like an inheritance, and the Petraia heads blurred between master and pupil because the school kept painting.

## M10. The Travelling Portraitist -- Bologna, Venice, Rome, and the Picture That Ships

She travelled on commission, and the portraits travelled further than she did. Around 1719 Violante sent her to Bologna to make likenesses in oil and pastel of the exiled James Francis Edward Stuart, his wife Maria Klementyna Sobieska, and their sons. From Bologna she travelled to Venice to portray Teresa Kunigunda Sobieska, Maria Klementyna's aunt -- and in Venice she met Rosalba Carriera. In 1728 James, then in Bologna and dissatisfied with Torelli, invited her to make a series of portraits to replace the Meytens models; the oils disappointed him, the pastel repetitions followed, and in January 1729 the five pictures were billed and paid for. Then the pictures shipped: four pastels went to Admiral Thomas Gordon, commander-in-chief of the Russian Baltic fleet; a repetition of Prince Charles went to Bishop Atterbury in France. All of the 1728-29 Stuart works are lost; they survive only as receipts and correspondence in the Stuart papers. The bill of 11 February 1729 is the mechanism in miniature: five crystals at a luigi each, five black pine cases, five fine canvases, packing -- the portrait as a parcel, the case and the crystal on the same invoice as the face. The watchable behaviour is the portrait leaving: the sitter painted from life in Bologna, the pastel packed in a pine case with a crystal over it, the case travelling to a Russian admiral in the Baltic or a bishop in France -- the court portrait as an export, and the painter as the one who goes to the sitter because the sitter cannot come to Florence. **Takeaway:** Bologna 1719, Venice, Rome 1728-29 -- she went to the sitters, and the pictures went further, in pine cases with crystals, to a Baltic admiral and a French bishop; the Stuart portraits are lost, and the receipts are the work that remains.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Miniature training and court education, c.1680s-1705. Presented as a child by her uncle to the court of Vittoria della Rovere, Grand Duchess of Tuscany; educated at the Medici court in miniature painting (Galantini), drawing and oil (Gabbiani) and pastel (Tempesti). Married Giuliano Fratellini in 1684 (Jeffares and the Italian Wikipedia; the English Wikipedia says 1685 -- see C3); son Lorenzo Maria born Saturday 14 January 1690. Work of this phase: miniatures, enamels and small portraits set in jewels or medallions as court gifts. No firmly dated surviving work from this phase was verified -- the miniature years are known from biography, not objects. The enamelled portrait is the only candidate object, and it is undated with unverified location.

Phase 2 -- Accademia and the Ferdinando commissions, 1706-c.1716. Accepted into the Accademia del disegno in 1706, full member 1710 -- a rare institutional honour for a woman. Religious paintings recorded for Cosimo III (no surviving named work). Prince Ferdinando commissions history and mythology in pastel before 1713: Baccanali, Lucrezia, Judgement of Paris, Venuses, Magdalene, Bacchante heads (Jeffares J.326.273-279). These were large, multi-figure, ambitious subjects -- pastel used as a history-painting medium, not just for heads. All are untraced. Fees up to 15 scudi per finished pastel portrait on paper. Portraiture already the economic base: dated portraits begin 1715 (Anna Guglielmini, Vernaccia, Suarez).

Phase 3 -- The Violante years: official court portraitist, 1717-1722. Violante Beatrix von Bayern, wife of Grand Prince Ferdinando, appoints her official portraitist. Between 1717 and 1722 she completes at least 20 pastels and miniatures on ivory for Violante; the Jeffares list of Uffizi inv. 1890 pastels is far longer, most undated. The product standardises: the circle of courtly ladies -- bust-length pastel portraits of Florentine noblewomen, mostly 55 x 43 to 65 x 52 cm, near-identical format, soft high-key colour, idealised faces, fluttering ribbons. The German connection deepens: Clemens August (1718, 12 scudi), Johann Theodor (1722, 15 scudi, hands included). The Self-Portrait of 1720 is the phase's summit and its deliberate exception: herself unidealised, at work, in pastel pretending to be oil. Travel: Bologna 1719 (Stuarts), Venice (Teresa Kunigunda; meets Carriera, who extends "gentilissime accoglienze"). Edward Wright, visiting Pistoia 1720-22, judges her the Florentine nearest to Carriera and "at least equal her for Crayons in large".

Phase 4 -- The Stuart commissions and late work, 1719/1728-1731. The Stuart thread runs alongside phase 3 and outlives it. 1719: James and Maria Klementyna in Bologna. 1728: James, dissatisfied with Torelli and wanting to replace the Meytens models, invites her to Rome; oil portraits of James and Klementyna at 20 pistoles each disappoint him; he commissions pastel repetitions and pastels of the sons. January 1729: five pictures billed (her own itemised bill survives in the Stuart papers -- five crystals at a luigi each, five pine cases, five fine canvases, packing; total paoli 239, 11 February 1729); James's letter of Saturday 19 February 1729 orders payment "for the five pictures she has lately done all at the rate of original ones". The sons' portraits go to Admiral Gordon (Baltic) and Bishop Atterbury (France). All of the 1728-29 Stuart works are lost; they survive only as receipts and correspondence in the Royal Archives. 1729: two female portraits for Marchese Acciaiuoli and two portraits for Cavaliere Guadagni -- the last dated commissions. Her son Lorenzo dies Wednesday 12 October 1729; she dies Wednesday 18 April 1731. Late work is repetition and copying: Stuart repetitions, self-portrait replicas, posthumous copies (Hugford 1767).

Phase 5 -- The school, during her life and after. She taught with her son Lorenzo: Violante Beatrice Siries (1709-1783), named after the patroness, took Fratellini's place as court painter after her death; Maria Maddalena Gozzi Baldacci (1718-1782) began in Fratellini's school. The Villa La Petraia fancy heads (peasant man, peasant girl, girls with flowers) are attributed "Fratellini or Piattoli" (Anna Bacherini Piattoli) -- the workshop idiom outlived her and the attributions blur.

What changed: scale (miniature and enamel to 72 x 57 cm pastel); subject (history and mythology ambition in 1713, then almost exclusively portraiture after 1717); geography (Florence only, then Bologna, Venice, Rome); clientele (Medici court to Jacobite exiles and German princes); the late turn to repetition and copies. What stayed the same: pastel on paper as the core medium; the bust portrait as the unit; the court-lady format (about 55 x 43 cm) repeated for a decade; the economics of the invoice (12-15 scudi, 20 pistoles); the idealised sitter against the unidealised self; the ribbon motif.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is the court as a format, and the portrait as a priced unit inside it. She learned small -- miniature on ivory, enamel in a jewel -- and the pastel never outgrew the miniature's discipline: the face finished to a small tolerance, the picture small enough to be held, given, packed in a pine case with a crystal over it. The court gave her the sitters and the format at once: one bust, about 55 x 43 cm, repeated for a decade with different names, the faces idealised because flattery was the duty of the office, the ribbons fluttering because the painter needed one place of her own inside the formula. The self-portrait is the exception that proves the rule -- the plain face, the oil paints in a pastel's hand, the miniature of her son inside the picture -- and the invoice is the other exception, the place where the woman, not the court, sets the terms: up to 15 scudi, hands extra, crystals and packing itemised, even a disappointed king paying the full rate. The history pictures, the most praised work of her early life, did not survive, so the career is remembered for the portraits; the school is remembered instead for the chain, Fratellini to Siries to Piattoli, because no single masterpiece carries her name. What she proved, a generation before Carriera carried pastel to Paris, is that pastel could be a court medium -- finished, priced, exhibited every St Luke's day at SS Annunziata beside Tempesti's, and taught from woman to woman inside a male academy world. The recovery came late and from outside: Greer's research file, Fortune's book and documentary, Jeffares's catalogue -- scholars looking for women, not the market re-valuing her pictures. The work sits now mostly in Uffizi deposits awaiting restoration and at Villa La Petraia, which is the honest image of the reputation: present, catalogued, and still largely unseen.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] She was a court artist, not a market artist. Her whole career runs inside the Medici court: presented to Vittoria della Rovere by her uncle, educated at the grand duchess's court, lady-in-waiting, then commissions from Cosimo III, Ferdinando and Violante, and Accademia membership in 1706/1710 (Jeffares). The court's judgement could be harsh: James Stuart found both her oils and her pastels wanting in 1728-29 (Jeffares, Stuart papers). Evidence: leg 3.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] She was trained by professionals, not by family. Davis: "Unlike most women artists, Giovanna Fratellini was trained by a professional artist who was not her father or a male relative." Her teachers were Mehus, Pietro Dandini, Galantini, Tempesti and Gabbiani (Jeffares) -- none of them a relative. Evidence: leg 3.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] Pastel was her second medium, after miniature. Jeffares: she learned "miniature painting and pastel with Anton Domenico Gabbiani, Padre Galantini and Domenico Tempesti" -- miniature with Galantini first, pastel with Tempesti after. Evidence: leg 3.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] She worked in four media, not one. Jeffares: she "worked in oil, pastel, miniature, enamel and chalk". Pastel is the medium she is remembered for, but the record shows a multi-media practice. Evidence: leg 3.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] Portrait was the means of her success; history and religious painting were secondary. The catalogue is dominated by portraits (Jeffares J.326.101-166, overwhelmingly sitters); the religious paintings for Cosimo III and the history/mythology pastels for Ferdinando are recorded but few, and the two Baccanali survive only as a biographer's praise (Moucke, via Jeffares). Evidence: leg 3.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] She invoiced by the portrait, at set rates. Jeffares and the Stuart papers: 12 scudi for a Violante commission of 6 October 1720; 12 scudi for the Bishop of Munster portrait, 20 June 1718; 15 scudi for the Bishop of Ratisbon, 10 November 1722; 20 pistoles each for the Stuart oils; her Bologna bill itemises 5 crystals, 5 black-pine cases, 5 fine canvases and packing, totalling paoli 239, for a month and a half's work at 8 luigi each. Evidence: leg 3.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] She invoiced in her own name and held Academy membership: a professional, not an amateur. Fortune: Fratellini, Siries and Cosway "are three women artists who issued invoices in their own names and were accepted as members of the city's historic drawing academy." Accademia del disegno 1706, full member 1710 (Jeffares). Even a dissatisfied royal patron paid her repetitions "at the rate of original ones": James Stuart's letter of 19 February 1729 (see Q4). Evidence: leg 3.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] The court-ladies circle was a commission, not a social round. Jeffares: she was "commissioned by Cosimo III, Ferdinando de' Medici and Violanta Beatrix von Bayern to portray many of the ladies at court, giving to her pieces 'all the tenderness and warmth of life' (Pilkington)". The series -- including the "child lady" portraits such as Cecilia Pazzi (1720) -- was Violante's project, executed by Fratellini. Evidence: leg 3.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] She flattered her sitters and painted herself plainly. Davis: "as the official portrait painter of the Medici court in Florence, she was obliged to flatter the members of the nobility to acquire commissions"; but of the self-portrait, "they were unflinchingly naturalistic" and "Fratellini's plain features and thin lips are a far cry from the extreme idealization... of the upper class Italians whom she painted." Evidence: leg 3.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] The self-portrait (1720, pastel, Uffizi, 72 x 57 cm) shows her holding oil paints and working on a miniature of her son Lorenzo -- while the work itself is a pastel. A display of range inside a single image: the pastellist presenting herself as an oil painter and miniaturist as well (Jeffares catalogue J.326.101; Wikipedia). Evidence: leg 3.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] Fluttering ribbons are her signature device. "Fratellini's signature elements, fluttering ribbons, are spryly worn" (source in search results). The ribbons recur across the court-lady portraits and mark the hand in a body of work otherwise bound by court likeness. Evidence: leg 3.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] Her "child lady" series paints children as small adults. The series includes Cecilia Pazzi (1720) and other children of the court presented in adult dress and adult pose -- the child as a small courtier, not as a child (search results; Davis). Evidence: leg 3.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] She travelled on commission: Bologna 1719, then Venice. Jeffares: "Around 1719 she was sent to Bologna by Violante Beatrix to make likenesses in oil and pastel of the exiled James Stuart, his wife Maria Klementyna Sobieska. From there she travelled to Venice to portray Violanta Beatrix's sister-in-law Teresa Kunigunda Sobieska." She spent a month and a half in Bologna working from life (Stuart papers, via Jeffares). Evidence: leg 3.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] The Venice meeting put the two pastel schools face to face. In Venice she met Rosalba Carriera, who extended "gentilissime accoglienze" (Jeffares). Edward Wright, writing in 1730, judged her the nearest to Carriera in miniature and at least her equal "for Crayons in large" (see Q2). The Florentine and the Venetian pastel traditions met in person, not just in reputation. Evidence: leg 3.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] She taught women, and her teaching made a school. Jeffares: "She taught Violante Beatrice Siries, as well as her son Lorenzo." Baldacci studied at her school. Siries then taught Piattoli and Cosway, and Piattoli's line runs on -- a female teaching chain inside a male academy world (Fortune). Evidence: leg 3.
- P16. [FROM RECORD] She taught alongside her son Lorenzo: a family workshop. Jeffares says she taught Siries "as well as her son Lorenzo", and the self-portrait shows her painting Lorenzo's miniature -- mother, son and pupil working in the same room, the workshop as a household (Jeffares). Evidence: leg 3.
- P17. [FROM RECORD] Her works survive mainly in Uffizi deposits and at Villa Petraia, many unrestored. Villa Petraia holds the best-known pastels; many works sit in Uffizi deposits pending restoration (search results; Jeffares catalogue entries repeatedly cite Uffizi inv. 1890 numbers and Villa Lappeggi/Petraia provenance). The survival is institutional storage, not continuous display. Evidence: leg 3.
- P18. [REPORTED] "Rosalba of Florence" -- the nickname was given in her lifetime. Vittoria della Rovere is reported to have called her "la Rosalba Carriera fiorentina" -- confirmed first-hand by the parent in the Italian Wikipedia's Baldacci article; see C18 for the sourcing, and Q8 for the exact wording. Evidence: leg 1; parent.
- P19. [FROM RECORD] Realistic but not naturalistic -- except in self-portraiture. Davis: "Like the portraits of Rosalba Carriera, Fratellini's were realistic, but not naturalistic (following nature precisely)" -- the court portrait follows the sitter's rank, not the sitter's face; the self-portrait is the exception, "unflinchingly naturalistic". Evidence: leg 3.
- P20. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] She is remembered through succession, not through a single masterpiece. No one work of hers carries her reputation; what carries it is the chain Fortune names -- Fratellini to Siries to Piattoli (and on to Cosway) -- and the archival recovery of that chain. The inference is drawn from the shape of the modern literature (Fortune 2009; Greer 2001), not from any 18th-century claim. Evidence: leg 3.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C1. "15 SCUDI PER PASTEL PORTRAIT" IS A CEILING, NOT A FLAT RATE -- AND #508'S TOP-PICK LINE IS WRONG IN THE FLAT FORM. The documented figure is "up to 15 scudi" per completed pastel portrait. Jeffares's invoices show a range: 12 scudi for a Violante commission (6 October 1720), 12 scudi for the Bishop of Munster portrait from life (20 June 1718), 15 scudi for the Bishop of Ratisbon portrait from life with hands (10 November 1722), 8 luigi each for the Bologna Stuart portraits, 20 pistoles each for the Stuart oils. VERDICT: say "up to 15 scudi", and cite the 15-scudi Ratisbon invoice (with hands) as the top documented rate. Do NOT say "15 scudi per pastel portrait" without "up to".
- C2. VIOLANTE WAS NEVER GRAND DUCHESS, AND THERE WAS NO "FERDINANDO III". Violante Beatrice of Bavaria (1673-1731) was the widow of Grand Prince Ferdinando de' Medici (1663-1713), who never reigned: Cosimo III reigned until 1723, then Gian Gastone. Her correct titles are Grand Princess of Tuscany and governor of Siena. The Davis Publications blog post (27 October 2025) calls her husband "Ferdinando III de' Medici (1663-1713)" and implies a childhood introduction at the court of Ferdinando II (1610-1670), which is shaky: Fratellini was born in 1666 and Ferdinando II died in 1670. VERDICT: Grand Princess, governor of Siena, widow of Grand Prince Ferdinando -- never "Grand Duchess", never "Ferdinando III".
- C3. MARRIAGE YEAR -- 1684 OR 1685. Jeffares and the Italian Wikipedia say 1684 ("aged 18"); the English Wikipedia says 1685. VERDICT: use 1684, with the English Wikipedia's 1685 flagged as the error; 1666 + 18 = 1684.
- C4. BIRTH DATE -- 27 OCTOBER 1666, OR YEAR ONLY. The exact day rests on the Italian Wikipedia and Davis Publications; Jeffares and the English Wikipedia give the year only. VERDICT: "27 October 1666" with the Italian Wikipedia as the source; do not present the day as Jeffares-attested.
- C5. SURNAME SPELLING -- "MARMOCCHINI", NOT "MARRMOCCHINI" OR "MARMochini". Jeffares and the Italian Wikipedia spell it "Marmocchini"; the English Wikipedia has "Marrmocchini" (double r), propagated to mirrors such as Alchetron and Hellenica World; the Art Newspaper review writes "Marmochini". VERDICT: use "Marmocchini".
- C6. WHO INTRODUCED HER TO COURT -- UNCLE, NOT AUNT. Jeffares ("Presented by her uncle") and the Italian Wikipedia ("introdotta dallo zio") say uncle; Davis and the Royal Collection text as quoted in palianshow say aunt. VERDICT: say "uncle" (the two stronger sources) and footnote the aunt variant; do not state "aunt" as fact.
- C7. MEHUS AND DANDINI AS EARLY TEACHERS -- ENGLISH WIKIPEDIA ONLY, UNVERIFIED. The English Wikipedia alone adds that before the ducal training she trained with Livio Mehus and Pietro Dandini (citing Fortune and WGA). Jeffares and the Italian Wikipedia do not mention them. VERDICT: treat as unverified; do not build a mechanism or a prompt on it.
- C8. ROSALBA CARRIERA'S DATES -- 1673-1757, NOT 1675-1757. The English Fratellini article gives Carriera as 1675-1757 in one passage; the correct dates are 12 January 1673 - 15 April 1757 (per study #508). 1675 is the birth year of Rosalba's sister Giovanna Carriera (1675-1737). VERDICT: use 1673-1757 everywhere, including in this study's network (leg 3's N17 gives 1673/1675-1757).
- C9. AGE AT DEATH -- 64, NOT 65. 27 October 1666 to 18 April 1731 is 64 years and about 6 months, so "65" is loose (65th year). VERDICT: use "aged 64" or "64-65".
- C10. VITTORIA DELLA ROVERE'S DEATH -- 5 MARCH 1694, NOT 1695. The English and Italian Wikipedias give 5 March 1694 (Friday, verified); one dealer page (Flavio Gianassi) gives "1622-1695". VERDICT: 5 March 1694; the 1695 is a typo.
- C11. RANKING CLAIMS ARE ATTRIBUTED OPINIONS, NOT A SINGLE RANKING. The Web Gallery of Art calls her "the second-greatest pastelist in Florence -- after her teacher, Domenico Tempesti", while Wright (1730) says she at least equals Carriera "for Crayons in large". These are attributed opinions from different centuries; keep them attributed, do not merge into a single ranking. VERDICT: attribute every ranking, never state one as the study's own.
- C12. SELF-PORTRAIT DISPLAY -- STATE THE COLLECTION, NOT THE DISPLAY. The English Wikipedia says the 1720 Self-Portrait "is displayed in the Vasari Corridor"; Commons still lists the current location as Vasari Corridor. The corridor has been closed to the public since the 1930s. VERDICT: state the collection (Uffizi, inv. 1890 no. 2064), not current display.
- C13. THE POSTHUMOUS "SELF-PORTRAIT" IS NOT HERS. Jeffares J.326.106, "AUTORITRATTO, Florence 1737 (A. Peronsi)", is dated six years after her death: a later copy, not a self-portrait by her. The related J.326.272 (Hugford, Florence 1767) is explicitly a posthumous copy. VERDICT: do not count either among her self-portraits.
- C14. GALANTINI'S MISSION -- GEORGIA OR INDIA, UNRESOLVED. Jeffares says Georgia, 1665; Wikipedia says India. VERDICT: do not cite either as settled.
- C15. TERESA KUNIGUNDA SOBIESKA'S DEATH -- 1730 OR 1739. Wilanow gives 10 March 1730, and Jeffares gives 1676-1730; Google Arts and Culture gives 27 March 1739. VERDICT: follow Jeffares (1730); treat 1739 as an error.

The 1720 date and the "child lady" reading come from Fortune 2009 via the English Wikipedia; Jeffares J.326.179 gives no date, lists the work as Villa di Lappeggi, pastel 55 x 42 cm, Berti 1979 n.r. The portrait of Violante "at the age of 30", c.1720, in the Dressing Room (sala da toeletta) of Villa La Petraia is attested only by the Italian Wikipedia image caption and the palianshow blog; no Jeffares J-number could be pinned to it. VERDICT: do not cite it as a Jeffares work.

The Villa La Petraia fancy heads (J.326.231-243, 249-253, 259) carry the attribution "Fratellini or Piattoli" throughout, several marked "attr." only; J.326.238 was deleted from Jeffares in 2024 as looking modern. - C22. NO DOCUMENTED BINDER, GROUND, FIXATIVE OR WORKING ORDER. A targeted search on pastel technique, ground and fixative returned only unrelated Cennino Cennini texts and a V and A miniature page. No source found gives her pastel binder, paper ground or colour, fixative, crayon recipe, or step-by-step working order. VERDICT: this study claims none of these; the honest record is pastel on paper, c. 55 x 43 cm, often oval, plus miniatures on ivory, enamel, oil and chalk.
