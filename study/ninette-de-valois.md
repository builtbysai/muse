## 1. Baltiboys, the jig, and the name

Born Edris Stannus on 6 June 1898 at Baltiboys, near Blessington, County Wicklow -- the Dictionary of Irish Biography's spelling, preferred here; Wikipedia says "Baltyboys House", and the study does not pretend the spelling is settled. Her father was Lieutenant-Colonel Thomas R. A. Stannus DSO, a British Army officer; her mother Elizabeth Graydon Smith, known as "Lilith Stannus", was a glassmaker. She was the second daughter, with one sister and two younger brothers, and her maternal great-grandmother was the diarist Elizabeth Smith, whose Irish journals of 1840-1850 were written at Baltiboys itself. The house survived until 1940, when it was submerged by the new Poulaphouca reservoir at Blessington -- the childhood home drowned, the name kept. Her first dance, the sources agree, was an Irish jig, taught by the family cook, or by a herdsman's wife. She had undiagnosed childhood polio, which would later damage her dancing and end her performing career in her mid-twenties. In 1905, when she was seven, the family moved to England, to Walmer in Kent, the upkeep of Baltiboys having proved too expensive; she started ballet lessons in 1908, aged ten. Her father was killed in action in June 1917 while serving with the Leinster Regiment. The name she danced under was not the name she was born with: she became Ninette de Valois as a young dancer, and the sources cannot agree when -- the Dictionary of Irish Biography ties it to early 1913, before the Wonder Children tour; Encyclopedia.com says around 1917; Wikipedia puts it at thirteen, when she began training. The study carries the split: the name was adopted somewhere between 1911 and 1917, and the DIB's early-1913 dating, tied to a tour, is the most specific. [C34388]

**Takeaway:** the founder of English ballet was born Irish, named herself French, and lost her childhood home to a reservoir -- and the polio that would end her dancing was already in her before she ever took a ballet lesson. [C34389]

## 2. The Wonder Children and the Lyceum

At thirteen she began professional training at the Lila Field Academy for Children in London, whose alumni included Noel Coward and Micheal MacLiammoir. Early in 1913, after changing her name, she went on tour with a company of children from the school, "The Wonder Children", and the gruelling touring instilled the iron discipline and stamina that never left her. Her first professional work is dated 1913 by Encyclopedia.com's timeline and by the DIB's tour dating; the Royal Ballet and Opera Collections dates her professional debut to 1914, as a principal dancer in pantomime at the Lyceum Theatre, London, where she worked every year for five years, and later boasted she had danced in every seaside theatre in England. The two dates have different referents -- the tour and the pantomime -- and the study keeps both. She studied with the leading teachers of the day: Edouard Espinosa, for strong, clear footwork; Enrico Cecchetti, for symmetry, detail and ports de bras; Nicholas Legat, who made her less tense and gave her a sense of her own worth. In 1919, aged twenty-one, she was appointed principal dancer of the Beecham Opera, the resident opera company at the Royal Opera House, and in 1921 she had her first performing contact with Russian ballet, dancing with Leonide Massine's small company at the Royal Opera House. The child performer had become a professional, and the professional had found the Russians. [C34390]

**Takeaway:** she learned the trade the hard way -- touring children, pantomime, seaside theatres -- and the discipline of those years is the discipline she later imposed on a whole national school. [C34391]

## 3. Diaghilev: three years that taught her everything

In September 1923 she joined Serge Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, and was promoted to Soloist. She danced in Bronislava Nijinska's two greatest ballets, Les Noces and Les Biches, created during her first year with the company. The record needs care here, because the popular summary overstates it. In Les Biches, premiered 6 January 1924 at Monte Carlo, she was in the corps -- but Nijinska worked out her own central Hostess role on de Valois's body while creating it, so de Valois was the rehearsal model for the Hostess, not its originator on stage; Nijinska herself danced it. In Le Train Bleu, premiered 20 June 1924 in Paris, no named role for her is recorded in any source read: the four principals were Nijinska, Lydia Sokolova, Anton Dolin and Leon Woizikowsky, and de Valois danced in the ensemble. She was also mentor to the child Alicia Markova during this period. The affinity with Nijinska ran deep: both from countries with strong national cultural traditions, Poland and Ireland, both working in a male-dominated sphere, both interested in folk dance and in applying classical technique in non-classical ways. In 1924 doctors detected damage from the previously undiagnosed childhood polio, and she stepped back from regular intense dancing; Britannica puts it bluntly, that at twenty-six she quit performing after learning of the polio. She left the company as a full member in 1925 and returned as a guest artist in 1926 and 1928; Wikipedia's "remained with the company for three years" counts the guest appearances. In her own later words, everything she knew about how to run a ballet company she learned from working with Diaghilev -- but she was determined not to follow his example in one respect: she wanted a permanent company with its own base, not a touring one. [C34392]

**Takeaway:** Diaghilev gave her the operating model and the polio took the dancing -- and the combination, a choreographer who could no longer dance and a director who knew exactly how a company should run, is the whole of her career. [C34393]

## 4. The Academy, the Old Vic, and Yeats

In 1926 she founded her own school in London, the Academy of Choreographic Art -- Wikipedia dates it 1927, every other source read says 1926, and 1926 has the weight of sources. Her goal was a repertory ballet company with dancers drawn from the school and trained in a uniquely British style. The same year she approached Lilian Baylis, director of the Old Vic, with a proposal to establish the nucleus of a ballet company under the theatre's auspices; Baylis could not accommodate the full plan but hired her to coach actors in movement and choreograph dances for plays, and her pupils got professional stage experience in opera and plays at the Old Vic. Later in 1926 she became resident choreographer at the Festival Theatre, Cambridge, run by her cousin Terence Gray, and her movement for the chorus of the Oresteia in October 1926 won particular praise. In 1927 she worked on two plays by W. B. Yeats at Cambridge, and Yeats saw her work and invited her to Dublin to set up a small ballet school at the Abbey Theatre and help restage his "Plays for dancers". From around November 1927 she took responsibility for setting up and programming the Abbey Theatre School of Ballet, which ran until June 1933. The first ballet season at the Abbey came in January 1928, and from 1928 to 1934 she divided her time between London, Dublin and Cambridge, producing nearly fifty short works from a repertory shared with her London group and commissioning music and designs from Irish composers and artists -- Harold R. White's The Faun in April 1928, Arthur Duff's The Drinking Horn and John F. Larchet's Bluebeard, both in July 1933. Her Yeats collaborations ran on: "Fighting the waves" in August 1929, with her in the central role of the Woman of the Sidhe; "The dreaming of the bones" in December 1931; "At the hawk's well" in June 1933; "The king of the great clock tower" in July 1934, written specially for her, their last collaboration. She had been choreographing her own ballets since 1925, and the Dublin years were the workshop: small stages, living composers, a national literature to draw on, and a founder learning to run a school and a company at once. [C34394]

**Takeaway:** before there was a Royal Ballet there was a school in Dublin, a chorus in Cambridge and a theatre manager in London who would lend her a stage -- the institution was assembled from loans, and she never forgot it. [C34395]

## 5. Six women and a company

In January 1931 the Sadler's Wells Theatre, refurbished by Lilian Baylis, reopened; de Valois moved her school into studios there as the Sadler's Wells Ballet School, and a company was formed, the Vic-Wells Ballet. At formation the company had only six female dancers, de Valois herself as lead dancer and choreographer. The first full ballet production came on 5 May 1931 at the Old Vic, with Anton Dolin as guest star, and the first performance at Sadler's Wells itself followed a few days later, on 15 May. Two months later, on 5 July 1931, Job had its world premiere, produced for the Camargo Society at the Cambridge Theatre, London, with its first public performance on 22 September 1931 at the Old Vic. Job -- libretto by Geoffrey Keynes after William Blake, score by Ralph Vaughan Williams, designs by Gwendolen Raverat -- was the first ballet produced by an entirely British creative team, and it is still the oldest ballet in the Royal Ballet repertoire. In 1933 she retired fully from the stage, after Alicia Markova joined the company and was appointed Prima Ballerina -- though Encyclopedia.com records her continuing to dance until the late 1930s, and in 1937 she created the role of Webster, the bossy maid, in Ashton's A Wedding Bouquet, a humorous portrait of herself. The study presents it as the split it is: retired from leading roles in 1933, last stage role Webster in 1937. The same year, 1933, she invited Nikolay Sergeyev, former director of the Maryinsky Ballet, to stage the Petipa-Ivanov masterpieces from the notations he had brought from Russia, and between 1933 and 1939 he produced Coppelia, Giselle, The Nutcracker, Swan Lake and The Sleeping Beauty. In 1935 she engaged Frederick Ashton as Principal Choreographer and Constant Lambert as musical director -- Lambert's own article says he was appointed in 1931, and that split is carried too -- and with de Valois and Ashton, Lambert was the third member of the Vic-Wells triumvirate. [C34396]

**Takeaway:** six women, one theatre manager's loaned stage, and a borrowed Russian notation book -- the company began as almost nothing, and the nothing was the point: it could be built exactly to her plan. [C34397]

## 6. Job: the picture becomes a ballet

Job is the mechanism in its purest form, and the study gives it a full section because everything else she made is a variation on it. The scenario, by Geoffrey Keynes, was built from eight of William Blake's twenty-one Illustrations of the Book of Job, published in 1825/1826; Keynes selected the eight illustrations suitable for stage adaptation, and he and Gwendolen Raverat built a toy theatre with small cut-out figures to model the main scenes and groupings, so the ballet's eight scenes reproduce Blake's climaxes. The music was Vaughan Williams's Job: A Masque for Dancing, orchestrated for the theatre pit and conducted by Constant Lambert. The original cast was Job, John MacNair (spelled McNair in Hyperion's note); Satan, Anton Dolin; Elihu, Stanley Judson; Job's Wife, Margery Stewart. The nine scenes follow Blake's sequence: the Saraband of the Sons of God, Satan's Dance of Triumph, the Minuet of the Sons of Job, Job's Dream (the Dance of Plague, Pestilence, Famine and Battle), the Dance of the Three Messengers, the Dance of Job's Comforters and Job's Curse, Elihu's Dance of Youth and Beauty, the Pavane of the Sons of the Morning, and the Epilogue. Vaughan Williams stipulated no "toe dancing", which he detested, and the piece was billed as a masque, not a ballet; de Valois choreographed it, in Wikipedia's words, "using predominantly mimed actions to create a simple decorative effect", with no difficult technical demands apart from Satan's solo, an acrobatic dance by Dolin. The Times called it "a completely satisfying synthesis of the arts", and Diaghilev had rejected the scenario as "too English and too old-fashioned" -- which is exactly what it was, and why it worked. The mechanism is repeatable and she repeated it: pick a picture series, take its climaxes as scenes, choreograph the transitions between them, and let the picture do half the storytelling. [C34398]

**Takeaway:** Job is not a ballet about a picture; it is a picture that learned to move -- eight engravings, nine scenes, and the oldest ballet in the Royal Ballet's repertoire. [C34399]

## 7. The mime-and-dance blend

De Valois's choreography mixes formal classical dance with acted mime, and in her most characteristic works mime carries the narrative while dance carries the emotion. She came to this from pantomime, revue and opera ballet before Diaghilev, and from choreographing plays at the Old Vic, the Abbey Theatre and Terence Gray's Festival Theatre. Job is the clearest case: long passages where dancers speak with their hands and bodies in codified gesture -- Job's curse, the messengers' tidings, the comforters' dance -- punctuated by set dance numbers, the sarabande, minuet, pavane and galliard. The Rake's Progress, in the Dancers Forum history's words, "in its blend of dance and mime, continues where Job left off": the minor girl's solos, the Rake's acting, the Dancing Master's lesson all combine acting and classical steps. The stage reads like a moving picture-book with captions acted out, not a display of virtuosity, and that is why her ballets could be danced by a young company still building its technique: the drama did not depend on bravura. The mechanism has a cost, and the study does not hide it: mime ages faster than dance, and a ballet that depends on acted gesture needs dancers who can act, which is why her company prized dramatic dancers and why the ballets that survived best are the ones where the system, not the acting, carries the drama. [C34400]

**Takeaway:** mime is the load-bearing wall of her ballets -- the story lives in the hands, and the dancing is what the story feels like. [C34401]

## 8. Checkmate: the rule-system ballet

Checkmate, premiered 15 June 1937 at the Theatre des Champs-Elysees in Paris, with its UK premiere on 5 October 1937 at Sadler's Wells, is the ballet where the mechanism is the whole work. The music was Arthur Bliss's, who proposed the idea and wrote his own scenario; the designs were Edward McKnight Kauffer's, known for his posters; Constant Lambert conducted. De Valois knew nothing about chess, so Bliss held sessions at his Hampstead home, moving pieces on a big board and demonstrating their characteristic moves -- the knight's jump, the bishop's diagonals, the queen's mobility, the king's tottering shuffle -- and de Valois translated each move into dance. The prologue shows two players: the Golden Player, Love, takes the Red side, and the Black Player, Death, takes the Black side; they turn the board three times to see who moves first. The ballet then runs through entries named for the pieces: Dances of the Red Pawns, Dance of the Four Knights, Entry of the Black Queen, the Red Knight's Mazurka, Ceremony of the Red Bishops, Entry of the Red Castles, Entry of the Red King and Queen, then The Attack, The Duel, and the Finale -- Checkmate. The cast was the Black Queen, June Brae; the Red Knight, Harold Turner; the Black Knight, Michael Somes; Death, Frederick Ashton; the Red King, Robert Helpmann; the Red Queen, Pamela May; and the leader of the Black Pawns, Margot Fonteyn. The drama is the rules breaking: the Red Knight falls in love with the Black Queen when she tosses him a rose, defeats her in a duel but cannot kill her for love, and turning his back is fatally stabbed; the Black Queen then hunts down the Red King and stabs him in the back with a spear. Bliss's own rule for the piece was to "woo your audience with a bit of realism first, and then they will accept your romantic departures", and de Valois obeyed it literally: the pawns enter in formation and line up at one precise moment in the exact starting position of a chess game, so the audience can see the rules before it watches them broken. It is her signature ballet and a cornerstone of the British repertoire, in uninterrupted performance by the company and its successors since 1937. [C34402]

**Takeaway:** Checkmate is a machine that runs on rules and breaks on love -- and the moment the pawns line up in the exact starting position, the audience knows the game, so it can feel the murder. [C34403]

## 9. The Rake: decay as movement loss

The Rake's Progress, premiered 20 May 1935 at Sadler's Wells, is the ballet where the mechanism is the body itself. The music and scenario were Gavin Gordon's, after William Hogarth's series of eight paintings A Rake's Progress; the designs were Rex Whistler's, after Hogarth; de Valois called it her "Homage to Hogarth". The Rake was created by Robert Helpmann -- some sources credit Harold Turner or Walter Gore in early casts, and the study carries Helpmann as the better-attested version -- and the Betrayed Girl by Alicia Markova. The six scenes plus Prelude follow Hogarth's plates in order: the Reception, the Orgy, the Street, the Gaming House, Near the Prison Gates, and the Madhouse. The mechanism is character decay shown as movement loss: the Rake begins as a young nouveau riche, his choreography classical, legs turned out, with exaggerated port de bras -- palms turned down, elbows lifted -- to show his gaucheness and establish the 18th-century period; as he deteriorates through brothel, gambling den, debtors' prison and Bedlam madhouse, he loses his classical movements, becoming wild and uncontrolled, like an animal. The Betrayed Girl is the inverse signal: she is the only innocent and honest person in the ballet and is distinguished by being the only woman to dance on pointe, and her solos are never technical display in the Petipa manner but continue the story and develop her character. The pointe shoe becomes a moral marker -- one woman on pointe in a room full of flat-footed corruption -- and no set change is needed to show ruin, because the body does it. Whistler's original sets and costumes were destroyed in the Netherlands during the Second World War and remade for revival, and the ballet was filmed in 1982 at Sadler's Wells. [C34404]

**Takeaway:** the Rake's fall is legible in his feet -- turnout gives way to flailing, and the one woman on pointe is the one person who is not lying. [C34405]

## 10. The 1934 burst: ghosts, a barmaid, and a notebook

The year 1934 shows how fast she could work and how wide her range was. The Haunted Ballroom, premiered 3 April 1934 at Sadler's Wells (Arnold Haskell's Ballet in Britain gives 4 April; the study carries 3 April on the strength of two sources including the V&A object record of Markova's premiere gloves), had music and libretto by Geoffrey Toye after a story by Edgar Allan Poe, designs by Motley, and a cast of Alicia Markova as Alicia, leader of the ghosts; Robert Helpmann as the Master of Tregennis; William Chappell as the Stranger Player; and the fourteen-year-old Margot Fonteyn, then Margaret Fontes, as the young Tregennis heir -- her first solo role. It is a Gothic melodrama in one act, three scenes and an interlude: the heads of the Tregennis family are cursed to dance themselves to death with ghostly partners, and the ballet closes with the young son realising he too will meet the same death. It stayed in the repertoire until 1957, its last performance on BBC television on 24 February 1957, though much of the choreography is now lost and survives only in fragments. Six weeks later, on 15 May 1934, Bar aux Folies-Bergere opened at the Ballet Club, Mercury Theatre, Notting Hill -- the only work de Valois ever choreographed for the Rambert company, on a programme with Ashton's Mermaid and Les Masques. The scenario and choreography were hers, the music Chabrier selected and arranged by Constant Lambert, the designs William Chappell's after Manet; Pearl Argyle was the Barmaid, Markova was La Goulue the can-can dancer, Ashton himself was Valentin le Desosse the waiter, and the ballet begins and ends as the picture: the curtain opens on the barmaid wiping bottles and glasses, and when everyone has left and the cleaner starts work, the barmaid resumes the pose of Manet's painting. Her thirty-six-page loose-leaf choreographic notebook for it, with comments by Marie Rambert, survives in the Rambert archive and is the subject of Beth Genne's monograph The Making of a Choreographer (1996) -- unusually complete evidence of how she actually built a ballet. [C34406]

**Takeaway:** 1934 is the proof of range -- a Gothic ghost story that gave Fonteyn her first solo role, and a Manet painting that opens and closes as itself, with the notebook to show the working. [C34407]

## 11. The English style as a fusion

De Valois's "English style" was a deliberate synthesis, not a single invention. She took what she judged the strongest elements from the French, Italian, Danish and Russian schools and merged them into a new methodology, aimed at the English temperament and physique. The base layer was Cecchetti: she studied with Enrico Cecchetti, Edouard Espinosa and Nicholas Legat, and Cecchetti's method supplied the small, fast, precisely beaten footwork, the low rounded arms with soft elbows, the eight port de bras and eight positions of the body, and above all the epaulement -- the oblique carriage of shoulders and head -- drilled before anything else. De Valois herself wrote that "Maestro Cecchetti left a great imprint on the English school" and that "we were expected to master the epaulement first", and that "although he had been a famous virtuoso dancer he demanded grace from women before anything else. Unity of movement was a fetish with him". Against the Russian style -- height, attack, bravura -- the English style privileges epaulement, a supple curving spine, rapid intricate footwork and phrasing that flows over the bar line; on stage it reads as restraint and lyric line, less fireworks, more phrasing. But the style was not fixed, and the study records the tension honestly. Julia Farron recalled de Valois's teaching before 1945 as "really all below the hip -- and really all beneath the knee", and that "anything we learnt about epaulement we learnt from Fred (Ashton)"; yet Lynn Seymour and Antoinette Sibley, as senior students in the late 1950s, were deeply impressed by de Valois's pronounced emphasis on epaulement, and Lesley Collier, around 1964, remembers her attention above all to expansive arms. The style shifted across her teaching lifetime, which is why it is hard to pin to a single definition -- and why the attribution is split: some sources credit Ashton as the choreographer who "effectively invented the English style", while de Valois is credited as founder of the school and the system that produced it. The honest statement is that de Valois built the institution and the training system, and Ashton supplied the choreographic language most people mean by the style; both claims are sourced, and neither cancels the other. [C34408]

**Takeaway:** the English style is not a step but a blend -- Cecchetti's feet, epaulement drilled first, and a restraint that reads as lyricism -- and it was never fixed, which is why it kept working. [C34409]

## 12. The school as a manufacturing pipeline

De Valois did not just found a company; she built a machine that manufactures dancers to a specification and feeds them into the company. The school and the company are one system: the school exists to supply the company, and the company exists to give the school's pupils a stage. The timeline is the mechanism. 1926: the Academy of Choreographic Art, a dance school for girls in London. 1927: the Abbey Theatre School of Ballet, Dublin, at Yeats's suggestion, running until June 1933. 1931: the school moves into Sadler's Wells as the Sadler's Wells Ballet School, co-educational from 1931, and the Vic-Wells Ballet forms the same year, using students of the school -- the first company had only six female dancers, with de Valois herself as lead dancer and choreographer. 1939: renamed the Sadler's Wells Ballet School and Sadler's Wells Ballet. 1947: the school moves to Barons Court, general education combined with vocational ballet training. 1955/56: the younger years move to White Lodge, Richmond Park, as a residential boarding school for ages 11-16, the older years staying at Barons Court. October 1956: the Royal Charter links school and companies as the Royal Ballet School and the Royal Ballet. The students follow a specifically designed "System of Training": an eight-year system, five years at the Lower School (White Lodge, ages 11-16) followed by three years at the Upper School (ages 16-19), extrapolated and collated, in the School's own words, from what de Valois believed were the strongest elements of the French, Italian, Danish and Russian schools, merged to forge a new methodology. The school also taught theatre art, not only technique: lessons in theatre design, costumes and music, plus ballet history and national dances. The pipeline worked, and the proof is the roster: Margot Fonteyn was invited to join the Vic-Wells Ballet School at fourteen, succeeded Alicia Markova as prima ballerina in 1935, and spent her entire career with the company; the school's graduates include Fonteyn, Beryl Grey, Kenneth MacMillan, Darcey Bussell, Antoinette Sibley, Lynn Seymour, Nadia Nerina and Svetlana Beriosova. Admission is by dancing talent and potential alone, and ninety percent of current students rely on financial support. [C34410]

**Takeaway:** the school is the mechanism that makes everything else possible -- an eight-year pipeline, age eleven to nineteen, that manufactures dancers to a specification and feeds them straight into the company. [C34411]

## 13. The canon as backbone

De Valois's most consequential repertory decision was to make the great nineteenth-century Russian classics -- Giselle, Coppelia, The Sleeping Beauty, Swan Lake, The Nutcracker -- the permanent backbone of a Western company's repertory. No earlier Western company had followed this policy; because of Sadler's Wells and the Royal Ballet, many have followed suit to this day. The mechanism was to import the canon, stage it faithfully, and use it as the technical and box-office foundation on which a native repertory could be built. In 1933 she invited Nikolai Sergeyev, former regisseur of the Imperial Maryinsky Ballet, to stage the Petipa-Ivanov masterworks from the notations he had brought with him from Russia. The first attempt was Coppelia, given in its first two acts at Camargo Society galas at the Royal Opera House on 27 and 29 June 1933, with Lydia Lopokova, Stanley Judson and Hedley Briggs as the three principals, and de Valois herself, Ursula Moreton and Frederick Ashton leading the character dances -- the proof of concept for the whole Sergeyev programme, staged when the company was still small. Giselle followed on 1 January 1934 at the Old Vic, with Alicia Markova, the first English-born dancer ever to dance the role, and Anton Dolin; the designer is split in the sources, Barbara Allen in Macaulay's chronology, William Chappell in Haskell's, and the study carries the split. The Nutcracker followed on 30 January 1934 at Sadler's Wells, the first complete performance of The Nutcracker outside Russia, with Markova heading the cast, Elsa Lanchester dancing the Arabian Dance, and the young "Margaret Fontes" -- later Margot Fonteyn -- and Annabel Farjeon as snowflakes. Between 1933 and 1939 Sergeyev produced all five classics, and the productions were modest but became the basis of the company's classical reputation in the 1940s and 1950s. The canon gives the audience a familiar frame; the English style shows inside it. [C34412]

**Takeaway:** the classics were not the museum wing of her company; they were the foundation -- import the canon, stage it faithfully, and build the native repertory on top of it. [C34413]

## 14. The revival as a living institution

De Valois treated the classics not as museum pieces but as productions she owned, revised and re-staged across decades. A production was an institution with a life: it could be cut, re-blocked, re-designed and re-opened, and each revision carried her authority. Her Coppelia revision of 1954 rearranged aspects of the ballet, giving Swanilda six girlfriends rather than eight and placing Act Three in the village square of Act One, shrinking its scale -- remarkable because the Act One theme and variations for Swanilda and her friends contains the ballet's best choreography. Balanchine, by contrast, gave Swanilda eight friends in New York City Ballet's 1974 Coppelia. Her swan port de bras is still danced by Royal Ballet swan-maidens to this day: she installed it in the mid-1950s after a visit to Soviet Russia, and Macaulay notes the evidence shows she was wrong to think it was a more authentic rendition of Lev Ivanov's choreography. Her Sleeping Beauty production of 1977 opened the 1977-78 season and replaced MacMillan's 1973 production; it was planned without consulting him, and it precipitated his resignation. Her 1977-1992 Covent Garden Sleeping Beauty lived on after her death: the Boston Ballet production based on it sends half the royalties to the Royal Ballet School. She was, in general, insistent that her dancers be faithful to the steps and patterns acquired from Sergeyev -- yet she herself changed several of them, and the contradiction is documented here, not smoothed over. The watchable trace is a Swan Lake or Coppelia that looks subtly different from every other company's version: six friends instead of eight, a village-square third act, a particular swan arm -- a house style in the classics themselves, not only in the new works. [C34414]

**Takeaway:** a production is an institution with a life, and she revised hers by fiat -- which is why the Royal Ballet's classics still carry her fingerprints, and why a revision could also be a power move. [C34415]

## 15. The division of labour: she builds the house, others build the dances

De Valois's company-building masterstroke was a deliberate split: she would build the school, the discipline and the administration, and hire a resident choreographer to build the dances. She recruited Frederick Ashton as Principal Choreographer in 1935 -- he had been her colleague at the Ballet Club and Rambert -- and Constant Lambert as musical director the same year, and the two men supplied the repertory while she supplied the institution. She said afterwards that everything she knew about how to run a ballet company she learned from working with Diaghilev, and Diaghilev's model was exactly this: a director who commissions, a choreographer who invents, a designer and composer as equal partners. After Alicia Markova left in 1935 to form her own company with Anton Dolin, de Valois rebuilt the company on its male star Robert Helpmann, choreographer Ashton, musical director Lambert and her new leading ballerina Margot Fonteyn -- and gave Fonteyn her stage name at that point. The partnership was abrasive, affectionate and mutually indispensable: she built the school, the discipline and the administration; Ashton built the dances. Their partnership produced a company with a distinct national identity rather than a Ballets Russes imitation. She also invited choreographers Kenneth MacMillan and George Balanchine to work with the company, and in 1961-62 gave Rudolf Nureyev his first permanent job in the West. Her own choreography was efficient rather than prolific after the 1930s: her best work was created in that decade -- Job, the Rake, Checkmate -- and she knew the company needed a wider-ranging base than her own ballets. The division of labour is why the company survived her: on founding the Vic-Wells Ballet in 1931 she wrote that it would fail in its mission if it did not survive its original director and the next several directors. [C34416]

**Takeaway:** the masterstroke is a split, not a solo -- she builds the house and hires the choreographer, and the company outlives them both because no one person is the whole of it. [C34417]

## 16. The director's eye

De Valois ran talent-spotting as a continuous function of the directorship: she watched children, invited them into the school, named them, cast them young, and promoted from within; and when the company needed something it could not grow, she imported it and made the import part of the house. Margot Fonteyn was spotted as a schoolgirl and invited to join the Vic-Wells Ballet School at fourteen on her return from China; she made her professional debut in the Vic-Wells Nutcracker in 1934, took her first solo role in Fokine's Les Sylphides mazurka the same season, was cast by de Valois in the non-dancing role of the young master Tregennis in The Haunted Ballroom, succeeded Markova as prima ballerina in 1935 at sixteen, and danced Aurora at nineteen in the 1946 Sleeping Beauty. Rudolf Nureyev, after his defection from the Kirov in Paris in 1961, was given his first permanent job in the West by de Valois, who said: "I knew when I first saw him that I wanted him in our company... he walked into a perfect paradise here, into a theater scene ready for his talents". He debuted with the Royal Ballet in Giselle opposite Fonteyn on 21 February 1962, when Fonteyn was considering retirement; the partnership lasted nearly twenty years. She cultivated the rest of the roster the same way: Nadia Nerina, Svetlana Beriosova, Lynn Seymour and Antoinette Sibley were all school products she promoted, and Ben Stevenson was invited by de Valois at eighteen to join the Sadler's Wells Royal Ballet after winning the Adeline Genee Gold Medal. The mechanism's signature is the long arc: talent is not bought for a season, it is grown for a career, and the casting tells the story of the institution -- a fourteen-year-old in the school becomes the sixteen-year-old prima ballerina, a nineteen-year-old dances Aurora at Covent Garden, a forty-two-year-old Fonteyn, supposedly retiring, is reborn opposite a twenty-three-year-old defector. [C34418]

**Takeaway:** the director's eye is a pipeline with a memory -- spot them young, name them, cast them young, and when you must import, import the defector and make him family. [C34419]

## 17. The Diaghilev operating model

De Valois imported Diaghilev's working method wholesale: a ballet is a collaboration of equals -- choreographer, composer, designer -- and the company commissions new scores and designs rather than dancing to borrowed music. Job was the proof: the first ballet produced by an entirely British creative team -- choreography de Valois, music Vaughan Williams, libretto Geoffrey Keynes, designs Gwendolen Raverat, orchestration Constant Lambert. Checkmate was the same model with a different team: composer Arthur Bliss proposed the subject and wrote the scenario, designer Edward McKnight Kauffer did costumes and decor, Lambert conducted. The Rake's Progress had music and scenario by Gavin Gordon and designs by Rex Whistler after Hogarth. The Prospect Before Us had music by William Boyce arranged by Lambert and designs by Roger Furse after Rowlandson. Orpheus and Eurydice, danced to Gluck's opera with chorus at the New Theatre on 28 May 1941, had designs by Sophie Fedorovitch. Don Quixote, her last major ballet, had music by Roberto Gerhard and designs by Edward Burra. Her collaborators were British by policy -- Vaughan Williams, Bliss, Gordon, Lambert, Raverat, Whistler, Furse, Fedorovitch, Kauffer, Messel -- and the British creative team was itself a statement. She applied the same policy in Dublin, commissioning from Irish contemporary composers Harold R. White, Arthur Duff and John F. Larchet, and later in Turkey, where Keloglan had music by Ulvi Cemal Erkin and Cesmebasi by Ferit Tuzun. The design is not decoration on top of the dance; it is one of the three legs the ballet stands on, and when the design is strong the choreography can afford to be simple -- mime and tableau carry the story because the picture is already doing half the work. [C34420]

**Takeaway:** three legs, not one -- choreographer, composer, designer as equals, and a British team by policy, so the ballet looks and sounds like the country that made it. [C34421]

## 18. The war years and the touring machine

De Valois kept the company alive through touring, and used touring as both revenue and reputation. In September 1939 war broke out, Sadler's Wells became a shelter for the homeless, and the company led a peripatetic existence on tour. In May 1940, on tour in the Netherlands, the company narrowly escaped the German invasion. In the summer of 1940 she created the comedy ballet The Prospect Before Us, premiered 4 July 1940 at Sadler's Wells -- a one-act comic ballet in seven scenes about the rivalry of two eighteenth-century theatrical managers fighting over a troupe of dancers that includes the historical figures Didelot, Noverre and Vestris, played by Frederick Ashton, Alan Carter and John Hart, with Robert Helpmann as Mr. O'Reilly and Claude Newman as Mr. Taylor. It was deliberate wartime escapism, premiered in the first year of the war, weeks after the Netherlands escape and days before Sadler's Wells was bombed. In 1941 the company was renamed the Sadler's Wells Ballet, and the war years were the making of it: regular tours to a huge range of venues made it well known across Britain, a London base was found at the New Theatre, and the company gave seasons in theatres across London, toured England while Sadler's Wells was used as a shelter, and travelled to Brussels then Paris in early 1945 performing for soldiers as part of ENSA, then toured Germany -- at times dancing with two pianos instead of an orchestra. The touring machine became an export machine after the war: the 1949 American tour made Fonteyn an international celebrity overnight, and the 1950-51 five-month North American tour was so lucrative during post-war austerity that the company was congratulated and thanked in Parliament for the revenue it brought to Britain. The company returned to North America biennially for the next twenty-one years, always starting in New York. The repertory fits the machine: one-act narrative ballets -- Job, Checkmate, the Rake -- that pack small and travel well, alongside the full-length classics that anchor the London seasons. [C34422]

**Takeaway:** the war made the company, not the other way round -- two pianos, ENSA tours, a five-month American tour thanked in Parliament, and a repertory that packs small because it had to travel. [C34423]

## 19. The Sleeping Beauty of 1946

On Wednesday 20 February 1946, the Sadler's Wells Ballet reopened the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, with a lavish new production of The Sleeping Beauty produced by de Valois -- a major achievement while strict rationing was still in force, and the company's first performance at its permanent home. Apollo magazine's June 2023 piece says a glittering audience arrived on 19 February; the programme date is 20 February, and the study carries the programme date with the split recorded. The production was by de Valois and Nicholas Sergeyev, the choreography Petipa's with additional choreography by de Valois and Frederick Ashton, the music Tchaikovsky's, the designs Oliver Messel's -- four sets and some two hundred costumes, made under post-war rationing -- and Constant Lambert conducted. The cast was Princess Aurora, Margot Fonteyn, aged twenty-six; Prince Florimund, Robert Helpmann, who also danced Carabosse; the Lilac Fairy, Beryl Grey; the second cast led by Pamela May and David Paltenghi. The idea came from John Maynard Keynes. The theatre had spent the war as a Mecca dance hall, and the ballet that reopened it was the company's signature ever after: the production was restaged nearly 1,150 times and revived in 2006 by Monica Mason and Christopher Newton with Messel's designs restored, and it made Fonteyn a global star on the 1949 American tour. The single most watchable image of the whole mechanism is here: a war-exhausted nation watching a twenty-six-year-old English ballerina dance a Russian classic in a reopened opera house. The date of the reopening, the scale of the production, and the youth of the ballerina are the argument, made in full light: the canon, the English style, and the school's product, all on one stage at once. [C34424]

**Takeaway:** the reopening is the whole project in one night -- a Russian classic, Messel's rationing-era opulence, and a school-made ballerina, in a theatre that had been a dance hall. [C34425]

## 20. Don Quixote: the failure

Her last major ballet was a failure, and the study says so plainly, because the failure is part of the mechanism. Don Quixote premiered on 20 February 1950 at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, with the Sadler's Wells Ballet: choreography by de Valois, music by Roberto Gerhard -- the score reached its final form in 1950, a suite having been performed in 1941 and a symphonic suite in 1947 -- designs by Edward Burra, Don Quixote danced by Robert Helpmann and Dulcinea by Margot Fonteyn. It was the first full-length Don Quixote mounted outside Russia, with entirely new choreography, not the Petipa and Minkus version, and it must not be confused with the later Acosta production of 2013, which uses a different score and a different libretto emphasis. Contemporary criticism thought it ahead of its time; Encyclopedia.com records that it was a failure and has never been revived. Fonteyn took twelve curtain calls on the first night, and the complete Gerhard score was not heard again in full until Barcelona in 2015, after decades of editorial work. No scene-by-scene synopsis of de Valois's staging was found in the sources read, and none is invented here. The failure matters because it marks the end of her choreographic career proper: after 1950 her energy went into the company, the school, and the Turkish project, and her later works were small or made abroad. A choreographer who could not be revived in her own house is a fact the institution had to absorb, and it did -- the company she built did not need her ballets to survive, because the division of labour had already put Ashton and the canon in place. [C34426]

**Takeaway:** the last English ballet failed and was never revived -- and the company survived it, which is the proof the institution worked. [C34427]

## 21. The Turkish second career

In 1947 she founded the first ballet school in Turkey, as the ballet school of the Turkish State Opera and Ballet in Istanbul -- Encyclopedia.com dates her as founder and director of the Turkish School of Ballet in 1948 and of the Turkish State Ballet in 1956, and the study presents it as school founded 1947, company following in the 1950s. The school later became part of the State Conservatory at Ankara. The Turkish project repeated the whole English experiment in a country with no ballet tradition at all: school first, then folk-based national ballet, with native composers and native material inside classical training. Keloglan, premiered in 1950, was a dramatic ballet based on traditional Turkish folk dance steps, with music by Ulvi Cemal Erkin, the first original Turkish ballet work, built from the country's own folk material rather than imported classics; it was the first performance given by the conservatory's ballet students trained under British teachers, after de Valois founded the Yesilkoy Ballet School on 6 January 1948. Cesmebasi, At the Fountain, premiered in 1965, was the first full-length work created for the new Turkish State Ballet, and the first to feature music by a Turkish composer, Ferit Tuzun, in a revision of his 1954 orchestral piece Anatolian Suite made at de Valois's request, with choreography incorporating Turkish folk dance. The Academia paper "From Petrushka to Cesmebasi" treats it as the case study for the Ballets Russes legacy in Turkish ballet, and reads de Valois's style as expressionistic, Central European inspired dance drama, with German expressionist and symbolist roots, partly through her contact with W. B. Yeats. She directed the Turkish State Ballet until 1973, founded the Iranian National Ballet in 1958, and in 1951 sent Celia Franca to Canada, where she founded the National Ballet of Canada and its school. The choreographer of English picture-ballets became the founder of two national ballets, and the Turkish strand outlived the English choreographic career: her last major choreographic work was made at sixty-seven, two years after retiring as Royal Ballet director. [C34428]

**Takeaway:** the Turkish project is the English experiment run a second time, from zero -- school, folk material, native composer, national ballet -- and it outlived her choreography at home. [C34429]

## 22. The Sibley doorway, 1959-1960

The doorway itself, verified across four sources. On 21 March 1959, de Valois, artistic director of the Royal Ballet, allowed Sibley to undertake her first major role at a matinee at the Royal Opera House: Sibley was cast as Swanhilda in Coppelia. On 24 October 1959, Sibley was unexpectedly allowed to dance the leading role in Swan Lake, partnered by principal dancer Michael Somes -- her big break, after only two weeks' rehearsal, a last-minute replacement. In 1959 Sibley was tutored by Tamara Karsavina. In 1960 Sibley became a principal of the company -- Dancewear Central, the Voices of British Ballet and Prabook all say principal in 1960, against Wikipedia's Sibley article, which says soloist in 1960, and the study carries principal on the weight of three sources against one. The late 1950s generation graduating under de Valois also included Lynn Seymour, Merle Park, Anthony Dowell and David Wall. The point of the doorway is not the single favour but the pattern: the director's eye, the school's pipeline, and the promotion from within, all operating on one twenty-year-old in eighteen months -- a first major role, a Swan Lake debut, a principal promotion -- exactly as they had operated on Fonteyn twenty-five years earlier. Sibley's rise is the mechanism working at full speed, and it is the last generation de Valois raised before she retired. [C34430]

**Takeaway:** the doorway is the mechanism in miniature -- a twenty-year-old, a matinee, a Swan Lake debut, a principal promotion, all in eighteen months, all by the director's own decisions. [C34431]

## 23. The succession machine and its shadow

De Valois designed the company to outlive her -- and then, after retiring, repeatedly intervened from retirement to choose her successors, going over the sitting director's head to the board. The mechanism has two faces: institutional continuity by design, and continuity enforced by a founder who would not let go. The 1931 founding document for the Vic-Wells Ballet said it would fail in its mission if it did not survive its original director and the next several directors. In 1956 the Royal Charter formally linked company and school; de Valois retired from the Royal Ballet directorship in 1963 after thirty-two years, succeeded by Ashton, who was director 1963-1970, and she retired from the School in 1970. In 1968, retired but still revered by the Royal Opera House board, she machinated to force Ashton's replacement by Kenneth MacMillan, placing daily phone calls to David Webster and John Tooley from Richard Glasstone's house in Turkey. In 1977 she forced MacMillan's resignation by planning her own new Sleeping Beauty production to replace his 1973 one, without consulting him; MacMillan announced his resignation suddenly in spring 1977, and her production opened the 1977-78 season. In 1985 she arranged the resignation of Norman Morrice, MacMillan's successor. Her judgement, the study records honestly, was more limited than is usually acknowledged: she never agreed that Ashton's Symphonic Variations (1946) was a breakthrough masterpiece; in 1948 she wrote to Joy Newton "We still await a master in this country" as Ashton's Scenes de ballet approached its premiere; in 1952 she compared Balanchine's plotless ballets to "Fred at his worst"; and in 1980 she told an American scholar Leonide Massine had been "the greatest choreographer of the century". From the outside, seamless successions: Ashton to MacMillan to Morrice, each announced as orderly. From the inside, a founder's hand on the lever every time. [C34432]

**Takeaway:** the succession machine worked -- the company outlived her -- but it worked because the founder kept her hand on the lever long after she retired, and the cost of that is visible in the repertory. [C34433]

## 24. The feedback loop: teaching that revises itself

De Valois ran the school's teaching as a live system that she revised in response to what she saw on stage -- including revising it to match how Ashton was deploying a step in choreography. The syllabus was not fixed; it was corrected, reversed and corrected again by the director's eye, sometimes within weeks. Barbara Fewster, a long-term senior teacher at the Royal Ballet School, told Macaulay that when asked whether de Valois, on seeing how Ashton was deploying a step in his choreography, revised the School's teaching of that step accordingly, Fewster replied: "She may have done -- but she may then have changed her mind about it a month later." The cou de pied incident is the proof: after a visit to Russia, de Valois told the school staff that cou de pied was now to be a different position; one male teacher thought about it over the weekend, resigned at the start of the next week because he could not change his teaching of cou de pied, and de Valois accepted his immediate resignation; a month later she announced cou de pied was to return to its original position, and she did not reinstate the teacher. The teaching changed across her lifetime: Julia Farron recalled pre-1945 teaching as "really all below the hip -- and really all beneath the knee"; Sibley and Seymour as senior students in the late 1950s were impressed by her pronounced emphasis on epaulement; Lesley Collier, around 1964, remembers her attention above all to expansive arms. Her musicality was inconsistent as a teacher: some dancers recall her attention to musicality as fascinating and useful, while others describe her setting combinations with no rhythmic connection to the music she had requested. She was, in Macaulay's words, "notoriously self-contradictory". The mechanism is not that she was always right; it is that the school's teaching was hers to revise, and she revised it constantly, by observation and by fiat. A student could be taught one cou de pied in September and another in October -- and the watchable trace is inconsistency across generations of graduates, which is also why the "English style" is hard to pin to a single definition. [C34434]

**Takeaway:** the syllabus was a live system, not a tablet -- revised by the director's eye, reversed a month later, and the teacher who could not change was allowed to walk. [C34435]

## 25. Marriage, retirement, and the long afterlife

On 5 July 1935, per the Dictionary of Irish Biography, de Valois married Dr Arthur Blackall Connell, a physician and surgeon from Wandsworth, general practitioner in Barnes, London, later Sunningdale, Berkshire; Wikipedia says only that she married him in 1935, at Windsor, and the study does not merge the two details into one source. She was his second wife; the union was childless; she had two step-sons, including Dr David Blackall Connell, born 1930; the marriage lasted more than fifty years, until his death in July 1987. She kept her private life very distinct from her professional life, making only the briefest references to her marriage in her autobiographical writings, and during the war she helped her husband most weekends at his London surgery. In 1963 she retired as director of the Royal Ballet after thirty-two years, succeeded by Frederick Ashton. How long she stayed as head of the school is split -- Wikipedia says she formally retired from the School in 1970, Britannica says she remained head until 1972, the Royal Ballet and Opera Collections says she devoted herself to teaching at the School until 1971 -- and the safe statement is that she remained involved with the School into the early 1970s. After retirement she devoted herself to teaching at the School, directing the Turkish State Ballet, and reviving her own ballets; in 1977 she supervised a new production of The Sleeping Beauty for the Royal Ballet. Her honours in later life: the Albert Medal 1964; the Erasmus Prize 1974, the first woman recipient; Companion of Honour, dated 31 December 1981 by Wikipedia with the most gazette-like precision, against 1980 in Britannica, 1982 in Encyclopedia.com and the funeral notice, and 1983 in the Royal Ballet and Opera Collections -- the study carries the split and prefers 1981; the Order of Merit on 2 January 1992; a Laurence Olivier Award Special Award in 1992. In April 1964 she was the subject of This Is Your Life, surprised by Eamonn Andrews at Frederick Ashton's home in London. In 1982-83 "Madam" -- the dancers called her Madam, pronounced with the emphasis on the first syllable, though Fonteyn's autobiography spells it "Madame" -- a two-part documentary about her was transmitted on Channel 4. In 1991 she appeared on BBC Radio 4's Desert Island Discs; her chosen book was a collection of poems, and her luxury item an everlasting bottle of sleeping pills. In 1998 she attended a number of events to celebrate her hundredth birthday, although by then very frail, and despite arthritis and other illnesses she spent her hundredth birthday applauding young dancers. Her books were Invitation to the Ballet (1937), Come Dance with Me: A Memoir, 1898-1956 (1957, reprinted 1992), Step by Step: The Formation of an Establishment (1977), The Cycle and Other Poems (1985) and Selected Poems (1998). She died at home, 1 Elm Bank Mansions, The Terrace, Barnes, on Thursday 8 March 2001, aged 102, three months short of her hundred and third birthday, and no reputable source read states a cause of death. There is a memorial bench outside St Mary's Church, Blessington, and the Ninette de Valois Festival of Dance has been held annually in Blessington since 2018. The Royal Ballet School's centenary "100 Stories" series (2026) begins with her, and its motto is her words: "Respect the past, herald the future, but concentrate on the present". [C34436]

**Takeaway:** fifty-two years of marriage kept entirely separate from the work, thirty-two years directing, and thirty-eight years of retirement in which she still chose the directors -- the afterlife was longer than the career. [C34437]

## 26. Aesthetic logic: the institution as the artwork

The aesthetic logic of de Valois is that the institution is the artwork, and the ballets are the evidence. Her choreography is simple on purpose: mime and tableau, pictures that hold and then break, rules that play out, bodies that lose their technique as they lose their souls. The simplicity is not a limitation but a division of labour -- the choreography can afford to be simple because the picture is doing half the work, the school is doing the training, and the canon is doing the box office. The ballets she made are small, literary, and English: a Blake masque, a Hogarth morality, a chess game, a Rowlandson comedy, a Manet bar. The institution she made is large, permanent, and exportable: a school, a company, a charter, a touring machine, a second national ballet in Turkey and a third in Canada by proxy, through Celia Franca. Her own words give the logic away. On the Royal Ballet, in Step by Step: "It was never meant to be a personal effort in a particularly specialised direction. It was to become something that would have a root in the country's theatre." On the Sadler's Wells years: "The progress of The Sadler's Wells Ballet from 1931-1941 was the most golden and happy period of my life." On her own mind: "My mind prefers adversity and complexity to any state of smooth living." And on the work itself: "Respect the past, herald the future, but concentrate on the present." The logic is architectural, not expressive: build the house, fill it with dancers you trained, give them classics to stand on and new ballets to speak in, and let the choreographer you hired write the language. The study's one hard correction to the received picture is the attribution split: she did not invent the English style single-handed. She built the machine that produced it, and Ashton wrote its language, and the two halves are inseparable -- the ballets are simple because the institution was doing the heavy lifting, and the institution worked because the ballets gave it something to teach. [C34438]

**Takeaway:** the artwork is the house, not the dance -- the ballets are simple because the institution is doing the heavy lifting, and the institution works because the ballets give it something to teach. [C34439]

## 27. What she did not do

The honest limits matter as much as the mechanisms, because they are what keep the prompts honest. There is no named lift associated with de Valois, and no lift of hers is cited as a signature. There is no codified teaching method with her name on it: a search for "de Valois method" returns nothing, and she appears only as an alumna of Cecchetti. There is no unique step vocabulary with her name on it: no source read attributes a specific step, position or combination to her as its inventor, the "Fred Step" belongs to Ashton, and there is no verified "English arabesque" as a codified term. There is no precise "home-grown casting rule" as a written policy: the practice of promoting school graduates into the company is documented, a formal rule with that name is not, and the study describes it as a practice, not a policy. She did not dance Les Sylphides with the Ballets Russes -- no source shows it, and her documented roles there are the corps and ensemble parts in Les biches and Le Train Bleu, with the Hostess worked out on her body in rehearsal. She did not create the original The Gods Go a-Begging: the 1928 date belongs to Balanchine's original, and her version premiered on 10 January 1936. **Takeaway:** what she did not do is the frame around what she did -- no method, no lift, no signature step, and a company that outlived her anyway. [C34441]
