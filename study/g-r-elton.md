## M1. The Tudor Revolution in Government thesis

In the 1530s, under Thomas Cromwell, medieval household government was replaced by modern bureaucratic government: the realm before was "the King's private estate writ large" and administration was done by the king's household servants; after Cromwell it was done by separate state offices. The change was "part of a planned revolution". The thesis is the piece: show a household, servants carrying the king's business in their hands, and then the same business moving into offices, desks, files, seals, and the household shrinking back to ceremony while the offices take the realm.

**Takeaway:** show the household becoming the bureau, not the king becoming modern.

## M2. The planned revolution by legislation

Cromwell's revolution was planned, legislated and executed by one man: statutes, above all the Acts that made the Church of England autonomous and the king its supreme head, established "for all time to come the sovereignty of statute law, and therefore of Parliament, the maker of statutes". The statute is the instrument and the plan is the point: show the plan first, a single mind drawing the shape of the new state, and then the statutes arriving one by one like hammer blows, each one a law and each law a wall, until the sovereignty of statute law stands where the pope's jurisdiction used to be.

**Takeaway:** the revolution arrives as legislation, one statute at a time.

## M3. The imperial crown

Cromwell translated royal supremacy into parliamentary terms: the preamble to the Act in Restraint of Appeals became "an unhesitating statement of the theory... of the imperial crown of England sovereign within its own realm over both laity and church". The crown is imperial not because it conquers abroad but because it answers to no one at home, and the piece must show the boundary closing: the realm as a closed circle, the pope's hand reaching in and finding no opening, the crown sovereign inside its own line, and the line drawn by words in a preamble.

**Takeaway:** draw the closed circle of the imperial crown, and the hand that finds no opening.

## M4. Household versus bureau

The diagnostic cut: before Cromwell the king's household did the state's work; after him, the household and the state were delineated, and "modern bureaucratic government" replaced "medieval, household-based government". The cut is visible: on one side a table in a great hall where the king's servants eat and govern in the same room, on the other a row of offices where men who never see the king govern in his name, and the piece must hold both sides in the frame at once, because the revolution is the line between them.

**Takeaway:** hold the hall and the office in one frame, and show the line between them.

## M5. The new revenue courts

Cromwell created specialised financial "courts", effectively ministries, Augmentations, First Fruits, independent of the Exchequer, "equally symbolic of impersonal, institutional continuity". The money stops flowing through the king's hands and starts flowing through institutions that outlive him, and the piece must show the stream diverted: the river of revenue leaving the household channel and cutting a new bed, and the new bed staying put when the king dies, because the institution, not the man, holds the money now.

**Takeaway:** divert the river of revenue into a bed that outlives the king.

## M6. The crystallised Privy Council

Cromwell was "more responsible than anyone else for the crystallisation of national government in a Privy Council: government by a collective, ancestral to the cabinets of more recent times". The Council hardens out of the household like a crystal out of solution, and the piece must show the hardening: a loose circle of the king's friends tightening into a table with a clerk and a register, the collective replacing the favourite, and the table surviving the man who set it. Later qualified by Starkey: the Council "was not Cromwell's" and acquired its own machinery only after his fall, see M24.

**Takeaway:** show the circle of friends hardening into a table with a register.

## M7. The in-tray method

Elton mined Cromwell's surviving papers, mostly letters from the in-tray, not the out-tray, so the student sees "exactly what Cromwell saw as he looked out from behind his desk". The in-tray is the viewpoint: show the desk first, the pile of incoming letters as Cromwell saw it, the world arriving as paper, and the minister deciding in the order the paper arrived, because the archive is not a source about the man, it is the man's own field of vision.

**Takeaway:** put the viewer behind Cromwell's desk, looking at the in-tray.

## M8. Reconstituting the archive in its original integrity

Elton's life's work was "to reconstitute that archive in its original integrity", his guiding principle being "one should stick as faithfully as possible to the primary historical record, which should make possible a history that is indisputably true, not the historian's invention". The trap: "Elton, the archival positivist, was to a great extent the prisoner of the very evidence that he claimed to have at his command", because Cromwell's centrality is partly an artefact of his papers surviving in bulk after his attainder. The piece must show both: the archive reassembled piece by piece into its original order, and then the shadow of the archive itself, the papers that survived because their owner fell, making the fallen man look like the centre of everything.

**Takeaway:** reassemble the archive, then show the shadow it casts.

## M9. The points of contact

Parliament, Council and Court are the places where crown and country met, the subject of Elton's three Royal Historical Society presidential addresses, "Tudor Government: The Points of Contact. I. Parliament" (TRHS 24, 1974), "II. The Council" (25, 1975), "III. The Court" (26, 1976), reprinted in Studies III, pp. 3-57. The Court essay is, per Starkey, "the best short description of the Tudor Court (indeed of any court I know) in print". The mechanism is the meeting point: show three doors where the crown and the country touch, and the traffic through them, because the state is not a building, it is the places where the two sides meet. The lectures themselves were not read end to end this leg; the mechanism is cited, not expounded from a reading.

**Takeaway:** show the three doors where crown and country touch.

## M10. Parliament as trinity

"We mean by Parliament the trinity of king and Lords and Commons", against both the Stubbsian equation of Parliament with the Commons and the Namierite neglect of the Lords. The trinity is the shape: three parts in one body, and the piece must show all three at once, the king, the Lords and the Commons in a single frame, because a Parliament drawn as the Commons alone is a body with two limbs missing.

**Takeaway:** draw all three parts of the trinity in one frame.

## M11. Parliament as a talking institution

"Parliament was part of the king's government, called to assist him by making grants and laws, but also designed to keep the Crown in touch with opinion and an accepted occasion for complaint and protest. It was, and is, a talking institution, a place for debate. The historian who supposed that debate must mean 'inevitable conflict' had better investigate his subconscious." The talking is the function: show the chamber as a room where speech happens, the complaint arriving as words and leaving as law, and the debate not as a battle but as the Crown listening, because the talking is the institution doing its work.

**Takeaway:** show the chamber listening, not fighting.

## M12. The managerial Commons

The Commons was normally run by "the managerial success of the official element" and "general co-operation", not conflict: "the people whom diarists note as speaking most frequently turn out to be the Privy Councillors and their band of reliable supporters". The managers are the mechanism: show the floor of the House with the Privy Councillors doing most of the talking and the rest of the House mostly agreeing, because the Commons worked when it was managed, and the managing is the history, not the shouting.

**Takeaway:** show the managers doing the talking, and the House mostly agreeing.

## M13. The fictional puritan opposition

Neale "had dreamed up an organised Puritan opposition which never existed"; Thomas Norton, typed by Neale as a Puritan opposition leader, "was one of the Privy Council's most active managers in the House". The opposition is a phantom: show the labelled figure first, the Puritan firebrand of the textbooks, and then the label peeling off to reveal a Council manager doing the Council's work, because the opposition never existed and the peeling is the correction.

**Takeaway:** peel the label off the supposed opposition leader.

## M14. The functional phase

Judge Parliament by what it did rather than what was talked of as doing: "if today we want to know what Parliament did rather than was talked of as doing... we wish to take our beginning from the Parliamentary record properly so called". The three phases: Stubbsian/political, Namierite/psephological, functional/revisionist. The functional phase is the turn from talk to record: show the speeches fading and the record remaining, the statutes and the grants and the journals, because what Parliament did is written in the record and what was talked of as doing is written in the memoirs, and the two are not the same.

**Takeaway:** start from the record, not from the talk.

## M15. "Thus the record says."

The self-sufficiency of the record: "All the historian is required to do is to approach the evidence with an open mind, study it faithfully, and write up his results clearly: 'that is how historical knowledge should advance, and that is how as a rule it does.'" The failure mode, per Starkey: the record "not being gifted with tongues, says nothing of the kind", and Elton sometimes ventriloquised it ("the middle ages were over"). The piece must show the record as a silent object, a page that says nothing until it is read, and then the reading putting words into its mouth, because the record does not speak and the historian who claims it does is speaking for it.

**Takeaway:** show the silent page, and the mouth put to it.

## M16. History deals in events, not states

"History deals in events, not states; it investigates things that happen and not things that are" (The Practice of History, 1967, p. 22, via Skinner). The anti-structural rule: no destiny, no deep structure, only what happened. The piece must show happenings, not conditions: a door opening, a signature, a fall, a vote, each one an event with a date, and no background force pulling the strings, because the strings are the historian's invention and the events are the history.

**Takeaway:** show what happened, not what was.

## M17. The apprentice carpenter

The aspiring historian is an apprentice, "at one point specifically as an apprentice carpenter", producing a first piece of work to be inspected and judged by a master craftsman. Craft, not theory, is the training. The piece must show the workshop: the apprentice at the bench, the master inspecting the joint, the first piece of work held up to the light, because history is learned by making, and the making is judged by a craftsman, not by a theorist.

**Takeaway:** show the bench, the joint, and the master's inspection.

## M18. No authorities

"In history there are no authorities: history is a free study in which no man can claim rule, or credence for his mere ipse dixit, and in which the only true sin is to deny a hearing to views with which one happens to disagree." Starkey quotes the same dictum as Elton's own ("historians 'neither are nor have authorities'"). The rule is a clearing: show the authorities first, the great names standing like statues, and then the statues coming down, because no man's word is evidence and every view gets a hearing, and the hearing is the only authority there is.

**Takeaway:** take the statues down, and give every view its hearing.

## M19. The Carr refutation

Against E. H. Carr's "whimsical" distinction between "historical facts" and "facts of the past": it reflected "an extraordinarily arrogant attitude both to the past and to the place of the historian studying it" (Practice of History, pp. 56-57). Carr's philosophy was "an attempt to provide a secular version of the medieval view of history as the working of God's master plan with 'Progress' playing the part of God" (p. 40). The refutation is a substitution: show the historian sorting facts into two piles, the historical and the merely past, and then the piles collapsing into one, because the sorting was the historian's arrogance, and the past does not come pre-sorted.

**Takeaway:** collapse the two piles of facts into one.

## M20. Events, not destiny

The early seventeenth century must be treated "as a sequence of events rather than the working out of a destiny"; the Civil War broke down "because the early Stuart governments could not manage or persuade, because they were incompetent, sometimes corrupt, and frequently just ignorant of what was going on or needed doing". And: "no revolution of the size claimed for this one ever so readily stopped short and reversed itself." The piece must show incompetence, not destiny: a government that cannot manage, cannot persuade, does not know what is going on, and the breakdown arriving as a series of small failures, because the destiny reading turns a mess into a plan, and the mess is the history.

**Takeaway:** show the small failures, not the destiny.

## M21. The first revisionist

"Geoffrey Elton was the first of the revisionists" (Jennifer Loach, in Revolution Reassessed, 1986, p. 117). In 1965 he was already warning against finding the Civil War's origins in Elizabeth's parliaments; "most historians would now accept that the century after 1530 was one of substantial harmony in the relationship between crown and Parliament". The revision is a clearing: show the old story first, the century of conflict between crown and Parliament, and then the conflict draining out of it, the harmony underneath revealed, because the first revisionist is the one who notices the story was wrong before anyone else does.

**Takeaway:** drain the conflict out of the century, and show the harmony underneath.

## M22. King or minister?

The 1954 question, answered "minister": the Henrician Reformation came as it did "because Thomas Cromwell produced a plan which achieved Henry's ends by destroying the papal power and jurisdiction in England and by creating in England an independent sovereign state". The question is a fork: show the two figures side by side, the king with his ends and the minister with his plan, and the plan doing the work, because the ends belonged to Henry and the means belonged to Cromwell, and the means are the history.

**Takeaway:** put the king and the minister side by side, and let the plan do the work.

## M23. Not biographable

Elton never wrote a biography of Cromwell, "holding the genre in contempt": "We know absolutely nothing about him until he was about thirty-five... A biography would be an absurdity." Only the public man, the man of action, is recoverable from the debris. The piece must show the debris: the papers, the statutes, the accounts, and no face behind them, because the private man is not in the record, and the record is all there is, and the biography would be an invention.

**Takeaway:** show the debris, and no face behind it.

## M24. The 98 per cent retreat

Softening without surrender. In an unscripted address in 1985: "I would no longer stand quite by the picture I presented thirty years and some ago, in which Cromwell is the eminence grise of everything. I now think that he was the eminence grise of about 98 per cent." And in "Thomas Cromwell Redivivus" (1977): "I made some rather extravagant claims for him, though I stand by the essence of my opinions there." The retreat is a dial, not a surrender: show the figure of Cromwell filling the whole frame, and then the frame widening by two per cent, the smallest possible concession, because the essence stands and only the extravagance goes.

**Takeaway:** widen the frame by two per cent, and keep the essence.

## M25. The polemic style

The "hound of heaven" ("A self-appointed hound of heaven ought to be more precise in his quest", against J. P. Cooper, 1961); postmodern theory as "the intellectual equivalent of crack" (Return to Essentials, 1991); dull historians deserving "the pillory" (M26). The style is the weapon: public impatience with naivety or error, private kindness. The piece must show the hound at work: the chase, the cornering, the precise bite, because the polemic is not decoration, it is the method applied to people, and the people are the errors made flesh.

**Takeaway:** show the hound cornering its quarry, precisely.

## M26. Readability as a test of quality

"One of the chief tests of the quality of historical work lies in its readability. History, even serious history, is interesting, and the historian who makes it dull deserves the pillory." The pillory is the test: show the dull book first, the heavy volume that nobody finishes, and then the same history written so it can be read, because readability is not a courtesy, it is the quality test, and the dull historian has failed it.

**Takeaway:** put the dull book in the pillory.

## M27. The German approach

"Elton's approach has been described as teutonic; it has been said that of all English historians his attitude is most akin to that of the great German nineteenth-century masters" (Kenyon). His concentration on government, institutions and the state "puts him in the mainstream of prewar German history writing. His application of the 'German approach' to early modern English history is an essential ingredient of his originality as a British historian" (Alter, THE, 1998). The approach is a transplant: show a German method, the study of the state and its institutions, planted in English soil, and growing into something neither German nor English but Eltonian, because the refugee brought the method with him and the method remade the field.

**Takeaway:** plant the German method in English soil, and watch it grow Eltonian.

## M28. The defence of political history

"There is nothing at all to be said for such attitudes: historians who can muster no interest for the active political lives of past societies have no sense of history at all" (Political History, 1970). Politics is "the active expression of a social organism". He "revived and restated Seeley's dictum" that history is past politics. The defence is a claim about what history is: show the political life first, the active expression, the organism moving, and the social history waiting in the wings, because the political life is the history and the rest is the setting.

**Takeaway:** show the organism moving, not the setting it moves in.

## M29. Tell the social scientists to shut up

"It is our task... to tell the social scientists to shut up... [for] they are bound to be wrong" (Elton, via Landrum eulogy, The Historian 59.1, 1996, p. 119). Against Fogel's cliometrics he admitted statistical methods for certain purposes but denied them priority (Which Road to the Past?, 1983); Starkey: "The real challenge to history comes not from statistics but from sociology." The piece must show the counting first, the tables and the graphs, and then the counting set aside, because the numbers are welcome as servants and refused as masters, and the refusal is the point.

**Takeaway:** welcome the numbers as servants, and refuse them as masters.

## M30. The outsider's England

The refugee's chosen country: he "proclaimed, a few months after his arrival in Britain, that this was the country in which he ought to have been born" (Alter, THE, 1998). And, "only half in jest, that as an atheist and a Jew he was uniquely qualified to write the history of the Reformation" (Tom Scott, Renaissance Studies 9.3, 1995, pp. 345-346). "Elton was deeply thankful for the opportunities which England offered him as a refugee; this debt he paid back in full measure by his hard work and dedication as a scholar and teacher for nearly forty years." The outsider is the mechanism: show the refugee arriving, and then the same man forty years on, having paid the debt in full, because the gratitude is the engine and the work is the repayment.

**Takeaway:** show the arrival, and the forty years of repayment.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife

Phase 1 (1949-1960): the young revisionist. First article on the Act in Restraint of Appeals (English Historical Review, 1949); the PhD thesis becomes The Tudor Revolution in Government (1953), the upstart thesis that made Cromwell the architect of modern government; "King or Minister?" (1954) answers "minister". The Tudor Constitution (1960) codifies the institutional map. Kenyon: "the upstart revisionist of the 1950s".

Phase 2 (1961-1977): the consolidator and the methodologist. England under the Tudors (1955, revised 1974) becomes the bestselling textbook; The Practice of History (1967) and Political History (1970) defend the craft against Carr and the sociologists; The Future of the Past (1968) attacks Tawney's framework-fitting; Policy and Police (1972) is the archival masterpiece ("Elton's best book", Collinson); Reform and Renewal (1973, Wiles Lectures) and Reform and Reformation (1977) extend the revolution into religion. The "first revisionist" (Loach) is now the man to beat: Harriss and Williams attack the thesis from the medieval side; Starkey and Guy attack it from inside Cambridge.

Phase 3 (1978-1994): the oracle and the polemicist. The RHS presidential addresses ("Points of Contact", 1974-1976) sketch the political structure; "Members' Memorial" (1982) places him in the functional phase; the 1983 climacteric (Regius chair, American Festschrift, Fogel debate, Studies III); the Butterfield Lecture (1983) pays tribute to the rival; The Parliament of England (1986) demolishes Neale's puritan opposition; the second inaugural, The History of England (1984); Return to Essentials (1991) declares war on postmodernism ("the intellectual equivalent of crack"); The English (1992) is the last book, a national history from 927, reviewed by Cowling as "Virtuous Machiavellian". Landrum: "The upstart revisionist of the 1950s became in the 1970s the self-appointed oracle of the discipline." The thesis softens ("98 per cent") but never surrenders; the method hardens into dogma in the hands of the Eltonettes, which Starkey already saw in 1984.

Posthumous afterlife: the 1997 TRHS special issue "The Eltonian Legacy" (Skinner, Holmes and others), which Collinson notes was "so critical of his achievements that many described it as 'Oxford's revenge'"; the March 1996 "Eltonfest" (James Campbell, "Elton on The English"); Bernard's The King's Reformation (2005) re-opens the 1954 question; "a toned down, less teleological Eltonianism seems to be the current orthodoxy" (Collinson, 2007); MacCulloch's Thomas Cromwell (2018) finally writes the biography Elton said could not be written.

## Aesthetic logic

Elton's aesthetic is the aesthetic of the record: the archive is the beautiful object, and the historian's job is to let it show. His prose is plain, impatient and exact, and his system is a refusal, a refusal of destiny, of structure, of theory, of the label the period did not use, of the opposition that never existed, of the biography that cannot be written. The beauty is in the cut: household against bureau, talk against record, events against destiny, the record against the ventriloquist. Everything in his work is a boundary drawn cleanly and then defended, and the defence is the style, the hound of heaven, the pillory, the crack, the telling of the social scientists to shut up. The pieces that follow from him should therefore be built on a cut and a boundary, not on a mood: a desk and an in-tray, a statute arriving, a label peeling, a pile of paper reassembled into its original order. The colour is the colour of paper and ink and sealing wax, the motion is the motion of paper moving, the interaction is the inspection, the lifting of the label, the widening of the frame by two per cent. Nothing in Elton is decorative, and nothing in a piece from Elton should be either: if it does not cut, it does not belong.
