# Armando Durante: Study Notes

**Site:** Armando Hector Durante, 1934 - 24 December 1996 -- Argentine kinetic artist, born in Buenos Aires, died in Madrid; a builder of motorised light boxes in the Paris GRAV years, a co-founder of Groupe Position in 1971, and a screenprinter in Spain for the last nineteen years of his life (C8401). The death year is in conflict: the Madrid gallery gives 24 December 1996, aged 62, while all four ICAA records print 1934-1997; this study prints 1996 and carries the conflict (see Corrections). His kinetic work is a single Paris episode, roughly 1960 to the end of 1973: light boxes built on Victor Vasarely's principles and inspired by the kaleidoscope, a rotating disc behind a striated screen generating unstable images that the spectator completes by being present (C8401, C8454). He was never a GRAV member -- no roster names him -- but he worked alongside its members in Paris from about 1960, was its invited guest at L'Instabilite in 1962 (a participation his own source flags as undocumented in the group's chronology), and exhibited beside it at Lumiere et mouvement in 1967 (C8408, C8411, C8512). At the end of 1973 he quit kinetic art, "detesting the precious drift it had taken", and turned conceptual (C8401); the rest of his career is participatory portrait installation and screenprinting, not light.

## The life: the painter's son who built boxes of light, then walked away from them

Armando Hector Durante was born in Buenos Aires in 1934 (C8401). No source read gives a day or month of birth, and no source gives his mother's name or any sibling. From childhood he was tied to the art world: his father was a painter devoted to decoration, and Durante did his first works with him (C8401, single-source). In his youth he entered the school of Fine Arts in Buenos Aires (C8401); the thesis source names the school precisely -- he graduated from the Escuela Nacional de Artes Visuales Manuel Belgrano (C8408, fn 214, single-source for the graduation). During his studies he created the Agrupacion Reformista together with Le Parc, Sobrino, Garcia Rossi and Eduardo Rodriguez (C8408, fn 214, single-source). In 1955, after the coup d'etat, the fine-arts schools were occupied by students; Le Parc became one of the leaders of the student movement, president of the student centre, and was expelled from the school along with other student leaders (C8408, fn 214). Durante's own part in the 1955 occupation is not stated -- the footnote describes Le Parc's role, not his. The 3 October 1955 occupation of the three art schools is independently attested in study #631 for Torres Aguero (C8412), which gives the date and the reform context, but does not name Durante. No teacher of Durante's is named in any source read.

He moved to Paris after finishing his first cycle, at the age of 26 (C8401), which puts the move at about 1960 (1934 + 26). The thesis source confirms the shape of the story from the other end: after winning the Braque in 1966 he "re-encountered his friends of GRAV -- Garcia-Rossi, Le Parc and Sobrino -- whom he had met in the mid-1950s at the Escuela Nacional de Artes Visuales Manuel Belgrano" (C8408). "Re-encountered" implies an earlier Paris stay and a return to Buenos Aires in between; the 1965 Braque final and 1965 Di Tella invitation (both Buenos Aires events, C8408 fn 214) fix him in Buenos Aires in 1965. So the working timeline is: Paris about 1960-1965, Buenos Aires 1965-1966, Paris again 1966-1974. The first stay's end date is inferred, not directly attested -- no source read gives a return date for it (C8401, C8408).

In Paris he encountered the works of GRAV, "a group that was founded in 1960 and led by two Argentineans, Julio Le Parc and Horacio Garcia Rossi" (C8402, C8403, C8404, C8405; the Spanish versions say the same). The encounter itself is corroborated by the gallery: "Alli conocio el Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel... fundado en 1960 y liderado por el artista Julio Le Parc" (C8401). But the ICAA annotation orders this AFTER the 1965 Di Tella prizes and the 1968 MNBA show, which cannot be right: Durante was in Paris by about 1960 and is recorded as an invited participant in GRAV's L'Instabilite in 1962 (C8408). The ICAA narrative compresses a decade into the wrong order. The gallery says the same thing about joining more strongly: "Este grupo de artistas, en el que se integro inmediatamente, compartia su interes por investigar las posibilidades de inclusion de los efectos luminicos, cromaticos y de movimiento en las obras de arte" (C8401). But "se integro" is the gallery's word for joining the milieu, and no source read lists Durante as a GRAV member. Every roster source gives the same six GRAV names -- Le Parc, Garcia Rossi, Sobrino, Morellet, Stein, Yvaral (C8406, C8407, C8412) -- and Durante is in none of them. The gallery's own list of the artists he worked with includes Hugo Demarco, who was also never a GRAV member, and the gallery hedges it as "La mayoria de ellos eran miembros del Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel" -- "the majority of them were members" (C8401). So: he worked alongside GRAV members; he was not one. The one source that comes close to claiming membership is dealer copy, and it hedges even as it overreaches; do not repeat "integrated into GRAV" as fact (C8512, C8505).

In 1962 Durante participated as an invited guest in GRAV's L'Instabilite at the Maison des Beaux-Arts, Paris (C8408, fn 214). The same footnote immediately adds: "La participacion de Durante en la exposicion parisina del GRAV de 1962 no aparece documentada en 'Chronologie raisonnee des activites du GRAV', en GRAV 1960-1968. Grenoble, Centre d'Art Contemporain de Grenoble, 1998, pp. 172-179." So the participation is single-source AND flagged as absent from the group's own reasoned chronology. Carry it as claimed-but-undocumented, not as settled. Note the MNBA page's "La inestabilidad, 1964, en el Pabellon del MNBA" (C8410) is a different show -- GRAV's first exhibition in Argentina -- and Durante is not named as a participant there.

He was a finalist for the Premio Braque in 1965 (C8408, fn 214, single-source) and won it in 1966: first prize in painting, for his "suggestive inestabilidades luminosas" (C8401); the winners list confirms "1966: pintura, 1o Armando Durante, 2o Delia Cancela y Pablo Mesejean" (C8408). The prize was instituted by the French embassy (C8401). The reward, per the gallery, was a stay in Paris of eight months financed with 480 francs (C8401). That figure sits uneasily with the thesis's account of the prize scheme: the 1965 edition offered two first prizes of 750 francs a month and two second prizes (under-35s) of 480 new francs over nine months, and the 1966 edition repeated the 1965 scheme with the same amounts (C8408). Durante won a first prize, so the scheme would predict 750 francs a month, not 480; the gallery's "480 francs" may be monthly, may be the second-prize rate misremembered, or may reflect a different arrangement. Unresolved -- record both figures and do not reconcile them silently. What the prize did is agreed: it let him travel to Paris again and settle there, for eight years, until the summer 1974 retreat to Ibiza (C8401).

The 1966 prize work was "Luz, color en movimiento mecanico inestable (con intervencion del espectador)", a box with a mechanism that moved a series of coloured lights projected from inside (C8408, single-source, citing Premio Braque 1966, MNBA, 1966, p. 23). His light boxes in general were based on Vasarely's principles and inspired by the kaleidoscope (C8401). The gallery calls the 1966 Braque "el maximo reconocimiento de su etapa geometrica" (C8401). No dimensions, no surviving example and no museum holding of a specific light box is attested in any source read beyond W01.

The Di Tella claim fails. ICAA, in all four records: he "won the Instituto di Tella's National and International Prizes in 1965" (C8402, C8403, C8404, C8405). Against this, the Moreno thesis (UTDT, 2018), working from the contemporary press and the prize catalogues, lists the 1965 Premio Nacional participants as twelve artists -- "Armando Durante, Leon Ferrari, Eduardo A. MacEntyre, Fernando Maza, Honorio Morales, Federico M. Peralta Ramos, Perez Celis, Rogelio Polesello, Dalila Puzzovio, Carlos Squirru y Carlos Silva, quien se consagrara como ganador de la VI edicion" -- and notes the winning national work was "Thalassios", a circular painting with two squares of different colours creating an optical illusion (C8409). So: Durante was a participant/invitee in the 1965 National prize, and Carlos Silva won it. The thesis footnote in the Braque study agrees: he was "invitado al Premio Nacional Instituto Di Tella" in 1965 (C8408, fn 214). The ICAA "won the National Prize" claim is therefore contradicted and must not be repeated. The "International Prize" half is unattested anywhere read -- no source found confirms or denies a 1965 International prize win, and the thesis covers the National prize only. Carry it as unattested, not as disproved. Correction to leg 3, recorded here rather than silently fixed: leg 3's Part 4 marked the Di Tella claim "CONFIRMED" on the four ICAA records alone, before leg 1's thesis evidence was available; the thesis evidence overturns that confirmation for the National prize (C8552).

ICAA also says he "went on to be acknowledged for his exhibition Materiales, nuevas tecnicas, nuevas expresiones (1968, MNBA)" (C8402). No source read says whether this was a solo show, a group show, or what he showed there; no catalogue, no work list and no review was found. Carry the title, year and venue as attested and nothing more.

The gallery divides his career into two clearly differentiated lines: the geometric, cultivated in his early years, and the conceptual, begun in 1973 and maintained until the end of his life (C8401). "A finales del ano anterior Durante habia decidido alejarse del movimiento cinetico -- detestaba la deriva preciosista que habia tomado -- y orientar su trabajo hacia una linea conceptual" (C8401). Read against the Ibiza summer of 1974, "the end of the previous year" is the end of 1973. The Verona kinetic exhibition of April 1973 therefore sits just before the break: one of the two texts in the CAYC gacetillas was written for that Verona show (C8403), and the other was produced in Ibiza in August 1974 (C8403). The May 1968 events in Paris are the stated frame for the texts' reflections (C8404). In Ibiza in the summer of 1974 he created his photographic series Margot en la playa, shown in the 2018 Funeral Cinetico exhibition (C8401, single-source). No source read names a venue for the Verona 1973 show, and no source read describes the Margot series beyond its title and medium.

Groupe Position was formed after GRAV's disbandment, by Garcia Rossi, Demarco, Asis, Leopoldo Torres Aguero and Armando Durante, "in order to reactivate the circulation of kinetic art" (C8407). The manifesto, quoted in both Rossi versions: "En abril de 1971 nos decidimos a formar el grupo en vista de lo dificil que resulta la lucha individual del artista en una sociedad que lo margina" (C8406; English: "In April 1971, we decided to form a group in light of both how hard the artist's individual struggle is in a society that marginalizes him and of how many new possibilities arise through organic work as a team", C8407). The group's stated programme: share the financing and organisation of exhibitions, create a common fund from sales (5% of members' sales in the English version, C8407), finance a shared workshop/studio with a permanent exhibition, do joint research, hold periodic critiques of work, and incorporate visual arts and architecture (C8406, C8407). The roster is corroborated three ways: Rossi in Spanish and in English (C8406, C8407) and the 1971 group photograph captioned "Grupo Position. Hugo Demarco, Antonio Asis, Armando Durante, Leopoldo Torres Aguero y Horacio Garcia Rossi, 1971" (C8406). The group's show route is given as Brussels, Brescia, Madrid, Seville and then a successful show in Zurich, after which the group dispersed (C8406); Arte al Dia via study #631 adds Paris, Bilbao and Bergamo and a show at Gallery Les Ambassadeurs, Zurich, in 1972, and dates the dissolution to the end of 1972 (C8412, single-source for the month, the dissolution date and the eight-city list). Durante's individual contribution to Position -- what he showed, whether his light boxes or something else -- is not stated in any source read. Note the timing: Position was formed in April 1971 while Durante was in his second Paris stay (1966-1974), and his kinetic period ended at the close of 1973, so his Position years and his kinetic years overlap only in 1971-1973.

In November 1975, the year he decided to move back to his native country, the Centro de Arte y Comunicacion de Buenos Aires (CAYC) dedicated him a large solo show (C8401). The gacetillas promoting it are dated 20 November 1975 (C8402, C8403, C8404) and the show opened on 28 November at the CAYC (C8402, C8403, C8404). The show consisted of portraits of celebrities -- the Pope, presidents, actors, sports figures, musicians -- arranged in a structure viewers could modify, playing with the possibilities of change and values within the piece (C8402). Against that backdrop the exhibition called for a reflection on the value of change in contemporary culture, "a proposal that was in line with the GRAV's sociological interests" (C8402, ICAA annotation, not Durante's words). The gacetillas reproduce two texts by Durante (Verona April 1973; Ibiza August 1974), a reproduction of Cezanne's Basket of Apples (1890-94) and a Bert Stern Marilyn Monroe portrait from the Vogue series, both included in the Buenos Aires show (C8402, C8403); one gacetilla also includes a portrait of the artist (C8404). ICAA's annotations frame the return against the Rodrigazo devaluation of June 1975 and the coming military coup (C8402); the coup itself fell in March 1976, after the show. No source read gives the show's closing date, attendance, sales or critical reception.

After 20 months in Argentina he decided to return to Europe, settling in the small municipality of Argecilla, in the province of Guadalajara, through his friendship with the artist and GRAV colleague Francisco Sobrino; from 1977 Spain became his permanent residence (C8401). ICAA's later gacetilla agrees and sharpens the cause: "After spending twenty months there, the military coup d'etat in 1976 prompted him to return to Europe. He settled in Spain where he continued reinventing his conceptual language in experimental screenprinted works" (C8405). In Spain he continued reinventing his conceptual language and began his passion for screenprinting, a technique in which he produced a large number of works until the day of his death (C8401). He took part in America Latina 76, the CAYC exhibition at the Fundacio Joan Miro in Barcelona, opening February 1977; the work reproduced in the announcing gacetilla is a portrait of Oscar Wilde with a quote in Spanish and French from The Soul of Man under Socialism (1891) (C8405). That is the last dated exhibition participation attested in the sources read before the posthumous 2018 show. No source read names a Spanish gallery, dealer, studio, teaching post or exhibition of his between 1977 and his death, and no source read dates any individual screenprint. The Spanish period -- nineteen years, more than half his working life -- is the least documented part of the biography after the Paris years.

He died in Madrid on 24 December 1996, aged 62 (C8401) -- but the ICAA records print 1934-1997 (C8402, C8403, C8404, C8405), the date study #631 carried, so the death year is in conflict and this study does NOT settle it; the 1996 date is the better attested of the two because it gives an exact day, a place and an age, while 1997 is repeated boilerplate in ICAA topic headings. No obituary, death certificate or third source was found to break the tie (C8413). No source read gives a cause of death or any circumstance beyond place and date.

## M1 -- The light box: a rotating disc behind a striated screen

The one fully attested kinetic work, W01, Verde, rojo, blanco en movimiento vertical opuesto e inestable, 1964, is a motorised light box, 130 x 130 x 30 cm, in the Museo Moderno, Buenos Aires (C8454, C8455, C8456). The front view, inspected as the work itself: a square white-framed box whose face is a dark, vertically striated screen; behind it, blurred blocks of green, red and white drift in a loose grid -- the "opposite vertical movement" reads as a soft moire shimmer rather than sharp geometry (C8454, C8554). Two rear views show the mechanism: a large circular disc (nearly the full 130 cm width) mounted on a wooden frame inside the open back of the box, with a small motor and belt/pulley at its hub -- the disc evidently rotates behind the striated screen to produce the unstable image (C8454). The mechanism is the whole work: a painted disc that never stops turning, a screen that never lets you see it clearly, and a motor that never lets the image settle. The 1966 Braque prize work was the same kind of object at a smaller scale of description: "Luz, color en movimiento mecanico inestable (con intervencion del espectador)", a box with a mechanism that moved a series of coloured lights projected from inside (C8408). The light box is not a picture of movement; it is a machine for making an image that cannot hold still.

**Takeaway:** the screen is the work and the disc is the secret. A piece that uses Durante should hide a rotating painted disc behind a striated screen and let the viewer see only the shimmer it throws forward; a disc shown openly is a wheel, and a screen with nothing behind it is a curtain.

## M2 -- The kaleidoscope: an image that never repeats

His "famous light boxes" were based on the principles of Victor Vasarely, inspired by the kaleidoscope and its generation of different unstable images (C8401). The kaleidoscope is the right model for what the boxes do: a small set of coloured elements -- green, red, white in W01 -- turned by a slow rotation into an image that is never the same twice, because the disc is always at a new angle behind the screen. The instability is the point, not a defect: the title of the 1964 box carries "inestable" as its last word, and the 1966 prize work was praised for its "inestabilidades luminosas", luminous instabilities (C8454, C8401). Nothing in the box is fixed -- not the colour blocks, not their positions, not the moire the screen makes of them -- and the viewer who watches for a minute sees a different picture from the viewer who watched a minute before. The kaleidoscope principle is also the limit of the device: it generates variation from a fixed set, and it never invents a new element; the box can surprise you, but only with the colours it already holds.

**Takeaway:** variation from a fixed set is the mechanism. A piece should take three or four colours and a slow rotation and generate an image that never repeats itself, the same elements recombined at every angle; a random scatter is not a kaleidoscope, and a fixed picture is not unstable.

## M3 -- The spectator completes the event

The gallery's theoretical gloss on the light boxes: the importance of the work lies not in the material result but in the situation it engenders; the spectator completes the work with "the event" (el acontecimiento) (C8401, gallery prose, single-source, not Durante's own words). The 1966 work's title itself carries "(con intervencion del espectador)" -- with the intervention of the spectator (C8408). The participation is evidenced in both the 1966 light box and the 1975 CAYC installation, not only in the GRAV years: the CAYC celebrity portraits were "arranged in a structure that viewers could modify, playing with the possibilities of change and values within the confines of the piece" (C8402). The gallery puts it as a rule: "En ellas, el espectador se convierte en parte fundamental de la obra... De este modo la pieza de arte deja de ser meramente pasiva" -- in them the spectator becomes a fundamental part of the work, and so the piece stops being merely passive (C8401). The spectator's role is not to press a button but to be present and to change something: to stand in front of the box and let the image happen to them, or to reach into the portrait structure and move a face. The work is unfinished until someone is there, and it is finished differently every time someone is there.

**Takeaway:** the viewer is a part of the machine, not its audience. A piece should leave its image unfinished until the viewer acts -- standing, watching, moving a piece -- and the action must change the work, not merely trigger it; a button that starts an animation is a switch, not a completion.

## M4 -- The grid that vibrates: discs, triangles, circles on a strict lattice

Observed in the three viewed untitled geometric works from the 2018 press release (C8451, C8554). W02: a wide black-ground composition, a grid of black discs, each with a small cream/white centre, set on cream trapezoid or diamond-shaped tiles that tilt at slightly varying angles, row by row; the tilting tiles against the strict disc grid produce a mild optical vibration -- Vasarely-derived op-art, consistent with Durante's geometric period. W03: a square, flat, hard-edge composition, a tight grid of right triangles in red, orange and purple/violet, the triangle orientations alternating so the surface reads as a flickering checkerboard of diagonals. W04: a square, flat composition, rows of red discs on a blue ground, each disc overlapped by a green semicircle/crescent, producing a regular interlocking circle pattern with a strong red/green/blue contrast. None of the three has a title, date or medium in its only source, and none is assigned one here; what they show is the mechanism -- a strict lattice, one small variation repeated across it, and the vibration that comes from the variation, not from the lattice. The disc grid tilts its tiles; the triangle grid alternates its diagonals; the circle grid overlaps its crescents. In each case the grid is the rule and the small repeated difference is the work.

**Takeaway:** one strict grid, one small repeated difference. A piece should lay a regular lattice of discs or triangles and vary one thing across it -- a tilt, an orientation, an overlap -- and let the repetition do the shimmering; a grid with no variation is wallpaper, and a variation with no grid is noise.

## M5 -- The break: quitting kinetic art at the end of 1973

At the end of 1973 Durante "had decided to move away from the kinetic movement -- he detested the precious drift it had taken -- and orient his work toward a conceptual line" (C8401). The Verona kinetic exhibition of April 1973 sits just before the break, and the Ibiza text of August 1974 just after it (C8403). The break is the most personal fact in his file: the only place where a source records what he felt about his own movement, and the feeling is disgust -- "la deriva preciosista", the precious drift. Kinetic art had become ornament, a pretty surface, and he left it. The conceptual line he turned to lasted until the end of his life (C8401): the CAYC portraits of 1975, the Wilde screenprint of 1976, the screenprinting of the Spanish years. Nothing in the sources says he ever built another light box after 1973. The break is therefore also a dating tool: a Durante light box is a work of 1973 or earlier, and a Durante screenprint is a work of the conceptual line, 1973 or later. A study that treats him as a lifelong kinetic artist has missed the point of his career; he was a kinetic artist for thirteen years and a conceptual artist for twenty-three.

**Takeaway:** the refusal is the mechanism. A piece that uses Durante's break should start as a kinetic machine and then deliberately abandon its own movement -- the rotation slowing, the shimmer draining out, the box going still -- and let the stillness be the statement; a piece that keeps spinning forever has not understood him.

## M6 -- The modifiable portrait structure: the value of change

The CAYC solo show of November 1975 consisted of "a collection of portraits of celebrities -- the Pope, presidents, actors, sports figures, musicians -- arranged in a structure that viewers could modify, playing with the possibilities of change and values within the confines of the piece" (C8402). Framed by the CAYC as a reflection on the value of change (valor de cambio) in contemporary culture, in line with GRAV's sociological concerns (C8402). The mechanism is a structure, not a picture: a set of faces held in an arrangement that the viewer is invited to re-arrange, so that the value of each face -- the Pope next to a footballer, a president next to a musician -- changes with its neighbours. The portraits themselves are not the work; the structure is, and the structure is only complete when a viewer has moved something in it. No individual portrait in the installation is titled in the gacetillas except the Marilyn Monroe portrait (W08); no dimensions, no surviving photographs of the installed structure found; no title for the installation as a whole is given in any source. The mechanism survives without the pictures: faces in a frame the viewer can re-order, and the meaning of each face changing with its place.

**Takeaway:** the arrangement is the work, not the faces. A piece should give the viewer a set of portraits in a structure they can re-order, and let the meaning of each portrait change with its neighbours; a fixed gallery of faces is a poster, and a shuffle with no structure is a slot machine.

## M7 -- Appropriation as portrait: Monroe, Wilde, Cezanne

The portraits in the 1975 show were not painted by Durante. The GT-584 gacetilla reproduces a portrait of Marilyn Monroe from Bert Stern's series for Vogue, which formed part of Durante's Buenos Aires exhibition (C8452) -- Stern's photograph as appropriated and re-presented by Durante within the W07 installation, not an original Durante photograph. The gacetilla also reproduces Cezanne's Basket of Apples (1890-94), included in the show (C8402, C8403). The 1976 America Latina 76 work reproduced a portrait of Oscar Wilde with a quotation in Spanish and French from Wilde's 1891 essay The Soul of Man under Socialism (C8405). The pattern is consistent: Durante takes a famous image -- a photograph, a painting, a portrait -- and puts it into a new structure with a new text, and the new structure is the work. The appropriation is not theft and not homage; it is a way of asking what a famous face is worth when you can move it, quote it, or put it next to a president. The ICAA gacetilla PDFs themselves could not be downloaded -- the ICAA site is Cloudflare-blocked to direct fetching -- so the Monroe and Wilde reproductions were not viewed in this run, and W08 and W09 stay PROSE ONLY (C8452, C8453).

**Takeaway:** a famous face is material, not a subject. A piece should take a recognisable portrait and put it into a structure where the viewer can move it, quote it, or set it beside an unlike face, and let the re-placement do the thinking; a portrait shown as it was is a reproduction, not a work.

## M8 -- Screenprint as late language: the conceptual line in Spain

From 1977, with Spain as his permanent residence, Durante "continued reinventing his conceptual language" and "began his passion for screenprinting, a technique in which he produced a great number of works until the day of his death" (C8401). The ICAA annotation agrees: he "settled in Spain where he continued reinventing his conceptual language in experimental screenprinted works" (C8405). The named late example is the Oscar Wilde portrait with a quotation from The Soul of Man under Socialism, reproduced in the CAYC gacetilla GT-685 (November 1976) (C8453) -- a portrait plus a text, printed, repeatable, portable. Screenprinting fits the conceptual line exactly: it is a technique of repetition and distribution, not of unique objects; it lets a portrait and a quotation travel together, in multiples, to Barcelona, to Madrid, to wherever the next show is. No individual late serigraph is titled, dimensioned or illustrated in any source found except W09, which slightly predates the permanent move. The 2018 exhibition included serigraphs among its representative selection (C8401). The late work is therefore a body, not a set of titles, and this study catalogues it as a body (W14). What can be said about it is its logic: the same face, the same text, printed again and again, each print the same and each print a new occasion for the words to be read.

**Takeaway:** print the portrait and the text together, and print them many times. A piece should pair a face with a quotation in a repeatable printed form, and let the repetition be the point -- the same image, the same words, again and again, each time read by someone new; a unique painted portrait is the old line, not the late one.

## The aesthetic logic: the box that will not hold still, and the hand that walks away from it

Durante's work has one subject and it is not the light box or the screenprint. It is the image that refuses to stay put, and the artist who refuses to stay with the image. The light boxes are the first half: a disc turning behind a screen, a kaleidoscope generating an image that never repeats, a spectator who completes the event by being there. Everything in the Paris years is a way of making an image that cannot hold still -- the moire shimmer of W01, the tilting tiles of W02, the alternating diagonals of W03, the overlapping crescents of W04, the luminous instabilities that won the Braque in 1966. And then, at the end of 1973, he walks away from the whole apparatus, because it has become precious -- a pretty surface, a drift -- and spends the remaining twenty-three years on the second half: portraits that the viewer can move, faces that are borrowed rather than painted, texts that are printed rather than written. The two halves are not a contradiction. They are the same refusal, applied twice: first he refuses the fixed image, then he refuses the machine that makes the unfixed image. What remains, in both halves, is the viewer. In the light box the viewer stands and watches the image change; in the portrait structure the viewer reaches in and changes the image; in the screenprint the viewer reads the words again on a new copy. The work is never finished on the wall. It is finished, differently each time, by whoever is standing in front of it. That is why his forms are so few -- the box, the grid, the portrait, the printed page -- and why they change so completely halfway through: the form is a housing for the refusal, and when the refusal moves on, the housing is discarded. Colour, for him, is a small set turned slowly: green, red, white in the box, red, orange, purple in the triangle grid, red, green, blue in the circle grid. He never needs more colours than the kaleidoscope holds, because the rotation does the work that a bigger palette would do. Subtraction is the design: one disc, one screen, one grid, one face, one text, and the patience to let the viewer finish what the artist started -- and the courage, at the end of 1973, to stop building the machine and start over (C8555).
