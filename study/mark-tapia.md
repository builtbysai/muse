**Relevance:** (1) Marking menus ask what a visual code must look like when the same gesture means different things depending on who is reading it: for the novice the radial menu is fully displayed, for the expert it vanishes and only the ink trail of the mark remains, so the display must encode trust in the reader's memory -- this value is shown, that one is hidden, this stroke is a command -- and the question the Vicente study left open, who decides which marks the reader is allowed to see, is answered here by a timing threshold and by graphical feedback rather than by a fixed legend. (2) The evidence is Tapia and Kurtenbach's UIST '95 paper itself, whose three stated principles are maintaining visual context, hiding unnecessary information, and supporting skill development by graphical feedback, and whose seven refinements -- including the 1/3-second press-and-hold, the labels-only display, and the idealized mark -- are a working system built on the marking-menu mechanism (the ink trail and the 1/3-second threshold are the underlying mechanism, not among the seven refinements), refined from the preferences of about 15 users of marking menus in StudioPaint V3 and ConEd without formal tests of the refinements themselves; the measured speed figure (marks 3.5 times faster than menu selection) comes from the CHI '94 field study of marking menus, not from a test of the refinements. [C37911]

**Doorway:** The 462nd doorway. Takes up the open question from the Kim J. Vicente study: no source read states an explicit rule for marking uncertainty or provenance on a display itself -- a visual code for "this value is trusted, this one is inferred, this sensor is suspect" -- so what must that code look like, and who decides which marks the reader is allowed to see? Tapia answers from the hand's side, and his answer is that the code need not be drawn at all: a 1/3-second press-and-hold threshold decides whether the menu appears, so the display shows everything to the reader who waits and nothing to the reader who moves at once, and the ink trail and the idealized mark are the record of what was done rather than a legend of what might be. The decision is made by time and by behaviour, not by a fixed mark, and the reader is trusted exactly to the extent that they do not ask to be shown. The open question carried forward is the one Tapia's two strands leave unjoined: the grammar that generates ornament and the menu that hides itself never meet in the sources read -- so what happens when the rule being hidden IS the generative rule, and can a shape grammar be taught the way a marking menu is taught, by press-and-wait and by a traceable demonstration, rather than by being written down? [C37912]

## M1 -- The ice-ray split rule: a grammar that reads an ornament and writes it back

**Subject:** A Chinese ice-ray lattice -- a field of irregular polygons that reads as cracked ice, generated inside a starting polygon.

**Visual principle:** Subdivision by near-equal area creates rhythm without repetition; every polygon is a different size and shape, yet the whole holds together because each split preserves the parent's area balance and the stick endpoints land on existing edges, so the lattice is one continuous network of lines, never isolated fragments.

**Computational mechanism:** Stiny's five-rule parametric ice-ray grammar, read end to end, which Tapia 1992 extends parametrically to generate ice-ray lattices; its abstract describes "a parametric shape grammar that generates ice-ray lattices" and "formal, graphical production systems that perform graphical computations with parametric labeled shapes". Rule 1: a triangle over a size constant splits by one stick between two edges into a triangle and a quadrilateral of approximately equal areas, with a dot label placed near the centroid to prevent re-application -- the dot's position is fixed by the ratio of the areas, and its presence is what stops the rule applying to the same triangle more than once. Rules 2-3: a quadrilateral splits into a triangle and a convex pentagon (adjacent edges) or into two convex quadrilaterals (nonadjacent edges). Rule 4: a pentagon splits into a quadrilateral and a pentagon. Rule 5: erase the dot, terminating generation. Stiny's worked example begins from the outside rectangle and applies the rules step by step, each frame bolding the polygon about to split; the seven lattices of his Fig. 3 come from Szechwan towns, 1725-1880, after Dye's catalogue of lattices from 1000 BC to 1900 AD. The dot label is state: it records which polygons have already been split, so the grammar never revisits its own work. Tapia's own 1992 rules are not established by any source read -- only the abstract and the lineage are -- so any piece built here must ground its mechanism in Stiny's grammar as read, with the Tapia extension marked provisional.

**Artistic opportunity:** The lattice grows in front of the viewer, one decision at a time, and the finished field looks hand-cut rather than computed because every split is locally decided. The mechanism is complete in five rules and needs no decoration.

**Takeaway:** Five rules, one dot that remembers, and the ice cracks itself. [C37914]

## M2 -- The third of a second: shown vs hidden, decided by time

**Subject:** A single gesture on a blank surface, and the display's answer to it -- a full radial menu, or nothing but an ink trail.

**Visual principle:** Presence against absence. The same surface carries two opposite states, and the boundary between them is not a place but a duration. The menu, when it appears, is a burst of structure; the ink trail, when it does not, is a single thin line that is the whole record of the act.

**Computational mechanism:** The UIST '95 selection rule, read end to end. Press and hold still for 1/3 of a second and the menu appears; release on the item to select it. Move immediately and no menu appears; an ink trail is drawn instead, and the angle of the mark selects the item on release. The CHI '94 timeline explains why a mark beats even an instant menu: 0.33 seconds press-and-wait plus about 0.15 seconds display plus reaction time -- a mark will always be faster than a menu that immediately pops up, because the menu still has to be displayed and read. The threshold is a single parameter (dwell time) with a binary outcome, and it is fixed at 333 ms in both papers; no source says it adapts to the user. It is the code that decides which marks the reader is allowed to see: below the threshold the system trusts the reader's memory and shows nothing; above it the system shows everything.

**Artistic opportunity:** The piece makes the threshold itself the subject. The viewer learns, by doing, that waiting summons structure and acting hides it -- a rule about revelation that is felt in the hand, not read in a legend. This is the doorway mechanism: the display marks its own uncertainty (wait, and I will show you; move, and I will trust you) in time, not in ink.

**Takeaway:** A third of a second is the whole legend. [C37915]

## M3 -- The ink trail as a record of provenance

**Subject:** The thin line left behind by a mark -- the only trace of a gesture that selected something without ever showing a menu.

**Visual principle:** A line is a history. Its direction, length, and straightness record the decision that was made. A field of old trails is a palimpsest of past selections, denser where the hand returned, empty where it never went.

**Computational mechanism:** In the marking-menu system the ink trail is drawn while the mark is made and is the visible record of the gesture. The 1993 experiments found the ink trail made no discernible difference to stroke shape, and that hidden and marking groups behaved nearly identically -- the trail is not a guide for the hand, it is a record for the eye. No source describes the trail persisting after the gesture, fading, or having a lifetime or decay: in UIST '95 the mark is removed from the screen on recognition and replaced by the idealized mark. A fading trail that accumulates into a palimpsest is therefore an artistic invention for a piece, not a property of the source system, and must be presented as such. The trail is what remains when the menu is hidden: provenance without revelation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that accumulates its own history. Every gesture leaves a mark that outlives the act, so the surface becomes a map of what the viewer has done -- and, by its empty regions, of what they have not. The aesthetic is forensic: the beauty is in the accumulation, not in any single stroke.

**Takeaway:** The menu hides; the trail remembers. [C37916]

## M4 -- The idealized mark: recognition answered as correction

**Subject:** A wobbly human stroke and the clean canonical stroke the system answers with, held on screen until the command completes.

**Visual principle:** Contrast between the irregular and the ideal. The human mark is noisy, hesitant, off-axis; the system's answer is straight, centered, and certain. Placed side by side -- or the second drawn over the first -- the pair shows the gap between intention and execution, and the system's correction closes it.

**Computational mechanism:** UIST '95 Fig. 6, known from caption plus OCR (image not inspected): (a) the mark a user draws to trigger a command; (b) the feedback given to the user to indicate how the system interpreted the mark and which command it invoked -- the clean idealized mark plus the invoked command's label, held until the system completes the command, with its size and position based on the user's mark. The mechanism is a feedback loop: classify the input stroke (angle, and for hierarchical marks the sequence of angles), replace it with the canonical form for that class, and hold the canonical form visible for the duration of the command it invoked. The hold duration is tied to the command, not to a timer: the display keeps the evidence up until the work is done. Idealized marks appear whenever the user makes a selection from the menu as well, not only after marks. If the stroke is too ambiguous to classify, the source system gives no such answer -- the failure is visible as an absence of correction.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about being understood. The viewer draws roughly and the system answers precisely, and the answer is not a correction to be ashamed of but a clean drawing to look at. The pleasure is in the transformation -- noise in, geometry out -- and in the held moment when the ideal mark stays up, like a receipt for the gesture.

**Takeaway:** The system answers a wobble with a straight line, and holds it up until the work is done. [C37917]

## M5 -- The zigzag mark: a path through a hierarchy

**Subject:** One continuous stroke that bends at the center of each menu it passes through, selecting a command buried several levels deep.

**Visual principle:** A single line that changes direction is a path through a tree. Each bend is a choice; the straight segments are the distance between choices. The zigzag reads as a signature and as a map at once -- the shape of the stroke IS the address of the command.

**Computational mechanism:** Hierarchical marking menus, as read in the 1993 experiments and UIST '95. A mark's successive segments select successive levels; the path is traced by lines joining parent-menu centers. Measured reliability limits from the Kurtenbach thesis: 4-item menus reliable to depth 4, 8-item menus to depth 2, and 12-item menus reliable to depth 2 as well -- the error rate rises significantly only when depth exceeds 2, the same threshold as for 8-item menus. Breadth and depth are an even tradeoff: 8,2 = 64 items in two strokes, about as fast as 4,3 = 64 items in three. The state is the current node in the tree; each segment moves one level deeper; the release commits the whole path. The InterCHI '93 hierarchical marking-menus paper itself was not opened in this study, so nothing about it is asserted beyond its existence and its relationship to thesis Chapter 5.

**Artistic opportunity:** The viewer draws a route and the route is the work. A field of zigzags, each one a different path through the same tree, is a drawing of decisions -- and the reliability limits give the piece its tension, because a path that is too deep or too wide starts to fail, and failure is visible as a stroke that does not resolve.

**Takeaway:** The stroke is the address. [C37918]

## M6 -- The donut: marks told apart by length, not angle

**Subject:** Concentric rings, and a stroke whose length -- not its direction -- decides what it means.

**Visual principle:** Radial symmetry with a new variable. Angle is freed from meaning, so the composition can be purely concentric; the discriminating dimension is reach -- how far the hand travels from the center. Short marks stay near the core, long marks cross the rings, and the field becomes a set of radii rather than a set of directions.

**Computational mechanism:** The "donut menu" proposal from the 1993 paper: a menu divided into concentric circles rather than sectors, where each concentric circle corresponds to a different command, so the corresponding marks are discriminated by length rather than angle -- credited in the paper's footnote to a suggestion by Prof. John W. Senders. This is a genuine alternative encoding within the same gesture vocabulary: same press, same stroke, but the classifier reads radial distance instead of angle. The parameters are the number of rings, the radius of each ring boundary, and the tolerance band around each boundary. It is a proposal, not a tested system -- the paper offers it as a direction worth evaluating, and no deployment or measurement of it was found in any source read -- so any piece built on it must say so.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that inverts the familiar radial menu. Viewers expect direction to matter; here only distance does, and the surprise is legible in the drawing: a short stroke and a long stroke in the same direction mean different things. The rings give the composition a target-like stillness that the zigzag's angles do not.

**Takeaway:** Same gesture, different reading: reach, not direction. [C37919]

## M7 -- Labels that violate their wedges

**Subject:** The text labels of a radial menu, drawn outside the pie slices they belong to, arranged in graphical symmetry around a compass star at the center.

**Visual principle:** Legibility over geometry. The labels refuse to be squeezed into their wedges; instead they sit where they can be read, and the symmetry of their arrangement -- not the wedge boundaries -- is what holds the menu together. The compass star at the center anchors the eight directions without drawing the slices.

**Computational mechanism:** Four of the seven UIST '95 refinements, read end to end: labels-only display (drop the pie slices, keep the labels); violating pie wedges (let labels cross wedge boundaries); graphical symmetry of labels; and the compass-star menu center (part of the "use eight item menus, compass star center" refinement). The rule is a layout constraint: place each label at its direction from the center, at a radius where it does not collide with its neighbors, and let symmetry -- not containment -- assign labels to directions. The center star is a fixed anchor, independent of the items. This is a display rule, not a generative system on its own -- it needs a host piece to serve, which is why it is held rather than prompted.

**Artistic opportunity:** A radial composition that is readable without being sliced. The labels become the ornament: eight words arranged like the points of a star, with the star itself as the only geometry.

**Takeaway:** Symmetry, not containment, assigns a label to its direction. [C37920]

## M8 -- Hiding the parent: context preserved by omission

**Subject:** A submenu that appears alone, its parent menu gone, the underlying work still visible behind it.

**Visual principle:** Occlusion as a cost. Every menu that appears covers the work beneath it; hiding the parent menu is the cheapest way to keep the work visible. The display is a negotiation between the tool and the thing being worked on, and omission is the tool's way of stepping aside.

**Computational mechanism:** The UIST '95 refinement "hiding parent menus", motivated by the ConEd piano-roll evidence: a delete mark leaves the speech-event data visible, while the popped-up pie menu obscures a large portion of the underlying data. The rule as the paper states it: when a submenu is selected, close the parent menu and leave only the "center hole" graphic of the parent menu, and restore the parent if the user backs out by pausing on the central hole. The state is kept; only its rendering is suppressed. The UIST '95 paper says only that the centers of the parent menu items are connected by lines, so the path to a selection is emphasized; any claim about animated submenu growth belongs to Kurtenbach, Moran and Buxton (1994), "Contextual Animation of Gestural Commands", Computer Graphics Forum 13(5), pp. 305-314 (UIST '95 ref. [8]), which was not read in this study, and is not asserted here as a UIST '95 finding. As with M7, this is a display rule that needs a host system; it is held, not prompted.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece in which the interface apologizes for itself. The work surface is the subject and the controls are guests that leave as soon as they have served.

**Takeaway:** The cheapest way to keep the work visible is to remove the tool. [C37921]

## M9 -- The two-way transition: experts return to the menu

**Subject:** A hand that has learned the marks, going back to the menu to remember -- and then returning to the marks.

**Visual principle:** A cycle, not a ladder. The display alternates between full revelation and bare gesture, and the alternation itself has a rhythm: long stretches of invisible marks, punctuated by brief reappearances of the menu. The piece's texture is the ratio of shown to hidden over time.

**Computational mechanism:** The CHI '94 field study, read end to end. Marks were used nearly 100% of the time with practice, but usage dipped after lay-offs, and the dips shrank with experience; the transition to using marks is not one way, and expert users still switch back to menus to refresh their memory of menu layout. The state variable is fluency: it rises with use and decays with disuse. There is no threshold variable for fluency stated in any source -- the "threshold at which the menu returns" is model language, not a measured rule -- and the 333 ms press-and-wait threshold is fixed, never shortened by fluency. The 3.5x speed advantage of marks over menus is the pressure that drives the cycle: the user wants the speed, but cannot hold the memory without the display. The third ConEd user in the same study made 16,026 selections in 36 hours and finished 3.66 hours sooner by marking than by using radial menus that popped up immediately -- the comparison is against instant menus, and the study file keeps that explicit.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about forgetting. Fluency is a resource that drains when the viewer is away and refills with practice, and the display breathes with it -- full when the viewer is a stranger, empty when they are fluent, full again when they have been gone. The viewer watches their own memory become visible.

**Takeaway:** Learning a mark is a cycle, not a climb. [C37922]

## M10 -- The crib-sheet: press-and-wait summons the lesson

**Subject:** A small sheet of marks for the current context, summoned by pressing and waiting, and an animated demonstration -- a pigtail-delete -- that the user can trace to invoke the command.

**Visual principle:** Teaching in place. The lesson does not live in a manual somewhere else; it appears exactly where the hand already is, shows the gesture, and lets the hand follow it. The figure is a filmstrip of a single act, and tracing it is both learning and doing.

**Computational mechanism:** Kurtenbach thesis Chapter 6, read in part. The crib-sheet (Fig. 6.11: "In a selection", the word "Tea" circled, functions Delete / Reselect / Move / Pen / Demo / Dismiss) is summoned by press-and-wait for the current selection context. The animated annotated demonstration is a six-panel figure (Fig. 6.12): panel (1) the user presses the delete icon; panels (2)-(4) the animated demonstration of the pigtail with its annotation, "A pigtail deletes the selected objects"; panel (5) the user traces along the example mark to invoke the function; panel (6) the completed state, with crib-sheet and animation gone. The animation proper is panels (2)-(4), not all six. The animation freezes when a user begins drawing a mark, so the user can trace the animated mark -- tracing is the input, and the demonstration is the control. The state is the current context (what is selected); the trigger is the same press-and-wait as M2; the mechanism is demonstration-as-input.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that teaches itself. The viewer never leaves the surface to learn it; the surface shows how, and the showing is the doing. The six-panel filmstrip gives the piece its own tempo -- a small animation that is also an invitation.

**Takeaway:** The lesson is the control. [C37923]

## Mechanism note -- the GEdit strand: drawing the rule instead of typing it

Tapia's second strand is the one this study cannot build from, and the reason is worth stating plainly. The 1999 abstract, available in the Crossref metadata for DOI 10.1068/b260059, says: "Shape grammars specify a mechanism for performing recursive shape computations. A general paradigm is established for a computer implementation supporting this computation in the algebras of points and lines in two dimensions (U02 and U12). The guiding principles and the actual implementation are described." The secondary literature, verified verbatim in Li (2018), says Tapia's GEdit "allowed users to specify shapes and rules by drawing them rather than by typing text", and that the implementation "was notable because it supported not only emergence and generality but also visual interaction". The thesis is described in later literature as the source of GEdit, with shapes represented as images (PICT files). That is the whole verified mechanism: shapes and rules specified by drawing, a standalone interpreter, the next shapes offered to pick from. No rule-level mechanism can be stated honestly from that -- the 1999 full text is paywalled, the 1996 thesis has no copy found, and the GEdit UI reprint (McKay et al. Fig. 2) was known from its caption only. Two details must not be asserted as verified: the phrase "the drawing is the computation" is a gloss, not a verified quotation from Tapia or from the secondary sources checked, and the "Macintosh (LISP)" implementation detail rests on secondary descriptions that were not re-verified verbatim, so it is stated only as "described in secondary literature as a Macintosh-based implementation". No prompt in this study is built from GEdit; the strand is held for a later leg with image access to the GEdit UI. The name must also stay clean: Tapia's GEdit is a shape-grammar interpreter, and Kurtenbach and Buxton's 1991 GEdit -- "Issues in combining marking and direct manipulation techniques", UIST '91, pp. 137-144, a toy graphical editor in which shorthand and proof-reader marks create and move squares, circles, and triangles -- is a different system sharing a name. [C37924]

## The arc: from generating ornament, to hiding the rule, to drawing it

Tapia's arc is one question asked in two media. In the first phase (1992) the question is generative: a grammar reads an existing ornamental language -- Chinese ice-ray lattices, catalogued by Dye from 1000 BC to 1900 AD -- and writes rules that regenerate it, with a dot label as the grammar's memory of its own work. In the second phase (1993-1995, with Kurtenbach and Buxton) the question turns to the hand: a gesture IS a rule application, and the system is about when the rule is shown versus when it is hidden, with the 1/3-second threshold as the hinge and the field study as the proof that the hidden form is worth learning -- marks 3.5 times faster on average, 7 times for one user, 4 times for the other, against a menu that still had to be displayed and read. In the third phase (1996-1999) the two strands are theorized together as representation, computation, presentation, and selection -- how a grammar is stored, run, shown, and chosen from -- and the thesis becomes a tool in which shapes and rules are drawn rather than typed. Three constants run through all three phases. First, the rule is the object and the image is its residue: the lattice is what the five rules leave behind, the menu is what the threshold leaves up, the next shape is what the grammar offers. Second, the mark is always both an action and a record: the ink trail selects and remembers, the idealized mark corrects and receipts, the dot label splits and prevents re-splitting. Third, the system never shows everything at once: the grammar shows one polygon at a time, the menu shows itself only to the reader who waits, the interpreter shows only the next shapes -- and the showing is always governed by a rule, never by decoration. [C37925]

## The aesthetic logic

Tapia's aesthetic is the shown and the hidden on the same surface. The beauty is in a rule you can watch deciding: a polygon bolded before it splits, a menu blooming after exactly a third of a second, a wobbly stroke answered by a straight one, a dot label erased when its work is done. Nothing is explained in a legend; the system is on the surface, where the viewer can see it and act on it, or it is deliberately absent, and the absence is the system. The palette is restraint itself -- ink on paper white for the lattice, a single luminous line on a dark field for the gesture -- because the mark must be readable as a rule's state, and decoration would break the reading. Slowness is not a mood here; it is the price of the question, paid in the third of a second before a menu appears, the beats before a lattice finishes, the lay-off before a fluent hand forgets -- and the honest piece shows the price rather than hiding it. [C37926]
