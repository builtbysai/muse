**Site:** Antonio Francesco Gramsci, born at Ales, Sardinia, on Thursday 22 January 1891, and died in Rome on Tuesday 27 April 1937, aged 46, and he is the Italian Marxist philosopher, journalist and politician, the founder and one-time leader of the Communist Party of Italy, the author of the Prison Notebooks (Quaderni del carcere) and the Letters from Prison (Lettere dal carcere), the son of Francesco Gramsci (1860-1937), a low-level official, and Giuseppina Marcias (1861-1932), of a Sardinian landowning family, of Arbereshe descent, the family name an Italianised form of Gramshi, and he won a scholarship to the University of Turin in 1911, studied philology under Matteo Bartoli, joined the Italian Socialist Party in late 1913, wrote for Il Grido del Popolo and the Turin Avanti!, co-founded the weekly L'Ordine Nuovo in spring 1919, founded the Communist Party of Italy at Livorno on Friday 21 January 1921, became its general secretary in summer 1924, was elected deputy for the Veneto on Saturday 24 May 1924, was arrested in Rome on Monday 8 November 1926, was sentenced in 1928 to twenty years, four months and five days, was held at Turi di Bari as prisoner 7047, and wrote the Prison Notebooks from Friday 8 February 1929 to 1935, about 3,000 pages in 33 notebooks by the critical edition's count, and died of a cerebral haemorrhage suffered on the evening of Sunday 25 April 1937, days after his sentence expired, and his Letters from Prison were published in 1947 and won the Viareggio Prize, and his Notebooks appeared in a six-volume thematic edition 1948-1951 under Palmiro Togliatti, and in the Gerratana critical edition in 1975, and he is, by the verdict quoted by Wikipedia, one of the most cited Italian authors, certainly the most cited Italian Marxist ever, and one of the most celebrated Marxist philosophers of the twentieth century, and this file is plain ASCII by house rule, so the Italian words in it are written without their accents here.

## M1. Hegemony as consent manufactured daily

Rule by "intellectual and moral leadership", not by force alone, and the consent is the mechanism, because a class rules when its outlook becomes the common sense of society, renewed every day through schools, churches, newspapers, associations and the family, and the renewal is the piece, and the piece must show the same slogan appearing in a schoolbook, a sermon and a newspaper on the same morning, a crowd nodding along to values that serve someone else, and then consent visibly thinning in a crisis until the batons come out, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 12, gives the two functions of the dominant group, "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life" and "the apparatus of state coercive power", and the consent comes first and the coercion waits behind it. The watchable behaviour is a crowd nodding along to values that serve someone else, the same slogan in three places on one morning, and consent thinning until the batons come out.

**Takeaway:** make consent daily and the batons the exception, and the piece should show the slogan arriving before the soldier does.

## M2. The war of position

In the West, revolution cannot be a frontal assault, because the state is protected by civil society, and the slowness is the mechanism, because the capture must be trench by trench, stronghold by stronghold, before any bid for power, and the trench line is the piece, and the piece must show a map of positions filling in one by one, a trench line advancing a few yards a day while the frontal assault waits, and the contrast with 1917 Russia, where civil society was "primordial and gelatinous" and the assault worked, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 238-239, is the source, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia and in Ashworth. The watchable behaviour is a map filling in one position at a time, the assault waiting while the trenches advance.

**Takeaway:** advance the trench, not the charge, and the piece should make the waiting visible.

## M3. The outer ditch

The state is only the outer ditch, and behind it stands "a powerful system of fortresses and earthworks", civil society, and the ditch is the mechanism, because the ditch can be stormed and nothing changes, and the nothing-changing is the piece, and the piece must show the government building taken and the schools, churches and newspapers still standing behind it, the camera pulling back from the captured ditch to reveal the fortresses ranged behind it, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 238, is the source. The watchable behaviour is the ditch stormed and nothing changing, the camera pulling back to the fortresses behind it.

**Takeaway:** take the ditch and find the fortresses, and the piece should pull the camera back.

## M4. The organic intellectual

Intellectuals defined by social function, not by education, and the function is the mechanism, because every class produces its own thinkers from its own ranks, managers and engineers for the bourgeoisie, organisers from the shop floor for the workers, who articulate what the class feels but cannot say, and the articulation is the piece, and the piece must show a worker who stops the line and explains, in the language of the shop, what the whole factory already knew, a new vocabulary spreading outward from one workplace, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 5-6: "Every social group, coming into existence on the original terrain of an essential function in the world of economic production, creates together with itself, organically, one or more strata of intellectuals". The watchable behaviour is a worker stopping the line to say what the factory already knew, the vocabulary spreading outward.

**Takeaway:** let the thinker grow from the class, and the piece should start on the shop floor, not in the library.

## M5. "All men are intellectuals"

Everyone thinks, but not everyone has the social function of an intellectual, and the hidden capacity is the mechanism, because the division of manual and intellectual labour hides a universal capacity, and the discovery is the piece, and the piece must show a room of people told they cannot think discovering they already do, the "philosophical event" Haider quotes from the Notebooks, a mass of people led "to think coherently and in the same coherent fashion about the real present world", because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 9: "All men are intellectuals, one could therefore say: but not all men have in society the function of intellectuals". The watchable behaviour is a room told it cannot think discovering that it already does.

**Takeaway:** the capacity is universal and the function is not, and the piece should stage the discovery.

## M6. Common sense vs good sense

Common sense (senso comune) is the fragmentary, uncritical set of beliefs people inherit and treat as eternal truths, superstition, folklore, religion, and inside it sits a "healthy nucleus" of good sense, practical, realistic attitudes that can be made coherent, and the nucleus is the mechanism, because the work is to renovate common sense, not to denounce the believer, and the renovation is the piece, and the piece must show a proverb that contradicts the speaker's own conduct, the same person holding two opposite beliefs and acting on the practical one, the healthy nucleus glinting inside the superstition, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 328, gives the "healthy nucleus", "more coherent and unitary", and p. 419 the fragmentary consciousness. The watchable behaviour is a proverb contradicted by its speaker's own conduct, the practical belief winning.

**Takeaway:** find the healthy nucleus inside the superstition, and the piece should let the practical belief win.

## M7. Contradictory consciousness

Ordinary people live with beliefs inherited from above that their own practical conduct contradicts, and the contradiction is the mechanism, because the task is to work on the contradiction, not to denounce the believer, and the working is the piece, and the piece must show a worker who votes for the boss's party and strikes against the boss's factory in the same week, the split made visible as two voices in one mouth, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 326, is the source. The watchable behaviour is one mouth speaking two voices, the vote and the strike in the same week.

**Takeaway:** work the contradiction, do not denounce the believer, and the piece should let both voices speak.

## M8. The historic bloc

A composite of distinct class and social forces joined politically and culturally under one hegemony, the economic base and its worldview fused into one durable coalition, and the fusion is the mechanism, because the bloc holds together so long as one story binds its unlikely allies, and the binding is the piece, and the piece must show northern industrialists, southern landowners, priests and teachers holding together because one story binds them, and the bloc cracking when the story stops paying, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 137, is the source, and the term is borrowed from Georges Sorel. The watchable behaviour is unlikely allies holding together on one story, the bloc cracking when the story stops paying.

**Takeaway:** bind the bloc with one story and watch it crack when the story fails, and the piece should show the crack, not the collapse.

## M9. Passive revolution

Deep economic and structural change carried out from above without a popular political transformation, revolution without the people, the rulers changing the furniture while keeping the house, and the from-above is the mechanism, because the change arrives as administration, not as uprising, and the arrival is the piece, and the piece must show a country modernising at speed while no one is asked, the old rulers reappearing in new uniforms, change arriving as paperwork, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 105-120, is the source, applied to the Risorgimento and suggested for fascism. The watchable behaviour is a country modernising while no one is asked, the old rulers in new uniforms.

**Takeaway:** change the furniture and keep the house, and the piece should show the paperwork, not the barricade.

## M10. The subaltern

Subordinate classes, peasants and workers, excluded from political power and from writing their own history, rebelling periodically but never adequately challenging the dominant classes, and the exclusion is the mechanism, because the subaltern appears in the record only when it riots, and the appearing-only-when-it-riots is the piece, and the piece must show a crowd that enters the history books only in the riot report, a history written from the margins, in fragments, because the centre kept no minutes of their meetings, because Notebook 25 (1934), "On the Margins of History [The History of Subaltern Social Groups]", is the source, and Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 52-120. The watchable behaviour is a crowd that appears in the record only when it riots, the history arriving in fragments.

**Takeaway:** the margins keep no minutes, and the piece should write the history from the fragments.

## M11. The Southern Question

Italy's South as an internal colony of the North, and the political task of allying northern workers with southern peasants, the proletariat winning "the consent of the broad peasant masses" by making their demands its own, and the alliance is the mechanism, because the wealth flows north and the blame flows south, and the reversal of that flow is the piece, and the piece must show a map of Italy with the wealth flowing north and the blame flowing south, and two crowds, factory hands and peasants, learning to stand in the same square, because "Some Aspects of the Southern Question", October 1926, is the source: "The Northern bourgeoisie has subjugated the South of Italy and the Islands, and reduced them to exploitable colonies"; "The Turin communists posed concretely the question of the 'hegemony of the proletariat'". The watchable behaviour is wealth flowing north and blame flowing south, two crowds learning to share one square.

**Takeaway:** ally the workshop and the field, and the piece should put both crowds in the same square.

## M12. The factory council as workers' self-rule

The council as the cell of a new state, grown inside the factory, workers taking over the organisation of production itself, not just bargaining over wages, and the taking-over is the mechanism, because a grievance committee turns, meeting by meeting, into a government, and the turning is the piece, and the piece must show hands that ran the machines now running the rota, the stores and the plan, the committee becoming a government one meeting at a time, because Selections from Political Writings I, p. 77: "institutions which will replace the person of the capitalist in his administrative functions and his industrial power, and so achieve the autonomy of the producer in the factory". The watchable behaviour is a grievance committee turning into a government, meeting by meeting.

**Takeaway:** let the committee become the government, and the piece should count the meetings.

## M13. The Modern Prince

The revolutionary party as Machiavelli's Prince remade as a collective organism, not a person but a body that forms the collective will, and becomes the measure by which acts are judged virtuous or wicked, and the organism is the mechanism, because a crowd stops looking for a hero and starts acting like one body, and the acting-like-one-body is the piece, and the piece must show the party as a laboratory, in Haider's phrase via Sotiris, rather than a general staff, the will forming in the room rather than arriving from above, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 125-133: "The modern prince, the myth-prince, cannot be a real person, a concrete individual. It can only be an organism"; "In men's consciences, the Prince takes the place of the divinity or the categorical imperative" (p. 133). The watchable behaviour is a crowd that stops looking for a hero and starts acting like one body.

**Takeaway:** the Prince is an organism, not a man, and the piece should show the will forming in the room.

## M14. The philosophy of praxis

Marxism as the unity of thought and action, a philosophy that is also politics, tested by whether it builds a new hegemony, not by whether it predicts like a science, and the unity is the mechanism, because the label, borrowed from Antonio Labriola, also served to slip past the prison censor, and the slipping-past is the piece, and the piece must show a theory that changes when it is used, the same sentence working as a slogan in the street and as a cipher in a cell, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 388 ("it is a philosophy which is also politics") and p. 465 ("absolute 'historicism', the absolute secularisation and earthliness of thought"). The watchable behaviour is a theory that changes when it is used, the slogan and the cipher the same sentence.

**Takeaway:** test the theory by using it, and the piece should let the sentence work twice.

## M15. The integral state

The state redefined as political society PLUS civil society, "hegemony protected by the armour of coercion", force and consent in one body, and the one-body is the mechanism, because the policeman and the schoolteacher are two arms of the same body, and the armour shows only when consent fails, and the showing is the piece, and the piece must show the classroom and the barracks as one building with two doors, the armour staying hidden while consent holds and appearing the moment it fails, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 12 and p. 247: "State = political society + civil society (in other words hegemony protected by the armour of coercion)", and pp. 220-221, "moments of crisis of command and direction when spontaneous consent has failed". The watchable behaviour is the armour appearing only when consent fails, the teacher and the policeman as two arms of one body.

**Takeaway:** the armour is the backup, not the body, and the piece should hide it until consent fails.

## M16. Civil society as the terrain

The "private" institutions, schools, churches, associations, newspapers, the family, as the real battlefield, the "sturdy structure of civil society" where hegemony is produced daily, and the daily production is the mechanism, because consent is made in the classroom, the pulpit, the newsstand and the kitchen table, and the making is the piece, and the piece must show a day in the life of consent, each place doing its quiet work, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 238, is the source, and Ashworth: "Consent is produced and reproduced in civil society: churches, schools, newspapers, associations, the family." The watchable behaviour is a day in the life of consent, four rooms doing their quiet work.

**Takeaway:** the battlefield is the private room, and the piece should spend the day there.

## M17. The national-popular

A culture that is both national and of the people, the party and its intellectuals making the "national-popular collective will" by gathering popular common sense and raising it to a worldview, and the raising is the mechanism, because a folk song, a serial novel and a dialect phrase are lifted, without condescension, into a national story, and the lifting is the piece, and the piece must show the lifting done gently, the folk material kept intact while it is raised, because Selections from the Prison Notebooks, p. 131, gives the Modern Prince as the "Jacobin force" that "awakened and organised the national-popular collective will", and the Notebooks' section on popular culture gives the concept of the "national-popular", the popular novel and the operatic conception of life. The watchable behaviour is a folk song, a serial novel and a dialect phrase lifted into a national story, intact.

**Takeaway:** raise the popular without despising it, and the piece should keep the folk material intact.

## M18. Language as hegemony

Language as the medium in which a class rules or resists, who gets to call their speech "language" and whose is dismissed as "dialect", grammar as politics, and the naming is the mechanism, because the Sardinian boy who spoke dialect at home learned, under Bartoli, that linguistic unification is a political act, and the correction is the piece, and the piece must show a child corrected for speaking Sardinian in school, a dialect word carrying a whole politics, translation as "cultural translation", because the Stanford Encyclopedia, section 3.4, and Carlucci, Gramsci and Languages, are the sources. The watchable behaviour is a child corrected for speaking Sardinian, the dialect word carrying the politics.

**Takeaway:** the grammar is the politics, and the piece should let the dialect word speak.

## M19. Folklore as the philosophy of the masses

Folklore, superstitions, proverbs, songs, saints' days, as the uncritical worldview of the "simple" folk, and therefore as the material any hegemony must work on, not despise, and the working-on is the mechanism, because a proverb does the work of a newspaper editorial and a saint's festival holds a village together after the union hall has closed, and the doing-the-work is the piece, and the piece must show the proverb and the festival as load-bearing, not as decoration, because the Stanford Encyclopedia, section 3.4, and "Observations on Folklore" are the sources, and common sense is "a 'philosophy of the popular masses', often born from religion". The watchable behaviour is a proverb doing an editorial's work, a festival holding the village after the hall has closed.

**Takeaway:** the proverb is load-bearing, and the piece should treat it as structure, not decoration.

## M20. Americanism and Fordism

The American factory system, Taylorism, the assembly line, high wages, mass consumption, moral regulation, including "some aspects of the sexual question", as a new way of life, a passive revolution that remakes the worker's body and morals, not just his output, and the remaking is the mechanism, because the line speeds up while the pay packet fattens, and the worker is trained to live cleanly, spend regularly and want the car he builds, and the training is the piece, and the piece must show "Babbitt" as the new man, the car at the end of the line bought by the hands that built it, because Notebook 22 (1934) and Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 279-318, are the sources. The watchable behaviour is the line speeding up while the pay packet fattens, the worker wanting the car he builds.

**Takeaway:** the factory remakes the man, not just the product, and the piece should show the wanting, not just the working.

## M21. Organic crisis

A crisis that is not a passing downturn but a rupture in which the ruling class can no longer lead and the old order loses consent wholesale, the ground on which a war of position becomes possible, and the rupture is the mechanism, because the institutions still stand but are no longer believed, and the standing-without-belief is the piece, and the piece must show parties that win elections and cannot govern, the ground opening under a building that has not yet fallen, because "Observations on Certain Aspects of the Structure of Political Parties in Periods of Organic Crisis" and Selections from the Prison Notebooks, pp. 181-185, on the relations of forces, are the sources. The watchable behaviour is institutions standing but no longer believed, elections won and government impossible.

**Takeaway:** the crisis is the loss of belief, not the fall of the building, and the piece should show the building still standing.

## M22. Caesarism

The "solution" to an organic crisis when no class can win: a strongman who arbitrates between exhausted forces, progressive or reactionary according to the balance, and the arbitration is the mechanism, because the crowd that has stopped believing in parties turns to one man on a balcony, and the turning is the piece, and the piece must show the arbiter arriving as rescue and staying as rule, the balance deciding whether he is progressive or reactionary, because "Caesarism" in the Selections, part 2, is the source. The watchable behaviour is a crowd that stopped believing in parties turning to one man on a balcony, the rescue staying as rule.

**Takeaway:** the arbiter arrives as rescue, and the piece should watch him stay.

## M23. The interregnum

The gap between dying and being born, where the old order cannot live and the new cannot be born, and "a great variety of morbid symptoms appear", and the gap is the mechanism, because the monsters are not the future but its failed delivery, and the symptoms are the piece, and the piece must show symptoms, not monsters, the popular "now is the time of monsters" being Zizek's paraphrase, not Gramsci's, see C10, because the source is a 1930 notebook, quoted by Ashworth via Achcar: "The crisis consists precisely in the fact that the old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear." The watchable behaviour is morbid symptoms appearing in the gap, the old dying and the new unborn.

**Takeaway:** show the symptoms, not the monsters, and the piece should hold the gap open.

## M24. Pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will

The split discipline, see the situation without illusion, act without surrender, borrowed from Romain Rolland and made into the socialist conception of the revolutionary process, and the split is the mechanism, because two dials move in opposite directions, the intellect's dial falling as the will's rises, until, in 1933, the will's dial falls too and only perseverance remains, and the falling is the piece, and the piece must show the two dials, and then the 1933 letter to Tatiana, "Today I no longer think in this way... I no longer see any concrete way out", and the perseverance that remains after the motto is abandoned, because Haider is the source: first used in "Address to the Anarchists", L'Ordine Nuovo, April 1920; repeated in the 1930-32 notes on Machiavelli; in the letter to Carlo, Thursday 19 December 1929 ("I am a pessimist because of intelligence, but an optimist because of will"); and abandoned in 1933, see C9. The watchable behaviour is two dials moving in opposite directions, then the will's dial falling too, and only perseverance remaining.

**Takeaway:** hold both dials, and when the will's falls, keep the perseverance, and the piece should show the dials, not just the slogan.

## M25. Writing under the censor

The Aesopian dodge, writing in code so the prison censor passes the page: "philosophy of praxis" for Marxism, "the Modern Prince" for the party, "fundamental group" for class, and the code is the mechanism, because a page reads as literary criticism and means revolution, and the double reading is the piece, and the piece must show the censor's pencil hovering, then moving on, the real sentence hidden inside the innocent one, because the Stanford Encyclopedia, sections 1.5 and 3.5, is the source: correspondence and Notebooks "read by the prison authorities and subject to censorship, meaning that political references to outside events had to be muted or entirely absent", and Labriola's term "probably used... to evade the prison censor". The watchable behaviour is a page that reads as criticism and means revolution, the censor's pencil moving on.

**Takeaway:** hide the sentence inside the innocent one, and the piece should let the censor pass it.

## M26. The prison notebook as form

Thinking in fragments because the cell allows nothing else, school exercise books, miscellanies, "special" notebooks, notes revised years later, no system, no order, 33 notebooks and about 3,000 pages written between 1929 and 1935, four of them translation exercises, and the fragment is the mechanism, because the notebook is a room with no door, entries arriving out of order, the same idea returning in a different notebook, the plan abandoned and restarted, and the returning is the piece, and the piece must show the rescue chain too, Tatiana, the bank, Moscow, because the Stanford Encyclopedia, section 3, is the source: the Notebooks are "fragmentary with no explicit guide as to how, or in even what order, the contents might be read", and the Selections editors' note: Gramsci "never had the chance to systematically organize them". The watchable behaviour is entries arriving out of order, the same idea returning in another notebook, the plan restarted.

**Takeaway:** the fragment is the form, not the failure, and the piece should let the idea return.

## M27. The undelivered letter

The party eating its own warning, and the eating is the mechanism, because in October 1926 Gramsci wrote to the Soviet Central Committee that its feuding endangered the movement, and Togliatti opened the letter, read it, and decided not to deliver it, and the not-delivering is the piece, and the piece must show an envelope opened, read, and put in a drawer, the sender in prison never knowing whether it arrived, the friendship that "they never completely resolved", because the Stanford Encyclopedia, section 1.4, Wikipedia and Ashworth are the sources, and Gramsci's own reply to Togliatti of Tuesday 26 October 1926 limits the letter: it had been "a requisition against the Oppositions", "our remarks were totally against the Oppositions", see C19. The watchable behaviour is an envelope opened, read, and put in a drawer, the sender never knowing.

## M28. "Stop this brain from functioning"

## Evolution: five phases, and the afterlife

PHASE 1 - SARDINIA, 1891-1911: the southern outsider. Born Thursday 22 January 1891 in Ales, Sardinia, fourth of seven children, see C1, of Francesco Gramsci, a low-level official, and Giuseppina Marcias, of a Sardinian landowning family from Sorgono. In 1898 his father was convicted of embezzlement and imprisoned, and the family fell into destitution and moved to Ghilarza; Antonio left school at eleven and worked two years in the tax office before returning to school. A hunched back and stunted growth, under five feet tall: the family blamed a fall from a servant's arms; in 2011 Pott's disease, a spinal tuberculosis, was suggested instead, see C3. Schooled at Santu Lussurgiu, then the Dettori Lyceum in Cagliari, lodging with his elder brother Gennaro, a former soldier turned militant socialist. Early sympathies were Sardinian autonomist, not socialist: resentment of the mainland's neglect, protectionist policies, and the repression of Sardinian nationalism. In a letter to Giulia Schucht of 6 March 1924 he recalled thinking then that Sardinia needed national independence; he later urged his sister Teresina to teach her children Sardinian. The South as colony, the North as exploiter: the grievance that becomes the Southern Question.

PHASE 2 - TURIN JOURNALISM AND CULTURAL SOCIALISM, 1911-1919. In 1911 he won a scholarship to the University of Turin, sitting the exam at the same time as Palmiro Togliatti, enrolled in the Faculty of Letters, studied philology and linguistics under Matteo Bartoli, with Umberto Cosmo, the Dante scholar, as friend and teacher. Turin was FIAT's city: industrial, unionised, conflictual. He read Antonio Labriola, Rodolfo Mondolfo, Giovanni Gentile, and above all Benedetto Croce. Joined the PSI in late 1913, see C4. Abandoned his studies in early 1915 at 24, poor health and poverty plus political commitment; never took a degree, never completed a planned linguistics thesis, see C21. 1914-1916: writing for Il Grido del Popolo; 1916 co-editor of the Turin Avanti!; the "Sotto la Mole" column and theatre reviews; first public talk 1916, on Romain Rolland, the French Revolution, the Paris Commune, women's emancipation. 1917: co-published the single-issue La Citta futura; December 1917, "The Revolution Against Capital" in Avanti!, hailing the Russian Revolution as proof that will beats economic law. After the August 1917 Turin riots and the arrest of socialist leaders, he became one of Turin's leading socialists, editor of Il Grido del Popolo, secretary of the Turin executive. Early stance: "active neutrality" on the war; "tendentially somewhat Crocean" by his own later admission; socialism as "an integral vision of life", a moral faith, not a historical law. The through-line: culture first, organisation from below, distrust of reform-from-above.

PHASE 3 - L'ORDINE NUOVO AND THE FACTORY COUNCILS, 1919-1920. Spring 1919, see C5: with Togliatti, Angelo Tasca and Umberto Terracini, founded the weekly L'Ordine Nuovo, first a journal of "socialist culture", then the organ of the factory-council movement. The councils: grievance committees turned organs of workers' control during the strikes and occupations of the biennio rosso (1919-1920). Gramsci's theory: the factory as the locus of a new workers' state. The April 1920 Turin general strike, after FIAT set the clocks back for daylight saving without asking the councils, and the September 1920 occupations: the peak, then defeat in spring 1920. Bordiga attacked the councils as syndicalist, Sorel-influenced; Gramsci was left almost alone defending them. In April 1920, in "Address to the Anarchists", he first used Rolland's "pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will". The L'Ordine Nuovo masthead slogan, from its first issue: "Educate yourselves because we will need all your intelligence. Rouse yourselves because we will need all your enthusiasm. Organize yourselves because we will need all your strength." Gobetti, the young liberal, was drawn in: drama critic for L'Ordine Nuovo from January 1921 at Gramsci's invitation, then founder of La Rivoluzione Liberale (1922).

PHASE 4 - PARTY LEADER, 1921-1926. Friday 21 January 1921, Livorno: the communist minority splits from the PSI; the PCd'I is founded, Bordiga first secretary, Gramsci on the central committee, L'Ordine Nuovo becoming the party daily. The party was built from two unequal parts: Bordiga's abstentionist faction, against participation in bourgeois elections, and Gramsci's Turin group. June 1922 to November 1923: Moscow, delegate to the Comintern Executive; meets Julija Schucht, violinist, marries her in 1923; two sons, Delio (1924) and Giuliano (1926, whom he never saw). Late 1923: Vienna, Foreign Bureau, initiates L'Unita, see C18. April 1924: elected deputy for the Veneto; summer 1924: general secretary, replacing Bordiga. January 1926: Lyons congress, the Lyons Theses with Togliatti adopted; united front, national conditions, worker-peasant alliance. October 1926: the letter to the Soviet Central Committee, which Togliatti does not deliver, and the unfinished "Some Aspects of the Southern Question", the first attempted definition of hegemony, the worker-peasant alliance as the social basis of the workers' state, the South as "a great social disintegration". Monday 8 November 1926: arrested. The arc of the phase: from the council as the cell of a new state to the party as the cell of a new state, the Modern Prince in embryo.

PHASE 5 - PRISON, 1926-1937: the Notebooks. Regina Coeli, Ustica (confinement, with Bordiga among others), Milan (isolation, interrogation), then Turi di Bari from June 1928 (prisoner 7047), Formia clinic from 1933, Quisisana clinic, Rome, from August 1935. Sentenced in 1928, see C6, to 20 years, 4 months and 5 days, commuted to 12 years in 1932. The prosecutor's line, in three reported wordings, see C8: "We must stop this brain from functioning for twenty years." Tatiana Schucht, his sister-in-law, became his lifeline: letters, clothes, food, medicine, books; Piero Sraffa, the Cambridge economist, supplied books and periodicals from his own funds and acted as interlocutor with Togliatti; without them, the Notebooks "would not have come to fruition". Permission to write came in 1929; the first notebook is dated Friday 8 February 1929; he wrote until 1935, about 3,000 pages, then mostly correspondence. Early plan (letter to Tatiana, 1927): write something "from a 'disinterested' standpoint, fur ewig", for eternity, on Italian intellectuals, Pirandello's theatre, comparative linguistics, popular literature. The Comintern's 1928 "Third Period" turn, united front abandoned, social democrats denounced as "social fascists", put him at odds with the party line; his criticism brought ostracism from fellow communist prisoners. Health collapsed: teeth fell out, digestive system collapsed, convulsions, vomiting blood, headaches so violent he beat his head against the cell walls. He refused conditional release if it meant renouncing political activity. Sentence expired April 1937; cerebral haemorrhage on the evening of Sunday 25 April 1937; died in the early hours of Tuesday 27 April 1937. Tatiana retrieved the 33 handwritten Notebooks; they were kept secretly in a bank, then smuggled to Moscow and passed to the PCd'I.

POSTHUMOUS AFTERLIFE. Letters from Prison published 1947 (Viareggio Prize). Notebooks in six thematic volumes 1948-1951, edited under Togliatti (with Felice Platone): a Gramsci made acceptable to Stalin's USSR, conflicts with Moscow played down, see C14. The critical edition (Gerratana) 1975 restored the original order; the National Edition began 2007. The late 1950s: Rosario Romeo disputed the Risorgimento-as-failed-bourgeois-revolution thesis, arguing economic conditions could not have permitted a Jacobin bourgeoisie. From the 1960s: read independently of the PCI, British cultural studies (Raymond Williams, Stuart Hall; Hall's "The Great Moving Right Show", Marxism Today, January 1979, first use of "Thatcherism"), Subaltern Studies (Ranajit Guha from 1982; Gayatri Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?", 1988), post-Marxism (Laclau and Mouffe, 1985), neo-Gramscian international relations (Robert Gill), the radical right's "metapolitics" (Alain de Benoist, GRECE, 1968; Sam Francis, 1991). The contested legacy: Togliatti claimed the PCI's post-war practice was congruent with Gramscian thought; Wikipedia records the speculation that Gramsci "would likely have been expelled from his party if his true views had been known, particularly his growing hostility towards Joseph Stalin".

## Aesthetic logic

Gramsci's work is beautiful in the way a prison notebook is beautiful, and that is the point. Taylor's mechanism is the sentence landing like a verdict and the scene freezing on it; Gramsci's is the fragment arriving out of order and the idea returning in a different notebook, and the beauty, where there is any, is in the stubbornness: a man sentenced for thinking who answers with 3,000 pages, a pen that outlasts a prosecutor. His aesthetic is the cell, the exercise book, the censor's pencil, the letter that never arrives, the proverb that does an editorial's work, the dialect word that carries a politics, and his colours are the colours of prison grey and exercise-book blue, the ink black of a smuggled page, the red of L'Unita, the ochre of Sardinian earth, the white of a clinic wall. His motion is the motion of consent thinning, the trench advancing a few yards a day, the page passed to the censor, the envelope put in a drawer, the two dials moving in opposite directions, the bloc cracking when the story stops paying, the idea returning. The piece that follows him should never be monumental or heroic; it should be small, stubborn, and written in a cell, and its one loud moment is the courtroom where the charge is thinking, and its quietest is the hand that keeps writing after the censor's pencil has passed, the pen still moving after the sentence has been read.

**Takeaway:** let the fragment carry the argument and the pen outlast the sentence, and the piece should be small, stubborn, and still writing.

The 33-versus-29 gap is the translation notebooks (4) plus counting method; this study uses 33 (critical edition) and footnotes 29 and 32.
C8. The prosecutor's words, three reported wordings. UNRESOLVED, reported speech only. "We must stop this brain from functioning for twenty years" (Stanford Encyclopedia, citing Pre-Prison Writings p. xxviii; Ashworth); "For twenty years we must stop this brain from functioning" (Wikipedia, citing Hoare and Smith p. lxxxix); "We must stop his brain from working for 20 years" (Britannica). No verbatim trial transcript was cited this leg. This study quotes the Stanford Encyclopedia wording in M28 and notes the variants here.
C9. "Pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will": not Gramsci's coinage. RESOLVED. Rolland's original, in his review of Raymond Lefebvre's The Sacrifice of Abraham, L'Humanite, 19 March 1920. Gramsci first used it in "Address to the Anarchists", L'Ordine Nuovo, April 1920, explicitly attributing it to Rolland. He returned to it in the 1932 notebook and in the letter to Carlo, 19 December 1929 ("I am a pessimist because of intelligence, but an optimist because of will"). And in the 1933 letter to Tatiana he abandoned it: "Today I no longer think in this way... I no longer see any concrete way out" (Haider). This study must not present the motto as Gramsci's invention, and notes the 1933 retraction.
C10. "The old world is dying, and the new world struggles to be born: now is the time of monsters." NOT GRAMSCI'S. RESOLVED. What he wrote, in a 1930 notebook: "The crisis consists precisely in the fact that the old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" (Ashworth, via Achcar). Zizek describes "time of monsters" as his own "slightly shorter, and, dare I say, pithier iteration" (New Statesman, 26 November 2025, via Ashworth). Achcar notes "cannot be born" differs from "is not yet born": Gramsci was not promising a new order would arrive. Do not quote the monsters line as Gramsci's.
C11. "False consciousness" is not Gramsci's. RESOLVED. The phrase comes from Engels's letter to Franz Mehring, 14 July 1893; the theory is Lukacs's, "Class Consciousness" (1920), the "imputed" consciousness (Ashworth). Do not attribute it to Gramsci.
C12. "The long march through the institutions" is not Gramsci's. RESOLVED. It belongs to Rudi Dutschke, first recorded in a 1967 conversation; the evidence Dutschke drew on Gramsci at all is thin, Gramsci does not appear in the index of Dutschke's biography or published diaries, where Mao and Lukacs appear often (Ashworth, via Frost). Do not quote it as Gramsci's.
C13. "Cultural hegemony" is the label later readers attached. RESOLVED. Gramsci's term was simply hegemony, egemonia (Ashworth). This study prefers "hegemony" and flags "cultural hegemony" as the reception's label.
C14. The Togliatti editing controversy and the "abandoned by the party" debate. UNRESOLVED as a verdict; documented as a dispute. Stanford Encyclopedia, section 4: the 1948-1951 thematic edition, "shaped by Palmiro Togliatti", presented an "acceptable" Gramsci suiting the PCI's cautious post-war politics; "selective editing... downplayed overt conflict with Stalin, emphasizing Gramsci's continuity with... 'dialectical materialism'". A Rivisteweb abstract is blunter: "this edition and Togliatti's interpretation constituted an adaptation of Gramsci's work that was compatible with Stalinism"; the Gerratana 1975 critical edition moved the "dynamic nucleus" to the Americanism notes. Separately, the prison years: Colletti (New Left Review I/65, 1971), following Fiori, records the Gramsci-Togliatti disagreement after the Comintern's Sixth Congress (1928): Gramsci opposed the "Third Period" turn and the expulsion of Leonetti, Ravazzoli and Tresso; his brother Gennaro, sent by Togliatti to visit him in Turi, reported back "Nino is in complete agreement with you", though Gramsci had told Gennaro the opposite. Spriano's Gramsci in carcere e il partito (1977) is the party-historian account. Wikipedia (Legacy): "It is speculated that he would likely have been expelled from his party if his true views had been known, particularly his growing hostility towards Joseph Stalin." This study presents the disagreement as documented (Colletti, Fiori, Spriano) and the "abandoned" framing as interpretation, not fact.
C15. Leadership wording: "sole leader" is wrong. RESOLVED. An older Wikipedia mirror says Gramsci was "a founding member and sole leader of the Communist Party of Italy"; current Wikipedia says "a founding member and one-time leader". Bordiga was the first secretary (January 1921-January 1924); Gramsci became general secretary in summer 1924; succeeded by Togliatti. "Sole leader" is wrong in either telling.
C16. Release: due for release 21 April 1937, died too ill to move. RESOLVED by nuance. Wikipedia: "He was due for release on 21 April 1937 and planned to retire to Sardinia for convalescence, but... he was too ill to move." Stanford Encyclopedia: "in April 1937 his sentence finally expired. By that time, he was gravely ill." Also: conditional freedom was granted at Formia, and he moved to Quisisana in August 1935, so the last two years were guarded clinic detention, not prison, and not freedom.
C17. Source error in Vectree: "He died there on 27 April 1937", in a paragraph about Turi. RESOLVED as an error. He did not die in Turi; he died in Rome at the Quisisana clinic. All other sources (Wikipedia, Stanford Encyclopedia, International Gramsci Society, Britannica) agree on Rome. Do not cite Vectree for the death place.
C18. L'Unita launch: Vienna 1923 versus Rome 1924. UNRESOLVED. Stanford Encyclopedia (Vienna, 1923): "In Vienna he initiated the publication of a new daily party paper, L'Unita ('Unity'), aimed at an inclusive audience of 'workers and peasants'." Wikipedia (Rome, 1924): "He started organizing the launch of the official newspaper of the party, called L'Unita (Unity), living in Rome while his family stayed in Moscow." Possibly initiation in Vienna and launch in Rome; not established this leg.
C19. The October 1926 letter: cited as evidence of an independent spirit, but Gramsci's own reply to Togliatti (26 October 1926) limits it. RESOLVED by quoting both. The letter had been "a requisition against the Oppositions", "our remarks were totally against the Oppositions", and it should not weaken "the position of the majority of the CC" (Ashworth, citing the Marxists Internet Archive translation). This study quotes the reply alongside the letter, not the letter alone.
C20. The "pseudo-history" remark: 1985, not 1954. RESOLVED by dating. Davis's footnote 10 ("Ibid.") traces it to Mack Smith's Introduction to the 1985 edition of Cavour and Garibaldi, thirty-one years after the book; footnote 12 cites the Preface to the 1985 edition. No source this leg shows Mack Smith using the phrase "pseudo-history" before 1985, or shows what he said about Gramsci in 1954 (when, per Riall, he had not read him). This study dates the dismissal to 1985, not to 1954.
C21. Brief correction: "the Lyceum thesis on language". RESOLVED as a misnomer. There is no completed Lyceum thesis. Gramsci studied linguistics under Bartoli (glottology course 1912-13, per Carlucci) and contemplated academic specialisation in the subject, but abandoned his university studies in early 1915 without a degree (Stanford Encyclopedia 1.2; Wikipedia). The "thesis" in the brief is a misnomer for the planned-but-unwritten linguistics thesis; this study does not claim a thesis exists. (His Cagliari lyceum years, 1908-1911, are the school years, not a thesis.)
C22. Mother's surname: Marcias versus Marcia. UNRESOLVED. Giuseppina Marcias (Wikipedia, International Gramsci Society) versus Giuseppina (nee Marcia) (Stanford Encyclopedia). One letter's difference; this study uses "Marcias" (two sources) and makes nothing of it.
C23. Delio Gramsci's death: 1981 versus 1982. UNRESOLVED. 1924-1981 (International Gramsci Society: "Delio (1924-1981)") versus 1924-1982 (Wikipedia infobox family note: "Delio (1924-1982)"). A one-year slip in one of the two. This study does not give Delio's death year.
