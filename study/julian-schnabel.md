## The life: the surface is the painting

Julian Charles Schnabel was born October 26, 1951, in Brooklyn, New York, the youngest of three children, to Jack Schnabel, an immigrant from Czechoslovakia, and Esther Schnabel of New York. The family moved to Brownsville, Texas, in 1965, when he was 14; the official biography describes Brownsville as a small Gulf Coast town on the Mexican border, where his experiences included crime, violence and drugs, and where he began a life-long practice of surfing. He attended the University of Houston from 1969 to 1973 and received his BFA in 1973. He is not a CalArts painter and never was: the Bleckner link runs through New York, not school, and the study says so plainly rather than borrowing a school tie that does not exist. (C24708, leg 1 C24400-C24402; synthesis C24754)

After graduating he applied to the Whitney Museum Independent Study Program, packing slides of his paintings between slices of bread in a paper bag to get noticed. He was accepted, moved to New York in the summer of 1973, briefly stayed with sculptor Joel Shapiro in exchange for painting his studio, then got his own studio from the Study Program, where he also lived against regulations. To earn money he sold sunglasses and drove a taxi. The ISP end date varies by source (1973-74 per the Vito Schnabel bio, Britannica, and the Pace list; 1973-75 per Wikipedia) -- the 1973-74 dating is better supported. In 1975 he returned to Houston, rented a studio in the Heights, worked as a short-order cook, and had his first solo exhibition at the Contemporary Arts Museum, Houston -- dated 1976 by the official bio, the Vito Schnabel bio, and Wikipedia (February 20 to March 7, 1976), and 1975 by Gagosian; the 1976 dating is better supported. (C24709, leg 1 C24403-C24405; synthesis C24754)

From late October 1976 he travelled through Europe, with extended stays first in Paris, then in Milan. He was impressed by Fra Angelico and Caravaggio and especially by a visit to the Giotto chapel in Padua. In Milan he made ten wax paintings for 3,000 dollars, materials and a studio in the basement of an apartment building owned by collector Ruggero Jannuzzelli. He returned to New York in March 1977. The wax paintings -- oil and wax with modeling paste that allowed him to open up the picture ground, tearing holes into it or modeling the surface -- run from 1975; he later called Jack the Bellboy (W01, 1975) his "first adult painting". No source read places him in Amsterdam or Rome in 1973-74; do not assert that trip. (C24710, leg 1 C24406, C24416; leg 2 W01; synthesis C24754)

The turn comes in 1978. Schnabel travelled to Italy again with his painter friend Ross Bleckner, visited Barcelona, and was especially interested in the architecture of Antoni Gaudi and his use of mosaic. Back in New York he began the plate paintings: buying china plates from a charity shop, breaking them and sticking them into a surface covered with putty such as Bondo or dental plaster, then painting over it. The first two works in the new technique were The Patients and the Doctors (W02) and The Death of Fashion (W03). He chose plates, he said, because of their reflective quality and because they were familiar things we eat off. He met Mary Boone in 1978; she agreed to be his dealer without offering an exhibition yet. The claim that Bleckner introduced Boone to Schnabel is unresolved -- Vanity Fair says so, Schnabel's own bio names no introducer -- and is not printed as fact anywhere in this study. (C24711, leg 1 C24407, C24409, C24411; leg 2 W02, W03, M1; synthesis C24755)

In February 1979 Schnabel had his first solo exhibition in New York at Mary Boone's Gallery, at 420 West Broadway alongside Leo Castelli and Ileana Sonnabend. They showed the wax paintings, holding the plate paintings back as too unusual for a first outing; the show sold out before the opening. In October 1979 he had a second solo exhibition at the gallery where the plate paintings were finally shown, to an overwhelming response. Meanwhile he was living in the White Street building Bleckner had bought in 1974 with his parents' help, renting three floors of it, with the Mudd Club occupying space in the same building. In April 1980 he took part in the trio exhibition with Bleckner and David Salle at Mary Boone's gallery -- the three perceived as part of the same scene, the gallery as a major force in the return of painting. The same year he took part in the Venice Biennale, married Jacqueline Beaurang, and was painting on velvet and incorporating found objects such as the deer antlers in Exile (W05). (C24712, leg 1 C24408, C24410, C24412; leg 3 C24603; synthesis C24755)

The rise is fast and documented. In 1981 Schnabel was the first new artist to join Leo Castelli's gallery since 1971; in April 1981 he had a double exhibition at both Mary Boone and Leo Castelli, showing 14 paintings and nine drawings. The same year he took part in "A New Spirit in Painting" at the Royal Academy of Arts, London, and was the youngest painter in that show -- the verified "youngest" claim, and it attaches to this show, not to the Whitney retrospective; do not transfer it. In 1982 he had an exhibition of thirty paintings at the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam as well as institutional solo exhibitions at the Tate, London, and the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, and made his first bronze sculptures. In 1984 he moved from Mary Boone to Pace Gallery: his first Pace show included new plate paintings including Vita and velvet paintings such as The Resurrection: Albert Finney Meets Malcolm Lowry. (C24713, leg 1 C24413-C24415; synthesis C24756)

The retrospective "Julian Schnabel: Paintings 1975-1986" was organised by Nicholas Serota for the Whitechapel Art Gallery, London, and Dominique Bozo for the Centre Pompidou, Paris, travelling to the Kunsthalle Duesseldorf and then to the United States. It arrived at the Whitney Museum of American Art in November 1987 (running to January 1988), retitled there "Julian Schnabel: Paintings 1975-1987", then travelled to the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art and the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston. The scale of the work by then was monumental and stayed that way: 19 works each 16 by 16 feet in Montauk in 1988, 22 by 22 feet tarps in Nimes in 1990, 24 by 24 feet paintings at the Legion of Honor in 2018. The hostile verdict is on record and is not smoothed over: Robert Hughes said Schnabel's work "is to painting what Stallone's is to acting". (C24714, leg 1 C24418, C24420, C24429; leg 3 C24618; synthesis C24756)

The second act is film, and it is kept brief here because the study is about painting. Basquiat (1996), his first film, written and directed by him, about Jean-Michel Basquiat, whom he had met in 1981 painting in the basement of Annina Nosei's gallery; he painted the works shown in the film himself because Basquiat's originals could not be used. Before Night Falls (2000) won the Grand Jury Prize and the Coppa Volpi for Javier Bardem at Venice. The Diving Bell and the Butterfly (2007) won Best Director at Cannes and the Golden Globes and was nominated for four Academy Awards including Best Director. At Eternity's Gate (2018) put Willem Dafoe as Van Gogh on screen, and props Schnabel painted of Dafoe as Van Gogh became models for new plate paintings -- painting and film feeding each other, neither replacing the other. His latest film, In the Hand of Dante, premiered at the 2025 Venice Film Festival, where he received the Cartier Glory to the Filmmaker Award. He built Palazzo Chupi, his studio and home at 360 West 11th Street -- a former horse stable converted into a Venetian palazzo, painted pink -- from 2004 to 2008, and moved in that year. He lives and works in New York City and Montauk, and married Louise Kugelberg on January 2, 2019. No source read supports a Montague Street, Brooklyn, address for him; do not assert one. (C24715, leg 1 C24422-C24423, C24425-C24426, C24428; leg 2 evolution; synthesis C24757)

The evolution is the proof. 1975-78: wax, the ground torn open. 1978-early 1980s: plates invented, scale growing fast, velvet in parallel. Mid-1980s: found cloth -- tarpaulins, Kabuki backdrops, boxing-ring covers, army tarps, sailcloth -- and text as a main image. Late 1980s-1990s: scale stays monumental, resin-smooth portraits, the Fox Farm sentence repeated until it becomes texture. 2001-08: the found image as model -- a thrift-store girl enlarged to 13 feet with her eyes blotted out, Chuck Manion's head disconnected from his body, Shiva from a yoga poster, X-rays from 1911. 2015-25: return to plates as a memory medium -- the Rose paintings by Van Gogh's grave, then Self Portraits of Others, then the Mnuchin survey of plate paintings 1978-2025. He changes support to change what painting can be, and he keeps returning to the plate painting for almost fifty years. (C24716, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C24757)


## M1 -- Broken crockery as support and as image

The plate painting is built before it is painted. Schnabel bought a box of plates from the Salvation Army, shattered them on the ground, and adhered the broken pieces to wooden panels sized like a closet or armoire, 95 to 120 inches, with Bondo (auto-body putty) or dental plaster; then he painted over the shards in oil. Rudi Fuchs, read end to end: the pieces of plate are fitted and pressed into layers of thick paste, which hardened like frozen ground; against a darker ground the white shards form an irregular pattern of interruptions; figuration emerges from between and among the intervals grown between the shattering of plates, and can seem crude and awkward. In the early works many plates stay bare -- in Blue Nude with Sword (W04, 1979), the first plate painting to include a human figure, most of the plates have no paint at all, and every move of the brush is executed in opposition to the surface activity of the plates. In the Rose paintings (W20, 2015) the logic reverses: the plates are submerged under paint and become undergrowth, their shapes mimicking leaves and rose heads. The Patients and the Doctors (W02, 1978) is a strange triptych with no centre: two side panels separated by a third that projects forward, the middle panel offset to the viewer's left, a hulking pink block with the hint of dismembered body parts floating as if in pink primordial soup. (C24717, leg 2 M1, W02, W04, W20; synthesis C24758)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that refuses to settle at one distance. Up close it is a mosaic of hard white edges; from far away a figure or a rose resolves, then breaks again. The shards catch light differently from the matte paint and the Bondo as the viewer moves, so the image appears and disappears with distance and angle. A generative piece could shatter a plane into shards, let light glint off shard faces while paint stays matte, and let the figure coalesce only at a distance -- never a flat picture with a crackle texture pasted on, never shards that stay still and decorative. (C24718, leg 2 M1; synthesis C24758)

**Takeaway:** build the support first as a broken field, then force the image to happen on top of a surface that resists the brush -- the figure must coalesce only at a distance. (C24719)


## M2 -- Wax, resin, and modeling paste encrustation

The wax paintings of 1975-78 are the plate paintings' first draft: oil and wax with modeling paste, layered, pearlescent, notched into the surface and built out of it, so the surface has depth you can read as time passing. The Glass House, read end to end: pearlescent layers of wax and modeling paste reveal the hand of the artist, who was building up the surface; he notched into the surface and built out of the surface. The same encrusting impulse moves later to resin over a printed image (Untitled (Shiva), W19, 2007) and to thick oil-and-wax that can blot out eyes with violent brushstrokes (Large Girl with No Eyes, W17, 2001). Wax is not a thin medium here. It is a fossil record: a mark can be buried under a translucent layer and then re-exposed by a notch, like growth rings. (C24720, leg 2 M2, W01, W17, W19; synthesis C24758)

The watchable behaviour is a surface that looks wet, then hard, then fossilised. Light sinks into wax and bounces off resin differently, so the same mark reads soft in one light and sharp in another. A generative piece could let paint accumulate in translucent layers, then carve notches that reveal earlier layers underneath -- the picture as an excavation, not a surface. Never a flat impasto effect; the layers must be real strata with something buried in them. (C24721, leg 2 M2; synthesis C24758)

**Takeaway:** let paint accumulate in translucent strata, then carve notches that reveal earlier layers -- the picture is an excavation. (C24722)


## M3 -- The found support with its own history

Schnabel selects his support; he does not make it. Velvet (black velvet for the Warhol portrait, red velvet for the Fox Farm paintings); tarpaulins from Mexican trucks; army tarps; boxing-ring covers; Kabuki theatre backdrops (1986, after a gift from Akira Ikeda); sailcloth from Egyptian felucca boats; market fabric from Mexico; maps; polyester printed with photographs, X-rays, or a Shiva poster. His line, quoted at the Glass House: "If you go somewhere and do not bring anything back with you, what is the point of going?" The support's stains, folds, tears, sun-bleaching, and printed image are already a picture before he touches it. He paints with, against, or on top of what is there: he lets a Kabuki backdrop's landscape and pink blossoms show through; in a 2017 work on found fabric the pink is not painted at all, it is sunburned cloth selected by the artist. Max Hetzler quotes him on found materials bringing a real place and time into the aesthetic reality. (C24723, leg 2 M3; synthesis C24759)

The watchable behaviour is a ground that keeps asserting itself. A stain becomes a monster when a line connects two existing marks, like an ink-blot test. A generative piece could start from a weathered texture with its own history -- a tarp, a backdrop, a sun-bleached cloth -- then let sparse marks coax figures out of stains rather than drawing on a blank field. The ground must never be a neutral backdrop; if the cloth could be swapped for white canvas without changing the picture, the mechanism is not there. (C24724, leg 2 M3; synthesis C24759)

**Takeaway:** start from a weathered ground with its own history and let sparse marks coax figures out of its stains -- never draw on a blank field. (C24725)


## M4 -- Monumental scale, and the figure as architecture

Scale is not enlargement for effect. The Walk Home (W15, 1985) is 112 by 232 inches on six wood panels; The Mud in Mudanza (W12, 1982) is 115 by 228 inches and 6 inches deep; War (W16, 1985) and its companions are 17 by 23 feet each on truck tarp; the Recognitions paintings were laid out as 24 stations of the cross on army tarps and shown in a ruined monastery in Seville; the Legion of Honor paintings of 2018 are 24 by 24 feet each. Schnabel says in the Max Hetzler catalogue note that when paintings are large, the interior of the painting seems to deconstruct itself, and he likes watching the painting deconstruct itself. A figure at 13 feet tall stops being a portrait and becomes a wall, a banner, a battle standard. The picture has a depth in inches, not just in illusion: 12 inches deep for The Patients and the Doctors, 10 inches for Self-Portrait in Andy's Shadow. (C24726, leg 2 M4, W12, W15, W16; synthesis C24759)

The watchable behaviour is a picture too big for one frame. The viewer cannot take the whole image in at once; the body has to walk the picture, and the image changes as the viewer moves along it -- the figure breaking into architecture at the edges. A generative piece could make the image larger than the viewport, forcing a slow pan, with the figure dissolving into structure at the frame's edges and reforming at the centre. Never a big picture that fits neatly on one screen; if it fits, it is not monumental. (C24727, leg 2 M4; synthesis C24759)

**Takeaway:** make the image too big for one frame, so the viewer must walk it and the figure breaks into architecture at the edges. (C24728)


## M5 -- Text and inscription as image

Text is painted large, often in block letters, across a tarp or velvet ground, sometimes over a religious banner or a found image. It is not a caption; it is the figure. The Recognitions paintings use words from William Gaddis's novel, laid out as 24 stations of the cross. The Fox Farm paintings (1989) carry a single repeated sentence: "there is no place on this planet more horrible than a fox farm during pelting season." Max Hetzler on Blessed Clara (1987): words become a form, the essence of an image, the subject matter; letters are pictorial elements with a sociological and historical connotation. Schnabel: "When I write on these works that look like abstract paintings but which are not, the words inform them, they color their meaning." In Humanity Asleep (W09, 1982) the words "humanity asleep" sit on a little circle above a head of Francesco Clemente next to a faceless head, on a raft, with an archangel above -- and Schnabel says his paintings are beyond logic and are not literal illustrations of the title. The Shiva works are inscribed "BEZ". (C24729, leg 2 M5, W09, W19; synthesis C24760)

The watchable behaviour is the eye reading, then stopping reading and seeing the letters as shapes, then reading again. A generative piece could let a sentence grow letter by letter until the letters become architecture, then let paint wash over them until only fragments stay legible -- the sentence as a wall that is also a picture. Never a caption under an image, never a slogan; the text must be the figure, not the label. (C24730, leg 2 M5; synthesis C24760)

**Takeaway:** paint the sentence as the figure -- let it grow until the letters are architecture, then wash it until only fragments stay legible. (C24731)


## M6 -- Portrait as icon and altarpiece, with the eyes taken away

The portrait is built like a devotional image: frontal, huge, centred or deliberately off-centre, with a halo-like field of paint around it. Then one expected part is removed or blocked. In Large Girl with No Eyes (W17, 2001) the eyes are barred by a long black bar, blotted out with extremely violent thick brushstrokes, so the viewer is forced to look at the painting and not the eyes. The model was not a live sitter: in 1987 Schnabel bought a small oil painting of a blonde girl in a blue outfit in a Houston bric-a-brac shop, painted four purple marks on it, one covering her eyes, and fourteen years later used that marked painting as the model for the large series. At this scale, the Max Hetzler note says, you no longer see her as a young girl or even as a portrait because you cannot look her in the eye any more. In Versions of Chuck 3 (W18, 2003) the head is physically attached to the body but visibly disconnected by the painting itself: the head modeled in yellow, baby blue, and shades of pink, the body articulated with striated graphic strokes. In Portrait of Andy Warhol (W11, 1982) the sitter is displaced to the left of a horizontal rectangle, a spectral figure in bone white and scumbled violets and yellows on black velvet, less a body than a transparent vessel, with white flecks flung across the velvet like dust in a projector's light. (C24732, leg 2 M6, W11, W17, W18; synthesis C24760)

The watchable behaviour is the gaze searching for eyes, failing, and dropping to the surface -- the collar, the brushwork, the frame. A generative piece could build a face, then erase or bar the eyes, and watch attention slide from the face to the texture around it. The denial must be total: one visible eye and the whole mechanism collapses into an ordinary portrait. (C24733, leg 2 M6; synthesis C24760)

**Takeaway:** build the face huge and frontal, then take the eyes away -- the viewer must enter through the paint, not through eye contact. (C24734)


## M7 -- Appropriation and palimpsest: painting over a picture that already exists

The underlying image is printed, not drawn, on polyester or on a plate ground, and Schnabel then paints over it with oil, resin, ink, or gesso, partly hiding and partly revealing it. The printed image is both the subject and the ground. He photographed a poster of Shiva on a yoga-studio wall, printed it on polyester, and painted over it (W19, 2007-2011); he printed 1911 X-rays found in France on polyester; he printed surf photographs by Herbie Fletcher; he enlarged Van Gogh's 26-by-21-inch self-portrait to 72 by 60 inches as a plate painting (Number 6, 2020). In the Rose paintings the same logic applies to the plate ground itself: the plates are the earlier picture, and the paint is the later one. Exile (W05, 1980) quotes Caravaggio inside a wash of oxide yellow bruised with gestural marks, under three real sets of antlers. The palimpsest is the point: two times at once, the found image and the new gesture, neither one winning. (C24735, leg 2 M7, W05, W19, W20; synthesis C24761)

The watchable behaviour is an image emerging from under paint, then sinking back under it. A generative piece could start from a recognisable old-master image, then let paint pour over it until only an eye or a swirl survives, then let it reappear -- the picture as two times sharing one surface. Never a filter over a photograph; the found image must be a ground that the new paint argues with. (C24736, leg 2 M7; synthesis C24761)

**Takeaway:** print an old image as the ground, then paint over it until only a fragment survives -- two times on one surface. (C24737)


## M8 -- Real objects protruding from the picture

Objects are not depicted; they are stuck on. Deer antlers in Exile (W05); a bronze cross with horns and iron chains in The Mud in Mudanza (W12); a bronze cast of a Christmas tree in The Raft; old pieces of wood in The Sea (W07), one burnt piece leaning against the canvas so the work intrudes into the viewer's reality; Mexican pots, Styrofoam, and antlers in The Sea; a bridle on tarpaulin in The Atlas Mountains I (2008); copper, bronze, and fiberglass in The Walk Home (W15); plates and cups projecting out like jagged, sculptural brushstrokes in Spain (1986). They cast real shadows on the painted surface, and they can fall off: plates sometimes crashed onto the gallery floor in the night. The picture has a depth in inches -- 12 inches for The Patients and the Doctors, 10 inches for Self-Portrait in Andy's Shadow -- and that depth is part of the image, not a frame around it. (C24738, leg 2 M8, W05, W07, W12, W15; synthesis C24761)

The watchable behaviour is the picture flipping between flat and built as the viewer moves: real shadows move across painted shadows, and the object and its painted double separate. A generative piece could let a 3D object sit on a 2D field, casting a shadow that does not match the painted light, so the picture keeps flipping between a picture and a thing. Never a painted antler; the object must be real enough to cast a shadow the paint cannot explain. (C24739, leg 2 M8; synthesis C24761)

**Takeaway:** stick a real object on the picture and let its shadow disagree with the painted light -- the picture must keep flipping between flat and built. (C24740)


## M9 -- Chance, weather, and the body: dragging, leaving out, breaking

The artist sets up a process and lets the world finish it. In the summer of Hurricane Bob, 1991, Schnabel dragged tarps behind a jeep so asphalt would leave a burning mark and dirt from unpaved roads would imprint the folds, then left the tarps exposed to the hurricane itself. He painted outside in Montauk in a roofless studio. The plate painting is not fully controlled either: the break decides the shard shapes, and plates left over in the morning, after some had crashed in the night, became part of the painting. The tarp is not a neutral ground; it is a record of a road, a storm, a market, a boxing match. The fold becomes a horizon; the stain becomes a monster when a line connects two existing marks. (C24741, leg 2 M9, W16; synthesis C24762)

The watchable behaviour is marks appearing that no hand made. A generative piece could simulate a cloth dragged over a rough surface, accumulating dirt and folds, then let the artist's marks respond only to what the cloth already shows -- the picture as a weather report, not a drawing. Never a random-noise texture; the chance must leave a legible trace, a road, a storm, a fold that becomes a horizon. (C24742, leg 2 M9; synthesis C24762)

**Takeaway:** drag the cloth through the world first, then paint only in answer to what the cloth already shows. (C24743)


## M10 -- Viewing time and distance: the picture changes as you look

Schnabel in Artforum, 1984, quoted in the Brooklyn Rail: "I think a painting should be able to make you examine your preconceptions by creating a viewing time with its own information"; "I try to cut out as much distraction as possible; jerk you outside of yourself and make you beside yourself." He says his paintings are about time -- "a lot to do with time and different levels of things that are having a parallel life". The surface is deliberately hard to read at one distance: up close it is shards, wax, and impasto; far away a figure, a rose, or a landscape resolves; at an angle the reflective plates flash and the matte paint goes dark. The Mnuchin show notes that the Van Gogh plate portraits are meant to be seen from varying distances because the texture of the paint-encrusted plates changes as light and shadows create new effects. The middle panel of The Patients and the Doctors projects forward and is offset to the viewer's left, so there is no centre to focus on. Rudi Fuchs: the paintings must be looked at with slow care; their turmoil is built slowly because the materials are resistant. (C24744, leg 2 M10, W02, W20; synthesis C24762)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that does not give itself up in one look. It rewards slow looking, walking closer and further away, and returning later. A generative piece could make the image resolve only at a certain distance or after a delay, and change with the light angle, so the viewer has to spend time with it -- the picture as an appointment, not a poster. Never a picture that reads fully in the first second; if it does, the mechanism is absent. (C24745, leg 2 M10; synthesis C24762)

**Takeaway:** make the image resolve only at a distance or after a delay -- the viewer must spend time with it. (C24746)


## The aesthetic logic: the support is the subject

Schnabel's career is one act performed fifty ways: take a thing that already exists -- a plate, a tarp, a velvet curtain, a photograph, a poster, a found painting -- and make it carry a picture it was never meant to carry. He never starts from a blank canvas if he can start from a thing with a history, because the thing does the work the brush cannot: it resists, it reflects, it casts a shadow, it breaks, it weathers. The painting is finished when the image and the support are in disagreement and neither one has won -- Schnabel's own words on the Rose paintings: the plates and the paint were in disagreement and fractured the homogeneity of the mosaic. That disagreement is the picture. (C24747, leg 2 M1, M3, M7, M10, W20; synthesis C24763)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the support is the subject: the plate, the wax, the velvet, the tarp is not a ground but a co-author, and changing the support is how he changes what painting can be. Second, scale is a dial, not a default: the same motif at 13 feet is a wall and at 30 inches is a specimen, and the picture is built to be walked, not just looked at. Third, time is a material: the break, the drag, the storm, the fourteen years between buying a thrift-store girl and enlarging her with her eyes barred -- the picture accumulates its own past and shows it. Bleckner paints the light that survives inside the dark; Schnabel paints the thing that survives inside the picture. The hostile verdict and the defence are the same fact stated two ways: Hughes said the work is to painting what Stallone is to acting, and Schnabel's answer, in the work itself, is that a painting should jerk you outside of yourself and make you beside yourself -- the excess is the point, not the accident. (C24748, leg 2 M2, M4, M6, M9, M10; leg 3 C24618; synthesis C24763)

The evolution is the proof. 1975-78: wax, the ground torn open. 1978-early 1980s: plates invented in Barcelona after Gaudi, scale growing fast, velvet in parallel, the first Boone shows selling out. Mid-1980s: found cloth takes over -- tarpaulins, Kabuki backdrops, boxing-ring covers, army tarps -- and text becomes a main image. Late 1980s-1990s: scale stays monumental, resin-smooth portraits, the Fox Farm sentence repeated until it is texture, Hurricane Bob tarps dragged behind a jeep. 2001-08: the found image as model -- the thrift-store girl, Chuck Manion, Shiva from a yoga poster, X-rays from 1911. 2015-25: return to plates as a memory medium -- roses by Van Gogh's grave, self-portraits of others, the Mnuchin survey of plate paintings 1978-2025. What stays constant is the question the painting asks without answering: what happens when you make the picture out of the thing itself, and let the thing fight back? (C24749, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C24763)
