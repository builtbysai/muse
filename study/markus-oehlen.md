Oehlen is the painter who came to painting through the drum kit. Born in 1956 in Krefeld, the son of the graphic designer and cartoonist Adolf Oehlen and the younger brother of the painter Albert Oehlen, he first trained as a technical draftsman, completing an apprenticeship from 1971 to 1973, and then studied at the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf from 1976 to 1982, where he was Alfonso Hueppi's student -- German Wikipedia adds that he was Hueppi's Meisterschueler, master student. The end year is split across sources (1981 in MoMA's 1993 text, 1982 in most sources, 1983 in the Stadel), and the split is carried, not settled. In 1977, while still a student, he met Martin Kippenberger, and that meeting anchors the circle that became the Neue Wilde milieu. The draftsman training matters: it is the reason the later pictures are built like drawings that have been flooded with paint, the line surviving underneath everything poured over it. [C26773]

The punk years are not a phase he passed through on the way to painting; they are the painting's operating system. Oehlen worked as a DJ at the Ratinger Hof in Duesseldorf from 1976 to 1979, in the close circle of the pub that Carmen Knoebel and Ingrid Kohlhoefer had taken over and renovated in 1971 -- so the gallery wording that calls him a "co-founder" of the Hof is loose at best, and the study says so plainly. He was a member of Charley's Girls, which had existed since 1977, then co-founded Mittagspause in the summer of 1978 with Peter Hein, Gabi Delgado-Lopez and Franz Bielmeier, drumming at its debut at SO 36 in West Berlin on 12 August 1978; the band's last live appearance was 31 December 1979, though MoMA's 1993 text gives 1979-83, an outlier. Fehlfarben formed in 1979 out of Mittagspause members; Oehlen is listed among its founding members, but no source read specifies his instrument there -- Uwe Bauer is named as Fehlfarben's drummer -- so the study says drummer in Charley's Girls and Mittagspause, founding member of Fehlfarben. Later projects include Flying Klassenfeind, Vielleichtors, Nachdenkliche Wehrpflichtige, Van Oehlen and Red Krayola. The discography runs through the art world too: the 1982 double LP "Kirche der Ununterschiedlichkeit" on ZickZack, and the 1984 "Die Rache der Erinnerung" with Albert Oehlen, Joerg Immendorff, Werner Buettner, Martin Kippenberger and A. R. Penck. The Church of Indifference itself -- founded in 1981 with Buettner and Albert Oehlen, per both Wikipedia biographies -- was a joke that became an institution, a church for people who believed in nothing, and that is exactly how the paintings work: a serious picture made out of unserious material. [C26774]

The painting career runs on the same dates as the music. First solo exhibition in 1977 at Konrad Fischer's Raum in Neubrueckstrasse, Duesseldorf; first significant public mark in Joerg Immendorff's "Finger for Germany" in 1980; Rundschau Deutschland in 1981; "Von hier aus" in Duesseldorf in 1984; regular shows at Galerie Max Hetzler from the early 1980s; and in 1993 the two-person MoMA show "Projects 39" with Georg Herold, 6 February to 23 March, organised by Robert Storr, the first museum showing in New York of recent work by both men. The Berlin Art Prize of 1987 is Wikipedia-only -- the Asbaek CV's prizes list records only the 1979 Kunstpreis junger westen -- so it is carried as Wikipedia-only. Since 2002 he has held a professorship at the Academy of Fine Arts in Munich, teaching painting and graphic arts; the newest gallery sources give the post as 2002-2023, with a 2023 Munich event titled "Prof. Markus Oehlen Sagt Ciao!" reading as a farewell, while older sources still say "since 2002", so the study gives 2002-2023 and does not assert a retirement without an Academy source. He lives and works in Munich, in a studio in the north of the city built by Westner Schuehrer Zoehrer since 2017. Later anchors include the 2015 retrospectives "Die 80er" at the Staedel and "Geniale Dillettanten" at Haus der Kunst, a 2018 solo show at Kunstsammlungen Chemnitz, "Celtic Funk Etcetera" at Karma International in Los Angeles in 2019 -- made while bedbound with a back injury, working from a digital photo library -- "Scumbag Rising" in Munich in 2023, Karma Zurich in 2024, and a new Asbaek solo show "Painting" with works from 2023 to 2026. Against his brother Albert, the distinction is one of temperature. Albert Oehlen's work is a system that deconstructs the medium -- colour, gesture, motion, time -- under self-imposed rules. Markus Oehlen's work is a pile: cheap materials, borrowed pictures, found objects and a drummer's sense of placement, left dense enough that the viewer cannot take the picture in all at once. The Van Ham note on "Am Wasser" (1987) says it plainly: the figurative and the abstract, light and shadow, line and form meet on the pictorial surface and cannot be taken in by the viewer all at the same time. That density is the through-line from the 1981 "Krankenhaus" to the 2023 "Studio pollock", and it is what the mechanisms below describe. [C26776]

## M1 -- Dispersion, wax and emulsion on untreated cotton: the punk surface

The early mechanism is material before image. The 1984 diptych (W03) is dispersion and wax on untreated cotton, 200 x 300 cm; the Stadel "Ohne Titel" of 1985 (W05) is dispersion on canvas at the same 200 x 300 cm; "Am Wasser" (1987, W07) is mixed media on canvas at 251 x 320 cm. Dispersion paint, wax, batik and emulsion are cheap, unglamorous materials, and the sources frame them through punk and the Neue Wilde rejection of Minimalism and Conceptual art: vivid colour, gestural brushwork, and a surface that is stained, patterned and colour-swabbed before any figure is drawn on it. The untreated cotton drinks the paint instead of holding it on the surface, so the picture is soaked in rather than applied. The mechanism is to let the material do the first half of the painting, and to make the cheapness visible rather than disguised.

**Takeaway:** paint on something that drinks, with something cheap; let the material make the first half of the picture. [C26777]

## M2 -- The dense picture: everything at once, nothing all at once

The Van Ham note on "Am Wasser" names the mechanism directly: a very dense pictorial structure, typical of Oehlen, in which the figurative and the abstract, light and shadow, line and form meet on the pictorial surface and cannot be taken in by the viewer all at the same time. The 1996 "Untitled" (W14, 270 x 266 cm) works the same way: colourful fragments of paint layered one upon another, vibrant lines, contrasting patterns, and scenic motifs such as an animal skull and a suggested mask, coming together in an almost psychedelic composition. The density is not clutter; it is a deliberate refusal of the single readable image. The viewer is given too much to hold, and the picture rewards the second and third look instead of the first.

**Takeaway:** layer until the picture cannot be taken in at once; the second look is the real picture. [C26778]

## M3 -- Borrowed pictures: television, xerox and the modernist lexicon

MoMA's 1993 account names the method behind the 1990s pictures: borrowed pictures from television screens, xerox-based collages and stylized figuration from the modernist lexicon, stencilled or drawn onto patterned, stained or colour-swabbed canvas fields. The picture is assembled from images that already exist -- a television frame, a photocopied figure, a modernist motif -- and the borrowing is the point: the painting does not invent its figures, it samples them. "Empty Symbol" (1990, W12) and "Void" (1992) are called "the brickladen canvases" in the MoMA catalogue, pictures built like walls out of borrowed bricks. The mechanism turns the canvas into a collage field where the sampled image is only one layer among many, and the painting's job is to hold the layers together without resolving them into a single scene.

**Takeaway:** never invent a figure when you can borrow one; the painting is the field that holds the borrowings together. [C26779]

## M4 -- Found-object sculpture: polystyrene, innertubes and the radio

The sculpture is the painting's impulse turned three-dimensional. The MoMA "Untitled" of 1990 (W11, 209 x 99 x 99 cm) is polystyrene, string and rubber tubes: multicoloured filaments wrapped tightly around carved styrofoam cores, with bicycle innertubes and inflated automobile tires. MoMA describes the objects as dirigible-like, the main coiled shape shifting around its displaced central axis, its top resembling a bent finger and its broad bottom lost sight of in its unstable meeting with the tire base -- towering presences that seem to swell and constrict like dress-maker's dummies, inviting association with familiar and human forms yet defying description. "Radioskulptur" (1995, W13, 53 x 47 x 47 cm) is an integrated radio and cassette recorder worked with dyed cotton yarn and wax: a dead consumer object wrapped until it becomes a body. The mechanism is the same as the painting's: take something cheap and discarded, wrap it, coil it, stain it, and let it suggest a body without ever becoming one.

**Takeaway:** wrap a discarded object until it suggests a body; stop before it becomes one. [C26780]

## M5 -- The cord as line, stamp and relief

The cord is Oehlen's most portable mechanism, and it runs through the whole career. In the 1995 "Radioskulptur" and in the Stadel's "Vera, Rosemarie, Brigitte, Giny" (W06, spray paint and oil on carpet), dyed cotton yarn is worked into the surface as a line that is also a material. In the 2018 Chemnitz group (W19), the cord becomes a tool: attached to wood and used as a stamp, it creates a special graphic texture and materiality on large-format nettle cloth, with printing plates printed in black and blue acrylic paint, and colour-intensive cord pictures in which the cord itself becomes a haptic relief line. The Chemnitz text says the pictures look like fleeting doodles made with a ballpoint pen -- heads, arms, animals, mythical creatures, plants, objects or schematic figures put together from lines, geometric forms and ribbon structures in ever new connections and overlaps. The cord is drawing, printing plate and relief at once, and the same gesture that draws the line also makes the surface.

**Takeaway:** let one line be the drawing, the stamp and the relief; the tool and the mark are the same thing. [C26781]

## M6 -- The grotesque figure steps forward

The figures in Oehlen's pictures are not heroes; they are intruders. The Kunsthaus Zurich "Untitled" of 2008 (W18, 250 x 200 cm) has a gnome-like creature with piercing eyes and a cheeky grin as its main motif, jumping out at the viewer, in a montage of abstract surfaces, gridded colour gradients and the figure in a more forward optical plane. The 1996 "Untitled" (W14) hides an animal skull and a suggested mask among its paint fragments. The 2005 "Securitator" (W17, 250 x 200 cm) superimposes human figures and other more ambiguous, somewhat ominous ones on planes of colour. The mechanism is placement: the figure is pushed into a forward optical plane, in front of the abstract surfaces, so it jumps out at the viewer rather than sitting inside the picture. The figure is alienating in a positive sense, the Kunsthaus curators say -- it exudes an enigmatic, luminous colourfulness with a great long-distance effect.

**Takeaway:** push the grotesque figure forward until it jumps out of the picture; let it grin at the viewer. [C26782]

## M7 -- Op Art grids and interference: the printed layer above and below

From 2000 the handling turns glossier and more optical. "Op to Lynth 1-3" (2000, W15) is three colour screenprints, 59.4 x 42 cm each. "Stooge" (2004, W16) is mixed media and enamel on canvas. "Securitator" (2005, W17) mixes mechanical processes with spontaneous pictorial stains, drawing on optical experimentation from kinetic art and abstract expressionism, with printed image interference in grid-like patterns. The Kunsthaus "Untitled" of 2008 (W18) combines drawing, gestural painting, technoid grids and decorative patterns. The mechanism is interference: a printed grid laid above and below the painted image, so the picture shimmers between a painted surface and a printed one. The grid is not decoration; it is a second picture laid over the first, and the two refuse to settle into one reading.

**Takeaway:** lay a printed grid over the painted picture and let the two readings interfere; never let the picture settle into one. [C26783]

## M8 -- The drummer's placement: rhythm as composition

The music is a mechanism, not a biography footnote. A drummer does not play the melody; he places the beat, and Oehlen's pictures are placed the same way. The diptych is his bar structure -- two panels of equal weight, the motif landing on one side and answering on the other, as in the 1984 diptych (W03) and "Am Wasser" (W07). The repetition is his hi-hat: the same large untitled canvas again and again, the same 200 x 300 cm format, the same dispersion handling, until the repetition itself becomes the picture. The 2018 cord pictures are drawn like drum fills -- fleeting doodles, heads and animals put together from lines in ever new connections and overlaps, the way a fill rearranges the same kit into a new pattern. The sources do not state this mechanism in so many words; it is read off the work and the biography together, and the study says so. The beat is the composition: place things in time, not just in space.

**Takeaway:** compose like a drummer: place the motif on the beat, repeat the format until the repetition is the picture. [C26784]

## M9 -- The record-groove surface: raked lines of translucent acrylic

The Karma International press release for "Celtic Funk Etcetera" (2019) describes the late surface directly: collaged images and acrylic paint, raked lines of semi-translucent acrylic like grooves on a record. The picture is combed. A rake or comb is dragged through wet acrylic, leaving parallel grooves that catch the light the way a record's grooves catch a needle, and the grooves run across the collaged images underneath, cutting them into bands. The mechanism connects the painting to the music in the most literal way available: the surface is a record, and looking at it is like listening to it. The same combed varnish appears in the 2000s pictures, over dense print-collage, and the 2023-2026 Asbaek "Painting" show carries it forward with manipulated photographs and pixel-like grids.

**Takeaway:** rake the wet paint like grooves on a record; let the surface be something you could listen to. [C26785]

## M10 -- Sampling until it dissolves: the manipulated photograph

The newest mechanism is digital. The Asbaek "Painting" show (works 2023-2026) names its newest feature as manipulated photographs combined with pixel-like grids, with image-based sampling letting familiar forms appear only to dissolve again. The Karma text gives the origin: the 2019 pictures were made while Oehlen was bedbound with a back injury, working from a digital photo library, adopting digital imagery because the body could not reach the studio. The mechanism is sampling with a dissolve: a photograph is taken, manipulated, gridded, and painted until the familiar form appears and then comes apart. It is the same move as M3's borrowed television pictures, forty years later, with the borrowing now done on a screen and the dissolving done by the pixel grid.

**Takeaway:** sample a photograph, grid it, and paint it until the familiar form appears -- then let it dissolve. [C26786]

## Aesthetic logic

Oehlen's logic is the pile, held together by a beat. The picture is built from cheap material (dispersion, wax, emulsion on untreated cotton), borrowed images (television, xerox, modernist lexicon, manipulated photographs), found objects (polystyrene, innertubes, a radio) and a line that is also a tool (the cord, the rake, the stamp). Nothing is invented from nothing; everything is taken, wrapped, stained, gridded and placed. The density is the point: the figurative and the abstract, light and shadow, line and form meet on the surface and cannot be taken in all at once, so the picture is a place to return to rather than an image to consume. The drummer's sense of placement keeps the pile from collapsing into clutter: the diptych gives it a bar structure, the repetition gives it a pulse, the grotesque figure gives it a face that jumps out at the viewer. Against his brother Albert's cool system, Markus's picture is warm, loud and crowded -- a punk record made of paint, with the grooves you can see. [C26787]
