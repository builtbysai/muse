## 1. The Trastevere boy who learned trumpet from his father

He was born in Rome on 10 November 1928, a Saturday, at a time when Italy was under fascist rule, the son of Libera Ridolfi and Mario Morricone, a professional trumpeter who performed in light-music orchestras, while his mother set up a small textile business; he had four siblings, Adriana, Aldo, Maria and Franca, and the family lived in Trastevere in the centre of Rome, and Aldo died aged three after his nanny mistakenly fed him cherries, to which he was severely allergic. [C31655] During his early schooldays Morricone was a classmate of Sergio Leone, his later collaborator, which means the most famous director-composer partnership in the western begins not in a studio but in a Roman classroom. [C31656]

His father taught him to read music and to play several instruments, and he went to the Saint Cecilia Conservatory (Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia) to take trumpet lessons under Umberto Semproni. [C31657] He formally entered the conservatory in 1940 at age 12, enrolling in a four-year harmony program that he completed within six months; an older mirror of the biography says he first went to Santa Cecilia for lessons at age 9, which conflicts with the formal-entry age of 12 and is flagged, not settled. [C31658] At the conservatory he studied the trumpet, composition and choral music under the direction of Goffredo Petrassi, to whom Morricone would later dedicate concert pieces, and in 1941 he was chosen among the students of the conservatory to be part of the Orchestra of the Opera, directed by Carlo Zecchi, on the occasion of a tour of the Veneto region. [C31659] He received his diploma in trumpet in 1946, then the Diploma in Instrumentation for Band Arrangement with a mark of 9/10 in 1952, and his studies concluded at the conservatory in 1954, when he obtained a final 9.5/10 in his Diploma in Composition under Petrassi -- and before receiving his diplomas he was already active as a trumpet player, often performing in an orchestra that specialised in music written for films. [C31660]

He wrote his first compositions when he was six years old, encouraged to develop his natural talents, and in 1946 he composed "Il Mattino" ("The Morning") for voice and piano on a text by Fukuko, the first in a group of seven "youth" Lieder. [C31661] Many of his orchestral and chamber compositions were written in 1954-1959, including Musica per archi e pianoforte (1954), Sestetto per flauto, oboe, fagotto, violino, viola, e violoncello (1955), Dodici Variazione per oboe, violoncello, e piano, Trio per clarinetto, corno, e violoncello, Variazione su un tema di Frescobaldi (1956), Quattro pezzi per chitarra (1957), Distanze per violino, violoncello, e piano, Musica per undici violini and Tre Studi per flauto, clarinetto, e fagotto (1958), and the Concerto per orchestra (1957) was dedicated to his teacher Goffredo Petrassi. [C31662]

**Takeaway:** The biography is already the method: a conservatory trumpeter trained by Petrassi, a child composer, a working orchestral player before he held the diplomas -- he arrives at film music as a man who can hear a full orchestration in his head and write it down without touching a piano, and the next sixty years are that ear applied to whatever the film puts in front of it.

## 2. The pop arranger: RCA, three million copies, eight key changes

Morricone's career as an arranger began in 1950, arranging the piece Mamma Bianca (Narciso Parigi), and on the occasion of the "Anno Santo" (Holy Year) he arranged a long group of popular songs of devotion for radio broadcasting. [C31663] In 1956 he started to support his family by playing in a jazz band and arranging pop songs for the Italian broadcasting service RAI; he was hired by RAI in 1958 but quit his job on his first day at work when he was told that broadcasting of music composed by employees was forbidden by a company rule. [C31664] Subsequently he became a top studio arranger at RCA Victor, working with Renato Rascel, Rita Pavone, Domenico Modugno and Mario Lanza, and throughout his career he composed songs for jazz and pop artists including Gianni Meccia, Gianni Morandi, Alberto Lionello, Edoardo Vianello, Nora Orlandi, Jimmy Fontana, Rita Pavone, Catherine Spaak, Luigi Tenco, Gino Paoli, Renato Rascel and Paul Anka. [C31665]

In 1963 Morricone co-wrote (with Roby Ferrante) the music for "Ogni volta" ("Every Time"), performed by Paul Anka for the first time during the Festival di Sanremo in 1964; the song was arranged and conducted by Morricone and sold more than three million copies worldwide, including one million copies in Italy alone. [C31666] Another success was his composition "Se telefonando", performed by Mina as a track on Studio Uno 66, her 4th studio album; Morricone's arrangement combined melodic trumpet lines, Hal Blaine-style drumming, a string set, a 1960s Europop female choir and intensive subsonic-sounding trombones, and the song had eight transitions of tonality building tension throughout the chorus, reaching No. 7 on the Italian Hitparade. [C31667] After playing the trumpet in jazz bands in the 1940s, Morricone became a studio arranger for RCA Victor and in 1955 started ghost writing for film and theatre, occasionally adopting Anglicized pseudonyms such as Dan Savio and Leo Nichols. [C31668]

The two men's paths crossed in the pop-arranger years as well: Rita Pavone's 1962 songs Come te non c'e' nessuno and Pel di carota were arranged by Luis Bacalov, in the same RCA Italiana milieu where Morricone was a top studio arranger. [C31669] And in 1962 Morricone peppered Edoardo Vianello's summer hit "Pinne, fucile, e occhiali", a cha-cha song, with added water effects, unusual instrumental sounds and unexpected stops and starts -- the pop single as a laboratory for the film scores, tin cans and typewriters and water effects tried out on three minutes of radio before they ever reached a western. [C31670]

**Takeaway:** The arranger years are not a detour from the film career; they are the training for it. Ten years of making other singers' songs work taught him the two skills his film scores run on: a melody that can carry a whole piece, and a sound -- a trombone under the floor, a splash of water in a cha-cha -- that makes the melody unmistakable.

## 3. The first films, and the first western

After graduation in 1954, Morricone started to write and arrange music as a ghost writer for films credited to already well-known composers, while also arranging for many light music orchestras of the RAI television network, working especially with Armando Trovajoli, Alessandro Cicognini and Carlo Savina. [C31671] In 1959 he was the conductor (and uncredited co-composer) for Mario Nascimbene's score to Morte di un amico (Death of a Friend), an Italian drama directed by Franco Rossi, and in the same year he composed music for the theatre show Il lieto fine by Luciano Salce. [C31672]

1961 marked his real film debut with Luciano Salce's The Fascist (Italian title Il federale), and with that film Morricone began a long-run collaboration with Salce. [C31673] In 1962 he composed the jazz-influenced score for Salce's comedy Crazy Desire, and his earliest scores were Italian light comedy and costume pictures, where he learned to write simple, memorable themes -- comedies such as Eighteen in the Sun (1962), Il Successo (1963), Lina Wertmuller's The Lizards (1963), Slalom (1965), Menage all'italiana (1965), How I Learned to Love Women (1966), Her Harem (1967) and A Fine Pair (1968). [C31674]

In 1962 Morricone met American folksinger Peter Tevis, and the two collaborated on a version of Woody Guthrie's "Pastures of Plenty"; Tevis is credited with singing the lyrics of Morricone's songs "A Gringo Like Me" (from Gunfight at Red Sands) and "Lonesome Billy" (from Bullets Don't Argue). [C31675] Gunfight at Red Sands (Italian: Duello nel Texas) was released 19 September 1963, directed by Ricardo Blasco and Mario Caiano, with music by Morricone credited as Dan Savio, and it was the first western Morricone scored, one of the first ever spaghetti westerns; its score is surprisingly conventional, closer in tone to the western scores of Moross and Bernstein than to anything Morricone would write later, and "A Gringo Like Me", with lyrics by Anne Carol Danell and Tino Fornai and sung by Tevis, was the first song Morricone wrote for a western film. [C31676]

**Takeaway:** The first western is the control experiment: a conventional orchestral score, a sung title song, a pseudonym on the credit -- everything the Dollars films will throw away a year later, which is how you know the noise was a choice and not a limitation.

## 4. The Dollars sound: noise as instrument

The turning point in Morricone's career took place in 1964, the year his third child, Andrea Morricone, who would also become a film composer, was born, when film director and former schoolmate Sergio Leone hired Morricone to score A Fistful of Dollars; the Leone collaboration would run to eight films from 1964 to 1984. [C31677] A Fistful of Dollars (Italian: Per un pugno di dollari) is a 1964 spaghetti western directed by Sergio Leone and starring Clint Eastwood in his first leading role, released in Italy on 12 September 1964 and in the United States in January 1967, filmed on a budget reported at US$200,000, with music by Morricone billed as Dan Savio, and the film identified as an unofficial remake of Akira Kurosawa's Yojimbo (1961), which resulted in a successful lawsuit by Toho, Yojimbo's production company; the Italian release date is flagged, because Simple English Wikipedia gives 20 October 1964 instead of 12 September, and the Spaghetti Western Database credits the music as Leo Nichols rather than Dan Savio. [C31678]

Because budget strictures limited Morricone's access to a full orchestra, he used gunshots, cracking whips, whistle, voices, Jew's harp, trumpets and the new Fender electric guitar, instead of orchestral arrangements of Western standards a la John Ford; Soundfly's timed analysis of the main title spells the mechanism out: an acoustic guitar pounds a Dm11 chord (spelled D A G) in a driving 16th-note pattern, a whistle carries the melody, a piccolo falls on beat 1 like a kick drum, the crack of the whip lands on beat 3 like a snare, and a bell marks beat 2, repositioning to the "&" of beat 3 when the harmony shifts to the relative major F. [C31679] Morricone used his special effects to punctuate and comically tweak the action, cluing in the audience to the taciturn man's ironic stance, so the noises are not decoration but commentary on the hero's attitude. [C31680]

With the score of A Fistful of Dollars, Morricone began his 20-year collaboration with his childhood friend Alessandro Alessandroni and his Cantori Moderni; Alessandroni provided the whistling and the twanging guitar on the film scores, while his Cantori Moderni were a flexible troupe of modern singers, and Morricone in particular drew on the solo soprano of the group, Edda Dell'Orso, at the height of her powers, "an extraordinary voice at my disposal". [C31681] Morricone employed an unconventional orchestral palette for The Good, the Bad and the Ugly that blended traditional instrumentation with distinctive sound effects, including gunfire, whistling by Alessandro Alessandroni and vocalizations; the soprano voice of Edda Dell'Orso features prominently in compositions such as "The Ecstasy of Gold", guitarist Bruno Battisti D'Amario contributed to tracks including "The Sundown" and "Padre Ramirez", trumpet players Michele Lacerenza and Francesco Catania performed on "The Trio", and the only vocal song, "The Story of a Soldier", with lyrics by Tommie Connor, is performed diegetically by prisoners of war during Tuco's torture sequence. [C31682]

Morricone subsequently scored Leone's other two Dollars Trilogy westerns, For a Few Dollars More (1965) and The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly (1966); for For a Few Dollars More, released in Italy on 18 December 1965 (flagged: an IPFS mirror of the film's page gives 18 November 1965 and another mirror gives 30 December 1965), Morricone began writing the score before production had started, under Leone's explicit direction, as Leone often shot to the music on set, and the music is notable for its blend of diegetic and nondiegetic moments through a recurring motif that originates from the identical pocket watches belonging to El Indio and Colonel Mortimer; the soundtrack album was originally released in Italy by RCA Italiana in 1965. [C31683]

The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (Italian: Il buono, il brutto, il cattivo) was released in Italy on 23 December 1966, directed by Sergio Leone and starring Clint Eastwood as "the Good", Lee Van Cleef as "the Bad" and Eli Wallach as "the Ugly", on a budget of about US$1.2 million, grossing US$25.1 million in the United States and Canada and US$38.9 million worldwide. [C31684] The main theme is built around a two-note motif designed to evoke the howl of a coyote, heard over the film's opening images, and the motif functions as a leitmotif for the three protagonists, each represented by a different timbre: flute for Blondie, ocarina for Angel Eyes, and human voices for Tuco. [C31685]

**Takeaway:** The mechanism is a drum kit made of found sounds: the whip is the snare, the bell is the hi-hat, the whistle is the lead, the gunshot is the accent -- and once the kit is built, the orchestra is optional, which is why the sound survived every budget and every genre that followed.

## 5. The theme written before the film

For The Good, the Bad and the Ugly, Leone and Morricone departed from their previous working method by developing the principal themes before filming began, which allowed the music to shape the film's rhythm and atmosphere, with Leone often playing the compositions on set and staging camera movements and editing patterns to match the score. [C31686] Morricone composed the score for Once Upon a Time in the West (1968, Sergio Leone) before filming began, on Leone's instruction and based solely on the screenplay, and Leone played the recorded music on set in the background for the actors while they performed. [C31687] Morricone said in 2007: "Some of the music was written before the film, which was unusual. Leone's films were made like that because he wanted the music to be an important part of it; he kept the scenes longer because he did not want the music to end", which according to Morricone explains "why the films are so slow". [C31688]

The score of Once Upon a Time in the West is built on leitmotifs: each of the main characters has a theme of his or her own, and there is a further theme for the spirit of the American West itself. [C31689] Harmonica (Charles Bronson) and Frank (Henry Fonda) share one common theme, because their fates are bound together: Harmonica's renderings are carried exclusively by his signature harmonica, while Frank's renderings are more menacing and are carried by electric guitar or strings. [C31690] Jill's Theme is built in ABAA form: the wistful opening A phrase is carried by harpsichord, vibraphone and cello and is raised to the sublime when joined by the wordless vocal of soprano Edda Dell'Orso; the B phrase is a horn-powered bridge that launches the closing A phrases by full orchestra, choir and soprano. [C31691] Cheyenne's Theme is carried by banjo, wood block, harp and at times whistling, a folksy plodding melody; the secondary Pacific Theme, attached to railroad magnate Mr. Morton, is an aspirational construct on woodwinds, vibraphone and the sound of waves crashing on the shore. [C31692]

For the opening station sequence Leone discarded Morricone's scored cue "The Train Station" and used only natural sounds -- a turning windmill wheel, a train, cicadas, a buzzing fly, pigeon wings -- plus the diegetic sound of Harmonica's harmonica, and except for about a minute of the "Judgment" motif no soundtrack music is played until Henry Fonda makes his first entry. [C31693] The score sold about 10 million copies worldwide, making it one of the best-selling original instrumental scores in the world, and the film was selected for preservation in the US National Film Registry in 2009. [C31694]

**Takeaway:** The inversion is the point: the composer writes first and the director shoots second, so the music is not laid over the film but built under it, and the long takes exist because the music refuses to end -- the slowness is the score's doing, not the camera's.

## 6. The Ecstasy of Gold: a build by accumulation

"The Ecstasy of Gold" builds by accumulation, and Soundfly's timed analysis gives the mechanism: it opens with an Am9 arpeggio on piano that propels the music forward, joined by a double-reed melody (Soundfly calls it oboe; Far Out, citing Classic Alex Burns, calls it cor anglais or English horn, flagged, not settled), then Edda Dell'Orso's coloratura enters at about 0:48 taking the melody, drums establish a gallop at 0:47, strings and backing vocals fill the space when the snare drops out at 1:16-1:25, a sharp tacet at 1:47 is followed by a galloping string ostinato, and the Fender electric guitar enters only at 2:59 to restate the melody; the harmony underneath shifts from Am9 to G9 while the melody stays inside the parent Am(7)9 chord, so the line evolves without mutating past the point of discomfort. [C31695] "The Ecstasy of Gold" plays over Tuco frantically running through the Sad Hill Cemetery looking for the grave of Arch Stanton, where he believes $200,000 in gold coins is buried, and the music swells to match his growing desperation; Leone edited the footage to match the rhythm of Morricone's pre-recorded track, a backwards way of doing things in the 1960s, when usually the composer followed the editor. [C31696]

The Good, the Bad and the Ugly soundtrack was a commercial success: released in 1968, it remained on the Billboard album charts for more than a year, reaching No. 4 on the pop chart and No. 10 on the Black Albums chart, and a cover version of the main theme by Hugo Montenegro reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 and No. 1 in the UK for four weeks; on 14 August 1968 the original score was certified by the RIAA with a golden record for the sale of 500,000 copies in the United States alone, and the score sold more than three million copies worldwide; the score's Grammy Hall of Fame induction is dated 2009 on the film's own page and 2008 in the lead of the composer's biography (the biography's own awards section says 2009), flagged, not settled. [C31697] "The Ecstasy of Gold" became one of Morricone's best-known compositions, and Metallica has used it as the introductory music for its concerts since 1983 according to the composer's biography, while the film's own page says since 1985, flagged, not settled; the Ramones used it as a closing theme during live performances, and Metallica's instrumental metal cover appeared on the 2007 tribute album We All Love Ennio Morricone and was nominated for a Grammy for Best Rock Instrumental Performance. [C31698]

**Takeaway:** The build is the composition: nothing new enters until the last minute, and everything that enters stays -- piano, reed, voice, gallop, strings, guitar -- so the climax is not a louder version of the opening but the opening with six layers standing on it, and the film has to cut to the music because the music will not cut to the film.

## 7. The giallo turn: twelve tones and no tune

The Bird with the Crystal Plumage (1970) was Dario Argento's directorial debut and a giallo; Morricone composed the score, his first for a giallo film and the first of five collaborations with Argento, and the film was released in Italy on 27 February 1970. [C31699] For Argento's first three films Morricone wrote music that was dodecaphonic, using 12 different tones at once, with no precise refrain or melody or easily recognisable sound, balancing it with simpler pieces for the most basic characters; producer Salvatore Argento complained the three films sounded alike, Morricone answered that the style was the same but there was a progression, the Argentos worked with another composer for a while, and Dario Argento later called Morricone himself so they could work together again. [C31700]

Morricone's giallo and crime output beyond Argento includes The Cat o' Nine Tails (1971), Four Flies on Grey Velvet (1971), The Black Belly of the Tarantula (1971) and What Have You Done to Solange? (1972), and on the poliziotteschi side Sergio Sollima's Revolver (1973) and Umberto Lenzi's Almost Human (1974). [C31701] Investigation of a Citizen Above Suspicion (1970, Elio Petri), music by Morricone, was released in Italy on 9 February 1970, won the Grand Prize and the FIPRESCI Prize at the 1970 Cannes Film Festival, and won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. [C31702]

**Takeaway:** The same hand that wrote the most hummable western themes in the world could also write music with no refrain and no recognisable sound at all -- the mechanism is not melody, it is control of what the ear is allowed to hold on to, and in the giallo he takes the handrail away on purpose.

## 8. The political ear

The Battle of Algiers (1966, Gillo Pontecorvo), premiered at Venice on 31 August 1966, credits its music jointly to Morricone and Pontecorvo, because Pontecorvo was classified in Italy as a "melodist-composer" and was required to work with another composer; the film's sound design opposes the two sides, with gunfire, helicopters and truck engines for the French and bomb blasts, ululation, wailing and chanting for the Algerians, over a solo military drum played throughout by Pierino Munari. [C31703] The Battle of Algiers won the Golden Lion at the 27th Venice Film Festival and was nominated for three Academy Awards in non-consecutive years: Best Foreign Language Film in 1966, and Best Screenplay and Best Director in 1968. [C31704]

Two years after the start of his collaboration with Sergio Leone, Morricone also started to score music for another spaghetti western director, Sergio Corbucci, writing music for Navajo Joe (1966), The Hellbenders (1967), The Mercenary / The Professional Gun (1968), The Great Silence (1968), Companeros (1970), Sonny and Jed (1972) and What Am I Doing in the Middle of the Revolution? (1972). [C31705] Morricone also composed music for the western films of Sergio Sollima, The Big Gundown (with Lee Van Cleef, shot in Almeria in late 1966 and released in Italy in March 1967, though commonly cited as a 1966 film, flagged), Face to Face (1967) and Run, Man, Run (1968), as well as the 1970 crime thriller Violent City and the poliziottesco film Revolver (1973); outside the western altogether, in the same years, he scored Gillo Pontecorvo's The Battle of Algiers (1966) and Queimada! (1969) with Marlon Brando, and Marco Bellocchio's Fists in the Pocket (1965). [C31706]

Novecento, released as 1900 (1976, Bernardo Bertolucci), premiered out of competition at Cannes on Friday 21 May 1976, runs 317 minutes in its original two-part version, and has a Morricone score that includes several melodies from Giuseppe Verdi operas, with cues including "Romanzo" and "Tema di Ada". [C31707] By director-collaboration count Morricone scored five films for Bertolucci between 1964 and 1981, seven for Elio Petri between 1968 and 1979, and seven for Pier Paolo Pasolini between 1965 and 1975, including Salo, or the 120 Days of Sodom (1975). [C31708] For Giuliano Montaldo's Sacco and Vanzetti (1971) Morricone wrote the folk song "Here's to You", sung by Joan Baez with lyrics inspired by a letter from Bartolomeo Vanzetti, and the score earned him his second Nastro d'Argento, a year after his first for Metti una sera a cena (1969). [C31709]

**Takeaway:** The political films get the same treatment as the westerns -- two opposed sound-worlds, one drum underneath -- and the mechanism travels: a military drum under Algiers, a Verdi melody under a five-hour epic, a folk song under a trial, each time the music doing the arguing the dialogue cannot.

## 9. The Hollywood eighties

Days of Heaven (1978, Terrence Malick), released 13 September 1978, credits its music to Morricone with guitarist Leo Kottke and Camille Saint-Saens, earned Morricone his first Academy Award nomination, and was cut against a temp track of Morricone's own Novecento music, with Malick flying to Italy because Morricone did not fly; the temp-track detail is search-extract only, not verified end to end. [C31710] The Mission (1986, Roland Joffe), premiered in competition at Cannes on 16 May 1986, has a Morricone score recorded at CTS Lansdowne Studios in London that Morricone himself defined as a duet between a "Spanish" liturgical theme and a "Guarani" native theme, whose main theme "Gabriel's Oboe" runs 2:14 and was released 5 August 1986 on Virgin. [C31711] The Mission sold over 3 million copies worldwide, was ranked 23rd on the American Film Institute's list of the 25 greatest film scores of all time, and was ranked number 1 in a Variety poll of 40 film composers for the greatest film score of all time. [C31712]

The Untouchables (1987, Brian De Palma), premiered in New York on 2 June 1987 and released in the US on 3 June 1987, has an Academy Award-nominated and Grammy Award-winning Morricone score; De Palma wanted a triumphal piece for the police that Morricone did not like, Morricone wrote nine different pieces in total and told De Palma "Please don't choose the sixth!" because it was the worst, and De Palma chose the sixth. [C31713] Cinema Paradiso (1988, Giuseppe Tornatore), released in Italy on 17 November 1988, credits its music to Ennio Morricone and his son Andrea, began a Tornatore collaboration that lasted until Morricone's death in 2020, won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film and the Special Jury Prize at Cannes, and earned Morricone a BAFTA shared with Andrea and a David di Donatello for the score. [C31714]

The Hateful Eight (2015, Quentin Tarantino), released in the US on 25 December 2015, has Morricone's only original score for a Tarantino film, his first complete Western score in 34 years, and his first score for a high-profile Hollywood production since 2000. [C31715] For The Hateful Eight Morricone built the score on bassoon, contra-bassoon and tuba, a combination that shocked Tarantino; Tarantino's only brief was to compose music about snow, he left Morricone totally free, the score was recorded in Prague, and Morricone had still not seen the film when interviewed at its London premiere. [C31716] Tarantino announced at San Diego Comic-Con in July 2015 that Morricone would score the film, the first Western Morricone scored since 1981 (Buddy Goes West); Morricone composed 50 minutes of original music, and according to Variety he composed the score without even seeing the film (search-extract). [C31717] The Hateful Eight soundtrack is reported in the Quietus interview to include segments of Morricone's earlier scores for The Thing (1982) and Exorcist II: The Heretic (1977) alongside the new score; this is reported, not confirmed against the soundtrack album credits, and was not verified end to end. [C31718]

**Takeaway:** Hollywood did not change the method, it only changed the budget: the same man who scored a western with a whip crack now scores a gangster film with nine versions of one theme and a snow film with three bassoons -- the instrument changes, the procedure does not, and the director still gets told which version not to pick.

## 10. The Oscar ledger

Morricone received six competitive Academy Award nominations for Best Original Score, listed by the Academy by film year: Days of Heaven (1978), The Mission (1986), The Untouchables (1987), Bugsy (1991), Malena (2000) and The Hateful Eight (2015). [C31719] He received the Academy Honorary Award, listed by the Academy as the 2006 (79th) Academy Awards, presented by Clint Eastwood at a ceremony on Sunday 25 February 2007 at the Kodak Theatre, "in recognition of his magnificent and multifaceted contributions to the art of film music". [C31720] His only competitive win came at the 88th Academy Awards on Sunday 28 February 2016 at the Dolby Theatre in Hollywood, Best Original Score for The Hateful Eight, against Thomas Newman for Bridge of Spies, Carter Burwell for Carol, Johann Johannsson for Sicario and John Williams for Star Wars: The Force Awakens. [C31721]

At 87 he was at the time the oldest person ever to win a competitive Oscar, aged 87 years and 110 days, and his nomination made him at the time the second-oldest Oscar nominee in Academy history, behind Gloria Stuart. [C31722] Morricone and Alex North are the only composers to receive the Academy Honorary Award since its introduction in 1928. [C31723] His score for Once Upon a Time in America (1984) was ruled out of Oscar consideration because the US distributor reportedly failed to file the proper paperwork; this is search-extract only, taken from a mirror of the Morricone article, and was not verified end to end. [C31724] The Mission lost the Best Original Score Oscar to Herbie Hancock's jazz arrangements for Round Midnight, a result Morricone called a theft in a 2001 Guardian interview: "There was a theft!", on the grounds that Round Midnight used existing pieces and was not an original score. [C31725] Days of Heaven lost at the 1979 ceremony to Giorgio Moroder's Midnight Express, against Jerry Goldsmith for The Boys from Brazil, Dave Grusin for Heaven Can Wait and John Williams for Superman: The Movie. [C31726]

**Takeaway:** The ledger is the shape of the career in numbers: six nominations across thirty-eight years, five losses, one win at 87 -- and the one loss he never forgave was the one where the winner had not written an original score at all, which tells you what he thought the award was for.

## 11. The method: no dailies, no piano, add value

On his working practice Morricone said in a PopMatters interview: "I never watch the dailies. What I usually do is have a look at the rough or final cut, and I just get something from the story. Sometimes I start composing even before the director has shot anything. The dailies don't help me at all." [C31727] The shorthand "Morricone does not watch the film first" is imprecise and must not be used as stated: he does watch a rough or final cut, and sometimes composes before shooting, but he never watches dailies and refuses to judge a film before agreeing to score it, saying it would be wrong to see a film and then refuse it because he did not like it; the second half of this claim rests on a search extract of a National Catholic Register interview, not an end-to-end read. [C31728]

Barry Levinson said of Morricone: "He doesn't have a piano in his studio, I always thought that with composers, you sit at the piano, and you try to find the melody. There's no such thing with Morricone. He hears a melody, and he writes it down. He hears the orchestration completely done." [C31729] He did the composition, orchestration and arrangement of his scores himself, calling that the mark of a real composer, and named Argentine composer Lalo Schifrin, born 1932, as the contemporary he felt a kinship with because Schifrin also writes everything from first composition to orchestration. [C31730] Morricone stated his credo for film music in 2015: "the goal of music in a film is to convey what is not seen or heard in the dialogue. It's something abstract, coming from afar... and that must add something. It must add value to the film." [C31731]

On the Dollars Trilogy, budget limits kept Morricone from a full orchestra, so he used gunshots, cracking whips, whistle, voices, Jew's harp, trumpets and the new Fender electric guitar instead of orchestral arrangements of Western standards, using the sound effects to punctuate and comically tweak the action. [C31732] Early in his career Morricone turned everyday sounds into music, including tin cans and typewriters, and in 1962 he peppered Edoardo Vianello's cha-cha hit "Pinne, fucile, e occhiali" with added water effects, unusual instrumental sounds and unexpected stops and starts. [C31733] In 1968 alone Morricone reduced his work outside the movie business and wrote scores for 20 films in the same year. [C31734]

**Takeaway:** The method has three rules and no exceptions: never watch the dailies, never hunt for the melody at the piano, never write music that adds nothing -- and the twenty-films-a-year pace is not haste, it is what the method makes possible, because a man who hears the orchestration complete does not need to try it out first.

## 12. The laboratory and the players

From 1966 to 1980 (the biography's career section says "from 1964", flagged) Morricone was part of Gruppo di Improvvisazione Nuova Consonanza (G.I.N.C.), a Rome-based avant-garde ensemble dedicated to the development of improvisation and new music methods, which functioned as a laboratory working with anti-musical systems and sound techniques; known as "The Group" or "Il Gruppo", they released seven albums across the Deutsche Grammophon, RCA and Cramps labels, and Morricone played a key role as trumpet player and director, with the ensemble contributing to some of his 1960s and 1970s Italian soundtracks. [C31735] From 1964 to the group's disbandment in 1980 Morricone was a core member of Gruppo di Improvvisazione Nuova Consonanza, an avant-garde ensemble performing free improvisations with anti-musical systems and sound techniques, which released seven albums and contributed to his 1960s and 1970s scores including A Quiet Place in the Country (1969) and Cold Eyes of Fear (1971); the end date conflicts with a second passage in the same article saying the group existed until 1978, flagged, not settled. [C31736]

Alessandro Alessandroni, childhood friend: a 20-year collaboration beginning with A Fistful of Dollars (1964), his whistling and twanging guitar central to the Dollars Trilogy and Once Upon a Time in the West; he founded the vocal octet I Cantori Moderni in 1961; born 18 March 1925 in Rome per English Wikipedia, while the Spaghetti Western Database gives "March 16 or 18, 1925" (flagged); died 26 March 2017 in Rome. [C31737] Edda Dell'Orso, born Edda Sabatini on 16 February 1935 in Genoa: a soprano with a three-octave range whose wordless vocals were deployed as an instrument, first to revolutionary effect in the Leone westerns, as Jill's theme in Once Upon a Time in the West and in "The Ecstasy of Gold"; she married Giacomo dell'Orso in 1958 and joined Alessandroni's Cantori Moderni in 1960; she also provided vocals for Bruno Nicolai, Piero Piccioni, Luis Bacalov and Roberto Pregadio. [C31738]

Bruno Nicolai: born 20 May 1926 per the English Wikipedia lead against 26 May 1926 per its own infobox, with AllMusic and TMDB also giving 26 May (conflict, unresolved this run); died Friday 16 August 1991 in Rome, aged 65; a Santa Cecilia piano and composition classmate of Morricone who scored more than 100 films himself, mostly giallo and exploitation, including The Red Queen Kills Seven Times (1972), with his last known score for the 1988 TV series La coscienza di Zeno. [C31739] The Nicolai-Morricone relationship: Morricone said, "I chose a great musician and friend to be my conductor: Bruno Nicolai, who conducted almost every score of mine from that point on until 1974"; Nicolai conducted For a Few Dollars More (1965) and The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966), and co-scored Thrilling, Agent 505: Death Trap in Beirut, O.K. Connery and Dirty Heroes with Morricone; the partnership ended over disagreements about credit attribution, and a fan-site account (search-extract, uncorroborated) says Morricone did not attend Nicolai's funeral. [C31740]

Franco De Gemini, harmonica soloist: his playing is "Man with a Harmonica", the motif of Charles Bronson's character in Once Upon a Time in the West (1968); he played on more than 800 film scores, tutored Bronson on set in how to hold the instrument, founded Beat Records, and published his autobiography Cosi insegnai a Charles Bronson ad impugnare l'armonica in 2008; he died in Rome in July 2013, exact day not stated in any source read. [C31741] Morricone lived in Italy his entire life and never desired to live in Hollywood, and he never left Rome to compose his music and never learned to speak English. [C31742] On 13 October 1956 Morricone married Maria Travia, born 31 December 1932, whom he had met in 1950; Travia wrote lyrics to complement her husband's pieces, including the Latin texts for The Mission; together they had four children, Marco (b. 1957), Alessandra (b. 1961), conductor and film composer Andrea (b. 1964) and Giovanni (b. 1966), a filmmaker, and they remained married for 63 years until his death. [C31743] In 1958 he attended the summer courses for new music at Darmstadt in Germany, per the 1000.rz.deno.net biographical piece (which cites Ennio Morricone: In His Own Words), read as search extract only, flagged. [C31744]

**Takeaway:** The sound was never one man: a whistler, a wordless soprano, a conductor who became a rival, a harmonica player who taught the actor how to hold the instrument -- Morricone's genius was partly casting, and the laboratory where he learned to trust other players' ears was the improvisation group that played anti-music for fun.

## 13. The late years: tours, a Mass, a private farewell

Morricone wrote the score for the Mafia television series La piovra seasons 2 to 10 from 1985 to 2001, including the themes "Droga e sangue" ("Drugs and Blood"), "La Morale" and "L'Immorale", and worked as conductor of seasons 3 to 5; he also worked as music supervisor for the television project La bibbia ("The Bible"). [C31745] In the late 1990s he collaborated with his son Andrea on the Ultimo crime dramas, resulting in Ultimo (1998), Ultimo 2 - La sfida (1999), Ultimo 3 - L'infiltrato (2004) and Ultimo 4 - L'occhio del falco (2013), and in the 2000s he kept composing for television: Il Cuore nel Pozzo (2005), Karol: A Man Who Became Pope (2005), La provinciale (2006), Giovanni Falcone (2007), Pane e liberta (2009) and Come Un Delfino 1-2 (2011-2013). [C31746] In June 2015 Morricone premiered his Missa Papae Francisci (Mass for Pope Francis) at Rome's Chiesa del Gesu, with the Orchestra Roma Sinfonietta and choruses from the Accademia Santa Cecilia and the Rome Opera Theater. [C31747]

In February 2016 Morricone won his first competitive Academy Award for The Hateful Eight, at the 88th Academy Awards on 28 February 2016: at 87 he became the oldest person at the time to win a competitive Oscar; it was his sixth nomination, after Days of Heaven (1978), The Mission (1986), The Untouchables (1987), Bugsy (1991) and Malena (2000); his nomination had made him the second-oldest nominee in Academy history, behind Gloria Stuart; he had received the Academy Honorary Award in 2007. [C31748] The Correspondence (La corrispondenza), released 14 January 2016, directed by Giuseppe Tornatore and starring Jeremy Irons and Olga Kurylenko, was Morricone's final film score; Movie Wave counts it his eleventh film with Tornatore (not counting TV shows and commercials) against 13 in the Wikipedia director table (flag: count difference, TV and commercials explain it); the score is sparse piano, synth and electric guitar over the Prague orchestra, including the 13-minute meditation "Una stella, miliardi di stelle", and the soundtrack album (Warner Music, January 2016) runs 65 minutes. [C31749] The Tornatore collaboration began in 1988 with Cinema Paradiso, whose international version won the Special Jury Prize at Cannes 1989 and the Best Foreign Language Film Oscar, and whose score won Morricone a BAFTA (shared with his son Andrea) and a David di Donatello; he then scored every subsequent Tornatore film through The Correspondence; Malena brought a fifth Oscar nomination and a Golden Globe nomination, and The Legend of 1900 (1998) won him the Golden Globe for Best Original Score. [C31750]

He conducted his own scores in concert, normally with 180 to 200 musicians and vocalists under his baton, joining smaller pieces into suites of 15 to 20 minutes; he had performed over 250 concerts as of 2001, and made his North American concert debut on 3 February 2007 at Radio City Music Hall in New York. [C31751] The 60 Years of Music Tour opened on 15 January 2016 at the Prague O2 Arena with the Czech National Symphony Orchestra, and ran through 2016-2019 across Amsterdam, Antwerp, Bologna, Bratislava, Brussels, Budapest, Dublin, Helsinki, Krakow, London, Moscow, Paris, Rome, Stockholm, Vienna, Wroclaw, Zurich and more; the final concerts were announced on 14 December 2018, billed as his very last time conducting his own music, and his last live concert was in Lucca on 29 June 2019, at age 90, conducting seated. [C31752]

On Monday 6 July 2020 Morricone died at the Universita Campus Bio-Medico in Rome, aged 91, as a result of injuries sustained to his femur during a fall at home days earlier; his death was confirmed by his lawyer Giorgio Assumma, who said Morricone "passed away in the early hours of July 6 with the comfort of his faith" and was "preserved until the final moment full lucidity and great dignity." [C31753] Per his own written farewell statement ("There's only one reason why I want to say goodbye like this and have a private funeral -- I don't want to bother anyone"), the funeral was private, held in the hospital's chapel and attended only by his close family and by Giuseppe Tornatore; he was entombed in Cimitero Laurentino, and the music heard as the casket was blessed was his score for The Mission. [C31754] On 10 May 2016 Mondadori published his official autobiography, Ennio Morricone. Inseguendo quel suono. La mia musica, la mia vita, written in conversation with Alessandro De Rosa; the English edition, Ennio Morricone: In His Own Words, followed from Oxford University Press in 2019, and Morricone called it in the preface "the best book ever written about me, the most authentic, the most detailed and well curated. The truest." [C31755]

**Takeaway:** The last decade is the catalogue conducted back at him: a Mass for a Pope at 86, an Oscar at 87, a farewell tour conducted seated at 90, a private funeral at 91 -- and the music at the casket is The Mission, the score the Academy passed over, which is the last word on what he thought mattered.

## 14. The afterlife: the record, the film, the opera

Morricone Segreto, his first posthumous album, was released on 6 November 2020, which would have been his 92nd birthday, through Decca/CAM Sugar: a 27-track collection with 7 previously unreleased tracks, drawn from the CAM Sugar archives and covering his 1960s to early-1980s work, curated by Pierpaolo De Sanctis; the companion box set Dimensioni Sonore, due out 30 October 2020, collected 103 tracks of library music by Morricone and Bruno Nicolai. [C31756] Ennio, the documentary by Giuseppe Tornatore, premiered on 10 September 2021 out of competition at the 78th Venice International Film Festival; it runs 156 minutes, was built from interviews with more than 70 filmmakers and musicians including Bernardo Bertolucci, Clint Eastwood, Quentin Tarantino, Hans Zimmer, Bruce Springsteen, Joan Baez and Edda Dell'Orso, and won the Nastro d'Argento 2022 for Best Documentary Film. [C31757] The posthumous concert legacy continues on two tracks: the opera Partenope, commissioned in 1995, was first premiered on 12 December 2025, five years after Morricone's death; and "Ennio Morricone - The Official Concert Celebration", a large-scale arena production curated by Morricone and conducted by his son Andrea, toured Europe in 2022. [C31758]

**Takeaway:** The afterlife is the archive talking back: a secret record of the library music, a film that lets seventy collaborators speak, an opera that waited thirty years for its premiere -- the catalogue was so large that it keeps producing new work after the composer stops.

## 15. The Bacalov contrast: atmosphere-first against melody-first

Morricone's method, from the Dollars Trilogy passage read end to end and study #768: atmosphere first -- budget limits on A Fistful of Dollars forced gunshots, cracking whips, whistle, voices, Jew's harp, trumpets and Fender electric guitar instead of orchestral Western standards, and the sound effects punctuate and comically tweak the action; in Once Upon a Time in the West each principal carries a distinct leitmotif (harmonica for Harmonica, electric guitar and strings for Frank, wordless soprano for Jill) and the score was written before a frame was shot. [C31759] Bacalov's method, from study #768 read end to end: melody first -- Django (1966, Corbucci) is built on a singable title song, sung in English by Rocky Roberts and conducted by Bruno Nicolai, developed through brass versions, mariachi-brass tracks and a Hank Marvin-style guitar solo; Il Postino runs one melody through many instrumental rooms; Misa Tango (1997, Deutsche Grammophon 2000) sets the Mass to tango rhythm with the bandoneon as lead voice. [C31760]

Shared ground: Nicolai conducted for both men (the Django title theme for Bacalov; Morricone's scores until 1974); Bacalov was Morricone's assistant in 1958 (single source, see C31649); the two co-founded Forum Music Village with Piccioni and Trovajoli; they share the joint soundtrack album Per amore, the 1976 film directed by Mino Giarda (not a 2007 album -- see C31649); and in 1962 Bacalov arranged Rita Pavone's "Come te non c'e' nessuno" and "Pel di carota" while Morricone was arranging for RCA Victor in the same rooms. [C31761] The Tarantino test, per study #768's takeaway: Tarantino lifts both men's music, but a tune survives being lifted in a way a texture does not -- Bacalov's Django song and "La Corsa" recur in Django Unchained (2012) as songs, while Morricone's cues in Kill Bill and Inglourious Basterds recur as atmosphere; the contrast is the mechanism, not a ranking, and the next study should keep it that way. [C31762] Tarantino reused Morricone cues in Kill Bill, Death Proof, Inglourious Basterds (eight tracks, from The Big Gundown, Revolver and Allonsanfan) and Django Unchained; the original song "Ancora Qui", written with Elisa for Django Unchained (2012), was a contender for the Best Original Song Oscar nomination but was not nominated; Morricone presented Tarantino with a Life Achievement Award on 4 January 2013; he was misquoted in 2014 as claiming he would "never work" with Tarantino again; The Hateful Eight was his only original Tarantino score and his first complete Western score in 34 years. [C31763]

**Takeaway:** The two methods are the two halves of the same Rome: Bacalov writes the song and dresses it differently each time it returns, Morricone builds the room the song would have to walk into -- and Tarantino, who has stolen from both, proves the difference every time he lifts them, because you hum Bacalov and you feel Morricone.

## 16. The aesthetic logic

His honours included two Academy Awards (the Honorary Award in 2007 and the competitive Best Original Score award for The Hateful Eight in 2016), three Grammy Awards, three Golden Globes, six BAFTAs, ten David di Donatello, eleven Nastro d'Argento, two European Film Awards, the Golden Lion Honorary Award, and the Polar Music Prize in 2010. [C31764] The "500+" score count rests on no single audited filmography: the English Wikipedia lead says "more than 400 scores for cinema and television, as well as more than 100 classical works", while Decca Records, uDiscover Music, the Quietus and PopMatters all say over 500 film and television scores, a National Catholic Register interview from the early 2010s says around 450, and a 2016 Variety piece says nearly 500; the commonly cited figure is over 500, and no source in this study counts the filmography title by title -- so this study uses more than 400 as the conservative figure and carries the rest as a flag. [C31765]

The logic, stated plainly: the sound comes before the image, the noise is an instrument, the theme is an identity, and the music must add what the dialogue cannot say. [C31766] Everything else in the career is a variation on those four rules: the whip crack is a snare because the budget would not buy a snare; the whistle is a lead voice because a whistle cuts through a desert; the twelve-tone giallo score is a theme with the identity removed; the bassoon trio is a snowstorm because three bassoons sound like weather; and the wordless soprano is the one sound no orchestra can fake, which is why it returns in every decade of the work. [C31767] The paradox that holds the whole career together is that the most prolific film composer in the record is also the most instantly recognisable: twenty films in a single year, and you can still tell a Morricone bar from any other composer's in four notes, because the four rules never change even when the instruments do. [C31768]

**Takeaway:** Morricone's music is not background and not accompaniment; it is a second script, written first, that the film has to live up to -- and the reason the films feel slow is that the music refuses to hurry, which is the same reason the music outlives the films.
