# Peter Douglas Kennedy: Study Notes

**Site:** Peter Douglas Kennedy, b. Saturday 18 November 1922, London, England; d. Saturday 10 June 2006, age 83, death place not established in any source read. Folklorist and folk-song collector; BBC fieldworker and presenter; co-editor with Alan Lomax of the 10-volume *Folk Songs of Britain*; founder of Folktrax (1957). The younger son of Douglas Neil Kennedy and Helen Karpeles Kennedy, the boy who helped film a folk dance festival at twelve, the man who spent fifty-six years recording other people's songs and ended by turning the recorder on his own family at Christmas -- and the only figure in the whole revival who spans it twice over: born inside Sharp's institution, and then the one who carried its songs out of the institution onto tape, onto radio, onto television, and into a catalogue of his own making. [C34989]

## 1. London, 18 November 1922: born into the revival's first family

Peter Douglas Kennedy was born on Saturday 18 November 1922 in London, the younger son of Douglas Neil Kennedy and Helen Karpeles Kennedy. His elder brother was born in the April quarter of 1917 and died on active service on 8 April 1940 aboard HMS *Glowworm* -- whether his name was David or John is unresolved (see the doorway check). His mother had been elected Honorary Secretary of the English Folk Dance Society at its founding in 1911 and was the sister of Maud Karpeles, Cecil Sharp's amanuensis; his father would succeed Sharp as the Society's director. His great-grandfather David Kennedy (1825-1886 or 1887, sources differ by a year) had been a Scottish concert singer who toured the English-speaking world singing Scottish folksongs; his great-aunt Marjory Kennedy-Fraser (1857-1930) collected Gaelic folk songs and published three volumes of *Songs of the Hebrides* (1909, 1917, 1921), recorded on Edison-Bell wax cylinder. He was educated at Leighton Park, the Quaker school at Reading. What is set in this beginning is a child born not into a family that *joined* the folk revival but into the family that *was* its second generation: the songs were already in the house before he could read, the institution was already his father's workplace, and the archive was already his aunt's life's work. He never had to discover folk song. He had to decide what to do with an inheritance that arrived complete.

**Takeaway:** He is born inside the revival, not into it. The songs are furniture before they are a vocation -- and everything he does afterwards is a negotiation with an inheritance, not a discovery. [C34993]

## 2. The two Helens: a name trap in the family

There are two Helen Kennedys in this story, and careless reading merges them. One is his mother, Helen May Karpeles Kennedy (b. 17 May 1888), the Honorary Secretary of 1911, the concertina and pipe-and-tabor player on FTX-041. The other is his father's older sister, Helen Kennedy (b. Friday 5 July 1889, Edinburgh; d. Monday 13 October 1975), the Chelsea Physical Training School student who met Sharp in her second year, was present when William Kimber demonstrated at Chelsea in May 1909, and brought her younger brother Douglas into the Karpeles sisters' Folk Dance Club in 1911 -- the woman Douglas called "a skilled Highland dancer", whose memoir "Early Days" (*EFDS News* 7, May 1924) is a primary source for the Chelsea years. The aunt is the doorway into the dance for Douglas; the mother is the doorway into the dance for Peter. The two women share a first name, a generation, and a role as the person who brings a Kennedy man into the revival -- and the record, which mostly lets the aunt go after 1920 (teacher, inspector, war worker, widow, emigrant, returnee), keeps the mother in frame only because her son eventually turned the recorder on her at Christmas 1961. The trap matters because it is the shape of the whole family story: the women open the doors, the men walk through them for fifty years, and the archive remembers the walkers.

**Takeaway:** Two Helens, one name, two doorways. The aunt brings Douglas in; the mother brings Peter in -- and the archive keeps the men. [C34994]

## 3. Leighton Park, theatre lighting, and the Architectural Association

His boyhood interest was not folk song. It was theatre lighting and set design. At Stratford-on-Avon before the war, Memorial Theatre stage manager Barbara Curtis let him operate lighting and moving stages; on Alexander Korda's advice he studied three-dimensional design at the Architectural Association School of Architecture. Weeks before war broke out, he sailed with his family in a Galway turf-boat to Aran, staying in the cottage Robert Flaherty built for *Man of Aran*. The theatre training matters because it is the first mechanism in his life that is not inherited: a boy who learns to light a stage learns to frame a performance for watchers, to put the thing where the eye can find it, to run the machinery that makes the showing possible. He would spend the next sixty years doing the same work with songs instead of plays -- framing the singer, running the machine, keeping himself out of the light. And the Aran trip matters because it puts him, weeks before the war, in a cottage built by a documentary filmmaker for a film about fishermen -- the first time the family story touches film as a way of keeping a way of life, not just watching one.

**Takeaway:** He trains as a stageman before he trains as a collector. The framing comes first; the collecting is framing applied to song. [C34995]

## 4. The schoolboy cameraman: London, 15-20 July 1935

In July 1935, aged twelve, Peter helped film the world's first international folk dance festival in London, organised by his aunt Maud Karpeles, with dancers from seventeen or eighteen nations (eighteen per the IFMC obituary, seventeen per the Dunin study -- Yugoslavia was invited but absent). The festival programme ran 15-20 July 1935; he was born 18 November 1922, so he was twelve, turning thirteen only that November. The film itself was made by Doris Plaister for the EFDSS (FF-3300, 35 minutes, black-and-white and mute); Peter helped at the event and, seventy years later, assisted, speed-corrected and edited it in 2005. The Folktrax history adds the vivid detail: the Fenestrelle sword dancers from Piedmont, climaxing with the fool *Arlecchino* lifted atop plaited swords -- a group he filmed again decades later. The correction matters beyond pedantry: every secondary that says thirteen is repeating a number that cannot survive the calendar, and the number is the least interesting part. What matters is that his first act in the revival is not singing or dancing or collecting but *filming* -- standing beside the event with a machine, keeping it. At twelve he is already doing the thing he will do for the rest of his life: pointing a recording device at a performance that will otherwise vanish, and keeping the record.

**Takeaway:** His first act is filming, at twelve, beside a festival. The collector starts as a cameraman -- the machine before the method. [C34996]

## 5. War: RAF models and aerial photography, 1939-45

Called up at seventeen to the RAF, Peter used his design training to make topographical models preparing crews for landing assaults in North Africa and Italy. The Folktrax history, his own account, elaborates: stereo aerial-photo interpretation, Mosquito reconnaissance, support for the Dambusters, and training an American model-making unit staffed partly by Walt Disney Studios craftsmen. In 1945 he co-founded a theatrical company of American and British personnel. The war years are the one stretch of his life where the sources split between a spare corroborated core (called up at seventeen, model-making, the 1945 theatre company) and a richer self-reported elaboration that no independent source in this run confirms. The models matter even in their sparest form: a young man who builds three-dimensional terrain so that other men can rehearse an assault is learning to make a miniature of a place that other people will then act inside -- the same shape as the dance revival, where a demonstration team shows a dance so that watchers can then join in, and the same shape as the field recording, where a machine keeps a performance so that listeners can enter it later. The war is not a gap in the collecting life. It is the collecting life's first draft, in plaster and photographs instead of tape.

**Takeaway:** The war teaches him to build miniatures people act inside. The model table is the first recording device -- a place kept so others can enter it. [C34997]

## 6. Joining the EFDSS, 1948: the staff son

In 1948, aged twenty-six, Peter joined the staff of the English Folk Dance and Song Society, working first in the North East, then in the West Country. His father was the Society's director until retirement in 1961, so he worked under his father for thirteen years -- that is a fair inference from the dates, not a verbatim statement in any source read. The Society had just adopted a "folk dancing for all" policy; he played drums in the Folk Dance Band alongside his parents Helen and Douglas. He toured with a one-man band -- melodeon in the hands, foot-operated bass drum underfoot, calling the figures himself -- reviving village barn dancing for Young Farmers and youth clubs. The staff-son position is the central tension of his whole career: he enters the institution as an employee of his father's, inside a policy his father's generation wrote, and spends the next decade building the tools -- the van, the tape, the catalogue, the label -- that will let him outgrow it. The drum is the honest image of the job: he is not the caller and not the dancer, he is the time-keeper, the one who makes a roomful of strangers move together, and nobody watches the drummer.

**Takeaway:** He joins as the staff son and the drummer. The institution gives him the room; the drum gives him the job of keeping its time. [C34998]

## 7. The North East: learn by ear, then notate

His first collecting method, in Durham from 1947 as EFDSS North-East Regional Organiser, was to learn dance tunes by ear from local fiddlers and melodeon players and only then write the melodies down for publication -- sound first, notation second. *The Fiddler's Tune Book: 100 Traditional Airs* (EFDSS, 1951; Hargail, New York, 1951) and *The Second Fiddler's Tune Book* (1954) came out of it; the books are still in print, and the combined reissue of 200 tunes followed in 1994. The method is a deliberate reversal of Sharp's: Sharp's notebook took the tune out of the room and into print, and the print became the authority; Kennedy's ear takes the tune into the body first, and the print is only the receipt. A tune learned by ear carries the fiddler's tempo, his ornaments, his breathing; a tune taken down in shorthand carries the collector's hand. The difference is small on the page and total in the room: one method preserves a text, the other preserves a performance, and the whole of Kennedy's later career is the argument that the performance is the thing worth keeping.

**Takeaway:** Sound first, notation second. The ear is the first recorder -- the tune exists in the body before it exists on paper. [C34999]

## 8. The battery van: first recordings, 1950

In 1950, using a prototype Scophony-Baird reel-to-reel tape recorder powered by two batteries and carried in his Morris Cowley van, Peter started making audio recordings -- driving to informants and recording in their own locality, in pubs, kitchens and farms, rather than bringing singers to a studio. His first field recordings, in February 1950, were of the Bristol shantyman Stanley Slade (one source spells the surname Shade), made after acquiring the recorder on moving to Bristol late in 1949. Before portable tape, BBC fieldwork used EMI "midget" disc-cutters -- roughly gramophone-sized machines cutting acetate discs -- and mobile recording vans; Kennedy's Kent trip in 1954 still began by collecting "Midget Tapes" at Broadcasting House, and his BBC report logs discs as FMK 321 (15 inch) and FMK 322 (7.5 inch) with timings to the second. The van is the mechanism that changes everything: the collector stops being a person who invites singers into his world and becomes a person who drives into theirs, and the recording stops being a performance for a machine and becomes a room with a machine in it. A short, fragile disc side ends mid-song and the performance stops; tape lets a whole evening run unbroken, with the talk and the interruptions kept in. The van is also, plainly, a theatre: a Morris Cowley arrives, a machine is set up on a pub table, a singer performs to a box while the tape runs.

**Takeaway:** The van moves the studio to the singer. The room, the talk and the interruptions stay in the frame -- the recording keeps the evening, not just the song. [C35000]

## 9. Lomax arrives: England and Yugoslavia, 1950-51

Alan Lomax arrived in Britain late in 1950, and the two men became close friends and colleagues -- the defining professional partnership of Peter's life. In 1950-51 they worked together recording material for the *England* LP in Lomax's Columbia *World Library of Folk and Primitive Music* series; his 1951 recording of a folk festival at Opatija became the *Yugoslavia* volume, with notes by the Harvard oral-literature scholar Albert B. Lord. He also made field recordings in the Basque Country. The Lomax partnership matters because it is the first time Kennedy's work is framed as part of a world project rather than an English one: Lomax arrived with a Columbia series that treated the folk music of every country as one library, and Kennedy's English recordings sat on the same shelf as Yugoslav ones. The Opatija recording is the proof: a festival in Yugoslavia, recorded by an Englishman with the aid of the Yugoslav Council for Science and Culture, issued in New York. The collector who started in a Durham pub is now, within a year of his first tape, recording for a world library -- and the van, the batteries and the ear are suddenly the instruments of an international system, not a local hobby.

**Takeaway:** Lomax puts the van on a world shelf. The English pub recording and the Yugoslav festival recording are the same act, in the same series. [C35001]

## 10. The BBC scheme: fieldworker, 1952-57

In 1952 the BBC appointed him one of two principal fieldworkers on its newly established Folk Music and Dialect Recording Scheme, under Marie Slocombe -- founder of the BBC Sound Archive in 1936 and Sound Recordings Librarian from 1941 -- and Brian George, head of Recorded Programmes. He made hundreds of recordings across the British Isles, often with Seamus Ennis, Sean O'Boyle and his aunt Maud Karpeles. After each trip he filed a dated, day-by-day written report to Slocombe, logging who was recorded, where, on which tape, for how long, with edit notes ("Edit out talk in between") and failures -- colds, missed appointments, a police raid. Thirty-one such reports survive in the British Library archive, and the Peter Kennedy Archive site turns them into interactive pages with recordings and photographs attached to the text; over 650 performance events by 550 different performers are detailed in them. The report is the second instrument, and in some ways the more revealing one: the tape keeps the song, but the report keeps the day -- the drive, the wait, the singer who was ill, the session that never happened. A collecting trip without its report is a shelf of tapes with no map; with the report, it is a diary of encounters, and the encounters are the work.

**Takeaway:** The report is the second recorder. The tape keeps the song; the logbook keeps the day -- and the day is the work. [C35002]

## 11. Chain collecting: Kent, January 1954

Kennedy worked by referral -- each informant supplied names and addresses of relatives and neighbours, and he followed the chain across a county in days. The Kent trip of 14-18 January 1954, made with his aunt Maud Karpeles and documented in his report to Marie Slocombe, is the cleanest surviving example. On 14 January he "picked up Miss M. Karpeles and drove to B.H. to collect Midget Tapes"; over the following days he recorded Albert Beale and Charlie Scamp (seven songs from Scamp, the session interrupted by a police raid at the Royal Oak, Chartham Hatch), and made contact with the Scamp and Smith Traveller families in Kent and Suffolk. On 15 January Mrs Stanley (Bird), ill with tonsilitis, "gave us address of her sister Mrs. Smith, Melton Meadows... Her husband, Joe, plays fiddle and melodeon"; Kennedy pursued Phoebe and Joe Smith for two years before recording Phoebe in July 1956. The chain is the mechanism: a name on a page becomes a door, a caravan, a pub; the chain branches (a sister in Suffolk, a husband who plays fiddle) and sometimes snaps (a singer too ill, a session that never happens). It is also the mechanism that makes the collector dependent on the collected -- he cannot plan a trip, only follow it, and the map of the tradition is drawn by the singers themselves, one referral at a time. The police raid is the sharpest single image of field recording meeting the world: the machine is running, the song is being kept, and the law walks in.

**Takeaway:** One singer hands you the next. The map is drawn by referrals, not by planning -- and the chain snaps as often as it holds. [C35003]

## 12. As I Roved Out: describe, then play, 1953-58

Between 1953 and 1958 Kennedy presented the BBC Sunday-morning series *As I Roved Out* on the Light Programme -- he chose the programme's name and its signature tune, "As I roved". The format was a "describe, then play" sandwich: Kennedy and Seamus Ennis described a singer and a place, then played the field recording, inside a variety format with a chamber orchestra arranging the recorded songs and Spike Hughes as compere; producer Harold Rodgers conceived it as a variety programme because a Sunday audience might not take unadulterated folk singing. Programme 6 of series 2, "Sussex and Kent", was first broadcast on Sunday 8 May 1955. The contrast case is the earlier *Country Magazine*, which used *trained* singers to deliver songs collected by Francis Collinson: Kennedy played his own field recordings of country singers instead. The first series ran 27 September 1953 to 28 March 1954; the second from 3 April 1955; the run ended in September 1958, and the series count -- five or six -- is unresolved in the sources. Two 1953-54 programmes were later issued as FTX-253. The mechanism is framing as hospitality: a voice describes a kitchen in Keady, then the kitchen's own recording plays, and a studio orchestra answers it. The singer is not brought into the studio; the studio is brought, by description, to the singer.

**Takeaway:** Describe, then play. The broadcast does not import the singer -- it visits him, and lets his own recording speak. [C35004]

## 13. Song Hunter and the television first, 1953

Kennedy later co-presented *Song Hunter* with Lomax from Alexandra Palace -- the first television series featuring traditional performers (Harry Cox, Charlie Wills, Margaret Barry, Michael Gorman), produced by a newly trained David Attenborough -- and the follow-up *A-Roving* in 1968. The 1953 *Song Hunter* ran six twenty-minute episodes, was broadcast live, and no longer exists. The television work matters because it is the same mechanism as the radio work carried one step further: the singer is not only heard in his own voice but seen in his own place, and the broadcast frame has to hold a body as well as a sound. That the series was broadcast live and is now lost is the mechanism's own irony -- the most perishable form of keeping, the one that keeps nothing, used to show the people whose songs Kennedy spent his life keeping. Attenborough's presence is a footnote that reads, in hindsight, like a hinge: the young producer who would spend his career framing the natural world learns the frame on traditional singers.

**Takeaway:** Television adds the body to the voice. The series is broadcast live and lost -- the keeping that keeps nothing. [C35005]

## 14. Oss Oss Wee Oss: filming the custom, 1951/1953

In 1953 Kennedy, Lomax and cameraman George Pickow filmed the hobby horse ceremonies at Minehead and Padstow, producing his first cine-film production, *Oss Oss Wee Oss* (FF-1103) -- filmed in 1951 and released in 1953, per the Folktrax film catalogue, against the Association for Cultural Equity's bare "(1951)". The film is black-and-white, sixteen minutes (eighteen in one academic review), and includes the May Eve sequence in the Golden Lion at Padstow. Kennedy filmed customs as moving events with a crew and a plan: he briefed Lomax and Pickow on what to expect at Padstow before May Day 1953, and the camera stays with the hobby horse as it charges through a crowd. Later films return to the same customs decades later -- the Helston Furry Dance and the Abbots Bromley Horn Dance, both re-shot in colour in 1983. The mechanism is filming the event, not just the song: a custom is a crowd, a route, a time of day, a horse that charges, and none of it survives in a recording of the tune alone. The thirty-year return is the second half of the mechanism -- the same dance filmed twice, black-and-white then colour, so the keeping itself becomes a record of change.

**Takeaway:** Film the event, not the song. A custom is a crowd and a route -- and filming it twice, thirty years apart, keeps the change too. [C35006]

## 15. One Potato Two Potato: children on the bomb-sites, 1957

His first experience as a cameraman was working with Leslie Daiken in 1957 filming children's games in the bomb-sites of London -- *One Potato Two Potato* (FF-1107), 32 minutes 40 seconds, black-and-white, shot on Camden Town, Kings Cross and East End bomb-sites with assistance from the BFI Experimental Committee, and a prizewinner abroad. The film matters because its subject is the opposite of the folk dance festival: not a staged tradition performed for an audience but children inventing games in the ruins left by the war, with no audience at all. The mechanism is the same -- a camera kept on a performance that will vanish -- but the performance is unselfconscious, and the keeping is the only reason anyone later knows the games existed. The bomb-site is also, quietly, the war coming back into the story: the boy who made topographical models for landing assaults is now filming children playing on the ground those assaults were launched to take, and the film is the one record that treats the children's games as worth the same attention as a May Day custom.

**Takeaway:** The camera keeps what no one staged. Children on a bomb-site are a tradition too -- and the film is the only reason the games survive. [C35007]

## 16. Keeping time: the drum and the one-man band

Kennedy played drums in the London-based Folk Dance Band, whose members included his parents Helen and Douglas -- the drum is the time-keeper that makes a roomful of dancers move together. He later reduced this to a one-man band for his Village Barn Dance tours: melodeon in the hands, foot-operated bass drum underfoot, calling the figures himself. He hosted *Village Barn Dance* for the BBC Home Service with his eight-piece Haymakers Village Barn Dance Band, and worked as a freelance BBC producer on the series around 1950-52. The one-man band is the whole of Kennedy's method in miniature: one body running three channels -- the hands play the tune, the foot thumps the beat, the voice calls the next figure -- so that a roomful of strangers can dance without a band, without a caller, without an institution. It is also the honest image of his collecting: the man who keeps time for others, who makes the event possible and is not the event. Nobody watches the drummer, and nobody watches the collector; both are the condition that lets the performance happen.

**Takeaway:** One body, three channels: hands, foot, voice. The drummer is the condition of the dance, not the dance -- as the collector is the condition of the recording. [C35008]

## 17. Revival as performance: calling, clogging, and the Belles

Kennedy revived dances by *doing* them in public, not by writing about them: calling American-style square dances nightly at the Lyceum with Pat Shaw (after Princess Elizabeth was seen square-dancing in Canada; BBC *Square Dance Revels*), running a newspaper appeal that produced pre-war clog dancers, putting up a silver belt for the first post-war Northumberland and Durham clog championship (won by Jackie Toaduff), taking Toaduff to demonstrate at the Royal Ballet Schools -- the clog work later feeding into *La Fille mal gardee* and, generations later, *Billy Elliott* -- and reviving Plough Stot longsword and rapper teams and the Beaux of London City morris, including a women's side, "The Belles", against Morris Ring convention. The mechanism is revival as performance: the dance is not argued back into existence, it is danced back into existence, in front of people, with a caller shouting the next figure over a band and a clog dancer's feet becoming percussion. The Belles are the sharpest edge of it: a women's side dancing where the rules said they could not, inside an institution whose director was his own father. The revival, in Kennedy's hands, is not preservation. It is staging -- and the staging is the preservation.

**Takeaway:** Revival is performance, not argument. Dance the thing back into existence, in front of people -- and let a women's side dance where the rules said they could not. [C35009]

## 18. Skiffle and pop's edge: the self-reported claims

With John Hasted and Redd Sullivan, Kennedy is credited in the Folktrax history -- his own account -- with naming "skiffle" at the London Reel Club in Cecil Sharp House, from the "swish-swashing" guitar sound, and with bringing young skiffle groups to George Martin at Abbey Road, including the Liverpool school group the Quarrymen; the same account says he arranged, via Brian Epstein, for Francie McPeake to make uilleann pipes for John Lennon, and that Lonnie Donegan recorded skiffle "within only a few days". No independent source in this run corroborates the Quarrymen, Epstein or Lennon claims; the Association for Cultural Equity independently confirms his role in "the formation and popularization of the first skiffle groups" and the Princess Margaret melodeon anecdote (he once tried to teach Princess Margaret to play the melodeon, and composed "Princess Margaret's Fancy" for her, FTX-323), but that is corroboration of the milieu, not of the specific claims. The skiffle episode matters for the study precisely because it cannot be verified: it is the point where Kennedy's own telling becomes the only source, and the study has to decide what to do with a collector who is also his own archive. This study carries the claims as Kennedy's account, not as established fact, and does not build prompts on them.

**Takeaway:** The skiffle story is his telling, not a verified fact. A collector who is his own archive has to be quoted, not believed -- and the study says so. [C35010]

## 19. The splice: a song built from two singers

On *The Folk Songs of Britain* LPs, songs were frequently assembled by splicing together recordings of two or more different singers -- different voices, sometimes from different places, edited into one continuous "song". It was a deliberate editorial technique and a much-criticised one. Wikipedia puts it plainly, citing Mike Yates's 2000 review of the Rounder Harry Cox reissue: "A curious, and frequently criticised feature of these albums is that songs are often presented by splicing together recordings of two or more singers." Kent Trad adds that Kennedy "was not averse to doctoring field recordings before releasing them". The splice is the mechanism that breaks the promise of the battery van: the van kept the room, but the splice keeps the song at the room's expense -- a verse sung in one voice, in one room, cuts mid-song to a different voice in a different room, and the seam is the mechanism. It is defensible as editing (the best verse from each singer, the complete song no single singer remembered whole) and indefensible as documentation (the listener hears a performance that never happened). Both readings are true, and the study does not choose between them: the splice is where Kennedy stops being a keeper and becomes an author, and the argument about him starts there.

**Takeaway:** The splice builds a song no one sang. One verse in one room cuts to another voice in another room -- and the seam is where the keeper becomes an author. [C35011]

## 20. Thematic sequencing: Folk Songs of Britain, 1961

With Alan Lomax, assisted by Shirley Collins, Kennedy edited ten LPs organised by *theme*, not by region: Volume I *Songs of Courtship*, II *Songs of Seduction*, III *Jack of All Trades*, IV-V *The Child Ballads*, VI *Sailormen and Servingmaids*, VII *Fair Game and Foul*, VIII *A Soldier's Life for Me*, IX *Songs of Christmas* (Caedmon) / *Songs of Ceremony* (Topic), X *Animal Songs* (Caedmon) / *Songs of Animals and Other Marvels* (Topic). The regional division sat underneath -- collecting mainly in England (Kennedy and Lomax), Scotland (Hamish Henderson), Ireland (Seamus Ennis, Sean O'Boyle) -- while A. L. Lloyd edited the series for Topic and James Boswell designed the sleeves. Caedmon issued the series in the USA from 1961 (TC1142-1146, TC1162-1164, TC1224-1225); Topic reissued it in the UK in 1968 and 1970 (12T157-161, 12T194-198), with the reissue span given as 1968-1971 by the Association for Cultural Equity. Some volumes were reissued expanded on CD by Rounder in 2000. The mechanism is sequencing by subject: songs from a Norfolk farm labourer, a Barra Gaelic singer and a Belfast family land side by side because they share a subject, not a postcode. The tradition is reorganised from a map into a set of human situations -- courtship, seduction, trades, ballads -- and the listener hears the subject before the place. Do not prompt this as regional volumes; the volumes are thematic, and the region is only the collecting route underneath.

**Takeaway:** Ten volumes by theme, not by region. Courtship sits beside courtship wherever it was sung -- the subject organises the tradition, not the map. [C35012]

## 21. Book-plus-tape: Folksongs of Britain and Ireland, 1975

The 1975 Cassell book *Folksongs of Britain and Ireland* (one word, "Folksongs", on the title page) gives 360 songs in a fixed order -- first by language (Scots Gaelic, Irish Gaelic, Welsh, Manx, Cornish, Channel Islands French, Romany and Tinkers' cant, and more), then by theme (courtship, false and true love, seduction, wedlock, trades, country life) -- and each song is illustrated by a Folktrax tape in the same order (FTX-001 to FTX-031), so book and recording run in lockstep; every entry carries full text, translation, music notation, background notes and references to other collected versions. It won the Library Association McColvin Award for the most outstanding reference book of 1975; the Oak paperback followed in 1985. The mechanism is a parallel system: a numbered spine, where song number 49 in the book is track 1 on FTX-005, and text, tune and voice are all indexed to the same number. The book is not an accompaniment to the tapes and the tapes are not an accompaniment to the book; they are two halves of one catalogue, and the number is the hinge between them. It is the most complete statement of Kennedy's archival idea: the tradition is not a pile of songs but a numbered, cross-referenced system, and the number is what lets a stranger find their way in.

**Takeaway:** Book and tape run in lockstep on one numbered spine. Song 49 in the book is track 1 on FTX-005 -- the number is the hinge between text and voice. [C35013]

## 22. The FTX number: a catalogue as a filing system

Folktrax releases carry FTX numbers that encode their place in the system: 001-031 the 1975 book's illustrations; 032-034 the three Harry Cox portrait albums; 035 and onward outstanding individual singers; 042-046 Romany and Gypsy families; the 100s regional singers; 103-114 mummers and customs; 121 barn dance; 141-142 shanties; 179-201 children's games; 203 worksongs (Portland stone quarries, recorded October 1954); 253-257 the 1950s radio programmes; 259 and onward Scots collections; 601-605 the 1951 Yugoslav festival; FF-1100s and FF-2200s the films. The catalogue eventually ran to more than 450 CDs and DVDs (over 500 audio and video compilations per the Folktrax history -- the count differs by source). The mechanism is a Dewey decimal for song: a number that tells you what kind of thing you are about to hear before a note sounds. It is also the mechanism that turns a private collection into a public one -- without the numbers, the tapes are a hoard; with them, they are a library, and a stranger can ask for FTX-203 and be given the Portland quarrymen. The numbers are the least romantic part of Kennedy's work and the most durable: the label ceased trading on 30 September 2007, a year after his death, but the numbers still index the tradition.

**Takeaway:** The number is the filing system. FTX-203 means Portland quarrymen before a note sounds -- without numbers the tapes are a hoard, with them a library. [C35014]

## 23. The family album: FTX-041, Christmas 1961

At Christmas 1961 Kennedy turned the recorder on his own family at Waldringfield, Suffolk: his father Douglas, then sixty-eight, sings Lowland Scots ballads learned from his grandfather David Kennedy (1825-1886 or 1887) and English songs collected by Cecil Sharp; his mother Helen plays English concertina and three-hole pipe and tabor; Peter plays fiddle, guitar and melodeon, joined by his wife and children. The Folktrax detail page reads: "FTX041 -- HOW DO YOU DO, SIR? AT HOME WITH THE KENNEDY FAMILY... recorded Waldringfield, Suffolk, Christmas 1961", and the performer index confirms Douglas "rec by PK (son), Waldringfield, Woodbridge Christmas 1961 5 inch RTR#423-5 (3 reels) / FTX-041 Family album". The track list, twenty-two items, includes "The Golden Vanity", "Hame Cam Oor Gude-Man at Een", "Ilkla Moor Baht 'At" and "The Trees They Do Grow High". FTX-015 is also a Kennedy Family album drawing on the same reels -- FTX-041 is not the only one. The mechanism is the domestic recording: the family as its own informants, three generations around one microphone at Christmas, a ballad passing from grandfather to father to son in a single take. It is the moment the collector's two lives meet -- the man who spent a decade driving a van to strangers' kitchens turns the machine on his own living room, and the family repertoire itself becomes the archive. Four generations of "Singing Kennedys" perform their own inheritance, and the inheritance turns out to be the same act as the fieldwork: somebody sings, somebody keeps it.

**Takeaway:** Three generations around one microphone at Christmas. The family repertoire is the archive -- and the collector's living room is the last field site. [C35015]

## 24. The assignment: rights paperwork and the controversy

Folktrax, founded in 1957 as a non-profit company, worked by *assignment*: informants and other collectors signed over rights, which Kennedy presented as protecting performers and paying royalties from broadcasting and publication -- Folktracks and Soundpost Publications were created to protect performers' and collectors' rights and royalties. The same paperwork is the centre of the posthumous controversy. The Musical Traditions appraisal by Rod Stradling (2006, "The Darker Side", 2008) counted 28 opinions against him and 8 in favour, and alleges that singers signed forms assigning copyright of the songs themselves, were told they could not sing them in public again or to any other collector, were published without permission or knowledge, and in most cases were paid nothing -- naming Bob Roberts, Lizzie Higgins, Bob and Ron Copper, Belle Stewart, Scan Tester, Jimmy MacBeath, Herbert Smith and John MacDonald as unpaid and unconsulted, with Jeannie Robertson, Phoebe Smith and Cyril Poacher paid "insultingly small amounts" -- and that he added his own recorded accompaniments to unaccompanied singers. Kent Trad puts the same point more mildly: "the rather proprietorial attitude he took towards the material... claiming copyright not just on the recordings, but on the songs themselves." The mechanism is a form on a kitchen table, signed after the song: the same song later appearing on a cassette the singer never saw. The study does not adjudicate the controversy -- the appraisal rests on testimony this run did not independently verify -- but it records it, because the assignment is the mechanism that turns keeping into owning, and the argument about Kennedy is an argument about whether a collector can own what he keeps.

**Takeaway:** A form on a kitchen table, signed after the song. The assignment turns keeping into owning -- and the controversy is whether a collector can own what he keeps. [C35016]

## 25. Dartington: fellowship and the Cider Press, 1969-

In 1969 Kennedy was invited by the Dartington Hall Trustees at Totnes on a three-year fellowship to lecture to degree students (dance, drama, music, art) and build cross-cultural activity; he advised on folk events at the Barn Theatre and the Cider Press at Shinners Bridge. His library and archive became DITA, the Dartington Institute of Traditional Arts, in the Archway Studio. Martin Graebe's tribute, first-hand, places him running the Cider Press Centre at Dartington in the 1970s -- a shop selling folk books and records and cider, a studio, a folk club -- and helping on the film *Children of the Moor* (with John Bartlett, for Westward Television), recording songs in Lustleigh Orchard. The Folktrax history also claims "for 15 years he was a degree lecturer at Dartington College of Arts", which sits uneasily with a three-year fellowship from 1969; whether the lecturing continued beyond the fellowship is not resolved. The Dartington years are the archive becoming an institution of its own: the tapes and books that had lived in a van and then in a house now have a studio, a shop, a lecture room. The Cider Press is the honest image of it -- folk books and records sold beside cider, the archive as a village shop, open to whoever walks in.

**Takeaway:** The archive becomes an institution of its own. Books and records sold beside cider -- the collection as a village shop, open to whoever walks in. [C35017]

## 26. Gloucester and the Deck House interview, 20 April 1983

He left Dartington for Gloucester, where his library and archive continued in his house in Brunswick Square as the Gloucester Institute of Traditional Arts. The Folktrax film catalogue records FF-2203, *The Kennedy Interview*: Douglas Kennedy (1893-1988) in retirement in his "Deckhouse" at Waldringfield, Suffolk, interviewed by Peter and Beryl Kennedy, 20th April 1983 -- colour, three hours on three DVD-Rs, tape references RTR 955-6. The date is carried here as verified from the Folktrax film catalogue (see the doorway check: two legs could not verify it, one leg verified it verbatim from the catalogue, and the direct read wins). The interview is the last witness made into a record: the son who filmed a festival at twelve, who recorded his parents at Christmas 1961, now sits his ninety-year-old father down in the Deck House and asks him for his life, on film, for three hours. It is also the doorway this study came through -- the #804 study's top pick for #805 rests, in part, on this interview being the last account of Douglas's life in his own words. The Deck House spelling is one word in the catalogue ("Deckhouse"); the place is the same house at Waldringfield where the family album was recorded twenty-two years earlier.

**Takeaway:** The son interviews the father for three hours on film. The boy who filmed a festival at twelve ends by filming his father's life -- the keeping comes home. [C35018]

## 27. The Traditions Reference: archiving as a system

Kennedy built a cross-referenced archive -- the "Traditions Reference", bringing together the *written* versions of each title with the *recorded* ones, searchable by title, performer, geography, type or subject keyword -- and housed it successively at Dartington (DITA) and then Gloucester (in his house in Brunswick Square). On his death the collection was dispersed by agreement: around 3,000 books and journals plus around 3,000 78s, 3,000 LPs, 500 CDs and DVDs and 50 HMV test pressings to Halsway Manor (the Kennedy Grant Memorial Library); the sound, photo and paper archive to the British Library (MS Mus. 1771: 1,660 open-reel tapes, 1,600 photographs, 500 DAT tapes, 170 boxes of correspondence and paper files per the Peter Kennedy Archive site -- the British Library catalogue gives 1,500 audio tapes, 200 video tapes, 1,562 photographs and 40 boxes, a different counting date and basis, cited with the source attached); the film library to TSW (Television South West, Plymouth) in safe storage since 11 December 1995; Topic Records obtained the recording rights and reissues them in the *Voice of the People* series. The mechanism is a keyword typed once pulling a song's printed version, its recording, its photograph and its film together -- the archive as a system, not a pile. And the dispersal is the mechanism's second half: after 2006 the same material fans out to three different homes, and the system that held it together now lives in the catalogue numbers, not in one building.

**Takeaway:** One keyword pulls the printed song, the recording, the photograph and the film together. After 2006 the system lives in the numbers, not in one building. [C35019]

## 28. Late work: Rounder, the Lomax Archive, Voice of the People

Since Lomax's death in July 2002, Kennedy worked with the Alan Lomax Archive in New York, and in his later years edited the *England* and *Harry Cox* volumes, the two *Child Ballad* volumes and the *Songs of Seduction* volumes of the *Alan Lomax Collection* on Rounder (the Rounder reissues of 2000 include *Classic Ballads of Britain and Ireland* Volumes 1 and 2, *Songs of Seduction*, and *Harry Cox: What Will Become of England?*, recorded in 1953 by Lomax and Kennedy, with notes by Kennedy). Topic Records' *Voice of the People* series carried his field recordings forward: a third series in 2014, selected by Reg Hall -- *Good Humour for the Rest of the Night* (Northumberland and Cumberland dance music, recorded 1954), *The Barley Mow* (Suffolk recordings and film, 1953-56), *The Flax in Bloom* (Ulster, recorded with Sean O'Boyle 1952-53), *Orkney* (recorded 1955) -- and a London-Irish series in 2016, two albums compiled from Topic's archive, the Peter Kennedy archive at the British Library and private collections. Wikipedia's "six albums of his recordings plus three partially" is accounted for as the four 2014 albums plus the two 2016 albums; the three partial albums are nowhere itemised and are not verified. He kept recording into the 2000s, played locally in Gloucester as The Hooleys with Ben Hall and Pete Hayward, helped set up Glosfolk, co-founded the Traditional Song Forum, and was the subject of the first "Noon Interview" at the Cheltenham Folk Festival in 1997. The late work is the collector becoming the editor of his own life's recordings -- and of Lomax's, too, after Lomax's death: the partnership outliving one partner by four years.

**Takeaway:** The collector becomes the editor of his own recordings -- and of Lomax's, after Lomax's death. The partnership outlives one partner by four years. [C35020]

## 31. The reputation split: 28 against, 8 for

The Musical Traditions appraisal by Rod Stradling (2006, with "The Darker Side" in 2008) counted 28 opinions against Kennedy and 8 in favour. The criticisms centre on the copyright assignments, on issuing others' recordings without permission or payment, and on dubbing accompaniments onto field recordings; the appraisal itself records that his scholarship was "rarely questioned". The split is the study's central unresolved question, and this study does not resolve it: the appraisal rests on testimony and on the assignment forms themselves, neither of which this run independently verified, and the defence -- that the assignments protected performers and paid royalties -- rests on Kennedy's own account. What the split establishes, without adjudication, is that Kennedy is the one figure in the revival about whom the argument is not about the songs but about the keeping: whether the man who kept the tradition also owned it, and whether the forms he asked singers to sign were protection or appropriation. ## 32. The natural-history crossover: playback as stimulus

At the BBC in Bristol, Kennedy worked with the ornithologist Ludwig Koch and Desmond Hawkins on field trials of the parabolic reflector microphone for spot-miking birds; in the zoo he played back recordings of African elephants to their Indian brothers and recorded their response -- the Folktrax history notes "we still have the historic blank tape in our archive!" -- and the same account claims a role in co-founding the BBC Natural History Unit. The mechanism is playback as stimulus: play a recording *to* its subject and record what answers -- the field-recording idea turned back on itself. It is a bonus mechanism for this study, evidenced from Kennedy's own account and not independently corroborated in this run, and it earns its place because it shows the collecting idea escaping folk song entirely: the tape is not only a keeper of performances but a probe, a way of asking a question to which only the subject can answer. The blank tape is the joke that proves the point -- the elephants answered, and the answer was silence, and the silence was kept too.

**Takeaway:** Play the recording back to its subject and record what answers. The tape is a probe, not just a keeper -- and even the silence gets kept. [C35024]

## 34. What the mechanisms add up to

Put the sixteen mechanisms together and they describe one machine with four stages. First, *go*: the van, the batteries, the chain of referrals -- the collector drives to the singer. Second, *keep*: the tape, the report, the film -- the performance is kept with its room, its day, its crowd. Third, *order*: the splice, the thematic sequence, the book-plus-tape spine, the FTX number, the Traditions Reference -- the kept things are arranged into a system a stranger can use. Fourth, *own*: the assignment, the label, the catalogue -- the system is held together by paperwork that says whose it is. The first three stages are the work everyone admires; the fourth is the work everyone argues about. And the argument is the proof that the machine worked: nobody argues about the ownership of a pile of tapes in a van. They argue about the ownership of a library, and Kennedy built the library.

**Takeaway:** Go, keep, order, own. The van, the tape, the number, the form -- and the argument is the proof the machine worked. [C35026]

## 35. The second generation: what changes when the collector is born inside the revival

Peter Kennedy is the second generation, and the second generation changes the work. Sharp's generation had to find the songs -- walk into a village, hear a singer, write the tune down before it was gone. Kennedy's generation inherited the finding and had to solve the keeping: the songs were already known to exist, the problem was how to hold them as sound, as film, as a system, across fifty years and three dispersals. That is why his mechanisms are all instruments of keeping rather than instruments of finding: the van, the tape, the report, the catalogue number, the film, the form. It is also why his life reads as a series of rooms -- the drawing room cleared for dancing, the pub table with the machine on it, the living room at Waldringfield at Christmas, the Deck House at ninety, the shop at Dartington selling books beside cider. The revival, in his hands, stops being a movement and becomes a set of rooms with machines in them, and the machines are what survive. The first generation found the tradition. The second generation built the furniture it sits on.

**Takeaway:** The first generation found the tradition; the second built the furniture it sits on. His rooms -- pub, living room, Deck House, shop -- are the revival made durable. [C35027]

## Aesthetic logic

Kennedy's aesthetic is the aesthetic of the keeping, not of the kept. The songs themselves are other people's -- the beauty is in the apparatus that holds them: the van arriving, the machine set up on a pub table, the logbook filling line by line, the number that tells you what you are about to hear, the book and the tape running in lockstep on one numbered spine. Visually, his world is small rooms with machines in them, and the drama is latency: the wait before the singer starts, the tape running while nothing happens, the chain that branches or snaps, the form signed after the song. The palette is the palette of the archive -- paper, ink, tape brown, the black of a disc, the grey of a winter field report -- against the sudden colour of the performance itself: the hobby horse charging, the clog dancer's feet, the children on a bomb-site. The motion is not the motion of the dance but the motion of the keeping: the van driving, the tape spooling, the logbook page filling, the number being written on a sleeve. A piece after Kennedy should feel like a room with a machine in it, and the machine should feel like it is waiting -- because the keeping is the event, and the song is what the keeping is for.

**Takeaway:** The beauty is in the apparatus, not the song. Small rooms, machines waiting, the tape running while nothing happens -- the keeping is the event. [C35028]
