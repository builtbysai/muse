## The life

Pieter Symonsz Potter was born in Enkhuizen in 1597 and died in Amsterdam in 1652 (EN Wikipedia; WGA; NGA; Knox; Rijksmuseum). The birth year is hedged in the study because the Rijksmuseum gives "1597/1601" in its Paulus biography and NGA gives "c. 1600"; no source read explained the alternatives, and no day or month is known for either event, and no burial record for Pieter himself was found. His father was Simon Jacobsz., a glassmaker; his mother Dieuwe Simons (WGA). "Symonsz" means son of Simon.

He was first trained as a glass painter and in 1628 moved to Leiden to learn painting in oils (EN Wikipedia, citing RKD). WGA: he "presumably trained with his father as a glass painter in Enkhuizen, where he worked until 1628, when he entered the Leiden glassmakers' guild as a master, becoming its headman in 1629" -- "presumably" is WGA's word, keep it. Knox: he purchased Leiden citizenship on 15 May 1628 and joined the glass-makers' guild as a master glass painter. "During the late 1620s, as his interests gradually shifted from glass painting to easel painting, his style became increasingly naturalistic" (WGA). No oil-painting teacher is named in any source read; do not name one. The "trained under Rembrandt in Leiden, 1628" claim appears only on WahooArt/ArtsDot reproduction-sales pages, which copy each other; no scholarly source read supports it, and the study does not repeat it. What IS documented is proximity a generation later: in the second half of the 1630s the family lived in Amsterdam's Sint-Anthoniebreestraat, "with the art dealer Hendrick Uylenburgh (c. 1587-1661) and Rembrandt (1606-1669) residing nearby" (Rijksmuseum). Proximity is not tutelage.

The family moved to Amsterdam in 1631 (Paulus aged 5); Pieter bought Amsterdam citizenship on 14 October 1635, "probably joining the painters' guild shortly thereafter" (Knox; Rijksmuseum says he became a member of the Guild of St Luke "shortly thereafter", undated). He was still active in Leiden circles in 1631: he "served as a witness for the baptism of de Heem's son Cornelis in Leiden in 1631" (WGA), and "would also have known the van Steenwyks there" (Harmen and Pieter van Steenwyk, and their uncle David Bailly). Jan van Goyen was his neighbour in Leiden and an influence on his early monochromatic landscapes (WGA). In 1640 a large landscape bought directly from him was the most highly valued painting in the inventory of the Leiden historian Jan Orlers (1570-1646) (WGA) -- the one documented case of a named buyer paying a top price for a Pieter landscape, a collector, not a court patron. No patron of Pieter himself was documented in any source read; the documented patrons (Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen, Amalia van Solms, Nicolaes Tulp) belong to his son Paulus. Do not transfer them to the father.

The family was in The Hague from 1647 -- the Rijksmuseum says Paulus applied to the Hague Guild of St Luke in 1649, "where his father and family had already moved in 1647" -- and back in Amsterdam by 1652, where Pieter died (EN Wikipedia; Rijksmuseum). The 1647-1649 Hague stay rests on the Rijksmuseum and a dealer listing; no direct archival citation was read.

Two guild dates are carried as unresolved, not as facts. EN Wikipedia (via RKD) says Pieter joined the Delft Guild of St Luke in 1646 -- but the Rijksmuseum and Knox document the same date (6 August 1646, 12 guilders, "the standard fee paid by artists from outside the town") for Paulus. It is possible the 1646 Delft date belongs to the son and has been attached to the father, the two P. Potters being chronically confused. And EN Wikipedia says he "became a member of the Confrerie Pictura in 1647 (although this record in The Hague guild archives may have been for drawing lessons for his oldest son Peter)" -- impossible as stated, because the Confrerie was founded in The Hague in 1656 by 48 dissatisfied painters (EN Wikipedia Confrerie Pictura; Piet Bakker, Early Modern Low Countries 2019: "it is now considered an indisputable fact that the artists of The Hague disassociated themselves from the St Luke's guild and founded a brotherhood in 1656"), four years after Potter's death. The study reports the 1647 claim only as a known error. WahooArt/ArtsDot repeat "Confrerie Pictura (established in 1647)" -- they copy Wikipedia's error and back-date the Confrerie's founding to fit it; that is error propagation, not a source.

His wife was Aaltje / Aechtie Paulusdr Bartsius, sister of the painter Willem Bartsius (RKD via Wikipedia; NGA; Knox; Rijksmuseum "Aechtie Pouwels"). Houbraken's "daughter of Paulus Bertius" is the same confusion -- "Paulusdr" (Paulus's daughter) misread as a surname -- and Houbraken's extra generation, a painter son "Pieter I Potter" making Pieter the grandfather of Paulus, does not exist in the RKD/NGA/Rijksmuseum accounts. The Rijksmuseum notes Paulus's parents "had at least eleven children, none of Paulus's siblings is known today as an artist" -- so Pieter II and Maria, named by RKD as Pieter's children, are not to be called painters. Paulus "trained first with his father, whom he quickly surpassed" (Knox); his only documented apprenticeship is with Jacob de Wet in Haarlem, 12 May 1642 (Rijksmuseum, citing Noord-Hollands Archief). Houbraken also calls Pieter city secretary of Enkhuizen -- Houbraken-only, unconfirmed, and Houbraken is already discredited on this artist's family. The gilded-leather manufacture ("for some time his father was a manufacturer of gilded leather hangings outside the city walls") is snippet-only from the Paulus Wikipedia page, not read in a primary or scholarly source; hedged here, not asserted.

His one documented guild office is Leiden glassmakers' guild headman, 1629 (WGA). No deacon or office role in any painters' guild was found. No pigment or binder analysis, no underdrawing or panel-preparation evidence, no documented studio practice, pupils (other than his son), or workshop size was found. No etchings or drawings are securely attributed to Pieter (the drawings discussed in the sources are Paulus's). The Wikipedia infobox image is labelled "Self-portrait" but no source read confirms the identification -- do not use it as fact; the 1634 self-portrait in cuirass and plumed beret (W10) is itself only REPORTED via an Alamy caption.

## M1. Glass to oil -- the flat-to-volumetric turn

The core technical story, and the only one the sources support with dated works. Glass painting is a flat, linear, outline-driven craft: vitreous paint on glass, fired, with light passing through. WGA's stylistic reading is the best-evidenced account of the transition: his first history scenes, e.g. Joseph Accused by Potiphar's Wife (1629, private collection), "reflect the mannered style of elongated figures and tilted space found in the more conservative field of glass painting"; the merry-company and history scenes after 1630, e.g. A Conversation (1631, Prague, National Gallery), show "more naturally proportioned figures and still-lifes carefully arranged within simple, box-like spaces." In other words, the early oils still carry the glass-painter's flatness -- elongation, tilted ground plane -- and by 1631 he has learned oil's volumetric space. That before/after pair (1629 vs 1631) is the study's strongest technique evidence. No surviving glass work by Potter was found anywhere in the sources consulted; the glass phase is documented biographically, not by an extant object. Its visible trace is in the oils, and the study says so plainly rather than describing glass panels that cannot be produced.

**Takeaway:** build the picture twice, once flat and once volumetric -- first the elongated figures on a tilted ground plane, the glass-painter's manner, then the same scene re-set in a box-like space with naturally proportioned figures and a still life arranged as solid volumes. The watchable behaviour is the turn itself: the ground plane righting itself, the figures shortening into proportion, the flat pattern becoming a room you could walk into.

## M2. The guardroom as stage -- soldiers at leisure, no violence

The guardroom scene (cortegaerdje, a corruption of the military term corps du garde) was "formulated and initially popularized by the Amsterdam painters Pieter Codde (1599-1678) and Willem Duyster (1598/99-1635)" and "came into vogue in the 1620s and remained popular throughout the century" (Leiden Collection, Wieseman 2017). The appetite was tied to the resumed war with Spain after the Twelve Years' Truce ended in 1621, running to the Peace of Munster (1648). Potter did not invent it; he belongs to the first follower wave. Steigrad (citing Rosen): "Such notoriety probably led Codde and his contemporaries William Duyster, Pieter Quast, Pieter Potter, and Jacob Duck to formulate these unique scenes; and Codde was the most dominant and innovative of the group." Rosen's 2006 definition (Artibus et Historiae no. 53): an interior "hosting soldiers and their camp followers passing the time in various activities", free of violent action. The scenes are "devoid of violence", showing "soldiers' off-duty hours spent gambling, drinking, sleeping, and carousing with camp followers", in requisitioned quarters, with class distinctions maintained (the officer recognisable by superior dress, attitude, prominent placement), "theatricality on a stage-like setting in which a series of scenes occur simultaneously with spot lighting" (Steigrad, citing Rosen 2006/2020). Potter's variant within the genre: "Deserted churches as backdrops for the Guardroom Scenes of Jacob Duck, Pieter Quast and Pieter Potter were commonly employed" -- barn and church-like hollow interiors rather than Codde's tidy rooms. His guardrooms cluster c. 1630-32 (W03 triktrak c. 1630; W07 barn with booty 1632), i.e. he picked the theme up at the moment of its invention in Amsterdam, just after his own move there in 1631, or possibly while still in Leiden via the Amsterdam market. Motifs: soldiers smoking clay pipes, playing triktrak or dice on a drum pressed into service as a table, foreground still lifes of military kit -- cuirass, bandolier, muskets on a rack, trunk -- the genre's signature "object portrait", a register Potter's still-life training made him fluent in. Gambling, drinking and smoking carried a moral charge (idleness, cheating, the unpredictability of a soldier's life), which is why the scenes read as both cosy and censuring. Do not confuse: "Guardroom Interior with Soldiers Smoking and Playing Cards" (c. 1640, Leiden Collection) is by Gerard ter Borch the Younger, not Potter.

**Takeaway:** build a hollow interior -- a barn or a deserted church -- as a stage, with several small scenes happening at once under one spot of light: soldiers gambling on a drum, smoking, sleeping, a woman among them, the officer standing out by dress and placement, the military kit arranged in the foreground as a still life of its own. The watchable behaviour is the simultaneity: the game going on while the sleeper sleeps and the smoker smokes, the officer lit and the men in shadow, nothing violent ever happening.

## M3. The box-like corner -- still life as volumes

"His clear definition of space in terms of three-dimensional volumes also characterizes his still-lifes" (WGA). The still life is a box-like corner, objects placed as solid volumes -- a transferable mechanism. Rustic Still-life (1631, Warsaw, National Museum, inv. M.Ob.450) is discarded utensils, old dishes heaped in the corner of a barn -- a monochrome still life, "stylistically and thematically close to works by Jan Davidsz. de Heem", whom Potter knew in Leiden (he witnessed the baptism of de Heem's son Cornelis in 1631). The corner is the whole device: a barn corner, a heap of used things, the volumes defined by light, the picture small enough to hold in the hand. The barn-interior strand runs alongside it: A Barn Interior (1637, oil on panel, 37 x 50 cm VERIFIED, private collection) is the inside of a barn as a container for still-life detail and small figures. The early still lifes are monochromatic, under Van Goyen's influence; the Vanitas Still-life (1636, Berlin) is "more tightly and colourfully painted" and recalls Harmen and Pieter van Steenwyk and David Bailly. So the palette trajectory is monochrome/tonal early 1630s, tighter and more colourful mid-1630s. The 1887 Cyclopedia independently describes his pictures as "painted in grayish tone; the treatment is powerful and elegant" -- consistent with a tonal painter, though the Cyclopedia is error-ridden elsewhere and is not trusted as a sole source.

**Takeaway:** build a corner, not a table -- a barn corner with a heap of discarded utensils, each object a solid volume defined by light, the space box-like and readable. The watchable behaviour is the stillness of the heap: nothing moves but the light, the volumes holding their places, the corner containing the whole picture.

## M4. The vanitas line -- skull, globe, hourglass, and the lion seal

The most considered serial project of the career after the guardrooms is the vanitas line, running 1636 to 1646. Vanitas Still Life, 1636 (Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, Gemaldegalerie): a globe, a skull, some books, letters and an hour-glass -- Potter's earliest signed and dated vanitas, "betray[ing] the influence of the Leiden still-life painters Harmen and Pieter van Steenwijck", less apparent in his later work (Rijksmuseum catalogue, Bikker 2007 no. 249, as quoted second-hand). Vanitas Still Life, 1646 (Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam): a vertical-format vanitas -- a skull, a small lamp, air bubbles, old books, dice, a flute and a book of music, pleasures and knowledge as transience -- plus a striking large seal with a lion, the Great Seal of the United Provinces. The catalogue calls it a rather monochrome, predominantly brown work, "probably painted quite quickly, given its simple composition", and no other vanitas attributed to Potter is dated as late as this one. Its provenance is unusually traceable to the 17th century: the estate of Adriana Balckeneynde, who married Potter's son Paulus. Dimensions 54 x 41 cm are REPORTED (Bikker 2007 no. 249 as quoted on artsdot/wahooart); the Rijksmuseum's own object page for that painting was not located in this session, and the object number SK-A-2358 that the sales pages attach to it is catalogued by the Rijksmuseum itself as a "Stevers" painting, not Potter's -- do not cite SK-A-2358 as Potter's Vanitas until resolved. The late work keeps the Leiden toolkit but loosens it: the 1646 Vanitas is a late, economical monochrome, the latest-dated vanitas attributed to him.

**Takeaway:** build a vanitas as a small vertical cabinet picture -- skull, globe, hourglass, books, dice, a flute -- in one predominantly brown monochrome, painted quickly and plainly, with the Great Seal of the United Provinces as the one large official object among the pleasures. The watchable behaviour is the inventory: each object of pleasure arriving in turn and then standing still, the seal last, the brown never warming into colour.

## M5. The pastoral staffage landscape -- the seed Paulus inverted

Potter's landscapes are staffage landscapes -- shepherds and cattle animate a landscape. Landscape with Ruins, 1636 (oil on panel, 40 x 40 cm VERIFIED, private collection): a square-format early Leiden-period landscape of the kind WGA links to Jan van Goyen's influence. Jacob Urging Leah and Rachel to Flee from Laban, 1638 (Mauritshuis, inv. 409): in a pastoral landscape under dense foliage, Jacob -- red garment, staff in hand, strongly lit -- gestures toward his two wives seated on the ground, one cradling an infant, the other with a child beside her; Genesis 31, the flight from Laban. The biblical conversation happens in a pastoral setting with livestock nearby, which is exactly the bridge to his son's work. Shepherd Boy and Young Shepherdess (dated 1638, REPORTED via Singleton 1908) is the shepherd pair with their flock -- the pastoral figure-pair that became the Potter family signature -- though its location is contested (Mauritshuis vs Budapest vs unknown; do not treat the Mauritshuis attribution as settled). The red-chalk drawing Landscape with Two Shepherds with Cattle by a Ruin (Rijksmuseum RP-T-1938-138, VERIFIED, object page read end to end) is the same grammar on paper: two herders, cattle, a ruin. Paulus inverted the hierarchy: the animal becomes the portrait subject, "making them the focus of his pictures, rather than as a backdrop for human action" (Sotheby's Paulus essay). Pieter's Jacob (1638) is the hinge: a biblical scene staged as a pastoral with livestock, painted when Paulus was thirteen and learning in the workshop. The first landscapes were "probably" produced in Leiden (WGA -- hedged), the early ones monochromatic, Van Goyen-influenced.

**Takeaway:** build a landscape with a ruin and a small human story in it -- shepherds, cattle, a gesturing figure in a red garment -- the animals as staffage, small against the land. Then, in the same picture, let one animal grow until it becomes the portrait: the father's landscape with the son's inversion inside it. The watchable behaviour is the hierarchy flipping: the shepherds talking, the cattle grazing, and then the animal holding the centre while the humans shrink to the edge.

## M6. The father eclipsed -- the signature confusion

WGA: Pieter "was overshadowed by his son, Paulus Potter, with whose works his have often been confused. Pieter signed his paintings P. Potter, whereas Paulus typically signed Paulus Potter." Hofstede de Groot, Catalogue Raisonne vol. 4 (1912), p. 599: works of the father "show, on the whole, little resemblance to the pictures of his son Paulus, [but] they are often ascribed to Paulus because the signature is similar." His rule of thumb: pictures later than 1652 cannot be by the father; those dated earlier than 1640 can scarcely be by the son. The confusion is structural, not accidental, and it contaminates even the reference works: the 1887 Cyclopedia's Pieter entry attributes Paulus's Stag-Hunt and a 1662 landscape to Pieter (ten years after his death), and gives his birth year as 1587. The RKD correction -- father, not grandfather -- is the same confusion in the family tree: Houbraken inserted a whole extra generation, a painter "Pieter I Potter", between Pieter and Paulus. The study keeps the two P. Potters separated with the signature rule and the 1652/1640 date bounds. Paulus, baptised 20 November 1625 in Enkhuizen, trained first with his father, "whom he quickly surpassed" (Knox); his earliest dated work is from 1640; he entered Delft's Guild of St Luke in 1646 -- the same year his father did -- and painted The Young Bull at 22. The father's highest-valued work in his lifetime was a landscape, not a guardroom: the Orlers inventory (1640) valued the large landscape bought directly from him above everything else in the collection.

**Takeaway:** build two pictures side by side with the same signature -- "P. Potter" on one, "Paulus Potter" on the other -- and let the viewer sort them: the father's small cabinet picture (guardroom, barn corner, vanitas) against the son's life-size animal portrait. The watchable behaviour is the sorting: the father's picture staying small and brown while the son's animal grows to fill the frame, the signature the only thing they share.

## M7. The barn as container -- booty, smoke, and women

The guardroom theme moved into the barn, and the barn changed it. Soldiers and Young Women Smoking Beside Their Booty in the Interior of a Barn, 1632 (Adolphe Schloss collection, RKDimages 210166; present location NOT FOUND -- the Schloss collection was seized and looted in WWII and its pictures dispersed): soldiers and young women smoking in a barn interior, beside plundered booty -- the guardroom theme with women present, unlike the all-male guardroom core. A Barn Interior, 1637 (oil on panel, 37 x 50 cm VERIFIED): the inside of a barn as a container for still-life detail and small figures. The barn is Potter's container the way the deserted church was Duck's and Quast's: a hollow farm building big enough to hold soldiers, women, booty, smoke and a still life at once, its timber and straw giving the brown tonality a reason. Two Peasants Smoking in a Tavern (sold at Christie's, lot 541, price realised EUR 1,875) is the low-life smoking piece, close kin to the guardroom soldiers' leisure. The "Straw-Cutter" in the Rijksmuseum could not be verified -- Singleton (1908) calls it his better work in the Rijks, the Cyclopedia lists "Straw Cutters, Still-Life (1646), Amsterdam Museum", but no Rijksmuseum object record for it was found; do not list it as a confirmed holding.

**Takeaway:** build a barn interior as a container -- soldiers and young women smoking beside a heap of plundered booty, the smoke rising, the barn's timber framing the scene, the booty arranged in the foreground as a still life of stolen things. The watchable behaviour is the idleness: the smoke curling, the game unplayed, the booty unsorted, the barn holding them all without judgement.

## M8. The small cabinet picture -- everything under 55 cm

The pattern of the oeuvre is its scale. Every dimension established in this study is under 55 cm on the long side: Landscape with Ruins 40 x 40 cm, A Barn Interior 37 x 50 cm, the 1646 Vanitas 54 x 41 cm (REPORTED), A Musical Company in an Interior 40 x 53 cm (REPORTED), Soldier in an Interior 38.1 x 27.3 cm (REPORTED), Perseus and Andromeda 29.2 x 38.8 cm (REPORTED). Potter worked small, mostly on panel, mostly under 55 cm -- consistent with the cabinet-picture market, not with large altar-scale history painting. The exception proves the rule: the large landscape in Jan Orlers' 1640 inventory, bought directly from him, was the most highly valued painting in the collection -- a large landscape, title unknown, dimensions unknown, whereabouts unknown, and the best-documented lost Potter. His landscapes, not his guardrooms, commanded his highest prices in his lifetime. The oeuvre is a steady cabinet-picture practice across genre, landscape, still life and history, with the guardroom and barn pictures clustering c. 1630-32 and the vanitas line running 1636-46. No late "breakthrough" is documented. The most useful evolution claim: glass mannerism (1629) to box-like naturalism (1630-31) to Leiden monochrome landscape and still life (1631-36) to pastoral history and late monochrome vanitas (1638-46).

**Takeaway:** build everything at cabinet size -- a picture you hold in the hand, under 55 cm, on panel -- and then, once, build the one large landscape that was worth more than all of them, and leave it lost: the frame empty, the title unknown, the inventory entry the only trace. The watchable behaviour is the scale: the small pictures arriving one after another, each complete in a hand's breadth, and the lost large one implied by the gap between them.

## Evolution

The arc the dated record supports is not a breakthrough but a steady cabinet-picture practice, and the most useful evolution claim is the sequence: glass mannerism (1629) to box-like naturalism (1630-31) to Leiden monochrome landscape and still life (1631-36) to pastoral history and late monochrome vanitas (1638-46).

Enkhuizen, to 1628 -- glass painting. Son of the glassmaker Simon Jacobsz.; "presumably trained with his father as a glass painter in Enkhuizen" (WGA). No surviving glass work by him was found; its visible trace is W01, the 1629 Joseph with elongated figures and tilted space.

Leiden, 1628-1631 -- the turn to oil and to naturalism. "During the late 1620s, as his interests gradually shifted from glass painting to easel painting, his style became increasingly naturalistic" (WGA). After 1630 the merry-company and history scenes (W05, W02) show naturally proportioned figures and still lifes "carefully arranged within simple, box-like spaces", and his still lifes gain "clear definition of space in terms of three-dimensional volumes." Leiden is where the still-life line starts: the monochrome Rustic Still-life (W06, 1631, de Heem circle) and the tighter, more colourful Vanitas (W11, 1636, Steenwyck/Bailly circle). His first landscapes were probably made in Leiden; the early monochrome landscapes suggest Jan van Goyen, "his neighbour in Leiden".

Amsterdam, 1631-1652 -- consolidation, small scale, all genres. He settled in Amsterdam in 1631. Late work keeps the Leiden toolkit but loosens it: the 1646 Vanitas (W16) is described by the Rijksmuseum catalogue as quickly painted, simple in composition, predominantly brown -- a late, economical monochrome, and the latest-dated vanitas attributed to him. The 1638 Mauritshuis Jacob (W14) shows the history subject fully absorbed into landscape: the biblical conversation happens in a pastoral setting with livestock nearby, which is exactly the bridge to his son's work. Guild memberships come late and are muddled in the sources: Delft Guild of St Luke 1646, Confrerie Pictura 1647 (Wikipedia/RKD), with the note that the 1647 Hague record "may have been for drawing lessons for his oldest son Peter". Do not present the guild dates as a career climax; they look administrative.

What stays constant. (1) Small scale throughout: every established dimension is under 55 cm on the long side. (2) All genres at once: history, merry company, guardroom, genre, farm landscape, still life, vanitas -- WGA: "produced paintings in all the main genres". (3) The tonal palette: monochrome early, tighter and more colourful mid-1630s, predominantly brown again late. (4) The pastoral staffage: shepherds and cattle animate the landscapes from Leiden to the end.

Where the record does NOT support a clean arc. (1) There is no late breakthrough and no documented change of direction after 1638; the guardroom and barn pictures cluster c. 1630-32 and the vanitas line runs 1636-46, but the oeuvre is a steady practice, not a development with a climax. (2) The guardroom is not the career: EN Wikipedia (RKD) says he is "known mostly for genre pieces, farm landscapes and still lifes", and the Orlers inventory shows a landscape, not a guardroom, was his highest-valued work in his lifetime. (3) The late chronology is thin: after 1638 there are few securely dated anchors (1642, 1646), so any claim about a "late style" rests on the 1646 Vanitas and a handful of undated cabinet pictures. (4) The glass phase has no surviving object: the whole glass-to-oil story rests on WGA's reading of two dated pictures (1629 vs 1631) and the biographical record.

## Aesthetic logic

Potter's aesthetic is the small picture that contains a whole room, painted in a brown that never quite leaves the glass. Everything is cabinet size -- under 55 cm, on panel -- and everything is tonal: the early monochrome landscapes, the box-like still lifes, the predominantly brown late vanitas. The largeness is in the container: a barn holds soldiers, women, booty and smoke; a deserted church holds a guardroom; a corner holds a heap of discarded dishes; a landscape holds shepherds, cattle and a biblical flight. The glass-painter's flatness never fully leaves him: the 1629 Joseph is elongated and tilted, and even the 1631 Conversation, for all its new volumetric space, arranges its still life "carefully" inside a simple box -- the glass-painter's window become a room. The guardroom is the thesis: a stage-like interior where several small scenes happen at once under one spot of light, the officer lit and the men in shadow, the military kit in the foreground as a still life of its own. The pastoral is the quiet half of the same thesis: the same small figures, the same cattle, but now the landscape is the container and the humans are the staffage -- and the son, learning in the workshop, saw that the staffage could become the portrait. The honesty limit is the one WGA states flatly about the father: he "was overshadowed by his son, Paulus Potter, with whose works his have often been confused." His afterlife is in the son's animals and in the guardroom genre, not in a school of his own -- and the highest price his work ever fetched in his lifetime was for a large landscape that is now lost, its title unknown, its inventory entry the only trace.

## Network

The family knot: father Simon Jacobsz., glassmaker of Enkhuizen, presumed teacher (WGA -- "presumably"); wife Aechtie Paulusdr Bartsius, sister of the painter Willem Bartsius (RKD via Wikipedia; Oud Holland 2008: "Their daughter Aagje later married the landscape and figure painter Pieter Simonsz. Potter ... and gave birth to the famous painter Paulus Potter"); son Paulus Potter (bapt. 20 November 1625, Enkhuizen), trained first with his father in the Amsterdam studio, "whom he quickly surpassed" (Knox); children Pieter II and Maria named by RKD, not documented as painters (legs 1 and 3).

The brother-in-law: Willem Bartsius (c. 1612 Enkhuizen - in or after 1639), history painter, only six signed works surviving, about ten more attributed, active less than a decade in the 1630s; "He might have accompanied his brother-in-law Pieter Potter to Leiden in 1628" (WGA Bartsius biography); Alkmaar Guild of St Luke 1634, where he took on the pupil Abraham Meyndertsz; moved to Amsterdam 1636; "little is known of him after 1639". Joint Enkhuizen business with Potter: on 30 July 1636 Potter, as guardian of his children, together with Bartsius, claimed two years' rent on a house on the Spaensche burchwal (Oud Holland, citing Bredius) -- a genuine family-and-business link, not just a name on a tree (leg 3).

The Leiden circle: Jan van Goyen (1596-1656), Leiden neighbour, named influence on the early monochromatic landscapes; the link doubles in the next generation -- by 1649 Paulus moved to The Hague, "next to Jan van Goyen", and "from 1649 to 1652 Potter lived in the house of the painter Jan van Goyen". Van Goyen is the only figure adjacent to both Potters (leg 3). Jan Davidsz. de Heem (1606-1684): Potter witnessed the baptism of de Heem's son Cornelis in Leiden in 1631; the Rustic Still-life (1631) is "stylistically and thematically close to works by Jan Davidsz. de Heem", and the Vanitas (1636) "recalls paintings by Harmen van Steenwyk and Pieter van Steenwyk ... and their uncle David Bailly" (WGA). De Heem was already studied as #557; Cornelis de Heem as #560 -- both excluded as candidates (leg 3).

The guardroom circle: Codde #566, Duyster #565, Quast #571, Duck #567, Palamedesz. #568, van Velsen #570, ter Borch #569 -- all already studied. Potter is the remaining named formulator from Steigrad's list; his study closes that set. Ter Borch's guardroom precursors per Sotheby's (ter Borch lot 33) are "Pieter Codde, Willem Duyster, Simon Kick and Jacob Duck" -- Potter is not in that particular list, so do not claim him as a ter Borch precursor; his guardroom standing rests on Rosen via Steigrad (leg 3).

The collector: Jan Orlers (1570-1646), Leiden historian -- a large landscape bought directly from Potter was the most highly valued painting in his 1640 inventory. The best patronage fact for Pieter himself. Do NOT transfer Paulus's patrons to the father: Amalia van Solms bought Paulus's The Farmyard, and Nicolaes Tulp owned several Paulus paintings and invited Paulus to Amsterdam in 1652 -- those belong to the son's network (leg 3).

The neighbours, not teachers: Hendrick Uylenburgh (art dealer) and Rembrandt -- Amsterdam neighbours in the Sint-Anthoniebreestraat, late 1630s (proximity only). Claes Moeyaert is a possible teacher of Paulus, not of Pieter; do not attach him to Pieter (legs 1 and 3).

The geography as network: Enkhuizen (birth, glass, to 1628) -- Leiden (glass guild master 1628, headman 1629; oils; van Goyen and de Heem contacts; Bartsius possibly alongside) -- Amsterdam (from 1631; father's studio where Paulus trained; Codde a neighbour on Sint Antoniesbreestraat per EN Wikipedia Paulus, hedged) -- Delft guild 1646 (institutional, residence unproven) -- died Amsterdam 1652. Every move is a network move: each city supplies a named contact (leg 3).
