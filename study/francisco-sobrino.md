# Francisco Sobrino: Study Notes

**Site:** Francisco Sobrino Ochoa, born 19 February 1932 in Guadalajara, Spain, died 10 May 2014 in Bernay, France, aged 82 -- Spanish kinetic artist, GRAV co-founder, the man who stacked flat sheets of smoked Plexiglas into depth: superposed and slotted together like building toys, tinted squares overlapping to make new tones where they cross, the whole shape-shifting as the spectator walks around it; then the same lattice built in mirror steel, so the street and the sky enter the work; then springs that carry a push from one form to the next, counter-rotating motors, spheres left to the wind, and finally solar cells and a whole bank building whose floors and staircases move with projected light. He moved from Buenos Aires to Paris in 1959 with Julio Le Parc, co-founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel in 1960 and GRAV in 1961, and stayed with the problem of light, space and the moving spectator for fifty years (C6401, C6403, C6405, C6407, C6413, C6417).

## The life: from Guadalajara grids to the building that moves

Francisco Sobrino Ochoa was born in Guadalajara, Spain, in 1932 (C6401, C6402). The day is flagged, not hidden: 19 February 1932 appears only in Wikidata-derived pages (French, Spanish and Russian Wikipedia) and an astrology site; no museum, gallery, foundation or dealer biography read end to end gives a day or month, all give the year only (C6419). This study uses 19 February 1932 with that caveat, and treats it as the weakest date in the file (C6553). He was born into a working-class family in Guadalajara and lived through the Spanish Civil War there as a child (C6402). He began studying painting and sculpture at the Escuela de Artes y Oficios in Madrid from 1946 to 1948 -- the Spanish Wikipedia and the MACVAC text say 1946-1949, one year longer; the 1946-1948 range has the weight of the sources (C6402).

He moved to Argentina in 1949 and studied at the Escuela de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires from 1950 to 1957, where he met Hugo Demarco, Julio Le Parc and Horacio Garcia Rossi, came into contact with the Asociacion Arte Concreto-Invencion, and began working in geometric abstraction (C6403). The foundation biography gives a 1949-1958 residence span with a teaching diploma; the 1950-1957 enrolment dates from Sicardi are the cleaner claim for study purposes (C6403). One source says he "moved to Argentina in 1946", which contradicts every other source and looks like a confusion with the 1946 Madrid start; it is not used (C6403).

In 1959 Sobrino moved to Paris with Le Parc, where he lived and worked for the rest of his career (C6404). The Artviewer conservation text says 1958; 1959 has two sources against one and is used here, with the variant flagged (C6517). What he found in Paris was Vasarely: Morellet's own account, quoted in #620 and carried into #621, says "The three Argentine artists, Horacio Garcia Rossi, Julio Le Parc and Francisco Sobrino, first visited Vasarely, who pointed them to Francois and Vera Molnar, then to me and to Vasarely's son Yvaral" (C6301). Sobrino is one of the three Argentines Vasarely pointed to Morellet. His work in this period was predominantly black and white, methodical shape progressions with optical effects (C6406).

In 1960 he co-founded the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel, which held its first exhibition in his studio that December, and in 1961 the Centre became the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel (GRAV), with the six-name core roster of Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein and Yvaral (C6405). The wider Centre founding circle was eleven names, including Demarco, Francois Molnar, Sergio Moyano Servanes and Francisco Garcia Miranda; the six-name GRAV core is stable, and the two lists must not be merged (C6405, C6509). GRAV's programme, as the Guggenheim page puts it, centred on the social function of art as a collective product rather than an individual one, and on light and movement not as ends in themselves but as ways of modifying an environment and creating an unexpected situation to which the audience could respond (C6406, C6404). GRAV was active in Paris from 1960 to 1968 and agreed to dissolve in November 1968 (C6412). The foundation biography repeats the dissolution under 1969 as well; that is a duplication typo in the source, not a second dissolution (C6412).

Evolution: from 1958 he used the straightforward language of geometric forms to eliminate subjective content, producing two-dimensional black-and-white grid-patterned works, then progressions and systematic sequences in search of virtual movement, in oil, gouache and collage, broadening his palette to black, white and colours (C6406). In 1960 he executed reliefs based on superposed flat geometrical forms in white, black and coloured Plexiglas; in 1961 he began using grey "smoked" transparent Plexiglas, the series called Espaces indefinis, Transformations instables -- each form occupying an undetermined position in space, matter escaping from the reality of its presence (C6407). From 1962 he built Structures permutationnelles in aluminium, modular square forms fitted together by juxtaposition and superposition so the forms lost the sense of their position in space; in 1963 the same series in mirror-polished steel, whose mirror effect incorporated and reflected forms, movement and light, creating virtual images constantly reconstructed as spectators moved (C6408). His concern for spectator participation led to playful modular works in motion: the spectator moved a form and the impulse was transmitted via springs to other forms, producing a series of uncontrolled movements -- Pulsations (C6408). In 1965 he incorporated electric light and mechanical movement (Oppositions indefinies, Rotations opposees); in the second half of the 1960s he made kinetic objects the viewer could manipulate (C6408). In 1970 he introduced random movement in works of spheres held together by a metallic structure on a fulcrum (Libres dans le vent) (C6413). In 1971 he designed costumes for Francoise Adret's ballet Requiem, set to music by Ligeti, each dancer wearing a dominant colour of a three-colour combination (C6413). In 1972 he began twisting sculptures, first in Plexiglas, then bronze, then stainless steel; in 1974 he experimented with stained glass (Mur lumineux, 200 x 1200 cm, Palais des Congres, Paris); in 1976 he investigated solar energy, producing his first renewable-energy sculpture with solar cells in 1981 (Scultura autoenergetica) (C6413). In 1979 he designed and built a Kinetic Bank for the Banco Exterior de Espana in Guadalajara, described as "the first kinetic architecture in the world", black and white with projections on floors, staircases and walls; it was later demolished (C6413). In 1992 he returned to a play of light and shadow on white in modular works in pressed paper or Plexiglas (Torsions opposees, Blanc sur blanc); in 1995 he made Lumiere-couleur in colourless transparent Plexiglas, whose juxtaposed forms exposed to daylight caused a prismatic effect that changed as spectators moved; he continued this research into reflection, refraction and dispersion of light until his death in 2014 (C6413).

Exhibition spine: first Centre exhibition, in his studio, December 1960; GRAV at Galerie Denise Rene, Paris, 1961, and the 2nd Paris Biennale the same year; Arte Programmata, Milan, Venice and Rome, 1962; 3rd Paris Biennale (L'Instabilite - Le Labyrinthe) and La Inestabilidad, Buenos Aires, 1963; Documenta III, Kassel, 1964 -- one Garcia Rossi biography dates this 1963, which is wrong; Documenta III ran in 1964 (C6410); The Responsive Eye, MoMA, New York, 1965; Une Journee dans la rue, GRAV, Paris, and Kunst Licht Kunst, Eindhoven, 1966; Lumiere et Mouvement, Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1967, described as a landmark in the history of kinetic art; GRAV games room A la recherche d'un nouveau spectateur, Museum Ostwall, Dortmund, 1968; retrospective 1958-1998, Guadalajara, 1998, with sculptures along the main street and a labyrinth of 64 cubes (80 x 80 x 80 cm) in eight colours that spectators could walk through; Dynamo: Un siecle de lumiere et de mouvement dans l'art 1913-2013, Grand Palais, Paris, 2013; Francisco Sobrino: Structure and Transformation, Sicardi Gallery, Houston, opened 30 May 2014, weeks after his death; solo exhibition, Espace de l'Art Concret, Mouans-Sartoux, 2019 (C6405, C6409, C6410, C6411, C6412, C6414). His work is held by the Albright-Knox, Buffalo; Centre Pompidou, Paris; Hirshhorn, Washington DC; Museum of Fine Arts, Houston; Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires; Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice; Tate Modern, London; Tel Aviv Museum of Art; and the Museo Francisco Sobrino, Guadalajara, among others (C6416). The museum in Guadalajara was agreed with the city council on 12 December 2008, to be housed in the old municipal slaughterhouse, and opened on 30 March 2015, a little less than a year after his death (C6415).

He died in Bernay, France, on 10 May 2014, aged 82 (C6417). The date is a genuine split and this study picks a side openly rather than smoothing it: the foundation biography on his own official site says "Francisco Sobrino died on 10th May in Bernay, France", the French Wikipedia gives 10 May 2014 in Bernay citing the INSEE civil death records, and Galerie Mitterrand and the city of Guadalajara agree on 10 May (C6417). Against that, the English and Spanish Wikipedias, the Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Glasstire, the Museum of Fine Arts Houston and the Lelia Mordoch gallery all give 11 May 2014 (C6418). The 11 May camp is larger by headcount but appears to share one origin -- the El Mundo article dated 12 May 2014, which neither Wikipedia actually quotes for the day -- while the 10 May camp rests on the foundation (closest to the family and atelier) and a civil record (C6417, C6418). This study uses 10 May 2014 and records 11 May as the live alternative (C6553). Two further errors are not repeated: two sources say he died in Paris, and the MFAH page says "Death place Brittany, France" -- Bernay is in Normandy, not Brittany (C6420).

## M1 -- The relief that stacks flat forms into depth

The founding mechanism is the superposed relief: flat geometrical Plexiglas forms, slotted together so they interlock, stacked and overlapped so that tinted squares produce tonal variations where they cross (C6407). Sans titre (W03, 1961, Plexiglas box, 122 x 70 x 11 cm) is the mechanism in a flat box: eight blue discs in two columns of four on a deep blue ground, each disc made of two or three overlapping circles of different blues and white, the overlaps changing tone where the discs cross, the layering reading differently from different angles, so the flat box holds a shallow virtual depth (C6456). Transformation instable, juxtaposition, superposition (628) (W05, 1962, smoked transparent Plexiglas, 84 x 42 x 42 cm) is the same mechanism as a lattice: small smoked Plexiglas diamond-shaped plates slotted together, light passing through overlapping tinted plates darkening where they cross, so new diamond shapes and tones appear and disappear as the viewer moves around the piece (C6455). Sans titre (W06, 1962, transparent grey Plexiglas) adds the shadow: a vertical tower of pierced transparent cubes, each face cut with a circular hole, casting a large patterned shadow on the wall behind it -- the shadow doubles the work as a flat pattern of circles and crosses that shifts as the light or the viewer moves (C6456). Unstable Displacements F.G. (W18, 1968, coloured Plexiglas in a metal box, 395 x 395 x 240 mm, edition of 100 published by Denise Rene) carries the mechanism into a small portable multiple: coloured layers overlapping, colours mixing and shifting with the viewing angle (C6460).

**Takeaway:** stack flat sheets and the depth is in the overlap, not in the carving. Tinted squares crossing make new tones where they meet, and the shadow on the wall is a second work the light paints for free.

## M2 -- Smoked Plexiglas: the work that changes as you walk

Espaces indefinis / Transformations instables (W02, from 1961) is the series title for the smoked transparent Plexiglas structures: superposed and juxtaposed transparent forms give rise to new forms, and as spectators move the work plays on effects of light and space, each form occupying an undetermined position in space, so matter escapes from the reality of its presence (C6451). The work is static; the movement is optical and comes from the spectator walking around it. The Flemish Community Collection's Transformations Instables of 1962 (S40) states the principle in conservation language: a static work that activates through the observer's movement and changes form according to distance, angle and the psychophysiological condition of the person looking, and in the absence of that relation it remains in a potential state, present as an object but not fully activated as an artwork (C6466). The large statement is Transformation instable, juxtaposition, superposition (W07, 1963/2011, tinted transparent Plexiglas, 340 x 170 x 170 cm): dozens of tinted Plexiglas squares with slits that let them interlock like large building toys -- assembly took seven people -- the tinted squares overlapping to create tonal variations, the real form hard to fix in the mind, the work shape-shifting as the viewer moves around it, becoming more or less transparent and integrating whatever and whomever is seen through it (C6454). Glasstire's eyewitness description: the sculptures are "put together like kid's building toys writ large -- the squares of Plexiglas have slits that allow them to interconnect" (C6407).

**Takeaway:** smoke the sheet and the viewer becomes the motor. Nothing in the object moves; the form changes because you do, and without you there it waits in a potential state, not quite a work yet.

## M3 -- The permutational structure: one module, endless orders

In 1962 Sobrino began Structures permutationnelles in aluminium, using modular square forms fitted together by juxtaposition and superposition, so the forms lost the sense of their position in space (C6408). Structure permutationnelle (W09, 1963, anodized aluminium, 184.5 x 28.5 x 28.5 cm) is the mechanism as a tower: identical geometric modules -- interlocking diamond and triangular aluminium units -- stacked into a tall narrow column, the same module repeated and rotated building a kaleidoscopic column, the angled metal faces catching light differently from each side, so the tower's pattern and brightness change as the viewer walks around it (C6453). Structure Permutationnelle (W10, 1963-2014, mirror-polished stainless steel, 55 1/4 x 23 5/8 x 23 5/8 in.) is the same lattice in mirror steel, and Structure Permutationnelle no. 22 (W17, 1968, mirror-polished stainless steel, 110 x 110 x 110 cm, unique) is the compact cubic version, a star-like burst of triangular mirror planes on a plinth (C6455). The late Structure permutationnelle of 1998/2015 (S27) shows the same title still in use fifty years on (C6457). The auction record shows the market reads the mechanism the same way: Permutationelle M sold for USD 47,500 in March 2020, the highest observed Sobrino auction result in the record consulted (C6463).

**Takeaway:** one module, repeated and rotated, is the whole work. The tower is not designed -- it is permuted, and the order you see is the order you walked into.

## M4 -- Mirror steel: the room enters the work

In 1963 Sobrino began working the permutational series in mirror-polished steel: the mirror effect caused interference between the work and its surroundings, incorporating and reflecting forms, movement and light, and creating virtual images constantly reconstructed as spectators moved (C6408). Structure Permutationnelle (W10) is the indoor statement; Structure permutationnelle, Sarcelles (W13, 1965, stainless steel, 620 x 225 x 225 cm) is the same lattice built at architectural scale outdoors -- Sobrino's first architectural sculpture -- its mirror faces reflecting the street, sky, buildings and passers-by, so the surroundings enter the work and its virtual images rebuild continuously as people move past it (C6451). The many different sides of the Structures permutationnelles multiply the reflections of light and transform the sculpture into a huge work of a centripetal nature, forcing the spectator to walk around it (C6457). The 6-metre mirror-steel tower appears under three captions with identical dimensions -- Untitled 1971/2013 (S09), Transformation Instable 1963-1971 (S10), and a Structure permutationnelle with no year in caption (S33) -- photographed outdoors among pine trees, its mirror faces reflecting foliage, sky and the viewer; whether one tower or three editions of one design is not resolved (C6453, C6455, C6456).

**Takeaway:** polish the module and the work stops being an object. It becomes a place where the room is folded in and rebuilt -- the street, the trees, you -- and it never looks the same twice because the room never is.

## M5 -- The push that keeps moving: springs transmit the touch

Pulsations (W11, 1964) is the first named spring-transmission work: a playful, modular work in motion in which the spectator moves one form and the impulse is transmitted via springs to other forms, producing a series of uncontrolled movements (C6451). The viewer's push is the input, the springs are the transmission, and the visible result is a cascade of secondary movements the viewer does not directly control. Sans titre (W15, 1965, mobile, green Plexiglas box with two yellow discs on springs, 33 x 59 x 7 cm) is the mechanism in a box: the springs let the discs bob and sway out of their rest position when the box is touched or disturbed, so the two discs move against the fixed green ground and against each other (C6456). Spheres pulsations (W20, 1970, black cork balls on springs, 91 x 38 x 38 cm) is the same principle at its clearest: four black spheres hanging at different heights from curved rods over a white plinth; a push sets the balls oscillating on their springs, each at its own rhythm, so the configuration keeps changing after the touch (C6456). The Espace de l'Art Concret text groups W19 and W20 together as works where motion is created by the viewer activating metal rods or springs (C6457).

**Takeaway:** push once and the work answers for a minute. The springs do the composing -- your touch starts it, their different rhythms finish it, and no two pushes ever give the same dance.

## M6 -- Counter-rotation: two turns against each other

In 1965 Sobrino incorporated electric light and mechanical movement in Oppositions indefinies and Rotations opposees, with Deplacements bleus, verts (Blue, Green Displacements) the same year (W14): two rotations run in opposite directions, so the visible pattern is the interference of two counter-rotating movements plus coloured light displacement in blue and green (C6451). The mechanism is opposition: counter-rotation makes the image unstable rather than merely spinning. This is the first named work in the catalogue to add programmed mechanical movement and electric light to the optical system, and it is also the thinnest-documented: no medium beyond the light and the movement, no dimensions, no image retrieved -- the official biography illustrates it as Fig.9, which was not downloaded or viewed (C6451). It sits between Garcia Rossi's motorised light boxes, where one motor turns one disk, and Sobrino's own spring works, where the spectator supplies the energy: here the machine supplies two energies that fight.

**Takeaway:** spin two things against each other and the picture is the fight, not the spin. One rotation is decoration; two opposite rotations are an argument the eye cannot settle.

## M7 -- The wind decides: random movement

In 1970 Sobrino introduced random movement in works of spheres held together by a metallic structure on a fulcrum: Libre dans le Vent R (W19, 1969, two mobile elements on a red metal structure, 285 x 240 x 140 cm) is red spheres on thin red rods, balanced on a red frame and left to the air -- the wind and the viewer's touch set the spheres swinging, and the movement is random rather than programmed (C6451, C6456). The viewed image shows a red frame with two branching assemblies of red spheres on rods (C6456). The same random movement scales up: the Mobile at Santa Cruz de Tenerife (S13, 1973) is the Libres dans le vent idea at 12 metres, a large outdoor mobile whose parts move in the wind (C6451). And it scales out to the site itself: Relief articule (S22, 1990, Orinoco River, 20 metres long) is a floating relief of articulated black and white forms joined by a cable -- the river caused a wave-like movement, the work moved briefly along the river, and once the cable uniting the forms had been cut it came apart and drifted down the river until it disappeared completely under the water, which the biography notes justified the name of the event it was made for (C6451). The mechanism is current and buoyancy: the work is designed to be unmade by its site. Against the spring works (M5) the contrast is exact: there the spectator's push starts a cascade the springs finish; here nobody pushes at all -- the weather and the water decide, and the work may not survive the decision.

**Takeaway:** leave it to the wind and the work is never yours again. The spheres swing when the air moves, the river takes the relief when it wants it -- random movement means the artist has genuinely given the ending away.

## M8 -- The flat ancestor: progression on a plane

Before the Plexiglas there was the flat progression, and it never left. Untitled (W01, 1959-1970, acrylic on canvas, 120 x 120 cm) is a black, white and grey grid whose squares shrink and darken toward a central vertical and horizontal cross, so the flat painting reads as a tunnel or cross-shaped hollow: systematic progression, repeated squares in a regular sequence whose size and tone steps make the eye travel inward and feel depth and movement on a flat surface (C6458). The 1959-1970 paintings and gouaches were made first in black and white and then in green, red, blue or yellow, with square or circular shapes producing a kinetic rhythm and an illusion of coming and going, an instability of the gaze (C6458). Deplacement instable KFRP (W04, 1961/67, Plexiglas relief, 8 colours) is the progression as a long low band: vertical stripes in eight colours, grouped in blocks whose stripe widths and colour order change from block to block, the eye comparing neighbouring blocks and reading shifting alignment, rhythm and after-image vibration as it travels along the band (C6453). The early flat works in the supplementary catalogue show the same grammar at small scale: Sans titre (S01, 1959, gouache on cardboard) is five small colour-block squares in a row, each divided into red, blue, green and orange rectangles, a systematic colour progression across five panels (C6453); Sans titre (S02, 1959, collage of coloured cardboard, 89 x 89 cm) is concentric coloured squares stepping from yellow-green at the outside to a small orange centre, a pulsing tunnel-like virtual depth on a flat sheet (C6456); Sans titre (S05, 1969, red and blue silkscreen, 80 x 80 cm) is a 4 x 4 grid of panels, each striped red and blue in alternating vertical and horizontal directions, the alternating stripe directions making the flat print vibrate and appear to shift as the eye moves across it (C6456).

**Takeaway:** the whole career is already in the flat grid. Shrink the squares toward the centre and the canvas becomes a tunnel; alternate the stripe directions and the print vibrates -- depth and movement with no machine, no light, no sheet of Plexiglas at all.

## The aesthetic logic: the sheet learns to stand up, then to disappear

Sobrino's logic runs in one direction for fifty years, and it is the opposite of Garcia Rossi's. Garcia Rossi starts with the flat plane, builds a box, puts a motor and bulbs inside it, and then -- when the motors stop -- paints the light back onto the canvas. Sobrino starts with the same flat plane, the black-and-white grid and the systematic progression (M8), and then the sheet itself learns to stand up: first into reliefs of superposed flat forms (M1), then into smoked transparent structures that change as the spectator walks (M2), then into permutational towers of one repeated module (M3), then into mirror steel, where the sheet stops being a surface and becomes a place that folds the room inside it (M4). At every step something is taken away from the object and given to the spectator: the relief gives the depth to the overlap, the smoked Plexiglas gives the movement to the walk, the permutational tower gives the order to the permutation, the mirror gives the image to the room. Then the energy is handed over too: a push travels through springs (M5), two motors argue against each other (M6), and finally the wind and the river decide (M7) -- the work is no longer made by the artist at all, only started by him. The late work completes the arc by making the material disappear: white-on-white torsions read only through their own shadows (S23), colourless Plexiglas splits daylight into colour by refraction alone (S24), a fountain of light gathers daylight and throws it back as refracted colour (S25), light beams outline pyramids in the night sky visible for 25 km (S21), and the Kinetic Bank makes the building itself move with projected light (S16). The 1998 Labyrinth -- 64 cubes in eight colours, colours never repeated one next to another, the spectator walking inside a three-dimensional colour progression -- is the flat grid of 1959 built at walk-through scale (C6451). Against Garcia Rossi the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Garcia Rossi's light is generated inside a closed box and watched in half-light, the penombre the label prescribes; Sobrino's light is ambient, daylight through stacked sheets, and the work is watched in the open, walked around, pushed, and finally left to the weather. Garcia Rossi dematerialised the picture into light you can only see while the motor runs; Sobrino dematerialised the picture into transparency, reflection and air, until there is almost no object left -- only the overlap, the reflection and the walk. Against Morellet the contrast is one of material inside the same group programme: Morellet's chance is numerical, a phone book read aloud, pi deciding the angles, the picture made by a system the artist refuses to own; Sobrino's chance is physical, a spring transmitting a push, a sphere swinging in the wind, a river taking a relief apart -- the system is built, not written, and the uncontrolled movement is real, not computed. Against Le Parc and Soto the contrast is one of scale inside the same group: Le Parc's light rooms and Soto's wires make spaces you walk through, while Sobrino's towers make objects you walk around -- a tall mirror lattice in a gallery corner, a 6-metre steel tower among pine trees, a red frame of spheres swinging in the air -- each one forcing the spectator to circle it, because the work is only complete from every side at once (C6457).
