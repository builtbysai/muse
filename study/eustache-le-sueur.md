# Eustache Le Sueur: Study Notes

**Site:** Eustache Le Sueur (19 November 1616, Paris -- 30 April 1655, Paris), French painter and draughtsman; son of a Paris wood-turner, pupil of Simon Vouet, the classicist who learned Poussin and Raphael from prints and drawings without ever leaving Paris, founder-member of the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture and one of its twelve original anciens, painter of the twenty-two-panel Life of Saint Bruno, the Hotel Lambert decorations and the goldsmiths' May of 1649 -- the "French Raphael" whose colour Roger de Piles scored 4 out of 20, and who died at thirty-eight with the Lambert decoration and the Saint-Gervais cartoons unfinished.

## The life

Eustache Le Sueur was born in Paris on 19 November 1616 -- the year is split in the literature, 1616 vs 1617, and the study uses 1616 on the strength of Alain Merot's catalogue raisonne (titled Eustache Le Sueur, 1616-1655), the Met and Sotheby's, against the single 1911 Britannica lineage behind EN Wikipedia's 1617 (see C1307) -- and died in Paris on 30 April 1655, aged thirty-eight on that dating. The day of his birth is not disputed; the year is, and no baptismal or parish record was consulted in this run. He was the son of Cathelin Le Sueur, a turner and sculptor in wood (tourneur sur bois). He entered Simon Vouet's studio in 1632 (Sotheby's; aged fifteen or sixteen), stayed about ten years, and "rapidly distinguished himself" (1911 Britannica). His fellow pupils there were Charles Le Brun and Pierre Mignard; Vouet, back from Rome since 1627, was Premier peintre du Roi under Louis XIII, and his studio was the dominant Parisian training ground of the 1630s and early 1640s. Early work was executed after Vouet's drawings and under his direction; Le Sueur's own style emerged as a progressive stripping-down -- "rigueur et depouillement toujours plus marques" (FR Wikipedia). The Cyclopedia of Painters and Paintings (1887) alone says he "quarrelled [with Vouet] in 1641, and began an independent career" (see C1309/C1345); no modern source read corroborates a quarrel or the year, and the study treats it as reported, not verified. He married in 1642, to Genevieve Gousse, daughter of a wax merchant -- stated only by Riva de Boheme (see C1311); FR Wikipedia's infobox corroborates the Gousse brother-in-law link in general but not the year or her name. His own studio, once independent, was a family workshop: his three brothers and his brother-in-law Gousse worked with him (1911 Britannica calls them his pupils, "who aided him much in his work"), alongside Claude Lefebvre and the landscape painter Pierre Patel -- though Patel's status as a formal pupil is doubtful (see leg 3, section 1.5).

He never left Paris. That is the single most load-bearing fact of his formation, stated identically by Sotheby's, the Met, NiceArtGallery, FR Wikipedia and Riva: "Somewhat unusually for a French artist of his stature, Le Sueur never left Paris for a tour of Italy" (Sotheby's). He learned instead by studying Italian pictures in Paris -- in the royal palaces (Fontainebleau named by FR Wikipedia and Sotheby's) and in rich private Parisian collections -- and by studying prints and drawings after Italian masters, particularly Raphael. Poussin's brief, fraught Paris stay, December 1640 to autumn 1642, is the dated point of contact: Le Sueur, then twenty-three to twenty-five and newly independent, had roughly twenty months in which to see Poussin's Paris work at first hand -- the Last Supper, the Triumph of Truth for Richelieu, the Louvre Grande Galerie designs -- and to absorb, at second hand, the paintings Poussin sent back to French patrons from Rome. Whether they met is not stated in any source read. The tradition that Poussin and Le Sueur were friends, and that Poussin was jealous of the younger man's talent, is reported by NiceArtGallery as tradition only (see C1350).

In 1648 he left the guild of master-painters (the Maitrise / Academie de Saint-Luc, into which he had been admitted as master c. 1644/45) and was one of the founders of the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture, elected one of the original twelve elders in charge of its administration (EN Wikipedia, citing Montaiglon's Memoires, vol. I, p. 36). The Proces-verbaux preserve the founding declaration of 5 September 1648, signed in this order: S. Guillain -- De la Hire -- Le Brun -- Charles Errard -- Jacques Sarazin -- Gerard Van Opstal -- M. Corneille -- Perrier -- E. Le Sueur -- Duguernier -- Sebastien Bourdon -- L. Testelin. His name sits ninth, between the engraver Francois Perrier and the painter Henri Duguernier, with Bourdon two places behind him. He took his turn as ancien in October 1648 and sat in the February 1649 session that elected two new anciens. Vouet was absent by design: Wikipedia's Vouet article says Le Brun "jealously excluded Vouet from the Academie Royale in 1648" and that Vouet "played no role in the foundation"; the Matthiesen gallery says the opposite, that Vouet "helped found" it (see C1348) -- the study leg must resolve this from Montaiglon before asserting either version, and this study asserts neither. Vouet died in June 1649, a year after the founding, outside the institution his pupils had built.

Patrons came in the order the institutions did. The Carthusians of the Chartreuse de Paris commissioned the Life of Saint Bruno in 1645, for the petit cloitre, to replace frescoes damaged by humidity -- twenty-two paintings, 1645-48, the work of his maturity and the centre of the oeuvre. The Lambert family commissioned the Hotel Lambert decoration: Jean-Baptiste Lambert built the hotel on the Ile Saint-Louis in 1640-44 and died in December 1644, eight months after moving in; his younger brother Nicolas Lambert de Thorigny inherited it and commissioned most of the interior decoration from the most famous French painters of the time, including Le Brun and Le Sueur. Le Sueur's share was the Cabinet de l'Amour (1646-47) and the Cabinet des Muses (the nine Muses, painted 1652-55, with Patel painting the landscapes of the five large canvases). The goldsmiths' corporation commissioned the May of 1649 -- Saint Paul Preaching at Ephesus, 394 x 328 cm, hung in Notre-Dame on 1 May, the same annual mechanism that made Bourdon's reputation in 1643 and that Loir used in 1650. He was appointed Peintre Ordinaire du Roi in 1649, the same year (Sotheby's only; see C1321). After the Fronde ended in 1653 and royal commissions resumed, he worked on the renovation of the Louvre palace -- the appartement des bains of Anne of Austria and the chamber of the young Louis XIV -- political allegories now mostly lost, entered in Bailly's inventory (1710), their subjects not listed in any source read (see C1365; and see C1349 -- no source read names Anne of Austria as his direct commissioner, so the study says "the king's and queen's apartments" and notes the regency context, not a direct Anne commission). Churches: Saint-Gervais (the six-cartoon series, 1652), Saint-Germain-l'Auxerrois (Martyrdom of St Lawrence), the destroyed abbey of Marmoutier-les-Tours (four paintings, 1654, now split between Tours and the Louvre), the Couvent des Chartreux (Noli me tangere, 1651, for Dom Anselme, prior of the order).

He died in Paris on 30 April 1655, aged thirty-eight. No source read gives a cause of death, a place of burial, or a deathbed circumstance beyond the fables Britannica dismisses (see C1326) -- the study invents none. What was left unfinished: the Hotel Lambert decoration, "frequently interrupted by other commissions" (Britannica), and the Saint-Gervais series, of which one cartoon was finished by Le Sueur, the second completed after his death by his brother-in-law Thomas Gousse, and the remaining four assigned first to Sebastien Bourdon and then to Philippe de Champaigne (Met; see C1324). The St Bruno cycle was not among the unfinished works -- it was delivered whole, and offered to Louis XVI by the convent in 1776.

## The evolution: three phases, one subtraction

**1. Early, c. 1636-1644 -- Vouet's studio and the break.** The work is made after Vouet's drawings and under his direction; figures are borrowed literally. The Hermitage Presentation of the Virgin (W06, c. 1640-45) is the single best piece of evolution evidence in this study: it was made "under the influence of Simon Vouet's Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple dated 1641", its figures "remind us of the personages in Vouet's composition" -- a poor old lady in the foreground with an identical profile, a young mother with child "precisely taken from Vouet" -- yet "Le Sueur gradually moved away from the painting manner of his teacher... this was reflected in the Hermitage painting, which is executed in a manner that is more calm and strict than Vouet's work." Same figures, different temperature. The Songe de Polyphile series (W02-W03, 1636-44, eight paintings after Colonna's Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, painted as cartoons for Gobelins tapestries never executed) shows the fault line inside a single commission: the first canvases are characterised by "an accumulation of figures and the absence of any real concern for composition"; the later ones are "less opulent and more balanced" (FR Wikipedia). Subjects are mythological and violent -- the Rape of Tamar (W04, c. 1640, 189.2 x 161.3 cm VERIFIED, Met), Bacchus and Ariadne (W07), Camma Offers the Poisoned Wedding Cup (W08, c. 1644, Plutarch, Moralia) -- and the Rape of Tamar is the early peak: "Classical formal features -- solid forms, careful balance, and harmonious colors -- temper the violence of this scene", a "freeze-frame effect... through staid yet dramatic gestures that Le Sueur derived from classical sculpture" (Met). The Reunion d'amis (W05, c. 1640-44) is the hinge picture, a sociable group portrait of Anne de Chambre's circle in which master and pupil were long confused -- bought for Louis XVI as a Vouet and long displayed as one.

**2. Middle, 1645-1649 -- the severity arrives, and the scale jumps.** The St Bruno cycle (1645-48) is the pivot: twenty-two panels in a uniform 193 x 130 cm format (REPORTED; the uniform format rests on two panels individually dimensioned, see C1343), three years, assistants, one subject -- monastic life rendered as ruled perspective, cool blues, greys, whites and ochres, emotion carried by posture and gesture rather than faces. In the same years the opposite pole is painted: the Cabinet de l'Amour (1646-47), six pictures of love's power, warmer, slender, fluid, in frieze and ceiling formats -- the warm pole against the Bruno cycle's cold pole, two stops on the same instrument. The May of 1649 (W16, 394 x 328 cm) is the public test of the new manner at monumental scale, and the contemporary criticism embedded in the theartofpainting.be analysis says he passed it with reservations: the pyramid is impeccable, the rigour slightly overdone -- "too much of the stiffness and strictness, too much of verticals", a top-heavy band of nondescript brown heads at Paul's shoulder height, the man in blue at left drawn too large, diminishing Paul's tallness. Alexander and His Doctor (W17, c. 1648-49, National Gallery NG 6576, rediscovered and acquired 2000) is the same year's private counterweight: 96 cm square (or round -- see C1334), psychological, a test of faces.

**3. Late, 1650-1655 -- reduction and the unfinished.** The last five years strip the apparatus away: the Annunciation (W18, c. 1650 / c. 1652, Louvre) is two figures and a book; the Muses (W20, 1652-55, 130 x 130 cm REPORTED) are three figures in a square with no architecture at all; the virtues -- Meekness (1650, Chicago), Justice (1650, Strasbourg), Magnificence (c. 1654, Dayton) -- are single-figure emblems; the St Gervais cartoons are white drapery against saturated soldiers, prepared by nine obsessive figure studies. The late commissions are increasingly left incomplete by death: the Cabinet des Muses unfinished, the St Gervais tapestry series two cartoons of six, the Louvre apartment allegories lost, the Marmoutier pictures split across museums. The drawings multiply as the paintings thin out -- the Beaux-Arts sheets (mise au carreau, black chalk with white heightening) show a painter who now thinks in single draped figures first and compositions second.

## M1 -- The classicism learned from paper: Poussin and Raphael at second hand, never Rome

Le Sueur is the purest term of the Vouet-to-Poussin gradient, and the gradient is the point. Vouet imported the Italian Baroque by living in Rome fourteen years; Bourdon went to Rome in the 1630s and took a career to adopt Poussin; Loir went to Rome in 1647-49 and converted in one trip; Le Sueur stayed home and invented the French version of Poussin from paper. The mechanism is second-hand transmission made complete: prints and drawings after Raphael, Poussin's pictures seen in Parisian collections and at Fontainebleau, and twenty months of Poussin's presence in Paris (December 1640 to autumn 1642) -- the Last Supper, the Triumph of Truth for Richelieu, the Louvre Grande Galerie designs. NiceArtGallery: he "assimilated Poussin's emphasis on intellectual rigour, carefully constructed compositions, and morally serious themes, but translated them into a gentler, more accessible idiom." The St Paul at Ephesus (W16) is "clearly influenced by Raphael's tapestry cartoons" -- Raphael at two removes, through the engraving and through the cartoon, and still the pyramid is exact: Paul's head at the summit, two lines descending to the lower corners, the burning books inside the triangle. The posthumous title "the French Raphael" rests on exactly this transmission -- grace and harmony learned from engravings, not from the Stanze -- and the title is debated, possibly backhanded: 18th- and 19th-century critics found the comparison damning, derivative, lacking Raphael's power (see C1364). No source read traces who first used the phrase.

**Takeaway:** build the classicism from paper, not from Rome -- a composition that quotes Raphael's frieze and pyramid correctly but feels like a copy made at two removes, clarity without the Roman air, and let the second-hand route be the subject: the print on the table, the engraving pinned to the wall, the master arriving as ink on paper.

## M2 -- Subtraction as a career arc: from accumulation to the single figure

The direction of travel is consistent across nineteen years: from accumulation to subtraction, from Vouet's opulence to a classicism of few figures, clear drawing, and quiet gesture. The Polyphile panels begin crowded, figure-accumulated, with "absence of a true concern for composition", and end balanced. The Bruno cycle holds one monk in one room at a time. The late Muses are isolated single figures in empty space -- Calliope and Uranie, c. 1650, oil on wood, 116 x 74 cm REPORTED, for the panelling of the Chambre des Muses, each with one attribute (trumpet, flute, mask, viol) and heavy antique drapery, attitudes "natural" rather than hieratic. The virtues of 1650-54 are single-figure emblems. Even the palette subtracts: in Jesus chez Marthe et Marie, "four colours for the clothing" only (Riva) -- a deliberate chromatic sobriety. The mechanism is progressive removal: fewer figures, fewer colours, more air, until the same story is told with half the cast each time, and finally with one figure standing in a room that is the picture.

**Takeaway:** tell the same story with half the cast each time -- start crowded and Vouet-warm, end with one draped figure in an empty square, and let the subtraction be visible as a sequence, not a single style: the early panel, the middle panel, the late panel, each with fewer figures than the last.

## M3 -- The drawing-first pipeline: nine studies, drapery before face

Le Sueur was a prolific draughtsman -- the Louvre's Cabinet des Dessins holds many of his chalk drawings, and he "left an incredible quantity, chiefly executed in black and white chalk" (Britannica). The working method, from the Met and the Ecole des Beaux-Arts holdings: black chalk (pierre noire) heightened with white chalk on toned (gray-beige / gray-brown) paper, often with squaring (mise au carreau) for transfer, contours pricked and repassed with a stylus for engraving transfer, successive repentirs and multiple figure and detail studies before painting. The Saint-Gervais commission makes the priority visible in numbers: nine figure studies for a single painting, one per saint, and no compositional study has come to light. The Met's Study for Saint Gervasius (42.9 x 26.2 cm VERIFIED, black chalk heightened with white chalk on gray-beige paper) makes it visible in the object: "Le Sueur paid very little attention to the features of Gervasius's face... the focus is on the drapery -- majestic and light, and brilliantly rendered with delicate white chalk highlights and careful shading in black chalk to model the folds." Face last, drapery first: the drawing is a machine for getting cloth and posture right. The same practice runs back to the Morgan's Study of Man's Figure Stooping (c. 1640, black chalk with white chalk on brown paper), studies after live models to fix a pose. And the pictures lent themselves to the engraver's art for the same reason: most of his works were engraved, chiefly by Picart, B. Audran, Leclerc, Drevet, Chauveau, Poilly, Desplaces -- the line-based, drawing-led picture survives translation into print, and the St Bruno cycle circulated as a twenty-two-plate engraved series (Leclerc and others, c. 1676), the same print mechanism by which Le Sueur had learned Raphael.

**Takeaway:** draw the cloth before the person -- nine chalk studies for one painting, the face left blank while the drapery is fully modelled, squaring and stylus transfer, and let the finished picture carry the drawing in it: the figure that exists first as drapery, second as a face.

## M4 -- The cloister as a twenty-two-panel slow narrative

Twenty-two paintings on the life of Saint Bruno, commissioned 1645 for the petit cloitre of the Chartreuse de Paris, executed 1645-48 by Le Sueur with a small group of assistants, to replace frescoes damaged by humidity. The panels are c. 193 x 130 cm (REPORTED), arched top (cintre) for at least some, one life told in quiet episodes: the dream (three angels advise Bruno to consult St Hugh of Grenoble), the sermon of Raymond Diocres, the message from the Pope, building the monastery, examining a drawing of the Baths of Diocletian, the Pope confirming the order, giving the habit, arriving in Rome, appearing to Comte Roger, the death on 6 October 1101. Offered to Louis XVI by the convent in 1776, now in the Louvre. The mechanism is seriality: the same frame repeated until the story becomes architecture, a narrative that advances by walking -- each panel a still room, the story carried by the viewer's movement between identical frames. The style is the turn it marks: severity, noble attitudes, "compositions tres calculees", lightened colours, worked perspective (FR Wikipedia); "clarity, restraint, and solemnity... cool palette, dominated by blues, greys, whites, and ochres" (NiceArtGallery); emotion through gesture and posture, not exaggerated faces; architecture and landscape used to build ordered, believable space with linear perspective. The original support is disputed -- WahooArt says the panels were originally painted on wood and transposed to canvas by Jean-Louis Hacquin between 1778 and 1783, then restored by Joseph Ferdinand Francois Godefroid 1783-86; Britannica says "removal from the wall" (see C1316) -- and the study does not settle it.

**Takeaway:** tell one life in twenty-two equal rooms -- the same arched frame repeated down a cloister wall, one quiet episode per panel, the story advancing by the viewer's walking, and let the seriality be the architecture: the frame is the building, the episodes are the furniture.

## M5 -- Emotion by gesture, not by face

In the Bruno cycle and the late religious pictures, feeling is carried by posture, the turn of a head, a hand withheld -- not by faces. The Noli me tangere (1651, Grenoble, 145 x 129 cm) is built on "a perfect diagonal" with equal weight given to figures and landscape, a "douce lumiere" and "halo vaporeux" (Grenoble commentary via Riva). The Annunciation (W18) deliberately withholds the Virgin's surprise: she keeps her eyes on her prayer book, receiving the announcement as an inward revelation rather than an apparition -- restraint as a compositional decision, not a failure of drama. In the St Gervais cartoon, Gervasius is "led forward by his captors, hands and arms bound and eyes cast downward to further emphasize his tranquil lack of resistance" (Met). The St Bruno Appearing to Comte Roger panel stages a vision as a conversation: Bruno standing at left and pointing toward a city in the background while Roger sits up in bed, a soldier in the foreground shaking a companion awake -- the dream given a room and a pointing arm. The counter-example proves the rule: in Reunion d'amis (c. 1640), the young Le Sueur still uses a tight Baroque crop, emphatic gesture and mimicry, gazes running off-canvas -- the Vouet manner he was leaving. By the 1650s the gesture has gone quiet, and the face has gone blank on purpose.

**Takeaway:** stage the drama as an inward event -- the announcement received with eyes down on a prayer book, the martyr led forward with eyes cast down, the vision staged as a conversation with a pointing arm -- and let the face refuse the drama the gesture performs.

## M6 -- Observed monks vs conventional types

Britannica's one unqualified praise, and the reason the Bruno cycle stands apart from the rest of the oeuvre: "whenever we get direct reference to nature -- as in the monks of the St Bruno series -- we recognize his admirable power to read and render physiognomy of varied and serious type." The Bruno monks are observed faces, not types -- every head a different age, weight and attention, inside an otherwise idealised picture. The counter-judgement, also Britannica's, is the reason the reputation fell: "his graceful facility in composition was always restrained by a very fine taste, but his works often fail to please completely, because, producing so much, he had too frequent recourse to conventional types." Facility plus conventional types is the 19th-century charge, and the reason Gautier could write "Tes moines, Lesueur, pres de ceux-la sont fades" against Zurbaran (FR Wikipedia, quoting Gautier's A Zurbaran). The mechanism is the split inside one painter: the monk painted from a man sitting in front of him, and the saint painted from a type in a book -- and the viewer can tell which is which, because the observed face has a weight the type does not.

**Takeaway:** put one observed face inside the idealised picture -- a row of monks where every head is a different age, weight and attention, the physiognomy read from nature against the conventional types everywhere else -- and let the contrast be the point: the observed face is the only one that holds the eye.

## M7 -- Cold colour, scored 4 out of 20

The agreed weakness, with an agreed exception, stated by Britannica and repeated everywhere: "he rarely saw colour except with the cold and clayey quality proper to the school of Vouet; yet his St Paul at Ephesus and one or two other works show that he was not naturally deficient in this sense." The mature palette is cool and lightened: blues, greys, whites, ochres in the Bruno cycle; four colours for the clothing in Jesus chez Marthe et Marie; light that models and calms rather than staging a coup de theatre -- a "douce lumiere" in the Noli me tangere, strong light from the right in the National Gallery Christ on the Cross (c. 1643), picking out the pale body against threatening clouds. De Piles made the same point numerically, and the numbers are the quantitative form of the judgement: in the Balance des peintres (1708, Cours de peinture par principes), Le Sueur scores composition 15, drawing 15, colour 4, expression 15, total 49 -- the 4 for colour is the lowest colour score among the major Frenchmen, verified via three independent secondary tabulations (Wikipedia "Roger de Piles"; the Brandeis/RePEc paper; the Italian Wikipedia), not against the 1708 Cours itself (see C1322). The 15s for composition, drawing and expression put him level with Le Brun on drawing (16) and ahead of him on expression. The mechanism is colour as a deliberate instrument: a picture whose colour refuses to seduce, so that line and arrangement have to do all the work -- and the single exception, the St Paul at Ephesus, where colour briefly catches fire: Paul's red cloak "the largest mass of pure hue in the painting", drawing the eye, colour placed symmetrically (blue left and right, white both sides, brown masses both sides) inside the pyramid.

**Takeaway:** refuse the seduction of colour -- blues, greys, whites and ochres, four colours for the clothing, the line and the arrangement doing all the work -- and then let one picture catch fire: a single red cloak as the largest mass of pure hue in the frame, the exception that proves the cold was a choice.

## M8 -- The unfinished series handed on: one story, three hands

The St Bruno cycle was finished; the two works that were not finished are the ones that show the mechanism. The Hotel Lambert decoration was left uncompleted, "for their execution was frequently interrupted by other commissions" (Britannica) -- the Cabinet de l'Amour done in 1646-47, the Cabinet des Muses painted 1652-55 and left unfinished at his death in April 1655, the ceiling Phaeton Asking Apollo to Drive His Chariot among the last things he worked on. The Saint-Gervais series is the cleaner case: a projected series of six cartoons on St Gervais and St Protais, commissioned 1652 by the churchwardens; Le Sueur prepared the first painting with nine figure studies and finished the first cartoon; a second cartoon, the Flagellation of Saint Gervasius (Lyon), was finished after his death by his brother-in-law Thomas Gousse; the remaining four were assigned first to Sebastien Bourdon and then to Philippe de Champaigne (Met; see C1324). One story told in three different hands, the seams visible between cartoons: Le Sueur's white drapery against saturated soldiers, Gousse finishing his brother-in-law's drawing, Bourdon and then Champaigne taking the rest. The May of 1649 is the counterpoint to the unfinished work -- the annual public contest, fixed to 1 May, a 394 x 328 cm canvas built to be judged once, in public, on a fixed day, and the one work where the rigour was called overdone (see the evolution section). The finished cycle, the interrupted decoration, the handed-on cartoons and the dated contest are four different fates for a commission, and Le Sueur's career contains all four.

**Takeaway:** hand the unfinished work on and keep the seams visible -- one cartoon finished, the second completed after death by the brother-in-law, the rest reassigned to Bourdon then Champaigne, a single story told in three different hands -- and let the handover be the subject: the drawing that outlives the painter, the commission that survives him.

## The aesthetic logic

Le Sueur's aesthetic is Atticism as a Parisian invention, not an import. The construction is Raphael's and Poussin's -- balanced, often pyramidal groupings, figures arranged in a frieze or a shallow, architecture-framed stage, linear perspective used to build ordered, believable rooms and cloisters -- and the colour is Vouet's, cooled: the "cold and clayey" palette disciplined into blues, greys, whites and ochres, four colours for the clothing, light that models and calms. He works by subtraction: the crowded Polyphile panels, the twenty-two equal Bruno panels, the three Muses in a square, the single-figure virtues -- fewer figures, fewer colours, more air, until the picture is one draped figure in an empty room. He works by drawing: black chalk heightened with white on toned paper, squaring for transfer, nine studies for one painting, the face left blank while the drapery is finished. The through-line is the second-hand route made complete: a painter who never left Paris, who learned his masters from prints, and who turned that paper classicism into the first fully Parisian Atticism -- clear outlines, balanced architecture, cool colour, quiet dignity. Merot's verdict, quoted by Riva from the Encyclopaedia Universalis, is the reception thread that matters: from Vouet's pictorial language Le Sueur developed "des recherches formelles de plus en plus radicales, dont s'est nourri tout un courant de l'art francais, de Subleyras et Peyron a La Fresnaye et Maurice Denis, en passant par Girodet, Ingres et Puvis de Chavannes" -- painters who "privilegient la surface, l'agencement harmonieux... des lignes, et de discrets accords colores." The lineage runs Subleyras to Ingres to Puvis de Chavannes to Denis: surface, harmonious arrangement of lines, discreet colour chords -- the subtraction, carried forward two centuries. The reputation curve runs with it: highly regarded in his lifetime and shortly after, at its apogee in the 18th century ("French Raphael"), dismissed in the 19th as pious, insipid (mievre) and academic (Gautier), rediscovered in the 20th century by historians of 17th-century French painting. The fables are the price of the curve: Britannica warns that his "simple history... has been decorated with as many fables as that of Claude" -- the jealous Le Brun, the Poussin correspondence, the broken-hearted retreat to the Chartreux and death in the prior's arms, all "pure fiction" -- and the modern popular sources still repeat them, including Riva's duel at the barriere de Lourcine (see C1312). The study's duty is to strip the fables back off and keep the plainer, better facts: a wood-turner's son who learned his masters from paper, founded an Academy, painted twenty-two quiet monks, and died at thirty-eight with two commissions unfinished.

## The network

**Teachers:** Simon Vouet (1590-1649), the only master -- entered his studio 1632, stayed about ten years, quarrelled (reported, 1641) and went independent at twenty-four; the decorative Baroque Le Sueur learned and then stripped down, its "cold and clayey" colour staying in his palette. **The models, at second hand:** Nicolas Poussin (1594-1665) -- not a teacher, not a correspondent (that is fable), but the decisive influence through his Paris pictures 1640-42 and the paintings he sent back to French patrons; Raphael (1483-1520) -- the model known through prints, the source of the "French Raphael" tag and of the compositional grammar (frieze, pyramid, clear contour); Annibale Carracci, Domenichino and Guido Reni, "whose works were known through originals and prints". **Fellow Vouet pupils:** Charles Le Brun (1619-1690) -- fellow Academy founder, fellow Hotel Lambert decorator, the real rival (de Piles, in the Le Brun life of the Abrege: "Le Sueur, dont nous avons parle, estoit le seul Concurrent qui luy put disputer" -- Le Sueur was the only competitor who could dispute Le Brun; whether because Le Brun was thought more able or more fashionable, or because he had more friends, Le Brun always carried off the great occasions); Pierre Mignard (1612-1695) -- fellow Vouet pupil, later Le Brun's chief rival. **The Academy circle, 1648:** the twelve anciens in declaration order -- Guillain, La Hyre, Le Brun, Errard, Sarazin, Van Opstal, Corneille, Perrier, Le Sueur, Duguernier, Bourdon, Testelin. Laurent de La Hyre (1606-1656), fellow Atticist, dead a year after Le Sueur; Sebastien Bourdon (#585), two places behind Le Sueur in the declaration, the Rome-traveller to Le Sueur's stay-at-home; Francois Perrier (1590-1650), engraver-painter, the print conduit through which Le Sueur knew Raphael; Charles Errard (1606-1689), later director of the French Academy in Rome. **Patrons:** Nicolas Lambert de Thorigny (Hotel Lambert, Cabinet de l'Amour 1646-47, Cabinet des Muses c. 1651-55); the Carthusians of the Chartreuse de Paris (the twenty-two-panel St Bruno cycle, 1645-48); the Paris goldsmiths' corporation (the May of 1649); the crown (the Louvre apartments, now lost, known only from Bailly's inventory of 1710); the churchwardens of Saint-Gervais (the six-cartoon series, 1652); the abbey of Marmoutier (four paintings, 1654). **Pupils and the line forward:** the family workshop (three brothers, brother-in-law Theodore/Thomas Gousse -- the given-name wobble is unresolved); Claude Lefebvre (bapt. 1632-1675), who studied with Le Sueur in 1654, then with Le Brun after his death, and whose own pupils were Francois de Troy and Jean Cotelle the younger -- so the line runs Le Sueur to Lefebvre to de Troy, and de Troy then studied under Loir as well; Pierre Patel the elder (c. 1605-1676), pupil in the 1911 shorthand, in fact collaborator and friend (Le Sueur was godfather to Patel's daughter Elisabeth in 1644), who painted the landscapes of the Cabinet des Muses canvases. **The engravers as transmitters:** Picart, B. Audran, Leclerc, Drevet, Chauveau, Poilly, Desplaces -- the print afterlife by which a painter who never left Paris and died at thirty-eight stayed visible. **No documented contact** in the sources read between Le Sueur and Loir (Le Sueur died when Loir was thirty-one and in Rome), or between Le Sueur and Bourdon beyond the Academy declaration. The link is institutional and genealogical, not personal: Le Sueur founded the Academy Loir entered fifteen years later.

## Future candidates

**Top pick for #588 is Simon Vouet (9 January 1590, Paris -- 30 June 1649, Paris).** The master of the whole generation, proposed now that his two most famous pupils have been studied in sequence (Bourdon #585, Loir #586, Le Sueur #587). Vouet spent 1613-27 in Italy, rose to president of the Accademia di San Luca in 1624, and was summoned home by Louis XIII in 1627 as Premier peintre du Roi -- the man who imported the Italian Baroque into France and adapted it to the grand decorative schemes of Louis XIII and Richelieu (Louvre, Luxembourg, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Richelieu's Palais-Royal and Malmaison). His Paris atelier was the largest and most significant studio of the second quarter of the century: its pupils include Le Brun, Mignard, Le Sueur, Valentin de Boulogne, Francois Perrier, Nicolas Chaperon, Michel Corneille the elder, Charles Poerson, Pierre Daret, Charles Alphonse du Fresnoy, Claude Mellan, Abraham Willaerts, Michel Dorigny and Francois Tortebat (the last two his sons-in-law), and Andre Le Notre learned drawing there. The study's spine is the reversal: the master who made the generation was left outside the institution his pupils founded in 1648 -- Wikipedia says Le Brun "jealously excluded" him and that he "played no role in the foundation"; the Matthiesen gallery says he "helped found" it (see C1348) -- and he died in June 1649, a year after the founding, at fifty-nine. He also quarrelled with Le Brun (Lempertz: a serious quarrel "resulting in Simon Vouet being forced to leave the Academie Royale") and, per the Cyclopedia, with Le Sueur in 1641 -- the master quarrelled with two of his three great pupils. Mechanisms no other candidate offers: the import of a whole style (Italian Baroque to French decorative Baroque) by one man's return; the atelier as a school, with the workshop system that produced Le Brun, Mignard and Le Sueur from the same benches; the founder excluded from his pupils' Academy; the reputation afterwards dwarfed by Poussin, "whose influence was more significant on succeeding generations" (Matthiesen). Doorway sketch: "Hop from the Le Sueur study: to the master they all quarrelled with and then left behind -- Simon Vouet, who brought the Italian Baroque to Paris, taught Le Brun, Mignard and Le Sueur in the same studio, and died outside the Academy his pupils founded." **Alternate 1: Francois de Troy (9 January 1645, Toulouse -- 1 May 1730, Paris), carried from #586.** The pupil who married into the family and outlived the system. De Troy came to Paris after 1662 and studied portrait painting under Claude Lefebvre and Nicolas-Pierre Loir, then in 1668/69 married Jeanne Cotelle, Loir's sister-in-law and sister of the decorative painter Jean II Cotelle, with whom he kept a profound friendship. He was agree by the Academy in 1671 and recu in 1674 with Mercury and Argus; after Lefebvre's death in 1675 he turned to portraiture and took Lefebvre's clients; in the 1690s he became principal painter to James II in exile at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, the only court painter the exiled king had, producing Jacobite portraits with his best student Alexis Simon Belle; professor of the Academy 1698, director 1708-11; teacher of his son Jean-Francois de Troy, Andre Bouys and John Closterman. **Alternate 3: Louis de Boullogne the elder (August 1609, Picardy -- June 1674, Paris), carried from #585/#586.** The friend who stayed Catholic and stayed.
