## M1. The voelkisch idea as transcendental essence

From The Crisis of German Ideology, 1964. The German people united through a transcendental essence, a "German soul" fusing pseudo-scientific nature philosophy with mysticism, and the mechanism is that the essence is not argued but felt, because an essence that must be argued is already lost, and the feeling is the proof, and the proof is the belonging, and the belonging is the exclusion, because an essence that unites some must leave others outside, and the outside is the point, and the point is the soul, and the soul is German, and the German is the essence, and the essence is the field, and the field pulls the scattered figures together, and the pulling is the ideology, and the ideology is the Crisis, and the Crisis is the book, and the book is the first attempt to treat voelkisch thought as intellectual history present in all parts of German society, not a subintellectual fringe. The watchable behaviour is a field of scattered figures slowly pulled into one glowing shape as the essence takes hold, and the shape excludes whatever does not fit.

**Takeaway:** let the essence pull the field together, and the piece should let the shape exclude what does not fit.

## M2. Subintellectual history: ideas that are believed

From the Crisis, 1964, and the American Historical Association obituary. Nazi ideas were not cynical manipulation tools; they were actually believed and formed a motive for action, however crude or irrational, and the mechanism is that a believed idea behaves differently from a used idea, because a used idea can be put down and a believed idea cannot, and the cannot is the danger, and the danger is that the crude idea is load-bearing, and the load-bearing is the motive, and the motive is the action, and the action is the history, and the history is the belief, and the belief is the subintellectual, and the subintellectual is the historian's subject, because the ideas that move masses are rarely the ideas that pass seminars, and the seminar is not the street, and the street believes, and the believing is the mechanism. The watchable behaviour is slogans that start as paper scraps and harden into load-bearing beams.

**Takeaway:** start with paper scraps, and the piece should let them harden into beams.

The watchable behaviour is a crowd that stops listening to speeches and starts moving in unison to a repeated gesture.

**Takeaway:** drop the speech, and the piece should let the gesture move the crowd.

## Evolution

Five phases, and the phases are the argument, because Mosse's work is one long answer to one question, how could it have happened, and the answer moves from the Reformation to the voelkisch idea to the mass rally to the body to the testament, and each phase keeps the last one inside it. Phase 1, the early modernist, 1944 to 1963: English constitutional history and Reformation thought, the dissertation on sixteenth- and seventeenth-century England, The Struggle for Sovereignty in England, 1950, The Reformation, 1953, The Holy Pretence, 1957, Europe in the Sixteenth Century, 1968, with Koenigsberger, and the method is history as a series of questions, not narrative, and the fate of liberalism is the constant theme, and reason of state is worked out WITHIN religious thought, against Meinecke, see M21, and Mosse himself later said there was continuity here, "not a big step from Christian belief systems to modern civic religions such as nationalism", see M20. Phase 2, the turn to Germany and the voelkisch idea, 1961 to 1970: The Culture of Western Europe, 1961, lectures, the 1963 Stanford seminar "What Is Fascism?", invited by Gordon Craig, to critique draft chapters of The Crisis of German Ideology, 1964, the first attempt to treat voelkisch thought as intellectual history present in all parts of German society, not a subintellectual fringe, and Nazi Culture, 1966, source anthology, and Germans and Jews, 1970, the "third force" book answering critics of the Crisis, and the motive stated by Mosse is that National Socialism was "a subject which I had avoided, perhaps because it touched me so closely". Phase 3, the cultural historian of mass politics, 1975 to 1980: The Nationalization of the Masses, 1975, written in Jacob Talmon's Jerusalem apartment surrounded by Rousseau, the new politics, political liturgy, secular religion, and Toward the Final Solution, 1978, the first in-depth history of European racism from the Enlightenment to the Holocaust, and International Fascism, 1979, and the general-theory essay, and Masses and Man, 1980, and the Italian connection deepens, De Felice, Gentile, the Intervista storm of 1974 to 1975. Phase 4, respectability, sexuality, masculinity, 1985 to 1996: Nationalism and Sexuality, 1985, his "coming out book", and German Jews beyond Judaism, 1985, and Fallen Soldiers, 1990, the myth of the war experience, and Confronting the Nation, 1993, and The Image of Man, 1996, and the outsider becomes the method, stereotypes of Jews, homosexuals, the mentally ill and the Roma read through one binary machine, see M10 and M23. Phase 5, testament and legacy, 1996 to 2000 and after: The Fascist Revolution, 1999, ten essays 1961 to 1996 with an introduction that reads as a methodological last testament, fascism as a cultural movement, understood from the inside out, see M17, and the memoir Confronting History, 2000, and the bequest and the Mosse Program, and the collected-works reissues from 2020 with introductions by Aschheim, Roberts, Browning, Griffin, de Grazia, Volkov, Wobick-Segev and Sheehan.

## Aesthetic logic

Mosse's aesthetic logic is that the surface is the politics, and the surface is not decoration, it is the mechanism, because a regime that rules by liturgy rules by what is seen, and what is seen is the monument, the festival, the uniform, the face, and the face is the stereotype, and the stereotype is the sorting, and the sorting is the exclusion, and the exclusion is the history. The logic runs in three moves. First, the visual turn: politics is drama, spectacle and aesthetics, not theory, see M5, and the historian must therefore read the parade ground the way another historian reads a cabinet minute, because the parade ground is where the decision is made, and the decision is the crowd moving in unison, see M3. Second, the sacred made civic: the nation borrows the church's forms, procession, silence, altar, martyr, and fills them with its own content, see M4 and M7, and the borrowing is not a metaphor, it is the mechanism, because the forms carry the feeling, and the feeling carries the allegiance, and the allegiance is the nation. Third, the outsider's eye: the man who stood outside the circle, German Jew, exile, gay man, sees the machine whole, see M23, and the seeing is the method, empathy before judgement, see M17, and the judgement when it comes is the humanizing, not the abolishing, see M24, because the flame answers a deep need and the need must be tended, not denied. The pieces that follow from this logic should therefore be built from visible forms, the square, the wall, the statue, the face, the flag, the library, and should let those forms do the work that an argument would do, and should show the mechanism from the outside, the way the outsider sees it, and should never celebrate the liturgy, because Mosse's whole work is a warning about what the liturgy does, and the warning is the point.
