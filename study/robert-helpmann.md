# Robert Helpmann: Study Notes

**Site:** Sir Robert Murray Helpmann (born Helpman), b. Friday 9 April 1909, Mount Gambier, South Australia, Australia; d. Sunday 28 September 1986, Royal North Shore Hospital, Sydney, Australia, age 77, of a chronic respiratory illness (emphysema), Australian ballet dancer, actor, choreographer and director; the man who danced the music the camera had already heard. He joined the Vic-Wells Ballet in 1932 under Ninette de Valois, partnered Alicia Markova and then Margot Fonteyn, took over as choreographer when Frederick Ashton was called up in 1941, choreographed the ballet inside The Red Shoes (1948) to Brian Easdale's score and danced the Boy in it, played all four villains in The Tales of Hoffmann (1951) without singing a note of his own, became co-director of the Australian Ballet in 1965 and was dismissed by its board eleven years later, and danced his last role, the Red King in Checkmate, at seventy-seven, weeks before he died. ## 1. Mount Gambier, Adelaide, and Pavlova at seventeen

He was born Robert Murray Helpman, the eldest of three children, to James Murray Helpman, a stock and station agent and auctioneer, and Mary, nee Gardiner. The family moved to Adelaide in 1914, and he was educated at Prince Alfred College there from 1914 to 1923, where his biographer calls him "an uninterested and recalcitrant scholar". He took his first dance lessons from Nora Stewart in Adelaide and made his stage debut in 1922, dancing at the Theatre Royal, Adelaide, in The Ugly Duckling -- Britannica says 1923, and the debut year is carried as 1922 on Live Performance Australia's specific venue and production, with Britannica's 1923 noted as the variant. He was professional by the age of twelve, earning four pounds a week in a summer revue. In 1926 Anna Pavlova toured Australia and took him on as a student apprentice; he trained under her leading male dancer Alexis Dolinoff, with daily lessons from Laurent Novikoff and Harcourt Algeranoff (the spellings differ across sources and are not merged here). Pavlova was, in his own words, "the most important single influence on my life". One number in the story does not add up and is not smoothed over: the Australian Dictionary of Biography obituary says his father packed him off to Melbourne as a fourteen-year-old to study with Pavlova's company, but the tour was in 1926, when he turned seventeen in April. The fourteen cannot both be right with the 1926 tour, so this study says he was taken on by Pavlova in 1926 and states no age. [C33793]

From 1927 he was principal dancer for J. C. Williamson Ltd, in musicals, revues and pantomimes, beginning with Lehar's Frasquita, and he made his first ballet, Business a la Russe, in Sydney in 1932. The commercial theatre is the whole of his formation in miniature: he learned to dance in shows that had to please a paying house every night, to act in revues where a step had to land a laugh, and to hold a stage without the protection of a classical company. Nothing in his later work -- the spoken verse inside Comus, the comedy of the Ugly Sisters, the Child Catcher sniffing out children -- makes sense without those five years of musical comedy, where dance was a means and never an end in itself. [C33794]

**Takeaway:** a choreographer formed in musical comedy and pantomime will always put the story first and the steps second -- the dancing interest is subservient, because that is what a paying house taught him.

## 2. London, de Valois, and Satan in Job

The English actress Margaret Rawlings, touring Australia, was impressed by him, encouraged a British career and gave him an introduction to Ninette de Valois, director of the Vic-Wells Ballet. He left Australia in 1932 and did not return until 1955. The arrival year is split in the sources and is stated as a split: Wikipedia and ACMI say he left Australia in 1932, while Britannica, glbtq, the SA History Hub and the Australian Dictionary of Biography obituary all put the London arrival in 1933. He may have left late in 1932 and arrived in 1933, but no source read says that explicitly, so neither year is asserted without the other. In London in the mid-1930s he added the second "n" to his surname -- at Margaret Rawlings's suggestion, or Pavlova's, or a numerologist's, depending on which account is believed; his brother Max and sister Sheila followed him in adding the letter. [C33795]

De Valois's verdict on him, quoted in Wikipedia and the SA History Hub, is the whole man in one sentence: "talented, enthusiastic, extremely intelligent, witty, cute as a monkey, quick as a squirrel... On the debit side, academically backward, technically weak... too busy having a good time." The break came when the leading dancer Anton Dolin left and Helpmann took over his role as Satan in de Valois's Job in 1933. In April 1934 de Valois created The Haunted Ballroom with Helpmann and Alicia Markova in the leading roles, and he became premier danseur the same year. When Markova left the company in 1935 he became the regular partner of the teenaged Margot Fonteyn -- then Peggy Hookham -- and Ashton choreographed Apparitions (1936) for the two of them; Sorley Walker calls them a "perfectly matched partnership". His signature roles in the years that followed were character parts and princes alike: Prince Florimund and Carabosse in The Sleeping Beauty, Dr Coppelius in Coppelia, Albrecht in Giselle, the Red King in Checkmate, Prince Siegfried in Swan Lake. [C33796]

**Takeaway:** de Valois hired a showman and got a choreographer -- the "technically weak" dancer who could not be beaten for character is exactly the dancer who ends up building ballets out of people rather than steps.

## 3. Oberon, and the dancer who could speak

In 1937-38 Tyrone Guthrie cast him as Oberon in A Midsummer Night's Dream at the Old Vic, opposite Vivien Leigh as Titania and Ralph Richardson as Bottom. The Times wrote that "his verse sings with his thought, his Oberon flashes with power", and added the sentence that ended any argument about what he was: "It will be useless in future for Mr. Robert Helpmann to pretend that he is exclusively a dancer of the first rank." James Agate called it "the best [Oberon] I have ever seen or ever shall see". The mechanism is visible in the reviews: his dancing gave him a gliding, "imagined invisibility" on stage, so the fairy king did not walk on, he arrived. He was not an actor who danced or a dancer who acted; he was a mover who could speak verse, and the speaking and the moving were the same instrument. [C33797]

**Takeaway:** a body trained to arrive without walking can make an entrance out of nothing -- the verse is carried by the glide, not the other way round.

## 4. Hamlet the play, and Shakespeare as a second career

Two years after dancing Hamlet in his own ballet, he played the part straight, in the title role at the Old Vic in 1944, and at Stratford in 1948 he alternated the role with Paul Scofield. The reviews were mixed -- Ivor Brown found him "eager, intelligent and exciting", Agate "most heart-breaking", Peter Brook highly exciting, others lightweight and doubtful of his verse-speaking -- but the run matters less for its verdict than for what it proves: by 1944 he was keeping two full careers in the air at once, dancing leads and playing Shakespeare in the same seasons. The other Shakespeare roles followed the same pattern of range over depth: Shylock, King John, Richard III, Petruchio, Angelo. In 1947, with Michael Benthall, he took over artistic direction of the Duchess Theatre in the West End; The White Devil, with Helpmann as Flamineo, was well received, while He Who Gets Slapped folded quickly. [C33798]

**Takeaway:** the mixed reviews are the point -- a dancer playing Hamlet badly by an actor's standard can still be the most exciting Hamlet in the room, because the body is doing work the verse cannot.

## 5. War: stranded in Holland, and the desk Ashton left behind

In May 1940 he was stranded in the Netherlands with the Sadler's Wells touring party when Germany invaded, and was evacuated on the last British boat as the Dutch surrendered. When Frederick Ashton was called up for military service in 1941, de Valois invited Helpmann to take over as the company's choreographer while he continued as a principal dancer -- sometimes three performances in one day. He was not liable for call-up: as an Australian citizen he was not drafted, and Wikipedia's note is blunt about it. The accident of nationality made his career. Ashton was the company's choreographer and Helpmann was its character dancer; the war removed the first and promoted the second, and the ballets Helpmann made in the next five years -- Comus, Hamlet, The Birds, Miracle in the Gorbals, Adam Zero -- are the whole of his choreographic reputation. He had little formal ballet technique training, so he did not build ballets around display technique; he built them around characterisation, dance as a means of characterising bodies in relation to other characterised bodies. His own credo, quoted from Caryl Brahms's 1943 monograph, is the sentence the whole study hangs on: "the dancing interest is subservient to that of the story." [C33799]

His first film roles came in the same years: De Jong in Powell and Pressburger's One of Our Aircraft Is Missing (1942), a supercilious Dutch traitor who first helps then betrays downed British airmen, and the comically fussy Bishop of Ely in Laurence Olivier's Henry V (1944). The two roles together are a catalogue of his screen gift: the traitor and the bishop are both men whose authority is a performance, and Helpmann, who had spent a decade playing kings and demons in tights, knew exactly how authority is performed. [C33800]

**Takeaway:** choreography by accident of war -- the desk fell to the man who had never trained for it, and the lack of training is visible in every ballet he made: no display, all character.

## 6. Comus: spoken verse inside dance

Comus, premiered 14 January 1942 at the New Theatre, London, with music by Henry Purcell arranged by Constant Lambert and designs by Oliver Messel, is the smallest and most radical of the wartime ballets. After Milton's 1634 masque, Helpmann danced Comus himself, the sinister forest demon who tries to seduce the lost Lady into debauchery -- and spoke lines from Milton's poem as he danced. The mechanism is exactly what it sounds like and it is almost never done: the dancer as actor-narrator, not a silent body, verse carried inside movement rather than between dances. A choreographer who believes the dancing interest is subservient to the story will, sooner or later, let the words in. Comus is that moment, and it sets the pattern for everything after it: Hamlet is pantomime with almost no dancing at all, and Hoffmann is a whole opera performed by a mime who never sings. [C33801]

**Takeaway:** let the words into the dance once, and the dance stops pretending it can say everything -- from Comus on, Helpmann's ballets are plays that happen to move.

## 7. Hamlet: the ballet that runs backward from a death

Hamlet, premiered 19 May 1942 at the New Theatre, with Tchaikovsky's Hamlet Overture-Fantasia and designs and costumes by the surrealist painter Leslie Hurry -- his first theatre design -- is about twenty minutes long, and it is the most original thing Helpmann ever made. It begins with Hamlet's death: four monks carry the dying Hamlet off, and after a blackout the piece runs backward through his memories as a succession of "swift cinematic images, economical in movement but rich in significance". The line that licensed it is "For in that sleep of death what dreams may come". It dispenses with danced movement almost entirely and is built out of pantomime, a monumental dumb show of key moments; the dances never last more than a minute. Characters waver, fade and replace one another: Hamlet reaches for one woman and finds he is clutching the other; Ophelia and Gertrude exchange places in a doorway; at Ophelia's funeral the body on the bier is the Queen's. The play-within-a-play is itself doubled -- the Ghost plays the murdered King, Ophelia the Queen, Hamlet Claudius. It ends as it began, the monks carrying Hamlet away. [C33802]

The look is the mechanism. The dancers wore intensely white luminous make-up with dark eyes and lips, like 1920s silent film; Hamlet in a black body suit with white belt and ornamental chain and cross; Ophelia in luminous lime-green; Gertrude ominous dark bluish-green with a spiked crown; the Ghost in an "alien" mask and costume like Chinese opera, with asymmetrical sleeves on everyone. Hurry's set was a decadent palace in red, orange, crimson, ochre and green; a column transforms into a hand clutching a dagger; a two-faced figure of Revenge looms; a blood-red sun; a vortex ring of flame above a staircase receding into depths. Helpmann asked for a set that "dominated" the dancers and reduced them to "pygmy stature" -- and the huge linear design let them move with extravagant, undulant rapidity without looking exaggerated. Audiences were overwhelmingly enthusiastic; only The Dancing Times complained it was scarcely a ballet. It was revived in 1947-48 with Helpmann and Fonteyn, in 1964 with Nureyev as Hamlet, and in 1981 with Anthony Dowell, when Anna Kisselgoff in the New York Times called it "one of the season's most interesting works" and the reading had become Freudian -- a Hamlet with an Oedipus complex, in love with his mother, unable to tell her from Ophelia. One source conflict is noted and not resolved silently: Toepfer says Hamlet "opened at the Theatre Royal York on December 26, 1942" with the same cast, which looks like a touring opening or an error against the 19 May 1942 New Theatre premiere in every other source; 19 May 1942 is used as the premiere. [C33803]

**Takeaway:** start at the death and the whole play becomes a memory -- time runs backward, people swap places in doorways, and the set does the work the steps cannot.

## 8. Miracle in the Gorbals: a slum street as a morality play

Miracle in the Gorbals, premiered 26 October 1944 at the Princes Theatre, London, with music by Arthur Bliss -- his second ballet score, written with the scenario and designs before him -- a scenario by Michael Benthall and designs by Edward Burra, widely regarded as Burra's finest stage achievement, is the wartime ballet that puts social realism on the ballet stage. The set is a Glasgow slum street, named and contemporary: a pub called "The Shamrock" on the left, a fish-and-chip shop called "Mac's" on the right, tenements crowding in. A despairing girl commits suicide; her body is carried in; a Christ-like Stranger enters at the back, the crowd parts, he bends over the body, she rises and warms to rebirth and dances -- the Dance of Deliverance. He converts the prostitute, Mary Magdalen-like, transfigured. The minister, jealous of the Stranger's authority, first tries to compromise him with the prostitute, then incites a razor gang to murder him; the Stranger offers no resistance. Dawn breaks over the street -- "the street itself is the real source of all the evil". Helpmann choreographed it and danced the Stranger; the original cast included Moira Shearer, who four years later would dance Victoria Page in the ballet he choreographed to Easdale's score. It was performed every season from 1944 to 1950, revived in 1958, and revived by Birmingham Royal Ballet in 2014 under Gillian Lynne, an original cast member. [C33804]

**Takeaway:** resurrection shown as a slow warming into dance, not a leap -- and a crowd that parts unconsciously for a stranger is the oldest stage mechanism there is, and it still works because it is about attention, not steps.

## 9. Adam Zero: a life told as a cast of functions

Adam Zero, premiered 10 April 1946 at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden -- the first new work staged at the reopened house -- with music by Arthur Bliss, a libretto by Benthall and designs by Roger Furse, is Helpmann's most schematic ballet and the one that shows his method most plainly. He choreographed it and starred as Adam Zero, an Everyman, in an allegorical life cycle from birth to death. Adam meets symbolic figures through his life, and every figure on stage IS a function: the Stage Director is his Creator and Destroyer, the Principal Dancer is Adam himself, the Designer is his Fate, the Ballerina is his First Love, Wife and Mistress, the Understudies are his Son and Daughter, the Character Dancers are his Cat and Dog, the Mime is his Spiritual Advisor. A young soldier survives war and carries its horrors in his subconscious; the ensemble is used as a sea of humanity Adam walks through almost imperceptibly. It was well received and revived the following year, but has not held a place in the repertoire. The mechanism is worth naming because it is Helpmann's whole dramaturgy in one cast list: the story is carried by who enters, not by what happens. [C33805]

**Takeaway:** when every figure is a function, the plot is a parade of entrances -- Fate walks in, the Advisor walks in, and the life is the order they arrive.

## 10. The Red Shoes: a score written to 120 drawings

The Red Shoes (1948), written, produced and directed by Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger, shot in Technicolor by Jack Cardiff with production design by Hein Heckroth, had its world premiere in London on 22 July 1948 and ran 107 weeks at the Bijou Theatre in New York. It was nominated for five Academy Awards and won two: Best Original Score for Easdale and Best Art Direction. Helpmann held three roles in it at once: Ivan Boleslawsky, the Ballet Lermontov's lead male dancer in the framing story; choreographer of the central ballet, "The Ballet of the Red Shoes"; and dancer of the Boy in the ballet. The order in which the ballet was made is the mechanism this study exists to record, and it runs the wrong way round from every assumption about how film music works. Easdale composed the ballet music from cartoon drawings and storyboards approved by Helpmann, assembled in the correct sequence: 120 drawings in total, executed by Hein Heckroth and Ivor Beddoes. As filming progressed the drawings were replaced, shot by shot, by the completed shots. So the order was drawings -- a visual plan, approved by the choreographer -- then music written to the drawings, then dance choreographed and filmed in short fragments to the pre-recorded music. The score was fitted to the film before the film existed, and the dance was fitted to the score. [C33806]

Two facts about the score complete the picture, and both correct the record. First, Allan Gray was first assigned the ballet score and his work was scrapped; Easdale, thirty-eight, fresh from Black Narcissus, replaced him -- the second time Easdale replaced Gray, after Black Narcissus itself. Second, Easdale conducted most of the film's music but insisted the ballet itself be conducted by Sir Thomas Beecham with the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra, and Beecham got prominent screen credit. Whether Beecham "conducted Easdale's Red Shoes ballet" is therefore a question of wording, not of fact: he conducted the ballet sequence, to Easdale's score, at Easdale's insistence. No source read mentions a stopwatch as a working tool; the timing device was the storyboard sequence itself. No rehearsal diary was found. That is a gap, not a finding. [C33807]

**Takeaway:** music written to drawings, dance written to music -- the storyboard is the score before the score, and the choreographer approves the pictures, not the notes.

## 11. The Red Shoes: the Boy who changes identity mid-phrase

The ballet has three characters only: the Girl, danced by Moira Shearer as Victoria Page; the Boy, danced by Helpmann; and the Shoemaker, created and danced by Leonide Massine with his own choreography. Helpmann had recommended Shearer for the lead; they had worked together in Miracle in the Gorbals. The Boy is the Girl's boyfriend, who turns into a sketch on transparent cellophane, then into a living counterpart of the Press -- "Le Jour" written on his forehead, an alter ego made of folded newspapers -- then the prince in a triumphant pas de deux, and finally the village parson who unties the red shoes as the Girl dies in his arms. A floating newspaper alternates between paper and Helpmann's human form, a callback to a windblown newspaper Victoria stepped on the night she got the lead role. The mechanism is identity as a costume change, not a plot event: one body becomes another character when the set and the music change around her, and the change happens mid-phrase, without announcement. [C33808]

The filming method follows from the drawings. Shearer recalled the ballet was "so cinematically worked out that we were lucky if we ever danced for as long as one minute" at a stretch; dancers used to live ballet had to spend hours preparing to shoot moments lasting only seconds. Filming the ballet took about six weeks. The dance was never performed as a continuous piece; it exists only as fragments cut together, and the fragments are cut on the beat of a score that was written to pictures. That is why the sequence feels like a film and not like filmed ballet, and why dance critics at the time complained it was unrealistic ballet: it is not ballet at all, it is choreography of the second degree, where the rhythm of the dance is served by the rhythm of the cinema. [C33809]

**Takeaway:** dance in sub-minute fragments to a pre-recorded score -- the body changes identity because the cut says so, and the cut is on the beat.

## 12. The Red Shoes: the ballet that is also the film

The ballet sequence is seventeen minutes long in Wikipedia's main text; its casting section calls it a fifteen-minute ballet. The fifteen-versus-seventeen figure is a rounding and source difference, not a settled fact, and it sits inside the wider conflict carried from #785, where the ballet's length is disputed at 15 vs 16 vs 17 vs 18 vs 20 minutes across sources. This study uses seventeen minutes and flags the rest. The look is not one continuous static shot of stage ballet: swiftly cut vignettes alternating with garishly decorated set pieces, close-ups, special effects, the action rocketing from stage right to stage left and then reversing into the ballerina's subconscious. Heckroth's own account, from 1947, is the best description of what the sequence is doing: "When the girl is dancing, she feels she is a bird, a flower, a cloud; when the spotlight hits her, she feels she is alone on a small island with waves breaking around; the figure of the conductor melts in turn into the form of the impresario, the magician, the lover, and at last into a figure made of newspapers." The red shoes themselves are the single saturated red object the whole sequence is organised around, in saturated Technicolor. [C33810]

The ballet duplicates Victoria's own story and foreshadows her fate: she dies in the film wearing the red shoes, paralleling her character, and the final image of the film is the company performing the ballet with a spotlight on the empty space where Vicky would have been. That is the whole of the mechanism in one image: a dance about a dancer who cannot stop dancing, performed by a company that has to go on dancing without her, with the light falling on an absence. The ballet is subjective as well as objective -- what she feels, not only what she does -- and that is why it survives as film rather than as a stage work: no stage could hold the bird, the flower, the cloud and the island in seventeen minutes. [C33811]

**Takeaway:** the spotlight on an empty space is the ending -- the dance continues and the dancer does not, and the audience watches the gap.

## 13. Hoffmann: one actor, four villains, one borrowed voice

The Tales of Hoffmann (1951), Powell and Pressburger's second ballet film, is the opposite of The Red Shoes in every way that matters, and Helpmann's place in it is the opposite too. He played all four villains -- Lindorf in the prologue and epilogue, Coppelius in the Olympia tale, Dapertutto in the Giulietta tale, Dr Miracle in the Antonia tale -- and his singing voice was dubbed by the Welsh bass Bruce Dargavel. In the prologue all of Lindorf's music is omitted, making him a silent character: pure mime. The choreography was Frederick Ashton's, not Helpmann's, and Helpmann performs no grand jetes here; one critic's verdict is that he "always moves purposefully and gracefully". He is an actor-mime inside someone else's dance, and the mechanism is villainy as a recurring shape, not a character: the audience recognises the same evil under four costumes, four wigs, four names, and the recognition is the pleasure. The film was shot 1-16 July 1950 at Shepperton and released in the UK on 26 November 1951 -- Britannica's "1950" is the production year, and 1951 is the release year used here. [C33812]

**Takeaway:** one antagonist played four times -- the audience does the work of recognising him, and the recognition is the character.

## 14. Hoffmann: the composed film

Powell's term for Hoffmann was a "composed film" -- a marriage of image to operatic music -- and the Red Shoes ballet and the Black Narcissus finale were earlier steps toward it. The method is the reverse of The Red Shoes: where Easdale's ballet score was written to drawings, here the entire opera was pre-recorded first -- the soundtrack recorded May to September 1950 under Beecham with the Royal Philharmonic -- and the film was edited to the rhythms of the music, with the acting fitted to the playback. Only Robert Rounseville as Hoffmann and Ann Ayars as Antonia sang their own roles; everyone else lip-synced to dubbed singers, and the film is completely without spoken dialogue. The acting is "highly stylised and similar to that of the silent-film era": eyebrows, eyes, gliding entrances. Colour is structure, not decoration: each tale has a primary colour -- Olympia in Paris yellow, Giulietta in Venice dark red, Antonia in Greece shades of blue -- and the sets are deliberately artificial, the Venice gondola moving through fake canals. Stella's profession was changed from opera singer to prima ballerina so the film could open with a new ballet, "The Ballet of the Enchanted Dragonfly", danced by Shearer with Edmond Audran. Bosley Crowther in the New York Times found it "splendid and cold" against The Red Shoes' "warmth and vitality"; George A. Romero later called it his favourite film of all time. Andre Bazin's verdict is the one that matters for this study: the cinema creates "a new artistic monster: the best legs adorned by the best voice... dance itself is renewed by the photography and the editing, which allows a kind of choreography of the second degree where the rhythm of the dance is served by that of the cinema." [C33813]

**Takeaway:** record the music first, then cut the film to it -- the edit becomes the choreography, and the mime only has to arrive on the beat.

## 15. The Ugly Sisters, and the comic gift

On 23 December 1948, the same month The Red Shoes opened, Helpmann and Ashton appeared together in comic drag as the Ugly Sisters in Ashton's Cinderella at Covent Garden, and the two men in drag became a much-loved institution in revivals into the 1970s. The roles were, in glbtq's phrase, "inspired by their own squabbles, antics, and attitudes". The mechanism is classical technique parodied from inside: the bossy sister does a fish-dive dreaming of the Sleeping Beauty pas de deux, the shy sister dreams of being Odile. The comedy works because both men could actually dance the steps they were mocking, and the audience knew it. The same gift runs through his whole career -- the comic roles in Coppelia, in Ashton's A Wedding Bouquet, in de Valois's The Prospect Before Us -- and it is the gift that made him, in the Australian Dictionary of Biography's phrase, "the complete man of the theatre": not a great dancer, not a great actor, but a great mime, and the mime is the bridge between the two. [C33814]

**Takeaway:** parody from inside the technique -- the joke lands because the dancer can do the real step and chooses not to.

## 16. The Child Catcher: balletic poise turned predatory

In Chitty Chitty Bang Bang (1968), directed by Ken Hughes, Helpmann played the Child Catcher, and it remains his most famous screen role. The mechanism is balletic poise turned predatory: a tall black figure with a long nose who sniffs out children in Vulgaria and lures them with sweets into a cage-cart. Empire magazine rated it among the 100 most frightening performances ever filmed; critics called him "the most sinister presence I have ever seen on film" and "a devastating turn". Off screen the opposite: he loved children, protected the child actors from the director's temper, and when his carriage overturned on set he swung free and "skipped" across the rolling vehicle -- Dick Van Dyke called it the most graceful thing he had ever seen. The role is the Ugly Sister mechanism inverted: the same tall, gliding, precise body that made audiences laugh in drag now makes children hide, because the glide has no warmth in it. The LA Times obituary dates the film to 1970; it is 1968, and the obituary's error is not repeated here. [C33815]

Ten years later, at sixty-nine, he broke his back during the filming of Patrick (1978), the Australian horror film in which he played Dr Roget, trying to lift Robert Thompson -- the comatose patient who kills by telekinesis -- in one scene. He was sixty-nine and still lifting another actor in a horror film. The anecdote belongs in a study of mechanisms because it is the same fact as the Child Catcher carriage: the body kept doing the work long after the career had supposedly moved on to directing. His final film was Second Time Lucky (1984), in which he played the Devil for the second time -- he had played the Devil in The Soldier's Tale (1964) and on television in 1959 and 1969 -- so the role bookends his screen career, from the Bishop of Ely in 1944 to the Devil in 1984, authority performed at both ends. [C33816]

**Takeaway:** the same glide that charms can terrify -- the difference is not the movement, it is whether the face is warm.

## 17. The Display: a lyrebird's courtship, watched for hours

The Display, premiered 14 March 1964 at Her Majesty's Theatre, Adelaide, during the Adelaide Festival, with music by Malcolm Williamson and sets and costumes by Sidney Nolan, is described as the first wholly Australian ballet -- all-Australian cast, Australian subject -- and it is the work that made Helpmann co-director of the Australian Ballet. Its origin is a dream in which he saw Katharine Hepburn naked on a dais surrounded by lyrebirds; in 1955 Hepburn had taken him to Sherbrooke Forest to watch lyrebirds for hours, and he claimed the lyrebird's movements were "learned after many hours of watching this beautiful creature". Patrick White was first asked to write the scenario; his manuscript was rejected by Helpmann and van Praagh. The plot is a bush picnic: the men practise Australian Rules football moves -- Ron Barassi of Melbourne and Carlton coached the dancers -- the lyrebird dances behind a gauze at the start, the Girl and the Outsider are attracted, the men drink, a fight breaks out over the Girl, the Outsider is beaten and left on the ground, he returns and attempts to rape the Girl, leaving her exhausted, and the Male reappears, tail feathers fully displayed, and enfolds her into his plumage. The four-metre lyrebird tail was built of bamboo, horsehair and leather. The sexual content nearly got the ballet banned by the Glasgow Presbytery on the 1965 Commonwealth Arts Festival tour, and the ban was narrowly defeated. [C33817]

The mechanism is an animal courtship display staged as a mirror of male human courtship: the bird's display and the footballers' display are the same dance. The lyrebird fans its tail and the footballers run their drills, and the audience watches one display comment on the other without a word being spoken about it. The themes the sources name -- mateship, gang brutality, hostility in Australian society -- are carried entirely by the parallel, not by any speech. It is the most Helpmann-like of all his ballets, because it is built the way he always built: watch something real for hours, find the display in it, and put the display on stage next to the human behaviour it explains. [C33818]

**Takeaway:** study the animal until the dance appears -- the lyrebird's tail and the footballers' drill are the same movement, and the ballet is the proof.

## 18. Yugen: beauty defined by absence

Yugen, premiered 18 February 1965 at Her Majesty's Theatre, Adelaide, with music by Yuzo Toyama and designs by Desmond Heeley, was freely adapted from the Japanese Noh play Hagoromo: the Moon Goddess Tsukiyomo comes to earth to bathe in a lagoon, and the fisherman Hakuryo steals her wings, mistaking them for rare shells. Helpmann defined "yugen" in his programme notes as "the most gracefully refined expression of beauty; beauty which is felt -- as the shadow of a cloud momentarily before the moon". Critics called it japonaiserie with screens, fans and parasols; one Australian critic called it "a highly refined Japanese Ziegfeld Follies". The mechanism is the negative space: beauty that is never shown directly. The wings are stolen, the goddess is defined by absence, the title concept is a shadow passing before the moon. It is the companion piece to The Display and its opposite: where The Display shows the tail fanned out in full, Yugen shows the wings taken away, and the dance is about what is missing. [C33819]

**Takeaway:** define the thing by its absence -- steal the wings and the dance becomes the shape of what was taken.

## 19. Don Quixote: the old knight played entirely by face

Don Quixote, the film of 1973, is Nureyev's staging of Petipa after the 1869 Bolshoi version, with music by Minkus and additional music by John Lanchbery, designs by Barry Kay and cinematography by Geoffrey Unsworth. Nureyev danced Basilio; Helpmann played Don Quixote, a non-dancing mime role, gaunt, wide-eyed, "more absent-minded than mad, but always heroic", his facial expressions "something at which to marvel". The two men co-directed it. It was filmed in 1972 in three unused hangars on an airfield near Melbourne, where a multi-level Spanish village was built around a huge central stage, at a budget of about one to one-and-a-half million dollars; it had its world premiere at the Sydney Opera House on 19 July 1973. The mechanism is a stage ballet re-staged for a hangar: the camera gets a whole village to move through, not a proscenium to look at, and the old knight is played entirely by face and gait, with no dancing at all. It is the Hoffmann mechanism -- the mime at full stretch -- carried into old age: at sixty-four, Helpmann no longer needed to dance to be the centre of a ballet film, because the face had become the instrument. The film dropped out of circulation for twenty-five years and was restored by the ABC in 1999. [C33820]

**Takeaway:** a mime role at sixty-four needs no steps -- the face and the gait are the whole performance, and the hangar is the stage.

## 20. The Merry Widow, and the dismissal

The Merry Widow, with its world premiere on 13 November 1975 at the Palais Theatre, Melbourne, was Helpmann's last major production for the Australian Ballet and the one that paid for the company. Scenario and staging by Helpmann, choreography by Ronald Hynd, music by Franz Lehar arranged by John Lanchbery, designs by Desmond Heeley: it was the company's first full-length, three-act ballet, commissioned by Helpmann as artistic director to make box-office money for a company in financial trouble, and it worked -- it remains in the repertoire and is one of the company's most valuable assets. The mechanism is operetta turned into dance: arias become pas de deux, the plot is carried by waltzes, lifts and can-can, and the comedy sits in the choreography -- people hiding in pavilions -- rather than in words. He had asked Hynd to choreograph it for Margot Fonteyn as Hanna, but insisted the premiere cast be Australian, with Marilyn Rowe as Hanna; Fonteyn danced it as a guest in 1976. [C33821]

The dismissal is the end of the Australian story and it is dated differently in the sources, so it is stated as a conflict. He had become co-director with Dame Peggy van Praagh in 1965 -- she was, in Sexton's phrase, "the pedagogue, teacher and administrator; he the restless 'jet-setting' star who spent six months of the year overseas and attracted international names" -- and he built the company by importing stars (Fonteyn, Nureyev, Erik Bruhn, Sonia Arova) and by creating Australian works with Australian collaborators. Van Praagh was forced by arthritis to retire in 1974; Benthall, his partner since 1938, died the same year. Helpmann became sole director in 1975. He was outspoken that the budget was inadequate and refused to cut costs as artistically and technically damaging; he said publicly the board had become "dominated by money-men who had no experience or understanding of artistic matters". The board decided he must go and tried to ease him out quietly; he refused to cooperate and made it public: "I want the public and the dancers to know that I didn't decide to leave them. I would have stayed with them until I dropped dead." Wikipedia, ACMI and Britannica date the dismissal to 1976, a year after he became sole director; the SA History Hub and the Australian Dictionary of Biography obituary say 1975, the obituary placing it six weeks before the Merry Widow premiere. The majority is used here -- sole director 1975, dismissed 1976 -- with the 1975 dating flagged. His own view, carried in the sources, was that his ballet was "museum theatre" to a board that wanted cheaper, plainer work: decor and costumes were the argument. He was succeeded by Anne Woolliams. [C33822]

**Takeaway:** the fight was never about the steps -- it was about whether a ballet company is allowed to spend money on decor, and the man who asked for a set that dominated the dancers lost to the men who wanted it cheaper.

## 21. The Red King at twenty-eight and at seventy-seven

In 1977 Peter Wright invited him back to Sadler's Wells Royal Ballet as the Red King in de Valois's Checkmate and Dr Coppelius in Coppelia; The Times called his Coppelius "a legend come to life". The Red King is the role that frames his dancing life: he first danced it at twenty-eight in 1937, and his last stage appearances were as the Red King with the Australian Ballet in Melbourne and Sydney in May 1986, at seventy-seven, weeks before his death. The same role revisited across a lifetime is the mechanism this study keeps finding in his work -- Hamlet danced in 1942, acted in 1944, revived in 1981; Don Quixote as a young man's mime and an old man's face; the Devil on television in 1959 and in his final film in 1984 -- and the Red King is the purest form of it, because the part itself is a chess piece, a figure who does not age, played by a body that does. The audience in 1986 was watching a seventy-seven-year-old man play a piece that does not move the way a man moves, and the gap between the two was the performance. [C33823]

**Takeaway:** keep the role, change the body -- the same chess piece at twenty-eight and at seventy-seven is a different performance, and the difference is the point.

## 22. The mime's credo, and what he could not do

Helpmann's own verdict on his gift, repeated by Moira Shearer and the Australian Dictionary of Biography, is that he was not a great dancer and not a great actor, but a great mime -- the bridge between the two. The verdict is honest about the limits and it explains the whole career. He had little formal ballet technique training, and de Valois's first assessment -- "academically backward, technically weak" -- never stopped being true; what she missed, and what the next fifty years proved, is that technique was never the instrument. The instrument was characterisation: dance as a means of characterising bodies in relation to other characterised bodies, in Britannica's phrase, and ballets "strongly theatrical and often contained elements of violence". Elektra (1963), his last Royal Ballet choreography, with music by Malcolm Arnold and designs by Arthur Boyd -- massive black-and-white images, a blood-red floorcloth, bold costumes painted on body stockings -- was "short-lived and critically damned", and the failure is as instructive as the successes: when the theatrical idea is only a look, and the character work is not underneath it, the ballet does not hold. [C33824]

**Takeaway:** know what the instrument is -- mime, not technique -- and the failures are as legible as the successes: a look without a character underneath it does not hold.

## 23. The set dominates the dancer

Across the whole career, the same staging decision recurs: the design is a character, and the dancer is small inside it. Hurry's Hamlet palace, with its column that turns into a hand clutching a dagger and its two-faced figure of Revenge, was built at Helpmann's request to dominate the dancers and reduce them to "pygmy stature". Burra's Gorbals street, with its pub and its chip shop and its tenements crowding in, is the real source of the evil in the piece, not any one dancer. Boyd's Elektra floorcloth, blood-red under black-and-white images, is the set the body stands on. Nolan's four-metre lyrebird tail, built of bamboo, horsehair and leather, is the display the men are measured against. The mechanism is consistent enough to be a rule: Helpmann puts the dancer inside a world that is bigger than the dancer, and the dance is the dancer's attempt to live in it. That is why the Red Shoes ballet works on film and not on stage -- the film can cut from a vignette to a set piece to a close-up, and the dancer is always small inside a world that keeps changing around her. [C33825]

**Takeaway:** build the world bigger than the dancer, and the dance becomes a negotiation with the set -- the dancer is small, and the smallness is the drama.

## 24. Aesthetic logic: the theatre man, not the dancer

The aesthetic logic of Helpmann's work is the logic of a man of the theatre who happened to dance, not of a dancer who happened to act. Everything he made runs on four rules, and each one is visible in more than one work. First, the story is senior to the steps: Comus speaks Milton, Hamlet is pantomime with dances under a minute, Adam Zero is a cast of functions, and the credo says so outright. Second, identity is a change of costume, not a change of person: the Boy becomes a newspaper man, a prince and a parson in one ballet; one actor is four villains with one borrowed voice; two men in drag are the Ugly Sisters; the same chess piece is danced at twenty-eight and at seventy-seven. Third, the set is a character and the dancer is small inside it, from Hurry's palace to Burra's street to Nolan's tail. Fourth, the crowd is the instrument: the Gorbals crowd parts for the Stranger, the court wavers and fades around Hamlet, the mob elevates the Girl in the Red Shoes, and the company goes on dancing around a spotlight on an empty space. [C33826]

What he never does is the thing a classical choreographer is supposed to do: he never builds a ballet around display technique, never lets a variation exist for its own sake, never trusts the step to carry the evening. The cost of that is visible in the failures -- Elektra damned, Perisynthyon poorly received after two commissioned scores were rejected and Sibelius was substituted at the last minute, Sun Music leaving no trace in the sources at all -- and the cost is the same as the achievement: when the theatrical idea is strong the work is unforgettable, and when it is weak there is nothing underneath to save it. He is the choreographer of the single strong idea carried by a crowd, a set and a change of costume, and the study of him is the study of how far a single idea can be carried when the steps are not allowed to help. [C33827]
