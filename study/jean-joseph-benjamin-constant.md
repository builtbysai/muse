# Jean-Joseph Benjamin-Constant: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean-Joseph Benjamin-Constant (1845-1902), French Orientalist, portraitist, muralist and etcher -- born Tuesday 10 June 1845 in Paris, not Toulouse as the brief for this study said (see C1); his mother died when he was two and in 1847 his father, the geographer Jean Baptiste Joseph Constant de Salinie, moved with him to Toulouse, where he was raised by two aunts; entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in Toulouse in October 1859, aged 14, under Jules Garipuy, and shared a municipal prize with Jean-Andre Rixens; entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in Paris in November 1866 and the studio of Alexandre Cabanel in 1867, failed the Prix de Rome in 1868 and 1869, and made his Salon debut in 1869 with Hamlet and the King, bought by the French state, the first of the dark, dramatically lit history pictures; travelled to Spain and then to Morocco in 1870 with Georges Clairin and Henri Regnault, under the influence of Mariano Fortuny in Spain, and returned to Tangier in 1871 at the behest of the diplomat Charles Joseph Tissot, staying about eighteen months (the National Gallery of Art says sixteen -- see C4) while Clairin pushed on to Fez and Marrakesh; came back to Paris in 1873, and from then Moroccan scenes replaced the history paintings of his youth -- Moroccan Prisoners won a third-class medal at the Salon of 1874 or 1875, the Entry of Mahomet II into Constantinople a second-class medal in 1876, and the great machines followed, Interior of a Harem in Morocco (1878), Evening on the Terrace and The Favourite of the Emir (1879), all fabricated in the Paris studio from sketches, collected props and hired Parisian models; after about 1880 he turned to portraiture and monumental decoration -- the Sorbonne allegories (1888), the Ames-Webster murals in Boston (1889-90), the Hotel de Ville plafond Paris Convening the World (1892), the Opera-Comique ceiling (c. 1898-1899), the Capitole at Toulouse (Entry of Urban II, 1900) -- and to the society portrait, which America made: "It is America which made me a portrait painter"; elected to the Academie des Beaux-Arts in 1893, taking Cabanel's vacant seat, honorary president with Jean-Leon Gerome of the Societe des Peintres Orientalistes Francais founded the same year, professor at the Academie Julian with pupils including Henry Ossawa Tanner, Angele Delasalle, Alice Beckington and W. S. Shanks; painted Queen Victoria from a single twenty-minute sitting in 1899, Queen Alexandra and Pope Leo XIII in 1901; caught flu in England in the autumn of 1901 and died Monday 26 May 1902 in Paris, aged 56, not 26 March as the brief said (see C2), and received a French state funeral; largely forgotten for most of the twentieth century -- "nothing had been published about him since his death except Regine Cardis' 1985 unpublished thesis" (Bondil) -- until the retrospective Benjamin-Constant: Marvels and Mirages of Orientalism at Toulouse and Montreal in 2014-15, the first monograph since his death. No catalogue raisonne exists and no oeuvre total can be stated: the legs assembled a 67-title list (34 dated, 33 undated) against the 250 works in the Montreal retrospective. ## M1. The Grande Machine as Theatre Set
A monumental architectural frame -- city gate, horseshoe arch, colonnade, throne dais -- is built first and the figures are staged inside it like actors. Entry of Mahomet II into Constantinople (Salon 1876) is the type: the triumphant sultan on horseback entering the conquered city amid spoils and the vanquished, flags, archway, ruins, on a canvas Zola said "could shade a whole battalion from the sun". The same frame holds Interior of a Harem in Morocco (1878), twelve nearly life-size figures inside a ten-by-seventeen-foot interior; Justinian in Council (1886), seven large figures around a throne; and Paris Convening the World (1892), a ceiling plafond. Bondil gives the rule: "successful dramatic perspectives using monumental architecture such as main city entrances, impressive arches, stocky colonnades, for placing his figures evokes theatre set design, especially that of Victorien Sardou"; Larroumet, in Le Figaro on 18 June 1896, put it in one line: "In Delacroix there is only history fixed by a few strokes of genius, in Benjamin-Constant there is drama and opera" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, as printed by Sotheby's). The watchable behaviour is an architecture that holds still while the crowd moves through it; the eye enters through the arch and is delivered to the single enthroned or mounted figure at the centre. **Takeaway:** build the arch first and let the crowd earn its scale by passing through it; the set is the picture.

## M2. White Tangier
High white walls, white terraces and white roofs are the luminous ground of the picture, rendered in a clear pale palette. On the Roofs is the purest case, a rooftop subject in white against the sparkling Mediterranean; Courtyard in the Sultan's Palace (1895-99) is the late version, a courtyard with high white walls open to the sky, flowering vegetation, a tower with keyhole-shaped openings, two clay pots on the wall, a carpet hanging over one side, three women in Moroccan dress looking at two small chained leopards in a keyhole-shaped wall opening, "bright sunlight streaming in, shadows on the walls and floor" (University of Michigan Museum of Art). Clairin, who met him on the first visit, gave the verdict Bonhams quotes: "his entire oeuvre was subjected to the fortunate and beneficial influence of Tangier; his entire Orient was Morocco... he always carried with him this first impression of Tangier, white and bright" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, as printed by Bonhams). The watchable behaviour is white surfaces that breathe -- light slides across them and shadows walk slowly across the wall as the sun moves; figures are small dark accents on a glowing field. **Takeaway:** make the wall the light source and let the figures be the shadows on it; white is the subject.

## M3. The Rooftop Dream at Twilight
Languorous women posed on a white rooftop terrace at dusk, figures nearly life-size, the composition openly imaginary. Evening on the Terrace (Le soir sur les terrasses, Maroc, 1879, Montreal Museum of Fine Arts) is the exhibited original, one of a series of rooftop pictures in which, in Sotheby's words, "languorous women, poised on a rooftop, appear as in a dream"; by 1900 it had become his most frequently reproduced work. Twilight Hours is the same world at larger scale: pale Parisian models hired for the day, a rug from the artist's own studio spread on the floor with an incense burner atop it, the foreground woman's gold-and-black damask robe reading as Turkish fabric and as tiger stripes, the cut of it Paris "aesthetic dress" rather than Moroccan. Sotheby's is explicit about what the pictures are: "It is this -- purely imaginary -- North African world of which Twilight Hours is a part" (VERIFIED VERBATIM). The Favourite of the Emir (c. 1879, National Gallery of Art) is the daylit version of the same dream: two pale-skinned women lounge and recline along a ledge while a turbaned musician plays, beyond them blue sea, three sailboats, pink mountains, white roofs. The watchable behaviour is a held breath -- nothing happens; the figures recline, the musician plays, the sea and sky behind them stay bright while the terrace cools; time passes only in the light. **Takeaway:** stage the dream at dusk on a white roof and let the light do the moving; the languor is the action.

## M4. Pale Flesh Against Patterned Cloth
Smooth pale skin -- arms, chest, face -- is set directly against richly embroidered, damask or gold-threaded fabric, and the contrast is the picture's pulse. The Favourite of the Emir is the fullest case: an orange-red dress with gold embroidery and a low-cut transparent blouse against pale arms and chests, one woman gazing directly at the viewer, the other lying in repose with eyes closed "as if lulled to sleep by the musician". The arthur.io essay on the picture states the mechanism: "Benjamin-Constant took great delight in the juxtaposition of the richly embroidered fabric against the smooth pale flesh of the women's arms and chests". Afternoon Languor works the same chord at smaller scale -- golden hues in the dresses counterbalancing gold touches in the pillow and curtain, a lion's pelt in the foreground -- and Judith (c. 1886, Metropolitan Museum of Art) sets the heroine's pale face and long hair against heavy dark red curtains, gold jewelry and draped fabric. The watchable behaviour is an eye that shuttles between skin and cloth -- the cloth seems to move, pattern shimmering, while the flesh stays still. **Takeaway:** put the still flesh against the moving cloth and let the contrast beat; the fabric is the motion.

## M5. The Studio Prop Emporium
Real objects collected in Morocco and Spain -- tiles, carpets, tapestries, shields, swords, lamps, a lion's pelt, a stuffed leopard, an incense burner, a rug -- are recombined in the Paris studio as set dressing, so the pictures feel like rooms in one house being redressed between scenes. A contemporary photograph of the studio, described by Christie's, shows the trove in place; Sotheby's, on Twilight Hours, names the props in the frame: "A rug from the artist's own, overstuffed studio has been spread upon the floor. Atop it sits an incense burner, a token of past travels". The University of Michigan Museum of Art notes the same practice in the Courtyard: "inclusion of local artifacts, rugs and costumes from his studio collection". The same rug, the same pelt, the same burner recur across pictures in new arrangements -- a rug here, a pelt there -- which is how a viewer who has seen two Benjamin-Constants knows the third is from the same house. The watchable behaviour is recognition across pictures: an object seen in one scene reappears in another, in a new place, so the oeuvre reads as one studio redressed again and again. **Takeaway:** keep one box of props and redress it for every scene; the recurrence is the signature.

## M6. Life-Size Architectural Cut-Outs
Excerpts of Moorish architecture, in the Arabo-Andalusian style seen in Spain, were built as life-size cut-outs in the studio and used interchangeably as backgrounds. Christie's, in the "Afternoon Languor" essay, describes the practice directly: the cut-outs were used interchangeably as backgrounds, and Constant then made "aggressive variations to the patterning to give it his signature touch by emphasizing the size of the scrolls, the shell motif at the center of the arch as well and the stylized grape motif". The same arch appears in Afternoon Languor, in Twilight Hours and behind the twelve figures of Interior of a Harem in Morocco (1878), but the ornament on it is never the same twice -- the scrolls grow, the shell at the centre of the arch swells, the grape motif is restyled. The watchable behaviour is a frame that stays fixed while its ornament grows, shrinks and re-patterns from picture to picture; the frame itself is a variable, not a constant, and the viewer who has seen one arch recognises the next as the same cut-out re-dressed. **Takeaway:** build one arch as a physical object and re-pattern it for every picture; the background is a prop, not a place.

## M7. Radical Chiaroscuro: Rembrandt Blacks Against Moroccan Light
Bondil's formula for the colour system is the mechanism stated as a recipe: "a clear palette inspired by his trips to Morocco, the depth of blacks from Rembrandt, and the colorful vividness from Delacroix, his two main masters" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, as printed by Islamic Arts Magazine). The picture is two rooms at once: a black room where objects dissolve and a lit stage where figures glow. Christie's, on Afternoon Languor, gives the clearest instance: "The chic bric-a-brac to the left of the composition enshrouded in shadow is barely distinguishable to the eye yet finds a harmonious juxtaposition with the bright light source directed on the models posed on their 'stage'". The same division runs through Entry of Mahomet II (1876), where the triumphant entry is lit against dark masses of the conquered city, and back to the beginning, Hamlet and the King (1869), a "dark-colored painting" in which Galerie Ary Jan already sees "the painter's interest in the dramatic function of light". The watchable behaviour is an eye that keeps crossing the border between the two rooms -- the black room swallows the bric-a-brac, the stage delivers the figures, and the picture's drama is the crossing itself. **Takeaway:** paint two rooms in one canvas, one black and one lit, and put the figures on the border; the shadow is the stagecraft.

## M8. The Direct-Gaze Ruler
Larger-than-life enthroned figures stare directly down at the viewer rather than at other figures in the picture. The Ames-Webster Byzantine cycle (1889-90, 306 Dartmouth Street, Boston) is the mechanism at full scale: four ruler portraits -- Justinian, Theodora, First Counsellor, Patrician Woman -- golden haloes, gold thrones, blue flowered backgrounds, "significantly larger" than the Venetian procession panels below them, and, in the words of the Nineteenth-Century Art Worldwide article, they "look directly at the viewer". The 1887 Theodora does the same at easel scale, bejewelled, in dark green, on a large marble throne, "staring directly at the viewer"; Justinian in Council (1886) seats the emperor on a large gold throne flanked by six advisors; and the Portrait of Queen Victoria (1899) puts the Queen on the Sovereign's Throne in the House of Lords, "rather lost within the splendour of Pugin's throne" (Chronicle250) yet still holding the centre by the stare. The watchable behaviour is a gaze that pins the viewer in place -- you are the subject being received, not a spectator; the figure does not move, you do, walking under it. **Takeaway:** seat the ruler, enlarge the throne, and point the eyes out of the picture; the viewer is the audience being received.

## M9. Gold-Leaf Mosaic Ground
Gold is applied in little squares of gold leaf "calling to mind the tesserae used in Byzantine mosaics" (Nineteenth-Century Art Worldwide) on haloes, thrones and blue flowered grounds, and in the easel pictures gold is sprinkled as touches through dresses, curtains and pillows. The Ames-Webster cycle is the fullest case: two tiers below a LaFarge stained-glass skylight, the Byzantine rulers on gold thrones with gold-leaf haloes, the Venetian processions above them in gold and colour. The artist's own explanation to Mrs. Ames gives the working reason, not the historical one: "I have used the gold in mosaic to lighten the vault" -- and, of the pairing, "Byzance and Venice go hand in hand". The watchable behaviour is a gold field that glitters as the viewing angle changes; the picture has a light source of its own, independent of the painted light, so the gold seems to move while the paint stays still. **Takeaway:** lay the gold in tesserae and let the picture light itself; the mosaic is the lamp.

## M10. The Throne That Swallows the Sitter
The sitter is set inside an architectural throne -- Pugin's Gothic throne, a gold Byzantine throne, a horse in a triumphal entry -- that is bigger, brighter and more detailed than the person. The Portrait of Queen Victoria (1899, 327.2 by 202.0 cm, Royal Collection) is the extreme: the Queen, painted from a single twenty-minute sitting, is set on the Sovereign's Throne amid Augustus Pugin's designs, and the 327-centimetre canvas is dominated by the throne's carving; Chronicle250 calls it "an almost spectral likeness, the result of a mnemonic visual impression gained in a single twenty-minute sitting" (VERIFIED VERBATIM). Justinian in Council (1886) does the same with a gold throne and six advisors; the Ames-Webster Justinian (1889-90) sits alone on a gold throne; Entry of Mahomet II (1876) mounts the sultan on a horse inside a triumphal arch, the horse and the gate doing the work the face does not. The watchable behaviour is a setting that grows and a person that shrinks -- power is shown as the furniture of power, and the sitter becomes a small still figure inside a large glittering machine. **Takeaway:** build the throne bigger than the sitter and let the furniture carry the power; the person is the smallest thing in the portrait.

## Evolution
Early, 1859-1870: academic history painting under Garipuy in Toulouse (from age 14, 1859-1866) then Cabanel in Paris (from November 1866); Prix de Rome failed twice (1868, 1869); first Salon 1869 with Hamlet and the King, bought by the state; dark-coloured, dramatic light already the signature; allegorical literary history (Too Late, 1866; Samson and Delilah, 1872, untraced). The California Art Club newsletter stresses he was "scarcely four-and-twenty" and already theorizing his own pictures in print. Morocco turn, 1870-1873: Spain in the Franco-Prussian War winter (Madrid, Toledo, Cordoba, Granada; Fortuny influence), then Tangier 1870 with Clairin and Regnault, then the eighteen-month Tangier stay 1871-72 at Tissot's invitation after Regnault's death. On return to Paris in 1873 the history painter is gone: "Scenes of life in Morocco now dominated his oeuvre, replacing the history paintings of his youth" (Sotheby's). First results are observational -- Riff Women at the 1873 Salon, a portrayal of local people rather than a harem fantasy -- then the big Salon machines: Moroccan Prisoners (medal 1874 or 1875), Entry of Mahomet II (1876, second-class medal, bought by Toulouse), Interior of a Harem (1878), Evening on the Terrace and Favourite of the Emir (1879). Scale peaks here: the Entry is 274 by 211 cm (and Zola's "twenty-three feet high" suggests the exhibited presence was larger still in effect), the Harem interior ten by seventeen feet. Mature Orientalist studio period, 1873-1880s: the work is no longer painted in Morocco but fabricated in Paris from the studio emporium -- sketches, collected objects, hired Parisian models, life-size arch cut-outs. The subject stays Moroccan but the method is theatrical: "drama and opera" (Larroumet, 1896). Smaller pictures for private collectors appear alongside the state-bought machines (Afternoon Languor; Christie's notes the deliberate scale-down). Byzantine and biblical history returns as a second Orientalism -- Justinian in Council (1886), Theodora (1887) -- the same devices (throne, gold, monumental arch) applied to Constantinople instead of Tangier. The folded mechanism of the sphinx servant belongs here: a dark-skinned attendant -- servant, eunuch or guard -- placed at the edge or behind the languid central women, upright and watchful while they recline (the Nubian servant in Twilight Hours, "sphinx-like in her garb"; the guard at the far right of Favourite of the Emir, who "serves as a reminder of the locale"; the eunuch attending the two women of Afternoon Languor). The attendant is the picture's still point -- vertical, alert, unmoving -- against which the reclining figures' languor reads. Indifferent repose belongs here too: the women exude "their characteristic air of indifference" (Christie's), the catalogue summary calls them "fierce-looking Moors and dispassionate odalisques", and in the Favourite of the Emir one woman gazes flatly at the viewer while her companion lies with eyes closed "as if lulled to sleep by the musician" -- the figures refuse the viewer, so the picture's energy sits in the fabrics, the light and the musician, not in the sitters. Decorative and portrait turn, 1880-1902: "After 1880, he changed his manner, devoting himself to mural decorations and to portraits" (Wikipedia); "By 1880, he moved away from Orientalist subjects for portraiture and more decorative works" (Web Gallery of Art). Murals: Sorbonne council room 1888, Hotel de Ville Salle des Fetes 1892, Opera-Comique ceiling c. 1898-99, Capitole Toulouse (Entry of Urban II, 1900), and the Boston Ames-Webster house 1889-90 -- the only surviving US murals, probably the only residential ones. America from 1888 ("It is America which made me a portrait painter"): Jay Gould 1889, Drexel 1893, Frederick Ayer 1898, then the crowning commissions, Queen Victoria 1899 and Pope Leo XIII 1900. Late Orientalist pictures still appear (Evening on the Seashore, c. 1891; Courtyard in the Sultan's Palace, 1895-99) but the centre of gravity is the grand portrait: sitter enthroned, swallowed by setting, painted from a twenty-minute sitting and finished in the studio. The folded mechanism of quick impasto against meticulous detail runs through the whole arc and is sharpest here: Christie's on Afternoon Languor -- "In his treatment of the pillow and the models' costume he achieves a meticulous detail -- in other areas, such as the lion pelt and the background he deliberately creates a blurred effect" -- and Bondil: "quick brushwork, often working in impasto... Not a draftsman -- only a few drawings are known -- Benjamin-Constant is the master of effect with efficient geometrical compositions" (VERIFIED VERBATIM). He dies in spring 1902, aged 56, after catching flu in England in autumn 1901; state funeral. Reputation collapses in the twentieth century ("intransigence toward modernism", Nineteenth-Century Art Worldwide; "fell into a kind of facility with chic portraits and repetitive work", Bondil) and is revived only by the 2014-15 retrospective, the first monograph since his death.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is the stage over the sitter, and effect over content. He never had a theory of Morocco; he had a studio, a box of props, and a wall to fill, and he filled it the same way whether the wall was a Salon canvas, a Boston ceiling, or a Paris portrait. Travel first, paint second (P1): the Morocco stay remade the career, but the painting happened in Paris, from sketches and collected objects, for the next ten years (P3) -- observation on the spot, invention in the studio, and the invention is the honest part, openly admitted: "It is this -- purely imaginary -- North African world" (Q10). The folded mechanism of subject as pretext is the logic stated in his own words: in November 1900 he said, "The subject in Painting is not of great importance... The Crowd gathers before the pictures which depict various kinds of occurrences... But this is of little or no significance" (as quoted by Carlsmith). Byzantium was chosen for the Ames ceiling because gold mosaic would "lighten the vault" -- "Byzance and Venice go hand in hand" as a decorative decision, not a historical one -- and the 1887 letter admits the Justinian "case concerning the rights of the Church" corresponds to no documented case. The picture announces its own artifice: the subject is a coat-hook and the real event is colour, scale and gold. Monumentality and theatre were the method, not an accident (P7): vast Salon machines, dramatic perspectives through monumental architecture, "drama and opera" against Delacroix's history. Colour balanced three sources (P8) -- Moroccan clarity, Rembrandt blacks, Delacroix vividness -- and speed was the technique (P9): quick impasto, no assistants, huge canvases painted fast because the effect had to land whole, not be built up. After 1880 the same method turns to the market (P15): the Salon and the French state bought the huge early canvases, monumental decoration served the Republican regime, and commissioned portraits in England and especially America paid for the rest -- the smaller pictures of women made to attract private collectors after the oversize works had been bought almost exclusively by the nation. He was a self-conscious conservative who fought modernity openly (P13), calling the triumph of the "oculists" in 1900 a shame (P14), a fierce lobbyist for the Academy who married into one of the most important French Republican families and ran an ostentatious studio as a reception salon. The critics saw both sides at once and could not settle the account: drama and opera, not history (P16); aesthetics and composition privileged over the people portrayed, which Bondil answers by saying the aesthetic sense "is exactly what we appreciate now" (P17); at his death an art star with a state funeral and already old-fashioned (P18); forgotten almost completely, then rediscovered (P19). The modern verdict is double (P20): superb colourist and master of effect, and a consolidator of colonial stereotypes -- the violent Muslim male, the oppressed Muslim woman, the indolent odalisque and the fierce Moor -- whose paintings Arab collectors themselves now buy and appreciate for what they are. The short version: he went to Tangier once, carried the white city home in his head, and spent thirty years rebuilding it in a Paris studio out of rugs, cut-out arches and gold leaf, with real people -- queens, popes, caids, his own sons -- set inside the machine like actors on a stage he had built for them.
