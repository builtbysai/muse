# Andries Both: Study Notes

**Site:** Andries Both (1612/13 - 23 March 1642), Dutch Golden Age genre painter of Utrecht, the elder brother of Jan Both, one of the leading Bamboccianti in Rome, who painted the low life of the Roman street - taverns, tooth-pullers, card players, beggars' concerts - in small cabinet pictures with broad strokes and caricature-like types, and whose designs outlived him as his brother's Five Senses etchings. He was born in Utrecht, the elder son of Dirck Joriaensz. Both, a glass painter or glazier; the brothers first learned to paint from their father, according to Houbraken, and Andries then studied under Abraham Bloemaert in Utrecht (Sotheby's lots 606 and 261; Wikipedia). He resided in Rouen in 1633 and travelled on to Rome, where his presence is documented from 1635 to 1641; he first shared a studio with the fellow Utrecht painter Jan van Causteren, and in 1638 his brother Jan joined him, the brothers living on the Via Vittoria in the parish of San Lorenzo in Lucina; both may have joined the Accademia di San Luca and the Bentvueghels, their bent-names not known. In Rome he came into contact with Pieter van Laer ("il Bamboccio") and his circle by 1637, and Sotheby's calls him "one of the most important Bamboccianti of his time, according to his contemporaries such as Jan Miel and Michelangelo Cerquozzi". Gerson's judgement is the sharpest sentence in the study: Andries "cannot simply be numbered among the successors to Pieter van Laer", because he had already developed his caricature-like style before Rome and "added a stylistic element of his own to the painting of Bambocciate". In 1641 the brothers left Rome to travel back to the Netherlands; Andries drowned in a Venetian canal, returning from some festivities, on 23 March 1642, aged only twenty-nine or thirty - the drowning in the parish of San Giovanni a Bragora is documented in the Venetian parish deaths register. Jan returned to Utrecht alone. His birth year is UNSETTLED across the sources read and is carried here as a spread, not a single year: Wikipedia and Sotheby's give 1612/13, Gerson Digital and the Te Papa blog give 1611/2, and no baptism record is cited in any source read (see C463). His death day-month, 23 March 1642, is given by Wikipedia only; Sotheby's, Gerson and Te Papa give the year 1642, while the Web Gallery of Art gives "c.1612-41" and old 19th-century catalogues give 1650 and even 1660, both wrong (see C463). His known paintings are FEW - Wikipedia's list of known paintings names only five - while his drawings survive in greater number and variety, and his designs outlived him as prints: Jan Both etched The Five Senses after Andries's designs in 1642-1650, after Andries's death, and the painted originals are lost. No words by Andries Both himself were found in any source read - no letters, contracts or statements; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Andries Both was born in Utrecht, the elder son of a glass painter, and he never really left the orbit of the Utrecht studio that taught him - except for the eight years in Italy that made him, and the canal in Venice that ended him. His father Dirck Joriaensz. Both painted glass; Houbraken says the brothers first learned to paint from him. Then, according to von Sandrart, Andries and his younger brother Jan were apprenticed together to Abraham Bloemaert, the man who ran the most important teaching workshop in the city - the same studio Hendrick Bloemaert grew up in. The apprenticeship is debated by Burke, and no primary document was read in this leg, but no other teacher is documented for him. He resided in Rouen in 1633, on the way south, and reached Rome, where his presence is documented from 1635 to 1641. Gerson adds precision and a dispute: Waddingham supposed Andries was already in Rome in 1633, but the earliest evidence documenting his presence is a Dutch notarial act from early 1637 stating he was living in Via Margutta in 1635. Whether he travelled to Rome via Venice is uncertain, even though a drawing in the Leiden Print Room bears a handwritten note "Andrea Bot fe / Venetsia 1632" - a single inscription, not documentary proof.

Rome is where the career is made, and it is made in the street. He first shared a studio with Jan van Causteren, another Utrecht painter; in 1638 his brother Jan joined him, and the brothers lived on the Via Vittoria in the parish of San Lorenzo in Lucina. Both may have joined the Accademia di San Luca and the Bentvueghels, their bent-names not known. He came into contact with Pieter van Laer and his circle soon after arriving, as a witness's statement of 1637 shows, and Sotheby's calls him one of the most important Bamboccianti of his time, according to contemporaries such as Jan Miel and Michelangelo Cerquozzi. But Gerson refuses to number him among van Laer's successors: he had already developed his caricature-like style during his time in Venice and must have brought it with him "from the land of Adriaen Brouwer" - he "added a stylistic element of his own to the painting of Bambocciate". A drawing made in Amsterdam in 1636 shows he had already assimilated the style and depictions of the Bamboccianti before or just after arrival. Rome did not invent him; it gave him a subject.

The Rome years end in Venice, on the way home. In 1641 the brothers left Rome to travel back to the Netherlands; Andries drowned in a Venetian canal, returning from some festivities, on 23 March 1642, aged only twenty-nine or thirty. Jan returned to Utrecht alone and went on to the golden-light Italianate landscapes; Andries never painted again. What he left is small and scattered: five known paintings by Wikipedia's count, a varied surviving drawing oeuvre - landscapes in pen and ink and black chalk, biblical subjects, diableries, a small group of metalpoint studies - and a posthumous career as a designer for prints, Jan etching The Five Senses after his designs in 1642-1650. His large late landscapes do not exist; his afterlife is a set of etchings of peasants guzzling pancakes and having teeth pulled, with comic Dutch verse underneath. He was twenty-nine or thirty, and the whole career fits into eight years in Rome and a handful of Utrecht pictures before it.

**Takeaway:** a short life with one move and one subject - the Utrecht pupil who went to Rome, learned the low life from van Laer and Brouwer, added a caricature hand of his own, and drowned on the way home, leaving five paintings, a pocketful of drawings, and a set of etchings his brother made after his death.

## M1. Caricature-like figure types

His peasants are drawn with exaggerated, stocky, ungainly bodies and coarse faces - more caricature-like than Pieter van Laer's good-humoured strollers. Gerson Digital (read end to end): "Andries' types are a little more caricature-like than Pieter van Laer's good-humoured strollers." Watchable behaviour: in a group scene the figures read instantly as types (the glutton, the drunk, the tooth-puller, the hag) rather than as individuals; the exaggeration is the joke and the recognition device at once.

**Takeaway:** the figure is a type pushed past likeness; you recognise the appetite before the person.

## M2. Broad, loose brushwork on a small surface

Paint is laid on with broad strokes rather than fine finish. EN Wikipedia (read end to end), on his work: "depicting the seamier side of Italian life with broad strokes." Watchable behaviour: at cabinet scale (a Scene in a Brothel is only 44 x 51 cm) the handling stays sketchy and energetic - forms are blocked in, not polished; the roughness is part of the low-life bluntness.

**Takeaway:** the brush stays broad even when the picture is small; the roughness is the bluntness.

## M3. Lateral raking light and dark shadow (Caravaggesque chiaroscuro)

The Bamboccianti idiom of dark shadows cut by bright light falling in from the side. Gerson Digital (read end to end): "dark shadows and the lateral incidence of bright light were elements of Caravaggism which Pieter van Laer included in his small panel paintings", and Both worked inside that idiom. Watchable behaviour: a tavern interior or street scene is staged as a pool of light against gloom; faces and hands catch the light, the rest sinks; the light does the moral sorting (who is lit, who is in shadow).

**Takeaway:** the light sorts the scene; the lit face is the subject and the dark is the rest.

## M4. The small cabinet format

His paintings are generally small cabinet paintings (or etchings) of everyday low life - the format of the Bambocciate. EN Wikipedia (read end to end): "These Bambocciata works were informed by existing traditions of depicting peasant subjects from 16th-century Netherlandish art. They were generally small cabinet paintings or etchings of the everyday life of the lower classes in Rome and its countryside." Watchable behaviour: the viewer leans in close; the scene is a peep-box, not a wall piece; the smallness makes the coarseness intimate rather than monumental.

**Takeaway:** smallness is the format of the joke; you lean in to a peep-box, not up to a wall.

## M5. The foreground anecdote cluster

The picture is built as a tight knot of figures in the immediate foreground, locked in a single bodily action - eating, drinking, gambling, tooth-pulling, singing. Sotheby's lot 308 (read end to end) describes the type exactly: "peasants eating and drinking in front of an inn, with a maid serving, a village beyond". Watchable behaviour: the eye lands on the knot first and stays there; the action is legible in one glance (a glass raised, a tooth gripped, a pancake snatched).

**Takeaway:** one knot, one action, one glance; the picture is a verb, not a view.

## M6. Two-plane composition: low-life foreground, village beyond

The foreground group is set against a receding second plane - a village, a road, a landscape vista - giving the anecdote a place without letting the place take over. Sotheby's lot 308 (read end to end): "The perspective of the village scene beyond and the colouring of the figures reminds of the works of his fellow-citizen Joost Cornelis Droochsloot." Watchable behaviour: the eye drops from the noisy foreground into a quieter, smaller background world, then bounces back; the depth is a joke about scale (big appetites up front, small orderly village behind).

**Takeaway:** the village behind is the straight man; the big appetites play against a small orderly world.

## M7. Staffage added after the landscape (the figure hand)

In the brothers' collaborative landscapes the figures were painted INTO a finished landscape afterwards. The Museo del Prado (read end to end), on Jan Both's Landscape with Fishermen and Shepherds on a Riverbank (1639-1641): "The X-ray image reveals that, like those in the previous two works, the figures were also added to the landscape, but are executed more densely." Watchable behaviour: the figures sit ON the landscape rather than IN it - denser paint, a slightly different touch, a seam a conservator can see on X-ray and a viewer can feel as a figure that doesn't quite breathe the same air as its ground. FLAG: attribution of those particular figures to Andries is Burke's 1976 belief, disputed - early Prado catalogues attributed them to Jan Miel, and the Prado notes the bare-chested fisherman's handling is "very similar" to figures elsewhere attributed to Both by Salerno. The attribution is UNRESOLVED across three candidates (Jan Miel / Andries Both / Jan Both himself). See C459 and Disputes.

**Takeaway:** the figure is added, not grown; denser paint on a finished ground is the seam of the collaboration.

## M8. Swirling parallel-stroke landscape drawing (Bloemaert inheritance)

His youthful landscape drawings in black chalk (and pen and ink) render humble cottages and barns "with a swirling pattern of parallel strokes" - a technique and compositional approach taken directly from Bloemaert. Sotheby's lot 606 (read end to end): "all are very reminiscent of Bloemaert's technique and compositional approach, with their detailed depictions of humble cottages and barns, rendered with a swirling pattern of parallel strokes." Watchable behaviour: the drawing's texture IS the subject: the hatch swirls make a thatch roof or a barn wall vibrate; the building is built out of rhythm, not outline.

**Takeaway:** the hatch is the building; rhythm draws the roof, not outline.

## M9. The pocket metalpoint notebook (tafeletten)

A group of six fine metalpoint drawings on prepared paper or vellum "tafeletten", likely bound into a reusable notebook the artist carried in his pocket "to make rapid sketches of what he saw, obliterating any unwanted studies by regrounding the sheets when back in the studio". Sotheby's lot 261 (read end to end). One of the group is dated 1632, so they were made just before Italy. Watchable behaviour: the drawings are fast, small, observational - a peasant caught mid-gesture, drawn on the spot and kept only if it worked; the medium itself (metalpoint on a prepared tablet) enforces a light, precise, unforgiving line.

**Takeaway:** the notebook is a pocket and the line is unforgiving; sketch fast, keep only what works, wipe the rest.

## M10. Diablerie: the demonic grotesque in pen and ink

A small, highly distinctive group of pen-and-ink drawings of demonic scenes (diableries) - devils, monsters, hellish crowds - in the Netherlandish tradition of Bosch and Bruegel carried on by Jacques de Gheyn, Cornelis Saftleven and Both. Sotheby's lot 261 (read end to end) confirms diableries among his surviving drawings. The monogram detail - one Berlin drawing in the group signed with Both's distinctive monogram, securing the attribution - is stated in Sotheby's lot 74, which the parent's search returned verbatim ("one of those is signed with Both's distinctive monogram, thus securing the attribution of the whole group"), so it is search-corroborated, NOT read end to end. Watchable behaviour: the same caricature hand as the peasant scenes, turned to the fantastic: the grotesque is drawn with the same rapid pen as a tavern brawl, so hell looks like a village festival gone wrong. FLAG: the existence of the diablerie group is grounded in a page read end to end (lot 261); the monogram detail is search-corroborated only. See C464.

**Takeaway:** hell is drawn with the tavern pen; the grotesque is a peasant scene with the volume turned up.

## M11. Flattened outlines that thicken the body

In the etchings after his designs, "Jan Both uses distinct, flattened outlines to emphasise the awkwardness of his peasants' thickset bodies". The flattening is Andries's design language carried into print: the outline does not model volume, it stamps the body's bulk. Te Papa blog (read end to end), describing Jan's etching after Andries, so the mechanism belongs to Andries's design, Jan's execution. Watchable behaviour: figures look pressed flat and wide - dough-faced, heavy, graceless - and the flatness makes their awkwardness funny rather than pitiable.

**Takeaway:** the outline stamps the bulk; flatness makes the body funny instead of pitiable.

## M12. Comic verse caption as a second channel

The Five Senses etchings carry comic Dutch verse captions under each image (Taste: a child cries as her pancake is guzzled; Touch: "What the devil are you doing to my teeth with those pliers?"). The caption is part of the mechanism, not decoration: image shows the sense, verse voices the peasant. Te Papa blog (read end to end), captions quoted in full there. Watchable behaviour: the picture is read twice - once as a scene, once as a voice; the joke lands in the gap between the dignified allegory title and the crude verse.

**Takeaway:** the verse is a second channel; the joke lives in the gap between the allegory and the voice.

## M13. The single-source interior (candlelight)

A nocturne/candlelit interior in which one flame is the only light source and the figures are sculpted out of darkness. Hunting by Candlelight (1630, oil, 34 x 27 cm, Museum of Fine Arts, Budapest) is the named example. Watchable behaviour: faces and hands emerge from black; the flame is a small bright centre and everything else is graded shadow; the scene feels like a moment caught, not posed. FLAG: the painting is listed on Wikipedia (read end to end) but the chiaroscuro description of it comes from search-result snippets (Wikioo/WahooArt) that were NOT read end to end; treat the mechanism as grounded in the listing plus the general Bamboccianti chiaroscuro of M3, not in a full reading of a candlelight analysis. The 1630 date itself is doubtful (see Disputes). See C464.

**Takeaway:** one flame, one pool of light; everything else is graded dark, and the moment is caught, not posed.

## M14. One sense per bodily act (the Five Senses system)

The allegory of the five senses is built as five peasant vignettes, each sense embodied by a single gross bodily act: Taste = guzzling a pancake; Touch = having a tooth pulled; Sight = peddling spectacles to a woman who won't see; Sound = two graceless old men singing; Smell = a nappy change and a child on a potty. Te Papa blog (read end to end). Watchable behaviour: an abstract allegory becomes a sequence of gags; each print is a one-act farce with the sense as the punchline.

**Takeaway:** the allegory is five gags; each sense is one bodily act, and the act is the punchline.

## M15. The figure study as rehearsal (black chalk on parchment)

Individual figure studies in black chalk - e.g. Seated Peasant with a Jug, a study "likely for the seated drinking figure in Both's painting 1634, in the Centraal Museum, Utrecht" - show him working out a single body before placing it in a group. Watchable behaviour: a figure is solved alone (the slump of a seated drinker, the grip on a jug) and then dropped into the cluster, which is why the clusters read as assemblies of solved poses. FLAG: the Swann Galleries lot page was NOT read end to end; this mechanism rests on a search-result snippet. The underlying practice (drawn figure studies feeding paintings) is corroborated by Sotheby's lot 261's metalpoint peasant studies (read end to end). See C464.

**Takeaway:** solve the body alone, then drop it into the crowd; the cluster is an assembly of solved poses.

## Works

### Phase 1 - Utrecht, c. 1632-1634: field notes become cabinet pictures

Both starts as a draughtsman on the move: the metalpoint tafeletten (W15, c. 1632) are pocket-notebook sketches of peasants, made to be wiped and reused. The three dated paintings of 1634 - Card Players (W1), Concert of Beggars (W2), Tooth-Drawer (W3) - convert those field notes into small cabinet pictures: dark interiors, figures pressed close, one low-life set piece per canvas (gambling, music, dentistry). The diableries (W16) run alongside as a parallel drawn practice, Bosch-derived and pen-driven. Everything is small, close, and Dutch: Brouwer's influence is the air the work breathes, and the light is interior and artificial or simply dim. No landscape, no open air, no Italian anything - even though Both was already travelling (Rouen 1633).

- W1. The Card Players (Les joueurs de cartes). Date: 1634 (dated; Christie's lot essay confirms the 1634 date and the Rijksmuseum holding). Medium: oil on panel (medium not stated in the sources read; listed as oil painting in secondary sources - treat as reported). Dimensions: 32 x 43 cm (REPORTED - artsdot/wahooart, citing the Rijksmuseum painting catalogues; the Rijksmuseum's own object page was NOT read in this leg). Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. A tight, dark interior of peasants gambling at cards - the classic Brouwer-derived low-life subject. Dated 1634, i.e. painted in Utrecht just before Both left for Italy (he was in Rouen in 1633, in Rome from 1635). Christie's uses it as the benchmark for his pre-departure Dutch manner: small cabinet scale, close-cropped figures, moralising card-play. It is the anchor work of the early phase.

- W2. A Concert of Beggars (Un concert de gueux). Date: 1634(?) - signed and dated 'ABoth 1634(?)' lower left; the question mark is Christie's own. Medium: oil on panel. Dimensions: 42 x 53 cm (VERIFIED - Christie's lot page, read end to end by leg 2; auction-house record, not a museum page - see the Dimensions tally for the definition). Location: private collection (sold from the Veil-Picard collection, Christie's Paris). Ragged musicians performing together - a beggars' concert. Christie's essay is explicit that, although painted in the year Both reached Rome, the picture "evoque ceux executes aux Pays-Bas juste avant le depart de l'artiste", i.e. it still belongs to the Dutch phase, not the Italian one. Paired with W1 it shows the 1634 moment: music, drink and gambling as a single subject-cluster, figures pressed close to the picture plane.

- W3. The Tooth-Drawer (De tandentrekker). Date: 1634. Medium: oil (support not stated in the source read). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (no dimensions in the source read). Location: Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen, Rotterdam - inv. 2199 (OK), RKDimages 287701. A barber-surgeon wrenching a tooth from a grimacing patient while onlookers watch - the dentist as public theatre. Dated 1634, it is the third securely dated work of the Utrecht year and completes the 1634 triptych of low-life set pieces (cards, music, dentistry). The same subject reappears as Touch in the Five Senses (W8), showing Both returning to his early motifs in drawn form at the end of his life.

- W15. Metalpoint peasant study (Ames group, Sotheby's 2018 lot 261). Date: c. 1632 (one drawing in the group is dated 1632; Sotheby's argues all six were made shortly before the Italian journey). Medium: metalpoint on prepared paper or vellum ('tafeletten'). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (no dimensions in the page read). Location: private collection (ex Winslow and Anna Ames, Springfield, Massachusetts; Christie's New York 30 Jan 1998 lot 367; Sotheby's New York 2018 lot 261). A rapid peasant study on a reusable prepared tablet - one of six fine metalpoint drawings formerly in the Ames collection. Sotheby's explains the technique: the little tablets were probably bound into a pocket notebook, sketched on the spot, and reground when back in the studio. This is the earliest securely placed work in the catalogue and the key to Both's method: observation first, in metalpoint, on the move - the field notes from which the painted peasants (W1-W3) were built. Sotheby's places Both's metalpoint in the Goltzius/Haarlem tradition (c. 1600 flowering) but the subjects in the Callot/Brouwer/Teniers line.

- W16. Diablerie scene (black chalk drawing, Sotheby's 2015 lot 31). Date: undated; group dated by association with signed Berlin pen drawings. Medium: black chalk on paper. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (no dimensions in the page read). Location: private collection (ex Jan Krugier; London Sotheby's 4 July 2007 lot 74). A lively scene of demons - a diablerie in the Bosch/Bruegel tradition, continued by Jacques de Gheyn and Cornelis Saftleven. Formerly attributed to David Teniers; reattributed to Both by comparison with a small group of pen-and-ink diableries (one in the Lugt Collection, inv. 6633; three through auction rooms 1942-2001) and three Berlin drawings in the same technique, one signed with Both's monogram. The attribution chain is unusually well documented for Both, and it opens a second, stranger side of his draughtsmanship: not just observed peasants but invented demons, drawn with the same rumbustious penmanship.

- W17. A Toothdrawer Operating on a Man, with a Woman and Child Looking On. Date: undated in the source read. Medium: not stated in the source read. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: listed on Art UK (collection not stated in the snippet read); Google Arts and Culture lists it among Both-related works. A tooth extraction watched by a woman and child - the onlookers now part of the composition, where W3's crowd is implied. FLAG: this may be a version or variant of W3 (The Tooth-Drawer, Boijmans, 1634) rather than a separate composition; the titles are near-identical and no source read distinguishes them. Do not count it as a securely independent work. It is catalogued here because the Art UK listing treats it as a distinct object, with the duplication flagged.

### Phase 2 - Rome, 1635-1641: the slow Italian turn

The break is slower than the biography suggests. The first Rome-year pictures - Brothel (W4, 1635) and Poor Painter (W12, 1635) - are still dark interiors in the Dutch manner; Christie's says the same of the 1634 Concert (W2), which "evoque" the pre-departure pictures. The Italian turn shows first in subject and setting rather than in handling: Travellers by a Well (W5, 1635-41) moves the action outdoors to a Roman well, with travellers, women and children instead of tavern brawlers, and the landscape begins to carry weight. Hunting by Candlelight (W6) and Flea Hunting at Candlelight (W18) push the artificial-light device harder - a single flame inside darkness, the Bamboccianti night piece. A Rocky Landscape with Figures, Sunset (W20) is the furthest point: landscape leading, figures small, the joint-work territory shared with Jan, who joined him in Rome in 1638. What does NOT change: the scale stays cabinet-small, the figures stay thickset and comic, and the humour stays cruel. Rome gave Both new stages (the well, the street, the sunset road), not a new sensibility.

- W4. Scene in a Brothel (Brothel; formerly 'The Return of the Prodigal Son'). Date: 1635. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 44 x 51 cm (REPORTED - Wikipedia; the Bredius Museum's own object page was NOT read - its site link is archived). Location: Museum Bredius, The Hague - inv. 116-1946, cat. no. 21 (Blankert 1991). Peasants and a woman at a table in a brothel interior; the old "prodigal son" title was a moralising misreading. Dated 1635, the first Rome year, yet still an interior in the Dutch manner - evidence that the Italian turn was gradual, not a clean break. The small canvas (44 x 51 cm) keeps the cabinet scale of W1-W3.

- W5. Travellers by a Well. Date: 1635-1641 (the whole Rome period; no tighter date in any source read). Medium: oil on wood (per Wikipedia); the NGV's own page as read gives no medium, date or dimensions. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (the NGV object page, read end to end, lists accession number and provenance only - no dimensions). Location: National Gallery of Victoria, Melbourne - accession E3-1980, International Painting. Travellers - men, women and children - drinking and resting at a well in the Roman countryside. This is the Rome-phase picture par excellence: the subject moves outdoors, the figures become travellers rather than tavern brawlers, and the landscape (the well, the open air) carries as much of the picture as the figures. Provenance is a cautionary tale: sold at Christie's London, 16-17 February 1810, lot 94, as "Jan Both (style of)"; bequeathed to the Art Gallery of South Australia 1956, sold as unsuitable in Adelaide 1957, bought by Dr John Orde Poynton, donated to the NGV 1980. The Jan/Andries confusion is documented in the object's own history.

- W6. Hunting by Candlelight. Date: c. 1630 - reported (artsdot/wahooart/WGA); FLAG: this date is implausible. Both was born 1612/13, so a 1630 date makes him 17-18, still a Bloemaert pupil in Utrecht, and it predates every other dated work by four years. No museum source was read to confirm it; treat as doubtful. Medium: oil. Dimensions: 34 x 27 cm (REPORTED - retailer sites only). Location: Szepmuveszeti Muzeum (Museum of Fine Arts), Budapest. A night scene lit by candlelight - hunters (or revellers) in a dark interior, the flame as the single light source. If the date is wrong and the picture is really Roman-period, it belongs with the candlelit interiors of the Bamboccianti; if 1630 is right, it would be a precocious Utrecht night piece in the Honthorst manner. The Web Gallery of Art lists it as Both's sole painting in its database. Either way it is the clearest evidence of Both's interest in artificial light as a device - light as a small hot pool inside darkness, a mechanism that recurs in the candlelit tavern scenes.

- W12. The Poor Painter in his Studio. Date: 1635 - reported. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 330 x 247 cm - reported by artsdot, and IMPOSSIBLE: a 3.3-metre canvas would be a public altarpiece, not a Both cabinet picture; the figure is almost certainly 33.0 x 24.7 cm with a misplaced decimal. Flagged as an error, not a measurement. NOT VERIFIED. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam - reported (artsdot; Rijksmuseum object page NOT read). A poor painter at work in his studio - a self-referential joke about the artist's own trade, and the only known Both that turns the low-life gaze back on the painter himself. If the 1635 date holds, it is a Rome-year work that is still an interior, like W4. Treat the whole entry as weakly sourced (single retailer source) - it needs a Rijksmuseum object-page check in a later leg.

- W13. Extraction of the Stone of Madness. Date: undated in the source read (no date given on the Commons/Suermondt-Ludwig list). Medium: oil (presumed; not stated in the source read). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Suermondt-Ludwig-Museum, Aachen - RKDimages 283135. The "stone of madness" operation - a quack surgeon cutting an imaginary stone from a patient's head, a Bosch-derived fool's-errand subject. It belongs with the dentist pictures (W3, W8) as a third medical-quack motif: surgery as street theatre, pain as comedy. Its presence in Aachen shows Both's small oeuvre scattered across German collections as well as Dutch ones. Placed in this phase only because the subject belongs with the Rome-period quack pictures; the date is not established.

- W18. Flea Hunting at Candlelight. Date: undated in the source read. Medium: not stated. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: not stated in the source read (Google Arts and Culture "related works"). A candlelit interior in which figures hunt fleas - a low-life night scene pairing the artificial-light device of W6 with the intimate, slightly disgusting domestic comedy of the Five Senses. Title-only evidence (Google Arts and Culture related-works list); the picture needs a holding-collection check. If authentic, it is the strongest link between the candlelight device and the peasant-interior subject.

- W20. A Rocky Landscape with Figures, Sunset. Date: undated in the source read. Medium: not stated. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: not stated in the source read (Google Arts and Culture "related works"). A landscape at sunset with small figures - the one catalogue entry in which landscape, not the figure group, leads. It matters because it is the point of contact with his brother Jan: Andries "embellished [Jan's] landscapes with figures and cattle" (old National Gallery catalogue), and a sunset rocky landscape with figures is exactly the joint-work territory. Title-only evidence; attribution (Andries alone vs. the brothers jointly) is unresolved in the sources read.

### Phase 3 - Late work and posthumous afterlife, c. 1640-1650

The last datable designs are the Five Senses (W7-W11): five peasant allegories - pancake-gluttony, tooth-pulling, spectacle-selling, bad singing, nappy-changing - drawn by Andries before 1641 and etched by Jan in 1642-1650, after Andries drowned. The move from painting to print is also a move from unique cabinet object to repeatable popular image, with Dutch comic verse captions doing the joke out loud. The theatre-character drawing (W14, Nijkodemus and Muyselaer) belongs here in spirit if not in date: named stock characters, gesture isolated, performance as the subject. The through-line of the whole career is visible in the Senses: the 1634 motifs (dentist W3 to Touch W8; beggars' concert W2 to Sound W10) return eight years later, distilled into types. Both did not develop away from his early subjects; he refined them into emblems.

- W7. The Five Senses: Taste. Date: designs by Andries Both, pre-1641 (he died March 1642); etched by Jan Both 1642-1650. Medium: etching. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (no dimensions on the Te Papa pages read). Location: Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa - 2015-0056-13 (purchased 2015). A woman cooks pancakes for eager peasants; a girl cries as a greedier child guzzles her pancake. Comic verse caption in Dutch. The design is Andries's - Te Papa is explicit that the originals inspiring the etchings are Andries's paintings, dating from 1641 or earlier - but the etchings were made by Jan after his brother's death, so the handling is Jan's. Taste is the gentlest in tone of the set only by comparison: the joke is gluttony, staged as a scuffle over food.

- W8. The Five Senses: Touch (The Dentist). Date: designs pre-1641; etched by Jan Both 1642-1650. Medium: etching. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Te Papa - 2015-0056-16. A dentist wrenches a patient's tooth with pliers while the victim cries for Doctor Lubbert; the verse is a scream in Dutch. Directly reworks W3 (The Tooth-Drawer, 1634) - the same gag, now as a print for a popular market, with the pain turned up and a crowd added. The arc from painted cabinet piece (1634) to etched allegory (post-1642) is Both's whole career in miniature: a single low-life motif, refined from observation into a repeatable comic type.

- W9. The Five Senses: Sight (The Spectacles Seller). Date: designs pre-1641; etched by Jan Both 1642-1650. Medium: etching. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Te Papa - 2015-0056-14. John Slodder the peddler tries to sell crystal glasses to "Grandma Lumpy-Furs"; at far right a young man in a wide-brimmed hat looks up through a telescope - possibly spotting the print's Dutch title inked into the sky. The gentlest print in the set (Te Papa's words), but the joke is still vanity and ugliness. Sight is the most self-aware of the five: a picture about looking, with a telescope and a mirror implied, that also hides its own title in the sky.

- W10. The Five Senses: Sound. Date: designs pre-1641; etched by Jan Both 1642-1650. Medium: etching. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Te Papa - 2015-0056-15. "Yawning Joachim and his friend" sing from a sheet of music while a curious crowd gathers. The singing is bad - the verse stresses yawning and the crowd's curiosity rather than harmony. Sound pairs with W2 (Concert of Beggars, 1634): music as a low-life social act, performed badly, watched by a ring of onlookers. The motif survives eight years and a change of medium.

- W11. The Five Senses: Smell. Date: designs pre-1641; etched by Jan Both 1642-1650. Medium: etching. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Te Papa - 2015-0056-12. The most scatological print in the set: a woman changes a toddler's nappy while another child sits on the potty in the corner and bystanders avert their noses; the verse complains that children "shit in the pots that we use for cooking and eating". Smell is the set's lowest register and its proof that Both's comedy was aimed at the peasants' expense - Te Papa's curator frames the whole series as exploiting "the image of peasants as comically earthy folk, driven by creatural appetites".

- W14. Two characters from popular theatre: 'Nijkodemus' and 'Muyselaer the piper'. Date: undated; style placed before the Italian journey (Sotheby's compares it to the metalpoint group, c. 1632). Medium: pen and brown ink and wash on paper, pasted to an album page. Dimensions: 150 x 189 mm (REPORTED - Sotheby's lot page, read via search result; the lot page itself was not opened end to end). Location: private collection (sale history: Amsterdam Sotheby's 15 Nov 1995 lot 28 as "Attributed to Andries Both"; New York Sotheby's 26 Jan 2011 lot 617; London Sotheby's 5 July 2016 lot 264). Two stock characters from popular theatre, each inscribed with his name - Nijkodemus and Muyselaer the piper. Sotheby's notes Both's figure drawings "normally contain at least some sort of reference to a setting", and this sheet is unusual in isolating the figures. It is the clearest evidence that Both drew from live popular performance, not just from paintings: his peasants are actors with names, caught mid-gesture. The Italian inscription on the mount wrongly attributes the sheet to Callot - a misattribution recorded on the object itself.

- W19. The Strolling Ballad Players. Date: undated in the source read. Medium: not stated. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: not stated in the source read (Google Arts and Culture "related works"). Itinerant ballad singers performing - a street-music subject that pairs with W2 (Concert of Beggars) and W10 (Sound): music made by the poor, in public, for small change. Title-only evidence; needs a holding-collection check. The recurrence of musician subjects across painting, drawing and print (W2, W10, W19) makes music Both's most repeated motif after dentistry.

## Evolution

What he kept from Utrecht: the Netherlandish peasant tradition itself - the observed body, the tavern interior, the quack operation, the theatre character. Gerson's sentence is the key: Andries "added a stylistic element of his own to the painting of Bambocciate", bringing his caricature style "from the land of Adriaen Brouwer" rather than taking it from van Laer in Rome.

What Rome changed: the stage, not the sensibility. The early-1630s training gives him drawing and the figure; Rome gives him the well, the street, the sunset road, and the Bamboccianti company - van Laer, Miel, Cerquozzi - who regarded him as one of their most important. The turn is gradual: the first Rome-year pictures are still dark interiors in the Dutch manner, and only Travellers by a Well moves the action outdoors for good.

What changed, in one paragraph: from small dark interiors of tavern brawlers painted in Utrecht (1632-34), to the same interiors painted in Rome with a village beyond (1635), to open-air travellers at a Roman well and candlelit night pieces (1635-41), to five peasant allegories drawn before his death and etched by his brother after it (1642-50) - the same motifs distilled from observation into repeatable comic types. The scale never changes, the bodies never straighten, and the humour never softens.

What stayed constant: small scale, thickset comic bodies, harsh humour, and the Brouwer/Bruegel lineage underneath the Roman sun. From the 1632 metalpoint peasant to the posthumous Smell print, the same three things do the work: one tight knot of figures, one bodily act, and one joke at the peasants' expense.

## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

- D1. JAN vs ANDRIES - the standing trap. The brothers collaborated in Rome and their hands were historically confused. The old National Gallery catalogue (Wornum, 1863 edition) states flatly that "Andries embellished his landscapes with figures and cattle, which his brother had little skill in painting" - and Bryan's Dictionary says of Jan's principal works that "his brother Andries assisted in the greater part; they are nearly invariably signed J. Both - the J and the B interlaced." A signature reading "J. Both" therefore proves nothing about whose hand painted the figures. Prado, Fishermen and Shepherds (Jan Both, P002066): figures attributed to Jan Miel in early catalogues; Burke (1976) believes them to be by Andries Both; the Prado notes the bare-chested fisherman's handling is "very similar" to figures elsewhere attributed to Both by Salerno. The attribution of the figures is UNRESOLVED across three candidates (Jan Miel / Andries Both / Jan Both himself). The #533 study file's Disputes D1 and Network sections state "Sandrart says the brothers probably collaborated with Andries painting the figures in Jan's landscapes" - that sentence is NOT repeated here, because no Sandrart passage saying it was found (see D2 and C459).
- D2. THE SANDRART CLAIM - NOT VERIFIED, probably a misattribution. Three searches (leg 1 S5, leg 3 S3 and S6, parent S1) found no source attributing the figures claim to Sandrart. Sandrart's Teutsche Academie (1675) does carry a Jan Both life, and Sandrart is a warm contemporary witness for Pieter van Laer (Gerson 2.4), but no passage found in this leg has Sandrart saying Andries painted Jan's figures. The nearest real claim is Bryan's Dictionary (19th century), "his brother Andries assisted in the greater part". Modern scholarship points the other way: Sotheby's on Jan Both (lot 33): "The figures are painted by Both himself" in the work discussed, and Jan "sometimes collaborated with specialist figure painters" - the named one being Cornelis van Poelenburgh (two female bathers in a secondary version). Sotheby's (lot 13): "Some of the staffage in Both's paintings have been attributed to Van Laer, Poelenbergh or Berchem, but modern commentators agree that here the figures are by Both himself." The Warburg Institute record for the National Gallery Judgement of Paris (NG209) likewise credits its figures to Poelenburgh, not Andries. Rule for the study: where a source says "figures by X", name X; where a catalogue says "attributed to", keep the qualifier; never silently promote an attribution, and never attribute the claim to Sandrart without reading Sandrart.net's Jan Both life directly. See C459.
- D3. BIRTH YEAR: 1612/13 (Wikipedia; Sotheby's) vs 1611/2 (Gerson Digital 2.4; Te Papa blog "1611/2 - 1642") vs c. 1612 (WGA). No baptism record is cited in any source read. The study uses "1612/13" as its working dates, the Wikipedia and Sotheby's convention, with the spread stated openly wherever it matters. See C463.
- D4. DEATH DATE: 23 March 1642 (Wikipedia only for the day-month) vs 1642 (Sotheby's, Gerson, Te Papa - year only) vs 1641 (WGA "c.1612-41" - treat as an error or loose rounding) vs 1650 (Wornum 1863, 1870, 1872 editions) vs 1660 (1875 edition) - the 19th-century catalogue dates are both impossible on the modern chronology. Christie's lot header gives "vers 1608 - vers 1641" (birth c. 1608 is four years too early; death "c. 1641" is a year early). Do not trust auction-house header dates. The modern consensus is 1642, age 29 or 30. See C463.
- D5. WHICH BROTHER IS ELDER - REVERSED IN OLD SOURCES. The 19th-century National Gallery catalogues call Jan the elder brother ("born about 1610") and Andries the younger; modern scholarship has it the other way (Andries b. 1612/13, Jan b. c. 1618/22). The old catalogues also say Andries "embellished [Jan's] landscapes with figures" - the joint-work model - which modern sources retain, but the age reversal corrupts every inference drawn from those catalogues about who taught whom.
- D6. ROME ARRIVAL: Waddingham supposed Andries was already in Rome in 1633; the earliest documentary evidence is a Dutch notarial act from early 1637 stating he was living in Via Margutta in 1635 (Hoogewerff 1952, via Gerson 2.4, note 47). Venice transit: uncertain; single handwritten note "Andrea Bot fe / Venetsia 1632" on a Leiden drawing. Not proven.
- D7. HUNTING BY CANDLELIGHT DATED 1630 (W6). Both would have been 17-18. No museum source confirms the date; treat as doubtful. If the picture is Roman-period (1635-41), the date is simply wrong.
- D8. THE POOR PAINTER DIMENSIONS (W12). 330 x 247 cm from a retailer (artsdot) is impossible for a Both cabinet picture; almost certainly 33.0 x 24.7 cm with a misplaced decimal. The whole entry rests on that one retailer; needs a Rijksmuseum object-page check.
- D9. BOORS CAROUSING (1634, Utrecht). Wikipedia lists it among Both's known paintings. The Art Fund/York Art Gallery record for a Boors Carousing (oil on oak, 22.2 x 17.1 cm, Lycett Green bequest 1955) attributes it to Adriaen Brouwer, not Both. Either there are two pictures with the same title, or Wikipedia's attribution is wrong. Unresolved; the picture is kept out of the W-catalogue.
- D10. JAN vs ANDRIES CONFUSION IN THE MARKET. Travellers by a Well (W5) was sold at Christie's in 1810 as "Jan Both (style of)"; a Sotheby's 2007 Jan Both landscape was sold at Christie's in 1941 as "Jan and Andries Both" and is now Jan alone; the National Gallery's NG71/NG209 are Jan, not Andries, despite the catalogue entry grouping the brothers. Any unsigned Italianate landscape with lively figures is liable to this confusion. Wikipedia's "List of painters in the National Gallery of Art" MISattributes Jan Both's An Italianate Evening Landscape (NGA 2000.91.1) to ANDRIES Both. The NGA itself catalogues it as Jan Both. Do not repeat the Wikipedia list's error. The Philadelphia Museum of Art, Landscape with Cattle, Italy (Cat. 614), is catalogued to ANDRIES Both, not Jan - do not fold it into Jan's oeuvre.
- D11. THE SPECTACLES SELLER DATED 1628; THE CONCERT DATED 1626. Both dates predate Both's career by years and come from retailer/AI-encyclopedia sources. Rejected as errors.
- D12. DEACCESSION IN THE OPEN: Travellers by a Well (W5) was bequeathed to the Art Gallery of South Australia in 1956, judged "unsuitable", and sold at auction in Adelaide in 1957 - a museum deaccession of a Both, documented on the NGV's own page. The same page notes the sale catalogue is so vague the picture "could be Lot 1, Dutch Oil; Lot 7, Small oil on Wood, or; Lot 76, Dutch School".
- D13. THE CALLOT MISATTRIBUTION ON THE OBJECT ITSELF: the Nijkodemus/Muyselaer sheet (W14) bears a long Italian inscription attributing it to Jacques Callot, with a biography of Callot - a contemporary or near-contemporary misattribution written on the mount.
- D14. FIVE SENSES AUTHORSHIP: the etchings are Jan Both's work after Andries's designs (Te Papa, SuperStock/Rijksmuseum records). They are routinely listed under either brother or both; catalogue them as "Jan after Andries", never as Andries's etchings. The painted originals behind the etchings are lost or unlocated in the sources read.
- D15. NO NATIONAL GALLERY (LONDON) ANDRIES BOTH. A search for Both at the National Gallery, London returns only Jan Both (NG71, NG209) and Andries Vermeulen. The old catalogues' "BOTH" entries are Jan's pictures. Do not claim a London National Gallery Both.
- D16. W17 MAY DUPLICATE W3; W18-W20 ARE TITLE-ONLY. W17 (A Toothdrawer Operating on a Man...) may be a version or variant of W3 rather than a separate composition; do not count it as securely independent. W18 (Flea Hunting at Candlelight), W19 (The Strolling Ballad Players) and W20 (A Rocky Landscape with Figures, Sunset) rest on Google Arts and Culture related-works listings only; their holding collections are not established and their attributions are unresolved.
- D17. THE JAN STEEN PUPIL CLAIM - A TRAP. One unreliable source (A Design Award encyclopedia pages) calls Jan Steen Andries's pupil and credits Andries with The Procuress, The Concert and The Merry Company - those are not Andries's works and the claim is not repeated by any scholarly source read. No source read in this leg records a pupil of Andries Both. Recorded here as a trap, NOT as a fact.
- D18. HOUBRAKEN'S "ALL FIVE FOUND THEIR END IN WATER". Houbraken's van Laer life says Andries was present when Bamboccio threw a papist into the water and that "all five of them found their end in water" - colourful Houbraken, not a documented fact about Andries's death, which the sources agree was drowning in a Venetian canal on the way home from festivities in 1642.

## Aesthetic logic

Andries Both's pictures are built on a single tension: the Dutch interior against the Roman street. Everything he paints is a negotiation between the two. The interior is Dutch - observed, close, low, a tavern you could drink in, a tooth you could have pulled, a peasant you could meet on the road. The street is Roman - sunlit, open, a well in the Campagna, a village beyond the inn door. The joke is what joins them: one tight knot of thickset bodies, one bodily act, one laugh at the peasants' expense, staged in a small cabinet picture that a collector could hold at arm's length and enjoy slumming in. That is the whole system. The knot is the subject, the act is the verb, the verse underneath is the voice, and the village beyond is the straight man that makes the foreground look big. Where Jan paints golden light, Andries paints the appetite; where Hendrick Bloemaert paints a face emerging from the dark, Andries paints a body emerging from the crowd. The picture does not illustrate a story. It is a moment you lean into, at close range, with one small hot pool of light and one loud body doing one crude thing in it.

## Positions

P1. Not a mere follower of van Laer. Both brought his caricature style from the north (Brouwer/Venice) and added his own element to the Bambocciate; Gerson explicitly refuses to number him among van Laer's successors. Source: Gerson Digital 2.4.
P2. Humour and harshness are one mechanism, not two moods. Wikipedia's summary - "humorous and outrageous quality, mixed with objectivity and harsh reality" - describes a single stance: the joke is the observation, and the observation is unsparing. Source: Wikipedia.
P3. He was the figure hand of the Both workshop. The Prado X-ray evidence (figures added to Jan's landscapes, executed more densely) plus Burke's attribution of the Prado figures to Andries make the collaboration concrete: Jan built the landscape, somebody else put the people in, and Andries is the leading candidate for that hand. Source: Museo del Prado P002066. (Disputed - see D1/D2.)
P4. Smallness is the point. Cabinet scale is not a limitation of his talent but the format of his market and his joke: low life for close looking, sold to collectors who enjoyed slumming at arm's length. Source: Wikipedia (small cabinet paintings); Gerson (aristocratic collectors "by no means looked down on the Bambocciate").
P5. His types are caricatures, not portraits. The figures are stock types pushed past likeness into emblem - the glutton, the hag, the tooth-puller - which is why they survive translation into print and into Jan's etchings without losing force. Source: Gerson Digital 2.4; Te Papa blog.
P6. He was a draftsman first, a painter second (in survival, if not in intent). Five known paintings against a varied surviving drawing oeuvre (landscapes, biblical scenes, diableries, metalpoint studies) suggests the drawing hand is where his range is visible. Source: Wikipedia list of known paintings; Sotheby's lot 261.
P7. His posthumous career was as a designer for prints. Jan etched the Five Senses after Andries's designs in 1642-1650, after Andries's death; Andries's afterlife is largely a print afterlife. Source: Te Papa blog.
P8. The laughter is at the peasants' expense. The Five Senses prints "get their laughs very much at the peasants' expense" and "exploit the image of peasants as comically earthy folk, driven by creatural appetites and wallowing in the muck of their own ignorance". This is a class joke told by and for people who are not peasants. Source: Te Papa blog.
P9. His Utrecht roots stay visible in the early work. The pre-1633 inn scene (Sotheby's lot 308) is judged by its village perspective and figure colouring to recall his fellow-Utrechter Joost Cornelis Droochsloot - the Dutch village painter is still in his hand before Rome remakes him. Source: Sotheby's lot 308.
P10. The style was formed before Rome. The 1636 Amsterdam drawing already shows Bamboccianti assimilation, and Gerson argues the style was developed in Venice and brought from Brouwer's land - Rome did not invent him, it gave him a subject. Source: Gerson Digital 2.4.
P11. The Bambocciata name is a slur turned into a creed. "Bamboccio" (large child, wretch, ugly doll) was a mocking nickname for the deformed van Laer; the genre was condemned by the Academy (Passeri, Baldinucci) yet became "something of an artistic creed" and was collected by aristocrats. Both worked inside a genre that was simultaneously despised and desired. Source: Gerson Digital 2.4; Wikipedia.
P12. The brothers are one collaborative system split across two studies. Jan painted golden light, Andries painted the street; they lived together on the Via Vittoria, left Rome together, and their hands are entangled in each other's pictures. WGA: "they are said to have collaborated, but Andries is best known for paintings and drawings of lively peasant scenes that have little in common with Jan's idyllic tone." Source: WGA Jan Both biography; Wikipedia.
