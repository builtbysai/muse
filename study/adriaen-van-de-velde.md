# Adriaen van de Velde: Study Notes

**Site:** Adriaen van de Velde (bapt. 30 November 1636 - bur. 21 January 1672), Dutch Golden Age painter of Amsterdam, the cattle painter the copycat son copied: the son of the marine painter Willem van de Velde the Elder who refused the sea, trained (on the standard account, disputed by the 2016 catalogue - see the Disputes section) in Jan Wynants's Haarlem workshop beside Philips Wouwerman and Paulus Potter, and became the most hired hand in Dutch landscape - the staffage painter who put the cattle, the herdsmen and the dogs into the pictures of Ruisdael, Hobbema, Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron, while painting about two hundred of his own small, tranquil pastorals, a celebrated group of etchings and a large body of red-chalk life studies, and dying at 35 in debt, his possessions auctioned for less than he owed. He was baptised in the Oude Kerk in Amsterdam on 30 November 1636, the son of Willem van de Velde the Elder and Judith Adriaens van Leeuwen; his elder brother Willem van de Velde the Younger was also a marine painter, and his sister Magdalena was born in 1632. He first studied with his father, but not wishing to follow the family tradition of marine painting he moved to Haarlem to continue his study with the landscape painter Jan Wynants (also spelled Wijnants), where he met Wouwerman and Potter. By 1657 he had settled in Amsterdam, where on 5 April 1657 he married Maria Pieters Oudekerck (or Ouderkerk), a Catholic; he converted to Catholicism, and all five of his children were baptised in clandestine Catholic churches around Amsterdam. His earliest known works are six etchings of 1653, made when he was sixteen, and dated paintings survive for every year from 1654 to 1671. His painted oeuvre is nearly two hundred catalogued paintings, or "more than 170 oil paintings and a large number of drawings" - the count is unsettled between the sources read - and his etchings number about twenty to twenty-eight depending on the count (Te Papa says 24, Sphinx Fine Art says 28, Wikipedia says "twenty-odd", the 1911 Britannica says "about twenty"). He never travelled to Italy, as far as is known; the National Gallery page is the one dissenting voice, saying "In his youth he probably visited Italy". His pupils were Johannes Innevelt, Johannes van der Bent, Dirck van Bergen and Jacob Coning. Despite success he was in dire straits throughout his life: his wife had to run a linen business to help make ends meet, and when he died his possessions and works were auctioned off but the proceeds were insufficient to cover his debts. He died in Amsterdam in January 1672, aged only 35, and was buried in the Nieuwe Kerk on 21 January 1672; the cause of death is not recorded. Later that same year the Franco-Dutch War broke out, driving his father and brother to London. His reputation was mostly posthumous: he was one of the most sought-after Dutch names among collectors in Germany, France and England in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, dipped in the early twentieth century, and was revived by the first retrospective, "Adriaen van de Velde: Dutch Master of Landscape", Rijksmuseum and Dulwich Picture Gallery, 2016, with 60 works. No words by van de Velde himself - no letters, contracts or statements - were found in any source read; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Adriaen van de Velde was baptised in the Oude Kerk in Amsterdam on 30 November 1636, the son of the marine painter Willem van de Velde the Elder and Judith Adriaens van Leeuwen; his elder brother Willem van de Velde the Younger was also a marine painter. He was, in other words, born into the most famous sea-painting family in Holland and then refused the sea. He first studied with his father, but not wishing to follow the family tradition of marine painting he moved to Haarlem to continue his study in the workshop of the landscape painter Jan Wynants (also spelled Wijnants). In Haarlem he met Philips Wouwerman and Paulus Potter, both animal painters who had an important influence on his work.

The earliest biographer, Arnold Houbraken, writing from the testimony of van de Velde's own daughter (then the wife of the Amsterdam broker Sodijn), tells the story differently in flavour but not in outline: the boy painted a dairy farmer on the board of his box bed while still in nursery school, was placed with Jan Wijnants because he would not paint ships, and when Wijnants was shown what the boy had drawn on his own, Wijnants's wife said, tapping her husband on the shoulder, "Wijnants your master is born". Houbraken gives the birth year as 1639 and the age at death as 33; both are wrong against the baptism record of 1636 and the burial record of 21 January 1672, aged 35. The 1911 Britannica repeats Houbraken's 1639 date, and artdaily repeats another error, giving Willem the Younger as "(1663-1707)" when he was born in 1633. These old errors still circulate; the 1636 baptism date is the documented one.

By 1657 Adriaen had settled in Amsterdam, where documents record his presence regularly until his death. On 5 April 1657 he married Maria Pieters Oudekerck (or Ouderkerk), a Catholic; he converted to Catholicism, and all five of his children were baptised in clandestine Catholic churches around Amsterdam. The conversion matters to the work: his few religious pictures, notably The Annunciation of 1667 and a Descent from the Cross for a hidden Catholic church, belong to his married, Catholic years.

His earliest known works are six etchings of 1653, made when he was sixteen or seventeen, and dated paintings survive for every year from 1654 to 1671. Pastures with cattle and herders predominate in his work of 1653-58. The paintings and prints of these years show no trace of Wijnants's influence; instead the young van de Velde emulated Paulus Potter and, to a lesser extent, Karel Dujardin. Potter's example shaped his choice of the cattle-piece, his meadow landscape, his tight precise technique for grass, foliage and fur, and the hard, cool sunlight of the mid-1650s. Around 1658 warmer hues and a softer, yellower sunlight entered his pictures, a response to the Italianate painters Dujardin, Nicolaes Berchem and Jan Asselijn, and he began to take on a broader range of subjects: farmyards, forests, beaches, dunes, winter scenes, portraits in landscape settings, at least one genre piece (Woman Drinking, 1662) and a few historical pictures.

He never travelled to Italy, as far as is known. The National Gallery page is the one dissenting voice, saying "In his youth he probably visited Italy"; every other source read says he almost certainly never left Holland and that the mountains and Italianate character of his landscapes sprang from imagination and from fellow artists' work. The mountainous scenery in a flat Dutch landscape is therefore an invented, borrowed Italy.

His great practical gift was figures and animals, and other painters hired it. He was frequently employed to add staffage to pictures by fellow landscape artists, including Jacob van Ruisdael, Meindert Hobbema, his master Wynants, Adriaen Hendriksz Verboom, Philips Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron. As early as 1664 an inventory lists paintings by Wijnants and Moucheron "gestofferd door Adriaen van de Velde", and his contribution demonstrably increased their market value. Houbraken says he died while in collaboration with Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron, painting animals on their pictures. The 1911 Britannica adds that he was soon employed by his master to introduce figures into his landscapes, and rendered the same service to Hobbema, Ruisdael and Verboom. Hofstede de Groot adds a caution: Jan van der Heyden was himself a clever figure-draughtsman and could add figures in van de Velde's manner to his own pictures, before and certainly after Adriaen's death, so not every van de Velde-like figure in a Heyden is Adriaen's.

He worked productively through the 1660s, most productive in the early to mid 1660s, when he completed a series of landscapes featuring portraits of cattle. His largest pictures date from the late 1660s: The Annunciation (1667, 128 x 176 cm) and Portrait of a Family in a Landscape (1667, 148 x 178 cm), both in the Rijksmuseum. The family portrait was praised through the nineteenth century as his masterpiece and assumed to be a self-portrait; X-ray studies show it was cut down considerably, and infra-red studies show the figures were overpainted on a completed landscape rather than painted into reserves.

Despite success he was in dire straits throughout his life. His wife had to run a linen business to help make ends meet, and when he died his possessions and works were auctioned off but the proceeds were insufficient to cover his debts. Documents survive about loans that needed to be repaid in the 1660s. He died in Amsterdam in January 1672, aged only 35, and was buried in the Nieuwe Kerk on 21 January 1672. The cause of death is not recorded. Later that same year the Franco-Dutch War broke out, driving his father and brother to London, where they finished their careers in the service of Charles II.

His pupils were Johannes Innevelt, Johannes van der Bent, Dirck van Bergen and Jacob Coning. Hofstede de Groot, following Houbraken, names Dirck van Bergen (about 1640-90) as the real pupil, whose works are so close to his master's later period from about 1665 onward as to be very often confused with them, though less neat in drawing, less careful in detail and less skilled in aerial perspective; Johannes Innevelt is known only from documents, by no picture; the Dordrecht painters Pieter and Gabriel van der Leeuw may be noticed with van Bergen. Houbraken's own account of van Bergen includes a separate anecdote - friends collecting coins in the inns van Bergen frequented to pay for his burial - which belongs to van Bergen, not to van de Velde.

His etchings number about twenty to twenty-eight depending on the count: Te Papa says 24, Sphinx Fine Art says 28, Wikipedia says "twenty-odd", the 1911 Britannica says "about twenty". Activity concentrated in 1653, 1657-59 and 1670. Most depict farm animals; the finest show strong light and deep shadow on the solid flanks of grazing and resting cows. They were highly prized by collectors in his own time.

His painted oeuvre is nearly two hundred catalogued paintings, or "more than 170 oil paintings and a large number of drawings". He was one of the most sought-after names among collectors in Germany, France and England in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. His reputation dipped in the early twentieth century and was revived by scholarship and by the first retrospective, "Adriaen van de Velde: Dutch Master of Landscape", Rijksmuseum and Dulwich Picture Gallery, 2016, with 60 works. Eugene Boudin, the pre-Impressionist beach painter and influence on Monet, was particularly keen on his beach scenes and copied at least one of them. The Stechow comparison of his work to Mozart's chamber music (delicate, carefully composed, mastery of lighting and the figure) was found only in a search snippet and was NOT verified against a page read end to end; treat as unverified.

His drawings are widely considered a high point of seventeenth-century Dutch draughtsmanship (the exact wording was found only in a search snippet and was NOT verified end to end). The 2016 exhibition reunited paintings with their preparatory studies for the first time, many in red chalk, including the four studies for The Hut (1671).

**Takeaway:** the sea-painter's son who refused the sea, drew his cattle from life, sold his hand inside other men's landscapes, and died at 35 in debt while his pictures were already being collected across Europe.


## M1. The three-stage preparatory system

Every painting was built in three documented drawing stages - (1) a rapid preliminary compositional sketch jotting down the broad outlines; (2) a second, fully resolved and detailed drawing of the whole composition, which was not transferred directly to canvas or panel but served as the model for the finished work; (3) separate chalk studies from life for the principal figures and animals. In addition he kept a reusable stock of life studies: the same nude model, the same seated woman, the same resting cow recur in different compositions. Watchable behaviour: a painting that reads as a casting call - each figure and animal individually posed and finished, then placed; the same cow reappearing in a new meadow; a clothed Virgin who is visibly a nude studio model dressed after the fact. Grounding: Sphinx Fine Art (the three types of study), Wikipedia (reuse of studies), HNA review (The Hut surrounded by four preparatory sketches; nude study reused as the Virgin in The Annunciation), artdaily (rough pen-and-ink sketch to separate red-chalk life studies).

**Takeaway:** the painting is assembled from a casting call of life studies, not invented on the canvas.

## M2. Staffage as a hired hand - the subordinated figure

Van de Velde sold his figure-and-animal skill as a service inside other painters' landscapes. He added the staffage to pictures by Ruisdael, Hobbema, Wynants, Verboom, Philips Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron, and a 1664 inventory already records Wijnants and Moucheron pictures "gestofferd door Adriaen van de Velde", with his contribution raising their market value. His rule, per Hofstede de Groot, was to subordinate his figures to the landscape he was entering. Watchable behaviour: small, exquisitely finished figures that animate a big landscape without ever owning it; a herdsman, a milkmaid, a dog that give the eye a scale and a story while the land stays the subject; in a Ruisdael the figures feel like they belong to a different, finer hand than the trees. Grounding: Wikipedia and Sphinx (collaborator lists; 1664 inventory; value increase), Hofstede de Groot (subordination as his greatest gift; Van der Heyden's own ability to imitate him as a caution). NOTE: this is the same mechanism as M8 and M11 - see C489.

**Takeaway:** the finest hand in the picture may belong to the man who did not compose it.

## M3. The cattle portrait - the animal as subject

Cattle drawn from life in the fields, then painted as individual portraits: solid flanks modelled with strong light against deep shadow, grazing and resting poses, horns set against the sky, coats rendered hair by hair with a tight, precise touch. In the etchings the same mechanism is reduced to the needle: forceful draughtsmanship, light and shadow on the flank doing all the modelling. Watchable behaviour: a cow that holds the centre of the picture the way a sitter holds a portrait; a resting cow whose flank catches the sun while its belly falls into shadow; a bull whose horns are silhouetted against open sky so the animal reads as a shape before it reads as a beast. Grounding: Sphinx (tight precise technique for fur; etchings, light and shadow on solid flanks), Eclectic Light (horns against the sky; "portraits of cattle"), Te Papa (bull in profile, imperturbable; sketching cattle in the fields).

**Takeaway:** the cow is the sitter and the meadow is the studio.

## M4. The light shift of 1658 - cool sun to warm sun

Before about 1658 his pictures use the hard, cool sunlight of Paulus Potter - tight, precise, silvery. From 1658, under the Italianate painters Dujardin, Berchem and Asselijn, warmer hues and a softer, yellower sunlight enter, with pronounced local colour: vivid clothing against rich brown sand and grey sea in The Beach at Scheveningen, deepened shadows, a Mediterranean fall of light laid over Dutch subjects. Watchable behaviour: two van de Veldes side by side - one cold, crisp and silvery, one golden and warm; the same meadow rendered first in cool morning light and later in a soft southern afternoon; the beach where the light, not the sea, is the event. Grounding: Sphinx (Potter's hard cool sunlight; warmer hues about 1658; Beach at Scheveningen luminosity and local colour), Eclectic Light (The Beach at Scheveningen, light and space), 1911 Britannica (clear silvery colouring). FLAG: the warm Italianate light is documented as adapted from Dujardin, Berchem and Asselijn; van de Velde's contribution is the refinement and the application to Dutch subjects, not the light itself. The early tight precise technique and hard cool sunlight are documented as Potter's manner which he emulated.

**Takeaway:** one career, two suns - the cold Potter sun, then the warm borrowed Italian sun.

## M5. The fugitive-pigment colour shift - the picture that changed itself

A chemical flaw in his materials. Eclectic Light reports a fugitive yellow, probably a lake, in his greens, which has faded so that most of his greens have become blue, most apparent from 1662 on. The HNA review of the 2016 catalogue reports the same phenomenon the other way round: blue pigments faded into yellow, now treated as a signature trait that can confirm attribution. Watchable behaviour: greens that read blue in the late pictures - the Vertumnus and Pomona of 1670 is the cited casualty; a landscape whose foliage is colder than the artist intended; a discoloration so consistent it has become a fingerprint for attribution. Grounding: Eclectic Light (Q9), HNA review (Q15). FLAG: the two sources contradict each other on the direction of the shift (yellow fading out of green leaves blue, versus blue fading to yellow). This study does not resolve it; the catalogue (Cornelis and Schapelhouman) is the authority neither leg read end to end. The same mechanism is described again as M10.

**Takeaway:** the picture kept painting itself after he died - and two authorities disagree about which way it went.

## M6. The beach as social stage

In the Scheveningen beach scenes (1658-1660) the beach is staged as a place where all ranks meet: well-dressed couples on a day trip, children playing in a tidal puddle, fishermen with rolled trousers and nets, a rider galloping along the dunes, a covered wagon on the tide line, and at the far right a lone man seen from the back gazing out to sea. Low horizon, high viewpoint, vast distance ending in low misty hills. Watchable behaviour: a wide, bright, windy sand stage with many small incidents instead of one big one; the eye travelling from group to group; the back-turned gazer who points the viewer's attention out to the invisible sea. Grounding: Eclectic Light (The Beach at Scheveningen; the man gazing out to sea in HNA), National Gallery (low horizon and high viewpoint; vast distance), theartsdesk (beach as playground and workplace for all strata; the man with a peg leg, the sacks, the steel grey sea). FLAG: the claim that these are the FIRST Dutch pictures of seaside leisure comes only from a search snippet (Royal Collection Trust) and was NOT verified against a page read end to end; do not state it as fact.

**Takeaway:** the beach is a stage where the whole town meets, and the sea itself is barely in the picture.

## M7. Three-stage preparatory drawing system, with reuse by counterproof

Van de Velde built a painting in three drawing stages (Sphinx). First a preliminary compositional sketch jotting down the broad outlines of the design. From that first idea he produced a second, fully resolved and detailed drawing of the whole composition, which served as the model for the finished work although it was not transferred directly to the canvas or panel. In addition he made chalk studies from life for the principal figures and animals. His surviving drawings include landscapes sketched from nature, compositional ideas for paintings and prints, cattle drawn from life, and nudes and other figures executed from studio models. Watchable behaviour: the same figure or animal reappears across different paintings, sometimes reversed, because studies were kept in the workshop and reused. The many nude studies in red or black chalk are highlights of his oeuvre and he reused these studies in different compositions. A nude study of a seated woman in Oxford served as the model for the clothed Virgin in The Annunciation. A counterproof of a lost study for a rider likely served as the inspiration for a similar figure, in reverse, at the centre of The Hut. The Hut itself was shown in 2016 with four preparatory drawings: a cursory sketch of the building, probably made in situ, plus red-chalk studies of individual figures and animals. Grounding: Sphinx Fine Art artist page, Wikipedia, Burlington review, HNA review. FLAG: none. This mechanism is documented for van de Velde himself. NOTE: this is the same mechanism as M1, extended with counterproof reuse - see C490.

**Takeaway:** the study is the painting, drawn twice, and the same cow earns its keep in picture after picture.

## M8. Staffage insertion: figures and animals dropped into another painter's landscape

Van de Velde was hired by fellow landscape painters to supply the living part of their pictures - the figures and animals - while the host painter supplied the landscape, buildings or seascape. Named collaborators: his master Wijnants, Meindert Hobbema, Jacob van Ruisdael, Adriaen Hendriksz Verboom, Philips Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron; also his elder brother Willem van de Velde the Younger on beach scenes. With Jan van der Heyden the division was explicit: van de Velde brought his skill with figures, van der Heyden his with buildings and the urban landscape. Watchable behaviour: a landscape that is otherwise a Wijnants dune view, a van der Heyden townscape or a Willem beach scene suddenly has small, precisely observed people and animals that carry the picture's life - a herdsman, a milkmaid, a dog, cattle at rest - painted with a different, tighter hand than the setting. The 1664 inventory listing paintings by Wijnants and Moucheron "gestofferd door Adriaen van de Velde" shows this was a recognised, priced service, and his contribution demonstrably increased market value. The Kunsthaus Zurich Dune Landscape with Herdsmen and Tavern (1660) is a documented case: Wijnants's landscape, with van de Velde's collaboration probably not limited to the staffage. Grounding: Wikipedia, Sphinx, National Gallery, Eclectic Light, Christie's lot essay. FLAG: none. This mechanism is documented for van de Velde himself. Note the reverse case exists too: other hands supplied landscapes for his figures in a few pictures, for example the Hofstede note that in one landscape with figures and cattle the cows, sheep and figures are by van de Velde but the landscape is probably by another unknown hand; that is reported, not verified. NOTE: this is the same mechanism as M2 and M11 - see C489.

**Takeaway:** the living part of the picture is a hired hand, and the hire raised the price.

## M9. Warm Italianate light transplanted onto Dutch subjects, without travel

From about 1658 van de Velde replaced the hard, cool, silvery Dutch sunlight of his early Potter-influenced work with a warmer, softer, yellower sunlight learned from the Italianate painters Dujardin, Berchem and Jan Asselijn, and applied it to Dutch subjects - meadows, dunes, beaches, farmyards - as well as to invented Italianate views. The National Gallery describes the result as a soft Mediterranean light, the sun still warm although night is falling, over distant hills not likely to be seen in a flat Dutch landscape, with idealised peasants walking at a leisurely pace. The hills themselves are entirely imaginary. Watchable behaviour: the same Dutch cows and herdsmen, but the light is southern: long warm shadows, figures' vivid colours caught against rich brown sand and grey sea, a sun that picks out one vignette after another as clouds shift, a twilight sky that does the emotional work. The viewer is asked to believe in a Mediterranean afternoon in a flat Dutch field, and the light, not the topography, sells it. Grounding: Sphinx, Wikipedia, National Gallery object pages, Christie's essay. FLAG: none for the mechanism itself. The supporting claim that he never went to Italy is negative evidence (no trip documented) and the Roman church drawing is probably after a print by another artist, so the "without travel" part is the scholarly consensus, not a proven fact.

**Takeaway:** the south is a light, not a place - laid over a Dutch field it still works.

## M10. Fugitive yellow: greens that turn blue (with a conflicting account flagged)

A technical fault in his materials. Eclectic Light says one technical problem plagues his paintings: his use of a fugitive yellow, probably a lake, in his greens, which has resulted in most of those greens becoming blue, most apparent in his paintings from 1662 onwards. The Burlington review agrees: the catalogue discusses the fading of fugitive yellow pigment, which has transformed many of his greens into blues. Watchable behaviour: in his later paintings the foliage and grass read blue where the painter laid down green. Eclectic Light says the problem is all too sadly evident in his otherwise superb Vertumnus and Pomona (1670). The effect is a diagnostic: the blue-shifted greens are now seen as a signature trait. Grounding: Eclectic Light, Burlington review. FLAG - CONFLICTING ACCOUNT: the HNA review describes the same conservation issue in the opposite direction, saying "the blue pigments used by Van de Velde have faded into yellow". That contradicts Eclectic Light and the Burlington review, which say yellow faded and greens became blue. This study reports both wordings and does NOT resolve the contradiction; the study writer must not assert a direction without checking the 2016 catalogue (Cornelis and Schapelhouman) itself. The mechanism is documented for van de Velde himself, but its direction is disputed between the sources read. The same mechanism is described as M5.

**Takeaway:** the greens turned blue - or the blues turned yellow - and the disagreement itself is now part of the attribution.

## M11. The staffage hand: hired figures and animals inside another man's picture

Adriaen was employed by other Amsterdam landscape painters to supply the figures and animals for their pictures. The landscape is theirs; the living things in it are his. His figures are large-scale, individual and carefully painted - more than anecdotal. Watchable behaviour: in a Ruisdael, Hobbema, Wynants, Koninck, van der Heyden or de Moucheron landscape, look for the cattle, herdsmen and travellers. They are painted with a different, more careful hand than the trees and sky, and they carry the picture's human interest. The animals are the most finished element in a picture someone else composed. Grounding: Wikipedia ("He collaborated with other artists in Amsterdam typically adding the staffage or animals to their paintings. These artists included his master Wynants, Meindert Hobbema, Jacob Isaakszoon van Ruisdael, Adriaen Hendriksz Verboom, Philips Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron."); National Gallery ("He was employed by other Amsterdam artists to supply figures for their paintings."); Houbraken via search: he died while in collaboration with Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron, painting animals on their paintings. NOTE: this is the same mechanism as M2 and M8 - see C489.

**Takeaway:** the hand you admire in a Ruisdael may be van de Velde's, and the landscape may be the only part Ruisdael painted.

## M12. The field-study library: draw the animal outside, reuse it inside

Adriaen prepared paintings meticulously from a working library of drawings made from life - landscape sketches, cattle in meadows, life figure studies, and nude studies in red or black chalk - and reused the same studies in different compositions. Watchable behaviour: the same cow pose, the same herdsman, the same nude figure turns up in different pictures, re-posed against a different landscape. The painting is assembled from field notes, not invented on the canvas. Grounding: Wikipedia ("He prepared his paintings meticulously by making preparatory drawings, many of which have been preserved. These drawings include sketches of landscapes, cattle in meadows and life figure studies. The many nude studies executed in red or black chalk are highlights of his oeuvre. He reused these studies in different compositions."); Houbraken via search: he daily carried his equipment outside into the field to draw cows, oxen, sheep and landscapes, and kept doing it once a week to the end of his life. The 2016 Rijksmuseum/Dulwich show paired The Hut (1671) with four of its preparatory drawings.

**Takeaway:** the field is the studio and the sketchbook is the stockroom.

## M13. The etched cattle piece: the first works are six small etchings, age 16

Adriaen's earliest known works are not paintings but a set of six etchings made in 1653, when he was 16. About twenty etchings survive, mostly cattle in landscapes, occasionally with herders, and they were highly prized by collectors in his own time. Watchable behaviour: small etched plates in which a cattle group is the whole subject - the herd, the herder, the meadow, nothing else. The print is the portable, collectable version of the painting system. Grounding: Wikipedia ("He also was active as an etcher. His earliest known works are a set of six etchings executed in 1653. His twenty-odd etchings depict mostly cattle in landscapes, occasionally with herders. These works were highly prized by collectors in his time.").

**Takeaway:** he began at sixteen with the whole subject already in miniature - cattle, herder, meadow, nothing else.

## M14. The tranquil room-pastoral: a quiet picture designed to be easy to live with

The mature Adriaen picture is a tranquil pastoral - low horizon, high viewpoint, a vast distance ending in low misty hills, soft Mediterranean light, idealised peasants at a leisurely pace - made to give a city dweller a sense of being in touch with the countryside. The hills are entirely imaginary. Watchable behaviour: a low horizon and high viewpoint that make the distance feel vast; animals rendered with close attention to the texture of coats and the turn of a head; a cool, crisp light in the early work and a soft, warm Italianate light in the mature work; nothing disturbs the scene - a milkmaid, a sleeping shepherd, a goat and a kid. Grounding: National Gallery ("Adriaen van de Velde's tranquil views made him one of the most successful Dutch landscape artists of his time. In this picture, the low horizon and high viewpoint give the impression of a vast distance ending in a range of low, misty hills. These hills are, like the rest of the scene, entirely imaginary."; "Adriaen van de Velde's pastoral scenes are, like this one, almost always tranquil and serene. They were designed to be easy to live with, and to give city dwellers a sense of being in touch with the countryside and traditional life."; "Painted with Adriaen van de Velde's usual attention to the detail of texture, the animals in the picture seem almost tangible. The kid turns its head quickly, its eye and coat soft, its pose dainty and alive."). FLAGS: two parts of this mechanism are documented for other men, not for van de Velde himself. (a) The soft sunlight and warm hues of the mature Italianate pictures are documented as the influence of Karel Dujardin, Nicolaes Berchem and Jan Asselijn - the Italianate light is borrowed, not his own invention. (b) The early tight, precise technique and hard, cool sunlight are documented as the influence of Paulus Potter. The National Gallery also says he "probably visited Italy" in his youth - probable, not verified.

**Takeaway:** the picture is a room you can live in - a countryside for a city wall, with imaginary hills.

### Phase 1 - Early years, Haarlem training and the Potter years, 1653-1658

Earliest known works are six etchings of 1653. Dated paintings survive for every year from 1654 to 1671. The standard account says he first studied with his father, did not incline to marine painting, and was sent to Haarlem to train with the landscape painter Jan Wijnants, where he met Philip Wouwerman and Paulus Potter. DISPUTE TO FLAG: the Burlington review says the 2016 catalogue by Cornelis dismantles misconceptions such as Houbraken's claim that the artist had been trained by Jan Wijnants, and HNA says the catalogue essay dispels myths regarding his training. The training story is therefore repeated in most sources but is contested in the most recent scholarship; this study does not resolve it. Pastures with cattle and herders predominate in his work of 1653-58. The paintings and prints of these years reveal no trace of Wijnants's influence; instead the young van de Velde emulated Paulus Potter and, to a lesser extent, Karel Dujardin. His early work shows tight, precise technique and hard, cool sunlight under Potter's influence. Potter's example shaped his choice of the cattle-piece, his meadow landscape, his tight technique for grass, foliage and fur, and the hard cool sunlight of the mid-1650s. By 1657 he had settled in Amsterdam, married Maria Pieters Oudekerck (Ouderkerk) on 5 April 1657, a Catholic, and converted to Catholicism. His children were baptised in clandestine churches.

### Phase 2 - The turn to warm light and broader subjects, about 1658-1663

Warmer hues and a softer, yellower sunlight, a response to the Italianate painters Dujardin, Nicolaes Berchem and Jan Asselijn, entered his pictures about 1658, and at the same time he began to engage a broader range of landscape subjects. This luminosity and pronounced local colour, adapted from the Italianate evocation of Mediterranean light, eventually extended to his Dutch pastures, woods and dunes as well as his own Italianate views. His mature style reveals the influence of Dutch Italianate painters such as Karel Dujardin, Nicolaes Berchem and Jan Asselijn, reflected in soft sunlight and warm hues. As far as is known he never travelled to Italy. His Italianate manner therefore comes from seeing paintings brought back by peers, not from first-hand Italian landscape. A drawing of the church of SS. Quattro Coronati in Rome was in the past used as evidence for an undocumented trip to Italy, but was more likely based on a print or drawing by another artist.

### Phase 3 - Mature Amsterdam years, most productive period, early to mid 1660s

Eclectic Light says he was most productive in the early to mid 1660s, completing a series of different landscapes featuring portraits of cattle. The Burlington review says his Italianate pastoral scenes predominate from the mid-1660s, many seeming like variants on a single theme. Scale: he produced over 170 paintings in a career lasting from about 1653 to 1672; over 200 drawings survive; 28 etchings per Sphinx or about 24 per Te Papa - the two counts differ and are both reported here without resolving the difference. Printmaking was concentrated in 1653, 1657-59 and 1670. Collaboration became a regular practice. As early as 1664 an inventory lists paintings by Wijnants and Moucheron "gestofferd door Adriaen van de Velde", and his contribution demonstrably increased their market value. He typically added staffage or animals to other artists' paintings, including Wijnants, Hobbema, Ruisdael, Verboom, Philips Koninck, Jan van der Heyden and Frederik de Moucheron. The National Gallery says he was employed by other Amsterdam artists to supply figures for their paintings.

### Phase 4 - Late work, larger and more complex, 1666-1671

In the late 1660s he painted increasingly complex figures and compositions, such as The Ferry (1666). Large-scale works appear: The Farm/Koekamp (1666), A Hawking Party Setting Out (1666, Royal Collection), The Annunciation (1667) and the family portrait (1667), both large canvases over 1.2 m on a side. Religious and mythological subjects cluster late: The Annunciation (1667), Vertumnus and Pomona (1670, Kunsthistorisches Museum Vienna, 76.5 x 103 cm per Eclectic Light), and a series of five paintings of Christ's Passion commissioned in 1664 for the attic storey of the Ons' Lieve Heer op Solder museum, a Catholic chapel. The Migration of Jacob (1663, Wallace Collection) is reported in search as a religious subject; it was not verified against a Wallace object page. Winter scenes, rare in his work, also cluster late: Colf Players on the Ice near Haarlem (1668) and Amusement on the Ice (1669, Gemaldegalerie Dresden, 33 x 40 cm per search, REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED). The Hut (1671) was one of his last paintings. He died in Amsterdam in January 1672, aged 35, and was buried 21 January 1672. Despite his success he was in dire straits throughout his life; his wife ran a linen business to help make ends meet, and when he died his possessions and works were auctioned but the proceeds did not cover his debts. Late in life he took on less edifying work, such as designing decorations for maps and producing finished landscape drawings for the open market. The Franco-Dutch War broke out later in 1672, driving his father and brother to London.

## Evolution

What changed between the phases: from tight, cool, Potter-like pasture pieces, through warm Italianate light and a widened subject range, to small-panel cattle portraits in the mid-1660s, then to larger, more complex figure compositions, large religious and portrait canvases, and finally the natural, almost anecdotal late pastoral of The Hut. The through-line is the animal: the cattle get larger in the frame, more individually characterised, and more central to the composition as the career goes on.

Apprentice etcher of cattle at sixteen (1653) -> Potter's cool-light pasture painter (1653-58) -> warm Italianate landscapist with a widened subject range (c. 1658-63) -> the most productive years, small-panel cattle portraits and the staffage trade (early to mid 1660s) -> larger, more complex figure compositions, large religious and portrait canvases, late winter scenes and the natural late pastoral of The Hut (1666-71). The dated anchors hold the sequence: six etchings 1653, The Beach at Scheveningen 1658, Figures on the Coast 1660, the 1661-63 small panels, the 1664 staffage inventory, The Farm and The Ferry 1666, The Annunciation and the family portrait 1667, Colf Players 1668, Bull Grazing etching 1670, The Hut 1671, burial 21 January 1672. Where the record is thin: the exact sequence of his training is disputed (see Phase 1); the Italy question rests on negative evidence (no trip documented, one Roman church drawing probably copied); several works carry split dates (View of a Beach 1663/1665; Hilly Landscape c.1663, dated 1660-1672 in the Rijksmuseum listing); and the late financial difficulty is documented only in general terms, with no dated breakdown of which late works were commercial.

**Takeaway:** eighteen years, four phases, one through-line - the animal gets bigger in the frame and more itself, while the light warms and the pictures grow.


## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

1. THE WYNANTS TRAINING. The standard account (Wikipedia, Sphinx, National Gallery) says he trained with Jan Wynants in Haarlem after leaving his father's marine workshop. The 2016 catalogue by Cornelis "dismantles misconceptions such as Houbraken's claim that the artist had been trained by Jan Wijnants" (Burlington review), and the HNA review says the catalogue essay "dispels myths regarding his training". The training story is therefore repeated in most sources but contested in the most recent scholarship. Unresolved in this study: the catalogue itself was not read.
2. ITALY. No trip to Italy is documented, and the consensus is that he never left Holland; the mountains and Italianate character sprang from imagination and fellow artists' work. The National Gallery says "In his youth he probably visited Italy". A drawing of the church of SS. Quattro Coronati in Rome was once used as evidence for a trip, but was more likely based on a print or drawing by another artist. The "without travel" claim rests on negative evidence.
3. THE FUGITIVE PIGMENT, DIRECTION. Eclectic Light and the Burlington review say a fugitive yellow faded, turning his greens blue, most apparent from 1662 on. The HNA review says blue pigments faded into yellow. Both wordings are reported; the direction is unresolved. The 2016 catalogue is the authority and was not read.
4. OEUVRE COUNTS. Paintings: "nearly two hundred" (1911 Britannica) against "more than 170" (Eclectic Light). Etchings: 24 (Te Papa) against 28 (Sphinx Fine Art) against "twenty-odd" (Wikipedia) against "about twenty" (1911 Britannica). Drawings: over 200 survive (Burlington review). None of the counts is settled.
5. BIRTH YEAR AND AGE AT DEATH, OLD ERRORS. ## Aesthetic logic

Adriaen van de Velde's pictures are built on a single refusal and a single service. The refusal: born into the most famous sea-painting family in Holland, he would not paint ships, and his whole career is the land at the water's edge - the beach where the sea is barely in the picture, the meadow, the farmyard, the dune, the frozen canal - never the water itself. The service: he sold the living part of the picture to other men. His cattle, herdsmen, milkmaids and dogs are the most finished things in landscapes other painters composed, small and exquisitely observed, subordinated to the place they enter, and priced - the 1664 inventory proves it, "gestofferd door Adriaen van de Velde", his hand raising the value of a Wijnants or a Moucheron. The two halves meet in the cattle portrait: the animal drawn from life in the field, posed, lit, reused from a sketchbook library across a dozen compositions, the same cow earning its keep in picture after picture, horns set against the sky so the beast reads as a shape before it reads as a beast. And the light does the travelling he never did: cool Potter sun to about 1658, then a warm, soft, yellow Italianate sun borrowed from Dujardin, Berchem and Asselijn and laid over Dutch subjects, so that a flat field believes in a Mediterranean afternoon. The picture is designed to be lived with - low horizon, high viewpoint, vast imaginary distance, nothing disturbing the scene - and then the materials betray it: the greens turn blue, or the blues turn yellow, and the disagreement between the two authorities is now itself a fingerprint for attribution. Underneath it all is the workshop economy: a drawing library, a hired hand, a son who copied him so well he was nicknamed thief, a pupil who copied him so well his pictures are still confused with the master's, and a life that ended at 35 in debt, the linen business keeping the family fed while the pictures were already being collected across Europe.

## Positions

P1. He was the family's defector, not its heir. Born into the most famous sea-painting dynasty in Holland, he is the only van de Velde who refused the sea, and his whole career is the refusal made productive: the land, the meadow and the beach at the water's edge, never the water itself. Grounded in leg 1 P1, P2, P14.
P2. His real subject was the animal, not the landscape. The cattle are portraits - individually studied from life, posed, lit and reused - while the landscape is the frame built to hold them. Grounded in leg 1 P2, P5, P7.
P3. His career is a two-act story of light: hard, cool Potter sunlight to about 1658, then warm, soft, yellow Italianate sunlight after. The date 1658 is the hinge, marked by The Beach at Scheveningen. Grounded in leg 1 P2, P4.
P4. He painted Italy without ever going there. His Italianate views are second-hand Italy, assembled from Dujardin, Berchem and Asselijn, and they are better for it: an imagined south, not a reported one. Grounded in leg 1 P2, P8.
P5. The drawings are the real oeuvre and the paintings are assemblies. He kept a stock of life studies - cattle, nudes, shepherds - and recombined them across compositions, so the same cow or the same seated woman recurs in different pictures. Grounded in leg 1 P1, P2, P7.
P6. He was the most quietly influential painter of his generation because his hand is inside other men's masterpieces. Ruisdael, Hobbema, Heyden and Moucheron all carry his figures, and an inventory of 1664 proves his staffage raised the market value of their pictures. Grounded in leg 1 P1, P2, P13.
P7. His figures are great because they are small. Hofstede de Groot's verdict - his greatest gift was always to subordinate his figures to the landscape - is the rule of his art: the figure serves the place, never the reverse. Grounded in leg 1 P13.
P8. Poverty, not obscurity, is the paradox of his life. He was successful, admired and constantly employed, yet died in debt, his possessions auctioned for less than he owed, his wife keeping a linen business to feed the family. Grounded in leg 1 P1.
P9. His fame is mostly posthumous. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries made him one of the most sought-after Dutch names in Germany, France and England; the twentieth century forgot him until the 2016 retrospective put him back. Grounded in leg 1 P8, P12, P4.
P10. The colour we see is not the colour he painted. A fugitive yellow in his greens has turned them blue, most visibly from 1662 on - so the modern viewer sees a colder, bluer van de Velde than his contemporaries did. Grounded in leg 1 P4; see the contradiction flagged in M5/M10.
P11. The beach scenes are his most original contribution, not the cattle. Scheveningen in 1658 treats the beach as a social stage - leisure, work, children, fishermen, the well-dressed and the poor on one sand - a subject no Dutch painter had made central before. Grounded in leg 1 P4, P14; the "first to show seaside leisure" formulation itself comes only from a search snippet (Royal Collection Trust, leg 1 S10) and is flagged, not verified.
P12. His Catholic conversion is visible as a late subject, not a lifelong one. The religious pictures - The Annunciation 1667, the Descent from the Cross for a hidden church - cluster after his 1657 marriage, and they are the work of a landscape specialist attempting history painting, awkward angel and all. Grounded in leg 1 P1, P4, P7.
