## The question: who is the code for?

Govindarajan's work is not one system but one question asked in many media: what does it take for a picture made by code to reach someone who cannot see it, cannot read the code, or was never taught the history it draws on? The 2015 sketches ask it about the eye (additive light on black). The 2016 body-and-building pieces ask it about the senses (brain signals tilt a maze; microbes glow to music; a floor's energy becomes a heat map). The 2017 work asks it about the learner (a canvas that describes itself aloud; a colour that can be spoken; a mandala tool that records a gesture as text). The 2018 work asks it about the citizen (famine numbers tied to places on a map; valid data made to lie, so the lie can be named). The through-line is access to education, stated on her own site and visible in the career: accessibility tooling for blind learners, a tool for under-supported computer-science teachers, a non-profit makerspace for children in Bangalore, and sustained university teaching -- with the creative-coding work serving that mission rather than standing apart from it. [C35856]

That is why she belongs in this sequence after Fritz. Fritz's system is closed: one grid, four rule families, one disobedience. Govindarajan's systems are open at the far end: the picture is not finished when it looks right, it is finished when someone else can enter it -- by reading its description, by hearing its state, by remixing its code, by clicking its map. The computational question she hands the gallery is: what is the second rendering? A piece that renders only pixels is half a piece; the other half is the rendering into words, sound, tables, or teachable steps. [C35857]

## M1. Depth from flat overlap: alpha-layered rectangles (genart-001)

The seed image, viewed directly from Hans's save: four near-identical panels in a 2x2 grid on a pale blue-grey ground, separated by white gutters. Each panel is a loose stack of overlapping translucent rectangles in a narrow blue family, pale sky to deep navy, denser and darker toward the bottom half; the bottom-right panel carries a single small peach square, the only warm accent in the whole image. The Instagram caption (from the manifest, not re-fetched -- the post sits behind a login wall) reads: "Day 8 - Made a tiny prototypey code of a painting that @khannushi found on reddit (swipe to see the original). Made a video showing the proc[ess]". The post was published Monday 19 August 2019 -- not September 2019, as the #821 log and the task brief carried it; the correction is verified with `date -d`. [C35858]

The mechanism is stated as rules. A fixed palette of N blue fills plus one warm accent; each rectangle drawn with alpha a < 1 (roughly 0.25-0.6, from the visible stacking); overlap count per pixel determines darkness -- the picture's only depth cue is the alpha sum, with no stroke, no shadow, no gradient. The four panels run the same generator with different seeds (same rectangle count range, same palette, same canvas fraction); the accent appears in exactly one panel. Variables: rectangle count, size range, alpha, palette, seed per panel, accent presence (0/1 per panel). Constraints: all rectangles axis-aligned; the accent must stay small (under ~5% of panel area) or it stops being an accent. [C35859]

**Takeaway:** darkness as a pure function of overlap count is the whole picture -- a depth cue with no geometry behind it. And the 2x2 panel system reads as a controlled experiment: hold the generator constant, vary the seed, compare four outcomes at a glance. That reading is interpretation, not sourced fact -- the sources do not say she intended it as an experiment -- but it is the piece-able reading, and it is what the prompts below build on. [C35885]

## M2. The tiny prototype of a found image, with a process video

The caption names the whole practice in one sentence: a painting found by someone else (@khannushi, on Reddit), re-implemented as a "tiny prototypey code", documented with a process video. The original painting itself was not seen by any leg (it sits behind the Instagram swipe); its identity is unverified, and no claim about it is made here. What matters is the method, stated as rules: (1) reduce the found image to its generative skeleton (here: overlapping translucent rectangles); (2) write the smallest program that reproduces that skeleton; (3) run it several times (here: four panels, varied seeds); (4) record the process as video. Variables: fidelity target (how close to the original), seed count, whether the process video is part of the work. Constraint: the prototype must stay tiny -- the point is the reduction, not a faithful copy. [C35860]

**Takeaway:** this is a working method, not a single piece: it converts any found image into a generator. For the gallery it is a prompt-machine with a built-in honesty test -- if the reduction cannot be stated in one sentence, the prototype is not tiny enough. [C35886]

## M3. Brownian: mirrored random walks, linear morph, hue sweep (2015)

Repo `mathuramg/Brownian` (created 7 November 2015, ITP first semester), described by its own header comment: "Recording random movement as a continuous line." The full source was read. Two walks start at canvas centre: a right-walk (x += random(0, range x 1.2), y += random(-range/1.2, range/1.2)) and a mirrored left-walk (x += random(-range x 1.2, 0), same y jitter), range = 20, points constrained to the screen, drawn as a continuous polyline. Every 100 frames a new walk is generated; between generations, the code linearly interpolates point-by-point from walk N to walk N+1, frame i of 100 giving lerp(p1, p2, i/100) -- so the line visibly morphs from one random path into the next, a continuous deformation, never a cut. Colour: HSB, hue = abs((frameCount % 130) - 65) (a 0-65-0 triangle wave over 130 frames), saturation 100, brightness 100, alpha 50; `blendMode(LIGHTEST)` on a black background, so overlapping strokes brighten. The whole run lasts 500 frames (5 walks). Variables: range, frames-per-walk, hue period, alpha, walk count. Constraint: the two walks share the same y-jitter distribution (the mirror is exact in distribution, not in values); the morph must be linear in frame index. [C35861]

The same sketch plays music, and the heading on screen reads "Listen to the motion -- Watch the music." Each frame, the code samples the canvas every 10 px and counts non-black pixels. The count selects exactly one loop by band: 0-300 lit pixels -> bass at amp countPixel/700; 300-700 -> drums at amp (countPixel-300)/700; 700-1000 -> piano at amp (countPixel-700)/700; above 1000 -> silence (no branch sets a volume). So as the drawing accumulates and the lit area grows, the music crossfades bass -> drums -> piano, then falls silent when the canvas is fullest. [C35862]

**Takeaway:** the sound is a function of the picture's state, not of time -- and it is deliberately coarse. The mapping is many-to-one (count only), so two very different pictures with the same lit-pixel count sound identical, and the silence above 1000 is the punchline: the fullest picture makes no sound at all. A sonification that admits its own coarseness is more honest than one that pretends to hear everything. [C35863]

## M4. p5.js accessibility: three outputs for a bitmap canvas

The problem, per the OSSTA lecture transcript and her project page: the `<canvas>` element is a bitmap -- a screen reader only knows a canvas exists, not what is drawn in it, and image alt text does not apply. The work began with Chancey Fleet, a blind technology instructor at the New York Public Library who wanted an accessible spatial diagram to plan a banquet, via Claire Kearney-Volpe's 2016 Processing Foundation fellowship; Govindarajan joined in late 2016, and it continued as a 2018 Processing Foundation Fellowship for Govindarajan and Luis Morales-Navarro with Kearney-Volpe as mentor, out of the NYU Ability Project. The blog post "Making p5.js Accessible" (Processing Foundation, Thursday 12 April 2018, co-written with Morales-Navarro) argues the HTML canvas is probably the single most inaccessible element in HTML and answers with text, table, and sound outputs for the canvas (via "monkey patching" p5.js functions to record what is drawn), packaged as p5.accessibility.js, plus a small colour-naming module, colornamer, that returns plain crayon-style colour names instead of fanciful ones. [C35864]

Three accessible outputs were built into the early p5.js web editor: (1) a text output -- a plain-text description of the canvas; (2) a grid output -- shapes mapped onto a table by position; (3) a sound output -- sonification of shape movement, vertical position -> pitch, horizontal position -> stereo pan. The project page adds editor-side features from the same work: audio warnings for lint messages (also shown visually) and a textual/sonified representation of canvas activity. The addon approach was then deliberately abandoned -- "accessibility should not be an addon but part of the core library" -- and replaced by four functions now in p5.js core: user-generated `describe()` (whole-canvas description, one string) and `describeElement()` (per-element descriptions), and library-generated `textOutput()` (general description + detail + a table of shapes) and `gridOutput()` (a 10x10 table; a shape's cell in the table is its position on the canvas, so a screen-reader user gets the spatial distribution). Descriptions are dynamic: they are generated inside `draw()`, so a moving shape's description updates ("green circle at x pos 5 moving to the right", "top middle" becoming "top right"). The accessibility code is distributed across 26+ files of the p5.js source, so any contributor touching the library encounters it; at one point an accessibility bug broke WebGL for about two weeks. The sound output did not survive into the core library -- only text and grid outputs did. Blind novice programmers (e.g. Antonio Guimaraes, NYU ITP, a colourful chessboard) used the outputs to create, not just to consume. [C35865]

**Takeaway:** the description is a second rendering, not a caption. A grid output is a picture remade as a table; a dynamic description is a picture remade as a sentence that changes as the picture changes. The gallery question is what a piece looks like when its second rendering is designed with the same care as its first -- and whether a piece that cannot describe itself is finished at all. [C35866]

## M5. India by Numbers: hand-built data -> interactive famine maps

Dataset first, picture second. The data was hand-collected from books into Excel sheets "that could put financial spreadsheets to shame" -- a timeline 1600-1947, treaties, rulers and governors-general, wars and battles, famines -- published openly in the indian-history repo (October 2017) with corrections invited "as long as there are solid sources". The anchor numbers: 31 serious famines in 120 years of British rule vs 17 in the 2,000 years before; famine deaths 1757-1947 just short of 60 million; India's share of the world economy fell from 25% to 3%; ~120 battles in the Anglo-Indian wars. The project grew out of her shock at the famine death toll and her frustration that Indian history education emphasised *when* events happened rather than *why* and *what*. [C35867]

Data-to-form mapping in the interactive visuals: each famine is a clickable map polygon whose boundary is traced from a historical famine map; severity is encoded by region shading (slightly / severely / intensely affected, after the 1885 Stanford's Geography map); each region carries mortality rates and further features; each famine is tied to events in the British Raj timeline for context. The Gujarat Famine 1812 panel was viewed directly: a white India outline on white ground; only Gujarat/Kutch/Kathiawar filled in, in two reds (dark red Kutch, lighter red Gujarat), with the labels "Mortality -- NA" and "British territories affected: Kutch, Kathiawar and Gujarat". Variables: dataset (which famines, which numbers), severity scale, map set, timeline events. Constraint: every number is linked to a time and a place -- the maps are what stop the numbers living on spreadsheets. [C35868]

**Takeaway:** the visual language is extreme restraint -- one outlined country, one coloured region, two lines of text. That restraint is the mature version of the 2015 impulse: let the system, not decoration, carry it. The famine map and the Brownian walk share a rule: the picture earns its effect from one mapping, stated plainly. [C35869]

## M6. What Our Numbers Don't Show: the deliberate-misinterpretation machine

Data Through Design 2018, with David Lockard: a freestanding physical station -- two screens, a laser-cut map panel, six buttons, headphones, printed handouts -- viewed in its front-view photograph. Input: valid NYC municipal datasets. Procedure: present the audience with a series of blatantly absurd deductions drawn from that valid data, then reveal the methodological error behind each one -- correlation vs causation, bias, overfitting, statistical anomalies. Six buttons, each a named fallacy: Tyranny of Averages, Significance, Fallacy of Division, Overfitting, Correlation vs. Causation, MAUP. Variables: dataset, deduction, error type. Constraint: the data must be genuinely valid and the deduction genuinely absurd; if either side is fudged the lesson collapses. Tangible interaction, humour, and a welcoming interface are the delivery, not the mechanism. [C35870]

**Takeaway:** this is the same question as M5 from the other side. M5 makes true numbers legible; M6 shows how legible-looking numbers lie. Together they are a pair: one machine that tells the truth with a map, one machine that tells a lie with a chart and then names the lie. [C35871]

## M7. Can My Computer Draw: gesture -> alphabet -> corpus -> new gestures

Goal stated on the project page: recreate a hand-drawn world map with mandalas using machine learning. She first drew a world map by hand with mandalas filling the oceans (original viewed: white continents on a grey mandala ocean, photographed on cloth). Then, using the MandalaDoodle tool, she drew ~250 mandalas, each recorded as a paragraph of direction letters (~0.2 million characters total), and trained a char-rnn on that text. The trained model generated new mandalas, which were tiled into the oceans of a fresh world map. The final map was viewed directly: a wide black-and-white world map; the continents are flat white silhouettes and the oceans are a dense pack of overlapping circular mandalas of many sizes -- rings, spokes, petals, dots -- in black line on white, with small black gaps between circles. The generated version is crisper and more uniformly circular than the hand-drawn original, whose mandalas are looser, greyer, and include star/compass motifs. [C35872]

The mechanism, stated exactly from the page: (1) a training-data tool mirrors the mouse movement on a circular axis, so circular symmetry is captured automatically; (2) mouse movement is recorded as letters -- e.g. 'a' = mouse moved one pixel right, 'd' = one pixel up -- a movement alphabet, direction -> character; (3) ~250 mandalas drawn with the tool, each recorded as a paragraph, total corpus 0.2 million characters; (4) the corpus fed to char-rnn with TensorFlow, 3 layers -- an algorithm normally used to generate text -- which generates new mandala movement sequences, rendered as sample mandalas and composed into the world map. The MandalaDoodle tool itself (January 2017) mirrors the stroke around a centre with radial symmetry selectable as 4, 8, 36, or 72 parts, the whole rosette rotated 23.5 degrees, stroke hue on a 1-360 slider; the part-count is pushed as a letter ('z' for 4, 'y' for 8, 'x' for 36, 'w' for 72). Variables: mirror symmetry (circular), alphabet size (movement quantisation), corpus size, network depth (3 layers). Constraint: the alphabet must be small enough that 250 examples give a learnable distribution, and the mirroring must happen at recording time, not in the model -- the model never learns symmetry, it inherits it. [C35873]

**Takeaway:** the beautiful decision is where the symmetry lives. It is not in the network and not in the rendering -- it is in the recording tool, before any learning happens. The model generates letters; the tool guarantees circles. Any piece that borrows this must decide the same thing: which invariant is enforced by the instrument, and which is left for the system to discover. [C35874]

## M8. EnergyViz: live sensor data -> heat map -> everyday comparisons

ITP Spring Show 2016, with Viniyata Pany. Real-time energy data from the Enertiv monitoring system on the ITP floor, rendered as a heat map of energy usage on the floor plan, on an interactive screen for passers-by, with a physical dial to set the time range (any point in the past year to the present). The design aim is translation: raw kilowatt numbers are re-expressed as daily facts and comparisons ("gamify energy usage through fun comparisons and contrasts") so passers-by can grasp them at a glance, with moment-by-moment data for the curious. Variables: sensor feed, floor-plan geometry, comparison set (which daily devices -- the exact set is not in the sources read), time window (moment-by-moment vs overview). Constraint: two levels at once -- an easy overview for a passer-by and moment-by-moment detail for the curious. The mapping is data -> colour on a plan + data -> familiar object. [C35875]

**Takeaway:** two levels at once is a compositional rule, not a UI nicety. The overview must read in a glance and the detail must reward staying; a piece that only has one of the two is a poster or a spreadsheet. [C35876]

## M9. color-namer and the teaching practice (minor)

Repo `mathuramg/color-namer` (August 2017): "A simple js tool to give back the color-name of a hex value." `rgbColorName(255,0,0)` returns "red"; `hexColorName('ff0000')` returns "red". Built to supply the colour words the p5.accessibility canvas descriptions need -- a blind coder's sketch cannot say "fill(255, 0, 0)" and mean nothing; it must be able to say the colour out loud. That is the whole tool, and it is accessibility-adjacent in exactly her sense: a colour that can only be seen becomes a colour that can be read aloud. Her current practice runs the same way: Paper Crane Lab (Bangalore non-profit combining STEM concepts with art and creative projects for education access; registered public charitable trust with 12A/80G status; makerspace in Indiranagar; programmes in Computer Skilling, Making at the Library with Buguri/Hasiru Dala, and a weekly After School Science Program) and teaching p5.js and interaction design. The Lab existed by March 2019 (Bauhaus Centenary presentation with David Lockard, Thursday 28 March 2019); no founding year is stated in any source read, and none is asserted here -- a GitHub repo dated June 2019 is not a founding date. [C35877]

## The arc: 2015 code sketches -> body and building -> teaching and access -> history as data -> the Lab

2015 (ITP, first semester): pure code sketches -- Brownian walks, pattern-generator tiles, fractal trees, all with a sound layer (five-note frequencies, preloaded loops). The visual language is black-ground additive light and procedural tiling. 2016: the body and the building enter -- brain signals drive a maze, microbes glow to music, a floor's energy becomes a heat map. Code now reads the physical world and translates it. 2017: teaching and access become the work -- the mandala tool and its ML offspring, the Cat In A Box thesis, the p5 accessibility library and color-namer. The mandala's circular symmetry is the through-line from code to craft. 2018: history and the city as data -- India by Numbers, the data-misinterpretation kiosk, Peblio, the Rube Goldberg welcome machine. The famine map's restraint (one outline, one red region) is the mature version of the 2015 impulse: let the system, not decoration, carry it. 2019 -> present: the daily p5 sketch (genart-001, Monday 19 August 2019) is the last pure generative image in the catalogue; after it the practice consolidates into Paper Crane Lab -- the same mechanisms (making, mapping, teaching through art) run as an organisation, not as pieces. [C35878]

## Aesthetic logic: restraint, additive light, and the hand-drawn dataset

Her visual language has three constants across media. First, restraint: one mapping, stated plainly -- one outline and one red region; one grid output; one accent square in one panel. Second, additive light on black in the early code work (Brownian, CirclesMove): overlapping traces brighten where they cross, so density reads as light, the same rule the genart-001 rectangles use in reverse on a pale ground (overlap reads as darkness). Third, the hand-drawn dataset: numbers are collected by hand, from books, into spreadsheets, before any picture is made -- the dataset is a crafted object, not a feed. The laser-cut world map rhymes with the ML mandala map (the world as a field of circles, land as void) and the CNC table's blue resin grid rhymes with the rectangle stacks, but those are craft objects, not systems; they are noted as motif rhymes, not mechanisms. What is absent throughout: decoration for its own sake, gradients as ornament, photorealism, text as image. The work trusts a single rule to carry the picture, and when the rule is a social one -- who can enter the picture -- it states that rule just as plainly. [C35879]

## Rejections (six)

1. Peblio as a piece: a teaching platform and lesson-sharing community, with no visual mechanism of its own -- a product, not a system for a canvas.
2. A Rube Goldberg chain-reaction piece: documented only in process photos and an unwatched video; chain reaction is generic, and the mechanism (pair-built segments combined on the last day) is a workshop schedule, not a picture rule.
3. A brain-controlled maze piece: Dark Maze is hardware-dependent (openBCI headset, servos, a physical maze); its signal chain is real but unbuildable as a canvas piece, and without the headset it is just a maze.
4. A glowing-microbes piece: BioMusic's glow was never observed -- no photograph of the glowing microbes was accessible -- and microbes on speakers cannot be a canvas system.
5. An accessibility-text-only piece: a canvas description without a visual system is a feature, not a piece; MTH4 below earns its place only because the description and the picture are built together, each driving the other.
6. A color-namer quiz: naming colours is a utility, not a system; the tool's value is inside the accessibility work, not as a standalone game. Also rejected as a subject, not a piece: using real famine-death numbers as a visual motif -- the MTH5 piece below therefore uses an explicitly invented dataset, and the real numbers stay in the study, not on the canvas. [C35881]
