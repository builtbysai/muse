## The life

Willem Cornelisz Duyster was baptised on 30 August 1599 in the Oude Kerk, Amsterdam, the eldest of four children from the second marriage of his father Cornelis Dircksz -- first a barber, later a carpenter -- to Hendrikje Jeronimus, who came from Norway. His birth name was Willem Cornelis Dircksz; "Duyster" is not a patronymic. In 1620 the family moved from the Paternostersteeg to the Koningstraat, into a house called "De Duystere Werelt" -- the Dark World, the dim world -- and Willem took his surname from the house (leg 1 F1-F3).

The surname first appears in a document dated 1 July 1625, concerning a violent quarrel between Duyster and Pieter Codde at a country-house party hosted by the painter Barent van Someren at Meerhuysen: Codde hit Duyster in the face with a pitcher -- tin, says EN Wikipedia; pewter, says Christie's. The two were exact contemporaries, both born in Amsterdam in 1599, and friends before the quarrel; NL Wikipedia calls Codde his "former friend" after it. The brawl is the first document of Duyster's adult life, and it is also the first document of the guardroom's inventors together in one room (leg 1 F4, F7; leg 3 N2, N11).

His training is likely but not proven. NL Wikipedia calls Codde Duyster's "voormalige vriend en leermeester" (former friend and teacher); FR and IT Wikipedia state flatly that he studied under Codde; the National Gallery, London is more cautious -- "He may have trained with Pieter Codde" -- and notes that the Amsterdam guild records are lost, so his early career "remains mysterious". The 1913 Valentiner/Johnson catalogue calls him "Pupil of Pieter Codde"; EN Wikipedia's Codde article states it flatly too: "Codde's pupil was Willem Duyster, who was about the same age." The study carries the pupillage as stated-by, with the hedge visible, and does not present it as settled fact (leg 1 F6; leg 3 K15).

On 5 September 1631 Duyster married Margrieta Kick, then aged 30, sister of the painter Simon Kick -- and on the same day Simon Kick entered into ondertrouw with Duyster's sister. A double betrothal binding the two families twice over, and both couples lived in De Duystere Werelt, the house Duyster was named after. His sister's name is Stijntje in NL Wikipedia and Christina in FR Wikipedia -- "Stijntje" is a Dutch diminutive of Christina, so the two may be the same person, but no source consulted states this outright, and the study does not assert either name without a check against the Amsterdam ondertrouw registers (leg 1 F9-F10, C3; leg 3 N3, K8).

He died of the plague in Amsterdam in 1635 and was buried in the Zuiderkerk on 31 January 1635, aged 35 -- EN sources round to 36. His period of activity is conventionally taken to begin in 1625, the year of the first document, and end with his death: a working career of about ten years (leg 1 F5, F12-F13).

After an early period as a portraitist, Duyster became a specialist in genre scenes -- merry companies and military scenes. His oeuvre is small, comprising genre scenes and portraits, and only three extant works by his hand are dated. The lack of dated works makes precise dating difficult, and almost everything is dated by style, costume and dendrochronology rather than signature (leg 1 F14-F15).

## M1. The invention of the kortegaardje: a room for soldiers off duty

The guardroom scene -- kortegaardje, a corruption of the French corps de garde -- is a genre painting depicting an interior of a temporary nature hosting officers, soldiers and their camp followers, passing their leisure time surrounded by military items and booty. That is Jochai Rosen's definition ("The Dutch Guardroom Scene of the Golden Age: A Definition", Artibus et Historiae 27, no. 53, 2006, p. 171), and Rosen's fuller abstract adds the mechanism's purpose: the scene "is free of any violent action, and it usually appears respectable and in some cases even merry"; it "usually does not revolve around a clear subject matter, but is a collection of motifs, anecdotes, and notions"; it "gave the middle-class man, nesting safely at home, a restrained drama devoid of violence while touching his social sensitivity."

The genre was formulated c. 1628-1638 by a group of Amsterdam and Utrecht painters -- Codde the most dominant and innovative, with Duyster, Pieter Quast, Pieter Potter and Jacob Duck -- and Duyster and Codde are its Amsterdam originators. The Eighty Years' War resumed in 1621, putting soldiers in Amsterdam streets and romanticising mercenary life; the first interiors with soldiers appear in the latter half of the 1620s, and the genre peaks in the 1630s-40s. Duyster's guardrooms belong to the first wave, c. 1625-1635; he died before the genre's later elaboration by De Hooch and others in the 1650s (leg 1 F28, F32; leg 2, Duyster vs Codde vs Kick).

The mechanism is the room itself: a temporary interior -- a stable, a barn, a guardroom -- that is not a home and not a battlefield, where soldiers do nothing military. Drinking, smoking, playing tric-trac, making music, courting, arguing over loot. The room is the subject; the men are its furniture, arranged by rank and by light. Borger (1996, via Sotheby's) corrected the old reading: these men were not regular soldiers but civic guards, and the genre's motifs -- smoking, gambling, tric-trac, drinking -- come from merry-company painting. The guardroom is a merry company with muskets leaning on the wall.

**Takeaway:** build the room first and the men second. The piece must be a temporary interior -- stable, barn, guardroom -- with soldiers doing nothing military, and the room's own logic (the table, the fire, the booty, the drum) must carry the picture before any single figure does.

## M2. The guardroom without prostitutes: hostages, victims, scorers

Unlike many painters of guardroom scenes, Duyster avoided depicting prostitutes; the women in his military paintings are often hostages for ransom or victims of looting "or worse" (EN Wikipedia, citing Franits 2004, p. 64). The mechanism is a substitution: where Codde's and Duck's guardrooms put professional courtesans with exposed breasts among the soldiers, Duyster's women are participants, scorers or victims -- never the courtesan.

The works bear this out. In Two Men playing Tric-trac, with a Woman scoring (W06), the woman chalks the score on the side of the board -- she keeps the game's accounts, she does not sell herself to its players. In Soldiers dividing booty in a barn with a captive couple (W13/W14), the woman is one half of a captive couple, loot's human form. In Interior with Soldiers (W01), the courting is present but the women are not professionals. The Sotheby's Duck lot note makes the contrast explicit: Duck's guardrooms show soldiers "in the presence of several elegantly dressed women", while Duyster's women are hostages (leg 1 F31; leg 2 W06, W13, Duyster vs Codde vs Kick).

It is the single most usable Duyster discriminator, and it changes the moral temperature of the room. A guardroom with courtesans is a brothel with uniforms; a guardroom with a hostage couple is a room where the war has come inside. Duyster's soldiers are not only at leisure -- they are holding people, and the picture knows it.

**Takeaway:** when a woman enters the guardroom, give her a function that is not the courtesan's -- the scorer chalking the board, the captive held for ransom, the victim of the loot -- and let that function change what the room means.

## M3. Textile virtuosity: satin, lace, and the mirror in the cloth

The single most repeated technical claim about Duyster is that he was exceptionally skilled at painting textiles -- clothing, fabrics, lace, satin with mirror-like reflections, embroidered fabrics, cuffs, collars. NL Wikipedia: "met name bekwaam in het weergeven van kleding en andere textielstoffen". EN Wikipedia: "a strong ability to paint textiles". The National Gallery of Ireland: "highly skilled painter of different textures, fabrics and light effects". Christie's, on a youth holding a silver cup and a pipe: "fine brushwork describing the cuffs of the boy's jacket and the white cotton of the garment on the chair". Sotheby's: "chaque bijou, chaque pan de dentelle... des tissus de satin brodes avec des reflets miroitant" -- every jewel, every piece of lace, embroidered satin fabrics with shimmering reflections (leg 1 F22).

The paint itself is thin and loose -- "The thin, loose application of paint is typical of Duyster's style" (Christie's lot essay) -- which is how the textiles are done: not by building up impasto but by laying a thin skin of paint that lets the ground breathe through it, the way satin lets light through. The Rijksmuseum catalogue on The Tric-trac Players (W07) notes "the costly materials of the costumes, especially of the two foreground figures, are depicted with a close eye for detail", and the National Gallery on W06: "He not only skilfully depicts the way light falls on silks but also dresses his figures in subtle tones that do not break the atmosphere of gentle conviviality and reverie."

The textiles are also the mechanism of the theatre (M6): the sumptuous costumes that "would have no place on the battlefield" are what tell the viewer this is an imagined scene, a stage, not a report. The cloth is the display. In a Duyster, the satin is doing the work that the action does in another painter's guardroom.

**Takeaway:** make the cloth the event. The piece should lavish its attention on a cuff, a collar, a slashed silk sleeve, a Turkey carpet -- the textile as the brightest, most worked thing in the room -- and let the thin loose paint be the technique, not a finish.

## M4. The dark box and the single light: chiaroscuro in the dim world

Duyster's guardrooms deepen, over five years, into a dark box with a single light. The Hermitage, on Officers in a Guardroom (W03, early 1630s): "in the depths of a room two men are playing cards and smoking pipes by the light of a candle"; "the sharp contrasts between the dark silhouettes and the bright patches of light produce a special somewhat mysterious atmosphere in the foreground." The Hermitage calls Duyster "one of the first [among the 'Little Dutch Masters'] to employ the eloquent language of chiaroscuro -- powerful contrasts of light and shade." IT Wikipedia: he painted genre subjects "anche con ambientazione notturna a lume di candela o di lampada" -- also with nocturnal settings by candle or lamplight, close to the Utrecht Caravaggisti in manner (leg 1 F25; leg 2 W03).

The house name is a suggestive but unproven link, and the study does not assert it as a cause: he was named after De Duystere Werelt, the dim world, and he painted dim worlds. The coincidence is worth one line and no more. What the sources do support is the trajectory: from the bright, crowded, quasi-masquerade companies of c. 1623-25, through the more restrained pictures of the late 1620s, to the early-1630s rooms lit by a single candle or a fire in the depths of the room. The NGI's Interior with Soldiers (W01, 1632) is praised specifically for "different textures, fabrics and light effects" -- the light is doing the textile work of M3 by the end.

The mechanism is a single source in a dark box: one candle, one fire, one window, and the figures picked out of darkness by it. Nothing in the room is evenly lit; everything is either in the light or in the dark, and the dark is most of the room.

**Takeaway:** one light, one dark box. The piece must be lit from a single source -- candle, fire, a shaft from the left -- with the rest of the room falling into dark, and the figures emerging from that dark rather than standing in it.

## M5. Silence between figures: nobody speaking, nobody looking

The National Gallery, on Two Men playing Tric-trac, with a Woman scoring (W06): "None of them is speaking and nor do they look at one another; all the interaction happens on the board." EN Wikipedia, citing Franits: Duyster could "accurately characterize his subjects, and... depict relationships between his figures". FR Wikipedia: "talent reel a depeindre les personnages et leurs expressions." The mechanism is a choice about how figures relate: not by eye contact, not by dialogue, not by gesture toward one another, but by proximity and posture -- by being arranged around the same board, the same table, the same game (leg 1 F24; leg 2 W06).

The National Gallery's reading of W06 gives the device in full: "He paints four of the figures in profile but each is distinctive, and he enlivens what could be a static gathering by arranging the central figures in a circle and leading the eye around them." The circle is the composition: the players sit around the tric-trac board, the woman chalks the score, one man sits smoking lost in his thoughts, another lights a pipe from a bowl of embers -- and the eye travels the circle while the figures do not look at each other at all. The relationship is the arrangement.

It is the opposite of the merry company's noise. A Hals merry company is a conversation; a Duyster guardroom is a set of people alone together, each absorbed in a separate action, and the picture's social fact is that they share a table and a light. The silence is not emptiness -- it is the mechanism that lets the viewer read each figure separately and the group as a whole.

**Takeaway:** arrange the figures in a circle around the game and let them not look at each other. The watchable behaviour is absorption -- each figure in his own action, the interaction happening on the board, not between the faces -- and the eye must travel the circle the figures will not.

## M6. Violence as theatre: the staged brawl, the safe viewer

Soldiers fighting over Booty in a Barn (W05, c. 1623-4, National Gallery NG1386) is the earliest dated work in this catalogue, and it already has the theatre device fully formed. The National Gallery's prose: "violence has broken out. An argument among a group of soldiers over the distribution of booty has turned nasty and a fatal brawl has developed." And then the turn: "Duyster is intent on showing that this is not a visual report of a real incident but an imagined scene akin to theatre. The sumptuous costumes worn by the soldiers would have no place on the battlefield and the skirmish takes place as if on a stage. The viewer can enjoy the thrill of violence in complete safety while simultaneously relishing the display of luxury and painterly skill."

The mechanism is the frame that says "this is not real": the costumes too fine for the field, the spoils spread on a table and on the door of a barn like props, the sword encounter in the right foreground like a scene blocked for the stalls. The NGI's general framing of Duyster's guardrooms says the same from the other side: "Sometimes the soldiers are shown looting, taking people hostage or fighting amongst themselves" -- alongside the peaceful motifs, drinking, smoking, tric-trac, music, courting. The violence and the leisure are the same room's two registers, and the theatre frame holds both (leg 2 W05, W01; leg 1 F31).

It is also what separates Duyster's violence from Brouwer's. Brouwer's brawls are tavern brawls -- low, comic, peasant. Duyster's brawl is a brawl in costume, on a stage, with loot as set dressing. The viewer is safe because the picture has told him it is a picture.

**Takeaway:** stage the violence, do not report it. The piece should show the fight as a blocked scene -- fine costumes, props arranged, the encounter at the front of the stage -- and the viewer must feel the thrill without ever believing it is happening.

## M7. The ten-year career and the plague hinge: the death that made a painter

Duyster's working career runs about ten years, c. 1625-1635, and it ends with plague. He was buried on 31 January 1635, aged 35. Simon Kick, his brother-in-law twice over, was 32 that year, and Rosen's 2021 synopsis says Kick was "driven to do so only due to the tragic death of his brother-in-law and since the economic situation necessitated it" -- driven to take up painting, at 32, because Duyster died. The claim rests on the synopsis, not the book, and the study carries it as Rosen's; but the chronology coheres, and the hinge is the chain's own: #564 studied the man the death made a painter, #565 studies the man whose death did it (leg 1 F12; leg 3 N3).

The compression shapes the oeuvre. The earliest firmly dated work is c. 1623-4 (W05) and the latest dated is 1632 (W01, and the Philadelphia Soldiers beside a Fireplace if its Wikipedia date holds); the repertoire -- tric-trac, cards, smoking, drinking, loot-division, music, courting -- is stable from first to last. What changes is light, restraint and scale, not subject: Duyster refined one room, not many. And he died before the genre's later elaboration: Codde carried the guardroom through its whole first arc, 1628-1638 at his height, and lived to 78; Duyster got ten years and died at 35, and the genre went on without him into the 1640s second phase, the church interiors, the gentrified Palamedesz. halls, and ter Borch's afterlife (leg 2, Evolution; leg 3 N2, N6, N8).

The mechanism is a career as a short fuse with a hinge at the end: the work is all first-wave, all invention, no late style, no decline, no second act -- and the death is not only the end of the career but the beginning of another painter's. A painter whose chief biographical fact, after the invention, is that he died and his brother-in-law picked up a brush.

**Takeaway:** a career that ends before it can repeat itself. The piece should feel like work made inside ten years -- the same room refined, the light deepening, the figures fewer -- and then stop, abruptly, with the hinge visible: the room emptying, the light going out, and the next painter's career beginning where this one ends.

## M8. The monogram and the lost name: WD, and the oeuvre that had to be recovered

Duyster signed with a "WD" monogram -- on the NGI.436 panel, "W D 1632"; on the side of a drum in Soldiers dividing booty (W13); "WC DVYSTE" on the carpet in The Tric-trac Players (W07). The monogram was the key to recovering his oeuvre: Hofstede de Groot first spotted it on the drum at the 1894 Utrecht exhibition, where the booty-division pictures hung as Gerard ter Borch. Many of Duyster's few surviving pictures were long attributed to other artists -- notably ter Borch; Sotheby's calls him "assez mysterieux" with most works still attributed to others. Soldiers dividing booty was sold as Ter Borch in 1777 and exhibited as "in the manner of Ter Borch" in 1894. A Wedding Feast (W08) was attributed to Herman Berntsz van Byler, Johann Liss, Pieter Codde and Jan Miense Molenaer before Bredius attributed it to Duyster on stylistic similarity to W07 -- and the attribution was confirmed only in 1972, when restoration removed overpainting and revealed the background, the tiled floor and Duyster's signature. The two little copper portraits (W09/W10) were acquired as Codde, reattributed to Duyster by A. Staring in 1960, and Beaujean still preferred Codde in 1998 (leg 1 F19-F21, F27; leg 2 W08, W09, W13).

The mechanism is authorship as something that has to be found, in the paint, on an object in the scene -- a drum, a carpet, a tiled floor -- rather than on the surface of the painting. And the same small-name problem runs the other way: EN Wikipedia's own note 5 cites wwar.com as "Willem Duyster (1599-1678)", giving him Codde's death year -- the two exact contemporaries, born the same year in the same city, co-inventors of the same genre, merged into one man by a database. The conflation is the monogram's mirror image: where the monogram recovers a name, the database erases the difference between two (leg 1 C1; leg 3 K12).

There is no Rosen catalogue raisonne of Duyster. Rosen did Duck (2017), Codde (2020) and Kick (2021); the Duyster catalogue raisonne in preparation belongs to Leonard Pouy (Sotheby's 2013), and whether it has since appeared could not be established from open web searches. The oeuvre's recovery is therefore still, in part, unfinished -- a small body of pictures, some of them still carrying the wrong name in old sales, waiting on a book that has been in preparation since at least 2013 (leg 1 F37-F38, C5).

**Takeaway:** the name is hidden in the picture, on an object in the scene. The piece should hide its maker's mark -- a monogram on a drum, a signature in a carpet, a date on a tile -- and let the picture be misattributed, passed from name to name, until the mark is found and the name comes back.

## Evolution

The career is short -- the earliest firmly dated work here is c. 1623-4 (W05) and the latest dated is 1632 (W01, W02) -- and almost everything is dated by style, costume and dendrochronology rather than signature, because so few works are dated. What the sources do support:

Early (c. 1623-25): small, bright, elongated, masquerade-like. Playter (via the Rijksmuseum W07 entry) dates the Amsterdam tric-trac panel to "around 1624" and describes "bright colours and small, elongated figures in quasi-masquerade dress". W05 (Booty in a Barn, c. 1623-4, the earliest work in this catalogue) already has the theatre device fully formed -- sumptuous, un-battlefield-like costume, staged violence. W08 (Wedding Feast, c. 1625) belongs to the same early phase: a large, crowded, brightly dressed company piece, 76 x 107 cm, the largest panel in this catalogue.

Mid-to-late 1620s: more restrained. Playter again: the St Petersburg (W03) and London (W06) tric-trac/card pictures are "more restrained" and "assumed to have been made in the second half of the 1620s". W06's own NG prose stresses the restraint -- "subtle tones that do not break the atmosphere of gentle conviviality and reverie", figures in profile, nobody speaking, all interaction on the board. The palette quiets; the figure count drops; the compositions become smaller and more concentrated (W07: 31.1 x 41 cm; W03: 43.5 x 45.5 cm).

Early 1630s: chiaroscuro deepens; the guardroom becomes a dark box with a single light. The Hermitage W03 (early 1630s) is lit by a single candle in the depths of the room; the NGI W01 (1632) is praised specifically for "different textures, fabrics and light effects". The trajectory is from bright, crowded, quasi-masquerade companies toward dim, candle- or fire-lit interiors in which a few figures are picked out of darkness.

Portraits run alongside throughout, not as a separate early phase only. The miniatures are dated 1627 and 1629 (W10, W09); the Delff engraving after Duyster is dated 1628 (W17); the double wedding (5 September 1631) and the group portraits (SK-C-514, SK-A-203) sit at the end of the career. EN Wikipedia's "after an early period as a portraitist" is therefore a simplification: portraiture persists to the end, but individual sitters are rare and the miniatures are small-scale (copper, 15 x 12 cm).

Format habits: a preference for oval or circular panels (Rijksmuseum W07 entry); small scale overall -- most verified panels are 31-68 cm on the long side, with W08 (76 x 107 cm) the outlier; almost everything is oil on (oak) panel, with copper for the miniatures and one panel transferred to canvas (W07).

What did NOT change: the subject-matter grammar is stable from W05 (1623-4) to W01 (1632): tric-trac, cards, smoking, drinking, loot-division, music, courting. The evolution is in light, restraint and scale, not in repertoire. That stability is itself the point -- Duyster refined one room, not many.

## Aesthetic logic

Duyster's aesthetic is a small dark room, a single light, and cloth. The guardroom scene as Codde and Duyster invented it is a room of men off duty, and the genre's pleasures are abundance, noise, and the textures of loot and drink. Duyster keeps the room and quiets the men: nobody speaks, nobody looks at one another, the interaction happens on the board. His light is a single candle or fire in the depths of the room, and his palette is the palette of the textiles the light falls on -- satin with mirror-like reflections, lace, a slashed silk sleeve, a Turkey carpet -- rendered in thin loose paint on small panels, often oval.

The same logic runs through the career in one direction: bright crowded companies at the start, restrained gatherings in the middle, dark boxes with a single light at the end. The repertoire never changes -- tric-trac, cards, smoke, drink, loot, music, courting -- and the refinement is all in the light and the restraint. He is the inventor who never got a late style: ten years, one room, the light deepening until plague stopped it.

The failures of the record are part of the logic too. Authorship hides on a drum, in a carpet, on a tiled floor, and has to be found by a restorer or a cataloguer; a database merges him with his co-inventor and gives him Codde's death year; the catalogue raisonne that would settle his oeuvre has been in preparation since at least 2013. A painter whose pictures kept losing his name is a painter the museum had to re-find, one monogram at a time -- and the re-finding always came through the same two tells: the textiles and the dark.

## Quotes

Rosen 2006, "The Dutch Guardroom Scene of the Golden Age: A Definition", Artibus et Historiae 27, no. 53, p. 171 (abstract, search result): "A guardroom scene is a genre painting depicting an interior of a temporary nature hosting officers, soldiers and their camp followers. These would be depicted passing their leisure time surrounded by military items and booty." / The scene "is free of any violent action, and it usually appears respectable and in some cases even merry"; it "usually does not revolve around a clear subject matter, but is a collection of motifs, anecdotes, and notions"; it "gave the middle-class man, nesting safely at home, a restrained drama devoid of violence while touching his social sensitivity."

## Network

The family web first. Duyster married Margrieta Kick, and Simon Kick married Duyster's sister, in a double wedding on 5 September 1631, and both couples lived in De Duystere Werelt. Duyster's death of plague in 1635 is the event Rosen's 2021 synopsis says drove Simon, 32, to take up painting. Simon's son Cornelis (#563) was trained under his father from c. 1650 in genre, portraits, schuttersstukken and guardrooms, then turned to flowers. Margrieta Kick was, per Christie's lot essay (snippet-level), "a cousin of the Haarlem painters Jan and Solomon de Bray" -- a de Bray link not in the #564 material.

The inventors: Willem Cornelisz Duyster (1599-1635) and Pieter Codde (11 December 1599 - 12 October 1678) "developed and popularised the guardroom scene" (NGI). Codde was, per Rosen via Steigrad, "the most dominant and innovative" of the group that formulated it, and "the most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s". Codde's pupil was Duyster, "who was about the same age" -- pupil and exact contemporary at once -- and the two came to blows with tin pitchers at Van Someren's Meerhuysen party in 1625. Codde's guardroom mechanism, per Rosen via Steigrad: scenes "devoid of violence... focusing on soldiers' off-duty hours spent gambling, drinking, sleeping, and carousing with camp followers"; class distinctions maintained, the commander always recognisable by superior dress, attitude, prominent placement; "an element of theatricality on a stage-like setting... with spot lighting". In 1637 Codde was commissioned to complete Hals's unfinished schutterstuk, The Meagre Company -- Hals painted only the left half; Codde's work is "recognizably smoother".

The circle, three cities in one sentence (Palamedesz. article): Codde, Duyster and Kick in Amsterdam; Jacob Duck in Utrecht; Palamedesz. and Jacob van Velsen in Delft. Duck (c. 1600-1667), the most successful exponent, painted guardrooms from 1628 to 1655, often wide and tall hallways or church interiors; he modelled his style on both Codde and Duyster (Salomon via Sotheby's), and was influenced by Codde, not his pupil. Palamedesz. (bapt. 9 November 1602 - 27 November 1673), the Delft branch, painted guardrooms into the 1660s -- a large hall of a ruin or castle, open at the rear, a large fireplace, a chubby officer in a tall hat at the centre -- and his guardrooms are unique in including women as mothers, not courtesans: "masculinity pacified". Van Velsen (1597-1656) is the one documented influence running into Duyster: the RKD says he influenced Duyster. Quast (bapt. 1605-1647) is the caricature and satire branch, the Dutch Callot, who competed with Codde and Duyster in the same market and shared the deserted-church guardroom with Duck and Potter. Pieter Symonsz Potter (1597-1652), father of Paulus Potter, painted a Guardroom Interior (Schwerin) and took in Codde's divorced wife as a neighbour, then a household -- and, snippet-level (WGA biography), "served as a witness for the baptism of de Heem's son Cornelis in Leiden in 1631", touching the de Heem line a generation before Cornelis Kick does. Gerard ter Borch (1617-1681) is the afterlife: his early-1640s guardrooms, painted after visits to Amsterdam in the early 1630s where he may have seen Duyster's and Codde's, carried the genre's lessons -- small groups, full-length figures, upright format, subtle psychological interplay -- into the rarefied domestic interiors of the 1650s and 1660s.

The named influences on Duyster, per the National Gallery: Dirck Hals, Anthonie Palamedesz., Jacob van Velsen and Thomas de Keyser. The host of the 1625 brawl, Barent van Someren (1572-1632), is also Codde's likeliest teacher (with Cornelis van der Voort). No documented teacher for Duyster beyond the hedged Codde line; no documented pupil; no documented workshop. The early influence is the merry-company circle, not a master.
