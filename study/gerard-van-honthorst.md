# Gerard van Honthorst: Study Notes

**Site:** Gerard (Gerrit) van Honthorst (4 November 1592 - 27 April 1656), Dutch Golden Age painter of Utrecht, the candlelight Caravaggist, and the most internationally successful Dutch artist of his time. Born in Utrecht, son of Herman Gerritsz. van Honthorst, a textile and tapestry designer (kleerschrijver) and decorative painter; his grandfather Gerrit Huygensz. was also a textile designer, and the family was large, Catholic, and full of artists. Trained first under his father, then in the Utrecht studio of Abraham Bloemaert from around 1606. Travelled to Italy: the year of arrival is uncertain and must not be stated flatly - the earliest document is a drawing dated 1616 after Caravaggio's Martyrdom of St Peter; Grove says he must have travelled between c. 1610 and 1615, probably closer to 1610; Sotheby's says around 1613; Gerson says 1609 (see C368). In Rome he lodged in the palace of the Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani, the great Caravaggio collector, painted Christ Before the High Priest (c. 1617) for him, and also worked for Cardinal Scipione Borghese and Cosimo II de' Medici. He left Rome in spring 1620 and his homecoming was celebrated on 26 July 1620 at the inn 'De Poortgen' in Utrecht. He married Sophia Coopmans, daughter of a wine merchant, in October 1620 - not 1623, and not his cousin (see C370). He joined the Utrecht Guild of St Luke in 1622 and served as dean in 1625, 1626, 1628 and 1629. He bought a large house on the Domkerkhof in 1627 to house his atelier, which held about 24-25 students at a time, each paying 100 guilders a year; Joachim von Sandrart studied with him c. 1625-1628. Rubens visited his studio in the summer of 1627 (Sandart's account, 1626 against 1627 in the sources - see C384). In England from April to early December 1628 he painted the huge allegory Mercury Presenting the Liberal Arts to Apollo and Diana (3.57 x 6.40 m, Hampton Court) for Charles I for the Banqueting House, Whitehall; Sandrart says he was paid 3,000 guilders plus many costly gifts, and on 28 November 1628 he was made an English citizen and provided with a lifetime pension of 100 pounds a year (Grove/Slatkes - single-sourced, see C367 in the Doorway). From 1630 he was court painter to the exiled King and Queen of Bohemia, Frederick V and Elizabeth Stuart, in The Hague; Elizabeth employed him as drawing-master for her children. First commissions from Christian IV of Denmark came from 1635 for Kronborg Castle. He moved to The Hague in 1637, joined the Hague Guild of St Luke, served as dean in 1640, painted the state double portrait of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia van Solms c. 1637-1638, and worked on the palaces Honselaarsdijk, Huis ter Nieuburch (1636-39) and Huis ten Bosch (1649-50), contributing allegories to the Oranjezaal, including the Allegory on the Marriage of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia van Solms and Amalia's portrait in the dome apex. He retired to Utrecht in 1652; few late works, almost all portraits, including pendant portraits of himself and his wife (1655) marking their 35th wedding anniversary. He died in Utrecht on 27 April 1656 and was buried in the Catharijnekerk. His younger brother Willem van Honthorst (1594-1666), also a portrait painter, was a pupil of Bloemaert and of Gerrit, and later court painter in Berlin to Louise Henriette of Nassau. He became extremely rich: he sold his Hague house to the city in 1645 for 17,250 guilders and lent Elizabeth, Princess of Hohenzollern, 35,000 guilders; he lived "like a grand seigneur rather than an artist or artisan" (Grove). His late style was criticised as "stiff" and "slick" ("stijve gladdicheyt") and he was esteemed a "much less great master than themselves" by his fellow artists working on the Oranjezaal (Bikker 2007, citing Van Hoogstraten 1678 and a 1649 letter from Constantijn Huygens to Amalia van Solms). ## M1. One in-picture candle as the sole light source

What it is: the scene is lit by a single candle that is itself visible inside the picture, placed on a table between the antagonists, and no window, lamp or second flame competes with it. Watchable behaviour: every face, gesture and fold is lit from that one point; everything else sinks into dark; the candle becomes the compositional centre and the moral centre at once. Sources: Web Gallery of Art on Christ Before the High Priest ("the visibility of the life-size figures depends entirely on that single candle flame"); EN Wikipedia Christ Before the High Priest ("a candle provides the only light"); EN Wikipedia Honthorst ("often painting scenes illuminated by a single candle"). Corroborated across sources.

**Takeaway:** one flame, one light, one judgement: the candle is the picture's lamp and its judge at once.

## M2. Even falloff: light that weakens evenly with distance, with a reddish cast

What it is: the candle's gleam is painted as illuminating the room with evenly decreasing intensity until its force is spent in the dark, and the falloff justifies a reddish cast over all the colours. Watchable behaviour: figures near the flame are warm and fully modelled; figures a step further back lose detail and colour in a smooth gradient; the far edges are not black paint but spent light. Source: Web Gallery of Art ("Its gleam unifies the whole, by giving the impression of illuminating the entire room with evenly decreasing intensity until its force is spent in the dark, and by justifying the reddish cast of all the colours"). SINGLE SOURCE (WGA close reading) - treat as one critic's reading, not a technical finding.

**Takeaway:** the dark is not painted on; it is the light running out.

## M3. A white surface as a secondary radiator

What it is: a pale or white garment is placed to catch and throw back more light than its surroundings, so the light appears to come from the figure rather than from the flame. Watchable behaviour: Christ's torn white robe reflects more light than Caiaphas's furred cloak, so the submissive prisoner reads as the brightest figure and the source of light in the room. Source: Web Gallery of Art ("Christ's white robe, torn from his shoulder when he was made prisoner, reflects more light than the priest's furred cloak - so that light seems to radiate from him"). SINGLE SOURCE (WGA close reading).

**Takeaway:** the prisoner outshines the judge because his shirt is the second lamp.

## M4. Tenebrism: the background dissolved into near-total dark

What it is: large areas of the canvas are plunged into shadow so that figures are sculpted out of darkness rather than set against a place. Watchable behaviour: backgrounds recede into near-total darkness, faces and hands emerge into the warm glow, and the viewer's eye is forced onto the lit figures because there is literally nothing else to look at. Sources: Grove/Dayton ("His predilection for turning the great Italian painter's dramatic patterns of natural light and shadow into nocturnal scenes"); niceartgallery search result ("His figures often emerge into the light, their faces and gestures animated by the warm glow, while backgrounds recede into near-total darkness"). Corroborated.

**Takeaway:** the dark is the room; the light is the only furniture.

## M5. Raking daylight as the Caravaggio alternative (not every Roman work is a night scene)

What it is: in some Roman-period works Honthorst uses dramatic raking daylight entering from one side, on the model of Caravaggio's Calling of St Matthew, instead of artificial light. Watchable behaviour: a shaft of daylight cuts across the picture from the right, picking out figures in sequence and leaving the rest in shadow, so the scene reads as a daytime interior with the same high-contrast grammar as the candle pieces. Source: Grove/Dayton on the Liberation of St Peter (c. 1616-20, Berlin): "use the dramatic raking daylight often found in Roman period paintings by Caravaggio, for example the influential Calling of St Matthew... in which the light enters the composition from the right as it does in the van Honthorst painting". SINGLE SOURCE (Grove/Dayton).

**Takeaway:** the same grammar works in daylight; the candle is a choice, not a crutch.

## M6. Di sotto in su (steep from-below) perspective in musical subjects

What it is: musical pictures are built with the steep foreshortened perspective of Italian ceiling and wall frescoes, as if the viewer is looking up at singers and players from below. Watchable behaviour: instruments and faces tilt down toward the viewer, the table edge and bodies loom overhead, and a flat wall picture feels like a ceiling fragment. Sources: Grove/Dayton ("a number of musical subjects... utilize the steep di sotto in su perspective associated with Italian ceiling and wall frescoes. The most interesting and unusual of these paintings is the Musical Ceiling (1622; Malibu, Getty Mus.), actually a fragment of what must have been a much larger ceiling decoration"). SINGLE SOURCE (Grove/Dayton).

**Takeaway:** the musicians are overhead, not opposite: the picture is a ceiling that fell onto the wall.

## M7. A window-like architectural frame that makes a flat picture into an illusionistic space

What it is: in single-figure compositions Honthorst adds a painted architectural framework around the figure, like a window the sitter leans out of. Watchable behaviour: the musician seems to occupy a real space just behind the picture plane, leaning toward the viewer, while the dark behind him reads as a room rather than a backdrop. Source: Grove/Dayton on the Merry Violinist (1623) and its pendant the Singing Flautist: "Van Honthorst added a strong sense of illusionistic space, however, by providing a window-like architectural framework. Neither painting uses the dramatic patterns of light and shade characteristic of van Honthorst's earlier development." SINGLE SOURCE (Grove/Dayton).

**Takeaway:** the frame is not decoration; it is the room the figure leans out of.

## M8. Compact half-length format with contrasting physiognomic types and profil perdu

What it is: genre pictures are cropped to three half-length figures in a shallow space, built from deliberately contrasting face types (young/seductive vs old/watchful), with at least one face turned away in three-quarter profile (profil perdu, after Manfredi). Watchable behaviour: the transaction on the table - purse, lute, cleavage - is pushed to the front plane; the old matchmaker watches from the edge; youth and age, light and dark, are paired as contrasts. Sources: Grove/Dayton on The Procuress (1625): "a typically Utrecht Caravaggesque composition on panel, with a candle as light source, contrasting physiognomic types, a Manfredi-inspired profil perdu and a compact, half-length compositional format"; EN Wikipedia The Matchmaker: "The painting abounds in contrasts, not only between light and darkness, but also between youth and old age." Corroborated.

**Takeaway:** three faces, one transaction: youth sells, age watches, the candle prices it.

## M9. Crisp paint surface with sharper outlines and edges from c. 1625

What it is: from about 1625 his handling tightens - a crisp paint surface and sharper outlines and edges replace the softer modelling of the early 1620s, across both panel and canvas, genre and pastoral alike. Watchable behaviour: contours read as clean cut-outs, fabrics and feathers have hard edges, and the picture looks polished rather than painterly even where the subject is a tavern scene. Source: Grove/Dayton on the two 1625 pictures (The Procuress and Granida and Daifilo): "both pictures from 1625 reveal a crispness in the paint surface, and sharper outlines and edges than van Honthorst's earlier works. This stylistic tendency was to dominate his later artistic production." SINGLE SOURCE (Grove/Dayton).

**Takeaway:** from 1625 the edge does the work the brush used to do.

## M10. Smooth paint with visible brushwork and impasto highlights (late portrait handling)

What it is: in the late court portraits the paint is in general smoothly applied, with brushstrokes visible in the costume and impasto highlights - a smooth face against a more worked costume. Watchable behaviour: the sitter's skin reads as polished and still, while the lace, silk and jewellery catch the light with thicker, visible strokes; the contrast between still face and busy costume is itself the technique. Source: Rijksmuseum technical notes on the Amalia van Solms portrait (workshop): "The paint has in general been smoothly applied, with brushstrokes visible in the costume and impasto highlights." SINGLE SOURCE (one technical examination of a workshop picture) - do not generalise to autograph works without further evidence.

**Takeaway:** the face is polished still; the dress does the painting.

## M11. Drawing-based working method after c. 1625, following Bloemaert not Caravaggio

What it is: unlike Caravaggio, who eschewed drawing in favour of direct painting, Honthorst after about 1625 worked like his teacher Bloemaert: preparatory drawings first, then the painting. Watchable behaviour: compositions are pre-planned and repeatable (several drawings can be related to specific paintings), and the workshop could produce replicas because the design existed on paper before paint. Sources: Grove/Dayton ("especially after c. 1625 he followed the working methods of his teacher Abraham Bloemaert rather than those of Caravaggio, who eschewed drawing in favour of direct painting"); Sotheby's lot 72 note (Judson and Ekkart's catalogue raisonne lists only 58 surviving drawings). Corroborated.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is the mould; the painting is the casting. The workshop could repeat what Caravaggio could only do once.

## M12. Bold brown washes for shadow in drawings

What it is: in the drawings, the contrasting areas of shadow are rendered in bold brown washes rather than in hatching or black chalk alone. Watchable behaviour: the drawing already looks like a candlelit scene - warm dark pools against pale paper - before any paint is applied; the wash does the work the candle will do in the painting. Source: Grove/Dayton: "Most of the drawings, however, reveal similar preoccupations with Caravaggesque themes and artificial light effects, the contrasting areas of shadow being rendered in bold brown washes, as in Brothel Scene (c. 1623; Oxford, Ashmolean) and Old Woman Illuminating a Young Girl with a Candle (c. 1625; Leipzig, Mus. Bild. Kst.)." SINGLE SOURCE (Grove/Dayton).

**Takeaway:** the candle is rehearsed in wash before it is painted in oil.

## M13. Portrait historie: sitters painted as gods and myth figures

What it is: the portrait is staged as a mythological or historical scene, with the sitter costumed and posed as Apollo, Diana, Mercury or a pastoral figure, rather than shown as themselves in contemporary dress. Watchable behaviour: Charles I appears as Apollo and Henrietta Maria as Diana in the clouds, Buckingham as Mercury presenting the Liberal Arts; the children of the King of Bohemia appear as Diana and Apollo; Amalia van Solms appears as Diana. The likeness is preserved but the identity is doubled. Sources: EN Wikipedia Honthorst (Hampton Court allegory; The Four Eldest Children of the King of Bohemia); Grove/Dayton (historiated portrait of Charles I, Henrietta Maria and Buckingham as Mercury Presenting the Liberal Arts to Apollo and Diana, 3.57 x 6.40 m); CODART (Amalia as Diana); Helmus blurb via search result ("Honthorst played a key role in the development of both the pastoral and allegorical portrait, as well as the so-called portrait historie"). Corroborated.

**Takeaway:** the sitter keeps their face and borrows a god's job: the portrait is a likeness wearing a myth.

## M14. Light as compositional structure and psychology, not just mood

What it is: in the night scenes the single light source does three jobs at once: it sets the mood, it structures the composition (what is seen first, second, not at all), and it deepens the psychology (who is exposed, who hides, who radiates). Watchable behaviour: the brightest figure is the psychological centre even when the story says otherwise (the prisoner outshines the judge); the order in which faces emerge from the dark is the order in which the viewer judges them. Sources: Helmus book blurb via search result ("dramatic scenes illuminated by a single artificial light source in which light not only sets the mood but also structures the composition and deepens the psychology of the representation"); Web Gallery of Art makes the same point concretely for Christ Before the High Priest (M1-M3). FLAG: the general formulation rests on a single source, a publisher's blurb for Helmus's book (search result only, not read end to end); the concrete evidence is the WGA close reading.

**Takeaway:** the light decides the verdict before the viewer does: first lit, first judged.

## M15. Large-scale decorative allegory and illusionistic ceilings

What it is: Honthorst scales the candlelight grammar up to palace decoration - illusionistic ceilings with flying putti carrying royal monograms, and multi-metre allegorical wall paintings in which the court itself is the subject. Watchable behaviour: putti fly overhead carrying the king's cipher; whole walls become triumphal or marital allegories; the viewer stands inside the picture rather than in front of it. Sources: Grove/Dayton (6,800 guilders for ceiling decorations at Rijswijk; Kronborg: "four illusionistic ceiling paintings depicting flying putti carrying royal monograms"; Oranjezaal allegories; the 1622 Musical Ceiling fragment suggesting he decorated an entire room in the Italian manner as early as 1622); Smarthistory (Oranjezaal: Allegory on the Marriage, 7.5 x 3 m; Amalia and her Daughters watching the Triumph, 382 x 200 cm). Corroborated.

**Takeaway:** the candle grows into a ceiling: the court itself becomes the picture, and the viewer stands inside it.

### Rome (c. 1616-1620)

**W1 -- The Liberation of St Peter (St Peter Being Freed from Prison), 1616-1618.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 129 x 179 cm (secondary sources only; the Berlin museum catalogue page was not read in this leg). Gemaldegalerie, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin. His earliest known commission in Rome, painted for the Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani, the great Caravaggio collector in whose palace Honthorst lodged. The angel's gesture is borrowed directly from Caravaggio's Calling of St Matthew in San Luigi dei Francesi. It was hailed as a masterpiece and recurs in numerous replicas and copies (13 examples known, per Dorotheum). This is the founding work of his Roman reputation: a biblical prison break staged as a single burst of light in darkness. Attribution: autograph (with many replicas and copies).

**W2 -- Christ Before the High Priest, c. 1617.** Oil on canvas. **[VERIFIED]** 272 x 183 cm (National Gallery, London, NG3679). National Gallery, London (Room 24); bought 1922. The monumental Roman showpiece, painted while lodged with Giustiniani. A single candle on the central table lights only the faces of Christ and Caiaphas; everything else falls away. The National Gallery notes it is shaped like an altarpiece but was made for a private palace gallery, not a church. It is the clearest statement of the candle system: one light source, symmetrical composition, psychological confrontation. Attribution: autograph.

**W3 -- Samson and Delilah, c. 1616.** Oil on canvas. **[VERIFIED]** 129 x 94 cm unframed (Cleveland Museum of Art, accession 1968.23). Cleveland Museum of Art, Cleveland, Ohio. An early Roman biblical scene reduced to three closely packed figures lit by a single candle. The Cleveland entry stresses how the old woman's silencing gesture pulls the viewer into complicity with Delilah's deceit. It shows Honthorst already using candlelight as a narrative device, not decoration: the furtive act needs the dark. Attribution: autograph.

**W4 -- Adoration of the Child, c. 1619-1620 (Uffizi); 1619-1621 per Wikipedia.** Oil on canvas. **[VERIFIED]** 95.5 x 131 cm (Uffizi, Florence, inv. 1890 no. 739). Uffizi, Florence (room E6, Painting by Candlelight). The most famous of his night Nativities. Here the light source is the Child himself: divine light radiates from the newborn's body and softens the Virgin's face, while Joseph leans on his cane and two angels bend over the manger. Bought by Grand Duke Cosimo II de' Medici in 1620. The Uffizi notes the models look taken from real life, including apprentices as angels. This is the "child as lamp" mechanism he would repeat and exaggerate the next year in the Adoration of the Shepherds (see uncounted list). Attribution: autograph.

**W5 -- Christ Crowned with Thorns, 1614.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** - no reliable figure retrieved; one AI-generated page gives 173.5 x 222.3 cm, which is not trusted. J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles (inv. 90.PA.26). An early, large Roman Passion scene, painted before the works above, showing the Caravaggesque programme already in place: realistic, bruised flesh, dramatic chiaroscuro, a bowed head as the emotional centre. Note there is a separate, later version of the same subject in the Rijksmuseum (c. 1622, SK-A-4837, 192 x 222 cm); the two must not be confused (see uncounted list). Attribution: autograph.

### Utrecht, first decade back (1620-1625)

**W6 -- Merry Company (also called The Prodigal Son / The Return / Celebratory Party), 1622.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 132.8 x 196.6 cm (Wikipedia infobox only; the Alte Pinakothek catalogue page was not read). Alte Pinakothek, Munich (inv. 1312). The homecoming picture. Honthorst returned to Utrecht in 1620 and was welcomed at a party in the tavern "Het Poortgen" on 26 July 1620; this painting may depict that gathering, with the central figure in the blue shirt possibly a self-portrait. He married Sophia Coopmans, daughter of a wine merchant, that October; the party was held at her mother's inn. The flickering candle held by an old woman, set against three beautiful young women, makes transience and vanity the theme. It marks the pivot from Roman religious drama to Dutch tavern genre, keeping the candlelight. Attribution: autograph.

**W7 -- Musical Group on a Balcony, 1622.** Oil on wood (per Fondation Custodia, citing Judson/Ekkart 1999). Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 309 x 114 cm (Getty catalogue page not read; figure from Judson/Ekkart 1999 via Fondation Custodia). J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles (inv. 70.PB.34). An illusionistic ceiling painted for Honthorst's own house, the earliest of its type made in the Netherlands. Figures seen from below burst through the flat panel by careful perspective calculation; a parrot and a dog gaze down from their perches. Possibly part of an allegory of Harmony and originally wider and twice as long. The head of the man at the top is a modern addition. This is Honthorst the decorator and perspective engineer, a skill learned from Italian ceilings and new to Dutch painting. Attribution: autograph.

**W8 -- King David Playing the Harp, 1622.** Oil on canvas (per Google Arts and Culture / Centraal Museum). Dimensions **[VERIFIED]** 65 x 81 cm (Google Arts and Culture, Centraal Museum feed). Centraal Museum, Utrecht (inv. 8670). A small, richly coloured musician picture from the first Utrecht years, showing the warmer, more saturated palette he developed after Rome. David as musician-king bridges his religious and musical subjects: a biblical figure treated as a performer, lit bright rather than nocturnal. It is the hinge between the dark Roman works and the bright Utrecht merry companies. Attribution: autograph.

**W9 -- The Merry Fiddler (Een vrolijke vioolspeler), 1623.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[VERIFIED]** 107.2 x 88.3 cm (Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, SK-A-180). Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. The signature single-figure entertainer. A man in exuberant, Italianate dress leans out of a window from behind a carpet and raises a glass to the viewer, as if stepping out of the frame. The Rijksmuseum stresses the direct address: he seeks contact with the spectator. This direct, smiling address to the viewer becomes his commercial trademark in the 1620s. (Leg 2's claim that 1623 was the year he married and became president of the guild is wrong: he married in 1620 and was dean of the Utrecht guild in 1625 - see C396.) Attribution: autograph.

**W10 -- The Concert, 1623.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 123 x 206 cm (1.23 x 2.06 m) (National Gallery of Art catalogue page not read; figure from Wikipedia and the 2013 Washington Post acquisition report). National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C. (acc. 2013.38.1). A large, daylight merry company of five musicians and three bystanders, bought by the NGA in November 2013 from a French private collection after not being on public view since 1795. First recorded in a 1632 inventory of a palace of Frederik Hendrik in The Hague; seized by Napoleonic troops in 1795 with some 200 other Dutch paintings. It proves the Utrecht merry company could work at large scale in broad daylight, not only by candlelight, and it documents his early court connection to the House of Orange. Attribution: autograph.

**W11 -- Saint Sebastian, c. 1623.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 101 x 117 cm (secondary sources only; the National Gallery catalogue page was not read). National Gallery, London (NG4503). His most sensuous religious body. Sebastian slumps, arrow-pierced, against the tree, skin tones carrying the picture. A contemporary scholarly note records the telling detail that blood from the leg wound runs in two directions: one from when he stood, one from after he slumped. Dated to the Rome/Utrecht boundary (painted in Rome or Utrecht, likely seen in Utrecht after 1620). It shows the Caravaggesque body surviving into the Utrecht period, now with an uncompromising naturalism. Attribution: autograph.

**W12 -- A Merry Group behind a Balustrade, c. 1623-24.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[VERIFIED]** 99.7 x 138.4 cm (Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, acc. 2019.2091). Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (gift of Rose-Marie and Eijk van Otterloo, 2019). The balustrade device at its most theatrical. A violinist and a lutenist make music behind a ledge while a third figure raises a glass and sings; the closeness to the ledge and the dramatic lighting create immediacy, as if the musicians are performing at the viewer's window. The woman's cleavage, fanciful dress and feathered turban mark her as a prostitute. A nearly identical version sits in the Musee des Beaux-Arts de Lyon (Les chanteurs, inv. H 657), and the MFA notes it has not been possible to determine with certainty which of the two the pre-1875 provenance belongs to. Signed "GHonthorst fec" in ligature. Attribution: autograph.

**W13 -- Granida and Daifilo, 1625.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 144.7 x 179 cm (Wikidata/Commons listing; some secondary sources give 145 x 179 cm; the Centraal Museum catalogue page was not read). Centraal Museum, Utrecht (inv. 5571). The classicising turn. From Pieter Cornelisz Hooft's pastoral play Granida (performed 1605, published 1615), the shepherd Daifilo meets the lost princess Granida. Google Arts and Culture notes that Honthorst, "mostly known for his Caravaggist work, shows in this painting his abilities as a full-fledged Classicist." Large, daylight, Arcadian, smooth: the future court painter is visible here, two years before London. The pastoral subject also becomes the standard disguise for his court portraits (Diana, Apollo, shepherds). Attribution: autograph.

**W14 -- The Procuress (De koppelaarster), also called The Matchmaker, 1625.** Oil on panel (Wikipedia) - CONFLICT: the Wikidata/Commons painting list says oil on canvas. See C377. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 71 x 104 cm (the Centraal Museum page read in this leg returned documentation only, no object dimensions). Centraal Museum, Utrecht (inv. 10786); bought 1951 with help of the Vereniging Rembrandt. His most famous single image. A young woman with a provocative neckline and seductive smile, a client holding a purse, and a toothless old matchmaker watching from the left; a candle on the table lights the woman's cleavage and feathered clothes. The lute is the erotic signal. The same model appears in other Honthorst paintings of this year (see W15). It is the peak of the Utrecht courtesan picture: shallow space, hard contrasts, youth against age, commerce against desire. Attribution: autograph.

**W15 -- Smiling Girl, a Courtesan, Holding an Obscene Image, 1625.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 64.1 x 81.3 cm (secondary source only; the Saint Louis Art Museum page read in this leg gave provenance but no dimensions). Saint Louis Art Museum, St Louis (acc. 63:1954; on view in Gallery 236). The companion piece to W14, same year, thought to use the same model. A prostitute in gold and red holds up a medallion showing a seated naked woman captioned in Dutch "Wie kent mijn naers van Afteren" ("Who knows my backside from behind") - an advertisement for her services, as painted likenesses of young women were displayed to advertise availability during the Eighty Years' War. Catalogue raisonne no. 215 (Judson/Ekkart 1999). It is the frankest of his courtesan pictures and shows how the Utrecht market rewarded wit and erotic directness. Attribution: autograph.

**W16 -- The Flea Hunt, 1621 per the Dayton Art Institute; signed and dated 1628 per Sotheby's (see C376 - the date is disputed).** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[VERIFIED]** 132.7 x 199.4 cm (52 1/4 x 78 1/2 in.) (Dayton Art Institute, acc. 1980.2). Dayton Art Institute, Dayton, Ohio. The most literal of his candlelight genre scenes. A young prostitute and an elderly procuress search the sheets for fleas by shielded candlelight, while young male clients watch the nude woman in secret. Honthorst introduced the flea-hunt theme into Netherlandish painting. It is a large horizontal canvas for a sordid, intimate joke, and it shows the voyeur structure that runs through his genre work: the viewer is placed with the peeping clients, not above them. Attribution: autograph.

### London and the court (1628-1653)

**W17 -- Apollo and Diana (The Liberal Arts Presented to King Charles and Henrietta Maria), 1628, inscribed "GHONTHORST f 1628".** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[VERIFIED]** 3.57 x 6.40 m (Grove/Dayton, Slatkes; see C395 - leg 2 marked this NOT VERIFIED, but the Grove text read end to end by leg 1 gives the figure; the fan-wiki 3.73 x 3.17 m is discarded). Hampton Court Palace, Royal Collection (RCIN 405746), on the Queen's Staircase. The most ambitious painting of his nine months in London (April-December 1628) working for Charles I. Commissioned probably by the Duke of Buckingham, who appears as Mercury leading the seven Liberal Arts out of a dark cave into the light of royal patronage, presenting them to Charles as Apollo and Henrietta Maria as Diana. Buckingham was assassinated in August 1628, probably while the work was in progress. It is a painted panegyric in the Rubensian mode, answering Rubens' Marie de' Medici cycle, and it marks Honthorst's arrival as an international court allegorist, not just a candlelight painter. Attribution: autograph.

**W18 -- The Four Eldest Children of the King and Queen of Bohemia, 1631.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 214.8 x 173.5 cm (secondary source only; the Royal Collection catalogue page was not read). Royal Collection, Windsor Castle (formerly Hampton Court). The intimate court group portrait. Four children of the exiled Frederick V and Elizabeth of Bohemia in a wooded setting, the two eldest as Diana (bow and arrow, crescent moon headdress) and Apollo. Honthorst was drawing-master to Elizabeth's children and became court painter to the exiled king and queen in The Hague in 1630. The Arcadian disguise first tried in Granida (W13) is now applied to real royal children: mythology as court portraiture. Attribution: autograph.

**W19 -- Portrait of Frederik Hendrik, Prince of Orange, his wife Amalia van Solms, and their Three Youngest Daughters, 1647.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 353.5 x 267 cm (secondary source only; the Rijksmuseum catalogue page was not read). Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam (SK-A-874). The great state family portrait, and the largest canvas in this catalogue. Frederik Hendrik stands in full armour beside Amalia in a dark elegant gown, their three youngest daughters in front. It was probably executed in 1647, the year Frederik Hendrik died, and it is the source image for the many workshop versions of Amalia's likeness (see uncounted list). Court painting at full scale: no candlelight, no joke, only dynastic display. Attribution: autograph.

**W20 -- Portrait of William III, Prince of Orange, and Maria, Princess of Orange, as Children, 1653.** Oil on canvas. Dimensions **[NOT VERIFIED]** 108.4 x 130.7 cm (secondary source only; the Mauritshuis catalogue page was not read). Mauritshuis, The Hague. The late, gentle court portrait, three years before his death. The future William III (born 1650) and his aunt Maria (born 1642) as children in a pastoral setting. It closes the arc: the candlelight painter of Roman taverns ends as the portraitist of the Orange dynasty's children, painting innocence in daylight. His late style was criticised by contemporaries as stiff and slick, but the tenderness of this late double portrait is the counter-evidence. Attribution: autograph.

## Evolution

### Phase 1 - Rome, c. 1616-1620: one candle in a Roman palace

Honthorst is first recorded in Rome in 1616 (the traditional "1610" start is undocumented; see C393). He lodges with the Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani, the great Caravaggio collector, and works for Cardinal Scipione Borghese and Cosimo II de' Medici. The subjects are large religious night scenes (W1, W2, W3, W4, W5): prison breaks, trials, betrayals, Nativities. The system is Caravaggio's tenebrism taken to an extreme of economy: one candle, or the Child as lamp, a few faces, and darkness doing the rest. The angel in W1 borrows Caravaggio's gesture from the Calling of St Matthew. Scale is large (W2 is 272 x 183 cm). Handling is smooth and sculptural, colour rich and Italianate.

### Phase 2 - Utrecht, 1620-1628: the smiling face at the window

He returns in 1620, marries Sophia Coopmans, joins the Guild of St Luke in 1622, and is its dean in 1625, 1626, 1628 and 1629; he runs a workshop with about 24-25 pupils at 100 guilders a year each. The subject matter shifts: religious painting largely gives way to Arcadian and domestic subjects and portraits (Royal Collection Trust). The merry company, the musician, the courtesan and the tavern become his market (W6, W8-W12, W14-W16), while the candle stays as his signature. Two new things appear: the illusionistic ceiling (W7, 1622, the first in the Netherlands) and the classicising pastoral (W13, 1625), both signs of a painter preparing for court work. Colour lightens; daylight enters (W10, W13). The pictures get smaller and more saleable, built around a single smiling face turned to the viewer (W9) or a shallow, stage-like space (W14).

### Phase 3 - London, 1628: the allegorist

Nine months (April-December) working for Charles I, reached through Elizabeth of Bohemia and the Duke of Buckingham. The work is allegory and state portraiture at large scale (W17). Buckingham's assassination mid-commission leaves the picture as a monument to a dead patron. Honthorst returns to the United Provinces the same year but keeps working for the English court.

### Phase 4 - The Hague court, 1630-1656: a whole royal family in daylight

Court painter to the exiled king and queen of Bohemia in 1630; first commissions from Frederik Hendrik's court in the mid-1620s; a second studio in The Hague in 1637; decorations at Honselaarsdijk, Huis ter Nieuburch (1636-39) and Huis ten Bosch (1649-50); principal artist for Christian IV of Denmark's Banqueting Hall at Kronborg in 1637. The Arcadian disguise becomes the court portrait formula (W18). Scale grows again to state size (W19, 353.5 x 267 cm). Light is now even, daylight, classical: the candle is gone. His late style was criticised by fellow artists on the Oranjezaal as stiff and slick ("stijve gladdicheyt"), and he was judged "a much less great master than themselves". The late children's portraits (W20, 1653) are the quiet answer: no drama, only likeness and tenderness. He dies in Utrecht on 27 April 1656 and is buried in the Catharijnekerk.

The arc in one line: one candle in a Roman palace, then a smiling face at a Utrecht window, then a whole royal family in daylight at The Hague.

## Aesthetic logic

Honthorst's career is two systems wearing one name, and the second system quietly eats the first. The first system is the candle: one visible flame, one point of light, and the whole picture organised around what it reaches and what it leaves dark. It is a moral technology as much as a pictorial one - the prisoner outshines the judge, the child is the lamp, the courtesan's cleavage is priced by the flame on the table. The light does the judging, and the painter only has to place the flame and let the room answer. That is why the Roman works feel so certain: the system decides the composition, the psychology and the mood at once, and the painter's job is to keep the single source honest.

The second system is the court: the portrait historie, the pastoral disguise, the multi-metre allegory, the ceiling full of putti carrying a king's cipher. Here the light is even and classical, the candle is gone, and the picture's job is not to judge but to display. The sitter keeps their face and borrows a god's job - Apollo, Diana, Mercury - and the painting becomes a piece of state furniture, 3.57 by 6.40 metres of it, hung on a staircase. The technique follows the market: the drawing-based method after c. 1625 (M11) makes the design repeatable, the workshop of 24-25 pupils makes it scalable, and the crisp surface from 1625 (M9) makes it polished enough for a palace. The candle system could not be scaled - one flame is one flame - but the court system could, and it did: the same Amalia likeness repeated across workshop versions, the same Bohemian children painted for two courts.

That is why the late criticism lands so hard and why it is also beside the point. "Stiff" and "slick" are the words of painters who watched the court system win: the smooth face, the worked costume, the even light - the very finish the market paid for. But the criticism misses what the late portraits keep: the tenderness of W20, two children in a pastoral setting, painted three years before his death, with no drama at all. The candle is gone, but the eye that placed it is still there, and it knows exactly how much light a child's face needs.

And then there is the nickname, the final irony of the career. "Gherardo delle Notti" - Gerard of the Nights - is the name the world remembers him by, and it is not a name anyone in Rome ever called him. His contemporaries called him "Gherardo Fiammingo", Gerrit the Fleming, or "Gherardo Hollandese", Gerrit the Dutchman. The night name only became widespread in the 18th century, after his death. The painter who lit his pictures with a single candle was renamed by the dark itself, a century late. The aesthetic logic of the whole career is in that delay: the candle was the system, the court was the living, and the name arrived only when both were finished - the world remembering the flame long after the painter had put it out and gone to paint kings in daylight.

## Positions

**P1.** Born Utrecht 4 November 1592, died Utrecht 27 April 1656; buried in the Catharijnekerk.

**P2.** Son of Herman Gerritsz. van Honthorst, textile and tapestry designer; grandson of Gerrit Huygensz., also a textile designer.

**P3.** Trained under his father, then Abraham Bloemaert from c. 1606; Rome by 1616 (arrival year uncertain, c. 1610-1613 per Grove/Sotheby's, 1609 per Gerson), left spring 1620.

**P4.** Married Sophia Coopmans, daughter of a wine merchant, October 1620; Utrecht Guild of St Luke 1622, dean 1625, 1626, 1628, 1629; Hague Guild 1637, dean 1640.

**P5.** In England April to early December 1628; made an English citizen 28 November 1628 with a lifetime pension of 100 pounds a year; paid 3,000 guilders plus gifts for the Hampton Court allegory.

**P6.** Court painter to the exiled King and Queen of Bohemia from 1630; court painter to the Princess of Orange from 1637; settled in The Hague 1637-1652, retired to Utrecht 1652.

**P7.** Workshop of about 24-25 students at a time in the mid-1620s, each paying 100 guilders a year; Sandrart pupil c. 1625-1628; second studio in The Hague 1637.

**P12.** "Gerrit Hermansz. was the most successful artist in the family and the most famous member of the group of Utrecht caravaggisti, the Dutch followers of Caravaggio" (Grove Art Online, Slatkes).

### Collaborators and early influences

Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio, 1571-1610): the source of the whole idiom; Honthorst never met him (Caravaggio fled Rome 1606; Honthorst arrived c. 1610-1616) but worked from his pictures, above all the Conversion of St. Paul in Santa Maria del Popolo. Bartolomeo Manfredi (1582-1622): Caravaggio's most important follower in Rome; Honthorst was "especially influenced by" Manfredi (Wikipedia); "His entire interest - and here he was at one with Bartolomeo Manfredi - was aimed at making this effect [of light and shade]" (Gerson Digital Italy); Manfredi's interpretation of Caravaggio shaped Honthorst's and Baburen's Utrecht work. His 'Manfrediana methodus' (half-length figures, actual size) was taken up by Baburen, Honthorst and ter Brugghen among others. The Carracci: Honthorst was "especially influenced by the contemporary artists, including Caravaggio, Bartolomeo Manfredi and the Carracci" (Wikipedia). Orazio Gentileschi (1563-1639): Italian Caravaggist whose works ter Brugghen saw in Rome/Naples and whose influence runs through the Utrecht group. Valentin de Boulogne (Valentin de Boullogne in Dayton's spelling): French Caravaggist in Rome; Honthorst's Denial of St Peter (c. 1623) "reveals the influence of the French Caravaggesque artist Valentin de Boullogne" (Dayton).

## Corrections
- C366. The doorway tally. The #527 run recorded the streak at one hundred and one holds and twenty-four non-holds after its check. This run's check is the one-hundred-and-forty-ninth in a row and is recorded as a HOLD on the substance (one-hundred-and-second hold) and a FAIL on the letter (twenty-fifth non-hold). The counting note in the Doorway section explains why 102 + 25 does not sum to 149.
- C367. The top pick for #529, verified first-hand. Leg 3 verified Hendrick ter Brugghen not studied (zero study headers; zero whole-log mentions; no local file; not carried in #520-#528). The parent re-verified first-hand (header grep 0, whole-log mentions 0, no local file) and confirms ter Brugghen as top pick for #529: Bloemaert pupil (National Gallery), in Rome c. 1604 within Caravaggio's lifetime, "the first important painter influenced by Caravaggio to return to Holland" (National Gallery), returned to Utrecht by 1615, worked with Honthorst on his return (Wikipedia). Caveat: the doorway's "reached Rome while Caravaggio was still alive" holds as the sourced claim - Wikipedia: "it is certain that he was the only Dutch painter in Rome during Caravaggio's lifetime" - but newer scholarship says he may have been in Italy only in 1606, and he never met Caravaggio in Rome (Caravaggio fled in 1606). His birthplace is unresolved: The Hague (Leiden Collection, Oberlin), Deventer (Britannica), Overijssel (Wikipedia), "not known for sure" (Thyssen). Do not state a birthplace or a Rome date flatly in #529.
- C368. Rome arrival range unresolved. 1616 is the year of the earliest surviving document (a drawing after Caravaggio, dated 1616), not the arrival year. Grove says he must have travelled to Italy between c. 1610 and 1615, probably closer to 1610; Sotheby's says around 1613; Google Arts and Culture says the year of arrival is uncertain; Gerson Digital Italy says he arrived in Rome in 1609 and stayed almost twelve years, travelling home via Florence in 1621. The range 1609-1616 is unresolved in sources read; the study states the range, not a single year. He left Rome in spring 1620 (all legs agree).
- C369. The nickname "Gherardo delle Notti" is an 18th-century label, not a contemporary Roman nickname. Lincoln's Getty Provenance Index study (126 entries) found "delle notti" variants exclusively after his death; Italian documents before 1656 call him "Gherardo Fiammingo" ("Gerrit the Fleming") or "Gherardo Hollandese" ("Gerrit the Dutchman"); the nickname only came into widespread use in the 18th century (Wikipedia, Lincoln blog). Grove's Q1 wording ("won him the Italian nickname") repeats the conventional error; the study quotes it as Grove's wording, not as fact.
- C370. Marriage: October 1620, not 1623, and no cousin. Dayton/Grove: he married Sophia Coopmans "in October of the same year" as the spring-1620 return; WGA agrees; the 1655 pendant portraits celebrate the 35th wedding anniversary (1655 - 35 = 1620). Wikipedia's "In 1623, the year of his marriage, he was president of the Guild" is wrong, and the 1911 Britannica's "married his cousin" is unsupported - Sophia Coopmans was the daughter of a wine merchant; no cousin relationship is mentioned in any modern source read. He was dean of the Utrecht guild in 1625, 1626, 1628 and 1629, not president in 1623.
- C371. Birth year: 4 November 1592, not 1590. Modern sources (Wikipedia, Dayton/Grove, Bikker) agree on 4 November 1592. The National Portrait Gallery person page dates him "(1590-1656)" and the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica says "born at Utrecht 1590"; both are old errors. The Uffizi, Google Arts and Culture and Royal Collection Trust headers also repeat 1590. Do not repeat 1590.
- C372. Brother Willem's dates: Willem van Honthorst 1594-1666 (Wikipedia, Dayton), not 1604 as the 1911 Britannica says. The brief's own "1594-1666" is the correct modern dating.
- C373. "Stayed in Rome until 1628" (visual-arts-cork.com, seen in search result) is wrong. He returned to Utrecht in spring 1620 (Wikipedia, Dayton/Grove, WGA, Bikker). The 1628 date confuses the Rome stay with the London visit.
- C374. The Matchmaker and The Procuress are the same painting. The Matchmaker = The Procuress = De koppelaarster, 1625, Centraal Museum inv. 10786. The brief listed both as separate key works; counting both would double-count one physical painting. It appears once here, as W14.
- C375. Samson and Delilah: c. 1616, not c. 1621. The Cleveland Museum of Art dates its painting c. 1616, and Wikidata agrees (1616). The brief's c. 1621 is wrong for the Cleveland painting; 1621 is the date of other works (The Flea Hunt, The Soldier and the Girl), which may be the source of the confusion.
- C376. The Flea Hunt date is disputed. The Dayton Art Institute dates its painting 1621, but Sotheby's (2017, lot 146) states the Dayton painting is "signed and dated 1628" and includes two peeping voyeurs. Both dates are reported in W16; neither is asserted.
- C377. The Procuress medium is disputed. Wikipedia says "oil on wood" (oil on panel); the Wikidata/Commons painting list says "oil on canvas". The Centraal Museum page read in this leg gave no medium. Treat the medium as disputed until the museum catalogue is checked.
- C378. No standalone Charles I / Henrietta Maria portraits verified. Honthorst did not paint a standalone state portrait of Charles I in the sources read. The 1628 English work is the allegory Apollo and Diana (W17), in which Charles appears as Apollo and Henrietta Maria as Diana. Separate portraits of Charles I and Henrietta Maria by Honthorst are not verified in this leg.
- C379. The Rijksmuseum Amalia van Solms portrait is workshop, not 1632 autograph. The Rijksmuseum's Portrait of Amalia van Solms (SK-A-573) is attributed to the workshop of Gerard van Honthorst, dated in or after c. 1651, based on the likeness in W19. The c. 1632 Amalia as Diana is a different painting in the Royal Collections, The Hague (see uncounted list, U10). Do not cite the Rijksmuseum portrait as a 1632 autograph work.
- C380. The Four Eldest Children: date and place. Wikipedia's Royal patronage paragraph places the picture inside the 1628 London visit; the Royal Collection dates it signed and dated 1631 (RCIN 404971); Dayton dates the large Bohemia family picture 1629 (Marienburg version). The 1629-vs-1631 conflict is unresolved in sources read, and neither date supports a 1628 London execution. Do not date the picture to the London visit, and do not place it in The Hague (see D3).
- C381. Buckingham was murdered in August 1628, during Honthorst's London stay. The Hampton Court allegory shows him alive as Mercury. Any claim that the picture postdates his death, or that he sat for it after August 1628, is wrong; the commission predates the murder (Gerson Digital Britain, footnote).
- C382. Leonora Christina portrait attribution conflict. Wikipedia's infobox captions a portrait "by Gerrit van Honthorst (1647), Frederiksborg Museum"; Nordic Women's Literature says "In 1647 the Dutch artist Karel van Mander painted a large full-length portrait of Christian IV's daughter, Leonora Christina (1621-1698)." Two different paintings may be involved, or an attribution dispute; do not assert the Honthorst attribution flatly. Note also that Wikipedia's text says he "painted a portrait of the King's daughter Leonora Christina Ulfeldt while she was in The Hague" - the king is Christian IV of Denmark, not Charles I; the phrasing needs the gloss.
- C383. Stom pupillage contested. The MFAH Houston bulletin calls Stom "the most successful student of the Caravaggesque candlelight scene painter, Gerrit van Honthorst" and asserts "an apprenticeship with Gerrit van Honthorst towards the end of the second decade of the seventeenth century in Rome". Wikipedia says the opposite: "It was traditionally believed that he was a pupil of Gerard van Honthorst... However, van Honthorst himself did not return from Italy to his native city Utrecht until 1620. It is unlikely that Stom would have embarked upon an apprenticeship with van Honthorst when he was already 20 years old... No documentary evidence which can shed light on Stom's apprenticeship has been discovered." Christie's suggests training "possibly under... Hendrick ter Brugghen, or in nearby Amersfoort" instead. Do not state the Honthorst pupillage as fact.
- C384. Rubens visit: year conflict and single sourcing. Wikipedia says "In 1626 Honthorst hosted a dinner for Peter Paul Rubens"; WGA and Dayton put the visit in the summer of 1627, and Dayton adds the account "is difficult to confirm: Rubens stayed at the Utrecht inn owned by ter Brugghen's brother rather than at 'De Poortgen', suggesting that Sandrart, in relating the facts about this visit, was biased in favour of his master." Year 1626 vs 1627 unresolved; the visit itself rests on Sandrart alone.
- C385. "Smooth finish" needs qualification. The single-candle mechanism is well attested (M1). But "smooth finish" is not a neutral description of his technique: the Rijksmuseum technical note on a workshop Amalia portrait says paint "smoothly applied, with brushstrokes visible in the costume and impasto highlights" - smooth in the flesh, worked in the costume. And the late smooth finish was criticised, not praised: his late style was called "stiff" and "slick" ("stijve gladdicheyt"). Grove instead stresses a crisp paint surface with sharper outlines from c. 1625 (M9). Do not present "smooth finish" as an unqualified virtue.
- C386. The Hague court phase is 1637-1652, not his whole later career. He lived in Utrecht until 1637 and only then moved to The Hague, joining the Hague guild in 1637 and serving as dean in 1640; he retired to Utrecht in 1652. He became court painter to the exiled King and Queen of Bohemia in 1630, seven years before the Hague move, and NPG dates his becoming court painter to the princess of Orange to 1637.
- C387. The Bohemian pictures were not all painted at The Hague. The large historiated portrait of Frederick V, Elizabeth and their children (1629, Marienburg) was executed after his return to Utrecht, and the 1631 picture of the king and queen of Bohemia and all their children was also painted after his return. Elizabeth herself was among his drawing pupils in The Hague, not only her children (GAC: "his students included Elizabeth, Queen of Bohemia and her daughters").
- C388. The Laughing Violinist is unverified as a distinct work. Named in the brief but not verified in this leg; likely a confusion with The Merry Fiddler (W9) or The Happy Violinist with a Glass of Wine (1624, Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum). Do not use it as a synonym for W9.
- C389. Slatkes's Bohemian-children identification rejected. Sotheby's lot 36: Slatkes identified the figures in a Honthorst-and-studio grape-presenting picture as the children of Frederick V and Elizabeth; "This particular identification of the figures in the painting has however has been rejected by Judson." Do not use that identification.
- C390. Gerson Digital Britain page 1.3 rendered footnotes only; its main text was not read. Any claim in the study sourced to "Gerson" for the English period should cite the Hearn/van Leeuwen 2022 updating or the Judson/Ekkart catalogue, not this page's unrendered main text.
- C391. Utrecht Caravaggisti membership varies by source. Wikipedia names the other three as Dirk van Baburen, Hendrick ter Brugghen and Jan van Bijlert; the National Gallery of Ireland says "the three artists" Honthorst, ter Brugghen and van Baburen "are jointly referred to as the 'Utrecht Caravaggisti'", omitting van Bijlert. The Mauritshuis Double Portrait of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia (inv. 104) is a copy made by Van Honthorst himself for Constantijn Huygens, the original for the Grote Zaal at Huis ter Nieuburch being lost. Neither is carried in the 20.
- C393. First Roman record 1616, not 1610. He is first recorded in Italy in 1616 (a drawn copy after Caravaggio's Martyrdom of St Peter, dated 1616). The 1610 start date is traditional and undocumented. His documented Roman period is c. 1616-1620.
- C394. Lute Player, 1614, unverified. Cited in the old Britannica and Wikipedia legacy text as the earliest example of his style, but no catalogue entry, inventory number or dimensions were retrieved in this leg, and the Louvre's online collection does not surface it under that title in the sources read. Treat as unverified; see uncounted list, U18.
- C395. Apollo and Diana dimensions, verified from Grove. Leg 2 marked W17 NOT VERIFIED, but the Grove/Dayton text read end to end by leg 1 gives the Hampton Court allegory as 3.57 x 6.40 m. The parent carries the dimensions as VERIFIED from Grove/Slatkes. The fan-wiki figure 3.73 x 3.17 m is discarded.
- C396. Merry Fiddler guild claim corrected. Leg 2's W9 note says the picture was "Painted in the year Honthorst married and became president of the Utrecht Guild of St Luke". Both halves are wrong: he married in October 1620 (see C370) and was dean of the Utrecht guild in 1625, 1626, 1628 and 1629 (Dayton/Grove). The 1623 date of the painting stands; the biographical claim does not. Leg 2's evolution narrative repeats the same error ("by 1623 is its president"); the study's Evolution section gives the correct dates.
