# Bernardo Bertolucci: Study Notes

**Site:** Bernardo Bertolucci (b. Sunday 16 March 1941, Parma, Emilia-Romagna, Kingdom of Italy; d. Monday 26 November 2018, Rome, Lazio, Italy, age 77, of lung cancer), Italian film director and screenwriter -- the poet's son who learned cinema as Pier Paolo Pasolini's first assistant on a film neither of them had ever made, who made the most beautiful fascist in film history at 29, who filmed a rape the actress did not agree to and then spent the rest of his life being asked about it, who was tried, convicted and stripped of his civil rights by the Italian state, and who then went to China, filmed inside the Forbidden City, and won nine Academy Awards including Best Picture and Best Director. Fifteen features in fifty years, from La commare secca (1962) to Me and You (2012), plus the unmade Red Harvest, the abandoned 3D, and a final screenplay filmed eight years after his death. [C32004]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Pier Paolo Pasolini study: Pasolini ends his study as the man who treated a poor face as a sacred painting and a present-day slum as the Holy Land -- and the boy standing beside him on Accattone, holding the script and learning the grammar of film from scratch, was Bertolucci. Where Pasolini stayed with the sacred poor and the non-actor face, Bertolucci took the same long take and the same political fury and aimed it at the bourgeoisie itself: the conformist, the playboy, the emperor, the tourist, the dreamer. The same Rome, the same Cinecitta cutting rooms, the same Morricone at the piano for a while -- but two opposite answers to the same question. Pasolini asked what the poor look like when you frame them as saints. Bertolucci asked what the rich look like when you frame them as prisoners. This study starts where Pasolini's leaves off -- with the apprentice who outgrew the master, outlived him by forty-three years, and then had to live with everything he had filmed in between. ## 1. The poet's son from Parma

He was born in Parma on Sunday 16 March 1941, the elder son of Attilio Bertolucci, a poet, anthologist and film critic, and Ninetta Giovanardi, a teacher born in Australia to an Italian father and an Australian mother of Irish and Scottish descent. [C32015] He was raised in an artistic household in Parma, began writing at fifteen, and made two amateur 16mm shorts in 1956, The Death of a Pig and The Cable, the second of which he also edited and shot himself. [C32016] His younger brother Giuseppe, born 27 February 1947, became a theatre director and playwright and later co-wrote 1900; his cousin Giovanni, born 24 June 1940, became a film producer and produced Partner. [C32017] He attended the Faculty of Modern Literature at the University of Rome from 1958 to 1961 and left without graduating -- his film career as Pasolini's assistant began during those university years, and the degree was never finished because the set had already replaced the lecture hall. [C32018]

His first poetry collection, In cerca del mistero ("In Search of Mystery"), was published by Longanesi in 1962 and won the Premio Viareggio for a first work the same year -- the hinge of the whole early career, a poet's son who wins the top first-book prize at twenty-one and then abandons verse for the camera in the same year. [C32019] The prize itself is flagged: Britannica, encyclopedia.com and Screening the Past all credit the collection and the 1962 first-work prize to Bernardo, but the Wikipedia Viareggio Prize table lists the 1962 first-work recipient as Attilio Bertolucci, his father, for the same title -- one of the two is wrong, the weight of the biographical sources favours Bernardo, and Attilio won the prize in other years (1951, poetry), which may explain a table error; the study follows Bernardo and does not settle it. [C32020] His age at the win is also flagged: one source says he was "only 22 years old" when he won in 1962, but born 16 March 1941 he was twenty-one for most of 1962 and turned twenty-two only in March 1963, unless the prize was awarded in early 1963 for the 1962 year. [C32021] He later said he moved from poetry to cinema because cinema was the "true poetic language", and the claim is visible in the work: his camera movements feel lyrical rather than purely narrative, the tracking shot doing the work a stanza used to do. [C32022]

**Takeaway:** The poet never leaves. The films are poems that learned to move -- the long take is a line of verse held past its expected length, the colour shift is a change of stanza, and the borrowed novel is the found text a poet sets himself to work on. He does not become a filmmaker instead of a poet; he becomes a poet who found a bigger page.

## 2. The apprenticeship: Accattone (1961)

The link was the father: Attilio Bertolucci had helped Pier Paolo Pasolini publish his first novel, and Pasolini reciprocated by hiring Bernardo as his first assistant in Rome on Accattone (1961). [C32023] It is listed in the main article's filmography as his only assistant-director credit, and it is the strangest apprenticeship in Italian cinema: neither man had ever made a film, and Pasolini was working out the grammar of film from scratch while his assistant watched -- one account calls it "like watching the birth of cinema". [C32024] He met his first wife, Adriana Asti, while working as an assistant on Accattone, according to one biography -- she later played Gina in Before the Revolution -- though the Wikipedia pages read do not verify the meeting, and the study carries it as extract-only. [C32025] Pasolini then supplied the story and screenplay for Bertolucci's first feature, so the apprenticeship did not end when the shoot did: the master's material became the student's first film. [C32026]

**Takeaway:** He learns cinema from a man who does not know it either. That is why his films never look schooled: his teacher's first lesson was that the grammar could be invented on the set, in front of the actors, and that a director who had never directed could still direct, provided he knew what he wanted to see. The confidence of the later epics -- the Forbidden City, the five-hour 1900 -- starts here, in a first film made by two beginners.

## 3. La commare secca (The Grim Reaper, 1962): the Rashomon retelling

His first feature, La commare secca, was released on Wednesday 19 September 1962, runs 88 minutes, and is credited to a screenplay by Bertolucci, Sergio Citti and Pasolini, based on Pasolini's short story of the same name, produced by Antonio Cervi, shot by Giovanni Narzisi, edited by Nino Baragli, with music by Piero Piccioni and Carlo Rustichelli. [C32027] The producer's name is flagged as a variant, not a conflict: the main article says "Tonino Cervi" and the film article says "Antonio Cervi", almost certainly the same man, Antonio "Tonino" Cervi. [C32028] The plot is a police procedural told as a set of slices: a prostitute's corpse is found on the bank of the Tiber, the police interrogate a series of suspects who were in a nearby park that day, and each suspect's account becomes a self-contained slice of life -- a young man robbing lovers while claiming to meet priests, a gigolo mistreating his girlfriends, a soldier failing to pick up women and falling asleep on a bench, two teenage boys stealing from a homosexual man -- until the final flashback shows the murder itself, by a man in clogs who had already been interrogated, apprehended at a dance. [C32029] The structure is very similar to Kurosawa's Rashomon, though Bertolucci denied in interview having seen that film at the time, and every narrative is interrupted by the same sudden thunderstorm, which in each case leads to an interlude at the prostitute's apartment as she prepares for her evening. [C32030]

His age at the debut is flagged: the film article says "directorial debut at age 21" while the main article says twice that he made his debut at 22, and the arithmetic from the article's own dates (born 16 March 1941; film released 19 September 1962) gives 21 -- a widely repeated inconsistency, not a single typo, and the study uses 21. [C32031] The reception was mixed and already prophetic: Segnalazioni cinematografiche found the ambition to adapt the work to "the most Pasolinian atmosphere and context of the marginal Rome" led to a "rather modest result", John Simon called it "pure trash", and Janet Maslin, writing in the New York Times in 1982, said it "has enough rough edges to mark it unmistakably as a debut effort" but also "a sophistication that, from so young and inexperienced a film maker, is quite unexpected" -- and that "Mr. Bertolucci gravitates instinctively to extremes here, shooting from the steepest, the broadest, the most eye-catching angle whenever he can. Even when these touches don't serve the narrative, that hardly matters. The narrative is always secondary to Mr. Bertolucci's very disciplined flamboyance." [C32032]

The mechanism is already visible at twenty-one. The Rashomon retelling: one fixed event, a corpse on the riverbank, rebuilt from several contradictory first-person accounts, each a self-contained slice of life. [C32033] The storm as a loop-back: every narrative is cut by the same thunderstorm, and the storm always returns the film to one fixed point, the victim's apartment, the victim preparing for her evening -- the victim's room is the still centre and the suspects' stories are the moving parts. [C32034] The extreme angle as signature: even at twenty-one the camera goes to the steepest, broadest, most eye-catching position whether the story needs it or not -- style first, narrative second, and the style is the argument. [C32035]

**Takeaway:** The first film is Pasolini's material directed by a twenty-one-year-old who already knows his camera belongs at the extreme angle. The storm that keeps returning the film to the victim's room is the first version of the device that will run the whole career: a fixed space, a set of conflicting accounts, and a weather event that resets the view to the same frame. He will spend fifty years finding bigger rooms and bigger storms.

## 4. Before the Revolution (1964): the first truly personal film

Before the Revolution premiered on Tuesday 12 May 1964 at the 17th Cannes Film Festival, in International Critics' Week, runs 112 minutes, and was written by Bertolucci and Gianni Amico, starring Adriana Asti as Gina and Francesco Barilli as Fabrizio, shot by Aldo Scavarda, edited by Roberto Perpignani, with music by Ennio Morricone -- the first Morricone score in the Bertolucci filmography. [C32036] The brief for this study asserted that Bertolucci called the film his "real debut", but no verbatim quote to that effect appears in any page read end to end: what the sources do say is that it was his "first truly personal film" (vectree.io) and his "first widely seen film" (NPR), and the study uses those wordings, not the unverified quote. [C32037] The title comes from a saying attributed to Talleyrand, "Only those who lived before the revolution knew how sweet life could be", and the character names are taken from Stendhal's The Charterhouse of Parma -- the novel's Fabrice del Dongo becomes Fabrizio, his aunt Gina Sanseverina becomes Gina, Clelia Conti becomes Clelia -- so the past haunts the present by naming alone. [C32038]

The plot is a portrait of his own class and his own city: in Parma in 1962, Fabrizio, a young student drawn to the Italian Communist Party, struggles with his middle-class roots and questions his engagement to Clelia, who shares his background and has no interest in ideology; his best friend Agostino, torn between his parents' Catholicism and Fabrizio's Marxism, drowns in the Po River, and Fabrizio becomes convinced it was suicide; Fabrizio begins an affair with his aunt Gina, newly arrived from Milan, and they read philosophy with his teacher Cesare and reflect on Italy's fascist past; after Gina returns to Milan, Fabrizio drifts from the party, marries Clelia, and keeps his ties to his social milieu, while at the wedding Gina is seen repeatedly kissing Fabrizio's younger brother Antonio. [C32039] It was shot in September-November 1963 in Parma and its surroundings, with one scene filmed in the camera ottica, the optical chamber, at the Sanvitale Fortress in Fontanellato -- a real dark room that projects the outside world upside down onto a wall, a literal device for seeing one's own city inverted. [C32040] The reception split the way it would keep splitting: lukewarm in Italy, enthusiastic abroad, now widely acclaimed -- Film4 calls it "one of the masterpieces of Italian cinema", Colin MacCabe in 1001 Movies You Must See Before You Die calls it "the perfect portrait of the generation who were to embrace revolt in the late 1960s, and a stunning portrait of Parma", and John Simon called it "murky, pretentious, and juvenile". [C32041]

The mechanism is the intercut as argument. [C32042] The final scene braids two unrelated actions, a bourgeois wedding and a teacher reading Moby-Dick to a class of youngsters, while a tearful Gina hugs and kisses -- and the meaning lives in the cut between them, not in either shot. [C32043] Politics and eroticism are carried by poetic sound and image rather than by plot, and the film's own critics name the through-line: Luana Ciavola writes that the film comes from within the bourgeoisie while being against it, and Fabrizio's revolt "finds support in political commitment... sustained by an erotic desire", while TimeOut's David Jenkins says "in all of Bertolucci's movies, there's a central conflict between the 'radical' impulses and a pessimistic (and/or willing) capitulation to the mainstream of bourgeois society and culture". [C32044]

**Takeaway:** This is the film where he stops being Pasolini's student and starts being himself: his own city, his own class guilt, his own affair, his own defeat. The wedding that ends the film is the first version of the ending he will write again and again -- the revolutionary marries into the world he meant to overthrow, and the camera watches him do it without blinking. The optical chamber is the whole method in one room: show the familiar city upside down, and it tells the truth.

## 5. Partner (1968) and the widening apprenticeship

Between the features the apprenticeship widened: in 1967 he directed La via del petrolio, a three-episode television documentary series about the Italian petroleum industry; in 1967 he wrote Ballata da un miliardo; in 1968 he took a story credit on Sergio Leone's Once Upon a Time in the West; and in 1969 he directed Agony, a segment of the anthology film Love and Anger -- television documentary, commercial screenwriting, a Leone story credit, an anthology segment, all before Partner. [C32045] Partner was released on Saturday 7 September 1968 in Italy, was written by Gianni Amico and Bertolucci, produced by his cousin Giovanni Bertolucci, stars Pierre Clementi, was shot by Ugo Piccone, edited by Roberto Perpignani, with music by Ennio Morricone -- though the Morricone score for Partner is indicated by a tracklist extract and not verified end to end, and the study carries it as indicated, not confirmed. [C32046] It is based on Dostoevsky's 1846 novella The Double, was entered at the 29th Venice Film Festival and the Directors' Fortnight at Cannes, and its Wikipedia plot section is only two sentences long: a college student with a routine life encounters a twin he is not related to, and discovers the twin has many qualities he does not have. [C32047] The fuller plot comes from search extracts, not from a page read end to end: Giacobbe, an unassuming drama teacher planning suicide, meets his doppelganger, a renegade revolutionary who can do everything he cannot, and invites the double to live in his mirrored closet so they can trade places when needed, while the double eggs him on to provocation and violence against the backdrop of the 1968 student movement. [C32048]

The Godard influence is verified by search extracts, since the Wikipedia page itself does not mention Godard: MoMA calls the film "more self-consciously indebted to Jean-Luc Godard in its theatrical confrontation between spectacle and revolution" and "Bertolucci's elliptical third feature", Film-Forward says "you can smell Godard in the mundane violence, the characters' free-wheeling discussions of Marxism, and the surreal declarations and criticism of art", and DVD Talk calls it "heavily influenced by Jean-Luc Godard and the French New Wave". [C32049] The counting is flagged: MoMA calls Partner the "third feature" while DVD Talk calls it "his fourth picture", and the difference is presumably whether the 1967 television documentary La via del petrolio is counted -- the main article's filmography lists it under Television, not Feature film, which supports "third feature". [C32050] Pauline Kael called it an "inventive but bewildering modernization of Dostoyevski's The Double", and Rotten Tomatoes records 91% of 11 critics positive. [C32051]

The mechanism is the double with opposite affordances. [C32052] One body, two selves: the timid teacher and the hard-hearted anarchist, played by the same actor, who swap places on demand -- and the double literally lives in a mirrored closet, a second room behind glass where the other self waits. [C32053] The giant shadow is the other image: in one of the film's best-noted sequences, Giacobbe wanders the streets at night casting large shadows on the walls of the buildings, so the shadow on the wall is larger and bolder than the character who casts it. [C32054]

**Takeaway:** Partner is the Godard detour, the attempt to wear another director's style the way Giacobbe wears his double -- and like the double, it does not stay. What survives into the later films is not the Godard essay-film set piece but the mirrored closet: the second room behind glass where the other self waits, which will return as the apartment in Last Tango, the palace in The Last Emperor, the cellar in Me and You. He tries on a double and keeps the closet.

## 6. The Conformist (1970): the frame as a cage

The Conformist premiered on Wednesday 1 July 1970 at the 20th Berlin International Film Festival, which was closed down three days later over a controversy about Michael Verhoeven's anti-war film o.k., so no prizes were awarded; it runs 108 minutes per the infobox, was directed and written by Bertolucci from Alberto Moravia's 1951 novel, produced by Maurizio Lodi-Fe, shot by Vittorio Storaro, edited by Franco Arcalli, with music by Georges Delerue -- not Morricone, a correction that matters, since the Morricone collaboration is often assumed to cover the whole early period and does not. [C32055] The Italian release date is flagged: the infobox says 7 October 1970, but the Release section says the film had a staggered release in Italy, opening in Milan on 29 January, Turin on 5 February and Rome on 25 March 1971, and the 7 October 1970 date is unexplained by the release section; the study carries both. [C32056] The runtime is flagged too: the infobox says 108 minutes while the home-media section says the US DVD includes the original theatrical version at 111 minutes, and the arithmetic does not line up cleanly with the five minutes trimmed from the first American release, so it is carried, not resolved. [C32057] The budget was $750,000, the Italian box office 207.3 million lire, and the cast is Jean-Louis Trintignant as Marcello Clerici, Stefania Sandrelli as Giulia, Gastone Moschin as Manganiello, Dominique Sanda as Anna Quadri, Pierre Clementi as Lino Semirama, and Enzo Tarascio as Professor Luca Quadri. [C32058]

The plot is a case study in the psychology of conformism and fascism. [C32059] In Paris in 1938, Marcello Clerici, a mid-level Fascist functionary, prepares to assassinate his former university professor, Luca Quadri, an anti-Fascist dissident in exile; flashbacks show his plan to marry Giulia for the sake of normality, his joining the Fascist secret police, visits to his morphine-addicted mother at the decaying family villa and his father in an insane asylum, and, in 1917, the boy Marcello being sexually assaulted by the chauffeur Lino, then firing a pistol at him and fleeing in the belief he has committed murder; in confession he admits the supposed murder, premarital sex and lack of guilt, and is absolved once the priest learns he works for the secret police. [C32060] Using his honeymoon as cover, Marcello takes Giulia to Paris, falls in love with Anna, Quadri's French wife, who also makes advances toward Giulia; at the dance hall Quadri tests Marcello's commitment; Marcello secretly returns his gun and gives Manganiello the location of Quadri's country house in Savoy; on a deserted alpine road, Fascist agents stab Quadri to death while Anna watches, she runs to Marcello's car for help, sees him in the back seat, realises his betrayal, and flees into the woods, where she is shot dead as Marcello watches. [C32061] In 1943, after Mussolini's fall, Marcello -- now settled with Giulia and a daughter -- recognises Lino alive on a Rome street and realises he never committed the murder he agonised over since childhood; he denounces Lino and Italo as Fascists to a passing crowd, then sits by a small fire and stares at the young man Lino had been speaking to, now naked on a bed. [C32062]

The film is the start of the Bertolucci-Storaro collaboration, and the film that launched Storaro's international career -- though one extract calls it their second collaboration, and the study does not name a first film, since no source read identifies one. [C32063] The visual system is the argument: Storaro photographs it with rich colours, authentic 1930s wardrobe, unusual camera angles and fluid camera movement, and critic Robin Buss notes the cinematography suggests Clerici's inability to conform with "normal" reality, because the reality of the time is "abnormal". [C32064] The colour scheme frequently shifts from fully washed-out neutrals to vibrant, almost garish primary colours, characters are framed in the centre of a vast, looming environment that threatens to engulf them, and the mise en scene deploys heavy contrasts of shadow and light throughout. [C32065] Using shadows, bars, and the camera as a surveillance device, Bertolucci and Storaro turn the cinematic canvas into a prison: the film consistently frames characters within bars, in the form of horizontal and vertical shadows as well as barred windows. [C32066] The EUR district of Rome -- commissioned by Mussolini as a model city, rationalist architecture -- is a key location, alongside the Palazzo dei Congressi, the Museum of the Ara Pacis, Sant' Angelo Bridge, Santa Marinella, the Theatre of Marcellus and the Colosseum, with studio scenes at Cinecitta. [C32067]

Plato's cave is staged, not quoted. [C32068] Marcello's unfinished senior thesis was on Plato's Allegory of the Cave, and in Quadri's study he recalls it; Quadri picks the thread up and turns it into an attack on Marcello's perception, describing the near-sightless prisoners reacting to shadows cast by their unseen captors' flames -- "Shadows - the reflections of things - as you experience in Italy today". [C32069] During the scene the room shades are drawn, leaving darkness save for a bright sliver of light through the window, which throws Marcello's shadow against the wall, so a thesis discussion becomes a lighting event: the protagonist's own shadow is thrown on the wall while his teacher tells him he mistakes shadows for reality. [C32070] The structure itself was decided in post-production: the use of in media res and non-chronological storytelling was not in the original script but was decided by Bertolucci and editor Franco Arcalli during the edit, their first film together, so the whole past is recalled during one long night drive from Paris toward Savoy -- memory as a road trip with the assassination waiting at the end. [C32071]

The reception was the first unqualified triumph: it won the David di Donatello for Best Film and the Sutherland Trophy, was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay and the Golden Globe for Best Foreign Language Film, won the National Society of Film Critics Awards for Best Director and Best Cinematography, and now stands at 98% on Rotten Tomatoes and 100/100 on Metacritic. [C32072] Pauline Kael called it "a sumptuous and emotionally charged experience", Vincent Canby wrote that it is "so rich, poetic, and baroque that it is simply incapable of meaning only what it says", and Kevin Thomas wrote that Bertolucci, at thirty, "manages to combine the bravura style of a Fellini, the acute sense of period of a Visconti and the fervent political commitment of an Elio Petri... with complete individuality". [C32073] Its influence is measurable: the blowing-leaves image influenced a similar scene in The Godfather Part II (1974), Coppola cast Gastone Moschin in that film on the strength of his Conformist role, the snowy-woods chase is echoed in The Sopranos episode "Pine Barrens" (2001), and in 2008 the film entered the Italian Ministry of Cultural Heritage's list of 100 Italian films to be saved. [C32074]

**Takeaway:** The Conformist is the synthesis, and it is also the film where the mechanism becomes the meaning. The bars of light and shadow are not decoration on a story about conformism; they are the conformism, made visible as a frame that will not let the character out. The false murder is the purest Bertolucci plot device ever written: a man builds his entire adult life -- his politics, his marriage, his crime -- on a murder he never committed, and only learns it when his victim walks past him alive. The whole career after this is men discovering that the crime they built their lives on never happened, and building anyway.

## 7. Last Tango in Paris (1972): the sealed room and the butter

Last Tango in Paris premiered as the closing film of the New York Film Festival on Saturday 14 October 1972, opened in France on 15 December 1972 and in Italy on 16 December 1972, runs 129 minutes, was written by Bertolucci and Franco Arcalli with French dialogue adaptation by Agnes Varda, produced by Alberto Grimaldi, shot by Storaro, edited by Arcalli, with music by Gato Barbieri, arranged and conducted by Oliver Nelson. [C32075] The cast is Marlon Brando as Paul, an American expatriate hotel owner mourning the suicide of his wife Rosa, Maria Schneider as Jeanne, a young engaged Parisian woman, Jean-Pierre Leaud as her fiance Thomas, a film director, and Massimo Girotti as Marcel, Rosa's former lover. [C32076] Bertolucci originally intended Dominique Sanda, who developed the idea with him, and Jean-Louis Trintignant; Trintignant refused, and when Brando accepted, Sanda was pregnant and declined. [C32077] The film cost $1 million and grossed $96 million worldwide, with Brando taking a percentage of the gross and estimated to have earned $3 million; Schneider stated in 2001 that her role in the original script was intended to be played by a boy. [C32078]

The plot is a contract and its collapse. [C32079] Paul meets Jeanne at an apartment both want to rent, takes it, and they begin an anonymous sexual relationship there, with Paul insisting they share no personal information, not even given names; at one point Paul anally rapes Jeanne using butter as lubricant; the affair continues until Paul leaves without warning, then, after his wife's viewing, he pursues Jeanne through Paris telling her his life story, breaking the anonymity; she rejects him, flees to her apartment, takes a gun from a drawer, tells him her name, and shoots him; he dies on the balcony while she rehearses telling police he was a stranger who tried to rape her. [C32080]

The butter scene is the film's centre and its disgrace, and the facts must be carried with both versions attributed, because the sources do not agree. [C32081] What is undisputed: the rape itself was staged and simulated, the use of butter was not in the script and was not told to Schneider in advance, only Brando and Bertolucci knew beforehand, and there was one take. [C32082] Schneider said in a 2006 interview, "when they told me, I had a burst of anger. Woo! I threw everything. And nobody can force someone to do something not in the script. But I didn't know that. I was too young." [C32083] In 2007 she said, "They only told me about it before we had to film the scene and I was so angry. I should have called my agent or had my lawyer come to the set because you can't force someone to do something that isn't in the script, but at the time, I didn't know that. Marlon said to me: 'Maria, don't worry, it's just a movie', but during the scene, even though what Marlon was doing wasn't real, I was crying real tears. I felt humiliated and to be honest, I felt a little raped, both by Marlon and by Bertolucci. After the scene, Marlon didn't console me or apologize. Thankfully, there was just one take." [C32084] She also said in 2001 that she felt "very sad because I was treated like a sex symbol -- I wanted to be recognised as an actress and the whole scandal and aftermath of the film turned me a little crazy and I had a breakdown", and that Brando and Bertolucci "made a fortune" from the film while she made very little. [C32085] Bertolucci's position, stated on Dutch television's College Tour in February 2013, two years after Schneider's death in 2011, and again at the Cinematheque Francaise in September 2013, was that the rape scene was in the script but the detail of using butter as lubricant was improvised on the day of shooting, that he and Brando "decided not to say anything to Maria to get a more realistic response", and that he admitted withholding the information to generate a real "reaction of frustration and rage" -- "I feel guilty, but I don't regret it." [C32086] Schneider's version, carried on both Wikipedia pages, is that the scene itself was not in the original script at all and was Brando's idea. [C32087] In November 2016 a re-uploaded clip of the 2013 interview went viral, newspapers falsely reported that Bertolucci had confessed Schneider was raped on set, and he released a statement saying the rape was "simulated" even though they never had a prior agreement on the use of butter, calling the controversy "ridiculous" in December 2016 and clarifying that Schneider had known of the violence to be depicted but had not been told about the butter. [C32088] Brando refused to speak to Bertolucci for fifteen years after production, and Schneider said, "Marlon said he felt raped and manipulated by it and he was 48. And he was Marlon Brando!" [C32089] After the film, following the "media glare" and her fame, Schneider became a drug addict and suicidal, and she died in 2011, aged 58. [C32090] The study does not settle the script conflict; it carries both versions, attributed, and it records the undisputed core -- she was not told about the butter in advance, and Brando originated the butter idea. [C32091]

The state answered the film with a trial. [C32092] In Italy, one week after release, police seized all copies on a prosecutor's order, the film defined as "self-serving pornography", and Bertolucci was put on trial for obscenity; following first-degree and appeal trials, the Italian Supreme Court sealed the film's fate on Monday 26 January 1976, when all copies were sentenced to be destroyed, though some were preserved by the National Film Library, and Bertolucci received a four-month suspended prison sentence and had his civil rights revoked for five years, depriving him of voting rights. [C32093] The preservation is complementary, not contradictory: in 1978 the Appeals Court of Bologna ordered three copies preserved in the national film library with the stipulation that they could not be viewed, until Bertolucci was later able to re-submit the film for general distribution with no cuts, and it was re-released in Italy in 1987. [C32094] In the United States the MPAA gave the film an X rating on release, United Artists Classics released an R-rated cut in 1981, and in 1997 the original cut was reclassified NC-17; it was also banned in Argentina, South Korea, Singapore and Venezuela, banned in Chile for nearly thirty years, censored in Spain until December 1977, suppressed in Portugal until the Carnation Revolution of 1974, and censored in Brazil until 1979. [C32095] The critical reception was extreme in both directions: Pauline Kael's review called it a landmark comparable to the 1913 premiere of Le Sacre du Printemps and United Artists reprinted her whole review as a double-page advertisement in the Sunday New York Times, the Village Voice reported walkouts and "vomiting by well-dressed wives", William F. Buckley and ABC's Harry Reasoner denounced it as "pornography disguised as art", and Robert Altman said, "I walked out of the screening and said to myself, 'How dare I make another film?'" [C32096] It was nominated for two Academy Awards, Best Director and Best Actor for Brando, and won the Nastro d'Argento for Best Director and a Special David di Donatello for Schneider. [C32097]

The visual system is Bacon. [C32098] The opening credits include two Francis Bacon paintings, Double Portrait of Lucian Freud and Frank Auerbach and Study for a Portrait of Isabel Rawsthorne, both 1964; Bertolucci visited a Bacon exhibit at the Grand Palais during pre-production, and the light and colour in Bacon reminded him of Paris in winter -- "the lights of the stores are on, and there is a very beautiful contrast between the leaden gray of the wintry sky and the warmth of the show windows" -- while Bacon's skin-like-raw-meat painting and butcher-shop imagery were an acknowledged source. [C32099] Storaro, who had worked with Bertolucci on The Conformist, asked himself after that film "what can come after azure? ... I did not have the slightest idea that an orange film could be born. We needed another kind of emotion... It was the case of Last Tango", and the palette is "rich oranges, light and cool grays, icy whites, and occasional reds" combined with "soft browns, blond browns, and delicate whites with bluish and pink shadings". [C32100] Bertolucci took Brando to the Bacon exhibit and told him he wanted Paul to be like Bacon's figures: "faces eaten by something coming from the inside." [C32101] Brando refused to memorise lines and wrote them on cue cards posted around the set, so his upward glance during the long monologue over his wife's body is a search for the next cue card -- and Bertolucci also shot a scene showing Brando's genitals but cut it, explaining in 1973, "I had so identified myself with Brando that I cut it out of shame for myself. To show him naked would have been like showing me naked." [C32102]

**Takeaway:** Last Tango is the sealed room with its own rules, and the film is honest about what the room is for: two people agree to know nothing about each other, and the agreement works until one of them tells the truth. The mechanism is borrowable and the ethics are not: a space whose rules are visible and absolute until biography leaks in and collapses it is a superb machine for a generative piece, and the butter scene is the proof that a machine built on withholding information from a person is a different thing from a machine built on withholding information from an audience. The film's lasting scandal is not the sex but the method -- the director who decided that a real reaction was worth a real deception, and said so, on camera, forty years later, without regret.

## 8. 1900 (1976): parallel lives on one timeline

1900, in Italian Novecento, premiered out of competition at the Cannes Film Festival on Friday 21 May 1976, opened in Italy in two parts on 3 September and 24 September 1976, runs 317 minutes in its original cut -- five hours and seventeen minutes, one of the longest commercially released films in history -- and was written by Franco Arcalli, Giuseppe Bertolucci and Bernardo Bertolucci, produced by Alberto Grimaldi, shot by Storaro, edited by Arcalli, with music by Ennio Morricone, including melodies from Giuseppe Verdi operas. [C32103] The cast is Robert De Niro as Alfredo Berlinghieri, Gerard Depardieu as Olmo Dalco, Dominique Sanda as Ada, Donald Sutherland as Attila Mellanchini, the fascist foreman, Burt Lancaster as Alfredo the Elder, Sterling Hayden as Leo Dalco, the peasants' spokesman, and Stefania Sandrelli as Anita Foschi; the opening credits are displayed over a zoom out of Giuseppe Pellizza da Volpedo's painting The Fourth Estate. [C32104] The budget was $8 million, after Grimaldi claimed Bertolucci extended the shooting schedule to almost a year and pushed the cost from $3 million to $8 million, and it is set in Bertolucci's ancestral region of Emilia. [C32105]

The plot is two lives sharing one clock. [C32106] In 1945, as Italy is liberated from fascism, peasants on an Emilia-Romagna estate put the landowner Alfredo Berlinghieri under arrest; the film then tells, from their shared birth in 1901, the parallel lives of Alfredo, son of wealthy landowners, and Olmo Dalco, an illegitimate peasant raised by his grandfather Leo -- their friendship surviving childhood strikes, the First World War, Giovanni's hiring of the fascist foreman Attila, who terrorises the workers, Alfredo's weak rule as the new padrone and his marriage to Ada, whose alcoholism mirrors the marriage's emptiness, and Olmo's rise as a socialist leader after his wife Anita dies in childbirth. [C32107] After the war the power shifts: Attila and Regina are imprisoned in the Berlinghieri pigsty, Attila confesses his murders and is executed, Alfredo is tried by a workers' tribunal and sentenced to death but saved when Olmo declares the padrone dead -- the social system itself abolished -- and the film closes with the two old men playfully tackling each other and walking the railroad tracks of their boyhood, Alfredo lying down on the tracks as the train passes over him, as in their childhood game. [C32108]

The cut is a second author. [C32109] Grimaldi was contractually obligated to deliver a 195-minute version to Paramount for the United States and Canada; Bertolucci wanted to release the film in two parts, 20th Century Fox picked up US distribution and then dropped out when he declined to shorten the film by eighty minutes, Grimaldi locked Bertolucci out of the editing room and assembled a 180-minute cut, and Bertolucci compromised on a 247-minute version initially released in the United States on 7 October 1977 -- though the same page also calls it "the 245-minute American cut" two sentences later, a minor unresolved discrepancy of two minutes. [C32110] The film exists in multiple lengths (317, 247, 195, 180 minutes) because producers, distributors and the director fought over it, and the "same" work is a different object at each length. [C32111] The Morricone soundtrack is itself a calendar: sixteen tracks named by season and year -- "Romanzo", "Estate - 1908", "Autunno - 1922", "Regalo di nozze", "Testamento", "Polenta", "Il primo sciopero", "Padre e figlia", "Tema di Ada", "Apertura della caccia", "Verdi e morto", "I nuovi crociati", "Il quarto stato", "Inverno - 1935", "Primavera - 1945", "Olmo e Alfredo" -- so the film's time is cyclical (harvest, winter, spring) laid over linear political time. [C32112] The reception was divided: Roger Ebert wrote that the film "doesn't seem to go anywhere. It's an epic only by virtue of its length", Rotten Tomatoes records 57%, and in 2008 it entered the Italian Ministry of Cultural Heritage's list of 100 Italian films to be saved, as films that "have changed the collective memory of the country between 1942 and 1978". [C32113]

**Takeaway:** 1900 is the estate as a stage for class war: one fixed place where every historical force -- strikes, war, fascism, liberation -- arrives and plays out in the same fields, and two boys born on the same day at opposite ends of the ladder whose paths are bent apart and together by outside events. The viewer watches the gap between them widen and close for forty-five years, and the closing image -- the old padrone lying on the tracks while the train passes over him, the childhood game replayed as an old man's joke -- says what the whole film says: history is a game children play, and then the game plays them.

## 9. La Luna (1979) and Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man (1981): the fallow years

La Luna, released in the United States as Luna, was written by Giuseppe Bertolucci, Clare Peploe and Bernardo Bertolucci, produced by Giovanni Bertolucci, shot by Storaro, edited by Gabriella Cristiani, with music by Morricone and Giuseppe Verdi, and stars Jill Clayburgh as Caterina Silveri, an opera singer, Matthew Barry as her teenage son Joe, Tomas Milian as Giuseppe, Caterina's former lover and Joe's biological father, and Fred Gwynne as Douglas Winter, the husband Joe believes is his father. [C32114] Its release date is flagged on its own page: the infobox gives 30 September 1979 while the lead text says the film "was released by 20th Century-Fox on August 29, 1979", and the page does not resolve them. [C32115] The plot is a road trip as a failed cure: when Joe witnesses Douglas's sudden death in New York, Caterina moves with him to Italy, where Joe falls in with a dangerous crowd and becomes addicted to heroin; Caterina tries everything to pull him back -- she even gives him a handjob when he is desperate for a fix, to get his mind off drugs -- then drives him toward Giuseppe's home hoping a fatherly bond will cure him; Joe, instead of revealing himself, tells Giuseppe he is a friend of his son who overdosed on heroin after lifelong turmoil over his absent father, and with that closure achieved he returns to his mother, embraces her, and together with Giuseppe -- who now knows Joe is his child -- hears Caterina sing at her best. [C32116] The reception was mixed and sometimes brutal: Clayburgh's performance was widely praised and earned a Golden Globe nomination for Best Actress in a Drama, Roger Ebert gave it two stars and wrote "He's got a soap opera and a Freudian case history (traditional enemies in their natural states) and he's forcing them to copulate", Angela Carter called Clayburgh's performance "bravura" and "the life force in person", and Andrei Tarkovsky wrote in his diary on 7 September 1979, "Saw Bertolucci's La luna. Monstrous, cheap, vulgar rubbish." [C32117] The film was briefly banned in Ecuador in 1980 by Abdala Bucaram, then major general of the Guayaquil police, who closed all the theatres that showed it, and it was broadcast years later on national television without repercussions. [C32118] Its box office is carried as stated on the page, not independently verified: $68.2 million worldwide on a $5.0 million budget, a surprisingly high figure for this film. [C32119]

Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man, in Italian La tragedia di un uomo ridicolo, is a 1981 film directed and written by Bertolucci, produced by Giovanni Bertolucci, shot by Carlo Di Palma -- not Storaro, the first of the four films in this leg without him -- edited by Gabriella Cristiani, with music by Morricone, and stars Ugo Tognazzi as Primo Spaggiari, Anouk Aimee as his wife Barbara, and Laura Morante as Laura, their son's girlfriend. [C32120] Its dating is flagged as a nuance, not a contradiction: the lead calls it a 1981 Italian film and Tognazzi won Best Male Actor at the 1981 Cannes Film Festival, but the infobox lists only one release date, 12 February 1982, the American release, and the page gives no Italian release date. [C32121] The plot is a ransom machine that outlives its reason: Primo, a small cheese factory owner from Parma of peasant origin who did not go beyond elementary school, a self-made man, learns his son Giovanni has been kidnapped by terrorists and must raise a billion lire for the ransom, while his dairy is hit by a serious economic crisis; from Laura and a worker priest, Adelfo, he learns his son has died -- yet he continues to collect the money, helped by his wife, to save his second creature, the factory on the verge of bankruptcy; following a false letter written by Laura, the couple deposits the ransom in the indicated place, Giovanni suddenly reappears, and in the end the billion lire is invested in the dairy, transformed into a cooperative company under the workers' control. [C32122] The film never settles whether the kidnapping is real: Vincent Canby called it "the story of what may or may not be a terrorist kidnapping of the sort that has been making Italian headlines with increasing frequency in recent years", the Bertolucci article calls it "darkly comedic", and Tognazzi won Best Male Actor at Cannes in 1981 while the film was nominated for the Palme d'Or. [C32123]

**Takeaway:** The fallow years are not a fallow at all; they are the laboratory where the later mechanisms are tested at small scale. La Luna proves that a lie can heal where the truth cannot -- Joe's false story about himself gives his father the story and the son the closure, and the opera at the end is the one room the family can finally enter together. Tragedy proves that a machine keeps running after its reason is gone, and that the money collected for a dead son can be re-pointed at a factory and become a cooperative. Both films are about a system that refuses to stop, and both end by re-pointing it rather than switching it off.

## 10. The Last Emperor (1987): the flashback that rewinds a life

The Last Emperor premiered on Sunday 4 October 1987 at the Tokyo International Film Festival, opened in Italy on 23 October 1987 and in the United States on 18 November 1987, runs 163 minutes, was written by Mark Peploe and Bertolucci from Puyi's 1964 autobiography From Emperor to Citizen, produced by Jeremy Thomas, shot by Storaro, edited by Gabriella Cristiani, with music by Ryuichi Sakamoto, David Byrne and Cong Su. [C32124] Its budget is flagged: $23.8 million in the infobox against $25 million in Thomas's own recollection in the Development section, and its box office is flagged too: $44 million in the infobox against $79 million in Simple Wikipedia. [C32125] It was the first Western feature film authorised by the People's Republic of China to film in the Forbidden City in Beijing: Bertolucci proposed two projects to the Chinese government, this one and an adaptation of Malraux's La Condition humaine, the Chinese preferred The Last Emperor, and he was given complete freedom to shoot in the Forbidden City, which had never before been opened up for use in a Western film, the ancient city used as the setting for the first ninety minutes of the film as well as the ending. [C32126] Nineteen thousand extras were needed over the course of the film and the People's Liberation Army was drafted in; Thomas raised the budget single-handedly for the independent production and at one stage scoured the phone book for potential financiers; the script was approved with notes and changed names, and then, as Thomas recalled, "the stamp went on and the door opened and we came". [C32127] Before shooting the Cultural Revolution parade scene, Bertolucci gathered four or five young Chinese directors he had met, including Chen Kaige, who also plays the Captain of the Imperial Guard, and Zhang Yimou, and asked them about the Cultural Revolution; they "started to act out and cry, it was extraordinary". [C32128]

The film's spine is the 1950 prison present, with the life told as flashbacks triggered from that cell. [C32129] By 1950 the 44-year-old Puyi, former Emperor of China, has been in custody for five years since his capture by the Red Army during the Soviet invasion of Manchuria; he arrives as a political prisoner and war criminal at Fushun Prison, attempts suicide, is rescued and told he must stand trial. [C32130] The life unspools in three blocks, each smaller and less gilded than the last: the child emperor, summoned in 1908 as a toddler by the dying Empress Dowager Cixi, confined entirely to the palace and forbidden to leave, his only real friend his wet nurse Ar Mo, until his brother tells him China has become a republic and Ar Mo is forced to leave the same day; the reformer turned playboy-puppet, given a Western education by his tutor Reginald Johnston (Peter O'Toole) in 1919, married to Wanrong (Joan Chen), expelled from the palace after the Beijing Coup in 1924, exiled to Tientsin, siding with Japan after the Mukden Incident, and in 1934 crowned "Emperor" of the puppet state of Manchukuo, his supremacy undermined at every turn, while Wanrong gives birth to an illegitimate child, murdered at birth by the Japanese and proclaimed stillborn; and the prisoner-gardener, captured by the Red Army, coerced under Communist re-education to renounce his collaboration with Japan, recanting after discussions with warden Jin Yuan and after watching a film detailing Japanese wartime atrocities -- a film-within-the-film as the device that breaks his resistance -- released in 1959, and by 1967 a simple gardener living a peasant proletarian existence during the Cultural Revolution, seeing Jin Yuan, now a punished political prisoner in a Red Guard parade, wearing a dunce cap and a sandwich board. [C32131] The coda is the cricket jar: Puyi visits the Palace Museum, climbs the throne dais, and a young Pioneer boy demands proof he was once emperor; Puyi reaches behind the throne and retrieves a jar containing the pet cricket given to him at his coronation by palace official Chen Baochen, the boy inspects the jar, notices Puyi has vanished, and sees the cricket emerge -- and in 1987 a tour guide leads a group through the palace and sums up Puyi's life in a few brief sentences, concluding that he died in 1967. [C32132]

At the 60th Academy Awards, ceremony Monday 11 April 1988, the film won all nine Oscars it was nominated for: Best Picture for Jeremy Thomas, Best Director for Bertolucci, Best Screenplay Based on Material from Another Medium for Mark Peploe and Bertolucci, Best Art Direction, Best Cinematography for Storaro, Best Costume Design, Best Film Editing, Best Original Score for Sakamoto, Byrne and Cong Su, and Best Sound -- and the Bertolucci article states he became "the first Italian filmmaker to win the Academy Award for Best Director" with this film. [C32133] It also won three BAFTAs, four Golden Globes, nine David di Donatello Awards, a Grammy for its score, the Cesar for Best Foreign Film and the Directors Guild Award, and its box-office run was an unusual slow build: it did not enter the US weekend box-office top ten until its twelfth week and peaked at number four in its twenty-second week, the weekend after winning the Oscar, with weekend gross up 306%. [C32134] The film's historical accuracy is contested on its own page: historian Alex von Tunzelmann writes that the movie "considerably downplays and misrepresents the Emperor's cruelty, especially during his youth", citing Puyi's sadistic abuse of palace servants, and the page lists further inaccuracies -- in real life Puyi left the Forbidden City when his mother died, per his memoirs he did not have sex with his wives, and the testimonies he gives his interrogators were in fact given at the Tokyo Trials. [C32135] The Shochiku Fuji Company edited out a thirty-second sequence depicting the Rape of Nanjing before distributing the film to Japanese theatres, without Bertolucci's consent; he called it "revolting", and the company quickly restored the scene. [C32136] An extended version of 218 minutes was created for television, and Storaro and Bertolucci confirmed it was created as a television miniseries and "does not represent a true 'director's cut'". [C32137]

**Takeaway:** The Last Emperor is the flashback machine perfected: the present is a cell, the past is replayed from it until the present changes, and the trigger is another film -- atrocity footage shown to a prisoner until he recants. The three acts are three bodies, each smaller than the last: child emperor, playboy-puppet, prisoner-gardener, and the cricket jar at the end is the whole film in one object, a living thing kept behind a throne for sixty years, released when the man who kept it has already vanished. He went to China to film an emperor and came back with the proof that a life can be told backwards, from the cell to the cradle, and still arrive at the truth.

## 11. The Sheltering Sky (1990): the desert as erasure

The Sheltering Sky, from Paul Bowles's 1949 novel, was written by Mark Peploe and Bertolucci, produced by Jeremy Thomas, shot by Storaro, edited by Gabriella Cristiani, with music by Ryuichi Sakamoto and Richard Horowitz, and stars Debra Winger as Kit Moresby, John Malkovich as her husband Port, and Campbell Scott as their friend George Tunner; it was released on 14 December 1990, runs 138 minutes, cost $25 million and took $2 million at the box office -- a flop. [C32138] The plot is an erasure in stages: three Americans arrive in Oran, French Algeria, in 1947, Port and Kit accompanied by Tunner, on a trip deep into the Sahara -- "We're probably the first tourists they've had since the war", says Tunner, and Kit answers, "We're not tourists. We're travelers." [C32139] Port's passport is stolen, then abandoned rather than recovered; the marriage breaks by mutual infidelity; Port contracts typhoid fever on the bus to El Ga'a, the hotel will not accommodate them for fear of infection, Kit nurses him herself at a French Foreign Legion post with no doctor, and he dies beside her. [C32140] Kit sets off alone into the Sahara, begs a ride from a camel train led by a Tuareg, Belqassim, who disguises her as a boy and locks her in a guest house; she welcomes his advances and they begin an affair, his wives discover her and order her to leave, disoriented in the marketplace and set upon by a mob she is found mute and almost insane in a Catholic mission hospital, and transported back to Tangier she flees into the city before Tunner can meet her. [C32141] Each stage removes one more layer of the traveler -- passport, marriage, body, language, clothing, sex, then speech itself -- until she refuses rescue and flees. [C32142] The film was shot in Morocco, in Tangier, Ouarzazate, Erfoud and Tamnougalt, although most locations named in the film are in present-day Algeria, and the novel's Wikipedia page says the movie was filmed in Morocco, Algeria and Niger -- a difference the study records, since the two statements do not quite match. [C32143] The reception was divided: Vincent Canby called it a "long, beautifully modulated cry of despair", Roger Ebert gave it two stars and said he was "left with the impression of my fingers closing on air", Rotten Tomatoes records 45%, and in 1998 Paul Bowles wrote a new preface to the novel stating "the less said about the film now, the better". [C32144] The score won the Golden Globe for Best Original Score, and Storaro won the BAFTA, the New York Film Critics Circle and the Nastro d'Argento for cinematography -- the desert light was the film's one undisputed triumph. [C32145]

**Takeaway:** The Sheltering Sky is the closed room at landscape scale: the desert is a room with no walls, and each stage of the journey removes one more support until the traveler is locked in a guest house in Niger, disguised as a boy, and then found mute. The mechanism is the subtraction itself -- a piece that works by taking things away, one per act, until there is nothing left to take but the refusal to be rescued. It is the only Bertolucci film where the room wins completely.

## 12. Little Buddha (1993): the parallel child

Little Buddha was written by Rudy Wurlitzer and Mark Peploe from a story by Bertolucci, produced by Jeremy Thomas, shot by Storaro, edited by Pietro Scalia, with music entirely composed by Ryuichi Sakamoto, and stars Keanu Reeves as Siddhartha, Chris Isaak as Dean Conrad, Bridget Fonda as Lisa Conrad, Alex Wiesendanger as their son Jesse, and Ying Ruocheng as Lama Norbu; it premiered in France on 1 December 1993, runs 140 minutes, and its budget is flagged on its own page -- $35 million in the infobox against $30 million in the box-office paragraph, internally inconsistent. [C32146] The plot is two searches braided together: Tibetan Buddhist monks from a monastery in Bhutan, led by Lama Norbu, search for a child who is the rebirth of a great Buddhist teacher, Lama Dorje, and believe they have found a candidate in Jesse Conrad, an American boy in Seattle; the monks come to Seattle, Jesse is fascinated, his parents are wary, and Dean changes his mind when a close friend and colleague commits suicide because he went broke, then travels to Bhutan with Jesse. [C32147] In Nepal two more candidate children are encountered, Raju and Gita, and in parallel Lama Norbu tells the life of Prince Siddhartha from a book -- a prince who turns his back on a comfortable protected life to solve the problem of universal suffering, battles Mara, a demon representing the ego, and through the realisation of the illusory nature of his own ego attains enlightenment and becomes the Buddha. [C32148] The ending resolves the braid: all three children are rebirths of Lama Dorje, separate manifestations of his body (Raju), speech (Gita) and mind (Jesse); a ceremony is held, Jesse's father learns essential truths of Buddhism, Lama Norbu enters a deep state of meditation and dies, the children distribute his ashes, and in a post-credits scene the sand mandala constructed during the movie is destroyed with a stroke. [C32149] In the final stages of the flashback sequences the past merges with the present, as the three children find themselves actually in the scene with Prince Siddhartha -- the story inside the film swallows the film itself. [C32150]

The format is the argument. [C32151] The Buddha flashback scenes were photographed in 65mm Todd-AO by Storaro while the rest of the film was shot in 35mm anamorphic Technovision, so the ancient story is bigger, wider and more saturated than the washed-out present -- the UCLA archive describes the present as "rendered in a washed-out palette" against "a fanciful and colorful recounting of the story of Buddha" -- and the audience can tell which layer of time it is in by the look before a word is spoken. [C32152] Bertolucci insisted on the real thing: Khyongla Rato Rinpoche plays the Abbot, Dzongsar Jamyang Khyentse Rinpoche appears near the end and supervised every gesture and ritual performed by Tibetan monks, and Sogyal Rinpoche appears as Khenpo Tenzin -- "I wanted the real thing," he said. [C32153] The reception was mixed: Ebert gave it two stars, calling it "a slow-moving and pointless exercise... whose The Last Emperor was a much superior telling of a similar story about a child who is chosen for great things", Janet Maslin called it "a crazily mesmerizing pop artifact" and "Mr. Bertolucci's first Spielberg movie, complete with awestruck faces and intimations of higher knowledge", and it was very successful in France, the nineteenth highest-grossing film of the year with 1,359,483 admissions, while in the United States it closed with $4.8 million. [C32154] One theme Sakamoto composed for the film was refused by the director and became the title track of his 1994 album Sweet Revenge, a facetious allusion to Bertolucci's decision, and in a 2018 interview Sakamoto said the final music cue, "Acceptance", was his favourite piece from all of his film scores. [C32155]

**Takeaway:** Little Buddha is the rebirth as a casting problem: three children, three manifestations -- body, speech, mind -- and the film's question is whether one soul can be split across three bodies the way one story can be split across two film formats. The 65mm past and the 35mm present are the mechanism made visible: change the format and you change the world, and the audience knows which world it is in before anyone speaks. The mandala destroyed with a stroke in the post-credits scene is the film's own verdict on itself: build it beautifully, then wipe it away.

## 13. Stealing Beauty (1996): the return to Italy

Stealing Beauty, in Italian Io ballo da sola ("I Dance Alone"), was written by Susan Minot from a story by Bertolucci, produced by Jeremy Thomas, shot by Darius Khondji -- the Storaro run ends here, the single break in the Storaro-Cianchetti line -- edited by Pietro Scalia, with music by Richard Hartley, and stars Liv Tyler as Lucy Harmon, Jeremy Irons as Alex Parrish, Sinead Cusack as Diana Grayson, Donal McCann as Ian Grayson, Jean Marais as M. Guillaume, and Rachel Weisz as Miranda Fox; it opened in Italy on 29 March 1996, runs 113 minutes, and took $12.7 million worldwide, the third highest-grossing Italian film of 1996. [C32156] The plot is a girl with three missions: Lucy Harmon, nineteen, daughter of the recently deceased American poet and model Sara Harmon, arrives at the Tuscan villa of her mother's friends Ian and Diana Grayson, near Siena, to have her portrait painted, to learn more about her late mother's past and identify her real father, and to reconnect with Niccolo Donati, the boy she kissed four years earlier and corresponded with -- she memorised one letter of his in particular. [C32157] Smoking marijuana with the dying writer Parrish, Lucy reveals she is a virgin and he shares it with the villa the next day; furious, she decides to leave, but the young people arrive, and that evening they smoke marijuana and take turns recounting how each lost their virginity, while Osvaldo, Niccolo's brother, demurs: "I don't know which is more ridiculous, this conversation or the silly political one going on over there [at the adults' table]." [C32158] Lucy shares her mother's last notebook with Parrish, a poem holding clues to her real father -- green sandals, olive leaves, a viper -- and asks probing questions: did Parrish ever know Sara to wear green sandals, had Ian ever eaten olive leaves, had Carlo Lisca ever killed a viper; looking out a window at Ian's sculpture of a mother and child she has an epiphany, asks Ian where he was in August 1975, when she was conceived, and they realise Ian is Lucy's biological father, a secret she promises to keep. [C32159] Meanwhile Osvaldo arrives, Lucy is stung by bees leaving Ian's studio and he puts clay on the welts, walking through the countryside Osvaldo confesses he wrote to her once -- it was the letter Lucy loved above all, the one she knew by heart -- he takes her to the tree from the letter, and Lucy and Osvaldo spend the night having sex under the tree, as they part the next morning Osvaldo reveals it was his first time too. [C32160] The film was made entirely in Tuscany during the summer of 1995, mainly at the estate of Castello di Brolio, and was officially selected for the 1996 Cannes Film Festival, Bertolucci's second Palme d'Or nomination after Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man. [C32161] Liv Tyler's first leading film role came with a fight: she "bitterly fought against appearing topless in the film" and fought it "until the very end", and Bjork's song "Bachelorette" was originally written for the soundtrack under the working title "Bertolucci" before she faxed him to say it would be used on her album Homogenic instead. [C32162] The villa is a refuge of former political people: Bertolucci is quoted as saying the cosmopolitan community "had been very politically engaged twenty or thirty years previously; but, out of despair, they decided to abandon their political dreams and seek refuge far from the vulgar crowd, at the top of a hill, overlooking a unique landscape whose incredible beauty inspired the Tuscan painters of the 14th and 15th centuries" -- carried as extract-only, from a study guide. [C32163]

**Takeaway:** Stealing Beauty is the return to Italy after nine years abroad, and the return is a confession: the political generation that once believed has retreated to a hilltop villa, and the only revolution left is a nineteen-year-old losing her virginity under a tree. The father's identity is deduced from a poem about the mother's body -- green sandals, olive leaves, a viper -- which is the Bertolucci method in miniature: the body is the document, and the clue is written on it. The film's gentleness is the point: after the trials and the bans and the nine Oscars, he films a summer, a villa, and a first time, and lets the light do the arguing.

## 14. Besieged (1998): the house as a room

Besieged, in Italian L'assedio, was written by Bertolucci and Clare Peploe from James Lasdun's short story "The Siege", produced by Massimo Cortesi, shot by Fabio Cianchetti, edited by Jacopo Quadri, with music by Alessio Vlad, and stars Thandiwe Newton, credited as Thandie Newton, as Shandurai, David Thewlis as Jason Kinsky, and Claudio Santamaria as Agostino; it premiered at Toronto on 14 September 1998, runs 93 minutes, and took $2,048,740. [C32164] Its dating in secondary sources is flagged: an IMDb mini-biography dates it 1997 and an encyclopedia.com entry dates it 1999, the American release year, against the film page's 1998, and the study uses 1998. [C32165] The plot is a love story told almost without words: Shandurai watches in an unnamed African town as a school teacher is taken away by police; in Rome she is a housemaid for Kinsky, an eccentric English pianist and composer and a promising medical student, who is in love with her and sends gifts down to her room via the dumbwaiter, one gift a ring, leading to an impassioned marriage proposal, which she rejects; asked how he could make her love him, she shouts, "Get my husband out of jail!" -- only then does Kinsky realise Shandurai is married. [C32166] Kinsky begins composing using Shandurai, vacuuming nearby, as his muse, and hearing his piano riffs she begins to fall in love herself; he bargains with an African priest over an undisclosed transaction, and coming home from school Shandurai looks up to see Kinsky's piano being lowered into a truck -- he has sold it, and from a letter she discovers he raised the money to secure her husband's release, and he is coming to Rome. [C32167] The night before his arrival, Shandurai unbuttons the sleeping Kinsky's shirt and curls up with him in bed; morning arrives with her husband ringing the doorbell below, Shandurai at first does not react, then she gets up, and the film ends with her husband still waiting by the door. [C32168]

The house is the film. [C32169] Most of the action was filmed and set at 8 vicolo del Bottino in Rome -- the spelling "Borttino" in one mirror is a stale-mirror variant, and the study uses "Bottino" -- a villa Bertolucci and Peploe first found largely unfurnished, with bare walls and an overgrown garden, which met the director's criteria for a spiral staircase and which he liked for its two contrasting facades, one overlooking a narrow street leading to a subway station, the other adjacent to the Spanish Steps; street scenes in both locations were filmed with a hidden camera, letting actors interact with passersby, and a hand-held camera was used for about half the shots. [C32170] The focal-length mechanism is documented on the film page itself, through Fernando Meirelles, who drew inspiration from Besieged for City of God: Bertolucci first used long focal length lenses, then shifted to shorter focal lengths as the movie progressed, thereby widening the view and deepening the focus, so that, as Meirelles put it, the audience begins "to see where the stairs lead, and Bertolucci begins to explain the geography. In almost the last shot, the camera looks out from the roof of the house, which by now you really know; I could draw this house. Then it pans across the metro and the square behind the building and you see the house in the world. The story begins in a labyrinth and at the end you understand everything." [C32171] Meirelles took the reverse approach with City of God, moving from 32mm and 40mm shots of the skyline to longer focal-length shots of alleyways to convey entrapment. [C32172] Bertolucci conceived the project as a one-hour television movie but expanded it for theatrical release after seeing the rushes projected on a large screen, and it remained a low-budget film with a small crew and a brisk shooting schedule of twenty to twenty-five scenes each day, roughly four times the director's usual pace. [C32173] By Peploe's and Bertolucci's own account the project -- his first love story -- was particularly apt for them as a married couple, Peploe served as both screenwriter and, informally, as co-director, and Bertolucci was intent on minimising exposition, leaving it to the audience to piece together the relationships, so that Thewlis too had to guess about his character: Kinsky was conceived as an "enigma" with no backstory other than having inherited his villa from an aunt. [C32174] Stephen Holden in the New York Times called the minimal dialogue "a refreshing departure in what he termed the age of television psychobabble" and "a purposefully romantic exploration of the nonverbal connections between people that can blossom into love... the feelings that this love story examines are built up more through music than through talking, and through mysterious deeds, carried out by one character and observed by the other, that don't seem to add up. Even after the story ends, a lurking sense of mystery hovers." [C32175] Roger Ebert was very negative, one star out of four: "a movie about whether two people with nothing in common, who have no meaningful conversations, will have sex... How can a director of such sophistication, in a film of such stylistic grace, tell such a shallow and evasive story?" [C32176]

**Takeaway:** Besieged is the late-period mechanism in its purest form: a house that starts as a labyrinth and ends as a map. The camera learns the house the way the characters do -- long lenses first, so the rooms are flat and unreadable, then shorter lenses, so the stairs lead somewhere and the geography explains itself -- and the last shot looks out from the roof at the house in the world. The piano is sold to free a husband, the music is the confession, and the film ends on a doorbell: the husband is waiting below, the wife is getting up, and the choice is still unmade. It is the only Bertolucci ending that refuses to end.

## 15. The Dreamers (2003): cinephilia as a closed room

The Dreamers, in French Innocents: The Dreamers, was written by Gilbert Adair from his 1988 novel The Holy Innocents, produced by Jeremy Thomas, shot by Fabio Cianchetti, edited by Jacopo Quadri, and stars Michael Pitt as Matthew, Eva Green as Isabelle, Louis Garrel as Theo, and Robin Renucci and Anna Chancellor as the parents; it opened in Italy on 10 October 2003, runs 115 minutes, cost 15.9 million euros -- Simple Wikipedia gives $15 million, a currency and figure difference the study records -- and took $23.7 million worldwide. [C32177] The plot is Paris in 1968 as a closed room: Matthew, a shy American exchange student in Paris for a year to study French, is at the Cinematheque Francaise protesting the firing of its director, Henri Langlois, when he meets free-spirited twins Theo and Isabelle, children of a famous French poet and his English wife, and the three bond over a shared love of film. [C32178] After dinner with their parents, the twins invite Matthew to stay while their parents leave town for a month; he accepts, and the trio invent games with forfeits, re-enact films -- including a run through the Louvre from Bande a part, breaking the world record -- and argue playfully about Chaplin versus Keaton, Clapton versus Hendrix, and Maoism, which Theo fervently believes in. [C32179] After Theo loses at a trivia game, Isabelle sentences him to masturbate to a Marlene Dietrich poster in front of them; after Matthew loses, Theo tells him to have sex with Isabelle in front of him, and Matthew reluctantly does, afterwards realising she was a virgin, and Theo is pleased. [C32180] The next morning the parents arrive home and find the sleeping trio naked in bed together; startled, they let them be, departing after leaving a cheque; Isabelle wakes, discovers the cheque, realises their parents have found them out, and wordlessly attaches a hose to the gas outlet and lies back down with the still-asleep Theo and Matthew, attempting murder-suicide. [C32181] Shortly afterwards they are awakened by a brick hurled through the window; hundreds of students are rioting in the streets; overjoyed, all three join the protesters, Theo joins a small team preparing Molotov cocktails, Matthew tries to stop Theo by kissing him and Isabelle and arguing against violence, but is shunned by both, and as Matthew walks away through the chaos Theo takes Isabelle's hand and hurls a Molotov cocktail at a line of police, and the police charge the crowd. [C32182] In pre-production Bertolucci made changes: he "peppered the narrative with clips from the films he loves" and dropped homosexual content, including scenes from the novel depicting Matthew and Theo having sex, which he felt was "just too much", and after release he said the film was "faithful to the spirit of the book but not the letter". [C32183] Eva Green, in her first credited film appearance and her breakthrough role, said "even though there were lots of nude scenes, I was desperate to do it... I loved the story", despite not being confident in her body, and her agent and parents begged her not to take the role, concerned the film -- full frontal and rear nudity, graphic sex scenes -- would cause her career to "have the same destiny as Maria Schneider". [C32184] Two versions exist, an uncut NC-17-rated version and an R-rated version about three minutes shorter; Fox Searchlight gave the uncut version a limited American theatrical release in 2004, and even with its NC-17 rating it grossed $2.5 million in its American theatrical release. [C32185] The reception was warm: A.O. Scott called it "disarmingly sweet and completely enchanting", "fus[ing] sexual discovery with political tumult by means of a heady, heedless romanticism", The Times called it "a heady blend of Last Tango and Stealing Beauty", and Roger Ebert gave it four stars, "poignant" and "extraordinarily beautiful". [C32186] In March 2024 a 4K restoration completed by Cineteca di Bologna with supervision from Cianchetti was released on Ultra HD Blu-ray, alongside a British re-release on 13 May 2024 to celebrate the film's twentieth anniversary. [C32187]

**Takeaway:** The Dreamers is the closed room as a film school: three young people who know cinema better than they know life, inventing games with forfeits in a Paris apartment while their parents are away, and the brick through the window is the outside world breaking in to end the game. The film's tenderness is that it loves the trio and still shows them throwing the Molotov cocktail -- the dreamers choose the street over the apartment, and the film does not pretend the choice is innocent. It is Bertolucci filming his own youth, forty years late, and finding it both beautiful and already doomed.

## 16. Me and You (2012): the cellar

Me and You, in Italian Io e te, was written by Niccolo Ammaniti, Umberto Contarello, Francesca Marciano and Bertolucci from Ammaniti's 2010 novel, produced by Fausto Brizzi, Mario Gianani and Lorenzo Mieli, shot by Fabio Cianchetti, edited by Jacopo Quadri, with music by Franco Piersanti, and stars Jacopo Olmo Antinori as Lorenzo and Tea Falco as Olivia; it screened out of competition at the Cannes Film Festival on Tuesday 22 May 2012, opened in Italy on 25 October 2012, runs 103 minutes, and took $2.6 million. [C32188] It was his final feature film before his death in 2018, his first feature in ten years since The Dreamers, and his first feature in Italian in thirty years by his own count in the Cannes press kit -- "It's been 30 years since I made a film in Italian" -- though Variety says thirty-two years and BFI Sight and Sound says "nearly 30", and the arithmetic from the last Italian-language feature, Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man (1981), to 2012 is thirty-one years, which matches none of the three wordings exactly; the study carries all three. [C32189] The premise is a cellar: Lorenzo, an introverted fourteen-year-old who has difficulties communicating with other people and pays more attention to his inner world, secretly settles in the basement of his apartment building when his class goes on a week-long ski trip to the mountains, and his loneliness is interrupted by an unexpected visit from his older half-sister Olivia, twenty-five, a heroin addict, over seven days. [C32190] Bertolucci changed the ending from the novel, in which Olivia dies, saying, "I don't like the fact that the character of the sister, who is a heroin addict, dies. Because in all movies and books that talk about addiction, the addict is punished and dies" -- carried as a search extract, from a Variety interview. [C32191] The cellar was shot in a big basement -- actually the studio of Italian artist Sandro Chia -- in Rome's Trastevere quarter, near Bertolucci's ground-floor residence, and he said in the Cannes press kit, "In ME AND YOU, I made sure that this one cellar had a different look in every scene, a basement storage room designed to be transformed by the boy, Lorenzo, and by the lighting", calling the film "a Kammerspiele-Kolossal", a chamber-play colossal, inspired by silent expressionist German movies of the 1920s. [C32192] He originally intended to shoot the film in 3D but was forced to abandon the plan due to cost, and after tests at Cinecitta he realised shooting in 3D would mean waits of two or three hours between each change in camera position. [C32193] The wheelchair is the production's other fact: following a fall in which he injured his back, an injury that a course of surgery failed to correct, he had been confined to a wheelchair, and it was widely believed -- not least by the director himself -- that he would never film again, yet after shooting he said in the press kit, "Having shot ME AND YOU I feel I'm back on the run and I'm ready to make another film as soon as possible." [C32194] The reception was respectful and muted: Rotten Tomatoes records 67%, "A minor entry in a magnificent filmography, Me and You doesn't stand with Bertolucci's greatest works, but it's still an engaging character study", and Philip French called it "a lightweight disappointing affair". [C32195]

**Takeaway:** The last film is the first film's room, shrunk to a cellar: one basement storage room, a different look in every scene, transformed by a boy and by the lighting, and a director in a wheelchair who was told he would never film again, filming anyway. The changed ending is the whole late career in one decision -- the addict does not die, because in all movies and books the addict is punished and dies, and he refuses to punish her. After fifty years of rooms with rules, the last room is the kindest one: two siblings, seven days, and nobody is punished for being in it.

## 17. Late life, death, and the screenplay that outlived him

In 2007 Bertolucci received the Golden Lion for his life's work at the Venice Film Festival, and in 2011 he received the Honorary Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival; the lead of his article also credits him with a BAFTA Award, a Cesar Award and two Golden Globes. [C32196] In spring 2018, in an interview with the Italian edition of Vanity Fair, he announced he was preparing a new film: "The theme will be love, let's call it that. In reality, the theme is communication and therefore also incommunicability. The favorite subject of Michelangelo Antonioni and the condition I found myself facing when I moved on from my films for the few, those of the sixties, to a broader cinema ready to meet a large audience." [C32197] He died of lung cancer in Rome on Monday 26 November 2018, at the age of 77; the article's Death section is a single sentence, and the circumstances come from same-day obituary reporting, carried as extracts: he died at his home in Rome at 7 a.m. on Monday, surrounded by family, confirmed by his publicist Flavia Schiavi, and Rome's city hall hosted a wake on the Tuesday, with a private funeral later in the week and a public commemorative ceremony planned. [C32198] The tributes named him the last of his line: the Venice Film Festival said "He will be remembered among the greatest in Italian and world film", Roberto Benigni and Nicoletta Braschi said "The last emperor of Italian film is gone", and his producer Jeremy Thomas said, "He was like a brother to me. We spoke very regularly. It is a tough day. He was a wonderful man, one of the greats and the best of collaborators. He was a monumental and inspirational figure, the last of the great Italian filmmakers from that era." [C32199] He was survived by his wife, the screenwriter and director Clare Peploe, and they had no children. [C32200] His final screenplay outlived him: in 2026, Bertolucci's final screenplay, The Echo Chamber, written shortly before his death, was turned into a feature film directed by Italian filmmaker Andrea Pallaoro, and the filmography lists it as writer only, director no. [C32201] He was an atheist "though he was fascinated by Buddhism", advocated Transcendental Meditation, and was a professed Marxist -- positions carried from the article, and the Buddhism fascination is directly relevant to Little Buddha. [C32202]

**Takeaway:** He announced a film about communication and incommunicability in the spring of 2018 and died in the autumn, and the film that finally got made from his last screenplay arrived eight years later, directed by someone else. The late honours -- the Golden Lion, the Honorary Palme d'Or -- are the industry's way of saying the career is over while the man is still alive, and the tributes' repeated word is "last": the last emperor, the last of the great Italian filmmakers from that era. He spent fifty years filming rooms with rules, and his own last room was a house in Rome at seven in the morning, surrounded by family, with the work still unfinished.

## 18. The network

Vittorio Storaro, born 24 June 1940 in Rome, is the visual half of the Bertolucci look: he shot The Conformist (1970), Last Tango in Paris (1972), 1900 (1976), The Last Emperor (1987, Academy Award for Best Cinematography), The Sheltering Sky (1990, BAFTA and New York Film Critics Circle cinematography awards) and Little Buddha (1993, with the 65mm Todd-AO flashbacks), and the run ends there -- Stealing Beauty (1996) was shot by Darius Khondji, and Besieged (1998), The Dreamers (2003) and Me and You (2012) by Fabio Cianchetti. [C32203] Giuseppe Bertolucci, the younger brother, born 27 February 1947 and died 16 June 2012, co-wrote the 1900 screenplay with Franco Arcalli and Bernardo, and died six months before Me and You was released in Italy. [C32204] Clare Peploe, born 20 October 1941 and died 23 June 2021, married to Bertolucci in 1979 by the infoboxes -- though obituaries say 1978, a flagged conflict -- was second assistant director on 1900, co-wrote La Luna (1979) and Besieged (1998, also informal co-director), and directed Triumph of Love (2001), which Bertolucci wrote and produced; her brother Mark Peploe co-wrote The Last Emperor, The Sheltering Sky and Little Buddha with Bertolucci. [C32205] Jeremy Thomas produced The Last Emperor, The Sheltering Sky, Little Buddha, Stealing Beauty and The Dreamers, won the Best Picture Oscar for The Last Emperor, and the article says Bertolucci worked with him "almost exclusively from then on" after The Last Emperor. [C32206] Ennio Morricone scored Before the Revolution (1964), Partner (1968, indicated but not verified end to end), 1900 (1976) and La Luna (1979) and Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man (1981) -- but he did NOT score The Conformist, whose music was by Georges Delerue, and he scored none of the 1987-2012 films, whose scores belong to Sakamoto, Byrne and Cong Su, Sakamoto and Horowitz, Sakamoto alone, Richard Hartley, Alessio Vlad, period songs, and Franco Piersanti; the Morricone collaboration belongs to 1964-1981, not to the late period. [C32207] Gato Barbieri composed the Last Tango score, arranged and conducted by Oliver Nelson, recorded 20-25 November 1972 in Rome, and the Grammy position is flagged: the Last Tango page lists a 1974 Grammy nomination for Best Score Soundtrack, while some secondary sources claim a win, unresolved from the sources read. [C32208] Ryuichi Sakamoto, David Byrne and Cong Su won the Original Score Oscar for The Last Emperor, Sakamoto with Richard Horowitz won the Golden Globe for The Sheltering Sky, and Sakamoto alone scored Little Buddha, whose final cue "Acceptance" he called his favourite piece from all of his film scores; Sakamoto also acted in The Last Emperor as Masahiko Amakasu. [C32209] The editors are a second family: Franco Arcalli cut La commare secca's successors through 1900, Gabriella Cristiani cut The Last Emperor (Oscar) and The Sheltering Sky, Pietro Scalia cut Little Buddha and Stealing Beauty, and Jacopo Quadri cut Besieged, The Dreamers and Me and You. [C32210] Ferdinando Scarfiotti designed The Conformist, Last Tango in Paris, The Last Emperor (Oscar) and The Sheltering Sky, so the production design of the fascist halls and the Forbidden City is one man's work across twenty years. [C32211]

The network is a household, and like a household it is built on marriage and blood: a brother co-writes the epic, a wife co-writes the love story, a brother-in-law co-writes the trilogy, a producer raises the money by scouring the phone book, and a cinematographer stays for six films and then hands the camera to two successors. [C32212] Bertolucci collects people the way Pasolini collected people -- from the family, from the set, from the marriage -- and then keeps them for decades, so the credits of the last film read like the credits of the first, with the same names in different jobs. [C32213]

**Takeaway:** Nobody in the network is hired for one film; everybody is kept. That is the production rule and the aesthetic rule at once: the same cinematographer, the same editor, the same producer, the same family, film after film, so that the look of a Bertolucci film is not a style applied to a story but a household that has learned to see together. When Storaro leaves after Little Buddha, the light changes -- and the films know it, and get smaller, quieter, closer to the room.

## 19. Aesthetic logic: what makes a Bertolucci image a Bertolucci image

The closed room with its own rules: Bertolucci repeatedly seals his characters inside one bounded space -- a palace, a flat, a house, a cellar, a desert leg of a journey -- and lets the space impose its own law until the outside breaks back in, and the exits and intrusions (the expulsion from the Forbidden City in 1924, the brick through the window in The Dreamers, the doorbell in Besieged, the parents' return) are the plot's hinges. [C32214] The flashback that rewinds a life: the present is a cell or a room, and the past is replayed from it until the present changes -- The Last Emperor opens in Fushun Prison in 1950 and the whole life from 1908 to 1967 unspools as flashback, with a film-within-the-film as the trigger that finally makes Puyi recant, and Little Buddha tells Siddhartha's life as a story read from a book until the past merges with the present and the children find themselves inside the scene. [C32215] History told through a body: large historical forces register as changes in one person's body -- confinement, illness, appetite, virginity, exhaustion -- so Puyi's three acts are three bodies, Port Moresby's body fails in the desert and the marriage ends with it, Siddhartha's body is the argument of Little Buddha, Lucy's body is the plot's clock in Stealing Beauty, and Shandurai's body is the bargaining chip in Besieged. [C32216] Colour and format as temperature: Storaro and his successors change the image's temperature, grain and format to mark which world the characters are in -- the Buddha flashbacks in 65mm Todd-AO against the washed-out 35mm present in Little Buddha, the Bacon oranges against wintry Paris grays in Last Tango, the washed-out neutrals flaring into garish primaries in The Conformist -- so the audience can tell which layer of time it is in by the look before a word is spoken. [C32217] Music as the emotional register: when words fail or are deliberately withheld, music carries the feeling -- Kinsky composes using Shandurai vacuuming nearby as his muse and sells his piano to free her husband, the Last Emperor score wins the Oscar and the Grammy, the Sheltering Sky score wins the Golden Globe, and The Dreamers has no original score in the usual sense, its register borrowed from Hendrix, the Doors, Piaf and Dylan plus clips from the films the characters love. [C32218] The camera that moves like a thought: the camera does not cut to explain, it travels, follows or floats until the space and the feeling are understood together -- the elegant lateral tracking shots of The Conformist, the hand-held camera for half of Besieged, the hidden camera in the Roman streets, the focal-length progression that widens the view as the house becomes a map -- with the honesty flag that none of the seven film pages read end to end in the late leg names a specific celebrated long take by name, so the mechanism rests on the documented movement vocabulary, not on a single famous shot. [C32219] The borrowed armature: he builds his films on other writers' skeletons -- Pasolini's short story, Stendhal's novel, Dostoevsky's novella, Moravia's novel, Puyi's autobiography, Bowles's novel, Lasdun's short story, Ammaniti's novel -- and then re-times them, deciding the flashback structure in post-production, intercutting the wedding with the lesson, and letting the cut do the arguing. [C32220] The false crime: a man builds his entire adult life on a murder he never committed (The Conformist), a ransom is collected for a son who is already dead (Tragedy of a Ridiculous Man), a father is identified from a poem about a mother's body (Stealing Beauty) -- the central event turns out never to have happened, or to have happened differently, and the revelation comes only at the end, when the dead figure walks back into frame. [C32221]

The synthesis is a room that thinks. [C32222] A bounded space with its own rules, a camera that learns the space before the characters do, a palette that changes temperature when the world changes, a body that carries the history the dialogue will not say, and a cut that re-times the borrowed story until the meaning lives between the shots rather than inside them. The system is not realism and not stylisation: it is a document of a room plus a quotation laid over it -- a painting, a novel, a piece of music, a childhood game on the railroad tracks. [C32223]

**Takeaway:** Every Bertolucci mechanism is the same move in a different costume: take a privileged person, seal them in a room with rules, and watch the room do the moral work the character will not do. The conformist frames himself in bars, the emperor is expelled from his palace, the tourist is erased by the desert, the dreamers are broken open by a brick, and the boy in the cellar is the only one who gets to choose his room -- and chooses to stay in it with his sister. The room is not the setting; it is the sentence.
