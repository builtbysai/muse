## The life: the Mexican who replaced literature with its distribution

Ulises Carrion Bogard was born 29 January 1941 in San Andres Tuxtla, Veracruz, Mexico, and died 6 October 1989 in Amsterdam, of AIDS; his last words as reported by Guy Schraenen were "Do not be sad. You have my books and videos." (C11300, C11302) His age at death is carried both ways: 48 by arithmetic (29 January 1941 to 6 October 1989) against "forty-nine" in Schraenen and the Reina Sofia page -- do not state one figure alone (C11458).

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is the book: the work leaves literature, enters the book as a physical sequence, and then leaves the book to become the shop, the press, the magazine and the postal system that carry it.

(1) Mexico writer, 1941-1970. He studied philosophy and literature at UNAM; won a state prize for short stories in 1960; held a Centro Mexicano de Escritores grant in 1963-64; studied at the Sorbonne and the Goethe Institute in 1964-66; took an MA in English at Leeds in 1972 with a thesis on "Judas' Kiss and Shakespeare's Henry VIII"; spoke seven languages; corresponded with Octavio Paz, whose Plural no. 41 (February 1975) first published "El arte nuevo de hacer libros". His first books are narrative fiction inside the literary system: La muerte de Miss O (1966) and De Alemania (1970), two short-story collections praised by literary journals. (C11304-C11317)

(2) Leaving and the Amsterdam turn, 1970-1975. He left Mexico for Europe in 1970 and settled in Amsterdam in January 1972, in an attic on the Nicolaas Maasstraat that Michel Cardena had left him (Monoskop's "from 1970" is the travel date, not the settlement date). In 1972-74 he co-founded the In-Out Center with eight others (Cardena, Raul Marroquin, Hreinn Fridfinnsson, Sigurdur Gudmundsson, Kristjan Gudmundsson, Pieter Huisman, Pieter Mol, Pieter de Rook). His first bookwork, Sonnet(s), appeared in December 1972: 44 permutations of Dante Gabriel Rossetti's sonnet "Heart's Compass" (1871), typewritten, staple-bound mimeograph, black and white, stapled between clear plastic wraps, edition of 200, dedicated to Raul Marroquin. At the In-Out Center opening, 24 November 1972, he hung The Collected Plagiarisms: six existing books whose authors' names he had taped over and replaced with his own. His first solo show, Texts and Other Texts, ran 28 May-9 June 1973; its invitation was torn into four parts and no documents of the show are said to survive. In 1973 Beau Geste Press published his Arguments and Looking for Poetry / Tras la poesia; twelve bookworks followed in 1972-1974 with Beau Geste Press and In-Out Productions. The break is theorised in "The New Art of Making Books", written May 1974 and first published February 1975 in Plural no. 41: "A book is a sequence of spaces... a space-time sequence"; "In the old art the writer writes texts. In the new art the writer makes books." (C11318-C11333)

(3) Infrastructure years, 1975-1980. In April 1975 he opened Other Books and So with Aart van Barneveld, in a basement at 227 Herengracht, Amsterdam; the shop moved in 1977 to 259 Herengracht (the Schraenen catalogue says 257 -- carry both; 259 has the E.A.M.I.S. imprint behind it). It was the first bookshop dedicated specifically to artists' books, magazines and multiples, and held over 50 exhibitions, readings and performances, with catalogues nos. 1-4 (1975-1977) and a 1977 open call inviting a thousand artists to mail their publications in. It closed at the end of 1978 for lack of funding and failure to get grants (DPUL; Hamerman says winter 1978-79; Reina Sofia gives the range 1975-79 as the rounded form), and became the Other Books and So Archive in 1980, at 121 Bloemgracht, which Carrion called an artwork with no time limit: "We've reached a privileged historical moment when keeping an archive can be an artwork." Daylight Press launched in 1975: an editorial imprint, a name and address other artists were free to use for their own publications; Accidence by Michael Gibbs was the first of five books issued under the name, and in 1976 Padin's Happy Bicentennial was published through Daylight Press and mailed to the United States by Carrion, because Padin could not safely mail it himself. Ephemera ran for 12 monthly numbers, November 1977 to October 1978, edited by Carrion with van Barneveld and Salvador Flores and published by Other Books and So: a tabloid assembling magazine of whatever arrived in the post that month, reproduced at original size in collage layout, with artistamps and multiples tipped in. No. 5 (February 1978) carried the Padin/Caraballo imprisonment on its cover -- "Padin and Caraballo are in jail!" -- with "Ask for information. Transmit it."; no. 7 is a Carrion special issue of handwritten mundane social interactions; no. 12 (Brazil, October 1978) is the final issue. In March 1977 the shop hosted Definitions of Art, an idea by H. W. Kalkmann: postcards sent out asking recipients to write their definition of art on the verso; 367 answers came back and all were displayed in the bookshop. "Mail Art and the Big Monster" was written in 1977, first read at the I.AM International Artist's Meeting in Warsaw in April 1978 (Galeria Remont), with leaflets distributed, and printed in February 1979; a Lund 1979 poster claiming a "first presentation" conflicts with Warsaw and should not be repeated. E.A.M.I.S., the Erratic Art Mail International System, was presented alongside that reading in Warsaw in 1978 (the essay is dated 1977 in some sources; carry essay 1977 / system presented 1978): a mimeographed flyer announcement, eight rules, Carrion as Postmaster, office at Herengracht 259, hours Tuesday-Saturday 14.00-18.00, telephone 020-257041; travelling friends as couriers, stamps and seals for verification, free delivery, undelivered items returned after three years, a duplicate kept in the archive. Hamerman calls it "largely a conceptual piece" -- and that is the point. (C11336-C11343, C11344-C11347, C11360-C11362)

(4) Late work, 1981-1989. The same structural attention turns from paper to people, television and film: Gossip, Scandal and Good Manners (1981; one source says 1982), in which Carrion asked ten friends to spread gossip about him over three months in Amsterdam, tracked its evolution, then lectured on the process at the University of Amsterdam and made a 45-minute video; The Death of the Art Dealer (1982), his only film work, 19 minutes 57 seconds, made with Danniel Danniel to document a performance in which he carried a small monitor playing an obscure Hollywood film, imitating with his body what happened on screen, switching the monitor off and on at every cut; The Robbery / Theft of the Year (1982); Trios and Boleros (1983); the Lilia Prado Superstar Film Festival (1984) at De Appel, Amsterdam (and Kriterion cinema plus three other locations), honouring the Mexican actress Lilia Prado -- Carrion went to Mexico to negotiate loans of four of her films and to invite her, "transfer a celebrity from one culture to another with completely different values"; Lilia Prado as his readymade; documented in the video The LPS File (1985, 35 minutes 39 seconds). He co-founded the Vereniging van Videokunstenaars / Time Based Arts -- 1982 by van Barneveld per DPUL, co-founded 1983 with Carrion per the catalogue chronology and the Toronto finding aid; carry both. Dutch citizenship in 1984 rests on a single source (Reina Sofia catalogue chronology) and is flagged as such. He died in Amsterdam on 6 October 1989, of AIDS. Aftermath: his archive went to Juan J. Agius; his books were dismantled to Switzerland (de Rook); the memorial "We have won! Haven't we?" was organised by Schraenen at Museum Fodor in 1992; the retrospective "Dear Reader. Don't Read" showed at the Reina Sofia, 15 March-10 October 2016, about 350 pieces, curated by Schraenen, and toured to Museo Jumex, February-May 2017; Princeton's "Bookworks and Beyond" ran 21 February-13 June 2024, the largest US retrospective to date. (Leg 1 phase 6)

In his own words. On books: "There are so many books", "They make reading unnecessary", "Art is not private property" -- the three sentences behind the Collected Plagiarisms, plagiarism as giving a book a second chance to be read. On the new art: "In the old art the writer writes texts. In the new art the writer makes books." On the archive: "We've reached a privileged historical moment when keeping an archive can be an artwork." On mail art: "It has been said that Mail Art is easy, cheap, unpretentious and democratic. All this is rubbish." And on the Monster, from "Mail Art and the Big Monster": the state postal bureaucracy owns the castle and separates us one from the others; every invitation to a mail-art project is part of the guerrilla war against it, every piece a weapon thrown at the Monster. On his last words: "Do not be sad. You have my books and videos." (C11328, C11330, C11336-C11343, C11348-C11349 proximity, C11300, C11302)

Evolution. Early Mexican work (1966-1970) is narrative fiction: two praised short-story collections, La muerte de Miss O (1966) and De Alemania (1970), written inside the literary system. The Amsterdam turn (1972-1975) breaks with literature from inside language itself: Sonnet(s) (1972) plagiarises one sonnet 44 times, Poesias (1972) reduces poems to rhythm, repetition and graphic outlines, and Arguments / Looking for Poetry (1973) make poems out of first names and the search for poetry itself; the break is theorised in The New Art of Making Books (1975), which declares there will be no new literature, only new ways to communicate, and makes the book -- not the text -- the work. The middle Amsterdam period (1975-1980) builds the infrastructure that the theory needs: a bookshop that is itself a work (Other Books and So), an imprint anyone may use (Daylight Press), an assembling magazine made of the daily mail (Ephemera), a theory and a counter-postal system for mail art (Mail Art and the Big Monster, E.A.M.I.S.), and participatory mail projects that invite the addressee to finish the work (Definitions of Art, Feedback Pieces). The late period (1981-1987) turns the same structural attention from paper to people, television and film: gossip tracked as a communication chain (1981), a film made by imitating a monitor (1982), a film festival that transplants a celebrity between cultures (1984), and video essays that treat television as a frame that makes everything equally real (TV-Tonight, 1987; Bookworks Revisited video, 1986/1987). Arc in one line: from stories told inside literature, to books that dismantle literature, to shops, presses, magazines and postal systems that replace literature's distribution, to gossip, television and festivals that test the same structures on living audiences. (C11377)

## Mechanisms

M1 -- The book as a sequence of spaces. The book is not a container for a text; it is a space-time sequence, and the message is the sum of all its material and formal elements. Works: W08 (the manifesto), W02 (one sonnet permuted 44 times), W04 (poems made of first names), W20 (a 400-page book with no text, only altered structure). Watchable behaviour: pages turning as rooms, each spread a different moment; the same text returning again and again in a new arrangement until the arrangement, not the words, is the content. Takeaway: change the sequence and you change the book, even when the words never change. (C11357, C11351, C11353, C11369)

M2 -- Plagiarism as the starting point. Existing books are taken, their authors' names hidden under black tape and replaced with his own, and the copy is exhibited as the work; a sonnet is plagiarised 44 times over. Works: The Collected Plagiarisms (1972, six books hung on the wall at the In-Out Center opening), W02. Watchable behaviour: familiar book covers sliding into frame with the author's name taped over and "Ulises Carrion" pasted in its place; the same sonnet re-typed, re-spaced and re-broken until the original is unrecognisable as anyone's but his. Takeaway: "There are so many books", "They make reading unnecessary", "Art is not private property" -- plagiarism gives a book a second chance to be read. (C11330, C11328)

M3 -- The bookshop as the work. A shop that sells only artists' books becomes the work itself: over 50 exhibitions, readings and performances in a basement, catalogues, theme shows, an open call to a thousand artists to mail their publications in. Works: W09 (Other Books and So), and its afterlife, the Other Books and So Archive (OBASA, from 1980), which Carrion called an artwork with no time limit: "We've reached a privileged historical moment when keeping an archive can be an artwork." Watchable behaviour: a basement shop filling with books that arrived by post, the shelves thickening as envelopes arrive daily; when the shop closes, the shelves do not empty -- they become an archive, and visitors are photographed as they enter it. Takeaway: the shop is not where the work is sold, the shop IS the work, and the archive is its second act. (C11336-C11343, C11358)

M4 -- The imprint anyone may use. A press that is only a name and an address, offered freely to other artists for their own publications -- affiliation, not commerce. Works: W10 (Daylight Press, launched 1975; Accidence by Michael Gibbs first of five; Padin's Happy Bicentennial printed and mailed to the US in 1976). Watchable behaviour: a name and address stamped onto books that were made elsewhere, by other hands; each new imprint line is a handshake, and the press grows without ever printing a page itself. Takeaway: the publisher's name is a signal, not a factory -- lend it and the network publishes itself. (C11344-C11347, C11359)

M5 -- The assembling magazine made of the daily mail. Each issue is whatever arrived in the post that month, reproduced at original size in collage layout, with artistamps and multiples tipped in; the editor does not commission, he receives. Works: W11 (Ephemera, 12 issues, Nov 1977-Oct 1978); no. 5 (Feb 1978) makes the Padin/Caraballo imprisonment its cover, "Padin and Caraballo are in jail!", and must be cut open along a dotted line to be read. Watchable behaviour: envelopes arriving daily and piling into a tabloid page; the month's mail becoming the month's magazine, and one issue's cover becoming a call to arms that reaches the US State Department. Takeaway: the magazine is the mail, edited by arrival -- the postman is the co-editor. (C11360)

M6 -- Mail art as guerrilla war against the Big Monster. The state postal bureaucracy is the Monster that owns the castle and separates us one from the others; every invitation to a mail-art project is part of the guerrilla war against it, every piece a weapon thrown at the Monster. Works: W12 ("Mail Art and the Big Monster", written 1977, first read Warsaw April 1978, printed Feb 1979). Watchable behaviour: envelopes thrown, one by one, at a castle wall; each lands as a small explosion of paper, and the wall never falls but the throwers keep coming, because the throwing is the point. Takeaway: "It has been said that Mail Art is easy, cheap, unpretentious and democratic. All this is rubbish." -- the mail is the support the way canvas is the support, and the Monster is real even when you cannot name it. (C11348-C11349 proximity, C11361)

M7 -- The alternative postal system. When the official post cannot be trusted, build your own: a Postmaster, eight rules, travelling friends as couriers, stamps and seals for verification, free delivery, undelivered items returned after three years, a duplicate kept in the archive. Works: W13 (E.A.M.I.S., Erratic Art Mail International System, presented Warsaw 1978); the Bruscky postcards carried by E.A.M.I.S. to Horacio Zabala (C11405). Watchable behaviour: an envelope handed from friend to friend across a map, never entering a post office; each hand-off stamped and sealed, the route drawn live, and the letter arriving later, more creased, and entirely outside the Monster's reach. Takeaway: the system is largely conceptual and that is the point -- the rules are the work, and the friends are the infrastructure. (C11362, C11414)

M8 -- Gossip and the address archive as the work. Communication itself is the material: gossip spread by ten friends over three months and tracked like a scientific experiment; an address archive of over 1,300 index cards, classified by feeling ("My Best Friends, People I Love", "There Has Been a Change in Our Relationship of Late", "I Detest or Despise Some People") and photographed as a book; postcards that ask the recipient to finish the work. Works: W17 (Gossip, Scandal and Good Manners, 1981), W15 (In Alphabetical Order, 1978/1979), W14 (Definitions of Art, 367 answers), Feedback Pieces (1981), Rob and Marta (1983). Watchable behaviour: a rumour travelling from mouth to mouth across a city map, changing slightly at each telling, then laid out as a diagram; a filing box of cards flipping open, each card a person and a verdict, the archive itself becoming a portrait of a life in correspondence. Takeaway: the network is not the audience for the work, it is the work -- track the chain and you have the piece. (C11366, C11364, C11363, C11371, C11372)

## Aesthetic logic

His logic is the logic of the bookshop counter, the post office queue and the filing box, not the studio. The materials are the cheapest communication systems that travel: mimeograph, black and white, stapled sheets, clear plastic wraps, rubber stamps on felt pages, torn A4 graph paper bound with string, cut-and-pasted paper, wallpaper samples, felt, string. The palette, as reported in sources, is the palette of the duplicating machine and the mail: black toner on cheap paper, the coloured paper of a mimeographed poem, the brown of a cardboard box of index cards, the white of a wallpaper sample typed with a room's name. Nothing is precious; the work is designed to be typed, stapled, taped over, mailed, cut open along a dotted line, filed, photographed, and reissued as a facsimile. The through line is the system as the work: early the system is the book itself, a sequence of spaces that can be permuted without changing a word (W02, W08); then the shop that sells only such books (W09); then the imprint that lends its name (W10); then the magazine that is only what arrived (W11); then the postal system that replaces the post (W13); then the gossip chain and the address file that make the network itself visible (W17, W15). The viewer is never a spectator: they are the recipient who must cut the magazine open, the friend who must pass the envelope on, the correspondent whose definition of art is displayed in the shop, the gossip who spreads the rumour. And the politics is never illustrated: it is built into the procedure -- a book that cannot be mailed safely is printed by a friend in another country, a magazine cover that is a call to arms, a postal system invented because the official one cannot be trusted. The work that began as two praised short-story collections in Mexico in 1966 ends as a filing box of over 1,300 index cards photographed as a book, and the distance between those two gestures is the whole career: the writer who left literature now exhibits the distribution system literature used to hide inside. (C11460)

## The network

The origin node is the mail-art correspondence itself, and Carrion is its switchboard: his Princeton address archive holds over 1,300 index cards (1975-1989), each a person and a verdict, and the finding aid names Artforum, the Arts Council, Theresa Hak Kyung Cha, Guillermo Deisler, Robert Filliou, General Idea, Carolee Schneemann and Printed Matter, with Bruscky, Padin, Rehfeldt and Schraenen added in the container list (C11412).
