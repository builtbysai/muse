# Susana Baca: Study Notes

**Site:** Susana Esther Baca de la Colina (born Wednesday 24 May 1944, Chorrillos, Lima), Peruvian singer, composer, researcher of Afro-Peruvian music, educator, and former Minister of Culture, the woman who spent eleven years travelling the Peruvian coast collecting the songs of the descendants of enslaved Africans before she ever recorded them, and whose career is the proof that a songbook can be gathered the way a harvest is gathered, town by town, elder by elder, and that the gathering is the career, not the preparation for it. She has won three competitive Latin Grammys, she was Peru's Minister of Culture for four and a half months in 2011, she founded the Instituto Negrocontinuo and the Cultural Center for Memory in Santa Barbara, Can~ete, and she performs barefoot in flowing dresses of a single saturated colour, eyes closed, holding the fabric of her dress like a sail. She is not a composer in the sense of Chabuca, and that is the point of studying her here: she is the singer the composer took up and named her successor, the woman who carries the other woman's song "Maria Lando" to the world, and the chain needs her because Chabuca brings the song itself and Eva brings the carrying home, and Baca brings the carrying outward, to North America, to Europe, to the ministry, to the archive. ## M1. Fieldwork-as-repertoire

The songbook is collected, not composed, and the collecting is the mechanism. For eleven years she travelled the small towns of the Peruvian coast with her husband Ricardo Pereira, asking old men to sing the songs their great-great-grandfathers sang as slaves, taking notes or memorising them, and the book that came out of it, "Del fuego y del agua" (1992), is the record of the collecting, and the album of the same name, "Fuego y Agua" (1992), is the collecting set to music. The Encyclopedia.com entry gives the mechanism in one sentence: she travelled to small towns, asked old men to sing, took notes or memorised. The mechanism is the repertoire as fieldwork: the songs arrive with a place and a witness attached, because somebody in a town sang them to her, and the singer is not the author, she is the carrier, and the carrying starts with listening, not with writing. The career that follows is the same mechanism at every scale: the Instituto Negrocontinuo, founded in 1992 with Pereira, is the collecting made permanent, an institution that gathers, preserves and teaches, and the Cultural Center for Memory in Santa Barbara, Can~ete, is the collecting given a room, where children from the region learn their history from the music. Watch the collecting: when the singer walks into a small town and asks an old man to sing, and the song he sings becomes her song, and the song keeps the town's name on it, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** collect the song, do not compose it. The old man is the author. Against the chain: Chabuca (#361) also wrote from the street, but hers was a composing from memory, the song made in the room; Baca's is a collecting from a witness, which is why her version is a making and Baca's is a gathering. Eva (#362 M2) also learned in a small room, the pen~a as school, but hers was a school that taught her to sing; Baca's is a field that taught her to listen, which is why her version is a school and Baca's is a harvest.

## M2. The poetry-first lyric rule

She refuses lyrics that are aggressive against women, and she sings poets instead, and the refusal is the mechanism. The Sounds and Colours interview of 3 October 2010 gives it in her own words: "I need to say something that sings to people's spirits, sing them poetry." She sings the poets, Cesar Calvo above all, whose poem Chabuca musicalised into "Maria Lando", and Nicomedes Santa Cruz, the poet and historian she names as one of the Peruvian artists she learned from, and the composers she champions are the ones whose words carry weight and image rather than hook and rhyme. The mechanism is the lyric as the main event: the voice treats the words as the thing to be heard, and the arrangement steps back so the words can be heard, and the refusal is audible, because the songs she does not sing are as much the repertoire as the songs she does. The career that follows is the same mechanism at every scale: the 2021 album "Palabras Urgentes", urgent words, is the rule given a title, ten tracks chosen for what they say, and the youngest composer she champions, Andres Soto, is championed for his words, "Negra Presuntuosa" among them. Watch the refusal: when the singer will not sing the lyric that insults the woman, and sings the poem instead, and the poem is the song, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** sing the poem, refuse the insult. The words are the main event. Against the chain: Eva (#362 M5) also carried three bloods in one voice, but hers was a carrying of the mixture; Baca's is a carrying of the words, which is why her version is a mixture and Baca's is a text. Sosa (#359) also sang the poets, but hers was a singing of the continent's poets; Baca's is a singing of one coast's poets, which is why her version is a continent and Baca's is a coastline.

## M4. The lando as lament vehicle

The slow Afro-Peruvian lando carries a worker's story, and the carrying is the mechanism. "Maria Lando" is Chabuca's musicalisation of Cesar Calvo's poem, and the woman in it has no time to lift her eyes, broken by sleep, only working, and the lando is the form that lets that story be sung without hurrying it, a slow, heavy, swaying pulse under a plainspoken narrative of labour, and the dance is tired, not festive. The mechanism is the lando as the lament: the rhythm does not lift the story, it carries it, the way a worker carries a load, and the singer does not decorate the tiredness, she lets the tiredness set the tempo. The breakthrough is the proof: David Byrne heard "Maria Lando" through a Spanish-class assignment to translate the song's lyrics, then watched a video of her performing it, and put it on Luaka Bop's "Afro-Peruvian Classics: The Soul of Black Peru" in 1995, and the song carried her to North America, and the Spanish Wikipedia says the contribution of David Byrne is as important as that of Chabuca Granda in her career, because Chabuca gave her the song and Byrne gave her the world, and the song that did it was a lament about a woman too tired to lift her eyes. Watch the lament: when the slow pulse starts, and the voice tells a plain story of work, and the dance sways instead of jumping, and the tiredness is the tempo, the mechanism is on. **Takeaway:** let the tired dance carry the tired story. The lando is the load. Chabuca (#361) also wrote the song, but hers was the writing; Baca's is the carrying of the writing to the world, which is why her version is a writing and Baca's is a journey.

## M14. The percussion of found objects
