## M1. Personality-as-cause biography
Write history by making one person's temperament the motor of events, so the king's character does the explaining that institutions would otherwise do. Henry VIII is built this way: foreign policy and religion took most of Henry's time, and the reign is explained by a man "talented but seriously immature, petulant and often self-righteous, clever but not subtle", not by a bureaucratic machine. The Times obituary states the theory directly: Scarisbrick "believed that history is made by force of personality" and that Henry's "temperament and force of personality" mattered more than his "political or intellectual genius".
Takeaway: make the person the cause and the reign follows.

## M2. The balance-sheet final chapter
End a biography with a credit/debit ledger of the reign, so moral judgement arrives as arithmetic rather than as rhetoric. The final chapter of Henry VIII, "Henry the King" (p. 498), opens: "maybe he was no more unaware and irresponsible than many kings have been; but rarely, if ever, have the unawareness and irresponsibility of a king proved more costly of material benefits to his people" (second-hand via a LibraryThing reviewer). The credits -- the foundations of the nation-state, the navy, the national church, codified laws -- sit against the debits -- a prosperous realm driven into debt, Wolsey and Cromwell destroyed, "the rule of the axe".
Takeaway: close the life with a ledger and the judgement does itself.

## M3. Canon-law deep dive
Spend four chapters on scriptural and canonical minutiae, so the divorce is proved to be a real legal-theological problem, not a pretext. The divorce case in Henry VIII runs through four chapters of canon law on marriage, and the Canadian Historical Review notice (1969, second-hand via search extracts) calls it "a conclusive description and analysis of the pre-Tridentine canon law on marriage". The mechanism is duration as proof: the length of the treatment is the argument that the king's conscience was a genuine driver, not a cover story.
Takeaway: stay in the canon law long enough and the pretext becomes a problem.

## M4. Archive novelty over theory
Make newness out of access rather than out of theory, so the revision is empirical. Henry VIII rests its claim on documents nobody had used for the divorce case -- reader reviews (second-hand) report Scarisbrick was "the first historian to make use of hitherto unused documents from the Vatican library", and the study records the "first" as NOT VERIFIED, see C12: the Vatican archives were opened to scholars in 1881, and no retrieved scholarly source confirms first use. What is established is the method: new archive, not new theory, carries the book's authority.
Takeaway: a new archive beats a new theory, but only the archive is the claim.

## M5. Event-organised biography
Structure a life around the major events of the reign rather than chronology, so the narrative follows crises instead of birthdays. Henry VIII is organised by event -- "The New King", "The Coming of Wolsey", "The Quest for Peace", "The Canon Law of the Divorce", "The Campaign against the Church", "The Royal Supremacy", "The Return to War", "The Last Months", "Henry the King" -- and the Canadian Historical Review notice calls the result "a superbly written series of evocative essays" rather than a cradle-to-grave life. The same notice records the cost: the book "concentrates on only two topics: diplomacy and the Reformation" and avoids social, economic and legal history.
Takeaway: organise by crisis and the life becomes a sequence of decisions.

## M6. Wills-and-account-books evidence
Read ordinary people's wills and parish accounts to prove what they believed, instead of reading what theologians wrote. The Reformation and the English People (1984) argues from wills, account-books, late medieval church building and, above all, the popularity of the lay fraternity, guilds and chantries: the evidence of what people paid for is the evidence of what they believed. The publisher's blurb states it: "there was little violent discontent with the old Church on the eve of the Reformation" because layfolk "had been able to fashion a Church which suited their needs well enough".
Takeaway: read the wills and the theology has to answer to the parish.

## M7. Lay-fraternity popularity test
Use the popularity of lay fraternities, guilds and chantries as a proxy for popular religion, so the old Church's health is measured by membership, not by doctrine. The 1984 book's central test is that people kept joining, giving and building: the lay fraternity is the instrument that turns "the Church was healthy" from a claim into a count. Collinson (London Review of Books, 25 April 2002) records the sequence the test founded: Scarisbrick 1984 first, then Bowker, Brigden, Haigh and Duffy.
Takeaway: count the members and the corruption story collapses.

## M8. Spoliation inventory
List the takings -- religious houses, shrines, colleges, chantries, guilds and parish churches, 1536-1553 -- to make the scale of state seizure visible in one inventory. The 1984 book's spine is the inventory of what was taken and when, and the Times obituary frames the mechanism: change "was wreaked by a tyrant who was led by his passions and manipulated by his ministers and supporters, who stood to gain materially from the dissolution". The inventory is the argument that the Reformation was expropriation, not conversion.
Takeaway: list what was taken and the theology has to share the page with the loot.

## M9. Marian revival test
Check whether guilds and chantries reappeared under Mary, so the old faith's survival is proved by its return rather than asserted. The 1984 book argues the Marian period was "not so unsuccessful after all": guilds reappeared, fresh chantries were founded, schools and hospitals were restored. The test is the mirror of M7: if the old religion was dead, it could not come back; its return is the evidence it had only been suppressed.
Takeaway: watch what comes back and you learn what never died.

## M10. Popular-rebellion citation
Cite the Pilgrimage of Grace as proof of resistance, so a rebellion becomes the evidence that the people did not want the change. Scarisbrick called the Pilgrimage "the largest rebellion in English history" (Times obituary): a popular revolt against the founding of the Church of England and the dissolution of the monasteries, spreading from Yorkshire to Cumberland, Northumberland, Durham and north Lancashire. Elton's opposite reading -- a planned conspiracy by a defeated Aragonese court faction, Darcy, Hussey and Constable, the commons a "rent-a-mob" -- is answered by Hoyle (2001) and Bush (1996), who found little or no hard evidence of high-level conspiracy in 1536; Collinson (2001) finds Elton's evidence "not all that plain" and Hoyle's account "as close to [definitive] as we may hope to get".
Takeaway: the rebellion is the plebiscite the Reformation never held.

## M11. "Slow above" model
Take Haigh's four-box classification (rapid/slow against above/below) and sit in "slow above": change imposed from above, accepted slowly. Scarisbrick's position in the model is that the Reformation came from the state and took generations to take hold -- the opposite corner, "rapid below", is Dickens's position that the people wanted it and responded enthusiastically. The model does the arguing: place the historian in a box and the whole historiography sorts itself around him.
Takeaway: name the box and the debate becomes a map.

## M12. Conscience-centred kingship
Treat Leviticus and the validity of a marriage as a genuine driver of state action, so conscience is a cause, not a cover story. Henry VIII treats the divorce as a real problem of canon law and conscience -- the king's case would have been canonically strongest, Scarisbrick argues, if he had accepted Catherine's insistence on the unconsummated first marriage -- and the break with Rome follows from the conscience, not from a minister's plan. Guy's middle position (via Brooks, Reviews in History 228) reframes the same material: the divorce became the occasion for Henry to discover a jurisdictional omnicompetence "similar to that enjoyed by Roman emperors".
Takeaway: take the conscience seriously and the king becomes the author.

## M13. Ministers-as-beneficiaries
Show that the ministers who pushed the dissolution stood to gain materially from the lands, so the tyrant is manipulated by the profiteers around him. The Times obituary's framing of Scarisbrick's Henry is that he was "led by his passions and manipulated by his ministers and supporters, who stood to gain materially from the dissolution of the monasteries and the apportioning of their extensive lands". The mechanism reverses Elton's agent twice over: not the minister as the author of modern government, but the minister as the profiteer of the king's passions.
Takeaway: follow the land and the minister shrinks from author to beneficiary.

## M14. The five-directions sequel
Show that dissolution did not end with the monasteries -- the attack continued after 1540 in five new directions -- so the taking is a programme, not an episode. The 1988 chapter "Henry VIII and the dissolution of the Secular Colleges" argues the surrender of Waltham Abbey on 23 March 1540 ended medieval English monasticism but not the attack on church possessions: the bishops' unfavourable "exchanges" of land, the pruning of cathedral deans and chapters, eight former monastic cathedrals refounded in 1540/1 as secular cathedrals, six new bishoprics created 1541-1543 (Bristol, Chester, Gloucester, Oxford, Peterborough, Westminster), and a fifth direction the chapter body, paywalled in all three legs, does not name; see C29. The chapter is the clearest small-scale statement of Scarisbrick's "change from above, by a state that profits" thesis.
Takeaway: the sequel proves the episode was a programme.

## M15. Opening-sentence thesis
Put the whole argument in the first sentence -- "On the whole, Englishmen and women did not want the Reformation and most of them were slow to accept it when it came" -- so the book is a proof of a sentence rather than an exploration. Collinson says the 1984 book "declared, in its opening sentence" the thesis; one summary says the thesis is stated in the second sentence, unresolved at C19, and the study does not assert opening against second. Either way the mechanism is the same: the sentence is the book in miniature, and the 203 pages that follow are its evidence.
Takeaway: write the sentence first and the book is its proof.

## M16. National amnesis
Frame the Reformation as a people accomplishing "a radical breach with its past" and "a remarkable act of national amnesis", so the change is cultural, not only institutional. The phrase is Scarisbrick's own, from Henry VIII (1968), second-hand via Gwyn's London Review of Books review of the 1984 book: the Reformation is not only a change of government or doctrine but a collective forgetting, a nation breaking with its own past. The mechanism gives the revisionism its scale: the loss is measured in memory, not in monasteries.
Takeaway: call it forgetting and the Reformation becomes a wound, not a reform.

## M17. Pioneer-takes-the-hit
Make the first revisionist move and absorb the "card-carrying Catholic" objection, so the successors can build without answering it. Scarisbrick's 1984 book was the first in the tidal wave, and Collinson records the objection it absorbed: "Scarisbrick, a card-carrying Catholic historian, would say such things" -- until the localities work, led by non-Catholics like Haigh ("an ex-Methodist Anglican agnostic") and Bowker, made the revision undeniable. Dickens fought back with "two long and detailed essays", and consoled himself "that some of the revisers were Catholics, and when told that [Haigh] wasn't exploded 'Then why does he say such things?'"
Takeaway: the first mover takes the bias charge so the school does not have to.

## M18. One man, not two Henrys
Refuse Starkey's split into a virtuous prince and a tyrant, and hold a single flawed character across the whole reign. Scarisbrick's Henry is one man throughout: "a complex man, talented but seriously immature, petulant and often self-righteous, clever but not subtle", judged whole in the final chapter's balance sheet. Starkey's answer is the opposite framing -- two Henrys, the young virtuous prince and the old tyrant, with the break organised around the split -- and Gwyn's review of Virtuous Prince wields Scarisbrick's sentence "We know very little about his early life" against Starkey's 370-page reconstruction of the young Henry.
Takeaway: hold the man whole and the split becomes the other historian's problem.

## M19. Archive-access novelty
Make newness out of access (Vatican documents, parish records) rather than out of theory, so the revision is empirical. Henry VIII claims authority from Vatican material not previously used for the divorce case; the 1984 book claims authority from wills and account-books nobody had read as religious evidence. Both books argue the same way: the sources were there, nobody had looked at them this way, and the looking is the revision. The study marks the "first historian" wording as NOT VERIFIED, see C12, and keeps the mechanism at the level the sources support.
Takeaway: the new archive is the new argument.

## M20. Scholarship-and-activism as twin obligations
Run a historical career and a public cause in parallel, so the historian's conscience and the citizen's conscience are the same faculty in two arenas. The Catholic Herald obituary (5 March 2026, introduction only, body paywalled) frames it as "twin obligations": the scholarship (Henry VIII, 1968; The Reformation and the English People, 1984) and the public action (Life, co-founded with Nuala in August 1970, chairman to 2017; Zoe's Place Baby Hospice, founded 1995; the 1985 two-million-signature petition; the 1987 Alton Bill, 296 MPs). Scarisbrick's own words, second-hand via the Times obituary: "It's so easy just to say 'thou shalt not'... It's got to be able to always show that it's a loving, positive thing and not judgmental, not sanctimonious, not on a high horse, but actually out there, rolling up their sleeves and helping people."
Takeaway: the conscience that writes the history also founds the charity.

## M21. The king, not the minister, drives the reign
Make Henry the sole policy-maker and Cromwell the instrument. The Canadian Historical Review notice (1969, second-hand via search extracts): "Without Wolsey or Cromwell, Henry is the sole policy-maker in 1530-1 and after June 1540; therefore, we see the reign end as it began, in war and waste." Elton's opposite: Cromwell is "the architect of most of what was planned and done", and Henry was "lazy" and "bankrupt in ideas" by 1531. Guy's middle position (via Brooks): by the 1530s Henry "was both his own master and fully cognisant of the possibilities for monarchical power". G. W. Bernard's The King's Reformation (2007) argues the same way, that Henry was "the determining mover of religious policy throughout this momentous period" and "ruled as well as reigned".
Takeaway: give the reign to the king and the minister becomes the instrument.

## M22. Wolsey's fifteen-year peace policy
Argue that Wolsey's policy was a peace policy and for about fifteen years he struggled to make it work. Scarisbrick's argument, second-hand via Peter Gwyn's article on the Calais conference of 1521: Wolsey "aimed to encourage peace by aligning England with strong powers and so forcing weak states to enter negotiations", and "tried to curb Henry's aggressive tendencies". Gwyn answers that at Calais in 1521 Wolsey "had never been very serious about his role as peacemaker" and that "this thesis cannot be sustained", at least for 1521; Guy answers differently, that Wolsey's reforming rhetoric "was a hostage to his need to raise money for the expensive foreign policy he directed", and the cardinal was "both good and bad". The mechanism is the counterfactual with a clock: fifteen years of peace policy is a reign's worth of evidence.
Takeaway: give the minister fifteen years of peace and the warmonger becomes the exception.

## M23. The break as Henry's own work
Make Henry responsible for the human and parliamentary acts reforming religion. The Canadian Historical Review notice: "With Wolsey's fall, Henry immediately took a lone offensive against the papacy; and Dr Scarisbrick thus completes his portrait by making Henry responsible for the human and parliamentary acts reforming religion. An ideology of caesaropapism wedded to conscientious scruples over church corruption, a personal claim to imperial status, an insistence on national immunity from the papacy, the attacks upon the clergy -- all become Henry's post-Wolsey personality development." Elton's opposite: Cromwell "planted the idea in Henry to not only break from Rome and papal authority in 1534, but also to consolidate England under exclusive sovereignty". Guy's version: the break "injected the language of 'imperial kingship'" into Tudor political thought.
Takeaway: the break is the king's, and the minister only carries it out.

## M24. The healthy old Church
Argue there was no sign of total Church corruption, so the Reformation cannot be explained as the collapse of a rotten institution. Scarisbrick's own words, second-hand via the Catholic World Report from the December 2000 Catholic Herald interview: "it was the first bit of Catholic revisionism that contested the notion that the whole Church had been sinking ever deeper into corruption... I would take the view that there was no sign of total Church corruption. But there were of course serious weaknesses. The Church is always corrupt, always failing, because it is made up of human beings." Haigh's parallel attack: anticlericalism "owes its popularity to utility and not veracity", and the evidence for it is "weak foundations for such a significant edifice". Duffy: "traditional religion had about it no particular marks of exhaustion or decay".
Takeaway: a healthy Church makes the Reformation an imposition, not a cure.

## M25. The slow and long Reformation
Argue the changes "encountered more resistance than has often been supposed" and ran on for generations, so the Reformation is slow and then long. The 1984 book argues the old faith survived and revived under Mary; Duffy's development calls the Reformation "a violent disruption, not the natural fulfilment, of most of what was vigorous in late medieval piety", which eventually proved "a runaway success"; Haigh calls them "Some Reformations", a series of disconnected, lower-case reformations that "had passed most people by"; MacCulloch carries the revision into the long view, a Long Reformation lasting until about 1700. Wikipedia, citing Stephen Taylor (2025), credits Scarisbrick, Haigh and Duffy as the shapers of the long-Reformation theory.
Takeaway: stretch the Reformation across generations and the event becomes a process.

## M26. The moral verdict
Judge the king whole and damn him for hubris in diplomacy and religion. The Canadian Historical Review notice: "Dr Scarisbrick prefers individuals of conscience and of the old religion; he emphatically dislikes Henry" and "rescues Henry VIII from his current de-emphasizers only to damn him for hubris in diplomacy and religion". Reader reviews agree: "Scarisbrick's Henry is an egotistical creature, subject to rages and acts of bloodthirsty vengeance, who unleashed forces he was barely able to control." The verdict is the mechanism: a biography that ends in judgement, not in neutrality, and the judgement is the book's spine.
Takeaway: like the men of conscience and judge the king by them.

## M27. Catholic revisionism as a programme
Name the revisionism and rank it above the biography. Scarisbrick himself said in December 2000 that the contribution to Catholic revisionism, not the Henry VIII biography, was what he was most proud of: "That's what I am most proud of really, is being part of that." The Times obituary: the 1984 book "exemplified Catholic revisionism, a growing academic movement that would be taken up by Professor Eamon Duffy among others and would lead to a more balanced historiographical representation of the subject." The Catholic Herald obituary calls him "a leading figure in Catholic revisionist history". The programme is the mechanism: one book founds a school, and the school does the work the book only began.
Takeaway: the proudest work is the school, not the book.

## M28. Force of personality against teleology
Use contingency and personality against teleology and structure. The Times obituary: Scarisbrick challenged the Whig view "leaning on the theory that force of personality could change history", against the Whig account of the Reformation as "an inevitable part of the country's evolution into a modern state that was welcomed by the people at large", with "Henry VIII merely circumstantial to forces already profoundly changing the religious order across Europe". Collinson agrees on contingency for Duffy -- "The Reformation was the result of contingency, not a teleology" -- while Hoyle prefers events as motors of history, "not policy decisions, or... the deeper underlying structures, the slowly shifting tectonic plates preferred by Braudel".
Takeaway: personality against teleology is the whole quarrel in one sentence.

## M29. The silence as a gap
Leave the gap visible and let the successors fill it. Collinson's two criticisms of the 1984 book stand unanswered in the retrieved material: nothing on pre-Reformation Lollard heresy, and only five words on the Marian persecution of Protestants: "Everyone now regrets the burnings." The Canadian Historical Review notice records the biography's parallel silence: it "avoids specific issues and general problems in social, economic, and legal history perhaps because he would distinguish a biography of the king from a history of his reign". The mechanism is the honest gap: the successors filled it -- Haigh on anticlericalism, Duffy on the vitality of the old religion, MacCulloch on the Protestant minority -- and the school is stronger for the gap being named.
Takeaway: name the silence and the school grows around it.

## M30. The Festschrift as a truce
Co-edit the other pole's retirement volume and conduct the debate inside a working relationship, not a feud. Law and Government under the Tudors (1988), edited by Claire Cross, David Loades and Scarisbrick, is the Festschrift for Elton's retirement: the man whose revolution Scarisbrick spent his career undoing is honoured by the man who undid it, with a bibliography of Elton's writings 1946-1986 and chapters by Guy, Brigden, MacCulloch and Loades alongside Scarisbrick's own. The debate was conducted inside the institution, not outside it.
Takeaway: edit the master's Festschrift and the quarrel becomes a conversation.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife
Early phase (1955-1968): the thesis and the biography. The Cambridge doctorate, "The conservative episcopate in England, 1529-1535", completed 1955; the two early articles, "The Pardon of the Clergy, 1531" (1956) and "Clerical Taxation in England 1485-1547" (1960); thirteen years teaching at Queen Mary College, London, with a year lecturing in Ghana; commissioned in 1960 to write Henry VIII and writing it over eight years; the book published in 1968 to immediate standing, Elton's review "The King of Hearts" the next year, the Canadian Historical Review notice calling it "a meticulous, scholarly re-casting of Henry VIII... refreshing heir to Pollard's standard study". The questions are Elton's questions -- Cromwell, the revolution in government, the break from Rome -- answered against Elton's answers, from the king's conscience rather than the minister's bureau. Middle phase (1969-1988): the professor and the revisionist. Professor of History at Warwick from 1969 or 1970 to retirement in 1994, Head of Department 1970-1977, Pro-Vice-Chancellor, the sole professor on arrival, dedicated to undergraduate teaching, a strong Conservative in a predominantly left-wing department whose only common ground with Edward Thompson was cricket; Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature 1969; "Cardinal Wolsey and the Commonweal" (1978); the Ford Lectures, Oxford, 1981-82, "Religious Attitudes in Reformation England"; The Reformation and the English People (1984), which Peter Gwyn called "Scarisbrick's Bomb" and which founded Catholic revisionism; Life, co-founded with Nuala in August 1970 and chaired for forty-seven years, the 1985 two-million-signature petition, the 1987 Alton Bill with 296 MPs; Law and Government under the Tudors (1988), the Elton Festschrift he co-edited with Cross and Loades. The phase is the argument made twice: once as biography, once as revisionism, and once as a public cause. Late phase (1988-2026): retirement and the long afterlife of the two books. Retired from Warwick in 1994; the later research on the colonisation of Africa, the history of the slave trade and Jesuit missionary history, with a planned global history of the Jesuit movement never written; Zoe's Place Baby Hospice founded 1995, in Liverpool, then Middlesbrough (2004) and Coventry (2011); Papal Knight of the Order of St Sylvester 1993, Nuala a Dame of the same order; MBE 2015; retired as Life chairman in 2017; Nuala died Tuesday 31 August 2021; the Yale second edition of Henry VIII (1997) with its updated foreword taking stock of subsequent research, and the paperback reissue of 31 May 2011, keeping the 1968 book in print for fifty-eight years. The phase ends on Saturday 28 February 2026: died at home at 5.40 am, aged 97, daughters Emma and Sarah beside him, announced the same morning by David Alton ("a mighty oak has fallen"), the Warwick memoriam on Monday 2 March, the Catholic bishops' statement on Tuesday 3 March, the Catholic Herald obituary on Thursday 5 March, the Catholic News Agency wire on Friday 6 March. The afterlife so far: Henry VIII remains the standard account -- Yale's words, "first published in 1968, it remains the standard account of the reign" -- and the 1984 book remains the founding text of the revision Duffy, Haigh and MacCulloch carried into the long Reformation; the Pilgrimage reading he defended against Elton is now, in Collinson's judgement, the account "as close to [definitive] as we may hope to get"; and the doorway this study hopped through -- Starkey's two Henrys answering his one Henry -- is still the live comparison in Tudor biography.

## Aesthetic logic
One move holds the work together at every scale: take the person nearest the centre -- the king, the parishioner, the martyr -- and read the age from what that person did, wanted and believed, rather than from the institution that governed them. The mechanism is biographical before it is constitutional: the king's conscience, the wills and account-books, the lay fraternity's membership roll, the rebellion's numbers, the guild that reappears under Mary. The tone is the moralist's -- the balance sheet, the judgement, the "emphatically dislikes Henry" -- but the moralism serves a historian's purpose, which is to restore agency to the people the Whig story had made into an audience: the king who drove the reign, the parishioners who did not want the Reformation, the rebels who were the conservatives. Methodologically he is the mirror of Elton: Elton's archival discipline kept, Elton's agent reversed, the minister replaced by the king, the revolution replaced by the imposition, the teleology replaced by the personality. The system is Elton's functionalism turned inside out from the other side: judge what was done, from the record, as Elton taught, but judge it in the conscience and the parish, where Elton did not look.
