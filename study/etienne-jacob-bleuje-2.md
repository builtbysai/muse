His credo, in his own words: "I consider that art does not need a story, a process or a message." ### 2026-1. Flow-field mandala (collaboration with jn3008, as captioned)

A square black field of thin white contour and flow lines, roughly radially symmetric, swirling around several white-dot singularities. Lines form closed, teardrop and leaf cells and long spikes radiating outward; a dense dotted cluster sits at centre. Between screenshots seconds apart the swirl phase changes: lines undulate and the whole field appears to breathe and rotate slowly, like a vector-field or marble pattern in slow rotation. Mechanism: periodic flow-field lines whose phase is a function of a looping time variable, M2 and M3.

### 2026-2. Dotted constellation / network (also the homepage loop on arrival)

White glowing dots of varying size on black, joined by dotted, curved trails; small asterisk and star motifs sit at grid nodes. The dots travel along the trails: in successive screenshots the same dot has moved along its arc and the trail-dash pattern has shifted. The loop is a set of particles moving on closed, precomputed paths, returning to their start after one period, M6. This is the loop a first-time visitor sees.

### 2026-3. Striped ellipsoids on a lattice

A regular triangular lattice of black ellipsoids and spheres, each wrapped in white parallel stripes, linked by thin white wavy, sine-like lines. The stripes rotate and shift and the wavy connectors undulate, giving a moire, op-art shimmer; the lattice itself is static, the motion is in the stripe phase and the wave phase, M2. Nothing moves position; everything moves phase.

### 2026-4. Coiled disc stacks / tipping springs

A hexagonal lattice of identical objects: squat stacks of white elliptical rings, like coiled springs or discs seen in perspective, standing on faint hexagonal ground-lines. A wave passes through the field: at any moment a band of stacks leans and tips over in sequence, like dominoes, while the rest stand upright, then they right themselves. The lean angle is a periodic function of position plus time, a textbook offset-by-position wave, M2. This loop is the pinned post on his Mathstodon, Wednesday 3 June 2026, 18 replies, 277 boosts, 566 favourites.

### 2026-5. Dotted cellular sphere

One large sphere filling the frame, its surface tiled by hemispherical bumps and domes, each outlined by a ring of white dots, with concentric dashed rings. It reads as a rotating and breathing bubble or virus sphere: the dot-rings shift as the sphere turns, so the same bumps reappear in a different orientation at the loop point, M2 and M3.

### 2026-6. Spiky faceted polyhedron

A rotating, faceted star or icosahedron-like solid, its faces filled with nested white triangles; small dotted orbit-rings circle it. The solid turns slowly, a different face or corner uppermost in successive screenshots, the triangular infill flexes, and the orbit dots circulate: a rigid rotation plus a periodic deformation, both returning to the start configuration, M1 and M4.

## The seven tutorials, in his own terms

Seven core Processing (Java) tutorials, dated, plus "Physarum-style simulations", "Processing little tricks" and "LLMs are not conscious" listed on the same page. These are the primary source for the mechanisms; the study quotes his parameters, not a summary of them.

### Tutorial 1. Making a first gif with Processing, Saturday 28 November 2020

The foundation: a black dot orbits a circle, radius 100. numFrames = 100; at each frame t = (frameCount - 1) / numFrames, so t runs 0 to 1 but never reaches 1; x = centre + r * cos(2 * pi * t), y = centre + r * sin(2 * pi * t); saveFrame("fr###.gif"). Assemble with gifsicle --delay=2 --loop, or ezgif.com. File-size advice: keep GIFs low-resolution, with fewer frames, fewer colours, few changed pixels. His GIFs are kept under 5 MB because of Twitter limits.

### Tutorial 2. Loop from periodic function and offset, the central recipe

"Everything is a function of t and p", p = t - offset(x, y), then use a periodic function of p. First p = t. Then vibration: sin(2 * pi * p), so x and y oscillate. Additive offset: each element gets its own p; all elements share one loop, but appear to vibrate at different phases. Basic offsets: radial geometry, offset = 0.01 * dist(x, y, centre), a wave radiating from centre, lines appearing to propagate; linear geometry, offset = (x + 2y) * 0.005, a wave in a chosen direction; spiral, offset = dist + atan2(y - centreY, x - centreX) / 2 * pi, ten examples, including a spiral drawn by a dot spaced 300 units apart. Noise as offset = 3 * noise(x * 0.001, y * 0.001), bounded so elements stay repeated. Vibro mode is the exception: frequency covers the whole loop. Also the "faces" example: colour lerp from red to green in one loop, face radius from 250 to 300 with circle_vibration = cos(2 * pi * (t - dist / 200)), eye size and mouth angle from sin(2 * pi * (t - 0.5 * |dx|)), head destination from (640, 360) to (640, 400) when the first destination goes off-canvas, frame-rate animation pipeline with numFrames = 80, 150, 60.

### Tutorial 3. Noise loop / noise propagation

(a) Noise loop: sample noise on a circle in noise space: eval(radius * cos(2 * pi * p), radius * sin(2 * pi * p)), a closed path, so the value at t = 0 equals that at t = 1. radius = 1.3; larger radius means more variation within the period, smaller means smoother. Demo: flwIn times lerp(0.6, 1.2, noiseLoop(0.35, p)), and a face rotating with the noise. (b) Noise propagation (4D): to show a spatial field he uses 4D OpenSimplex noise: eval(seed + radius * cos(2 * pi * p), radius * sin(2 * pi * p), scl * x, scl * y), scl = 0.018, seeds 0 and 123 for independent dx and dy, 450 by 450 grid, stroke(255, 50), d = 20 px; extra trick: radial displacement 0.015 * dist(x, y, centre) so dots move outward. numFrames = 38 for flow-field rotation and spiral. He admits he does not use this precise technique very often: runtime is often spent on smooth looping, and he prefers direct visual intuition sometimes.

### Tutorial 4. Replacement technique

Key fix for discontinuous motion: p = (i + t) / K for K objects in a queue; each object takes the place of the next after one whole loop, so nothing jumps. To hide the swap, objects must be invisible (or identical) at the endpoints p = 0 and p = 1: for example a disc whose radius is sin(pi * p), 0 at both ends, max at mid-loop. Examples: a pie and spiral, K = 130, q = sqrt(p), theta = 7 * 2 * pi * q, r = 0.4 * width * q; 3 colour slices per pie from 1D noise pow(noise, 4) * 2, rotation alpha = 10 * 2 * pi * p; palette #f7f7f7, #cceabb, #3f3f44, #fdcb9e; numFrames = 70. Also: rotation with no back-tracking (an object turning 180 degrees, then some other object takes over), replacement of a 2D grid object by the next one, fluid, sphere and obstacle simulations, Moore-curve queue, two-level sliding objects, Moore plus Voronoi diagram.

### Tutorial 5. Replacement technique with 2D grid of objects

The workhorse behind the lattice loops of 2025-26. 70 by 4 grid of tiles; each tile moves along y, and at the end each tile takes the place of the tile 4 rows later: translate(0, -p * L * ny); K = 10 copies (i = -K..K) so the field is covered while tiles leave and re-enter. Each tile has its own delay: delay = 0.5 * randomGaussian() + yIndex / ny - 0.3; changer = constrain(map(p - delay, 0, 2.5, 0, 1), 0, 1); easing: changer = changer * changer * (3 - 2 * changer) (smoothstep), then easeOutElastic(elasticNo = 1, g = 1.4); rotation xRot = PI + PI * elasticChanger (tile starts flipped at PI, showing its back, and ends at TWO_PI, showing the front; a digit floor(random(10)) is drawn on the front only); stroke fades with 255 * (1 - changer). His own caveat: the "changer" is not necessary for a 2D-grid replacement, pure translation already loops, it is there for the flip and elastic character. Noise: he uses 1D noise on p, and notes a grid of tiles "does not need to look like a grid": the same math can drive scattered dots, spirals, waves, sphere and fluid simulations.

### Tutorial 6. Motion blur template (by beesandbombs)

Credited to David Klein, beesandbombs. samplesPerFrame (default 5, example 7) sub-frames per output frame; shutterAngle (0.6, and 2.0 in the long example) controls blur length; numFrames = 80, and 400. In test mode t = mouseX / width, c = mouseY / height (click prints c); in record mode t = map(frameCount - 1 + sa * shutterAngle / samplesPerFrame, 0, numFrames, 0, 1) % 1, the averaged colour is accumulated in result[][] (RGB ints, a pixel buffer, not a post-filter), and the frame is saved as fr###.gif; the loop stops when frameCount == numFrames * samplesPerFrame. shutterAngle above 1 makes sub-frames overlap across frame boundaries for longer trails. Loop-check trick: t = (mouseX * 1.3 / width) % 1 to preview the seam at t near 1. Links to Dave's "spiral thing" and to his own "new sketch template". The OpenSimplexNoise tab is a separate included class, not a Processing library.

### Tutorial 7. Drawing on paths computed by steps / simulation

To loop a simulation that cannot be written as a closed-form function of t: run it once in setup(), store the whole trajectory, then position(p) by linear interpolation between recorded points (floatIndex, lerp, with lerpFactor = index - floor(index)). This makes any t available, including the fractional sub-frame times motion blur needs. Example 1: bouncing ball, 3,200 steps at dt = 0.1, gravity 9.8, floor at y = -0.95, wall bounce; radius grows for p in [0, 0.02] and fades for p in [0.9, 1]; K = 10 replacement balls with random per-ball offset; numFrames = 500 for a single ball, 80 for many. Example 2: Hilbert curve, depth 5, N = 4^5 = 1024 points (d2xy taken from Wikipedia's Hilbert-curve page), 2,345 small dots travelling on it, K = 456 moving dots. Also: rotation, tint, and a final example at 512 by 512, numFrames = 300, with repetition of a pattern along the curve, a direct ancestor of the dotted-curve loops above.

## M1. One master clock

t = (frameCount - 1) / numFrames, t in [0, 1), never reaching 1, so the last frame is one step before the first and the GIF closes exactly. Everything, position, size, colour, rotation, noise, is a function of t. The watchable behaviour is a loop with no seam: the end state is the start state by construction, not by editing. **Takeaway:** make everything a function of t, and the loop closes itself.

## M2. Periodic function of a shifted time

sin and cos of 2 * pi * (t - offset) give oscillation; the offset is a function of position: radial (waves radiating from a centre), linear (waves travelling in a direction), spiral (dist + atan2 / 2 * pi), or noise (irregular phase). This is why the same wave appears in the disc-tipping field, the striped spheres and the constellation trails: one periodic motion, propagated across space with a spatial phase offset. The watchable behaviour is a wave that travels without anything travelling: the elements stay put, their phases move. **Takeaway:** shift the time by position, and a static field waves.

## M3. Cyclic noise

Sample noise on a circle in noise space, (r * cos 2 * pi * p, r * sin 2 * pi * p), and for fields use 4D OpenSimplex (two noise dimensions, the time circle, spatial x and y), so organic fields loop without a visible seam. radius = 1.3 in the demo; larger radius means more variation within the period, smaller means smoother. The watchable behaviour is randomness that returns: the field looks organic and never repeats a shape, yet closes exactly. **Takeaway:** put the noise on a circle, and the randomness loops.

## M4. Replacement

K identical objects, p = (i + t) / K, each taking the next one's state over one period, made invisible or identical at p = 0 and p = 1 (a disc whose radius is sin(pi * p), 0 at both ends, max at mid-loop), so a swap is never seen. This is how objects, tiles and even whole grids travel, flip and re-enter without jumping. The watchable behaviour is motion with no back-tracking: an object turns 180 degrees, then some other object takes over, and the eye never catches the handoff. **Takeaway:** hide the swap at the endpoints, and discontinuous motion loops.

## M5. 2D-grid replacement with elastic flip

The workhorse behind the lattice loops of 2025-26. A 70 by 4 grid of tiles moves along y, each tile taking the place of the tile 4 rows later, K = 10 copies covering the field while tiles leave and re-enter. Each tile has its own delay, a smoothstep changer, then an easeOutElastic flip from PI to TWO_PI, a digit on the front only, stroke fading with the changer. His own caveat: the changer is not necessary, pure translation already loops, it is there for the flip and elastic character. The watchable behaviour is a grid that flips tile by tile, elastic, out of order, and never shows a seam. **Takeaway:** delay and ease the replacement, and a grid becomes a performance.

## M6. Precomputed paths

Simulate once, record the trajectory, and interpolate linearly between recorded points for any t. This lets non-analytic physics (a bouncing ball, 3,200 steps) and space-filling curves (Hilbert, depth 5, 1,024 points, 2,345 dots) loop, and lets motion blur sample fractional t. The watchable behaviour is dots travelling on a curve that could never be written as a formula, yet returning exactly. **Takeaway:** record the path once, and any simulation loops.

## M7. Motion blur by sub-frame averaging

beesandbombs' template: samplesPerFrame sub-frames per frame, shutterAngle controlling trail length, colours accumulated in an RGB pixel buffer, not a post-filter. shutterAngle above 1 makes sub-frames overlap across frame boundaries for longer trails. The soft trails on the constellation dots and the U-orbit comets come from this. The watchable behaviour is butter: fast dots leave a trail that reads as speed, and the trail itself loops. **Takeaway:** average sub-frames, and speed becomes visible.

## M8. The GIF constraint as style

A GIF under 5 MB, black ground, white dots, lines and stripes only, closed symmetric structures (spheres, lattices, grids, orbits, triangulations) whose end state is geometrically identical to the start. The constraint forces simple, high-contrast, low-colour motion, and the simplicity is the style. The one exception, the separate Colorful gallery, proves the rule: the main archive is strictly black and white. The watchable behaviour is restraint: nothing on screen that the loop does not need. **Takeaway:** let the file size discipline the picture, and the picture stays clean.

## M9. The dotted-surface and lattice-wave family

The 2025-26 visual signature: dotted domes, striped ellipsoids, tipping stacks, cellular spheres, triangulated surfaces. The lattice is static; the motion lives in phase: stripe phase, wave phase, lean phase. The dots are the material: rings of white dots outline every bump, dome and orbit, and the dot is at once the texture and the moving part. The watchable behaviour is a surface that is all dots, and the dots are all moving, and nothing else moves. **Takeaway:** make the texture the motion, and the surface comes alive.

### Evolution 2017 to 2026, as observed across the full archive

2017 (simplest, flattest): mostly 2D and flat - fractals (Sierpinski carpet and triangle), plain geometric primitives (a square, a triangle, a star), dotted flow fields, an op-art striped vortex, and one black-on-white diagram. Motion slow and subtle: breathe, pulse, rotate, wind rather than fly. Several pieces portrait-format with a thin white border, as if made as static images animated only slightly. The repertoire is small: dots, lines, simple shapes, one vortex. The earliest page, 2017_1, ends with the text "(the end)".

2018: the vocabulary widens - 3D space appears (grids in perspective, networks on spheres, clusters of textured balls), and many more materials: thick sticks, zigzag springs, flame tongues, feathered ribbons, open C-rings, hinged panels, peeling fractal sheets. Still strictly black-on-white, line-based, mostly slow rotation and churn rather than fast flight. Digits enter at the end of the year (a digit tunnel and a digit disc, 2018_6), and the single closed line becomes a slow-morph subject (2018_4 loop 3, circle to spiky outline over about a minute; 2018_6 loop 9, squarish to circular).

2019: particle systems take over - dotted terrains, starfields, warps, flows, galaxies, tunnels and funnels of dots and streaks. The triangular mesh becomes a signature (tunnels, terrains, nets). Numbers and digits enter properly (a digit-covered cylinder, digit grids, a counting field of flipping digit tiles in the Additional gallery). Wide 2:1 formats appear. Motion becomes faster and more directional: streaming, spiralling, flying through tunnels. The heart appears twice (a particle heart in 2020_2 is the same family as the branching heart in Additional gallery 2).

2020: the most complex and spatial - 3D voxel worlds (cube pits and heaps, block tunnels, wireframe cubes), particle swarms (birds, hearts, filaments), layered mechanisms (tiles flipping in waves, vortexes of blocks). The same dot/line grammar is now embedded in 3D spaces with camera-like movement: flying through a lattice room, pushing forward through a block tunnel, a breathing voxel crater.

2021: dot- and grid-driven. Dot constellations, dotted-ring discs, wireframe grid tunnel and grid plane, rolling spools, a radial particle explosion, a field of counting digits as a travelling wave, tadpole flow-field particles. Motion mostly flight and rotation through space (tunnel, terrain, warp) and surface waves. 2022 shifts toward objects and mechanisms: cubes dominate (tipping cubes, hinged panels, starfield-speckled cube), morphing fractal/maze sheet, Hilbert maze, honeycomb-to-vortex metamorphosis, faceted geodesic sphere, the heart of arrows. Motion more articulated (hinges, peeling, tipping) rather than pure flight. 2023 is the most varied and abstract: travelling-dot network, rotating digit sphere, recursive spheres, a true flow-field of streamlines, gyroscope of rings and springs, two kinds of voxel cubes, scrolling fractal subdivision, dotted rope coil, double dotted torus. Dotted spheres recur in all three years (2021 loop 2, 2022 loops 5-6, 2023 loop 3), as do cube/grid assemblies.

2024: mechanism-first set-pieces - a travelling distortion wave deforming a braced lattice, per-tile polygon morphing, migrating hairpin folds, a pattern-swap front trading one tiling for another, a spiral-tiled sphere, a digit-covered trefoil knot, a single line morphing diamond to maze by recursive subdivision, digit-cubes rolling over a digit grid, a heart redrawn by a rotating fan of bending spokes.

2025: isometric cubes and organic branching networks - cubes detaching and tumbling out of grids, striped domes inflating, folding cube panels, grids of rising cubes, cubes rolling on a diagonal bar, a dotted woven sphere, a wireframe spiral sphere. 2026 concentrates on dotted surfaces, flow-fields, spheres and lattice waves, all still black-on-white line, dot and stripe vocabulary, with collaborations (jn3008) and browser spin-offs running alongside. The arc over the whole archive: the vocabulary narrows as the technique deepens - fewer shapes, more phase. The palette never changes in the main archive; evolution is in mechanism and motif, not colour.

Additional galleries: the experiments that did not fit the yearly pages - more algorithmic and pattern-driven: Hilbert curves, digit grids, number tiles, folding planes, rope knots, voxel balls, flow fields, a ring maze, a tile tornado. Visually consistent with the black-and-white archive but more "math-toy" in subject.

Colorful gallery vs the black-and-white work: the mechanisms are the same (voxel waves, tunnels, vortexes, cube grids, sliding tiles) but rendered in full 3D with gloss and light - pastel pink/blue voxel cities, metallic gold cubes, glowing orange vortex cores, candy-coloured cube galaxies, chartreuse/blue paint swirls. Colour is used as a material (glossy porcelain, gold, translucent glass, pastel tile facets) rather than as decoration. Where the black-and-white loops are line, dot and wireframe, the colourful ones are solid, shaded and reflective; where the early black-and-white loops are flat and minimal, the colourful ones are volumetric and spectacular - yet the underlying motion grammar (waves travelling through grids, spirals, tunnels and vortexes) is identical.
