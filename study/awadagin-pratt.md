# Awadagin Pratt: Study Notes

**Site:** Awadagin Pratt (b. 6 March 1966, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; living), American pianist, violinist and conductor; the first student in Peabody history to receive diplomas in three performance areas -- piano, violin and conducting; the first African-American pianist to win the Naumburg International Piano Competition (1992); Avery Fisher Career Grant 1994; professor of piano at the San Francisco Conservatory of Music since July 2023; Principal Conductor of the Miami Valley Symphony Orchestra since the 2023/24 season; founder and director of the Art of the Piano Festival and the Nina Simone Piano Competition. Awadagin Pratt was born on 6 March 1966 in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. He began piano lessons at six, with Leslie Sompong. After the family moved to Normal, Illinois, he took up the violin at nine. The town's name later titled his debut album: A Long Way From Normal (EMI, 1994). On a violin scholarship he enrolled at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign at sixteen, studying piano, violin and conducting together -- the three disciplines that would define his whole career. He transferred to the Peabody Conservatory of Music in Baltimore, where he became the first student in the school's history to receive diplomas in three different performance areas: piano (PC '89), violin (PC '89) and conducting (GPD '92). His conducting education began at Illinois and continued at Peabody under Frederik Prausnitz; he later studied with Gunther Schuller, at the National Conducting Institute under Leonard Slatkin (where he led the National Symphony Orchestra), and in private mentorship with Lorin Maazel for three years. Johns Hopkins gave him its Distinguished Alumni Award; Illinois Wesleyan University, the Berklee College of Music and the Boston Conservatory gave him honorary doctorates, and he delivered commencement addresses at those institutions and at Peabody. No source read says he attended the Curtis Institute; his training was Illinois, then Peabody, and this study asserts no Curtis connection. [C29399]

In May 1992 Pratt won the Naumburg International Piano Competition at Lincoln Center, the first African-American pianist -- and, in the broader wording of several sources, the first Black artist -- to win it, playing Bach-Busoni, Beethoven and Liszt. The prize brought a $5,000 award, a 40-city concert tour and a recording contract; he had nearly skipped another competition the previous month because he could not raise the $60 entrance fee. His debut recital at Alice Tully Hall on 9 February 1993 sold out; Edward Rothstein wrote in The New York Times that the 26-year-old's programme was "a survey of the fugue in the Baroque, Classical and Romantic periods", praising "power and dexterity and poise". In 1994 he received an Avery Fisher Career Grant and made his Lincoln Center debut with the New York Philharmonic. The Naumburg win launched a schedule of 40 to 50 concerts that year and 70 the next, when he signed with IMG Artists. The Washington Post called his playing "forceful, imaginative, and precisely tinted"; WGBH called him "one of the great and distinctive American pianists and conductors of our time." [C29400]

Since 1992 Pratt has performed with nearly every major orchestra in the United States -- Boston, Chicago, New York Philharmonic, Minnesota, Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Baltimore, Indianapolis, Atlanta, St. Louis, National, Detroit -- and in solo recitals at Carnegie Hall, Lincoln Center and the Kennedy Center, at Ravinia, Wolf Trap, Caramoor, Aspen and the Hollywood Bowl, and on six continents. He played at the White House three times: for the Clintons, and in November 2009 at the Obama White House classical-music event hosted by First Lady Michelle Obama, with student workshops. He has appeared on Sesame Street (at Big Bird's invitation, as his bio likes to put it), the Today Show and Good Morning America. His EMI/Angel recordings: A Long Way From Normal (1994), Beethoven Piano Sonatas (1995; sonatas 7, 9, 30, 31), Live From South Africa (1997, recorded in Cape Town), Transformations (16 November 1999; Mussorgsky's Pictures at an Exhibition, his own transcription of Bach's Passacaglia and Fugue in C minor BWV 582, Brahms's Handel Variations, on a Bosendorfer Imperial Grand), Play Bach (2002; Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 and keyboard concerti with a one-to-a-part chamber ensemble, the St. Lawrence String Quartet), plus later Brahms cello sonatas with Zuill Bailey (Telarc, 2011) and Judith Lang Zaimont with the Harlem String Quartet (Navona, 2011). He is a Yamaha artist. Conducting, the third diploma, became a parallel career rather than a side line. He has led the Toledo, New Mexico, Vancouver WA, Winston-Salem, Santa Fe and Prince George's County symphonies, the Northwest Sinfonietta, the Concertante di Chicago, orchestras in Japan, the Tokyo City Philharmonic and the Georgian Philharmonic in Tbilisi. He made his operatic conducting debut leading Porgy and Bess with Greensboro Opera in January 2022, conducted Bang on a Can in an Ornette Coleman programme at the Brooklyn Academy of Music, and plays and conducts Mozart and Beethoven concertos as soloist-conductor in the US, Europe and Asia. In 2024 the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette called his Beethoven Coriolan Overture and Symphony No. 8 with the Chamber Orchestra of Pittsburgh "energetic and cohesive". [C29401]

In fall 2004 Pratt joined the University of Cincinnati College-Conservatory of Music as Assistant Professor of Piano and Artist in Residence, rising to Professor of Piano; he stayed nineteen to twenty years. He founded and directs the Art of the Piano Festival (a spring festival at CCM inspired by Franz Liszt's master classes) and the Art of the Piano Foundation, and was artistic director of the Cincinnati World Piano Competition. In spring 2023, with Sphinx Organization funding, he organised the first Nina Simone Piano Competition for Black pianists aged 10 to 35, in collaboration with the Cincinnati Symphony, CCM and the Art of the Piano Festival; the inaugural finals on 6 October 2023, with the Cincinnati Symphony under Louis Langree, were won by Clayton Stephenson playing the Tchaikovsky Piano Concerto No. 1, for a $50,000 grand prize. In July 2023 he joined the San Francisco Conservatory of Music as professor of piano, where he mentors a select studio, has led the SFCM Chamber Orchestra, and continues to lead the Art of the Piano Festival and the Nina Simone Piano Competition. SFCM president David Stull: "Awadagin is a brilliant teacher and prodigiously gifted artist." Piano chair Yoshikazu Nagai: "one of the most intriguing and unique artists of his generation but an equally dedicated educator." [C29402]

In response to the murder of George Floyd, Pratt created a podcast that evolved into a multimedia musical experience, Awadagin Pratt: Black in America, fusing music of Bach, Messiaen and Liszt with still and moving pictures by filmmaker Alrick Brown and narration in which Pratt chronicles his life -- from Peabody student to international acclaim -- through graphic accounts of numerous police stops and arrests for Driving While Black, starting when he was a teenager and continuing through postgraduate study into adulthood. It is performed primarily on college campuses, with students, followed by a panel discussion on race in America. A documentary version directed by Michelle Bauer Carpenter was screened at film festivals nationwide in 2023 and aired on 90 PBS stations. [C29403]

The centrepiece of Pratt's recent career, and the direct tie to #742 Judd Greenstein, is Stillpoint. In 2016 Pratt and Greenstein were invited by executive producer Mark Rabideau to adjudicate a chamber ensemble contest at DePauw University's 21CMposium. Greenstein, on his own homepage: "In 2016, I was invited to speak at the 21CMposium at Depauw University... What happened next, over the years that followed, was that a conversation became a question, a question became another set of conversations, and before long, Awadagin and Mark were visiting our farm in Massachusetts and we were all making plans to produce a new album together." Pratt gave each of the composers lines from T.S. Eliot's "Burnt Norton" (Four Quartets) as a jumping-off point, without setting the text. The works were written for piano (Pratt), the conductorless string orchestra A Far Cry and the eight-part amplified vocal ensemble Roomful of Teeth, in varying combinations. The album, six works, was released 25 August 2023 on New Amsterdam Records, produced by Mark Rabideau, Judd Greenstein and Jesse Lewis. NPR named it one of the 10 Best Classical Albums of 2023. Montgomery's Rounds, which opens the album, won the 2024 Grammy for Best Contemporary Classical Composition; Pratt had performed it with 30 orchestras by August 2023, and by December 2024 the count was 80-90 performances with nearly 50 ensembles -- the numbers move with the date and are quoted here with their dates. Greenstein's closing title track, Still Point (12:40), is "another pint-sized piano concerto... built from two chords that engender a wide color palette of emotion, alternating turbulent outbursts with quieter rippling passages and retro, pulsating voices straight out of Steve Reich's Music for 18 Musicians" (NPR). Pratt: "Still Point is a profoundly moving work that touched me from the first moment of seeing and playing through the score." Greenstein's note: the work "contains many spirits: Tyner's, Eliot's, those of A Far Cry and Roomful of Teeth... and of course Awadagin's, whose towering musical presence is at the center of this work." It is dedicated to Awadagin, in memoriam McCoy Tyner. Pratt on Eliot: "These lines have meant a lot to me over the years. A great expression of a particular kind of tension, an understanding of a duality that can exist in life, the struggle for balance, and an acknowledgement of the inexpressible -- that which cannot be tidily communicated." One discrepancy is recorded plainly and not resolved: the album has six tracks, but Pratt's press kit and the Detroit Symphony Orchestra both say SEVEN composers were commissioned -- Jessie Montgomery, Alvin Singleton, Judd Greenstein, Tyshawn Sorey, Jonathan Bailey Holland, Paola Prestini and Peteris Vasks -- and all seven works were recorded in Boston in summer 2022. Holland's piece is not on the six-track album; no title, premiere date or recording for it was found this run, and this study does not credit Holland with a Stillpoint track. [C29404]

The twenty works inspected run 1992-2025 and trace one practice finding its engine, then widening it. Phase 1, the canon laureate, 1992-1999 (W01-W06): the Naumburg win, an EMI contract, four albums of Bach, Beethoven, Brahms, Franck, Liszt, Mussorgsky and Rachmaninoff -- dense, introspective Austro-German Romanticism plus Bach -- while the press writes as much about the 14-inch low bench, the dreadlocks and the T-shirts as about the playing. He refuses Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue for about fifteen years to avoid being typecast as "the Black pianist who plays jazz", and conducting is a credential held in reserve. Phase 2, the working virtuoso, 2000-2010 (W07-W08): one more EMI album (Play Bach, 2002), then a nine-year recording silence while the major-label system, in Pratt's words, "imploded" and he "never saw royalties from them anyway"; the work continues off-disc -- teaching at CCM from 2004, festivals, guest conducting -- and the Honolulu Mozart cadenza of 2000 already shows the improvising habit live, decades before he names it. The low bench ends around 2003, after a shipping snafu forces him onto a conventional bench: "It went well enough that I was like, OK, let me reconsider my address to the piano", and he sits higher ever after. Phase 3, the brief return, 2011 (W09-W11): two chamber discs in one month (Brahms with Zuill Bailey, debuting at No. 3 on the Billboard classical chart, and Zaimont with the Harlem String Quartet) plus the North American premiere of Erkki-Sven Tuur's Piano Concerto with Paavo Jarvi and the Cincinnati Symphony -- canonical chamber music beside living-composer music beside a new concerto premiere, the pattern of the later career visible in miniature. Then another twelve-year album gap. Phase 4, reinvention, 2020-2023 (W12-W15): COVID lockdown gives him practice-room time to get comfortable improvising; the preluding habit becomes a recital programme; Black in America turns autobiography into a touring multimedia work; Porgy and Bess (2022) makes him an opera conductor; Rounds (2022) makes him a commissioner; Stillpoint (2023) makes him a curator of new music; the SFCM professorship and the Nina Simone Piano Competition (2023) make him an institution-builder. The repertoire pivot is total: from four EMI albums of canon to an album with zero canonical repertoire. Phase 5, the surge, 2024-2025 (W16-W20): the Grammy for Rounds converts the commission into a touring engine (80-90 performances, nearly 50 ensembles); conducting ramps up -- "The plan is to ramp that up more aggressively... it's something that has been important to me for a long time. I love conducting, and that's sort of an itch that needs to be scratched" -- and the Miami Valley principal conductorship runs recovery programming (Dett, Coleridge-Taylor, Walker). He is, at 58-59, playing more major-orchestra debuts than at any point since 1992. ## M1 -- The returning round: rondo as a groove engine

The mechanism Pratt himself named, in the title of the piece that remade his career. Jessie Montgomery's Rounds, written for Pratt and premiered by him on 27 March 2022, is "a rondo, within a rondo, within a rondo" -- a main theme alternating with contrasting episodes, with deliberate foils (ABCBCBA instead of ABCBA), so the audience locks into the return as a groove. Pratt, to the Chicago Symphony: he loves how "groove comes back" in the piece. The cadenza is his own, and it is the rupture in the cycle on purpose: "The cadenza is mine, and it's probably 10- to 20-percent improvisation each night depending on what is going on. It stays pretty fresh." He also uses an EBow on the piano strings mid-piece, "giving it something of an archaic sound -- it is almost like hearing time being looped upon itself". Greenstein's Still Point does the same work with two oscillating chords: turbulence, then rippling calm, then turbulence again, the two chords returning like a theme. The mechanism is the return as the engine: the theme comes back, the room settles, a rupture interrupts the cycle, then the cycle reasserts itself, and the pleasure is in recognising the return, not in being surprised by the new. The watchable behaviour is a theme that arrives, leaves, returns slightly changed; the room audibly settling when it comes back; a rupture (the cadenza) breaking the pattern, then the groove returning. Seen in W13, W15, W16, W19. [C29406]

**Takeaway:** let the theme come back, and let the return be the groove. [C29406]

## M2 -- Preluding: improvisation between the pieces

The lost nineteenth-century practice, recovered from recordings. Pratt heard Josef Hofmann play extended 9th, 11th and 13th chords at the bottom of the piano in Schumann's Kreisleriana, and Dinu Lipatti add a scalar flourish before a Mozart sonata, and found the practice described in Kenneth Hamilton's After the Golden Age. He now links short recital pieces (4-7 minutes) with improvised passages, and the Liszt Sonata in B minor as the second half. The habit is documented twenty years before he named it: in Honolulu in March 2000 he improvised along with the orchestral part of Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 23, added embellishments, and played his own improvised cadenza instead of Mozart's -- "a rhapsodic meander through heavily pedaled arpeggios to recurring themes, through transformations, modulations and back again, ranging further afield than Mozart's version". The reviewer: "Was the cadenza Mozartean? No, but it was Prattean, which is the point of cadenzas: to allow performers expressive elbow room." The mechanism is the seam as the music: the written piece dissolves into free playing and re-forms into the next piece, so the recital is one continuous act of playing rather than a row of separate exhibits, and the cadenza is different every night because it is composed in the moment, not retrieved. The watchable behaviour is the music never fully stopping between pieces; a written piece dissolving into free playing and re-forming into the next; the cadenza different every night. Seen in W07, W13, W15. [C29407]

**Takeaway:** play the seams, not just the pieces -- improvise between them. [C29407]

## M3 -- The single-poem commission brief

The commissioner as the composer of the conditions. For Stillpoint, Pratt gave six composers the same few lines from Eliot's "Burnt Norton" -- "At the still point of the turning world... there the dance is" -- and recorded the results with one piano, one string orchestra and one vocal ensemble. The brief produces unity without uniformity: a rondo-concerto (Montgomery), a bird-call love-letter piece built from Eliot's letters to Emily Hale (Prestini's Code), a jazz-Bach fantasia with jagged chords recalling Monk or Stravinsky (Singleton's Time Past, Time Future), a solo-piano mystic miniature, St. Teresa of Avila's Interior Castle arranged for solo piano by Pratt himself (Vasks's Castillo Interior), a Feldman-like vocal study of dissonant vibrato-free clusters punctuated by sparse piano chords (Sorey's Untitled Composition), and a two-chord oscillation (Greenstein's Still Point). The mechanism is the shared seed: one image -- stillness inside motion -- rendered six ways in a row, with the same pianist as the common thread and the same three forces as the shared room, so the listener hears the brief itself, not just six pieces that happen to share a disc. The watchable behaviour is one shared seed, six divergent growths; the same image rendered six ways in a row, the pianist the only constant. Seen in W15, and in W13 as the brief's first product. [C29408]

**Takeaway:** hand every composer the same poem and let the answers differ. [C29408]

## M4 -- The pianist as conductor: the third diploma, cashed in

The triple Peabody diploma (piano, violin, conducting) finally cashed in, thirty years after it was earned. Pratt appears as soloist-conductor for Mozart and Beethoven concertos in the US, Europe and Asia -- the same hands that shape a phrase at the keyboard now shaping it from the podium. He conducts opera from the pit with singer-first care: his Porgy and Bess debut at Greensboro Opera in January 2022, with Rhiannon Giddens as Bess, drew praise for conducting "with great care", engaging with the singers and treating each number with individuality, and for "deft handling of the jazz rhythms that frolic in Gershwin's music". He takes a principal conductorship (Miami Valley, 2023/24) and programmes Beethoven, Brahms, Mahler and Part alongside Walker, Coleridge-Taylor and Dett, and conducts Beethoven from the podium, not the keyboard -- Coriolan Overture and Symphony No. 8 with the Chamber Orchestra of Pittsburgh, "energetic and cohesive". His stated plan, in 2024: "ramp that up more aggressively". The mechanism is the role change as the career's second act: the interpreter who spent thirty years being told what to play now decides what an orchestra plays, and the authority comes from the same source -- he knows the score from inside, because he has played it. The watchable behaviour is the same hands that shape a phrase at the keyboard now shaping it from the podium; a concerto played and conducted by one person; a community orchestra playing a score its own timpanist reconstructed. Seen in W12, W17, W20. [C29409]

**Takeaway:** play the concerto, then conduct the orchestra -- cash in the third diploma. [C29409]

## M5 -- The stage image as demystification

Refusing the tuxedo, the standard bench and the standard persona so the music, not the uniform, is what the audience meets. The 14-inch custom bench -- he sat "as if sitting at a toy piano", on a short-legged wooden bench he disassembled into his luggage -- the dreadlocks borrowed from tennis star Yannick Noah, the T-shirts and colourful shirts on stage, the socks with Van Gogh on them. Pratt's own line: "When I wear t-shirts at a performance, that's what makes me comfortable. A tux, that creates barriers." The same logic governs repertoire: refusing Rhapsody in Blue for fifteen years so nobody could file him as "the jazz guy", then conducting Porgy and Bess on his own terms, from the pit, as a conductor -- the return to Gershwin as a choice, not a typecasting. The mechanism is the frame as a decision: the concert's visual frame is deliberately un-formal, so the audience's first impression is a person, not a uniform, and the repertoire refusal is visible as a choice -- what he will not play is as much the programme as what he will. The watchable behaviour is the visual frame of the concert deliberately un-formal; the audience meeting a person before they meet a uniform; the refusal visible as a choice. Seen in the W01-W07 era image (documented 1992-2000), and in W12 as the return to Gershwin as conductor, not as typecast soloist. [C29410]

**Takeaway:** take off the uniform and let the audience meet the person first. [C29410]

## M6 -- Multimedia autobiography: narration, film and live music

A concert that is also a testimony. Black in America, born as a podcast in the isolation of the pandemic and the unrest after George Floyd's murder, became a one-hour presentation in which Pratt's spoken narration is the centrepiece, Alrick Brown's film "Histoire(s) des Noires" -- a kaleidoscopic collage of more than 150 images confronting African-American representation in visual media across a century -- runs as a visual layer, and live music (Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 first movement and Keyboard Concerto BWV 1055, Liszt's Funerailles pre-recorded with film, Messiaen's Quartet for the End of Time, movement VIII, "Praise to the Immortality of Jesus") is performed with local students and faculty at each stop. The narration chronicles his life from Peabody student to international acclaim through graphic accounts of police stops for Driving While Black. The 2023 documentary version, directed by Michelle Bauer Carpenter, aired on PBS in more than one million US households, screened at film festivals nationwide, won multiple awards, and now ships with NAfME lesson plans for grades 4-12. The mechanism is autobiography as programme structure: the music is not background to the story -- Bach opens, Messiaen closes, the film runs between them, so the concert's arc IS the life story, and the piano playing is evidence. The watchable behaviour is a concert that stops being a concert: the piano stops, the film runs, the narrator speaks, the piano answers; the programme's arc is a life, from Peabody practice rooms to police stops to international stages. Seen in W14. [C29411]

**Takeaway:** let the concert's arc be a life, told in narration, film and music. [C29411]

## M7 -- The commission as career engine

One new piece, co-commissioned widely, generating more major-orchestra debuts in two years than the previous decade of canonical repertoire had. Rounds was commissioned by Pratt's Art of the Piano Foundation with nine co-commissioning orchestras (Baltimore, Kansas City, Indianapolis, Rochester among them). It won the 2024 Grammy for Best Contemporary Classical Composition. By August 2023 Pratt had played it with 30 orchestras since the March 2022 premiere; by April 2024 the count was 50-plus performances by 30 ensembles; by December 2024, 80-90 performances with nearly 50 ensembles. The association drove his Boston Symphony debut (September 2022) and his Chicago Symphony debut (January 2023), and his debut with the Sphinx Virtuosi (25 February 2025). Pratt: "I knew it was a beautiful and effective piece when I first got it. I was expecting some interest, but I did not expect it would be like this." The mechanism is the commission model as much as the notes: one soloist plus nine orchestras buying the same new piece is why it could reach 30 ensembles, and each performance is a debut with a new orchestra, because the piece travels with the soloist. The watchable behaviour is one piece opening doors orchestra after orchestra; the same concerto, a new hall every week, the count climbing with the date. Seen in W13, W16, W19. [C29412]

**Takeaway:** commission one piece with nine orchestras and let it carry you everywhere. [C29412]

## M8 -- Conducting as recovery: lost scores, rebuilt from the podium

The most recent mechanism, and the one where the conductor's job becomes archival. On 12 October 2025, in his third season as Principal Conductor of the Miami Valley Symphony Orchestra, Pratt programmed George Walker's Lyric for Strings, Samuel Coleridge-Taylor's Toussaint L'Ouverture -- a handwritten fragmented score transcribed by the orchestra's own principal timpanist, Marty Jenkins, "among the very first" performances worldwide of the revived work -- and Robert Nathaniel Dett's The Ordering of Moses, Dett's 1932 Eastman graduation thesis, premiered in 1937 by the Cincinnati Symphony and May Festival Chorus on NBC radio: a continuous oratorio in four themes, the score calling for the "clanking of chains" in percussion and weaving in the spirituals "Go Down, Moses" and "He is King of Kings". All seats $10. The mechanism is recovery as programming: scores by Black composers that were lost, fragmented or unheard are rebuilt with the orchestra's own players -- the timpanist as the transcriber, the community orchestra as the archive -- and the $10 ticket and the community-orchestra setting are part of the mechanism, access, not prestige. The watchable behaviour is a score coming back from the dead on a community stage: the chains clanking in the percussion, the spirituals surfacing inside the oratorio, the whole hall in for ten dollars. Seen in W20. [C29413]

**Takeaway:** rebuild the lost score with your own players, and charge ten dollars. [C29413]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Pratt's career is a long argument about who decides what a Black classical pianist plays. The early answer was refusal: no tuxedo, no standard bench, no Rhapsody in Blue for fifteen years -- the frame and the repertoire both chosen by him, not by the room's expectations. The middle answer was pairing: canon beside living composers, Brahms beside Zaimont in the same month, Bach beside a Tuur premiere. The late answer is authorship: he no longer waits to be asked, he commissions -- one poem, six composers, three forces, one pianist as the common thread -- and the commission becomes the career engine, the debuts, the Grammy, the album. The preluding is the same move at the smallest scale: between the written pieces, he plays what is not written, so even inside someone else's programme there is a passage that is his alone. The conducting is the same move at the largest scale: from the podium he decides what an orchestra plays, and what he decides is the music that was lost -- Dett, Coleridge-Taylor, Walker -- rebuilt by the orchestra's own hands. And Black in America is the same move told straight: the life, narrated, with the music as the evidence. Greenstein, the study before this one, brings a sketch and lets the room rewrite it; Pratt brings a poem and lets six composers rewrite it, and the difference between the two is the difference between a composer who builds the room and a pianist who commissions the music the room will play. His music teaches its language as it tells a story, and the story it tells is always the same one underneath: the still point is not the absence of motion, it is the place where the motion turns -- the theme returning, the cadenza breaking the pattern, the lost score coming back, the pianist who refused the uniform now standing on the podium in his own clothes. [C29414]
