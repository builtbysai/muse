# Madeleine Hope Dodds: Study Notes

**Site:** Madeleine Hope Dodds (1885-1972), historian of the North Country -- born Friday 2 January 1885 at Home House, Kells Lane, Low Fell, Gateshead, second of the five children of Edwin Dodds (1852-1929), bookbinder and historian, and Emily Bryham Mawson (1855-1896); Gateshead High School for Girls; Newnham College, Cambridge, 1904-1906, Second Class Honours in the History Tripos, 1906, in the years when women could study but not be awarded a degree; Creighton Memorial Prize 1909 for the college thesis on the bishops of Durham's boroughs; Victoria County History contributor from 1907, taken onto William Page's staff as an outworker paid per 1,000 words; Society of Antiquaries of Newcastle upon Tyne from 1908, its Librarian 1941-1946, Honorary Member from 1947; editor of A History of Northumberland vols XII-XV (1926, 1930, 1935, 1940); Quaker, pacifist and Socialist; with her sisters Ruth and Sylvia a founder member of the Progressive Players (1920) and the funder, project manager and namer of the Little Theatre, Gateshead, opened Wednesday 13 October 1943, believed to be the only theatre built in Britain during the Second World War; died Saturday 13 May 1972, aged 87, at Home House, her sight failed, Braille the great occupation of her last two years -- and, with her younger sister Ruth Dodds (1890-1976), author of The Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-1537, and the Exeter Conspiracy, 1538 (Cambridge University Press, 1915, two volumes, viii + 388 and 381 pp.), the book that was, in the obituary's words, "the principal book on this North Country Rebellion and its sequel" for over half a century, that Elton judged "beyond challenge", that Collinson called "a massively well-documented, impeccable narrative, a quarry for all subsequent historians of the subject", and that Hoyle's 2001 book finally superseded as interpretation while leaving it the standard as narrative. The book is the subject of this study; the life is the frame that explains how it was made, by whom, and at what cost. ## M1. The Turning-Point Survey
Open with a dated still-life of the political position before the storm. Chapter I, "The Turning-Point", lays out the succession settlement of June 1536 (Anne Boleyn executed 19 May; Jane Seymour; Mary and Elizabeth declared illegitimate; Henry empowered to name his heir; Richmond dead 23 July), the fiscal crisis (royal revenues fixed and customary while prices rose after the import of American gold and silver), and the cascade of measures (supremacy, Ten Articles, injunctions, dissolution of the lesser monasteries in March 1536, curbs on sanctuary and benefit of clergy, treason and succession laws, the Statute of Uses, market and cloth regulations), and then argues that if Henry had acknowledged Mary, beheaded Cromwell, burnt Latimer and reconciled with the Pope, "the greater part of England would have rejoiced" -- but "there was one fatal obstacle; the King could not afford it." What it IS: a pre-rising inventory, a balance-sheet in which every grievance is a weight. Watchable behaviour: a calm tableau that accumulates weights until one more measure tips it; the rising reads as the tipping of a scale already loaded, not as a sudden explosion.

## M2. Plots and Tokens
A faction is established by its tokens: badges, liveries ("the Buck's Head"), the White Rose as a name. The token is treated as forensic evidence of conspiracy -- who wears it, who exchanges it, who has a hundred of them ready "virtually at a moment's notice". The Dodds use the token to do the work Elton later uses it to do, but in the opposite direction: for them the badges are evidence of a failed aristocratic plot, a party that whispered and exchanged tokens and dreamed, not of a rising already planned. What it IS: the object as the proof of the affiliation. Watchable behaviour: a badge passing hand to hand; a livery that marks a side; a hundred badges counted out and the count itself becoming the argument.

## M3. Affinity and Confederacy
Map the power structure before narrating the events. Chapter III, "Affinity and Confederacy", lays out the kin-networks (Percy, Clifford, Neville, Bowes, Bulmer, Bigod), the feuds, the Council of the North, the border lawlessness, so that the rising is then the network catching fire. The chapter is placed before any rising happens, because the rising cannot be understood without the map of who owed whom what. What it IS: the social graph as the first chapter of the narrative. Watchable behaviour: a map of alliances lighting up node by node; a feud line drawn between two houses that later becomes a battle line.

## M4. Facts and Rumours
Rumour as a historical actor, not noise. Chapter IV, "Facts and Rumours", is built from the words of "numberless forgotten men and women, who often paid for their devotion to the religion of their fathers with their lives": friars as wandering preachers, the "articles of St Thomas" preached from church windows, the Grey Friars of Grantham deposition, parish priests blotting out the Pope's name with a piece of paper fastened down with balm, the belief "When the King is dead all these fashions will be laid down". The chapter's thesis is that discontent was already general and articulate before any rising, and that rumour -- of taxes on burials and christenings, of church goods to be seized -- was the fuse, not the aftermath. What it IS: the deposition as the unit of evidence, and the rumour as the cause. Watchable behaviour: a whisper travelling parish to parish, gaining weight each time it is repeated, until the whole county believes it at once.

## M5. The Bell Rung Backwards
Bells as the alarm system. "To sound the alarm, generally by ringing the peal backwards, was to proclaim to all the surrounding country that the parish had risen." Aske's cross-listening protocol is the mechanism inside the mechanism: Marshland waits for Howdenshire's bells, Howdenshire for Marshland's, so the alarm cannot sound without his order, and the rising is held back until the Lincolnshire answer is known -- "a mistake", the Dodds judge. What it IS: a distributed signal with a single gatekeeper. Watchable behaviour: two parishes listening across a river; one bell answering another; a whole county held silent by a man listening for a bell that has not yet rung.

## M6. The Forged Letter
A proclamation in Aske's name raises Beverley; Aske "utterly denied making or consenting to" it. The Dodds' move is the attribution sidestep: "On the whole, we incline to accept his word, but fortunately the question, though interesting, is of little importance... if we suppose this letter to have been 'forged in his name' it proves him to have been admittedly the man with most influence." Doubt about authorship becomes proof of authority: the letter works whether or not he wrote it, because the crowd swears on his name. What it IS: the forged document as evidence of the forger's target, not of the target's act. Watchable behaviour: a letter read aloud in a market-place; the crowd swearing on a name whose owner is miles away.

## M7. The Oath on Pain of Death
Swearing as the machine that converts a crowd into an army. The oath -- "to be true to God, the King and the Commonwealth, and to maintain Holy Church", taken on pain of death -- is administered by the commons riding around "swearing" their social betters, and the gentry are sworn at sword-point. The oath does three things at once: it binds the taker, it incriminates him (he is now a traitor whatever happens), and it levels him with the man administering it. What it IS: a speech act that manufactures an army out of a crowd. Watchable behaviour: an oath administered in a market-place, each man repeating it, the gentry kneeling to take it from men they would normally command.

## M8. The Invented Name
Aske invents "Pilgrimage of Grace" and the Five Wounds badge, rebranding rebellion as crusade: "a movement for the defence of the Church and the defenestration of the King's heretical councillors". The name does the political work -- it makes the rising unnameable as treason, because a pilgrimage cannot be treason, and it gives nine separate hosts a single identity. The Dodds treat the naming as Aske's chief act of leadership, the thing that turns a set of local risings into one rising. What it IS: the brand as the army. Watchable behaviour: a banner being sewn and painted; the word "pilgrimage" replacing the word "rebellion" on men's lips; the Five Wounds carried where a rebel flag would have been hanged for.

## M9. The Reply Never Sent
Henry writes an answer to the five articles in his own hand, then refuses to send it and has the Lincolnshire reply printed instead -- "thus returning an indirect answer to the rebels, without exposing himself". The withheld document is the policy: pride as a strategic variable, because to grant a safe-conduct would be "a kind of recognition of belligerency", and the King will not recognise the rebels as an enemy he must negotiate with. Norfolk's instructions are the same logic in the field: "to gain as much time as possible by discussion, and at the first favourable opportunity... break off the negotiations and straightway attack the rebels". What it IS: the unsent letter as an instrument of rule. Watchable behaviour: a letter written, sealed, and put away; a printed proclamation doing its work instead; a negotiation conducted entirely through substitutes.

## M10. The Rumour Autopsy
A spy's report (Knight, servant of Sir Francis Brian) says Aske is wavering and will accept the pardon; the story is traced back by writing to its supposed originator (Vavasour), who "dared not sign his letter, lest it should be intercepted" and denies it -- "This application to the supposed originator of the roundabout story demolished it altogether." The Dodds' method is on display: a rumour is not dismissed, it is unwound thread by thread to its source and then cut. What it IS: source criticism performed as detective work inside the narrative. Watchable behaviour: a rumour unwound thread by thread to its source, then cut; a letter that dares not be signed, and the unsigned denial that kills the story.

## M11. The Numbered Verdict
The Conclusion's four causes of failure, ranked, each stated as a thesis sentence: (1) the Pilgrims' "fundamental misconception of Henry's character", whom they took for "a weak, good-tempered sensualist, always the tool of some favourite", when "to change the King's policy they must change the King"; (2) "It had no leader of genius. The leaders of the Pilgrimage were honest men and men of ability, but they were nothing more"; (3) the conflict between gentlemen, who "wanted higher rents and lower wages", and commons, who "wanted lower rents and higher wages", so that "it seemed impossible that anything could reconcile these discordant aims"; (4) the Church's failure -- the lower clergy devoted themselves, the higher ecclesiastics were "supine", the Papacy "inert", Pole "refused to stir". The list is the argument: the book ends not with narrative but with a diagnosis, and two great errors summarised -- "over-confidence in themselves and over-trust in the King". What it IS: the numbered list as the final form of a two-volume argument. Watchable behaviour: four weights placed on a scale, one by one, until the scale tips against the Pilgrims.

## M12. The Long Shadow
Genealogy as continuity. The Conclusion traces the line forward to the Civil War: Philip and Brian Stapleton, Richard Aske (who drew up the indictment of Charles I), Fairfax, Sir William Constable (who signed the death-warrant) were descendants of Pilgrims. "These are not mere genealogical freaks. The spirit which had defied Henry VIII overwhelmed Charles I." The rising's political significance, in the Dodds' positive thesis, is "the union between the gentlemen and the commons" -- for two hundred years revolts had been either feudal or social, and the King-gentlemen alliance had been the throne's chief support; "The Pilgrimage of Grace was the first indication of the manner in which this alliance was to be broken." What it IS: the family tree as the argument's afterlife. Watchable behaviour: the same surnames recurring a century on, the same opposition wearing new clothes; a name on a 1536 muster roll reappearing on a 1649 death-warrant.

## M13. Death for a Wish
The Exeter conspirators "died for a few careless words -- for a wish -- for a dream of majesty": treason graded by severity. The Dodds' sentence is the scale: "The catholic martyrs died for their opinions. The Pilgrims died for maintaining their opinions with their swords. The 'Exeter Conspirators' died for a few careless words." The White Rose party "only whispered together and exchanged tokens and dreamed of better days", and missed their one chance -- "If the Marquis of Exeter had raised the west and Lord Montague had raised Hampshire, the south would have been plunged into turmoil... But, as a matter of fact, Exeter marched to join Norfolk... not one of the conservative nobles raised a man to second the Pilgrims" -- and Henry nevertheless resolved to "exterminate the house of Pole". What it IS: a scale of treason, from thought to word to deed, with the punishment the same at every step. Watchable behaviour: a scale with three pans -- opinion, sword, wish -- and the same sentence falling on all three.

## M14. The Strange Anecdote as Evidence
The wax child with two pins stuck through it, found in a shroud and rumoured to be an attack on the baby Prince Edward, born 12 October 1537; Mabel Brigg, executed for keeping the "Black Fast" against the King; Thomas Miller, Lancaster Herald, kneeling to Aske and later hanged with his head set up by Aske's body. Small, possibly unreliable stories told "for the sake of such light as they may throw on the state of England during 1538" -- the anecdote as a window into the mental world, not as proof of a fact. The Dodds print the story and then stand back from it, and the standing-back is the method: the story is evidence of what people believed, even where it is not evidence of what happened. What it IS: the lantern-slide, a single odd scene lit so that the whole dark room shows around it. Watchable behaviour: one strange scene, lit like a lantern-slide, that tells you what people believed while the great events pass offstage.

## M15. The Errata as Method
The Additions and Corrections (Vol. I pp. vii-viii; Vol. II) publicly correct the text: "Merlione" is a misreading of "Meliore", i.e. Mallory; "For William Bowyer read Richard Bowyer"; "Delete Lord Cobham's name". Self-correction printed in the book itself as part of the apparatus, signed in the authors' note of July 1915. The errata are not an embarrassment to be hidden; they are the method made visible, the proof that the transcription was checked against the originals and the check was honest. What it IS: revision in public, the historian correcting herself in print. Watchable behaviour: a correction slip pasted into a finished book; a name struck out and another written in, in the same hand.

## M16. The Piecework Economy
The history was written by the thousand words. Short of funds in 1907, Hope Dodds wrote to the Victoria County History editor William Page with her Newnham tutor's support, offering to work more on Durham or on Northumberland; Page took her onto his staff, then kept her on as an outworker paid per 1,000 words. The unit of her early career is the paid word-count, and the 1915 book -- two volumes, 769 pages, a bibliography of about 150 titles -- is the pieceworker's answer to piecework: the same discipline of accumulation, now applied to a whole rebellion. What it IS: the economic base of the scholarship, visible in the scholarship's form. Watchable behaviour: a ledger of words counted and paid for, growing into a book; a scholar paid by the thousand words who writes a book of hundreds of thousands.

## M17. The Borough Thesis
The local record as national history. Her college thesis on the bishops of Durham's boroughs won the Creighton Memorial Prize in 1909, and the thesis's method -- the borough as the place where the bishop's power met the town's liberties -- is the 1915 book's method in miniature: the rising understood parish by parish, county by county, through the local record. The Dodds' book is a county history that grew into a national history, and its authority comes from the same place the thesis's did: the local document read in the local archive. What it IS: the small place as the unit of the large argument. Watchable behaviour: a borough charter laid beside a rebel proclamation, and the same hand reading both.

## M18. The Cook's Identity
A woman historian recovering a woman's identity. "The Rival Cooks: Hannah Glasse and Ann Cook", Archaeologia Aeliana, 4th series, vol. 15 (1938), pp. 43-68, established Hannah Glasse's identity as Hannah Allgood, half-sister of Lancelot Allgood, and Ann Cook's feud with Allgood; the identification stood, and "information about her identity was lost until uncovered in 1938 by the historian Madeleine Hope Dodds". The mechanism is the archival identification: a name in a parish register, a will, a family connection, and a famous anonymous author becomes a named person with a family. What it IS: the recovery of a lost identity from the record, by the same documentary patience that built the 1915 book. Watchable behaviour: a cookery book with no author on its title page, and a name written back onto it from a will.

## M19. Minstrels and Folk Drama
Performance as evidence. "Northern Minstrels and Folk Drama", Archaeologia Aeliana, 4th series, vol. 1 (for the year 1924; published 1925), pp. 121-146, read before the Society on 30 July 1924, treats the minstrel and the folk play as historical sources: what people sang and acted is evidence of what they believed, in the same way the Dodds' Chapter IV treats the rumour as evidence. The paper is the book's method applied to a different century: the performed thing as the document. What it IS: the song and the play as the archive. Watchable behaviour: a minstrel's song collected like a deposition, a folk play staged like a parish rising.

## M20. The Theatre Built in Wartime
Three sisters buying land and building the only wartime theatre in Britain. With Ruth and Sylvia, Hope founded the Progressive Players in 1920, bought the Saltwell View site in 1939 (plot 400 pounds, the house at No. 3 450 pounds), built the Little Theatre, Gateshead, during the war -- the site requisitioned by the RAF, a barrage balloon on it until 1941 -- and opened it on Wednesday 13 October 1943 with A Midsummer Night's Dream; Hope was the named project manager, the plans were drawn up in her name, and the theatre's name was her choice. The theatre is still Gateshead's only theatre. What it IS: the historian as builder, the same energy that built a two-volume book applied to bricks and a stage. Watchable behaviour: three sisters measuring a plot of land in wartime, and a theatre rising where a barrage balloon stood.

## M21. The Sister's Obituary
The archive's voice is the sister's. Ruth Dodds wrote the obituary of Madeleine Hope Dodds, Archaeologia Aeliana, 5th series, vol. 1 (1973), pp. 223-224 -- the principal biographical source for Madeleine, and the source this study leans on for the life. The obituary is itself a mechanism: the sister who co-authored the book writes the author's life, and the life is told in the same documentary voice as the book, with the same attention to the small fact (the postal vote in May 1972, the Braille of the last two years, the humour "which sometimes appeared even in the sober volumes of NCH"). What it IS: the co-author as the biographer, the partnership outliving the subject. Watchable behaviour: a sister writing her sister's life in the journal they both published in, the obituary as the last joint work.

## M22. The Diary as Archive
Ruth's diaries, 1905-1974, Tyne and Wear Archives, ref DF.DOD, kept from the age of fifteen until shortly before her death, mix poems, short stories, fantasies, "deep ontological discussions with herself", and everyday entries; the First World War entries are the best studied, through Newcastle University's "War and the Moral Outdoors" project. The diary entry for Thursday 20 May 1915 -- "To Aidos and Nemesis this altar amidst the green boughs of the orchard is set up" -- was written in the same weeks the sisters' book was going through the press, and the diary is the household record behind the book: the same house, the same sisters, the same war, one writing history and the other writing the day. What it IS: the private record as the public book's shadow. Watchable behaviour: a diary kept for seventy years in the house where a two-volume history was written, the daily entry and the chapter growing side by side.

## M23. The Whig Predicament
The liberal historian celebrating a Catholic rising. Collinson's diagnosis, read end to end: as "liberal, Whiggish historians" the Dodds "wanted to celebrate the Pilgrimage as a noble episode in the struggle between liberty and despotism. Aske was their idealised hero. But the Pilgrims were on the wrong side. They were Catholics, which meant they could not really be progressive. So the Doddses over-emphasised the non-religious factors in the movement, and perhaps drew after them Dickens". The predicament is the book's engine and its flaw at once: the politics that made the book possible are the politics that distort it, and every later historian works on the fault line the predicament opens. What it IS: the author's politics as the book's load-bearing tension. Watchable behaviour: a hero who cannot be a hero on the author's own terms, and a book that praises him by looking away from what he believed.

## M24. The Quarry
The standard that stands above controversy. Collinson: the book "has tended to stand above controversy as a massively well-documented, impeccable narrative, a quarry for all subsequent historians of the subject; in the Dodds' own perception, 'scientific history'; in G.R. Elton's judgment, 'beyond challenge'." Loughlin, 2016: "by common consensus, the Dodds' account remains the standard in this respect [narrative]". The quarry is the mechanism by which a book outlives its own argument: the narrative is so documented that every later historian must dig in it, even the ones who demolish its conclusions. What it IS: the book as a mine, worked by its enemies as much as its friends. Watchable behaviour: a later historian quarrying the same documents the Dodds transcribed, and building a different book out of the same stone.

## M25. The Supersession
The replacement, stated as a programme. Hoyle's The Pilgrimage of Grace and the Politics of the 1530s (Oxford, 2001), xvi + 487 pp., is "the first full account of the Pilgrimage of Grace since 1915", and Palmer's review states its aim plainly: Hoyle "wishes to replace the eighty-year-old narrative of the Dodds sisters with a readable text, reflective of modern scholarship" and "has certainly produced a narrative that supercedes the Dodds in every important respect"; Kesselring: it "finally supersedes the impressive but dated study by Madeleine and Ruth Dodds". Collinson's verdict on Hoyle -- "There will never be a definitive history of the Pilgrimage. But this is as close to it as we may hope to get" -- is also a verdict on the Dodds: superseded as interpretation, standard as narrative, and the two verdicts held together without contradiction. What it IS: the explicit replacement, with the replaced book honoured in the replacing. Watchable behaviour: a new book placed on the shelf beside the old one, and the old one still consulted first.

## M26. The Conspiracy Inversion
The same evidence, read the other way. Elton took the Dodds' material -- the Darcy-Chapuys correspondence, the Five Wounds badges produced "virtually at a moment's notice", Darcy, Hussey and Constable as organisers -- and inverted the book's picture: the Pilgrimage as a court-faction conspiracy, "the effort of a defeated Court faction to create a powerbase in the country for the purpose of achieving a political victory at Court", the commons a "rent-a-mob". Collinson's reply, endorsed from Hoyle: "there is in fact little or no hard evidence of high-level conspiracy in 1536". The inversion is the mechanism by which a book generates its own opposite: the Dodds supplied the evidence, Elton supplied the reading, and the field has argued about the reading ever since. What it IS: the archive turned inside out. Watchable behaviour: the same badge, the same letter, the same muster roll, read once as a failed plot and once as a planned rising.

## M27. The Religious-Secular Fault Line
The question every later historian must answer, and the question the Dodds left open by answering it twice. Dickens, "Secular and Religious Motivation" (1967): the Pilgrimage had "a 'predominantly secular and economic causation'", it "was not a war, not even a potential war between Protestants and Catholics", and it was "not a unitary movement but actually four risings loosely connected by a broad, inchoate spirit of resentment and opportunism" -- and Dickens corresponded with the sisters while composing it, fifty years after the book. Davies, "The Pilgrimage of Grace Reconsidered" (1968): "the revolt should be understood primarily as a response to religious reforms", with religion doing three jobs -- precipitation, bonding, legitimation. Haigh, Scarisbrick and Bernard on the religious side; Reid, Dickens and Elton on the secular and conspiratorial side; Bush, "both the faith of Christ and the commonwealth"; Hoyle, populist and religious. Collinson: "the religious/secular dichotomy is modern and anachronistic", and "the Pilgrimage was not a monolith" -- Richmondshire and Westmorland about rents and entry fines, Lincolnshire about religion, Aske specifically denying his Pilgrimage was "for the Commonwealth". What it IS: the fault line the book opened and could not close. Watchable behaviour: the same rising split into four risings, then into two causes, then into one question asked differently by every generation.

## M28. No Conspiracy at All
The modern answer to the second volume. Desmond Seward, "The extermination of the White Rose", History Today, 1 January 2011, read end to end: the White Rose circle "were a group of friends rather than a party and while they sometimes spoke of Henry's replacement by his daughter Mary, with Reginald Pole (a cardinal, though not yet a priest) as king consort, it was only wishful thinking. Instead of joining the Pilgrimage, they sent troops to crush it." At the trials "the sole proof produced was Geoffrey's hearsay"; "there was not a scrap of documentary evidence". "Henry VIII convinced himself that he had escaped death by a narrow margin... But the 'Exeter Conspiracy' existed only in his deranged mind." The Dodds' own Conclusion already leans this way in part -- "the Poles were all but exterminated" is listed among the Pilgrimage's results, not among its causes -- and Seward finishes the move: the second volume's subject dissolves into Henry's paranoia plus Cromwell's frame-up. What it IS: the conspiracy unmade, by the absence of evidence rather than by a counter-narrative. Watchable behaviour: a trial with no documents, a confession that is only hearsay, and a conspiracy that exists only in one man's mind.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife so far

Phase one, the making (1904-1915). Newnham College, Cambridge, 1904-1906, Second Class Honours in the History Tripos; one extra year in research; the college thesis on the bishops of Durham's boroughs wins the Creighton Memorial Prize in 1909. From 1907 she writes for the Victoria County History, taken onto William Page's staff as an outworker paid per 1,000 words. The Pilgrimage of Grace is written with her sister Ruth, the authors' note signed "M. H. D. / R. D., July 1915", the thanks going first to her Newnham friend Myra Curtis, then to Professor A. F. Pollard, Mr I. J. Bell of the British Museum, Mr H. R. Leighton, the Rev. J. Wilson and Mr T. C. Hodgson. Cambridge University Press publishes it in 1915, two volumes, viii + 388 and 381 pp., five sketch maps, a list of works cited of about 150 titles, the documents transcribed from the originals in original spelling. Roger B. Merriman reviews it in the American Historical Review 21(4), July 1916, pp. 787-789. The phase ends with the book: two sisters in Gateshead, no university post, no archive of their own, have written the book the field will work from for eighty-six years.

Phase two, the standard (1915-2001). The book stands above controversy, "a quarry for all subsequent historians", Elton's "beyond challenge". Meanwhile the life goes on in Gateshead: "Topography of Durham" (1910, before the book), "The Problem of the 'Ludus Coventriae'" (1914), "Political Prophecies in the Reign of Henry VIII" (1916), "Northern Minstrels and Folk Drama" (read 30 July 1924, published 1925), "The Rival Cooks" (1938), "Barnabe Barnes" (1946); editor of A History of Northumberland vols XII-XV (1926, 1930, 1935, 1940), the obituary calling it her "most impressive work"; Society of Antiquaries Librarian 1941-1946, Honorary Member 1947; the Progressive Players from 1920 and the Little Theatre built and opened in 1943; the DNB biographies, subjects unidentified. Bush's The Pilgrimage of Grace (1996) works against the Dodds' narrative with a new method and a new conclusion; Bush and Bownes, The Defeat (1999), re-study the postpardon half as a subject in its own right. The phase ends with the book still the standard as narrative and no longer the standard as interpretation, and nobody has yet said so in one sentence.

Phase three, the superseded standard (2001 to now). Hoyle's The Pilgrimage of Grace and the Politics of the 1530s (2001) states the replacement as a programme and, in Kesselring's and Palmer's verdicts, achieves it; Collinson's London Review of Books essay of Thursday 1 November 2001 fixes the book's place -- "a massively well-documented, impeccable narrative, a quarry" -- and its predicament, the Whig historians who over-emphasised the non-religious factors. Bush's The Pilgrims' Complaint (2009) deals with the grievances systematically, and the field settles into the fault lines the book opened: religious or secular, popular or gentry-led, pardon or deception, continuation or new rising. The phase is still running: the book is cited as the standard narrative and superseded as interpretation in the same breath, and both verdicts are true.

Afterlife so far. The 1971 Frank Cass reprint, "after more than half a century, during which it has remained the principal book on this North Country Rebellion and its sequel"; the Cambridge University Press paperback reissue of 2015 (Vol. I ISBN 9781107502031, Vol. II ISBN 9781107501980); Project Gutenberg eBooks 77706 (Vol. I, released Friday 16 January 2026) and 78639 (Vol. II, released Saturday 9 May 2026), which put the whole text in the public domain for the first time. Ruth's diaries, 1905-1974, are at Tyne and Wear Archives and are being read as moral landscapes by Paul Wright's Newcastle project; the Little Theatre still operates in Gateshead; the plaque to Hope, Ruth and Sylvia was installed on Home House in 2005. Gillian Sutherland's In Search of the New Woman (2015) carries a single index entry for Dodds, p. 71 -- a page, not a chapter, and the study records it as such. The book's afterlife is the quarry's afterlife: it is still being dug in, and the digging is the proof that it was built to last.

## Aesthetic logic

The book and the life share one aesthetic: history written from the surviving thing, by the thousand words, in the house where it happened. The 1915 book is built from depositions, bills, rhymes, oaths, petitions, songs and prophetic rhymes, county by county; the life is built from the same materials at a smaller scale -- the borough charter, the parish register, the cookery book, the minstrel's song, the diary entry. In both, the object is not an illustration of the argument, it is the argument: the rumour's journey is the rising's map, the badge's count is the conspiracy's proof, the correction slip is the method's honesty. The logic is documentary before it is narrative. A chronicle tells you what happened; a deposition tells you what a forgotten man believed, and a rumour tells you what a whole county believed at once. The book's structure follows the same logic: fourteen chapters of narrative in Vol. I and ten in Vol. II before the argument in the Conclusion, because the argument has to be earned by walking the ground first, and the Conclusion's four numbered causes are the verdict of a book that has done its walking. The Whig predicament is the aesthetic's shadow: the same liberal politics that made Aske the idealised hero also made the book look away from what Aske believed, and the book is most itself where the predicament is most visible -- in the Conclusion, where the gentlemen and the commons want opposite things and religion is the one power that could have united them, and in the anecdote, where the wax child and the Black Fast are told for the light they throw, not for the facts they prove. The study's own form follows it too: where the book is closed, the study says so, because a gap honestly stated is worth more than a gap smoothly filled, and because the Dodds themselves printed their corrections in the book.
