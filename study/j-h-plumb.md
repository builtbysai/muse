## M1. The panoramic opening

Sir Robert Walpole vol. I opens not with Walpole but with two brilliant synthetic chapters on early eighteenth-century England, setting the stage before the narrative starts, and the Canadian Historical Review, on vol. II, names the move exactly: in those two chapters he was employed in setting the stage. The opening is the mechanism, because a biography that begins with the country rather than the man has already decided that the man is an effect of the country and not its cause, and the stage is the piece, and the piece must show the century arriving first, the institutions, the parties, the money and the manners, and only then the boy from Houghton walking onto a stage that is already lit, and the lighting being the argument.

**Takeaway:** open with the century, not the man, and the piece should show the stage before the actor.

## M2. The character thumbnail

George III is fixed in a single sentence in The First Four Georges, p. 146: "a pathetic figure in his purple dressing-gown, with his wild white beard and hair, totally blind, totally deaf, playing to himself on his harpsichord". The thumbnail is the mechanism, because a king reduced to one image, dressing-gown, beard, blindness, harpsichord, can be carried in the reader's head for the rest of the book without another word of description, and the carrying is the piece, and the piece must show the portrait arriving whole in one frame, the purple against the dark, the music no one hears, and the reader knowing the man from the image alone, and the image doing the work of a chapter.

**Takeaway:** fix the person in one image, and the piece should show the portrait, not the biography.

## M3. The aphoristic simile

Walpole on Pitt, in Chatham: "as well might he attempt to stop a hurricane with a hairnet". The simile is the mechanism, because a comparison that compressed makes the argument without stating it: Pitt's force is a hurricane, Walpole's instruments are a hairnet, and the mismatch is the politics, and the mismatch is the piece, and the piece must show the hurricane and the hairnet in the same frame, the small thing held up against the weather, and the weather winning, and the winning being the whole of the argument in one line.

**Takeaway:** argue by comparison, not by paragraph, and the piece should show the mismatch.

## M4. The single-problem book

The Growth of Political Stability in England 1675-1725 is built around one question, why did the rage of party end, and the book is built around the question, not around a period. The question is the mechanism, because a book that asks one thing for three hundred pages has a spine that a survey can never have, and the spine is the piece, and the piece must show the question asked at the start, the rage of party filling the room, and every chapter being an attempt at the same answer from a different side, and the answer arriving only when the question has been worn thin.

**Takeaway:** build the book around one question, and the piece should show the question, not the period.

## M5. Stability as made, not inevitable

The thesis move of Stability: stability was made, not delivered by time; institutions are made by men. "The Tory Party was destroyed..." (Stability, p. 172). The making is the mechanism, because a stability that was built can be unbuilt, and a party that was destroyed was destroyed by someone, and the agency is the piece, and the piece must show the hands doing the making, the party being taken apart piece by piece, and the stability standing at the end as something constructed, not something that happened, and the construction being the history.

**Takeaway:** show stability being made, and the piece should show the hands, not the tide.

## M6. The past-vs-history distinction

The Death of the Past (1969) defines by antithesis: "the past" is memory, heritage and sanction, while "history" is critical and scientific. The antithesis is the mechanism, because a book that splits its own subject in two has given the reader a test to apply to everything that follows, and the test is the piece, and the piece must show the two halves side by side, the warm past on one side and the cold history on the other, the reader being asked which one he is looking at, and the asking being the argument, and the argument being that the two are not the same thing at all.

**Takeaway:** split the subject in two and make the reader choose, and the piece should show both halves.

## M7. The tract for the times

A short book as an intervention in the present, not a monograph: The Death of the Past (1969), from the Saposnekov Lectures at City College of New York in early 1968, and the introduction to Crisis in the Humanities (1964). The tract is the mechanism, because a short book that speaks to the present does not need to prove everything, it needs to be heard now, and the hearing is the piece, and the piece must show the lecture hall, the present tense, the argument landing on the year it was written in, and the book being short because the moment is short, and the shortness being the point.

**Takeaway:** write the short book for the present year, and the piece should show the intervention, not the archive.

## M8. The editor-as-patron

The Festschrift and the series as argument: Studies in Social History: A Tribute to G. M. Trevelyan (ed., 1955), a tribute that states a credo in its introduction (p. xiii); editor of the Fontana History of Europe; general editor of Hutchinson's History of Human Society (9 vols). The editorship is the mechanism, because a man who edits a series has chosen the field's shape without writing a word of it, and the choosing is the piece, and the piece must show the table of contents as a manifesto, the contributors arriving as allies, the tribute volume doing the work of a declaration, and the declaration being that social history is the history that matters.

**Takeaway:** edit the field into shape, and the piece should show the table of contents as the argument.

## M9. The teacher's praise-and-blame method

H. E. Howard, the history teacher at Alderman Newton's, taught by unpredictable praise and blame, and Plumb copied the method. The method is the mechanism, because a pupil who never knows whether the next word will be praise or blame listens harder than one who knows, and the listening is the piece, and the piece must show the praise arriving without warning, the blame arriving without warning, the pupil kept off balance, and the off-balance being the teaching, and the teaching being the thing the pupil carries into his own classroom thirty years later.

**Takeaway:** teach by surprise, and the piece should show the praise and the blame arriving unannounced.

## M10. The television history

Royal Heritage: The Story of Britain's Royal Builders and Collectors (1977, BBC; with Huw Wheldon), produced by Michael Gill, in the Silver Jubilee year: history as royal buildings and collectors, told in pictures, the book that "sold in massive numbers". The television is the mechanism, because a history told through buildings that the camera can walk through does not need to argue for its subject, the buildings argue for themselves, and the walking is the piece, and the piece must show the camera moving through the rooms, the collections accumulating on the walls, the royalty as builders rather than rulers, and the building being the history, and the history being something you can stand inside.

**Takeaway:** tell the history through the building, and the piece should show the camera walking through it.

## M11. The connoisseur's eye

Objects as evidence and as pleasure: porcelain, wine, painting, the finest private wine cellar in Cambridge, a 16th-century rectory at Westhorpe, a mill in the south of France, a Manhattan pied-a-terre in the Carlyle Hotel, a Rolls-Royce. In Search of China (1986, on porcelain: "Master the scholarship; never begrudge a dealer his profit"); Vintage Memories (1988, on claret: "Petrus is a Gothic wine, Lafite pure Palladian"). The connoisseurship is the mechanism, because a historian who collects the century he writes about has put his hands on his evidence, and the handling is the piece, and the piece must show the porcelain held up to the light, the wine named by its architecture, the collection as a second archive, and the archive being lived in, and the living in it being the proof that the century is still touchable.

**Takeaway:** collect the century you write about, and the piece should show the object held up to the light.

## M12. Autobiographical identification with the subject

The historian in the book: Sir Robert Walpole vol. II dedicated to H. E. Howard, the schoolmaster who made him; Collected Essays I: The Making of a Historian (1988) as self-history. The identification is the mechanism, because a dedication that names the teacher says the book is a repayment, and the repayment is the piece, and the piece must show the debt stated on the dedication page, the schoolmaster standing behind the statesman, the historian writing his own making into the life of his subject, and the subject becoming the mirror, and the mirror being the book.

**Takeaway:** state the debt on the dedication page, and the piece should show the teacher behind the subject.

## M13. The thumbnail essay

The short piece that fleshes out a thesis without proving it: the Harvard Crimson on In the Light of History (1973): "These essays don't prove his thesis, but they flesh it out, and they are, as a group, a remarkably pleasant way of presenting supporting evidence." The thumbnail is the mechanism, because an essay that sketches Detroit, Bedlam, Pepys, Victorian and Edwardian life in a few pages each does not need to carry the argument, it needs to make the argument visible, and the visibility is the piece, and the piece must show the sketches accumulating, each one a small window, the windows adding up to a house, and the house being the thesis, and the thesis never having been stated outright at all.

**Takeaway:** sketch, do not prove, and the piece should show the windows adding up to a house.

## M14. The leisure-as-industry argument

Quantifying the unquantified: leisure as "an industry with great potentiality for growth" by the 1750s (The Commercialization of Leisure in Eighteenth Century England, 1974, Stenton Lecture, Reading). The quantification is the mechanism, because a pleasure that can be counted as an industry stops being a decoration on the century and becomes one of its engines, and the counting is the piece, and the piece must show the pleasure being measured, the assemblies and the gardens and the races adding up to a trade, and the trade being as real as the coal and the cloth, and the realness being the surprise.

**Takeaway:** count the pleasure as an industry, and the piece should show the measurement.

## M15. The consumer-revolution thesis

Naming a revolution: the commercialisation of leisure, children, pets and indoor plants as a consumer revolution (The Birth of a Consumer Society: The Commercialization of Eighteenth-Century England, 1982, with Neil McKendrick and John Brewer). The naming is the mechanism, because a century that bought things is a different century from one that made things, and the renaming is the piece, and the piece must show the shops filling, the children and the pets and the plants becoming goods, the buyers becoming the actors of the century, and the buying being the revolution, and the revolution having no barricades, only shopfronts.

**Takeaway:** name the buying a revolution, and the piece should show the shopfronts, not the barricades.

## M16. The archive-first biography

The big book grounded in one archive: the Cholmondeley papers at Houghton plus Archdeacon Coxe's Memoirs (1798) for Walpole. The archive is the mechanism, because a biography that sits on one family's papers has a floor under it that no synthesis can give, and the floor is the piece, and the piece must show the papers arriving in boxes, the letters being read in order, the statesman emerging from his own filing cabinet, and the cabinet being the biography, and the biography being true because the paper is there.

**Takeaway:** ground the big book in one archive, and the piece should show the papers in their boxes.

## M17. The planned-but-unfinished third volume

The panoramic survey design: Walpole's England, Walpole's Europe, Walpole's Empire, after Macaulay, of which only two volumes appeared; vol. I to 1722, vol. II from Sunderland's death (1722) to the election of 1734, and vol. III never came. The design is the mechanism, because a plan that outruns the life leaves a shape the reader can see even where the book is missing, and the missing is the piece, and the piece must show the three panels drawn, two of them filled, the third one empty, and the empty panel being as legible as the filled ones, and the legibility being the tragedy, and the tragedy being that the design was bigger than the years.

**Takeaway:** draw the plan larger than the life, and the piece should show the empty third panel.

## M18. The lecture-to-book pipeline

Lectures become books: the Ford Lectures, Oxford, 1965-66, become The Growth of Political Stability (1967); the Saposnekov Lectures, City College of New York, early 1968, become The Death of the Past (1969); the Stenton Lecture, Reading, 1972, becomes The Commercialization of Leisure (1974). The pipeline is the mechanism, because a book that was first spoken has a voice in it that a book written straight for print never gets, and the voice is the piece, and the piece must show the lecture hall first, the argument tried out on a live audience, the applause and the silence teaching the author what holds, and the book arriving second, already tested, and the testing being the polish.

**Takeaway:** speak the book before you write it, and the piece should show the lecture hall first.

## M19. The defence of the master

Answering the verdict: the 1983 Scribner essay "G.M. Trevelyan" answers Jenkins's "pontificating old windbag" (Portraits and Miniatures, 2011, p. 254) with literary craftsmanship and the liberal tradition. The defence is the mechanism, because a pupil who answers an attack on his dead master twenty years after the master's death is not defending a reputation, he is defending a lineage, and the lineage is the piece, and the piece must show the attack quoted in full, the counter arriving in the same register, the master defended not as a scholar but as a writer, and the writing being the thing the attacker never saw, and the seeing being the defence.

**Takeaway:** answer the attack on the master, and the piece should show the verdict quoted and the counter in the same register.

## M20. The literary-craftsmanship claim

Style as the historian's merit: "Trevelyan was a born writer and a natural storyteller... If one quality is to be singled out, is should be this, for all historians he is the poet of English history" (1983). The claim is the mechanism, because a historian defended on the grounds of his sentences is a historian defended on grounds his enemies cannot measure, and the immeasurability is the piece, and the piece must show the sentences doing the defending, the storytelling named as the merit, the poet standing where the scholar was attacked, and the standing being the answer, and the answer being that the best history is written, not just researched.

**Takeaway:** defend the historian as a writer, and the piece should show the sentence as the merit.

## M21. The dedication as argument

Sir Robert Walpole vol. II dedicated to H. E. Howard: the dedication states the debt and the lineage. The dedication is the mechanism, because a book that opens by naming the schoolmaster who taught its author has already told the reader where the author comes from, and the telling is the piece, and the piece must show the dedication page before the first chapter, the name standing alone, the reader understanding that the statesman's life is being written by a boy from a Leicester shoe town, and the boy being the real subject, and the subject being the debt.

**Takeaway:** put the lineage on the dedication page, and the piece should show the name standing alone.

## M22. The populariser's range

From Detroit to Bedlam to Pepys in one volume (In the Light of History, 1972): range itself as the argument for the liberal essayist. The range is the mechanism, because a book that moves from a riot in Detroit to an asylum in London to a diarist in the seventeenth century has refused to stay in its period, and the refusal is the piece, and the piece must show the jumps, each one a different century and a different city, the reader following because the voice stays the same, and the voice being the argument that a historian can go anywhere, and the anywhere being the liberalism.

**Takeaway:** let the range be the argument, and the piece should show the jumps, not the staying put.

## M23. The convert's rage

The political arc as method: Communist in the 1930s, radical and Labour supporter, elated at the 1964 Wilson win, moved right in the 1970s, ardent Thatcherite by the 1980s, "There's no rage like the rage of a convert", claimed in the Collected Essays to have been "a Thatcherite before Thatcher". The arc is the mechanism, because a historian who states his own conversion instead of hiding it has made his politics part of his evidence, and the stating is the piece, and the piece must show the arc drawn in the open, the left and the right both named, the convert's rage doing the arguing, and the arguing being honest because the arc is visible, and the visibility being the method.

**Takeaway:** state the conversion, do not hide it, and the piece should show the arc drawn in the open.

## M24. The patronage machine

Promoting pupils' careers at Christ's: McKendrick: "At Christ's alone he promoted the careers of historians of the calibre of Rupert Hall, John Kenyon, Frank Spooner, Barry Supple, Eric [Stokes]..." The patronage is the mechanism, because a teacher who places his pupils has built a school without writing a manifesto, and the placing is the piece, and the piece must show the pupils arriving one by one, the careers being opened, the school forming around the man, and the man being the institution, and the institution being the pupils, and the pupils being the work that outlives the books.

**Takeaway:** build the school by placing pupils, and the piece should show the careers opening one by one.

## M25. The college-as-base

Christ's as the operating platform: Fellow 1946, Tutor, Steward, Vice-Master, Master, "for over half a century devoted his life to the college" (McKendrick). The college is the mechanism, because a historian who stays in one college for fifty-five years has a base that no chair and no publisher can give, and the staying is the piece, and the piece must show the same rooms over the decades, the wine steward and the tutor and the master being the same man in the same place, the college absorbing the career, and the career being the college, and the college being the argument that a life can be lived in one place and still go everywhere.

**Takeaway:** stay in one place and let it be the base, and the piece should show the same rooms over fifty years.

## M26. The American circuit

Lectures and visiting posts as a second audience: Columbia 1960, City College 1968, New York City University 1971-72 and 1976, Cecil and Ida Green Honors Chair at Texas Christian University 1974, Lewin Visiting Professor at Washington University in St Louis 1977; the Collected Essays II: The American Experience (1989) as the American record, 41 essays from Columbus's journal to Watergate. The circuit is the mechanism, because a historian with a second audience in another country has a second hearing for every argument, and the hearing is the piece, and the piece must show the Atlantic crossed again and again, the same century told to a different room, the room answering differently, and the difference being the proof that the history travels, and the travelling being the second career.

## M28. The obituary as verdict

## M29. The unwritten book as negative space

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife

- Phase 1 -- Early, slow start (1911-1950). Leicester origins, Howard, the failed St John's entry, the first-class degree at Leicester (1933), Christ's under Trevelyan (1933 or 1934, see C1), the PhD (1936), the thesis he later disowned, Bletchley Park (Hut 8, Hut 4, Block B; the German Navy hand cipher Reservehandverfahren), the Ehrman Fellowship at King's (1939), Fellow of Christ's (May 1946). First book at 25 (the Equity Shoemaking history, 1936), first major book at 39 (England in the Eighteenth Century, 1950). The driver: the outsider who had to write to live ("had to write a great deal in order to support himself and his mother", Albion 1993 comment).
- Phase 2 -- The twenty golden years (1950-1969). 23 books between 1950 and 1973. Walpole I (1956) and II (1960); Chatham (1953); The First Four Georges (1956, written in six weeks); The Italian Renaissance (1961); Stability (1967, the Ford Lectures); The Death of the Past (1969, the Saposnekov Lectures). Reader 1962, Professor 1966, Chair of Faculty 1966-68, FBA 1968. The driver: the single-problem book and the panoramic biography, written for the general reader as well as the profession. Cannadine's arc: slow start to 1950, twenty golden years 1950-69, the long diminuendo 1970-2001.
- Phase 3 -- The long diminuendo (1970-2001). Resigned the professorship early 1974. In the Light of History (1972); the leisure work and the consumer-society book (1974, 1982); Royal Heritage (1977) and the television turn; the collected essays (1988-89) as self-history; Master of Christ's 1978-82; knighted 1982. The driver shifts from argument to celebration: leisure, consumption, royalty, porcelain, claret. The politics move right with him. The chairs he wanted never come: Modern History 1965 to Charles Wilson, Regius 1963 to Herbert Butterfield and 1968 to Owen Chadwick, the British Academy presidency to Chadwick; his name on Harold Wilson's 1976 "Lavender list" for a peerage, then removed.
- Phase 4 -- Afterlife (2001- ). Six obituaries in six days (Guardian 22 October, Telegraph, Times, Washington Post and New York Times 23 October, Independent 27 October 2001); the Cambridge tribute (24 October 2001); Cannadine's History Today appreciation (2002) and British Academy memoir (2004); the Death of the Past reissue (2003) with Schama's foreword and Ferguson's introduction; Cannadine's "Historians in the 'Liberal Hour': Lawrence Stone and J. H. Plumb Re-Visited" (2002); McKendrick's memoir, Sir John Plumb: The Hidden Life of a Great Historian (2020). The afterlife question is the memoir's: the great historian whose college and university never gave him the chairs he wanted, remembered by his pupils.

## Aesthetic logic

Plumb's aesthetic is the readable historian's: the sentence first, the thesis second, and the archive underneath both. Everything in the oeuvre serves readability without surrendering argument, and the two are not in tension for him because the argument is carried by the sentence. The panoramic opening (M1) and the character thumbnail (M2) are the same move at different scales: put the whole thing in front of the reader at once, the century in two chapters, the king in one sentence, and then let the detail fill in what the opening already showed. The aphoristic simile (M3) is the argument compressed until it can be quoted, and the single-problem book (M4) is the argument stretched until it can hold a whole career's reading. What holds the two together is the conviction that stability is made (M5), which is a writer's conviction as much as a historian's: if things are made, they can be described as made, with hands and decisions and accidents, and the description is the history. The past-vs-history distinction (M6) is the same conviction turned into a definition: the past is what you inherit, history is what you make by looking critically, and the looking is the work. The tract (M7) is the short form of that looking, the editor's patronage (M8) its institutional form, and the lecture-to-book pipeline (M18) its spoken form. Late in life the aesthetic shifts from argument to celebration, from the rage of party to the commercialisation of leisure (M14, M15), from the archive to the connoisseur's eye (M11), from the biography to the television history (M10), and the shift is not a decline but a change of subject: the same eye, the same sentence, turned from politics to pleasure. The unwritten books (M29) are the limit of the aesthetic: the design was always larger than the years, and the empty third panel of Walpole is the honestest image of the career, a plan drawn in full and filled in part. And the defence of the master (M19, M20) is the aesthetic stated as a creed: the historian is a born writer and a natural storyteller, and for all historians Trevelyan is the poet of English history, and the poet is the standard Plumb set for himself, and the standard he is judged by. The politics arc (M23) is stated in the open because the aesthetic demands it: the convert's rage is visible, the arc is drawn, and the reader is never asked to pretend the historian has no position. The college (M25) is the base that makes the aesthetic possible: fifty-five years in one place, the same rooms, the wine cellar, the pupils placed (M24), the trusteeships held (M27), the American circuit crossed (M26), and the life told six times in the obituaries (M28), and then written once more by the man himself (M30). The aesthetic, in one line: write it so it can be read, and make the reading the argument.
