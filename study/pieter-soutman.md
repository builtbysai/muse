# Pieter Soutman: Study Notes

**Site:** Pieter Claesz. Soutman (c. 1593/1601, Haarlem -- 16 August 1657, Haarlem), Dutch Golden Age painter and printmaker; trained as an engraver under Jacob Matham in the Goltzius circle, "collaborative assistant" in Rubens's Antwerp workshop c. 1615/16, Polish court painter to Sigismund III Vasa 1624-28, then Haarlem portraitist, civic-guard painter and print publisher who designed and published more than 120 portrait prints and painted two works in the Oranjezaal at Huis ten Bosch. ## The life

Pieter Claesz. Soutman was born in Haarlem, the youngest of six children of the wealthy Catholic owner of the Haarlem beer brewery De Werelt. His birth year is the study's first dispute: Barrett's archival work moved it from the older c. 1580 to c. 1593/1601, and the study uses the Barrett range -- the Stadel still lists c. 1580, and the Met's artist headers still give "ca. 1580-1657". He died aged 55-64, on 16 August 1657, in Haarlem; no cause of death, will or burial place is given in any source read.

His training was in the burin, not the brush. He was trained as an engraver under Jacob Matham, Goltzius's stepson, and Barrett places his early training "in the circle of Hendrick Goltzius". He did not join the Haarlem painters' guild as a young man; instead he travelled south. He arrived in Antwerp in 1616, was first mentioned in the Antwerp Guild of St Luke in 1619, when he registered a pupil, and was granted Antwerp citizenship in 1620. Whether he officially entered Rubens's studio is unknown -- Barrett describes him tentatively as a "collaborative assistant" whose primary occupation was producing workshop replicas and preparing preliminary designs for engravings after Rubens's paintings. The older tradition says otherwise: Houbraken claims he trained with Rubens, and Rubens's nephew Philip (in De Piles, 1677) lists him among Rubens's six pupils. The study presents both and asserts neither.

In 1624, probably during Prince Wladyslaw Vasa's visit to Antwerp and on Rubens's recommendation, he entered the service of King Sigismund III Vasa of Poland and was appointed royal court painter; he served as servitor until 1628, when he returned to Haarlem. He painted the king and Queen Constance of Austria in coronation robes (1626) and Prince Wladyslaw. The post was long contested -- Barrett writes that "until recently there was little proof of his position beyond his own claim" -- and the recent proof is tentative: Leitsch's 1999 publication of Sigismund's court finances lists a payment receipt to "the painter Pieter", and only one other Pieter (Danckerts de Rij) has been linked to that court, so the identification rests on elimination. Soutman himself never doubted it: he signed his letters "Pittore di Sua Maestra de Polania" (Painter to the King of Poland) and continued to use the title until his death in 1657. Whether he ever travelled to Poland is unresolved -- the Stadel says he entered Sigismund's service while the king was "in exile in the southern Netherlands", which suggests the service may have begun in Antwerp.

He returned to Haarlem in 1628 and settled there until his death. The Haarlem guild dates conflict: the guild's membership-grants list gives 1628, the Stadel says he became a full member in 1633, the year he married Gudula Fransdr. van de Sande. The two may be different steps (grant vs full membership); no source read reconciles them, and the study does not collapse them. He became a respected portrait painter in Haarlem and won the lucrative schutterstukken, the civic-guard group portraits -- two survive in the Frans Hals Museum, c. 1642. An early approach to the Orange court failed: in 1630 he sent a portrait of the Prince of Orange to the States General and it was rejected. Ten years later he sold eight series of Nassau portraits to the mayor of Haarlem.

His Catholicism was lifelong and open. The family brewery, the affiliation with the Catholic Hofje Codde en Van Beresteijn (probably regent of the Codde hofje), and the letter to a priest signed with de Bray and Claas van Bouckhorst all say so, in a Protestant city. The Beresteyn family portrait he painted for the hofje regents was later sold to the Louvre in the 1860s as a Frans Hals -- a famous misattribution in the Dutch press, not a deception, and the first of several Soutman pictures to spend decades under Hals's name.

The Orange court came to him in the end through his kinsman Jacob van Campen and the stadhouder's secretary Constantijn Huygens. Two unpublished letters from Soutman to Huygens survive in the British Library (Add 34195), published by Barrett in Oud Holland in 2009, and they are the key documents of his court patronage. The 30 January 1638 letter sends 33 portrait prints at a shilling apiece and asks in return for portraits of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia van Solms to engrave; Huygens annotated it with the prices at which he sold the prints on. On 10 January 1639 Soutman was paid 350 guilders for his paintings of the King and Queen of Spain for Huis Ter Nieuburch in Rijswijk. On 15 July 1640 he was paid 150 Carolus guilders for dedicating a book of eight or ten Nassau and Orange portraits to the stadhouder. The 11 March 1649 letter, written jointly with Van Campen ("us"/"ours" throughout), presses Huygens for overdue Oranjezaal payment from Amalia van Solms and promises Soutman will come to The Hague in 6-8 days to "give corallin to the remaining brushes" -- corallin being an organic substance used to keep painters' brushes supple -- proof he still had unfinished work in the room. The postscript notes young Pieter de Grebber also expected compensation, since his father Frans Pietersz. de Grebber had been buried on 9 March 1649.

## The evolution: from the burin to the wall, and back to paper

The 1610s (W01-W03) are almost entirely graphic: the large Battle of the Amazons design after Rubens (c. 1619, Christ Church, Oxford) for Vorsterman's six-plate engraving, the two-plate Last Supper etching (c. 1618, nearly a metre wide), and the Rape of Proserpina etching after Rubens (c. 1620-25 or c. 1628 -- the dating is disputed). Soutman arrives in Antwerp already trained as an engraver, and his first decade is translation work: Rubens's and Van Dyck's inventions turned into printable designs inside the studio's reproductive machine.

The Polish years (W04-W06) put painting on the record: the Sigismund III coronation portrait (1626) and the Van der Capellen marriage pair (1626, Milwaukee and dealer hands), the first securely dated, securely located Soutman portraits, painted in the grand Van Dyck-inflected court mode -- large panel, dark ground, lace and satin as the real subject -- two years before he even resettled in Haarlem.

The 1630s (W07-W12) are the Haarlem turn: etchings after Van Dyck (The Taking of Christ, 1628), the reworked Turkish Prince (c. 1630, a print as palimpsest -- Rubens's drawing, Soutman's additions), the Emerantia van Berensteyn portrait (1634-36, Waddesdon), and the Lamentation after Rubens (after 1636), inscribed "P. Soutman effigiavit et excud." -- maker and publisher in one line, issued in Haarlem under his own privilege, granted 1636. The hand moves from the burin to the business.

The 1640s (W13-W17) are the peak, and the most productive: the tronie A Young Man Holding a Staff (1640, NGA Washington, the monograph's cover and Barrett's most recent discovery among the twenty accepted paintings), the Princes of Nassau series (1640-43, eleven princes, engraved by Suyderhoef after Soutman's designs), the two civic-guard pieces (c. 1642, Frans Hals Museum -- thirteen officers of the Calivermen, seventeen of St George, each about 2 by 4 metres), and the Christ at Emmaus (1643, engraved by Van Sompel, published by Soutman). Painting becomes his main activity only now, in his forties, and the catalogue's proportions say it plainly: 20 accepted paintings against 193 prints.

The years 1648-50 (W18-W19) are the palace: the Oranjezaal at Huis ten Bosch, where Soutman painted a Triumphal Procession in the lower frieze of the north wall and the Allegory on the Excellent Rule of Frederik Hendrik on the north ceiling -- the ceiling only recognised as his during the 1998-2001 restoration, after three centuries as Pieter de Grebber's. The late 1640s close with the Counts and Countesses of Holland series (title page 1649, plates 1650, engraved chiefly by Cornelis Visscher after Soutman's copies of the town-hall portraits): Soutman ends his career as Holland's visual historian, turning painted rulers into a printed dynasty.

## M1 -- The collaborative assistant: inside the machine, never quite a pupil

Soutman's Antwerp mechanism is the position Barrett names for him: "collaborative assistant". He was in Rubens's workshop, he did the workshop's work -- workshop replicas, preliminary designs for engravings after Rubens's paintings -- and yet the record will not say he was a pupil. The early sources call him one of Rubens's six pupils; the archival reading says formal entry is unproven. The key exhibit is the Battle of the Amazons design (W01): Barrett catalogues the large Christ Church drawing as Soutman's c. 1619 preliminary design for Vorsterman's six-plate engraving, while Bellori (1672) attributed it to the young Van Dyck and the HNA reviewer sides with Bellori, allowing Soutman only a follower's share. The mechanism is the translator's position: the man who turns the master's invention into something printable, whose hand is everywhere in the plates and nowhere in the signature. He also worked the same way with Van Dyck -- his 1628 Taking of Christ etching after Van Dyck's compositions -- and Barrett suggests Van Dyck's and Rubens's interest in etching in the early 1620s may have been sparked by Soutman, who had learned the technique from Goltzius. The claim is doubly hedged ("suggests... may have been... most likely"), and the study keeps the hedges: Soutman as a possible vector of etching into the Rubens studio, not a proven one.

**Takeaway:** build the piece around the translator, not the master -- the hand that turns the big invention into the printable sheet, visible in every plate and signed on none of them, and let the attribution argument itself be the drama: two names on one drawing, and the drawing does not say which.

## M2 -- The title that outlived the post: "Pittore di Sua Maestra de Polania"

Soutman's second mechanism is a signature that became a career. He held the Polish court post, if he held it, from 1624 to 1628 -- four years, contested, proved only tentatively by a payment receipt to "the painter Pieter" published in 1999. He signed himself "Pittore di Sua Maestra de Polania" in his 1638 letter to Huygens, in his 1649 letter, and, Barrett states, until his death in 1657 -- twenty-nine years of using a title for a post he had left after four. The mechanism is the title as portable capital: the court appointment, real or claimed, becomes the credential that sells the next commission, the next print series, the next dedication. It worked: the Nassau princes series, the Orange court letters, the Oranjezaal all trade on the man who had been a king's painter. The study does not resolve whether he ever went to Poland -- the Stadel's "in exile in the southern Netherlands" leaves the service plausibly begun in Antwerp -- and that unresolved geography is part of the mechanism: the title travels better than the man.

**Takeaway:** let the title do the work -- one line of signature, carried for twenty-nine years after a four-year post, and build the piece as a calling card that keeps arriving long after the court that issued it has moved on.

## M3 -- The division of labour: the print as a small factory

Soutman's Haarlem print enterprise is a production line, and its mechanism is the split of the plate. "Soutman's division of labor consisted of his providing detailed portrait drawings for the engravers Suyderhoef, Van Sompel and Louys while he would add the freely etched ornamental frames surrounding them. This combination of highly finished engraved portraits surrounded by etched borders simulating ornate stucco frames, evoke painted portrait galleries." The face is cut by the specialist; the frame is etched freehand by the master; the sheet is sold as a picture in a gallery that does not exist. The Lamentation (W12) signs the whole operation in one line -- "P. Soutman effigiavit et excud.", maker and publisher -- and the Princes of Nassau (W14) and Christ at Emmaus (W17) show it running at scale: Soutman draws, Suyderhoef or Van Sompel engraves, Soutman etches the frame and publishes under his own privilege. His Haarlem assistants were Jonas Suyderhoef (c. 1613-1686), Pieter van Sompel (c. 1600-after 1644) and Jacob Louys (1595-after 1644); his pupils in printmaking were Cornelis Visscher, Louys and Van Sompel. The first states came from Antwerp (1616-24); the majority of second and third states were produced in Haarlem, where he received his own privilege in 1636.

**Takeaway:** split the plate and keep the frame -- the face by the specialist, the ornamental border by your own hand, and the sheet becomes a gallery wall; the frame is the signature, and the signature is the business.

## M4 -- The paper portrait gallery: dynasties on paper

The late career is not a set of single portraits but a machine for producing dynasties on paper, and that machine is the fourth mechanism. The Princes of Nassau (1640, Pr-63-73) -- eleven princes, dedicated to the stadhouder -- then the Counts and Countesses of Holland, Zeeland and Friesland (title page 1649, plates 1650, Pr-154-192) -- thirty-nine rulers copied from the portraits in Haarlem's town hall, which he was permitted to copy in 1646, with text by Petrus Scriverius. In all, Barrett's catalogue counts some 132 portraits of members of the various dynasties and famous people, out of 193 prints. The same production line runs every series (M3), and the same instinct runs the Oranjezaal (W18-W19): a painted gallery of one man's life, the triumphal procession as a dynasty in a single frieze. The two undated early series -- the Habsburg, Stuart and Bourbon nobility and the Dukes of Burgundy, 35 portraits plus title pages united by analogous etched frames -- show the system already forming before the dated series begin. The mechanism is the series as the unit: no single portrait matters as much as the set, and the set is a history -- Holland's rulers, Nassau's princes, the kings of Poland -- printed, priced (a shilling apiece in the 1638 letter) and sold.

**Takeaway:** do not paint one portrait, print the dynasty -- the same frame, the same production line, thirty-nine rulers in a row, and the set is the history; a single face is a sample, a series is an argument about who counts.

## M5 -- The long horizontal: the frieze habit

From the two-plate Last Supper etching (W02, c. 1618, nearly a metre wide) through the Rape of Proserpina (W03) to the Oranjezaal frieze (W18), Soutman keeps returning to the long lateral procession -- a frieze-maker's instinct that survives the move from etching to monumental painting. The Last Supper is built from two plates on two sheets because one plate cannot hold the table; the Oranjezaal Triumphal Procession marches soldiers, captives, spoils and allegorical figures in a long horizontal band beneath the room's great allegories. The mechanism is the lateral march itself: figures in a line, moving one way, the story told by the order of the march rather than by any single figure's gesture. It is the engraver's habit -- the plate is wider than it is tall, the procession fits the format -- carried into the palace, where the frieze is the room's own horizontal band. The civic-guard pieces (W15-W16) are the same habit at portrait scale: thirteen and seventeen figures in a line, 2 by 4 metres, the company as a procession that has stopped to be painted.

**Takeaway:** lay the picture out sideways and let it march -- one long band, figures in a line moving one way, and the story is the order of the march; the frieze is the format, and the format is the idea.

## M6 -- The Hals confusion: Flemish smoothness mistaken for Haarlem bravura

Soutman's sixth mechanism is a case of mistaken identity that lasted three centuries. His pictures were so often taken for Frans Hals's that the confusion is itself the evidence: the Emerantia van Berensteyn portrait (W10) was attributed to Hals until 1959, when it was reattributed to Soutman; the Catharina Both van der Eem (W11, Louvre) was proposed as Soutman's by Foucart in 1970-71 and agreed by Grimm in 1989, while Slive maintained the Hals attribution throughout, and the Louvre still lists it as "entourage de Hals"; the Beresteyn family portrait was sold to the Louvre in the 1860s as a Hals. The difference, once seen, is the handling: Hals's broken touch against Soutman's Flemish smoothness, the Rubens and Van Dyck training showing in the finish. The same sitters, the same city, the same decade, different hands -- and the civic-guard pieces (W15-W16) make the comparison concrete: in 1642 the Calivermen gave Soutman the commission Hals had held for five group portraits between 1616 and 1639, and the St George guard's treasurer Michiel de Wael sits in Soutman's canvas after Hals had painted him in 1626/27 and 1639, so the two painters' versions of the same man can be compared head to head. The mechanism is the boundary between two Haarlem hands, drawn in the same paint.

**Takeaway:** paint the same sitter twice and let the handling argue -- the Flemish smoothness against the Haarlem bravura, the same face under two hands, and the picture that was a Hals for three hundred years becomes the proof of the difference.

## M7 -- The letter to Huygens: the business in the archive

The two surviving letters to Constantijn Huygens are Soutman's most modern mechanism: the print business conducted as correspondence, with prices in the margins. The 30 January 1638 letter sends 33 portrait prints at a shilling apiece, offers Huygens's own copies as a gift, and asks for portraits of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia to engrave; Huygens annotated the letter with the prices at which he sold the prints to Frederik Hendrik, his brother Maurits and secretary Jacob Junius -- "His Excellence 16-10-0, My [his] brother 9-18-0, Junius 9-18-0, total 36-6-0". The letter proves his print production began well before 1638, though the earliest date securely connected to his publications is 1640. The 11 March 1649 letter presses for overdue Oranjezaal payment and promises the "corallin" for the remaining brushes. He dedicated prints strategically: the Fall of the Damned after Rubens (1642) to Huygens, permission requested in a now-lost letter of 15 January 1643, after the print was already completed and dated; an engraving after Rubens's Rest of Diana after the Hunt to his cognato Van Campen. The mechanism is the archive as the artwork's other half: the picture is the product, the letter is the price, the dedication and the overdue payment -- and without the letters, the whole Orange-court decade would be invisible.

**Takeaway:** show the letter with the picture -- the print at a shilling, the patron's prices in the margin, the corallin for the brushes, the payment eleven years overdue -- and the business becomes the biography; the archive is the second half of the work.

## M8 -- The reattributed ceiling: the late proof of standing

Soutman's last mechanism is a reattribution. The Allegory on the Excellent Rule of Frederik Hendrik (W19, 1648-50) hung on the Oranjezaal's north ceiling as Pieter de Grebber's for three centuries, until the restoration of 1998-2001 recognised it as Soutman's -- the second Soutman in the room, doubling his share of the cycle, beside his Triumphal Procession in the lower frieze (W18). The three Haarlem artists divided the room: Soutman, Van Campen and De Grebber each painted a triumphal procession for the lower walls (De Grebber two, Soutman and Van Campen one apiece), painted on canvas in their own studios and installed later; on the vaulted ceiling Soutman painted the Allegory on Excellent Rule, Van Campen Apollo and Aurora, De Grebber The Ascension of Frederik Hendrik into Heaven (1650). The mechanism is the ceiling as promotion: at the end of the 1640s, the engraver-turned-publisher was trusted with an allegory in the same room as Jordaens, Honthorst and Van Campen, and the room's own restoration had to correct the record to give him his share. The Stadel singles out his "instrumental" role in the Oranjezaal, "one of the most prominent projects in the northern Netherlands at that time".

**Takeaway:** put the late work on the ceiling and let the restoration find it -- the allegory overhead, three hundred years under another man's name, and the room itself correcting the record is the proof of standing no signature could give.

## The aesthetic logic

Soutman's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the logic of a man who lived between two art histories. Translate, do not invent: the career begins in the burin, turning Rubens's and Van Dyck's inventions into printable designs, and the translation is the skill -- the preliminary design, the plate, the state, the privilege. Print the system, not the picture: the portrait gallery, the dynasty on paper, the frieze that marches, all run on the same production line, and the set is the work. Carry the title: the Polish court post, contested and brief, becomes a signature carried for twenty-nine years, because a court painter's title travels better than the court. And let the room correct the record: the ceiling allegory, three centuries as de Grebber's, recognised as Soutman's only when the restorers looked up -- the standing is proven by the work, not by the name on it.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. Soutman was a Catholic from a brewing family painting civic guards and palace ceilings in a Calvinist republic, a Netherlandish artist in Barrett's phrase -- "more flexible than has been suggested and as a result, impossible to categorize in national terms that have roots in the nineteenth-century structure of art history" -- whose oeuvre fell between the Dutch and Flemish art histories for three centuries because it belonged to both. He never becomes a Protestant Dutch genre painter; he remains a court-and-corporation artist working in a Flemish idiom inside a Dutch city, and the confusion with Hals is the price of that position: the same city, the same decade, the smoother hand.

## The network

Jacob Matham (1571-1631): engraving teacher; Goltzius's stepson and favourite pupil, who took over the family print workshop c. 1600; over 400 engravings, more than a quarter after Goltzius; Haarlem guild president 1605; served a Catholic ideal in his saints -- the confessional thread Soutman inherited. Hendrick Goltzius (1558-1617): the circle Soutman's early training began in; the source of the etching technique Barrett suggests Soutman carried into the Rubens studio. Peter Paul Rubens (1577-1640): Antwerp workshop c. 1615/16; "collaborative assistant" per Barrett, pupil per Houbraken and Philip Rubens; recommended Soutman to Prince Wladyslaw in 1624; the source of most of Soutman's reproductive prints, made after Rubens's death from Haarlem. Anthony van Dyck (1599-1641): fellow Antwerp colleague; painted Soutman's portrait in Brussels c. 1628 (identified as Soutman in 2018); Soutman etched after Van Dyck's Taking of Christ compositions and acted as intermediary draughtsman/etcher for engravings after Van Dyck. Sigismund III Vasa (1566-1632): Polish king, Soutman's patron 1624-28; his successor's court painter was Peter Danckerts de Rij from 1638. Salomon de Bray (1597-1664, #581): fellow Haarlem Catholic, co-signatory of the priest letter, fellow Oranjezaal painter, fellow guild member. Jacob van Campen (1595/1596-1657, #578): Soutman's "neef" and promoter, coordinator of the Oranjezaal programme; the 1638 Huygens letter opens by citing him. Constantijn Huygens (1596-1687): secretary to Frederik Hendrik, art adviser for the Oranjezaal; recipient of Soutman's two surviving letters (1638, 1649). Pieter de Grebber (c. 1600-1652/53, #580): fellow Haarlem classicist and Oranjezaal painter; the Allegory ceiling was his for three centuries before the restoration gave it to Soutman. Caesar van Everdingen (1616/17-1678, #577): fellow Oranjezaal painter. Frans Hals (c. 1582/83-1666): fellow Haarlem portraitist; the man Soutman's pictures were mistaken for, and the man whose civic-guard commission Soutman took in 1642. Hendrick Gerritsz. Pot and Pieter Claesz: named by Wikipedia among Soutman's influences. Rembrandt van Rijn (1606-1669): adapted Soutman's Rape of Proserpina etching for his own 1631 painting (Berlin). Jonas Suyderhoef (1613-1686), Pieter van Sompel (c. 1600-after 1644), Jacob Louys (1595-after 1644): Haarlem engraving assistants; Suyderhoef later became Frans Hals's principal engraver. Cornelis Visscher (1628/29-1658): pupil, engraver of the Counts of Holland series; Willem van der Leeuw: engraver trained in his workshop. Amalia van Solms (1602-1675) and Frederik Hendrik (1584-1647): Orange court patrons; the Oranjezaal was Amalia's memorial to Frederik Hendrik. Gudula Fransdr. van de Sande (wife, married 1633). Modern scholarship: Kerry Barrett, Pieter Soutman: Life and Oeuvre (OCULI vol. 12, 2012); Anne-Marie Logan's HNA review; Barrett, "Mijn Heer: Letters from Pieter Soutman to Constantijn Huygens", Oud Holland 122 (2009).

## Future candidates

Top pick for #583 is Peter Danckerts de Rij (1605, Amsterdam -- buried 15 December 1660, Amsterdam). The direct succession: Soutman held the Vasa court post 1624-28 and Danckerts took it up a decade later, in 1638, as court painter and architect to Wladyslaw IV, then moved in 1648 to Queen Christina's court in Stockholm (Charles X Gustav held him in high regard), returning to Amsterdam only in 1659. Son of Cornelis Danckerts de Ry of the great Danckerts family of printers, painters and engravers, he carried the Dutch court portrait -- the coronation-robe state portrait Soutman had invented for Sigismund III -- through a second reign and a second kingdom, painting Wladyslaw IV, Sigismund Casimir Vasa (1644) and the 22 effigies of Polish monarchs in the Royal Castle Marble Room. Houbraken prints a poem praising his work in Poland; Baldinucci wrote his life. Doorway sketch: "Hop from the Soutman study: the man who took his chair in Warsaw ten years after he left it -- Peter Danckerts de Rij, Soutman's successor as the Vasa court's Dutch painter, who carried the coronation portrait from Poland to Christina's Stockholm."
