## 1. Peggy Hookham and the telephone-directory name

She was born Margaret Evelyn Hookham on 18 May 1919 in Reigate, Surrey, the daughter of Felix John Hookham, a British mechanical engineer with the British-American Tobacco Company, and Hilda, nee Acheson Fontes, the illegitimate daughter of an Irishwoman, Evelyn Acheson, and a Brazilian industrialist, Antonio Goncalves Fontes. She had one sibling, an older brother Felix, who later became a dance photographer and also adopted the surname Fonteyn. Ballet lessons began at four, with Grace Bosustow, taken with her brother; her mother attended the earliest lessons, learning the basic positions alongside her daughter, and became a famous backstage presence nicknamed the "Black Queen". The first newspaper review came in July 1924, when she was five: a charity concert solo, "a remarkably fine solo", "vigorously encored". The family followed her father's postings -- first to Louisville, Kentucky, where her mother distrusted the local school and there were no ballet lessons, then, when Peggy was nine, to China: about a year in Tianjin, a brief stint in Hong Kong, then Shanghai from 1931, where she studied with the Russian emigre Georgy Goncharov. She was a reluctant student who disliked Cecchetti drills and preferred the fluid Russian style; competitiveness with a classmate, June Brae, pushed her to work. Her mother brought her back to London at fourteen to pursue a ballet career -- the return is 1933 in the Royal Ballet and Opera account and English Heritage, "at 14" in Wikipedia, a 1933-34 window that is not pinned to a month here. In 1934 her father wrote from Shanghai announcing an affair and asking for a divorce; he was later interned by the Japanese in Shanghai from 1943 to 1945. The name was manufactured, and the manufacture is the point: she first performed as "Margot Fontes" (1933, as a child actress in de Valois's The Haunted Ballroom; 1934, a snowflake in The Nutcracker), her mother asked the Fontes relatives for permission to use the family name and was refused, possibly to avoid association with a theatrical performer, and mother and daughter then picked "Fontene" from the telephone directory and altered it to Fonteyn -- from her maternal grandfather's surname Fontes, Portuguese "fonte" meaning fountain, "fonteyn" being the English spelling until the sixteenth century. She took the name "Margot Fonteyn" in 1935. [C33994]

**Takeaway:** the first homegrown English ballerina was invented twice over -- a name chosen from a telephone directory, and a style built on a lower-middle-class girl with a Brazilian grandfather and a formidable mother who sat in on her lessons. [C33995]

## 2. The succession at sixteen

In 1934 Ninette de Valois invited her to join the Vic-Wells Ballet School, where she studied with Serafina Astafieva, Olga Preobrajenska and Vera Volkova. Her debut with the Vic-Wells Ballet was as a snowflake in The Nutcracker, and her first solo role was the Young Tregennis in The Haunted Ballroom. 1935 was the hinge year: Ashton created the Bride in Le Baiser de la fee for her and cast her as the Creole girl in Rio Grande, and Alicia Markova, the company's first prima ballerina, left the Vic-Wells later in 1935. Fonteyn, aged sixteen, succeeded her, sharing the lead at first and quickly rising to the top. She spent summers in Paris studying with the exiled Russian ballerinas Olga Preobrajenska, Mathilde Kschessinska and Lubov Egorova. Her relationship with the composer and conductor Constant Lambert, the company's music director, began in 1935 and continued on and off for the rest of his life. One piece of internal arithmetic in the sources is flagged and not used: English Wikipedia says she retired in 1979 "45 years after becoming the Royal Ballet's prima ballerina", which points to 1934, while the same article dates her succession to Markova to 1935 and her formal appointment as Prima Ballerina to 1939 -- the 1935 succession is the best-supported date and is used here. [C33996]

**Takeaway:** she did not climb to the top of the company, she was handed it at sixteen when the only other candidate left -- and then spent the next forty-four years earning a succession she had been given before she was ready for it. [C33997]

## 3. Le Baiser de la fee: the first created role

Le Baiser de la fee, premiered 20 May 1935 at Sadler's Wells Theatre by the Vic-Wells Ballet, to Stravinsky after Tchaikovsky, with Ashton as choreographer, gave Fonteyn her first created role: the Young Bride. The date rests on the Mary Clarke chronology reproduced in Ballet in Britain; Wikipedia gives the year 1935 only, and the role name "Young Bride" follows Wikipedia's performance list, the scanned chronology's cast line being garbled. Ashton at this point admired her lyric qualities and elegance but judged her to have "brittle stubbornness" and to lack polish; the role was built on the delicate, feline grace he saw in her rather than on finished technique. The ballet is a bleak Andersen-derived tale, a young man stolen from his bride by a fairy; Fonteyn was the mortal bride left behind. The mechanism is already the whole forty-year pattern: Ashton does not give her a display, he gives her a state -- the abandoned one, the waiting one -- and builds the role on what her body already does rather than on what it cannot yet do. [C33998]

**Takeaway:** the muse relationship starts with a limitation honestly seen -- brittle, unpolished -- and a role built on the grace that was already there, not on the technique that was not. [C33999]

## 4. Apparitions and Nocturne: the turning point

Apparitions, 1936, Sadler's Wells, Ashton to Liszt arranged by Lambert, cast the seventeen-year-old Fonteyn as the Woman in the ball gown -- the Poet's vision, the unattainable muse, opposite Robert Helpmann as the Poet -- in a Gothic-Berlioz fantasy of a poet searching for l'amour supreme and finding death. The exact premiere day is unverified in every source consulted, and a scanned contemporary chronology appears to place the work in late 1935, but its OCR is unreliable and every standard source dates it 1936. It consolidated the Fonteyn-Helpmann partnership that carried the 1930s and 1940s; Helpmann, Fonteyn later said, was her favourite partner across her whole career, and he taught her theatricality. By modern reconstruction accounts (Sarasota Ballet, 2019) the role's steps were "baldly simple: single pirouettes, mincing walks on pointe and tiny jumps scooping the feet under the legs" -- the early Fonteyn effect came from line, epaulement and presence, not virtuoso display. Nocturne, also 1936, to Delius's Paris, was the hinge: the Flower Girl, a wistful, poverty-stricken flower seller, a small, touching, unglamorous role, and her success in it changed Ashton's view of her -- he recognised she could become the heir to Alicia Markova as the company's lead dancer. Where Apparitions used her as an ideal, Nocturne used her for pathos, the quality that would later define her Cinderella and her Giselle. The Times called it "the touching Nocturne". Both works have vanished from the repertory. [C34000]

**Takeaway:** the turning point is not a triumph but a flower girl -- pathos, not display, is what convinces the choreographer she can lead the company. [C34001]

## 5. Les Patineurs: the White Couple

Les Patineurs, premiered 16 February 1937 by the Vic-Wells Ballet at Sadler's Wells, to Meyerbeer arranged by Lambert, is a Victorian skating party on a frozen pond, and Fonteyn was the female half of the White Couple, in pas de deux with Robert Helpmann. The device is the translation of skating into ballet -- gliding, spinning, poise -- and the White Couple's pas de deux is romantic lyricism in its purest early form. It is a measure of her position at seventeen that she held the romantic centre of the ballet, and a measure of its ensemble nature that the night's real star was Harold Turner as the Blue Boy, not the White Couple. The ballet ran in London every season from 1937 to 1968 except 1960, and was among the first ballets televised, by the BBC in May 1937; Fonteyn danced it with Helpmann again on BBC television in 1937 and in later revivals. [C34002]

**Takeaway:** the romantic centre of a ballet can belong to a seventeen-year-old who is not yet its star -- the position comes first, the stardom catches up. [C34003]

## 6. The classics inherited: Giselle, Swan Lake, Aurora

Fonteyn took over the classical roles when Markova left in 1935, and by 1939 she had danced the leads in Giselle, Swan Lake and The Sleeping Beauty and was appointed Prima Ballerina of the Vic-Wells. Her Giselle dates to 1937, in the Coralli-Perrot-Petipa staging to Adam; the early Giselle showed the lyric-mad-scene tradition, fragility, musicality, mime. Swan Lake came first: Odette in 1935, with Odile danced separately by Ruth French in split casting, and the dual role of Odette-Odile in 1938. It was the turning point that convinced critics and audiences a British ballerina could carry a full-length classical Russian ballet: Arnold Haskell wrote that never before had her performance been "so regal in manner or half so brilliant", and Tangye Lean wrote that she "rose to it with a stability that one had not seen in her before". The dual role demanded the two opposed registers -- white, vulnerable Odette and black, glittering Odile -- that became the template for her whole career: lyricism and dramatic fire in one body. Aurora came in 1939: 2 February (gala) and 22 March (command performance for the French president), in the Sergeyev staging after Petipa billed as The Sleeping Princess. Britannica judges the 1939 Aurora "still considered the definitive Aurora of the era". The National Portrait Gallery's summary is the measure of the speed: by the time she was twenty she had danced the lead in three of the great classics. [C34004]

**Takeaway:** inheritance is the mechanism -- she did not create the classics, she took them over from the ballerina who left, and the taking-over is what made her the prima. [C34005]

## 7. Dante Sonata: barefoot gravity

Dante Sonata, first performed 23 January 1940 at Sadler's Wells Theatre by the Vic-Wells Ballet, to Liszt orchestrated by Lambert, with Fonteyn as leader of the Children of Light (with Pamela May, Julia Farron and Michael Somes, against the Children of Darkness led by June Brae, Joy Newton and Robert Helpmann), is Ashton's reaction to the whole stupidity and devastation of war -- his sole venture into expressionistic choreography, devised on tour as war broke out, after Dante's Inferno. About seventeen minutes, danced almost entirely barefoot with the women in long white transparent gowns and hair unbound, it stages two equally tortured groups, Light against Darkness, with no victory possible. Fonteyn's leadership of the Light required sculptural, processional stillness and rarefied anguish rather than bravura: a complete break from the lyric muse of 1936-38, and the first evidence that her range included stark, adult gravity. It was one of the company's pre-eminent wartime ballets, revived at Covent Garden on 20 March 1946, then lost until Birmingham Royal Ballet revived it in 2000 with help from three original cast members. The "Children of Light / Children of Darkness" labels did not appear in the opening-night programme; they were added later. [C34006]

**Takeaway:** the first proof she could carry gravity -- barefoot, unbound hair, no victory, and the stillness doing the work the steps used to do. [C34007]

## 8. The 1946 reopening: Aurora at Covent Garden

On 20 February 1946 the Sadler's Wells Ballet moved into the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, and Fonteyn danced Aurora in The Sleeping Beauty at a command performance before King George VI, Queen Elizabeth, Queen Mary, Princesses Elizabeth and Margaret, and Prime Minister Clement Attlee. The majority of sources -- the Victoria and Albert Museum, Royal Ballet and Opera Collections, Madeleine's Stage -- give 20 February; Apollo magazine dates the evening 19 February, and the conflict is carried, majority 20 February. Costumes were pieced together from donated silk, velvet, old opera costumes, furs and velvet curtains under post-war rationing, to designs by Oliver Messel; Robert Helpmann was Prince Florimund and Beryl Grey the Lilac Fairy. With no living Russian reference to learn Aurora from, she created her own interpretation, and the ballet became her signature and the company's. The 1949 American tour made it her international coronation: the Metropolitan Opera premiere on 9 October 1949 drew curtain calls lasting half an hour, impresario Sol Hurok called it the "most outstanding" performance he had ever presented, the New York Herald Tribune called her "unmistakably such a star", and she was on the covers of Time and Newsweek. She went on dancing Aurora for decades -- NBC live colour Producers' Showcase on 12 December 1955, watched by about thirty million viewers; La Scala 1950; Paris 1954 -- and kept returning to the ballet at the end, as the Queen in Miami in February 1986. In her own account and in criticism, the later Aurora was "stripped to the essence of the role and constantly improved": less display, more authority. [C34008]

**Takeaway:** a signature role made without a model -- no living Russian to copy, so she invents the Aurora, and the invention becomes the company's signature for forty years. [C34009]

## 9. Symphonic Variations: stillness as the centre

Symphonic Variations, premiered 24 April 1946 by the Sadler's Wells Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Cesar Franck, with designs by Sophie Fedorovitch and a cast of Fonteyn, Moira Shearer, Pamela May, Michael Somes, Brian Shaw and Henry Danton, conducted by Constant Lambert, is Ashton's first ballet for the company in its new Covent Garden home, and his foray into abstraction: no story, no scenery beyond a backcloth, six dancers in white who never leave the stage and end the ballet in the same position as they began it. Fonteyn was the lead female -- Shearer danced one of the three ballerina roles, May the third -- and her performance was dubbed "brilliant". The work made such physical demands that in the first production and early revivals the dancers took no part in any other ballet on the same evening's bill. For Fonteyn it was the proof that her lyricism could carry a plotless work on line and musicality alone. The watchable behaviour that matters most here is the stillness: the central ballerina stands still in a corner, one foot crossed over the ankle of her supporting leg and resting on point, arms by her sides, while the others move. The critic James Monahan called that stillness "the most marvelous achievement of Margot Fonteyn's long and exemplary career"; Keith Money described her there as "the still centre of the world". Macaulay, citing the teacher Patricia Hutchinson McKenzie: "Fonteyn, standing still, conveyed not only repose but rapturous meditation... Her head was... slightly raised, her eyes more so, gazing upward on a diagonal into space... part of this moment's beauty lay in the calm at the base of Fonteyn's neck." [C34010]

**Takeaway:** stillness is not the absence of dancing -- held with a raised gaze and a calm neck, while five other dancers move, it is the loudest event on the stage. [C34011]

## 10. Cinderella: the role she missed, then remade

Cinderella, premiered 23 December 1948 by the Sadler's Wells Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Prokofiev, with Ashton and Robert Helpmann en travesti as the Ugly Stepsisters and Michael Somes as the Prince, had Moira Shearer as Cinderella -- not Fonteyn. Fonteyn missed the premiere because she tore a ligament in her ankle on the first night of Ashton's Don Juan, 25 November 1948, as La Morte Amoureuse, and could not dance for several months. She then took over the role, and this is where the sources diverge: Wikipedia's main article, citing Daneman, says she "recovered sufficiently to dance with Michael Somes in the Christmas presentation of the ballet", still in December 1948, while Wikipedia's performance list, citing Monahan, says "Fonteyn opened in Cinderella on 25 February [1949]". The conflict is carried unresolved; what is agreed is that she danced the role with Somes in the 1948-49 season, and that the same cast with Fonteyn substituted for Shearer gave the production its United States debut on 18 October 1949. What her Cinderella showed was a remake, not a takeover: she challenged the traditional Act I costume, replacing the usual brown outfit with a stark black dress and a kerchief tied severely over her hair, and observers said she "inserted a new, stronger sense of pathos into the performance". Where Shearer's Cinderella was porcelain-delicate, Fonteyn's was suffering made visible -- the Nocturne flower girl grown into a full-length heroine. She went on dancing the role into the 1960s. One asymmetry belongs here: Ashton left the rights to Cinderella (and Symphonic Variations) to Michael Somes, not to Fonteyn, while Fonteyn inherited Daphnis and Chloe and Ondine. [C34012]

**Takeaway:** the role she missed becomes the role she remakes -- a black dress and a severe kerchief, and pathos is a costume choice before it is a step. [C34013]

## 11. Daphnis and Chloe: the pastoral creation

Daphnis and Chloe, to Ravel, with Fonteyn as Chloe created for her and Michael Somes as Daphnis, is a music-led pastoral narrative after Longus, and one of her two most noted roles of the early 1950s, with Sylvia. Its premiere day is split and is carried as a conflict: the Royal Ballet and Opera's production page says 3 April 1951, while Wikipedia's performance list says 5 April 1951 and lists performances on 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11 April. The role asked for sensuality without coarseness and for the long, singing line that was her signature; she danced Chloe intensively in the premiere season, a dozen or more performances from April to July 1951, and kept it in repertory into the 1960s, the last listed performances in June and July 1966. It is the middle-period Fonteyn at her most purely Ashtonian. One small device in it is the most precisely described piece of Fonteyn choreography in any source read: in Chloe's flute dance she "simply taps her foot against her hand (one) and then against the floor (two) before releasing the energy upward (three) with her arms, hands, and eyes" -- percussive punctuation on the beat, then a release that travels up through the whole body and the gaze. Mark Morris, shown the film after her death, reportedly said: "It's the greatest thing I've ever seen." [C34014]

**Takeaway:** rhythm made visible as a wave -- tap, tap, release -- and the smallest cell in the ballet is the one the choreographers remember. [C34015]

## 12. Sylvia: the garland and the inverted catch

Sylvia, premiered 3 September 1952 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Delibes, with Fonteyn in the title role created for her, Somes as Aminta, John Hart as Orion and Alexander Grant as Eros, is Ashton's re-choreographing of the 1876 Merante ballet as a vehicle. Clive Barnes's phrase is the mechanism: "the whole ballet is a garland presented to the ballerina by her choreographer". Ashton designed the entire ballet around Fonteyn's talent: Sylvia is a huntress-nymph of Diana, arrogant and athletic in Act I, surrendering to love by Act III, and the role is almost entirely the ballerina's, with no supporting roles for female soloists. The legend Ashton told was that in 1946 he dreamt Delibes charged him with reviving the neglected score. The production was well received but Ashton kept trying to restructure it; it disappeared from the repertory after 1959 and was only revived in full by Christopher Newton in 2004. For Fonteyn it was the summit of the "ballerina vehicle" phase: strength, not fragility -- a huntress, not a muse. The partnering mechanism in it is the one Macaulay singles out: "as Sylvia, the trust with which she hurls herself across the stage to arrive upside down in Michael Somes's arms is the revelation that brings the plot to its resolution" -- a full-speed throw into an inverted catch, resolved by absolute trust in the partner's hands. [C34016]

**Takeaway:** a vehicle is not a showcase -- it is a garland, the whole ballet bent around one body, and the plot resolves when she trusts the catch enough to throw herself upside down into it. [C34017]

## 13. Ondine: a concerto for Fonteyn

Ondine, premiered 27 October 1958 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to a commissioned score by Hans Werner Henze, titled Undine, with designs by Lila de Nobili, was created for Fonteyn, with Michael Somes as Palemon, Julia Farron as Berta and Alexander Grant as Tirrenio, and Henze guest-conducting the premiere. It is the only full-length ballet that Ashton choreographed to original music. A critic called it "a concerto for Fonteyn": a three-act vehicle in which the water spirit who longs to be human -- and whose love is fatal to the mortal man -- is on stage almost throughout, in choreography that demands the liquid, weightless line she owned. The first-night reviews were unanimous about one thing only: Fonteyn's triumph in the title role; the rest of the ballet, and Henze's difficult score, got mixed notices. From the premiere until the work left the repertory in 1966, nearly every performance had Fonteyn in the lead, the only exceptions being Nadia Nerina and Svetlana Beriosova. The role's backstory matters: Ashton first wanted Walton for the score, then Macbeth -- Fonteyn firmly opposed playing Lady Macbeth -- and then Miranda in The Tempest, which she was unenthused by; Ondine was the subject they settled on. The Reigate statue of Fonteyn, by Nathan David (1980), depicts her in her favourite role -- Ondine. What is not verified, and so not asserted: no source read gives the specific step-vocabulary for Ondine's water quality, for instance any undulating port de bras, and no prompt in the #789 block is built on one. [C34018]

**Takeaway:** continuity is the mechanism -- like the water, the ballet flows along with none of the set pieces one might expect, and the star has to carry three acts on flow alone. [C34019]

## 14. The Firebird: Karsavina's gift, body to body

Fonteyn's first Firebird was 23 August 1954 at the Empire Theatre, Edinburgh, in Fokine's 1910 original to Stravinsky, with Michael Somes as Prince Ivan, Ashton himself as Kastchei and Svetlana Beriosova as the Tsarevna. She was taught the part by Tamara Karsavina, who had created the role in Paris on 25 June 1910: a photograph in the Victoria and Albert Museum catches the sixty-nine-year-old Karsavina in day clothes raising her arms "like wings" for Fonteyn in tutu and feathered headdress at a 1954 dress rehearsal. Fonteyn's Firebird was counted "among her greatest achievements", above all for her ability to use her jetes to simulate flight -- the lyric jumper turned into a creature of air. The production played the Metropolitan Opera House in September 1955 with the same principals; she danced the role through 1955-56 and returned to it in February 1962, on the eve of the Nureyev partnership. It is the clearest case of Fonteyn as inheritor: a role learned body-to-body from its creator, not from notation. The same transmission runs through Le Spectre de la rose, in which she danced the Young Girl from February 1944, coached by Karsavina, who had created the role in Monte Carlo in 1911 opposite Nijinsky -- a role she danced again on the 1962 Australian tour and once more in the summer of 1979 with Nureyev, bookending her career from wartime to her sixtieth year -- and Petrushka, in which she danced the Ballerina Doll from the company premiere on 26 March 1957. Britannica pairs the three as her outstanding Fokine revivals. [C34020]

**Takeaway:** some roles are not learned, they are handed over -- a sixty-nine-year-old woman raising her arms like wings in day clothes, and the dancer in the tutu takes the flight from her. [C34021]

## 15. Musicality: arrival without haste

Fonteyn's first conductor was Constant Lambert, who "kept strict tempi that were generally much faster than those the Royal Ballet uses today" -- his 1939 Sleeping Beauty highlights are "the fastest Sleeping Beauty recordings ever made". Ashton and Lambert "would marvel together at how Fonteyn would be in one place on the stage at one point in the music and would make them think 'But she has to cross the stage in the next moment -- she won't have time!' But she not only always made it there, she also never seemed in haste". Her close New York friend Joy Williams Brown's summary, quoted by Macaulay: "A ballerina always has time." That is the operative principle: her phrasing was built on covering distance inside fast tempi while keeping the upper body unhurried, so the musical line reads as spacious even when the feet are working at speed. Macaulay studied Victor Jessen's live films of Fonteyn in The Sleeping Beauty and Swan Lake from the Sadler's Wells North American tours of 1949-1956, edited to a live Sadler's Wells recording conducted by Robert Irving, who followed Lambert's precedent on tempo: the film "shows you how Fonteyn, within Irving's fast tempo, finds time to make every moment register with marvellous immediacy and drama". In Swan Lake her Odile grand pas de deux has "astounding speed, attack, definition -- and footwork, with a good many steps that today's ballerinas simply omit". The fouettes are a musical device, not a trick count: Fonteyn "knew that she seldom if ever could manage all thirty-two without travelling" and quoted a 1949 New York critic's phrase for her travelling fouettes, "Cook's tours of the stage", but she kept attempting all thirty-two "until past age fifty", where her idol Alicia Markova had abandoned them in her late twenties. Richard Jarman recalled a Swan Lake with Fonteyn in her late forties or later when "his heart was pumping in his chest because of the phenomenal musical timing she achieved with each fouette turn". Macaulay's gloss is the mechanism: "Today, most of our ballerinas throw in double and triple turns -- but thereby lose the musicality. Fonteyn, always doing single turns, showed their musical point." Singles, one per beat, each turn landing on the music -- the count served the score rather than the applause. What was not found, and so is not attributed: no source read gives a specific account of her using rubato, deliberately stretching or delaying against the beat. Her timing is consistently described as exact arrival inside fast tempi, and as holding a pose a fraction longer, not as bending tempo. [C34022]

**Takeaway:** musicality is not keeping up with the music -- it is arriving on the beat without ever seeming in haste, one single turn per beat, while the feet do the hurrying the upper body refuses to show. [C34023]

## 16. Line: invisible joints and the Margot arabesque

She was 5ft 4in, and the proportions are the point: "her perfect proportions; her relatively small (five feet, four inches), light, and supple body". Macaulay corrects a common error -- he once described her as having "long limbs" and was corrected by a dancer friend: "No, she did not have long limbs. She did not have short limbs. She had perfect proportions." The proportions that mattered were "her neck, her waist, her shoulders". Fonteyn herself wrote in her Autobiography that her proportions "helped distribute the strain of dancing evenly around her physique". Ashton, in a 1979 television documentary, called her "the proportions of Venus and the mind of Minerva". The line was continuous: "She was the dancer with invisible wrists, it seems, and invisible knees. Everything about her style became part of the larger continuity of line. Her arabesque founded a style: which became the Royal Ballet style, in which the fingers, wrists, elbows, and knees make no intrusion." The hands were learned, not natural: in her mid-teens she modelled herself on Alicia Markova until her partner Robert Helpmann told her that by imitating Markova she was dancing with what he called "dead hands", and, stimulated by his remark and by working with Ashton and de Valois, she developed a use of the fingers that brought great fullness to the line without any of the separation of digits that characterises ballet style elsewhere. The feet were her weak point, rebuilt: "she laughed about it a lot -- were her feet, which were initially weak and problematic. When she arrived at the Vic-Wells, de Valois said 'We are just in time to save her feet'; Ashton called them 'Margot's little pats of butter'". In her mid-twenties she "completely restudied her own technique with the great teacher Vera Volkova, who helped to reshape the entire arch of Fonteyn's feet as well as the enunciation of her footwork", going back to the most basic steps of the classroom, and Karsavina in the 1960s said: "With Fonteyn alone the foot is so lightly poised that the toe seems to kiss the ground." The arabesque has two distinct, named forms. The low front arm: "with Fonteyn the relatively low arm is a thing of beauty. The eyes look ahead, over the arm, into space". And the "Margot arabesque", Macaulay's label, from Symphonic Variations in 1946-48: "Although her face, arms, and raised leg are in profile, her shoulders are squared to address the audience, while she holds her arms in symmetrical lines that continue the downward slope of her shoulders -- and her raised leg is parallel to her back leg... Although this arabesque is held with serenity, it distils a quality of ecstasy in the exposure it places on the neck. And when the ballerina raises her head to look upward, it echoes the depiction of Maenads... in the art of the ancient Greeks... The throat and jawline are exposed." The eyes are part of the line: in the Prelude of Les Sylphides, "as she reached the back of the stage, [she] would open her arms and arch her neck and upper back, as she looked up at the moon... You felt you knew exactly where the moon was in that 'Sylphides' night sky, just by watching the angle of the back of her head. And she still made this register in her late, late fifties". The body is a pointer, not a display. And in Cinderella, dancing her variation's third phrase with her back to the audience, "while remaining on a single point, she does a backbend toward the audience and then, without losing balance, returns to vertical stance" -- a full upper-body arch balanced on one pointe. The one exception proves the rule: in The Firebird, "the hands seem to shake off sparks". Macaulay's summary is the yardstick: "The roles of Odette and Giselle are often danced with plenty of Romantic embellishment. When you return to the films and photographs of Fonteyn, you find how strictly she observes them as classical roles, with unexaggerated lines and unmannered phrasing. She became the yardstick for classicism." Ashton's verdict, quoted by Macaulay: "she was apparently incapable of unharmonious or ugly movement." [C34024]

**Takeaway:** the line is one unbroken curve from toe to fingertip, the joints invisible, the front arm low so the gaze travels past it -- and the eyes aim at a moon the audience cannot see but knows exactly where to find. [C34025]

## 17. Partnering: Somes, the calm base

The partnerships run in sequence: Robert Helpmann in the 1930s and 1940s, who taught her theatricality and whom she later named her favourite partner; Michael Somes from 1946 through the 1950s, her most frequent partner through the decade after the war; Rudolf Nureyev from 1962 to 1979. Somes was "the archetypal Royal Ballet prince, noble and mature, and although never the most brilliant of technicians, his stage presence and authority gave a weight to the classical roles rarely seen nowadays". As a young man he "was not the most reliable of partners. Hard work changed that... he danced with Fonteyn through the greatest years of her career, his physique, looks and temperament providing a perfect foil for her: there is a film of Act 2 of Swan Lake which wonderfully shows the sympathy between them and the strength of his support". His partnering manner was "calm and unobtrusive help" -- he "often [was] happy to give a young debutante the immense benefit of his calm and unobtrusive help". In the 1958 BBC Nutcracker pas de deux, "Somes turn[ed] Fonteyn in tight, brilliant pirouettes" and there is "the unusual lift where she floats atop Somes' shoulders". The mechanical picture is a stable, unshowy base: the ballerina's line is presented, not competed with; lifts are secure and classical -- a shoulder sit, a supported arabesque, and in Sylvia the full-speed throw into the inverted catch of section 12. No source read quantifies weight or gives a lift-by-lift comparison of Somes against Nureyev; what is documented is the direction of change, and that direction is the subject of the next section. [C34026]

**Takeaway:** the first great partnership is a base, not a contest -- calm, unobtrusive, secure -- and the ballerina's line is presented by it, never competed with. [C34027]

## 18. Partnering: Nureyev, the equal duet

Nureyev defected in Paris in June 1961. De Valois invited him to the Royal Ballet and offered Fonteyn the chance to dance his debut; she was reluctant over the nineteen-year age gap -- she was forty-two, he was twenty-three -- and agreed. Their first performance together was Giselle at the Royal Opera House on Wednesday 21 February 1962: fifteen minutes of applause and twenty curtain calls (EBSCO gives twenty-three; the split is carried, not resolved), and The Observer called it "history-making". She died on 21 February 1991, exactly twenty-nine years to the day after that Giselle. The partnership lasted seventeen years, 1962 to her retirement in 1979, and they toured the world -- Sydney to Melbourne in 1964, Stuttgart, Vienna, New York, Washington, Monaco, Greece -- with the 1965 film An Evening with the Royal Ballet grossing over one million US dollars, a record for a dance film at the time. What each supplied that the other lacked is the EBSCO synthesis: "It combined two very different styles of dance: the calm and ordered simplicity of Fonteyn with the fiery, impulsive passion of Nureyev, his pyrotechnics with her cool restraint." Physically "they were well matched -- both small, her slender femininity contrasting with his muscular frame, her delicate oval face with his wild Slavic features". Temperamentally, "she was methodical while he was wildly exuberant". The partnering change is stated explicitly by Alexander Bland: their conception of partnering, "in which -- as in opera -- a duet involves equal contributions from both, was something new... 'Sometimes,' Bland continued, 'part of the man's role is to show off the ballerina; but Nureyev never became a passive "porter" in the old manner, just as Fonteyn never lost touch with him to make an effect with the audience. They seemed aware of each other even when their backs were turned. When their eyes met, a message passed.'" Somes's own verdict: "the pairing of Nureyev and Fonteyn was brilliant, as they were not partners but two stars of equal talent who pushed each other to their best performances." Macaulay, who saw Nureyev from the mid-1970s, describes his three onstage manners toward ballerinas: with most, as if saying "You are lucky to share this ballet with me"; with a few, "You are a worthy partner for me"; "With Fonteyn alone did he behave as if he was the lucky one." The physical signature of that deference: in Romeo and Juliet, "Nureyev would sink to his knees to kiss the hem of her robe as if this was the most precious object in the world". The working system was mutual spontaneity: "If the mood struck, they were so much in harmony with each other as characters on the stage that she might initiate a new emphasis in a role, and he would respond instantly. It gave their performances a constant freshness, and their audiences experienced a unique blend of confidence and expectation, coming to the theater with the sense that any night might be like no other." Loyalty was a working rule: "Fonteyn would not approve an unflattering photograph of Nureyev, nor would she dance with other partners in ballets within his repertoire." In the 1990 documentary The Margot Fonteyn Story, Nureyev said they danced with "one body, one soul". Her biographer Meredith Daneman: "Most people are on level A. They were on level Z." Clement Crisp's phrase: "It was an artistic love affair conducted in public." The curtain-call record, each figure separately: Giselle, 21 February 1962, twenty calls; Marguerite and Armand, 12 March 1963, twenty-one; Swan Lake, Vienna State Opera, October 1964, eighty-nine, still cited as the Guinness World Record for a ballet; Romeo and Juliet, 1965, forty-three. No sourced mechanical description of a specific Nureyev lift by name was found in any source read, and none is invented here. [C34028]

**Takeaway:** the second partnership is not a porter and a display -- it is two stars of equal talent, aware of each other even with their backs turned, and the man behaves as if he is the lucky one. [C34029]

## 19. Marguerite and Armand: one extended pas de deux

Marguerite and Armand, premiered 12 March 1963 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, at a Royal Gala in the presence of Queen Elizabeth II, to Liszt's Piano Sonata in B minor orchestrated by Humphrey Searle, with designs by Cecil Beaton, was created for Fonteyn as Marguerite and Nureyev as Armand, with Michael Somes as Armand's father. Ashton heard the Liszt sonata on the radio and only afterwards discovered that Liszt had been a lover of Marie Duplessis, the real Marguerite. The mechanism is the flashback as a single extended pas de deux: Ashton set the ballet as a fevered dream in which Marguerite, from her sickbed, looks back on the great romance of her short life. The entire work contains no ensemble numbers and is effectively, with the exception of the scene between Marguerite and Armand's father, one extended pas de deux between the two principals. Six short scenes: first meeting, escape to the country, the father's confrontation, renunciation, return to Paris, deathbed reunion; she dies in his arms. "Less a ballet than a series of psychological closeups", in Richard Buckle's phrase. It was one of the first works of modern ballet in which the male lead was of equal dramatic weight to the female lead. The Royal Gala audience gave it twenty-one curtain calls. Ashton restricted it to them for decades; Sylvie Guillem was the first other Marguerite, in 2000. Fonteyn's Marguerite showed the new register Nureyev had unlocked in her: not the frail muse or the radiant princess but a courtesan who chooses, renounces and dies -- acted with an economy and intensity her 1930s roles had never asked for. Macaulay's eyewitness account from the Coliseum in 1977 gives the acting mechanism in one image: "after Armand has humiliated Marguerite by hurling banknotes at her and after her protector has offered to support her: Fonteyn's hands came fluttering into her waistline, her upper body subtly crumpled, she turned into profile, and her feet stuttered as they took her in anguish offstage." The collapse is distributed across the whole body, and the feet themselves act -- they stutter. Buckle's verdict on the work's dependence on its dancers is the honest limit: "its choreography is not important enough to make it worthwhile giving without these two powerful personalities". [C34030]

**Takeaway:** strip away the corps, the ensemble, the divertissements -- two bodies, six scenes, one continuous duet -- and the partnership itself is the whole ballet. [C34031]

## 20. Romeo and Juliet: Juliet at forty-five

Romeo and Juliet, premiered 9 February 1965 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Prokofiev, with Fonteyn as Juliet and Nureyev as Romeo, is Kenneth MacMillan's first full-evening ballet, and the casting was a deliberate provocation of age against role. MacMillan had made the ballet on Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable -- the balcony scene was first created for them for Canadian television in September 1964 -- but Royal Opera House manager David Webster, at the American impresario Sol Hurok's insistence, gave the premiere to Fonteyn and Nureyev, to MacMillan's disappointment. She was forty-five at the premiere, not forty-six: she turned forty-six three months later, on 18 May 1965, and Juliet is fourteen. The audience response was legendary: forty-three curtain calls, the safety curtain eventually lowered to get the audience to leave, and a year later the production was still drawing nightly queues. Wikipedia's main article says "Fonteyn's depth as an actor made the performance unique, making Juliet one of her most acclaimed roles". The part was not choreographed on her body, unlike the Ashton creations, so what she showed was interpretive: a mature woman's Juliet, played through acting depth, timing and the physical trust of the Nureyev partnership rather than through girlishness. It was filmed by Paul Czinner, cinema release 1966, and she went on dancing Juliet into the 1970s, the last listed full performances in January 1976. Macaulay's eyewitness account of that January 1976 performance is the mechanism in one image, and the honest counterweight in the same breath: after Nureyev sank to his knees to kiss the hem of her robe, "Fonteyn, standing in profile to the audience, looked down at him in complete wonder and amazement. And then she threw her arms up and looked past them to the sky. And then she brought them down, over her face, over her body, in a single wave, until they arrived at the sides of her body with palms open." Macaulay: "the whole Amphitheatre of Covent Garden gasped out loud." And in the same performance: "I was aware that Fonteyn hadn't seemed perfect... At times, she was forcing the energy. At times, she was visibly uncomfortable." The acting is a single continuous wave of the arms over the body -- no mime vocabulary, no gesture sequence -- and the reinvention was real but not a fairy tale: the late performances had visible strain, and what overwhelmed the audience was the dramatic moment, not technical perfection. [C34032]

**Takeaway:** a forty-five-year-old Juliet works when the acting does what youth once did -- one wave of the arms over the body, and an amphitheatre gasps. [C34033]

## 21. The late career: economy and the second flowering

The "she got better" claim is real and multiply attested, but it is a critical consensus in paraphrase, not a quotable line. Three forms, from three sources: EBSCO -- "Fonteyn went into a second flowering, this time as an even stronger and finer ballerina in her forties than she had been in the glorious days of her youth. It was an unprecedented 'second wind,' as most ballerinas retire in their forties"; ballet.co -- "Fonteyn -- far from being overshadowed by her young Tartar -- seemed rejuvenated: even her technique seemed to improve. Certainly her career was extended by at least 15 years"; Dance Magazine's review of Daneman's biography -- "Perhaps the crucial reason for this professional longevity, and certainly the impetus for her to improve technically after the age of 40, itself a virtual impossibility, was her superstar partnership with... Nureyev". The mechanism behind it, per EBSCO, is that Nureyev "showed Fonteyn a different way to work, strengthening her technical gifts and thus allowing her to bring a raw passion to her dancing that had not been seen before". What she changed technically is documented in three economies, not in a named programme -- no source read describes a specific late-career technical method, a changed class routine, or a deliberate reduction of jump height or turn count, and none is invented here. First, economy of dynamics, not of tempo: the one precisely documented technical adjustment in her career comes from Ashton coaching her Aurora at Covent Garden in 1946, when she was twenty-seven, and it is the mechanism she carried forward -- he "walked around the Covent Garden opera house and then once, when she held one pose a fraction longer, he immediately understood that this was what her dancing needed to register in the larger theatre: not by slowing down but by changing her dynamics so that each fixed position lasted a mini-moment longer". Hold each position a fraction longer; change the dynamics, not the speed. Second, placement and stillness over display: the Symphonic Variations stillness of section 9 and the low arabesque arm of section 16 are both economies, maximum effect from minimum movement, held longer, and Macaulay notes she "still made [the Les Sylphides moon-gaze] register in her late, late fifties" -- the upper-body mechanism survived when the legs could no longer do the same work. Third, repertoire narrowing: she went into semi-retirement in 1972, "relinquishing parts in full ballets and limiting herself to only a variety of one-act performances", and the November 1975 Broadway run, Fonteyn and Nureyev on Broadway at the Uris Theatre, "confirmed that Fonteyn was no longer able to execute more demanding roles". New work in the 1970s was chosen for dramatic weight rather than technical display: Martha Graham's Lucifer in 1975, Jose Limon's The Moor's Pavane as Desdemona in 1975 -- modern dance, not ballet, four dancers, Othello's tragedy in twenty minutes, at fifty-six -- and Cranko's Poeme de l'extase in 1972. And the late career was sustained by necessity as well as by artistry, which is worth knowing before romanticising the second flowering: after her husband Roberto Arias was shot on 8 June 1964 and left quadriplegic, "thoughts of retirement receded, as she needed to continue working to pay Arias' medical bills"; within two weeks of the shooting she was back in London, and "over the next ten days, Fonteyn danced in six performances of La Bayadere, Giselle, and Marguerite and Armand while rehearsing Nureyev's production of Raymonda". She danced MacMillan's Divertimento the night after the shooting, 9 June 1964, before flying home to Panama. Bentley's image for it: "she danced on and on as if she were wearing the Red Shoes". [C34034]

**Takeaway:** the second flowering is three economies -- hold each pose a fraction longer, let stillness do the work, narrow the repertoire to what the drama can carry -- and a bill that never stops coming. [C34035]

## 22. The Panama double life

She married Roberto "Tito" Arias in Paris on Sunday 6 February 1955, taking the formal name "Margot Fonteyn de Arias". Arias was a Panamanian international lawyer, diplomat and journalist, the son of former president Harmodio Arias; they had met in 1937 at Cambridge, when she fell for him watching him dance a rumba at a party, spent a week together, and then he returned to Panama and went silent. In January 1959 the couple visited Cuba, and by Fonteyn's own account to the British ambassador, Castro promised arms or men for a coup against President Ernesto de la Guardia. On the night of 24-26 April 1959 they sailed on their yacht The Nola to raise a buoy loaded with arms; fishermen reported them; Arias jumped ship to the shrimp boat Elaine while Fonteyn used the Nola as a decoy, then returned to Panama City and turned herself in. She threw overboard the white rebel armbands, thinking they were incriminating letters, while the letters and machine guns were buried and found; she confessed her involvement to Ambassador Sir Ian Henderson, and the Foreign Office kept her statement confidential. She was arrested, held twenty-four hours in a Panamanian jail -- the prison's "presidential suite", with fresh flowers -- and deported to New York on 22 April 1959; the dates in the sources wobble between 20 and 22 April for arrest and release, and 22 April is the Wikipedia narrative date. Henderson wrote that her conduct was "highly reprehensible and irresponsible" and "I do not regard her conduct as fitting in any British subject, let alone one who has been highly honoured by Her Majesty the Queen". Daneman's verdict, via Smithsonian: "a good girl who met a bad man." Arias took refuge in the Brazilian embassy and reached Lima the same day; they were reunited in Rio de Janeiro in June, and she was back on stage by November 1959. The second act lasted twenty-five years: on Monday 8 June 1964, while Fonteyn and Nureyev were performing in Bath, news came that Arias had been shot by the rival politician Alfredo Jimenez -- four shots, leaving him a quadriplegic for the rest of his life. The motive is described as a political rival, or, in Bentley's phrase, "said to be a cuckolded husband"; it is not settled, and is stated as disputed. She took Arias, in his wheelchair, on most of her travels thereafter, and kept dancing in large part to pay for his care -- Bentley: an afternoon of swimming cost 700 dollars. She retired to a small farmhouse near El Higo, Panama -- no telephone, a herd of four hundred cattle which she genuinely took to raising -- and wrote and presented the six-part BBC series The Magic of Dance in 1979, with a book the same year, and A Dancer's World in 1979 and Pavlova: Portrait of a Dancer in 1984. She served as Chancellor of Durham University from 1982 until her death, travelling annually for degree ceremonies. Arias died on Wednesday 22 November 1989, aged seventy-one. Shortly before his death Fonteyn was diagnosed with cancer; having spent her savings on twenty-five years of his care and retired without a pension, she sold her jewellery to pay for treatment, Nureyev anonymously helped with the bills, her stepdaughter Querube Arias took her to M. D. Anderson Hospital in Houston, and there were three operations by 1990. A May 1990 Covent Garden gala, at which Placido Domingo sang and Somes and Nureyev danced, raised 250,000 pounds for her support. She died on 21 February 1991, exactly twenty-nine years after Giselle with Nureyev. [C34036]

**Takeaway:** two irreconcilable stages -- Covent Garden and a cattle farm near El Higo with no telephone -- held in one life, and devotion, not ambition, as the load-bearing structure. [C34037]

## 23. The handover: The Dream she did not dance

The Dream, premiered 2 April 1964 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Mendelssohn arranged by John Lanchbery, had Titania created by Antoinette Sibley, with Anthony Dowell as Oberon, Keith Martin as Puck and Alexander Grant as Bottom. Fonteyn did not dance Titania, and does not appear in the performance list for The Dream at all. Ashton created the ballet for the younger generation -- Sibley and Dowell, whose partnership it launched -- in the Shakespeare quatercentenary year, at exactly the moment Fonteyn's career was being remade by Nureyev. This is the cleanest marker of the generational handover inside the company: the Ashton muse of 1935-58 was no longer the dancer Ashton built his new Shakespeare ballet on. Sibley had danced her first Aurora on 27 December 1961, praised as "for her generation what Fonteyn's had been to mine", and she inherited Fonteyn's great roles -- Aurora, Odette-Odile, Giselle, Chloe -- and on Fonteyn's death in 1991 was elected President of the Royal Academy of Dance directly in her place, serving until 2012. Ashton left the rights to The Dream and A Month in the Country to Dowell. Fonteyn's Shakespearean heroine of the period is MacMillan's Juliet, not Ashton's Titania. The arc closes where it opened: at her sixtieth-birthday gala in May 1979 she danced Salut d'amour with Ashton himself -- a gentle duet reworking the timorous-sister step he had danced in Cinderella -- and a Facade tango with Robert Helpmann, her partner of the 1930s, and at the end of the evening she was officially pronounced prima ballerina assoluta of the Royal Ballet. Ashton, Helpmann, and a small, intimate duet rather than a spectacle. [C34038]

**Takeaway:** the handover is a negative finding -- the ballet Ashton did not build on her, in the year her career was remade by someone else -- and the farewell is a small duet with the two men who were there at the start. [C34039]

## 24. Her own words

All of the following are quoted in a source read end to end; the attribution chain is given each time, and nothing here is paraphrase presented as quotation. "From that moment I danced strong, with every bit of energy I could muster." -- Autobiography (1975), via Macaulay. The context: Ashton told her in 1939 of Markova and Danilova vying in the same part at rehearsal; Danilova announced "I am Russian. I dance strong!" and attacked the solo. Fonteyn's takeaway, in her own words, was to dance strong -- the closest thing to a stated technical credo in the sources. "It was paradoxical, that the young boy everyone thought so wild and spontaneous in his dancing, cared desperately about technique, whereas I, the cool English ballerina, was so much more interested in the emotional aspect of the performance." -- Fonteyn on Nureyev, quoted in the EBSCO Marguerite and Armand study. "I don't think this is the moment for more coaching, Fred." -- to Ashton in Manhattan, the afternoon before the 1949 New York opening of The Sleeping Beauty, via Macaulay; Ashton, Macaulay notes, knew she "performed best when she was petrified by stage fright". "Take your terror and turn it into stage energy." -- coaching Maria Almeida on Aurora's entrance, via Macaulay; preceded by "That's fine. I was scared too", and followed, at the Rose Adagio balances, by "Maria, with shoes like these, why on earth are you nervous?" after inspecting Almeida's large pointe-shoe block. "But when you consider how many thousands of fouette turns I performed on that left foot, that's not so bad." -- on her cancer, which affected her left foot, to Paddy Foy in Panama, via Macaulay. "Well, Bob, it's true that it is sometimes tiring to answer the same question several times a day. And it is sometimes annoying how little they know about my career. But you know, Bob -- it's so much easier than dancing." -- to her publisher Robert Gottlieb during the 1975 Autobiography publicity tour, via Macaulay. Bentley's framing, for the portrait rather than the voice: she "gave Pavlova's sylph a woman's body and an English accent"; "Fonteyn offered physical symmetry as a moral force"; and "No less than Admiral Nelson, she personified the very best of British bravery... she remained the jewel of the empire when the empire was no more." [C34040]

**Takeaway:** dance strong, take the terror and turn it into stage energy -- and when the left foot that turned thousands of fouettes fails, count the turns and call it not so bad. [C34041]

## 25. Aesthetic logic: the line, the arrival, the stillness, the duet

The aesthetic logic of Fonteyn's dancing runs on four rules, each visible in more than one work. First, the line is continuous: one unbroken curve from toe to fingertip, the wrists, elbows and knees invisible, the front arm in arabesque low so the gaze travels past it into space, the back of the head aiming the audience's eye at a moon it cannot see. Second, arrival without haste: speed lives in the feet while the upper body stays calm, positions are held a fraction longer, and the dynamics change while the tempo does not -- singles, one per beat, each turn landing on the music. Third, stillness as an event: the held pose in Symphonic Variations, the single wave of the arms over the body in Romeo and Juliet, the crumple in Marguerite and Armand -- the loudest moments are the ones where the movement stops or slows to a single gesture. Fourth, the duet as a conversation: the man is never a passive porter and the woman never loses touch with him to play to the audience; they are aware of each other even with their backs turned, a message passes when their eyes meet, and she may initiate a new emphasis one night and he answers it instantly. [C34042]

What she never does is the thing a ballerina of her standing is supposed to do: she never throws in a double or a triple where a single turn will land on the beat; she never acts through mime as recitation, when a crumple of the torso and stuttering feet will do it; she never lets youth be the point of a role, playing Juliet at forty-five through depth rather than girlishness; and she never lets display outrun the line, holding the pose a fraction longer instead of adding a step. The cost of that is visible in the record -- the late performances where she was visibly forcing the energy, the Broadway run that confirmed she could no longer execute the demanding roles, the career that had to be paid for in bills rather than in triumphs -- and the cost is the same as the achievement: when the line holds, a simple pose can pierce the heart, and when it does not, there is nothing underneath to save it. She is the dancer of the single strong line carried by a score, a gaze and a partner, and the study of her is the study of how far a continuous line can be carried when the steps are not allowed to help -- and of what it costs when the body that carries it is also the body that has to pay the bills.

**Takeaway:** know what the instrument is -- a line, an arrival, a stillness and a duet -- and the failures are as legible as the successes: a fouette that travels is the same fact as a pose that pierces the heart, a turn landed on the beat. [C34043]
