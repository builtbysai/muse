## The life: the blur is the meaning, the system is the author

Gerhard Richter was born February 9, 1932, in Dresden, and grew up in Reichenau (now Bogatynia, Poland) and Waltersdorf, where his father Horst worked as a village teacher. His mother Hildegard Schonfelder gave birth to him at 25; his younger sister Gisela was born in 1936. Two brothers of his mother died as soldiers in the war, and his aunt Marianne, who had schizophrenia, was starved to death in a psychiatric clinic, a victim of the Nazi euthanasia program -- the family history that the 1965 portraits Uncle Rudi and Aunt Marianne would later paint, one photograph each, one perpetrator and one victim inside a single family. (C25156, leg 1 C25083; synthesis C25144)

Richter left school after 10th grade and apprenticed as an advertising and stage-set painter; in 1950 his application to the Dresden Academy of Fine Arts was rejected as "too bourgeois", and he finally began his studies there in 1951, with teachers Karl von Appen, Heinz Lohmar and Will Grohmann. The Dresden dates carry a variant: Britannica gives 1952 to 1956, one encyclopedia source gives admission in 1952 with training until 1957. Early in his career he prepared the wall painting Communion with Picasso (1955) for his Academy refectory and a second mural, Lebensfreude (Joy of life), at the German Hygiene Museum for his diploma; from 1957 to 1961 he worked as a master trainee and took commissions for the East German state, including murals, portraits and a panorama of Dresden. (C25157, leg 1 C25084, C25085; synthesis C25144)

Together with his wife Marianne, Richter escaped from East to West Germany two months before the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961. Both his wall paintings were then painted over for ideological reasons; much later, after German reunification, two "windows" of Joy of life (1956) were uncovered in the German Hygiene Museum, and a large portion of the mural was finally uncovered and restored in 2024. The escape is the hinge of the biography: everything after it is painted in the West, from photographs, in a language the East would not have allowed -- and the first thing he did with a photograph, in Table (1962), was overpaint and partly erase it. (C25158, leg 1 C25086; synthesis C25144)

In West Germany Richter began to study at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf under Karl Otto Gotz, together with Sigmar Polke, Werner Hilsing, HA Schult, Kuno Gonschior, Franz Erhard Walther, Konrad Lueg and Gotthard Graubner. Joseph Beuys taught at the same academy and was a major influence on the Dusseldorf cohort, but no source read this run calls Richter a Beuys student -- the correction that breaks this study's doorway, recorded honestly in D1(c) above. With Polke and Konrad Fischer (pseudonym Lueg), Richter introduced the term Kapitalistischer Realismus (Capitalistic Realism) as an anti-style, appropriating the pictorial shorthand of advertising -- a mocking mirror of Soviet Socialist Realism and a comment on the consumer-driven art doctrine of Western capitalism. He took part in the "Demonstrative Ausstellung", a store-front exhibition in Dusseldorf with Manfred Kuttner, Lueg and Polke, in May 1963; a second, related event, "Leben mit Pop", organised by Lueg and Richter at the Berges furniture store on October 11, 1963, may be the same event told two ways or two distinct events -- carried unresolved. His first gallery solo show was in 1964 at Galerie Schmela in Dusseldorf. (C25159, leg 1 C25087, C25088; synthesis C25144)

Richter created painting pictures from black-and-white photographs during the 1960s and early 1970s, basing them on newspapers and books, private snapshots, aerial views of towns and mountains, seascapes and a large multipart work for the German Pavilion at the 1972 Venice Biennale. For Forty-eight Portraits (1971-72) he chose mainly the faces of composers and writers; Atlas was first exhibited in 1972 with 315 parts and has continued to expand to approximately 4,000 photographs on approximately 600 panels. Landscapes became an independent work group from 1968, after a first vacation in Corsica; Dietmar Elger reads them in the context of traditional German Romantic painting and compares them with Caspar David Friedrich. In a 1988 series of 15 ambiguous photo paintings entitled 18 October 1977, he depicted four members of the Red Army Faction from black-and-white newspaper and police photos -- the arrest, the cells, the dead figures, the funeral -- first exhibited in Krefeld in 1989. (C25160, leg 1 C25090-C25092; synthesis C25144)

As early as 1966 Richter made paintings based on colour charts, using the charts as found objects; his experiments culminated in 1973-74 in large-format pictures such as 256 Colours, and between 1966 and 1974 he painted three series of Color Chart works. He began in 1966 with 10 Colors, and his friend Blinky Palermo randomly called out colours, which Richter then adopted -- chance playing its role in the first series. In the 1970s he shifted from the readymade to a conceptual system, developing mathematical procedures for mixing colours and employing chance operations for their placement; 4900 Colours (2007) consisted of bright monochrome squares randomly arranged in a grid. In 1969 he produced the first of a group of grey monochromes that consist exclusively of the textures resulting from different methods of paint application. In 1976 he first gave the title Abstract Painting to one of his works, saying he felt he was "letting a thing come, rather than creating it." From the mid-1980s he began to use a homemade squeegee to rub and scrape the paint he had applied in large bands across his canvases, and in the 1990s he began to run the squeegee up and down the canvas to produce vertical columns that take on the look of a wall of planks. (C25161, leg 1 C25093, C25094; synthesis C25144)

Richter began to use glass in his work in 1967, when he made Four Panes of Glass, plain sheets that could tilt away from the poles on which they were mounted at an angle that changed from one installation to the next. In 1970 he and Blinky Palermo jointly submitted designs for the sports facilities for the 1972 Olympic Games in Munich, proposing glass windows in twenty-seven different colours. In 1981, for a two-person show with Georg Baselitz in Dusseldorf, he produced the first of the monumental transparent mirrors that appear intermittently thereafter; at Documenta 9 in Kassel in 1992 he presented an ensemble of paintings and coloured mirrors in a special pavilion designed with architect Paul Robbrecht. Richter taught at the Hochschule fur bildende Kunste Hamburg and the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design as a visiting professor, then returned to the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf in 1971, where he worked as a professor for over 15 years; among his students were Thomas Schutte, Thomas Struth, Katrin Kneffel and his second wife, Isa Genzken. In 1983 he resettled from Dusseldorf to Cologne, where he still lives and works; in 1996 he moved into a studio designed by architect Thiess Marwede. (C25162, leg 1 C25095, C25096; synthesis C25144)

In 2002, the same year as his MoMA retrospective, Richter was asked to design a stained glass window in Cologne Cathedral; in August 2007 his window was unveiled -- a 113 square metre abstract collage of 11,500 pixel-like squares in 72 colours, randomly arranged by computer (with some symmetry), reminiscent of his 1974 painting 4096 colours. The artist waived any fee, and the costs of materials and mounting came to around 370,000 euros, covered by donations from more than 1,000 people. Cardinal Joachim Meisner did not attend the unveiling, preferring a figurative representation of 20th century Christian martyrs. In September 2020 Richter unveiled three 30-foot-tall stained-glass windows for Tholey Abbey, one of the oldest monasteries in Germany, and called them his last major work, adding that he would focus on drawings and sketches from then on. Earlier late work includes the Cage paintings (2006), six large works named after the composer John Cage; the November sheets (begun November 2008); the Birkenau cycle (2014), four paintings using the Sonderkommando photographs from Auschwitz-Birkenau, made permanently available to the International Auschwitz Committee in October 2021; and September (2005), one small painting about the planes crashing into the World Trade Center. (C25163, leg 1 C25098, C25099; synthesis C25144)

Richter's first retrospective took place at the Kunsthalle Bremen in 1976; a travelling retrospective at Dusseldorf's Kunsthalle in 1986 was followed by a Tate Gallery retrospective in 1991, the touring "Gerhard Richter: Malerei 1962-1993" in 1993, a first North American retrospective in 1998, a 40-year retrospective at MoMA in 2002, Gerhard Richter: Panorama at Tate Modern in 2011, and Gerhard Richter: Painting After All at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 2020, with Gerhard Richter at the Fondation Louis Vuitton, Paris, in 2025-2026. His awards include the Golden Lion for painting at the 47th Venice Biennale (1997), the Praemium Imperiale for painting (1997), the Wolf Prize (1994/5), the Wexner Prize (1998) and the State Prize of North Rhine-Westphalia (2000); for the last 18 years he has been number one on the Kunstkompass scale of most important world artists. He was at one time the most expensive living painter at auction: Abstraktes Bild (809-4) (1994) sold for $34.2 million in late 2012, Abstraktes Bild (599) (1986) for $44.52 million in February 2015, and Cathedral Square, Milan (1968) for $37.1 million in May 2013. He is widely regarded as one of the most important contemporary German artists and has been called the "greatest living painter" and the "Picasso of the 21st century". As of the latest sources read (Wikipedia crawled November 17, 2026, listing a 2025-2026 Fondation Louis Vuitton show and a 2025 David Zwirner show, and no obituary), he is alive and based in Cologne at 94; the "still lives and works today" line in older sources is not independently re-verified beyond that dated evidence. (C25164, leg 1 C25100-C25102; synthesis C25144)

## M1 -- Photo-painting blur: the dry brush over wet paint

Project a photograph (newspaper clipping, family snapshot, police photo) onto the canvas, copy it in oil as smoothly as possible, then drag a dry brush or soft squeegee across the wet surface so the contours smear. The blur is applied while the paint is still wet, in one pass; it cannot be undone. Works: W01 Table 1962, W02 Helen 1963, W03 Woman Descending the Staircase 1965, W04 Uncle Rudi 1965, W05 Aunt Marianne 1965, W06 Domplatz, Mailand 1968, W13 Betty 1988, W14 October 18, 1977 1988. (C25165, leg 2 M1, W01-W06, W13, W14; synthesis M1)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that refuses to hold still at any single distance. Close up it is smeared paint; at a distance it snaps back into a recognisable image, and the snap never quite holds -- the figure is there and not there at once, which is exactly what the photograph promised and could not deliver. In W04 and W05 the blur takes face, uniform and insignia together, so the man is present and faceless at once; in W14 the motifs are slurred, murky and repeated, so the cycle reads as slow-motion fragments, not a narrative. (C25166, leg 2 M1; synthesis M1)

**Takeaway:** the blur is the meaning, not a softening of it; a sharp copy of the photograph is a different picture. (C25167)

## M2 -- The grey monochrome: colour refused

Paint a whole canvas, or a whole series, in grey alone, the only variation coming from how the paint is applied -- brushed smooth, stippled, rolled, scraped, layered in translucent coats. The first grey monochromes date from 1969; grey returns as the ground of the late work (W14, W15, W19). Grey is Richter's non-statement: it refuses symbol, emotion and description at once, and the refusal is what makes the surface worth looking at. Works: W06 Domplatz, Mailand 1968, W08 48 Portraits 1971-72, W14 October 18, 1977 1988, W15 Mirror Painting (Grey) 1991. (C25168, leg 2 M2, W06, W08, W14, W15; synthesis M2)

The watchable behaviour is a surface that changes with light and angle, never with the subject, because there is no subject left to change. The grey is not one grey: it is a field of application marks, and the eye keeps finding texture where it expected nothing. A grey Richter next to a grey Polke is the whole contrast of the two men in one wall -- Polke's grey is a photograph losing its colour, Richter's grey is colour refusing to arrive. (C25169, leg 2 M2; synthesis M2)

**Takeaway:** grey is not emptiness, it is a decision; adding a colour accent to "warm it up" breaks it. (C25170)

## M3 -- Colour-chart system: mathematics plus chance

Mix colours by a fixed mathematical system (primaries plus grey, then green as a fourth primary; 4 x 4 = 16 x 4 = 64 x 4 = 256 x 4 = 1,024), then place them on the grid by a chance operation -- Palermo calling out colours in 1966, numbers on slips drawn from a box in 1973, dice in 2007. Assistants paint the squares; the artist's hand is absent from execution. Works: W09 1024 Colours 1973, W10 4096 Colours 1974, W17 4900 Colours 2007, W18 Cologne Cathedral window 2007. (C25171, leg 2 M3, W09, W10, W17, W18; synthesis M3)

The watchable behaviour is a field that looks ordered and feels arbitrary: the grid is rigorous, the arrangement random, and no square means anything by itself. At the density of 4096 Colours the white grid is dropped so the squares abut directly, and adjacent hues start to dissolve before the viewer's eye. The same system at cathedral scale stops being a painting and becomes light: 11,500 squares in 72 colours, and the window is the chart, grown up. (C25172, leg 2 M3; synthesis M3)

**Takeaway:** the system must be real and the chance must be real; a decorative rainbow grid chosen by taste is not this mechanism. (C25173)

## M4 -- Squeegee drag: build up, scrape off, read what remains

Apply paint in broad bands with a brush, then drag a homemade squeegee -- a long blade, sometimes two metres wide -- across the wet surface, pushing paint forward, scraping it off, exposing earlier layers. Repeat for days: add, drag, remove, respond. From the mid-1980s the squeegee is the principal tool; in the 1990s Richter runs it vertically to make plank-like columns. Works: W12 Abstract Painting (613-3) 1986, W16 Cage (1) 2006, W19 Birkenau 2014. (C25174, leg 2 M4, W12, W16, W19; synthesis M4)

The watchable behaviour is a palimpsest the viewer has to walk past: ruptured surfaces, lines where the squeegee paused, skins of dried paint rippled, colours never placed next to each other now colliding. No photograph captures it; the surface has to be walked past, because the depth is in the layers the scrape exposed, not in the top coat. The squeegee is the blur grown up: where the dry brush smeared a photograph, the blade cancels a painting -- same refusal, larger tool. (C25175, leg 2 M4; synthesis M4)

**Takeaway:** the painting is the residue of repeated cancellation; a single dramatic scrape on a fresh canvas is a gesture, not this system. (C25176)

## M5 -- Overpainted photographs: paint dragged across the print

Take a standard 10 x 15 cm colour print -- a holiday snap, a street, a family room -- and pull it through wet oil paint on a spreading knife (doctor blade), or place it on the paint-covered blade and lift it off like a monotype. The paint lands as amorphous islands millimetres thick, or as directional gradients, sometimes with a palette knife drawn across the running colour. Over 2,000 pieces since 1989, including the Firenze series of 2000 (small Florence views, 12 x 12 cm). Works: W20 September 2005 (the same move at canvas scale), W13 Betty 1988 (source method). (C25177, leg 2 M5, W13, W20; synthesis M5)

The watchable behaviour is two images fighting for one surface: the photograph still legible at the edges, the paint swallowing the middle. The photograph must stay partly visible -- burying it completely turns the work into an abstract, leaving it clean turns it into a print -- and the tension lives exactly in the middle, where the paint has won and the photograph is still arguing. (C25178, leg 2 M5; synthesis M5)

**Takeaway:** the photograph must stay partly visible; burying it completely turns the work into an abstract, leaving it clean turns it into a print. (C25179)

## M6 -- Glass and mirror: the painting that reflects instead of depicting

Use plain glass as the work: four panes on tilting stands (W07); grey mirrors with pigment on the back of the glass (W15); seven parallel panes refracting the room (Dia, 2002); eleven stacked panes (2004); five panes as House of Cards (2020). Nothing is painted on the front surface. Works: W07 Four Panes of Glass 1967, W15 Mirror Painting (Grey, 735-2) 1991. (C25180, leg 2 M6, W07, W15; synthesis M6)

The watchable behaviour is that the viewer becomes the image: the work shows the room, the light and whoever stands in front of it, changing with every step and every visitor. What changes over time is not the work but its content, which is whatever happens to be in front of it. A mirror painting is Richter's grey monochrome with the subject handed back to the room -- the refusal to depict, performed by the glass itself. (C25181, leg 2 M6; synthesis M6)

**Takeaway:** the mirror must be the picture, not a frame for one; adding a painted image on the glass turns it back into a painting. (C25182)

## M7 -- The grid as system: same size, same crop, many times

Take one format and repeat it without variation of size or treatment: 48 portraits each 70 x 55 cm (W08); 1,024 squares each the same size (W09); four Birkenau panels each 260 x 200 cm (W19); six Cage canvases near-identical in size (W16). The sameness is the point: it strips hierarchy from the set, so no single panel can claim to be the important one, and attention is forced onto the differences that remain -- a turned head, a red trace, a green field. Works: W08 48 Portraits 1971-72, W09 1024 Colours 1973, W16 Cage (1) 2006, W19 Birkenau 2014. (C25183, leg 2 M7, W08, W09, W16, W19; synthesis M7)

The watchable behaviour is a slow scan across equals, not a look at a centre. The viewer reads across the set, comparing, and the comparison is the experience -- which is why the 48 Portraits hang in a single row with gazes turning from the centre outward, and why the four Birkenau panels increase in colour panel by panel instead of all at once. (C25184, leg 2 M7; synthesis M7)

**Takeaway:** the grid must be strict enough to be boring; varying the sizes for composition reintroduces the hierarchy the grid was built to refuse. (C25185)

## M8 -- Scale shifts: the same image at postcard and mural size

Paint the same kind of image at wildly different sizes and let the size change what it is: a table at 90 x 113 cm (W01) against a cathedral square at 275 x 290 cm (W06); a candle at 120 x 100 cm (W11) against a Cage canvas at 290 x 290 cm (W16); a 10 x 15 cm overpainted snapshot against a 260 x 200 cm Birkenau panel (W19); a colour chart at 299 x 299 cm (W09) against the same system as a 20-metre cathedral window (W18). Works: W01, W06, W09, W11, W16, W18, W19. (C25186, leg 2 M8, W01, W06, W09, W11, W16, W18, W19; synthesis M8)

The watchable behaviour is a change of relation, not of size: small works are intimate and held close; large ones fill peripheral vision and become architecture. The viewer does not just see a bigger picture, they stand in a different relation to it -- and the cathedral window is the proof, because the same chart that hangs as a painting at 299 cm becomes light itself at 20 metres. (C25187, leg 2 M8; synthesis M8)

**Takeaway:** scale is a material, not a format choice; shrinking a mural composition to a postcard does not preserve the work. (C25188)

## M9 -- The blur as erasure: painting over the painting

Paint a figurative image, then paint over it until it is gone: Table (W01), overpainted and erased with newsprint in 1962; the October cycle's Blanket (Decke), a white-painted canvas hiding one of the Meinhof corpse paintings underneath; Birkenau (W19), where the figurative transfer is buried under layer after layer until nothing of the photograph remains visible; September (W20), where the overpaint mimics the smoke it hides. Works: W01 Table 1962, W14 October 18, 1977 1988, W19 Birkenau 2014, W20 September 2005. (C25189, leg 2 M9, W01, W14, W19, W20; synthesis M9)

The watchable behaviour is a surface that knows something is underneath it. The erasure is not a correction of a mistake, it is the work's second act, and the finished surface is the record of what was removed. The viewer senses something underneath without being able to name it, which is exactly the feeling the work is about -- the photograph was evidence, and the painting is the evidence being withdrawn. (C25190, leg 2 M9; synthesis M9)

**Takeaway:** the erased image must have been really painted first; starting from a blank abstract and calling it erasure is a shortcut, not the mechanism. (C25191)

## M10 -- Repetition and series logic: the same motif, painted again

Paint the same motif several times and let the repetitions disagree: three paintings of the corpse of Ulrike Meinhof from one photograph (Dead 1-3, in W14); two Bettys, 1977 and 1988 (W13); the Candle and Skull series, 32 works in 1982-83 (W11); four Birkenau panels from four photographs, colour increasing panel by panel (W19); six Cage variations on one theme (W16). Works: W11 Two Candles 1982, W13 Betty 1988, W14 October 18, 1977 1988, W16 Cage (1) 2006, W19 Birkenau 2014. (C25192, leg 2 M10, W11, W13, W14, W16, W19; synthesis M10)

The watchable behaviour is a slow-motion sequence: the same image returning, slightly different each time, until repetition itself becomes the subject. The series is not a set of copies but a comparison machine -- the viewer reads across the set, and the comparison is the experience. Richter's series are the photo-painting's answer to the colour chart's grid: same source, different result, the difference doing all the work. (C25193, leg 2 M10; synthesis M10)

**Takeaway:** the repetitions must be near-identical in source and different in result; a set of merely similar pictures is a group show, not a series. (C25194)

## The aesthetic logic: the picture is smarter than the painter

Richter's whole career is one refusal performed fifty ways: refuse to choose between figuration and abstraction, between the photograph and the painting, between system and chance, between the personal and the impersonal -- and let the refusal itself do the work. The blur refuses the photograph's claim to truth without denying that the photograph shows something real. The grey refuses colour's symbolism without becoming empty. The colour chart refuses taste by handing the arrangement to mathematics and dice. The squeegee refuses composition by letting the tool decide what survives. The mirror refuses the image altogether and hands the picture back to the room. In each case the artist sets up a procedure, steps back, and lets the procedure finish the painting -- which is why Richter can say, and mean, that the painting is smarter than he is. The intelligence is not in his hand, it is in the system he built and then obeyed. (C25195, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25145)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the procedure is the author: the blur, the chart, the squeegee, the mirror and the series each do work the artist could not do alone, and the artist's job is to set the conditions and then get out of the way -- which is why Richter's work is the opposite of Polke's chemistry even though the two men are co-founders and fellow Gotz students: Polke sets a material loose and watches it change, Richter sets a procedure and obeys what it returns. Second, distance is a dial: every major mechanism only works when the viewer moves -- close for the smear, back for the figure, sideways for the mirror, tomorrow for the erasure -- so the picture is never finished in one viewing position. Third, the refusal is always deadpan: the grey canvas, the blurred corpse, the chart of random colours are all presented without a wink, and the absence of the wink is what makes them serious and what makes them bearable at the same time. (C25196, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25145)

The evolution is the proof. 1962-75: the photo-painting is the whole project -- small grey paintings from found photographs (W01-W06), the banal object and the family photograph treated with the same deadpan, the blur invented as a way of painting a photograph without believing it, the colour charts (W09, W10) and the grey monochromes arriving as the systematic counterweight. 1976-2020s: the abstract paintings take over in scale and in number, the squeegee replacing the dry brush as the erasing tool (W12, W16), while the photo-paintings never stop -- they return as candles (W11), as the Baader-Meinhof cycle (W14), as Betty (W13), as September (W20). The late work pushes both poles further apart and closer together at once: the abstracts get bigger and more purely procedural, the figurative subjects get more unpaintable, and the answer in both cases is the same -- paint over it, scrape it off, let the procedure carry what the hand cannot. The Tholey Abbey windows of 2020 close the circle: the colour chart as church window, the system as light. (C25197, leg 2 evolution; leg 1 C25093, C25094, C25098, C25099; synthesis C25145)
