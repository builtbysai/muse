## The life: the suburbs, then the stage

Eric Fischl was born March 9, 1948, in New York City, and grew up on suburban Long Island; his family moved to Phoenix, Arizona, in 1967. Skarstedt describes that upbringing as a backdrop of alcoholism and a culture obsessed with image over content, and the description is not decoration: it is the subject of the early paintings. He studied two years at Phoenix College, then Arizona State University, then the California Institute of the Arts in Valencia, where he received his BFA in 1972. (C24008, leg 1 C23800-C23802; synthesis C23959)

The CalArts point carries the correction this study exists to record. John Baldessari was a founding CalArts faculty member and the dominant conceptual presence at the school during Fischl's time there -- but Fischl never took a class with him. His own words to ARTnews: "I never took a class with him, but many of my friends took his class and would talk about it. He was a very influential figure who introduced humor as a creative tool." Baldessari himself, in Hertz, is explicit: "I never worked with Ross Bleckner nor with Eric Fischl, who were both painting with Allan Hacklin and Paul Brach." Fischl was in the painting division; Bloom, Brauntuch, Goldstein, Mullican, Salle and Welling were the students who worked with Baldessari and, in Baldessari's own distinction, "didn't paint." The split is the story of Fischl's CalArts years: in a school that was absolutely anti-painting, Fischl, Bleckner and Salle were the painters, and they have been friends ever since. (C24009, leg 1 C23803; leg 3 C23902, C23911-C23912; synthesis C23975)

After graduating in 1972 he moved to Chicago and worked as a guard at the Museum of Contemporary Art. From 1974 to 1978 he taught painting at the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design in Halifax, where he met his future wife, the painter April Gornik. His first solo show, curated by Bruce W. Ferguson, was at Dalhousie Art Gallery in Halifax in 1975; he moved back to New York in 1978, and his first New York solo show was at Edward Thorp Gallery in 1979, when suburbia was not considered a legitimate genre for art. The Halifax years are the transition from abstraction to realist narrative painting. (C24010, leg 1 C23804-C23808; synthesis C23959)

The breakthrough is the suburban paintings, 1979-1986: large, human-scale figures in the pool, the unmade bed, the barbecue, the boat deck, caught in private, often sexualised moments, with the viewer put in the position of an intruder. Sleepwalker (1979), Bad Boy (1981), Birthday Boy (1983), Barbecue (1982), The Old Man's Boat and the Old Man's Dog (1982), Dog Days (1983). In 1986 the Whitney showed 28 of his paintings in a retrospective; John Russell in the New York Times compared Fischl's tactic to Degas -- setting up a charged situation and leaving the viewer to figure it out -- while noting that his society has "an unstructured brutality and a violence never far from release." (C24011, leg 1 C23809-C23812; leg 2 W01-W09; synthesis C23959)

In the late 1980s he turned to sculpture, small bronzes first, then larger and monumental pieces: the Arthur Ashe memorial (2000) in Queens, and Tumbling Woman (2002), commemorating the bodies that fell from the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001, unveiled at Rockefeller Center on the first anniversary and covered up and removed within days after public outcry. His sculptures were first shown in a one-man exhibition at Gagosian, New York, in 1998 (search summary only). The mid-1980s onward also widened the picture: fragmented multi-panel paintings, diptychs that set privilege against desperation on the same beach, staged photographic fictions in a modernist house. (C24012, leg 1 C23811, C23814-C23815; leg 2 W09-W14, W18; synthesis C23959)

From 2002 the method changes and the subject does not. For the Krefeld Project he refurbished Haus Esters in Krefeld, Germany, a 1928 Mies van der Rohe house, hired models who for several days pretended to be a couple living there, took 2,000 photographs, reworked them digitally, and painted from them. He continued to paint from photographs, often manipulating them in Photoshop to create disorienting or ambiguous scenes: Beach Paintings (2006-10), Art Fair (2013-16), Complications from an Already Unfulfilled Life (2018-19), My Old Neighborhood (2021). (C24013, leg 1 C23816-C23817; leg 2 W15, M2; synthesis C23959)

The civic turn runs alongside. He founded America: Now and Here, a multi-disciplinary exhibition of 150 artists designed to spark a national conversation about American identity; it launched May 5, 2011, in Kansas City and travelled in six 18-wheeler trucks coast to coast. With Gornik he led fundraising to renovate the Sag Harbor Cinema, burned December 2016, and an abandoned Methodist church into an artist residency and exhibition space called The Church; both venues opened in 2021. He moved to Sag Harbor in 2000, where they share a home and matching studios. His memoir, Bad Boy: My Life On and Off the Canvas, was published in 2013. He received Guild Hall's Lifetime Achievement Award in 1994, American Academy of Arts and Letters membership in 2006, and CalArts' honorary Doctor of Arts in 2013. He is represented by Skarstedt Gallery, New York; his earlier New York champion was Mary Boone Gallery. On May 10, 2022, The Old Man's Boat and the Old Man's Dog sold at Christie's New York for $4,140,000, against an estimate of $2,000,000-3,000,000, a then record for the artist. Eric Fischl: Stories Told ran at Phoenix Art Museum November 7, 2025-June 14, 2026 (closing date variant carried), bringing together 40 large-scale works from the late 1970s to today. (C24014, leg 1 C23822-C23829; synthesis C23959)

The evolution is the proof. 1979-83: suburban interior and backyard, the intruder-viewer, glassine layering. 1984-96: large scale widened to diptychs, staged fictions, beach and boat crowds. 2004-23: Krefeld staging, family portraiture, a 9/11 sculpture, storm beach, post-2016 pool paintings, a 2023 self-portrait coda with Tumbling Woman visible inside the studio. The subject stays the same -- the body, exposed, in a comfortable setting that will not stay comfortable -- while the method moves from glassine to photography to Photoshop to staged live actors, and the scale stays large throughout. (C24015, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C23960)


## M1 -- Layered glassine testing

Fischl paints figures and objects on separate sheets of glassine, a translucent paper, then layers the sheets to test relationships between elements and to incubate tension or suspense. For Sleepwalker (W01, 1979, oil on canvas, 69 x 105 in.) the study was built in three layers: two empty pool chairs on the first sheet, the pool on the next, the boy on the third. The method survives as a preparatory technique and, Apollo notes, later as cut-out oil figures pinned to a painted background (pinholes visible) and as Photoshop compositing. (C24016, leg 2 M1, W01; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a scene that assembles itself in visible layers. Start with furniture and room only, hold, then fade in the pool or water plane, hold, then drop in the figure last. Let the viewer -- or an automatic timer -- nudge one layer a few pixels and watch the tension change: chairs perpendicular versus parallel, figure centred versus off-centre. The "wrong" arrangement should read as flat; the "right" one should read as charged. Show the seams: a brief ghost of the layer edges, or pinhole marks, so the construction stays visible. (C24017, leg 2 M1; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** build the scene in visible transparent layers -- furniture, then water, then figure -- and let one nudge of a layer change the charge. (C24018)


## M2 -- Photographic capture, then digital rearrangement

From the mid-1980s Fischl works increasingly from photographs, not memory, and for large oils he experiments in Photoshop, moving figures in and out of a room, enlarging, shrinking and redirecting their gaze before painting. Photoshop studies of Late America (2016) show the central boy and later his Stars and Stripes towel introduced while other figures and objects were cut. For Krefeld (W15, 2004) he staged live actors and took 2,000 photographs. For the art-fair series he photographs real fairgoers, but the paintings are stereotypes, not portraits. (C24019, leg 2 M2, W15, W19; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a loop that visibly re-casts itself. Figures slide in from off-frame, scale up and down, swap gaze targets, and are deleted mid-scene while the room stays fixed. Run it as a slow "casting" pass before the scene settles: the same bedroom with three different figure counts, then a freeze on one. The viewer should feel the painting deciding, not just displaying. (C24020, leg 2 M2; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** let the scene re-cast itself in front of the viewer -- figures slide in, scale, swap gazes, get deleted -- before it settles. (C24021)


## M3 -- The suspended, off-balance body

Fischl's figures are always "off balance... starting to droop, starting to sit up, starting to turn," in his own finding from photographs, reported by Apollo. American Hula (2020, not numbered) is a woman in athletic contrapposto, balance affirmed by the hoop's horizontal, precarious because equilibrium in motion usually is. Tumbling Woman (W18, Ten Breaths: Tumbling Woman II, 2007-2008, bronze, 58.4 x 66.0 x 124.5 cm, SAAM 2013.86) is the extreme form: a body with no equilibrium at all, lateral motion, not in control. (C24022, leg 2 M3, W18; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is never a held pose; always a body mid-transition. A figure leans, droops, starts to sit, starts to turn, and the loop holds it just before the resolution -- then eases back, never completing the move. A hula hoop, or any ring, holds a clean horizontal while the body tilts against it, so the eye can measure the imbalance. A falling figure should tumble laterally, not drop straight down. (C24023, leg 2 M3; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** hold every body mid-transition -- leaning, drooping, starting to turn -- and never let the move complete. (C24024)


## M4 -- The back-turned figure and the averted gaze

Apollo names "the figure with its back turned, gaze and intention unknowable" as a core strategy. In Bad Boy (W03, 1981) the boy has his back to us, watching a nude woman sprawled on an unmade bed, his hand slipping into her purse; in American Hula the woman has her back to the viewer. The viewer cannot read intention from the face, so the body and the situation must carry it. The one deliberate exception is W05, The Old Man's Boat and the Old Man's Dog (1982), where the nude man leans back, takes a swig from a can, and meets the viewer's gaze. (C24025, leg 2 M4, W03, W05; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a back turned for most of the loop. The figure's head turns a few degrees, never enough to show the face; its shoulders, not its eyes, signal attention. Other figures look at each other or at an object, never at the viewer -- except for the deliberate exception, used sparingly, as a shock, not a default. (C24026, leg 2 M4; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** keep one figure back-turned for the whole loop -- shoulders signal attention, the face never does -- and spend the direct gaze once, as a shock. (C24027)


## M5 -- Fractured picture: diptych, multi-panel, and the caesura

Fischl splits the image. A Visit To / A Visit From / The Island (W09, 1983, oil on canvas, 84 x 168 in., Whitney 83.17a-b) is two adjoining canvases with opposite scenes in the same setting: a white family vacationing at a sunny resort on the left, Black men and women pulling themselves from a churning sea on the right, based on a photograph of Haitian refugees arriving on the Florida coast. Costa del Sol (W12, 1986, oil on linen, two panels, 262.3 x 397.5 cm, MoMA 353.1997.a-b), Dog Days (W06, 1983, diptych, 84 x 168 in.) and Salad Days (W10, 1984, oil on linen, two panels, 84 x 168 in., SAM 2014.25.18) are the same move. Bayonne (W11, 1985) uses overlapping canvases and a caesura, a metrical break in verse, in Fischl's own term: viewed straight on it is one piece; viewed from an angle the break appears. In the mid-1980s he made paintings on multiple canvases, some cantilevered from the wall, denying the integrity usually expected of a figurative scene. (C24028, leg 2 M5, W06, W09, W10, W11, W12; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a seam that does the meaning. Build the scene as two or three panels with a visible seam, each panel on its own slow drift: different light, different crowd density, different weather. Left panel leisure, right panel distress, same beach. Or let the viewer's angle -- pointer position, device tilt -- open and close the caesura: head-on the panels align into one image; off-angle they split and the gap shows. (C24029, leg 2 M5; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** split the picture and let the seam do the meaning -- head-on one image, off-angle two, and the gap is the subject. (C24030)


## M6 -- The leisure stage: pool, beach, bedroom as a set with staffage

Skarstedt says of Late America that "the backyard swimming pool serves as a stage set for a variety of personal dramas." Apollo says the same of the recent pool and luxury-residence paintings, where a pair of faceless gardeners act like staffage in a 17th-century landscape, arranged symmetrically as if they have become part of the scenery. TheArtStory makes the same point for the island and beach paintings: wealthy families at leisure, with the dynamics of that stratum as the real subject. The bedroom works the same way: a set (bed, chair, sitter) with figures placed in it. (C24031, leg 2 M6, W19; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a fixed set with a varying cast. One pool, one beach, one bedroom; the furniture, water and light stay put while figures enter, leave, change costume (nudity, rumpled evening wear) and change grouping. Keep one or two background figures deliberately faceless and symmetrical, moving like gardeners -- slow, repetitive, scenery-like -- while the foreground figures carry the drama. The contrast between busy foreground and indifferent background is the point. (C24032, leg 2 M6; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** fix the set and vary only the cast -- the pool stays, the people change, and the background figures move like scenery. (C24033)


## M7 -- Light as moral weather

Apollo lists "the strong contrast of light and shade" and "the sharp crop" among Fischl's strategies. TheArtStory describes Bad Boy as "slatted with gauzy light" through a blind, and Whitehot describes Best Western (W08, 1983, oil on canvas, 108 x 78 in.) as darkness illuminated by pool lights, with colour ceding to darkness as an active part of the composition. In American Hula and Like Explaining the End of the World to a Dog (2020), dark blue skies press on leisure; in Scenes from Late Paradise: Stupidity (W17, 2007, oil on linen, 84 x 108 in.) the sky churns and the sea smashes while the figure does not notice. Whitehot names Caravaggio-like light-and-dark with cinematic composition as a Fischl inheritance. (C24034, leg 2 M7, W03, W08, W17; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is light driven before figures. A blind's slats sweep slowly across a bed; pool lights come up as daylight fails; a dark sky lowers over a bright beach over the course of a loop. Skin should catch the light (sweaty highlights, low post-coital glow, per Whitehot) while the background retreats into shadow. The light change should arrive before any figure reacts to it -- the weather knows first. (C24035, leg 2 M7; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** drive the light, not just the figures -- the slats sweep, the sky lowers, and the weather knows first. (C24036)


## M8 -- The loaded object and the animal witness

Apollo names "the foregrounded objects, here the loaded fruit bowl, which may or may not be symbolic" in Bad Boy, and TheArtStory reads the bananas and the purse as Freudian puns. The dog is the recurring animal witness: Fischl is quoted in Apollo as joking that "any painting that doesn't have a dog in it is probably half a painting." Dogs appear in Dog Days (two mutts returning a woman's gaze), The Old Man's Boat and the Old Man's Dog (a lively Dalmatian on deck), and Like Explaining the End of the World to a Dog (a dog undaunted by a vacant scene). In Barbecue the two fish in a bowl face the grill. (C24037, leg 2 M8, W03, W04, W05, W06; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is the object and the animal holding the eye at intervals, not the people. The dog watches the viewer, or watches a figure, and its head tracks the action while the humans ignore each other. The foreground object (bowl, bag, bottle, fishbowl) should be lit like a still life inside the narrative scene -- a small, still, bright thing that may mean something and may mean nothing. Do not explain it. (C24038, leg 2 M8; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** put one lit object in the foreground and one dog watching -- let them hold the eye while the humans ignore each other, and explain nothing. (C24039)


## M9 -- Ambiguity as the finish line

Apollo reports Fischl's own test: paintings are finished, paradoxically, when they convince him of "the irresolvable mysteries of their content," when the viewer thinks "what the fuck is going on here? Who are these people and what do they want?" He is quoted: "ambiguity is the essential quality of art." The Krefeld paintings eschew sequential narrative while playing on how far we rely on it, per Apollo. Skarstedt says of Late America that "the cause and effect of the depicted tumult is always off canvas." TheArtStory says of Bayonne that all interpretations are valid, though only a few are consciously intended. Sleepwalker (W01) holds the same test in one figure: masturbation, sleepwalking, or urinating -- the painting refuses to say. (C24040, leg 2 M9, W01, W11, W19; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a refused payoff. The loop must not resolve into a story with a beginning and an end. Two figures approach each other and stop; a gesture starts and is interrupted; a look is exchanged and neither face is shown. Offer two incompatible readings at once (sleepwalking or urinating; theft or complicity; leisure or disaster) and hold both for the full loop. If a viewer can summarise the piece in one sentence, the mechanism has failed. (C24041, leg 2 M9; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** refuse the payoff -- hold two incompatible readings for the whole loop, and if the piece summarises in one sentence it failed. (C24042)


## M10 -- Return and self-review

Skarstedt notes that Daddy's Gone, Girl (2016) is the first time Fischl returns to a character from an earlier work: the girl from Daddy's Girl (1984), now a young woman alone, in funereal garb. The large scale is constant: Phoenix calls his works operatic in scale and ambition, and most numbered works here are 65 in. or more on the long side. The arc closes on self-portraiture: Self-Portrait: An Unfinished Work (2011), Study for Portrait of the Artist as an Old Man (1985), and Broken Hallelujah (W20, 2023, acrylic on linen, 68 x 96 in.), where the artist reviews his life with Tumbling Woman standing inside the studio behind him, his hands bloodied and wrapped in cotton. (C24043, leg 2 M10, W20; synthesis C23961)

The watchable behaviour is a loop that returns. Bring back a figure from an earlier scene in the same piece, visibly older, alone where she was held, in different clothes, looking into middle distance. Or run the same pool scene twice at different scales: first small and private, then large and operatic, with the same gesture. End a longer sequence on a studio interior: the maker slumped over a bench, the earlier works visible as small objects in the room, the loop looking back at itself. The return should feel like a reckoning, not a repeat. (C24044, leg 2 M10; synthesis C23961)

**Takeaway:** bring the figure back visibly older and alone -- or run the same pool scene twice at different scales -- and end on the studio looking back at itself. (C24045)


## The aesthetic logic: the comfortable setting that will not stay comfortable

Fischl's career is one scene staged fifty ways: a comfortable American setting -- pool, beach, bedroom, barbecue, boat deck -- with a body exposed inside it and the cause and effect left off the canvas. He never explains the scene. He sets it up, lights it like a stage, splits it into panels or layers, and leaves the viewer to figure it out, the way John Russell said Degas did -- except Fischl's society has "an unstructured brutality and a violence never far from release," and the viewer is put in the position of an intruder who cannot look away. (C24046, leg 1 C23811-C23812; leg 2 M1-M10; synthesis C23959-C23961)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, stage, do not narrate: the Krefeld actors, the Photoshop re-casting, the glassine layers are all ways of building a scene without telling a story, and the painting is finished when it cannot be resolved. Second, split the picture: diptych, caesura, cantilevered panels, translucent layers -- the fracture is where the meaning lives, because the two halves will not agree with each other. Third, let the light and the weather know first: slatted blind light, pool lights in darkness, a churning sky over a beachgoer who does not notice -- the setting turns before the figures do, and the figures are always a beat behind their own scene. Baldessari replaced the hand with a rule and the picture with a photograph; his school's painters -- Fischl, Bleckner, Salle -- kept the hand and the body, and made the painting a room you walk into as an intruder. (C24047, leg 2 M1, M2, M5, M7, M9; synthesis C23961-C23963)

The evolution is the proof. 1979-83: the intruder-viewer, glassine layering, the backyard at night. 1984-96: the split widens -- diptychs of privilege against desperation, staged fictions in a modernist house, beach and boat crowds. 2004-23: the staging becomes the method itself -- 2,000 photographs of a pretend couple, Photoshop re-casting, the art fair as a scene where the artworks watch the people. 2016-23: the pool becomes a political stage, and the arc closes on the artist reviewing his own life with his most controversial sculpture standing in the studio behind him. What stays constant is the question the viewer is left holding: what is going on here, who are these people, and what do they want -- asked by a painting that refuses to answer. (C24048, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C23960)
