# Carlos Silva: Study Notes

**Site:** Carlos Silva, born Buenos Aires 13 November 1930, died Buenos Aires 12 June 1987, aged 56 -- Argentine painter, writer, graphic and textile designer, the counter of the Buenos Aires geometric circle, whose displaced points -- rows of dots shifted by counted steps, expanding from a central point toward infinity by arithmetic progression -- made a flat square swell, recede and seem to move, and whose judge at the 1965 Di Tella prize, Giulio Carlo Argan, is reported to have said he anticipated Vasarely by a year with them (C9401, C9402, C9502). He came to painting through letters, not art school: three months in the workshop of Vicente Puch, then the 1949 Cubist show at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, then Kandinsky, Mondrian and Tomas Maldonado, then the grid (C9401, C9402, C9406). He painted flat on a table, never on an easel, and said that is why his pictures show no gravity -- no up, no down, no horizon (C9402). He was the same age as Ary Brizzi, born in the same city in the same year, and the two shared four rooms across sixty years, 1961 to 2021 (C9504, C9505, C9509, C9511). The work that matters is the dot that obeys a rule: two million of them, by his own count, marching outward from a centre until the picture breathes (C9451).

## The life: the writer who counted his dots

Carlos Silva was born in Buenos Aires on 13 November 1930 (C9401, C9406). Letters came first: his first artistic expressions were through writing, and he kept the writer's profile all his life -- essays and fiction, a reflective intellectual's stance toward his own painting, Brizzi's memoir says (C9401, C9406). His family environment is described on his official site as free of prejudice and attachments, encouraging his curiosity; no parent, sibling, spouse or child is named anywhere in the sources read (C9401). He spent three months in the workshop of Vicente Puch -- classmates Carlos Alonso, Jose Fernandez Muro and Sarah Grillo (spelled Grilo in the 1991 catalogue) -- studying live models, and left "brutally decided", in his own words, to set aside any academic instruction (C9401, C9402, C9406). Arte-Online, quoting Cristina Rossi, spells the teacher "Vicente Puig"; this study follows "Puch", the official site and the 1991 catalogue (C9412).

The turn came in 1949, at the Cubist exhibition at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, where he discovered Braque and Picasso (C9402, C9406). His first Cubist work was a copy of Picasso's "Portrait of Gertrude Stein", fascinated by the fracturing of each plane; he worked on paper, mixing oil with binders for different textures (C9402). He began reading Kandinsky, Mondrian and Tomas Maldonado, and through Romero Brest's circle, Max Bill (C9402, C9406). The 1950-1954 years have no record at all; the first dated shows are the Van Riel "Non-figurative Art" exhibitions of 1955, 1956, 1957 and 1958, and "Arte No-Figurativo" at the Museo Municipal E. Sivori in 1956 (C9403). He showed at the First Pan-American Show in Porto Alegre and at Punta del Este in 1959, and in the Museo de Arte Moderno's "Rio de la Plata Paintings" and First International Exhibition of Modern Art in 1960 (C9403).

1961 is the hinge year: his first solo exhibition at Galeria Witcomb, and on 8 May 1961 the three-man show Brizzi-Santiso-Silva, where Brizzi's memoir says the friendship began (C9403, C9406). The venue of the 8 May show is not named in the passage read; Silva's own CV lists Witcomb the same year, which is consistent but not proof (C9510, C9511). Santiso's first name was not found anywhere. In July 1963 came "Ocho artistas constructivos" at the MNBA, convened by director Jorge Romero Brest: Brizzi, Espinosa, Lozza, MacEntyre, Martorell, Sabelli, Silva, Vidal -- the anchor room of the whole network, and the occasion of Silva's lecture at the MNBA explaining the mathematical foundation of his art (C9406, C9413, C9414). In 1964 the four-man show "Brizzi, MacEntyre, Silva, Vidal. Pinturas" at Galeria Bonino, and "Argentine New Art" at the Walker Art Center, Minneapolis (C9403, C9414). In 1965 the 8th Sao Paulo Biennial and the Di Tella prize -- the contested win, carried in full below (C9403, C9408, C9409).

The rest of the decade is a steady institutional climb: "Mas alla de la Geometria" at the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella in 1967, with the Premio Internacional Di Tella in Caracas the same year; two first prizes at the I Salon Hisisa de Arte Aplicado a la Industria Textil, 1967; the Gran Premio Adquisicion of the Fundacion Lorenzutti, 1968; solo shows at El Taller (1967, 1968), Estudio Actual in Caracas (1969), Carmen Waugh (1970), Rubbers (1971), Arte Nuevo (1974, 1977, 1979, 1980), Knoll International in New York (1975), Ottawa (1976), Chapultepec in Chicago and Banco Comercial del Norte in Tucuman (1977) (C9403, C9406, C9407). In 1973 "Proyeccion y Dinamica" at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1 March - 31 May, with Brizzi, Espinosa, MacEntyre, Ocampo and Vidal -- the date is 1973 in every source read, and the 1972 date carried in the #636 brief is wrong (C9403, C9405, C9406, C9509). Brizzi's presence in the Paris show, which leg 1 could not confirm from Silva's sources, is confirmed by the Paris Musees record, which lists all six painters (C9509). In the harsh winter of 1977 Silva and Brizzi shared a small apartment in central Manhattan for some months, visiting the museums of New York and Washington together (C9406).

The 1980s are thinner in the record. A lifetime retrospective at the Museo de Arte Moderno, Buenos Aires, in 1981 (C9403, C9405, C9406). The Premio Konex in 1982 -- year attested by three sources, category unverified; do not write Pintura Geometrica until the Konex record is opened (C9401, C9404, C9406). The Premio Cristobal Colon in Madrid in 1984 -- attested as a prize in 1984, but its form is unclear, listed as a group exhibition on the artist's own site (C9403, C9404, C9405, C9406). A solo show of paintings, watercolours and acrylics at the Biblioteca Nacional, Caracas, 1984 (C9406). In the early 1980s he co-founded the Grupo de la Abstraccion Sensible with Raul Mason, pushing a return to figuration and artisanal work against conceptual art -- his only documented group co-founding, and a late turn away from the geometry that made him (C9520). The last dated work in the 1991 catalogue list is "Surgente", 1986, acrylic on canvas, 90 x 90 cm (C9406). He died in Buenos Aires on 12 June 1987, after an illness against which he fought, painting even while in hospital; no source names the illness, and no obituary was opened this run (C9401, C9406). Posthumous shows include the Fundacion San Telmo retrospective, 17 April - 24 May 1991, curated by Yina Ruiz Moreno de Bunge, and "Brizzi - Lozza - Silva" at Alejandro Faggioni Estudio de Arte, 22 September - 12 November 2021, billed as "Tres grandes maestros de la abstraccion constructiva" (C9406, C9411). His work is held by the MNBA, MAMBA, Fondo Nacional de las Artes and the Di Tella collection (FADLA, C9404), and -- the one holding verified independently -- by MoMA, "Deyber" (1968), gouache and pencil on paper, object 844.1969 (C9410). No verified holding at MALBA or Museo Moderno was established this run. No teaching post and no named student were found.

**Takeaway:** the biography is the argument for the method: a writer by formation who reached painting through Cubism and mathematics, and who spent forty years counting the same small mark outward from a centre. He never joined a group with a manifesto -- not Generative Art, not Grupo 13, not Arte Nuevo -- and that is the point: the grid was his group.

## M1 -- The displaced-point grid: counted steps from a centre to infinity

Rows of dots shifted by counted steps, expanding from a central point toward infinity by arithmetic progression. Silva's own account: he composed "an arithmetic progression made up of a rule of three on the square root; through mathematics a grid appeared on the paper that served as a structure for different lines of points", the grids "expanding from a central point toward infinity" (C9402). The device itself is the subject: the grid is not a scaffold under the picture, it IS the picture. Attested in the earliest viewed work, W02 "Sin titulo" (1963): a nearly square field packed edge to edge with tiny dots in strict rows, pale blue-green ground, dots mostly green and blue with a few larger red, yellow, white, black and turquoise accents, faint rectangular outlines buried under the dots (C9453). The method in prose, from the Cecilia de Torres text on the 1972 tempera (W13): "A grid lightly drawn in pencil was altered and painted with black and bright color dots to create within the paper sheet an illusion of depth and undulating motion" (C9461). The watchable behaviour: a flat field that swells, recedes and seems to move as the eye travels it -- the movement belongs to the eye, not to the paint.

**Takeaway:** the rule is the picture. A piece that scatters dots decoratively has the look and none of the mechanism; the dots must be counted, in rows, from a centre, by a rule the viewer can almost read.

## M2 -- Dot size as depth: the tunnel without perspective

Dot size follows distance from a vanishing point, so a flat square reads as a tunnel, or the centre erodes to white. Seen in W04 "Agoo" (1965): a lime-green square of pale dots growing larger as they move away from a bright vanishing point left of centre and shrinking to pinpricks as they approach it, diagonal bands of denser dots crossing the field, no frame, no second motif, one green, one pale tone, one rule (C9453). The study author viewed W04 directly: the tunnel reads immediately, the dots swelling outward from the bright point exactly as leg 2 describes, and the picture is 180 x 180 cm, a wall-filling effect (C9551). Seen again in W06 "Evohe" (1965): a pale yellow-ochre square whose centre has been eaten away into a soft white cloud, roughly diamond-shaped, with small dashes in red, blue, green and yellow spraying outward like sparks -- depth by erosion rather than by construction (C9453). And in W05 "Tinnio" (January 1965): a dense red field of tiny vertical dashes where a pale cross sits right of centre, emerging only at a distance and dissolving up close -- depth from tone, not from dot size (C9453, C9460). The rule is strict: the size of the mark is a function of its distance from the centre, and the eye falls into the picture and is pushed back out.

**Takeaway:** depth in Silva is a count, not a drawing. A piece that adds perspective lines or shading to make depth has left his system; change the size rule and the depth changes by itself.

## M3 -- No gravity: the table, not the easel

He worked each picture horizontally on a table instead of on an easel, and said that is why his things have no gravity, neither top nor bottom; the position also kept the paint from dripping (C9402). The tunnels and funnels of W04 and W07 point in every direction at once; the late scatter of W19 "Hidra" (1983) has no orientation at all; the final work W20 "Up and Down" (1987) is a loose column of dashes on white paper that reads the same turned either way (C9451, C9453, C9455). The title of W20 says the movement, up and down, and the picture does not need to prove it with a tunnel any more. The watchable behaviour: the picture can be turned and still reads; orientation is a viewer choice, not a given. This is the mechanism that most separates Silva from Brizzi: Brizzi's fan has a pivot and a direction, a searchlight that sweeps; Silva's field has a centre and no direction at all.

**Takeaway:** no up, no down. A piece that gives Silva's grid a horizon, a floor, or a preferred orientation has imported a gravity he deliberately removed.

## M4 -- Shaped supports: the edge joins the system

The rectangle is abandoned where the system needs it. W07 "Eutropos" (1966): a square turned 45 degrees so it hangs as a diamond, a broad mustard-yellow border forming an X across the work, dividing it into four kite-shaped panels, each a funnel of red dots on a different ground -- turquoise at the top, pink at the left, blue at the right, green at the bottom -- the dots shrinking toward the centre of the whole, so four tunnels meet at one point (C9453). The study author viewed W07 directly: the diamond, the yellow X, the four coloured funnels are exactly as leg 2 describes, and the yellow X reads as both frame and structure (C9552). Later: W14 "Clairevaux" (1973), the first round work, 90 cm diameter, acrylic on wood, listing-only (C9457); W15 "Alma" (1973), a round tondo, 90 cm diameter (C9454); W16 "Hipona" (1974), an equilateral triangle, point up, 120 x 120 cm, its dots forming nested triangular frames shrinking toward a small pale centre (C9454); W11 "Kamploops, April 1969", a faint disc of tiny pale dots in concentric rings inside a painted box of black bands and mustard walls (C9459); W19 "Hidra" (1983), a round tondo 130 cm diameter (C9455). The watchable behaviour: the edge of the picture joins the system instead of cutting it -- the support is the first decision, not the last.

**Takeaway:** the shape is part of the rule. A piece that puts Silva's dots on a default rectangle has cut the system off at the frame; let the support be a diamond, a triangle, a disc, and the grid must be fitted inside it.

## M5 -- Colour rationing and zone separation: one ground, sparse accents

Colour is rationed. In W02 the ground is pale blue-green and the dots mostly green and blue, with a few larger dots in red, yellow, white, black and turquoise acting as sparse accents (C9453). Or colour is used to separate zones: the four coloured funnels of W07; the green bands against dot strips in W10 "Cauce" (1968), flat bright green vertical bands divided by thin hard lines in red, black and blue, the dot field pushed to the margins where it still does the optical work (C9453); the red-white-green-blue stripes against orange dot panels in W12 "Scores" (1969), a wide horizontal canvas split into zones like a musical score, the dots behaving like notes -- same mark, different size, in sequence (C9453). Cristina Rossi, quoted by Arte-Online, summarises the arc: the first compositions gave priority to simple forms, later circles and points spread over a reticular weave, and in the 1960s colour took primacy and the formal and chromatic relations gave greater mobility to the plane, producing vibration and flicker (C9412). The watchable behaviour: colour marks territory inside the grid, and the eye reads zones before it reads dots.

**Takeaway:** one ground, one ration. A piece that floods Silva's field with many colours has lost the discipline; the colour must be countable too -- a ground, an accent, a zone.

## M6 -- Hard edge against the dot field: stillness makes the motion

A flat, hard-edge element is placed against the displaced-point field, and the stillness of the flat shape makes the dot field look as if it is moving. Seen in W08 "Diacono de Tredos" (1967), the largest work in the catalogue at 250 x 200 cm: a stack of flat vertical stripes in blue, black, red, yellow and white at the left edge, against a field of gold-ochre dots in rows swelling toward a vanishing point right of centre -- though the saved image looks cropped at the left, so the stripe column is seen in this reproduction, not verified as a full-canvas fact (C9453, C9465). Seen in W10 "Cauce" (1968): the green bands are the flat element, the dot strips at the edges are the moving one (C9453). Seen in W11 "Kamploops" (1969): the painted box of black bands and mustard walls holds a faint dotted disc that only appears on looking closer (C9459). The mechanism is a contrast: the hard edge does not move, the dots do not move, and the eye, caught between the two, supplies the motion.

**Takeaway:** stillness and motion in one frame. A piece with only dots has half the mechanism; it needs the flat thing that does not move, so the field can seem to.

## M7 -- Airbrush haze as depth-maker: the late weather

From the 1970s, tonal haze replaces dot size as the depth-maker. The biography says Silva used the airbrush to make shapes spin and turn diffuse across the paper (C9402). Seen in W15 "Alma" (1973): a round tondo, cream and ivory at the rim, darkening through a soft smoky grey-black halo toward the middle, and at the very centre a tight disc of small red dots in concentric rings -- the black halo looks airbrushed, with no hard edge, and the sprayed tonal gradient makes the centre sink before the dots even start (C9454). Seen in W17 "Sin titulo" (1977), tempera and ink on paper, 38 x 57 cm: diagonal rows of large black oval dots shrinking steadily toward a vanishing point right of centre, soft brown-rust clouds, airbrushed, sitting behind the dots at left and right (C9462, C9454). The watchable behaviour: depth becomes weather -- a glow or cloud the dots sit inside, rather than a tunnel they march down. The medium shift is attested alongside it: in the last decade he used acrylic and pastel colours more often (C9402).

**Takeaway:** the haze is a second depth rule, not decoration. A piece that sprays a gradient over a dot grid without letting the haze do the sinking has added fog to a tunnel; the haze must replace the size rule, not sit on top of it.

## M8 -- Dissolution: the system lets go

The grid unwinds in public. The dots get bigger, fewer and more legible (W17, 1977), then stop marching altogether: W18 "Herisson" (1979), a red ground crossed by broad diagonal bands of orange and yellow light, blue dots in loose rows crossing the red, dark curved shapes cutting across the bands -- the dot grid still there, but no longer the whole picture (C9454). Then W19 "Hidra" (1983): a round tondo filled edge to edge with a dense all-over scatter of tiny short strokes and dots in red, blue, yellow, green and black on a cream ground, no grid, no centre, no vanishing point (C9455). Then W20 "Up and Down" (1987): a vertical sheet of white paper, mostly empty, a loose column of short dashes and strokes in red, blue and grey running up the centre, denser at the bottom, thinning and scattering toward the top, hand-written notes in the bottom corners (C9455). The study author viewed W20 directly: the looseness is exactly as leg 2 describes, a handful of strokes where the 1960s canvases held two million dots, and it reads as a work of his final year -- he died in June 1987 (C9553, C9463). The watchable behaviour: the same small mark, first obeying a grid, then a vanishing point, then nothing at all -- the mechanism unwinding in public.

**Takeaway:** the end of the system is part of the system. A piece that only builds the strict 1960s grid has shown Silva at his most disciplined and missed the arc; the dissolution is the second half of the story, and a piece that lets the grid go, slowly, over its own runtime, has told it.

## Evolution: system, architecture, weather, dissolution

Early (1963-1965, W02-W06): the dot grid IS the painting. Small marks, dense all-over fields, muted or single-colour grounds -- pale blue-green, warm beige, lime green, red, yellow-ochre. The system is already there in 1963 (W02), but the optical payoff is still quiet: faint rectangles under dots, a small cluster of shapes in a large field (W03 "Inicios", 25 October 1963, a tall narrow panel, 160 x 70 cm). By 1965 the rule hardens: dot size follows distance from a vanishing point and the flat square becomes a tunnel (W04), or a pale shape is cut out of a dense red field (W05), or the centre is eroded to white (W06). Medium is oil, on board or pressed wood; scale grows fast, from 105 cm (W02) to 180 cm (W04) (C9453).

Middle (1966-1969, W07-W12): the system goes public and architectural. Supports are shaped and rotated (the diamond of W07); colour separates zones (four coloured funnels in W07, green bands against dot strips in W10, stripes against dot panels in W12); a flat hard-edge element is placed against the dot field (the stripe column in W08, the green bands in W10). The largest work, W08 at 250 x 200 cm, sits here, and the most framed work, W11, puts the dot disc inside a painted box. Paper enters as a serious support with the MoMA gouache W09 "Deyber" (1968), 47 x 47.6 cm, the smallest dated work in the catalogue (C9458). The method is described in prose as a pencil grid painted over with black and bright-colour dots (W13, 1972) (C9461).

Late (1973-1987, W14-W20): the grid loosens and the airbrush arrives. Round and triangular supports dominate (W14, W15, W16, W19); tonal haze replaces dot size as the depth-maker (the smoky halo of W15, the rust clouds of W17); the dots get bigger, fewer and more legible (W17), then stop marching (W18's diagonal bands, W19's all-over scatter). Medium shifts from oil to acrylic, and in the last decade to acrylic and pastel colours on paper and wood (C9402, C9451). The final work, W20 (1987), drops the grid entirely: a few dozen loose dashes on white paper. The arc is from system to suggestion: the 1960s prove the rule, the 1970s bend it around shaped supports and sprayed tone, the 1980s let it dissolve.

What does NOT change across 24 years: the dot never leaves. Even the loosest late works are built from the same small mark; what changes is whether the mark obeys a grid, a vanishing point, or nothing at all. And there is never an up or a down: painted on a table, the tunnels and funnels point in every direction at once.

## The network: the circle, the eight, the judge

The anchor is Brizzi. Four shared rooms, 1961-2021, all evidenced: 8 May 1961, Brizzi-Santiso-Silva at Galeria Witcomb (venue consistent with Silva's CV, not proven from the passage read); July 1963, "Ocho artistas constructivos" at the MNBA; 1 March - 31 May 1973, "Projection et dynamisme" at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris; 22 September - 12 November 2021, "Brizzi - Lozza - Silva" at Alejandro Faggioni Estudio de Arte (C9511, C9505, C9509, C9504). The two men met toward the end of the 1950s and the start of the 1960s, and in the harsh winter of 1977 shared a small Manhattan apartment, visiting the museums of New York and Washington together (C9406). No personal correspondence, friendship statement beyond Brizzi's memoir, or studio visit was found; the link is exhibition-based and circle-based, and it is dense: four shared rooms over sixty years is the strongest exhibition link in Silva's file.

MacEntyre: the contrast seam. Co-founder of Generative Art with Vidal in 1959; Silva's nearest generational neighbour, but Silva was NOT a member of the Generative Art group -- no source found names him as one, and the Zurbaran catalogue note on the group names MacEntyre, Brizzi and Vidal, not Silva (C9506, C9523). Shared rooms 1963 (the eight), 1965 (both invited to the Di Tella prize), 1973 (Paris six) (C9505, C9513, C9509). The mechanisms contrast exactly: MacEntyre draws lines by hand from a generating point, following random algorithms; Silva counts points on a ruled grid from a centre (C9502, C9523). Two Buenos Aires answers to the same question -- one by hand-drawn line, one by counted point.

Vidal: the other half of Generative Art's founding pair; shared rooms 1963 and 1973 with Silva; no founding role for Silva, no presence at the 1960 first Generative Art exhibition at Galeria Peuser -- do not claim either (C9523). Vidal as principal of the Pueyrredon school (1989-1991) with Brizzi teaching beside him is a Vidal-Brizzi link, not a Silva link; Silva is not attested at that school (C9523).

Paternosto: the strongest unstudied adjacent, and the #638 pick. Same Buenos Aires geometric generation, same city circuit, opposite doctrine -- "geometria sensible" and the oblique view against Silva's ruled arithmetic. No joint exhibition was found, and none is claimed; the link is generational and doctrinal (C9516, C9517, C9518).

Martorell: the only woman among the eight of 1963, and Silva's fellow exhibitor in the two most institutional rooms of the decade (MNBA 1963, Di Tella 1967, "Mas alla de la Geometria") (C9507, C9508). She shared more than 14 exhibitions with Brizzi, the first in July 1963 -- a Martorell-Brizzi fact, not a Silva fact, and the reason she stays visible in this network even though she cannot be picked: the MAR prompt prefix is taken by Gustave Marchoul (#611) (C9507). Not picked for #638.

Polesello (#633): the younger Op Art star, fellow invitee in the 1965 Di Tella prize room and fellow exhibitor at the 1965 Sao Paulo Biennial; the kinetic cousin by mechanism -- both make a flat surface appear to move, Polesello by colour and material, Silva by counted displacement (C9513, C9512, C9514). ## Aesthetic logic

Silva's aesthetic is the logic of a counter, not a painter. Pick one mark -- a dot -- and one rule -- it grows or shrinks by counted steps from a centre -- and let the accumulation do the work. The beauty is not in any single dot; no dot in a Silva is interesting on its own. It is in the field the dots produce together, the way a thousand identical marks become a tunnel, a funnel, a cloud, a weather. That is why the table matters even though no photograph of it survives: the pictures were made flat, with no gravity, and the rule was computed, not felt -- a rule of three on the square root, Poincare's geometry reduced to essence, a grid that appeared on the paper and then served as the structure (C9402). It is also why the late work, which lets the grid go entirely, is still Silva: the rule was never the grid, it was the counting, and when the mark stopped obeying the grid it was still the same mark, counted down to a handful. The square format changes -- diamond, triangle, disc -- but the mark never changes, the palette never exceeds one ground, one ration and one accent in the mature work, and the flat hard edge never changes. Restraint is not a limitation here; it is the proof. A Silva that is busy, curved, asymmetrical or multicoloured has not added to the system; it has left it.

The contrast with Brizzi is the study's spine, and it is a contrast inside one circle, between two men born in the same city in the same year. Brizzi's generator is a plane rotated around a pivot, his wedges fanning until a beam of light seems to sweep; Silva's generator is a grid of points expanding from a centre, his dots swelling until a tunnel seems to open. Brizzi's light comes from a ramp, a scale of values along the fan; Silva's depth comes from a count, a size rule along the rows. Brizzi drew like a designer laying out a stand; Silva counted like a writer working out a progression. The circle held both, because the question was the same -- how does a flat painting move -- and the two men gave the two opposite answers: one by rotation, one by counting. That is why the two studies belong together, and why Silva is the peer, not the follower: same year, same city, same rooms, opposite mechanism.
