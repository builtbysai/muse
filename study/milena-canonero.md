## M1. Start with the head

Kubrick's first lesson to her, and the one she never unlearned: cinema begins in the close-up, so build the character outward from the head. "Milena, you start with the head", per Metrograph, and the head is where the costume is decided, because the head is what the camera sees first and remembers longest. The evidence is a roll call of heads: the bowler hat and the single false eyelash on one eye that make Alex in A Clockwork Orange, with the bloody-eyeball cufflinks as the same joke told twice, once on the face and once on the wrist; the red wool beanie that makes Zissou in The Life Aquatic, an homage to Jacques Cousteau's red watch cap, which became a hipster uniform worldwide; the tricorn hats that carry Lady Lyndon in Barry Lyndon, one of which Canonero fashioned in ten minutes from cardboard and fabric while the young Joanna Johnston watched, per Nadoolman Landis's Hollywood Costume; the finger-wave that carries Diane Lane in The Cotton Club, and the finger-wave Canonero wanted for Madonna in Dick Tracy and was refused, "I would have preferred the hair less curly with finger waves instead, and have it coming over the eyes... But Warren thought that was too sophisticated"; the white ribbon sash and Peter Pan collar that carry the Grady twins in The Shining, where the matching heads are the whole horror. The head department is in the room from day one because the method demands it: make-up artist Barbara Daly and hairdresser Leonard worked with her as a trio from her first film, and "start with the head" only works because the head is a collaboration, see N14. Against this, the failure mode is the film where the head is left to the actor, and Canonero complained about exactly that on The Cotton Club: "In some films, especially in America, you may have some very good actor or actress who decides to do period films, but they keep their own look... It just upsets the whole thing."

**Takeaway:** the head is the costume's anchor, and change the hat and the character changes, and the piece should design the head first and let the body follow it.

## M2. The painting as pattern book

For period work she does not start from other films or from academic costume history; she starts from paintings of the time. Barry Lyndon is the cleanest statement, in her own words via the Sunday Times of 3 May 2009, quoted by Frock Flicks: "Stanley prepared the look of Barry Lyndon through selections of painters of the time. I did not go for an academic look, nor the realistic look that could have come by looking at Hogarth's work... Stanley [wanted] a romantic and elegant view of the period. So we went for other inspirational painters." The painters are Gainsborough and Reynolds for the portraits, Chardin and Hogarth for the life, and Hogarth is present as a warning, not a model: the realistic look was available and refused. The rule never changes, only the painter does. For The Godfather Part III the pattern book is Titian, Caravaggio and Rembrandt, "a palette of jewel tones: deep scarlets, midnight black velvets, iridescent ambers", per Metrograph. For Marie Antoinette it is Fragonard, Ducreux and Charpentier, plus New Romantic visuals and Galliano's Dior. For The Grand Budapest Hotel it is the photographers Hurrell and Man Ray and the painters van Dongen, Klimt, Grosz and Lempicka. This is the Tosi method inherited, and the Guardian obituary of Tosi states the reason: he "researched through art... since an artist showed both the architecture of clothes and how they were worn". Pescucci's version is the same rule, see #429 M1, but Canonero's version adds the refusal: the painting is chosen against the academic alternative, and the choice of painter is the choice of film.

**Takeaway:** a painting gives cut AND carriage at once, and a costume book gives only cut, and the piece should paint its references first and sew second.

## M3. The genuine article and the made copy

Where period clothes survive, buy the real thing and copy from it; where they do not, make it, but the real thing is the standard. Barry Lyndon: she and Soderlund examined original 18th-century attire at London's Victoria and Albert Museum and copied patterns from the collection, and sourced vintage fabrics, laces and clothing from auction houses and private collections, and combined crafted film pieces with original garments. With Umberto Tirelli she "scoured Europe to buy genuine 18th-century garments at auctions, dissecting them to understand the cut, the shoulder lines, and the soul of the fabric", per The Ambassador, and the verb matters: dissecting, not admiring. Out of Africa states the rule as a sentence, from the New York Times 1986 profile via Frock Flicks: "Once I've done my research, I go into what each character should wear. For a period movie where period clothes exist, you try to combine real things with things you design and make. The real thing, you can never make it as good as that." The standard is not the picture of the garment but the garment itself, and the copy is judged against the original in the hand, not against the painting on the wall. The same rule runs in the other direction on Titus, where nothing could be rented or hired, so everything was made from scratch in a Cinecitta workshop of 15 to 20 costume makers, metalworkers and plasterers, including the weapons and armor, and the film still came in under budget: when there is no genuine article, the workshop becomes the genuine article.

**Takeaway:** authenticity is a material fact before it is a look, and the piece should put one real object in the room and copy from it, not from a picture of it.

## M4. The patina of time

New cloth must be made to look lived in, and the ageing is a process with named operations, not a filter. Barry Lyndon: she and Soderlund "dyed and boiled fabrics to give them a lovingly worn quality that did away with the artificial stuffiness inherent to the period drama genre", per Metrograph. The Ambassador adds the hand operations: new velvets and silks treated "with sandpaper and dyes to make them look lived-in", reproducing the "patina of time". Compare Pescucci's breakdown, see #429 M2: weave, dye, sew, break, four operations in order, and the same Italian school, the same rule that age is a timeline, not a filter. A filter is applied once and looks the same everywhere; a process leaves a history, and the history is different on the shoulders, where the sun hit, than at the hem, where the mud did. The enemy is not inaccuracy but newness: a costume that looks new looks like a costume, and a costume that looks worn looks like clothes, and the actor stops wearing a costume and starts wearing clothes. The failure mode is the period drama where everything is crisp, and the audience reads the crispness as a rental.

**Takeaway:** age the cloth in successive passes, each pass visible, and the order of the passes is part of the design.

## M5. Costume must read in the light

Barry Lyndon's candlelight forced the design: much of the film was shot in low, flickering candlelight, Kubrick's natural-light decision, so "the costumes had to stand out noticeably both in shape and texture", per Wikipedia. The constraint is the design: in candlelight, a subtle costume disappears, so the shape must be big and the texture must catch what little light there is. The same logic inverted on Marie Antoinette, shot in bright white light, where "every small, bejeweled or fragrant floral detail" can overwhelm the senses, per Metrograph, so the design must survive being fully lit, every bead and flower visible at once. Ozpetek's summary of her school, in the Eye For Film interview, is the mechanism stated as a boast: "maniacs about detail, maniacs about colour, maniacs about light". Light is a costume material, not a condition the costume endures: the designer must know what light the film will actually use, and design for that light, and a costume designed for daylight will fail in candlelight, and a costume designed for candlelight will shout in daylight. The Kubrick training is visible here too: he taught her to use a light meter, and had her assist production designer John Barry on location scouts through London for months before she conceived the wardrobe for A Clockwork Orange, so she learned to read a room's light before she designed for it.

**Takeaway:** light is a costume material, and design for the light the film will actually use, or the costume will be invisible or shouting.

## M6. Colour as a closed system

A film gets a fixed palette and the designer never leaves it. Dick Tracy states the mechanism most explicitly anywhere in this leg, per the Berlinale homage page: "For it Canonero drew on classic cuts from 1930s fashion, while referencing the original comic strip by choosing clear bright colours for the fabrics of the costumes. She limited herself to the three primary colours, plus black and white, as well as five mixed colours, and used them consistently throughout the film for all the costumes and accessories." Three primaries, black, white, five mixes: thirteen colours, and nothing else, for a whole film. Titus runs the same discipline on a different palette: "The colors had to be very basic... Blacks, whites, grays, and reds, with touches of blue and brown for the Goths", per Live Design, and the palette is worked out with the director and the production designer before a sketch is coloured, see M11. The Grand Budapest Hotel: mauve and purple for the hotel, gray and black for the military, red, yellow, pink, violet and gold overall, and felt as the most used fabric, because felt holds those five hues flat and matte. Marie Antoinette: the macaron box, pistachio and powder blue and pink, hot pinks, baby blues, bright peaches, and the palette arrives as a gift, Coppola presenting Canonero with "a big box of delicious Laduree macarons", partly as a gift, partly to inspire the colour palette. The closed palette turns costume into a code the audience can read without thinking: the colour tells you who the character is, which tribe they belong to, and what is about to happen to them, and a colour that appears outside its system is a plot event.

**Takeaway:** pick the palette first, close it, and never leave it, and the audience will read the code without being told it exists.

## M7. The uniform as character shell

Clothes that mark rank, tribe and protection, and the uniform is a character arc you can put on and take off. The Grand Budapest Hotel is the uniform film: the hotel staff in dense mauve and deep-purple felt instead of the subdued greys typical of hospitality uniforms, and the felt "intended to give the most discerning and attentive spectators the impression that something dreadful was on the way", and The Ambassador reads the uniform as psychology, "a shell that protects the character's dignity". The badges are the plot: Zero's uniform is "more directly inspired by military costume, specifically a drummer boy's hat with 'Lobby Boy' stitched on the front, and shiny gold buttons", and the Society of the Crossed Keys badge is the one thing separating Gustave and Zero, and Zero earns his own badges only when he becomes concierge, and Gustave gives the golden keys pendant to Agatha before Zero has earned it, and retired Zero keeps the pendant as a keepsake of Agatha though he has removed his own lapel badges, per Clothes on Film. The Life Aquatic runs the same mechanism on a crew: the whole team in matching baby blue with red beanies, a team made visible, and the beanie is the one warm note in a cold sea. A Clockwork Orange runs it as a gang and a class insult: the Droogs' white cricket whites perverted with a codpiece worn outside the trousers, the respectable uniform of the ruling class turned into a threat by wearing its protection on the outside. The uniform protects the wearer and marks the wearer at the same time, and the protection and the marking are the same garment.

**Takeaway:** a uniform is a character arc you can put on and take off, and the badge is the smallest plot device in the film.

## M8. The archive that eats itself

Costumes are reused, restyled and recycled across films and decades, and the reuse is part of the method, not a budget accident. The chain is documented: Lady Lyndon's hats from Barry Lyndon re-worn in Marie Antoinette, with Rose Byrne as Polignac wearing costumes first worn by Marisa Berenson as Lady Lyndon; several costumes from The Affair of the Necklace restyled and recycled for Marie Antoinette, a hot pink jacket among them; one Necklace gown recycled from earlier stock in Blackadder II; one Necklace jacket probably originally from Jefferson in Paris of 1995, itself based on an extant 1790s embroidered jacket in the Kyoto Costume Institute, per the Frock Flicks revisit. Her own archive of garments, books and materials is a working tool, per Metrograph, and the archive is not a museum: it is a parts bin, and a costume's second life is also design. The mechanism has a discipline inside it: the reuse is never a straight repeat, it is a restyling, and the restyling is where the new film's design lives, because the old garment arrives with its own history and the new film must answer it. The Affair of the Necklace states the standard the reuse must meet: "The cut and the silhouette has to be 100% accurate; I never compromise on that", and the recycled gown is acceptable only because its cut was right in the first place.

**Takeaway:** a costume has a life after its film, and the second life is also design, and the archive is a parts bin, not a museum.

## M9. The deliberate anachronism

Accuracy is a choice, not a rule, and breaking it on purpose is the point. Marie Antoinette is the cleanest case: one brief frame in the "I Want Candy" montage shows a pair of Converse high-tops among the Manolo Blahnik pumps, and Roman Coppola, second unit director and Sofia's brother, says in the Vogue oral history that it was his "in-the-moment idea of, Is this crazy?" on set, and the shoes belonged to Kirsten Dunst's stand-in, and it was left in on purpose because "these are young people", and the four sources disagree on the shade, lavender-tinted, baby-blue, light blue, pale blue, see C16, which is itself the proof that the anachronism is read as an idea, not as a shoe. Canonero's own statement of the rule, to Footwear News, 9 October 2006: "Sofia [Coppola] didn't want the film to have the expected look of the period. This is not a classic vision of Marie Antoinette, but Sofia's personal vision of her. The film is a very modern look at her inner experience and therefore, the costumes had to respect that kind of language. We took the essence of how things were and stylized them. It's more of a fashion statement. At times, it was very rock and roll." Asteroid City restates the licence as a standing rule: "Anachronism is definitely allowed in Wes's movies", per the Little White Lies interview. The Godfather Part III runs the same mechanism without a joke in it: the story takes place in 1979, and Canonero chose fabrics like silk taffeta, velvet and leather that lean closer to the fashion of the late 1980s, which "imbues the film with an anachronistic quality that elevates it... into the operatic register... that Coppola so desired", per Metrograph. The Louis Vuitton AW20 runway states it as a slogan: "Anachronism becomes an attitude", per Ghesquiere's show notes. The failure mode is the anachronism that is not deliberate, which is an error, and the test is whether the break says what the period cannot: the Converse says these are young people, and no 18th-century shoe could say it.

**Takeaway:** an anachronism works when it says what the period cannot, and the break must be legible as a deliberate break, or it is an error.

## M10. The research steep

"I like to steep myself in an era", her phrase, via Metrograph, and the steep is the method: research is not reference-gathering, it is living in the era until the clothes suggest themselves. A Clockwork Orange: months on location scouts with a Nikon and a light meter, hundreds of photos of Londoners, skinheads on the streets of London as the seed, and she told Nadoolman Landis, "I went out taking hundreds of photos all over London. I was inspired, for the film costumes, by the Skinheads on the streets of London. I designed all the costumes to create a surreal imagery." Barry Lyndon: museums and libraries all over Europe, original garments at the V and A, auctions and private collections. Out of Africa: three months, strict term, to research, design and produce hundreds of costumes, and the journey runs from the New York Public Library to museums and costume houses across England and Italy, to Blixen's home in Denmark, to Africa, where she met the anthropologist Richard Leakey, who consulted on less known aspects of African fashion in the 1910s. The Cotton Club: research the whole history of Harlem before designing a stitch, so that Coppola could "point the camera in any direction" and find the period correct. The steep has a visible output: the designer who has steeped can answer any question the camera asks, including the ones from the edge of the frame, and the crowd is dressed to the same standard as the leads, see M20.

**Takeaway:** research is not reference-gathering, it is living in the era until the clothes suggest themselves, and the steep is what makes the frame safe to point anywhere.

## M11. The sketch in black and white first

On Titus and "in general", her sketches are initially done in black and white, with other colours filled in later, and "The palette is usually worked out with the director, along with the production designer", per Live Design. The order is the mechanism: silhouette and value come before colour, and the palette is a negotiation with the director and the production designer, not a decision the costume designer takes alone. If the shape does not read in black and white, no colour will save it, and the black-and-white sketch is the test of whether the shape reads at all. The same discipline appears in the closed palette, see M6: the palette is decided first and closed, and then the sketches are coloured inside it. The sketch is also where the film's world is agreed: Titus was built in a Cinecitta workshop where the costumes were made from beginning to end, because the look could not be rented, and the black-and-white sketch is the contract the workshop builds from. The failure mode is the design that starts with colour, which is decoration before it is construction, and decoration cannot carry a shape that does not read.

**Takeaway:** silhouette and value come before colour, and if the shape does not read in black and white, no colour will save it.

## M12. Costume as architecture and set

She takes the production-design job when the film needs one pair of hands for the whole world. Single White Female is the first double billing as costume and production designer, and the second is the Amadeus stage production of 1999, and Metrograph notes she has "reprised this double billing only once since", which makes the double billing a rare decision, not a habit. In Single White Female the Ansonia apartment and Bridget Fonda's wardrobe are one design: the sparse, chic early-90s Manhattan loft-meets-Parisian Haussmann apartment, chaise lounge, coromandel screen, paint artfully peeling, tulips lazing over their vase, and Fonda's angular jackets belted with power miniskirts, sheer black tights, a pivotal silver mid-length trench, and the critic Janet Maslin read the production design as "of such ravaged urban beauty that it suggests Last Tango in Paris on the Upper West Side". The Grand Budapest uniforms are built in the same workshops as the hotel's world, and the Louis Vuitton AW20 choir is a set made of 200 costumed bodies, and the mechanism is the same in both: when costume and set are one decision, the frame has no seams, and the actor and the room agree with each other. The opera cross-training, see M17, is the same mechanism at stage scale: the designer who dresses a crowd and a soloist in the same breath is already doing production design.

**Takeaway:** when costume and set are one decision, the frame has no seams, and the actor and the room agree with each other.

## M13. The period that is not a period

Titus is the no-period film, and the mechanism is the toy box: the film is "seen from the point of view of a 12-year-old child... he mixes his toy soldiers on the kitchen table, and they range from Roman and Goth soldiers up to G.I. Joes and Star Wars figures. So all manner of time is mixed", per Taymor, and Canonero's label for the look is "retro contemporary futuristic". The references are Roman and Etruscan art "in a very free way, not historically or academically accurate", plus the Italian Fascist era "and maybe the future", and the Goths take their inspiration from "the Niebelungs and German Gothics, a bit Wagnerian in a way... They come from the forests of the north, so they're in furs and gold leaf. And blond; the Romans were always dark." The unity is not historical but systematic: a basic palette, blacks, whites, grays and reds, with touches of blue and brown for the Goths, and hair colour as a tribal marker, Romans dark, Goths blond, and metal as a tribal marker, iron and cold metals for Romans, silver and gold for Goths. The film's own statement of the rule, from Canonero in Live Design: "Julie wanted to make a movie where there were no specific, direct references to a period, but to create a world unto itself... That gives a symbolic relevance to the violence and revenge, makes it more eternal." When history is a toy box, the designer's job is to pick the toys that mean something together, and the picking is the design.

**Takeaway:** when history is a toy box, the designer's job is to pick the toys that mean something together, and the system that unifies them is palette, hair and metal, not date.

## M14. The fashion-house collaboration

She borrows the fashion world's hands and lets them sign the work. The Hunger is the first of them: Yves Saint Laurent dressed Deneuve alongside Canonero's wardrobe, and the fashion house is in the film as a collaborator, not as a product placement. Marie Antoinette: Manolo Blahnik shoes, the macaron-hued satin heels, made by Blahnik and Pompei. The Darjeeling Limited: Marc Jacobs created the classically handsome gray suits and one-of-a-kind luggage, together with Louis Vuitton, for the three Whitman brothers. The Grand Budapest Hotel: Prada collaborated on many of the costumes, Jopling's black leather coat was made by Prada, Madame D's silk velvet coat-and-gown ensemble with Klimtesque handprint patterns and mink trim was by Fendi, and the film traded "the look of the typical colors of the hospitality industry, various shades of grey, to the more vivid shades of purple and mauve", per Frock Flicks. The Godfather Part III: the men's costumes were "expressly realized by Verri", the Milan fashion house, per the credit line. The French Dispatch: Prada and Louis Vuitton again, continuing the Anderson pattern. The rule is the same as with Tirelli, see N5: a fashion house is a specialist workshop, and you use it the way you use Tirelli, for what only it can make, and you let it sign the work, because the signature is the proof that the specialist made it.

**Takeaway:** a fashion house is a specialist workshop, and use it for what only it can make, and let it sign the work.

## M15. The clothes that leak into fashion

Her film wardrobes repeatedly become street fashion, and the leak is measurable, which is what makes it a mechanism rather than a compliment. Chariots of Fire is the most documented case: the preppy revival, European ready-to-wear houses playing Vangelis's soundtrack behind their shows the following autumn, New York Seventh Avenue designers doing the same weeks later, Bloomingdale's basing a 1982 ad campaign on the film featuring Ben Cross, Perry Ellis and Ralph Lauren echoing the look, Lauren's Polo line drawing on the Chariots ethos for capsule collections as recently as 2022, per Metrograph, and the success leading to an offer to create a clothing line for the menswear manufacturer Norman Hilton, for which she received a special Coty Award. Out of Africa: the safari craze, the film "set off another nostalgic trend... creating a demand for colonial turn-of-the-20th-century fashion reimagined through a late 20th-century lens", and "It was as though the fashion world was ready for the styles of the film", she told the LA Times, "The costumes just caught something that was in the air." A Clockwork Orange: Bowie's Ziggy Stardust from 1972, Jean Paul Gaultier, Madonna. The Life Aquatic: the red beanie as a hipster uniform worldwide. The leak has a cause, and Canonero states it for Chariots of Fire in the LA Times via Metrograph: she enjoyed designing for the early 1900s because the clothes were not "costumey", they could be worked with from what was already in one's closet, and "In favoring historical precision over theatrical costuming, Canonero made the film's wardrobe feel aspirational but accessible." A costume that escapes its film has done the one thing fashion cannot fake: it has been lived in by a character first.

**Takeaway:** a costume that escapes its film has been lived in by a character first, and the leak is the proof that the clothes were clothes, not costumes.

## M16. Costume as behaviour and promotion

Clothes show how a body moves and where a character stands, and the costume is a biography you can watch change. Metrograph states the general rule: "it's not even the garments themselves as much as it is how people move in them, a certain confidence and ease". The Godfather Part III states it as a promotion ladder: Andy Garcia's leather jackets "lengthen and become increasingly sophisticated (from a double-breasted sport coat to a broad-shouldered trench) as he rises up the family ranks". Barry Lyndon states it as an ascent and an extinction in the same film: Barry's clothing reflects his ascent to feudal dandy, "in much the same way that the pale, almost colourless rococo costumes of Lady Lyndon reflect the character of the wearer, whose personality is virtually extinguished by her despotic husband", per the Berlinale programme page. The Shining states it as a breakdown: Wendy's increasingly consuming layers and oversized boots make a woman unprepared for the horror, and Jack's decaying corduroy jacket mirrors his mental unraveling. The mechanism is the opposite of the costume drama's usual logic, where a new dress marks a new scene: here the same garment changing, lengthening, decaying, paling, marks the character changing, and the audience reads the biography off the cloth without being told.

**Takeaway:** the costume is a biography you can watch change, and the same garment changing is the character changing.

## M18. The refusal as a career move

**Takeaway:** in her story, saying no is as load-bearing as saying yes, and the career is the films she did not make as well as the films she did.

## M19. The single-colour psychology
