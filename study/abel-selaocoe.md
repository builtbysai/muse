Abel Selaocoe was born in 1992 in Sebokeng, a township south of Johannesburg, to a Tswana father, a mechanic, and a Zulu mother, a domestic worker; he has three elder siblings, two sisters and a brother, Sammy, eight years older, a budding bassoonist. His older brother found a Saturday-morning outreach programme run by the African Cultural Organization of South Africa (ACOSA) in Soweto, established by the violinist Michael Masote, and hauled his younger brother along. Selaocoe started on recorder, then his brother suggested the cello -- and there was no cello to take home, only one shared instrument at the school, available once a week. So the brothers drew the four strings on a piece of paper, worked out which hand positions produced which pitches, stuck the paper to his chest and pretended to play, while he learned repertoire by ear from the radio; after a year of dramatic progress the school got him a donated cello, and his first cello teacher was Kutlwano Masote, Michael Masote's son. At 13 he won a full scholarship to St John's College in Johannesburg, where he studied with Matlhaela Michael Masote; his audition programme combined Bach and hymn tunes, an early prototype of the music he makes now, and he spent five years there, entering what he calls a completely different world of privilege, speaking English only at a minimal level. The mechanism that matters for the whole practice is already visible in the paper cello: learning by listening before touching -- "actually it's very helpful to learn to listen before the challenges of playing" -- and making do with what is in the room. [C29466]

He moved to Manchester in 2010, enrolled at the Royal Northern College of Music, and completed his International Artist Diploma in July 2018 with Hannah Roberts; he has stayed in Manchester since, calling it his home base. The RNCM was, in his words, "a place for experimentation, a place to fail a lot, to make a lot of mistakes, and to learn classical music at its core" -- and his professor told him to bring his culture with him: "We're saturated with cellists who are all doing the same thing." During those years he made his Wigmore Hall debut in 2013 with the composer Colin Matthews, performed with the Johannesburg, KwaZulu-Natal and Cape Town Philharmonics, played regularly with the BBC Philharmonic, and co-founded Kabantu, a global folk fusion quintet and BBC Introducing ensemble that played across the UK. In 2016 he formed Chesaba, the trio that is the first full statement of his practice: Selaocoe on cello and voice, Sidiki Dembele from Ivory Coast on djembe, kora, n'goni, African percussion and voice, and Alan Keary from Ireland on electric bass and voice -- three musicians, three continents, one groove, specialising in music from the African continent including many of his own compositions. The direct inspiration, named by Selaocoe himself, was Ernst Reijseger's Down Deep, where the cello shape-shifts between percussion instrument and bass. With Manchester Collective he devised Sirocco (from 2019), a six-musician programme staging the meeting of Europe and Africa as a weather system -- the wind that blows from Africa toward Italy -- which toured North America in April 2024. [C29467]

The public arrival came fast. He signed to Warner Classics as an exclusive recording artist in August 2021, ahead of his solo BBC Proms debut on 15 August 2021, a programme he curated himself: Chesaba, the Moroccan Gnawa master Simo Lagnawi and Gnawa London, the choral group Bantu Voices, and the BBC National Orchestra of Wales under Clark Rundell, with his own Qhawe, Zawose, As You Are, Lerato and Ka Bohaleng beside Giovanni Sollima's L.B. Files and two short Rameau excerpts, placed there for the shared ground of Baroque ground bass and an African culture of repetition. In January 2022 his three-part BBC Radio 3 series Cello Retold used four pieces -- Platti, Britten, Reijseger, Sollima, plus Tavener -- to teach the cello's many homes. In May 2022 he was appointed Artistic Partner of The Saint Paul Chamber Orchestra for an initial three seasons from 2022/23, a role he still holds in 2026-27; he was Artist in Association with the BBC Singers in 2022/23 and Artist in Residence with Kolner Philharmonie in 2024-25/2025-26, and Southbank Centre Artist in Residence for 2022/23. In 2021-22 he was an inaugural Power Up Music Creator participant and received a Paul Hamlyn Foundation award for his compositional work. On 1 March 2023 he won the RPS Instrumentalist Award at Queen Elizabeth Hall, for his debut album and for performances drawing capacity audiences back to concert venues after the pandemic. [C29468]

The debut album Where Is Home (Hae Ke Kae), released 23 September 2022 on Warner Classics, is 16 tracks in 55:18, and its system is a three-way splice that repeats with variations: his own songs in Sesotho and isiZulu, cello and voice, with strings, electric bass, calabash, djembe, congas and talking drum; Baroque cello music -- Platti's Cello Sonata No. 7 in D and Bach Sarabandes from Suites Nos. 3 and 5 -- played alone or with theorbo and kora; and short solo improvisations slotted between the Baroque movements and between the songs, so the composed and the improvised alternate as the album's joints. The track architecture, from his own liner notes, opens with Ibuyile iAfrica, an apartheid-era Zulu hymn arranged as an ode to Desmond Tutu with Yo-Yo Ma as guest cello; Zawose answers the Tanzanian musician Hukwe Zawose's Mateso by translating the high-pitched one-string zeze stick zither onto the cello; Hlokomela states ubuntu -- umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, "you are because I am" -- as a watering hole where springbok, lion and hippo drink together; Qhawe, for his nephew, is built from Apostolic church music where children lead the singing and a woman beats a large bass drum on either side; Lerato takes its bass from the congregation beating Bibles as a group; Seipone, a solo improvisation, copies the circular bowing of the Lesotho one-string segankula zither; the two Bach Sarabandes are the pieces his mother used to sing along to at home, inventing her own countermelody over the harmony; Ka Bohaleng, "On the Sharp Side", celebrates mothers; Ancestral Affirmations closes as a prayer with his family's voices, asking "if I say 'heal' as many times as I can, will the world heal?" The governing idea, in his words: "Home ... is never just a geographical space. It's full of nostalgia, full of memories ... If you have a place of refuge, you have a place to build." [C29469]

The second album, Hymns of Bantu, released 21 February 2025, is 12 tracks in 55:34, framed as a homecoming after the debut's restless searching -- a celebration of those who came before, drawing on the modal scales and overtone harmonic systems of South African music before Western four-part harmony was introduced. It runs from his own Bantu Ensemble with African percussion to orchestra, solo cello and electric bass, interpreting traditional Bantu music alongside Bach and Marais: Tsohle Tsohle, "everything is everything", as the opening hymn and returning refrain; Emmanuele, a grooving hub of competing rhythms; Tshepo, faith as hope, in two parts; Dinaka, a fractal whole-group improvisation in the shape of an ancestral prayer, drawing on Pedi traditions; Voices of Bantu, an improvisation on Marin Marais' Les Voix humaines; Takamba, a Northern Mali and Niger Songhai and Tuareg style; Sollima's L.B. Files as the cello at its most dynamic and rhapsodic; Camagu closing on a groove built from the twangy sound of a toy violin. Between the albums came his first cello concerto, Four Spirits -- for cello, voice, orchestra and percussion, orchestrated by Benjamin Woodgates, with the percussionist Bernhard Schimpelsberger as a kind of secondary soloist, co-commissioned by BBC Radio 3, the Seattle Symphony and Philharmonie Zuidnederland, first performed 23 March 2023 by Selaocoe with the BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra under Taavi Oramo at City Halls, Glasgow. Its four movements are each named for a pillar of township life -- MaSebego (Traditional Healer), Bana (Children), Tshepo (Faith), Simunye (We Are One) -- with no stops between movements, sung in Southern Sotho and Zulu, and recorded live with Aurora Orchestra under Nicholas Collon at Queen Elizabeth Hall on 8 March 2025, released 15 August 2025. The Bantu Ensemble, founded in 2022, is the live vehicle for all of it: Selaocoe, Alan Keary, Fred Thomas and Dudu Kouate, with no fixed repertoire, every performance unique, from the Concertgebouw to Glastonbury (debut 2025) to a first US tour with Carnegie Hall and La Jolla in 2024/25. [C29470]

Then came the year that turned him from a soloist into a composer other soloists commission. Jessie Montgomery's These Righteous Paths, a cello concerto in three movements -- All the Ancestors and I; Another Train Poem; A New Song -- about twenty minutes, co-commissioned by ten partners, was written for him and in collaboration with him, inspired by the plays and poems of Montgomery's late mother Robbie McCauley; Montgomery went to Norway to watch him perform, heard him sing a piece about mothers, and decided to write around his facility to both sing and play, abandoning the idea that it would work for any cellist. The world premiere was 20 March 2026 at the Philharmonie Berlin, and the tour ran through Brussels, Wroclaw, Toronto, the BBC Proms, Lincoln Center, Aspen and Oregon, with Selaocoe the soloist in every performance. In the same year he wrote in the other direction: Ramblings of Modisana for Yo-Yo Ma, premiered by Ma in February 2026 at UC Santa Barbara, and Toro Tsa Kwa for Gautier Capucon, premiered in November 2025 on a San Francisco Symphony commission and recorded as track 15 of Capucon's album Gaia (Erato, 7 November 2025), with Selaocoe himself on vocals and programming. In May 2026 he curated the opening night of the Barbican's Mohopolo/Ancestral Memory Weekend at Milton Court around his three closest collaborators, Nduduzo Makhathini, Gontse Makhene and Mbuso Khoza; in June 2026 the Bantu Ensemble played Wigmore Hall and Harrogate; the 2026-27 season lists the Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra, the Deutsches Symphonie-Orchester Berlin, the Deutsche Kammerphilharmonie Bremen and a European tour with Amsterdam Sinfonietta. He plays a copy of a circa 1735 Montagnana, made by Robin Aitchison in 2020 with the support of the Alan Powell Trust, and he has a daughter, Alva, celebrated in Colin Alexander's Alva's Riff. [C29471]

## M1 -- The singing cello: voice and cello at once, as one storytelling instrument

The mechanism is literal simultaneity: Selaocoe sings while bowing, so voice and cello sound at the same time from one body, and the two lines can be in counterpoint, in unison, or in different registers. The watchable behaviour is the surprise -- Montgomery, in the Playbill account: "It's always a surprise to audiences when Abel starts singing in the middle of the first movement." The Oregon review of These Righteous Paths: "superb ability to sing and play -- often at the same time"; his voice "soared into the highest tenor range... descended into the basso basement, alternating between an enticing growl and resonant throat singing". The Strad's Proms round-up calls his voice "his uncannily floated baritone as, if you like, a fifth sympathetic string to his cello". The vocal palette, per the sources read end to end: lyrical singing, chant, incantation, prayer, growls, clicks, pops and snaps generated vocally, and overtone or throat singing that produces two pitches at once -- a fundamental plus harmonic overtones, which Selaocoe calls Umkolo, "mostly sung by women", and which NPR names umngqokolo; the spelling difference is recorded as an open discrepancy, not resolved. His own technical account of the overtone: "It's basically using your normal voice but relaxing your vocal cords so much that they dip into the lower octave without taking your actual range to that place. I wanted to incorporate it into my music because it felt like a kind of spiritual inhalation, so I went to learn with people who'd been doing it all their lives." The rule that governs it, in his words: "If I don't sing, I feel very muted as a human being"; "The voice gives an immediate connection to imagination. If you imagine a sound, it can simply come out. Whereas if you imagine something on the cello, you have to navigate where your fingers need to go." Seen in W09 and W11-W14, throughout both albums and both concertos, and most nakedly in the opening of the 2021 Proms programme, where Selaocoe played and sang alone, very lightly accompanied, and in the Oregon encore, where he sang a low throaty Mama while brushing the cello strings like windscreen wipers.

**Takeaway:** one body, two voices -- and the voice is the faster instrument. [C29472]

## M2 -- Improvisation inside composed frames: start from the voice, then let the cello follow

Selaocoe's stated method, from his own liner notes: "Improvisation starts with a bit of your own singing. You have to sing inside of yourself and then your personality starts to come out... I start from the voice, then go from the voice to the cello, then begin to let this sound reflect my thoughts or the world around me." The mechanism has three distinct placements. First, improvisation as the joint between composed blocks: short solo improvisations interpolated between the four movements of the Platti sonata on Where Is Home, and the 45-second Dipolelo and 46-second Invocation as hinges between songs. Second, improvisation as the substance of a track: Seipone is just voice and improvising cello; Dinaka on Hymns of Bantu is where "improvisation... now becomes an end in itself. The result is a fractal whole-group improvisation in the shape of an ancestral prayer"; Voices of Bantu is billed as an improvisation on Marais. Third, improvisation as the form itself: Four Spirits has "no stops between movements... extended improvisations" and challenges the audience "to stay with it and believe in it, even when they wondered where the improvisation was going"; These Righteous Paths builds its architecture on the balance between fixed notation and the soloist's break, with the last movement opening for his improvisation and his incantation style. The origin story he gives is material, not mystical: improvisation "started with how we made music at home: we had to 'make do' a lot... we didn't have access to much sheet-music, so I would get a cassette and tape-record whatever cello concerto was on the radio station... I think that was an experience that allowed my ears to be quite malleable"; and "we've always sung and always danced, and all of that requires a creative impulse where nobody tells you what to do." The corollary is oral transmission: for the SPCO programme many of the pieces were taught orally by Selaocoe, without any printed sheet music, and the orchestra's planning manager sat in on rehearsals scribbling notes and taking dictations. Seen in W09, W11-W14.

**Takeaway:** the voice starts it, the cello follows, and the score is a frame the player is allowed to leave. [C29473]

## M3 -- Bantu song forms and the languages they are sung in

The song material is hymns, prayers and praise songs, and the languages are named in the sources -- verified here, language by language, with no guessing. Sesotho: Hae ke Kae ("Where is Home?"); Hlokomela, Qhawe, Lerato, Seipone, Ka Bohaleng, Tsohle Tsohle ("everything is everything"), Tshepo via the Sesotho verb tshepa ("hope"); Southern Sotho is also one of the two sung languages of Four Spirits. isiZulu: Ibuyile iAfrica ("Africa is back"); the ubuntu maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu; Simunye ("We Are One"); Zulu is the second sung language of Four Spirits. isiXhosa: Selaocoe sang the words of These Righteous Paths "in his native language, Xhosa" (Oregon review); Xhosa-style throat singing; the overtone tradition named umngqokolo (NPR) and Umkolo (Selaocoe himself). Tswana / Setswana: Tshepo means "faith" in Tswana; the ubuntu maxim is also given in Sesotho/Setswana as motho ke motho ka batho. Pedi traditions, not a verified sung language: Dinaka draws on "the musical and spiritual traditions of the Pedi people", but no source read this run states that Sepedi words are sung on the album, so Sepedi is NOT claimed as a sung language here. The click consonants come from Khoisan and some Bantu languages and shape his phrase lengths and tonal inflections: "So much of what I write is literally related to our speech-patterns at home, including the lengths of phrases, the tonal inflections, and the click consonants that we use in Khoisan and some Bantu languages... I can't even remember learning how to do the clicks: I just remember being able to recite poems that used them a lot when I was very young." The hymn logic: the albums' governing idea is that South African music before Western four-part harmony ran on "unique modal scales and overtone harmonic systems", and Selaocoe's thread connecting it to the Western choral tradition is "the world of beautiful four-part harmony... we still share the concept of different voices singing together". His governing aphorism: "Percussion is imitating the language, melodic instruments are imitating the voice." Seen in W09, W11-W14.

**Takeaway:** the song comes first, in its own language, and the cello learns to speak it. [C29474]

## M4 -- Body percussion and rhythmic drive: the cello as a drum, the room as a drum

Two distinct percussive practices, and the sources keep them apart. First, the cello and the body used as percussion: "rhythmic tapping, plucking, and looping"; Selaocoe uses "your body (and the body of the cello) as a percussive instrument". His own correction matters and is recorded here: "That way of playing doesn't actually feature much in African string music: I took a lot of it from playing contemporary music by the likes of Simon Steen-Andersen. I'm still very much experimenting with ways of really playing outside of the body of the cello and translating African rhythms and styles onto that." So the percussive cello is a contemporary-music borrowing applied to African rhythm, not a traditional African string technique -- do not present it as the latter. Second, rhythm as the music's engine: "Groove is an essential part of Selaocoe's vernacular"; Emmanuele is "a grooving hub of rhythms competing joyously"; Takamba makes groove "the texture's central building block, this time closer to a shuffle feel"; Tshepo in Four Spirits runs on "an ecstatic seven-beat groove" taken from Postola worship; the Lerato sound world includes the congregation beating Bibles "as a group, creating this wonderful bass sound"; the Postola tradition puts "a woman beating a large bass drum on either side throughout a song" while children sing. In Four Spirits the middle of Tshepo becomes an extended passage of Selaocoe and Schimpelsberger "bouncing off each other" like Steve Reich's Clapping Music -- "pure rhythmic elan, with pitch and melody temporarily set aside". In These Righteous Paths Montgomery writes him, by turns, as narrator, as "a percussion instrument -- or several", and as a singer in counterpoint with himself. Seen in W09, W11-W14.

**Takeaway:** the groove is the ground, and everything else -- voice, cello, orchestra -- stands on it. [C29475]

## M5 -- The cello as a voice, not an accompaniment

The inversion at the centre of the practice: the cello does not accompany the voice; the voice and the cello are the same instrument in two forms, and the cello's job is to sing. Selaocoe: "I believe that nearly all musical instruments aspire to sound like the human voice in some way, and drums and percussion instruments are all connected strongly with language." The biographical root, from his own liner notes: as a child practising Bach in a small house, his mother "sitting and sewing nearby as I played, would react to the music and sing along in a very African way -- a very South African hymn-singing way -- devising her own countermelody to the chord progression. That became a part of my experience of these pieces... it informs my very personal approach to them to this day." So the Bach Sarabandes on both albums are not classical interludes; they are the pieces his mother sang over, and his singing over them restores the missing voice. The cello-as-voice also takes a narrative form in Four Spirits: "At times the cello is almost more of a prop and a tool that sits in the background -- one imagines perhaps a medieval bard strumming a chord or two while delivering a dramatic narration"; the soloist's role is narration as much as display. And in These Righteous Paths the cello-voice becomes a duet partner: Selaocoe sings "melodies in counterpoint" with orchestral soloists and "in a duet with himself". The cello techniques that serve the voice: left-hand pizzicato, double notes, tremolo, glissandos, complex rhythms; bowed harmonics; the segankula-derived circular bowing in Seipone; "brushing the strings of his cello" in the Oregon encore. The instrument itself is a copy of a circa 1735 Montagnana, made by Robin Aitchison in 2020 -- an old voice, newly built, for a player who treats it as a body that breathes. Seen in W09, W11-W14.

**Takeaway:** the cello learned to sing by listening to his mother sing over Bach. [C29476]

## M6 -- Collaboration as composition method: works built with and for specific players, and the audience as a player

Selaocoe's works are not finished objects handed to performers; they are built in the room with the people who will play them, and the audience is routinely recruited as a performer too. The evidence, work by work: Fred Thomas is arranger and co-producer across both albums -- the Bach Sixth Suite Sarabande arrangement, the Tsohle Tsohle and Emmanuele arrangements, the prepared piano on Dinaka, the Voices of Bantu co-arrangement. Benjamin Woodgates orchestrated Four Spirits -- Selaocoe composes, Woodgates orchestrates, and the work "has evolved since its premiere", gaining the orchestration and extended improvisations. Bernhard Schimpelsberger is a secondary soloist in Four Spirits, with a vocal duet with Selaocoe in Tshepo and the Reich-like rhythmic dialogue in the middle of the movement. The Manchester Collective is the string body of Hymns of Bantu and the Sirocco and Oracle partner. Yo-Yo Ma plays guest cello on Ibuyile iAfrica -- Selaocoe's role model, "someone who has paved the way for more cellists to seek out their own identities". Jessie Montgomery wrote These Righteous Paths in discussion with Selaocoe about "where, when and how the improvisation could interact with what the orchestra and cello are doing", so that "in many ways, the architecture of the piece is very dependent on these elements coming into balance with one another". And the audience is written into the work: in Simunye the audience joins the Uthando chant as a mantra and "we end up singing together in this movement, performers and audience"; at Oregon, Selaocoe had "everyone in the hall, including the string players... singing and harmonizing". The Hymns of Bantu launch at the Troxy on 26 March 2025 was "a huge get-together of all the musicians on the album... a very communal project... a massive string section, percussionists from the Ivory Coast and Senegal, a pianist from Argentina and a bassist from Ireland, all coming together". Seen in W05, W09, W11-W15.

**Takeaway:** the piece is finished by the people in the room -- players, collaborators, audience included. [C29477]

## M7 -- The programme as a journey: the concert as an exchange, not a recital

Selaocoe does not give recitals; he takes the room on a journey through time and place, and he says so before a note sounds. He opens concerts by telling the audience "Tonight, you are not listeners, but participants. Our meeting is an exchange." The curation is the composition: the 2021 Proms programme was a self-curated gathering -- Chesaba, Gnawa London, Bantu Voices, a symphony orchestra -- arranged so that the sparsest moments, Selaocoe playing and singing alone, carried the most weight; the SPCO programmes pair his Ka Bohaleng with Beethoven's Heiliger Dankgesang from Op. 132; the Bantu Ensemble's programmes have no fixed repertoire and change every performance, "shaped by Selaocoe's compositions and music that inspires them"; the Milton Court night of May 2026 was curated as a ceremony, built on Zulu rhythms, culture and cosmology, with his three closest collaborators. The albums are journeys in the same sense: Where Is Home stages one question -- "Where is home?" -- as a collision of Baroque and South African music, with the cello as the room they share, and Hymns of Bantu answers it as a homecoming, "celebrating those that have come before us", with home "felt in the past as much as the present". Four Spirits has "no stops between movements; it just goes on and on and on into different worlds. I wanted to challenge the audience to stay with it and believe in it, even when they wondered where the improvisation was going." The journey has a destination, and the destination is participation: the last movement of These Righteous Paths is "meant to draw the audience deeply into the meaning of my mother's poems, and to create an opening for his improvisation and his incantation style", and by the end "his vocalisations become a kind of prayer meant to uplift and imprint the poems' final meaning in the space". Seen in W07, W09, W11-W15.

**Takeaway:** the concert is not a display; it is a journey the room takes together, and it ends with everyone singing. [C29478]

## M8 -- The oral method: learn by listening first, and teach by ear

The paper cello is not a childhood anecdote; it is the method, stated once and then repeated at every scale of his career. He learned the cello by listening before touching, by drawing four strings on paper and learning repertoire by ear from the radio, by tape-recording whatever cello concerto was on the station because there was no sheet music in the house. The RNCM years kept the method: a conservatoire used as an experimentation lab, where the professor said "bring your culture here". The albums keep it: Baroque music and his inherited South African culture "both lend themselves to improvisation", because "the hymns my forefathers sang have bass lines not unlike those in Baroque music" -- the continuo is a shared oral grammar, and the theorbo and the kora trade it as improvising partners. The orchestral work keeps it: for the SPCO programme the pieces were taught orally, without printed sheet music, the planning manager taking dictations in rehearsal. The teaching keeps it: he tells young players to sing inside themselves first, because "If you imagine a sound, it can simply come out" through the voice, while on the cello "you have to navigate where your fingers need to go". The method is why his pieces travel: a work learned by ear can be taught by ear, remade by a new room, and finished by whoever is in it. Seen in W01-W03, W09, W11-W14.

**Takeaway:** listen first, sing second, play third -- and the score is the last thing the music needs. [C29479]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Selaocoe makes music the way he learned the cello: by ear, by voice, by what is in the room. The voice is the first instrument and the fastest; the cello is the voice given a wooden body; the groove is the ground they stand on; the song, in its own language, is the material; the score is a frame to be left and returned to; the piece is finished by the people playing it and the people listening to it; the concert is a journey the room takes together; and home is not a place on a map but a refuge you build, out of hymns, mothers, ancestors and whoever is singing beside you. Every work in the survey is the same move at a different scale: the paper cello is a boy making an instrument out of nothing; Chesaba is three continents making a groove out of a trio; Where Is Home is Baroque and South African music making a home out of one album; Four Spirits is a township making a concerto out of four pillars; These Righteous Paths is a composer's mother and a cellist's voice making a prayer out of an orchestra; and the Oregon encore is a whole concert hall making a song out of two notes. The through-line is not fusion, which would imply two finished things joined, but hospitality: a room is opened, a song is invited in, and everyone in the room is asked to sing. [C29480]
