# Bartolomeo Bimbi: Study Notes

**Site:** Bartolomeo Bimbi (Friday 15 May 1648 - Friday 14 January 1729), Florentine painter in oil, known almost exclusively for still life and portraits; pupil of Lorenzo Lippi (c.1661-65) and then of Onorio Marinari; in Rome in 1670 with Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici for the conclave that elected Clement X; worked primarily for the House of Medici, his main patron Grand Duke Cosimo III, who commissioned from 1685 the still-life decorations of the Villa Medicea L'Ambrogiana and the Villa Medicea della Topaia. His large canvases catalogue the Medici botanical collections -- 34 varieties of citrus on one canvas, 115 types of pears on another -- and his life-size "portraits from nature" record freak specimens: two-headed animals brought by peasants to the Grand Duke, a monstrous cauliflower, a giant pumpkin, a life-size sunflower painted at 73. He died in Florence aged 80, on the day Lorenzo Fratellini turned 39. The record is strong where Lorenzo's was thin: a full Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani entry (Silvia Meloni Trkulja, vol. 10, 1968, read in full), a 1998 monograph cataloguing 140 paintings, the National Gallery of Art's 2002 "The Flowering of Florence", and some 30 of his canvases in the Museo Botanico with the main holding at the Museo della Natura Morta, Villa Medicea di Poggio a Caiano. What is missing: no high-resolution reproduction with legible numbered labels was found anywhere in any leg (photography is banned at Poggio a Caiano), no named pupil of his was found, no named early figure painting of his was found, and the 1998 monograph and the NGA catalogue were not opened in full. ## M1. The Numbered Inventory -- The Painting as a Labelled Catalogue

His great canvases are databases with a picture attached. The English Wikipedia: "These large canvases are enclosed in frames decorated with accurately represented plant motifs; each displays many different fruits, all numbered with reference to an inscription in which the various species are listed with their scientific names." The Web Gallery of Art: "His highly illustrative style was utilised to document various types of plants and vegetables, and often his paintings contain numbered items which relate to a list, which also appears as part of the picture, either in the form of a scroll or plaque." The DBI gives the physical form: plates and baskets full of pears, apples and figs from the Medici farms, "numerati e descritti sulla base di una colonna, o su un cartiglio che si srotola elegantemente dall'orlo del tavolo" -- numbered and described on a column, or on a cartouche unrolling elegantly from the edge of the table -- and "talora, un cartellino pendente dal cesto o dal vassoio indica anche la stagione in cui i frutti maturano" -- sometimes a little tag hanging from the basket or tray also indicates the season in which the fruits ripen. The numbers and the text are painted into the composition, not added later; the cartouche and the column are structural devices, part of the picture's architecture. The counts are the point: "Citrus Fruits" shows 34 varieties of citrus; "Pears" (1699) shows 115 different types of pears; the Reeds review counts "thirty-four kinds of cherries and more than a hundred kinds of pears". The watchable behaviour is the index arriving with the image: the fruits appear one by one, each one takes a number, the number finds its line in the painted list, and the canvas fills until it is a catalogue you can read. **Takeaway:** the numbered inventory -- the painting IS the index, specimens numbered and keyed to a painted inscription, the cartouche unrolling from the table edge, the frame itself decorated with accurately painted plant motifs.

## M2. The Specimen Label -- Date, Place, Weight, Dimensions Painted In

The metadata is the picture content. The vegetable series in the Museo Botanico -- "Beets", "Spanish Thistle", "Bean Plants", "Truffle", all of extraordinary size -- are "each labelled with the date and place of discovery and often with the weight and dimensions of the subject. Similar information accompanies the Two-headed Calf and the Two-headed Lamb" (English Wikipedia). The 2002 press: "These large-scale scenes ... are complete with inscriptions of the name of each specimen, when and where it was gathered, and its weight." The giant pumpkin of 1711 is a named pumpkin from a named convent, with size and weight inscribed; the melon of 1694 is the "Popone di Ponte a Cappiano", a melon from a named place, and the place is in the title. This is a field record on canvas, an accession card that happens to be a painting: the specimen cannot be separated from the circumstances of its finding, because the finding is what made it worth painting. The watchable behaviour is the label writing itself onto the picture: the specimen appears, then the date, the place, the weight and the dimensions arrive beside it like a caption that refuses to stay outside the frame. **Takeaway:** the specimen label -- date, place of discovery, weight and dimensions painted in with the specimen, the painting functioning as a field record and an accession card at once.

## M3. Life-Size Scale -- A Grandezza Naturale

Scale is data, not style. The DBI: he paints oversized fruit "a grandezza naturale" -- at life size. The Sunflower of 1721 is life-size; the 1715 citrus canvas "Melangoli, limoni e limette" is 174 x 233 cm; the "Cauliflower and Wild Radishes" of 1706 is 87.6 x 118.1 cm; the inventory canvases are "huge oil paintings" (Reeds). A canvas at 1:1 is a measuring surface: the fruit on it is the size the fruit was, and the painting can be checked against the thing the way a ruler can. That is why the monstrosity paintings work -- a cauliflower at life size is a fact, not an exaggeration -- and why the census paintings are wall-filling: 115 pears at life size need a wall. His style, the Web Gallery of Art says, "remained constant throughout his long career", so scale is one of the few things that does change, and it changes with the commission: single specimen portraits at 60 x 66 cm ("Large Citron in a Landscape", 1690), census walls from 1685, monster portraits at life size through the 1710s. The watchable behaviour is the fruit arriving at true size: the canvas grows to fit the specimen instead of the specimen shrinking to fit the canvas, and the viewer stands before it at the same scale as the thing. **Takeaway:** life size as measurement -- "a grandezza naturale", the canvas as a measuring surface, the specimen painted at the size it was so the picture can be checked against the thing.

## M4. The Theatrical Background -- Curtain, Colonnade, Trellis, Sky

The backgrounds are a small repertory of stage-sets, and they change the register of the same documentary act. The DBI: "Belli e svariati sono gli sfondi, dalla tenda di velluto trattenuta da cordoni dorati all'austero colonnato, dal graticcio della limonaia inquadrato da cariatidi sorridenti al cielo afoso dell'estate" -- beautiful and varied are the backgrounds, from the velvet curtain held by golden cords to the austere colonnade, from the lemon-house trellis framed by smiling caryatids to the sultry summer sky. Four sets, no more: curtain, colonnade, trellis, open sky. The specimen table is the same -- plates and baskets on a table edge -- but the set behind it decides whether the painting reads as a court display, a classical monument, a garden record or a summer's day. The single-specimen variant drops the table altogether and isolates one fruit against a landscape ("Large Citron in a Landscape", 1690); the shells variant grounds the specimens on a patterned carpet instead ("Mollusks and Corals"). The watchable behaviour is the set changing behind the specimen: the fruit stays put, the curtain draws, the colonnade rises, the trellis frames, the sky opens, and the same documented thing reads four different ways. **Takeaway:** the theatrical background -- a repertory of four stage-sets (velvet curtain, austere colonnade, lemon-house trellis, sultry summer sky) behind the same specimen table, changing the register of the same documentary act.

## M5. The Specimen Portrait -- One Thing, Isolated

The opposite pole to the census wall, and the same hand. The "Large Citron in a Landscape" (1690, 60 x 66 cm) is a single oversized citron isolated against a sweeping landscape; the "Melon of Ponte a Cappiano" (1694) is a named melon from a named place; the Moluccan cockatoo, delivered 6 October 1716 and hung 12 November at the Villa Ambrogiana, is one white bird on a perch over nuts and dried fruits, the dark ground making the white plumage glow; the life-size Sunflower (1721) is one plant at 73; the "Olive Tree from the Garden of Cardinale del Giudice" (1723) is a specific named tree, painted at 75. Each one is a portrait in the strict sense: one named individual, painted from nature, with a date and a provenance, the way a court portraitist paints one sitter. The DBI's word for the whole practice is "ritrattistiche" -- portrait-like -- and it is exact: Bimbi painted fruit the way other painters painted faces, one at a time, at life size, with the name attached. The watchable behaviour is the isolation: the crowd of the census wall thins to one thing, the one thing takes the whole frame, and the portrait holds still while the background -- landscape, dark ground, sky -- does the talking. **Takeaway:** the specimen portrait -- one named fruit, animal or tree, life-size, isolated against landscape or dark ground, painted the way a court portraitist paints one sitter.

## M6. The Monstrosity as Commission -- The Freak Specimen Is the News

He painted freaks because the freak was the commission. The DBI lists the subjects in one breath: he paints at life size "frutta spropositate, o animali con due teste portati dai contadini al granduca, gli uccelli migratori bloccati dall'intenso freddo del 1708 nelle paludi pisane, un grosso lupo ucciso in Mugello, un inquieto gattopardo riportato da un esploratore ed esposto nel giardino di Boboli, frutta esotica, spighe multiple, ecc." -- preposterously oversized fruit, or two-headed animals brought by peasants to the Grand Duke, migratory birds frozen by the intense cold of 1708 in the Pisan marshes, a big wolf killed in Mugello, a restless ocelot brought back by an explorer and exhibited in the Boboli garden, exotic fruit, multiple ears of grain, and so on. The two-headed calf and the two-headed lamb (Palazzo Pitti, attributed to Bimbi by Mina Gregori in 1964, previously thought to be Andrea Scacciati) carry the same date-place-weight inscription apparatus as the vegetables; the "Cauliflower and Wild Radishes" (1706) is a freak cauliflower and a deformed radish harvested in Tuscany in 1706 and rushed to Cosimo III; the giant pumpkin of 1711 came from the St. Francis Convent. The Web Gallery of Art: "He specialized in painting from nature animals or vegetables that had some extraordinary or freakish aspect; he classified these by species, season or place of origin." The freak is not a joke and not a moral: it is a data point that happens to be strange, and the painting records it with the same rigour as a normal pear. The watchable behaviour is the anomaly arriving: the normal specimens fill the frame, and then the two-headed calf, the monstrous cauliflower, the giant pumpkin arrives among them, takes its number and its label like all the rest, and the catalogue absorbs the impossible without flinching. **Takeaway:** the monstrosity as commission -- the freak specimen painted because it was news, classified by species, season and place of origin with the same rigour as a normal pear, the catalogue absorbing the impossible.

## M7. Dense Order -- The Census Wall and the Isolated Portrait

Two poles, one hand, one documentary purpose. The census wall: dozens of specimens in plates and baskets on a table edge, in parallel rows or clusters, "compositions, while often dense with subjects, are carefully arranged" -- the Pears of 1699 with 115 types, the Citrus Fruits with 34 varieties, the two Basket of Grapes and four Basket of Citrus Fruits from 1685. The isolated portrait: one citron, one melon, one cockatoo, one sunflower, one olive tree, alone against landscape or dark ground (M5). The DBI's two words cover both poles at once: nature morte "didascaliche" e "ritrattistiche" -- didactic and portrait-like still lifes. The painting teaches AND portrays: the wall teaches the range of a species, the portrait portrays the individual. Nothing in the record suggests he thought of them as different kinds of work; they are the same commission at two densities. The watchable behaviour is the density breathing: the frame fills with dozens of numbered specimens until it is a wall, then empties to a single fruit, then fills again, the same hand, the same accuracy, the density itself the variable. **Takeaway:** dense order -- the crowded census wall and the isolated specimen portrait as two densities of the same documentary act, didactic and portrait-like at once.

## M8. The Living and the Stuffed Model -- Animals from the Serraglio

The animal pictures were painted from living animals in the Medici menagerie and from stuffed ones, and the two kinds of model leave different marks. With Pietro Neri Scacciati he painted the roughly 100-picture animal series for the Villa Ambrogiana, from the first four decades of the 18th century, for Cosimo III and then Gian Gastone: living animals from the Serraglio degli animali rari, and stuffed animals including Princess Violante's collection. The Moluccan cockatoo was a living pet -- a salmon-crested cockatoo, a gift from the Indies, Cosimo III's prized pet, delivered 6 October 1716, hung 12 November at the Ambrogiana among the rare-animal paintings -- and its composition was copied in reverse for Saverio Manetti's Storia Naturale (1767-76), so the living bird became a printed bird. The two-headed calf and lamb were dead on arrival, brought by peasants, and painted with the same apparatus as the vegetables. The 1708 frost-killed birds were dead too. Precision from dead models, naturalism from live ones: the stuffed animal holds still for the detail, the living animal gives the posture, and the painting does not say which it used. The watchable behaviour is the model changing under the paint: the animal holds a pose too still to be alive, then shifts, then holds again, the living and the stuffed trading places inside one picture. **Takeaway:** the living and the stuffed model -- animal portraits painted from the Serraglio's living animals and from stuffed specimens, precision from the dead, naturalism from the living, in one picture.

## M9. The Melancholy Flower Piece -- The One Place Personality Shows

The flower paintings are a small part of the oeuvre, and the only place the painter's personality shows. The English Wikipedia: "His flower paintings form a small part of his oeuvre yet perhaps best reveal his personality; they are touched with melancholy, in the manner of Willem van Aelst, whose works Bimbi may have seen in the Medici residences." The DBI agrees, in its own way: as a flower painter he is "more conventional than in the 'portraits' of fruit". The giant carnations of 1699 are enormous blooms at near life size; the "Still life with bunch of flowers in an embossed metal vase" (106 x 67 cm, attributed by Mina Gregori) names its flowers one by one -- tulips, anemones, irises, lilies, Maltese crosses, tuberoses and convolvuli -- in a historiated metal vase. No numbers, no inscriptions, no census: the flowers are not a catalogue, they are a mood, and the mood is melancholy where the fruit is triumphant. The contrast is the mechanism: the same hand that counts 115 pears without blinking turns to flowers and goes quiet. The watchable behaviour is the register dropping: the bright census colours dim, the numbered labels fall away, the flowers arrive one by one in a tall vase, and the picture holds still and sad where the fruit pictures bustle. **Takeaway:** the melancholy flower piece -- the small, non-census flower paintings in the manner of Willem van Aelst, the one place the cataloguer's personality shows, melancholy where the fruit is triumphant.

## M10. Style Constant, Subject Moving -- The Control Case

The handling does not evolve; the commission does. The Web Gallery of Art states it outright: "His style, which remained constant throughout his long career, is distinguished by its scientific accuracy, and he must have had close contact with scientists and naturalists." No stylistic break, no late looseness, no colour shift across sixty years of work, from the Turkish Arms of 1680 to the Olive Tree of 1723. What changes is the subject and the scale: single-subject portraits (1680-90), the great Medici census from 1685, the monstrosity phase (1706-16), the late narrowing to single named individuals (1721-23). The evolution is institutional, not painterly -- the patron's project changes, the painter's hand does not. That makes Bimbi a control case for "style as instrument": the accuracy is not a phase he passed through, it is the tool he was hired for, and it stayed sharp for six decades because the job never changed, only the specimens did. The watchable behaviour is the hand staying still while the world changes around it: the same precise rendering, the same dark ground, the same jewel-bright fruit, while the subjects cycle from citron to pears to cauliflower to cockatoo to sunflower, and the painter, at 73 and 75, paints as he painted at 32. **Takeaway:** style constant, subject moving -- sixty years of the same scientific accuracy, the evolution in the commission and the specimen, never in the hand, the control case for style as instrument.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Formation: figure painting, then Rome, 1661-c.1680. Born Friday 15 May 1648 at Settignano, son of Nicolo. About 1661 he entered the workshop of Lorenzo Lippi and stayed until the master's death in 1665; then apprentice to Onorio Marinari. The older scholarship (Lanzi onward, and the Web Gallery of Art) also lists Mario de' Fiori (Mario Nuzzi) and Angelo (Agnolo) Gori as teachers; the DBI (Trkulja 1968) explicitly corrects this: with Gori and de' Fiori the relationship was one of collegiality, not pupilage (see C2). In 1670 he travelled to Rome with Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici for the conclave that elected Clement X; in Rome he was in contact with Mario de' Fiori. His training fitted him for figure painting, and the only alternative to flowers, fruit and animals in his oeuvre are portraits (DBI): he painted portraits in Rome and frescoes for the Ubaldini family in Florence, but the figure-painting side of his work is still largely unrecovered -- "resta pero ancora da recuperare il B. pittore di figura" (DBI). No named early figure painting was found in any leg.

Phase 2 -- The object study, 1680-1690. The earliest firmly dated work found is already a still life, and already a specimen portrait: "Turkish Arms" (1680, Uffizi), Ottoman weaponry and armour rendered with cold reflective metal -- proof his precision extended beyond the organic. Then the "Large Citron in a Landscape" (1690, 60 x 66 cm), a single oversized citron isolated against a sweeping landscape: the specimen portrait in its purest form, one fruit, atmospheric vista. Small-to-medium scale, single subject, the specimen as hero.

Phase 3 -- The census, from 1685, peak 1690s. The Medici commission for the Villa Medicea L'Ambrogiana and the Villa Medicea della Topaia turns the single portrait into the inventory: two compositions "Basket of Grapes", four "Basket of Citrus Fruits", and series of cherries, plums, figs and peaches (all Palazzo Pitti); "Pears" (1699) with 115 types; "Citrus Fruits" with 34 varieties; "Giant Carnations" (1699); "Melon of Ponte a Cappiano" (1694). Scale jumps to wall-filling canvases; the new devices appear -- numbered specimens, an inscription or scroll listing scientific names, plant-motif frames. The painting becomes a database with a picture attached. He was also author of the "Medici Citrus" collection (Italian Wikipedia).

Phase 4 -- The monstrosity, 1706-1716. "Cauliflower and Wild Radishes" (1706, 87.6 x 118.1 cm), a freak cauliflower and a deformed radish harvested in Tuscany in 1706 and rushed to Cosimo III; "Giant Pumpkin of the St. Francis Convent" (1711); the two-headed calf and lamb; the animal "portraits" for the Villa Ambrogiana, a roughly 100-picture series with Pietro Neri Scacciati, from living and stuffed animals in the Medici menagerie and the Serraglio degli animali rari; the Moluccan cockatoo, delivered 6 October 1716. The subject narrows again to the single freak specimen -- but now the freakness is the point, and the inscription records date, place of discovery, weight and dimensions. The cockatoo is the animal equivalent of the 1690 citron: one pet, one portrait, delivered on a recorded date.

Phase 5 -- Late narrowing, 1721-1723. Life-size "Sunflower" (1721, age 73), "Olive Tree from the Garden of Cardinale del Giudice" (1723, age 75). Still life-size, still precise, still named individuals. He dies in 1729 aged 80, working to the end; the animal series outlived Cosimo III (d. 1723) and continued under Gian Gastone into the 1730s via Scacciati.

Phase 6 -- Death and afterlife, 1729 onward. Dismissed from Lanzi to Marangoni as a mediocre flower painter, pupil of Mario de' Fiori and Angelo Gori. Rediscovered by Giuseppe Delogu ("Un nuovo piccolo maestro: B. B.", Emporium, 1960) and by Mina Gregori's attributions in the 1964 "La natura morta italiana" catalogue (including the two-headed animals, previously Scacciati). The 1998 monograph (Trkulja and Tongiorgi Tomasi, 223 pp., 140 paintings assembled chronologically). 2002: one of three focus artists in the National Gallery of Art's "The Flowering of Florence: Botanical Art for the Medici" (3 March-27 May 2002, 68-70 works), with Ligozzi and Garzoni. 2006-08: "Natura Morta: Still-Life Painting and the Medici Collections" -- 11 of 42 works were Bimbi's, the most visible painter in the show. 2007: the Museo della Natura Morta opens at the Villa Medicea di Poggio a Caiano; two large rooms are dedicated to him, and his paintings moved there since 1990 for their scientific documentation value. 2008: a solo exhibition "Stravaganti e bizzarri" at Poggio a Caiano, 11 May-20 July 2008, is attested only by the German Wikipedia in this leg and is not independently verified (see C8). Today his paintings are used by botanists and zoologists to study extinct varieties and species; about 30 of his canvases hang in the Botany Section of the University of Florence Natural History Museum.

What changed: subject (single specimen to census to monstrosity to single named individual); scale (60 x 66 cm to wall-filling 174 x 233 cm); the commission (from general still life to the Medici catalogue project from 1685). What stayed the same: oil on canvas throughout; scientific accuracy as the style; life-size scale as the claim; the Medici court as the whole world of the work; the numbered label as the device.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is the painting as a record that refuses to be decoration. Cosimo III wanted a visual catalogue of Tuscan flora -- "comprehensive visual and textual catalogues of Tuscan flora" (Schmiedel), where "aesthetics and curiosity seemed to go hand in hand with a demonstration of knowledge and prosperity" -- and Bimbi delivered identification-grade rendering at life size, numbered, inscribed, and framed in accurately painted plant motifs. The "gardens on canvas and paper simultaneously acted as representatives of taste and erudition, of possession and the geographical reach of the grand ducal influence" (Schmiedel). That is the whole logic in one sentence: the picture proves the Grand Duke owns the garden, knows its names, and can show it. The census wall proves abundance -- 115 pears, 34 citrus, cherries by the dozen -- and the monstrosity portrait proves reach: the two-headed calf brought by peasants, the ocelot from an explorer, the cockatoo from the Indies, all of it arriving at the court and being recorded. The theatrical backgrounds are the court dress on the record: the velvet curtain, the colonnade, the trellis, the summer sky, four sets that say this is a Grand Duke's catalogue, not a gardener's notebook. And then the flower pieces break the logic open: small, melancholy, unnumbered, in the manner of van Aelst, the one place the cataloguer stops counting and paints a mood. The logic holds because the exception proves it -- the flowers are the only pictures where he is not working for the catalogue, and they are the only ones that reveal a personality. Under Cosimo III, Reeds writes, "the art of the still-life moved from Garzoni's intimate scale to the grandiose, and from the scientific to the horticultural and culinary. Bartholomeo Bimbi's huge oil paintings celebrate (sometimes with unconscious humor -- see the monster horse-radish in pl. 64) Tuscany's astonishing abundance of fruits and vegetables." The abundance is the subject, the catalogue is the form, the life-size scale is the proof, and the monstrosity is the joke the catalogue tells about itself: nature, counted, outdoes the counter.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C1. DEATH YEAR -- 14 JANUARY 1729, WITH A 1730 VARIANT. The DBI (Trkulja 1968) gives 14 January 1729; the English and Italian Wikipedias and the Library of Congress authority record agree. The 1730 date appears in the French, Polish and Swedish Wikipedias, in the Schmiedel (2016) abstract ("1648-1730"), in the 2007 Natura Morta checklist and on many commercial sites; an old English Wikipedia revision gives 1723, and Farquhar (1855) gives 1725. VERDICT: use 14 January 1729, the day of Lorenzo Fratellini's 39th birthday, with the 1730 variant noted as a widespread error.
- C2. TEACHERS -- THE DBI CORRECTS THE OLDER LIST. Older scholarship (Lanzi onward, and the Web Gallery of Art) lists Mario de' Fiori (Mario Nuzzi) and Angelo (Agnolo) Gori as Bimbi's teachers. The DBI (Trkulja 1968) explicitly corrects this: with Gori and de' Fiori the relationship was one of collegiality ("colleganza"), not pupilage. VERDICT: use the DBI version -- Lippi, then Marinari -- and mention the older claim only as superseded.
- C3. TEACHER ORDER CONFLICTS BETWEEN SOURCES. The English Wikipedia gives Lippi, then Marinari, then Rome with Nuzzi; the Web Gallery of Art gives Rome with Mario dei Fiori, THEN Lippi, Marinari and Gori. The two orders cannot both be right. VERDICT: use the DBI/English Wikipedia order (Lippi c.1661-65, Marinari, Rome 1670), and flag the Web Gallery of Art's order as a conflict, not a rival fact.
- C4. FERDINAND II EMPLOYMENT IS UNVERIFIED. The Web Gallery of Art names Ferdinand II (reigned 1621-1670) as an employer as well as Cosimo III; the DBI does not mention Ferdinand II. Bimbi was 22 when Ferdinand II died, in the same year as the Rome trip. VERDICT: treat it as a secondary-source claim, not verified in the DBI, and do not build on it.
- C5. LIGOZZI DATES -- 1547-1626, NOT 1541-1587. The Antiques and the Arts article gives Ligozzi 1547-1626; the NGA exhibition page gives 1541-1587, which looks like a typo. Leg 3 also flags birth 1549 and death 1626 as variants. VERDICT: use 1547-1627 with a flag, or omit exact dates; never use 1541-1587.
- C6. THE LARGE CITRON'S UFFIZI LOCATION IS UNCONFIRMED. "Large Citron in a Landscape" (1690, 60 x 66 cm) is listed as Uffizi only by commercial and reproduction sites (wahooart, artsdot); no official Uffizi page was found in any leg. VERDICT: name the work, give the date and dimensions, and flag the location as unconfirmed on an official page.
- C7. "ACRYLIC ON CANVAS" IS AN ERROR -- IGNORE COMMERCIAL-SITE TECHNIQUE CLAIMS. Wahooart calls the 1690 Large Citron "acrylic on canvas" and offers an uncorroborated "glazing technique" paragraph; artsdot, wikioo and niceartgallery make similar specific paint-handling claims. The reliable sources (DBI, WGA, NGA) describe accuracy, scale and labelling, not brushwork or layering. VERDICT: oil on canvas throughout; no glazing, chiaroscuro or layering claim is made in this study, because none is corroborated.
- C8. THE 2008 SOLO SHOW "STRAVAGANTI E BIZZARRI" IS ATTESTED ONLY BY THE GERMAN WIKIPEDIA. Villa Medicea di Poggio a Caiano, 11 May-20 July 2008, appears in the German Wikipedia only in this leg. VERDICT: mention it with the flag, and do not treat it as independently verified.
