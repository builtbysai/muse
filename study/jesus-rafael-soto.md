# Jesus Rafael Soto: Study Notes

**Site:** Jesus Rafael Soto, born 5 June 1923 at Ciudad Bolivar, Venezuela, died 14 January 2005 at Paris, aged 81 -- Venezuelan kinetic artist, the man who made the viewer the motor and then invited the viewer's whole body inside the work: serial repetitions that break composition down, Plexiglas planes that moire as you walk past, wire tangles that vibrate against striped grounds, rods that tremble on nylon threads in the air, free wire "writing" against strict striation, and from 1967 the Penetrables -- floor-to-ceiling curtains of hanging rods and tubes that you push through, that brush your body and ring like rain, until the work is not an object but a space you are inside. The eldest of four children of a violin player, he painted cinema posters at sixteen, trained in Caracas beside Cruz-Diez and Otero, directed the art school at Maracaibo, and arrived in Paris in 1950 on a scholarship, where he stayed for the rest of his life, taking part in Le Mouvement at Galerie Denise Rene in 1955, refusing MoMA's Responsive Eye in 1965, and building the Museo de Arte Moderno Jesus Soto in his home town in 1973 (C5401, C5402, C5403, C5404, C5405, C5406, C5408, C5409, C5455, C5456, C5463).

## The life: the poster painter who made the viewer the motor

Soto was born on 5 June 1923 in Ciudad Bolivar, "an old colonial town on the edge of the virgin forest on the banks of the Orinoco", the eldest of four children of Emma Soto and Luis Garcia Parra, a violin player (C5401, C5402, C5404). He wanted to help support his family, and art was the most interesting route: he picked up the guitar and recreated famous artworks from books and magazines, and at sixteen began his serious artistic career painting posters for the cinemas of Ciudad Bolivar (C5402, C5405, C5406). In 1938 he joined a student group affiliated with surrealist ideas and published poems in the local journal that scandalised society, learning automatic writing and charcoal drawing; a petition and the bishop's involvement followed, and in Soto's own quoted words, "I got a scholarship" (C5402). The scholarship took him to Caracas in 1942, to the Escuela de Artes Plasticas y Aplicadas, where he finished in 1947 with a teaching degree; the director, Antonio Edmundo Monsanto, brought the latest from the avant-garde to his students, including cubism, and among Soto's fellow students were Carlos Cruz-Diez and Alejandro Otero (C5403, C5408). On entering the school he saw a Braque still-life reproduction and "the color started to separate off the form" -- an experience he linked to multiplicity of viewpoints (C5403). From 1947 to 1950 he directed the Escuela de Bellas Artes at Maracaibo, held his first exhibition at the Taller Libre de Arte in Caracas in 1949, and on Otero's encouragement, "intrigued by the works of Malevich and Mondrian", applied for and won a government scholarship to Paris (C5403, C5404, C5405, C5406).

Paris in 1950 was the decisive break, and he never left it. On arrival he encountered the members of Los Disidentes -- the group founded in Caracas in March 1950 -- and attended Leon Degand's lectures at the Atelier d'Art Abstrait; he exhibited in the Salon des Realites Nouvelles and made the acquaintance of Iris Clert, Denise Rene, Vasarely, Yves Klein, Jean Tinguely, Pol Bury and Daniel Spoerri (C5404). In France he discovered Paul Klee and Piet Mondrian, and Mondrian suggested the idea of "dynamizing the neoplasticism", in association with Yaacov Agam, Jean Tinguely, Victor Vasarely and others connected with the Salon and the Galerie Denise Rene (C5403). His early Paris work moved from post-impressionism through cubism to experiments with optical phenomena; in 1953 he explored three-dimensionality by superimposing two layers of painted Plexiglas that appeared to move when the viewer moved (C5403, C5404). In 1955 he took part in Le Mouvement at Galerie Denise Rene, 6-30 April, with Agam, Duchamp and Vasarely -- "the exhibition widely credited with launching Kinetic Art" (C5403, C5405, C5412) -- which prompted the Yellow Manifest about kinetic art and visual research involving spectators and stimulus; the official site adds that he "exhibited with the ZERO Group, who were also exploring the notion of the immaterial" (C5404). That phrase is the limit of the evidence: no source read says he joined or co-founded ZERO, Los Disidentes, or any group, and he must not be called a member or co-founder of any of them (C5404, C5505, C5511).

The breakthrough came in two steps. In 1957 he produced the first of his Vibrations -- a tangle of metal wires or materials found in the street set against a striped black-and-white ground to create a moire effect (C5404) -- and in 1958 he made two kinetic murals for the Venezuelan Pavilion at the Brussels World's Fair (C5406, C5416). The prizes followed: the National Painting Prize of Venezuela in 1960, the Wolf Prize at the Sao Paulo Bienal in 1963, the David E. Bright Foundation Award at the XXXII Venice Biennale in 1964, first prize at the first Salon Panamericano de Pintura in Cali in 1965 (C5405, C5406, C5414, C5418). In 1965 MoMA's advance press release for The Responsive Eye listed Soto among the artists to be shown -- and he refused to take part. Tate Papers states it plainly: "fellow Venezuelan Jesus Soto refused to exhibit his works at MoMA" in that show, while Cruz-Diez participated; ArtReview agrees, "he refused to participate in moma's fabled 1965 exhibition The Responsive Eye" (C5408, C5409). The refusal is the most important correction in this study: the #616 future-candidates line's "both in The Responsive Eye (1965)" is wrong on Soto's half, and leg 3's own network section repeats the error twice -- Soto was announced, not exhibited, and the timing and reason for the refusal are not established (C5552). His own official group-shows list carries no Responsive Eye entry in the 1965 section read (C5412).

In 1967 he produced his first Penetrables, "works composed of metal rods and nylon strands hanging in space", inviting the viewer to enter the work and walk around inside it (C5404, C5419, C5409), and showed in Lumiere et Mouvement, Art Cinetique a Paris, at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, May-August 1967 (C5412). The same year he made Volume suspendu for the Venezuelan Pavilion at Expo 67 in Montreal (C5416). The retrospectives came in sequence: Kunsthalle Bern 1968, the Stedelijk Museum Amsterdam 1968 or 1969 (the year is disputed, Q5), the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris 1969, where he presented a Penetrable (C5404, C5405, C5406). In 1969 the Venezuelan government created the Jesus Soto Foundation, and four years later the Museo de Arte Moderno Jesus Soto opened in Ciudad Bolivar -- built with his architect friend Carlos Raul Villanueva, opened on 25 August 1973 by President Rafael Caldera, filled with Soto's own private collection, which he had donated to his native city (C5404, C5406, C5413). The Guggenheim retrospective in New York followed in 1974 (its own image caption says 1973, Q2), then the Centre Pompidou in 1979, the Jeu de Paume retrospective in 1997, and the French Grand Prix National de Sculpture in 1995 (C5404, C5405). He died in Paris on 14 January 2005, aged 81; ten days later the first Brazilian retrospective of his work opened in Rio de Janeiro, and the travelling Vision en movimiento went on to Mexico, Argentina and Bergamo the same year (C5401, C5404, C5406). His last work, the Houston Penetrable, commissioned in 2004, was completed after his death and installed in 2014 (C5456).

## M1 -- Serial repetition on the flat plane: the fragment of an infinite structure

The first mechanism is painted only, and nothing in it moves. Repetition optique No. 2 (W01, 1951, wood and enamel, Museo Soto) repeats formal elements across the plane to depersonalise the work and reveal "the relativity of the vision"; the work is conceived as a fragment of an infinite reality, repeatable without varying its essential structure, with no beginning, end, up, down, right or left (C5451). Dos cuadrados en el espacio (W02, 1953) retakes Malevich, adopting the square as the "only valid form" (C5452). The vibration here is entirely in the repetition: the eye reads the same element again and again until composition and equilibrium break down, and the panel stops being a picture and starts being a sample cut from something endless. No image of either work was obtained; both rest on prose (C5554).

**Takeaway:** repeat one element until the composition gives up. A fragment of an infinite structure vibrates without anything moving.

## M2 -- Superimposed Plexiglas planes: the eye is the only motor

In 1953 Soto layered two or more transparent Plexiglas sheets, each painted with straight or curved drawings, so the drawings changed how they were seen as the spectator moved -- the first spatial effect on a plane surface (C5404, C5452). Desplazamiento de un cuadro transparente (W03, 1953-54) is the type work; Permutacion (W05, 1956) is the first production of dematerialisation in the retina, the superposition of different planes making the solid body "break out" and dematerialise (C5453); Estructuras cineticas de elementos geometricos (W06, 1955-57) adds colour to the layered planes, previously relegated in his research (C5453). Spirale (W04, 1955/1959) is the mechanism made legible: a white spiral printed on Plexiglas mounted a couple of inches above a support bearing a black spiral, so that as the viewer moves, the spirals intersect at different points -- the moire effect, an interference pattern from overlaying similar but slightly offset templates (C5459). Soto was inspired by Duchamp's Rotoreliefs but said: "I wanted to do something similar, but without a motor... as a motor, I have only ever used the eye" (C5459). The viewed thumbnail shows a square panel of dense concentric spiral lines, black on white; the mechanism is the spectator's own displacement, and the work is static. No motor, no moving part; the spectator is the mechanics.

**Takeaway:** layer painted transparencies and let the viewer do the moving. The eye is the only motor the work is allowed.

## M3 -- The Vibration: a wire tangle against stripes, purely optical

The canonical early black-and-white vibration work is Premiere Vibration (W07, 1957, paint on wood and metal, private collection): a tangled mass of wires juxtaposed against a background of black-and-white vertical stripes, so the viewer's movement creates a shift in the visual effect (C5458). The wires are fixed; Soto insists the vibration "is not felt as something tangible... it is purely optical, without physical substance" (C5458). The viewed thumbnail shows a pale white-framed panel with a faint tangle of thin wires on a light ground; at thumbnail size the striping is barely resolvable, but the wire tangle is visible. In Vibracion pura (1960) the irregular background itself becomes camouflage for twisted metal rods suspended in front of it, so the rods seem to emerge from the support rather than float before it (C5470). The mechanism is the difference between M2 and M3: in M2 the layers are painted and parallel; here the foreground is a real tangle, chaotic against a rigid ground, and the vibration is the eye failing to decide which layer it is looking at.

**Takeaway:** fix a tangle in front of stripes and move the viewer, not the work. The vibration is optical only -- nothing in it has any physical substance.

## M4 -- Suspended rods on nylon threads: the air does the moving

Cardinal (W12, 1965, Tate) is the first catalogue work where the elements genuinely move on their own. A vertically oriented rectangular black backboard, its regularly spaced grooves painted alternately black and purple with fine white lines between them, holds 45 steel rods suspended from a single nylon thread at various off-horizontal angles; the rods are painted black on one side and purple on the other, and they move with shifts in environmental air currents, creating subtle optical illusions through interaction with the painted, textured backdrop (C5460). The viewed thumbnail shows a tall dark vertical panel with fine horizontal lines and rod traces; the 45 rods and their angles are not individually resolvable at that size. The title may allude to cardinal purple robes and/or the cardinal directions (C5460). From 1962 Soto's main focus became paintings with suspended bars, fixed plaques and other materials (C5470); the Agujas (Needles) series of 1961-62 is the same family (C5457). The mechanism changes the whole system: in M2 and M3 the viewer supplies the motion; here the room's own air supplies it, and the viewer may also set rods swinging by passing. The work is alive when nobody is looking at it.

**Takeaway:** hang light rods on threads and let the air move them. The first mechanism where the work itself moves, and the viewer is only one more draught in the room.

## M5 -- Escritura: free wire writing against strict striation

The Escrituras begin in 1958 -- Escritura (W09, 1958) and Muro de Bruselas (W10, 1958) together "already contain all the elements that will be developed later" (C5454) -- and run through Ecriture (W11, 1964, thin threads of steel hanging in front of a background of vertical lines, Nasjonalmuseet Oslo) to Escritura Hurtado (W17, 1975, paint, wire and nylon cord on wood, 68 x 40 x 18 in., Art Museum of the Americas) (C5470, C5462). The formula: new wire bent into unpredictable, handwriting-like curves, suspended before an evenly painted ground of straight vertical lines (C5469). The regular rhythmic background evokes Bach-like mathematical precision; the free-flowing wire "writing" evokes experimental modern music (C5462). The surface "appears to reveal a mysterious script. A message forms but then visually recedes in a vertical forest of painted lines" (C5462): move closer and the curving lines dance into the foreground; at the bottom the wires have a physical presence that defines space, but against the striations their materiality dissipates and space becomes ambiguous, indefinite (C5462). The viewed thumbnail of W17 shows a dark panel with dense vertical striations and pale curving wire traces at the bottom. The mechanism is a counterpoint, not a contrast: order and improvisation in the same plane, and the viewer, by approaching or retreating, chooses which register dominates.

**Takeaway:** write free curves over strict lines. The message forms as you approach and recedes as you retreat -- the viewer tunes the counterpoint.

## M6 -- Freestanding rod frames: the drawing becomes a thing

Estructura Cinetica (W08, 1957) is the decisive step from drawn frame to real element: the frames that had previously only been drawn on Plexiglas become real metal rods welded between frames, and Soto's works become real spatial objects that visitors are able to penetrate (C5454). The viewed image is a freestanding open cube-frame of vertical rods -- blue, black and silver panels with diagonal rod patterns -- standing outdoors on legs at the Universidad Central de Venezuela; the file is titled "Pre-Penetrable (1957)" and is presented here as a 1957 freestanding rod structure of the Estructura Cinetica type, NOT asserted to be the same individual object (C5454). The viewer now walks around a cage of rods rather than standing before a panel; the "drawing" has a back and an inside, and its diagonal and vertical patterns change with every step. This is the bridge from wall relief to environment, and the direct ancestor of the Penetrable: once the frame is real and freestanding, there is nothing stopping the viewer from stepping through it.

**Takeaway:** build the drawn frame in welded rod and stand it in space. The drawing is now a thing with an inside, and the viewer walks around it.

## M7 -- The Penetrable: a curtain you walk through, that rings like rain

The breakthrough, and the mechanism this study turns on. In 1967 Soto produced his first Penetrables, and the idea had emerged in 1966-67 by multiplying the rods until they covered the whole space and became an autonomous work (C5404, C5463). The first was small -- "not more than a meter wide" -- shown at Galerie Denise Rene in May-June 1967: a floor-to-ceiling metal Penetrable with wire rods that were unpainted, "which gave them a very beautiful silvery color", and when you entered, "since they were metal rods, they produced a sound like... an enveloping sound, like that of rain" (C5463, C5455). French critic Jean Clay was the first to call these works Penetrables -- "to get into", "to walk through" -- and Soto adopted the term (C5455). No image of the 1967 original was obtained; the viewed photograph is Soto himself standing inside the Petit penetrable of 1969 at Zagreb, his face partly obscured by the white strands (C5464). Three senses are engaged at once: sight (the mass dissolves into strands and light), touch (the tubes brush the body; "figures appear vague, one can no longer trust the sense of distance... the light touch of the moving tubes" startles), and sound (metal rods ring like rain when entered; PVC tubes muffle sound) (C5463). Soto: "When you enter a Penetrable, you have the sensation of being in a light swirl, a total plenitude of vibrations... I make people move and make them feel the body of space" (C5455). Murale Panoramico Vibrante Sonoro (W14, 1968) is the sonorous variant: uniquely among his free-standing murals, music is integral -- when the mural is touched, the vibrations of the metal rods become sound and the piece becomes a sonorous work (C5469). Volume Suspendu (W15, 1968, Centre Pompidou) is the hinge between Vibrations and Penetrables, a wall panel of painted lines with a blue volume of rods and a black volume of rods, in which "the eye panics and loses its bearings" (C5461). The mature form is Penetrable (W18, 1982, Museo Reina Sofia, 485 x 787 x 867 cm, painted wood and silicone rubber thread): a vast rectangular volume of thousands of fine vertical threads hung from a steel ceiling grid, seen at full resolution as a huge translucent curtain fading to a pale haze at the far end (C5467). Penetrable BBL Bleu (W19, 1999, edition Avila 2007) is the late, colour-saturated form: hundreds of thin vertical blue PVC tubes, the visitor walking through and immersed in its kinetic and optical functions (C5465, C5464). The work is not an object but "an idea of space that can materialize in any situation and at any scale... you could even make it cover the whole planet" (C5455). Serpentine counts more than seventy versions (C5512); MFAH counts 25 to 30 different Penetrables over his career (C5456) -- versions against distinct designs, not a disagreement.

**Takeaway:** hang thousands of vertical elements floor to ceiling and let the visitor in. Sight, touch and sound at once -- the work is a space, not an object.

## M8 -- Virtual volumes: a solid made of nothing but rods and colour

The last mechanism makes a solid out of air. In the Progressions, aerial forms protrude from floor or ceiling to meet in a kinetic sequence; in Virtual Volumes, vertical works suggest large geometric forms suspended in air (C5467). Two sub-devices. Rod-length grading: rods of different heights outline a sphere or dome that exists only as an envelope -- Sphere Lutetia (1996, painted iron and aluminium, 600 x 600 x 600 cm) is a sphere made only of hanging rods, solid from a distance, nothing up close (C5467); Duomo centro rosso (1997) is the same device at table scale (C5467). Colour-coding: the Houston Penetrable (W20, 2004-14) hangs 24,000 individually hand-painted and tied PVC tubes 28 feet from ceiling to floor across 2,600 square feet of Cullinan Hall, each tube assigned a specific colour code so a huge yellow ellipse appears to float in a clear field -- the illusion of an object suspended in the middle of the installation (C5456). It is the only Penetrable Soto designed as permanent, and one of the few intended for indoor display, completed after his death by Atelier Soto with architect Paolo Carrozzino (C5456). The viewed thumbnail shows the glowing yellow ellipse floating in a field of fine vertical strands. What moves is the viewer walking around or through the form, which assembles and dissolves with viewpoint; nothing in the form itself is motorised.

**Takeaway:** grade the rods, or code the colours, and a solid appears where there is nothing. The volume is virtual -- it exists only in the viewer's assembly of it.

## The aesthetic logic: dematerialisation as hospitality

Soto's logic is the opposite of the monument and the opposite of the picture, and it runs in one direction for fifty years: from the solid, toward the immaterial, toward the viewer. It starts with a painted square repeated until the composition breaks down (M1), moves to painted layers that only move when the viewer moves (M2), to a wire tangle whose vibration has no physical substance at all (M3), to rods that tremble on the air (M4), to writing that forms and recedes as you approach (M5), to a drawn frame built in real rod that you can walk around (M6), to a curtain you walk through (M7), to a solid that is not there (M8). At every step something real is taken away -- the motor, the surface, the substance, the object -- and something is handed to the viewer: first the eye, then the body, then the whole person, inside the work. "As a motor, I have only ever used the eye" is the early form of the rule; "I make people move and make them feel the body of space" is the late form, and they are the same rule. Against Le Parc the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Le Parc's light waits for the viewer and the viewer activates it; Soto's line vibrates as the viewer moves and then the viewer walks inside the vibration. The same Paris, the same decade, the same Denise Rene circle -- and the motor moved from the spectator's eye to the spectator's body. The hospitality is the point: the Penetrable is not a sculpture you admire but a space that receives you, brushes you, rings around you, and lets you out changed. He refused the Responsive Eye, the show that would have made him an Op artist in a museum's terms, and built instead a museum in his home town that holds the work of the whole constructive, Op and kinetic circle -- the refusal and the gift are the same gesture: the work belongs to the person who enters it, not to the room that frames it.
