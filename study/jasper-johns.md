## M1. The familiar thing made strange

Take an image so familiar it is seen and not looked at, a flag, a target, a number, a letter, a map, and paint it exactly, and the exactness is what makes it strange, because the eye recognises the thing in a second and then has nowhere to go, and the remaining looking is about surface, edge, and colour, and the Getty description is the mechanism stated once: things the mind already knows, and The Art Story adds that the images are seen and not looked at, and the evidence is Flag, 1954 to 1955, Target with Four Faces, 1955, and Map, 1961, and Johns's own rule is that the motif is chosen because it is already designed, the flag's stripes and stars, the target's rings, the numbers' grid, and he says, what is interesting to me is the fact that it isn't designed, but taken, it is not mine, and the watchable behaviour is that recognition completes instantly and looking starts after it, and a piece built on this mechanism should let the viewer recognise first and look second. **Takeaway:** take an image everyone already knows and paint it exactly, so recognition finishes in a second and the looking that follows is about the surface, not the subject. Against the chain: Rauschenberg (#395) also started from the given, but his given is an object found on the street, a goat, a tire, an eagle, and Johns's given is an image found in the mind, a flag, a target, a number, and the difference is between admitting a thing and reworking a sign, and Twombly (#394) also painted a familiar mark, but his is a scribble the hand makes, and Johns's is a sign the culture made, and the difference is between a mark that is yours and a sign that is everyone's.

## M2. Encaustic as skin

## M12. The dumb system: crosshatch as literalness without meaning

In 1972 Johns hit on the crosshatch, a motif he would explore for a decade, and traditionally crosshatch creates shadow gradation, and Johns divorced it from that use, repeating it in primary colours as a pulsating abstract composition, and Johns said, I only saw it for a second, but knew immediately that I was going to use it, it had all the qualities that interest me, literalness, repetitiveness, an obsessive quality, order with dumbness, and the possibility of a complete lack of meaning, and the evidence is Corpse and Mirror II, 1974, Scent, 1973 to 1974, Usuyuki, 1979 to 1981, Dancers on a Plane, 1979 and 1980 to 1981, and Between the Clock and the Bed, 1981, 1982 and 1983, and the crosshatch origin is told two ways, the passing car and a pattern on a polished stone found in a South African cave, both Johns's own tellings at different times, and the study records both and does not pick one silently, see C21, and the watchable behaviour is that lines repeat until they stop meaning anything and start meaning everything, the eye cannot rest, the pattern vibrates, and the title, Corpse and Mirror, Scent, Usuyuki, supplies the meaning the lines refuse, and the Philadelphia Museum of Art frames the crosshatch paintings from 1974 as Johns's first purely abstract canvases. **Takeaway:** repeat a dumb pattern, a hatch, until it stops meaning anything, and let the title supply the meaning the lines refuse.
