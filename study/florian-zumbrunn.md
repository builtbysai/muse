# Florian Zumbrunn: Study Notes

**Site:** Florian Zumbrunn (also Floz), a French creative developer and multimedia artist born in 1987, living and working between Tokyo and Paris; science baccalaureat (maths, physics), failed art-school entry, multimedia school, then Gobelins, l'ecole de l'image, Paris; two years as a creative technologist at Firstborn, New York, then freelance; co-founder of the Makio&Floz media-art studio (Paris/Tokyo, two years, projection mapping and street projections onto temples in Japan); moved to Tokyo late April 2017, solo generative-art practice after the studio closed; breakout 2023 (Art Blocks drop *Tout trace*, Verse drop *Scents of Spring*, two solo shows); 2024: *Odysseys* on Art Blocks x Bright Moments (13 February 2024, edition of 100), DISRUPT two-person show with Aleksandra Jovanic at Galerie Data (3-26 October 2024), Hennessy X.O 40 robot-painted decanters (December 2024). ## The life: the younger self, listened to late

The biography is unusually well documented because he tells it himself, at length, in two interviews: Bright Moments (21 March 2024, 108 lines) and CanvasRebel (92 lines), plus a Rock Badger curated interview (83 lines) and the Galerie Data DISRUPT press kit (560 lines). The study does not fill the gaps those interviews leave.

Florian Zumbrunn, born 1987. The birthplace Montmorency (a commune north of Paris) and the label "Franco-Swiss" appear only in the 100collectors directory profile; the press kit gives only "Born in 1987" and his own site calls him simply a "French artist" (C1778). As a teenager he wanted to be an illustrator or a painter, and began in traditional fine art, studying illustration and oil painting under the mentorship of an artist named Achot Achot, who introduced him to the history of art. His school background was scientific: a lot of math and physics during his baccalaureat in France. He wanted to join an art school but did not succeed in his first year of trying, and enrolled in a multimedia school instead.

The turning point came at a Flash Festival at the computer centre during that year. Flash was still big then. Erik Natzke spoke about his evolution from motion design to coding, using code to work more efficiently. Zumbrunn went home and began experimenting with code, "starting with drawing a square. I went from creating one square to two squares and three squares. Gradually, I progressed to animating them." He then joined Gobelins, l'ecole de l'image, in Paris, where he was "surrounded by amazing creative pairs, designer and developer," and his focus on coding grew stronger. No graduation year can be derived: CanvasRebel has him saying "13 years ago, I graduated from a school called 'Gobelins, l'ecole de l'image'" but the interview page shows no publication date (C1781).

After Gobelins he worked in advertising as a creative technologist, almost two years at Firstborn in New York (his portfolio also lists Make Me Pulse and Digitas), then freelance. "After a while I realized that when I first started to learn code, it was to make art. When I was a teenager, I wanted to become an illustrator, or a painter. I forgot this on the way. But then it struck me: I need to listen to my younger self." He switched career, travelled to Japan, and co-founded Makio&Floz, a media-art studio "situated between Paris and Tokyo." Living in Tokyo, they worked on physical installations involving projection mapping and webcams, and "even took our projectors to the streets, projecting onto temples and other buildings across Japan. This lasted two years, and then we decided to close the studio." His co-founder "Makio" is unidentified in every source read, and the studio years are unknown; the Tokyo move is dated late April 2017 on his own site (C1789).

After closing the studio he went solo to delve deeper into generative art, just as NFTs emerged: "Though I was a bit late to jump on the bandwagon, I soon realized platforms like ArtBlocks and fxhash were perfect platforms and ecosystems for generative art, allowing algorithms to truly express and reveal themselves." The base is stated inconsistently across sources -- between Tokyo and Paris (October 2024 press kit), "Based in Paris" (own site, 2023), "Based in Tokyo" (older portfolio) -- and no single current city is stated as fact here (C1779).

## The turn: 2022, fatherhood, and the grid

The turn has a date and a cause, and both are his own words. 2022 "came with a new birthday for me, the one of being a father," and *Foundations* grew out of questioning what it means to be a father and what values to transmit to his son. Leg 2's full read of the *Foundations* page confirms the framing (resolving L1-F6). "The foundations of an identity are laid in the early days of a child's life, shaped by parenting, environment, and experience." The series is grid-based -- dense or sparse -- with interferences and movement standing for the individuality in each of us, in a sketching, drawing, inky style that speaks to perpetual evolution and constant building. Development ran March to December 2022 and produced about 150 GB of exports across sizes and directions; he worked through three different series and returned to the "rigid" grid structure with a clear vision.

The grid is the decision the whole practice hangs on. *Foundations* is Identity chapter 1. *Tout trace* (Art Blocks Presents, 8 February 2023, edition of 300, animated) is chapter 2: "Tout trace" means "all mapped out" -- a ready-made path, a planned future -- and the series is the counterpoint, that life is full of surprises and each moment impacts us, that we are surrounded by expectations (society, culture, religion, parents, friends, family) but only our choices matter. The composition is again a grid -- "full of boxes expected to be filled" -- but the series is about growth, evolution, expression, and re-definition: fighting the structure, finding one's own voice, re-shaping reality. "It is finding its uniqueness and identity that will be revealed through the millions of lines drawing each artwork, technically signing the idea of 'Tout trace', that comes with a double meaning: 'All drawn'." The 2023 Art Blocks drop is presumed to be *Tout trace* -- the Bright Moments interview never names the series (C1782).

## The practice: code is the brush, the atelier finishes it

His working method is stated plainly and it is a pipeline, not a style. "My process is unique, I guess, because of the tool I use and the fact that I start in front of my computer but end in my atelier in front of a physical, tangible piece. Code is my main brush. It opens up endless ways of working, and mine is very iterative and introspective. ... The beautiful thing with code is that you can create an algorithm, give it a lot of parameters, and play with its components, like randomness. On top of that, I build my own tools to use and manipulate my algorithm. ... The algorithm becomes like a new member of the team. ... Then I export the image and print it, usually on watercolor paper. It has a strong texture that adds to the textures I added in my code. I leave the screen and go to my atelier, where I work with traditional tools: crayons, pastels, and oil pastels... I rediscover the artwork I have seen on screen so many times. The scale is different, the texture is different. ... Only then is the artwork finished and ready to be sold."

Three standing habits organise the practice. First, the concept evolves during creation rather than arriving fixed: "Most of the time, rather than having a completely clear predefined idea, he starts with a concept that evolves during creation: using code to give birth to algorithms, he iterates in an unlimited way. Exploration is put at the heart of his creative process." He likes "to let himself be surprised by the notion of randomness in creative programming." Second, the accident is kept: "If a small, unexpected 'mistake' occurs in the algorithm, I'm inclined to keep it, adding to the artwork's unique character." Third, the screen version is never the finished work: the print on watercolor paper, reworked by hand, is where the piece ends -- and the hand is a second author, not a finisher.

The stack is JavaScript and WebGL, with Three.js, PixiJS, Canvas, and MadMapper for the projection years. The themes are nature and seasons ("I work with the natural world because of what it represents: it always changes, it's never the same. Each season comes with its own colors, temperature, wind, and intensity, and with a shift in mood. I'm not the same person in summer as in winter"), memory and nostalgia ("I want people to look at my artworks and go back to their own memories, to connect with them through their own perspective"), identity and transition, and doubt as material ("In generative art, I feel that the place of 'doubt' is quite present. One of the advantages of this practice is to be able to generate tons of outputs during the same day. Your brain sees so many things from the same algorithm, that, sometimes, the next steps become blurry. One night, you can be sure of what you want to do, and the next morning only questions remain").

## The evolution: from the room to the grid to the tree

The arc has seven phases, and the constant is the pipeline: code generates, print fixes, hand finishes. What migrates is the structuring element -- grid, then shoji, then tree -- and the disruption, from accidental glitch to deliberate warp to the buyer's own palette choice.

Phase 1, the commercial technologist (2017-2020): as Floz and Makio&Floz, real-time WebGL, VJ, and projection mapping for brands and stages -- VEJA 360 degrees (a 13-minute sound-reactive sequence mapped around a full room at La Gaite Lyrique, 2018), Hublot, Call of Duty, Time Warner Cable, TikTok and Divimove, and the Noh theatre projection *Hell Says Noh* (c. 2019-2020). *Psykato* (c. 2017) is the private hinge: a personal photo series made with the same WebGL tools, ending as prints.

Phase 2, the first generative algorithm (2018-2020): *MOYO* starts as street projection mapping in Kanazawa and becomes a rule-based pattern generator inspired by kimono embroidery. The move from one-off installation to repeatable algorithm is the career's first turn. The site labels it "2018 - fxhash", which is impossible -- fxhash launched in November 2021 -- so 2018 is the algorithm's origin and the platform date is unverified (C1784).

Phase 3, identity and the grid (2022-2023): *Foundations* and *Tout trace* formalise the practice -- a strict grid, layered palettes, millions of lines, identity as something built inside a structure and then fought for.

Phase 4, the seasonal landscape cycle (2023): *Scents of Spring* (Verse, 17 April 2023), *Scents of Summer* (solo, Paris, July 2023), *Brise d'Automne* (solo, Bois-Colombes, December 2023) -- the grid softens into landscape; the code print is finished by hand in pastel and oil. Memory (Hanami, wisteria, Shirakawa-go, childhood summers) becomes the subject. Winter was planned for mid-to-late 2024 and the site still lists "Winter (TBD)"; no release is evidenced (C1787).

Phase 5, the storybook (2024): *Odysseys* (Art Blocks Engine + Bright Moments, 13 February 2024, edition of 100, animated) -- pixels, glitch, and childhood treasure maps; small algorithmic mistakes deliberately kept. The work gets playful and narrative where the Identity series was introspective.

Phase 6, disruption made explicit (2024): *Becoming* (fxhash, DISRUPT) turns the grid against itself with a warp map and GPU noise and displacement. *Doubts* (hand-enhanced prints) makes the same break physical -- watercolor and dry pastel over the print, doubt as subject.

Phase 7, memory, figuration, and the buyer's hand (2024-2025): Hennessy X.O externalises the hand entirely (a robotic arm paints a customer-parameterised design, 40 pieces); *Moments* (2025) replaces the abstract grid with the remembered shoji lattice; *Impressions* (Code+Matter, Paris, 21-26 October 2025) takes a small step toward figuration with algorithm-drawn trees holding blurred landscapes together. The image grows softer as the structure grows more organic, and the viewer is increasingly asked to complete it.

**Takeaway:** the evolution is a grid learning to let go. It starts as a room the audience stands inside, hardens into a structure the self must fight, softens into a landscape the hand finishes, and ends as a tree the viewer completes. The disruption was always there -- first as accident, then as warp, then as the buyer's choice.

## M1 -- The grid as structuring framework

The mechanism is a grid that decides where things may happen, and the work is what happens inside it. In *Foundations* the grid is dense or sparse, up to 41 palettes and up to 12 colour layers per work, with interferences and movement standing for individuality. In *Tout trace* the grid is "full of boxes expected to be filled" -- the expectations of society, culture, religion, parents, friends, family -- and the millions of lines are the self asserting itself against it. In *Becoming* the grid "determines spatial placement; elements render only when conditions are met (particular alignments of lines or colors)." In *Moments* the grid is no longer abstract at all: it is the lattice of a shoji door, remembered from four years in Japan, acting as windows into fragmented yet familiar landscapes.

The mechanism matters because the grid is never decoration; it is the argument. The same grid, across four series, is in turn a childhood, a set of expectations, a set of conditions, and a remembered room. Jovanic's line walks; Zumbrunn's grid holds. Her disruption fractures a line; his warps a grid. The grid is what makes the warp legible -- without the order, the disruption has nothing to break.

**Takeaway:** the grid is not a layout; it is the thing the work is about. A piece whose grid is only a scaffold is a diagram; a piece whose grid is a childhood, an expectation, a room, is a biography written in straight lines.

## M2 -- The warp: disruption by GPU noise and displacement

The mechanism is the best-documented pipeline in his whole practice, and it is the DISRUPT thesis made literal. *Becoming* proceeds in five stages: (1) a structuring grid determines spatial placement, elements rendering only when conditions are met; (2) an initial gray sketch establishes basic patterns; (3) colour is applied, the first lines customised with a warp map incorporating areas of distorted colour through a specific blending mode; (4) colours are selected and lines further customised per the deformation map, again via blending modes; (5) the final reveal uses the GPU -- a noise algorithm and displacement mapping -- to create distorted, stretched textures. "This final disruption generates a multitude of visual combinations that challenge and redefine the regularity of the underlying motif."

The subject is the in-between phase: "that delicate phase where everything changes -- the moment when childhood, still full of dreams and simplicity, starts to confront the complexities of adult life... each piece represents a collision of realities. Like two aquariums in friction, ideas, beliefs, and experiences confront one another until a crack forms, allowing things to blend, and a new balance emerges." Forms stretch, deform, and intertwine "reminiscent of micro-organisms... on a grid that's always changing." The press kit states the premise for the whole show: "any system, however regular and structured, can be destabilized by a disruptive intervention," and code is "a tool of construction, but also of subversion... a dialogue between apparent order and the possibility of controlled chaos."

**Takeaway:** the warp is not a filter applied at the end; it is the fifth stage of a pipeline whose first four stages built the order it breaks. A piece that warps a random field is noise; a piece that builds a grid, colours it, and then warps it on the GPU is a system breaking itself on purpose.

## M3 -- Code generates, print fixes, hand finishes

The mechanism is a pipeline with three authors in sequence: the algorithm, the printer, the hand. The algorithm generates and the artist iterates without limit, building his own tools to manipulate it. The image is exported and printed, usually on watercolor paper, whose texture adds to the textures already in the code. Then he leaves the screen and goes to the atelier, where crayons, pastels, and oil pastels rework the print -- and only then is the artwork finished. The *Doubts* prints are the extreme: Canson Aquarelle Arches 310 g paper, reworked with watercolor and dry pastel, unique pieces, 50 x 70 cm, the most physical and least digital endpoint of the pipeline. The seasonal prints are the same pipeline at full form: generative landscapes printed on paper and completed by hand in pastel and oil.

The mechanism matters because the hand is not a finisher; it is a second author with a different kind of knowledge. The code is precise and unlimited; the hand is imprecise and once-only. The dialogue between them -- "the precision of the code and the unpredictability of human gesture" -- is what makes each piece unique, and it is why the screen version is never the work. Whether the *Doubts* prints derive from the *Becoming* algorithm or a separate one is not stated anywhere read (C1786).

**Takeaway:** the print is not a copy of the screen; it is the second half of the work. A piece that stops at the export is a screenshot; a piece that goes through paper and pastel is finished twice, by two different hands.

## M4 -- The kept mistake

The mechanism is an error the artist chooses not to fix. On *Odysseys*: "If a small, unexpected 'mistake' occurs in the algorithm, I'm inclined to keep it, adding to the artwork's unique character." The series is built from pixel art and video-game glitch, Van Gogh's swirly waves, Japanese byobu panels and estampes, and childhood treasure maps; the kept mistakes sit inside that mix as part of the character, not as accidents. His stated keywords for the series are playfulness, surprise, and detail -- and the mistake is the surprise made permanent.

The mechanism belongs to a family across this chain: Jovanic owns the resize bug in her first fxhash trilogy ("it was so early no one really complained about it"), Ridgley keeps the sandy miscalculation in her brush ("this delicious, sandy texture in the paint"), Zancan releases a bugged forest. What distinguishes Zumbrunn's version is that it is a policy stated in advance, not a bug discovered after the fact: the mistake is kept because it is a mistake, and the keeping is the signature.

**Takeaway:** the mistake is not a flaw in the system; it is the proof the system is alive. A piece with no kept error is a demonstration; a piece with one kept error is a work with a memory of its own making.

## M5 -- The seasons as lived experience

The mechanism is a cycle timed to the body, not the calendar. *Scents of Spring* is an ode to spring and to the feelings and scents marking its arrival: Japanese Hanami and French wisteria, four distinct springs in Japan and a re-discovered French one, made as climate change alters what each season feels like. *Scents of Summer* is warmth, freedom, flourishing -- secluded Japanese villages such as Shirakawa-go and intimate childhood recollections, each canvas a page of a travel diary. *Brise d'Automne* is "a melancholic, colorful, and shifting autumn, a season that brings welcome freshness, reflection, and transformation" -- and the artist chose to wait for autumn to arrive before making the work, wanting to "fully immerse" in the season first. The process is timed to lived experience rather than the calendar.

The mechanism matters because the season is the palette and the subject at once: each season comes with its own colours, temperature, wind, intensity, and shift in mood, and the artist is not the same person in summer as in winter. The cycle is also honest about being unfinished: Winter was planned for mid-to-late 2024 and the site still lists "Winter (TBD)" -- the fourth chapter is a gap, not a pretence (C1787).

**Takeaway:** a season is not a palette; it is a duration the artist lived through. A piece that picks autumn colours in June is a costume; a piece that waits for autumn to arrive is a diary entry.

## M6 -- Proximity as interaction

The mechanism is the visitor as a variable. In *Symbiose* (UltraSuperNew Gallery, Harajuku, Tokyo, 2019), two interactive works invite the visitor to "make one with the art": observed from afar, the painting is one thing; as the visitor moves closer, the painting changes and the visitor merges into the digital artwork. The interactions are deliberately subtle, avoiding any gaming effect, aiming for an emerging, magical, poetic experience. The imagery bridges new media and traditional gesture: Japanese ink, brush strokes, Van Gogh-style iterations. The same philosophy runs through the commercial years: VEJA 360 degrees is a 13-minute sound-reactive sequence mapped around a full room, the audience standing inside the image; the room responds to sound in real time.

The mechanism matters because it states his interaction philosophy in full: subtle, meditative, never game-like. The viewer does not control the work; the viewer is composited into it. Proximity is the only input that earns interaction here -- the closer you stand, the more of you is in the picture.

**Takeaway:** interaction is not a control; it is a distance. A piece that asks the viewer to play is a game; a piece that changes as the viewer approaches is a painting that notices you.

## M7 -- The kaleidoscope from a photograph

The mechanism is a photograph folded into symmetry. *Psykato* (c. 2017) is a WebGL kaleidoscope engine: each visual starts from a photograph that mattered to the artist at some point, and the kaleidoscope multiplies and mirrors it into a new composition. Eight named visuals: Epiphany, Serendipity, Shinjuku, Friends, Jeju, Seoul, 2047, First visuals. The viewer sees mirrored, radial, photographic mandalas -- and the photo is still faintly legible inside the symmetry. The date is circa, resting on the embedded Vimeo video (a 2017 upload) and the work's position in the portfolio (C1796).

The mechanism matters because it is the earliest documented instance of his signature move: a digital process that ends as a physical print, and a personal memory -- a photo from a place, a friend, a trip -- transformed by a system into something the memory no longer resembles but still contains. The kaleidoscope does not erase the photograph; it multiplies it until the multiplication becomes the subject.

**Takeaway:** the kaleidoscope is not a filter; it is a memory folded until it becomes a pattern. A piece that mirrors a random image is a screensaver; a piece that mirrors a photograph that mattered is a souvenir the system made strange.

## M8 -- The buyer's hand

The mechanism is the customer as co-author. For Hennessy X.O (December 2024), the customer selects a colour palette in the Cognac boutique; the artist's algorithms generate a random composition from millions of potential variations; the customer previews it on a tablet and approves it at purchase; a state-of-the-art robotic arm (engineered by robotic expert Jean-Marie Ollivier, the experience conceived by agency OK C'EST COOL) then paints the design onto the paper-pulp "second skin" shell encasing the decanter, in front of the customer. Forty numbered pieces at 1,200 euros each (price single-sourced, C1794), each with a signed 60 x 100 cm fine-art matching print, available exclusively in Cognac. He travelled to Cognac earlier in 2024 to study the blending of eaux-de-vie and frames the parallel explicitly: blending cognac against blending code, pastel, watercolor, and machine output. "I blend the old with the new, human gestures with machine output, traditional techniques such as pastel and watercolor with lines of code."

The mechanism matters because it completes the migration of the hand across the whole practice: first the artist's hand finishes the print (M3), then the robot's arm paints the object, then the buyer's choice sets the palette. The hand leaves the artist entirely -- and the work is still his, because the algorithm is his and the range of millions of variations is his. A smaller version of the same mechanism sits inside *Tout trace*: press S to export a still, press H to open the artwork in HD -- the collector's keys as a quiet co-authorship.

**Takeaway:** the buyer is not a customer; the buyer is the last parameter. A piece that lets the buyer pick a colour is a customisation; a piece that lets the buyer set the seed while the arm paints is a collaboration with a stranger.

## The aesthetic logic

The logic is order, then disruption, then the hand. Every series in the practice is a regular system -- a grid, a season, a map, a decanter shell -- and every series contains a deliberate break in that system: the lines fighting the grid, the warp map stretching the colour, the kept mistake in the pixels, the buyer's palette choice, the hand's pastel over the print. The DISRUPT premise is not a show title; it is the practice stated once, in public, with Jovanic beside him: any system, however regular, can be destabilised, and code is a tool of construction and of subversion.

The second logic is the pipeline as identity. The work is not the algorithm and not the print; it is the passage between them -- code to export to paper to pastel -- and the passage keeps lengthening: the hand finishes the print, the robot paints the object, the buyer picks the palette, the viewer completes the blurred landscape. The artist's control retreats one step in each phase while the system stays his, because the system is the range of variation, not the single output.

The third logic is memory as the subject that survives every change of medium. The photographs in *Psykato*, the Hanami springs, the Shirakawa-go summers, the childhood treasure maps, the ryokan shoji doors, the blurred landscapes the viewer completes -- the practice keeps returning to moments the artist loved, and asking the viewer to supply their own. "I want people to look at my artworks and go back to their own memories."

**Takeaway:** the aesthetic is a system that breaks itself on purpose and a memory that the viewer finishes. A piece that is only orderly is a demonstration; a piece that is only broken is a mess; a piece that builds the order, breaks it on the GPU, and hands the remains to a hand or a stranger is Zumbrunn.
