# Michelangelo Buonarroti: Study Notes

**Site:** Michelangelo di Lodovico Buonarroti Simoni (6 March 1475, Caprese - 18 February 1564, Rome), sculptor, painter, architect and poet, the first Western artist to have a biography published in his lifetime (Vasari 1550; Condivi 1553) and the only major artist of the period to claim supremacy in all three visual arts on a single ground, disegno. Born at Caprese, where his father Lodovico was serving as podesta, he was nursed at Settignano by the wife of a stonecutter, was apprenticed to Domenico Ghirlandaio on 1 April 1488, and within a year was taken into Lorenzo de' Medici's household and the sculpture garden of San Marco under Bertoldo di Giovanni, where he carved the Madonna of the Stairs (c.1490-91) and the Battle of the Centaurs (c.1491-92) and had his nose broken by the fellow pupil Pietro Torrigiano. After Lorenzo's death in 1492 he carved a wooden Crucifix for Santo Spirito in exchange for the right to dissect corpses, fled Florence in 1494, worked in Bologna on the Arca di San Domenico, and reached Rome in June 1496, where the Bacchus (1496-97) and the Pieta (1498-99, his only signed work) made his name. Back in Florence he carved the David (1501-04) from a block abandoned for some forty years, painted the Doni Tondo (c.1507) and drew the Battle of Cascina cartoon (1504-06), then spent forty years on the tomb of Julius II (1505-45), a commission that shrank from forty statues to a wall tomb and produced the Moses and the Louvre Slaves. The Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508-12), painted largely alone and standing, not lying down, made him the school of modern painting; the Medici Chapel (1520-34) and the Laurentian Library (from 1524) made him the founding architect of Mannerism; the Last Judgement (1536-41) ended his twenty-year friendship with Sebastiano del Piombo over oil against fresco; and as architect of St Peter's from 1546 he returned the building to Bramante's centralised plan and left a wooden dome model that Giacomo della Porta built, raised and made more pointed, in 1588-90. He died in Rome aged 88, worked on the Rondanini Pieta six days before his death, and was buried in Santa Croce, Florence, his body smuggled out of Rome in a bale. He left more than 300 poems and some 500 letters, and was employed in succession by nine popes. ## M1. Disegno as the Common Root of All Three Arts
Disegno -- at once "drawing" and "design" -- is for Michelangelo the single intellectual act behind sculpture, painting and architecture, and the ground of his supremacy in all three. In his letter to Benedetto Varchi (1549, on the paragone debate over which art is nobler) he defines sculpture as "that which is made by taking away" and argues that painting and sculpture share one intelligence, disegno; earlier he had called sculpture the lamp of painting, as the sun is to the moon, and later changed his mind. The mechanism is visible in practice: the same figure is first fixed in a red-chalk drawing, then transferred to marble, fresco or stone with no loss of authority -- the David's pose is already complete in the drawings, the Sistine ignudi are drawn figures before they are painted ones, and the St Peter's dome is a drawing in wood before it is a building. Watchable behaviour: every medium shows the same hand -- the figure is designed first, the material second. Source: letter to Varchi 1549 (Milanesi, Le lettere, 1875); Dundas article on disegno; Britannica.

**Takeaway:** one intelligence, three materials; the drawing is the work and the medium is the execution.

## M2. Buon Fresco, Giornata and Cartoon Transfer
True fresco (buon fresco) means painting in water-based pigment on wet lime plaster (intonaco); as the plaster dries the lime carbonates and locks the pigment into the wall, so the colour is the wall, not a skin on it. Because the plaster must be painted while wet, the wall is laid in daily patches, each a giornata -- one day's work -- and the seams between patches are still visible on the Sistine ceiling. The design is transferred from a full-scale cartoon in two ways: at first by spolvero (pouncing) -- pricking the cartoon outline and dusting charcoal through the holes -- and later by punteggiato, direct incision with a small awl, and finally by freehand incision with a stylus as his confidence grew; Raphael used punteggiato too. Restoration counts imply roughly 570 giornate across the ceiling, about 300 in the first two years and 270 in the final year, which is itself evidence of how his speed and assurance changed. Detail in faces could be finished a secco (on dry plaster, per Britannica). Assistants ground colours and laid plaster; Michelangelo claimed to paint alone, and the giornata seams are the diary of where he actually stood. Watchable behaviour: look for the faint patchwork seams and the shift from pricked outlines (early lunettes, the Flood) to incised, almost drawn lines (the later prophets and sibyls). Source: Wikipedia "Sistine Chapel ceiling" and "Michelangelo"; Britannica; CiNii paper on the restoration.

**Takeaway:** the wall keeps a diary of the working days; the transfer method changes as the hand grows surer, and you can read the change in the plaster.

## M3. Non-Finito: Direct Carving, Front to Back
Michelangelo carved without the standard pointing machine. Most sculptors made a plaster model and marked the block up from it; he worked freehand, starting at the front of the block and working backwards, so the figure emerges as if surfacing from a pool of water -- Vasari's image, taken from Michelangelo's own method of laying a wax figure in a vessel of water and raising it gradually to see which parts appear first. The Accademia Prisoners show the system in four stages: the Awakening Slave (c.1520-23, 267 cm) is barely outlined, writhing in the block; the Young Slave (c.1530-34, 256 cm) is more defined, face just beginning to emerge, the rough tracks of large tooth chisels still visible at the back; the Bearded Slave (c.1530-34, 263 cm) is the most finished, hands and part of an arm still rough; the Atlas (c.1530-34, 277 cm) carries a weight whose head has not emerged from the stone at all. Point-chisel (subbia) marks are left as the final surface in the Battle of the Centaurs -- his first work without a bow drill. Watchable behaviour: the figure is legible from the front while the back is still raw block; the chisel grooves read as motion lines. CAVEAT (C89): the Prisoners were made for the Julius tomb and abandoned when the tomb was reduced, so the unfinished state is at least partly circumstantial, not purely a deliberate aesthetic; the Accademia presents the deliberate reading, thehistoryofart.org the circumstantial one. Source: Accademia Gallery, "Michelangelo's Prisoners or Slaves" (91 lines, read end to end); Vasari, Life; Wikipedia.

**Takeaway:** the figure is freed by taking away from the front; the unfinished back is not a failure of finishing but the record of the method -- though part of the unfinished state is circumstance, not choice.

## M4. Contrapposto and the Serpentine Figure
The standing figure is never at rest: weight falls on one leg, the hip kicks out to that side, the shoulders counter-angle against the hips, and the spine draws an S-curve that stores tension in the torso. In the David the weight is on the right leg, the left leg relaxed, the hips and shoulders twisted in opposition, so the body reads as a coiled spring a moment before the sling is thrown. In the Victory (c.1530) and the Medici Chapel figures the twist tightens into the figura serpentinata -- the figure spiralling around its own vertical axis, lower limbs turning one way and the torso the other, so no single viewpoint exhausts it (National Gallery glossary attributes the term to Michelangelo's Victory; an earlier example is Raphael's St Catherine). In the Prisoners the same device is exaggerated by the narrowness of the block: bent knees and twisted shoulders make the contrapposto itself the subject. Watchable behaviour: walk around the figure and the silhouette keeps changing -- the twist only resolves from a second angle, which is why his figures demand to be circled. Source: Wikipedia "Michelangelo"; Accademia Prisoners page; National Gallery glossary via search.

**Takeaway:** the twist is the subject; the figure is built to be circled, not faced.

## M5. Terribilita: Awe as a Mechanical Effect
Terribilita is the quality in the work that provokes terror and awe in the beholder -- not a mood but a build: colossal scale, hyper-muscular bodies twisted past comfort, furrowed brows, deep-set eyes under a heavy brow, and a gaze that does not meet yours. Julius II was the first to call him "uomo terribile" (a terrible man); Sebastiano reported to Michelangelo that Leo X had said of him (letter, 15 October 1520) "he is terribile... one cannot deal with him". The mechanism is clearest in the Moses: seated, beard in a torrent of curls, one hand gripping the tablets, the other tugging the beard, the brow knotted, the body half-rising as if about to leap up -- the figure is arrested mid-motion and the viewer supplies the violence. The David's oversized hands, the Sistine prophets' bulk, the Last Judgement's Christ as a beardless athlete hurling the damned down -- all use the same levers: scale beyond life, muscle beyond anatomy, a face caught between rage and grief. Linked by scholars to the Neoplatonism of Marsilio Ficino, for whom the beautiful could terrify. Watchable behaviour: the figure is too big, too tense and too close -- the body reacts before the mind names the subject. Source: Wikipedia "Terribilita" via search; Britannica; Vasari.

**Takeaway:** awe is engineered, not inspired: scale, muscle past anatomy, and a face that will not meet your eye.

## M6. Architecture as Sculpture
Michelangelo treats a building the way he treats a block: the classical members are plastic masses to be modelled, compressed and set in tension, and the rules are broken where the body of the building demands it. In the Laurentian Library vestibule (ricetto, commissioned 1523/24), the walls were already built on pre-existing walls, so the columns could not carry weight; he made that necessity into style -- columns recessed into the wall, appearing to rest on corbels, tapering DOWNWARD (a reversal of Greek entasis), blind windows with alternating triangular and segmental pediments, and a wall surface stretched taut like skin between vertical supports. The staircase, built 1559 by Ammannati from Michelangelo's clay model and described by him in a letter to Vasari of 28 September 1555 as "oval boxes", spills down like lava (Pevsner) -- the first freestanding staircase in European architecture. At St Peter's the same logic works at civic scale: the giant order of paired Corinthian pilasters binds the drum into one sculptural mass, and the dome's double shell, sixteen stone ribs and lantern are modelled as a single rising form. Even the failure is sculptural: his St Peter's model cost 25 crowns and took a fortnight, against Sangallo's 4,000 crowns over years (Vasari). Watchable behaviour: stand in the ricetto and the room feels too tall and too narrow at once -- the architecture presses on you the way a carved figure does. Source: Wikipedia "Laurentian Library"; Smarthistory via search; Vasari.

**Takeaway:** a wall is a block and a column is a limb; when the structure cannot carry weight, the impossibility becomes the style.

## M7. Design-Supply Collaboration (The Sebastiano System)
For the best part of a decade Michelangelo ran a production system in which he supplied the disegno and another painter executed it in colour -- a deliberate split of the two halves of painting that let him extend his reach while fighting Raphael, the master of both. The partner was Sebastiano Luciani, called del Piombo after 1531, when he became Keeper of the Papal Seal. Michelangelo gave him drawings and cartoons for the Viterbo Pieta (1512-16, San Francesco, Viterbo), the Borgherini Chapel in San Pietro in Montorio (1516-20: the Flagellation in oil on plaster, the Transfiguration in the vault, two prophets, St Peter and St Francis), and above all the Raising of Lazarus (1517-19) -- commissioned late 1516 by Cardinal Giulio de' Medici for Narbonne Cathedral and engineered as a contest against Raphael's Transfiguration, which was being painted for the same destination. Michelangelo supplied the drawings for Lazarus himself and for the two attendants (sheets now in the British Museum); Sebastiano painted the crowd and landscape. The two pictures were displayed together in the Hall of the Consistory after Raphael's death in 1520; Raphael's was generally preferred. The friendship itself lasted about twenty-three years, 1511-1534, but the active design supply clusters in 1512-1519; the study file does not repeat #515's "for twenty years" for the supply, which is the friendship's length, not the supply's (see C126 and C145). The system ended in 1534-1536 over the Last Judgement wall (see the Doorway). Watchable behaviour: in the Lazarus the central group has Michelangelo's anatomy and torsion while the surrounding crowd is Venetian in colour and calm -- two hands, one picture. Source: National Gallery, "Raising of Lazarus" and the 2017 exhibition "Michelangelo and Sebastiano"; Wikipedia "Sebastiano del Piombo" and "The Last Judgment (Michelangelo)"; Hirst appendix (1981) cited via search.

**Takeaway:** design and colour can be split between two hands and the picture still holds -- until the split becomes the argument that ends the friendship.

## M8. The Cartoon as a School
A full-scale cartoon was not only a transfer device but a public exhibition piece that taught a generation. The Battle of Cascina cartoon (1504-06), showing Florentine soldiers surprised while bathing in the Arno, was displayed in the Palazzo Vecchio and studied, drawn and copied by Raphael, Andrea del Sarto, Pontormo, Baccio Bandinelli and others before it was cut up and dispersed; Vasari says it became "the school of the world" for artists. The mechanism: a cartoon at full scale, in charcoal and chalk, shows every muscle, foreshortening and twist at life size, so a young artist could learn the figure by tracing it -- the cartoon is a textbook you can measure with your hands. The same function attached to the Sistine cartoons and to the drawings he gave Sebastiano: the design circulates and multiplies while the master keeps the idea. Watchable behaviour: copy after copy of the bathers survives (notably the engraving after the cartoon) even though the original is lost -- the copies ARE the work's afterlife. Source: Vasari, Life; Wikipedia "Michelangelo" and "Battle of Cascina".

**Takeaway:** a drawing at life size is a school; the copies are the work's second life, and for the Cascina they are its only life.

## M9. Poetry as Design Thinking
Michelangelo's 300+ sonnets and madrigals are not decoration on the career but a second workshop where he thinks through the same problems as the sculpture -- and sometimes says what the marble cannot. The marble sonnet (Q1) states the whole carving system in four lines: the idea is already circumscribed in the block, and the hand only removes the excess, obeying intellect. The Night quatrain (Q5) is spoken by the sculpture itself, in the first person, answering Strozzi's epigram -- the statue argues for its own sleep, turning a political complaint (Florence under Medici rule) into a statue's voice. The late poems reverse the system: poem 285 (1554, Q8) says neither painting nor sculpture can any longer calm a soul turned toward divine love -- the maker renounces the medium that defined him. The letters do the same work in prose: the Varchi letter (Q2) defines sculpture by subtraction; the Cavalieri letters (Q6) test how far a name can nourish a soul. Watchable behaviour: read a late sonnet beside the Rondanini Pieta -- the poem and the carving are doing the same subtraction. Source: thehistoryofart.org "Michelangelo Quotes" (540 lines, read end to end); Vasari, Life; Wikipedia.

**Takeaway:** the sonnet is a second workshop; late in life the poem and the carving perform the same act, which is taking away.

## M10. Late-Life Reworking: Destruction as Method
In old age the method turns on itself: the work is revised by removal until almost nothing remains, and destruction becomes a form of finishing. The Florentine Pieta (c.1547-55), meant for his own tomb, was attacked with a hammer by Michelangelo himself -- the arm and leg of the Christ smashed -- before his servant Antonio stopped him; Tiberio Calcagni repaired it. The Rondanini Pieta (1552-64) was carved, recarved and thinned over twelve years until the two figures fused into a single elongated form, barely detached from the block; he worked on it six days before his death. At St Peter's the same discipline operates at institutional scale: he discarded Sangallo's model, simplified the plan back to Bramante's central cross, strengthened the piers, and left a wooden model (1558-61) precise enough that della Porta could build the dome from it twenty-six years later -- the model IS the building, deferred. The mechanism is M3 pushed to its limit: taking away, continued past the point where most makers would stop, until the work holds only what cannot be removed. Watchable behaviour: the Rondanini figures read as two separate bodies from the front and one fused column from the side -- the subtraction is the subject. Source: Vasari, Life; Wikipedia "Michelangelo" and "Rondanini Pieta" via search; Missed History on the dome.

**Takeaway:** taking away, continued past the stopping point, becomes the finish; the last work is two bodies becoming one column of stone.


## Evolution
Six phases, from leg 2. Phase 1, Florence apprenticeship, 1488-1496 (ages 13-21): apprenticed to Ghirlandaio 1488; taken into the Medici sculpture garden and Platonic Academy 1490-92 (Ficino, Pico, Poliziano); nose broken by Pietro Torrigiano at 17. Technique: learning -- copying engravings (Torment of Saint Anthony), stiacciato relief after Donatello (Madonna of the Stairs), classical battle relief (Battle of the Centaurs). Scale: small -- panels under 50 cm, reliefs under a metre. Subject: Christian and classical, chosen by patrons and mentors. Handling: already precocious, but the carving is careful, the surfaces finished, the debt to others visible. The Santo Spirito Crucifix (1493) is the pivot: a life-size wooden body carved in exchange for the right to dissect corpses -- anatomy becomes his foundation. Lorenzo dies 8 April 1492; the snow statue for Piero de' Medici (20 January 1494) and the flight from Florence (1494) close the phase. Phase 2, Rome and Bologna, 1496-1501 (ages 21-26): arrived Rome 25 June 1496; Bacchus begun 4 July 1496; Pieta contract August 1498. Technique: full-scale marble carving, learned fast; the Sleeping Cupid fraud shows he already understood the antique market better than his patrons. Scale: jumps to life-size and over (Bacchus 203 cm; Pieta 174 x 195 cm). Subject: pagan and devotional, for cardinals. Handling: the Pieta is polished to a finish no later work matches -- smooth, idealized, youthful, serene. The contrast with the late Pietas could not be sharper: here the Virgin is a beautiful girl holding a perfect body; sixty years later the Rondanini Pieta is two rough columns of stone barely separated from each other. The Bologna interlude (1494-95, three small shrine figures) is the bridge between the two cities. Phase 3, Florence, 1501-1505 (ages 26-30): returned 1499; David contract 16 August 1501. Technique: colossal marble from a damaged, abandoned block; carving began 13 September 1501. Scale: monumental -- David 517 cm, the largest nude since antiquity, and the first colossal marble of the Renaissance. Subject: civic and biblical heroism (David), devotional tondi (Pitti, Taddei), the unfinished apostle St Matthew. Handling: the turn toward non-finito begins here -- the Pitti and Taddei tondi and St Matthew are left deliberately rough, the figure emerging from the block. Painting returns with the Doni Tondo (c.1507) and the unfinished Manchester Madonna and Entombment: hard, sculptural color, bodies like carved stone. The Battle of Cascina cartoon (1504-05) -- lost, but copied for generations -- sets his figure style against Leonardo's across the same hall. Phase 4, Rome under Julius II, 1505-1513 (ages 30-38): tomb of Julius II commissioned 1505 (40 statues, five years); left Rome secretly 17 April 1506 after the pope diverted him; bronze Julius II, Bologna 1506-08, destroyed 1511. Technique: fresco on a scale he had never attempted -- the Sistine ceiling (1508-12) was commissioned as 12 Apostles plus ornament and he negotiated a free hand. Scale: architectural -- a 40-metre vault, over 300 figures, and a tomb meant to hold 40 statues. Subject: Genesis, prophets, sibyls -- the whole of sacred history overhead. Handling: the ceiling's figures grow larger and broader as the work progresses eastward; the painting is sculptural, the bodies twisted in space. The Moses (c.1513-15) and the two Louvre Slaves (1513-16) carry the same energy into marble. This is the heroic phase: everything finished, everything enormous, everything public. The tomb's repeated shrinking (six phases to 1545) is the counter-story -- ambition checked by papal politics and money. Phase 5, Medici Florence, 1516-1534 (ages 41-59): San Lorenzo facade 1516-20, cancelled before real progress; Medici Chapel 1520-34; Laurentian Library from 1524; siege and fortifications 1528-29; hid about two months in a secret room under the Medici Chapel (drawings rediscovered 1975/1976, opened to small numbers of visitors 2023); pardoned; left Florence 1534 and never returned. Technique: architecture becomes his main medium -- his first major building (New Sacristy) and the Laurentian Library, where he treats stone like sculpture: columns sunk into walls, a staircase like a flow of lava. Sculpture turns inward: the Medici allegories (Night, Day, Dusk, Dawn) are heavy, dreaming, unfinished; the Boboli Slaves are bodies that never escape the block; the Medici Madonna is left unfinished. Scale: intimate again after the Roman colossi -- the Crouching Boy is 54 cm, the allegories recline at human scale on sarcophagi. Subject: death, sleep, time -- the tombs of two minor dukes, not the great Lorenzo. Handling: non-finito is now the rule, not the exception; faces left rough, bodies half-carved. The republican Brutus (1538) and the Apollo (c.1530) belong to the same mood: political defeat carved into stone. Phase 6, late Rome, 1534-1564 (ages 59-88): met Vittoria Colonna in Rome 1536 or 1538 (she died 1547); Tommaso dei Cavalieri met 1532. Technique: fresco again, but changed -- the Last Judgement (1536-41) is more monochromatic than the ceiling, dominated by flesh and sky, its 300+ figures in rotary agitation around a beardless, Apollonian Christ; the Pauline Chapel frescoes (1542-50) abandon grace for narrative directness. Then architecture takes over entirely: Piazza del Campidoglio (designed 1536-46), Palazzo Farnese upper floor (1546), St Peter's (chief architect 1546/1547 to his death). Scale: the largest of all -- a city square, the dome of Christendom (41.5 m across, 136.57 m to the cross). Subject: judgement, crucifixion, conversion, death. Handling: the late Pietas are the endpoint -- the Florentine Pieta (c.1547-55), smashed by his own hand in frustration, its Nicodemus a self-portrait; the Rondanini Pieta (1552-64), recut three times, worked on six days before his death, two elongated figures fused into one rising form. Poem 285 (1554): "Neither painting nor sculpture will be able any longer to calm my soul..." He wrote 300+ sonnets and madrigals across his life; the late poems and the late Pietas say the same thing.

Early vs recent, stated plainly. Early Michelangelo (1488-1512) finishes things: the Pieta is polished smooth, the David is complete to the last toenail, the Sistine ceiling is a finished, legible, heroic system -- ideal bodies, clear narrative, public scale, confident anatomy. Late Michelangelo (1534-1564) does not finish things: the Medici tombs, the Boboli Slaves, the Florentine Pieta, the Rondanini Pieta are all unfinished, rough, or broken -- by design, by interruption, or by his own hammer. Early bodies are perfect and separate; late bodies fuse, elongate, and dissolve into the stone. Early work is about mastery of the body; late work is about the soul leaving it. The Sistine ceiling looks up at creation; the Last Judgement looks down at damnation; the Rondanini Pieta looks at nothing but two figures becoming one. The through-line is the body in torsion -- from the Battle of the Centaurs to the Rebellious Slave to the Rondanini Pieta, the twist never leaves -- but what the twist means changes completely: first virtuosity, then struggle, finally surrender.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is one operation repeated at every scale: take away until only what cannot be removed remains. In marble the operation is literal -- the figure is already in the block and the hand only removes the excess, obeying intellect (Q1) -- and the same operation governs the fresco, where each giornata is a day's patch laid and left, the architecture, where Sangallo's model is discarded and the plan simplified back to Bramante's cross, and the poetry, where the late sonnets renounce the medium itself. The second constant is the body in torsion: weight on one leg, shoulders against hips, the spine in an S-curve, tightened into the figura serpentinata, so that no single viewpoint exhausts the figure and the work demands to be circled. The third constant is scale used as argument: the David is 517 cm because the civic claim needs a body larger than life; the Sistine ceiling is 40 metres of vault because the claim is the whole of sacred history overhead; the St Peter's dome is 41.5 m across because the claim is Christendom. And the fourth constant is that the system turns on itself late: the same taking-away that freed the David ends by smashing the Florentine Pieta and thinning the Rondanini Pieta until two figures fuse into one column. The aesthetic is not beauty as finish but beauty as the residue of removal: what survives the chisel, the giornata, the cancellation and the hammer is the work. That is why the unfinished state is the honest centre of the career rather than its accident -- the Prisoners, the Medici allegories, the Boboli Slaves and the late Pietas are not studies for finished works but the finished statement of a method that only ever took away. Against Sebastiano's Venetian colour the logic is a deliberate refusal: disegno against colore, the drawn figure against the painted surface, fresco against oil -- and the refusal itself became the friendship's end, which is the one place where the logic fails as a human system even as it succeeds as an artistic one.

## POSITIONS
- P1. Disegno was the single foundation of all three arts for Michelangelo, and the ground of his claim to supremacy in each. Source: letter to Benedetto Varchi, 1549 (Milanesi, Le lettere, 1875); Dundas article on disegno; Britannica on disegno vs colore.
- P2. He painted the Sistine ceiling largely alone after dismissing his Florentine assistants, who had been brought in to help with the fresco work. Source: Vasari, Life; Wikipedia "Michelangelo".
- P3. He painted the ceiling standing on a scaffold of his own design, not lying on his back -- the lying-down image is a Vasari-era legend popularised by "The Agony and the Ecstasy". Source: Britannica; Wikipedia; Vasari (who does not describe him lying down).
- P4. The unfinished state of the Accademia Prisoners is at least partly circumstantial -- they were made for the Julius tomb and abandoned when the tomb was reduced -- and should not be read purely as a deliberate aesthetic of non-finito. Source: thehistoryofart.org, Michelangelo pages (St Matthew / Awakening Slave); counter-reading at the Accademia Gallery site (see C89).
- P5. He carved freehand, front to back, without a pointing machine or a plaster model marked up on the block -- the wax-in-water method described by Vasari. Source: Accademia Gallery, "Michelangelo's Prisoners or Slaves"; Vasari, Life.
- P6. Terribilita was a contemporary label, not a later invention: Julius II first called him "uomo terribile", and Sebastiano reported Leo X saying of him in a letter of 15 October 1520, "he is terribile... one cannot deal with him". Source: Wikipedia "Terribilita" via search; Britannica.
- P7. The Raising of Lazarus was deliberately engineered as a contest against Raphael's Transfiguration, commissioned for the same destination (Narbonne Cathedral) at the same time, with Michelangelo supplying the designs so that Sebastiano could beat Raphael in colour what Raphael had in invention. Source: National Gallery, "Raising of Lazarus" (NG1); Wikipedia "Sebastiano del Piombo".
- P8. Appointed architect of St Peter's in 1546 at the age of 71, he returned the building to Bramante's centralised Greek-cross plan, discarding Antonio da Sangallo the Younger's model. Source: Wikipedia "Michelangelo"; Britannica.
- P9. The dome as built is taller and more pointed than Michelangelo's wooden model: Giacomo della Porta, with Domenico Fontana, raised the outer profile by about 4.8-7 metres and completed the dome in 1588-90, with the lantern finished in 1593. Source: Missed History, "The Man Who Actually Built St Peter's Dome"; VisitVaticanCity.org, "The Dome of St Peter's Basilica"; Symonds, Life of Michelangelo, ch. 11 (via search).
- P10. Only about eleven of his statues were fully finished, most famously the Bacchus, the Pieta, the David, the Christ of the Minerva, the Medici dukes, Night, Dawn and Moses; the rest exist in states from barely blocked-out to nearly complete. Source: Vasari, Life (as summarised in the research notes); Accademia Gallery Prisoners page.
- P11. The Sistine ceiling was commissioned as 12 Apostles plus ornament, and Michelangelo negotiated a free hand, "to do as I liked", before painting the nine Genesis scenes instead. Source: leg 2, works table entry 21; Wikipedia "Sistine Chapel ceiling".
- P12. The tomb of Julius II ran forty years, 1505-1545, through six phases from more than forty statues to a wall tomb, and Julius II is buried in St Peter's, not in the San Pietro in Vincoli tomb that bears his name -- it is a cenotaph. Source: leg 2, works table entries 23-24; Wikipedia "Michelangelo".
- P13. The David was carved from a block roughed out by Agostino di Duccio in 1464 and left abandoned for decades, and a committee of thirty, including Leonardo and Botticelli, decided its placement in the Piazza della Signoria in January 1504. Source: leg 2, works table entry 13; Wikipedia "David (Michelangelo)".
- P14. Nine popes in succession employed him: Julius II, Leo X, Adrian VI, Clement VII, Paul III, Julius III, Marcellus II, Paul IV and Pius IV. Source: leg 1, biography.
- P15. Source: leg 2, evolution phase 5 and works table entries 29-34.
