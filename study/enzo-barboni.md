# Enzo Barboni: Study Notes

**Site:** Enzo Barboni (b. Monday 10 July 1922, Rome, Italy; d. Saturday 23 March 2002, Rome, Italy, age 79), Italian film director, cinematographer and screenwriter, as E. B. Clucher the director of the Spencer/Hill slapstick comedies -- the man who shot Sergio Corbucci's *Django* (1966) as a cinematographer and then, four years later, emptied the spaghetti western of its killing and refilled it with slapstick, beans and a whistled theme. Camera operator from 1942, cinematographer from 1961, director from 1970: twenty-eight years behind the camera before he ever called action, and then twenty-five years running one formula -- the mismatched duo, the long brawl, the meal as a set piece -- until the formula ran out of settings and he retired. [C30118]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Sergio Martino study: Martino ends his study with the nine-film De Angelis partnership and the western line running Corbucci to Castellari to Martino, and the hinge of that whole line is the man who hired both composers. Barboni is the only director on record who hired Franco Micalizzi (#754) AND the De Angelis brothers (#755) for Spencer/Hill films -- Micalizzi for *They Call Me Trinity* (1970), the brothers for the four that followed. Where Martino brings the black gloves and the dream, Barboni brings the pan of beans and the hammer punch: the same western furniture, the same composers, the same Rome studios, with the killing taken out. The study starts where Martino's leaves off -- with the Trinity origin story, the chain's founding event, and the cinematographer who learned the violent western by shooting it before he learned to laugh at it. [C30119]

## Corrections to the brief

Three films in the brief handed to this study were NOT Barboni's, and all three are removed before any claim is built on them. *The Two Missionaries* (*Porgi l'altra guancia*, 1974) was directed by Franco Rossi, not Barboni (Wikipedia; Beat Records soundtrack note). *A Friend Is a Treasure* (*Chi trova un amico trova un tesoro*, 1981) was directed by Sergio Corbucci, not Barboni (Wikipedia; Letterboxd; Clicker). *Cane e gatto* (1983, released in English as *Cat and Dog*) was directed by Bruno Corbucci, not Barboni (Wikipedia); Barboni's actual 1983 film is *Go for It* (*Nati con la camicia*), and the brief's "Odd Squad / Cane e gatto (1983)" conflates two films -- *Odd Squad* is *Ciao nemico* (1981, Barboni). The three errors are instructive, not incidental: by the mid-1970s the Hill/Spencer brand had outgrown Barboni and continued under other directors, which is why his own filmography is so easily confused with the brand's. Year conflicts resolved: *Even Angels Eat Beans* is 1973, not 1974 as Barboni's Wikipedia filmography lists; *Odd Squad* is 1981 by Wikipedia against 1982 (MUBI, TV Guide) and 1983 (Minerva production year, IMDb release 10 January 1983) -- use 1981 with the variants noted; *Speaking of the Devil* was produced in 1990 (Intramovies) and released 11 January 1991 (IMDb) -- use 1991; *The Unholy Four* is 1970 (film page, SWDB 11 March 1970, Letterboxd) against 1969 (WesternsAllItaliana) -- use 1970; *Sons of Trinity* is 1995 by Wikipedia against 1994 by WesternsAllItaliana -- use 1995. [C30122]

## Works

**W01 -- The Unholy Four (*Ciakmull - L'uomo della vendetta*; also *Chuck Moll* / *Chuck Mool*), 1970.** Serious spaghetti western. Grade A. The directorial debut, in which Barboni replaced Ferdinando Baldi, fired by producer Manolo Bolognini for insisting on casting Annabella Incontrera as Sheila. A bank gang sets a prison (in some accounts an insane asylum) on fire as a diversion for their getaway, and four inmates escape in the turmoil, among them the amnesiac Chuck Mool, who remembers only that he lived in the town of Oxaca. The rival Udo clan exploits Mool's memory loss to turn him against his own family, sending him to kill a man who is in fact his father, until a fellow fugitive warns him in time and Mool learns his half-brother Alan framed him out of hatred. Riz Ortolani wrote the score, Leonard Mann was being groomed as a next Franco Nero down to the Union-style clothing, and Genre Grinder judges the film made with a stylistic confidence ahead of most B-product westerns of the cycle, an episodic, wheel-spinning middle act but a plot that comes together nicely with a double-twist ending. Evidence: Wikipedia "The Unholy Four (1970 film)"; IONCINEMA; Genre Grinder; Once Upon a Time in a Western.

**W02 -- They Call Me Trinity (*Lo chiamavano Trinita...*), 1970.** Spaghetti western comedy. Grade A. Written and directed by Barboni as E. B. Clucher, produced by Italo Zingarelli, released in Italy on 22 December 1970. Trinity, a lazy gunfighter with an unnaturally fast draw, is dragged into town on a travois by his horse, finds his brother Bambino posing as the new sheriff, and the two defend pacifist Mormon settlers against land-grabber Major Harriman; the famous bean-eating scene was shot in a single take after Terence Hill reportedly fasted for 24 hours. The brothers train the Mormons in dirty fighting, the Mormon leader finds in Ecclesiastes that there is a time for fighting, and Trinity quietly brands all of Harriman's unbranded horses with the Mormons' brand, legally giving the herd away and ruining Bambino's plan to steal it. Franco Micalizzi wrote the music, and the title song "Trinity: Titoli" was later used as the closing theme of Quentin Tarantino's *Django Unchained* (2012). Aldo Giordani shot it, interiors at Incir De Paolis Studios in Rome with valley locations in Campo Imperatore, Abruzzo, and it drew 8,742,787 admissions in Italy. Hill and Spencer did their own stunts, with the rest of the supporting cast portrayed predominantly by stuntmen. Evidence: Wikipedia "They Call Me Trinity"; The Indian Messenger; Dyerbolical.

**W03 -- Trinity Is Still My Name (*...continuavano a chiamarlo Trinita*), 1971.** Spaghetti western comedy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni, released in Italy on 21 October 1971, music by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis, cinematography by Aldo Giordani. It opens with Bambino stealing beans and horses from four escaped convicts and knocking one addlepated with a single blow to the head, a condition that becomes a running gag, and Trinity then arrives on his travois and tricks the same convicts again. The brothers are bound by their dying father's wish to work together, fail at teaching Trinity horse thievery, keep rescuing the same pioneer family, and Trinity humiliates card sharp Wild Card Hendricks by drawing, holstering and slapping him in the face repeatedly before Hendricks can react. They buy new suits with their winnings, pass as federal agents, gorge in a smart restaurant, take a $4,000 bribe, start a bar brawl in San Jose, expose a mission used for gun-running, and win a long fight with the real monks' help before Rangers arrive. It was the highest-grossing Italian film up to that point, with 14,554,172 admissions in Italy, and with 12,267,000 visitors it is the seventh most successful film in Germany to date. Evidence: Wikipedia "Trinity Is Still My Name"; Letterboxd; Achievers essay on the slap routine and restaurant scene.

**W04 -- Man of the East (*E poi lo chiamarono il magnifico*), 1972.** Spaghetti western comedy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni as E. B. Clucher, released 9 September 1972, starring Terence Hill without Bud Spencer -- the film's Wikipedia lead misspells the pseudonym as "E.B. Clutcher". Young English nobleman Sir Thomas Fitzpatrick Phillip Moore arrives in the West on his late father's wish and joins his father's former pals, the stagecoach robbers Monkey, Holy Joe and Bull. Tom refuses to touch a weapon, prefers his bicycle to a horse and reads Byron, falls for Candida, the landowner's daughter, and only after an intensive course in brawling, shooting and spitting from the trio can he stand up to the ranch foreman Morton, who also wants her. The recurring theme is modernisation crowding the frontier out: the trio feel civilisation closing in and plan to keep drifting west ahead of the railroad, and Barboni makes do with stock footage of the Rio Grande Scenic Railroad under the opening credits where Leone could afford real trains. Guido and Maurizio De Angelis scored the film. Evidence: Wikipedia "Man of the East"; Cinema Retro; DVDBeaver; Blu-ray.com Guido De Angelis filmography.

**W05 -- Even Angels Eat Beans (*Anche gli angeli mangiano fagioli*), 1973.** Crime comedy. Grade A. Written and directed by Barboni in 1973 with Giuliano Gemma and Bud Spencer, originally intended for Hill and Spencer, but Hill dropped out because he was concerned about overexposure. In 1930s New York, Sonny, a former ice-cream man fallen on hard luck, meets wrestler Charlie Smith while cleaning a gym, and the two are hired by mobster Angelo to hit a gambler but erroneously beat up a US Senator instead, who declares war on the underworld. Angelo sends them to Little Italy as debt collectors, where they befriend the poor Gerace family, beat up two rival collectors sent by Angelo's rival Mr Colosimo, and trigger a gang war until police captain Mackintosh intervenes and is forced to let them go because he is himself implicated with Angelo and Colosimo. Guido and Maurizio De Angelis composed the soundtrack, which produced a minor hit with the single "Angel and Beans" performed by Kathy and Gulliver, and the film won the Golden Screen Award in 1974. Reviewers note a prototype daydream sequence and substantial Chaplin homage, and Gemma's Sonny as naive and resolutely cheerful, dancing quietly to himself when happy. Evidence: Wikipedia "Even Angels Eat Beans"; IMDb tt0069713 (release 22 March 1973); Letterboxd. Flag: Yidio's plot summary describes two cops and is wrong; disregarded.

**W06 -- Charleston (*Anche gli angeli tirano di destro*), 1974.** Buddy comedy. Grade B. The sequel to *Even Angels Eat Beans*, written and directed by Barboni in 1974, with Ricky Bruch, at his film debut, replacing Bud Spencer, who refused to reprise his role. Sonny, a New York wise guy who dreams of becoming a feared gangster, runs errands for the mob with some success, and as a reward the local Don gives him control of Mulberry Street, which also faces a boxing gym. Guido and Maurizio De Angelis scored it, Francisco Marin was cinematographer, and it runs 111 minutes. Evidence: Wikipedia "Charleston (1974 film)"; MUBI; IMDb tt0071139 (release 24 October 1974).

**W07 -- Crime Busters (*I due superpiedi quasi piatti*), 1977.** Action crime comedy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni in 1977 with Hill and Spencer, set in Miami, scored by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis. Unemployed Wilbur Walsh (Spencer) and Matt Kirby (Hill) plan to rob a supermarket, blunder into a police station instead, and volunteer to become police officers to evade arrest. After training they work the streets in unorthodox style, Kirby befriends a Chinese family whose uncle was murdered, and the trail leads back to the same dockside gang that refused them longshoremen's work, now running a trafficking ring; Laura Gemser appears as Suzy Lee, the murdered man's niece. The running gags are physical and luck-based: the pair repeatedly wreck the dock boss's cars, and as soon as they are assigned a police car Hill crashes it leaving the lot into another car whose occupants happen to be big criminals the police have been hunting. The film parodies the poliziotteschi, and a Letterboxd review notes its Years of Lead echoes, with red-masked robbers and radio reports of random shootings running through a story otherwise set in the US. Evidence: Wikipedia "Crime Busters"; IMDb tt0074442 (release 6 April 1977); Letterboxd; Clicker.

**W08 -- Odd Squad (*Ciao nemico*; also *The Bridge Between*), 1981.** War comedy. Grade B. Directed by Barboni in 1981, an Italian war comedy starring Johnny Dorelli as Lt Federico Tocci, Giuliano Gemma as Lt Joe Kirby, and Vincent Gardenia. After the Allied landing in Sicily, the US Army and the Italian Army face each other across an ancient Roman bridge that both want to blow up, but both sides' sappers do their best not to do it. The two lieutenants devise an absurd plan to make the invading American division believe the Italians have surrendered and the war is over, and forty years later the American lieutenant, now an older college engineering teacher, watches the bridge and prepares to go to the theatre because his old same-rank enemy has become a conductor and that evening will direct Verdi's *Nabucco*. Reception was mixed: Morando Morandini praised it as "pleasant and full of comical inventions", while Flaminio Di Biagi described it as a "quite ramshackle little comedy". Year variants: Wikipedia 1981, MUBI and TV Guide 1982, Minerva production year 1983 with an IMDb release date of 10 January 1983. Evidence: Wikipedia "Odd Squad (film)"; Minerva Pictures catalogue PDF; MUBI; IMDb tt0083736. NOT FOUND: no scene-level staging or gag detail located.

**W09 -- Go for It (*Nati con la camicia*), 1983.** Action comedy / spy parody. Grade A. Directed by Barboni in 1983, an Italian action comedy and spy-film parody starring Hill and Spencer. Charming dodger Rosco Frazer and Doug O'Riordan, a recently released convict imprisoned for sinking a yacht that annoyed him, meet in a rough roadhouse brawl, are then mistaken for two bank robbers by the traffic police, and while trying to catch a flight are confused for two secret agents, Steinberg and Mason. Sent to Miami with a briefcase full of millions, they must stop the villain K1, who plans to erase numbers from the human mind, posing as visiting Texan millionaires in the most expensive hotel while surviving assassination attempts, car chases and no-holds-barred fights. The screenplay was by Marco Barboni, Barboni's son, and the film was first released 1 February 1983. Evidence: Wikipedia "Go for It (1983 film)"; Letterboxd; Wubly; Plex; Mymovies.

**W10 -- Double Trouble (*Non c'e due senza quattro*), 1984.** Action comedy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni as E. B. Clucher in 1984, an Italian action comedy starring Hill and Spencer. Mild-mannered Brazilian billionaire cousins Bastiano and Antonio Coimbra de la Coronilla y Azevedo, fearing for their lives after several assassination attempts, hire two look-alikes, stuntman Elliot Vance and saxophone-playing small-time criminal Greg Wonder, to temporarily take their place and find who is behind the attempts. The doubles take to the jet-set life in Rio, learn the cousins' effeminate manners, and enjoy themselves until the real cousins return early and walk into the trap, leading to a long battle with hired mercenaries at a farm, where the mastermind is unmasked as Donna Olympia Chavez, avenging her father. The twist undoes the revenge: Antonio reveals Donna's father was himself the real criminal, who stole from Antonio and ran off with a dancer to Paris, so her murder campaign was for nothing. Both leads play double roles, and Rio de Janeiro, with Christ the Redeemer, samba parades and Carnival, replaces Miami as the postcard backdrop. Evidence: Wikipedia "Double Trouble (1984 film)"; Film&Clips; Chilimovie; 123Movies (release 19 October 1984).

**W11 -- They Call Me Renegade (*Renegade*), 1987.** Road movie / western comedy. Grade A. Directed by E. B. Clucher in 1987, a road movie starring Terence Hill and his son Ross Hill, written by Marco Barboni from a story by Marco Barboni, Terence Hill, Sergio Donati and Steven Siebert. Drifter "Renegade" Luke travels the Southwestern United States in his Jeep CJ-5 Renegade with a chestnut colt named Joe Brown, a horse with a homing instinct he repeatedly sells. His jailed friend Moose asks Luke to become legal guardian of Moose's son Matt and to look after a ranch in Arizona won in a poker game, and the reluctant pair's journey brings crazy truckers trying to drive them off the road, a hassling biker gang, welcoming Amish neighbours, and suits from Lawson Enterprises pressing them to sell the land. The horse refuses to get up and then explodes, a booby trap, and Luke learns Mr Henry Lawson (Robert Vaughn) framed Moose and had him jailed because Moose could recognise him as Capt. Lenny Covacks, who killed men of his own platoon to abscond with 500 phials of morphine. Radio Times calls it amiable and far more sedate than most spaghettis, good value in the trucker, biker and knife-thrower encounters, but falling away once Vaughn's villain takes over. It is Barboni's last film built around Hill as co-lead, and Ross Hill died at age 30, noted in a Letterboxd review. Evidence: Wikipedia "They Call Me Renegade"; Radio Times; Letterboxd; Wubly (US release 13 November 1987).

**W12 -- Speaking of the Devil (*Un piede in paradiso*; also *Standing in Paradise*), 1991.** Comedy fantasy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni in 1991, an Italian comedy film, and the last collaboration between Barboni and Bud Spencer, with the pair's sons co-writing the film together and Spencer's daughter also playing a role. The role of Victor was originally intended for Terence Hill, but Hill had to refuse because he was still engaged on the set of the Lucky Luke TV series. Taxi driver Bull Webster fights the Spider Corporation, a giant financial holding company buying out his taxi co-operative to bankrupt it and buy its land cheap, until he finds in his pocket the winning ticket to a lottery with a $150 million prize, at which point Heaven and Hell send an Angel and a Devil to tempt him into evil or convince him to do good. The appearance of these characters creates a maze of situations that are amusing, dramatic and full of action, and for once it is the humans, especially Bull, who come out winning. Evidence: Wikipedia "Speaking of the Devil"; IMDb tt0102667 (release 11 January 1991); Intramovies (production year 1990); Blu-ray.com.

**W13 -- Sons of Trinity (*Trinita & Bambino... e adesso tocca a noi*; also *Trinity & Babyface*), 1995.** Spaghetti western comedy. Grade A. Directed by Barboni as E. B. Clucher and released 29 June 1995, an Italian-Spanish-German co-production and the last film directed by Enzo Barboni. It was directed and produced by the creators of the original films, Italo Zingarelli and Barboni, and stars Heath Kizzier and Keith Neubert as the sons of Trinity and Bambino, with neither Hill nor Spencer appearing. The sons bear their fathers' names: Trinity Junior is a bounty-hunter prankster and womanizer, Bambino is the gruff sheriff and jailer, and their quiet peace is interrupted by the arrival of two gangs, one Anglo and one Mexican, of horse-stealing criminals in a small Mexican town. Barboni's biography records it as a disaster at the box office, after which he retired. A review of the whole series notes the crew tried hard to make it resemble the first two films, with the leads emulating the original characters, but that it feels more like a TV movie and is considerably less impressive. Evidence: Wikipedia "Sons of Trinity"; Spaghetti Western Database; Encyclopedia.com; 10000tip review.

**W14 -- Nightmare Castle (*Amanti d'oltretomba*; also *The Faceless Monster*), 1965.** Gothic horror (cinematography). Grade A. Directed by Mario Caiano with cinematography by Enzo Barboni, released 16 July 1965, a black-and-white Italian horror film with Barbara Steele in a dual role. Greedy Dr Stephen Arrowsmith murders his unfaithful wife Muriel (Steele), and her stepsister Jenny (Steele again) arrives at the castle, where sadism, spectral revenge and a battle for Jenny's sanity play out. Caiano shot it at Villa Parisi in Frascati and at Incir-De Paolis Studios in Rome over 18 days on a low budget, and some scenes were originally planned to be given a red tint in post-production. Barboni's high-key black-and-white photography is the film's visual identity: stark lighting and chiaroscuro on Steele's face, a camera gliding through the villa, and long soft-focus dressing sequences that make the film read as a love poem to Steele's haunted beauty. Evidence: Wikipedia "Nightmare Castle"; Moria; Classic-Horror; DVD Talk; IHorrorDB.

**W15 -- Django, 1966.** Spaghetti western (cinematography). Grade A. Directed by Sergio Corbucci with cinematography by Enzo Barboni, starring Franco Nero, released in April 1966. Django trudges through mud in the rain dragging a coffin behind him, rescues the prostitute Maria from Major Jackson's men, and the coffin is revealed to hold a rapid-fire Gatling gun, which he uses to mow down Jackson's Klansmen. Barboni experimented with the usually crisp Eastmancolour system, producing a surrealistic colour scheme that gives the film a much more expensive look than its meagre budget would usually allow. The film belongs to Corbucci's "mud and blood" run: a damp, desolate town where the adjacent cemetery seems the most populated site, an aura of doom, and Django as a furtive, almost spectral figure whose presence signals upheaval. Its violence -- whipping, an ear sliced off, mass shootouts in the mud, Django's hands crushed with a rifle butt -- got it banned in England and several other countries on release. The Spaghetti Western Database counts Barboni as cinematographer on 11 spaghetti westerns including Django, and repeats the rumor that it was Barboni who first gave Sergio Leone the idea of adapting Kurosawa's *Yojimbo* into a western. In a Segno Cinema interview Barboni explained that during two weeks of shooting at the Elios Film set, low available sunlight and near-permanent grey cloud made filming problematic; many scenes came out underexposed, but the film negative used permitted this and the crew liked the visual effects created. Critic Alex Cox praised Barboni's "claustrophobic" and "brutal, uncompromising style" of cinematography, including "some striking wide-angle establishing shots" and "a good hand-held fight" sequence. Evidence: Wikipedia "Django (1966 film)"; Tainted Archive; Filmint; Dennis Schwartz; SWDB Hall of Fame / Cinematographers.

**W16 -- Texas, Adios (*Texas, addio*), 1966.** Spaghetti western (cinematography). Grade A. Directed by Ferdinando Baldi with cinematography by Enzo Barboni, released 26 August 1966, starring Franco Nero, and the second film in Nero's planned three-picture contract with producer Manolo Bolognini after *Django*. Taciturn Texas sheriff Burt Sullivan resigns and crosses into Mexico with his younger brother Jim to bring sadistic crime boss Cisco Delgado to justice for murdering their father, only to learn Delgado is also Jim's biological father. During the shoot Barboni, who aspired to direct, tried to pitch a more comedic take on the western to Nero, who was not interested -- and that pitch would become the runaway hit *They Call Me Trinity* a few years later. The film takes a more classical, Hollywood-inspired approach than *Django*, with close-ups of eyes and stubble, southern Spanish locations, horse stunts, and a surf-guitar-heavy score by Anton Garcia Abril. Evidence: Wikipedia "Texas, Adios"; Spaghetti Western Database; Blueprint Review; DVDBeaver.

**W17 -- The Five Man Army (*Un esercito di 5 uomini*), 1969.** Spaghetti western / heist (cinematography). Grade A. Directed by Don Taylor with cinematography by Enzo Barboni, released 16 October 1969, from a script by a young Dario Argento, with a score by Ennio Morricone and produced by Italo Zingarelli, who would produce the Trinity films. Mexican rebels hire the "Dutchman" (Peter Graves) to rob a train carrying $500,000 in gold, and he enlists four specialists: Mesito, a strong man played by Bud Spencer, Luis, a circus acrobat turned outlaw, Augustus, an explosives expert, and a samurai earning a living in a sideshow. The set piece is a train robbery staged with engineering intelligence: the plan is to board the moving train and uncouple the gold car without stopping it, and it nearly fails when Samurai falls off and must run cross-country after the train and Augustus drops a key piece of equipment and must improvise. AndersonVision credits Barboni with a visual authority exceeding what the production budget implied, and the Spaghetti Western Database lists the photography as Metrocolor, Deltavision 1.85:1. Evidence: Wikipedia "The Five Man Army"; Spaghetti Western Database; AndersonVision; Trailers from Hell. Flag: SWDB lists the film as 1970 against Wikipedia's 1969; unresolved, minor.

**W18 -- They Call Him Cemetery (*Gli fumavano le Colt... lo chiamavano Camposanto*), 1971.** Spaghetti western comedy (screenplay). Grade A. Directed by Giuliano Carnimeo from a screenplay by Enzo Barboni, released 23 September 1971, starring Gianni Garko as Cemetery and William Berger as Duke, with music by Bruno Nicolai. Two naive McIntire brothers return from an eastern upbringing to visit their rancher father and run afoul of a local extortion gang, and a mysterious roving gunfighter named Cemetery protects them and teaches them to fight back. The Spaghetti Western Database judges that Carnimeo maintains a balance between irony and parody as successfully as any, working with a good script from Barboni, the creator of the Trinity films, on a predominantly light-hearted palette without getting lost in open comedy or slapstick. Evidence: Wikipedia "They Call Him Cemetery"; Spaghetti Western Database; IMDb tt0067142 (release 23 September 1971). [C30123]

## Evolution: early vs late

**1942-1969 -- The cameraman.** Barboni moves from camera operator to one of Italy's premier cinematographers, shooting Corbucci's *Django* with experimental Eastmancolour and Baldi's *Texas, Adios* in the same year, and on the *Texas, Adios* set he is already pitching the comedy western that will become *Trinity* -- to Nero, who is not interested. The signature is already there in negative: he knows exactly how the violent western is lit, framed and cut, because he lit, framed and cut it himself for a decade. **1970-1972 -- The breakthrough.** A serious debut that flops, then the same western grammar turned inside out into slapstick, with *Trinity Is Still My Name* becoming the highest-grossing Italian film to that point and *Man of the East* testing whether the formula works without Spencer -- it does, with Hill alone and a bicycle instead of a horse. **1973-1977 -- The transplant.** The buddy formula is lifted out of the West into 1930s New York and Miami, Hill is swapped for Gemma once, and the De Angelis brothers score the run, while the same duo's brand continues under other directors in films wrongly attributed to Barboni in the brief. **1981-1995 -- The long fade.** A war comedy without the duo, a spy parody and a double-role farce, a father-son road movie, a fantasy with Heaven and Hell, and finally a recast Trinity sequel that flops and ends his directing career. Through it all he never strayed from the slapstick formula after *Trinity*, and his films stayed slapstick comedies featuring Hill or Spencer almost throughout; when Hill and Spencer's careers started to wane, so did Barboni's. [C30124]

## M1 -- The slapstick brawl as choreographed dance

Barboni replaces the spaghetti-western quick-draw shootout, which lasts three seconds, with a long, escalating, room-wrecking fistfight in which the pleasure is the choreography, not the outcome. Bud Spencer's weapon is the overhead hammer punch that sends men flying, while Terence Hill adds flips, dodges and open-handed slaps. In the saloon scene in *They Call Me Trinity* (1970), Trinity outdraws two professional hitmen without even standing up, then slaps them repeatedly: the point is humiliation, not killing. In *Trinity Is Still My Name* (1971) the monastery fight runs about seven minutes, with the real monks joining in, and no blood is spilled. Hill and Spencer did their own stunts in the first film. What a viewer watches happen: a fight that keeps finding new objects and new bodies to throw, the room getting wrecked in stages, and everybody walking away alive.

**Takeaway:** a fight is a dance when the blows are the music -- stretch the three-second shootout into seven minutes of escalating wreckage, and make humiliation, not death, the payoff. [C30125]

## M2 -- The Trinity character machine

A hero who is simultaneously the most dangerous man in the West and the laziest, so every victory looks like an accident. Trinity is introduced asleep on a travois dragged by his horse through the desert, filthy and unbothered, and he stays unbothered for two films. Trinity is "the Right Hand of the Devil" and his brother Bambino is "the Left Hand of the Devil". Trinity is a lazy, ne'er-do-well gunfighter with unnaturally fast drawing ability and marksmanship, while Bambino is a horse thief posing as the town sheriff while he waits for his gang. The holy name against the devil's hands is the joke, and Trinity's power is never explained, only demonstrated: he drops two bounty hunters with one smooth back-handed movement, apparently without aiming.

**Takeaway:** give the hero a name that promises damnation and a body that promises sleep -- the gap between the two is where every gag lives. [C30126]

## M3 -- The eating gag (beans as running motif)

Eating is the plot engine and the character signature; food is what Trinity wants, what he is paid in, and what he fights over. *They Call Me Trinity* (1970) opens with Trinity eating a whole pan of beans at a way station, scraping the plate back into the pan and burping loudly; Hill reportedly fasted for 24 hours before filming so he could eat the whole pan in one take (reported, not confirmed). *Trinity Is Still My Name* (1971) opens with Bambino stealing beans and horses from four escaped convicts, and then shows both brothers eating a pan of beans each. Later in the sequel the brothers eat huge quantities of food at a fancy French restaurant with a notable lack of table manners. Eating scenes are a frequent comic element across the Hill/Spencer films, not a one-off -- and producer Italo Zingarelli is credited with inventing the "Beans Westerns" genre label for them.

**Takeaway:** make appetite the character sheet -- a hero defined by what he eats, in what quantity, and with how little ceremony, needs no backstory. [C30127]

## M4 -- The score as character

A whistled, singable title song that announces the film's tone before a word is spoken, then returns as a leitmotif that tells the audience nobody is really in danger. Franco Micalizzi composed *They Call Me Trinity* (1970), and the main theme "Trinity: Titoli" is a whistled, guitar-driven march with vocals by Annibale Giannarelli; the Trinity score was Micalizzi's first big success. Producer Italo Zingarelli first wanted a slower, broader, lullaby-like theme, so Micalizzi wrote one, but the editor complained the two heroes would almost have to move in slow motion to fit the music. At a meeting with Zingarelli and director Barboni, Micalizzi got his lively original theme accepted, partly because Barboni liked it. Quentin Tarantino reused Micalizzi's Trinity melody for the finale of *Django Unchained* (2012). The De Angelis brothers scored the sequel *Trinity Is Still My Name* (1971) and three more Barboni films, *Man of the East* (1972), *Even Angels Eat Beans* (1973) and *Crime Busters* (1977); the brothers also co-composed one track, "In Due Contro Tutti", on the first film's soundtrack (single-sourced).

**Takeaway:** the theme is the contract -- a whistled march tells the audience before the first punch that nobody will die, and the film then keeps that promise for two hours. [C30128]

## M5 -- The mismatched-duo formula

Two bodies that cannot be confused, two speeds that cannot sync, forced to share every scene. Hill and Spencer first teamed as a duo in Giuseppe Colizzi's *God Forgives... I Don't!* (1967). The contrast is the joke: Hill light, quick, blue-eyed and charming; Spencer hulking, grumpy, and settling arguments with a fist to the top of someone's skull. The sequel is built on a fake deathbed promise that forces the brothers to work together, and the film is a chain of them failing to do so while accidentally doing good. After Trinity the two co-starred in over a dozen more films. Barboni directed Hill and/or Spencer in both Trinity films (1970, 1971), *Man of the East* (1972, Hill), *Even Angels Eat Beans* (1973, Spencer), *Crime Busters* (1977), *Go for It* (1983) and *Double Trouble* (1984). Before acting Spencer -- born Carlo Pedersoli -- was a professional swimmer and water polo player, which is the physical base under the strongman half of the duo.

**Takeaway:** cast the contrast, not the characters -- one quick body and one heavy body, locked in the same frame, generate gags by physics alone. [C30129]

## M6 -- The western-to-comedy genre transplant

Keep every piece of spaghetti-western furniture, remove the killing, refill the space with slapstick. Barboni's directorial debut, *The Unholy Four* (1970), was a serious western that had little success. He then made his next film a slapstick parody of the spaghetti western, and *They Call Me Trinity* was an enormous hit; the sequel became, up to that point, the highest-grossing Italian movie ever. The furniture is all kept: ponchos, the travois, the saloon, bounty hunters, Mexican bandits, and the land-grabbing Major Harriman. The violence is emptied out: in the climax the pacifist Mormons win with dirty-fighting tricks after their leader finds in Ecclesiastes that "there is a time for fighting". Even the seven-minute monastery brawl spills no blood, with the monks participating.

**Takeaway:** a genre is its furniture, not its body count -- keep the saloon, the poncho and the bounty hunters, take out the killing, and the room that is left is a comedy stage. [C30130]

## M7 -- The sequel / variation machine

Same duo, same jokes, new setting each time, until the machine runs out of settings. Barboni never strayed from the successful formula: his subsequent films were all slapstick comedies and almost all featured either Hill or Spencer. *Man of the East* (1972) keeps Hill in the West, while *Even Angels Eat Beans* (1973) moves Spencer to 1930s New York alongside Giuliano Gemma as Sonny, a karate expert, with Spencer as Charlie, a wrestling champion. *Even Angels Eat Beans* got a same-series follow-up, *Charleston* (1974), directed by Barboni, with Gemma now paired with Ricky Bruch. *Crime Busters* (1977), *Go for It* (1983) and *Double Trouble* (1984) carry the duo into modern settings. In 1994 Barboni wanted to direct *Troublemakers* as the duo's swan song, but Terence Hill insisted on directing instead. *Sons of Trinity* (1995) tried to revive the formula with new actors and was a disaster at the box office, after which Barboni retired.

**Takeaway:** a formula is a machine with a finite number of settings -- change the city, the decade and the co-star, keep the duo, and the machine runs until the duo itself is gone. [C30131]

## M8 -- The cinematographer's wide frame

Comedy staged in wide, deep landscape shots by a man who shot the violent westerns first, so the joke lands against the same horizon the killings used to happen on. Barboni advanced to cinematographer in 1961, working several times with director Sergio Corbucci and gaining a high reputation. He shot 11 spaghetti westerns including *Django* (1966), *Texas, Adios* (1966), *The Hellbenders* (1967) and *The Five Man Army* (1969/1970). *Trinity* uses deep focus and the Gran Sasso landscape standing in for the American West, with a shot of Trinity being dragged across a dry riverbed; the sequel was shot extensively in Campo Imperatore, Abruzzo. This is the weakest of the eight mechanisms: the wide-frame staging claim rests on the Diffsense piece and the location credits, not on a shot-by-shot source -- and it is carried as a cinematographer's habit, not a proven staging rule. A candidate mechanism was tested and DROPPED: sped-up / undercranked comic timing -- two targeted searches found no source tying undercranking, or any camera-speed technique, to Barboni or to the Trinity fights, and the fast feel of the fights is evidenced as choreography plus sound effects plus slaps, not as a camera-speed claim.

**Takeaway:** shoot the joke in the same wide frame you shot the killing in -- the landscape remembers the violence, and the comedy is funnier for standing where it stood. [C30132]

## Aesthetic logic

Barboni's aesthetic is subtraction performed by a man who knew exactly what he was subtracting. He spent twenty-eight years learning the spaghetti western from inside the camera -- its mud, its wide angles, its claustrophobic close-ups, its ritual of the draw -- and then, in a single film, removed the one thing the genre could not do without and discovered the genre still stood. The logic runs: the western's furniture is funnier than its violence; the duo's bodies are funnier than their dialogue; the meal is funnier than the motive; the whistled theme is funnier than the gunshot. Everything in the films is built to be rewatched rather than followed: the plot is a clothesline, the gags are the clothes, and the audience already knows the clothes by the second film. That is why the machine could run for fourteen years on one idea and why it stopped dead the moment the duo was gone -- the formula was never Barboni's, it was Hill and Spencer's, and Barboni was its best stage manager. The memorial fact is the aesthetic fact: the most successful Italian film of its day was a comedy about a man too lazy to stand up, shot by a cinematographer who had spent his career making men die beautifully in the mud.
