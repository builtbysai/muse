# Rosalba Carriera: Study Notes

**Site:** Rosalba Giovanna Carriera (Thursday 12 January 1673 - Friday 15 April 1757), Venetian pastellist -- born in Venice, daughter of Andrea Carriera, a lawyer and clerk in the administration of the Republic, and Alba Foresti, an embroiderer and lacemaker; learned lace from her mother, painted miniatures on ivory for snuffbox lids, and became the pastellist who made pastel a finished portrait medium; was made Accademico di merito of the Accademia di San Luca in Rome on Sunday 27 September 1705 (Wikipedia says 1704); spent March 1720 to 1721 in Paris as the guest of the banker-collector Pierre Crozat, painting some 36 to 50 portraits including several of the boy king Louis XV and one of Antoine Watteau, and was elected to the Academie royale by acclamation on Saturday 26 October 1720; worked at Modena in 1723 and Vienna in 1730, where the Emperor Charles VI collected her pastels and she taught the Empress Elisabeth Christine; became the Dresden court's pastellist, with 157 pastels there at the peak and a Cabinet of Rosalba set up in 1746; her eyesight failed in the mid-1740s, cataract operations in 1749 gave only temporary relief, and she was completely blind by about 1750, living her last seven years in darkness; died in Venice, unmarried, leaving an estate of about 24,556 ducats. More than 700 artworks survive, over 400 of them pastels and miniatures; Dresden holds 73 pastels today, still the world's largest group. The signature works are the Self-Portrait Holding a Portrait of Her Sister of 1715 (Uffizi), the Louis XV as Dauphin of 1720-21 (Dresden), the Portrait of Antoine Watteau of 1721 (Treviso), the Self-Portrait as "Winter" of 1730-31 (Dresden), the Four Seasons, Four Elements and Four Continents allegory sets, and the Self-Portrait as an Old Woman of c.1746 (Royal Collection Trust). The modern record is Neil Jeffares's Dictionary of Pastellists Before 1800 entry and his translation of her Paris journal, Angela Oberer's monograph The Life and Work of Rosalba Carriera (2020), and Bernardina Sani's edition of her letters, diaries and fragments (1985, 2 vols.). This file is plain ASCII by house rule, so the French and Italian words in it are written without their accents here.

## M1. The Bound Stick -- Chalk Bound with Tailor's Chalk, Not Gum, and the Colour Range It Made

The mechanism is a stick, and the stick is a technology she made, not one she found. Wikipedia's Influence section states it plainly: she "revolutionised the world of technology by binding coloured chalk into sticks, which led to the development of a much wider range of prepared colours. This expanded the availability and the usefulness of the pastel medium." The point is prepared colours -- pre-mixed tints in stick form -- against loose chalk. Her own account survives in a letter to Giovan Battista Casotti of 26 April 1718, quoted by Jeffares from his Treatises: those who make pastels "non li legano con goma, ma con gesso da sartore o scagliola" -- they bind them not with gum but with tailor's chalk (gesso da sartore) or scagliola (gypsum). She adds that without some tint the sticks break and crumble ("si rompono e si sfarinano"), "ma tuttavia cosi piu m'accomodano" -- but they suit her better that way. The nuance is the whole mechanism: she preferred a weak, crumbly binder -- chalk and gypsum, not gum -- and accepted the fragility as the price of colour purity. The sticks she bought ready-made from Rome (Luti's circle, c.1704-05) and later preferred French pastels (by about 1712); Christian Cole's letter of 10 January 1705 records a shipment sent from London including "qualche pastelle del piu bel turchino, jallo et rosso" -- the finest blue, yellow and red, the expensive colours, made in his presence. She also wrote a handbook on making colours, "Different ways to form colors", kept in manuscript at the Venice State Archive, "the only 18th-century Venetian text of its kind". The watchable behaviour is the colour arriving as a stick that would rather break than hold together: a medium whose range exists because someone bound chalk loosely on purpose, and whose dust is the price of its freshness. **Takeaway:** a stick bound with tailor's chalk instead of gum, crumbly by choice -- the fragility is the trade for colour purity, and the prepared-colour range is the invention.

## M2. The Blue Paper on Canvas -- A Taut Ground That Does the Shadowing

Her support is a construction, and the construction does compositional work. The Royal Collection Trust's conservation of her Four Seasons pastels and Jeffares agree: a sheet of blue paper was wrapped around a canvas nailed to a wooden strainer -- a taut support like an easel painting, the blue paper attached to the back of the canvas with adhesive and small metal tacks at the edges. Jeffares: "in almost but not all cases the paper was blue, with a few white examples (she does not seem to have used parchment). In a good many cases, she worked directly on prepared canvas without paper; most (but not all) of these are larger, suggesting she adopted the method to avoid difficult paper joins." The blue ground shows through as shadow and mid-tone, unifies the image, and lets white chalk read as light; chain lines from the paper mould are visible in raking light. Pastels were smaller than oils, portable and quick -- ideal Grand Tourist souvenirs. The watchable behaviour is the blue doing half the drawing: a face that emerges because the ground is already a shadow, and the white chalk only has to say where the light is. **Takeaway:** blue paper wrapped on canvas over a strainer -- the ground is the shadow, the white chalk is the light, and the support is taut like a painting.

## M3. The Wet Paste and the Dry Brush -- Flesh Brushed On, Lace Dragged On

She did not only draw with the stick; she painted with it, wet and dry, in the same picture. The Royal Collection Trust: she began by loosely drafting detailed areas (fingers, flowers), probably with black pastel stick or charcoal; filled in large areas; worked faces and flesh tones last. "Dry powdery pastel in the background; flesh tones probably powdered pastel mixed with water, applied wet with a brush" to block out areas of colour. Eclectic Light: "She didn't just apply her pastels dry and from the stick, but in places made them into a paste using water, and brushed that onto the paper, a technique still thought innovative in the late nineteenth century." Light colours first, then darker sticks for shadowing. Blending -- "sweetening" -- with a finger or stump (rolled leather or paper), visible in the smoother area below the lips in Winter. Linear striations in the bust of Spring suggest ground pastel mixed with water, brushed on, then reworked with a modeller's comb, possibly to smooth skin. In Autumn she scraped away the top pastel layer to reveal white layers beneath, giving pearls their iridescent shine. Layering wet and dry pastel built rich impasto in the flowers and foliage of Spring. Highlights were pressed in with a pasty white stick and blended with finger or stump; dark outlines were drawn with the sharp edge of a harder pastel stick. And the dry brush: dragging the flat side of white chalk across a darker under-drawing to catch the shimmer of lace and satin and powdered hair -- Whitney Chadwick's phrase, "dragging the side of a piece of chalk across an under drawing in darker tones, she was able to capture the shimmering textures of lace and satin, and highlight facial features and soft cascades of powdered hair". The watchable behaviour is the hand changing tools mid-face: wet paste for the flesh, a dry drag for the lace, a scrape for the pearl, a comb for the skin -- four techniques in one head, and the lace shimmering because the chalk was dragged, not drawn. **Takeaway:** flesh as a wet paste brushed on, lace as a dry drag of white chalk, pearls as a scrape down to white -- the stick used as a brush, a comb and a knife in the same sitting.

## M4. Light First, Then Dark, Always from the Left -- The Gentle Light as a Rule

Her working order is a rule, and her lighting is a rule, and both are the same decision: nothing harsh. Light colours first, then darker sticks for shadowing (Royal Collection Trust). The light itself is "uniform and gentle, avoiding harsh shadows, invariably from the left" (Jeffares) -- except for the confusing catchlights in the Nymphe de la suite d'Apollon. The palette is "delicate and light... vaporous" (Jeffares), "purity and freshness of the tones" (Cochin, quoted by Jeffares), soft powdery greens and pinks for shadows, white and light highlights last. The dating consequence matters: "limited technical development after the first few known works" (Jeffares), so dating on stylistic grounds alone is hazardous -- the early pastels already resemble her tempera miniatures, luminous faces standing out from the surface. The watchable behaviour is the light never moving: every sitter lit from the same left side, gently, so the face is modelled by softness rather than by shadow, and the picture stays fresh because the darks were added last and sparingly. **Takeaway:** light first, dark last, and the light always from the left, gentle -- a portrait lit by a rule, so the softness is the style and the freshness survives.

## M5. The Ivory Miniature -- The Support That Is the Flesh

Before the pastel there was the miniature, and the miniature taught her everything the pastel then scaled up. With the fashion for snuff-taking she painted miniature portraits for snuffbox lids and as independent objects, and she was among the first -- Britannica says the first -- to use ivory instead of vellum as the support for miniatures. At least 80 such miniatures survive (History Walks); Jeffares, from the Sani 2007 catalogue, counts about 82 miniatures in tempera on ivory, plus about a dozen drawings and one oil painting. The technique is the support doing the work: she used the ivory's own tone for flesh, leaving it unpainted in places -- like watercolour white -- with pointillist-style shading in tiny areas. Her earliest known pastel portrait, the collector Anton Maria Zanetti (c.1700), is already a miniature's logic at a larger scale: a luminous face standing out from the surface. The suggestion to paint miniatures on ivory came, the sources say, from a French painter visiting Venice -- "Jean Steve" in the repeated citation -- though Jeffares notes Vleughels was in Venice in 1707 and c.1711, too late to be the artist who initiated her to pastel. The watchable behaviour is the ivory showing through: a face whose skin is not painted at all but is the support itself, left bare, and the paint only shading around it -- the smallest possible picture, made by not painting. **Takeaway:** ivory instead of vellum, the support left bare as the flesh -- the miniature is a portrait made by not painting the skin, and the pastel inherits the trick at full size.

## M6. The Speed of Pastel -- Fifty Portraits in Eighteen Months, and the Diary as the Ledger

Pastel is fast, and she turned the speed into a business. EBSCO: "Carriera found that she could work much faster with pastels than she could using oils. She could churn out a large number of portraits in a fraction of the time with pastels, and her subjects would not have to sit for her for many tedious hours like they had to when she was using oils." In Paris, March 1720 to 1721, she executed about 50 portraits (Jeffares, from the diary) -- Britannica counts 36 commissions -- at 22 zecchini a portrait, rising to 25; up to 240 zecchini for a set of the Four Seasons, 100 for the Prince Charles Edward Stuart portrait of 1740, her highest single-portrait fee; usually paid within the week. The diary she kept in Paris, August 1720 to September 1721, is the ledger of that speed: Jeffares, on the journal, says it is "principally of interest because of the people she encountered" and "there is little of literary interest in a document which had no such purpose" -- "on the two occasions she comments on her feelings, no context is given". Her preoccupations are sittings, commissions and payments, entertainment, dresses, religious observance. The record of the work IS the record of the life. The costumes in Paris were painted without the sitters present, from memory and pattern -- diaphanous muslin instead of heavy brocade -- because there was no time to sit for them. The watchable behaviour is the sitting as a short transaction: a face caught fast, the dress finished later without the sitter, the diary noting the price and the next appointment -- and fifty faces in eighteen months because the medium lets the sitter leave early. **Takeaway:** pastel as speed, the diary as the shop's ledger -- the face in one sitting, the dress without the sitter, fifty portraits in eighteen months because the medium is fast and the record is the work.

## M7. The Fragile Picture -- No Fixative, Glass Instead, and a Saint Behind the Pastel

Pastel falls off, and she never fixed it -- she protected it instead, and worried about it for fifty years. Jeffares's 3,222-line essay contains no mention of a fixative, and no fixative recipe of hers was found anywhere in the sources read. What she did instead: glass and careful packing. Pastels were framed under glass like oils; for travel she used thin black "cassette" frames -- a thin black wooden frame holding glass, a spacer and a backing board -- meant to be swapped into gilt frames on arrival. Her letters discuss packaging, glass types, and whether to ship with glass at all; to Crozat on 28 October 1718 she wrote that pastels need "un tres grand soin pour les conserver", asking whether she could use "pastels dont la couleur fut plus solide"; her instruction on a pastel sent to the Gran Principe Ferdinando de' Medici was that "nothing moist must come near it". And she tucked small devotional woodcut santini -- usually the Three Magi -- behind pastels as lucky charms for safe transit; examples have been found in Dresden (29 pastels), the Royal Collection, the Frick and the Met. "Throughout her career, Rosalba and her patrons were continually concerned with the safe arrival and well-being of her creations." The watchable behaviour is the picture travelling like a relic: a fragile face in a black cassette, a saint tucked behind it for luck, and a letter worrying about the light and the damp -- the fragility is the price of the colour, and the protection is part of the work. **Takeaway:** no fixative anywhere in the record -- glass, the travel cassette and a saint behind the pastel instead; the picture is fragile by design, and the worry about its arrival is the mechanism.

## M8. The Allegory Sets -- Four of Everything, Repeated Until the Workshop Knows Them by Heart

Alongside hundreds of portraits she made sets, and the sets are the product line: the Four Seasons, the Four Elements, the Four Continents, and the teste di fantasia -- idealised beautiful heads, like A Muse, ivy-wreathed, made for Augustus III. The sets were repeated many times, in many versions, by her and by the workshop -- copies and repetitions were a large part of output, sometimes entrusted to her sister Angela; her Four Seasons compositions were copied "starting with artists ranging from La Tour and Louis Vigee to Boucher". The Four Elements (Earth and Fire 1744; Air and Water 1746, Dresden) are the late statement of the form: baroque torsions developing into rococo compositions with freely floating bodies, painted as her eyesight was failing. The Continents (America c.1730, National Museum of Women in the Arts; Africa, Dresden) put a personification in the same small format as a portrait -- 42 x 33 cm -- so an allegory is the size of a face. The watchable behaviour is the set as a system: four pictures that belong together, each one a variation on the same format, repeated until the hand knows the composition by heart -- and the repetition is the point, because the buyer is buying one of four, and the four together are the work. **Takeaway:** four of everything, repeated by the workshop -- the allegory as a product line in portrait format, and the set, not the single picture, is the work.

## M9. The Self-Portrait Without Idealisation -- The Face at Every Age, Painted as It Was

She painted herself all her life, and she never flattered the self. The Uffizi Self-Portrait Holding a Portrait of Her Sister (1715, pastel, 71 x 57 cm) is "brusque and honest" -- a larger nose, thin lips, a dimple -- acquired by Cosimo III de' Medici for his self-portrait collection; the diploma miniature Self-Portrait as "Innocence" (1705, watercolour on ivory) got her into the Accademia di San Luca. The Self-Portrait as "Winter" (1730-31, Dresden, 46.5 x 34 cm) wears fur and ermine, and fur and hair become strengths -- looser, painterly marks. The Self-Portrait as an Old Woman (c.1745-46, Royal Collection Trust, 56.7 x 45.8 cm; a second version c.1746, Gallerie dell'Accademia, Venice) shows mismatched eyes, hinting at the eye problems -- one eye turned brown after surgery, the other not -- and a laurel crown on a dark ground. She painted herself as she was, at every age, and the last self-portraits are the record of a face losing its sight. The watchable behaviour is the honesty compounding: the same face at thirty, at fifty-seven and at seventy-three, each time unidealised, so the three portraits together are a life -- and the last one is a face watching itself go blind. **Takeaway:** a self-portrait at every age, never idealised -- the honest face is the through-line, and the old woman with mismatched eyes is the final statement, not a decline.

## M10. The Blindness -- The Work Ends Because the Sight Ends

She did not retire; she stopped because she could not see. Her eyesight began to deteriorate in the mid-1740s (1745-46); the last paintings are dated 1746; cataract operations in May 1749 gave only temporary relief before complete blindness ensued, as commonly occurs with the couching procedure; she was completely blind by about 1750 -- "for the last seven years of her life Carriera described living 'as though I were in the darkness of the night'" -- and died in Venice on Friday 15 April 1757. The sources disagree on the single year and on the operation count (two operations per Wikipedia and Eclectic Light; three per Hyperallergic and Oberer; blind 1746 per Mariette and Jeffares, 1749 per the National Gallery and the National Museum of Women in the Arts, 1750 per Hyperallergic, 1751 per the Apollo review of Oberer), so this study gives the sequence, never a single year. The slowdown began earlier, after her sister Giovanna's death in 1737 (1738 in Wikipedia; 1734 in History Walks), which precipitated depression and declining output; her mother died in 1738. The possible cause -- miniature painting in youth damaging the eyes -- is speculative, and is recorded as such. The watchable behaviour is the light going out of the work itself: the late Four Elements of 1744-46 are the last pictures, the self-portraits of c.1746 show the mismatched eyes, and then nothing -- eleven years of darkness in which the most famous pastellist in Europe made no more pastels. **Takeaway:** the career ends when the sight ends, not when the commissions end -- the last pictures are dated 1746, the operations of 1749 fail, and the blindness is the full stop.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Miniatures and first pastels, to c.1710. Born Thursday 12 January 1673 in Venice (7 October 1675 per Britannica, Fembio, Wikidata and most museum labels -- Monday 7 October 1675); lace-making from her mother Alba, drawing in a household where her father Andrea painted in his spare time; formal training undocumented, possibly brief instruction from Giuseppe Diamantini and Antonio Balestra, the National Gallery calling her "most likely self-taught". By 1700 painting miniatures on ivory for snuffbox lids, among the first to use ivory instead of vellum; at least 80 miniatures survive. First pastel portraits c.1703, the earliest known the collector Anton Maria Zanetti (c.1700); early royal sitters in Venice include Maximilian Emanuel II of Bavaria (1704), Frederick IV of Denmark (1708-09) and Augustus the Strong (1711). Made Accademico di merito of the Accademia di San Luca on Sunday 27 September 1705, her reception piece the miniature Self-Portrait as "Innocence". Scale is tiny; sitters are foreign visitors and collectors; the face is already luminous against the surface.

Phase 2 -- Paris, 1720-21, and the return. Guest of Pierre Crozat from March 1720, travelling with her mother, both sisters and her brother-in-law Pellegrini (employed by John Law to paint the ceiling of the Grande Salle, Hotel de Nevers); about 50 portraits in eighteen months, mostly pastel, at 22 zecchini rising to 25; several portraits of the boy king Louis XV, one of Watteau (February 1721, for Crozat, after several sittings), the Regent and the court; elected to the Academie royale by acclamation on Saturday 26 October 1720, the first foreign-born woman artist to receive such an honour; her reception piece, the Nymphe de la suite d'Apollon, followed from Venice and reached Paris in January 1722. The Rococo lightness crystallises here: diaphanous muslin costumes painted without the sitters present, from memory and pattern, under the pressure of the work. Returned to Venice in 1721; then Modena in 1723 (five months, portraits of Princess Enrichetta Anna for prospective husbands), Parma, and Gorizia in 1728 (three imperial-prince portraits, all lost).

Phase 3 -- Vienna and Dresden, 1730-1746. Six months in Vienna in 1730 at the court of Emperor Charles VI, who collected over 150 of her pastels; she gave the Empress Elisabeth Christine formal artistic training; the Maria Theresa portrait of 1730 and the Self-Portrait as "Winter" of 1730-31 date from the Vienna years. The Dresden court accumulation follows: Augustus III, who had sat for her in 1711 and 1713, amassed 150 or more pastels, bought everything in the studio on his 1739 visit, and in 1746 set up a separate pastel cabinet, the Cabinet of Rosalba, in the Johanneum. Mature work is larger in scale -- Crown Prince Friedrich Christian of Saxony 1739-40 (63.5 x 51.5 cm), Gentleman in Red 1740 (65 x 50 cm) -- and mixes court portraiture with allegorical fancy pictures: the Four Continents (c.1730), the Four Seasons, the Four Elements (1744-46). Sitters are now emperors, kings, electors and the English Grand Tourists (Coulson Fellowes 1724, sold September 2025 for 508,000 pounds, a record for the artist; Sir James Gray c.1744-45, Getty).

Phase 4 -- Failing sight and blindness, 1746-1757. Output slows after Giovanna's death in 1737 and her mother's in 1738; eyesight deteriorates from the mid-1740s; the last paintings are dated 1746; cataract operations in May 1749 give only temporary relief; completely blind by about 1750. The Self-Portrait as an Old Woman (c.1745-46) is the last datable statement, the mismatched eyes its subject. She lives blind for another seven years in her small house in Dorsoduro, outliving all her family, and dies on Friday 15 April 1757, buried in San Vio church, Dorsoduro (the church later demolished; no tomb survives). Her estate at death is 24,556 ducats gross by the posthumous inventory of 7 May 1757. The afterlife is immediate and long: her pastels fill the Dresden cabinet; the French generation -- La Tour, Perronneau, Liotard -- copies her; the English line -- Cotes, Russell, Read -- calls itself by her name; the 2023 Dresden exhibition "Elegant Encounters. Rosalba Carriera -- Perfection in Pastel" marks the 350th anniversary of the 1673 birth date.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is softness as truth, and fragility as its price. Pastel had been a sketch medium; she made it a finished portrait medium by binding the chalk loosely, working it wet and dry on a blue ground, and lighting every face gently from the left -- so the picture is fresh because the hand was light, and fragile because the binder was weak. The face comes first and the dress comes later, often without the sitter; the likeness is flattering yet realistic, the softening is the style, not a failure of likeness. The picture is small, portable and quick, made for a market that came to her -- the Grand Tourists, the courts, the collectors -- and the diary that records the sittings and the prices is the same document as the life. The allegory sets repeat the portrait format for gods and seasons, so a personification is the size of a face. The self-portraits refuse idealisation at every age, and the last ones record the sight failing. The work ends when the sight ends, and the eleven blind years are the proof that the career was the seeing, not the selling. What she made was not a new medium but a new status for one: pastel as the serious portrait, the chalk stick as a technology, the blue paper as the shadow, the glass as the only fixative. The Rococo lightness that dominated the arts after her Paris year is, in part, her lightness -- the vaporous style, the dancing lights, the subtle surface tonalities -- carried into France in a satchel of crumbly sticks.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Pastel is a serious portrait medium, not a sketch medium. Until her, pastels were seen as a means for creating sketches; she used them for finished portraits, and the academies followed -- Carriera, La Tour, Vigee Le Brun, Cotes and Russell were all accepted into their country's academy or appointed as pastelists to royalty. Evidence: Met essay; PSU Unspoken Artists; EBSCO.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] The chalk stick itself is a technology. She bound coloured chalk into sticks, widening the prepared-colour range. The medium's range is a thing she made, not a thing she found. Evidence: Wikipedia, Influence; her 1718 letter to Casotti.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] Ivory over vellum for miniatures. She was the first to use ivory, not vellum, as the support for miniatures; she used transparent washes on the areas where her subjects' skin showed, allowing the ivory to peek through. Evidence: Real Pearl Co; EBSCO.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] Speed is a virtue. She could work much faster with pastels than with oils, churn out a large number of portraits in a fraction of the time, and her subjects would not have to sit for tedious hours. Pastel's speed is why the volume was possible. Evidence: EBSCO.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] The face first: flattering yet realistic. Her "flattering yet realistic style and soft colours"; Clement: she "excelled in portraits of pretty women". The softening is the style, not a failure of likeness. Evidence: Real Pearl Co; Clement 1904.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The diary as a working ledger. Jeffares on the Paris journal: the diary is "principally of interest because of the people she encountered"; "there is little of literary interest in a document which had no such purpose"; "on the two occasions she comments on her feelings, no context is given". Her preoccupations are sittings, commissions and payments, entertainment, dresses, religious observance. The record of the work IS the record of the life. Evidence: Jeffares journal.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] Fragility is the price. Her pastels were inherently fragile and "throughout her career, Rosalba and her patrons were continually concerned with the safe arrival and well-being of her creations"; she "entrusted the 'safe outward journey' of her pastels to small woodcuts representing the Three Kings". Evidence: 2023 conference paper abstract, based on first-hand examination of almost two hundred pastels by Carriera and her circle; Jeffares.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] No court appointment, no single patron. Oberer's monograph frames her as "an independent single artist in eighteenth-century Venice"; Gribaudi: she "never had or wanted a patron". Tag the "never wanted" as a claim about her, contradicted in part by how much of her income came from courts -- the independence is in having no single master, not in having no patrons. Evidence: Oberer; Gribaudi via L'Italo-Americano.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] The family workshop. Giovanna, unmarried, was her lifelong assistant, laying in backgrounds and underdrawings; Angela married Pellegrini, who worked beside her; the household travelled together (Paris 1720: mother, both sisters, brother-in-law). The 30 June 1720 journal entry shows Giovanna's own works being given to Crozat. Evidence: Jeffares journal.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] Self-portrait without idealisation. The Uffizi self-portrait (1715) is "brusque and honest" -- larger nose, thin lips, a dimple; the Self-Portrait as an Old Woman (c.1746) shows mismatched eyes. She painted herself as she was, at every age. Evidence: leg 1; leg 3.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] Allegory as the non-portrait outlet. Alongside hundreds of portraits: the Four Seasons, the Four Elements, the Four Continents (America c.1730, NMWA; Africa, Dresden), mythological selves (Self-Portrait as "Winter", 1730-31; Nymph from Apollo's Retinue, the Academie reception piece). Evidence: leg 2; leg 3.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] Teaching as transmission. The Empress Elisabeth Christine was given formal artistic training; female pupils learned in the studio (Carlevarijs, Terzi, Sartori); Katherine Read, at 30, received instruction from the 80-year-old blind Carriera in Venice in 1753. The line runs through women. Evidence: leg 3.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] Academy membership by acclamation, against the rule. Elected to the Academie royale by acclamation on Saturday 26 October 1720, despite the rule against more women members; Accademia di San Luca 1705 (1704 per some sources). Evidence: EBSCO; Jeffares.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] The Rococo as a style she helped originate in France. Her work "contributed to form the new tastes of the time" and her "flattering yet realistic style and soft colours were injected into the Rococo style which soon dominated the arts"; she "became a major proponent of the Rococo art movement". Evidence: Real Pearl Co; PSU.
- P15. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The studio as a shop. The diary's repeated English sitters, the promised and re-promised sittings, the Dresden bulk purchases and the 1739 studio buy-out: the work was made for a market that came to her, and the diary is the shop's ledger. The trade itself is from the record; the "shop" framing is mine. Evidence: Jeffares journal; leg 3.
- P16. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The pretty-woman head as a product. The volume of similar heads, the erotic-pastel strand (Oberer's chapters "Carriera's Daring Eroticism" and "Carriera's Clients of Erotic Art"), and the workshop repetitions point to a repeatable type, not only a likeness. Flag: the erotic strand is documented in scholarship; the "product" reading is mine. Evidence: Oberer; leg 3.
- P17. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] Blindness as the end of work, not a choice. She stopped because she could not see -- deterioration from 1745, blind 1746/1749, two failed cataract surgeries -- not because she retired. The facts are from the record; the "not a choice" is the reading. Evidence: National Gallery; Jeffares; leg 1.
- P18. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The Dresden monument. The 150+ pastels, the Cabinet of Rosalba (1746), the 73 that survive there now: the collection is the career's archive, assembled by a buyer, not by her. The numbers are from the record; the framing is mine. Evidence: SKD; Jeffares.
- P19. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The English line as her second inheritance. Cotes to Russell to Read, each called "the Rosalba of England/Britain" in turn: the English pastel school is her lineage by another name. The people and the nicknames are from the record; the lineage claim is mine. Evidence: leg 3.
- P20. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The sister as the shadow career. Giovanna's hand is inside the famous works (backgrounds, underdrawings, her own pieces given to Crozat), uncredited and unmarried, dying in 1737/38 and leaving Carriera in the depression the sources record. The facts are from the record; the "shadow career" is mine. Evidence: Jeffares journal; leg 3.

Reception position (for the record, not counted above): she was "often treated as an exception, a rarity as a woman artist" and "very often ignored" (Wikipedia, after Parker and Pollock) -- dismissed when the Rococo fell from fashion, then recovered as the woman who made pastel a serious medium.

- C1. "BROUGHT PASTEL TO FRANCE" IS FALSE AS STATED -- AND #507'S TOP-PICK LINE IS WRONG. The briefed doorway ("the woman who brought pastel to France") and #507's top-pick line repeat Macfall's 1909 romantic line, "Pastel came into France out of Italy, brought thereto in the satchel of the Venetian lady Rosalba Carriera, in 1720". The record says otherwise: EB1911 (Pastel article) says that when she accepted the invitation to Paris in 1720 she "found the art of pastel-painting well established" there, and Thea Burns's research (via Eclectic Light) establishes Robert Nanteuil's portraits of the 1660s as among the first true pastel paintings -- pastel was in France sixty years before her. VERDICT: she did not import pastel; she made it fashionable, elevated it from a sketch medium to finished portraiture, and set the model the French generation copied. The doorway holds in substance and fails in the letter, which is how the check is recorded.
- C2. "PAINTED WATTEAU TWICE" IS NOT VERIFIED -- AND #507'S NEW FROM #507 BLOCK REPEATS IT. One Crozat-commissioned portrait is solidly documented: Watteau sat for her at Nogent-sur-Marne in February 1721, after several sittings (Mollett 1883; Goncourt 1875; Jeffares journal). Only the Penn State student site "Unspoken Artists" says she painted him twice, and no second distinct portrait appears in Jeffares, in the Wikimedia paintings list, or in the Watteau literature searched -- the parent's own search (319 lines) returned the single portrait in every source that names one. VERDICT: say "she painted Watteau (1721, for Crozat, after several sittings)"; do NOT say twice. The carried #507 block below keeps its "painted twice" wording verbatim, as the carry discipline requires; the correction lives here.
- C3. THE RECEPTION PIECE DID NOT ARRIVE IN 1720. The pastel of Louis XV she brought to the Academie assembly on 26 October 1720 was NOT her morceau de reception. After returning to Venice she sent the Nymphe de la suite d'Apollon -- a nymph presenting a laurel wreath to the Academie -- announced to Crozat 4 July 1721, completed 10 October 1721, arrived Paris January 1722, now in the Louvre. VERDICT: "elected 26 October 1720 by acclamation; her reception piece, the Nymphe de la suite d'Apollon, followed from Venice and reached Paris in January 1722."
- C4. THE FATHER IS ANDREA, NOT GIOVANNI -- AND THE BRIEF IS WRONG. The task brief says her father was "Giovanni Carriera". All sources say Andrea Carriera (Chioggia 24 February 1645 - Venice 1 April 1719, per Jeffares's genealogy; lawyer and clerk, son of a painter, painter in his spare time). Giovanni Antonio Pellegrini (1675-1741) is her brother-in-law. VERDICT: never call the father Giovanni anywhere.
- C5. "700" IS SURVIVING ARTWORKS, NOT LETTERS -- AND #507'S TOP-PICK LINE IS WRONG. Getty (2023): "more than 700 surviving artworks" attributed to her; Jeffares and Sani 2007 catalogue: about 440 works accepted, three-quarters in pastel, about 82 miniatures. The letters: no total count was found in any source consulted; what is established is that she began collecting her correspondence in 1700 and Bernardina Sani published Rosalba Carriera: lettere, diari, frammenti (Olschki, 1985) in two volumes. The network leg notes "~700 (Sani ed. 1985, 2 vols., 1700-death)" against "550 letters" (L'Italo-Americano) -- both figures circulate for the correspondence, and neither is verified here. VERDICT: do not write "700 surviving letters" anywhere; write "more than 700 artworks survive; her correspondence, published in two volumes by Sani (1985), is unusually extensive for an 18th-century woman artist".
- C6. BIRTH YEAR -- 1673 OR 1675, AND #507'S DATE CONFLATES THE TWO. Jeffares (the scholarly standard), Wikipedia, SKD and Oberer give 12 January 1673 (Jeffares: "two years earlier than widely reported", citing Zava Boccazza 1996a and Moretti 2011); Britannica, Fembio, Wikidata and most museum labels give 7 October 1675; History Walks adds 12 January 1672 M.V. (Venetian calendar, new year 1 March -- i.e. 1673 modern). #507's top-pick line writes "7 October 1673", which takes the day and month of the 1675 date and the year of the 1673 date -- a date no source gives. VERDICT: born in Venice in 1673 (12 January, per Jeffares; older sources give 7 October 1675). Died Friday 15 April 1757, uncontested, aged 84 on the 1673 date.
- C7. ACCADEMIA DI SAN LUCA -- 1704 OR 27 SEPTEMBER 1705. Wikipedia says made Accademico di merito in 1704; Jeffares says admitted 27 September 1705 as "pittrice e miniatrice veneziana", reception piece a miniature on ivory; Frick and Stephen Ongpin also say September 1705. VERDICT: prefer 1705, note 1704.
- C8. GIOVANNA'S DEATH -- 1737, 1738 OR 1734. Jeffares's essay says 1737; Wikipedia's biography says 1738; History Walks says 1734 in one passage (likely a slip -- it also says she died "aged 54", which fits 1683-1737). VERDICT: use 1737 with the others flagged; the production slowdown follows it either way.
- C9. BLINDNESS -- A SEQUENCE, NOT A YEAR; OPERATIONS TWO OR THREE. National Gallery: eyesight began to deteriorate 1745, permanently blind by 1749. Jeffares: blind "by 1745 as recorded by Katherine Read (who visited her in 1753), or, more reliably, in 1746, according to Mariette, who related her decision in May 1749 to undergo cataract operations"; History Walks: eye illness 1746, last paintings that year, operation 1749, completely blind again the year after (1750); Hyperallergic and Oberer: three operations, blind from about 1750 ("for the last seven years of her life"); NMWA: by 1749 permanently blind; Britannica: "After 1750 Carriera's sight failed"; Apollo review of Oberer: blindness in 1751; Wikipedia and Eclectic Light: two unsuccessful cataract surgeries. VERDICT: "Her eyesight failed in the mid-1740s (1745-46); cataract operations in 1749 gave only temporary relief, and she was completely blind by about 1750." Never give a single precise year or a single operation count.
- C10. DRESDEN HOLDINGS -- 157 AT PEAK, 73 TODAY. EB1911: "The Dresden Museum contains 157 examples of her work in this medium"; Jeffares: the collection "at one stage held 157 pastels by her (although now reduced to some 72)"; SKD (2023 exhibition): "With 73 pastels, Dresden possesses the world's largest collection". VERDICT: "Dresden once held 157; 73 survive there today, still the world's largest group." Do not write "Dresden 100+" as a current figure without qualification, as #507's top-pick line does.
- C11. PARIS OUTPUT -- ABOUT 50 OR 36; "ABOUT 40 DAUPHIN PORTRAITS" IS NOT CREDIBLE. Jeffares, from the diary: about 50 portraits on the trip; Britannica: 36 commissions; Women's Art Tours: "about 40 portraits of the Dauphin". The last figure garbles the trip total into a single sitter. VERDICT: "several portraits of the boy king, among some 36 to 50 Paris commissions".
- C12. LEG 1 LINE-COUNT CORRECTION (PARENT). The biography leg claimed 1,217 lines viewed in the Jeffares essay PDF, but its own windows -- lines 0-255, 255-629 and 900-1217 -- sum to 946 lines (255 + 374 + 317). The leg's in-part total is therefore 1,137 lines (191 Wikipedia + 946 Jeffares), not 1,408; its article total is 1,698, not 1,969; the study's grand total is 17,600, not 17,871. VERDICT: the corrected figures are used in the Depth line above.
- C13. LOUIS XV "AS DAUPHIN" IS A MISNOMER. He had been king since 1715, aged 5; "Dauphin" is the traditional title of the Dresden picture, not his rank in 1720-21. Dresden correctly calls him King. VERDICT: "Louis XV as a boy", with the traditional title noted.
- C14. "150 PASTELS" IS ATTACHED TO TWO PATRONS. Wikipedia says Charles VI amassed "more than 150"; NMWA says Augustus III "amassed more than 150". The figure circulates for both; attribute it carefully per source, never as a single count.
- C15. COTES "WORKED UNDER ROSALBA CARRIERA" IS AN OVERSTATEMENT. EB1911 says Cotes "worked under Rosalba Carriera"; Wikipedia softens it to admirer, trained with George Knapton (strongly influenced by Carriera). VERDICT: admirer in her line, not her pupil.
- C16. THE VIENNA PUPIL -- EMPRESS OR FUTURE EMPRESS. Wikipedia, NMWA and Britannica say she gave formal artistic training to the Empress Elisabeth Christine; biographs.org says the pupil was the future Empress Maria Theresa instead. VERDICT: Elisabeth Christine, with the alternative flagged.
- C17. THE GRANDFATHER-A-PAINTER CLAIM IS REFUTED. Jeffares says the suggestion her grandfather was a painter "seems to be a confusion with Andrea Pasqualino arising from Vianelli" -- do not repeat it.
- C18. CATERINA SAGREDO BARBARIGO IS A SITTER, NOT A PUPIL. The brief's suggestion she was a pupil is not supported by any source found; she is a Venetian aristocrat, salonniere and casino holder, the sitter of several famous Carriera pastels (including as Berenice, Detroit; Dresden c.1735-40). VERDICT: sitter only, never a #509 candidate.
