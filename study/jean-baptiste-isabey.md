# Jean-Baptiste Isabey: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean-Baptiste Isabey (Saturday 11 April 1767 - Wednesday 18 April 1855), French miniaturist, portraitist, lithographer and designer of ceremonies -- born in Nancy, in the Duchy of Lorraine, the second son of Jacques Isabey, a grocer; died in Paris, at his apartment at the Institut, 25 quai Conti, of a chest fluxion, aged 88, and buried in Pere-Lachaise cemetery, 20th division, alongside both wives, his son Eugene and his brother Louis. He was David's pupil from 1788 and his assistant on Les Amours de Paris et d'Helene; he painted snuff-box lids and buttons to live before he painted queens; he made 228 portraits of deputies of the Revolutionary Assembly at six francs each and survived the Revolution on the strength of them; he was Marie-Antoinette's miniaturist, then drawing master to Josephine's children, then "Premier Peintre de ma Chambre" to the Empress, then drawing master to Marie-Louise, then portraitist to Louis XVIII, designer of the coronation of Charles X, deputy curator of the royal museums under Louis-Philippe, and pensioner and Commander of the Legion of Honour under Napoleon III -- painter, in substance if not in a sourced title, to every sovereign of France from Marie-Antoinette to Napoleon III. His fatherhood is the doorway of this study: his son and pupil was Eugene Isabey (1803-1886), the Romantic marine painter of #503, and in 1821 he titled a lithograph of the boy "Mon fils, mon eleve et mon ami". The signature works are Napoleon at Malmaison (April 1801 - September 1802), the Review of Troops by the First Consul (1802), the coronation costumes and the Livre du Sacre (1804-1815), the Table des Marechaux (1808-1810), the King of Rome breast-pin miniature (1811), the Congress of Vienna watercolour (1814-15, the delegates on Friday 3 February 1815), the Sevres breakfast service of the twelve months (1817), and La Barque (1797, with a Salon date that will not settle -- see C1). He exhibited at twenty-one Salons, 1791 to 1841, and never won a Salon medal -- none was found in any source, and the medals that surface in searches belong to his son and to his pupil Thomas (see C5). The modern revival is the exhibition "Jean-Baptiste Isabey (1767-1855), portraitiste de l'Europe", Malmaison, Tuesday 18 October 2005 to Monday 9 January 2006, then Nancy, Saturday 28 January to Tuesday 18 April 2006. ## M1. Miniature Watercolour on Ivory -- Stipple (Pointille) and Fine Brush over a Luminous Ground
The whole practice starts here, and it starts small: a King of Rome head 3 by 2.2 cm for a breast-pin, a Marie Louise 3.4 by 2.4 cm inside a bonbonniere, a Met Napoleon of 1810 five by three centimetres, a Murat enamel 3.5 by 3 cm. The technique is translucent watercolour and gouache laid in tiny dots -- stipple, pointille -- and hairline strokes on a thin ivory plaque. The ivory itself supplies the skin light: pigment is kept thin so the ground glows through, and modelling is built by dot density, not by opaque blending. NiceArtGallery: "stippling technique alongside fine brushstrokes to achieve delicate modelling and luminous skin tones"; Google Arts and Culture on the Portrait of a Woman (1835, watercolour on vellum): "delicate stipple technique"; Wikioo on Mrs Rufus Prime (1828, watercolour on ivory board): "stipple technique to achieve refinement and depth within a shallow perspective". A miniature is a density field, not a small painting: tone is a count of dots per square millimetre, and the ground is the lamp. The same hand, retouching the Portrait of a Woman eight years after painting it, replaced the sitter's "Apollo's knot" hairstyle with ringlets and added gauzy sleeves -- the portrait as a living object, maintained like a garment (see the eleventh mechanism, folded into JBI7). **Takeaway:** build the face from dot density on a glowing ground; never paint the light, only the density that lets it through. Smallness is not a limitation of the medium; it is the medium's argument.

## M2. The Ground as Light Source -- Ivory against Vellum and Paper
Ivory is non-absorbent and reflective: washes sit on the surface and stay luminous. Vellum and paper absorb more and give a matter, chalkier face. Isabey switches ground with register: ivory for court and diplomatic miniatures, vellum and paper for private or late portraits -- the Portrait of a Woman of 1835 is watercolour on vellum, 12.3 by 17.4 cm, larger and softer than the ivory court pieces. The choice is visible in the museum medium lines; the optical explanation (gum-arabic binding, ivory versus vellum supports) comes from aggregator text, not a conservator's report, and is graded plausible, not lab-verified. What matters for the work is the rule: the ground does the lighting, the pigment does the interrupting. **Takeaway:** choose the ground before the subject; the ground is the light source and the palette is the shadow cast on it. A portrait that changes ground changes register.

## M3. Maniere Noire -- Pencil and Stump Simulating Engraving
Black chalk and pencil worked with a paper stump (estompe) to smudge continuous tone, imitating mezzotint and engraving without a plate. The Departure and The Return (1794) made his reputation in this fashionable late-18th-century mode; La Barque (1797, see C1) is its peak -- Web Gallery of Art: he "exploited contrasts of light and shade with considerable success". Light is rubbed into being, not drawn as line; figures emerge from a dark ground by smudge gradient. The mode matters because it is the bridge between the miniature and the drawing at larger scale: the same hand that stippled a three-centimetre face could smudge a family in a boat at near-life size. **Takeaway:** tone first, line second; chiaroscuro by smudge gradient, figures emerging from dark. The watchable behaviour is the emergence: the picture is rubbed, not drawn.

## M4. Bistre and Sepia Wash -- The Fast Portrait and the Caricature
Brown ink (bistre) and wash over graphite on paper, worked quickly at near-life scale. The Salon-1824 set shows the mode's range: eight sheets, 20 to 28 cm, affectionate and biting likenesses of fellow artists -- Adolphe Ladurner, Emile the son-in-law, Aubry, Machen, Mengal the horn player, "Six Pouces !!!", Thomas, Eugene himself -- drawn from life, not from state. The same wash language serves the Sevres month designs (Morgan: "brown ink and wash over graphite") and the Sevene factory visit (pen and grey ink, wash over black chalk, 339 by 508 mm). One warm monochrome plus wash density equals volume; speed and shorthand are the style, not a compromise. **Takeaway:** one warm monochrome, wash density as volume, speed as the signature. The caricature and the factory design are the same drawing in different clothes.

## M5. The Intimate State Portrait -- The Malmaison Code
Instead of throne and regalia, Isabey puts the ruler in a private place -- the Malmaison garden, the house sketched behind -- in uniform, with a small set of repeatable signs: bicorne worn parallel to the shoulders, hand slipped into the waistcoat, a determined and thoughtful face. Napoleon.org (Malmaison curators Caude and Tamisier-Vetois, July 2021): "a whole range of symbolism accompanies this portrait... an image that is more intimate than official"; the portrait, April 1801 to September 1802, is "one of those that best embodies that period of the Consulate". It is repeatedly called the best likeness of Napoleon ever executed (Britannica, Web Gallery of Art, Moeller, NiceArtGallery). The portrait works because the signs are few and fixed: the same hand-in-waistcoat reappears in the Belliard lithograph of 1832 and in engravings for decades. Power is signalled by restraint and repetition, not by accumulation. The Napoleon anecdote -- "Isabey is the only painter who has made me look like a man" -- was NOT found in any source searched (leg 1, targeted search, 245 lines) and must not be used as fact; what is sourced is Napoleon's own doctrine of likeness, said to David: "It is not the exactitude of the features, every small spot on the nose, that make the resemblance. It is the character of the physiognomy, what animates it, that must be painted" (see C0). **Takeaway:** three or four fixed attributes plus a private backdrop equals a portable political identity; change the backdrop, keep the attributes. The likeness is a code, and the code is the portrait.

## M6. Serial Documentary Portraiture -- The Assembly as a System
228 deputies of the Assemblee Legislative (c. 1791-93) as a set, not single portraits -- six francs each, paid portrait by portrait; the Congress of Vienna watercolour as a dated moment (Friday 3 February 1815, Wellington's arrival to replace Castlereagh) with every delegate individually likened and then framed by a border programme (Truth, Prudence, Wisdom and Justice at the corners; the rulers' heads above; their arms below); the Review of Troops (1802) as a crowd composed of identifiable officers. The unit is the roster: likeness times N, organised by occasion. Vienna studio practice: many sittings, signed "Isabey a Vienne" -- the signature itself marks the documentary claim. One anecdote is flagged, not promoted: that he used dolls or mannequins in miniature costume to plan group compositions (NiceArtGallery, single source; see C18). **Takeaway:** a crowd is a database of heads plus one staging rule plus one border programme. The watchable behaviour is the census: the picture fills up, head by head, and the frame tells you what the occasion was.

## M7. Ceremony Design -- Costume, Staging and the Official Book as One Image System
Isabey does not just paint the coronation; he designs it. The coronation mantel working drawing (c. 1804, Christie's): the garment is engineered -- fabric Vacher, tailor Chevallier, embroidery Picot, 10,000 francs of gold and silver thread, tailoring 500 livres -- and then recorded in the Livre du Sacre (1805-15, 38 plates after Isabey, Fontaine and Percier, 194,436 francs, edition of 100 sequestrated after the fall). The same role for Josephine's gown (bee motif, made by LeRoy) and for court fetes at the Tuileries, Saint-Cloud and Malmaison (Dessinateur du Cabinet, 1805; Ordonnateur des rejouissances, 1804). He staged the 2 December 1804 coronation at Notre-Dame with about 100 dressed wooden dolls and a scale plan of the cathedral (napoleon.org). The portrait, the costume and the publication are three outputs of one design. **Takeaway:** design the event first, then the image is a record of the design. Costume plates are a system, not decoration; the ceremony is the work and the picture is its residue.

## M8. The Reproduction Network -- Lithography, Engraving and the Afterlife of a Drawing
A single Isabey drawing spawns a tree: Malmaison to the Lingee and Godefroy engraving, to Geoffroy in Thiers' Histoire du Consulat et de l'Empire (1845), to the Belliard lithograph (1832); La Barque to Landon's engraving; Louis XVIII to Debucourt's engraving; the Congress to Godefroy's print (1819). He was "one of the earliest artists to experiment with lithography" (Annex; AbeBooks): Divers Essais Lithographiques (Paris, for the author, 1818) -- 14 leaves, lithographed by Engelmann, Langlume, Lasteyrie and Motte, seven with a sepia tint, small blind stamp; Mon fils, mon eleve et mon ami (1821) as a lithographic portrait statement; Voyage en Italie (1822, 30 lithographs). Lithography lets a draughtsman publish drawing itself, without an engraver's translation. **Takeaway:** one source drawing, many print states; the print is the distribution system for the likeness. The watchable behaviour is the branching: the same face reappears, slightly translated, in a new medium each time.

## M9. Porcelain Translation -- Drawing to Sepia on Sevres; the Month and Hour Cycle
Designs made in watercolour and brown wash are translated by factory painters (Develly) into sepia on porcelain cups and plates. The 1817 breakfast service (Morgan, 12 drawings) is the fullest case: 12 scenes equal 12 months times 12 two-hour slices of a country man's day, cycling from January 11pm-1am (Sleep) to December 9-11pm (Vigil in the Stable) -- Threshing in the Barn, Stable Chores, Livestock Heading to the Fields, Procession of Rogations, The Market, Haymaking, The Harvest, Sowing, Pressing of Grapes, Supper at the Farm -- so the service is a clock and a calendar you drink from. The Table des Marechaux (Napoleon enthroned, surrounded by his marshals' medallions, on a large porcelain slab, Salon 1810, now at Malmaison) is the same translation at monumental scale. Studio at Sevres from 1809; he directed the studio under Louis-Philippe. The second service, on the life of a man of the world, was proposed and never executed -- and its subject has been crossed with the first service's in one leg's notes (see C27). **Takeaway:** a cycle (month times hour) mapped onto a set of identical objects; the set, not the single piece, is the work. The watchable behaviour is the rotation: the same cup, a different hour.

## M10. The Miniature as a Diplomatic Object -- Mounting, Portability, Gift Economy
The image is inseparable from its mount: snuff-box lids (V and A gold box, Napoleon in imperial robe with a bee on the reverse; diplomatic snuff-boxes revived in 1804 with a strict hierarchy and budget, "from simple tortoiseshell to more lavish versions"), bonbonnieres (Marie Louise, 3.4 by 2.4 cm in blond tortoiseshell, provenance Eugenie to Prince Victor Napoleon to Prince Louis Napoleon), bracelets (King of Rome, 25 fine gold chains, given by Napoleon to Marie-Louise after the birth), breast-pins (the King of Rome tiny head). Napoleon commissioned "hundreds of miniatures for distribution as gifts" (New International Encyclopaedia); fr.wikipedia: "Il livre des dizaines de portraits en miniature de l'Empereur, que celui fait monter en boite pour ses presents." He bequeathed snuff-boxes in his will. The miniature's power is that it travels -- in a pocket, on a wrist, on a box -- and its smallness is its intimacy and its politics. **Takeaway:** scale plus mount plus occasion equals meaning; the frame is part of the portrait. The watchable behaviour is the handover: the object leaves the hand it was painted in and arrives in another.

## Evolution
Phase 1 -- Nancy craft and David's studio, 1785-1794. 1785: leaves Nancy for Paris, earns his living painting snuff-box lids and decorated buttons; Nancy training with Girardet (d. 1778) and Claudot (1733-1805), master of the miniaturist Augustin. 1786: lessons from Francois Dumont, miniaturist to Marie-Antoinette; 1787: presented to the Queen at a masked ball, dressed as a young woman by the comtesse de Calignac, and given the medallion portraits of the ducs d'Angouleme and de Berry for a snuff-box, a birthday gift from the Queen. 1788: enters David's studio on David's return from Italy; assists on Les Amours de Paris et d'Helene, painting details and background architecture. 1789-91: 228 portraits of deputies, six francs each; 1791: first Salon (Basily-Callimaki) or 1793 (WGA, Annex, Moeller) -- see C4; Monday 15 August 1791: marries Justine Laurisse de Salienne. 1792: captain commanding the troupe of arts students guarding the Louvre. 1794: The Departure and The Return, maniere noire pendants, Salon 1794 -- first success. Work: craft miniatures, documentary portraiture by the dozen, tone by smudge.

Phase 2 -- La Barque and the private drawing, 1797-1802. 1795: drawing teacher at Mme Campan's school at Saint-Germain; pupil Hortense de Beauharnais; through her meets Josephine and General Bonaparte. 1797: La Barque, sepia drawing of himself, his wife and three children in a boat (Salon 1797 per fr.wikipedia and napoleon.org; 1798 per WGA; great success at the Salon of 1820 per en.wikipedia and Annex -- see C1). 1798: Boilly paints Reunion d'artistes dans l'atelier d'Isabey, the Louvre atelier as a gathering of thirty-one named figures. 1799: Louvre studio apartment no. 23. April 1801 to September 1802: Napoleon at Malmaison, the full-length drawing that becomes the best likeness of Napoleon ever executed; 1802: Review of Troops by the First Consul in the Tuileries courtyard; designs the five-rayed star of the Legion of Honour; Tuesday 2 November 1802: the First Consul's visit to the Sevene Freres manufactory at Rouen. Work: the private drawing at larger scale, the intimate state portrait, the crowd as a system.

Phase 3 -- Empire peak: ceremony and the diplomatic miniature, 1804-1814. 1804: draws all coronation costumes; stages the coronation at Notre-Dame on Sunday 2 December 1804 with about 100 dressed wooden dolls and a scale plan of the cathedral; Ordonnateur des rejouissances; the Sevene visit exhibited at the Salon of 1804. 1805: "Premier Peintre de ma Chambre" to Josephine, then draughtsman of the Emperor's cabinet; painter-decorator of the theatres of Saint-Cloud and the Tuileries; buys the hotel particulier at 7 rue des Trois-Freres, where his wife runs a salon. 1806: Napoleon visiting the Jouy manufactory, pendant to Rouen. 1808: Portrait of the Empress Josephine; Table des Marechaux designed. 1809: lodging and studio at Sevres. 1810: Table des Marechaux exhibited at the Salon; miniatures of Napoleon and Marie-Louise on their marriage; Alexandrine marries Pierre-Luc-Charles Ciceri, 24 February 1810. 1811: watercolour teacher to Marie-Louise; the King of Rome in the helmet of Mars; Napoleon presenting the newborn King of Rome to Marie-Louise; the tiny head for a breast-pin. 1812: to Vienna at Marie-Louise's commission, sixteen miniatures of her family, exhibited at the Salon in November. Monday 21 March 1814: son Hector killed at Arcis-sur-Aube, aged 17. Work: the ceremony as the medium, the miniature as its portable residue, the gift economy at industrial scale.

Phase 4 -- Vienna and the transfer of regimes, 1814-1825. 1814: portrait of Louis XVIII, engraved by Debucourt; accompanies Talleyrand to Vienna for the Congress; the Congress of Vienna watercolour, the delegates at the moment Wellington arrived, Friday 3 February 1815; decorates Vienna cathedral for the solemn mass of 21 January 1815 in memory of Louis XVI. Tuesday 31 January 1815: knight of the Legion of Honour. Hundred Days: homage to Napoleon on his return from Elba, then Restoration favour retained. Spring 1816: brief exile in England; 1817: returns, portraits of the duchesse d'Angouleme; Escalier du musee, watercolour, 1817; the Sevres months breakfast service for Louis XVIII. 1818: Divers Essais Lithographiques, 14 leaves. 1821: Mon fils, mon eleve et mon ami. 1822: Voyage en Italie, 30 lithographs. 1823: Louis XVIII names him designer and organiser of the court's fetes and spectacles. 1824: arranges the chapelle ardente for Louis XVIII's funeral; eleven grisailles, Scenes de la vie au chateau de Rosny, for the duchesse d'Angouleme; organises the Reims coronation of Charles X with Ciceri; the eight bistre caricature-portraits of the Salon. 1825: officer of the Legion of Honour. Work: the same system, new sovereigns; the portraitist of Europe replaces the portraitist of the Emperor, and nothing in the hand changes.

Phase 5 -- Lithography, Sevres months, late miniatures, 1826-1855. 1828: named dessinateur du Cabinet, 8 August 1828; Mrs Rufus Prime, miniature, 1828, for a transatlantic clientele. Saturday 31 January 1829: first wife dies; he remarries his pupil Eugenie Rose Maystre, and has two more children, Henri and Henriette. 1830-31: deputy keeper of the Musees royaux under the July Monarchy (year disputed, see C14); directs the Sevres artists' studio. 1832: Portrait of Louise d'Orleans, queen of the Belgians. 1834: moves to 25 quai de Conti, at the Institut. 1836, 1838, 1843: fails three times to be elected to the Institut. 1841: exhibits a Saint Jean at the Salon as "Isabey, pere" -- his twenty-first and last Salon. 1843: writes his Abrege de ma biographie (manuscript, BnF, not read); stops painting in his last years, though the 1841 Saint Jean and the 1842 Return of the Ashes attribution sit uneasily with that claim (see C8). Monday 14 April 1845: officer of the Order of Leopold (Belgium). 1852: the Louvre devotes a room to his Empire costume drawings. Wednesday 12 January 1853: Commander of the Legion of Honour (insignia presented 1854 per fr.wikipedia); pension of 6,000 francs from Napoleon III, expressly as the drawing teacher of his mother Hortense. Wednesday 18 April 1855: dies, aged 88. Work: no Romantic turn, ever. Unlike his son Eugene (and Delacroix, his son's friend), Jean-Baptiste stays polished, elegant and courtly to the end; the hand barely changes while the regimes change five times around it.

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is a machine for making likeness portable, repeatable and survivable. He starts from the opposite problem to his son's: not a painter who found one cliff and rotated genres across it, but a painter who found one unit -- the small, finished, mountable portrait -- and let the occasion change around it: deputies, emperors, congresses, coronations, opera sets, porcelain services. The miniature is the thesis: a face three centimetres wide, built from dot density on a glowing ivory ground, carries full imperial presence because the ground does the lighting and the dots do the modelling. The code is the second thesis: three or four fixed attributes (bicorne parallel to the shoulders, hand in the waistcoat, determined face) plus a private backdrop make a ruler recognisable in any medium, at any size, in any regime. The roster is the third: 228 deputies, sixteen Vienna miniatures, a Congress of delegates, a coronation's hundred dolls -- likeness times N, organised by occasion, framed by a border programme. And the ceremony is the fourth: the portrait, the costume and the official book are three outputs of one design, and the man who designs the coronation does not need to paint its history picture, because he has already staged it. What makes him usable for generative work is not the Napoleonic subject -- that is the biography -- but the system underneath it: a density field on a luminous ground, a fixed attribute code, a roster that fills up head by head, a cycle mapped onto a set of identical objects, and a mount that turns a picture into a gift. And the survival is the point: regimes change their pictures, but they all need someone who can stage the room, and Isabey staged the room for every sovereign of France for sixty-eight years, from Marie-Antoinette's snuff-box to Napoleon III's pension, without once changing his hand.

## POSITIONS
Tags: [FROM RECORD] = directly evidenced in a source read; [INFERRED] = read from the work and the record; [ABSENCE] = stated plainly. P1-P17 are his own practice and life; P18 is inferred; P19 is an absence stated plainly; P20 is reception.

P1 [FROM RECORD]: David's pupil, 1788, and his assistant on a history picture. Fr.wikipedia: "En 1788, il devient l'un des eleves de Jacques-Louis David, avec qui le lie une profonde amitie et il l'assiste dans la realisation du tableau Les Amours de Paris et d'Helene, dont il peint les details et l'architecture du fond." En.wikipedia and WGA agree he became David's pupil at 19 after lessons from Francois Dumont. The friendship, not just the pupillage, is the point: David later gave him financial backing in his hard early Paris years (da.wikipedia via search) and Isabey rebuilt his client base "through his friendship with J-L David" after the Revolution (Annex).

P2 [FROM RECORD]: Nancy training, then snuff-boxes and buttons in Paris. WGA: trained in Nancy with Jean Girardet (d. 1778) and then with Jean-Baptiste-Charles Claudot (1733-1805), master of the miniaturist Jean-Baptiste Augustin; in 1785 went to Paris, began by painting snuff-boxes; in 1786 received lessons from Francois Dumont, who had also studied with Girardet in Nancy, before entering David's studio. Annex agrees: studied architecture and decoration with Girardet and landscape with Claudot in Nancy; at 19 (1786) went to Paris with a letter of recommendation to Dumont, the Lorraine artist and "Premier peintre" to the Queen; made his living painting snuff-box covers and ornamentation for buttons. Fr.wikipedia adds he was presented to the queen dressed and made up as a young woman by the comtesse de Calignac at a masked ball in 1787.

P3 [FROM RECORD]: The 228 deputies, six francs each. Fr.wikipedia: he made "228 portraits des deputes de l'Assemblee nationale payes chacun 6 francs". WGA: "He executed 228 portraits of deputies for a work on the Assemblee Legislative and from 1793 exhibited miniatures and drawings in the Salon." Annex: "he created 228 portraits of deputies for a work on the Legislative Assembly during the French Revolution." The deputies portraits are the hinge of his career: a monarchist-trained miniaturist who documented the Revolution portrait by portrait, for pay, and survived it.

P4 [FROM RECORD]: Mirabeau told him to quit history painting. Fr.wikipedia: "Ce serait a la suite de son portrait de Mirabeau que ce dernier lui conseille de renoncer a la peinture d'histoire pour se consacrer aux portraits, a la miniature et a l'aquarelle." The advice, if the tradition is right, fixed his whole career: he never became the history painter David trained him to be, and the miniature -- the small, portable, repeatable portrait -- became his empire.

P5 [FROM RECORD]: Twenty-one Salons, 1791 to 1841. Fr.wikipedia (citing Basily-Callimaki 1909, p. 440): "Isabey exposa a vingt-et-un salons; d'abord en 1791 pour la premiere fois... ensuite ses succes continuent aux salons de 1793, 1795, 1796, 1799, 1804, 1806, 1810, 1812, 1817, 1824, 1827, 1831, 1834, 1835, 1836, 1837, 1838, 1839, 1840, 1841." He appears in the 1841 catalogue as "Isabey, pere", showing a Saint Jean in the painting section. Fifty years of Salon exhibiting, from the Revolution to the July Monarchy.

P6 [FROM RECORD]: The Boat, with a muddled Salon date. WGA: success came in 1794 with two drawings in "maniere noire", The Departure and The Return; "this type of drawing... reached its peak with Isabey's The Boat (exhibited Salon 1798; Paris, Louvre), an informal scene including a self-portrait". Fr.wikipedia: his wife, "entouree de leurs trois premiers enfants", figures in his sepia drawing presented at the Salon of 1797 and titled La Barque. En.wikipedia: "Isabey's Boat... was a great success at the Salon of 1820." Annex: "He had an immense success at the Salon of 1820 with Isabey's Boat, a drawing of himself and his family." The 1797/1798 and 1820 dates cannot both be the first exhibition of the same drawing; either there are two states/versions or one source misdates. Do not state a single Salon year for The Boat without noting the conflict. What is agreed: it is a self-portrait with his family in a boat, engraved by Charles Paul Landon, and his best-known drawing.

P7 [FROM RECORD]: The Malmaison intimate, and the best Napoleon ever painted. En.wikipedia: "His portrait, Napoleon at Malmaison, is considered his best." WGA: "His portrait of Napoleon at Malmaison is held to be the best ever executed, and even his tiny head of the king of Rome, painted for a breast-pin, is distinguished by a decision and breadth which evidence the hand of a master." Fr.wikipedia: he became "un intime du chateau de la Malmaison, ou il dessine le portrait de Bonaparte" (1796, via Madame Campan's Saint-Germain school, where he taught drawing and met the Bonaparte family). The Malmaison portrait (1801, drawing; 1802 in some listings) is the likeness against which other Napoleons are judged, and it was made as a private drawing, not a state commission.

P8 [FROM RECORD]: The coronation was his staging, costumes and all. En.wikipedia: "Patronized by Josephine and Napoleon Bonaparte, he arranged the ceremonies of their coronation and prepared drawings for the publication intended as its official commemoration. He was paid for this work by Louis XVIII after the Restoration." Fr.wikipedia: in 1804 named "Ordonnateur des rejouissances publiques et des fetes particulieres aux Tuileries, Dessinateur du Sceau des titres"; in 1805 "Premier Peintre de ma Chambre" by Josephine, then draughtsman of Napoleon's cabinet; "il participe a la mise en scene de leurs sacres a Notre-Dame, il cree les costumes et robes de tous les participants." Annex: 32 drawings for the official commemoration. Christie's (search text): the coronation mantel design was "almost certainly... the working preparatory design for Napoleon I's coronation mantel" worn 2 December 1804. Osenat (search text): the Livre du Sacre "involved no less than seventeen engravers and four draftsmen" under Isabey's direction; the book was commissioned in March 1805 by Segur, cost 194,436 francs, and was not finished until 1815, after the fall.

P9 [FROM RECORD]: The miniature as a diplomatic gift, by the dozen. Fr.wikipedia: "Il livre des dizaines de portraits en miniature de l'Empereur, que celui fait monter en boite pour ses presents." The New International Encyclopaedia: "He was in high favor with Napoleon, for whom he painted hundreds of miniatures for distribution as gifts." American Cyclopaedia: Napoleon "appointed him his miniature painter in ordinary, and the members of the Bonaparte family and the marshals and great dignitaries of the empire sat to him, besides many sovereigns and statesmen of Europe, of whom he painted a greater number than any contemporary." The miniature was not a keepsake; it was Napoleon's calling card, and Isabey was its factory.

P10 [FROM RECORD]: Opera decorator, with Ciceri and Daguerre in his team. Fr.wikipedia: in 1805 painter-decorator of the theatres of Saint-Cloud and the Tuileries; chief designer at the Paris Opera from 1810; "le dernier peintre academique a diriger l'atelier des decors de l'opera de Paris jusqu'en 1815 ou 1828"; "Il a dans son equipe son gendre Pierre-Luc-Charles Ciceri et Louis Daguerre." Sets credited to him: Les amours d'Antoine et Cleopatre (1808), Armide (1811), L'enfant prodige (1812, Pierre Gardel's ballet-pantomime; Aguttes lot, search text: Gardel entrusted him with the sets, and he collaborated with his son-in-law Ciceri, then painter-decorator at the Opera), Les Abencerages (1812-1813), Oreste (1815). He also drew dresses, costumes, shawls and cashmeres for the Lyon and Jouy manufactures.

P11 [FROM RECORD]: Sevres, from a studio in 1809 to the Table des Marechaux and the months service. Fr.wikipedia: a lodging at the Sevres manufacture in 1809, where he drew models for cups and plates with the Emperor's effigy; in 1810 he drew the miniatures of the Table des Marechaux, a gueridon made at Sevres, now at Malmaison, showing Napoleon on a brazen throne surrounded by his marshals. Morgan Library (search text): in 1817, at the behest of Alexandre Brongniart (1770-1847), administrator of the Sevres porcelain factory, Isabey completed the decoration of six cups and plates of a Sevres breakfast service commissioned by Louis XVIII; the 12 Morgan drawings are designs for pieces painted with 12 different scenes in sepia, each a month and a two-hour period of the day, in the life of a country man; the serving pieces were painted by Jean Charles Develly (1783-c.1849) after Isabey's watercolour designs. Annex: under Louis-Philippe he "directed the artists' studio at the Sevres porcelain factory."

P12 [FROM RECORD]: Vienna, 1814-1815, on Talleyrand's commission. En.wikipedia and WGA: he followed Marie-Louise to Vienna on Napoleon's abdication in 1814; his picture of a conference at Vienna "is valuable from the number of historic portraits it embraces." Royal Collection Trust (search text): the watercolour shows the delegates on 3 February 1815, when the Duke of Wellington (far left) arrived to take over the British delegation from Castlereagh; "commissioned by the French delegate at the Congress, Charles Maurice de Talleyrand, but remained in the possession of the artist until it was purchased by George IV in 1820"; a print after it was published by John Godefroy in 1819. Niceartgallery (search text): he was "tasked with capturing the likenesses of the key figures attending the Congress, such as Talleyrand and Metternich" and "reportedly used dolls or mannequins dressed in miniature versions of their attire to plan his compositions" -- the dolls detail is reported, not verified against another source. Fr.wikipedia: at Vienna he organised a grand fete for the Empress Beatrix, painted the portrait of the King of Rome, and handed it to Napoleon in Paris on his return.

P13 [FROM RECORD]: The survivor of six regimes. En.wikipedia: "Although Isabey did homage to Napoleon on his return from Elba, he continued to enjoy the favour of the Restoration, and took part in arrangements for the coronation of Charles X." Fr.wikipedia: after his wife's death in 1829 he left his hotel particulier; in 1823 Louis XVIII named him designer and organiser of the court's fetes and spectacles; in 1824 he arranged the chapelle ardente for Louis XVIII's funeral; Charles X named him designer of the king's cabinet and he organised the Reims coronation, assisted by his son-in-law Ciceri; officer of the Legion of Honour; the July Monarchy gave him the title of deputy curator of the royal museums under Francois Marius Granet (1837, Annex: "Deputy Curator of the Royal Museums"); Napoleon III gave him a pension of 6,000 francs "pour celui qui fut le professeur de dessin de sa mere Hortense de Beauharnais" and the Commander's cross of the Legion of Honour in 1854 (fr.wikipedia; en.wikipedia says Napoleon III granted the pension and the Commander's cross, undated). He signed "Isabey, patriote de 1789" under the Second Republic. First commission from Marie-Antoinette, last honours from Napoleon III: 68 years of successive rulers, no exile except a brief spring 1816 stay in England.

P14 [FROM RECORD]: The teacher, in the studio and in the palace. Fr.wikipedia infobox: his pupils (eleves) are listed as Hortense de Beauharnais, Eugene Isabey and Marie-Louise of Austria; his full eleves list (fr.wikipedia, read end to end) names 21, including Louis-Francois Aubry, Auguste Garneray, Jean-Francois Hollier, Edouard Lienard, Eugenie Rose Maystre, Wilhelm Unger, Jean-Claude Rumeau, Charles Tournemine and Arnold Wallick, and notes "Certains de ses eleves deviennent des pionniers de la photographie apres 1840." Nelson-Atkins (search text): "The copyists he employed included his students Jean-Desire Muneret, Jean-Francois Hollier, Jean-Urbain Guerin, Daniel Saint, and Louis-Francois Aubry." Annex: after his first wife's death in 1829 he remarried "one of his pupils, Eugenie Maystre". He was drawing master to Josephine's children Hortense and Eugene de Beauharnais (Annex), and in 1811 became watercolour teacher to the Empress Marie-Louise (fr.wikipedia); he succeeded Prud'hon as Marie-Louise's drawing teacher (AbeBooks listing, search text).

P15 [FROM RECORD]: The Louvre atelier as a gathering place, painted by Boilly. Fr.wikipedia: in 1799 he obtained a studio in the galleries of the Louvre palace, No. 23, "entoure de nombreux eleves." Boilly's Artists in Isabey's Studio (1798, Louvre, read end to end): from left to right the composer Etienne Nicolas Mehul, the critic Hoffman, the sculptor Charles-Louis Corbet, the painters Michel Martin Drolling, Jean-Louis Demarne, Isabey himself leaning towards the easel, Francois Gerard seated before the easel, Nicolas-Antoine Taunay, Swebach-Desfontaines, the miniaturist Charles Bourgeois, Guillon Lethiere, Carle Vernet, the engraver Jean Duplessis-Bertaux, the architects Pierre-Francois-Leonard Fontaine and Charles Percier, the actor Baptiste aine, Jean-Thomas Thibault, Jan-Frans van Dael, Pierre-Joseph Redoute, the actor Francois-Joseph Talma, Charles Meynier, Boilly himself, the actor Chenard, Xavier Bidault, Girodet-Trioson, the sculptors Denis Chaudet and Francois-Frederic Lemot, and the painter Gioacchino Serangeli. The picture is a group portrait of his working world at 31.

P16 [FROM RECORD]: An early lithographer. Annex: "He went on to be one of the first painters to work in the relatively new medium of stone lithography, specializing in portraits." Fr.wikipedia: he practised lithography and heliogravure assiduously, reproducing his models and drawings; publications: Divers Essais Lithographiques (Paris, 1818) and Voyage en Italie (1822, 30 lithographs). Pennell, Lithography and Lithographers (search text): "J. B. Isabey, Robert, Baron Atthalin worked for him [Engelmann], contributing largely, he thought, to the progress of the art." His 1821 family drawing of Eugene was lithographed by Godefroy Engelmann as "Mon fils, mon eleve et mon ami" (see Q2). En.wikipedia: The Boat was produced "at a time when Isabey was occupied with lithography."

P17 [FROM RECORD]: The private caricaturist. Annex: "Less known than the portraits are Isabey's caricatures of many famous European politicians of the first half of the 19th century. Though Isabey was primarily a portrait painter he was in contact with all the great continental political personalities of the first half of the 19th century, and he caricatured many of them privately. Most of the caricatures were not published, but they had an effect among artists of the time, as did the satirical prints of Francisco de Goya." Fr.wikipedia lists "caricaturiste" among his activities and notes preparatory drawings including a "Portrait charge de Dupin, aine". The public miniaturist and the private caricaturist are the same hand: likeness as flattery in public, likeness as weapon in private.

P18 [INFERRED]: The court artist as infrastructure, not author. The record shows a man whose largest works are not pictures but occasions: a coronation's costumes and staging, a Livre du Sacre, an opera's decor, a Sevres service, a Congress's group portrait, a royal collection's curation. The inference: his medium was the ceremony itself, and the miniature was the portable residue of it -- the thing a sitter could carry away from the occasion. That is why he survived six regimes: regimes change their pictures, but they all need someone who can stage the room.

P19 [ABSENCE, stated plainly]: No published statement of his own about his art was read in this leg, and his own book was never published. Fr.wikipedia, note 23: "Jean Baptiste Isabey, Abrege de ma biographie, manuscrit, Paris, BnF" -- he wrote an abbreviated autobiography in 1843 (fr.wikipedia: "S'il peint jusqu'a ses 70 ans, il ecrit sa biographie abregee en 1843 puis renonce a peindre dans les dernieres annees") and it sits in manuscript at the Bibliotheque nationale, not read here. The biographies are all posthumous and none was read end to end: Edmond Taigny, J.-B. Isabey: sa vie et ses oeuvres (1859); Eva de Basily-Callimaki, J.-B. Isabey: sa vie, son temps, 1767-1855 (1909); Cyril Lecosse, Jean-Baptiste Isabey: petits portraits et grands desseins (2018); Marion W. Osmond, J.-B. Isabey: The Fortunate Painter (1947, cited in the Callimaki paper). Two autograph letters survive in the trade (see Q3-Q4) but neither was read in full. Everything about his aims must be inferred from the commissions, the pupils and the critics.

P20 [RECEPTION]: "Miniaturiste des Rois" against "peintre mineur" and "petit maitre". Fr.wikipedia, Reception critique (read end to end): "Miniaturiste celebre de son vivant, ordonnateur des fetes imperiales et royales, Jean-Baptiste Isabey est souvent considere comme un peintre mineur par rapport a la figure centrale de David. La miniature n'etant pas reconnue par l'Academie des beaux-arts, sa figure est rapidement eclipsee de son vivant par la figure majeure du romantisme et pre-impressionniste son fils Eugene Isabey et l'invention de la photographie." In 1900 he was still "le miniaturiste des Rois" (Louis Gillet, Revue des Deux Mondes, 1910); for Basily-Callimaki (1909) he appears as a "petit maitre". The 2005-2006 exhibition "Jean-Baptiste Isabey: portraitiste de l'Europe" (Malmaison, October 2005-January 2006; Nancy, 28 January-18 April 2006) is the modern revival, and it frames him as the portraitist of a continent, not of one court.

## Load-bearing corrections
C0. The Napoleon anecdote -- "Isabey is the only painter who has made me look like a man" (or similar) -- was NOT found in any source searched. Leg 1 ran a targeted search (245 lines) at the quote and returned no instance of the sentence, in English or French, and no Wikiquote-grade citation. Do not use the line as fact; treat it as apocryphal unless a French source is found. What IS sourced and adjacent: the Malmaison portrait is repeatedly called the best likeness of Napoleon ever executed (Britannica, WGA, Moeller, NiceArtGallery), and Napoleon's own doctrine of likeness, said to David and recorded by napoleon.org: "It is not the exactitude of the features, every small spot on the nose, that make the resemblance. It is the character of the physiognomy, what animates it, that must be painted" -- a real, sourced Napoleon remark about portraiture, addressed to David, not a compliment to Isabey.
C1. La Barque: Salon 1797 (fr.wikipedia, napoleon.org) vs "exhibited Salon 1798" (WGA) vs "great success at the Salon of 1820" (en.wikipedia, citing Britannica 1911; Annex). Most likely drawn and exhibited 1797, shown again 1798, and a version or the Landon engraving succeeding in 1820 -- but no source read says that explicitly. Do not collapse into one date.
C2. Death date D2 HOLDS: the briefed "died 18 April 1855, Paris" is unanimous across en.wikipedia, fr.wikipedia, napoleon.org, Moeller, Annex Galleries, American Cyclopaedia, NiceArtGallery and Wikidata; no source anywhere in this study gives another death date or place. Age 88, not 78 -- NiceArtGallery's "at the advanced age of 78" is arithmetically wrong (1767 to 1855 is 88). (See the Doorway section.)
C3. D1 HOLDS: the 1821 inscription exists, is dated 1821, and is of Eugene; Alamy and an Invaluable listing corroborate independently, and the National Gallery of Canada holds an impression. The Engelmann lithographer attribution rests on #503 and fr.wikipedia; the Alamy record confirms title, date and medium only. (See the Doorway section.)
C4. Salon debut: 1791 (Basily-Callimaki 1909, via fr.wikipedia -- not in the livret, but critics already call him "premier peintre en miniature") vs 1793 (WGA, Annex, Moeller, the standard English date). Last Salon: 1841 (Basily-Callimaki list, 21 Salons) vs 1844 (Moeller timeline only, no corroboration found; treat as likely error).
C5. No Salon medal for Jean-Baptiste Isabey was found in any source searched. The medals that surface in searches belong to others: his son Eugene (first-class medals 1824, 1827, 1855; Legion of Honour 1832, officer 1852) and his pupil circle (Antoine-Baptiste Thomas, medal Salon 1822). Do NOT attribute a medal to J.-B. Isabey. His honours were the Legion of Honour grades and court appointments, not Salon medals -- consistent with miniature not being recognised by the Academie des Beaux-Arts (fr.wikipedia, Reception critique).
C6. "Peintre des rois" -- the substance is fully supported (he worked for every sovereign of France from Marie-Antoinette to Napoleon III), but the exact phrase was NOT found verbatim in the sources searched. Do not quote it as a sourced title.

C9. Mother's name: Marie Poirel (fr.wikipedia) vs Marie-Francoise Morel (napoleon.org). First wife's name in three variants: Justine Laurisse (fr.wikipedia infobox), Jeanne Laurice (Annex), Laurence (napoleon.org text); marriage Monday 15 August 1791 (fr.wikipedia) vs 13 August 1791 (napoleon.org).
