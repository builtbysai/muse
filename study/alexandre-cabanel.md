# Alexandre Cabanel: Study Notes

**Site:** Alexandre Cabanel (1823-1889), French academic painter -- born Sunday 28 September 1823 in Montpellier, the son of a modest carpenter; died Wednesday 23 January 1889 in Paris, aged 65, in his hotel at 14 rue Alfred de Vigny, 8th arrondissement. He showed talent early and was apprenticed at the Montpellier School of Fine Arts in the class of Charles Matet, curator of the Musee Fabre; his earliest surviving works are two self-portraits, 1836 (age thirteen) and 1840. In 1839 he moved to Paris on a scholarship from his hometown, and in 1840, at seventeen, he entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts as a pupil of Francois-Edouard Picot (1786-1868), himself a pupil of Francois-Andre Vincent and a protege of Jacques-Louis David, so Cabanel stands in the direct David line. He failed the Prix de Rome twice, with Cincinnatus Receiving the Ambassadors of Rome (1843) and Christ in the Garden of Olives (1844), and then won the Second Grand Prix in 1845 with Jesus in the Praetorium; he took the Rome residency only because a prize went unawarded in musical composition, freeing a place (see C0). He was a pensionnaire at the Villa Medici from 1846 to 1850, sending annual envois back to Paris: Orestes (1846), The Fallen Angel (1847), Saint John the Baptist (1849), Saint Paul Among the Faithful of Caesarea (1849) and The Death of Moses (1850/1851). In Rome he met the Montpellier collector Alfred Bruyas, who became his patron and commissioned the 1848 trilogy La Chiaruccia, A Thinker, Young Roman Monk and Albayde. His Salon debut was 1844 with Christ in the Garden of Olives; The Death of Moses, shown at the Salon of 1852, won a second-class medal and launched his Paris career. The 1850s brought large religious machines and state decoration: The Glorification of Saint Louis (1853-1855) for the Gothic chapel at the Chateau de Vincennes, the twelve pendentives The Months for the Hotel de Ville (destroyed in the fire of 1871), Christian Martyr (1855, gold medal at the Universal Exposition), and wall paintings for the Hotel Pereire (1859). He was made a knight of the Legion of Honour in 1855 and married Marie-Clementine Legrand the same year; three children, of whom she and two died in 1867; he remarried in 1869 and his second wife died four years later. In 1863 The Birth of Venus, exhibited at the Salon of 1863, was bought immediately by Napoleon III for his personal collection, and Cabanel signed a contract with the Goupil house for engraved reproductions; the same year he was elected to the Academie des Beaux-Arts (10th chair) and sat on the Salon jury whose refusals produced the Salon des Refuses. In January 1864 he was appointed professor and head of the painting atelier at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts, a post he held until his death, twenty-five years; between 1868 and 1888 he sat on the Salon jury seventeen times, and he took the Medal of Honour at the Salons of 1865, 1867 and 1878. His pupils numbered in the hundreds, and 112 exhibitors at the Salon of 1886 signed themselves "Pupil of Cabanel"; through them, in the Dictionary of Art's phrase, he did more than any other artist of his generation to form the character of Belle Epoque French painting. The 1860s and 1870s are the peak: Nymph Abducted by a Faun (1860, bought on the spot by Napoleon III for 15,000 francs), Paradise Lost (1867, commissioned by Ludwig II of Bavaria for the Maximilianeum in Munich, destroyed in the bombing of Munich in 1945), the Pantheon Life of Saint Louis cycle (1878), Thamar (1875), and the late doomed-woman theatre -- The Death of Francesca da Rimini (1870), Phaedra (1880), Ophelia (1883) and Cleopatra Testing Poisons on Condemned Prisoners (1887), his last large composition, commissioned for the Royal Academy in Antwerp. In 1881 he publicly defended Manet's Pertuiset, le chasseur de lions: "Gentlemen, there is not one among us who is capable of doing a head like that in the open air!" He was promoted Commander of the Legion of Honour in 1884. His funeral was held in Paris on Saturday 26 January 1889 at Saint-Philippe du Roule, and he was buried in the Saint-Lazare cemetery in Montpellier on Monday 28 January 1889; a monument was erected in Montpellier in 1892, with a marble bust by Paul Dubois and the sculpture Regret by Antonin Mercie. Octave Mirbeau's funeral oration in L'Echo de Paris, 8 February 1889, is the standing avant-garde verdict: "He had a horror of nature; or rather, he ignored it. He never suspected there was air, light, life. If I had an epitaph to inscribe on his tomb, I would simply put: 'Here lies a professor: he professed.'" For much of the twentieth century he stood, with Bouguereau and Gerome, as the conservative foil to Impressionism, his work dismissed as kitsch; the reassessment came late, with the first retrospective since his death, "Alexandre Cabanel: La Tradition du Beau", Musee Fabre, Montpellier, 10 July 2010 to 2 January 2011, then Wallraf-Richartz Museum, Cologne. His works sit principally in the Musee Fabre (about 39 entries in the works table, the largest holding), the Musee d'Orsay (six, including The Birth of Venus, the Paradise Lost reduction, Francesca, Thamar and the Comtesse de Keller), the Metropolitan Museum of Art (four, including Echo and the 1875 Venus replica), the Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp (Cleopatra and the 1885 self-portrait), the Hermitage, the Walters, the Dahesh, the Louvre, the Pantheon, and American collections from the Speed to Kedleston Hall. ## M1. The Opalescent Flesh Machine
Invisible, silky brushwork; sfumato modelling of lights and darks; a pale pink-white flesh built from opalescent, velvety colour, so the body looks poured, not painted. The Birth of Venus (1863, oil on canvas, 130 by 225 cm, Musee d'Orsay) is the type: the sources agree on "pale, opalescent finish", "smooth, pearlescent rendering", "silky brushwork", "graceful modeling, and perfected forms", "velvety opalescent colors". Zola's verdict, quoted in the Orsay record and widely repeated, names the effect exactly: "The goddess, drowned in a sea of milk, resembles a delicious courtesan, but not of flesh and blood -- that would be indecent -- but made of a sort of pink and white marzipan." (The network leg records the same passage as "drowned in a river of milk"; the two wordings are translation variants of the same French, see C15.) The machine was rehearsed three years earlier in Nymph Abducted by a Faun (1860), whose nymph has "the same complexion and elegance, in her pale skin, physical shapes and hair color, of the Venus" of 1863. It recurs in the portraits: the Comtesse de Keller (1873) is rendered with "delicate application of oil glazes" giving a luminous quality and "soft brushstroke technique, which gives the Countess's skin an almost ethereal finish" -- secondary-source description, not a primary technical record, but consistent with the Venus. The hand disappears so the body looks poured. The watchable behaviour is a surface that has no visible work in it: the closer you look, the smoother it gets. **Takeaway:** hide the hand completely; the flesh is the finish, and the finish is the subject.

## M2. The Reclining Nude as Salon Machine
A life-size body laid full-length across a wide horizontal canvas, filling the foreground edge to edge, so the viewer cannot avoid it. The Birth of Venus is the type: Venus reclines full-length across the foreground on the waves, attended by five putti, her body filling the 130-by-225-cm field; she does NOT emerge from a clamshell and there is no contrapposto. The Fallen Angel (1847, about 121 by 190 cm, Musee Fabre) is the same machine with the sexes reversed: Lucifer moments after expulsion, a life-size nude male body stretched almost edge to edge on a long horizontal, "like an antique river god or a reclining sarcophagus figure". Nymph Abducted by a Faun (1860, 243 by 142 cm) and Echo (1874) and Ophelia (1883, drifting on the water) repeat the device. The horizontal format IS the device -- the body becomes a landscape, a horizon line the eye travels along instead of a figure it looks at. The Salon of 1863 was nicknamed the "Salon of Venus" for its many classical nudes, and Cabanel's was judged the best of three rival Venuses (Baudry's The Pearl and the Wave, 1862, and Amaury-Duval's Birth of Venus, 1862). The watchable behaviour is a body that will not let the eye leave the foreground: the picture is one long held note. **Takeaway:** lay the body flat and wide; the format does the seducing, the figure only has to lie there.

## M3. The Mythological Alibi
A classical subject that licenses an erotic image the public would otherwise reject: Venus, a nymph, Echo, a goddess. "The theme of the Birth of Venus was popular in the 19th century because it allowed artists to explore eroticism without offending public sensibilities, all under the guise of classical subject matter." Nymph Abducted by a Faun "passed it off as classical homage". Cabanel said so himself, in the one letter that survives (to his niece, 30 May 1863): "usually the subject is merely a pretext to hint at or to express an underlying ideal, an ideal for which the public is more receptive on account of its familiarity with the subject" -- and "occasionally it is a mistake to view a painting only in moral terms". The control case is Manet's Olympia (1863, shown 1865): the same reclining pose, but a confronting stare, a modern courtesan, flat lighting, dark outlines -- no myth, and scandal. Zola's attack on the Venus is exactly the charge that the alibi is a lie: the goddess is a courtesan in myth dress. The watchable behaviour is a picture that is two pictures at once: a goddess for the Salon catalogue and a courtesan for the eye, and the viewer knows both readings at the same time. **Takeaway:** give the erotic image a classical name and the public will hang it; the alibi is the mechanism, not the decoration.

## M4. The Languorous Gaze
Eyes only slightly open, looking out from beneath the crook of the elbow; a coy, sleepy, half-averted look that invites the viewer in without confronting him. The Birth of Venus is the type: her eyes are only slightly open, "a languorous gaze, looking out from beneath the crook of her elbow". Robert Rosenblum, quoted in the network leg, names the effect: "This Venus hovers somewhere between an ancient deity and a modern dream"; her eyes "seem to be closed but... a close look reveals that she is awake... A nude who could be asleep or awake is specially formidable for a male viewer." The 19thc review, on the 1863 Venus room at the retrospective, notes "variations in coy expressions" placing Cabanel's Venus in context against Baudry and Amaury-Duval -- the coy expression is a competitive variable, tuned picture against picture. The direct contrast is Olympia's confrontational stare: same pose, opposite gaze, opposite verdict. The watchable behaviour is a look that will not settle: asleep or awake, inviting or indifferent, the picture changes while you watch it because the eyes will not commit. **Takeaway:** half-open the eyes; a gaze that refuses to decide is more watchable than one that stares you down.

## M5. The Doomed-Woman Theatre
Single tragic heroines staged as public spectacle in opulent interiors: wealth and despair in one frame. Phaedra (1880, Musee Fabre) is the type: Phaedra after confessing her desire for her stepson Hippolytus, "most of the elements within this scene are there to provide an impression of wealth and despair", and "private torment becomes theatrical public spectacle". Ophelia (1883, 77 by 117.5 cm, private collection) is the same mechanism at the instant the tree branch breaks, "unable to bear her weight any longer", her gold-trimmed silk dress and garland rendered meticulously, drifting "suspended in a moment between life and death". Cleopatra Testing Poisons on Condemned Prisoners (1887, 162.6 by 287.6 cm, Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp) is the mechanism at its largest: Cleopatra slouched languidly on a divan, watching indifferently, on fabrics and a tiger skin whose head is preserved. The Daughter of Jephthah (c. 1879, private collection) gives the heroine her two months' grace in the mountains, "to weep for her virginity" before her sacrifice. Thamar (1875, 180 by 248 cm, Musee d'Orsay) gives the moment after the rape, the textiles and interiors as the stage. The mechanism: take a literary woman at her worst moment, dress the room richly, freeze her. The watchable behaviour is a room that is too beautiful for what is happening in it: the eye enjoys the wealth and then remembers the doom. **Takeaway:** stage the worst moment in the richest room; the opulence is what makes the tragedy watchable.

## M6. The Split-Stage Composition
The canvas divided into two zones: a calm, beautiful watcher in the foreground and the suffering in the background. Cleopatra Testing Poisons is the type: Cleopatra slouched languidly on a divan in the right foreground, watching indifferently; the poisoned prisoners dying in the left background. The Death of Francesca da Rimini and Paolo Malatesta (1870, 184 by 255 cm, Musee d'Orsay) is the same split: the dead lovers at the centre, Giovanni "lurking in the shadows at the right". Paradise Lost (1867) is the same split vertically: God and his angels descending to punish above, Adam and Eve prostrate below, Eve shielding her face with her arm, Adam slouched beside her "glowering outward". The split lets one picture hold both beauty and horror without mixing them: the watcher does not touch the suffering, and the suffering does not spoil the watcher. The watchable behaviour is an eye that travels between two registers and cannot reconcile them: the calm face and the dying body are in the same frame and in different worlds. **Takeaway:** split the stage; let the beautiful watcher and the dying stay apart, and the gap between them is the picture.

## M7. Body Language as Melodrama
Emotion carried by the pose, not the face: folded arms and a hidden face (The Fallen Angel); Eve prostrate, shielding her face, body contorted "in the shameful agony of her expulsion"; Adam hunched, "glowering outward, his hunched shoulders and darkened visage indicating his own dejection and contrition"; Tamar cast out; Ophelia's extended arm. The face is often half hidden; the body does the talking. The gallery19c note traces this to "nineteenth-century theater and opera": "Such melodramatic gestures were typical of Cabanel, whose explorations into the expressive potential of body language through nineteenth-century theater and opera may be traced directly from this picture to his most famous work, The Birth of Venus of 1863" -- the chronology in that sentence runs backwards, Venus predates Paradise Lost, but the point is the shared gestural language, not the direction of influence. The Fallen Angel shows the mechanism in revision: the 1846 study showed Lucifer looking downwards; the 1847 final changed the head so he peers from behind his arm, turning a bowed figure into a confronting one -- the same body, a different gesture, a different picture. The watchable behaviour is a figure whose feeling you read in the shoulders and the arms before you ever reach the face, and sometimes the face is never reached at all. **Takeaway:** hide the face and let the body speak; the shoulders carry what the features cannot.

## M8. The Diagonal-and-Triangle Multi-Figure Machine
Large history painting organised by geometry: a diagonal between the two principal figures, a stabilizing triangle of secondary figures, a tertiary group dissolved into drapery. The Death of Moses (1850/1851) is the type: a twelve-figure composition, many life size, in which "God and Moses define a diagonal; secondary figures flanking Moses create a stabilizing triangle; a tertiary group fades into God's billowing drapery". The discipline comes from the Rome envois: Saint Paul Among the Faithful of Caesarea (1849), an esquisse with at least twelve figures, was judged a failure "for confusion of his composition" -- the machine breaking down is itself evidence of the machine. Orestes (1846) was criticised by the Academy as "an oversized and inept composition". The models are named: Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling and his Moses for the tomb of Julius II, and Raphael's Vatican frescoes; Theophile Gautier wrote in 1852 that The Death of Moses was "the Salon painting that most directly follows on from elevated, serious, profound art, whose prototypes are Michelangelo and Raphael". The watchable behaviour is a crowd that resolves into geometry as you look: first the diagonal, then the triangle, then the drapery that swallows the rest. **Takeaway:** build the crowd on a diagonal and a triangle; the geometry is what keeps twelve figures from becoming a mob.

## M9. The Portrait as Status Machine
Pyramidal composition, dark undefined background, soft light from the left, and an inventory of textures -- satin, lace, fur, jewelry, embroidery -- rendered as proof of wealth and rank. The Portrait of the Comtesse de Keller (1873, 99 by 73 cm, Musee d'Orsay) is the type: a seated sitter in a white dress with a shimmering green overdress, "pyramidal composition drawing the eye up to her serene face", dark undefined background isolating the figure, the textures rendered -- "the smooth satin, the delicate lace, the luxurious fur" -- and the jewelry. Pandora (1873, 70.2 by 49.2 cm, Walters Art Museum) is the same machine in mythological fancy dress: the Swedish soprano Christine Nilsson shown as Pandora, "clearly not intended as an account of the myth" -- a portrait in costume. Mrs Louise Van Loon-Borski (1887) is the machine at its most literal: the sitter's actual dress survived and was hung beside the portrait in the 2010-11 retrospective. The counter-case is the Portrait of Napoleon III (Salon 1865): the emperor in civilian dress despite the ermine-lined cloak and imperial accessories, "the frankness and lack of idealization in Napoleon III's expression that makes the portrait strikingly modern for its time" -- the same machine with the grandeur deliberately removed, which critics read as a hotel manager; the court liked it, it hung in the Tuileries, and the Empress Eugenie took it into exile in Britain after 1870. The watchable behaviour is a portrait that is an inventory first and a face second: you read the wealth, then you meet the sitter. **Takeaway:** paint the textures as the proof and the face as the seal; the dress is the argument, the sitter is the signature.

## M10. The Salon Machine / Institutional Power
The painting is only half the system: jury service seventeen times (1868-1888), professorship 1864-1889, hundreds of pupils at the Salons, the emperor buying the picture on the wall, Goupil engraving it for the mass market. The Birth of Venus was bought by Napoleon III at the Salon of 1863 and engraved by Goupil under contract; the 1875 replica was painted for the banker John Wolf and offered to the Metropolitan Museum in 1893. "He was elected regularly to the Salon jury and his pupils could be counted by the hundred at the Salons. Through them, Cabanel did more than any other artist of his generation to form the character of the belle epoque French painting" (Dictionary of Art, 1996, quoted in Wikipedia). The machine's founding act is a refusal: his refusal with Bouguereau to admit Manet and others to the Salon of 1863 led to the Salon des Refuses -- the gatekeeper in the founding myth of modern art. And the machine's late act is a defence: in 1881 he defended Manet's Pertuiset, "Gentlemen, there is not one among us who is capable of doing a head like that in the open air!" The watchable behaviour is a picture engineered to be bought, engraved, and hung by power -- and a system that outlives the pictures it was built to protect. **Takeaway:** the jury is part of the painting; a picture that cannot get hung, bought and engraved is only half a picture in this system.

## Evolution
SCALE: The early work is small and conventional -- student oils mostly under 50 cm, Joseph's tunic, Coriolanus, Priam -- with one early exception, Cincinnatus (1843, 114 by 146.5 cm), already frieze-like. Rome scales up: Orestes 221 by 236 cm, Saint John the Baptist 195 by 141.5 cm, The Death of Moses 279 by 391 cm. The 1850s stay large for history and religion (Christian Martyr 286 by 200 cm; Glorification of Saint Louis 442 by 432 cm) while portraits of Montpellier families stay small and intimate. The 1860s are the peak of the large Salon machine (Venus 130 by 225 cm; Paradise Lost for Munich, the largest foreign commission). The 1870s and 1880s split the scale: the late history pictures stay large (Francesca 184 by 255 cm; Phaedra 194 by 286 cm; Cleopatra 165 by 290 cm) while the society portraits shrink to the saleable single figure (Pandora 70.2 by 49.2 cm; Miss Fanny Clapp 73 by 55.9 cm). His last picture, Tragedy (1888), is small, 41 by 29 cm -- an emblem of the career's end rather than a new direction.

SUBJECT: 1836-1845: student history and religion, Joseph, Coriolanus, Priam, Cincinnatus, Christ in the Garden of Olives, Jesus in the Praetorium. 1846-1850: Rome envois, Orestes, the Devil, Saint John, Saint Paul, Moses -- ambition outrunning approval, and a turn toward the romantic: Albayde (1848), after Hugo's Les Orientales, and the Bruyas trilogy "playing with body and facial positioning as well as settings to fulfill the refined taste of his new patron". 1851-1862: large religious machines, saints and martyrs, Shakespeare (Othello, 1857), the Florentine poet, and the first decorations. 1863-1869: the erotic mythological nude perfected (Nymph and Satyr, 1860; Birth of Venus, 1863) and the official portrait (Napoleon III, 1865; McCormick, 1867). 1870-1879: after the fall of the Second Empire, dramatic literary subjects -- Dante (Francesca, 1870), the Bible (Thamar, 1875; Samson and Delilah, 1878; Daughter of Jephthah, c. 1879), Shakespeare (Portia, 1881, just over the decade boundary) -- and the society portrait in number: American Gilded Age sitters (McCormick, Catharine Lorillard Wolfe, John William Mackay), European aristocracy (Gorchakov, Keller, Vorontsova-Dashkova), actresses and singers (Sarah Bernhardt, 1872; Christina Nilsson as Pandora and as Ophelia, 1873). 1880-1888: the late heroines, Phaedra, Ophelia, Cleopatra, and the Pantheon Life of Saint Louis as the late monumental statement in decoration.

FINISH AND COLOUR: Early: academic discipline with flashes of hot colour -- the "vibrant orange drapery" in Cincinnatus that "alienated his critics", and Jesus in the Praetorium praised for "energetic movement and emotional depth" and for using "tonal contrast to draw the viewer's eye to the center". Rome: the finish is already the smooth academic fini, but the subjects are hotter -- the Fallen Angel shocked the Academy as no student had ever submitted a painting of the Devil from Rome. Middle: the polish perfected, colour "harmonious and controlled rather than the vibrant, broken color of the Impressionists". Late: the finish never loosens -- there is no late turn toward visible brushwork or plein air light; the fini stays. What changes is the register: richer, more decorative colour in the late pictures (Ophelia's gold-trimmed silk dress and flowers; Cleopatra's opulent interior), and a darker, more theatrical mood. McQueen is critical of the late portraits: "Cabanel's handling of paint often seems stiff and the results can be prosaic", and the unrestored Mrs Louise Van Loon-Borski shows handling "surprisingly lacking in depth and texture", though the sitter's face is sincerely presented.

CROWDS VS SINGLE FIGURES: Early, the multi-figure history machine -- twelve figures in The Death of Moses, the diagonal and the triangle. Middle, the single body as the whole picture -- Venus alone on the waves, the Fallen Angel alone on his rock, Echo alone with her hands to her ears. Late, the single heroine again, but now watched: Phaedra in her opulent interior, Cleopatra on her divan with the dying behind her, Ophelia alone on the water. The crowd never disappears -- the Pantheon Saint Louis cycle is four panels of it -- but the career's centre of gravity moves from the crowd organised by geometry to the single body organised by the gaze.

HANDLING: The constant is drawing first -- Picot's Neoclassical training, "the primacy of drawing... compositional clarity based on classical and Renaissance models, and a highly finished, polished execution that concealed the artist's brushwork". What changes is the object of the research: from a frieze composition in Rome, to a nude body rehearsed from Nymph to Venus, to a jewelled dress hung beside its portrait, to a tiger skin under a languid queen. The method scales; the eye does not change.

FOLDED IN: The atelier that made its own opposition (main-leg M11): Cabanel taught the painters who replaced him -- Bastien-Lepage, Regnault, Debat-Ponsan, Pelez, Buland, Dagnan-Bouveret, Gervex, Friant -- the Naturalists. "No fewer than 112 exhibitors in the Salon of 1886 signed themselves 'Pupil of Cabanel'" and "seven out of ten is often the proportion of his pupils accepted to enter into loge for the Prix de Rome". His atelier was large and over-subscribed; its methods (drawing discipline, figure construction) were carried into rural realism. Eclectic Light's verdict: "Perhaps we should consider Alexandre Cabanel to be the father of Naturalism." Friant, his pupil from 1879, "became a bitter critic of both academic painting and the academy system during that time". The repetition and replica economy (main-leg M12): major pictures exist in multiple states -- the Venus replica of 1875 for John Wolf; the Paradise Lost reduced version (Musee d'Orsay) plus "numerous preparatory studies"; Cleopatra's five preparatory drawings plus a small oil sketch plus an 1883 smaller first version; Thamar known widely through the 1878 Baude lithograph. The system: sketch, esquisse, oil study, Salon canvas, reduced replica, engraving -- a picture as a production line. The early colour shock (main-leg M13): before the polish, Cabanel used intense, even jarring colour -- the "vibrant orange drapery" in Cincinnatus that "alienated his critics". The mechanism is the negative of the later flesh machine: colour as provocation, later disciplined into opalescence. The preparatory-drawing foundation (main-leg M14): the finished polish rests on draughtsmanship -- pierre noire with white highlights on chamois paper (Paradise Lost angel study, Nancy); black chalk study for Tamar and Absalom (Morgan Library); five drawings and an oil sketch for Cleopatra; the esquisse as a required academic step. The 19thc review: "If Cabanel's sometimes mechanical painting practice appeals less to certain viewers, the drawings will not disappoint."

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic of Cabanel is a logic of pretexts, and the pretexts are the point. First, the subject as pretext: he said it himself in 1863, "usually the subject is merely a pretext to hint at or to express an underlying ideal" -- the Venus is a goddess so that she can be a courtesan, the nymph is a myth so that she can be an erotic body, Pandora is a myth so that she can be a portrait of a soprano. The picture's defence and its prosecution both rest on the same fact: Zola calls the Venus a courtesan of marzipan, Gautier calls her a young woman asleep on the crest of a wave, and both are looking at the same pink-white body. Second, the finish as the argument: the opalescent flesh is not decoration on the picture, it IS the picture -- the hand disappears so the body looks poured, and the poured body is what the emperor buys, what Goupil engraves, what the critics attack as a balloon and a marzipan. Huysmans' charge -- "a badly inflated balloon", "gaseous paint... flabby lick... something like soft octopus flesh" -- is aimed at the finish, not the subject: the finish is the offence. Third, the gatekeeper who made the Refuses: the man who sat on the jury seventeen times and refused Manet in 1863 is the man whose refusal created the Salon des Refuses, the founding institution of modern art -- the academic machine manufactured its own opposition twice over, once in the jury room and once in the atelier, where his pupils became the Naturalists who displaced him. Fourth, the teacher who fathered the revolt against himself: the atelier's best pupils mostly failed the Prix de Rome and turned Naturalist instead, carrying his drawing discipline into rural realism; the professor's system was so strong its graduates outgrew it, and the man Mirbeau called a professor who only professed was, by the Dictionary of Art's account, the single most formative teacher of Belle Epoque French painting. Fifth, the same Venus, opposite verdicts: bought by the emperor, praised by Gautier, attacked by Zola and Huysmans, defended by its own painter as an ideal behind a pretext -- the picture holds all four verdicts at once and none of them cancels the others. Sixth, the reversal in the other direction: the Venus hovers and the Angel rises -- the picture that was the hit of 1863 is now disputed as his most famous work, because the Fallen Angel, coldly received in Rome in 1847 and criticised as too romantic, has become the widely reproduced image of defiant Lucifer in popular culture, while the Venus remains the type-image of Second Empire academic painting. The aesthetic, stated plainly, is this: a classical name licenses a body, a vanished brushstroke licenses a purchase, and a system built to keep the new painting out ends up teaching the new painters how to paint. The pieces this study feeds should keep the poured flesh, the horizontal body, the alibi, the half-open gaze, the doomed woman in the rich room, and the split stage -- and they should keep the system too: the jury, the engraving, the replica, and the pupil who leaves.

## Load-Bearing Corrections
C0. The Prix de Rome of 1845 was the Second Grand Prix, not the First Prize. His "Jesus in the Praetorium" ranked second to Francois-Leon Benouville's, and he received the Rome residency only because a prize went unawarded in musical composition, freeing a place. Most English sources simplify this to "won the Prix de Rome"; the short form is used in this study only as shorthand, never as the record.
C1. Death date: 23 January 1889, not 24 January. The current English and French Wikipedia both say 23 January 1889, the French citing the Paris death certificate no. 172; an older English Wikipedia revision, as mirrored by the aguse gateway, said 24 January 1889. The 23rd is used throughout; weekday Wednesday, verified with date -d.
C2. Salon debut: 1844, not 1843. The 1844 date is corroborated by the 1844 Salon showing of "Christ in the Garden of Olives" (English Wikipedia, Viardot 1882); the 1843 claim (Gallery 19C, "a regular exhibitor from 1843") has no named work attached and is treated as unverified.
C3. The Death of Moses is dated 1850 by the Musee Fabre, Eclectic Light and Daily Art Magazine, and 1851 by the French Wikipedia table and the Dahesh Museum version (279 by 391 cm); the Salon showing was 1852 either way. This study gives 1850/1851 and does not pick one silently.
C4. Phaedra dimensions disagree between sources: the Musee Fabre object record gives 225 cm high by 314 cm wide (framed 245 by 333.5 cm); Eclectic Light gives 194 by 286 cm. Unresolved; the smaller figure may be the unframed canvas or an older measurement. Both figures are recorded here; neither is asserted as the only one.
C5. The Birth of Venus measures 130 by 225 cm on the Musee d'Orsay record. The figure 150 by 250 cm appears in an older Wikipedia mirror and is not used.
C6. Michelangelo in His Studio, Visited by Pope Julius II is dated 1856 by the French Wikipedia table (Musee Fabre) and 1859 by Daily Art Magazine (Musee Goupil, Bordeaux); possibly two versions, or one source misdates. Both are recorded; the Goupil commission story attaches to the 1859 date.
C7. The Glorification of Saint Louis is dated c. 1853 by English Wikipedia and 1853-1855 by the French Wikipedia table (Luneville). The range is used.
C8. There are two Portias, two different compositions: the 1881 Salon picture, "The Merchant of Venice" (Act III scene 2, the caskets), and "La Belle Portia" (1886, Sotheby's: "Five years after Cabanel's first painting of Portia"). They are listed separately and must not be merged.
C9. "Thamar" is dated 1875 (Musee d'Orsay); the NYPL Digital Gallery lists "Tamar and Absalom" as Salon 1878. These may be the same canvas exhibited twice, or two versions. Both entries are kept, with the flag, rather than merged without evidence.
C10. The retrospective end date: the McQueen review says the Musee Fabre show ran 10 July 2010 to 2 January 2011; the French Wikipedia says 10 July to 5 December 2010. The Cologne dates agree (4 February to 15 May 2011). Both end dates are recorded.
C11. Hooper (1883) calls Cabanel "unmarried"; this is her error. Wikipedia records two marriages (1855 and 1869), both wives dead young. The quote is verbatim as she printed it; the claim is not repeated as fact.
C12. Tancrede Bastet's surname is spelled "Baster" in the Eclectic Light text and "Bastet" in the Wikipedia caption and search results. The painting (Cabanel's Studio, 1883, Musee de Grenoble) is the same. Verification used "Tancrede Bastet"; a "Baster" slug check was not run, and no study header or local file exists under either spelling.
C13. The Cincinnatus of 1843: Eclectic Light suspects the 1843 date is an error and the picture is the 1845 Prix de Rome second-place entry. The 1843 date is not asserted firmly here.
C14. Paradise Lost (1867, Maximilianeum, Munich) was destroyed in the bombing of Munich in 1945. Only Cabanel's own reduced version (121.9 by 94 cm, Musee d'Orsay) and preparatory studies survive. The original must not be described as viewable.
C15. Zola's marzipan passage on the Venus survives in translation variants: "drowned in a sea of milk" (Orsay record, main leg) against "drowned in a river of milk" (Google Arts and Culture, network leg). Both are the same French in English dress; neither wording is asserted as the unique original.
C16. "Perhaps we should consider Alexandre Cabanel to be the father of Naturalism" is the Eclectic Light Company's verdict (2018), not Cabanel's claim and not a contemporary one. It is quoted as a modern reassessment.
C17. The Academy's verdicts on Orestes ("an oversized and inept composition") and The Fallen Angel ("The movement is wrong, the draughtsmanship imprecise, the execution deficient") rest on a blog's search snippet of the proces-verbaux (via Sybille Bellamy-Brown), not on a page read end to end in any leg. They are quoted with that provenance.
C18. "Won the Prix de Rome in 1845" is the simplification found in most English sources; the accurate record is the Second Grand Prix (see C0).
C19. The 1883 Art Journal pairing is verified, not assumed: Lucy H. Hooper's "Contemporary French Artists. Meissonier and Cabanel", The Art Journal, 1883, was read end to end in the network leg (858 lines), Meissonier section lines 152-429, Cabanel section lines 434-642.
C20. No museum object page was read end to end in any leg. Dimensions and locations in this study rest on Wikipedia, the French Wikipedia works table, Eclectic Light, KMSKA and the McQueen review; where those disagree, the disagreement is recorded (see C3, C4, C5, C6).
C21. The 2010 retrospective catalogue (Hilaire and Amic, Somogy, 2010) was not read in any leg; it was quoted only through McQueen's review. It is the obvious next source for a deeper leg.
C22. "Velleda" (after Chateaubriand's "Les Martyrs") is spelled "Vellela" in the French Wikipedia table; the difference is treated as a transcription variant.
C23. "St. Monica in a Landscape" is known only from Rebecca Kidd's 2019 thesis, cited in English Wikipedia ref 1. No date or location was found in the sources read; it is not counted in the works list and not dated here.
C24. The 1875 Venus replica was painted for the banker John Wolf and offered to the Metropolitan Museum in 1893. An older mirror dates the replica c. 1864; the 1875 / John Wolf / Met account is the consistent one and is used.
C25. The "Salon of Venuses" nickname for the Salon of 1863 is Theophile Gautier's, as quoted in the sources read. It describes the crowd of nude goddesses that year, not Cabanel's picture alone.
C26. The Fallen Angel's modern popularity -- the widely reproduced image of defiant Lucifer -- is the Nice Art Gallery's claim and the main leg's observation of its online afterlife, not the result of an independent survey of reproductions in any leg.
C27. The full pupil roll (46 names) was read via search results only, not opened end to end. Pupil status for the lesser-known names in the Network rests on that roll plus, for some, the Wikipedia pupils-list snippets.
