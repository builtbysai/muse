# Francois Dumont: Study Notes

**Site:** Francois Dumont (Thursday 7 January 1751 - Saturday 27 August 1831), French painter of portrait miniatures -- born at Luneville, in Lorraine, the son of the coachman of the equipages of the King of Poland, Stanislaw Leszczynski; left an orphan when quite young, with five brothers and sisters to support; died in Paris, where he had resided the greater part of his life. He was apprenticed from 1762 to 1768 to Jean Girardet, peintre ordinaire to Stanislaw; he came to Paris in 1768; by 1773 he was undertaking portraits of the royal circle; the Comtesse d'Artois introduced him to Marie-Antoinette, who made him one of her favourite miniaturists and named him her miniaturiste in 1780; he painted a miniature of Benjamin Franklin in 1779, copied from the Duplessis portrait and set into a snuffbox lid, for which Franklin paid 8 louis; he was received into the Academie royale in 1788 with two pastel heads and granted an apartment in the Louvre; he married Marie-Nicole Vestier, daughter of the miniature painter Antoine Vestier, in 1789, and had two sons, Aristide and Bias, both of whom became painters; he continued to work through the Revolution, signing his miniatures by the Republican calendar, but did not succeed in being admitted to the imperial court; at the Restoration he worked again for the Bourbons; and he is remembered, in the Britannica's phrase, as "one of the three greatest miniature painters of France, painting portraits of Louis XVI, Marie Antoinette, Louis XVIII, and Charles X, and of almost all the important persons of his day." His one documented encounter with the next generation is the hinge of this study: in 1786 the nineteen-year-old Jean-Baptiste Isabey arrived in Paris with a letter of introduction to Dumont, the queen's premier peintre en miniature, and Dumont told him "I can do nothing for you... I do not take pupils for miniature" -- then gave him the address of a life studio he sometimes visited, and Isabey went on to eclipse him. The signature works are the Franklin miniature (1779), the full-length Marie Antoinette at St Cloud (1784), the Marquis de Chastellier-Dumesnil in its tortoiseshell bonbonniere (c. 1785), the Marion de Tiville bust with its surviving ten-louis receipt (1787), the Lady Playing the Harpsichord (c. 1790), the self-portrait (1791), the Marguerite Gerard before her canvas (1793, Wallace Collection), the Portrait of an artist with a scarf (1793, hairwork on the reverse), the smiling Count of Saint Paul (1795, signed "l'an 3"), the Pagin family miniatures (1796-1800, signed by the Republican year), Mlle Mars with a lyre in a wood (1797-98), the double-sided V and A portraits with their urn designs on the reverse (c. 1800), and the second portrait of Anne Vallayer-Coster (1804), painted thirty-five years after his first portrait of the same woman had first attracted notice to his art. The modern record is Bodo Hofstetter's catalogue raisonne, Le miniaturiste Francois Dumont (1751-1831) (1994), cited everywhere and read nowhere in this study. ## M1. Watercolour and Gouache on Ivory -- Stipple and Fine Brush over a Luminous Ground
The whole practice starts here, and it starts small: the Marion de Tiville bust, 3.3 by 2.7 cm; the Chastellier-Dumesnil head, 45 by 37 mm, set in a bonbonniere lid; the Met souvenir miniature, an oval 3.5 by 2.9 cm; the artist with a scarf, 7 cm across; a lady miniature 6.5 by 5.8 cm. The technique is translucent watercolour and gouache -- bodycolour -- laid in tiny dots and hairline strokes on a thin ivory plaque, the same ground Isabey worked on, and the same rule: the ivory itself supplies the skin light, pigment is kept thin so the ground glows through, and modelling is built by dot density, not by opaque blending. The period manuals the leg read (Day 1853, Payne 1829) give the working sequence in four terms -- floating, a broad fluid wash left to dry level, mostly for draperies; washing-in, broad faint strokes for hair, backgrounds and the general flesh tint; handling, the manner in which all fleshy parts are worked after washing-in; marking, sharp spirited touches to the features -- with stippling, small dots in varying density, building the tone and hatching as its line-based equivalent. Opaque bodycolour is reserved for the lights: linen, lace, pearls, drapery. The manuals are period practice, not Dumont's own method -- no Dumont-specific step-by-step exists in the sources read -- but they describe the craft he practised, and the museum medium lines agree: "watercolour and gouache on ivory" is the repeated formula. **Takeaway:** build the face from dot density on a glowing ground; never paint the light, only the density that lets it through. Smallness is not a limitation of the medium; it is the medium's argument.

## M2. The Ground as Light Source -- Tooth, Ox-Gall and Gum Arabic
Ivory is non-absorbent and greasy: watercolour will not take on it unless the ground is prepared. Day's manual gives the preparation: the ivory's polish is removed to give it "tooth" -- pumice stone sifted on the wet ivory and rubbed in circular motions with a glass muller for fifteen to twenty minutes until the scratches disappear and the surface roughens enough for colour to adhere -- and fingers must never touch the ivory, because colour will not settle where touched. Colours are ground in powder and mixed with gum arabic water, and prepared ox-gall is used to make the watercolour take on the greasy surface. Sable brushes only are "indispensable"; camel-hair is "worthless... from their want of elasticity"; a larger No. 5-6 sable lays the first shadow washes, to get "broadness of touch", and a small sable does the stippling -- but Day warns the stippling brush "should not be too small; since, if it be too small, it leads to an excess of finish, or rather stippling, which distracts the attention from the higher objects of art, namely, depth, solidity, and colour." That warning is the bridge to M3: the craft's own literature knows that too much stipple kills the picture. The ground does the lighting, the pigment does the interrupting, and the preparation is the work that makes the interruption possible. **Takeaway:** prepare the ground before the subject; the ground is the light source and the palette is the shadow cast on it. A portrait that changes ground changes register.

## M3. Breadth over Finish -- The Unfinished as the Most Beautiful
The standing critical verdict on Dumont is Britannica's: "The work of both painters is distinguished by breadth, precision and a charming scheme of coloring, and the unfinished works of the elder brother are amongst some of the most beautiful miniatures ever produced." Breadth is the operative word: not over-finished. The Greene introduction traces the same arc across four works: the early Marie-Josephe de Saxe is "urbane" and indoor; the Lady Playing the Harpsichord of c. 1790 shows "more robust modeling and more brilliant brushwork"; the Count of Saint Paul of 1795, smiling, "reveals the ease and dash with which Dumont could paint"; and the 1804 Vallayer-Coster is a "return to a more precise technique... perhaps due to the influence of Augustin." So the hand loosens through the Revolution -- robust, dashing, easy -- and tightens again late, under the influence of the next generation's classic precision. The unfinished works are the evidence for the loose hand: a miniature that stops before the stipple closes is more beautiful than one carried to full finish, because the ivory still shows and the breadth survives. **Takeaway:** stop before the finish closes; breadth is the device, and the unfinished state is a legitimate end, not a sketch. The watchable behaviour is the loosening: the dots thin out and the ground takes over.

## M4. The Full-Length Miniature Scene -- Portrait as a Small Scene
Most Dumonts are busts in a circle or an oval, 35 to 73 mm. The ambitious exceptions are all full-length figures in a setting, and they are the distinctive Dumont format: the Marie Antoinette at St Cloud of 1784, full-length in a white satin hooped dress trimmed with gold fringe and tassels, a blue velvet train with gold fleur-de-lis, hair high with feathers and aigrette, a vase and a crown on a table beside her; the Marguerite Gerard of 1793, full-length before a blank canvas, palette and brush in hand; Mlle Mars of 1797-98, standing in a wood holding a lyre, dark hair, white robe and gold girdle; Jean Pierre Pagin of 1799-1800, standing before a harpsichord playing the violin, powdered hair in a queue, blue coat, striped waistcoat, a statue beside him; the Vicomte d'Arlincourt with his son, father in a red robe holding a miniature, the boy in light blue pointing at it. These are portraits as small scenes: landscape, furniture and props do the work that a bust leaves to the face, and the sitter is shown at what they do -- painting, playing, holding a miniature -- rather than simply facing the viewer. The format is what separates Dumont from the pure bust miniaturist: the picture is a room, not a head. **Takeaway:** put the sitter in a scene with one prop and one action; the miniature is a stage three centimetres wide, and the action is the likeness. The watchable behaviour is the doing: the bow moves, the brush touches the canvas, the boy points.

## M5. The Artist-Portrait Series -- The Miniaturist Painting His Own Profession
Dumont "commenced a series of miniature portraits of the celebrated artists of his time" (Chiswick Press catalogue), and the series is the thread that runs from his first noticed work to his last: Anne Vallayer-Coster in 1769, the portrait that "first attracted notice to his art", and Anne Vallayer-Coster again in 1804, thirty-five years later, holding a paintbrush in one hand and a vase with flowers in the other, a still life -- believed to be one of her own paintings -- in the background to her right; Marguerite Gerard in 1793 and again in 1804, the second time in a pale green dress with a blue sash and a falling white veil, still holding brush and palette before a canvas; Luigi Cherubini at the Salon of 1793; Jean-Pierre Pagin playing the violin and his daughter Louise Marie Augustine Pagin; the unidentified Portrait of an artist with a scarf (1793); his own self-portrait (1791), engraved by Audouin and by Tardieu. The series is a network in itself: the miniaturist painting his own profession, artist by artist, so that the oeuvre doubles as a group portrait of the Paris art world -- and it is the one part of his practice that survived the Revolution unchanged, because artists were still there to paint when the court was not. No source read gives a full sitter list; the list lives in Hofstetter's 1994 catalogue raisonne, not accessed. **Takeaway:** paint the profession, not just the person; a series of makers is a portrait of a world, and each new sitter extends the census. The watchable behaviour is the accumulation: the series fills up, maker by maker.

## M6. The Mount as the Object -- Snuffbox, Bonbonniere and Hairwork
The image is inseparable from its mount, and for Dumont the mount is often the commission: the Franklin miniature of 1779 is set into a snuffbox lid; the Chastellier-Dumesnil head sits in the lid of a gold-mounted tortoiseshell bonbonniere; the Met souvenir of 1776-77 is an oval miniature set into a gold and enamel notebook case; the artist with a scarf of 1793 has braided hair in a chequerboard pattern worked into the reverse of its mount; the Pagin daughter's miniature sits in an amboyna-wood frame with an ormolu border; Mlle Mars wears a large rectangular pearl frame; the plum-satin lady has a glazed silk reverse. The Met states the general function, which is the medium's court role: "Gold snuffboxes and boxes decorated with portrait miniatures were prized and frequently given as royal gifts, often to ambassadors or members of the court in lieu of cash payments for their services." For Dumont himself no source found documents a specific miniature given as a diplomatic present -- the documented case is his contemporary Sicardi's, whose Louis XVI snuffbox miniatures were commissioned for diplomatic presents and recorded name by name in the Affaires etrangeres archives (see C6) -- so the study presents the gift function as the medium's role, evidenced through Sicardi, not as a Dumont fact. What is Dumont's own is the objecthood: the miniature as a jewel, a keepsake with hair on its back, a lid that opens. **Takeaway:** the mount is part of the portrait; scale plus mount plus occasion equals meaning, and the picture is an object before it is an image. The watchable behaviour is the opening: the lid lifts, the box turns, the hairwork shows on the back.

## M7. The Account Book and the Ten-Louis Bust -- Price as a Mechanism
Dumont's commercial practice is unusually well documented, and the documents are the mechanism. His account book, begun in 1788, records prices that year from 7 to 45 livres, and 10 louis was his most frequent rate for a bust portrait (Proantic, expert Nathalie Lemoine-Bouchard). The receipt survives for one of them: the Marion de Tiville bust of 1787, 3.3 by 2.7 cm, sold with Dumont's original signed receipt dated Friday 7 December 1787 -- ten louis for the portrait (see Q2). The Franklin miniature of 1779 was paid at 8 louis. The Pagin daughter's miniature carries his Paris address on its reverse: "Monsieur Dumont, Quai de la Megisserie 66". A bust has a price, a price has a standard rate, and the rate is written down year by year in a book the artist kept himself -- the miniature as a trade with a tariff, not a favour with a tip. The account book is the key primary source for his practice -- prices, sitters, dates -- and it was not accessible in full in this study; it is cited only through Lemoine-Bouchard. **Takeaway:** a fixed price for a fixed unit makes a practice legible; the bust is the unit, ten louis is the rate, and the book is the record. The watchable behaviour is the transaction: the receipt is written, the louis change hands, the bust is delivered.

## M8. The Republican Signature -- Dating by the Revolution
Dumont worked straight through the Revolution, and his signatures prove it: the Count of Saint Paul is signed "Dumont f. l'an 3" (1795); the Pagin daughter "Dumont f. l'an 5" (1796-97); Mlle Mars "Dumont l'an 6" (1797-98); Jean Pierre Pagin "Dumont f. l'an 8" (1799-1800). There is no gap in production; the Revolution changed his sitters, not his output. The signature itself is the dating system: the artist adopts the Republic's calendar on the face of a luxury object, a miniature on ivory for a private sitter, and the year of the Republic sits beside the surname that two brothers share. It is also the most practical of mechanisms for the study of his work: where a Dumont miniature is signed with a Republican year, it is dated to the year, and the dated run from l'an 3 to l'an 8 is the spine of the Revolutionary oeuvre. **Takeaway:** let the signature carry the date; the calendar on the picture is the history, and a dated run is a timeline you can read off the work. The watchable behaviour is the year changing: the same surname, a new number, a new regime's count.

## M9. Pastel and the Academy -- The Reception Pieces and the Savaged Portrait
Dumont's Academy career runs on pastel, not on ivory, and it ends in a public humiliation. He was agree and recu in 1788 with two pastel heads -- the required reception pieces, per Pierre's letter to d'Angiviller of Sunday 27 April 1788 ("ce nouveau candidat dut tres feste") -- sponsored by Duplessis, and granted an apartment in the Louvre. He exhibited two pastels at the Salon of 1789 (no. 133), including one of his brother Tony. His Salon 1791 pastel of M. Mentelle (no. 116) was savaged by the critic La Bequille, who said he could correct his mistake "by sticking a sheet of paper over the portrait" (Jeffares, quoting the critique). Jeffares: "He seems to have heeded this advice, as no further pastels are recorded." The pastel episode matters because it is the one place Dumont steps out of the miniature and is judged in public, at scale, in a medium the Salon could see -- and the judgement ends the experiment. No source found lists his miniature exhibits at the Salon; whether he exhibited miniatures there, and when, is unresolved (see C14). **Takeaway:** a medium can be a detour, and a public failure can close it; the miniature was his medium because the Salon told him the other one was not. The watchable behaviour is the covering: the sheet of paper goes over the portrait, and the pastel never comes back.

## M10. The Shared Surname -- Two Hands, One Signature
The structural fact of the whole oeuvre is that Francois and his younger brother Tony both signed "Dumont" only, and Tony was Francois's pupil: "Each artist signed with the surname only, and there is some controversy concerning the attribution to each artist of his own work" (Britannica 1911). Tony (1772-1810) was a frequent exhibitor, took an Academy medal in 1810, and was an expert violinist who "delighted in painting portraits of persons who were playing upon the violin" -- which is to say the brother who painted violinists signed the same name as the brother who painted a violinist (Jean Pierre Pagin, 1799-1800) and a violinist's daughter. Any unsigned or "Dumont"-signed miniature of the period is potentially either brother's, and the attribution hazard is not a footnote but the condition of the corpus: the oeuvre is a two-handed body of work under one name, and the "Francois Dumont" of the catalogues is partly a joint signature. The study carries the caveat on every work it lists. **Takeaway:** a shared signature makes the oeuvre a question, not a list; the attribution is part of the picture, and the doubt is the device. The watchable behaviour is the split: one name, two hands, and the picture resolving into one brother or the other and back again.

## Evolution
Phase 1 -- Luneville and Girardet, 1751-1768. Born Thursday 7 January 1751 at Luneville, son of the coachman of the King of Poland's equipages; left an orphan young, with five brothers and sisters to support. Apprenticed 1762-1768 to Jean Girardet, peintre ordinaire to Stanislaw Leszczynski, from whom he learned to draw -- Girardet was a portrait and mural painter, not a miniaturist, so the miniature technique was not learned from him. On the advice of a Luneville academician, "Madame Coster", he set up a studio for himself; the identity of Madame Coster is unresolved, and the obvious candidate, Anne Vallayer-Coster, does not fit the chronology (see C8). 1768: comes to Paris, where he meets Joseph Vernet and the architect Richard Mique, who had known him in Nancy; Mique probably introduces him to princely circles. Work: drawing, learned in Luneville; the miniature still to come.

Phase 2 -- Paris rise and the queen, 1769-1788. 1769: the first portrait of Anne Vallayer-Coster, the work that "first attracted notice to his art"; she becomes his patron and, the Greene introduction says, enables the Rome trip. By 1773 he is undertaking portraits of the royal circle. 1776-77: the Met souvenir miniature, an oval set into a gold and enamel case -- early work already aimed at the snuffbox and souvenir trade. 1779: the Franklin miniature, copied from Duplessis and set into a snuffbox lid, completed by Saturday 4 September 1779, paid 8 louis; Georgiana Shipley writes on Thursday 3 February 1780 that "the miniature is admirably painted, the Artist... appears inferior to none we have in England." 1780: named miniaturiste to Marie-Antoinette; the Comtesse d'Artois introduced him to the queen, who made him one of her favourite miniaturists; the contemporary title is "premier peintre en miniature de la reine". 1784: the full-length Marie Antoinette at St Cloud, signed "Palais de St. Cloud, 1784, Dumont, f.", the most ceremonial Dumont found. Rome: the dates will not settle -- Britannica and Wikipedia say he journeyed to Rome in 1784 and returned after four years' careful study; Jeffares says he travelled to Rome in 1786; Proantic says he had returned from Rome in 1786 (see C0). c. 1785: the Chastellier-Dumesnil head in its bonbonniere. 1787: the Marion de Tiville bust, with its ten-louis receipt of Friday 7 December 1787; the dealer note calls 1786 "the height of his career". 1788: received into the Academie royale with two pastel heads, sponsored by Duplessis, granted a Louvre apartment. Work: the court miniature at its height -- satin, gold fringe, blue velvet with fleur-de-lis, powdered hair, the queen's face three centimetres wide.

Phase 3 -- Revolution, 1789-1799. 1789: exhibits two pastels at the Salon (no. 133); marries Marie-Nicole Vestier, daughter of the miniaturist Antoine Vestier. 1791: the self-portrait; the Salon pastel of M. Mentelle (no. 116) savaged by La Bequille; no further pastels are recorded. 1792: a Marie Antoinette miniature, captioned in Wikipedia. 1793: the Marguerite Gerard before her canvas (Wallace Collection), "one of the most beautiful miniatures by Francois Dumont"; the Portrait of an artist with a scarf, signed and dated "Dumont/1793", hairwork on the reverse; the Cherubini miniature at the Salon of 1793. c. 1790: the Lady Playing the Harpsichord, "more robust modeling and more brilliant brushwork". 1795: the smiling Count of Saint Paul, signed "l'an 3", "ease and dash"; the Portrait of a Woman in a Brown Dress, "quiet austerity... a fascinating counterpoint to Dumont's pre-revolutionary miniature portraits of women portrayed in the opulent trappings of aristocratic ancien regime society". 1796-97: the Pagin daughter, "l'an 5", white bodice with orange trimming, purple scarf, un-powdered curling hair. 1797-98: Mlle Mars with a lyre in a wood, "l'an 6". 1799-1800: Jean Pierre Pagin playing the violin at the harpsichord, "l'an 8". Work: no gap in production; the sitters change decisively -- from the whole royal family to artists, actresses and musicians -- and the handling loosens, then the palette brightens and the powder comes off.

Phase 4 -- Empire and Restoration, 1800-1831. c. 1800: the V and A double-sided portraits, a young man in a blue coat and a portrait of a girl, each backed by a decorative urn-and-pedestal design on the reverse ivory -- the only non-portrait Dumont work found, ornamental work, possibly for box lids. 1804: the second Anne Vallayer-Coster, signed and dated "Dumont f. 1804", a "return to a more precise technique... perhaps due to the influence of Augustin"; the second Marguerite Gerard, in pale green with a blue sash and a falling white veil, eleven years after the first. He continued to work during the Revolution, but did not succeed in being admitted to the imperial court; no Napoleon portrait by Dumont was found in any source. At the Restoration he worked again for the Bourbons. The late sitters are professionals and old friends: the architect Vaudoyer and his wife as a married-couple pair, his old patron Vallayer-Coster, a second Gerard. Saturday 27 August 1831: dies in Paris, aged 80. Work: the same hand, the same ivory, the same ten-louis bust -- the court gone, the Empire passed over, the Bourbons back, and the miniature unchanged except that it tightened again at the end.

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is a machine for making a face portable, affordable and repeatable at a fixed price. He starts from the opposite problem to his pupil's: not a painter who found one cliff and rotated genres across it, but a painter who found one unit -- the small, finished, mountable bust, ten louis -- and let the sitters change around it: queens, then artists, then actresses, then architects. The ivory is the thesis: a face three centimetres wide, built from dot density on a glowing ground, carries full court presence because the ground does the lighting and the dots do the modelling, and the breadth of the touch -- stopping before the finish closes -- is what makes the unfinished works the most beautiful. The scene is the second thesis: when the bust is not enough, the miniature becomes a small stage, a painter before her canvas, a violinist at his harpsichord, an actress with a lyre in a wood, and the action is the likeness. The series is the third: the celebrated artists of his time, painted one by one, so that the oeuvre doubles as a census of a profession. And the mount is the fourth: the snuffbox lid, the bonbonniere, the hairwork on the reverse -- the picture is an object before it is an image, and it travels in a pocket. What makes him usable for generative work is not the royal subject -- that is the biography -- but the system underneath it: a density field on a luminous ground, a breadth that stops short of finish, a portrait that becomes a scene, a series that fills up maker by maker, a mount that turns a picture into a jewel, a signature that carries the date of a revolution, and a surname shared by two brothers so that the oeuvre itself is a question. And the refusal is the point: the man who told Isabey "I do not take pupils for miniature" taught him anyway, by giving him a studio address and letting him watch -- and the pupil who was refused went on to serve every sovereign of France, while the master who refused him served four kings and was passed over by the Emperor, and kept painting, at ten louis a bust, until he was eighty.

## POSITIONS
Tags: [FROM RECORD] = directly evidenced in a source read; [INFERRED] = read from the work and the record; [ABSENCE] = stated plainly. P1-P17 are his own practice and life; P18 is inferred; P19 is an absence stated plainly; P20 is reception.

P1 [FROM RECORD]: Luneville orphan, Girardet apprentice, drawing not miniature. Jeffares: born 7 January 1751 at Luneville, son of the coachman of the King of Poland's equipages, left an orphan young with five brothers and sisters to support; "from 1762 to 1768 he was apprenticed to Augustin's teacher Jean Girardet". Thierry de Maigret: "Francois Dumont learned to draw with Jean Girardet, the ordinary painter of King Stanislas." Girardet was a portrait and mural painter, not a miniaturist, so the miniature technique was not learned from him; the apprenticeship taught drawing, and the miniature came later, in Paris.

P2 [FROM RECORD]: Paris 1768, Vernet and Mique, the Comtesse d'Artois introduction, the royal circle by 1773. Thierry de Maigret: he came to Paris in 1768, met Joseph Vernet and the architect Mique, who had known him in Nancy, and "It is probably the latter who introduced him to the princely circles"; the Comtesse d'Artois introduced him to Marie-Antoinette; Jeffares: "By 1773 he was undertaking portraits of the royal circle." The route to the queen ran through introductions, not through the Salon or the Academy.

P3 [FROM RECORD]: The queen's miniaturist, 1780. Founders Online (editorial note, citing DBF/Benezit): named miniaturiste to Marie-Antoinette in 1780. Thierry de Maigret: "The queen made him one of her favorite miniaturists." The Maine paper gives the contemporary title as "premier peintre en miniature de la reine". He painted the whole royal family (Chiswick), and the queen's face is his signature subject -- two Marie Antoinette miniatures in the Chiswick collection alone, the St Cloud full-length of 1784 the most ceremonial.

P4 [FROM RECORD]: The Franklin miniature, 1779, and the Shipley verdict. Founders Online: the miniature of Benjamin Franklin, copied from the Duplessis portrait and set into a snuffbox lid, completed by 4 September 1779; Franklin paid 8 louis. Georgiana Shipley to Franklin, Thursday 3 February 1780: "the miniature is admirably painted, the Artist... appears inferior to none we have in England." The earliest documented Dumont outside France is a copy, in a lid, for cash -- the miniature as an export and a commodity from the start.

P5 [FROM RECORD]: Rome -- the dates will not settle. Britannica 1911 and Wikipedia: "In 1784 he journeyed to Rome, returning after four years careful study." Jeffares: "In 1786 he travelled to Rome." Proantic: "Having returned from a trip to Rome in 1786, Francois Dumont was at the height of his career." The 1786 return fits the career arc -- three months after the December 1787 Tiville receipt he was accepted into the Academy in May 1788 -- but no source reconciles the 1784 date. Do not pick one silently (see C0).

P6 [FROM RECORD]: The Academy, 1788, two pastel heads, a Louvre apartment. Britannica: "in 1788 was accepted as an Academician and granted an apartment in the Louvre." Jeffares: agree and recu in 1788 with two pastel heads, the required reception pieces, per Pierre's letter to d'Angiviller of Sunday 27 April 1788; sponsored by Duplessis. He exhibited two pastels at the Salon of 1789 (no. 133), including one of his brother Tony. The Academy admitted him on pastel, not on ivory -- the miniature itself was not an Academic medium.

P7 [FROM RECORD]: The Mentelle pastel and La Bequille; no more pastels. Jeffares: his Salon 1791 pastel of M. Mentelle (no. 116) was savaged by the critic La Bequille, who said he could correct his mistake "by sticking a sheet of paper over the portrait"; "He seems to have heeded this advice, as no further pastels are recorded." The one public judgement of Dumont outside the miniature ended the experiment.

P8 [FROM RECORD]: The Vestier marriage, 1789, and the sons who became painters. Jeffares: married Marie-Nicole Vestier, daughter of miniature painter Antoine Vestier, in 1789; two sons, Aristide and Bias, both became painters. Britannica agrees. The Louvre's Dumont holdings were presented by the heir of Bias Dumont. The marriage joins two miniature families -- Vestier's daughter, Dumont's sons, a workshop by blood.

P9 [FROM RECORD]: The account book, ten louis, the Tiville receipt. Proantic (expert Nathalie Lemoine-Bouchard): his account book, begun in 1788, records prices that year from 7 to 45 livres; 10 louis was his most frequent rate for a bust portrait. The Marion de Tiville bust of 1787, 3.3 by 2.7 cm, survives with Dumont's original signed receipt dated Friday 7 December 1787 for ten louis (see Q2). The Franklin miniature was paid at 8 louis in 1779. A bust has a price, and the price is written down.

P10 [FROM RECORD]: Working through the Revolution; the Republican signature. The dated run: Count of Saint Paul, "Dumont f. l'an 3" (1795); Pagin daughter, "Dumont f. l'an 5" (1796-97); Mlle Mars, "Dumont l'an 6" (1797-98); Jean Pierre Pagin, "Dumont f. l'an 8" (1799-1800). "He continued to work during the Revolution, but did not succeed in being admitted to the imperial court" (Thierry de Maigret). There is no gap in production; the Revolution changed his sitters, not his output.

P11 [FROM RECORD]: The sitters change -- court to artists, actresses, musicians. Pre-1789: the whole royal family and court -- Marie Antoinette twice, Princess Elizabeth, Madame Victoire, the comte de Provence, marquises and officials. 1793-1800: Marguerite Gerard (twice), Mlle Mars, Mlle Maillard as Liberty, the Pagin family, the Comte de Saint Paul, Luigi Cherubini. After 1800: professional couples (the Vaudoyers), his old patron Vallayer-Coster, a second Gerard. No Napoleon portrait by Dumont was found in any source.

P12 [FROM RECORD]: No Napoleon; failed at the imperial court; Restoration Bourbons. Thierry de Maigret: "He continued to work during the Revolution, but did not succeed in being admitted to the imperial court. At the Restoration, he worked again for the Bourbons." The contrast with Isabey is the point: the pupil served Napoleon with hundreds of miniatures as gifts and then the Restoration and Louis-Philippe; the master served four kings and was passed over by the Emperor. The arc -- royal favourite, Revolution survivor without imperial admission, Restoration court painter again -- is the political spine of the career.

P13 [FROM RECORD]: The artist-portrait series. Chiswick Press: he "commenced a series of miniature portraits of the celebrated artists of his time." Named in the sources read: Anne Vallayer-Coster (1769 and 1804), Marguerite Gerard (1793 and 1804), Luigi Cherubini (Salon 1793), Jean-Pierre Pagin and his daughter, the unidentified Portrait of an artist with a scarf (1793), his own self-portrait (1791). The series is the thread from the first noticed work to the last, and the one part of his practice that survived the Revolution unchanged.

P14 [FROM RECORD]: The full-length miniature scene. The ambitious exceptions to the bust format are all full-length figures in a setting: Marie Antoinette at St Cloud (1784); Gerard before her canvas (1793 and 1804); Mars with a lyre in a wood (1797-98); Pagin playing violin at the harpsichord (1799-1800); the Vicomte d'Arlincourt with his son. The portrait as a small scene, with landscape, furniture and props, is the distinctive Dumont format -- the sitter shown at what they do.

P15 [FROM RECORD]: Breadth, precision, charming colour; the unfinished works. Britannica 1911: "The work of both painters is distinguished by breadth, precision and a charming scheme of coloring, and the unfinished works of the elder brother are amongst some of the most beautiful miniatures ever produced." The Greene introduction traces the handling across four works: the early Saxe "urbane"; the c. 1790 harpsichord lady "more robust modeling and more brilliant brushwork"; the 1795 Saint Paul "ease and dash"; the 1804 Vallayer-Coster a "return to a more precise technique... perhaps due to the influence of Augustin." The hand loosens through the Revolution and tightens again late.

P16 [FROM RECORD]: The mount -- snuffbox, bonbonniere, hairwork. The Franklin miniature set into a snuffbox lid (1779); the Chastellier-Dumesnil head in a gold-mounted tortoiseshell bonbonniere (c. 1785); the Met souvenir in a gold and enamel case (1776-77); the artist with a scarf with braided hair in a chequerboard pattern on the reverse of its mount (1793); the Pagin daughter in an amboyna-wood frame with an ormolu border; Mlle Mars in a large rectangular pearl frame. The miniature is an object before it is an image, and the mount is part of the portrait.

P17 [FROM RECORD]: The shared surname. Britannica 1911: "Each artist signed with the surname only, and there is some controversy concerning the attribution to each artist of his own work." Francois and Tony both signed "Dumont" only; Tony was Francois's pupil, a frequent exhibitor, Academy medal 1810. Any unsigned or "Dumont"-signed miniature of the period is potentially either brother's, and the oeuvre is a two-handed corpus under one name.

P18 [INFERRED]: The court miniaturist as a one-man institution. The record shows no workshop and no pupils outside the family -- his own reported words are "I do not take pupils for miniature" -- and the business is a book, a rate and an address: the account book begun 1788, ten louis the standard bust, "Monsieur Dumont, Quai de la Megisserie 66" on the back of a miniature. The inference: Dumont's practice was a sole trade with a tariff, not a studio with assistants, which is why it survived the Revolution intact (a trade needs only sitters) and why it could not scale into the imperial gift factory Isabey ran (a factory needs hands). The miniature stayed a one-man unit because the man never built the machine around it.

P19 [ABSENCE, stated plainly]: No words of his own about his art exist in the record found -- no published statement, no manifesto, no letters, no interview. The only first-person Dumont text found anywhere in this study is the 7 December 1787 receipt for the Marion de Tiville portrait (see Q2), a business sentence, not an artistic one. Everything about his aims must be inferred from the commissions, the prices and the critics.

P20 [RECEPTION]: "One of the three greatest" against the eclipse by his pupil and by Augustin. Britannica 1911: "one of the three greatest miniature painters of France" -- the other two unnamed in the source (see C4). Jeffares: "He remains the oustanding [sic] miniaturist of his generation." The eclipse is the record itself: Isabey, the pupil he refused, "had achieved fame greater than that of Dumont, who had embarrassed him with his splendor not so very long before" (Maine), and Augustin's classic precision is the likely influence on Dumont's late tightening (Greene). The modern record is Hofstetter's 1994 catalogue raisonne, cited everywhere and read nowhere here.

## Load-bearing corrections
C0. Rome dates conflict, unresolved. Britannica 1911 and Wikipedia: journeyed to Rome in 1784, returning after four years' careful study (i.e. ~1788). Jeffares: "In 1786 he travelled to Rome." Proantic: returned from a trip to Rome in 1786, and "at the height of his career". The 1786 return fits the career arc (Academy admission May 1788, three months after the December 1787 Tiville receipt), but no source reconciles the 1784 date; do not pick one silently. The study states both.
C1. Marie-Antoinette et ses enfants au pied d'un arbre: Durham PhD thesis (Perna 2021) says 1790; University of Florida dissertation (Walter 2022, fig. 2-34) says 1789. Both cite the Louvre. Unresolved; the Louvre's own collection page was not opened.
C2. The Chiswick Press catalogue's artist list says of Dumont "Died in Paris, 1806." That is wrong against the unanimous 1831 (en.wikipedia, NGV, Britannica 1911, Thierry de Maigret) and is not used anywhere in this file except as a recorded error. Do not cite 1806.
C3. The V and A record for the Mlle Mars miniature gives "Francois Dumont (1751-1847)". 1847 is wrong; he died 27 August 1831. Treat as a V and A data error, not a fact.
C4. "One of the three greatest miniature painters of France" is Britannica's phrase; the other two are not named in the source. The natural inference (Isabey, Augustin) is an inference, not a sourced statement -- do not present it as fact.
C5. The lessons: #504's top-pick line says "Isabey's teacher in 1786-87". The record supports lessons in 1786 only (WGA, en.wikipedia, Philbrook), and the fuller account (Maine, after Taigny 1859) has Dumont refusing formal pupillage -- "I do not take pupils for miniature" -- and giving Isabey a life-studio address instead. Isabey returned home in 1787 after his father's death; no source puts lessons in 1787. The study uses "lessons in 1786" and records the refusal.
C6. No documented Dumont diplomatic gift. No source found ties a specific Dumont miniature to a diplomatic present. The documented case is Sicardi's: Louis XVI miniatures for snuff boxes to be given as diplomatic presents, recorded in the Affaires etrangeres archives (Sotheby's lot 129). The gift function is presented as the medium's court role, evidenced through Sicardi and the Met's general statement (Q20), not as a Dumont fact. Isabey's hundreds of miniatures as Napoleonic gifts are likewise Isabey's, not Dumont's.
C7. The kings list is traditional, not verified work-by-work. Britannica asserts Dumont painted Louis XVI, Marie Antoinette, Louis XVIII and Charles X; no individually dated miniature of any of the three kings was found in the sources read. The only royal male portrait verified by name is the undated comte de Provence (the future Louis XVIII) in the Louvre, via a plate caption. Do not list dated king portraits.
C8. "Madame Coster" identity unresolved. Britannica says Dumont set up his studio "on the advice of a Luneville Academician, Madame Coster." Anne Vallayer-Coster is the obvious candidate (Academician 1770, married a Coster of a Lorraine family, Marie-Antoinette's protegee) -- but she was unmarried until 1781 and Paris-based, while the advice belongs to Dumont's Luneville years (he left for Paris in 1768). The identification is plausible but not proven, and the chronology cuts against it. Do not assert it.
C9. Self-portrait, 1791: reproduced on Wikipedia with no holding institution stated; location unverified. It may be among the Louvre works presented by Bias Dumont's heir, but that is not established.
C10. Louvre holdings not directly verified. Britannica says works are in the Louvre "presented by the heir of Bias Dumont", and La miniature francaise captions one Dumont (comte de Provence) as Musee du Louvre, but no Louvre online collection record was opened by any leg -- no inventory numbers, dates or dimensions for any Louvre Dumont.
C11. V and A Mlle Mars dimensions (167 x 120 mm) are far larger than any other Dumont miniature and almost certainly include frame or mount, but the V and A record does not say so. Flag, do not quote as the ivory size.
C12. No Dumont-specific technical step-by-step exists in the sources read. The Day (1853) and Payne (1829) method -- pumice tooth, ox-gall, gum arabic, floating, washing-in, handling, marking -- is period ivory-miniature practice, not Dumont's own method, and is labelled as such wherever it appears in this file. The silver-foil paillon trick is a cognate from the Nelson-Atkins catalogue on a contemporary, not Dumont.
C13. Jean-Baptiste Guerin vs Jean-Urbain Guerin. Anticstore (citing Pappe 2015) names a "Jean-Baptiste Guerin" alongside Isabey and Augustin as pre-eminent among French miniaturists; the Isabey copyist is Jean-Urbain Guerin (Nelson-Atkins). They are plausibly one man under a variant name, or two men; no source read settles it. The study offers Jean-Urbain Guerin only, with the variant noted, and does not offer a separate "Jean-Baptiste Guerin".
C14. No Salon miniature exhibits found. Jeffares documents only pastels at the Salon (1789, no. 133, two portraits; 1791, no. 116, the Mentelle pastel). Whether Dumont exhibited miniatures at the Salon, and when, is unresolved. Hall exhibited 1769-1789, Sicardi 1791-1819, Augustin from 1791 -- the comparison set is documented, Dumont's own miniature entries are not.
C15. D1 HOLDS and D2 HOLDS: the briefed "Isabey received lessons from Francois Dumont in 1786" is confirmed by WGA, en.wikipedia and Philbrook, with the refusal nuance of C5; the briefed "Dumont died 27 August 1831, Paris" is unanimous across en.wikipedia, NGV and Britannica 1911. (See the Doorway section.)
C16. The "premier peintre en miniature" title: the Maine paper gives it as the contemporary title for Dumont; the dated appointment is miniaturiste to Marie-Antoinette in 1780 (Founders Online, citing DBF/Benezit). Do not backdate the "premier" wording to 1780 or present it as the formal appointment.
C17. Republican-year conversions are as given by the catalogues: "l'an 3" = 1795 (Saint Paul), "l'an 5" = 1796-97 (Pagin daughter), "l'an 6" = 1797-98 (Mlle Mars), "l'an 8" = 1799-1800 (Jean Pierre Pagin). The Revolutionary calendar year began in late September, so a signature reading "l'an 5" spans the end of 1796 and most of 1797; the study gives the catalogue's conversion, not its own.
