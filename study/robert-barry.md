Robert Barry was born on 9 March 1936 in the Bronx, New York City, in a hospital in the Bronx, and grew up in the Fordham Road / Grand Concourse area. [C28687] His mother was Frances Benenati, born in Italy -- the family came from Sicily, from Catania -- and his father was John Arthur Barry, five years older than his mother; Barry described himself as "half-Italian and half-Irish". [C28687] His father never finished high school, worked for Western Electric hauling wire and putting up telephone lines, later became an electrical engineer, and was also a disk jockey in the 1940s using his own equipment; he took the young Barry to the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Bronx botanical gardens. [C28687] Barry had an older brother, John, nine years his senior, who served in the Army in World War II and studied electrical engineering, and a younger sister about a year younger than him. [C28687] He attended Our Lady of Mercy parochial school in the Bronx, an all-boys school run by Dominican nuns, where art was the one subject he looked forward to, then Fordham Preparatory School for about a year, which he disliked and left; he has said he was a mediocre student who "got by with a B" but was always good at art. [C28687]

Barry began attending Hunter College in 1957, studying with William Baziotes (watercolour), Robert Motherwell (his advisor), Tony Smith and Ray Parker, and taking classes with the scholar and curator William Rubin. [C28688] After his undergraduate degree he worked briefly selling Christmas cards at Georg Jensen on Fifth Avenue, then taught art for about a year as a substitute teacher at Junior High School 55 in the South Bronx. [C28688] He was then drafted into the US Army and served two years, around 1959-1960 -- he recalled entering when Eisenhower was still president and being in service during the 1960 Nixon-Kennedy election -- never leaving Fort Dix, New Jersey, and working as an assistant cook preparing diets in the kitchen at Walson Army Hospital. [C28688] After the Army he returned to Hunter to finish a graduate degree in 1963, studying with Tony Smith, and was then hired as a professor at Hunter by department chair Eugene C. Goossen, teaching there from 1964; the end date of his Hunter teaching is disputed between sources (1979, 1977 or 1982 -- see What is NOT settled). [C28688] He later worked as a visiting lecturer and teacher internationally, including at CalArts during Douglas Huebler's tenure and at Bard College. [C28688]

Like many first-generation Conceptualists, Barry started out as a painter. [C28689] His early work was serial painting built from elementary forms and colours; the oldest work in his 2015 Hunter retrospective was a 1962 student painting of gridded red and black squares, and a 1963 work, *9 Squares*, is reproduced in the Buchloh interview. [C28689] He had his first solo exhibition at the Westerly Gallery, New York, in 1964, and in 1966 he was included in the seminal exhibition *Systemic Painting* at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum. [C28689] In 1968 he made his consequential turn away from painting toward non-material work: nylon thread stretched between spaces and buildings, electromagnetic fields, ultrasonic radiation, small quantities of radioactive material, and measured volumes of inert gases released into the atmosphere; in 1968 he said, "Nothing seems to me the most potent thing in the world." [C28689] His father built the small radio-wave transmitter boxes for him: *Electromagnetic Energy Field* (1968) was a battery-powered transmitter in a metal box that filled the gallery with an invisible force and emitted a whistle-like sound on a portable radio tuned to its frequency, and *Carrier Wave* (1968-69) used a radio station's carrier waves for a prescribed length of time, "not as a means of transmitting information, but rather as an object". [C28689] For the *Inert Gas Series* (1969) he bought glass containers of the noble gases from a school supplier and, with his dealer Seth Siegelaub, drove a rented Mercedes convertible into the Mojave Desert, laid the containers on newspaper and smashed them with a hammer; he smashed more bottles at a Beverly Hills hotel, and released 2 cubic feet of helium into the atmosphere on the morning of 5 March 1969. [C28689] His most radical dematerialisation was *Telepathic Piece* (1969), in which he undertook to telepathically transmit a work of art to the audience during the exhibition -- a work with no physical component at all. [C28689] In the same period he published blank pieces in *0 to 9* magazine that appeared only in the table of contents, and for *Prospect '69* he said the piece "consists of the ideas that people will have from reading this interview... The piece in its entirety is unknowable because it exists in the mind of so many people." [C28689]

From 1969 Barry produced works that allow the viewer an intense encounter with individual words -- drawn on paper, spoken on tape, projected as slides, and imprinted directly on walls. [C28690] He has said his point is to let a word be itself and to let the audience experience words one at a time, "the way little children read"; he composes word pieces clockwise, placing words in relation to the edges of a space, choosing from a constantly adjusted personal list (for a 1991 Holly Solomon show he chose "unnescessary", "remind", "not enough", "someone", "feeling", and "trouble"). [C28690] His *Closed Gallery* (1969) -- first done at Eugenia Butler's space in Los Angeles, and also in Amsterdam and Turin -- consisted of locking the gallery and notifying the public by postcard; a 2026 recreation at Timothy Hawkinson Gallery reprised the 1969 presentations. [C28690] In the 1970s he published several book projects and worked with slide projections showing language fragments on the wall, the succession interrupted by empty slides; a 1970 class assignment sent to David Askevold at the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design asked students to share a single secret idea that would cease to exist if revealed outside the group. [C28690] He began exhibiting regularly at the Leo Castelli Gallery in 1971, showed at the Holly Solomon Gallery through the 1990s, and has been represented by Yvon Lambert in Paris since 1973. [C28690]

Barry's work was shown at the Paris Biennale (1971), Documenta 5 in Kassel (1972), and the Venice Biennale (1972), and he took part in *When Attitudes Become Form* at Kunsthalle Bern and the ICA, London. [C28691] A comprehensive retrospective of his early work, *A Place To Which We Can Come, Works from 1963 to 1975*, was organised by the Kunsthalle Nürnberg and the Aargauer Kunsthaus, Aarau (the Kunsthalle Nürnberg retrospective is dated 2003 by Crown Point Press); a further retrospective, *Robert Barry: All the things I know ... 1962 to the present*, was held at Hunter College in 2015. [C28691] Since the 1980s, colour and geometric symbols, often combined with text fragments, have increasingly complemented his work; his later word works include wall pieces, paintings, acrylic casts, projections, and a 2007 installation *Art and War* with large words such as DOUBT, WITHOUT, and DESPERATE placed at random angles on the walls of a residential space. [C28691] He was the first living artist to have a work permanently installed at the Musée d'Orsay, in the Salle du Fumoir. [C28691] No honours, prizes, fellowships or grants list was found in any source consulted; the Musée d'Orsay permanent-installation distinction is the only distinction of that kind recorded, and it is unknown whether Barry received e.g. NEA or Guggenheim support -- none is asserted here. [C28691]

Barry is living. [C28692] He lives and works in Teaneck, New Jersey, where he moved with his wife and two sons in 1974 (Barry himself recalled the move as "'73 or something" -- see What is NOT settled). [C28692] Fresh evidence of activity: he turned 90 on 9 March 2026, marked by the solo exhibition *Robert Barry: Another Time...* at Timothy Hawkinson Gallery, Los Angeles, 11 March-4 April 2026, and a parallel *Another Time* show at Alfonso Artiaco, Naples; a 544-page career-spanning monograph, *Robert Barry: The Defining of It...*, edited by Mathieu Copeland, was published by Buchhandlung Walther und Franz König in 2025/2026, and he was the subject of a solo booth at Art Basel Paris in 2025. [C28692] His work is in the permanent collections of the Museum of Modern Art, New York; the Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden, Washington, DC; the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York; the Musée d'Orsay, Paris; the Whitney Museum of American Art, New York; the Centre Pompidou, Paris; the Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles; and the National Gallery of Art, Washington, DC, among others. [C28692]

The twenty works inspected run 1962-2015 and trace one practice shedding its objects until only the word is left, and then letting the word stand alone. Early, 1962-1967: the work is still a painting, but a serial one -- gridded squares, repeated units, multi-panel canvases where a line crosses the wall gap -- and the viewer is a reader of regularity, not of images: Untitled gridded red and black squares (W01, 1962), Untitled red squares on blue (W02, 1963), Green Line (W03, 1966), Untitled four-part painting (W04, August 1967), Red Seconds (W05, 1966-1967) and Scenes (W06, 1967). Middle, 1968-1970: the object disappears entirely -- nylon thread between buildings, a radio wave, ultrasound above hearing, a buried radioisotope, gas smashed open in the desert, a telepathic transmission, a locked gallery -- and the viewer must take the work on trust from the statement: Nylon thread installations (W07, 1968), Electromagnetic Energy Field (W08, 1968), 88mc Carrier Wave (FM) (W09, October 1968), 40kHz Ultrasonic Sound-wave Installation (W11, 1969), 0.5 Microcurie Radiation Installation (W12, 5 January 1969), Inert Gas Series (W13, 1969), Telepathic Piece (W14, 1969), Closed Gallery (W15, 1969), All the things I know but of which I am not at the moment thinking -- 1:36 PM; June 15, 1969 (W16, 1969), It Has Order (W17, 1969-1970) and Marcus Piece (W18, 1970). Recent, 1977-2015: the word is the durable medium, projected, cast, vinyl-lettered across whole buildings, and layered back over image and sound -- and the viewer is a walker who reads a room, one word at a time: Belmont 1967 (W19, 1977), Art and War (W20, 2007) and the Bethlehem Baptist Church Installation (W10, 2015). ## M1 -- The invisible medium: the room is full and you cannot tell

A battery-powered transmitter in a nondescript metal box fills the gallery with an invisible, immeasurable but real force, and with a radio tuned to the frequency it gives off a sound "kind of like a whistle" (W08). The carrier wave (W09) is the base frequency through which signals would be carried, with no sound, song or words; amplified through a transistor radio tuned to 88mc it produces tones of varying intensity depending on signal strength, and Barry said it "blots out all other signals in the area". The 40kHz installation (W11) arrived a day before the January Show, was inaudibly played, and then removed -- at 40 kHz the sound is above human hearing, so the gallery was sonically full and the visitor heard nothing. The radiation installation (W12) is detectable only with a Geiger-Müller counter, which would make it audible as ticks -- the only one of Barry's January-show works that could produce sound at all. The watchable behaviour is standing in an apparently empty room and knowing it is full: the work's boundary is the reach of the signal, not the walls, and the viewer must accept the work on faith -- or bring a radio and hear the whistle. Seen in W08, W09, W11 and W12.

**Takeaway:** fill the room with something real and imperceptible; the empty room is the work. [C28694]

## M2 -- The measured volume to indefinite expansion: a fixed measure, an unbounded outcome

The gases are bought in measured volumes -- helium 2 cubic feet, krypton 1 litre -- and then smashed open and released, expanding from a measured volume to indefinite expansion, still floating in the Earth's atmosphere (W13). The containers are laid on newspaper and smashed with a hammer in the Mojave Desert, at a Beverly Hills hotel, in the mountains, on the beach; the gases are odourless, colourless, chemically inert, with "beautiful Greek names", and Barry described the series as "a kind of large environmental sculpture" through undetectable but pervasive materials, and as simply returning the gas to the atmosphere, completing a cycle. The viewer encounters only the poster, the typeset text and black-and-white photographs: the work itself is gone the moment it happens, and can never be recovered or re-shown. The watchable behaviour is reading a precise quantity and knowing the thing it measures is now everywhere and nowhere -- the measure is exact, the outcome is unbounded. Seen in W13.

**Takeaway:** fix the measure, release it, and let the work expand forever. [C28695]

## M3 -- The decay schedule: a work that outlives the show on a physical clock

On the first day of the January Show Barry buried a small container of barium-133 somewhere in Central Park (W12). The stated duration is about ten years, following barium-133's half-life: every ten years its energy halves, "but it goes on to infinity, it never goes to nothing". The work decays on a physical schedule nobody can watch; there is no object, no site marker, only the catalogue entry, and the burial site itself is the work's location. Restaged in 2025 at Art Basel Paris with mildly radioactive torbernite buried in Parc de la Villette, the same logic holds: the piece is a clock that keeps ticking after the exhibition closes, after the artist dies, after the viewer forgets. The watchable behaviour is knowing a duration without being able to watch it pass -- the work is a date, a half-life, and a place you cannot find. Seen in W12.

**Takeaway:** bury the work and let a half-life be its duration. [C28696]

## M4 -- Telepathy: the work exists only in the audience's mind

Barry's statement for *Telepathic Piece* (W14): "During the exhibition I will try to communicate telepathically a work of art, the nature of which is a series of thoughts that are not applicable to language or image." In performance he sat in silence before an audience and said, "I'm trying to telepathically transmit an idea to you," while the audience sat silent. There is no object, no photograph, not even a sound -- only the proposition and the audience's reception (or non-reception). Barry later said (2017) he "didn't believe in it, of course", but thought it was "fun", and that "people sometimes would say that they were receiving something". The work exists, if at all, in the minds of the audience; each person can know only the part in their own mind. The watchable behaviour is sitting in silence and wondering whether anything is arriving -- the attempt is the work, and the doubt is part of it. Seen in W14.

**Takeaway:** transmit nothing and let the audience supply the work. [C28697]

## M5 -- The closed gallery: the show you cannot enter

The only physical artefact is the invitation card stating the gallery will be closed for/during the exhibition (W15). The premises stay locked and inaccessible for the whole run; anyone who does not believe the card finds the entrance locked. The work turns the exhibition into a private, internal experience: spectators must picture the art solely in their own heads. The wording varies -- "the gallery will be closed" (a service) versus "during the exhibition" (as if a show were going on somewhere else, in the ether, behind locked doors). At the Los Angeles showing the gallery's telephone was answered by two women from a telephone answering service in a small office on Sunset Boulevard -- the answering-service message describing the "work" was the only access. The watchable behaviour is standing outside a locked door and imagining the show inside; the refusal is the exhibition. Seen in W15.

**Takeaway:** lock the door and let the invitation be the show. [C28698]

## M6 -- The isolated word: one word at a time, the word being itself

From 1969 Barry's durable medium is the single word, drawn on paper, spoken on tape, projected as a slide, imprinted directly on a wall. His point is to let a word be itself and to let the audience experience words one at a time, "the way little children read"; he composes word pieces clockwise, placing words in relation to the edges of a space, choosing from a constantly adjusted personal list of roughly 200 words. In *It Has Order* (W17) four nearly blank pages carry eight typewritten sentences -- "... it has order ... it is always changing ... it is affected by other things ... it affects other things ... it is not confined ... it is not in any specific place ... it can be presented, but go unnoticed ... to know of it is to be part of it ..." -- and the "it" is never named, so the viewer must supply the subject. In *Art and War* (W20) 21 words selected at random are placed on walls, stairs and ceiling, moving in and out of any coherent syntax as the viewer walks, so the "narrative" is experienced in fragments, recompiled differently by each visitor. In the Bethlehem Baptist Church installation (W10) isolated words in white vinyl lettering flow across the interior walls, and the viewer must walk around and read the space, moving through the words to get at them from inside. The watchable behaviour is slow, single-word reading: the eye lands on one word, holds it, and only then moves to the next, and the meaning is the word itself, not a sentence it belongs to. Seen in W16, W17, W20 and W10.

**Takeaway:** give the viewer one word at a time and let the word be itself. [C28699]

## M7 -- The timestamped thought: a sentence naming what is not being thought

*All the things I know but of which I am not at the moment thinking -- 1:36 PM; June 15, 1969* (W16) is a single sentence that names, at a precise timestamp, the entire contents of the artist's mind that he is not currently thinking about. The timestamp is part of the work -- it testifies to the statement's existence and fixes a thought that by definition cannot be exhibited. The viewer reads the sentence and is forced to imagine an unknowable, ever-shifting body of knowledge; the work exists only through its formulation. Later materialisations (vinyl wall letters, the book) change nothing about the mechanism -- the words do the work, the support is incidental. The watchable behaviour is reading a sentence that points at everything it does not say, and feeling the size of what is left out. Seen in W16.

**Takeaway:** timestamp the thought you are not thinking; the date is the work. [C28700]

## M8 -- The blank film and the dark between slides: waiting is the work

*Scenes* (W06) is almost entirely blank leader -- empty film running through the projector -- punctuated only by an occasional image from Belmont, the 1967 Whitney residency farm. The viewer watches near-nothing for minutes at a time, hearing the soft whir of the spinning reel; the film draws attention to itself as it cycles through the projector in its own real time. In *Belmont 1967* (W19) colour photos of daily life at the artist colony are intercut with single typewritten words placed centre screen -- "hesitate", "aspire", "summon", "realm" -- and the circular crop of the projection and the duration of darkness between successive slides make time itself the subject: finite, measurable increments against the perpetual rotation of the carousel, which figures time as interminable. The viewer waits in the dark between images; the darkness is part of the work. The watchable behaviour is waiting -- for the next image, for the next word -- and noticing that the waiting is what the piece is made of. Seen in W05, W06 and W19.

**Takeaway:** run the empty film and let the waiting be the work. [C28701]

## M9 -- The line across the gap: the interval is the sculpture

In *Green Line* (W03) three canvases hang separated by four inches, and a single thin green line runs horizontally from inside the first panel, through the blank middle panel, to within an inch of the third canvas's right edge. The line continues across the wall gap, so the wall itself becomes part of the painting and the three panels read as one continuous work; the viewer must follow the line across empty space -- the gap is doing the work. The nylon thread installations (W07) push the same logic past the canvas entirely: virtually invisible threads stretched between buildings connect two houses with a line the eye cannot see and a photograph cannot record, so the viewer stands in a space that is physically altered but perceives nothing, and the "sculpture" is the interval between buildings. The watchable behaviour is following a line across a space that is not part of the work, and finding that the space is the work. Seen in W03 and W07.

**Takeaway:** draw the line across the gap and let the interval be the sculpture. [C28702]

## M10 -- Appropriation as designation: a borrowed sentence reframes the room

*Marcus Piece* (W18) quotes Herbert Marcuse -- "A place to which we can come and for a while 'be free to think about what we are going to do'" -- and Barry, in his words, "plagiarized his idea and made art from it". The piece literally designates spaces, mental and physical, where viewers are free to think about what they are going to do. The mechanism is appropriation-as-designation: a borrowed sentence re-frames whatever room it is placed in as a place for free thought. No installation details, dimensions or exact wording of the exhibited form were found in the sources read, so the mechanism is carried on the lecture account alone -- the sentence does the designating, and the room does the rest. The watchable behaviour is entering a room and finding it has been declared a place for thinking; the declaration changes the room without changing anything in it. Seen in W18.

**Takeaway:** borrow a sentence and let it declare the room. [C28703]

## Aesthetic logic

Barry's logic is subtraction carried to the point where the work is no longer a thing at all, and then the careful return of one thing -- the word -- as the only material left that can still be shown. The early paintings are already systems, not pictures: grids, repeated units, a line that crosses the wall between panels. In 1968-69 the system leaves the canvas and becomes a condition -- a field of radio waves, a volume of gas expanding forever, a half-life ticking in a park, a thought that may or may not arrive, a gallery that will not open -- and the viewer is asked to believe in it, to take it on trust from a label, a postcard, a catalogue entry. Against Weiner's sentence, which is a specification the world can build, Barry's word refuses to specify anything: it does not tell you what to do, it does not name a material, it does not describe an action -- it stands alone, clockwise around a room, and asks to be read the way a child reads, one word at a time, with the meaning arriving in the pause after the word rather than in the word itself. What changes across sixty years is not the question but the support it is asked on: in the 1960s the work asks it of a viewer standing in an empty gallery with a radio; in the 1970s of a reader turning near-blank pages; from the 1980s on of a walker moving through words pasted across a church, a staircase, a ceiling. The work is always the interval -- between the statement and the mind that receives it, between the buildings the thread connects, between the slides in the dark, between one word and the next -- and the viewer supplies what cannot be shown: the gas, the wave, the thought, the word's meaning.
