# Fred Penelle: Study Notes

**Site:** Fred Penelle (d. March 2020, Saint-Gilles, Belgium; birth year disputed -- 1973 per French Wikipedia, La Cambre and Artsper, 1976 per his own project's site and the 2022 monograph; see C3803). Belgian engraver and visual artist who took the woodcut off the sheet of paper and onto walls, into rooms and onto facades. Trained at La Cambre, Brussels, 1994-1999, under Gabriel Belgeonne; teacher of engraving at La Cambre 2012-2019 and at the Ecole des Arts d'Ixelles from 2015. His solo practice was a growing archive of reusable woodcut matrices -- a dictionary of generic images, cut out, enlarged, pasted and recombined, never editioned. His public practice was Mecaniques Discursives (2011-2020) with the video artist Yannick Jacquet of AntiVJ: Penelle's black-and-white woodcut fields on the wall, Jacquet's mapped projections animating them, 70+ exhibitions, from a cardboard box of pre-cut prints and living-room projectors at the Mapping Festival in Geneva in 2012 to a 700 sq m hall at LABoral in Gijon in 2021, completed after his death as a tribute.

## The life: from the paper sheet to the wall

Nothing is known from the sources read about Penelle's life before 1994 -- no childhood, no family background, no schooling before La Cambre. The record starts when he enters the Gravure et image imprimee (Engraving and printed image) workshop at La Cambre in Brussels in 1994, under Gabriel Belgeonne, learning the printmaking techniques and specialising in woodcut and lithography, graduating in 1999 (C3704). The 1994 start, the teacher's name and the 1999 diploma year rest on French Wikipedia alone; the monograph corroborates only the graduation. Belgeonne matters beyond the biography: he co-founded the Centre de la Gravure et de l'Image Imprimee at La Louviere, the institution that would host Penelle's late solo exhibition Chroniques in 2018-2019, and Penelle later taught in the same workshop he trained in (C3812).

Recognition came fast. He won the Prix de la Gravure of the Centre de la Gravure in 2005, first prize at ArtContest in 2007, the Prix Paul Artot of the Academie Royale de Belgique in 2008, the Prix Mediatine in 2009, the Art Collector's Prize at Slick Brussels in 2013, and the Milano Design Week Tech Award in 2014 (C3705, C3706). The full prize list is single-sourced to French Wikipedia; the 2005 prize, the ArtContest prize, the Slick prize and the Milano award are corroborated by the monograph dossier. Alongside the art he worked a string of hands-on trades -- workshop leader, framer, graphic designer, illustrator, model-maker, exhibition manager, scenographer, welder (C3708, single-sourced). The welding is not incidental biography: the Mecaniques Discursives installations stand on welded metal armatures, and the monograph describes them dressed in cotton thread, cardboard and welded iron structures. He taught engraving at La Cambre from 2012 to 2019 and at the Ecole des Arts d'Ixelles from 2015; La Cambre's obituary calls him "an exceptionally appreciated teacher" at Ixelles (C3709).

His birth year is the study's first honest gap. French Wikipedia gives 15 March 1973, Etterbeek; La Cambre's obituary, published 31 March 2020, says "Ne en 1973"; Artsper also lists 1973. The official Mecaniques Discursives site gives 1976-2020, and the 2022 monograph dossier gives Fred Penelle (1976-2020) in its artist biographies (C3701, C3803). The 1973 date has the institutional obituary and the encyclopedia behind it; the 1976 date has the artist's own project site and monograph. Neither side can be dismissed from the sources read, and the RTBF obituary that might settle it was not opened. This study uses neither year as settled in its header and records the conflict here. His death is March 2020 in Saint-Gilles -- the exact day is also disputed, 24 March per French Wikipedia, 25 March per La Cambre -- and no source read states a cause of death; none is given here (C3702).

The solo practice, 2006-2011, is where the mechanism is born. The first listed solo show is Eleve a la farine de mouton at the Galerie du Botanique, Brussels, in 2006 (W01). By 2011, with Creatures obliques at Modulab in Metz and Cameleon C. in Brussels (W03), the vocabulary is set: a hybrid bestiary of animals, chimeras, machines and ordinary people. But the decisive move is not the imagery, it is the support. Penelle cuts his woodcuts out, glues them, enlarges them, stages them -- diverting everyday objects, building three-dimensional installations and murals -- freeing engraving from the sheet of paper and using it as one element of mixed technique (C3714). The images are never final; they circulate, mutate and reappear in new configurations. He called the small framed images "my base, my vocabulary, my dictionary" -- images reused constantly, modified, inserted into different collages; matrices reused, so there was an effective reproducibility, but not for the purpose of edition printing. He disliked printing in multiple copies; playing and composing always with the same images was his way of playing with the multiple. He refused to give keys to decode his engravings, saying it would not be funny and would reduce the field of interpretations he needed to feel unlimited (C3714).

In 2008 he joined the Brussels engraver Bruno Hellenbosch to form The Two Jimies (W02): frescoes made directly in front of an audience from collages of enlarged woodcuts plus painting and drawing, an ephemeral work at each performance -- White Hotel and Nuit Blanche in Brussels in 2008, Slick Dessin in Paris in 2009, Jubilation Heroique at the Centre Wallonie-Bruxelles in Paris in 2012 (C3707). The live, public, ephemeral fresco is the direct bridge between his solo wall practice and the in-situ logic of Mecaniques Discursives: the image is made in the room, for the room, and does not survive it.

He met Yannick Jacquet in Brussels in 2008; the Mecaniques Discursives adventure began in 2011 (C3711). Jacquet, born Geneva 1980, French-Swiss video artist living in Brussels since 2005, had co-founded the international visual label AntiVJ in 2007 (C3710). Penelle was not an AntiVJ founder; the label connection runs through Jacquet. The first experiments were deliberately modest: a selection of printed engravings pre-cut in a cardboard box, small living-room video projectors, a few nails, a hammer and a computer were enough to deploy in space (C3712). The first showing was the Mapping Festival in Geneva on 12 May 2012 (W04). From there the project ran 2011-2020, 70+ exhibitions, each a different scenography of engravings, metal armatures, video projections and a sound environment, mostly created in situ, adapting to the sites, walls and spaces that hosted them and turning technical constraints into a creative force (C3713, C3717). The project's own framing: a parenthesis between two epochs, Gutenberg's and Big Data's -- by contrasting the oldest form of image reproduction (woodcut) with the most recent digital technologies, the installation straddles centuries and contracts time.

The work continued after his death, carried by Jacquet (C3720). The CFC book Mecaniques discursives appeared in 2021 (144 pages, with 8 augmented-reality creations via a free app). The monographic touring exhibition "Ne suivez pas le guide !" ("Don't Follow The Guide!") opened at LaVallee, Brussels, in 2021, gathering about a dozen works from more than 70 creations, and has toured since. The LABoral installation in Gijon, shown 23 July 2021 to 23 January 2022, is the largest indoor configuration of the project to date and is described by the project site as the pair's last joint work and a tribute. In Brussels, the City of Brussels and Beliris commissioned a large-scale semi-permanent version for 21 Boulevard Anspach (2021-2026); an enlarged permanent version was unveiled in 2024, described as the first augmented work integrated into Brussels public space -- that last claim is single-sourced to French Wikipedia. Most recently, Sarabande en Temps de Crise, a performative extension of a line of research begun in 2019, was filmed at La Halle aux draps, Tournai, in October 2025. The arc is closed: Penelle died in 2020; everything after that date is Jacquet carrying the shared vocabulary forward, not new work by Penelle.

## M1 -- The dictionary: a reusable archive of matrices

The founding mechanism, and the one everything else is a variation on. Penelle kept a growing archive of woodcut matrices -- a dictionary of generic images -- and continuously recomposed them, assembling fragments into dense, unstable constellations (C3714, C3752). Nothing is a fixed composition: the same moth-woman, the same swordsman, the same hare and tank reappear in Geneva in 2012, in Valence in 2014, in Paris in 2015, in Thionville in 2016, in Gijon in 2021, each time in a different arrangement, at a different scale, against a different wall. The image is not printed; it is dealt, like a hand of cards, and the hand changes with the room. What makes it Penelle and not a collage is the discipline: the vocabulary is small and stable, the recombination is total, and the hand that cuts the block is the same hand that pastes the enlargement on the wall. The archive is the work; any single hang is one throw of it.

**Takeaway:** build the vocabulary first and the composition second. A fixed set of 20-30 cut figures, recombined by the room, beats a new drawing for every wall.

## M2 -- The chain reaction: wiring the wall into a machine

The second mechanism is what the figures are wired into. On the wall the figures are linked by pipes, cables, dotted lines, arrows, numbers and percentages -- the wall as an absurd machine diagram (C3808). The project's own summary: "a mixture of engraving and video projection representing a sort of machinery of the absurd and the poetic, unfolding across walls like some exquisite skeleton using the principle of a chain reaction as its starting point." In the inspected Autoportrait (Option 1) of 2014 (W06), a goat head in a glowing circle, a bearded face, a bird, a cuckoo clock with a speaker, a skull and a glass globe are wired together by pipes, cables and dotted lines and projected light circles. At Centquatre in 2015-2016 (W09), the wiring becomes data: projected "8%" and "15%" rings, hex codes ("0x5A786E6C"), dotted network lines linking framed animal thumbnails. The wiring is the plot: follow a pipe from a smoking funnel to a skeleton and you have read the machine. Nothing is labelled, nothing is explained -- Penelle refused decoding keys -- so the wiring reads as causality without a cause, a diagram of a machine that does nothing except connect.

**Takeaway:** the line between two figures is the work. Draw the connection first -- pipe, dotted line, percentage -- and let the figures hang off it.

## M3 -- The bestiary at every scale

The third mechanism is scale. The same black-and-white woodcut figures are pasted at wildly different scales on the same wall: a moth-woman in a long gown ten feet tall beside a beetle the size of a coin; a pig-faced smoker in a triangle-pattern dress inside a light circle; a swordsman, a hare, tanks, hooded children, checkered trees, a bird on a molecule-like node diagram (W06, W07, W12). Real objects are mounted among the prints -- a cuckoo clock, a speaker, a wooden box, a black box with a smoking funnel -- so the wall is half image, half shelf. The scale jump is what makes the wall feel inhabited rather than decorated: the figures are not illustrations at a uniform size, they are presences, some looming, some hiding, and the visitor's body is the measuring stick. At LABoral in 2021 (W18) the inspected image shows a giant owl-headed suited man reaching toward a lightning bolt, a bird-headed woman in a striped dress, a headless man in a black suit, a haloed sheep, rows of hooded children -- and two visitors, small, for scale.

**Takeaway:** one figure, five sizes, one wall. Scale is the casting decision: who looms, who hides, who is only found by standing close.

## M4 -- Light as the second ink

The fourth mechanism is Jacquet's: projected light as the animating layer over the static print. The early setup was three projectors mapping short videos onto Penelle's wall objects; at LABoral it was 15+ simultaneous projections (C3808, C3810). What the light does, in the inspected images, is specific: glowing circles and sunbursts behind heads, data readouts (percentages, hex codes, dotted networks), lamp cones falling on a cut-out bird, fireworks in a corner, Tetris-like blocks sliding across a wall. The print is black and white and still; the light is warm, coloured and moving, and it is the light that tells you where to look next. In Montreal in 2016 (W11) the light layer took its furthest step: Jacquet's first digital 3D optical illusions inspired by Renaissance techniques -- trompe-l'oeil arched windows and rooms projected onto a flat wall, a standing suited man inside a painted arch, a landscape with tanks inside another, a grid of six small lit box-rooms each holding a tiny scene. The wall stays flat; the light builds rooms on it.

**Takeaway:** the print is the stage, the projection is the actor. Keep the print still and monochrome, and let a second, moving, coloured layer do all the changing.

## M5 -- The visitor inside the machine

The fifth mechanism is the visitor's position. The work is not looked at; it is entered. At Centquatre in 2015-2016 (W09) the installation first unfolded as a complete spatial environment extending beyond the wall into the room -- floor pieces, freestanding boxes, cut-out figures standing in space. At LABoral (W18) the whole 700 sq m hall, 12 m high, becomes the stage: animated and inert beings spread over floor and walls, connected by narrative lines and light signals, and "the visitor walks inside it and becomes another element" (C3810). The inspected LABoral image shows visitors standing among the figures at the figures' scale, not in front of them. The machine of M2 is not a picture of a machine; it is a room you are inside, and your body completes a circuit between two wired figures just by walking between them.

**Takeaway:** put the viewer in the diagram, not in front of it. The piece is a room with a path through it; the path is the reading order.

## M6 -- Press-size squares: the modular print

The sixth mechanism is Penelle's late return to pure print, and it is the most directly generative of his solo devices. For La peur du vide (The Fear of the Void, 2018, W13), the three large woodcuts were built from smaller squares matched to his press size, each square a complete image in itself, combinable in any other arrangement -- "mobile, permutable platforms" rather than a fixed image (C3765). For the greys -- a break from his stark black and white -- he pressed fabrics, lace and construction materials directly onto the carved block, giving weave effects and vibratory, random sensations. The described content: bewitched limbs floating, a body thrown backwards, a flowering grimacing head, eyes and skulls drifting in a nocturnal deluge. The square is the unit and the wall is the shuffle: the same squares can be recomposed by mood, by moment, by venue. It is the dictionary of M1 reduced to its smallest honest unit -- the press, not the wall, sets the tile size, and the tile is complete alone.

**Takeaway:** make the tile the size of the tool, and make each tile complete. A grid of complete tiles can be shuffled forever; a fragment of a fixed image cannot.

## M7 -- The live fresco: made in front of an audience

The seventh mechanism predates Jacquet and explains the in-situ logic of everything after it. With The Two Jimies (2008-2013, W02), Penelle and Bruno Hellenbosch made frescoes directly in front of an audience from collages of enlarged woodcuts plus painting and drawing, an ephemeral work at each performance (C3707, C3813). The fresco is built live, watched, and then it is gone -- the next performance makes a different one from the same vocabulary. This is the dictionary of M1 performed as theatre: the recombination is the event, the audience watches the archive get dealt, and the ephemerality is the point, not a loss. It is also the most portable of Penelle's mechanisms for a generative piece, because it needs no projector and no wall: a vocabulary, a surface, a hand, and witnesses.

**Takeaway:** build the image in public, once, and let it vanish. The performance is the recombining; the wall is only where it happens to land.

## M8 -- The thinning wall: horror vacui inverted

The eighth mechanism is the late turn, and it is a reversal. The early MD walls are horror vacui -- every inch crowded, the swarm at maximum density. The late wall pieces, Tout etait grave and Horror Vacui (2019, W15), thin out: the inspected image shows two long white walls meeting at a corner, sparsely populated -- a bare tree with an owl, a horse-head flag-bearer in a light circle, small figures, projected numbers -- light and emptiness doing more work than density. The same Modena hang shows the darker pole beside it: the Sarabande image, a black stage with rows of small hooded children, checkered trees, giant headless figures with glowing geometric heads (a ring reading "88%", a triangle), a skeleton and a skull. The vocabulary did not change; the density did. Penelle's own late summary of the format, from the Chroniques visit in 2018: "My format, here, is that of the wall, I can cover everything, at the same time I try to come out of the wall, not to stay stuck to the surface" (C3715). The wall is covered, then it is left mostly empty, and the empty wall is still the format.

**Takeaway:** density is a dial, not a style. The same vocabulary at 10% coverage is a different piece from the same vocabulary at 95% -- and the empty wall is doing the work, not failing at it.

## The aesthetic logic: Gutenberg against Big Data

Penelle's logic is a collision of two reproduction technologies, and the artists said so themselves: a parenthesis between two epochs, Gutenberg's and Big Data's (C3808). The woodcut is the oldest form of image reproduction; the mapped projection is among the newest. The installation straddles centuries and contracts time -- not by illustrating the contrast, but by making the two technologies do one job together: the press supplies the still, black-and-white, hand-cut field; the projector supplies the moving, coloured, data-bearing layer; the wall supplies the scale; the visitor supplies the body that walks between them. Nothing in the work is new in itself -- the bestiary is borrowed from art history, daily life and memory, unified only because it passed through his hand (C3714) -- and nothing is explained, because explanation would shrink the field of interpretations he needed to feel unlimited. The machine is absurd on purpose: Bruno Munari's 1937 line, quoted as the project's epigraph, says a useless machine that does not represent anything is the perfect device through which to revive an imagination afflicted daily by useful machines (C3808). The critic Francois Delvoye's summary, quoted in the AntiVJ press text, is the sharpest: "Frederic Penelle's ferocious woodcuts dialogue with the sharp video art created by Yannick Jacquet -- or perhaps it's the other way around -- to recount, carefully and amusingly, the incontrovertible evidence of the meaninglessness of the world" (C3809). The meaninglessness is the point, and it is recounted carefully and amusingly, with pipes and percentages.

The evolution runs in one clean arc: print, then cut-out fresco, then animated wall system, then spatial environment, then facade and public monument, then posthumous repertory (leg 2). The early solo work is paper-bound, then breaks the frame; the middle MD years turn the image into a system that recombines per venue and grows spatially -- single wall (LKFF 2013), multi-room (LUX 2014-2015), complete environment (Centquatre 2015-2016), outdoors (Bordeaux 2016), curved architecture (Thionville 2016), trompe-l'oeil rooms (Montreal 2016); the recent years distil the system into portable formats (the framed tableaux, the PLAY box-dioramas, the Retable triptych) while scaling it up to its largest forms (the 50 m Geneva facade of December 2019, the 700 sq m LABoral room of 2021, the permanent Brussels mural). Colour is essentially absent throughout -- black-and-white woodcut against white and grey walls, the only exception seen being the lime-green Chroniques invitation art -- and the projected light carries whatever colour and motion the piece has.
