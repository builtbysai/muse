# Jacopo Ligozzi: Study Notes

**Site:** Jacopo Ligozzi (probably 1547, Verona - buried Friday 26 March 1627, Florence), Veronese painter, illustrator, designer and miniaturist; Medici court artist from Saturday 1 January 1577, when he left Verona for the service of Grand Duke Francesco I, and the first name of the National Gallery of Art's 2002 trio Ligozzi-Garzoni-Bimbi. His most acclaimed work was naturalistic illustration: 78 tempera plants and 65 tempera animals in the Gabinetto Disegni e Stampe degli Uffizi, most of them life-size, made in his first ten Florentine years for Francesco I, who directed the vast encyclopedic cataloguing of nature of which Ligozzi was the principal painter. Ulisse Aldrovandi, the naturalist who commissioned sheets from him for his own catalogue, called him another Apelles and said his plants and animals lacked nothing but a spirit. After Francesco died in October 1587 the botanical programme stopped, the salary and the Casino di San Marco apartment went, and Ligozzi became a Counter-Reformation altarpiece painter, signing the 1592 slate history for the Salone dei Cinquecento "miniator". He served four grand dukes -- Francesco I, Ferdinando I, Cosimo II, Ferdinando II -- painted the Ognissanti St Francis lunettes (begun 1600, left unfinished), the Marmot of 1605, the portable altar of 1608, the La Verna guidebook of 1612, and the four dark Passion canvases of 1621-22, his last Medici commission. The record is strong where Bimbi's was thin in one way and thin where his was strong in another: a full Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani entry (Luca Bortolotti, vol. 65, 2005, read in full, 226 lines), the 2014 monographic shows "pittore universalissimo" (Palazzo Pitti) and "altro apelle" (GDSU), the National Gallery of Art's 2002 "The Flowering of Florence", and a 2018 University of Washington MA thesis on the Daphne laureola read in part (887 of 3,096 lines). ## M1. The Whole-Plant Portrait, Roots and All

The plant is shown entire -- root system, stem, leaves, bud, flower, dying petal -- in one sheet, centred on a blank ground. The working method is documented in the unfinished plates: a light black-chalk outline first, then a first lay-in of colour, patiently glazed and finished in tempera with very fine brushes to get the most refined colour variations, finally fixed with a glossy varnish, perhaps egg white. Hirschauer: "many of his plants are shown with their intricate root systems intact, and he includes newly formed buds as well as dying petals and leaves, not just flowers in full bloom." The Daphne laureola (inv. 1955 O, 67 x 46 cm) is the type: the exposed root clump centred at the foot of the sheet, a tortoiseshell butterfly at left, three flies at right, chewed leaf edges, lit from the left. The Mandrake is painted with scientific, almost tactile clarity, abandoning the anthropomorphic tradition that drew mandrakes as little men. Nothing is cropped, and nothing is tidied: the root is as drawn as the flower, the damage is part of the portrait. The watchable behaviour is a plant growing, flowering, wilting and re-rooting in a single frame; the root system is as drawn as the flower; nothing is cropped. **Takeaway:** the whole-plant portrait -- the specimen shown entire, roots and all, on a blank ground, damage included, the root as drawn as the flower.

## M2. The Specimen on the White Void

The subject is lifted out of its habitat and set on an undifferentiated blank ground -- no landscape, no shadow ground, sometimes no shadow at all. The Aldrovandi viper sheet adds shadow; the Uffizi version of the same vipers does not. The blue-and-yellow macaw (Psittacus Ararauna, c.1580-1600, c.67 x 45.6 cm) is the famous case: the bird on a blank white ground, no context, framed by an undifferentiated field of white. Hyperallergic calls the macaw the perfect capture, "as clear as when Columbus's sailors had first seen them off the American coast". The void is the claim: this is a specimen, collectable, measurable, categorizable. The eye has nowhere to go but the detail, and the blankness itself reads as a cage. The same habit carries into the religious painting: the saint, the decomposing head, the portable altar's Christ, all isolated on a void. The watchable behaviour is a creature or plant floating in white; the eye has nowhere to go but the detail; the blankness itself reads as a cage. **Takeaway:** the specimen on the white void -- the subject lifted out of its habitat onto blank ground, the void itself the claim that this is a specimen.

## M3. Miniature Precision at Any Scale

Detail is not a scale, it is an identity. The miniaturist's thin brush and finish are applied to every format: a 67 cm botanical plate, a copper portable altar 26.6 cm high, and the 1592 slate history painting for the Salone dei Cinquecento, signed "miniator". Tonelli: Ligozzi was "capable of using such thin brushes for his works that they acquired details fine enough to seem like miniatures." Sotheby's, on the Gran Serraglio sheet: "The subtlety and precision of the artist's work reflect his early training as a miniaturist, a skill for which he was admired by the Medici court, and which he himself clearly valued, as he continued to sign his works Jacopo Ligozzi miniator as late as 1592." He signed the 1592 slate painting "miniator" almost ironically, given the scale -- a deliberate self-definition, miniature finish claimed as identity at monumental scale. The watchable behaviour is zooming in never exhausting the image; a huge picture rewards close looking the way a miniature does; gold heightening catches light at small scale. **Takeaway:** miniature precision at any scale -- the miniaturist's hand and the "miniator" signature carried from a 67 cm sheet to a copper altar to monumental slate, detail as identity, not as size.

## M4. The Life-Cycle in One Image

Bud, bloom and decay are painted simultaneously on the same stem -- newly formed buds beside dying petals, the brown curl at a leaf tip, the pucker of rot in a fig. Time is collapsed into a single sheet rather than sequenced. The Fig Branch with Exotic Finches carries the brown leaf-tip curl and the puckered rot in the fig flesh beside the living finches; the Daphne carries chewed leaf edges beside fresh growth. The same hand extends the device from plant to man: the Vanitas -- a decomposing head painted with the same fidelity as a plant, the face becoming a skull in stages, eyes and skin still present, reflected in a mirror among jewellery, an hourglass and a debtors-and-creditors book (Natura Morta Macabra, c.1604, the versos of a pair of nuptial portraits). ArtTrav, on the macabre still life: "His is a true 'natura morta'!" -- dead nature, not still life. The decay thread runs the whole life: rotting fig flesh in the 1580s botanical sheets becomes the rotting head in 1604. The watchable behaviour is a flower opening and rotting without a cut; decay spreading visibly from a leaf tip while a bud opens elsewhere; a face becoming a skull in stages at the same fidelity as a leaf. **Takeaway:** the life-cycle in one image -- bud, bloom and decay painted simultaneously on the same stem, time collapsed into a single sheet, the same fidelity extended from plant to decomposing head.

## M5. Print-to-Costume Translation -- The Gran Serraglio

A second-hand source is redrawn with first-hand finish. The Gran Serraglio album, about thirty known pages (twenty in the Uffizi, ten elsewhere), painted probably 1580-1585 in the earliest Florentine years under Francesco I, is loosely based on the Venetian edition of Nicolo de' Nicolay's Le navigationi et viaggi fatti nella Turchia (Antwerp 1576, Venice 1580). Ligozzi never travelled to Turkey. The figures are redrawn in tempera and shell gold on paper, in elaborate costume, often paired with fantastic or symbolic animals -- a turbaned pasha with an elephant, an usher with parrots, a mufti and a monster, a Turkish man with a flamingo, a janissary and a bear, Sultan Selim II with a dragon -- sometimes heightened with gold. One sheet's inscription wrongly identifies the figure as "Solimano Imperator de Turchi". The patron is unknown; Lucilla Conigliello proposed the collector Niccolo Gaddi rather than a Medici. The image is then re-used as a model: the album passed from the Gaddi family to the Doccia porcelain factory around 1740, and Turkish subjects from it were used as models for Doccia porcelain decoration. The watchable behaviour is a figure assembling itself from print lines into coloured, gilded costume; the animal beside it is invented, not observed; the finished sheet becomes a porcelain pattern. **Takeaway:** print-to-costume translation -- a second-hand engraving redrawn with first-hand miniature finish, tempera and shell gold, the invented animal beside the costumed figure, the sheet then re-used as a porcelain pattern.

## M6. Nature Study Becomes Object Design

The same plant or animal study is re-deployed as a design source across media: pietre dure table tops (marble mosaic, "a painting made with precious stones"), embroidery, glass, jewellery, tapestry, metalwork, porcelain, a telescope for Galileo. As director of the grand-ducal Galleria dei Lavori he supplied designs for export embroidery and pietre dure, and his plant and animal studies served as designs for table tops in marble mosaic. A pietre dure table top in the Galleria Palatina, chalcedony inlaid with vases, grape clusters, sheaves of wheat, birds, insects and butterflies, is Ligozzi's design executed by the Opificio. The Gran Serraglio subjects became Doccia porcelain decoration; the portable altar (1608) sets a lapis-lazuli Sacrifice of Isaac in pietre dure below an oil-on-copper Agony, and paints its case doors with a garland of roses, tulips and lilies around the IHS monogram. The drawing is a pattern book for the grand-ducal workshops. Nothing in his oeuvre stays in its first medium. The watchable behaviour is a painted leaf hardening into coloured marble; a drawn flower repeating as embroidery, then as porcelain, then as stone. **Takeaway:** nature study becomes object design -- the same specimen re-deployed as pietre dure, embroidery, porcelain and telescope decoration, the drawing as a pattern book for the workshops.

## M7. The Combination Sheet -- More Than One Thing in One Object

He habitually puts two things in one object and lets them argue. The botanical sheets pair plant with animal: a plum branch with an Alexandrine parakeet (1577), a fig branch with male and female finches, a garden dormouse and a mole in showdown on one sheet, a marmot with a branch of plums (1605). The portable altar (1608) stacks typology: the Agony in the Garden in oil on copper above, the Sacrifice of Isaac in pietre dure on lapis lazuli below, the case doors painted with a flower garland around the IHS monogram -- three registers, one devotional object. The nuptial portraits (c.1604) are double-sided: bland portraits of a young man and a young woman on the recto, decomposing heads on the verso. The Ecce Homo and Christ Carrying the Cross tondo (1588) is painted on both sides of a parchment. The combination is not decoration; it is the argument: the living thing beside the dead thing, the type beside its fulfilment, the portrait beside its end. The watchable behaviour is the sheet turning over: the living side first, then the dead side; the plant, then the animal that eats it; the Agony above, the Isaac below, the garland around both. **Takeaway:** the combination sheet -- two things in one object, plant with animal, portrait with macabre verso, type with fulfilment, the turning-over as the argument.

## M8. The Serial Cycle -- Narrative in Sequence

Where Bimbi counted specimens, Ligozzi counted scenes. The Ognissanti cloister holds seventeen lunette frescoes of the life of St Francis, commissioned around 1599 and worked on 1599-1606, of which he finished two walls plus two lunettes before being replaced by Giovanni da San Giovanni. The Dante drawings of 1587-88 are five sheets that walk a reader through Hell and Purgatory: Dante in the Dark Forest, Dante and the Three Wild Beasts, Dante and Virgil about to take ship with Charon across the Acheron (dated 1588, inscribed "a Montui"), the Second Dream of Dante, Beatrice Appearing to Virgil. The Seven Deadly Sins drawings (1590) are six surviving allegories, four of them the Cardinal Sins reunited from Paris and Hanover for the 2014 show. The late Passion cycle (1621-22) is four small dark canvases: Agony in the Garden, Flagellation, Crowning with Thorns, Christ Carrying the Cross (dated 1622). The La Verna guidebook (1612) is twenty-two engraved views after his drawings, a mountain walked in sequence. Each cycle is the same mechanism: one hand, one subject, repeated until the repetition becomes the meaning. The watchable behaviour is the sequence advancing: lunette after lunette around a cloister, sin after sin, Passion scene after Passion scene, the same hand returning to the same subject until the cycle closes. **Takeaway:** the serial cycle -- seventeen lunettes, five Dante sheets, six Deadly Sins, four Passion canvases, twenty-two La Verna views, narrative as repetition until the repetition becomes the meaning.

## M9. The Macabre Verso -- Decay Painted Lush

The decomposing head is painted with the same fidelity, and the same lush colour, as the plant. The Natura Morta Macabra (c.1604) shows a decomposing female head with eyes and skin still present, reflected in a mirror, with jewellery, an hourglass and a debtors-and-creditors book -- on the back of a nuptial portrait. Tonelli: "one can almost smell the stench of rotting meat." The Allegory of Avarice (c.1590, 139.4 x 84.5 cm, Metropolitan Museum of Art) sets a pale woman holding a money bag against a skeleton, with the same woman falling before a swordsman in the background. The Allegory of Fortune (44 x 26.5 cm, Uffizi) balances Fortune on a globe, coins sliding into a vase and emerging as butterflies, while a winged Time or Death offers an hourglass and flowers. The macabre is not a separate genre for him; it is the botanical eye turned on the human body, the life-cycle mechanism (M4) with the plant replaced by a face. He was devout -- he read sacred meditations, attended the confraternity of Saint Paul, which met on Saturday nights in a room with two wooden skeletons as memento mori, and one son and two daughters took religious vows -- and the devotion and the decay are one practice. The watchable behaviour is the portrait turning over to its verso: the bland face first, then the rotting head behind it, painted just as carefully, the mirror showing what the sitter will become. **Takeaway:** the macabre verso -- the decomposing head painted with the botanical eye and the Veronese palette, the portrait turning over to its own end, decay as the devotional subject.

## M10. The Veronese Colour Transfer -- The Palette as Cargo

He carried a palette to Florence the way other men carry a trade. Oberlin: Ligozzi's paintings were "especially influential in transmitting the richer and more vibrantly colored Veronese style to Florentine painters active around 1600." The English Wikipedia credits him with "bringing the lusher color-palette of Verona to Florence." Sotheby's, on the Abduction of the Sabine Women (1596): his large-scale paintings show "a debt to Paolo Veronese's art" in their classical settings and mise-en-scene. The colour is the cargo: the lush, high-key Veronese palette set against Florentine academic sobriety, in the slate histories, the altarpieces, the Passion canvases -- "composition, colour, hats, armour, jewelled costumes and landscapes" are what Blue Guides singles out in the four Passion scenes. The mechanism is not that he painted like Veronese; it is that he moved a colour system from one city to another and planted it in a court that had been painting in a different key, and the court kept it: Bimbi's jewel-bright fruit, a generation later, is painted in the light Ligozzi brought. The watchable behaviour is the palette arriving: a Florentine picture in sober tones, and then the Veronese colour flooding in -- silvery whites, lush reds, jewelled costume -- until the whole court is painting brighter. **Takeaway:** the Veronese colour transfer -- the lush Veronese palette carried from Verona to Florence and planted in the Medici court, the colour itself the cargo, the court painting brighter afterwards.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Verona, Trent and Venice training, to c.1576. Born Verona, probably 1547, son of Giovanni Ermanno Ligozzi, painter and embroiderer, of a family of Milanese origin. His father moved to Trento in 1557 in the service of Cardinal Cristoforo Madruzzo; Jacopo's first documented works are Trentine: the Sant'Anna altarpiece, 1566, signed alone (S. Antonio Abate, Bivedo), and a Madonna with Child and Saints Silvester and Lawrence, 24 May 1567, signed jointly with his father (S. Silvestro, Vigo Lomaso). The DBI says his training was in his father's workshop, not with the great Veronese masters as older literature claimed. Recent scholarship puts the early career in Venice rather than Vienna, and the Habsburg-collection works were probably not acquired by direct patronage. Links already forming: the Verona apothecary Francesco Calzolari and the Tuscan-Venetian herbalist Leone Tartaglini; Calzolari's letter of 12 December 1571 recommending an unnamed painter to Aldrovandi -- "he finished my book to the amazement of everyone, and he made some fish and birds that surpass live ones" -- is proposed to be Ligozzi, not proven. He copied Durer's botany and zoology prints. What is constant from the start: small scale, miniaturist finish, work from a specimen or a print rather than from invention.

Phase 2 -- Medici court botanical period, 1577-1587. Arrival in Florence on 1 January 1577 at Francesco I's invitation, on Aldrovandi's recommendation; Aldrovandi visited Florence months after the arrival and then wrote requesting copies for woodblock publication. Studio in the Casino di San Marco, in front of the Giardino dei Semplici, next to Francesco's alchemical workshop. Monthly income 25 a month (ducati per the DBI, scudi per other sources), the second-highest paid court artist after Giambologna. Output: tempera-on-paper plant portraits at life scale on large sheets (about 67 x 46 cm), animals on the same sheets or alone, roots exposed, insects included and duly labelled, damage and decay shown -- chewed leaves, drying edges, fungal affliction. Subjects include New World arrivals (pineapple, agave, finches, macaw) from the Medici gardens and menagerie. Parallel work in the same years: the Turkish costume album (c.1580-1585, from Nicolay's book, not from travel), the Tribuna decoration (1584, lost), small Tribuna oils (1587), the Dante drawings (1587-88). The botanical sheets were collectable objects as well as science: Schoening's argument is that their desirability as miniatures helped win court support for natural history. Aldrovandi called him the new Apelles and compared him to Parrhasius, Titian, Raphael and Michelangelo.

Phase 3 -- Court painter under Ferdinando I, 1587-1599. Francesco I dies in October 1587; Ferdinando I dismantles the laboratories, moves Ligozzi to Via Larga and a Uffizi corridor workshop, and stops the botanical programme -- after Francesco's death Ligozzi produced few botanical works. Court painter from 1587. Scale jumps: the two slate histories for the Salone dei Cinquecento (1591-92) are his first monumental public commissions. The moral allegories arrive at the same moment: the Seven Deadly Sins drawings (1590), the Allegory of Avarice, the Allegory of Fortune, the Allegory of the Redemption. Private devotional painting grows (the Ecce Homo / Christ Carrying the Cross tondo, 1588). The San Giovannino degli Scolopi oils (1593, c.1593) are the first mature altarpieces. He signs "Jacopo Ligozzi miniator" as late as 1592 -- the miniaturist title carried into large painting. Travel: brief return to Verona around 1591; Verona and Mantua 1593; Gonzaga commissions 1593-1602, relations breaking down in 1602. Dismissal by the court follows an accusation by a valet, and court activity is suspended around 1600-1620.

Phase 4 -- Private bottega and Counter-Reformation religious painting, c.1600-1620. He opens a private workshop; patrons become churches and confraternities rather than the duke. The Ognissanti St Francis lunettes (17, 1599-1606) dominate the turn of the century; the 1607 La Verna visit produces the 1612 Descrizione guidebook. Large altarpieces across Tuscany: Santa Maria Novella, San Marco, San Giovannino degli Scolopi, Santissima Annunziata, Santa Croce (St Lawrence, 1611), San Martino in Pisa (Magdalen, 1607, over 3 x 2 m), Santo Stefano dei Cavalieri in Pisa (the Lepanto ceiling), Pescia (Dorothea), Bibbiena, Poppi, Arezzo, Lucca, San Gimignano, Monte Oliveto Maggiore. Small devotional objects continue in parallel: the Oberlin portable altar (1608), the Marmot (1605) -- the nature-study hand is still active a decade after the botanical programme ended. The macabre turn intensifies: the Natura Morta Macabra versos (c.1604), the Vanitas, the decomposing head rather than the clean skull. Tonelli ties this to confraternity membership (St Paul, meeting Saturday nights in a room with two wooden skeletons as memento mori), heavy religious reading, and a family in religion. The altarpiece manner itself is conventional -- staged crowds, academic -- and the originality sits in the detail and the devotional objects, not the big compositions.

Phase 5 -- Late return to court and the Passion, 1621-1627. Re-engaged as court artist in the early 1620s on terms tied to draughtsmanship: the Medici document quoted by Ongpin says "have him do drawings to keep him in the gallery... just have him draw, leave it up to him, whatever he does he will produce sheets worth keeping". Last Medici commission: four Passion paintings, 1621-1622, for Maria Maddalena of Austria (Toledo says for Christina of Lorraine -- the patron is disputed, see C9). The 1622 Christ Carrying the Cross (Galleria Palatina) is the last dated painting found. Late in 1620 Cosimo II granted him a pension of 15 a month for three years on his petition, confirmed until his death. He dies in Florence in 1627 and is buried on 26 March in the church of San Marco. Late work is small, devotional and drawn rather than monumental.

Phase 6 -- Death and afterlife, 1627 onward. In the 17th to 19th centuries his reputation narrows to the botanical sheets. The Turkish album is dispersed (Gaddi to Doccia porcelain around 1740; the Uffizi group gifted in 1867 by Niccolo Bazzani). The Locko Park pictures are catalogued as Beccafumi as late as 1901 before reattribution to Ligozzi. The Ognissanti lunettes stay in place; the altarpieces stay in their churches. Twentieth-century scholarship: Mina Bacci, "Jacopo Ligozzi e la sua posizione nella pittura fiorentina", Proporzioni IV (1963) -- the foundation biography and attribution study; Bacci and Forlani Tempesti, Mostra di disegni di Jacopo Ligozzi, GDSU, Florence, 1961; Lucia Tongiorgi Tomasi and Sara Ferri, I ritratti di piante di Jacopo Ligozzi, Pisa, 1993; Lucilla Conigliello, Ligozzi, Louvre exhibition catalogue, Paris, 2005. The Flowering of Florence: Botanical Art for the Medici, National Gallery of Art, Washington, 3 March - 27 May 2002 -- 68 works, three artists, seventeen Ligozzi works displayed, "the largest number ever seen outside of Italy". Jacopo Ligozzi, "pittore universalissimo", Galleria Palatina, Palazzo Pitti, Florence, 27 May - 28 September 2014, with the GDSU -- the first monographic show attempting the whole career, over 100 works -- with the companion botanical volume "altro apelle" (44 botanical plates). A dedicated volume of Mitteilungen des Kunsthistorischen Institutes in Florenz, "Jacopo Ligozzi 2015", followed.

What changed: patron (Medici duke to churches and confraternities to Medici regent again); scale (67 x 46 cm sheets to 3 x 2 m altarpieces and back to small devotional copper); subject (specimen to history to sin and death to Passion); the botanical programme stops dead in 1587 with Francesco's death and never resumes at the same intensity. What stayed the same: the miniaturist hand -- thin brushes, hair-by-hair and leaf-by-leaf finish, gold heightening, at every scale and in every genre; the blank-ground specimen habit (plant, animal, saint, or decomposing head isolated on a void); the combination sheet (plant plus animal, portrait plus macabre verso, Agony plus Isaac typology); the Veronese colour (lush, high-key, against Florentine academic sobriety); the habit of signing as miniator. The decay thread runs the whole life: rotting fig flesh in the 1580s botanical sheets becomes the rotting head in 1604.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is that wonder and measurement are one practice, not two careers. The same hand that painted the Tribuna naturalia and the Grotto of Thetis also painted the Gran Serraglio costumes from prints and the Vanitas skulls. Aldrovandi's verdict -- another Apelles, his plants and animals lacking nothing but a spirit -- is the contemporary certificate: a naturalist, not a painter, certified the fidelity, and the fidelity is what made the sheets collectable as miniatures, which is what won court support for natural history. Hyperallergic: "Jacopo Ligozzi gave visual expression to the developing myth of empirical objectivity, of being able to successfully collect, measure, analyze, and categorize." The plant portrait with its roots, the animal on the white void, the insect duly labelled, the chewed leaf edge -- all of it says the picture can be checked against the thing, the way Bimbi's life-size fruit could be checked, a generation later, at wall-filling scale. But the same eye turns on the human body and finds the same truth: the decomposing head is painted with the same fidelity as the plant, and the life-cycle in one image -- bud, bloom and decay -- is the whole of his theology. The Veronese colour is the other half of the logic: the lush palette carried to Florence is what lets the measurement sing, and it is what the court kept. The transfer between registers is the deepest mechanism of all: a specimen becomes a picture, a picture becomes a porcelain pattern, a pattern becomes a stone mosaic, a natural study becomes a Vanitas, a portrait becomes its own verso. Nothing stays in its first medium, and nothing stays in its first meaning. The NGA trio is a curatorial construction of 2002, not a historical school -- Ligozzi (died 1627), Garzoni (1600-1670) and Bimbi (1648-1729) never overlapped as working contemporaries -- but the line is real as a tradition of Medici botanical documentation, not as a master-pupil chain: Ligozzi documented the gardens, Garzoni painted them at intimate scale in the scientific tradition of Ligozzi, Bimbi catalogued them at wall-filling scale with numbers and inscriptions, and the line runs through the miniaturist's hand from first to last.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Veronese by birth and training (father's workshop, Trento 1557-1570s), Florentine by career (1 January 1577 to death 1627). The move to Florence, not a Veronese apprenticeship, is the hinge of his life. Evidence: leg 1.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] The natural-history decade 1577-1587 is the core of his reputation: 78 plant + 65 animal tempera plates in the Uffizi GDS, made for Francesco I, in a miniaturist's tempera technique (black chalk, glaze, varnish). Evidence: leg 1; leg 2.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] Aldrovandi's "another Apelles" is the contemporary verdict that matters: a naturalist, not a painter, certified the fidelity. The scientific-illustration root of the whole Medici botanical line runs through him. Evidence: leg 1; leg 3.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] He is the hinge between wonder and measurement: the same hand that painted the Tribuna naturalia and the Grotto of Thetis also painted the Gran Serraglio costumes from prints and the Vanitas skulls. Court splendour and empirical record are one practice, not two careers. Evidence: leg 1.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] The 1587 break is structural, not anecdotal: Francesco I dies, the salary, the Casino apartment and the workshop go, and the altarpiece career begins. His second career (1593-1623) is a different artist -- Counter-Reformation devotional painter -- using the same hand. Evidence: leg 1.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The "miniator" signature on the 1592 slate history painting (Bonifacio VIII riceve gli ambasciatori fiorentini) is a deliberate self-definition: miniature finish claimed as identity at monumental scale. Evidence: leg 1.
- P7. [REPORTED] The Veronese colour thesis: he transmitted a richer, more vibrantly coloured Veronese style to Florentine painters around 1600 (Oberlin); the English Wikipedia credits him with bringing the lusher Veronese palette to Florence. Reported by two museum sources; no dissenting source found in any leg. Evidence: leg 1.

Reception position (for the record, not counted above): high in his lifetime -- Baldinucci called him "pittore universalissimo", Aldrovandi called him the new Apelles and compared him to Parrhasius, Titian, Raphael and Michelangelo, his monthly 25 made him the second-highest paid court artist after Giambologna, and his drawings were sought after as finished works in their own right. Then the reputation narrows for three centuries to the botanical sheets, the Locko Park pictures are catalogued as Beccafumi as late as 1901, and only in recent decades has the oeuvre been treated as a coherent whole -- Bacci 1963, the 1961 GDSU drawings show, the 2005 Louvre drawings show, the NGA 2002 exhibition, and the 2014 Pitti monographic show. The rediscovery made him the "Audubon of late Renaissance Florence" in the English Wikipedia's phrase -- a modern journalistic label, not a period title; the period titles are "pittore universalissimo" and "altro Apelle".

- C5. - C7. OGNISSANTI FRESCOES: "SEVENTEEN LUNETTES COMPLETED 1600" IS TOO NEAT. Ongpin and the English Wikipedia say seventeen lunette frescoes completed in 1600. Toledo says painted "between 1599 and 1606". The DBI says he began in 1600 and worked through much of the first decade, finishing two walls plus two lunettes before being replaced by Giovanni da San Giovanni. VERDICT: 17 lunettes is the cycle's scope; "completed 1600" is a simplification. Say "begun 1600, left unfinished, replaced by Giovanni da San Giovanni".
- C8. SALARY UNIT: DUCATI vs SCUDI. The DBI: 25 ducati a month. The Schoening thesis and PMC-adjacent sources: 25 scudi a month. VERDICT: the figure 25 is stable, the unit is not. This study says "25 a month" and notes the unit flag where the figure is cited.
