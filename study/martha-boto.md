# Martha Boto: Study Notes

**Site:** Martha Boto, 27 December 1925 - 13 October 2004 -- Argentine kinetic artist, born in Buenos Aires, living in Paris from 1959 until her death, 45 years; LACMA calls her "a major figure of the kinetic art movement" and "one of the only women to work in this vein" (C7604). She began with Plexiglas mobiles moved by air and water in Buenos Aires, put electric motors and lamps inside black boxes in Paris in the 1960s -- the kinetic-light boxes, helicoids, disk columns and programmed light sequences -- and then, in the early 1970s, left the machines behind and painted the same circles instead. She co-founded ANFA (Artistas No Figurativos Argentinos) with her husband and collaborator Gregorio Vardanega in 1956, and with him coined the word chromocinetisme for their joint 1964 show at the Maison des Beaux-Arts. She was never a member of GRAV: she exhibited in its orbit, at Denise Rene and in the Nouvelle Tendance shows, while the six GRAV men argued about anonymity. Her own words, from a 1970 Studio International statement: "I seek an art capable of arousing different emotions, psychological reactions of joy or tension, an art that can become medicine for the spirit. My particular means of movement, colour and light can give the illusion of contraction, diminishment or multiplication, so that by optical means the spectator undergoes a series of reactions" (C7604, C7608).

## The life: the woman who put motors inside the box

Martha Boto was born on 27 December 1925 in Buenos Aires, Argentina, and died on 13 October 2004 in Paris, aged 78 (C7601, C7602). She was drawn to art as a child, her family supporting her studies, and as a teenager she joined the first abstract art movements in Buenos Aires, including the Asociacion Arte Nuevo (C7603, C7606). In 1944 she entered the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Carcova in Buenos Aires, where she took classes in drawing and painting, and graduated in 1950 (C7603, C7601, C7605). The brief's school name is wrong: no source links her to the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon; the school named in the sources is Ernesto de la Carcova, and the Maldonado Collection's "Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes" is given without the Pueyrredon name (C7603, C7605). Her early paintings were figurative -- shown twice at the Galeria Van Riel in Buenos Aires, in 1952 and 1953 -- and in 1954 she began to experiment with geometric abstraction (C7605).

In 1956 she joined the Grupo Arte Nuevo, along with Gregorio Vardanega, Eduardo Jonquieres, Luis Tomasello and Carmelo Arden Quin, and co-founded the Agrupacion de Arte no Figurativo Argentino (ANFA) with Vardanega (C7605, C7603, C7604). The brief's expansion is wrong: ANFA was Argentine -- Artistas No Figurativos Argentinos, in English Argentine Non-Figurative Artists -- founded in Buenos Aires before the Paris move, not a French association (C7603, C7604, C7605). The date is contested: Sicardi, LACMA, Davis and Cheffins say co-founded in 1956, while Wikipedia, the Maldonado Collection and the Artsy / Christie's essay say she joined or started it in 1957; this study gives 1956 with the 1957 variant noted (C7603, C7604, C7606, C7611, C7601, C7605, C7608). She married Vardanega -- sources disagree on the year, 1957 implied by the Maldonado Collection's "whom she married the following year", 1959 stated by Prabook alone -- and no marriage year is asserted here (C7605, C7610). In the 1950s she began to create structures and transparent mobiles in Plexiglas with colored water, perhaps in dialogue with Gyula Kosice (C7603, C7606). Wikipedia calls her "among the first artists in Buenos Aires to use movement as a component in her sculptures"; the Guggenheim page makes the stronger joint claim for Vardanega and Boto as "the first artists in Argentina to create works of art that involved movement" -- attributed, not asserted as settled fact (C7601, C7602).

In 1959 Boto and Vardanega moved to Paris, where she lived until her death -- 45 years (C7601, C7603, C7604). The Vardanega Wikipedia sentence that says he moved to Paris in 1959 "where he met the Argentinian artist Martha Boto" is wrong; every Boto source has them already partners in Buenos Aires and moving together (C7612, C7601, C7603, C7604). In 1960 she took part in the first Biennale de Paris and began to explore movement in sculpture (C7603, C7601). In 1961 she showed for the first time at the Galerie Denise Rene, in the group exhibition Art abstrait constructif international, and Denise Rene promoted her work from then on; LACMA calls the gallery "the epicenter of op and kinetic art" (C7603, C7602, C7604). In Paris she added a motor or colored lights to her sculptural practice, focusing on the effects of movement, illumination and color, and on the ability to modify, absorb and reflect light, working in Plexiglas, aluminum and stainless steel (C7603, C7602). Critics of the time described her works in relation to science fiction and the seeming magic of space travel (C7603, C7606).

In 1964 she approached an even more articulated aesthetic, utilizing electric mechanisms and projected light on objects in movement, creating the first kinetic-light boxes (C7602). The first-use date is contested -- Prabook says 1963 for the first use of electricity, the Guggenheim page says 1964 -- and both dates are reported with their sources (C7610, C7602). The same year she and Vardanega exhibited Chromocinetisme at the Maison des Beaux-Arts, coining the term chromocinetisme for the show; sources disagree whether it was her first Paris solo show or a two-person show with Vardanega, and both descriptions are reported (C7605, C7613, C7608). In 1967 her mid-1960s light-and-motion boxes were included in Lumiere et mouvement (Light and Motion) at the Musee d'Art Moderne de Paris, May to August, curated by Frank Popper (C7607, C7609); Cheffins dates the show to 1965, which is an error, and 1967 is used here. In 1969 she had a major show at the Galerie Denise Rene, described by Sicardi as her first solo there and by the Maldonado Collection and Prabook as a retrospective; this study reports both labels without insisting they describe different shows (C7603, C7605, C7610).

At the beginning of the 1970s Boto left behind her movable structures, returned to painting, and began creating more traditional sculptures (C7605, C7608). Circular forms and wave-like rhythms predominated: the Cosmos series of concentric circles in the 1970s, the Ecume wave sculptures from 1979, the Les cyclistes and Communications painting series in the 1990s (C7605). She continued to work, making sculptures and paintings, through the early 2000s (C7603, C7606). Her work is held by the Albright-Knox, LACMA, the Musee d'Art Moderne de Paris, the Musee National d'Art Moderne - Centre Pompidou, MALBA, the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires, the Museu de Arte Moderna do Rio de Janeiro, the Museum of Fine Arts Houston and the Peggy Guggenheim Collection (C7603) -- though no individual Centre Pompidou or MALBA object page was found in this run, and no Tate or MoMA holding is attested (C7652). Posthumous shows include Contact: Le cyber-cosmos de Boto et Vardanega at Sicardi Gallery, Houston, 2006; Lo[s] Cinetico[s] at the Reina Sofia, 2007; Real/Virtual at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires, 2012-2013; Un tournant at Sicardi, 2015; Kinesthesia at Palm Springs, 2017; Women in Abstraction at the Centre Pompidou, 2021 (C7603, C7601).

Her network is the Buenos Aires circle and the Paris gallery. Vardanega is the central link: husband, collaborator, co-inventor of chromocinetisme, ANFA co-founder, Paris move together in 1959, joint shows from 1964 to 2015, and shared materials at conservation level -- the bulbs in Boto's Deplacements optiques were the same type Vardanega used in his Espaces chromatiques (C7705, C7706). The Buenos Aires circle is Arte Nuevo and ANFA: Jonquieres, Tomasello (#615, already studied) and Arden Quin beside her (C7702, C7704). Denise Rene is the hinge of the Paris career: first showing 1961, major show 1969, the dealer who promoted her after Paris (C7701, C7703, C7708). She is on no GRAV or CRAV roster -- the orbit, not the membership: she exhibited in Nouvelle Tendance collective shows and alongside Vasarely, Le Parc, Cruz-Diez and Soto at Denise Rene (C7702, C7704). The Vardanega GRAV flag from #627 is RESOLVED, not carried: the Wikipedia/Prabook claim that he was a GRAV member 1960-68 is false as stated -- its own cited source, Getty's Keep It Moving? chapter 9, names only Le Parc and Garcia Rossi as GRAV members and lists Vardanega among artists who "also worked in Paris"; he appears on no roster (Motus, CRAV ten, GRAV six) and his own CVs list only Arte Nuevo/ANFA, never GRAV (C7705, C7710, C7711, C7712, C7714, C7706, C7707). The 1960-68 dates are GRAV's lifespan pasted onto him. One secondary flag is carried into #629: his Nouvelle Tendance membership rests on Wikipedia alone, and the #629 study must check the Rossi citation before printing it.

## M1 -- The motorised kinetic-light box: the box is the machine

The box, usually black or painted wood, with a Plexiglas front, containing a motor, lamps and reflective or colored elements that move and change the light inside it. Labyrinthe (W04, 1965, aluminum and electric motor, 48 x 48 x 26.5 cm, Princeton) is inscribed on its verso as a "boite lumineuse mouvement" -- a light box in movement -- with metal cylinders and multiple mirrors (C7656). The Peggy Guggenheim page states that in 1964 Boto created the first kinetic-light boxes, utilizing electric mechanisms and projected light on objects in movement (C7654); JCMC states that by then she was using electrical motors, projectors and curved mirrors to build kinetic boxes of distorted reflections creating kaleidoscopic effects (C7653). The Houston Press, describing the 2006 Sicardi show, states that most of the works were made in the late 1960s and early 1970s, wonderfully low-tech, using simple motors to control the lights and rotate objects, and that the nearly forty-year-old works came out of their crates and started up as soon as they were plugged in (C7664). Deplacements Optiques (W11, 1968), viewed as the work itself, is the box at its most legible: a black rectangular box, open at the front, in which a central vertical rod carries a stack of thin horizontal disks in bright acid green, each tilted slightly, glowing against the black interior (C7652).

**Takeaway:** the box is the machine; the art is what the machine does to light.

## M2 -- The helicoid: one rod, one spiral, rotation does the work

A central rod with a series of chromo-polished disks arranged as a spiral, or helicoid, that turn and reflect environmental light on the curving mirrors -- LACMA's definition, for Optique Helicoidale (W07, 1967), Boto's first work to enter its collection (C7655). "The cadence of the rotations engenders a quiet, mesmerizing effect that repeats endlessly but can never be fully grasped, as forms appear and disappear" (C7655). The helicoid appears at three scales: the unique floor-standing Deplacement helicoidal lumineux (W08, 1967, Plexiglas, metal and wood, 220.5 x 66 x 51 cm), nearly five times the height of Labyrinthe and the largest work in the catalogue; the motorised prototype Multiple prototype plus helicoidals (W09, 1967, metal, motor, painted wooden case, Plexiglas, 43 x 42 x 20 cm); and the editioned Plus Helicoidal (W10, undated in the AGO record, metal, foil, Plexiglas, electric motor, lightbulb, 44 x 41.6 x 22.5 cm, edition 19/200) -- the only work in the catalogue whose medium names both an electric motor and a lightbulb in one line, so the helicoid is both moved and lit from inside the box (C7659, C7660, C7661). W10's placement with the 1967 helicoids is ordering, not a date.

**Takeaway:** one rod, one spiral of disks, and rotation does all the work.

## M3 -- Colored Plexiglas disks in transparent columns: the column is a machine for stacking light

Movable structures, motorised or not, built from colored Plexiglas disks inside transparent Plexiglas columns. JCMC states that Plexiglas discs of colors, which Boto used in previous years' kinetic boxes, became by 1968 a frequent resource to build movable structures (motorized or not) inside transparent Plexiglas columns, and that later these structures evolved as independent entities freed of the Plexiglas cover, the discs replaced by reflecting elements, colored or non-colored spheres or hoops (C7653). Transparent Structure (W15, 1969, Plexiglas, motorized, 123.2 x 47 x 47 cm, Albright-Knox), viewed as the work itself, is the catalogued example: a tall, narrow, clear vitrine on a solid pale base, inside which several vertical rods carry stacked columns of small clear Plexiglas cubes, each cube offset from the one below, rising the full height of the case, the mirrored base doubling the columns by reflection (C7657). Critics compared it to science fiction and the visual effects of space travel (C7657).

**Takeaway:** the column is a transparent machine for stacking light.

## M4 -- Mirrors, reflection and kaleidoscopic distortion: one object becomes many

Mirrors, multiangular surfaces and reflective metal used to distort space and multiply the image. The Peggy Guggenheim page states that mirrors, multiangular surfaces, or reflective metal played a fundamental role in Boto's ability to distort space and to transform the appearance of each ordinary element of an object in order to suggest an undefined depth (C7654). In the images seen, the effect is visible in Optical Structure (W02), where the back rows of disks show through the front rows of the transparent tower (C7663), and in Transparent Structure (W15), where the mirrored base doubles the columns (C7657). Labyrinthe's mechanism is the same device in miniature: metal cylinders seen through multiple mirrors, so the labyrinth is optical, not architectural (C7656). The 1964 boxes used curved mirrors to build kinetic boxes of distorted reflections creating kaleidoscopic effects (C7653).

**Takeaway:** reflection is not decoration; it is how one object becomes many.

## M5 -- Chromocinetisme: the name she gave her own system

The term Boto and Vardanega coined for their joint exhibition at the Maison des Beaux-Arts in 1964, embracing the aesthetics and new technologies of optical and kinetic art, making mechanical the movement of light, color and sound and exploring the cosmic dimensions of their practice (C7658). The term names the whole program: color in motion, mechanically produced. Mouvements chromocinetiques (W17, 1971, Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes) carries the term as a title seven years later -- though its medium and appearance are not attested in the sources read, and it must not be described as a box or a painting (C7651). Chromocinetisme is the name Boto gave her own system, not a label applied afterwards.

**Takeaway:** name the system yourself. A coined word is a claim about what the work is.

## M6 -- Programmed and rhythmic light sequences: the sequence is the composition

Light that turns on and off, or changes, in a sequence, rather than shining steadily. Cheffins states that Boto's 1960s works are defined by rhythmic light sequences and reflective surfaces, and that they are not passive objects but demand interaction, creating shifting optical experiences (C7666). The Houston Press states that Boto and Vardanega use flickering lights and motors, where Soto and Cruz-Diez primarily combine color with 3D elements to create a sense of movement, and that the sounds of clicking switches and humming motors fill the gallery (C7664). Accelerations (W12, 1968, electric lighting system, Plexiglas, white painted metal discs and mylar in a black metal case, 36 x 35.5 x 22.5 cm, edition of 200, Editions Denise Rene) is the electric lighting system as the work; Acceleration A (W14, 1969, enamelled metal, electric motor and lighting elements, 37 x 35.5 x 23 cm) is a distinct work, not to be merged with it (C7666, C7662). Dynamo (W16, 1969, metal, plastic, electric motor), viewed as the work itself, is the sequence made visible: a square box with a white face carrying four short cylinders in a two-by-two grid, each with a slatted side through which warm orange light glows, throwing soft pink-orange halos onto the white face between the cylinders (C7652). The title names the mechanism: a motor-driven light work, the electricity made visible as glow through the slats.

**Takeaway:** the sequence is the composition; the light is the material that is composed.

## M7 -- Air-current suspension: the motor is the air in the room

Forms hung or suspended so that existing air currents move them, with no motor at all. Davis states it for Untitled (W06, 1966, plastic, 48.9 x 21.6 x 21.6 cm, Hirshhorn), viewed as the work itself: a tall, narrow, transparent column on a white plinth, vertical clear rods carrying a diamond-shaped cluster of clear disks in the middle, denser at the centre and thinning toward the top and bottom; the suspended forms move from existing air currents, and walking around the piece provides a different view, the suspended objects distorting light and color as they move (C7657). The early Buenos Aires mobiles in Plexiglas with colored water belong to the same family (C7652). This is the quietest mechanism in the catalogue, and the only one that depends on the room rather than on electricity -- the Buenos Aires beginning, kept alive inside the Paris machine decade.

**Takeaway:** sometimes the motor is the air in the room.

## M8 -- Repetition, multiples and the edition: repetition is the method twice over

The same form made again, in an edition, as a deliberate system. Plus Helicoidal is edition 19/200 (C7661); Accelerations is unnumbered from an edition of 200, published by Editions Denise Rene (C7666); Deplacement optique D (W13, 1969, enamelled metal, perspex, electric motor 220 V, 47.5 x 38.5 x 22 cm) is edition EA 4 (C7669); Multiple prototype plus helicoidals names itself a multiple and a prototype in one title (C7660). Wikipedia states that Boto was known for investigations led on the principle of repetition in the world of reflection (C7651). In the images seen, repetition is also the visual system: 144 identical rings in Relief Optique (W01, 1962, Plexiglass and wood, 45 x 45 cm), viewed as the work itself -- a square white panel carrying a regular 12-by-12 grid of small clear Plexiglas rings, almost colorless, reading as faint circles, shadows and highlights on white, nearly invisible at a glance (C7653); stacked identical disks in Optical Structure; identical cubes in Transparent Structure. The edition is not a commercial afterthought; it is the same principle as the grid.

**Takeaway:** repetition is both the manufacturing method and the visual method.

## M9 -- The circle as the constant form: forty years on one form

The disk, the ring, the cylinder, the sphere and the concentric circle, from the first work to the last. Relief Optique is a grid of rings (C7653); Optical Structure is a tower of disks (C7663); the helicoids are spirals of disks (C7655); the columns are stacks of disks or cubes (C7653); the 1970s paintings are superpositions of concentric circles in the Cosmos series (C7653); Eclipse chromatique No. 1 (W18, 1973, acrylic paint) is colorful circles vibrating against a black backdrop, the movement now painted on a flat surface (C7668); Boules d'air No. 2 (W20, 1997, acrylic and plexiglas on panel, 33 1/4 x 43 1/4 in.), viewed as the work itself, is five large dark spheres built from concentric rings in blue, black, gold and white, joined by thin black rods ending in small colored dots, like antennae or molecular bonds (C7665). The form never changes; only its means of movement changes, from air, to motor, to paint. The arc is from real movement, through mechanical movement, to implied movement.

**Takeaway:** Boto spent forty years on one form, and changed everything around it.

## The aesthetic logic: air, motor, paint -- the circle never moves, only its means does

Three continuities hold the career together. The form never changes: disk, ring, cylinder, sphere, concentric circle, from the 144 rings of Relief Optique (1962) to the ringed spheres of Boules d'air No. 2 (1997). The material never leaves: Plexiglas as a light material, from the colored-water mobiles of Buenos Aires to the transparent columns of Paris to the plexiglas panel of 1997. And the criterion never changes: the work must move -- by air, by motor, by paint -- and the movement must give the spectator the illusion of contraction, diminishment or multiplication, "so that by optical means the spectator undergoes a series of reactions" (C7658). What changed is the means of movement, twice: Buenos Aires mobiles moved by air and water give way to Paris boxes moved by motors and electricity, and those give way in turn to painted circles that only seem to move. The viewer is the second motor throughout: LACMA states the goal of kinetic artists was to transform viewers from passive observers to active participants (C7655), and Davis states it concretely for the 1966 Untitled -- walking around the piece provides a different view (C7657). The work is not finished until somebody walks around it. Boto's wager is the opposite of Garcia Miranda's: he left the group, kept the question for six more years, and then let the question go and played music instead; she never joined a group, put the motor inside the box, and kept the same circle moving for forty years, changing only what moved it. She is the artist who put motors inside the box -- and then, when the motors were done, painted the movement the motors had made.
