# Nicolas-Pierre Loir: Study Notes

**Site:** Nicolas-Pierre Loir (1624, Paris -- 6 May 1679, Paris), French painter, draughtsman and etcher; eldest son of a Paris goldsmith, pupil of Sebastien Bourdon and Simon Vouet, disciple of Nicolas Poussin after two years in Rome, painter of the goldsmiths' May of 1650, member of the Academie Royale from 1663, and the royal decorator whose ceilings at the Tuileries, the Gobelins designs and the queen's apartment at Versailles are mostly lost -- the Poussinist who painted so close to his master that his own pictures were later taken for Poussin's, and forgotten once the attribution habit set in.

## The life

Nicolas-Pierre Loir was born in Paris in 1624 and died there on 6 May 1679, aged 55. The day of his birth is not known -- fr.wiki and it.wiki say so explicitly, and the Catalan Wikipedia's 5 May is unsourced (see C1252); even the year is disputed at the margins, the Renard catalogue and one Joconde notice giving 1623 (see C1271). The study uses 1624.

He was the eldest son of Nicolas I Loir, a Paris goldsmith received as master on 29 October 1616, of an old goldsmith family of the parish of Saint-Jacques-la-Boucherie. His younger brother Alexis I Loir (1640-1713) followed the father into the trade as goldsmith and engraver, and later engraved Nicolas's designs, including the twelve ceiling designs and the reception piece. The Gazette Drouot article of June 2026 revives the old suggestion that the precision and delicacy of Loir's art may derive from the goldsmith background -- as a suggestion, not a finding. Unlike Alexis, the young Nicolas chose to train as a painter.

He was a pupil of Sebastien Bourdon and of Simon Vouet. That is the RKD's statement as reported by en.wiki, repeated by the dealer and press biographies; fr.wiki names Bourdon alone as master, and adds the texture found nowhere else -- Loir met Bourdon at the house of Louis I Du Guernier (1614-1659), described as an ally of goldsmiths and jewellers, a plausible meeting point for a goldsmith's son. The order of the two masters is not settled (see C1253): de.wiki, drawing on the AKL, reverses it (Vouet first, then Bourdon). Vouet died on 30 June 1649, so any training under him belongs to Loir's teens or early twenties, before the Italy trip; Bourdon was in Paris in the 1640s. No source read gives dates for either apprenticeship.

Italy, 1647-49, is the hinge. Loir travelled with Andre Felibien (1619-1695), who became embassy secretary and later the great historiographer of French art; the two were friends, and Felibien introduced Loir to the great collectors of Rome. In Rome he discovered the work of Nicolas Poussin, and the decorations of Raphael and Guercino; the party visited the collection of the cavaliere Cassiano dal Pozzo and the vigne -- the country houses of the papal nobility -- and went to Grottaferrata, where Domenichino had painted. Whether Loir actually met Poussin in person is asserted only by the Gazette article (see C1254); fr.wiki and de.wiki say only that he discovered Poussin's work and moved in his circle. The one Italian work of which there is any trace is Darius Visiting the Tomb of Semiramis, which reportedly had some success in Rome and is now lost (see C1255). Sotheby's catalogue note says he "spent almost a decade in Italy" -- every other source gives 1647-49, two years, and the decade is treated as an error (see C1276/C1291).

Back in Paris in 1650, his first notable commission came from his own milieu: the Confrerie des Orfevres commissioned the year's May for Notre-Dame de Paris -- Saint Paul Blinds the False Prophet Bar-Jesus and Converts the Proconsul Sergius (W03), a 410 x 345 cm canvas (REPORTED only) still listed at the cathedral, with an oil sketch at the Musee Carnavalet (see C1270 for the post-fire location question). After that came private commissions, altarpieces and pictures for religious houses: Christ Appearing to Saint Teresa for the Carmes Dechaux (not recovered), The Taking of the Habit of Saint William of Aquitaine for the parlour of the Feuillants in the rue Saint-Honore (W12, Louvre, 259 x 469 cm VERIFIED), a Saint Mary of Egypt for the Carmelite convent in the rue Saint-Jacques (now Musee des Beaux-Arts, Marseille, W11), and decorations for private interiors -- the gallery of the Hotel de Senneterre (destroyed when the Place des Victoires was cut through), ceilings and vaults at the Hotel de Vigny, and a great salon and gallery for Henri du Plessis-Guenegaud at the Chateau du Plessis-Belleville. He painted and etched several Holy Families himself, mythological compositions and a series of studies of faces.

In 1665 he married Marguerite Cotelle (the year is single-sourced, see C1267), daughter of the painter Jean I Cotelle (1607-1676) and sister of the painter Jean II Cotelle; the couple had two children. The marriage was a working alliance as much as a family one. From the end of the 1650s Loir designed the interior decoration of private hotels and chateaux together with Jean Cotelle -- the Chateau du Plessis-Belleville (1657/61) and the Paris Hotel de La Ferte-Senneterre (1664), neither decoration surviving -- and fr.wiki says he did many decors with the frequent help of his father-in-law Jean I Cotelle, a specialist in camaiieu (monochrome painting). In 1669 his pupil Francois de Troy married his sister-in-law Jeanne Cotelle -- the one hard link between Loir's teaching and the next generation: de Troy, the future portraitist of the Regence, learned his trade in Loir's studio.

He entered the Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture in 1663, with the support of Charles Le Brun and of Louis XIV. The mechanics of that entry are not uniformly reported (see C1258): the members' list gives his reception as 31 March 1663 and notes he was first received without a reception piece, by royal decision; the Versailles collections notice for the reception piece says the morceau de reception was presented on 2 October 1666, confirming the session of 31 March 1663, while the members' list dates the morceau to 28 October 1666. The piece itself is the Allegorie de la fondation de l'Academie royale, also called Le Progres des arts du dessin sous le regne de Louis XIV (W16, 141 x 184.5 cm VERIFIED, Versailles MV 8650). Within the Academie he became successively professor, then adjoint (adjoint a recteur) and then -- according to fr.wiki and de.wiki -- recteur: professor from 1666, deputy rector in 1668, rector in 1675 (de.wiki, citing the AKL). But the one primary document read, his burial notice, styles him only "Peintre ordinaire du Roy et Adjoint a Recteur de l'Academie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture" at his death (see C1259), and de Piles (1699) calls him "Professeur en l'Academie" at death. Whether he ever actually held the rectorship is unresolved; do not state it as fact without Massat. He was a king's pensioner from 1668, with a pension of 4,000 livres according to it.wiki and the dealer biographies -- a figure no scholarly source read confirms (see C1260). He also gave Academy Conferences, including one delivered before Poussin's Deluge; the Academy did devote an exceptional conference to the Deluge in 1668, but Loir's authorship of it was not confirmed in the sources read (see C1265).

From the 1660s he was busy on the royal building sites, which is given as the reason his reception piece was three years late: the Gobelins (designs -- embroidery per fr.wiki, tapestry cartoons per de.wiki, see C1268), Vincennes (1666, with Cotelle), the Tuileries (1668-71: the Salle des Gardes and the antichamber of the king's appartement haut -- a ceiling with the Sun seated on his chariot with the Hours and the four parts of the day, a trompe-l'oeil ceiling, vaults and four bas-reliefs imitating bronze at the corners; all of it lost, the palace burned in 1871), Versailles (the appartements des Attiques and des Bains, 1671/72, and paintings for the grand apartment of Queen Marie-Therese -- seven paintings according to Proantic and de.wiki, twelve according to fr.wiki and the Gazette article, see C1261/C1301; fragments survive, among them Pithes et Pithopolis, 1673, now at the Musee de Brou), Saint-Germain-en-Laye (1673: the apartments of Madame de Montespan; two pictures survive there), and Fontainebleau, named by the Gazette article without specifics. Much of this decorative work has disappeared. What survives in situ is thin: fr.wiki notes a single surviving in situ decor, a cabinet at the Hotel de Vigny in Paris, made with his brother-in-law Jacques Gervaise.

He died in Paris on 6 May 1679, a Saturday, at his house in the rue de Richelieu, aged 55. The burial notice (Fidiere, Etat-civil, 1883, p. 32, no. LXI, transcribed from the Academie's register) invites the members to the convoy and burial on Sunday 7 May 1679 at five in the evening; the weekday checks out (7 May 1679 was a Sunday). At his death he left two children with Marguerite Cotelle. The estate figure is disputed -- 200 paintings (fr.wiki) against 24 (it.wiki) -- from which his brother Alexis, Cochin, Boulanger and others made many prints (see C1262/C1285). Jean Tiger (1623-1698) painted his portrait in 1675, showing him holding a drawing of Hercules on the Pyre; it is at Versailles. He died, on de Piles' account, while still professor at the Academie -- a working academician to the end, not a retired master.

## The evolution: four phases, one conversion

**1. Early Paris, to 1647 -- Vouet, then Bourdon; the goldsmith's son.** The earliest dated work, the small copper Annonciation of 1644 (W01, 21.5 x 16 cm REPORTED, Rennes), is a cabinet devotional in a precious format -- copper, the scale and finish of a goldsmith's household. De.wiki's judgement, worth keeping: technically accomplished, but "a certain lack of independent inventive power" is held against him; his historical role was spreading Poussin's manner under Louis XIV.

**2. Italy, 1647-1649 -- Poussin, Raphael, Guercino.** Travelled with Felibien; visited the Cassiano dal Pozzo collection and Roman villas, Grottaferrata (Domenichino); met Poussin, or at least his pictures, and absorbed him so thoroughly that his Poussin adaptations were later taken for originals (fr.wiki; Gazette Drouot). The Darius was his Roman success -- and is lost. What survives from the return is the Poussinist grammar itself: pyramidal groups on stone pedestals, landscape as a full second subject, classical facades and antiquities (sphinx, bas-reliefs) punctuating successive planes, a light palette of pinks, greens and blues from the Parisian Atticists (La Hyre, Le Sueur) fused with Poussin's Roman classicism (Gazette Drouot on the 2026 Holy Family).

**3. Return and church work, 1650-c.1662 -- the May, altarpieces, the Holy Family serial.** The 1650 May (W03) was the breakthrough public commission. Altarpieces followed for Paris churches, most now lost or ruined. The Holy Family becomes his serial laboratory: the legend of twelve different Holy Families painted in a single day to meet a painters' challenge, then engraved as a set of twelve etchings (c.1655); the Louvre Adoration exists in at least four states -- first thought (W09), Yale version (W10), Louvre altarpiece (W08), Louvre copy (INV 30698) -- with the composition reworked, reversed and re-geometrised between states (Renard/Sterling). The mechanism is visible: a V-shape enclosing the Child against a triangular figure group, primary-colour cloaks (Virgin blue, shepherd orange-red, Joseph yellow), brown-red preparation left bare in the upper corners, two cherubs with phylacteries borrowed from Poussin's Martyrdom of Saint Erasmus, the kneeling shepherd reversed from Poussin's Et in Arcadia ego.

**4. Academie and the royal machine, 1663-1679 -- allegory at architectural scale.** Admitted to the Academie Royale in 1663 with Le Brun's support; morceau de reception only in 1666 (W16), a 141 x 184.5 cm allegory that puts Louis XIV's portrait-medallion at the centre of Olympus, Minerva pointing, Nike sounding the trumpet, a putto with a laurel crown, Saturn drawing a purple curtain -- the king as a god among gods, propaganda by antique machinery (Histoire par l'image). From here the work is royal decoration at scale: Gobelins cartoons, Tuileries, Vincennes, Versailles, Saint-Germain; the queen's apartment cycle at Versailles (1671-79) of which only fragments survive (W18, W19); Colbert protecteur des Arts (W17) as ministerial allegory. The late altarpiece Resurrection (W20, 290 x 168 cm VERIFIED, in situ at Le Neubourg) shows the same classicism grown to retable scale.

## M1 -- The pupil who completes the master's arc: eclectic training, one Roman trip, Poussin

Loir is the middle term of the Bourdon-to-Poussin gradient, made complete. Bourdon went to Rome in the 1630s, imitated everybody -- Claude, Castiglione, the Bamboccianti, Caravaggio -- and only took up Poussin in his last two decades, after about 1640. Le Sueur never left Paris and learned Poussin at second hand, from prints and from Poussin's brief, fraught return to Paris in 1640-42. Loir went to Rome in 1647-49, discovered Poussin's work, and converted so completely that his own pictures were later attributed to the master (fr.wiki; Gazette Drouot). The arc his master took a career to walk, the pupil walked in one trip -- and then spent the rest of his life inside the institution his other master's generation had founded, the Academie Le Sueur helped found in 1648 and Loir entered in 1663. What he took from Bourdon was the trajectory itself, not a style; what he took from Vouet was the Parisian workshop formation, the decorative Baroque manner he then disciplined toward Poussin's classicism. And the conversion had a cost built in: a painter so close to Poussin that his own work dissolved into the master's, and was forgotten once the attribution habit set in. The 2026 rediscovery of the Holy Family with a Plate of Figs -- authenticated by Pinta and Weil-Curiel after direct examination, unknown until now -- is the mechanism running in reverse: a Loir surfacing from under Poussin's name.

**Takeaway:** let the pupil finish the master's sentence in one trip -- eclectic training, a Roman conversion, then a lifetime inside the institution, and the slow erasure that follows when the conversion is too complete: pictures taken for the master's, the name forgotten, the work resurfacing centuries later under its own name.

## M2 -- The first thought: a composition reworked, not sketched

The Adoration of the Shepherds exists in at least four states, and the sequence is the most mechanically documented Loir in this study. The "first thought" (W09, 21.5 x 16.5 cm VERIFIED, private collection) is a small oil on canvas with the brown-red preparation left bare in the two unpainted upper corners -- typical of mid-17th-century French painting and of Poussin. Catherine Sterling's argument, taken up by the Renard catalogue: this is a first thought, not a preparatory study, because the Louvre altarpiece (W08, INV 2803, 136 x 102 cm REPORTED, painted for the church of Saint-Honore) was completely reworked from it -- the V-shape enclosing the Child on a white cloth against a triangular figure group, five shepherds and Joseph reversed relative to the first thought, the ox moved to centre behind the Child, the Virgin shifted right with arms outstretched in wonder (see C1282). The Yale version (W10, c.1660-70, 27.8 x 36.2 cm REPORTED) is a third state, the main figures as in W08 but the Child's position reversed. The borrowings are itemised, not vague: the two cherubs with phylacteries come from Poussin's Martyrdom of Saint Erasmus; the kneeling shepherd is reversed from Poussin's Et in Arcadia ego. The palette is a system, not a taste: primary-colour cloaks -- Virgin blue, shepherd orange-red, Joseph yellow -- against the bare brown-red ground. The same small scale recurs across the corpus: the 1644 Annonciation on copper is 21.5 x 16 cm, the first thought 21.5 x 16.5 cm -- Loir's intimate scale for first thoughts, against the 410 cm May and the 469 cm Saint William.

**Takeaway:** build the picture in states, not in one pass -- a first thought at copper scale, the composition reworked and reversed at altarpiece scale, the borrowings named and placed, the palette as three primary cloaks against bare ground, and the small version kept as the record of the thinking rather than discarded as a sketch.

## M3 -- The May: the guild commission that makes the reputation

On his return in 1650 Loir's budding talent was rewarded with a commission from the goldsmiths for a May scene at Notre-Dame: Saint Paul Blinds the False Prophet Bar-Jesus and Converts the Proconsul Sergius (W03). It is the same corporation, and the same May-picture mechanism, that made Bourdon's reputation with the 1643 Crucifixion of Saint Peter. The goldsmiths are Loir's family trade as well as his first patron: the May picture is the point where family, guild and Academy meet. The goldsmith's son paints the goldsmiths' altarpiece for the cathedral in the year of his return from Rome, at twenty-six, and the commission is the public proof of the Roman conversion -- a 410 x 345 cm canvas (REPORTED only) that is still listed at the cathedral, with a small oil sketch at the Musee Carnavalet and another sketch at Rennes. The mechanism repeats across the generation: the May is the one commission that makes the career, a guild altarpiece for the cathedral, painted young, and the reputation that follows from a single public picture rather than from a lifetime of small ones. Loir's May is also the largest surviving Loir by the list's figures -- and the one whose present location, after the 2019 fire and restoration, was not verified in any source read (see C1270).

**Takeaway:** stage the one commission that makes the career -- a guild altarpiece for the cathedral, painted the year of the return from Rome, the goldsmith's son painting for the goldsmiths, and the public picture that carries the private conversion into the open.

## M4 -- The lost ceiling, the surviving print: ornament as a working model

Most of Loir's largest and most prestigious work does not survive, and the study must say so rather than describe it as if it could be seen. The Tuileries ceilings (1668-71), the Gobelins designs, the Saint-Germain ceilings, the Hotel de Senneterre gallery, the Plessis-Belleville rooms, the Vincennes work, the Versailles appartements des Attiques and des Bains -- gone, destroyed, or unpreserved. What survives of the ceiling practice is not the ceiling but the print: two ornament suites, Plafonds a la moderne (twelve engraved ceiling designs, published Paris by Nicolas Langlois, c.1660; V and A holds plates, Fuhring nos. 8927-8938) and Divers Ornemens, Trophees et Panneaux (twelve plates of vertical grotesques, Paris 1660-70; V and A acc. 16391, signed "N.L.I.F" with "C.P.R" at lower right). The suites were used by architects, decorators and craftsmen as working models for ceilings and interiors -- Loir as a transmitter of French Baroque decorative design, not just a painter of it. The mechanism is the portable ceiling: the decorative programme that perishes in plaster survives as a printed system, twelve plates a suite, engraved after the painter (and in part by his brother Alexis), circulating through workshops that never saw the room. The modello works the same way at smaller scale: Junon sur son char (W14, c.1660, private collection) is the surviving trace of a ceiling for the ground-floor antechamber of the Hotel d'Aumont -- the practice preserved in miniature while the room is gone.

**Takeaway:** when the ceiling is lost, build the print that outlived it -- twelve engraved ceiling designs as a working model for architects and decorators, the decorative programme surviving as a portable system, and the modello as the miniature that remembers the room.

## M5 -- The reception allegory: the king as a god among gods

The morceau de reception (W16, 1666, 141 x 184.5 cm VERIFIED) is propaganda by antique machinery, and the machinery is worth itemising because it is the whole picture. Time unveils Painting and Sculpture, two women whose beauty had until then been ignored, while Minerva, descended from her chariot, shows them a portrait medallion of Louis XIV held by Fame -- indicating for whom they must now work. Nike sounds the trumpet; a putto brandishes a laurel crown; Saturn draws a purple curtain; a fallen antique torso lies at the base. The king's medallion sits at the centre of Olympus, and the allegory's argument is that the arts exist to serve him -- the reception piece as a contract, signed in myth. The picture's own history is a second mechanism: Academie collection; revolutionary seizure 9 December 1793; depot de Nesle; Versailles 28 March 1798; Louvre; Compiegne 1827-1832; back to Versailles. The small sketch (W15, 36.5 x 52 cm REPORTED, Perpignan) is the same allegory at cabinet scale. The three-year gap between entry (1663) and reception piece (1666) is normal for the period -- Lefebvre, received the same year, presented his Colbert portrait only on 30 October 1666 -- but the study should not collapse the two dates. The members' list also attaches the title Allegorie de la Reduction de Dunkerque to this reception, which no other source read uses (see C1258).

**Takeaway:** build the allegory as a machine with named parts -- Time unveiling, Minerva pointing, Fame holding the medallion, Nike trumpeting, Saturn drawing the curtain -- and let the king's portrait sit at the centre of Olympus, the contract between the arts and the crown drawn in myth.

## M6 -- The queen's apartment: fragments of a cycle

Around 1673 Loir produced paintings for Marie-Therese's grand apartment at Versailles -- seven according to Proantic and de.wiki, twelve according to fr.wiki and the Gazette article (see C1261/C1301); the count and the date are both unsettled, and the study asserts neither number without a stated source. What survives are fragments, and the fragments are the mechanism: Pithes et Pithopolis (W18, 1673, 102.5 x 181 cm REPORTED, Musee de Brou), an element of the coving (voussure) of the former Salon de Jupiter, showing Pithopolis serving dishes of gold; La Reine s'adressant a ses soldats (W19, c.1674-77, 141 x 158 cm REPORTED, Louvre INV 8715), an over-door (dessus-de-porte) of the same salon, formerly attributed to the French 17th-century school before the Loir attribution; La Reine de Saba donnant une audience (c.1674-77, 148 x 115 cm REPORTED, Perpignan), a companion over-door. A coving and two over-doors: the cycle survives as the architectural pieces of a room -- the curved band above the wall, the picture above the door -- and the room itself is gone. Some of the queen's-apartment canvases were later moved to the Chateau de Meudon. Whether Pithopolis femme de Pithes is a second Brou picture or the alternate title of W18 is likewise unresolved (see C1284).

**Takeaway:** build the cycle as its surviving fragments -- a coving and two over-doors, the curved band and the picture above the door, the room gone and the architectural pieces carrying the whole programme in miniature.

## M7 -- The Holy Family serial: twelve variations, one grammar

The Holy Family is Loir's serial laboratory. The legend says twelve different Holy Families were painted in a single day to meet a challenge among painters, then engraved as a set of twelve etchings (c.1655) -- the Gazette article itself introduces this as legend (see C1257), and the study treats it as anecdote, not fact. What is documented is the serial itself: twelve etched Holy Families, Loir etching his own designs (British Museum holds eleven of the twelve plates, plate 6 missing); the 2026 Holy Family with a Plate of Figs, resurfaced from a Puy-de-Dome family collection and authenticated by Pinta and Weil-Curiel after direct examination on 9 March 2026, sold at Clermont-Ferrand on 9 July 2026 (result unverified, see C1279); the Narbonne Holy Family (102 x 140 cm REPORTED) as a public-collection touchstone. The 2026 picture shows the grammar: a pyramidal group on a stone pedestal after Poussin's Holy Family on the Steps (Cleveland), four teenagers offering a dish of figs to the crossed-legged Child, Joseph standing well back "as is his custom", and behind them a full Poussin landscape -- successive planes, classical facades, a sphinx, antique bas-reliefs, a riverside fortress and steep mountains under a cloudy sunset -- in a light Atticist palette of pinks combined with greens and blues, "great gentleness", in the manner of La Hyre and Le Sueur. The serial is also the attribution trap: pictures so close to Poussin that they were taken for his, and the serial is how a painter disappears -- not by failing, but by succeeding too well at the model.

**Takeaway:** run the subject as a serial -- twelve variations on one grammar, the pyramidal group on a stone pedestal, the Child crossed-legged, Joseph standing back, the full Poussin landscape behind, the Atticist pinks and greens -- and let the serial be the trap: the variations so close to the master that the author vanishes into him.

## M8 -- The ordinary painter: facility without elevation

Roger de Piles, writing twenty years after Loir's death, is the contemporary verdict that counts, and it is mixed to the point of damning. Read in the original (Abrege de la vie des peintres, 1699, p. 504): "NICOLAS LOIR, de Paris, fils d'un habile Orfevre, ne manquoit pas de Genie pour inventer, ni de feu pour executer. Il n'y avoit neanmoins rien en cela qui passat le Peintre ordinaire... Il avoit bon Gout de Dessein et de la facilite dans tout ce qu'il faisoit; a peine les avoit-il produits qu'il les executoit, souvent meme discourant avec le monde, par la grande memoire des choses qu'il avoit vues en Italie. Il ne demeuroit court sur rien. Il faisoit egalement bien les Figures, le Paisage, l'Architecture et les Ornemens." He had genius to invent and fire to execute, "nevertheless there was nothing in it that passed the ordinary painter", nothing with any particular elevation. He could execute an idea almost as soon as he had conceived it, often while talking with company, from his great memory of the things he had seen in Italy. He was never at a loss for anything, and did figures, landscape, architecture and ornaments equally well. The modern summaries keep the praise and drop the reservation: the standard "very good draughtsman" paraphrase inflates the 1699 text (see C1264) -- de Piles wrote that Loir had good taste in dessein and facility, and in the same breath judged that nothing in his work passed the ordinary painter. The praise and the reservation must travel together. The modern scholarly verdict is the same judgement minus the warmth: de.wiki, following the AKL, calls him a technically accomplished painter charged with a certain lack of independent inventive power, whose merit was to have spread Poussin's manner under Louis XIV. And the Italian Wikipedia's claim that Loir belonged to the perfectly balanced group of de Piles' Balance des peintres is wrong -- Loir does not appear in the Balance table at all (see C1263). Dezallier d'Argenville, who owned several of Loir's drawings, some heightened in colour, adds the personal portrait: a man of gentle and modest temperament, profoundly honest, much esteemed by his contemporaries, including as a portraitist. Cornelis de Bie wrote a commemorative poem to his art in Het Gulden Cabinet (1662), p. 491, while Loir was still alive -- the earliest literary notice of him found, its text not read.

**Takeaway:** let the verdict be mixed and carry both halves -- the facility that executes an idea while still talking, the memory of Italy that never runs dry, the figures and landscape and architecture and ornaments all equally well done, and the reservation that nothing in it passes the ordinary painter: the ordinary as the ceiling, not the floor.

## The aesthetic logic

Loir's aesthetic is the Atticist filter on Poussin. The construction is Poussin's -- balanced, geometric, pyramidal groups on stone pedestals, successive planes receding through classical facades -- and the colour is Bourdon's and the Paris Atticists' -- a light palette of pinks combined with greens and blues, primary-colour cloaks against bare brown-red ground, great gentleness rather than Roman weight. He works in states: the first thought at copper scale, the altarpiece reworked and reversed, the serial varied twelve times, the ceiling reduced to a print suite. He works at architectural scale when the commission demands it -- 410 cm for the May, 469 cm wide for Saint William, 290 cm for the Resurrection -- and at cabinet scale when the thinking demands it -- 21.5 cm for the first thought. The goldsmith's son keeps the goldsmith's scale for the thinking and the king's scale for the showing. The through-line is drawing: sanguine signed "LOIR PEINTRE", pen and brown wash, the Louvre sheets of angels on clouds, the etched Holy Families in his own hand, the ornament suites as working models. De Piles saw it exactly: facility, memory, and the ordinary as the limit. Loir is the painter who proves that a system can be complete and still not be great -- the Poussinist grammar mastered so thoroughly that the master disappears into it, and the pupil with him.

## The network

**Teachers:** Sebastien Bourdon (1616-1671), first master -- Loir repeats his arc, eclectic training to Poussin conversion, one generation on; Simon Vouet (1590-1649), second master -- the Parisian workshop formation, the decorative Baroque manner disciplined toward classicism. Vouet was also the master of Le Sueur and Mignard. **The model:** Nicolas Poussin (1594-1665) -- discovered in Rome 1647-49, converted to, copied, and later mistaken for. Raphael and Guercino also named; Domenichino at Grottaferrata. **Rome companion:** Andre Felibien (1619-1695), historiographer and embassy secretary, who introduced Loir to the great collectors of Rome -- a documented personal link between Loir and the Academy's intellectual circle. **Family workshop:** father Nicolas I Loir, goldsmith (received 29 October 1616); brother Alexis I Loir (1640-1713), goldsmith-engraver who engraved Nicolas's compositions; father-in-law Jean I Cotelle (1607-1676), camaiieu specialist, frequent collaborator on hotel and chateau decors; brother-in-law Jean II Cotelle; wife Marguerite Cotelle (married 1665). **Patron and gatekeeper:** Charles Le Brun (1619-1690), whose support secured the Academy entry in 1663; Loir then worked on Le Brun's royal decorative teams. Jean-Baptiste Colbert as ministerial patron (W17); Louis XIV as patron of the 1666 allegory and the queen's apartment cycle. **The goldsmiths' corporation:** first patron, the 1650 May -- the same mechanism as Bourdon's 1643 May. **Pupil:** Francois de Troy (1645-1730), who studied under Loir and Claude Lefebvre, married Loir's sister-in-law Jeanne Cotelle in 1668/69, became principal painter to James II in exile at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, and directed the Academy 1708-11 -- the Loir line carried into the portraiture of the Regence and the early Rococo. **Contemporaries in the same Atticist orbit:** Laurent de La Hyre (1606-1656) and Eustache Le Sueur (1616/17-1655), through whom the Gazette reads Loir's palette; Claude Lefebvre (bapt. 1632-1675), Le Sueur's pupil, Le Brun's assistant, co-teacher of de Troy with Loir, received into the Academy the same year as Loir (1663). **The rival pole:** Pierre Mignard (1612-1695), the Vouet pupil who refused Le Brun's Academy and inherited all his posts in 1690 -- Loir sits on the opposite side of that fault line, the Academy man sponsored by Le Brun. **Later witnesses:** Roger de Piles (listed Loir among notable French artists); Cornelis de Bie (commemorative poem, 1662); Dezallier d'Argenville (owned several Loir drawings); Jean Tiger (portrait of Loir, 1675, Versailles). **No documented contact** in the sources read between Loir and Bourdon after the pupilage, or between Loir and Ehrenstrahl, Schultz, Danckerts or Beck -- the Stockholm/Warsaw circuit belongs to Bourdon's generation, not Loir's. Loir's network is Paris-Rome-Paris, Academy-centred.
