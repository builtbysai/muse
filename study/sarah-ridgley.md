## The life: law, letterpress, and no art school

The biography is unusually well documented because Ridgley tells it herself, at length, in two interviews: Verse (2023, "Capturing Human Essence in the Machine") and Bright Moments (2024, "SARAH RIDGLEY: FIGS"), plus a 2014 Boxcar Press studio tour of her letterpress shop. The study does not fill the gaps those interviews leave.

Sarah Ridgley, American, based in Arkansas -- Fort Smith as of 2014, her home town, where she says she has "lived all my life in Arkansas except for one glorious year in London after I graduated college." She is married (Kevin, a Texan from Dallas) and a mother; the 2014 tour names one son, Finnegan, and the 2024 interview mentions sons in the plural, so the family has grown since 2014 and the study states no fixed number of children. No birth date or birthplace is evidenced anywhere; Arkansas residence is lifelong by her own words, but that is not a birthplace claim (C1760).

She earned a JD in 2006 (Verse bio: "A few years after earning her JD in 2006, Ridgley decided to change course and began to pursue her passion for art"). The law school's name was not found in any source read, and no source says what legal work she did, whether she practised, or whether she still practises. Do not name a school or a practice. The JD matters for a different reason: it is the second of three self-teachings. "I don't actually have a formal art background, but I've always enjoyed being creative" (Bright Moments). She has no formal art background at all, and says so plainly.

Letterpress came first, as a hobby and an outlet from reading case law in law school. She fell in love with it while researching invitations for her own wedding, having first seen a letterpress invitation in Martha Stewart Weddings; she bought her first press on eBay -- a Kelsey 5x7 with a cabinet of type for $100, formerly owned by a pharmacist, with skull-and-crossbones ornaments. The shop was Pheasant Press: greeting cards, wedding invitations, other projects, sold on Etsy. By 2014 the studio was a 1,600 sq ft garage in a house bought at auction, holding a Chandler & Price 8x12, a Vandercook 1, the little Kelsey, and a C&P 10x15 in bad shape awaiting restoration; a Heidelberg Windmill 10x15 bought at a local bank auction for $100 had been sold because hand-feeding suited her better. She was "completely self taught, but I had a lot of help from people on Briar Press", starting from George Mills' Platen Press Operation -- Mills himself from Fort Smith. Favourite eras: Victorian and Midcentury modern, the blend she aimed for in a cohesive Pheasant Press style. "No, printing is more of a hobby for me. It would be fun to be able to spend all my time printing and designing, but I am not pursuing it as a main goal."

The press is the first "machine that makes marks" in her practice, and the hand/machine negotiation that defines her generative work starts here, in physical type and impression. It also sets the method she will reuse twice more: research skills, Google, forums, trial and error, no teacher. When she teaches herself code four years later she explicitly credits the letterpress self-teaching process -- "a lot of hands-on learning and problem-solving, often resorting to google and forums" -- with laying the groundwork.

## The turn: calligraphy on an iPad, late 2017

She came to code through calligraphy in Procreate on her iPad. Her own words (Bright Moments): "I came across creative coding around the end of 2017 or early 2018. At the time, I was exploring calligraphy in Procreate on my iPad. The discovery of coding was a revelation -- the idea of creating lines and shapes through code was fascinating." She taught herself with her research skills; Daniel Shiffman's Coding Train was her favourite resource.

Her first goal in code names the whole practice: "to create lines that were wobbly, natural, and organic, but in a more pleasing way than I could do holding pen to paper" -- humanising shapes a computer renders perfectly, an imperfect, organic circle. The early influence she names is Matt Pearson's Generative Art -- A Practical Guide Using Processing, especially its section on order and chaos: generative artists "start from order and head towards chaos" to make something organic from logical functions (Verse). The book's arc becomes her doctrine, restated in her own phrase: "All my work is the result of highly-ordered chaos. This means that I write very organized instructions to direct the code, but then introduce random parameters so that the computer can try out an infinite variety of results."

She kept the coding separate from the letterpress shop at first, under a pseudonym. Her own words (Bright Moments): "This was before NFTs, and I was balancing my new interest in coding with my established letterpress shop on Etsy, Pheasant Press. To avoid mixing these two worlds, and because I wasn't really sure where I was going with the coding, I used the pseudonym 'glaem' on Instagram to share my creative coding work. I also started finding other generative artists and getting involved with the community." The pseudonym rests on her own statement in this one interview -- a primary source, but the only one; no second source was found across ten search passes (C1756).

The on-chain sequence that follows is short and steep: a Valentines series on Stellar in 2019 (her first blockchain experience); selling on Rarible and OpenSea in 2020; Hic et Nunc on Tezos in 2020-21 ("just lovely" community, in her words); then Himinn on Art Blocks, 30 September 2021. The Tezos move matters: low cost let her experiment freely, which is the precondition for the 2022 long-form Tezos projects. She also names a community fact the study should keep: "I have a group of fellow gen art moms I regularly speak to and other online communities where generative artists share, collaborate, and support each other."

## The practice: photographic, not mathematical

Her technical identity is stated plainly and it inverts the usual generative-art story. She has no maths background and says so: "I don't have a mathematical background, so my approach is a bit different than finding a beautiful mathematical formula to express. I would say I have a more photographic style, maybe. I use a lot of image processing techniques in my code" (Bright Moments). Her systems are built around pixel arrays and underpaintings, not equations. Tools: JavaScript and p5.js historically ("I use Javascript and the p5js library to build my programs, and I design unique algorithmic brushes and rendering techniques to complete each series"), increasingly vanilla JavaScript later: "I've also been moving away from using libraries like p5js, as I've discovered it's much faster when I manipulate basic javascript myself to do exactly what I want." Do not present her as p5-only.

Her stated focus is "investigating the materiality of pixels and exploring new techniques and ways of using code as a method of mark-making on a digital canvas." On Figs.: "There's a concrete reality to it. Consider the brightness and saturation of the tiny lights, the tactile, staccato sensations of typing -- there's a physicality to coding that might not be immediately apparent."

The method, as she describes it across the two interviews, has five standing parts. First, the underpainting: generate an underpainting, then "give the computer freedom to sift through the pixel array and select elements to surface for the final image" (Verse, Ateliers notes). Before Himinn the underpainting was a photograph; for Himinn she had to generate it entirely in code. Second, the brush path: for Ateliers she wanted lines to follow paths made from pixel sorting rather than mathematically calculated positions, and built "a simplified nearest neighbor sorting. This allows the paint to follow a more organic path through the pixels, and furthers my goal to incorporate the human hand into my work with the computer." Third, the writing brush carried into painting: "I've been searching for a long time to find a way to incorporate the brushstrokes I use in my writing algorithms for painting. The biggest difficulty was finding a way to rearrange the points so that the rendering could follow a more natural path. I finally had one of those 'aha!' moments, and when it started working, it really felt magical. I also made a small miscalculation with part of the brush, and the result was this delicious, sandy texture in the paint." The sandy texture was an accident kept on purpose. Fourth, contours last, after Matisse: "When I first started working on Ateliers, I was painting the lines first, and then filling in the shapes with color. Then, I read an article describing how Matisse would reverse the normal order and add his contours at the end. I decided to try that, and once I reversed the order suddenly everything popped and looked so much more cohesive." Fifth, imperfection by layers: "It's interesting how easy it is to make a perfect shape using the computer, and how much extra code is required to create more organic forms. Even randomness and noise can start to feel formulaic if you're not careful, so I usually use multiple layers of noise to push the 'imperfect' as much as possible. It's the exact opposite of painting by hand, which I find fascinating."

And the render itself is part of the work: "My particular approach is to use a lot of tiny pixels to make up my images. The smaller the better, although that can make things run really slowly. So it's about finding a balance, or a fun rendering technique that makes the on-screen drawing an essential part of the work." Watching the work draw itself matters: "One of the most interesting things to me about generative art is that we can show the work being created in real time, and watch as the computer navigates the instructions and decides what actions to take."

Her influences, all in her own words: Henri Matisse ("consistently one of my favorites. His contour line work amazes me, and was part of the inspiration for me in working towards my own algorithmic lines"); Harold Cohen ("I'm very inspired by the work of Harold Cohen, and I'm eager to explore figuration with code" -- the Verse interview opens with Cohen's Adam and Eve, AARON, 1986); colour from Helen Frankenthaler, Elaine de Kooning, Alice Neel, built into palettes with Coolors.co from paintings she admires; Sol LeWitt ("the most interesting characteristic of the cube is that it is relatively uninteresting"); Claude Monet (Memory of Nymphaeas paints water lilies automatically "in a style reminiscent of Claude Monet"); and typewriter art ("typestracts", late 1800s, largely made by women), a direct influence on Figs.

## The evolution: from the press to the pixel and back to print

The arc has three phases, and the constant is the hand.

Phase 1, the press (pre-2018): letterpress and risograph, learned by hand, sold on Etsy as Pheasant Press. The machine is physical; the mark is impressed.

Phase 2, the photograph under the code (2018-2021): self-taught JavaScript, the "glaem" sketches, then the on-chain sequence Stellar to Rarible/OpenSea to Hic et Nunc to Himinn. The method is photographic: a photo under-layer, pixels sifted for colour and brushstrokes, chaos forced into the system. Himinn (2021) is the hinge -- the photo under-layer is removed because the chain cannot hold it, and she invents the coded underpainting instead. The constraint makes the system.

Phase 3, the system as subject (2022-2025): Memory of Nymphaeas, Grapheme, the Basel live-mint, Ateliers, Figs., Florilege, the MoMI diptych, the zeroone GIFs. Two strands braid and tighten: (a) writing -- asemic script, graphemes, hand-encoded letters, words as coordinates, the fragility of language; (b) nature, painted -- clouds, lilies, fruit, flowers, rendered by algorithmic brushes that behave like paint. What changes: the tool moves from p5.js toward raw JavaScript, the underpainting method matures into the Ateliers pixel-sorting algorithm, the editions shrink (536 to 256 to 161 to 4), and the work returns to physical print (riso, GIFs categorised as printmaking, the 2025 tagline "JavaScript + Risograph"). What stays constant: representational abstraction ("I love taking something familiar and working it into a less precise form, something looser and indefinite"), tiny pixels as the material ("the smaller the better"), highly-ordered chaos as the doctrine, and the hand -- wobbly circles, cursive that cannot be read, brushstrokes that had to be taught to a machine that prefers straight lines.

**Takeaway:** the evolution is a return, not an escape. She leaves the press for code and then brings code back to the press -- letterpress to pixels to risograph -- and each move is driven by the same question, which medium lets the hand show through the machine. The shrinking editions are the tell: as the system gets more capable, she gives it less to say at once.

## M1 -- The coded underpainting

The mechanism is a constraint turned into a system. Her pre-Himinn painting program used a photograph as structure: generate an underpainting from the photo, then let the computer sift the pixel array and surface elements for the final image. A photograph cannot be stored on-chain, so for Himinn -- her first long-form project -- she had to generate the underpainting entirely inside the coded program. The chain's size limit forced the invention: a system that paints its own ground, then paints on it.

The numbers make the mechanism visible, and she states them herself: one Himinn at 2155 x 1212 pixels is approximately 1,142,543 bytes; the entire Himinn code to create ALL the clouds is only 6,595 bytes. "Generative Art is really the ultimate form of image compression!" The program is the painting, compressed 173-to-1. The work asks "an entire possibility space within a painting" and "the minimum amount of form I could give to a cloud so that it would still be recognizable." To keep the on-chain script small she first removed textural elements, then refined the algorithm over time to recover finer, more complex detail -- the system grown back toward the picture, byte by byte.

**Takeaway:** the underpainting is not a background layer. It is the whole picture computed twice -- once as a ground the code invents, once as the painting the code sifts from it. A piece that paints clouds on a sky is a picture; a piece that generates its own sky in 6,595 bytes and then paints clouds out of it is a compression argument.

## M2 -- The pixel-sifting brush

The brush is the signature tool, and it works by sorting, not by drawing. For Ateliers she wanted lines to follow paths made from pixel sorting rather than mathematically calculated positions, and built "a simplified nearest neighbor sorting. This allows the paint to follow a more organic path through the pixels." The paint does not go where a formula sends it; it goes where the pixels lead it, one nearest neighbour at a time. The path is organic because the path is discovered, not computed.

This is the technical form of her stated goal: "furthers my goal to incorporate the human hand into my work with the computer." A mathematically calculated path is the computer's handwriting -- smooth, straight, perfect. A nearest-neighbour path through a pixel field is a walk, and a walk wobbles. The same mechanism powers the underpainting sift of M1: the computer is given freedom to choose which pixels surface, and the choosing is the painting.

**Takeaway:** the brush is not a stroke the code draws; it is a route the code walks through pixels. A piece whose lines follow formulas is a diagram; a piece whose lines follow nearest neighbours through a sifted field is a hand learning to walk again.

## M3 -- The kept miscalculation

The mechanism is an error the artist refuses to fix. While building the Ateliers brush -- the writing-algorithm brushstrokes carried into painting, the breakthrough she had chased for years -- she made "a small miscalculation with part of the brush, and the result was this delicious, sandy texture in the paint." She kept it. The texture is in the work because the bug is in the code, and the bug stays because the texture is delicious.

Note the difference from Zancan's kept bug (M3 of #593, A Bugged Forest): his bug is a co-author that changes what the system grows; hers is a texture that changes what the paint feels like. Same family, different organ. And note what it is not: not randomness for its own sake. She is explicit that "even randomness and noise can start to feel formulaic if you're not careful, so I usually use multiple layers of noise to push the 'imperfect' as much as possible." The miscalculation is not noise; it is a wrong turn that turned out to be a road.

**Takeaway:** the bug is not a defect the artist tolerates; it is a texture she hires. A system that can only do what it was told is a printer; a system with a kept miscalculation has a grain, and grain is what makes paint feel like paint.

## M4 -- Contours last

The mechanism is the order of operations, and reversing it changes everything. "When I first started working on Ateliers, I was painting the lines first, and then filling in the shapes with color. Then, I read an article describing how Matisse would reverse the normal order and add his contours at the end. I decided to try that, and once I reversed the order suddenly everything popped and looked so much more cohesive." Matisse, her long-standing favourite ("His contour line work amazes me"), supplies not a look but a sequence: fill first, line last.

The mechanism matters because it is testable in the work and in a piece: the same shapes, the same colours, the same brush -- drawn in one order they are flat, in the other they cohere. The contour is not decoration added on top; it is the last decision, and the last decision is what the eye reads first. The background is deliberately kept a single block of colour "so the paint's texture and depth take centre stage and the code's role stays visible" -- the field stays quiet so the order of work can be seen.

**Takeaway:** the contour is not a line around a shape; it is the moment the shape becomes a shape. A piece that draws its lines first is a colouring book; a piece that fills first and contours last lets the viewer watch the picture decide what it is.

## M5 -- Asemic writing

The mechanism is writing with the meaning removed and the gesture kept. The Lover's Case (2021, theVERSEverse) is made with her asemic writing algorithm "to explore symbology in dialogue with the meaningless": code-generated cursive-like script that looks like writing but carries no readable meaning. Grapheme (2022, fxhash #20276) extends the strand into long-form -- a grapheme is the smallest unit of a writing system, and the work takes letterforms as the generative atom. Figs. (2024) hand-encodes letters into small coordinate arrays because no font file could be stored on-chain, and builds its shadows out of tiny letters spelling the French names of fruit -- "la peche", "la pomme". The Words Were Only Coordinates (2025, MoMI) grows the strand to museum scale: words reduced to coordinates, meaning held only by position, on a 50-foot wall.

The strand has a human root, and she names it: her son could read a note from his grandmother in cursive; his cousin could not. Cursive is being removed from school curriculums, and the machine is being taught a gesture humans are forgetting. The work is not about text; it is about the hand that writes, preserved as an algorithm after the handwriting itself fades. Figs. is also about what text loses: emojis and shorthand as secret languages, the non-verbal cues text drops, the hours agonised over a single tweet.

**Takeaway:** the writing is not text the machine renders; it is a gesture the machine remembers. A piece that shows words is a poster; a piece that writes cursive nobody can read is a hand preserved in code.

## M6 -- Highly-ordered chaos

The mechanism is her doctrine, stated in her own words: "All my work is the result of highly-ordered chaos. This means that I write very organized instructions to direct the code, but then introduce random parameters so that the computer can try out an infinite variety of results." Order first, chaos second -- the Pearson arc, internalised. The Verse interviewer quotes it back to her and she confirms the framing, linking it to analysis paralysis in her own life: "Using algorithms combined with randomness helps me to let go and embrace the unpredictable."

The mechanism has a discipline inside it. Chaos is not one dial; it is layered. "Even randomness and noise can start to feel formulaic if you're not careful, so I usually use multiple layers of noise to push the 'imperfect' as much as possible." One layer of noise is a texture; several layers is a hand. The difficulty she names at the start is the same difficulty throughout: "I really had to force chaos into the system" because computers draw straight lines too well and organic, natural-looking structures need finesse. The system must be taught to misbehave, and taught carefully, or the misbehaviour looks like a preset.

**Takeaway:** chaos is not the absence of order; it is order with a release valve, layered until the release stops looking like a setting. A piece with one noise layer is a filter; a piece with several, tuned against each other, is a hand letting go.

## M7 -- The live mint as performance

The mechanism is the system generating in public, for a stranger, in real time. At Art Basel Hong Kong 2022, in the Tezos installation "NFTs + The Ever-Evolving World of Art" (Hall 1A, 27-29 May), visitors scanned a QR code and live-minted a 1/1 generative artwork from a participating artist's series; it was projected in the installation and sent to the visitor's wallet as a gift. Five thousand to eight thousand editions were expected across the eight live-mint artists. Her piece took roughly 10 seconds to generate, in JavaScript. Her own words on it: "The sequence of the choices are interpreted by the computer according to the artist's design. The beauty of these choices are determined by the viewer."

The viewer is not a spectator here; the viewer is the seed. The choices are the visitor's (the scan, the moment), the interpretation is the computer's, the design is hers, and the beauty -- she says -- is determined by the viewer. The mint is the performance: the system does its work once, for one person, in front of a room, and then the result leaves with them. The 2025 MoMI fragment mint extends the same idea: a free-mint fragment of The Words Were Only Coordinates, collectible onsite and online, the public taking a piece of the wall home.

**Takeaway:** the mint is not the sale around the art; it is the performance the art is for. A system that generates in private and ships the result is a factory; a system that generates once, in public, for the stranger standing in front of it, is a performance with an audience of one.

## M8 -- The return to print

The mechanism is the loop closing: code output given a physical grain, print given motion. Her homepage tagline from the 2024-25 period reframes the whole practice: "Media artist using JavaScript + Risograph to build systems that explore digital gesture and physical print." The 2024 Code + Riso animation (24 frames, JavaScript code run through risograph) is the print/code loop closed on screen. The 2025 zeroone.art GIFs -- tilt through the paper halo and gesture toward the hum rim, both edition of 4, both categorised as "Creative coding" and "Printmaking" -- are the smallest editions in her catalogue since the 1/1 Nymph, and the first works explicitly filed under both headings at once.

The return is also a measure of the arc: the editions shrink as the system matures (536 for Himinn, 256 for Grapheme, 161 for Ateliers, 4 for the zeroone GIFs), and the work circles back to the medium it started in. She said in 2023, "as a printer myself, I have a particular appreciation for the process of bringing digital work into the analog in a beautiful and tangible way." The 2025 still Be All My Sins Remembered, edition of 27 by Chromium Dioxide Press, is a still from the animated Nymph -- a moving painting stopped, printed, and editioned like a letterpress card.

**Takeaway:** the print is not a souvenir of the code; it is the code's next body. A system that only ever lives on screen is a demonstration; a system that returns to paper -- riso grain, edition of 4, a GIF that is also a print -- has come home to the press it started from.

## Aesthetic logic: the hand inside the machine

The logic is one sentence, hers: "reflecting the human hand in collaboration with the computer." Everything in the practice is a way of getting the hand into a machine that prefers straight lines: wobbly circles taught to code, cursive taught to an algorithm, brushstrokes taught to follow pixels instead of formulas, contours delayed until the end so the picture can cohere like a painting, noise layered until the perfect shape forgets it is perfect. The machine is not the subject; the machine is the press, the second press, and the hand is what is being pressed through it.

Two strands braid through the whole catalogue and never separate: writing and nature. Writing -- asemic script, graphemes, hand-encoded letters, words as coordinates -- is the hand at its most human, the gesture that means nothing and carries everything. Nature -- clouds, lilies, fruit, flowers -- is the subject the hand keeps returning to, painted by brushes that behave like paint. The representational abstraction she names ("I love taking something familiar and working it into a less precise form, something looser and indefinite") is the meeting point: a cloud with the minimum form that is still a cloud, a lily pond painted automatically in the manner of Monet, a fruit still life whose shadows are made of the French names of fruit.

The palette follows the same logic. Colour comes from paintings she admires -- Frankenthaler, de Kooning, Neel -- built into Coolors.co palettes, not from a formula. The picture is built the way a painter builds it: underpainting first, sift second, contour last, background a single quiet block so the paint's texture and depth take centre stage. The code's role stays visible on purpose. That is the difference between her and the formula-first generative artist: the system is not there to find a beautiful equation; it is there to hold a brush the way a hand would, badly at first, then with practice, then with a miscalculation kept because it feels like sand.

## Works catalogue

**W01 -- Letterpress printing / Pheasant Press (c. 2010s, pre-code).** Self-published / Etsy shop "Pheasant Press" (greeting cards, wedding invitations). Letterpress on a second-hand press she taught herself to operate. The pre-code practice the whole arc grows out of: no formal art background, letterpress the hobby that balanced law school, the self-teaching method (Google, forums, trial and error) later applied to code. The press is the first "machine that makes marks" in her practice.

**W02 -- Early generative sketches as "glaem" (late 2017 / early 2018 - 2019).** Instagram (@glaem), self-published sketches, no chain. JavaScript / p5.js, self-taught via Daniel Shiffman's Coding Train; calligraphy in Procreate on iPad just before coding. The method from the start was photographic, not mathematical: a photograph as the under-layer, the program exploring the photograph's pixels and using selected ones to define colour and brushstrokes -- a process she likens to glitch art and pixel manipulation. The early problem was already the signature one: "I really had to force chaos into the system."

**W03 -- Valentines series (2019, Stellar).** Her first blockchain experience, a series of Valentines released on Stellar. Thinly evidenced (a single sentence in the Verse profile, echoed by Electric Artefacts' "experimented with Blockchain since 2019"), but it fixes the start of her on-chain practice two years before Himinn.

**W04 -- Rarible / OpenSea singles (2020).** Selling single generative works on the Ethereum marketplaces before moving to Tezos. No titles, images, or descriptions were found; the entry exists only to keep the chronology honest.

**W05 -- Hic et Nunc works (2020-2021).** Tezos entry point, in the early Hic et Nunc community she later describes as "just lovely." No individual titles were recoverable this run (platform dead, no archive read). The platform move matters: Tezos' low cost let her experiment freely, the precondition for the 2022 long-form Tezos projects.

**W06 -- Himinn (30 September 2021, Art Blocks).** Project 171; Art Blocks' own page lists it as "An Art Blocks 500 release, in the Factory category" -- not Curated, despite the shorthand in earlier candidate lines (C1755). Fixed edition of 536 unique artworks. JavaScript; "a combination of many different algorithms" that "relies more on an artist-defined system than any particular math algorithm." Output 2155 x 1212 px; one output about 1,142,543 bytes, the entire program 6,595 bytes. Her first long-form generative project and the breakthrough: a painterly cloud study exploring light, colour, and the generative composition of clouds, "probing an entire possibility space within a painting" and "the minimum amount of form I could give to a cloud so that it would still be recognizable." "Himinn" means "a sky." Exhibited at The Digital (Miami Art Week 2021), Perceive(Light) (Art Blocks Gallery, Marfa 2022), Personal Structures (Venice Biennale 2022), Collecting the Future (Assembly, Houston 2023); physical prints available on request.

**W07 -- The Lover's Case (2021, theVERSEverse on Foundation).** Visual poetry made with her asemic writing algorithm "to explore symbology in dialogue with the meaningless": code-generated cursive-like script that looks like writing but carries no readable meaning. The first named work in the writing strand -- the cursive-like marks, the preservation-of-cursive concern (her son could read his grandmother's cursive note; his cousin could not), the machine taught a human gesture as a visual system.

**W08 -- Memory of Nymphaeas (2022, Endless Ways / Tezos).** Her first on-chain generative Tezos project, by her own account; Endless Ways is now closed, but "my work and code will always live on." Edition count not evidenced (numbered outputs #30 and #89 are illustrated in the TZ APAC article, so the edition runs to at least 89). A JavaScript program that "paints water lilies automatically in a style reminiscent of Claude Monet." The second long-form system after Himinn, moving the subject from sky to water -- and the seed algorithm for the later Nymph work (W11).

**W09 -- Grapheme (2022, fxhash #20276).** fxhash generative token #20276; 256/256 minted (first sales 12.88K tez, 135 second sales at time of the page crawl). One of the three fxhash collections Verse credits her with in 2022, and the only one whose title was recoverable this run (C1759). The title is the mechanism: a grapheme is the smallest unit of a writing system, and the work extends the asemic-writing strand into long-form -- letterforms as the generative atom. Visual specifics are not described in any source read; the fxhash project page itself did not load beyond marketplace stats. Her Basel reporting says her fxhash work "takes roughly 10 seconds to generate."

**W10 -- Art Basel Hong Kong 2022 live-mint series (May 2022, fxhash / Tezos).** 27-29 May 2022 (invite-only 24-30 May), Hong Kong Convention & Exhibition Centre, Hall 1A, in "NFTs + The Ever-Evolving World of Art" (TZ APAC / Tezos), 250 sq m. Visitors scanned a QR code, a new 1/1 generative artwork was minted, projected in the installation, and sent to the visitor's wallet as a gift; 5,000-8,000 editions expected across the eight live-mint artists. Her own words: "The sequence of the choices are interpreted by the computer according to the artist's design. The beauty of these choices are determined by the viewer." The series title was not found in any source read -- the reporting names the installation and the artists, not her series title -- so this entry is the event, not a titled work. The first time her system generated in public, for strangers, in real time: the mint as performance.

**W11 -- Nymph, in Thy Orisons (undated in sources; after 2022).** Edition of 1; custom generative software, JavaScript/HTML/CSS, 9:16, colour, silent; an animated painting hosted on Arweave and listed by Electric Artefacts. Flowers bloom then fade, "inscribing and overwriting themselves in a constant attempt at a permanence that can never be found." The title quotes Hamlet ("Nymph, in thy orisons be all my sins remember'd"), and the same line gives the 2025 still its title (see coda). The most explicitly temporal work in the catalogue: not a picture of flowers but a system of blooming and erasure. No year is stated for it in any source read; placement after Nymphaeas rests only on the Chromium Dioxide Press note's statement that it derives from an extension of the Nymphaeas algorithm (C1758).

**W12 -- Ateliers (2 March 2023, Verse).** Released 2 March 2023, 18:00 UTC; 161 artworks; part of the group exhibition "Imperfections." JavaScript and p5.js. The new painting algorithm: generate an underpainting, then let the computer sift through the pixel array and select elements to surface; lines follow paths made from pixel sorting via a simplified nearest-neighbour sort. A small miscalculation in part of the brush produced "this delicious, sandy texture in the paint" -- kept, not fixed. The cube, made un-boring: from Sol LeWitt ("the most interesting characteristic of the cube is that it is relatively uninteresting"), the algorithm breaks the cube into subsidiary partitions and generates a malleable space where boundaries are removed. The breakthrough she had been chasing: finally getting the brushstrokes from her writing algorithms into painting. Contours last, after Matisse, and "suddenly everything popped." Background a single block of colour so the paint's texture and depth take centre stage. Colour from Frankenthaler, de Kooning, Neel via Coolors.co palettes built from paintings she admires.

**W13 -- Viridaria (2023).** Title only: listed in the Generative Art Index under the p5.js tool ("Viridaria, sarah-ridgley, 2023") and nowhere else found this run. No platform, link, or description evidenced. The Latin suggests a green place / pleasure-garden, which would fit the nature strand, but that is etymology, not evidence about the work, and no visual description is offered here.

**W14 -- Figs. (2024, Bright Moments Paris).** Made for Bright Moments Paris (Feb 2024). JavaScript generative program; no font file could be used (on-chain size limits), so she hand-encoded the letters into small coordinate arrays. Still life as a communication system: letter-filled screens with painterly shapes and distorted, surrealist shadows revealing the fruit; the shadows are composed of tiny letters -- the French names of each fruit ("la peche", "la pomme"). The background words convey the essence of fruit through related sensations, so the piece is meant to be sensed -- touched, smelled, tasted -- as much as seen. The subject is text-based communication itself: emojis and shorthand as secret languages, the non-verbal cues text loses. The title plays four ways at once: fruit, figurative, the "fig." abbreviation of botanical illustration, and figuration. Draws on typewriter art ("typestracts", late 1800s, mostly made by women). No figs appear in the project -- and no strawberries; she is allergic to them.

**W15 -- 0110 (2024, Bright Moments Finale Collection, Venice).** Named only in her news archive as a 2024 series for the Bright Moments Finale Collection in Venice. The title "0110" reads as binary, which would fit the code/text strand, but that is a guess about the title, not evidence about the work, and no description is offered here.

**W16 -- Florilege (8 July 2024, Art on Ledger Stax).** Burn-to-mint (burn 1 Art on Ledger Stax Mint Pass); max supply 4,779 works across the five artists in the collection (Studio Yorktown, Andreas Gysin, Linda Dounia, Sarah Ridgley, Sasha Stiles); her individual supply not evidenced. Each edition has both a Stax version and a full version (per the sibling piece's description of the shared format -- not directly evidenced for Florilege itself). A florilegium -- a gathering of flowers, a flower book -- made for the Ledger Stax's small e-ink screen and its full version. "Florilege #1" was circulating on X on launch day. Visual specifics are not described in any source read.

**W17 -- Code + Riso animation, 24 frames (2024).** Self-published on her site (sarahridgley.com); video file uploaded December 2024. JavaScript code + risograph printing; 24 frames. The print/code loop closed on screen -- code output run through risograph, the physical print medium she worked in before code. It anticipates the 2024 Art Blocks Marfa "Riso Fun" workshop and the 2025 zeroone GIFs: the pixel given a physical grain, the print given motion. Her homepage tagline from the same period reframes the whole practice: "Media artist using JavaScript + Risograph to build systems that explore digital gesture and physical print." (Her homepage header is dated 13 September 2024 while the animation file it embeds is dated December 2024 -- the page's own dating is internally inconsistent, likely a later file upload onto an older page revision; the work is 2024 either way. See C1762.)

**W18 -- The Words Were Only Coordinates (2025, Museum of the Moving Image).** On view 8 May - 29 June 2025 as the LIA & Sarah Ridgley diptych in "Compositions in Code: The Art of Processing and p5.js" (6 Mar - 23 Aug 2025), Herbert S. **W19 -- tilt through the paper halo (11 April 2025, zeroone.art).** Edition of 4; sold out. GIF; categories "Creative coding" and "Printmaking."
