# Barbara Kruger: Study Notes

**Site:** Barbara Kruger -- born January 26, 1945, in Newark, New Jersey; lives and works in New York and Los Angeles -- American artist of the Pictures Generation who takes a found photograph and lays a sentence across it until the photograph says the opposite of what it sold. ## The life: the art director who kept the layout and changed the client

Barbara Kruger was born in Newark, New Jersey, in 1945 -- January 26 in nearly every source read (Wikipedia, Britannica, the Jewish Women's Archive, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Spencer Museum, the National Museum of Women in the Arts, which posted a birthday note on January 26, 2021); the brief's January 17 was corroborated by no source found in any leg and is carried as an open conflict. She was an only child in a working-class family: her father a chemical technician for Shell Oil, her mother a legal secretary. She grew up in the South Ward of Newark, graduated from Weequahic High School, and in her own words grew up in "a three-room apartment in a Black neighborhood" -- an experience she said shaped her ideas about "power, control, hierarchy, and marginalization." In 1964 she enrolled at Syracuse University as an art major and left after one year on her father's death; in 1965 she enrolled at Parsons School of Design in New York, studying art and design with photographer Diane Arbus and graphic designer Marvin Israel. She did not graduate from either institution and held no degree when she entered magazine publishing in 1966. (C20908, leg 1 C20700-C20702; synthesis C20850)

Kruger joined Conde Nast in 1966, working in the design department of Mademoiselle magazine, and was promoted to head designer within a year, at the age of twenty-two. She later worked part-time as a picture editor at House and Garden, Aperture, and other publications, and freelanced designing book jackets. She worked in graphic design until the mid-1970s while also painting and experimenting with textiles, and she herself called the design years "the biggest influence on my work... [it] became, with a few adjustments, my 'work' as an artist." The design career is not a prelude to the art; it is the art's operating system. The paste-up -- a found image, a cropped layout, a headline set in type -- is a magazine page with the client removed and the artist's own sentence put in the headline's place. (C20909, leg 1 C20703; synthesis C20855)

Her earliest artworks date to 1969: large wall hangings incorporating yarn, beads, sequins, feathers, and ribbons, a feminist reclamation of craft. Her work was included in the 1973 Whitney Biennial (textile pieces, selected by Marcia Tucker). Her first solos were at Artists Space, New York (1974), Fischbach Gallery, New York (1975), John Doyle Gallery, Chicago (1976), and Ohio State University (1977). Dissatisfied with work she felt lacked meaning, she quit making art entirely in 1976, moved to Berkeley, California, taught at the University of California, Berkeley, and steeped herself in Walter Benjamin and Roland Barthes. She resumed in 1977, photographing modest California houses, and self-published the artist's book Picture/Readings in 1978 (1979 per some sources -- carry both). In 1979 she developed the signature practice: large-scale black-and-white found photographs overlaid with declarative text, usually white on red. Her first works combining appropriated photographs with superimposed text fragments were shown at P.S. 1 in 1979 (1980 per the Zwirner CV -- carry both). Her first institutional solo was at the Institute of Contemporary Arts, London, in 1983: "We Won't Play Nature to Your Culture," touring to Bristol, Villeurbanne, and Kunsthalle Basel. (C20910, leg 1 C20704-C20706)

The exhibition spine runs in CV order: Whitney Biennials 1973, 1983, 1985, 1987, 1991, 2000, 2008; Documenta 7 (1982), Documenta 8 (1987), Documenta 12 (2007); Venice Biennale 1982 (representing the United States), 2005 (a vinyl mural across the Italian pavilion facade, with the words "money" and "power"), 2022 (Untitled (Beginning/Middle/End) in the Arsenale). The first retrospective was organised by the Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles, curated by Ann Goldstein, at the Geffen Contemporary, October 17, 1999-February 13, 2000, travelling to the Whitney, July 13-October 22, 2000 -- the brief's "LACMA 1999-2000" is wrong; LACMA hosted the later survey, not this one. The large survey "Barbara Kruger: Thinking of You. I Mean Me. I Mean You." opened at the Art Institute of Chicago, September 19, 2021-January 24, 2022, travelled to LACMA, March 20-July 17, 2022, and was presented at MoMA as a large-scale site-specific atrium commission, July 2022-January 2, 2023 (MoMA's own page gives July 16; the Art Institute press release gives July 18 -- carry both). Awards, CV-corroborated: CAPS Grant 1976-77; NEA Grant 1983 (1983-84 in the Spruth Magers and Mary Boone CVs); Wexner Center artist in residence 1995-96; MOCA Award to Distinguished Women in the Arts 2001; Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement, 51st Venice Biennale, 2005; Goslarer Kaiserring 2019; Time 100 in 2021; CAA Distinguished Artist Award for Lifetime Achievement 2023; Art Basel Icon Artist 2026. A National Medal of Arts 2021 is claimed by exactly one weak design-history site and contradicted by the published 2021 NEA honoree list, which does not include her -- do not claim it. (C20911, leg 1 C20707-C20710, C20713, C20716-C20717; synthesis C20851)

Dealers, by solo exhibitions -- no source read states a formal dated representation contract: Gagosian Gallery first (Los Angeles shows 1982 and 1983); Monika Spruth Galerie, Cologne, from 1985, Spruth Magers continuously since; Mary Boone Gallery from 1987 (first solo that year) or 1988 (Wikipedia and the Mary Boone article) -- carry both -- its first woman artist, nine solos, until the gallery closed in 2019; David Zwirner from November 21, 2019, announced in collaboration with Spruth Magers, first Zwirner show June 30-August 12, 2022 across three New York locations. Current representation: Spruth Magers and David Zwirner. No source found in any leg links Kruger to Sperone Westwater as a dealer or representative, despite targeted searching -- do not claim it. She also showed with Rhona Hoffman Gallery, Chicago, and L&M Arts, Los Angeles. Teaching: University of California, Berkeley; California Institute of the Arts; School of the Art Institute of Chicago; Whitney Museum Independent Study Program; University of California, San Diego, for five years; then UCLA, where she is now Emerita Distinguished Professor of New Genres. She wrote film, television, and music columns for Artforum and REALLIFE Magazine, designed magazine covers from 1992 on (Ms., Esquire, Newsweek, The New Republic), and in 2017 made a Performa 17 pop-up shop and limited-edition MetroCards for New York's MTA. (C20912, leg 1 C20711-C20712, C20719; synthesis C20859)


## M1 -- The text bar slapped across the found photograph

Take a black-and-white found photograph -- a 1950s ad, a magazine page, a camera annual, a film still -- crop it hard, lay a solid bar, usually red, sometimes black or white, across it, and set a short sentence in white Futura Bold Oblique (or Helvetica Ultra Condensed) inside the bar. Add a red border or a red enamel frame. The bar is not a caption under the picture; it sits ON the picture and breaks it. The early paste-ups were small monochrome pre-digital works, the largest 11 by 13 inches, altered found images plus texts culled from media or invented, a negative of each made to produce enlarged versions -- the cut-and-paste method straight from her Mademoiselle editorial-design work. In "Untitled (You are not yourself)" (W04, 1981) the text zigzags to follow a shattered mirror instead of sitting in one bar, which proves the rule by breaking it: the bar goes where the picture breaks. In "Untitled (We don't need another hero)" (W08, 1987) a red border frames a 1950s-style photograph of a boy flexing his bicep while a girl pokes his arm, and the red banner cuts across it. The eye reads the sentence first, then re-reads the picture through the sentence, and the picture's original sales pitch collapses. The same device works at 11 inches and at 30 feet because the bar scales and the photo stays legible as a type. (C20913, leg 2 C20751-C20752, C20756, C20759; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** lay the sentence across the picture, not under it, and the sentence reads first and the picture's sales pitch collapses second. (C20914)


## M2 -- Pronoun direct address: the unstable "you"

Build every sentence around I / you / we / they, with no stable speaker. "You are not yourself", "Your body is a battleground", "Your gaze hits the side of my face", "I shop therefore I am" -- the pronoun points at the viewer, at the pictured figure, and at Kruger herself at once, so nobody can settle who is accusing whom. The viewer is caught mid-look and has to answer the sentence as if it were spoken to them personally; two viewers standing together get accused differently. In the profile works the averted, side-glancing figure refuses to meet the stare while the text confronts the viewer straight on: "Your gaze hits the side of my face" (W03, 1981) names the gaze as a physical hit that only lands on the side of the face -- it bounces off. "Untitled (Know nothing, Believe anything, Forget everything)" (W10, 1987/2014) stacks three pronoun-free commands in three red bands across a woman's face in profile, and the absence of a pronoun is the same move in reverse: the commands have no speaker, so they could be anyone's, including the viewer's own. (C20915, leg 2 C20751, C20755, C20761, C20778; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** point the pronoun at the viewer and refuse to say who is speaking, and the sentence accuses everyone in the room at once. (C20916)


## M3 -- The slogan as verdict

Compress a whole argument into one short declarative sentence that lands like a ruling, not a suggestion: "I shop therefore I am", "Your body is a battleground", "We don't need another hero", "Admit nothing. Blame everyone. Be bitter." The sentence is usually a twist on a familiar formula -- Descartes, a proverb, a pop lyric, a political chant -- so the viewer recognises the shape and then trips on the substituted word. "I shop therefore I am" (W09, 1987) swaps thinking for shopping in Descartes and sets "therefore" smaller, forcing a pause after "I shop"; the hand on the card holds the sentence like a credit card. "Your body is a battleground" (W12, 1989) splits a woman's face into positive and negative halves down the middle, and the sentence cuts the line the picture already drew. The line is instantly repeatable -- it leaves the wall on posters, bags, T-shirts, and protest signs and keeps working without the picture. That portability is the point: the verdict travels. Kruger and students plastered the battleground poster around Manhattan the night before the Women's March on Washington in April 1989, and it has been reprinted ever since, including a French version ("Savoir c'est pouvoir") and a Wexner Center billboard version in 1990 installed next to an anti-abortion billboard. (C20917, leg 2 C20760, C20762-C20763; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** twist a familiar formula into one short verdict, and the line leaves the wall and keeps working without the picture. (C20918)


## M4 -- The question as weapon

Replace the verdict with an open question that has no supplied answer -- "What do we own?", "What is the same?", "Who is beyond the law?", "Who speaks? Who is silent?", "Who is free to choose?" -- print the question as large as a statement, and leave it hanging. Whole installations are built from nothing but questions. The Flue centerfold (W02, December 1980) is two questions and mostly blank paper: Alberto Moravia's short story "The Wardrobe" photographed in four quadrants between black bars, the opening and closing pages almost blank white, each carrying one small question between black bars, signed by four names -- Moravia, Kruger, Lawler, Levine. "Untitled (Questions)" (W13, 1989-90) painted nine questions in the colours of the American flag across the south wall of the Temporary Contemporary in Los Angeles, reinstalled at the Geffen Contemporary 2018-2020 and re-imagined city-wide for Frieze LA 2020. "Belief+Doubt" (W19, 2012) covered about 6,700 square feet of the Hirshhorn lobby -- walls, floor, escalator sides -- in red, white, and black vinyl, lettering up to 12 feet high, and the visitor only reads the whole thing by walking through it: "BELIEF + DOUBT = SANITY" on the escalator descent, floor questions ("WHO IS BEYOND THE LAW? WHO IS FREE TO CHOOSE? WHO SPEAKS? WHO IS SILENT?"), and on the bookstore side, "YOU WANT IT. YOU BUY IT. YOU FORGET IT." The viewer cannot nod and move on; a question demands a mental reply, and in a room full of questions the replies collide. Blank space around the question makes the silence part of the work. (C20919, leg 2 C20754, C20764, C20772; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** print a question as large as a statement and give it no answer, and the viewer has to answer it themselves. (C20920)


## M5 -- Image-text friction: the picture says one thing, the words say another

Deliberately mismatch the found image and the overlaid text so neither can settle the other. Howdy Doody, the friendly sales-puppet, announces that culture is bought with a checkbook (W07, 1985): the text riffs on Hans Johst's line "Whenever I hear the word 'Culture,' I reach for my revolver" via Godard's "Le Mepris" (1963), the gun becomes a checkbook, and the puppet that sold Wonder Bread, Hostess Twinkies, and Halo shampoo now announces that culture is bought. The work sold at Christie's in 2011 for $902,500, then a record for the artist. A boy flexing for an admiring girl is told no hero is needed (W08). A woman's leaf-covered eyes refuse the nature/culture assignment the sentence names: "We won't play nature to your culture" (W06, 1983) lays a model's face on grass, eyes closed, each eye covered by a single leaf, and the text cuts diagonally across. A shattered mirror agrees with the sentence "You are not yourself" and also contradicts it -- she IS herself, split (W04). The viewer flips back and forth between picture and text trying to resolve them, and the flip itself is the experience; the work never resolves into a single message. This is the mechanism that separates Kruger from a poster: a poster aligns image and text, she collides them. (C20921, leg 2 C20756-C20759; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** collide the picture with the words so they never settle, and the flip between them is the work. (C20922)


## M6 -- Scale shift: the same sentence from paste-up to building

The work is designed as a layout, not an object, so the identical sentence-and-image unit is re-set at every size: an 11 by 13 inch hand-cut paste-up, a gallery photomontage at 72 by 48 inches (W04), a billboard (W08, November 1988-January 1989; W12), a bus shelter (W14, February-April 1991, three posters of stylised American men with "HELP!" superimposed, imagining men and women both conceiving and delivering children), a building banner (W15, 2000, a 1950s-era woman holding a magnifying glass to her eye across from the Port Authority Bus Terminal), an elevator shaft about 70 to 86 feet tall (W16, "Shafted," 2008, LACMA: a pair of beady eyes above the Orwell quote "If you want a PICTURE of the future, imagine a boot stomping on a human face, forever," a litany of products running the shaft -- MOISTURIZER, COMPUTER, SNEAKERS, SWEATERS -- bisected by "PLENTY SHOULD BE ENOUGH - RIGHT?"), a glass lobby (W17, "Between Being Born and Dying," 2009, Lever House windows inside and out, letters up to 17 feet high, black and white only), 6,700 square feet of lobby vinyl (W19), a four-story atrium about 110 feet high (W20, MoMA 2022-23, image-free text only, the vinyl peeled up and the surfaces repainted after closing). The negative of a paste-up was made to be enlarged from the start. The viewer's body changes role with the scale -- reader of a page, passer-by of a billboard, rider inside an elevator shaft, walker inside a sentence at the Hirshhorn and MoMA. The sentence does not get louder; the viewer gets smaller inside it. (C20923, leg 2 C20752, C20766-C20767, C20769-C20770, C20772-C20773; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** re-set the same sentence at every size, and the viewer shrinks inside it instead of the sentence getting louder. (C20924)


## M7 -- Appropriation with a re-caption: stealing the ad's voice to talk back

Take an image that already exists in mass media -- a 1950s ad, a classical bust, a detergent-era housewife, a Moravia short story, the US Pledge of Allegiance, marriage vows, a will -- and keep it recognisable while replacing its original caption with Kruger's own text. Nothing is drawn from scratch; the found material carries its own authority and Kruger hijacks it. This is subversive mimicry: use the dominant advertising medium to criticise the patriarchal and consumer structures that medium serves. "Messages to the Public" (W05, November 1983) is the mechanism without a picture at all: a 30-second scrolling text animation on the Times Square Spectacolor board, repeated more than 50 times a day -- "I'm not trying to sell you anything. I just want you to think about what you see when you watch the news on T.V." -- an ad slot that refuses to sell anything. The work is political without persuading the viewer toward any system; it rattles a position the viewer already holds. The instant familiarity (the viewer has seen this picture before) is followed by a jolt (it now says the opposite of what it sold). (C20925, leg 2 C20754-C20755, C20758, C20760, C20765, C20779; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** keep the ad recognisable, replace its caption with your own sentence, and the familiar picture says the opposite of what it sold. (C20926)


## M8 -- Replay / remediation: the static paste-up learns to move and sound

From 2019 Kruger remakes her 1980s paste-ups as animated single-channel LED videos with sound -- "replays": the image shatters into puzzle pieces and rebuilds itself ("I shop therefore I am," 1987/2019, 57 seconds; "Your body is a battleground," 1989/2019, 1:04), words change one at a time to their opposites ("Admit nothing / Blame everyone / Be bitter," 1987/2020, 17 seconds), familiar texts are typed on screen as if composed and revised in real time ("Pledge, Will, Vow," 1988/2020). The multi-channel turn came earlier: "The Globe Shrinks" (W18, 2010) puts the viewer inside four walls of competing video and text -- performers delivering commentary, stand-up comedy, road rage, prayer gatherings, religious rituals, a woman on a cellphone swerving on a highway -- between which Kruger's text panels flash imperatives: TEMPT IT, SHOVE IT, KISS IT, FEAR IT, BELIEVE IT, SHAME IT, DOUBT IT, BLAME IT, BUY IT, LOVE IT, BLIND IT; about 13 minutes looped (12:44 per Art Observed). "Untitled (No Comment)" (2020) mimics doomscrolling -- memes, cats, selfies, hair tutorials -- in a three-channel barrage. The viewer can no longer take the sentence in at a glance; they must wait, watch it break and reform, and listen. The slogan becomes a process instead of a verdict, and the work admits that its own 1980s statements are now part of the media flow they criticised. (C20927, leg 2 C20762, C20771, C20774-C20775; synthesis C20855)

**Takeaway:** let the slogan break apart and rebuild itself on screen, and the verdict becomes a process the viewer has to wait for. (C20928)


## The evolution: the layout outgrows the page, then the building

Early (1969-79): craft and photography, no found image yet -- woven wall hangings, then modest California houses photographed by Kruger herself and set against short prose panels in Picture/Readings (W01, 1978-79), the word/image pairing as prototype. Middle (1979-89): the paste-up decade -- the Flue centerfold (W02), the shattered mirror (W04), the Times Square text animation (W05), Howdy Doody with a checkbook (W07), the hero billboard (W08), the shopping card (W09), the battleground poster (W12). The sentence is the work and the photograph is its host, and the work leaves the gallery on posters, bags, and billboards. Late (1989-2010): the question replaces the verdict and the scale replaces the frame -- nine questions on a museum wall (W13), bus shelters asking men for help (W14), a building banner (W15), an elevator shaft (W16), a glass lobby (W17), four walls of video (W18). Latest (2012-23): the image drops out entirely -- Belief+Doubt (W19) and the MoMA atrium (W20) are text only, and the replays (M8) admit the 1980s slogans into the media flow they criticised. What does not change across fifty years: a found image or a bare wall, a declarative sentence in Futura Bold Oblique, a pronoun that points at the viewer, and a scale that keeps growing until the viewer is standing inside the sentence. (C20929, leg 2 C20753-C20754, C20765, C20771-C20773; synthesis C20856)

## Aesthetic logic: the sentence is the work, the photograph is the host

Kruger's aesthetic logic is that a sentence is a complete work and a photograph is only its host. Everything in the practice serves that one equation. The found image is never drawn, never photographed by her, never original -- it is chosen, cropped, and captioned, because the image already has authority and the sentence hijacks it. The red bar is not decoration; it is the editorial device that makes the sentence the first thing read, the way a magazine headline is the first thing read. The pronoun is not a style; it is the delivery mechanism that makes a sentence printed on a wall into a sentence spoken to one person. The scale is not presentation; it is content -- a slogan at 11 inches is a remark, the same slogan at 110 feet is an environment, and the viewer standing inside it is the work's subject. That is why the work survives being copied image by image -- on posters, bags, T-shirts, matchbooks, buses, building facades -- and fails when it is shown as one picture without its sentence: the system is the sentence, and the sentence needs its host. The palette follows the same logic: black-and-white found photography, white type, one red -- the colours of a newspaper, a warning sign, and a sale tag at once. The colour is never decoration; it is the ad's own colour, turned against the ad. The design training is the whole explanation and the whole limit. Kruger learned at Mademoiselle how a layout makes a reader stop, read, and believe, and she has spent fifty years running that machine with the client removed. The machine is so good at stopping a reader that it works even when the sentence is a question with no answer, even when there is no picture at all, even when the sentence is the whole building. What the machine cannot do is whisper: every Kruger is a headline, and a headline cannot be quiet. A prompt that takes the red bar and leaves the sentence will make a poster; a prompt that takes the sentence and leaves the pronoun will make a slogan; a prompt that takes both will make a Kruger. (C20931)
