# Hubert Kiecol: Study Notes

**Site:** Hubert Kiecol (b. 26 October 1950, Bremen-Blumenthal, Germany; living), German sculptor and graphic artist; post-minimalist / post-concrete sculpture and large-format graphic works -- small concrete houses, stairs and courtyards set on the floor, black drawings of ordinary buildings, steel reliefs that read as signs, and tableaus recombined from his own archive. Kiecol was born 26 October 1950 in Bremen-Blumenthal, and before art school he trained as a typesetter (Schriftsetzer) and worked as a journeyman (Geselle) -- the training itself is undated, and nothing was found about his family, his childhood in Bremen-Blumenthal, or his schooling before the apprenticeship. From 1971 he attended the Werkkunstschule Hamburg, where his teacher was Gisela Buehrmann; from 1975 he studied at the Hochschule fuer bildende Kuenste Hamburg, with Hans Thiemann, Kai Sudeck, Gotthard Graubner, Sigmar Polke and Ulrich Rueckriem named as his teachers there. The biography also records travels to Crete and Egypt, without dates, and no graduation or diploma year is recorded in any source read. One auction-house summary writes "Gotthard Graupner"; the German Wikipedia gives Gotthard Graubner, the correct name of the well-known painter -- a transcription error in a secondary source, not a second teacher. [C27244]

The arc is a slow lightening. His early preferred material was concrete; his later work moves to the opposite of that density, the lightness and brightness of glass houses and glass windows. The German Wikipedia characterises his position in his own terms: sculpture is not something added to a situation, a room or a motif, but an attitude ("Sculptur ist eine Haltung"), expressed through faithfulness to the material and perfection of making; "the other side of everyday life" ("Die andere Seite der Alltaeglichkeit") was always the starting point, accompanied by works on paper and prints, some in large format. His materials are everyday materials taken from the built environment -- concrete, wood and steel -- and his motifs are everyday architectural things such as stairs, windows and houses. His graphic work is a major second field alongside sculpture, not a side activity: the Museum fuer Gegenwartskunst Siegen's 2010 exhibition "Golden" placed selected sculptural pieces "into context with the artist's other major thematic field, the graphic works", including large-format drawings, wood prints, screen-prints and etchings. The museum describes his position as "post-minimalist" or "post-concrete" sculpture, whose works "oscillate between mass and fragility, heaviness and lightness, openness and unity, interior and exterior space". ARTZUID makes the break with the previous generation explicit: where sculptures by Carl Andre or Donald Judd were autonomous and not intended to interact with the viewer, and metaphors and associations were considered reprehensible, Kiecol "leaves the viewer free to interpret and discover his work". Exhibiting began in Germany from 1981, and abroad from 1988. He has realised numerous projects of art in public spaces, among them Die hohe Treppe, 1991, concrete, on the forecourt of the Badische Landesbibliothek in Karlsruhe. [C27245]

In 1993 Kiecol was appointed professor at the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf, his chair Professor for Integration, Bildende Kunst und Architektur (Integration, Fine Art and Architecture). The end year is split: the Academy's own official list of former professors gives "1993 bis 2016", while Artsy, in a biography submitted by Haeusler Contemporary, says "Until 2015, Hubert Kiecol was professor at Kunstakademie Duesseldorf". This study writes 1993-2016, the Academy's own list, with 2015 carried as the conflicting secondary-source figure -- possibly his last full teaching year before retirement took effect in 2016. His prizes run steadily through the career: a Mies-van-der-Rohe-Stipendium in 1984, an Annemarie- und Will Grohmann-Stipendium in 1985, the Will-Grohmann-Preis of the Akademie der Kuenste Berlin in 1991, and the Wolfgang-Hahn-Preis fuer Gegenwartskunst of the Gesellschaft fuer Moderne Kunst am Museum Ludwig in Koeln in 2000, the last confirmed by three sources read in this study. [C27246]

The evolution is a vocabulary that never changes and a scale that keeps being renegotiated. Early (1984-1987, W01-W06): small, hand-scale objects and black drawings -- concrete houses, courtyards and industrial buildings of about 25-28 cm standing on the floor or on a base, and charcoal drawings reducing an ordinary house side to one black geometric shape. The early move is subtraction: one architectural element, taken out of its building, made in a common material with no ornament, placed so that scale does the talking. The Saint Louis note makes the intended effect explicit: structural features removed from spatial context leave the viewer slightly disoriented and bewildered. Middle (1994-2012, W07-W16): the same vocabulary tested in new containers and at new scales -- a concrete stair put inside a printed box as an edition of 110 (W07); prints pairing Kiecol with another artist (W09); graphic works growing to large format and hung singly, in pairs and in rows, developing fundamental variations (W10, W11); site work entering with Rheinwein (W15), a relief made for a specific wall that quotes that building's windows. Recent (2014 onward, W18-W20): two directions at once -- outward to public scale and backward into the artist's own archive. Bundesbank (W19) stacks nine boxes and eighteen slabs in open landscape, the largest piling in the twenty; the tableaus (W20) recombine drawings, photographs and prints made since the 1970s into new imaginary spaces. What does not change across the forty years is the vocabulary: houses, stairs, windows, gates and fences, reduced, in concrete, wood, steel or black on paper. [C27248]

## M1 -- Architectural extraction

Take one functional element out of a building -- a stair, a house, a fence, a courtyard, an industrial block -- and present it alone, with no building attached. W02, Alter Hof (Old Courtyard) (1985), is two parts, each concrete, 25.5 x 12 x 21 cm, shown at Museum Haus Esters, Krefeld, 1985 and the Stedelijk, 2000, hammer EUR 14,000 at VAN HAM in 2021 against a EUR 3,500-4,500 estimate. W03, Industrial Building (1985), is concrete, 28 x 23.5 x 14 cm, accompanied by a handwritten certificate by the artist reading "Industrial building concrete height 28cm 1985 Hubert Kiecol", its foot minimally chipped at two corners. W05, Stair with Base (1986, cement), was shown beside a black charcoal drawing in the BOMB Magazine Koeln survey of 1987, courtesy of Galerie Max Hetzler. W06, Untitled (1987, charcoal, 45 x 30 cm, Saint Louis Art Museum, object 58:2003), is "a striking black and white drawing of a geometric, simplified shape representing the side of an ordinary house", part of an untitled series of drawings featuring common buildings and isolated structural elements; the museum notes that "black is appropriate for the building the form represents" and that the drawing "refers to sculptures the artist was making at the time". The watchable behaviour is the naming that fails: the viewer first names the element ("a stair", "a house") and then finds no use for it -- no floor above, no door to open -- so attention falls back onto the shape itself and onto the missing context.

**Takeaway:** extract one element and leave it useless; the missing building is the subject. [C27249]

## M2 -- Floor placement and scale shift

Small concrete houses and stairs are placed directly on the floor instead of on a plinth, at a size (about 25-28 cm in W02 and W03) far below the real thing. W18, the early houses and stair sculptures shown at Haeusler Contemporary, "Early", 2014, is the anchor: "the houses stand directly on the floor, with the scale being an important part of the metaphor", and the works "question the environment and the way we function". The watchable behaviour is the mismatch between body and eye: the viewer looks down at what should tower overhead; the body reads "building" while the eye reads "model", and the mismatch -- scale as metaphor -- is the work's main event.

**Takeaway:** put the building on the floor at hand scale; the mismatch is the work. [C27250]

## M3 -- Stacking and piling

Build the sculpture by piling countable units -- nine boxes with eighteen slabs in Bundesbank, two concrete parts in Alter Hof. W19, Bundesbank (2015, concrete), is the anchor: made especially for ARTZUID 2015, Amsterdam, on the Apollolaan / Minervalaan sculpture route, "nine boxes stacked on top of each other with eighteen concrete slabs", described in a contemporary visit report as "piled white storage boxes ... carefully placed in the landscape", their rectangular forms interacting with the children's corner and the box-shaped Hilton hotel roof. The watchable behaviour is the count: the viewer counts, checks the alignment, and judges stability course by course; the stack reads as both a building and a pile that could be unstacked.

**Takeaway:** build from counted units; let the viewer count and weigh the stack. [C27251]

## M4 -- Near-fall tension

Rest a heavy mass on a carrier in a forward-leaning position so that it looks as if it might topple, while in fact it holds. W16, Traeger (Bearer / Carrier) (2012), is the single anchor, and it is grade A: a wooden block on a concrete carrier, shown in GROUPSHOW at Galerie Christian Lethert, Cologne, 16 January - 28 March 2015, the show that introduced Kiecol as a new gallery artist. The gallery's description is the mechanism stated plainly: "The wooden block heavily rests on its concrete carrier and at the same time might fall over due to its forward-leaning position. This leads to a sensible relationship of tension". The watchable behaviour is the held fall: the viewer holds the fall in mind the whole time -- the wooden block "might fall over" -- so looking becomes a sustained weighing of balance, not a glance.

**Takeaway:** lean the weight forward until the eye refuses to believe it holds. [C27252]

## M5 -- The stair that leads nowhere

Make the stair -- the element of ascent -- as an object with no arrival: a stair on a base, a stair in a box, a central stair as a collection title. W05, Stair with Base (1986, cement), is the stair on its base. W07, Oben (Above / Up There) (1994), is the stair in a box: a concrete staircase presented in an opened printed cardboard box, edition of 110 copies plus artist's proof, publisher Edition Nr. 5 Sony, Cologne, signed (stamped in the wet concrete) on the underside of the staircase "Kiecol", the box printed with typographic work details -- hammer EUR 1,300 at VAN HAM in 2026 against a EUR 500-700 estimate. Its dimensions split across two VAN HAM listings of the same edition (16 x 16 x 26.5 cm vs 16.5 x 16.5 x 30 cm for the artist's proof), recorded here, not resolved. W14, Treppe Central (Central Staircase) (2009), is held in the collection of the Museum fuer Gegenwartskunst Siegen. The watchable behaviour is the climb that loops: the eye climbs the steps out of habit, reaches the last step, and finds no floor, door or landing; the climb is repeated mentally because the form promises movement it does not deliver.

**Takeaway:** build the stair and remove the arrival; the climb must loop. [C27253]

## M6 -- Graphic flattening: sculpture that reads as a sign

Reduce the object to a flat black shape or a black frame, so that a three-dimensional thing reads like a drawing, a window or a pictogram. W01, Zaun (Fence) (1984), is a woodcut on paper, 50 x 35 cm, hand-signed, listed by Haeusler Contemporary -- no colour stated in the record, so none is given here. W06, Untitled (1987), is the charcoal house side that reads as a black geometric shape. W15, Rheinwein (Rhine Wine) (2011), is a steel, powder-coated relief made for the interior wall of the Garden Gallery by Sou Fujimoto in the Skulpturenpark Koeln: "with its black frame elements, [it] represents a graphic contrast to the architecture, whose window motifs it also quotes. The recognizability of the motif is intended to establish a connection between the Garden Gallery and the park and create a relationship across the dimensions of the park". The watchable behaviour is the flip: the viewer flips between reading the work as an image (a black house side, a fence woodcut, a framed window motif) and as a solid object; in Rheinwein the black frame elements make the relief read as a graphic contrast against the architecture it quotes.

**Takeaway:** flatten the solid until it reads as a sign; let the viewer flip between image and object. [C27254]

## M7 -- Serial variation in singles, pairs and rows

Present the same elemental form singly, in pairs and in rows, letting small differences between near-identical units carry the meaning. W02 is two-part; W11, Golden (2001), is the title work of the Siegen 2010 show, reproduced by the museum with the caption "Hubert Kiecol, Golden, 2001, Photo: Rosenstiel" -- though no medium, size or colour is stated for Golden itself. W12, Palace Girl, and W13, Separee, are named by the Siegen museum as sculptural pieces shown beside the graphic works in "Golden", 2010, and both are grade C: title and exhibition context only, no dates -- do not date them from the 2010 show. The Siegen text says ensembles "interface and develop fundamental variations". The watchable behaviour is comparison, not contemplation: the viewer compares across the row instead of contemplating one object, a slow left-to-right check of what changed.

**Takeaway:** show the form in singles, pairs and rows; let the small difference do the work. [C27255]

## M8 -- Material contrast and the weight paradox

Set heavy, dull materials (concrete, cement, steel) against light or soft ones (wood, paper, charcoal) and let the heavy thing look fragile and the light thing look load-bearing. W05 (cement stair), W07 (concrete stair in a cardboard box), W15 (powder-coated steel relief) and W16 (wooden block on a concrete carrier) are the anchors, and the Siegen framing names the governing tension directly: the works oscillate between mass and fragility, heaviness and lightness. The watchable behaviour is the misjudgement: the viewer judges weight by eye and gets it wrong on purpose -- a wooden block "heavily rests" yet might fall; concrete is cast small enough to hold in the hand; steel is powder-coated flat and reads as a drawing.

**Takeaway:** make the heavy look fragile and the light look load-bearing; let the eye misjudge the weight. [C27256]

## M9 -- Archive recombination (the tableau)

Take the artist's own older material -- drawings, photographs and prints made since the 1970s, including photographs of his own sculptures, travel photographs of buildings, old posters and a preliminary drawing -- and join them into a new formation that makes a new, imaginary space. W20, the tableaus / formations shown after 2016, is the anchor: "Der richtige Augenblick" (The Right Moment), Haeusler Contemporary, Muenchen, presented as recent work connecting to the Josef Albers Museum Quadrat Bottrop exhibition of 2016, where Kiecol first showed excerpts from the cosmos of his work. The gallery's description is the mechanism: "by connecting images of different spatial form-findings he creates a new, imaginary space open to the viewer's discoveries; the materials are chosen from drawings, photographs and prints he made since the 1970s; photographs of his own sculptures appear beside pictures of historical buildings or urban architectural situations he photographed while travelling; sometimes his own exhibition posters or invitations are integrated, and in one case a preliminary drawing". The watchable behaviour is re-reading: the viewer re-reads familiar images in unfamiliar company; an old sculpture photograph gains a new meaning beside a historical building, and the work is watched as a set of relations between images rather than as one image.

**Takeaway:** recombine your own archive into a new imaginary space; the relations are the picture. [C27257]

## M10 -- Time carried by the title

Let a temporal or positional word in the title do the work of a fourth dimension -- "Oben" (Above), "Der richtige Augenblick" (The Right Moment) -- so that the object is read as a moment, not just a shape. W07, Oben, is a concrete stair in a printed box, the title placing it above; W20 is the tableau show whose title names the right moment. The Haeusler text states that temporal aspects often play a role in his titles and that in his two- and three-dimensional work he always also considers the fourth dimension. The watchable behaviour is waiting: the title primes waiting -- the right moment, the place above -- and the viewer looks for when, not only what.

**Takeaway:** put the time in the title; let the object be read as a moment. [C27258]

## Aesthetic logic

Kiecol's logic is the house reduced to its grammar and then set down where the body has to reckon with it. The everyday element is extracted, shrunk, and placed on the floor, so the building that should tower overhead becomes a model the eye looks down on -- and the model, because it is cast in concrete, keeps the weight of the real thing. Against Foerg's lead and grey, Kiecol's materials are the builder's yard: concrete, wood, steel, charcoal on paper -- and the joke, where there is one, is scale: a house you can pick up, a stair that fits in a box, a bank built from storage boxes in a park. Where the minimalists before him forbade association and made the sculpture autonomous, Kiecol leaves the viewer free to interpret, and the work does its meaning in the gap between the name the viewer gives it and the use it refuses. The through-line is the element: one stair, one house, one fence, one courtyard, taken out of its building, set down on the floor, and left there until the viewer has weighed it, counted it, climbed it in the mind, and found no arrival. Late in the career the archive itself becomes the element: the old drawings and photographs are the houses now, recombined into a new imaginary space. [C27259]
