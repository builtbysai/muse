# Johnny Friedlaender: Study Notes

**Site:** Johnny Gotthard Friedlaender, born 21 June 1912 at Pless, Prussian Silesia (now Pszczyna, Poland), died 18 June 1992 at Paris, three days short of his eightieth birthday -- German-French printmaker and painter, the master of colour etching whose Paris atelier schooled a generation. He enrolled at the Breslau Art Academy in 1928 under Otto Mueller of Die Brucke, fled to Paris in 1937 as a political refugee, was interned 1939-1943 and worked with the Resistance, co-founded the Atelier de l'Ermitage with Albert Flocon in 1949, taught at the Museu de Arte Moderna in Rio on a UNESCO post in 1959, became professor at Salzburg in 1966, and was naturalised French on 28 November 1952. From 1960 to 1965 Rene Carcan worked in his Paris studio, where Friedlaender taught him the techniques of colour etching; on 20 October 1989 Friedlaender opened the Fondation Rene Carcan in person. His preferred medium was aquatint etching, of which he was a pioneer; his own summary of the practice was "Je ne suis qu'un peintre qui grave" -- "I am just a painter who makes prints" (C4701, C4703, C4704, C4706, C4707, C4708, C4710, C4711, C4801, C4803, C4805, C4806, C4820). The spelling is Friedlaender, not Friedlander: it is his own pencil signature form, the transliteration of the umlaut form used by the Schmucking catalogue raisonne, his domain johnnyfriedlaender.eu, and Getty ULAN's preferred form endorsed by MoMA, Grove and RKD; "Friedlander" is the anglicised variant used by English Wikipedia's title and by auction aggregators (C4704, C4709, C4718, C4832). The birth day is 21 June 1912: the catalogue raisonne says so, Annex, Glenhyrst, Artsper, lot-art, Bertrand-Cayeux and French Wikipedia agree, and only a June birth makes "three days short of his eightieth birthday" true; the 26 December 1912 of English Wikipedia is uncited, internally inconsistent, and propagated to its mirrors -- no civil registry record was consulted, so the day is best-supported, not proven (C4701, C4708, C4703, C4710, C4716, C4717, C4833).

## The life: the refugee who became the school

Friedlaender is the hinge of the chain so far: Mueller of Die Brucke taught him in Breslau, he taught Carcan in Paris, and Carcan founded Cap d'Encre. The hinge was forged by flight. Born the son of a pharmacist in Pless, he moved to Breslau in 1921 and enrolled at the academy in 1928, attending Mueller's master classes and working in lithography and etching; Carlo Mense is named as a second teacher in one source only (C4701, C4708, C4801, C4808). In 1930 he moved to Dresden, joined the revolutionary artists' association ASSO, and in 1933 was interned in a Nazi camp; discharged, he took refuge in Czechoslovakia, then Holland, held his first one-man show of etchings in Ostrava, and in 1937 fled with his young wife -- an actress -- to Paris as political refugees (C4702, C4705, C4711, C4719). The dates of the flight years disagree between sources (Czechoslovakia 1933, 1935 or 1936; The Hague show 1936 or 1937) and are recorded as found, not reconciled. He worked at the literary weekly Marianne in 1938-1939, was interned again from 1939 to 1943 in a series of camps whose names no source gives, joined the English army, escaped, and worked with the Resistance in southern France (C4702, C4710, C4711, C4717).

After freedom in 1944 he began Images du Malheur, twelve etchings by one count, ten by another, published by Sagile -- the first work in the catalogue (W01) is a hooded figure holding a Torah scroll overhead, black aquatint on cream, fully narrative (C4706, C4717, C4751, C4752, C4754). In 1947 he produced Reves Cosmiques and joined the Salon de Mai, which he held until 1969; in 1948 he befriended Nicolas de Stael; in 1949 he showed first at Galerie La Hune, whose director Bernard Gheerbrant stayed faithful to him until his death, and founded the Atelier de l'Ermitage with Albert Flocon and printer Georges Leblanc -- a workshop Flocon called an "experimental facility" whose goal was to give, in fraternal collaboration, "the taste of research and precise knowledge needed in order to engrave on one's own", frequented by Vieira da Silva, Zao Wou-Ki and de Stael (C4706, C4711, C4717, C4815). The school formed around him: Piza, Coudrain, Carcan, Rodo Boulanger, Nottebohm and Zelt on the Annex/Wikipedia list; fourteen further names, none overlapping, on Getty ULAN's teacher-of list (C4803, C4804, C4807). He exhibited in Tokyo in 1951 and at the Milan Triennale the same year, took the Kamakura Prize in 1957, the UNESCO teaching post at the Museu de Arte Moderna in Rio in 1959, the Salzburg professorship in 1966 -- the year he resumed painting, abandoned in the 1940s -- and travelled to Puerto Rico, New York and Washington in 1968, buying a house in Burgundy the same year (C4706, C4707, C4711, C4717). Retrospectives came at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris in 1978, Bremen in 1987 for his seventy-fifth birthday, and Bonn in 1992 for his eightieth; he died in Paris on 18 June 1992, was cremated, and was mourned at the synagogue of Paris (C4703, C4705, C4707, C4710).

## M1 -- The aquatint ground: tone bitten into copper

The first mechanism is the ground everything else stands on, and the sources name it outright: aquatint is Friedlaender's preferred medium, "a technically difficult artistic process of which Friedlaender has been a pioneer" (C4704), and the Aquatint article names him alongside T. F. Simon as a notable twentieth-century user (C4715). The mechanics are a clock, not a wash. Rosin dust is blown up inside an aquatint box and settles on the copper; the plate is heated so the resin melts into a fine even coat; the plate is dipped in acid, and the acid bites the gaps between the grains, so where the grains sit prints white and the bitten ground around them holds ink (C4713, C4714). The first etch runs thirty seconds to a minute; more than thirty minutes makes a very dark area; the artist then immerses the plate again and again, progressively stopping out -- protecting with varnish -- each area that has reached its designed tone, so the tones are frozen one at a time and the image is shaped in large sections (C4714). The bite is shallow, which is why the plates wear out: the depressions flatten under the press (C4713). What the eye reads as Friedlaender's watery, atmospheric ground -- the ochre field under the birds of 1956, the brown sky band of The Blue Hill (W10, 1965), the dark brown ground of Red bird III (W13, 1971) -- is rosin grain, acid time, and stop-out varnish, counted in minutes.

**Takeaway:** build the ground from grain and time. Rosin dust, heat, acid, stop-out -- the tone is a schedule of bites, and the watery field is the schedule made visible.

## M2 -- The etched line as sign: the needle draws, the grain feels

The second mechanism is the division of labour inside the same plate, and it is the exact rule Carcan took from him: etching imposes the structure, aquatint serves the feeling (see #612, M2). The needle scratches through hard ground and the acid bites a line; the aquatint grain bites a field (C4713, C4714). In Friedlaender the line does less and less as the career goes on, and that is the mechanism's visible form. In W04 The rooster III (1952) the animal is a few angular black calligraphic strokes inside a hand-drawn frame; in W05 Les deux chevaux I (1954) the horses are looping scribbled curves, still readable as bodies; by W06 Two birds III (1956) the birds are black silhouettes perched on a striped heart, and by W08 Composition (1959) the fish is a flat yellow-ochre shape with a round graphic eye, hatched scales, triangular fins -- the line has become contour and emblem, and the grain does the weather (C4752, C4755). MoMA's record of his work as "aquatint and soft ground etching" adds the third mark: soft ground (vernis mou) for crayon-like, textured strokes, and a pupil's account of his toolkit runs the whole intaglio list -- eau-forte, burin, vernis mou, relief, berceau, aquatinte -- with the demand that everything be "truly engraved in intaglio, without cheating; absolute craft" (C4712, C4715). The rule for a piece: line draws the sign, grain holds the sky, and neither does the other's job.

**Takeaway:** let the needle sign and the grain weather. One plate, two bites -- the line is the animal's name, the grain is the air it stands in.

## M3 -- Many colours in one sheet: plates, inking, and the Hayter analogy

The third mechanism is the colour count, and it climbs through the catalogue like a ladder: 6 colours in Octave I (W14, 1972), 8 in Red bird III (W13, 1971), 9 in Red and blue spots (W11, 1968), 11 in Madrigal (W15, 1973) -- dealer listings record etchings in 7, 9 and 11 colours on a single sheet, aquatint, signed in pencil (C4712, C4752). Colour etching is an ambiguous trade term: it most often means multiple plates printed in register, one per colour, or multiple inking of one plate, and Friedlaender's practice is described by Champetier as an evolution -- from expressionist etching toward coloured abstraction "with signs, etchings with multiple inking, finally similar to Hayter's technique" (C4712, C4713). What Hayter's technique is: Atelier 17's simultaneous colour printing, or viscosity printing, where a copper plate incised with grooves of staggered depths works with inks of different oils applied by hard and soft rollers, so several colours land on one plate and print in a single pass (C4720). The caution is firm and must travel with the mechanism: Champetier says "finally similar to" -- an analogy, not a statement that Friedlaender used viscosity inks, and no source consulted says he did (C4712). What is evidenced is multiple inking and/or multiple plates producing multi-colour impressions, plus the standard colour-aquatint route of separate plates per colour (C4713). A piece built on M3 shows its colours arriving -- plate by plate, or ink by ink -- and never pretends the viscosity is proven.

**Takeaway:** count the colours out loud. Six, eight, nine, eleven -- each colour is a separate decision laid on the sheet, and the sheet is the sum of the decisions.

## M4 -- Animal as sign: horse, bird, fish, reduced to emblem

The fourth mechanism is the reduction that runs the whole middle career: the animal stops being observed and becomes a sign. The catalogue shows it in dated order. W01 (1945) is a narrative figure with a Torah scroll; W03 La coiffure I (1950) is a modelled nude dissolving into grain; W04 (1952) reduces the rooster to angular calligraphic strokes; W05 (1954) keeps the horses as looping gesture; W06 (1956) makes the birds black silhouettes with a spiky sunburst and a triangle beside them; W07 Bird VI (1958) is barely a bird at all -- a totem of black spiky marks, a triangular form, two small brown-red discs, red and blue accents on tan-ochre; W08 (1959) makes the fish a flat yellow-ochre shape with one round eye (C4752, C4755). The eye is the last thing to go: the fish keeps its round eye, the red bird of 1971 keeps a white-grey disc for an eye or a moon above a flat red slab, and the disc eye survives into the late geometry as a sun or moon disc (C4752). The bestiary album of 1963, Petit bestiaire, eleven etchings with text by Jean Cassou, already shows the endpoint: the plates in the composite photograph are dense black aquatint masses with white scratch lines, and the animals are no longer visible as animals (C4752). The mechanism is a subtraction with a fixed endpoint -- body, then gesture, then silhouette, then a disc eye on a flat shape -- and a piece built on it performs the subtraction live, never starting from the emblem.

**Takeaway:** reduce the animal until only the eye is left. Horse to gesture, bird to silhouette, fish to a flat shape with one round eye -- the sign is what survives the subtraction.

## M5 -- The fixed emblem kit: circle, triangle, stripe, checkerboard, sun

The fifth mechanism is the vocabulary the reduction arrives at, and it never widens again: circle, triangle, stripe, checkerboard, sun or moon disc, recombined across the late plates as a closed kit. The images show it. W14 Octave I (1972): a brown ground of fine horizontal stripes, a large white crescent at top centre, a blue hatched rectangular block, a red vertical bar, one thin white zigzag. W15 Madrigal (1973): eleven colours as flat geometric patches fitted like a mosaic -- a white curve holding a blue triangle and a green diamond, a black mass, a striped brown-red block with a small red spot. W16 Contrepoints (1977): a white triangle, a yellow sunburst, checkered and striped shapes, orange and gold wedges, linked by thin lines. W18 In the circle (1980): a large dark circle filling the plate, crossed by thin white concentric arcs, an orange triangle at top, a green vertical bar at right, a checkered block at centre. W20 Without title I (c. 1990): a red vertical spike, a red-blue checkered column, a yellow fan or sun disc, a stack of ochre, blue and red triangles -- the whole kit in one plate (C4752). The kit is closed: after 1971 no new motif enters, and the work after the 1978 retrospective keeps the same emblems -- striped totem, checkered grid, sun disc, red spike -- with no return to figuration (C4751, C4752). A piece built on M5 deals from a fixed hand of five signs and never invents a sixth.

**Takeaway:** deal from five signs and no more. Circle, triangle, stripe, checkerboard, sun -- the whole late career is recombinations of one hand, and the hand never grows.

## M6 -- The frieze: dark bands top and bottom, signs laid in a row

The sixth mechanism is the late compositional form, clearest in W16 Contrepoints (1977): a wide horizontal plate with dark bands top and bottom and a pale middle band, and along the centre a horizontal assemblage of small forms -- white triangle, yellow sunburst, checkered and striped shapes, orange and gold wedges -- linked by thin lines, reading as a frieze of signs laid on a dark ground (C4752). The form recurs as a habit, not a one-off: W08 Composition (1959) is already a wide horizontal plate, 25.4 x 49.4 cm, with a dark mass at left and a flat fish at right and thin horizontal lines crossing the field; W12 Paysage jaune (1969) is a wide pale field with a low dark band across the lower third carrying the yellow and ochre; W10 The Blue Hill (1965) stacks its picture as bands -- dark sky, pale dome, ochre wash, blue-green crescent (C4752, C4755). The frieze is the emblem kit given a sentence structure: the dark bands are the frame, the thin lines are the grammar that links the signs, and the signs are the words. Against the early vertical narrative plates (W01, W03) the horizontal frieze is the opposite move -- the image stops being a scene you enter and becomes a line you read left to right.

**Takeaway:** lay the signs in a row between two dark bands. The frieze is the kit with grammar -- bands frame, thin lines link, and the picture reads left to right like a sentence.

## M7 -- Dark-ground reversal: the pale field turns brown, then black

The seventh mechanism is the ground itself flipping. The 1950s and 1960s plates sit on pale grounds: cream paper under the black of W01, mottled grey-white under W03, pale grey-beige under the rooster, warm ochre-tan under the birds of 1956-1958, pale grey under the 1959 Composition (C4752, C4755). From 1971 the grounds darken and stay dark: dark brown under Red bird III (W13), brown striped ground under Octave I (W14), dark bands top and bottom in Contrepoints (W16), a large dark circle on pale ground in W18 that inverts the relation, and a warm brown ground under Without title I (W20), with black vertical masses at right (C4752). The reversal changes what colour does: on the pale grounds colour is an accent -- one red spot, one blue triangle, a small yellow scribble; on the dark grounds the flat colour patches glow, red dominating for the first time in W13, and the eleven colours of Madrigal read as fitted mosaic tiles because the dark holds them apart (C4752). The same emblem kit behaves differently on either side of the flip, which is why the mechanism matters for a piece: the ground is not a background, it is the switch that decides whether colour whispers or glows.

**Takeaway:** flip the ground and the colour changes its voice. Pale ground, colour accents; dark ground, colour glows -- the switch is the ground, not the palette.

## M8 -- The sketch-first score: coincidence refused as master

The eighth mechanism is the method, stated by the artist himself and quoted from the catalogue raisonne of the etchings: "I eschew coincidence in my work; it is not my master. I have made stringency my rule. I jot down my fleeting ideas in the form of numerous sketches and, as if for a musical score, they continue to be worked out" (C4708). The score metaphor is not decoration: the series titles are musical -- Octave I-VIII (1972), Madrigal (1973), Contrepoints (1977) -- and the plates behave like scored music, strict geometry fitted patch by patch, the zigzag in Octave I like a phrase mark, the frieze in Contrepoints like a staff (C4752). The pupil's account of the craft standard is the same stringency from the other side: everything truly engraved in intaglio, without cheating, absolute craft, and the painter bringing the engraver "a new dimension, transparency" (C4712). Against Carcan, whose plates arrive one colour at a time and can be counted, Friedlaender's stringency is the sketch: the fleeting idea is caught in numerous sketches first, then worked out with the rigour of a score, so that nothing on the plate is an accident of the acid. A piece built on M8 shows the sketch becoming the score -- the loose mark first, the strict working-out second -- and never lets a random bite pass as a decision.

**Takeaway:** sketch first, then score it. Catch the fleeting idea in sketches, work it out with stringency, and let nothing on the plate be an accident -- coincidence is not the master.

## The aesthetic logic: the painter who engraves, from figure to emblem

Friedlaender's logic is the opposite of Carcan's and the completion of it. Carcan kept one technique and changed everything inside it; Friedlaender kept one method -- sketch, then score -- and let the subject drain out of it. The arc is a single subtraction: narrative black-and-white figure (1945-1954), to coloured animal-sign on an ochre ground (1956-1969), to abstract emblem-field built from a fixed kit on dark warm grounds (1971-1990), with no return to figuration. The constants are the plate and the ground: rosin grain and acid time make the tone, the needle makes the sign, the colours arrive one decision at a time, and the ground flips from pale to dark so the colour learns to glow. The variable is the motif, and it narrows until it is five signs. The chain reads through him: Mueller's Die Brucke line gave him the strong etched line and the expressionist start; the camps and the flight gave him the Images du Malheur and the refusal of coincidence; Paris gave him the atelier and the school -- Piza, Coudrain, Carcan, Rodo Boulanger, and the fourteen ULAN names who carried the practice to Brazil, Uruguay, Argentina, Denmark, Slovenia, Finland and Switzerland; and Carcan gave him the ending, opening the Fondation in person three years before his death, the master outliving the pupil's institution by opening it first. His own sentence is the logic in one line: a painter who engraves, sketching fleeting ideas and working them out like a score, until the animal is a sign and the sign is a disc with an eye.
