# Jan Both: Study Notes

**Site:** Jan Both (c. 1618 - 9 August 1652), Dutch Golden Age painter, draughtsman and etcher of Utrecht, the main pioneer of Italianate landscape in 17th-century Holland, the fellow pupil of Abraham Bloemaert who took the same Utrecht training the other way from Hendrick Bloemaert - out of the dark interior and into the sunlit Campagna. He was born in Utrecht, the younger son of Dirck Joriaensz. Both, a glass painter or glazier, and the younger brother of Andries Both (1612/13 - 1642), the genre painter of the bamboccianti; the brothers first learned to paint from their father, according to Houbraken, and Jan was then, according to von Sandrart, apprenticed with Andries to Abraham Bloemaert in Utrecht, a record debated by Burke (James D. Burke, Jan Both: Paintings, Drawings and Prints, doctoral thesis, Garland, 1976, p. 35 and p. 37, note 13) and given by the National Gallery as "probably trained there under Abraham Bloemaert". Gerrit van Honthorst has also been suggested as a teacher, on no documentary evidence cited in any source read. Jan attended the Utrecht academie between 1634 and 1637, travelled to Rome via France, arriving in 1637 by one account (Gerszi) and by 1638 by most others, lived with Andries in Rome, and in 1638-39 and 1640-41 collaborated with Claude Lorrain and Herman van Swanevelt - and with Gaspard Dughet in the wider commission - on two series of large landscapes for the Buen Retiro Palace in Madrid, commissioned by Don Manuel de Moura, Marques de Castel Rodrigo, ambassador of King Philip IV of Spain. The brothers left Rome in 1641, travelling via Venice, where Andries drowned in a canal, according to von Sandrart; Jan returned to Utrecht alone, in 1641 by the auction-house accounts, "certainly by 1646" by Wikipedia's cautious wording, and remained in Utrecht for the rest of his life, becoming head of the city's painters' guild in 1649 on the single authority of a Christie's lot essay, and dying - buried 9 August 1652 - in his native city. His birth year is UNSETTLED across the sources read and is carried here as a spread, not a single year: Wikipedia gives "between 1610 and 1618", the National Gallery gives "about 1615", Christie's and Sotheby's give "Utrecht c. 1618", and Wikimedia Commons / RKD-derived data give "between 1618 and 1622". The study uses "c. 1618-1652" as its working dates, the auction-house and RKD convention, with the spread stated openly wherever it matters (see C455). His output is more than 300 paintings attributed to him (WGA), but none can be convincingly dated to his Italian sojourn, and he rarely dated his works at all, so the chronology of the late large Utrecht landscapes cannot be established - only one of them is dated (NGA). His pupils were Barend Bispinck, Willem de Heusch and Hendrick Verschuring. A portrait of Jan by Cornelis van Poelenburch, painted in 1648, survives. No words by Jan Both himself were found in any source read - no letters, contracts or statements; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Jan Both was born in Utrecht, the younger son of a glass painter, and he never really left the orbit of the Utrecht studio that taught him - except for the four years in Rome that made him. His father Dirck Joriaensz. Both painted glass; Houbraken says the brothers first learned to paint from him. Then, according to von Sandrart, Jan and his elder brother Andries were apprenticed together to Abraham Bloemaert, the man who ran the most important teaching workshop in the city - the same studio Hendrick Bloemaert grew up in, seventeen years earlier. The apprenticeship is debated by Burke, and the National Gallery hedges it to "probably trained there under Abraham Bloemaert", but no other teacher is documented for him except the suggestion of Honthorst, which rests on a single Wikipedia line. He attended the Utrecht academie between 1634 and 1637, a drawing school rather than a guild, and then followed Andries to Rome, arriving in 1637 by Gerszi's account and by 1638 by most others, to join a brother who had been there since 1635.

Rome is where the career is made, and it is made fast. Within a year or two of arriving, Jan is working beside Claude Lorrain and Herman van Swanevelt on two series of large landscapes for the Buen Retiro Palace in Madrid - twenty-two pictures for Philip IV's summer palace, commissioned by the Marques de Castel Rodrigo, the first series 1638-39 and the second 1640-41. The Prado still holds three of Both's: Taking the Cattle out, the Fishermen and Shepherds on a Riverbank, and the Baptism of the Eunuch, all dated 1639-1641, all about 210 x 155 cm, all landscapes first with the figures added afterwards - X-rays show it - sometimes by other hands. From Claude, Both takes the light, not the construction: the National Gallery's distinction is the sharpest sentence in the study. He keeps his own naturalism - the rhythms and shapes of branches and leaves, thinly applied paint, small highlights - and he peoples the golden distance not with Claude's biblical or mythological figures but with peasants driving cattle, muleteers, travellers gazing at ruins, in red smocks and sheepskin waistcoats. That was the innovation, and it was recognised at once: WGA calls the contemporary figure an innovation, and the National Gallery calls him the main pioneer of Italianate landscape in Holland.

The Rome years end in Venice, on the way home. The brothers left Rome in 1641, travelling via Venice, where Andries drowned in a canal, according to von Sandrart - 1641 by Sotheby's and the Jan Both article, 23 March 1642 by the Andries Both article, a one-year spread that is not resolved here. Jan returned to Utrecht alone, in 1641 by the auction-house accounts, "certainly by 1646" by Wikipedia, and stayed there for the remaining decade of his life. What he painted there is the work he is known by: large imaginary Italianate landscapes, painted in the Utrecht studio from drawings made in Italy, freely combining real motifs - Tivoli waterfalls, the Tiber, the Ponte Molle - into places that cannot be found on a map. None of his later paintings portray identifiable places, the National Gallery of Ireland notes. The scale jumps - the late group runs from about 100 x 125 cm to 187 x 240 cm - and only one of them is dated, so their internal chronology cannot be established. The golden evening light becomes the subject itself: Sandrart already noted in 1675 Both's ability to evoke the differing light effects at various times of the day. He became head of the Utrecht painters' guild in 1649, on the single authority of a Christie's lot essay, and he died in Utrecht on 9 August 1652, buried that day, aged about thirty-four if born in 1618, about forty-two if born in 1610. His large late landscapes probably decorated the houses of upper-class clients in Amsterdam and Utrecht, were prized by Dutch patricians over the native Dutch landscape, and proved immensely popular with collectors, not least in England, helping to shape ideas about Italy for two centuries.

**Takeaway:** a career with one move and one light - the Utrecht pupil who went to Rome, learned Claude's golden hour, and spent the rest of his short life painting an Italy that existed only in his studio, from drawings, from memory, and from the light itself.

## M1. The golden unifying light - low sun, warm wash over the whole picture

The entire landscape is bathed in one warm, low, golden light, inspired by Claude Lorrain, so that foreground, middle ground and distance share one colour temperature. Christie's (read end to end): "Like Claude and van Swanevelt, Both arranged his landscapes along diagonal lines in order to achieve a greater feeling of depth, unifying the composition by means of a glowing, golden light that was also inspired by Claude and came to characterize his entire output." The National Gallery (read end to end): "The tranquillity of the Campagna... stretches far into the distance under a soft golden light." Watchable behaviour: the eye reads the picture as one warm hour of the day rather than as separate objects; shadows are warm, not grey; the far distance glows instead of fading to blue-grey.

**Takeaway:** the light is the subject before the landscape is; one hour unifies the whole picture.

## M2. The receding diagonal - sandy road or river running corner to corner

The main route through the picture - a sandy road, a river, a path - is laid as a sweeping diagonal from one side of the frame to the other, pulling the eye from foreground to distance. EN Wikipedia (read end to end), on Landscape with Bandits Leading Prisoners: "the sandy road makes a sweeping diagonal from the left." Christie's: "Both arranged his landscapes along diagonal lines in order to achieve a greater feeling of depth." Watchable behaviour: the viewer's gaze slides along the road or river instead of stopping at the frame edge; depth feels walked, not stacked.

**Takeaway:** the road is the perspective; the eye travels it instead of reading it.

## M3. Layered depth in three bands - dark foreground, lit middle, hazy distance

The picture is built as successive horizontal bands - a dark, detailed foreground, a sunlit middle ground with figures and animals, and a pale, atmospheric distance. The Prado (read end to end), on the Fishermen landscape: "the background is abruptly enclosed by a mountain above which only the sky is visible" and "a stream zigzags from the background to the foreground". Google Arts and Culture (read end to end): "Our eye meanders happily through this large painting. If we follow the road and cross the bridge, we see in the distance a well-to-do traveller in a sedan chair." Watchable behaviour: the eye meanders in stages, lingering on each band; the distance reads as a reward at the end of the walk.

**Takeaway:** depth is walked in stages; the distance is the reward, not the backdrop.

## M4. Foreground realism against idyllic distance - two registers in one picture

The foreground is rendered with down-to-earth, precisely observed detail - individual leaves, rough bark, worn clothes - while the distance is idealised and golden. EN Wikipedia (read end to end): "Touches of realism in the down-to-earth figures and detailed vegetation of the foreground contrast with the idyllic golden distance." Watchable behaviour: the near ground feels touchable and present while the far ground feels like a dream; the picture holds both a real place and an imagined one at once.

**Takeaway:** the near is touched and the far is dreamed; the picture holds both at once.

## M5. The warm green-and-brown palette bound by luminous atmosphere

The colour range is deliberately narrow - warm greens and browns - and the parts are held together not by outline but by a shared luminous atmosphere. Christie's (read end to end): "In them, Both excels in his use of a warm palette of green and brown, brought together by the luminous atmosphere, creating a romantic ideal of the Italian countryside." Watchable behaviour: greens and browns that would look muddy side by side instead glow; the whole picture feels lit from inside the air itself.

**Takeaway:** the air is the binder; the colour is narrow and the light is what makes it glow.

## M6. The sunset / evening glow as the default hour - pink-orange Mediterranean sunset

Both sets most of his mature landscapes at evening, with the sun low and the sky and distance flushed pink and orange. Christie's (search snippet, wooded-landscape lot essay): the picture presents "an idealised view, illuminated by the luminous pinks and oranges of a Mediterranean sunset." The National Gallery (read end to end), on Peasants with Mules and Oxen: "The sun sinks behind a steep Italian hillside, casting a soft, gold light over a meandering river in the valley below." Watchable behaviour: long soft light, warm rims on hills and animals, a mood of the day ending rather than beginning.

**Takeaway:** evening is the default hour; the picture is about the day ending, not the place.

## M7. The tree as monument - colossal foliage on a diagonal axis, seen slightly from below

One or two large trees dominate the composition, their foliage masses arranged along a diagonal, drawn from a slightly low viewpoint so they tower over the small figures. Google Arts and Culture, Terez Gerszi (read end to end), on Forest Landscape (Budapest, 1643): "The robust trees with their colossal foliage arranged along a diagonal axis are the embodiments of vegetative life force. The trees are depicted slightly from below, which intensifies their monumentality, as does the small size of the figures." Watchable behaviour: the tree feels huge and alive; the human figures shrink to scale-markers beneath it.

**Takeaway:** the tree is the monument and the people are the scale; look up, not across.

## M8. The arabesque limb - outstretched branches that embrace across the picture

The big tree's limbs stretch out in long, curving, almost embracing gestures that knit the left and right of the picture together. Gerszi (read end to end): "The outstretched limbs of the trees seem almost to embrace in an arabesque." Watchable behaviour: the eye follows the branch curves across the frame; the canopy becomes a drawn line, a gesture, not just a mass.

**Takeaway:** the branch is a line drawn across the picture; the canopy gestures, it does not just sit.

## M9. The tiny contemporary figure - peasants, muleteers, travellers in red smocks and sheepskin

Instead of biblical or mythological heroes, Both peoples his landscapes with contemporary working people - peasants driving cattle, muleteers, travellers gazing at ruins - often in a recurring costume of red smock and sheepskin waistcoat. WGA (read end to end): "His landscapes are typically peopled by peasants driving cattle or travellers gazing on Roman ruins in the light of the evening sun. Such contemporary scenes were an innovation, for Claude Lorraine and the earlier Dutch painters of the Italian countryside had populated it with biblical or mythological figures." Sotheby's (read end to end): "the peasant resting beneath the tree, dressed in a red smock and sheepskin waistcoat, recurs throughout his oeuvre." Watchable behaviour: the figures are small, ordinary, and repeatable - the viewer recognises the same red-smocked herdsman picture after picture, like a signature.

**Takeaway:** the hero is a herdsman in a red smock; the small ordinary figure is the innovation.

## M10. The winding path with a turn - figures placed at the bend

The path is drawn to wind and turn, and the picture's human focus is placed exactly at the turn, so the bend becomes a small stage. The National Gallery (read end to end), on A Rocky Landscape with Peasants and Pack Mules: "In the centre, a group of peasants - the picture's focus - are at the turn of a path. One man pushes his laden mule up the slope towards us." Watchable behaviour: the eye arrives at the bend and stops there; the push of the mule uphill gives the still landscape a small push of effort.

**Takeaway:** the bend is the stage; the effort is small and the landscape is large.

## M11. The shaft of light that points the way - a beam through cliffs or trees

A single shaft of low sunlight is aimed down the route the traveller must take, like a finger pointing forward. The National Gallery (read end to end), on A Rocky Landscape with an Ox-cart: "A shaft of light from the low sun seems to point the way forward for the ox cart between the craggy cliffs." Sotheby's condition report (read end to end) notes "The light coming over the horizon beyond the trees to the left is finely preserved catching the grasses in scumbled brushstrokes". Watchable behaviour: the light itself becomes a direction sign; the cart's journey and the light's journey are the same line.

**Takeaway:** the light is a direction sign; the journey and the beam are one line.

## M12. The glazed sunset sky - thin translucent layers over opaque paint

The luminosity of the sky and distance is built by glazing - thin translucent layers of pigment laid over opaque underpainting - so light seems to come from inside the paint. Sotheby's condition report (read end to end, Sarah Walden): "rich unworn glazes in the shadows" and "The glazing of the sunset light in the sky is also finely intact". The technique name itself rests on a single source: the word "glazing" as Both's method is asserted in the wahooart / artsdot aggregator pages, which are AI-written copy and were NOT read end to end; the Sotheby's conservator's report, read end to end, confirms glazes survive in the sky and shadows, which grounds the mechanism without relying on the aggregators. Watchable behaviour: the sky glows rather than shines; the sunset looks deep and layered, not flat orange.

**Takeaway:** the sky is built in layers; the glow comes from inside the paint, not off its surface.

## M13. Figures added after the landscape - the two-hand picture

The landscape was painted first and the figures added afterwards, sometimes by another hand - a workshop division of labour visible in X-rays. The Prado (read end to end), on the Fishermen landscape: "The X-ray image reveals that, like those in the previous two works, the figures were also added to the landscape, but are executed more densely." The Prado (search snippet) on Taking the Cattle out: "The X-ray image shows that they were added after the landscape was completed". EN Wikipedia (read end to end): in Judgement of Paris (National Gallery) "the figures were painted by a fellow Utrecht artist, Cornelis van Poelenburch"; Sotheby's (read end to end) notes a version of the river landscape whose figures are by Poelenburgh. Watchable behaviour: the figures sit slightly apart from the ground they stand on - denser, sharper, a little more present than the landscape around them.

**Takeaway:** the picture was made in two passes; the figures arrive after the place, and it shows.

## M14. The imaginary view built from drawings - studio synthesis, not plein air

The finished landscapes are not painted on the spot; they are composed in the Utrecht studio from drawings made in Italy, freely combining real motifs (Tivoli waterfalls, the Tiber, the Ponte Molle) into invented places. Google Arts and Culture (read end to end): "By placing a draughtsman near the bridge over the waterfall, Both would have us believe that he painted this scene from life, but in reality it was painted at home in his Dutch studio." Sotheby's (read end to end): "the view depicted is unidentified but is probably based on one of the many drawings he made during his sojourn in Italy." Gerszi (read end to end): the Budapest drawing "shows a landscape around Utrecht" yet "the composition and thoughtful execution suggest that this is not a direct representation from life." Watchable behaviour: the place feels real but cannot be found on a map; familiar motifs (a waterfall, a bridge, a ruin) recur in new combinations.

**Takeaway:** the place is invented from true parts; the draughtsman in the picture is the lie that tells the truth about how it was made.

## M15. The drawing-to-etching pipeline - pen and brown ink, wash, then the plate

Both worked out landscapes first as drawings in pen and brown ink with grey/brown wash over black chalk, and a small number of these drawings were transferred to etched plates - only 10 original etchings after his own drawings (National Gallery of Ireland, search snippet). The Met (search snippet): Rocky Landscape, "Pen and brown ink, gray wash, over traces of black chalk, heightened with white". Art Institute of Chicago (search snippet): Italian Landscape with a Bridge, "Pen and brown ink, with brush and gray wash, over traces of black chalk". NGI (search snippet): the Bridge in a Mountain Landscape with Waterfall drawing is "a preparatory drawing used to make an etching entitled 'The Wooden Bridge, Sulmona, Tivoli'. Indentations can be seen on the paper." Watchable behaviour: in the drawings the landscape is built from tonal wash rather than line - light and dark washes do the work that colour does in the paintings; the etchings carry the same wash logic into black and white.

**Takeaway:** the wash is the painting before the painting; tone does the work colour will do later.

Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (NG object page not opened in this leg; the Commons table figures "29 52" are catalogue codes, not dimensions). Location: National Gallery, London, inv. NG958. - W7. View of the Ripa Grande in Rome. Date: 1643. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED (the Commons table figures "100 35 90" are catalogue codes, not a dimension; the Stadel object page was not opened in this leg). Location: Stadel Museum, Frankfurt, inv. 42. Dated 1643, so painted just after the return to Utrecht, but still a topographic Roman view - the shift to fully imaginary Campagna scenes was not instant. A second Stadel entry, View of the Ripa Grande and the Dogana Vecchia in Rome (1640s, RKDimages 239716), may be a related or variant work; do not merge the two.

The Buen Retiro commission (W3, W4, W5, plus the Carmelites) is the pivot of the Rome phase: very large canvases (about 210 x 155 cm), worked alongside Claude, Swanevelt and Dughet in 1638-41. From Claude, Both takes the golden light and the repoussoir schema (a tree or tree group at one side, beyond which the light-filled distance opens); the NGA essay stresses he imitates Claude's LIGHT, not Claude's pictorial construction. His own contribution is naturalistic detail: rhythms and shapes of branches and leaves, thinly applied paint, small highlights. Topography is still identifiable: the Tiber and Ripa Grande (W6, W7), the Cascatelle south of Tivoli (W4). Figures are often added AFTER the landscape (X-ray evidence at the Prado) and often by other hands (Andries, Jan Miel). WGA's key fact: none of the 300+ attributed paintings can be convincingly dated to the Italian sojourn, so the Rome phase is reconstructed from the Buen Retiro canvases, the dated 1637 works, drawings made on countryside expeditions, and the etchings - not from a dated sequence of paintings.

The mature and best-known Both: large-scale, IMAGINARY Italianate landscapes painted in Utrecht from Roman drawings and memory. "None of Both's later paintings portray identifiable places" (NGI). Scale jumps: the late group runs from about 100 x 125 cm (W9) to 187 x 240 cm (W18) and 165 x 218 cm (W19). The NGA essay: only ONE of these large late landscapes is dated, so their internal chronology cannot be established; dating rests on comparison (Sotheby's: mid-1640s works keep the shadowy foreground against a well-lit road; toward the end of his career Both paints larger, with a palette of deeper greens and blues). Light: the golden evening light becomes the subject itself. Palette: warm green and brown unified by luminous atmosphere (Christie's); deeper greens and blues late (Sotheby's). Thin paint on a grey ground, with a possible aqueous emulsion sponged into foliage (NGA technical report). Composition: diagonal roads/paths and zigzagging streams pull the eye from a dark, detailed, repoussoir foreground (trees, rocks, travellers) into a luminous, hazy, golden distance. The sandy road's sweeping diagonal (W19) is the signature move. Foreground trees often reach beyond the canvas edge (W17). Figure scale and type: figures stay SMALL relative to the landscape and stay CONTEMPORARY - peasants, muleteers, goatherds, travellers, ox-carts - rather than Claude's biblical or mythological staffage. That was the innovation (WGA): contemporary figures in an ideal landscape. Mythological figures appear only as collaborations: Poelenburgh paints them (W11, and the Tokyo Landscape with Nymphs); Mercury and Argus subjects exist but are a minority. Market: no commissions are known for the late large landscapes; they probably decorated the houses of upper-class Amsterdam and Utrecht clients with a love of Italy and Arcadian ideals (NGA). They were prized by Dutch patricians over native Dutch landscape (NG), and proved immensely popular with collectors, not least in England, shaping ideas about Italy for two centuries (WGA).

## Evolution

What he kept from Utrecht: the Netherlandish landscape tradition itself - the detailed foreground, the observed tree, the peasant figure, the winding road. Gerszi's sentence is the key: "The secret of his success was the extraordinary way in which he fused the great tradition of Netherlandish landscape painting with the lessons he learned from contemporary Italian art, creating a mode of painting that unified realism and classical idealism."

What Rome changed: the light and the scale. The early-1630s training gives him drawing and the figure; Rome gives him Claude's golden hour, the repoussoir tree, the diagonal road, and the large canvas. The Buen Retiro commission is the hinge: before it, small genre landscapes with low-life figures painted beside van Laer; after it, the Italianate landscape as a system.

What changed, in one paragraph: from small genre landscapes with low-life figures painted in Rome (1637) and large topographic commission pieces beside Claude (1639-41), to large imaginary Campagna landscapes painted in Utrecht (1642-52) in which golden evening light, a diagonal road, and small contemporary travellers do the work that mythology did for Claude. The landscape gets bigger, the place gets less identifiable, the light gets warmer and more specific (morning vs evening), the palette deepens into green and blue late, and the figures shrink from subjects into staffage - except when a collaborator (Poelenburgh) supplies a mythological foreground.

What stayed constant: the light as the unifier, the road as the route, and the small contemporary figure as the measure. From the 1637 Morra Players to the c. 1650 Draughtsman, the same three things do the work: one golden hour, one diagonal path, and one small person walking it.

## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

- D1. JAN vs ANDRIES - the standing trap. The brothers collaborated in Rome and their hands were historically confused. Prado, Taking the Cattle out (W3): figures attributed to Andries in early museum catalogues; X-ray shows figures added after the landscape. Prado, Fishermen and Shepherds (W4): figures attributed to Jan Miel in early catalogues; Burke (1976) believes them by Andries Both. Prado, Baptism of the Eunuch (W5): figures attributed to Andries in early catalogues. Bandits Leading Prisoners (W19): 1934 Art News report said figures by Andries Both and Cornelis van Poelenburgh. Wikipedia's "List of painters in the National Gallery of Art" MISattributes An Italianate Evening Landscape (NGA 2000.91.1, W17) to ANDRIES Both. The NGA itself catalogues it as Jan Both. Do not repeat the Wikipedia list's error. Sandrart says the brothers probably collaborated with Andries painting the figures in Jan's landscapes; the NGA essay repeats this. Modern consensus (Sotheby's 2008, on the Mountain Path and Ford) is that in the MATURE works the figures are by Jan himself; some staffage has been attributed to van Laer, Poelenburgh or Berchem by older writers. Philadelphia Museum of Art, Landscape with Cattle, Italy (Cat. 614), is catalogued to ANDRIES Both, not Jan - do not fold it into Jan's oeuvre. Rule for the study: where a source says "figures by X", name X; where a catalogue says "attributed to", keep the qualifier; never silently promote an attribution.
- D2. BIRTH YEAR: 1610-1618 (Wikipedia) vs 1618/1622 (Wikidata/Commons) vs about 1615 (National Gallery). The 1618 date in the doorway is one convention, not settled fact. See C455.
- D3. ANDRIES' DEATH: 1641 (Jan Both article; Swann; Sotheby's) vs 23 March 1642 (Andries Both article). Venice drowning is agreed; the year is not. Christie's says 1642 in the huntsmen-landscape essay and 1641 in the evening-landscape essay - the house disagrees with itself. See C456.

## Aesthetic logic

Jan Both's pictures are built on a single tension: the Netherlandish foreground against the Italian light. Everything he paints is a negotiation between the two. The foreground is Dutch - observed, detailed, down-to-earth, a tree you could climb, a mule you could feed, a peasant in a red smock you could meet on the road. The distance is Italian - golden, hazy, ideal, a Campagna that exists in the mind more than on the map. The light is what joins them: one low golden hour washes over both registers and makes them one picture, so the eye walks from the touchable near ground into the dreamed far ground without noticing the join. That is the whole system. The road is the route the eye walks, the tree is the monument that measures the walk, the small figure is the scale that proves the walk is human, and the evening is the hour that makes the walk feel like the end of a day rather than the start of one. Where Claude paints mythology in the distance, Both paints a muleteer; where Hendrick Bloemaert paints a face emerging from the dark, Both paints a landscape emerging from the light. The picture does not illustrate a story. It is a place you enter, at one hour, by one road, with one small person ahead of you on it.
