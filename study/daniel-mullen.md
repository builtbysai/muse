**Relevance:** Mullen's practice asks how a flat surface can hold a space a viewer moves through -- perspective that flips between convex and concave, colour made only where translucent planes overlap, a grid that will not sit still. Those are computable questions (projective ambiguity, multiply blending, near-regular repetition with deviation), and his own one-variable-at-a-time method is already the experimental method a generative practice needs.

## 1. The brief: the painter of rooms that aren't there

Mullen paints architecture that was never built and never will be: weightless transparent volumes, glass planes arranged in precise forms, structures that float, recede, and invert. The subject is not the building but the perception of it -- what it feels like to sense a space that is out of reach, to stand at the threshold of a room that recedes forever. He calls himself a devoted craftsman and works entirely by hand, without digital or mechanical aid, which is the first paradox of the practice: the paintings look like renders, like plotted diagrams, like something a machine extruded, and the closer you get the more the hand shows -- a wobble in a taped edge, a bleed under the mask, a brushstroke that hesitates. The second paradox is the ground: he paints on raw linen, unprimed, so the support itself is part of the image, and the unpainted linen is the philosophical subject, not the leftover. Everything else in this study follows from those two paradoxes -- a machine look made by hand, and a void that is the painting.

**Takeaway:** Read him as a phenomenologist with a roll of tape: the work is not a picture of a room, it is a machine for sensing a room that is not there. [C35392]

## 2. Glasgow and the Steiner school: veil painting

He was born in Glasgow in 1985, into a household where the arts were highly valued, and his formative schooling was at a Rudolf Steiner school -- which school, where, and when, no source names, so this study does not guess. What matters is what the school gave him: veil painting, heavily diluted paint applied in rhythmical overlapping layers. In Steiner pedagogy the technique is believed to create an atmosphere in which colour fields can permeate beyond the support surface, and Mullen has carried it whole into his adult practice: the glaze is not a finishing effect but the entire method, the way colour arrives at all. "The visual language originates from my formative education at a Rudolf Steiner school, where I learned the technique of veil painting, which uses heavily diluted paint applied in rhythmical overlapping layers." It is the oldest layer of the practice and the one he has never replaced -- every later invention (the tape, the triad, the steel) is built on top of a childhood exercise in letting thinned paint soak into cloth.

**Takeaway:** The technique predates the art: a school exercise in diluted paint became a thirty-year method. Everything he builds, he builds in veils. [C35393]

## 3. Rietveld 2006-2011: the academy named for an architect

He went to Amsterdam to study at the Gerrit Rietveld Academie, BFA Fine Art 2006-2011, and the name matters: the academy is named for the De Stijl architect, and Mullen's whole visual language descends from that lineage -- Dutch architecture, formalistic abstract painting, the grid as a world. His early references are Neo-Plasticism and De Stijl, Mondrian and Van Doesburg, most notably El Lissitzky and Constructivism. The graduation-era work is still literal: oil on gessoed canvas, reproducing existing architecture from photographs -- "Untitled Space No 7" (2011, 150 x 300 cm) is a perspective study of the interior of the Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh, light and form and colour transposed from a photograph. It is the last representational phase before abstraction, and it shows what he was learning to leave behind: the depicted room, the filled ground, the photograph as authority. After 2011 he keeps the architecture and discards the building.

**Takeaway:** He trained inside De Stijl's house and then emptied it: the architecture stayed, the building went. [C35394]

## 4. 2013: the raw-linen breakthrough

The break came in 2013, and Mullen dates it himself: he started painting on unprepared canvas, and the raw linen changed what the painting was. "Raw canvas seemed like an open emptiness in the sense that it was already something before I interacted with it. So I see the raw canvas as the place between reality and my imagination." The gessoed white ground -- a negative emptiness, something to be filled -- is abandoned for linen as a positive void, a universe that is already there before the painter touches it. The early titles from this moment read like a research program stated out loud: "Rendering the fundamentals" (2013), "Resurrecting the monumental" (2013), "Un-Intentional Space: A research into space beyond function" (2013), "Rendering layers" (2014), "Repetition in movement" (2014). Slabs, beams and columns are abstracted into "space encroaching mass"; architecture is freed from function and form is left to the observer. From here on the linen is never primed away, the paint is always thin acrylic glaze, and the painting is always a negotiation between a ground that is already something and forms placed into it.

**Takeaway:** 2013 is the hinge: the ground stops being a blank and starts being the subject. Every later painting is a variation on that one decision. [C35395]

## 5. The glaze: saturation built by repetition (M1)

The rule: heavily diluted acrylic is laid down in thin, translucent, rhythmical overlapping layers on raw linen; colour is never mixed to full strength on the palette but built up, pass by pass, until saturation arrives. ArtConnect: "He works by applying thin layers of diluted acrylic paint, building up the saturation on the surface of raw linen." Zipper: "built up in thin, translucent layers of acrylic on raw linen." From across the room the colour reads as flat, luminous, almost backlit -- like stained glass or a screen. As you approach, the single colour dissolves into separate translucent skins; you start counting layers and seeing the linen weave through them. The painting seems to breathe: the longer you look, the deeper the colour goes, because the depth is literally stacked in the material. Mullen describes himself as an impatient person and painting as the discipline against it -- "a regenerative and meditative exercise" of silent repetition, the brush as an extension of the body, the rhythm of the work as the work.

**Takeaway:** Saturation is a count, not a mixture: the colour arrives by repetition, and the depth you see is depth that was laid down one skin at a time. [C35396]

## 6. The tape line: digital afar, hand close (M2)

The rule: every plane is measured, masked with tape, painted, and the tape pulled -- entirely by eye and hand, with no digital aid, no CAD, no mechanical assistance. Artnet: "achieved through an exacting and time-consuming process of measuring and taping his canvases" -- "appearing impeccable from a distance. Up close, however, small vacillations and shifts in brushstrokes reveal evidence of the artist's hand." Colossal: the work "incorporat[es] irregularities, brush strokes, and bleeding paint into a work that breathes, floats, and expands through the energy of color." The first read is digital -- the lines look plotted, the painting looks like a render. That is the trap. Step closer and the edge frays: a hair of bleed under the tape, a brushstroke that hesitates. The work flips from machine-made to hand-made in a single step forward, and that flip is the point. His artist statement says it plainly: "As a devoted craftsman who meticulously creates all of my work without digital aids, I hope to create the impression of reproducibility, which is precisely what I seek to highlight in an era of mass consumption." He is staging the tension between the reproducible and the unique, and the tape edge is where the argument happens.

**Takeaway:** The line is a lie told twice: from afar it says machine, up close it says hand, and the painting is the distance between those two readings. [C35397]

## 7. The flip: convex, concave, neither (M3)

The rule: oblique transparent planes are drawn with perspective cues that are deliberately under-determined -- the same edge can be read as the front face of a solid jutting toward you or the back wall of a void receding from you. No shading, no cast shadow, no occlusion is allowed to settle the question. Artsy: "Mullen depicts oblique planes of transparent color that challenge the viewer's perception of dimensionality. Alternating between convex and concave perspectives, his paintings appear like floating abstract structures and portals into the picture plane." You settle on one reading -- a glass box floating out of the wall -- and hold it for a few seconds; then, without anything changing, it inverts into a tunnel cut into the wall. The flip repeats, unbidden, as long as you look. It is a Necker-cube effect executed in colour: the painting never lets you own a stable depth, so looking becomes a small, repeated act of losing and re-finding the object. Works where it shows: "Horizon" (2018), "Helix No. 5" (2024), "Pulse No. 4" (2018), "Augmenting Space" (2015).

**Takeaway:** Depth is a decision the painting refuses to make: it hands you two readings and lets them alternate forever. [C35398]

## 8. The overlap: colour the eye mixes (M4)

The rule: translucent planes overlap, and where they cross, the colour is the optical product of the layers beneath -- a third colour that was never squeezed from a tube. The overlap zone is the painting's real subject. Zipper (SP-Arte 2025): "Each layer changes the colour of the one beneath it." Artsy: "illusionistic abstract paintings give the appearance of architectural spaces seen through layers of colored glass." Marian Cramer: "Canvases gain volume throughout the artist's technique; it appears to look like an encrustation of dozens of multicolored glass plates that move toward the observer." The eye does the mixing. You notice a violet or green that exists nowhere on the palette -- it lives only in the crossing. Move your head and the apparent colour shifts slightly with the light on the glaze, so the overlap feels like glass rather than paint. The painting rewards slow scanning: each intersection is a small discovery, and the "architecture" is really a diagram of colour relationships. Works where it shows: the "Proximity" series (2025) -- overlapping translucent stripes forming a tunnel at the canvas centre; "Vortex No. 1" (2021); "Synesthesia 64 / 85".

**Takeaway:** The palette is a lie of omission: the painting's best colours were never bought, they were born where two planes cross. [C35399]

## 9. Three tubes: the restricted palette (M5)

The rule: the palette is deliberately capped -- most often three primary pigments -- so that every colour event in the painting is a consequence of layering and overlap, not of buying a new tube. Zipper: "a deliberately restricted palette, often limited to three primary pigments, to investigate how subtle chromatic and structural variations can produce significant perceptual shifts." The trio is named as phthalo turquoise, quinacridone magenta, and primary yellow -- AATONAU confirms the triad, and a Zipper Instagram caption names the same three pigments; this study carries the names on those two sources and no stronger one. The extreme form is the White Series: white paint only, on raw linen. And the restriction has a stated purpose, from a 2016 interview: "For this new series of works I decided to restrict myself to two colours and a two dimensional surface that through repetition could create the illusion of a three dimensional object/form... The reason for restricting my pallet and motif is to allow my subconscious to explore and wonder thinking as little as possible." Restriction makes difference audible: with only three pigments, a one-millimetre shift in overlap or one extra glaze reads as a large event. In the White Series the effect inverts -- with colour removed entirely, the viewer starts seeing depth in near-whites, in the weave, in the gap between white and linen.

**Takeaway:** Restriction is the instrument: cap the palette and the smallest variation becomes the whole content. [C35400]

## 10. The rectangle: grid as depth engine (M6)

The rule: the rectangle is the primal unit. It repeats -- as bands, grids, stacked sheets, modules -- with small deviations in spacing, angle, or colour. Repetition is not decoration; it is the instrument that manufactures depth, rhythm, and optical instability on a flat surface. Zipper: "Rectangles, grids, and repeated modules function as a compositional axis and, at the same time, as devices for generating depth, optical instability, and rhythms that never fully stabilize." His own site: "Daniel Mullen's primal form is the rectangle... The rectangle recurs as a compositional anchor and as a framework for generating depth beyond the surface. Within this constraint, rhythm and deviation, transparency and interruption, are allowed to unfold." The grid first reads as order -- a calm, architectural scaffold. Then the small deviations accumulate: one band a fraction wider, one angle a degree off, one colour a touch warmer, and the flat field starts to ripple, fold, and recede at the centre. The rhythm "never fully stabilizes": the eye keeps trying to lock the pattern and keeps failing by a hair, which is what makes a static painting feel as if it is moving. Works where it shows: the "Proximity" series (2025, largest "Proximity XIX", 205 x 250 cm); "Future Monuments 16 / 21 / 37 / 43"; "Ephemeral Field" (2020); the "Helix" series (2024-25), modules bent into rotation.

**Takeaway:** The grid is not the structure, it is the engine: repeat the rectangle, deviate by a hair, and flatness starts to move. [C35401]

## 11. The ground: linen as emptiness (M7)

The rule: the linen is never primed away. Unpainted ground is not background or leftover; it is the philosophical subject -- Eastern emptiness (a positive, holistic fullness) set against the painted white surface as Western nothingness (an absolute, negative void). His artist statement, in full because it is the clearest thing he has written: "I see the plain canvas as the emptiness and the painted white surface as the nothingness. I want to explore the potential space between them, essentially creating paintings within the surfaces that would normally be the starting point for a painting. Therefore all surfaces are flat, depth can only be created through gradation, scale, repetition, and spacing." Marian Cramer glosses it the same way: "Pure white as Western experience of absolute Nothingness and the raw linen as the Eastern universal approach of a holistic positive sense of being in emptiness." At first the White Series looks like almost nothing -- a pale field with faint geometry. Stay with it and the nothing differentiates: the raw linen warms, the white cools, a plane advances a millimetre, a void opens between two whites. Works: "Across the Void (White Series 000)", "Above the Void (White Series 001)", "Becoming (White Series 002)", "Slant (White Series 003)", all 2015; "Observing the Void" (2019).

**Takeaway:** The void is not the absence of painting, it is the painting: the linen is the subject and the white is the question asked of it. [C35402]

## 12. Scale: model or monument (M8)

The rule: no human figure, no horizon line, no familiar object, no architectural detail at a known scale. A volume could be a tabletop model or a monument; the painting refuses to say. "I create images of abstract architectural volumes and shapes, that seem weightless and transparent, and in which the scale is left to the imagination of the viewer." Colossal: "The forms might seem to reference glass panels or other architectural configurations but that is only the scaffolding for the viewer to locate themselves within." The viewer's body becomes the scale reference. Standing close, the planes feel like a small glass model you could pick up; stepping back, the same planes read as a building, a plaza, a monument. The painting oscillates between model and monument as you move, and because there is no figure to anchor it, you keep re-deciding. The joke is in the titles: "Monument to the Future" (2015, 180 x 180 cm) is a monument to nothing built, light and space rather than stone. Works where it shows: "Constructing the Horizon" (2015), "Threshold" (2020, 240 x 205 cm), "Platonic Formations" (2020), the "Future Monuments" series generally.

**Takeaway:** Scale is the viewer's job, not the painting's: the same planes are a model up close and a monument at a distance, and the painting will not choose. [C35403]

## 13. One variable at a time (M9)

The rule: each work changes a single parameter from the last -- the colour, or the distance between lines -- and holds everything else constant. The practice is systematic and cumulative: one work leads to the next, and series are revisited cyclically rather than finished. His own words, verbatim because they are the method statement of the whole practice: "I'm a maker first and foremost. And as such, I'm experimentally driven -- meaning I research by doing. Even changing one aspect of a composition, for instance, the color or distance between lines can have a vastly different effect. In shifting one variable at a time, there grows a long-term steady progression and evolution in my practice. Because of this, my methodology becomes almost systematic, and there is a continuity between each work, as one leads to the next." The process is cyclical, "like a cycle of breath" -- Heraclitus, "you cannot step twice in the same river" -- and he returns to series such as "Future Monuments" (2015, revisited 2020-21) believing change is perpetual. Hung together, a series reads as a single argument unfolding: you can see the one thing that changed and feel how much it changed. The "Proximity" series (2025, I-XIX) is the clearest demonstration -- same band system, the variable is the colour shift ("Pink Shift", "Purple Shift", "Orange Shift", "Green Shift", "Turquoise-Blue Shift"). He also culls hard: "I usually produce far more than ever leaves the studio, and some works stay with me while others are destroyed."

**Takeaway:** The series is the experiment: change one thing, hold the rest, and let the difference between near-identical works become the content. [C35404]

## 14. Future Monuments, 2015 and again 2020-21

The series that made his name began around 2015 -- "Monument to the Future" (2015, 180 x 180 cm), "Constructing the Horizon" (2015), "Event Horizon" (2015) -- and the concept is stated in the Kogan Amaro text: "The monument of the future does not uphold existing structures. It is neither dominating, oppressive, predetermined nor physically definable. Instead, it is light, space and movement expanding our perception in order to open our imagination to other futures." Artnet: the series "tries to translate the intangible experiences of sound, light, and space" and "attempts to assign visual corollaries to the amorphous experiences of sound, movement, light, and shadow." The works are numbered rather than titled within the series ("Future Monuments 006, 007, 009, 010, 015, 016, 020, 021, 036, 037, 043, 048"), and the numbering is the point: the monument is a system with instances, not a set of unique pictures. He revisited the series in 2020-21 -- "Slouching Towards No. 5 (Future Monuments 048)" (2021), "Continuum (Future Monuments 009)" (2020, 178 x 240 cm per Zipper; 240 x 205 cm per Zurich Art Weekend, and STIR dates it 2021 -- the dimensions and the year are both split, and this study carries Zipper's figure with the split flagged) -- and the revisit changed the geometry: flat planes became vortices ("Vortex No. 1", 2021), circles ("Circular Horizons No. 4", 2021), helices ("Helix" series, 2024-25). The motif rotates and bends; the rectangle stops being the default container.

**Takeaway:** The monument is a system with instances: number it, revisit it, and let the geometry learn to turn. [C35405]

## 15. The White Series

The White Series is the most reduced work of the practice and the clearest statement of its philosophy. Only plain canvas and white paint -- white as a substitute for gesso, so that the painting is made entirely out of the surfaces that would normally be the starting point for a painting. "In the current White Series I am bringing together two philosophical standpoints, from Western thoughts on nothingness to eastern philosophical thoughts on emptiness. Emptiness is considered a spiritual state of being to be aspired; 'Stare' for example suggests that nothingness is a positive state of being and emptiness a negative." The works are numbered, not titled -- "Across the Void (White Series 000)", "Above the Void (White Series 001)", "Becoming (White Series 002)", "Slant (White Series 003)", all 2015 -- and "Stare" is the work he himself cites as the example of the idea, though its year and dimensions were not found anywhere in this run. "Observing the Void" (2019) is the later continuation, in the Stichting Strand Links collection. All surfaces are flat, so depth can only be created through gradation, scale, repetition, and spacing -- which is why the White Series is the purest test of the method: with colour removed, only the system is left, and it has to do all the work.

**Takeaway:** Take away the colour and the system has nowhere to hide: the White Series is the method with its clothes off. [C35406]

## 16. Synesthesia: dates as colour

The most collaborative mechanism in the practice belongs to two authors. With his wife, the artist and filmmaker Lucy Cordes Engelman, Mullen paints as a conduit for her sensorial experience between colour and time: she is a synesthete, he is not, and she defines the chromatic matches while he builds the line systems. Each number 0-9 is a fixed hue, and a date becomes a layered colour-field -- "16th-17th Century" (2018), "37-67" (2019), "27-83" (2020, 180 x 150 cm), "80-49", "90's-10's" (2019). The centuries become stacked translucent sheets of time; two numbers become two colour-fields, layered glass-like plates that appear to move toward the viewer. The works were shown together with the White Series in "Shifting Perceptions" (Marian Cramer, Amsterdam, 2019) and in "The Color of Sound" (Sun Valley Museum of Art, Idaho, 13 January - 18 March 2023), which is the right pairing: the White Series is colour removed, Synesthesia is colour assigned by a rule the painter does not own. The mechanism is the same as the rest of the practice -- a restricted system, a fixed mapping, variation by repetition -- but the mapping comes from another person's nervous system, and the painter is the instrument, not the author of the code.

**Takeaway:** Let someone else's senses write the palette: the system is the same, but the colours arrive from outside the painter. [C35407]

## 17. False Empire and the 2017-19 shows

The mid-period shows mark the moment the system stabilises and the scale grows. "False Empire" (Appels Gallery, Amsterdam, 2017) is the solo that gives the era its name -- "Hope" (2017, acrylic on linen, 180 x 160 cm) is its large canvas, luminous planes rising through a dark ground, the title naming the affect the light is asked to carry. The same year he wins the public prize at the Amsterdam Summer Show and shows in "Radiating Spaces" at the Direktorenhaus Museum, Berlin, and "A World Away" with Neil Fortune and Niek Peters. Then 2018: "In Search of Higher Worlds" (Privateview Gallery, Turin). Then 2019, the busiest year: "Colour Equation" (Kogan Amaro, Sao Paulo, curated by Ana Carolina Ralston), "Shifting Perceptions" (Marian Cramer, Amsterdam), "In a Realm of Luminescence" (Elan Fine Art, Vancouver), the Aesthetica shortlist show at York Art Gallery, the OSTRALE Biennale in Dresden, "Dusk Till Dawn" in Milan. The 160-180 cm canvas becomes standard; the triad palette settles; the planes begin to tilt, swirl and recede -- monoliths ("Aligning Monolith", 2017), labyrinths ("Labyrinth (Surfacing Series 001)", 2016), "interactions" (2018-19). The references shift with the work: Hilma af Klint, Josef Albers, Bridget Riley, Emma Kunz join the earlier De Stijl set, and he places himself "somewhere between Geometric Abstraction and the [California] Space and Light movement."

**Takeaway:** 2017-19 is the system at full pressure: bigger canvases, a settled triad, and the planes learning to tilt. [C35408]

## 18. Ephemeral Fields, Zurich 2021: scale grows

"Ephemeral Fields" (Kogan Amaro, Zurich, 2021) is the big-canvas show: 23 paintings averaging 150 x 140 cm, the largest a 400 x 190 cm diptych, led by "Ephemeral Field" (2020, acrylic on linen, 190 x 170 cm). The Kogan Amaro text says the eye tracks "between images" across multiple horizons, and the transparently primed linen lets paint seep into the weave so the forms feel weightless. The scale matters because the mechanisms scale with it: a glaze that reads as atmosphere at 40 cm reads as weather at 240 cm, and the convex/concave flip that is a parlour trick at small size becomes architectural at two metres -- you stand inside the ambiguity instead of looking at it. The same years bring "Threshold" (2020, 240 x 205 cm), "Platonic Formations" (2020, 220 x 205 cm), "Rose" (2020, 180 x 150 cm), "87-03" (2020, 205 x 220 cm per panel, a commission at diptych scale). The largest works of the practice are all from 2020-21, and they are the proof that the system -- three pigments, one rectangle, one variable at a time -- does not run out at scale. It gets stranger.

**Takeaway:** Scale is not size, it is immersion: at two metres the flip stops being a trick and becomes a room you stand inside. [C35409]

## 19. (In)Visible Within, 2022: Barragan's light

The first Zipper Galeria solo, "(In)Visible Within" (Sao Paulo, opened November 2022, curated by Ana Carolina Ralston), is about luminosity and its delicate and decisive relations with its surroundings -- 15 paintings on the first floor, works on paper under bevelled wood on the second. The show carries a direct import from the September-November 2022 Casa Lu residency in Mexico City: Luis Barragan, whose ability "to use natural light to bathe the spaces of his design in different colour hues blew my mind" (STIR). For the Brazil show Mullen hung coloured plastic filters over the skylights, so the gallery's own daylight became part of the palette -- the room's light, filtered, falling on the glazes. It is the first time the exhibition architecture is treated as a material of the work rather than a container for it, and it points straight at the sculpture turn: if the light in the room is part of the painting, then the room is part of the painting, and the wall is next. The show's epigraph is Albers, quoted by Ralston: "One line plus one line results in many meanings." Goethe is quoted too, in the same text: "Light produces an organ that becomes its equal."

**Takeaway:** Let the room in: coloured filters over the skylight made the gallery's daylight a pigment, and the painting stopped ending at its edge. [C35410]

## 20. The frame breaks: shaped panels, 2022

The break is dated to 2022, and it is a break with the rectangle itself. "Recently, he has also started making work on cutout wooden panels. 'What excites me in particular with these works is that the frame of the painting disappears and instead it is the wall that the work hangs on which becomes the universe surrounding it. And due to the perspective that deviates from that of the architecture, the work can subvert our sense of the built environment.'" (STIR, March 2023). "Spatial-drift, No. 7" (2022, acrylic on shaped wood panel) is the dated example: the support is cut to the form, so the painting's edge is a drawn line, not a manufactured one. The move was already announced years earlier -- ArtConnect, c. 2016-17: "in just the last few months I have begun exploring making works on paper and works on shaped wooden panels... I am also excited in the coming years to consistently and systematically expand into materials that have a physical presence: such as glass, ceramic, and also non-physical materials such as light." The glass and light works are still aspiration -- none were found in this run -- but the ceramic arrived (EKWC, 2023) and the panels arrived first. With no frame, the wall is the ground, and the panel's deviating perspective argues with the real architecture of the room.

**Takeaway:** Cut the support to the form and the wall becomes the canvas: the painting stops ending at its edge and starts arguing with the room. [C35411]

## 21. Ceramics at EKWC, 2023

The 2023 residency at the European Ceramic Work Centre (EKWC) in Oisterwijk is the quiet hinge between the panels and the steel. Pigmented porcelain enters the practice -- confirmed as a medium by Zipper and by his own about page, and the Nada Isolado porcelain reliefs were "developed through sustained engagement with each medium, including experiments carried out in residencies such as the European Ceramic Work Center (EKWC)". But no individual porcelain work with a title and date was found on any page read in this run, and this study does not invent one. What the residency proves is the method travelling: the same logic -- restricted palette, repeated module, glaze as depth -- applied to a material that is fired, not painted, and that holds colour inside its body rather than on its skin. The porcelain matters less as output than as evidence that the system is material-independent: it is a set of rules about layering, repetition and restriction, and it survives the move from linen to clay. The "Test Case XXXI" show at EKWC (2024) is the exhibition record of the residency.

**Takeaway:** The rules survive the material: the same system in porcelain proves the system was never about linen. [C35412]

## 22. The 3D turn: steel, MDF, porcelain (M10)

The rule: the painted logic is carried off the canvas into real space -- cut and shaped wooden panels with no frame; stacked mirror-polished steel planes; porcelain and painted-MDF reliefs. The wall the work hangs on replaces the linen as the universe, and the viewer's own reflection and movement become part of the image. Zipper (Nada Isolado, 2025): "The mirrored steel sculptures extend these questions into three-dimensional space, implicating the viewer's own reflection"; "the polished metal of the sculptures reflects the colors and light of the surrounding environment, incorporating space as an active element in the work." His site on the first steel works ("Edge of Illusion", Enlace Lima, 2025): they "explore the same geometry of his two-dimensional compositions within a three-dimensional space... formed by stacked planes that seem to capture a moment of movement." Named works: "Liminal Suspensions -- Ascent / Plume / Helical" (2025, polished metal plates; "Helical" 110 x 43 x 42.5 cm; "liminal suspensions -- helix in tension" 100 x 36 x 50 cm); "Responsive Forms I: echo of mass" (2025, MDF, 150 x 90 x 14 cm), "Responsive Forms II: lateral accent" (160 x 95 x 15 cm), "Responsive Forms III: stacked alignment" (150 x 110 x 17 cm). In the paintings you move your eyes; in the sculptures you must move your body. Walk past a Liminal Suspension and your reflection slides across the polished planes, the room's colours wash over the steel, and the "painting" re-composes itself with every step -- the work is never the same twice because you are in it.

**Takeaway:** The endgame of the whole practice: perception as something that happens between object, light, room, and moving body -- not in isolation, but through interaction. [C35413]

## 23. Nada Isolado, Sao Paulo 2025

"Nada isolado / nothing in isolation" (Zipper Galeria, Sao Paulo, 2025) is the second Zipper solo and the pivotal show of the practice so far: the first time several bodies of work are brought together -- paintings, mirrored steel sculptures, porcelain reliefs, wooden constructions -- as one spatial composition across both floors of the gallery, with light reflections bouncing from steel to canvas, shadows spilling across surfaces, the gallery architecture become part of the work. The title is drawn from Matisse on the relational nature of colour: no element -- hue, point, form -- exists in isolation; meaning arises only in encounter. "Conceived as a space for shared perception, the exhibition invites viewers into an environment where meaning is not delivered but generated -- through relation, presence, and movement." The 2025 paintings in the show are the "Proximity" series (I-XIX, acrylic on linen, 90 x 100 cm to 205 x 250 cm; largest "Proximity XIX: quinacridone scaffold with blue-ochre modulation", 205 x 250 cm; "Proximity X: optical scaffold in red-blue compression", 130 x 250 cm) and the "Color interaction" paintings of 2026 that follow from them. The dates are split -- Artsy lists 31 March - 3 May 2025, his own site lists 6 April - 10 May 2025 -- and the study carries both. The related publication is the book "Transfigurations" (with Zipper), 43 works on paper, launched at ART RIO 2024.

**Takeaway:** The show is the thesis in a room: nothing exists in isolation, and the exhibition is the proof -- steel, porcelain, linen and daylight in one argument. [C35414]

## 24. Echoes, Vancouver 2026: colour goes quiet

"Echoes" (Paul Kyle Gallery, Vancouver, April - 16 May 2026) is the newest work and the show Hans shared -- developed over a year, his second exhibition with the gallery (the earlier Vancouver representation was Elan Fine Art; whether Paul Kyle succeeded or simply replaced Elan was not established in this run). The newest interview, Les Nouveaux Riches (5 May 2026), is the record of it, and its central claim is a change of temperature: "Earlier works were more chroma-forward, whereas now colour has become subtler and less definable... I am not really looking for a palette with a strong colour identity. I am looking for a way to hold atmosphere, light, and spatial tension so that the colour remains transitional and inherently unstable." The "INFRA" works (XIX, XX, XXI, 2026; "INFRA XX", 240 x 205 cm) and the "Color interaction" paintings (120 x 240 and 130 x 250 cm) are the large-scale evidence: the same triad, the same bands, but the colour refuses to name itself. Repetition is now the explicit method of looking: "I might work with a single composition and shift the chromatic conditions, and those shifts can completely alter the feeling and perceptual energy of a work. The closer the works are to one another, the more those subtle differences begin to matter... Repetition becomes a way of slowing down the act of looking." And the boundary is now stated as a principle: "The physical boundary of the work is not necessarily its perceptual boundary."

**Takeaway:** The late turn is toward quiet: the colour stops announcing itself and starts holding atmosphere, and repetition becomes a way of slowing the eye down. [C35415]

## 25. The physical and the digital: a painting mistaken for a render

The tension Mullen names as his biggest challenge is also the most generative fact about the work for anyone building with it: "My work happens to translate very well into a digital image -- so well in fact that it can be mistaken for being a digital rendering in the first place. I am very fascinated by this when the work is seen in the physical. All of this creates a presence and perception that is the antithesis of how the work operates digitally. But maybe this tension between the physical and the digital illustrates a broader question about our perception of reality." His about page frames it as the era's question: "How might our visual and emotional sensitivities be shifting as we find ourselves flowing between our physical environment and the techno-sphere, aided in part by back-lit digital devices?" The work is designed to be misread on a screen and correctly read in a room -- the glaze needs raking light, the tape edge needs a close step, the flip needs a body that moves. That is not a flaw in the work, it is the work: a painting that performs differently in the two worlds, and asks which one you trust. For a generative practice the warning is direct: a piece that only works as an image has missed the point, because Mullen's pieces are instruments for a body in a room, and the screen is where they go to be misunderstood.

**Takeaway:** Built to be misread on screen: the work performs in a room and only pretends on a phone, and that gap is the subject. [C35416]

## 26. Influences I: Light and Space

The influence he names first, in his own words, in two separate interviews, is the California Light and Space movement. ArtConnect: "I seek reference and inspiration from those working in the Light and Space Movement of California, such as James Turrell, Peter Alexander, and Eric Orr." Artsy adds Larry Bell to the same sentence. STIR: "Mullen has always had a special interest in the works of artists such as James Turrell and Fred Sandback." Artnet, his own placement: "I would visually place my work somewhere between geometric abstraction and the Light and Space movement of California. I'm interested in phenomenology -- to sense something that is not present, or at least that which seems undefinable, a space out of reach beyond our perception." The link is not a style borrowing but a medium borrowing: Turrell's medium is light and the viewer's perception of it; Mullen's medium is glaze and the viewer's perception of it; in both, the object dematerialises and the viewer is centralised. The stated influence is not a personal relationship -- no meeting, correspondence, or shared exhibition with Turrell or Bell was found in this run -- and this study does not claim one. The mechanism it maps to is M1/M4/M7: the painting as a field of light rather than an image of a thing.

**Takeaway:** Turrell is the lodestar, not the model: light as the medium, perception as the work, the object dissolved. [C35417]

## 27. Influences II: Bauhaus, the three J's, and spiritual abstraction

The second lineage is colour theory, and Mullen names it as a course of study: "Formalistic elements in my work are derived in part from Bauhaus, and the color theory pioneered by Johannes Itten and expanded upon by Joseph Albers." And: "I have studied the three J's of colour theory -- Johannes Itten, Johann Goethe, and Josef Albers -- all of whom in various ways were busy with the study of colour perception. In my work, I am interested in expanding on these fundamental colour lessons, by bringing spatial qualities to the pieces which together create opportunities to challenge our sense of perception." Albers is the operating system under the palette -- restricted palette, relational colour, one-variable experiments are "Interaction of Color" pedagogy translated into spatial painting -- and Albers is quoted as the epigraph to (In)Visible Within: "One line plus one line results in many meanings." The third lineage is spiritual abstraction: "Mullen sees his works as rooted in a spiritualized abstraction and the visual idioms of Hilma af Klint, Josef Albers, Bridget Riley, and Swiss artist Emma Kunz, in particular" (Artnet); "I'm drawn to the esoteric works by Hilma Af Klint" (ArtConnect). Riley is the perceptual link -- the flickering grids and never-stabilising rhythms descend from op-art, translated from black-and-white dazzle into translucent colour. The early lineage is De Stijl and Constructivism -- Mondrian, Van Doesburg, El Lissitzky -- which is where the grid and the rectangle come from, and Rietveld, whose name is on the academy and whose architecture is in the paintings' bones.

**Takeaway:** Three schools, one practice: Bauhaus colour as the operating system, spiritual abstraction as the motive, De Stijl as the bones. [C35418]

## 28. Aesthetic logic: perception is the medium

Put the mechanisms together and the aesthetic is one sentence: the painting is not an image of a space, it is an instrument for perceiving one. Every choice serves that sentence. The glaze makes colour arrive slowly, so looking takes time. The tape makes precision that is visibly handmade, so the eye cannot settle on "machine" or "hand". The flip makes depth undecidable, so the eye must keep working. The overlap makes colours that were never mixed, so the eye does the painting's chemistry. The restricted palette makes small differences large, so attention has nowhere to hide. The grid makes a rhythm that never stabilises, so the static surface feels in motion. The raw linen makes the void the subject, so the painting is about what is not painted. The scale refusal makes the viewer's body the ruler. The one-variable series makes the practice an experiment, so each painting is a measurement. The 3D turn makes the room the ground, so the viewer is inside the instrument. And the current framing -- "meaning is relational... perception is not a static experience, but something that configures itself continuously -- between elements, materials, durations, and presences" -- says what the whole practice has been saying since 2013: the work happens in the encounter, and without a viewer there is no work, only linen and paint.

**Takeaway:** He does not paint spaces; he paints the act of sensing a space. The medium is perception, the linen is only where it happens. [C35419]
