## 1. The brief: the rock star who became the rehearsal man

Kurtenbach's own master plan, after high school, was to become a rock star -- "the computer thing was just a day job to pay the bills." He played in bands in the late 1970s, played The Beatles' *Abbey Road* over and over on his cousin Mervin's little record player, and did grade-school projects on the Space Race, the Beatles, and Pollution. The computer won the day job and then ate the plan: BSc in computer science from the University of Saskatchewan in 1984, graduate school at Toronto from 1986, MSc 1988, PhD 1993. But the musician never quite leaves. Buxton is a tenor saxophonist who became an input theorist; Kurtenbach is a would-be rock star who became one too, and the rhyme is not decorative. A marking menu is a rehearsed gesture -- the same physical movement, practised until the menu is no longer needed -- and rehearsal is a musician's idea before it is an interface idea. He is still writing and recording songs in 2025, in Toronto, with his brother Robert. The day job became the career; the music stayed the through-line.

**Takeaway:** Read him as a musician first. The whole invention is practice made visible: the novice plays from the score, the expert plays from memory, and the movement never changes. [C35308]

## 2. Saskatchewan: farm, Beatles, pre-cast concrete

He grew up on a farm in Saskatchewan -- grade school in the 1970s, self-defined projects, a cousin's record player -- and the farm stays in the biography as more than scenery. The high-school aptitude test, the kind of instrument that is supposed to tell a farm kid his future, recommended exactly one career: pre-cast concrete worker. He tells the story himself, in his thesis acknowledgments, and it is worth keeping because it sets up everything that follows: a system that claims to know what you should do, and a person who declines to be told. The farm also gives him the uncle he never met (section 3) and the sustainability worldview he credits to a grade-school project on Pollution. Nothing in the Saskatchewan years predicts Toronto, except the habit of defining his own projects and ignoring the test that told him what to be.

**Takeaway:** The aptitude test is the first bad menu in his life. He spends the next forty years building menus that do not tell you what to do -- they wait for you to show them. [C35309]

## 3. The uncle he never met: Nestor Burtnyk

The family thread is the one biographical fact that reads like a plot device and happens to be true. His uncle, Nestor Burtnyk, was a pioneer of Canadian computer graphics at the National Research Council in Ottawa -- co-author, with Marcelli Wein, of a 1976 paper on interactive skeleton techniques for animation, and a collaborator with Peter Foldes on the 1973 National Film Board animated film *Hunger*. Kurtenbach grew up on a farm in Saskatchewan and Burtnyk worked in Ottawa; they never met while he was growing up. He only connected the dots in Ron Baecker's advanced computer-graphics graduate class at Toronto. Then, in 1996, Buxton told Kurtenbach he would be presenting the CHCCS Achievement Award to Burtnyk -- not knowing they were related -- and Kurtenbach turned up with his Uncle Wayne and his mother and father. Twenty-two years later, Kurtenbach received the same award himself. The uncle is not a collaborator and not a doorway to the work; he is the proof that the field was already in the family, unrecognised, until Toronto made it visible.

**Takeaway:** The lineage was there before he knew it. Some doorways are not walked through; they are recognised, late, from the inside. [C35310]

## 4. Toronto 1986: entering the Dynamic Graphics Project

He arrives at the University of Toronto in 1986, entering a graduate programme in the Dynamic Graphics Project -- the lab Leslie Mezei founded in 1967 and Ron Baecker co-founded in 1969, the lab Buxton joined in 1975 as an informal artist-in-residence. Mezei himself had left academia in 1978, eight years before Kurtenbach arrived, so there is no personal overlap; the connection is institutional, a grandfather, not a teacher. The teachers are Buxton, Baecker, and the Input Research Group. The MSc comes in 1988; the PhD in 1993. What Toronto gives him, beyond the degrees, is a problem that is already in the air: mode errors -- the user who types navigation commands while the system is in insert mode, the system that punishes a correct action performed in the wrong state. His earliest dated co-authorship, INTERACT '90 with Sellen and Buxton, is an experiment on preventing exactly that. Everything after it is an answer to the same question: how do you build an interface that cannot be in the wrong mode?

**Takeaway:** He enters through the lab's open wound. Mode errors are the problem; the marking menu is the answer; the PhD is the proof. [C35311]

During graduate school he interns twice, and the order matters. First Apple Computer Advanced Technology Group, researching gesture-based input for graphical interfaces. Then, in a summer internship at Xerox PARC -- the year is not pinned in any source read, but the work lands in 1991-1993 -- he is daydreaming, in his own word, when the insight comes: a path through a set of pie menus could make a unique mark, and that was the invention of marking menus. PARC also gives him Thomas P. The PARC interlude is where the gesture vocabulary gets its patents and where the marking-menu idea gets its first airing outside Buxton's basement.

**Takeaway:** The internships are the two halves of the career in miniature: Apple, the gesture; PARC, the mark. He brings both back to Toronto. [C35312]

## 6. The insight: a path through pie menus

The invention, in his own account, is one sentence: "a path through a set of pie menus could make a unique mark." Pie menus already existed -- Don Hopkins had built them in Sun's NeWS and MIT's X, and Callahan, Hopkins, Weiser and Shneiderman had measured them in 1988 (about 15% faster than linear menus, 42% fewer errors, selection time roughly independent of item position). What they did not do is what Kurtenbach adds: the mark *is* the selection. In a pie menu you must see the menu to choose; in a marking menu you press, flick in a direction, and lift, and the direction is the choice -- no menu need ever appear. The path through the pie is the command. The insight is small enough to fit in a daydream and large enough to carry a PhD, four papers, a thesis, and a shipping product. It is also, characteristically, an insight about *removing* something: the menu, the visible affordance, the thing the user has to look at. The gesture replaces the display.

**Takeaway:** The invention is a subtraction. Take the menu away and the movement is still the command -- that is the whole of it. [C35313]

## 7. GEdit 1991: the debut

The debut is UIST '91, Hilton Head, Monday 11-Wednesday 13 November 1991: Kurtenbach and Buxton, "Issues in Combining Marking and Direct Manipulation Techniques", pp. 137-144, with GEdit, a toy graphical editor of squares, circles and triangles, as the testbed. A shorter version, "GEdit: a testbed for editing by contiguous gesture", appears the same year in *SIGCHI Bulletin* 23(2), pp. 22-26 -- the two 1991 publications must not be conflated, and "GEdit" is not the UIST paper's title. In GEdit every command is a single continuous pen stroke, and different aspects of the stroke carry different arguments: a shorthand symbol creates a shape centred on the stroke's starting point; a horizontal strike-through deletes; circling a group and extending the line to a destination moves it -- verb, direct object and indirect object in one uninterrupted mark, exactly like a proof-reader's move mark; append a small "C" and the same stroke copies instead. There is no tool, no palette, no current mode: the command is the mark, so there is nothing to be in the wrong state of. The paper won the UIST Lasting Impact Award in 2005, presented Sunday 23 October 2005 in Seattle, fourteen years after publication -- the award is for this paper specifically, not for the thesis or the programme.

**Takeaway:** The debut already contains the whole programme: one stroke, three arguments, no mode. Everything after 1991 is the same idea, measured and shipped. [C35314]

## 8. The three principles: self-revelation, guidance, rehearsal

The thesis states three design principles as definitions, and they are the load-bearing wall of the work. **Self-revelation:** the system interactively provides information about what commands exist and how to invoke them -- menus and buttons are self-revealing; marks are not, because the user must generate them from memory. **Guidance:** the way revelation happens should guide the user *through* invoking the command, without interrupting the articulation of it -- the counter-example is the UNIX man page, which forces you to stop invoking in order to learn how to invoke. **Rehearsal:** the guidance must be a physical rehearsal of the expert's action. Built on these is the **unfolding interface**: no explicit novice/expert modes; the interface starts with a small amount of information ("press and wait for more") and the user incrementally adds functions to their repertoire by exploring -- contrasted with Carroll and Carrithers' "Training Wheels" (1984), which has explicit novice/expert modes with different function sets. The principles are Kurtenbach's specific definitions, explicitly distinguished from similar general principles by Norman and Draper (1986) and Shneiderman (1987). Buxton and Moran co-author the GI '94 restatement; the formulation is Kurtenbach's.

**Takeaway:** Three words do the work of the thesis. A system that reveals itself, guides you through using it, and makes every use a rehearsal of expertise -- that is the design, and the marking menu is only its first instance. [C35315]

## 9. Press-and-wait: the third of a second

A marking menu has no visible affordance until the user presses the pen down and holds it still. After a short dwell -- approximately one third of a second, 0.33 seconds in the thesis -- a radial menu pops up directly under the pen tip. Move before the threshold elapses and no menu ever appears: the movement is read as a mark instead. The wait is the entire mode switch, and it is the only mode switch: no key, no button, no menu bar to enter "menu mode". The number is worth getting exactly right, because the sources disagree in a way that has already misled one study: the thesis, the InterCHI '93 paper, the CHI '94 paper and the UIST '95 paper all say one third of a second, and the CHI '94 case-study implementation used 0.33 seconds; one passage of the 1993 *Human-Computer Interaction* journal paper says "approximately 1 second", most plausibly a draft value from an earlier implementation. This study uses 0.33 seconds throughout and does not mix the two figures. The watchable behaviour is the fork: press, stillness, and after a beat a ring of labels blooms under the cursor; press, immediate flick, and no ring at all, only a stroke. The same starting action forks into two visibly different outcomes purely on timing.

**Takeaway:** A third of a second is the whole interface. Hold still and the system shows you; move and it trusts you -- and the trust is the point. [C35316]

## 10. The expert mark: the flick that executes

Press down and move immediately, without waiting: the system reads the stroke's direction -- and, for hierarchies, its sequence of direction changes -- as the command. No menu is displayed, nothing is occluded, nothing needs to be read. The mark is the selection; lifting the pen confirms and terminates. In the CHI '94 case study the numbers are stark: average mark time 0.18 seconds for one user and 0.40 seconds for the other, from press to release, against 1.10 and 1.54 seconds for the same selection via the menu; average mark length about one inch. Marks were on average 3.5 times faster than menu selection in that case study (7 times for one user, 4 times for the other; even with the 0.33-second wait subtracted, still 4.2 and 3.0 times faster). Tapia and Kurtenbach's 1995 paper cites "up to 10 times faster" from laboratory research -- a different measure, laboratory not field, and the two figures must not be presented as one number. With practice, case-study users "tended towards using the marks 100% of the time", and the more frequently a command was invoked, the more likely it was invoked by mark.

**Takeaway:** The expert does not go faster by hurrying. The expert goes faster by not waiting for permission -- the flick is the permission. [C35317]

## 11. Rehearsal: the same movement, novice or expert

The physical movement needed to select an item from the displayed radial menu is *identical* to the physical movement needed to draw the mark for that item. The sentence is verbatim in the InterCHI '93 paper and in the UIST '91 paper: "the physical movement involved in selecting an item from the menu is identical to the physical movement required to make the mark corresponding to that item." A novice selecting "square" by dragging up-and-right from the pie menu is already practising the up-and-right mark that will later create a square without any menu. There is no second protocol to learn -- contrast with accelerator keys, where the novice uses the mouse and the expert presses a key, two unrelated physical actions. Watch a user over weeks and the transition is visible as the fading out of the menu, not a change in the hand: the ring appears less and less often, the hand makes the same flick earlier and earlier, until the ring never appears at all. Case-study users passed through observable intermediate stages -- mark-confirm (draw the mark, wait at the end, the menu pops up along the mark to verify) and mark-corrected (verify, then reselect) -- each occurring under 10% of the time and disappearing with experience. This is the thesis's central design principle, and the reason the whole technique works.

**Takeaway:** Learning is not acquiring a new skill. It is the menu getting out of the way of a movement you already know. [C35318]

## 12. Hierarchies: the zig-zag and its limits

Hierarchic marking menus stack radial menus: selecting an item with a submenu displays the submenu, and the user continues in the same stroke. The expert equivalent is a **zig-zag mark** -- a single continuous stroke whose successive straight segments pick one item per level, so the shape of the mark *is* the path through the hierarchy. The InterCHI '93 paper, "The Limits of Expert Performance Using Hierarchic Marking Menus" (Amsterdam, Saturday 24-Thursday 29 April 1993, pp. 482-487), measures the bounds: error rate, not speed, is the limiting factor as breadth and depth grow, with a significant error-rate jump when depth exceeds 2 and breadth is 8 or 12. Reliable: 4-item menus up to 4 levels deep, or 8-item menus up to 2 levels deep -- a 2-level by 8-item hierarchy gives fast access to 64 items with one mark. Even numbers beat odd: 8-item menus were no worse than 7-item, and 12-item menus were faster and more accurate than 11-item, the clock-face familiarity effect. The pen beats the mouse slightly for hierarchic marks, but the difference is small. The hard problem underneath is ambiguity: a straight line drawn through a 2-level menu does not say whether it selected the parent or the child. The thesis offers four solutions -- boundary crossing (level by distance travelled, but marks become size-dependent), no-category selections (parents do nothing, works only to 2 levels), no-oping (waste one sector per menu), and **axis-shifting** (rotate each child menu so no child item sits at the parent's angle, so every level change is signalled by an angle change) -- and recommends axis-shifting for menus deeper than 2 levels. The experiment was designed by Kurtenbach with Sellen's experimental-design help, and the thesis credits her for it.

**Takeaway:** The zig-zag is the hierarchy made drawable. And the limit is honest: past two levels of eight, the hand starts to lie, and the system should not ask it to. [C35319]

## 13. The ink trail: the stroke you can see

In a marking menu the cursor's path leaves a visible **ink trail**, "similar to a pen stroke on paper." This is what distinguishes a marking menu from a plain pie menu: selecting a sector *creates a visual mark*. The trail is both feedback and the command's physical trace, and the screen accumulates a calligraphy of recent commands. The experimental subtlety is worth keeping, because it cuts against the romance: in the thesis's Chapter 3 experiment, a "Marking" group (hidden menus plus ink trail) and a "Hidden" group (hidden menus, no trail) behaved almost identically -- subjects treated both as "selecting from menus I must imagine", and the ink trail made no discernible difference to stroke shape. Users *reported* the trail disturbing with a trackball ("paths looked erratic and inaccurate"). So the trail's value is metaphorical and confirmatory, not articulatory: it tells you what you did, it does not help you do it. Marks were approximately straight in all conditions, and no subject used an alternate strategy like starting at the top item and sliding over. The honest version of the ink trail is a record, not a guide.

**Takeaway:** The trail does not teach the hand. It witnesses it -- and witnessing is enough, because the command needs a visible past. [C35320]

## 14. Angle, not length: scale-invariant marks

A mark is recognised by the **angle** of its stroke, not its length -- a deliberate design choice, argued in the thesis in four parts. First, speed: all items in a radial menu are equidistant from the centre, so every item takes the same time to reach, where in a linear menu the last item is slowest. Second, scale invariance: a small "L" means the same as a large "L", and the user is never forced to draw at a prescribed size -- marks are scale-insensitive but rotation-sensitive, like handwriting. Third, control-display immunity: with a mouse, gain and pointer acceleration change the *length* of a mark but never its *angle*, so a 45-degree mark is 45 degrees whether it is one inch or two. Fourth, everyday semantics: marks in daily life are interpreted by shape and direction, not size. The corollary, noted by Tapia and Kurtenbach in 1995, is that expert speed comes from drawing *very small* marks: "a particular selection from a menu four levels deep may be made by a four-inch long mark, or more quickly by a one-inch mark of the same shape." The failure mode is the mirror of the strength: rotate the same stroke 45 degrees and it silently becomes a different command.

**Takeaway:** The mark has no size. It has a direction, and the direction is the whole address. [C35321]

## 15. The thesis, 1993

*The Design and Evaluation of Marking Menus*, PhD thesis, Graduate Department of Computer Science, University of Toronto, 1993 -- Gordon Paul Kurtenbach, supervisor Bill Buxton, committee Ron Baecker, Mark Chignell, Marilyn Mantei, Ken Sevcik, Cathy Wolf. The abstract states six findings: 4-, 8- and 12-item menus are advantageous for marks; 4-item menus are reliable to 4 levels deep and 8-item menus to 2 levels; the pen is slightly more accurate than the mouse; in a practical application users used marks about 100% of the time; marks were 3.5 times faster than menu selection in that application; and the principles generalise to other mark types. The structure is the argument in order: introduction and principles; the marking-menu design space; the non-hierarchic evaluation (condensed from the 1993 *Human-Computer Interaction* paper, Kurtenbach, Sellen and Buxton, 8(1), pp. 1-23); the hierarchic evaluation (expanded from the InterCHI '93 paper); and a case and design study, including the pull-out menu for screen edges and the crib-sheet animator. The acknowledgments carry the pre-cast concrete worker joke and the thanks to Sellen for experimental design. One access note for the next reader: the old Autodesk Research thesis URL now redirects to the Autodesk Research home page; the live copy is on research.autodesk.com, also mirrored in a Valve v. Immersion patent filing. The thesis body itself was not read end to end for this study -- front matter and mechanism sections only -- and that is stated here rather than smoothed over.

**Takeaway:** A thesis is a design space with measurements. The principles are chapter one; the numbers are chapters three to five; the shipping product is chapter six, still to come. [C35322]

## 17. The restaurant: Lloyd, bring me a number 30

The InterCHI '93 paper opens with a different anecdote, and it must not be confused with the basement story. Kurtenbach, first-person narrator, goes to a restaurant: on early visits he studies the menu and orders "dish number 30"; on the fifth or sixth visit he just tells the waiter, "Lloyd, bring me a number 30", without waiting for the menu. "Things happen faster when you know what you want." It is an anecdote about expertise, not about invention -- about the transition from reading the menu to naming the dish, which is exactly what a marking menu lets a user do. Whether Kurtenbach or Buxton wrote the anecdote itself is unverified; the paper is Kurtenbach-first-authored and the voice is first-person. It belongs in the study because it is the paper's own framing of its subject, and because it shows the difference between the two stories this study carries: the PARC daydream is where the invention came from, the basement floor is where its meaning was named, and the restaurant is where its behaviour was explained. Three stories, three jobs, none of them interchangeable.

**Takeaway:** The expert does not stop using the menu. The expert stops needing to read it -- and that is a different sentence. [C35324]

## 18. Mode errors: Sellen's pedal

Before the marking menu there was the mode error, and the mode-error work is Sellen's, not Kurtenbach's -- he is a co-author, middle of three, and the study says so. Sellen, Kurtenbach and Buxton (INTERACT '90, Cambridge, Monday 27-Friday 31 August 1990, pp. 667-673; expanded in *Human-Computer Interaction* 7(2), 1992, pp. 141-164) ran two experiments on mode errors in a text-editing task: kinesthetic feedback (keyboard versus a **foot pedal** to change mode) crossed with presence or absence of visual mode feedback. Both help, but kinesthetic beats visual -- in fewer errors and in lower cognitive load. The second experiment isolates why: the advantage comes from the pedal requiring the user to *actively maintain* insert mode. A non-latching pedal beats a latching one. The conclusion, stated as a principle: **user-maintained mode states prevent mode errors more effectively than system-maintained mode states.** This is the theoretical ground for everything Kurtenbach builds next: GEdit's "no modes at all", press-and-wait, hold-the-spacebar for the Hotbox -- in each, the mode exists only while the body sustains it, and the instant you let go, the mode is gone and cannot be forgotten. Sellen did a postdoc with Buxton, Baecker and Marilyn Tremaine at Toronto's Input Research Group before seven years at EuroPARC; her book with Richard Harper, *The Myth of the Paperless Office* (2001), comes later. She is the third-ranked pick for #813, and the marking-menu core's strongest alternative to Fitzmaurice.

**Takeaway:** A mode you can feel in your foot cannot be forgotten, because forgetting requires letting go, and letting go ends the mode. [C35325]

## 19. The crib-sheet: ghost demonstrations

Marking menus only teach zig-zag gestures. For arbitrary *iconic* gestures -- the Tivoli whiteboard app, where a pigtail deletes and a circle selects -- Kurtenbach, Moran and Buxton (Graphics Interface '94; *Computer Graphics Forum* 13(5), 1994, pp. 305-314) built the **crib-sheet animator**, the single most cinematic mechanism in Kurtenbach's work. Press-and-wait over an object or location, the same trigger as a marking menu, and instead of a menu a **crib-sheet** pops up *some distance away* from the pen tip, so it does not occlude the context and leaves room to draw. It names the context at the top ("In a selection") and lists the applicable functions with example gestures. Draw one of the gestures and the sheet vanishes. And the discipline that matters: you *cannot* invoke a command by tapping the crib-sheet. You must draw the gesture. The help cannot become a crutch that bypasses learning. Press a gesture's "demo" button and the gesture is **animated full-size, in context, emanating from the exact spot originally pressed**, with text annotations appearing at labelled moments ("A pigtail deletes the *selected objects*"). The animation **freezes the moment the user starts drawing**, so the user can *trace* the animated gesture; completing it executes it and dismisses everything. A static example shows neither the gesture's variations nor its context-dependence -- a straight line over a bullet moves a list item, the same line in a margin scrolls -- and displaying all iconic gestures at once causes overlap and confusion. Behind it sits a Gesture Animation Database of hand-drawn examples and annotations, labelled by when they occur in the animation cycle. Kurtenbach is first author; Moran co-led the Tivoli application work at PARC.

**Takeaway:** The help that teaches is a ghost you trace, not a button you press. The demonstration *is* the interface. [C35326]

## 20. Tapia's refinements: labels only, compass star, idealized marks

The industrial-strength revision comes from the field, not the lab. Tapia and Kurtenbach (UIST '95, Pittsburgh, Tuesday 14-Friday 17 November 1995, pp. 189-195) report on marking menus shipping in Alias StudioPaint V3 and in ConEd, a speech-event timeline editor, with about fifteen users. Seven refinements under three principles (maintain visual context; hide unnecessary information; support skill development by graphical feedback): **labels only** -- drop the pie wedges, show only text labels in opaque subtitle-style boxes, so the menu occludes far less of the artwork underneath (born from the observation that a full pie menu "blinded" users in ConEd, like a camera flash); labels may extend beyond their wedge boundaries; all label boxes sized to the largest item, so the menu does not look busy; **hide parent menus** when descending, leaving only the center hole; constrain menus to the **eight compass directions**; draw the menu center as a **compass star**, so a label spilling across a wedge boundary is still unambiguous; and show **idealized marks** -- after a mark is recognised, the system keeps displaying a clean, idealized version of it with the selected item's label until the command completes, the Mac-menu-flash equivalent for gestures, which also teaches, because as users draw smaller marks the smaller idealized mark reinforces the image of smaller, faster marks. Tapia is first author; the thesis explicitly credits "Mark Tapia for his assistance in designing and implementing the alternate graphical layout". These refinements are Tapia-led and must not be credited to Kurtenbach alone. The paper is explicit that there were no formal tests of the refinements -- validation is fifteen users' preference -- and that honesty is part of the record.

**Takeaway:** Shipping is a second experiment, and its results are refinements, not theorems. The menu got quieter because real users asked it to. [C35327]

## 21. Alias: the industrial turn

## 22. T3: two hands and a sheet of glass

T3, the CHI '97 prototype, is the most choreographed system Kurtenbach ever built, and its mechanisms are the most visibly bimanual. Two rotation-sensing pucks, one per hand, on two Wacom tablets (one tablet could not sense rotation for both). One button per puck gives only **four button states** (00/01/10/11), deliberately, so a user can try all combinations while learning: 00, dominant puck moves the cursor and non-dominant moves the **toolglass**; 01, dominant drags; 10, non-dominant pans the artwork; 11, both pressed, **stretch-zoom** -- pull apart to zoom in, push together to zoom out, "stretching the artwork". Rotation is sensed simultaneously in each state. The **toolglass** is a transparent palette that *tracks the non-dominant puck* -- always in the hand, never picked up and put down -- and its buttons are **click-through**: click on a colour button while the cursor is over an object and the object changes colour *through* the glass. The toolglass itself is not Kurtenbach's; it is Bier, Stone, Fishkin, Buxton and Baudel (CHI '94), from Xerox PARC, and Kurtenbach led the *integration*. **Two-handed stretches:** press a toolglass shape button, drag with the dominant hand to sweep out a rectangle from one corner while the non-dominant hand stretches it from the opposite corner -- translate, rotate and scale simultaneously, holding both corners. **Curve guides:** french-curve sheets on the toolglass, the non-dominant hand positioning, rotating and scaling the curve while the dominant hand lays ink, the cursor **snapping to the curve's contour**. A hierarchic marking menu embedded in the top of the toolglass switches toolglass "sheets": 8 items by 2 levels, 64 sheets by 10 tools, 640 commands, each reachable by a quick flick. The paper is honest about the cost: panning, rotating and zooming are *not* self-revealing, and a small amount of a-priori instruction is assumed -- in exchange, no scrollbars, which the paper notes consume about 6-7% of window space, and the eye never leaves the artwork. Then the retrofit into StudioPaint, with the compromises honestly reported: no real-time rotation, no two-tablet setup, toolglass sheets dropped for paint because artists preferred rehearsing gestures.

**Takeaway:** Two hands, one glass, no chrome. The interface disappears into the hands, and the hands disagree productively -- that disagreement is the design. [C35329]

## 24. Tape drawing: the tape corrects the theory

Digital Tape Drawing deserves its own section because it is the one place where the practice corrects the theory, in print. Automotive designers sketch life-size cars on upright walls with black photographic tape: the right hand **unrolls** the tape, the left hand **slides along it, fastening it** to the surface -- even left-handed artists do it this way. The digital version, on an 8-by-6-foot back-projected powerwall with a 6-DOF tracker and one button per hand, keeps the division: right hand is the tape roll, left hand the fastening end; an unfastened tape segment always spans the two cursors; the left button fastens, release cuts; the left cursor is constrained to the segment so straight lines are enforced in software; curves happen by moving both hands, and **segment length regulates smoothness** (long means gentle curves, short is near freehand). Straight versus curve needs **no mode switch**: hold the right hand fixed while the left pins and the line is straight; move the right hand while the left pins and it curves. Curvature corresponds to the distance between the two hands. Field test: about twenty professional tape artists at five studios in England, France and the United States walked up and used it within a minute of instruction, and outperformed the developers. And the theoretical payoff, stated in the paper: tape laying **violates Guiard's kinematic-chain principles** for right-handers -- the right hand is supposed to set the frame of reference while the non-dominant hand does the high-frequency precision work, and tape drawing reverses that -- and the paper argues the tape itself promotes the non-dominant hand's role, with handedness overridden by the left-to-right task direction. The lead is Balakrishnan, second Fitzmaurice, third Kurtenbach, last Buxton; the CHI '02 extension to 3D curves is Grossman-led. Do not present tape drawing as Kurtenbach's invention.

**Takeaway:** The tape knows something the theory does not, and the paper says so. That is what a good prototype is for: letting the practice talk back. [C35331]

## 25. The Hotbox: twelve hundred commands at the cursor

Maya had about 1,200 commands. At most about twenty menus fit in a menubar across a 1,280-pixel screen, so average menus held about sixty items -- information overload -- and hotkeys reached only a small fraction. The Hotbox (Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Owen and Baudel, CHI '99) is the marking-menu idea at commercial scale: hold the **spacebar with the non-dominant hand** and the Hotbox **instantly appears centred at the cursor**, in any Maya window; it **stays displayed as long as spacebar is held**, so a whole series of commands can be fired without re-invoking it; release spacebar and it vanishes. Rows of menus behave like traditional menubars; multiple menubars multiply virtual capacity by four. The screen is divided into **five zones -- North, South, East, West, Center** -- each with a *different* marking menu, accessed by pressing a mouse button while the cursor is in that zone; each mouse button, alone or combined, can carry a different marking menu per zone, up to fifteen custom menus. The design rationale is stated in five categories -- Quantity, Speed (Fitts's law; pop-up at the cursor versus centre of screen), Unification (modes and actions together; one access key; novice operation rehearses expert operation), Graphic Design, Interaction Design -- and the evidence is field evidence: ten in-house users over sixteen months, about a hundred beta customers over thirteen months, shipping in Maya for six months at the time of writing, and a survey of twelve experts, five of whom had removed the menubars entirely. The zoned-menu-bar patents (US 6,915,492; US 7,246,329; US 8,522,159) protect the design. The Hotbox is Kurtenbach-led, first author, at Alias|Wavefront -- the thesis, eleven years on, with a product's name on it. And the held key is the mode (section 18, again): user-maintained, not system-maintained, gone the instant the hand lets go.

**Takeaway:** Twelve hundred commands, one held key. The scale changed; the mechanism did not -- hold, and the world comes to the cursor. [C35332]

## 28. Aesthetic logic: the gesture is the menu

The aesthetic of Kurtenbach's work is an aesthetic of disappearance. Nothing in it wants to be looked at: the menu appears only when you wait, and vanishes the moment you know; the ink trail is a record, not a decoration; the toolglass is transparent by design; the Hotbox dies on release; the crib-sheet forbids you to tap it, because tapping would be looking instead of doing. The visible thing is always the *movement*, never the *control*. That is why the work translates so directly into generative pieces: a marking menu is already a piece -- a field, a hand, a stroke, a consequence -- with the interface removed. The palette of the work is the palette of a pencil test: paper, ink, the faint ghost of the ideal mark laid over the wobbly real one. The drama is timing, not colour: the third of a second that separates showing from knowing; the weeks over which the ring fades; the single stroke that kinks twice and fires a three-level command. The failure modes are part of the aesthetic too, because they are honest: past two levels of eight the marks wobble and misfire; rotate a stroke 45 degrees and it becomes another command; a full pie menu blinds you like a camera flash. A system that admits where it breaks is a system you can trust with your hand. And the through-line, from the Saskatchewan aptitude test to the Hotbox, is a refusal: no system gets to tell you what to do. It waits. It shows, briefly, if you ask by holding still. And then it gets out of the way.

**Takeaway:** Build the gesture, not the menu. If the viewer has to read anything, you have built the wrong thing; if the viewer has to move, you have built the right one. [C35335]
