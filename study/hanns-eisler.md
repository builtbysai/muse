## M1. The Kampflied: a march a crowd can stumble through

March rhythm, walking crotchets in the bass, unexpected syncopations set against them, and Betz, quoted in The Wire, gives the mechanism its sentence: "Unexpected syncopations are set off against the 'walking crotchets' in the bass of the accompaniment. This feature adds a special elasticity and a bracing springiness to the works' aggressive energy." The parts are borrowed, not invented: from 16th- and 17th-century German song come the asymmetrical melody construction and the changes of metre, and from the Russian revolutionary funeral march comes the alternation of major and minor between verse and refrain, and the whole is built so that a hall of workers with no musical training can march to it and still trip on it, and the trip is the point, because a march that never stumbles is a parade, and a march that stumbles is a crowd deciding to keep going. The Solidaritaetslied of 1929-1931 and the Einheitsfrontlied of December 1934 are the pure forms, and Komintern of 1929 is the travelling form, circulated from 1931 in Russian, Polish, French, English, Spanish and more, a marching song built to cross languages the way a slogan crosses a border. **Takeaway:** put the steady step in the bass and the stumble in the tune, and the crowd marches and thinks at the same time. Against the chain: Rzewski also built a march the people could own, M6, but his march is a piano piece a virtuoso plays about a crowd, and Eisler's march is a song the crowd sings itself, and the difference is between a picture of a march and a march, and Teitelbaum also took the body as material, M1 and M2, but his body is one woman blinking on a chair, and Eisler's body is three thousand workers counting "links, zwei, drei" in Strasbourg in 1935, and the difference is between a signal and a crowd.

## M2. The open ending: the song that refuses to close

The Solidaritaetslied ends on the dominant, not the tonic, and the last bars leave the song open on the question "Wessen Welt ist die Welt?" (Whose world is the world?). The mechanism is exact and small: the first four syllables of "Solidaritaet" repeat on one pitch, D over a subdominant G minor chord, and the final syllable drops a semitone to C sharp over a dominant A major chord, and the coda ends on the dominant, and the melody uses the B-A-C-H motif on the first beats of bars 1, 2, 5 and 6, so the cipher and the open cadence are the same gesture, a signature that will not resolve. The song was first performed in 1932 by several working-men's choirs, and it spread through the Weimar Republic's final months, especially at sporting events, and in 1961 eight East German composers, several of them Eisler's students, wrote Orchestervariationen ueber Eislers Solidaritaetslied, and the final two bars were the interval signal of the East German broadcaster Stimme der DDR, which means the open ending became a station identification, a question asked every day between programmes. **Takeaway:** end on the dominant and the audience has to supply the answer, and the song keeps working after it stops. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also refused to close his pieces, M14, but his drift closes by dissolving, and Eisler's song closes by asking, and the difference is between a fade and a question.

## M3. Quotation as argument: plant a tune the audience already knows

The Internationale, the B-A-C-H cipher, Unsterbliche Opfer, the Komintern melody, the Marseillaise tradition: Eisler plants a tune the audience already knows inside a new piece, so the quotation does the political work and the new music only has to frame it. In the Tagebuch of 1926 he quotes the Internationale against Schoenberg's Op. 9 Chamber Symphony, which is the quotation as a punch thrown at his own teacher. In the Deutsche Sinfonie the Prelude climaxes with the Internationale on trumpets against Unsterbliche Opfer on trombones at the same moment, two anthems fighting in the same bar. In Hangmen Also Die! of 1943 he smuggled the Komintern melody over the final credits as "No Surrender", which is the quotation as contraband, a banned tune inside a commercial Hollywood film. The B-A-C-H cipher is the same mechanism turned into a signature: a Prelude and Fugue on B-A-C-H, Op. 46, for string trio in 1934, the cipher as a compositional device in its own right, and the same motif embedded in the Solidaritaetslied, so the song carries Bach's name in its first bars. **Takeaway:** quote a tune everybody knows and the audience finishes your argument for you. Against the chain: Rzewski also quoted, M3, and his quotation is a variation, a tune taken apart and rebuilt, and Eisler's quotation is a flag planted, a tune left intact so it can be recognised in one bar, and the difference is between analysis and allegiance.

## M4. Found text as libretto: the document is the poem

Zeitungsausschnitte (Newspaper Clippings), Op. 11, of 1925-27, sets newspaper text as song, parodying a paper's layout with headline-like song titles, and it is a forerunner of the "news items" genre, and it premiered in 1927 at Baden-Baden and Berlin alongside the Tagebuch, and neither piece uses serial technique, which is the break made audible, the new method set aside so the document can speak. Thirty years later the same mechanism returns in Bilder aus der Kriegsfibel (Pictures from the War Primer) of 1957, settings of Brecht's Kriegsfibel, the photo-epigrams that pair press photographs of war with four-line verses, and the introduction was later recycled as the Epilogue of the Deutsche Sinfonie, so the caption became the symphony's last word. The mechanism is inventory, not invention: take a text that already exists in the world, a clipping, a caption, a headline, and set it as it stands, and the layout of the newspaper becomes the form of the song cycle. **Takeaway:** set the document, not the poem, and the news sings itself. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also took found material, M9, but his found material is other people's sounds, sampled and reintroduced, and Eisler's found material is other people's words, printed and sung, and the difference is between a memory and a headline.

## M5. The Lehrstueck machine: the chorus is the judge

In Die Massnahme (The Measures Taken, or The Decision), Op. 20, of 1930, the Control Chorus is both audience and judge, and the four agitators re-enact their deed and ask for a verdict, and the music, choral pieces, speaking choir, recitatives and songs, is the mechanism that delivers the verdict. The facts are exact: text by Brecht, direction by Slatan Dudow, tenor soloist, three speakers or actors, mixed choir (SATB) and small orchestra, eight sections, six major songs, premiere Wednesday 10 December 1930 at the Grosses Schauspielhaus, Berlin, conducted by Karl Rankl, with Ernst Busch among the four agitators and Helene Weigel as the Young Comrade, and the Arbeiterchor of Gross-Berlin as the Control Chorus, and the plot is four agitators reporting to the Control Chorus that they shot a young comrade who endangered the mission, and the comrade consents. The piece was rejected by the Neue Musik Festival that summer after Brecht refused to submit the text for inspection, and Brecht later banned performance, and the first postwar performance was London, election night 1987, and the HUAC used a hostile translation, "The Rule (or Doctrine)", made 18 September 1947, to interrogate Brecht and Eisler. The theatre is a school, and the spectators are the pupils, and the chorus sings the sentence. **Takeaway:** make the chorus the judge and the audience has to decide, not just watch. Against the chain: Curran also made the audience part of the piece, M6 in his study, but his audience brings a sound and adds it to the pool, and Eisler's audience brings a verdict and the verdict is a death, and the difference is between participation and judgement.

## M6. Counterpoint against the image: the music must argue with the picture

Eisler's film-music doctrine, written with Adorno in Composing for the Films (1947) and practised in eight Hollywood scores, is that music must not illustrate or double the picture's emotion, it must stand against it and comment on it, so the audience has to think. In the Long Voyage Home re-scoring for the Rockefeller Film Music Project, a sailor's death gets music of hysteria, fear and struggle instead of a sombre lament, and the gap between what the eye sees and what the ear hears is the meaning. The book attacks Hollywood's leitmotif system, its factory division of labour, five or six composers per film, each a genre specialist, the "music office workers", its sentimentality, and its use of music to illustrate rather than to comment, and it proposes counterpoint, music against image, small chamber ensembles, new instruments like the electric violin and the theremin, twelve-tone material, and direct sound-on-film synthesis, and it rebuts Eisenstein's shot-by-shot graph for Prokofiev's Alexander Nevsky as static and dogmatic. In Hollywood practice the same doctrine is a smuggling route: Hangmen Also Die! of 1943, story by Brecht with Fritz Lang, after the assassination of Reinhard Heydrich, carries the Komintern melody over the final credits as "No Surrender", and None But the Lonely Heart of 1944, Clifford Odets, Cary Grant, is scored the same way, and both were Oscar-nominated, at the 16th Academy Awards in 1944 and the 17th in 1945, and six further Hollywood scores followed, including The Spanish Main (1945), A Scandal in Paris and Deadline at Dawn (1946), and The Woman on the Beach (Jean Renoir, 1947). **Takeaway:** set the music against the picture and the audience has to think, and set it with the picture and the audience only has to feel. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also scored films, M14, but his soundtrack is a slow drift that leaves the picture alone, and Eisler's score is an argument that interrupts the picture, and the difference is between accompaniment and opposition.

## M7. Small forms for fast cutting: the row as a riff

Eisler argued that tonal development needs long spans, while a row piece does not depend on periodisation, so short forms fit montage, and the variation form, the twelve-tone row as a short riff, the etude and the miniature are his cutting tools. Vierzehn Arten den Regen zu beschreiben (Fourteen Ways of Describing the Rain), Op. 70, of 1941, is the proof: for flute, clarinet, violin, viola, cello and piano, the Pierrot lunaire instrumentation, written for Joris Ivens's silent film Regen of 1929 as part of the Rockefeller Film Music Project, later dedicated to Schoenberg for his 70th birthday in 1944, a variation form whose periodic, episodic shape coincides with the film's rain-shower structure, opening and closing with a cadenza-like monogram of Schoenberg's initials, and Eisler called it an "anatomy of melancholy" and "fourteen ways in which one can be acceptably sorrowful", and Orel calls it the most strictly organised piece in the Eisler catalogue. The music changes as fast as the cut, because the form is built from episodes, not from a span. **Takeaway:** cut the music into short forms and it can keep up with the picture, and build it in long spans and it will always lag one cut behind. Against the chain: Rzewski also built from short units, M4, but his units are variations on a theme that return and accumulate, and Eisler's units are episodes that pass and are gone, and the difference is between a theme and a shower.

## M8. Tonal serialism: a strict system that sounds like a folk song

Eisler's rows behave like tunes: they allude to traditional syntax, yield short riffs, and survive being sung by workers or hidden under a documentary voice-over, and that is the mechanism that lets him keep serial technique while publicly renouncing it. The early proof is the Piano Sonata No. 1, Op. 1, of 1923, serial writing whose harmonies in their progressions often allude to traditional syntax, per Orel, with jazz and vernacular rhythms inside the discipline, and Sequenza21 judges the 1920s piano music among the best serial piano writing not by Schoenberg, Berg or Webern, while noting its rhythmic rigidity, endless 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 with steady eighth notes. The late proof is the documentary scores of the New York period, The 400 Million (Joris Ivens, China, premiered 7 March 1939) and The Living Soil of 1941, which use twelve-tone technique exclusively, and audiences who did not know the method received them well, and Eisler's own line, in Modern Music in 1941, is that "apparently advanced musical material, which average concert-goers may find indigestible and nonrelevant, when applied to films loses something of this forbidding quality". The Silone chamber cantatas of 1937 are the exile proof: serial writing described by Orel as "especially rigorous" in the contemporary Two Sonnets on Brecht texts, yet speaking "an utterly clear musical message", per Schebera. **Takeaway:** write the row so it sounds like a tune and nobody will ask whether it is a system. Against the chain: Schoenberg's row is a law and Webern's row is a crystal, and Eisler's row is a tune that happens to be twelve notes long, and the difference is between a system you obey and a system you smuggle.

## M9. The singable march: accessibility as a compositional constraint

The Einheitsfrontlied (United Front Song) of December 1934 is deliberately simple and march-like so workers without musical training could sing it: text by Brecht, written at Erwin Piscator's request, music by Eisler, refrain "Drum links, zwei, drei!" (So left, two, three!) with the call "Reih dich ein in die Arbeitereinheitsfront", first performed by a 3,000-strong workers' choir at the First International Workers' Music Olympiad, Strasbourg, 1935, first record 1937, Busch singing in four languages in Spain, published in Brecht's Svendborger Gedichte of 1939, and Eisler wrote a symphonic version in 1948, and Shmerke Kaczerginski's 1943 Partizaner-Marsh reworks its text. The mechanism is accessibility as a compositional constraint: range, rhythm and repetition are all chosen for a crowd, not a soloist, and the counting refrain is the whole trick, because a crowd that can count together can march together, and a crowd that can march together can sing together, and the song is learned in the first hearing because there is almost nothing to learn. Der heimliche Aufmarsch (The Secret March) is the same mechanism applied to a borrowed tune: poem by Erich Weinert 1929, first music by Wladimir Vogel 1930, Busch sang it in the 1931 film Hell on Earth, and Eisler's 1938 remake became the rally version, and Cardew's Thaelmann Variations of 1971-72 are variations on it. **Takeaway:** write for the crowd's throat, not the soloist's, and the song will travel without you. Against the chain: Weill also wrote songs a crowd could sing, but his crowd is a theatre audience that paid for a seat, and Eisler's crowd is a choir of three thousand that paid nothing, and the difference is between a hit and a march.

## M10. The diary song: forty-six small dated entries that add up to an exile

The Hollywooder Liederbuch (Hollywood Songbook) of 1938-43, completed December 1943, is a musical diary of exile: 46 or 47 songs for voice and piano, sources disagree on the count, see C12, 28 or 30 on Brecht texts, the rest on Hoelderlin, Moerike, Goethe, Eichendorff, Pascal, Anakreon and Eisler's own words, including the Hollywood Elegies of 1942 on Brecht, and Brecht's Steffinische Sammlung poems of 1937-41, named for Margarete Steffin, who died in Moscow in June 1941, supply much of the material: exile, hotel rooms, lost books, escape. These are the first art songs Eisler had written since 1927, and they are concentrated, objective and anti-sentimental, and Sequenza21 calls Eisler one of the last composers to contribute meaningfully to the Romantic art song. The mechanism is inventory: small, dated, objective, anti-sentimental units that add up to a life in exile, and no single song carries the weight, because the weight is in the accumulation, and the diary works the way a diary works, one entry at a time, each too small to be a statement and all of them together too large to be ignored. **Takeaway:** write the exile one small dated song at a time, and the accumulation is the piece. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also kept a diary, M10 in his study, but his diary is a threshold, a volume matched to a room, and Eisler's diary is an inventory, a list of what was lost, and the difference is between a measurement and a mourning.

## M11. The anthem as state instrument: the march, aimed at a government

Auferstanden aus Ruinen of 1949 takes the Kampflied's singability and aims it at a state: lyrics by Johannes R. Becher, music by Eisler, simple, singable, major-key, built for massed unison singing, a rising line, Becher's text about rising from ruins and a united fatherland, adopted Monday 14 November 1949, and two composers submitted settings, Ottmar Gerster and Eisler, and Eisler's was selected. The mechanism is the same as M9 with the address changed: the crowd is still the instrument and the tune is still simple enough to be learned in one hearing, but the crowd is now a nation and the song is now a state, and the same tune that once mobilised a crowd now legitimises a government. The irony is the study's sharpest detail: the man who wrote the most famous workers' marches ended by writing a state anthem whose lyrics the state itself later silenced, dropped from official use in 1973, instrumental only, because of their reunification language, and the anthem lapsed with reunification on 3 October 1990. **Takeaway:** a tune simple enough for a crowd is simple enough for a state, and the state will take it, and then it will take the words too. Against the chain: the Solidarity Song ends on an open dominant and asks a question, M2, and the anthem ends on a closed tonic and gives an answer, and the difference is between a song and a state.

## M12. Restraint as response: refuse sentimentality where the subject forbids it

The score for Nuit et brouillard (Night and Fog) of 1955, Alain Resnais's documentary on the camps, with Herr Puntila and His Servant Matti the same year, is haunting melodies over images of Auschwitz, avoiding sentimentality, and Portside calls it a potent classic. The mechanism is refusal: where the subject forbids the march and forbids the lament, the only honest response is restraint, and Eisler's late work replaces the march with the quiet inventory and the open question. The Ernste Gesaenge (Serious Songs) of spring 1961 to August 1962, seven songs after Hoelderlin, Berthold Viertel, Giacomo Leopardi, Helmut Richter and Stephan Hermlin, Eisler's last work, written while working through depression and taking the 20th Congress of the CPSU of 1956, the denunciation of Stalin, as a sign of hope for a future "life without fear", are the same mechanism turned inward: the same serial tools, the same short forms, but the crowd is gone and the singer is alone, and the question is no longer whose world it is, but whether a life without fear is still possible. **Takeaway:** when the subject forbids the march, do not march louder, go quiet, and let the quiet do the work. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also went quiet at the end, M14, but his quiet is a drift, a sound left alone in a room, and Eisler's quiet is a reckoning, a man counting what is left, and the difference is between a rest and a verdict.

## M13. The quotation-collage cantata: a filing cabinet that sings

The Deutsche Sinfonie, Op. 50, of 1935-1958, principally 1935-1947, is eleven movements for soloists, chorus and orchestra, texts by Brecht, mainly Songs, Poems and Choruses of 1934, and Ignazio Silone, Bread and Wine, adapted by Eisler, conceived in July 1935 in a letter from Moscow as a "Concentration Camp Symphony", and the first two movements, subtitled An Anti-Hitler Symphony, won the ISCM prize and a promised performance at the 1937 Paris World Exhibition, and the Nazis got the French government to cancel it, the chairman Jacques Ibert offering to replace the chorus with saxophones so Brecht's texts would not be heard, and Eisler refused. The movements are a filing cabinet: Praeludium; An die Kaempfer in den Konzentrationslagern, a passacaglia on a B-A-C-H ground; Etude for orchestra, a recycled 1930 Suite No. 1 finale; Erinnerung (Potsdam); In Sonnenburg, the singer instructed to give a fascist salute on the word "leer"; Intermezzo, the same row as movement 2, written against the Hitler-Stalin pact; Begraebnis des Hetzers im Zinksarg; Bauernskantate, Silone texts, his name kept out of the published score because of his break with Moscow; Arbeiterkantate, Lied vom Klassenfeind, the longest movement, about 15 minutes; Allegro; Epilog, added 1957, an arrangement of the introduction to Bilder aus der Kriegsfibel. The Prelude climaxes with the Internationale on trumpets against Unsterbliche Opfer on trombones, and all but the last movement are twelve-tone, in Eisler's tonal serialism, and the full premiere was Friday 24 April 1959 at the Berlin State Opera, East Berlin, and Brecht had died in 1956, and Sequenza21 notes it was then suppressed by communist censors for Schoenbergian atonality, despite Eisler's own 1948 Prague endorsement of the Zhdanov doctrine. The unity is the row and the subject, Germany under fascism, not the form. **Takeaway:** assemble eleven incompatible materials over twenty-three years and hold them together with one row and one subject, and the form is whatever survives. Against the chain: Rzewski also built a large form from small quoted units, M13 in his study, but his units are variations that return to a theme, and Eisler's units are documents that never return, and the difference is between a variation and an archive.

## M14. The refused work: an empty stage where an opera should be

Johann Faustus, libretto published autumn 1952, music never written, is the work whose absence is its content. Eisler's own libretto makes Faust an indecisive man who betrays the working class by not joining the German Peasants' War, and it was attacked in Neues Deutschland on 14 May 1953 as "a slap in the face of German national feeling" and a formalistic deformation of Goethe, Ulbricht's line, and it was debated in three Mittwochsgesellschaft meetings of the Berlin Academy of Arts, beginning Wednesday 13 May 1953, the last one Wednesday 10 June 1953, and a week later the East German uprising of Wednesday 17 June 1953 pushed the debate off the agenda, and Eisler fell into depression and never wrote the music. The mechanism is refusal from both sides at once: the party refused the libretto, and the composer refused to write the music for a libretto the party had already condemned, and the work exists as a published text, three meeting transcripts and a gap, and the gap is the only honest record of what happened, because any music written after that debate would have been a lie about it. **Takeaway:** sometimes the work is the empty stage, and the refusal is the piece. Against the chain: Brecht also banned his own Massnahme from performance, M5, but his ban was a choice he could lift, and Eisler's Faustus was a work the state made impossible, and the difference is between a ban and a defeat.

## Evolution

Five phases, and the through-line is constant: the same man who wrote a serial piano sonata in 1923 is still writing twelve-tone music in 1962, and what changes is who the music is for, and how loudly it is allowed to say so. Phase 1, Vienna schoolboy to Schoenberg pupil, 1918 to 1925, the driver is mastery of the new method: self-taught, then Weigl, then Schoenberg from 1919 to 1923, serial technique learned as craft but used with levity, jazz rhythm and traditional harmonic allusion, the Piano Sonatas Op. 1 and Op. 6, the Divertimento Op. 4. Phase 2, Berlin agitprop, 1925 to 1933, the driver flips, and technique is now judged by whether a hall of workers can sing it: the break with Schoenberg in March 1926, the move to the Kampflied, the workers' choirs, Das rote Sprachrohr, the meeting with Brecht, the Lehrstuecke, Kuhle Wampe, serialism publicly renounced and privately kept, the Tagebuch and Zeitungsausschnitte as the break made audible, the Kleine Sinfonie of 1932 as proof the serial hand survived inside the political period. Phase 3, exile, 1933 to 1938, the driver is survival, and the same material must work as a concert piece, a film score and a marching song: wandering through Prague, Paris, London, Moscow, Denmark, Spain, Mexico and New York, the serial hand returns in the B-A-C-H Prelude and Fugue of 1934, the Silone cantatas of 1937, the Deutsche Sinfonie begun in 1935, the Einheitsfrontlied written in December 1934 and sung in Spain in 1937. Phase 4, America, 1938 to 1948, the driver is infiltration, and advanced material is hidden inside popular forms: New York teaching and documentary scores, The 400 Million and The Forgotten Village, then Hollywood from April 1942, eight scores, two Oscar nominations, the Film Music Project and Composing for the Films turning practice into theory, the Hollywood Songbook turning exile into a diary, the film scores smuggling serial technique and the Komintern melody into commercial cinema. Phase 5, East Germany, 1948 to 1962, the driver is accommodation and its cost: the anthem, the Volkslieder, the theatre music, seventeen productions from 1948 to 1961, the Faustus defeat, depression after Brecht's death in 1956, the late reckoning of the Ernste Gesaenge, the simplified public style against the private serial continuity, ending in a song cycle about living without fear.

## Aesthetic logic

The song is the weapon and the weapon is the song, and that is the logic that runs through all fourteen mechanisms, and the piece is not an object but a tool you pick up, and the march is the tool's handle, and the quotation is the tool's edge, and the chorus is the tool's court, and the newspaper is the tool's material, and the row is the tool's hidden spring, and the film score is the tool's disguise, and the diary is the tool's record, and the anthem is the tool's capture, and the restraint is the tool's respect, and the collage is the tool's archive, and the refusal is the tool's limit. The early Schoenberg years are the same logic in its quietest form, a craftsman learning the sharpest tool in the shop, and the Berlin years are the logic at its loudest, a tool handed to a crowd, and the exile years are the logic in disguise, the same tool hidden inside a concert piece and a film score, and the Hollywood years are the logic as theory, a book about why the tool must argue with the picture, and the East German years are the logic captured, the tool taken by the state that commissioned it, and the music is not about him, it is about what happens between the tune and the crowd, and between the quotation and the room, and between the row and the song, and when the situation changed, the music changed with it, all the way down to the notes, but the row never left the pocket, and the crowd never left the song.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. The Solidarity Song is a quotation inside Rzewski's The People United, not its theme. The theme is Sergio Ortega's Chilean song "El pueblo unido jamas sera vencido". Eisler enters at variation 26, "In a militant manner", with Bandiera Rossa at variation 13. Sources: The Wire, Quartz Music, the Rzewski study #404 log entry.

C2. "Nono was shaped by Eisler's political music" is UNVERIFIED and should not be stated. The Nono biography read end to end, 595 lines, never mentions Eisler. The defensible claim is shared Schoenberg lineage plus a shared programme, political serialism. See D7.

C3. "Eisler joined the KPD in 1926" (Portside; IMDb) is WRONG. Per the FBI files as reported by Wierzbicki and Orel, he APPLIED for membership in 1926 and was DENIED, mostly because he failed to pay his dues on time. He told HUAC he had "put in an application" and let it lapse. "Applied, not admitted" is the correct form.

C4. "He was deported early in 1948" (Wikipedia) misleads. He left voluntarily on Friday 26 March 1948, flying from LaGuardia to Prague, under a deportation order, after reading a departure statement. He was not physically removed. His brother Gerhart, by contrast, fled by stowing away on a ship while out on bail.

C5. "The HUAC under Senator Joseph McCarthy reconvened in 1946" (Portside) is WRONG twice. McCarthy was a Senator and never sat on the House Committee on Un-American Activities; the chairman was J. Parnell Thomas. The Eisler hearings were 24 to 26 September 1947, not 1946. Brecht testified Thursday 30 October 1947.

C6. "Two interrogations by HUAC" (Wikipedia) understates the record. The published hearings run Wednesday 24, Thursday 25 and Friday 26 September 1947, three days. "Two interrogations" is a simplification; cite the three dates.

C7. "In 1938, the Eislers emigrated to the United States with a permanent visa" (Wikipedia) is WRONG. Eisler arrived in January 1938 on a temporary visa, spent April to September 1939 in Mexico after visa trouble, including a 1940 arrest-and-deportation order, and only then obtained an unlimited visa at the Mexican-Californian border through what HUAC later called a "sleeping consular official" (Portside; Bick; IRCAM).

C8. "In early 1937, he enlisted in the International Brigades" (IRCAM) OVERSTATES. Portside: he was invited to Spain, spent a few weeks with the XI Brigade in Murcia, wrote four songs on the day he arrived, and left. He was a visiting composer, not an enlisted combatant.

C9. Draft year conflict, unresolved. Portside and IRCAM put his drafting and front service in 1916 ("sent to the front in 1916", "drafted shortly after graduating in 1916"); Wikipedia says "in 1917, one year after Eisler graduated high school". Do not state a single year without flagging the conflict.

C10. Die Massnahme premiere date conflict, unresolved. Wikipedia infobox: Wednesday 10 December 1930. The German-literature site (sites.google.com/site/germanliterature): "first performed on 14-16 December 1930". The 10 December date is the one the study uses, with the alternative noted; the 14th was a Sunday, which supports treating 10 December as the premiere and 14 to 16 December as further performances.

C11. Deutsche Sinfonie completion conflict. Orel: "started in 1935 and completed in 1939". Wikipedia, the American Symphony Orchestra and Sequenza21 agree: principally composed 1935-1947, epilogue added 1957/58, full premiere Friday 24 April 1959. Orel's "completed in 1939" is wrong as a statement about the finished work; at most it describes the main body. This study uses 1935-1958.

C12. Hollywood Songbook count conflict, unresolved. MusicWeb's CD review: 46 songs. The Squarespace Steffin project: 47 songs, 28 on Brecht. MusicWeb: 30 of 47 tracks on Brecht. Dates agree on completion December 1943; Wikipedia's range 1938-43 stands. Do not state a single song count without noting the 46/47 split.

C13. "Lied der Werktatigen (Song of the Working People), 1929, with Stephan Hermlin" (Wikipedia compositions list) is IMPLAUSIBLE as printed. Hermlin was born in 1915; he would have been 14 in 1929. No source found this leg confirms Hermlin as the 1929 text author, and a targeted search returned nothing on the attribution. Do not repeat it; treat the song's text author as unverified.

C14. "Ballad of Paragraph 218 was the world's first song protesting laws against abortion" (Wikipedia) is a single-source superlative with no supporting citation in the article. Flag as unverified; do not repeat "world's first" without a second source.

C15. "Eisler was the first of Schoenberg's disciples to compose in the twelve-tone or serial technique" (Wikipedia) is single-source. It is plausible, the Op. 1 Piano Sonata of 1923 predates Webern's first twelve-tone works of 1924, but no second source was found this leg. State it as "among the first" or attribute it to Wikipedia.

C16. Solidaritaetslied dating is a range, not a year. Wikipedia: written 1929-1931. The Wire: written 1931 for Kuhle Wampe. Quartz: written 1932. Goethe-Institut: 1931/32. Use "1929-1931, film version 1931/32".

C17. "Ruth Fischer... from 2010, confirmed to have been a CIA spy" (Classic Cat, citing declassified information) is single-source and contested in tone. Portside's version is safer: she became a key figure in the anticommunist activities of the early CIA forerunner "The Pond". Do not state CIA employment as settled fact.

C18. "Schweik in the Second World War (1957)" (Wikipedia prose) conflates composition and production. The songs were written in 1943; the play was produced in 1957. Use "music 1943, production 1957".

C19. "Eisler lived [in the United States] from 1937 until 1948" (Orel) is WRONG on the start year. He was in Spain, Denmark and Prague through 1937 and arrived in the US in January 1938.

C20. Die Mutter dating: Wikipedia prose says "The Mother (1932)" while its own compositions list gives incidental music 1931 and a cantata version 1935. Use 1931 for the incidental music.
