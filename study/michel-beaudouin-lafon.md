**Doorway:** The 468th doorway. Takes up the open question from the Bill Gaver study (#847), quoted verbatim: "when ambiguity must resolve into a single usable action -- when the playful reading meets a tool that has to be learned, used, and trusted -- what survives of the openness, and who does the resolving, the hand or the tool?" Beaudouin-Lafon answers it, and his answer is a relocation of the resolving. Gaver's artefacts never ask, never correct, and never resolve -- the decision about what a gesture meant is left open. Beaudouin-Lafon's instruments do resolve, but the hand never does the resolving directly: the hand acts on the instrument, the instrument transduces the action into a command on the object, and the object answers back through the instrument. The resolving is done by the mediator, in the open, where it can be seen -- the scrollbar highlights as it is touched, the search string highlights every match at once, the toolglass shows the tool and the object in the same gesture. What survives of the openness is the gap the instrument itself keeps: its degree of indirection (the spatial and temporal offset between hand and object), its degree of integration (how much of the hand's freedom the instrument actually uses), and its degree of compatibility (how much the object's response resembles the hand's motion). And the openness survives a second time in co-adaptation: the hand learns the tool, and the tool learns the hand, so the single usable action is jointly authored and never settles into one side's property. The open question carried forward is the one the instrument leaves standing: when the instrument between hand and object thins to nothing -- when the hand touches the drawing itself, as in Sutherland's Sketchpad -- what is lost when there is no tool left to learn, and who carries the meaning then, the gesture or the object? [C39001]

## M1 -- The instrument is the mediator: a two-way transducer

**Subject:** Interaction is never hand-to-object. It is hand-to-instrument-to-object, and the instrument is a two-way transducer between the two.

**Visual principle:** Two visible layers with a visible gap between them. The hand's action and the object's response are never in the same place at the same time; the instrument sits between them and shows the translation happening.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from Instrumental Interaction (CHI 2000). Interfaces are described as domain objects (the objects of interest, e.g. text, a sphere, a style, a material) plus interaction instruments (mediators between user and domain objects). The user acts on the instrument; the instrument transforms the action into a command on the target object; the instrument gives a reaction (e.g. a scrollbar arrow highlights) and the object gives feedback (e.g. the thumb moves, the view updates). Instruments have a physical part (input device) and a logical part (on-screen representation). Activation is spatial (point inside the scrollbar) or temporal (click a palette tool; it stays active until replaced -- a mode). The executable rule in this study's formalisation: state is instrument I with logical part L, physical part P, target object O with attributes A. On each input event: (1) P acts on L, producing reaction R (immediate local feedback on L itself); (2) L transduces the action into command C sent to O; (3) O updates A and returns response S; (4) L transforms S into feedback F shown on O and on L. Variables: spatial offset d_s (distance between L and O) and temporal offset d_t (time between action on L and visible response on O). Constraint: R must be immediate even when d_t is long; F must be distinguishable from R -- the instrument's own reaction is not the object's change.

**Artistic opportunity:** Make the transducer visible as a material thing -- a lens, a lever, a membrane -- so the viewer watches meaning change as it crosses the instrument, rather than watching an object change directly.

**Takeaway:** The hand never touches the object; it touches the thing that touches the object. [C38990]

## M2 -- Three degrees as a live, navigable space

**Subject:** The three properties of instruments from CHI 2000: degree of indirection, degree of integration, degree of compatibility.

**Visual principle:** One gesture, three visible traces. The hand's path, the instrument's path, and the object's path are drawn as three separate lines, so their divergence is the picture.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from CHI 2000. (1) Degree of indirection: spatial offset between instrument and object plus temporal offset between action and response; dialog boxes score high on both, handles score low. (2) Degree of integration: ratio of degrees of freedom in the logical part to degrees of freedom captured by the device -- a scrollbar is 1/2, a 2D panner is 1, controlling 3 rotations with a 2D mouse is 3/2. (3) Degree of compatibility: similarity between the physical action and the object response -- dragging an object is high, typing a number for font size is low. The paper's Table 1 compares WIMP techniques on exactly these three axes. This study's formalisation: for a single continuous gesture g(t), indirection is computed as spatial offset |p_hand - p_object| and lag in ms; integration is DOF_instrument / DOF_input; compatibility is the cosine similarity between the hand's motion vector and the object's response vector (1 = object follows the hand, -1 = object moves opposite, as with a scrollbar thumb moving down while content moves up). Constraint: the three values are computed from the same gesture, never set independently by a menu.

**Artistic opportunity:** The evaluation metric becomes the artwork. The viewer learns the instrument by seeing how far the object strays from the hand, and can steer the gesture to close or widen that gap.

**Takeaway:** The gap between hand and object can be measured -- and drawn. [C38991]

## M3 -- Reification: a command becomes an object

**Subject:** Reification (CHI 2000; AVI 2000 with Mackay). A concept is turned into a first-class object that can be represented on screen and operated upon.

**Visual principle:** Verbs made solid. A command that was invisible (align, gather, split, replace) appears as a small physical token with weight, edges, and a place it can be put down.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from CHI 2000 and AVI 2000. Two kinds of reification: reifying a set of attributes into a domain object (a text style reifies a collection of formatting attributes; a material reifies rendering properties), and reifying a command into an instrument (a scrollbar reifies the scroll command). Because instruments are themselves potential objects of interest, they can be operated upon. This study's formalisation: state is a command K with parameters (target type, effect function f, parameter values v). Reify(K) creates object O_K with position, size, and affordance: O_K can be dragged, dropped onto a domain object D, stored in a tray, or duplicated. Applying: drop O_K on D executes f(D, v) and leaves O_K intact (reusable) or consumes it (single-use), decided per token. A reified attribute-set works the same way: a style token S carries attribute values; dropping S on D sets D's attributes to S's values. Constraint: a reified command must remain operable after reification -- it can be picked up again, moved, and applied to a different object; if it can only be used once from a fixed menu, it is not reified, it is a button.

**Artistic opportunity:** The interface's hidden verbs become the picture's furniture. The composition is a workshop table of verbs, and the artwork is what happens when verbs are carried to objects.

**Takeaway:** A verb you can pick up is no longer a command; it is a thing. [C38992]

## M4 -- Meta-instruments: instruments that operate on instruments

**Subject:** Meta-instruments (CHI 2000). Because instruments are themselves potential objects of interest, there are instruments that operate on instruments: menus and tool palettes select and activate instruments; a toolbox organizes them; a tailoring tool reshapes one instrument into another.

**Visual principle:** A visible chain of attention. Each level of the chain is drawn at a different scale or depth, and the focus of the piece physically moves down the chain as tools break, wear, or are retuned.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from CHI 2000. The paper's chain: a pencil writes text; when the lead breaks, attention shifts to the pencil sharpener, which operates on the pencil; if the sharpener breaks, attention shifts to a screwdriver, which operates on the sharpener. This study's formalisation: state is a chain I_0 (acts on domain object D), I_1 (acts on I_0), I_2 (acts on I_1), each with a condition c_n (wear, breakage, misfit) and an operation o_n that repairs or retunes I_{n-1}. Rule: attention A is an index into the chain, starting at 0. When c_n becomes true for the instrument at A, A increments: the piece's focus shifts to the meta-instrument that can fix it. Acting on I_n applies o_n, resets c_n, and returns A to n-1. Constraint: at most one level is active at a time; the inactive levels remain visible but dimmed, so the whole chain is always legible. The chain must be finite and closable: the deepest meta-instrument is fixed (it cannot break), or the chain terminates in a reset.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about maintenance rather than making. The drama is not the mark on the page but the moment the tool itself becomes the object, and the viewer must fix the fixer.

**Takeaway:** When the tool breaks, the tool becomes the work. [C38993]

## M5 -- Polymorphism: one instrument, many kinds of object

**Subject:** Polymorphism (AVI 2000). The same command can be applied to objects of different types.

**Visual principle:** One unchanging tool shape moving across visibly different kinds of matter -- a line, a field, a grid, a text -- and producing a different, type-appropriate effect on each, while the tool itself never changes its form.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from AVI 2000. The paper's example is the color selector: the same tool works in different contexts and on different object types. The 2016 slides repeat it: "the same tool can be used in different contexts" and "free the tools from the applications where they are trapped." This study's formalisation: state is instrument I with a single operation op and no type-specific branches in its own definition; domain objects D_1..D_n each with a type T_i and a handler h_i(op) that interprets op for that type. Rule: when I is applied to D_i, the effect is h_i(op), computed by the object, not by the instrument. Constraint: I's visual form and gesture are identical across types; only the effect differs. If the tool changes shape per type, the mechanism is not polymorphism, it is a mode switch.

**Artistic opportunity:** The tool is proved general by its indifference. The picture is a test bench: the same hand, the same tool, and the world answering differently each time because the world, not the tool, decides what the gesture means.

**Takeaway:** The tool does not change; the world answers differently. [C38994]

## M6 -- Reuse: input reuse and output reuse

**Subject:** Reuse (AVI 2000). An interaction, or a set of interactions, can be reused as a pattern.

**Visual principle:** The past as visible material. Previous gestures leave behind traces that are themselves graspable; the picture accumulates a sediment of earlier actions that can be picked up and played again.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from AVI 2000. Two kinds: input reuse (commands: redo, macros -- a past gesture replayed) and output reuse (objects: copy-paste -- a past result reused as material). "Reification creates new objects that can be acted upon by a small set of polymorphic commands, creating more opportunities for reuse." This study's formalisation: state is a history log H of gestures, each entry (gesture g, target D, effect e, timestamp t), and a tray of reusable entries. Input reuse: selecting an entry replays g on a new target D' (macro), or re-executes e (redo). Output reuse: selecting a result object O produced by an earlier action copies it into the current scene (paste) as a new live object. Rule: every action appends to H automatically; entries are never deleted by the system, only by explicit user action; replay is exact (same parameters) unless the user edits the entry, in which case the edited entry becomes a new entry and the original is preserved. Constraint: reuse must be visible -- the tray shows the entries as objects, not as a hidden menu; a replayed gesture must be distinguishable from a fresh gesture (e.g. drawn in a different weight or color) so the viewer can tell what is live and what is remembered.

**Artistic opportunity:** Time becomes a material. The piece is not a single moment but a growing archive of its own making, and the viewer's later gestures are conversations with their earlier ones.

**Takeaway:** The past is not gone; it is sitting in the tray, waiting to be played again. [C38995]

## M7 -- Activation: spatial vs temporal, and two-handed instruments

**Subject:** Instrument activation (CHI 2000) and the CPN2000 techniques. An instrument is activated when its physical part is associated with its logical part.

**Visual principle:** The cost of switching tools is drawn. Instruments are either places (you go to them) or modes (you summon them and they stay), and the piece makes the travel time and the mode-stickiness visible as distance and as residue.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from CHI 2000 and the AVI 2000 account of CPN2000. Spatial activation: pointing inside the instrument's area (scrollbar active while the pointer is within it). Temporal activation: a former action puts it in effect until another instrument is activated (palette button = a mode). Costs differ: spatial costs screen space and attention; temporal costs an explicit action and is slower and less direct. CPN2000 combines toolglasses (semi-transparent palettes moved by the non-dominant hand, clicked through with the dominant hand, specifying action and object in one gesture), marking menus (a flick in a direction selects a command, learned as a gesture until the menu need not appear), and floating palettes; the AVI 2002 experiment found no single technique superior -- palettes win in copy contexts, toolglasses and marking menus win in problem-solving contexts. This study's formalisation: state is a set of instruments I_1..I_k, each with activation kind (spatial or temporal) and an activation cost. Spatial: I is active iff pointer position p is inside region R_I; deactivates on exit. Temporal: I is active from the moment it is selected until another instrument is selected; it persists across pointer moves. Toolglass variant: a translucent instrument T held at position p_T; a click through T at point q applies the tool under q in T to the domain object at q -- action and object specified in one gesture. Constraint: at most one temporal instrument is active at a time; spatial instruments deactivate on exit; the toolglass click-through must act on the object beneath, not on the glass itself.

**Artistic opportunity:** The interface's hidden tax -- the travel and the mode -- becomes the subject. The piece asks how much of the picture is the work and how much is the cost of choosing the tool.

**Takeaway:** Some tools are places; some tools are moods that linger. [C38996]

## M8 -- Substrates: the document is also the tool

**Subject:** Information substrates (CHItaly 2017; Webstrates, UIST 2015). Substrates are software artifacts that embody content, computation, and interaction, blurring the distinction between documents and applications.

**Visual principle:** Layers that change jobs. The same sheet is at one moment a picture, at another a tool, at another a rule-book; the layers are visibly stacked, and the viewer can see which layer is currently giving meaning to which.

**Computational mechanism:** Beaudouin-Lafon's own, from CHItaly 2017 and Webstrates (Klokmose et al., UIST 2015). A substrate can act as a document in one context and an application in another; one substrate can give meaning and structure to another (a bar-chart substrate visualizes a statistical-data substrate); instruments are themselves substrates that operate on other substrates. Webstrates adds three properties: malleable (users appropriate media idiosyncratically), shareable, distributable. The Othello example: the board is a surface, a move instrument queries governors for valid moves, and a governor computes consequences (flipping pieces); a drawing instrument can draw on the same board because the board is just a surface. This study's formalisation: state is substrates S_1..S_n in a stack, each with content, computation, and interaction, and a role (data, view, rule) that can change. An operation on S is validated by a governor G(S) that answers "is this move valid here?" and computes consequences before the change is committed; an invalid move is refused visibly. Layering: S_a placed over S_b gives structure to S_b. Malleability: any substrate can be edited as a document and extended as a tool at runtime. Constraint: roles are assigned by use, not by kind -- no substrate is permanently "just data" or "just a tool"; the piece must show at least one substrate changing role during its run.

**Artistic opportunity:** The picture is also the workbench. The viewer watches a sheet stop being a picture and start being a machine, and the rules of the picture are themselves on the table, editable.

**Takeaway:** The page is not under the tool; the page is the tool. [C38997]

## M9 -- Co-adaptation: the hand learns the tool and the tool learns the hand

**Subject:** Co-adaptive instruments (Mackay and Beaudouin-Lafon). Users adapt their behavior to a system through learning, and adapt the system to their own purposes through appropriation; computers adapt their behavior to people.

**Visual principle:** A tool with a memory of its user. The instrument visibly changes its own shape, sensitivity, or defaults in response to how it has been handled, and the hand visibly changes its own gesture in response -- two learning curves drawn in the same frame.

**Computational mechanism:** Mackay and Beaudouin-Lafon's, from the co-adaptive instruments work and the 2019 "Human-Computer Partnerships" lecture. The musical-instrument analogy: learning to play from novice to virtuoso, where the tool is worth learning because it is stable enough to build expertise on, unlike software that changes its interface at every upgrade. CoReach (CHI 2017) is the cooperative instance: gestures from multiple users combine to trigger actions for passing or showing content, reducing fatigue and supporting collaboration. This study's formalisation: state is instrument I with tunable parameters theta (sensitivity, aperture, default values) and a usage history U; user skill estimate s (0 novice to 1 virtuoso) updated from gesture smoothness, speed, and error rate. Two adaptation rules run together: (1) user adaptation: as s rises, the piece offers less guidance (labels fade, menus shrink to pure gesture, as with marking menus) and demands more precision; (2) instrument adaptation: theta drifts toward the user's observed preferences -- e.g. the tool's active zone migrates toward where the hand actually lands, its gain matches the hand's typical speed, its defaults become the user's most-used values. Constraint: adaptation is slow and visible -- theta changes by small increments per use, never jumps; the user can always see what the tool has learned (a ghost of the old defaults remains); the tool never adapts silently in a way that makes a learned gesture fail. State transitions: novice (guided, forgiving) -> practiced (guidance fades, tool tuned) -> virtuoso (bare gesture, tool fully fitted) -- with regression possible if the tool is retuned.

**Artistic opportunity:** The piece is a duet with a learning partner. The question is not what the tool does but who is shaping whom, and the picture keeps a record of that negotiation.

**Takeaway:** The tool learns the hand while the hand learns the tool. [C38998]

## The arc: from the toolkit, to the model, to the room, to the theory

Beaudouin-Lafon's career reads as one long move across four questions, and the move is the through-line. First, "how do we build it" (1982-1995): the engineering of interactive systems -- graphical interfaces, toolkits, state machines -- with CHARADE (1993) already gestural and post-WIMP in spirit, a free hand controlling objects at a distance. Second, "how do we describe it" (1996-2010): conceptual models take centre stage -- instrumental interaction (2000), reification, polymorphism and reuse (2000), "Designing interaction, not interfaces" (2004), pointing science (Semantic Pointing 2004, multiscale worlds 2004), Touchstone (2007) and SwingStates (2006/2008) making experiments and interaction state explicit, and the institutional peak of the LRI directorship and the CHI Academy (2006). Third, "how big can it get" (2011-2017): scale and sharing -- the WILD room and multi-surface interaction (2012), the Digiscope network of ten interactive rooms (2011), Webstrates (2015), the ERC Advanced Grant ONE (2016) on unified principles, BIGnav and CamRay (2017). Fourth, "how do we make the theory itself" (2019-2025): the instrument becomes a partner and a theory -- Enact (2019), Textlets (2020), Generative Theories of Interaction (2021), the CNRS Silver Medal and ACM Fellowship (2022), the Academie des Sciences (2024), Interaction Substrates (2025). What changed technically: from a glove and a gesture, to a model with three measurable degrees, to a wall and a web of shared pages, to a method for generating models. What did not change: interaction, not the interface, is the object of study; instruments mediate, substrates contain, and users appropriate. The scale moves from a single desktop, to a wall, to rooms, to a national network of rooms; the partner moves from the user alone, to the user plus the system, to the user plus remote collaborators. This arc paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-2, not Beaudouin-Lafon's own words. [C39004]

## The aesthetic logic

Beaudouin-Lafon's aesthetic is the tool made visible, and the visibility is the design. The scrollbar is not a chrome strip but a reified command with a measurable degree of indirection; the search string is not a dialog box but an instrument that answers the whole field at once; the toolglass is not a palette but a translucent sheet the hand holds over the work; the substrate is not a document but a place where the rules sit on the table next to the marks. Nothing in his work hides the mediation: the instrument is always on the surface, between the hand and the object, showing its own reaction apart from the object's feedback. The most Beaudouin-Lafon-like visual in the whole study is not any one system: it is the gap between what the hand did and what the object did back -- the 120 ms lag, the scrollbar thumb moving down while the content moves up, the tool that bends a line in one quadrant and inks a letter in another without changing its own shape. And the honest boundary is stated on the surface too: the model refuses to say which instruments are good, only how to compare them, so a piece built from him should carry that honesty with it -- the three degrees on display, the adaptation visible in both directions, the resolving done by the instrument in the open rather than by a hidden guess. A piece built from Beaudouin-Lafon should make the viewer feel the tool in their hand: its weight, its lag, its learning, and the moment it becomes the thing they are actually touching. This paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-3, not Beaudouin-Lafon's own words. [C39005]

## Rejections

Fifteen candidate directions were considered and not queued. A toolglass literal demo (translucent palette overlay with click-through) was rejected: a UI technique demo, not a piece; it illustrates the paper instead of staging a question. A marking-menu radial pie was rejected: the interaction is a menu, however gestural; the picture would be a control, not a system. A CoReach two-user cooperative piece was rejected: it requires two simultaneous users; gallery pieces are single-viewer and this could not be played or verified solo. A Webstrates live-collaboration piece was rejected: it requires real multi-user sync; not a single-canvas piece and not verifiable in this environment. A portrait/biography piece ("the French HCI pioneer") was rejected: a biography or a palette is not a computational mechanism. A scrollbar piece was rejected: a one-dimensional instrument with a single mapping; too thin to carry a piece or develop over time. A dialog-box satire piece was rejected: literal WIMP mockery; the joke is the whole piece and there is no system underneath it. A keyboard-shortcut piece was rejected: the interaction is invisible (key presses with no visible instrument); nothing to see, nothing to draw. A two-handed bimanual piece requiring two simultaneous pointers was rejected: untestable on a single-touch phone, which is the gallery's real target. A three-degrees numeric dashboard was rejected: it displays the numbers as text and gauges; the metric is shown rather than embodied in a visual system. A "degree of integration" slider toy (one slider, one value) was rejected: a single control with no domain object; no mediation, no development. A palette piece (CHI-2000-era grays and blues as a color study) was rejected: a palette is explicitly not a mechanism. A drag-to-reseed generic field with an instrument skin was rejected: the instrument would be decoration on a generic system; the mechanism must be the system. Any candidate whose primary interaction is click-to-reload was rejected: a forbidden pattern, and no prompt uses it. Any candidate using ?seed= as a clock or scene index was rejected: a forbidden repurposing, and no prompt varies by seed-as-time. [C39006]
