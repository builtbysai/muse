## The life

Adriaen Brouwer was born in Oudenaarde, Flanders, in 1605 or 1606 -- the year is unresolved in every serious source; EN Wikipedia gives "c. 1605/06", the Met "1605/6", the National Gallery London "1606? - 1638". Do not use artble's precise "02 Apr 1606"; it is unsourced. His father, also Adriaen, was a tapestry designer in Oudenaarde, then an important tapestry centre, and died in poverty when the younger Adriaen was 15 or 16 (EN Wikipedia, citing Unger 1884). Brouwer had already left the paternal home by then. He "worked in Antwerp in 1622" (EN Wikipedia); no source read says what he did there.

By March 1625 he is recorded in Amsterdam, lodging in the inn of the painter Barend (Barent) van Someren, another Flemish artist in the Dutch Republic; on 23 July 1626 he signs as a notary's witness to a statement by van Someren and Adriaen van Nieulandt about a sale of pictures. By about 1626 he is in Haarlem, active in the Chamber of Rhetoric 'De Wijngaertranken' (the vine tendrils), motto "In Love Above All Else" -- the UT Austin thesis (abstract only) calls 1626-27 his most productive years and argues his genre choices track his membership in that Rederijker milieu.

The Frans Hals pupillage is Houbraken's error, not a fact. EN Wikipedia: Houbraken's "most glaring mistakes" were placing Brouwer's birth in Haarlem and identifying Frans Hals as his master. Encyclopedia.com: "There is no reliable account of his training as an artist... In Haarlem he undoubtedly knew and was influenced by Frans Hals." Lempertz: "Research has however disputed this." But the Met's own Heilbrunn essay (2008) still calls him "Hals' Flemish pupil" -- an internal contradiction in museum literature, flagged here and not resolved. The safer documented influence is Dirck Hals (EN Wikipedia), and the Haarlem loose-painterly milieu generally. Liedtke calls Adriaen van Ostade Brouwer's "supposed co-pupil under Hals" -- "supposed" is doing work there.

In 1631 he returned to Antwerp and registered as a master in the Guild of St Luke in 1631-32, even before he had become a poorter (citizen) of Antwerp. He lived and worked in Antwerp until his death. Antwerp records mention him regularly, usually in connection with debts -- on 12 February 1635 he assigned three pictures to his landlord Du Pont for a debt of 297 florins (Hofstede de Groot); Lempertz notes he had to sell his modest effects in 1632. In 1633 he was jailed in the Antwerp Citadel. The reason is unsettled: possibly tax evasion, or political -- the authorities may have suspected him of being a spy for the Dutch Republic (EN Wikipedia, citing Van den Branden 1882). Lempertz says "probably for political reasons." The citadel bakery was run by Joos van Craesbeeck; the two are assumed to have met there, and Cornelis de Bie (Het Gulden Cabinet, 1661) says Craesbeeck became Brouwer's pupil and best friend -- "Soo d'oude songhen, soo pypen de jonghen" ("As the old ones sang, so the young ones chirp"). On 26 April 1634 Brouwer took lodgings in the house of the prominent engraver Paulus Pontius; the two became close friends and both joined the chamber of rhetoric Violieren that year. Fat Man / Luxuria (Mauritshuis) is suggested to be a portrait of Pontius as well as the deadly sin of lust. His only officially registered pupil was Jan-Baptist Dandoy, taken on in 1635.

He died in Antwerp in January 1638, aged about 31-33; burial recorded 1 February 1638. The cause is disputed: early biographers blamed his party lifestyle and alcohol; Houbraken attributes death to the plague -- his remains were first buried in a common grave, then a month after his death re-interred in the Carmelite Church of Antwerp after a solemn ceremony, at the initiative and expense and in the presence of his artist friends. The common-grave burial supports plague; the question is not settled. Houbraken's claim of a Paris visit shortly before death "cannot be verified" (Hofstede de Groot). Early biographers describe Brouwer and his artist friends spending much of their time partying in local taverns, and Hofstede de Groot (via de Bie) says he would draw the company with pen and ink and pay his score with the sketches; drawings still in existence show this. But the traditional picture of the artist as a dissolute and irresponsible buffoon is, per Encyclopedia.com, "largely an invention of early biographers" -- the study treats it with care, not as biography.

## M1. The face as the picture -- the tronie

The central discovery, and the one the sources state most plainly: a face mid-gesture is a complete picture, needing no setting, no props, no story. Brouwer "contributed to the development of the genre of tronies, i.e. head or facial studies, which investigate varieties of expression" (EN Wikipedia). Youth Making a Face (c. 1632-1635, NGA, 1994.46.1, 13.7 x 10.5 cm VERIFIED) is nothing but a young man making a funny face -- fingers pulling at his mouth, a satirical, mocking gesture, a knife stuck through his fur hat, unkempt hair, chin stubble -- on a panel small enough to slip into a pocket. The NGA: the gesture "humanises him, however uninviting he may appear"; the mocking gesture's tradition reaches back to depictions of Christ before Pilate (Luke 22:63). The Bitter Draught (1636-1638, Stadel, Inv. 1076, 47.4 x 35.5 cm REPORTED) is the same device at the other end of the scale: a young man has just swallowed a bitter liquid, most likely medicine, and his face is contorted in utter disgust -- mouth gaping open, brows deeply furrowed, eyes tightly screwed up. The whole head recoils from a single swallowed mouthful, and the picture needs nothing else. The sitter is sometimes said to be Brouwer himself, a claim the secondary literature calls questionable. A recent medical-history article uses the painting as its illustration of the early-modern patient's gestural reaction to bitter physic. Both pictures are painted with short, unmodulated brushstrokes that EN Wikipedia says increase the dramatic effect; the NGA (via Google Arts & Culture) calls Brouwer's handling "vigorous... with his characteristically short, unmodulated brushstrokes."

**Takeaway:** build one face doing one thing -- a mouth pulled wide, a face recoiling from a bitter mouthful -- and let the face be the whole picture, no room, no second figure, no story. The watchable behaviour is the expression arriving and holding: the grimace tightening, the eyes screwing shut, the mouth staying open, the face performing itself until the viewer looks away first.

## M2. The brawl as an emotion study -- rage in stages and degrees

The brawls are not events with a cause; the brawl IS the picture, and the faces are the plot. Peasants Brawling over Cards (c. 1630 / 1631-1633, Dresden, Gal.-Nr. 1059, 26.5 x 34.5 cm REPORTED) shows two drunkards punishing a fellow card player for cheating: the fight is already on the floor, cards scattered, while other drinkers watch from the back of the room. Brawl between Peasants Playing Dice (1635, Dresden, Gal.-Nr. 1058, 22 x 17 cm REPORTED) is the smallest brawl in the catalogue and the most directly confrontational: two dice, a piece of chalk and an upset jug of liquor lie on the table to the left; a peasant in a blue jacket shouts at the spectator while aiming a blow at the mouth of the man behind him, who has seized him by the hair and holds up a jug to brain him; a third man in grey tries to stop the raised arm. Peasant Brawl (1620s, Mauritshuis 919) has peasants fighting hand to hand in and around an inn, one man seizing another by the throat or hair while others pile in. EN Wikipedia, on the Munich brawls: they "depict how rage in its varying stages and degrees is reflected in the facial expressions." One scholarly reading treats anger as lack of self-control, from Seneca via Justus Lipsius's neostoicism, current in Antwerp's humanist circle of which Brouwer formed part; the NGA reading is Bruegel-derived moral warning -- fighting, drinking, sleeping warn of gluttony (gula), wrath (ira), sloth (desidia), comic images "both to delight the viewers and instruct them about proper behavior." Whether he intended a moral message is "still contested" (EN Wikipedia). The study takes no side; it takes the mechanism -- the same fight carried at different stages on different faces at once.

**Takeaway:** build a fight with no cause and no winner -- one man shouting, one grabbing hair, one raising a jug, one trying to stop the raised arm, each face carrying a different moment of the same rage. The watchable behaviour is the stages: the shouting face, the grabbing face, the restraining face, all in one small room, the fight never resolving because the faces are the fight.

## M3. The turned head and the smoke -- The Smokers

The Smokers (c. 1636, Met, 32.100.21, 46.4 x 36.8 cm VERIFIED, signed lower left "Brauwer") is the largest and most famous work in the catalogue and the natural climax of the Antwerp group. Five young men sit around a table in a tavern, smoking pipes and drinking beer; Brouwer himself is the figure in the centre foreground, turned around to face the viewer, eyes wide open, lifting a drinking mug and exhaling smoke. The others are his fellow Antwerp artists -- Jan Davidsz. de Heem is the figure in black and white at the far right, the one identification made with most certainty; Jan Lievens (far left), Joos van Craesbeeck (middle) and Jan Cossiers (blowing smoke from his nose) are suggested identifications, none certain. The mechanism is the turned head plus the exhaled smoke: Brouwer breaks the group portrait open by having one sitter notice the viewer, and the curling smoke is both the action and the signature effect -- the Met's text singles out "ephemeral effects, ranging from curling smoke to grimaces." The picture doubles as a friendship portrait, a representation of Taste, and a "dissolute artist" self-portrait in the inverted pictor doctus tradition. It innovated the friendship-portrait type: full-length figures, tavern setting, expressive faces, nonchalant dress -- closer to his tavern scenes than to contemporary portraiture. Note the title trap: "The Smokers" is three different pictures (the Met's; Apsley House's, WM.1522-1948, 1636; and the Budapest Museum of Fine Arts', Inv. 566, 1630s), plus a possible fourth at English Heritage in the unreliable secondary literature -- do not conflate them. The Met dates it ca. 1636; the Wikidata row says 1637.

**Takeaway:** build a group portrait that breaks open -- five men around a table, and the one in the centre foreground turns his head, opens his eyes wide, lifts his mug and exhales smoke straight at the viewer. The watchable behaviour is the smoke: the curl rising, thinning, drifting across the faces, the turned head holding the viewer's eye while the rest of the table goes on drinking.

## M4. The senses as a system -- Five Senses and Seven Deadly Sins

In Antwerp the subjects systematise into series: the face becomes a system, not a one-off. The Five Senses pictures turn an abstraction into a body -- Smell (1630s, Alte Pinakothek, Inv. 2095), Taste in its Munich form as Scene with Smokers (1630s, Alte Pinakothek, Inv. 626), Hearing as Tavern Scene (The Village Fiddler) (c. 1634-38, Hermitage, GE-643, 25 x 33.5 cm VERIFIED), Touch as The Village Surgeon (1635, Alte Pinakothek, Inv. 581). "Taste" is two different pictures -- EN Wikipedia ties The Smokers (W18) to Taste, and the Alte Pinakothek separately catalogues its Scene with Smokers as Allegory of Taste; both claims can stand, since Brouwer returned to the sense, but the study must not treat them as one work. The Seven Deadly Sins series survives in one example: Fat Man / Luxuria (c. 1634-1637, Mauritshuis 607, 22.9 x 16.1 cm VERIFIED) -- a slovenly man with his hand in his half-opened jerkin, possibly a portrait of Paulus Pontius, the only surviving example of the series (de Clippel). The Village Fiddler carries a corrected misreading worth keeping: until recently scholars read the picture as an argument about to turn violent -- a man peering through the inn window with spectacles, another about to hit him with a broom. In fact the figure in the left foreground holds not a broom but a violin, as a variant in the Musee Granet, Aix-en-Provence, makes clear. The picture's drama is listening, not fighting -- the same crowded tavern, the same faces, but the energy runs inward to the music instead of outward to violence. The Hermitage notes a tonal manner and a slightly subdued palette, occasionally enlivened with patches of bright colour: late Brouwer. Date flag: the Hermitage dates it c. 1634-38; Wikidata and FeelTheArt say 1624. The owning museum wins -- use c. 1634-38 and note the 1624 figure is wrong.

**Takeaway:** build a sense as a body -- one face for smell, one table of mouths for taste, one fiddler for hearing, one flinching patient for touch -- and let the same tavern hold all of them. The watchable behaviour is the sense arriving through the body: the smoke inhaled, the music listened to, the flinch felt, the same room running four different senses in four different faces.

## M5. The surgery pictures -- pain as touch

The barber's shop is the tavern's sober twin: the same low room with the same low people, but the action is a procedure rather than a pleasure, and the face under the knife carries the picture. Village Barber's Shop (1631, Alte Pinakothek, Inv. 561) is the first of Brouwer's surgery pictures and the hinge between the tavern scenes and the later operation pictures; no detailed description of this specific panel surfaced in the sources read, so the study states its mechanism only from its place in the series. The Village Surgeon (1635, Munich, W16) is the touch allegory in the Five Senses set; a version titled "Feeling" (Residenzgalerie Salzburg, Inv. 533, also 1635) is catalogued as a copy of this composition, which suggests the Munich panel was the model. The best-described surgery pictures sit just outside the 20: The Back Operation (c. 1635-36, Stadel, Inv. 1050, 34 x 27 cm REPORTED) -- a barber-surgeon operating on a man's back; and The Foot Operation (1636, Stadel, Inv. 1039) -- a barber-surgeon pulling a plaster from a screaming patient's instep, with a second version at Aachen (Suermondt-Ludwig-Museum, GK 67, 1630) and a Schloss-collection replica. Village Charlatan (The Operation for Stone in the Head) (1620s, Hermitage, GE-668, 28.2 x 25 cm REPORTED) is the earliest work in the catalogue and the only one from the 1620s: a village quack in a bright yellow shirt pretends to cut the "stone of stupidity" out of a seated patient's head, while two shiny stones lie at his feet as proof of the cure. The mechanism is the fake cure staged as theatre: the operation is not medicine but a performance, with the patient's credulity and the bystanders' attention doing the work. The subject descends from Bosch and Bruegel the Elder, who painted the same stone-extraction joke a century earlier -- Brouwer inherits the gag and replays it as a face study.

**Takeaway:** build a procedure, not a pleasure -- a barber-surgeon's hands on a patient's back or instep, the patient's face doing all the work, the onlookers watching the flinch the way a tavern crowd watches a brawl. The watchable behaviour is the pain arriving: the plaster pulled, the mouth opening, the body recoiling, the surgeon's hands steady while the face is not.

## M6. The small panel -- postcard scale, thin paint

Almost all compositions are small format, oil on wood (panel). The Smokers (46.4 x 36.8 cm) is comparatively large for him, and one of few signed works. Youth Making a Face is 13.7 x 10.5 cm -- postcard size. A Peasant Woman Picking Fleas off a Dog (ca. 1626-27, Met, 32.100.1, oval 18.1 x 13.7 cm, set in a rectangular panel 20.3 x 15.9 cm, VERIFIED) is the smallest format in the catalogue: a peasant woman in a white cap sits on a bench, bent over the dog in her lap, picking fleas off it, a jug beside her and a cottage behind. The mechanism is the tiny, intimate labour made monumental by the oval frame: the picture is postcard-sized, and the oval crops the world down to one pair of hands and one animal. It is the clearest proof that Brouwer's "small format" is a choice, not a limitation -- the whole scene fits inside a hand. The paint is thin and sketchy throughout: "a free, sketchy manner of painting and applied paint thinly" (EN Wikipedia); "vigorous handling of paint, with his characteristically short, unmodulated brushstrokes" (NGA). Hofstede de Groot: "a rich impasto gradually gives place to a more sketchy handling" over his career; "He renders his personages with the fewest possible means... One looks vainly in Brouwer's work for the types that are repeated indefinitely by Teniers or Ostade" -- his figures read as individuals, even portraits, not stock types. Madegooddesigns (observational, from the Met's scan of The Smokers): "the dark wall and floor are laid in thin browns and greys, and the lights are saved for faces, the white collar and cloth, and the window. Sleeves and hands are built from a few fluent strokes rather than careful blending." What was NOT found, and must not be invented: no source in this pass gives Brouwer-specific evidence for monochrome underpainting, grisaille, imprimatura or underdrawing practice. The "Flemish Technique" underpainting sequence found in search is a generic 15th-century / Jan van Eyck account, not Brouwer-specific. No infrared-reflectography study of Brouwer's underdrawings was found. The study describes thin paint and short strokes, and stops there.

**Takeaway:** build the picture at postcard scale -- one pair of hands, one dog, one oval frame -- and let the smallness be the point, not a constraint. The watchable behaviour is the intimacy: the whole world cropped down to the hands doing the work, the thin paint leaving the panel showing through, the lights saved for the face and nothing else.

## M7. The bright-to-tonal turn -- the palette that darkens

The early/late arc is the scholarly consensus, presented as stylistic inference, not a dated sequence -- no Brouwer is dated, and EN Wikipedia (following Renger/Grove) states his stylistic development "cannot be traced with certainty." Hofstede de Groot: "As dates are extraordinarily rare on Brouwer's pictures, it is hard to trace his artistic evolution in order of time, although this has often been attempted." Early pictures (1620s) are in bright natural colours and strong local colours -- "very richly coloured, because of the strong tones both in the costumes and flesh" (Hofstede de Groot); the early Pancake Man has "lumpish, misshapen peasant types and strong local colors" recalling Bruegel the Elder (Encyclopedia.com). The V&A's re-dating of the Luteplayer is the clearest single piece of evidence: previously dated to the late 1630s, it was moved to c. 1625 because its bright colouration and composition match the Pancake Baker, while the later work is "darker and more sparing." Around 1630 the palette shifts to a "strong preference for browns, greys and greens" (EN Wikipedia). Hofstede de Groot's three-stage account: (1) rich colour, strong tones; (2) transition -- more delicate general effect, "only one strong tone, perhaps that of the principal figure's dress or cap in full light, now compels attention. Light green, bright red, and a bluish purple are the favourite colours"; (3) late -- "all the values are subordinated to the general effect, with an obvious preference for neutral purplish-grey tones." In the Antwerp works "the sharp local colors of the early period have been replaced by an all-embracing tonality and a more painterly handling, probably derived from Hals" (Encyclopedia.com). The early mechanism is accumulation -- many figures, many small actions, a room full of things (mussels, shells, a cat, a jug). The late mechanism is concentration -- one face, one action, the light saved for it. A palette measured from a scan, flagged as method not pigment: madegooddesigns measured colours from the Met's scan of The Smokers -- deep umber #281407, olive black #333227, smoky olive #5a5335, dark red-brown #5c1c11, rust #814116, orange-brown #a46538, dull ochre #9d8d52, pale ochre highlight #b2a575. The page itself warns these "describe the painting as photographed today, not the original pigments." Usable as a tonal guide only.

**Takeaway:** build the same tavern twice -- first bright, the room full of things and strong local colours in the costumes and the flesh; then dark, the browns and greys and purplish tones swallowing the room until only one face in full light is left. The watchable behaviour is the darkening: the colours draining out of the room over the years, the crowd thinning to a single lit face.

## M8. The late turn outward -- moonlight after the tavern

In his final years Brouwer paints a few atmospheric landscapes of what EN Wikipedia calls "a tragic intensity," and the tavern painter steps outside. Dune Landscape by Moonlight (c. 1635-1637, Gemaldegalerie, Berlin, Inv. 853B, 25 x 34 cm REPORTED) is the catalogue's closing note: a farm with a cottage to the left and trees in the right foreground stands in moonlit dunes, a man in the left foreground, the whole scene held in a loose, atmospheric touch. The mechanism is the late turn outward: after a career spent inside taverns staring at faces, Brouwer paints open air and night light, and the same thin paint and loose handling that made smoke and grimaces now makes moonlight and haze. Hofstede de Groot on the landscapes: "far in advance of his time... their qualities of atmosphere and colour were not again attained until the best works of Ruisdael appeared." The subjects "seem to have been taken from the Haarlem rather than the Antwerp district." The late landscapes were influential on Lodewijk de Vadder, whose dune landscape with travellers draws directly on this picture. Hofstede de Groot again: Brouwer "never sought to represent a concentrated flood of light" in the Dutch manner except in very few cases; the late landscapes show instead "sensitive and sophisticated suggestion of light, atmosphere, and space" (Liedtke). Support flag: artble says oil on wood; allpaintingsstore says oil on canvas. Unresolved; the study gives no support for W20.

**Takeaway:** build the tavern painter's last subject -- the world outside the tavern, a cottage and trees in moonlit dunes, the same thin paint now making haze instead of smoke. The watchable behaviour is the opening: the walls falling away, the faces gone, the moonlight doing the work the lamplight used to do, the painter looking out at last.

## Aesthetic logic

Brouwer's logic is the face as a complete picture, and the small panel as its proper size. He does not build a scene and put faces in it; he finds the one face doing the one thing -- grimacing, shouting, recoiling, listening, smoking -- and lets it carry the picture, with the tavern, the cards, the medicine or the operation serving as the trigger. The crowd is organised by a single organising face: the cry in the National Gallery Tavern Scene (W15) draws every eye in the room the way the shout in the dice brawl (W14) draws the spectator's; the turned head in The Smokers (W18) breaks the group portrait open by noticing the viewer. The small format is not a limitation but a discipline: at 13.7 x 10.5 cm a face fills the picture the way a life-size portrait fills a wall, and the thin paint -- the lights saved for faces, the white collar and cloth, and the window -- makes the face the only lit thing in a dark room. The palette darkens across the career from bright local colour to purplish-grey tonality, and the subject concentrates from the crowded kitchen to the single tronie to the moonlit dune: the same arc in both registers, from accumulation to concentration, from the room full of things to the one face in the light to the empty landscape. The "learned painter" versus "vulgar painter" tension is the point: the NGA text notes 17th- and 18th-century critics praised his humour and truthfulness, and that despite a reputation as a "learned painter," his observation and wit suggest he cultivated a "vulgar painter" persona. The tavern is his laboratory, and the experiment is always the same: how much of a human being can one face, caught mid-gesture, contain.

## Positions

1. The Frans Hals link is affinity, not apprenticeship. Houbraken's pupillage claim is an error; the documented influence is Dirck Hals and the Haarlem loose-painterly milieu. The study never calls Brouwer Hals's pupil, and it flags that the Met's own 2008 Heilbrunn essay still repeats the claim.
2. The brawl is an emotion study, not a moral cartoon. The moral reading (gluttony, wrath, sloth) is one reading, and whether Brouwer intended a moral message is contested. The study uses the faces, not the sermon.
3. The "dissolute buffoon" is biographers' invention. The tavern-partying image comes from early biographers; Encyclopedia.com calls it largely invention. The study does not repeat it as biography, and it does not use the Rubens-fired-him-for-drunkenness anecdote (artble/sartle only, no scholarly source).
4. "About 60 paintings" is the count; "about 130 works" counts drawings and sketches. The study never repeats 130 as a painting count.
5. No dated works means the early/late arc is stylistic inference. The bright-to-tonal turn is the scholarly consensus; it is not a dated sequence, and the study never dates a work more precisely than its sources do.
6. Attribution is checked per work, not assumed. The Louvre Smoker is now Craesbeeck; the Met's The Brawl is a copy; the National Gallery's Four Peasants in a Cellar is now "Style of"; the Rijksmuseum Smoker (SK-A-4040) is dendro-dated plausibly to 1640, after Brouwer's death. A Brouwer title is not a Brouwer picture until the catalogue line says so.

## Network

The training that is not there: no reliable account of Brouwer's training exists (Encyclopedia.com). The Hals pupillage is Houbraken's error (see Positions 1). The one named influence in the EN Wikipedia article is Dirck Hals, the Haarlem genre painter -- the shared Hals hinge with van Velsen (#570, influenced by Dirck Hals). The early work's other influence is Pieter Bruegel the Elder, stylistic and by subject, not a teacher (Bruegel died 1569): Hofstede de Groot calls the early pictures "the last products of the school of Brueghel," and EN Wikipedia's Bruegel article names Brouwer among the early figures heavily influenced by Brueghel's peasant genre scenes outside the Brueghel family.

The Haarlem room: De Wijngaertranken, the chamber of rhetoric, motto "In Love Above All Else" -- its artist members and affiliates included Karel van Mander, Hendrick Goltzius, Frans Hals, Jan Steen and Esaias van de Velde (UT Austin thesis, via the repository). Brouwer shared a room with Hals, Esaias and Steen: a documented shared milieu, not a documented collaboration.

The Quast link: Quast "was influenced [by] Adriaen Brouwer" (EN Wikipedia Quast); Grove pairs Brouwer and van de Venne as Quast's comparators (see D1). Van de Venne was Quast's fellow Hague resident. Both already studied (#571, #572) -- excluded as candidates. The guardroom chain: formulated by Codde and Duyster in Amsterdam in the 1620s (Leiden Collection, Wieseman 2017); Brouwer painted the Flemish parallel in Antwerp in the 1630s (Peasants and Soldiers Playing Cards, ca. 1631-32); ter Borch's guardrooms "recall" Brouwer and Teniers II; Quast, influenced by Brouwer, painted guardrooms; Pieter Symonsz Potter painted a Guardroom Interior (Schwerin); Paulus Potter is his son. The chain is subject-based and influence-based; no Brouwer-Codde or Brouwer-Duyster meeting is documented. All chain members #566-#574 are studied and excluded.

The pupils and followers: only officially registered pupil Jan-Baptist Dandoy (1635). Joos van Craesbeeck, believed to have become Brouwer's pupil and best friend (de Bie; met in the Citadel bakery, 1633; the stylistic similarities of Craesbeeck's early work with Brouwer's "seem to corroborate such pupilage"). The influence list (EN Wikipedia): Adriaen van Ostade and his brother Isak, Cornelis Saftleven, David Teniers the Younger, Joos van Craesbeeck, David Ryckaert, Gillis van Tilborch, Mattheus van Helmont, Hendrik Martenszoon Sorgh, Horatius Bollongier, Giacomo Francesco Cipper, Daniel Boone, Joseph Danhauser. Encyclopedia.com names the principal followers as Adriaen van Ostade (Dutch) and David Teniers the Younger (Flemish). Jan Steen: WGA says Brouwer's best works "are comparable with those of Jan Steen and David Teniers II (both of whom were influenced by him)"; Wikipedia's own influence list omits Steen, so the Steen influence rests on WGA and visual-arts-cork (second-tier sources), not on the article -- usable with that caveat, not as a first-tier claim. Adriaen van Ostade was studied as #535 (excluded). Isack van Ostade, Cornelis Saftleven, David Ryckaert, Gillis van Tilborch, Mattheus van Helmont, Hendrik Sorgh, Horatius Bollongier: not checked for studied status this leg -- do not pick without checking.

The collectors and friends: Rubens owned 17 works at his death, at least one bought before they met personally; Rubens declared on 4 March 1632 that he had bought a Peasant Dance from Brouwer about a year earlier. Rembrandt owned six paintings and a sketchbook (Liedtke, fn 7, citing Bode and Denuce); Titus van Rijn also had Brouwer paintings. Van Dyck painted Brouwer's portrait, c. 1631. Paulus Pontius lodged him from 26 April 1634; both joined the Violieren that year; Fat Man / Luxuria is possibly Pontius's portrait. The Smokers is the friendship portrait: self-portrait with Jan Cossiers, Jan Lievens, Joos van Craesbeeck and Jan Davidsz. de Heem -- only de Heem's identification (far right) is firm. De Heem was studied as #557 -- the link is a fact, not a candidate.

The late landscapes: "In his final year he produced a few landscapes of a tragic intensity" (EN Wikipedia); they were influential on Lodewijk de Vadder, whose dune landscape with travellers draws directly on Dune Landscape by Moonlight. De Vadder: not checked for studied status this leg.

The geography as network: Oudenaarde (birth, to c. 1622) -- Antwerp 1622 (work unknown) -- Amsterdam (by March 1625; van Someren's inn) -- Haarlem (c. 1626; De Wijngaertranken) -- Antwerp (1631-1638; Guild master; Citadel 1633; Pontius's house 1634; death January 1638). Every move is a network move, and the last one is the Flemish turn: the Dutch years make the bright tavern pictures, the Antwerp years make the dark faces.
