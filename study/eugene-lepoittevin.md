# Eugene Lepoittevin: Study Notes

**Site:** Eugene Modeste Edmond Poidevin, known as Eugene Lepoittevin or Le Poittevin (31 July 1806 - 6 August 1870), French painter, lithographer and caricaturist -- born in Paris at 8 rue de Ventadour, the son of Nicolas Potdevin (later Poidevin), of Rouen origin, chief cabinetmaker at Versailles; died at the house of his daughter Eugenie and her husband, the tenor Leon Achard, in Auteuil, aged 64, on Saturday 6 August 1870, in the summer the Franco-Prussian War began. Pupil of a local Versailles painter named Ledoux, then of Louis Hersent and Auguste Xavier Leprince at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts (enrolled 1826). Salon debut Sunday 4 November 1827 under the name "Potdevin", with three pictures and the studio interior he had finished for his dead master; he exhibited at the Paris Salon every year until his death, over 150 pictures in all, 12 to 15 a year in the late 1830s by the Journal des artistes' count. Second-class Salon medal 1831, first-class medal 1836; knight of the Legion of Honour Tuesday 6 June 1843; knight of the Order of Leopold Monday 1 December 1845; Peintre de la Marine (official painter of the French navy) 1849. He built the chalet La Chaufferette at Etretat in 1851, about a hundred metres from the house where Guy de Maupassant spent his childhood, with a two-storey seafront studio and a fishing boat converted to a cabin in the garden; from 1859 he lived at Etretat for part of each year. Alexandre Dumas wrote of him: "Le Poittevin a invente Etretat" -- Lepoittevin invented Etretat. His studio was sold at the Hotel Drouot, Tuesday 9 April to Friday 12 April 1872: 144 paintings and finished sketches, 52 drawings and watercolours, 20 paintings by friends, realising about 40,000 francs. He had no monograph before 2020; the Fecamp exhibition L'invention d'Etretat (14 July - 15 November 2020, 22,000 visitors despite COVID) carried the first substantial biographical essay, and less than a month after it closed his "lost" Les Bains de Mer, Plage d'Etretat (1864), bought by Napoleon III on Saturday 10 June 1865 for 7,000 francs for the Elysee and lost after the fall of the Empire, reappeared at Sotheby's Paris on Thursday 3 December 2020 and sold for EUR 226,800, a record for the artist. The signature works are Interieur de l'atelier de feu Leprince (1827), the devil lithograph albums (1830-1834), Les Naufrages (1839), the Dutch-masters-at-work series (1840-c. 1860), the Versailles history pictures (1836-1845), Approaching a Surprise (1852), and the two wide Etretat bathing friezes, Les Bains de Mer, Plage d'Etretat (1864) and Bains de mer a Etretat (1866). ## M1. The Precise Place-Name Title
Salon titles that pin the picture to an exact, named spot and an exact state of sea or weather. The 1831 Salon list is the purest run: "Vue prise aux bords de la Tamise", "Une marine, Vue prise pres de Calais", "Un sloop echoue, vue prise sur les cotes d'Angleterre, a la maree basse", "Vue d'un vieux pont de bois, prise en Normandie", "Un effet apres la pluie, environs de Versailles". The title does the work of a caption, a map pin and a weather report at once. The habit never leaves him: "Vue de la base de l'aiguille a maree basse" (1860), "Pecheurs de rocaille au pied de l'aiguille d'Etretat" (1861), "Vue des environs d'Etretat pendant la saison des bains" (1870). **Takeaway:** the label IS the composition anchor. A generative piece can stamp its own place-name and tide-state on the frame and let the scene assemble around that caption; the words fix the picture before the paint does.

## M2. The Wide Beach Frieze of Named Faces
A very long, low panorama (roughly 2.4:1) filled edge to edge with a crowd of bathers and onlookers, in which individual figures are real, identifiable people. Les Bains de Mer, Plage d'Etretat, 1864, 63 x 149.4 cm: Maupassant in a blue cap at left, Charles Landelle in a red cap at centre, Bertall reading a newspaper at right, Lepoittevin himself in the crowd. Bains de mer a Etretat, 1866, 66.7 x 150.2 cm, Troyes: Maupassant about to dive, a self-portrait head at left. The crowd is not a mass; it is a row of small portraits, each figure doing one legible thing (diving, reading, changing, watching). Lindon's 1967 identification work used preliminary sketches and period photographs to name the faces. **Takeaway:** a slow lateral scan across a wide strip, each figure a distinct vignette that resolves as the eye travels. The watchable behaviour is the scan itself: a panorama that reads like a guest list.

## M3. The Cliff as a Pale Vertical Wall, Tiny Workers at Its Foot
In the Etretat views the chalk cliff is treated as one huge, pale, near-vertical mass -- the falaise d'amont (1842), the base of the aiguille at low tide (1860), the needle (aiguille) with rock fishermen at its foot (1861) -- and the human figures are small, dark, and busy at the bottom edge: fishermen hauling nets, gathering shellfish on the rocks, mending tackle. The scale contrast is the picture: monumental geology above, anecdotal labour below. The same grammar appears on the far side of the arch in Pecheurs a Etretat (c. 1840, oil on oak panel, Fecamp), where fishermen prepare nets on the beach beyond the famous chalk arch. **Takeaway:** one huge pale wall + tiny dark workers at its foot. The wall barely moves; the workers scurry, haul and bend. The watchable behaviour is scale itself -- geology as a slow backdrop, labour as fast small motion.

## M4. The Devil Silhouette Swarm
Flat black cut-out imps swarming a pale ground. Ombres Fantastiques (c. 1830, 12 plates) borrows the ombres chinoises shadow-play: each plate teems with silhouettes that evoke and subvert the shadow-play figures, "a great many mischievous (and often flatulent) devils". The devils then take centre stage in the erotic albums -- Diableries (1832-1834, 20 plates), Les Diables de Lithographies (1832), Charges et decharges diaboliques (1830) -- which became a sensation, spawned imitators and a whole genre, and were banned: Charges et decharges was censored and condemned to destruction in 1845/1846, and seized by US Customs as late as 1951. A single-sheet lithograph of 1831 (Wellcome Collection, 19.3 x 24.9 cm) shows two devils in a laboratory manufacturing statutes with a genie's help -- a satire of Louis-Philippe's press laws, the foremost devil resembling minister d'Argout, the other Louis-Philippe himself. **Takeaway:** black imps multiply, couple, scatter and regroup across a white field; the picture is a swarm, not a scene. Motion = the swarm's churn, with one devil always doing something rude at the edge of the frame.

## M5. The Painter-at-Work Anecdote (Dutch Masters Series)
A series of about twelve paintings, 1840-c. 1860, each showing a 17th-century Dutch or Flemish master at work in the open air, at the exact moment his famous picture is being made. Adriaen van de Velde Disembarking on Blanckenberg Beach (1840); Paul Potter Painting The Young Bull (1843); Van Dyck Receives a Drawing Lesson from his Mother (c. 1843); Van de Velde Draws a Naval Combat from Nature (1843); Van de Velde Studying the Effect of the Cannon his Friend Ruyter Fired for this Purpose (1845); Bakhuizen Telling Stories of Piracy (1845); Young Bakhuizen Contemplating the Effects of the Storm (1847); Bakhuizen Offering his Purse to Sailors to Take Him on in Bad Weather (1848); David Teniers leading Don Juan of Austria to a Fair (1848); Van de Velde Embarks to Go after Admiral Ruyter (1850); Bakhuizen Drawing from Nature in the Dunes of Skeveningen (1850); Adriaen Brouwer Painting an Inn Sign (c. 1860). The joke is structural: Lepoittevin, a painter who learned Dutch painting in the Netherlands, paints Dutch painters painting -- and one of them (Brouwer) is painting a sign, which is what Lepoittevin himself did for the Hotel Blanquet in 1842 (see M9). **Takeaway:** the painting shows its own making. A figure draws inside the picture; the landscape is also a studio. The watchable behaviour is recursion: a small painter-figure whose canvas slowly fills with the very scene the viewer is watching.

## M6. The Problem Picture / The Event Just Around the Corner
A picture that withholds its event. Approaching a Surprise (1852): a priest, head buried in a book, walks along a track; just around the corner is a pile of clothing that clearly belongs to a woman who has stripped off to bathe. The woman is never shown; the collision has not happened yet. Eclectic Light calls it one of the pioneering "problem pictures" of the 19th century, with a wry humour. The same withholding appears in A Picnic (1866), where the foreground clothes, parasol and hat echo the 1852 picture -- the people are present, the meal is the event, and the picture is about what is about to happen next. **Takeaway:** the key action happens just off-frame, just around the bend. Tension from a hidden second scene: two walkers on converging paths, one of them blind to what the viewer already sees. The watchable behaviour is the approach -- the gap closing, the reveal forever one step away.

## M7. The Second-Hand Sublime (Arctic Shipwreck Spectacle)
Polar disaster painted from published accounts, never from sight. Les Naufrages (1839): survivors of a wreck that struck an iceberg, a sailor in a red jacket fending off a polar bear. Overwintering of a Team of Dutch Sailors on the Eastern Coast of Novaya Zemlya (1839, 108 x 156 cm): a Dutch crew wintering on a Siberian archipelago. L'Attaque des ours blancs, bought by the State for the Luxembourg museum. Eclectic Light notes the point bluntly: neither Lepoittevin nor Biard (who painted Fight with Polar Bears the same year, 1839) had visited northern waters or could have seen a live polar bear except in a zoo; both pictures trace to a published account of Dutch sailors. Shipwreck off the Cliffs of Dover at Night adds the same spectacle closer to home: wreck, night, Dover Castle in the distance. **Takeaway:** assemble a disaster from reports. Ice, wreck, bears and a red jacket against the white -- the system is collage of second-hand extremity, and the watchable behaviour is the staging: the wreck arrives, the ice closes, the bear rears, all of it imagined.

## M8. The Scale Switch (State Epic and Beach Anecdote, Same Hand)
The same anecdotal eye applied at two wildly different scales. For Versailles, official history at large format: Combat de Wertingen (1836), Bataille navale d'Embro 1346 (1842, 121 x 135 cm), Prise de Baruth 1109 (1845), Dejeuner offert a la reine Victoria (1844) -- a 14th-century naval battle and a royal picnic rendered with the same crowded, incident-filled staging as a beach scene. For the Salon, the same crowd logic shrunk to fishermen and bathers. He was, in the Journal des artistes' 1839 words, a painter of such facility that he abused it, showing 12-15 pictures at the Salon every year. Gallery 19C (quoting Desjardins, 2020) reads the genre-hopping not as irresolution but as adaptation to evolving taste. **Takeaway:** one staging system, two scales. A generative piece can run the same crowd-and-incident engine at epic scale (a battle, a royal lunch) and at beach scale (a picnic, a haul of nets) and let the viewer feel the switch. The mechanism is the zoom, not the subject.

## M9. The Painting That Is Also a Signboard
Art as wayfinding. The Enseigne de l'hotel Blanquet (1842, oil on wood, Musee des Pecheries, Fecamp) is a painted sign for the artists' hotel at Etretat, made from a study, Vue de la falaise d'amont d'Etretat (1842). It hung on the facade for decades -- a postcard of c. 1890 still shows it -- and the hotel housed the artists who followed him there, including Claude Monet. The sign had to do two jobs at once: be a picture of the cliffs and be legible from the street as the mark of the house. The same double job appears in his portrait of the proprietress, Madame Cesaire Blanquet (Salon 1846). **Takeaway:** a picture that must read at street distance and name the place. The watchable behaviour is the sign's double register: up close it is a cliff view; from far away it is a beacon that says "artists stay here".

## M10. The Opportunist's Genre Hop (The Moment System)
A career built by recognising which genre is having its moment and painting it first, well, and fast. Erotic caricature when the July Revolution made satire sell (1830-1832); polar shipwrecks the year a survivor's tale circulated (1839); Dutch-master anecdotes while Dutch painting was fashionable in Paris (1840-1850); state history while Louis-Philippe built Versailles (1834-1845); beach bathing as the seaside holiday was invented (1860s); fairy tales modernised when the Salon wanted them (Le Reve de Cendrillon 1864, Suzanne au bain c. 1865, Petit Chaperon rouge). Eclectic Light's verdict: "a lifelong opportunist, quick to recognise a genre, theme or motif which was enjoying a moment of fame... able to capture the right moment every time." The place never changes -- the Normandy shore -- but the genre rotating across it does. **Takeaway:** one fixed stage (the shore), a rotating cast of genres. A generative piece can cycle its genre -- shipwreck, picnic, devil swarm, bathing crowd -- while the cliff and the tide stay put. The watchable behaviour is the hop itself: the same beach, a different story each time.

## Evolution
Phase 1 -- Precocious academic (1826-1830). History and landscape in the atelier line; finishes his dead master's big machine at 21; Prix de Rome second prize at 23, judged too young for the first. The driver is academic facility plus a very early commercial instinct: the Duchesse de Berry buys his first Salon picture, Des Moissonneurs, and grants the 15-year-old a pension of 500 francs. Two tracks from the start: official oil painting and cheap, fast print.

Phase 2 -- Lithographer, caricaturist, Salon regular (1830-1839). The diableries -- erotic/devil lithographs that made his name in a different register and got two albums banned. At the same time he becomes a prolific Salon exhibitor (12-15 pictures a year) and turns to the sea: fishermen, rescues, shipwrecks, polar subjects he never saw (Novaya Zemlya 1839, painted from published accounts, like Biard's polar pictures the same year). State history commissions for Versailles run alongside.

Phase 3 -- History-marine and the Dutch homage series (1840-1850). Versailles battle pictures (Wertingen, Embro, Baruth) plus a long series (1840-1850) imagining 17th-century Dutch marine painters at work (Van de Velde, Bakhuizen, Potter, Brouwer). European success: Brussels, Berlin, Leipzig; honours 1843, 1845; Peintre de la Marine 1849. The Dutch series is the most mechanism-like habit in his career: a painter painting painters, usually outdoors, usually in bad weather.

Phase 4 -- Etretat (1851-1870). The sea, fishermen and their families become central; the big innovation is the beach-crowd panorama -- Les Bains de Mer (1864) and Bains de mer a Etretat (1866) -- wide, frieze-like, full of identifiable contemporaries, the cliffs as a stage flat. Alongside, humorous and fairy-tale pictures (Cinderella, Little Red Riding Hood, Approaching a Surprise 1852) and modernised Bible scenes (Susana and the Elders c. 1865). Eclectic Light's verdict: "a lifelong opportunist, quick to recognise a genre, theme or motif which was enjoying a moment of fame... able to capture the right moment every time." Gallery 19C / 1stDibs: style "romantic in the 1820s and 1830s... gradually became more realistic thereafter."

What the sources do NOT support: no source read gives a year-by-year Salon title list, so the phase boundaries above are approximate and the 1850s are thinly documented (17 named works against 32 in each of the 1830s and 1840s -- almost certainly a source gap, not a fall in output, since he kept exhibiting yearly to 1870; no dated work at all was found for 1851, 1854 or 1856, the years of the chalet build and his wife's death, which should not be read as inactivity). The "drift to realism" is asserted by dealer pages, not by a technical or pigment study; treat as a received claim, not a verified mechanism. Whether the Etretat panoramas were painted outdoors, in the seafront studio, or composited from sketches is not settled in the sources read; Lindon (1967) worked from preliminary sketches and photographs, which implies studio assembly. No evidence in sources read that he taught Monet or directly influenced the Impressionists; the link is chronological and geographic (same cliffs, Courbet's studio loan, Monet's later series), plus the 2020 exhibition's framing "at the dawn of Impressionism".

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is a machine for catching the moment, and the moment is always somebody else's. He starts from academic facility -- the atelier line, the Prix de Rome second prize, the Salon machine that could put 12 to 15 pictures on the wall in a year -- and then aims that facility at whatever subject is paying that year: devils when satire sells, polar bears when a survivor's tale circulates, Dutch masters when Dutch painting is fashionable, Versailles battles while Louis-Philippe is building his museum, bathing crowds as the seaside holiday is invented. The place-name title is the warrant: the picture is pinned to a named spot at a named tide, and the caption does the explaining so the picture does not have to. The crowd is the unit: whether it is a Salon history, a beach frieze or a devil swarm, the picture is a row of small incidents, each figure doing one legible thing, and the viewer reads it by scanning. The cliff is the one constant -- a pale vertical wall that never changes while the genre rotating across its foot does -- and the late work narrows to that single wall and the people under it. The signature is the unstable element in the opposite direction from the Merles: his name moved (Potdevin to Poidevin to Poitevin to Lepoittevin, formalised around 1846) while the pictures stayed legible, and the trade then confused him with Corot instead of with his father -- a good painter whose name was a discount label and a signature to be scraped off. The career is a forty-three-year run from everywhere (Thames, Novaya Zemlya, Capri, Algeria) to one cliff, painted over and over. What makes him usable for generative work is not the opportunism -- that is the biography -- but the staging system underneath it: one fixed shore, a caption that fixes the picture, a crowd of small incidents to scan, and a genre that can hop while the cliff stays put. And the signboard is itself a system: a picture that must read at street distance and name the place.

## POSITIONS
P1-P13 are his own practice. Each is marked [FROM RECORD] (directly evidenced) or [INFERRED] (read from the work and the record) or [ABSENCE] (stated plainly). P14-P20 are reception.

P1 [FROM RECORD]: The Salon machine. He debuted at the Paris Salon in 1827 under the name Potdevin with three pictures, and exhibited at the Salon every year until his death in 1870 -- over 150 Salon pictures in all (en.wikipedia). At the Salon of 1831 he showed nine pictures under the name Lepoittevin and won a second-class medal for a Thames view (fr.wikipedia). The Journal des artistes noted in 1839 that he showed 12 to 15 pictures at the Salon each year (fr.wikipedia). He also showed at Brussels (1836), Bruges (1837), Berlin (from 1839), Leipzig and Dresden (1841).
P2 [FROM RECORD]: He finished his dead master's picture at twenty. When Auguste Xavier Leprince died on 26 December 1826, Lepoittevin was commissioned to complete Leprince's unfinished Interieur de l'atelier de feu Leprince, exhibited at the Salon of 1827. The 1827 Salon livret, quoted on en.wikipedia, says: of the thirty figure-portraits in the composition, nineteen had been completed by Leprince and the eleven others and various details are by the hand of M. Eugene Potdevin. Fr.wikipedia says instead that he painted 11 of 36 portraits; the livret is the primary source and the "36" is not relied on (see C4). Biographer Nadege Sebille presumes the seated figure at far left is a self-portrait of the young Lepoittevin (en.wikipedia).
P3 [FROM RECORD]: The name was a moving target. He was born Eugene Modeste Edmond Poidevin on 31 July 1806 in Paris, son of Nicolas Potdevin (later Poidevin) of Rouen, chief cabinetmaker at the Palace of Versailles. The family name moved Potdevin to Poidevin to Poitevin to Lepoittevin; he exhibited as Potdevin in 1827 and as Lepoittevin from 1831. The name change was formalised around 1846, the year his first daughter was born (fr.wikipedia). The spelling variants are a standing trap: he is NOT a relative of Guy de Maupassant, whose first cousin and close friend was a different painter, Louis Le Poittevin (1847-1909) (en.wikipedia).
P4 [FROM RECORD]: State history painter for Versailles. From 1834 the government of Louis-Philippe commissioned him for the historical museum at Versailles: Combat de Wertingen, 8 October 1805 (Salon 1836); the naval combat off Embro, 1346 (Salon 1842); the siege of Beirut/Baruth, 1109 (Salon 1845); and Dejeuner offert a la reine Victoria sous la tente au Mont d'Orleans, en foret d'Eu, 4 September 1843 (1844). His Attaque des ours blancs was bought by the State and shown at the Musee du Luxembourg (fr.wikipedia).
P5 [FROM RECORD]: The devil lithographer. In the 1830s he was a prolific lithographer and caricaturist for La Caricature. His diableries -- erotic lithograph albums of imps and their hapless human partners, among them Charges et decharges diaboliques (1830) and Diableries (1832) -- became a sensation and spawned imitators and a whole genre. At least two albums were banned in France; Charges et decharges diaboliques was censored in 1845 and condemned to destruction as an outrage to public morals (en.wikipedia; fr.wikipedia dates the condemnation 1845; the en.wikipedia infobox says "ordered destroyed in 1846" -- the year differs between the two articles, see C2). As recently as 1951 the album was seized by US Customs. The Bibliotheque nationale holds Diableries, an album of 20 erotic lithographs, freely accessible online.
P6 [INFERRED]: The devils were political before they were erotic. The same years produced Souvenirs patriotiques (1830), sardonic lithographs of the July Revolution, and Ombres Fantastiques, twelve plates of silhouettes after the ombres chinoises of Dominique Seraphin. A Wellcome Collection lithograph of 1831, Laboratoire infernal, shows two devils in a laboratory producing repressive press laws for the government of Louis-Philippe. The inference: the imp was first a vehicle for press-censorship satire under the July Monarchy, and only then a vehicle for erotica. The erotic albums are the ones that got banned; the political ones are the ones that explain why he chose devils at all.
P7 [FROM RECORD]: The Dutch-master ventriloquist. In the 1840s and 1850s he painted a historical series showing earlier marine painters at work in the open air: Adriaen van de Velde Disembarking on Blanckenberg Beach (1840); Paul Potter Drawing from Life in the Hague (1843); Van de Velde Drawing a Naval Combat from Nature (1843); Van Dyck Receives a Drawing Lesson from his Mother (c. 1843); Van de Velde Studying the Effect of the Cannon his Friend Ruyter Fired (1845); Bakhuizen Telling Stories of Piracy (1845); Young Bakhuizen Contemplating the Effects of the Storm (1847); Bakhuizen Offering his Purse to Sailors (1848); David Teniers the Younger leading Don Juan of Austria to a Fair (1848); Van de Velde Embarks to Go after Admiral Ruyter (1850); Bakhuizen Drawing from Nature in the Dunes of Skeveningen (1850); The Painter, Scene from the Life of Adriaen Brouwer (c. 1860) (en.wikipedia). He painted his own masters as working men, not as monuments.
P8 [FROM RECORD]: The Etretat move. Eugene Isabey introduced him to Etretat. He painted the sign for the Hotel Blanquet in 1842 (kept at the Musee des Pecheries, Fecamp). In 1851, after his first wife's death, he built the chalet La Chaufferette about a hundred metres from the house where Guy de Maupassant spent his childhood, and a two-storey seaside workshop with a large French window on the studio floor; in the garden he installed a fishing boat converted into a cabin where Maupassant liked to sleep. From 1859 he lived at Etretat for part of each year. His atelier stood on a cliff overlooking the sea, where, as Gustave Nicole remarked in 1861, he could contemplate the immense sea at all hours of the day (gallery19c). The fishermen were his friends and beneficiaries: the 1861 Dwight's Journal anecdote calls him their "friend, sponsor, crony, counsellor, and benevolent giver of old clothes".
P9 [FROM RECORD]: The beach crowd as a portrait gallery. His two best-known Etretat pictures are Les Bains de Mer, Plage d'Etretat (1864) and Bains de mer a Etretat (1866, Musee des Beaux-Arts, Troyes). The 1864 picture was shown at the Salon of 1865, bought by Napoleon III on 10 June 1865 for 7,000 francs for the Elysee, considered lost after the fall of the Empire in 1870, and rediscovered 150 years later; it sold at Sotheby's Paris on 3 December 2020 for 226,800 euros, a record for the artist. Raymond Lindon's 1967 Gazette des Beaux-Arts article identified the figures from preliminary sketches and period photographs: Guy de Maupassant (blue cap, at left), Charles Landelle (red cap, centre), Bertall (reading a newspaper, at right), and Lepoittevin himself. In the 1866 Troyes picture the young man about to dive has been identified as Maupassant and the head at left is thought to be a self-portrait (en.wikipedia).
P10 [INFERRED]: The opportunist's eye. The range is the practice: erotic devil lithographs, polar shipwrecks, Versailles battle pieces, Dutch-master pastiches, fairy-tale pictures (Cinderella, Little Red Riding Hood, Susannah and the Elders), and bathing crowds. Marie-Helene Desjardins (2020, via gallery19c) argues the diversity was not irresolution but an ability to adapt to evolutions in taste; Hoakley (Eclectic Light, 2021) calls him "a lifelong opportunist, quick to recognise a genre, theme or motif which was enjoying a moment of fame". The inference: he worked the market the way a marine painter works the tide, moving to whatever subject was paying that year, and the Etretat pictures are the one subject he kept returning to for himself.
P11 [ABSENCE, stated plainly]: No statement of intent survives in the record read. No manifesto, no published letters, no interview, no diary was found in any source searched or read. The only words of his own that surface are one short social letter of 13 February 1864 (see Q1), preserved as an image on en.wikipedia. Everything about his aims has to be inferred from the pictures, the commissions and the company he kept. This leg found no evidence that he ever explained his own painting in words.
P12 [FROM RECORD]: The German market. He travelled and exhibited regularly in Berlin from 1839, then in Belgium, Leipzig and Dresden in 1841, and was promoted there by the Prussian dealer Louis Sachse, a pioneer of the art market (fr.wikipedia, citing Anna Ahrens 2017). His Paris courses took many German pupils (see the Eleves list on fr.wikipedia: eleven named pupils, most of them German). He was a member of the academies of Antwerp and Berlin, and in 1837 was listed as a member of the Academie des Beaux-Arts of Brussels (fr.wikipedia).
P13 [FROM RECORD]: The institutional man. Knight of the Legion of Honour 1843; Knight of the Order of Leopold 1845; appointed Peintre de la Marine in 1849. In 1848 he sat on the committee charged with organising the arts and watching over artists' interests. In 1851 he was among 28 artists -- including Corot, Delacroix and Rousseau -- who petitioned for a gallery specifically to exhibit French art at the Great Exhibition in London; the project failed (en.wikipedia, citing Mainardi 1986). In 1860 he was a founding member of the Cercle de l'Union Artistique, the fashionable Paris club of artists, writers, architects and musicians (en.wikipedia, citing Yriarte 1864).
P14 [RECEPTION]: The Corot double. During his lifetime and for a generation after, his work was compared or confused with Corot's, and the confusion entered popular culture. In Eugene Labiche's comedy Edgard et sa bonne (1852) a character says a Le Poittevin is worth a Corot and much cheaper. In Georges Feydeau's Dindon (1896) a character is excited to be offered a Corot for 600 francs: "It is signed Poittevin, but the dealer guarantees me the signature is fake... I will have Poittevin removed and only the Corot will remain!" (en.wikipedia). The joke is the reception: a good painter whose name was a discount label and a signature to be scraped off.
P15 [RECEPTION, reported secondhand]: Baudelaire mocked him for "painting too well". The wording comes from a dealer listing (Proantic, Galerie Normandie) summarising his career; the Baudelaire text itself was not located this leg, so the jibe is reported, not verified verbatim. The same listing credits him with bold framing reminiscent of Japanese prints, a setting-aside of Romanticism, and a primary importance given to activities and the human figure -- "a player in modernity".
P16 [RECEPTION]: The critics' mixed verdict. Charles-Paul Landon (Salon de 1831) called him an unequal painter of great talent who, showing works of such different quality at the Salon, must guard against himself. The Journal des artistes (1839) praised the facility of his talent and said he abused it, showing 12 to 15 pictures a year. At the Brussels Salon of 1848 the critic Louis Van Roy received his David Teniers conduisant Don Juan d'Autriche severely for drawing and for colour judged false, while noting its elegance and distinction (fr.wikipedia).
P17 [RECEPTION]: The 2020 revival. The Musee des Pecheries in Fecamp mounted L'invention d'Etretat: Eugene Le Poittevin, un peintre et ses amis a l'aube de l'impressionnisme, 14 July to 15 November 2020, with a companion book carrying the first substantial biographical essay (Nadege Sebille). Despite COVID-19 the exhibition drew 22,000 visitors. Less than a month after it closed, the "lost" Bains de Mer reappeared at Sotheby's and set his auction record. Sebille tweeted on 3 December 2020 that her article had "borne fruit in putting this great artist in the spotlight... but what a pity for the @museedefecamp!!!" (en.wikipedia).
P18 [RECEPTION]: The "invented Etretat" verdict. While later artists such as Courbet and Monet and writers including Maupassant and Alphonse Karr would make Etretat famous and fashionable, it was Lepoittevin and his colleague Eugene Isabey who "discovered" the place, and Lepoittevin who "invented" Etretat -- so wrote Alexandre Dumas: "Le Poittevin a invente Etretat" (en.wikipedia, citing Dumas, "Causeries sur la mere", Cahiers Alexandre Dumas, vol. 22, 1995). The Stadel Museum's 2026 exhibition "Monet on the Normandy Coast. The Discovery of Etretat" still opens the story with Isabey and Lepoittevin (artdaily).
P19 [RECEPTION]: The polar-bear coincidence. In 1839 both Lepoittevin and his friend Francois-Auguste Biard painted Arctic shipwreck scenes with polar bears -- Lepoittevin's Les Naufrages (Musee de Picardie, Amiens) and Biard's Fight with Polar Bears -- and neither artist had visited northern waters or seen a live polar bear except in a zoo (Eclectic Light). Two years later Biard exhibited his Nova Zembla Coast (1841) on the same Dutch-sailors source Lepoittevin had used for his Overwintering of a Team of Dutch Sailors on the Eastern Coast of Novaya Zemlya (1839). The shared subject came from a published account, not from travel.
P20 [RECEPTION]: The studio sale as a measure of a life. He died on 6 August 1870 at the house of his first daughter Eugenie and her husband, the tenor Leon Achard, in Auteuil. Two years later his studio was sold at the Hotel Drouot, 9-12 April 1872: 144 paintings and finished sketches and 52 drawings and watercolours by Lepoittevin, 20 paintings by friends, plus his paints and utensils, realising around 40,000 francs (en.wikipedia). At the Mass after his funeral, the son of his friend Bellange recalled that the church filled quickly with artists, farmers, proprietors, chateau owners and fishermen, all assembled to mourn the town's great loss (gallery19c).
