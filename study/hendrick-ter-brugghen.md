# Hendrick ter Brugghen: Study Notes

**Site:** Hendrick Jansz. ter Brugghen (1588 - 1 November 1629), Dutch Golden Age painter of Utrecht, the first of the Utrecht Caravaggisti to come home from Rome, and the most gifted of them in the judgement of the modern literature. Born in 1588, birthplace unresolved: The Hague per the Leiden Collection (Piet Bakker, 2017: "born probably in The Hague in 1588") and Oberlin ("The Hague? Utrecht?"), Deventer per Britannica ("born c. 1588, Deventer?, Neth."), Overijssel per the Wikipedia article body following the Bodart print inscription, "not known for sure" per the Thyssen ("It is not known for sure where the artist was born, although surviving records in The Hague on his father, the lawyer Jan Egbertsz. ter Brugghen, suggest it may have been in that city"). The best current argument (Bakker/Leiden, citing Bok and Kobayashi 1985) is that the parents were recorded living in Utrecht in 1581, sold a house there on 19 April 1589, bought in The Hague nine days later, and a "Ter Brug deurwaerder" (bailiff) appears in a Hague tax register of 1585, so birth in The Hague is "reasonably assumed" - still not proven. Do not state a birthplace flatly. His father Jan Egbertsz. ter Brugghen (1561-1626), originally from Overijssel, was appointed secretary to the Court of Utrecht by William the Silent (Prince of Orange) and in 1588 became bailiff to the Provincial Council of Holland in The Hague, later first bailiff of the States of Holland; his mother was Sophia Dircx; his paternal grandfather was a Catholic priest from a distinguished Utrecht/Overijssel family, not allowed to marry, so his father was illegitimate. The family settled in Utrecht probably in about 1591 (National Gallery), where he was a pupil of Abraham Bloemaert (1566-1651), the Mannerist master, from about the age of thirteen - though the archives prove the father's settlement in Utrecht only before 1613, because the son trained under Bloemaert, who lived in Utrecht continuously from 1593. He travelled to Italy after April 1607 at the earliest - the old tradition says Rome c. 1604, ten years in Italy, from the Bodart print inscription of 1708 commissioned by his son Richard; newer scholarship (Bok and Kobayashi 1985; Leiden; Thyssen: "summer of 1607 at the earliest") puts his departure after his service as a cadet in the army of Ernst Casimir of Nassau-Dietz in spring 1607 - and remained there about seven years, in Rome, until the summer of 1614, when he is documented in Milan with Thyman van Galen, returning over the Gotthard Pass with three other Utrecht painters and arriving in Utrecht at the end of 1614; first record back, 1 April 1615, witness before the Utrecht court with Thyman van Galen. He never met Caravaggio, who had fled Rome on a murder charge in 1606, but he was, per Wikipedia following Liedtke, "the only Dutch painter in Rome during Caravaggio's lifetime" - he arrived while Caravaggio was still alive (died 1610) and studied the pictures Caravaggio left behind, above all the Calling of St Matthew in San Luigi dei Francesi, which he reworked twice (c. 1617 and 1621), and the work of Orazio Gentileschi and Carlo Saraceni. No paintings are definitely known from his Italian period. He enrolled as a master in the Utrecht Guild of St Luke in 1616 and married Jacomijna Verbeeck, a lawyer's daughter, on 15 October 1616 in a Dutch Reformed church - he himself never became Protestant, though four of the couple's eight children were baptised in Reformed churches, and his widow joined the Reformed Church shortly after his death; he is best described as religiously ambiguous, painting Catholic subjects for a mixed Utrecht audience. His first painting attributable with certainty is the Adoration of the Magi, signed and dated 1619 (Rijksmuseum), though Thyssen calls the Supper at Emmaus (Toledo) his first known work and the National Gallery says his first dated painting is 1620 (Christ Crowned with Thorns, Copenhagen) - see C403. In 1620-21 the return of Gerard van Honthorst and Dirck van Baburen from Rome "had a decisive influence on his stylistic development" (Thyssen) and "revived Ter Brugghen's interest in the art of Caravaggio" (Leiden); the three established "a fruitful dialogue based around Caravaggio" (Thyssen), and his first genre pieces, the Flute and Fife Players of 1621 (Kassel), are so indebted to Baburen that a joint workshop c. 1620/22-1624 is hypothesised (19 pictures merge both hands; the hypothesis goes back to Slatkes's 1965 Baburen monograph and is not proven). The stadholder's court commissioned his portrait of the Roman emperor Claudius (1618-1625) for a series of Twelve Roman Emperors by various artists, which may have prompted Constantijn Huygens to include him among the greatest artists of the Dutch Republic. By 1626 he had moved to the Snippevlucht, a narrow street along Utrecht's Oude Gracht near the town hall, renting a large house from Johan Wtewael (solicitor and burgomaster, brother of the painter Joachim Wtewael); he paid 18 guilders house tax in 1629, indicating a large house. Rubens spent a few days in Utrecht in the summer of 1627 and stayed at the inn Het Casteel van Antwerpen, run by ter Brugghen's elder brother Jan, very close to the Snippevlucht; Sandrart, who was Rubens's guide in Utrecht, later reported visits to Honthorst, Bloemaert and Poelenburch - but did NOT mention ter Brugghen - and the famous praise ("only one painter") comes ONLY from Houbraken, who derived it from Richard ter Brugghen's 1707 pamphlet; Leiden: "Whether Rubens really said this is impossible to determine, but the two artists do appear to have met" (see C402). Charles I of England owned a viola da gamba player of his in the 1630s, and his sister Elizabeth Stuart probably commissioned a mantelpiece from him; in 1644 a Bacchus of his was valued at 280 guilders in the collection of Boudewijn de Man. He died in Utrecht on 1 November 1629, aged about forty-one or forty-two (Wikipedia infobox says 40-41; the Bodart inscription says 42; Leiden says forty-two), probably a victim of the plague epidemic raging in Utrecht that year, and was buried in the Buurkerk on 7 November 1629 (Oberlin/Leiden; Britannica says 9 November - prefer 7 November, see C407); his wife was expecting their eighth child, a daughter born 14 March 1630, four months after his death. No references to ter Brugghen written during his lifetime have been identified; the earliest brief reference is Cornelis de Bie, Het Gulden Cabinet (1661), where he is mistakenly called "Verbrugghen"; Sandrart, Teutsche Academie (1675), calls him "Verbrug" and gives a few lines only. His son Richard ter Brugghen (1616-1710) campaigned from 1707 to rehabilitate him - a letter from Adriaen van der Werff (15 April 1707), four Evangelists paintings presented to Deventer town council (5 August 1707), and the Bodart engraved portrait (1708) after a lost drawing by Gerard Hoet - and de Bie (1708) and Houbraken (1718-1721) repeated Richard's claims. Neglected by 18th- and 19th-century collectors and historians, he was rediscovered in the 20th century as part of the reappraisal of Caravaggesque art; de Bie in 1708 called him one of the "most celebrated and talked about painters of his time", and today he is "widely regarded as the most gifted of the Dutch Caravaggists" (Leiden). ## M1. Softened chiaroscuro: silvery, atmospheric light instead of Caravaggio's stark black-and-white

What it is: he takes Caravaggio's tenebrism but drops the hard cut between lit and unlit. Britannica: "his light has a more atmospheric and silvery quality, as seen in his half-length The Flute Player (1621)." UEN Pressbooks: he "dispensed with Caravaggio's stark contrasts of dark and light and instead presented the viewer with a more colorful palette of soft tints." National Gallery: "a soft handling of paint and pale vibrant colouring." Oberlin: "cool, silvery light that plays over the greenish forms of Sebastian's flesh." This is his single most distinctive technical signature and the one every source agrees on. Watchable behaviour: a lit face or hand emerges gradually out of a grey-green dusk rather than being sliced out of black.

**Takeaway:** the light is a weather, not a knife. Where Honthorst cuts with a candle, ter Brugghen lets the light arrive like overcast.

## M2. Pale, bright, colourful palette with "exquisite gradations of light and colour"

What it is: unlike the dark-ground Caravaggisti, his colour stays high-key and varied. Thyssen: "His palette is bright and colourful, and he exquisitely handles gradations of light and colour, and takes delight in detail." The Concert (c. 1626) and The Flute Player use clear reds, blues and flesh pinks against pale grounds. Watchable behaviour: colour does not die in the shadow; even shaded cloth keeps a readable hue.

**Takeaway:** the shadow is coloured, not black. A ter Brugghen dark is a dark blue or a dark red, never a void.

## M3. Dark figures against a LIGHT background (inversion of the Caravaggesque norm)

What it is: instead of lit figures on black, he sometimes sets dark silhouetted figures against a pale, luminous ground. National Gallery: "He sometimes painted dark figures against a light background, which may have influenced such painters as Vermeer and Fabritius." The Kassel Flute Players (1621), "in their subtle tonality - with dark figures placed against a light background - anticipated by a generation the achievement of painters of the Delft school such as Fabritius and Vermeer" (WGA text, second-hand - see C409). Watchable behaviour: a dark head or shoulder reads as a flat cut-out shape on a glowing field.

**Takeaway:** he flips Caravaggio's polarity. The figure becomes the shadow and the wall becomes the light.

## M4. Half-length drinker/musician figures at close range, cropped tight to the picture plane

What it is: his favourite format - a single half-length figure (flute player, lute player, bagpipe player, drinker, bravo) pressed close to the viewer, often turning to meet our gaze. Wikipedia: "Ter Brugghen's favourite subjects were half-length figures of drinkers or musicians." National Gallery on A Man Playing a Lute: "drawing us close to the man by using strong, dramatic lighting to highlight the folds under his eyes, the long shadows of his fingers and his shiny red nose." National Gallery on The Concert: "We seem to have crept to within touching distance of this small group of musicians who turn towards us with surprise." Watchable behaviour: the figure fills the frame; the viewer is a fellow drinker or listener, not a distant spectator.

**Takeaway:** the crop is the invitation. One figure, one instrument, no room to stand back from.

## M5. Compression: many figures squeezed into a small, well-lit, shallow space for intimacy

What it is: in narrative pictures he packs the cast into a tight box instead of Caravaggio's spacious staging. UEN Pressbooks on the Calling of St Matthew (1621): "the Dutch painter compressed the figures into a small but well-lit space, creating an intimate effect compared with Caravaggio's more spacious setting." Oberlin on St Sebastian: "The powerful immediacy of the three figures within the shallow space is heightened by the close cropping of the composition, and by the figures' seemingly inextricable fusion into a single pyramidal form." Watchable behaviour: elbows, heads and hands overlap at the frame edges; nothing recedes.

**Takeaway:** depth is refused. The picture is a room with the back wall removed and the viewer pushed into it.

## M6. Pyramidal / fused group construction with parallel heads and clustered hands

What it is: in St Sebastian (1625) the three figures lock into one pyramid; the three heads sit on a parallel line and the hands gather at one point. Oberlin: "The parallel alignment of the three heads, and the close proximity of the hands at the upper right, are concise reiterations of the greater pathos projected by the juxtaposition of Saint Sebastian's awkwardly slumped and inert body with the alert and tender efficiency of Irene and her maid." The same device - a still, heavy body held by two alert attendants - is borrowed from Deposition compositions (Oberlin). Watchable behaviour: the group reads as one sculptural mass; the eye is pulled to the knot of hands.

**Takeaway:** the group is one object. Three people, one shape, and the hands are the hinge.

## M7. Working from life: unidealised, careworn, "unpleasant" naturalism - ugly faces kept on purpose

What it is: he paints models as found - red noses, underbites, coarse mouths, sunken flesh - where his master Bloemaert idealised. Seaman (Venue): comparing his Adoration of the Kings (1619) with Bloemaert's (1624), "in the older man's canvas, each is idealized and poised, where in ter Brugghen's they are careworn and graceless... Mary is hunched over Christ, who is bald and sunken into rolls of flesh - suggesting the use of an unknown but very eager eater, and perhaps that same child's exhausted mother, as models." Sandrart (1675, via Leiden): he followed "natuur" (nature) but it was "unangenehm" (unpleasant). Visual-arts-cork: he "prefers in all subjects contemporary and plebeian types, and always emphasizes, often over-emphasizes, character." Watchable behaviour: faces that refuse to be beautiful; a holy figure with a red, crying nose (Seaman on the Crucifixion's St John: "John's nose is red with crying, and his face is awkward and coarse, his mouth agape").

**Takeaway:** ugliness is a choice, not a failure. The model stays ugly because the truth of the face is the subject.

## M8. Archaism: deliberate quotation of pre-1550 Northern art inside a Caravaggesque picture

What it is: the core of Seaman's thesis. In the Crucifixion (c. 1625, Met) Christ is a re-creation of a 15th/16th-century Northern type - narrow-waisted, greenish, emaciated, torso collapsed at the waist, long nose, blood hanging in bright drops from hand, foot and chest wounds "in a manner not found in painting after 1550" - while Mary and John beside him are fully 17th-century Caravaggesque figures in modern dress (John's red and green robes layered around a 17th-century doublet). Seaman: the archaic Christ "has the uncanny quality of a work of art brought to life, even as he is dead." Possible models debated: Grunewald's Small Crucifixion, the Van Rijn Calvary (1363), Jan van Eyck's Crucifixion; Nicolson suggested a medieval Gothic wood carving. In the Calling of Matthew (1621) the archaisms are details: crumpled paper, an old man with an underbite after Marinus van Reymerswaele's Tax Collectors, a tilted table after Jan van Hemessen. Watchable behaviour: two time periods inside one frame - one figure looks 100 years older than the figures next to him.

**Takeaway:** time is a material. He paints a 15th-century Christ into a 17th-century room and lets the seam show.

## M9. Blood (and light) painted ON the picture plane - asserting the materiality of the canvas

What it is: in the Crucifixion the rivulets of blood "appear to pour down upon the surface of the canvas rather than upon the body or objects within the illusory space of the picture" (Seaman, quoted in the HNA review). Seaman: this "overtly calls attention to the materiality of the canvas and paint that made the illusion, not just illusion itself." By contrast, in St Sebastian the blood "drips onto the fictive surfaces, not in free fall" (Seaman, Venue abstract). Met Museum: "Christ's contorted body, with blood streaming from his wounds, intentionally refer to the work of early-sixteenth-century German artists." Watchable behaviour: blood that reads as red paint sitting on glass, breaking the illusion on purpose.

**Takeaway:** the paint confesses. The blood is allowed to be paint, and the picture admits it is a picture.

## M10. Crepuscular / eerie light and diseased skies; the "bile-coloured" atmosphere

What it is: his late religious pictures use a sickly, low, grey-green or ash-grey light that is itself a subject. Oberlin: "the artist's skilled manipulation of eerie, crepuscular lighting effects" and "cool, silvery light." Hedquist (JHNA): "The bile-colored sky in Ter Brugghen's painting provides an appropriately diseased backdrop for an image that speaks to concerns raised by a plague epidemic. An ash-gray atmosphere predominates the right side of the composition" and resembles the sky of the Crucifixion, which Helmut Nickel linked to the annular solar eclipse of 21 May 1621. Seaman: both 1625 pictures "share a low horizon with a delicately colored sky." Watchable behaviour: the sky is not a backdrop but a pall - grey-green, low, and slightly wrong.

**Takeaway:** the air is sick. The light itself carries the plague year, and the picture breathes it.

## M11. The turning-point pair of 1625: from ugly/archaic to beautiful - light effects prevail over materiality

What it is: Seaman's developmental claim. The Crucifixion and St Sebastian were likely painted simultaneously or serially in 1625, and together they mark a pivot: after 1625 he increasingly concerns himself with artificial light and with beauty. "In the Saint Sebastian, the sixteenth-century elements are reduced to details... With this work, ter Brugghen moves toward a new phase in his painting in which light effects prevail over effects of materiality, and beauty prevails over the ugliness which was so often present in his earlier paintings." Evidence: the two Annunciations (1624 vs 1629) - the 1624 Mary is "plain and stolid," the dove ragged, Gabriel in profil perdu with a filthy foot in the viewer's face; the 1629 Mary is graceful, the dove "sleekly feathered and gleamingly white." Same shift between the Liberation of Peter (1624) and the 1629 version. Watchable behaviour: the same subject painted twice, five years apart, with the ugliness ironed out and the light made the event.

**Takeaway:** 1625 is the hinge of the whole career. Before it, paint; after it, light.

## M12. Artificial-light pictures in the last four years (1625-1629): candlelight as a claim of artistic imagination

What it is: late works built around a single flame - Old Man Writing by Candlelight (c. 1626-27, Smith College), Melancholia (1627-28, Art Gallery of Ontario), The Denial of Peter (c. 1626-28, Art Institute of Chicago). Seaman: "As it is not actually possible to paint by candlelight because of its effects on colors, the depiction of artificial light not only implies artistic imagination, but also, like the selection of more beautiful subjects, greater artistic agency." Contrast with Honthorst: Honthorst "frequently placed a hidden light source in his pictures and used it as a pretext to work with dramatic and starkly contrasting dark and light effects" (UEN Pressbooks) - Honthorst's candle is a trick of contrast; ter Brugghen's candlelight is softer, more atmospheric, and arrives late as a deliberate display of invention. Watchable behaviour: a face lit only by a flame it holds, the rest of the room dissolving into warm dark.

**Takeaway:** the candle is a boast. You cannot paint by candlelight, so painting it proves the painter invented the light.

## M13. Large areas of transparent shadow; modelling by rounded mass, not small definition

What it is: his modelling avoids fussy detail in favour of big passages of shadow that round a form. Visual-arts-cork (old survey, treat as secondary): "Ter Brugghen's skill in modelling with large areas of transparent shadow, his avoidance of smaller definition in favour of the larger truth of rounded mass... The light, very broadly disposed, is most subtly modulated." Corroborated by Oberlin on the St Sebastian's "simple folds of the women's garments" and "monumental forms with subtle painterly effects." Watchable behaviour: a sleeve or cheek modelled in two or three broad tonal steps, not dozens of small strokes.

**Takeaway:** the form is built in slabs. Two or three tones do the work of fifty.

## M14. Brushwork: broad, sometimes loose, sometimes tight - and the limits of attribution

What it is: his handling varies within a single picture. Franits (JHNA 2017) on the Turin Crucifixion: "an ever so slightly broader or looser touch of the brush is indeed evident in the figure of Christ and the light-streaked sky... these passages simply display a more spirited technique on ter Brugghen's part... other passages... display a slightly harder facture, best evidenced in the contour of Saint John's face." And: "the degree of broadness or smoothness inherent to this brushwork varied in both canvases." The same variability is what defeats the Italian-period attributions: the Spier Collection Denial of St Peter has brushwork "far too schematic for an authentic painting by ter Brugghen," the Fortune Teller has a "high-key palette and pronounced painterly facture" that points to Johann Liss's orbit, and the St Jerome shows "marked stylistic differences" from authentic works. Also: alabaster highlights in the hands of Mary and John recur across the Crucifixions and the St Sebastian (Franits). Watchable behaviour: hands picked out with small, hard, pale highlights against broadly brushed flesh.

**Takeaway:** the hand is the signature and the trap. The same uneven hand that marks him authentic is what makes the fakes fail.

## M15. Hands as the narrative instrument: pointing, counting, plucking, untying

What it is: across his work the story is told by hands. In the Calling of Matthew the play of hands around the tilted table and the coins carries the moment of the call (Seaman). In the Concert the light picks out the musicians' fingers. In St Sebastian, Irene's hand on the saint's sternum and the maid's hand untying the strap at his wrist do the work of the picture: the maid's fingers are "posed as if holding a brush" and her hand is "tightly wrapped in a length of the same strap" with "no logical reason for her hand to be bound in the act of untying" - Seaman reads this as a self-referencing signature gesture, the painter's hand inside the painting, placed next to his monogram. In the Crucifixion the "alabaster highlights in the hands of Saint John and the Virgin Mary" are the most finished passages (Franits). Watchable behaviour: the eye is led from hand to hand; each hand is a small, bright, precisely finished event.

**Takeaway:** read the hands first. The faces feel; the hands decide.

## Works

### Utrecht, first decade back (1614-1622)

W1. The Supper at Emmaus. Date: 1616 (disputed: 1614 per the Thyssen artist text, which calls it his first known work after the return from Italy; 1616 per Wikimedia/Toledo-derived sources; the Toledo object report itself leaves the Date field blank). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 160.3 x 188 cm. Location: Toledo Museum of Art, Ohio, object no. 1983.1 (on extended loan to the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, through 2027). What is observable: four figures around a table at the moment of recognition - Christ in the centre breaking bread, two disciples across the table exchanging a tense, awed look. Modelled directly on Caravaggio's Supper at Emmaus but without Caravaggio's dramatic gestures; the drama is in the held look, not in flung arms. Large, early, and comparatively dark and restrained. Slatkes/Franits 2007 cat. no. A22. Autograph status not universally accepted (see C403).

W2. The Adoration of the Magi. Date: 1619. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 132.5 x 160.5 cm. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, SK-A-4188. NOT the Centraal Museum (see C399). What is observable: the Virgin seated with the Christ Child receiving three kneeling Magi; a large, crowded, early religious history piece. Franits groups it with the Le Havre Calling of St Matthew and the Lublin Pilate Washing His Hands as among the earliest indisputably authentic religious paintings after the return to Utrecht - the baseline for judging the Turin Crucifixion attribution. Exhibited Utrecht 1986-87 (Nieuw licht, cat. no. 2).

W3. Christ Crowned with Thorns. Date: 1620. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 207 x 240 cm (order varies by source: 240 x 207 cm in the Wikipedia biography caption; 207 x 240 cm in the selected works list and in aggregator records) - verified as a monumental ca. 2.1 x 2.4 m canvas; the height/width order is a source-ordering dispute, not a size dispute. Location: Statens Museum for Kunst (National Gallery of Denmark), Copenhagen. NOT Detroit (see C399). What is observable: Christ kneeling at the centre, surrounded by jeering Roman soldiers pressing the crown of thorns onto him. Very large scale for ter Brugghen at this date; a crowd scene rather than a half-length group. Google Arts and Culture (SMK text) notes the mood is "hushed and contemplative" rather than Caravaggio's high drama, and that the signature/date are original but "the attribution ... is not generally accepted" - an attribution caution, not resolved in this leg (see C404).

W4. The Calling of St Matthew. Date: 1621, signed and dated. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 102 x 137 cm. Location: Centraal Museum, Utrecht, inv. 5088 (Jacques Goudstikker collection). What is observable: Christ (right, with St Peter beside him in the Caravaggio model) calls Matthew, who sits counting money at a table ringed by onlookers in contemporary dress. Ter Brugghen takes Caravaggio's most dramatic moment and his table arrangement from San Luigi dei Francesi, but replaces St Peter with St John (Seaman's reading) and inserts archaic Northern figure types - the painting is a deliberate Protestant-inflected reworking, not a copy. Seaman pairs it with the Incredulity of St Thomas (W6) as produced "with one another in mind". A second, earlier Calling (c. 1617, Le Havre, MuMa) and a Budapest version exist; they are in the separate list.

W5. The Flute Player AND The Fife Player (Boy Playing a Fife) - Kassel pair. Counted as ONE catalogue entry because they are a documented pendant pair, painted together in 1621 as ter Brugghen's first genre musicians. Date: 1621 (both). Medium: oil on canvas (both). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED for either picture in this leg; no dimensions were returned by any search. Do not invent them. Location: Gemaldegalerie Alte Meister, Staatliche Kunstsammlungen, Kassel. NOT Karlsruhe (see C399). The Flute Player is the transverse-flute player in a colourful costume with striped sleeves and a feathered beret at a rakish angle; the Fife Player (also called the Shepherd Flute Player) is its pendant. What is observable: single half-length boy musicians against a light background, dark figure against light ground - the "subtle tonality" the WGA/Britannica tradition says anticipated Fabritius and Vermeer by a generation. The NGA Bagpipe essay says these two 1621 pictures were the initial foray, prompted in part by a Venetian flute-player picture then in Utrecht (attributed to Correggio, now thought Giorgione/Titian). The pair sets the template: one figure, one instrument, plain ground, theatrical "Burgundian" costume.

W6. The Incredulity of St Thomas (Doubting Thomas). Date: c. 1621-1623; commonly given as 1622. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 108.8 x 136.5 cm (Wikipedia); Franits gives 108.1 x 133.2 cm - a small measurement discrepancy, record both. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, SK-A-3908. What is observable: Christ forcefully clasps Thomas's wrist and thrusts Thomas's finger into the wound in his side; disciples crowd round in a tight half-length group against a neutral ground. Directly dependent on Caravaggio's composition (reversed), but Seaman argues ter Brugghen reverses the Catholic reading: Northern sixteenth-century types make Thomas spiritually blind, while praying holy figures model the faith he lacks. Do NOT confuse with Wouter Crabeth II's Incredulity of St Thomas, c. 1626-30, Rijksmuseum SK-A-1965, a different painting on the same subject that cites ter Brugghen's version.

### Mature 1620s (1623-1626)

W7. The Liberation of St Peter (The Deliverance of St Peter). Date: 1624. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 104.5 x 86.5 cm (Wikipedia); 105 x 87 cm in aggregator rounding. Location: Mauritshuis (Koninklijk Kabinet van Schilderijen), The Hague. What is observable: Peter asleep in chains in a dark prison cell, woken by the angel; a nocturnal interior lit by the angel's light, chains as the visual anchor. A second, later Liberation of St Peter at Schwerin is dated 1629 (see separate list); the 1624 Mauritshuis picture is the one named in the brief. The angel's pointing-heavenward gesture is reused from the Annunciation (W16) per Sotheby's catalogue note.

W8. The Bagpiper (Bagpipe Player; Bagpipe Player in Profile). Date: 1624. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 101 x 83 cm. Location: National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C., 2009.24.1 (Paul Mellon Fund and Greg and Candy Fazakerley Fund). Acquired 2009 after restitution to the heirs of Dr Herbert von Klemperer from the Wallraf-Richartz-Museum, Cologne (inv. 2613). What is observable: a muscular, rough-hewn musician in strict profile, larger than life, filling the picture plane; he squeezes the leather bag between his forearms, blows the pipe, fingers the chanter, two large drones resting on his bare shoulder. Face in shadow; light falls hardest on shoulder, shirt and fingers. NGA essay: broad, sure brushstrokes, wet-into-wet on a red ground, many pentimenti in the drapery and pipes; the point is the sensuality of music, not the individual. A second 1624 version, facing the viewer with a three-drone instrument, is in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford (see separate list); workshop versions exist, e.g. Sarah Campbell Blaffer Foundation, Houston.

W9. A Man Playing a Lute (The Singing Lute Player; Lute Player). Date: 1624. NOT 1626 (see C399). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 100.5 x 78.7 cm. Location: National Gallery, London, NG6347, Room 24. Bought 1963. What is observable: a single half-length lute player, lost in his song, mouth open on a note, long shadows of his fingers on the strings, folds under his eyes, and a shiny red nose - the National Gallery text says ter Brugghen may be exaggerating the red nose for comic effect, hinting the man has been drinking. Strong, dramatic side light. The picture is the type-specimen of the Utrecht single-musician half-length. Two Singing Lute Players (1624, Algiers and a second version) and autograph repetitions exist; see separate list.

W10. St Sebastian Tended by Irene (St Sebastian Tended by Irene and Her Maid). Date: 1625, signed and dated. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 149.1 x 119.4 cm. Location: Allen Memorial Art Museum, Oberlin College, Oberlin, Ohio, R. T. Miller Jr. Fund, 1953, inv. AMAM 1953.256. On view. What is observable: Sebastian, grey and bloodless, slumped forward with one arm tied by a leather strap to a tree, tended by Irene and her maidservant in a tight, monumental pyramidal group; strong diagonals, shallow space, cool chiaroscuro against an orange sunset glow and black birds over a desolate landscape. Allen text: "one of the most important Northern Baroque paintings in the United States", a "strikingly sensitive vision of physical suffering". Hedquist (JHNA 2017) reads the body and setting as plague references: Sebastian as plague intercessor, Irene as the caregiver who stays, in Utrecht's 1624-25 outbreaks (over 1,000 dead). Composition likely after Caravaggio's Deposition and a lost Baburen. Hailed as ter Brugghen's masterwork (NGA exhibition "Larger Than Life", 2011). Provenance: Pieter Fris, Amsterdam (1660s) onward; purchased 1953 via Frederick Mont, New York.

W11. The Crucifixion with the Virgin and St John. Date: ca. 1624-25 (Met: "ca. 1624-25"; Wikipedia: c. 1625; signature partly illegible, last digits read as 16[2][.] - the final digit is lost). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 154.9 x 102.2 cm. Location: Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, 56.228. Funds from various donors, 1956. What is observable: dead Christ on the cross, contorted body, blood streaming from the wounds, flanked by the Virgin and St John as mourning surrogates for the viewer; rigorous symmetry, flat star-studded night sky, skull and bones at the foot of the cross. Met text: the archaism is deliberate - a Baroque emulation of early sixteenth-century German art, not naive imitation. Seaman pairs it with W10 as sharing a low viewpoint and a date. Found in a bombed-out church in South Hackney, London, 1956; sold Sotheby's 28 Nov 1956, lot 115, for 15,000 pounds; acquired by the Met the same year. A second, larger autograph version (188 x 173 cm, cut down from ca. 300 x 185 cm) is in a private collection, Turin; Franits argues the Turin picture may be the primary version and the Met picture an autograph replica, reversing Liedtke. See separate list.

W12. The Concert. Date: about 1626 (National Gallery); 1627 in Wikipedia's selected works list. DISPUTE: 1626 vs 1627. Use "about 1626" as the museum date (see C406). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 99.1 x 116.8 cm. Location: National Gallery, London, NG6483, Room 24. Bought 1983 with National Heritage Memorial Fund, Art Fund and Pilgrim Trust. What is observable: a small group of half-length musicians crowded to the edges of the frame, turning toward the viewer with surprise, as if we have crept up on them; a bunch of grapes in the foreground painted with Caravaggesque clarity. Highly focused light, sharp highlights, intense shadows. National Gallery text: the half-length crowd filling the frame is Caravaggio's device, and ter Brugghen was the first important Dutch painter to bring it back to Holland. The female model (headdress/costume) reappears in the Denial of St Peter (W18) and in Girl Blowing on a Firebrand, c. 1626-27.

W13. Esau Selling His Birthright (Esau Sells His Birthright). Date: c. 1627 (Thyssen version). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 107 x 139 cm (Thyssen version). Location: Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, Madrid, inv. 393 (1981.16). What is observable: Esau, exhausted and hungry from the fields, faces Jacob across a plate of lentil stew; the fateful deal is concentrated in their hands at the centre. Behind them, their parents Isaac and Rebekah (named "Sarah" in one file record - that name is an error in that record; the mother is Rebekah). The picture "draws life entirely from the expressive, silent dialogue between the brothers" (Prestel / Google Arts and Culture, Gemaldegalerie Berlin text). Sparse still-life objects; the hands do the storytelling. The Berlin version is given as 1625 / c. 1626 elsewhere - see correction 9 in leg 2 and C411.

W14. Jacob Reproaching Laban (Jacob Reproaching Laban for Giving Him Leah in Place of Rachel). Date: 1627, signed and dated on the base of the table: "HTBrugghen fecit 1627" (HTB in monogram). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 97.5 x 114.3 cm. Location: National Gallery, London, NG4164. Bought 1926 (Claude Phillips Fund; W. Haffety sale, London, 26 March 1926, lot 119). Not on display at time of record. What is observable: Jacob confronts the seated Laban across a table; Leah stands beside her father, Rachel is in the left background (Genesis 29:21-26 - seven years' service for Rachel, given Leah instead). The same young model in profile, with a serrated beret, reappears in Esau (W13) and in Boy in Profile, Drinking from a Tankard. Ter Brugghen reinterpreted the subject the following year in a Cologne picture (Wallraf-Richartz-Museum) - see separate list.

### Late (1627-1629)

W15. The Duo (Duet; Two Singing Lute Players / The Duet). Date: 1628. Medium: oil on canvas. (One aggregator says "acrylic on canvas" - an error; disregard.) Dimensions: 101 x 81 cm. NOT VERIFIED by a museum page in this leg; dimensions come from a secondary aggregator only. Flag as such. Location: Musee du Louvre, Paris. NOT the National Gallery (see C399). What is observable: a young man playing a lute and a young woman singing beside him, in exuberant, brightly coloured, quasi-theatrical dress (feathered beret, low-cut dress) that reads as Burgundian stage costume rather than contemporary Dutch bourgeois dress. WGA text: the lute as erotic symbol / attribute of Voluptas; the pair's gaiety is the subject, with an undercurrent of indecency in the period's emblem language. The 1624 two-figure versions (Algiers; NG-related) are earlier treatments of the same duet idea.

W16. The Annunciation. Date: c. 1624-25 (Slatkes, per Sotheby's lot 12, 2005; Nicolson first suggested c. 1622). DISPUTE: 1622 vs 1624-25. A second, larger and later Annunciation is dated 1629 (Diest) - see separate list. Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 134 x 85 cm. VERIFIED only via Wikipedia's selected works list ("The Annunciation (1624), 134 x 85 cm, Whitfield Fine Art, London"); no museum page read end to end confirms them in this leg. Treat as secondary-verified, not museum-verified (see C413). Location (small version): formerly Whitfield Fine Art, London; private collection at last record (Christie's, London, 8 July 1994, lot 80; Sotheby's, 2005, lot 12). Provenance: with Edward Speelman, London, 1974. Current location NOT VERIFIED in this leg. What is observable: Gabriel pointing heavenwards (the gesture ter Brugghen reuses in the 1629 Annunciation and in the Schwerin Liberation of St Peter), the Virgin in a tall, narrow, upright composition. Sotheby's note: the picture only came to light ten years after Nicolson's 1958 monograph; Nicolson discussed it in a 1973 addendum, noting influence from a lost Baburen Annunciation known through a copy by Jan Janssens. Possibly the picture named in a promissory note of 18 April 1638.

W17. A Laughing Bravo with a Bass Viol and a Glass (standing in for the brief's "A Laughing Man with a Beer Mug" - see C399; no picture with that exact title was verified). Date: c. 1625 (aggregator date; not museum-verified). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 104 x 85 cm. NOT VERIFIED by a museum page; aggregator figure only (ArtsDot). Flag as such. Location: Royal Collection, London (per aggregator). NOT VERIFIED by a Royal Collection page in this leg. What is observable: a laughing bravo (soldier/mercenary type) mid-laugh with a bass viol and a raised glass; the laugh is the subject, spilling out at the viewer. Related verified drinker pictures: The Merry Drinker (Man with a Tankard and a Fish), 1625, Centraal Museum, inv. 11480; Boy in Profile, Drinking from a Tankard (kannekijker), c. 1627-28, Sotheby's 2009 lot 29, endorsed by Franits; A Laughing Bravo with his Dog, 1628 (Wikipedia list, no location verified in this leg).

W18. The Denial of St Peter (The Denial of Saint Peter). Date: DISPUTED. Art Institute of Chicago: c. 1626. Wikipedia: 1628. JHNA (Franits, fig. 4 caption): ca. 1626-27, 132.2 x 178 cm. Use "c. 1626-28" and record the dispute; do not pick one silently (see C406). Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 132.3 x 178 cm (Wikipedia) / 132.2 x 178 cm (JHNA). Location: Art Institute of Chicago, inv. 1969.3 (Charles H. and Mary F. S. Worcester Collection; purchased Feb 1969). What is observable: a nocturnal scene around a fire: Peter seated, head bowed, accused by a standing maidservant whose pointing arm is the picture's accusation; soldiers and onlookers crowded in the dark. Wikipedia/JHNA: "a marked departure from ter Brugghen's earlier painting in its emphasis on play of light, its baroque quality and a resolved sensibility." The maidservant's closest parallel is a fragmentary Denial at Stourhead (97.8 x 101.6 cm); the same female model appears in The Concert (W12). A very large (288 x 190 cm) Denial sold at Tajan, Paris, 2007 as ter Brugghen, now Spier Collection, London, is regarded by Franits as Franco-Flemish, ca. 1630-40, not autograph - see separate list.

W19. Democritus AND Heraclitus - Rijksmuseum pair. Counted as ONE catalogue entry because they are a documented pendant pair, conceived together. Date: 1628 (both, per Rijksmuseum / Wikimedia). DISPUTE: an inscribed pair, Laughing Democritus and Weeping Heraclitus, signed "T. B. fecit Ano 1622", is discussed by Franits/Bijl-Van-Urk (see separate list); whether the 1622 pair and the 1628 Rijksmuseum pair are the same pictures, copies, or two pairs is unresolved in the sources read (see C414). Medium: oil on canvas (both). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED in this leg for either picture; no dimensions were returned. Do not invent them. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. Democritus SK-A-2783; Heraclitus SK-A-2784. What is observable: two pendant half-length philosophers as moral opposites. Democritus, young, laughing, points into the distance where the folly of mankind is to be found; Heraclitus, old, melancholy, forehead in hand, weeps at the same folly. Google Arts and Culture: "whether you laugh or cry, the world remains incurably foolish." ATTRIBUTION DISPUTE: Slatkes/Franits 2007 catalogued them as joint workshop of ter Brugghen and van Baburen; Franits 2013 kept them in the supposed shared atelier; Franits has since said he no longer believes the two masters shared a studio (Bijl-Van-Urk, Democritus Laughing, 2020). Nicolson 1958 had listed a related Democritus as anonymous. The Rijksmuseum attribution to ter Brugghen stands, but the study file must carry the dispute, not present the pair as unproblematically autograph (see C405).

W20. Bacchante with an Ape. Date: 1627, signed lower right "HTBrugghen fecit / 1627". Medium: oil on canvas. Dimensions: 102.9 x 90.1 cm (Wikipedia rounds to 100 x 90 cm). Location: J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles, 84.PA.5. What is observable: a bacchante (follower of Bacchus, god of wine), flushed and dishevelled after too much revelry, with a small ape as her companion - the ape as the traditional emblem of folly, lust and mimicry. A late genre-mythology hybrid: the single half-length reveller format turned to a classical subject, bright, warm and openly sensual. Getty education text frames it as "one too many parties with her animal friend".

## Evolution

### Phase 1 - Early, Rome-influenced / return to Utrecht (1614-1619): the history painter comes home

W1 Supper at Emmaus (1616, Toledo, 160.3 x 188 cm) and W2 Adoration of the Magi (1619, Rijksmuseum). The work is large-scale religious history painting, learned in Rome and brought home. Palette: dark, earthy, restrained; light is Caravaggesque in contrast but the handling is quieter than Caravaggio's - gestures are withheld (W1's disciples exchange a look instead of throwing up their hands). Scale is ambitious from the start (W1 is his largest early canvas in this catalogue). Subject is almost entirely biblical history; no genre musicians yet. The Thyssen text says his first known work is the Toledo Emmaus; the Repentant St Peter of 1616 (S26) belongs here too if its date holds. No securely identified Italian-period painting survives, so "Rome-influenced" here means influence carried home in the head and in drawings, not a documented Italian oeuvre.

### Phase 2 - Return to Utrecht / re-Caravaggism (1620-1622): two scales at once

W3 Christ Crowned with Thorns (1620, Copenhagen, ca. 2.1 x 2.4 m), W4 Calling of St Matthew (1621, Centraal), W5 Flute/Fife pair (1621, Kassel), W6 Incredulity of St Thomas (c. 1621-23, Rijksmuseum). What changed: (a) Scale stays monumental in religion (W3 is the biggest canvas in the catalogue) while genre shrinks to the single half-length figure (W5) - the two scales now coexist. (b) Palette brightens and warms: W5's striped sleeves, feathered beret and light ground against a dark figure invert the Phase 1 dark-ground formula; the "silvery, atmospheric" light Britannica singles out in the Flute Player replaces plain tenebrism. (c) Light becomes the subject in the musicians: a plain light ground, one figure, one instrument, no narrative. (d) The trigger is external: Honthorst and Baburen return from Rome in 1620-21, and Thyssen/NGA agree this "had a decisive influence" - ter Brugghen turns afresh to Caravaggio after six years back home, now in dialogue with his Utrecht peers rather than from memory alone. (e) Subject: the religious pictures now argue theologically - W4 and W6 are paired reworkings of Caravaggio that insert Northern archaism and a Protestant reading (Seaman), where Phase 1 simply followed the model.

### Phase 3 - Mature 1620s (1623-1626): the system

W7 Liberation of St Peter (1624, Mauritshuis), W8 Bagpiper (1624, NGA), W9 Singing Lute Player (1624, NG), W10 St Sebastian (1625, Oberlin), W11 Crucifixion (ca. 1624-25, Met), W12 The Concert (c. 1626, NG), plus, outside the W count, S18 Roman Charity (1623, Met) and S22/S23 The Gamblers and David Saluted (both 1623). What changed: (a) The single-musician formula becomes a system: 1624 alone yields at least five music compositions (NGA essay) - bagpiper, lute players, violinists - with autograph repetitions and workshop copies, i.e. a market, not experiments. (b) Light is now controlled and selective rather than merely contrasted: in W8 the face is left in shadow while shoulder, shirt and fingers take the light; in W10 cool chiaroscuro is set against a single orange sunset. (c) Palette is at its brightest and most colourful - Thyssen's "bright and colourful ... exquisitely handles gradations of light and colour, and takes delight in detail" is a Phase 3 description. (d) Scale differentiates by function: intimate half-lengths around 100 x 80-115 cm for genre and for the paired religious pictures (W6, W7), monumental uprights around 150 x 120 cm for the prestige religious commissions (W10, W11). (e) Subject: the religious pictures reach their most deliberate archaism (W11's starry sky, symmetry and Germanic Christ) and their most topical (W10's plague reading), while genre turns comic and sensual (red nose in W9, the duet's erotic lute in W15's earlier versions). The two lanes - sacred and profane - are now fully parallel, sharing the same half-length, close-up, plain-ground grammar.

### Phase 4 - Late (1627-1629): light becomes the drama, and the judgement after the miracle

W13 Esau (c. 1627, Thyssen), W14 Jacob Reproaching Laban (1627, NG), W15 The Duo (1628, Louvre), W17 Laughing Bravo (c. 1625, but its drinker type matures here), W18 Denial of St Peter (c. 1626-28, Chicago), W19 Democritus/Heraclitus (1628, Rijksmuseum), W20 Bacchante with an Ape (1627, Getty), plus S19 Mars Asleep (1629, Centraal) and S6/S7 the 1629 Annunciation and Schwerin Liberation. What changed: (a) Light becomes the drama itself: W18 is organised around a fire in the dark, and the sources describe it as "a marked departure ... in its emphasis on play of light, its baroque quality and a resolved sensibility" - looser, more nocturnal, more resolved than the 1624-25 pictures. (b) Palette stays warm but the modelling loosens: Franits notes a "slightly broader or looser touch" in the late Crucifixion-related work, and the late genre pictures are painted with a freer, more confident hand. (c) Scale: the late history pictures go wide - W18 is 132 x 178 cm, a horizontal, multi-figure nocturne, against the upright 150 x 120 cm of W10/W11; W13/W14 are compact, horizontal, hand-centred dialogues (the deal in Esau is in the hands). (d) Subject turns to moral philosophy and to the consequences of a choice: Esau's bargain, Jacob's accusation, Peter's denial, Democritus laughing vs Heraclitus weeping at the same folly, Mars asleep (war disarmed). The late pictures are less about the miracle and more about the judgement after it. (e) The genre lane reaches its most theatrical: W20's bacchante and ape, W15's duet in stage costume - the Burgundian dress is now the point, not the disguise. He dies 1 November 1629, aged 40-42, with Mars Asleep dated to that final year.

## Aesthetic logic

Ter Brugghen's career is one long argument about what light is for, and the argument changes sides halfway through. In the first half, light is evidence. He comes home from Rome with Caravaggio's grammar in his head and no Italian pictures to prove it, and he spends six years painting history pieces in which the light is the thing that makes the old Northern types visible again: the archaic Christ in the Crucifixion is lit like a museum object, the blood is painted on the glass, the crumpled paper and the underbite in the Calling of Matthew are lit so the viewer can read them as quotations. The light is a scholar's lamp. It says: look at what I found.

Then 1625 happens, and the light stops being evidence and becomes the subject. The two pictures of that year are the same painter arguing with himself in public: the Crucifixion pushes archaism and materiality to their limit, and St Sebastian lets the light win - the orange sunset, the cool chiaroscuro, the beautiful bodies, the plague sky that is itself the disease. Seaman's claim is that after this pair he chooses beauty, and the pictures bear it out: the second Annunciation is graceful where the first was stolid, the late Denial is organised around a fire, the candlelight pictures of the last four years are impossible by their own admission - you cannot paint by candlelight, so the candle is the painter signing his name in light.

That is why the ugliness matters and why it disappears. The early ugliness - the red nose, the bald sunken Christ-child, the coarse mouth of St John - is not a lack of skill. It is the same instinct as the archaism: he paints the model as found because the found thing is the evidence, and the evidence is the argument. Sandrart called it "unpleasant" and meant it as a reservation; Seaman reads it as the whole point. When the ugliness goes, it goes because the argument is won: the painter no longer needs to prove he saw the world, he needs to prove he can invent the light that falls on it. The candlelight pictures are the victory lap of a man who spent his career proving he had been to Rome without ever meeting Caravaggio - the light is finally his own, not borrowed from a church in Rome.

And then there is the silence around him, which is part of the logic too. No references written during his lifetime. No named pupils. No named sitters. No documented church patron. The network is thin on documents and heavy on pictures, and that is exactly what the pictures do: they carry the biography. The son's 1707 pamphlet had to invent a Rubens meeting because the father left no paper trail - the only life is in the paint, in the hands that count coins and untie straps, in the maid's fingers posed as if holding a brush next to his monogram. He painted his own hand into the St Sebastian because there was nowhere else to sign. The aesthetic logic of the whole career is in that hand: the painter who had no documents left a self-portrait in a gesture, and the gesture is the only biography that cannot be disputed.

## Positions

**P1.** Born 1588, probably in The Hague (unresolved; see Site).

**P2.** Family settled in Utrecht c. 1591 (traditional); pupil of Abraham Bloemaert. (Archives prove settlement only before 1613.)

**P3.** April 1607, serving as a cadet in the army of Ernst Casimir of Nassau-Dietz; earliest possible departure for Italy is after this month.

**P4.** Late 1607 / early 1608, arrives in Rome (Hedquist); stays until 1614. Caravaggio already gone since 1606.

**P5.** Summer 1614, documented in Milan with Thyman van Galen on the way home; reaches Utrecht by end of 1614.

**P6.** 1 April 1615, witness before the Utrecht court with Thyman van Galen; first record of his return.

**P7.** 1616, enrolls as master in the Utrecht Guild of St Luke; 15 October 1616 marries Jacomijna Verbeeck in a Dutch Reformed church; couple live on the Korte Lauwerstraat.

**P8.** 1619, Adoration of the Magi (Rijksmuseum), first painting attributable with certainty, signed and dated 1619.

**P9.** 1620-21, Honthorst and Baburen return from Rome; ter Brugghen's genre phase begins: The Flute and Fife Players, 1621 (Kassel). Possible shared studio with Baburen until Baburen's death in February 1624.

**P10.** 1618-1625, stadholder's court commission: Emperor Claudius portrait for the Twelve Roman Emperors series.

**P11.** Summer 1627, Rubens visits Utrecht, stays at his brother Jan's inn; the two artists appear to have met (praise anecdote second-hand only).

**P12.** 1 November 1629, dies in Utrecht, probably of plague, aged about 41-42; buried in the Buurkerk 7 November 1629; eighth child born 14 March 1630.

## Network

### Family

Jan Egbertsz. ter Brugghen (1561-1626), father. Secretary to the Court of Utrecht under William the Silent (appointed 1581), then first bailiff of the States of Holland in The Hague (1588); the family moved with his offices, which is why Hendrick's birthplace (The Hague, probably) is argued from his father's postings. Sophia Dircx, mother. Married to Jan Egbertsz; named in the biographical record as Hendrick's mother. No further role documented. Jan ter Brugghen, elder brother. Ran the inn Het Casteel van Antwerpen in Utrecht, one of the finest in the city, very close to Hendrick's Snippevlucht house; Rubens stayed at Jan's inn during his Utrecht visit in summer 1627. Jacomijna Verbeeck (d. 1634), wife. Lawyer's daughter; married Hendrick 15 October 1616 in a Dutch Reformed church (he himself never became Protestant); bore eight children; was expecting the eighth when he died. Richard ter Brugghen (1616-1710), son. Published the 1707 Notificatie, a propagandist biographical sketch defending his father's reputation; presented four Evangelists paintings to Deventer town hall in 1707 as a permanent memorial; commissioned the Bodart engraved portrait (1708) after Gerard Hoet. Henrickgen ter Brugghen (b. 14 March 1630), daughter. Eighth child, born four months after her father's death (1 November 1629); her posthumous birth fixes the death date against the plague narrative. Unnamed: Hendrick's paternal grandfather, a Catholic priest from a distinguished Utrecht/Overijssel family, not allowed to marry, making Hendrick's father illegitimate. Not counted in the 54 (unnamed).

### Teachers and training

Abraham Bloemaert (1566-1651), teacher. Ter Brugghen trained as a painter under Bloemaert in Utrecht, who lived there continuously from 1593; Sandrart (1675) is the source for the pupilage. Bloemaert's studio also trained Honthorst, Poelenburgh, Bijlert, Jan Both and others, so the whole Utrecht Caravaggist generation shares this one master. No other teacher is documented; an earlier "rudimentary" Utrecht teacher is sometimes asserted but not evidenced. ALREADY CARRIED - do not propose.

### Pupils, workshop and followers

No painter is documented by name as a pupil of ter Brugghen in any source read this session. What IS documented: a workshop existed and produced replicas and variants - Slatkes/Franits catalogue lists 54 pictures associated with the artist and/or his workshop, and multiple signed/unsigned versions of the Flute Player, Bagpipe Player, Luteplayer and Violinist subjects, some with "some participation by the master" (Sotheby's lot note, Dorotheum lot note); a probable joint workshop with Dirck van Baburen c. 1620/22-1624 (19 pictures merge both men's stylistic attributes; the hypothesis goes back to Slatkes's 1965 Baburen monograph and is not proven); followers in the loose sense (painters who copied or imitated him) are unnamed in the literature read - "follower of Terbrugghen" is an attribution label, not a named person. Gap to state plainly: ter Brugghen's teaching line is a blank. Do not invent a pupil.

### Collaborators and early influences

Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio (1571-1610), master studied at second hand. Ter Brugghen was in Rome during Caravaggio's lifetime (the only Dutch painter in Rome then, per Liedtke/Nicolson) but the 1604 arrival date is now doubted (he was serving in Ernst Casimir's army in April 1607); he certainly studied Caravaggio's pictures, above all the Calling of St. Matthew in San Luigi dei Francesi, which he reworked twice (c. 1617 and 1621). No personal meeting is evidenced. Orazio Gentileschi (1563-1639), Italian Caravaggist. Early influence on ter Brugghen's colour and subject choice; Sotheby's notes a "clear recollection" of Orazio's frescoes in the Casino of the Muses, Palazzo Pallavicini Rospigliosi, Rome (before 1614) in the Luteplayer picture. Carlo Saraceni (1579-1620), Italian Caravaggist. Named by the Leiden Collection alongside Gentileschi as a northern-Italian master shaping ter Brugghen's early colour and history pieces; also the teacher of Jean LeClerc in Rome. Annibale Carracci (1560-1609), Bolognese classicist. Named by Wikipedia/Britannica tradition among the Italian painters who influenced ter Brugghen in Rome (composition and figure drawing), alongside Domenichino and Guido Reni. Domenichino (1581-1641), Bolognese classicist. Same tradition as Carracci: listed among ter Brugghen's Roman influences; no specific picture-to-picture link documented in sources read. Guido Reni (1575-1642), Bolognese classicist. Same tradition as Carracci/Domenichino; listed among Roman influences; no specific documented link. Bartolomeo Manfredi (1582-1622), Italian Caravaggist. Not a documented personal contact, but the key transmitter: Manfredi, more than Caravaggio himself, popularised low-life genre painting among the second-generation Caravaggisti, including the French and Dutch painters in Rome; ter Brugghen's musician/drinker half-lengths descend from this Manfredi-mediated line. CANDIDATE - reserve for #530. Valentin de Boulogne (1591-1632), French Caravaggist in Rome. Named by Adam Williams as one of the Frenchmen working in Rome at the same time as ter Brugghen, part of the same first Roman Caravaggesque milieu; later a major naturalist painter in his own right. CANDIDATE - alternate for #530. Simon Vouet (1590-1649), French painter in Rome 1613-1627. Named alongside Valentin by Adam Williams as a Frenchman in Rome during ter Brugghen's stay; president of the Accademia di San Luca 1624; later Premier peintre du Roi to Louis XIII. CANDIDATE - spare. Thyman van Galen, Utrecht painter and travel companion. With ter Brugghen in Milan in summer 1614; the two travelled home together over the Gotthard Pass, arriving in Utrecht at the end of 1614; joint witnesses before the Utrecht court on 1 April 1615. Dirck van Baburen (c. 1595-1624), collaborator and closest peer. Returned from Italy 1620; his work (with Honthorst's) revived ter Brugghen's interest in Caravaggio and pushed him into genre painting in 1621; the Flute and Fife Players (1621) are "so indebted to Van Baburen" that a joint workshop c. 1620/22-1624 is hypothesised (19 pictures); Baburen's early death in 1624 ended the arrangement. CANDIDATE - top pick for #530. Gerard van Honthorst (1592-1656), collaborator and peer. Returned from Italy 1620; ter Brugghen "worked with" him after his own return; Honthorst's Merry Fiddler (1623) became the typological model that ter Brugghen and Baburen both followed for half-length musicians; Rubens visited Honthorst (and Bloemaert and Poelenburch) in Utrecht in 1627. ALREADY STUDIED (#528) - do not propose.

### Patrons (Utrecht, The Hague, London)

Maurice of Nassau, Prince of Orange (1567-1625), stadholder. The stadholder's court commissioned ter Brugghen's portrait of the Roman emperor Claudius (1618-1625) as part of a series of Twelve Roman Emperors by various artists; Maurice was stadholder for almost the whole commission window (to April 1625). Frederick Henry, Prince of Orange (1584-1647), stadholder from 1625. Succeeded Maurice in April 1625, inside the Claudius commission window (1618-1625); the commission is attributed to "the stadholder's court" rather than to one prince by name, so both are listed with the succession caveat. Charles I of England (1600-1649), collector. Owned a ter Brugghen painting of a viola da gamba player in the 1630s; evidence of his international reputation among elite connoisseurs (Leiden Collection, citing Slatkes/Franits). Elizabeth Stuart (1596-1662), "Winter Queen", sister of Charles I. In all probability commissioned ter Brugghen to paint a mantelpiece (Leiden Collection); widow of Frederick V of the Palatinate (1596-1632). Boudewijn de Man, Delft collector. Owned a ter Brugghen Bacchus valued at 280 guilders in 1644 (posthumous ownership, not a commission); evidence of the high prices his work commanded after his death. Note: no Utrecht regent, church, or civic-guard commission is documented in sources read; Bok (JHNA 2009) states explicitly that "we know hardly anything about his circle of friends, his patrons, or the early buyers and owners of his works." The 1986-87 Centraal Museum exhibition catalogue's patronage essay (Franits) is the place to look, but it was not read end to end here. Do not invent a church patron. The Deventer town hall Evangelists (1707) were a posthumous gift from his son Richard, not a commission.

### Sitters, named beyond the patrons

None. No named sitter from life is documented in any source read. His one portrait commission, the emperor Claudius, depicts a historical figure, not a living sitter. His genre figures (musicians, drinkers, bravos) are types/tronie, not identified portraits. Bok (JHNA 2009) notes a possible self-portrait "hitherto ignored" - ter Brugghen himself as sitter is the only candidate, and it is speculative. Do not invent sitters.

Jan van Bijlert (1597/98-1671), Utrecht Caravaggist. Fellow Bloemaert pupil; one of the Dutch Caravaggisti named alongside Honthorst, Baburen and ter Brugghen in the Caravaggisti survey; his Calling of St. Matthew (1625-30) treats the same subject ter Brugghen painted twice. CANDIDATE - main for #530. Cornelis van Poelenburgh (1594/1595-1667), Utrecht painter. Verdict: contact probable, quotation not proven. Rembrandt van Rijn (1606-1669), younger Dutch master. Frans Hals (c. 1582/83-1666), Haarlem portraitist. No personal contact documented; "elements of his style can also be found in the paintings of Frans Hals" (Wikipedia) - a one-way stylistic echo, not a relationship. Johannes Vermeer (1632-1675), Delft painter. Born three years after ter Brugghen's death; influence at two removes: ter Brugghen's dark figures against a light ground and silvery tonality "anticipated by a generation" the Delft school (National Gallery; Leiden Collection) and "may have influenced" Vermeer. Carel Fabritius (1622-1654), Delft painter. Same Delft-school anticipation as Vermeer: ter Brugghen's 1621 Flute Players anticipated "the achievement of painters of the Delft school such as Fabritius and Vermeer" (Leiden Collection). Ernst Casimir I of Nassau-Dietz (1573-1632), Frisian stadholder. Constantijn Huygens (1596-1687), poet and courtier. Johan Wtewael, Utrecht solicitor and burgomaster. William the Silent, Prince of Orange (1533-1584). Gerard Hoet (1648-1733), painter/draughtsman. Drew the (lost) portrait of ter Brugghen on which Pieter Bodart's 1708 engraving is based; a posthumous image-maker, not a contemporary. Adriaen van der Werff (1659-1722), Rotterdam painter. Wrote a letter dated 15 April 1707, secured by Richard ter Brugghen, attesting to his appreciation of Hendrick's work - part of the son's rehabilitation campaign.

### Scholars and sources

### Adjacent artists (3, evidenced)

**Dirck van Baburen (c. 1595-1624): the man who shared his studio and rewired his painting.** The closest figure in ter Brugghen's life - the probable joint workshop of 1620/22-1624, the 19 pictures that merge both hands, and the Flute and Fife Players of 1621 that ter Brugghen effectively learned from him; he died at about 29, four years before ter Brugghen, so his whole career fits inside ter Brugghen's Utrecht decade. Top pick for #530.

**Cornelis van Poelenburgh (1594/5-1667): the Utrecht painter Rubens actually visited in 1627, while ter Brugghen went unmentioned.** Fellow Bloemaert pupil who took the same Roman road in the opposite direction - small Italianate landscapes with nude nymphs instead of tenebrist half-lengths; lived to 72, became the longest-lived of the Utrecht circle and painted for Charles I. The contrast with ter Brugghen (monumental vs miniature, dark vs light, dead at 41 vs court painter at 70) is the mechanism.

**Georges de La Tour (1593-1652): the Frenchman who learned Caravaggio through the Dutch - and is so often compared to ter Brugghen himself.** NAME TRAP: this is Georges de La Tour of Vic-sur-Seille, the candlelight painter, NOT Maurice-Quentin de La Tour (1704-1788), the pastellist already studied as #507. Georges probably never went to Italy; his tenebrism likely reached him through the Dutch Caravaggisti, which makes him ter Brugghen's mirror in France - same single-candle mechanism, opposite temperament (stillness vs theatre).

- C400. Rome dates: old c. 1604 tradition vs post-April-1607 revision. The 1604 date comes from the Bodart print inscription (1708), commissioned by the artist's son Richard; newer scholarship (Bok and Kobayashi 1985; Leiden; Thyssen) puts his departure after April 1607, summer 1607 at the earliest, on the strength of a cadet of the same name serving in Ernst Casimir's army in spring 1607. He therefore NEVER met Caravaggio, who fled Rome in 1606 - but he was, per Wikipedia following Liedtke, "the only Dutch painter in Rome during Caravaggio's lifetime" (he arrived while Caravaggio was still alive, 1607-1610, and saw the work in place). Do not state either date flatly without the qualification, and never state that he met Caravaggio. A second journey to Italy (c. 1620) has been postulated (Schuckman 1986, from an unknown manuscript by Cornelis de Bie) but is not documented; Franits judges it unlikely.
- C401. Birthplace unresolved. The Hague (Leiden Collection, Oberlin), Deventer (Britannica), Overijssel (Wikipedia, following the Bodart inscription), "not known for sure" (Thyssen). The leading hypothesis is The Hague, on the archival argument in Bakker/Leiden (parents in Utrecht 1581, house sold 19 April 1589, house bought in The Hague nine days later, a "Ter Brug deurwaerder" in a Hague tax register of 1585) - still not proven. Do not state a birthplace flatly.
- C402. The Rubens praise is second-hand. The famous "only one painter" quotation comes ONLY from Houbraken, who derived it from Richard ter Brugghen's 1707 Notificatie, a propagandist pamphlet intended to bring about a reevaluation of his father's work; the Bodart print inscription (1708) is the same son's campaign in visual form. Sandrart, who was Rubens's guide in Utrecht in summer 1627, later reported visits to Honthorst, Bloemaert and Poelenburch - but did NOT mention ter Brugghen. Leiden: "Whether Rubens really said this is impossible to determine, but the two artists do appear to have met." Present the praise as an anecdote from the son's rehabilitation campaign, not as a documented fact. The Naples work and the meeting with Rubens in Rome are Richard's claims only; flag as unverified.
- C403. The Supper at Emmaus date and autograph status. Thyssen calls the Toledo picture his first known work after the return from Italy and dates it 1614 ("his first known work ... dates from that year" - the year of his return, 1614); Wikimedia/Toledo-derived sources give 1616; the Toledo object report itself leaves the Date field blank in the extraction read. Franits notes the Toledo picture's autograph status is not universally accepted, and its date is only partly legible as "16--". Leg 1's "first painting attributable with certainty: Adoration of the Magi, signed and dated 1619" (Leiden) stands as the first certain work; the National Gallery's "first dated painting is 1620" (Christ Crowned with Thorns) is a third formulation. All three are recorded; none is asserted alone.

- C405. Democritus/Heraclitus attribution dispute.
