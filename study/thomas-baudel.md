## M1 -- Charade: the zone that decides whether a gesture counts (CACM 1993)

**Subject:** Gestural commands issued in mid-air, in front of a display, during a presentation -- a hand that talks to a machine while it is also talking to a room. Baudel and Beaudouin-Lafon, "Charade: Remote Control of Objects Using Free-Hand Gestures", Communications of the ACM 36(7), July 1993, pp. 28-35. DOI 10.1145/159544.159562. [C37074]

**Visual principle:** The body as a switch, and a boundary as a predicate. Charade's central device is the active zone: the projection of the display onto the space in front of it. The same hand movement is ordinary communication outside the zone and a command inside it -- the zone decides what counts. The second device is tension. A gesture is defined as a triple: a start position, a dynamic phase, and an end position. Start positions are tense -- full extension of the fingers, clenching of the fist, palm turned up -- and end positions are relaxed. Tension is the signal channel: the hand announces "this is a command" by how it holds itself, not by what it points at. The visual language is the body made legible: a tense hand against a display is a switch thrown; a relaxed hand is a hand at rest. The paper also gives each command a notation of three icons -- one per phase -- so a gesture can be written down and read back, the way a dance step can be notated. [C37075]

**Computational mechanism:** Sixteen gestural commands, recognized from a DataGlove, driving a HyperCard prototype. Recognition rates: 72-84 percent for first-time users, 90-98 percent for trained users. The system was used "in vivo", in real presentations, with few errors per presentation. The paper's design guidelines carry the mechanism: use hand tension to segment commands from communication; make commands fast, incremental, and reversible; keep them easy to learn; choose appropriate tasks (presentation control, not text entry). Future work named in the paper: video-camera capture instead of the glove, multi-user gestures, and home control -- the active zone generalized from a display to a room. [C37076]

**Takeaway:** A gesture counts only inside the zone, and only while the hand is tense. [C37077]

## M2 -- Overloaded strokes: redraw a curve to reshape it (UIST '94)

**Subject:** Free-hand drawing in which the same stroke means different things depending on where it lands: draw a curve to create it, draw over a curve to reshape it, draw across it to delete it, draw between two curves to join them. Baudel, "A Mark-based Interaction Paradigm for Free-Hand Drawing", ACM UIST '94, pp. 185-192. DOI 10.1145/192426.192496. UIST '94 opened Wednesday 2 November 1994. [C37078]

**Visual principle:** The stroke is overloaded -- one mark, four meanings, the meaning decided by the geometry of what is already on the page. The drawing surface is the interface: there is no mode menu to consult, because the existing curves are the menu. The curve under the pen is the operand, and the stroke over it is the operator. The visual argument is that editing can be drawing, not a separate activity with separate tools: the hand never leaves the page to go and fetch a command. The paper's test case is Ligne Claire, the clear-line drawing style, chosen because its curves are clean enough that a reshape is visible as a change in the line itself rather than in a cloud of control points. [C37079]

**Computational mechanism:** Four modes -- create, edit, delete, join -- selected by the stroke's relation to existing curves. The target curve is found by a distance measure D(f_uv, m) minimized over portions of the curve; the stroke is then reparametrized onto the target with a transition polynomial P(x) = -2x^3 + 3x^2 and a smoothness constant k = 3, so the reshaped curve blends from the old shape into the new one without a kink. Evaluation: an ampersand redrawn from Times into Helvetica shape, matching the target by the curve-error function the paper defines. Hardware: Wacom UD1212 tablet, 205 points per second, plus or minus 0.15 mm; the working resolution is about 40 dpi, coarse enough that the paper is explicit about what the tablet can and cannot resolve. [C37080]

**Takeaway:** The curve you draw over is the command you give. [C37081]

## M3 -- See-through tools: a sheet of glass laid over the work (CHI '94)

**Subject:** Tools that are transparent sheets laid over the artwork: you see the work through the tool, position the tool with one hand, and act through it with the other. Baudel and Beaudouin-Lafon, "A Taxonomy of See-Through Tools", ACM CHI '94, pp. 358-364. DOI 10.1145/191666.191786. [C37082]

**Visual principle:** The tool is a window, not a panel. A conventional tool palette sits beside the work and demands divided attention; a see-through tool sits on top of the work and the work shows through it, so the eye never leaves the thing being changed. The tool's position is its operand: where the sheet lies is what it acts on. The paper's catalogue is the visual argument -- a color editor that samples the color under it, a clip-art tool that stamps what lies beneath, an X-ray filter that reveals hidden vertices, alignment circles that pull objects into place like gravity, a font-size tool that moves with the text it sizes. The sheet makes the rule visible: the toolglass is the rule, held up against the work. [C37083]

**Computational mechanism:** Five operational steps define any see-through tool: Trigger (what summons it), Action (what it does), Appearance (what it looks like), Motion (how it follows the hand), Instantiation (how many copies exist). Fourteen taxonomy axes classify the space: Trigger Type; Input Transparency; Data Direction; Data Magnitude; Application Independence; Application; Operation Class; Composition; Output Transparency; Moves With; Lens Presence; Customization; Persistence; Complexity. The mechanisms the paper names: click-through (operation and operand in a single gesture -- click on the tool and the object beneath is acted on at once); drop-and-drag (pick up an object, drop it through the tool); the property sampler and applicator (sample a property from one object, apply it through the sheet to another); the gesture-interpreting tool; the graphics clipboard; the Print Tool; the Clip Art tool; the macro tool (pick up buttons from a control panel and carry them as a tool); the color editor; the X-ray filter with vertex selector; alignment circles implementing snap-dragging gravity; the font-size tool that moves with text objects; and composition by overlapping -- two tools laid over each other compose into a third. [C37084]

**Takeaway:** Put the rule on a sheet of glass, lay it on the work, and act through it. [C37085]

## M4 -- T3: two tablets, two hands, one sheet of glass (CHI '97)

**Subject:** A drawing environment built on two tablets, two hands, and transparency: the non-dominant hand positions a toolglass sheet while the dominant hand draws through it, and the two hands together stretch, rotate, and zoom the work. Baudel and Beaudouin-Lafon, "The Design of a GUI Paradigm based on Tablets, Two-hands, and Transparency", ACM CHI '97, pp. 35-42. DOI 10.1145/258549.258574. CHI '97 published Thursday 27 March 1997. [C37086]

**Visual principle:** Three design goals, stated in the paper and visible in every figure: maximize the artwork (no palettes eating the screen), minimize divided attention (the eye stays on the work), and increase the quality of the input (two hands carry more information than one). The division of labor follows Guiard: the non-dominant hand frames, the dominant hand acts. The toolglass tracks the non-dominant puck, so the sheet is always where the framing hand put it; the drawing hand works through it. The two-handed stretchy is the purest image in the paper: each hand holds one corner of a rectangle and the rectangle translates, rotates, and scales all at once, the way two hands stretch a sheet of paper. Curve guides -- french-curve templates -- add snapping and masking (frisket) so a drawn stroke can follow a template edge or be masked by it, with hard-edge glow effects where the stroke meets the mask. The paper measures the cost of the old way: scrollbars take about 6 percent of the window, and the new way takes none of it. [C37087]

**Computational mechanism:** Two Wacom tablets with rotation-sensing pucks, one button per hand, giving four binary states: 00 moves and rotates the cursor and the toolglass; 01 drags and rotates the object under the cursor; 10 pans and rotates the artwork; 11 zooms by pulling the two pucks apart. A marking-menu hotspot switches toolglass sheets: 6 sheets in the prototype, with 64 sheets times 10 commands giving 640 addressable commands. Integration with StudioPaint (Alias|Wavefront's paint program) supplied sweeps, a shelf palette, automatic handedness detection, and a puck offset so the two pucks do not collide. The paper's supporting measurement: handwriting is 20 percent faster when the non-dominant hand manipulates the paper -- the framing hand is not overhead, it is part of the act. [C37088]

**Takeaway:** One hand frames, one hand acts, and the sheet between them is the rule. [C37089]

## M5 -- Rockin'Mouse: tilt is a dimension (CHI '97)

**Subject:** A mouse with a rounded base that you rock: tilt it forward, back, left, and right, and the tilt is input -- a flat mouse given two more degrees of freedom. Balakrishnan, Baudel, Kurtenbach, and Fitzmaurice, "The Rockin'Mouse: Integral 3D Manipulation on a Plane", ACM CHI '97, pp. 311-318. DOI 10.1145/258549.258778. [C37090]

**Visual principle:** The device is the gesture. A normal mouse moves in the plane; the Rockin'Mouse adds tilt about the x and z axes, so one hand controls four degrees of freedom at once -- position in x and y, plus tilt in two axes. The flat spot at the centre of the rounded base is the visual and tactile argument: the mouse self-rights to level, so level is a home the hand can always find, and the tilt can be constrained back to two degrees of freedom by resting on the flat. The paper's task is 3D manipulation on a plane -- positioning and orienting an object in three dimensions without leaving the desktop -- and the claim is that tilt, being integral with movement rather than a separate mode, lets the hand do in one motion what otherwise takes a sequence of mode switches. [C37091]

**Computational mechanism:** A Wacom tablet tilt sensor reads about 1 unit per degree of tilt. Experiment: 14 subjects, 5,376 trials, a 3D positioning and orientation task against a conventional mouse. The Rockin'Mouse was 30 percent faster (F(1,12) = 21.08, p < .001). Forty-nine percent of movements were Euclidean -- all three dimensions changing at once, by a 10 ms / 0.1 mm threshold -- and about 70 percent of those Euclidean movements happened in the ballistic phase, the fast first part of the movement, which is the evidence that the extra degrees of freedom are integrated into the movement rather than tacked on at the end. First-trial planning cost was about 1.5 seconds; the last three trials were 40 percent faster than the first three, so the advantage grows with practice. Eleven of 14 subjects preferred the Rockin'Mouse. Patents: US 6115028 (tilt input) and US 7138997. [C37092]

**Takeaway:** Rock the mouse and the tilt becomes part of the move, not a mode after it. [C37093]

## M6 -- Two hands on one curve: the advantage grows with difficulty (GI 2005)

**Subject:** A curve-editing task done with one hand and with two, to find out when the second hand actually helps. Owen, Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Baudel, and Buxton, "When it gets more difficult, use both hands -- exploring bimanual curve manipulation", Graphics Interface 2005, pp. 17-24, session "Two Hands Are Better Than One", Monday 9 May 2005. The work began as a 1998 unpublished manuscript, "Bimanual Manipulation in a Curve Editing Task" (billbuxton.com/CurveMatch.html); the GI 2005 paper is the published form, and the two are one work, not two mechanisms. [C37094]

**Visual principle:** Two hands, one curve, and the question of who holds what. The task is the UIST '94 curve-matching task: reshape a curve to match a target. In the two-handed conditions one hand holds the curve (or the view) while the other reshapes it -- the framing hand and the acting hand again, now on a single object. The paper's three design principles are the visual argument stated as rules: the two hands' work must be visually integrated (both hands' effects visible in one place), conceptually integrated (the two roles must make sense as one action), and in integrated device spaces (both hands working on the same surface, not one on a tablet and one on a keyboard). [C37095]

**Computational mechanism:** Six conditions: one-hand, two-hand integrated, and two-hand non-integrated, each with the target curve's control vertices shown and hidden. Six subjects, 1,080 data points, scored by Baudel's UIST '94 curve-error function. Four hypotheses: H1, two hands are faster -- true, about 40 percent faster (F(2,1072) = 13.685, p < .05). H2, the advantage comes from epistemic action -- using the second hand to look, by moving the curve to see it better -- NOT true: after subtracting switching time there was no remaining difference, so the epistemic-action evidence was not isolated. H3, the advantage grows with task difficulty -- true, about three times larger on the harder tasks (F(2,1069) = 4.534, p < .05). H4, integrated device space beats non-integrated -- no significant difference found. The honest result is the split: the second hand helps, and helps more the harder the task, but the paper could not prove why. [C37096]

**Takeaway:** The second hand pays off exactly when the task gets hard. [C37097]

## M7 -- Discovery: the picture becomes the editor (UIST 2006)

**Subject:** A generic visualization framework extended so that large datasets can be edited directly, through the picture itself, instead of through a separate editing panel. Baudel, "From information visualization to direct manipulation: extending a generic visualization framework for the interactive editing of large datasets", ACM UIST 2006, pp. 67-76 (HAL record and citing papers; the IxDF record says pp. 57-66 -- discrepancy flagged in the Depth paragraph, not settled). DOI 10.1145/1166253.1166265 (IxDF says ...1166264). Solo-authored, from his ILOG period. UIST 2006 programme Monday 16 October 2006; the paper published Sunday 15 October 2006 per OpenAlex. Companion publications: "Browsing through an information visualization design space", CHI Extended Abstracts '04, pp. 765-766 (CHI EA '04, Saturday 24 April 2004), and the French "De la visualisation a la manipulation directe d'information avec ILOG discovery", IHM 2004, DOI 10.1145/1148613.1148642. [C37098]

**Visual principle:** The rendered data is the editing surface. A visualization framework draws the dataset; Discovery adds direct manipulation on top of that drawing, so the marks that show the data are also the handles that change it. The move is the same one as M2 and M3, one level up: where the mark-based paradigm overloaded a stroke and the toolglass overloaded a sheet, Discovery overloads the visualization itself. The picture stops being a report on the data and becomes the place where the data is worked. [C37099]

**Computational mechanism:** Stated honestly: the full text of the UIST 2006 paper was NOT obtained in this leg (the ACM PDF fetch returned paywall HTML), so no mechanism detail beyond the abstract and the IHM 2004 companion is asserted here. What the abstract and companion establish is the framework-level move: a generic visualization framework -- ILOG Discovery, built for large datasets -- extended with direct-manipulation editing, so the same machinery that lays out the picture also accepts edits through it. No prompt is built on this mechanism in this study; any future prompt must carry a read-before-build flag and the builder must read the paper end to end first. [C37100]

**Takeaway:** A visualization that can be edited through its own picture. [C37101]

## M8 -- Space-filling layouts: five dimensions hold every treemap (TVCG 2012)

**Subject:** The whole family of rectangular space-filling layouts -- treemaps, mosaic plots, stacked bars -- captured as points in a five-dimensional design space, with new layouts (spiral treemaps among them) falling out of the same space. Baudel and Broeksema, "Capturing the Design Space of Sequential Space-Filling Layouts", IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 18(12), 2012, pp. 2593-2602. DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2012.205. Short version: "A generic algorithm for sequential, rectangular, space filling layouts", Infovis 2011 poster, two-page text read end to end. The TVCG paper was read in substance: abstract, introduction, and the five-dimension sections read end to end, the score-function listings and figure captions verified against the text. [C37102]

**Visual principle:** A layout is a sentence in five words. The paper's claim is that every sequential space-filling layout -- slice-and-dice, squarified, strip, pivot, and the new ones -- is one point in a space spanned by five independent dimensions, and that the differences between famous layouts are differences in one dimension at a time. The visual consequence is that a treemap is not a single picture but a family: change the phrasing and the same data spirals instead of stacking; change the chunking and the same data groups differently. Figure 1, read as text, shows four points in the space side by side: a strip layout with recursion, a spiral, a pivot-by-middle, and a Hilbert phrasing -- chunks separated by black lines, individual items by red lines -- so the eye can see the dimensions doing their work. [C37103]

**Computational mechanism:** Five independent dimensions -- Order, Size, Score, Recurse, Phrase -- are the parameters of one universal algorithm. Order decides the sequence items are laid out in; Size decides each item's area; Score (the chunking function) decides whether the next element joins the current block or starts a new one -- BestMinAspectRatio, PivotBySize, PivotByMiddle, HalfItemCount, Grid, and the slice-and-dice rule (maximal score when the chunk has only one item) are all score functions; Recurse decides when a block is laid out recursively; Phrase decides the block configuration -- four sides times stack direction gives eight configurations -- with data-independent phrasings Strip, Zigzag (more accurately Boustrophedon), Spiral, and Spikes, and data-dependent phrasings such as Hilbert. The paper shows slice-and-dice, squarified, strip, and pivot layouts are various points in this five-dimensional space, and introduces new points: spiral treemaps and variations on the strip layout. Complexity is O(n) or O(n log n) on average, O(n^2) worst case -- the paper's point being that useful layouts belong to a class of limited complexity, not the NP-hard problem the literature had suggested. The core algorithm ships as a JavaScript prototype, usable as a layout component in visualization toolkits. [C37104]

**Takeaway:** Five choices make every treemap; change one and the picture changes its mind. [C37105]

## M9 -- Decision Exploration Lab: decisions as islands on a map (TVCG 2013)

**Subject:** Business decisions -- a hundred thousand car-insurance decisions, each made by a tangle of rules -- laid out as a landscape the analyst can walk: concept islands on a map, rules ranked by how surprisingly often they fired. Broeksema, Baudel, Telea, and Crisafulli, "Decision Exploration Lab: A Visual Analytics Approach to Explaining Decision Models and Their Execution", IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 19(12), December 2013, pp. 1972-1981. Read in substance: abstract and sections 1 through 3.2.5 read end to end, about 11,372 words of extracted text. [C37106]

**Visual principle:** A decision model is a territory. The Decision Map is a two-dimensional scatterplot of the model's factors, and a Voronoi diagram turns it into concept islands -- each island a cluster of decisions that were made the same way, for the same reasons. Two colors code the two kinds of thing on the map: input in blue, decision in green. A merge slider lets the analyst dissolve island boundaries and watch small islands join into continents, trading detail for overview in one gesture. The Rule Triggering view is the map's ledger: every rule ranked by the absolute difference between how often it was expected to fire and how often it actually fired, so the surprising rules rise to the top. The DataSpace tree -- categorical, numerical, and temporal subtrees with histograms and binning -- is the index to the territory. The visual argument is that a decision model with thousands of rules cannot be read as a list, but can be walked as a landscape. [C37107]

**Computational mechanism:** Three data spaces: DS1 the decision model (ontology plus rules), DS2 the decision instances, DS3 the execution traces. The running scenario is car insurance: DM1 eligibility, DM2 pricing, 22 objects, 94 attributes, 166 rules, over 100,000 decisions. Scale is stated, not hidden: a loan model with 100 concepts and 2,600 rules; a credit-card model with 1,300 concepts and 10,000-20,000 rules per rule-set, deciding 2,400 times per second with peaks of 55 million per day. Multiple Correspondence Analysis on the Burt matrix B = I^T x I compresses the decisions: 23 variables, 158 distinct values, against 100,000 decisions, down to two factors f1 and f2 for the map. Core tasks T1-T4 (in the paper's numbering): understand the decision model, understand the decisions, understand the execution, and find the decisions that matter. Natural-language queries run against the same space ("find decisions where the vehicle has antilock brakes"). [C37108]

**Takeaway:** A hundred thousand decisions, drawn as islands, with the surprising rules on top. [C37109]

## M10 -- ObjectivAIze: the optional recommendation (INTERACT 2021)

**Subject:** What happens when a human and a recommender decide together -- and how the way the recommendation is shown changes whether the human resists it when it is wrong. Baudel, Verbockhaven, Cousergue, Roy, and Laarach, "ObjectivAIze: Measuring Performance and Biases in Augmented Business Decision Systems", INTERACT 2021, pp. 300-320. DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-85613-7_22. HAL hal-04292393. Preprint: arXiv, 17 September 2020, "Addressing Cognitive Biases in Augmented Business Decision Systems" -- the same work as the INTERACT chapter, not a second mechanism. INTERACT 2021 opened Monday 30 August 2021. Read at abstract-plus-findings level: the abstract and the findings of both experiments, with the metrics definitions. The application domain is financial compliance -- anti-money-laundering alert filtering -- and Baudel was by 2023 CTO of AI Decision Coordination at IBM, the role this work feeds. [C37110]

**Visual principle:** The recommendation is an offer, not an order -- and the offer's presentation is the mechanism. The paper's key finding is about optionality: a recommendation the user must dismiss is different from one the user may take up, and the difference shows up in the numbers. The visual principle, stated as a perceptual structure rather than a picture, is that the system should present its suggestion the way a colleague would -- placed beside the work, available, ignorable -- rather than the way a gate would, blocking the path until answered. [C37111]

**Computational mechanism:** Metrics of performance, automation bias, and resistance. Experiment 1: subjects deciding alone succeed 72 percent of the time; aided by a recommender that succeeds 75 percent, they reach 76 percent -- the human-system collaboration beats each party alone. But complacency and authority bias degrade decisions by 5 percent when the recommender is wrong: any lingering algorithmic bias is amplified by the aid. Experiment 2 tests five presentation variants for reducing complacency bias and finds that optional presentation increases subjects' resistance to wrong recommendations. The collaboration window the paper draws: an algorithm below 70 percent accuracy is counter-productive as a recommender; above 80 percent, human intervention degrades performance; 70-80 percent is the window where the partnership pays. [C37112]

**Takeaway:** Offer the recommendation; let the human refuse it. [C37113]

## M11 -- HighC: draw your music (2007)

**Subject:** A graphical tool for creating music by drawing it: notes, envelopes, and wave shapes as objects on a canvas, grouped into rhythms and linked into modulations, with no programming. Baudel, HighC, highc.org, dated 8 April 2007 on his site. [C37114]

**Visual principle:** The score is the instrument. Sounds are visible things -- drawn, moved, resized, grouped -- and the music is what those things do. The visual language is synesthesia by construction: the paper's framing is "transforming objects made of bits into representations made of atoms and vice-versa" -- a drawn shape becomes a sound, and a sound is a shape you can pick up. Grouping is rhythm: sounds grouped together play as a pattern. Linking is modulation: a link between two sounds routes one into the other. The canvas holds the whole piece the way a score holds a symphony, except the notation is the sound itself. [C37115]

**Computational mechanism:** Draw notes, envelopes, and wave shapes; move and resize sounds; copy and paste; group sounds into rhythms and patterns; link sounds to each other for modulations. Synthesis covers FM, ring modulation, additive, and granular synthesis -- four synthesis families, all driven from the same drawn objects, with no code written anywhere. No evaluation is published on the site; the tool is presented as a working instrument, not an experiment. [C37116]

**Takeaway:** A sound you can draw is a sound you can hold. [C37117]

## The arc: the instrument decides what counts

Thirty years of work, one sustained argument: between the hand and the material there is always an instrument, and the instrument decides what counts. The early period (1993-1997, Paris-Sud and Alias|Wavefront, Toronto) works on the hand itself. Charade (1993) asks when a movement is a command and answers with a zone and a tense hand. The mark-based paradigm (1994) asks what a stroke means and answers: whatever the curve underneath it says it means. The see-through-tools taxonomy (1994) and T3 (1997) put the rule on a sheet of glass and give one hand to the sheet and the other to the work. The Rockin'Mouse (1997) puts the extra dimensions into the device so the hand never has to switch modes. The middle period (1998-2006, ILOG) moves the same instinct up a level: the bimanual curve work (1998/2005) asks when the second hand pays and answers, when the task gets hard; Discovery (2006) asks what happens when the whole visualization becomes the editor; transactional graphic objects (2002) ask what happens when the picture itself is a transaction over a network. The late period (2011-2023, IBM) turns from the hand to the system that decides: the space-filling-layouts design space (2011-2012) treats layout as five choices, any point in the space a valid picture; the Decision Exploration Lab (2013) treats a hundred thousand decisions as a landscape to walk; ObjectivAIze (2020-2021) treats the recommender as a colleague whose suggestions must be refusable. The through-line: Baudel never asks the user to adapt to the system's clock or the system's categories. He builds an instrument -- a zone, a tense hand, a sheet of glass, a tilt, a second hand, an optional recommendation -- and the instrument carries the decision about what counts, so the human can get on with the work. [C37118]

## The aesthetic logic

Baudel's systems share a visual grammar that is directly portable: the rule is always a visible, holdable thing, and the correction is always a gesture made through it. Three structures recur across the whole corpus and give a piece its tempo. First, the boundary that decides: the active zone, the edge of the toolglass sheet, the flat spot at the centre of the Rockin'Mouse -- a line or a surface that says "inside here, this means something". Second, the two hands in two roles: one frames, one acts; one holds the rule, the other corrects it; the non-dominant hand is never idle, it is the hand that makes the dominant hand's gesture meaningful. Third, the offer that can be refused: the optional recommendation, the reversible command, the stroke that can be redrawn over, the rule whose firing is ranked by surprise rather than by authority. Nothing is hidden behind a black box; the computation shows its work as a sheet you can see through, a zone you can step into, an island you can walk to, a recommendation you can leave on the table. That is why his mechanisms transfer to generative pieces so cleanly: each one is already a small machine for deciding what counts -- and the piece is that machine running on new material. [C37119]
