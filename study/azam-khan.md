## The question: when the hand moves, what moves with it?

Khan's decade at Alias/Autodesk Toronto is one long answer to a single question, asked at four scales. At the scale of the display, Boom Chameleon asks: what if the screen itself were the camera -- you walk around a fixed world holding a window, and the view is wherever the window is? At the scale of the widget, Tracking Menus asks: what if the tool followed the hand instead of waiting to be fetched -- a menu you push around by its edge, like a jar lid under a pencil tip? At the scale of the room, Spotlight and VizStudio ask: what if attention were a light you could move, and the room itself were staged so the meeting is a performance? At the scale of the camera, StyleCam and ShowMotion ask: what if the camera moved for you, along authored surfaces and shots, so every view is a composed picture? The through-line is that the user never chases the interface: the interface comes to the user, by a stated rule. [C36269]

The second question is what the rule costs. Every one of these systems trades something for its following: Boom Chameleon trades free navigation for a 1:1 pose mapping (you cannot fly above the car to look straight down); Tracking Menus trades a visible boundary for an invisible one (the tracking edge need not match the visual edge); Spotlight trades the whole field for one lit circle (everything else darkens 75%); StyleCam trades camera control for authored views (the viewer drives, but the piece composes). The study therefore has to do two things at once: take the following rules seriously as executable mechanisms, and refuse to pretend the trade is free -- each prompt below names what the system gives up, because the giving-up is part of the system. [C36270]

**Takeaway:** Do not build "a Khan" as a big screen with windows on it. Build the following rule -- one thing that travels with the hand, by a stated mechanism, at the cost of something else. [C36271]

## The work in four phases

In 2001-2002 the work is about the display as a thing you move and model on: Interaction Techniques for 3D Modeling on Large Displays (I3D 2001) proposes two-handed 3D modelling on an 8 x 6 ft back-projected screen with a six-degree-of-freedom tracker in each hand; Creating Principal 3D Curves with Digital Tape Drawing (CHI 2002) extends tape drawing so a designer builds a car's defining curves by drawing 2D curves on working planes and extruding them, with a full 3D curve network for a Dodge Viper built with no planar curves at all; Boom Chameleon (UIST 2002) rebuilds a Fakespace BOOM3C as a window-in-hand; StyleCam (UIST 2002) hands camera control to an author. Two attribution corrections belong here: Large Displays in Automotive Design (IEEE CG&A 2000) is the lab's framing paper for the whole programme but Khan is not an author on it (verified in its byline, in DBLP, and in Bill Buxton's CV); nor is he an author on the original Digital Tape Drawing paper (UIST 1999), which predates his joining that line of work in the CHI 2002 extension. Both are context and precursor, not Khan works. [C36277]

In 2003 the centre of gravity shifts to widgets and a vision: Tracking Menus (UIST 2003) keeps tools in place under the hand; Sentient Data Access (ACM Queue 2003) proposes a whole society of devices -- terminals, identifiers, containers -- in which a designer scans a barcode on a physical picture of a car with a PDA, carries it to the wall-sized Powerwall, and the 3D model appears; Game-like Navigation (CACM 2003) synthesises the programme in game terms, arguing non-game applications should borrow real-time response, interactive narrative, simultaneous navigation and action, and replay. [C36278]

In 2004-2005 the problem becomes distance and attention across rooms: Frisbee (UIST 2004) portals remote content to a local telescope; Spotlight (CHI 2005) directs attention on wall-sized displays by darkening everything except a circle around the cursor; HoverCam (I3D 2005) collapses tumble, pan and zoom into one drag driven by a satellite-orbit, gravity-field model; and the VizStudio paper (IEEE CG&A 2005) treats the meeting room itself as the designed artefact. [C36279]

In 2006-2009 the work consolidates into navigation as a solved, safe, cinematic system: ShowMotion's authored shots (I3D 2006), Safe 3D Navigation's seven properties and SteeringWheels (I3D 2008), ViewCube's 26-view orientation widget (I3D 2008), PieCursor's merged pointing and selection (CHI 2008), PenLight's projector pen (CHI 2009), and the multiscale and analytic-drawing work -- and the 2009 Digital Design Studio survey (Human-Computer Interaction, 24(1), pp. 9-47) writes the decade up, organising ten years of the lab's large-display work by display size crossed with design stage, and judging every component by flow: techniques that impede flow are rejected, the "Minority Report" standing-and-hand-waving style is explicitly set aside because users "really just want to sit down and let someone else stand at the board". After 2010 the display changes scale once more: the building becomes the display (Project Dasher and BIM visualisation; Big Data from the Built Environment, UbiComp 2011, solo), the audience becomes analysts of data (Dive In!, EgoLines, Annotation Graphs, Knowledge-Transfer Graphs), and the mechanism becomes simulation itself (SyDEVS, SimAUD, Trax). [C36280]

**Takeaway:** The arc is a widening of the unit: display, then widget, then room, then building. The mechanisms that survive as pieces are the ones from the narrow end -- one hand, one rule, one following thing. [C36281]

## Boom Chameleon: the window in hand

Boom Chameleon: Simultaneous Capture of 3D Viewpoint, Voice and Gesture Annotations on a Spatially-Aware Display (Tsang, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach, Khan, Buxton; UIST 2002, Paris, 27-30 October 2002, pp. 111-120; DOI 10.1145/571985.572001; reprinted in ACM Transactions on Graphics, July 2003) rebuilds a Fakespace BOOM3C by replacing its stereo goggles with a 15-inch 16:9 SGI LCD mounted on a tracked, counterbalanced armature with a 10-foot spherical range, giving a 1:1 mapping between real and virtual space with the model fixed at the base, plus an Elo touchscreen overlay, a podium microphone and speakers. The annotation application runs in Java/Java3D on a dual Pentium III 500 MHz machine under Windows 2000 with a GeForce2 and a VRML loader. The correction matters: this is not windows that move to stay near the user. It is a window-in-hand -- a flat-panel display on a tracked boom acting as a physical window into a fixed 3D world, with a 1:1 mapping between display pose and virtual viewpoint. The "stay near the user" mechanisms are the Tracking Menu and the Frisbee. [C36282]

It offers three gesture modes. Pen draws lines on 3D objects by continuous picking, offset slightly above the geometry. Flashlight is an invisible flashlight that places a spotlight at the fingertip, positioned 1/10 of the viewpoint-model distance above the model, with a small ball marking the exact point. Snapshots are flat double-sided 2D images floating in the 3D scene on which the user writes while the viewpoint is frozen, with an animated transition back on release, layering, and a titlebar that toggles visibility. A record button turns red while recording; viewpoint, voice and gesture are captured as separate timestamped streams and played back in a separate window while the main window stays live under user control. The evaluation is candid about the trade: annotation events were highly interspersed rather than batched (users segmented their work per defect -- locate, then flashlight, then speak and draw in parallel), voice was the predominant channel, and the paper records the limitations plainly -- the user cannot fly above the car to look straight down, the base blocks movement through the object's centre, and the armature's inertia gives the motion a Steadicam-like quality. A "herding effect" was also documented -- a group flocks behind the presenter -- and fixed by adding a duplicate display. [C36283]

**Takeaway:** The window-in-hand is the cleanest statement of the whole study: the world is fixed, the view is a function of where the window is, and return is repeatable -- return to the same physical point and you get the same view. A piece built on it should make return legible. [C36284]

## Tracking Menus: the tool that follows

Tracking Menus (Fitzmaurice, Khan, Pieke, Buxton, Kurtenbach; UIST 2003, Vancouver, 2-5 November 2003) is a widget built from a cluster of graphical buttons inside which the cursor moves freely; when the cursor hits the exterior edge, the menu moves to keep tracking it, so it behaves as a "multi-headed tool" with two or more tools attached to the cursor, by analogy with a jar lid pushed around by the tip of a pencil. Pen states are formalised: out-of-range (more than about 1.5 cm from the surface, State 0, menu visible but stationary), in-range (State 1, free movement inside), edge contact (State 1E, menu moves with the cursor), and pen-down (State 2, action invoked, menu typically hidden); a mouse has no State 0. The case study is a pan-and-zoom tracking menu in Alias SketchBook on a Tablet PC: an outer ring for pan (the frequent operation) and a centre for zoom, semi-transparent, hiding on pen-down and reappearing on pen-up, supporting "pawing" -- repeated ballistic drags in which any direction eventually hits the edge, so the outer region is always selectable, unlike clutching. A deluxe version adds buttons (+ double zoom, - half, 1:1, and a * marking menu); a concentric-ring layout was rejected because its symmetry was confusing and inner rings lacked the easy-select property. The paper also defines a pushpin for temporary deactivation (the menu grays out and auto-releases on re-entry) and a lock, and explores variations: the tracking boundary need not match the visual boundary (elliptical versus circular, holes, interior walls), drag algorithms (stick-at-edge versus elastic/string, gravity/weight), permeable zones entered by dwelling about 0.5 s (for reset/undo/redo), and an x-ray dotted outline where the menu slips under the main tool palette. It was tested in three rounds (6 external users, then 6 art-college students, no training); all completed the tasks within minutes, two thirds preferred the combined pan-and-zoom menu, two thirds felt faster, and measured timings matched or exceeded those perceptions. The simple version shipped in SketchBook 1.0; the deluxe version was deferred by schedule. (Page-range note: leg 2 cites pp. 71-79, leg 3's full-PDF read cites pp. 71-80; the range is left uncited here pending a check against the ACM record.) [C36285]

**Takeaway:** The tracking menu is the richest mechanism in the study because it is a complete little machine: four states, an edge rule, a pushpin, a lock, a boundary that can lie about its shape, and a gesture (pawing) that cannot miss. Every prompt below that touches it uses the state machine, not the look. [C36286]

## Frisbee, Spotlight, and the room as a play

A Remote Control Interface for Large Displays (Khan, Fitzmaurice, Almeida, Burtnyk, Kurtenbach; UIST 2004, Santa Fe, 24-27 October 2004, pp. 127-136; DOI 10.1145/1029632.1029655) is the "Frisbee": a portal pairing a local "telescope" with a remote "target", where the remote content is drawn inside the telescope and input made in the telescope is translated to the remote data. Telescope controls (a blue ring) position and size the telescope; target controls (a pink ring) position and size the target; dragging the target pans, resizing the target zooms, dragging the telescope moves the window without changing its content, and a Link icon pans both together so the 1:1 coupling feels like a hole in the display. Transfer uses four gaps, or channels, in the telescope's position ring: dragging an object through a channel warps it between local and remote space, while crossing anywhere else keeps it local, under a state machine of LOUT/LIN/RIN/ROUT transitions; setup is two pen taps. In the experiment, 8 subjects moved blocks between two 50-inch plasma displays at five separations (4.5 to 20.5 ft); Frisbee's cost stayed flat with distance while walking cost rose linearly, crossing over at 11.2 ft for novices and near 4.5 ft for experts. The paper's design principles are to minimise physical travel, support multiple concurrent users, minimise visual disruption, maintain visual persistence of space, and stay application-independent. The year correction matters: this is UIST 2004, not 2003 -- the CHI 2006 overview miscites it as 2003; the UIST 2004 programme and OpenAlex both give 2004. The full text was paywalled this run; the mechanism above is from the abstract as reconstructed from the OpenAlex abstract, labelled as such, and the leg-2 read end to end of the author PDF is the source for the experiment figures. [C36287]

Spotlight: Directing Users' Attention on Large Displays (Khan, Matejka, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach; CHI 2005, Portland, 2-7 April 2005, pp. 791-798; DOI 10.1145/1054972.1055082) darkens the display exterior by about 75% except for a large circular region around the cursor; while the cursor moves, the exterior brightens to about 50%, then slowly re-darkens to maximum after a dwell. The spotlight behaves like a tracking menu (the cursor moves freely inside and pushes the region at the edge), its diameter is resized with the mouse thumbwheel, and an optional "beam" or searchlight mode draws a line from the display centre to the spotlight to help re-acquire it. Figure 12 catalogues variations: multi-light, circular, curtain, cone-light, searchlight, shape-light, multiple searchlights and cone-lights, and an elliptical shape-searchlight. Experiment 1 used 12 subjects on a six-panel semi-circular wall 72 ft wide by 9 ft tall at 7680 x 1024, at fields of view of 108, 180 and 240 degrees: the spotlight beat the cursor by a factor of 1.5 at the narrow field of view and 3.4 at the wide one. Experiment 2, on a 21-inch monitor, found the spotlight beating the cursor by a factor of 2.3, and all subjects preferred the spotlight or searchlight over the cursor on both displays. The paper also records that dimming changed the room lighting to a calm, theatre-like atmosphere, that images inside the spotlight appeared sharper and better in contrast, and that light scattering onto tables, chairs and people gave an ambient cue to the spotlight's location. [C36288]

Cinematic Meeting Facilities Using Large Displays (Fitzmaurice, Khan, Kurtenbach, Binks; IEEE Computer Graphics & Applications, July/August 2005, 25(4), pp. 17-21) describes the Alias Visualization Studio (VizStudio) at the Toronto headquarters, a room of about 40 x 80 ft built as an eight-sided enclosure ("octagon") whose walls are each 12 x 9 ft except the front and back walls at about 20 x 9 ft; three walls are fixed and five rotate and slide on a ceiling track, and the common half-octagon configuration uses five walls and six projectors for a 64 x 9 ft display at 7680 x 1024. Curved walls funnel visitors toward a red semitransparent door for arrival and departure drama; the reception area has a water wall, food and beverages, and projectors showing the visitor's logo. The paper defines five use scenarios (customer executive briefing, design activity, seminar workshop, strategic planning, public event) across four distinct meeting spaces, and reports observations after about a year of use: groups reserve the whole space for privacy; formal activity gathers near the front display and informal activity at the back; the half-octagon works best while the closed octagon is rarely used; "digital beams" -- the front-projection light paths -- blind people walking through them; presenters must produce widescreen content because 4:3 PowerPoint leaves empty space; desktop content magnified about 10x shows scale effects; auxiliary material goes on the side walls; translated presentations are shown simultaneously; and the wide landscape dominates attention flow so the presenter competes with the content. The Spotlight technique is used in the room; a dedicated staff member is needed for smooth operation; and the room-state problem is unsolved -- whether the room is unoccupied, in setup, in a dry run, in debrief or on a break is not legible from outside. The design basis is named as Pine and Gilmore's The Experience Economy and Brenda Laurel's Computers as Theatre, with natural materials (slate, wood, water), daylight and acoustics. [C36289]

**Takeaway:** The room paper is the only one in the study with no executable rule -- it is a sequencing premise (arrival, work, departure) and a staging vocabulary. It is recorded honestly as a premise, not a mechanism, and no prompt is built on it alone. [C36290]

## Mechanisms

The flagged doubt is tested here and the flag does not stand in the way it stood for Roberts (#826): Khan's papers do state rules, procedures, and parameters -- a state machine, a 1:1 pose mapping, a distance formula, a portal mapping. What follows is therefore his procedure made executable, not a formalisation from pictures. Labels: OBSERVED means the rule is stated in the named paper; FORMALISATION means this study's construction on the work, labelled where it appears; no formalisation is presented as the authors' own claim. Every mechanism separates Subject, Visual principle, Computational mechanism, and Artistic opportunity. [C36291]

## M1 -- The window-in-hand: viewpoint = display pose, 1:1 (Boom Chameleon, UIST 2002)

Subject: a 15-inch LCD on a tracked mechanical boom acting as a physical window into a 3D world, used for automotive design review; the virtual car stays fixed in physical space and the user walks around it. Visual principle: occlusion by the frame; parallax. The frame crops a world that stays put. Proximity is scale: moving closer to the centre zooms to a close-up; moving to the side shows the side. Return is repeat: to return to a view, return to the same physical point in space. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): virtual camera pose = physical display pose, continuously, 1:1. World coordinates are fixed; the view is the projection of the fixed world through the display rectangle. There are no zoom/pan/tumble widgets -- all navigation is the single pose mapping. The paper is explicit that this replaces discrete viewpoint functions with "simple physical manipulation of the display". Formalisation for a 2D piece: the "display pose" becomes viewport position over a world larger than the screen; the 1:1 mapping becomes "window position alone determines the view; the world never moves". Artistic opportunity: the viewer's position becomes the camera, so navigation is bodily rather than widget-driven, and the determinism (same place = same view) makes the world feel solid and memorable instead of slippery. [C36292]

**Takeaway:** The view is a function of where the window is. Build the window, not the zoom control. [C36293]

## M2 -- The distance-scaled light (Boom Chameleon flashlight mode)

Subject: a fingertip "flashlight": an invisible light whose visible effect is a pool of light on the model, plus a small ball floating on the model marking the exact pointed location. Visual principle: scale by distance; focus and precision as two readings of one gesture -- the broad pool says "around here", the ball says "exactly here". Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the virtual spotlight is placed above the model at one-tenth of the distance between the viewpoint and the model. The lit area therefore grows when the viewer is far away and shrinks when close, while the ball sits exactly on the model surface at the pointed location and stays correctly aligned when the model is viewed from other angles (the paper notes the annotator's original location can be inferred from the shape of the spotlight). Artistic opportunity: one gesture reads at two scales at once, and the trade-off between them is continuous and physical -- far away you get the region, close up you get the point. [C36294]

**Takeaway:** Distance is the parameter. One gesture, two readings, and the ratio between them is the content. [C36295]

## M3 -- Frozen views that stack (Boom Chameleon snapshots)

Subject: snapshots -- frozen 2D views placed as double-sided planes floating in the 3D scene, layered, hideable by clicking a titlebar. Visual principle: layering and occlusion; time rendered as depth. The paper reports a natural layering in which the earliest snapshots sit closest to the centre of the scene and new ones further away. A hidden snapshot leaves only its titlebar, whose position, size and orientation still show the hidden annotation's scope. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): on capture, the current frame is taken as a JPEG and used as a texture on both sides of a flat polygonal plane placed in front of the camera at the camera pose; each snapshot is one scene-graph node, so hiding is detaching the node and showing is reattaching it. Capturing freezes the viewpoint, which lets the pen draw in screen space while the system keeps recording voice. On release, a smooth animated transition eases the live view from the frozen pose back to the current display pose, so the context change is never abrupt. Artistic opportunity: the piece accumulates its own past as a visible stack; time becomes a spatial dimension the viewer can orbit, and the hidden planes leave traces (titlebars) rather than vanishing. [C36296]

**Takeaway:** Time becomes depth when the past is stacked instead of passing. Build the stack, not the timeline. [C36297]

## M4 -- Screen-space vs object-space marks; ink drift (Boom Chameleon)

Subject: pen marks drawn directly on 3D objects versus marks drawn on a frozen snapshot. Visual principle: perspective distortion as truth-condition. A perfect circle drawn on the ground plane from one viewpoint reads as a long thin oval from another; a mark's legibility depends on where you stand. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): a mark is either anchored to scene geometry (continuous picking places it slightly offset above the surface; it follows the geometry and deforms with viewpoint change) or anchored to the screen plane (it stays exactly as drawn, viewpoint-independent). If the viewpoint moves while drawing on an object, the pen tip drifts relative to the scene -- the paper names this "ink drift" -- and the frozen snapshot exists precisely to remove it. Drawing on an object also risks a category error the paper calls out: the drawer may assume screen-space while the mark is in object-space. Artistic opportunity: anamorphosis -- a drawing that only resolves from one viewpoint, so the piece is the search for the viewpoint where the marks become true. The distortion is not a bug to hide; it is the content. [C36298]

**Takeaway:** A drawing that is only a drawing from one place. The search for the place is the piece. [C36299]

## M5 -- Edge-push tracking (Tracking Menus, UIST 2003)

Subject: a cluster of graphical buttons that follows the cursor; the paper's physical analogy is a jar lid pushed around by the tip of a pencil -- pushed from the side, without pressing down. Visual principle: containment and pursuit. The frame follows the point; the boundary is something you push against, not something you cross. The tool is always under you and close at hand. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): an explicit state machine. State 0, out of range: the menu is visible but stationary. State 1, in range: the cursor moves freely inside the menu and can highlight items. State 1E, edge contact: the menu translates with the cursor, so the cursor stays inside. State 2, touching: the menu is hidden during the press and reappears on release. On edge contact the menu moves by the cursor delta. A pushpin variant pins the menu (it grays out, the cursor may leave); re-entering the menu auto-releases the pin. A lock variant pins it until explicitly released. Artistic opportunity: a shape you herd rather than drag -- the pointer never grabs it, only pushes it, so the shape can never be lost or left behind. The interaction is a relationship (pursuit), not a command. [C36300]

**Takeaway:** Push, never grab. A shape that follows because you push its edge can never be left behind. [C36301]

## M6 -- Pawing: selection by direction, not position (Tracking Menus)

Subject: repeated ballistic panning drags ("pawing") over a large image, and the outer ring of the pan-and-zoom tracking menu. Visual principle: radial symmetry; the gesture's direction, not its landing point, is the selector. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the outer region is selected by any movement large enough to hit the edge, in any direction. It cannot be missed -- movement in any direction eventually hits the edge -- and it cannot be overshot, because the menu moves on contact. Selection is therefore by crossing direction rather than by absolute position. The paper distinguishes pawing from clutching (repositioning the device without moving the cursor); pawing needs no clutching. The same direction logic appears in the split-exterior-region design: two tools assigned to the left and right halves of the outer region, selected by quick ballistic movements left or right followed by a press. Artistic opportunity: a control that cannot be missed. Fling in a direction and the piece answers; the imprecision of the hand becomes a feature, not an error to correct. [C36302]

**Takeaway:** Direction is the selector. A fling that cannot miss turns the hand's imprecision into the interface. [C36303]

## M7 -- The multi-headed tool (Tracking Menus; PieCursor, CHI 2008)

Subject: the cursor carrying two or more tools at once. The Tracking Menus paper frames it explicitly: a tool in a GUI is conventionally a single function attached to the cursor; a tracking menu is "a multi-headed tool". PieCursor is the miniaturised form: a radial cluster of command wedges shrunk to roughly cursor size (~32 x 32 px). Visual principle: the cursor as a cluster, not a point; several behaviours sharing one location. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): N tools are attached to one pointer; the active tool is determined by which sub-region of the cluster the pointer occupies (radial wedge in the PieCursor form). Pointing and command selection happen in the same action -- the PieCursor "merges the normal cursor function of pointing with command selection into a single action". Measured result (PieCursor paper): for small clusters of 4 and 8 command wedges, the PieCursor outperformed a toolbar by 20.8% for coarse pointing. Artistic opportunity: a pointer that is itself a small machine -- the tool you get depends on where inside the tool you are, so the act of choosing and the act of using are one gesture. [C36304]

**Takeaway:** Choosing and using are one gesture when the pointer is a machine, not a point. [C36305]

## M8 -- Permeable zones: dwell as a gate (Tracking Menus)

Subject: the 3D camera-control tracking menu, where the most-used control (tumbling) sits in the large outer region and reset/undo/redo sit in "permeable zones". Visual principle: threshold and hesitation -- a boundary that yields only to waiting. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): the user must dwell over the zone border with the cursor for about half a second before entry is allowed; otherwise the border deflects. Accessibility is deliberately reduced for rarely used functions. Artistic opportunity: slowness as a gate -- the piece can distinguish a passing touch from a staying touch. Rejected as a standalone prompt -- a gate rule, not a piece; see R3. Used as a parameter inside KHA6. [C36306]

**Takeaway:** A gate is a parameter, not a piece. Dwell belongs inside a system, never as the system. [C36307]

## M9 -- The tracking boundary is not the visual boundary (Tracking Menus)

Subject: a circular graphical tracking menu whose tracking border is elliptical; holes and interior walls in tracking menus. Visual principle: the felt shape differs from the seen shape; hidden geometry. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): two shapes are maintained: a visible shape and an invisible interaction boundary. The tracking boundary need not map to the visual boundary -- it can be larger or smaller, a different shape, non-contiguous, contain holes, or have interior walls that bias the cursor into a sub-region and let it remain there more easily. Behaviour follows the invisible shape, always. Artistic opportunity: a field that feels different from how it looks. You discover the true boundary by pushing against it; the visible circles are decoys and the real geometry is learned by touch. [C36308]

**Takeaway:** The visible form can be a decoy. What you feel by pushing is the real geometry. [C36309]

## M10 -- The Frisbee portal: telescope + target (UIST 2004)

Subject: a widget for reaching parts of a large display that are difficult or impossible to access directly. A "frisbee" is a portal to another part of the display. Visual principle: portal and scale shift; action at a distance. The small window is a live, editable copy of the far region. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED, from the abstract -- full text paywalled): a frisbee consists of a local "telescope" and a remote "target". The data surrounded by the target is drawn in the telescope, and interactions performed within the telescope are applied to the remote data. The paper's experiment reports a frisbee is preferred over walking back and forth between local and remote spaces at a distance of 4.5 feet. Artistic opportunity: two places at once -- what you do here happens there. The mapping between the two regions is the piece, not either region alone. [C36310]

**Takeaway:** Action and effect can live in different places. The mapping between them is the piece. [C36311]

## M11 -- The spotlight: darken all but a circle (CHI 2005)

Subject: a circular region of full brightness on a wall-sized display; everything else darkened. Visual principle: attention by darkening; figure/ground reversal. The lit circle is the figure and the dark field is the ground; the circle is the only place anything can be seen clearly. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED, from the abstract and technique description -- full text not read end to end): the display is darkened by about 75% except for a large circular region around the current cursor position. The spotlight follows the cursor and behaves like a tracking menu: the cursor moves freely inside the region, and when it reaches the edge it pushes the region along, so pointing and repositioning are one tool. The diameter is resizable (thumbwheel in the paper). Measured result: the spotlight outperformed the standard cursor by a factor of 3.4 on a wall-sized display, and users preferred it. Artistic opportunity: a field that is mostly hidden, revealed only through a moving circle -- and the hidden parts keep changing while you cannot see them, so every return to a place shows something new. [C36312]

**Takeaway:** Hide the field and reveal it through one moving circle. What changes in the dark is the piece's memory. [C36313]

## M12 -- Camera surfaces and authored transitions (StyleCam, UIST 2002)

Subject: a 3D viewing experience where the camera is constrained to surfaces, with animated clips between them; the authored example is a product-style presentation of a 3D model. Visual principle: constrained viewpoint; composed views ("money-shot" viewpoints); pacing as a visual quality -- how fast the view moves is part of the style. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED, from the abstract and opening sections -- full text not read end to end): the camera position is constrained to a 2D manifold (a "camera surface") in 3D space; dragging moves the camera across the surface. Control-display gain is author-set: it fixes how fast dragging moves the camera, which sets the pacing. Animation clips carry the camera between surfaces to the next surface's money-shot viewpoint. One unified gesture moves seamlessly between spatial control of the camera and temporal control of the animated transitions, and the user's focus of attention is kept on the content, not on interface widgets. In the evaluation, 7 participants used it with minimal instruction in a car-model advertisement scenario; none realised they had switched between spatial and temporal control, uninterruptable animations annoyed them when the content seemed unrelated, and every participant with 3D experience wanted full camera control at least some of the time. Artistic opportunity: a journey through composed views. The viewer drives, but the piece composes -- the camera never shows a bad angle, and the pace of looking is part of the work. [C36314]

**Takeaway:** Constraint can be generous: a camera that never shows a bad angle, and a pace that is part of the style. [C36315]

## M13 -- Constant capture: recording is always on (Boom Chameleon)

Subject: all annotation streams -- viewpoint, voice, gesture -- recorded continuously, with no mode switching and no explicit action to start recording. Visual principle: none of its own. The visible consequence is continuity: nothing is lost between modes, and the record is richer because the user never stops to operate the recorder. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED): capture is always on; the three streams are timestamped and stored in parallel; pressing Record only marks the session (the button turns red) and pressing it again ends it; playback replays the streams synchronised in a separate window while the live view continues. The design philosophy is stated explicitly: capture many types of annotation data "without requiring explicit action for each data type" so the annotations are "richer and more fluid" than a modal approach. Artistic opportunity: the piece remembers everything the viewer did -- its memory is the interaction. Rejected as a prompt: ISH6 already occupies the recorded-interaction piece; see R1. [C36316]

**Takeaway:** Recording is a memory, not a feature. When another piece already owns the memory, record the mechanism and move on. [C36317]

Visual principle: staging and sequencing; attention directed the way a scene is directed; the interplay of display technology, interior design, and social dynamics. Computational mechanism (OBSERVED as a design approach; no executable rule): none stated. The residue is a sequencing premise -- the experience proceeds in acts in a fixed order -- which is a state machine with one transition per act, not a mechanism. No variables, constraints, or state transitions capable of producing an original artwork are given. Artistic opportunity: a piece in acts, each act with different rules. Rejected as a standalone prompt; see R4. The act structure may frame a piece's development, but it cannot be the piece. [C36318]

**Takeaway:** A premise is not a mechanism. Acts can frame a piece; they cannot be the piece. [C36319]

## Aesthetic logic

Khan's logic is a laboratory logic: one room, one display, one rule at a time, each tested against people doing real work. The vocabulary never grows -- window, menu, light, lens, camera -- and the palette of the papers is the palette of the lab: dark rooms, bright screens, the red record button, the blue telescope ring and the pink target ring. What grows is the unit the rule applies to: from one display to one room to one building. The discipline is measurement: every paper reports a number (3.4x, 11.2 ft, 20.8%, 0.5 s), and the number is the argument. A piece built on Khan should feel like a tested instrument -- one rule, one gesture, one measurable behaviour -- and it should never add a second rule to decorate the first. The most Khan-like thing a generated piece can do is follow the hand by a rule it states plainly, and charge the honest price for it. [C36327]
