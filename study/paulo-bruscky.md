## The life: the man who mailed his own body

Paulo Roberto Barbosa Bruscky was born 21 March 1949 in Recife, Pernambuco, the third child in a family of seven siblings (C11100, C11101). His father was an artist from Belarus who was touring Brazil when he met Bruscky's mother at the Teatro Santa Isabel in Recife; BOMB gives the same story as a photographer whose family emigrated from Belarus in the 1930s, and his mother once ran for city council (C11101). Carry Belarus, not "Russian": Newcity calls the father "a Russian photographer", the outlier against Portuguese Wikipedia and BOMB (C11135). He grew interested in art working in his father's photography studio, studied music at the conservatory, and studied journalism, spray-painting anti-regime graffiti on walls during the course (C11102). He was arrested in 1968 and dismissed from his job at the Fundacao Joaquim Nabuco (C11103); his earliest exhibitions were held in 1966-67 (C11104). He is LIVING as of 17 November 2026: the Lisbon anthological show "BRUSCKY em BRUSQUE" is scheduled 23 May - 31 August 2026, and his most recent solos were "Banco de ideias" (Nara Roesler Sao Paulo, 27 May - 29 July 2023) and "Atitude Politica" (IAC Sao Paulo, from 3 June 2023); no obituary was found (C11122, C11123). He would be 77 in 2026.

The career runs in six phases, and the hinge is the machine: the work leaves the studio, enters the cheapest communication system available, and the system answers by copying it.

(1) Formation, 1949-1966. Recife, family, conservatory, journalism, first arrest 1968. The 1968 arrest is the first of the three imprisonments he describes in the BOMB interview: "The first time, in 1968 or '69, I was 18 or 19 years old. It was a short imprisonment since they couldn't fit anybody else in the cells at the DOPS [Department of Political and Social Order]; I was released on the same night" (C11110).

(2) Poetry/process, street actions, first Xerox, 1967-1973. He began his research in the 1960s with the poetry/process (poema/processo) movement, where he met Robert Rehfeldt, a member of Fluxus (C11105). His work "Poem Without Measure" dates to 1967 (C11151). He began his first experiments with the Xerox copier in 1970: "They were not actual distortions (the machine then did not have that capacity), I painted over the copies, worked each one differently; this was around 1973. Xerography is everything you can't obtain from other means of reproduction" (C11107). In 1971 he staged his own burial in "Arte Cemiterial": the police closed the gallery exhibition the next day, and he also scheduled a mass at the Catholic University (C11125). In 1972 he and Daniel Santiago launched a coffin marked "ARTE" on the Capibaribe River, an action called "Enterro Aquatico I"; the Fire Department retrieved the coffin after several minutes, and the action referenced the military regime throwing victims' corpses into rivers (C11125). In 1973 he re-inaugurated a bridge in central Recife by tying a ribbon across it during a red light and letting the crowd wait for an authority to cut it; it took about 15 minutes before someone broke through, and police arrived as he retrieved the ribbon as a souvenir (C11127). The bridge's 1733 construction date is the artist's own claim in BOMB and is not independently verified here. He was jailed a second time in 1973, for between 8 and 10 days (C11110). He entered mail art at the start of the 1970s: Nara Roesler says he engaged with the international Postal Art circuit in 1973 and pioneered it in Brazil; Itau Cultural says he became one of the first Brazilian artists to engage in the International Mail Art Movement in 1973 (C11106). In his own words: "After I got involved in arte correio (mail art) at the beginning of the '70s, I became aware of what was going on in the world thanks to my correspondence with other artists. I felt that I didn't need to leave even more strongly than before" (C11106).

(3) Dictatorship years, 1974-1979. In 1974 he launched the Manifesto Nadaista (Nothing-ism manifesto) (C11108). Newcity's account: he was arrested in Recife by the military police in 1974 when he presented the Nadaista manifesto, was threatened by the army on release with "causing an accident" if he did anything on the streets again, spent six months in fear with two men tailing him morning and night, and then organized a Nadaista exhibition at the Nega Fulo gallery at which there were no artworks -- he stood on a bench, read the manifesto aloud, and said he would go back to making art and that if he died it would not be an accident (C11108). Itau says he organized two international mail art exhibitions in Recife in 1975 and 1976, the second censored and closed down by the military (C11109). Gilbert gives the fullest account of the 1976 closure: the International Exhibition of Mail Art in Recife in 1976 was to be held in the Central Post Office of Recife, was closed before the opening could take place, and the organizers (Bruscky and Daniel Santiago) were imprisoned in solitary confinement for three days; Santiago recalled the Federal Police representative closing the show because some mailed works were "an affront to common decency" and others criticised the government, and that both men were arrested the next day, held Saturday, Sunday and Monday in separate cells, and released on Tuesday (C11109). CORRECTION TO #653, carried here: the #653 study's version ("1975 Recife mail-art exhibition with Daniel Santiago was closed immediately after opening and both artists were imprisoned for three days") compresses the 1975/1976 pair into 1975 -- the closure belongs to 1976 per Itau and Gilbert (C11134). Whether a 1975 show opened normally is attested only by Itau. His third imprisonment came in 1976: "The other two times I was tortured at the army barracks. I was picked up, taken to the Federal Police, and had my house surrounded... 1973 and 1976... The second time I spent two weeks with the federal police. They blindfolded me and transported me somewhere -- to this day, I don't know where -- just to scare me" (C11110). CARRY BOTH DURATIONS, DO NOT MERGE THEM: the 1976 mail-show arrest per Gilbert/Santiago lasted three days, while Bruscky in BOMB describes the 1976 detention as two weeks with the Federal Police; these may be two different 1976 detentions, or two accounts of one detention that disagree on duration (C11110). BOMB also records that his name was later found on a list of people to be killed, that the military invaded his house while he was in hiding, and that he turned himself in at the army barracks with a witness and photographers present so the fate of Vladimir Herzog would not happen to him (C11110). Through the same years he built the classified-ad work with Santiago: poetic and inventive advertisements published from 1977 in newspapers in Recife and other Brazilian cities, including an ad with a hose to "put out" words and an "Auroral Composition" proposing a colourful artificial tropical aurora at 100 km altitude; the newspapers eventually refused to keep publishing them (C11126). His first classified ad was in 1973 or 1974 in the Jornal do Brasil, per the SciELO article (C11126, search-only). He also made the EEG/brain works in this period: using a neurologist friend's office once a week, he made drawings directly from his brain via electroencephalogram, making facial expressions to shape the traces; the series "O meu cerebro desenha assim" (My brain draws like that) dates to 1976 (C11116). His 1976 work "Sentimentos: um poema feito com o coracao" (Feelings: a poem from the heart) recorded his heartbeat on an ECG machine, re-enacted in 2017 with a nurse and a hospital stretcher (C11116).

(4) Xerox, fax, film, 1977-1982. The first Xeroperformance happened in 1977, when Bruscky invited artists and friends to a copy centre in central Recife; 1980 became the key year, when he carried out and exhibited the series at a solo show at the Museu do Estado de Recife and taught the first Xerox art course in Brazil at the Universidade Catolica de Recife, developing his first xerofilms, "Xeroperformance" and "LMNUWZFogo!" (C11111). In the Xeroperformances he pressed parts of his body against the photocopier glass to create copies (body doubles) of himself, then sent the copies through the mail; the 1980 performance generated 1,350 photocopies filmed in stop motion as an experimental film (C11111). He made three Xerofilms in total, from thousands of Xerox copies produced in performances; for the second he set fire to a photocopier, which documented its own destruction in 25 seconds (C11111, search-only via CORE article). Between 1979 and 1982 he made 30 artist's films and video artworks, and from 1983 onward video installations (C11113). The first artistic fax transmission in Brazil was executed between Bruscky in Recife and Roberto Sandoval in Sao Paulo on 31 October 1980 (C11112). In 2000 he coined the expression "E-mail art"; "Mail art incorporates all the technology, including Fax art, the first example of Mail art in real time" (C11130). He calls mail art "the first step to the Internet" and says: "All of us, Mail artists of the world, have worked with the net and con/science since the early 1970s. For us all, the Internet is a natural outcome" (C11131). His day job ran in parallel the whole time: he worked his whole life in the health sector, starting in hospital administration, working for several years at the Hospital Agamenon Magalhaes in Recife, and becoming Director of Human Resources for Pernambuco's Ministry of Health; he is now retired (C11115). The hospital's medical equipment -- X-rays, encephalograms, electrocardiograms -- became his artistic material, and he used the hospital's equipment to trace his own brain waves (C11116).

(5) Guggenheim year and international recognition, 1981-2004. He won a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1981, which allowed him to live in New York for a year (C11114); that same year he took part in the 16th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo with a special room devoted to mail art (C11114). Carry the Guggenheim as award 1981, year abroad 1982: Itau, Portuguese Wikipedia and the Newcity interview all give the fellowship as 1981; a Nara Roesler press release calls him "Recipient of the Guggenheim Fellowship in 1982"; Bruscky himself says "In 1982, when I moved to New York thanks to a grant of the Guggenheim Foundation, I did a lot of research" and BOMB's interviewer places the Netherlands stay in 1982 (C11133). Do not state either year without the other. In Amsterdam he visited the bookshop of Ulises Carrion, "one of the pioneers of the artist's book", whom he had met through mail art and had invited in 1978 to perform and lecture at the Winter Festival at the Universidade Catolica de Pernambuco; Carrion lent him his bicycle for two months (C11114). He also met Robert Rehfeldt face to face for the first time in 1982, on an eventful trip to East Berlin, after corresponding since about 1975 (C11118). In New York he kept contact with Fluxus artists Dick Higgins, Alison Knowles, Felipe Ehrenberg and Ken Friedman, with whom he made "Performance for a Dog" (Performance para um Cachorro), among others (C11114). He featured in the 16th, 20th, 26th and 29th editions of the Sao Paulo Biennial (1981, 1989, 2004, 2010) and the 10th Havana Biennial, Cuba (2009) (C11119). In 2004 his studio was moved in its entirety from Recife to Sao Paulo and reconstructed as one of the special rooms of the 26th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo (C11120). He coined the term "communication art" for his own practice (C11139). He lists the copy maker as a co-author in his catalogues: "I study equipment to see how I can subvert it, pluck it from what it's meant to do -- I mean, make it our ally, right?" (C11140).

(6) Retrospectives and late career, 2013-2026. "Art Is Our Last Hope", curated by Antonio Sergio Bessa, showed at the Bronx Museum, New York, in 2013-2014, with 140 works made between 1971 and 2011 (C11121). A solo show followed at the Museu de Arte Moderna de Sao Paulo (MAM-SP) in 2014, and "Paulo Bruscky: Artist Books and Films, 1970-2013", curated by Clara Kim, at The Mistake Room, Los Angeles, and Another Space, New York, in 2015 (C11121). In May 2017 he re-enacted three 1970s/1980s performances in the official programme of the 57th Venice Biennale ("Viva Arte Viva", curated by Christine Macel), and a selection of his works was on view at the Centre Georges Pompidou, Paris, 18 October 2017 - 19 April 2018 (C11119, C11137). A large retrospective curated by Rodrigo Moura was presented by CAIXA Cultural in Recife earlier in 2017, described by Newcity as his largest retrospective to that date (C11137). In 2018 came "Paulo Bruscky. Eteceterate" at Fundacion Luis Seoane, A Coruna, Spain (C11121). In 2023 Nara Roesler Sao Paulo presented "Banco de ideias" (Index of Ideas), his fourth solo show at the gallery, curated by Jacopo Crivelli Visconti, 27 May - 29 July 2023, alongside "Atitude Politica" at the Instituto de Arte Contemporanea (IAC), Sao Paulo, opening 3 June 2023, presenting a selection from his archive: thousands of items over five decades, including correspondence from approximately 1,000 artists from 52 countries (C11122). A Lisbon anthological exhibition, "BRUSCKY em BRUSQUE", curated by Natxo Checa at Galeria Ze dos Bois, is scheduled 23 May - 31 August 2026 (C11123). A Nara Roesler news item also announces "Transliquidificacao Poetica" at the Museu de Arte Contemporanea da Bahia (MAC_BAHIA), opening 4 July -- the year was not stated in the text as read, so it is not dated here (C11149).

In his own words. On Recife: "My relationship to Recife has been quite visceral." (C11141) On staying: "For a year I lived in New York and Amsterdam on a Guggenheim Fellowship but aside from that, I never had any desire to move to Rio or Sao Paulo. After I got involved in arte correio (mail art) at the beginning of the '70s, I became aware of what was going on in the world thanks to my correspondence with other artists." (C11141) On life and art: "For me this is important because I don't separate my life and my art. It's been a long time since I knew which was which." (C11141) On art as seeing: "For me, art is a form of seeing and not of doing. It might seem utopian, but the day will arrive when the artist will no longer be necessary. The artist makes things only because people don't know how to see for themselves." (C11141) On the interrogator: "If you do it, no, but if I do it, yes, it is art. That's the big difference between you and me." (C11141) On mail art's reach: "We weren't very worried about preservation. The works were made to be manipulated, to be passed from hand to hand. The big conquest of the movement was that it broke out all over the world. Dadaism, Futurism, and all those other movements -- even Pop art -- existed merely in the context of a few cities or a few countries, but mail art was global." (C11141) On mail art and amnesty: "News traveled through the mail. If there was an exhibition of prints by exiled Chilean artists, for instance, the amnesty committees would send thousands of letters about it to various people, including the authorities. This was a type of accusation and a way to keep the artists alive, because the military knew that the whole world was aware of what was going on." (C11141) On mail art and nationality (Newcity Brazil interview, 2017): "Mail Art is the sole art movement without a nationality. Its oeuvre is based on the exchange of concepts/information. Today art is communication, art in transit in every sense." (C11142) On Xerox (Newcity Brazil interview, 2017): "I began my first experiments with the Xerox Copier in 1970. They were not actual distortions (the machine then did not have that capacity), I painted over the copies, worked each one differently; this was around 1973. Xerography is everything you can't obtain from other means of reproduction. ... I never consider a xerographic piece finished." (C11142) On machines as co-authors (Newcity Brazil interview, 2017): "In working with new technologies, the artist has to dissect the machine, the same way as a medicine student dissects a corpse, in order to detract its function and instill some soul into it so the machine can become a co-author and work along the artist with chance and daring. I always thank the machines for their creative partnerships." (C11142) On art as last hope (Newcity Brazil interview, 2017): "Art is the Last Hope." / "Art remains the Last Hope." (C11142) On advice to a young artist (Newcity Brazil interview, 2017): "Information, research, work. Don't get anxious. Don't be dishonest to yourself." (C11142) On subverting equipment (Nara Roesler 2017 exhibition page, quoted from the artist): "I study equipment to see how I can subvert it, pluck it from what it's meant to do -- I mean, make it our ally, right?" (C11140)

## Mechanisms

M1 -- The body pressed on the glass (Xeroperformance). The artist presses body parts against a photocopier's glass and the machine returns a flat body double; the copies are then mailed, so the body travels as paper. Works: W13, W10. Watchable behaviour: a figure flattening, smearing and re-posing against a glowing glass plate while copies accumulate and peel away; the double is then shaken by the hand, mailed, and answered by another double. Takeaway: the copier is not a reproduction device, it is a second body -- the flat, smeared double is the portrait, and mailing it is the performance. (C11111, C11151)

M2 -- The post as the first internet (mail art as witness network). A work mailed to dozens of countries becomes a distributed alarm: when the police ban a show, the letter reporting the ban is itself the evidence that the mail got through. Works: W08, W15, the 2 March 1977 letter to Padin. Watchable behaviour: envelopes, stamps and postcards arriving from everywhere, piling into a wall that takes every card, while one censored show's notice keeps circulating; the ban letter is read aloud and re-mailed onward. Takeaway: the network is the witness -- the mail does not report the event, the mail IS the event, and a banned show that is reported by post has already escaped the police. (C11202, C11141)

M3 -- The classified ad as artwork (declassified art). Poetic, impossible proposals are bought as classified ads in real newspapers -- a hose to "put out" words, an artificial aurora at 100 km, a machine to film dreams -- until the papers refuse to print them. Works: the classified series, about 50 ads, 1970s onward. Watchable behaviour: a newspaper page where ordinary ads are quietly replaced by impossible commissions, one after another; each ad is typeset exactly like a real one, and the reader only notices when the proposal cannot be bought. Takeaway: the classified page is a gallery with no door -- anyone who can pay for the space can exhibit in it, and the refusal to print is the review. (C11126, C11153)

M4 -- The body mailed as its own image (Radium Self-Portrait). X-rays of the artist's skull and body are stuck to envelopes and posted; the interior of the body becomes the outside of the letter. Works: W09. Watchable behaviour: glowing bone-images sliding out of envelopes and reassembling into a skeleton that waves back; the letter is opened and the sender is inside it, in X-ray. Takeaway: the envelope usually hides its contents -- here the contents are the sender, turned inside out, and the postman carries a body that cannot be arrested. (C11152)

M5 -- The brain drawing itself (encephalogram drawings). A hospital EEG machine traces the artist's brain waves while he makes faces to bend the lines; the drawing is made without hands. Works: W14. Watchable behaviour: a live trace that spikes, loops and flattens as an unseen face grimaces, the line itself becoming the portrait; the hand never touches the paper. Takeaway: the machine is the draftsman and the face is the stylus -- the portrait is a recording of an inner event, not a likeness of an outer one. (C11116)

M6 -- The fake ceremony (Art/Stop and the staged burial). A ribbon is tied across a bridge during a red light and the crowd waits for an authority to cut it; a funeral is held for a living artist. Works: W04, W02. Watchable behaviour: a solemn civic ritual performed with full seriousness until an impatient pedestrian breaks the spell and traffic floods through; a coffin is carried in procession and the police close the gallery the next day. Takeaway: the ceremony works because everyone agrees to pretend -- the art is in the waiting, not the cutting, and the interruption is the only honest moment. (C11127, C11125)

M7 -- The tongue stamp (Linguistic Poem). Colour is stamped onto a blank canvas with the tongue, so chance and the body's own rhythm compose the picture. Works: W16. Watchable behaviour: a canvas filling with soft, irregular colour marks in no pattern a hand would choose, each mark arriving with a small, deliberate gesture; the mouth does the work the hand was hired for. Takeaway: the tongue is the least governable stamp -- it cannot draw a straight line, and that failure is the composition. (C11154, C11171)

M8 -- The archive as the work (the containers a life arrived in). The whole studio is moved and rebuilt as an exhibition room; sixty years of medical files are packed in a briefcase; a suitcase is collaged shut. Works: W18, W19, W20. Watchable behaviour: a room that is itself a packed suitcase -- drawers, folders and stamped envelopes opening one by one to release the life stored inside them; the medical file is read as a biography, the suitcase as a self-portrait. Takeaway: when the work has always travelled, the container is the only original left -- exhibit the suitcase, not what was in it. (C11120, C11163)

## Aesthetic logic

His logic is the logic of the copy shop, the post office and the hospital ward, not the studio. The materials are the cheapest communication systems that travel: banner, coffin, ribbon, telegram, postcard, photocopy, encephalogram, tongue, briefcase, suitcase. The palette, as reported in sources, is the palette of the machine and the mail: black toner on cheap paper, the blue of the postmark, the grey of the X-ray, the white of the hospital sheet, the red of the ribbon across the bridge. Nothing is precious; the work is designed to be pressed, copied, mailed, seized, re-performed forty years later, and packed into a suitcase. The through line is the machine as co-author: early the machine is the copier that flattens the body into a double (W13), then the EEG that draws the brain without hands (W14), then the fax that sends the work in real time (1980), then the archive that files the life itself (W19). The viewer is never a spectator: they wait at the ribbon, they carry the coffin, they open the envelope and find a skeleton inside, they read the classified ad and wonder whether to answer it. And the politics is never illustrated: it is built into the procedure -- a show that is banned before it opens, a letter that reports the ban, a body that is mailed because the man cannot travel, a tongue that stamps because the hand would obey. The work that began as a banner poem in Recife in 1967 ends as a briefcase of medical files from 1949 to 2010, and the distance between those two gestures is the whole career: the artist who mailed his body in pieces now exhibits the containers his life arrived in. (C11263)

## The network

The origin node is the mail-art correspondence itself: Bruscky names his contacts in the BOMB interview -- "I was in touch with people like Robert Rehfeldt, the German Fluxus artist, and Klaus Groh, Clemente Padin, Edgardo Vigo, Horacio Zabala, Jorge Caraballo, Robin Crozier, Ray Johnson, Shozo Shimamoto, John Armleder, Felipe Ehrenberg, and A. Ferro e Sarenco, among many others" (C11200). The Padin link is correspondence, not just a shared roster: in a letter to Clemente Padin dated 2 March 1977, Bruscky wrote that a mail-art exhibition planned for August 1976 "was prohibited and censored by the police, and even we (the organizers) were prisoners for three days" (C11202). The Argentine pole: Edgardo Antonio Vigo and Horacio Zabala in Argentina, Guy Schraenen in Belgium, Diego Barbosa in Venezuela, and Robin Crozier in England are listed by Gilbert as Bruscky's early-1970s correspondents (C11201); Jorge Caraballo is in the BOMB list (C11200). Graciela Gutierrez Marx appears alongside Bruscky in mail-art archive listings (Galeria Superficie, 1973 list; a 1982 exhibition list includes both, with Padin and Vigo) (C11214), but no direct Bruscky-Gutierrez Marx correspondence was located this run -- carry her as a shared-network contemporary, not as a documented correspondent. The East Berlin exchange: Robert Rehfeldt (1931-1993), the best-documented exchange -- first direct contact a postcard sent by Bruscky on 11 January 1975 reading "Art in Contact. Its Life in Art!", mirroring Rehfeldt's own slogan; the two exchanged works for about ten years from 1975 onward and met face to face only in 1982, when Bruscky travelled to East Berlin; Bruscky mailed empty envelopes with photocopied self-portraits on the inside flap daily to Rehfeldt over four days, 15-19 August 1975, each image progressively more faded; Rehfeldt replied with self-portraits, including a serial self-portrait stamped "ARTWORKERS UNITE" sent 3 November 1977; their mailings are now split between the two home archives, Bruscky's mailings to Rehfeldt in the home of Ruth Wolf-Rehfeldt in Berlin, Rehfeldt's mailings to Bruscky in Bruscky's Recife studio (C11201, C11217). The Amsterdam switchboard: Ulises Carrion (1941-1989) -- Bruscky met him through mail art, and in 1978 invited him to give a performance and a lecture at the Festival de Inverno (Winter Festival) at the Universidade Catolica de Pernambuco; Princeton University Library holds Carrion's address archive of over 1,300 index cards, and the finding aid lists "Paulo Bruscky" among the mailing recipients (and "Clemente Padin" among the P entries) (C11200, C11205). The New York pole: Ray Johnson (1927-1995) is named in Bruscky's BOMB list of people he was in touch with (C11200), and Osthoff states Bruscky's correspondents included Johnson, Ken Friedman (with whom he performed in New York) and Dick Higgins (C11203, search-only). Note the distinction: the Frieze "Tightrope Walker" piece also ties Bruscky to Johnson through the 2017 Frieze x Ray Johnson Estate project, in which artists including Bruscky responded to Johnson's New York Correspondence School -- that is a later homage, not evidence of correspondence, and must not be cited as such (C11204). The local collaborator: Daniel Santiago (b. 1939) of Recife -- co-organized the Primeira Exposicao Internacional de Arte Postal in Recife (December 1975, with Ypiranga Filho per Pianowski) and the 1976 International Exhibition of Mail Art in Recife, held in the Central Post Office, which was closed before it opened; both men were then imprisoned in solitary confinement for three days (Saturday, Sunday, Monday, released Tuesday), per Santiago's own testimony as quoted by Gilbert (C11201). Other names in the BOMB list -- Klaus Groh, Shozo Shimamoto, John Armleder, Felipe Ehrenberg, and A. Ferro e Sarenco -- are confirmed as named contacts only; no individual exchange with any of them was verified this run (C11200). Adjacent artists for the chain: Robert Rehfeldt, the East Berlin exchange; Daniel Santiago, the Recife collaborator; Ulises Carrion, the Amsterdam switchboard. (C11264)
