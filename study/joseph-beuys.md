## The life: the wound that became the material

Joseph Heinrich Beuys was born on 12 May 1921 in Krefeld, Germany, the son of the merchant Josef Jakob Beuys and Johanna Maria Margarete Beuys, nee Hulsermann, and soon after his birth the family moved to Kleve, an industrial town in the Lower Rhine region close to the Dutch border. In Kleve he attended the Katholische Volksschule and then the Staatliches Gymnasium Cleve, took piano and cello lessons, and pursued interests in the natural sciences and Nordic history and mythology; by his own account, at the Nazi book-burning in his school courtyard on 19 May 1933 he salvaged Carl Linnaeus's Systema Naturae from the flames, an account TheArtStory calls unsubstantiated. (C25860, leg 1 C25736, C25737; synthesis C25826)

He joined the Hitler Youth in 1936, the year membership became compulsory, and took part in the Nuremberg rally in September 1936 at the age of 15; he had first considered a medical career, then turned toward sculpture, possibly influenced by pictures of Wilhelm Lehmbruck's sculptures, and around 1939 worked part-time at a circus for about a year, posturing and taking care of animals, before graduating with his Abitur in the spring of 1941. In 1941 he volunteered for the Luftwaffe and trained as an aircraft radio operator under Heinz Sielmann in Posen, where both men attended lectures in biology and zoology; in 1942 he was stationed in the Crimea in combat bomber units, and from 1943 onward he was deployed as rear-gunner in a Ju 87 "Stuka" dive-bomber. (C25861, leg 1 C25738, C25739)

On 16 March 1944 his plane crashed on the Crimean Front close to Znamianka; the records cited by Wikipedia state that Beuys remained conscious, was recovered by a German search commando, that there were no Tatars in the village at the time, and that he spent 17 March to 7 April in a military hospital. Britannica, read end to end, dates the crash to 1943 and repeats the fat-and-felt rescue without qualification, while TheArtStory dates it to March 1944 -- the year is an unresolved variant. Beuys himself told a different story, quoted at length by Wikipedia from his 1979 account: that nomadic Tartars found him in the snow after the German search parties had given up, that he came round only after about twelve days, and that they covered his body in fat to help it regenerate warmth and wrapped it in felt as an insulator to keep warmth in. That account is recorded here as Beuys's own myth, not as fact. It was challenged in print by Benjamin Buchloh in "Beuys: The Twilight of the Idol", Artforum, 1980, a polemical attack on Beuys's fictionalisation of his own biography, his rhetoric and his persona, arguing that the myth-making disavowed past trauma and evaded engagement with Nazism and the Holocaust; Wikipedia notes that Buchloh mistakenly dated the crash story's entry into Beuys's biography as early as his 1964 Lebenslauf/Werklauf, and that his critique has since been revised in light of new archival material, above all Beuys's 1958 proposal for an Auschwitz-Birkenau memorial, although the charges of romanticism and self-mythologising remain. (C25862, leg 1 C25740-C25742; synthesis C25827, C25828)

Despite his injuries Beuys was deployed to the Western Front in August 1944, assigned to a poorly equipped paratrooper unit, and received a gold Wound Badge for having been wounded in action more than five times; on the day after the German unconditional surrender (8 May 1945) he was taken prisoner in Cuxhaven and brought to a British internment camp, from which he was released three months later, on 5 August, returning to his parents. On 1 April 1946 he enrolled in the monumental sculpture programme at the Dusseldorf Academy of Fine Arts, first assigned to the class of Joseph Enseling, then after three semesters joining the small class of Ewald Matare in 1947, who had rejoined the academy the previous year after having been banned by the Nazis in 1939; the anthroposophical philosophy of Rudolf Steiner became an increasingly important basis for his thought. Britannica gives his Dusseldorf study as 1947 to 1951, against Wikipedia's enrolment on 1 April 1946 -- an unresolved variant. In 1951 Matare accepted Beuys into his master class, where he shared a studio with Erwin Heerich that he kept until 1954, and Beuys finished his education in 1953, graduating at 32 as a master student of Matare's class; through the 1950s he lived on modest craft commissions, suffered a physical and psychological crisis with serious depression in 1956 from which he recovered at the house of his patrons the van der Grinten brothers in Kranenburg, and in 1958 entered an international competition for an Auschwitz-Birkenau memorial whose proposal did not win and was never realised. In 1959 he married Eva Wurmbach, and the couple had two children. (C25863, leg 1 C25743-C25746; synthesis C25829)

In 1961 Beuys was appointed professor of monumental sculpture at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf, and his formal students named by Wikipedia were Anatol Herzfeld, Katharina Sieverding, Jorg Immendorff, Blinky Palermo, Peter Angermann, Walter Dahn, Johannes Stuttgen, Sigmar Polke and Friederike Weske, with Elias Maria Reti the youngest, beginning study in his class at the age of 15. (The Polke entry is not relied on here: the Polke "studied under Beuys" wording is a carried contested variant from #708.) Beuys called teaching his greatest work of art in a 1969 Artforum interview with Willoughby Sharp, saying the rest was the waste product, a demonstration, and that he wanted to get to the origin of matter, to the thought behind it; at the academy he did not impose his artistic style or techniques on students, kept much of his own work and exhibitions hidden from the classroom, was strict about punctuality and draftsmanship classes while letting students set their own goals without set curricula, and held open "ring discussions" (Ringgesprache) on politics, philosophy, democracy and the role of art, yet Palermo and Immendorff disapproved of the classroom disorder and anarchic character and eventually rejected his methods and philosophies altogether. (C25864, leg 1 C25747, C25748; synthesis C25832)

He abolished entry requirements for his Dusseldorf class, a renegade policy that came to a head in October 1972, when he found 142 applicants who had not been accepted whom he wished to enrol; Beuys and 16 students occupied the academy offices to force a hearing, the students were admitted but the relationship was irreconcilable, he occupied the offices again, the police were called and he was escorted laughing from the building, an image used for a 1973 silkscreen titled Democracy Is Funny, and he was dismissed from his post, a dismissal he refused to accept and which drew protests from students, artists and critics, although the walkway on the academy's side of the Rhine is named for Beuys, and later in life he was a visiting professor at various institutions from 1980 to 1985. (C25865, leg 1 C25749; synthesis C25830)

The Kiefer link is informal, not formal: Tate's Kiefer-and-Beuys essay states that although Beuys "has in many accounts been named his teacher", "this description is inaccurate as Kiefer never actually studied under Beuys at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf while a student there in 1970-2", while search-summary material conflicts with Tate, with a Stadel Museum summary saying Kiefer studied under Beuys from 1970 to 1972 and the Anselm Kiefer Wikipedia summary saying he was studying informally under Beuys by 1970; this study does not resolve the contradiction and records Kiefer as an informal associate of the early 1970s, not a formal pupil. What the Tate essay does establish as shared ground is that both artists confronted the Nazi legacy through performance in order to come to terms with the past, drawing on the German Romantic tradition and its tree and forest mythology; the generational difference matters, with Beuys belonging to the Tatergeneration (perpetrator generation) who had spent his formative years in the Hitler Youth and the Luftwaffe, so that his confrontation was a belated experiencing of his own traumatic history centred on suffering, healing and catharsis, while Kiefer, born after the war, re-enacted the Nazi salute as moral self-incrimination aimed at critically examining fascism's hold on his own and the collective consciousness. (C25866, leg 1 C25750, C25751; leg 3 C25797)

Beuys befriended his Dusseldorf colleague Nam June Paik in 1962, a member of the Fluxus movement, beginning a brief formal involvement with Fluxus, a loose international group championing the radical erosion of artistic boundaries and bringing creative practice out of the institution and into the everyday; he entered wider public consciousness in 1964 at a festival at the Technical College Aachen coinciding with the 20th anniversary of the assassination attempt on Adolf Hitler, where his action was interrupted by students, one punching him in the face, and the photograph of Beuys with a bloodied nose and raised arm circulated in the media, and for that festival he produced the self-consciously fictionalised CV Lebenslauf/Werklauf (Life Course/Work Course), in which he calls his birth the "Exhibition of a wound" and claims his Ulysses Extension was carried out at James Joyce's request. (C25867, leg 1 C25752; synthesis C25852)

His signature materials in the sources read are fat and felt above all, joined by honey, gold leaf, iron and copper, and his best-known action, How to Explain Pictures to a Dead Hare, opened his first solo exhibition in a private gallery on 26 November 1965 at Galerie Schmela, with his face covered in honey and gold leaf, an iron slab attached to his boot, a felt pad on the other foot, and a dead hare cradled in his arms into whose ear he uttered muffled explanations of the drawings on the walls; for Beuys honey, the product of bees, stood for thinking as a living substance and, following Steiner, bees represented an ideal society of warmth and brotherhood, gold had its importance in alchemical enquiry, and iron, the metal of Mars, stood for a masculine principle of strength and connection to the earth. Variants: Wikipedia's teaching section says he spent three hours explaining to the hare, Britannica says about two hours; TheArtStory places Galerie Schmela in Dresden, Wikipedia in Dusseldorf; Fat Chair was created in 1964 with the fat decayed by 1985 (TheArtStory) against Fettecke listed as a 1965 notable work (Wikipedia infobox). (C25868, leg 1 C25753)

In May 1974 Beuys flew to New York for I Like America and America Likes Me at the Rene Block Gallery, 409 West Broadway, travelling by ambulance and lying on a stretcher swathed in felt, and shared a room with a coyote for eight hours over three days, at times standing wrapped in a thick grey felt blanket and leaning on a shepherd's staff, at times lying on straw, striking a large triangle or tossing his leather gloves to the animal, before hugging the coyote and leaving in a veiled ambulance without having set foot on American ground, explaining afterwards: "I wanted to isolate myself, insulate myself, see nothing of America other than the coyote." He first took part in documenta in Kassel in 1964 (documenta 3), returned for documenta 4 in 1968 and documenta 5 in 1972, showed the Honey Pump at documenta 6 in 1977, and delivered 7000 Oaks at documenta 7 in 1982; he represented Germany at the Venice Biennale in 1976 (Tram Stop) and 1980, and the only major retrospective of his work organised in his lifetime opened at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum in New York in 1979, described as a lightning rod for American criticism. (C25869, leg 1 C25755, C25756; synthesis C25831)

During the 1960s Beuys formed his central theoretical concept of social sculpture, indebted to the Romantic writers Novalis and Schiller and founded in Rudolf Steiner's Social Threefolding, in which society as a whole was to be regarded as one great work of art (the Wagnerian Gesamtkunstwerk) to which each person can contribute creatively, in his most famous phrase, borrowed from Novalis, "Everyone is an artist"; in 1973 he wrote that only on condition of a radical widening of definitions could art provide evidence that it is now the only evolutionary-revolutionary power, capable of dismantling a senile social system in order to build "A SOCIAL ORGANISM AS A WORK OF ART", and in his first major American public discussion in 1974 he set out three principles, Freedom, Democracy and Socialism, each depending on the other two to be meaningful. For documenta 7 in 1982 he delivered a large pile of 7,000 basalt stones in front of the Fridericianum in Kassel, which from above formed a large arrow pointing to a single oak tree he had planted, and announced that the stones should not be moved unless an oak tree was planted in the stone's new location, so that 7,000 oak trees were planted across Kassel with volunteers, a project Dia says took five years to complete, with the last tree planted at the opening of documenta 8 in 1987; Wikipedia adds that the operation officially ended in 1987, a year after the artist's death, yet has yet to be completed, since it will take about 300 years for the 7,000 oak trees to become the great forest Beuys imagined. Dia carried 7000 Oaks to New York, installing five basalt stone columns paired with five trees outside 548 West 22nd Street in 1988, extending the installation in 1996 by planting twenty-five new trees each paired with a basalt stone, and adding one more pair during the Dia Chelsea renovation of 2020-21, bringing the total to thirty-eight pairings (older secondary summaries give thirty-seven). (C25870, leg 1 C25757-C25759; synthesis C25833)

He founded or co-founded the German Student Party (1967), the Organisation for Direct Democracy through Referendum (1971), the Free International University for Creativity and Interdisciplinary Research (1974, with Heinrich Boll, Johannes Stuttgen, Caroline Tisdall, Robert McDowell and Enrico Wolleb) and the German Green Party Die Grunen (1980), was a founding member and life-long supporter of the Greens, became a pacifist and vocal opponent of nuclear weapons, was elected as a Green Party candidate for the European Parliament, and in 1982 recorded the anti-Reagan music video "Sonne statt Reagan" ("Sun, not Rain/Reagan"). Between 1972 and 1985 he carried out his most important Italian project in Bolognano (Pescara), Operazione Difesa della natura (In Defense of Nature), invited there in 1972 by Lucrezia De Domizio and Buby Durini and returning many times, with the Paradise Plantation planting of 7,000 plants to restore biodiversity in 1982 and the work Olivestone in 1984, receiving honorary citizenship of Bolognano in 1984. He cultivated a shaman persona, adopting a felt hat, a felt suit, a cane and a vest as his standard look, saw his role as a teacher or shaman who could guide society in a new direction, and considered his art both instructive and therapeutic; his severe depression between 1955 and 1957 has been characterised as his shamanistic initiation, and he said he did not use shamanism to refer to death but, through shamanism, to refer to the fatal character of the times, which could be overcome in the future. Beuys died of heart failure on 23 January 1986 in Dusseldorf, aged 64, after what TheArtStory describes as a long illness, and the trees of 7000 Oaks continued to be planted by others after his death. (C25871, leg 1 C25760-C25763; synthesis C25834)

Beuys is widely regarded as one of the most influential artists of the second half of the 20th century, described by MoMA as the central figure in postwar German art whose identity as artist, teacher, performer, political activist and social reformer can be traced through his drawings, generally agreed to exceed 10,000 sheets, which he likened to an energy bank and drew as constantly as breathing, and by Tate as the artist of the expanded concept of art and of social sculpture, for whom art was central to all aspects of human existence. (C25872, leg 1 C25764)

## M1 -- Fat as stored energy and as warmth

Fat is the material Beuys trusts with warmth: it is banked on a chair, melted on a hot plate, smeared into the corners of a room, packed onto a sled as food, and spread as butter to repair a broken plaster block. It is a solid that softens and melts with heat, so it works as a battery for warmth -- it can be banked, spread, smeared and re-formed, and it changes state in front of the viewer. The fat is not a colour choice -- a yellow paint could do that -- it is a state-change choice: the wedge on the chair would slump if the room warmed, the blocks on the hot plate are visibly melting, the butter repair is soft where the plaster is hard. Evidence: W01 Fat Chair (wedge of fat on a chair seat beside a thermometer; the fat had almost entirely decomposed by 1985), W11 Auschwitz Demonstration (two blocks of fat on a hot plate, melting beside a camp photograph), W12 Economic Values (butter repairing the damaged corners of a plaster block), W06 The Pack and W19 Sled (a lump of fat on every sled as food). (C25873, leg 2 C25786; synthesis C25840)

The watchable behaviour is slow change: gloss, slump, smell and softness shifting with temperature, never all at once. The viewer reads the warmth before the subject -- the fat tells the eye that what is shown is alive, perishable, and warm to the touch.

**Takeaway:** use a material that changes state with heat, so the picture carries its own temperature; the warmth must be felt in the motion, slow and reluctant, never instant. (C25874)

## M2 -- Felt as insulation and as protection

Felt is pressed wool fibre that traps air, muffles sound, blocks sight and holds warmth in, so wrapping a thing in felt removes it from the world while keeping it alive inside. It is Beuys's second signature material, and it does opposite work to fat: fat gives warmth out, felt keeps warmth in. The suit hangs empty with lengthened arms and legs and reads at once as armour, bandage and empty portrait; the piano wrapped in felt cannot sound, condemned to silence; the blanket hides the artist and only his amplified breathing escapes; Beuys arrives in New York and leaves wrapped in felt, having seen nothing of America but the coyote. Evidence: W02 Felt Suit (edition 100, modelled after one of his own suits with arms and legs extended), W14 Infiltration Homogen for Grand Piano (grand piano covered entirely in felt, two red crosses on its sides), W16 The Chief (artist wrapped in a felt blanket for hours, two dead hares at either end), W04 Coyote (arrival and departure wrapped in felt), W06 The Pack (a rolled felt blanket on every sled as shelter). (C25875, leg 2 C25787; synthesis C25841)

The watchable behaviour is disappearance: the wrapped thing goes quiet, dark and still, and the viewer must imagine what is inside. The felt does not decorate the object; it takes the object out of circulation.

**Takeaway:** wrap the thing to take it out of the world -- felt is the material of insulation, and insulation is a way of keeping something alive by hiding it. (C25876)

## M3 -- The action as the work

An action is a timed performance with a fixed duration, a barrier between performer and public, and a repeated task: locked doors and windows in the Dead Hare, a steel mesh barrier in the Coyote, a doorway the audience may not cross in The Chief, three timed sections in the Celtic Symphony. The work is the time spent, not an object left behind. The public stands outside looking in, the performer repeats a small cycle -- explaining to the hare, bowing to the coyote, striking a triangle, tapping a stick -- about thirty times in the Coyote action, each cycle lasting over an hour and cued by a strike of the triangle, and the work is that time, watched. Evidence: W03 Dead Hare (three hours, doors locked, public outside watching through the windows), W04 Coyote (three days, eight hours each, behind a steel mesh barrier, about thirty repeated cycles), W16 The Chief (hours inside a felt roll, audience in the next room, able to hear but not to see or touch), W15 Celtic Symphony (three timed sections: 45 minutes, 90 minutes, 45 minutes). (C25877, leg 2 C25788; synthesis C25842)

The watchable behaviour is waiting and repetition: the same small gesture again, the same cycle again, until the repetition itself becomes the content. The barrier is part of the mechanism -- without the locked door or the mesh, there is no action, only a man in a room.

**Takeaway:** make the work a fixed time with a barrier and a repeated task; the audience watches the time pass, and the passing is the work. (C25878)

## M4 -- The animal mediator

Beuys puts an animal between himself and the audience because the animal will not follow a script: a dead hare that must be carried and spoken to, a live coyote that circles, growls, shreds felt and finally lies beside him, a stag lit by a flash, two dead hares marking the ends of a felt roll. The human must copy the animal's rhythm -- roaming when it roams, resting when it rests -- instead of commanding it. The hare, which burrows and makes its home in the earth, stands for incarnation; the coyote, which Beuys never touches on American ground except by embracing it at the end, is the America he will meet on its own terms. Evidence: W03 Dead Hare (dead hare carried on the arm, whispered explanations into its ear), W04 Coyote (live coyote Little John, circling, growling, shredding felt, finally lying beside him), W07 Lightning with Stag (aluminium stag lit by a hanging bronze flash), W16 The Chief (two dead hares at the ends of the felt roll like the head and tail of a single creature). (C25879, leg 2 C25789; synthesis C25843)

The watchable behaviour is negotiation with a non-human partner: the hare's head wobbles as Beuys moves it, the coyote chooses when to engage and when to ignore, and the detente, when it comes, is earned by repetition, not by command.

**Takeaway:** put a living partner in the work that cannot be scripted; the human adapts to the animal, and the adaptation is the performance. (C25880)

## M5 -- Social sculpture as participation

Social sculpture means the work is only finished when other people do something: volunteers plant trees and carry stones away, visitors argue, listen and attend workshops for a hundred days, citizens debate direct democracy in the offices and on the posters. The artist sets the frame and the rules; the public supplies the labour and the change. The stone pile outside the Fridericianum shrinks day by day as each stone is carried to a newly planted tree; the city greens tree by tree; the room fills with talk and chalk dust. Evidence: W05 7000 Oaks (7,000 trees planted across Kassel with volunteers over five years, each paired with a basalt stone), W08 Honey Pump (more than a dozen Free International University workshops, discussions, lectures and film screenings over a hundred days), W18 La rivoluzione siamo Noi (the striding figure at life size, demanding the bystander join the cause). (C25881, leg 2 C25790; synthesis C25844)

The watchable behaviour is a crowd doing the work: a pile shrinking, a city greening, a room filling. The piece is not finished when the artist leaves; it is finished when the public has done its part, which may take years.

**Takeaway:** build the work so it cannot be finished alone -- the frame is the artist's, the labour is the public's, and the change is the piece. (C25882)

## M6 -- Honey as social warmth and as flow

Honey is a viscous, living substance made by a collective, the hive, so pumping it through a building models how ideas and warmth circulate through a society. Two ship motors push a couple of tons of honey and several hundred pounds of margarine through plastic tubing around the discussion rooms for a hundred days, and the flow stops if the machine stops; on the head, honey stands for thought made living, against dead, intellectualised thought. Evidence: W08 Honey Pump (motors pushing honey through tubes around the discussion rooms for the full hundred days of documenta 6), W03 Dead Hare (honey and gold leaf on the head as living thought). A diagram Beuys drew of the system in 1984 shows a copper cylinder turning through fat, marked with the words for will, economy, heart and law. (C25883, leg 2 C25791; synthesis C25845)

The watchable behaviour is slow circulation: liquid creeping through clear tubes, motors humming, the whole building fed like a body supplied with nutrients, with the flow stopping if the machine stops. The honey is not decoration on the discussion; it is the discussion made visible as a bodily flow.

**Takeaway:** pump something living through the space so the circulation of ideas becomes a flow the eye can follow; the building is the body, the honey is the blood. (C25884)

## M7 -- The blackboard and lecture as diagram

Beuys draws and writes on a blackboard while he talks, so thinking becomes a visible line that the audience watches being made, and the board is then left standing as the residue of the discussion. Lines grow, are corrected, are carried across the room, and remain after the speaker has gone. The blackboard is where the lecture leaves its trace: the first use of a blackboard in performance was the Celtic Symphony, where it is drawn on, pushed across the floor with a stick and stood beside during the final stillness; the piano performance includes blackboard writing; two boards in Show Your Wound carry the chalk order to show your wound; the Honey Pump workshops happen among chalkboards and folding chairs. Evidence: W15 Celtic Symphony (blackboard drawn on in the first section), W14 Infiltration Homogen (blackboard writing during the performance), W20 Show Your Wound (two boards bearing "zeige deine Wunde"), W08 Honey Pump (workshops among chalkboards). (C25885, leg 2 C25792; synthesis C25846)

The watchable behaviour is drawing as talking: a line appears while the words are spoken, the board fills, and what remains is a diagram of a thought that was made in public, in real time.

**Takeaway:** let the thinking be drawn while it is spoken, and leave the board standing; the diagram is the residue of the discussion, not an illustration of it. (C25886)

## M8 -- The suit and the chair as body-absent portrait

A garment or a seat holds the shape of a person, so the viewer supplies the missing body: the felt suit hangs empty with lengthened arms and legs, and the fat on the chair waits for a sitter who never comes. The portrait works because the person is not there. An empty suit on a hanger, an empty chair with a warm mass on it, and the viewer's own body answers the invitation to sit or to wear it. Evidence: W02 Felt Suit (felt multiple, edition 100, modelled after one of his own suits with arms and legs extended), W01 Fat Chair (wedge of fat banked on an ordinary chair seat beside a thermometer, held as a vitrine). (C25887, leg 2 C25793; synthesis C25847)

The watchable behaviour is absence made present: the shape is exact and the body is missing, so the viewer stands in for it. The chair and the suit are portraits in the only form Beuys trusts -- the form the body leaves behind.

**Takeaway:** portrait the person by the shape they leave -- an empty garment, an empty seat -- and let the viewer supply the body. (C25888)

## M9 -- Multiples as distribution

A multiple is an editioned object that carries an action's kit into many hands at once: the felt suit in an edition of 100, the sled in an edition of 50, the revolution poster in an edition of 180. The mechanism is repetition with small differences: each example is signed and numbered, each sled's kit is packed slightly differently, and the same survival system or the same striding figure turns up in many collections and many cities. One idea, printed or packed a hundred times, travels further than any single unique object could. Evidence: W02 Felt Suit (edition 100, published by Galerie Rene Block, Berlin), W19 Sled (edition 50 plus 5 hors commerce; the Broad example measures 36.83 x 90.17 x 34.93 cm against Montreal's 39 x 91 x 35.5 cm, the small difference coming from the hand-packed kit), W18 La rivoluzione siamo Noi (diazotype, 191.5 x 100.7 cm, edition 180; the figure strides toward the viewer at nearly life size, as if poised to step out of the image). (C25889, leg 2 C25794; synthesis C25848)

The watchable behaviour is spread: the same kit, the same figure, appearing in place after place, each copy slightly different because a human packed it. Distribution is the work, not the reproduction of it.

**Takeaway:** edition the kit, not the image -- let the same survival system travel in a hundred slightly different hands, and the spread is the sculpture. (C25890)

## M10 -- The wound and its treatment

Beuys makes an injury visible and then dresses it: bore a conical hole in basalt and line it with clay and felt before putting the plug back in; lay a body on a corpse stretcher above a vat of fat with a thermometer in it; put fat on a hot plate beside a camp photograph; smear fat into the corners of a room. Healing is shown as a procedure on a visible wound, not as a finished cure. The rocks evoke bones or corpses and their scattered alignment suggests a world beyond control, while the clay and felt lining suggests new life emerging at the end of a dark century. Evidence: W13 The End of the Twentieth Century (basalt slabs bored with conical holes, lined with clay and felt, plugs re-inserted), W20 Show Your Wound (paired corpse stretchers above fat vats with thermometers, a blackbird's skull in a test tube, blackboards ordering "zeige deine Wunde"), W11 Auschwitz Demonstration (fat on a hot plate beside a photograph of Auschwitz), W09 Eurasian Staff (fat smeared into the corners of the room). (C25891, leg 2 C25795; synthesis C25849)

The watchable behaviour is damage and care side by side: dark holes in pale stone, paired stretchers and paired tools, a skull in a test tube, a thermometer measuring a vat that should be a body. The wound stays visible; the treatment is the work.

**Takeaway:** show the wound and dress it in the same gesture -- bore, line, close -- and never claim the cure is finished. (C25892)

## The aesthetic logic: the kit stays the same, the field gets bigger

Beuys's whole career is one procedure performed fifty ways: take a small set of materials -- fat, felt, honey, copper, a staff, a sled -- and one set of actions -- wrap, smear, bore, pump, plant, show -- and apply them to a larger and larger social field. The early works (W01, W03, W14, W16, W17) are small, bodily and enclosed: one chair, one piano, one blanket, one hare, one television studio, with fat, felt, honey and silence doing the work and the audience kept outside the glass. The middle works (W04, W06, W09, W15, W18, W19) let the kit travel: sleds leave the van, a staff joins four corners, a poster strides out of the frame at life size, and the audience moves from peering through a window to sharing a room for three days. The late works (W05, W07, W08, W12, W13, W20) open the scale to cities, forests, museums and centuries -- trees and stones, groceries and paintings, basalt and whole rooms -- while the gestures stay exactly the same: wrap, smear, bore, pump, plant, show. (C25893, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25850)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the material is the argument: fat for stored warmth, felt for insulation, honey for social flow, copper for connection -- a description of the materials is already a description of the meaning, and a description of the materials is all the meaning there is. Second, the action is the work: a fixed time, a barrier, a repeated task, and the object left behind is only the residue -- the suit, the sled, the blackboard -- of a time that was spent. Third, society is the material: the same gestures that wrap one body in felt and smear fat into one corner of a room are applied, unchanged, to a city (plant 7,000 trees), to a building (pump honey through it for a hundred days), to a century (bore a wound in basalt and dress it). That is why Beuys is the opposite pole of Kiefer even though the two men are now tied by a teacher thread: Kiefer sets up a procedure that loads content into a material until the lead itself is the history; Beuys sets up a procedure that loads a material into society until the society itself is the sculpture. (C25894, leg 1 C25757; leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25844, C25850)

The evolution is the proof. 1963-65: the enclosed actions (W16, W17, W03) -- a man in a blanket, a man in a shed on live television, a man behind locked doors explaining pictures to a dead hare, the audience outside. 1966-70: the kit forms (W14, W09, W06, W19, W02) -- the felt piano, the copper staff, the sleds pouring out of the van, the suit in an edition of a hundred. 1972-77: the room opens (W18, W20, W04, W08) -- the poster strides out at life size, the wound is shown in an underpass, the coyote is negotiated with for three days, honey is pumped through a museum for a hundred days. 1980-87: the city and the century (W12, W13, W05, W07) -- 510 packets of East German groceries priced at one value each, basalt bored and dressed, 7,000 oaks planted across Kassel, a stag lit by a frozen flash. The late work does not change the procedure; it widens the field it is applied to, from a single body to a whole city, and the answer in every case is the same -- wrap it, smear it, bore it, pump it, plant it, show it. (C25895, leg 1 C25752-C25762; leg 2 W01-W20; synthesis C25850)
