# Judd Greenstein: Study Notes

**Site:** Judd Greenstein (b. 1979, Manhattan, New York City; living), American composer of contemporary classical music; co-founder and co-director of New Amsterdam Records since 2008; co-artistic director of NOW Ensemble; founding curator of the Ecstatic Music Festival (2011-2022); Visiting Assistant Professor at Amherst College (per his own bio). His practice is the pop surface over a polymetric background: a small cell handed around an ensemble and grown by accumulation, a dance beat or an 80s-pop rhythm on top, a rhythmic complexity underneath, and every piece written for the particular players in the room. [C29355]

Judd Greenstein was born in 1979 in Manhattan, in Greenwich Village, and the day of birth does not appear in any reliable source -- Wikipedia gives the year only, Wikidata records the birth date at year precision, and his own site gives no date at all. He went to Hunter College Elementary School and Hunter College High School, started composing on the piano at nine, and at fifteen or sixteen his piano teacher Mary Jo Pagano pulled him aside and asked, "Do you want to actually be a composer?" -- after which he went to the library, took out scores and recordings, and studied the late twentieth-century masters: Stravinsky, Bartok, Carter, Boulez, and George Perle, who "for some reason, was a big person for me at that time". The other half of his musical childhood ran in parallel: at eleven or twelve he began writing hip-hop beats with his friend David, cobbling beats together on an old Casio, sampling the piano with a crude recorder and overlaying as many sounds as possible, then a 4-track, then a professional synthesizer, writing hip-hop beats "more earnestly than I was writing classical music" for a time, alongside classical pieces in the manner of Rachmaninoff and Prokofiev, composers he was playing at the time. He has said being a hip-hop producer "was always something that seemed very on the table, even into my 20s", and that in 1994, with different friends plugged into the Queensbridge scene, "I could have been on Stillmatic. That's pure fantasy, but I definitely would have said yes!" He met Nico Muhly in 1997 at the Boston University Tanglewood Institute. He grew up a few blocks from the Ukrainian Village, remembers street festivals on Taras Shevchenko Place, and every year on his birthday went to the old Kiev restaurant for mushroom barley soup and blintzes. [C29359]

He studied at Williams College, class of 2001, majoring in Music and Political Science and studying composition with David Kechley, and there he revived the Independent Music Project, a student series for new music. In the summer of 2002 he was an artist-in-residence at the first Bang on a Can Summer Institute of Music at MASS MoCA, studying with David Lang; a college internship with Bang on a Can helped set him on his path. At the Yale School of Music he took his Master of Music in composition, studying with Martin Bresnick, Aaron Jay Kernis and Ezra Laderman, and in the summer of 2004 he was a fellow at the Tanglewood Music Center. At Princeton he earned a second master's degree and became a PhD candidate with a dissertation on hip hop music under Steven Mackey -- but completion of the doctorate is NOT confirmed in any source checked: Princeton Alumni Weekly (c. 2020) says he "is still working on his dissertation", and older bios say only that he "is completing" it, so this study does not call him Dr. Greenstein and asserts no doctorate. [C29360]

The institutions are the other half of the career, and he built most of them himself. NOW Ensemble was formed in 2002 at the Yale School of Music as a composer/performer collective bridging performers and composers, in its modern form by 2004 -- flute, clarinet, electric guitar, piano, double bass, plus the non-performing composers Patrick Burke, Mark Dancigers and Greenstein -- with Greenstein a co-founder and co-artistic director; its first album, NOW, came out in 2008 on New Amsterdam Records. New Amsterdam Records was founded in 2008 by Greenstein, Sarah Kirkland Snider and William Brittelle, "to promote classically trained musicians who fall between traditional genre boundaries", set up in Greenstein's childhood bedroom because "not many labels were recording music of the kind we were making in the way we wanted"; he has served as co-director since, the label took 501(c)(3) status in 2011, revamped in 2017 into a label, presenter and artist-service organisation, partnered with Nonesuch Records in 2019, and has released more than a hundred albums. In 2011 Merkin Concert Hall asked him to help create a new music series, and the project became the Ecstatic Music Festival -- first edition 14 concerts, 17 January to 28 March 2011, 150 composers and performers, opening with a seven-hour marathon -- with Greenstein as its founding curator until 2022, and the model later exported to Ventspils, Latvia, and to Zurich as Currents. He is currently a Visiting Assistant Professor at Amherst College, per his own bio, has been a visiting professor at Williams College and a Mentoring Artist at the Atlantic Center for the Arts, was a Distinguished Guest Composer at the 2025 Mizzou International Composers Festival, 23-26 July 2025, was composer-in-residence with the Alabama Symphony Orchestra (c. 2013), and won the Charles Ives Scholarship from the American Academy of Arts and Letters in 2004. He lives in Gill, Massachusetts, a small farming community in the Connecticut River Valley, with his wife, the composer and violinist Michi Wiancko, and their two young children, and together they run Antenna Cloud Farm, a music festival, artist's retreat and community organisation out of their small farm. [C29361]

His working method has been stated in his own words, most fully in the 2011 NewMusicBox interview, and it has not changed since. "My music is often very repetitive in certain ways and things not only come back but they stick around for a while once they're in play." What he is trying to avoid most is "something like boredom or any feeling of disinterest or a moment of something being used only as a rhetorical device." "I usually write from beginning to end... I can't think of a piece outside of the general conception that I have of it as an arc from beginning to end." "The greatest challenge I have as a composer is to remove the process that I'm in when writing the piece from the experience that I know I'm going to have listening to it." "I need to want things to stay in play longer than they actually do. I want to be disappointed anytime something stops happening. If something changes, I want to make sure that the thing that happens is even more engaging... than the thing that has just left. It's an escalation up from silence." The threshold test for every change is: "Is it worth changing from what was there before?" The test comes from hip hop: at 2 a.m. in 1992, tweaking a beat, "waiting for that moment when I would think, 'I could listen to this forever.' And I find myself doing that a lot -- tweaking some pattern, some texture... Not the moments that are clearly discrete, clearly short term, but something that's going to go on for a while." On genre: "In my dream world we don't have a world of genres, we have a world of difference, and we understand really specifically what the differences are." Genre "stops people from hearing things that they might like otherwise." On the composer's role he rejects Charles Wuorinen's "I don't believe that a composer has any obligation to the world whatsoever" -- "people have obligations to the world, so, aren't you a person?" -- and says, "Outside of making music for other people to listen to, I don't think we really have any function at all." On collaboration, his viola work Escape exists only because violist Nadia Sirota plays it -- "I never would've written it if I weren't imagining Nadia actually playing it out in the world, so it wouldn't exist." The Mizzou bio (2025) puts the same method from the listener's side: "Judd's music teaches its language as it tells a story, with clear musical ideas coalescing into rich structures and tapestries of sound. Much of Judd's work is built on contrasts and juxtapositions between the jittery, competing pulses of the New York City streets where he grew up and the placid landscapes of the rural farmland he now calls home." [C29362]

The twenty works inspected run 1999-2023 and trace one practice finding its engine, then widening it. Phase 1, apprenticeship and string lyricism, 1999-2004 (W01-W03): a 35-minute piano trio, orchestral pieces and choral works known only from works lists, and the one early work with a documented mechanism, The Night Gatherers, a memorial viola quintet in a Schoenberg/Ravel/Debussy lineage -- lyrical, string-driven, programmatic, a grandmother's paintings as the starting point -- with nothing yet in it of the pop-groove language that defines him. Phase 2, the NOW Ensemble core and the pop surface, 2004-2010 (W03-W12): with NOW Ensemble he finds his instrumentarium -- flute, clarinet, electric guitar, double bass, piano -- and his engine, a small melodic cell handed around the quintet over percolating, dance-derived rhythms with Reich-like repeating piano patterns underneath; alongside it runs the hip-hop line, What They Don't Like sampling Chuck D and James Brown outright, and Four on the Floor building a string quartet from a dance beat and a 10-note DNA strand; and the phase closes with the Roomful of Teeth triptych of summer 2009, the pop-groove composer meeting eight voices trained in Tuvan throat singing, yodeling and belting, and writing by adapting rhythm-and-harmony etudes to the singers' actual techniques. Phase 3, expansion, 2010-2016 (W13-W17): the NOW language extends to yMusic's sextet and onto the ballet stage, Sh'lomo turns to the Hebrew Bible with vintage keyboards and electric guitars, In Teaching Others turns the concerto inside out for seven violas answering the Voyager Golden Record, The Mend scores a feature film in modernist dread, and The Answer builds a ballet from a single basketball play. Phase 4, opera, pandemic and the two-chord concerto, 2016-2025 (W18-W20): A MARVELOUS ORDER is the decade's project, developed through site-specific excerpts in the Fulton Center, Times Square and the Brooklyn Public Library facade before its complete premiere; Together rewrites itself to mirror the pandemic in three movements; Still Point is the most concentrated recent mechanism, two chords generating 12:40 of turbulence and rippling calm. ## M1 -- The DNA strand: a small cell grown by accumulation

The signature device of his whole practice, and the one every other mechanism hangs off. Four on the Floor (2006) is built the way a dance track is built -- from a single small cell. Greenstein calls it "a little 10 note strand of musical DNA"; across the piece it expands and evolves into a "beautiful genetic mutation". Group dynamics are the key theme: different instrument pairings work with and against one another in constantly changing teams, and a second listener calls it "a muscular, driving string quartet" and compares the move to Brahms taking the then-new dance, the waltz, as a platform for his own musical expression. The same device, seventeen years later, is Still Point (2023): a "pint-sized piano concerto" built from two chords that engender a wide color palette of emotion across 12:40. Between the two sits Change (2009), where the mechanism is the same cell handed on: "as the insistent opening flute line is taken up by the quintet, it gathers force over percolating rhythms that bear the DNA of 80s pop". The mechanism is accumulation, not development: the cell does not get taken apart and argued with, it gets passed around, mutated, and grown, and the piece is the record of how far one small thing can travel. The watchable behaviour is a tiny shape that keeps coming back bigger -- the same ten notes, the same flute phrase, the same two chords, never quite the same twice, always recognisable. Seen in W04, W11, W20. [C29364]

**Takeaway:** take ten notes and let the mutation do the composing. [C29364]

## M2 -- The pop surface over a polymetric background

The sound the critics keep naming, and the sound his own words describe from the inside. Musicologist Kyle Gann names the underlying structure: Greenstein's "typically polymetric compositional structures", giving his music a "foot-tapping pop surface in front of a background rhythmic complexity", akin to the 1980s advent of totalism. Justin Davidson says the same thing in one line: his style "maintains a pulse-driven poppish sensibility while employing a sophisticated harmonic language". NOW Ensemble's own bio puts it as "an urban, beat-oriented sensibility with a late Romantic classical harmonic language". The surface is always something a body can follow -- the four-on-the-floor dance beat of W04, the percolating 80s-pop rhythms under Change, the Chuck D and James Brown samples of What They Don't Like -- and the background is always counting something the foot never hears. The mechanism is the split itself: the pop surface is the door, and the polymetric background is the room behind it, and the listener is invited in by the beat and kept by the complexity. The watchable behaviour is a room full of people tapping their feet to something they could not count if they tried. Seen in W04, W06, W11. [C29365]

**Takeaway:** put the dance beat on top and the counting underneath. [C29365]

## M3 -- The handover: one line taken up in turn, gathering force

The social version of the DNA strand: the cell does not just grow, it changes hands. Change (2009) is the cleanest statement: the opening flute line is taken up by the quintet, instrument by instrument, and as it passes from hand to hand it gathers force over the percolating rhythms underneath. City Boy (2010) does the same move faster: "a colorful musical depiction of a free and fearless young boy... The piece moves rapidly from one melodic idea to the next, switching from a jazzy guitar groove to a circling piano motive to a flowing clarinet melody within a matter of minutes" -- rapid melodic relay, ideas handed from instrument to instrument, each with its own groove character, in quick succession. The mechanism is the relay as the form: no single instrument owns the line, and the piece's energy comes from the passing itself, each handover adding a little more force than the last. The watchable behaviour is a melody that never sits still -- it is always arriving somewhere new, in a new player's hands, with a new groove under it. Seen in W11, W12. [C29366]

**Takeaway:** hand the line to the next player before it settles. [C29366]

## M4 -- Sketch-then-adapt: write to the singers' actual techniques

The collaborative signature, learned in one room in the summer of 2009 and never unlearned. Greenstein arrived for the second week of Roomful of Teeth's two-week residency at Mass MoCA carrying sketches -- "etudes of sorts, studies in rhythm and harmony that left a lot of room for different sounds in different places" -- and, once he heard the singers, adapted them with their varied techniques in mind into fully-formed pieces combining his sketches with the sounds of the ensemble. That is the whole method: the sketch is deliberately unfinished, the room finishes it, and the finished piece could not have been written anywhere else, for anyone else. The same logic governs the rest of his catalogue from the other side: Escape exists only because violist Nadia Sirota plays it -- "I never would've written it if I weren't imagining Nadia actually playing it out in the world, so it wouldn't exist" -- and In Teaching Others exists because seven violas, all played by Sirota, were in the room. The mechanism is the unfinished sketch as an invitation: leave room in the material for the performers' actual techniques, and let the piece be the record of what happened when they filled it. The watchable behaviour is a piece that fits its performers like it was tailored in the room -- because it was. Seen in W08, W09, W10, W05. [C29367]

**Takeaway:** bring a sketch, listen to the room, let the players rewrite it. [C29367]

## M5 -- The vowel set: five vowels as an ordered set

The most concentrated of the three Roomful of Teeth pieces, and the one that turns the voice into an instrument by the most literal means. A E I O U (2009) uses the five basic vowel sounds as an ordered set, while the sonic landscape is a tapestry of interwoven yodels, throat singing and straight-tone clarity. Greenstein calls it "an extremely challenging work" that "could only be performed by the singers of this incredible ensemble". The mechanism is the vowel as pitch material: five sounds, always the same five, in an order that does the work a scale or a row would do in another piece, and the rest of the piece -- the yodels, the throat singing, the straight tone -- is the landscape those five vowels move through. The watchable behaviour is eight singers making music out of the sounds a voice makes before it makes words -- the vowels arriving in order, the techniques weaving around them, nothing else needed. Seen in W10. [C29368]

**Takeaway:** take five vowels and order them like notes. [C29368]

## M6 -- The pad: one voice over a mellowed drone

The simplest mechanism in the catalogue, and the one that makes Run Away the piece it is. Run Away (2009) is "a simple song that I wrote to feature the voice of Virginia Warnken. She sings over a 'pad' that the men lay down, a blend of vocal sounds that approximates Tuvan singing but is mellowed considerably. The other women join Virginia at the end of the song, carrying the story on into the future." The text is Greenstein's own, a run-away-into-the-forest lyric: "run away / into the forest / pick the flowers that you find..." The mechanism is the drone as a floor and the solo voice as the figure on it: the men's pad never changes, never competes, it only holds the ground, and the one voice is free to run because the ground is held. The arrival of the other women at the end is the only structural event the piece needs -- the solo becomes a chorus, the run-away becomes a future. The watchable behaviour is one voice floating over a low male hum, the others arriving at the end to carry the story on. Seen in W08. [C29369]

**Takeaway:** lay down a mellowed drone and let one voice run away over it. [C29369]

## M7 -- Throat-singing vs yodel: timbre as an equal partner

Montmartre (2009) is the argument that sound and timbre belong alongside harmony, counterpoint and voice leading, made as a piece of music. It opens with a counterpoint between throat-singing men and yodeling women, then moves on through a variety of more familiar vocal production techniques, belted and bell-like. Greenstein named it after the Paris neighborhood because that is where the concepts of sound and timbre were finally elevated to their rightful place alongside harmony, counterpoint and voice leading in the Western classical tradition: French composers in the early twentieth century wrote pieces open to sounds they heard from around the world, and Roomful of Teeth is doing the same kind of exploration with the human voice. The mechanism is the technique as the counterpoint: the two kinds of voice production are not decoration on a harmonic structure, they ARE the structure, set against each other the way two themes would be. The watchable behaviour is two kinds of voice in counterpoint, neither one background, the throat-singing and the yodeling arguing with each other in sound alone. Seen in W09. [C29370]

**Takeaway:** let timbre argue with harmony on equal terms. [C29370]

## M8 -- The borrowed frame: a story from outside music as the form

The widening move of the middle career: when the cell alone is not enough, borrow a frame from outside music and let the music live inside it. The Night Gatherers (2004) takes a friend's grandmother's paintings as its starting point, an atmospheric opening giving way to melodies cascading through a haze of strings that finally coalesce into a single strand, a winding viola vocalise. In Teaching Others We Teach Ourselves (2012) answers the Golden Record shot into the solar system in 1977 by NASA, meant to share the sounds and images of humankind with alien life -- one viola against six more, the same voice multiplied, as the answer. Sh'lomo (2011) takes the Hebrew Bible, King Solomon, and texts from the kabbalah, after Greenstein studied with a rabbi, and becomes a four-movement symphony blending indie rock, folk and pop. The Answer (2016) takes a photograph of a signature play by basketball legend Allen Iverson, interpreted through architect Carlos Arnaiz's eyes, and builds a staccato ballet from Iverson's idiosyncratic leap. Together (2022) takes the pandemic itself. A MARVELOUS ORDER (2022) takes the 1960s fight between Robert Moses and Jane Jacobs over Washington Square Park and the Lower Manhattan Expressway. The mechanism is the frame borrowed whole: the piece's shape comes from somewhere else -- a grandmother's paintings, a space probe, a Bible story, a basketball play, a plague, a highway fight -- and the music is what happens inside that shape. The watchable behaviour is a familiar container arriving with unfamiliar contents: a viola quintet that is also a memorial, a septet that is also a message to aliens, an opera that is also a fight over a park. Seen in W02, W15, W14, W17, W18, W19. [C29371]

**Takeaway:** borrow the frame from outside music and let the music live in it. [C29371]

## M9 -- The same voice multiplied: solo vs tutti as one instrument

The concerto turned inside out. In Teaching Others We Teach Ourselves (2012) is scored for solo viola and viola sextet -- seven violas, all played by Nadia Sirota on the recording -- and it opens Sirota's album Baroque with "a different paradigm of 'solo' versus 'tutti' than more famous efforts in the [concerto] form": one solo viola against an accompaniment built from six more violas, all the same instrument, so solo and ensemble are the same voice multiplied rather than opposed. The piece answers the Voyager Golden Record, and the answer is itself the mechanism: humankind's message to alien life, sent as one voice that becomes seven, the soloist and the ensemble indistinguishable because they are the same instrument, the same player, the same sound, seven times over. A Brisbane listing adds that the viola septet enforces Greenstein's "passion for rhythm and texture, haunting lows and soaring highs". The mechanism is multiplication instead of opposition: where a concerto usually pits one against many, here the many ARE the one, and the drama is in how far a single voice can stretch before it becomes a crowd. The watchable behaviour is seven violas, one voice, and you cannot tell where the soloist ends and the ensemble begins. Seen in W15. [C29372]

**Takeaway:** make the ensemble out of the soloist, seven times over. [C29372]

## M10 -- Tension held at the edge: the score that never lets peace settle

The film mechanism, and the darkest in the catalogue. The Mend (2014), co-composed with Michi Wiancko for John Magary's feature film, is scored for strings, keyboard and woodwinds, and it is built around themes of family tension and existential dread, playing as a psychologically-rooted "counterpoint to the film's existential funk" (Wall Street Journal). The score "suggests at all moments that any minor peace might unravel into violence" (Village Voice); MoMA calls it a "bracingly modernist score". Director Magary asked the composers to listen to Bernard Herrmann and wanted the film "music forward"; the score was created in the scoring process, piece by piece, with revisions against Magary's notes. The mechanism is tension held at the edge of unravelling: a modernist string/keyboard/woodwind texture that keeps a domestic scene sounding as if it could tip into violence, and never lets it tip, and never lets it settle either -- the peace is always minor, always provisional, always about to come apart. The watchable behaviour is a kitchen-table scene that sounds like a held breath: nothing happens, and everything might. Seen in W16. [C29373]

**Takeaway:** hold the peace at the edge of unravelling and never let it settle. [C29373]

## M11 -- The three-room piece: distance, hybrid, in-person

The pandemic mechanism, and the one where the piece's own making becomes its form. Together (2022), for yMusic, was rewritten to mirror the progression of the pandemic in three movements: the first was written for players collaborating from a distance; the second for hybrid play; the third for in-person performance. The piece begins with solo flute, "like a single bird flying over a softer city"; other voices join in, "like faces appearing in apartment windows or on Zoom screens" -- violin offering consolation and encouragement, clarinet striking a more serious tone acknowledging grief and struggle, cello reflecting the drama of the early days. In the second movement the players nudge closer, the string trio establishing a hummable theme before the trumpet enters with only a few notes, then recedes, "reflecting the ups and downs, the changing risks and regulations". By the end Together turns into a dance, "like those in Medieval times that celebrated the end of winter and the return of warmth". It was released 11 February 2022, the day after mask mandates were lifted in New York, a decade since Greenstein last worked with yMusic. The mechanism is the making as the form: the three movements are the three rooms the players lived in -- apart, half-together, together -- and the piece is the record of the ensemble reassembling itself. The watchable behaviour is the ensemble coming back together, movement by movement, until it can dance again. Seen in W18. [C29374]

**Takeaway:** let the piece's own making become its form -- distance, then hybrid, then together. [C29374]

## M12 -- The city as a stage: opera developed in public space

The decade's project, and the mechanism that makes A MARVELOUS ORDER different from an opera that simply premieres. Conceived in 2013 by Greenstein, Tracy K. Smith and Joshua Frankel together, it was developed through excerpt presentations and site-specific adaptations in public space, each followed by a phase of creative conversation and iteration: first musical and visual studies ("Mannahatta", on Walt Whitman's poem) previewed at the BAM Next Wave Festival in November 2013; the short film "Plan Of The City" performed live by NOW Ensemble at the Winter Garden in November 2013; the Sundance New Frontier Story Lab in fall 2013; a "pre-premiere" of ten of the eventual twelve scenes at Williams College in March 2016; a 25-minute excerpt, site-specific, at the Fulton Center transit hub in June 2017, with Moses moving from the subway level up to street level and the top balcony and the animation taking over fifty video advertising screens; a three-minute animation excerpt on the Times Square screens in May 2017; excerpts at the Brooklyn Public Library in October 2021, the animation projected onto the Central Library facade. The completed opera -- about two hours, twelve scenes, libretto by Smith, animation and direction by Frankel, NOW Ensemble as the house orchestra, Megan Schubert as Jane Jacobs and Rinde Eckert as Robert Moses -- premiered 20 October 2022 at Penn State's Eisenhower Auditorium. The opera is set in the 1950s-60s and centers on the feud between Robert Moses and Jane Jacobs over Washington Square Park and the Lower Manhattan Expressway, including Moses' plan to demolish Jacobs' home and the West Village for housing towers; the opening scene, "The Ballet of the Street", describes Greenwich Village as seen through Jacobs' eyes, the neighborhood she sets out to defend. The opera's own site describes it as "not only an opera but a body of work organized around a journey of creation". The mechanism is the city as the rehearsal room: the opera was tried out in the stations, the squares and the facades of the city it is about, piece by piece, for nine years, before it ever arrived whole. The watchable behaviour is an opera arriving in pieces, in a subway station and on a library wall, before it ever arrives on a stage. Seen in W19. [C29375]

**Takeaway:** rehearse the opera in the city it is about, piece by piece, before it opens. [C29375]

## M13 -- The two-chord concerto: everything from two chords

The most concentrated recent mechanism, and the proof that the DNA strand of M1 can be reduced to almost nothing and still hold a piece. Still Point (2023) is a "pint-sized piano concerto" for Awadagin Pratt, string orchestra (A Far Cry) and eight-part amplified vocal ensemble (Roomful of Teeth), 12:40 long, built from two chords that engender a wide color palette of emotion, alternating turbulent outbursts with quieter rippling passages and "retro, pulsating voices straight out of Steve Reich's Music for 18 Musicians". Pratt: "Still Point is a profoundly moving work that touched me from the first moment of seeing and playing through the score." Greenstein's own note: the work "contains many spirits: Tyner's, Eliot's, those of A Far Cry and Roomful of Teeth... and of course Awadagin's, whose towering musical presence is at the center of this work". It is dedicated to Awadagin, in memoriam McCoy Tyner, in friendship and with great admiration and respect. The six works on the Stillpoint album each respond to a passage from T.S. Eliot's "Burnt Norton" in the Four Quartets, without setting the text; the project began in 2016, when Pratt and Greenstein were invited to adjudicate a chamber ensemble contest at DePauw University's 21CMposium. The mechanism is two-chord generation: everything in the 12:40 span grows out of two chords, swinging between turbulence and rippling calm, with Reich-like pulsating voices -- the smallest possible seed, the widest possible weather. The watchable behaviour is two chords, and everything that can happen to them. Seen in W20. [C29376]

**Takeaway:** take two chords and let them be the whole piece. [C29376]

## M14 -- The curator-composer: building the infrastructure the music needs

The mechanism that is not in any single piece, because it is the mechanism that makes the pieces possible. Greenstein does not only write music; he builds the rooms, the labels and the festivals the music needs, and then writes the music for them. New Amsterdam Records (2008) exists because "not many labels were recording music of the kind we were making in the way we wanted", and it was set up in his childhood bedroom. NOW Ensemble (2002) exists so that composers and performers "would be continuously creating and collaborating". The Ecstatic Music Festival (2011) exists to pair artists from different musical worlds in new collaborations, all premieres. His own account of the motive, to Princeton Alumni Weekly: "So much of what I'm trying to do is create opportunities for musicians to pursue music in the way they want, as opposed to being constrained by the existing infrastructure for music. It's more interesting when you allow all those things to get messier and speak to each other in new ways." His thesis adviser Steven Mackey calls him "prodigiously skillful and unfettered by taboo and dogma", and says his label and festival work has "contributed to the formation of musical scenes or subcultures that will have historical importance". The mechanism is the institution as the instrument: build the room, the label, the festival, and the music follows -- and the music that follows is different from the music that would have been written without them, because the room was built for it. The watchable behaviour is the scene itself, not just the piece: the label, the ensemble, the festival, the farm in Gill where the next piece gets written. Seen across the career, not in one work. [C29377]

**Takeaway:** if the infrastructure does not exist, build it -- the room, the label, the festival -- and let the music follow. [C29377]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the fourteen mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Greenstein's music is built to be entered: a beat you can stay inside, a room you can gather in, a city you can walk through. The pop surface is the door -- the dance beat, the 80s-pop rhythm, the hip-hop sample, the hummable theme -- and the polymetric background is the room behind it, the complexity that keeps you there once you have come in. The test for everything is the 2 a.m. test from 1992: "I could listen to this forever." That is why things stay in play longer than they do, why a change has to pass "Is it worth changing from what was there before?", why the piece is "an escalation up from silence" rather than a sequence of effects. The borrowed frame and the sketch-then-adapt method are the same move in two directions: the frame comes from outside music, the sketch comes from inside the room, and in both cases the piece is finished by something that is not the composer alone -- the singers' techniques, the players' hands, the city's streets, the pandemic's three rooms. And the curator's infrastructure is the same move at the largest scale: if the room does not exist, build it, and let the music follow. Ruehr, the study before this one, borrows a structure and fills it with feeling; Greenstein brings a sketch and lets the room rewrite it, and the difference between the two is the difference between a composer who carries the structure with her and a composer who builds the room and lets the room do the work. His music teaches its language as it tells a story, and the story it tells is always the same one underneath: stay in the room, keep the beat, and let the small thing grow until it fills the space. [C29378]
