# Jules Bastien-Lepage: Study Notes

**Site:** Jules Bastien-Lepage (1848-1884), French painter -- born Jules Bastien on Wednesday 1 November 1848 at Damvillers, Meuse, Lorraine, the son of Claude Bastien, who grew grapes and was an amateur artist and his first teacher, and of Catherine Adele Lepage; his younger brother Emile Bastien-Lepage (born Friday 20 January 1854, died Wednesday 19 January 1938) became a painter and architect. He was schooled at Verdun, arrived in Paris in 1867 and supported himself with a supernumerary Post Office job, sorting mail from 3:00 to 7:00 a.m., while studying drawing in the afternoons and evenings; he failed the Ecole des Beaux-Arts concours in 1867, attended as an aspirant, and was received first in the concours on Tuesday 20 October 1868, entering Alexandre Cabanel's atelier with a letter of introduction from William-Adolphe Bouguereau, and adding his mother's maiden name Lepage to his own. He served as a volunteer sharpshooter in the Franco-Prussian War, was wounded in the chest by a clod of hardened earth thrown up by a bursting shell, and debuted at the Salon of 1870 with a portrait that passed unnoticed. The grandfather portrait, Son grand-pere, painted in 1873 and shown at the Salon of 1874, won a third-class medal and was bought by the State, and made his name. He entered the Prix de Rome twice and lost twice: in 1875 his Annunciation to the Shepherds was rejected on the technicality that he had painted a night scene at twilight, the prize going to Leon Commere, with a Premier Second Grand Prix as a consolation of no official significance; in 1876 Priam at the Feet of Achilles failed again. After the second failure he abandoned academic subject matter for the country life of Damvillers, and built the Naturalist formula in a run of large plein air machines: Les Foins (Haymaking, 1877, Salon 1878), October, or Potato Gatherers (1878, Salon 1879), Joan of Arc (1879, Salon 1880), and the Portrait of Sarah Bernhardt (1879), the three Salon entries of 1879 winning him the cross of the Legion of Honour at 31. He worked in London in 1880-1882, painted the Prince of Wales, and turned to single-figure child portraits -- Pas Meche, London Bootblack, Going to School -- before his last major work, L'Amour au Village (1882, Salon 1883). By 1883 a Salon critic could write that the whole world painted so much like M. Bastien-Lepage that M. Bastien-Lepage seemed to paint like the whole world. He was diagnosed with a tumour on his left testicle by his friend and physician Dr. Jules Watelet in May 1883, was operated on, convalesced at Concarneau, travelled to Algiers with his mother early in 1884 in the hope the sun would cure him, and died in Paris on Wednesday 10 December 1884, aged 36, at 6 p.m., in the arms of his friend Prince Bojidar Karageorgevitch, six weeks after Marie Bashkirtseff died on Friday 31 October 1884. He was buried at Damvillers, where his mother and brother planted an apple tree at his grave's head. The memorial exhibition at the Hotel de Chimay, Paris, opened on Tuesday 17 March 1885 with more than 200 pictures; Auguste Rodin made the statue for the Damvillers monument designed by his brother Emile, erected in 1889; the first one-man Paris show since 1885 was the Musee d'Orsay retrospective of 6 March to 13 May 2007. His works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay (Les Foins, the grandfather portrait, the portrait of his brother Emile), the National Gallery of Victoria, Melbourne (Annunciation to the Shepherds, October), the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (Joan of Arc, gift of Erwin Davis, 1889), the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Tournai (La Communiante, Le Petit Colporteur endormi), the Palais des Beaux-Arts, Lille (Priam at the Feet of Achilles), the Musee Marmottan Monet, Paris (Diogenes), the Milwaukee Art Museum (Le Pere Jacques), the Pushkin Museum, Moscow (L'Amour au Village), the Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek, Copenhagen (The Beggar), the Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (The Grape Harvest), the Scottish National Gallery, Edinburgh (Pas Meche), Kelvingrove, Glasgow (Poor Fauvette), and the Royal Collection (Portrait of the Prince of Wales). ## M1. The High Horizon and the Field That Fills the Canvas
The horizon is pushed to the top fifth of the canvas, so the land -- not the sky -- fills the picture; the viewer looks DOWN across a wide, receding field as if from a standing position in it. In Les Foins the hay "like a very pale yellow cloth shot with silver" fills the main part of the canvas (Musee d'Orsay); in October the same scheme gives "deep recession here enhanced by the distant figures, and broad land" (Eclectic Light); in Joan of Arc the horizon is so high that little sky is visible beyond the trees. The effect is a wide-angle panorama that makes two small figures feel exhausted and desolate inside a huge space. The artist's own rule, quoted in The Art Story: "Nothing of the usual willow arrangement, with its branches drooping over the heads of the people to frame the scene. Nothing of that sort." **Takeaway:** raise the horizon to the top of the frame and the field becomes the picture; the figures shrink inside it and the weariness reads as scale.

## M2. Differential Detail (Two-Speed Painting)
One canvas, two rendering speeds. The foreground figure is painted with painstaking, almost photographic exactitude; the background is loose, broken Impressionist flecks that are barely finished. The face and hands of the haymaker are crisp while the meadow behind her dissolves into visible brushstrokes; in Marie Samary "the fine white scarf is painted in crisp detail, when compared with the background of papers"; in the London Bootblack "the documentary realism of the foreground gives way to a more sketchy and jumbled background"; in The Little Chimneysweep the boy and his bread roll are careful, the white cat sketchy. Billcliffe, quoting the Glasgow Boys literature: "Detailed marks are used to render the foreground grasses or stones; broader, square brushes model the middle distance; and a series of softer, less definite strokes indicate the far distance. Perspective, therefore, is indicated by a change of handling rather than by any pictorial indication of recession." Weisberg (via Wikipedia): "other sections of a work reflected a painstaking exactitude, suggestive of a classical training and an interest in photographic detail", while other areas were left free from reworking, "reflecting an awareness of Impressionistic broken brushstroke painting". Zola's summary of the whole trick: "impressionism corrected, sweetened and adapted to the taste of the crowd." **Takeaway:** paint the figure at one speed and the world at another; the change of handling is the perspective.

## M3. The Snapshot Pose
Figures caught in ungainly, mid-movement poses that look cut out of a photograph -- angular, foreshortened, frozen at a moment that has no classical balance. The foreground potato gatherer bends with a "staccato angular" movement, her gesture incongruous with the task, while her companion smiles and relaxes; the haymaker sits with arms apart and "fixed eyes seeing nothing"; the shepherds in the Annunciation stand in "catatonic suspension". Contemporary critic P. de St Victor, La Liberte, 3 June 1879, on October: "leur mouvement photographique n'a rien de vivant ni de naturel, il est fige" -- their photographic movement has nothing living or natural about it, it is frozen. Feldman (NGV): "an evanescent movement of reality has been isolated by the artist and immutably transfixed to canvas, in a manner anticipating snapshot photography", and the poses probably derive from photography, at the moment Muybridge was conducting his locomotion experiments. **Takeaway:** freeze the body mid-task at an ungainly angle; the frozen instant is the realism, and the stiffness is the proof it was seen, not posed.

## M4. The Figure Standing on the Frame
A single life-size figure placed on the frontal plane of the canvas, silhouetted against a receding landscape or low horizon, so large it nearly fills the frame and seems ready to step out of it. Poor Fauvette's girl stands under her leafless tree and stares out; Pas Meche's boy "looks directly out of the painting"; the Pere Jacques old man "dominates the center" of the frame. Billcliffe (p. 34): "His models stand on the frame of his paintings, almost ready to walk out of them. They are placed against a landscape background which Bastien-Lepage painted from a standing position as he believed it to be crucial in our understanding of the relationship of figures to their surroundings... In many of his paintings the figure defines the size of the canvas by filling it completely". Vertical features rising from the bottom edge -- trees, tall grasses, a teasel stalk -- anchor the figure to the frontal plane and chart the change of brushstroke by distance (M2). **Takeaway:** put one figure on the frame, life-size, and let the landscape recede behind it; the figure is the measure of the canvas.

## M5. The Two-Reality Split
One canvas holding two incompatible registers of reality side by side, separated by a hard architectural or spatial division. In Joan of Arc the corner of a house "sharply divides the painting into halves. On its right is the very real and tangible figure of Joan of Arc... On the left are the ethereal figures of Saints Michael, Margaret, and Catherine" (Eclectic Light), rendered "with considerably less detail" (The Art Story). A commenter on 2blowhards puts it plainly: "there seems to be two realities -- just as there was for Joan of Arc -- and the viewer seems to be sharing her out-of-body experience of both 'being there but, at the same time, being somewhere else.'" The Met: the saints are "depicted at left", faint in the foliage, while Joan is solid. The split is what the Salon critics attacked and what makes the picture unforgettable: the sacred is painted as if it were a smudge on the lens. **Takeaway:** divide the canvas in two and paint each half in a different reality; the seam is the vision.

## M6. The Incongruous Face
The face does not match the task. The body labours; the face is elsewhere -- glazed, smiling, dreaming, tranced. The haymaker's "fixed eyes seeing nothing"; the foreground potato gatherer's "glazed look" against her companion's smile, "incongruous with their vigorous tasks" (Feldman); Joan's trance-like upward gaze while her hands have just dropped the wool. Feldman reads this as proto-Symbolism: "the two potato gatherers are turned in on themselves, sharing a psychological compatibility that belies their physical detachment. Their complex interchange of meditative states is prophetic of Symbolist painting in the following decade." The tension between strenuous body and absent mind is the picture's real subject. **Takeaway:** give the labouring body a face that has left the work; the mismatch is the psychology.

## M7. The Diametric Pair (Old Against Young)
Two figures who are exact opposites in age and condition share one frame, so the painting becomes a picture of time passing. Pere Jacques -- "an old man bent with his load of firewood, who at any moment could keel over and die, and a young child who runs free among the wild flowers"; The Beggar -- a withered old man in dour colours against "a well-dressed young girl" in a bright blue dress, with bright blooms in a potted plant beside the door; Poor Fauvette -- a child of seven or eight alone with a grazing cow under a dead tree. The Art Story on Pere Jacques: the pair represent "the heavy weariness of old age and the innocence of youth, as well as the passage of time". Grove Art: The Beggar and Pere Jacques "effectively contrasted aged peasants to children", and in Pere Jacques the woodcutter theme shows "that all forest inhabitants, including children, had a place in nature". **Takeaway:** pair the oldest body with the youngest in one frame; the contrast does the work of a lifetime.

## M8. The Closing Door (The Threshold Divide)
A door, fence or gate that is literally closing between two people, making class separation a visible mechanical action. In The Beggar the door "is in the process of being closed behind him but has been blocked by a young girl in a bright blue dress who watches him intently as he prepares to leave"; the closing door reminds viewers "that these two people and by extension the two societal groups of which they are a part will always remain separate" (The Art Story). In Village Love the fence does the same work more gently: the lovers stand on opposite sides of it, talking across it, and the girl's body is turned away. The threshold is the picture's argument -- charity given and the door shut, love declared across a barrier. **Takeaway:** put a closing door or a fence between the two figures; the barrier is the class system, made mechanical.

## M9. The Turned Back
The key figure refuses the viewer -- back turned, face hidden, gaze averted over the shoulder -- so the emotion must be read from posture alone. The Village Love girl "turns her back to the spectator"; Crastre: "although her back is turned, we are sure that she is blushing as she listens"; the Grape Harvest woman "walks towards the viewer, but does not make eye contact. She looks over her shoulder instead, turning away in a pose that draws the viewer's eye and leads it, as it were, into the landscape" (Musings). Mette Gauguin's early reading of Village Love -- that the girl was under age -- turned on exactly this refusal: "The girl not only faces away from the viewer, but her whole body is turned away, leaving that issue unresolved and unresolvable" (Eclectic Light). The mechanism generates interpretation by withholding the face. **Takeaway:** turn the face away and let the back do the talking; what the viewer cannot see is what the viewer invents.

## M10. The Pale Symphony (High-Key Portrait)
Portraiture painted in a deliberately light key -- whites, creams, pale golds -- where the sitter's distinction is carried by tone rather than by colour or setting. Sarah Bernhardt draped in white china silk on a white fur robe, holding an old-ivory Orpheus -- "a pale symphony of tenderest whites, blending harmoniously with the warmest tones of gold" (Crastre); La Communiante's girl in starched white veil and gloves "standing out from a creamy background"; the Bernhardt portrait "painted in high key values" at only 17.5 x 13.5 in. The Art Story: "The execution is such that the drawing of the gown and the lines of the face seem to have been traced by an engraver's tool" (Revue des Beaux-Arts). The same light key appears in the landscapes: Les Foins is "a light grey-green" with "pearly tones: half-dry hay and flowering grasses... like a pale yellow tissue with silver threads running through it" (artist's letter, August 1877). Light key is the bridge between the portraits and the field pictures. **Takeaway:** paint the portrait in whites and pale golds and let tone carry the distinction; the light key is the signature that joins the portraits to the fields.

## Evolution
1867-1873, apprenticeship. He arrives in Paris, sorts mail from 3:00 to 7:00 a.m. to pay for study, fails the Ecole concours in 1867, is admitted first in the concours on 20 October 1868 into Cabanel's atelier, and paints what the atelier prescribes: copies of Watteau landscapes with fashionable young women inserted. Salon debut 1870, unnoticed; a second acceptance in 1872, unnoticed. The war interrupts him -- a volunteer sharpshooter, wounded in the chest by a clod of earth, a shell destroying his first composition in his studio -- and he spends most of 1871 recovering at Damvillers.

1874-1876, academic success and the break. The grandfather portrait (1873, Salon 1874) wins a third-class medal and is bought by the State; La Communiante (1875) shows the Holbein-like gravity; two Prix de Rome failures (1875 Annunciation, 1876 Priam) on technicalities and taste. The 1875 rejection -- twilight instead of night -- is the pivot: he decides academic rules are not worth obeying and turns to Damvillers. The credo he gives Fourcaud dates from this break: "Nothing is good but truth. People ought to paint what they know and love. I come from a village in Lorraine. I mean, first of all, to paint the peasants and landscapes of my home exactly as they are." The twilight itself stays with him as a chosen hour: the Annunciation was painted at twilight because the quality of light at twilight was more aesthetically pleasing than the limited light of a night scene, and in the early 1880s he returns to it as a series, Soir au Village and La Chaine (both 1882) examining the particular character of twilight and evening hours.

1877-1879, the Naturalist formula. Les Foins (1877), October (1878), Joan of Arc (1879), the Bernhardt portrait (1879). The monumental plein air series, planned with Theuriet as twelve monthly compositions, "Months in the Country"; only six were completed. High horizon, differential detail, snapshot poses, the same model across three Salons: Marie-Adele Robert, the artist's cousin, modelled the foreground figure of Haymakers (1877), Potato Gatherers (1878) and Joan of Arc (1879) in succession -- three Salon paintings, one body, three roles, resting labourer, working labourer, saint. Legion of Honour 1879, age 31. Zola names him the grandson of Millet and Courbet and calls Les Foins the masterpiece of naturalism.

1880-1882, high Naturalism. Grape Harvest, Beggar, Pere Jacques, Poor Fauvette, Ophelia, the 1882 child portraits, Village Love. The formula perfected and then pushed to its limit; the single-figure child portrait becomes the dominant unit; London subjects (Bootblack, Flower Market) extend the formula to the city. He is summoned to Marlborough House in July 1879 to paint the Prince of Wales, shown at the Royal Academy in 1880, and a small retrospective at the Grosvenor Gallery follows. By 1883 a critic could write, "The whole world paints so much today like M. Bastien-Lepage that M. Bastien-Lepage seems to paint like the whole world." The child portraits soften the Naturalist edge: Going to School's girl is "a little too clean and perfect to be objective" (Eclectic Light), and Pas Meche's grubby boy is "nearer the mark" of true Naturalism than the too-clean children of the same year.

1883-1884, illness and loosening. Diagnosed with testicular cancer (operation May 1883, Dr. Watelet); trips to Concarneau, Brittany, Algiers with his mother; output falls sharply. The Little Chimneysweep (1883) shows a looser hand and a sketchy foreground animal against careful figure detail. Last dated work The Forge (1884); the Burial of a Young Girl (1884) is left an unfinished oil sketch. He dies on 10 December 1884, aged 36; the memorial exhibition of 200+ works opens at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in the spring of 1885; Rodin makes the monument at Damvillers. The standing painter's eye is the method he leaves behind: he painted from a standing position because "it is crucial in our understanding of the relationship of figures to their surroundings" (Billcliffe), and when critics reproached his open-air perspective with "want of air", he answered that it was "the criticism of people who have never looked at a landscape, except crouching down or sitting. When you sit down to paint, you naturally see things quite differently from the way you see them standing... We need to renew the education of our eye, by looking with sincerity upon things as they are in nature, instead of holding as absolute truths the theories and conventions of the school and the studio."

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic of Bastien-Lepage is a logic of kept apparatus and changed subject, and then of kept subject and abandoned system. He never abandons the academic technique he learned from Cabanel; the finish stays, the scale stays, the Salon stays -- he exhibits through 1883 and takes the royal portrait -- and what changes is who stands in front of the finish, and then where the painter stands. That is the whole move, and it is why the pictures are legible in a way a looser painting would not be: a haymaker painted with the care of a Venus is a claim, not a sketch, and a beggar painted with the care of a history picture is a document, not a joke. The first logic is the standing eye: the high horizon, the field that fills the canvas, the figure standing on the frame -- the painter stands in the field and paints what the standing eye sees, and the viewer is put on their feet in the same field. The second logic is the two speeds: the figure finished to photographic exactitude, the world behind it left in broken Impressionist flecks -- Zola's "impressionism corrected, sweetened and adapted to the taste of the crowd" -- so that the picture is both true and readable, both modern and acceptable, the juste milieu that made him the leader of a school he never meant to found. The third logic is the withheld face: the glazed eyes, the turned back, the snapshot freeze -- the picture gives the body in full detail and refuses the mind, and the viewer, given a pose and no expression, has to supply the interior life. The fourth logic is the pair: old against young, beggar against girl, lover against lover across a fence -- the picture is built as a contrast, and the contrast is the meaning. The fifth logic is the loosening: after 1882 the formula softens into child portraits and then into illness, the careful figure against an ever sketchier world, until the last dated work is a forge and the last sketch a burial, and the man who painted the peasants of Damvillers for the Salon dies at 36 with the whole world painting like him. The village is the last mechanism: the man who painted what he knew and loved kept painting the neighbours anyway, because the truth was the point, not the market.

## POSITIONS
P1. Paint what you know and love, and paint it exactly as it is. His credo, as reported by Feldman (NGV essay) from Fourcaud/Cartwright: "Nothing is good but truth. People ought to paint what they know and love. I come from a village in Lorraine. I mean, first of all, to paint the peasants and landscapes of my home exactly as they are." Evidence: the whole Damvillers programme of 1876-1882, the series of large plein air pictures of his own village. Status: secondhand wording (see Q2); the practice is uncontested in every source read.
P2. Paris taught him his business, not his art. Letter fragment after the 1875 Prix de Rome loss, quoted in Theuriet (Gutenberg lines 1101-1119): "I learned my business in Paris, I shall not forget that; but my art I did not learn there." He kept the academic craft -- drawing, finish, the large "machine" -- and rejected the academic subjects: "In the school I have drawn gods and goddesses, Greeks and Romans, that I knew nothing about, that I did not understand, and even laughed at."
P3. Paint standing, never sitting. Spoken to Theuriet in the woods of Reville (Theuriet, Gutenberg lines 2331-2371): "When you sit down to paint, you naturally see things quite differently from the way you see them standing. Sitting, you see more sky... But it is not in this way that we generally see a landscape. We look at it standing." His high horizons and figures silhouetted against fields rather than sky follow directly from this rule.
P4. Renew the education of the eye. Same passage (Gutenberg lines 2365-2371): "We need to renew the education of our eye, by looking with sincerity upon things as they are in nature, instead of holding as absolute truths the theories and conventions of the school and the studio." Sincerity of looking, not a new system, is the whole programme.
P5. The particular, not the general. Billcliffe (quoted in 2blowhards, from "The Glasgow Boys", pp. 33-34): "Where Millet was concerned with the general, a synthesis of rural life, Bastien-Lepage applied himself to the particular. Millet's paintings reflected the life of rural France but Bastien-Lepage chose one particular task, performed by one particular person at a specific moment and recreated it on his canvas." One haymaker resting, one wood gatherer, one boy with nothing to do.
P6. Perspective by handling, not by recession. Billcliffe (2blowhards, p. 34): "in many of his paintings the figure defines the size of the canvas by filling it completely; in a similar way the landscape around the figure is defined by the size of the brushstrokes used to depict it. Detailed marks are used to render the foreground grasses or stones; broader, square brushes model the middle distance; and a series of softer, less definite strokes indicate the far distance. Perspective, therefore, is indicated by a change of handling rather than by any pictorial indication of recession."
P7. Landscape painted in the open air, figures finished in the studio. Locke (musings-on-art) on Potato Gatherers: "Bastien-Lepage has created a landscape in plein air, while finishing his figures inside his studio." The hybrid method -- Impressionist landscape envelope plus academic figure finish -- is what Zola called corrected Impressionism (P13).
P8. High horizon, close framing, photographic composition. The Musee d'Orsay on Haymaking (via The Art Story): the composition is "daringly photographic" in the way "the horizon is unusually high, allowing the hay 'like a very pale yellow cloth shot with silver' to fill the main part of the canvas". The light palette and close framing are "signs of modernity within the naturalist approach". The same scheme -- high horizon, fine foreground detail, deep recession -- runs through Haymaking (1877), Potato Gatherers (1878) and The Wood Gatherer (1881) (Eclectic Light).
P9. No romanticising, no staging to flatter. The Art Story: Salon critics objected that he refused "to embellish his work with chiaroscuro or to stage scenes in order that his subjects might be represented in a more appealing light", which they called a limitation; he "continued painting his subjects exactly as they appeared to his eye". His peasant woman in Haymaking is haggard, hot and red-faced, eyes seeing nothing -- the opposite of the Salon pastoral.
P10. Tell the whole story of country life as a series of large pictures. Theuriet (Gutenberg lines 1279-1295): he wanted "to tell the whole story of country life in a series of large pictures: hay-making, harvest, seed-time, the lovers, the burial of a young girl.... He also wanted to paint a peasant woman as Jeanne d'Arc... then, a Christ in the Tomb." With Theuriet he planned twelve compositions, Les Mois Rustiques (The Months in the Country), he to furnish the drawings and Theuriet the text; only six were completed before his death.
P11. History painting must be human. Remarks to Fourcaud about the Prix (NGV essay, citing Cartwright p. 19): "if you take subjects from ancient history, at least let them be represented in an altogether human manner, exactly as you see the same things happen around you". Joan of Arc (1879) is the proof: France's greatest heroine as a thirteen-year-old Lorraine peasant girl in her parents' garden, a local teenager as model, the saints the only unnatural element -- and the element the critics attacked.
P12. Portraiture was a side issue, a relaxation between landscapes. Crastre (via The Art Story): "to Bastien-Lepage portrait painting was only a side issue, a form of relaxation between two landscapes; his predilection, his one object in life, so to speak was to return constantly to his peasants, his scenes of toil, his fields of Lorraine". Yet the portraits -- grandfather, Bernhardt, Prince of Wales, Marie Samary -- are what paid, won the Legion of Honour (1879) and spread his name across Europe.
P13. Reception: Zola made him the heir. Zola called Haymaking "the masterpiece of naturalism in painting" and Bastien-Lepage "the grandson of Millet and Courbet"; and of his method, "impressionism corrected, sweetened and adapted to the taste of the crowd". Zola and Albert Wolff saw him as the inheritor of Millet and Courbet and affirmed his superiority over the Impressionists, because he could "factually recreate his impressions in a most organized way" (science.jrank.org, Naturalism in Art and Literature).
P14. Reception: by 1883 he was everywhere. Unsigned Salon critic, Ville de Paris, 1 May 1883 (quoted in Wikipedia and NGV): "The whole world paints so much today like M. Bastien-Lepage that M. Bastien-Lepage seems to paint like the whole world." Feldman (NGV): "a school of Bastien-Lepage followers emerged virtually overnight, attracting practitioners from France, Great Britain, and throughout Europe."
P15. Reception: the expected successor to Manet. Locke (musings-on-art, citing the Milwaukee catalogue): "By the early 1880s, Bastien-Lepage had become the leader of the Naturalist school, and many of his contemporaries believed that he would one day succeed Manet as the leader of modern painting." Feldman (NGV): the series of immensely popular genre themes he produced until his death "earned him an influence comparable to Manet, some sixteen years his senior".
P16. Reception: Joan of Arc divided the Salon of 1880. Critics "praised Bastien-Lepage's use of pose and facial expression to convey Joan's spiritual awakening but found the inclusion of the saints at odds with his naturalistic style" (Locke); the picture "creates a very closed-in feeling, suffocating the figure. The placement of the saints seems forced, contrary to the artist's naturalistic approach." The public loved it anyway; it remains his best-known painting.
P17. Reception: the want-of-air quarrel. His Wood Gatherer (Father Jacques) "was reproached with want of air" (Theuriet, Gutenberg lines 2331-2335); Billcliffe: "His grasp of aerial perspective was much criticised but he defended it vehemently." The criticism -- that his standing viewpoint flattens distance -- is the same fact as his position P3, seen from the other side.
P18. Reception: Roger Fry's retrospective verdict. Fry, "An Essay in Aesthetics" (1920), quoted in Wikipedia: the public's acceptance of the Impressionists, especially Monet, required "the teaching of men like Bastien-Lepage, who cleverly compromised between the truth and an accepted convention of what things looked like, to bring the world gradually around to admitting truths which a single walk in the country with purely unbiassed vision would have established beyond doubt." The mediator, not the revolutionary.
P19. Reception: eclipse, then rediscovery. HASTA/visual-arts-cork: "Despite Bastien-Lepage's international fame, his reputation dimmed soon after his death and his work became little more than a footnote in most early studies of French 19th century painting. It was not until the last quarter of the 20th century that his paintings were rediscovered and underwent a scholarly reappraisal." Feldman (NGV): "If Bastien-Lepage's reputation has dwindled within the present century from its former lofty heights, recent research has challenged the prevailing designation of his work as peripheral."
P20. Reception: founder of a school without meaning to be. Crastre (via The Art Story): "as painter of the open air, he became in a certain sense the founder of a school, without meaning to be; for his conception of the painter's art won over a whole group of young artists who united in hailing him as their master". Feldman (NGV): for a younger generation "disenchanted with the excesses of both conservative and radical camps, the 'juste milieu' ['middle ground'] approach of Bastien-Lepage represented the viable alternative."

C3. Prix de Rome 1875: he did NOT win. He was one of ten finalists, spent ninety days in the loge, and the jury gave the Prix to Leon Commere, awarding Bastien-Lepage a Premier Second Grand Prix as a consolation prize "which held no official significance" (NGV). Wikipedia's infobox "Prix de Rome (runner-up, 1875)" is the same fact in shorthand. His second attempt, Priam at the Feet of Achilles (1876), also failed.
C4. Haymaking (Les Foins) is 1877 and was exhibited at the Salon of 1878, not 1879. Locke (musings-on-art) says it was exhibited at the Salon in 1879; Wikipedia, NGV and the Musee d'Orsay say 1878. Carry 1878. Potato Gatherers (October) is 1878, exhibited at the Salon of 1879 with the Bernhardt portrait.
C5. Joan of Arc (1879) is in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, exhibited at the Salon of 1880. One search result (artsdot) wrongly places it at the Musee d'Orsay; the Met holds it (gift of Erwin Davis, 1889).
C6. Legion of Honour: chevalier in 1879, at 31, for the three Salon entries of that year. One low-quality source (masterful-artists) claims it was awarded posthumously; that is wrong.
C7. Cause of death: testicular cancer, diagnosed by Dr. Watelet in May 1883 (tumour on the left testicle, removed by operation); he died of the cancer in Paris, 10 December 1884. Two errors to avoid: 2blowhards repeats a source saying "cancer of the stomach"; Eclectic Light's 1882-84 piece says his health failed "as a result of what was most probably tuberculosis". The primary source is Watelet's letter to Le Matin, 13 December 1884. Also note Wikipedia's own inconsistency: its lead says "In 1882, Bastien-Lepage was diagnosed", its body says May 1883; carry May 1883 per the body and Theuriet. The "affaire Watelet": Watelet was convicted of violating medical confidentiality for revealing the diagnosis to silence rumours of venereal disease.
C8. Portrait of Sarah Bernhardt (1879) is in a private collection (Ann and Gordon Getty collection; sold at Christie's New York, 20 October 2022, for $2,280,000). The Art Story's caption listing the Legion of Honor Museum, San Francisco, as its collection is wrong; it was on loan there from the Getty collection. Its dimensions are also in conflict: 17.5 x 13.5 in. (about 44 x 34 cm) in one source against 110 x 82 cm in another; unresolved, and no dimension is carried in the Site paragraph.
C9. The Wood Gatherer (Le Pere Jacques) is 1881 (Milwaukee Art Museum), exhibited at the Salon of 1882. Do not date it 1882.
C10. Son grand-pere (Portrait of My Grandfather): painted 1873, exhibited at the Salon of 1874, third-class medal, Musee d'Orsay. Locke captions it "1873 (also listed as 1877)" and places it at the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Nice; the Nice attribution is wrong and 1877 is wrong.
C11. Potato Gatherers was exhibited under the official title "Saison d'Octobre: Recolte des Pommes de Terre" (October: Potato Harvest). "October" is the National Gallery of Victoria's short title for the same picture, not a separate work.
C12. He died in Paris, at his studio on rue Legendre, and was buried in the family cemetery at Damvillers. Do not say he died in Damvillers.
C13. Memorial exhibition: March-April 1885, Ecole des Beaux-Arts, more than 200 pictures. The Damvillers monument was designed by his brother Emile and surmounted by a statue by Rodin (1889), not the other way round.
C14. Les Mois Rustiques (The Months in the Country): twelve compositions planned with Theuriet, six completed. Do not claim twelve finished works.
C15. Marie Bashkirtseff died 31 October 1884 (Friday), aged 25, of tuberculosis -- six weeks before Bastien-Lepage. One source (thefreelibrary) implies 23; she was born 24 November 1858, so 25. She did not die of the same disease as him.
C16. Portrait of the Prince of Wales: 1879, Royal Collection; painted after the July 1879 summons to Marlborough House; shown at the Royal Academy in 1880. Not 1880 as a painting date.
C17. Franco-Prussian War wound: a shell burst near him in the trenches and a clod of hardened earth struck his chest (Theuriet). 2blowhards calls it a "severe chest wound" without the mechanism; do not describe it as a bullet or shrapnel wound.
C18. First Communion (La Communiante, 1875) is at the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Tournai. The "Holbein-like gravity" is critics' wording about the picture, not his own claim.
C19. "Impressionism corrected, sweetened and adapted to the taste of the crowd" is Zola's phrase about his work, not Bastien-Lepage's self-description. Keep the attribution to Zola.
C20. No modern biography exists. The three principal biographies are all 19th-century (Fourcaud 1885, Theuriet 1892, Cartwright 1894); the only comprehensive modern study is Feldman's unpublished 1973 NYU dissertation. The 2007 Musee d'Orsay exhibition catalogue (Lemoine and Lobstein) is the modern scholarly reference. Do not cite a modern biography; there is not one.
C21. Name and titles: born Jules Bastien; he added his mother's maiden name Lepage after enrolling at the Ecole. "Les Foins" is one work with many English titles (Haymaking, Haymakers, The Hayfield, The Hay); "Potato Gatherers", "October" and "Saison d'Octobre" are one work. Do not count them as separate pictures.
C22. The Gutenberg memoir text (eBook #71623) is the 1892 English translation of Theuriet by Mathilde Blind and others; the Clausen and Sickert essays and the Blind study of Bashkirtseff are included in the same volume. Quotes Q1, Q3-Q6, Q8, Q12, Q13 are from that translation and are translations from French, not Bastien-Lepage's English.
C23. Les Foins dimensions: 181 x 199 cm (majority, including the Orsay) vs 160 x 195 cm (Eclectic Light). The study carries 181 x 199 with the conflict flagged; the main leg's notes use 160 x 195.
C24. Diogenes: 1873 (German and Japanese Wikipedia) vs 1877 (English and French Wikipedia, Eclectic Light). Unresolved; list both.
C25. London Bootblack location: Musee des Arts Decoratifs (French Wikipedia) vs Musee d'Orsay (English Wikipedia gallery). Unresolved; the Orsay holds 13 works, so a transfer is possible.
C26. Snow Effect, Damvillers location: Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco (Eclectic Light) vs Legion of Honor, San Francisco (English Wikipedia gallery). The Legion of Honor is part of the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco, so these are compatible.
C27. Viardot's Masterpieces of French Art (1882) gives his birth year as 1850. Wrong; 1848 is certain.
