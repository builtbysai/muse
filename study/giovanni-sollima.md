# Giovanni Sollima: Study Notes

**Site:** Giovanni Sollima (b. 24 October 1962, Palermo, Sicily, Italy; living), Italian cellist and composer; post-minimalist style fusing minimalism, rock, electronic and Mediterranean and ethnic music on a classical base [C29498]; teacher at the Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia since 2010, where he holds the title of Academician [C29499]; founder and artistic director of the 100 Cellos ensemble (2012, with Enrico Melozzi) [C29500]; film scores for Peter Greenaway, John Turturro, Carlos Saura and Marco Tullio Giordana [C29501]; Anner Bylsma Award 2018 [C29502]; plays a Francesco Ruggeri cello, Cremona 1679 [C29503]; his music played by Yo-Yo Ma, Riccardo Muti, Gidon Kremer with the Kremerata Baltica, Patti Smith and Stefano Bollani [C29504]. His practice is the cello taken out of the concert hall and made to survive anywhere: on a glacier, in a desert, underwater, in a rock band, in a hundred-strong open orchestra, and inside a lost Vivaldi concerto rebuilt from a single surviving part.

**Doorway:** Hop from the Abel Selaocoe study: Selaocoe ends his study at the man he waited five hours for in Manchester -- the Sicilian cellist-composer who showed him the percussiveness of the cello and that improvisation could be free rein rather than another type of learned behaviour, and whose L.B. Files sits on Selaocoe's own album as the most lyrical thing he has ever come across. Selaocoe is the player who took the cello as his voice, his church as his orchestra, and his township as his concert hall; Sollima is the teacher who gave him the licence, the player who took the cello as a rock instrument, a folk instrument and a voice, and Sicily as a room where Norman, Arab, Greek and Spanish cultures reverberate. Where Selaocoe brings a place and lets the piece become that place, Sollima brings an instrument and lets the instrument become every place it is played in -- a glacier, a desert, a water tank, an occupied theatre, a Baroque chapel. The study starts where his leaves off -- with the man Selaocoe waited for, still playing. [C29539]

Sollima was born in Palermo on 24 October 1962, into a family of musicians; his father Eliodoro Sollima was a pianist and composer, and his siblings are musicians [C29497] [C29511]. He studied cello with Giovanni Perriera and composition with his father at the Conservatorio di Palermo, graduating with highest honours -- AllMusic says he began cello with Perriera at age ten -- then studied with Antonio Janigro (cello) and Milko Kelemen (composition) [C29506]. The teachers are undisputed; which institution each belongs to is not -- Wikipedia, AllMusic and German Wikipedia assign Janigro and Kelemen to Stuttgart and Salzburg in different combinations, so the study names the teachers and does not pin the institutions. He has taught at the Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia since 2010, where he holds the title of Academician, and he also taught at the Fondazione Romanini in Brescia and is artistic director of the Societa Italiana del Violoncello [C29499].

The early catalogue is a prodigy output that turns civic fast. A Concerto grosso at fourteen (W01, 1976) opens the list; the teenage chamber cluster that follows -- Musica per sonar a piu stromenti dialogando fra antica et moderna (1979), Notturno (1980), Maithuna (1981), Orgy (1982), Tantra (1983) (W03) -- already names the lifelong method in its first title: a dialogue between ancient and modern. Then the two memorial works that anchor the Sicilian identity in documentary fact rather than decoration: Segno (1992), in memory of the victims of the Capaci massacre, and Anno uno (1993), in memory of the victims of the via D'Amelio massacre (W04) -- the 1992 mafia bombings that killed Judges Falcone and Borsellino. Violoncelles, vibrez! (1993, W05) is the first work to be recorded -- the title piece of Mario Brunello's 1998 CD -- and it already holds the multiple-cello texture that becomes a signature: two cellos against a string ensemble, later six cellos, later a hundred.

The middle period is where the signature forms arrive, one per year at the turn of the century. Spasimo (1995, W06) is a 33-minute span for solo cello and an amplified ensemble of cello, viola, violin, synthesizer and percussion, written to celebrate the restoration of Palermo's Church of Santa Maria dello Spasimo -- duration as a structural device, amplification inside a classical scoring, and a site-specific occasion in Palermo. Aquilarco (1997-98, W07) is the full method in one object: text by Christopher Knowles, electric bass and guitar among the forces, American players from the Bang on a Can All-Stars and Steve Reich ensembles, Robert Wilson's voice in the interludes, six months in New York in 1997-98, recorded in Sicily and New York, released 1998 on PolyGram's Point Music at Philip Glass's invitation -- and the aquilarco itself, an instrument of his own invention. Intersong I (1999, W08) and Alone (1999, W09) are the solo-cello laboratory in compact form -- an obsessively circling toccata, Near-Eastern tunings -- and Intersong I is still in active repertory twenty-six years later, reworked as a 2025 version for Wild Love. Viaggio in Italia (2000, W10) sets Michelangelo, Giordano Bruno and Borromini, puts a sampler and a bass guitar inside a string orchestra, and carries him to his Carnegie Hall debut with the Lark Quartet. The year 2000 is also when screen and stage become a parallel career: his music in Giordana's I cento passi, and Casanova as a ballet for Karole Armitage (W11). Ellis Island (2002, W12), an opera in two acts with a libretto by Roberto Alajmo, premiered at the Teatro Massimo in Palermo with pop singer Elisa as the protagonist. Terra Aria and Concerto Rotondo (2003, W13) become Lasse Gjertsen's two-part Daydream video of 9 May 2007 -- multiple cello lines superimposed on the lone cello figure onstage, driving rhythmic bowing, percussive left-hand pizzicato, rhythms slapped from the left-hand strings, eerie harmonics, a surprise ending -- the solo cello as a one-man orchestra, and the video as a distribution form. Caravaggio (2003, W14), for solo cello and live electronics, brings the electronics into the solo practice and the Baroque painting into the instrument workshop: a tenor violin made for him after the violins in Caravaggio's paintings.

The second middle period scales everything up. We Were Trees (2008, W15), a duo album with Monika Leskovar, and Astrolabioanima (2008), an improvisation album with saxophonist Alessandro Gandola, make the duo and the recorded improvisation into forms. Then 100 Cellos (W16, founded 2012 with Enrico Melozzi [C29512]): born as an experiment during the occupation of Rome's historic Teatro Valle, a dozen cellists asked why not an orchestra of a hundred cellos, and three days of concerts became 72 hours of continuous music with flash mobs in town -- professionals, conservatory students and amateurs aged 8 to 70, arrangements by Sollima and Melozzi, repertoire from Bach and German Baroque to an arrangement of Pink Floyd's The Wall. Antidotum Tarantulae XXI (2014, W17), a concerto for two cellos commissioned by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra, premiered with Riccardo Muti conducting and Yo-Yo Ma and Sollima as soloists [C29513] -- about 25 minutes in five interlinked sections without pause, the tarantella as a rhythmic engine at orchestral scale, Muti calling the music "very earthy, from the Earth, dealing with the roots of civilization", the premiere review honestly mixed. The exact premiere date is disputed between sources (see What is NOT settled); the commission, the forces and the year are not.

The recent period turns from invention to reconstruction. L.B. Files (W18), a four-movement piece for cello and string orchestra written in 2005 as a homage to Luigi Boccherini -- I. Concerto; II. Igiul; III. Frandango del Signor Bouqueriny; IV. Boccherinero -- was recorded at the Liszt Academy in Budapest on 8 April 2017 with the Amsterdam Sinfonietta under Candida Thompson, and the Australian Chamber Orchestra tour material calls it his signature composition; its first two movements are the piece Selaocoe recorded on Hymns of Bantu. Al-Bunduqiyya - The Lost Concerto (W19, Warner Classics, released 18 April 2025, 28 tracks, 1:14:09) rebuilds a lost Vivaldi concerto from its one surviving part: Il Concerto Perduto was composed by Sollima in 2021, inspired by the only surviving fragment -- the orchestral viola part -- of Vivaldi's Cello Concerto in E minor, RV 787 "Per Teresa" [C29514], following and "breathing" the viola part "almost maniacally -- very precisely even in the number of measures of rest" so that "the form and duration of a Vivaldian concerto remained intact". The album frames it with complete Vivaldi concertos, a Tartini transcription, Cypriot Kartsilamades and Albanian Moj e Bukura More folk arrangements, five solo improvisations, and "When We Were Trees: The Family Tree" [C29515]. Wild Love (W20, Arcana, 2026), with Il Pomo d'Oro and Vanni Moretto, turns Frank Zappa's mistaken ancestry into a historiographic game: Frank Zappa's 1984 album dedicated to his supposed ancestor Francesco Zappa, the 18th-century Milanese composer, becomes an album juxtaposing Francesco's galant compositions with Frank's contemporary works, bridged by Sollima's own pieces -- Intersong I and Aquilarco Nos. 5 ("Leonardo's Ornithopterus") and 7 ("Rotating Dance") in 2025 versions, and his arrangement of Frank Zappa's "Wild Love" [C29516] -- performed live at Stresa on 2 September 2026, Florence on 17 October 2026 and Rimini on 12 November 2026. The Bach Suites recording (Brilliant Classics, November 2024, 3 discs) interleaves all six suites with sixteen extra tracks including his own Aparecidos (2020), Jook-urr-pa (2019) and Allemanda for hay cello (2020) [C29517] -- Bach as the lifelong reference, with his own pieces as commentary. And the teaching strand continues into 2025: the Orsolina28 residency, 28 October-2 November 2025, "Traditions and Innovations", had him guiding an ensemble of young cellists in sound research and musical experimentation [C29518].

## M1 -- The cello as a rock instrument, a folk instrument, and a voice

Sollima treats the cello as a plugged-in, percussive, sung instrument rather than a polite classical voice. Aquilarco (1997-98) blends chamber music with rock -- electric bass and guitar in the instrumentation, American players drawn from the Bang on a Can All-Stars and Steve Reich ensembles, recorded in Sicily and New York after six months in New York [C29530]. At Festival Maribor on 5 September 2011 he played his own arrangements of Nirvana's "Dumb" and "Polly" alongside L.B. Files, the reviewer noting he "issued a challenge to all who think the cello is for the faint of heart" and played "with a rock 'n roll attitude that mixes Mick Jagger's loose lips with Edvard Munch's screams and howls"; the same concert's encore had Sollima and Monika Leskovar playing "a silly but highly diverting exercise in soul and 16th notes ... on a single cello". The press label follows him -- "the rock star of the cello", "the world's coolest musician" -- and he deflects it with a Corelli anecdote: Corelli "really was like a rock star. When he was on the stage, the audience would scream so loudly that he would have to play a D and a D sharp together very loudly to make them be quiet! But I am not a rock star like Corelli". The watchable behaviour is a cello that growls, thumps and sings over a rock backline, and a performer who walks around the stage while playing. The extended-technique vocabulary underneath it is documented in detail: clothespins pinching the bridge for muted resonances, percussive sounds produced with a pencil, left-hand pizzicato, col legno, glissando, double and triple chords, fast string crossings, and asking the cellist to sing along in a drone in Lamentatio; in Daydream, rhythms achieved by slapping the left-hand strings; multiple tracks superimposed on the lone figure onstage [C29519] [C29520]. The invented and made instruments extend the same idea: the aquilarco, a tenor violin after Caravaggio's paintings, the ice cello, the hay cello, a 5-string soprano viol played with a Baroque bow [C29521]. Seen in W07, W09, W13, W14, W18, W20.

**Takeaway:** the cello is not a polite voice; it is a plugged-in, percussive, sung instrument that growls and thumps. [C29530]

## M2 -- Post-minimalism built from Mediterranean material

The critical frame is Kyle Gann's: Sollima's music "is influenced by minimalism, with his compositions often featuring modal melodies and repetitive structures", but "characterized by a more diverse and eclectic approach to material than the early American minimalist composers", so Gann calls him a postminimalist. The material is Mediterranean and Sicilian, and the agency bio gives the formula plainly: "minimalism, rock, electronic and ethnic music from all over the Mediterranean area, with echoes of ancient and baroque music, on the basis of a thorough classical training" [C29498]. Sollima's own account: "We Sicilians are the product of Norman, Arab, Greek and Spanish cultures. My name, for example, has Arabic roots. ... There is a room in my head in which dialects, languages, scales and ancient songs reverberate." The instances: Canti rocciosi (2001), commissioned by I Suoni delle Dolomiti, for orchestra, choir and video projection, setting Dante, Hemingway, Buzzati and street rhymes in Sicilian, Italian and Ladin -- its 2001 premiere played "as quickly as we could" ahead of a forecast thunderstorm, "and then ran for cover". Viaggio in Italia (2000) sets Michelangelo, Giordano Bruno and Borromini, opening and closing on Frederick II of Sicily. Africa (1992), a quintet for two violins, viola and two cellos, treats the Mediterranean as a crossing-point, not a backdrop. The watchable behaviour is short modal cells repeated until they turn into dance rhythms, then cut by a sudden folk tune or Baroque quotation. The folk strand never leaves; it just gets better documented -- the Capaci and via D'Amelio memorials (1992-93), I cento passi (2000), the tarantella in Antidotum Tarantulae XXI (2014), Sicilian, Cypriot and Albanian folk on Al-Bunduqiyya (2025). Seen in W04, W10, W17, W19. [C29541]

**Takeaway:** the repetition is minimalism; the material is the Mediterranean, and the cut is always a folk tune or a Baroque quotation. [C29541]

## M3 -- Improvisation inside composed frames: free rein, not learned behaviour

Sollima is a performer-composer who improvises in performance and treats the score as a frame to be escaped. Classic Melbourne: "Sollima is utterly fearless in his approach to performance. In a world where classical musicians obsess over perfection and wrong notes, he dares to take the musical risks that others will not", and he is "a great proponent of the lost art of improvisation". At the 2008 Sounds of the Dolomites edition he joined Ernst Reijseger -- "a cellist and a brother" -- having rehearsed a programme in advance, then "when we actually got to the concert location we decided it all needed to change. ... There was lots of joking with the audience, lots of improvisation, and all around madness. By the end they were all on their feet and dancing." The Oriente Occidente programme note describes his improvisation as "a game of recording, assembly and recomposition" using the D-Touch -- coloured wooden cubes, an electronic interface by Enrico Costanza and Simon Shelley. And the Selaocoe testimony is the mechanism stated by its beneficiary: improvisation as "free rein rather than another type of learned behaviour", learned from a man with "this enviable way of not caring about the rules" [C29526]. His own credo: "If you ask me to quit cello and keep composing, I would quit all music completely. If you ask me to quit composing and keep playing cello, I would be happy. For me, everything is from the cello. ... It is my home." The watchable behaviour is a set that visibly changes mid-concert: a solo cello line that starts as written melody and dissolves into live looping and re-assembly. Seen in W15, W19, and the 2008 Dolomites concert. [C29542]

**Takeaway:** the score is a frame to be escaped, and the escape is the point -- free rein, not learned behaviour. [C29542]

## M4 -- The 100 Cellos method: a massed, open, self-teaching cello orchestra

Founded in 2012 with Enrico Melozzi during the occupation of Rome's Teatro Valle, 100 Cellos is not an ensemble in the ordinary sense; it is a social instrument [C29529]. The method, from the MyCello interview and the Istituto Italiano di Cultura Abu Dhabi page: it is a "social orchestra" -- professionals, conservatory students and amateurs, normally aged 8 to 70, from Italy and abroad (Japan, USA, Turkey, Germany, Holland, Slovenia, Spain); anyone registers via a form a few months before a reunion, stating level, cello history, "her own magic move on the cello", a photo with the instrument, and preferred section; parts are assigned by request, with flexibility expected; arrangements are always prepared by Sollima and Melozzi; reunions last three days -- two days of rehearsals "until midnight/midnight and a half" and a final evening concert, plus small pop-up concerts in the host town. A core of about twenty regulars "always teach their 'tricks' to those in need with great humility". The repertoire runs "from ancient music to modern and popular rhythms"; the project has played Rome, Milan, Budapest, Turin, Ravenna, Lucca and Dubai (Expo 2020, 25-26 March 2022), and staged cello flash mobs in Milan. The origin story, in Sollima's own telling: asked to do three days of concerts at the occupied Teatro Valle, he called other cellists, "and the idea of a large gathering evolved... we played 72 hours of music. We did flash mobs in town along with smaller solo performances." Teaching is the same mechanism at smaller scale: Santa Cecilia since 2010 [C29499], and the Orsolina28 residency of 2025, guiding young cellists in sound research [C29518]. The massed-cello idea was already in place in 1993 -- Violoncelles, vibrez! for two cellos and strings -- and the six-cello La Sostanza dei Sogni (2015) sits between; 100 Cellos is the 1993 texture scaled into a social form [C29512]. Seen in W05, W16.

**Takeaway:** a hundred cellos is not an orchestra; it is a room that teaches itself, for three days, until midnight. [C29529]

## M5 -- Extreme instruments and extreme sites: the instrument must survive the place

Sollima repeatedly puts the cello where it does not belong, and lets the place change the sound. The ice cello: built by American artist Tim Linhart from water of the Presena glacier (2,800 m, Trentino), played by Sollima on a 1,500 km tour from the Alps to the Mediterranean -- the route "the same distance, in reverse, as the route taken by migrants who, fleeing lands without water, brave the Mediterranean heading north" -- ending with the instrument's release and melting in the Mediterranean; the N-Ice Cello documentary (2021, dir. Corrado Bungaro) follows the journey and the world premiere at Palermo's Politeama of Sollima's original score for ice cello and string orchestra. Earlier, in March 2007, Sollima with Monika Leskovar and Bungaro performed at 3,200 m in an igloo theatre on the Val Senales glacier on Linhart's glacial instruments -- a separate event from the 2018 tour, not the same one. The Dolomites, in his own words: "You get a completely different feedback, and you have to create the acoustic yourself by deciding where exactly to play. ... The public is extremely close to you; they could almost touch the instruments. There are no dressing rooms. It's just you and the audience. The music is laid bare." And the standard bio line, repeated across sources: he has played "in the Sahara desert, underwater and with an ice cello", on "ancient, oriental, electric and inventive instruments" -- the Sahara performance and the underwater performance in a Sicilian gebbia (irrigation water tank) among them. The watchable behaviour is an instrument visibly melting, sweating, or fighting wind and altitude while it is played. Seen in W13, W14, and the ice-cello events. [C29524] [C29531]

**Takeaway:** put the cello where it does not belong, and let the place change the sound. [C29531]

## M6 -- Electronics and live processing: the cello doubled by its own ghost

The electronics mechanism is two-sided: live processing that multiplies the cello in performance, and an anti-perfection recording ethic that treats the take as one unrepeatable moment. Caravaggio, originally composed in 2003 for solo cello and live electronics, is the first side; Aquilarco (1998) already mixes acoustic cello with electric instruments and electronica, "drawing upon European and especially Mediterranean folk, Middle Eastern music, electronica, and more"; the D-Touch improvisation system is live recording, assembly and recomposition of cello sound through an electronic interface. The second side is the 2021 Bach Suites recording: the complete suites "recorded on an analog medium, without cuts or edits in the final version" -- "I think of this recording as a photograph of a moment, capturing one possible version of a piece. ... This is not a recording that is the definitive version of a work. It is a snapshot." The same ethic runs through the Daydream video (2007), where multiple cello lines are superimposed on the lone figure onstage -- the cello doubled by its own ghost, on tape as well as on stage. Seen in W07, W13, W14, and the Bach Suites recording. [C29532]

**Takeaway:** the electronics multiply the cello, and the recording is a photograph of one moment, not a definitive version. [C29532]

## M7 -- History as material: homage, montage, and the lost concerto

Sollima raids the past and re-stages it, rather than quoting it politely. L.B. Files (2005) harvests Boccherini's melodies and re-cuts them as a "mini-movie" of his life -- the Warner booklet calls it "Sollima's narrative tribute to cellist Luigi Boccherini ... that imagines his life as a mini-movie, and leans into stylistic melange that's become the hallmark of Sollima's creative voice" [C29527]. The Costanzi revival: he "brought to light the eighteenth-century musician Giovanni Battista Costanzi", recording his sonatas and symphonies for cello and basso continuo for Glossa. Wild Love (Arcana, 2026) bridges Frank Zappa and the 18th-century Milanese composer Francesco Zappa -- Frank's mistaken ancestor, subject of his 1984 album -- with Sollima's own 2025 versions of Intersong I and Aquilarco Nos. 5 and 7 and his arrangement of Frank Zappa's "Wild Love" [C29516]. Al-Bunduqiyya (2025) imagines how a Vivaldi concerto might have unfolded "had Vivaldi lived to complete it", rebuilding RV 787 from its one surviving viola part [C29514]. The habit is early: Reperto n. 12 "from a fragment by Schubert" (1998), J. Beuys Song (2001), Pasolini fragments (1998). The watchable behaviour is a Baroque ensemble playing Zappa, and a "lost" Vivaldi concerto completed live on stage. The evolution of the whole career is the same move at larger scale: early invention (an invented instrument, the aquilarco, 1997-98) becomes recent reconstruction (a lost Vivaldi concerto, a Boccherini homage, a Zappa ancestry game) -- the method is the same, fill the gap with your own material, but the gap is now historical rather than instrumental [C29523]. Seen in W07, W18, W19, W20. [C29534]

**Takeaway:** the past is not quoted; it is raided, re-staged, and completed on stage. [C29534]

## M8 -- Collaboration as method: film, dance, theatre, and the opera house

Sollima's catalogue is built through other people's stages. Film scores for Peter Greenaway (The Tulse Luper Suitcases, Nightwatching), John Turturro (Evidence for a Sicilian Tragedy), Carlos Saura (La Jota), Marco Tullio Giordana (One Hundred Steps, The Best Youth), and Lasse Gjertsen, whose two-part Daydream video set Terra Aria and Concerto Rotondo and circulated widely [C29522]. Dance and theatre: Casanova (2000, choreography Karole Armitage), J. Beuys Song (2001, Carolyn Carlson), work with Bob Wilson, Peter Stein, Anatoly Vasiliev and Alessandro Baricco. Opera: Ellis Island (2002, Teatro Massimo Palermo, libretto Roberto Alajmo, with singer Elisa); Istoria di Sant'Agata (world premiere 29 January 2023, Teatro Massimo Bellini, Catania); Elettra (Sophocles, INDA Siracusa, 2025). Civic commissions: the sound logo of Expo Milano 2015 and the inauguration of the Pieta Rondanini museum space; artistic director of the Notte della Taranta festival, Salento, 2013-2014. The big-orchestra commissions: Tempeste e Ritratti (La Scala, 2001) and Passiuni (Ravenna, 2008), both premiered by Muti, and Antidotum Tarantulae XXI, the double cello concerto premiered by the Chicago Symphony under Muti with Yo-Yo Ma and Sollima as soloists in early 2014 [C29513]. The Selaocoe tie is the same mechanism from the other side: Selaocoe's Intermusica bio names Sollima among his close collaborators [C29525], and Selaocoe's own account of waiting five hours for a lesson in Manchester ends with the licence that remade his playing [C29526]. Seen in W11, W12, W17, W18. [C29533]

**Takeaway:** the catalogue is built on other people's stages -- film, dance, theatre, opera, and another cellist's album. [C29533]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Sollima makes music the way he was raised to hear it: as a Sicilian room where Norman, Arab, Greek and Spanish cultures reverberate, and as a cello that is a home you carry into every other room. The cello is the whole orchestra -- massed in a hundred, multiplied on tape, doubled by its own ghost in electronics, sung over in a drone. The material is always the same at every scale: a short modal cell, repeated until it turns into a dance rhythm, cut by a folk tune or a Baroque quotation. The frame is always the same too: a composed structure with a door left open -- an improvisation, a revision, a reconstruction -- and the open door is where the music happens. The place is never decoration: a church restoration in Palermo, a mafia massacre in Capaci, an occupied theatre in Rome, a glacier in the Dolomites, a lost viola part in Venice -- each one is a gap, and the piece is what he puts in the gap. Early he invented the instrument to fill it; late he rebuilds the history to fill it; in between he filled it with a hundred cellists who registered on a form and taught each other their tricks until midnight. The through-line is not fusion, which would imply two finished things joined, but hospitality of a different kind from Selaocoe's: not a room opened to singers, but an instrument opened to every place it can be carried, and a past opened to every present that can complete it. [C29543]
