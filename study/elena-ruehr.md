Elena Ruehr was born in August 1963 in Ann Arbor, Michigan, the daughter of a mathematician father and an English-professor mother, both amateur musicians; the day of birth does not appear in any reliable source. When she was four, about 1967 or 1968, the family moved to the Upper Peninsula -- first to South Range, population about 300, then to Houghton when she was in fifth grade. Her father, Otto Ruehr, was a jazz pianist who went to the University of Michigan at sixteen, worked the 3pm-to-11:30pm shift at the Ford plant in Ypsilanti while studying, played in a jazz band on weekends, later worked at IBM and in the Radiation Lab, and ended as a mathematics professor at Michigan Tech; he played Gershwin after dinner every night and built his own hi-fi and reel-to-reel system in the 1950s with the complete Beethoven and Bartok string quartets, which he listened to in a mixed-up pile while his daughter sat in his study colouring -- until about fifteen she believed Bela Bartok was a girl, "Bella", who had written all of the Beethoven and Bartok quartets. Her mother was a folksinger who collected Pete Seeger and Joan Baez, taught Ruehr folk songs in the car, sang and played guitar, and taught her piano from age four; a great-grandmother had accompanied silent films in Plymouth, Michigan. Her mother became, in Ruehr's words, a "rabble-rouser feminist politician", taught at the university in the Upper Peninsula, and died in 2020, on a ventilator -- Ruehr made the decision to take her off it, and the death is the subject of her secular Requiem. Her earliest mentor was Melvin Kangas, a Finnish kantele player and composer in Houghton who tuned the family piano and taught her, from ninth or tenth grade, "how to push the boundaries of the tonal music" she was writing. She asked for drums in second grade and was told "Girls don't play drums", so she played flute until twenty; she played keyboards -- a Fender Rhodes she tuned herself -- and sang in a high-school rock band doing fifties songs, jazz and pop, a band whose name she refuses to disclose ("it's that embarrassing"). She has two older brothers, one into Led Zeppelin and heavy metal, the other, Fritz, into Morton Subotnick, the Doors and indie bands. [C29321]

She studied composition as an undergraduate at the University of Michigan, where her first and main teacher was George Balch Wilson -- who taught her to think in line and form, to "draw pictures of the piece, rather than pitches, and then put the pitches in" -- alongside Leslie Bassett and Bill Albright, with William Bolcom becoming her teacher later; she played in the university's Javanese gamelan and studied West African drumming, and turned down a touring Top-40 band offer to go to Michigan, the deciding moment being accidentally sitting in a graduate class discussing Berg's Wozzeck -- "I was just completely astounded by the fact that music could be so deep and real and complicated." She arrived at the Juilliard School in 1985 and took her M.M. in 1987, her principal teachers Vincent Persichetti and Bernard Rands; she knew Milton Babbitt but he was not her private teacher. In 1992 she took a teaching position at MIT, where she stayed until 2025 -- lecturer in Music and Theater Arts, Baker Undergraduate Teaching Award 1995 -- and she now lives in the Boston area, in Brookline, Massachusetts. She was a Guggenheim Fellow in 2014, to write Cassandra in the Temples, and a Radcliffe Institute fellow in 2008, where she wrote Averno; she was the Boston Modern Orchestra Project's first composer-in-residence, 2000-2005, premiering Shimmer, Sky Above Clouds, Ladder to the Moon and the opera Toussaint Before the Spirits with them, and she is composer-in-residence with Lincoln's Symphony Orchestra, in its third year in the 2025-26 season, with Tightrope premiered 25 April 2025 and the Violin Concerto No. 3, "Three Pieces for Anton", as her third and final residency piece. She is married to Seward Rutkove, a doctor, and has one daughter, Sophie, a climate scientist and pianist, for whose twenty-fifth birthday she wrote twenty-five short piano pieces during the pandemic. [C29322]

Her working method has not changed since her twenties, and she states it plainly. She composes five hours a day, noon to 5pm, every day -- about thirty pieces in the two pandemic years -- and her guiding philosophy, formed in her twenties with Mozart as the model, is that "the idea is that the surface be simple, the structure complex." The process is: sit in an emotion, improvise at the piano until she captures the sound, then write it down -- "It's all about turning emotion into sound. As far as I'm concerned, that's my job." And the material is anything she likes: "Anything that I like, I will just incorporate or steal" -- medieval to contemporary, world music, pop -- and the references need not be recognised by the listener: "if they have the references, it enriches it. But, if they don't, the emotional thing is hopefully contained in it." [C29323]

The twenty works inspected run 1986-2025 and trace one practice holding its credo steady while the borrowed structures get bigger. Phase 1, the Juilliard and early MIT years, 1986-1995 (W01-W03): octatonic and serial tools worn lightly inside a lyrical, audience-friendly surface -- a Babbitt hexachord in Of Water and Clouds, combinatorial five-note sets in Sky Above Clouds and Shimmer -- and the credo is fixed early. ## M1 -- Combinatorial diatonic pitch sets: five notes doing the work of twelve

The signature device of her first decade, and the one her whole harmonic thinking turns on. Sky Above Clouds (1993) is built from two five-note series -- do re fa sol la, and re fa sol la do, transpositions of each other, both fitting inside a major scale. Ruehr calls the method "cyclical, basically serialism, but instead of being a 12-note series, it might be a five-note series." The pitch material is combinatorial diatonic: the two sets interlock, and a little ostinato motive is "constantly there. It morphs and it changes, but it's always there. And to me, it's the clouds repeating." The piece was written in graduate school under the spell of Steve Reich and John Adams -- "just a little bit minimal" -- and the clouds are Georgia O'Keeffe's huge Sky Above Clouds, which Ruehr saw as a child at the Art Institute of Chicago. Shimmer (1995) runs the same device harder: a ten-note set built from two combinatorial five-note series, a whole step apart, and taking the lower notes yields one resultant melody and the higher notes another -- the Steve Reich resultant-melody idea -- "and so the whole piece is full of canons and counterpoint, and then some of the ornamentation is a little Vivaldi-esque." The mechanism is serial discipline without serial sound: the sets generate the counterpoint, the surface stays diatonic and singable, and the ear hears clouds and Vivaldi while the structure does the counting. The watchable behaviour is repetition that never quite repeats -- the same five notes, transposed, inverted, canonised, always recognisable and never identical. Seen in W02, W03. [C29325]

**Takeaway:** take five notes, transpose them inside one scale, and let the canon do the composing. [C29325]

## M2 -- The borrowed structure: a novel, a painting, a myth as form

Ruehr does not invent forms; she borrows them, whole, and fills them with feeling. The borrowed object is always something with its own structure already built in. Toussaint Before the Spirits (2003) takes Madison Smartt Bell's novel All Souls' Rising -- the historical Toussaint Louverture raised into an archetypal figure, as in vodou religion ancestors become gods -- and the opera's form follows the novel's arc. Cloud Atlas (2011) takes David Mitchell's novel, whose prose style changes by era, "from flowery 19th century prose to futuristic minimalism", and Ruehr's musical language likewise "weaves through eras, from the lively Baroque dance that appears several times to grieving post-Romantic melody" -- all of it generated "from a single musical kernel, a pair of perfect fifths, a half-step apart", with the solo cellist as one of the novel's characters, the goddess Somni-451. The Thrilling Adventures of Lovelace and Babbage (2023) takes Sydney Padua's graphic novel and builds four adventures, "each episode its own aesthetic and design parameters". The O'Keeffe triptych -- Sky Above Clouds (1993), Ladder to the Moon (2003), Summer Days (2013) -- takes three paintings as three jumping-off points, ten years apart. The mechanism is structure borrowed, not story: she does not set the plot, she takes the object's skeleton -- the novel's eras, the painting's clouds, the graphic novel's episodes -- and lets her own emotion move through it. The watchable behaviour is a familiar container arriving with unfamiliar contents: a cello concerto that changes style by century, an opera that changes design by episode, a triptych that returns to the same painter ten years later. Seen in W05, W12, W18, W02, W06. [C29326]

**Takeaway:** borrow the whole structure -- the novel, the painting, the myth -- and fill it with your own feeling. [C29326]

## M3 -- Quotation as engine: the old aria as ostinato seed

Quotation in Ruehr is never decoration; it is the motor. Bel Canto, String Quartet No. 5 (composed 2009, premiered 2010), is built on Ann Patchett's novel Bel Canto, about an opera singer held hostage, and Ruehr chose four pieces the singer sings in the book and quotes them as jumping-off points. Puccini's "Vissi d'arte" -- "I just take the first note, and put it down an octave, and I make this little ostinato out of it" -- becomes the first movement, "Rebels in the Vents". Dvorak's "Song to the Moon" from Rusalka is played in pizzicato as the tune under new layers, in the movement "Carmen Studies Grammar" -- "to me, the Dvorak is the grammar" -- a Bizet and Carmen expectation deliberately answered with Rusalka instead. "All of the pieces in that quartet refer back to the source material. But sometimes it's altered enough that you can't tell. Sometimes it's very, very, very close to the source material." She calls it "one of the only pieces I have that's really literally based on something extra-musical in a way that matters." The same engine drives the silent-film score Manhattan Trade School for Girls (2007): three ragtime pieces by women composers -- Adaline Shepherd's "Pickles and Peppers" and "Wireless Rag" and May Aufderheide's "Richmond Rag", all published 1906-1910 -- fitted to the film's closing credits, chosen "partly for their contrasts and partly because they have appealing themes that are not difficult to sing". And in Cloud Atlas the kernel itself is a quotation of a kind: two perfect fifths a half-step apart, generating whole eras of style. The mechanism is the found fragment as seed: one note dropped an octave, one tune in pizzicato, one ragtime chorus -- and the piece grows from it, the quotation sometimes close enough to recognise, sometimes altered past recognition, always doing structural work. The watchable behaviour is the ear catching something it knows inside something it does not: a Puccini aria turned into an ostinato, a Dvorak melody hiding under new harmony, a 1906 rag closing a 1911 film. Seen in W10, W09, W12. [C29327]

**Takeaway:** take one note of the old aria, drop it an octave, and let it grow the whole piece. [C29327]

## M4 -- Response-to-a-model: answering the quartet, not imitating it

Ruehr's quartets answer their models the way a letter answers a letter. String Quartet No. 4 (2005), commissioned by the Cypress String Quartet, was written as a deliberate response to two named models -- Mozart's "Dissonant" Quartet and Beethoven's Quartet Op. 59 No. 3. The Cypress asked her to write it "in response to Beethoven, Opus 59, No. 3 and the Mozart 'Dissonant'", and MIT News heard the result as "shifting light-dark moods and textures; great dancing rhythms; and, best of all, aria-like songs." The Radcliffe Institute profile names the device plainly: "continuing historical traditions, sometimes directly influenced by specific works" -- a standing Ruehr method. Shimmer does the same move with Vivaldi: frustrated drafts sounded like "Beethoven's Fifth with a drumset", and then she heard Vivaldi's Four Seasons ("Spring") over a shopping-mall sound system in Chestnut Hill Mall, Newton, and decided to "get into Vivaldi's head" -- to write a piece that looks like Vivaldi on the surface but is constructed modern underneath, with combinatorial five-note sets and Reich resultant melodies doing the building. "You're not supposed to go: aahhh, oh, Vivaldi. Obviously." Sky Above Clouds does it with O'Keeffe, whom Ruehr calls "minimalism made as a visual object", and says O'Keeffe's representational courage gave her the courage to write narrative, emotional music. The mechanism is the model as sparring partner: the old work sets the terms -- a dissonance, a spring, a sky -- and the new work answers it in its own voice, keeping the shape and changing the substance. The watchable behaviour is a piece that feels familiar before you can say why: the quartet that moves like Mozart and Beethoven without quoting either, the string orchestra that shimmers like Vivaldi without ever being Vivaldi. Seen in W07, W03, W02. [C29328]

**Takeaway:** answer the old quartet, don't copy it -- keep its shape, change its substance. [C29328]

## M5 -- The single day: Cassandra's thirteen hours

Cassandra in the Temples (2014) is Ruehr's most concentrated formal device: a whole opera inside a single day. The a cappella opera -- 13 movements, about 40 minutes, for Roomful of Teeth alone, SSAATTBB, no instruments except the human voice -- unfolds over the course of one day, with each hour tied to events in Cassandra's life, death and mythic afterlife. The temples are both Vitruvian and anatomical: the space between the temples of the head houses thoughts and memories. Snakes recur as a motif in four guises -- sacred healing serpents, monstrous sea serpents, historic map markers designating terra incognita, and the dormant yogic serpent that aids meditation. The third movement, "Whispering of the Snakes", is "constructed entirely using sibilance (stressed 's' sounds), unlike the vowel sounds librettists often use to carry sound forward". The sixth movement turns on homophonic wordplay -- "Lie here", "Lyre?" and "Liar!" -- conveying the difficulty of reconstructing fragmentary texts. The vocal writing uses Roomful of Teeth's full technique palette -- throat singing, Bulgarian women's-choir singing, vocal percussion, microphones -- and as the ensemble's director Brad Wells put it, "The voice becomes an oboe; now, it's a clarinet. It has a broader range than we give it credit for." The origin is Al Gore's An Inconvenient Truth: Cassandra predicting disaster no one believes, mapped onto climate change. An a cappella opera had been Ruehr's dream for years; hearing Roomful of Teeth made it real. The mechanism is the single container with a clock in it: one day, thirteen hours, one voice that becomes every instrument, and the prophecy nobody believes arriving hour by hour until it is too late. The watchable behaviour is eight singers becoming an orchestra with nothing but their throats, the day ticking past in movements, the sibilance hissing like the snakes it names. Seen in W14. [C29329]

**Takeaway:** give the prophet one day, thirteen hours, and eight throats -- and let the disaster arrive on schedule. [C29329]

## M6 -- The poet's voice kept intact: setting as listening

Ruehr sets poetry the way she borrows structures: she keeps the poet's voice intact and lets her music move inside it. Averno (written 2008 at Radcliffe, premiered 3 April 2011) sets Louise Gluck's 2006 volume Averno -- twelve poems "selected in order" per the Washington Chorus press release (the Avie booklet says eleven; the count differs between sources and is flagged, not resolved) -- retelling Demeter and Persephone, the cantata changing perspective with the poetry, "from the personal to the collective, from myth to history", in "long lyrical lines and rhythmic drives, combined with... references to the great choral music of Gluck, Bach, Beethoven, and Mahler". Ruehr met Gluck in May 2007 and chose the cantata form because Gluck's poetry "masterfully combines a sense of personal intimacy and collective utterance". Songs for Stephen (released 2 May 2025, Avie AV2672) is the same method across twenty-five years with one singer: five cycles, each keeping its poet's voice intact -- Elizabeth Alexander's Five Men, Black men speaking across two centuries about freedom; Rainer Maria Rilke's Lied, in German; Richard Blanco's Travel Songs; Adrienne Rich's Wonderful Bears; Langston Hughes's Lullabies and Spring Songs, some of them recorded as far back as August 1999. The poets she sets, per the Radcliffe profile, are Gluck, Hughes, Rich, Sappho, Mary Ellen Solt and Elizabeth Spires -- and her first Sappho settings date to when she was nineteen. The mechanism is setting as listening: the poem's voice leads, the music follows, and the cycle's unity comes from the poet, not from the composer imposing a shape. The watchable behaviour is a singer carrying one poet's voice for twenty minutes, then another's, the music changing its gait with each -- the Alexander cycle speaking, the Hughes cycle lulling, the Rilke cycle singing in German. Seen in W11, W20. [C29330]

**Takeaway:** let the poet keep her voice, and let the music walk at her pace. [C29330]

## M7 -- The feminist cut: ending on the opened eyes

Eve (2014) is a cantata that changes one thing about the oldest story in the book, and the change is the whole piece. The text is Genesis 3, but Ruehr cuts it short: instead of the expulsion, she ends on "and their eyes were opened." The characters are Eve, the serpent, and the voice of God, sung by the choir -- solo soprano, solo baritone, chorus and orchestra -- and the piece was commissioned by the Cantata Singers and premiered 8 November 2014 at Jordan Hall, Boston, opening their fifty-first season alongside two Bach cantatas. Ruehr, a secular humanist asked for sacred text, read Genesis 3 at twelve "like text, not with the religious context", and approached it with "Miltonian emphasis on the choice to gain knowledge rather than the forfeiture of paradise" -- a feminist re-envisioning. The Boston Musical Intelligencer hears "webs of slithery lines" under the serpent's insistence; the Boston Globe calls it "a reflective fable, thoughtful rather than fierce". The mechanism is the cut as argument: one ending removed, one line kept, and the story's moral inverts -- the fall becomes the opening, the punishment becomes the knowledge, and the choir that would have pronounced the expulsion instead pronounces the eyes opening. The watchable behaviour is the audience waiting for the expulsion that never comes, and hearing the piece end, instead, on the moment of seeing. Seen in W15. [C29331]

**Takeaway:** cut the expulsion, keep the opened eyes, and let the ending argue. [C29331]

## M8 -- Grief made formal: the ventilator requiem

The Requiem (written 2020, premiered November 2021) is the most personal mechanism in the catalogue, and the most exact. It is a secular Mass -- SATB soloists, SATB choir, oboe and strings, premiered at Boston's Emmanuel Church in November 2021 by Emmanuel Music -- dedicated to Ruehr's mother, Ruthann Richwine Ruehr, who died in January 2020, and to all who died in 2020 in the Covid-19 pandemic. The idea came from her husband, the physician Seward Rutkove. Ruehr took a standard Latin Requiem text in English translation and changed all the religious words -- "instead of saying Lord have mercy, I just say, oh have mercy" -- so the Mass reads as "a kind of map of the five stages of grief": denial, bargaining, sorrow, anger, acceptance, and the stages guide the form and the emotional expression. The middle movement, "Oh Have Mercy", is built on "a modern 12-tone harmonic structure" and its phrases move in and out "in a very regular way like breathing, but in this case it is the very regular sound of a person on a ventilator" -- and about two-thirds through, the regular rhythm breaks, "a freeing moment... the moment of my mother's passing." The mechanism is the hospital machine as metronome: the ventilator's regularity is the form, the break in it is the death, and the five stages are the map the music walks. The watchable behaviour is a choir breathing in strict time until the breath stops being strict -- the regularity itself becoming the grief, and its breaking becoming the release. Seen in W16. [C29332]

**Takeaway:** let the ventilator keep the time, and let the break in it be the passing. [C29332]

## M9 -- The flute as a voice

The flute is where Ruehr's vocal identity starts. Of Water and Clouds (1986), her earliest recorded piece, was written at Juilliard for her friend, the flutist Su Lian Tan, and the two played it at Alice Tully Hall; it is built from study of early Stravinsky and octatonic music, and from a six-note hexachord from Milton Babbitt -- she was reading Babbitt's Words about Music. Ruehr on the flute, in the same interview: "the flute is a conduit for lyrical and vocal writing... To me the flute is a voice" -- the seed of her vocal and lyrical identity, and of everything that follows: the quartets as narrative, the cantatas as speech, the operas as song. Jane Wang Considers the Dragonfly (2007) pushes the same idea through technology: flute with digital delay, for flutist Sarah Brady, named for Z. Jane Wang, the insect-flight expert -- the delay line is the whole electronic device, letting one flute layer against its own echo as a dragonfly's wings beat against air. No source read states the delay settings or how the flight data maps to sound, so the mechanism is stated only at this level: one flute, doubled by its own past. The mechanism is the instrument as a throat: the flute sings because it is treated as a voice, not as a machine for notes, and the delay is a second throat answering the first a fraction of a second late. The watchable behaviour is a single player sounding like two -- the melody and its echo, the wingbeat and its shadow, the voice and the voice answering itself. Seen in W01, W08. [C29333]

**Takeaway:** treat the flute as a voice, and let its echo be the second singer. [C29333]

## M10 -- Intertitles as lyrics: the silent film scored by its own words

Manhattan Trade School for Girls (2007) is a silent-film score that lets the film write its own libretto. The 1911 film was commissioned by the National Film Preservation Foundation for Treasures III: Social Issues in American Film, 1900-1934, performed by the QX Quartet with the Winsor School Small Chorus. Ruehr's method, in her own account on the NFPF site: all lyrics are taken directly from the film's intertitles; three ragtime pieces by women composers are adapted for the final credits -- Adaline Shepherd's "Pickles and Peppers" and "Wireless Rag" and May Aufderheide's "Richmond Rag", all published 1906-1910, chosen "partly for their contrasts and partly because they have appealing themes that are not difficult to sing"; and the girls' chorus is combined with string quartet rather than piano, "because I wanted a different blend of sounds". The chorus sings the closing credits. The NFPF's Martin Marks calls it "a fascinating demonstration of audiovisual counterpoint." She also wrote a second silent-film score, Deschutes Driftwood, whose date and forces were not established in any source read. The mechanism is the film's own text as the song: the intertitles are the lyrics, the period ragtime is the closing voice, and the quartet-and-chorus blend replaces the piano that silent film expects. The watchable behaviour is a girls' chorus singing the words the silent screen shows -- the film speaking, at last, in the voice of the girls it depicts. Seen in W09. [C29334]

**Takeaway:** let the silent film sing its own intertitles, in the girls' own voices. [C29334]

## M11 -- Technology as character: the robot, the headset, the delay

In Ruehr's late operas, technology stops being a tool and becomes a character on stage. Cosmic Cowboy (2022), a poli-sci-fi opera with librettist Cerise Lim Jacobs for White Snake Projects, collides the Sumerian creation myth (Tiamat and Apsu) with a Mars colonisation project and a robotic space probe, Cooper -- a personification of the Philae lander on Comet 67P -- who dances a pas de deux with Tia. The technology is part of the scoring apparatus: a six-minute VR-film introduction, with audiences given Google Cardboard; an onstage industrial robotic arm portraying Cooper; CGI cutscenes; White Snake's Tutti Remote software. Ruehr on the score: "echoes of John Adams and Barber, with world music and ancient scales that sound surprisingly modern thrown into the mix." The premiere, 16-18 September 2022 at ArtsEmerson's Robert J. Orchard Stage, came after a two-year pandemic postponement of the originally announced September 2020 dates, and the opening night was plagued by technical failures -- the VR app, the supertitles, a keyboard synthesiser changing settings, a broken water main -- yet reviewers still called it "a strong premiere". The twin inspirations, per Jacobs, were the 400th anniversary of the Mayflower landing and the Philae landing -- both "lead to questions about the consequences of the colonizing impulse". The same move, smaller, is Jane Wang's digital delay: the electronics are not an effect laid over the flute, they are the second flute. The mechanism is the machine given a part to play: the robot dances, the headset shows the world, the delay answers -- and when the machine fails on opening night, the failure is part of the piece's argument about colonisation, about systems that promise and break. The watchable behaviour is a robot arm dancing with a soprano, an audience in cardboard headsets, a flute answering its own echo -- technology on stage as a fellow performer, not as a backdrop. Seen in W17, W08. [C29335]

**Takeaway:** put the robot on stage and let it dance -- the machine is a character, not a backdrop. [C29335]

## M12 -- The long collaboration as instrument

Ruehr writes for specific people, over decades, and the pieces carry that. The central collaboration is with the baritone Stephen Salters, nearly three decades long: he approached her after a premiere of hers at Boston University and asked her to write a song cycle for his debut recital; "The first note I heard Stephen sing sent a bolt of energy down my spine: this was the singer I had always wanted to write for!" He created the title role in Toussaint Before the Spirits, premiered Crafting the Bonds, and three CDs of her music were all written for him; Songs for Stephen, released 2 May 2025 on Avie, documents the collaboration, with pianists Donald Berman and David Zobel, some of the music dating back to recordings from August 1999. The Cypress String Quartet is the same kind of instrument: the relationship began through an anonymous tape solicitation recommended by the composer Dan Coleman, and the Cypress commissioned her String Quartets Nos. 4, 5 and 6 -- No. 4 in response to Mozart and Beethoven, No. 5 Bel Canto on Patchett's novel, No. 6 dedicated to the death of the Cypress's manager. QuartetES is the current standing collaboration, commissioning Nos. 9, 10 and 11, The Northern Quartets; the Borromeo Quartet recorded the Six String Quartets with the Cypress and Salters. The mechanism is the collaborator as a known quantity: she knows Salters's voice the way she knows the Cypress's sound, so the piece can be written for the person rather than for the abstract voice or quartet -- the baritone's breath, the quartet's attack, the singer's German, the cellist's reach. The watchable behaviour is a piece that fits its players like a tailored coat: the baritone singing a role made for his throat, the quartet playing a response made for its hands. Seen in W04, W05, W07, W10, W13, W20. [C29336]

**Takeaway:** write for one voice for twenty-five years, and the voice becomes the instrument. [C29336]

## M13 -- The painting triptych: ten years between panels

The O'Keeffe triptych is Ruehr's slowest mechanism: three paintings, three pieces, ten years apart, and she did not notice the spacing until someone pointed it out. Sky Above Clouds (1993) is the first panel -- Georgia O'Keeffe's huge painting of the same name, which Ruehr saw as a child at the Art Institute of Chicago, with a little ostinato motive that morphs and changes but is always there, "the clouds repeating". Ladder to the Moon (2003) is the second panel, premiered by BMOP during her residency. Summer Days (2013) is the third. The ten-year spacing was something Ruehr had not noticed until Frank Oteri pointed it out in the 2022 interview. She calls O'Keeffe "minimalism made as a visual object" and says O'Keeffe's representational courage gave her courage to write narrative, emotional music. The mechanism is the painting as a long appointment: return to the same painter a decade later, take another canvas as the jumping-off point, and let the intervening ten years of composing show in the difference between the panels -- the same ostinato thinking, the same clouds, but a different composer hearing them. The watchable behaviour is a triptych hung across thirty years: three orchestral panels, each one a decade's distance from the last, the clouds repeating and the painter staying the same. Seen in W02, W06. [C29337]

**Takeaway:** take one painter, return every ten years, and let the decade show in the panel. [C29337]

## M14 -- Climate dread as narrative: the cello against the piano

The Second Sonata for Cello and Piano (composed 2024, premiered 13 March 2025 at Kerrytown Concert House, Ann Arbor) is Ruehr's climate piece, and its mechanism is a character against an environment. Written for the cellist Sophie Shao and the pianist Amy I-Lin Cheng -- Curtis and Yale roommates, Shao commissioning -- it is, in Ruehr's programme note, about the cognitive dissonance of modern life: "we are in a constant state of cognitive dissonance... our use of airplanes, cars, heat, plastic, etc. are endangering our future, but we have no choice but to live our lives in the way the world works." The cello is "the main character in a journey, reacting and interacting with the environment that the piano creates." Anxiety is the primary emotion, "interspersed with the joy and comfort we find in the natural world, as well as hope." The compositional tools, per the SLO Review: "the mostly post-19th-century compositional environment called the octatonic scale, combined with a much earlier structural technique known as a passacaglia." Three movements, titled Intense, Floating, Energetic. Ruehr on the sound: "the cello's voice plunges heavily downward, before rising with a kind of defiance. At other times, the piano presses against this movement, offering both resistance and consolation... a meditation on a world shaped by beauty and decay." Her own site's 2024 events page misfiles the premiere under Wednesday 13 March 2024; the 2025 events page and the Middlebury programme give Thursday 13 March 2025, and the 2025 date is used here. The mechanism is the soloist as a person inside a climate: the piano builds the world, the cello reacts to it, the octatonic scale supplies the dread and the passacaglia supplies the ground that will not move -- the same pairing of an old structure and a new anxiety that runs through the whole catalogue, here at its most direct. The watchable behaviour is a cello plunging downward and rising in defiance while the piano presses against it, the ground bass circling underneath, the dread and the hope taking turns. Seen in W19. [C29338]

**Takeaway:** put the cello in a world the piano built, and let it rise in defiance. [C29338]

## Aesthetic logic

Ruehr's logic is the credo, kept for forty years: the surface simple, the structure complex. Against Shaw, whose work knows it is a borrowing and shows you the borrowing happening -- the form filled with sounds it was not built for until it breaks -- Ruehr's work knows it is a borrowing too, but shows you the feeling happening: the novel's eras, the painting's clouds, the Mass's stages, the myth's hours are scaffolds, and the piece is the emotion moving through them. She refuses the avant-garde's difficulty and the tonal past's nostalgia at once: the surface stays singable -- a five-note set inside a major scale, a Puccini aria dropped an octave, a hymn's words kept while its tune is thrown away -- and the structure carries the complexity, the canons, the combinatorial sets, the 12-tone ventilator, the passacaglia under the octatonic dread. What changes across forty years is not the question but the size of the borrowed thing and the number of hands on it: first a hexachord and a painting, then other composers' quartets and other writers' novels, then a Mass and a myth, then a graphic novel and a Mars probe, each step handing more of the piece to the people it was written for -- a baritone across twenty-five years, a quartet across twelve quartets, an octet's eight throats. She refuses the blend twice over, in her own way: the borrowed form keeps its shape against the new feeling, and the collaborators keep their voices against each other -- the quartet does not absorb the quoted aria, the Mass does not absorb the ventilator, the robot does not absorb the soprano. And she names the debt every time: the quoted aria, the named poet, the teacher credited, the librettist on the title page, the singer the disc is named for. The meaning is the making, and the making is shared: the emotion sat in until it has a sound, the structure borrowed until it holds it, the collaborator trusted until the piece fits them. [C29339]
