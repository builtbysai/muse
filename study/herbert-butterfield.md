## M1. The Whig fallacy

The historian reads the past through the questions and values of his own present, and then sorts past actors into those who helped "us" arrive and those who obstructed "us", and the sorting is the mechanism, because the preface to The Whig Interpretation of History says it is "the tendency in many historians to write on the side of Protestants and Whigs, to praise revolutions provided they have been successful, to emphasise certain principles of progress in the past and to produce a story which is the ratification if not the glorification of the present", and the ratification is the fallacy, and the fallacy is the book, and the book is the piece, and the piece must show a timeline that bends every event toward the viewer, and the present sitting at the end like a prize, and everything before it leaning toward it. The watchable behaviour is a timeline bending toward the viewer, the present as a prize at the end, every earlier event leaning toward it.

**Takeaway:** judge the past by the present and the timeline bends, and the piece should let the viewer see the bend.

## M2. Abridgement

**Takeaway:** abridge the story and the line straightens, and the piece should make the shortening visible.

## Evolution: four phases, early vs recent

PHASE 1, THE YOUNG ICONOCLAST, 1924-1939. The Historical Novel, 1924, the impossibility of history and the defence of the historical imagination; The Peace Tactics of Napoleon, 1929, four hundred pages of diplomatic history that tries to make diplomacy evocative, the story of men wrestling with moral dilemmas; The Whig Interpretation of History, 1931, the critique that made his name, a phrase that had never appeared in print before, coined by a slight, shy man of thirty-one; Napoleon, 1939, the short popular life in Duckworth's Great Lives, written while the Fox biography stalled. The driver is recovering particularity, narration as explanation, the essay as a weapon.

PHASE 2, WAR AND THE TURN TO RELIGION AND SCIENCE, 1940-1949. The Statecraft of Machiavelli, 1940, Machiavelli as a theorist of technique, not a villain; The Englishman and His History, 1944, the volte-face written to win the chair, M24, "We are all of us exultant and unrepentant whigs"; Lord Acton, 1948, the pamphlet on the admired adversary; then the October 1949 triple publication, George III, Lord North and the People, The Origins of Modern Science, Christianity and History, three books in one month, and Christianity and History selling 30,000 copies in four years. The driver is providence moving from the background to the foreground, the lecture becoming his public form, 1,000 students, BBC radio, and science and religion as the twin subjects, both given the Whig critique.

PHASE 3, THE PUBLIC INTELLECTUAL, 1950-1968. History and Human Relations, 1951, the dualism softening, the cardinal points of his method now called Christian; the Namier war, the Encounter article of 1957, the TLS affair of 1953, the George III books of 1949 and 1957, the second containing at least one error of fact he had to apologise for publicly; Man on His Past, 1955, historiography as the subject; Master of Peterhouse 1955, Vice-Chancellor 1959-1961, Regius Professor 1963-1968; founder-chairman of the British Committee, 1959; Diplomatic Investigations, 1966; the Gifford Lectures, 1965-1967, a disappointment, small audiences, the lecturer underprepared. The driver is from writing history to writing about the writing of history, from the nation to the society of states.

PHASE 4, RETIREMENT AND THE LONG TAIL, 1968-1981. Resignation and retirement to Sawston; strokes in 1971 and 1976; the Gifford lectures withheld from publication in his lifetime; The Politicization of Society, 1979, the last essays published in his lifetime; The Origins of History, published posthumously in 1981, edited by Adam Watson from an incomplete manuscript, the distillation of the thought and research to which he devoted the last twenty years of his life. The driver is from history to the history of historical consciousness, religion as the frame that holds the whole, the unwritten Fox biography as the permanent absence at the centre.

POSTHUMOUS AFTERLIFE. Bentley's The Life and Thought of Herbert Butterfield, 2011, the first full biography, and McIntire's Herbert Butterfield: Historian as Dissenter, 2004, and Sewell's Herbert Butterfield and the Interpretation of History, 2005, the resolver of the volte-face problem; Kuhn's debt in The Copernican Revolution keeping the thinking cap in circulation; the English School carrying the states-system forward; the phrase "Whig interpretation" now a commonplace of the discipline, used by historians who have never read the book, which is itself a Whiggish fate for a book against Whiggery.

## Aesthetic logic

Butterfield's work is beautiful in the way a short, strange sentence is beautiful, and that is the point. Trevelyan's mechanism is the story opening outward and carrying the reader with it; Butterfield's is the sentence snapping shut and holding the whole argument in it. His aesthetic is the lecture hall, the aphorism, the paradox, the dissenting edge, and the beauty, where there is any, is in the phrase that lodges in the memory because it is strange: "harlot and a hireling", "strange bridges", "Hold to Christ, and for the rest be totally uncommitted", "more whig in proportion as it becomes more abridged". He writes for the hall and the broadcast, he refuses the judge's chair, he stands at the edge of the high table, and the edge is the style: a Yorkshire accent in a palace of high-Anglican refinement does more work than a chapter of theory, because the outsider sees the room whole. His colours are the colours of Peterhouse stone and Yorkshire grey, lecture-hall amber, the ink black of a docket, the red of a Whig shirt worn by the man who denounced Whiggery and then wore it again. His motion is the motion of the squeeze, the cap change, the widening circle, the box filling: abridgement tightening the line, the same dots reconnecting into a different constellation, the circle widening until the outsiders are inside, the slips of paper accumulating while the shelf stays empty. The piece that follows him should never be grand or monumental; it should be small, paradoxical, and performed, and its one quiet moment is the hand that never appears on screen, the weather that is only weather, the narrator refusing to name it.

**Takeaway:** snap the scroll into one hard sentence, and the piece should let the paradox hold the whole argument.
