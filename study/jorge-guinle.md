## The life: New York, Paris, Rio, and seven years of painting

Jorge Eduardo Guinle Filho was born in New York on 14 March 1947 and moved with his family to Brazil in the year of his birth, remaining in Rio de Janeiro until 1955. From 1955 to 1965 he lived in Paris with his mother, then in New York. He returned to live in Rio de Janeiro in 1965 -- his first listed group show that year was at the Galeria do Hotel Copacabana Palace -- and lived in Rio from 1965 to 1974, with periods in London and Paris. His first listed solo show was in 1973 at Galeria Grupo B in Rio. From 1974 to 1977 he returned to Paris and settled there for three more years, then moved back to Rio de Janeiro in 1977 and settled in Brazil, his work gaining growing recognition from that point. (C16408, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16350)

He was the son of Jorginho Guinle (Jorge Eduardo Guinle, 5 February 1916 - 5 March 2004), the Brazilian businessman and socialite. His mother is NOT named in any source read; her name is a gap, not a fact to guess. His partner was the photographer Marco Rodrigues; after Guinle's death Rodrigues went to the Brazilian courts to receive part of his estate, and the case was one of the first in Brazil on a stable union between homosexuals -- the Danielian Galeria article calls it the first same-sex union recognised by Brazilian justice. Like his father, he was a lover of jazz. (C16409, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16368)

No formal art-school enrolment is named in any source read. In France, alongside his regular schooling, he began studying painting on his own, visiting museums and art galleries, a practice he continued after moving to the United States; contact with works by old masters and contemporary artists was decisive in his formation. He was particularly influenced by Henri Matisse and by action painting and American Pop Art. He attributed his discovery of painting to contact with Matisse, especially "Woman Reading against a Black Background" (1939). His dialogue in painting was with the Abstract Expressionists, especially Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning and Franz Kline. He is NOT recorded in the sources as a student at Parque Lage: his Parque Lage link is as an exhibitor and writer in the Geracao 80 show there in 1984 (and "Territorio Ocupado" in 1986). Do not present him as a Parque Lage student. (C16410, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16363)

Between 1980 and 1982 he interviewed important Brazilian artists for the nationally circulated magazine Interview, among them Helio Oiticica, Rubens Gerchman, Antonio Dias, Lygia Clark, Cildo Meireles and Mira Schendel. That is an interviewer relationship with the previous generation, not a peer circle. In 1980 he held two solo shows in Rio, "14 Erotic Paintings" at Galeria Anna Maria Niemeyer and "Oblique Memory" at Galeria Andrea Sigaud; in 1982 a solo show at Galeria Luisa Strina in Sao Paulo and a solo show at MAM/RJ, Espaco ABC. He took part in the 17th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo in 1983, and in 1978 in the 1st National Visual Arts Salon at the Museu Nacional de Belas Artes in Rio. (C16411, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16350, C16358)

In 1984 he took part in "Como Vai Voce, Geracao 80?" at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage, and wrote the text for the special issue of the magazine Modulo devoted to that show, as well as a text in the show catalogue. The show's curatorial team was Marcus de Lontra Costa, Paulo Roberto Leal and Sandra Mager, bringing together 123 artists. On 3 July 1984 the Simoes de Assis gallery opened in Curitiba, Guinle present and exhibiting two paintings, "O Minotauro" and "Interior Atavico", with a critical text by Ronaldo Brito. In 1985 he took part in the 18th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo and held a solo show in Munich at Galeria Irene Maeder. In the winter of 1985-1986, in New York with his mother, he created 14 large-scale paintings at Kaufman's Studio in Queens, for an exhibition that was never realised. In 1986 he held solo shows at Galeria Luisa Strina in Sao Paulo and at Galeria Usina in Vitoria, and took part in "Territorio Ocupado" at EAV/Parque Lage. (C16412, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16350, C16358)

At the end of 1986 he contracted pneumonia and received inconclusive results after an HIV test. In April 1987 he travelled to New York for more detailed examinations and began treatment for AIDS. On 9 May 1987 he was admitted to Memorial Hospital, and on 18 May 1987 he died there, aged 40. His final works were exhibited in December 1987 at the Grande Galeria of the Candido Mendes Cultural Center at Praca XV in Rio; the final series was titled "L'Heure Bleue / The Blue Hour". Posthumous shows followed: a tribute in May 1988 at Galeria de Arte Sao Paulo and in July 1989 at Galeria Anna Maria Niemeyer; the retrospective "Jorge Guinle - Belo Caos" at Fundacao Ibere Camargo, Porto Alegre, in 2008 and at MAM/SP, Sao Paulo, in 2009; "Jorge Guinle: ao fim e ao cabo" at Simoes de Assis, Sao Paulo, in 2020. A book on his life and work was published by Cosac Naify in 2001, with text by Christina Bach, who called his career a "dynamo of solar energy". His mature production was concentrated in the last seven years of his life and was devoted above all to painting. One secondary source puts his output at only 500 oil-on-canvas paintings plus hundreds of designs, and notes his work is not well documented; the "500 oils" figure comes from Invaluable, a secondary price guide, not a catalogue raisonne, and is not treated here as exact. (C16413, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16350, C16355, C16362)

He was represented by Simoes de Assis, whose connection with his work dates to the gallery's inauguration on 3 July 1984 in Curitiba, where he was present and exhibited two paintings; the Danielian Galeria article calls Danielian the representative of the artist's work, and says the gallery had for about three years been caring for Guinle's personal archive, of more than seven thousand drawings, plus other objects, which had belonged to the photographer Marco Rodrigues, his partner. Public and institutional collections, as listed by Simoes de Assis: Museo de Arte Contemporaneo de Monterrey, Mexico; Museu de Arte Moderna do Rio de Janeiro (MAM/RJ), including the Gilberto Chateaubriand collection; Museu Nacional de Belas Artes (MNBA), Rio de Janeiro; Museu de Arte Contemporanea de Niteroi (MAC/Niteroi), Joao Sattamini collection; Centro Cultural Candido Mendes, Rio de Janeiro; Instituto Figueiredo Ferraz, Ribeirao Preto; Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo. Private collections named by Simoes de Assis: Colecao Gilberto Chateaubriand; Colecao Roberto Marinho; Colecao Jose Olympio Pereira; Colecao Hecilda e Sergio Fadel; Colecao Ronaldo Cezar Coelho; Colecao Ricardo Akagawa; Colecao Orandi Momesso. NOT verified: holdings at MAM/SP and MAC/USP, which appeared only in a search snippet for an Arremate Arte lot page that was not opened; do not cite them as confirmed collections. (C16414, leg 1 C16200; synthesis C16361)


## The 1984 show he joined and the text he wrote for it

Guinle is the mirror image of Venosa on the 1984 show. Venosa stayed out of "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" and was included only in the 2004 revisit; Guinle was in the show itself, and he wrote for it -- a text for the special issue of Modulo devoted to the show, titled "Papai era surfista profissional, mamae fazia mapa astral legal. 'Geracao 80' ou como matei uma aula de arte num shopping center" (Modulo, Rio de Janeiro, July 1984), and a text in the show catalogue, confirmed by the ICAA record. The title alone is the man: irreverent, funny, and serious underneath -- a surfer father and an astrologer mother, a generation named in a shopping centre. Write it as the Modulo special-issue text plus a catalogue text, never as "the text for the show" in the singular, as if Guinle were the show's sole author. (C16415, leg 3 C16300; synthesis C16358)

The false claim about Venosa's participation still circulates and is re-confirmed this run: a search-result snippet from the Nara Roesler / Contemporary Art Library portfolio text reads "having been part of the iconic exhibition Como vai voce, Geracao 80?", and the #676 leg recorded the same error in the Nara Roesler 2024 press release. ## The mechanisms

### M1 -- Frenzied gestural attack, then a slowing hand

The painting is made fast, with abrupt attacks and nervous, contradictory gestures that fulfil their own corporeal mechanics; late in life the gesture becomes less feverish and more rhythmic. The evidencing works are W12 and W16 for the frenzy, and W19, W20 and the unlisted Iasmin and O Corpo for the slowing. (C16417, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: strokes land in bursts across the whole field, then the tempo visibly drops in the 1986-87 works, with marks spaced and rhythmic instead of piled. A piece built on this mechanism should let the viewer feel the tempo change -- the same hand, first in a hurry, then keeping time.

**Takeaway:** the hand hurries until it learns to keep time. Speed is the first style; rhythm is the second. (C16418, leg 2 C16250)

### M2 -- Full-field saturation, no empty space, no focal point

Layering without apparent hierarchy or pause, in a frenetic endless flow; few and rare were the empty spaces on his canvases. Guinle himself said the eye must glide across the entire canvas without being caught on any particular detail. The evidencing works are W12, W16 and W15. (C16419, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: the image never resolves into a centre and a background; every zone stays equally busy, and the eye keeps sliding. A piece built on this mechanism should refuse the viewer a place to rest -- there is no centre, and that is the composition.

**Takeaway:** fill the field so full that the eye cannot land. The glide is the picture. (C16420, leg 2 C16250)

### M3 -- Rotating the canvas on the floor

His orthodox working method: frame on the floor, the painter turning it around from one side to the other, head up, upside down; a canvas is only finished when those casual manoeuvres end in a hard, fortunate concatenation. The evidencing works are W05, W11 and W16. (C16421, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: orientation is unstable; a motif that reads as a figure from one side becomes pure gesture from another, and the composition balances at a point about to come apart. A piece built on this mechanism should be built from several orientations at once -- the picture worked from every side, and finished at the moment it holds together.

**Takeaway:** turn the picture until it clicks. The right way up is the one where it almost falls apart. (C16422, leg 2 C16250)

### M4 -- Colour as paradox: Matissean joy against expressionist tragedy

Guinle's own account: the decorative, Matissean joie de vivre of the colours is negated by the rhythmically exacerbated construction of Abstract Expressionism, and the tragedy of the brushstroke is negated by the optimism of the colour. His chromatic sensitivity from the Ecole de Paris gains "tropical thunder", acidity and aggressiveness. The evidencing works are W03, W06, W12 and W19. (C16423, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: sunny yellows punctuated by vivid reds sit inside violent, dark gestures; the palette feels joyful and aggressive at once. A piece built on this mechanism should hold both feelings in the same square inch -- the colour must smile while the stroke shouts.

**Takeaway:** joy in the colour, tragedy in the stroke, and neither one wins. The paradox is the painting. (C16424, leg 2 C16250)

### M5 -- Oscillation between figuration and abstraction

His works oscillate between figuration and abstraction; early paintings contain figurative references, then by the 1980s they fluctuate. Beings, landscapes and heads are suggested, never fixed: real or imaginary beings in W05, suggested landscapes in W07 and W08, a fisherman's head in the unlisted Cabeca de Pescador. The evidencing works are W05, W07, W08 and W19. (C16425, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: a shape almost becomes a creature, a coastline, a head, then dissolves back into brushwork as the eye moves. A piece built on this mechanism should keep the figure at sixty percent -- present enough to name, never enough to hold.

**Takeaway:** the figure is a rumour the brushwork keeps starting. Let it almost arrive, and never let it stay. (C16426, leg 2 C16250)

### M6 -- Dilution and liquefaction: paint that thins as life thins

From 1986 the paint is diluted -- large planes of softer colour, translucent and shimmering; in the final L'Heure Bleue series the pictorial process is diffuse, the pigments over-diluted, the fields serene and subdued. Fabio Miguez commented that "the depletion of painting, in a way, coincides with the depletion of life". The evidencing works are W17, W18, W19 and W20, and the unlisted Iasmin and O Corpo, where "the paints liquefy, become light". (C16427, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: thick impasto gives way to washes that bleed, run and pool; edges soften; the surface loses body. A piece built on this mechanism should let the paint run out -- the thinning is the subject, and the last wash is the lightest thing in the room.

**Takeaway:** when the paint thins, the picture lightens. The last works are the paint letting go. (C16428, leg 2 C16250)

### M7 -- Large scale, thick oil, corporeal density

He uses large canvases with vast areas of colour, the eye carried by the rhythm of the brushstrokes; the Infinity canvases carry thick layers of oil and a dissonant chromatic repertoire of corporeal density. The paint is laid on so heavily that few canvases lack "the odd craquelure or the odd film wrinkled by excess medium". The evidencing works are W06 (160 x 200 cm), W14 (147 x 86.4 cm) and W15 (167.6 cm square), and the 14 large-scale paintings made in one New York winter, 1985-86, at Kaufman's Studio in Queens for an exhibition never realised. (C16429, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: the surface reads as a body, not a picture -- ridges, cracks and wrinkled skins of paint catch light; scale makes the gesture a whole-body movement. A piece built on this mechanism should be big enough that the arm, not the wrist, makes the mark -- and the paint should be thick enough to crack.

**Takeaway:** the paint is a skin, and the skin wrinkles. Scale is what lets the body into the picture. (C16430, leg 2 C16250)

### M8 -- Irreverent titles as encrypted keys, and serial production

His titles are irreverent and accurate, and may serve as keys to an encrypted reading of the paintings -- Shoehorn for Understanding Freud, Copacabana Fools Me, The Vowels, Trojan Horse. At the same time he works serially and fast: about 500 oils in seven years, 14 large-scale paintings in a single New York winter, and a named final series, L'Heure Bleue. The evidencing works are W07, W13, W17 and W18, and the Queens group of 14. (C16431, leg 2 C16250; synthesis C16357)

Watchable behaviour: each piece arrives with a wry, off-kilter title that reframes what you just saw, and pieces come in runs where the same move is tried again with a different title. A piece built on this mechanism should never arrive untitled -- the title is half the work, and the series is the other half.

**Takeaway:** the title is the key, and the key is a joke. Make the same move twice, and give it a different name the second time. (C16432, leg 2 C16250)


## The aesthetic logic: joy that the stroke keeps cancelling

Guinle's logic is a paradox held in the hand. The colour is Matissean -- sunny, decorative, joyful, learned from the Ecole de Paris and given "tropical thunder" -- and the construction is Abstract Expressionist, rhythmically exacerbated, tragic in the stroke. Each negates the other, in his own account, and neither wins: the joy of the colour is cancelled by the violence of the gesture, and the tragedy of the gesture is cancelled by the optimism of the colour. The painting is the place where the cancellation happens, over and over, at speed.

The watchability follows from that cancellation. A canvas saturated edge to edge (M2) with no focal point makes the eye glide without landing. A gesture made in bursts (M1) and then slowed in the last two years makes the tempo itself visible. A canvas turned on the floor until it clicks (M3) makes orientation unstable, so a figure is a figure from one side and pure paint from another. A figure suggested at sixty percent (M5) keeps dissolving as the eye moves. Late, the paint itself thins (M6), and the surface loses body as the body fails -- the depletion of painting coinciding, in Miguez's words, with the depletion of life. The scale (M7) makes the gesture a whole-body movement, and the titles (M8) hand the viewer an encrypted key that is also a joke.

What he is not: not a colourist in the decorative sense (the colour is joyful but the stroke is tragic, and the two are never allowed to agree); not a pure abstractionist (the figure keeps surfacing -- a being, a coastline, a fisherman's head -- and keeps dissolving); not a slow painter (seven years, hundreds of oils, fourteen large canvases in one winter); not a Parque Lage student (he exhibited and wrote there, he did not study there); not a long-career artist with a late style (the late style is two years long and it is the paint thinning). The closest kin in the studied corpus is Venosa, who also lets a material do the picturing -- but Venosa builds a body that was never there, while Guinle paints a body that is already leaving. His operation is his own: attack fast, fill the field, turn the canvas, let the colour smile and the stroke shout, and in the last two years let the paint thin until it is light.

Early vs late arc: Early (1980-1983, W01-W08): Matissean colour, some figuration, flatter painting with breathing spaces, landscapes only suggested. Middle (1984-1985, W09-W14): full gestural flood, whirlwind chromatic impressions, ordering attempts inside the chaos. Late (1986-1987, W15-W20): a change noted by scholar Christina Bach, moving away from the experimental anxiety of the earlier period toward diluted paints, large soft planes, subtler tones, a less feverish gesture, and paints that liquefy and become light. The final series, L'Heure Bleue / The Blue Hour, is described as more diffuse and reluctant to reveal its process, with excessive diluent, more serene and subdued visual fields. (C16434; synthesis C16366)
