## 1. The brief: the man who gave objects handles

Fitzmaurice's whole career is one question asked in different rooms: where should the handle be? A mouse is a handle that lives in one place and does everything, one function at a time, and you must travel to the menu to change what it does. Fitzmaurice spent thirty years moving the handle to the work instead: a brick you put down on the thing itself, a window you carry through the world, a strip of tape that is the curve, a cube in the corner that is the scene's orientation. He is not a visual artist -- there is no Instagram account, no gallery, no image to study -- and that matters for this study, because the visual material has to be translated, not quoted. What he made is a set of rules about hands and objects, and the rules are unusually watchable: a shape that only lives while a block sits on it, a viewpoint that only exists where the window is, a line whose smoothness depends on how far apart two hands are. The study reads those rules as a generative artist would -- as systems with visible state -- and the prompts are the translation.

**Takeaway:** Read him as a handle-maker, not a menu-maker. Every invention is the same move: put the control where the work is, and let the hand stay there. [C35350]

## 2. MIT and Brown: mathematics, agents, van Dam

The degrees are all primary-source, from the theses themselves: ScB in Mathematics with Computer Science from MIT in 1988 (stated on the title page of his Brown master's thesis); MSc in Computer Science from Brown in August 1991, thesis *Form-Centered Workflow Automation Using an Agent Framework* (CS-91-M18), advisor Andries van Dam, initial advisor Norman Meyrowitz; PhD from Toronto in 1996. The Brown thesis is about agents automating form-centred workflow -- a prototype agent framework co-designed with Mum Palaniappan, forms from Hitachi Europe via Karen Smith Catlin, reviews from BLOC Development -- and it already shows the preoccupation that never leaves: the system should do the carrying, and the person should do the deciding. He wrote it in under three months, juggling classes and a full-time job, calling the experience a "PhD bootcamp", with tennis as his outlet. The acknowledgments are the richest first-person material this study found, and they give the texture no bio supplies: cooking is a hobby ("During the last months of my thesis, I took a cooking class to keep my sanity and to keep me well fed"), and producing a thesis is compared, at length, to cooking a dish -- stock, searing, deglazing, reducing. The parents are George and Rose -- he is evidently named after his father -- and the sisters are Jean, Julie and Elizabeth. Boston runs through both theses as the place of family and friends, trips back to see them the rejuvenation; that is a family tie, not a birthplace claim, and no birthplace is stated anywhere read.

**Takeaway:** The first thesis is already about delegation -- agents doing the form-work -- and the first-person voice is a cook's, not a theorist's. Keep the cooking metaphor: he reduces things down to what matters. [C35351]

## 3. Toronto 1991: recruited by Baecker, taught by Buxton

He arrives at the University of Toronto in 1991, recruited by Ron Baecker, encouraged toward the PhD by Andy van Dam, and supervised by Bill Buxton in the Dynamic Graphics Project and the Input Research Group. **Takeaway:** He enters through the lab's friendship network, and the thesis acknowledgments are the cast list for the next thirty years. Read the acknowledgments as the doorway. [C35352]

## 4. Chameleon 1993: the window in the hand

The first published idea is the Chameleon, and it is the purest statement of the handle question: a 4-inch hand-held monitor with a 6D input device and a response button, acting as a window onto a three-dimensional situated information space roughly a three-foot cube. Move the display and the viewpoint moves with it, one-to-one -- the screen is a porthole onto a virtual object pinned at a fixed location in physical space, visible only through the screen. The paper is Fitzmaurice solo, *Communications of the ACM* 36(7), July 1993, pp. 39-49 (cited as 38-49 in the Bricks paper's own reference list, which is wrong), with the companion *ACM Transactions on Information Systems* paper the same year with Zhai and Chignell, "Virtual Reality for Palmtop Computers", which tested depth perception on the small moving screen against a static 21-inch display and found it roughly equal, with very little training. The telling detail is the failure: about 25% of first-time users felt the controls were "completely backwards" -- they expected an object view, where the gesture moves the object, rather than the egocentric view, where the gesture moves the viewer. That confusion is evidence the mapping is a real, learnable rule and not a gimmick: the object never moves, the window does, and a quarter of people have to unlearn the opposite. The Chameleon is also the origin of everything that follows -- the brick is a Chameleon you put down, the Boom Chameleon is a Chameleon on an arm, the volumetric display is a Chameleon you can walk around.

**Takeaway:** The window is the handle. The object stays put; the frame moves; and the quarter of users who feel it backwards are proof the rule is real. [C35353]

## 5. Bricks 1995: LEGO-sized handles

The paper that names the field is "Bricks: Laying the Foundations for Graspable User Interfaces" -- Fitzmaurice, Ishii and Buxton, CHI '95, Denver, Sunday 7-Thursday 11 May 1995, pp. 442-449. The apparatus is the ActiveDesk, a rear-projected 4.5-by-3-foot horizontal desk, with two Ascension Bird 6D trackers as bricks and a drawing application called GraspDraw, written in C/GL on an SGI Indigo2. A brick is a LEGO-sized physical block, tracked for position and orientation, and a graspable object is a physical handle plus a virtual object. The rules are few and absolute. Place a brick on the display and the virtual object beneath it attaches; lift the brick and the object releases -- touch and release are the delimiters, "very natural start and stop points" for segmenting commands, where bare-hand gestures have none. One brick moves and rotates its object one-to-one, with the centre of rotation at the centre of the brick, not the object -- so the same twist swings a wide arc from a corner and spins in place from the middle. Two bricks on one object make an anchor and an actuator: hold one still and move the other and the object stretches between them; rotate both at once and it bends like clay, the paper's example starting from a rectangle and bending it by rotating a brick at each end. Many bricks on many control points make a spline you can grab anywhere: each point follows its brick and the curve re-solves through the moved points in real time. Tool selection is by dunking a brick in a physical tray or ink-well. And the paper's design-space table is itself a prompt generator: function assignment from inert to smart, interaction time span from transient to permanent, one brick to a hundred. The empirical claim behind it all is space-multiplexed input: one dedicated physical transducer per function, left sitting in space, so its position *is* its state and it stays put when released -- parkable, re-acquirable by muscle memory -- against the mouse's time-multiplexed one-device-many-functions.

**Takeaway:** The brick is the thesis in miniature: attach by touching, release by lifting, deform by holding two ends. A shape that only lives while a block sits on it is a piece already. [C35354]

## 6. The thesis 1996: Graspable User Interfaces

The PhD thesis, *Graspable User Interfaces*, University of Toronto, 1996, supervisor Bill Buxton, committee Mark Chignell, Calvin Gotlieb, Marilyn Mantei (internal examiner), Michiel van de Panne, Christine MacKenzie (external examiner), is the Bricks paper expanded into a system -- and the front matter, which is what this run read end to end, is where the human detail lives. Kurtenbach read early drafts and "gave sage advice"; Ishii drew most of the hand drawings in the thesis; Zhai did the statistics; Harrison and Baudel are in the acknowledgments alongside Balakrishnan. The cooking metaphor returns at full length: producing a thesis is like cooking a dish, stock and searing and deglazing and reducing, and he took a cooking class in the last months "to keep my sanity and to keep me well fed". The body chapters were not read in this run -- the same honest gap as #812, where the Kurtenbach thesis body was also front-matter-only -- so the mechanism detail in this study rests on the papers, not the dissertation. What the thesis adds that the paper does not is the frame: graspable interfaces are not a trick, they are a claim about what input is *for* -- specialised physical form factors, one per function, left in space, against the general-purpose mouse that must pretend to be everything.

**Takeaway:** The thesis is the paper with the receipts: a roomful of people, a cooking metaphor, and a claim that specialisation beats generality. The gap is honest -- the body chapters are unread -- and the study says so. [C35355]

## 7. The empirical test 1997: one function, one brick

CHI '97, Atlanta, Saturday 22-Thursday 27 March 1997, carries two Fitzmaurice papers, and the second is the one that proves the first. "An empirical evaluation of graspable user interfaces: towards specialized, space-multiplexed input" (Fitzmaurice and Buxton, pp. 43-50 -- one secondary bibliography says 43-58, which is an error in that bibliography) runs the tracking experiment: space-multiplexed input, one dedicated physical transducer per function, beats time-multiplexed mouse input, and specialised physical form factors beat generic ones, and attachment persists across interactions. The companion paper the same week is the T3 paradigm (section 8). The pairing is deliberate in the study's reading: one paper states the paradigm, the other measures it, and the measurement is what turns a demo into a claim. The Alias affiliation is already on the papers -- the first Alias-affiliated publication is CHI '97 -- and the work, the CHI '97 empirical paper says, "was conducted in the User Interface Research Group at Alias|Wavefront".

**Takeaway:** Measure the handle. The experiment is the difference between a charming demo and a principle: dedicated beats generic, and the win is in the measurement. [C35356]

## 8. T3 1997: tablets, two hands, transparency

"The design of a GUI paradigm based on tablets, two-hands, and transparency" -- Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Baudel and Buxton, CHI '97, pp. 35-42 -- is the paradigm paper, and its title is a trap worth naming twice: Buxton's own papers index adds "and evaluation" to the title in two places, and the ACM DL title has no such words. The three design goals drive everything: maximise screen for artwork, never divert visual attention from the artwork to a palette at the edge, increase input quality. The mechanisms that serve them are the Toolglass -- a semi-transparent sheet of widgets floating between application and cursor, positioned by the non-dominant hand, clicked *through* by the dominant hand onto the object beneath (Bier, Stone, Pier, Buxton and DeRose, SIGGRAPH '93, which Kurtenbach integrated rather than invented) -- marking menus appearing at the cursor, and two hands working concurrently, the non-dominant positioning and scrolling coarsely while the dominant draws finely. A shorter version with a different author order appears in the CHI '97 Extended Abstracts and must not be confused with the full paper. The paper reports the paradigm was introduced into a full-featured professional graphics application, and that the added functionality was measured not only in performance but in "the quality of interaction, which allows artists to create new graphic vocabularies". That last phrase is the one this study keeps: the test of an interface is not speed, it is whether new vocabularies become possible.

**Takeaway:** Transparency in both senses: you can see through the tool, and the tool does not hide the work. The interface that is mostly not there is the interface. [C35357]

## 11. Artwork orientation 1999: the rotating UI

CHI '99, Pittsburgh, Saturday 15-Thursday 20 May 1999: "An exploration into supporting artwork orientation in the user interface" (Fitzmaurice, Balakrishnan, Kurtenbach and Buxton, pp. 167-174). The finding is observational before it is technical: artists rotate the paper constantly and subconsciously -- to stay in an articulation comfort range, to keep the hand from obscuring the key area, to evaluate the drawing -- and rotation is integral to drawing, not incidental. The proposed solution, the Rotating User Interface, is a question the device must answer on every rotation: what turns with the paper, and what stays put? The artwork rotates; some UI -- menus, palettes -- may need to counter-rotate to stay readable, or stay fixed to the desk. The paper defines the concept and its design issues from an exploratory study (three drawing configurations, five tasks) rather than shipping one fixed rule, and that is the honest form of it: the mechanism is the *question*, and every rotation re-asks it. For a generative piece this is a gift, because the tension is visible in every frame: a canvas that turns under the pen while the labels around it must choose, visibly, whether to turn with it or stay upright.

**Takeaway:** The paper turns; the UI must decide. Rotation is not a feature, it is a question the device answers every time the hand moves. [C35360]

## 12. Tape drawing 1999: the tape is the curve

UIST '99, Asheville, Sunday 7-Wednesday 10 November 1999: "Digital tape drawing" (Balakrishnan, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach and Buxton, pp. 161-169), Balakrishnan-led. The physical technique is old -- automotive designers laying black photographic tape on large upright surfaces to draw curves -- and the digital system back-projects onto an 8-by-6-foot screen. The mechanism is the most watchable in Fitzmaurice's whole oeuvre: an unfastened tape segment, a polyline stretched between two cursors, one per hand, each a 6DOF tracker with one button, in absolute one-to-one position control. The distance between the two hands sets the length of the unfastened segment. Press the non-dominant button ("fasten mode") and slide the non-dominant hand along the segment: the tape fastens as it moves, and the cursor is software-constrained to travel only along the segment toward the dominant hand. Hold the dominant hand still while fastening and the line is straight -- the tape is the ruler. Move both hands while fastening and the line curves. The segment length regulates smoothness: a long segment constrains the fastening point's range of movement, so the tangent changes gradually and the curve is smooth; a short segment, approaching zero, reduces to freehand sketching with the non-dominant hand, wiggly and free. Release the non-dominant button and the tape is cut at that point. While laying, the user can back up and undo; a cut operation removes laid sections. No mode switch: straight versus curve is decided by whether the dominant hand moves. The paper notes they tried spring-loaded cords for tension and dropped them -- wrong-feeling feedback is worse than none. The 2002 extension (Grossman et al., CHI 2002) builds non-planar 3D principal curves by drawing 2D tape curves on planar cross-sections of a working volume, and the I3D 2001 paper's construction planes (section 16) are the same idea in a box.

**Takeaway:** The line is drawn by the relationship between two moving points, not by one point. Smoothness is a distance between hands, and you can see the rule in the line itself. [C35361]

## 13. Sampling, not synthesis 1999: import, don't invent

"Sampling, synthesis, and input devices" (Fitzmaurice, Balakrishnan and Kurtenbach, *Communications of the ACM* 42(8), 1999, pp. 54-63) is the argument paper, and it is the one that speaks most directly to a generative practice. The claim: input devices let graphics shift from start-from-scratch synthesis to *sampling* the physical world -- scanning and importing real 2D and 3D content -- and the shift matters because sampling brings the world in with its texture intact. It is a framing device for a whole piece rather than a single mechanism: import, don't invent. The brick samples a position, the tape samples a gesture, the Chameleon samples a viewpoint; none of them synthesise from nothing. Read alongside the Bricks paper, it is the theory of why the brick works: the hand already knows where the thing is, and the system should sample that knowledge rather than ask the hand to describe it in a menu. The paper is also the bridge to the later career -- the sampling idea returns as the Datasaurus (section 24), where the sample is a dataset, and as WorldSmith (section 26), where the sample is a prompt.

**Takeaway:** Import, don't invent. The hand already knows; the system's job is to sample what the hand knows, not to ask it to start from scratch. [C35362]

## 14. Hotbox 1999: twelve hundred commands at the cursor

"The Hotbox: efficient access to a large number of menu-items" (Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Owen and Baudel, CHI '99) is the marking menu grown up into a product: a pop-up widget combining menubar, marking menus, radial menus and accelerator keys, built for Maya, which then had about 1,200 commands. The mechanism is zones: the Hotbox shows the menubar's rows (Modelling, Animation, Dynamics, Rendering) as a transparent overlay in the centre of the work area, organised in overlapping zones that generalise the four-to-five-zone limit so the whole command set becomes reachable; the rows match the traditional menubar's organisation so learning transfers; a marking menu toggles individual rows on or off, or isolates one row. It is summoned at the cursor by a hotkey or pen gesture, and it is easy to learn because it looks like the menubar you know, yet fast to operate because it is at the cursor and experts flick. The visible beat is the same as the marking menu's: wait and you see the fan, flick and you don't. For a piece, the Hotbox is a vocabulary made briefly visible -- the entire application's command set, ghosted over the artwork for a blink, then gone.

**Takeaway:** Twelve hundred commands, no travel. The whole menu, briefly, exactly where the hand already is -- and then it dissolves. [C35363]

## 15. Volumetric displays 2001: reaching into the image

"User interfaces for volumetric displays" (Balakrishnan, Fitzmaurice and Kurtenbach, *IEEE Computer* 34(3), March 2001, pp. 37-45) is a mockup study, and the study says so plainly: the display does not exist yet as a product, so the authors explore it with physical props -- a 3-inch hand sphere, a 1-foot dome, a 1-foot cube, a 2-foot dome, a 4-foot room dome -- and ask what interaction would have to be. The image is true 3D: points illuminated in a physical volume, inside a clear enclosure, viewable from any direction by multiple people at once, no glasses, no head tracking. The rules they derive are the ones a piece can steal directly. Physical rotation: users will feel compelled to rotate the display itself to see the other side, and at room scale rotation becomes critical because you cannot reach around a 4-foot dome. Touch the enclosure: given the realism, users try to interact by touching the glass. Input mappings, four of them: 3D-to-3D (track hands in a volume below the display, superimpose a virtual hand); planar 2D-to-3D (a tablet controlling a subset, risking spatial ambiguity); planar 2D-to-planar-2D within 3D (map the tablet to a plane in the scene, or use a cube's faces as orthographic input planes); nonplanar 2D-to-3D (use the dome surface itself as input, casting a ray from the touch point into the scene). Cursors, three classes: a 2D point cursor constrained to the surface or a plane, with the ray shown as a visible line; a 3D point cursor, a small object in the scene; a 3D volume cursor, a semi-transparent volume that selects multiple objects at once. Parkable devices: devices that stay where released keep their position and orientation, and a mouse loses parkability on a dome, so a parkable-on-a-dome device is desirable. Perimeter controls: buttons, sliders and touch-rings around the enclosure's edge, and a single control panel on a rail that can be dragged around the display, the system inferring the user's location from the panel's orbital position. Viewpoint-independent widgets: since users walk around the display, widgets must be reachable from any viewpoint. Two later papers that look like his are NOT his and must not be added: the UIST '06 volumetric-selection paper and the UIST '04 multi-finger volumetric-gestures paper are Grossman, Balakrishnan and Wigdor without Fitzmaurice.

**Takeaway:** The image has real depth and a real enclosure, and the hand operates from outside on something floating inside. The dome is the frame, and the frame is meant to be spun. [C35364]

## 16. Large displays 2001-2005: the room as interface

The large-display programme runs from the I3D 2001 paper ("Interaction techniques for 3D modeling on large displays" -- Grossman, Balakrishnan, Kurtenbach, Fitzmaurice, Khan and Buxton, pp. 17-23; not CHI 2001, as the brief's framing invited) through the July/August 2005 *IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications* special issue "Applications of Large Displays", which Fitzmaurice and Kurtenbach guest-edited together (their introduction, pp. 22-23), to "Cinematic meeting facilities using large displays" in the same issue (Fitzmaurice, Khan, Kurtenbach and Binks, pp. 17-21). The I3D paper gives the construction planes: a 3D model inside a cuboid bounding volume, three planes (top, side, front) displayed as faces of the cuboid, only the active plane visible at its remembered position ("construction plane memory"), the others retreating to the cuboid's edges; the active plane semi-transparent, with the points where it intersects the model highlighted and dynamically updated as the plane slides through the volume -- colour-coded by the orientation of the curve being intersected, acting as "portholes" between 2D and 3D; enhanced orthographic views where curves are drawn in different grey levels by their distance from the plane, so one flat view carries depth; symmetry about the medial axis; and switching between perspective and orthographic as a smooth animation of the window outline *and* the underlying data, not a cut, so spatial correspondence is preserved. The 2005 paper gives the room: the Alias Visualization Studio ("VizStudio"), an eight-sided "octagon" of projected walls, designed as a meeting room like a theatre -- arrival, staging, social dynamics -- not just a big screen. The journal version of the synthesis, "Toward the digital design studio: large display explorations" (Khan, Matejka, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach, Burtnyk and Buxton, *Human-Computer Interaction* 24, 2009, pp. 9-47), is a journal article, not a conference paper. The through-line is scale: at wall size, the menu bar at the screen edge is "cumbersome", the marking menu appears at the dominant hand's cursor "requiring very little movement from the user", and the interface must come to the hand because the hand cannot cross the room.

**Takeaway:** At wall size, the room is the interface and the hand cannot travel. The plane slides, the grey levels grade, the view morphs instead of jumping -- and the meeting room is staged like theatre. [C35365]

## 17. Boom Chameleon 2002: the frame on an arm

"Boom chameleon: simultaneous capture of 3D viewpoint, voice and gesture annotations on a spatially-aware display" (Tsang, Fitzmaurice, Kurtenbach, Khan and Buxton, UIST 2002, Paris, Sunday 27-Wednesday 30 October 2002, pp. 111-120) is the Chameleon grown a body: a flat-panel LCD (SGI 15-inch, 16:9, replacing the goggles of a Fakespace BOOM3C) mounted on a tracked mechanical armature, with a touch screen overlaid and a microphone and speaker on the frame. The display is a physical window into the 3D virtual environment with a direct one-to-one real-to-virtual mapping -- moving the display changes the viewpoint -- and the annotation application captures viewpoint, voice and gesture *simultaneously*: a spoken note and a drawn stroke are recorded against the viewpoint they were made from, and sessions can be replayed. The watchable consequence is the one this study keeps: an annotation that only exists from the angle it was made at. Turn away and the note is gone; return to the angle and it is back. The camera has weight and momentum because it is a physical object on an arm, and the review is a flight path with a voice attached to it.

**Takeaway:** A comment anchored to a viewpoint is a comment that disappears when you turn away. The frame has weight, the note has an angle, and replay is a re-flight. [C35366]

## 18. StyleCam 2002: every frame a Rembrandt

"StyleCam: interactive stylized 3D navigation using integrated spatial and temporal controls" (Burtnyk, Khan, Fitzmaurice, Balakrishnan and Kurtenbach, UIST '02, pp. 101-110) is the most composed motion in Fitzmaurice's work, and the paper states its goal as a slogan: "every frame is a Rembrandt" -- a viewing experience with the pacing of a TV commercial, not an unconstrained camera. The mechanism is constraint made generous: the camera is confined to authored camera *surfaces* (spatial constraint -- the author decides what the user can see and when); transitions between surfaces are authored animation *clips*; and one unified drag lets the user move seamlessly and continuously between spatial control of the camera (dragging around on a surface) and temporal control of the animated transitions (scrubbing the clip forward and back). The user's focus stays on the content, never on widgets. The watchable result is a camera gliding along invisible rails around an object -- it never goes somewhere ugly, because the rails forbid it -- handing off at the end of one rail to a swooping authored transition that carries it to the next, the drag sometimes steering and sometimes scrubbing time, with no visible switch between the two. The authored example and the evaluation were not read in detail in this run, and the study does not invent them.

**Takeaway:** Constraint is generosity: rails that forbid the ugly frame, clips that carry the handoff, and one drag that is sometimes space and sometimes time. [C35367]

## 19. Tracking Menus 2003: the menu that follows

"Tracking menus" (Fitzmaurice, Khan, Pieke, Buxton and Kurtenbach, UIST '03, Vancouver, DOI 10.1145/964696.964704) is the smallest mechanism in the set and the most charming: a cluster of buttons that *moves to keep following the cursor*. The pen has three sensed states -- out-of-range (more than about 1.5 cm above the surface, menu visible but stationary), hovering/tracking (pen near, cursor follows pen), touching (pen on surface) -- and the rule is that the cursor moves freely *inside* the menu, and when the cursor hits the menu's edge, the menu moves to keep tracking the cursor. The paper's physical analogy is a jar lid pushed by a pencil: the pencil floats inside the lid until it touches the rim, then the lid slides with it. A tracking menu is a "multi-headed tool" -- two or more tools simultaneously attached to the cursor, so tool switching needs no trip to a palette, no keyboard, no buttons, just point and press. It can be pinned in place, and when pinned it stops following. It was integrated into SketchBook for Tablet PC. In a piece the comedy and the mechanism are the same: the menu is lazy until pushed, then it glides -- a control cluster that never needs to be chased, because it follows.

**Takeaway:** The menu is lazy until pushed. The tray follows the thing sitting on it, and the tool never needs to be chased. [C35368]

## 20. ViewCube and SteeringWheels 2007-2008: navigation that ships

The two navigation tools that shipped in Autodesk products are often named together, and this run splits their dates, because they are different years and different kinds of credit. SteeringWheels is the 2007 one: the provisional "Steering Wheels" was filed 26 September 2007, Fitzmaurice is first-named inventor on the patent (US 9280257 B2, "Navigation system for a 3D virtual scene", with Matejka, Kurtenbach and Khan), and the wheel travels with the cursor -- a circular on-screen wheel divided into segments (Orbit, Pan, Zoom, Rewind, Walk, Look, Up/Down, Center), appearing at the cursor so the common navigation tools are at hand without a trip to a toolbar, with different wheels for different users (View Object for the novice, Tour Building, Full Navigation for the expert) and cursor wrapping at the view edges. The ViewCube is the 2008 one: the paper is Khan, Mordatch, Fitzmaurice, Matejka and Kurtenbach, "ViewCube: A 3D Orientation Indicator and Controller", I3D 2008, Redwood City, 15-17 February 2008, Fitzmaurice third of five authors, not lead -- do not date it to 2007. The cube is a proxy object for the scene: as an indicator it turns continuously to reflect the current view direction; as a controller, clicking a face snaps the scene to that orthographic view, clicking an edge or corner snaps to the corresponding angled view, dragging it tumbles the scene ArcBall-style; users were almost twice as fast dragging the ViewCube as clicking, and preferred dragging; when inactive it goes partially transparent so it does not obscure the model, on hover it becomes opaque, and a compass ring below it shows North and can be dragged to rotate around the pivot. The companion paper the same year, "Safe 3D Navigation" (Fitzmaurice, Matejka, Mordatch, Khan and Kurtenbach, I3D 2008), lists seven properties for navigation that cannot get lost; this run verified three from the paper text -- the visible pivot point (a small geometry sphere placed on the model surface where the cursor intersects it, fixing the three failure modes where orbit acts like zoom, like pan, or looks into empty space), the Rewind tool (the navigation history as on-screen thumbnails, scrubbed to fly the camera backwards through everywhere it has been), and the Walk tool's quiet-motion zone around the cursor -- and did not enumerate the remaining four, which the study does not claim.

**Takeaway:** The indicator is the controller. A little cube in the corner that is always turning in sync with the world -- and grabbing it tumbles the world. [C35369]

## 21. PieCursor 2008: the cursor is the tool

"PieCursor: merging pointing and command selection for rapid in-place tool switching" (Fitzmaurice, Matejka, Khan, Glueck and Kurtenbach, CHI 2008, Florence, pp. 1361-1370 -- not UIST 2008) is the marking menu reduced to its smallest honest form: a 32-by-32-pixel radial cluster of tool wedges that *replaces the cursor*. Pointing and tool selection merge into one action -- there is no cursor *and* a menu, there is a cursor that is the menu -- and the experiment says it beats the toolbar by 20.8% for coarse pointing with four to eight wedges. It is the same rule as the Hotbox at a different scale (section 14) and the same rule as the marking menu (section 8 of #812), and the study keeps it because the scale matters: at 32 pixels the mechanism has nowhere to hide. If the wedge layout is wrong, the piece fails in plain sight; if it is right, the tool switch is invisible, which is the point. The PieCursor is also the clearest evidence that Fitzmaurice's programme is one programme: the brick, the tape, the tracking menu and the pie cursor are all the same move -- the control is where the hand is, and the hand never travels to a palette.

**Takeaway:** Thirty-two pixels, no travel. The cursor is the tool, and the tool switch is invisible -- which is the whole point. [C35370]

## 22. PenLight and MouseLight 2009-2010: light as annotation

"PenLight: combining a mobile projector and a digital pen for dynamic visual overlay" (Song, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, Guimbretiere, Khan, Attar and Kurtenbach, CHI 2009, Boston, pp. 143-152 -- not UIST 2009) puts a miniature projector inside the pen: it projects dynamic visual feedback onto the paper or printout as you draw, so the light is the annotation and the overlay is the drawing. "MouseLight: bimanual interactions on digital paper using a pen and a spatially-aware mobile projector" (Song, Guimbretiere, Grossman and Fitzmaurice, CHI 2010, Atlanta) decouples the two: a spatially-aware standalone mobile projector the size of a mouse, used bimanually with a digital pen on paper, so the projected image can stay still while the pen writes elsewhere -- including writing a search keyword *outside* the projection area and having the result update inside it ("displaced interaction"). Toolglass interactions are re-applied to physical paper, and a halo technique guides the user to move the projector when a result lies outside the viewport. The pair is the late form of the Chameleon idea: the display is no longer a window you carry, it is light you throw, and the pen writes where the light is not. The full interaction set of MouseLight was not read in this run -- abstract plus sections on Toolglass-on-paper and displaced interaction -- and the study claims no more than that.

**Takeaway:** Light is the annotation. The projector throws the display where the pen is not, and the pen writes where the light is not -- and the two still agree. [C35371]

## 23. Learnability: ToolClips, CommunityCommands, Draco

The 2009-2014 learnability programme is the other half of the career, and it asks the question the marking menu first posed -- how does a novice become an expert without being taught -- at the scale of a whole application. "A survey of software learnability: metrics, methodologies and guidelines" (Grossman, Fitzmaurice and Attar, CHI 2009) maps how learnability is measured. "ToolClips" (Grossman and Fitzmaurice, CHI 2010) attaches short contextual video clips to UI functionality, so the help is the demonstration. "CommunityCommands: command recommendations for software applications" (Matejka, Li, Grossman and Fitzmaurice, UIST '09, Victoria, pp. 193-202) recommends commands by collaborative filtering over community usage data -- item-based filtering giving 2.1 times as many good suggestions, an ambient peripheral palette, a three-month study with professionals -- and the deployed story (Li, Matejka, Grossman and Fitzmaurice, *AI Magazine* 36(3), 2015) is a public AutoCAD plug-in with 1,100-plus users over a year. "Draco: bringing life to illustrations with kinetic textures" (Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Zhao and Fitzmaurice, CHI 2014, Toronto, pp. 351-360) is the closest thing in his oeuvre to a generative-artist's brief: sketch-based animation of still drawings via "kinetic textures" -- emitting textures (speed lines, particles that flow) and oscillating textures (things that sway and pulse) -- Honorable Mention, Best Video, Best Talk at CHI '14, the first ACM DL paper to embed an animated figure, and the research behind SketchBook Motion. "Kitty" (Kazi et al., UIST '14, pp. 395-405) extends it to illustrations that are dynamic *and* interactive -- the drawing responds to the viewer. The programme's through-line is the same as the bricks': the system should show you what to do by doing it, and the community's usage is itself a material a piece can surface.

**Takeaway:** The help is the demonstration, and the community's usage is a material. Draco is the proof: a still drawing that emits and oscillates is already a generative piece. [C35372]

## 24. Same stats, different graphs 2017: the Datasaurus

"Same stats, different graphs: generating datasets with varied appearance and identical statistics through simulated annealing" (Matejka and Fitzmaurice, CHI 2017, Denver, pp. 1290-1294) is the Datasaurus Dozen, and it is the single most portable mechanism in the late career: simulated annealing perturbs points -- about 200,000 iterations per shape -- until a dataset has identical summary statistics to a seed but draws a dinosaur, a star, a circle, a bullseye. One statistical identity, many visible bodies. The code and data are open. For a generative practice the mechanism is ready-made and honest: perturb the points, hold the statistics, chase the shape -- and the shape that emerges is the argument, because the numbers alone cannot tell a dinosaur from a circle. It is also the sampling idea (section 13) turned back on itself: the dataset is sampled into a new body while its identity is held fixed. The study ranks it among the strongest prompt sources precisely because the rule is countable -- the statistics do not move, the picture does -- and a piece built on it can be verified by its own numbers.

**Takeaway:** Hold the numbers still and let the picture move. A dinosaur and a circle with identical statistics is the whole argument for looking instead of counting. [C35373]

## 28. Aesthetic logic: the handle is the interface

Fitzmaurice's aesthetic is a subtraction, and it is the same subtraction at every scale. The mouse is general-purpose and therefore nowhere; the brick is special-purpose and therefore *here*, on this object, in this place, and its place is its state. The Chameleon window is small because the world is big; the ViewCube is small because the scene is big; the PieCursor is 32 pixels because the tool is the cursor. Nothing travels to a palette. Nothing is hidden in a menu at the edge of the screen. The interface that is mostly not there -- transparent sheets, parked bricks, a cube that goes translucent when idle -- is the interface, and the work is always the biggest thing on screen. For a generative piece, the logic translates into three rules. First, the control must be visible as an object in the scene, not as chrome around it: a block, a frame, a strip of tape, a cube. Second, the state must be readable from the object's position, not from a label: where the brick sits is what the piece is doing. Third, the interaction must be the same gesture at every skill level, differing only in whether you wait: press and wait and the fan blooms, press and flick and the command fires, and the learning is the fading of the wait. The piece that follows this logic does not need an explanation, because the handle explains itself by being held.

**Takeaway:** The handle is the interface, the position is the state, and the wait is the lesson. A piece built on those three rules needs no explanation. [C35377]
