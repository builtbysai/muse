## M1. Mathematical acceleration against a slowing pulse

What it is: in Pacific 231 of 1923 the metronome mark falls while the rhythmic subdivisions keep splitting, so the ear hears acceleration inside a deceleration, and Honegger's own words are "giving the impression of a mathematical acceleration of rhythm, while the movement itself slows", and the notation shift is half note equals 80 at the start to quarter note equals 126 at the end, and the beat itself slows, and a long Bach-like chorale of 41 whole notes, heard complete only twice, is chopped into ever smaller units, and the piece is five sections, standstill, start, increasing speed, top speed, deceleration and stop, and the watchable behaviour is a machine that seems to speed up as the ground itself slows.

**Takeaway:** the acceleration must come from subdivision, not from tempo, and a piece that only gets faster has not built the paradox.

## M2. The chorale as machine

What it is: the locomotive is not imitated with noise, it is built from a "great varied chorale, criss-crossed with alla breve counterpoints" in the manner of Bach, and Halbreich calls it "a monolith carved out of full granite, as solid as a figured chorale by Bach (its true model)", and Honegger insisted the title came after the music and the point was abstract, not descriptive, and the watchable behaviour is a solemn hymn tune carrying the whole piece, and the train emerging from counterpoint, not from sound effects.

**Takeaway:** the machine must be built from counterpoint, and a locomotive made of noise is an imitation, not a construction.

## M3. Sport as rhythmic argument

What it is: in Rugby of 1928 a game is rendered as attacks and counter-attacks, with rhythm doing the work of bodies, and Honegger says "I am very fond of soccer, but rugby is closer to my heart... I am more keenly attracted by rugby's rhythm, which is savage, abrupt, chaotic and desperate", and unlike Pacific 231's strict rhythm, Rugby is rhythmically riotous, phrases collide, stop and restart, like a scrum breaking and reforming, and he denied it was program music, it describes "in musical language the game's attacks and counterattacks, and the rhythm and color of a match", and the watchable behaviour is phrases colliding and reforming, and the game argued in rhythm rather than pictured in sound.

**Takeaway:** the sport must be argued in rhythm, and a game pictured in sound effects is a postcard, not a match.

## M4. The oratorio-drama, narrator plus small forces

What it is: in Le Roi David of 1921 incidental music for a play is rebuilt as a concert oratorio by replacing the stage with a narrator and scoring for exactly the forces at hand, 15 to 17 instruments, see C2, no strings except double bass, winds, percussion, piano, harmonium, celesta, plus chorus and soloists, and the work is 27 short sections, each a different colour, and spoken text carries the plot and music carries the feeling, and the Dance before the Ark is the set piece, and the 1923 full-orchestra version keeps the same skeleton, and the watchable behaviour is a scene changing colour every few minutes while one voice tells the story.

**Takeaway:** the narrator must carry the plot so the music can carry the feeling, and a scene that needs staging has not been rebuilt as an oratorio.

## M5. Style-collage and polytonality

What it is: many historical styles inside one work, and several keys at once, and the Song of Victory march in Le Roi David goes beyond bi-tonal to quadri-tonal, four keys at once, per the program notes quoted in the Docslib search-read, and Gregorian chant, organum, Phrygian mode, Bach chorale, Faure-like sicilienne and jazz all sit in one piece, and the Ashgate book, Rhythmic and Contrapuntal Structures in the Music of Arthur Honegger, states his works "incorporated elements of atonality, tonality, extended tonality, and polytonality, at times all within a single composition", and the watchable behaviour is each scene changing its musical century, and the shepherd David singing a simple diatonic melody against a chromatic, dissonant accompaniment.

**Takeaway:** the collage must change centuries scene by scene, and a piece that stays in one style has not collaged anything.

## M6. The liturgical symphony

What it is: Symphony No. 3 "Symphonie Liturgique" of 1945-46 is a three-movement symphony whose movements are named after the Requiem Mass and Psalm 130, Dies irae, De profundis clamavi, Dona nobis pacem, and there is no direct quotation of chant melodies, per the Schweizer Musikzeitung, the liturgy supplies the shape, not the notes, and the first movement is storm-like and aggressive, and the slow movement is meditative, and the finale builds an insistent brutal march to a dissonant climax, then a long lyrical coda, and a robin-song melody from Jeanne d'Arc au bucher returns near the end of each movement, and Honegger says the symphony "musically depicts the struggle in the heart of man between abandonment to the blind forces that enslave him and the instinct for happiness, the love of peace, the feeling of divine refuge", and the watchable behaviour is a storm, a prayer and a march that ends in peace, and the same small bird-song returning at the end of every movement.

**Takeaway:** the liturgy must supply the shape, not the notes, and a symphony that quotes chant has borrowed, not built.

## M7. The withheld instrument

What it is: Symphony No. 2 of 1941 is a symphony for strings alone in which the trumpet is held back until about a minute from the end, then enters with a chorale, "like pulling out an organ stop", per the composer, and the trumpet part is marked ad libitum, and the watchable behaviour is 25 minutes of turbulent, troubled string music, then one brass voice changing the ending from tragic to hopeful, and the mechanism is scarcity, one instrument, one minute, the whole meaning reversed.

**Takeaway:** the withheld voice must reverse the meaning, and a trumpet that merely joins the strings has not been withheld.

## M8. The three-D signature

What it is: Symphony No. 5 "Di tre re" of 1950, "of the three Ds", ends each of its three movements on the note D, played three times by solo timpani and basses, and the watchable behaviour is the whole 22-minute symphony driving toward the same low thud, three times, and the ending is a "vision of nothingness", per Wikipedia, stark and somber, the composer's belief that the end of civilization was near, and a signature that is also a verdict.

**Takeaway:** the signature must be a verdict, and a repeated note that says nothing is a tic, not a signature.

## M9. The spoken-word oratorio, mystere lyrique

What it is: in Jeanne d'Arc au bucher of 1935-38, Claudel's eleven-scene poem is the backbone, and key scenes are SPOKEN, not sung, with the music, especially the choral writing, carrying the weight, and the frame sits "between Heaven and Earth, past and present", letting Honegger mix styles and build "sculpted blocks of sound" from monody, harmony and counterpoint, and the ondes Martenot, played at the premiere by its inventor Maurice Martenot, gives the supernatural a voice no acoustic instrument has, and three saxophones replace standard brass in places, and the watchable behaviour is a speaking Joan against singing saints and crowds, and the unearthly instrument entering exactly where the drama needs it.

**Takeaway:** the spoken word must carry the scene and the music the weight, and a sung Joan has not used the mystere's division of labour.

## M10. New instruments, used as themselves

What it is: Honegger scores for instruments other composers treated as novelties and makes them structural, and the ondes Martenot in Jeanne of 1938 and in the film L'Idee of 1934, and three saxophones in Jeanne, and the musical saw in Antigone of 1927, and the tam-tam in Pacific 231 and many other pieces, and a bouteillophone, bottles filled with varying amounts of water, for No. 7 of Le Dit des Jeux du monde, and bowed instruments invented by Leo Sir in Hymne, and four ondes Martenot in the Andante H 215 of 1943, and the watchable behaviour is strange timbres arriving exactly where the drama needs an unearthly colour, then leaving, and they are never decoration for its own sake.

**Takeaway:** the strange timbre must arrive where the drama needs it, and a novelty instrument kept on stage all evening is decoration.

## M11. The operetta turn

What it is: the serious symphonist writes light operetta with named models, Mozart, Chabrier and Messager, and Les Aventures du roi Pausole of 1930 runs 800 performances, per Halbreich, and the same architect builds a joke, and the humour is structural, not a holiday from seriousness, and after Antigone, Halbreich notes, Honegger aimed at "the immediate approval of a vast popular audience, not that of a hypothetical and elitist posterity", on L'Aiglon of 1937, with Ibert, and the watchable behaviour is the same hand that built the liturgical symphony building a comedy that runs for years.

**Takeaway:** the joke must be built by the same architect, and an operetta that apologises for itself has not turned, it has escaped.

## M12. Film scoring as laboratory

What it is: about 40 film scores, Encyclopedia.com lists 7 named plus "36 others", written from 1922, when he was one of the first major composers after Saint-Saens to write music specifically for film, and La Roue of 1922, Napoleon of 1927, Les Miserables of 1934, Mayerling of 1936, Pygmalion of 1938, Mermoz of 1943, Bourdelle of 1950, and material migrates, the La Roue montage music is reused in Pacific 231, and the watchable behaviour is music that must hit a cut, carry a montage, then get out, and the discipline of the cue feeding the concision of the symphonic movements, and collaboration is the norm here, with Arthur Hoeree, Maurice Jaubert, Darius Milhaud, Tibor Harsanyi and Alexis Roland-Manuel.

## M13. Local colour quoted, not pasted

What it is: in Symphony No. 4 "Deliciae Basilienses" of 1946, the second movement ends with a solo horn quoting Franz Abt's setting of Johann Peter Hebel's Basel poem "Z'Basel, a mim Rhi", and the third movement stages a carnival march of Guggenmusik, Basel brass bands with piccolo and Basel drum, then a nostalgic phrase and a brief mocking farewell, and in Mimaamaquim of 1946-47, Halbreich hears "the typical intonations of synagogue chant", and the watchable behaviour is a place evoked by its own tunes, placed inside a symphonic argument, then gently mocked and let go, and Swiss identity as material, not as flag.

**Takeaway:** the local tune must be argued with, not pasted on, and a place evoked by a flag has not been quoted.

## M14. Architecture first, Bach as model

What it is: Honegger's stated credo, quoted in Wikipedia's Style section, "I place great importance on musical architecture, which I would never want to see sacrificed for literary or pictorial reasons. I have a perhaps exaggerated tendency to seek polyphonic complexity. My great model is J. S. Bach... I believe we should use the harmonic materials developed by the school that preceded us, but in a different way, as a base for lines and rhythms... I do not subscribe to the cult of the Music Hall or of the street fair; on the contrary, I am committed to chamber music and symphonic music in their most serious and austere aspects", and the book's own credo, per Hyperion, "write a music which would be understandable to the great majority of listeners and at the same time sufficiently free of banality to interest the connoisseurs", and the watchable behaviour is even the descriptive pieces, Pacific 231 and Rugby, built like buildings first and pictures second.

**Takeaway:** the architecture must come before the picture, and a piece that sacrifices its building for its image has broken the credo.

## M15. The poet as co-architect

What it is: Honegger's two great collaborations give the librettist the structure and the composer the sound, and Morax's play supplies Le Roi David's 27 sections, and Claudel's eleven-scene poem with prologue supplies Jeanne's backbone, and Claudel also wrote La Danse des morts of 1938 and Le Soulier de satin of 1943, incidental music, and Honegger said of Jeanne that he had merely put his services "at the disposal of" the poet, and the watchable behaviour is the words setting the joints and the music filling the spans, and the collaboration a division of labour, not a setting of a text.

**Takeaway:** the poet must set the joints, and a libretto treated as words to decorate has not been a co-architecture.

## M16. Chamber music as the laboratory

What it is: the quartets and small pieces are where the language is tested before the orchestra gets it, and Quartet No. 1 of 1917 is the first fully accomplished masterpiece, and Quartet No. 3 of 1936-37 is "unquestionably the pinnacle of his chamber music", per Jacques Tchamkerten, quoted in Wikipedia, and after Quartet No. 3 he largely stopped writing chamber music, and the watchable behaviour is Danse de la chevre as one flute, one line, no hiding place, and the Rhapsody of 1917 mixing two flutes, clarinet and piano in a combination no orchestra would allow, and small forces, hard problems.

**Takeaway:** the small piece must test what the orchestra will later carry, and a quartet that only charms has not been a laboratory.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Honegger's work is that a Swiss in Paris built buildings first and pictures second, and the locomotive is a chorale chopped into smaller and smaller pieces, and the rugby match is a rhythm argued by colliding phrases, and the oratorio is a play with the stage replaced by a narrator and the orchestra replaced by the instruments at hand, and the symphony is a liturgy that supplies the shape and not the notes, and the trumpet is withheld until the last minute because scarcity is the mechanism, and the symphony ends three times on the same low D because the signature is a verdict, and the spoken Joan stands against the singing saints because the poet set the joints, and the strange instrument arrives exactly where the drama needs an unearthly colour and then leaves, and the serious symphonist writes the operetta that runs 800 performances because the same architect builds the joke, and the film cue teaches the symphony its concision, and the Basel tune is quoted inside the symphony and then gently mocked, and Bach is the model and architecture is never sacrificed for the picture, and the pupil who came to Paris on a Fulbright learned more from the scores on the shelves than from any lesson, and the whole life is one long proof that a composer can be both the machine and the oratorio, the scandal and the communicative turn, the Swiss and the Parisian, and the proof is the fifth symphony, and it ends on three Ds, and the Ds are the verdict, and the verdict is that the end of civilization was near, and the music says it anyway, and the saying of it is the architecture.
