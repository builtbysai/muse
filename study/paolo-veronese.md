# Paolo Veronese: Study Notes

**Site:** Paolo Caliari, known as Paolo Veronese (1528, Verona - died Tuesday 19 April 1588, Venice), Italian Renaissance painter based in Venice, known for extremely large history paintings of religion and mythology, such as The Wedding at Cana (1563) and The Feast in the House of Levi (1573); with Titian, a generation older, and Tintoretto, a decade senior, one of the "great trio that dominated Venetian painting of the cinquecento"; known as a supreme colorist. Born in Verona in 1528, fifth child of the stonecutter Gabriele Caliari, apprenticed by 1541 to Antonio Badile, whose daughter Elena he married in 1565 or 1566; moved to Venice in 1553 on his first state commission, ceilings for the Sala del Consiglio dei Dieci in the Doge's Palace; decorated San Sebastiano from 1555 to 1570 and was buried there; frescoed the Villa Barbaro at Maser with Palladio c.1560-61; painted the Wedding at Cana (1562-63, 677 x 994 cm, more than 130 figures, 324 silver ducats plus a tun of wine) for the refectory of San Giorgio Maggiore; summoned before the Venetian Holy Inquisition on Wednesday 18 July 1573 over the Feast in the House of Levi and answered by re-titling the picture, not repainting it; headed a family workshop -- brother Benedetto, sons Gabriele and Carlo, nephew Luigi Benfatto -- which traded as "Haeredes Pauli" for a decade after his death. The record is strong: the National Gallery's Penny 2008 catalogue entries on the Four Allegories of Love and The Family of Darius, the National Gallery of Art artist page, the full transcript of the 1573 Inquisition hearing, and a 2023 pigment study, all read end to end. ## M1. The Luminous Mid-Tone Ground

The canvas is prepared with a thin gesso ground (calcium sulphate, probably gypsum) coated with a thin imprimitura of light greyish-brown -- lead white with finely ground black and an earth pigment (Penny 2008, National Gallery Four Allegories of Love catalogue, "Materials and Technique"). The same tinted ground is found in the three Frick/Met allegories and in the small copper painting studied by the Granada group (gypsum mixed with ochre earths, MDPI 2023). Mechanically: instead of painting up from white or down from dark, Veronese starts on a warm mid-value, so both lights and darks are built as departures from a middle that already glows; the ground stays active in thin passages and unifies the picture. This is the physical basis of the "silvery" Veronese light: shadows are coloured, not black, because they sit on a light, warm ground rather than on darkness. The watchable behaviour is a picture that glows from the middle out: lights and darks both departing from a warm mid-tone that never goes black. **Takeaway:** the luminous mid-tone ground -- a thin gesso ground with a grey-brown imprimitura, light built on a middle that already glows, shadows coloured because they sit on light, not on dark.

## M2. The Luminosity Sketch -- Value First, Colour Second

In the copper painting studied by the Granada group, above the gypsum and ochre ground there is a sketch made with lead white in the highlights and bone black in the dark and shadow areas -- "suggesting that the artist performed a preliminary study of the luminosity of the scene" (MDPI 2023, abstract and section 3.2). XRF and XRD show lead white is more abundant in the inner than the upper layer, and in flesh tones the artist mixed lead white with a low proportion of vermilion plus Naples yellow "to soften the high whiteness" and "lower the color saturation... and increase their lightness". The same logic appears in the Allegories: a bold preparatory underdrawing, drawn in black chalk or charcoal and painted in a liquid black medium (and here and there, very finely, in orange-brown), is discernible beneath the paint (Penny 2008). Mechanically: value is decided first, in monochrome; colour is then laid over a value map that is already complete, so the finished picture reads as light first, colour second. The watchable behaviour is the picture arriving as light before it arrives as colour: a monochrome value map first, then the hues laid over it. **Takeaway:** the luminosity sketch -- lead white for light and bone black for shadow before any colour, value decided first in monochrome, colour laid over a complete value map. (Attribution caveat: the Granada copper painting is attributed to Veronese only by an inscription on its back, "Boceto di Pablo Veronese"; the authors state explicitly the study "has no intention of attributing this painting to the brush of Veronese". Its palette findings are consistent with his known palette, not proof of his hand -- see C39.)

## M3. The Sky Painted Last, Thickened Around the Figures

In the Allegories, "the sky was completed late in the painting process and it is often thicker around the figures, redefining the position of a head (as in the man's head in Respect) or of an arm (as in the man's arm in Happy Union). The very last passages were, however, often painted on top of the sky -- some of the leaves and also the yellow shoulder-buttons in Respect, and some of the foliage above Cupid's bow in Scorn" (Penny 2008). Mechanically: the background is not a backdrop laid down first; it is a late, corrective layer that re-cuts the silhouettes, so figures are carved out of the sky rather than pasted onto it, and the edge between figure and air stays crisp and luminous. There are many minor revisions and pentimenti (the girdle in Happy Union moved higher, Cupid's arms repositioned in Scorn, the red curtain in Respect greatly extended). The watchable behaviour is the sky arriving last and re-cutting the figures: silhouettes sharpened by the background, not by the outline. **Takeaway:** the sky painted last -- a late, thickened background layer that re-cuts heads and arms, figures carved out of the sky, the edge between figure and air crisp and luminous.

## M4. Glaze-Built Saturation -- Transparent Colour over White and over Verdigris

Deep greens are "very rich, deeply saturated greens composed of copper glazes over verdigris and lead white" (Penny 2008). In the copper painting, the greyish passages are "mixing lazurite and lead white and traces of vermilion applied in a smooth layer as a glaze... in very thin coats, [which] reveals the tonality of the previous undercoats", and dark tones are obtained by intentional addition of lazurite (51-60 percent in the darkest zones). Flesh tones are lead white mixed with a low proportion of vermilion (MDPI 2023). Mechanically: colour is built by stacking thin transparent layers so light passes through the glaze, hits the white underlayer and returns -- the source of the "stained-glass" saturation and the silvery flesh. The Frick/Met study notes Veronese "made a very refined sketch, worked the chiaroscuro, deepened his study of color, and applied light impastos with innumerable transparencies" (quoted in MDPI 2023, section 4). The watchable behaviour is colour that glows from inside: light passing through thin glaze, hitting white, and coming back. **Takeaway:** glaze-built saturation -- transparent colour stacked in thin layers over white and over verdigris, light returning through the glaze, stained-glass saturation and silvery flesh.

## M5. Colour-Modelled Shadow -- Hue Preserved in Shadow, No Violent Contrast

Delacroix wrote that Veronese "made light without violent contrasts, 'which we are always told is impossible, and maintained the strength of hue in shadow'" (Gowing, Paintings in the Louvre, 1987, via Wikipedia). Gautier (1860) said he "established the harmony of natural tones in place of the modeling in dark and light that remained the method of academic chiaroscuro". Britannica: his mature work shows "luminous passages that endow even the shadows with colour". Mechanically: shadow is a darker, cooler or warmer version of the local colour, never a blackened version of it; the picture is modelled by hue-shift across a narrow value range, which is why his crowds stay legible and festive instead of collapsing into chiaroscuro drama. This is the single most transferable mechanism: light without contrast, strength of hue in shadow. The watchable behaviour is a crowd that stays readable in shadow: every dark passage still coloured, nothing falling to black. **Takeaway:** colour-modelled shadow -- shadow as a darker version of the local hue, never blackened, modelling by hue-shift across a narrow value range, light without violent contrast.

## M6. Architecture Parallel to the Picture Plane -- The Stage, the Frieze, the Low Horizon

"In the refectory paintings, as in The Family of Darius before Alexander (1565-1570), Veronese arranged the architecture to run mostly parallel to the picture plane, accentuating the processional character of the composition... the narrative of the picture could best be absorbed as a colorful diversion... they illustrate the carefully composed movement of their subjects along a primarily horizontal axis. Most of all they are about the incandescence of light and color." (Wikipedia.) TheArtStory: "the architectural surroundings are constructed to form a tableau with the low horizon helping replicate the experience of watching a popular stage production." Mechanically: a flat, frontal architectural frame (colonnades, loggias, balustrades) holds a horizontal procession of figures; depth is lateral, not plunging, so a hundred figures can be read at once without confusion. The watchable behaviour is a frieze that reads left to right like a procession: the architecture a stage, the figures a pageant moving across it, the eye never forced into depth. **Takeaway:** architecture parallel to the picture plane -- a flat frontal frame holding a horizontal procession, depth lateral not plunging, a hundred figures readable at once, the low horizon of a stage production.

## M7. Di Sotto in Su -- Ceiling Foreshortening and the Oblique View

Ceiling painting "requires the foreshortening of figures with the effect (when seen from the ground up) that the figures are suspended in air" -- di sotto in su, "from below to above" (TheArtStory, on the Esther ceiling). For ceiling compartments Veronese used "oblique perspective": "the angle of foreshortening corresponds to a viewpoint obliquely beneath the painting, avoiding the extreme distortion of figures imagined as directly above the viewer's head" (Web Gallery of Art, on the Allegories). In the Allegories the compositions are built on strong diagonals with a "studied avoidance of verticals", and the architectural elements are tilted because the paintings were meant to be seen from below (Penny 2008). Mechanically: figures are foreshortened to a viewpoint below and to one side, so the ceiling reads as open sky rather than as a picture hung overhead. The watchable behaviour is a ceiling that opens: figures suspended in air, architecture tilted to a viewpoint below, the roof dissolved into sky. **Takeaway:** di sotto in su -- foreshortening to a viewpoint obliquely below, strong diagonals, no verticals, the ceiling reading as open sky.

## M8. Trompe-l'Oeil Architecture That Breaks the Wall

At the Villa Barbaro the frescoes are "the triumph of Venetian colorism and trompe-l'oeil, the art of painted architecture: columns and arches painted by the artist frame landscapes that echo the real views seen through the large windows" (villadimaser.it). Britannica: he "brilliantly interpreted the villa's Palladian rhythms, breaking through the walls with illusionistic landscapes and opening the ceilings to blue skies with figures from classical mythology". At Maser, painted doors open onto painted servants, and the family appears at a painted balustrade above the real room; painted shadows are matched to each room's real light source (leg 2). Mechanically: painted architecture continues the real architecture, so the wall dissolves; the picture is not a window in the wall but a continuation of the room. The same device, in oil, governs the refectory feasts, which were painted as visual extensions of the dining room itself -- the Wedding at Cana was built to be seen from below, its bottom edge 2.5 m from the refectory floor, so the painted architecture reads as an extension of the room. The watchable behaviour is the wall giving way: painted columns continuing real ones, a painted door opening onto a painted servant, the room extending past its own walls. **Takeaway:** trompe-l'oeil architecture that breaks the wall -- painted architecture continuing the real, the wall dissolving, the picture a continuation of the room rather than a window in it.

## M9. The Workshop as a Production System -- Modelli, Ricordi, Variants, and the Heirs

Veronese made compositional sketches, chiaroscuro modelli and ricordi (records of finished compositions) that the workshop reused for decades; he "produced variants of his own most successful compositions", and after his death the family "continued to use his drawings" and signed "Haeredes Pauli" (NGA; Wikipedia). A single sheet of pen-and-wash studies for the Allegories contains figure designs for all four paintings at once, showing compositions developed simultaneously as a system of reusable figure groups, with trees and architecture "merely accessories" (Penny 2008). The wedded couple in Happy Union were conceived advancing from the left and then reversed in the painting "to balance the diagonal movement". Mechanically: the invention is modular -- figure groups are designed once, then recombined, reversed, and re-scaled across commissions. The workshop is a parts library, not a copy shop. The watchable behaviour is the same figure group returning in a new picture, reversed or re-scaled: a cast of reusable bodies, recombined like a repertory company. **Takeaway:** the workshop as a production system -- modelli, ricordi and variants, figure groups designed once and recombined, reversed and re-scaled, the family firm trading as Haeredes Pauli after his death.

## M10. Pigment Economy -- Four Blues, Deployed by Cost and Place

"Earlier studies have reported Veronese's use of four blue pigments: smalt, ultramarine, azurite, and indigo. The particular way in which Veronese applied the first three to construct the skies in the paintings investigated for this study appears unique in comparison with his reported use in other paintings." (Frick/Met technical study, via ResearchGate.) In the National Gallery's Veronese holdings, "smalt is used for the skies in The Family of Darius before Alexander, The Dream of Saint Helena, and the four Allegories of Love; smalt glazed with ultramarine is used in a small area in The Consecration of Saint Nicholas; and azurite is used in..." other works. The Wedding at Cana contract specified lapis lazuli for the blues -- the expensive pigment reserved for the most prestigious commission -- while the Allegories use no ultramarine at all. Mechanically: blue is a budget and a hierarchy -- cheap smalt for large sky fields (which then fades, changing the picture over centuries), azurite for mid-tones, costly ultramarine only where the contract or the patron paid for it, sometimes glazed over smalt. The present grey skies of the Allegories are the long-run cost of that economy. The watchable behaviour is the sky changing colour over centuries: smalt fading to grey, the picture ageing visibly, the cheap blue exacting its price in time. **Takeaway:** pigment economy -- four blues deployed by cost and place, smalt for the big sky fields, ultramarine only where the contract paid for it, the faded grey skies of today the long-run cost of the choice.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Verona training and early Verona, c.1541-1553. Apprenticed to Antonio Badile by 1541 (registered as a helper; Badile later his father-in-law), then to Giovanni Francesco Caroto in 1544; by 1544 he was no longer living with Badile -- his gifts had outgrown the workshop. The earliest independent works (from c.1546) are for local Veronese patrons. The Bevilacqua-Lazise Altarpiece (1548) and the Lament over the Dead Christ (c.1548) are devotional, upright altarpieces: figures stacked in a shallow, symmetrical order (Virgin above, saints below, donors at the corners), colour still carrying the imprint of Badile and of the Mannerism then dominant in Verona. The Conversion of Mary Magdalene (c.1545-48) is a single-figure devotional panel. The move outward comes with the Temptation of St Anthony (1552-53) for Mantua Cathedral: a competition piece, painted in situ, in which Giulio Romano's muscular Mantuan mannerism is the visible model. Scale is still altarpiece scale (198 x 151 cm); the drama is in the bodies, not yet in the architecture. What is already there: the radiant palette breaking away from Mannerist gloom, and an appetite for crowded, incident-filled scenes.

Phase 2 -- Early Venice: ceilings and the church of San Sebastiano, 1553-1556. He moves to Venice in 1553 on the strength of his first state commission -- fresco ceilings for the Sala del Consiglio dei Dieci and Sala dei Tre Capi in the Doge's Palace, replacing rooms lost in the 1547 fire. Jupiter Hurling Thunderbolts at the Vices (c.1553) is the breakthrough: a single god plunging from the clouds, painted di sotto in su, figures foreshortened so they seem to float above the viewer. The same years bring the San Sebastiano commission from his fellow Veronese, Prior Bernardo Torlioni (1555): first the sacristy ceiling -- Coronation of the Virgin (1555) with the Four Evangelists -- then, on a contract signed December 1555, the three great Esther ovals of the nave ceiling, installed 1556. The change from Verona is total: the picture is now a ceiling, the viewer is below, and the whole composition is engineered for that viewpoint -- steep foreshortening, horses that seem to trample the spectator, architecture (stairs, columns, balustrades) that continues past the frame. Colour becomes the structure: the Esther ceiling is a riot of reds, blues, greens, oranges, yellows and gold, and the skies are already the pale, luminous blue that will fade (smalt) in later centuries. The 1557 Marciana Library ceiling wins him the prize judged by Titian and Sansovino and establishes him as a master among Venetian contemporaries. By 1556 he is also painting the first refectory feast -- the Turin Feast in the House of Simon -- and the Annunciation (attribution debated) shows the same moment: an altarpiece format already loosening into a horizontal, architectural scene.

Phase 3 -- Maturity and the feasts, 1556-1570. This is the phase of the great horizontal machines. Supper at Emmaus (c.1559-60) is the first biblical feast treated as a large anachronistic Venetian group portrait, and it sets the template: Christ at the centre of a table, an aristocratic family in contemporary dress around him, children and dogs in the foreground, four classical columns and a pedimented door behind. The Villa Barbaro frescoes (c.1560-61) transfer the same system to a house: trompe-l'oeil architecture that extends Palladio's real rooms, life-size figures who live in the painted space, shadows painted to match each room's real light. The Consecration of Saint Nicholas (1562) -- three altarpieces in about three months -- shows the workshop speed that will define his practice. Then the Wedding at Cana (1562-63): 677 x 994 cm, more than 130 figures, a contract that demands the maximum number of figures and the best colours (lapis lazuli specified). The compositional discovery of this phase, stated by the sources read: architecture arranged mostly parallel to the picture plane, which accentuates the processional character -- figures move along a horizontal axis like a frieze, and the narrative is absorbed as a colourful diversion rather than a drama. Emotion is deliberately moderate; the subject is light and colour. The Martyrdom of Saint George (1566) and Saint Barnabas Healing the Sick (c.1566) apply the same crowded, processional machinery to martyrdom and miracle, in direct answer to the Council of Trent's 1563 decrees on saints. The Family of Darius (c.1565-70) is the secular summit of the phase -- the same frieze, the same parallel architecture, but more lucid, with the rhythm of arches and fountain niche tied to the movement of the figures, and X-rays showing Veronese reworking the centre mid-painting. The two Frick allegories (c.1565) open a new genre -- the large-scale moral allegory, his first works to cross the Alps. He marries Elena Badile in 1565 or 1566; the family workshop (brother Benedetto, sons Carlo and Gabriele, nephew Luigi dal Friso) is in place. Scale peaks in this phase; colour is at its lightest, with pale shadows and sensuous textures.

Phase 4 -- The Inquisition and after, 1573-1578. The Feast in the House of Levi (1573) is the hinge. Painted as a Last Supper to replace Titian's burnt original, it is the biggest and most crowded of the feasts -- and the one that breaks the old licence. Three months after completion he is summoned before the Venetian Holy Inquisition (Wednesday 18 July 1573) to explain the buffoons, drunken Germans, dwarves and animals; told to repaint within three months, he instead retitles the picture and changes nothing. The sources read stress that the tribunal was cautionary rather than punitive, and that his survival implies discreet patrician support -- but the terms of the trade have changed: a decade earlier the Benedictines told him to fit in as many figures as he liked; now the Church polices who and what may appear. What changes in the work: the NGA biography notes a gradual darkening of his palette after about 1570, and his mature style otherwise never undergoes radical alteration. The Four Allegories of Love (c.1575) belong to this moment -- near-square ceiling canvases, figures as the whole composition (trees and architecture reduced to accessories), diagonals with a studied avoidance of verticals, painted for a ceiling and disconcerting on a wall. The small Rape of Europa (c.1570) and Mars and Venus United by Love (1570s) show the turn toward smaller mythologies for northern courts (Maximilian II, then Rudolf II in Prague). The workshop's role grows; attribution problems begin here, not earlier.

Phase 5 -- Late work, 1578-1588. The last decade is dominated by the Doge's Palace redecoration after the fires of 1574 and 1577: the large Rape of Europa (c.1578-80) in the Anticollegio, and the Apotheosis of Venice (c.1579-82) -- 904 x 579 cm, the centrepiece of the Sala del Maggior Consiglio ceiling, Venice personified and crowned above the Doge's throne, its perspective computed for one precise observer position. Britannica's assessment, quoted in the sources read, is that in these late palace decorations "a greater participation of his workshop" is discernible; the NGA states that after 1580 "it is rare that we can feel confident that Veronese's was the sole hand involved". Alongside the public machines he returns to small, close, devotional formats with a new intimacy: Venus and Adonis (early 1580s), painted after a period in Rome and modelled on antique sculpture; the Crucifixion (c.1582), a 102 x 102 cm square; the nocturnal Adoration of the Magi in Santa Corona, Vicenza, praised by Britannica for a new intimacy "without renunciation of the characteristic Veronesian richness of colour, laid on with the minute, precious brushstrokes also used in small canvases, both sacred and profane, executed during this period". The Dream of Saint Helena exists in two versions, the later (c.1580, Vatican) very different from the National Gallery one -- evidence of his willingness to re-think a subject rather than repeat it. The last known work, the Conversion of Saint Pantaleon (1587), closes the career where it began -- an altarpiece, but one with a donor painted larger than convention allows, a winged serpent-dragon fleeing at the corner, and, per the 2025-26 conservation, dramatic brushwork and powerful chiaroscuro that "characterize his late style". He dies of a fever on Tuesday 19 April 1588, aged 59-60, and is buried in San Sebastiano, the church he decorated for fifteen years.

What changed, what stayed constant: scale -- altarpiece scale (c.150-200 cm) to ceiling ovals and refectory walls (up to 677 x 994 cm and 555 x 1309 cm) to a late return to small devotional and mythological canvases (102 x 102 cm, 162 x 191 cm) alongside the largest palace ceilings. Colour -- from a Mannerist, Badile-tinted palette to the lightest, most luminous colour in Venetian painting (pale shadows, ultramarine blues, smalt skies, orpiment and realgar golds), then a gradual darkening after c.1570 and stronger chiaroscuro in the last works. Composition -- from stacked, symmetrical altarpiece order to the horizontal processional frieze with architecture parallel to the picture plane; from a single vanishing point to the multiple converging diagonals of the Levi (a deliberate answer to a 13-metre wall seen from many angles); from wall pictures to ceilings engineered for a viewer below (di sotto in su, tilted architecture, cut-off feet). Figure handling -- from devotional stillness to crowds of 130 figures, contemporary portraits inserted as musicians and guests, children and dogs in the foreground, then, late, to a few figures in close, intimate formats and to allegorical figure groups where architecture is only an accessory. Architecture -- at first a backdrop, then the machine itself: Palladian loggias, triumphal arches, staircases that funnel the eye to Christ, trompe-l'oeil rooms that extend real ones, and in the allegories, deliberately reduced to scaffolding. Constant throughout: colour as the organizing principle rather than drawing or drama; the Venetian pageant as the vehicle for sacred and mythological narrative; the ceiling as his native format; the workshop system (family, "Haeredes Pauli" after his death); and the refusal to repaint under pressure -- the Levi was retitled, never altered.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is that the pageant is the subject, and the sacred event is the excuse for it. In the Wedding at Cana the miracle itself -- water turning to wine -- is visible only to the spectator and two servants in the foreground; the bride and groom are displaced to the far right, and the centre of the table belongs to Christ, Mary and the apostles as the still point of a feast that is really about 130 Venetians in their best clothes. In the Feast in the House of Levi the Last Supper is a small central episode inside a vast Venetian pageant, and the tribunal's objection was exactly the point: the Germans, the dwarves, the jester with the parrot, the man with the nosebleed and the dog are not decoration on the sacred subject, they are the subject, and Veronese's defence -- "we painters take the same liberties as poets and madmen take", "when I have some space left over in a picture, I adorn it with figures of my own invention" -- is a theory of painting in which the filling of the space IS the work. Colour is the structure, not the ornament: the picture is organized by hue-shift across a narrow value range, shadows kept coloured, light made without violent contrast, so a hundred figures stay legible at once and the whole wall reads as one luminous field. The architecture is the machine that makes this possible: a flat, frontal, Palladian frame holds the procession, the low horizon makes the viewer a spectator at a stage production, and the ceiling is engineered for a viewer standing below, so the roof dissolves into sky. The workshop is the other half of the logic: figure groups designed once, recombined and reversed across commissions, the family firm keeping the drawings in production after his death as Haeredes Pauli -- the invention is modular because the demand was industrial, one feast after another, one ceiling after another, for thirty-five years. And the present-day appearance of the pictures is part of the logic now, whether he intended it or not: the smalt skies have faded to grey and the greens have darkened, so the silvery, muted tonality the nineteenth century loved is partly an accident of pigment chemistry -- the pictures are still changing colour, four centuries after they left the studio, and the cheap blue is still exacting its price. The Inquisition episode is the proof of the whole system: told to repaint, he changed a title instead, and the picture survived unchanged because the pageant was never the Church's to police -- it was Venice's, and Venice paid for it.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Veronese was the third member, with Titian and Tintoretto, of the "great trio that dominated Venetian painting of the cinquecento" -- and the youngest of the three. Evidence: leg 1.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] He is known as a supreme colorist: his reputation rests on colour before anything else, and the French 19th century ranked him above Titian, Rubens and Rembrandt as a colorist. Evidence: leg 1.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] His mature style was essentially fixed by the late 1550s and never radically altered afterwards; the only documented shift is a gradual darkening of the palette after about 1570. Evidence: leg 1.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] He was the leading Venetian painter of ceilings, and his first Venetian reputation was made as a ceiling painter (Doge's Palace 1553, Marciana Library 1557, San Sebastiano Esther ceiling 1556), before the great feasts. Evidence: leg 1.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] His most famous works are elaborate narrative cycles in which the sacred event is deliberately secondary to the spectacle: in the Wedding at Cana the miracle is visible only to the spectator and two servants; in the Feast in the House of Levi the Last Supper is a small central episode inside a vast Venetian pageant. Evidence: leg 1.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The Inquisition hearing of Wednesday 18 July 1573 was the only real setback of his career, and it was resolved by re-titling, not repainting -- evidence of both his diplomatic skill and his patrician protection. Evidence: leg 1.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] His workshop was a family firm (brother Benedetto, sons Gabriele and Carlo, nephew Luigi Benfatto), and after 1580 it is rarely possible to be confident Veronese's was the sole hand involved; the firm traded as "Haeredes Pauli" for a decade after his death. Evidence: leg 1.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] He was a painter of prodigious facility who "very rarely produced small pictures: the spacious was his element" (1911 Britannica); his masterpieces are measured in tens of square metres, and the Wedding at Cana (67.29 m2) is the largest painting in the Louvre. Evidence: leg 1.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] He was admired by the greatest later painters -- Rubens, Watteau, Tiepolo, Delacroix, Renoir, Velazquez (who acquired his Venus and Adonis), and Cezanne -- while modern criticism has often ranked him the least appreciated of the great trio, finding his work unable to express "the profound, the human, or the sublime". Evidence: leg 1.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] His colour today is partly not his colour: the smalt skies have faded to grey, copper-resinate greens have darkened, red lakes and orpiment have degraded, so the muted pearlescent tonality of the Allegories of Love as now seen is in part an accident of pigment chemistry. Evidence: leg 1.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] The Wedding at Cana (1562-63, 677 x 994 cm, 67.29 m2) is the largest painting in the Louvre; the contract of 6 June 1562 demanded the maximum number of figures and lapis lazuli blues, and the price was 324 silver ducats, plus living expenses and a tun of wine. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] The Feast in the House of Levi has no single vanishing point; its diagonals converge at several points, a deliberate choice for a 13-metre wall seen from many angles. Evidence: leg 2.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] The Inquisition order was answered by re-titling, not repainting -- the only documented case of its kind in his career: the tribunal decreed he correct the painting within three months at his own expense, and instead he added the Luke 5 inscription, re-titled it Feast in the House of Levi, and was not bothered again. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] After 1580 it is rarely possible to be confident Veronese's was the sole hand involved; Britannica notes greater workshop participation in the late Doge's Palace decorations, including the large Rape of Europa. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] His palette darkened gradually after about 1570, but his mature style never underwent radical alteration; this consistency, plus his habit of producing variants of his own most successful compositions, creates problems of dating. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P16. [FROM RECORD] The Villa Barbaro frescoes (c.1560-61) are his most important fresco cycle; Britannica calls Maser "a fundamental stage in the evolution of the art of Veronese and in the development of Venetian painting": Mannerism had given way to harmonious rhythms and superb colour. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P17. [FROM RECORD] The Apotheosis of Venice (c.1579-82, 904 x 579 cm) was computed for one precise observer position above the Doge's throne (2018 photogrammetry study); only two known Veronese modelli survive, one the subject of a 2015-16 export case. Evidence: leg 2.
- P18. [FROM RECORD] The small Rape of Europa (c.1570, 59.5 x 70 cm, National Gallery) is the smallest dimensioned work in the table, not the 102 x 102 cm Crucifixion (c.1582) -- see C44. Evidence: leg 2, corrected by the parent.
- P19. [FROM RECORD] The last known work is the Conversion of Saint Pantaleon (1587, San Pantalon, Venice), commissioned by parish priest Bartolomeo Borghi, who is painted at left holding the boy dead of a snake bite -- an unusually prominent donor in a high altarpiece; the 2025-26 conservation revealed the vibrancy of the late palette, dramatic brushwork and powerful chiaroscuro of his late style. Evidence: leg 2.
- P20. [INFERRED] The feast paintings were painted as extensions of the rooms they hung in -- Cana's bottom edge 2.5 m above the refectory floor, Levi's twin staircases funnelling the eye to Christ, the painted architecture reading as a continuation of the dining room -- so the picture's job was to continue the room, not to hang in it. Inferred and labelled as such: no source in the legs states the room-extension purpose in exactly these words, though the architecture-as-continuation is documented at Maser and the Cana viewing height is documented. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C30. DEATH DATE -- 19 VS 9 VS 20 APRIL 1588. Wikipedia, the Catholic Encyclopedia, Wikimedia Commons and the San Samuele parish death register (cited by Craste, 1911) all give 19 April 1588. Britannica (current online article) gives 9 April 1588 in both its header and its FAQ -- leg 1 judges this a transposition error in Britannica's AI-assisted summary, and 19 April is the better-attested date. The 1911 Britannica gives "the 20th (or perhaps 19th) of April 1588", and the 1855 Farquhar catalogue also says 20 April. Use 19 April 1588, and flag Britannica's 9 April as suspect. (Leg 1, contradiction 1; parent re-ran the dates: Tuesday 19 April 1588, Saturday 9 April 1588, Wednesday 20 April 1588.)
- C31. BIRTH YEAR -- 1528 VS 1532. The Verona census attests 1528; Carlo Ridolfi (1648) gave 1532, repeated by the 1911 Britannica ("born in Verona in 1528 according to the best authorities (Zanetti and others), or in 1532 according to Ridolfi"). 1528 is settled. (Leg 1, contradiction 2.)
- C32. MARRIAGE YEAR -- 1565 VS 1566. Wikipedia says he married Elena Badile in 1565; the National Gallery (London) and TheArtStory say 1566. Unresolved; both dates appear in reputable sources. (Leg 1, contradiction 3.)
- C33. CAUSE OF DEATH -- FEVER VS PNEUMONIA. Britannica and the 1911 Britannica say he "contracted a fever and died after a few days of illness"; TheArtStory says he "died from pneumonia in 1588". Fever is the contemporary account; pneumonia is a modern gloss. (Leg 1, contradiction 4.)
- C34. ROME TRIP -- DATE AND EXISTENCE DISPUTED. Thyssen-Bornemisza: Rome in 1560. 1911 Britannica: Rome in 1563, in the suite of ambassador Girolamo Grimani. Mrs Arthur Bell (1904): some authorities place the journey considerably later, "and some few deny that he was ever in the Eternal City". No document settles it. (Leg 1, contradiction 5.)
- C35. THE FEAST IN THE HOUSE OF SIMON -- WHICH VERSION, WHICH DATE. Sources conflate at least three versions: 1556 (Turin, Galleria Sabauda), 1567 (Brera, Milan, 275 x 710 cm) and 1570 (Versailles, 454 x 974 cm).
