## The life: the painter who removed the object

Regina Silveira was born in Porto Alegre in 1939 and began studying art in 1950 under the expressionist painter Ibere Camargo, with whom she formed a lasting relationship. (C13370, leg 1) She took her BFA at the Instituto de Artes of the Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul in 1958 -- the year this study prints, following her own words in the 2018 Newcity interview ("In 1958, Silveira graduated"), the Hammer Museum and EN Wikipedia; the Alexander Gray CV, Luciana Brito, PT Wikipedia, Itau and Escritorio de Arte give 1959, and Intelligere reconciles the split by recording the bacharelado concluded in 1958 followed by an "Aperfeicoamento em Pintura" (improvement course) with Malagoli in 1959, so 1959 may be the improvement course misread as the degree year. See Corrections. (C13543, leg 1 C13370, C13372, C13377, C13380; leg 3) She also took a teaching degree in Drawing at the Pontificia Universidade Catolica do Rio Grande do Sul. (C13381, leg 1) Her first series came out of woodcuts made with patients of the Psychiatric Hospital of Porto Alegre -- "As Loucas" -- and she was already exhibiting in salons before graduating, with her first solo shows from 1958 (Edificio Mallet, Porto Alegre). (C13371, leg 1; C13380, leg 1) A formative anecdote she retells: in Camargo's 1961 class at the Atelie Livre, Camargo threw a student's soft-bristled brush out of the window onto a departing tram, then repainted the student's canvas with a stiff brush -- for Silveira, "the first clear notion of art as abstraction and language". (C13379, leg 1)

The career runs in seven phases, and the hinge is the same as the doorway: the successful painter and engraver who met Julio Plaza in Madrid in early 1967, abandoned that identity, and spent the next fifty years removing the object and keeping its shadow.

(1) Porto Alegre, 1950s-1966. Academic painting training, expressionist tutelage, lithography and woodcut; salon exhibiting from 1958; teaching at the Instituto de Artes, UFRGS, from 1964 to 1969 (Luciana Brito gives 1960-1969 -- see Corrections). (C13370, leg 1; C13377, leg 1)

(2) Madrid and the rupture, 1967-1969. A 1967 scholarship from the Instituto de Cultura Hispanica took her to Madrid, where she met Plaza. In her words she "left behind my origins in Porto Alegre as a successful painter and engraver to embrace the newest and most radical experiences in progress in contemporary art in my generation", and Plaza, then working in Concretism and Programmed Art, influenced "successive ruptures in my personal work". She also cites Pop Art, minimalism, situationist artists, Fluxus and new technologies as influences of the period. (C13378, leg 1) She married Plaza in February 1969. (C13470, leg 3)

(3) Puerto Rico, 1969-1973. The hinge period. The 34th Bienal says it directly: she "received her first training as an artist in Porto Alegre, Brazil, and Madrid, Spain, but it was in Puerto Rico that she developed the first exercises that delineated the core of her work". (C13375, leg 1) The couple taught at the University of Puerto Rico, Mayaguez campus, implanting an experimental teaching model in a small, closed, highly creative campus community; visitors included Germano Celant, Robert Morris and Barbara Rose. (C13378, leg 1) What changed in the work: she "experimented intensively with graphic techniques for the reproduction of the image and participated in an environment that was debating art as a territory for the circulation of images, discourses and ideological systems of representation". (C13375, leg 1) The ICAA summary of the period is a split: artisanal development on one side, modern reproduction techniques on the other, plus immersion in the "dematerialization" of the object. (C13384, leg 1) In Mayaguez in 1972 she organised with Plaza the mail-art publication "On/Off" (1972-1974), three editions of envelopes containing reproductions of works by different artists, distributed free to art institutions and people in the milieu. (C13207, #665; C13476, leg 3)

(4) Sao Paulo and the political 1970s, 1973-1979. She returned to Brazil in 1973 and settled permanently in Sao Paulo. (C13372, leg 1) She taught engraving at FAAP 1973-1985, invited by Walter Zanini, and joined the ECA-USP faculty in 1974, also on Zanini's invitation. (C13371, leg 1; C13477, leg 3) Under the military dictatorship (1964-1985) she made ephemeral conceptual work -- videos, pamphlets, mail art -- subverting expected meanings through paradox and enigma "with the aim of destabilizing perception", and became one of the first Brazilian artists to produce art for the electronic medium. (C13372, leg 1; C13378, leg 1) The 1970s series are print as politics: "Labirintos" (1971), "Middle Class & Co." (1971-1972), "Destruturas Urbanas", "Executivas", "Brazil Today" (1977). (C13384, leg 1) In 1978 she co-founded the Centro de Estudos ASTER with Plaza, Zanini and Donato Ferrari, a hybrid workshop-school in a house in the Perdizes neighbourhood offering lithography, photomechanics, offset, printmaking, photography and screenprinting, training visual artists outside the formal university apparatus; it ran to the first semester of 1981. (C13382, leg 1; C13383, leg 1)

(5) The shadow turn, c. 1980-1984. Her master's project "Anamorfas" (1980, supervisor Wolfgang Pfeiffer) distorted perspective from photographs of everyday objects, redrawn with compressions, dilations and folds; her doctorate "Simulacros" (1984, also under Pfeiffer) worked with shadows from invented projections, the casting object absent, in dialogue with Dadaism and Surrealism. (C13371, leg 1; C13381, leg 1) Between them came "Enigmas" (1981), four photograms in which she photographed an object and drew an opaque mask over it during exposure, "producing the illusion of shadows with no visible volume casting them". (C13374, leg 1) She dates her first shadows to 1981-1982, the "Enigma" series -- "four photograms of shadows of absent objects on top of a mound of banal things like a land line telephone, pan, my own handbag, typewriter". (C13378, leg 1) The public statement of the turn was "In Absentia M.D." at the 17th Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1983: painted shadows of Duchamp's "Bottle Rack" and "Bicycle Wheel" filling a 10 x 20 m room, cast from patently empty pedestals. (C13374, leg 1; C13381, leg 1) She studied Duchamp with Zanini at USP 1976-1980 but only saw Duchamp's work face to face at the Philadelphia Museum of Art in the early 1990s, while living in New York on a Guggenheim. (C13378, leg 1) She stresses the shadows "have never been natural images -- such as those focusing light over opaque objects -- rather they have been geometric constructions from the start". (C13378, leg 1)

(6) Architecture becomes the support, 1990s-2000s. After 1990 she moved to large-format installations and public works using mass-communication graphic production means to intervene in architectural space. (C13371, leg 1) Technique evolution, in her words: shadows first designed on graph paper and scaled up laboriously in black paint on walls, then black vinyl tape cut by hand or machine and adhered to vertical and horizontal surfaces; from the mid-1990s the computer gave control over remote projects and scaling to larger spaces, and works no longer needed physical transport -- they could be produced locally. (C13378, leg 1) The New York years, 1991-1994, were supported by the Guggenheim Fellowship (1990), the Pollock-Krasner Foundation Grant (1993) and a Fulbright (1994), with a Banff Centre residency in 1993 and a Civitella Ranieri residency in 1995 (fellowship dated 1996 in the CV -- see Corrections). (C13380, leg 1; C13372, leg 1) The indexical family widened from shadows to tracks: coyote tracks at MCA San Diego ("Gone Wild", 1996), a stampede of tracks across a whole building flank ("Tropel", 1998, 600 m2), human footprints accumulated into invasions ("Irruption" series, 2005-2006, up to 1,800 m2). (C13425, leg 2)

(7) Light, VR and public art, 2010s-2020s. Her own periodisation for the post-2000 work: migration "from the paradigm of the shadow to that of the light (two extremes of the same axis)" in works related to architecture and urban space. (C13378, leg 1) "Lumen" (2005) at the Palacio de Cristal in Madrid put a vinyl lightbulb on a glass building, dark by day and lit at night, with blue glass additions casting shadows and patterns on the floor; "Tramazul" (2010) covered the entire Paulista Avenue facade of MASP in printed vinyl sky, 2,400 m2; "Mundus Admirabilis" (2007) tattooed a government building with giant insects; "Abyssal" (2010) turned a gallery floor into a four-storey void. (C13370, leg 1; C13425, leg 2) The VR works "Infinities" (2017), "Odisseia" (2017) and "Borders" (2018) made the labyrinth a space the participant walks inside, trapped by rising walls or fleeing a glass maze that shatters underfoot. (C13379, leg 1) Recent work: the retrospective "Outros Paradoxos" (MAC-USP, 2021-22); "Destructuras de poder", La Virreina, Barcelona, 2024-25; "Modus Operandi", Instituto de Arte Contemporanea, Sao Paulo, 2025; and the permanent public commission "Paradise" at Houston airport IAH Terminal D, 2024-2025. (C13386, leg 1; C13385, leg 1; C13377, leg 1) She is represented by Luciana Brito Galeria and Alexander Gray Associates, and lives and works in Sao Paulo. (C13377, leg 1; C13380, leg 1)

Her own framing, for the study's voice: "I always understood that the representation of the real is pure artifice, coded and culturalized"; and on media: "I have been making free use of the media, without any of them representing me... I choose the medium that best solves my intentions." She rejects the label "conceptual artist" ("I define myself solely as an artist") but accepts "expanded graphics". (C13379, leg 1)

The through-line of the life is not a style but a subtraction repeated for fifty years: take a familiar thing, remove it, keep its shadow or trace, distort the trace in perspective, blow it up, and put it where it cannot belong -- on a ceiling, a facade, a floor that becomes an abyss, inside a light bulb. The support changes -- paper, wall, building, city, virtual space -- and the move does not. (C13544)

## M1 -- Shadow without a referent (skiagraphia)

A shadow is drawn, photographed, painted or cut in vinyl, but the object that should cast it is absent, empty, or never existed. Silveira: shadows are "geometric constructions from the start", never natural images; "representing shadows as indexes of absent objects". (C13428, leg 2; C13422, leg 2) The shadow is computed in distorted perspective -- double exposure with a masked silhouette, or a projective drawing transferred to wall and floor. The hinge work is "Enigma" (1981): each print made by double exposure, first a negative of the primary object (corded telephone, typewriter, pressure cooker, handbag), then the silhouette of a phantom object (fork, comb, hammer, saw) introduced using red film; MoMA: she photographed an object and "carefully drew an opaque mask over it during the exposure process" so the masks "produce the illusion of shadows, absent of visible volumes from which they were cast". (C13422, leg 2; C13421, leg 2) At monumental scale it is "In Absentia (M.D.)" (1983): the shadows of Duchamp's "Bottle Rack" and "Bicycle Wheel", "stretched in oblique opposition across the floor and up the panels that enclosed the space", cast from two absolutely empty sculpture pedestals. (C13423, leg 2; C13449, leg 2) The single-readymade reduction is "Masterpieces (In Absentia)" (1983): an oblique vinyl shadow of the "Bicycle Wheel" adhered to the wall, displaced from a real (empty) wood pedestal -- the shadow is the work; the pedestal is the joke. (C13422, leg 2) Watchable: the eye hunts for the caster and fails; the shadow is the only solid thing in the room. A familiar object appears only as a huge black silhouette sliding across floor and wall while its plinth stands empty. Lean in to an "Enigma" postcard and a fourth shadow appears: your own, holding the card. (C13422, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** a shadow earns its absence when the viewer can name the object that should be there and cannot find it. If the caster is visible anywhere in the frame, the shadow is decoration; if the plinth is empty and the shadow is the only solid thing in the room, it is the mechanism.

## M2 -- Anamorphosis: one viewpoint resolves, all others distort

An image stretched, compressed and folded on a grid so it is legible only from a single precise viewpoint (or while holding or tilting the surface). "Anamorfas" (1980) redrew household objects (corkscrews, scissors, sunglasses, coffee cups) from photographs with distortions -- "compressions, dilations and folds" -- mapped on a rigorous grid; the image is coherent only from one oblique viewpoint, and from everywhere else it is stretched and wrong. (C13423, leg 2; C13422, leg 2) The same move recurs in "Enigma" (the phantom shadows layer over the object instead of falling across it), in "Escada Inexplicavel II" (a staircase drawn in false perspective on a flat wall -- steps that rise, turn, and lead nowhere), and in "Desaparencia" (dotted lines converging on a viewpoint a few metres from the door). (C13422, leg 2; C13428, leg 2; C13426, leg 2) Watchable: walk and the image smears into abstraction; stop at the marked spot and a corkscrew, a staircase or a whole studio snaps into 3D. The viewer's movement is the interaction; on a phone, tilt or drag to find the angle where the smear resolves.

**Takeaway:** an anamorphosis is a game with one winning spot. If the image reads from everywhere, it is a picture; if it smears everywhere except one marked place where it snaps into 3D, the viewer's body is the lens and the mechanism is working.

## M3 -- Monumental scale subversion (small object, giant shadow)

A miniature object on a plinth casts a shadow many times its size, derived from a different, monumental referent (a civic statue, a weapon, a readymade). The shadow is black vinyl or paint, stretched in oblique perspective. "O Paradoxo do Santo" (1994) is the purest case: a small toy-like wooden figure of St James (Santiago, military patron saint of Spain) on a pedestal casts a monumental, distorted shadow derived from the monument to Duque de Caxias in Sao Paulo; the discrepancy "highlights... the relationship between religious and military authority, reflecting the country's continued commemoration of military power in the post-dictatorship era". (C13422, leg 2) Frieze: miniature equestrian military statues on white plinths, "behind which disproportionally large and distorted shadows, in black vinyl, loom ominously on the wall". (C13431, leg 2) The same subversion in the weapon key is "Encuentro" (1991): an encounter of shadows of arms and weapons for an exhibition commemorating the 500 years of the Discovery of America -- guns, swords and blades present only as looming black silhouettes, nearly 4 m wide, so the viewer stands inside the encounter. (C13435-search, leg 2; C13423, leg 2) Watchable: a toy saint becomes a rearing equestrian monument; approach and the object shrinks, retreat and the shadow swallows the wall. Power is a function of distance -- the piece stages how authority is projected.

**Takeaway:** scale subversion works when the shadow's referent is bigger than the object in the room. If the shadow matches its caster, it is a shadow; if a toy casts the shadow of a monument, the shadow is the argument about power.

## M4 -- The false window / doorpost of light (threshold paradox)

At a doorway, window or glass facade, the expected light is replaced by its opposite: a window that projects a solid black shadow into the room, a sunlit opening that delivers darkness. "Solombra" (1990) is the threshold paradox in its first architectural form: a large window exposed to sunlight "paradoxically, projects a black shadow into the building's interior". (C13450, leg 2) Silveira groups it with "Vortice" (1994) as "doorposts of light that shed shadows inside architectonic spaces, but in a paradoxical way, since they displace the effect of light, which virtually would go through windows and openings and illuminate a closed space. Instead of a 'halo' or an illumined place, light expands itself to form... an extensive box of shadows". (C13424, leg 2) Her genealogy groups these as "a paradoxical encounter between light (imagined or real) and the projected cast shadows (always fictional)" at a threshold between inside and outside. (C13425, leg 2) The same mechanism in its purest "captured light" form is "Behind the Glass" (1991): black and silver paint registering the night light-and-shade pattern of Washington Square inside NYU's Grey Art Gallery windows. (C13424, leg 2) Watchable: you cross a threshold expecting brightness and step into a slab of black shaped exactly like the window; the room's real daylight and the painted darkness argue with each other.

**Takeaway:** a false window earns its paradox when the room's real light and the painted darkness disagree in the same frame. If the black slab could be mistaken for a real shadow, it is a trick; if the brain insists light should be there while the eye reports shadow, the threshold is the mechanism.

## M5 -- Inverted light (the bulb that casts darkness)

A light source depicted or built so it emits shadow instead of light -- a switched-on bulb projecting a black drop; a lit cube whose luminous double shares its shadow. "Lumen" (2005) is built in three parts at the Palacio de Cristal, "an enormous receptacle of natural light, a colossal transparent box": "Quimera" is "a switched-on light bulb projecting not light but a vast shadow in the form of a black drop" -- a black skin over the portico, with two large backlights; "Transluz" cuts the word LUZ as a transparent void in a blue filter on the apse vault glass, so real sky shows through the letters -- "a circular equivalence between the word and the phenomenon"; "Memoriazul" is the fiction that every pane of the glass vaults has shattered. (C13426, leg 2; C13427, leg 2) The light-phantasm family around it: "Double" (2003), a real wooden cube plus its projected luminous double sharing one shadow; "Pulsar" (2003), a matchbox labelled "Fiat Lux" leaking a star of light; "Moonscape" (2001/2006), one sphere projected identically on two walls at right angles, with a small random delay, rolling and shrinking in an 8-minute loop. (C13424, leg 2) Watchable: the brightest thing in the room is also the darkest; stand off-axis and the alignment breaks, the magic leaks.

**Takeaway:** inverted light works when the brightest object in the room is also the darkest. If the bulb merely glows, it is a lamp; if the bulb casts a black teardrop and the glow and the cube share one shadow that lines up only from one spot, the inversion is the mechanism.

## M6 -- Indexical invasion: tracks that accumulate until they own the building

Footprints, paw prints, hoof marks or tire tracks, repeated and accumulated in perspective, plotter-cut in vinyl (or hand-painted in latex) across floors, walls, ceilings and facades until they read as infestation, stampede or traffic jam. The tracks are traces of a past event -- something passed here and is gone. "Gone Wild" (1996) is the pivot from shadows to tracks: coyote tracks "arranged and painted painstakingly... along the walls of the entrance hall" of MCA San Diego, in response to the Dalmatian-dog tracks inlaid in the granite floor designed by Robert Venturi; the coyote is the border-crosser (San Diego/Tijuana); the tracks are "traces of a past event: the coyotes went by". (C13425, leg 2) Paw prints "appear as though they begin on the floor in amorphous shapes and lead onto the walls as if an animal were actually running around the room. Many of the prints are distorted so that they can only be viewed from certain angles". (C13420, leg 2) "Tropel" (1998) scales the move to a building: a stampede of animal tracks in perspective -- "tracks made by various species of predators and preys from different latitudes, to deal with the theme of cultural devouring" -- 600 m2 of digital image cut from adhesive vinyl on the lateral wall of the Oscar Niemeyer building at Ibirapuera Park, the first large-scale use of the computer-cut vinyl process. (C13425, leg 2) The family continues through the "Irruption" series (2005-06, human footprints accumulated into an invasion, up to 1,800 m2), "Derrapando" (2004, tire tracks climbing a facade vertically) and "Octopus Wrap" (2019, five toy motorcycles whose tire tracks wrap walls, windows and floor, forming an octopus from a distance). (C13425, leg 2; C13432, leg 2) Watchable: a few tracks at the door, then hundreds climbing the walls and wrapping the facade; from far away the mass forms one larger image (a band, an octopus, a river); up close it is individual steps. The viewer walks through the trail, following it around corners.

**Takeaway:** tracks become an invasion when the accumulation crosses from decoration to occupation. If the prints stay on the floor, they are a pattern; if they climb the walls, cross the ceiling and wrap the facade until the building looks contaminated, the trace is the mechanism.

## M7 -- Dotted-line disappearance (the code for the invisible)

Objects and whole rooms drawn only in dotted or dashed lines -- the technical-drawing convention for hidden or invisible edges -- in latex or vinyl, running across floor, wall and ceiling in exaggerated perspective. "Desaparencia" (2001) is the mechanism entire: an artist's easel holding a canvas, with a stool beside it, outlined in dotted lines, "extend[ing] from the floor up onto the walls and towards the ceiling" in a bare white room; Alexander Gray: "black and white diagrams of artists' ateliers, graphically suggesting the ghosts of easels, tables, palettes, and other tools of the artist's studio". (C13420, leg 2; C13435, leg 2) A planned Palacio de Cristal version would have been "a large drawing consisting of dotted lines (the geometric code for invisible things) with a highly accentuated perspective converging on the viewpoint situated a few metres from the entrance". (C13426, leg 2) Watchable: an empty white room that is visibly an atelier (easel, stool, palette, canvas -- all dashes); stand at the marked viewpoint and the ghost-room is solid; step aside and it unspools into dashes crawling up the walls. Nothing is there, and everything is indicated.

**Takeaway:** the dotted line is a code, not a style. If the dashes merely decorate a wall, they are wallpaper; if they are the technical-drawing convention for hidden edges applied to a whole room, so that an empty room is visibly furnished with ghosts, the code is the mechanism.

## M8 -- Catastrophic light fiction (the building breaks, in light)

An imaginary disaster -- a skylight shatters, a ceiling collapses -- rendered in two steps: the event overhead (transparent printed vinyl on glass) and its aftermath underfoot (fallen fragments printed as a floor carpet), so floor mirrors ceiling. Light is the agent of demolition. "Memoriazul" (2005), part of "Lumen": the fiction that every pane of the glass vaults has shattered -- transparent vinyl "broken panes" on the ceiling above, and the fallen shards printed on a floor carpet below, "where the shards falling from above seem to be strewn about"; "the ground [acts] as a mirror and recollection of what has happened overhead". (C13425, leg 2; C13426, leg 2) The floor is 460 m2 and the ceiling 780 m2 of the same fiction. (C13426, leg 2) The family extends to "Entreceu/Intersky" (2007) and "Observatorio" (2006/2009). (C13425, leg 2) Watchable: look up at a broken glass vault; look down and walk on its wreckage -- sky and clouds printed under your feet. Daylight moving through the real building animates the fiction all day; the piece is never the same twice.

**Takeaway:** a catastrophe fiction earns its two steps when the floor is the mirror of the ceiling. If the break is only overhead, it is a picture of damage; if you walk on the wreckage of the sky you are looking up at, the building is the mechanism.

## The aesthetic logic: the representation of the real is pure artifice

Silveira's own sentence is the whole programme: "I always understood that the representation of the real is pure artifice, coded and culturalized". (C13379, leg 1) The shadow is never a natural effect in her work; it is a code -- a geometric construction, an index of an absent object, a dotted line for the invisible, a track for a past event. The artifice is the point, and it is also the politics: a postcard of Rio with a condor's silhouette over it ("Brazil Today", 1977) is a souvenir that has been tampered with; a toy saint casting the shadow of a military monument ("O Paradoxo do Santo", 1994) is a civic ritual exposed by its own scale; a stampede of tracks across a Biennial building ("Tropel", 1998) is cultural devouring rendered as a traffic jam. The work never argues in words. It puts a false thing in a real room and lets the room argue with it -- real daylight against painted darkness, a real floor against a printed abyss, a real plinth against an absent wheel.

The second logic is the viewer as the last component. Almost nothing in her work resolves from everywhere: the anamorphosis resolves from one spot, the dotted room from a marked viewpoint, the abyss from a bird's-eye stance, the luminous double from one alignment. The piece is unfinished until a body moves through it, and the movement is the reading. That is why the same four moves survive fifty years of changing supports: take a familiar thing, remove it, distort its trace, put the trace where it cannot belong. The medium is chosen per problem -- "I have been making free use of the media, without any of them representing me... I choose the medium that best solves my intentions" (C13379, leg 1) -- which is why the oeuvre runs from woodcut to vinyl to VR without ever changing its question: what is left of a thing when the thing is gone, and who completes it by looking? (C13547)


## The network: the wife, the inviter, the co-organiser

1. **Julio Plaza (1938-2003)** -- husband, collaborator, co-teacher, co-founder; the doorway itself. Met Madrid early 1967; married February 1969; twenty years of shared life and work 1967-1987; Puerto Rico together 1969-1973 (UPR Mayaguez, artists in residence); joint work "On/Off" (1972-1974, mail-art publication, three editions); joint teaching FAAP 1973 and ECA-USP 1974; ASTER co-founders 1978; joint CAYC show, Buenos Aires, July 1975 (simultaneous); together created the ECA/USP postgraduate programme in Visual Arts, including the Multimeios research line, with Carmela Gross and other professors. (C13548, leg 3 C13470, C13475, C13476, C13477, C13480)

Caiana calls the Plaza-Silveira-Zanini relationship a triangulation with ASTER and the 16th/17th Biennials as its high points. (C13549, leg 3 C13470, C13471, C13473, C13477, C13481, C13490)
