## The life: the mail artist who let the post office carry the message

Graciela Gutierrez Marx was born in La Plata, the only child of Antonio Raul Gutierrez and Blanca Marx -- the mother later renamed Mamablanca, who becomes the work's co-host, deadline and venue. The family was of humble origin (C10656). She finished secondary school at the Bachillerato de Bellas Artes in 1960, took a bachelor's in teaching and sculpture at the Universidad Nacional de La Plata 1962-1967, graduated Profesora y Licenciada en Escultura in 1967 and joined the UNLP faculty the same year, in the chair of Filosofia y Estetica; her teachers were Manuel Lopez Blanco (philosophy and aesthetics: Hegel, phenomenology, semiotics) and Aurelio Macchi (sculpture) (C10655, C10656). The 1960 school-leaving date fits a 1942 birth (age 18) better than a 1945 birth (age 15) -- circumstantial only, not evidence for either year on its own (C10683). The birth year itself is a genuine sourced conflict, 1942 vs 1945, and this study does not settle it; see the footnote below. She died in La Plata on 30 January 2022 (C10674, C10782).

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the envelope: the work leaves the gallery and goes into the post.

(1) Formation and sculpture, 1960s. She moved in the Grupo Si milieu (participation vs membership unresolved -- the Gustavino roster of Grupo Si members does not name her) and made rusted scrap-metal sculptures, shown at Galeria Lirolay 1968-1969, winning a "Ver y Estimar" prize (C10654, C10656, C10682).

(2) Entry into mail art, late 1960s-1975. She met Edgardo Antonio Vigo in 1968; he introduced her to mail art and visual poetry. In 1975 she took part in the Ultima exposicion internacional de arte correo '75 at Galeria Arte Nuevo -- the first mail-art exhibition in Argentina, organised by Horacio Zabala and Vigo -- showing woodcut envelopes bearing her face and rubber-stamp works (C10654, C10655). The same year Zabala invited her to a rubber-stamp exhibition organised by Ulises Carrion in the Netherlands, which put her on the international mail-art exchange lists (C10754).

(3) The MarxVigo duo and dictatorship years, 1976/77-1982/83. She and Vigo worked jointly and signed G.E. MarxVigo. The standard span is 1977-1983 (Hammer, AWARE, CVAA: "seven years"); two Argentine institutional sources give 1977-1982 (Museo Moderno map, Nimio 2019), and dated joint works support 1983 (Anteproyecto de fusion, 1983; a joint work mailed June 1984 in the Princeton inventory) -- carried unresolved, dated evidence leaning 1983 (C10675). The duo was also a couple, 1976-1983 per es.wikipedia (C10657). Joint work included Nuestro Libro Internacional de Estampillas y Matasellos and Acuse de recibo (1979), proposing a "marginal creative and parallel philately" (C10657, C10669). The campaign at the centre of these years was "Set Free Palomo", for Vigo's disappeared son Abel Luis Vigo (Palomo): she and Vigo "often stamped the envelopes they sent out through the mail art network with the English phrase 'Set Free Palomo'" (C10753). She was fired from her UNLP post in 1976; the military regime ran 24 March 1976 to December 1983; her base-Peronist militancy took her into vulnerable Greater Buenos Aires neighbourhoods (C10652, C10654, C10657, C10662). Her 1975 rubber-stamp works anticipated the disappearances (C10654).

(4) Collective poems and the democratic return, 1979-1988. Estafeta Postal Lateral (1979), Grupo de familia (1980), Mamablanca Treasure (1981), the Compania de la Tierra Malamada founding action (9 September 1982), El tendedero (1984), the Asociacion Latinoamericana y del Caribe de Artistas-correo (1984), Hoje-Hoja-Hoy (1984), Maraton de Antiheroes (1988) (C10655, C10662). In 1984, after the return of democracy, she changed her signature to GGMarx -- several sources date the democratic transition to 1984, but Alfonsin took office 10 December 1983; the signature change is dated here to "after the return of democracy", not to 1984 (C10678).

(5) Painting, performance and writing, 1990s-2010s. She returned to painting from the 1990s (encouraged by her son Martin Eckmeyer); took a master's in aesthetics and art theory at UNLP in 2007; published the book Artecorreo in 2010; published the memoir "Invisible Artists, or the Net without a Fisherman... (My Life in Mail Art)" in ARTMargins in 2012; lost much of her personal archive in the 3 April 2013 La Plata flood; no exhibitions after 2015 recorded; died 30 January 2022 (C10653, C10654, C10658, C10665). No anthological exhibition of her work was held in her lifetime (Giunta, MALBA obituary: "No ha existido de ella una sola exposicion antologica...") (C10653). AWARE calls her "one of Latin America's most outstanding mail artists"; Hammer calls her "one of the foremost Latin American exponents of mail art" (C10653, C10654, C10655).

Mail art in her own terms: a "tendency characterised by long-distance communication" promoting "the decentralisation of art", opposed to the art market and official centres; the receiver becomes author and the author receiver; "invisible artists" (C10663). Her theoretical concept was "arte intimo" (inmost art): modesty (it escapes art-as-spectacle) and marginalisation (an alternative space for genuine communication); slow communication based on listening, incorporating postal waiting time, messages as open invitations the receiver completes (C10664). Materials: postcards, envelopes, rubber stamps, woodcuts, photocopies, office offset, mimeograph, serigraphy (C10663). Scale: more than 400 national and international exhibitions from the mid-1970s on -- a single-source claim (Museo Moderno map) (C10661). Holdings: 54 pieces in the Lomholt Mail Art Archive (Denmark); two works at Migros Museum, Zurich; a MALBA installation (1981); the Princeton Vigo/Gutierrez Marx Mail Art collection C1814; the Archivo Lafuente holding of the 138 Grupo de familia postcards (C10651, C10653, C10659, C10664). The mail-art function under dictatorship, as Giunta and the Escaner review put it, was a channel evading censorship: "ambiguous practices that might go unnoticed by the watchful eye of the censor were the only viable means to convey a powerful critique" (Hammer, on Grupo de familia) (C10653, C10663, C10756).

Evolution. Early (1967-1975): the object, then the envelope -- rusted scrap sculpture at Lirolay, then the 1975 face-envelope turn. ## Mechanisms

M1 -- The artistamp as witness. A fake postage stamp that carries a name the regime erased. The stamp mimics official postal authority (perforations, first day covers, "PRIMER DIA EMISION" seals) and spends that borrowed authority on a disappeared person. Works: W03, W07, W11. Watchable behaviour: a sheet of stamps peels, perforates and multiplies; each stamp carries the same face and the same demand; the viewer watches a private grief acquire the look of state issue. Takeaway: the stamp is not decoration on the work, it IS the work -- a counter-postage that uses the post office's own visual language against it. (C10729)

M2 -- The face on the envelope: self-portrait as return address. She prints her own face on the envelope and names the exchange: "My face is your mask. Your face is my mask." Works: W02, W04. Watchable behaviour: a face repeats across envelopes, stamps and postcards, each time slightly different (woodcut, rubber stamp, photocopy) until the face is less a likeness than a signature the network can pass hand to hand. Takeaway: identity as a circulating image, not a fixed portrait; the artist mails herself. (C10730)

M3 -- The call and the return: an open invitation with a blank card and a deadline. The work is not finished by the artist; it is finished by whoever answers. Works: W09, W10, W20, W13. Watchable behaviour: blank cards fly out, filled cards fly back, and the pile on the table grows until it becomes the work -- 138 postcards in partitioned sleeves. Takeaway: the invitation is the medium; a call with no returns is an empty work, and the deadline is what makes the network answer. (C10731)

M4 -- The family as co-author: mother and son inside the work, not beside it. Mamablanca is the project's co-host, its 75th birthday its deadline, its white archive-box its gallery. Her son Martin draws with her for a decade. Works: W10, W12, W14, W20, plus collaborative drawings with Martin Raul Eckmeyer 1978-1988. Watchable behaviour: the same two figures -- the mother, the son -- recur across years of postcards, each time re-drawn by someone else in the network; the family portrait is assembled by strangers. Takeaway: the domestic is not a theme applied to the work; it is the work's address list, its deadline and its venue. (C10732)

M5 -- Burial as completion: the work is finished by being hidden. Mamablanca Treasure is a wooden box of mail artists' gifts buried in her garden, documented only by a poster and a flyer stamped "Residuos Poeticos / Poetical Detritus". Works: W14. Watchable behaviour: objects go into a box, the box goes into the ground, and the work continues underground, unseen -- the viewer watches the burial, not the object. Takeaway: disappearance as a deliberate act, not a loss; the buried work accuses the regime's disappearances by choosing its own. (C10733)

M6 -- Domestic display: the school lobby, the clothesline, the garden as gallery. She hangs the work where life already happens: a primary-school lobby, a washing line, a home exhibition in a white archive-box. Works: W09, W17, W10, W14. Watchable behaviour: clothes pegged to a line fill with notecards; a school lobby fills with stamp designs; the viewer watches an ordinary domestic fixture become an exhibition wall. Takeaway: the venue is the mechanism -- move the work out of the gallery and the audience changes from visitors to participants. (C10734)

M7 -- The shared signature: authorship dissolved into a duo name, then a collective. G.E. MarxVigo (1977-1983) is a single signature for two people; GGMarx (from 1984) is a single signature for one person working collectively; the 1984 manifesto is signed by an association. Works: W05, W06, W18, W17. Watchable behaviour: a signature that is two initials fused, then a name that shortens as the circle widens; the viewer watches the "I" thin out until the work belongs to the network. Takeaway: the signature is a valve -- it opens authorship to others instead of closing it around one hand. (C10735)

M8 -- The question instead of the slogan: a question smuggled past the censor. Under dictatorship the work rarely shouts; it asks. "Would you mind opening our jails?" is mailed as a postcard to a Dutch project in December 1980. "Jail" prints the word FREE and then ties it down with thread (W06). "Primavera Subterranea" celebrates a spring that cannot be seen (W07). Works: W06, W07, and the uncatalogued jails postcard (C10722). Watchable behaviour: a question arrives in the mail looking like a greeting; the viewer reads it twice -- once as politeness, once as accusation -- and the second reading is the work. Takeaway: the question is deniable on the surface and unmistakable once seen; an open slogan would have been stopped at the censor's desk. (C10736)

## Aesthetic logic

Her logic is the logic of the post office and the kitchen table, not the studio. The materials are the cheapest that travel: woodcut, rubber stamp, photocopy, graphing paper, postcards, envelopes, thread, grommets, a wooden box, a clothesline. The typography is the typography of bureaucracy turned back on itself -- first day covers, oval seals, "PRIMER DIA EMISION", perforated sheets -- because the stamp is a small piece of state authority, and she learned to print her own. The palette, as reported in sources, is the palette of the mail: black ink, blue stamp ink, green floral stamp backgrounds, the white of the archive-box and the white handkerchief. Nothing is precious; the work is designed to be mailed, returned, pinned to a garment, buried in a garden, and re-issued as a call five years later. The through line is the network as the work: early the network carries a face (W02), then a name the regime erased (W03), then a question about family that 138 strangers answer (W10), then a flag sewn from strangers' clothes (W17). The viewer is never a spectator: they fill in the card, bring the garment, imagine the mother, open the jail. And the politics is never illustrated: it is built into the procedure -- a stamp that should not exist, a word tied down with thread, a birthday that is also a protest. The work that began as rusted scrap in a Buenos Aires gallery in 1968 ends as a call asking the whole world to imagine one anonymous 75-year-old woman, and the distance between those two gestures is the whole career. (C10737)

## The network

She met Vigo in 1968; he introduced her to mail art and visual poetry, and they worked jointly as G.E. MarxVigo 1977-1983 (C10752). In 1975 Vigo and Zabala invited her to the Ultima exposicion internacional de arte correo '75, the first mail-art exhibition in Argentina (C10751), and Zabala's invitation to the Carrion rubber-stamp show in the Netherlands put her on the international exchange lists (C10754). With Vigo she campaigned for Palomo's return, stamping "Set Free Palomo" on envelopes sent through the network (C10753); MoMA on circulation: "Despite government censorship, Vigo's moving letters and graphic works reached artists the world over" (C10753). Grupo de familia (1980-1981): 200 missives sent, 138 postcards returned for Mamablanca's 75th birthday (C10756, C10757). In 1984 she co-founded the Asociacion Latinoamericana y del Caribe de Artistascorreo in Rosario with Jorge Orta, Clemente Padin, Susana Lombardo, Claudia del Rio, Mamablanca, and Marina Rothberg (AWARE) or Noni Arganaraz (Hammer) -- the roster disagrees on the seventh founder, carried unresolved (C10755, C10745).

The Romero link (the chain hop) is not Grupo de los Trece membership -- no source puts her on the roster, and no source lists her as a CAYC exhibitor; do not claim either (C10685). The link is the shared CAYC / Vigo / Zabala milieu and the shared mail-art network: Pianowski lists among Argentine mail artists of the 1970s "Edgardo Antonio Vigo, Horacio Zabala, Carlos Ginzburg, Graciela Gutierrez Marx, Juan Carlos Romero, Luis Iurcovich, Luis Catriel and Luis Pazos", and records the 1975 show as organised by Vigo and Zabala (C10759). Romero's side: Grupo de los Trece from November 1971, Violencia at the CAYC April 1973, leading Argentine mail-art promoter in the 1970s (C10772). The CAYC was founded in 1968 by Jorge Glusberg (C10772). Zabala (#650) is the hinge in both directions. ARTMargins names the key Argentine mail-art network participants as Vigo, Zabala, Carlos Pazos and Juan Carlos Romero, singling out Gutierrez Marx as "one of the few women participating in this alternative circuit" (C10673). Adjacent artists for the chain: Clemente Padin, in mail-art exchange with Vigo from 1967, organiser of the First Latin American Mail Art Exposition, Montevideo, 1974, and her co-founder at Rosario in 1984 (C10760, C10761, C10755); Paulo Bruscky, Recife mail artist in early-1970s correspondence with Vigo and Zabala (C10765); Luis Pazos, fellow Grupo de los Trece founder and later Grupo Escombros co-founder with Romero in 1988 (C10770, C10771). (C10811)
