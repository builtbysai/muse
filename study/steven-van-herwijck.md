# Steven van Herwijck: Study Notes

**Site:** Steven Cornelisz. van Herwijck (Utrecht c.1530 - London, died before Easter 1567), Netherlandish sculptor and gem engraver, famous for portrait medallions and medals. He worked in Italy in 1557, was made Master of the Utrecht artists' guild (in fact the saddlemakers' guild, which Utrecht artists shared) in 1558, worked in Antwerp in 1559, fled religious persecution to Poland in 1561, travelled to England in 1562, was back in the Low Countries in 1564, and returned to London in 1565. No master or training is documented anywhere in the sources read; his birthplace is disputed, Utrecht per the standard account, possibly Flanders or Brabant per G. F. Hill in 1920. His medals are always cast, never struck, modelled in wax built up on a flat slate disk, finished by chasing with the graver, and typically signed small in the field "STE-H" or "STE-H-F". That signature was misread by George Vertue as "Stephen of Holland", and the phantom "Stephen of Holland" carried the whole oeuvre until Victor Tourneur identified him as Steven van Herwijck in 1922 (the Web Gallery of Art says the identity was not discovered until 1921, see C261). The earliest surviving medals are from Utrecht 1558, George van Egmond, Bishop of Utrecht, and Engelken Tols, with a medal of Wouter van Bijler the same year. Nine medals survive of his Antwerp work of 1559, the one named being the portrait of Jacobus Fabius; a silver medal of Hieronymus van Tuyll van Serooskerken dated Antwerp 1558 sits beside that count, see C271. In Poland in 1561 he made a signed series of the Jagiellonian royal family: Sigismund II Augustus, Catherine Jagiellon, Bona Sforza, John Sigismund Zapolya, unfinished medals of Isabella Jagiellon, and a second Catherine; whether he went to Sigismund's court in Vilnius is unconfirmed in source materials. In England in 1562 he produced the first portrait medals of private individuals made in that country: William Parr, 1st Marquess of Northampton, and his wife Elizabeth Brooke; William Herbert, 1st Earl of Pembroke; Anne Heneage; Thomas Stanley, Under-Treasurer of the Royal Mint; Richard Martin, Lord Mayor of London, and his wife Dorcas; Edmund Withypoll, twice; and Maria Dimock. The 1562 medals are small silver cast-and-chased plaques, 38 to 57 mm, many with the sitter's age stamped in the legend (AET. 28, AET. 48, AET. 50) and, increasingly, a delicately modelled allegorical reverse instead of a coat of arms: Pembroke's green dragon beside a woman pointing at the temple of Virtue, Dimock's woman holding a stag by the horns at a fountain, the Marchioness of Northampton's Faith with book and cross. In 1564 he was back in Utrecht and Antwerp: Anthonis Mor's Portrait of a Goldsmith, identified as van Herwijck, is dated 1564, and a portrait medallion of Mor by van Herwijck likely dates to the same exchange; a portrait of his wife Jonekin by Mor, also 1564, is in the Hunterian Museum, Glasgow. His last London year, 1565, produced the lead medal of Queen Elizabeth I, 48 mm, National Portrait Gallery 4294, the only known example, found in a ploughed field at Great Moulton, Norfolk, in 1962, the subject of a 1565 correspondence between the Flemish poet Charles Utenhove and William Cecil, and a medallion of the French ambassador Michel de Castelnau, dated 1565 by Wikipedia and Hawkins but listed among the 1562 medals by the Web Gallery of Art, see C262. He died in London sometime before Easter 1567, leaving his widow Jonekin or Johane and children; the death year is contested across sources (1563, 1563-64, c.1565, before Easter 1567), see C259, and the 1563-64 date belongs to the other Steven, Steven van der Meulen, see C260. He is also described as a gem engraver and, per Hill, as a painter of some merit whose paintings were Holbeinesque, but no surviving gem, cameo or intaglio by him was identified in any leg, and whether he is the "famous paynter Steven" of the 1590 Lumley inventory is contested, see C266. The record is strong on the medals and the English year, and thin on the Italian year (no work from 1557 is named anywhere), the eight unnamed Antwerp medals, and the gem work. ## M1. Wax Built Up on a Slate Disk
The portrait is not carved out of hard material but built up, layer by layer, in warm wax on a flat black slate disk. Hill's chapter on medallic technique, read in part, describes the process the cast medals follow: the artist took a flat disk, most usually black slate, as the basis and field of the model, and built up the type in wax on it, wax mixed with a little tallow for suppleness, turpentine for tenacity and black pitch for hue and firmness. The face grows out of the field instead of being cut into it; soft transitions, fingertip modelling, the relief highest at the nose and brow and sinking into the field at the hair and shoulders. Obverse and reverse were modelled either on two separate disks or on the two sides of a single disk. Source: leg 1 (Hill, Medals of the Renaissance, ch. I, read in part; the general process Herwijck's cast medals follow).

**Takeaway:** the portrait is grown, not cut; the face rises out of the field the way a thought rises out of a sentence.

## M2. Cast, Never Struck
Every Herwijck medal is a casting, poured molten into a two-part mould, never hammered from a die. Hill: "His medals are always cast." The Web Gallery of Art: "All are cast and chased, and each is a masterpiece of portraiture." The moulds were made in tufa or sand or a mixture of gesso, pith of sheep's horn, tripoli and pumice ground with water; the two sides of the model were impressed in the moulding material, the moulds joined, and molten metal poured in. The cire perdue (lost-wax) process was probably not used by early medallists. Watchable behaviour: a soft, slightly rounded edge to the relief, the warm mould-surface skin Hill describes, occasional tiny pits from air bubbles; the medal looks poured, not punched. The Elizabeth medal is the proof in the negative: Wikipedia says he "struck" it, but the NPG says it is a cast lead medal, and a lead medal is cast, not struck (see C263). Source: leg 1 (Hill p. 133; Web Gallery of Art, read end to end); leg 2 (cast-then-chased, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the medal is poured, not punched; its softness is the process showing, not a lack of finish.

## M3. Chasing as the Second Hand
After the pour, the graver reworks the surface to remove flaws. Hill: after casting, flaws from air bubbles or sand grains were removed by chasing with the graver; the ideal medal needed no retouching, preserving the warm surface from direct contact of metal with the smooth mould, which reproduces the wax model. Watchable behaviour: crisp lines, hair strands, letter edges, jewellery, sitting on top of a softer modelled ground; the two textures, modelled wax-softness and chased sharpness, are visible side by side in one face. The finish is not in the mould but in the second pass. Source: leg 1 (Web Gallery of Art, "cast and chased"; Hill ch. I on chasing); leg 2 (cast-then-chased, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** two hands make the medal, the wax hand and the graver hand, and both stay visible.

## M4. The Signature That Lied for Two Hundred Years
"STE-H" / "STE-H-F" / "STE-H-FEC" worked small into the field. George Vertue read the H as "Hollandus" and the man became "Stephen of Holland" for the whole 18th century; Victor Tourneur identified him as Steven van Herwijck only in 1922 (the Web Gallery of Art says the identity was not discovered until 1921, see C261). Hill: "A much more admirable and at the same time sympathetic medallist than Jonghelinck, produced by the Low Countries in the second half of the sixteenth century, was the mysterious artist who has since the eighteenth century been miscalled Stephen of Holland." Watchable behaviour: a tiny signature that a viewer must hunt for, smaller than any word in the legend, and that misled everyone who found it. The work signs itself in a whisper and the whisper was misheard for three centuries. Source: leg 1 (Hill pp. 132-133; Wikipedia); leg 2 (the signature misread for 300 years, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** the smallest mark on the medal carries the whole authorship, and it carried the wrong name for two hundred years.

## M5. Obverse Face, Reverse Story
The portrait is only half the object; the reverse carries a second, independent image, and the medal is a sentence in two halves: the obverse states who, the reverse states what they claim. Heraldic reverses in the Netherlandish tradition (the Stanley medal reverse is his coat of arms, a shield surmounted by a crest, a stag). Delicately modelled allegorical reverses (Pembroke: a dragon beside a woman pointing at the temple of Virtue, "guardian of virtue"; Dimock: a woman holding a stag by the horns at a fountain, "as the hart panteth after the water brooks"; the Marchioness of Northampton: Faith with book and cross, "faith alone protects"). Classical subjects (Bacchus and Ceres medals). A political emblem (Elizabeth's fountain of Faith with a distant castle, "divine fountain of the realm"). Watchable behaviour: the medal must be turned over; the flip is the second act, and the reverse often explains why the portrait was made. The reverse re-reads the face. Source: leg 1 (Web Gallery of Art; Hill p. 133; NPG 4294); leg 2 (the two-face argument, folded in here).

**Takeaway:** one face names the sitter, the other face argues for them; the medal is not complete until it is turned.

## M6. The Inscription Ring as Architecture
Letters are modelled in wax inside compass-drawn circles, framing the bust like a building frames a statue. Hill: lettering was laid out with compasses, incised concentric circles as guides; on the most careful models, radii drawn with a blind point from centre to circumference to fix the axes of the letters, letters drawn in ink on parchment laid round the edge, wax built up on the drawings. The Web Gallery of Art praises his "elegant lettering showing the influence of 16th-century Italian medals". Watchable behaviour: the legend runs in a perfect ring, the letters evenly spaced and upright on their radial axes, holding the portrait centred; when the bust is too big it can crowd or cover the letters, a known period accident Hill describes for other medallists. Source: leg 1 (Hill ch. I; Web Gallery of Art).

**Takeaway:** the ring of letters is the building the bust stands in; the portrait is centred because the circle holds it.

## M7. The Homely Portrait
His most praised quality is intimacy with ordinary sitters: Mary Dimock, Richard and Dorcas Martin, Engelken Tols, Hillegoent van Alendorp. Hill: "There is something extraordinarily attractive about his medals of homely people, such as Mary Dimock, Richard and Dorcas Martin, and the Dutch lady Engelken Tols. The portraits are as charmingly intimate as any you will find in the best period of Dutch painting", and medals like Floris Allewyn and Jacobus Fabius "show that he understood how to give dignity to a portrait". The Web Gallery of Art calls the Hillegoent van Alendorp medal "the homely medal". Watchable behaviour: unidealised faces, direct gaze, domestic dress and jewellery rendered without flattery, the sitter presented as a person rather than a rank. Source: leg 1 (Hill pp. 132-133; Web Gallery of Art); leg 2 (WGA, "the homely medal").

**Takeaway:** the medal dignifies ordinary people without flattering them; intimacy is the style.

## M8. The Private-Sitter Market
In England in 1562 he made the first portrait medals of private individuals in the country: merchants, a mint official, a lord mayor, courtiers' wives. The medal stops being a royal monopoly and becomes a portable portrait for the middling elite. The same small bronze disk that held kings now holds a merchant's wife; the sitters are people who could never have had a state portrait. Source: leg 1 (Wikipedia; Web Gallery of Art; Grove Dictionary of Art 2002 via Wikipedia).

**Takeaway:** the medal leaves the court and goes to the merchant's house; the technology of kings becomes the portrait of neighbours.

## M9. The Paired Medal
Husband-and-wife pairs struck as matching objects: the Northamptons, the Martins. Two disks of the same size and hand, designed to sit side by side, the pair completing the portrait. The Martins go further: husband on one side and wife on the other of a single 57.2 mm medal, Richard Martin on the obverse, Dorcas on the reverse, so the pair is one object with two faces. Source: leg 1 (Wikipedia, both couples listed among the 1562 English medals); leg 2 (Grueber no. 35, the Martin double portrait).

**Takeaway:** marriage is a matched pair of disks, or a single disk with a face on each side; the portrait is incomplete alone.

## M10. The Wearable Medal and the Lost Original
The Elizabeth medal (NPG 4294) has a suspension hole: it hung on a ribbon or loop, and the NPG suggests the loop broke and the wearer did not notice, which is how the only known example ended up in a ploughed field at Great Moulton, Norfolk. The Pembroke medal is also pierced for suspension. Watchable behaviour: the medal as a thing worn on the body, not shelved in a cabinet; the hole at the top is the wear point, and its loss is the object's biography. The only surviving Elizabeth medal survives because it was lost. Source: leg 1 (NPG 4294 page, read end to end); leg 2 (British Museum Pembroke record, pierced for suspension).

**Takeaway:** the medal was worn, and being worn is how it was lost; the hole is the biography and the field is the archive.

## M11. Age Stamped into the Legend
AET. 48, AET. 50, AET. 28, AET. 25: the sitter's age is engraved as part of the inscription, so the medal is also a dated document of a life at a moment. Thomas Stanley, AET. 50; Edmund Withypoll, AET. 48; Richard Martin, AET. 28; Dorcas Martin, AET. 25. Watchable behaviour: the number sits in the same ring as the name, read in the same pass; the portrait is pinned to a year of a life, not just a year of the century. The age is what lets a later reader date the sitter's birth: Richard Martin's birth year, c.1534, is estimated from his age of 28 on the 1562 medal. Source: leg 2 (Grueber nos. 32-35; leg 3 network entry 18).

**Takeaway:** the medal dates the person, not just itself; the number in the ring is a birth certificate in miniature.

## M12. The One-Sided Plaque
The Withypoll medals and the De Spinster are plaques, not two-faced medals: one worked face, one blank back, made to be set, mounted, or worn rather than handled as a coin. Watchable behaviour: the object has a front and a nothing; it is displayed, not turned, the opposite of M5, and the choice between plaque and medal is itself a decision about how the sitter is to be met. The Withypoll in a toga, bare-headed, is a plaque; the same sitter in a bonnet, three-quarters, is a second plaque, two different medals of one man in one year, both one-sided. Source: leg 2 (Grueber nos. 32-33; Rijksmuseum De Spinster, copy after).

**Takeaway:** a medal with no reverse is a portrait for a wall, not a sentence for the hand; the blank back is a choice, not an omission.

## M13. Scale Follows the Room
The same hand makes a 38 mm silver plaque for a merchant's wife, a 48 mm lead medal for a queen, a 63.7 mm bronze medallion for a fellow painter, and an 82.5 mm cast-iron dynastic portrait for a Polish king. Size is not a style; it is a function of who must see it from how far away and in what company. The portrait grows or shrinks with the patron's stage, private, court, or dynasty, while the modelling hand stays the same. The same logic runs the other way in time: the 1562 English run clusters at 38-57 mm, small precious plaques for private hands, while the Polish series jumps to dynastic scale and the late Mor medallion, 63.7 mm, is the largest portrait piece after the Catherine cast-iron piece. Source: leg 2 (dimensions verified for 11 entries).

**Takeaway:** the medal is sized to its audience; the hand does not change, the room does.

## Evolution
Phase 1, Utrecht, 1558. Small portrait medals of local notables: George van Egmond, Bishop of Utrecht, Engelken Tols, Wouter van Bijler. The earliest surviving work, and the register is local and ecclesiastical, a bishop and two Utrecht citizens, in silver, at a scale for the hand. The guild context matters: Utrecht artists shared the saddlemakers' guild rather than having their own Guild of St. Luke, so the medallist begins inside a craftsman's guild, not a painters' academy.

Phase 2, Antwerp, 1558-1559. Nine medals survive, the named one the portrait of Jacobus Fabius, called "the fine portrait" by the Web Gallery of Art; a silver medal of Hieronymus van Tuyll van Serooskerken dated Antwerp 1558, 4.7 cm, sits beside the nine and may or may not be one of them, see C271. Antwerp is the step up: the port city, the Habsburg medal scene, Jonghelinck cutting seals for Philip II's government from 1556 and da Trezzo working in the Low Countries until 1559. The Web Gallery of Art says of Herwijck here: "He must also have known the work of Jacques Jonghelinck." The influence is Italianate, the lettering elegant after 16th-century Italian medals, but Hill insists he "never loses his freshness and directness of vision; he is not a pupil of the Italian Academy."

Phase 3, Poland, 1561. The scale jumps. The Catherine Jagiellon medallion is cast iron, 82.5 mm, part of a signed dynastic series for Sigismund II Augustus and the last Jagiellonians: Bona Sforza, Catherine, John Sigismund Zapolya, unfinished Isabella, a second Catherine. The Sigismund obverse is a bust; the reverse shows the king on horseback. The series is dynastic portraiture, a family set, and the metal is cast iron, the biggest scale of his career. Whether he went to Vilnius is unconfirmed in source materials.

Phase 4, England, 1562. The famous run: small silver cast-and-chased plaques, 38-57 mm, each with the sitter's age stamped in the legend and, increasingly, a delicately modelled allegorical reverse instead of a plain back or a coat of arms. Pembroke's dragon and temple of Virtue; Dimock's stag at the fountain; the Marchioness of Northampton's Faith with book and cross. The Stanley medal keeps the older Netherlandish habit, a coat of arms on the reverse. The register changes with the sitters: these are the first portrait medals of private individuals made in England, merchants, a mint official, a lord mayor, courtiers' wives. Two different medals of Edmund Withypoll in the same year, one in a toga, one in a bonnet; a double portrait of the Martins, husband one side, wife the other. The medal has become a portable portrait for the middling elite.

Phase 5, return, 1564-1565. A "homely" Utrecht medal, Hillegoent van Alendorp, proves the return to Utrecht by 1564; the large bronze Antonis Mor medallion, 63.7 mm, the biggest portrait piece after the Catherine cast-iron piece, marks the reciprocal exchange with the painter who painted him; the French ambassador Castelnau, dated 1562 by one source and 1565 by another; and the 1565 lead Elizabeth I medal, 48 mm, unique survivor, found in a ploughed field in 1962. The arc closes on the political medal: the queen's face on the obverse, a fountain with Faith and a distant castle on the reverse, a Greek inscription meaning "divine fountain of the realm", the letters an anagram of "Elizabeth the Queen", devised by Utenhove and approved through Cecil. The arc is: local likeness, then dynastic scale, then small precious English plaques with literary reverses, then late large bronze and political medals.

What changed, in one line each. Portrait style: local likeness (1558) to Italianate elegance with directness kept (1559) to dynastic bust and equestrian reverse (1561) to intimate private portrait with the age in the legend (1562) to large bronze exchange portrait and political emblem (1564-1565). Reverse: plain or heraldic (1558-1562, Stanley) to allegorical and literary (Pembroke, Dimock, Northampton, Elizabeth). Scale: small silver (1558-1559) to 82.5 mm cast iron (1561) to 38-57 mm silver plaques (1562) to 63.7 mm bronze and 48 mm lead (1564-1565). Production: cast and chased throughout, one process from first medal to last; what changes is the sitter, not the hand. Sitters: bishop and citizens, to Antwerp notables, to a royal dynasty, to private English merchants and officials, to a queen.

## Aesthetic logic
Herwijck's aesthetic is the logic of the small thing that must hold a whole person. Everything he makes is a container for a likeness, and the container is always smaller than the person: a 38 mm disk holds a merchant's wife, a 48 mm lead disk holds a queen, a 57 mm disk holds a husband and a wife at once. The portrait is the anchor and the reverse is the argument, and the two faces together make a complete sentence, who on the front, what they stand for on the back, with the age cut into the legend so the sentence is dated to a year of a life. Scale is not a property of the work but a register he plays across: the same hand makes a 38 mm plaque and an 82.5 mm cast-iron dynastic portrait, and the size follows the room, private, court, or dynasty, while the modelling stays the same. Material is never neutral in his hands: silver is the private sitter's metal, lead is the queen's medal because lead is what could be cast and worn and lost in a field, bronze is the fellow painter's medal, cast iron is the dynasty's. He works the medal as a technology of likeness for people who had no other portrait: the merchant, the mint official, the lord mayor, the wife, the bishop, and only at the end the queen, and the queen's medal is the one that was lost, worn on a ribbon until the loop broke. The beauty in his best work, the Dimock stag at the fountain and the Pembroke dragon, is the beauty of a whole allegory modelled inside a space too small for it, a woman, a stag, a fountain, a temple, in 38 to 44 mm, the figure doing in relief what the motto does in words: carrying the sitter's claim on its back. What he avoids is the unique masterpiece; his genius is the system, one mould, one hand, many sitters, many sizes, and the work's meaning changes with each sitter because the sitter is the meaning. He is, in Hill's words, "a much more admirable and at the same time sympathetic medallist than Jonghelinck", and the sympathy is the point: the homely people are the ones he renders best, and the medals are "as charmingly intimate as any you will find in the best period of Dutch painting".

### Sitters: the Polish year, 1561
6. Sigismund II Augustus (neither) - sitter, 1561. King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania (reigned 1548-1572). Herwijck fled religious persecution to Poland in 1561 and made medallions of the king and other members of the Polish royal family. The National Gallery of Art holds the Sigismund II Augustus medal, NGA 45436, filed as c.1562 (see C269). Sigismund was a passionate collector of jewels and gemstones.

8. Catherine Jagiellon (neither) - sitter, Polish series. Daughter of Sigismund I, later Queen of Sweden. Her medal belongs to the same signed series (Vilnius catalogue); a 19th-century cast iron copy after Herwijck's medal is recorded in the Wawel collection.
9. Isabella Jagiellon (neither) - sitter, Polish series. An unfinished medal of Isabella belongs to the same signed series (Vilnius catalogue).
10. John Sigismund Zapolya (neither) - sitter, Polish series. His medal belongs to the same signed series (Vilnius catalogue). The same catalogue notes Herwijck "may have gone to Sigismund Augustus' court in Vilnius for that purpose - although there is no confirmation of that in source materials", so a Vilnius stay is NOT asserted.

### Sitters: the English year, 1562 - the first private portrait medals made in England
11. Elizabeth I (neither) - sitter, 1565. Queen of England (reigned 1558-1603). Herwijck returned to London in 1565 and made a lead medal of the queen, 48 mm, the only surviving example found in a ploughed field at Great Moulton in Norfolk in 1962; now National Portrait Gallery NPG 4294, purchased 1962. The medal was the subject of a 1565 correspondence between the Flemish poet and scholar Charles Utenhove and William Cecil, in which Utenhove sought permission to produce a portrait medal of the Queen with a Greek inscription on the reverse meaning "divine fountain of the realm", the Greek letters being an anagram of "Elizabeth the Queen".
12. William Parr, 1st Marquess of Northampton (neither) - sitter, 1562. Brother of Katherine Parr, Henry VIII's sixth wife; created Marquess of Northampton 16 February 1547, stripped of his titles under Mary I, restored by Elizabeth I in 1559. Herwijck's 1562 medal portrays him and his wife (entry 13).
13. Elisabeth Brooke, Marchioness of Northampton (neither) - sitter, 1562. Sister-in-law of Katherine Parr; performed much of a queen's role during Edward VI's reign. Her medallion by Herwijck is dated 1562; she died of breast cancer in 1565, three years after sitting.
14. William Herbert, 1st Earl of Pembroke (neither) - sitter, 1562. British Museum BNK,EngM.16: silver medal, cast and chased, pierced for suspension, 44 mm, designed by Steven Cornelisz. van Herwijck, production date 1562. Obverse bust right, bare-headed, in armour, mantle, ruff, and George of the Garter; inscription "GVILI COMES PENNEBROCK A0 1562"; reverse inscription "DRACO HIC VER' VIRTVTV CVSTOS". Medallic Illustrations (1885) calls it very rare. Donated by the Bank of England.
15. Anne Heneage (neither) - sitter, 1562. Wife of Thomas Heneage (entry 16). Listed among the 1562 English sitters in the Wikipedia Herwijck article.
16. Thomas Heneage (neither) - husband of a sitter; the Heneage bridge to Hilliard. Anne Heneage's husband. A generation later Elizabeth gave Sir Thomas Heneage the Armada Jewel, a miniature of the Queen by Nicholas Hilliard in an enamelled gold locket (V and A), so the Heneage family connects Herwijck's 1562 medals to Hilliard's jewel-miniatures.
17. Thomas Stanley (neither) - sitter, 1562. Under-Treasurer of the Royal Mint. Grueber's 1891 Guide to the Exhibition of English Medals lists "Sir Thomas Stanley, 1562. Obv. Bust r., wearing bonnet, doublet, etc. THOMAS STANLEY. AET. 50. STE. H." - signed with Herwijck's "Ste. H." and giving the sitter's age as 50.
18. Richard Martin (neither) - sitter, 1562; goldsmith and mint man. Born c.1534 (the birth year is estimated from his age given as 28 in Herwijck's 1562 portrait medallion), died July 1617. Liveryman of the Goldsmiths' Company 1558; Warden of the Mint 1560-1595 (DNB says appointed 1559-60, see C276); Master of the Mint 1582-1617 (DNB says 1580-1, see C276); Sheriff of London 1581; Lord Mayor 1589 and 1593-94; knighted 1588-89; investor in Drake's circumnavigation 1577-1580. His portrait medallion is therefore a primary source for his own biography.
19. Dorcas Ecclestone (neither) - sitter, 1562. Wife of Richard Martin, married sometime before 1562; born 1537, died 1599. Spelling varies across sources: "Dorcas Eglestone or Eccelstone" in the Herwijck article, "Dorcas Ecclestone" in the Martin article.
20. Edmund Withypoll (neither) - sitter, 1562. Spelling varies: "Withypoll" in the Herwijck article, "Withipoll" in Grueber. Grueber lists two 1562 variants, both silver, cast and chased, 1.7 in.: "EDMVND WITHIPOLL. AET. 48. 1562", bust in a toga; and a second with bust three-quarters left in bonnet and doublet. Builder of Christchurch Mansion in Ipswich; High Sheriff of Norfolk and Suffolk 1571; knighted 1601. His father Paul was a merchant tailor of London.
21. Maria Dimock or Dymock (neither) - sitter, 1562. Wife of the merchant John Dymock (entry 22). The Dymock link matters beyond the sitting: Grosvenor's 2009 case for Herwijck as the "famous paynter Steven" rests partly on the Dymock family connection (see C266, C272).
22. John Dymock (neither) - merchant; husband of Maria; the Sweden link. In 1561 the English merchant John Dymock visited Sweden in connection with negotiations for a marriage between Elizabeth and Erik XIV, taking with him a Netherlandish painter described as "Master Staffan" to paint the king's portrait. Whether "Master Staffan" was Herwijck or van der Meulen is disputed (see C272).

### Family

### Fellow makers: the Antwerp medal scene and the London emigres
25. Jacques Jonghelinck (already studied, #521) - adjacent Antwerp medallist. The Web Gallery of Art biography of Herwijck states "He must also have known the work of Jacques Jonghelinck". Both men were in Antwerp in 1559-1564: Jonghelinck cutting seals for Philip II's government from 1556 and moving to Brussels in 1562; Herwijck working Antwerp 1559, then England 1562, then back in Utrecht and Antwerp 1564. Jonghelinck is the subject of #521; Herwijck was #521's top pick for #522.
26. Jacopo da Trezzo (already studied, #520) - the Milanese end of the same Habsburg medallic court. Da Trezzo worked in the Low Countries 1555-1559, overlapping Herwijck's Antwerp year of 1559; Jonghelinck's smaller marriage medal was made after da Trezzo's gold original. No direct Herwijck-da Trezzo contact is documented; the link is the shared Antwerp medal scene of 1559.
27. Anthonis Mor (already carried, #519) - painter of Herwijck, and sitter of Herwijck. Mor's Portrait of a Goldsmith, dated 1564, oil on oak, 1.185 by 0.90 m, signed "A. TATIS. A'A'A'T'. 1564", from the Cabinet of Willem V, is in the Mauritshuis and is identified as Steven van Herwijck. The exchange ran both ways: "it is likely that a portrait medallion of Mor by van Herwijck dates to this period", and the National Gallery of Art holds a Steven van Herwijck medal of Antonis Mor. Mor also painted Margaret of Parma, Granvelle, Mary Tudor and Thomas Gresham, and was in regular contact with Granvelle. Mor's dates are disputed (see C268).
28. Steven van der Meulen (neither; proposed as alternate 7 below) - the other Steven. Flemish artist, born Antwerp, studied under Willem van Cleve the Younger in 1543, admitted to the Guild of St Luke in Antwerp 1552; in London by September 1560, member of the Dutch congregation June 1562, denizen 4 February 1562; buried at St Andrew Undershaft, London, 24 October 1563; will dated 5 October 1563, proved 20 January 1564; wife Gertrude Stubbeleeren, children Rinnold and Eric. Best known for the "Barrington Park" portrait type of Elizabeth I; a life-size full-length Elizabeth of c.1563 sold at Sotheby's in November 2007 for 2.6 million pounds. His portraits in a simplified version of Mor's sombre style appealed to the English court in the early 1560s. The 1590 Lumley inventory's "famous paynter Steven" was traditionally identified with him by Roy Strong; Grosvenor 2009 proposed Herwijck instead; Town 2014 and Goldring 2014 reject the Herwijck identification (see C266, C272).
29. Nicholas Hilliard (neither; proposed as main 1 below) - the English successor in small-scale royal portraiture. Born Exeter c.1547, died before 7 January 1619, London; English goldsmith and limner, best known for portrait miniatures of Elizabeth I and James I. Apprenticed to the Queen's jeweller Robert Brandon; free of the Goldsmiths' Company 1570; appointed limner and goldsmith to Elizabeth I; first known miniature of the Queen dated 1572. Produced the Lyte Jewel (British Museum), the Armada Jewel given to Sir Thomas Heneage and the Drake Jewel given to Sir Francis Drake; author of The Art of Limning (c.1600). Farquhar's 1908 paper calls him "Embosser of Medals of Gold": the gold Armada medal of Elizabeth (c.1588/1589, British Museum M.6903) and the Phoenix Jewel are attributed to him. His birth year is disputed, 1537 vs 1547 (see C280).
30. Levina Teerlinc (neither; proposed as alternate 6 below) - the bridge between Holbein and Hilliard. Flemish Renaissance miniaturist, born Bruges in the 1510s, died Stepney, London, 23 June 1576. Daughter of Simon Bening, renowned illuminator of the Ghent-Bruges school, and granddaughter of Catherine van der Goes (closely related to Hugo van der Goes). Married George Teerlinc of Blanckenberge 1545, in England by 1546, court painter to Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I at 40 pounds a year from 1546 until her death, more than was provided to Holbein. The most important miniaturist at the English court between Hans Holbein the Younger and Nicholas Hilliard; Strong and Murrell suggest she taught Hilliard the art of limning, though whether they ever met is not known. No surviving work has been firmly attributed to her; her documented Elizabeth portraits run 1559-1567.
31. Hans Holbein the Younger (neither; proposed as main 2 below) - the court portraitist whose London pattern Herwijck's medals joined. German-Swiss painter and printmaker, c.1497/98 - between 7 October and 29 November 1543. Arrived in England 1526 with a recommendation from Erasmus; returned 1532; King's Painter to Henry VIII by 1535/1536 on 30 pounds a year; produced portraits and festive decorations as well as designs for jewellery, plate and other precious objects. Hans Eworth painted two full-length copies in the 1560s of Holbein's Henry VIII derived from the Whitehall pattern and included a Holbein in the background of his Mary Neville, Lady Dacre.
32. Hans Eworth (neither; proposed as alternate 5 below) - the Antwerp Fleming who got to Tudor London first. Flemish painter, c.1520-1574. Recorded as "Jan Euworts", freeman of the Guild of St Luke in Antwerp 1540; "Jan and Nicholas Ewouts, painter and mercer" were expelled from Antwerp for heresy in 1544; resident in London by 1545, well recorded from 1549. About 40 paintings attributed, among them portraits of Mary I (signed "HE", dated 1554, National Portrait Gallery) and Elizabeth I; Elizabeth I and the Three Goddesses (1569); Sir John Luttrell with the goddess Pax; decorative commissions for Elizabeth's Office of the Revels into 1573; died, believed, 1574.
33. Marcus Gheeraerts the Elder (neither; proposed as reserve 4 below) - the Flemish emigre a generation after Herwijck. Flemish painter, draughtsman, print designer and etcher, c.1520 - c.1590. Born Bruges, son of Egbert Gheeraerts; trained in his father's workshop; took up residence in London on arrival in England in 1568, fleeing religious persecution; worked for Sir William More of Loseley Hall and the Sidney family; made the 1562 bird's-eye view map of Bruges (ten plates) and the illustrated Aesop's Fables, De warachtighe fabulen der dieren (1567, with Edewaerd de Dene); the Allegory of Iconoclasm (c.1566-68, Tate Britain); the Peace Portrait of Elizabeth I is traditionally attributed to him but the attribution is contested. His son Marcus the Younger became a prominent court painter. Death date unresolved (see C273).
34. George Gower (neither; proposed as alternate 8 below) - the native English portraitist who inherited the Flemish court portrait. English portrait painter, c.1540-1596; Serjeant Painter to Queen Elizabeth I from 1581, responsible for painting portraits and for copying and restoring paintings at the palace (Hilliard painted miniatures; Gower painted the large portraits). Plimpton "Sieve" Portrait of Elizabeth, 1579 (Folger Shakespeare Library); self-portrait 1579. The Woburn Abbey Armada Portrait was formerly attributed to Gower; all three extant versions are now thought to be by different unknown English artists. In 2020 Edward Town attributed the Hampden portrait of Elizabeth, formerly attributed to van der Meulen, to Gower. In 1584 Gower and Hilliard together drafted a patent for a monopoly on all portraits of the Queen; there is no evidence it was ever granted.
35. Isaac Oliver (neither; proposed as alternate 9 below) - Hilliard's pupil, the next generation of the miniature. English portrait miniature painter, c.1565 - buried 2 October 1617. Born Rouen c.1565, moved to London in 1568 with his Huguenot parents Peter and Epiphany Oliver; studied miniature painting under Nicholas Hilliard; developed a naturalistic style influenced by Italian and Flemish art; used light and shade (chiaroscuro) for a softer, more illusionistic style than Hilliard's. Court painter to James I; miniaturist to Anne of Denmark and to Henry, Prince of Wales; son Peter Oliver was also a miniaturist. Elizabeth I and the Three Goddesses, c.1588 (NPG 6947), is attributed to him. Birth year disputed (see C274).
36. Domenico Poggini (neither; proposed as main 3 below) - the Florentine medallic parallel. Sculptor, medallist, die-engraver, goldsmith, bronze-caster and poet, 1520-1590. Earliest training probably from his father Michele; worked mostly with his brother Giampaolo until Giampaolo's departure between 1553 and 1555 to work for Philip II, King of Spain, in the Netherlands and Spain, the same Habsburg Netherlands world as Jonghelinck and da Trezzo. Duke Cosimo I's die-cutter from 1556 and master of the Florentine mint; bronze medal of Cosimo I, 1561 (reverse: the building of the Uffizi; V and A 759-1865) and 1567 (reverse: Neptune and the fortified harbour of Portoferraio; V and A 132-1867); Apollo in the Boboli Gardens 1559; bust of Francesco I de' Medici 1564; bronze figure for the Studiolo of the Palazzo Vecchio; marble Bacchus 1554; medal of Cosimo crowned by Victory, c.1571 (British Museum, Warburg record).
37. Pastorino de' Pastorini (neither; proposed as alternate 10 below) - the Sienese wax-and-medal parallel. Italian medallist, glass painter and die engraver, born Castelnuovo della Berardenga c.1508, died Florence 6 December 1592. One of the most productive medallists of the Italian Renaissance, producing about 200 medals; held mint posts at Ferrara (1554-9), Bologna (1572), Novellara (1574) and Florence (1576); well known and respected as a portraitist in coloured wax, developing new materials and techniques to represent hair and skin. Medals include Francesco de' Medici 1560, Ariosto, Ippolito Malaguzzi 1554, Girolama Sacrata 1555, Federico Zuccaro 1578 (all V and A). The Salton Collection catalogue groups him with Antonio Abondio (already carried #520) and Alessandro Abondio as the major creators of the wax portrait medium.
38. Pisanello (neither; proposed as alternate 11 below) - the origin of the form. Antonio di Puccio Pisano, c.1395 - c.1455 (birth range 1380/1395, death range 1450/1455, probably between 14 July and 8 October 1455; see C277). Painter of the early Italian Renaissance and Quattrocento; the most important commemorative portrait medallist of the first half of the 15th century, who "can claim to have originated this important genre" and "became the inventor of the fields of portrait medals and related medallic art": a portrait on the front and a symbolic feat on the reverse. His first medal, of the Byzantine Emperor John VIII Palaeologus, was made at Ferrara in 1438; about three dozen portrait medals for 23 sitters, including the Leonello d'Este medal of 1444 (V and A A.165-1910), made to commemorate Leonello's marriage to Maria of Aragon. He signed his medals "Pictoris" (painter).
39. Hans Reinhart the Elder (neither; researched spare, NOT proposed) - the German medallic parallel. Goldsmith and medallist, c.1510-1581; born Dresden (or Torgau) c.1510; citizen of Leipzig 1539; worked for the Court of Saxony in Dresden; student of the painter Lucas Cranach the Elder (per CNG), his portraits often following Cranach's paintings. Two medals of Charles V, 1537 and 1544 or 1547 (see C275); Trinity Medal, first version 1544, with versions dated 1556, 1561, 1566, 1568 and 1574; Johann Friedrich I medal 1535 (CNG Nomos 1, lot 189). Held back as a spare: the German medallic line is a step sideways from Herwijck's Antwerp-London axis; use if any of the 11 proposed fails.
40. Giovanni Giacomo Caraglio (neither) - a parallel medallist of the same sitter. Goldsmith; his portrait medal of King Sigismund Augustus, featuring the figure of Faith with the Virgil quote "DVM SPIRITVS HOS REGET ARTVS", is in the Ashmolean Museum and features in Caraglio's portrait painted by Paris Bordone in 1553, so it is dated to the same year, eight years before Herwijck's Polish medals of the same king. 41. Hans Schwarz (neither) - a parallel medallist of the same dynasty. Augsburg medallist, 1492/1493 - after 1527. A medal of King Sigismund I the Old, made as a gift for the king's sixtieth birthday on 1 January 1527, has been attributed either to Schwarz or to the Augsburg school (Vilnius catalogue). ### Scholars, collectors and the record
43. George Vertue (neither) - 18th-century English engraver and antiquary. He speculated that the "Ste. H." / "Ste. H. F." signatures on Herwijck's medals stood for "Stephen of Holland" (Stephanus Hollandus fecit). The misconception stood until 1922.
44. Victor Tourneur (neither) - scholar. In 1922 Tourneur showed that the medallist "Ste H." could be identified with the Steven van Herwijck born in Utrecht around 1530.
45. Bendor Grosvenor (neither) - scholar. In the Spring 2009 issue of The British Art Journal he proposed that the "famous paynter Steven" of the 1590 Lumley inventory was Herwijck, not van der Meulen. The identification was accepted by Tate Britain and the V and A, who exhibited a portrait of Elizabeth I as attributed to Herwijck; it is rejected by Goldring (2014) and Town (2014) (see C266).
46. Roy Strong (neither) - scholar. Identified the "famous paynter Steven" with Steven van der Meulen and built a body of works dated roughly 1560-1568 around him; the discovery of van der Meulen's 1563 will and the dendrochronology of the Lumley portrait (1570s-80s) have forced reassessment of those attributions.
47. Edward Town (neither) - scholar. A Biographical Dictionary of London Painters, 1547-1625 (Walpole Society, 2014): rejects the Herwijck identification of the "paynter Steven" and argues the Dymock/Sweden sequence fits van der Meulen better; in 2020 attributed the Hampden portrait of Elizabeth I to George Gower.
48. Elizabeth Goldring (neither) - scholar. Painting and Patronage at the Elizabethan Court (2014): raises concerns with the identification of van Herwijck with the "paynter Steven".
49. John Lumley, 1st Baron Lumley (neither) - collector. His 1590 inventory of paintings refers to a number of works dated to the early 1560s by "the famous paynter Steven", the document at the centre of the Herwijck/van der Meulen dispute. Dendrochronological study of his portrait in the National Portrait Gallery (NPG 5262) dates it to the 1570s or 1580s, after the documented deaths of both Stevens; the NPG concludes the artist "must remain as an as yet unidentified emigre".
50. William Cecil (neither) - Queen's Secretary. In 1565 he received the correspondence from Charles Utenhove seeking permission to produce the portrait medal of Elizabeth I (entry 11). The minister who had to approve a foreigner's medal of the Queen.
51. Charles Utenhove (neither) - Flemish poet and scholar. In 1565 he wrote to William Cecil seeking permission to produce a portrait medal of Elizabeth I with a Greek inscription on the reverse meaning "divine fountain of the realm", the Greek letters being an anagram of "Elizabeth the Queen". The medal that resulted is Herwijck's NPG 4294.

## Corrections
- C259. The death date. Four different death windows across sources: the Wikipedia body says he "died in London sometime before Easter 1567, leaving his widow Jonekin or Johane and children" (citing Carson 2006, p. 326); the NPG gives "circa 1530-1567"; the Wawel/Vilnius 2009 catalogue gives "[c. 1530-c. 1565]"; the Wikipedia notes say "The National Portrait Gallery web site accepts a date death of 1563-64" and a Sotheby's condition report gives "Fl. 1543-1563". The pre-Easter-1567 date is the one the Wikipedia body asserts with a source; the others are catalogue shorthand. Do not state a single death year as settled. The study uses "died before Easter 1567" and treats 1565 as the last certain activity year.
- C260. The 1563-64 death-date trap, resolved. The Wikipedia note "The National Portrait Gallery web site accepts a date death of 1563-64" belongs to Steven van der Meulen, not Herwijck: van der Meulen was buried 24 October 1563 and his will was proved 20 January 1564, and the van der Meulen article confirms the NPG accepts 1563-64 for him. In the Herwijck article the note sits in the "famous paynter Steven" section, where it is easily misread as Herwijck's death date. Do not cite 1563-64 for Herwijck.
- C261. The year the identity was discovered. Web Gallery of Art: "Although he was a skilled artist, his identity was not discovered until 1921." Wikipedia: "In 1922 Victor Tourneur showed that the medallist 'Ste H.' could be identified with the Steven van Herwijck born in Utrecht around 1530." 1921 versus 1922; the Web Gallery of Art's 1921 is likely the publication year of the discovery versus Tourneur's 1922 demonstration, but neither source in this study explains the gap. The study uses 1922 for Tourneur's demonstration and flags 1921.
- C262. The Michel de Castelnau medal date. Web Gallery of Art lists Michel de Castelnau among the 1562 English medals ("all 1562"). Wikipedia: "His medallion of the French ambassador Michel de Castelnau is also dated 1565" (citing Hawkins 1885, p. 113). 1562 versus 1565 for the same medal. Castelnau's own movements make 1565 in London unlikely on the face of it: he left England in 1562 and was fighting in France 1562-1563, but the medal could have been made from an earlier sitting or in 1565 during a diplomatic visit; no source read resolves this. Both dates are on record; the study does not pick one silently.
- C263. "Struck" versus "cast" for the Elizabeth medal. Wikipedia: "Van Herwijck returned to London in 1565, where he struck a medal of Queen Elizabeth". NPG 4294: "This cast lead medal of Queen Elizabeth I...". Lead medals are cast, not struck, and Hill states his medals are always cast. "Struck" is wrong here. The study uses "cast" throughout.
- C264. The birthplace. Wikipedia: "the Steven van Herwijck born in Utrecht around 1530". Hill (1920), p. 134: the assumption that he was a native of Utrecht, drawn from the signature misreading plus the Utrecht medals, "is by no means justified. Artistically, we must rather look for his origin in the school which is responsible for that little group of medals, produced between 1552 and 1555... Stephen may very well have been a native of Flanders or Brabant, and it would be desirable to search the archives there, rather than in Utrecht, in order to discover something of his history." Utrecht birth is the standard account; Hill's objection stands unanswered in the sources read. The study uses "Utrecht c.1530" as commonly cited and flags the dispute.
- C265. The later years and the Polish court in 1571-1572. Hill (1920): "in 1558 and 1559 he was working in Utrecht; from 1559 to 1561 chiefly in Antwerp; in Poland in 1561 and 1562; in England in 1562 and 1563. In 1564 he was back in Utrecht. Then he disappears; for the evidence that he was working at the Polish court in 1571 and 1572 is of no value." Against Hill's "then he disappears": Wikipedia has him returning to London in 1565, making the Elizabeth and Castelnau medals, and dying before Easter 1567. Hill wrote before Tourneur's 1922 identification, so his 1564 cut-off reflects the pre-identification record; the 1571-1572 Polish court claim is one Hill explicitly rejects, and no source in this study revives it.
- C266. The "famous paynter Steven" identification is contested, not settled. Grosvenor (British Art Journal, Spring 2009) proposed Herwijck as the "famous paynter Steven" of the 1590 Lumley inventory, displacing the traditional Steven van der Meulen; Tate Britain and the V and A exhibited a portrait of Elizabeth I attributed to van Herwijck on that basis. Against: Elizabeth Goldring (2014) "raises concerns with identification of van Herwijck with the 'paynter Steven'"; Edward Town (2014) rejects it; dendrochronology dates the John Lumley portrait (NPG 5262) to the 1570s or 1580s, after both men's deaths, and the NPG leaves its artist as "an as yet unidentified emigre". Do not present Herwijck as the "paynter Steven" as fact. The study does not count the contested paintings (L3-L5) as Herwijck works.
- C267. The Mor portrait sitter identification. Wikipedia and the Antonis Mor article present the sitter of Portrait of a Goldsmith (1564) as Herwijck as fact. The 1910 Mauritshuis catalogue lists the picture only as "Portrait of a goldsmith" with no sitter named. The identification is an attribution, not a documented fact. The study states the claim with its provenance, as the accepted identification, not as a documented fact, and the doorway records the letter-fail on that basis.
- C268. Anthonis Mor's dates. Wikipedia: Anthonis Mor (c. 1517-1577). The wahooart and wikioo mirrors give Antonis Mor (1519-1575). The Alamy/RKD snippet gives "c. 1517/1521-1576/1577". Three different spans; the 1564 date of the Portrait of a Goldsmith is agreed by all. The study uses c.1517-1577 as commonly cited and flags the spread.
- C269. The Sigismund II Augustus medal date. Wikipedia and the Web Gallery of Art place the Polish portraits in 1561, the year of the Poland trip. The NGA dates its specimen (NGA 45436) "c. 1562", and the Sigismund II Augustus article illustrates it as "Medal obverse featuring Sigismund from 1562". The Wawel catalogue dates the Catherine medal "Vilnius [in] 1561". A medal dated circa 1562 may have been modelled in Poland in 1561 and cast later, or modelled after his return; no source in this study resolves it. Do not assert the Sigismund medal was made in Poland in 1561 as a single dated fact; the study says circa 1561-1562.
- C270. Name spellings. Dorcas: "Eglestone" (Wikipedia, Web Gallery of Art, Grueber) vs "Eccelstone" (Wikipedia body variant) vs "Ecclestone" (Martin article). Edmund: "Withypoll" (Wikipedia) vs "Withipoll" (Web Gallery of Art, Grueber). Maria: "Maria Dimock or Dymock" (Wikipedia) vs "Maria Newce, wife of John Dymock" (Grueber, maiden name Newce). These are spelling and naming variants for the same sitters, not different people; Grueber's forms are the ones engraved on the medals (EDMVND WITHIPOLL; MARIA NEWCE VX. IOHS DIMOCK; DORCAS EGLESTONE VX. RICHARD MARTIN). The study uses the engraved forms for the inscriptions and the Wikipedia forms for the sitters.
- C271. The Antwerp count. "Nine medals survive of his work there [Antwerp 1559]" (Wikipedia) versus the Hieronymus van Tuyll medal dated Antwerp 1558 (Rijksmuseum, 4.7 cm, 37.67 g). If the nine are all 1559, the 1558 Tuyll piece is a tenth Antwerp medal; if the count is loose, it may be one of the nine. No source read lists the nine by name, so the count cannot be checked. Do not state "nine" as a verified total alongside the Tuyll piece without flagging this.
- C272. "Master Staffan" in Sweden, 1561. John Dymock took a Netherlandish painter called "Master Staffan" to Sweden in 1561 to paint Erik XIV for the marriage negotiations. Grosvenor's case for Herwijck rests partly on the Dymock link (Herwijck's medal of Maria Dimock). Town (2014) argues the sequence of events after the Swedish embassy fits van der Meulen's subsequent career in England better than Herwijck's. Unresolved. The study does not assert Herwijck was "Master Staffan".
- C273. Marcus Gheeraerts the Elder's death date. Wikipedia: date of death not known, "must have been before 1599"; myopenmuseum: c.1590; wahooart/artsdot: 1591, unsourced. The 1591 date is not trusted; the study uses "c.1520 - c.1590" as commonly cited with the warning.
- C274. Isaac Oliver's birth year. Wikipedia: c.1565; Dictionary of National Biography: 1556?; V and A: "born about 1558-1568 possibly in Rouen". The study uses c.1565 as commonly cited and flags the spread.
- C275. Hans Reinhart's Charles V medals. Google Arts and Culture says he made two medals for Charles V, in 1537 and in 1544; the V and A records give 1537 (accession 2384-1855) and 1547 (accession 2277-1855). The 1544 vs 1547 conflict stands unresolved. Reinhart is held as a spare, not proposed.
- C276. Richard Martin's mint offices. DNB: appointed Warden of the Mint 1559-60, Master 1580-1; Wikipedia infobox: Warden 1560-1595, Master 1582-1617. Minor; the offices themselves are not disputed. The study gives the Wikipedia infobox dates and flags the DNB variant.
- C277. Pisanello's dates. Born between 1380 and 1395, died between 1450 and 1455 (probably between 14 July and 8 October 1455). The study uses c.1395 - c.1455 as commonly cited and flags the ranges.
- C278. Steven van der Meulen's death, resolved. Hellenicaworld/Alchetron say he "died in London between October 1563 and January 1564"; Wikipedia gives the burial: buried at St Andrew Undershaft on 24 October 1563, will proved 20 January 1564. The burial date settles it; the "between October and January" phrasing confuses the will's proving date with the death. The study uses buried 24 October 1563.
- C279. The Heneage Jewel presentation, parent correction. The #523 doorway says Hilliard's Armada Jewel went to Thomas Heneage. The V and A object record for the Heneage Jewel states: "There is no evidence that the jewel was a presentation from Queen Elizabeth, although it is possible that she gave the miniature by Hilliard to a recipient who had the magnificent case made in tribute to the queen", and Sir Roy Strong dates the pendant c.1600, after Sir Thomas Heneage's death in 1595, so the first owner may have been Michael Heneage, who died in 1600. The doorway holds as family tradition, the jewel stayed in the Heneage family until 1902, but not as documented presentation to Sir Thomas. The study states the doorway with this caveat.
