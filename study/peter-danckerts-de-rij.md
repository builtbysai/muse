# Peter Danckerts de Rij: Study Notes

**Site:** Peter Danckerts de Rij (1605, Amsterdam -- buried 15 December 1660, Amsterdam), Dutch Golden Age portrait painter; son of the Amsterdam architect and sculptor Cornelis Danckerts de Ry, court painter and architect to Wladyslaw IV Vasa of Poland from 1638, then to Queen Christina of Sweden in Stockholm from 1648, returned to Amsterdam 1659. He painted the Vasa court in its representative mode -- coronation robes, red zupans, dynastic series -- and is the second of the dynasty's two Dutch court painters, succeeding Pieter Soutman after a ten-year gap. His surviving oeuvre is thin and loss-heavy: most of the court work was plundered in the Deluge or burned in the Tre Kronor fire of 1697, and several of his best-known pictures survive only as engravings after him.

## The life

Peter Danckerts de Rij was born in Amsterdam in 1605, the son of Cornelis Danckerts de Ry (1561-1634), architect and sculptor, and a member of what Houbraken's circle calls "a large family of printers, painters and engravers". The family is the first fact and the first trap: there are two Cornelises in it, and the literature confuses them. Pieter's father Cornelis de Ry (1561-1634) built houses and churches in Haarlem, according to Cornelis de Bie in Het Gulden Cabinet; the other Cornelis Danckerts the Elder (c. 1603-1656) was a map- and print-publisher whose sons Dancker and Justus ran the Danckerts atlas firm into the 1720s. The study keeps them apart (see C1167). Pieter's mother is unnamed in every source consulted. His teacher is not named anywhere: a commercial gallery page says he was "likely" trained by his father, which is conjecture and is not stated here as fact. No record of a wife or children of Peter himself was found.

The earliest secure works are a family commission. In 1634 he painted pendant portraits of his parents on the occasion of their fiftieth wedding anniversary: his father's portrait hangs in the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium in Brussels, his mother's in the Johannesburg Art Gallery. The father's portrait became the model for Pieter de Jode II's engraving in Het Gulden Cabinet (1649). A contemporary judgement survives on the pair: Van Dyke (1914) called the Brussels portraits "substantial portraits, intelligently done, and with sobriety and skill, but not masterpieces" -- sober, dark-ground, half-length burgher work, the likeness carried by attributes (in the engraving the father holds a compass and a scale model) rather than by costume display.

The Amsterdam years also show him inside the family's print economy. The 1642 engraving of the poet P. C. Hooft was painted by Joachim von Sandrart, etched by Reinier van Persijn, and printed by Danckerts, with a Latin poem by Caspar Barlaeus at the foot -- Danckerts as printer-publisher, not as painter of the portrait. Sandrart engraved some of Peter's paintings after the same period, and Cornelis de Ry is named in Houbraken as one of Sandrart's many teachers in 1640-41 -- though Sandrart, just back from his Italian Grand Tour at 34, was a collaborator more than a pupil, and the study says so.

The move to Poland is dated 1638, and the trigger is a wedding. Wladyslaw IV, who had reigned since 1632, had just begun preparations for his marriage to Cecilia Renata of Austria, daughter of Emperor Ferdinand II -- married by proxy in Vienna on 9 August 1637, in person in Warsaw on 12 September 1637, crowned the same day at St John's Cathedral -- and needed artwork for decorating the royal residence. Danckerts, 33, "already a mature artist", took the post Soutman had left ten years earlier. The departure date itself is contested: the current Wikipedia revision says "Between 1634 and 1638 he moved to Warsaw, and later Danzig, and Vilnius", while the old revision says he was "active until 1640 in Amsterdam, and then he moved" -- both citing the RKD, which no leg read directly (see C1168). The 1638 appointment is firm: the Kazanowski portrait is dated 1638, and the court work begins there.

At the Vasa court he painted the royal couple, the aristocracy and the patriciate, in the representative mode: portraits that "show carefully all details of the rich dress". The best-known single picture is the Adam Kazanowski of 1638, the king's childhood friend and Court Chamberlain, in a red zupan, praised for "the perfection of the drawing, the subtlety in running lights and a great devotion to red". He painted both of Wladyslaw's queens -- Cecilia Renata (1630s and c. 1640, plus the 1643 Nationalmuseum portrait) and Louise Marie Gonzaga -- the crown prince Sigismund Casimir (1644), the king's sister Anna Catherine Constance (with a fan, 1642, a dowry portrait for her Warsaw wedding), the king's brother John Casimir in full length (c. 1638, attributed, 210 x 137 cm, Germanisches Nationalmuseum), and the king's children by his mistress Jadwiga Luszkowska. And he painted the dynasty itself: 22 images of Wladyslaw's ancestors and relatives -- Vasa, Jagiellon, Habsburg -- on octagonal tin plates for the Marble Cabinet at the Royal Castle in Warsaw. Only five of the twenty-two survive, in Nieborow (3), Moscow and Vilnius; the rest were lost in the Deluge, the Swedish plunder of 1655-57 that also took the Vasa collection's Titians, Tintorettos, Rembrandts and Rubenses.

In 1648 he moved to Stockholm, to paint at the court of Queen Christina. Her successor Charles X Gustav "held him in high regard". He returned to the Dutch Republic in 1659 and was buried in Amsterdam on 15 December 1660. That is the whole of the Stockholm record as the sources give it: eleven years at a major northern court, and no titled Danckerts painting from those years surfaced in sixty search passes. Christina's surviving portraits in those years are by David Beck, Sebastien Bourdon, Justus van Egmont, Jacob Heinrich Elbfas, Sandrart and Abraham Wuchters -- not by Danckerts. The study does not invent Stockholm works (see M7 and C1175).

His death is the study's one resolved dispute, and it is resolved against the picturesque version. The older story -- repeated by the 2012 Polen article and an earlier Wikipedia revision -- says he died on 9 August 1661, murdered by highway robbers in the Rudninkai Forest near Vilnius, having first sketched his attackers so they could be caught. The current Wikipedia and RKD account says he was buried in Amsterdam on 15 December 1660, and that the robbery victim was an unnamed artist aged 78, while Danckerts would have been 55: the incident "possibly concerns one of Danckerts' successors at the Polish court instead". The sketching-thugs detail appears in no scholarly source and looks like folklore. The study uses 1660 and records the robbery tale only as a superseded error (see C1166).

Houbraken's life of him is short, and it is the only contemporary critical judgement found: "PIETER DANCKERTS was born in Amsterdam in 1605. He was a good painter of portraits. His brushwork was esteemed both in the Netherlands as in other regions." A verse follows, praising the hand that worked "many a time at the Polish court". Baldinucci wrote him up at p. 379 of the Notizie: "Fu anche buon pittore di ritratti Pietro Danckerse de Ry, nato in Amsterdam l'anno 1605, dell'opera del quale molto si servi la Maesta di Vlasislao IV. Re di Pollonia" -- a good portrait painter, born in Amsterdam in 1605, whose work His Majesty Wladyslaw IV, King of Poland, made much use of. The Baldinucci entry has prompted speculation that Danckerts visited Italy at some time; no evidence of an Italian journey was found, and the study does not assert one. No named pupil, no workshop, no Amsterdam activity in his final year 1659-60 was found in any source read. The RKD lists him as court painter, decorative painter (of interiors) and painter, and his Warsaw title was "court painter and architect" -- but no building or interior by him is named anywhere, and the study invents none (see C1173).

## The evolution: from the pendant to the dynasty, and then the gap

The 1634-35 Amsterdam works (W01-W03) are small-scale, half-length, dark-ground and intimate: the parents' pendants for a golden wedding, and a young man costumed as a hunter with javelin and hunting horn -- the sitter dressed as something, a dress-up device that anticipates the court portraits' reliance on costume as meaning. Mechanism: likeness plus one or two attributes. Palette: sober, dark, the face as the single light event. No serial production, no dynastic programme, no landscape.

The Gdansk transition (c. 1637-40, W04-W05, reported only, low confidence) introduces the background as information: a portrait bust set against a named city panorama -- Gdansk, the port that was his first Polish base -- instead of a neutral ground. The portrait stops being a face on a dark ground and starts being a person in a place. This is the hinge between the Amsterdam pendant and the Warsaw state portrait: same half-length handling, new documentary ambition. (W04 and W05 may be the same picture under two titles; the study does not merge them without evidence.)

The Polish court years (1638-48) change four things at once, all evidenced. Scale: from half-length pendants to the 210 x 137 cm full-length John Casimir (W17) and the lost full-length equestrian queen (W11) -- the body gets bigger because the portrait's job gets bigger: diplomacy, dowry, dynastic display. Costume as the subject: the Kazanowski red zupan (W06) is painted with "great devotion to red", and the royal portraits "show carefully all details of the rich dress" -- textile, its colour, its fold, its jewel, becomes the primary descriptive labour, while the face is rendered with "subtlety in running lights", a lighter, more economical touch. Seriality: the 22 octagonal tin effigies (W19) are a production system, not 22 individual sittings -- one format, repeated, spanning dead kings -- alongside repeated royal likenesses (W08, W09/W10, W12) and dowry portraits that travel (W15 to Neuburg and Vienna, W17 mislabelled for centuries as a Habsburg emperor). And the painting-to-print pipeline: Hondius engraves after Danckerts (W11, and a Kazanowski print of 1646 after a portrait), Sandrart engraves some of Peter's paintings, Danckerts himself prints (Hooft, 1642) -- the painting is increasingly the matrix for a print that travels further than the canvas. Palette: the court years are dominated by red (Kazanowski's zupan), black court dress, gold (chains, orders, embroidery) and the pale flesh and blond hair of the child heir (W12). The early Amsterdam sobriety is retained in the drawing, but the colour is now declarative and heraldic.

Stockholm (1648-59) leaves no verifiable change in format, scale, palette or handling, because no work can be securely placed there. The honest evolution statement is negative (see M7). Late Amsterdam (1659-60) is silence: no work dated 1659 or 1660 surfaced. The career effectively ends with the Polish court pictures; the return is a biographical fact, not an artistic phase, on current evidence.

The summary arc: burgher pendant (1634) -- located civic portrait (c. 1640) -- full-length dynastic state portrait plus serial tin effigies plus print distribution (1638-48) -- eleven years at Stockholm with no surviving attributable work -- return and death (1659-60). The arc is not a stylistic development toward looseness or darkness (no evidence for either); it is a change in commission and format -- from likeness, to location, to dynasty as a system.

## M1 -- The successor's chair: the post outlives the man

Danckerts's first mechanism is a ten-year gap. Soutman served Sigismund III from 1624 to 1628 and went home to Haarlem; Danckerts took the same post in 1638, under the son. The Polen article states the interval in so many words -- "his place was taken -- but only after ten years" -- and the gap is the mechanism: a court post is an office that waits, and the man who fills it inherits not a master's teaching but a predecessor's format. Danckerts carried forward the coronation-robe state portrait Soutman had made for Sigismund III in 1626, now for Wladyslaw IV and his queens, and added two things Soutman never had: the architect's half of the title, and the 22-effigy dynastic gallery. No evidence was found of the two men meeting or corresponding; the link is institutional, not personal, and the study says so. The mechanism is succession without contact: the same chair, the same dynasty, a decade apart, and the format is what travels between them.

**Takeaway:** build the piece around the empty chair -- the post that waits ten years, the new man sitting down into a format the old man left behind, and let the gap itself be the subject: what changes when the same office is filled by a different hand.

## M2 -- Devotion to red: the costume is the subject

The Kazanowski portrait of 1638 is the single most-cited Danckerts picture in the Polish literature, and the citation is always about the same three things: "the perfection of the drawing, the subtlety in running lights and a great devotion to red". The mechanism is the division of labour inside one canvas: the face is modelled with fine, running light -- a light, economical touch -- while the costume, above all the saturated red textile of the Polish zupan, is painted with devotional exactness. The royal portraits work the same way: they "show carefully all details of the rich dress". Rank is not in the face, which any good portraitist can render; it is in the cloth, the fold, the jewel, the embroidery, and the painter's willingness to spend his best attention there. The red is not decoration on the portrait; it is the portrait's argument about who this man is. (A second Kazanowski portrait, c. 1643 at Wawel, is reported in a blog and not verified end to end -- see C1171 -- so the study builds on the 1638 picture and flags the second.)

**Takeaway:** paint one colour as if it were the sitter -- a single red coat, rendered stitch by stitch, with the face kept quiet, and let the textile do the ranking; the devotion is the portrait.

## M3 -- The octagonal dynasty: twenty-two plates, one format

The Marble Room commission is Danckerts's most conceptually distinctive work, and almost none of it survives. Twenty-two images of Wladyslaw's ancestors and relatives -- Vasa, Jagiellon, Habsburg -- painted on octagonal tin plates to decorate the Marble Cabinet at the Royal Castle in Warsaw. The sitters include Sigismund Augustus (d. 1572) and Bona Sforza (d. 1557): these are retrospective portraits, painted two centuries after death from older models, not from life. The octagon is the mechanism: a shape that tiles a room, turns ancestors into ornament, and makes the living king the culmination of a visible lineage. The support matters too -- tin, not canvas or panel: a uniform, durable, architectural material for a set that was meant to function as wall, not as twenty-two separate pictures. Only five plates survive, in Nieborow (3), Moscow and Vilnius. The rest were lost in the Deluge and later dispersals, and the present Marble Room in the Royal Castle is a post-war reconstruction that does not hold them -- so the series must be described in the past tense, with the survival qualification (see C1172). Daniel Schultz the Younger's coffin portrait of John II Casimir was later placed in the Marble Room among Danckerts's effigies: the succession made literal, one frame hung among the other's.

**Takeaway:** build the serial set, not the single portrait -- twenty-two identical octagonal plates, same format, same framing logic, sitters spanning two centuries, and the set is the history; a single face is a sample, a dynasty is an argument made by repetition.

## M4 -- The located portrait: the city behind the sitter

The Gdansk works (W04-W05, reported only, low confidence) carry the study's smallest and most portable mechanism: a portrait bust set against a named city panorama instead of a neutral ground. The young man against the background of Gdansk is the hinge of the whole career -- the Amsterdam pendant's handling with a new documentary ambition, the sitter located in the port city that was Danckerts's first Polish base (Gdansk 1637-40 per the authority data). The landscape background does documentary work: it says where this person belongs, and it says it in the picture rather than in a caption. The mechanism generalises: the portrait's ground stops being a void and becomes a place, and the place is part of the likeness. The flag travels with it: W04 and W05 may be the same picture under two titles, and neither location is museum-verified, so the study presents the mechanism as reported, not as a secure pair.

**Takeaway:** put the city in the portrait -- one named panorama behind a bust, the ground doing the locating, and the sitter becomes a person in a place rather than a face on a void.

## M5 -- The painting that survives as a print

Danckerts's most emblematic fate is the Equestrian Portrait of Queen Cecilia Renata (W11): the painting is lost -- "probably destroyed during the Deluge or in the fire of the Tre Kronor Castle in Stockholm (1697)" -- and what survives is Willem Hondius's 1645 engraving after it, 55.5 x 43.9 cm, in the National Museum in Warsaw, inscribed "Petro Danckerss pinxit" and dedicated to Prince Sigismund Casimir. The mechanism is the print as the painting's afterlife: Hondius engraved after Danckerts, Sandrart engraved some of Peter's paintings, and Danckerts himself printed the 1642 Hooft engraving -- the family print economy running alongside the easel. The painting was increasingly the matrix for a print that travels further than the canvas, and for Danckerts the print is often all that is left: "some paintings are known only from engravings". The equestrian format itself matters -- the only equestrian composition in his known work, the most ambitious type he attempted, a full horse-and-rider state portrait in the format Rubens had established for the Vasas -- and it survives only in copper. The engraving's Latin dedication and its "vera effigies" claim are part of the work: the print asserts the likeness the lost painting can no longer defend.

**Takeaway:** show the print where the painting should be -- the engraving as the surviving half of the work, the copper plate carrying the likeness the fire took, and let the absence be visible: the picture exists, but only in its second medium.

## M6 -- The travelling portrait: dowry, mislabelling, and the wrong emperor

The court portrait was a diplomatic instrument, and Danckerts's pictures travelled further than he did. The Lady with a Fan (W15, Anna Catherine Constance Vasa, 1642) was painted in the year of her Warsaw wedding to Philipp Wilhelm of Neuburg and travelled with her -- it now hangs in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna, its location consistent with Habsburg and Neuburg circulation of Vasa dowry pictures. The full-length John Casimir (W17, c. 1638, attributed, 210 x 137 cm, Germanisches Nationalmuseum) is "most probably one of portraits from a dowry of his sister Anna Catherine Constance Vasa" -- and for centuries it was mislabelled as Emperor Leopold I, the old inscription reading "KAISER LEOPOLD I 1658-1705", while the attribution itself moved from Frans Luycx to Danckerts. The mechanism is the portrait as a travelling object that outruns its own label: the same face, the same court formula, filed under the wrong emperor in the wrong country, because the Vasa and Habsburg court portraits were interchangeable enough to be confused. The misattribution is evidence, not embarrassment: it proves how formulaic -- and how portable -- the state portrait had become.

**Takeaway:** let the portrait travel and lose its name -- one full-length figure, painted for a dowry, relabelled as an emperor, and the picture that survives under the wrong name is the proof of how portable the court formula was.

## M7 -- The court without a surviving oeuvre: eleven years, no picture

The Stockholm years are the study's negative mechanism, and it is stated as a negative because the alternative would be invention. Danckerts moved to Queen Christina's court in 1648, served her until her abdication in 1654, served Charles X Gustav until 1659 -- eleven years at a major northern court -- and no titled Danckerts painting from those years surfaced in sixty search passes: no Christina, no Charles X Gustav, no Swedish courtier, no dated 1649-58 work at all. Christina's portraits in these years are by David Beck (her travelling portraitist, at court from 1647), Sebastien Bourdon (first court painter from 1652), Justus van Egmont, Jacob Heinrich Elbfas, Sandrart and Abraham Wuchters. The likely explanation is institutional, not personal: his Swedish work was absorbed into the royal collection at Tre Kronor, much of which burned in 1697, and what survived the fire was dispersed or reattributed -- the W17 misattribution shows how easily a Danckerts becomes a Luycx or a Leopold. The mechanism is the gap as evidence: a court painter can be documented and invisible at once, and the absence of a Swedish oeuvre is itself the fact about his Swedish years. The one plausible continuity is institutional: he remained a court painter, so the serial and diplomatic portrait machine of Warsaw transferred to Stockholm, and its products suffered the same fate -- fire, dispersal, reattribution -- on a larger scale.

**Takeaway:** build the absence -- eleven years at court with no surviving picture, the painter present in the documents and missing from the walls, and let the empty room be the portrait of the Swedish years.

## M8 -- The printer-painter: the family economy in one hand

Danckerts's last mechanism is the one his family gave him: he worked inside a print economy, not only as a painter. He printed the 1642 Hooft engraving -- painted by Sandrart, etched by Persijn, printed by Danckerts, poem by Barlaeus -- and he was the son of an architect-sculptor in a family that also ran a map- and print-publishing house (the other Cornelis's firm, the Danckerts Atlas, wall maps, city views). Sandrart engraved some of Peter's paintings; Hondius engraved after him; Pieter de Jode II engraved his father's portrait after his painting for Het Gulden Cabinet. The mechanism is the division of the image across hands and media: one man paints, another cuts, a third prints, and the picture exists in several states at once -- oil, engraving, book illustration. The RKD's labels for him -- court painter, decorative painter (of interiors), painter -- point the same way: a man whose job was images in several forms, not easel pictures alone. What the study does not claim is a workshop: no named pupil, no studio practice, no drawings catalogue was found, and the "architect" half of his Warsaw title names no building.

**Takeaway:** split the picture across hands -- one paints, one cuts the copper, one prints, and the image lives in several states at once; the painter is one station in a family machine, not a solitary easel.

## The aesthetic logic

Danckerts's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the logic of a man who painted a dynasty rather than a person. Paint the rank, not the face: the costume -- the red zupan, the coronation robe, the fan, the chain -- carries the meaning, and the face is rendered with a lighter, more economical touch, because the face is the least informative part of a state portrait. Paint the set, not the picture: twenty-two octagonal plates, repeated royal likenesses, dowry portraits that travel in sets -- the series is the work, and a single portrait is a sample from a system. Let the print carry the picture: the painting is the matrix, the engraving is the distribution, and when the painting burns or is plundered, the copper is the record. And accept the gap: eleven years at Stockholm leave no picture, and the honest account says so rather than inventing a Swedish manner -- the absence is part of the oeuvre, not a hole in it.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. Danckerts was an Amsterdam burgher's son painting kings in two foreign courts, and his career is an arc of increasing distance from the intimate: from parents painted for a golden wedding, to a queen painted for a dowry, to dead kings painted from older models on tin plates, to eleven years at a court whose pictures do not survive. The intimacy drains out of the work as the commission grows, and what replaces it is system -- format, seriality, distribution. He never becomes a Rembrandt, his exact contemporary (b. 1606, on the same Houbraken pages), and no source connects them; he remains a court-and-corporation painter working in a Flemish-inflected representative mode inside two foreign courts, and the confusion of his pictures with Luycx's and the mislabelling of his prince as an emperor are the price of that position: the same formula, the same decade, the interchangeable hand.

## The network

Cornelis Danckerts de Ry (1561-1634): father, Amsterdam architect and sculptor, builder of houses and churches in Haarlem; sitter of the 1634 pendant portrait, engraved after it by Pieter de Jode II for Het Gulden Cabinet. Cornelis Danckerts the Elder (c. 1603-1656): a different Cornelis, map- and print-publisher, founder of the Danckerts atlas firm; his sons Dancker (1634-1666) and Justus (1635-1701) carried it into the 1720s -- keep the two Cornelises apart. Johan Danckerts (c. 1616-1686) and Hendrick Danckerts (c. 1625-1680): Hague-born landscape painters of the same broad family, Hendrick in Charles II's London service; their exact link to Peter's branch is unresolved, and the DNB rejects both old parentage theories on dates. Pieter Soutman (c. 1593/1601-1657, #582): predecessor at the Vasa court, 1624-28; the post Danckerts took ten years later; no evidence the two met. Wladyslaw IV Vasa (reigned 1632-1648): first patron, court painter and architect from 1638, the marriage to Cecilia Renata the trigger. Cecilia Renata of Austria (1611-1644): first queen, married 1637, died in Vilnius 1644 of infection after childbirth; the 1643 Nationalmuseum portrait may show Gryzelda Konstancja Wisniowiecka instead (see C1170). Sigismund Casimir Vasa (1640-1647): crown prince, painted 1644, dead at seven. John II Casimir Vasa (reigned 1648-1668): served by Danckerts before Stockholm, then by Daniel Schultz the Younger from 1649. Anna Catherine Constance Vasa (1619-1651): the king's sister, fan portrait 1642 for her Neuburg wedding. Adam Kazanowski (c. 1599-1649): the king's childhood friend, Court Chamberlain from 1637, Court Marshal from 1643; the red-zupan portrait of 1638. Albrecht Stanislaw Radziwill (1593-1656): Grand Chancellor of Lithuania, portrait 1640 (reported only). Willem Hondius (c. 1598-1652 or 1658): Danzig engraver, Chalcographus Regius to Wladyslaw IV and John II Casimir; engraved after Danckerts, including the 1645 equestrian Cecilia Renata; the print counterpart to the painted court. Daniel Schultz the Younger (c. 1615-1683): Danzig-born, private painter to John II Casimir from 1649; his coffin portrait of the king now hangs in the Marble Room among Danckerts's effigies. C1172 -- Marble Room naming and contents: the set was 22 images of ancestors and relatives of Wladyslaw IV (Vasa, Jagiellon, Habsburg), not 22 reigning monarchs; the Polen article calls the room the Marble Cabinet, the Castle's own material the Marble Room, and the present Marble Room is a post-war reconstruction that does not hold the surviving plates. ## Future candidates

Top pick for #584 is David Beck (25 May 1621, Delft -- 20 December 1656, The Hague). The other Dutchman at Christina's court -- and the one who got there first. Pupil of Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt in Delft, then to London in 1640 as Van Dyck's pupil and assistant, drawing teacher to the young Charles II and his brothers; after the Civil War he toured the European courts, and in 1647 Christina made him her portrait painter and valet de chambre in Stockholm. From 1647 she sent him from court to court to paint notabilities -- the portrait as diplomacy, her own likeness travelling out as a present while other sovereigns' likenesses travelled back to fill her gallery. He rejoined her in Rome in 1655, followed her to France in 1656, asked leave to visit his family in the Netherlands, and died at The Hague at 35. Danckerts arrived in Stockholm in 1648, a year after Beck; the two Dutch court painters overlapped for five years, one painting the queen's gallery by travel, the other by residence.
