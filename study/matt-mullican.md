## The life: the artist who filed the universe

Matt Mullican was born September 18, 1951, in Santa Monica, California, the son of artists Lee Mullican and Luchita Hurtado. He was born Matthew Angus Paalen, and his last name was Paalen until his father legally adopted him in the late 1950s, around 1957-1958. He grew up inside art -- two artist parents, a house full of pictures -- and his life's work has been to build a filing system for everything a picture can contain, as if the world were a drawer that could finally be organised. (C22808, leg 1 C22600-C22601; synthesis C22759)

He started at the California Institute of the Arts in the fall of 1971, accelerated, graduated one year early, and was in the first graduating class of CalArts, receiving his BFA in 1974. The start year carries a variant he himself created: in the 2019 Smithsonian oral history he wavers between fall 1970 and fall 1971 and settles on 1971. The BFA year 1974 is not in dispute anywhere. At CalArts he studied in John Baldessari's Post Studio Art class; Baldessari started teaching at CalArts in 1970 and Mullican was among his first students. His other named teachers were Alison Knowles (who taught him in his first semester, when Baldessari was on leave in Europe, and ran the graphics lab), Jack Goldstein -- whose Temporary Structures sculpture class was Mullican's first class -- Michael Asher (who substituted in Baldessari's class), and Wolfgang Stoerchle and Harold Budd (who taught the performance class); visiting lecturers in Baldessari's circle included Richard Serra, Vito Acconci, Joan Jonas, and Al Ruppersberg. The student was taught by his predecessor in the chain: Goldstein, the #697 subject, taught Mullican's first class. (C22809, leg 1 C22602-C22605; synthesis C22760-C22761)

The early 1970s are the laboratory years, and the whole later system is already in them. In 1973 Mullican created the stick figure Glen, a character who experiments in an imaginary, fictional studio, and the idea of Details from an Imaginary Universe -- the claim that a picture is a real place. In 1973 he also entered a picture as a performance at Project Inc. in Boston, projecting his mind's eye into a Piranesi print and describing what he saw to the audience. His first exhibition was in 1973, and his second was at CalArts, Gallery A402, in April 1974, a mix of stick figures and colours. Glen is the seed of everything: a minimal figure given a life, a studio, and feelings, so that a drawing can be treated as data about a person who does not exist. (C22810, leg 1 C22607-C22608; synthesis C22762)

After graduating he moved to New York City and became associated with the Pictures Generation, alongside Troy Brauntuch, Jack Goldstein, David Salle, James Welling, Sherrie Levine, Cindy Sherman, Louise Lawler, Richard Prince, and Robert Longo. He began using hypnosis in his performances in 1978, with his first performance at the Kitchen in New York; through hypnosis he developed the alter ego That Person, whom he has described as ageless and genderless. His first performance under hypnosis used actors embodying a demon and an angel, and in the second he himself was hypnotised. The performances could last several hours: while hypnotised he yelled profanity, acted like a 5-year-old, crawled on the floor, and made black ink drawings of numbers, symbols, and phrases on paper, mounted to bedsheets and hung between wooden poles in a maze-like installation. He took a break from the mentally and physically draining hypnosis performances during the 1980s and resumed them in the 1990s. The switch is the point: the same body, two hands, two authors, and the audience watches the second one take over. (C22811, leg 1 C22606, C22609-C22610; leg 2 W05-W06; synthesis C22762)

Over his career Mullican developed two personal cosmologies: the first, devised in 1973, based on his childhood understanding of fate and what comes before birth and after death; the second, dating to 1983, the Five Worlds -- a classification of the universe into five categories with a colour code, verified end to end in four independent sources: green for the material world and the elements, blue for everyday life or "the world unframed", yellow for ideas or "the world framed", black and white for language, and red for the subjective. He works with pictographs, or signs -- universally recognisable images from public places -- and depicts them on banners, bulletin boards, maps, stone reliefs, and canvas rubbings. An example is Untitled (Bath Banner The Arts) (1988), a large black and yellow nylon banner with eight squares in a two-by-four layout showing a music note, a painter's palette, and a camera. He has made rubbings since the early 1980s, working by frottage: a comic or image is projected onto masonite, cut with a knife to make a relief, canvas is laid over it, and the image is rubbed up with carbon and then oil paint sticks, with colours painted in under masking tape. His flags are hand-sewn and often very large, used as architecture in exhibitions: eight red banners (Untitled, 1986) were originally shown on the facade of the Museum of Contemporary Art in Los Angeles, and four hanging banners were originally commissioned for Le Magasin in Grenoble in 1990. (C22812, leg 1 C22611-C22614; leg 2 W12, W14; synthesis C22762)

From the 1980s onward he developed his charts into maps of a fictitious city, and in 1986 a partnership with the Hollywood company Digital Productions let him build the Computer Project (1986-1990; Metalocus gives 1986-1989, unresolved), a re-creation of his cosmology as a cityscape of 18 square kilometres divided into five districts for the five worlds; he made virtual reality environments in the early 1990s. The same system at pavilion scale is the M.I.T. Project (1990- ), a walk-in cosmology first presented at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1990, shown in variants including Documenta IX in 1992 and complete at the 28th Sao Paulo Biennial in 2008, now in the MACBA collection. Its green zone holds natural elements (skeletons, stuffed animals, boxes of insects and minerals, crystals, rocks, fossils); its blue zone holds everyday objects (radio, generator, boiler, 16 mm projector, a monitor showing the video "Five into One"); a yellow rectangle in the middle is the arts and sciences; a black space holds bulletin boards; a red semicircle represents the subjective realm. The artist's own summary: "You could say that the M.I.T. Project is a map, a way of organising information." The bulletin board has been a format for him since the early 1970s, used to display drawings, photographs, objects, book designs, prints, notebook pages, and illustrations -- more than 120 of them at HangarBicocca in 2018. (C22813, leg 1 C22615-C22616; leg 2 W09-W10; synthesis C22762)

The exhibition record runs from P.S.1 in 1986 and the Dallas Museum of Art in 1987 through MoMA Projects 18 in 1989 (August 24 to November 7 per the Peter Freeman CV; the MoMA catalogue title gives October 24, unresolved) and the Hirshhorn the same year, to Documenta VII (1982), IX (1992 -- MASSIMODECARLO and Artsy give 1993, unresolved), and X (1997), the Whitney Biennials of 1989 and 2008, Skulptur Projekte Munster in 1987, the Tate Modern hypnosis performance on January 27, 2007, the Met's "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" (April 21-August 2, 2009, 160 works by 30 artists), Haus der Kunst in 2011, the HangarBicocca retrospective "The Feeling of Things" in 2018, and the de Young show "Between Sign and Subject" in 2019, described by the museum as his first solo project in the United States in twenty years. He was Professor of Time-Based Media at the Hochschule fur bildende Kunste in Hamburg from 2009 to 2018 (Artsy says until 2019, unresolved), and he has also taught at Columbia, the School of Visual Arts, the Rijksakademie, the London Institute, and Chelsea College of Art. He lives and works in Berlin and New York. His recent work continues the same systems: the 2023 Peter Freeman show "Sunday, August 9, 1908" was based on a single page of a Little Nemo in Slumberland comic from that date, in twelve chapters as separate rubbing paintings, and in 2026 he had solo shows at Peter Freeman, New York ("Matt Mullican: The Universe", February 28-April 11) and Galerie Thomas Schulte, Berlin ("Above and Below the Three Worlds", February 14-April 18). His works are held by Tate, the Art Institute of Chicago, MoMA, LACMA, the Cincinnati Art Museum, and the National Gallery of Art, among 17 further public collections listed by MASSIMODECARLO; he is represented by Peter Freeman (New York), Capitain Petzel (Berlin), Mai 36 (Zurich), and Marc Selwyn (Los Angeles); his papers at the Archives of American Art measure 27.8 linear feet, circa 1968-2017, including over 100 notebooks. (C22814, leg 1 C22617-C22628; synthesis C22763-C22764)

## M1 -- Five-colour world tagging (colour IS the category)

Every element in Mullican's work carries exactly one of five colours, and the colour IS its category: green = the material world and the elements, blue = everyday life, the world unframed by consciousness, yellow = culture, science, and art, the world framed by consciousness, black (and white) = language and signs, red = subjective experience. Nothing is coloured for decoration; colour is the filing system. The code runs at every scale: in the 40 drawings of Untitled (Details from the Cosmology) (W08, 1982/83), where each detail is isolated in its colour; in the Computer Project (W09, 1986-1990), where the virtual city is divided into five districts for the five worlds; in the M.I.T. Project (W10, 1990- ), where the visitor walks through colour-differentiated compartments; in the Bath Banner (W12, 1988), black and yellow squares holding the signs of the arts; in the rubbing Untitled (Cosmology Signs) (W14, 1991, 237.5 x 121 cm -- the only work in this study with verified dimensions); and in Notating the Cosmology (W18, 1973-2008), 123 handwritten sheets that show the system complexifying over three decades. The IAC text adds the eschatological edge: green is also death and hell, red is also paradise -- the filing system reaches past life into what comes after it. (C22816, leg 2 M1; synthesis C22768)

The watchable behaviour is a change of colour, which is a change of world. An object visibly changes category -- and therefore colour -- when it crosses a zone boundary or gets framed: a coffee cup in blue is an everyday thing; the same cup, framed, turns yellow and becomes art. At HangarBicocca in 2018 the whole retrospective was hung this way, the visitor walking from a green area (Light Patterns, 1972) through blue (films and videos) and black (bulletin boards, notebooks) to a red semicircle (That Person's work) -- the exhibition itself was the filing cabinet, and the visitor was the thing being filed. A prompt built from this mechanism must make colour do the sorting: five colours only, each one a category, and a visible event when something changes colour. (C22817, leg 2 M1, W08-W10, W12, W14, W18; synthesis C22768)

**Takeaway:** give every element exactly one of five colours -- green material, blue everyday, yellow framed culture, black language, red subjective -- and let colour be the filing system, not decoration; when something changes colour it has changed worlds. (C22818)


## M2 -- Sign extraction: lift, isolate, re-grid

A pictogram is taken from its home context -- an airport sign, a comic strip, a music note, a camera, a painter's palette -- stripped of its surroundings, and placed in a regular grid cell where it must stand alone as a "sign of a sign". Meaning comes from the grid position and the company it keeps, not from the original scene. In Details from a Fictional Reality (W02, 1973) the cutouts come from cartoon strips, and the artist imagines the existence of the characters outside, or before, the story itself. In the Bath Banner (W12, 1988) each of the eight black-and-yellow squares holds one identifiable sign -- a music note, a painter's palette, a camera -- at architectural scale, a sign-board you could hang on a museum facade. In Untitled (Cosmology Signs) (W14, 1991) the cosmology is rendered as a single large rubbing of signs. In Learning from That Person's Work (W16, 2005) each panel contains nine patterns matching the themes That Person is interested in -- love, beauty, work, truth -- the grid itself doing the sorting. (C22819, leg 2 M2; synthesis C22769)

The same icon reads differently in a black cell (language) than in a yellow cell (art): the grid is the grammar. The signs are "universally recognisable images from public places" -- that is the whole vocabulary, and it is deliberately shallow: no source assigns a fixed meaning to the sun, the moon, the coffee cup, or the bed, and no sign lexicon with definitions was found, so no prompt may invent one. What matters is the operation: lift, isolate, re-grid. A prompt built from this mechanism must show recognisable icons appearing one by one in grid cells, filling cell by cell, never as a free composition -- and the same icon must be allowed to read differently when its cell changes colour. (C22820, leg 2 M2, W02, W12, W14, W16; synthesis C22769)

**Takeaway:** lift a pictogram out of its home context, strip its surroundings, and set it alone in a grid cell -- the meaning comes from the cell and its neighbours, never from the original scene. (C22821)


## M3 -- Chart to city to pavilion: scale the same system up

Build one cosmology once, then render it at three scales with the same rules: as a chart on paper, as a map of a fictitious city, and as a walkable space. The rules do not change; only the scale and the material do. The chart is Untitled (Details from the Cosmology) (W08, 1982/83), 40 drawings isolating the individual elements of the cosmology in 40 steps, the first work in which Mullican showed different details within his cosmology. The city is the Computer Project (W09, 1986-1990), built with the Hollywood company Digital Productions: the cosmology re-created as a cityscape expanding over 18 square kilometres, divided into five districts associated with the five worlds -- Mullican was, as MACS notes, "subconsciously prefiguring the virtual spaces of present-day video games, whose landscapes are formed as the player advances through them." The pavilion is the M.I.T. Project (W10, 1990- ), a three-dimensional cosmology the visitor walks through, green zone to red semicircle, which the artist calls "a map, a way of organising information." (C22822, leg 2 M3; synthesis C22770)

The watchable behaviour is a zoom that never changes the rules. The piece zooms out from a diagram to a city plan to a walkable floor plan; the viewer -- or the camera -- moves through the same five zones at each scale, and objects placed in the chart reappear as buildings, then as rooms. A skeleton in the green compartment of the chart becomes a museum of skeletons in the green district of the city, then a room full of skeletons in the green zone of the pavilion. The mechanism is scale-invariance: the system is the work, and the scale is just the output format. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep one system constant across three scales and let the viewer feel the same rules operating at each. (C22823, leg 2 M3, W08-W10; synthesis C22770)

**Takeaway:** build one cosmology once and render it at three scales -- chart, city, pavilion -- with the same rules throughout; only the scale and the material change. (C22824)


## M4 -- Rubbing as index: the shadow is the image

Never draw the thing directly. Carve or assemble a relief, lay the surface over it, and rub. The image is the transferred shadow of the relief, not a depiction. The hand disappears; the result looks manufactured. Mullican has made rubbings since the early 1980s, working by frottage: a comic or image is projected onto masonite, cut with a knife to make a relief, canvas is laid over it, and the image is rubbed up with carbon and then oil paint sticks, with colours painted in under masking tape. The Dallas Project (W11, 1987) brings the whole cosmology together as around 400 black-and-white rubbing sheets; the New Edinburgh Encyclopedia Project (W13, 1991) re-makes an 1825 reference book as 449 magnesium relief plates, then re-makes the plates again as rubbings on paper -- the plates are the origin from which reproductions can continuously be made, the rubbings faithful copies and also translations of marks from one material to another; Untitled (Cosmology Signs) (W14, 1991) is the cosmology as a single large rubbing at near-human scale; Notating the Cosmology (W18, 1973-2008) pins 123 handwritten sheets to the wall, the notebooks themselves treated as a relief to be indexed. (C22825, leg 2 M4; synthesis C22771)

The watchable behaviour is transfer, not drawing. A relief pattern is built first, visible as geometry; then a surface passes over it and the image appears by transfer, flat and even, with no brushwork. Re-rubbing the same relief reproduces the same image with small variations -- the copy is never quite the original, and the difference is the evidence that a hand was there and then removed. The 2023 Little Nemo paintings were all made this way: a single page of a 1908 comic, projected, cut, rubbed, twelve chapters as separate rubbing paintings. A prompt built from this mechanism must show the relief first and the image second, and must never let a drawn line appear where a transferred one belongs. (C22826, leg 2 M4, W11, W13, W14, W18; synthesis C22771)

**Takeaway:** never draw the thing -- build a relief, lay a surface over it, and rub; the image is the transferred shadow, and the hand disappears into the transfer. (C22827)


## M5 -- Bulletin-board accumulation: pin everything, rank nothing

Collect without hierarchy. Drawings, photographs, objects, book designs, prints, notebook pages, and illustrations are pinned side by side on a plain wooden board. No centre, no climax, no cropping to fit a story; the board grows by addition. The bulletin board has been a format for Mullican since the early 1970s -- his display system and template for his ideas, representing his "encyclopedic hunger for images". At the de Young in 2019 a bulletin board from 1982 was shown alongside three further billboards holding household items (blue world), drawn studio views (yellow world), and comics (black world); at HangarBicocca in 2018 there were more than 120 bulletin boards. Notating the Cosmology (W18, 1973-2008) is the same habit in notebook form: 123 sheets pinned to the wall, showing the path and gradual complexification of his mental images over three decades. The Meaning of Things (W19, 2014) is the same habit in book form: over 600 paper collages, each sheet an image glued in the centre, framed with organic interlacings drawn by That Person. (C22828, leg 2 M5; synthesis C22772)

The watchable behaviour is scanning, not following. The screen fills as a pinboard; items arrive in no obvious order and stay where pinned; new items are added at the edges, never by rearranging the old ones. The viewer scans rather than follows, because there is no story to follow -- the board is an archive that refuses to become a narrative. A prompt built from this mechanism must let the accumulation itself be the spectacle: hundreds of small units, each complete, none privileged, the frame filling until the density is the point. (C22829, leg 2 M5, W07, W18, W19; synthesis C22772)

**Takeaway:** pin everything side by side on a plain board and rank nothing -- no centre, no climax, no story; the board grows by addition and the density is the point. (C22830)


## M6 -- Fictional-life biography: give a drawn figure a whole life

Invent a minimal figure, then catalogue its life from birth to death as a sequence of small dated events or states. Treat the fiction as data: the figure's pain, memories, and routines are recorded as seriously as real ones. Untitled (Birth to Death List) (W03, 1973) is a poetic description of an anonymous female figure from her birth to her death, in a long sequence of brief utterances -- the same "analyse a fictitious life" idea as Details from a Fictional Reality (W02, 1973), applied to a whole lifespan rather than a comic-strip moment. The Glen drawings (W04, 1974; the figure also appears as the inhabitant of a fictional studio first drawn in 1973) are a stick figure named Glen -- spelled "Glenn" by Mai 36, spelling unresolved -- drawn in different situations: pinching himself, remembering a ski trip, falling asleep on the floor. Mullican wanted to "create a model presence" with Glen and to understand the pain the figure felt inside the picture space. Glen stands for both Mullican and a generic artist. (C22831, leg 2 M6; synthesis C22773)

The watchable behaviour is accumulation without development. A stick figure appears in successive small scenes -- pinching itself, remembering a trip, falling asleep -- and a timeline runs birth to death while the scenes accumulate; the figure never gains detail, only more events. The fiction is handled as data: each event is dated, listed, filed, and the list is the biography. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the figure minimal and the events specific -- the pathos comes from the filing, not from the drawing. (C22832, leg 2 M6, W03, W04, W02; synthesis C22773)

**Takeaway:** invent a minimal figure and catalogue its whole life, birth to death, as a sequence of small dated events -- the figure never gains detail, only more events, and the list is the biography. (C22833)


## M7 -- That Person switch: a second hand takes over

The piece has two authors in one body. In the normal mode the artist files, charts, and measures; in the switched mode (trance) a second persona -- ageless, genderless, with its own tastes -- produces work in a different hand: looping calligraphy, profanity, childlike behaviour, obsessive repetition. The switch is visible, not hidden. The first experiment was Entering the Picture: Entrance to Hell (W05, 1976), Mullican seated facing a Piranesi painting into which he entered through thought, giving a real-time detailed description of it to his audience -- the first appearance of the hypnosis method and of That Person, "a sort of subjective double who experiences emotions and creates works different from Mullican's own." The performances under hypnosis (W06, 1978-2020) began at the Kitchen in 1978 and ran at Tate Modern in 2007, the Louvre in 2012, and elsewhere: yelling profanity, acting like a 5-year-old, crawling on the floor, making black ink drawings of numbers, symbols, and phrases on bedsheets hung in a maze. Learning from That Person's Work (W16, 2005) is the labyrinth of those sheets: each panel contains nine patterns matching the themes That Person is interested in, such as love, beauty, work, or truth. The Meaning of Things (W19, 2014) lets That Person reveal itself through the artist's body in trance, framing each collage with organic interlacings; Untitled (Yellow Monster) (W20, 2018) overlays internet images with signs in That Person's hand. (C22834, leg 2 M7; synthesis C22774)

The watchable behaviour is an abrupt change of hand. The drawing style changes mid-piece -- tidy grids give way to looping organic interlacing, repeated phrases ("I love to work for Truth and Beauty"), numbers and symbols in a different hand -- then the tidy mode returns. The two modes alternate, never blend. That Person belongs to the red world, the subjective: the switch is the moment the filing system hands over to the person being filed. A prompt built from this mechanism must make the switch visible and total -- a different hand, a different tempo, a different vocabulary -- and must never let the two modes merge into a compromise style. (C22835, leg 2 M7, W05, W06, W16, W19, W20; synthesis C22774)

**Takeaway:** give the piece two authors in one body -- a tidy filing hand and a trance hand -- and let the switch between them be visible and total; the two modes alternate, never blend. (C22836)


## M8 -- Encyclopedic re-copy: re-make an existing book plate by plate

Choose a real reference book and reproduce its entire contents in a new material, one plate at a time, in the book's own order. The copy is faithful and also a translation: marks move from print to relief to rubbing, changing material at each step. Untitled (New Edinburgh Encyclopedia Project) (W13, 1991) is the pure case: 449 magnesium relief plates carrying the contents of the 1825 "New Edinburgh Encyclopedia", plus the rubbings on paper made from them, the plates standing propped on narrow shelves in long parallel rows that weave alphabetically up and down. Notating the Cosmology (W18, 1973-2008) is the same operation applied to the artist's own notebooks: 123 sheets, hand-written, pinned to the wall, the path of a mental image from its premises to its modelling. The Dallas Project (W11, 1987) is the same operation applied to the cosmology itself: around 400 rubbing sheets bringing the entire system together, the Cube walls entirely covered with rubbings produced from 1984 onwards. (C22837, leg 2 M8; synthesis C22775)

The watchable behaviour is accumulation in strict order. Pages and plates appear row by row, shelf by shelf; each plate is shown first as relief, then as its rubbed copy; the accumulation itself -- hundreds of near-identical units -- is the spectacle, not any single plate. The order is the book's order (alphabetical, in W13), not the artist's: the system being copied supplies the sequence, and the artist supplies only the labour of translation. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the order strict and the units uniform, and must let the count itself -- 449 plates, 400 sheets, 123 pages -- do the work of awe. (C22838, leg 2 M8, W13, W18, W11; synthesis C22775)

**Takeaway:** take a real reference book and re-make its entire contents plate by plate in a new material, in the book's own order -- the copy is faithful and a translation at once, and the count is the spectacle. (C22839)


## M9 -- Framed versus unframed flip

The same image belongs to the blue world while unframed (everyday, unperceived) and to the yellow world once framed by consciousness or art. Framing is an act, not a border: applying a sign or a frame to an image moves it between worlds. The Computer Project (W09, 1986-1990) builds the flip into a city: its five districts are the framed and unframed worlds as neighbourhoods. Untitled (Experiment with Light) (W15, 2001) pursues the same question with positive films in five diptychs, patterns of cosmology, crystals, and newspaper cuttings on shelves. Untitled (Yellow Monster) (W20, 2018) takes images from the internet and overlays them in oil pastel with signs -- notably the symbol representing the "unframed" world -- so that the signs operate "like an abstract, symbolic frame of reference": the internet image is exposed through the sign, and the sign frames it. Details from a Fictional Reality (W02, 1973) does the same thing in reverse: comic cutouts removed from their storyline, unframed from the narrative that used to hold them. (C22840, leg 2 M9; synthesis C22776)

The watchable behaviour is a jump, not a blend. An everyday image -- a coffee cup, a street scene, an internet picture -- sits plain; a sign or frame is drawn over it and the image jumps from blue to yellow -- same pixels, new world. The flip is the event. Nothing in the image changes except its status, and the status change is rendered as a colour change: the whole frame re-files itself in front of the viewer. A prompt built from this mechanism must make the flip instantaneous and total -- one frame blue, the next frame yellow -- and must never let the image drift through a gradient between worlds. (C22841, leg 2 M9, W09, W15, W20, W02; synthesis C22776)

**Takeaway:** framing is an act, not a border -- apply a sign or a frame to an everyday image and it jumps from the blue world to the yellow world, same pixels, new category. (C22842)


## M10 -- Life-cycle loop: birth and death meet at one point

Lay the cosmology out as a circle, not a line. Birth and death -- in the material and the spiritual sense -- culminate at the same point, so the cycle repeats instead of ending. The model describes existence from before birth to after death and then starts again. The cosmology model itself (W08, 1982/83) describes human existence from before birth to after death, and the notebooks of Notating the Cosmology (W18, 1973-2008) model the same span over 35 years of entries. The circular pattern is corroborated by the later rubbing Untitled (Between Chapters) (2022), a black-and-white sketchbook rubbing of the full circular cosmology. The first cosmology of 1973 is described only in general terms in the sources read -- its internal structure (named stages, colours) was not spelled out anywhere read end to end -- so this mechanism rests on the circular-pattern description and the before-birth-to-after-death summary, not on a stage-by-stage chart; do not print a stage list the sources never gave. (C22843, leg 2 M10; synthesis C22777)

The watchable behaviour is a return, not an ending. Events travel around a circular chart and return to their starting point; the point where the loop closes is marked -- birth and death coincide there -- and the animation loops seamlessly because the system is a circle, not a timeline with an end. The green world (material, death, hell) and the red world (subjective, paradise) meet at that point, which is why the IAC text can call green death and red paradise without contradiction: they are the two ends of the same circle. A prompt built from this mechanism must be a loop by construction -- the last frame must be the first frame -- and the closing point must be marked, not smoothed over. (C22844, leg 2 M10, W08, W18; synthesis C22777)

**Takeaway:** lay the cosmology out as a circle, not a line -- birth and death meet at one point, the cycle repeats instead of ending, and the last frame is the first frame. (C22845)


## The aesthetic logic: a model of the universe you can walk through

Mullican's career is one system run for fifty years: take the whole universe, sort it into five categories, give each category a colour, give everything in it a sign, and then render the system at every scale a human can meet it -- a chart on paper, a city on a screen, a pavilion you walk through, a banner on a facade, a rubbing you can hold. The artist's contribution is not the image; it is the filing. The signs are borrowed from public places, the books are other people's encyclopedias, the comics are other people's strips, the internet images are other people's pictures. What is his is the system that files them, and the hand that does the filing -- two hands, in fact, his own and That Person's. (C22846, leg 2 mechanisms M1-M10; synthesis C22768-C22777)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, colour is a category, not a decoration: green is material, blue is everyday, yellow is framed, black is language, red is subjective, and nothing is allowed a colour it has not earned by being filed there. Second, the copy is the work: rubbings, re-copied encyclopedias, re-drawn comics, re-filed notebooks -- the original is never shown, only its transferred shadow, and the transfer is where the hand disappears. Third, the system is a circle: birth and death meet at one point, the chart becomes a city becomes a pavilion becomes a chart again, and the viewer is filed along with everything else -- at HangarBicocca the visitor walked through the five worlds the way a sign walks through a grid. The teacher burnt his paintings and replaced the hand with a rule; the student Baldessari named first took the rule further than anyone: he replaced the picture with a filing system, and then filed the universe in it. (C22847, leg 2 M1, M4, M10; synthesis C22768-C22777)

The evolution is the proof. 1972-73: light and a stick figure -- Light Patterns (W01) investigates perception through light alone, Glen (W04) is given a life, and a picture is claimed to be a real place you can enter. 1973-76: the first cosmology and the fictional life -- Birth to Death List (W03), Details from a Fictional Reality (W02), and the first entry into a picture (W05). 1978 onward: the second author -- hypnosis, That Person, the performances (W06) and the labyrinth of sheets (W16). 1982-83: the system is drawn -- Details from the Cosmology (W08) isolates the elements in 40 steps, and the Five Worlds arrive. 1984-1991: the system is rubbed and re-copied -- Dallas (W11), the Bath Banner (W12), the New Edinburgh Encyclopedia (W13), Cosmology Signs (W14). 1986-1990: the system is built -- the Computer Project city (W09), then the walk-in pavilion (W10). 2001-2018: the system is applied to everything, including the internet -- Experiment with Light (W15), The Meaning of Things (W19), Yellow Monster (W20). What stays constant is the unit: one sign, one colour, one world. What changes is the scale: a sheet, a banner, a city, a pavilion, a life. (C22848, leg 2 W01-W20 evolution; synthesis C22762)
