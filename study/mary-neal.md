# Mary Neal: Study Notes

**Site:** Mary Neal, born Clara Sophia Neal, b. Tuesday 5 June 1860, Edgbaston, Birmingham; d. Thursday 22 June 1944, Green Bushes, 31 St Flora Road, Littlehampton, Sussex, age 84. Co-founder with Emmeline Pethick of the Esperance Club (autumn 1895); Cecil Sharp's partner in the folk revival from 1905 until the parting of January/February 1908, then his rival; publisher of the Esperance Morris Books (1910, 1912); CBE 1937. The organiser who did not dance, the woman whose working girls carried the morris out of Headington Quarry into schools across England -- and the co-founder the institution Sharp built then wrote out of its own history for seventy years. [C35039]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Peter Douglas Kennedy study: that study is about the son who grew up inside Sharp's institution and carried its songs out onto tape, onto radio, onto television, into a catalogue of his own making. This one is about the woman who built the first stage he was born onto -- or rather, the woman who built the first stage, and then lost it. Neal is the origin story of the institution Peter inherited through both his parents: his father Douglas learned the dances through Sharp's Chelsea School and the Karpeles sisters' Folk Dance Club, and the dances themselves reached London because Neal went to Oxford in October 1905, hired William Kimber, and taught her Esperance girls to teach everyone else. Where Peter's study is about keeping sound, this one is about keeping people moving: the club evening, the demonstration, the chain of teachers, the dance passed from body to body until it no longer resembled what the old man taught -- and the quarrel over whether that drift was life or decay. [C35040]

## 1. Edgbaston, 5 June 1860: Clara Sophia Neal

Mary Neal was born Clara Sophia Neal on Tuesday 5 June 1860 in Edgbaston, Birmingham, the eldest daughter of David Neal, a moderately prosperous Birmingham button manufacturer; her mother's name is not established in any source read. She was educated at Birmingham Ladies' College. Spartacus alone says she later became "a student of economics" -- no institution or date is given, and no other source read confirms it, so it is carried as unverified. Emmeline Pethick-Lawrence's memoir, quoted on the Mary Neal Project site, says she was sent to Bournemouth because she had "rapid consumption of the lungs" and that her brother had died of it soon after -- single-sourced and undated, presumably before her London move. Her intellectual formation is named consistently: Keir Hardie and Edward Carpenter, Christian Socialism, William Morris and Walt Whitman; she lectured at the Nottingham Labour Church (Spartacus only). What is set in this beginning is not a folk life but a settlement life: the girl who will run the revival's first schoolroom is first a sickly manufacturer's daughter who decides the beautiful things of life belong to working girls.

**Takeaway:** She starts as a settlement worker, not a collector. The folk revival lands on a social project that already exists -- and that is why it spreads like one. [C35043]

## 2. Sister Mary: the Mission, 1888-1895

In 1888 -- Judge via Wikipedia; Spartacus says 1890, and the discrepancy is unresolved, though Judge is the scholarly source and 1888 is preferred -- Neal began voluntary social work with the West London Methodist Mission of Hugh Price Hughes, helping the poor of Soho, Fitzrovia and Marylebone. She took the name "Sister Mary", ran a Club for Working Girls at the mission's Cleveland Hall, and wrote for the Mission Magazine. In 1891 she met Emmeline Pethick at the Mission, and the two became lifelong friends and collaborators; Pethick's verdict, quoted everywhere, is that Neal had "a strong sense of humour and a profound aversion from unreality; she had also a sharp tongue" -- and was "a challenging person who provoked others to violent reactions of like and dislike". Neal on the Girls' Club, quoted in Wikipedia from Judge: "No words can express the passionate longing which I have to bring some of the beautiful things of life within easy reach of the girls who earn their living by the sweat of their brow..." The Mission gave her the method she never abandoned: find the living source, put it in a room with working girls, and let them make it their own.

**Takeaway:** The Mission is the first draft of the revival. The room, the girls, the beautiful things within reach -- the folk dance is only the last thing put into it. [C35044]

## 3. The Esperance Club, autumn 1895

In the autumn of 1895 Neal and Pethick left the Mission to set up their own Esperance Club for girls in Cumberland Market, Somers Town -- "Esperance" is hope -- to escape the Mission's institutional constraints and experiment with dance and drama. Alongside it they started Maison Esperance, a tailoring and dressmaking establishment on Wigmore Street providing employment on decent wages, a co-operative run by the girls themselves; the Orlando entry dates Maison Esperance to 1897, and that 1897 date is sometimes misattached to the Club itself, so the two dates must be kept apart -- Club 1895, business 1897. In 1900, Spartacus alone says, they developed a hostel at Littlehampton for working girls' holidays -- the Littlehampton connection is long-standing, not a retirement move, though the 1900 date is single-sourced. The partnership with Emmeline broke up in practice when she married Frederick Lawrence in 1901, but they remained close for life, and Neal ended her life living with the Pethick-Lawrences. By 1905 the Club had a musical director, Herbert MacIlwaine, running a singing class, a programme of music, dance and drama, and a room open four evenings a week with a Friday Bible class. This is the platform the folk revival lands on: not a concert hall, not a university, but a working girls' club with a sewing room attached.

**Takeaway:** The Club is the machine and the dance is the fuel. She does not build a folk society; she builds a room where working girls are allowed to be beautiful -- and the revival walks in through its door. [C35045]

## 4. Suffrage: the parallel thread

Neal's suffrage work runs alongside the folk work and is inseparable from it -- the same network of women staffed both. In 1906 Neal and Emmeline Pethick-Lawrence attended the meeting at Sylvia Pankhurst's Chelsea home where the Women's Social and Political Union was established; Neal joined and later sat on its national committee. She refused to take part in militant action -- unlike Emmeline, who was imprisoned six times between 1906 and 1912, and the six imprisonments are Emmeline's, not Mary's, and must not be attributed to her -- and earned money writing for Votes for Women. On 10 September 1907 she was present when Emmeline Pankhurst tore up the WSPU constitution and was appointed to the new committee. In October 1908 she invited Lady Constance Lytton along to the Littlehampton hostel, and Lytton was persuaded to join the suffrage movement after days there -- a scene later dramatised in the BBC's Shoulder to Shoulder (1974). In 1912, after Christabel Pankhurst's proposed arson campaign and the Pethick-Lawrences' expulsion from the WSPU, Neal left the WSPU too and became a special commissioner for Votes for Women. On 6 February 1914 she became a founding member of the United Suffragists, alongside Evelyn Sharp, Lena Ashwell, George Lansbury and Laurence Housman. She was also a member of the Tax Resistance League (Spartacus only). The suffrage thread matters to the folk story because it is one of the causes of the break: Sharp distanced himself from the increasingly militant campaign, and MacIlwaine stepped down as the Club's Musical Director in 1908 explicitly over suffrage.

**Takeaway:** The same women, the same rooms, the same fight. The folk revival and the suffrage movement share a network -- and the break with Sharp is political as well as artistic. [C35046]

## 5. The meeting: 29 July 1905

On Saturday 29 July 1905, at an Esperance Club party, Neal asked MacIlwaine where English songs could be found for the girls. He had read a Morning Post interview with Cecil Sharp about his Somerset song-collecting the day before and suggested she speak to him. Sharp's own note records what followed: "Sept. 1905 Miss Neal called upon me in consequence of above interview. Gave her 1st vol. and a fortnight later went to Club. Oct. 1905 Suggested Morris dances. Had Kimber up." The songs electrified the club -- Neal: "It was as if the club had gone mad, they were perfectly intoxicated with the music" -- and then she asked Sharp for dances to go with the songs. Wikipedia, citing Judge, places the meeting at the Hampstead Conservatoire, where Sharp had been Principal; but the Mary Neal Project archive says Sharp had resigned as Principal by the time she went to see him, and Sharp's note gives no venue, so the study keeps "met in 1905" and drops the venue. The substance is not in dispute: Sharp had been collecting folk songs for two years and wanted them taught in schools; Neal wanted beautiful things within reach of working girls; and in the first couple of years, as Tradfolk puts it, "Mary Neal was seen, rightly, as the principal partner... Sharp was the supporting actor" -- hers were the dancers, the audiences and the organisation, his the songs, the lectures and the scholarly apparatus.

**Takeaway:** She asks for songs and gets a movement. The partnership works because each of them has what the other lacks -- her room and her girls, his notebook and his tunes. [C35047]

## 6. Kimber and the Headington dances

Sharp remembered meeting the Headington Quarry Morris dancing out of season on Boxing Day 1899 at Sandfield Cottage, and had noted five tunes from their musician William Kimber the next day. Headington had not performed between 1888 and 1899; Kimber, born 1872, had learned the dances from his father in the gap, and the side had been re-formed only when the antiquarian Percy Manning paid for a performance for a lecture in March 1899 -- so the thing salvaged was already a revival, not an unbroken survival, and any piece that presents the dance as timelessly continuous is repeating the myth, not the history. Neal went to Oxford on 2 October 1905, settled financial terms with Kimber, and he and his cousin Richard Kimber came to London to teach the Esperance girls in person. The first songs learned were "The Seeds of Love", "Mowing the Barley", "Lord Rendal" and "Blow Away the Morning Dew" (Neal's autobiography, Extract 40). On Friday 15 December 1905 the Esperance Christmas party at the Passmore Edwards Settlement was the pivotal performance: the girls danced the Headington morris in public for the first time, and the guests urged a public restaging. MacIlwaine then notated the dance steps from the Esperance dancer Florrie Warren, and Sharp notated the music -- the steps of Neal's girls becoming the text of Sharp's book.

**Takeaway:** The revival starts with a train ride and a handshake. An old man who has not danced for ten years teaches working girls in a London club -- and the dance crosses a ten-year gap on their feet. [C35048]

## 7. An English Pastoral: the teaching explosion, 1906

The same dances moved along an audience gradient in four months: a private display on 15 February 1906 at the Esperance Club, with Labour MP Keir Hardie in the audience, then the first public performance, "An English Pastoral", at Small Queen's Hall, Portland Place, with a lecture by Sharp -- dated 13 April 1906 by the Mary Neal Project archive and 3 April 1906 by Schofield, a split neither side of this run resolves. By the end of 1906 the girls were teaching in six counties -- Somerset, Devon, Derby, Monmouth, Norfolk and Surrey -- and six London clubs, and within a year, in Atherton's phrase, "nearly every county in England". Neal invited over thirty traditional dancers to London to teach the girls, who then taught others. The division of labour held: Neal supplied the organisation, the dancers, the audiences and the teaching network; Sharp supplied the songs, the lectures and the scholarly apparatus; MacIlwaine notated the steps from Florrie Warren; Sharp notated the music. Both saw the material "not simply as a nostalgic entertainment, but as an instrument for good" (Judge, quoted in Tradfolk). The dance did not change across that year; the room did -- and with it the stakes: respectability, publicity, money, and the question of who controlled it.

**Takeaway:** A club entertainment becomes a national pedagogy in a year. The mechanism is the chain: girls learn from old men, then teach, then their pupils teach -- and drift is the price and the proof of life. [C35049]

## 8. The Morris Book and Set to Music, 1907

In 1907 Sharp and MacIlwaine published The Morris Book Part I (Novello), dedicated to the Esperance Club, acknowledging Florrie Warren for the dance steps and Neal for "organisation reach" -- the month is split, April per the Project archive against July per the Morris Ring, and a July 1906 date in Lucy Neal's Sheffield paper is almost certainly an error, so the study prints the year and carries the month as unresolved. The Morris Book is the publication of Neal's dancers' repertoire, not her book, and must not be catalogued as hers. Her own publication of the year was Set to Music, a 26-page fundraising pamphlet issued by the Association for the Revival and Practice of Folk Music, dedicated to Sharp in 1907 and published in 1908 -- dedication date and publication date differ and must not be conflated. Sharp said the dances had "'given wings' to the songs". The two books of 1907 already contain the whole quarrel in miniature: his book fixes the dance in notation and dedicates it to her girls; her pamphlet sets the songs to music and dedicates it to him; each of them is thanking the other for the thing the other thinks they own.

**Takeaway:** Two books, two dedications, one quarrel already written. His fixes the dance on paper; hers sets the song in the room -- and the question of which one is the revival has not been settled yet. [C35050]

## 9. The break: Goupil Gallery, 14 November 1907

The break was not a single event in November 1907 but a dated sequence, and the brief's clean date must be corrected. On Wednesday 13 November 1907 Punch printed an article promoting the next day's conference, with a cartoon -- this "provokes Sharp's first pulling away from Esperance Club activities" (Project archive). On Thursday 14 November 1907 the conference met at the Goupil Gallery, chaired by Lord Neville Lytton, to found a national folk-music society; Neal insisted on a private society whose members would have no right to appoint the executive, and no experts, and Mrs Gomme and Sharp refused -- Sharp's own note: "Meeting therefore abortive." In January or February 1908 Neal formed her private Association for the Revival and Practice of Folk Music, and Sharp's note is explicit: "This marked the parting of the ways; but for 6 months before matters were considerably strained." On 11 April 1908 she wrote in the Saturday Review that she had "no intention of doing collecting work done so well by Cecil Sharp" -- a public de-escalation that did not hold. On Tuesday 10 November 1908 Sharp wrote to Lucy Broadwood formally disassociating himself from the Association -- a full year after Goupil, and the formal break. The public press war then ran through 1909 and 1910: Sharp attacked Neal in the Morning Post on 1 April 1910; she replied in Vanity Fair on 14 April 1910, calling his approach "the blighting touch of the pedant"; on 5 May 1910 she wrote in the Morning Post, "I recognise no expert in Morris Dancing"; on 8 November 1910 she attempted reconciliation by letter, calling the early years of the revival the "happiest years of [her] life" -- and no reconciliation followed. Her own summary, quoted in Schofield (1986): "In 1907 Mr Cecil Sharp and I disagreed over the constitution of a committee, and from that date have worked on entirely separate lines."

**Takeaway:** November 1907 is the visible rupture, not the break. The conference collapses in a day; the partnership takes three years to finish dying -- and the dates matter, because the myth of a single clean break hides how long both of them kept trying. [C35051]

## 10. The causes: joy against accuracy

The causes are agreed in substance and differ only in emphasis. Standards against joy: Sharp insisted on correct traditional practice and graceful dancing, and called the Esperance dancers "too boisterous and 'hoydenish'"; Neal regarded his approach as "the blighting touch of the pedant and the expert" and valued the songs and dances as amusement and happiness for her girls. Transmission: her model was chain-teaching -- girls learn from traditional dancers, then teach others, who teach more, even if the dance drifted from the original -- against his fixed, notated yardstick of authenticity. Authority and credit: Sharp felt written out of the picture and Neal usurping his role as "morris authority", while Neal was in fact leading the dancers and the teachers. Suffrage: Sharp distanced himself from the increasingly militant campaign, and MacIlwaine's departure as Club Musical Director in 1908 was explicitly over suffrage. Art against folk: by 1912 Neal called Sharp's displays "beautiful, graceful and charming" but "art dancing, not folk dancing -- far removed from what I saw at Bampton", while Sharp considered that Neal could "not see further than philanthropy" and did not appreciate the revival as an "artistic movement". The Times put it as "the spirit of joy" against "the spirit of accuracy". Douglas Kennedy, Sharp's successor, later reframed it as "content versus form" and said he had "seen the pendulum swing from side to side" ever since. The story is told two ways -- as an ideological disagreement about authenticity against participation (Judge's even-handed account), and as a gendered power struggle in which Sharp and the EFDS wrote Neal out (Boyes, Sutcliffe, Atherton) -- and both readings cite the same dated facts; the dispute is over motive and blame, not over the timeline, so this study carries both and chooses neither silently.

**Takeaway:** The quarrel is a dial, not a verdict. Joy against accuracy, drift against the yardstick, the girls against the experts -- and the revival has been turning that dial ever since, through Kennedy's pendulum to the present day. [C35052]

## 11. The Esperance Morris Books, 1910 and 1912

Neal answered Sharp's book with her own. Part I, The Esperance Morris Book: A Manual of Morris Dances, Folk-Songs and Singing Games (J. Curwen, 1910; Edition 5694; five shillings; third edition, copyright 1910), carries 12 morris dances, 7 folk-songs and 7 singing games, an introduction by E. V. Lucas, and a dedication to Emmeline and Frederick Pethick-Lawrence, "true and unfailing friends of The Esperance Club... for Old Sake's Sake" -- the date is split, 1910 against 1911 (Dowling; Project archive), and the study adopts the title-page copyright of 1910. Part II (1912; edited by Neal; notes and steps written by Clive Carey; music collected and arranged by Geoffrey Toye and Carey; dedicated "to the traditional dancers of England, in whose memory there still linger the dances and melodies of long ago") carries 17 dances and 6 sea shanties, the bulk from Headington with two from Ilmington, plus the Sussex dances and the Flamborough sword dance -- its date is split three ways, 1912 against 1911 against 1910, and the study adopts the title-page copyright of 1912. The contents are the map of her method: Headington first (Bean Setting, Rodney, Blue-Eyed Stranger, Old Mother Oxford), then Ilmington (Bumpus o' Stretton, The Lively Jig), then the Sussex stick dance "Over the Sticks" and the Flamborough sword dance of the Plough Stots. Two conflicts must be carried, not settled: the Morris Ring bibliography says all 12 Part I dances are "from Abingdon" while the Abingdon history says Part I "included three Abingdon dances" and the scan itself assigns no village per dance, so the study does not assert either; and the spellings differ between the scan ("Shepherd's Hay", "Rigs o' Marlow", "Jockey to the Fair") and the bibliography ("Shepherd's Aye", "The rakes of mallow"), with the scan adopted as the better witness.

**Takeaway:** Her books are the other yardstick. Same dances, same villages, a different claim: the book as a souvenir of bodies that already know, not a touchstone for bodies that must be corrected. [C35053]

## 12. The Guild, the curriculum, and the American tour

In the rival years Neal built a parallel infrastructure. The Association for the Revival and Practice of Folk Music was founded in January or February 1908; the Esperance Guild of Morris Dancers was formed in March 1910, open to all men and women who wanted to dance the morris, and lapsed in 1914; in 1911 the Board of Education approved Neal's curriculum for schools, and the Part II introduction records that Esperance instructors were "the only ones who had been directly taught by the traditional dancers". On Saturday 10 December 1910 -- the Morris Matters account says 3 December, aboard the SS Arabic, a split this run does not resolve -- Neal and Florrie Warren sailed for a lecture-demonstration tour of the United States, New York and Boston. They arrived to find their engagements cancelled: a friend of Sharp's, presumably Elizabeth Burchenal, had told the societies that the English education authorities had "thrown [them] over" in favour of Sharp -- the attribution is explicitly presumptive in the source and is carried as such. They rebooked, the New York Times praised the performance, and the tour was judged a great success; Warren met her future husband Arthur Brown there, and they married on 14 February 1912. Neal's letter to Carey of 30 December 1911, written from America, gives the tour's bitterness in one line: "Cecil Sharp has done his best to poison people's minds over here. But we are here and he is not!" Wikipedia spans the trip "between 1910 and early 1912"; no source read dates the return independently, so the study keeps the span attributed and does not pin the return.

**Takeaway:** She loses the institution and keeps the audience. The Guild, the curriculum, the Atlantic crossing -- the rival years are not a retreat but a second organisation, built in the open, with the same girls. [C35054]

## 13. The rival years: EFDS, Earl's Court, the Globe

In December 1911 Sharp constituted the English Folk Dance Society without Neal, and the institutional race was decided in his favour -- Helen Karpeles was elected its Honorary Secretary at the founding, and Maud took the role over in 1922 and held it to 1930, the correction carried from #805. The rivalry was also fought over platforms. In January 1911 Blanche Payling, aged eighteen, went alone to Thaxted, invited by Miriam and Conrad Noel, to teach children from Lee's sweet factory, three classes a day; that summer sixty children danced for the Thaxted Flower Show on Walnut Tree Meadow. In 1912 Neal staged Shakespeare's England at Earl's Court, described as "directing the spirit of the movement", with 4,000 addresses of followers to her name; Vaughan Williams appeared at a fancy-dress party dressed as Neal, under the slogan "power before accuracy" -- anecdotal, Project archive only, and carried as such. In April 1913 Neal and Clive Carey staged Mayday Revels at the Globe Theatre, London, and Votes for Women noted "the grace of male dancers". In 1913 she travelled to Ireland to help families affected by the Dublin lock-out (Project archive only). Meanwhile Sharp's Morris Book second edition of 1912 was re-notated directly from Kimber, cutting out the Esperance girls -- the book as an instrument of the quarrel, the girls written out of the text as they were written out of the institution.

**Takeaway:** Two movements, one repertoire, fought over schoolrooms and stages. She has the crowds and the curriculum; he has the Society -- and the Society is what survives. [C35055]

## 14. English Folk-Song and Dance, 1915

Her last book is the one post-break tie she kept with the first-revival establishment. English Folk-Song and Dance (Frank Kidson and Mary Neal, Cambridge University Press, 1915) is two halves: Part I, "English Folk-Song", by Kidson (pp. 3-86); Part II, "English Folk-Dance", by Neal (pp. 97-173) -- the division is stated in the book itself. Her chapters run from the Morris dance today through tunes, musical instruments, dress, extra characters, the sword dance, the Furry Dance, the country dance, the present-day revival, to conclusions. Bampton is her standard throughout: "an unbroken tradition going back some hundreds of years", slow dignified processional, circles and half-circles -- the village against which she measured Sharp's displays and found them "art dancing". The Abingdon horn dance is here too, the Mayor of the Morris with his bull's head and horns on St John the Baptist's Eve, and the Kirtlington "Lady" and lamb ceremonies. Her theory is also on the record: the Morris derives from sun-worship and primitive religious ceremonial, via Frazer's Golden Bough, with a Celtic derivation of "Mor" (great) and "uasal" (noble) -- her published theory, recorded here as content, not endorsed. No published book by Neal after 1915 was found in any source checked; the autobiography is the only later writing, and it looks back at 1905-14 rather than recording new work.

**Takeaway:** The book is her last word in the quarrel, written with Kidson, not Sharp. The dance half of a song book -- the organiser insisting, in print, that the dance is half the subject, not the illustration. [C35056]

## 15. War, Gardiner, and the self-blame, 1914-1925

With war coming, Esperance activity waned, the Guild lapsed in 1914, and Neal put her energies into the war effort -- the later record names the Pensions Office on the Isle of Dogs, though the detail is thinly sourced and no specific wartime role is well documented. After 1918 she recognised that, "in the face of Sharp's supremacy, it would be impossible to resume her folk dance role" (Tradfolk), and her contacts with folk dance became only occasional. Sharp died on 23 June 1924; Neal outlived him by twenty years. In 1922 -- not 1924, as Judge dated it before Chris Bearman corrected the dating from her letters -- she came into contact with the young rural revivalist Rolf Gardiner; her letter to him dated "Aug. 1" [1922] ends "I have not yet lost the happy feeling your visit gave me -- please come again". Gardiner convinced her that morris was an essentially masculine ceremonial form, and she later wrote that "by putting women on to this masculine rhythm I had quite innocently and ignorantly broken a law of cosmic ritual, and stirred up disharmony... I believe now that this misuse of the morris dance was the reason for the bitter estrangement between my colleagues and myself" (quoted in Dowling, from her autobiography). Gardiner later sympathised with Nazism, specifically its pro-ruralist policies, and took part in inter-war far-right politics -- context for the source of her self-blame, not a judgement on Neal herself. In 1925 she and Lily Montagu bought the house in Littlehampton and called it Green Bushes.

**Takeaway:** The eclipse is not a decline in energy but a change of stage. The folk revival was the 1905-1914 vehicle for a settlement-welfare project that began in 1888 -- and the saddest line in her life is the one where she blames herself, on a younger man's theory, for a quarrel that was never hers to lose. [C35057]

## 16. Later life: Green Bushes, the bench, the CBE

Green Bushes, 31 St Flora Road, Littlehampton, bought in 1925 with Lily Montagu, became a holiday home for girls -- a different, later house than the Littlehampton hostel of 1900, and the two must not be conflated. In 1934 the Morris Ring was founded and Neal was approached by Francis Fryer for advice on the morris, a small late recognition from the men's morris world; in 1938 she was invited to the Abingdon Morris Ring meeting as recognition of her work. She became a justice of the peace in West Sussex in 1934, dealing with child delinquency, and was a member of the Howard League for Penal Reform -- both Wikipedia-only, single-source, and carried as such; the stronger claim that she was "the first female magistrate on a county bench" is dropped, since women became justices of the peace after the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act 1919 and the first woman JP sat in 1922. In 1937 she was appointed CBE for services to the revival of folk songs and dances -- the exact honours date not established -- and in the same year she completed her autobiography, As a Tale That Is Told, unpublished in her lifetime: the manuscript was kept in a grey-black lever-arch file, guarded after her death by Esther Knowles, a former Esperance girl who had become the Pethick-Lawrences' secretary, and her niece Nita Needham, then handed to her great-great-niece Lucy Neal in 1993 and published online in 2014 (VWML record MN/1). In 1940 she left Littlehampton because of bombing raids along the coast and moved to live with the Pethick-Lawrences at Fourways, Peaslake, Surrey. On 12 October 1942 she wrote to Clive Carey about working for the Gallup Survey -- one of the last documented facts of her life, and the same fieldwork instinct as the 1905 trip to Headington, minus the folk label. She died on Thursday 22 June 1944 at Green Bushes, aged 84; the cause of death and burial place are not established in any source read. Emmeline Pethick-Lawrence wrote the eulogy in June 1944, held in the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library and known only in summary here: it notes that on the day after her death she had been due to "confer with promoters of new plans for people's holidays enriched with the delights of drama and folk-dancing" -- still planning, at eighty-four, the holidays with folk dancing in them.

**Takeaway:** Thirty years after the break, the establishment finally honours her -- and she dies planning the next holiday camp. The welfare project outlives the folk quarrel by decades, because it was never really about the quarrel. [C35058]

## 17. Learn by watching: the demonstration team

Neal's "intuitive method" (Knevett) is the first mechanism and the one everything else hangs on: a traditional performer demonstrates -- rhythm, style, fluency, combined action -- and learners watch and copy the whole moving body, not a description of it. Kimber and his cousin Richard came to London in October 1905 and taught the girls in person; MacIlwaine then formulated the steps from Florrie Warren's dancing. Knowledge lives in a body in a room, not in a text, and the teaching is itself a performance: the teacher never stops moving, and the learner's job is to join the movement, not to memorise it. Sharp's method runs the other way -- collect from elderly dancers, notate, publish -- and the two methods produce two different kinds of knowledge: one you can dance, one you can read.

**Watchable translation:** One figure dances a phrase; the others watch, then join in one by one, each a beat late and slightly wrong, converging over repetitions. The "teacher" never stops moving. No text, no diagram -- the pattern is only ever shown, never told. A viewer should be able to see the moment a watcher becomes a dancer. **Takeaway:** The demonstration is the textbook. Show the dance and the teaching is done -- write it down and you have a different thing. [C35059]

## 18. Copy-with-drift: the telephone chain

Neal's stated method was that girls learn from traditional dancers, "then for the girls to teach others, and for them, in turn, to teach yet more dancers, even if the resulting dances, several stages down the line, bore little resemblance to what the traditional dancer had originally taught" (Tradfolk). Drift is not failure; it is the method working. By the end of 1906 the girls were teaching in six counties and six London clubs; within a year, in Atherton's phrase, "nearly every county in England". The chain is the point: each generation is both pupil and teacher, and the dance survives by changing hands, not by being preserved. Sharp's horror at the drift -- the girls "taught villainously badly", in his July 1909 note -- is the same fact seen from the other side: what he calls corruption, she calls transmission.

**Watchable translation:** A phrase is passed down a line of six dancers; each copy inherits the previous dancer's version with a small mutation -- a step higher, a turn earlier, a handkerchief flick added. After four generations the dance is visibly a cousin, not a clone. Show the lineage as a branching tree, not a single line -- drift compounds and forks. **Takeaway:** Drift is the method, not the error. A dance that never changes hands dies; a dance that changes hands changes -- and that is how it lives. [C35060]

## 19. The six-dancer set and the one musician

Most Cotswold dances use a rectangular set of six dancers, plus a musician and often a Fool; figures are floor patterns -- lines forward and back, weaving, circles -- executed in unison, and the set is a machine with six moving parts in fixed relative positions. The tune is the timebase: one musician, Kimber on concertina on Boxing Day 1899, playing while six men danced "Laudnum Bunches", and the dance follows the tune, not the other way round. Figures are "a sequence of steps danced to a phrase of music", and the musician owns the tempo -- speed up and the set speeds up, stop and the set freezes mid-figure. The number six is a visible constraint, not a decoration: a dancer leaving the set breaks it, and a seventh cannot join without re-forming it.

**Watchable translation:** Six dots in two rows of three. The whole piece is the geometry deforming: rows cross, diagonals swap, the rectangle becomes a circle and back -- but always six, always in formation. One small figure, the musician, sets the pulse; the tempo is visibly owned by a body, and when that body stops, everything stops. **Takeaway:** Six dancers, one tune, one clock. The set is a machine and the musician is its mainspring -- change the tempo and you change the dance. [C35061]

## 20. Bells, hankies and sticks: the body as percussion

Morris is "rhythmic stepping and the execution of choreographed figures" with bell pads on the shins, and handkerchiefs or sticks "to accompany the hand movements" -- the bells make the step audible, the handkerchief extends the hand, the stick is clashed against a partner's. The dance is heard as much as seen: capers, skips, jumps, single and double steps, the bell-ring marking every beat. This is why the Esperance girls could teach by demonstration alone -- the sound pattern is the step pattern, and a learner who hears the bells has already half-learned the dance. One qualification, carried from leg 3: Morris kit in general (white, bells, ribbons, baldricks) is well evidenced, and the Esperance Morris Book photographs were used by 1970s women's sides as kit inspiration, but no source consulted specifies the Esperance girls' exact kit colours or cut, so a piece must not invent a specific "Esperance uniform" -- use generic Morris white and bells, or the photographic record, and say so.

**Watchable translation:** Every step rings -- a visual bell-flash and a tick. Handkerchief dances: white shapes bloom at the hands on each beat. Stick dances: pairs meet and clash, sparks at the contact point, on the off-beat. Mute the bells and the dance looks wrong, because the sound is part of the step. **Takeaway:** The body is the instrument. Bells, hankies and sticks are not costume -- they are the percussion section, worn on the dancer. [C35062]

## 21. Figure and chorus

A Morris dance alternates figures -- set floor patterns danced to a phrase of music -- with choruses, repeated sections danced between the figures. The dance is not one continuous flow but a repeating architecture: distinctive figure, shared chorus, new figure, same chorus. The pleasure is recognition, not surprise: the viewer learns the chorus and anticipates its return, and the figure is the variation that earns it. This is also the shape of Neal's whole project -- the club evening as a chorus that returns every week, with a new figure (a new village's dances, a new tour, a new book) set inside it each time.

**Watchable translation:** Alternate two visual modes on a strict cycle: a "figure" section where each dancer traces a different path, and a "chorus" where all six do the same step in place, together. The viewer learns the chorus and anticipates its return -- the pleasure is recognition, not surprise. **Takeaway:** Figure, chorus, figure, chorus. The dance is a building with rooms you return to -- and the return is the point. [C35063]

## 22. Two old men disagree

In October 1910 Neal, who had praised Kimber's "traditional" dancing, seized upon discrepancies in stepping between Kimber and the older dancer Joseph Trafford and brought Trafford and the fiddler Mark Cox to the Esperance Club. Trafford, born 1839, had stopped dancing around 1880; Kimber, born 1872, had learned from his father in the 1888-99 gap. There was no single "Headington" to recover -- there were two memories of it, and the dispute was dragged into the public Neal-Sharp spat, with Sharp getting Kimber to agree on 7 November 1910 that "the way I have taught you is right". The mechanism matters because it breaks the salvage story: the revival did not recover a dance, it chose between two old men's versions of one, and the choice was political as much as musical.

**Watchable translation:** Two elder figures demonstrate the "same" step differently, side by side; the learners split, half following each. The piece holds both versions without resolving them -- a visible fork in the transmission line. The lesson is watchable: tradition is a disagreement, not a text. **Takeaway:** There is no original to recover, only witnesses who disagree. The dance is the argument between them -- and the argument is still going. [C35064]

## 23. Notation against the body

Sharp "would collect the dances, often from elderly village dancers, make a clear notation and publish"; MacIlwaine notated the steps from Florrie Warren for The Morris Book Part I (1907), dedicated to the Esperance Club. Neal's side also published -- the Esperance Morris Book (1910, 12 dances) -- so the difference is priority, not medium: for Sharp the book is the yardstick, "a touchstone... ensuring that the dances were being performed 'properly'"; for Neal the book is a souvenir of a body that already knows. The book freezes motion into symbols, and the symbols can then be checked against the body -- or the body checked against the symbols, which is where the quarrel lives. Sharp's second edition of 1912, re-notated directly from Kimber and cutting out the Esperance girls, shows the book being used as an instrument: notation as a way of writing the girls out.

**Watchable translation:** Two dancers: one reads from a book (steps appear as notation, then the body follows, stiffly, exactly); the other copies a live demonstrator (fluid, drifting). Over time the book-dancer stays identical and the body-dancer diverges -- until the book is closed and both must remember. Show the book as a character that freezes motion into symbols. **Takeaway:** The book is a second dancer, and it never forgets -- which is its strength and its crime. Notation preserves the dance and arrests it, in the same gesture. [C35065]

## 24. Joy against accuracy: the dial

The Times contrasted "the spirit of joy" of the Esperance dancers with "the spirit of accuracy" of Sharp's supporters. Sharp called the girls "boisterous" and "hoydenish" and wanted graceful dancing; Neal called his approach "the blighting touch of the pedant and the expert". Douglas Kennedy, Sharp's successor, reframed it as "content versus form" and said he had "seen the pendulum swing from side to side" ever since; his memory of an Esperance display was "all impulse and impressionistic but a bit of a mess". The dial is the whole revival in one control: turn it one way and the dance gets neater, more uniform, more correct, and a little deader; turn it the other and it gets ragged, laughing, off-beat, and alive. Neither end is the "right" Morris. The dial is the subject -- and it is still being turned, by every teacher who has to choose, in a school hall on a Tuesday night, between the child who is enjoying it and the child who is doing it right.

**Watchable translation:** A single visible dial: at one end, dancers are precise, synchronised, graceful -- and a little dead; at the other, they are ragged, laughing, off-beat -- and alive. Let the piece (or the viewer) turn the dial and watch synchrony trade against energy. Neither end is the "right" Morris; the dial is the subject. **Takeaway:** Joy against accuracy is not a quarrel to settle but a dial to turn. The revival is the turning. [C35066]

## 25. The fork: two institutions, one dance

14 November 1907: the Goupil Gallery conference to found a national society. Early 1908: Neal's Association for the Revival and Practice of Folk-Music. March 1910: the Esperance Guild of Morris-Dancers. December 1911: Sharp's English Folk Dance Society. Same dances, two institutions, "entirely separate lines" (Neal) -- and the split was also political, since Neal was a suffragette and Sharp distanced himself from the increasingly militant suffrage actions. The fork is the mechanism by which a single tradition speciates: the same Headington dances, taught by the same chain of teachers, diverge into two styles, two books, two curricula, two audiences, and eventually two histories -- one of which gets written by the victor and one of which has to be reclaimed seventy years later. The fork is also the shape of the network after 1907: Sharp keeps Kimber, the Karpeles sisters, the EFDS and, after 1924, Douglas Kennedy, while Neal keeps Carey, Warren, the Guild and the suffrage network, and loses the institutional centre.

**Watchable translation:** One dance, two stages, side by side, diverging in real time: the left stage gets neater, more uniform, more "correct"; the right stage gets wilder, more various, more joyful. A line down the middle of the canvas. The viewer watches a single tradition speciate -- the fork is the piece. **Takeaway:** One dance, two institutions, and the split is the story. The fork does not divide the tradition; it is how the tradition survives -- by becoming two things at once. [C35067]

## 26. The club as a social machine

The Esperance Club (Cumberland Market, St Pancras, from autumn 1895) was open four evenings a week for working girls -- seamstresses, sweatshop workers -- offering drama, song, dance, play-acting and a Friday Bible class, explicitly as "a bright and happy Christian home" and "living schools for working women". Respectability was the price of admission and the product: the club made working girls presentable to patrons and gave them a room of their own, and Neal left the West London Mission to escape its institutional constraints and experiment with dance and drama. Alongside it, Maison Esperance paid a decent living wage for tailoring -- the girls literally sewed for a living and danced in the evenings, the economic base and the art the same women's same hands, and later teaching itself became paid work. And the organiser at the centre did not dance: neither Neal nor Sharp was a dancer (Knevett), and her gift was organisation -- "her ability for making things happen", "a born organiser" -- finding Kimber, negotiating his terms, booking halls, arranging tours, publishing books. The revival's engine was a non-performer who assembled performers, and her motion is arrangement -- lines, groupings, entrances -- while the dancers supply the steps.

**Watchable translation:** A room with a weekly rhythm: figures enter tired and grey after "work" (slow, hunched, desaturated), the room warms as music starts, and they leave upright, bright, synchronised. The same figures switch between a sewing rhythm (small, repetitive, seated, hands) and a dancing rhythm (large, stepping, standing, whole body). And one figure never dances but moves everyone else: she points, and a dancer enters; she opens a door, and a tour begins. **Takeaway:** The club is the engine; the dance is what it burns. And the choreographer is the woman who never dances -- she conducts people, not notes. [C35068]

## 27. Teaching as a way out

The girls "earned money for their teaching" -- teaching was not just mission work but employment, an alternative to cleaning or sewing. Blanche Payling, eighteen, went alone to Thaxted in January 1911 to teach children of Lee's Sweetie Factory, three classes a day. Florrie Warren, eighteen, sailed to America in December 1910, danced at Carnegie Hall, and was "considered a great star". A skill learned in a club evening became a wage and a passage. And the teaching carried a political charge that Atherton states plainly: "taking the despised girls of the slums out to teach the wealthy and the influential; girls of little education... were respected for their expertise and skill" -- teaching reversed the class direction, and the poor taught the rich. The mechanism is economic as well as social: knowledge that can be taught can be sold, and selling it is freedom -- which is why the question of who is allowed to teach, and who counts as an expert, was worth a three-year public quarrel.

**Watchable translation:** A dancer teaches a step; a coin (or a ticket, a train, a ship) appears. Follow one girl's arc: learner, demonstrator, teacher, traveller, each stage marked by a widening map (club, county, country, Atlantic). In the teaching scene, status is shown by who follows whom -- and here the following runs uphill: the experts are the ones society called unskilled. **Takeaway:** Teaching is the way out. The step is a wage, a train ticket, a ship -- and the girl who teaches the rich has already reversed the world. [C35069]

## 28. Salvage and the gap

The revival was salvage of something nearly lost: Headington had not performed between 1888 and 1899; Kimber "may perhaps have done a little dancing c.1887 when he was 15 but... had to wait 10 years to participate again". Sharp collected from 1907 to 1914 from old men, "about 50 years too late -- many teams had by this time folded" (Sutcliffe). And the "original" was itself a revival, Manning's paid re-formation of March 1899. Neal's own verdict, via Tradfolk, holds the balance exactly: without her, Sharp's knowledge "would have stayed in his 1899 notebook"; without Sharp's notes, "we would have had no record at all". The gap is the mechanism: a decade of empty floor between the last old dancer and the first young one, and the dance has to jump it on the feet of girls who never saw it danced in its home. What crosses the gap is brighter but thinner; what stayed behind is fuller but fading. The piece is about the jump, not about either side alone.

**Watchable translation:** Old figures (slow, few, in a dim room) teach young figures; between them, a visible gap -- a decade of empty floor, 1888-1899, shown as a blank stretch the dance must jump across. The young dancers' version is brighter but thinner; the old men's is fuller but fading. The piece is about the jump across the gap, not about either side alone. **Takeaway:** Salvage is a jump across a gap, not a rescue of a relic. The dance survives because someone is willing to leap ten empty years on somebody else's feet. [C35070]

## 29. Erasure and return

"The history was written by the supporters of Cecil Sharp and, for many years, Mary Neal's role... was glossed over" (Tradfolk). Rehabilitation came from the 1970s women's movement: women challenging male-dominated Morris looked for precedents, found Neal, and used photographs of the Esperance dancers as inspiration for their kit (Val Parker, Bath City Morris); a new Islington side called itself New Esperance, claiming "a lineage that was older than most of the men's teams"; Janet Dowling founded Mary Neal Rapper. Lucy Neal lodged Mary's papers at the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library -- the counter-narrative entering the victor's archive -- and the EFDSS now celebrates both: Mary Neal Day on 7 February 2009, the Mary Neal Lecture from 5 February 2010 (Lucy Neal, introduced by Shirley Collins), the Full English Extra digitisation of 2015-16, Roy Judge's 1989 article, Sue Swift's play The Forgotten Mary Neal (1998), and Kathryn Atherton's book of 2024. The mechanism is a palimpsest: the same floor, danced twice, with a different cast credited each time -- and the second dancing does not erase the first, it restores the half that was covered.

**Watchable translation:** A dance is performed, then a curtain (or a book, or a name) covers one half of the stage -- the women's side fades to a footnote. Decades later the footnote brightens, the dancers step back out, and the same dance is performed again with the missing half restored. The piece is a palimpsest: the same floor, danced twice, with a different cast credited each time. **Takeaway:** History is written by the victor, and then rewritten by the footnote. The dance outlives both versions -- it only needs someone to dance the missing half back in. [C35071]

## Aesthetic logic

Neal's aesthetic is not a look but a test: does it move people, and do the people move it on? Participation outranks perfection; joy is a standard, not a lapse; drift is life, not decay; the book is a receipt, not the thing; the organiser's art is arrangement -- who is in the room, who teaches whom, what the evening is for. Her pieces would be built the way her club was built: a room, a tune, six dancers, a chain of teachers, and a quarrel at the edge about whether the dance is right -- with the quarrel left in, because the quarrel is the history. What she would have refused: the dance as a museum object, the expert's yardstick as the whole story, the single authoritative version, the timeless unbroken tradition that never was. What she kept: the girls, the evening, the teaching, the next county. The through-line of her life is visible in the shape of it: she begins by giving working girls a room, she spends nine years giving them a dance, and she ends by giving them a holiday home -- the vehicle changes, the project does not. And the revival she co-founded is best understood the way she understood it: not as a collection of dances saved from the past, but as a chain of evenings in which living people teach each other something worth dancing, and the chain itself is the tradition.

**Takeaway:** The aesthetic is the chain. A room, a tune, six dancers, a teacher who was a pupil last week -- and the willingness to let the dance change hands until it belongs to everyone. [C35072]
