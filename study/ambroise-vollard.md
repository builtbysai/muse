# Ambroise Vollard: Study Notes

**Site:** Ambroise Vollard (1866-1939), French art dealer and publisher -- born Henri-Louis-Ambroise Vollard, Tuesday 3 July 1866, in Saint-Denis, Reunion, eldest of ten children of a notary; arrived in France in November 1885 to study law (Montpellier, then the Ecole de droit in Paris, degree 1888; he reached Paris itself about 1887); while still a law student became a clerk for the art dealer Alphonse Dumas at l'Union Artistique, a small Paris gallery specialising in academic painting; from 1890 sold modern art on his own from a seventh-floor two-room apartment near Sacre-Coeur, dealing in drawings and prints by Rops, Steinlen and Guys bought along the Seine quays; opened his first real shop in September 1893 at 37 rue Laffitte, then the centre of the Paris art market, moved to 39 rue Laffitte in 1895-96 and to larger premises at 6 rue Laffitte in May/June 1896, where the gallery stayed until the First World War; after the war received customers at his apartment at 28 rue de Grammont, and from about 1924 ran the business from his mansion at 28 rue de Martignac; the dealer who gave Cezanne his first solo exhibition (November 1895), Van Gogh his first solo show (June 1895), Picasso his first French exhibition (June 1901) and Matisse his first solo show (June 1904), and who published 22 illustrated-book projects between 1900 and 1939, including the Vollard Suite (100 Picasso etchings, 1930-37); author of monographs on Cezanne (1914), Renoir (1919) and Degas (1924) and of the memoir Recollections of a Picture Dealer (English 1936, French Souvenirs d'un marchand de tableaux 1937); died Friday 21 July 1939, aged 73, in a car crash near Versailles on the night of 21-22 July, his chauffeur-driven Talbot skidding on a wet road near the junction to Pontchartrain and somersaulting twice on the way from his cottage at Le Tremblay-sur-Mauldre to the rue de Martignac mansion, where he had stored between 5,000 and 10,000 artworks with no inventory and no direct heir; his estate was handled by the fellow dealer Martin Fabiani with his brother Lucien Vollard, and the stock scattered through the war years, the Slomovic vault sale of 2010, and the Musee d'Orsay restitutions of 2022-23. ## M1. The Dusty Shop as a Filter
Vollard's shop did not look like a picture gallery. Gertrude Stein's account, in The Autobiography of Alice B. Toklas, is the famous one: "It did not look like a picture gallery." By day the shop was dark; at night a flickering lamp revealed a dusty room with rolled canvases and pictures stacked floor to ceiling, and at its centre, seemingly asleep, Ambroise Vollard. The stock was not displayed, it was piled: the buyer had to upset the piles of canvases and find what he liked in the heap -- Gertrude's phrase is "They had soon the privilege of upsetting his piles of canvases and finding what they liked in the heap." Only a small painting in the window hinted at what was inside. What it IS: discovery by slow looking, not display; the shop is a heap with a filter built into it, and the filter is the buyer's own patience -- the pictures that surface are the ones somebody bothered to dig for. **Takeaway:** build a dark room full of stacked canvases where nothing is hung and nothing is labelled; let the viewer dig through the pile, and let a picture surface only when it is pulled out and held up to the light.

## M2. Buy the Whole Studio, Hold for Years
Vollard's buying move was wholesale, not retail. In June 1894, at the Hotel Drouot auction of the paint-seller Julien "Pere" Tanguy's pictures, he spent 845 francs on four Cezannes, one Gauguin, a Van Gogh oil study of boots, one Pissarro and one Korochinsky. Then he tracked Cezanne down in Provence by questioning paint-sellers for the old studio address and former neighbours for the new one, approaching Cezanne's son first, and bought about 150 canvases at a modest price -- effectively the artist's available output, securing a monopoly. He did the same again and again: the contents of Cezanne's studio in 1899, of Emile Bernard's studio in 1901, of Derain's studio in 1905, of Vlaminck's studio in 1906; 60-80 Van Gogh canvases bought in 1895-96. And then he sat on them. "Buying low and selling high, Vollard could hold on to the artists' works for years... His sloth-like strategies became his modus operandi" (France Today). What it IS: purchase an artist's entire output in one stroke and wait for the market to ripen, selling only when the time is ripe; the stock is the bet, and the waiting is the work. **Takeaway:** let one agent buy a whole field of canvases at once and then refuse to sell any of them; the field must ripen on the wall over years, and a sale happens only when a single canvas has ripened past a threshold the viewer can see.

## M3. The First Show as a Coronation
A single-artist exhibition that converts an unknown or forgotten painter into a master in one room, with the dealer staking his own reputation on the act. The model is the Cezanne exhibition of November-December 1895 at 37 rue Laffitte: about 50 of the roughly 150 canvases Vollard had bought, shown as Cezanne's first solo exhibition -- Cezanne's work had not been exhibited in Paris for about twenty years. The public reaction was lukewarm except for a glowing review by Thadee Natanson in La Revue Blanche; the buyers were fellow artists -- Degas, Renoir, Monet, Pissarro. Sotheby's dates the opening to Wednesday 13 November 1895, and Pissarro wrote to his daughter-in-law that same day about the display, which included remarkably finished still lifes alongside works he thought extraordinary in their apparent incompletion. The art historian Robert Jensen's verdict, via The Art Story: the show made Cezanne "the first important French artist to forge his reputation within the context of a commercial gallery rather than through public art exhibitions." Vollard repeated the model: the first Van Gogh solo show, June 1895; Gauguin, 1898; Picasso's first French exhibition, 24 June 1901; Matisse's first solo show, June 1904; first solo shows for Emile Bernard (1901) and Maillol (1902). What it IS: one room, one name, and the dealer's own name on the door -- the show is a coronation, and the crown is the dealer's reputation. **Takeaway:** stage one room with one artist's name on the door and fifty canvases inside; let the room start empty of reputation and fill, over the course of the piece, until the name on the door is the one everybody else is copying.

## M4. The Pretended Nap
Vollard sold by not selling. "His tactic was to pretend to nap in his chair so he could listen to his customers without being obviously interested... if he felt he was being talked down, he would successfully talk the price back up" (France Today). Picasso was impressed by the technique. Renoir, at the sitting for the 1917 toreador portrait, warned him: "All I ask of you is that you do not fall asleep" -- and France Today adds that many artists gave him the same warning, and that Cezanne wished Vollard could be as inert as one of his apples. The nap was a listening device: the dealer asleep in the middle of his own shop, hearing everything, committing to nothing, and letting the buyer's own desire do the work. What it IS: feigned sleep and pointless delay as a sales technique -- the seller withdraws, the buyer talks himself into the purchase, and the price goes up when it is talked down. **Takeaway:** put a huge figure asleep in the middle of the shop and let the viewer talk; the figure never answers, but the longer the viewer stays and the more he wants, the more the price tag beside the picture quietly climbs.

## M5. Pay in Paint, Not Money
Gauguin in Tahiti, in his impoverished final years: Vollard paid him a regular allowance and supplied art materials, and to Gauguin's irritation sometimes shipped tubes of paint in lieu of money. The 1900 agreement formalised it: 300 francs a month as an advance on sales, plus 200 francs for every painting sold. The same logic ran the other way at the start: in 1894 Vollard convinced Manet's widow to let him sell the artist's drawings and unfinished works, and Degas, Gauguin, Guillaumin, Pissarro and Renoir swapped their own works for Manet sketches -- those swaps formed the basis of a new stock. What it IS: supporting a distant artist with materials and an allowance instead of cash, keeping the artist producing while the dealer accumulates the output; or seeding a stock by barter, not by purchase. **Takeaway:** show a crate of paint tubes travelling by ship to a far island, and a painting travelling back; the dealer never sends money, only the means to make more pictures, and the pictures pile up at his end while the tubes pile up at the other.

## M6. The Cellar as a Salon
Below the shop at 6 rue Laffitte was a white vaulted room without a single picture on the walls -- the Cave de Vollard -- where chicken curry Reunionnaise was served to artists, writers and collectors for thirty years. Brassai, a regular guest, recalled it: "for thirty years, his famous cellar -- a white vaulted room without a single picture on the walls -- had been the center of Parisian artistic life. What joyful feasts, what parties and conferences, what planning sessions had been held there with all those artists, writers, critics, and collectors who were now famous." Renoir said to Vollard: "Wherever else I go, Vollard, I can say that I know beforehand whom I shall meet, and what we shall talk about. At your place one does at least meet with the unforeseen." Guests included Rodin, Misia Natanson, and Renoir, who once brought a woman disguised as a nun. What it IS: hospitality as the dealer's network -- a bare room under the shop where the people who make the pictures eat together, and the unforeseen meeting is the product. **Takeaway:** build a white vaulted cellar with no pictures on its walls and a long table down the middle; let figures arrive, eat, and talk, and let a deal be struck at the table that no gallery wall could have produced.

## M7. The Book as a Second Gallery
The livre d'artiste: a deluxe book pairing original prints by a living painter with a classic text, so the painter's work reaches collectors as a book rather than a canvas. Vollard's own account of the idea, secondhand via The Art Story: "I was hardly settled in the rue Laffitte when I began to dream of publishing fine prints, but I felt they must be done by 'painter-printmakers.' My idea was to obtain works from artists who were not printmakers by profession." He commissioned original prints from painters -- Degas, Derain, Denis, Bonnard, Vuillard, Redon, Munch -- and had the master printer Auguste Clot execute them, raising the colour lithograph to a fine-art form. The first modern livre d'artiste was Verlaine's Parallelement, illustrated by Bonnard in 1900: 109 lithographs printed in rose ink plus 9 wood engravings, edition of 200, printed by Clot -- and a commercial failure, like Daphnis et Chloe (1902). The Imprimerie Nationale, thinking it was printing a geometry textbook, recalled Parallelement when the lesbian imagery was discovered, and Vollard had to replace the state vignette with a Bonnard one. He switched from lithography to woodcut, which collectors preferred, and Maurice Denis's Imitation of Christ was a sales success. In total: 22 illustrated-book projects between 1900 and 1939, and 45 fine-press books by the Toledo Museum's count -- the two counts measure different things and must not be merged. The publishing was a money-loser, subsidised by picture sales: Wikipedia notes his success in the sale of paintings "allowed him to subsidize editions of prints purely out of personal interest, since they rarely brought him profits." What it IS: a second gallery made of paper -- the same painters, the same eye, but the work arrives as a book, and the book loses money on purpose. **Takeaway:** let a painting be remade as a book: the same image printed in rose ink on fine paper, bound, and shelved; the book must lose money and still be made, because the book is the gallery that fits on a shelf.

## M8. The Commission That Outlives Its Maker
A project so long in gestation -- years of plates, changing texts, abandoned collaborators -- that it is finished only after the dealer's death. The Vollard Suite is the type case: 100 etchings by Picasso, commissioned in 1930 in exchange for a Renoir and a Cezanne from Vollard's private collection; 97 plates handed over by 1936, three portraits of Vollard added early in 1937 to round the number to 100; the edition printed by Roger Lacouriere in 1939, about 300 sets, weeks before Vollard died, and never published in his lifetime -- the planned pairing with two poems by Andre Suares never happened, and the unsigned edition was bought from his estate by Henri Petiet between 1942 and 1945 for 10,000 francs and not issued until the mid-1950s. The same pattern runs through the publishing line: Chagall's Bible, 1930-39, published by Teriade only in 1956; Rouault's Miserere, 1916-27, which Vollard deemed unsaleable, and which Rouault had to sue Vollard's heirs for the plates before it was published in 1948; Chagall's Fables, finished 1930, published 1952. What it IS: the work as a slow relay, not a product -- a commission that passes from hand to hand across decades and outlives the man who ordered it. **Takeaway:** start one commission and let it run past the dealer's lifetime: plates arrive one by one over years, the text changes, the publisher dies, and the book is finished by strangers -- the piece is the relay, not the book.

## M9. The Portrait as a Competition
Vollard sat for portrait after portrait, and each artist tried to outdo the last. Cezanne painted him in 1899 (he posed 115 times, by Sue Roe's secondhand count, under Cezanne's instruction to sit "still as an apple"); Renoir painted him in 1899 (in a red headscarf), in 1908, and as a toreador in 1917; Bonnard painted him with his cat, about 1924; Picasso painted the Cubist portrait in 1910. Picasso's remark, secondhand: "they all did him through a sense of competition, each one wanting to do him better than the others. He had the vanity of a woman, that man [...] my Cubist portrait of him [...] is the best one of them all." And Picasso again, secondhand: "the most beautiful woman who ever lived never had her portrait painted, drawn, or engraved any oftener than Vollard -- by Cezanne, Renoir, Rouault, Bonnard, Forain, almost everybody in fact." What it IS: the sitter as a shared subject that measures rival painters against each other -- one man, many portraits, each one a bid to be the best. **Takeaway:** seat one figure in a chair and let five painters paint him in turn; each portrait must try to beat the last, and the sitter must sit still as an apple through all of them.

## M10. The Memoir as Self-Mythology
Late in life Vollard wrote his own legend, anecdote by anecdote, and admitted nothing about his business methods. Recollections of a Picture Dealer appeared in English in 1936 (Constable, translated by Violet M. MacDonald) and in French as Souvenirs d'un marchand de tableaux in 1937; the 1926 debut date given by The Art Story conflicts with every other source and is treated as an error. The sources agree on the book's character: heilig-meyers calls his account "largely anecdotal and reveals nothing of his private life and his business methods"; France Today says "Artistic licence abounds in Vollard's 1936 memoir." The memoir is the source of nearly every quotable Vollard line in this study -- and not one of those lines was read in the book itself by any leg, so every one of them is secondhand here. What it IS: the dealer writing his own legend late in life, charming and unreliable, with the methods left out. **Takeaway:** let an old dealer tell his own story in anecdotes, each one funnier than the last, while the ledger books behind him stay shut; the story must charm and the numbers must never appear.

## M11. Never Show What They Asked For
The Met curator Gary Tinterow, on Vollard's selling, as reported by NPR -- the single verified-verbatim item in this study, and it is Tinterow's words, not Vollard's: "He would never sell anybody what they wanted; he would never show people what they wanted." Tinterow's gloss in the same piece: "If they came in to see a Cezanne, he would bring out a Gauguin. If they wanted a still life, he would say, 'Well, here's a landscape.'" The same refusal runs through the anecdotes: Louise Havemeyer, kept waiting an hour for a Cezanne and saying she needed to catch a boat, was told, secondhand, "Well, madame, if you want to buy a Cezanne, there's always another boat." Sue Roe's summary, via The Picture Sellers: "If his ultimate goal was to make sales, Vollard's primary passion was collecting; on his own admission, he was always reluctant to sell his precious acquisitions, hence his reputation for hiding things away in cupboards and bringing out works reluctantly one by one." What it IS: a dealer who refuses the request and offers the opposite -- the sale happens, if it happens, on the dealer's terms, never the buyer's. **Takeaway:** build a shop where asking for a picture by name guarantees you will be shown a different one; the picture you wanted is in a cupboard, and it comes out only when the dealer decides, one at a time.

## M12. The Miss, Admitted
Vollard bought 60-80 Van Gogh canvases in 1895-96, mounted the first Van Gogh solo show in June 1895 and a fuller retrospective in 1896-97, sold almost nothing, and then gave up promoting Van Gogh's paintings. What makes the mechanism is that he said so in print. Three independent sources agree on the wording, secondhand: "I was totally wrong about Van Gogh! I thought he had no future at all, and I let his paintings go for practically nothing." The miss is part of the record because the dealer put it there himself -- the same man who bought 150 Cezannes and held them until the market ripened also sold the Van Goghs for a song and admitted it. What it IS: a dealer who records his own worst mistake in his own book, so the miss sits in the catalogue beside the triumphs. **Takeaway:** show a wall of Van Goghs sold too cheap and too early, with the dealer's own admission printed underneath in his own hand; the piece must keep the mistake on the wall, not hide it.

## Evolution

EARLY (1866-1895, Reunion, Montpellier, Paris, the garret and 37 rue Laffitte): born in Saint-Denis, Reunion, eldest of ten children of a notary; law in Montpellier from November 1885, then Paris, degree 1888; clerk for the dealer Alphonse Dumas at l'Union Artistique, which "actively discouraged Vollard's interest in Impressionism"; selling drawings and prints from his seventh-floor apartment from 1890; first shop September 1893 at 37 rue Laffitte. The early Vollard is a clerk who learned the trade from the bottom: he saw his first Cezanne landscape in the window of Pere Tanguy's colour shop in the rue Clauzel and, secondhand, "It was as if I [had] received a blow to the stomach." The first capital came from a trusting banker and from barter, not from sales of fashionable work -- the Manet estate show of November 1894, bought from the widow, brought him Degas and Renoir and the Nabis.

MIDDLE (1895-1914, the reputation engine): the Cezanne show of November-December 1895 (about 50 works shown of about 150 bought; opening dated Wednesday 13 November 1895 by Sotheby's, with Pissarro's letter of that day discussing the show); the Van Gogh shows of 1895 and 1896-97, both commercial failures; the first print album 1896 and the second 1897; the Gauguin show of 1898 with Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?; the move to 6 rue Laffitte in May/June 1896, with the mezzanine and the soon-legendary cellar; the first Picasso exhibition, 24 June - 14 July 1901 (64 paintings by the Met's count, 65 by Mallen's appendix, 75 by one chronology -- the Met's 64 is used here); Matisse's first solo show, June 1904, which sold nothing; the Steins' first purchase, Friday 28 October 1904; Derain's studio bought out in 1905 and the Thames commission about 1906; 27 Picassos bought for 2,000 francs in 1906, his first Picasso purchase since the 1901 show; the Picasso retrospective of December 1910; the Rouault bargain of 1912 (the Miserere bought in exchange for illustrating Vollard's own Pere Ubu book). The middle period is the engine: buy whole studios, mount first shows, hold the stock, publish books at a loss. Clients who mattered: Barnes, Havemeyer, the Steins, Shchukin and Morozov.

LATE (1914-1939, publisher first, dealer second): the gallery closed during the First World War; after it he received customers at his apartment at 28 rue de Grammont and from about 1924 at 28 rue de Martignac. The monographs: Paul Cezanne (1914, edition of 1,000 with an original Cezanne etching as frontispiece), La vie et l'oeuvre de Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1919, the first complete life of Renoir), Degas (1924). The long commissions: Chagall's Dead Souls (1924-25), Fables (1926-31), Bible (1931-39); Picasso's Balzac (1931) and the Vollard Suite (1930-37); Rouault's Cirque de l'etoile filante (1938) and Passione (1939), his last editions. The memoir (1936/1937) and En ecoutant Cezanne, Degas, Renoir (1938). He lectured at the Barnes Foundation in early November 1936. He died in July 1939, weeks after the Vollard Suite was printed, with 5,000 to 10,000 works stored at the rue de Martignac mansion and no inventory and no declared heir.

AFTERLIFE SO FAR: the Fabiani/Bignou shipment -- fearing the Nazi invasion, Fabiani shipped 560 paintings to Etienne Bignou's New York gallery; they left Lisbon on the SS Excalibur on 25 September 1940, were intercepted by the Royal Navy in Bermuda on 3 October 1940, designated enemy property and stored at the National Gallery of Canada in Ottawa, and were released by a London prize court on 19 April 1949 to Fabiani, who returned them to Vollard's sisters (in gratitude the sisters donated the Rouault and Chagall lithographs and a single Gauguin to the National Gallery of Canada). The Slomovic thread: Vollard's former secretary and protege Erich Slomovic (born 1915), a young Serb of Jewish origin, had connections with Vollard, Fabiani and Lucien Vollard from about 1938 and amassed about 600 works by exchange, gift, purchase and donation; in 1940 he put about 200 works in a Societe Generale bank vault in Paris and took about 450 to Yugoslavia, exhibiting them in Zagreb in 1940; he, his brother and his father were arrested and killed by the Nazis in 1942 in Belgrade; after the war the Yugoslav authorities appropriated the paintings, shown officially only once, in 1989 in Belgrade and Zagreb as the "Slomovic Collection", with ownership in legal dispute as of 2014. The Paris vault works were discovered in 1979 when the bank opened the vault to recover unpaid storage fees; after an 11-year legal dispute a court in Amiens ruled in 1996 that they belonged to the Vollard estate; Sotheby's sold them in Paris and London in June 2010 -- 140 works for 23 million euros per The Art Newspaper, 141 works for 30 million euros per Wikipedia (see C13). The archives were given to the French government by Vollard's heirs in 1989 in lieu of estate taxes and are held as Fonds Vollard, MS 421, Bibliotheque et Archives des Musees Nationaux, Paris, with the photographic archive at the Musee d'Orsay; a digital archive of the Galerie Vollard (1893-1939) was published online by the Wildenstein Plattner Institute. Restitution, 2022-23: in May 2022 a French court confirmed four works at the Musee d'Orsay were Vollard's property at his death in 1939 before being stolen by the succession experts (Bignou and Fabiani, working with the Germans); in February 2023 a Paris administrative court ordered their restitution -- Renoir's Marine, Guernsey (1883) and a sanguine study for the Judgment of Paris (about 1908-1910), Gauguin's Still Life with Mandolin (1885), Cezanne's Undergrowth (1890-1892, watercolour) -- and the four were sold at Sotheby's New York on 16 May 2023, the proceeds split among Vollard's legal heirs; three works remain outstanding (two Renoirs and a Cezanne head of an old man). Scholarship: the first comprehensive exhibition devoted to Vollard, "Cezanne to Picasso: Ambroise Vollard, Patron of the Avant-Garde", ran at the Met 14 September 2006 - 7 January 2007, then the Art Institute of Chicago and the Musee d'Orsay; catalogue edited by Rebecca A. Rabinow (Met, 2006).

## Aesthetic Logic

Vollard's aesthetic is an aesthetic of patience, and it is the opposite of the critic's. Where the critic judges a picture, Vollard buys the whole studio and waits; where the dealer sells what is asked for, Vollard shows what was not asked for; where the publisher counts the profit, Vollard subsidises the book out of the picture sales and calls it personal interest. The unit of his system is not the picture but the stock: "his strategy focused on building a large stock of works by diverse artists, as opposed to securing exclusive contracts with a select few" (the Met). The stock is the bet, the waiting is the work, and the first show is the coronation -- one room, one name, the dealer's reputation on the door. He trusted his eye over theory: he believed Cezanne was the greatest painter who had ever lived because Cezanne trusted his eyes instead of relying on theory (France Today, paraphrase, not a verbatim quote), and he bought 150 Cezannes before the artist had a dealer, on the strength of one landscape seen in a paint-seller's window. The same eye had limits, and he printed them: he never collected Braque, Juan Gris or Fernand Leger, he stayed unresponsive to Cubism and Surrealism, and he let Picasso go after Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907) -- having bought nothing from him between the 1901 show and 1906, then 27 works for 2,000 francs in 1906, mounted the December 1910 retrospective, and after Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907) let the painting relationship lapse, returning to Picasso only as a publisher -- the Balzac etchings of 1927 and the Vollard Suite of 1930-37. The whole system is held together by a collector's reluctance to sell at all: he hid things away in cupboards and brought them out reluctantly one by one, he pretended to nap so he could listen, and he told a buyer who had waited an hour that there was always another boat. A piece built on Vollard fails when it decorates (adds a Paris accent, adds a beret, adds a Picasso) and succeeds when it makes the viewer do the work of waiting: one room, one pile of canvases, one dealer asleep in the middle, and the picture surfacing only when somebody digs for it.
