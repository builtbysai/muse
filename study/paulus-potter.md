## The life

Paulus Potter was baptised on 20 November 1625 in Enkhuizen, the son of the painter Pieter Symonsz Potter (1597-1652) and Aechtie (Aaltje) Paulusdr Bartsius (d. 1636), sister of the painter Willem Bartsius. No birth date is recorded; 20 November is the baptism date, used as the conventional birth date. Through his mother the family was connected to the von Egmont and Semeyns families, "who held important offices in Enkhuizen and at the court in The Hague" (Dayton/Grove). The family moved to Leiden in 1628 (his father bought citizenship on 15 May 1628 and joined the glass-makers' guild as a master glass painter) and to Amsterdam in 1631 (his father bought citizenship on 14 October 1635).

He trained first with his father in the Amsterdam studio in the 1630s and "quickly surpassed" him (Knox). Dayton/Grove: he "followed his father more than Moeyaert in searching for ways to integrate his figures with the landscape, suggesting space by carefully positioning the forms of the figures." A "P. Potter" is recorded as a student of painting in Jacob de Wet's notebook in Haarlem, May 1641 -- the strongest documented teacher link after the father (leg 3, C1015 on the 1642 variant). Claes Moeyaert is a possible teacher: Dayton/Grove says the early history subjects "show the strong influence of Claes Moeyaert, with whom Paulus may also have studied"; NGA notes "other writers have argued that Potter studied with" Moeyaert. Pupilage is "may" only; influence is certain. Houbraken is the source for the father-as-teacher claim via NGA.

On 6 August 1646 he joined the Guild of St Luke in Delft (12 guilders, "the standard fee paid by artists from outside the town") -- he may actually have been living in The Hague, where his parents were. He joined the Hague Guild of St Luke in 1649. From 1649 to 1652 he lived in The Hague in the house of the painter Jan van Goyen (rented; studio at 17 Dunne Bierkade), where he met and married Adriana van Balckeneynde in July 1650 -- daughter of his neighbour Claes Dircksz. van Balckeneynde, court architect and master carpenter, who "introduced him to the Dutch elite" (EN Wikipedia). Amalia van Solms-Braunfels bought The Farmyard (1649); Houbraken says she rejected it because of the urinating cow, and that court ladies advised against the purchase. Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen "showed a keen interest in his work and even visited him" (Mauritshuis). By May 1652 he was back in Amsterdam at the invitation of Nicolaes Tulp (burgomaster and surgeon, Rembrandt's former patron), who owned several of his paintings; Potter painted Tulp's son Dirck (Diederik) as a noble equestrian in 1653. The equestrian portrait was reportedly first a portrait of Johan Maurits (his castle at Cleve visible between the horse's legs), changed to Tulp only after Potter's death -- Dayton/Grove flags the story as "somewhat confused" itself.

He composed a will in January 1653 and died of tuberculosis in Amsterdam, buried 17 January 1654, aged 28 (baptism 20 November 1625 to burial 17 January 1654 is 28 years and about eight weeks; Hofstede de Groot). Family accounts claimed he died of "excessive painting"; his wife said he carried his sketchbook everywhere. Output: about 100 paintings in some thirteen working years (work period 1641-1654), plus about 18-20 etchings, all animal subjects, and a few dozen accepted drawings. Bartholomeus van der Helst's Portrait of Paulus Potter (1654, Mauritshuis) shows him as an elegant gentleman seated before his easel, painted shortly before or after his death.

The father-son confusion is structural. WGA: Pieter "was overshadowed by his son, Paulus Potter, with whose works his have often been confused. Pieter signed his paintings P. Potter, whereas Paulus typically signed Paulus Potter." Hofstede de Groot: pictures later than 1652 cannot be by the father; those dated earlier than 1640 can scarcely be by the son. Houbraken's extra generation -- a painter "Pieter I Potter" making Pieter the grandfather of Paulus -- does not exist in the RKD/NGA/Rijksmuseum accounts; the RKD says Pieter was the direct father. Do not repeat Houbraken's version.

## M1. The low vantage point -- animals against the sky

The signature device, and the one the sources state most plainly. Potter painted his animals from a low viewpoint so they dominate the canvas and stand out against the sky and their surroundings; the Mauritshuis calls the "respectful and detailed portrayal" of farm animals "innovative for his era". The low horizon is the mechanism: the animal's back breaks the horizon line, the sky becomes its backdrop, and a cow or a bull becomes a portrait subject rather than staffage. In the small works the same device runs at cabinet scale -- Two Horses in a Meadow (1649, 23.5 x 30 cm) and Four Cows in a Meadow (1651, 25 x 30 cm) put the animal against the sky in a picture you hold in the hand. The father's landscapes had done the opposite: shepherds and cattle small against the land, the ruin anchoring one side. Paulus inverted the hierarchy his father had built -- the animal grows until it becomes the portrait, and the humans shrink to the edge or leave the picture altogether. Sotheby's, on the son: he made the animals "the focus of his pictures, rather than as a backdrop for human action."

**Takeaway:** build the picture from the ground up -- the camera at the animal's knee height, the horizon low, the animal's back breaking the skyline, the beast filling the frame while the humans shrink to the edge or vanish. The watchable behaviour is the monumentality: a cow at cabinet scale standing as tall as a portrait sitter, the sky behind it doing the work a wall would do in a portrait.

## M2. The about-life-size animal -- The Young Bull

The Bull (1647, Mauritshuis, inv. 136), also called The Young Bull, is 235.5 x 339 cm, oil on canvas -- about life-size, and monumental against a career of mostly small paintings. Mauritshuis: "He completed The Bull at the age of 22." Its fame rests on the life-size everyday farm animal plus extreme detail: a lark in the sky, sunlight on the meadow, the cow's whiskers, tiny flies on the bull's hide, the moist nose, shear-marks on a shorn sheep. Fromentin, in the 1870s, counted it with The Night Watch and The Anatomy Lesson as "the three most famous paintings in the Netherlands"; it was a Louvre sensation in 1795 and a 19th-century "crowd-pleaser".

The 2024-25 Mauritshuis X-ray and infrared research (Schilder and Vandivere, with the Rijksmuseum) overturned the idea that the bull is a portrait of one animal. It is a composite: horns of a two-year-old, teeth of a four-year-old, shoulders of a full-grown adult, hindquarters of a youngster. The first version was a smaller, thinner, sexless bovine; testicles (at first much larger) and the dewlap were added late; the coat pattern was changed. Potter also cut the top edge off the original painting and sewed on canvas strips left, right and top, with fresh ground, after part of the first composition was already painted; the farmer, one or both trees, the ram, sheep and lamb, and the distant landscape (castle, church of Rijswijk) were added only after the enlargement. The first background was closer, with a farm and a hay barn and a fence. Why he enlarged it is not known. Grijzenhout (JHNA 2021) overturned the older provenance: the earliest known mention is the 1749 inventory of Willem Fabricius (Haarlem); the painting "may have been produced, or adapted, as a giant piece of decoration for a private house in The Hague" and "it is unclear whether the painting was a commission." Do NOT present it as a commission for Prince Maurice of Orange -- that claim lives only in the 1911 Britannica and derivatives (leg 1, C1017).

**Takeaway:** build one animal at about life size, and build it as a composite -- the horns of a young bull, the teeth of an older one, the shoulders of an adult, the hindquarters of a youngster, the coat pattern changing as you watch. The watchable behaviour is the assembly: the beast arriving piece by piece, the canvas itself growing wider and taller mid-painting to make room for it, the farm and fence of the first background giving way to the distant church.

## M3. The sketchbook system -- studies from life, reused as motifs

The drawings are the engine of the whole oeuvre, and the sources describe the system precisely. Sotheby's, citing Ben Broos (1994 catalogue): three drawing types. (1) Free studies from life of animals, individually or in small groups, "surely sheets from a dismantled sketchbook or sketchbooks", reused and adapted as motifs in paintings -- examples in the Abrams, Lugt and British Museum collections. (2) Chalk studies directly relating to etchings, intermediate in finish. (3) Ambitious, highly finished, signed drawings -- either independent works or ricordi, slight variants of paintings made so a successful composition would not be lost when the painting left the studio. The sketchbooks themselves are lost; Hofstede de Groot: he "never took a country walk without carrying his sketch-book... Unfortunately, these sketch-books have been lost." The earliest surviving dated drawing, Boar Hunt (1641, graphite on vellum, Stadel), is inscribed "Paulus Potter. f. out 14 jaer ao 1641" -- though he was actually 15 or 16, not 14.

The etchings run on the same system: about 20 plates, all animal subjects. The first, The Cowherd (1643, Bartsch 14), was made at 17-18 -- proof impression, then name and date and "In[ventor] et fecit" added in the second state, forename changed "Pauwelus" to "Paulus" in the third; the plate was reworked in 1649 (cropped 6.5 cm on the left, rectangle to square, three cattle replaced by a pond and a vista). Early graphic style is tight and linear, hatched marks recreating drawing precision; later work is looser, more fluid. Marcus de Bye made etchings after Potter. The drawings show the same hand as the paintings in miniature: in Mercury and Argus (1642, V&A, pencil on parchment, one of about ten known compositional drawings in that medium) he left light edges undescribed and accented dark shadows with crisp lines, often paralleled by a negative "line" of light to suggest reflection.

**Takeaway:** build the animal from a sketchbook, not from a single sitting -- a sheet of studies (a cow lying, a cow standing, a bull's head, a pig in a sty), each study drifting off its page and settling into a painting, the same beast reappearing in a second picture slightly changed. The watchable behaviour is the reuse: the study becoming the motif, the motif returning as a ricordo after the painting has left the studio.

## M4. Reflected light -- the underbelly, the ear, the morning

Potter's light is observed, not invented, and the sources give the observations. Dayton/Grove: careful observation of direct and reflected light -- light reflecting off the ground onto a cow's underbelly, glowing through translucent ears, following a farmer's hat brim; morning light filling the air (Cattle Going out to the Fields in the Morning, 1647, Salzburg). The system is settled by 1647: the small mature works of that year (W05, W07, W08) show the low vantage point, animals silhouetted against sky above a low horizon, dawn or backlight, reflected light on underbellies and translucent ears. The varnish removal in the 2024-25 restoration revealed "vibrant colours, vivid textures and more depth" in The Bull -- the painting had been duller than Potter made it. Fromentin, dissenting in the 19th century, called the painting "monotonous, thick, heavy, dull, and dry" with light "distributed at random" -- a period view, not a palette analysis. No pigment-level palette account was found in any source read; do not invent one (leg 1, C1021).

**Takeaway:** build the light before the animal -- morning light filling the air, the ground throwing light back up onto the underbelly, the sun glowing through a translucent ear, the hat brim catching the same light as the hide. The watchable behaviour is the reflection: the belly lit from below by the meadow itself, the ear going bright as the sun passes behind it, the whole animal lit twice, once from the sky and once from the ground.

## M5. The animal as portrait, the human as staffage

The career turn is dated and documented. Early (1640-43): history and biblical subjects under his father's and Moeyaert's influence -- God Appearing to Abraham at Sichem (1642, W01) is a biblical scene already hijacked by a pair of cows in the centre; compositions borrowed (Moeyaert figures, van Uyttenbroeck landscape) but redistributed, animals and figures pushed to one side to open a deep view. By the mid-1640s he worked almost exclusively on animal pieces in flat, carefully arranged landscapes and farmyards, under the influence of Pieter van Laer (returned from Italy; his 1636 domestic-animal etching series) and Gerrit Claesz. Bleker's prints -- animals grew more monumental, compositions clearer, animals placed at strategic points to lead the eye through a doorway, into the distance, over a hill. The Hague years (late 1640s) brought a more elegant style and subject matter -- elegant couples visiting the countryside (The Reflected Cow, 1648; The Great Farm, 1649); horses entered the repertory around 1650 (Hunting Party, 1653, Woburn Abbey). Later compositions grew "increasingly complex, and also more anecdotal", with narrative and genre elements and somewhat caricatural facial types, van Laer's influence persisting.

The inversion is the point of the study: his father's Jacob Urging Leah and Rachel to Flee from Laban (1638) stages a biblical conversation as a pastoral with livestock nearby; the son, learning in that workshop, saw that the livestock could become the portrait. The father's shepherds talk while the cattle graze; the son's cattle stand and the shepherds, when they appear at all, are small figures at the edge. The 1642 Abraham is the hinge in the son's own work: the biblical scene is still named, but the cows already hold the centre.

**Takeaway:** build the hierarchy flipping -- first the biblical scene with the cows small at the edge, then the same landscape with the cows grown until they hold the centre and the humans shrunk to the edge. The watchable behaviour is the inversion arriving slowly and staying: the staffage becoming the portrait, the portrait never going back.

## M6. The farmyard as stage -- the pissing cow and the court

The farmyard is Potter's stage the way the guardroom was his father's: a bounded, worked space where animals, humans and objects share one light. The Farmyard (1649, Hermitage, GE-820, 81 x 115.5 cm VERIFIED) was commissioned by, or at least bought by, Amalia van Solms-Braunfels; Houbraken says she rejected it because of the urinating cow, and that court ladies advised against the purchase. The anecdote is Houbraken, not a document, but the picture itself is the evidence: a farmyard with a cow urinating in the foreground, the vulgar fact painted with the same miniaturist finish as the bull's whiskers. The farmyard pieces are the middle register of the oeuvre -- larger than the cabinet meadows (81 x 115.5 cm against 25 x 30 cm), smaller than the two giants, and the place where the elegant Hague world meets the farm: elegant couples visiting the countryside, a herdsman with his herd, pigs in a sty (Two Pigs in a Sty, 1649, 32.4 x 45.1 cm). The Bear Hunt (1649, Rijksmuseum SK-A-316, 305 x 338 cm VERIFIED) is the second monumental canvas, likely also a house-decoration piece, one centimetre narrower than The Bull. The manure caked on cattle and the dirt under farmers' nails (Knox; Dayton) are the same miniaturist dedication applied to the unlovely: frogs, lizards, poppies, butterflies enliven the meadows, and the farmyard smells of the farm.

**Takeaway:** build a farmyard as a stage -- the cow in the foreground doing something unlovely and unmissable, the elegant visitors at the edge in their clean clothes, the pigs, the hens, the manure, all painted with the same finish. The watchable behaviour is the court's flinch: the visitors arriving, the cow urinating, the visitors leaving, the farmyard staying exactly as it was.

## M7. The enlarged canvas -- a painting that grows

The Bull is not one painting but a painting that grew, and the growth is visible in the object. The 2024-25 research: Potter cut the top edge off the original painting and sewed on canvas strips left, right and top, with fresh ground, after part of the first composition was already painted; the farmer, one or both trees, the ram, sheep and lamb, and the distant landscape (castle, church of Rijswijk) were added only after the enlargement; the first background was closer, with a farm and a hay barn and a fence. The X-rays show pentimenti throughout -- changed legs, tail, head, back, coat pattern -- painted changes, not underdrawing; no source read describes an underdrawing stage for Potter, so do not claim one. The Bull's first version was a smaller, thinner, sexless bovine; the testicles (at first much larger) and the dewlap were added late. The same growth logic appears in the etchings: The Cowherd plate reworked in 1649, cropped 6.5 cm on the left, rectangle to square, three cattle replaced by a pond and a vista. Potter enlarged and reworked rather than starting again.

**Takeaway:** build a painting that grows while you watch -- the first version small and thin and sexless, the canvas strips sewn on at the edges, the farmer and the trees and the distant church arriving only after the enlargement, the bull's body changing (legs, tail, head, back, coat) as the picture widens. The watchable behaviour is the growth itself: the frame expanding to make room for the animal it contains.

## M8. Twenty-eight years, a hundred pictures -- the short life and the long afterlife

About 100 paintings in thirteen working years, all before 28; the last works are 1653 (Cows Grazing at a Farm; the Dirck Tulp equestrian portrait). He died of tuberculosis, buried 17 January 1654. The afterlife was immediate and long: he directly influenced Karel Dujardin and Adriaen van de Velde, was formative for Aelbert Cuyp's cattle scenes of the 1640s-50s (per the National Gallery; leg 3 warns that no source read names Cuyp as influenced by Potter and no documented contact was found -- treat Cuyp as a parallel, never as a follower), and Albert Klomp (c. 1618-88) was a notable imitator. The animal piece as a sub-genre was taken up by others, including Adriaen van de Velde (already studied as #538 -- do not re-pick). The 19th century made him famous: The Bull as one of the three most famous paintings in the Netherlands (Fromentin), the Louvre sensation of 1795, the Mauritshuis crowd-pleaser. The father, by contrast, has no school: his afterlife is in the son's animals and in the guardroom genre. The study's honest limit is the one the legs kept hitting: no image was visually inspected, no pigment analysis exists, no underdrawing is documented, and the RKD entry -- the single most important source for the Delft 1646 date and the family -- was not read.

**Takeaway:** build a career at high speed -- a hundred pictures in thirteen years, the small cabinet pieces arriving one after another, the two giants rising among them, and then the work stopping at 28, the sketchbook carried everywhere and then lost. The watchable behaviour is the pace: the pictures arriving faster than the years pass, the bull growing, the farmyard filling, and the afterlife -- the imitators, the etchings after him, the Louvre crowd -- arriving after the painter has stopped.

## Aesthetic logic

Potter's aesthetic is the animal made monumental by looking up at it. Everything is a portrait, and the portrait's subject is a beast: the low vantage point puts the animal against the sky the way a portrait puts a sitter against a wall; the about-life-size bull is a state portrait of a farm animal; the cabinet meadows are portrait miniatures of cows. The detail is miniaturist -- flies on the hide, shear-marks on a shorn sheep, manure caked on cattle, dirt under farmers' nails, frogs and lizards and poppies in the meadow -- but the scale is not miniature: the same hand that painted the 25 x 30 cm Four Cows in a Meadow painted the 235.5 x 339 cm Bull. The light is the second half of the thesis: morning light filling the air, the ground throwing light back up onto the underbelly, the sun glowing through a translucent ear -- the animal lit twice, once from the sky and once from the ground. The career is a single inversion, learned in his father's workshop and then turned against it: the father painted shepherds with cattle as staffage, the son painted cattle with shepherds as staffage, and the inversion never went back. The farmyard is the stage where the two worlds meet -- the elegant Hague visitors and the urinating cow, the court and the manure, in one light. And the pictures themselves grow: the canvas sewn wider mid-painting, the plate cropped and reworked six years later, the same motif returning as a ricordo after the painting has left the studio. The brevity is part of the logic -- a hundred pictures before 28, the sketchbook always carried, the sketchbooks lost -- and the afterlife is the proof that the inversion held: Dujardin, van de Velde, Klomp, and a 19th century that counted a bull among the three most famous paintings in the Netherlands.

## Network

The family knot: father Pieter Symonsz Potter (study #573), first teacher, Amsterdam studio, 1630s; mother Aechtie (Aaltje) Paulusdr Bartsius, sister of Willem Bartsius (c. 1612 Enkhuizen - in or after 1639), history painter of six signed works, the brother-in-law in the shadows (leg 3); through the mother, connections to the von Egmont and Semeyns families, office-holders in Enkhuizen and at the court in The Hague. Bartsius may have been initiated into painting by Pieter Potter in Enkhuizen (Oud Holland article, suggested not proven). The Codde's-wife affair story (father's affair with Pieter Codde's wife after the mother's death, 1636) is single-source gossip from EN Wikipedia, uncited in the excerpt -- not used in this study (legs 1 and 3).

The teachers: Jacob de Wet (c. 1610 Haarlem - 1675 per Jager 2018; EN Wikipedia gives "between 1675 and 1691"), whose notebook lists 34 pupils, "most famously Paulus Potter" -- the strongest documented teacher link after the father. Claes Moeyaert (1591 WGA / 1592 Wikipedia - 1655), Pre-Rembrandtist history painter, influence certain, pupilage "may". Gerrit Claesz. Bleker (fl. 1625-56) and Pieter van Laer: formative influences on the turn to animals; Bleker's etchings shaped the Cowherd (1643) and Shepherd (1644); van Laer's animal prints (1636) taught him to use animals "to define space" (legs 1 and 3).

The landlord: Jan van Goyen (1596-1656), Hague landlord 1649-52, studio at 17 Dunne Bierkade, the house where Potter met his wife. Van Goyen is the best-evidenced adjacent artist and is held back as the obvious next-but-one hop -- do NOT pick for #575 (leg 3).

The patrons: Amalia van Solms-Braunfels (The Farmyard); Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen (interest, visits; the equestrian portrait was reportedly first his); Nicolaes Tulp (Amsterdam invitation, owner of several paintings, father of the sitter in the 1653 equestrian portrait). Marriage into the Hague elite: Adriana van Balckeneynde, daughter of court architect Claes Dircksz. van Balckeneynde; a cousin counsellor to Prince William II; Willem Piso, family friend and Johan Maurits's personal physician (leg 3).

The followers: Karel Dujardin and Adriaen van de Velde (already studied as #538 -- do not re-pick) show the influence of the small animal paintings; Albert Klomp (c. 1618-88), notable imitator; Marcus de Bye (1639-c. 1690) made prints after Potter. No documented pupils of Paulus himself (leg 3). Aelbert Cuyp (bapt. 20 October 1620 - buried 15 November 1691): parallel, NOT evidenced influence -- Cuyp painted Landscape with Cattle c. 1639 at about nineteen, before Paulus's mature animal pieces; no documented contact; never present him as a follower (leg 3).

The geography as network: Enkhuizen (birth, to 1628) -- Leiden (1628-31, father's glass guild; van Goyen neighbour) -- Amsterdam (from 1631; father's studio; de Wet in Haarlem 1641) -- Delft guild 1646 (institutional, residence unproven) -- The Hague (1649-52; van Goyen's house; court patrons; marriage) -- Amsterdam (1652-54; Tulp; death). Every move is a network move (leg 3).

## Corrections

C1011. Doorway -- 195th check, HOLD substance / FAIL letter, holds stay 139, non-holds move 62 to 63; tally arithmetic flagged, gap carried (see C863).

C1012. D4 repair -- "by 22 had painted The Young Bull, the life-size animal piece that made his father's name a footnote" replaced by "at 22 painted The Young Bull, the about-life-size animal piece whose fame left his father's name a footnote": "at 22" is the Mauritshuis's wording; "about-life-size" is what the sources say; the causal "that made" is replaced because no source ties the father's eclipse to The Bull specifically (leg 3).

C1013. Age at death -- 28, not 29. Most modern sources (EN Wikipedia, Mauritshuis, NGA, Knox) say 28; Dayton/Grove ("died aged only 29"), the 1911 Britannica and Sotheby's ("died before his 29th birthday") say 29. Arithmetic from baptism 20 November 1625 to burial 17 January 1654 gives 28 years and about eight weeks (Hofstede de Groot). Use 28; the "29" is inclusive/rounded counting (leg 1).

C1014. Death date -- 17 January 1654 is the BURIAL date, the only documented date; the 1911 Britannica and Prabook give "died on the 15th of January". Several secondary sites conflate burial with death ("died on January 17"). Correct form: died January 1654, buried 17 January 1654 (leg 1).

C1015. Jacob de Wet -- May 1641, not 1642. Grove/Dayton (May 1641, "P. Potter" in de Wet's notebook) vs Universal Compendium ("In 1642, Potter studied under Jacob de Wet the Elder in Haarlem"). The 1641 notebook record is the stronger, primary-adjacent evidence; 1642 appears to be a rounding/derivative error. Note the task brief's "Jacob de Wet in Haarlem 1642" is the weaker variant (legs 1 and 3).

C1016. The Bull vs The Young Bull -- ONE work, not two. The Mauritshuis calls the 1647 monumental painting The Bull; Dayton/Grove and Sotheby's call the same painting The Young Bull; EN Wikipedia treats them as two different works ("The Young Bull (circa 1647)... is not to be confused with his work The Bull") -- this appears to be a Wikipedia error, or a confusion with another work such as the Northbrook Young Reddish-Brown Bull (1647). The 235.5 x 339 cm Mauritshuis painting is one work; use "The Bull (1647), also called The Young Bull" (legs 1 and 2).

C1017. "Maurice, Prince of Orange" as patron of The Bull -- REPORTED only in the 1911 Britannica and derivatives. Not corroborated by the Mauritshuis, Dayton/Grove or Grijzenhout; Grijzenhout (2021) overturns the older provenance (earliest known mention the 1749 Fabricius inventory; possibly a house decoration, commission unclear). Do NOT present it as a commission for Prince Maurice (leg 1).

C1018. First signed works -- 1641 vs 1643. Knox: "first signed works date from 1641, when he was aged barely fifteen"; Universal Compendium: "first signed painting, The Herdsman, dates from 1643." Both can be true if 1641 refers to drawings (Boar Hunt, 1641) and 1643 to paintings/etchings, but the distinction is not made explicit in the sources; flag rather than assert (leg 1).
