# Hiroshi Ishii: Study Notes

**Site:** Hiroshi Ishii -- Japanese computer scientist, founder of tangible user interfaces; Jerome B. Wiesner Professor of Media Arts and Sciences at the MIT Media Lab, Associate Director of the Media Lab, and Director and founder of the Tangible Media Group, which he founded on joining the Media Lab in October 1995. Born 1956 in Tokyo, raised in Sapporo; the day and month, 4 February, are reported but NOT verified -- they rest on a biographical-encyclopedia page title (Prabook) and Ishii's own 2008 slide deck ("born 2/4/56"), while MIT's own biography and Wikipedia give the year only, so 4 February is carried here as reported, not as fact. Living and active: AVI 2026 keynote in Venice in June 2026, IDSA Special Achievement Award received in person on Wednesday 19 August 2026 in Pasadena, teaching at MIT in Spring 2026. [C35989]

**Relevance:** Ishii helps investigate tangibility: what happens when the code leaves the screen and becomes something the hand can move -- can a computational system be grasped, pushed, and felt rather than merely read? His evidence is a specific documented corpus: the Tangible Bits / tangible user interface body of work -- inFORM (an actuated-pin dynamic shape display), TRANSFORM, the Urp urban-planning table, SandScape, musicBottles, Triangles, curlybot, Topobo, and the "Radical Atoms" vision -- where computation is embodied in physical material, offering transferable mechanisms (actuated grids, physical constraints as computation, material state as display, recorded gesture as memory). [C35990]

**Doorway:** The 445th doorway. Hop from the #824 Giorgia Lupi study, which ends on data made legible, describable, hearable, teachable -- who the code is for. Ishii asks the next question after accessibility: tangibility -- what the code can be touched as. Lupi's code is drawn on a postcard and read by a stranger; Ishii's code is a bottle you uncork, a building model that casts a real shadow, a pin field that rises to meet your hand. Fifth-study rule: #825 is a multiple of five, and tangible media is a different visual language from the last four studies -- #821 Kunibert Fritz (concrete painting), #822 Mathura Govindarajan (data and accessibility technology), #823 Andrew McWhae (generative prints), #824 Giorgia Lupi (data visualisation) -- so the sequence turns here from pictures of data to matter that computes. [C35991]

**Depth:** Twenty-five works inspected in depth through their Tangible Media Group project pages (text read end to end), the MIT Media Lab overview and biography pages, and the CHI '97 Tangible Bits paper, the CHI '99 Urp paper, the CHI 2001 musicBottles paper, the UIST 2013 inFORM paper record, and the Interactions 2012 Radical Atoms paper. Honest accounting: no video was watched by any leg (every Vimeo embed was present as a link, none played), no project image was rendered as pixels (badge and Flickr URLs were listed, not viewed), and no audio was heard (musicBottles, Audiopad, PingPongPlus, MirrorFugue all make sound central) -- so every claim about motion, timing, sound, and colour in this study is text-only, attributed to the project pages and papers, never observed. Twelve further works (JamJar, PICO, Actuated Workbench, GeoScape, Furp, Tangible CityScape, ChainFORM, LineFORM, aeroMorph, HERMITS, inDepth, Dream Brush 2026) were confirmed on the TMG projects index but not inspected in depth. No work was handled in person; all are archived lab prototypes or exhibition pieces. All weekdays stated in this study were verified with `date -d` in America/New_York. [C35992]

## The question: what can the code be touched as?

Lupi's systems are open at the far end: the picture is not finished until a stranger can decode it. Ishii's systems are open at the hand: the picture is not finished until someone picks it up. His founding claim, from the CHI '97 paper with Brygg Ullmer, is that digital information should be given physical form -- graspable objects in the foreground and ambient media in the background -- and every work after 1997 is an instance of one or both channels. The question he hands the gallery is a reversal of the usual generative premise: instead of a screen that shows a computation, a material that IS the computation. A bottle is the track and the switch; a building model is the data and its shadow; a pin field is the display and the constraint. The gallery has pieces you watch and pieces you disturb; Ishii asks for pieces where the disturbance is the picture -- where the hand's work and the system's reply are the same event, and where the material pushes back. [C35993]

## A life in tangibility: NTT, Toronto, MIT

Ishii was born in Tokyo in 1956 and raised in Sapporo. He took all three degrees at Hokkaido University: B.E. in electronic engineering 1978, M.E. in computer engineering 1980, Ph.D. in computer engineering 1992. He was a visiting research associate at GMD in Bonn in 1986-87, then led the CSCW (Computer-Supported Cooperative Work) research group at NTT Human Interface Laboratories in Yokosuka from 1988 to 1994, where his team invented TeamWorkStation (1990) and ClearBoard (1992) -- shared drawing surfaces for remote collaborators, the prehistory of the tangible programme. He was a Visiting Assistant Professor at the University of Toronto's Computer Systems Research Institute in 1993-94, overlapping the end of the NTT period. The NTT start date itself is unverified (commonly stated as 1980, after the B.E.) and is not asserted here. [C35994]

In October 1995 he joined the MIT Media Laboratory as Professor of Media Arts and Sciences and founded the Tangible Media Group the same year, beginning the ongoing Tangible Bits project. He was Associate Professor 1995-2001, awarded tenure in July 2001, co-director of the Things That Think consortium since July 2002, Associate Director of the Media Lab from May 2008, and holder of the Jerome B. Wiesner Professorship since April 2011 (an older MIT bio page still calls him Muriel R. Cooper Professor -- that was an earlier named chair). The visions came in sequence: Tangible Bits (CHI '97, with Brygg Ullmer), Radical Atoms (first presented at a CHI 2009 panel on Thursday 9 April 2009 in Boston; full paper in ACM Interactions, January-February 2012), TeleAbsence (concept formulated 2020-21; adopted as the headline third vision in 2025). His honours include election to the CHI Academy in 2006, the ACM SIGCHI Lifetime Research Award in 2019 (lecture "Making Digital Tangible", Monday 6 May 2019, CHI 2019, Glasgow), the Japan ACM SIGCHI Chapter Lifetime Community Contribution Award in 2022 (lecture "Evolving Visions", Friday 11 February 2022), ACM Fellow (class of 2022), and the IDSA Special Achievement Award 2026, received Wednesday 19 August 2026 in Pasadena. His 2026 activity, re-verified fresh this run: CMU Design Nexus talk "Vision-Driven Design: From Tangible Bits to Radical Atoms to TeleAbsence", Monday 6 April 2026, McConomy Auditorium; a CHI 2026 talk "Human Arc -- TeleAbsence", Tuesday 14 April 2026, Barcelona; Media Lab Immersion Session at Sant Pau, Barcelona, 17 April 2026; AVI 2026 keynote "Vision-Driven Design", San Servolo, Venice, 8-12 June 2026; teaching MAS.S62 TeleAbsence in Spring 2026. His motto, on his MIT overview page and in the AVI abstract: "Be Artistic and Analytic. Be Poetic and Pragmatic. Be Romantic and Realistic." [C35995]

## M1. The actuated height-field: the display that pushes back (inFORM, TRANSFORM, Relief, Recompose)

inFORM (2013, UIST 2013, with Daniel Leithinger, Sean Follmer and colleagues) is a Dynamic Shape Display: a 30 by 30 grid of 900 motorised white polystyrene pins in a 381 by 381 mm area, each pin 9.525 mm square with 3.175 mm spacing and up to 100 mm of travel, driven by push-pull rods from slide potentiometers below, with a Kinect depth camera for input, a projector overlaying graphics on the pin surface, and variable-stiffness haptic feedback. The paper names three uses: dynamic physical affordances (pins rise to form a button or a handle where one is needed), dynamic physical constraints (pins rise as walls that guide or block the hand), and object actuation (pins push passive objects around the surface). Relief (2009) is its precursor at about one-seventh the pin count (120 motorised pins under a malleable skin, each sensing push and pull, open-source hardware and software). TRANSFORM (2014) scales the idea to furniture: three dynamic shape displays, more than 1,000 pins in total -- the precise count is not stated here because published figures conflict (1,152 vs 1,500) -- first shown at Lexus Design Amazing, Milan Design Week, April 2014, with viewer kinetic energy in the room driving wave motion. Recompose (2011) adds the body as input: direct pressing on the surface plus hand gestures above it, used together. [C35996]

The mechanism is a height-field that is both display and constraint, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- the pin counts and dimensions are documented; the grid sizes and speeds below are the leg's translation to a browser piece). State: a grid H[i][j] in [0, 1], N = 24 to 40 per side, each pin with a capped slew speed so pins cannot teleport. Drivers, at least two live at once: travelling waves from two to four sources (origin, wavelength, speed, amplitude, decay with distance); an input field (the pointer depresses or raises a Gaussian bump); an object field (discs resting on the field dent it and are cradled at their rims). Coupling rule, the Ishii move: the field is both display and constraint -- a disc rolls downhill on the gradient of H, so waves move the discs and the discs dent the field, which changes the waves' local shape. Discrete state transitions: when a disc comes to rest in a basin, a ring of pins rises briefly (BASIN -> RING-UP 0.6 s -> HOLD 1.0 s -> SUBSIDE); when two discs approach within distance D, a wall of pins rises between them (APPROACH -> WALL-UP -> WALL-HOLD -> WALL-DOWN). Constraints: H stays in [0, 1]; adjacent pins differ by at most a capped amount per frame (no cliffs steeper than the mechanism allows); total displaced volume sum(H) is conserved within a band unless a wave source injects energy. If the pointer is idle the waves continue -- the system is alive -- but no new affordance rings fire. [C35997]

**Takeaway:** the rising wall is the most Ishii image in this study: a constraint built by the system in response to the objects, visible as it happens, not drawn by the artist. A pin grid that only plays a sine wave is a screensaver, not inFORM -- the test is whether the material pushes back, and whether the push is computed from the state rather than scripted. [C35998]

## M2. Tokens constraining a simulation: the shadow is the clock (Urp)

Urp (CHI '99, Underkoffler and Ishii, built on the 1998 I/O Bulb -- a lightbulb that both projects and captures video of the surface below) is a luminous-tangible workbench for urban planning. Physical building models sit on a table; a projector draws light exactly onto and around them. A physical clock tool sets the time of day, and the models cast digital shadows accurate for that sun position. A material wand binds brick or glass to a building -- glass facades throw reflections as well as shadows. A compass sets wind direction, a wind tool starts a projected wind-flow simulation that bends around the models, and an anemometer probe reads wind speed at a point. Moving or rotating a model transforms its shadow, reflection, and wind immediately; planners found high wind between adjacent buildings and verified it with the probe. The claim that rotating a glass token by five degrees swings its reflection streak across the table is attributed to the Urp paper and was not independently re-verified. [C35999]

The mechanism is a simulation whose inputs are physical poses, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- the tools and behaviours are documented; the exact formulas are the leg's). State: tokens with position, rotation, footprint, height, and material (brick or glass); a sun with azimuth and elevation driven by a clock variable t in [0, 24); a wind vector with direction and speed. Shadow rule: each token casts a shadow polygon -- its footprint extruded along the sun ground vector by length h / tan(elevation), clipped to the table; overlapping shadows do not darken further. At low sun (morning, evening) shadows are long and overlap heavily; at noon they shrink to near the footprint -- time of day is a shape parameter, not a colour grade. Reflection rule (glass only): a parallelogram streak from the sun-facing face, length proportional to height and to the angle between face normal and sun azimuth, brightness falling with distance. Wind rule: a flow field from the wind vector and the tokens as obstacles -- base flow everywhere, reduced speed in wake cones behind tokens, and AMPLIFIED speed in a gap between two tokens whose separation is less than 2.5 token widths and whose line is roughly perpendicular to the wind (the venturi or channelling effect the planners actually found), visualised as streaks or particles advected by the field, never as a static arrow field. Probe rule: a probe token reads the flow at its position, numerically and by streak length, and the reading must change when a building moves, or the coupling is fake. Tools are state, not buttons: the clock is a dial you drag, the compass a token you rotate, the wand toggles material on touch. Constraints: shadows are geometric, derived from token pose and sun only, never hand-drawn; tokens cannot overlap; wind amplification is capped; reflection streaks exist only for glass and only on the sun side. [C36000]

**Takeaway:** Urp is the cleanest translation in this study because its image is entirely derived from the geometry of its own tokens -- there is nothing to draw that the simulation does not compute. The five-degree rotation moment (if the paper's example holds) is the discovery a viewer makes by playing: a tiny physical gesture with a huge optical consequence. [C36001]

## M3. Continuous material with projected analysis: the sand answers back (SandScape, Illuminating Clay)

SandScape (developed summer 2002; exhibited at the Ars Electronica Center in 2003, which is why the TMG index files it under 2003) is a sandbox of sand and glass beads lit from below with infrared; an IR camera reads the surface geometry, and simulations -- height, slope, contours, shadows, drainage, aspect -- are projected in real time onto the sand as the user reshapes it. Illuminating Clay (CHI 2002, Piper, Ratti, Ishii) is the same loop in clay: a laser scanner reads the sculpted surface in real time, and landscape simulations (shadow casting, erosion, visibility, travel time) are projected back onto the clay. Ishii and colleagues call these "continuous TUIs": rapid form-giving in a soft material plus computational feedback. The material is the model; there is no separate model file to edit. [C36002]

The mechanism is a continuous height-field with live analysis, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- the simulation list is documented; the brush and conservation rules are the leg's). State: a height-field H[x][y] on a grid (64 to 128 per side), continuous values, NOT discrete tokens -- the deliberate contrast with M2. Sculpting rule: the pointer drags a Gaussian brush that adds or removes material, conserving volume locally: material removed from a pit is deposited at the brush rim, so total volume stays within tolerance; a light smoothing pass each frame gives sand its angle of repose, so slopes steeper than a threshold slump. Analysis modes, exactly one visible at a time: CONTOUR (iso-lines of H that bunch where slope is steep), SLOPE (shade by gradient magnitude), DRAINAGE (rain falls uniformly, each cell passes water to its lowest neighbour, accumulated flow drawn as blue lines thickening downstream, basins filling), SHADOW (hillshade from H), HEIGHT (hypsometric tint bands). The analysis is recomputed from H every frame; it is never a static overlay. Mode switching: the piece cycles modes on its own slow clock (about every 20 s) and the user can switch with a deliberate gesture, fading one analysis into the next over one second while H is unchanged. Constraints: H in [0, 1]; volume conserved (drift over 2% triggers renormalisation); drainage flow is conservative; contour lines are closed or exit at the edge, never dangle mid-field. [C36003]

**Takeaway:** the SandScape test is unmistakable and unfakeable: sculpt a ridge, and the drainage lines must reroute around it within a second. A height-field you can sculpt with no analysis projected back is a sandbox toy, not SandScape; an analysis that is a pre-baked texture is a poster. The mechanism is the feedback loop, not the sand. [C36004]

## M4. The container is the control: uncorking releases a stream (musicBottles)

musicBottles (CHI 2001, Ishii, Mazalek, Lee; TMG index 1999) uses three corked glass bottles as containers and controls for digital information. Each bottle holds one instrument's track -- violin, cello, and piano in Edouard Lalo's Piano Trio in C Minor, Op. 7 (confirmed; the additional classical, jazz, and techno sets mentioned in the paper were not re-verified). Electromagnetic tags identify each bottle; placing a bottle on the table's stage area produces a coloured aura as feedback that it is active; opening the bottle (removing the cork) starts its track, reinserting the cork stops it. Multiple synchronised audio streams run in parallel, and the table carries dynamic coloured lighting that follows pitch and volume. The sound was never heard by any leg; all audio claims are text-only. [C36005]

The mechanism is a phase-locked stream released by a physical switch, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- the container, stage, cork, and synchrony are documented; the visual translation to ribbons is the leg's, since the gallery is a visual medium). State: vessels, each with position (on or off stage), open or closed, a stream identity (a visual voice: a line quality, a particle behaviour, a colour), a phase in a shared loop, and amplitude in [0, 1]. Stage rule: a vessel counts only inside the stage region; entering fades its aura in over 0.5 s, leaving fades it out, and an open vessel's stream fades out too. Uncork rule, the core transition: CLOSED -> OPEN is a discrete event (cork lifts, 0.4 s); on opening the stream starts at the shared loop's current phase -- all streams are synchronised to one clock, so they are voices in one piece, not independent loops -- amplitude ramping 0 to 1 over 1.5 s; on closing, 1 to 0 over 1.0 s. The cork is the only switch; there is no other mute button. Stream rendering: each open vessel emits a continuous visual stream from its mouth -- a ribbon or particle flow whose path, speed, and density encode that voice (long smooth ribbon, slow wide ribbon, discrete pulses) -- flowing toward a common centre where streams interleave and braid (draw order alternating by phase) rather than erasing each other. Parameters: three vessels (the original number; cap at five), a 16-second shared loop, the ramp times above, stage radius. Constraints: at most one stream per vessel; streams phase-locked to the shared clock (opening mid-loop joins mid-phrase, never restarts the piece); total visual density capped so the centre stays legible when all vessels are open. The piece is alive with zero vessels open: the stage shows a faint idle shimmer and the shared clock keeps running, so opening a bottle later joins a piece already in progress. [C36006]

**Takeaway:** the cork as the sole switch is the most literal Ishii gesture in this study: the container is the control, and restraint is the aesthetic -- three voices, one clock, and the only action is opening and closing. The test is phase: open two bottles at different times and their streams must be in phase with each other, not each restarting. [C36007]

## M5. Constructive assembly: adjacency is syntax (Triangles)

Triangles (Gorbet, Orth, Ishii, CHI '98, 18-23 April 1998, Los Angeles -- the paper is CHI '98 only; no DIS '97 Triangles paper exists) is a kit of identical flat plastic equilateral triangles, each with a microprocessor and magnetic, electrically conducting edge connectors. Connecting two triangles forms a communication circuit; each triangle reports its identity and relative position to the host, and specific connections trigger specific digital events. Users build 2D tilings and 3D polyhedra; the physical configuration IS the digital topography. The triangle's abstract form is deliberate: it carries no everyday semantics, so meaning comes only from arrangement. [C36008]

The mechanism is a graph whose edges are physical connections, stated as rules (leg formalisation). State: triangular tiles, each with a fixed identity (a value, voice, or colour family) and three edges; a graph G with nodes as tiles and edges as shared-edge connections, always planar in the 2D piece. Connection rule: edge-to-edge only, at 60-degree multiples, snapped to a triangular lattice; CONNECT (snap, 0.15 s, visual pulse) and DISCONNECT (pull apart beyond a threshold) are discrete events; partial overlaps are refused -- a tile either snaps to a free lattice edge or returns to where it was. Event rule: meaning is in the graph, not in a single tile. Local patterns map to events: a closed loop of three tiles (a larger triangle) is one event; a straight chain of four is another; a tile with all three edges connected (a hub) is a third; a cycle of six (a hexagon) is a fourth. When a pattern forms it fires once and is marked so it does not refire every frame; when the pattern breaks its effect ends. Effects must be visible in the field: a closed triangle starts a slow rotation of its interior pattern; a chain becomes a conduit carrying a pulse from one end to the other; a hub pulses outward. Parameters: 9 to 15 tiles (enough for two patterns at once, few enough to stay legible), lattice spacing, snap radius. Constraints: tiles identical in shape (only identity differs); connections only edge-to-edge; a connection is binary and mutual, with no dangling half-edge; a pattern fires only on formation, never continuously. [C36009]

**Takeaway:** Triangles is the quietest mechanism in this study and the most exacting: most arrangements do nothing, and only the patterns the system recognises light up. That refusal is the point -- the viewer learns the language by playing, because the system will not reward a random pile. [C36010]

## M6. Record and replay: gesture as memory (curlybot, Topobo)

curlybot (Frei, Su, Mikhak, and Ishii; archival paper CHI 2000, 1-6 April 2000 -- SIGGRAPH '99 was an Emerging Technologies demo, not the paper, and Bakhtiar Mikhak is a co-author) is a small toy that records how it is moved -- pauses, accelerations, shaking -- and replays the gesture indefinitely, drawing expressive patterns with motors that retrace the logged path. Topobo (2004) generalises the idea to assemblies: snap together Passive (static) and Active (motorised) parts, build an animal, twist its body to teach it to walk or gesture, and it replays the motion in a loop. Input and output coincide in the same parts -- Ishii calls this "coincident I/O". The creature's gait is taught, not coded. [C36011]

The mechanism is a recorded gesture with drift and forgetting, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- record and replay are documented; the drift and forgetting rules are the leg's artistic addition, flagged as such). State: a gesture is a time series P(t) = (x, y, theta) sampled at 30 Hz while recording, plus a speed profile, maximum length 12 s, stored as a closed loop only if its end is near its start. Record rule: RECORDING starts on a deliberate gesture (press and hold the agent, then move) and ends on release or at maximum length; while recording the path is drawn as a fading trail; the recorded path is simplified (Douglas-Peucker, tolerance epsilon) so playback keeps the shape, not the noise. Playback rule: an agent retraces P(t) at recorded speed, leaving a fresh trail that decays; states IDLE -> RECORDING -> PLAYING -> IDLE; during PLAYING the agent cannot be dragged (it is performing); a second agent can record while the first plays, so two gestures can overlap and interfere visually. Drift and memory rule: each replay adds a small deterministic perturbation (about 2% of path amplitude, seeded per replay count), so the tenth replay is recognisably the same gesture but visibly worn; after N replays (6 to 10) the gesture "forgets" itself -- amplitude decays to zero and it must be re-recorded. Constraints: only one gesture per agent; a gesture shorter than 0.5 s or 20 px is discarded; playback speed equals recorded speed (no time-stretching); the agent stays inside the field. [C36012]

**Takeaway:** this is the only mechanism in the study about memory as a physical process -- not a loop, but a wearing-out. Perfect infinite replay would be a loop, not a memory; the test is that the fifth replay must differ visibly from the first, and that the moment the agent stops and waits to be taught again is the piece. [C36013]

## M7. Tokens in constraints: syntax made physical (token + constraint)

Tokens are discrete physical objects representing digital information; constraints are confining regions mapped to digital operations, frequently embodied as structures that mechanically channel token movement, often limiting it to a single degree of freedom. The framework is Ullmer, Ishii, and Jacob, ACM TOCHI vol. 12 no. 1, pp. 81-118, March 2005; the tangible query interfaces paper is Ullmer, Ishii, and Jacob, INTERACT 2003, pp. 279-286 -- not UIST '98, which does not exist in the record. Two phases: association (a token placed in a constraint) and manipulation (a token moved inside it). In tangible query interfaces, tokens represent database parameters and constraints map token compositions to queries, views, and Boolean operations. Constraints physically express what interactions are and are not supported. [C36014]

The mechanism is a filter whose rule is legible in the hardware, stated as rules (leg formalisation). State: tokens, each carrying a value (a number, a category, a range); constraints, each a region (slot, rack, ring, track) with a mapping function from token configuration to an output. Association rule: a token is IN a constraint iff its centre lies inside the region; entering fires ASSOCIATE (0.2 s snap), leaving fires DISSOCIATE; a constraint accepts only compatible tokens (shape and size match), and an incompatible token is refused (slides back out) -- compatibility is visible, the constraint's opening matching the token's shape. Manipulation rule: once associated, the token's position along the constraint's single degree of freedom maps to a parameter (along a linear rack, position p in [0, 1] maps to a threshold; rotation inside a ring maps to an angle); the output updates continuously while the token moves and holds when it stops. Composition rule: the output is a function of the WHOLE configuration -- two tokens in two racks define a range (low, high); a token in an AND slot plus a token in an OR slot compose a Boolean filter over a visible field of marks, which light up or dim accordingly; moving any token re-filters immediately. Parameters: 3 to 5 tokens, 2 to 3 constraints, each with one degree of freedom. Constraints: a token belongs to at most one constraint at a time; a constraint holds at most its capacity (usually one token, or two for a range rack); incompatible tokens cannot associate; the filter function is deterministic and shown (a label reads "AND" / "OR" / the numeric range). [C36015]

**Takeaway:** M7 is formalised here for the record but is not a standalone piece in this study: as a visual artwork it reads as a control panel -- the field of marks is the image and the tokens are the UI, and the two never fuse into one picture the way ISH2's shadows or ISH3's drainage do. Its place is folded into ISH2 as an extension (a rack setting which analysis the city shows), where the constraint is part of the picture rather than its panel. [C36016]

## M8. Radical Atoms: the material itself as interface (vision, 2012)

Radical Atoms (Ishii, Lakatos, Bonanni, Labrune, ACM Interactions, January-February 2012) is a vision, not a built device: a hypothetical material that can change form and appearance dynamically, as reconfigurable as pixels, and is bidirectionally coupled to a digital model -- physical change is reflected in digital state in real time and vice versa. The material can "conform to constraints, and inform the users of their affordances". The iceberg metaphor: the graphical interface shows only the surface; the tangible interface shows the tip; Radical Atoms raises the whole iceberg. The Material User Interface (MUI): the material itself is the interface. Later work in this line includes (Dis)Appearables (Nakagaki et al., CHI '22, 29 April-5 May 2022, New Orleans): actuated tangibles that appear and disappear by stage. inFORM, Relief, TRANSFORM, and bioLogic are all presented as steps toward the vision; bioLogic (2015) pursues it through living material rather than motors -- Bacillus subtilis natto cells that expand and contract with humidity, bio-printed into a "Second Skin" garment whose flaps open around heat and sweat zones, made with New Balance, CHI 2015 Best Talk and Best Paper Honourable Mention. [C36017]

The mechanism is a bidirectional coupling with rising and sinking affordances, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- the vision is documented; this formulation stages the vision, since the material is hypothetical and the piece must not pretend to be it). State: a material field of cells, each with form (height, shape) and appearance (colour, pattern), plus a set of affordances (places where action is possible), each with a visibility value in [0, 1]. Bidirectional rule: any change to the digital model must produce a visible material change within 0.5 s, and any user deformation of the material must update the digital model (a readout, a count, a new target) within the same frame -- if one direction is missing it is animation, not Radical Atoms. Affordance rule: affordances RISE when they are relevant and SINK when they are not; relevance is computed -- an affordance for action A is visible iff the current state is within the precondition of A (a "divide" affordance appears only when a form exceeds a size threshold; a "merge" affordance appears only when two forms touch), with hysteresis (appear at threshold plus epsilon, disappear at threshold minus epsilon) so affordances do not flicker. Stage rule (from (Dis)Appearables): the piece has stages (FORM -> DIVIDE -> MERGE -> REST); in each stage a different subset of affordances is present, and transitions are triggered by the material reaching a condition (total area exceeding a bound), not by a timer alone. Constraints: form and model stay in sync (a displayed number always equals the measured form); affordances never all vanish at once (at least one action is always available); stage transitions are announced by the material itself (a form rises to mark the change), not by a text label alone. [C36018]

**Takeaway:** M8 is also formalised for the record rather than shipped as a standalone piece: staged on its own it is a morphing field with pop-up affordances -- too close to a generic morphing blob with buttons -- and its strongest concrete instances (inFORM, TRANSFORM) are already ISH1. Its design rule survives inside ISH1: the affordance rings and walls are M8's "inform the user of affordances" in miniature. [C36019]

## The arc: bits get a body, the body becomes matter, matter moves itself, moving matter means something

What stayed constant from 1995 to 2026: the tabletop as the primary stage (even the room-scale and furniture works are tables or rooms you stand around); direct manipulation with the hands, no menus, no screen as the primary channel (screens appear as overlays, never as the work); the demo scenario, a small narrative carrying each system (two planners, a trio, a dog that learns to walk); vision-led research, each phase announced as a vision first and the artefacts following as steps toward it; and collaboration as the norm -- almost every work lists three to eight named collaborators, with Ishii's role as direction and concept, not sole making. [C36020]

What changed is what the material can do, in four phases. Phase 1, Tangible Bits, 1995-2001: the work gives bits a body on a table or in a room -- metaDESK, ambientROOM, Triangles, inTouch, Urp, PingPongPlus, curlybot, musicBottles -- rigid physical tokens, a projector or sensor rig doing the digital work, the computer underneath or above the table, never inside the object. Phase 2, continuous material and kinetic memory, 2002-2008: the token dissolves into a material you can shape -- Illuminating Clay and SandScape replace discrete models with clay and sand the computer reads as a height-field and paints with simulation; Topobo and curlybot's kinetic memory make motion itself recordable and teachable, and input and output coincide in the same object; I/O Brush makes the world the palette. Phase 3, Radical Atoms and actuation, 2009-2016: the display itself moves -- Relief, inFORM, Recompose, ZeroN (a ball levitated and steered by computer in 3D space, which the person can also grab), TRANSFORM as furniture and spectacle -- and in parallel the material programme widens past motors into living matter (bioLogic, 2015) and soft materials. Phase 4, meaning, memory, and absence, 2013-2021 and after: MirrorFugue (2010-2013; MirrorFugue I 2010, II 2011, 2013 iteration) stages presence as a ghost -- a grand piano whose keys move by themselves, with a reflected ghost pianist -- and TeleAbsence (concept 2020-21, headline vision 2025) redirects the same tangibility repertoire from connecting the living across space to remembering the dead across time, asking whether an unanswered gesture can still soothe grief. The arc of the whole corpus: bits get a body (1997), the body becomes shapable matter (2002), matter starts moving itself (2009), moving matter starts meaning something about being human (2013-2021). [C36021]

## Aesthetic logic: the coupling is the picture

Three constants. First, the representation IS the control: a token, a pin, a grain of sand, a bottle is simultaneously the data, the display, and the handle -- there is no separate screen and no separate mouse, and a piece that only depicts pins or sand has kept the look and lost the mechanism. Second, the constraint is visible: shadows are geometric, drainage reroutes, walls rise, patterns fire only when they form -- the system shows its own rules in the material rather than hiding them behind a panel. Third, the system is alive without the viewer (waves travel, the sun moves, water flows, the clock runs) and the hand joins a process already in progress rather than starting one -- which is why direct manipulation (drag, place, sculpt, open) is the only interaction this work earns, and why a button panel or a click-to-reload would be a category error. What is absent throughout: the screen as the primary channel, menus, decoration for its own sake, and any claim that the computation is finished before the hand arrives -- the picture is the coupling, and the coupling happens in the hand. [C36022]
