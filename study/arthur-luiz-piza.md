# Arthur Luiz Piza: Study Notes

**Site:** Arthur Luiz Piza, born 13 January 1928 at Sao Paulo, died 26 May 2017 at Paris, aged 89 -- Brazilian printmaker, the pupil of Johnny Friedlaender who rebuilt the plate, the "sculptor of plates" who hammered thick copper with gouge and chisel until etching became relief. He trained in painting and fresco with Antonio Gomide in Sao Paulo (start year disputed, 1943 per Itau against 1948 per Annex), exhibited at the 1st Sao Paulo Biennial in 1951, an experience that turned him toward engraving, and moved to Paris in 1951, where he studied in Friedlaender's Paris atelier. He was first expert in etching and aquatint, using sugar-lift extensively, then from 1958 devoted himself primarily to burin engraving and abandoned traditional etching for the gouge technique: very thick copper plates incised with hammers and shaped chisels, grooves deep and wide enough to hold handmade special inks, at least thirty minutes of re-inking and wiping between colours on cold plates so the inks do not dry, wiping direction changing the impression. He took the Acquisition Prize at the 2nd Sao Paulo Biennale in 1953, the National Prize of Engraving at the 5th Biennale in 1959, exhibited at Documenta the same year and at the Venice Biennale in 1966, where he won the David Bright Prize, and was given retrospectives at the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo and MARGS, Porto Alegre, in 2002. From 1980 to 1993 he co-founded and animated the Espace latino-americain in Paris with Julio Le Parc, Luis Tomasello and others. He acknowledged no difference between artist and artisan, never started from a preparatory drawing, and made a matrix like a composer writes a score, the printer acting like the instrumentalist interpreting it, each copy an original. Holdings verified direct this run: MoMA, New York (Land, 1966, object 2465.1967), the Victoria and Albert Museum, London (Cosmogonia, E.2475-1966), and the Musee d'Art moderne de Paris (La grande verte, AME 933). ## The life: the Brazilian who hammered Paris copper

Piza is the inversion of the chain so far: Friedlaender kept one method and let the subject drain out of it; Piza kept one object, the copper plate, and let the technique drain out of it until the plate was a block to sculpt. He was born in Sao Paulo on 13 January 1928 and trained there in painting and fresco with Antonio Gomide -- the start year is 1943 per Itau Cultural against 1948 per Annex, left open, possibly compatible (a course begun in 1943, private lessons from 1948), though no source states that (C4851, C4853, C4855). He exhibited at the 1st Sao Paulo Biennial in 1951 and the experience turned him toward engraving; that year he moved to Paris, settled in Montparnasse, and studied in Johnny Friedlaender's Paris atelier, where Friedlaender lent him not only knowledge but his tools and his press -- Piza once damaged a press by inserting wire between roller and plate, and Friedlaender celebrated the result (C4851, C4852, C4853, C4951, C4952, C4956). In 1953 he took the Acquisition Prize at the 2nd Sao Paulo Biennale and held first major solo shows, one in Brazil and one at Galerie La Hune in Paris (C4851, C4853). The earliest dated work in the catalogue, Broken Form (W01, 1953), is still traditional intaglio: a single dark stone-like oval mass on a large blank cream sheet, black and grey on off-white, no colour (C4908). Cellules (W02, 1954) still combines drypoint, etching and aquatint in one work -- Durer's drypoint, Rembrandt's etching, Goya's aquatint juxtaposed, in one dealer's reading -- and it is the last of the early phase the catalogue can show in that mixed manner (C4906).

From 1958 he devoted himself primarily to burin engraving, and the gouge took over: hammers and shaped chisels into very thick copper, grooves deep enough to hold handmade inks, thirty minutes between colours (C4854, C4856, C4901, C4902). In 1959 he won the National Prize of Engraving at the 5th Sao Paulo Biennale, exhibited at Documenta in Kassel, and began relief collages from cut watercolours -- a second practice running beside the plate, chopping up painted sheets and reassembling the fragments like mosaics (C4851, C4853). Some dealer text says the 1959 prize was at Documenta; the CV and Annex place it at the 5th Biennale and list Documenta as a separate 1959 exhibition, and the dealer wording is a conflation, not followed here (C4853). The 1960s are the gouge decade: Land (W06, 1966) enters MoMA; Beryl (W08, 1967 per Swann against 1971 per a Chiswick listing) and Calcite (W13, 1971) name the work for minerals; the Venice Biennale of 1966 brings the David Bright Prize (C4854, C4901, C4903). Galeria Raquel Arnaud began representing him in 1973 (C4854). The Capachos series of 1979 puts metal reliefs on sisal; the Corte e Recorte series of 1984/1985 cuts deep incisions into sheets of paper; a three-dimensional mural went to the French Cultural Center in Damascus in 1986 (C4851, C4853). From 1980 to 1993 he co-founded and animated the Espace latino-americain in Paris with Julio Le Parc, Luis Tomasello and others, partly funded by Roberto Matta, who donated paintings to be sold for housing and art supplies -- the Latin American Paris circle that is also his network (C4851, C4855, C4951, C4956). The late work leaves the plate altogether: Tatu (W20, 2008) is folded sheets arranged on the gallery floor, the armadillo freed from the 1971 incision; Presages, the late wire-netting, paper and amalgam works, hung as dream-catchers at Galerie Jeanne Bucher Jaeger (C4906, C4911). He died in Paris on 26 May 2017; the Arthur Luiz Piza Award was created with his wife Clelia Piza in 2014, and after his death the Fonds Arthur Luiz Piza was formed in his Paris studio to preserve the work and prepare the catalogue raisonne (C4851, C4853, C4855).

## M1 -- The gouge cut: hammer and chisel, not acid

The first mechanism is the break with his master, and the sources name it outright: traditional etching abandoned for the "gouge" technique, very thick copper plates incised with hammers and various shaped chisels (C4901, C4902, C4856). The plate is carved, not bitten: the tool strikes, the copper gives, and the mark is a wound with a depth, not a line with a width. Against Friedlaender, whose ground is rosin grain and acid time, Piza's ground is force -- each mark arrives one strike at a time, and the plate remembers the order of the strikes in the depth of the cuts. The early work proves the break is a choice, not a limitation: Cellules (W02, 1954) shows he could already do drypoint, etching and aquatint together, and he gave them up (C4902, C4906). What the eye reads as Piza's surface -- the rugged topography of Tatu-bola, the scale-like overlapping marks of Crescendo du noir II -- is hammer work, not acid work, and a piece built on M1 never lets a shallow acid line appear.

**Takeaway:** cut the plate, do not bite it. Hammer, chisel, thick copper -- the mark is a wound with a depth, and the depth is the image.

## M2 -- Depth as ink reservoir: thirty minutes between colours

The second mechanism is the clock the gouge imposes, and it is the direct contrast to Friedlaender's multi-colour sheet. The grooves are precisely deep and wide enough to hold handmade special inks, some leaving a gloss that adds dimension; the plate is re-inked and wiped for at least thirty minutes between colours, on cold plates so the inks do not dry out; and wiping direction directly affects the final impression, because of the depth of the grooves (C4901, C4902, C4856). Against Friedlaender, whose colours arrive one decision at a time on a scored plate, and against Hayter, whose viscosity printing lands every colour in a single pull, Piza's colours arrive one slow re-inking at a time, and the print is the record of how long the plate was worked. A piece built on M2 shows its colours arriving slowly -- a colour laid down, the plate wiped and re-inked in real time, the same groove printing differently after a wipe in the other direction -- and never lands all the colours at once.

**Takeaway:** let each colour take thirty minutes. Deep groove, handmade ink, cold plate, wipe, re-ink -- the colour is a schedule, and the schedule is slow.

## M3 -- Relief on paper: hollows become volumes

The third mechanism is what the depth does to the paper, and the sources give it a sentence: the plate is "cut, gashed, gouged, hammered, sculpted in small, successive marks that, like scales, interlock and overlap; hollows become volumes" (C4901, C4903, C4915). The deep gouging embosses the paper, so the print is a relief the eye can read and the finger could feel: light rakes across the sheet and the marks cast tiny shadows, and the surface rises where the plate was cut deepest. Petit Rouge, the best image in the run, shows it directly: glossy red triangles sitting proud of the surface with visible gloss and relief, framed by hundreds of tiny black triangles, the red ones cut deeper and holding thicker ink (C4906, C4983). At the limit the mechanism eats itself: in Tatu-bola (W12, 1971) the burin carves the copper to a topography so rugged the engraving becomes a kind of dry stamp, the groove so deep as to no longer necessitate ink, and in places the incision became an orifice and the paper did not come into contact with the matrix -- the print is the absence of contact (C4906). A piece built on M3 is a surface before it is an image: the light moves, the shadows move, and the picture is the relief.

**Takeaway:** make the hollows into volumes. Cut deep enough that the paper rises where the plate was wounded -- and at the limit, let the print be the place the paper never touched.

## M4 -- The geometric figure kit: triangles and diamonds at varying depths

The fourth mechanism is the vocabulary the gouge arrives at, and rhythm is its centre: rounded, rectangular and triangular figures excavated at varying depths, with rhythm the central element of his research (C4853, C4854). The figures are cut at different depths, so some sit proud and glossy and others sink into the ground: hundreds of tiny black triangles frame a cluster of larger glossy red ones in Petit Rouge; colourful triangles and diamonds fluctuate in a half-grey, half-brown space in Le rouge et le jaune (W18, 1995), where the ground, made with delicate strokes, is entirely tactile, "a surface to be seen with the eyes and felt with the fingers" (C4906, C4983). Against Friedlaender's closed kit of five signs, Piza's kit is smaller and deeper: the triangle and the diamond, repeated at different depths until the repetition itself is the rhythm. A piece built on M4 deals from two shapes only and varies the depth, never the vocabulary -- the rhythm is in how deep each figure is cut.

**Takeaway:** deal from two shapes and vary the depth. Triangle, diamond, cut deeper or shallower -- the rhythm is the depth, not the shape.

## M5 -- The armadillo: a figure that balls itself up to survive

The fifth mechanism is the one figure that is also a story, and it runs thirty-seven years. In Tatu-bola (W12, 1971) the burin carves the copper to a rugged topography and the ink sits around the matrix, highlighting a figure that appeared as a balled-up armadillo, turned into a sphere to protect itself from the world and its fury; the groove is so deep the engraving becomes a kind of dry stamp (C4906). In Tatu (W20, 2008) the same incision is freed: folded sheets arranged on the gallery floor, the armadillo emerging from a cut made decades before, freed along paths of ink imprints, absence of ink, folds on paper (C4906). The mechanism is a figure with a defence: flat, it is an incision; threatened, it curls into a ball; released, it unrolls off the plate and stands on the floor as a folded object. A piece built on M5 performs the curl live -- the flat figure balls itself up when the world presses in, and unrolls when the pressure lifts -- and never starts from the ball.

**Takeaway:** let the figure ball itself up to survive. Flat incision, curled sphere, folded object on the floor -- the defence is the shape, and the shape is the story.

## M6 -- Mineral colour: Beryl, Calcite, ochres

The sixth mechanism is the palette, and it is named for stones. The works are named for minerals -- Beryl, Calcite, Fantome -- and the colour follows: often ochres, muted and subdued, with single saturated accents, the red sun, the red triangles (C4903, C4910, C4915). A colour is introduced by its mineral name and arrives as a stone-like patch: green checks in a white oval in Emerald, red flecks denser along the bottom edge in Fragmentation, both viewed as images this run at low resolution (C4983). Against Friedlaender, whose eleven colours are counted out loud as decisions, Piza's colour is geological -- the ochre ground is the rock, the saturated accent is the mineral found in it. A piece built on M6 names each colour for a mineral and lets it arrive as a stone: the ochre field first, the red or green found inside it second, never a rainbow.

**Takeaway:** colour it like a mineral. Ochre ground, one saturated stone found inside it -- the colour is a specimen, not a spectrum.

## M7 -- Cut paper and collage: the sheet chopped up and reassembled

The seventh mechanism is the second practice that runs beside the plate from 1959: reliefs made by chopping up watercolours and using the fragments for collages on paper or canvas, like mosaics; late-1960s assemblages incorporating glued fragments of paper, copper and wood; the Corte e Recorte series of 1984/1985, deep incisions in sheets of paper; the Capachos series of 1979, metal reliefs on sisal; Presages, late wall and free-standing works in wire netting, paper and hand-modelled amalgam (C4851, C4853, C4911). The mechanism is the same cut at a different scale: on copper the cut is a groove, on paper it is a severance, and the pieces reassemble as a mosaic or hang as a dream-catcher. A piece built on M7 cuts a painted sheet and lets the pieces reassemble -- the cut is the composition, the fragments are the palette -- and never glues what the gouge has not first cut.

**Takeaway:** cut the sheet, then reassemble it. Watercolour chopped into fragments, paper incised until it is relief, netting hung as a dream-catcher -- the cut is the composition.

## M8 -- The plate as score: each copy an original

The eighth mechanism is the method, stated by the artist himself: he makes a matrix like a composer writes a score, and the printer acts like the instrumentalist interpreting it; each copy is an original, and engraving is not reproduction (C4853). He never starts from a preparatory drawing, at most a vague idea of the outline, and gives more weight to intuition than to any theory; the artist and the artisan are one and the same (C4853, C4901). Against Friedlaender, whose score is the sketch worked out with stringency so that nothing is an accident, Piza's score is the opposite discipline: the instructions are the cuts, the depths, the wiping directions, and the performance -- the impression -- is slightly different every time, because the ink, the wipe and the cold plate never repeat exactly. A piece built on M8 shows the plate scored first as a set of instructions -- cut here, deep here, wipe this way -- and then performs it, each impression a little different, never a reproduction.

**Takeaway:** score the plate, then perform it. Cut here, deep here, wipe this way -- and let each impression be a performance, slightly different every time, never a copy.

## The aesthetic logic: the plate as a block to sculpt

Piza's logic is the inversion of Friedlaender's and the completion of it. Friedlaender kept one method -- sketch, then score -- and let the subject drain out of it until the animal was a sign. Piza kept one object -- the copper plate -- and let the technique drain out of it: first the acid (etching, aquatint, sugar-lift), then the burin, then the gouge and hammer, until the plate was no longer a surface to bite but a block to sculpt, and the print was no longer an image on paper but a relief the paper carries. The constants are the groove and the ink: deep enough to hold handmade ink, wiped in a direction that matters, re-inked for thirty minutes between colours. The variable is the depth, and it widens until the groove stops needing ink at all (Tatu-bola) and the figure walks off the plate onto the floor (Tatu). Against Carcan, whose plates arrive one colour at a time and can be counted, and against Friedlaender, whose colours arrive one decision at a time on a scored plate, Piza's colours arrive one slow re-inking at a time, and the print is the record of how long the plate was worked. His own sentence is the logic in one line: a matrix like a score, a printer like an instrumentalist, each copy an original.
