## The life

Gerard ter Borch the Younger was born in December 1617 in Zwolle, Overijssel. His father, Gerard ter Borch the Elder (1582/83-1662), was a draftsman who had sojourned in Italy (Rome and Naples, 1600-1607) and from 1621 held the lucrative office of license-master (tax official). His mother Anna Bufkens (1587-1621) died when he was four; the Elder married twice more, and the third marriage (to Wiesken Matthys of Deventer) produced three children, all artists: Gesina (1631-1690), Harmen (1638-c. 1677) and Moses (1645-1667) (leg 1, Leiden Collection). The father's dates carry a discrepancy: the 2017 Leiden text gives 1582/3-1652, the 2020 revision 1582/83-1662; the National Gallery gives 1584-1662. The study uses 1582/83-1662 and treats the 1652 figure as an error corrected in revision (leg 1).

He was a child prodigy at the drawing board. The Rijksmuseum holds drawings he made as a seven-year-old; the earliest dated drawing is 25 September 1625. His father meticulously annotated the young Gerard's drawings with comments and dates (leg 1). First instruction came from his father in Zwolle. A study of a head inscribed "in Amsterdam 1632" proves he was in Amsterdam at fifteen, but "it is not known with whom he lodged" (Leiden Collection). The older sources hedge the teacher: he "studied possibly under Willem Cornelisz Duyster or Pieter Codde" (Wikipedia / 1911 Britannica). In 1634 he moved to Haarlem to study under the landscape painter Pieter de Molijn (1600-1661); he completed training in 1635 and joined the Haarlem Guild of St Luke. *Consultation* (1635, Gemaldegalerie Berlin) is the record of the Haarlem period (legs 1 and 2). One source (V&A) places Molijn in Amsterdam in 1634; the Leiden Collection and Wikipedia place him in Haarlem. The study follows Haarlem and treats the V&A wording as an error (leg 1).

In the summer of 1635 he was in London, living with his step-uncle Robert van Voerst, engraver to Charles I. His father sent him a wooden manikin (lay figure) by post, with the instruction "Use the manikin and do not let it stand idle... but draw a lot: large, dynamic compositions" (letter of 3 July 1635, reproduced in the Wheelock catalogue). The same letter urged him to paint "modern compositions" -- scenes of everyday life -- the programmatic push behind his genre turn (legs 1 and 3). He was back in Zwolle by spring 1636 (leg 1).

The travels after that are the study's biggest dispute. Wikipedia and the 1911 Britannica assert "it is certain that he was in Rome in 1641, when he painted the small portraits on copper of *Jan Six*, *A Young Lady*..." The Leiden Collection (Bakker, following Gudlaugsson 1959-60 and Wheelock 2004) holds the opposite: "It is highly unlikely that Ter Borch went to Italy as his father did, and all of the arguments put forward in support of such a journey ultimately prove untenable." The Wheelock catalogue foreword lists his travels as "England and Spain" only. Stronger evidence exists for Spain: a poem recited at his 1654 wedding says his work was esteemed at the Madrid court and the king himself sat for him; an autograph replica of the lost Philip IV portrait seems to confirm it; Velazquez's influence is visible in portraits painted shortly after his return. Bakker dates the Spain stay to the late 1630s, not after Munster 1648 as Houbraken supposes, based on the fashionable costume in the Philip IV replica; whether he ever met Velazquez is unknown. The study follows the Leiden position and flags the Britannica Rome claim as superseded (leg 1). The Madrid knighthood from Philip IV is Houbraken anecdote, not confirmed by modern scholarship: after the treaty he stopped in Brussels, where he received a gold chain with a medallion bearing the Spanish kings' image -- that much is documented (leg 1).

From 1645 he was invited to join the Dutch delegation brokering peace with Spain at Munster, probably through Amsterdam connections. He painted Count Penaranda, leader of the Spanish delegation, and other ambassadors including Adriaen Pauw, Adriaan Clant van Stedum and Godard van Reede. His *Swearing of the Oath of Ratification of the Treaty of Munster*, 1648, oil on copper, 45.4 x 58.5 cm (National Gallery NG896), depicts the six Dutch and two Spanish delegates swearing the oath; ter Borch painted himself into it at far left, "with a reddish moustache and long fair hair, looking out at us" (National Gallery). It is "the first known oil painting to depict such a political event in a 'factual' way" (National Gallery). Some sixty small heads, "full of character and expression" (Hofstede de Groot). Houbraken says he demanded 6,000 guilders for it but was offered less, so he kept it; History Today repeats the 6,000-florin figure and contrasts it with Rembrandt's 1,600 florins for the *Night Watch*. The two forms are carried, not resolved: the asking price is Houbraken's, the payment is History Today's. The picture was later bought by the Marquess of Hertford at the Demidoff sale for 7,280 pounds and given to the National Gallery by Sir Richard Wallace (legs 1-3). He also commissioned an engraving by Jonas Suyderhoef to disseminate it (leg 3).

The 1640s and early 1650s: an equestrian portrait of Elector Palatine Charles Louis painted in The Hague, 1649; paid 100 guilders for 23 prints by Kampen town council, 1650; in Delft in 1653, where he jointly signed a document with Johannes Vermeer; portraits of Jan van Goyen, Willem Everwijn and wife in Arnhem, early 1650s (leg 1). The guardroom scenes with soldiers at rest or playing cards in the manner of Codde and Duyster gave way after Munster to tranquil middle-class interiors -- "he was the first Dutch painter to develop this kind of imagery, which he did entirely independently" (Leiden Collection). In Deventer he "radically changed the genre yet again by replacing the average citizens with members of the elite presented in all their finery" (Leiden Collection).

He married his step-aunt Geertruit Matthys (1612-1672) in Deventer in 1654, was granted partial citizenship the following year, and settled in Deventer and painted there for the rest of his life. Appointed *gemeensman* (representative of one of the eight wards, advising the town council) in 1666; commissioned for a large group portrait of the entire Deventer town council in 1667. His most important pupil, Caspar Netscher, studied under him in Deventer from the mid-1650s. The Rampjaar of 1672 likely caused a temporary move to Amsterdam (1672-74), where he painted the De Graeff regent family (1673-75) and stayed with his brother-in-law Sybrant Schellinger, husband of his sister Jenneke (1640-1675). He returned to Deventer after the occupation forces withdrew on 3 May 1674 (leg 1). One loose end is carried, not resolved: the Leiden Collection mentions Jenneke but its own family list names only three children of the third marriage (Gesina, Harmen, Moses); Jenneke's parentage is not explained in the sources read (leg 1).

He died in Deventer on 8 December 1681; at his express request his body was transferred to Zwolle to be buried next to his father in the St. Michaelskerk (leg 1). About eighty paintings are catalogued (Wikipedia; 1911 Britannica); two-thirds of his paintings are portraits, "likenesses of prominent citizens" (Rijksmuseum). Six are at the Hermitage, six at the Gemaldegalerie Berlin, five at the Louvre, four at Dresden, three at the Getty, two at the Wallace Collection (Wikipedia). His patrons included Amsterdam burgomaster Andries de Graeff, "one of his great patrons" (leg 3). No source read by any leg says whether Gerard had children of his own; the study does not assert either way (leg 1).

His half-sister Gesina ter Borch (1631-1690; the Rijksmuseum caption gives 1630) was a watercolourist and draftswoman, the family archivist who preserved the estate in three albums -- the *Materi-boeck* begun as a teenager, the *Poetry Album* begun 18 November 1652, the *Konstboek* from 1660 -- plus 59 loose sheets; Bredius rediscovered the albums in 1882 and the Rijksmuseum acquired them (estate 1886). She modelled for Gerard's satin ladies and they collaborated on the posthumous portrait of their brother Moses (c. 1667-69). Her only signed painting was acquired by the Rijksmuseum at TEFAF 2024 (leg 3). Moses ter Borch (1645-1667), the younger brother, was a promising artist whose drawings survive in Gesina's albums; he "died young" (Rijksmuseum). One secondary source says he died in battle (NetGalley description of Eaker 2024); no museum source read corroborates it, and the study does not assert it (legs 1 and 3). Harmen ter Borch (1638-c. 1677) contributed 216 sheets to the family legacy, many copies after Gerard (leg 1).

## M1. The satin as light-engine

The mechanism that makes ter Borch ter Borch is not a subject but a fabric. Contemporaries called his interiors "satijntjes" -- satin pieces (SMB Gemaldegalerie). Arie Wallert's "The Miracle of Gerard ter Borch's Satin" (in Wheelock 2004, pp. 31-42, read in full by leg 1) gives the mechanics. Satin's loose weave -- weft over one warp, under four -- makes the face almost all warp threads, so light is directly and almost fully reflected rather than scattered; the shimmer comes from varying the contrast between strongest highlights and midtones (Wallert, pp. 32-33). For non-shiny cloth (a wool sleeve in *A Young Woman at Her Toilet with a Maid*) he blended paint mixtures smoothly and minimised highlight/shadow contrast; for satin, contrasts are pronounced -- "frosty white highlights set against a darker midtone and even darker shadows" -- and for metal brighter still, brightest white with almost complete elimination of midtones against very deep shadows (pp. 33-34).

Reflected light is a system, not a highlight. In *Gallant Conversation* / *Paternal Admonition*, the shadow on the man's chamois waistcoat lightens and reddens at its edge from light bounced off the red chair-back; the projected shadow on the chair is darker than the shadow on the waistcoat itself (pp. 32-33). "The artist... must consider the complicated play of projected shadows and mutual reflections in the entire ensemble."

The build-up, from technical examination of *Paternal Admonition*: a grey ground (single ochre-coloured layer with quartz sand); dead colour of lead white, red lake, lamp black; then a few thin layers only -- shadows of lead white mixed to cool grey with very fine charcoal black plus a grain of red lake and fine light ochre; midtones white with a trace of black and ultramarine; highlights pure "scallop-white" lead white in "a fine network of tiny strokes, licks, and dabs... so thin that the image in an x-radiograph is pale and ghostly" (pp. 36-37). For reds: dead colour of vermilion + red lake, glazed with red lake, touched up with ivory black (per De Mayerne); blues dead-coloured with cheap smalt, finished with thin expensive ultramarine (p. 36). In *Curiosity* he refined this further: highlights of lead white and shadows of red lake "in extremely thin layers of paint, applied in a very fine network of overlapping touches... almost imperceptible transitions... thin gradations of scumbles of lead white over a similarly thinly applied pink dead color" (p. 35).

There is a second manner, the "modern manner" his father urged in the 1635 letter: paint "modern compositions... by rummaging about and working them up in one go, because that goes most quickly and [the paint] stays most beautiful and flowing while drying." *Woman at a Mirror* was painted this way: loosely, with many more, freer brushstrokes, over a sketchy monochrome underdrawing in umber/ochre/lead white (pp. 37-38). And a subtractive technique in the same picture: he laid a smooth even layer of whites/pale greys as the satin base, scumbled black particles thinly over it, then with "a fine piece of cloth, a thin bristle, or a clean brush... selectively wiped off the gray film, thereby revealing the bright white of the paint underneath" -- "highlights-by-wiping-off", visible only on the most protruding areas (skirt edge, top of right sleeve, right shoulder); elsewhere tone varies only through modulation of the thin grey film (pp. 38-39). Wallert notes the approach also appears in Netscher, Eglon van der Neer and Adriaen van der Werff -- but ter Borch's satins remain more convincing, which Wallert attributes to "acuity of observation" rather than tricks (pp. 39-40).

He almost certainly did not paint the satin from a live model wearing it. The working method Wallert argues: textiles draped over a wooden manikin, drawn in meticulous preliminary studies, then transferred to the support -- probably by laying the drawing on the canvas with pigment-coated transfer paper between and tracing contours with a pointed tool (method described by Vasari 1550, Borghini 1584, Karel van Mander 1604) (pp. 34-35). The evidence is the repetition: the satin dress in the Amsterdam *Paternal Admonition* is identical to the Berlin version's; the same dress design recurs in at least six pictures; the *Glass of Lemonade* drapery repeats in a private-collection version; seven years later (c. 1671) the same gown design reappears in a *Portrait of a Lady*; the silk dress in the Cincinnati *Music Party* repeats in the Toledo *Music Lesson* (pp. 34-35). "Ter Borch's oeuvre is full of partial or complete repetitions of figures, either by the master himself or by studio assistants" -- standard workshop practice inherited from Dirck Hals, Duyster and Codde. And a telling absence: "Quite a few drawings by Ter Borch are still extant, although not one of them depicts a lady in satin" -- Wallert suggests the best satin studies were traced to destruction and thrown away (p. 35).

The palette around the satin is deliberately sober. Britannica: "In colour they tend to be subdued, largely because of the sober costume of the times, but by subtlety of tonal gradations and mastery in rendering diverse surface textures he was able to achieve an extraordinary richness of effect." Hofstede de Groot (1913, vol. 5, p. 19): as coloured costume gave way to black-and-white dress, "he has such a keen eye for his black velvets, cloths and silks, and for his white satins... that although he is painting in monochrome the total effect is attractive as a piece of colour" -- a single silver object, book or inkpot supplies the colour accent (leg 1).

**Takeaway:** build the fabric as the light source. A white satin surface rendered as frosty-white highlight strokes sinking into a darker midtone and deeper shadow, with the colour of neighbouring objects bounced into its shadow edges; the face secondary, the dress doing the lighting. The watchable behaviour is the shimmer travelling as the light moves -- the satin is the lamp, not the thing lit.

## M2. The letter as interior-life device

Around 1650 ter Borch invented a new kind of picture: a young woman writing or reading a letter, alone or watched, in a small room. The Met, on *Curiosity*: "Ter Borch uses letter writing and reading as a way to gesture toward the interior life... something that Ter Borch passed on to his acquaintance, Johannes Vermeer." The letter is never read by the viewer; its contents are never given. That is the device: an object that makes an inner life visible without showing it. *Woman Writing a Letter* (c. 1655, Mauritshuis) -- the model appears to be his half-sister Gesina -- shows the heavy decorated table cover pushed back to make room for the quill, inkpot and letter, the bed behind with heavy drapery, a "brilliant red flash of the seat" at lower right (leg 2). *Curiosity* (c. 1660-62, Met) has three women: the eldest writes a letter while the youngest peers over her shoulder; the third, "dressed in the radiant satin often associated with Ter Borch -- appears pensive or lovelorn"; they are likely drafting a reply to a love letter (leg 2). *The Letter* (c. 1660-65, Royal Collection) has two young women working together, apparently writing a reply to the letter being read, while a boy brings a tray with an ornate pitcher and "a small dog is curled up asleep on a stool" (leg 2). *A Lady Reading a Letter* (c. 1665, Wallace Collection) is "remarkable for its subtle light and shadow and the artist's confident use of colour" (Wallace Collection, via leg 1).

The letter pictures are the guardroom's descendant in the most literal sense: the soldier dictating a letter (*An Officer Dictating a Letter*, c. 1655-58, Warsaw) is the guardroom officer with his orders turned inward, and *The Messenger* (1653, Mauritshuis) -- a booted, spurred messenger with a trumpet, the recipient in breastplate and riding boots "taken aback", his wife leaning on his thigh, a bed behind, a sword on the bedpost, a gun and powder horn -- is the letter arriving as news, still wearing the war (leg 2). The device survives the war: the same envelope, the same held breath, now in a room with no soldiers in it.

**Takeaway:** stage a letter being written or read, never its contents. One figure absorbed, one figure watching, the quill or the paper as the only moving thing. The watchable behaviour is the pause -- the hand stops, the reader looks up, the watcher leans in -- and the letter stays unread.

## M3. Ambiguity by construction

Ter Borch's signature narrative move is to refuse to say what is happening. The Rijksmuseum label on *Gallant Conversation* (SK-A-404) states it plainly: "He also loved ambiguous scenes: it is often difficult to determine what his figures feel." The picture was long titled *The Paternal Admonition* -- a father reproving his daughter -- but is now read as an amorous seduction: a standing woman with her back to us in a "plush going-out dress", a man who has perhaps just come in, hat still in his hand, a table with a reading stand and books "almost certainly including a Bible", the father wearing a sword, a dog that "looks on from the gloom", a bed behind (legs 1 and 2). Goethe described the "fine, striking figure, in a folding drapery of white satin" in *Elective Affinities* after J. G. Wille's 1765 engraving (SMB). No fewer than 24 repetitions, partial copies and imitations attest to its popularity (SMB). The "deliberate depiction of ambiguous moments" is his narrative talent (SMB).

The same refusal runs through the oeuvre. *Glass of Lemonade* (c. 1664, Hermitage): "masterly command of painting combines with psychological subtlety"; "eloquent dialogue of looks and touches"; probably a variant on the visit-to-a-procuress subject; "Ter Borch was fond of such ambiguities" -- the seduction question is unresolvable by design (leg 2). *Curiosity* leaves the letter's contents unknown and the third woman's lovelorn look unexplained. The mechanism is structural, not vague: two or three figures, gazes that do not meet, a letter or a glass whose meaning the viewer must supply, and no fourth figure to explain it. The Rijksmuseum's summary of his whole manner: "a master of suggestion. His scenes usually feature just a few figures and often only one. Even when the artist depicts a single person, he still conjures up an entire world"; the scenes are open-ended -- "What is the conversation about and how does it end?" (leg 1).

**Takeaway:** build the scene so two readings stay live at once -- admonition or seduction, letter or love letter, visit or proposition -- and never resolve it. The watchable behaviour is the viewer-side wobble: the figures hold their poses, the gazes never meet, and the meaning keeps flipping without anything changing.

## M4. The upright small-group interior

Around 1650 he started to concentrate on upright compositions, "allowing him to show small groups of figures, depicted full-length or nearly so, interacting within a small interior space" (Sotheby's essay, via leg 2). The room is a stage, not a location: two or three elegantly clad figures, a bed almost always in the room (*Messenger*, *Gallant Conversation*, *Woman Writing a Letter*, *Glass of Lemonade*, even the late *Music Lesson*), a table, a dog, and not much else. Panels shrink to about 28-48 cm in the early 1650s; the figures grow to near full-length within the frame. Then, from about 1655, the format roughly doubles in area: *Gallant Conversation* (71 x 73 cm, canvas), *The Suitor's Visit* (80 x 75 cm), *Curiosity* (76.2 x 62.2 cm), *The Letter* (81.9 x 68.4 cm) (leg 2).

*The Suitor's Visit* (c. 1658, NGA Washington) is the formula at its most theatrical: the encounter "at the doorway of this elegant, high-ceilinged room, decorated with gilded leather wall covering"; a "debonair young man, hat in hand, bows slightly" (NGA). The doorway is the hinge of the composition -- the suitor enters, the woman receives, and the room is the negotiation. The light, per Sotheby's, becomes the subject in the first half of the 1650s: ter Borch was "very much concerned with light and its effects in his genre pictures" (leg 2).

The room's furniture is fixed and few. The bed is the tell: it is in the room in almost every interior, which is what makes the ambiguity in M3 possible -- a bedroom that is also a parlour. The dog is the other fixed piece of furniture (see M7). The table carries the props that do the narrative work: the letter, the inkpot, the songbook, the glass of lemonade, the tray with the ornate pitcher.

**Takeaway:** build a shallow upright room with two or three full-length figures, a bed at the back, a table with one telling prop, and a doorway where the second figure enters. The watchable behaviour is the threshold -- the suitor bowing at the door, the messenger stopped mid-room -- the room holding its breath between arrival and answer.

## M5. Model and drapery reuse: the repertory company

Ter Borch ran a repertory company. The same sitters and the same dresses return across pictures, and the repetition is the method, not a shortcut. Gesina modelled for his satin ladies -- "Gerard often used his younger half-sister Gesina as a model in his paintings. She can be recognized by her pointed nose and timid gaze" (Rijksmuseum); "His sister Gesina modelled for his painting *Sitting Young Woman* (c. 1650)" (Wikipedia); the sitter of *Woman Writing a Letter* (1655) is Gesina, "deeply absorbed in a letter" (Leiden Collection). Caspar Netscher, his pupil, "also served as a model in many of Ter Borch's genre scenes from the mid-to-late 1650s" (Wheelock catalogue); he is the suitor in *The Suitor's Visit* (NGA). The same dress design recurs in at least six pictures (Wallert); the *Glass of Lemonade* drapery is the same as in a private-collection version of the same subject; the silk dress in the Cincinnati *Music Party* repeats in the Toledo *Music Lesson* (legs 1 and 2). The *Gallant Conversation* satin dress is identical between the Amsterdam and Berlin versions (Wallert).

The reuse is inseparable from the manikin method in M1: textiles draped over a wooden lay figure, drawn in meticulous studies, transferred by tracing -- which is why the same dress can appear on different sitters in different rooms years apart, and why the dress is more constant than the face. The family workshop is the infrastructure: Gesina's albums document the family (the dog in *Curiosity* is named Acteon, per the Met audio guide); Harmen made 216 sheets, many copies after Gerard; the father annotated and corrected the sons' drawings; the Rijksmuseum Print Room holds the family's complete workshop legacy, which entered the museum 1887-1890 (legs 1 and 2).

**Takeaway:** cast the same two or three figures and the same dress across the whole piece, and let the room and the situation change around them. The watchable behaviour is recognition -- the same satin, the same pointed nose, the same suitor bowing -- in a new room, so the audience learns the company before the story starts.

## M6. Miniature precision at any scale

Ter Borch's early training was miniature: small portraits on copper, and the Munster oath picture is the proof that the miniature technique scales to history. *The Swearing of the Oath of Ratification of the Treaty of Munster* (1648) is oil on copper, 45.4 x 58.5 cm, holding some sixty individually characterised heads -- "full of character and expression" (Hofstede de Groot) -- plus the artist himself at far left. It is "the first known oil painting to depict such a political event in a 'factual' way" (National Gallery). The copper-portrait technique is the bridge to the interiors: small, smooth, exact. The early Rome portraits on copper (Jan Six, 1641, per the older sources) are the same scale discipline applied to a single face (leg 2).

The point for the study is the ratio: sixty heads in a panel smaller than a laptop screen, each one a likeness. The same precision governs the late interiors, where a face is a few centimetres of paint and still carries the ambiguity of M3. The National Gallery's summary of his manner: "Both portraits and genre scenes are meticulously painted, with particular attention paid to the quality of the costumes, the textures of satins and silks" (leg 1). Google Arts & Culture, on *The Music Lesson*: "exquisite painting technique, which consisted of delicate touches with the brush and the use of thin glazes to suggest transparencies" (leg 1).

**Takeaway:** build small and exact. A crowd of sixty distinct faces in a copper-sized frame, or one face rendered in a few centimetres of paint, each head a likeness and no two alike. The watchable behaviour is the zoom: from the whole congress down to one moustached face looking out at us, and back.

## M7. The dog as witness

Dogs recur in ter Borch as the silent onlooker -- the one figure in the room that is not negotiating anything. *Gallant Conversation*: a dog "looks on from the gloom" (leg 2). *The Spinner* (c. 1655, Boijmans): a small dog on the spinner's lap, while she "looks intently at her work" (leg 2). *The Letter*: "a small dog is curled up asleep on a stool" (leg 2). *The Music Lesson* (c. 1668, Getty): a small dog asleep on a chair, while the teacher stands over the young woman learning the lute and a cello rests on the table (leg 2). *Curiosity*: the dog on the cushion is named Acteon, recorded in Gesina's albums (Met audio guide, via leg 2). The dog is the room's conscience and its clock: awake, it watches the seduction; asleep, it certifies that nothing is happening that needs watching. It is also the most domestic thing in the room -- the bed's companion piece, the proof that this is a home and not a stage.

**Takeaway:** put a small dog in the room as the fixed witness -- awake in the gloom when the scene is ambiguous, asleep on a stool or a chair when the scene is settled. The watchable behaviour is the dog's state: it wakes, looks, settles, sleeps, and its sleep is the verdict on the scene.

## M8. The guardroom's afterlife: the domestication arc

This is the mechanism the chain has been walking toward since #565. The guardroom does not die in ter Borch; it is domesticated in three moves. First, the guardroom itself, learned in Amsterdam in 1632 in the manner of Duyster and Codde: *Cavaliers* (inscribed 1638, likely early 1640s, V&A) -- "guardroom scene with cavaliers and peasants resting in a domestic interior or a tavern" -- already showing "great refinement in the handling of colour, light, and texture and... subtle psychological interplay" (V&A, via leg 2). *Card-playing Soldiers* (1645, Berlin) is the same inheritance. Second, the turn after Munster: the guardroom scenes "gave way to the tranquil interiors of middle-class dwellings" (Leiden Collection); the soldier becomes the officer dictating a letter, the messenger with news, the suitor at the door -- the guardroom's leisure, minus the guardroom. Third, the Deventer elevation: he "radically changed the genre yet again by replacing the average citizens with members of the elite presented in all their finery" (Leiden Collection) -- the satin ladies, the regent portraits, the town council of 1667.

The arc is visible in scale and light as well as subject. The early 1650s panels are small (28-48 cm) and dark; the backgrounds lighten in the later work (*The Music Lesson*, Eclectic Light); the lute/theorbo series runs to about eleven pictures; civic and family portraiture returns to the fore (the van Duren/van Haexbergen pair, the *Memorial Portrait of Moses*, the *Self-portrait* of 1668 as a new Deventer citizen). The late *Flea-Catcher* (*A Boy with his Dog*, after 1666, Munich) is a small, barren outlier -- a boy catching fleas, "fairly barren surroundings", paralleling the lice-combing of *Mother Delousing her Child's Hair* as domestic pest-removal (leg 2). The war ends, the soldiers become suitors, the suitors become burghers, and the last picture is a boy and his dog in an empty room.

**Takeaway:** play the career as a three-state arc on a slow timer -- guardroom, tranquil interior, elite Deventer room -- with the same leisure carried through all three states and the soldiers gradually replaced by suitors, then by burghers, then by nobody but a boy and his dog. The watchable behaviour is the room emptying of war while the leisure stays.

## The teachers question

The Amsterdam teachers are the study's central hedge. The sources say he "studied possibly under Willem Cornelisz Duyster or Pieter Codde" (Wikipedia / 1911 Britannica), and the Leiden Collection says only that he was in Amsterdam in 1632 and "it is not known with whom he lodged". Duyster's influence is traceable in a dated-1638 picture (Ionides Bequest, V&A); the guardroom scenes are "in the manner of" Codde and Duyster. Documented teachers: his father in Zwolle, and Pieter de Molijn in Haarlem 1634-35. No source in any leg connects ter Borch to Frans Hals as teacher, influence or contact -- do not assert a Hals connection (leg 3). Velazquez's influence is noted by the Leiden Collection on the portraits after the Spain stay; whether he met Velazquez is unknown (leg 1). Gerard de Lairesse's praise of ter Borch was NOT verified by any leg: what the sources say is that de Lairesse's *Groot Schilderboek* (1707) disapproved of realist genre painters. Do not assert that de Lairesse praised ter Borch without a direct citation (leg 3).

## Evolution

1630s -- apprenticeship and soldiers. Amsterdam/Haarlem training under Duyster/Codde/Molijn; guardroom (*cortegaertje*) scenes and merry-company subjects inherited from Duyster, Codde, Simon Kick, Jacob Duck (Sotheby's essay). *Man on Horseback* (1634): a lone slumped rider seen from behind -- melancholic, small panel, no interior, no satin. *Consultation* (1635) is a Haarlem-period record piece. The V&A: the guardroom pictures already show "great refinement in the handling of colour, light, and texture and... subtle psychological interplay" -- the seed of everything later (leg 2).

1640s -- travel, small copper portraits, history. Rome 1641 in the older sources (disputed -- see The life); tiny portraits on copper (Jan Six). Munster 1646-48: the pivot. *Adrian Pauw's Arrival* (100 x 161.5 cm, with van der Horst landscape) is the one large outdoor exception; the Munster oath itself is the opposite -- 45.4 x 58.5 cm on copper, about sixty individually characterised heads, the first "factual" political painting (National Gallery), self-insert at the left. Portraiture on copper at miniature scale is the technical bridge to the interiors: small, smooth, exact (leg 2).

c. 1650-55 -- the invention of the upright interior. Sotheby's essay: "Around 1650 he started to concentrate on upright compositions, allowing him to show small groups of figures, depicted full-length or nearly so, interacting within a small interior space." Panels shrink to about 28-48 cm; figures grow to near full-length within the frame; two or three figures only; the bed is almost always in the room. Subjects turn domestic and ambiguous: letters arriving, lice being combed, a spinner, a toilette. Light becomes the subject (Sotheby's: "very much concerned with light and its effects in his genre pictures in the first half of the 1650s") (leg 2).

c. 1655-62 -- the satin peak, larger canvases. *Gallant Conversation* (71 x 73 cm, canvas), *Suitor's Visit* (80 x 75), *Curiosity* (76.2 x 62.2), *The Letter* (81.9 x 68.4): the format roughly doubles in area versus the early-1650s panels. White satin becomes the signature material -- the "satin pieces" nickname; Kettering's "Ter Borch's Ladies in Satin" and Wallert's "The Miracle of Gerard ter Borch's Satin" (2004 Wheelock catalogue) are the technical literature. Same drapery designs recur across pictures; models recur too: Gesina as letter-writer, Netscher as suitor. Ambiguity hardens into method: the Rijksmuseum label stresses that "it is often difficult to determine what his figures feel"; the Hermitage calls the lemonade scene's seduction question unresolvable by design (leg 2).

1660s-70s, Deventer -- lighter, more portraiture, more elegy. Backgrounds lighten (*Music Lesson*, Eclectic Light); the lute/theorbo series runs to about eleven pictures; civic and family portraiture returns to the fore (van Duren/van Haexbergen, *Memorial Portrait of Moses*, *Self-portrait* 1668 as new Deventer citizen). Scale stays in the 67-82 cm canvas band for genre works; the late *Flea-Catcher* (35 x 28 cm) is a small, barren outlier. The late portraits (e.g. *Portrait of a Young Lady*, Frick, 53.7 x 40.6) are quieter and darker than the satin interiors (leg 2).

## Aesthetic logic

Ter Borch's aesthetic is the small room made large by attention. Everything is small -- the panels, the figures, the cast, the furniture -- and everything is exact: the satin, the letter, the dog, the bed. The largeness is in the suggestion: a letter implies a whole correspondence, a suitor at the door implies a whole courtship, a treaty on copper implies a whole congress, a dress implies a whole wardrobe. The Rijksmuseum's line is the thesis: "a master of suggestion... Even when the artist depicts a single person, he still conjures up an entire world." The mechanism of that conjuring is always the same: one telling object (letter, glass, songbook, watch), one telling fabric (satin), one telling witness (dog), and the refusal to explain. The guardroom taught him the leisure; Munster taught him the miniature exactness; the manikin taught him the dress; Gesina taught him the face. The interior is the guardroom with the war taken out and the light left in.

## Positions

- The Italy/Rome 1641 trip is NOT accepted: Leiden Collection (Bakker, following Gudlaugsson and Wheelock) says "highly unlikely... all of the arguments... ultimately prove untenable"; the Wheelock foreword lists travels as England and Spain only. The Wikipedia / 1911 Britannica "it is certain that he was in Rome in 1641" is the superseded form; the study does not use it (leg 1).
- The Spain stay is dated to the LATE 1630s, not after Munster 1648 as Houbraken supposes (Bakker, on the costume in the Philip IV replica). The Madrid knighthood is Houbraken anecdote, not confirmed (leg 1).
- The Amsterdam teachers are "possibly" Duyster or Codde -- influence, not documented pupilage. The study never writes "pupil of Duyster" or "pupil of Codde" as fact (legs 1 and 3).
- No Frans Hals connection is asserted (leg 3). No de Lairesse praise is asserted (leg 3).
- The Vermeer influence is influence, inferred from analogous subjects, not documented teaching. Google Arts & Culture: "there is no documentary evidence that Vermeer was directly influenced by their work". The 1653 co-signed Delft document is the hard fact of contact (leg 3).
- Ter Borch is NOT a fijnschilder: he painted "in a flowing manner, smoothly blending colors using small, rapidly applied brushstrokes", while Dou worked in invisible-brushwork fijnschilder technique (NGA, via leg 3). He is the fluent-paint counterpoint to the Leiden fine painters, converging on the same upper-class domestic subjects in the 1650s from a different technique.
- The 6,000 guilders for the Munster picture is carried in both forms: Houbraken says he demanded 6,000 and kept the picture when offered less; History Today reports 6,000 as the price (legs 1 and 3).
- Moses ter Borch "died young" (Rijksmuseum); "died in battle" is uncorroborated and not asserted (legs 1 and 3).
- Gesina's birth year: 1631 (Leiden Collection); the Rijksmuseum caption gives 1630. The study uses 1631 and notes the variant (leg 1).
- The father's dates: 1582/83-1662 (2020 Leiden revision; National Gallery 1584-1662); the older Leiden text's 1652 is treated as an error (leg 1).
- Deventer settlement: 1654 (Leiden Collection; National Gallery). The V&A's "settled definitively at the end of the 1640s" likely conflates earlier Deventer stays with the 1654 settlement; the study uses 1654 (leg 1).
- Molijn taught him in Haarlem, not Amsterdam (Leiden Collection; Wikipedia); the V&A's Amsterdam wording is treated as an error (leg 1).

## Network

The teachers and the guardroom inheritance: Gerard ter Borch the Elder (father, first instruction, the 1635 letter and the manikin); Willem Cornelisz Duyster and Pieter Codde (Amsterdam 1632, "in the manner of", not documented pupilage); Pieter de Molijn (Haarlem 1634-35, documented). The guardroom chain runs Codde/Duyster (#565/#566) -> Duck (#567) -> Palamedesz. (#568) -> ter Borch (#569): he learned the guardroom's leisure in Amsterdam and carried it home. The V&A's single-source link for Palamedesz. ("adopting compositions recalling designs by Duyster and Jacob Duck") has its ter Borch counterpart in the Ionides *Cavaliers*, where Duyster's influence is traceable in a dated-1638 picture (legs 1 and 3).

The family workshop: Gesina ter Borch (half-sister, model, watercolourist, archivist of the three albums, collaborator on the *Memorial Portrait of Moses*); Moses ter Borch (younger brother, died young, 1645-1667); Harmen ter Borch (younger brother, 216 sheets, many copies after Gerard); the father annotating and correcting the sons' drawings; the Rijksmuseum Print Room holding the family's complete workshop legacy (estate 1886) (legs 1-3).

The pupil and model: Caspar Netscher (1639-1684) -- "his most important pupil"; worked with ter Borch in Deventer c. 1654 and again 1658-59; "the most faithful disciple of his style" (Vergara; National Gallery London); modelled the suitor in *The Suitor's Visit* and other mid-to-late-1650s pictures (legs 1-3).

The influence outward: Johannes Vermeer (the central line -- the letter motif, the interior, the 1653 co-signed Delft document; inferred, not documented), Gabriel Metsu, Pieter de Hooch, Frans van Mieris, Gerrit Dou, Eglon van der Neer (legs 1-3). The direction with Dou is mutual/parallel: Dou is the Leiden fijnschilder; ter Borch is the fluent-paint counterpoint, and they converged on upper-class domestic subjects in the 1650s from different techniques (leg 3).

The patrons and stations: the Munster congress (1645-48, Count Penaranda, Adriaen Pauw); Amsterdam burgomaster Andries de Graeff ("one of his great patrons"); the Deventer elite and town council (1666-67); the Madrid court in the late 1630s (the Philip IV portrait, the gold chain from Brussels) (legs 1 and 3).
