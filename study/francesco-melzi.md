# Francesco Melzi: Study Notes

**Site:** Francesco Melzi (c.1491-1570; birth year disputed, see C208), Italian painter of the Milanese school, Leonardo da Vinci's favourite pupil, closest companion, executor and heir, and the compiler of the Codex Urbinas, the manuscript on which the Treatise on Painting rests. He was born into Milanese nobility, the son of Girolamo (Gerolamo) Melzi, an engineer in the Sforza military and a captain in the Milan militia under Louis XII, and grew up at the family seat, the Villa Melzi at Vaprio d'Adda, east of Milan -- not the Villa Melzi at Bellagio, built 1808-1810 for a Napoleonic-era descendant of the same name. Leonardo returned to Milan c.1505 and stayed with the Melzi family; he met Francesco there, reportedly "enticed by his good nature and handsomeness," and took him into the workshop c.1506, aged about 15. Melzi could write, copy correspondence and organise papers -- skills that mattered more than painting to Leonardo at that point -- and he quickly became, in Wikipedia's words, "his master's favorite pupil, and the most devoted." He was the only pupil who stayed with Leonardo until his death, travelling and working with him in Milan, Rome and France: Leonardo's own notebook records "I left Milan for Rome on the 24th day of September 1513, with Giovanni Boltraffio, Francesco de' Melzi, Lorenzo di Credi, and il Fanfoia," and the party went on to France in 1516, living at the Chateau de Cloux, Amboise, where French court accounts paid Leonardo 1,000 gold crowns a year and Melzi 400. Antonio de Beatis's diary of an October 1517 visit to Cloux records Leonardo living with Melzi and the servant Battista de Villanis, his right hand paralysed, no longer able to paint "with such sweetness as formerly" but still drawing and giving instruction. Leonardo's will, drawn up at Cloux on 23 April 1518 in the Florentine style -- 23 April 1519 in modern reckoning, see C210 -- names Melzi sole executor and bequeaths him "each and all of the books the Testator is at present possessed of, and the instruments and portraits appertaining to his art and calling as a painter," plus the remainder of his pension. The paintings went to Salai and the Milan garden was split between Battista de Villanis and Salai, so Melzi inherited the intellectual estate, not the whole estate -- a correction to the common shorthand, see C211. After Leonardo's death on 2 May 1519 Melzi returned to Italy, married Angiola di Landriani in 1519, fathered eight children including the lawyer Orazio, and brought the manuscripts to Vaprio. In a tight cluster around 1518-1522 he painted the three works that are certainly his, all signed in Greek letters: Vertumnus and Pomona (Gemaldegalerie, Berlin), Flora (Hermitage, St Petersburg) and the Portrait of a Young Man with a Parrot (Gallarati Scotti collection, Milan). He also compiled the Libro di pittura, gathering 944 short chapters from Leonardo's scattered notes into a systematic treatise on painting, organised in eight books, leaving some pages blank where he could not organise the material; the manuscript is now Codex Urbinas Latinus 1270 in the Vatican Library. He was the only person who could decipher Leonardo's left-handed mirror writing, spelling and enigmatic abbreviations, and that -- not painting -- is the mechanism that made the compilation possible. He never published it in his lifetime: the compilation was made sometime before 1542, and for the remaining roughly thirty years he kept the papers at Vaprio, lent manuscript copies to Vasari, Lomazzo, Antonio Gaddiano, Cardano and others, and guarded them -- Vasari, visiting Milan in 1566, found him a beautiful and gentle old man who kept Leonardo's anatomy papers "with as much care as though they were relics." He died c.1570. After his death the papers were neglected in Orazio's attic at Vaprio, then dispersed: the household tutor Lelio Gavardi abstracted 13 volumes in 1587, Giovanni Ambrogio Mazenta recovered them, and Pompeo Leoni bribed Orazio, took the volumes to Madrid, and cut the notebooks apart with scissors and glue to reassemble them as the Codex Atlanticus and the Windsor collection. The central scholarly point about Melzi the painter is his near-invisibility: Dietrich Seybold writes, "We don't see Melzi since he disappears within the stylistic paradigm that Leonardo established (with him)," and as long as there is no basis to distinguish him from the other Leonardeschi or from the master, workshop pieces and autograph works cannot be told apart with confidence. ## M1. Sfumato as Soft Translucent Modelling
Sfumato, absorbed wholesale from Leonardo, is Melzi's default modelling system and the reason his hand is hard to tell from the master's. The National Gallery, on Flora: the picture shows "the mastery of subtle modulations of tone, known as 'sfumato'"; the Hermitage describes "soft translucent chiaroscuro modelling volume" in the same painting. Mechanically it is what Leonardo taught in the Treatise (M1 of #517): flesh and drapery built from tone, with no hard contour anywhere, so the form emerges from graded shadow rather than from a drawn edge. In Melzi the system is slightly softened and slightly warmer than Leonardo's late work: the grotto in Flora is a leafy bower, not a cavern; the darkness is foliage, not geology. Watchable behaviour: no visible edge on Flora's face, neck or breast; the half-smile and the downcast gaze read through tone alone; the white gold-embroidered stola and the blue palla sit in the same soft light, with the blue (solid ultramarine, see M8) holding its depth without a hard boundary. Source: National Gallery "Leonardo's Legacy" press release; Hermitage Flora object page; Wikipedia "Sfumato" ("Francesco Melzi used it to model forms").

**Takeaway:** the edge is never drawn, it is dissolved -- the pupil's first skill is the master's smoke, and he never leaves it.

## M2. The Leonardesque Female Type
A soft regular face, high clear forehead, evenly arched eyebrows, downcast gaze and faint half-smile -- the Leonardesque female face as a repeatable type. The National Gallery: "the female facial type with its downcast look is characteristic of Leonardo's Milanese style." It is the face of Flora (c.1520), of Pomona in Vertumnus and Pomona (c.1518-1522), and of the Madonnas in the St Anne and the Jasmine Bower. The mechanism is a template: once the type is fixed, a new painting is a new arrangement of the same face, the same turn of the head (Flora's head turned gracefully to her left), the same lowered eyes. The type does the emotional work -- modesty, inwardness, tenderness -- so the painter does not have to invent a new psychology for each picture. Watchable behaviour: the same face recurs across Flora, Pomona and the Madonnas with only the hair, the dress and the plants changing; the half-smile is never a grin and the gaze never meets the viewer. Source: National Gallery press release; Hermitage Flora page; leg 2 works catalogue.

**Takeaway:** one face, many pictures -- the type is a machine for producing tenderness on demand.

## M3. Botany as a Symbol-Lexicon
Each plant in Flora is named and meaningful, a precise symbolic lexicon rather than background. The Flora analysis (Wikipedia "Flora (Francesco Melzi)"): columbine (aquilegia) = fertility, jasmine = purity, anemone = rebirth (and Zephyrus the west wind, Flora's husband), ivy = eternity, fern = solitude of the grotto. Flora sits in the grotto among ferns and ivy, white jasmines in her lap, a spray of columbine in her left hand; the 2019 conservation found flowers growing from the grotto walls and powerful ultramarine blue under the yellowed varnish. The mechanism is Leonardesque in method -- Leonardo's botanical accuracy, learned from observation -- but Melzi's in application: the plants are the picture's argument, a legible programme the viewer can read flower by flower, where Leonardo's plants are usually atmosphere first and symbol second. The same habit governs Vertumnus and Pomona, where the elm embraced by the grapevine is the picture's argument (see M5). Watchable behaviour: the plants are individually identifiable and individually placed, not a green wash; each one carries a meaning, and the meanings add up to the subject (fertility, purity, rebirth, eternity, solitude). Source: Wikipedia "Flora (Francesco Melzi)"; Hermitage conservation findings via leg 1 and leg 2.

**Takeaway:** the garden is a sentence -- every plant is a word, and the bouquet is the meaning.

## M4. Borrowed Figure, Invented Ground
Copy the figures faithfully from Leonardo's cartoons, then invent the ground they stand on. The observable Melzi move, clearest in the St Anne with the Virgin and the Child Embracing a Lamb (Uffizi, c.1520): a fairly faithful copy of Leonardo's Virgin and Child with St Anne in the Louvre, except that the landscape is entirely reformulated -- "an alluvial riverbank is replaced by a sumptuous meadow" (Web Gallery of Art). The Web Gallery of Art suggests Melzi worked not from Leonardo's painting but from a now-lost cartoon showing only the figures, inventing the landscape himself. The same move is visible in Flora, whose pose is indebted to the Madonna in Leonardo's Burlington House Cartoon (Christie's) while the grotto, built from named plants, is Melzi's own. The mechanism separates authorship into two layers: the figure layer is transcription, the ground layer is invention. It is the copyist's licence -- fidelity where the master is present, freedom where he is absent. Watchable behaviour: a familiar Leonardesque group standing in an unfamiliar place; the figures feel borrowed and the landscape feels new, and the seam between them is the picture. Source: Web Gallery of Art St Anne page (read end to end); leg 2 works catalogue; Christie's via leg 2.

**Takeaway:** the figures are Leonardo's and the ground is Melzi's -- the copyist invents exactly where the master left nothing to copy.

## M5. The Elm-and-Vine Emblem
A living diagram of marriage: two organisms that only stand together. Vertumnus and Pomona (c.1518-1522, Gemaldegalerie Berlin, 185 x 134 cm) illustrates Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV -- Vertumnus disguised as an old woman pleading with the orchard-goddess Pomona -- and the elm tree embraced by a grapevine is the picture's argument: the elm without the vine is bare shade, the vine without the elm creeps on the ground, and together they are the marriage emblem. Two life-size figures in conversation, Pomona seated on a stone bench, Vertumnus standing; Leonardesque sfumato, warm flesh against dark foliage, botanical detail (fruit, vine) doing the symbolic work. It is Melzi's only large-scale, multi-figure independent composition -- by far his biggest painting -- and the only one where the emblem, not the face, carries the meaning. The old man's head in it is conjectured to reuse his 1510 Head of an Old Man (see works table, entry 9). Watchable behaviour: the vine climbs the elm in the middle of the picture and the two figures talk across it; the tree is not scenery, it is the thesis. Source: Warburg Institute Iconographic Database (date c.1518-1522); Wikipedia "Vertumnus and Pomona" via leg 2; leg 2 works catalogue.

**Takeaway:** the tree is the argument -- two organisms, one standing, and the painting is the proof.

## M6. Grotesque Heads as Physiognomic Exercise
Leonardo's caricature mode copied in chalk and pen: exaggerated profiles, grimaces, the four temperaments around a central classical profile. Melzi's Five Grotesque Heads (c.1515), Seven Caricatures and Two Grotesque Heads are exercises in Leonardo's grotesque manner -- the study of extreme facial character, physiognomic types pushed until the face becomes a diagram of a humour. The mechanism is copying as training: the pupil reproduces the master's grotesques to learn how far a profile can be bent before it stops being a face. Caution: Leonardo himself drew a Five Grotesque Heads c.1494 (Windsor, RL 12495r), and the two are routinely confused; Melzi's sheet is the pupil's version of the master's exercise, and its location is not verified in this run (see C221). Watchable behaviour: five heads in a row, each one a different exaggeration -- a nose, a chin, a brow taken to the edge -- and the middle head holding the classical profile the others depart from. Source: Wikipedia "Francesco Melzi" (illustrates the sheet as Melzi's); leg 2 works catalogue.

**Takeaway:** the caricature is a scale -- push the profile until it breaks, and the break is the lesson.

## M7. Copying as a Discipline
Punctilious, close-worked transcription of the master's sheets -- fidelity, not variation, is the early skill. The Windsor drawings attributed to Melzi (Royal Collection): Horses' fore-legs (c.1510-20, RCIN 912299 and 912302), Studies of the hind-quarters of horses (c.1517-18, RCIN 912333), A dog's head and chest, and hind-leg (c.1510-20, RCIN 912361), The head of a devil (c.1510-20, RCIN 912371), The castle of Amboise (c.1516-19, RCIN 912727), The head of a girl wearing a cap (c.1510-20, RCIN 990065). The observable mechanism across the group is close-worked copying in chalk and pen -- animal anatomy, horse legs, heads, a topographical record of the French years -- preserving the master's studies rather than inventing new compositions. This is the documentary core of Melzi's early career: the same hand that scribed the Codex Trivulzianus and whose italic script is scattered through Leonardo's papers in copies, annotations and collation marks. Nicholl calls him Leonardo's "secretary or amanuensis... intellectual confidant" -- a framing that comes from search snippets and is flagged (see C227). Watchable behaviour: a horse's leg drawn as carefully as a face, a devil's head copied line for line; the sheet is a record, not a performance. Source: Royal Collection Trust entries via leg 2; Wikimedia Commons category listing via leg 2; leg 1 on the scribal role.

**Takeaway:** the first skill is fidelity -- copy the master's sheet until the copy is indistinguishable, and the hand learns what the eye cannot be told.

## M8. Material Wealth Legible in Paint
Solid pure ultramarine, because the painter was a nobleman who did not need to economise on pigment. The 2019 Hermitage conservation of Flora (restorer Maria Shulepova) found the palla -- the blue cloth over Flora's shoulder -- was painted in pure ultramarine throughout: Melzi did not glaze ultramarine over cheaper azurite, as a working painter would, and the yellowed varnish had made the blue look green for a century. The mechanism is economic as much as technical: ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli, was the most expensive pigment in the Renaissance, and using it solid rather than as a glaze is a statement of means. Mazzenta's contemporary verdict, quoted in Rigaud's Treatise on Painting (p.66), fits the same fact from the other side: "Francisco Melzi approached nearer than any one to the manner of De Vinci; he worked little, because he was rich; his pictures are very much finished, they are often confounded with those of his master." Watchable behaviour: the blue in Flora holds a depth no glaze can fake, and it sits beside the white gold-embroidered stola without either one shouting. Source: Hermitage conservation findings via legs 1 and 2; Mazzenta via Wikisource Rigaud p.66 (see Q4).

**Takeaway:** the pigment is the biography -- a rich man's blue, laid on solid, because the painter never had to save it.

## M9. The Greek-Letter Signature
The authorship claim itself as a device: classicising, discreet, on only three works. The Vergine delle Rocce site states the secure core plainly: "The only certain works by Francesco Melzi, signed in Greek letters by the artist, are the Vertumnus and Pomona at the Gemaldegalerie in Berlin, the Flora at the Hermitage in St. Petersburg, and the Young Man with a Parrot, from the Gallarati Scotti collection in Milan." Every other attribution is argued by stylistic similarity to these three. The Greek letters are a learned signature -- a classicising mark, a claim to be a painter in his own right, not a copyist -- and its rarity is the point: three signatures in a career of about five painting years, then silence. Traces of Melzi's signature in Flora's lower-left corner were uncovered only in 1963, which is why the picture spent a century being re-attributed (Leonardo, then Solario, then Giampietrino, then Luini, before Somov's 1899 catalogue settled it as Melzi). Watchable behaviour: the signature is small, Greek, and almost invisible -- the claim is made quietly, and the century of misattribution that followed is what happens when the claim is missed. Source: Vergine delle Rocce site via leg 2; Hermitage Flora page; leg 1 on the attribution history.

**Takeaway:** three signatures, three certain pictures -- the claim is the work, and the rest of the oeuvre is an argument about the claim.

## M10. The Compilation as a Work: Selection, Ordering, Chaptering
The words are Leonardo's; the selection, ordering and chapter structure are Melzi's. The Libro di pittura gathers 944 short chapters from Leonardo's scattered notes, drawn from 18 of his "books," organised into eight books, compiled sometime before 1542; Melzi had difficulty organising the material and left some pages blank. At least five handwritten copies were made from his original before publication (some now at UCLA's Elmer Belt Library of Vinciana). The mechanism is editorial: Leonardo himself never finished a treatise -- his notes were a "collection without order," in his own phrase (Arundel MS 263, 1508: "made up of many sheets which I have copied here, hoping afterwards to arrange them in order," see Q7) -- and Melzi imposed the order, choosing what counted as painting, in what sequence, under what headings. The renaissancetea synthesis puts it plainly: "The Libro di pittura contains Leonardo's ideas, but its organization also reflects editorial decisions made after his death." Scholars still debate how closely the organisation reflects Leonardo's intentions, and how the compilation date bears on it. The technical precondition was unique to Melzi: he was the only person who could decipher Leonardo's left-handed mirror writing, spelling and enigmatic abbreviations (Wikipedia, citing Steinitz 1958). Watchable behaviour: a book that reads as Leonardo thinking, but thinks in Melzi's order -- the chapters are short, the sequence is deliberate, and the blank pages are where the editor could not decide. Source: Wikipedia "A Treatise on Painting" (read end to end); renaissancetea.org; leg 1 on the compilation; Britannica via leg 3.

**Takeaway:** the book is Melzi's painting -- Leonardo supplied the pigment, Melzi supplied the composition, and the blank pages are the pentimenti.


## Evolution
Three phases, from legs 1 and 2. Phase 1, under Leonardo, c.1506-1519 (ages about 15 to 28): the scribe and copyist. Melzi enters Leonardo's circle c.1506 (age about 14-15), travels to Rome in September 1513 (Leonardo's notebook lists the party) and to France in 1516-17, staying at Cloux, Amboise until Leonardo's death on 2 May 1519. His function is scribal and custodial before it is artistic: his elegant italic script is scattered through Leonardo's papers in copies, annotations and collation marks, and he scribed Leonardo's Codex Trivulzianus. The work of this phase is small and derivative in the productive sense: the signed 1510 Head of an Old Man (after a Leonardo relief, inscribed with his age, 17), the Windsor copies of Leonardo's horse, devil and dog studies, the red-chalk Portrait of Leonardo (c.1515-18), the Leda copy (if his) and the Borghetto Virgin of the Rocks (c.1517-20, possibly painted in France under Leonardo's eye while the master's right hand was paralysed). Subjects are Leonardo's subjects; scale is sheet-size; the skill on display is fidelity -- close, punctilious copying -- plus the unique ability to read mirror-writing. French court accounts: Leonardo paid 1,000 gold crowns a year, Melzi 400 -- a salaried companion, not an independent master. Phase 2, back in Milan and Vaprio, c.1519-1520s: the break is sharp and brief. He returns to Italy, marries Angiola di Landriani in 1519 (eight children), is named chamberlain by Francis I in 1520, and produces the three signed paintings in a tight cluster: Vertumnus and Pomona (c.1518-22, begun in France or just after return), Flora (c.1520), and the St Anne and Jasmine Bower Madonnas (c.1520). What changes: subject -- from Leonardo's compositions to his own choices, Ovidian mythology (Vertumnus and Pomona, Flora), erotic-pastoral subjects Leonardo never painted as independent panels, alongside Leonardesque Madonnas; scale -- from chalk sheets to a 185 x 134 cm two-figure panel, the only altarpiece-scale work in his oeuvre; authorship asserted -- the Greek-letter signatures on the three certain works, a learned, classicising signature, a claim to be a painter in his own right, not a copyist; handling -- the Leonardesque toolkit fully internalised and slightly softened, sfumato without Leonardo's geological darkness, the grotto in Flora a leafy bower not a cavern, colour restrained but materially rich (solid ultramarine); composition -- still borrowed (Flora's pose from the Burlington House Cartoon; St Anne's figures from Leonardo's cartoon), but the landscape, the plant programme and the pairing of figures are his own invention. Phase 3, Vaprio, 1520s-1570: he mostly stops painting and administers the papers. This is the honest answer to "did he keep painting?": no securely dated painting exists after c.1520 except the Young Man with a Parrot, which Pietro Marani dates to the 1550s on stylistic and psychological grounds (a nostalgic self-portrait of his younger self) -- an argument, not a document (see C220). For about fifty years he lives at the Villa Melzi, Vaprio d'Adda, as a wealthy nobleman, husband and father of eight, compiling the Codex Urbinas and guarding the manuscripts. Vasari met him in Milan in 1566 and added a passage to the 1568 Lives describing him only as the handsome, courteous old gentleman who owned Leonardo's anatomical manuscripts -- Vasari says nothing about Melzi as a painter. Lomazzo praised him extravagantly as a miniature painter; a 1523 letter from the Ferrarese ambassador Bendedei calls him a skilful painter "as an amateur or dilettante" (1911 Catholic Encyclopedia's reading). Freud's verdict, quoted in the Melzi article, is that Leonardo's pupils could not distinguish themselves from their master and so their careers could not flourish after his death. The 1911 encyclopedia went further and doubted whether he was a painter at all ("There is not an actual authentic work by him that can be mentioned") -- wrong on the facts as we now have them (the Greek-letter signatures settle three works), but right about the shape of the career: a decade of painting inside Leonardo's shadow, a roughly five-year burst of signed independent work, then half a century of custodianship. His pupil Girolamo Figino ("illuminator and disciple of Francesco Melzi") carried the Leonardesque manner into the next generation -- Madonna and Saints after Vertumnus and Pomona, Portrait of Margherita Colleoni after the Mona Lisa -- which is Melzi's real legacy as a teacher: transmission, not innovation.

Early vs mature vs late, stated plainly. Early Melzi (1506-1519) copies: chalk sheets after Leonardo, fidelity as the skill, subjects the master's subjects, scale sheet-size, salary 400 crowns against the master's 1,000. Mature Melzi (1519-1520s) signs: three Greek-letter paintings in about five years, Ovidian subjects Leonardo never painted, one altarpiece-scale panel, solid ultramarine, invented grounds under borrowed figures. Late Melzi (1520s-1570) guards: no dated painting after c.1520 except the argued Parrot portrait, fifty years at Vaprio as custodian of the papers, the Libro di pittura compiled and never published, the manuscripts lent to scholars and kept "with as much care as though they were relics." What stayed constant across all three phases: (1) sfumato as the modelling system, from the 1510 Head of an Old Man to Flora; (2) the Leonardesque female type, downcast gaze and half-smile, from the early copies to Pomona; (3) copying as the primary act -- the early Windsor sheets, the mature St Anne figures, the late compilation are all transcriptions, and the invention is always in the ground, the plants or the chapter order, never in the figure; (4) the invisibility Seybold names -- the closer the copy, the less visible the copyist. The through-line is not a style change but a role change: from the boy who could read the mirror writing, to the painter who signed three pictures in Greek, to the old man who kept the papers like relics and never got them into print.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is one operation repeated at every scale: take the master's system whole, and let the copy be the work. In paint the operation is literal -- sfumato absorbed wholesale, the Leonardesque face repeated, the figures transcribed from the cartoons (M1, M2, M4) -- and the same operation governs the plants, where each one is named and meaningful (M3); the emblem, where the tree is the argument (M5); the grotesques, where the master's caricature is copied as an exercise (M6); the Windsor sheets, where fidelity is the skill (M7); and the book, where Leonardo's words are gathered into Melzi's order (M10). The second constant is the ground: wherever Leonardo left nothing to copy, Melzi invents -- the sumptuous meadow in place of the alluvial riverbank, the leafy grotto built from named plants, the chapter order of a treatise Leonardo never wrote. The third constant is wealth without display: solid ultramarine because the painter did not need to save pigment (M8), a Greek-letter signature so discreet it took a century to notice (M9), a career that stops painting and starts guarding. Against Leonardo the contrast is exact and is the point of the doorway: Leonardo's logic is observation that never ends, the notebooks multiplying faster than the paintings; Melzi's logic is custodianship that ends the observation, the notebooks gathered, ordered, chaptered and then kept like relics. One multiplies the sheets, the other binds them into a book; one leaves the panel wet for sixteen years, the other leaves the pages blank where he cannot decide; one is the master who could not finish a treatise, the other is the pupil who finished it for him and never published it. The essay voice of the study is this: Melzi is not a lesser Leonardo, and not a failed Leonardo; he is the condition without which Leonardo would be a rumour -- the hand that could read the mirror writing, the eye that could copy the horse's leg, the nobleman who could afford the ultramarine and the fifty years, and the editor who turned a pile of sheets into the book the next three centuries learned painting from.

## POSITIONS
- P1. Only three paintings are certainly Melzi's, all signed in Greek letters: Vertumnus and Pomona (Berlin), Flora (Hermitage) and the Portrait of a Young Man with a Parrot (Gallarati Scotti collection, Milan). Every other attribution is argued by stylistic similarity to these three. Source: Vergine delle Rocce site via leg 2; Hermitage Flora page; legs 1 and 2.
- P2. Melzi was the only person who could read Leonardo's left-handed mirror writing, spelling and enigmatic abbreviations -- the technical fact that made the Codex Urbinas possible. Source: Wikipedia "Francesco Melzi" (citing Steinitz 1958); leg 1.
- P3. The Codex Urbinas gathers 944 short chapters from Leonardo's scattered notes, drawn from 18 of his "books," compiled sometime before 1542 and organised into eight books, with some pages left blank where Melzi could not organise the material. Source: Wikipedia "A Treatise on Painting" (read end to end); leg 1.
- P4. Melzi's birth year is unsettled: 1491 is conventional (Wikipedia infobox and lead); the National Gallery gives 1493-1570; Richter's footnote says born 1493; Sorce (2009) says "around 1491 or 1493"; the Wikipedia body says "Born 1481," almost certainly an error; the 14 August 1510 drawing inscribed with his age, 17, implies 1492/93. Source: legs 1 and 2; see C208.
- P5. Melzi inherited the intellectual estate -- books, instruments and portraits -- not the paintings (Salai) and not the garden (split between Battista de Villanis and Salai). The will's own wording is "in remuneration for services and favours done to him in the past." Source: Richter, Notebooks, section 1566, via leg 1; will text via leg 3; see C211.
- P6. French court accounts paid Leonardo 1,000 gold crowns a year and Melzi 400 -- a salaried companion, not an independent master. Source: Wikipedia "Francesco Melzi"; legs 1 and 3.
- P7. Flora was bought for Tsar Nicholas I in 1850 as a Leonardo, re-attributed in turn to Andrea Solario (1871), Giampietrino (1892) and Bernardino Luini (1899), before Somov's 1899 Hermitage catalogue settled it as Melzi; traces of Melzi's signature were uncovered in the lower-left corner in 1963; the 2019 conservation found the palla painted in solid ultramarine and the naked figure modelled first, then clothed. Source: Wikipedia "Flora (Francesco Melzi)" (read end to end); Hermitage Flora page; legs 1 and 2.
- P8. Vertumnus and Pomona (185 x 134 cm) is Melzi's only large-scale, multi-figure independent composition -- by far his biggest painting. Source: leg 2 works catalogue; Warburg Institute Iconographic Database.
- P9. The elm embraced by the grapevine in Vertumnus and Pomona is the picture's argument: the elm without the vine is bare shade, the vine without the elm creeps on the ground, and together they are the marriage emblem of Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV. Source: leg 2 works catalogue.
- P10. Melzi mostly stopped painting after c.1520: no securely dated painting exists after c.1520 except the Young Man with a Parrot, which Pietro Marani dates to the 1550s on stylistic and psychological grounds -- an argument, not a document. Source: leg 2 evolution section; see C220.
- P11. Vasari visited Melzi in Milan in 1566 and found him guarding Leonardo's anatomy papers "with as much care as though they were relics"; Vasari says nothing about Melzi as a painter. Source: Vasari via McCurdy, discoveringdavinci.com; leg 1 (see Q3).
- P12. The papers' dispersal ran from Orazio's attic at Vaprio through Gavardi's theft of 13 volumes in 1587 and Mazenta's recovery to Leoni's cutting of the notebooks into the Codex Atlanticus (1,119 leaves) and the Windsor collection; the Atlanticus passed to Polidoro Calchi, was sold in 1625 to Galeazzo Arconati, and was donated with 11 other Leonardo manuscripts to the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in 1637; the Ambrosiana's Leonardo codices were seized to Paris in 1796 and the Atlanticus returned to Milan in 1815. Source: McMurrich (1930) via leg 3; Wikipedia "Codex Atlanticus"; discoveringdavinci.com.
- P13. The 1651 du Fresne edition of the Treatise was abridged and drawn from copies of Melzi's manuscript, not from the Urbinas codex itself; the full text waited until Manzi's 1817 edition. Source: discoveringdavinci.com; Wikipedia "A Treatise on Painting"; leg 3.
- P14. Queen Christina of Sweden was the dedicatee of both 1651 editions of the Trattato della pittura, not the owner of the Codex Urbinas manuscript; Pope Urban VIII's link is the Barberini Library copy of the Treatise text in Rome in 1630, not ownership of the codex. Source: Sotheby's Bibliotheca Brookeriana lot note; Gutenberg/Rigaud preface; leg 3; see C213 and C214.
- P15. How the Libro di pittura travelled from Melzi's hands to the Urbino ducal library is not established by any source found in this run; do not assert a route. Source: leg 3; see C215.
- P16. La Belle Ferronniere cannot be Melzi's: it is dated 1490-1498 and Melzi was born 1491. Source: leg 2 works catalogue; see C217.
- P17. The Borghetto Virgin of the Rocks was painted on canvas, not panel, and the site argues it was executed in France so it could be rolled and carried back to Milan, possibly under Leonardo's eye while the master's right hand was paralysed. Source: Vergine delle Rocce site (read end to end); leg 2.
- P18. Melzi's pupil Girolamo Figino carried the Leonardesque manner into the next generation: his Madonna and Saints references Melzi's Vertumnus and Pomona, and his Portrait of Margherita Colleoni references the Mona Lisa. Source: Wikipedia "Francesco Melzi" via leg 3; Francesco Albuzio, Memorie (1776).
- P19. The 1911 Catholic Encyclopedia doubted Melzi was a painter at all ("There is not an actual authentic work by him that can be mentioned") -- wrong on the facts as they now stand, the Greek-letter signatures settling three works, but right about the shape of the career. Source: Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) via leg 1.

Melzi's only known verbatim words are the June 1519 letter phrase "sviscerato e ardentissimo amore" about Leonardo's love for his pupils -- translated "uncontrolled and passionate love" or "a selfless and incandescent love." Source: Wikipedia, citing Nuova Antologia (1873), p.960, via leg 1 (see Q2).

## Load-bearing corrections
- C208. Birth year: 1491 (Wikipedia infobox and lead; conventional), 1493 (National Gallery 2019; Richter's footnote to the Notebooks: "born 1493 -- a youth therefore of about 17 in 1510"), "around 1491 or 1493" (Sorce 2009, via renaissancetea.org), "Born 1481" (Wikipedia body text -- almost certainly an error: it would make him 25 when he joined Leonardo and 29 at the 1510 drawing, contradicting the drawing's own "17" inscription), "about 1490" (Catholic Encyclopedia 1913). The evidence: two inscriptions on a drawing dated 14 August 1510 identify him as either 17 or 19 years old -- implying birth 1491 or 1493. This study uses "c.1491-1570" with this note. (Legs 1 and 2.)
- C209. Death year: 1570 (standard; Wikipedia), 1568 (Catholic Encyclopedia 1913; Prabook), 1569/70 (Wikipedia body). This study uses c.1570. (Legs 1 and 2.)
- C210. Will date: McCurdy's 1906 Note-books prints "April 23, 1518"; Wikipedia and the Clos Luce sources say 23 April 1519. The two reconcile: the will is dated 23 April 1518 in the Florentine style (the year beginning 25 March, "before Easter" -- Easter 1519 fell on 24 April), which is 23 April 1519 in modern reckoning. This study gives both, following #517 C199, which treated 1519 as correct. (Leg 1.)
- C211. Vineyard correction: Leonardo's will gives one half of the Milan garden and vineyard to Battista de Villanis (his servant) and the other half to Salai -- NOT to Melzi. Melzi received the books, instruments and portraits, plus the remainder of the pension. One secondary source (oldmasters.academy) wrongly claims Melzi and Salai each got half the vineyard; the will text contradicts it. Sotheby's confirms Salai as "joint heir to half of Leonardo's vineyard" (joint with de Villanis). The Alchetron summary's claim that Salai "inherited several paintings including the Mona Lisa" is disputed -- the will gives the paintings to Melzi; Salai's copies are the likely explanation. (Leg 3.)
- C212. "Fifty years turning the papers into a book" (Doorway D4): 1519 to 1570 is 51 years, but the compilation was made sometime before 1542, within about 23 years of Leonardo's death; the remaining roughly 28 years Melzi kept the papers at Vaprio, lent copies to scholars, and never published. The book appeared in abridged form in 1651, more than eighty years after his death, and in full only in 1817. (Leg 1; parent.)
- C213. Christina of Sweden: dedicatee, not owner. Both 1651 editions of the Trattato della pittura (Italian by du Fresne and French by Freart de Chambray) were dedicated to Queen Christina of Sweden; Farago, Bell and Vecce (Brill 2018) discuss "the rulership of Queen Christina of Sweden, whose patronage of the 1651 printed book" they suggest; the Prado holds a copy of the treatise dedicated to her. No evidence was found that Christina owned the Codex Urbinas manuscript itself. The Codex Argenteus (Silver Bible) story -- Prague loot, Christina's library, De la Gardie, Uppsala -- is a different manuscript and the likely source of the confusion. (Leg 3.)
- C214. Pope Urban VIII: indirect link only. The Treatise text was in the Barberini Library in Rome in 1630 (Barberini = Urban VIII's family; he was pope 1623-44); Cassiano dal Pozzo obtained a copy from it, Poussin designed the figures in 1640, and those copies underlie the 1651 du Fresne edition (Gutenberg/Rigaud preface, citing Venturi). Urban VIII's connection is via the Barberini copy, not ownership of the Urbinas codex itself. (Leg 3.)
- C215. The Melzi-to-Urbino transfer route for Urb. lat. 1270 is NOT established by any source found in this run. What is established: the Libro di pittura "had formerly belonged to the Library of the Dukes of Urbino" (discoveringdavinci.com), and the Urbino ducal library was bought by Pope Alexander VII in 1657 and moved to the Vatican. Do not assert a route between Melzi and Urbino. (Leg 3.)
- C216. Flora re-attribution fringe: an Academia.edu paper argues Flora (and other works) were begun by Leonardo and finished by Melzi, and that the Melzi attribution is a 19th-century error. Mainstream scholarship (Hermitage, National Gallery, Somov 1899, the 1963 signature traces) holds Melzi. Note the dispute; do not adopt the fringe claim. The same paper contains sensational, unsourced claims (for example Salai's death in 1513, a scalp-wig) contradicted by Sotheby's and the will -- do not use it. (Legs 2 and 3.)
- C217. La Belle Ferronniere: dated 1490-1498, Melzi born 1491 -- chronologically impossible as his. Include only as a historical misattribution. (Leg 2.)
- C218. Nymph at the Spring: listed in the Melzi article as "Washington Gallery of Art" (citing Bora et al. 1998); leg 2 could not verify it, and the National Gallery of Art, Washington's Nymph of the Spring is Lucas Cranach the Elder, after 1537 -- a different painting. Probable conflation; do not catalogue as a Melzi work without a fresh check. (Leg 2.)
- C219. Besso "Mona Lisa": a 2021 conservation-science paper argued by histogram analysis that the Besso Mona Lisa "may have been painted by Melzi," but the paper's own result is that its histograms matched none of the three reliably attributed Melzi paintings -- and the three Melzi paintings did not match each other either. Fringe, unaccepted; flagged, do not catalogue. (Leg 2.)
- C220. Young Man with a Parrot: signed in Greek letters, but no verified dimensions, medium or location beyond "Gallarati Scotti collection, Milan (private)"; the 1550s date is Pietro Marani's argument via Nicholl (a nostalgic self-portrait of his younger self), not a document. (Leg 2.)
