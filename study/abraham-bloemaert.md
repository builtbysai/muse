# Abraham Bloemaert: Study Notes

**Site:** Abraham Bloemaert (born Christmas Eve, 25 December 1566, Gorinchem - died 27 January 1651, Utrecht, aged 84), Dutch Golden Age painter, draughtsman and print designer of Utrecht, the teacher who made the Utrecht school and then learned its style back from his own pupils. He was the son of Cornelis Bloemaert I (c. 1540-1593), a Catholic sculptor, architect and surveyor, and Aeltgen Willems; the family moved to 's-Hertogenbosch in 1567, where Cornelis worked on restoring the interior of St Janskerk after the Iconoclastic Fury, returned to Gorinchem around 1571, and in 1576 Cornelis was appointed city architect and engineer of Utrecht. Abraham's mother died some time before the move (exact date not established in the sources read), and Cornelis remarried Marigen Goortsdr, innkeeper of het Schilt van Bourgongen. Bloemaert probably attended the Latin school in Utrecht. His first art education came from his father, drawing copies after the Antwerp master Frans Floris (1519/20-1570). He was then a pupil of Gerrit Splinter (himself a pupil of Floris) and of Joos de Beer (also Floris-trained); Karel van Mander, who describes Bloemaert at length in his Schilder-boeck (1604), calls his subsequent training "fairly erratic", and Bloemaert himself is quoted as sighing to his pupils that he wished he had once in his life seen a good master paint. From the age of 15 or 16 he spent three years in Paris (1581-1583), studying six weeks under a "Jehan Bassot" (possibly Jean Cousin the Younger) and then under a "Maistre Herry" (Henri?), and receiving further training at the School of Fontainebleau from his fellow countryman Hieronymus Francken I. He returned to Utrecht in 1583, just before the French Wars of Religion destroyed much of the work at the Chateau of Fontainebleau. When his father was appointed city architect of Amsterdam in 1591, Bloemaert accompanied him; on 1 May 1592 he married Judith van Schonenburch, from a prominent Utrecht patrician Catholic family, and in Amsterdam he rapidly rose to become a respected painter whose work entered important collections. His celebrated Mannerist Death of the Children of Niobe dates from 1591. On his father's death at the end of 1593 he returned to Utrecht and remained there until his death; within six months of his return he was appointed dean of the Saddlers' Guild (painters in Utrecht were then still folded into the saddlemakers' guild, having no Guild of St Luke of their own), received burgher status, and moved with his family into a former priest's house on the Nieuwe Gracht. His first wife died of the plague at the end of 1599; he remarried within a year to Gerarda de Rooij (de Roij), daughter of a brewer, who bore him fourteen children, including four sons - Hendrick, Adriaen, Frederick and Cornelis - who all became painters or engravers. Along with Joachim Wtewael (1566-1638) and Paulus Moreelse (1571-1638), Bloemaert became one of the leading Utrecht painters of his generation. In 1611, together with Wtewael and Moreelse, he was one of the founders of the new Utrecht Guild of Saint Luke, and became its deken (dean) in 1618; in 1612, together with Paulus Moreelse, he founded an "academy" where artists could receive drawing lessons, and Bloemaert and Moreelse were its most important teachers. Note the two foundings are distinct events a year apart: the guild (1611) was co-founded with Wtewael and Moreelse; the academy (1612) was with Moreelse alone (see C433). In 1618 Bloemaert had to resign as elected dean of the painters' guild when the Counter-Remonstrants assumed power in the city - his Catholicism barred him from an active role in Utrecht city politics, though it also brought him commissions from Utrecht's clandestine Catholic churches, from Jesuits, and from patrons in the southern Netherlands. His reputation extended far beyond Utrecht: commissions for high altars in 's-Hertogenbosch in 1610, 1615 and 1622, a monumental altarpiece for the Jesuit church in Brussels c. 1624, work for the stadtholder's court at The Hague, and visits from Elizabeth Stuart, former queen of Bohemia, in 1626 and Peter Paul Rubens in 1627 (see C431). He was a noted teacher: the names of thirty-three of his pupils are known, and dozens more may have passed through his studio between 1590 and 1650. His pupils included the acclaimed Caravaggisti Hendrick ter Brugghen (1588-1629), Gerrit van Honthorst (1592-1656) and Jan van Bijlert (1597/98-1671), the Italianate landscapists Cornelis van Poelenburch, Jan Both and Jan Baptist Weenix, and his four sons. He was NOT the teacher of Dirck van Baburen: the 1611 Utrecht Guild of St Luke records list Baburen as a pupil of Paulus Moreelse, and the study does not repeat the loose secondary claim that Baburen was Bloemaert's pupil (see C427). He never travelled to Italy and never met Caravaggio; he absorbed Caravaggism second-hand, through his pupils returning from Rome, above all Honthorst (back 1620), ter Brugghen and Bijlert. In his later years he focused on a teaching manual, published by his son Frederick in 1650 under the title Artes Apellae Liber, later known as De Teeken-const / Konstryk Tekenboek, which stayed in use as the basis of artistic training in Utrecht into the 19th century. He died in Utrecht on 27 January 1651 and was buried in the Catharijnekerk. More than 200 paintings are known, about 1,500 drawings (some counts give 1,700), and over 600 prints designed by him and engraved by others. ## The life

Bloemaert's life is a long arc with no single crisis in it, which is itself the point: he lived 84 years, painted for about 65 of them, and spent almost all of them in one city. He was born on Christmas Eve 1566 in Gorinchem, the son of a Catholic sculptor-architect who moved the family first to 's-Hertogenbosch, then back to Gorinchem, then to Utrecht in 1576 when Cornelis became city architect there. The boy's training was, by his own later admission, a mess. His father taught him to copy Frans Floris; Gerrit Splinter, a pupil of Floris whom van Mander calls a "cladder" (dauber) and a drunk, lasted barely two weeks; Joos de Beer, another Floris pupil, was "not the best of painters"; a bailiff named Coenraet Strick "could but little paint"; and the one good plan - an apprenticeship to Anthonie Blocklandt van Montfoort, the famous Floris pupil - died with Blocklandt's sudden death in 1583. So at fifteen or sixteen Bloemaert was sent to Paris, where he spent three years: six weeks under a "Jehan Bassot" (possibly Jean Cousin the Younger), eighteen months under a "Maistre Herry", and then real training at Fontainebleau under his fellow countryman Hieronymus Francken I, who worked at the court and gave the boy the elegant Mannerist figure-groups that would shape his early painting. Neither Bassot nor Herry is documented beyond van Mander; their identities are conjectural and are not asserted here. He came home in 1583 (the National Gallery says 1585; the 1583 date is better sourced through van Mander, but the conflict is unresolved, see C430), just before the Wars of Religion wrecked Fontainebleau.

The next decade is the making of the career. Amsterdam, 1591-1593, following his father to the city-architect post: marriage to Judith van Schonenburch in May 1592, rapid rise, the Niobe of 1591 aimed at Rudolf II's Prague through the Amsterdam banker Sion Luz. Then his father's death at the end of 1593 brings him back to Utrecht for good, and within six months he is dean of the Saddlers' Guild - the painters had no guild of their own yet - and a burgher in a former priest's house on the Nieuwe Gracht. His first wife dies of plague at the end of 1599; he remarries within a year to Gerarda de Rooij, daughter of a brewer, who bears him fourteen children. Four sons become artists. The institutional work follows: the Guild of St Luke in 1611 with Wtewael and Moreelse, dean in 1618, forced to resign the deanship the same year when the Counter-Remonstrants take power - his Catholicism bars him from city politics, and the same Catholicism feeds him the clandestine-church altarpieces that sustain his devotional practice for the next thirty years. The drawing academy of 1612 with Moreelse is the other founding, and the more consequential one for this study: it is the machine that makes the Utrecht school, and Bloemaert runs it.

The teaching is the biography's centre. Thirty-three named pupils, dozens more unrecorded, between 1590 and 1650. The three Caravaggisti - ter Brugghen, Honthorst, Bijlert - all go to Rome and come home around 1620, and the master, then in his mid-fifties, learns from them: the Flute Player of 1621 and the Supper at Emmaus of 1622 are a 55-year-old painting in the new Roman manner, a subject he and ter Brugghen had introduced to Utrecht and which was "greatly imitated". Rubens visits the studio in 1627 (Stadel says 1626; Schwartz, from Sandrart, dates it July 1627 - see C431) and is fete by Honthorst; Elizabeth Stuart visits in 1626 and asks for a portrait of her dog. He keeps painting into his eighties - Landscape with Peasants Resting is dated 1650, the year before his death - and in his last years he turns his drawings into the Tekenboek, the part-to-whole drawing manual his son Frederick publishes in 1650, which teaches Utrecht to draw into the 19th century. He dies on 27 January 1651 (the Leiden Collection says 13 January; unresolved, see C429) and is buried in the Catharijnekerk.

**Takeaway:** a career with no rupture and no Italy, held together by two institutions he founded and one reversal - the teacher who becomes his pupils' pupil at fifty-five.

## M1. The figura serpentinata figure

The early Mannerist body is elongated, muscular, and twisted along its own axis - head turned one way, hips the other, limbs spiralling. Watchable behaviour: no figure ever stands at rest; every body is mid-twist, so the eye is forced to travel around the figure rather than rest on it. In the Niobe (1591) the children die in a cascade of such twists, each body a different rotation of the same spiral grammar. The influence runs from Fontainebleau through Spranger (known through Goltzius's engravings) and the Haarlem circle of Goltzius and Cornelis Cornelisz. van Haarlem.

**Takeaway:** the twist is the unit of the early picture; a Bloemaert figure is never a pose, it is a rotation caught halfway.

## M2. Crowd-as-frieze composition

Early history paintings pack many large figures into a shallow, frieze-like band across the canvas, with little recession. Watchable behaviour: the picture reads as a wall of bodies; the eye moves laterally, figure to figure, rather than into depth. The 2011 exhibition summary states the shift plainly: "overly crowded compositions with twisted, long bodies" gave way to "clearly constructed compositions with large figures and one key moment."

**Takeaway:** the early Bloemaert is a wall, not a window; depth arrives only when the crowd thins.

## M3. The single key moment

From c. 1610 onward, the crowd thins and the composition organises around one legible action - one gesture, one exchange of looks. Watchable behaviour: a late Bloemaert can be summarised in one sentence ("she offers the grapes"; "he plays the flute"), where an early one needs a paragraph. This is the compositional mechanism the exhibition singled out as his mature signature, and it is the direct opposite of M2: the frieze collapses into a sentence.

**Takeaway:** maturity, for Bloemaert, is subtraction - one action, clearly staged, instead of twenty bodies competing.

## M4. Pastel-to-bright palette arc

His palette evolved across his career "from pastels to brighter colours" (Centraal Museum). Early Mannerism uses cool, acidic, artificial colour - pale flesh, icy blues and sharp pinks that announce artifice. Later work warms and saturates: the Caravaggesque pictures use deep shadow against hot, lit flesh and saturated drapery. Watchable behaviour: you can roughly date a Bloemaert by temperature - cool and pastel means early, warm and bright against dark means late.

**Takeaway:** temperature is chronology; the palette warms as the composition cools down.

## M5. Colourist over draughtsman

The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica (quoted in Wikipedia) judged that Bloemaert "excelled more as a colourist than as a draughtsman." Watchable behaviour: colour does the emotional and structural work - a red cloak or a patch of blue sky carries the composition - while drawing stays fluent rather than exact. This is a contemporary-critical mechanism, not a technical one, and it is arguable (see P7, which reverses it for the drawings).

**Takeaway:** in the paintings, colour leads and line follows; the claim is a judgement about the oils, not the sheets.

## M6. The motif stock / Berlin Album method

Bloemaert drew motifs from life - peasant cottages, dovecotes, trees, sheds, fences, crops, utensils - on wanderings around Utrecht, sketching summarily in black chalk on the spot and working them up afterwards in the studio with pen, brown ink and coloured washes. The studies were kept in albums and portfolios (the "Berlin Album": 50+ landscape studies, 1583-1591, now mostly in the Berlin Kupferstichkabinett; 65 drawings in the Bolten catalogue nos. 1336-1400) as a workshop stock of motifs. Watchable behaviour: the same thatched shed, the same dovecote, the same crooked fence recurs across drawings, prints and paintings, seen from different angles and in different combinations - a modular landscape built from a personal clip-library. A cart-shed in one drawing reappears in a Matham print of 1605 and a Visscher print of 1620.

**Takeaway:** the landscape is assembled, not observed whole; Bloemaert walks, collects, and recombines.

## M7. Small narrative, large landscape

From the first decade of the 17th century he formulated landscape paintings in which "religious or mythological figures play a subordinate role" - the biblical or mythological incident is small, tucked into a corner or middle distance, while the country scene does the work. Watchable behaviour: in a landscape with ruins and the rape of Ganymede, Ganymede is a speck in the upper right; the eye spends its time on the ruin, the trees, the peasants. Country life, depicted with increasing naturalism, became his favourite subject.

**Takeaway:** the story shrinks until the landscape is the subject; the sacred incident is staffage, and the farm is the sermon.

## M8. The picturesque ruin / dilapidation aesthetic

His landscape eye is drawn to the broken, the leaning, the patched: "dilapidated and ruined buildings, sheds and dovecots," "two ramshackle houses," farms with sagging thatch. Watchable behaviour: verticals lean, roofs sag, walls are patched with different materials - the landscape is built out of imperfection, and the imperfection is the charm. This is the direct opposite of the idealised Mannerist landscape of his early history paintings.

**Takeaway:** the ruin is not a backdrop, it is the taste; Bloemaert's landscape loves what is falling down.

## M9. Foreground still-life of vegetables and plants

Plants and vegetables frequently appear as foreground motifs, and in a small group of highly finished drawings (c. 1610, the Unicorno group) they provide "more or less the entire composition" - a large cabbage at the centre, leaves and roots filling the sheet, with a separate preparatory study for the cabbage alone. Watchable behaviour: the foreground is not a stage but a subject; the viewer is put at ground level among cabbages. The same motif migrates into the Landscape with Vegetables in the Foreground painting and into Bolswert's 1613-14 farmhouse print series.

**Takeaway:** the cabbage is allowed to be the whole picture; the foreground stops being a threshold and becomes the subject.

## M10. Pen + wash drawing technique

His characteristic drawing procedure, described by van Mander and confirmed by the surviving sheets: a summary sketch in black chalk, then a drawing in pen and brown ink, then coloured washes (brown, green, blue-grey) applied with the brush. Van Mander: "he does a great deal from life and he has a very clever manner of drawing and penmanship to which he then adds some watercolours so that it looks particularly good." Watchable behaviour: the line stays visible and calligraphic under the wash - the wash tints rather than covers - so a Bloemaert drawing reads as a drawing that has been coloured, not a painting in miniature. The green wash of the Unicorno vegetable drawings is unusually decorative and is a dating and attribution marker.

**Takeaway:** line first, wash second, and the line never disappears; the drawing advertises that it is a drawing.

## M11. The motif-sheet / study-sheet system

Separate from finished drawings, Bloemaert produced sheets of studies - three arms and hands, a head, a foot - drawn in red chalk, not as preparation for a specific painting but as a "model-book or sketchbook of motifs to be copied by Bloemaert's students." Around 140 such studies, mostly red chalk, were once in the collection of Andre Giroux and dispersed in Paris in 1904; Bolten dates them 1595-1630. Watchable behaviour: a single hand or arm floats on a blank sheet, drawn large, complete in itself - a body part as a reusable unit.

**Takeaway:** the body is taught in pieces; a hand on a blank sheet is a complete lesson.

## M12. Drawing-to-print transfer by stylus

Engraver Jacob Matham transferred Bloemaert's Danae Receiving the Golden Rain (drawing c. 1610) to copper by laying a black-coated intermediary sheet on a waxed plate, placing the drawing on top, and tracing every compositional line with a metal stylus. Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI) at the Morgan Library shows incised lines following Bloemaert's pen lines throughout the entire composition - figures and background alike - and reveals that Matham changed the composition during transfer (a cherub's arm, hidden by drapery in the drawing, is shown holding back a curtain in the print; Jupiter's head is far more realised in the print than the few strokes in the drawing). Watchable behaviour: the print is not a copy of the drawing but a second, slightly different work - the engraver as co-author.

**Takeaway:** the print is a second original; the stylus copies the lines and the engraver edits the picture.

## M13. The print-publishing machine

Bloemaert designed 600+ prints, engraved by others: Jacob Matham, Jan Saenredam, Jan Muller, Boetius a Bolswert (and his brother Schelte), and his sons Cornelis and Frederick. The pattern: Bloemaert supplies the inventor design; the engraver translates it; series are issued by publishers (Cornelis Dankerts, Amsterdam, 1614 farmhouse series; Hendrick Aertssens, Antwerp, 1619 Sylva Anachoretica - 25 male and 25 female hermits with Latin texts). Bolswert "carefully imitated aspects of his graphic style" and has been described as his pupil; the two "worked closely." Watchable behaviour: the same Bloemaert composition exists in multiple media at once - drawing, engraving, painting - each version slightly different, spreading his inventions across Europe without him leaving Utrecht.

**Takeaway:** he never left Utrecht, but his designs did; the workshop is a publishing house, not a room.

## M14. The Tekenboek pedagogy

The Konstryk Tekenboek (first issued c. 1650, published by Frederick; 100 plates in five parts, growing to 120 in six parts, then 160 in eight fascicules under Visscher in 1682, 166 subjects in the 1740 Ottens/Picart edition) is a loosely organised collection of visual models with no theoretical text - unlike his friend Crispijn van de Passe's didactic La prima parte della luce del dipingere (1643-44). Its internal sequence: heads, hands and limbs, then entire figures, then religious figures, plus a dozen domesticated animals. The drawings were recopied by Abraham in a uniform technique (four dated 1648, one 1650) and engraved mostly in reverse by Frederick. Watchable behaviour: learning by copying body parts in a fixed order - the eye is trained on the fragment before the whole - and the book stayed in use as the basis of artistic training in Utrecht into the 19th century. "In almost every large collection of old master drawings, there are drawn copies ... after single leaves or parts of Bloemaert's influential Tekenboek."

**Takeaway:** the curriculum is the order of the plates - fragment first, whole second, and no words at all.

## M15. Reverse influence: the master who learns from his pupils

Bloemaert never went to Italy, yet his style changed in his fifties and sixties because his pupils came back from Rome with Caravaggio. The National Gallery puts it plainly: "Bloemaert was a good learner too and rapidly assimilated the new ideas his pupils brought back from Italy." Watchable behaviour: a 55-year-old master painting a Flute Player (1621) in the new Roman manner - half-length, single light source, a musician caught mid-note - a subject he and ter Brugghen had introduced to Utrecht and which was "greatly imitated." The teacher becomes the student of his students; the workshop is a two-way street.

**Takeaway:** the influence runs backwards; the school teaches its founder, and the 1621 Flute Player is the proof.

**W1. Bacchus and Ceres (two tondi, a pair; a third, Venus, lost) - late 1580s, probably Utrecht.** Oil on panel, tondo, 27 in. diameter (c. 68.6 cm) each. Faringdon Collection, Buscot Park. The two panels were formerly attributed to Frans Floris (Bacchus) and Hendrick Goltzius (Ceres); Roethlisberger reattributed them to Bloemaert ("Early Abraham Bloemaert", Tableau, Dec 1994) and calls them among his earliest and finest works. Aernout van Buchell records seeing three roundels - Bacchus, Ceres and Venus - in the Utrecht home of Adriaan van der Burch in April 1590, and again singles out the Bacchus and Ceres roundels in 1591; Roethlisberger argues the Buscot pair are those very pictures, with the Venus now missing (he reconstructs it as a frontal, bare-breasted figure of the same size between them). What it shows: the Fontainebleau/Haarlem manner already fully formed before 1590 - small, precious, eroticised mythological heads in the round, learned from prints after Floris and Spranger, and the earliest datable evidence of his hand. The lost Venus is a genuine loss, not a maybe: van Buchell saw three, two survive.

**W2. Apollo and Diana Punishing Niobe by Killing her Children (The Death of the Children of Niobe / Niobe Mourning Her Children) - signed and dated 1591.** Oil on canvas. Statens Museum for Kunst, Copenhagen, inv. KMSsp342. Dimensions: SMK gives 203 x 249.5 cm; geerts.com gives 204 x 249.5 cm - a 1 cm discrepancy, flagged (see D2 in the disputes list below). Signed and dated 1591 (Leiden Collection, citing Roethlisberger and Bok 1993, no. 14). Owned in Amsterdam by the banker Sion Luz. Van Mander (Schilder-boeck, 1604) says it was originally intended for Emperor Rudolf II in Prague. SMK's own text: the style "suits the drama of the tale, full of contrasting shifts between colours, light, and shade as well as complicated poses with bodies viewed from extreme angles", and at 24 Bloemaert was "closely approximating his great role model Cornelis Cornelisz van Haarlem", who with van Mander and Goltzius had made Haarlem the centre of Haarlem Mannerism. The Leiden Collection calls it "clearly reflect[ing] the influence of Bartholomeus Spranger (1546-1611), court painter in Prague". What it shows: the peak of the young Mannerist - a huge, crowded, multi-figure massacre staged as a competition piece in the Spranger/Haarlem idiom, painted for the most ambitious market in northern Europe (Prague, via Amsterdam). The first securely dated large-scale history painting in the oeuvre.

**W3. The Judgement of Paris - c. 1590-1600 (a "Wooded landscape with the Judgement of Paris" is listed as 1592).** Oil on canvas, 73 x 99 cm (28 3/4 x 39 in.). Private collection (London, per the Warburg Institute Iconographic Database, c. 1590-1600). Dimensions and Roethlisberger catalogue no. 17 from a Christie's lot note; the same picture was sold as "Spranger" (Randall Davies collection by 1929; Sotheby's London, 10 February 1947, lot 154; Arcade Gallery; Christie's London, 2 December 1983, lot 65; exhibited Santa Barbara Museum of Art, 1951). What it shows: the small mythological cabinet picture running alongside the big Niobe - Paris, Venus, Juno and Minerva set in a wooded landscape, the figure group still Sprangerian but the landscape already doing compositional work. FLAG: the Wikimedia Commons paintings list separately records a "Wooded landscape with the Judgement of Paris", dated 1592, with no location given. Whether that is this same picture or a second version was NOT established in this leg; do not merge them without checking Roethlisberger no. 17 against the 1592 listing.

**W4. The Marriage (Wedding) of Peleus and Thetis - c. 1593 (Leiden) / about 1595 (National Gallery).** Alte Pinakothek, Munich. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (no museum page opened end to end; no dimensions surfaced in search). The Leiden Collection: "Bloemaert's Marriage of Peleus and Thetis from around 1593 derived directly from Spranger's famous Marriage of Amor and Psyche, of which Goltzius had made a print in 1587." The National Gallery agrees on the Spranger influence and dates it "about 1595". What it shows: the working method of the early phase laid bare - a Spranger composition taken over wholesale through a Goltzius print, re-painted in Bloemaert's own hand. Compare the 1638 Feast of the Gods (separate list): the same wedding subject returns 45 years later as a completely different kind of picture. This is the early pole of that pair.

**W5. Moses Striking the Rock - 1596.** Oil on canvas, 79.7 x 108 cm (31.4 x 43 in.) per EN Wikipedia (citing metmuseum.org); geerts.com and the content farms round it to 80 x 108 cm. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, acc. 1972.171. The Wikipedia article (27 lines, read end to end): "Bloemaert's painting features many hallmarks of Late Renaissance Mannerism... The figures seen in Moses are preternaturally muscled, and have noble bearings... inspired by contemporary Italian art." What it shows: Mannerism at its most muscular and Italianate - a crowd of over-muscled Israelites, women, cattle and a dog arranged around the rock, the biblical narrative serving as a pretext for the body. Dated the same decade as the Niobe, it is the smaller, denser counterpart: history painting as anatomy exhibition. FLAG: dimensions rest on Wikipedia citing the Met; the Met catalogue page itself was NOT opened end to end in this leg (one attempted open hit the wrong object).

**W6. The Preaching of St John the Baptist (Landscape with the Ministry of John the Baptist) - 1595-1600.** Oil on canvas, reported 139 x 188 cm. Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, inv. SK-A-3746 (per the Wikimedia Commons paintings list, which titles it Landscape with the Ministry of John the Baptist). Dimensions NOT VERIFIED: the 139 x 188 cm figure comes only from geerts.com; the Rijksmuseum object page was not opened end to end. What it shows: the turn that will dominate the next fifty years - the sacred subject demoted to a staffage group inside a landscape. John preaches to a crowd at the left while the landscape, trees and distance carry the picture. FLAG: title variance - geerts calls it The Preaching of St John the Baptist, the Commons/Rijksmuseum data call it Landscape with the Ministry of John the Baptist; treat as one picture, but the identification of the two titles was not confirmed against the Rijksmuseum catalogue in this leg. A second, later John the Baptist Preaching (Nancy, c. 1620) is W12 below; do not confuse them.

### Phase 2 - The classicising turn: landscape takes over, figures get weight (c. 1600-1615)

**W7. Adoration of the Shepherds - 1600.** Oil on canvas, reported 78 x 107 cm. National Museum of Art, Architecture and Design (Nasjonalmuseet), Oslo. Date, dimensions and location from the content farms only (artsdot/wahooart) - dimensions NOT VERIFIED; the Nasjonalmuseet object page was not opened. The farms agree the Oslo museum holds "a version" of the subject, "considered one of the finest examples of Bloemaert's work" - that superlative is farm copy, not a museum judgement, and is not repeated here as fact. What it shows (if the 1600 date holds): the devotional subject that Bloemaert will paint repeatedly for the next forty years, already at the turn of the century - and the beginning of the version problem that runs through his whole career (multiple Adorations in Oslo, the Louvre, and elsewhere; see the separate list). FLAG: there is also a Louvre Adoration of the Shepherds dated 1612 (287 x 229 cm, farms only) and the farms conflate the versions; the Oslo 1600 picture must not be given the Louvre dimensions.

**W8. Farm Landscape with Tobias and the Angel - 1610.** Centraal Museum, Utrecht, inv. 21031. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (Commons list entry only). What it shows: the mature form of the W6 device, now fully confident - a farm landscape is the picture, and Tobias with the angel is a small travelling pair inside it. Dated 1610, it sits exactly on the National Gallery's hinge ("around 1610 his style shifted from Mannerism to a more classicising approach") and is the earliest dated landscape in the catalogue where the landscape, not the history, is the subject. Compare the Hermitage Landscape with Tobias and the Angel (undated, inv. GE-3545) in the separate list - a second, later treatment of the same pairing.

**W9. Juno - c. 1610 - DEMOTED: NOT A PAINTING.** The National Gallery of Art, Washington, lists a Juno by Abraham Bloemaert and Boetius Adams Bolswert, c. 1610, accession 2012.92.120 - as an etching on laid paper, not a painting; the Art Institute of Chicago holds the same subject, c. 1610, as an etching on paper (reference 2023.84). Leg 2 catalogued it as a painting on the strength of a Wikipedia gallery caption; the parent's search verified first-hand that the surviving NGA Juno is a Bolswert etching after Bloemaert. The entry is therefore demoted from the paintings catalogue to the separate list, where it belongs with the prints (see C436). What the confusion shows is worth keeping: the Juno subject itself is real in Bloemaert's painted oeuvre - van Mander's friend Jacques Razet owned three "large, circular tronies of Venus, Juno, and Pallas Athena" by Bloemaert, probably the same type as the Bacchus and Ceres tondi of W1 - but no Juno painting is securely located in this leg, and the c. 1610 Washington Juno must not be cited as one.

**W10. The Four Evangelists - ca. 1612-15.** Oil on canvas. Princeton University Art Museum, object no. y1991-41. Signed on the book, bottom left: "A. Bloemart fe." Dimensions NOT VERIFIED (the Princeton page snippet did not include them). Princeton's text: the four evangelists with their symbols (Matthew/angel, Mark/lion, Luke/ox, John/eagle); the subject is "notable for its relative doctrinal neutrality at a time when Protestantism was the dominant religion of the Dutch Republic and Catholicism was officially restricted yet secretly practiced" - over a third of Utrecht was Catholic, Bloemaert among them, and he took commissions from the city's hidden Catholic churches. Provenance: Gommer Spranger collection, Amsterdam (1638 inventory); sales 1734, 1769; Sotheby's London 1988; Christie's New York 1991. What it shows: the classicising turn as a Catholic statement - four massive, closely packed, half-length male figures, each with his attribute, painted for a confessional audience that needed its saints without a public altarpiece. The theme, Princeton notes, disappears after 1621; Bloemaert's pupil ter Brugghen and Rubens prolonged it. The signature on the book is part of the picture's game: the painter signs inside the sacred text.

### Phase 3 - The Caravaggesque contact: the pupils come home (c. 1619-1624)

**W11. Farm with the Prodigal Son (Gehoft mit dem verlorenen Sohn) - 1619.** Oil on oak panel, 56 x 71.5 cm. Kunsthaus Zurich, inv. KS 18 (Betty and David Koetser Foundation, 1986). Signed and dated lower right on a stone: "ABloemaert fe: / 1619". FLAG: the Kunsthaus entry itself notes the inscription as "1619 [1612?]" - the last digits are not entirely secure. Roethlisberger 1993, no. 261. The Kunsthaus description: the son, having squandered his inheritance and become a swineherd, "is now excluded from eating them, turns to God in prayer full of repentance"; the parable (Luke 15:11-32) "was repeatedly treated by Bloemaert". What it shows: the small panel as moral theatre - the prodigal kneeling in prayer at the farmyard edge, pigs and farm buildings doing the storytelling. Painted the year before Honthorst and Baburen returned to Utrecht (1620-21) and the year ter Brugghen was already back; the panel format and the low-life piety are the ground the returning Caravaggisti will build on. Compare the large Prodigal Son as Swineherd of 1637 (Ranger's House, London) in the separate list.

**W12. John the Baptist Preaching (La predication de saint Jean-Baptiste) - c. 1620 / 1620s.** Musee des Beaux-Arts de Nancy, inv. MNR 461 (D61 1-1). Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (Commons list entry and the Nancy collections notice only). The MNR inventory number matters: MNR = Musees Nationaux Recuperation - works recovered from Germany after the Second World War, held by the French state; the Nancy notice also carries the tags "1797" and "Fuhrermuseum" (the Linz museum project), i.e. this picture passed through Nazi-era looting channels before reaching Nancy. What it shows: the W6 subject twenty-five years on, now with the Caravaggesque crowd - a dense, half-length congregation listening in the open air, the preacher's gesture doing the work that the landscape did in 1595-1600. FLAG: the Commons row also carries the stray figure "1589" alongside "1620s"; the 1589 is unexplained (possibly a cataloguing artefact) and is NOT adopted. Do not confuse with W6.

**W13. The Flute Player - 1621.** Centraal Museum, Utrecht, inv. 6083b. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (Commons list entry only). What it shows: the single-figure genre-musician picture, painted the year Honthorst and Baburen came home - Bloemaert, at 55, taking up the motif his pupils were bringing back from Rome (the Leiden Collection notes Bloemaert's Supper at Emmaus of 1622 and a Flute Player "painted a year earlier", i.e. 1621, as the pair that shows him learning from them; the same motif runs through ter Brugghen's 1621 Flute and Fife Players, on which see study #529). FLAG - location correction: this picture is in the Centraal Museum, Utrecht. It is NOT in the Residenzgalerie Salzburg; the Salzburg Bloemaert musician picture is The Bagpiper. Several secondary sites conflate the two (see C432).

**W14. Supper at Emmaus (Emmaus Disciples) - 1622.** Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium, Brussels, inv. 3705. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (Commons list entry only). The Leiden Collection biography names "Bloemaert's Supper at Emmaus from 1622" as the companion to the 1621 Flute Player in the passage about his response to his pupils' Caravaggism. What it shows: a sacred subject re-staged as a candlelit interior with half-length figures at a table - the compositional grammar of the Utrecht Caravaggisti (Honthorst's candlelight, ter Brugghen's table groups) adopted by the teacher himself. It is the clearest single picture of the "Baburen story in reverse": the master learning the new manner from his own pupils.

**W15. Lot and His Daughters - signed and dated 1624.** Oil on canvas. National Gallery, London, inv. NG6701, Room 24. Dimensions DISPUTED between two good sources (see C434): the Leiden Collection catalogue entry (AB-100, 2017) gives 167 x 232.4 cm; the National Gallery's own page gives 165.5 x 228.5 cm. Signed and dated in dark paint, lower right: "A. Bloemaert fe. 1624". The National Gallery page (39 lines, read end to end): "a spectacular example of the later style of Abraham Bloemaert... attributed to several artists over the years, including Peter Paul Rubens. However, the discovery of Bloemaert's signature and the date 1624 during the picture's restoration in 2004 showed it to be a fine example of a work painted at the height of the painter's artistic maturity." Attribution history (Leiden + CODART): around 1900 attributed to Rubens on the basis of a false signature applied in the 19th century; most of the 20th century ascribed to Jacob Jordaens; sold in 2004 attributed to Bloemaert's son Hendrick; the 2004 restoration found Abraham's signature and date; identified as the "grand gallery picture" auctioned 14 February 1811 in London, said in the sale catalogue to have belonged to King Charles II. Acquired by the National Gallery in January 2024 (announced 3 January 2024; on loan from a private collection for the four previous years; bought with a legacy from Mrs Martha Doris and Mr Richard Hillman Bailey, 2023). The first Bloemaert in the National Gallery collection. CODART: "indebted to the work of Bloemaert's pupils, notably that of Gerrit van Honthorst of the 1620s" and "keeping up with the latest trends in painting with the gusto of a budding young artist" at 57. Sources: Lucas van Leyden and Goltzius treatments of the same subject (Leiden). What it shows: the 1624 moment in its fullest form - a monumental, morally charged Old Testament scene (Lot, drunk, seduced by his daughters after Sodom), painted with the scale and flesh of Rubens/Jordaens and the candlelit warmth of Honthorst, but signed by the 57-year-old teacher. The picture that proves the "Caravaggesque phase" was not imitation but absorption.

**W16. Parable of the Wheat and the Tares (Landscape with the Parable of the Tares among the Wheat) - 1624.** Walters Art Museum, Baltimore, acc. 37.2505 (Walters artwork ID 26419). Dimensions reported as 100 x 133 cm - NOT VERIFIED (content farms only; the Walters object page was not opened). Medium: oil on canvas (the farms' "acrylic on canvas" is false and is rejected). Gift of the Dr. Francis D. Murnaghan Fund, 1973; formerly Justice James A. Murnaghan Collection, Dublin, by 1929. RKDimages ID 296275. The Walters/Commons description (via the Commons file page): the devil, horned and tailed, sows tares among the wheat while lazy peasants sleep; the two naked sleepers allude to Adam and Eve; the dovecote stands for the morally lazy, the goat for lust, the peacock for pride. "Bloemaert was gifted in depicting natural detail, but he never painted pure landscapes, preferring pictures with a lesson." What it shows: the landscape-with-a-lesson at its most systematic - every animal and object is a moral emblem, and the landscape itself (wheat field, dovecote, farm buildings) is the sermon. The Leiden Collection groups it with the other two 1624 pictures (W15 and W17) as stylistically coherent: 1624 is the year the new manner locks in. FLAG: a second, much later treatment of the same parable, The Devil Sowing Tares among the Wheat, dated 1650, is in the Centraal Museum (inv. 9575) - see the separate list.

**W17. Adoration of the Magi - 1624.** Oil on canvas, 168.8 x 193.7 cm. Centraal Museum, Utrecht, inv. 2575. Dimensions from the Leiden Collection Lot and His Daughters entry, fig. 4 caption (a museum-catalogue source, accepted; the Centraal Museum object page itself was not opened end to end). The Leiden Collection: the Lot "corresponds stylistically to the two other paintings by Bloemaert dated 1624: Parable of the Wheat and the Tares at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore, and Adoration of the Magi in the Centraal Museum, Utrecht". The Leiden biography adds the sting that defines Bloemaert's late manner: "his work would always retain Mannerist traits, as can be seen clearly in the 1624 Adoration of the Magi, in which the group of soldiers in the background could also pass as a separate work by Spranger." What it shows: the coexistence thesis in one canvas - a Baroque adoration in the foreground, a Mannerist soldier group surviving in the background, thirty years after the Niobe. The old manner is not abandoned; it is demoted to the background and kept as decoration. FLAG: there is a second Adoration of the Magi, c. 1624, in the Musee de Grenoble, said (farms only) to have been commissioned for the Jesuit church in Brussels; the Grenoble picture's dimensions (420 x 290 cm in the farms) are NOT VERIFIED and the two versions must not be conflated.

### Phase 4 - Late classical synthesis and the pastoral close (1625-1651)

**W18. The Lamentation of Christ - 1625.** Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen, Rotterdam, inv. 2062 (OK). Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (Commons list entry only). What it shows: the devotional lamentation as a compact, sculptural group - the year after the 1624 triad, the history picture contracts from the monumental (W15) to the intimate, with the Caravaggesque chiaroscuro now serving grief rather than display. Thinly documented in this leg; the Boijmans object page was not opened.

**W19. Shepherdess with Grapes (Shepherdess with Grape Bowl) - 1628.** Staatliche Kunsthalle Karlsruhe. Dimensions reported as 104 x 83 cm - NOT VERIFIED (wikioo only). What it shows: the pastoral as an independent subject - a shepherdess with a bowl of grapes at the apex of a pyramid of figures, attendants, horses, cattle and birds around her, in a landscape that is no longer a backdrop but the world of the picture. The grape bowl is abundance and fertility; the shepherdess is innocence and piety (the farms' reading, plausible but not museum-sourced - flagged as such). With the Shepherd and Shepherdess of 1627 (Hanover, see separate list) it forms a small pastoral group at the end of the 1620s, the moment the landscape-with-figures finally becomes simply the pastoral. FLAG: the title varies (Shepherdess with Grapes in the Wikipedia gallery; Shepherdess with Grape Bowl on wikioo); the Karlsruhe location is from wikioo only and should be confirmed against the Kunsthalle catalogue.

**W20. The Four Fathers of the Latin Church (venerating the Eucharist) - signed and dated 1632.** Oil on canvas. Museum Catharijneconvent, Utrecht, inv. RMCC s331. Dimensions NOT VERIFIED. Signed and dated lower right: "A. Bloemaert. fe. / 1632". Acquired by the Catharijneconvent at Sotheby's London, December 2010, with support from the Mondriaan Foundation, KfHein Foundation, Friends of the museum and the Rembrandt Society (CODART). Roethlisberger calls it "a work of major importance". CODART: "No other work by Bloemaert is as explicit a statement of Catholic doctrine as this one" - Augustine (standing, monumental, opening the composition at left) expounds transubstantiation, pointing at the Eucharist in the monstrance; the half-nude ascetic Jerome argues with Ambrose and Gregory; putti hover and heaven bursts open; the host blocks out a Last Supper behind it. The theme descends from Raphael's Disputa. Roethlisberger: the only known case in Bloemaert's oeuvre of an autograph painting repeating one of his own prints (same direction as the print). Probably an altarpiece for a clandestine Catholic church, though no commission is recorded. What it shows: the late Bloemaert as a Catholic theologian in paint - the classicising turn put to confessional work, monumental figures, explicit doctrine, and the print-to-painting loop that made his compositions travel. The Catharijneconvent also holds his Crucifixion altarpiece of 1629 and the tondo Angel Flying with a Banderole (1615-20).

**W21. Venus and Adonis - 1632.** Statens Museum for Kunst, Copenhagen, inv. KMSsp343. Medium and dimensions NOT VERIFIED (no SMK object page opened for this picture; the Commons list gives the inventory number and date only). Web Gallery of Art: "This painting is regarded as one of Bloemaert's masterpieces"; Bloemaert looked to Titian and Rubens, who had painted the motif before him. A red-chalk study sheet (Stephen Ongpin, ex-Giroux group) preserves the hand of Adonis holding a spear as drawn for this picture; the sheet is datable to the late 1620s-30s by the facial type and hairstyle of the two women's heads on it. What it shows: the erotic mythological subject of W1, forty years on, now at monumental scale and with the full Baroque apparatus - Venus restraining Adonis from the hunt, the greyhound, the spear-hand studied from life in red chalk. The Mannerist subject survives, but the bodies are heavier, the light is warmer, and the drawing is done from a model rather than a print. It is the right closing picture for the catalogue: the same gods as the Buscot tondi, painted by a 66-year-old who had outlived the manner he started in.

## Evolution

1. Early Mannerism, 1580s-1590s (W1-W6). Fontainebleau training (1581-83) plus the Haarlem circle (Goltzius, Cornelisz van Haarlem, Spranger via prints) produce a painter of elongated, over-muscled bodies, acidic colour, complicated poses seen from extreme angles, and compositions borrowed wholesale from Spranger through Goltzius engravings (W4). The market is courtly and international: the Niobe (W2) is aimed at Rudolf II's Prague; the early pictures are collected by Amsterdam bankers and Utrecht patricians. Scale is already ambitious (W2 is 2 x 2.5 m at age 24).
2. The landscape turn, c. 1595-1610 (W6, W8). Already in 1595-1600 the sacred subject is demoted to staffage inside a landscape (W6). Bloemaert draws cottages, dovecotes and trees from life and works them up in the studio into imaginary scenes. By 1610 (W8) the farm landscape is the picture. This is the first half of his originality: not the history, but the Dutch countryside as a moral stage.
3. The classicising turn, c. 1610-1615 (W7, W9 as print, W10). Around 1610 (National Gallery) the Sprangerian poses give way to more naturalistic attitudes and heavier, more sculptural figures (W10). The Four Evangelists (W10) shows what the turn is for: doctrinally neutral, monumentally Catholic altarpiece-scale devotion for Utrecht's hidden churches. But the old manner never leaves: the Leiden Collection's verdict on the 1624 Adoration (W17) - soldiers in the background who "could also pass as a separate work by Spranger" - applies to the whole late career. Bloemaert does not convert; he layers.
4. The Caravaggesque contact, 1619-1624 (W11-W17). The hinge of the whole study. Bloemaert never went to Rome and never met Caravaggio; he learned Caravaggism second-hand, from the pupils who came home - ter Brugghen back by 1614, Baburen and Honthorst in 1620-21. The pictures show him learning in public: the small Flute Player (W13, 1621) and the Supper at Emmaus (W14, 1622) adopt the single-figure musician and the candlelit table group that his pupils were painting; the Leiden Collection pairs them explicitly as the evidence. Then 1624 delivers three monumental pictures in one year (W15, W16, W17) in which the new chiaroscuro, the half-length figure group and the moralised landscape are fully absorbed - and CODART's remark on W15 is the verdict on the phase: at 57 he was "keeping up with the latest trends in painting with the gusto of a budding young artist", and the picture is "indebted to... Gerrit van Honthorst". The teacher becomes the pupil's pupil.
5. Late classical synthesis and pastoral close, 1625-1651 (W18-W21, plus the separate list). The 1630s pictures (W20, W21) are large, doctrinal or mythological, classically composed, with the Carracci classicism the National Gallery notes for his later years layered over the surviving Mannerist reflexes. The pastoral strand (W19) runs alongside: shepherds, grape bowls, farmyards - the landscape-with-a-lesson of W16 grown gentle. The very late work (separate list: The Devil Sowing Tares, 1650; Landscape with Peasants Resting, 1650, painted in his 84th year) shows no decline, only a loosening: the same wheat-field parable painted at 58 and again at 84, the same resting peasants, the lesson now carried entirely by the landscape. He dies in Utrecht in 1651, having taught the painters who taught Europe what Utrecht painting was.

**The one-sentence driver:** Bloemaert's career is a long absorption - he takes a style whole from prints (Spranger), then from a landscape he walks in, then from his own pupils (Caravaggio via Utrecht), and in each case he keeps the previous layer visible inside the new one, so that a 1624 altarpiece still has a Spranger soldier group standing in its background.

## Aesthetic logic

Bloemaert's aesthetic is the aesthetic of the palimpsest: nothing is ever thrown away, only demoted. The Sprangerian twist survives inside the classicising figure; the Mannerist soldier group survives in the background of a Baroque adoration; the Fontainebleau elegance survives inside the farmyard. The career reads as a series of absorptions, each one complete enough to be called a conversion and each one incomplete enough to leave the previous style visible. That is why the period labels fit him so badly - Mannerist, classicist, Caravaggesque, Carracci classicist - and why the Getty's phrase, "a distinctive, decorative synthesis of both approaches", is the closest any summary gets: the synthesis is the point, and the decoration is not a flaw in it but the method. Colour leads (M5), the line stays visible (M10), the composition is either a wall of bodies or a single sentence (M2 against M3), and the landscape is built from a clip-library of real sheds and dovecotes (M6, M8) with the story shrunk to a speck inside it (M7). The late work adds the one genuinely new thing: the reversal of the teaching relation (M15). A painter who never left Utrecht and never saw Italy ends his career painting like a man who had just come home from Rome, because his pupils had, and he was still learning at fifty-five. The cabbage drawings (M9) are the purest statement of the logic: a foreground motif, promoted until it is the whole picture, with no narrative alibi at all - the mechanism of demotion and promotion, run in reverse, on a vegetable.

## Positions

**P1.** Bloemaert is best understood not as a Mannerist who became a Baroque painter but as a synthesiser: at every stage he kept the previous style's residue (Sprangerian soldiers in 1624; Mannerist grace inside Caravaggesque lighting). The "phases" are a convenience; the work is a palimpsest.

**P2.** His real originality is in the landscape, not the history painting: the subordinate-narrative landscape (M7) and the dilapidation aesthetic (M8) are his inventions, while the history paintings are competent syntheses of Spranger, Goltzius and Caravaggio.

**P3.** The workshop-stock method (M6) makes Bloemaert an early modular artist: landscape as a kit of recombinable motifs anticipates collage and the modern asset library by three centuries.

**P4.** The Tekenboek's lack of text (M14) is a strength, not a gap: a purely visual curriculum trains the eye without the interference of theory, and its 200-year lifespan proves the method.

**P5.** Bloemaert's influence on Rembrandt, Vermeer and Hals (claimed by the Centraal Museum press release) is real but indirect - transmitted through his pupils and his prints, not through direct contact - and the claim is often overstated in exhibition publicity.

**P6.** The "Father of the Utrecht school" title is half true: he was the patriarch and the teacher, but the Utrecht school's style (Caravaggism) was invented by his pupils in Rome, and he adopted it from them (M15). He fathered the school's people, not its idiom.

**P7.** The Britannica judgement that he "excelled more as a colourist than as a draughtsman" (M5) is backwards for the drawings: his ~1,500 to 1,700 surviving drawings are the stronger claim to greatness, and his reputation as a draughtsman (Rubens's admiration, van Mander's praise) outweighs the colourist tag.

**P8.** His Catholicism was a career asset, not just a handicap: it gave him the clandestine-church altarpiece market and Jesuit/southern-Netherlandish patrons, while the 1618 guild resignation is usually over-read as marginalisation - he remained Utrecht's leading painter for another 30 years.

**P9.** The 2011 "Bloemaert Effect" framing - colour and composition as timeless, with parallels to Marlene Dumas and Cesar Domela - is a curatorial stretch: it tells us more about 2011 Centraal Museum audience strategy than about Bloemaert.

**P10.** The engraver-as-co-author evidence (M12) undermines the single-author model of his prints: Matham's Danae changes are creative decisions, and "after Bloemaert" should be read as "Bloemaert-and-Matham."

**P11.** Bloemaert's longevity is itself the mechanism: a 65-year career (c. 1585-1650) let one man embody the whole Mannerism-to-Baroque transition, which is why he reads as a hinge figure rather than a period artist.

**P12.** The vegetable-foreground drawings (M9) are his most modern works: a cabbage as the entire composition, at ground level, with no narrative alibi - a still life that refuses to be a still life - and they deserve to anchor any contemporary reading of him.

## Corrections

- C427. Baburen was NOT Bloemaert's pupil - corrected first-hand by the parent. Several secondary pages loosely call Baburen a Bloemaert pupil or say he "likely had contact with Bloemaert's circle"; leg 3 flagged it, and the parent verified first-hand: EN Wikipedia - "The earliest reference to the artist is in the 1611 records of the Utrecht Guild of St. Luke as a pupil of Paulus Moreelse"; Google Arts and Culture - "Baburen received his early artistic training from Paulus Moreelse in Utrecht"; Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza - "registered as an apprentice in the studio of Paulus Moreelse in 1611". The #530 study file's top-pick paragraph for #531 did not itself claim Baburen as Bloemaert's pupil - it said "taught ter Brugghen, Honthorst and Bijlert" - and leg 1's brief ("teacher of ter Brugghen, Honthorst, Baburen and Bijlert") carried the loose claim into this run; the study does not repeat it. Baburen joined the Utrecht school on his return in 1620/21 and painted alongside Bloemaert's pupils, but he was Moreelse's pupil. Do not cite Baburen as a Bloemaert pupil.
- C428. The birth year, corrected. 1566 (Leiden Collection, National Gallery, Getty ULAN, Wikipedia, Stadel) against 1564 (National Galleries of Scotland, the Morgan Library, some dealer pages, and the Wikimedia Commons/Wikidata authority file). The 1564 date is an older tradition, now superseded; the study uses 25 December 1566. Age at death follows: 84 on 1566, 86 on 1564. The National Gallery says "at the considerable age of eighty-six" (twice), which is arithmetically inconsistent with its own 1566 birth date and presumably inherits the old 1564 birth year; the Leiden Collection's 2019 text likewise says "died at the age of eighty-six", inconsistent with its own 1566 birth date. The study uses 84. Do not cite 86 without the flag.
- C429. The death date, left unresolved. 27 January 1651 (Wikipedia infobox, used in this study's Site line) against 13 January 1651 (Leiden Collection 2019 text: "died at the age of eighty-six on 13 January 1651, and was buried in the Catharijnekerk"). Both are recorded; neither is verified against a primary source in this leg. The Leiden figure carries the extra inconsistency of the eighty-six (see C428), which weakens it, but does not settle the date. Flag before use.
- C430. The Paris return date, left unresolved. 1583 (Wikipedia and the Leiden Collection, via van Mander) against 1585 (National Gallery; the Leiden Collection also gives c. 1585 in leg 3's reading). The 1583 date is better sourced; the study uses 1583 with the flag. The Paris departure is likewise a range: 1581 (Wikipedia) against "1581 or 1582" (Stadel).

The study uses July 1627. The brief's phrasing ("co-founder of the Utrecht guild/academy with Paulus Moreelse") collapses the two; the study keeps them apart.

- C436. The Juno, demoted by the parent.
