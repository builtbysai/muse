# Fred Forest: Study Notes

**Site:** Fred Forest -- born 6 July 1933 in Mascara, French Algeria; died 4 November 2026 at Anserville, aged 93. A French new-media artist who worked, in turn, with video, photography, the printed press, mail, radio, television, telephone, telematics and the internet, in installations, performances and public interventions about the workings and the potential of media space. Co-founder of the Sociological Art Collective (1974) and of the Aesthetics of Communication movement (1983). He never had secondary education, never attended art school or university; his only school qualification was the Certificat d'Etudes primaires, and he was entirely self-taught as an artist -- then spent fifteen years as a Post and Telecommunications employee before he ever touched a video camera, so the day job is the biography of the work. Guard: do not credit Forest with a satellite first or a "first live TV" for Space-Media -- no source read supports either. Guard: do not assert he directed MAMAC Nice; the claim is uncorroborated in every source read end to end. ## The life: Algeria, the post office, the Portapak

Fred Forest was born on 6 July 1933 in Mascara, French Algeria, into a modest Pied-Noir family. He had to work very early to earn a living and support his family; he never had secondary education, never attended art school or a university, and his only school qualification was the Certificat d'Etudes primaires. He was entirely self-taught as an artist. His official archive biography divides his life in two: a first life in Algeria until about the age of thirty, then -- after increasing attacks forced him to leave overnight what he still called his first country -- a second life in France, embraced as an exile. He returned from Algeria in 1962, aged about thirty, with only the Certificat d'Etudes primaires in his suitcase. (C17908, leg 1 C17700, C17701, C17702; synthesis C17851)

He began his working life as a basic Post and Telecommunications (PTT) agent, posted to the depths of Kabylia and to the Moroccan border at Nedroma, while painting as an amateur -- fifteen years in total, first in Algeria and then in France, first selling stamps at the counter, then as a controller of posts and telecommunications. In Paris he worked as a telephone operator at the PTT exchange on rue des Archives, supporting his family, then living in an HLM in the suburbs at Hay-les-Roses. The man who would spend fifty years hijacking telephone networks, postal systems and broadcast channels had spent fifteen years inside them, at the counter and at the switchboard. (C17909, leg 1 C17702; synthesis C17853)

In Paris in the early 1960s he worked as a satirical cartoonist and illustrator for the newspapers Combat and Les Echos, and experimented with projecting moving and still images onto tableaux-ecrans (screen-paintings). He took part in group exhibitions at the Maison de la Culture, Paris (1962) and at the Ligoa Duncan Gallery, New York (February 1965). In 1967 Sony France gave him one of its first Portapak video recorders as part of a promotional campaign, making him one of the very first artists anywhere to work with video. His first experimental video tapes, The Telephone Booth and The Wall of Arles, date from 1967; his first formal exhibition of video art, the interactive installation Interrogation 69, took place in May 1969 in Tours. From 1968 he was creating the first interactive environments in France combining computer and video. Influenced by May 1968, Situationist critiques of the society of the spectacle, Marshall McLuhan and Umberto Eco's "open work", he stopped producing traditional art objects in 1969 and turned to a utopian "social praxis" operating "under the cover of art". (C17910, leg 1 C17703, C17704; leg 2 C17751, C17753, C17754; synthesis C17853)

The rest of the arc runs fast because the mediums run fast: Space-Media 1972; Sao Paulo 1973 (prize and arrest); Sociological Art Collective 1974 -- its manifesto published in Le Monde on 10 October 1974, signed Fischer, Forest and Thenot; Artistic M2 1977 and Territory of the M2 1980, Anserville; Aesthetics of Communication 1983 with Mario Costa; Sorbonne state doctorate 1985 (the first in France defended with a video set-up); taught at Cergy, the Sorbonne and Nice; sued the Pompidou 1994-97; first website sold at auction 1996; Techno-Wedding 1999 (Sophie Lavaud, son Adrien); Second Life from 2008; INA archives 2004; Pompidou retrospectives 2017 and 2024; NFT-Archeology 2021, donated 2023. He died on Wednesday 4 November 2026, aged 93, at Anserville, and announced his own death in a prepared letter, Lettre annoncant mon deces par moi-meme, transmitted by his son Adrien to AFP and published on his social networks on Thursday 5 November 2026 -- "J'ai grace a la complicite affectueuse de mon fils Adrien le plaisir de vous annoncer ma propre disparition". The letter imagines him lying in the hole dug in the earth at Anserville, near the Territory of the M2, with all the time in the world to think about what he will do tomorrow. He was buried at Anserville; no cause of death was disclosed. The Centre Pompidou paid tribute on 5 November, saluting "a pioneer of video, of sociological art and of net art". The final work is a press release about a death, issued by the dead man through his son through the wire service: the parasitic blank, one last time, with himself as the content. (C17911, leg 1 C17705-C17719; synthesis C17852, C17853)


## The works: six phases, one protocol

The catalogue runs W01-W20, about thirty named works and actions, 1967-2014. No video was watched directly in any leg, so no grade A exists; the grades below are B where a good secondary description was read end to end (usually the artist's own archive or the Web Net Museum retrospective), C where only a title and date or a secondhand account exists. The evolution falls into six phases, and the phases are the argument: the medium changes every decade and the protocol does not. (C17912, leg 2 C17750-C17779; synthesis C17864)

**W01, Sociological Animation / Family Portrait, 1967** -- C. Title and date only, action no. 1 in the Web Net Museum retrospective index; the long biography calls a 1967 Portrait de famille at L'Hay-les-Roses an early participatory/sociological work, but no mechanism, duration or visual description was found. Do not use as a mechanism example. (C17913, leg 2 C17752)

**W02, The Telephone Booth and The Wall of Arles, 1967** -- B. His first video tapes, Sony Portapak half-inch black-and-white. The Telephone Booth was shot in real time from the third-floor window of his flat in L'Hay-les-Roses, fixed on the suburb's only public telephone booth and the residents who used it, visually linked to a nearby tree that dwarfs the booth "like a giant antenna". The Wall of Arles is a study of pedestrians' curiosity at a wooden construction barricade in Arles, pressing their eyes to the gaps; Forest removed the audio, so the noise of earth-moving machinery digging a huge hole disappears -- a rupture of meaning. The mechanism is fixed-camera observation of an everyday site; the work is the framing plus the removal (sound) or the duration (real time), not any staged event. (C17914, leg 2 C17753)

**W03, Interrogation 69, May 1969, Tours** -- B. Interactive video installation, described as the first-ever video installation created in France: 5 video cameras, closed-circuit television, a computer, and 15 fluorescent screens. Visitors' own movements and gestures were electronically transfigured in real time and returned to them on screen -- an "electronic mass" in a Gothic chapel. This is the first full system in his career: camera, processing, screen, body, camera. (C17915, leg 2 C17754)

**W04, Space-Media / 150 cm2 de papier journal, January 1972** -- B. A small blank square ("150 cm2 of Newspaper") appeared in Le Monde on 12 January 1972; readers were invited to fill it with commentary, creative writing or artwork of their own and mail it back to Forest for a subsequent public exhibition. Ten days later Forest appeared on the midday news on French national television (Channel 2) and asked viewers to observe a moment of silence "designed to help them make sense of their lives". Both experiments were later repeated in newspapers, radio and television in locations from Belgium to Brazil. The mechanism is a parasitic blank inserted into a one-way medium: the newspaper's distribution becomes the work's distribution, the readers' mail becomes its content, the exhibition its second showing. The blank evokes censored newspaper space (an article forbidden leaves a blank) and, in Forest's own account, a space "onto which to project one's own content in a world saturated with information". (C17916, leg 2 C17755)

**W05, Les Gestes (Gestures in Work and Social Life), 1972-74, with Vilem Flusser** -- B. A video series, Sony half-inch Portapak; the four documented parts are Les gestes du professeur (Fontevrault, with Flusser), Les gestes du photographe (Montpellier, Place de la Comedie, 20 min), Les gestes du coiffeur (Le Vigan), and Video Troisieme age (La Font-des-Horts, with Flusser; see also W08). Forest met Flusser in 1972 when Flusser brought the invitation to the 12th Sao Paulo Biennale. The Professeur protocol was agreed in advance: Forest films continuously for the 20-minute mechanical duration of one Sony tape without interruption; as sole spectator he may manifest his presence (speech, unwanted camera movements); if Flusser's flow dries up before the tape ends, both wait in silence to the mechanical end. Flusser lectures on gestures while gesturing; Forest's handheld camera follows his hands, and Flusser's gestures alter in answer to the camera's gestures -- a feedback loop. Flusser runs dry, grows visibly annoyed waiting ("Something's wrong!"), Forest hands him a mirror so he can try to capture the filmer, and uses Flusser's shadow on the wall as image. The tape was then screened immediately and debated as a "metadialogue". In the Photographe part, two street photographers on the Place de la Comedie stop passers-by (preferably women with children), take the photo and hand over a ticket for later collection, filmed for 30 minutes without the photographers ever "over-playing" for the camera -- repetitive gestures with the camera as work tool, one hitting it against his thigh in two sharp blows, acrobatic poses. In the Coiffeur part, a hairdresser in Le Vigan treats heads "like a sculpture", scissors clicking rhythmically; Forest is himself filmed in synchrony, reflected in the large mirror above the shampoo sinks; the social relation flips during the cut, servile "sir" at the start, then the hairdresser jerks the client's head into position -- Delilah cutting Samson's hair. The mechanism is the camera as epistemological tool that changes what it observes; the protocol (fixed duration, no cuts, filmer allowed to intrude) is the work's engine. (C17917, leg 2 C17756, C17757)

**W06, Le Capitole, December 1972, Institut de l'Environnement, Paris** -- B. Networked multimedia experiment: 25 people in 5 groups of 5, each group in a work cell with a landline telephone and a TV monitor connected in closed circuit to a control room occupied by Forest; each cell also has that day's newspapers and an instruction sheet. Each group must invent a fictional narrative about strangers sharing a compartment of "Le Capitole", the Paris-Toulouse express about to leave, anchoring the fiction to a credible news item found in the day's papers. Information arrives via closed-circuit TV and telephone correspondents; the groups work in parallel, Forest orchestrates from the control room. An early example of his lifelong interest in alternative media-network hybrids and long-distance agency. (C17918, leg 2 C17758)

**W07, Archeologie du present (Archaeology of the Present / Autopsie de la rue Guenegaud), April-May 1973, Galerie Germain, Paris** -- B. Video installation and animation, billed as the first video installation produced in Paris. Flusser, Pierre Restany, Rene Berger and anonymous passers-by act as "archaeologists of the present". Flusser and Restany each walk one pavement of the Rue Guenegaud end to end, tape recorder in hand, describing everything they see -- an urban sociological report spoken aloud. Meanwhile Forest collects objects from the street's bins between 5 and 7 a.m. (15-30 April 1973) and displays them in the gallery. The spoken survey plus the rubbish archive is an autopsy of one street. (C17919, leg 2 C17759)

**W08, Video Troisieme age (Video -- Third Age / Senior Citizen Video), 1973, Hyeres** -- C. A video action in a retirement home in Hyeres, listed in the retrospective index and in the 1972-74 video works list; no dedicated work page with mechanism was found. What is evidenced is Flusser's secondhand account (December 1975): Forest filmed daily life in the home, projected the tapes back to the residents (who saw themselves "as other people" and were fascinated), taught them elementary manipulations of the equipment, and invited them to make their own tapes in groups; the resulting "primitive movies" (residents as actors, singers, dancers, clowns) were screened at a kind of film festival with discussion, which Forest filmed again. Grade C because no primary work page was read and the account is Flusser's recollection inside a critical text, not a work description. (C17920, leg 2 C17760)

**W09, XII Sao Paulo Biennale: Le blanc envahit la ville (Hygiene of Art: The City Invaded by Blank Space), October 1973** -- B. A series of participatory actions and installations at the 12th Sao Paulo Biennial, under Brazil's military dictatorship; awarded the Biennial's Grand Prize in Communication. Several interlocked devices: (a) fifteen telephone lines on Forest's stand, each with an answering machine and loudspeaker broadcasting callers' contributions -- callers got two minutes of free, uncensored speech before a technical device cut them off; calls poured in from all over Brazil, the invitation repeated daily in major newspapers, radio and TV; (b) blank newspaper spaces inviting reader responses, and press releases inviting written messages that were posted on the Biennial walls as soon as they arrived by post; (c) urban actions in supermarkets, stadiums, squares, samba schools; (d) the closing action "White invades the city": fifteen people walking central Sao Paulo holding blank white placards at arm's length, inviting passers-by to talk among themselves -- at a time when police punished any group of more than three people on the pavement. Forest was detained by the political police and released only after the French Embassy intervened. The blank from W04 is now carried into the street under a dictatorship -- same device, higher stakes. (C17921, leg 2 C17761)

**W10, Le petit musee de la consommation (The Small Museum of Consumption) and Sociological Walk in Brooklyn, November 1973, Sao Paulo** -- B. Video installation, Galerie Malvina, Rua Augusta, Sao Paulo, during the Biennale. A Philips closed-circuit camera fixed to an electricity pole transmitted the whole length of the busy shopping street onto a wall screen of some 15-40 television sets inside the gallery; in the windows, display cases held objects Forest had collected from the street's shopkeepers (trinkets, medicine boxes, sports caps, grocers' tins, cookery books, curtain poles, bottles of Batida de coco), meticulously referenced. Companion piece, same Biennale: Sociological Walk in Brooklyn -- advertised in the national press to recruit participants for a guided promenade through the working-class Brooklyn district on Sao Paulo's outskirts, Forest commenting on the urban environment and interviewing merchants and residents about their social conditions. The mechanism: the street watched at a distance (camera to wall of monitors) while its commodities are catalogued as museum objects; the walk inverts it -- the artist walks the street and listens. (C17922, leg 2 C17762)

**W11, The Video Family (La famille video), May-June 1976, Cologne** -- B. Video installation in an empty rented apartment in a residential neighbourhood of Cologne. Six video tapes: five portraits, one per family member, plus a sixth of the whole family at a domestic meal in the kitchen; six video recorders and monitors, one per room, plus a living-room monitor carrying the ARD network programme live 6-8 p.m. The fictional family was announced through classified "for rent" ads in the Cologne daily press -- the apartment, currently occupied by the Video Family, could be visited; 1,000 invitations were mailed to the cultural public; visits were fixed for 25 June, 6-8 p.m. exactly, the same slot as would-be tenants. Visitors walk a real flat whose inhabitants are five monitors showing blurry images -- "the perfect 'cathodic' bliss of their blank minds" -- while a real TV plays ARD non-stop in the living room. The family is a fiction sustained by the rental market's own channels: ads, viewings, invitations. (C17923, leg 2 C17763)

**W12, The Artistic M2 (Le metre carre artistique), 1977** -- B. Legal-financial performance. Forest formed a certified real-estate company ("The Artistic Square Meter"), bought 20 m2 of undeveloped land near the Franco-Swiss border, and placed ads in the national and international press (including Le Monde, 10 March 1977) offering "artistic" square metres as an investment with excellent resale value, for auction 22 March 1977 at Espace Cardin, Paris. The ad prompted a police real-estate fraud investigation; the auctioneer was forbidden by the Chambre de Discipline des Commissaires-priseurs (20 March, on the Public Prosecutor's orders) from selling the land. At the last minute Forest substituted a square metre of common cloth that auction attendees had trampled crossing the threshold; this officially "non-artistic" m2 sold for 6,500 francs, and Pierre Restany publicly declared it a bona fide work of art. The work's material is speculation itself. (C17924, leg 2 C17764)

**W13, The Territory (Territoire du m2 artistique), 1980, Anserville; Territory of the Networks (Territoire des reseaux), 1996** -- B. The M2 idea extended into a durable territory. In 1978/1980 Forest founded an independent territory on his property at Anserville, near Paris -- the Territory of the Artistic Square Meter, an imaginary state of which he was president for life; the Centre Pompidou's 2017 retrospective took this territory as its organising theme, 40 years on. The territory is a symbolic space divided into square metres, its "standard metre": each square metre is a space of expression offered to individuals for speech, music, drawing or critique -- "a space to be filled in like the blank spaces in newspapers (Space-medias) that I did for the first time in Le Monde in 1972", now in square metres instead of square centimetres. In 1996 it was transposed into cyberspace as Territory of the Networks, a hypertext work. The mechanism: the blank (W04) to the plot (W12) to a jurisdiction. The unit stays the same; the scale and the legal fiction grow. (C17925, leg 2 C17765)

**W14, La Bourse de l'imaginaire, la Bourse du fait-divers (The Stock Exchange of the Imaginary), June 1982, Centre Pompidou, Paris** -- B. Five-week interactive installation and event, funded at the last minute by Henkel after the Centre refused money; Forest, named curator of his own exhibition, ran it from a borrowed office and telephone inside the Centre. The public is invited via press, radio and TV to invent faits divers (tabloid news items -- death, anxiety, pleasure, sexuality, transgression, the absurd) and send them by post or telephone. A 15-person team works around the clock collecting, displaying, filing, archiving and entering items into a computer database; some stories are sent to print media and published daily; others are distributed as leaflets outside Metro stations, department stores, public places and at the Stock Exchange itself. Equipment: telephone lines, answering machines, press-agency telex, fax, photocopiers, video control, telematics, radio, exhibition walls, daily-quotation noticeboards, an improvisation podium, a mail-processing area. A national telephone number took up to 8,000 calls a day for the highest-valued news item; Forest was the first artist to make public use of Minitel (experimental, Velizy) to make submitted items remotely consultable. The stock exchange in which the listed securities are invented news stories, their price the volume of public attention. The ritual of quotation is the work; the content is whatever the public invents. (C17926, leg 2 C17766)

**W15, The Communicating Space (L'espace communicant), 10 December 1983-5 February 1984, Electra, Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris** -- B. Multimedia installation and experiment; an early example of "instant messaging" art. Forest provides the infrastructure for an independent telecommunications network inspired by the "wildcat" networks that flourished in 1982 by exploiting cracks in the national network -- unassigned numbers, normally kept secret, that allowed free calls between strangers. The artist builds the network rather than the message; users discover each other through the loophole. A parallel phone system inside the museum -- communication as a place, not a channel. (C17927, leg 2 C17767)

**W16, The Press Conference of Babel (1983), Learn to Watch Television by Listening to Your Radio (19 October 1984), Electronic Blue: Homage to Yves Klein (March-April 1984)** -- B. Three hijackings, one mechanism. Babel (Galerie Creatis, Paris, 18-31 January 1983): with an independent FM station, Forest hijacks Europe 1's weekly political talk show "Club de la presse" -- a simultaneous broadcast in which journalists from the alternative magazine TEL and guests (politician Bernard Stasi, writer Philippe Sollers, advertising man Jacques Seguela) listen to and caustically dissect the Europe 1 programme live. Learn to Watch TV (FIAC, Paris, 19 October 1984): a confederation of 10 independent FM radio stations in the Paris area broadcasts 4 hours of nonstop programming replacing the normal audio of the television programming airing at the same time; viewers are told to turn down their TV sound and listen to the radio instead. Electronic Blue (Del Sanio Museum, Benevento, Italy, March-April 1984; electronic conversion of Klein blue by Eric Maillet): the artist orchestrates a regional television broadcast of an electronically synthesised form of Yves Klein's signature blue, with commentary and reminiscences by Pierre Restany speaking live via telephone conference call, in a gallery bathed in blue light. Take an existing broadcast, keep its image or its slot, and replace its sound, its commentary, or its colour -- the viewer sees the familiar channel with its authority hollowed out and re-inhabited. (C17928, leg 2 C17768)

**W17, Telephonic Rally (1986), The Telephonic Faucet (Le robinet telephonique) and The Global Faucets (1992)** -- B. The Telephonic Faucet: media event and telecommunications installation, Ars Lab, Mole Antonelliana, Turin, 18-26 April 1992. Through newspaper ads and fliers the public is invited to dial a special number that triggers the electronic valve of a faucet on site, spewing a small quantity of water into a bucket below -- until, 1,500 calls later, the bucket overflows and water spills onto the marble floors of the historic Antonelliana. Telephonic Rally (Artcom, Moltekerei Gallery, Cologne, 22 June 1986, with Radio-France/France Inter) and The Global Faucets (Cite des Sciences, Paris, July-October 1992; visitors dial a New York number, automatically diverted to Tokyo) extend the same device: a phone call in one place performs a small physical act somewhere else, routed around the earth. One call equals one unit of remote agency; accumulation crosses a threshold (overflow) that no single caller intended. (C17929, leg 2 C17769)

**W18, Zenaide et Charlotte a l'assaut des medias (Zenaide and Charlotte Take the Media by Storm), 1988/1989, Toulon** -- B. Multi-media campaign for the centenary of the Musee des Beaux-Arts de Toulon, built around David's 1822 Portrait of Zenaide and Charlotte Bonaparte. The regional daily Var-Matin (circulation 200,000) published a full-page spread of the painting with blank speech bubbles above each sister; readers were asked to fill them in and return them. Press plus Minitel (readers chat with the two heroines in the museum, and can meet them at the Agence des telecoms) plus radio (Radio Nostalgie) plus television (France 2) plus 200 4x3 urban billboards around Toulon near shopping centres, aimed at a working-class public unused to museums, plus a permanent telephone answering machine carrying the sisters' "sweet voices" and taking messages, plus live street appearances by the two heroines, strolling arm in arm followed by local radio reporting live. Returned speech-bubble sheets were displayed immediately in the museum around the painting. More than 3,000 responses arrived in the fortnight after launch. A 167-year-old oil portrait talking back -- in the newspaper, on billboards, on Minitel, on the street. The W04 blank returns as a speech bubble; the museum painting is released into the city's media. (C17930, leg 2 C17770)

**W19, The Electronic Bible and the Gulf War (1991), For a Utopian and Nervous Television / Bulgarian TV presidency campaign (1991), The Watchtowers of Peace (1993)** -- B. Electronic Bible: installation of LED message boards juxtaposing Bible verses and Gulf War news dispatches. Bulgarian TV: a public campaign for the presidency of Bulgarian National Television. Watchtowers of Peace (1993): peace messages broadcast into the former Yugoslavia via radio and loudspeakers mounted on towers near the border. A 1989 precursor, Telematic Rituals for Sleepless Nights (4-26 August 1989, Arctic Circle, former USSR), staged a phony scientific voyage Moscow to Leningrad to polar north aboard an icebreaker to "confirm" the hypothesis that the Arctic Circle is in fact a square metre. The broadcast apparatus turned outward as a political instrument -- scripture against war copy on the same board; a voice carried across a war border by towers; a fake expedition whose only discovery is the artist's own unit of measure. (C17931, leg 2 C17771)

**W20, Web era and late performances: From Casablanca to Locarno (1995), Network-Parcel (Parcelle-Reseau, 1996), The Techno-Wedding / The Centre of the World (1999), Meat: The Territory of the Body and the Networked Body (2002), Second Life works (2008-2010), MoMA Sociological Walk with Google Glass (2014)** -- B. From Casablanca to Locarno (1995): Forest's first work using the internet -- a multimedia public-participation redubbing of famous scenes from the Bogart-Bergman Casablanca; it won an award at the 1995 Festival of Electronic Arts of Locarno. Network-Parcel (Drouot auction house, Paris, 1996): the commodity is a password granting the buyer exclusive access to a website hosting an original digital work; the performance is the public auction -- the first of its kind carried live on the internet -- at which it is sold. Like the M2 (W12), an artistic commodity of conceptual nature takes on aesthetic value through its use as a prop in a performance about how art is invested with symbolic capital, traded, distributed and consumed. The Techno-Wedding (1999, with Sophie Lavaud): Forest and Lavaud's real-life wedding, webcast live alongside a virtual-reality variant of the ceremony -- a rite of passage as web work. The Centre of the World (1999): the public could make a physical or telepresent pilgrimage to a shrine-like installation containing a digital relic of the old territorially centred world. Meat (2002): the territory of the body and the networked body. Second Life (from 2008): The Experimental Research Center of the Territory (2008, Nice / Sao Paulo / New York / Beirut, adapted to each location), with debates at an avatar think tank, public access to a "mystical disintegrator of trash", and the philosophical musings of Forest's digital alter ego Ego Cyberstar; The Traders' Ball (2010) examined the 2008 financial crisis through an online performance in Second Life coupled with an installation at the Lab Gallery, New York; Ebb and Flow: The Internet Cave (2011, Albi) let visitors encounter their own digital shadows in a cave-like setting after Plato's allegory. Late performances: in 2012, at the Centre Pompidou's Video Vintage exhibition, Forest had himself bound head to toe in old Portapak videotape and invited the public to cut him free while he critiqued the institutional memory of early video art. On 9 May 2014, uninvited, he performed a "sociological walk" at MoMA, New York, wearing Google Glass -- which let him photograph despite the ban, the guards not yet knowing what the device enabled -- with live reporting on Facebook and Twitter and a public debrief the next day. The same operations as 1967-77 (blank to fill, remote act, invented institution, public as author) now running on the web, in a virtual world, and on a wearable camera. The medium changes; the protocol does not. (C17932, leg 2 C17772, C17773, C17774, C17775)


## The mechanisms

**M1 -- The parasitic blank.** A void inserted into a one-way medium; the medium's distribution becomes the work's distribution and the public's return becomes its content. W04 (Le Monde blank, readers mail it back), W09 (blank placards under dictatorship), W13 (square metre "to be filled in like the blank spaces in newspapers"), W18 (blank speech bubbles on a David portrait). Watchable behaviour: nothing where news should be, then the public's handwriting arriving by post. **Takeaway:** a blank is not an absence in Forest's hands; it is a socket the public plugs itself into, and the socket is installed inside the enemy's own distribution. (C17933, synthesis C17869)

**M2 -- The protocol as engine.** A fixed, agreed rule-set (duration, no cuts, filmer may intrude) generates the work; observation changes the observed. W05 (20-minute tape, Flusser runs dry and waits in silence; mirror handed to capture the filmer), W03 (visitor's gestures transfigured and returned in real time). Watchable behaviour: a loop tightening until the subject notices the camera and answers it. **Takeaway:** Forest does not direct the scene; he writes the rules and lets the machine run the scene to its mechanical end, even when the end is silence. (C17934, synthesis C17870)

**M3 -- One call, one remote act; accumulation crosses a threshold.** Distance plus counting: each caller performs a tiny physical act elsewhere, and the sum does what no single caller intended. W17 (1,500 calls overflow the Turin bucket), W09 (two minutes of uncensored speech per caller, cut off by a device), W14 (8,000 calls a day for the highest-valued invented news item). Watchable behaviour: a bucket slowly filling from invisible hands. **Takeaway:** the telephone is not a way to talk in Forest's work; it is a way to act at a distance, and the act only becomes visible when enough strangers have done it. (C17935, synthesis C17871)

**M4 -- The invented institution sustained by real apparatus.** A legal or financial fiction (company, state, family) is run through the real channels of that fiction -- ads, auctions, police files -- until the apparatus itself certifies it. W12 (real-estate company, police ban, cloth m2 sold for 6,500 francs), W13 (Territory with a president for life), W11 (family sustained by classified ads and viewings). Watchable behaviour: a property ad, a police file, an auction -- and a doormat transfigured into art. **Takeaway:** Forest never fakes the institution; he founds it, files it, advertises it, and lets the state's own paperwork make it real. (C17936, synthesis C17872)

**M5 -- Hijack the broadcast, keep the slot.** Take an existing channel, keep its image or its time slot, and replace its sound, its commentary or its colour. W16 (pirate FM dissecting Europe 1 live; ten FM stations replacing TV audio; Klein blue re-broadcast with Restany by phone), W15 (wildcat phone network inside the museum), W19 (scripture against war copy on the same LED board). Watchable behaviour: the familiar channel with its authority hollowed out and re-inhabited. **Takeaway:** Forest does not build a rival channel; he moves into the existing one and changes what comes out of it while the audience is still tuned in. (C17937, synthesis C17873)

**M6 -- The street as archive: survey plus rubbish.** Describe a street exhaustively aloud while collecting what it discards; the spoken survey plus the object archive is an autopsy. W07 (Flusser and Restany walk each pavement of Rue Guenegaud; bin objects 5-7 a.m.), W10 (Rua Augusta live on a wall of monitors, its goods catalogued beside it), W09 (urban actions in supermarkets, stadiums, samba schools). Watchable behaviour: a street described to exhaustion, its litter laid out as evidence. **Takeaway:** the street already contains its own portrait; Forest's job is to make the portrait speak and to keep what the street throws away. (C17938, synthesis C17874)

**M7 -- The artist builds the network, the users make the work.** The work is infrastructure, not message: a parallel phone system, a stock exchange of invented news, a fiction built from the day's papers. W15 (independent network on wildcat loopholes), W14 (15-person team collecting, filing, publishing the public's faits divers), W06 (five cells inventing fiction from the day's news under Forest's orchestration). Watchable behaviour: strangers discovering each other through a loophole the artist opened. **Takeaway:** Forest's signature move is to build the room and leave; the work is whatever strangers do in it once the door is open. (C17939, synthesis C17875)

**M8 -- Same protocol, new carrier.** The operations of 1967-77 (blank to fill, remote act, invented institution, public as author) recur unchanged on each new medium. W20 (redubbing on the internet 1995, password-as-commodity auctioned live 1996, webcast wedding 1999, Second Life think tank 2008, Google Glass walk through a museum that banned performance 2014), W13 (territory as hypertext 1996). Watchable behaviour: the same game played on a new machine -- the carrier changes, the rules do not. **Takeaway:** Forest's career is one proof, repeated for fifty years, that the artwork is not the medium but the protocol, and the protocol survives every upgrade. (C17940, synthesis C17876)


## The network: Flusser, Restany, Fischer, Costa

Forest met Flusser in 1972: Flusser came to Forest's home in Hay-les-Roses to bring him an invitation to take part in the 12th Sao Paulo Biennale, of which Flusser was one of the advisors; under Flusser's leadership the Biennale administration had decided to set up an international section on the theme of communication. Mutual sympathy followed, and Flusser shared with Forest the work he was then doing on gestures in the professions. The collaboration lasted twenty years and produced about twenty Flusser texts on Forest's actions. Flusser's own December 1975 Fontevrault text describes the hot afternoon in the garden in 1974 when Forest filmed him lecturing on gestures: the camera followed Flusser's hand and body gestures, Flusser's gestures altered in answer, and a dialogue settled "whose numerous levels were not entirely in conscience for Forest, nor for me" -- camera as epistemological tool, observation that changes the observed. The same text covers the Hyeres retirement-home video action and the Space-Media blank-press-space actions. Flusser's Bienal role, from the scholarly side: he was named technical advisor to the Biennial in early 1972, in a committee with Antonio Bento, Bethy Giudice and Mario Wilches, and helped develop the theme "Art and Communication" for the 1973 XII Bienal. Lofgren states that one of his suggestions to the XII Bienal "which was actually realized (along with Eric McLuhan [Canada]) was his invitation to Fred Forest", which culminated in the Sao Paulo interventions and earned the Communications Prize and got Forest temporarily detained by the military police. Lofgren adds that Flusser "would initiate a life-long collaboration with the artist" from that invitation. Two guards from the same source: Flusser's wider restructuring proposal was NOT realized -- funding was cut in January 1973 and he broke with Matarazzo Sobrinho -- and "it is unclear whether Flusser attended the XII Biennial de Sao Paulo". (C17941, leg 3 C17802, C17804, C17805)

Pierre Restany is the second node: critic, curator, lifelong friend, co-performer. Forest joined forces with Herve Fischer and Jean-Paul Thenot in 1974 to form the Collectif d'Art Sociologique; the members were invited to represent France at the 1976 Venice Biennale by Restany, "who became a lifelong friend and supporter of Forest and his work". Restany curated the French pavilion at the 1976 Venice Biennale (the 37th), bringing together the Sociological Art Collective with Raymond Hains, Alain Jacquet, Bertrand Lavier, Jean-Pierre Raynaud and Jean-Michel Sanejouand. Restany was not only a curator for Forest but a co-performer: in Archeologie du present the invitation names "Vilem Flusser and Pierre Restany, my two usual accomplices, who will be investigating the Rue Guenegaud with their bodies equipped with a tape recorder, each on one of the two pavements, describing everything they see, from one end of the street to the other, producing a kind of urban sociological report". Restany wrote on Forest across decades, including "From the sociological art to the aesthetic of the communication, a humanism of mass" (Paris, November 1994) and "The great naive of Internet" (Paris, May 2000) -- read via search snippets only, not end to end. Guard: Restany is a critic and network node, not a visual artist; study #649 already excluded him on exactly that ground. He is network evidence for Forest, not a study pick himself. (C17942, leg 3 C17809, C17810, C17811)

The Collective itself is the third node: in 1974 Forest joined forces with Herve Fischer and Jean-Paul Thenot to form the Collectif d'Art Sociologique (disbanded 1979 per the Forest article; a sociological-art history gives November 1980, when Forest and Thenot published "Mis au point" calling an end to the Collective -- the end date is unresolved, do not print one). The Collective's first manifesto was published in Le Monde on 10 October 1974, signed by Fischer, Forest and Thenot. The ICAA/CAYC annotation dates the creation of the collective to 1971, against the 1974 manifesto date in the other sources: the 1971 date most plausibly marks when the category was first proposed, the 1974 date when the Collective was formally declared. A UPenn dissertation (Erickson) confirms the shape: Fischer, Forest and Thenot founded the Collective in 1974, with critics Francois Pluchart, Pierre Restany and Bernard Teyssedre associated. (C17943, leg 3 C17808)

The fourth node is telematics and the Aesthetics of Communication. In 1983 Forest and Professor Mario Costa of the University of Salerno formed the International Research Group for the Aesthetics of Communication, joined by media theorist Derrick de Kerckhove and "a wide array of artists including many of the pioneers of telecommunications and telematic art", among them Robert Adrian X, Roy Ascott, Stephan Barron, Jean-Pierre Giavonelli, Eric Gidney, Natan Karczmar, Tom Klinkowstein, Mit Mitopoulos, Antoni Muntadas, David Rokeby, Christian Sevette, Norman White and Horacio Zabala. Forest wrote the group's manifesto, "For an Aesthetics of Communication" (1985). The network is documented in joint work, not just membership lists: in Espace communicant (Electra, 10 December 1983-5 February 1984) Forest organised a debate bringing together Pierre Restany, Mario Costa, Frank Popper, Derrick de Kerckhove and Leonard Henny, and on 14 December 1983 a telephone exchange linking Restany in Paris, de Kerckhove in Toronto, Henny in Utrecht and Costa in Naples. Frank Popper, who conceived the Electra exhibition, writes that Forest's means of communication "include the telephone, radio, television, telematics and cable", and that Forest was a postal and telecommunications inspector in Algeria from 1954 to 1970 before becoming a communications artist -- the day job is the network biography. Celebration du present (Artmedia, Theatre Verdi, Salerno, 4 May 1985) had Derrick de Kerckhove, Natan Karczmar, Jean-Marc Philippe and Roy Ascott taking part, with Mario Costa as curator: a 30-minute performance in which Forest phones a number that rings on the television screen itself, then rides a motorbike from the theatre to the Telecolore studios to answer his own call. De Kerckhove's essay in the same pages says he met Forest in the presence of Marshall McLuhan around 1970 and kept crossing him "at Sao Paulo, Berlin, Salerne or on his own 'Territory' in Anserville". (C17944, leg 3 C17812, C17813, C17814)


## Aesthetic logic: the postman as artist

Forest's aesthetic logic is that the medium is not the message and not the material either; the medium is a public utility with doors in it, and the artist's job is to find a door and leave it open. He learned the doors from the inside: fifteen years selling stamps, controlling posts and telecommunications, working the switchboard on rue des Archives. Every one of his signature moves is a postal or telecoms operation turned into art. The blank in Le Monde is a letter the reader writes and posts back. The two-minute uncensored call in Sao Paulo is a switchboard opened to strangers. The Artistic M2 is a property listing filed with the state. The Telephonic Faucet is a long-distance call that does a small physical job in another country. The Territory is a postal address that declares itself a state. The Techno-Wedding is a marriage certificate issued over the web. The self-announced death is a press release filed by the deceased.

That is why the work survives every change of carrier. A blank works in a newspaper, on a placard, on a speech bubble, on a square metre of land. A phone call works on a landline, on Minitel, on the web, in Second Life. The protocol is the artwork; the medium is only the current address of the protocol. Forest's own theory, as summarised in the standard account, is that the meta-communicational artwork does not convey a message or imagery but creates experimental micro-environments of communication in which normally hidden features of the media themselves may be discovered; the work is created by the users of the system, emerging from their interaction with the system and each other, and the artist's role is that of an "architect of information". His works fall into three broad families: koan-like media performances on altered time and space; whimsical exercises in telepresence and long-distance agency; and ambitious alternative interfaces soliciting large-scale public participation.

The logic has a hard edge that the prompts must keep. Forest is not a prankster; the prank is the delivery mechanism for a civic act. The blank placard in Sao Paulo is funny until you remember the police punished any group of more than three people on the pavement, and Forest was detained for it. The cloth square metre is funny until you remember the police fraud investigation that preceded it. The Google Glass walk is funny until you remember the museum banned the performance and the guards did not yet know what the device enabled. The work is funny the way a loophole is funny: it is a rule, followed exactly, to a place the rule-makers did not expect. **Takeaway:** build the socket, not the picture; the public will bring the picture, and the institution will bring the resistance that proves the socket was real. (C17945, leg 2 C17776; leg 3 C17813; synthesis C17864)
