## The life: the Bolivian boy who named concrete poetry

Eugen Gomringer was born on 20 January 1925 in Cachuela Esperanza, Bolivia, to Eugen Gomringer senior, a Swiss director of a rubber factory, and Delicia Rodriguez, described as an illiterate mestiza Bolivian. (C12700, C12701, leg 1) He was brought to Switzerland at two and a half and grew up with his grandparents in Herrliberg. (C12702, leg 1) He returned to Bolivia only four times in his life; among Bolivian friends his nickname was el Beniano genial. (C12746, leg 1) He studied economics and art history in Bern and Rome, 1944-1952. (C12703, leg 1) A 1944 exhibition by the Zurich Concrete poets inspired the experimental turn, and his first concrete poems, the "Konstellationen", date from 1950. (C12704, C12705, leg 1)

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the move from writing poems to building a system for poems: the constellation is invented, named, theorised, published, and then carried into design, industry, teaching and the museum, while the poems themselves stay almost absurdly small.

(1) Invention, 1950-1954. The founding acts come fast: the magazine Spirale, founded with Dieter Roth and Marcel Wyss in 1953; the first volume, konstellationen constellations constelaciones, in 1953; the coining of the term "Konkrete Poesie" in 1953; and the manifesto vom vers zur konstellation in 1954, its first version in the Neue Zurcher Zeitung of 1 August 1954, called by the Swiss Literary Archive one of the most influential theoretical writings on concrete poetry to date. (C12706, C12707, C12708, leg 1) The manifesto says: "Our languages are on the road to formal simplification"; the new poem "is simple and can be perceived visually as a whole as well as in its parts"; and the constellation "is the simplest possible kind of configuration in poetry which has for its basic unit the word, it encloses a group of words as if it were drawing stars together to form a cluster". (C12753, C12756, leg 2) The constellation "is an invitation" and "an arrangement, and at the same time a play-area of fixed dimensions". (C12756, leg 2)

(2) Ulm and the international turn, 1954-1960. From 1954 to 1957 Gomringer was secretary to Max Bill at the Hochschule fur Gestaltung Ulm, where he taught Sprache als Gestaltungsmittel (language as a means of design); he met Josef Albers and Max Bense there, and was in touch with the Brazilian Noigandres group and the Vienna Group. (C12709, C12710, leg 1) His own account of the debt runs the other way from the usual art history: "there was no concrete poetry without Concrete art". (C12818, leg 3) In 1955 Decio Pignatari met Gomringer at Ulm while Gomringer was Bill's secretary; Solt's account calls this meeting "the beginning of the international movement of concrete poetry". (C12821, C12825, leg 3) In 1959 he became head of communications at SIA Abrasives; for about thirty years he wrote advertising copy for the ABM department store, with E+U Hiestand. (C12718, leg 1) He founded the Eugen Gomringer Press in 1960 and edited the series konkrete poesie -- poesia concreta, 1960-1965 (MAMCO says 11 issues, 1960-1964). (C12711, leg 1)

(3) Publisher, promoter, cultural manager, 1960-1977. He headed the Swiss Werkbund 1961-1967 and led Rosenthal AG's cultural advisory board 1967-1985. (C12716, C12717, leg 1) He was a documenta council member 1966-1968 for documenta 4. (C12719, leg 1) He co-published Edwin Morgan's Starryveldt in 1965, Morgan's first full collection of concrete poetry, in his multilingual pamphlet series. (C12715, leg 1; C12826, leg 3) The books accumulate: 33 konstellationen (1960), with six constellations by Max Bill; das stundenbuch (Max Hueber, 1965); worte sind schatten (Rowohlt, 1969); the Reclam anthologies konkrete poesie (1972) and konstellationen, ideogramme, stundenbuch (1977). (C12712, C12748, leg 1)

(4) Professor and collector, 1977-2000. He was professor of aesthetic theory at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf 1977-1990 -- German Wikipedia says 1978-1990, an unresolved conflict -- with a guest professorship for poetics in Bamberg in 1986, and Intendant of the International Forum for Design in Ulm in 1988. (C12720, C12721, C12722, leg 1) He was a member of the Akademie der Kunste Berlin from 1971; the AdK obituary summary says ordinary member of the Literature Section from 1979. (C12723, leg 1) In 1981 he sold his private art collection to the City of Ingolstadt; it became the basis of the Museum fur Konkrete Kunst, opened in 1992. (C12724, leg 1) In 2000 he founded the IKKP (Institut fur Konstruktive Kunst und Konkrete Poesie) in Wurlitz/Rehau; it closed on 31 August 2023 and has since been under the Kunstverein Rehau. No standalone IKKP website was found accessible. (C12725, C12726, leg 1)

(5) Late life, sonnets, centenary, 2000-2025. He returned to the sonnet in youth and again in old age, publishing four volumes with more than a hundred sonnets from 2008 onwards; his autobiography welt im sonett (2020) is written in verse. (C12731, leg 1) His archive has been in the Swiss Literary Archive, Bern, since 2018 -- 552 archive boxes, 55 special-format boxes, about 45 shelf metres of library, 170 running metres in total. (C12727, leg 1) He wrote in German, Spanish, French and English. (C12728, leg 1) He turned 100 in January 2025; Rehau showed 100 works for the centenary. (C12742, leg 1) He died on 21 August 2025 in Bamberg, aged 100 -- not in Rehau, as the brief for this study said; Rehau was his long-term adopted home, 57 years in Upper Franconia, and he spent only his last year in a Bamberg senior home. He was buried on 1 September in the family grave in Rehau, per the Rehau town bulletin. No cause of death was found in any source read. (C12743, C12744, C12745, leg 1) He was married to the German scholar Nortrud Gomringer (nee Ottenhausen, died 2020), who worked as his editor and proofreader; the Historical Dictionary of Switzerland says his first marriage was in 1950 and his second, to Nortrud, in 1982. His daughter Nora Gomringer (born 26 January 1980), poet and writer, is their only daughter, sister of seven half-brothers. (C12740, C12741, leg 1)

Honours (confirmed): Rehau Culture Prize 1997; Bavarian Order of Merit 2008; Rilke Prize 2009; Alice Salomon Poetry Prize 2011; Condor de los Andes 2022; Pro meritis scientiae et litterarum 2022. (C12733-C12738, leg 1) Unverified (other-language Wikipedia/Wikidata only): Bavarian Culture Prize 2015, David Burliuk International Mark, 1986 Bamberg Poetik-Professur. (C12739, leg 1)

The Avenidas controversy is the one place the plainness broke into public argument. His most famous poem, "Avenidas", was displayed at the Alice Salomon Hochschule Berlin until it was taken down after people found it discriminatory to women; the facade was on display 2011-2018, criticised by the student committee in 2017, painted over with a text by Barbara Kohler, and moved to a building wall in Rehau in 2018. (C12730, leg 1) A poem of twenty words, built by permutation, was read -- by its viewers, not by its author -- as a statement about women, and the institution answered by painting over it. The constellation, designed as a play-area, turned out to be a public place too.

Evolution. Phase 1, 1950-54: the tiny lexicon -- four nouns in W02, two words in W03, one four-letter word in W04, one word repeated fourteen times in W05 -- meaning carried by position, repetition and blank space; Neue Haas Grotesk / Helvetica with "almost spiritual rigour" and a "reduced set" of typographical elements. (C12755, C12796, leg 2) Phase 2, 1960-69: 33 Konstellationen systematises the form (square pages, Bill drawings by rule) and Worte sind Schatten replaces the single-word field with a small combinatorial grammar; found commercial language enters as material (Roads 68); the form travels across languages, Rothenberg presenting multiple English versions of the same German constellation. (C12768, C12769, C12771, C12772, leg 2) Phase 3, 1971 and after: einsam gemeinsam moves the constellation across the gutter, using the book's physical structure as part of the poem; the variation-series form (13 baumwind squares, 3 variations on "kein fehler im system") becomes the dominant late device; late titles keep the tiny-lexicon method but apply it to near-identical word pairs and borrowed systems such as the I Ching. ## Mechanism M1: single-word field with a blank centre

In Silencio (W05) the title word is arranged fourteen times around a blank central space, emulating a sonnet's lines; the void both expresses the theme and inflects the letterforms around it; the ground (absence of text) becomes figure. (C12766, leg 2) The poem "is not a picture of anything; it is a structure". (C12766, leg 2) It was written the year after Cage's 4'33", though Gomringer notes no connection. (C12766, leg 2) The mechanism is the empty centre as the subject: one word repeated to fill a rectangle, the middle left empty, so the empty space -- not the printed words -- carries the meaning, and the eye keeps returning to the gap because the gap reads as the subject itself (silence).

**Takeaway:** the centre must be empty and the emptiness must be the subject. A piece built on this mechanism must repeat one word around a void and let the void do the meaning -- and must never fill the centre, label it, or explain it; the gap is the poem.

## Mechanism M2: multi-path letter field

In Wind (W04) the four letters w-i-n-d are arranged so "wind" is recoverable repeatedly: four diagonal "wind" sequences (the four winds); from any "w" there are always eight ways to complete "wind"; "in" sits at the centre of the word and of the surface; the letters are a fragment of a potentially infinite pattern. (C12764, leg 2) There is no single correct reading order and a still centre. The mechanism is the field as a maze with many exits: one word's letters arranged so the word is recoverable along many diagonal and winding paths, the eye hunting and re-finding the word from any starting letter, the four diagonal readings suggesting wind from all four cardinal points.

**Takeaway:** the word must be findable from everywhere and readable in no single order. A piece built on this mechanism must scatter one word's letters so the word can be completed along many paths, with a still centre -- and must never fix a reading order; the hunt is the reading.

## Mechanism M3: diagonal rally

In Ping Pong (W03) six replicas of the legisign "ping pong" are staggered on a diagonal axis in a 2-3-3-2 rhythm of overlapping lines, suggesting a rally; the "o" letters act as the ball; it reads identically in English and German. (C12761, C12762, C12788, leg 2) The mechanism is the eye as the ball: two alternating words staggered on a diagonal so reading becomes a bounce, back and forth, and the round letters are the thing being hit. Finlay's "Paper Pear" directly mimicked this zigzag design. (C12807, leg 3)

**Takeaway:** the reading must bounce. A piece built on this mechanism must stagger two alternating words on a diagonal so the eye rallies between them, with a round form as the ball -- and must never lay the words out in a straight line; the diagonal is the game.

## Mechanism M4: additive staircase

In Avenidas (W01) three nouns are permuted in pairs, then all three combined at once, and the single admirer is added only in the final line: a 20-word poem, a slow accumulation rather than a picture. (C12758, leg 2) The mechanism is growth by combination: the poem grows by recombining what it already has, never by new vocabulary, until the last line completes the set. The viewer watches a set assemble itself, pair by pair, and the final line is the only place anything new appears.

**Takeaway:** the set must assemble itself and complete only at the end. A piece built on this mechanism must permute a small set of elements in pairs, combine them all at once, and add one new element only in the final move -- and must never introduce new material early; the ending is the only surprise allowed.

## Mechanism M5: combinatorial grammar

In Worte sind Schatten (W10) twelve three-word lines are generated from 3 nouns (worte, schatten, spiele) and 2 verbs (sind, werden); it is not conjugation but the location of the words that decides the meaning. (C12769, leg 2) The same few words rearrange into statements and questions (by inversion); the poem is self-generating from a closed set. The mechanism is position as syntax: a fixed lexicon and a fixed field, and meaning made by where words sit, not by how they are inflected.

**Takeaway:** position must be the syntax. A piece built on this mechanism must generate all its variation from one small closed set of elements permuted in place -- and must never add a word to say something new; if it cannot be said by rearrangement, it cannot be said.

## Mechanism M6: letter-squares / variation series

In Baumwind (W17) 13 variations of "baumwind" are set, each in a square, letters forming corners and centre; the squares in turn form a larger square on a white surface, the interstices triggering associations. (C12778, leg 2) The word dissolves into its letters and reassembles differently in each square; the grid of variants is itself the poem. The variation series continues in "3 variationen zu 'kein fehler im system'". (C12785, leg 2) The mechanism is the series as the work: one compound broken into squares, varied by rule, and the squares arranged as a larger square, so the viewer reads both each variant and the whole grid at once.

**Takeaway:** the series must be the poem, not a set of drafts. A piece built on this mechanism must vary one element by rule across a grid of variants, each complete, the grid itself composed -- and must never present one variant alone; the meaning is in the differences between them.

## Mechanism M7: mirror spread

In Einsam gemeinsam (W16) six lexical units only (einsam, gemeinsam; das teil, das ganze; das benannte, das unbekannte) are combined and recombined; each page holds a single couplet with its syntactic inversion on the facing page -- quasi-mirror images across the gutter -- with prints by Gunther Uecker; the fine-press edition was 100 numbered/signed copies (Duisburg, Guido Hildebrandt, 1971). (C12777, leg 2) The mechanism is the fold as a mirror: a statement on one page answered on the facing page by its inversion, the reader's eye crossing the gutter as the poem crosses its own meaning -- alone/together, part/whole, named/unknown, each pair turning into its opposite across the fold.

**Takeaway:** the turn must invert the meaning. A piece built on this mechanism must put a statement and its syntactic inversion on facing sides of a fold, so crossing from one to the other flips the sense -- and must never put the inversion anywhere else; the gutter is the mirror.

## Mechanism M8: found-name list with a collapsing twist

In Roads 68 (W12): "TEXACO and ESSO / ESSO and BP / BP and SHELL / the common smell" -- brand-name pairs recombined, closing rhyme "smell"/"SHELL" collapses the brands into one odour. (C12771, leg 2) The mechanism is the list that snaps shut: paired names repeated in sequence, marching in pairs, then a closing line that rhymes and collapses them into one shared thing. The same device family is W20's colour-address list ("you blue / you red... you"), a series that never quite closes -- the list that refuses to snap shut is the same mechanism with the twist withheld. (C12781, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** the list must march in pairs and end in a snap. A piece built on this mechanism must repeat paired found names in sequence and close with a rhyme that collapses them into one thing -- and must never let the list just stop; either it snaps shut or it hangs open on purpose, and the choice is the point.

## The aesthetic logic: reduction as rigour, the page as a play-area

Gomringer's aesthetic is reduction enforced as rigour. Nearly all his publications keep Helvetica, lowercase, grid typography and left flush, with "almost spiritual rigour" and a "reduced set" of typographical elements. (C12755, leg 2) The manifesto's claim is that languages are "on the road to formal simplification", and the poem answers by being simple enough to be "perceived visually as a whole as well as in its parts". (C12753, leg 2) Nothing is added for decoration: the typeface is the same book after book, the words are few, the page is mostly empty, and the empty part works hardest. The constellation is "an arrangement, and at the same time a play-area of fixed dimensions" -- a bounded field with fixed rules inside which the reader is invited to play, and the invitation is the point: the poem does not tell the reader what to see, it gives the reader a field in which to find it. (C12756, leg 2) "The poem is not a picture of anything; it is a structure." (C12766, leg 2)

But the rigour is not cold. The materials are the plainest things in the world -- tree, child, dog, house; avenues, flowers, women; petrol brands; colours addressed to a "you" -- and the plainness is what lets the structure show. The word is the basic unit, position is the syntax, repetition is the rhythm, and blank space is an active element, not a background. The scale of the field grows across the career -- one word, one page, one spread, one book -- while the mechanism never changes in kind. (C12756, leg 2) And the Avenidas controversy is the logic's limit case: a play-area put on a public facade stopped being a game, because a list of nouns in public is also a statement about the world, and the viewers answered by painting over it. (C12730, leg 1) The through-line for a builder is this: Gomringer's work is small, exact, and open -- a fixed field, a few words, and a reader who must do the work -- and a piece that fills the field, decorates the words, or does the reading for the viewer has missed him. (C12859)

## The network: Ulm, Sao Paulo, Edinburgh -- the international movement

The strongest chain is Ulm to Sao Paulo to Edinburgh: Gomringer at Max Bill's side in Ulm, Pignatari walking into that office in 1955, Augusto de Campos naming Gomringer from Brazil as the first European poet in whom the Noigandres group located the affinities they were searching for, and Finlay in Edinburgh answering the constellations in print. (C12817, C12820, C12821, C12825, leg 3) Max Bill is the hinge: Gomringer was his secretary at the Hochschule fur Gestaltung Ulm from 1954; Bill was the school's first rector and a former Bauhaus student whose 1940s work and theory shaped Concrete art; Gomringer: "there was no concrete poetry without Concrete art". (C12817, C12818, leg 3) Augusto de Campos (2008) called Gomringer "the first European poet in whom we located the affinities we were searching for" and said he "represents for me, more than any other European poet, the fundamental concept of concrete poetry". Contact ran through Pignatari; no source shows Gomringer meeting Augusto himself. (C12820, C12822, leg 3) Decio Pignatari met Gomringer at Ulm in 1955 while Gomringer was Bill's secretary; Solt's account calls this meeting "the beginning of the international movement of concrete poetry"; the two planned an international anthology that was never published, and Gomringer agreed by letter (30 August, year given as 1955) that the endeavour should be called "concrete poetry". Pignatari is the only adjacent artist with a documented face-to-face meeting with Gomringer. His "Beba Coca Cola" (1957) deconstructs the Coca-Cola slogan through phonetic transposition. (C12821, C12825, leg 3) Dieter Roth co-founded Spirale with Gomringer and Marcel Wyss in 1953; the magazine ran nine issues 1953-1964 and provided the forum for Gomringer's first concrete poems. (C12823, C12824, leg 3) Edwin Morgan is the Edinburgh link: Gomringer published Morgan's first full collection of concrete poetry, Starryveldt, in 1965 in his multilingual pamphlet series; Morgan alerted Finlay to concrete poetry in May/June 1962 and passed on Augusto de Campos's address. In a 2021 interview the 96-year-old Gomringer named Finlay and Dom Sylvester Houedard as "interesting and important" British figures in the movement. (C12826, leg 3) Ian Hamilton Finlay is the European pole's answering voice: correspondence listed in the Lilly Library; "6 Small Pears for Eugen Gomringer" (1966), made "in homage to" Gomringer, "the two poets were friends and colleagues"; "Paper Pear" mimics Gomringer's "Ping Pong"; a "Homage to Gomringer" poem in the Lilly manuscripts, distinct from the c.1970 screenprint "Prinz Eugen -- Homage to Gomringer" (with Ron Costley); shared publication in Image (1964), Konkrete Poesie International (1965) and Six Concrete Poems (1967). No source states Finlay and Gomringer ever met in person. (C12804-C12816, leg 3) (C12860)
