# Amelie Beaury-Saurel: Study Notes

**Site:** Amelie Elise Anna Beaury-Saurel (1848-1924), French portrait painter and director of the Academie Julian -- born Amelie Beaury in Barcelona on 17 or 19 December 1848 (Sunday 17 December or Tuesday 19 December; the day is unresolved, see C0), the middle child of Camille Georges Beaury and Irma Catalina Saurel, French carpet and tapestry manufacturers in Barcelona since 1845; her father died in the late 1850s, the factory faltered, and her widowed mother moved with the three daughters to Paris around 1859. The mother trained the girls in porcelain painting as a trade that would let them earn a living; Amelie worked as a porcelain painter and copied the Old Masters in the Louvre from the age of ten, and at twelve, while copying Rubens' Christ on the Cross, praise from Joseph-Nicolas Robert-Fleury fixed her mother's determination to pay for proper training. She showed at the Salon for the first time in 1873 (Portrait de M. P. R, no. 73), enrolled at the Academie Julian in 1874 under Pauline Coeffier, Tony Robert-Fleury, William Bouguereau, Jules Lefebvre, Benjamin-Constant, Jean-Paul Laurens, Pierre Auguste Cot, Felix-Henri Giacomotti and Gustave Boulanger, and paid for her studies by doing the Academie's administrative and financial work -- managing the expenses of the women's studio and standing between instructors and students. She was a Salon regular for fifty years, 1873-1924, with 108 catalogued Salon exhibits and 69 individually named dated works in the sources read; she took a third medal at the Salon in 1885, a bronze medal at the Exposition Universelle in 1889, the Prix d'Honneur at the Blanc et Noir exhibition in 1891, and membership of the Societe des Artistes Francais in 1898. The 1890s are the manifesto decade: Academie (1890), Deux vaincues (1892), Portrait de Severine (1893), Dans le bleu (1894), Apres dejeuner (1899). She married the founder Rodolphe Julian on Wednesday 9 January 1895, aged 46, kept signing her work Beaury-Saurel, and was put in charge of the women's workshop; when Julian died on Saturday 2 February 1907 -- her mother died two months later, on Wednesday 10 April 1907 -- she assumed direction of the whole Academie, with help from her nephews Gilbert and Jacques Dupuis, and held it until her death. The late work is portraits of the establishment -- Jean-Paul Laurens (1919), Leonce Benedite (1923) -- with one great exception, the group portrait Nos Eclaireuses (1914), seven pioneer women around a table of books. She was appointed Chevalier de la Legion d'honneur in 1923. She died at home, 93 avenue Niel, Paris, on Friday 30 May 1924, aged 75; her funeral was on Tuesday 3 June 1924 at the Church of Saint Francois de Sales, and she is buried at Pere Lachaise. After her death she fell into oblivion, as fr.wikipedia puts it, "effacee par les avant-gardes, le modernisme et la main-mise masculine sur le monde de l'art"; the rediscovery runs through the Academie Julian scholarship and the women-artists survey shows, above all Women Artists in Paris, 1850-1900 (Denver, Speed, Clark, 2017-18) and Magdalena Illan Martin's papers (2021, 2023). Her works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay, Paris (Jean-Paul Laurens, Leonce Benedite), the Musee des Augustins, Toulouse (Academie, Dans le bleu, Apres dejeuner), the Musee Carnavalet, Paris (Severine, Mme S. Carnot), the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Mulhouse (Deux vaincues), the Musee de Louviers (Leon Say), the Musee des Beaux-Arts et d'Archeologie de Besancon (La reine Juana), the Musee des Beaux-Arts Jules Cheret, Nice (Souvenir des Toros), the Musee municipal de La Roche-sur-Yon (Nos filles, Portrait de Femme), the Musee d'Amiens (Le Repos du modele), and the Biblioteca Nacional de Espana (Autoportrait, 1894). ## M1. The Nude as the Curriculum (Academie, 1890)
The life-model's nude body is presented not as myth or allegory but as the literal content of the training women were barred from. The nude is the curriculum itself, and the title names the institution that kept women out of the life class. In Academie (1890, pastel on canvas, Musee des Augustins, Toulouse) a standing nude woman grips upright bamboo stalks and stares out at the viewer; the bamboo gives her something to grip and a vertical rhythm, and her stare turns the viewer into the examiner. She holds her pose and holds the gaze, refusing the reclining or averted nude. DailyArt calls it a "marvelous drawing" playing with texture and light. **Takeaway:** paint the forbidden lesson itself; the nude is not the subject, the exclusion is.

## M2. The Chained Pair: Slump Versus Upright (Deux vaincues, 1892)
Two figures in identical constraint, differentiated only by posture, so the painting's argument is a binary state. In Deux vaincues (1892, oil on canvas 215 x 113 cm, Musee des Beaux-Arts, Mulhouse, Salon 1892 no. 97) two nude women are chained to a wall; one has her back turned and slumps forward in defeat, the other stands upright and stares defiantly into the distance. The chain is literal; the difference between the two figures is purely postural -- slump versus upright -- which makes the whole argument turn on body mechanics. Illan Martin and both secondary pages read it as a rallying call to women painters: do not let yourselves be defeated by the unwelcoming world of art education and professional art. **Takeaway:** put two bodies in the same chain and let posture do the arguing; the viewer reads the difference as a choice, not a fate.

## M3. The Red Accent as a Political Switch (Severine, 1893; Apres dejeuner, 1899)
A single saturated red note placed on the sitter's clothing names her politics without a word of text. In Portrait de Severine (1893, oil on canvas 122.5 x 88 cm, Musee Carnavalet, Salon 1893 no. 101) Caroline Remy de Guebhard, dite Severine, anarchist, socialist and feminist journalist, stares directly at the viewer, unsmiling, in a delicate white dress, with a vivid red flower at her sash; against the pale dress the red reads instantly as a leftist signal. Renoir painted the same sitter around 1885, gently and almost matronly; Beaury-Saurel paints her as unmovable, and the contrast with Renoir is itself part of the work's meaning. The same device returns six years later in Apres dejeuner (1899, pastel, white chalk and charcoal on paper, Musee des Augustins), where a flower at the sash sparkles bright against a monochrome, serious composition -- now in a domestic scene, after lunch, with a hand decisively on the hip. **Takeaway:** muffle the field, then let one small hot red point say what the picture cannot say in words; the eye keeps returning to it.

## M4. Smoke as Thought Made Visible (Dans le bleu, 1894)
Cigarette smoke is drawn as a rising, unfurling column that carries the sitter's attention upward and outward, making an interior state visible. In Dans le bleu (1894, pastel on canvas 75 x 82 cm, Musee des Augustins, Toulouse, inv. RO494, Salon 1894 no. 1926) a young woman sits smoking a cigarette, chin in hand, in reverie, watching blue smoke unfurl from her lips and rise; the blue of the smoke matches the blue background, so the thought and the room are the same colour. It is unclear whether she is at home or in public, in a kitchen or a cafe -- and the ambiguity is the point: a woman smoking was not seen in public, so the painting smuggles a public act into a private-looking moment. Possibly a self-portrait, the model resembling the artist, but unconfirmed in every source (see C10). **Takeaway:** give the still figure one moving thing -- a thin column that rises, curls, thins and dissolves -- and the stillness becomes thinking.

## M5. The Unposed Private Moment (Dans le bleu; Apres dejeuner)
The unguarded, everyday instant -- smoking after lunch, daydreaming at a table -- is chosen instead of the formal portrait pose, and treated as worthy of a finished work. In Dans le bleu and Apres dejeuner the sitter is not performing for the viewer; she looks away, hand on hip or chin in hand, mid-habit, absorbed in her own activity. The viewer catches her rather than being presented to her. Illan Martin reads this as the femme moderne: "emancipated, professional, intellectual, well-travelled". **Takeaway:** paint the moment between poses; the unguarded instant is the portrait.

## M6. The Group Portrait as a Roster (Nos Eclaireuses, 1914)
A compact group of named professionals is arranged around a shared table, each identified by the tool of her trade, so the painting functions as a directory of a movement. In Nos Eclaireuses (1914, Salon 1914 no. 134), known from a postcard reproduction held at the Bibliotheque Marguerite Durand, seven pioneer women lean together in solidarity around a table with a pile of books: on the left, Belgian cycling champion Helene Dutrieu holding her handlebars in a gloved hand; next, holding a paintbrush, the publisher Anna-Catherine Strebinger, Mme Henri Rochefort, herself a Julian student; the collector Marguerite Roussel looking at the viewer; in the centre, in professional attire and pointing to an article in a code, the lawyer Suzanne Grinberg, member of the French Union for Women's Suffrage (founded 1909); leaning on her, in the outfit she adopted to travel in the Middle East, archaeologist and explorer Jane Dieulafoy; then novelist and journalist Lucie Delarue-Mardrus; and aviator Elise Deroche, the first woman to obtain a pilot's licence. Each woman is identified by the tool of her trade; the table of books anchors the centre. The identification of which figure is Beaury-Saurel herself is unstable between sources and is not asserted (see C9). **Takeaway:** paint the movement as a roster; seven professionals leaning inward around one table is a manifesto you can read like a list.

## M7. Pastel as the Professional's Medium
Pastel on canvas or paper is used not as a sketch medium but as the finished, exhibited, prize-winning vehicle -- fast, direct, portable, and associated with women portraitists through her first teacher Pauline Coeffier. Dans le bleu is pastel on canvas at exhibition scale (75 x 82 cm); Apres dejeuner is pastel, white chalk and charcoal on paper; the Barres portrait is pastel with two preparatory drawings; Portrait of a girl (1895) is pastel; Le travail de M. Frey won the Prix d'Honneur at the Blanc et Noir exhibition of 1891. The soft, powdery modelling with visible stroke direction, colour laid in broad fields rather than glazed layers, is the handling: the blue ground of Dans le bleu is a single dominant wash. The critical reception is the tell: she was praised for the "virility" of her technique -- the medium coded feminine, the handling praised as masculine. **Takeaway:** take the medium they call slight and exhibit it at full scale; the softness is the speed, not the weakness.

## M8. The Hand as the Portraitist's Proof (Severine; Barres)
Meticulous, observed hands are the signature of a serious portraitist, and the working method starts from a photograph and enlarges it into colour. In Severine the hands receive "exceptional attention" (DailyArt, musings-on-art); the attitude of the subject, not the finery, is the portrait. In the Portrait of Maurice Barres (pastel, 31.3 x 23.4 cm, sold at Ader, Paris, 28 March 2025 for 2,080 euros, with two preparatory drawings in black pencil and in red chalk and black pencil on tracing paper) the preparatory sheet echoes the framing of Barres' carte-de-visite photograph (Bibliotheque nationale de France, dated 1880-1910): face in profile, back in three-quarter view; the finished pastel enlarges the framing, extends the body, and brings in bright colour -- a choice that contrasts with the portraits of Barres by Jacques-Emile Blanche and Henri Rondel. The photograph claim is single-source (Ader); flag it. **Takeaway:** render the hands with the same care as the face, and let a small photographic armature grow outward into colour.

## M9. The Signature That Refuses the Husband's Name
The hyphenated birth signature "Beaury-Saurel" -- paternal plus maternal surnames -- is kept after marriage, instead of taking the husband's name. She signs her works Beaury-Saurel throughout her career; she presents herself as Madame Julian only on very rare occasions and never signs a painting as such -- which is why her oeuvre is identifiable without the usual married-name confusion. The signature block stays constant across five decades while her institutional role changes completely: paying student, administrator, teacher, wife of the founder, director. The motive is documented: the hyphen honours her mother's family, who traced their lineage to the Byzantine emperors of the 11th century -- repeated across sources, but the lineage claim itself is family lore, not verified genealogy (see C16). **Takeaway:** a signature is a position; keep it constant and it becomes the one thing the institution cannot rename.

## M10. The Administrator's Eye: The School as the Work
Running the women's atelier as a system -- fees, expenses, instructors, students, mediation -- is treated as continuous with the painting, not separate from it. She pays for her own tuition with bookkeeping; she manages the women's studio expenses; she stands between instructors and students; she is put in charge of the women's workshop in 1895; she directs the whole Academie 1907-1924 with her nephews' help; she uses her Societe des Artistes Francais seat to move other women (Francine Richard-Hennecart, Laure Boucher, Renee de Royer, Marthe Debes, Marguerite Babin) into the Societe's subcommittees. The studio is a machine with inputs (fees, students) and outputs (exhibited professionals); the painter is visible in the doorway of the institution rather than only at the easel. She was, per the National Museum Warsaw journal, the first woman on the Julian teaching staff, in 1895, alongside Edouard Toudouze. **Takeaway:** the school is the work; run the room and the pictures follow.

## Evolution
1848-c.1859, Barcelona childhood and family collapse. Born in Barcelona, 17 or 19 December 1848, middle child of Camille Georges Beaury and Irma Catalina Saurel, French carpet and tapestry manufacturers in Barcelona since 1845, with more than twenty looms. Older sister Irmeta (1846-1933), also a painter; younger sister Dolores (1858-1944). Her father dies in the late 1850s; the factory falters; the widowed mother moves with her three daughters to Paris around 1859, Amelie recalling living in Paris at age ten, the family in financial difficulty.

c.1859-1873, porcelain painting and the Louvre copyist. The mother trains her daughters in porcelain painting as a trade that will let them earn a living and be independent. Amelie works as a porcelain painter and later calls it commercial painting that damped her creativity. At ten her mother takes her to the Louvre to copy the masters; at twelve, while copying Rubens' Christ on the Cross, painter Joseph-Nicolas Robert-Fleury praises her work, which fixes her mother's determination to pay for proper training. She hides her progress in class, Illan Martin reports, so her teachers will not propose more ambitious studies that would cost her porcelain wages.

1873-1889, Salon debut and the Julian student. First Salon participation in 1873 with Portrait de M. P. R (no. 73); also in 1873, Christ en croix (after Rubens) for the church of Saint-Etienne, Issy-les-Moulineaux. She enrols at the Academie Julian in 1874, first under Pauline Coeffier, then under Robert-Fleury, Bouguereau, Lefebvre, Benjamin-Constant, Laurens, Cot, Giacomotti and Boulanger; Marie Bashkirtseff, also a Julian pupil, writes distrustfully in her journal about "l'espagnole", the Spanish woman; Amelie is friends with Louise Breslau. Salon presence resumes in 1879 and runs, with gaps, to her death; Portrait de Leon Say in 1880; third medal at the Salon in 1885 for Portrait de Mlle S...; illustrations for La Revue illustree in 1886; a self-portrait drawing at the Salon of 1887 that "received superlative praise"; member of the Union des femmes peintres et sculpteurs in 1888; bronze medal at the Exposition Universelle in 1889 and a first medal at Barcelona the same year (single source); Portrait de Mme S. Carnot in 1889. The work is portraiture in the academic mode, learned from inside the system and paid for with administrative labour, and praised for "virility" of technique -- for painting like a man.

1890-1899, professional establishment and the femme moderne. Academie (1890), the standing nude as a claim on the life class; the Prix d'Honneur at the Blanc et Noir exhibition in 1891 for Le travail de M. Frey, Maitre d'armes; an allegory of work incarnated by a woman artist, used by La Joie de la maison in 1893 to illustrate an article in favour of women's entry to the Ecole des Beaux-Arts; Deux vaincues (1892); Portrait de Severine (1893); Dans le bleu (1894); marriage to Rodolphe Julian on Wednesday 9 January 1895 and charge of the women's atelier and the women's workshop at Passage des Panoramas no. 27; Echec et mat (1896); La reine Juana (1897, Besancon); Apres dejeuner (1899); admission to the Societe des Artistes Francais in 1898. The portraiture does not change its craft; it changes its subject and its argument. The sitter is now a woman who refuses the pose: nude and defiant, chained and unbowed, smoking, staring, hand on hip. The medium shifts toward pastel as a primary vehicle. Her image is used on the label of La Feria perfume from the Letheric house, advertising aimed at independent and creative women (single source, but the market catching up with the argument).

1900-1914, directing the Academie. Eve (1901); Hamlet (1902); Dans l'train (1903); Nos filles (1904, La Roche-sur-Yon); Au declin (1905), a portrait of the aged William Bouguereau; Aux varietes (1906). Exhibits in London in 1902, and, single-source, in Brussels, Liverpool and Tananarive. On Saturday 2 February 1907 Rodolphe Julian dies, aged 67; on Wednesday 10 April 1907 her mother dies; Amelie assumes direction of the Academie Julian with her nephews' help, and buys and lays out the Chateau Julian at Lapalud, her husband's birth village, in his memory. Salon entries continue (Andre Corthis in 1908; Vingtieme siecle in 1909; Mme G. C... in 1913, reproduced in L'Illustration of 3 May 1913). In 1914 she exhibits Nos Eclaireuses at the Salon -- no longer one defiant woman, but seven professionals around a table of books.

1914-1924, war and late portraits. During the First World War she remains in Paris at the helm of the Academie Julian and takes part in solidarity exhibitions for the Societe des Artistes Francais, the Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts and the Fraternite des Artistes; there are no Salon entries for 1915-1919, and a war-benefit exhibition at the Petit Palais in May-June 1918 shows her portraits of Reverend Pere Sertillanges and of J. P. The Salon return in 1920 brings five works, including Cardinal Amette, Pere Sertillanges, Georges Clemenceau and Une Eve; Marshal Foch in 1921 (cover of Le Noel, 10 November 1921); Gilbert Dupuis in 1922; Leonce Benedite in 1923. Appointed Chevalier de la Legion d'honneur in 1923. Her last Salon entries, shown in 1924, include portraits of Marcel Baschet, Mme Andre Corthis and Edouard Risler. She dies on Friday 30 May 1924.

Afterlife: oblivion, then a partial rehabilitation. fr.wikipedia states it directly: after her death she "tombe dans l'oubli, comme beaucoup d'artistes de sa generation, effacee par les avant-gardes, le modernisme et la main-mise masculine sur le monde de l'art", and is "peu-a-peu redecouverte au travers des travaux de recherche et expositions consacres a l'Academie Julian". No monographic exhibition or catalogue raisonne was found in any source read; the rediscovery runs through group shows and scholarship, not a solo retrospective. 2017-18: Women Artists in Paris, 1850-1900, guest curator Laurence Madeline (Denver 22 October 2017 - 14 January 2018; Speed 17 February - 13 May 2018; Clark 9 June - 3 September 2018), with Dans le bleu shown. 2021: Illan Martin's Arenal paper. 2022: Parisiennes Citoyennes! at the Musee Carnavalet mentions her (pp. 115, 137). 2023: Illan Martin and Custodio Velasco Mesa's chapter "maitresse et 'ferule bienveillante' a l'academie Julian" in Eleves and maitresses (Presses universitaires de Bordeaux); and La Roche-sur-Yon, Espace Malraux, exhibition "Nos Eclaireuses" -- the only retrospective-type show found, a single-work, single-theme show, not a full retrospective. 2025: the Ader sale of the Barres pastel, 28 March 2025, 2,080 euros; DailyArt Magazine article, 10 September 2025. Nos Eclaireuses (1914) is the work that now carries her name, recast as a feminist landmark.

## Aesthetic Logic
Beaury-Saurel's logic is the logic of the institution entered from below and taken over from inside. She arrives as a paying student who does the books, learns the academic system from its teachers, and then turns the system's own instruments -- the life class, the portrait commission, the Salon, the school itself -- against the exclusion that built them. The system has five rules, all sourced. First, pay for training with labour, not money: she proposes the bargain to Julian, does the admin, manages the women's studio expenses, stands between instructors and students, and ends by directing the school for seventeen years. Second, paint the modern woman as she is: through her work she "proposed change in the social perception of women by confronting the traditional and sexist stereotypes in force during the period of the Belle Epoque" and "reflected the attitude and personality of the femme moderne" -- emancipated, professional, intellectual, well-travelled. Third, the nude is a claim, not an offering: in Academie the model stands and stares, and in Deux vaincues the chain is literal and the posture is the argument. Fourth, the portrait is a political act: a red flower at a sash, a cigarette in a woman's hand, seven named pioneers around a table of books -- the iconography insists, in Weisberg's phrase, on "making political statements about equality of the sexes". Fifth, keep the name and the medium your own: she signs Beaury-Saurel for fifty years, and she makes pastel -- the medium coded feminine -- the vehicle of her most important recognitions.

The opposite ending to Breslau is the point of the doorway: Breslau painted the room and lived in it for forty years with one model; Beaury-Saurel painted the school and ended by running it. Breslau's mechanism is intimacy; Beaury-Saurel's is administration -- the same Julian women's atelier, seen from the desk instead of the easel. What she never does: she never paints history, never paints the crowd, never paints a sitter she does not know or cannot place in a system. Her whole oeuvre is a network of people she taught, married, directed, or sat across a table from, and the network is the work.

The through-line for prompts: a system of refusal, not a style. A nude who grips her bamboo and stares back; two chained women, one slumped and one upright; one red flower in a pale field; blue smoke rising while the figure stays still; seven professionals leaning inward around a table of books; a pastel ground laid in broad fields; hands rendered with the care of a face; a signature that never changes while everything else does; a school run from the women's atelier outward. All of it is watchable because it is all about who is allowed in the room -- and who ends up running it.

## POSITIONS
- P1. The studio as refuge and the whole of pleasure. Her own words, from an interview of 1906 as reported by Illan Martin: "My greatest pleasure is isolating myself between the four walls of my studio and work." She centred her personal life "fundamentally... on the development of her professional career, to which she devoted herself with full exclusivity". Source: musings-on-art.
- P2. Porcelain painting is commercial painting that damps creativity. She began as a porcelain painter; she "would qualify [it] as commercial painting which, according to her statements, did not satisfy her creative restlessness" and "later said she considered what she was doing as commercial painting which in many ways damped down her creativity". Source: musings-on-art; mydailyartdisplay. [SINGLE SOURCE for the wording: the 1906 interview as reported by Illan Martin.]
- P3. Hide your progress if showing it costs you your living. As a girl she "chose not to show her progress in class, in order to prevent her teachers from proposing that she train in more ambitious studies", because the family needed her porcelain wages. Source: musings-on-art. [SINGLE SOURCE]
- P4. Pay for training with labour, not money. She proposed to Rodolphe Julian that in return for doing the Academie's administrative and financial duties he let her attend classes free; he agreed; she "paid for her studies by carrying out administrative duties and managing the finances" and later "managed the expenses for the women's studio and served as an intermediary between instructors and students". Source: mydailyartdisplay; Wikipedia; Clark. [SINGLE SOURCE for the bargain itself: mydailyartdisplay; the admin role is multi-source.]
- P5. Keep your own name. She added "Saurel" to "Beaury" for her mother's family, kept signing "Beaury-Saurel" after marrying Julian in 1895, "continued to sign her works with her paternal and maternal surnames. Only on very rare occasions did she present herself as Madame Julian and never signed her paintings as such". Source: musings-on-art.
- P6. Marry late or not at all; the career comes first. She married at 46 (the sources say 47; see C2), "when the average age of women marrying was around 23 years old", which the Illan Martin paper reads as "revealing of her independent personality and deep dedication to her career". Source: musings-on-art. [SINGLE SOURCE]
- P7. Paint the modern woman as she is, not as convention wants her. Through her work she "proposed change in the social perception of women by confronting the traditional and sexist stereotypes in force during the period of the Belle Epoque" and "reflected the attitude and personality of the femme moderne". Source: musings-on-art.
- P8. The nude as a claim, not an offering. In Academie (1890) the model "stands grasping stalks of bamboo and stares out at us, challenging us"; "it can be no coincidence that Amelie has depicted her model naked and this nude pose empathises the strong and defiant attitude women embraced as artists". The title itself names the academy that barred women. Source: mydailyartdisplay; DailyArt.
- P9. Defeat is a choice the picture refuses. Deux vaincues (1892) shows "two nude women chained to the wall... one has her back bent in a defeated pose, while the other stands upright, unapologetically, and throws an upsetting gaze in the distance" -- read by both secondary pages as "a rallying call to all female painters to be fearless" and "a plea to female artists to not allow themselves to be defeated". Source: DailyArt; mydailyartdisplay.
- P10. The portrait as a political roll-call. Nos Eclaireuses (1914) gathers seven named pioneers -- cyclist/aviator Helene Dutrieu, publisher Anna-Catherine Strebinger, collector Marguerite Roussel, lawyer Suzanne Grinberg, archaeologist Jane Dieulafoy, novelist Lucie Delarue-Mardrus, aviator Elise Deroche -- "lean[ing] together in solidarity, trailblazing the feminist cause". Source: musings-on-art; mydailyartdisplay.
- P11. Pastel as the serious medium. Her first teacher Pauline Coeffier was "a specialist in pastel portraits"; the influence "was noticed in her use of pastels, a technique with which Beaury-Saurel would achieve some of the most important recognitions of her career" (Prix d'Honneur, Blanc et Noir 1891; medals 1885, 1889). Her best-known works (Dans le bleu 1894, Apres dejeuner 1899) are pastels. Source: musings-on-art; Ader; Clark.
- P12. The sitter's gaze and hands carry the character. In the Severine portrait (1893) "Severine unsmilingly stares directly at us, almost through us"; "notice the meticulous attention the artist has given to illustrating Severine's hands"; the red flower at the sash "suggests her leftist political leanings". The attitude of the subject, not the finery, is the portrait. Source: musings-on-art; DailyArt.
- P13. Run the school and lift other women through it. As director after 1907 she ran the Academie with her nephews' help, and "from her position at the Societe des Artistes Francais, Beaury-Saurel promoted entry of artists such as Francine Richard-Hennecart, Laure Boucher, Renee de Royer, Marthe Debes or Marguerite Babin... into the Societe's subcommittees". She was "the first woman to hold this position" on the Julian teaching staff (1895). Source: musings-on-art; National Museum Warsaw journal.
- P14. Early standing: "one of the most important artists of the Salon in 1880", six years after her Salon debut (1874; first participation 1873 per Ader/musings, see C1). Source: Wikipedia; DailyArt. [Single claim, repeated; treat as reported, not independently verified.]
- P15. The "virility" verdict. She "was admired for the 'virility' of her technique", paired by Yeldham with Anna Bilinska; her 1887 Salon self-portrait, a drawing, "received superlative praise". The period's highest praise for a woman painter was to call her technique manly. Source: Wikipedia, citing Yeldham 1984.
- P16. The Bonnat endorsement. She "became a very popular portrait painter, thanks to Leon Bonnat" and "was soon recommended for her talent by Leon Bonnat (1833-1922)". A male academician's recommendation was the currency that opened portrait commissions. Source: Wikipedia; Ader.
- P17. Conservative critics were cool, and talked about her looks instead. "Her personal appearance was widely highlighted by art critics evaluating her work, which was not always well-received, especially by the most conservative critics." Source: musings-on-art. [SINGLE SOURCE]
- P18. The modern scholarly verdict: a feminist painter, not a society portraitist who happened to be a woman. Illan Martin's papers are titled "An excellent plea in favor of the rights of the woman by a painter" and "A female painter's excellent argument in favor of women's rights"; Chapman reproduces her Severine as the glamorous militant feminist. Source: musings-on-art; Chapman.
- P19. The Weisberg pairing with Breslau. Gabriel P. Weisberg "points out that the career of Beaury-Saurel, who had begun classes at the Academie Julian in 1878, was well established by the time she dated the pastel in 1894"; the #497 leg records that "Weisberg pairs her with Breslau as the two who worked personally and dynamically and made political statements about equality of the sexes". Source: 19thc-artworldwide; louise-breslau.md N4. [The 1878 enrolment date conflicts with the 1874 consensus; see C4. The pairing is a trio with Rosa Bonheur; see the Doorway, D5.]
- P20. Institutional afterlife: revival, not continuity. She was "included in the 2018 exhibit Women in Paris 1850-1900" (Clark/Denver/Speed; 37 women, curated by Laurence Madeline), and her image was used c. 1900s on the label of La Feria perfume from the Letheric House, "targeted to independent and creative women, traits symbolized by Beaury-Saurel". Source: Wikipedia; musings-on-art. [The perfume-label claim is single-source.]

## Load-Bearing Corrections
- C0. Birth year and day. Born 1848, not 1849: the en.wikipedia infobox says 1849, but the article body, Illan Martin, Clark, Ader and DailyArt all say 1848, and the tomb inscription reads "1848-1924" (APPL). The birth DAY is unresolved: 17 December 1848 (Illan Martin via musings-on-art; mydailyartdisplay; eo.wikipedia) against 19 December 1848 (fr.wikipedia; it.wikipedia; ro.wikipedia; APPL citing base Leonore; es.wikipedia infobox). The 19 December side is the larger group and has the Leonore backing; the 17 December side is the specialist scholarly paper. The study carries "17 or 19 December 1848" and states no single day as settled. Weekdays verified: Sunday 17 December 1848; Tuesday 19 December 1848.
- C1. Salon debut. First Salon participation was 1873, not 1874: Ader (with catalogue no. 73, Portrait de M. P. R), the fr.wikipedia Salon list, and musings-on-art all give 1873; en.wikipedia and DailyArt say the debut was in 1874, apparently conflating debut with Julian enrolment. 1873 is carried.
- C2. Marriage age. She married at 46, not 47: born December 1848, married Wednesday 9 January 1895. The musings-on-art summary says 47, and legs 1 and 3 repeat it; the arithmetic is against them. The "average marriage age around 23" comparison still stands.
- C3. Third Medal year. Modern sources and fr.wikipedia, citing Theodore Veron's Dictionnaire Veron, Salon de 1885, give the third medal at the Salon of 1885 for Portrait de Mlle S...; the contemporary Clara Erskine Clement Waters (Women in the Fine Arts, 1904) says "third-class medal, Salon, 1883". Unresolved; 1885 is carried with the conflict flagged.
- C4. Julian enrolment year. 1874 is the consensus (Illan Martin, Clark, Wikipedia); Weisberg, via Hauptman's review, says she "had begun classes at the Academie Julian in 1878", and Ader says she began attending the women's studio "around 1875, at the age of twenty-five". Unresolved; 1874 is carried with the variants flagged. Note Ader's "age twenty-five" fits 1874 better than 1875, since she was born in December 1848.
- C5. Legion of Honour date. Appointed Chevalier de la Legion d'honneur in 1923 (musings-on-art; fr.wikipedia), nine months before her death. APPL lists "chevalier de la Legion d'honneur (7 aout 1928)" -- impossible, she died in 1924; APPL's date is an error, probably a misread of a Leonore record. Do not repeat the 1928 date.
- C6. Felix Voisin portrait date. The Salon catalogue dates Portrait de M. Felix Voisin to 1888 (Salon 1888, no. 162); the en.wikipedia gallery captions it 1898. The Salon catalogue date 1888 is carried; 1898 is flagged.
- C7. Gilbert Dupuis portrait date. The fr.wikipedia Liste des oeuvres dates Portrait de Gilbert Dupuis to 1922; the fr.wikipedia Galerie captions a Gilbert Dupuis portrait 1920. The Liste date 1922 is carried; 1920 is flagged.
- C8. The Weisberg pairing. It is a TRIO with Rosa Bonheur, not a pair with Breslau alone; Bonheur is the "eminence grise" and "mere sainte", the fixed point by which much was judged. The source is Weisberg's essay in the 2001 Breslau catalogue (Lepdor, Kruger and Weisberg), known only through Hauptman's review -- not Overcoming All Obstacles (1999), where Weisberg's essay is on training methods. The exact wording "making political statements about equality of the sexes" is Hauptman's paraphrase of Weisberg, not a verified direct quotation.
- C9. Nos Eclaireuses identification. The seven are carried per mydailyartdisplay and musings-on-art: Dutrieu, Strebinger (brush-holder), Roussel, Grinberg, Dieulafoy, Delarue-Mardrus, Deroche. Leg 2's Phase 5 text naming "Marguerite Vervoort / Mme Henri Rochefort" is not the standard identification and is not carried; the brush-holder is Anna-Catherine Strebinger, Mme Henri Rochefort.
- C10. Dans le bleu as self-portrait. Suggested ("the model resembles the artist") but unconfirmed in every source; do not assert.
- C11. "Most important artists of the Salon in 1880". Repeated widely (Wikipedia, DailyArt, fr.wikipedia) but traces to a single claim, flagged "reference necessaire" in fr.wikipedia; treat as reported, not independently verified.
- C12. Academie Julian founding year. 1868 is the standard date; mydailyartdisplay says Julian opened his own school in 1863 and founded the Academie in 1868 in the same article, and the Academie Julian Wikipedia article gives 1867. 1868 is carried.
- C13. Who directed the Academie, 1908-1914. An APPL photo caption calls Gilbert Dupuis "directeur de l'Academie Julian de 1908 a 1914", which sits uneasily with "she directed the Academie until 1924" (Clark, musings, Ader, fr.wikipedia). The operational directorship may have sat with him in those years while she held the title; the division of labour between her and her nephews is not pinned down in any source read. Neither claim is asserted over the other.
- C14. mydailyartdisplay errors. The post calls her "nineteen-year-old" in 1874 (she was 25 if born December 1848) and says Julian "in 1863 he opened his own art school" (see C12). Neither error is carried, and the post is not cited alone for dates.
- C15. Single-source claims carried only as flagged. The La Feria perfume-label use (musings; fr.wikipedia); the award at the 1900 Exposition Universelle (Artnet); the first medal at Barcelona 1889 (fr.wikipedia); the London 1902 / Brussels / Liverpool / Tananarive exhibitions (fr.wikipedia, no dates or works for the last three); Tristesse (1894, ro.wikipedia gallery only); Portrait de jeune homme au beret (1894, APPL caption only).
- C16. The Saurel lineage. The claim that her mother's family traced their lineage to the Byzantine emperors of the 11th century is repeated across sources but is family lore, not verified genealogy.
- C17. Possible double-counted works. Portrait of a girl (1895, pastel, private collection) may or may not be the same work as Portrait of a Girl with Blue Bow (1874); the two are carried separately and the possible identity is flagged. Le Repos du modele (1894, Amiens) may be the same canvas as the Salon 1896 entry "Modele au repos" (no. 2139, pastel); counted once. The Salon 1891 entry "Travail" (no. 89) is treated as Le travail de M. Frey under a short title, not a second work. L'ecrivain public (1896, Barbezieux) may be a second version of the Salon 1893 entry "Ecrivain public; souvenir de Barcelone" (no. 1855, pastel); counted once.
- C18. The 1880 Salon standing arithmetic. DailyArt and Wikipedia say she was considered one of the most important artists of the Salon in 1880 "with her first appearance just six years earlier" -- six years before 1880 is 1874, but the debut was 1873 (see C1), so the arithmetic in the quote is off by a year.
- C19. Yeldham "virility" quote. The underlying French review begins "On chercherait en vain, parmi les..." and is truncated in the Wikipedia citation of Yeldham 1984; the full sentence was not retrieved. Yeldham's book itself was not opened.
- C20. Q19 is about Bilinska, not Beaury-Saurel. The Athena Art Foundation line on Bilinska's 1887 Self-Portrait is included only because the same year's paired "virility" verdict (Q4) ties the two women together; it is tagged as such and must not be quoted as a verdict on Beaury-Saurel.
- C21. Salon record gaps. No Salon entries are listed for 1874-1878, 1881, 1882, 1895, 1907, 1912, or 1915-1919 (the war gap). The 1895 gap is the marriage year; the 1907 gap is the year Julian and her mother died. The gaps are as listed in fr.wikipedia's Salon list, which cites the Musee d'Orsay Salons database for 1873-1914 and the Societe des Artistes Francais catalogues for 1920-1924; the database itself was not consulted directly.
- C22. The 1906 interview. It survives only as reported by Illan Martin via the musings-on-art summary; the interview itself was not read in full, and the porcelain-painting view (Q2) is a paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation.
- C23. "First woman on the Julian teaching staff". The National Museum Warsaw journal says Beaury-Saurel was the first woman on the teaching staff in 1895, with Edouard Toudouze joining the same year; the claim rests on that single journal source.
- C24. The father's death date. Leg 2's phrasing is garbled ("at the end of 1850, most likely due to the death of her father"); the carried reading is that her father died in the late 1850s, the factory faltered, and the family moved to Paris around 1859. No exact year was found in any source read.
