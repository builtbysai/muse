## The life: the painter who quit painting to measure seeing

Francois Molnar -- Hungarian name Ferenc Molnar -- studied at the Academy of Fine Arts in Budapest and graduated in 1947; he met his future wife Vera Molnar (nee Gacs, 1924-2023) there, and both moved to Paris in 1947, marrying in 1948 (C7201, C7203, C7205). For their first decade in Paris they made paintings and collages collaboratively, working to a shared ideology in the current of post-war Concrete Art; their friend Francois Morellet, whom they met in 1957, called them "un peintre a deux tetes" -- a two-headed painter (C7207). The method was already experimental: they pinned paper cut-outs to the wall of their home-studio and alternately modified them until they agreed the composition was finished, Vera then doing the painting and gluing (C7207). Because the shared ideology de-emphasised individual authorship, solo and collaborative works from the 1950s are nearly impossible to tell apart without signatures (C7207).

The turn to science was prepared long before the break. He had begun studies in experimental psychology at the University of Paris in 1952 (C7201) -- eight years before CRAV -- so the science was a parallel track taken up in the middle of the painting years, not a repudiation adopted cold in 1960. His first theoretical work, the 1959 essay on Vasarely in the Galerie Denise Rene catalogue, already argued from information theory and cybernetics that a painting's countable, combinable units announced a possible "science of art" (C7201, C7251).

In July 1960 Francois and Vera Molnar were among the founders of the Centre de Recherche d'Art Visuel in Paris: ZKM lists twelve founders -- Hector Garcia Miranda, Horacio Garcia Rossi, Hugo Rodolfo Demarco, Julio Le Parc, Vera and Francois Molnar, Francois Morellet, Sergio Moyano, Servanes (Simone Revoil), Joel Stein, Francisco Sobrino and Yvaral -- who rented a small garage in the Marais as a common meeting and exhibition space (C7202, C7302). Four months later the Molnars left, over a fundamental disagreement about the definition of "research": Francois's letter of resignation is dated 30 November 1960, and Vera left in solidarity, along with Servanes (C7207, C7208). The Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes states the split in one sentence that is the network fact of this study: "Later, Demarco, Garcia Miranda, Molnar, Servanes and Moyano separated from the group" (C7304). Molnar and Demarco were not just co-founders; they were co-leavers, both out before the July 1961 rename, while six others carried the group on as GRAV (C7302). Their last co-signed canvas, Effet esthetique de l'inversion des fonctions par la fluctuation de l'attention (1960), was shown that same year in Max Bill's Konkrete Kunst at the Helmhaus, Zurich (C7207).

After leaving, he quit making paintings to tackle the science of art "from the other side of the 'Two Cultures' debate, becoming a researcher in experimental aesthetics, eventually pursuing a doctorate and directing l'Institut d'esthetique et des sciences des arts at the Sorbonne" (C7208). The posts, per ZKM's single timeline: from 1963 at the Institut d'Esthetique et des Sciences de l'Art, University of Paris I; from 1969 teaching at the University of Paris; director of research at the CNRS; director of the Centre de Recherche Experimentale et Informatique des Arts Visuels (C7201). In 1965 he took part in the first SIGMA festival of contemporary art in Bordeaux (C7210); the same year he published "Contribution a l'etude experimentale de la composition picturale" (C7201). In 1967 the Molnars co-founded the group Art et Informatique at the Sorbonne institute, mostly music composers and poets, Vera the only painter (C7210). In 1968 he published "Towards Science in Art" in DATA, the year Vera first gained access to a real computer (C7201, C7252).

The collaborations did not end in 1960; they changed shape. For the next three decades, until his death in 1993, the couple remained each other's primary interlocutors, Vera's painting influencing his research and vice versa (C7207). In 1974 they visited Bell Laboratories in New Jersey with their friend Bela Julesz and returned with a computerised "double self-portrait" made from a snapshot of the couple (C7210). Between 1974 and 1976, in collaboration with Vera, he devised the Molnart programme, written in Fortran for a large computer linked to a display screen and a plotter, "a manifesto of the artistic use of the computer" (C7204, C7264); plotter printouts bore the header "JOB FROM MOLNAR" (C7210). The New York Times states plainly "Mr. Molnar died in 1993" (C7209); no exact death date was found in any source read, and none is stated here.

His network is the CRAV room and the laboratory. Demarco is the fellow founder and fellow leaver, the immediate predecessor of this study (C7302, C7304, C7311). Vera Molnar is wife, fellow founder, co-leaver, Art et Informatique co-founder and Molnart co-author -- already studied, context only (C7305, C7306, C7307, C7308). Francois Morellet is the fellow founder who stayed into GRAV and the one founder with a documented joint publication with Molnar, "Pour un art abstrait progressif" for New Tendencies 2, Zagreb, 1963 -- the control case for the fork (C7305). No dated one-to-one Demarco-Molnar collaboration and no joint exhibition was found; the evidenced link is the shared founding and the shared exit, and it must not be overstated (C7311, C7320).

## M1 -- The picture as experiment: vary one thing, compare the series

The seed of everything is the 1947-1960 joint practice (W01): abstract, systematically determined paintings made as "scientific experiments" (C7252), varying simple geometric elements against classical composition -- symmetry, golden ratio, pictorial balance -- and comparing serial images to isolate what changes visually. Apres Mondrian (W02, 1950, gouache and pencil on paper, 49.5 x 50.2 cm) simplifies Mondrian's modular grid and colour field by emphasising heavy intersecting vertical and horizontal lines; Lent Mouvement (W03, 1955, gouache and collage on paper, 48.3 x 24.1 cm) isolates one Malevich quadrilateral inside a deep blue ground (C7256). The homage-to-a-master structure -- Mondrian, then Malevich -- is the pair's shared method of learning by systematic variation: reduce a master painting to its grid logic, change one thing, look at what happens.

**Takeaway:** treat the picture as a test, not an expression. One motif, one change, a series to compare.

## M2 -- The imaginary machine: write the rule first, execute it by hand

By 1959 Vera was anticipating the computer with a "machine imaginaire" -- a method of drawing images generated by algorithms and programmes she developed herself, executed painstakingly by hand, one parameter altered at a time (C7255, C7260). Francois's role is the theoretical frame: the picture as a rule-governed system whose outputs can be compared (W04). The hand is the plotter before any plotter exists. The mechanism is the order of operations -- rule first, execution second, selection third -- and it never changes for the rest of the career: the executor changes (hand, then text, then plotter), the order does not.

**Takeaway:** write the rule before you draw. The hand is a slow plotter; the rule is the work.

## M3 -- The essay as the work: a painting is a message with countable units

The 1959 Vasarely essay (W05) is the hinge of the career: Vasarely read through information theory and cybernetics, his countable, combinable units taken as announcing a "science of art" and an "experimental aesthetics" (C7201). The mechanism is measurement: treat a painting as a message, count its units, measure redundancy against information. The essay, not a canvas, is the work -- and it is the text that later justifies the computer work, because once a picture is countable units it can be generated by a machine. The 1963 text with Morellet, Pour un art abstrait progressif (W07), defends the same programme as a manifesto: abstract art not as style but as a progressive, research-based practice (C7257).

**Takeaway:** count the picture before you paint it. Redundancy is the variable; the essay is where the work happens.

## M4 -- The four-month membership: research first, group second

The CRAV episode (W06) is four months long and it is a statement in itself: founded July 1960, left 30 November 1960, over the definition of "research" -- the other members' work was, in the couple's words, insufficiently scientific (C7205, C7207). The mechanism is a refusal: the collective, anonymous visual research was the right programme, but the group would not hold it to a scientific standard, so the research left the room rather than compromise. Morellet stayed and became GRAV; Molnar left and became the laboratory. The resignation letter, reproduced in the 1998 GRAV catalogue (pp. 59-61), is the document of the fork (C7207).

**Takeaway:** leave the group before you dilute the question. The research is the membership, not the room.

## M5 -- Composition as a variable: the eye is the measuring instrument

The 1965 paper "Contribution a l'etude experimentale de la composition picturale" (W08) is the laboratory version of the 1959 essay: composition treated as a variable to be tested on viewers, not intuited by the painter (C7201). "Towards Science in Art" (W09, 1968) states the programme in English for an art-theory audience, the same year Vera first used a real computer (C7201, C7252). The mechanism is the reversal of the usual roles: the painter does not compose and the viewer does not admire -- the researcher composes a test and the viewer's eye reports. The picture is the stimulus; the reading is the result.

**Takeaway:** make the viewer the instrument. A composition is a question asked of an eye, and the answer is the work.

## M6 -- The 1% disorder: a strict order, disturbed just enough

Interruptions (W10, 1968-69) is the first plotted proof: a regular grid of short line segments disrupted by randomly omitted and rotated elements -- the Morgan account says "the voids in the drawing are the result of randomness that Molnar built into the program", and the V and A calls the series "among her earliest computer-generated images, made using an iterative process" (C7258, C7259). (Des)Ordres (W13, 1974, plotter drawing, coloured ink on paper, 91.7 x 77.3 cm) runs the same engine in colour: a 20 x 20 grid of concentric squares, "creating a sense of jostling", the V and A label saying "the contrast between order and disorder creates the impression of movement, as if the squares are vibrating against one another" (C7261). The criterion is Vera's own, quoted by Guillermet: "For something to happen on a surface and for an 'aesthetic state' to be created, one must break with the original monotony, which amounts to lowering the redundancy rate" (C7252). The answer to the question M5 asked is a number: about one percent.

**Takeaway:** disturb the order by one percent and the surface wakes up. The disorder is the dose, not the accident.

## M7 -- Molnart: the program is the artwork's score

Between 1974 and 1976, in collaboration with Vera, he devised the Molnart programme (W15), written in Fortran for an IBM System/370 mainframe with a Benson drum plotter: "a flexible programme permitting systematic pictorial experimentation", "a manifesto of the artistic use of the computer" (C7204, C7264). The V and A states it plainly for Parcours: the software "allowed her to set mathematical rules while adding small random variations" (C7263). The mechanism is four steps, always in this order: set the rules; add small random variations; plot; select. Hommage a Barbaud (W12, 1974) shows it working: nine sets of seven concentric squares in a 3 x 3 grid, the number of squares per set randomly altered, the central square randomly displaced, the sides replaced by curves and diagonals, ending in "a mess of squiggles" (C7260). The program is the score and the plotter is the instrument; the code does not survive -- only the "JOB FROM MOLNAR" header on the plotter sheets (C7260).

**Takeaway:** write the program, then let it draw. Rules, a little chance, a plot, a choice -- the score is the work and the sheet is the performance.

## M8 -- The Mondrian experiment: the experiment and the artwork are one object

Francois ran A. Michael Noll's "Human or Machine" test in his own laboratory with computer-generated versions of Mondrian's Composition in Line (second state, 1916-17) -- but unlike Noll's stimulus, his experiment "did not merely simulate the painting; rather, it generated a series of original variations on the original painting" (W18, C7252). Vera produced a large number of the variations during 1974, plotted as the Molndrian series and published in his 1977 account, "L'ordinateur dans l'art et dans la science de l'art" (C7252, C7266). The mechanism is the cleanest instance of the whole programme: take a canonical modernist composition as a stimulus, generate variations by rule instead of copying it, and ask viewers -- and the artist -- which version holds. The experiment and the artwork are the same object seen from two sides. The late work is the same idea grown old: Lettres de ma mere (W19, 1981-1991) transcribes his mother-in-law's handwriting into abstract forms, "using an increasingly random process, the lines ... become more and more chaotic as they advance to the right", legibility decaying as entropy rises (C7268); 100 Squares I-XII (W20, 1987) re-issues the 1% idea as twelve unique silkscreens "according to these [Molnart] principles" (C7264).

**Takeaway:** do not copy the master -- vary him by rule and see which version holds. The test is the picture.

## The aesthetic logic: hand, then text, then code -- the question never changes

Four continuities hold the three phases together. The vocabulary never changes: square, grid, concentric square, straight line, from Apres Mondrian (1950) to 100 Squares (1987). The method never changes: one rule, one parameter varied, serial comparison. The criterion never changes: the "visual event" or "aesthetic state" produced by lowering redundancy a little -- about one percent. And the executor changes twice: the hand executes the rule in the 1950s, the text states the rule in the 1960s, the plotter executes it in the 1970s. What changed is the locus of the work -- from the canvas, to the essay, to the program -- while the question never changes: how much disorder does a strict order need before the eye calls it art? Molnar's wager is that art can be a science, and that the science is not a metaphor: composition is a variable, the viewer is the instrument, and the picture is the experiment. He is the founder who left twice -- first the painting, for the laboratory, then the group, for the rigour -- and the programme he built kept producing pictures through Vera until long after his death in 1993.
