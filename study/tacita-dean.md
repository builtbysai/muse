# Tacita Dean: Study Notes

**Site:** Tacita Dean (b. 1965, Canterbury, Kent, England; living), British visual artist working primarily in film, best known for her 16mm and 35mm films, large-scale chalk drawings on blackboard, found photographs and artist's books, whose practice is a defence of analogue film and a record of things that no longer function in their own time; lives and works in Berlin, Germany, and Los Angeles, California. [C29035]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Bruce Nauman study: Nauman takes the body out of the picture and puts it where it cannot be avoided -- in a corridor too narrow to pass comfortably, on a monitor showing your own back, in a room shouting at you from five directions at once -- and removes the artist so the viewer's own body has to do the work; Dean takes the film out of the picture and puts it where it cannot be avoided -- on a 13-metre monolith at the end of a darkened hall, on a lighthouse beam turning in the dark, on a green flash that lasts a fraction of a second -- and removes the image's own certainty so the viewer's own watching has to do the work. Where Nauman's task contradicts the body that tries to perform it, Dean's film contradicts the eye that tries to see it, and the failure to see clearly is the work. Where Nauman came back to drawing after twenty years and found he could not go wrong with his eyes closed, Dean met him in February 2025 and filmed them both drawing blind, so the two studies share a single pair of hands. Tacita Charlotte Dean was born in Canterbury, Kent, in 1965, the daughter of Joseph Dean, a lawyer who studied classics at Merton College, Oxford, and Jenefer Dean; her brother is the architect Ptolemy Dean, her sister is named Antigone, and her grandfather was Basil Dean, the founder of Ealing Studios. She was educated at Kent College, Canterbury, studied at Falmouth School of Art, graduating in 1988, spent time at the Supreme School of Fine Art in Athens, and took an MA in painting at the Slade School of Fine Art in London, 1990-92, where she began working seriously with 16mm film. She has said of her student years, "I was a dysfunctional painter and from early on I used paper instead of canvas. I often added words in my drawings and made them in series so it was perhaps inevitable I started working with film. I have not changed much at all since I was an undergraduate." She is married to the artist Matthew Hale; they have a son, Rufus. In 2000 she moved to Berlin on a DAAD fellowship, where she has lived since, in Charlottenburg with a studio in Westend, and she later established a base in Los Angeles after an artist residency at the Getty Research Institute in 2014-2015. [C29039]

She emerged in the mid-1990s as one of the "key names" of the Young British Artists -- included in General Release at the 1995 Venice Biennale alongside Jake and Dinos Chapman, Gary Hume, Sam Taylor-Wood, Fiona Banner and Douglas Gordon -- though her work had little in common with Damien Hirst and Tracey Emin, and the assignment was always inaccurate. Disappearance at Sea (1996) brought the Turner Prize nomination in 1998; the Hugo Boss Prize followed in 2006, election to the Royal Academy in 2008, the Kurt Schwitters Prize in 2009, appointment as OBE in 2013, the Cherry Kearton Medal in 2019, an honorary doctorate from the University of the Arts Helsinki in 2024, National Academician status in 2025 and the Queen Sonja Print Award in 2026. She was a founder of savefilm.org and has campaigned to save photochemical film since FILM (2011), appearing with Christopher Nolan at the Getty's Reframing the Future of Film event in 2015; she was also instrumental in saving the archive and home of Derek Jarman, whom she met by chance on a train. In 2018 three London museums presented her simultaneously -- LANDSCAPE at the Royal Academy, PORTRAIT at the National Portrait Gallery, STILL LIFE at the National Gallery -- and the Museum of Contemporary Art Australia gave her the largest showing of her work in the southern hemisphere in 2023-24. [C29040]

Her practice crosses film, drawing, photography, printmaking, found images and sound, but film is the working material: she has written that she needs "the stuff of film as a painter needs the stuff of paint", and that her relationship to film "begins at that moment of shooting and ends in the moment of projection. Along the way, there are several stages of magical transformation that imbue the work with varying layers of intensity." The films use static or slowly shifting shots, long takes and ambient optical sound, with no commentary since the mid-1990s; the drawings are chalk on blackboard, coloured pencil on photographs, silverpoint and goldpoint on blackboard and paper; the books are found photographs kept silent; the texts she calls "asides" are anecdotal, not explanatory -- "similar to when an actor speaks suddenly directly to the audience in a play without affecting the action on stage." Her subjects are the sea and its wrecks, lighthouses and eclipses, obsolete buildings and dying technologies, and the artists and writers of the last fifty years, approached slowly and from a distance. [C29041]

The career has one constant and one change. The constant is the method: work blind, inside the camera, and let the medium decide. "Chance is present in everything I do. My films are effectively made inside the camera because of my masking technique, and I only see them when the negative is processed and printed. Sometimes there can be a great treasure, but sometimes it can all go wrong. This is how digital differs from film as you can see what you are doing at all times working digitally. Digital is like working with the lights turned on all the time. Whereas with analogue, everything is made in darkness. Things you did not intend are sometimes the best things. I am a believer in the unintended act." What changed is the scale of the blindness: early, she was blind to a single phenomenon -- a green ray, a lighthouse beam, an eclipse -- and waited for it; middle, she was blind to a whole building or factory -- a tower, a parliament, a film plant -- and let it turn or close; late, she is blind to her own hand, drawing with her eyes closed in silverpoint, and in 2026 she filmed another artist doing the same, so the blindness became a collaboration. [C29042]

## M1 -- The long take that waits: a fixed camera, and the event arrives on its own schedule

The camera does not move, the take does not cut, and the event arrives when it arrives -- or does not. In Disappearance at Sea the lighthouse beam turns for fourteen minutes while day gives way to dusk; in Banewl an eclipse changes the light on a Cornish farm over sixty-three minutes; in The Green Ray the sun sets in real time for two and a half minutes while the viewer watches for a flash that lasts a fraction of a second; in Fernsehturm the only movement in the entire film is the restaurant turning, and the camera, mounted on the moving platform, never moves at all. The mechanism is waiting as the method: a static position, a long duration, and an optical sound track that describes the atmosphere of the place, so that small changes in light, weather or gesture become meaningful events. Dean's own rule for Fernsehturm was one strict constraint -- no camera movement -- and the film's content is what the constraint lets through: windows going from transparent to translucent to opaque as the interior lights come on, diners reacting to a view that disappears. The watchable behaviour is the vigil: nothing happens, then the light changes, then the beam comes around again, then the green bead flashes and is gone, and the viewer who blinked missed it. Seen in W04, W07, W08 and W09. [C29044]

**Takeaway:** fix the camera, set one long take, and let the light be the event. [C29044]

## M2 -- The failed search as the work: go looking for what is not there, and keep the recording

Dean goes looking for something and does not find it, and the record of the failure is the piece. In 1997 she went to the Great Salt Lake to find Robert Smithson's Spiral Jetty and could not -- it was submerged -- and the sound recording of the attempt, Trying to Find the Spiral Jetty, is the work; sixteen years later JG returns to the same salt lake with Ballard's challenge to solve the jetty's mystery, and the film is built from a correspondence about a connection that may or may not exist. Donald Crowhurst sailed out to fake a circumnavigation and was lost; Dean's films do not reconstruct him, they film the lighthouse and the sea he disappeared into. The mechanism is the search without an object: the camera or the tape recorder goes to the place, the place refuses to yield its subject, and the refusal becomes the content -- "the redness of the lake, the fact that all the weed was covered in a shell of salt, the scruffy no-time of it." The watchable behaviour is a journey that ends where it began, with the viewer holding the absence instead of the thing: a lighthouse beam with no ship, a salt flat with no jetty, a sea with no sailor. Seen in W04, W05 and W17. [C29045]

**Takeaway:** film the search, not the find, and let the absence be the picture. [C29045]

## M3 -- Made inside the camera: masking, and the blindness of analogue

Dean's films are made inside the camera, not in post-production. Her masking technique -- aperture gate masking, patented for JG -- lets her combine multiple scenes, shot at different times, into composite images on a single frame: several times and several places occupying one picture, layered in the camera itself. FILM was built the same way, with masking, double-exposure and glass matte painting, and the images were deliberately created during the shoot, edited by hand by the artist alone. The mechanism is blindness as a working condition: she does not see the film until the negative is processed and printed, so the edit happens in the dark and the accidents survive. "Digital is like working with the lights turned on all the time. Whereas with analogue, everything is made in darkness. Things you did not intend are sometimes the best things." The watchable behaviour is the composite that could not exist any other way -- a waterfall and a smokestack and an ostrich egg sharing one vertical strip, a salt lake holding two different days at once -- and the flash frames, fade-outs and colour-filter shimmers that digital would have cleaned up, left in as part of the image. Seen in W15 and W17, and in W20, where the masking lets two artists appear to draw side by side. [C29046]

**Takeaway:** build the picture inside the camera, never see it until it is developed, and keep the accidents. [C29046]

## M4 -- The portrait at a tender distance: approach slowly, never look them in the eye

Dean's portraits of artists approach from afar and stay there. In Michael Hamburger (2007) she trains the camera on apple branches, a clearing, ripe fruit bobbing in the breeze, before the poet appears at all, and when he does he picks apples, describes the varieties and reads a poem aloud, coming nowhere near Dean or the camera -- "a way to address, slowly, trepidatiously, a person near the end of life, not by looking him in the eye but by studying him from afar." In Edwin Parker (2011) Twombly is filmed through window blinds and trees, reading the paper, ordering a turkey sandwich in a diner, sipping Coca-Cola; hardly any art is made, mentioned or seen. In Claes Oldenburg Draws Blueberry Pie (2023) the camera shoots from many angles but keeps its distance while the old man's hands draw with crayon, coloured pencil, a brush and his fingers, and there are no words, only traffic. In Craneway Event the 90-year-old Cunningham arrives in a wheelchair and the film watches him work for three afternoons without ever staging him. The mechanism is distance as respect: the camera waits in the room the subject already occupies, and time -- "time is manifested in their bodies," Dean says -- does the portraiture. The watchable behaviour is ordinariness held long enough to become intimate: a man eating lunch, a man picking apples, a man drawing a pie, a choreographer napping in the afternoon sun. Seen in W11, W14, W16 and W19. [C29047]

**Takeaway:** film the person from across the room, never interview them, and let time do the portrait. [C29047]

## M5 -- The found image kept silent: no caption, no explanation, no story imposed

Dean collects photographs she did not take and refuses to explain them. Floh (2001) is a book of found photographs from the flea markets of Europe and America, presented without captions: "I do not want to give these images explanations: descriptions by the finder about how and where they were found, or guesses about what stories they might or might not tell. I want them to keep the silence of the fleamarket; the silence they had when I found them; the silence of the lost object." Czech Photos (1991-2002) is over 326 unedited photographs in a box; Washington Cathedral (2002) is over 130 found postcards of imagined versions of a cathedral before it was completed; from 2005 she paints out the backgrounds of found postcards of trees with white gouache, isolating the tree; in 2019 she made dozens of monoprints on postcards of oranges, beaches, dams and temples, each invaded by blobs of colour. The mechanism is the found object left lost: the image keeps the silence it had when found, and the viewer supplies the story the book withholds. The watchable behaviour is turning pages of strangers' photographs that refuse to explain themselves -- a frozen Niagara Falls, an Austrian edelweiss, a cathedral that was never built that way -- and feeling the gap between the picture and everything it has lost. Seen in W10. [C29048]

**Takeaway:** find the photograph, print it as found, and say nothing about it. [C29048]

## M6 -- Things out of their own time: the building, the factory, the technology that no longer functions

"Everything that excites me no longer functions in its own time." The Bubble House is a futuristic house that was never contemporary; the Teignmouth Electron is Crowhurst's beached catamaran, a failed vessel rotting on Cayman Brac; the Palast der Republik is a parliament that met 28 times and hosted enforced culture, filmed while the government debated flattening it before the World Cup; the Fernsehturm is an East Berlin tower filmed after the East had gone; Kodak is a film factory filmed weeks before it closed for good. The mechanism is filming a structure at the moment its purpose has expired: the camera records the building doing nothing, or doing the wrong thing, and the obsolescence becomes the subject rather than the backdrop. In Kodak the machinery still threads miles of film through the plant while the viewer knows the plant is about to stop; in Palast the mirrored windows reflect a regime that is already gone. The watchable behaviour is a building outliving its use -- light moving across an empty restaurant, emulsion running through a doomed factory, a parliament reflecting an empty square -- and the poignancy is that the film, too, is a technology on the same road. Seen in W08, W12 and W13. [C29049]

**Takeaway:** point the camera at the thing that has outlived its purpose, and film it until the purpose drains out. [C29049]

## M7 -- Chalk on blackboard: the drawing that can be erased

Dean's large drawings are made in the most erasable medium there is. The Roaring Forties: Seven Boards in Seven Days (1997) are stormy seas in chalk on blackboard, with written fragments like navigation notes; The Montafon Letter (2017) and The Wreck of Hope (2022) are each about twelve by twenty-four feet of white chalk on blackboard -- an avalanche and a collapsing glacier -- made with a small stick of school chalk, with no fixative applied and the blackboard unglazed, so that, in the Menil curator's words, the drawing "has such physical fragility that it actually teeters on the brink of erasure." The Blind Folly slates (2024) are found green school slates, their marks made by pulling a finger through dust. The mechanism is fragility as form and content at once: the chalk drawing of a melting iceberg is itself melting, the drawing of an avalanche could be wiped away by a sleeve, and the subject -- a glacier disappearing in an afternoon, a coastline crumbling -- is the same disappearance the medium enacts. Dean had to retouch the Menil drawings for installation, and at the bottom-left corner of The Wreck of Hope she scrawled "Sorry, Dave" in chalk, apologising to a rental-car owner for filling his tank with diesel. The watchable behaviour is standing twenty feet from a wall of white chalk and seeing both the iceberg and the dust it is made of, knowing a touch would end it. Seen outside the W01-W20 set, in the Menil/Artnet/NYRB accounts read end to end, and in W06's blackboard sea drawings. [C29050]

**Takeaway:** draw the disappearing thing in the disappearing medium, fix nothing, and let the dust be the drawing. [C29050]

## M8 -- The film about film: sprocket holes, flash frames and the factory floor as memorial

Dean's most famous works take film itself as the subject. FILM (2011) is a portrait of the analogue, photochemical, non-digital medium: a Cinemascope lens turned 90 degrees, a normal widescreen stood on end, a 13-metre monolith that is itself a strip of film, sprocket holes exposed on the emulsion, the transparency of celluloid made visible so the viewer seems to see through the screen to the wall behind. Kodak (2006) films the making of the last run of 16mm stock, the factory as memorial. The mechanism is showing the apparatus: the sprocket holes that normally stay hidden, the flash frames of over-exposure as the camera stops and starts, the picture fading at the tail end of a roll, the projector left in view with its low whirring audible in the dark hall. At the Marciano in 2026 the projection is grainy, flickering, mottled by dust, interrupted by flashes of overexposure -- and that is the point, not a flaw: "It is not only emulsion versus pixels, or light versus electronics but something deeper -- something to do with poetry." The watchable behaviour is looking up at a column of light and watching the medium declare itself -- grain, flicker, dust, sprocket holes -- while the images (waterfall, clock reading 11:11, ostrich egg, smokestack) drift through it like a visual poem. Seen in W13 and W15. [C29051]

**Takeaway:** turn the lens sideways, show the sprocket holes, and make the medium the picture. [C29051]

## M9 -- The blind hand: drawing with eyes closed, and the unintended act

Dean draws blind, and in 2026 she made blindness a duet. She had begun using silverpoint on blackboard surfaces when she met Nauman in February 2025; he had returned to drawing after nearly twenty years in gold- and silverpoint and was filming himself drawing blind with a 3D iPhone rig, observing, "You can't go wrong when you are drawing with your eyes closed." In Drawing Blind Drawing (2026) only their hands are visible: one artist is blind and assisted by the other who can see, and who leads and who is led changes in the other half; in one drawing they use only one hand, in the other both; the diptych is divided diagonally, so four sequences interweave and the hands appear in different scales, intertwining and merging. The mechanism is the unintended act as a discipline: the hand draws without the eye correcting it, the mask decides where the hands appear to be, and the drawing emerges over around four minutes as a choreography of gestures. It is the same mechanism as the masking in FILM and JG -- work in the dark, trust the process, keep the accident -- applied to the hand instead of the camera. The watchable behaviour is sitting and watching a line arrive without error, because no error is possible when the eye is closed, and watching the roles of blind and seeing swap halfway through. Seen in W20, and in the Menil slates, where the finger smudges the dust without a plan. [C29052]

**Takeaway:** close your eyes, let the other hand guide, and film the line arriving without error. [C29052]

## M10 -- The cut-up self-portrait: outtakes, postcards and the accidental autobiography

Dean's most personal film is made from what she threw away. Geography Biography (2023) is her "accidental self-portrait": a diptych film collage from the cutting-room floor, splicing outtakes from early Super 8 and standard 8mm footage with 16mm films and ephemera collected over the years -- Super 8 of a summer storm in south-west France, 35mm of a friend's baby's foot, 16mm of her son Rufus at six months basking in the Irish sun, intercut with stills of 1920s sepia postcards, a frozen Niagara Falls, an Austrian edelweiss, and personal photographs of Dean wearing a fake beard in the early 1990s and her husband Matthew Hale celebrating his birthday. The mechanism is the archive as autobiography: nothing is shot for the film, everything is found in the artist's own cutting room, and the life emerges from the scraps in the order they happen to fall, screened on two four-metre-high projections that never agree. It is Floh's found photograph turned inward -- the lost object is her own footage -- and the "asides" turned into a film: anecdotal, not explanatory, adding to the work without explaining it. The watchable behaviour is watching a life flicker past in fragments that refuse to add up to a story, two screens changing at different rates, the personal history conveyed through fleeting images that speak to the transitory nature of the medium itself. Seen outside the W01-W20 set, in the Guardian and Ocula accounts read end to end. [C29053]

**Takeaway:** cut the film from your own outtakes, run it on two screens that never agree, and let the scraps be the life. [C29053]

## Aesthetic logic

Dean's logic is the eye given a job it cannot do cleanly, in a medium that will not let it check its work. Against Nauman, whose task contradicts the body that tries to perform it, Dean's film contradicts the eye that tries to see it: the green ray cannot be isolated in a single frame, the lighthouse beam is gone by the time you look for it, the jetty is underwater, the eclipse is over, the factory is closed, the chalk will wipe away. What changes across thirty-four years is not the question but what is doing the waiting: first the artist alone, waiting for a phenomenon with a fixed camera; then the artist waiting for a building to finish dying; then the artist waiting for the film itself to come back from the lab; then the artist waiting for her own hand to draw without her. The work is always the interval -- between the shutter opening and the negative coming back, between the sun touching the water and the green bead flashing, between the found photograph and the story it refuses to tell, between the blind hand and the seeing one -- and the interval is where the viewer has to stand, usually in the dark, looking up. She refuses to convert to digital, and the refusal is the point: if analogue film is rubbed out, she says, she can draw, she can write, she can paint -- she does not need to make that compromise -- but the films themselves are the argument that the compromise would change the medium entirely, because the meaning is the waiting, the dust, the flash frame, the sprocket hole, the chalk that could be erased. [C29054]
