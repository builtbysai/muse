Evan Ziporyn was born in 1959 in Chicago, Illinois, and raised in Evanston, in what Pytheas calls "a musically ecumenical household" -- his father's violin, his grandmother's Yiddish Socialist chorus, his mother's folk and jazz collection, top 40 and Motown on AM radio. Violin at age three at his father's insistence, given up for piano within weeks; a trumpet ambition thwarted by his fourth-grade bandmaster, and his career as a clarinetist began. He began composing at thirteen, after a high-school teacher played him, in rapid succession, Stravinsky's Le Sacre, Charles Ives' Quarter Tone Studies, Alvin Lucier's I Am Sitting in A Room, and Steve Reich's Come Out. He managed his father's small record store in high school, writing for full orchestra, jazz ensemble, the school musical, and a basement prog-rock band called Chronosynclastic Infundibulum. He studied at Eastman with Joseph Schwantner, at Yale with Martin Bresnick, David Lewin and Robert Moore while also studying piano and clarinet, and at UC Berkeley for an MA and PhD in composition with Gerard Grisey and Andrew Imbrie. [C29212]

The turn that made the career came in 1979, while an undergraduate at Yale working part-time filing vinyl at a record store: over the speakers he heard a field recording of traditional Balinese gamelan, a self-described "conversion experience" -- "It was Miles Davis, it was Coltrane, it was Bartok, it was Stravinsky, it was Weather Report and Herbie Hancock -- basically everything I liked in music... the first ten seconds of the track was all it took." That summer he worked in Oakland with Wayan Suweca and Michael Tenzer in the newly formed Gamelan Sekar Jaya; in 1981, on a Yale Murray Fellowship, he travelled to Bali and studied gender wayang, the music of the shadow play, with Suweca and pelegongan drumming with I Made Lebah, who had been Colin McPhee's driver and chief musical informant in the 1930s. He returned on a Fulbright in 1987 to learn the Sukawati gender wayang repertoire from I Wayan Loceng, and became Sekar Jaya's music director in 1989; the three compositions he wrote for Sekar Jaya all included Western instruments. He joined the MIT faculty in 1990, became Kenan Sahin Distinguished Professor of Music, served as inaugural Director of MIT's Center for Art, Science and Technology (CAST) from 2012 -- now Faculty Director -- and curates the MIT Sounding performance series. In 1993 he founded Gamelan Galak Tika at MIT with Desak Made Suarti Laksmi, one of the first Balinese female composers, and traditional dancer Nyoman Catra; the name means "fierce togetherness" in classical Javanese-Sanskrit. The group began in a damp basement and a Kresge storage closet, borrows tools from the MIT Model Railroad Club to tune and repair its instruments, runs an open policy -- "If you want to compose for the group, you can compose for the group" -- has built an electronic gamelan and collaborated with robots, and has performed at Lincoln Center and Carnegie Hall, with the Boston Pops, and on tour in Bali; in May 2025 it gave its debut concert in the new Linde Music Building, reuniting players from thirty years, the youngest eighteen and the oldest seventy-eight. [C29213]

One correction belongs in the record before anything else: Ziporyn did NOT co-found Bang on a Can. The organisation was founded in 1987 by three composers, Julia Wolfe, David Lang and Michael Gordon. What Ziporyn did -- and it is the larger fact for this study -- is perform a clarinet solo at the First Bang on a Can Marathon in New York in 1987, and in 1992 co-found the Bang on a Can All-Stars, the organisation's amplified sextet, with whom he toured the globe and premiered over a hundred commissioned works across twenty years, co-producing their 1996 recording of Brian Eno's Music for Airports and their final CD Big Beautiful Dark and Scary in 2012, before leaving in fall 2012 to form the trio Eviyan with Iva Bittova and Gyan Riley. He recorded what his own site calls the definitive version of Steve Reich's multi-clarinet New York Counterpoint in 1996, as a member of Steve Reich and Musicians, with whom he shared a Grammy Award for Best Chamber Music Performance -- the year is 1998 on the two-source version (Wikipedia and the Silkroad bio), while the MIT Music page says 1999 for Music for 18 Musicians; this study uses 1998 and carries the discrepancy. His honours include the American Academy of Arts and Letters Goddard Lieberson Fellowship (2004), the MIT Gyorgy Kepes Prize (2006), the USA Artists Walker Fellowship (2007), and Massachusetts Cultural Council awards listed as 2011 in some bios and as a Massachusetts Arts Council Fellowship in 2012 in the MIT Music page -- carried as two related listings, not merged. His commissions run from Yo-Yo Ma's Silk Road Ensemble and the Kronos Quartet to So Percussion, Roomful of Teeth, the Boston Modern Orchestra Project and the American Repertory Theater. Recent work is unabated: in June 2025 he and Eran Egozy premiered EV6, a concerto grosso for string orchestra and hundreds of cellphones, at FUTURE PHASES, ICMC 2025, MIT; he wrote Earth Studies for the PARTCH Ensemble, premiered at REDCAT, Los Angeles, in June 2025; In Light of Sound for the Kinetic Ensemble premiered 22 February 2025 at MIT and in Houston in May 2025; the Gamelan Showdown, a battle-of-the-bands mebarung with Gamelan Yowana Sari, is set for 21 March 2026; and the MIT Chapel calendar lists him performing In My Mind and In My Car with Christine Southworth on 2 December 2026. [C29214]

The twenty works inspected run 1986-2025 and trace one practice learning to speak through other bodies, then handing the speaking over entirely. Early, 1986-1999: the solo instrument as laboratory -- solo clarinet and bass clarinet works (Waiting by the Phone, What She Saw There, Walk the Dog, Two Obsessions), the cross-cultural ventriloquism already explicit, made programmatic in Four Impersonations (1999), where one clarinet speaks Japanese shakuhachi, Balinese gamelan and East African nyatiti in succession. The 1990s: gamelan fused with a rock band -- Tire Fire (1994) wires bronze metallophones into distorted electric guitars, Amok! (1996) doubles live gamelan with samplers -- and the tuning clash is left audible; the New World liner notes name the stance a "willful misreading", and the falsification is the point. The 2000s: scale -- the evening-length theater works ShadowBang (2001), Oedipus Rex (2004) and A House in Bali (2009) put two complete systems on one stage at once, while the orchestra is re-thought through gamelan logic, additive minimalism accumulating into unison in Frog's Eye (2002), and non-Western soloists -- lap steel, tabla, pipa -- take concerto roles. 2005-2006: the robot turn -- Belle Labs (2006) makes a MIDI-controlled xylophone, the Heliphon, a performer with its own timing, and from here technology is a collaborator, not a tool. The 2010s: the personal archive and the pop canon -- In My Mind and In My Car (2013/2014) turns his own field recordings and road footage into the material, and Blackstar (2017) turns David Bowie's last album into a cello concerto. 2017-2025: the audience and the network become the instrument -- Tutti (2017) turns a thousand phones into an orchestra, Arachnodrone (2018) turns a spider's web into a harp, The Demon in the Diagram (2018) turns visitors pushing screens into composers, Poppy 88 (2023) turns 88 musicians on six continents into one ensemble with no click track, and EV6 (2025) folds the phone orchestra back into the concert hall as a concerto grosso. ## M1 -- Ventriloquism: one instrument, three incompatible voices

The founding move of Ziporyn's practice is ventriloquism, and Four Impersonations (1999) states it in the composer's own words. In Balinese trance, he writes, subjects are inhabited by specific people or entities who speak through them: their voice remains their own, but the words they speak are foreign to them, often in ancient or foreign languages they themselves do not understand. In these pieces the voices of three different cultures -- Japanese shakuhachi (Honshirabe), Balinese gamelan (Pengrangrang Gede and Bindu Semara), and East African nyatiti (Thum Nyatiti) -- speak through the clarinet. The method is transcription first, then reproduction on clarinet through extended technique: microtonal fingerings, multiphonics, circular breathing. The clarinet is a medium, not a soloist: one instrument ventriloquising three incompatible tuning systems and timbres in succession, never blending them into one voice. The same move is already there in the earliest works -- What She Saw There (1988) sets a bass-clarinet monologue against the dappled chiming of two marimbas, the gamelan's interlocking kotekan logic translated onto Western tuned percussion with a single Western wind voice on top -- and it runs all the way to In My Mind and In My Car (2013/2014), where the clarinet speaks over archival recordings from Bali, Tanzania and the Scottish Highlands. The mechanism is impersonation without translation: the host instrument keeps its own voice while speaking another music's words, and the friction between the two is the content. The watchable behaviour is a single line that changes its accent completely from one section to the next -- same instrument, same player, a different music speaking through it. Seen in W07, W02 and W15. [C29216]

**Takeaway:** let one instrument be inhabited by another music, and keep the accent foreign. [C29216]

## M2 -- Two complete systems on one stage: come together and drift apart, mesh and clash

The large works put two whole musical worlds on one stage at the same time and refuse to resolve them. A House in Bali (2009) is the fullest statement: an amplified sextet (the Bang on a Can All-Stars) and a full Balinese gamelan (Gamelan Salukat) share the stage simultaneously, Western opera singers in Baroque-derived vocal style beside Balinese traditional singers and dancers, live video feeds and recorded footage, the score quoting McPhee's own transcriptions -- and the gamelan players perform without any written score and with barely any conducting. ShadowBang (2001) does the same thing in miniature: a Balinese dalang -- traditionally singer, narrator, puppeteer and gamelan-leader in one -- shares the stage with the amplified All-Stars, the puppet screen as the visual field and the Western ensemble rebuilt as the "gamelan". Ziporyn's own description of the device, from the 2009 MIT News interview on A House in Bali, is the usable sentence: the disparate sounds "come together and drift apart, mesh and clash -- just as they do in my own imagination". The mechanism is simultaneity without synthesis: two systems, each complete, each audible in full, allowed to coincide and to collide, with the meshing and the clashing both kept. The watchable behaviour is two ensembles visibly playing different musics in the same room, their patterns locking for a passage and then sliding apart, the stage holding both at once. Seen in W14, W08 and W10. [C29217]

**Takeaway:** put two whole systems on one stage and let them mesh and clash. [C29217]

## M3 -- Interlocking cycles that accumulate into unison

The gamelan's core device -- kotekan, interlocking parts that no single player holds whole -- becomes Ziporyn's orchestral grammar. Frog's Eye (2002) is the cleanest demonstration: the title is literal, a frog sitting almost entirely immersed in water with only its eyes above the surface, and the music does the same thing -- simple gestural elements and additive processes of minimalism build contrapuntal and textural complexity until they coalesce into a driving rhythmic unison at the end, the whole orchestra surfacing at once. The opening carries clear Steve Reich references that go, in one reviewer's words, "beyond the mathematical" into something hypnotic and lyrical. What She Saw There (1988) is the same device in chamber dress: two marimbists, four hands, interlocking tuned percussion under a single sustained clarinet voice. Tire Fire (1994) wires it into a rock band: bronze metallophones and gongs interlocking against distorted electric guitars and bass. The mechanism is accumulation as form: scattered fragments, interlocking cycles, a slow lock-in, and then the snap -- the unison is earned by the accumulation, never given at the start. The watchable behaviour is fragments gathering, patterns locking one by one, and the whole field suddenly moving as one body. Seen in W09, W02 and W05. [C29218]

**Takeaway:** let the fragments interlock until the whole field snaps into unison. [C29218]

## M4 -- The tuning clash, left audible

Where most cross-cultural composition smooths the seam, Ziporyn leaves it open. Aneh Tapi Nyata (1992), one of three works for Gamelan Sekar Jaya, all of which included Western instruments, puts a Western chamber group and Balinese percussion in the same piece with the tuning clash -- equal temperament against pelog and slendro -- left audible rather than smoothed over; the title is Indonesian for "strange but true". Sulvasutra (2006) sets a Western string quartet, a Chinese pipa and an Indian tabla on an ancient Sanskrit treatise about altar geometry, and the three tuning systems and rhythmic logics coexist without being forced into unison. Bayu Sabda Idep (2007) goes further and builds the clash into the instrument: a just-intonation slendro chamber gamelan against a chamber string orchestra. The New World liner notes name the stance a "willful misreading" -- the falsification is the point, and the point is audible: two pitch worlds that do not agree, held in the same frame, beating against each other. The mechanism is refusal of the blend: the seam is the material, and a piece that resolved it would have nothing to say. The watchable behaviour is two tuning grids laid over each other, the beats where they disagree shimmering, the piece never settling into one key. Seen in W04, W05 and W12. [C29219]

**Takeaway:** do not resolve the tuning clash -- the clash is the piece. [C29219]

## M5 -- The sampler as a second ensemble; the archive as material

From the mid-1990s, recorded sound stops being documentation and becomes a second performer. Amok! (1996) doubles a live gamelan with percussion and keyboard samplers, so the piece layers live interlocking patterns against their own sampled ghosts -- the sampler is an early instance of Ziporyn treating recorded sound as a second ensemble. In My Mind and In My Car (2013/2014) turns the device into the whole piece: an electroacoustic travelogue of "objects to be held onto, material and otherwise", archival recordings from Bali, Tanzania and the Scottish Highlands layered under live clarinet, with Christine Southworth's videos using processed footage from beneath mangrove islands in Panama, stone quarries in the Sierra Nevada, and Route 225 between Bedford and Carlisle -- the title track shot entirely from the windshield of a yellow 1979 MGB, and the piece test-driven, literally, on that car's sound system en route to Walden Pond. The two sets began separately, worked on adjacently in shared workspaces, and gradually merged: Ziporyn improvising over Southworth's video soundscapes, Southworth making video for Ziporyn's pieces. The mechanism is the archive as a living layer: the past is not quoted, it plays alongside the present, and the live performer drives through it. The watchable behaviour is a soloist moving through his own recorded past -- live wind against field recordings and road footage, the car as concert hall. Seen in W06 and W15. [C29220]

**Takeaway:** let the recording play beside the performer, not behind it. [C29220]

## M6 -- The machine as a performer with its own timing

Belle Labs (2006) introduces a non-human performer, and from here technology is a collaborator, not a tool. The Heliphon is a MIDI-controlled, double-helix-shaped robotic xylophone that plays by striking metal keys with solenoids -- a machine percussionist with inhuman speed and precision -- and the piece is, in MIT News' words, "a virtuosic dialogue between two humans and a robot": violinist Todd Reynolds and Ziporyn on clarinet must keep up with, and play against, a partner that never tires and never bends tempo. The same concert premiered Christine Southworth's Heavy Metal for gamelan, robots and electric strings. The device recurs in larger form: in The Demon in the Diagram (2018), with Matthew Ritchie and Kelley Deal, visitors push moveable transparent painted screens around the gallery, and the movement triggers different soundscapes by Deal and Ziporyn in different combinations -- a composition exactly 25,000 minutes long, the length of time the show is open, with a VR component casting the viewer as the "demon" inside the diagram; and in The Invisible College (2020-2021), a site-specific performative VR game inside MIT's Sol LeWitt floor piece, using StyleGAN and CycleGAN imagery and GPT-2 text, with masked AR avatars as quark and anti-quark particles. The mechanism is a partner that will not accommodate you: the machine has its own timing, its own voice, and the humans must play to it. The watchable behaviour is two humans chasing a tempo that never wavers, and a room whose sound changes because a visitor pushed a screen. Seen in W13 and the appendix works. [C29221]

**Takeaway:** give the machine its own tempo, and make the humans keep up. [C29221]

## M7 -- The web as instrument: the spider is the first mover

Spider's Canvas / Arachnodrone (2018) is the collaboration made playable, and Ziporyn's part in it is the player's part. A 3D laser scan of a real Cyrtophora citricola tent-web spider's web -- thousands of silk strands -- is turned into a playable instrument: the web becomes "a modulating drone-harp with thousands of strings", in Ian Hattwick's phrase, each strand assigned a frequency from its length and its tension and elasticity, like the strings of a harp. The performance has four named roles: Isabelle Su drives the audience through the virtual web as the spider would see it; proximity to particular strings sends sonic information to Hattwick, who sculpts it in real time; Ziporyn on EWI (electronic wind instrument) and Christine Southworth on electric guitar with EBow improvise on top. Only fibres within a "hearing distance" of the viewer sound, shown blue and white; fibres outside it stay silent, green and brown. The music is in just intonation: "We play in the canvas of the spider, using sonification of 3D models and 2D images in the harmonic language of Just Intonation. Rather than playing with the spider herself, we are using her webs as the basis of our music, reading her scores, using her geometries as the foundation for our vibrations." Each performance is newly generated, never the same twice. It was preceded by the 2015 Spider Salon jam at Saraceno's Berlin studio, where Ziporyn improvised with two live vibrating spiders, and by the Arachnid Orchestra sessions of 2015. The mechanism is vibration as a common language with a gate: the web, the spider, the performer and the visitor all report in frequency, and proximity decides who gets heard -- and the spider, in Ziporyn's words carried from the Buehler study, is the real first mover. The watchable behaviour is a performer moving through a virtual web while nearby strings light and sound and far strings stay dark and silent. Seen in W18. [C29222]

**Takeaway:** tune every strand, let only the near ones sound, and let the spider move first. [C29222]

## M8 -- The audience as orchestra: a thousand phones, one clock

Tutti (2017) turns the audience into the orchestra, and EV6 (2025) turns that orchestra into a concerto. In Tutti, an audience of up to about a thousand people, each with a cellphone, becomes a one-button instrument: the audience is divided into four sections -- winds, strings, brass, percussion -- and each phone can play only the notes of its section's part. All the phones must be precisely synchronised to a common clock so they play together, and a large video display cues when to start and stop; the three-minute piece, Engineered Engineers, based on the MIT "Engineer's Drinking Song", is built to show off ensemble dynamics between the phone-sections: unison play, call-and-response, gradual buildups, a grand pause. No app install: the phone's browser is the instrument. The system was developed by Eran Egozy with Ziporyn and Nick Joliat, originating as Danielle Penny's senior thesis project, and launched in September 2017 at the MIT Campaign for a Better World. EV6 extends it: a concerto grosso in which the concertino is not a handful of soloists but the entire audience's phones, spatially spread through the hall, answering and surrounding the string orchestra A Far Cry on stage, in the round hall's 24 built-in surround speakers. The title reads as Evan plus Eran -- but that expansion is not confirmed in any source and is not asserted here. The mechanism is participation as the generative rule: the piece exists only if a thousand unsynchronised consumer devices agree on one clock, and the audience is not listening to the orchestra, it is a section of it. The watchable behaviour is a hall full of phone-lights pulsing in four colours, the string orchestra on stage answered by the room itself. Seen in W17 and W20. [C29223]

**Takeaway:** give every phone one note, one clock, and a section to belong to. [C29223]

## M9 -- The telematic ensemble: no click track, no conductor, one live mix

Poppy 88 (2023) is Ziporyn's arrangement of Terry Riley's Poppy Nogood and the Phantom Band (1967) for telematic performance: 88 musicians in 60 locations, 23 countries, six continents, live at midnight EDT on 24 June 2023 for Riley's 88th birthday -- no click tracks, no pre-recorded material, the streams mixed in real time by Jeff Morton in Wawken, Saskatchewan, via Audiomovers ListenTo, with Riley himself attending live from Japan. The MIT bio gives 64 locations; ContaQt's own project page gives 60, and this study uses 60 as the primary source. The same device, at smaller scale, is In My Mind and In My Car: two artists working adjacently, sets merging gradually, no master plan. And the same device, at the largest scale, is the gamelan itself: thirty players, no conductor, no written score, the piece held together by listening. The mechanism is synchrony without a centre: no click, no conductor, no score -- only a shared time, a live mix, and players who must listen across continents the way gamelan players listen across the room. The watchable behaviour is eighty-eight windows lighting up around the planet, each playing its own part, the mix arriving as one piece only because someone is listening to all of them at once. Seen in the appendix works. [C29224]

**Takeaway:** take away the click track and the conductor, and let listening hold the piece together. [C29224]

## M10 -- The pop canon, re-inhabited: a cello sings Bowie

Blackstar (2017) reimagines David Bowie's final album as an evening-length cello concerto: solo cello (Maya Beiser) with the Ambient Orchestra, sixty players at the premiere, twenty-six in the touring form, arrangements by Ziporyn and Jamshied Sharifi. The mechanism is a range match stated as a fact: Bowie's four-octave vocal range "matches that of the cello almost exactly", so the cello becomes Bowie -- "vocalist one moment, lead guitarist the next" -- and the songs keep their shapes while the voice changes bodies. The Ambient Orchestra, founded by Ziporyn in January 2016 as "a People's Orchestra for the 21st Century" to make "living orchestral versions of great ambient recordings", plays the album straight through as a concerto. The same re-inhabiting runs through Art Decade (released 17 October 2025, Cantaloupe), with Toronto's ContaQt ensemble: new interpretations of Bowie's "Berlin trilogy", Brian Eno's Ambient 1-4, early-1970s King Crimson and Harold Budd, with Ziporyn on clarinet and bass clarinet as producer and arranger -- though the release date rests on a Bandcamp snippet and is flagged as such. The mechanism is ventriloquism at the scale of a canon: a beloved record is not covered, it is re-bodied, the voice moved into an instrument with the same range, the band rebuilt as an orchestra, and the songs keep their shapes while everything that makes them sounds different. The watchable behaviour is a familiar album arriving in an unfamiliar body -- the melody you know, sung by an instrument that should not be able to sing it. Seen in W16. [C29225]

**Takeaway:** move the voice into a body with the same range, and the song stays itself. [C29225]

## Aesthetic logic

Ziporyn's logic is the interval between two systems, with a body standing in it. Against Buehler, whose science knows it is a language and shows you the language -- the graph, the slice, the frequency -- Ziporyn's music knows it is a translation and shows you the translation happening: the clarinet with another music's accent in its mouth, the gamelan and the rock band sharing one stage, the robot holding a tempo no human would choose, the audience holding a note it did not write, the spider's web sounding because someone is standing inside it. The work is always the interval -- between the shakuhachi and the clarinet, between pelog and equal temperament, between the phone in your hand and the orchestra on the stage, between the strand and its pitch -- and the interval is where a player has to stand, with their own breath, their own listening, their own body as the last component. What changes across forty years is not the question but the size of the body doing the speaking: first one clarinet, then an ensemble, then an opera stage, then a robot, then an archive, then a thousand phones, then a spider, then eighty-eight musicians on six continents -- each step handing more of the authorship to the system itself, and each step keeping the same rule, that the voice is borrowed and the borrowing is audible. He refuses the blend, and the refusal is the point: a piece that smoothed the tuning clash, or resolved the two systems into one, would have nothing to say, because the saying is the seam. The meaning is the making, and the making is shared: the transcribed phrase, the interlocking cycle, the synchronised phone, the named spider -- because the work is only honest about what it cost when the cost is on view, and only true when the ear can hear the join. [C29226]
