## M1. The fragment as the unit: a piece that lasts seconds is complete

The piece is built from movements lasting seconds to a minute or two, each fully formed, with no development in the classical sense, and the fragment is not a sketch for something longer, it is the finished thing. Wikipedia's summary is blunt: his primary forms of expression are fragments, bagatelles, moments, games and splinters. Tom Service, quoted in Wikipedia, puts it as a programme: Kurtag's music involved reducing music to the level of the fragment, the moment, with individual pieces or movements lasting mere seconds, or a minute, perhaps two. The Strad says the same from the other side: his music is built instead from fragments, silences and moments of extreme concentration. Grove, quoted in Wikipedia, names the result: work characterized by compression in scale and forces, and by a particular immediacy of expression. The evidence is everywhere in the catalogue. The String Quartet op. 1 of 1959 fragments in its opening measures. The 12 Microludes op. 13 of 1977-78 are twelve aphoristic movements, one per semitone of the chromatic scale, each a balanced sound object. Kafka-Fragmente op. 24 is about 55 minutes made of 40 short movements for soprano and violin, and one movement is a single word, and the movements last 13 seconds to six minutes. The piano piece \"Flowers We Are, Mere Flowers\" from Jatekok volume 8 consists of just seven notes. The mechanism is: make the unit so small that nothing in it can be filler, and then trust the listener to hold it whole. A tiny event appears, is fully present, and is gone, and the audience must listen at the scale of a breath.

**Takeaway:** do not write a long piece and cut it short; write a short piece and let it be whole, because a fragment that is complete needs no development.

## M2. Silence as material: the rest is the frame, not the gap

Rests and held silences carry structural weight equal to notes, and the music is, in Kurtag's own Jatekok preface, the quality of sound and silence. The preface says the written image must be taken extremely seriously as regards the musical process, the quality of sound and silence, and the arrangement in time of even the most free pieces. In Kafka-Fragmente, bow contact and silence carry structural weight, and the violinist and the singer stop and the stopping is the structure. In ...concertante... op. 42, the juxtaposition of motifs and silence highlights the fragmentary writing, and the silence between two motifs is what makes them two motifs instead of one. In Jatekok, the printed image governs the arrangement in time, so that a page with a few notes and much white space is a score in which the white space is scored. The mechanism is: treat the silence as a composed object with a duration, a weight and a shape, and the notes become events inside a field of stillness rather than a stream that occasionally pauses. The silence is the frame, not the gap, and the frame is what makes the picture visible.

**Takeaway:** compose the silence first and then place the notes inside it; a rest that is composed is louder than a note that is merely played.

## M3. Quotation and the stolen object: a familiar tune surfaces and dissolves

A phrase whose origin is audible is lifted into the piece, the \"stolen object\" in IRCAM's phrase, often as homage, and the quotation is recognised and then dissolved. The Hommage a Tchaikovsky in Jatekok parodies the opening of Tchaikovsky's First Piano Concerto, played with both palms laid side by side on the keyboard, so that the most famous piano opening in the repertoire arrives as a joke made with the flat of the hand. In Omaggio a Luigi Nono op. 16 of 1979, Rimma Dalos's lines, \"Love for months, suffering for years, this is how everything happened\", suggest to Kurtag a quotation from the opening of Wagner's Tristan und Isolde: an ascending sixth, a hold on the high note, a descent beginning with a semitone, so that the homage to Nono is delivered through Wagner, and the listener hears Tristan inside a tribute to a serialist. In Hommage a R. Sch. op. 15d of 1990, for clarinet, viola and piano, a genealogy is traced back through Schumann, and through the poet Attila Jozsef and the medieval composer Guillaume de Machaut, and back to Kurtag's own pieces, so that the quotation is a family tree with the composer at both ends. The mechanism is: steal something the audience already knows, put it inside an unfamiliar texture, let it be recognised for a second, and then let it dissolve before it can become a reference. A familiar tune surfaces inside an unfamiliar texture, is recognised, then dissolves.

**Takeaway:** quote what the audience already loves and then take it away before they can settle into it; recognition is a spark, not a shelter.

## M4. The found object: the hand before the instrument

The player's immediate, instinctive apprehension of the instrument comes first: palms, clusters, the whole body at the piano, hands doing things hands do to objects before they do things hands do to instruments. In Jatekok, large black dots in the score mean play with both palms laid side by side, and the piano is approached as a surface to touch, not as a machine with keys. Grabstein fur Stephan op. 15c sends slow ascending arpeggios up the six open strings of the guitar, the simplest thing a guitar can do, treated as a monument. The whole body is engaged: the Jatekok preface insists that playing is just playing, and that the performer should tackle bravely even the most difficult task without being afraid of making mistakes, and the pieces are pedagogical performance pieces that are also poetry of the short form. The mechanism is: let the hand find the sound before the mind names it, and the sound will carry the trace of the hand that found it. The palm on the keys, the open string, the cluster struck with the forearm, these are not effects, they are the instrument met as an object in the world.

**Takeaway:** let the hand meet the instrument as an object first and as a machine second; the sound of a hand finding something is different from the sound of a hand executing something.

## M5. The message, the homage, the dedication: every piece is a letter

Almost every piece is written for a specific person or circumstance, and the catalogue is a diary and a correspondence, and each fragment arrives addressed, like a letter. The later volumes of Jatekok, volumes V to VIII from 1995, are explicitly personal messages and diary entries. Signs, Games and Messages is a cycle of solo pieces, each a message to a player, and the title is the programme. Hommage a Andras Mihaly, the 12 Microludes op. 13, was written for Mihaly's 60th birthday, and Mihaly was a composer, conductor and trusted colleague who performed many early Kurtag works. Omaggio a Luigi Nono op. 16 is a message to a composer Kurtag had never met, sent in music. Stele op. 33 is in part a memorial for Mihaly, who died in 1993. Die Stechardin is dedicated to his late wife Marta, who died in October 2019, ending sixty years of performing together. The mechanism is: the dedication is not a label pasted on afterwards, it is the reason the piece exists in that shape, and the listener hears a named gift rather than an abstract form. The piece is a named gift, and the name is part of the music.

**Takeaway:** address the piece to someone, living or dead, and the fragment will have a reason to exist in exactly that length; a letter is never too short.

## M6. The game and the pedagogy: serious play, and the performer chooses

Music as serious play, and the performer experiments, chooses and composes the programme. Jatekok, \"Games\", was commissioned by the piano teacher Marianne Teoke for children, and the pieces are pedagogical performance pieces that are also poetry of the short form: exercises, dances, popular songs, Gregorian chants, memorial pieces, tributes. The preface is the doctrine: \"Playing is just playing... let us tackle bravely even the most difficult task without being afraid of making mistakes.\" The performers must CHOOSE pieces from the compilation of more than 300 pieces and compose a programme from them, and the Portraitkonzert of 10 August 1993 is the model, performers assembling fragments into an order. The whole body is engaged, and children play spontaneously, and the piano is a toy before it is an instrument. The mechanism is: the game is not a metaphor for the music, it is the method of the music, and the performer visibly explores, repeats a found harmony, chooses the next fragment, and the audience watches choosing happen. The choice is the performance.

**Takeaway:** make the performer choose, and the choosing becomes the piece; a game with no choice is a drill, and a piece with no choice is a recording.

## M7. Text as material, parlando-rubato: the voice speaks before it sings

Vocal writing is built on the syllabics and speech rhythms of the language set, Hungarian first, then Russian, French, English, German and ancient Greek, and the melody follows speech. The Sayings of Peter Bornemisza op. 7, 1963-68, sets sermons by a 14th-century Hungarian bishop, and the vocal technique is built on the syllabics of Hungarian, flowery to violent and vulgar, with parlando-rubato melody and tone painting, and the architecture is built from short recurring motifs with symbolic weight, the \"Virag az ember\", \"Man Is a Flower\", motif, later reused in Jatekok. Kafka-Fragmente sets German diary fragments, and the voice speaks Kafka's splinters in the rhythm of a man writing to himself. What is the Word op. 30b sets Beckett's last poem Comment dire in French, with its English and Hungarian translations side by side, and the voice moves through recitation, Sprechgesang, murmurs, shouts, declamation, laughter, parlando-rubato and stammering. The mechanism is: the line is a sentence before it is a melody, and the voice speaks, stammers, murmurs, shouts and laughs, and the music follows the mouth, not the other way round. Kurtag learned Russian to read Dostoevsky and then set Mandelstam, Tsvetaeva, Akhmatova, Yesenin, Lermontov and Blok, because the language had to be lived in before it could be set.

**Takeaway:** set the sentence, not the poem; if the voice cannot speak the line, it cannot sing it either.

## M8. Space as a parameter: the listener sits inside the piece

The ensemble is dispersed through the hall so that distance itself is composed. ...quasi una fantasia... op. 27/1, 1987-88, is the first piece in which Kurtag explores music that spatially embraces the audience: piano and timpani on stage, the rest of the players in small groups scattered through the hall, separated from each other as far as possible, and the title alludes to Beethoven's Piano Sonata op. 27/1. The Double Concerto op. 27/2, 1989-90, for piano, cello and two spatialised groups, does the same with two groups. Grabstein fur Stephan uses guitar and spatialised groups. The mechanism is: the hall stops being a container for the music and becomes an instrument in it, and sound arrives from far corners, and the listener sits inside the piece rather than in front of it. The distance between two groups is a composed interval, as real as the interval between two notes.

**Takeaway:** put the players far apart and the hall becomes a chord; distance is an interval, and it can be composed.

## M9. Recomposition and self-quotation: the same figure returns older

Material is returned to the workbench, transposed, rewritten, rescored, and pieces are product AND process, intended to be perpetually revisited and offered up as is, incomplete, in IRCAM's phrase. Op. 6 was revoked and its movements republished as Splinters op. 6c for cimbalom in 1973 and op. 6d for piano in 1978, so that the same material exists in two bodies. Stele's third movement elongates a piano piece written on the death of his teacher Andras Mihaly. Microlude no. 5, \"Scraps of a Colinda Melody\", which intercuts a remembered children's Christmas carol from his native Banat with held harmonics, is reprised in the last movement of ...quasi una fantasia.... Signs, Games and Messages transcribes older pieces with new elements added. The \"Virag az ember\" motif from The Sayings of Peter Bornemisza returns in Jatekok, twenty years later, in a child's game. The mechanism is: the same tiny figure returns in a new costume, older and changed, and the listener who knows the earlier version hears the time between the two. The catalogue is a workshop, not a shelf, and nothing in it is finished, because the composer keeps going back to it.

**Takeaway:** never throw a fragment away; return to it in ten years and it will have grown older with you, and the older version is a new piece.

## M10. The memorial and the epitaph: brevity is grief's form

Pieces are written for the dead or the lost, and brevity is grief's form. Officium breve in memoriam Andreae Szervanszky op. 28, 1988-89, for string quartet, is compact and densely layered, weaving homage, quotation and personal reflection, and it is among his most haunting and frequently performed string works. Stele op. 33 is in part an epitaph for Andras Mihaly, and a stele is literally a stone slab bearing an inscription, so the piece is a gravestone that sounds. Requiem for a Friend op. 26, 1982-87, and In Memoriam Gyorgy Zilcz of 1975, and Sinfonia breve per archi, Fried Marta emlekere, of 2004, do the same work in the same form: a fragment stands like a gravestone, and the brevity is the point, because grief does not develop, it stands. The mechanism is: the memorial does not narrate a life, it marks a place, and the fragment is the marker, and the silence around it is the cemetery. A fragment stands like a gravestone, and the inscription is the whole piece.

**Takeaway:** when someone dies, do not write a long piece; write a short one and let it stand, because a gravestone is not a biography.

## M11. The chromatic path: twelve climates in a row

A cycle travels through all twelve semitones, one per movement, as a hidden spine. The 12 Microludes op. 13 are twelve aphoristic movements, one per semitone of the chromatic scale, like Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier, and many movements use a simple undeveloped tripartite form, call, response, coda, and each is a balanced sound object. The aphoristic cycles generally carry the same spine: Kafka-Fragmente's four-part architecture, 19, 1, 12 and 8, is a visible jointing, and the twelve in the third part is not an accident of counting. The mechanism is: twelve distinct climates in a row, each complete, and the set adds up to a whole octave of character, and the listener feels the path even without knowing the map. The chromatic scale is not a system to be obeyed, it is a road to be walked, one semitone at a time, and each step is a different weather.

**Takeaway:** walk the whole octave one semitone at a time, and each step will be a different climate; the map is hidden, but the weather is felt.

## M12. Aphasia and relearning speech: the voice tries again

The voice enacts the loss and slow recovery of language. Samuel Beckett: What is the Word op. 30b, 1990-91, sets Beckett's last poem Comment dire, written in French in 1988, with its English and Hungarian translations, and it was written for the actress Ildiko Monyok, who was temporarily speech-deprived, and like Beckett, Monyok suffered from aphasia, and the work's progression is a difficult reintroduction to speech: recitation, Sprechgesang, murmurs, shouts, declamation, laughter, parlando-rubato, stammering, and the voice relearns language in front of the audience. ...pas a pas, nulle part... op. 36, 1993-98, sets Beckett poems for baritone, string trio and percussion, and the title, step by step, nowhere, is the same method in miniature. The mechanism is: a voice tries to say a word, fails, tries again, and the trying is the music, and the audience does not hear a song about aphasia, they hear aphasia itself, turned into form. The failure to speak is the subject, and the attempt to speak is the piece.

**Takeaway:** let the voice fail in public and then try again; the trying is the music, and a voice that relearns to speak is more moving than a voice that never lost it.

## M13. The cycle as architecture: a large shape made of small rooms

Many fragments are assembled into a large form whose joints are visible: parts, scenes, numbered movements. The Sayings of Peter Bornemisza is 40 minutes from sermon excerpts, and its architecture is built from short recurring motifs with symbolic weight. Kafka-Fragmente is four parts, asymmetrical, 19, 1, 12 and 8, and Part II is a single piece, \"Der wahre Weg\", \"The True Way\", standing alone like a room with one wall. Messages of the Late R.V. Troussova op. 17 is 21 songs as a monodrama, and the soprano Adrienne Csengery called it a monodrama, a single voice carrying a whole life. Fin de partie is scenes and monologues, and the opera is assembled from fragments in the same way the quartets are. The mechanism is: the audience feels a large shape made of small rooms, and the joints are visible, and the visibility is the honesty, because the composer does not pretend the fragments were always one thing. The form is an assembly, and the assembly is the form.

**Takeaway:** build the large form out of small rooms and leave the doors visible; a house that shows its joints is more honest than a house that hides them.

## M14. Large forces, miniature method: a huge ensemble that whispers

Orchestral scale is achieved without abandoning the fragment, and the orchestra is treated as chamber groupings and soloists. Stele op. 33, 1994, is for a Mahler-sized orchestra, six flutes, Wagner tubas, and it lasts about fourteen minutes in three brief movements unfolding without pause, and it is a sombre musical epitaph, in part an arrangement of existing pieces. ...concertante... op. 42, 2003, puts two solo strings, violin and viola, in dialogue with the orchestra, and the juxtaposition of motifs and silence highlights the fragmentary writing, and it won the 2006 Grawemeyer Award. Fin de partie uses an orchestra of 60 dominated by low instruments and tuned percussion, alto and bass flute, cor anglais, contrabassoon, tuba, celesta, a pianino permanently muted, piano, cimbalom, steel drum, bayan, and it is played in block-like sections for different combinations of instruments, typically treated as soloists or in small chamber groups. The mechanism is: a huge ensemble whispers, stammers and falls silent in turns, and the size of the orchestra is not used for volume, it is used for colour, and the fragment survives the scaling because the scaling is done by adding rooms, not by inflating the walls. The miniature is not a small piece, it is a method, and the method works at any size.

**Takeaway:** scale the forces, never the method; an orchestra that whispers in fragments is louder than an orchestra that shouts in paragraphs.

## Evolution

P1. Apprenticeship and withdrawal, 1946-1957. Budapest Liszt Academy, piano with Pal Kadosa, composition with Sandor Veress and Ferenc Farkas, chamber music with Leo Weiner, theory with Lajos Bardos, and he met Marta Kinsker, married 1947, and Gyorgy Ligeti at the entrance exam, a lifelong friendship. Bartokian early works, and most 1940s-50s pieces were withdrawn, so the surviving Suite for piano four hands of 1950-51 is a rare early trace, and the Movement for viola and orchestra of 1953-54, originally a Viola Concerto, survives with its final movement removed from the catalogue, the act of cutting already a compositional method. The viola concerto degree piece of 1955 won the Erkel Prize. The Hungarian uprising of 1956: he planned to emigrate like Ligeti but was hindered by circumstances, and he stayed.

P2. Paris reset and op. 1, 1957-1962. Paris 1957-58: Messiaen, Milhaud, Max Deutsch, Beckett's plays, Webern's scores, and a severe depression, and therapy with the art psychologist Marianne Stein, whose \"try to combine two sounds, just two sounds\" unlocked the fragmentary aesthetic. Cologne, spring 1958: Stockhausen's Gruppen, heard on tape from Stockhausen himself, having missed the premiere by months, and Ligeti's Artikulation, and Kurtag later said of Gruppen that the whole of twentieth-century music after 1950 comes from it. Return to Budapest, and the String Quartet op. 1 of 1959 as the declared new beginning, numbered op. 1 as a true starting point, with fragmentation in the opening measures, discreet serial technique, Webern chromaticism with Bartok rhythmic folk roots, and gesture and interval as the unit. Op. 2 to op. 5 follow in the same trenchant miniature language: the Wind Quintet op. 2, the Eight Pieces for piano op. 3, the Eight Duos for violin and cimbalom op. 4, Jelek, Signs, op. 5 for solo viola. Repetiteur at the Bartok Music School 1958-63 and the National Philharmonia 1960-68.

P3. The voice as laboratory, 1963-1980. The Sayings of Peter Bornemisza, 1963-68, premiered at Darmstadt at the end of the 1968 summer session, a 40-minute setting of sermons by a 14th-century Hungarian bishop, described as a concerto for voice and piano, cool critical response, overshadowed by Stockhausen's Musik fur ein Haus. Small vocal and chamber pieces follow, and Jatekok is begun in 1973, at Marianne Teoke's request for children's pieces during another mental-health crisis, and the game becomes the lifelong laboratory. S.K. Remembrance Noise op. 12, 1974-75, seven songs to poems by Dezso Tandori, and the 12 Microludes op. 13, 1977-78, premiered Friday 21 April 1978 at Witten by the Eder Quartet, and Omaggio a Luigi Nono op. 16 in 1979, the first half of the mutual homage. Professor of piano, later chamber music, at the Liszt Academy from 1967, students including Zoltan Kocsis and Andras Schiff.

P4. Breakthrough and the vocal decade, 1981-1989. Messages of the Late R.V. Troussova op. 17 premieres in Paris in January 1981, and Kurtag, then 55, receives definitive international recognition. A run of vocal aphorism cycles follows, written mainly for Adrienne Csengery: Scenes from a Novel op. 19, 1981-82, Attila Jozsef Fragments op. 20, 1981-82, Seven Songs op. 22, 1981, Requiem for a Friend op. 26, 1982-87, Kafka-Fragmente op. 24, 1985-86, premiered Saturday 25 April 1987 at Witten by Csengery and Andras Keller, and Three Ancient Inscriptions op. 25, 1986-87. Kafka-Fragmente is the summit, 40 movements in four parts. The spatial turn arrives with ...quasi una fantasia... op. 27/1, 1987-88, premiered October 1988 with Zoltan Kovac at the piano and Peter Eotvos conducting, and Officium breve op. 28, 1988-89, closes the decade. No large-scale pieces in the 1980s, only juxtaposed aphorisms, in IRCAM's phrase. Kurtag wrote no theoretical texts; his only written thoughts are the Laudatio for Ligeti, a few interviews, and three short score prefaces.

P5. Large forces, same method, 1990-2008. What is the Word op. 30b, 1990-91, the aphasia piece for Ildiko Monyok. Stele op. 33, 1994, premiered Wednesday 14 December 1994 in Berlin, Claudio Abbado conducting the Berlin Philharmonic, the first orchestral piece in forty years after the abandoned 24 Antiphonae op. 10 of 1970-71, of which only numbers 1 to 3 were composed. Messages op. 34, 1991-96, and New Messages op. 34a, 1998-2008, for orchestra. ...concertante... op. 42, 2003, Grawemeyer Award 2006. Hipartita op. 43, 2000-04, for solo violin. Composer in residence at the Vienna Konzerthaus 1995, Berlin Philharmonic residency 1993-95. Lived in the Netherlands 1996-98, Berlin 1998-99, Paris 1999-2001, Ensemble Intercontemporain, Cite de la Musique, Festival d'Automne. Co-compositions with his son Gyorgy Kurtag Jr.: Memoire de Laika for tape, 1990, and Zwiegesprach for string quartet and electronics, 1999-2006. Taught until 1993, see C15.

P6. The operas and the centenary, 2010-2026. Fin de partie, 2010-18, premiered Thursday 15 November 2018 at La Scala, Milan, conducted by Markus Stenz and directed by Pierre Audi, a co-production with Dutch National Opera, and Kurtag, 92 and frail, did not attend. Lived near Bordeaux 2002-15, back in Budapest 2015. Marta died in October 2019, ending sixty years of performing together. ## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Kurtag's work is that a fragment is enough, and the silence around it is part of it, and the hand that makes it is part of it, and the person it is addressed to is part of it. The fragment is not a piece that failed to grow, it is a piece that refused to grow, because growth would have diluted it, and a seven-note piece, \"Flowers We Are, Mere Flowers\", says what it has to say in seven notes and then stops, and the stopping is the composition. The silence is not an absence, it is the listener hearing the fragment settle, and the white space on the Jatekok page is scored as carefully as the notes, because the arrangement in time of even the most free pieces is the music. The hand is not a machine for producing notes, it is a body meeting an object, and the palm on the keys, the open string, the cluster, are the instrument met honestly, before virtuosity puts its varnish on. The quotation is not a reference, it is a stolen object, and Tristan inside a tribute to Nono, and Tchaikovsky under two palms, are familiar things made strange by being placed where they do not belong, and the strangeness is the point, because recognition followed by dissolution is a sharper feeling than recognition alone. The message is not a label, it is the form, and a piece written for a 60th birthday, or for a dead teacher, or for a wife of sixty years, has a length and a weight because it is addressed, and an addressed fragment is never too short. The voice is not an instrument that carries words, it is a mouth that speaks, stammers, murmurs, shouts and laughs, and the line follows the sentence, and the sentence follows the breath. The large form is not a different kind of music, it is the same fragments assembled with the joints showing, and the opera at 92 is the proof: sixty players, low instruments, tuned percussion, a pianino permanently muted, and still the music arrives in splinters, in block-like sections, in chamber groupings, because the miniature is a method, not a size. The whole oeuvre is one long education in enoughness, from the two sounds Marianne Stein asked him to combine in Paris, through the games for children, through the forty fragments of Kafka, through the voice relearning to speak, to the opera that is still made of splinters at a hundred years old, and the lesson is that a fragment, honestly made, addressed to someone, surrounded by composed silence, is a complete work, and a lifetime of such fragments is a complete life.

## Network

N1. Luigi Nono (1924-1990). Mutual homage, not friendship. Kurtag's Omaggio a Luigi Nono op. 16, 1979, for mixed voices, to poems by Anna Akhmatova and Rimma Dalos, predates Nono's Omaggio a Gyorgy Kurtag, 1983, revised 1986, for alto voice, flute, clarinet, tuba and live electronics, and no source read this leg documents a personal meeting, a correspondence or a friendship between the two men, so the study claims none. Nono was studied as #409, so he is NOT re-proposed.

N2. Gyorgy Ligeti (1923-2006). Lifelong friend since the Liszt Academy entrance exam, and both crossed into Hungary to study, and Ligeti fled after 1956 while Kurtag stayed. Ligeti's Artikulation, heard in Cologne in 1958, was a decisive influence, and Ligeti owned the few Western recordings Kurtag first heard. Kurtag's book of interviews includes \"Mementos of a Friendship: Gyorgy Kurtag on Gyorgy Ligeti\", and op. 48 Ligeti evszazada, \"Ligeti's Century\", 2023, is the late tribute. FUTURE CANDIDATE, top pick for #412.

N3. Peter Eotvos (1944-2024). Conductor of the spatial turn. Conducted the premiere of ...quasi una fantasia... in October 1988, Zoltan Kovac at the piano, and recorded Troussova, ...quasi una fantasia... and Scenes from a Novel with Ensemble Modern, Sony SK53290, 1993, and conducted the world premiere of the Akhmatova songs op. 41 at Zankel Hall, New York, 31 January 2009. Kurtag said of Gruppen that the whole of twentieth-century music after 1950 comes from it, and Eotvos is the conductor who carried Kurtag's spatial music into the hall. FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N4. Samuel Beckett (1906-1989). The playwright Kurtag spent thirty years setting. Kurtag discovered Beckett's plays in Paris in the late 1950s, and set Comment dire, Beckett's last poem, in What is the Word op. 30b, 1990-91, and set ...pas a pas, nulle part... op. 36, 1993-98, and made his first opera from Endgame, Fin de partie, 2018. Beckett already stands in the carried list from the Rauschenberg and Johns studies, and the Kurtag evidence strengthens the doorway, so he is NOT re-proposed as new here.

N5. Franz Kafka (1883-1924). The writer of the fragments. Kurtag became fascinated with Kafka in Paris, notably The Metamorphosis, and Kafka-Fragmente op. 24 sets fragments from Kafka's diaries, letters and posthumous manuscripts, selected by Kurtag himself, not excerpts from the published works. FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N6. Robert Schumann (1810-1856). The Romantic in the genealogy. Hommage a R. Sch. op. 15d, 1990, for clarinet, viola and piano, traces a genealogy back to Schumann, and to Jozsef, Machaut and Kurtag himself, and Patricia Kopatchinskaja compares Kafka-Fragmente to Schumann's Dichterliebe as a song cycle that refuses a coherent story. FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N7. Anton Webern (1883-1945). The miniature's ancestor. Kurtag's music is often compared to Webern's for the miniature, and he knew Webern's work inside out, in IRCAM's phrase, and he heard Boulez conduct the Second Viennese School in Paris, and Grove names Webern beside Bartok as the main influence. FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N8. Bela Bartok (1881-1945). The mother tongue. Kurtag arrived at the Liszt Academy in 1945, the year Bartok died in the United States, and Bartok is his mother tongue, in IRCAM's phrase, and the early Suite and the Viola Concerto theme are Bartokian in dynamics and tritone use, and Grove names Bartok first among influences. FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N9. Pierre Boulez (1925-2016). The discoverer. Boulez discovered The Sayings of Peter Bornemisza while assembling Ensemble Intercontemporain repertoire, and received the Troussova score from Kurtag, and Kurtag heard Boulez conduct the Second Viennese School in Paris, and Petite musique solennelle, En hommage a Pierre Boulez 90, 2015, for orchestra, is the late tribute. Boulez already stands as an alternate from #402 and #408, so he is NOT re-proposed as new here.

N10. Karlheinz Stockhausen (1928-2007). The shock of Gruppen. Kurtag heard Gruppen in Cologne in spring 1958, on tape, played by Stockhausen himself, and called it decisive, and Looking Back: Old and New for Four Players, Hommage a Stockhausen, 1993, is the tribute. Stockhausen already stands as an alternate from #402 and #408, so he is NOT re-proposed as new here.

N11. Olivier Messiaen (1908-1992). The Paris teacher. Kurtag studied with Messiaen, and Milhaud, at the Paris Conservatoire in 1957, and the encounter is listed in every biography as part of the Paris reset. Messiaen is NOT yet studied in this series, verified this leg, and he is a FUTURE CANDIDATE.

N12. Marta Kurtag (1927-2019, nee Kinsker). Wife since 1947, piano duet partner for sixty years. They performed Jatekok selections and Bach transcriptions together on one piano, and lived near Bordeaux 2002-15, then back in Budapest, and Die Stechardin is dedicated to her, in memoriam Marta Kurtag. She is not a study subject, she is the addressee of the work, and the study records her as the person the music was written for and with.
