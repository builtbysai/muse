# Ana Victoria Jimenez: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #346.

**Site:** Ana Victoria Jimenez, born Ana Victoria Jimenez Alvarez (Mexico City, Sunday 31 August 1941 - Mexico City, Sunday 23 November 2025), Mexican feminist photographer, editor, activist, and archivist of the second-wave feminist movement in Mexico. No high school diploma; studied graphic arts at the Sindicato de Pintores, Escultores y Artistas Graficos de Mexico; worked as a linotype typesetter and then at IBM. Co-founder of the Union Nacional de Mujeres Mexicanas (1964); member of Mujeres en Accion Solidaria, the Movimiento Nacional de Mujeres, the Coalicion de Mujeres Feministas, and the Frente Nacional por la Liberacion y los Derechos de las Mujeres; still photographer and researcher for the Cine-Mujer collective (1975-81); co-creator of Tlacuilas y Retrateras (1984). Her archive, the Archivo Ana Victoria Jimenez, donated in 2011 through the Proyecto Memora to the Biblioteca Francisco Xavier Clavigero of the Universidad Iberoamericana, was recognized by UNESCO as Memory of the World in 2024. She received a MUMA recognition for her trajectory in 2016. Works shown in Radical Women (Hammer Museum 2017, Brooklyn Museum 2018), Mexican Feminism in Protest (Wadham College, Oxford, 2019), Poeticas feministas (Museo de Arte Moderno, 2023-24), Ensayos y emergencias (Museo de Mujeres Artistas, 2025), and Huellas del cuidado (Museo de Arte Carrillo Gil, 2025).

**Doorway:** From Lara's notebooks, which cite Jimenez's Cuaderno de tareas and open with the hands that Rosa's article begins with, to Jimenez herself, the photographer who kept the movement's pictures so Mayer could archive them and Lara could draw from them. The bridge is literal and dated. In 1978, in the same season that Mayer hung eight hundred pink cards at the Museo de Arte Moderno and Lara hung ten framed collages in the same show, Jimenez was photographing Mercedes Maya's hands at work in a Mexico City kitchen, and the hands she photographed are the hands that open Rosa's article on Lara and Mayer: Rosa's Yulinda essay cites Cuaderno de tareas in its third footnote and begins with the hands, and Lara's notebooks begin with the same hands. Mayer's mechanism was the question that strangers answer; Lara's was the sentence written inside the drawing; Jimenez's is the photograph that keeps the evidence so the other two can be read at all. Mayer handed the sentence to strangers; Lara kept it for herself; Jimenez kept the pictures of both, and of everyone else, in boxes numbered 001 to 011 and beyond, so that forty years later the movement could be re-read from its own photographs. Where Mayer's line grows sideways because strangers keep pinning cards to it, and Lara's line grows in a spiral because she keeps returning to the same house, Jimenez's line grows by accumulation: one more march, one more poster, one more pair of hands, filed in order, until the filing itself is the work.

**Depth:** 10 sources read end to end: the English and Spanish Wikipedia articles on Jimenez; Andrea Ancira Garcia's La Escuela essay "Ana Victoria Jimenez: Don't You Hear the Hum of the Future?"; the Hammer Museum Radical Women page on Cuaderno de tareas; the Artishock report on Poeticas feministas; Yohanna Magdalene Roa's INES Magazina obituary and her Work planner review; the PUEDJS Revista Tlatelolco article on Jimenez and the visual memory of Mexican feminism; the Casa del Lago UNAM conference page "Gestos de las manos, gestos del archivo"; and the INBA press release on the Museo de Arte Carrillo Gil jornada Huellas del cuidado. Partial reads: the UAM master's thesis by Ekhine Graell Larreta (2018, abstract, table of contents, and chapter 4 case list only); the Archivo AVJ Inmujeres presentation (search snippets only); and the Spanish Wikipedia article on the Archivo Ana Victoria Jimenez (search snippets only). 41 works cataloged, spanning the Primer Congreso Femenil (1967) to the Cuaderno de tareas / Work planner book (2025), plus a selected publication list and exhibition list. 26 image files viewed first-hand, 24 distinct works photographed: the MNM Monumento a la Madre intervention of 1976, the Primer Congreso Femenil of 1967, the Camara de Diputados abortion protest of 1979, the 1991 abortion march, the FNLIDM banner of 10 May 1979, the three Miss Mexico protest frames of 1978, the Okiabeth Pride contingent of 1991, the two Red contra la Violencia frames of 1991, the "somos madres, y que mas?" poster of 1971, the Presas politicas poster of 1975, the 8 de marzo poster, the Okiabeth blue poster, the Coalicion circle dance of 1976-1979, the sewing-machine hands of Cuaderno de tareas, the 3x3 Cuaderno contact grid, the color Coalicion Mothers Day group of 10 May 1979, the Tlapa march, the 2025 portrait by Nina Hoechtl, the black-and-white portrait at the table with the Somos madres book, the Work planner spread, and the archive boxes 001-011. One file, the factory-worker photograph captioned Cuaderno de tareas in Artishock, is excluded as a Cuaderno image (see the study-method note). What was NOT accessible: no photograph yet viewed of the arboles raros series, of Columpio sobre el precipicio (1985), of Jimenez: 1985, of El juego de la sirena (1984), of La sirena (1984, mixed media), or of the Ensayo sobre todo trabajo domestico; no AWARE or MUAC artist page found; no full solo-show list; Rosa chillante (2004), Aceves Sepulveda's Women Made Visible (2019), and the Abelleyra/Nierika interview not read end to end; the Graell Larreta thesis close readings not read; the exact form of the 2016 MUMA recognition not stated in any source read; no pre-1971 photographs viewed. ## M1. The hand instead of the face

Cuaderno de tareas (1978-1981) is a photographic essay on domestic work in which the camera shows only the hands of Mercedes Maya (Meche), a colleague from the Universidad Nacional Autonoma de Mexico, and never her face. The Hammer text states the rule plainly: Jimenez recorded Maya's activities "focusing exclusively on her hands as she cooked, cleaned the bathroom, washed and folded clothes by hand, wrote on her typewriter, and used a sewing machine." The two Cuaderno frames viewed first-hand confirm the rule in opposite registers. The sewing-machine frame is a close crop of two hands at the needle: one hand feeds dark cloth under the presser foot, the other holds small scissors, the machine's steel plate and the cloth's weave filling the frame, no face anywhere, no room visible, only the work and the tool. The 3x3 contact grid shows nine hands at nine tasks: a hand lifting a pot lid on a stove; hands slicing on a plate among round fruit; hands wringing a cloth over a sink; a hand pressing white laundry; two hands spread on crumpled sheets; hands wringing a dark cloth; a hand scrubbing a toilet bowl with a brush; a hand rinsing a brush in a basin; hands washing under a pair of taps. Every frame is the same refusal: the worker is present only as the part of her that works. The mechanism is the crop as a political act. A face can be pitied, admired, or recognized; a hand can only be watched working, and watching it work is what makes the work visible. The hands are also old hands in several frames, veined and spotted, which is the second refusal: the work is not young, not pretty, not a symbol, it is a life spent doing the same tasks every day. The Hammer text gives the theory: the images "dignify and recognize the value of home labor... without recourse to rhetorical devices." No rhetoric means no face, no smile, no tear, no caption pleading for sympathy. The hand is the whole argument, and the argument is that the hand is already doing the work. **Takeaway:** the face is the rhetoric and the hand is the evidence. Watch the crop: when a photograph shows only hands at a task and no face at all, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's notebook page (#345 M3) was also a field where the evidence of a life is laid out, but her page holds childhood photos, red stars, and lipstick kisses; Jimenez's page holds only hands, which is why her version is a timesheet and Lara's is a report card.

## M2. The planner as a tool, not a book

Cuaderno de tareas was conceived as a planner, not as a book of photographs. The series was originally intended to become a weekly agenda for the Colectivo Atabal, a civil association founded in 1987 by feminist women to recognize and value housework, in which domestic workers could record their days, document their tasks, and reclaim their time. The book that finally appeared in 2025, Cuaderno de tareas / Work planner, keeps the tool logic in its physical form: open-thread binding, uncut pages, no cover, a ribbon bookmark, 160 x 225 mm, 192 pages, edition of 700, designed by Astrid Seme. The pages are sealed. To read it, the reader must insert a hand from below and separate the upper seams, pressing gently, touching the paper. The review states the mechanism directly: "To read it, one must insert a hand from below and separate the upper seams. One must press gently. One must touch." The sealed page is the work: the reader's hands must do to the book what Maya's hands did in the photographs, which is to open something closed and keep it open by hand. The planner has no fixed year; it can begin at any moment, and along its margins appear phrases and slogans drawn from the archive. The book does not administer productivity; it enables inscription. The mechanism is the object that refuses to be only read. A normal book opens when you open it; this book resists opening, and the resistance is the point, because the labor it documents also resisted being seen, and the reader must do a small piece of that labor to see it. The title page, viewed first-hand, states the program in two languages: "viajar en el tiempo. archivo. tiempo y espacio en los cuidados... manos que trabajan. practiconsejos. rutinas invisibles... Hacer visible lo invisible! Make the invisible visible!" **Takeaway:** the book is a tool and the tool must be worked by hand. Watch the seam: when a book will not open until a hand separates its pages, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's archive box (#344 M5) was also a container that had to be opened, but her box holds ten thousand texts and is opened by a researcher; Jimenez's planner holds one woman's week and is opened by a worker, which is why her version is a tool and Mayer's is a library.

## M3. The banner as a sentence

The banner is the sentence the march writes on cloth and carries through the street. The photographs viewed first-hand are full of banners that are also the only text in the frame, and the text is always a demand written by hand in large black letters. The FNLIDM banner of 10 May 1979 reads "MOVIMIENTO DE LIBERACION DE LA MUJER" in letters a meter tall, with a raised fist drawn inside the Venus symbol, carried by two women at the front of a column crossing a street, the bearers' legs visible under the cloth, their faces hidden behind it. The 1991 abortion march banner reads "DESPENALIZACION y LEGALIZACION DEL ABORTO / COORDINADORA FEMINISTA DEL D.F.", the words painted in uneven capitals, the banner sagging between its poles as the marchers walk. The Tlapa banner reads "LLEGO LA HORA DE UN DESPERTAR DE LAS MUJERES!" with a sun-like rosette of joined figures, carried through a dusty street by women in skirts and aprons, children walking beside them. The mechanism is the sentence made portable and collective. A banner is too big for one person to carry and too big to read at a glance, so it must be carried by several people and read by a crowd, and the carrying is the politics: the sentence only moves when several hands hold it up together. The banner is also the photograph's composition: Jimenez frames the march so the banner fills the frame and the marchers are reduced to legs and hands at its edges, which is the same crop as Cuaderno de tareas, applied to the street. The sentence is the subject; the people are its bearers. **Takeaway:** the banner is a sentence that only moves when several hands carry it. Watch the cloth: when a hand-painted sentence fills the frame and the marchers are only legs and hands at its edges, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's pink card (#344 M2) was also a sentence carried in public, but her card is small, pink, and pinned by one stranger; Jimenez's banner is large, white, and carried by many women, which is why her version is a column and Mayer's is a crowd.

## M4. The back of the protester

Jimenez photographs protesters from behind, and the back is the mechanism. The clearest case is the MNM intervention at the Monumento a la Madre in 1976, viewed first-hand: two women seen from behind, short dark hair, one in a light shirt with a sweat stain down her back, the other in dark clothes, both facing the monument, and the placard hanging from the first woman's shoulders fills the lower half of the frame, its hand-painted text reading "MOVIMIENTO NACIONAL DE MUJERES AC. CODIGO CIVIL ART. 382 PROTECCION AL MACHO IRRESPONSABLE". The women have no faces in the photograph. The placard is their face. The same shot recurs across the archive: the Miss Mexico protest frames of 1978 show placards from behind, the text facing the camera while the bearer faces away; the FNLIDM banner bearers are photographed from the front only because the banner hides their bodies. The mechanism is the refusal of the portrait. A protest photograph that shows faces invites the viewer to judge the women: are they angry, are they pretty, are they respectable. A photograph that shows backs invites the viewer to read the placard instead, and the placard is the only thing the protester wants read. The back also does the work of identification: the viewer stands behind the protester, in the march, looking where the protester looks, instead of standing in front of her, looking at her. The camera joins the march instead of observing it. **Takeaway:** the back is the invitation and the placard is the face. Watch the shoulders: when a photograph shows a protester from behind and the placard does the talking, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's window (#345 M2) also looked in instead of out, but her window frames a private room; Jimenez's back frames a public demand, which is why her version is a march and Lara's is a house.

## M5. The circle of joined hands

The Coalicion de Mujeres Feministas photograph of 1976-1979, viewed first-hand, shows women and a small child holding hands in a ring on a paved surface, shot from above, their long shadows stretching across the stones. The ring is not a march and not a meeting; it is a dance, or a game, or a ritual, and the photograph does not say which. The women wear long skirts and loose shirts; the child stands in the ring holding two women's hands; the hands are the joints of the circle, each hand holding the next. The mechanism is the hand as a link. In Cuaderno de tareas the hand works alone; here the hand holds another hand, and the holding is the work. The circle has no front and no back, no leader and no audience, and the high angle makes the ring the whole picture: the viewer looks down on a closed shape made of bodies, and the shape is the argument, that the movement is a circle that holds together because the hands hold. The shadows double the circle on the pavement, so the ring appears twice, once in bodies and once in shade, and the doubling is the proof that the circle is real enough to cast a shadow. The photograph is dated only as a span, 1976-1979, which is itself the mechanism: the circle is not an event with a date, it is a practice that happened across years. **Takeaway:** the hand is a link and the circle is the movement. Watch the ring: when bodies join hands in a closed circle and the shadows join them on the ground, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's clothesline (#344 M1) was also a line that held a collective together, but her line is a straight row of cards pinned by strangers; Jimenez's circle is a closed ring of hands held by women who know each other, which is why her version is a dance and Mayer's is a census.

## M6. The archive as self-defense

The Archivo Ana Victoria Jimenez is the work, and the work is the filing. Jimenez began collecting in 1964, keeping newspapers and leaflets from the groups she marched with, and only later began to analyze and catalog them; her own account is that the archive began because she liked to collect, then became a chronology, then a catalog. The archive holds, by the counts that must be kept separate, 4,250 documentary records, 250 posters, 300 pamphlets and programs, 100 copies of magazines and newspapers, 3,319 photographs, and 550 books, organized in six sections, and it took twenty-one years after her last photograph for a university to accept it. The photograph of the boxes, viewed first-hand, shows the mechanism in its finished form: white archive boxes on two shelves, each with a tan label reading "ACERVOS HISTORICOS / ARCHIVO ANA VICTORIA JIMENEZ / CAJA 001 / CONTENIDO Folleteria", document numbers printed at the bottom (4200-4260, 4261-4285, 4286-4314, 4314-4329, 4330-4358), the boxes numbered in order, the labels identical, the shelves full. The mechanism is the number. A photograph without a number can be lost; a document with a number can be found, cited, and defended. The numbering is the self-defense: institutions forget women artists, and the filing is the fight against forgetting, the same fight Mayer named for Pinto mi Raya, but Jimenez fought it alone, in her house, for decades, before any institution helped. She said she never intended to create an archive, which is the point: the archive was not a project, it was the habit of keeping, continued until the keeping became a collection too large to ignore. **Takeaway:** the number is the defense and the box is the work. Watch the label: when a plain white box carries a printed number and a list of document numbers, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's archive (#344 M5) was also a filing system exhibited as boxes, but hers was built with her husband as a public platform with a newspaper and a radio show; Jimenez's was built alone in her house for forty years before anyone asked to see it, which is why her version is a habit and Mayer's is a program.

## M7. The poster as a kept object

The archive does not only hold photographs; it holds the paper the movement printed on, and the keeping is the mechanism. Three posters viewed first-hand show the range. The "somos madres, y que mas?" poster of 1971 is a yellow sheet with a black line drawing of a braided woman holding a baby, the words "somos madres" at top left and "y que mas?" at bottom, bold black type on flat yellow, the drawing reduced to a few lines, the question doing the work. The Presas politicas poster of 1975 is a red mimeograph on cream paper, the words "1975 LIBERTAD Presas Politicas" in large red type, a dense typed text below naming Eva Forest and Ma. Luz Fernandez in Spain and listing twenty Mexican political prisoners by name, the red ink uneven, the paper creased, the text running to the edges. The 8 de marzo poster is a pink sheet with a black drawing of four women, one carrying firewood, one with a rifle, one grinding, one nursing, and the words "8 DE MARZO dia internacional de la mujer trabajadora / marcha 9 de marzo / mnto. a la madre al zocalo. 16 hrs." The mechanism is the cheapness kept. A poster is printed to be pasted on a wall and to rot in the rain; Jimenez took it off the wall, or took it before it was pasted, and filed it, so the throwaway object survives as the only record of the demand it carried. The poster is also the cheapest art in the archive: one color on cheap paper, drawn in a few lines, printed fast, and the cheapness is the argument, because a movement that cannot afford a painting can afford a poster, and the poster is what the movement actually looked like. **Takeaway:** the poster is the movement's painting, and the filing is what saves it. Watch the paper: when a one-color poster on cheap paper is kept flat in a box instead of pasted on a wall, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's posters (#342 M4) were also printed cheap and pasted in the street, but his posters were made to be seen and then replaced; Jimenez's posters were made to be kept and then filed, which is why her version is an archive and his version is a wall.

## M8. The still photograph inside the film

Jimenez's work for Cine-Mujer was still photography, and the still is a different mechanism from the film it serves. Cosas de mujeres (1975-78) and Rompiendo el silencio (1979), both directed by Rosa Martha Fernandez, were 16mm black-and-white films mixing interviews, fictional narrative, still photography, and statistics, about abortion restrictions and about raped women. Jimenez worked on them as still photographer and researcher, and the stills she made were then taken out of the films and used in the campaigns to decriminalize abortion, printed in newspapers and magazines and carried on placards. The mechanism is the photograph that leaves the film. A film still is a frame stopped in time, and stopping it is what lets it travel: a film must be screened in a room at a set hour, but a still can be printed in a magazine, pasted on a wall, filed in an archive, and shown to a deputy. The still is also the photographer's revenge on the film: the film moves and the viewer watches it pass, but the still stays, and staying is what makes it evidence. The same logic runs through her whole practice: the march passes, the photograph stays; the meeting ends, the poster stays; the performance happens once, the still stays. The still is the part of the moving image that refuses to move. **Takeaway:** the film passes and the still stays, and the staying is the evidence. Watch the frame: when a photograph taken for a film is printed in a magazine or carried on a placard instead, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's animation (#345, El ojo avizor 2001, No me acuerdo 2008) also stopped time in frames, but her frames were drawn by hand to remember a mother; Jimenez's frames were shot on a set to accuse a law, which is why her version is evidence and Lara's is memory.

## M9. The board game as a fate machine

El juego de la sirena. Tratando de romper el circulo sin fin (1984) is a board game, and the game is a machine for showing a fate. Jimenez made it for La fiesta de XV anos, the Tlacuilas y Retrateras performance at the Escuela Nacional de Artes Plasticas in August 1984, remaking Snakes and Ladders with her own photographs. The game showed the life of Mexican women as a path dictated by celebrations, traditional myths, and female archetypes including the mermaid and Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz, and by social issues including menstruation and violence against women. The mechanism is the path that cannot be escaped by playing well. In Snakes and Ladders the player climbs ladders and slides down snakes by luck, and the luck is the point: no skill changes the outcome, only the dice. Jimenez's version keeps that luck and changes the squares: the ladders are the celebrations and the myths, the snakes are the violence and the menstruation, and the player moves through a woman's life the way a woman moves through it, by turns, by chance, by rules she did not write. The title says the game is about trying to break the endless circle, and the trying is the point: the game cannot be won by breaking the circle, because the circle is the board, and the board is the society. The satire and irony Ancira names are the mechanism's second half: the game is funny to play and serious to lose, and the fun is what lets the seriousness be seen. **Takeaway:** the board is the fate and the dice are the luck, and the playing is the proof. Watch the path: when a game board shows a woman's life as squares she must land on by chance, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's board-game path (#345 M3, En medio de este estupido silencio) was also a path with circles for mama, papa, and yo, but her path was drawn in a notebook about a child; Jimenez's path is a game played by adults at a party, which is why her version is a fate and Lara's is a diary.

## M10. The photographic narrative that exceeds documentary

Columpio sobre el precipicio (1985) is a photographic narrative, not a documentary report, and the narrative is the mechanism. Jimenez was its author and editor, and the book is self-published, which matters: no editor, no institution, no funder decided what it would be. The same excess runs through Jimenez: 1985, her first photographic essay, in which a woman climbs up a tree to speak of her youth and her dreams of getting married, is beaten by her husband, has her children taken away, and goes mad. The story is told in photographs, but the photographs do not document an event; they stage a fate, the way the board game stages a fate. The mechanism is the photograph used as a sentence in a story instead of as a record of a fact. A documentary photograph says "this happened"; a narrative photograph says "this is what happens", and the difference is the tense: the documentary is past, the narrative is always. The swing over the precipice is the image of that tense: a swing moves back and forth over a drop, and the movement is the story, the going out and the coming back, the danger and the return. The book is also the proof that Jimenez's practice had two directions from the start: the camera that records the march, and the camera that invents the story, and the two directions share one hand. **Takeaway:** the photograph is a sentence in a story, not a record of a fact. Watch the swing: when a sequence of photographs tells a fate instead of reporting an event, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's artist's books (#345 M8, Lealtad 1980-81, La gigante 1987) were also narratives told in sequences, but hers were drawn and written by hand with a poet; Jimenez's were photographed and self-published alone, which is why her version is a precipice and Lara's is a leporello.

## M11. The tree that is a body

The strange tree is Jimenez's most private image, and it is also her most repeated. For many years, in different landscapes across Mexico, she photographed rare trees, different, twisted, with erotic and/or monstrous forms, and she worked the subject in prose and visual poetry as well as in photographs. No image of the series was viewed for this study, and the mechanism is therefore carried on text alone, which the study states plainly. The tree returns in her first photographic essay, Jimenez: 1985, where the woman climbs a tree to speak of her youth, and the climbing is the mechanism's second form: the tree is a place a woman can climb to speak, above the house, above the husband, above the ground where she is beaten. The mechanism is the body translated into a plant that cannot be married off, beaten, or silenced, because a tree does not marry and does not speak, it only grows in the shape the wind and the ground gave it. A twisted tree is a body that grew wrong by the standards of the straight tree, and the wrongness is the point: the erotic and monstrous forms are the forms a body takes when it is allowed to grow without being pruned into a shape. The tree is the opposite of the hand in Cuaderno de tareas: the hand works every day in the house, the tree grows for years outside it, and the two together are the whole practice, the daily and the wild. **Takeaway:** the tree is the body that grew without permission. Watch the trunk: when a twisted tree stands in a landscape and looks like a body, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's pruned tree (#345 M10) was also a body translated into a plant, but hers was pruned and identified with, a self-portrait that breathes fire; Jimenez's tree is unpruned and photographed from a distance, which is why her version is a witness and Lara's is a self.

## M12. The seamstress's testimony

After the earthquake of 19 September 1985, Jimenez worked with the seamstresses, and the testimony is the mechanism. The seamstresses were the women who sewed in the textile workshops of San Antonio Abad and the Colonia Obrera, in buildings designed as housing and converted into workshops, some clandestine, overloaded with rolls of cloth and machinery; the earthquake collapsed the buildings and killed the workers, and no official count was ever given. Jimenez was an active collaborator in the movement of the surviving seamstresses, and she co-authored two collective testimonies: "De entre las ruinas emerge la reconstruccion de las conciencias" (Problemas del Desarrollo, Nos. 62-63, 1986) and "La conciencia, puntada a puntada" (Nueva Sociedad, No. 93, January-February 1988). The mechanism is the stitch as a unit of testimony. A seamstress sews one stitch at a time, and the testimony is written the same way: one voice at a time, one memory at a time, stitched together into a collective text, and the stitching is the authorship, because no single seamstress wrote the testimony and no single editor wrote it either. The title says it plainly: consciousness, stitch by stitch. The photograph that belongs to this mechanism was not viewed for this study, and the study does not invent one; the mechanism rests on the two texts and on the archive's own description, which names the seamstresses who died in the earthquake as one of its substantial themes. **Takeaway:** the stitch is the sentence and the testimony is sewn, not written. Watch the seam: when a collective text is built one voice at a time like stitches in a garment, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's tapestry (#345 M12) was also a line turned into thread, but hers was woven by professionals from her drawing; Jimenez's stitch is sewn by the workers themselves about their own dead, which is why her version is a testimony and Lara's is a blanket.

## M13. Editing as feminist practice

Jimenez's first training was not in photography but in type, and the type is the mechanism. After studying graphic arts, she worked as a typesetter in a print shop, checking for spelling errors in linotype presses, and later at IBM on a portable machine. She then spent her working life as an editor: at the Instituto de Investigaciones Economicas of the UNAM in the 1980s to 1992, editing Problemas del Desarrollo and many books; from 1993 as an independent editor, designer, and adviser; editing the literacy primer LUPE. Cartilla de alfabetizacion para mujeres (1986 and 1996), Sembradoras de futuros (2000), La lucha por la reivindicacion de las empleadas del hogar (2002), and the Rosa chillante of Monica Mayer (2004). The mechanism is the correction as a political act. A typesetter checks spelling; an editor checks facts, cuts sentences, and decides what is printed and what is not, and the deciding is the power. Jimenez used that power for the movement: she edited the books that taught women to read, to know their labor rights, and to remember their own organizations, and she taught courses in graphic techniques to women in the urban popular movement so they could make their own publications. The photograph and the book are the same practice in her hands: both are ways of deciding what is kept and how it is shown. The darkroom she built at home, where she learned photolithography to make negatives for print, is the same room as the typesetting shop: a small room where one woman decides what the public will see. **Takeaway:** the correction is the politics and the editing is the authorship. Watch the proof: when a typesetter becomes the editor of the movement's books, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's Raya (#344 M14) was also a line drawn by an editor, but hers was a fortnightly compilation of art criticism; Jimenez's editing was literacy primers and labor-rights booklets, which is why her version is a primer and Mayer's is a review.

## M14. The photograph published without credit

Jimenez's photographs were published many times in newspapers and magazines without her name, and the missing credit is the mechanism. Her photographs of feminist demonstrations appeared in Fem, Interviu, Siempre, La Cultura en Mexico, In These Times, El Mexicano, Nosotras, and La Opinion, and the English Wikipedia states plainly that they "were many times featured in the newspapers and magazines, but were not given credit." The mechanism is the invisible authorship. A press photograph without a credit is a photograph that belongs to the event, not to the photographer, and the event is what the reader remembers; the photographer disappears into the movement, and the disappearing is both a loss and a choice. Jimenez herself said she decided to leave photography when she realized it was not her line of work, unlike editing, and yet she continued photographing until the 1990s, which is the same ambivalence in her own words: the camera was not her profession, it was her militancy, and militancy does not sign its name. The archive reverses the mechanism: every photograph in the boxes now carries her name, the Archivo Ana Victoria Jimenez, and the name on the box is the credit the newspapers never printed. The study does not resolve the ambivalence; it carries it as she stated it. **Takeaway:** the missing credit is the signature, and the box is the correction. Watch the byline: when a photograph of a march carries no photographer's name, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's signature (#345, the "89" and "86" on the gouaches) was also small and handwritten, but hers was always on the work; Jimenez's name was left off the work and put on the box instead, which is why her version is an archive and Lara's is a painting.

## M15. The delayed publication

Cuaderno de tareas waited more than forty years to be published, and the waiting is the mechanism. The photographs were made between 1978 and 1981; the planner they were intended for could not be published at the time, for lack of resources and lack of conditions; the book appeared only in 2024 as Las tareas de Mercedes (Miau Ediciones) and in 2025 as Cuaderno de tareas / Work planner (Tumbalacasa, Mark Pezinger Books, Academy of Fine Arts Vienna). Around 2018, Jimenez herself asked Nina Hoechtl to take up and edit the planner, and Hoechtl and Andrea Ancira returned to the archive to reactivate it as a question, not as a relic. Jimenez saw the final PDF before her death on 23 November 2025; she did not hold the printed proof. The mechanism is the delay as a form of survival. A book published in 1978 would have been a planner for domestic workers in 1978; a book published in 2025 is a question about who organizes time, who measures it, and who decides what counts as work, asked to a present in which housework is still largely informal, precarious, and without full rights. The delay is also the proof that the archive works: the photographs survived because they were filed, and the filing outlasted the conditions that prevented the book. The sealed pages of the 2025 book are the delay made physical: the reader must open what could not be opened for forty years, by hand. **Takeaway:** the delay is the survival, and the opening is the publication. Watch the date: when a work made in 1978 is published in 2025 and the author sees only the PDF, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's Lealtad alterado (#345 M16, 2021) also revisited a book forty years later, but hers was a clipping and pasting of her own old book by her own hand; Jimenez's book was finished by two younger editors after she asked them to, which is why her version is a handover and Lara's is a revision.

## M16. The archive that disagrees with itself

The archive keeps feminisms that disagree with each other, and the disagreement is the mechanism. Ancira states it directly: the archive embraces facets of the women's movement even where they posed internal disagreements, and it holds records of groups that were in favor of abortion, lesbianism, and sex work, and of those that were against; of groups that saw legal action as feasible and those that did not; of separatists and of those who proposed bridges with men; of those who saw art as a tool of transformation and those who saw it as a bourgeois apparatus. The photographs viewed first-hand show the disagreement in the street: the Okiabeth contingent of 1991 carries a pink banner for homosexual liberation and a red banner reading "LESBIANISMO ES ACTITUD ANTE LA VIDA" in the same Pride march where other groups carried other demands; the 1975 Presas politicas poster defends women prisoners in Spain and in Mexico in the same sheet; the Tlapa march carries a banner for a women's awakening in a town far from Mexico City. The mechanism is the filing without reconciling. An archive that only kept one feminism would be a manifesto; an archive that keeps all of them is a pedagogy, and Ancira calls it exactly that, an archival pedagogy: the lack of a common or universal object of indignation is not a failure but an indication of the capacity of feminist activism to become a movement. The boxes do not argue; they sit side by side on the shelf, numbered in order, and the order is the only agreement they need. **Takeaway:** the disagreement is the collection, and the shelf is the argument. Watch the boxes: when neighboring boxes hold groups that opposed each other and both are kept, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's Tendedero (#344 M13) was also a collective sentence made of many voices, but her voices all answer the same question; Jimenez's voices answer different questions and sometimes contradict each other, which is why her version is a shelf and Mayer's is a line.

## Aesthetic logic

Jimenez's aesthetic is the cheapest possible photograph, taken from inside the march, filed in order, aimed at the most patient possible target: the future reader. The materials are 35mm black-and-white film, hand-painted banners on cloth, one-color posters on cheap paper, mimeograph ink, and white archive boxes with printed labels: the materials of the print shop, the street, and the filing cabinet, and the cheapness is the argument, because a work made of a poster and a box cannot be owned by a collector and cannot be stopped by a museum. The palette is black and white first, then the red of the mimeograph and the placard, the pink of the 1991 placards and the Okiabeth banner, the yellow of the Somos madres poster, the blue of the Okiabeth poster, the orange of her fleece in the 2025 portrait: black and white are the first colors, and color arrives late, in 1991, when the movement's placards turn pink. The composition is the crop: the hand instead of the face, the back instead of the portrait, the banner instead of the marchers, the box instead of the collection. The motion is the march and the filing: the march moves through the street and the photograph stops it; the hand moves through the day and the photograph stops it; the archive grows by one more box and the growth is the motion. The logic is the keeping: the work is made day by day, march by march, and the keeping accumulates into a memory, and the memory is the work. The viewer is a reader of the future, opening a box numbered 001 and finding a poster from 1971, and the finding is the reading.
