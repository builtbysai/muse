## The life: Prague, flight, Sao Paulo, Robion

Vilem Flusser was born in Prague on 12 May 1920, into a family of Jewish intellectuals. His father Gustav studied mathematics and physics (Einstein among his professors), lectured at the German and Czech University of Prague, and sat in parliament as a Social Democrat; his mother Melitta was a singer. The family lived in the Dejvice district with his maternal grandparents and his sister Ludovika. In 1938 he began studying philosophy at the Juridical Faculty of Charles University. The Nazi occupation of Prague in March 1939 ended his formal education there; he never earned a college degree, a lack of credentials that followed him for the rest of his life and that he turned into a method: thinking from outside every institution that would have certified him. (C17608, leg 1 C17400, C17401, C17402; synthesis C17552)

In 1939 he emigrated with Edith Barth, his later wife, and her parents to London, where he studied one term at the London School of Economics. He lost all of his family in the German concentration camps: his father was murdered in Buchenwald in 1940, which he learned on reaching the port of Rio de Janeiro; his mother, grandparents and sister were killed in the camps, which he learned only after the war ended in 1945. He emigrated to Brazil in 1940, arriving at Rio de Janeiro and settling in Sao Paulo. He married Edith in 1941; their daughter Dinah was born in 1941, son Miguel Gustavo in 1943, son Victor in 1950. For twenty years he worked outside the university: at a Czech import/export company, then at Stabivolt, a manufacturer of radios and transistors, where he became director in 1959 and managed a factory for electronic transformers until 1961. The man who would write the philosophy of the apparatus spent his working life inside one. (C17609, leg 1 C17400, C17401, C17402; synthesis C17552)

His public intellectual life in Brazil began around 1960. He collaborated with the Brazilian Institute of Philosophy (IBF) from 1960 and published in the Revista Brasileira de Filosofia; his closest interlocutor was the philosopher Vicente Ferreira da Silva, until Ferreira da Silva's death in 1963. His first articles on linguistics and philosophy appeared in 1961 in the Suplemento Literario of O Estado de Sao Paulo. His first books, all published in Brazil: Lingua e realidade (Language and Reality), 1963; A historia do diabo (The History of the Devil), 1965; Da religiosidade, 1967. He taught communication theories at the University of Sao Paulo from 1963, held the chair at FAAP from 1964 to 1970, lectured at ITA in 1965, substituted for his friend Milton Vargas at the Escola Politecnica from 1967, and was appointed professor of philosophy of communication at the Escola Dramatica and the Escola Superior de Cinema in 1967. He was never accepted into the University of Sao Paulo's Philosophy Department, for lack of formal academic credentials. In 1964 he was nominated to the advisory board of the Sao Paulo Bienal and served briefly as its Technical Director; in 1971-1973 he worked as technical advisor on restructuring the XII Bienal (1973), proposing an "Art and Communication" nucleus. The 1964 military coup made lecturing and publishing progressively harder; the 1970 USP reform dismissed all Lecturers of Philosophy, and Flusser, who taught at the Engineering School, had to leave the university as well. (C17610, leg 1 C17400, C17401, C17402, C17406; synthesis C17552)

He left Brazil in 1972 -- the weight of sources favours 1972 over the 1971 given by Flusser Studies, which likely conflates the preparatory Biennale journey with the departure -- settling first in Meran, Italy (1972-75), then in the south of France (1975-81), then in Robion, Provence, from 1981, where he and Edith bought a house. In Europe he lived a nomadic lecturing life: Paris, Aix-en-Provence, Marseille, Arles, and the USA. In 1981 he met his later publisher Andreas Mueller-Pohle at a symposium on photography, and wrote the essay that became Towards a Philosophy of Photography, first published in Germany in 1983 -- his publishing breakthrough, translated into nine languages during his lifetime and 23 since. From 1986 he wrote the column "Curie's Children" in Artforum; in 1988 he got his own column, "Reflections", in European Photography. In summer 1991, shortly before his death, he gave a series of lectures as guest professor at Ruhr University Bochum at the invitation of Friedrich Kittler. On 26 November 1991 he gave the lecture "Paradigmenwechsel" (Change of Paradigm) at the Goethe-Institute in Prague -- his first return to his native city in over fifty years -- and the very next day, 27 November 1991, he died in a car accident near the German border. He was 71. He is buried at the Jewish cemetery in Prague. (C17611, leg 1 C17400, C17401, C17402, C17403, C17410; synthesis C17550, C17551, C17552, C17553)

Two corrections to the record, carried from the legs: the Archive version of the death trip is preferred over Wikipedia's "while trying to visit his native city, Prague, to give a lecture" -- the lecture had already happened and he was leaving after it. And Monoskop's summary line that the Flussers "lived in Rio de Janeiro (1950-72)" is an error against its own Archive-sourced timeline: the family moved to Rio in 1950 for his job and returned to Sao Paulo at the end of that year. (C17612, leg 1 C17402; synthesis C17553)


## The books: a trilogy and its satellites

The core of the work is the media-theory trilogy. **W01, Towards a Philosophy of Photography (1983; English 2000)** is the spine of this study: the hypothesis is that human culture has had two fundamental turning points, the invention of linear writing (mid-2nd millennium BC) and the invention of technical images, now under way. Its chapters run The Image, The Technical Image, The Apparatus, The Gesture of Photography, The Photograph, The Distribution of Photographs, The Reception of Photographs, The Photographic Universe, and Why a Philosophy of Photography Is Necessary, plus a Lexicon of Basic Concepts that defines the load-bearing terms: apparatus as "a plaything or game that simulates thought", functionary as "a person who plays with apparatus and acts as a function of apparatus", program as "a combination game with clear and distinct elements", information as "an improbable combination of elements", technical image as "a technological or mechanical image created by apparatus", photographer as "a person who attempts to place, within the image, information that is not predicted within the program of the camera". Grade A: large parts of the full text read directly this run. (C17613, leg 2 C17450, C17451, C17452; synthesis C17556)

**W02, "The Freedom of the Photographer"**, is not a book; it is the freedom thesis of W01's final chapter. The task of a philosophy of photography is "to question photographers about freedom, to probe their practice in pursuit of freedom". Four answers are given: one can outwit the camera's rigidity; smuggle human intentions into its program that are not predicted by it; force the camera to create the unpredictable, the improbable, the informative; show contempt for the camera and turn one's interest to information. "In short: Freedom is the strategy of making chance and necessity subordinate to human intention. Freedom is playing against the camera." And the philosophy of photography is necessary because "it is the only form of revolution left open to us." (C17614, leg 2 C17450, C17452; synthesis C17557)

**W03, Into the Universe of Technical Images (1985; English 2011)**, second book of the trilogy: twenty chapters, most titled with infinitive verbs -- "To Abstract", "To Imagine", "To Make Concrete", "To Touch", "To Envision", "To Signify", "To Interact", "To Scatter", "To Instruct", "To Discuss", "To Play", "To Create", "To Prepare", "To Decide", "To Govern", "To Shrink", "To Suffer", "To Celebrate", plus "Chamber Music" and a summary. It forecasts either "the first truly human, infinitely creative society in history or a society of unbearable, oppressive sameness, locked in a pattern it cannot change". Technical images are "visualisations" produced by machines processing raw data, not representations of reality. **W04, Does Writing Have a Future? (1987; English 2011)**, third book of the trilogy: "Writing, in the sense of placing letters and other marks one after another, appears to have little or no future." It was published in 1987 as a book and on floppy disk, an interactive program -- the book's own form performs its argument: writing speculating on its own replacement. Only two escape routes from writing are proposed: back to the image or forward to the codes, and the two can merge, since figures can be computed into images. (C17615, leg 2 C17454, C17455; synthesis C17556)

The satellites, each read at contents/review level, not end to end: **W05, The Shape of Things (1993 German; English 1999)**, 22 short essays -- design is giving form to formless chaos; "Design means, among other things, fate", and designing is "the collective attempt to seize hold of fate and, collectively, to shape it". **W06, Post-History (Brazil 1983; English 2013)**, twenty essays written as lectures: our times may be characterized by the term "program" as the seventeenth century was by "nature", the eighteenth by "reason", the nineteenth by "progress"; "Only malfunctioning programs and apparatus allow for freedom." **W07, Gestures (1991 German; English 2014)**, sixteen phenomenological descriptions -- gestures of writing, filming, videotaping, photographing, pipe smoking, shaving, telephoning, listening to music, planting, searching, doing; a gesture is "a movement of the body or of a tool attached to the body for which there is no satisfactory causal explanation", and gestures "are not just reflexes; they articulate a moment of freedom". The gesture of photographing: jumping between viewpoints, "categorial states", releasing the shutter. **W08, The Freedom of the Migrant (1994 German posthumous; English 2003)**: the "refugee" is stuck with the blanket on, the "emigrant" has the blanket pulled off; the first step of emigration, the blanket pulled off, is irony; emigres become free not by denying the lost homeland but by making networks out of their connections. **W09, History of the Devil (Sao Paulo 1965; English 2014)**: the devil studied through the seven deadly sins, reframing the evolution of the universe, from minerals to technology, as the Devil playing with time. **W10, Vampyroteuthis infernalis (1987; English 2012)**: the vampire squid as "a paradoxical absolute Otherness that is not external or ulterior, but which emanates from within us", the Devil inside us and inside our culture; illustrated in the original project by Louis Bec. **W11, What If? (1989; English 2022)**: twenty-two "scenarios of the future" whose opening note ("Wanted!") asks readers to recode the scenarios as video images -- called his only film script. **W12, Communicology (lectures 1970s; German 1996; English 2022)**: the most exhaustive compendium of his communication theory, addressing dialogue and discourse, texts and images, and the six communication structures -- theater, pyramid, tree, amphitheater, circle, net. His final lecture series, given at Bochum in summer 1991 shortly before his death, was intended as the definitive introduction to communicology; fragments appear as Thinking Further (2026). (C17616, leg 2 C17456, C17457, C17458, C17459, C17460, C17461, C17462, C17463, C17464; synthesis C17556)

Two pieces of infrastructure keep the work alive. **W19, Flusser Studies**, the international e-journal founded 2005 by Rainer Guldin, published twice a year, peer-reviewed, open access, in seven languages; by 2026 it has published 42 issues with 360 authors, and it also publishes previously unpublished Flusser texts. **W20, the Vilem Flusser Archive**, held since 2007 at the Berlin University of the Arts: about 2,500 manuscripts, publications and letters, plus video and audio files, photographs, drawings, watercolours, and his private library -- including the first hypertext he composed in 1989 at the Karlsruhe Nuclear Research Centre. The archive's own history is nomadic (first set up in The Hague in 1992, then Cologne, then Berlin), mirroring Flusser's exile. (C17617, leg 2 C17466, C17467; synthesis C17556)


## The concepts: technical image, apparatus, programme

Three concepts carry the whole edifice, and all three were read directly in the primary text this run. **W13, the technical image**: "The technical image is an image produced by apparatuses. As apparatuses themselves are the products of applied scientific texts, in the case of technical images one is dealing with the indirect products of scientific texts." Traditional images are abstractions of the first order (from the concrete world); technical images are abstractions of the third order: they abstract from texts which abstract from traditional images which abstract from the concrete world. The concept first appears in the 1979 essay "Our Images", read in full this run: "Technical images are essentially different from traditional images. Traditional images are produced by men and technical images by apparatus... Apparatus are black boxes that are programmed to devour symptoms of scenes and to spew out these symptoms in the form of images." The fraud at the centre of the concept: technical images "pretend that they are not symbolic like traditional images are. They pretend that they are symptomatic, 'objective'". Observers "do not believe them as they do their own eyes... they do not criticize them as images, but as ways of looking at the world". Decoding a technical image means reading off its actual status: it signifies concepts, not the world. (C17618, leg 2 C17450, C17468; synthesis C17556)

**W14, apparatus**: Latin apparare, "to prepare"; an apparatus is a thing that "lies in wait or in readiness for something... The photographic apparatus lies in wait for photography; it sharpens its teeth in readiness." Apparatuses are produced things whose intention is symbolic, unlike tools and machines; they encode, process, and communicate information rather than modify the natural world. The definition reached in W01: "a complex plaything, so complex that those playing with it are not able to get to the bottom of it; its game consists of combinations of the symbols contained within its program; at the same time this program was installed by a metaprogram and the game results in further programs". Apparatuses simulate thought processes: "thinking expressed in numbers. All apparatuses (not just computers) are calculating machines and in this sense 'artificial intelligences', the camera included." They range from gigantic (management apparatus) to microscopic (chips). (C17619, leg 2 C17450, C17451; synthesis C17556)

**W15, programme**: lexicon definition, "a combination game with clear and distinct elements". The camera's program is a finite but vast set of possible photographs; redundant photographs carry no information; with every informative photograph "the photographic program becomes poorer by one possibility while the photographic universe becomes richer by one realization". Programs nest: the camera's program sits inside the program of the photographic industry, inside the industrial complex, inside the socio-economic system, "and so on... every program requires a metaprogram by which it is programmed. The hierarchy of programs is open at the top." Ownership is therefore irrelevant; "the real issue here is who develops its program" and "Power has moved from the owner of objects to the programmer and the operator". Programs are also the new magic: prehistoric magic ritualizes myths, current magic ritualizes programs, whose authors ("functionaries") are inside the communication process. In "Our Images": "'Program' is 'prescription': writing is prior to it. It is post-historical magic, and history serves it as pretext." (C17620, leg 2 C17450, C17468; synthesis C17556)

**W16, dialogue vs discourse**: the social fabric relies on the balance, cooperation, and complementarity of dialogue and discourse. Dialogue occurs in an atmosphere of responsibility; its intention is to generate new information, or negentropy, by synthesizing available information. Discourse ensures information generated through dialogue is transmitted to an audience or storage devices; its function is to accumulate information. Dialogue has two types: circular dialogues (roundtables, parliaments) and networked dialogues (telephone system, public opinion). Discourse has four types: theatric (lectures, concerts), pyramidal (churches, states, armies, companies), tree (sciences, arts), and amphitheatric (radio, press). Pyramidal discourse is top-down, emphasizes obedience, allows neither questioning nor liberal paraphrasing, and "precludes dialogue"; it is "the vehicle and expression of authoritarianism and conservatism". Amphitheatric discourse shapes modern technical mass media: recipients "become stored information: they can only receive... This structure excludes any responsibility or 'revolution'" and is called "by far the best form of discourse... In other contexts, this perfection of communication is called totalitarianism". Dialogue is "the only way to cognition: information is produced in a dialogue, and passed on in a discourse". **W17, the camera as apparatus**: the camera is "a prototype of the apparatuses that have become so decisive for the present and the immediate future". It is a black box: input (feeding the camera) and output (photographs) are known and controlled, the interior is impenetrable: "The functionary controls the apparatus thanks to the control of its exterior (the input and output) and is controlled by it thanks to the impenetrability of its interior... Functionaries control a game over which they have no competence. The world of Kafka, in fact." Two interweaving programs run in the camera: one motivates it to take pictures, the other permits the photographer to play. What you pay for when buying a camera "is not so much the metal or the plastic but the program that makes the camera capable of creating images in the first place"; hardware gets cheaper, software more expensive. "Photographers have power over those who look at their photographs, they program their actions; and the camera has power over the photographers, it programs their acts." **W18, exile as method**: his philosophical autobiography Bodenlos (Groundless), written from 1973 and published posthumously 1992, organizes his thought around groundlessness: his entire oeuvre "mirrors such fragmentation: his biography, his books, and his essays are marked by incompletion and border crossings". Exile strips away the taken-for-granted ground, so the exile must think from outside every system, translate between languages, and build networks instead of roots. Flusser wrote most of his work in German and Brazilian Portuguese, also in English and French, and rewrote the same ideas across languages, so translation itself becomes a thinking method. (C17621, leg 2 C17450, C17453, C17457, C17460, C17465; synthesis C17556)


## The mechanisms

### M1 -- The black box: control the outside, never see the inside

Mechanically: an apparatus is a black box. The operator knows the input (what is fed in) and the output (what comes out) and controls both, but the interior that converts one to the other is impenetrable -- and the operator is controlled by it in return. "The functionary controls the apparatus thanks to the control of its exterior (the input and output) and is controlled by it thanks to the impenetrability of its interior." The camera is the prototype: two interweaving programs run inside it, one motivating it to take pictures, the other permitting the photographer to play. The evidencing works are W14 and W17. (C17622, leg 2 C17450; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: a device on screen with visible IN and OUT slots and an opaque middle. The viewer feeds things in -- gestures, images, words -- and watches transformed things come out, but the middle never opens. The drama is the gap between what went in and what came out. The viewer keeps feeding it to test the gap.

**Takeaway:** show the input and the output and refuse to show the middle. (C17623)

### M2 -- Exhausting the programme: every new image spends one possibility

Mechanically: a programme is a finite but vast set of combinations. Every informative image realizes one possibility and the programme becomes poorer by exactly one; redundant images carry no information and do not count. Photographers "endeavour to exhaust the photographic program by realizing all their possibilities" but "the possibilities contained within it have to transcend the ability of the functionary to exhaust them". Programs nest inside metaprograms, and the hierarchy is open at the top. The evidencing works are W15 and W01. (C17624, leg 2 C17450; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: a visible counter of remaining possibilities that ticks down with each new image, and a field that visibly empties or fills as combinations are used up. Repetition produces grey, dead duplicates; only a combination never used before lights up. The piece ends, or changes state, when the counter nears exhaustion.

**Takeaway:** make the programme finite and let the viewer watch it run out. (C17625)

### M3 -- Playing against the apparatus: the four freedom moves

Mechanically: freedom inside a programmed world has exactly four moves, from the final chapter of W01: (1) outwit the camera's rigidity; (2) smuggle in human intentions the programme did not predict; (3) force the apparatus to create the unpredictable, the improbable, the informative; (4) show contempt for the apparatus and turn to information itself. "Freedom is playing against the camera." W06 adds the hinge: "Only malfunctioning programs and apparatus allow for freedom." The evidencing works are W02, W01 and W06. (C17626, leg 2 C17450, C17458; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: a machine runs its smooth routine; the human player interrupts it four ways in sequence -- a dodge, a smuggled input, a forced glitch that yields something the machine never foresaw, then a walk-away that abandons the machine's output entirely. Each move is legible as a distinct gesture against the same machine.

**Takeaway:** freedom is not escaping the machine; it is playing against it, four moves in order. (C17627)

### M4 -- The hunt: stalking, hesitation points, decision points

Mechanically: the gesture of photographing is a hunt. Photographer and camera merge into one indivisible function, stalking through a jungle of cultural objects, each of which obscures the prey. The act has "a quantum [structure]: a doubt made up of points of hesitation and points of decision-making". The photographer leaps between viewpoints, which Flusser calls categorial states, then releases the shutter. The evidencing works are W17 and W07. (C17628, leg 2 C17450, C17451, C17459; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: a figure moves through a cluttered field in stops and starts, freezing at hesitation points, then leaping to a new viewpoint. The camera's viewfinder jumps with it. The shutter fires only at the decision point, after the stalk. The audience watches the hesitation, not the shot.

**Takeaway:** the hunt is the work; the photograph is only the receipt. (C17629)

### M5 -- Scanning vs reading: the circular gaze against the line

Mechanically: an image is a significant surface; the gaze wanders over it in a complex path, can return to an element already seen, so "before" can become "after" -- the time of the image is eternal recurrence, the space is mutual significance. A text is a line; reading follows it in one direction, producing linear, historical time. Technical images displace texts and replace historical consciousness with second-order magical consciousness. The evidencing works are W01, W13 and W04. (C17630, leg 2 C17450, C17454; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: two modes on one screen. In line mode, elements appear one after another and cannot be revisited; meaning accumulates forward. In surface mode, the eye (or a cursor, or a spotlight) wanders, revisits, and the same elements recombine into new meanings each pass. The piece lets the viewer feel the switch between the two times.

**Takeaway:** a line can only be read forward; a surface can be wandered forever. (C17631)

### M6 -- The window fraud: a symbol pretending to be a symptom

Mechanically: a technical image looks like a window onto the world, a symptom causally linked to what it shows, like a fingerprint. In fact it is a symbol: apparatus transcode symptoms into images in function of texts (chemical and optical equations, programmes), so the image signifies concepts, not the world. "Technical images pretend that they are not symbolic like traditional images are. They pretend that they are symptomatic, 'objective.'" Observers do not criticize them as images, but as ways of looking at the world. The evidencing works are W13 and W01. (C17632, leg 2 C17450, C17468; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: an image that first reads as a transparent window -- a street, a face, a room -- then peels: the same image re-renders as code, as a grid of programme choices, as a text of equations. The window cracks open to show it was a surface all along. The viewer watches their own trust in the image break and reform.

**Takeaway:** the window is a lie the apparatus tells well; peel it and show the code. (C17633)

### M7 -- Distribution as feedback: the image programmes its viewers

Mechanically: photographs are not only made, they are distributed through channels, and distribution is an encoding act, not a mechanical one. The camera's programme uses society as a feedback mechanism: photographs programme their receivers to act in ways that feed back into improving the camera. "Photographers have power over those who look at their photographs, they program their actions; and the camera has power over the photographers, it programs their acts." The evidencing works are W01 and W17. (C17634, leg 2 C17450, C17451; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: images leave the maker and travel through channels; each viewer who receives one changes slightly -- posture, gesture, next action -- and that change flows back and visibly reprogrammes the machine that made the image. A loop with a visible return arrow: output becomes input. The audience sees itself inside the loop.

**Takeaway:** the image does not end at the viewer; the viewer is the return path. (C17635)

### M8 -- Dialogue makes, discourse transmits: circle, net, pyramid, tree, theatre, amphitheatre

Mechanically: dialogue generates new information (negentropy) by synthesizing available information; discourse transmits and accumulates it. Dialogue is circular (roundtable, parliament) or networked (telephone, public opinion). Discourse is theatric, pyramidal, tree-shaped, or amphitheatric. The pyramid allows no questioning and "precludes dialogue"; the amphitheatre (mass media) lets receivers only receive, which "excludes any responsibility or 'revolution'". A healthy society needs both, in balance. The evidencing works are W16, W12 and W03. (C17636, leg 2 C17457, C17465; synthesis C17558)

Watchable behaviour: six shapes of communication staged as six rooms or six flows. In the circle and the net, messages bounce, combine, and come back changed -- new information visibly appears. In the pyramid, tree, theatre, and amphitheatre, messages travel one way and arrive unchanged, and the receivers sit still. The piece lets the viewer walk from a one-way room into a circle and feel the difference.

**Takeaway:** one-way rooms transmit; only the circle and the net make anything new. (C17637)

Two reserves, solid A/B and held back to keep the prompt set at eight: M9, the telematic net (W03, W06, W16) -- fingertip nodes improvising like chamber music, then the same net closing into a repeating command loop; M10, exile as method (W18, W08) -- ground removed, the figure builds networks and multi-language translations instead of roots. (C17638, leg 2 C17458, C17460; synthesis C17559)


## The aesthetic logic: a philosophy that refuses the picture

Flusser's aesthetic logic is a refusal, and the refusal is the point. He does not make images; he explains why images cannot be trusted, and why that mistrust is the only honest starting position for anyone who does make them. The logic runs in four moves, and every piece built from this study should keep all four in view.

First, the surface is never innocent. A traditional image is a symbol and knows it; a technical image is a symbol that pretends to be a symptom, an objective trace of the world. The pretence is what makes it powerful and what makes it dangerous: the viewer stops criticizing the image and starts criticizing the world through it. Any piece that uses Flusser must at some point break its own window -- show the code behind the picture, the programme behind the photograph -- or it has learned nothing from him and is only decorating his vocabulary.

Second, the machine is the subject, not the tool. The camera does not serve the photographer; the photographer serves the camera's programme, and the camera serves the programme of the photographic industry, which serves the socio-economic system, and so on up the open hierarchy of metaprograms. A piece built on Flusser therefore stages the apparatus as a character with intentions -- it lies in wait, it sharpens its teeth, it programmes its operator -- and the human in the piece is the functionary, playing a game whose rules were written elsewhere. The drama is never "what will the human make" but "what will the programme allow, and what happens when the human plays against it".

Third, freedom is small, local, and procedural. It is not escape and not revolution in the grand sense; it is four moves against one machine -- outwit, smuggle, force, walk away -- and it only works when the programme malfunctions. This is why Flusser's pieces should feel like games with visible rules rather than like expressive outpourings: the viewer must be able to see the rule, see the move against the rule, and see the machine absorb or fail to absorb the move. A piece that offers "creative freedom" without a visible programme to play against has missed him entirely.

Fourth, the ground is gone and the network is what remains. Exile is not a biographical accident in Flusser; it is the method. The refugee keeps the blanket on; the emigrant has it pulled off, and the first step is irony. Without ground, the exile translates between languages, thinks from outside every system, and builds networks of connections instead of roots. The aesthetic consequence is that a Flusser piece should never rest on a foundation -- no single home, no single language, no single viewpoint. It should be built from links, translations, and returns: images that travel, viewers who change, machines that get reprogrammed by what they made. The telematic net is his utopia and his warning at once: chamber music or command loop, the same net, and the piece should be able to show both fates. (C17639)

## The network: the mentor, the circle, the collaborators

The doorway tie is Mario Ramiro: mentee, correspondent, recommendee, verified via Osthoff's Flusser Studies 08 article, read end to end -- the meeting in Sao Paulo in the late 1970s via Gabriel Borba, the correspondence, the DAAD recommendations by Zanini and Flusser, and the 1988 studio visit in which Flusser convinced Gottfried Jager to accept Ramiro. Gabriel Borba was Flusser's assistant on the early-1970s Bienal reformulation project and Ramiro's teacher; he brought the philosopher to talk with young students. Walter Zanini was head of the Art department at ECA-USP during Ramiro's university years and, with Flusser, one of the two DAAD recommenders. Gottfried Jager, the German professor, lectured with Flusser in Sao Paulo in 1988, visited Ramiro's studio, and accepted him as a student at Flusser's urging, which led to the DAAD grant. No birth years were verified for Borba, Zanini or Jager this run; do not print any. (C17640, leg 3 C17501, C17502, C17503; synthesis C17562, C17563)

Eduardo Kac is a fellow member of the small 1980s Brazilian generation of technology artists interested in Flusser's transdisciplinary thinking, and Ramiro's collaborator on the 1988 fax/TV event Retrato Suposto -- Rosto Roto. Guard: no direct personal Kac-Flusser meeting, correspondence, or collaboration is documented in any source read this run; the tie is shared-circle only. Do not write that Kac met Flusser or studied with him. Kac remains the carried alternate for a future study; his birth year was not verified this run. (C17641, leg 3 C17504; synthesis C17564)

Haroldo de Campos is a concrete-poetry dialogue: Flusser translated a fragment of Galaxias for the experimental journal rot, and the Noigandres group is named as most important for him. Three adjacent figures carry the network forward. Louis Bec, born in Algeria in 1936, is Flusser's closest friend and only co-author: a collaboration of more than fifteen years, ritual Saturday-afternoon conversations, a trip to Prague in 1986, and the co-authored Vampyroteuthis infernalis (1987). Guard: Bec is a zoosystematician and bio-art theorist, not a visual artist -- the same mechanism-set problem as Flusser himself; his death year 2018 rests on a summary source (CCCB) and was not verified end to end. Fred Forest is the collaborator of twenty years: Flusser met him in 1972, invited him to the 12th Sao Paulo Biennale, filmed his gesture lectures on video at Fontevrault, and wrote about twenty texts on his actions over twenty years; joint video works include "Les gestes du professeur". Guard: Forest's death date, 4 November 2025, rests on an obituary search summary only and was not verified end to end; do not print it as confirmed, and his birth year was not verified this run. Mira Schendel is the best practice fit -- a real visual artist, an extended 1960s dialogue partner, face-to-face conversations on the terrace of the Flussers' Sao Paulo apartment between the 1950s and early 1970s, and the subject of Flusser's Bodenlos essay "Mira Schendel". Guard: no joint work, and the mutual-influence claim is explicitly "cannot be definitively proven" per Tate author Roth; her dates 1919-1988 rest on search-summary sources only. Further evidenced ties, named not developed: Max Bense, an early and important model; Vicente Ferreira da Silva, his closest interlocutor until 1963; Samson Flexor, artist friend; Andreas Mueller-Pohle, publisher, met 1981; Friedrich Kittler, who invited him to Bochum in 1991. (C17643, leg 3 C17505, C17506, C17507, C17509; synthesis C17566, C17567, C17568)

The closest Flusser comes to a visual-art collaboration is the 1973 XII Bienal: as technical advisor he proposed restructuring it as a networked "laboratory" for art and communication rather than a platform for nations, and invited Fred Forest and Eric McLuhan as guest artists. Forest's 1972 video series Gestures, four of which feature Flusser himself, is practically the index of the book Gestures (W07). That is documented, not speculative, and it is the closest this study gets to Flusser inside a work of art -- on camera, performing his own theory, filmed by a friend. (C17644, leg 2 C17459, C17469; synthesis C17572)
