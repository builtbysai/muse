# David Klocker Ehrenstrahl: Study Notes

**Site:** David Klocker Ehrenstrahl (23 September 1628, Hamburg -- 23 October 1698, Stockholm), Swedish court painter; the Hamburg tailor's son who arrived in Stockholm as a chancery scribe, learned to paint in weeks in Osnabruck and in consultation with a Dutch animal painter, went to Italy and came back Baroque, and spent thirty-seven years painting the Swedish kings -- as Apollo, as Roman emperors on horseback, as a family in one room, and finally as allegories on ceilings so large they had to be painted on canvas and hoisted up -- becoming the father of Swedish painting, the painter who wrote his own key to his own pictures, and ending as court intendant, carried to his painting room in his last two years.

## The life

David Klocker was born in Hamburg, son of Johann Klocker, a tailor; his mother is said to have been named Otman (SBL hedges the name -- see C1516). Fifth of nine siblings. The birth date is the first of the study's unresolved disputes: 23 September 1628 (English Wikipedia, Alchetron, French/Indonesian/Norwegian Wikipedia), 25 April 1628 (Swedish Wikipedia infobox), baptized 27 April 1628 at St. Nikolai, Hamburg (SBL, citing the St. Nikolai baptismal register; Swedish Wikipedia body agrees), 23 August 1629 (NiceArtGallery), 1629 (Nationalmuseum 1942 catalogue; Treccani) -- and Wikidata lists three at once (see C1498/C1539). The baptismal record (27 April 1628) is the strongest documentary anchor, and a birth a few days before baptism fits 25 April better than 23 September; the study uses 23 September 1628 in its header because that is the standard encyclopaedic date and the date the chain has carried since #584, and flags it. The ennoblement letter's claim that he belonged to a German noble family is unproven (SBL). He spelled his own surname variably, Kloker / Klöcker.

He reached Sweden as a scribe, not a painter. In 1646-48 he was a scribe (skrivare) at the Swedish peace chancellery in Osnabruck during the negotiations before the Peace of Westphalia. There he received a few weeks' painting lessons from "an insignificant teacher" and showed such aptitude that he soon produced fairly good portraits; this gave him the courage to devote himself entirely to art (SBL). This is the first Swedish contact in his life. After the peace (1648) he continued his training in the Netherlands, 1648-50, "in consultation with" his countryman, the portrait and animal painter Juriaen Jacobsz (c. 1624-1685), whom SBL calls only "a mediocre ability". SBL's judgement: his greatest gain from the Netherlands was the widened experience of seeing the great masters' works -- not Dutch technique as such. His earliest work, Wrangel's playing children (1651), "shows no lessons from the extraordinarily accomplished Dutch painting technique" but is "an engaging depiction in German spirit" (SBL). Where he studied under Jacobsz is disputed -- Amsterdam (English Wikipedia; SBL; Finnish Wikipedia) versus Hamburg (Swedish Wikipedia) -- and the RKD itinerary for Jacobsz (Antwerp 1652-58, Amsterdam only 1659-64) does not fit a 1648 Amsterdam pupilage at all (see C1501/C1538). The teaching link is attested twice over; the place and year are not. Do not smooth this over: the study says "in consultation with Jacobsz, 1648-50" and stops there.

In 1651 he was employed by Carl Gustaf Wrangel in Wolgast. Joachim von Sandrart, in his *Academie der Bau-, Bild- und Mahlerey-Kunste* (1675), says a "high lord" had seen some of Ehrenstrahl's works and summoned him from Holland; SBL identifies that lord as Wrangel, then governor-general of Pomerania, and notes Ehrenstrahl is called "Field Marshal Wrangel's portraitist" directly in 1652. Early works in Wrangel's service: portrait of Wrangel's playing children, 1651 (Skokloster); equestrian portrait of Wrangel himself, dated 1652 (Skokloster), "one of his most meritorious works"; three pictures of Wrangel's court fool Henrik (Hinric) Hasenberger, 1651-52 (two at Skokloster, one dated 1652 in the Nationalmuseum, NM 1633); portraits of officers and officials in Wolgast. He arrived in Sweden in October 1652, almost certainly travelling with Wrangel (SBL) -- the year is disputed three ways (1651, October 1652, about 1650; see C1500), and SBL's dated account is the strongest. About 50 of his paintings are at Skokloster Castle. In Dowager Queen Maria Eleonora's service the same year (1652), still there in January 1654, at 300 daler silvermynt a year plus travel money (SBL). What he painted for her is not known.

The study journey: Italy 1654-58, then France and England (SBL) -- the dates disputed (1654-61 in the English Wikipedia and NiceArtGallery; begun 1655 in the Swedish Wikipedia; "in Venice and also in Rome until 1660" in the ASP paper; see C1502). Venice by spring 1654: his sketchbook (now with his descendants, the Braunerhielm family at Sandemar) contains drawings of Venetian buildings -- San Marco, the Procurators' Palace facade toward the Piazzetta, the nearly completed Santa Maria della Salute, the Rialto Bridge. End of December 1654: Erik Dahlbergh arrived in Venice as governor for the two young barons Marten and Henrik Cronstierna. No direct record of a meeting survives, but closely matching drawings in Ehrenstrahl's album and Dahlbergh's Venice sketches (Royal Library) show they certainly met; they probably began preparatory studies for a joint topographical work. Dahlbergh went on to Rome via Ferrara, Bologna, Ravenna (arrived March 1656); Ehrenstrahl probably travelled with him. Their collaboration continued partly in Rome; Dahlbergh left in June. Their friendship lasted for life, sustained by a lively correspondence (67 letters 1658-97 survive in the National Archives). In Rome he painted a portrait of his friend Dahlbergh and a portrait of Cardinal Landgrave Frederick of Hesse, who gave him a garden pavilion (lusthus) on Monte Cavallo as a studio -- a single room "adorned all around with statues", from whose door or window he could sit and paint "the most beautiful garden views after nature". The cardinal gave him access to ceremonies in St Peter's and seems to have been his patron. Ehrenstrahl's own words, quoted by SBL: "I would wish I could take my painting-room with me when I travel, for I am sure I shall never in my whole life get one so beautiful." His Roman sketchbook holds the temples of Faustina and Vespasian, the Arch of Constantine, Colosseum motifs, Ponte Rotto, the Pyramid of Cestius, and Tivoli (Temple of Vesta). Pietro da Cortona was "without doubt" important to him as a model -- he admired the bold frescoes in S. Maria della Vallicella and the ceiling paintings in Palazzo Barberini, built on mass effect and effective distribution of light and dark parts -- but the claim that he was Cortona's pupil has not been confirmed (SBL; see C1509). The ASP paper adds that he visited Palazzo Barberini and was probably familiar with Andrea Sacchi's *Divina Providentia* ceiling (c. 1630), a likely model for the Drottningholm ceiling. Late September 1658: he began preparing to leave Rome; asked Dahlbergh to petition Karl X Gustav for a passport "as if he were the king's painter"; the king's reply also urged him to come home and become his court portraitist. Short of travel money, he stayed the summer, probably left Rome in late autumn. Route: Florence and Livorno to Marseille; planned to send boxes of paintings and art objects via Paris to a brother in Hamburg. April 1659: recorded in the Swedish congregation's register in Paris. February 1660: bailed the young Henrik Cronstierna out of debtors' prison for 900 riksdaler. He did not thrive in Paris, but tangled business and illness kept him there; he had commissions to execute. Charles Le Brun, "Louis XIV's dictator of taste", made a strong impression on him, visible in his later monumental paintings and in the style of several portraits (SBL) -- an impression, not pupilage: the French/Italian/Danish Wikipedias list Le Brun as a teacher, wiki-only, not in SBL (see C1509). November 1660: in England. The portraiture cherished at Charles II's court did not leave him untouched (SBL); the Swedish Wikipedia names the influence as Sir Peter Lely, Charles II's portrait painter. Returned via the Netherlands and Hamburg (collecting his things from Italy at a brother's), visited his "patron" Dahlbergh near Nykoping, and reached Stockholm in August 1661.

23 August 1661: audience with Queen Dowager Hedvig Eleonora, well received, commissioned to paint a portrait of one of her sisters. His rival for the court-painter post was the Danish-loaned Dutchman Abraham Wuchters, a naturalistic portraitist "not familiar with High Baroque illusionism, mobile composition and allegorical and classicist imagery". Ehrenstrahl intrigued Wuchters out; Wuchters returned to Denmark in spring 1662. Ehrenstrahl became the leader of Carolinian painting (SBL). 16 December 1661: the king's letter of appointment as *hovkonterfejare* (court portraitist), annual salary 600 daler silvermynt, with the obligation to execute eight bust portraits of Karl XI per year without separate payment, plus other portraits and paintings at an agreed tariff (SBL). Salary later raised: 1678 Karl XI ordered the 600 daler smt to be paid in riksdaler specie, i.e. raised to 1,200 daler smt; 1681 tariff: bust portrait 60 rdr, knee-piece 140 rdr, full-length portrait 200 rdr, equestrian portrait 300 rdr (SBL).

He married Maria Momma in 1663 (SBL Meriter: b. 18 Nov 1635 Amsterdam, d. 6 Mar 1680 Stockholm -- the death year given as 1681 in the German Wikipedia; see C1516; daughter of brukspatron Willem Momma and Maria de Bruyn; niece of Abraham and Jacob Momma, ennobled Reenstierna). Their only child: Anna Maria Ehrenstrahl (b. 4 September 1666, Stockholm -- d. 22 October 1729), painter, one of Sweden's earliest recognized female professional artists. Second marriage: 3 May 1681, Stockholm (German congregation), to Emerentia Baumann (d. 2 Aug 1693), daughter of Wismar councillor Michael Baumann, widow of pastor Johannes Gerdes. He sought the king's permission for the marriage since Emerentia was not of noble family (SBL). Her son from the first marriage, Frans Joachim Gerdes (b. 25 July 1670), was adopted onto his stepfather's noble name and number as Frans Joachim Ehrenstrahl; through him the family continued on the male line until 1814. David von Krafft was his nephew (his sister's son). April 1666: bought the plot at Svartmangatan 20 in Stockholm and built his own house there (SBL) -- the address given as 22 in the Swedish Wikipedia (see C1507).

Hedvig Eleonora's court accounts show he executed 63 works in a little over two years -- mainly the Dowager Queen, her German relatives and Karl XI, plus portraits of Georg Stiernhielm (Gripsholm) and Beata De la Gardie (Nationalmuseum). Payment was irregular, per the custom of the time; he eventually received assignments on customs income from his father-in-law Willem Momma's brass works in Nykoping. In September 1677 Momma transferred the brass works to him; long lawsuits with Momma's brothers Abraham and Jacob (Reenstierna) followed, settled only after his son-in-law J. Wattrang took over the works (SBL).

Late 1660s: large decorative commissions -- the state bedchamber at Drottningholm (allegories of Karl X Gustav's and Hedvig Eleonora's marriage, Karl XI's birth and upbringing, etc.) and in 1669 the vault of the great staircase with her name cipher surrounded by allegorical figures (SBL). The Riddarhus (House of Nobility) ceiling, *Dygdernas radslag* (The Consultation of the Virtues): the nobility seems to have approached him c. 1665; a sketch was ready in spring 1669 and shown to the board; the order was settled in autumn 1669 for 2,000 riksdaler specie; the painter drew the final payment only in July 1674. The painting fills the mirror of the ceiling vault and sat isolated in the otherwise whitewashed ceiling until 1719, when it received its still-preserved framing. Subject: Mother Svea (Sweden personified) enthroned, surrounded by the Virtues; Eternity (Eterna) lifts the star-wreath over Svea's throne and Fame (Fama) trumpets the new great power's glory (SBL). The technique is the Swedish adaptation: in Italy ceilings were painted directly on the plaster; the Swedish climate would have made the paint fail to adhere, so this one was painted on canvas and hoisted up into the ceiling (Stockholm University, 2023). It is Sweden's largest painting, over 100 m², about 8 m above the floor, painted c. 1675 by Ehrenstrahl and his collaborators. Since 2023 it has been studied with UV light, infrared reflectography, pigment sampling and (planned) X-ray as part of Sabrina Norlander Eliasson's Vetenskapsradet project on mobility and cultural transfer. He had the composition engraved in copper by Georg Christoph Eimmart in Nuremberg, 1678, which made it widely known. A royal letter of 7 December 1671 forbade copying in Sweden anything he had had engraved or would have engraved, and importing or selling such copies (SBL).

Ennobled 23 November 1674, taking the name Ehrenstrahl (SBL). The Swedish Wikipedia's work articles call him ennobled 1674 "som forsta konstnar" (as the first artist) -- SBL does not make this claim, and the study does not assert it (see C1508). *Certamen equestre:* at the festivities for Karl XI's accession, 18 December 1672, he was commissioned to produce in copperplate the carousel held on the tilting ground at Hotorget, Stockholm, where the king appeared in Roman general's dress; soon after, the king also ordered an equestrian portrait "a la romaine". Only in 1685 did he seriously decide to finish the engraving book, in collaboration with Eimmart (SBL). The printed work: *Certamen equestre caeteraque solemnia Holmiae Suecorum ao: 1672 m. decbr. celebrata*, transverse folio, 63 plates.

Atelier by 1672: in a letter to Nils Bielke (1672) he writes he has "ein Stuck von seinen Leuten machen lassen" -- had a piece made by his people, i.e. he already had a workshop where assistants and pupils helped execute the many commissions. He called his nephew David Krafft to Stockholm in 1675 and taught him. The sitters' faces he generally did not entrust to the assisting pupils (SBL). The Swedish Wikipedia: as commissions flooded in he had to take on helpers, "so that he finally only made minor retouches on the works that left his atelier".

Portraits of the 1670s-80s: Karl XI's coronation (1676, Drottningholm); allegories of 1677 -- *Truth Unveiled by Time*, *Prudence Restores Justice*, *History and Fame*; from 1680 many portraits, including M. G. De la Gardie. *Brunnsmastaren vid Medevi och hans soner* (The Well-Master at Medevi and his sons, Gripsholm, signed 1689, billed to Hedvig Eleonora Sept 1690) -- praised by SBL for its simplicity and the models' unassuming poses, unusual for the time. Per Olsson (Pehr Olofsson), spokesman of the peasant estate, 1686 (Gripsholm) -- another representative of the simpler classes immortalised. Double portrait of Prince Karl and Princess Hedvig Sofia in antique dress, 1687 (Gripsholm).

Gout ("podager") increasingly afflicted him from the early 1680s; he sought cure at Medevi (spa cure 1687), and in May 1688 had attacks so severe he had to be carried. He recovered his energy (SBL).

Animal painting, ordered by the king: the king took him to Kungsor and had him paint moose -- "E. sighed. It did not help." Horses, bear and wolf hunts, *Orrspel* (Black Grouse Lek, 1675) and *Tjaderlek* (Capercaillie Lek), foxes, owls and dogs -- from small coquettish salon specimens on velvet cushions to powerful Ulm mastiffs. On his two large bird paintings (Drottningholm) he also has a landscape background, making him one of the first Swedish landscape interpreters in painting (SBL). Royal Palaces: painting animals was not common practice -- allegory/mythology and portraits of famous people topped the hierarchy of motifs -- "but the entrepreneurial Klocker did so" for Hedvig Eleonora and Karl XI; his work was so appreciated that he was raised to the nobility. Named dogs: Nespelina, Don-Don, Gredlinetta, Courtisan (spaniel, Gripsholm), Turck (large white hunting dog with brown spots; collar with gold monogram CRS -- Carolus Rex Sueciae), Griep the greyhound, Fortun the setter-type. Karl XI's horses were painted as large full-length portraits, no less than 270 x 250 cm; the 1736 inventory lists 23 horse portraits, 20 hung in the orangery. The horse Kortom's portrait (1684) carries a Latin inscription: "This is King Karl XI's loyal riding horse [...] painted in the thirtieth year of his life and the 84th year of the century. This dumb beast had few qualities that were unlike those of a human intellect."

Court intendant, 16 June 1690 (SBL Meriter; "thanks to Dahlbergh", spring 1690 in the body text). The post is *hovintendent*. The great series of the royal councillors (Gripsholm): in March 1691 he reported twelve finished and eight begun to Dahlbergh; the generals' portraits (Karlberg) should therefore date to 1691 or 1693, not 1680 as the inscriptions on the backs say (see C1514). Self-Portrait with Allegories, 1691 (Nationalmuseum, NM 949): he is portrayed with two women -- to his right Painting and painterly effects; to his left the intellectual part of painting, pointing at a piece of paper reading **"Immortales pinge Majestatum"** (paint the immortal reputation of the Majesties). He consults her as if asking what to paint. The portrait was conceived to be exhibited at Drottningholm. Since 2025 it is part of Sveriges kulturkanon (Sweden's official cultural canon; SOU 2025:92, pp. 143-144).

Drottningholm, the king's audience room (Ehrenstrahlssalongen): begun 1693, four wall paintings and a plafond, finished 1695 (SBL). The ceiling, *The Glorious Deeds of Swedish Kings* (*Svenska konungars beromliga bedrifter*), oil on canvas, c. 593 x 344 cm, inv. NMDrh 131 (ASP paper): Virtus, Gloria and Fortitude present a shield inscribed "CG" for the brave Swedish kings Gustav I, Gustav Adolf, Karl X, Karl XI and Crown Prince Karl XII; Immortality descends and crowns the shield with a ring of stars signifying eternity, while Fame blows her trumpet; below the shield a large lion -- "the Nordic lion" in his own description; around them constellations marked with white spots for individual stars -- Ursa Minor, Draco, Ursa Major, Corona Borealis, Hercules, Pegasus, Lyra, Aquila, Cygnus, Perseus, Andromeda, the Milky Way, Stella Polaris and the fixed stars -- "busy admiring the valour of the Swedish kings". The constellations chosen are all visible from the north and associated with valour and heroic deeds. The room was created in 1690 as Hedvig Eleonora's grand audience hall next to her state bedchamber; Ehrenstrahl made six large wall paintings in framed fields and a ceiling plafond, installed 1696-1698 (aroundus.com). Dates disputed -- 1695 (English Wikipedia) vs 1695-97 (NiceArtGallery) vs c. 1694-96 (ASP paper) vs 1693 (FeelTheArt object page) vs SBL (begun 1693, finished 1695) vs installed 1696-98 (see C1506/C1542). The study uses "begun 1693, finished 1695" with the spread stated.

Two huge canvases for the palace church choir, **The Last Judgement** (put up in the palace church November 1695) and **The Crucifixion**, described by him in 1695; cut down from the wall, somewhat damaged, in the palace fire of 1697; later placed in Storkyrkan (SBL; Swedish Wikipedia dates them to the 1690s, painted for the chapel in the later-burnt Tre Kronor castle). The Last Judgement is more than 10 m tall, the Crucifixion 7.4 m (REPORTED, Wikipedia Storkyrkan citing Roosval 1927); the Last Judgement is called Sweden's largest painting in the tourist literature, though no measured catalogue entry for its width was found (see C1536). The Last Judgement was cut into strips and rolled up to be rescued from the Tre Kronor fire, then reassembled -- the seams are still visible.

Last years: in 1693 again at Medevi for his health; lost his second wife the same year; lived his remaining years alone under increasing illness. In his last two years he had to be carried up to his painting room. He worked eagerly on preparations for extensive engraving works. Even the despised animal painting occupied his last years: Karl XI wrote from Kungsor 12 Feb 1697 that "konterfejaren Krafft" (his nephew D. v. Krafft) had indeed "taken the white squirrel" a stable hand found in July 1696, "snow-white with red eyes", but the king still wanted it painted by Ehrenstrahl himself. In another letter (18 Feb) the king acknowledges receipt of "the two paintings on which the dog Courtisan and the two grouse cocks stand depicted". He died in autumn 1698, buried in Storkyrkan (epitaph there probably by B. Foucquet). The death date is disputed: 23 October 1698 (English, Swedish, Hungarian Wikipedia) versus 27 October 1698 (SBL -- gravestone in Storkyrkan, soul-ringing 28 October; French, Danish, Norwegian, Indonesian, Arabic Wikipedia; Wikidata) -- see C1499/C1539. SBL is the scholarly source and should be preferred, but the 23rd is the common encyclopaedic date; the study uses 23 October 1698 in its header with the flag attached.

The honorific "father of Swedish painting" is universal: Nationalmuseum's 1942 catalogue, the Runeberg handbooks, Stockholm University ("anses vara den svenska malarkonstens fader"), Swedish Wikipedia. Grounds given: he introduced and adapted international Baroque styles, trained the next generation, and produced the body of work that visually defined Sweden's Great Power era; he founded Sweden's first informal art academy (single-sourced -- see C1511); his pupil von Krafft was "the artistic heir" who carried the continuity from Great Power painting into the Age of Liberty (Nationalmuseum catalogue). A counter-voice exists in the older Danish literature: Salmonsens Konversationsleksikon (2nd ed., 1923, via Danish Wikipedia) calls him "an eclectic and continuator of Cortona's decadent art, a routine painter who in the end worked entirely workshop-wise with a staff of apprentices", but credits him with breaking ground in Swedish art -- bringing a previously unknown painterly skill in the distribution of light and shadow and in the luminous effect of colour application, and showing the way to honest nature study in several portraits.

## The evolution: Dutch observation to dynastic allegory, with the animals running alongside

Five phases, all evidenced by the works. The through-line is a scaling-up that never drops its first mode: a painter who starts by looking closely at one jester, learns in Italy how to fill a ceiling, and ends by painting the kings on the ceiling and the dogs by name -- the allegory getting larger and the observation getting quieter, at the same time.

**1. Apprentice / Skokloster, 1648-1654 -- the observed individual.** Amsterdam training under Juriaen Jacobsz (flagged, C1501/C1538); then Skokloster under Wrangel. Small-scale, observed individuals: a jester (W01, 1651/52), a patron on horseback (W02, 1652). Dutch palette, close looking, no allegory yet except a first attempt (W03, Allegori, 1653) and a mythological portrait the same year (W04, Unknown Lady as Diana, 1653, Gothenburg). The equestrian portrait -- the genre that will define his mature career -- is already present in the Skokloster years.

**2. Italy, 1654-1661 -- the documented gap.** Venice, Rome; Cortona's Baroque ceilings and Sacchi's allegorical heavens enter his visual memory. No datable surviving oil can be attached to these years (see C1533) -- the journey's evidence is in what comes after. The catalogue resumes in 1662, with the Italian lesson visible in the work itself.

**3. Early court, 1661-1670 -- the portrait at its most individual and its most classical.** Court painter 1661. The portraiture is at its most individual (Dahlbergh 1664, W06 -- Nyblom/Laurin's touchstone, proof he "had begun as an excellent portrait-painter") and its most classical (Bengt Horn as a Roman General, W05, 1662; a Swedish nobleman dressed as a Roman general, the antique-dress portrait as the Italian journey applied to Stockholm sitters). The 1668 allegory set (four small allegories, NMDrh 27-30: the king's upbringing, his guardian angel, Karl X Gustav's wedding, Karl XI's birth) rehearses the dynastic-allegory mode at cabinet scale. Animal pictures begin (Hare 1668). The hinge year is 1670: the same year gives the exotic-animal cabinet picture (W08, Boy with Parrots and Monkeys, NM 1407) and the 15-year-old king as Apollo (W09, Karl XI as Apollo Pythias, NMDrh 179) -- portraiture becoming myth.

**4. Mature, 1670-1685 -- the scale jump.** The Riddarhus ceiling (W10) turns allegory architectural; the equestrian portraits (W11, Skokloster, 291.5 x 280.5 cm VERIFIED; W12, Rijksmuseum, 290 x 275 cm VERIFIED) and the horse portraits (W13, W15) turn portraiture monumental -- canvases of 2.5-2.9 m, the horse life-size, the king in antique dress with baton and laurel. The 1672 carousel book (W14, Met etching 30 x 40 cm VERIFIED) extends the same image into print and festival. The king is now Apollo (W09), a Roman emperor, a commander at Lund (W12, the battle of 4 December 1676 smoking behind the rearing white horse). The sitter stops being an individual and becomes a type: the king as idea.

**5. Late, 1686-1698 -- family, theory, ceilings again, colossal religion.** Three moves at once. (a) The family group (W16, 1691) gathers the whole dynasty, living and dead, into one room -- king, queen, children, dowager queen, dead king Karl X Gustav present as a portrait-within-the-portrait, Holstein-Gottorp kin; two further versions exist (1692, 1697), prime unresolved (see C1526). (b) Theory: the 1691 self-portrait (W17) declares painting's purpose -- immortal reputation -- and the Drottningholm ceiling (W18) redoes the Riddarhus invention with harder, more learned machinery: constellations as living witnesses, placed like a map, approving the regency. (c) Scale peaks in religion: the Tre Kronor chapel pair (W19, W20), 7.4 m and over 10 m tall. Alongside all of it, the small natural-history pictures continue to the end -- Two Parrots 1696, White Squirrel 1696, Blackcock 1697 -- precise, quiet, unallegorical.

What changed, in one line per register. Palette: Nyblom/Laurin (1913 handbook): "a rather lively appreciation of colour" throughout his activity -- colour is the constant. What changes is its deployment: Dutch sobriety in the Skokloster portraits; high-Baroque, sky-blue and gold in the ceilings; the dark grounds of the horse portraits setting off dappled greys, chestnuts and blacks. SBL's stylistic judgement, the closest thing to a technique account: "The colouring is strong from the beginning, but from being in the 1660s and 1670s aimed at separating figures and objects through harmonising local colours, Ehrenstrahl in the 1680s goes over to observing more the descriptive value of the brushstroke, colouristically as well as formally. His brush becomes freer and more expressive." Hahr (1900, via search snippet) adds that the early portraits have a warm, caressing lustre with a brownish overall tone, while the portraits from 1663 are held in grey. Scale: cabinet portraits (1650s) to 2.5-2.9 m equestrians (1670s-80s) to a 5.9 m ceiling (1690s) to a 10 m altarpiece (1696). Each scale jump follows a commission, not a whim: the nobility's hall, the king's horses, the queen's audience chamber, the royal chapel. Figure: from observed individuals (jester, Dahlbergh) to idealised bodies -- antique dress, laurel, baton; then to allegorical personifications (Virtus, Gloria, Fortitude; constellations as women) and finally to the family as an institution. Nyblom/Laurin's verdict stands across the career: he "lacks again and again the power of individualizing" -- the weakness and the programme are the same thing. The face matters less than the office. Subject: portraiture to dynastic allegory to ceiling-as-cosmos to colossal religion, with animal portraiture as the unbroken counter-thread from 1651 to 1697. The late work does not abandon the early work; it stacks it: the 1691 family group contains portraiture, allegory and animal (dogs) in one frame.

## M1 -- The ceiling as statecraft: the kings' deeds overhead

Ehrenstrahl's driving mechanism is the ceiling as an argument about who rules. The Riddarhus ceiling (W10, ordered autumn 1669, 2,000 riksdaler specie, final payment July 1674) puts Mother Svea enthroned in the middle of the nobility's own hall, surrounded by the Virtues, Eternity lifting the star-wreath over her throne and Fame trumpeting the new great power's glory -- the assembled nobility sits underneath its own allegory and looks up at the reason for its existence. The Drottningholm ceiling (W18, begun 1693, finished 1695) repeats the invention a generation later and harder: Virtus, Gloria and Fortitude present a shield inscribed "CG" for the brave Swedish kings -- Gustav I, Gustav Adolf, Karl X, Karl XI and Crown Prince Karl XII -- Immortality descends and crowns the shield with a ring of stars signifying eternity, Fame blows her trumpet, and below the shield a large lion, "the Nordic lion" in his own description. The mechanism is the same in both: the dynasty is not painted, it is enthroned -- put overhead, in the room where power meets, so that every meeting happens underneath the claim. And the mechanism is physically Swedish: the ceiling is not fresco, as in Italy, but oil on canvas, painted in the studio and hoisted up, because paint would not adhere to plaster in the Swedish climate (Stockholm University). The argument is made the way the climate allows. The title confusion in the sources (English Wikipedia calling the Riddarhus ceiling *The Great Deeds of the Swedish Kings*, which is the Drottningholm ceiling's title) is itself evidence of how completely the two ceilings are the same machine run twice (see C1505). The study does not repeat the conflated title.

**Takeaway:** put the claim overhead -- the kings' deeds as allegory in the room where power meets, Mother Svea enthroned among the Virtues, the shield crowned by Immortality, Fame trumpeting below, and paint it on canvas and hoist it up, because the climate will not take fresco: the argument is the ceiling, and the ceiling is the room's reason.

## M2 -- The king as a Roman emperor: the equestrian as a type

The equestrian portrait is Ehrenstrahl's second machine, and it runs the same way: the sitter stops being an individual and becomes a type. The template is fixed by the early 1670s and never changes: the king in blue antique dress with harness and gold-embroidered cloak, field-marshal's baton in the right hand, laurel wreath, full figure on horseback facing right (W11, Skokloster, inscribed "Carolus XI Rex Suecia A:o 1674", 291.5 x 280.5 cm VERIFIED). The Rijksmuseum version (W12, 290 x 275 cm VERIFIED) adds only a battle -- the horse rears, the sword is drawn, Lund smokes behind -- and the Gripsholm original it replicates, and the near-identical equestrians at NMGrh 1261 (1672), NMGrh 459 (1676) and NMGrh 1245 (1685), show the formula repeated across two decades with the prime-versus-copy status unresolved (see C1534). The king is shown as a Roman commander, not a contemporary soldier; the baton and the laurel do the work the face does not. Nyblom/Laurin's verdict is the mechanism stated as a weakness: he "lacks again and again the power of individualizing". The weakness and the programme are the same thing -- the face matters less than the office, and the office is antique dress, baton, laurel, horse. The 1672 carousel book (W14) extends the same image into print and festival: the king appears as a Roman emperor at the tilting ground at Hotorget, and the festival book, 63 plates with Eimmart, makes the type public. The horse portraits (W13, W15) are the same machine with the king removed: the horse alone, three-quarter view, life-size, named in an inscription -- Stenbock, Falck, Bla Tigern, Favorit Hispanicus, Precieux, Brilliant, Tott, Kortom, Monarcha, Pegasus, Sultan, Frisk, Favourite -- seventeen of twenty-plus survive, seven on display at Stromsholm Palace. Kortom's portrait (1684) carries a Latin inscription: "This is King Karl XI's loyal riding horse [...] painted in the thirtieth year of his life and the 84th year of the century. This dumb beast had few qualities that were unlike those of a human intellect." The horse is given the eulogy the king's portrait does not need.

**Takeaway:** make the king a type, not a man -- antique dress, baton, laurel, the horse rearing and the battle smoking behind, the same formula repeated for twenty years until the prime and the copy cannot be told apart, and when the king is absent, give the horse the portrait and the eulogy: the office is the sitter, and the face is only where the office sits.

## M3 -- The workshop that only retouches: the faces by the master's hand

By 1672 Ehrenstrahl ran an atelier with assistants and pupils; in a letter to Nils Bielke that year he writes he has "ein Stuck von seinen Leuten machen lassen" -- had a piece made by his people. Hedvig Eleonora's court accounts show 63 works in a little over two years, mainly the Dowager Queen, her German relatives and Karl XI. The volume is the mechanism, and the division of labour is its rule: he generally painted the sitters' faces himself, and in the end "only made minor retouches" on the works that left his atelier (SBL; Swedish Wikipedia). The face is the contract and the only part the master will not delegate; everything else -- the drapery, the horse, the ceiling's lesser figures -- is the workshop's. The same rule runs through the family: his daughter Anna Maria was assigned from childhood to paint details in his work, confirmed as his assistant from c. 1680, and produced a large number of copies of his works, among them a portrait of Charles XI. The versions are the evidence: the Charles XI family group exists in three versions (1691, 1692, 1697), prime unresolved (see C1526); the Astronomy-Lesson-style multiplication is here the family group; the equestrian formula exists in five near-identical versions (see C1534); the Hedvig Eleonora portraits split between those graded "by" and those graded "attributed" (see C1531). The Danish counter-voice (Salmonsens, 1923) calls him "a routine painter who in the end worked entirely workshop-wise with a staff of apprentices" -- and credits him with breaking ground in Swedish art in the same breath. The study takes the counter-voice seriously: the workshop is not a failure of authorship, it is the system working. A court that orders 63 works in two years cannot wait for the master to finish each alone; the master paints the faces and retouches the rest, and the retouch is the signature.

**Takeaway:** paint the faces yourself and retouch the rest -- the workshop makes the piece, the master makes the likeness, and let the versions be the evidence: when the demand is sixty-three works in two years, the original is the one with the master's face in it, and the retouch is the signature.

## M4 -- The animal as a state portrait: the named dog, the named horse

Animal painting ranked low in the hierarchy of motifs -- allegory and mythology and portraits of famous people topped it -- "but the entrepreneurial Klocker did so" for Hedvig Eleonora and Karl XI (Royal Palaces), and his work was so appreciated that he was raised to the nobility. The mechanism is the animal treated exactly as a sitter: named, inscribed, life-size, and given the portrait's full apparatus. The dogs have names -- Nespelina, Don-Don, Gredlinetta, Courtisan (spaniel, Gripsholm), Turck (large white hunting dog with brown spots; collar with gold monogram CRS -- Carolus Rex Sueciae), Griep the greyhound, Fortun the setter-type -- and the horses have names and inscriptions, painted as large full-length portraits, no less than 270 x 250 cm, twenty-three of them in the 1736 inventory, twenty hung in the orangery. The king took him to Kungsor and had him paint moose -- "E. sighed. It did not help." The white squirrel found in July 1696, "snow-white with red eyes", was to be painted by Ehrenstrahl himself, not by his nephew Krafft, who had "taken" it -- the king's letter of 12 February 1697 insists on the master's hand for a squirrel. The small natural-history pictures run the whole career, 1651 to 1697, as the unbroken counter-thread to the ceilings: Hare 1668, blackcocks 1672, hounds 1674, *Orrspel* (Black Grouse Lek, 1675), Two Parrots 1696, White Squirrel 1696, Blackcock 1697 -- precise, quiet, unallegorical, and given the same care as the kings. On his two large bird paintings (Drottningholm) he also has a landscape background, making him one of the first Swedish landscape interpreters in painting (SBL). The mechanism is a reversal of rank: the lowest subject in the hierarchy gets the highest treatment, the named animal gets the state portrait, and the painter who sighs at the moose paints it anyway, because the king ordered it and the king's order is the commission.

**Takeaway:** give the animal the state portrait -- name the dog, name the horse, inscribe the Latin eulogy on the horse's portrait, paint the white squirrel with the master's own hand when the king insists, and let the lowest subject in the hierarchy get the highest treatment: the moose is the commission, and the sigh is part of the work.

## M5 -- The painter who wrote his own key: the riddle picture

Ehrenstrahl wrote about his own work, and the writing is part of the mechanism. *Die vornehmste Schildereyen, welche in denen Pallasten des Konigreiches Schweden zu sehen sind, inventiret, verfertiget und beschrieben* (Stockholm, 1694, folio, 34 pp.) is a catalogue-like booklet in which he himself described and interpreted the deep symbolism of his large canvases and ceilings. The ASP paper notes it makes the Drottningholm iconographical programme identifiable "with certainty", while also noting it is "odd that the placing of some of the paintings in the room as well as some details of the paintings do not conform with the description" -- the key does not quite fit the lock, and the mismatch is itself evidence that the key was written after the pictures. Whether the 1694 publication is one work under two titles or two separate 1694 publications -- *Die vornehmste Schildereyen* versus *Kurtzer Unterricht* -- was not resolved this run (see C1540). *Kurtzer Unterricht, Observationes und Regulen von der Mahlerey* (Brief Instruction, Observations and Rules of Painting) was written after 1680, in parts after foreign handbooks, and published only in 1918, edited by G. M. Silfverstolpe; the Swedish Wikipedia calls it "Sweden's first art-theoretical writing" and NiceArtGallery "one of the first treatises on art theory written in Swedish", but the title is German and the language of the manuscript was not verified this leg (see C1512). *Explication ofwer the twenne stora schilderier...* (1695) describes the two great paintings in the choir of the palace church. His aesthetic credo, as quoted by SBL: *"den, som liknar en konstrik, vacker tavla vid en klok och valuttankt gata, han begar forvisso icke nagot misstag"* -- he who likens an artful, beautiful picture to a clever, well-thought-out riddle makes no mistake. The short English form, "Art presents riddles that could not be solved by everyone", is a compression, not a verified quotation (see C1513). The self-portrait (W17, 1691) is the credo painted: he is portrayed with two women -- to his right Painting and painterly effects; to his left the intellectual part of painting, pointing at a piece of paper reading **"Immortales pinge Majestatum"** (paint the immortal reputation of the Majesties). He consults her as if asking what to paint. The mechanism is the picture as a riddle with a key, and the painter as the man who holds the key and writes it down: the allegory is not meant to be transparent, it is meant to be solved, and the solution is the painter's own booklet.

**Takeaway:** make the picture a riddle and write the key yourself -- the allegory solved only by those who know the programme, the painter consulting the intellectual part of painting about what to paint, the booklet describing the deep symbolism of the ceilings, and the key not quite fitting the lock: the riddle is the work, and the painter is the one who holds the answer.

## M6 -- The sky as witness: constellations that admire the kings

The Drottningholm ceiling (W18) is the mechanism at its most learned: the stars themselves approve the dynasty. Around the shield of the brave Swedish kings, the constellations are painted as living women holding attributes, their stars marked with white spots -- Ursa Minor, Draco, Ursa Major, Corona Borealis, Hercules, Pegasus, Lyra, Aquila, Cygnus, Perseus, Andromeda, the Milky Way, Stella Polaris and the fixed stars -- "busy admiring the valour of the Swedish kings". Only constellations visible from Sweden are chosen, placed roughly as on a map. The model is Andrea Sacchi's *Divina Provvidenza* ceiling (c. 1630, Palazzo Barberini), which Ehrenstrahl probably knew from his Roman visit to the Palazzo Barberini; but the invention is his own: the stars are not decoration, they are witnesses, and their testimony is the regency's legitimacy -- the ASP paper dates the theme of the room to the reign of the dowager queen Hedvig Eleonora, 1660-1693 (the formal end of the regency needs verification before the span is repeated as a regency; see C1543). The mechanism is astronomy as flattery: the heavens are rearranged so that the constellations of the north -- the ones Sweden can see -- are the ones that admire the kings, and the map of the sky becomes the map of the dynasty's right to rule. The white spots are the technical detail that makes it work: each star is a spot of white paint on a living figure, so the figure is the constellation and the constellation is the figure, and the viewer can check the sky against the ceiling and find the ceiling true.

**Takeaway:** let the sky testify -- the constellations as living women, their stars marked in white, placed like a map of the northern heavens, all of them busy admiring the kings, so that the ceiling is not a decoration but a witness statement: the stars approve the dynasty, and the map of the sky is the map of its right to rule.

## M7 -- The plain sitter: the jester, the peasant, the well-master

The earliest dated work in the catalogue is not a king but a jester: Hinric Hasenberger, the court fool, 1651 (Skokloster) and 1652 (Nationalmuseum, NM 1633) -- two versions or one work with conflicting dates, unresolved (see C1519). Ehrenstrahl was painting the household, high and low, from the year he arrived. The mechanism runs the whole career beside the ceilings: the plain sitter, painted without allegory, without antique dress, without laurel. *Brunnsmastaren vid Medevi och hans soner* (The Well-Master at Medevi and his sons, Gripsholm, signed 1689) is praised by SBL for its simplicity and the models' unassuming poses, unusual for the time. Per Olsson (Pehr Olofsson), spokesman of the peasant estate, 1686 (Gripsholm) -- another representative of the simpler classes immortalised. The double portrait of Prince Karl and Princess Hedvig Sofia in antique dress, 1687, sits beside them in the same years, and the contrast is the point: the same hand paints the princes in antique dress and the well-master in his own clothes, and the well-master's portrait is the one SBL singles out for its simplicity. The Danish counter-voice credits him with "showing the way to honest nature study in several portraits" -- the honest nature study is the plain sitter. The mechanism is the court painted whole: the king as Apollo and the jester as himself, the princes in antique dress and the peasant spokesman as himself, the household from the top to the bottom, and the bottom painted with the same care as the top, because the observation is the same and only the costume changes.

**Takeaway:** paint the household whole -- the jester first, before the kings, the well-master and his sons in unassuming poses, the peasant spokesman as himself, and let the plain sitter be the proof of the observation: the king is Apollo, the jester is himself, and the same hand paints both, because the looking is the same and only the costume changes.

## M8 -- The institution-builder: court painter, noble, intendant, school

Ehrenstrahl's career is the only one in the chain that builds the institution it serves from inside it. He arrives as a scribe, becomes court portraitist by the king's letter of 16 December 1661 at 600 daler silvermynt a year with eight bust portraits of Karl XI per year as the standing obligation, is ennobled 23 November 1674 taking the name Ehrenstrahl, and becomes court intendant 16 June 1690 -- the same two honours his nephew and successor David von Krafft will take a generation later, ennobled 1719, court intendant 1720. The mechanism is institutional: the painter who never painted a fresco (the climate would not take it), never held the post of Premier peintre du Roi, never went to Rome as a master -- he went as a student -- ends up running the court that governs Swedish painting, because the portrait trade, practised for thirty-seven years at one court, is the institution by then. And he builds the school that outlives him: he called Krafft to Stockholm in 1675 and taught him; Mikael Dahl joined the Hannibal and Ehrenstrahl group in 1674 at fifteen -- first lessons from the Hungarian-born drawing-master Martin Hannibal, whom Ehrenstrahl had brought from Italy to help found a portrait academy, students who showed skill picked out to continue with Ehrenstrahl himself -- and Dahl left for London in 1682 and became Godfrey Kneller's most significant competitor, painting Queen Anne; his daughter Anna Maria, assigned from childhood to paint details in his work, assistant from c. 1680, completed an allegory of the four seasons at twenty-one (1687) and in 1717 donated six portraits of presidents of the Svea Hovratt to the court, becoming Sweden's leading female painter of her generation -- trained as her father's "artistic secretary" and working in his Baroque manner all her life, and the study says plainly that he never intended her to become an independent artist, and that she became one anyway. The succession is the cleanest in the whole chain: uncle to nephew, Apollo to campaign coat -- Krafft's main task the representative portraits of Charles XII, the bareheaded king in a simple blue campaign uniform, the image that replaced his uncle's laurelled Apollo. The school is the vehicle of Swedish painting into the 18th century (Hahr 1900, *David von Krafft och den Ehrenstrahlska skolan*); SBL notes only one painter truly carried the continuity, von Krafft, in whose atelier the artists who chiefly represent Swedish painting in the first half of the 18th century received their first training. The parallel with de Troy is exact and inverted: de Troy served two kings at once and ended directing the Academy his master's master never joined; Ehrenstrahl served one court for thirty-seven years and built the school his successor inherited. The northern mirror of de Troy, and the mirror held the other way round.

**Takeaway:** build the institution from inside it -- scribe to court painter to noble to intendant, the school founded with a drawing-master brought from Italy, the pupil who leaves for London and rivals Kneller, the daughter who was never meant to be an artist and becomes one anyway, the nephew who replaces the laurelled Apollo with the plain-blue campaign coat: the portrait trade becomes the institution, and the succession is the work.

## The aesthetic logic

Ehrenstrahl's aesthetic is scale over likeness, allegory over individuality, and the institution over the picture -- in that order. The construction is Italian Baroque learned in Rome -- Cortona admired, Sacchi's *Divina Provvidenza* as the model for the Drottningholm ceiling, the mass effect and the distribution of light and dark -- laid over a Dutch base of close observation (Jacobsz, Snyders) and adapted to Swedish conditions: ceilings in oil on canvas hoisted up, not fresco, because the climate will not take plaster. The colour is the constant: "strong from the beginning" (SBL), a warm, caressing lustre with a brownish overall tone in the early portraits (Hahr), held in grey from 1663, then from the 1680s a freer, more expressive, descriptively loaded brushstroke. The allegory is the system: the kings' deeds overhead, the constellations as witnesses, the shield crowned by Immortality, the riddle picture with the painter's own key -- and the key is a booklet the painter wrote himself, *Die vornehmste Schildereyen* (1694), because the allegory is not meant to be transparent, it is meant to be solved. The portrait is the contract and the face is the only part the master will not delegate; the workshop makes the piece and the master makes the likeness, and the retouch is the signature. The animal is the counter-thread: the lowest subject in the hierarchy given the highest treatment, the named dog, the named horse, the white squirrel painted by the master's own hand, the moose painted because the king ordered it and the painter sighed. The plain sitter is the proof: the jester first, the well-master and the peasant spokesman as themselves, the honest nature study the Danish counter-voice credits him with, running beside the ceilings for forty-six years. The reputation curve runs with it: the father of Swedish painting, the leader of Carolinian painting, the man who introduced and adapted international Baroque styles and trained the next generation -- and the counter-voice in the same breath, "an eclectic and continuator of Cortona's decadent art, a routine painter who in the end worked entirely workshop-wise with a staff of apprentices", who broke ground in Swedish art by bringing a previously unknown painterly skill in the distribution of light and shadow and in the luminous effect of colour application. The study's duty is to keep both voices: the institution-builder and the routine painter are the same man, and the workshop is the system working. His Self-Portrait with Allegories (1691) entered Sveriges kulturkanon in 2025 -- the painter who painted the immortal reputation of the Majesties has himself become part of the canon he was painting for.

## The network

**The doorway in:** Nils Bielke (7 February 1644, Stockholm -- 26 November 1716), Swedish ambassador -- de Troy's first important commission, in 1679, was Bielke's portrait; Bielke was Sweden's ambassador to France 1679-82 and again 1684-87, so de Troy painted him in the first year of his first Paris posting. Bielke entered the Swedish Army and Royal Court in the 1660s, was Lieutenant General in 1678, Governor-General of Swedish Estonia and Swedish Pomerania 1687-98, Field Marshal 1690, and was created Reichsgraf of the Holy Roman Empire. He later fell out with Charles XI over the king's policy towards the old aristocracy, was sentenced to death in 1705, pardoned, and redeemed in 1715. The doorway #589 -> #590 is the ambassador himself: de Troy painted the man in Paris; Ehrenstrahl painted the court the man served in Stockholm.

**Masters and formation:** Juriaen Jacobsz (17 December 1624, Hamburg -- 1685, Leeuwarden), portrait and animal painter, pupil of Frans Snyders in Antwerp, later court painter to Henry Casimir II, Prince of Nassau-Dietz, in Leeuwarden; RKD lists Ehrenstrahl among his pupils. The teaching link is attested twice over; the place and year are not (see C1538). The Netherlands, 1648-52: four years of Dutch study before Sweden, the portrait and animal-painting tradition (Snyders via Jacobsz) as the base layer under everything later. Italy, 1654-61: Venice and Rome until 1660, return via Florence, Marseille, Paris and London. No single Italian master is named in any source read this run; the study does not invent one. The Italian years are the hinge of his manner all the same: he arrived in Sweden a Dutch-trained painter and came back an Italianate one, which is what made him usable to a court that wanted Baroque grandeur, not Dutch plainness. Models, not masters: Pietro da Cortona (admired, not his teacher), Andrea Sacchi (*Divina Provvidenza*, the likely model for the Drottningholm ceiling), Charles Le Brun (a strong impression in Paris 1659-60, not pupilage), Sir Peter Lely (the London influence, Swedish Wikipedia).

**Patrons:** Carl Gustaf Wrangel (1613-1676), the first patron -- at Wrangel's request Ehrenstrahl left his Dutch studies in 1652 and moved to Skokloster Castle on Lake Malaren; Wrangel, Governor-General of Swedish Pomerania and from 1664 Lord High Constable and Privy Councillor, was building Skokloster into the largest private palace in Sweden and filling it with art. Ehrenstrahl painted Wrangel on horseback in 1652, the year he arrived. Queen Dowager Hedvig Eleonora of Holstein-Gottorp (1636-1715), the principal patron -- on his return in 1661 Ehrenstrahl became court painter to Hedvig Eleonora; widow of Charles X Gustav, the dominant cultural force of the minority of her son Charles XI, and Drottningholm Palace was built from 1662 for her. She commissioned the decoration of several rooms at Drottningholm, including the Ehrenstrahl parlour ceiling. Charles XI (1655-1697), the repeated sitter -- as a young king, on horseback (Skokloster SKO 619; Rijksmuseum SK-A-1234), as Apollo Pythias 1670 (NMDrh 179), with his family and Holstein-Gottorp relatives 1691. The Stockholm aristocracy as sitters: Erik Dahlbergh (portrait 1664, Uppsala), Georg Stiernhielm, Agneta Horn, a child of Carl Gustaf Wrangel, Countess Christina Piper in her youth, Hinric Hasenberger the court jester (1652, NM 1633), Adolph John I Count Palatine of Kleeburg, Nils Brahe the younger.

**Pupils, successors and family -- the Stockholm school:** Mikael Dahl (1656/1659 -- 20 October 1743, London), the pupil who left -- joined the Hannibal and Ehrenstrahl group in 1674 at fifteen; left Sweden on a passport in July 1682, via Antwerp to London, worked for the engraver Robert White, then for Godfrey Kneller, toured Paris and Rome, where he painted the exiled Queen Christina, who put him in contact with Pope Innocent XI, and returned to London in 1688/89 to open his own studio in open competition with Kneller, painting Queen Anne and Prince George of Denmark (see C1541 on his birth year and early teachers). David von Krafft (1655 -- 20 September 1724), the nephew and successor -- Ehrenstrahl's nephew (his mother was Ehrenstrahl's sister), orphaned young, called to Sweden in 1675 by Ehrenstrahl as assistant and apprentice; after Ehrenstrahl's death in 1698 Krafft was appointed his successor as court painter, his main task the representative portraits of Charles XII, the bareheaded king in a simple blue campaign uniform; ennobled in 1719 and court intendant in 1720, the same two honours his uncle had taken in 1674 and 1690, a generation later. Anna Maria Ehrenstrahl (4 September 1666 -- 22 October 1729), the daughter and assistant -- student of her father, assigned from childhood to paint details in his work, assistant from c. 1680, allegory of the four seasons at twenty-one (1687), portraits of Prince Fredrik and Prince Karl Gustav by commission of the Queen Dowager (1690), six portraits of presidents of the Svea Hovratt donated in 1717. Johan David Swartz (1678-1729), pupil of Ehrenstrahl and later of Krafft, present with Charles XII at Altranstadt, painter of the 1706-07 portrait that set the plain-blue king type. Martin Hannibal, the Hungarian-born drawing-master Ehrenstrahl brought from Italy to help found the portrait academy and teach the first elements of painting -- no work of his has been identified; the man who taught the basics to Dahl, Krafft and the rest, and left nothing to look at. **The Stockholm court circle:** Erik Dahlbergh (1625-1703), sitter and sketchbook parallel -- Ehrenstrahl's portrait of Dahlbergh (1664, Uppsala) is cited by Runeberg as proof he "had begun as an excellent portrait-painter"; Nisser's 1948 study pairs Dahlbergh's and Ehrenstrahl's Italian sketchbooks, but no meeting in Italy is evidenced this run, so the study presents it as a parallel, not an encounter. Georg Stiernhielm (1598-1672) and Agneta Horn (1629-1672), sitters -- the poet and the memoirist, the court's literary faces. Maria Momma (m. 1663), wife -- mother of Anna Maria; no artistic role evidenced; the study does not invent one.

**Contemporaries beyond Stockholm:** Queen Christina in Rome, a second-hand link via Dahl -- Ehrenstrahl never painted Christina (she abdicated 1654, the year he left for Italy); his pupil Dahl painted the exiled Christina in Rome in the 1680s, and she connected him to Innocent XI. The master's Rome and the pupil's Rome, thirty years apart, with the same exiled queen in the middle. Pierre Mignard (1612-1695), the Rome overlap -- Mignard was in Rome 1635-57; Ehrenstrahl studied in Rome 1654/55-60; they were in the same city in the same years, 1655-57, Mignard the established French portraitist of popes and cardinals, Ehrenstrahl the young Hamburger learning. No meeting is evidenced in any source read this run; the study says so. Daniel Schultz (c. 1615-1683), the parallel court across the Baltic -- the Warsaw court portraitist of the Polish Vasas while Ehrenstrahl painted the Swedish Vasas in Stockholm; both painted kings for the same extended dynasty in the same decades. **No evidenced link to:** Charles Le Brun (HELD; no link found); Nicolas Poussin and Claude Lorrain (HELD; Mignard knew Poussin in Rome, but no Ehrenstrahl link); Joachim von Sandrart (HELD).

**Adjacent artists (the map):** Mikael Dahl (1656/1659-1743) -- the pupil who left Stockholm for London and became Kneller's rival; the export of Ehrenstrahl's portrait academy. David von Krafft (1655-1724) -- the nephew who succeeded him as court painter in 1698 and replaced the laurelled Apollo with the plain-blue campaign king; the succession as a change of image, not just of hands. Anna Maria Ehrenstrahl (1666-1729) -- the daughter-assistant who copied her father's kings and then painted her own allegories and the six presidents of the Svea Hovratt; the workshop's second generation, and Sweden's first professional woman painter.

## Future candidates

**Top pick for #591 is Pierre Mignard (17 November 1612, Troyes -- 30 May 1695, Paris).** The biggest unstudied name left in the chain, and the French counterpart to Ehrenstrahl's Stockholm career. Vouet pupil (1633, after training with Jean Boucher at Bourges and copying the Mannerists at Fontainebleau); Rome 1635-57, twenty-two years, where he painted Madonnas so popular they were called "Mignardises", altarpieces, and portraits of popes, cardinals and Italian nobility -- and where Poussin hired him to copy his works; summoned to Paris in 1657, probably by Mazarin; met Moliere at Avignon on the way back and became his close friend, painting him several times; the most successful portrait painter of Louis XIV's Paris, sitters including Turenne, Bossuet, Maintenon, La Valliere, Sevigne, Montespan, Descartes; Le Brun's bitter, lifelong rival -- he refused to enter the Academie while Le Brun directed it and headed a rival guild instead; on Le Brun's death in 1690 he succeeded him as BOTH Director of the Academie (1690-95, succeeded by Noel Coypel) and Premier peintre du Roi; died in 1695 as he was about to begin work on the cupola of Les Invalides (en.wiki Mignard, read end to end, 111 lines). The bridge to Ehrenstrahl is real and twofold: they overlapped in Rome in 1655-57, and each man held, at opposite ends of Europe, the same double post -- court portraitist and institutional head -- that the other held in Stockholm. The study writes itself as the mirror: the Hamburger who built Swedish painting from a court appointment, against the Troyen who waited thirty years to take the Paris establishment from his rival. Danzig-born, trained under his uncle Daniel Schultz the Elder, studied in France and the Netherlands 1646-49; private painter to John II Casimir Vasa from 1649, then to Michael Wisniowiecki and John III Sobieski; the leading artist at the Warsaw court in the second half of the 17th century. *Crimean Falconer of King John II Casimir with his Family* (1664, Hermitage); portraits of Polish kings and nobles, the astronomer Johannes Hevelius, animals and hunts; his coffin portrait of John II Casimir now stands in the Marble Room of the Royal Castle in Warsaw among Peter Danckerts de Rij's 22 effigies of the Polish monarchs. His works are held in Stockholm's Nationalmuseum as well as Warsaw, Wawel, the Hermitage and Gdansk -- the Baltic circuit, both shores. The Ehrenstrahl parallel is exact: two Germans painting Vasa kings in northern capitals in the same decades, one in Stockholm, one in Warsaw. Doorway sketch: "Hop from the Ehrenstrahl study: across the Baltic to the other northern court portraitist of the Vasa world -- Daniel Schultz, who painted the Polish Vasas in Warsaw while Ehrenstrahl painted the Swedish Vasas in Stockholm, and whose coffin portrait of John II Casimir now hangs in the Marble Room among Danckerts's 22 kings."
