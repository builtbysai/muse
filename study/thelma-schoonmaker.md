## 1. Aruba to Ridgewood: the diplomat who became an editor

Thelma Colbert Schoonmaker was born 3 January 1940 in Algiers, French Algeria, the daughter of American parents, Thelma and Bertram Schoonmaker; her father was an agent of the Standard Oil Company, working extensively abroad, and the family was evacuated to the United States shortly after the Fall of France in 1940. In 1941 the family moved to Aruba, in the Dutch Caribbean, where her father continued with Standard Oil and her mother ran nursery schools, and she was primarily raised there, in a community she described as "a colony of expatriates from over the world"; she also spent part of her childhood in Portugal. She did not live in the United States until she was an adolescent in 1955, settling in Ridgewood, New Jersey, and graduating from Ridgewood High School in 1957; arriving at fifteen, she said, was "quite a shock... I felt like I was on Mars". [C33372]

She went to Cornell University, 1957 to 1961, studying political science and Russian, and wanted a career in international diplomacy; after graduating she took the State Department tests, passed the exams, and failed the final "stress test" after expressing distaste for South African apartheid -- the State Department, she later said, claimed her views were "too politically liberal". While taking a graduate course in primitive art at Columbia University she saw a New York Times advertisement offering training as an assistant film editor, answered it, and got the job: assisting an "editor" who randomly cut frames from classic European films -- Truffaut, Godard, Fellini -- to fit American television running times. The diplomat manquee had found the cutting room by answering a newspaper ad, and the first films she ever touched were other people's masterpieces, cut down to fit a broadcast slot. [C33373]

**Takeaway:** She did not come to editing through film school or a family trade -- she came through diplomacy refused and a classified ad -- which is why her whole method is practical and untheoretical: she learned by handling footage, not by studying it.

## 2. The six-week course and the rescue

She then signed up for a brief six-week filmmaking course at New York University, and there she met Martin Scorsese, who was struggling to complete his short What's a Nice Girl Like You Doing in a Place Like This? A negative cutter had butchered the film, leaving too few negative frames for hot splicing, and a film professor asked Schoonmaker to help Scorsese rescue it. She went on to edit his feature directorial debut, Who's That Knocking at My Door (1967), and on that film, in her own words, "Marty taught me everything I ever knew about editing" -- how to build a scene between two actors, when to use a close-up, when a medium shot, how long to hold on one actor as opposed to the other, "that way you build character and a dynamic relationship between two actors". [C33374]

The apprenticeship matters because it was not a school but a rescue: she arrived knowing nothing about editing, Scorsese arrived with a film that had been physically damaged, and the two of them learned the work together, on footage that could not be re-shot. "He taught me everything I know, actually. I knew nothing about editing when I met him." The partnership that resulted -- fifty-six years and counting -- began with her saving his film from a bad cut, which is, in miniature, what she has done on every film since: take what was shot, including what went wrong, and make it play as if it had always been meant to. [C33375]

**Takeaway:** The longest editor-director partnership in American film began as triage on a student short -- and the method never changed: she does not impose a cut on the footage, she rescues the film the footage wants to be.

## 3. Woodstock: the documentary school

At NYU she also met filmmaker Michael Wadleigh, and she joined the editing team on his music-festival documentary Woodstock (1970), one of seven credited editors alongside Wadleigh, Scorsese, Stan Warnow, Yeu-Bun Yee and Jere Huggins -- never sole editor, and any study claim must say co-editor. The crew shot eighteen hours a day; Schoonmaker and Wadleigh spent months editing 315,000 feet of exposed film down to three hours. Her use of superimpositions and freeze frames, Wikipedia records, brought the performances to life and helped raise documentary filmmaking's artistry and visibility to a new level -- the split-screen, multi-image collage and the frozen face that would recur, in fiction, for the next fifty years. [C33376]

The Academy nominated her alone for Best Film Editing at the 43rd Academy Awards, 15 April 1971 -- the first documentary ever nominated in the category, a Wikipedia-only claim carried with that flag -- and she was the only member of the editing team nominated. The role label varies by source and is recorded, not settled: BAFTA's 2019 press release says she "supervised the editing" of Woodstock, TV Insider calls her and Scorsese "editorial supervisors", Wikipedia's filmography gives "assistant director and editor". What all agree on is that she was on the editing team and was the sole Oscar nominee for the film's editing, and that a twenty-nine-year-old who had learned to cut three years earlier was now an Academy nominee for a film made from unshaped footage -- the documentary training that would later let her shape two hours of De Niro improvising and two hours of Pesci improvising into a scene. [C33377]

**Takeaway:** Woodstock is the school: 315,000 feet of unshaped footage, seven editors, superimpositions and freeze frames -- and the lesson that a story can be built from footage that was never scripted, which is the skill every Scorsese improvisation would later demand.

## 4. The union wall and the lost decade

Despite the Oscar nomination, she could not work on feature films unless she joined the Motion Picture Editors Guild, and entry required five years as an apprentice and three as an assistant; she refused. "And I just couldn't see why I, who had been a full editor and had been nominated for an Academy Award, should suddenly have to become an apprentice." So she did not work with Scorsese in a formal capacity in the 1970s, though Wikipedia records an uncredited contribution to Taxi Driver (1976) -- Scorsese had saved all editing until after shooting wrapped and was short of time under Columbia's contract, and brought her in to help, with Steven Spielberg also visiting and contributing to the final edit -- and the corroboration for that uncredited work is thin (a single-source magazine claim elsewhere, uncorroborated), so the study carries it as Wikipedia's account, not as a settled credit. During the gap she cut documentaries for WQED in Pittsburgh and a short film for Paul McCartney on a world tour. [C33378]

The reunion came through Marcia Lucas: "Marty called me again after Marcia Lucas, George Lucas's wife, had to stop working for him because the Star Wars films were starting. So he found me through a mutual friend and decided to move back to New York. And that's when we started working on Raging Bull." In the 1980s she was eventually accepted into the union, with some help from Scorsese; how exactly remains unclear even to her -- Fresh Air: "So to this day you don't know how he got you in the union?" / "No. I don't ask." The exact year of admission was not verified by any source read this run. The decade matters because it is the negative space of the partnership: the two of them lost the 1970s to a guild rule, and when they returned, on Raging Bull, they worked as if making up for lost time -- which is one reason the film feels so concentrated, so fought-over, frame by frame. [C33379]

**Takeaway:** The guild rule cost them a decade, and the return on Raging Bull carries that cost in every frame -- two people who had waited ten years to work together again, cutting like it might be taken away.

## 5. Raging Bull: the return and the first Oscar

Raging Bull (1980) won her the Best Film Editing Oscar at the 53rd Academy Awards, 31 March 1981; her acceptance speech thanked Scorsese ("he edited this film with me every minute of the time") and Robert De Niro ("pure gold"). It was voted number one on the Motion Picture Editors Guild's 2012 list of the 75 best-edited films, and it is the richest single case in the sources for how she actually cuts. Scorsese storyboarded the climactic Sugar Ray Robinson fight thoroughly, and then the edit departed from the storyboard when the footage offered something better: when Vickie puts her head down into her hands in the audience, and when she picks her head up, became the key moments to hang the whole construction of the montage around -- "things like that, that you don't expect, occur when you get footage that's been shot, and so you sometimes have to change things a little bit. We did violate the initial structure of the storyboard." One shot even went in upside down -- De Niro's legs buckling, which "just worked better that way". [C33380]

The fights are built from speed and sound as much as from cutting. Scorsese changed speed from 24 to 48 and 120 frames per second mid-shot, in camera; on the final fight he had a rheostat on the camera, so as Sugar Ray steps back the camera slows down and the lights go down, and he breathes in slow motion while the sound editor, Frank Warner, puts in the sound of an animal breathing -- "Taking sound away, often, is much more powerful than bringing it in" -- and then the rheostat brings the camera back up to speed and the lights back up for the final onslaught. Flashbulbs, ninety thousand dollars' worth, go off as cutting devices, and the cut from one flashbulb flare to another makes the transition look extremely smooth. Each fight is a different emotional state, built into the shoot: the first time Jake knocks down Sugar Ray the ring is large and sweeping and brightly lit; when he loses on a technical decision he cannot understand, the ring is filled with smoke and dark and murky, shot with flames below the lens for a mirage-like, queasy feeling. And the film is black and white because Michael Powell, watching colour tests in the cutting room, said "There's something wrong about the red gloves" -- and Scorsese said, "My God, you're right." [C33381]

**Takeaway:** Raging Bull is the proof that her cutting is not assembly but discovery -- the storyboard is the plan, the footage is the truth, and the edit is where the film is found, one upside-down shot and one animal breath at a time.

## 6. The dailies protocol: he talks, she writes

She cuts during production, day for day, not after the shoot -- verified across four sources -- and the single most repeated mechanism in the interviews is the dailies protocol. Every night in dailies Scorsese talks continuously about each take; Schoonmaker writes it all down; the notes plus the script supervisor's notes become the selects; the selects become the first assembly; only then do the two of them cut together. "One of the most important times for me is when he's telling me what he thinks every day during dailies. He's constantly talking to me and I'm writing like the wind what he says about each take. I use that to build my selects and, from my selects, I start cutting." [C33382]

The protocol has not changed since 1967, and it survived even COVID: on Killers of the Flower Moon they could not go to Oklahoma, so they stayed in New York and worked on Zooms to look at the dailies together, discussing each shot as it came along. "From my screenings with Marty, I make extensive selects with notes attached so that Marty and I can always go back and look at an option if we want, so that we're not poring through the entire dailies... I find that really an excellent way to edit." The mechanism matters because it makes the first assembly an act of listening, not of decision: the editor's first job is attention -- writing like the wind while the director talks -- and the cutting starts only after that attention has done its work. Kahn works the same way with Spielberg, which is the point of the parallel: both rooms run on the director's talk and the editor's notes, and both partnerships last because the editor hears the film before she cuts it. [C33383]

**Takeaway:** The edit begins with a pen, not a blade -- she writes down everything he says in dailies, and the film is built from those notes before a single cut is made.

## 7. Cold perspective: stay off the set

She visits the set to watch friends work, but as a rule she does not stay, because knowing how hard a shot was to get would prejudice her judgment of it on screen. "I tend not to like -- I love to go to the set to watch Marty and the actors and the crew, many of whom are old friends... But I do find it prejudices my eye. My job, I think, is to sit in dailies with Scorsese every night, and he wants to know my reaction. Do I believe something? Is there something wrong? Is there something I don't understand? And if I go on the set and people say to me, 'Oh, gosh, wait till you see this shot. We just laid 20 feet of track, and we're going to crane up here and' -- then I don't get the same feeling about it if I see it being made as I do when I see it on the screen." She calls what she protects the "cold perspective": she wants to see only what is on film. [C33384]

On The Irishman she never got to the set at all -- almost everything was shot in or around New York, the production moving all over the place, and she could not afford the time. The rule is the opposite of the romantic image of the editor hovering at the director's elbow: her authority in the cutting room comes precisely from not having been there, from judging the shot as the audience will judge it, with no memory of the track laid or the crane raised. A shot that cost a day to get and does not work on screen is a shot that does not work, and the editor who saw it being made is the last person who can say so honestly. [C33385]

**Takeaway:** She stays away from the set so the footage stays honest -- the cold perspective is the editor's real instrument, and it only works if she never saw the effort behind the frame.

## 8. Read the script once, then put it away

She reads the script (and the book, where there is one) once, then deliberately stops referring to it, because Scorsese routinely shoots things that are not in the script. "I read the script once and then put it away because I really don't need it anymore. What I'm looking for is how Marty is creating the movie from that script, because sometimes a lot of what he does is not in the script. It's what he has in his mind about how he's going to do the camera work or his concept for the editing." The film she cuts is the film in the dailies, not the film on the page. [C33386]

The exception proves the rule, and it happened exactly once: Kundun. That was the only time she was called in before shooting, so she could give suggestions -- she watched monks make a sand mandala in a studio in New York and supplied stills of the images she liked, which Scorsese then photographed for use as transitions, and mandala images recur through the film, particularly when the Dalai Lama is forced to flee. The mandala itself is the image of her method: made grain by grain over two weeks, then washed away in the river -- creation and dissolution, which she cites, via Buddhist concepts, as an influence on her work. [C33387]

**Takeaway:** The script is a starting point she reads once and abandons -- the film is what the dailies show, and the one time she was brought in early, the image she brought back was a mandala: built slowly, then let go.

## 9. Cut on feeling: the gut doctrine

The nearest thing to a stated doctrine, and she never calls it a system. The intellectual conception is Scorsese's -- how a scene is directed; the cutting itself is decided kinetically, by running a passage over and over and feeling whether the hold is too long or too short. "When we're editing it, it's kinetic, it's a feeling from your gut... running it over and over again and feeling, Oh, no, we're waiting too long on the boy here, or not." On the Hugo train-dream sequence that meant thousands of decisions a day -- how long to let it go on, how many times to cut to him, what he should be doing each time, when to show the track shaking, how quickly the engineers react, how long the boy can get away with not jumping off the track as the train approaches -- and "it took a long, long time to really come to exactly the right pace for that". [C33388]

The doctrine has a second half, which is time: "The main thing to remember, always, is that it's just a tool; that the main thing about editing is living with the film -- hopefully being given enough time to live with it, to figure out what it needs. It's so important to live with a film for two or three months, and then suddenly you begin to see something, and you realize what needs to be done to it." First impressions are the instrument and they decay -- "Your first impression of something is very important, and you have to hold on to that when you are editing" -- so her job, as she puts it, is never to get tired, always to keep perspective, and to screen with one other person in the room so she starts seeing the film through somebody else's eyes. Dede Allen told her they were the only editors left who had enough time to live with their movies, and the whole fifty-year run is built on that luxury: time to feel, and time to feel again. Two famous phrases were NOT verified and are not used in this study: "Marty shoots the film, I cut it" appears in no source read, and "I cut on feeling" is a paraphrase -- the verified wording is "it's kinetic, it's a feeling from your gut". [C33389]

**Takeaway:** There is no Schoonmaker system, only a gut and a calendar -- run the passage until the feeling tells you the hold is right, and live with the film for months until it tells you what it needs.

## 10. The duet: we edit it all together

She is explicit, repeatedly, that she gets too much credit: the edit is a duet. "I get way too much credit for the editing on his movies. I keep trying to make people understand that, but they all say, 'Oh yeah! Sure! Right!' But we edit it all together in the same room." The division of labour is precise: Scorsese identifies the takes he likes in dailies, she builds the selects from his talk plus her own feeling, she makes the first assembly alone, and then everything after that is joint. "Marty's favorite part of filmmaking is editing, and he directs the editing after he finishes shooting. I do an assembly based on what he tells me in dailies and what I feel, and then we do all the rest of the editing together." He thinks like an editor when he conceives a film, when he co-writes it, and when he shoots it -- "and that's 50% of my job, y'know? He gives me footage that works." [C33390]

The cutting room itself is a small world: he always has Turner Classic Movies running silent in the corner, and every once in a while he will say, "Oh, look at this great shot." When they disagree -- which is rare -- there is never an ego battle: she first delivers exactly what he asked for, then shows four or five options another way, and they decide together which is better; if it is a major disagreement they screen both versions for trusted friends. The one famous case is Raging Bull's final mirror scene, fifteen takes spanning warm to cold: Scorsese wanted the coldest, take fifteen, Schoonmaker and De Niro preferred a warmer one, they screened it both ways, and "of course, Marty was absolutely right". "We work very compatibly. We're very different. I'm very optimistic and calm and patient, and Marty is emotional and dramatic. We balance each other." [C33391]

**Takeaway:** The partnership works because it is not a division of labour but a duet -- he shoots like an editor, she cuts like a director's second mind, and disagreement is settled by showing, never by arguing.

## 11. The long hold on a face

Her most consistent device across forty years is to stay on the actor past the point a classical editor would cut, and let the performance do the work. Three verified instances, each with the decision described in her words. In Killers of the Flower Moon, Scorsese phoned from the set about DiCaprio's courtroom testimony: "Leo is so brilliant in this first take. I don't want to use anything else. I don't want to even cut to the prosecutor. I want to just hold on him through his testimony." They cut away only twice -- when the prosecutor says "the person he is implicating here is" and swish-pans to De Niro -- and held Mollie's reaction until the end, "and that was a powerful decision". "We hold on the characters and make you feel what they're going through." [C33392]

In Goodfellas, the "What's so funny about me?" scene was shot in medium shots only -- no close-ups at all, because Scorsese did not want to be tempted to use them; the point was to show how the people around Liotta and Pesci were progressively getting more and more freaked out. The hold durations were found by screening variants: "Marty and I experimented for a very long time in exactly how long to hold on Ray Liotta... We waited nine seconds, or we waited six seconds." And in The Wolf of Wall Street, the Quaaludes crawl -- DiCaprio crawling to his car, camera holding in a wide shot for a long time -- had no coverage at all: Schoonmaker worried in dailies, "Did you even do any coverage of him?", and Scorsese said, "Oh no no no. The humor is in the body language, in the wide shot. That's my favorite shot in the movie, and I do not intend to shoot any coverage on it. It will hold, and it will work." At the first screening people were roaring all the way through. Body language, he told her, is almost fifty percent of acting. [C33393]

**Takeaway:** The hold is the edit: stay on the face, stay on the body, refuse the cut that would explain what the performance already says -- and the audience, trusted to look, finds it funnier and more frightening than any cut could make it.

## 12. Cutting on the reaction, not the speaker

A conversation is not shot/reverse-shot ping-pong; the cut goes to the listener whose face is changing. The Goodfellas "funny" scene works because the cut stays on the room, not the talker -- on the people getting progressively more freaked out around Pesci. In Killers of the Flower Moon, DiCaprio realising he must agree with De Niro's lie about the private investigator is played in looks, not dialogue: "The looks on his face for 2 or 3 cuts after that are terrific, because he says everything just in the look, with no dialog." And the first Ernest/Hale scene is built from De Niro's silences: "The one important thing there was to wait sometimes before De Niro spoke. You see him looking. He's analyzing his nephew... That was the way we worked on the rhythm of that scene, to make sure that we sometimes paused for a few seconds more than we normally would." [C33394]

The limit of the craft is stated plainly: "An editor can not make a bad film good. You can only go so far." Performance first, cut second -- and the corollary she repeats to students is that you can make an actor look not as good or even better depending on how you edit their footage, which is why the job is wonderful: "You get to shape things and build drama, build rhythm, build pacing." The Raging Bull mirror scene is the same mechanism at its purest: fifteen takes, warm to cold, and the choice between them IS the edit. [C33395]

**Takeaway:** Cut to the face that is listening, not the mouth that is talking -- the reaction is where the scene actually happens, and the pause before the line is where the rhythm lives.

## 13. Freeze frames: his script, her execution

The freeze frame is Scorsese's device, not hers, and she is explicit that he designs it in advance, including writing it into the script -- which corrects the common attribution that credits the editor. "The script of Goodfellas is so tight we only dropped one thing... the freeze frames are, by the way. Marty had put in the script exactly where to freeze the frame. That is such brilliant directorial and editing thinking, which is why he is a wonderful editing director, because he thinks like an editor when he's shooting." The mechanism as she describes it is a scripted full stop, placed where the narration needs a beat: freeze frames introduce characters and mark key moments in Henry's narration, and the script was so solidly constructed that even the freezes were written in. [C33396]

No statement by Schoonmaker was found claiming the freeze frame as her own invention or giving a doctrine for when to freeze, and the study does not invent one. What her account proves is larger than the device: the editing is already happening in the script and on the set, before she ever touches a frame, because the director is thinking like an editor. Her job is to execute a freeze that was designed before the camera rolled -- and to know, when the footage arrives, whether the scripted freeze still earns its place. The same honesty runs through the whole study: the famous devices belong to the partnership, not to either half of it, and she is the one who keeps saying so. [C33397]

**Takeaway:** The freeze frame is written before it is cut -- the director thinks like an editor, the editor executes like a director, and the credit belongs to the room, not to a name.

## 14. Jump cuts and violating the structure

When a scene will not land, she violates its structure. In The Irishman, the montage where De Niro repeatedly throws guns into the river after he has killed someone "took some time to get right. It was very normal at first -- and then we started violating the structure and jump cutting and shortening until we got the right feeling. It was fun." The odd jump cuts in The Irishman generally were made, she says, in order to get the best performance, and were worth it: they added to the film's deceptive simplicity. The jump cut here is not style for its own sake but a tool for finding a feeling the straight cut could not reach. [C33398]

The same device runs back to the beginning of the partnership. In Raging Bull, LaMotta's home-movie sequences -- the only colour in the film -- are cut with jump cuts and "helter skelter editing", a deliberate roughness against the black-and-white polish of the fights. And in The Aviator, the screening-room madness of Howard Hughes was cut the same way: she lived with different edits for a long time and finally went "with a very kinetic, emotional approach to it and just slashing into the footage and picking out of it just what made me feel something for Howard and putting it together not in a linear way, but just an emotional way. So when that suddenly happened, it was great. Something just clicked, and we knew we were finished with it." The cut is allowed to break its own rules when the feeling demands it -- and the feeling, not the rule, is what tells her she is finished. [C33399]

**Takeaway:** Structure is a starting position, not a law -- when the scene will not land, violate it: jump-cut, shorten, slash into the footage, and stop when the feeling clicks.

## 15. Comedy beats: two or three seconds

Comedy cutting is a real skill, and she learned it on The King of Comedy (1982), from the timing instructions Jerry Lewis was giving to other actors. "Comedy cutting is a real skill. I learned a lot when we were doing The King Of Comedy, from the timing instructions that Jerry Lewis was giving to other actors. The slight beat, maybe two or three seconds, before you answer a question, or the beat you put in the middle of a line, is all where comedy lies." The same beat logic carries straight into The Wolf of Wall Street thirty years later: the pause before the answer, the beat in the middle of the line, the two or three seconds that make the line land. [C33400]

After Hours (1985) taught the other half of the lesson, speed: "We found that the faster we moved, the funnier it got" -- Scorsese on cutting After Hours with Schoonmaker, matching whip-pans and sped-up footage with lively quick-fire editing, and dropping forty-five minutes of wonderful improvised scenes from the assembly, including, she says, her favourite scenes and Marty's favourite scene. And the Raging Bull kitchen scene, two hours of De Niro improvising and two hours of Pesci improvising, holds a comic beat inside the same mechanism: Theresa Saldana's unscripted "She didn't mean nothing" and Pesci's "Who asked you?" work because "the timing of the head turn is so wonderful, the pause before the line is so perfect. Of course, comic lines are built out of beats. You know, waiting just a moment or two can make the line funnier." Speed and the beat are the same skill at two tempos: know when to run, know when to wait. [C33401]

**Takeaway:** Comedy lives in the beat -- two or three seconds before the answer, a pause before the line, or the whole film run faster -- and the editor's job is to find which tempo the joke wants.

## 16. Music: audition, never temp

Music is Scorsese's choice, always; her job is trying the candidates against the cut until one "pops". "Marty chooses the music. Marty has one of the greatest gifts for putting music to a movie out of anybody, I think. Sometimes on a film like Casino, we would have six or seven pieces of rock and roll that he wanted to use in each scene. We would try each one against the first cut of the scene and usually one popped out. One would just fit perfectly. But it's his design and he comes up with what to even play. I am part of putting it to the scenes and seeing if it works." On Casino they had walls covered with options for each scene, and would play seven pieces against the cut -- "let's say approaching a first rough cut -- and usually one would click. It would just feel right." The montage is built by auditioning songs, not by cutting to a beat she chose. [C33402]

The refusal that goes with it is the strongest in any source read: temp tracks are "absolutely anathema. We would never do that. It's quite common for movies to be edited to temp tracks, but not with us. Oh no, no, no. Marty has such an incredible ear for music, it would be anathema to both of us to use a piece of music that's not going to be in the final film, because it affects the film so much." And music must not tell the audience what to think: "Marty doesn't want to tell the audience what to think. That's one of his big no-nos. And if you use music to tell the audience what to think, it weakens it, we feel. So he's often going against the grain with the music. It's not what you would expect." The same principle, from Michael Powell: "Never explain and always try to be ahead of your audience. Respect them. They are probably ahead of you, so you try and be ahead of them." On Killers of the Flower Moon the Robbie Robertson score worked the same way -- the throbbing bass line, sent as a great deal of material, structured by Scorsese until the oil-burst theme hit right away: "I think this could be our theme," and it was. [C33403]

**Takeaway:** Never cut to music that will not be in the film, and never let music tell the audience what to think -- audition until one track pops, then get out of its way.

## 17. Sound as half the edit

Sound is half the edit, and sometimes its absence is the whole edit. On Raging Bull she ends up spending about a quarter of her time talking to students about what sound effects editor Frank Warner did for the film: a very simple drum, played by Warner himself, distorted, played backwards, put in "in this astonishing, subtle way" throughout the film, plus animal sounds -- a horse shuddering, an elephant braying -- standing in for punching sounds, and the animal breathing in the Sugar Ray pause. "They're only violent after I get done with them," she says of the fight scenes: all the technical work of the stunt people and the design of the shot, and then how it is edited, is what makes one feel the violence. [C33404]

The Irishman states the stripped-down version of the same doctrine: Scorsese did not even want amplified sound effects -- when people walk across a room, the sound editors usually create effects for that, and add them in, and Marty did not want those -- "So that was a big adjustment for all of us. But a great one." And Silence (2016) opens with sound as editing: permission was got to remove the sound for all the production logos, so at first you hear hardly anything -- maybe a little wind over the logos, very low -- and then you realise it is the sound of insects, cicadas, building until the logos end, and then a sharp cut to black, the cicadas continuing to build over black, and then the cicadas cutting off suddenly as the title "SILENCE" appears on the screen, with no sound at all -- dead silence. The pace experiment behind it -- "just how slow it could be, without losing the audience" -- is carried on PostPerspective's account; the deeper Quietus interview on Silence was read only at excerpt level this run, and the study does not cite it as a full read. [C33405]

**Takeaway:** Sound is not laid over the cut, it IS the cut -- a drum, an animal breath, a removed footstep, cicadas cut to dead silence -- and taking sound away is often more powerful than bringing it in.

## 18. The digital turn: Lightworks, not Avid

The brief for this study assumed she cuts on Avid. She does not, and the correction matters, because the tool changed the method. She made the switch to digital on Casino (1995), ordered onto it by the producer, and was, in her own words, "a very bad girl" about it: "I didn't want to do it and the producer told me I had to do it, and I was very grumpy. I had a wonderful trainer who came to work with me and he made me put a quarter in a jar every time I said, 'I could do this easier on film.' Then finally about two weeks in I just clicked in and I was off, because it is faster." The system was Lightworks, an English system, and she still uses it through Killers of the Flower Moon -- because it retains qualities of flatbed editing, with a controller device similar to what they used on the flatbed, and because its sync handling is different from every other NLE: if you go out of sync, Lightworks gives you a red icon with the number of frames you are out, and you choose where to correct it. [C33406]

What digital unlocked was fearlessness. "I experiment a lot more now. I can make four edits for Marty of a scene instead of one just by copying it. In a second I can copy it. On film I used to have to, if I wanted to show him a different edit, I'd have to take it apart, hang it in the bin, remember how I did it, re cut it and then if he didn't like that go back and remember how I had constructed it before. So now I'm fearless about improvising and coming up with different ideas, which is great." A dissolve that once meant sending the film to an optical house and waiting a week can now be changed fifteen different ways in a second; she can copy an edit in one second, slash into the copy, turn the scene upside down, put the beginning at the end, and try anything that kinetically seems worth trying. The cost is real, and she names it: she loves cutting on film and always will, Scorsese never quite learned the digital format and can no longer cut a scene himself the way he used to -- "he's a great editor -- and now he can't" -- and film is a much better preservation element than digital, which is fragile and sometimes just disappears, which is why their movies are backed up on film and magnetic tape. [C33407]

**Takeaway:** The tool is not the method, but the tool changed what the method could risk -- four versions where film allowed one, and fearlessness bought with a quarter in a jar.

## 19. The long films: shaving, not gutting

The Wolf of Wall Street (2013) arrived as a four-hour first cut -- never a screened assembly, she insists; the first cut was a four-hour edit -- and had to be shaved to three hours, very carefully, "not losing whole scenes, but just shaving it down. And it was working just as well at three as it was at four." She keeps every version digitally: the 4:00 edit, the 3:50 edit, all of them. The structure was never the problem -- "the film stayed basically in the same structure it always was" -- but the rhythm was new: "this rush and then stop, and then rush and then stop", the opening designed shot by shot to cut to each shot, and then a brilliant improvised scene like the lunch with Mark Hanna where you have to stop and relish it. A two-page scene became five minutes long. [C33408]

The Irishman (2019) was the easy long film, and the contrast is the lesson: only twenty minutes dropped -- "quite unusual for us. Usually we drop 40 or an hour" -- a year to edit, the structure already there in Steve Zaillian's script, no restructuring, only a few minor scenes dropped, and the length never planned and never a problem: screened just the two of them at first, they could see the movie was long but that it worked at that length, with "a very long, slow build to the end of the movie", and an intermission was considered and rejected because it would have broken the build. Killers of the Flower Moon (2023) went the other way: about a four-hour assembly, about twelve versions, screen and recut and screen again with a small invited audience debriefed after each, "sometimes we don't agree with what they say". The big change was the love story, decided when DiCaprio switched from the FBI man to Ernest -- "They were still working on that as they were shooting" -- and the editing moved scenes around the love story until it became the central force of the film. "It's a big story, and it needed the length. Most people tell me they have no problem at all with it." [C33409]

**Takeaway:** A long film is not a long problem -- Wolf was shaved, Irishman was already built, Killers was rebuilt around a love story -- and the length is right when the build holds, not when the clock says so.

## 20. De-aging and the wrinkle rule

The Irishman's de-aging was done the way everything in her cutting room is done: picture lock first, then the effect, so no money was wasted on shots that would be cut. "We screened a lot without them being de-aged, because we want to lock the picture first and then de-age so we didn't waste money on shots we would be removing. It would be terrible to have all the costs of de-aging and then throw [the shot] out." After lock, ILM needed about seven months for the "youthifying" -- a process Pablo Helman invented because Scorsese said his actors were not going to work with a helmet, dots, and two chin cameras. [C33410]

The wrinkle rule is the whole doctrine in miniature: de-aging must not erase the performance. "Sometimes, if you take all the wrinkles out of his face, you lose some of his vulnerability, and his vulnerability was very important in this movie. So several times we would say, 'No, no, no, we've lost it. Go back, put back in a few of the wrinkles -- not all of them,' and then we would get his acting back." The notes to ILM were that specific: "Take this wrinkle out, and put another one in." The technology exists to make De Niro young; the edit exists to make sure he is still De Niro. It is the same judgment as the long hold and the cold perspective: the performance is the film, and everything else -- cameras, effects, schedules -- serves it or gets cut. [C33411]

**Takeaway:** Lock the picture before you spend the money, and put the wrinkles back -- a face with no age in it has no performance left to cut.

## 21. Intercutting invented in the edit

Killers of the Flower Moon holds two intercuttings that were not in the script, both invented in the cutting room, and both show what the edit can add that the shoot never planned. The first: the Tribal Council speech and the autopsy. Everett Waller's speech was unscripted -- Scorsese was going to shoot the scripted council scene, took a heat break, asked Waller to "talk", then decided to film it -- and in the edit they decided to make the speech into two parts, using the autopsy and Mollie's incredible reaction to it as a break: "it would be more powerful, and it was Marty's idea to think of using the autopsy and Molly's incredible reaction to it as a way to make a break for a moment." The second: the field-burning flames, which Scorsese saw driving home from the set one day, intercut with the investigation and Mollie's sickness -- "We start out crisp and then go deeper and deeper into a beautiful distortion of the image... And I think it's sort of like the fires of hell that the part played by DiCaprio is becoming drunker and drunker." [C33412]

The smaller inventions are the same method at tabletop scale. The lantern transition -- a maid blowing out a lantern before the Ernest/Mollie dinner scene -- was placed by trial: "That was a choice we made. We could have put it any place in the sequence and we tried it various places, but the reason it ended up there is because they're going to talk about her mother..." The private-investigator scene was rebuilt so the beating is shown, left, and returned to, so the audience sees Ernest take the bribe money the second time -- "we decided to do that to make clear -- to put closer together -- the scene where you see Ernest, played by DiCaprio, take the money from the man." And the early Mollie voiceover tableaux -- an overhead shot, Mollie's voice over men and women who have died with no investigation of it as a criminal act -- "That was kind of a daring thing we did with Mollie's voice there. We haven't seen her yet. We don't know who she is. Hearing a woman's voice over these men who have died... hearing that female voice is such a shock." None of it was scripted. All of it was found by living with the film. [C33413]

**Takeaway:** The edit is not where the script is executed but where the film is invented -- a speech split by an autopsy, flames found on a drive home, a lantern moved until it lands where the mother is about to be mentioned.

## 22. Restraint: the late style

The arc of the fifty years ends in restraint, and Schoonmaker says so herself, film by film. Hugo (2011) is "not classical editing at all": in the train nightmare the boy does not react even though the rail beside him is shaking, and finally he looks over, but slowly -- he does not jump up and run off the track -- and the cut has to give the audience "this very slow, strange... something's wrong here", delaying the reaction far past the classical pattern until it dawns on you that this is not real. "We fooled around with it for a long time." The Age of Innocence (1993) was the first deliberate slowing -- "We certainly had to slow down and figure out how to reach a pace that reflected nineteenth century New York" -- and Silence (2016) is the pace experiment carried furthest: "We experimented a great deal with just how slow it could be, without losing the audience", a slowness appropriate to the seventeenth century, "far closer to The Age of Innocence in terms of its pacing". [C33414]

The Irishman states the late doctrine in two words, "deceptive simplicity": "The violence needed to be very stripped down, with no flashy editing, no flashy camera moves. It's very quick, and shot in a very ordinary way. No wide shots, not a thousand different cuts and crane shots... Here he wanted to show the banality of it." And Killers of the Flower Moon is "a simpler style than some of the other movies he's made in the past", long scenes, a dinner scene that starts as a little joust and gradually lets you feel the attraction building, pauses of "a few seconds more than we normally would" before De Niro speaks. The Wolf of Wall Street (2013) is the exception that proves the turn: a deliberate return to ferocious improv cutting, but now with digital fearlessness, a four-hour cut shaved carefully, held wide shots with no coverage. The arc ends where it began -- holding on a face -- but now the hold is a choice made after fifty years of knowing when NOT to cut. Her own explicit contrast: in Raging Bull the final mirror scene took fifteen takes, warm to cold; in The Irishman, De Niro had been thinking about Frank Sheeran for so long that they often used the first take. [C33415]

**Takeaway:** The late style is the early style with the showing-off removed -- hold longer, cut less, let the face do it -- and the proof is that the first take now does what fifteen takes used to do.

## 23. The films that are not Scorsese's

Non-Scorsese features are rare, and the study is honest about how thin the record is for them: six feature editing credits outside Scorsese, all credit-only in the sources read, with no editing observation found for any of them. The Virgin President (1968, Graeme Ferguson), co-edited with Mark Rappaport and Burt Rashby; Grace of My Heart (1996, Allison Anders), co-edited with James Y. Kwei and Harvey Rosenstock, her clearest verified non-Scorsese feature; Learning to Drive (2014, Isabel Coixet), co-edited with Keith Reamer; Bombay Velvet (2015, Anurag Kashyap), co-edited with Prerna Saigal; The Snowman (2017, Tomas Alfredson), co-edited with Claire Simpson; and Woodstock (1970), the co-edit that started it. She also edited the Michael Jackson short Bad (1987), and was special consultant -- not editor -- on The Kids Are Alright (1979), which was edited by Ed Rothkowitz; the study does not cite it as an editing credit. She was executive producer, not editor, on Letters from Baghdad (2016) and Made in England: The Films of Powell and Pressburger (2024). [C33416]

The thinness is itself evidence: she works almost exclusively for Scorsese, by choice, and the few exceptions prove the rule rather than breaking it. "I think I have the best job in the world," she says, and the career is the proof -- a sixty-year practice built inside one partnership, with the outside credits clustered at the beginning (1968, 1970) and a small handful later, when the partnership could spare her. What Happens at Night, the next Scorsese feature, is in post-production as this study is written -- shot February to May 2026, Schoonmaker editing, Rodrigo Prieto shooting -- so the partnership is still running, and the count of Scorsese features (23 as of February 2019, per BAFTA) is already out of date, with no current total sourced. The study does not assert one. [C33417]

**Takeaway:** The outside credits are the exception that defines the rule -- she has the best job in the world, and the best job in the world is cutting one director's films for fifty years.

## 24. Powell: the marriage and the restorations

She was introduced to Michael Powell by Scorsese and London-based film producer Frixos Constantine, and married him on Saturday 19 May 1984 -- she was 44, he was 78 -- until his death from cancer on Monday 19 February 1990, at home in Avening, Gloucestershire, aged 84. No children. Powell asked her to put "film director and optimist" on his gravestone, and she did. She has called it the happiest ten years of her life -- her own words, in the BAFTA Fellowship speech, likely counting from when they met, since 1984 to 1990 is under six years, and the study quotes her as spoken rather than correcting her. Powell gave Scorsese the Raging Bull black-and-white idea ("There's something wrong with the red gloves") and the After Hours ending (Paul must finish back at work), so the marriage sits inside the editing room, not beside it: the man she married is the man whose eye changed her two most argued-over decisions. [C33418]

Since his death she has dedicated herself to preserving Powell's films and the Powell-Pressburger legacy, working with Scorsese's Film Foundation on restorations -- fundraising for The Red Shoes spearheaded by Scorsese and his longtime editor, Powell's widow -- and to getting Powell's diaries published, and she executive-produced Made in England: The Films of Powell and Pressburger (2024). Her five favourite films of all time, in an A.Frame interview cited by Wikipedia, are The Red Shoes, The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp, Faces, The Flowers of St. Francis, and Raging Bull -- two of them her husband's, one of them her own. She cites Buddhist concepts, particularly the mandala -- creation and dissolution of an artwork -- as an influence on her work, and the mandala is the image the whole study keeps returning to: built grain by grain, then washed away. [C33419]

The honours around the career, for the record: nine Oscar nominations and three wins (Raging Bull 1981, The Aviator 2005, The Departed 2007), the record nomination total and a tied record win total shared with Ralph Dawson, Daniel Mandell and Michael Kahn; eleven BAFTA nominations and two wins (Raging Bull 1982, Goodfellas 1991) plus the BAFTA Fellowship on 10 February 2019; four ACE Eddie wins from ten nominations, including the historic 2007 tie with Babel, plus the ACE Career Achievement Award on 27 January 2017; one Emmy nomination (2004); the BFI Fellowship 1997 (Wikipedia only, no second source read); the Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement at Venice 2014; and the IBC International Honour for Excellence, presented Sunday 14 September 2025 at the RAI Amsterdam. Three of her films sit on the Motion Picture Editors Guild's 75 best-edited list: Raging Bull first, Goodfellas fifteenth, Hugo sixty-ninth. [C33420]

**Takeaway:** The marriage lasted under six years and the restoration work has lasted thirty-five -- she married the films as much as the man, and she has spent the longer half of her life keeping them alive.

## Aesthetic logic: the cut that holds, and the cut that refuses

Put the mechanisms together and a single logic appears: Schoonmaker's editing is a discipline of attention, not of display. The dailies protocol means the film is heard before it is cut; the cold perspective means it is judged as the audience will judge it; the gut doctrine means the decision is felt, not reasoned; the duet means the credit is shared and the disagreement is shown, not argued; the long hold means the audience is trusted to look where it wants; the reaction cut means the scene happens in the listener's face; the Lightworks fearlessness means four versions can be tried where film allowed one; and the wrinkle rule means the performance is the film and everything else serves it. The invisibility critics praise is the sum of those disciplines -- a cut you do not see because every step that produced it was an act of attention, and attention, paid well, leaves no trace. [C33421]

The second half of the logic is that the system can be refused, and the refusal is itself a statement. The Copacabana entrance refuses the cut by staying whole; the Goodfellas "funny" scene refuses the close-up by never shooting one; the Wolf crawl refuses coverage by holding the wide shot; the Killers trial refuses every cut but two; the Irishman refuses amplified footsteps, flashy editing, and an intermission; Silence refuses sound itself at the title. A style that can be refused is a style that is owned, and Schoonmaker owns hers the way a documentary editor owns it -- by knowing exactly what the cut would do, and choosing, scene by scene, whether to make it. [C33422]

The contradictions in the record are part of the logic, not failures of it. The Oscar count is 7 in 2019, 8 in 2020, 9 in 2024, and all three numbers were right when they were printed; the Woodstock credit is assistant director and editor, editorial supervisor, and supervised the editing, depending on who is writing; the marriage lasted under six years and she calls it the happiest ten years of her life; the freeze frames are hers to execute and his to design; she gets way too much credit and she is the most decorated editor alive. A career built on feeling rather than doctrine is documented the way feeling documents things -- approximately, with the important facts exact (the birth day, the nine nominations, the three wins, the fifty-six-year partnership, the Lightworks controller) and the edges soft. The study carries the conflicts rather than settling them, because settling them would require sources this run did not have, and inventing a settlement would be worse than the gap. [C33423]

**Takeaway:** She does not cut films -- she pays attention to them, one take at a time, until the attention itself becomes the cut, and the proof of the method is that after sixty years the audience still cannot see it working -- only feel it, in the hold that lasts one second longer than comfort allows.
