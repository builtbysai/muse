**Relevance:** Wigdor investigates what the hand knows before the screen answers -- how much of an interaction's meaning lives in the contact itself (pressure, area, approach, hover, which hand, which finger) rather than in the picture the contact produces. His evidence is measured, not asserted: a touch prototype that answers in 1 millisecond against commercial devices at 50-200 ms; dragging performance that keeps improving as latency falls, with no floor down to 1 ms; a tap predicted 128 ms before the finger lands, to within 1.05 cm; a per-contact visualisation that cut errors from 46.9% to 19.4%. Alongside the measurements he built the instruments that turn a sketch into a specification -- DataInk, where one drawn glyph becomes a population bound to data -- and the systems that read the hand itself: a fist that pins, a rail that constrains, a texture that switches the action. Those are computable questions with visible answers: a finger that trails its object by a visible gap; a highlight that blooms before the touch lands; a ghost bridge between two fingers of one hand and none between two people. That is the question a generative practice needs from the other side of Chevalier's: not what the eye follows once a picture moves, but what the hand says before the picture exists, and how the system should listen. [C35575]

## The question: what the hand knows before the picture answers

Wigdor's programme has one question under it, asked for twenty years in different hardware: what does the hand already know, and how fast can the system hear it? The answers come back as numbers, and the numbers are the point. A commercial touchscreen of 2012 took 50-200 ms from touch to display update; his demonstrator answered in 1 ms, and users could reliably perceive latency as low as 5-10 ms, with preference still improving well below 10 ms. On dragging there is no performance floor: performance keeps improving as latency falls to 1 ms, unlike mouse input, where a floor had been reported. Tapping is different from dragging -- users cannot perceive tapping latency below about 24 ms, while dragging latency is perceived down to about 2 ms, because the finger itself is a zero-latency position reference that exposes the lagging on-screen object. The just-noticeable differences make the asymmetry precise: on a touchscreen, 11 ms for dragging and 69 ms for tapping; on a touchpad, 55 ms and 96 ms. Even an 8.3 ms improvement is noticeable across a wide range of baseline latencies. The hand is not a pointer. It is a sensor with its own clock, and the system is late by a measurable amount.

**Takeaway:** Latency is not a delay. In dragging it is a visible gap between fingertip and object, and the gap is the percept. Any piece built on Wigdor must treat the finger-object gap as a drawable quantity, not as a technical footnote. [C35578]

## A life in input: from TiltText to AXL

Wigdor joined the University of Toronto as an undergraduate in 1998 (Wikipedia only) and never really left the question he started with. His M.Sc. thesis (2004, "Chording and tilting for rapid, unambiguous text entry to mobile phones") is TiltText: press a 12-key phone key and tilt the phone in one of four directions to pick which letter on that key -- left for the first, forward for the second, right for the third, back for the fourth, no tilt for the digit. Ten participants, 640 phrases each over about five hours: TiltText was 23% faster than MultiTap by the end (22% in the thesis abstract -- both figures are carried, not resolved), with a higher error rate (9% vs 3%). The Ph.D. (2008, "The Design of Table-Centric Interactive Spaces", sole advisor Balakrishnan, de facto advisor Chia Shen at MERL, where most of the work was done) is the tabletop years: Under the Table Interaction (UIST 2006), LucidTouch (UIST 2007), the bimanual pen-and-touch work (AVI 2008). A Harvard Initiative in Innovative Computing fellowship (2007-2008) overlapped the end of the Ph.D., and a Microsoft period followed -- User Experience Architect in the Entertainment & Devices division (2008-2010), the architect of the Microsoft Surface Table's user experiences, then Microsoft Research (2010) and an affiliate assistant professorship at the University of Washington (2010-2012) -- before the return to Toronto as assistant professor in January 2011. [C35579]

## The latency programme: one millisecond and no floor

The latency arc is the spine of the study, and it is the most completely measured thread in Wigdor's work. Designing for Low-Latency Direct-Touch Input (UIST 2012) built the 1 ms demonstrator -- a low-latency sensor plus a high-speed projector plus custom processing, no standard OS stack -- and proposed the hybrid that matters for any piece: render a low-fidelity visual response immediately, then replace it with the high-fidelity response at standard latency. The two layers are the mechanism. How Fast is Fast Enough? (CHI 2013) varied latency from 1 to 50 ms on dragging and found performance falling monotonically with no floor, worse for smaller and farther targets, and analysed the coping mechanisms users adopt. How Much Faster is Fast Enough? (CHI 2015, Honorable Mention) unified direct and indirect touch with the just-noticeable differences above. Zero-Latency Tapping (UIST 2014) stopped making the sensor faster and instead predicted the touch: from a corpus of 3D finger-movement trajectories through the air, a model with three granularities at different phases of the approach (initial direction, final touch location, time of touchdown) predicts the landing point with a mean accuracy of 1.05 cm, 128 ms before touchdown, validated for targets at least 25.5 cm away -- enough for the application to pre-buffer its response so the tap appears to have zero latency. The paper distinguishes apparent latency (time to soft feedback, a button depressing) from hard feedback (the application actually responding), and proposes soft feedback as the thing to show early: a provisional response, retracted if the prediction is wrong. [C35581]

Underneath the studies sits the sensor that made them possible. High Rate, Low-Latency Multi-Touch Sensing with Simultaneous Orthogonal Multiplexing (UIST 2014) -- the Tactual Labs "Fast Multi-Touch" (FMT) sensor, read in full -- drives all rows simultaneously, each with its own orthogonal signal: a comb of sinusoids spaced 163 kHz apart from 5.371 to 10.091 MHz, with direct-sequence spread-spectrum modulation to avoid interference. Each column receiver uses a matched filter / FFT to measure how much of every row signal is present, producing a 2D heat map of signal strength; post-processing flattens the field, finds local maxima, and fits a paraboloid to the neighbourhood to interpolate sub-pixel touch points, and the Moore fit's cross-term yields the touch ellipse's aspect ratio and tilt -- the finger angle, read from the shape of the contact. The demonstrator runs at 4,000 Hz with 40-microsecond touch-to-data latency and about 110-microsecond touch-to-light latency through a high-speed DLP projector, measured with a piezo tap sensor and a photodiode. The paper closes by proposing a "Touch Turing Test": whether a virtual interaction can be made indistinguishable from a real one. Hammer Time! (CHI 2016) is the instrument for that test at the cheap end: an electrically actuated brass contact about fingertip size triggers a capacitive touch, a photodiode watches the screen for the brightness change of the visual response, and a microcontroller times the interval, with a design goal of accuracy and repeatability within 1 ms -- near the lower bound of human latency perception -- so large latency datasets can be collected with no human in the loop. [C35582]

**Takeaway:** The latency programme is a complete instrument, not a single finding: a sensor that reads the finger's shape and angle at 4,000 Hz, a model that predicts the landing 128 ms early, a two-layer feedback rule that lies fast and tells the truth slow, and a brass finger that measures the whole loop. A piece can use any one layer, but it should know the other three exist. [C35583]

## Ripples: every contact gets its own visible state

Ripples (UIST 2009, read in full) names the problem the latency programme leaves behind: touch removes the mouse pointer, so a failed tap has many indistinguishable causes -- was it received, misread, ignored, or did the finger slip? The answer is a system-level visualisation layer drawn around every contact point, on top of any application, without modifying the application. Six visualisations span fourteen states and transitions: a shrinking circle when a contact captures an object versus an outward splash when it does not; a trail behind a moving contact that makes lag look like a design element; a lift-off animation that contracts to the exact hit-test point so users learn their finger-to-point mapping over time; tethers that show when a contact has slipped off a control it still holds, or when a stationary object on the table (debris) is slowing a list scroll. A thirteen-participant study found errors fell from 46.9% to 19.4% per trial with Ripples on, the gain largest for the smallest targets, and 62% of participants preferred it. The mechanism is a per-contact state machine -- contact down, recognised, rejected, slipped off, at a manipulation limit -- and the visualisation updates on every transition, reporting the system's reading of the contact, not the application's. [C35584]

**Takeaway:** Ripples is the most directly piece-able mechanism in Wigdor's work: the touch itself becomes the drawing, and each finger's history is legible in its mark. No other study in the chain gives the audience's own fingers a visible biography. [C35585]

## DataInk: the sketch is the specification

DataInk (CHI 2018, Paper 223, Honorable Mention -- not CHI 2017 as the task brief said; the 2017 date is wrong and must not be carried forward) closes the gap between sketching, which is fluid and expressive but draws every data point by hand, and visualisation tools, which are rigorous but rigid. The user sketches a glyph freehand with a pen -- a dog, a tree, a mark -- taps it, and binds it to data dimensions; the system instantly duplicates the drawing into one glyph per data point and spreads them on the canvas. Three mechanisms inside it, each separately usable. First, design-by-example: one hand-made mark, repeated with variation, reads as a population rather than a chart, and rebinding a variable re-renders all copies at once while redrawing the prototype re-renders all copies in the new style. Second, the layout is an object you draw: the user sketches a distribution path on the canvas and binds a data dimension to it, so the glyphs arrange along the drawn path in data order, and redrawing the path re-maps the layout -- the path is both the composition and the control. Third, the mapping works in both directions at both levels: start from a visual property and try data dimensions, or start from a data dimension and try encodings; work at the data-point level (design by example on the canvas) or at the data-dimension level (generative rules in the legend), and an edit at either level reaches the other -- touching a legend entry lights up its population, redrawing one member rewrites the rule for its siblings. A study with eight designers and non-experts found the tool supports direct, fluid, and flexible authoring of expressive visualisations. [C35586]

The two companion systems sharpen the same idea from different sides. Object-Oriented Drawing (CHI 2016) makes attributes into graspable things: colour, opacity, and line weight are not menu values but objects on the canvas that can be moved, cloned, and linked -- dragging an attribute object onto a drawing object sets that attribute, cloning copies its value, linking attribute objects makes them share a value so changing one changes all linked. Collection Objects (CHI 2017) unifies selection, grouping, and manipulation into a single object: a lassoed aggregate becomes a persistent container that can be moved, scaled, and rotated as one while the objects inside stay directly accessible, and multiple selection-action cycles chain into one fluid workflow instead of restarting after each action. Both were read at abstract level only; the nesting of collections and the exact quasi-mode rules of Collection Objects were not verified beyond the abstract. [C35587]

**Takeaway:** DataInk is the sketch-as-specification system the #817 Kazi study approached from the drawing side: Kazi's sketch is a crowd, a force field, a family of forms; Wigdor's sketch is a binding, and the binding is inspectable from both ends. The one-to-many explosion -- one stroke becoming a field -- is the visual event, and the data is the choreography. [C35588]

## Rock & Rails: the second hand is a tool

Direct-touch manipulation is fast and intuitive but cannot isolate degrees of freedom, so precise layout work -- rotate only, scale in one axis only -- is hard. Rock & Rails (CHI 2011) lets one hand form a recognised shape on the surface while the other hand does the ordinary two-finger translate, rotate, and scale. A fist ("rock") pins the object down; a straight hand edge acts as a rail constraining movement; a curved hand sets a pivot. The constraint is a visible body: a straight hand edge on the glass is a ruler, a fist is a pin, and the object slides along the rail or pivots around the fist, so the geometry of the hand is legible in the geometry of the motion. Detection is by contact shape -- the blob pattern of the hand on the surface -- not by a menu or a handle, so there is no ambiguity with the direct manipulation itself, and placing or lifting the shape hand switches the constraint on or off mid-gesture without breaking the manipulation. The paper names on-demand gain control as part of the set, though the exact gain values were not in the excerpts read. Eight expert graphic designers evaluated the set and found it easy to learn and effective for precise layout. [C35589]

Plane, Ray, and Point (UIST 2019) is the 3D descendant: in VR, users create three kinds of shape constraints with symbolic gestures -- a plane the object slides on, a ray it runs along, a point it hangs from -- and objects that collide with or intersect a constraint are limited and guided by it, with the other hand adding further degree-of-freedom control. The scaffolding is drawn in space and the objects visibly obey it. The symbolic gestures that create each constraint type were not read beyond the abstract, and a piece should use Rock & Rails or Plane, Ray, and Point, not both -- the constraint-by-hand-shape is one mechanism in two dimensions, not two mechanisms. [C35590]

**Takeaway:** The second hand is not a second pointer. It is a ruler, a pin, or a pivot, and the shape it makes is the command. That is a cleaner idea than any on-screen handle, and it is the one Wigdor mechanism that needs two simultaneous touches to exist at all. [C35591]

## The back of the glass and the finger that reads texture

Two systems invert the usual arrangement of finger, surface, and screen, from opposite directions. LucidTouch (UIST 2007) moves touch input to the back of a mobile device so the fingers no longer occlude the small screen, and restores the lost visual link with pseudo-transparency: a camera image of the user's hands is overlaid on the screen, background-subtracted and flipped, so the device looks semi-transparent and the user can see their fingers through it. The device supports multi-touch with all ten fingers and adds fingertip cursors for precise land-on selection; early study results reported that many users preferred touching the back, citing reduced occlusion, higher precision, and multi-finger input. The occluder becomes visible through the occluded surface -- occlusion solved by showing what is hidden, not by moving anything. [C35592]

Magic Finger (UIST 2012) inverts it the other way: the finger is instrumented, not the surface. A thimble-like device worn on the fingertip carries a low-resolution, high-speed optical mouse sensor that tracks movement, so any surface becomes a touch screen, plus a micro RGB camera that reads the texture of the surface being touched. Texture becomes an input channel: the same gesture on wood, cloth, skin, or paper can trigger different contextual actions -- the paper's examples are touching a bag to mute a phone, touching a shirt logo to start an app. A technical evaluation reported 22 textures recognised at 98.9% accuracy; a summary reports 32 textures (22 environmental plus 10 artificial) at 98.9% -- both figures are carried, the discrepancy not resolved. The device also recognises artificial textures and fiduciary markers such as Data Matrix codes. The world is the interface: the picture changes not with where you touch but with what you touch. [C35593]

**Takeaway:** Both systems solve a problem by moving the sensing to the place nobody was looking -- behind the glass, onto the fingertip. The lesson for a piece is not the hardware but the inversion: the input channel is not where the picture is. [C35594]

## Who is touching: ghosts and arms

Ordinary capacitive touch can track fingers but cannot tell whether several touches come from one hand, both hands of one person, or several people, which blocks multi-user interaction. GhostID (CHI 2017) turns a defect into a signal: in a frequency-division controller, touches from the same body produce faint ghost signals coupling across the sensor, while touches from different bodies do not. Analysing that ghosting classifies touch origins with 77% reliability for same-hand versus different-hands of one user, and 95% for two different users, both at 60 fps, with no enrolment, no wearable, and no persistent identity -- hence "non-persistent". The ghost-signal magnitude threshold and the classifier itself were not in the excerpts read; only the reliability figures are verified. The social fact -- one body or two -- is readable in the marks, and the piece that uses it must say plainly that a web page cannot read capacitive ghosting, so the two-pointer convention is a simulation, not a sensing. [C35595]

Medusa (UIST 2011, abstract only) attacks the same attribution problem from above the table: 138 inexpensive proximity sensors ring a multi-touch tabletop and sense what the touch surface alone cannot -- a user's presence and location around the table, body and arm positions, left arm versus right arm, and therefore which user and which hand each touch point belongs to. Sensor activations cluster into arm tracks; each arm is classified left vs right and attributed to a user by position around the table; each touch point is then mapped to the arm track above it. The sensing is exercised in Proxi-Sketch, a multi-user UI-prototyping tool in which the table's behaviour follows who is touching it and with which hand. The sensor-ring geometry, attribution radius, and tracking algorithm were not read beyond the abstract. Medusa is about who and which arm; zero-latency tapping is about when and where the touch will land. A piece should use one of those questions, not blur them. [C35596]

**Takeaway:** GhostID and Medusa agree on one thing: the touch point is not the whole input. The hand it belongs to, the body it belongs to, and the arm above it are all signal, and the sensor's defect -- ghosting, proximity -- is signal too. [C35597]

## Two channels at once: tilt, pen, and the second hand

TiltText (UIST 2003) is the earliest statement of the two-channel idea: one press selects the group, a second simultaneous channel selects within it. Combining and Measuring the Benefits of Bimanual Pen and Direct-Touch Interaction (AVI 2008, abstract only) generalises it to the tabletop: pen in the preferred hand, touch in the non-preferred hand, the non-dominant hand setting the frame -- holding, positioning, orienting -- while the dominant hand works inside that frame, the two channels concurrent rather than sequential, so the drawing surface itself can move under the pen. Users were faster and made fewer errors with pen-plus-touch than with touch-plus-touch or pen-plus-pen on the representative bimanual task. The division of labour is Guiard's: the non-dominant hand frames, the dominant hand draws, and the picture shows two different kinds of touch at once -- a framing gesture and a drawing gesture. On a phone this needs two simultaneous touches, not one finger plus a pen, and a piece built on it should say so. [C35598]

**Takeaway:** One channel selects the group; the second selects within it. That is the whole of TiltText, and it generalises to any grouped choice, not just letters -- a countable mechanism, one press and four outcomes, that a piece can state in a single sentence. [C35599]

## Duet: two devices, one interaction

Duet (CHI 2014, Best Paper) coordinates a smart phone and a smart watch as one interaction, based on the devices' spatial configuration. Three rules. Cross-device gestures: the same gesture performed on both devices simultaneously is one command -- a pinch-to-close swipe on both mutes notifications. The watch as palette: the watch displays tools for the phone task, so the small device is a second hand, not a second screen. Finger-part sensing: the watch's orientation indicates which part of the finger -- pad, side, or knuckle -- is touching the phone, and the mode switches seamlessly, pad writing, side scrolling, knuckle selecting text, with no explicit mode command. The gesture synchrony window and the technical-evaluation accuracy figures were not in the excerpts read. A single-screen staging of Duet -- two pointers with different roles -- is possible, but the two-device fact is the point, and a staging should say so rather than pretend the phone is two devices. [C35600]

**Takeaway:** Duet's rule is that mode can be sensed, not commanded: which part of the finger touches is the mode, and the device never asks. That is the most humane idea in the study, and the hardest to stage honestly on one screen. [C35601]

## Five kinds of gesture, and the machines that push back

Understanding Mid-Air Hand Gestures (MSR-TR-2012-111) is a study, not a system, and the distinction matters: twelve participants directed each other using only hand gestures, no speech, across six tasks; 5,500 gestures were analysed with a classification scheme combining three existing taxonomies. The finding is that meaning chooses the form: pointing or deictic gestures indicate a person, object, or direction; pantomimic acting acts out the action; direct manipulation guides movement in a tight feedback loop, the object following the hand; semaphoric gestures signal, in three sub-types -- static (a posture, a flat palm for stop), dynamic (a movement, a circular motion for rotate), stroke (a single flick, a left flick for dismiss); iconic gestures depict the shape or idea with the hand. The per-effect preference table -- which gesture type people prefer for each expected effect -- was not in the excerpts read, and a piece built on the taxonomy must define its own recognition rules, even crude ones such as pointer-path shape, and state them, not claim the study's taxonomy as a working system. [C35602]

The haptic thread is the latency programme's physical twin: if the picture cannot answer fast enough, make the hand feel the answer instead. Snake Charmer (TEI 2016) revisits encountered-type robotic graphics with commodity hardware: a user in a head-mounted display reaches for a virtual object, and a robotic arm moves a physical prop into place so the hand meets a real surface at exactly the virtual object's position, synthesising size, shape, temperature, weight, and motion, and letting objects physically push back on the hand. Annexing Reality (CHI 2016) solves the prop problem the other way: instead of a custom prop for every virtual object, it scans the surroundings, selects the physical objects already there that best match the virtual objects by shape similarity, and overlays the virtual objects onto them, so a mug, a book, or a bottle becomes the tangible proxy for whatever the scene needs, re-matching continuously as the scene changes. Thor's Hammer (CHI 2018) is a hand-held cube with six motors and propellers, one blowing outward normal to each face, pushing up to 4 N in any of three axes with no ground connection at all, tracked in six degrees of freedom, so the user feels weight, resistance, and impact from a virtual object while holding nothing but air; the control law mapping propeller speeds to a desired force vector was not read. VibraForge (CHI 2025) is a prototyping toolkit of self-contained vibration units, each with its own actuator and microcontroller, daisy-chained so one control unit independently drives up to 128 units with fine-grained intensity and frequency control, plus a GUI editor for authoring spatial vibration patterns; the technical evaluation showed 200 Hz bandwidth and 16 ms latency, and the toolkit is reported adopted by over twenty labs. Dive In! / Splash (CHI 2014) is the same idea in data rather than force: a coarse version of missing data is downloaded and displayed immediately on pan, then replaced by fine data when it arrives -- the low-fidelity-first rule, applied to content instead of feedback. [C35603]

**Takeaway:** The haptic thread and the latency thread are one argument in two materials: the system should answer the hand in the hand's own time, whether by light, by force, or by a coarse sketch that arrives first. A piece that only draws the answer has heard half the argument. [C35604]

## The companies: the lab bench as a business

The companies are not a sideline; they are how the research shipped. Tactual Labs' public output is the research itself -- the FMT sensor, the zero-latency tapping work, the latency studies -- and no shipped consumer product under the Tactual name was found; the company site hosts the papers. Chatham Labs left no public product or paper trail under its own name; its trace is the acquisition and the Reality Labs Toronto directorship that followed. AXL, as of this run, has no public product, paper, or demo under its name either -- only the launch, the Dentons partnership, and the Faculty Fellows cohort. That absence is itself informative: Wigdor's commercial record is patents and acquisitions, over 60 patents across AI systems, novel sensing technologies, and manufacturing methods, with the innovations used in more than a billion devices worldwide, per AXL and Wikipedia. The work reaches the hand through other companies' hardware, not through a product with his name on it. [C35605]

**Takeaway:** The measure of this career is not a product line. It is a latency budget -- 100 ms stalled, 1 ms demonstrated -- and a patent count, and the fact that the billion-device claim is plausible precisely because the sensing sits underneath other people's screens. [C35606]

## Aesthetic logic: the interface that disappears into the hand

Wigdor's systems share one visual logic, and it is the opposite of Chevalier's. Chevalier's pictures are for watching; Wigdor's are for touching, and the picture is often the least interesting part of the system. The logic has four rules. First, the input is the picture: Ripples draws the contact, Magic Finger draws the texture, Rock & Rails draws the hand shape, GhostID draws the ghost. The mark is not a representation of the interaction; it is the interaction, made visible. Second, the fast layer is crude and the slow layer is true: the accelerated-touch sketch arrives first and is overwritten by the real response, the coarse data arrives first and is replaced by the fine data, the soft feedback blooms before the touch and is corrected on landing. The picture is two pictures at two speeds, and the gap between them is where the drama lives. Third, the constraint is a body: a fist pins, a rail guides, a tilt selects, a finger part switches mode. There are no on-screen handles because the hand is the handle. Fourth, the system never asks: mode is sensed from the finger part, the owner from the ghost signal, the landing from the hover trajectory, the layout from the drawn path. The interface disappears not by being minimal but by being already answered -- the hand's state is the command, and the command is never typed.

That logic is why the work transfers to generative art without translation. A piece built from Ripples is a drawing of its own use. A piece built from DataInk is a population that re-binds when the viewer redraws one member. A piece built from the latency programme is a finger-object gap made visible and tunable. The risk is the opposite one: a piece that illustrates the mechanism -- a picture of a hand, a diagram of latency -- instead of running it. Wigdor's own test, the Touch Turing Test, is the test for the piece too: can the interaction be made indistinguishable from a real one, or is it a picture of an interaction? [C35607]
