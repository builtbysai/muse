## The life: fifty years of looking slowly

Troy Brauntuch was born in 1954 in Jersey City, New Jersey, and took his BFA at the California Institute of the Arts in 1975. At CalArts he was in John Baldessari's circle of influenced students, in the post-studio milieu under Baldessari's dictum "Make No More Boring Art", and he is counted in the five-man nucleus -- Brauntuch, Goldstein, Mullican, Salle, Welling -- that Richard Hertz calls the "CalArts Mafia". The group moved to New York in the early 1970s and led the transition from conceptualism to Pictures art, using images from television and movies. Brauntuch contributed first-person reflections to Hertz's oral history "Jack Goldstein and the CalArts Mafia" (2003). One conflict is recorded, not resolved: Design+Encyclopedia (search summary only) claims he was born in Connecticut and took a Rutgers BFA in 1977 and an MFA in 1979, against Wikipedia, Petzel, CalArts, Artsy and UT Austin, which all give Jersey City and a CalArts BFA in 1975; the Rutgers version is treated as unreliable, the same site contradicting itself across entries. No source read states in so many words that Brauntuch personally enrolled in a specific named Baldessari class in a specific term; the CalArts record places him in Baldessari's circle and in the post-studio milieu, and that is as far as the evidence goes. His exact birth day and month were not found. (C23408, leg 1 C23200-C23210; synthesis C23350-C23351)

He first gained recognition in Crimp's "Pictures" at Artists Space, September 24 to October 29, 1977, with Goldstein, Levine, Longo and Philip Smith -- the show that gave the Pictures Generation its name. Early solo shows ran from The Kitchen (1979) through Metro Pictures (1981), Mary Boone (1982, 1983, 1985), Gagosian Los Angeles (1986) and Kent Fine Art (1988, 1990); he showed at documenta 7 and the Venice Biennale in 1982, and later at Mai 36 Zurich from 1994 and Petzel from 2006, his eighth Petzel show in 2020 and a new Petzel show in 2026. Metro Pictures lists him among the artists in its inaugural exhibitions, but no source found makes him an inaugural artist in the sense Welling and Longo were -- he showed there; do not print him as inaugural. Landmarks include the Magasin Grenoble survey of 1990-2007 work (summer 2007), the Whitney Biennial 2006 ("Day for Night"), the Met "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" (April 21-August 2, 2009), the JRP/Ringier monograph of 2010 (works 1975-2008), and awards: NEA grants 1981-83, Joan Mitchell Foundation Grant 1999, Guggenheim Fellowship 2010. (C23409, leg 1 C23211-C23218, C23222-C23226; synthesis C23353-C23354)

He came to teaching late, in his 40s, having never planned on it: adjunct at Columbia University in New York, then at the University of Texas at Austin, where he lives and divides his time between New York and Austin. No start date for the UT Austin post was found in the sources read, and his current title there was not stated on the department page as retrieved; Wikipedia's "currently teaches" rests on a UT page archived in 2010, so his 2026 teaching status is unverified and is not printed as current. He formerly worked only at night and now works a daytime 9-to-5 schedule in a windowless studio. He hates attending his own openings. He does not use the Internet to source images -- he works from newspapers, magazines and images he has lived with, often years after the event. His most recent work is the "Untitled (Display Case)" paintings from Heinrich Hoffmann's photographs of the Grosse Deutsche Kunstausstellung (1937-1944) and, in "Rodeo" at The Warehouse Dallas (2025 to January 30, 2026), the first true oil paintings of his career, thirteen works including studies of his dog Rodeo. (C23410, leg 1 C23227-C23229; synthesis C23355-C23356)

The evolution is the proof. 1976-1983: print and photographic process first, drawing second, from Third Reich sources at extreme scales, presented without caption. 1992-2009: the same white-on-black conte on cotton turned to private and domestic subjects at body and room scale -- tennis court, doorway, shower, fur, shirts, pages, gloves, a cat on a chair, a paper sack of garbage at the curb. 2019-2025: digital re-archiving of the GDK archive, then first true oils, with the public archive and the private dog archive shown together in the same medium. What stays constant is the method: an image is taken, its context is removed, and it is re-presented so slowly and so darkly that the viewer has to do the work of seeing it. (C23411, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C23358)

One correction to the working summary that arrived with this study: the brief described Brauntuch as known for dark drawings from found press photographs of violence, accidents and war, in charcoal, by photocopy. The sources do not support that summary as written. No charcoal medium and no photocopy process were found in any source read; the stated media are white pencil, pencil, conte, conte crayon, pigment, ink and photographic processes (silkscreen, offset lithograph, photographic screenprint, C-print, photo-engraving, letterpress, rubber stamp). The verified early sources are Third Reich drawings and photographs -- Hitler's sketches (W03), Speer's captioned Mercedes photograph (W04), Thorak's studio (W10) -- not general press images of accidents. The verified press-derived works are specific, not general: the O.J. Simpson gloves, the Pan Am Flight 103 cockpit, the Long Island Railroad door. This study uses the verified versions only. (C23412, leg 2 correction; synthesis C23358)


## M1 -- Appropriation without caption

Take an existing image -- a sketch, a press photograph, a museum archive photograph, a newspaper advertisement -- reproduce it, and present it with no caption, no credit line, no explanatory text. W03 gives the pure form: three sketches by Adolf Hitler, titled only "1 2 3", a 1977 series of photographic screenprints with no accompanying caption or other text. W04 reproduces the photograph captioned in Albert Speer's memoirs "Hitler asleep in his Mercedes, 1934" and drops the caption. W19 takes digitized photographs of the Great German Art Exhibitions and re-presents them as photo-engravings. (C23413, leg 2 M1, W03, W04, W19; synthesis C23359)

The watchable behaviour is a flip in the viewer, not a change in the picture. The viewer first sees an insignificant image -- a tank, a vestibule, a stage set -- and then, when the source is learned, the same marks suddenly carry overwhelming weight. The picture does not change; the viewer's knowledge does, and the work stages that flip. A prompt built from this mechanism must present the image bare and let the revelation arrive second -- never print the caption on the image itself. (C23414, leg 2 M1; synthesis C23359)

**Takeaway:** reproduce the image and withhold the caption -- the picture stays the same, and the viewer's knowledge does the work. (C23415)


## M2 -- Enlargement past legibility

Blow a small source image up far beyond its native size, until the halftone screen, the grain or the paper texture becomes visible as pattern. W04 enlarges an eighteen-inch-wide book illustration, its halftone screen visible, onto a seven-foot bloodred field, with a further enlarged excerpt beside it and two flanking vertical panels of blown-up photographs too abstracted to read as such. W07 triples a head from a small photograph across three eight-foot panels. W06 to W07 is the same move across two works: the head tried at seven inches square, then at eight feet. (C23416, leg 2 M2, W04, W06, W07; synthesis C23360)

The watchable behaviour is an oscillation between picture and pattern. The viewer steps closer expecting detail and finds only dots and smears; stepping back, the head coheres. The enlargement itself becomes the subject. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the source small in origin and the enlargement extreme -- never enlarge a clean image, or there is nothing to dissolve into. (C23417, leg 2 M2; synthesis C23360)

**Takeaway:** blow the small source up until its screen becomes the pattern -- step close and it dissolves, step back and it coheres. (C23418)


## M3 -- White-into-black drawing

Draw with white pencil, white pastel or white conte crayon onto black paper, black construction paper or black cotton, adding light to a dark ground instead of shading a white one. W09 is the museum anchor: white pencil on three sheets of black construction paper, 87 x 104 inches overall, at MoMA. W10 draws Thorak's studio in pencil on paper, 84 x 31 inches; W12-W18 carry the method into conte on black cotton at body and room scale. Petzel's phrase is exact: "Drawing white-into-black conte crayon onto the surface of a cotton canvas." (C23419, leg 2 M3, W09, W10, W12-W18; synthesis C23361)

The watchable behaviour is an image arriving. The image appears to rise out of darkness rather than sit on a surface; at first glance the field can read as empty or near-monochrome, and the figure coagulates slowly as the eye adjusts. The viewer watches an image arrive, not an image that is already there. A prompt built from this mechanism must start from a black field and add light -- never start from a white ground and shade down. (C23420, leg 2 M3; synthesis C23361)

**Takeaway:** draw white into black and let the image rise out of darkness -- the viewer watches it arrive. (C23421)


## M4 -- Cropping to a fragment

Cut the source image down to one detail -- a head, a pair of gloves, a doorway, a shower, a paper sack -- and discard everything that would place it. W05-W06 crop to the head alone at postcard scale. The O.J. Simpson gloves works are six double panels of extreme close-up. The 2025 Display Case paintings crop small sculptures to legs or feet only. Mai 36 states it plainly: "Brauntuch cuts up or crops the pictures he has sourced elsewhere." (C23422, leg 2 M4, W05, W06, W20; synthesis C23362)

The watchable behaviour is a search with no answer. The fragment refuses to explain itself; the viewer scans the edges for the missing context, finds none, and is left with a detail that feels both precise and unplaceable. The crop creates the mystery; the drawing only records it. A prompt built from this mechanism must cut hard and show the cut -- never restore the context the crop removed. (C23423, leg 2 M4; synthesis C23362)

**Takeaway:** cut to one fragment and discard what would place it -- the detail is precise and unplaceable at once. (C23424)


## M5 -- Repetition and serial panels

Repeat the same image across two, three or four panels, or return to the same subject in separate works with marginal differences. W07 puts the same head on three eight-foot panels in a U-shape, installed so the viewer stands inside it, calling to mind an altarpiece. W08 articulates the image across four parts; W11 is a diptych. The shirts return again and again from slightly different angles with marginal differences; Bag with Garbage is the same paper sack in three separate works; Mickey 1985 and Mickey 2000 are the same name fifteen years apart -- a film star, then a cat. (C23425, leg 2 M5, W07, W08, W11, W17; synthesis C23363)

The watchable behaviour is comparison instead of reading. The viewer compares panels instead of reading one image; small differences -- a shift in light, a slightly different angle -- become the event, and the sequence feels ritualistic, even altar-like in the U-shaped W07 installation. A prompt built from this mechanism must repeat visibly and change one thing per repeat -- never show a single panel and call it the work. (C23426, leg 2 M5; synthesis C23363)

**Takeaway:** repeat the image across panels and let the small differences become the event -- comparison, not reading. (C23427)


## M6 -- Public and private at the same temperature

Render a historically loaded image and a domestic image with identical technique, scale and detachment, and show them together. Petzel 2008: Brauntuch depicts a dry cleaner interior or a butterfly pavilion "with the same cool detachment he employs when picturing the back of his girlfriend's new coat". The 2010 show puts the O.J. Simpson gloves, the Pan Am cockpit and the Long Island Railroad door beside a dead pit bull, empty shoes, his wife sleeping in 1984, a dry-cleaning shop window and a handprint. Rodeo (W20) puts GDK display cases and dog portraits in the same oil-painting series. (C23428, leg 2 M6, W20; synthesis C23364)

The watchable behaviour is a sorting habit failing. The viewer cannot use tone to sort the images into important and unimportant: a coat and a cockpit demand the same slow look, and the leveling is the point. A prompt built from this mechanism must hold the temperature identical across both images -- never let the loaded image glow and the domestic one dim. (C23429, leg 2 M6; synthesis C23364)

**Takeaway:** draw the coat and the cockpit at the same temperature -- the viewer cannot sort them, and the leveling is the point. (C23430)


## M7 -- Threshold legibility: the slow image

Keep the tonal range so narrow and the contrast so low that the image sits just at the edge of visibility. Conte on black cotton with the subtlest pressure; "ghost-like, obfuscating traces" (Petzel Early Work); images that "feel as though they were conjured from the deep recesses of phantasmic space" (Whitney 2006). W17-W18 are the clearest cases: from a distance the shirts are grey and ghostlike, and could stand in for any material heaped in a grid; only on close inspection do the shirts or pages resolve. (C23431, leg 2 M7, W17, W18; synthesis C23365)

The watchable behaviour is time spent looking. The viewer must dwell, move closer, let their eyes adapt: a quick look sees a black rectangle, a long look sees a room. The picture rewards patience and punishes a glance, and time spent looking is part of the work. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the image at the edge of visibility and let the viewer earn it -- never brighten it into readability. (C23432, leg 2 M7; synthesis C23365)

**Takeaway:** keep the image at the edge of visibility -- a glance sees black, a long look sees a room. (C23433)


## M8 -- Re-archiving: editing the document

Take an archival photograph and alter its contents digitally before re-printing it by a historical process, so the result looks like a document but is not one. W19 is the full statement: Brauntuch "digitally edited its contents, as well as the various combinations of artworks that convincingly appear reflected in its glass panes" (Art in America on the White Cases), then fused the manipulation with industrial letterpress printing, traditional photographic processes and metal image substrates such as magnesium. Petzel states the stakes: the "manipulations, deletions, and additions of information to the images... exposes the ease by which information in sensitive archives can be manipulated and referenced at later points in time, as truth." (C23434, leg 2 M8, W19; synthesis C23366)

The watchable behaviour is trust dissolving. The viewer trusts the image because it looks archival -- empty museum, marble, glass case -- and then notices something wrong: a transparent Thorak nude rising behind a case, a reflection that cannot belong. The document unravels in front of them, and the viewer watches their own trust in photographs dissolve. A prompt built from this mechanism must look archival first and wrong second -- never announce the edit up front. (C23435, leg 2 M8; synthesis C23366)

**Takeaway:** edit the archive and re-print it as a document -- the viewer trusts it, then watches that trust dissolve. (C23436)


## M9 -- The display case, glass and reflection

Make the vitrine -- the glass, the armature, the reflection -- part of the image, not a frame around it. W19's White Cases show a Thorak female nude rendered transparent, rising behind a glazed display case, with contents digitally edited and reflections altered. W20's Display Case oils carry it further: "Miniature horses, rabbits, and sheep disappear and emerge from behind the armature of the display case and reflections on the glass" (Warehouse). The case is both the subject and the obstacle: the animals are seen through bars and glare. (C23437, leg 2 M9, W19, W20; synthesis C23367)

The watchable behaviour is peering. The viewer looks through layers -- glass, reflection, armature, figurine -- and the figurine keeps slipping behind a bar or a glare spot. Seeing becomes a process of peering around obstructions, and the obstruction is what the painting is about: curation, display and omission as acts of power. A prompt built from this mechanism must keep the obstruction in the picture -- never remove the glass to show the object cleanly. (C23438, leg 2 M9; synthesis C23367)

**Takeaway:** paint the vitrine, not just what is in it -- the glass, the bars and the glare are the picture. (C23439)


## M10 -- The afterimage in color: oil as a late return

After five decades of black-ground drawing and photographic process, paint in oil, in color, from a private photographic archive, but keep the image spectral. W20 is the break: the first true oil paintings of his career (Warehouse), thirteen works, three of his dog Rodeo, ten of GDK display cases in "dim shades of silver and copper". The Rodeo studies are "painted in color" yet "retain the quality of an afterimage rather than a faithful representation". The earlier color hint is Mark's Camera 3 (2013): layers of multi-colored pigment on dark cloth, from a photograph of a friend's charred camera after a house fire. (C23440, leg 2 M10, W20; synthesis C23368)

The watchable behaviour is an expectation disappointed on purpose. The viewer expects oil paint to deliver solidity and finds instead a faded, hovering presence -- a dog that is there and not there, a figurine that is silver and copper and also a ghost. Color arrives, but legibility does not improve. A prompt built from this mechanism must let color in without letting clarity in -- never let the oil painting become a faithful portrait. (C23441, leg 2 M10; synthesis C23368)

**Takeaway:** paint in oil and in color, but keep the image an afterimage -- color arrives, legibility does not improve. (C23442)


## The aesthetic logic: the viewer does the work

Brauntuch's career is one question asked fifty ways: what does an image do once its context is taken away? He never answers it by making a picture. He answers it by taking someone else's picture, removing what would explain it -- the caption, the scale, the context, the light -- and watching what the viewer does with what is left. The work is not in the drawing; the work is in the looking. The drawing is only dark enough, slow enough and cropped enough to force the viewer to do it. (C23443, leg 2 mechanisms M1-M10; synthesis C23359-C23368)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, take, don't make: silkscreen, C-print, conte copy, digital edit -- the image always arrives from elsewhere, from Hitler's sketches to Hoffmann's GDK photographs to a private archive of dog photographs, and the artist's job is selection and re-presentation, never invention. Second, remove until it barely holds: the caption goes, the context goes, the light goes, the scale goes wrong in both directions -- and the image is left at the edge of visibility, where only a patient viewer finds it. Third, level everything: the rally and the tennis court, the cockpit and the coat, the display case and the dog are drawn at the same temperature, so no image can borrow importance from its subject. Baldessari replaced the hand with a rule; his student replaced the picture with the act of looking at one. (C23444, leg 2 M1, M3, M6, M7; synthesis C23358)

The evolution is the proof. 1976-83: public history, extreme scale, no caption -- the insignificant image that becomes overwhelming once its source is known. 1992-2009: private life at body scale, the same darkness and detachment, the charge now infrathin: the gap between a thing and our idea of it, not between an image and a war crime. 2019-25: the archive returns, but now the artist edits it openly, and oil finally arrives -- in color, from a private archive -- without making anything clearer. What stays constant is the viewer: the picture is never finished on the wall, it is finished in the looking, and the looking takes time. (C23445, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C23358)
