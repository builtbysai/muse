# Adriaen van Ostade: Study Notes

**Site:** Adriaen van Ostade (bapt. 10 December 1610 - buried 2 May 1685), Dutch Golden Age genre painter of Haarlem, the stay-at-home peasant painter who spent fifty years in one town painting the low life of the cottage, the tavern and the dooryard - brawls and card quarrels at first, then work and family, music and sunlight - in small panels, in fifty etched plates, and in finished watercolours that glow like oils. He was born Adriaen Jansz Hendricx in Haarlem, the eldest son of Jan Hendricx van Eyndhoven, a weaver from the hamlet of Ostade near Eindhoven who had moved north to Haarlem under religious persecution; the brothers Adriaen and Isack adopted "van Ostade" as painters. He was a pupil of Frans Hals in 1627 (RKD), was registered in Utrecht in 1632, and was back in Haarlem and in the Guild of St Luke by 1634 - the 1634 date is the standard one, but the sources disagree (see C468). He joined a civic-guard company at 26 (1636); married first Machtelgen Pietersen (1636 per Binyon, 1638 per Wikipedia, Christie's and Lempertz - see C468), who died in 1640 or 1642; married second Anna Ingels in 1657, from a respected Amsterdam Catholic family, with one daughter, Johanna Maria; Anna died 1666. He was guild hoofdman (leader) in 1647 and 1661 and dean in 1662. In the rampjaar 1672 he packed his goods to flee to Lubeck, got as far as Amsterdam, and was persuaded to stay by the collector Konstantyn Sennepart, in whose house he made a series of coloured drawings later bought for 1,300 florins by Jonas Witsen. He died 27 April 1685 per Christie's, Dayton/Grove and the 9th-edition Britannica, and was buried 2 May 1685 in the Groote or St Bavo Kerk, Haarlem - the Wikipedia infobox gives 2 May as the death date, conflating death and burial (see C468). At his death over 200 unsold pictures remained in his studio; the estate sale (3 July 1685) included his 50 etched plates, bought back by his son-in-law Dirck van der Stoel, a surgeon. He never travelled beyond Holland and never changed his home except from one street of Haarlem to another (Binyon). His pupils included his brother Isack, Thomas Wijck (probably), Cornelis Bega, Jan Steen (probably), Michiel van Musscher, Jan de Groot, Frans de Jongh, Evert Oudendijck and Cornelis Dusart, who inherited much of the studio contents in 1685. He also supplied staffage figures to Saenredam, Ruisdael, Cornelis Decker and Jacob van Mosscher. His death date is carried here as died 27 April 1685, buried 2 May 1685, not as a single date, because the sources split (see C468). His first marriage year (1636 vs 1638) and his first wife's death year (1640 vs 1642) are likewise carried as spreads. No words by Ostade himself were found in any source read - no letters, contracts or statements; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Adriaen van Ostade was born in Haarlem, the eldest son of a weaver from a hamlet near Eindhoven, and he never really left Haarlem - except for the year in Utrecht that taught him the village scene, and the afternoon in 1672 when he packed to flee to Lubeck and got no further than Amsterdam. His father Jan Hendricx van Eyndhoven had moved north under religious persecution; the sons took "van Ostade" from the hamlet as painters. In 1627, at sixteen or seventeen, Adriaen entered the studio of Frans Hals - the RKD's date, and the one fact about his training that is solid. Hals influenced him very little, the Grove entry says bluntly; the decisive influence was Adriaen Brouwer, the Fleming whose peasant types Ostade learned before Brouwer died in 1638. He was registered in Utrecht in 1632, where he was probably influenced by Droochsloot's village scenes, and back in Haarlem by 1634, a member of the Guild of St Luke - or from 1641, if Christie's is right; the guild records disagree and the study does not pick one silently.

The career that follows is fifty years in one town, and its shape is the shape of the work: he starts with the brawl and ends with the dooryard. He married twice - first Machtelgen Pietersen, in 1636 or 1638, who died in 1640 or 1642; then Anna Ingels in 1657, from a respected Amsterdam Catholic family, with one daughter, Johanna Maria, who died in 1666. Whether he himself converted to Catholicism in the late 1650s is stated only in a prabook snippet and is not asserted here. He was guild hoofdman in 1647 and 1661 and dean in 1662, a prosperous, well-to-do middle-class citizen whose portrait shows a grave man of severe, keen countenance in plain attire - not a boor, and not a man in want: at his death over 200 of his own pictures remained unsold in his studio. In the rampjaar 1672 he packed his goods to flee to Lubeck, got as far as Amsterdam, and was persuaded to stay by the collector Konstantyn Sennepart, in whose house he made the coloured drawings Jonas Witsen later bought for 1,300 florins. He died 27 April 1685 and was buried 2 May in the Groote or St Bavo Kerk. The estate sale on 3 July 1685 included his 50 etched plates, bought back by his son-in-law Dirck van der Stoel, a surgeon. His last pupil, Cornelis Dusart, inherited much of the studio contents and finished his unfinished paintings - so close in the late manner that the two are often hard to tell apart. He never travelled beyond Holland; he never changed his home except from one street of Haarlem to another. What he left is large and scattered: over 900 pictures attributed (Hofstede de Groot), 404 drawings catalogued (Schnackenburg 1981), 50 etched plates, and a Haarlem peasant line - Steen, Isack, Bega, Dusart - that runs a generation past him.

**Takeaway:** a fifty-year stay-at-home career with one move that mattered - the year in Utrecht - and one subject that never changed: the peasant cottage, inside and out, painted from the brawl to the dooryard while the light warmed and the joke softened.

## M1. The concentrated light pool against broad gloom

In the early work (1632-38) the picture is built as one small, hard-lit patch set against a large dark field. Light is not spread evenly; it is concentrated on a small surface while the rest of the room or yard sinks into gloom. This is what separates him from Brouwer, who spreads the frolic across the whole plate. EN Wikipedia (read end to end), quoting the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica text: "he is distinguished from his rival by a more general use of light and shade, especially a greater concentration of light on a small surface in contrast with a broad expanse of gloom." Watchable behaviour: the eye is pulled to one bright knot - a face, a hand, a jug - while the rest of the scene stays dark; the gloom itself is the frame.

**Takeaway:** one bright knot in a dark room; the gloom is the frame, not the background.

## M2. Steel-grey early harmonies, dry and careful detail

The 1632-38 pictures are keyed in steel-grey, a narrow scale of greys and browns, painted dryly and carefully, shunning no difficulty of detail. The caricature is exaggerated, but the handling is not loose: every object is finished. EN Wikipedia: "The key of his harmonies remained for a time in the scale of greys, but his treatment is dry and careful in a style which shuns no difficulties of detail." Also: "it was easy to study the steel-grey harmonies, the exaggerated caricature of his early works between 1632 and 1638." Watchable behaviour: a cold grey room, figures like caricatures, yet every plank, pot and rag rendered with deliberate finish; the comedy sits inside a grey, almost monochrome world.

**Takeaway:** the joke is drawn in grey and finished to the last plank; the caricature is deliberate, not loose.

## M3. Diagonal rafters as spatial recession

From the early 1630s he builds interior depth by placing the roof rafters on a diagonal, so the ceiling itself pulls the eye back into the room. In the tranquil domestic scenes the action matters less than the psychological state, and the setting gains significance because the diagonal rafters give the space recession. Dayton/Grove (Schnackenburg): "the figures and the space blend in an atmospheric treatment of light... Adriaen van Ostade employed stronger chiaroscuro light contrasts and stressed the element of spatial recession through the diagonal placing of his rafters." Watchable behaviour: look at the ceiling first - the rafters run on a diagonal from the foreground into the back of the room, and the figures sit inside that tunnel of space rather than pasted on a flat wall.

**Takeaway:** the ceiling draws the room; the rafters are the perspective.

## M4. The concealed artificial light source

A candle or lantern hidden behind a figure or object, so the light appears without a visible source and the faces are lit from within the dark. A device borrowed from the Utrecht Caravaggisti, used in the single-figure candlelight pictures of the mid-1630s. Dayton/Grove: "In the Old Woman with a Candle (1636...) Adriaen van Ostade used a concealed, artificial light source, a device favoured by the Utrecht Caravaggisti." Watchable behaviour: a face or hands glow in a black room with no window and no visible flame; the light seems to come from inside the picture, and the edges of the figure dissolve into dark.

**Takeaway:** hide the flame and the face glows from inside the dark; the Utrecht candle, borrowed, not invented.

## M5. The Rembrandtesque sunbeam: warm brown transparency after c. 1640

From about 1640 the palette warms from blue-grey to green-brown, and the light becomes a single sunbeam entering through a casement, falling across a dark loft or chimney corner. The tone is brownish and transparent, with strongly impasted lights set into sketchy rubbings of dark brown tone. The effect is an effect of light first and a subject second. EN Wikipedia on the 1642 Louvre interior: "the darkness of a country loft dimly illumined by a sunbeam shining on the casement... there is nothing attractive, indeed, except the wonderful Rembrandtesque transparency, the brownish tone, and the admirable keeping of the minutest parts." National Gallery: "From about 1640 the influence of Rembrandt is detectable in the use of light and shade in his paintings." Dayton/Grove: "the tonality shifts from blue-grey to a warmer green-brown." Watchable behaviour: one shaft of warm light cuts a dark brown room; a mother and child, or a pig-slaughter, sit inside the beam while everything else stays in brown shadow; the paint is thin and rubbed in the darks, thick and pasty in the lights. FLAG: the Rembrandt causation is weaker than the older literature claims - the Annunciation, the key exhibit, was reattributed to Benjamin Gerritsz Cuyp in 1982 (see C470). The light change is documented; the Rembrandt cause is cautious at best.

**Takeaway:** one sunbeam through a casement does the whole picture; the darks are rubbed thin, the lights laid on thick.

## M6. The cottage as inventory: rafters, thatch, ladder stairs, vine-cloaked walls

He shows the cottage inside and out as a complete inventory of poverty - patchwork rafters and thatch, tumble-down chimneys, ladder staircases indoors; vine leaves cloaking the poverty of the outer walls. Nothing decorates the interior except the structure itself; the vegetation outside does the decorating. EN Wikipedia: "He shows us the cottages, inside and out: vine leaves cloak the poverty of the outer walls; indoors, nothing decorates the patchwork of rafters and thatch, the tumble-down chimneys and the ladder staircases, the rustic Dutch home of those days." Watchable behaviour: the room is a catalogue of beams, thatch, a ladder to the loft, a crumbling chimney; outside, the same wreck is softened by green vines. The viewer reads the house as a list of things before reading the people.

**Takeaway:** the house is a list first and a setting second; the vine does the decorating the poverty cannot.

## M7. The short, ill-favoured figure type, marked by adversity

His peasants are short and ill-favoured, stubby and ungainly, with the stamp of adversity in feature and dress - a deliberate figure type, not failed anatomy. In the etchings this is achieved by a detailed study of physiognomy and sure, confident strokes that make the peasant type identifiable at a glance. In the later work the grotesque softens into amiable, mellowed peasants, but the type stays short and compact. EN Wikipedia: "the people depicted by Ostade are short and ill-favoured, marked with adversity's stamp in feature and dress." Knox: "Ostade applied a detailed study of physiognomy and sure confident strokes that made the peasant figure type identifiable." Watchable behaviour: figures read as squat blocks with big heads, heavy jowls, and coarse clothes; even in a crowd you can pick the Ostade peasant by silhouette alone. Later figures keep the same build but lose the snarl.

**Takeaway:** the peasant is a type you know by silhouette; the build stays, the snarl softens.

## M8. The three-phase etching line

The etched line changes in three dated phases. 1647-52: loose, sketchy, curving strokes, hooks and dots; contours soft and dissolving in light, with fine gradations of chiaroscuro and a painterly character. 1653-54: a graphic system of hatchings and crosshatchings takes over. 1671-79: the hatched line becomes simpler and more rigid, with more contour drawing and strong contrasts enforcing sculptural solidity. Dayton/Grove: "The graphic repertory consists of loose, sketchy, curving strokes, hooks and dots. The works of 1653-4, by contrast, show a graphic system of hatchings and crosshatchings, which becomes simpler and more rigid in the late etchings of 1671 and 1679." Knox, same in other words: "A concentration on sketchy, curving strokes and hooks and dots are found in prints from 1647 to 1652, hatchings and crosshatchings became prominent during the years 1653 to 1654, and from 1671 to 1679 the hatched line tends to be more rigid and simpler." Watchable behaviour: an early print looks like a sketch that breathes - soft edges, air in the lines; a middle print is built of woven hatching; a late print is hard-edged and sculptural, the figure cut out of the white paper by contour.

**Takeaway:** the line has three ages - breathing sketch, woven hatch, hard contour - and you can date a print by how it breathes.

## M9. States and re-biting: the plate as a working draft

He works toward the desired effect through a succession of states, re-biting and correcting the plate as he prints. The first biting often fails - The Cobbler (1671) "was a failure in the first biting" - and the second state is generally the most representative. Some plates are over-bitten (Man Laughing, Saying Grace, Fiddlers). Many more states were created after his death by other hands reworking the worn copper, so the last state touched by the artist, not the last state printed, is the one that counts. Binyon: "Ostade undoubtedly made alterations in his plates during printing and thus created 'states'; but many more states were created after his death by other hands re-working the worn copper." And: "As Ostade was not always successful in his first biting, the second state is generally the most representative." Dayton/Grove: "The artist worked towards the desired effect through a succession of states." Watchable behaviour: the same image exists in several versions that differ in darkness and finish; the early state is light and open, the artist's later state is denser, and posthumous states are harsh and retouched. The print is a process, not a single image.

**Takeaway:** the print is a draft with versions; the artist's last state is the work, the posthumous ones are noise.

## M10. Correction from the back of the plate: hammer, punch, re-etch, drypoint

The copper plates are cold-hammered, thick, and hard to bend. On the reverse many show fine hammer and punch marks, used to adjust the level of the copper in parts that needed correction. By partly re-etching the plates or using drypoint in those areas, he created the numerous different states. The backs of the plates also carry scratched numbers or letters, some possibly applied in his own time. Christie's technical details: "On the reverse many of them show fine hammer and punch marks, which were needed to adjust the level of the copper on the etched side of the plate in parts that needed corrections to the subject. By partly re-etching the plates or using drypoint in these areas, the artist created the numerous different states we find in his etchings." Watchable behaviour: hold the plate, not the print: the back is dented and numbered, a working tool. In the print, a corrected passage looks slightly different in texture - a drypoint burr softening a re-etched area - because the correction was hammered in from behind.

**Takeaway:** the correction is hammered in from the back; the plate is a tool with a dented reverse and a numbered face.

## M11. Second-hand copper: cut plates, shared source with Rembrandt

He bought second-hand copper plates and cut them down for his own use. The reverses of the plates for B.12 and B.25, and for B.36 and B.39, show engraved geometrical subjects that together form two plates used to illustrate a rectangle and a half circle in Juan Alfonso de Molina Cano's Descubrimientos Geometricos (Arnhem edition 1620). He cut both plates in two, and may have left one half unetched from c. 1653-60 until he used it by 1670-71. Rembrandt's plate for The Return of the Prodigal Son bears a similar geometrical illustration on its reverse, proving both artists bought their second-hand plates from the same source. Christie's: "It has now been discovered that the reverse of Ostade's etched plates used for B.12 and B.25, as well as those used for B.36 and B.39, show similar engraved geometrical subjects on the reverse... this proves that both artists bought their second-hand plates from the same source." Watchable behaviour: the print on the front is Ostade; flip the plate and there is a 1598 geometry diagram. The economy of the workshop is visible in the object itself - nothing wasted, old plates cut in half and re-used years apart.

**Takeaway:** the plate is second-hand and cut in half; the geometry on the back is the receipt for the copper.

## M12. The drawing pipeline: pen-and-wash compositions, chalk studies from life, stylus transfer

The drawings fall into a working sequence. Until the mid-1640s he concentrated on multi-figured compositions in pen and brown ink with brown wash and sometimes watercolour. Then he began preparatory figure studies, for paintings and etchings, in black and white chalk on tinted paper, adopting the study "from life" (naer het leven) pioneered by Roelandt Savery; there are also highly detailed studies of heads and shoulders, or heads alone, in red and black chalk. Composition sketches were traced through with a stylus to transfer them to the copper plate, the panel, or another sheet. About half his drawings date from after 1670. Dayton/Grove: "Until the mid-1640s Adriaen concentrated on multi-figured compositions, drawn in pen and brown ink, with brown wash and sometimes watercolour; then he began to produce preparatory figure studies, both for paintings and etchings, in black and white chalk on tinted paper. These show the artist adopting the type of study 'from life' (naer het leven) that was pioneered by Roelandt Savery." And: "These miniature paintings, like the etchings, are mostly based on composition sketches traced through with a stylus." Watchable behaviour: a rapid chalk figure on blue paper - a woman wiping a baby, a violinist, a standing peasant - is a rehearsal, not a finished work; the same pose later appears in a painting or an etching, slightly adjusted. The finished watercolours and etchings are the last step of a chain that starts with a life study.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is a rehearsal in chalk; the painting and the print are the same pose, performed twice.

## M13. The finished watercolour as an autonomous work (1672-84)

In his sixties he turned to highly finished watercolours, about fifty of which survive, mostly dated 1672-84, signed and usually dated - which shows he considered them works of art in their own right, not studies. The watercolour is not used to fill in a pen drawing; it is freely applied to build, in a chromatic rather than linear way, both the structure of the whole composition and its lighting scheme. He combines pen, brush, transparent and opaque watercolours, with strategically applied bodycolour highlights; in some the preliminary underdrawing is in black and red chalk rather than pen, and the support is sometimes parchment. Sotheby's: "the watercolour is applied not simply to fill in or flesh out a pen drawing, but is freely applied to build, in a chromatic rather than linear way, both the structure of the entire composition and its lively lighting scheme." Dayton/Grove: "Adriaen van Ostade set out to duplicate the effect of his oil paintings through a combination of pen, brush and transparent as well as opaque watercolours. In a number of these watercolours the preliminary underdrawing is in black and red chalk rather than pen, and the support is sometimes parchment." Watchable behaviour: a small sheet that glows like an oil painting - colour doing the work of structure, light built up in washes with opaque white touches picking out the highlights, the whole scene finished to the edges like a miniature painting.

**Takeaway:** the watercolour is not a study for the oil; it is the oil, done in washes on a small sheet.

## M14. The fijnschilder turn: smooth paint, strong local colour in tonal twilight

In the late 1650s and early 1660s the paint becomes smoother and more delicate, and by the early 1660s he is working in a fully fledged "Fine" painting technique as practised by the Leiden artists. The colouring is rich but subdued, with a finely attuned cool tonality. In the 1650s interiors the strong local colouring of the figures stands out powerfully from the tonal twilight of the interior setting. After 1670 the colouring becomes brighter and more intense, the local colour values tied together by a warm, mostly greenish tonality. Dayton/Grove: "At the end of the decade the paint becomes smoother and more delicate, as in Peasant Dance at an Inn (1659...), and the works of the early 1660s show a fully fledged 'Fine' painting technique as practised by Leiden artists. The colouring is marked by rich but subdued hues accompanied by a finely attuned cool tonality." And: "The strong local colouring of the figures stands out powerfully from the tonal twilight of the interior setting." And: "After 1670 Adriaen's paintings become brighter and more intense in their colouring. The local colour values are now tied together by a warm, mostly greenish tonality." Watchable behaviour: a 1660s interior looks enamel-smooth, each figure a patch of strong local colour - a red jacket, a blue apron - floating in a cool grey-brown twilight; a 1670s outdoor scene is brighter and greener, the colours tied together by one warm wash.

**Takeaway:** the figures are patches of strong colour floating in twilight; the paint goes smooth while the subject stays rough.

## M16. The expressive body: posture and gesture as the carrier of humour

His chief glory as a draughtsman is imaginative drawing of the human body in action - the attitudes and gestures of players and onlookers caught at the exact moment. A defeated backgammon player scrutinising the board; the loose, ineffectual, lurching stroke of a drunken man in a brawl; the fat man springing up from his barrel; the woman clasping her baby closer; the angler's lax body but firm wrist, the slackening line, the indolent gaze of the boy leaning on the rail. The humour is in the body, not in a caption. Binyon: "In the attitudes, the gestures of players and onlookers, everything is vital; the moment is admirably caught." And on The Brawl: "The loose, ineffectual, lurching stroke of the drunken man, the startled effort of the fat man as he springs up from his barrel, the terror of the woman clasping her baby closer, the mingled fear, anger, and surprise of the little man who has provoked the quarrel and prepares to defend himself - all are excellent." And on The Angler: "The stationary posture, the muscular habit of the angler, with lax body but firm wrist, is perfectly given; as is the slackening of the line, the indolent gaze of the boy leaning on the rail, and the sleepy impression of a still summer day without breezes." Watchable behaviour: freeze any Ostade crowd and each body is doing one readable thing - leaning, lunging, clutching, dozing - and the comedy or tenderness comes from reading those postures against each other, like a silent film still.

**Takeaway:** the body is the joke and the tenderness both; read the postures against each other and the scene plays itself.

## Works

### Phase 1 - Early: caricature and the brawl, 1632-1638

The starting point is Adriaen Brouwer, not Frans Hals. Grove is blunt: Hals "influenced him very little", while Brouwer "had a decisive influence on the evolution" of the peasant portrayal. The early pictures are fight scenes, card quarrels, and carousing taverns, in steel-grey harmonies with pale pastel pinks and blues, figures as exaggerated caricatures in colourful rags in settings of chaotic squalor. Compositions are figure-dominated and crowded, with a repoussoir device at the left margin on the side the light comes from. The signature reads "Ostaden" up to 1635. But the quiet counter-current is present from the outset: small tranquil interiors where psychological state matters more than action, and single-figure studies by candlelight. The brawl was never the whole early Ostade.

- W1. Peasants Playing Cards, 1633. Oil on panel (medium not confirmed in this leg; panel assumed from period practice, NOT stated as fact). Dimensions: NOT VERIFIED. Location: Hermitage, St Petersburg. The earliest firmly dated painting (Dayton/Grove). The anchor for the whole chronology: everything before it is dated "before 1633" or "c. 1635" by style. A card game, the subject that recurs as quarrel and brawl for the next two decades.

Tiny format, four figures only - the quiet early Ostade that complicates the caricature story.

### Phase 2 - Transition: the barn, the open air, and the light, 1636-1647

Three changes arrive together and can be dated. First, from 1636, spacious barn interiors with diminutive figures at work, and the tonality warms from blue-grey to green-brown. Second, from 1637, open-air scenes, beginning rudimentarily, then, probably under his brother Isack's influence, a real concentration on landscape around 1640. Third, the Rembrandt turn around 1638-1640: strong chiaroscuro, a small concentrated light against a broad expanse of gloom, the sunbeam across a dark loft. FLAG on this last point: the Dayton/Grove entry warns that the Annunciation to the Shepherds, the key evidence for a Rembrandt influence, was reattributed to Benjamin Gerritsz. Cuyp in 1982, and that the Hermit Reading is now "the only work by Adriaen van Ostade that shows any affinity, in form or in content, to the work of Rembrandt". The light change is real and visible in W7; the Rembrandt-causation story is weaker than the older literature claims. The phase closes with the first etchings (W9, W10, 1647) and the interior growing larger, flat-ceilinged and better furnished. W7 (1642) is the hinge work: same cottage, new tenderness.

- W7. Cottage Interior (Peasant Family in a Large Hut), 1642. Oil on panel. Dimensions: 34.5 x 44 cm (REPORTED - Wikimedia Commons works list, read end to end). Location: Louvre, Paris. The famous 1642 interior of Wikipedia: "a mother tending her cradled child, her husband sitting nearby, beside a great chimney; the darkness of a country loft dimly illumined by a sunbeam shining on the casement". Grove lists it as the type-example of barn interiors carried into the 1640s. The pivot work: same peasant cottage, but the light is now Rembrandtesque transparency and the mood is tenderness, not satire. The barn interior translated into print in the same year as the painted barn of W8.

### Phase 3 - Mature: the definitive interior and fine painting, 1647-1670

Around 1647 the characteristic interior finds its definitive form: greater depth through marked orthogonals, subtle chiaroscuro gradation, more individualized figures, costumes and furnishings shown in more detail. The 1650s add an increasing emphasis on detail and strong local colour standing out from tonal twilight. The subject matter softens decisively: by the 1650s excessive drinking and gambling "became the exception rather than the rule" - Peasants Brawling 1656 is the late exception. The implicit meaning shifts with the subject: alongside the old satire on human frailty, peasant simplicity is held up as a model ("Vivitur parvo bene: one may live well on little") and even idealized in the manner of bucolic poetry. The range widens: single-figure craftsmen and tradespeople, the Five Senses allegories, group portraits of prosperous sitters, an urban market, and the one biblical subject (Adoration of the Shepherds, 1667). At the end of the decade the paint turns smoother and more delicate, and the early 1660s show a fully fledged "Fine" painting technique in the Leiden manner - rich but subdued hues with a finely attuned cool tonality. The 1662 pair is the mature summit: schoolroom and tavern, both orderly, both crowded, both legible.

### Phase 4 - Late: brighter colour, open air, and the finished watercolour, 1671-1685

After 1670 the colouring becomes brighter and more intense, local colour values tied together by a warm, mostly greenish tonality. Interiors become relatively fewer; open-air compositions multiply. The fiddler (W16, 1673) and the cottage dooryard (W17, 1673) are the late emblems: music and family, outdoors, in warm light. In the last years he confines himself to modest single-figure compositions - three small Five Senses paintings, all 1681 - and the last dated paintings are Village Street and Skittle Players, both 1676, "without any sign of declining powers" (Wikipedia). Running parallel, and dominant after 1670, is the finished watercolour: about half of his 404 catalogued drawings date from after 1670, signed and usually dated collector's pieces that duplicate the effect of the oils in pen, brush, transparent and opaque watercolour. The etchings close with The Doll (W20, 1679). The final dated works are 1676-1681; he died in 1685 with over 200 unsold pictures in his studio.

## Evolution

What he kept from Brouwer: the Netherlandish peasant tradition itself - the observed body, the tavern brawl, the quack, the card quarrel, the figure type pushed past likeness. Grove's sentence is the key: Hals "influenced him very little", while Brouwer "had a decisive influence on the evolution" of the peasant portrayal. The caricature hand, the squat body, the appetite as subject - all of it comes from the north, not from Rome, and not from Hals's loose brush.

What the 1640 light change did: the palette warmed from blue-grey to green-brown, the light narrowed to one sunbeam through a casement, and the mood followed the light from satire toward tenderness. The 1642 Louvre interior is the hinge: the same cottage, the same peasants, but the mother and the cradled child inside the beam, and nothing in the picture is there except the transparency, the brownish tone, and the keeping of the minutest parts. The Rembrandt causation for that change is now cautious at best - the Annunciation was reattributed in 1982 - but the change itself is visible work by work.

What changed, in one paragraph: from steel-grey caricatured brawls and card quarrels in crowded taverns (1632-38), to spacious barn interiors with diminutive figures at work and a warming palette, the first open-air scenes and the first sunbeam (1636-47), to the definitive large flat-ceilinged interior with individualized figures, strong local colour in tonal twilight, and the fully fledged "Fine" technique of the early 1660s (1647-70), to brighter, greener, open-air dooryards, fiddlers and finished watercolours after 1670, closing with the last dated etching, The Doll, in 1679 - the same motifs distilled from satire into domestic virtue, and the same cottage carried from the brawl to the family at the door.

What stayed constant: the peasant cottage as the one true subject, inside and out, for fifty years; the small panel (almost everything is under 50 cm on the long side - the largest catalogue entry here is 47.5 x 39 cm); the signature-and-date habit that makes the chronology traceable at all (104 signed and dated pictures in public and private collections, per Wikipedia); the drawing habit underneath everything - figure studies from life feeding paintings and etchings alike; and the single light source doing the moral work, from the lantern of 1637 to the sunbeam of 1642 to the dooryard sun of 1673.

## Aesthetic logic

Adriaen van Ostade's pictures are built on a single tension: the peasant cottage against the light that falls into it. Everything he paints is a negotiation between the two. The cottage is Dutch - low, thatched, ladder-staired, vine-cloaked, a wreck you could live in, a room you could drink in, a loft you could sleep in. The light is the visitor - a lantern, a hidden candle, a sunbeam through a casement, the afternoon sun on a dooryard - and it does the moral work: it picks out one face, one hand, one jug, and leaves the rest in gloom. The joke is what joins them at first: one tight knot of thickset bodies, one bodily act, one laugh at the peasants' expense, staged in a small panel a collector could hold at arm's length. Then the joke softens, and the same mechanism carries a different meaning: the same cottage, the same short figures, the same single light - but now the act is a mother tending a cradle, a fiddler playing while the room listens, a family shelling mussels at the door, and the light says "Vivitur parvo bene: one may live well on little." Where Andries Both paints the appetite, Ostade paints the household; where Jan Both paints golden distance, Ostade paints the room you are already standing in. The picture does not illustrate a story. It is a moment you lean into, at close range, with one small hot pool of light and one small body doing one ordinary thing in it - and over fifty years the ordinary thing stops being funny and starts being enough.

## Positions

P1. After Rembrandt, Ostade is the most important "peintre-graveur" of 17th-century Holland. Source: Christie's introduction, read end to end.
P2. Hals influenced him very little; Brouwer had the decisive influence. Source: Dayton/Grove: "Hals influenced him very little, whereas Brouwer... had a decisive influence on the evolution of Adriaen van Ostade's always idiosyncratic portrayal of peasant life." National Gallery agrees: "More important for him than Hals was the influence of the Flemish painter, Adriaen Brouwer."
P3. The early Rembrandt influence is overstated by the older authorities. The supposed key work, the Annunciation to the Shepherds (Brunswick), was reattributed in 1982 to Benjamin Gerritsz Cuyp, and the Hermit Reading is the only work showing any affinity to Rembrandt in form or content. Source: Dayton/Grove.
P4. His meaning shifts from satire to ideal: in the 1650s, in place of or alongside the traditional satire on human frailty, the simplicity of peasant life is held up as a model - "Vivitur parvo bene: 'One may live well on little'" - or even idealised in the manner of bucolic poetry. Source: Dayton/Grove.
P5. Ostade stresses the humble dignity of the Dutch peasant, where Steen stresses materialistic pride and prosperity; the peasant serves as a moralising emblem and a vehicle for anti-bourgeois satire, but the treatment is sympathetic. Source: Knox.
P6. His etched work is the complement, not the supplement, of his painting; Binyon goes further and says his etchings have more interest than his pictures. Source: Binyon: "As with Rembrandt, his etched work is the complement, rather than a supplement merely, of his painting. To the present writer, indeed, his etchings have more interest than his pictures."
P7. In etching there is no real development to speak of: he was not, like Rembrandt, a master of many styles, and he seems to have attained his greatest mastery in a middle period, about 1650. Source: Binyon: "The etchings, as a matter of fact, do not present any marked variety of treatment... We can only say that he seems to have attained his greatest mastery in a middle period, about 1650." Note this sits in tension with the three-phase line change in M8, which is a technical change, not a stylistic development.
P8. He never travelled beyond Holland and never changed his home except from one street of Haarlem to another. A stay-at-home career of fifty years in one town. Source: Binyon.
P9. He was a prosperous, well-to-do middle-class citizen, not a boor: his portrait shows a grave man of severe, keen countenance in plain attire, and his estate sale contained 200 of his own paintings, proving him far removed from want. Source: Binyon; Wikipedia (over 200 unsold pictures at death).
P10. His works employ allegory, metaphor and double-entendres in the manner popular at the time; the drawings are layered with meaning and present images which both delight and instruct. Source: Christie's, quoting Martin Royalton-Kisch.
P11. The Beggar in a Large Coat cannot automatically be read as a moralising critique: the figure lacks the identifying traits of the suspect beggar (no tattered clothing, no crutch), the apron and vest may indicate an injured shopkeeper or artisan, and the concealed hand was also used to connote a philosopher. Source: Knox, citing Linda Stone-Ferrier.
P12. His pupils Bega and Dusart reveal his finer qualities by contrast: Bega's frankly artificial chiaroscuro - light and shadow as masses of black and white - contrasts with Ostade's solid modelling, pervading atmosphere, and preoccupying human interest. Source: Binyon.
P13. The late turn to watercolour, and the drop in oil production, is attributed by Houbraken to the strictures of the French invasion of 1672. Source: Sotheby's, citing Houbraken vol. I, p. 347. Houbraken is a weak witness (see D7), so this is an attribution, not a fact.
P14. Against Hamerton's charge of deadness of heart and apathy of intellect toward the poor, Binyon argues Ostade is free from real vulgarity: in Saying Grace there is a tenderness and dignity, the toil-hardened hands trembling as they are clasped, the boy shutting his eyes resolutely tight; in The Child and the Doll there is true feeling expressed with fine reticence. Source: Binyon.
