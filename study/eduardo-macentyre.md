# Eduardo MacEntyre: Study Notes

**Site:** Eduardo MacEntyre, 20 February 1929 - 5 May 2014, Buenos Aires -- Argentine painter, self-taught, co-founder of Generative Art (Arte Generativo) in 1959 with Miguel Angel Vidal, the movement that turned a single drawn line into a machine for making movement on a flat canvas (C8801, C8803). He died aged 85; the dates agree across the English and Spanish Wikipedias, Encyclopedia.com and Artsy, and this study prints them without a conflict (C8801, C8802, C8803, C8804). He never held a formal teaching post that any source read names, and no teacher of his is named either: he graduated as an industrial draughtsman in 1948, worked in graphic design, and taught himself painting by studying old masters -- Durer, Holbein, Rembrandt -- before passing through figuration, neo-impressionism, cubism and constructivism to reach geometric abstraction (C8802, C8804, C8805). His base was Buenos Aires for his whole life, with shows in Washington, Rio, New York, Paris, London and Sao Paulo (C8812, C8813). The work that matters is the generative line: hundreds of fine lines, drawn by hand with a compass, multiplied by displacement from a small generating point, until the flat field shimmers, lifts, and seems to move (C8805, C8879, C8882).

## The life: the draughtsman who taught himself to make lines move

Eduardo MacEntyre was born in Buenos Aires on 20 February 1929, to a Scottish father and a Belgian mother (C8801, C8802). The Spanish Wikipedia narrows the birthplace to Barracas, Buenos Aires (C8802). His Belgian maternal grandfather, a journalist writing for Buenos Aires newspapers, and his mother encouraged his vocation by giving him paints, brushes and paper; he began pursuing sketching at about twenty, studying compositional standards such as Durer, Holbein and Rembrandt (C8801, C8802). He studied industrial drawing from 1945 and graduated as an industrial draughtsman in 1948, then destroyed all the work he had made up to that point (C8805, C8820). He worked in graphic design in the late 1940s (C8804), and passed through figurative painting, neo-impressionism, cubism and constructivism before reaching geometric abstraction (C8805).

He was active in Grupo Joven in the early 1950s alongside Miguel Angel Vidal, Victor Magarinos, Carlos Filevich and Federico Martino, studying the roots of Concrete Art, Max Bill, Georges Vantongerloo and the Bauhaus (C8805, C8905). The dates conflict -- Artsy gives 1949-1951, Arte-Online and the Spanish Wikipedia say he joined in 1952 -- and this study follows 1952, the version with two independent sources, carrying 1949-1951 as unresolved (C8804, C8805, C8802). He also joined the commission of the Asociacion Arte Nuevo, helping write and edit its magazine A.N. (C8802, C8805). His first public display was in 1954, in a group show at the Comte Art Gallery, Buenos Aires (C8801, C8802).

From 1958 he and Vidal began investigating Generative Art (C8805). A 1959 show at the Peuser Art Gallery brought his work to the attention of arts patron Ignacio Pirovano and Rafael Squirru, director of the Buenos Aires Museum of Modern Art (C8801, C8802). The same year he founded Generative Art with Vidal and Pirovano, according to Encyclopedia.com and the Spanish Wikipedia (C8803, C8806); the narrower version, from Arte-Online and the MACBA material, says he co-founded it with Vidal only, and that the term was suggested by Pirovano to describe an art that generates new forms (C8805). Artsy adds Squirru as a co-conceptualiser (C8804). This study follows the safest wording: founded with Vidal in 1959; Pirovano proposed the name and was a patron and promoter; Squirru presented the first show and is not a co-founder. The manifesto, signed by MacEntyre and Vidal, was presented at the first Generative Art exhibition at Galeria Peuser in 1960, sponsored by the Museum of Modern Art, with a prologue by Squirru and a concert by the contemporary-music group EUPHONIA in support (C8806). About thirty works were shown (Arte-Online anniversary note, reported as that note's claim only). The 2025 Fondo Nacional de las Artes exhibition presents the 1960 manifesto as the first optical-art manifesto in Latin America (C8819).

The 1960s carried him out of Buenos Aires without ever moving him out of it. He took part in the VI Sao Paulo Biennial in 1961, showed Generative Art at the Museum of Modern Art in Rio de Janeiro in 1962, and toured with "Arte Nuevo de la Argentina / New Art of Argentina" from the Di Tella in 1964 (C8812). In 1965 his "Generative Painting: Black, Red, Orange" entered MoMA (object 3.1967, Inter-American Fund) (C8814), and he was a participant in the 1965 Premio Nacional at the Di Tella, where Carlos Silva won (C8913; see Corrections for the conflicting prize-list claim). In 1967 he showed in MoMA's "Latin-American Art, 1931-1966" and in "The Emergent Decade" at the Guggenheim (C8812). In 1968 he showed with Brizzi, Polesello and Vidal at the Bonino Gallery in New York -- the four men in one room, two years before the cybernetic turn (C8813).

The cybernetic turn came in 1969. In March 1969 a group of Argentine artists, assisted by programmers, engineers and systems analysts from the University of Buenos Aires and the Escuelas Tecnicas ORT, began exploring computer-generated creative work under the Centro de Arte y Comunicacion (CAYC) (C8808). In August-September 1969 his computer-generated work was exhibited in "Arte y Cibernetica" at Galeria Bonino, Buenos Aires, the first exhibition in Argentina to explore digital imaging; it then toured other South American cities before being shown in the UK in 1970 (C8807). He made the drawings with software developed by IBM, working with the CAYC group invited by Jorge Glusberg; one of his two prints from the show is now in the Victoria and Albert Museum (C8809). The V&A holds a 1969 untitled screen print made from a computer-generated drawing (museum no. E.170-2008) (C8911), and two 1973 screenprints, catalogued as Computer Art, given by his estate (C8817).

The later decades are a long tail of prizes and retrospectives rather than new systems: First Prize at the Second Biennial of Latin American Printmaking, Puerto Rico, 1972; Premio Konex Diploma al Merito, Pintura Geometrica, 1982; honoured by the Organization of American States in 1986 for his contribution to modern art in the Americas (C8811, C8810, C8803). In the late 1990s he made a group of works interpreting African art, shown in the final section of the MACBA retrospective (C8805); the African series was shown as "Arte Africano" at the Centro Cultural Borges in 2001, the same year he showed jointly with his son Cristian Mac Entyre at the presidential residence in Olivos (C8813). He died in Buenos Aires on 5 May 2014, aged 85 (C8801, C8802). The posthumous retrospective "Eduardo Mac Entyre: Sutilezas de la linea" ran at MACBA from 28 October 2017 to 11 March 2018, curated by Cristina Rossi (C8805). In 2025 the Fondo Nacional de las Artes showed "Eduardo Mac Entyre y Miguel Angel Vidal: Arte generativo, el futuro a crear" at Casa Victoria Ocampo, forty works, curated by Maria Jose Herrera (C8819).

## M1 -- The generating point: every line has a birthplace

The manifesto definition, in the Buffalo AKG translation: "Generative painting ENGENDERS a series of optical sequences through the development generated by a form, for example a circle, a square, etc." (C8882). The practice, as Constellations describes it: the paintings begin with the point and the line, which through a series of interventions become circles that pulse with vitality (C8857). MacEntyre's own summary is the clearest statement of the mechanism in the whole file: "We started from this little spot, or this small straight line and we gave it movement. We have generated movement... We have made them move from one place to another, vibrate, spin" (C8879). The AKG Pintura generativa of 1969 (W09) makes the mechanism visible in a single image: a huge white line sphere fills the black square, and inside it a four-petaled flower of faint gold, green and blue arcs radiates from a small rainbow pinwheel disc at the centre -- every line family terminates at that one small coloured centre (C8861). The study author viewed this plate and confirms it: the pinwheel is not decoration, it is the origin the whole picture is generated from. The generating point is also the void's twin: the system produces a negative shape at its centre, and the point sits inside it (see M7).

**Takeaway:** start from one point and let everything be generated from it. A piece that uses MacEntyre should pick a single small generating point, draw every line family so it converges on or radiates from that point, and let the picture be the record of what the point generated; lines that start nowhere are decoration, and a picture with two generating points is two pictures.

## M2 -- Multiplication by displacement: one form, moved again and again

A generator -- a line or geometric figure -- through its movement multiplies and creates new objects and geometric forms; paintings are composed of hundreds of lines, some swirling in space, imitating movement and fading away (C8854). The Generative Art Index summary matches: lines and geometric elements proliferate under systematic rules of rotation, progression and colour to produce optically regenerating, seemingly moving fields (C8881). Davis describes the same operation: units of curved lines organized in accordance with constraint laws and subjected to inventive variations characterized by impeccable technique, bright color and energy (C8883). The method, as the 2025 Fondo Nacional de las Artes exhibition states it, consisted of repeating straight or curved lines over a uniform colour field, starting from a point or a line and producing consecutive displacements that suggest movement of forms on the plane (C8819). In the pictures this reads as density: where the displaced copies pack tight, the field shimmers and seems to lift off the ground; where they loosen, the form fades (C8856, C8883). Desarrollos Opuestos of 1964 (W02) is the cleanest demonstration: two opposed interlocking ring forms, one pale pink and one green, built from hundreds of fine parallel curved lines, crossing to make a figure-eight (C8860).

**Takeaway:** multiply by moving, never by copying. A piece should take one curved-line form and repeat it with small rotations and translations, hundreds of times, letting the copies pack tight where the form is dense and fade where it loosens; a scatter of unrelated curves is not multiplication, and a form repeated without displacement is a stamp.

## M3 -- The circumference as the core form: the circle is the purest movement

MacEntyre based his practice around the circumference, the enclosing boundary of a circle, which he considered the purest expression of movement (C8857). Every image-viewed work in the catalogue is built from circles, arcs or rings; squares and straight lines are rare and, in the African series, deliberately introduced as an aggressive contrast to the curve (C8874). Seis formas en dos circunferencias of 1966 (W05) is the thesis in one picture: two overlapping circumferences drawn in fine red and orange concentric lines make a wide horizontal vesica shape with a vertical almond void at the centre, on a white ground (C8858). The circle is never a container here; it is the generator itself, the form whose development engenders the optical sequence (C8882).

**Takeaway:** build everything from circles and arcs, and let the circle do the moving. A piece should take one circumference and generate the whole image by displacing it, overlapping it, and stepping its lines outward; a square used as a frame is fine, a square used as the generator is a different artist. The straight line is a spice, not a staple: use it once, late, to break the curve, never as the default.

## M4 -- Symmetry as the frame that keeps multiplication legible

Typical generative pieces are characterized by symmetry (C8856). Seen in the images: bilateral symmetry in Black, Red, Orange (W04) and in the 1971 serigraph (W13), radial symmetry in Concentracion (W07) and the AKG sphere (W09), twofold opposition in Desarrollos Opuestos (W02) (C8871). Symmetry is the frame inside which the line multiplication stays legible: hundreds of displaced lines would be noise without a mirror or a rotation to organise them, and the symmetry is what lets the eye read the displacement as a system rather than a tangle. The 1971 serigraph (W13) is the strictest case: a white and pale-yellow line field forming a four-lobed figure, mirror left-right and top-bottom, knotted at a diamond centre (C8871).

**Takeaway:** hold the multiplication inside a symmetry, and break it exactly once. A piece should generate its lines under bilateral or radial symmetry, then introduce a single deliberate asymmetry -- one accent colour, one shifted lobe, one bar out of place -- so the eye has somewhere to rest; perfect symmetry with no break is a mandala, and no symmetry at all is a tangle.

## M5 -- Vibration and optical movement: the shimmer is the picture

Multiplications of thin lines, sometimes barely visible, create chiaroscuro, vibration and movement in space, an optical illusion on the retina of the viewer; paintings based on rhythm bring to mind music (C8856). The mechanism is visible in W05 and W09: where line spacing tightens, the field shimmers and seems to lift off the ground; where it loosens, the form fades (C8856, C8883). MacEntyre's theories relied on random algorithms to achieve designs that caused optically perceived movement (C8883) -- and the randomness matters: the Fibonacci/nautilus comparison that follows his work everywhere is a critical analogy, not a claim he computed spirals; each work forms a helix pattern in no two sketches alike, and the randomness is the point of difference (C8876, C8883). The vibration is not animation and it is not a trick of playback; it is in the geometry, in the spacing of the lines, and it works on a still image.

**Takeaway:** the movement must be in the spacing, not in the playback. A piece should be judged by one question: does the flat image seem to move while nothing in it moves? Tighten line spacing where the form should shimmer and loosen it where the form should fade; if the answer needs animation to be yes, the piece has missed him. Randomise the displacement slightly -- no two passes alike -- because the helix is a likeness, not a formula.

## M6 -- Colour in families: one hue, stepped, never a mixed palette

Colour is organized in families, not mixed palettes: one or two hues plus white, stepped through close values -- red to orange in W04 and W05, pink to green in W02, blue-violet to white in W18 (C8856, C8871). Bright, contrasting palettes are typical, and have been compared to expanding goblets of flowers or exotic birds' plumes (C8856). The ground is usually solid and dark, and the lines glide over it, meet and separate, open and close, overlap, and move away from each other, or crash together with a sudden impact that produces its own movement and luminous energy (C8880). The white-ground exception, W05, proves the rule by inversion: on white, the same red-orange lines read as a delicate drawing rather than a luminous object. Late work breaks the sequence: the African series adds deep dark brown and violet, colours he had not used before, and the straight line as an aggressive element (C8874).

**Takeaway:** one family, stepped through close values, on a dark ground. A piece should pick a single hue family -- red to orange, pink to green, blue-violet to white -- step it through close values, and lay it on a solid dark ground so the lines read as luminous objects; a mixed palette is a different painter, and a pale ground turns the light into a drawing. Save the brown and violet and the straight line for the late break, never for the first picture.

## M7 -- The void: the empty centre where the eye rests

Nearly every image-viewed work has a deliberate empty centre: the almond void in W02 and W05, the diamond void in W04, the teardrop void in W07, the circular holes in the tori of W14 and W15, the bright point in W18 (C8871). The generating system produces a negative shape at its centre, and the void is where the eye rests. Concentracion of 1968 (W07) is the purest case: one large circle fills the square frame, built from concentric rings of fine lines in white, rust red, olive green and blue-violet, and all lines converge on a small teardrop-shaped dark void at the exact centre -- a target or mandala with a single generating point (C8871). Punto luminoso of 1987 (W18) inverts the void into a point: a vertical hourglass of fine blue, violet and white lines converges on a small bright point at the centre, the only bright spot in a dark field, with a short black dashed bar at the exact middle (C8871). The study author viewed this plate and confirms it: the hourglass pinches to nothing at the point, and the point is the picture.

**Takeaway:** leave a hole at the centre and let the system make it. A piece should generate its lines so they converge on a deliberate empty shape -- almond, diamond, teardrop, torus hole, or a single bright point -- and never fill it; the void is where the eye rests, and a picture with no void has nowhere to look. The void is not a gap in the design, it is the design's destination.

## M8 -- Hand execution despite the algorithmic look: the draughtsman's hand

Despite the algorithmic look and the Computer Art cataloguing of the 1973 screenprint (C8869), the paintings were acrylic (earlier oil) on canvas, with compositions transferred from huge sheets of tracing paper and paint applied with surgical precision using thin tools and guides (C8855). The works were sketched by hand following a series of random algorithms (C8876). Britannica notes the intersecting geometric lines give an illusion of volume, and the acrylics recall early 20th-century Constructivist Plexiglas sculpture, but their lack of tangible scale makes them read as pure light and line (C8884). The 1969 computer work is the exception that proves the rule: he drew with IBM software through CAYC and the ORT programmers, and the results were prints, not paintings -- the hand stayed in charge of the canvas (C8808, C8809). The tri-dimensional works -- curved acrylic arcs arranged in a circular rosette of interlocking C-shapes, casting soft shadows -- carry the same line system off the canvas and into relief (C8870). The hand is the point: a computer could generate the lines, and in 1969 it did, but the paintings are valuable because a draughtsman drew them.

**Takeaway:** draw it by hand, even when the system looks algorithmic. A piece should generate its lines by rule -- displacement, rotation, progression -- and then render them with a hand's irregularity, a slight wobble in the spacing, a line that is almost but not quite parallel to its neighbour; perfect machine regularity is a plot, and a plot is not a painting. The computer is for the print, not for the canvas.

## The aesthetic logic: the line that generates its own movement

MacEntyre's aesthetic is a single sentence: a line, repeated under a rule, makes its own movement. The manifesto says it ("engendering a series of optical sequences which are produced by the evolving of a given form"), his own summary says it ("we gave it movement... we have made them move from one place to another, vibrate, spin"), and the pictures say it without words (C8882, C8879). Everything else in the file is a consequence of that sentence. The circle is the form because it is the purest expression of movement (C8857). The symmetry is the frame because multiplication without a frame is noise (C8856). The colour family is the palette because one hue stepped through close values lets the eye read displacement as depth (C8856). The dark ground is the ground because lines on black read as luminous objects, and on white they read as drawings (C8880). The void is the centre because a system that generates outward must leave somewhere for the eye to rest (C8871). The hand is the execution because a rule followed by a hand has a wobble a plotter cannot fake (C8855, C8876).

The evolution is the sentence thinning out. Dense dark-ground mandalas from 1964 to 1974 (W02, W04, W05, W07, W09) give way to the single vertical hourglass of Punto luminoso in 1987 (W18), then to sparse loose arcs on pale cardboard in 1993 (W19), then to the African series of the 2000s, where a figurative source -- masks, fetishes, statuettes from his own collection -- is abstracted through the same generative curves but with new colours and the straight line used because it is more aggressive than the curve and creates the tension needed to release the power of the fetish (C8874). Density drops away late; the generating line is reduced to a few strokes. The 2021-2022 Misiones retrospective showed 22 works spanning 1960 to 2012, including the tri-dimensional reliefs (C8870). He is the self-taught half of Generative Art -- Vidal was formally trained and later taught, MacEntyre was not (C8901, C8906) -- and the work carries that difference: a draughtsman's precision, a graphic designer's rigour, and a painter who never learned the academy well enough to unlearn the compass.

## Network

The Generative Art core is a pair, not a crowd: MacEntyre and Vidal founded it together in 1959, Pirovano named it from Vantongerloo, Squirru promoted it (C8902, C8903, C8904). Vidal is the equal partner -- born 27 June 1928, died 15 January 2009, graduated from the Prilidiano Pueyrredon National School of Fine Arts in 1952, principal of that institution 1989-1991, professor at the Ernesto de la Carcova school, studied under Lino Enea Spilimbergo and Eugenio Daneri (C8906, C8907). The two men are treated as a pair in every source read: "Generative Art in Argentina is associated mainly with two names -- Eduardo Mac Entyre (1929-2014) and Miguel Angel Vidal (1928-2009)" (C8906). The earlier circle is Grupo Joven, with Vidal, Magarinos, Filevich and Martino (C8905). The shared rooms with Polesello (#633) are Peuser 1959, the Di Tella prize 1965, "Beyond Geometry" 1967, and Arte y Cibernetica 1969 (C8914, C8913, C8923, C8911). The cybernetic turn runs through CAYC and Jorge Glusberg: the Grupo de Arte y Cibernetica's founding artist members were Luis F. Benedit, Antonio Berni, Eduardo Mac Entyre, Osvaldo Romberg and Miguel Angel Vidal, working on an IBM 1130-16K with two plotters at the ORT School Computation Centre (C8910, C8911). The later circle is Brizzi and Silva: "Projection and Dynamics" 1972 at the Museo de Arte Moderno, Buenos Aires, then Paris, with MacEntyre, Vidal, Ary Brizzi and Carlos Silva (C8908); Brizzi is a second-generation generativist, not a 1959 founder (C8918, C8919). The 1940s roots are contextual only: no direct contact with Carmelo Arden Quin was found; the link runs through the Buenos Aires geometric tradition Arden Quin helped found with Madi in 1946, and through Polesello, who joined Arte Nuevo (founded by Arden Quin and Aldo Pellegrini) in 1959 (C8916, C8917). Vasarely and Max Bill are ambient models -- Vasarely exhibited in Buenos Aires in 1958, one year before the founding -- with no meeting, letter or direct statement of influence found; do not upgrade it to a mentorship (C8915). The older models MacEntyre himself is recorded as studying are Durer, Holbein and Rembrandt (C8921). Family: his son Christian Mac Entyre is also a well-known artist (C8920, C8803).
