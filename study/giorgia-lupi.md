# Giorgia Lupi: Study Notes

**Site:** Giorgia Lupi -- Italian information designer, partner at Pentagram New York since May 2019 (announced Monday 20 May 2019); co-founder of the data-driven research and design firm Accurat, Milan and New York, 2011; author with Stefanie Posavec of *Dear Data* (2016). Born Friday 24 July 1981, Finale Emilia, outside Modena, Italy -- the day and month are established from her own project documentation (the *Book of Life* stitches one stitch per day from her birth on 24 July 1981, per Pentagram and PRINT), not from a civil record. Based in New York City since 2012; living and active, with a first solo exhibition in Vicenza 30 April-2 August 2026 and a Laguna College of Art + Design show opening Thursday 3 December 2026. [C35941]

**Relevance:** Lupi helps investigate how a hand-built dataset becomes a legible picture: what encoding rules turn personal, irregular data into a glyph system a stranger can read without a legend, and whether those rules can run as code. Her evidence is the strongest in the sequence for that question -- fifty-two invented visual codes in *Dear Data*, each drawn by hand on a postcard with its key on the back, plus the petal-day composite glyph of *Bruises*, the brushstroke accumulation of *1,374 Days*, and the stitch-per-day ground of *Book of Life*. [C35942]

**Doorway:** The 444th doorway. Hop from the #823 Andrew McWhae study, which took the #822 question back to pure generative systems -- what a rule draws when nobody needs to read it, when there is no dataset behind the picture and no one the picture must reach, only a field, a mark, and a rule. Lupi is the return swing of that pendulum: after a study of systems that need no reader, the sequence turns back to data-to-form with the strongest possible exponent of it -- a designer who spent a career answering Govindarajan's question (data made legible, describable, hearable, teachable) by inventing a new visual code for every dataset and drawing the key on the back of the picture. [C35943]

**Depth:** Fifteen works viewed directly as images (six *Dear Data* postcards front and back, plus nine later works: *Bruises*, *The Room of Change*, *Book of Life*, *My 2020 in Data*, TED Data Portraits, two RAND pieces, the Gender Equality platform mark, *1,374 Days*), and seven further works described from textual sources only and flagged as such (Weeks 42, 24, 17, 41, 50, the TED talk, the & Other Stories collection). Read end to end: Lupi's "Data Humanism" manifesto (PRINT, Monday 30 January 2017); the Pentagram project pages for Dear Data, Bruises, Book of Life, The Room of Change, TED Data Portraits, RAND Art + Data, and the Gender Equality platform; the Fast Company and D&AD accounts of *1,374 Days*; the TED talk transcript; the full 52-week topic list at dear-data.com. Not read: the *Dear Data* book itself at full resolution (individual postcards were inspected as published reproductions, front and back where available); the MoMA collection images (Cloudflare challenge on every MoMA artwork page); the dear-data.com weekly posts (no longer served as indexable pages); the Adweek inline postcard images (removed from the current page); the Week 16 key in full (the published back image is cropped at its left edge). No work was handled in person. All weekdays stated in this study were verified with `date -d` in America/New_York. [C35944]

## The question: who is the code for, and can a stranger read it?

Govindarajan's systems are open at the far end: the picture is not finished until someone else can enter it. McWhae's systems are closed at both ends: no dataset, no user, no second rendering. Lupi's systems are open in a different direction from both. The dataset is hers -- complaints, apologies, laughter, phone pickups, symptoms, a child's bruises, a whole life -- but the picture is not finished until a stranger can decode it, which is why the key is drawn on the back of every postcard and the legend sits at the front of *Book of Life*. That is the whole question she hands the gallery: legibility is not a property of the data, it is a property of the code, and the code is the artwork. A generator that invents a fresh glyph grammar per dataset and renders its own key produces pictures that are genuinely different every run, yet all recognisably Lupian in method -- and a generator that skips the key produces decoration, however pretty its marks. [C35945]

## A life in data: Ferrara, Accurat, Pentagram

Lupi's biography explains the method without being the method. Only child, grandmothers who were seamstresses; as a girl she collected and categorised objects -- buttons, stones, receipts -- by size, colour, and dimension, and she traces her data practice to that childhood sorting. Master's degree in architecture, University of Ferrara, 2006; she has said applying to architecture was her "way of not choosing", that she has never built a house, and that an architect's job is designing representations of buildings -- a system of symbols conveying information. After graduating she worked with two interaction-design firms in Italy (names not stated in any source read), mostly on interactive installations and mapping projects. In 2011 she co-founded Accurat with three other partners and began a PhD in Design at Politecnico di Milano; in 2012 she moved to New York City, where she is still based. Accurat's clients included Google, IBM, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, Starbucks, the United Nations, the WHO, MoMA, the World Economic Forum, TED, and Corriere della Sera, for whose Sunday supplement *La Lettura* she made full-spread experimental visualisations in 2012-2014. She left Accurat and joined Pentagram New York as partner in May 2019 -- the first new Pentagram partner in New York in seven years, and the only partner focused on information and data design. [C35946]

*Dear Data* ran from Monday 1 September 2014: for one year Lupi in New York and Stefanie Posavec in London exchanged a hand-drawn data postcard every week, 52 pairs, 104 postcards, each week a different slice of daily life, drawing on the front and a handwritten decoding key on the back. The book followed in 2016 (Princeton Architectural Press, foreword by Maria Popova), and the original postcards and sketchbooks entered MoMA's permanent collection -- acquired 2016 (accession numbers 962.2016.x; a Medium announcement is dated Tuesday 22 November 2016), though Wikipedia says 2017, a conflict carried, not settled. In January 2017 she named the approach in PRINT: "Data Humanism: The Revolutionary Future of Data Visualization" (Monday 30 January 2017), with four rules -- embrace complexity, move beyond standards, sneak context in, remember data is imperfect. Her TED talk "How we can find ourselves in data" was filmed Wednesday 8 March 2017 at TEDNYC and published Friday 7 April 2017 (one aggregator lists 4 May 2017 -- the split is flagged in the corrections section; the 7 April date follows the TED Talks episode listing). A second TED talk, "What Long Covid Has Taught Me About Life (and Data)", followed in 2024 (TED at BCG, September 2024). The honours: 2022 Cooper Hewitt National Design Award in Communication Design, announced Wednesday 7 September 2022 -- Pentagram notes she was the first data-visualisation practitioner ever named in that category -- plus an honorary doctorate from MICA the same year; Compasso d'Oro 2025 for *1,374 Days*, awarded Friday 5 September 2025 at the Italian Pavilion, Expo 2025 Osaka. Her books after *Dear Data*: *Observe, Collect, Draw!* (2018, with Posavec), *This Is Me and Only Me* (Corraini, 2024, a children's book introducing data to kids), and *Speak Data* (with Phillip Cox, Princeton Architectural Press, Tuesday 11 November 2025). [C35947]

## M1. One mark per event, one code per week: the glyph grammar (Dear Data)

Six postcards were viewed front and back, and they are six different visual languages for six different weeks. Week 1, "What's the Time?" (September 2014): a dense field of tiny hand-drawn glyphs in vertical columns grouped by hour of day, almost typographic; the key reads "each symbol represents every moment I glanced at the clock, grouped by hours of the day", with a different symbol for each motive -- on purpose, just glanced, thought of the project, thought "don't look!" but did, bored, hungry, heard somebody say the time aloud -- plus attributes for lateness, analog support, alarm, and texting Stefanie. Week 7, "Musical Complaints" (October 2014): ten horizontal pencil staves, one per complaint typology, filled with black-ink notes, a few in red; the key is a full notation system (see M2). Week 8, "Phone Addiction!" (November 2014): eight circles of varying size, each a place she checked her phone, ordered left to right by frequency, every radial tick one phone interaction in chronological order, ticks coloured by reason; outside the circle means picked up purposely, inside means because of an alert. Week 16, "How Many Dresses!" (December 2014): hundreds of vertical coloured lines hanging like garments on rails, dresses positioned exactly as ordered inside the wardrobe -- the most literally pictorial card viewed, the wardrobe drawn as itself. Week 39, "Beauty!" (June 2015): six large coloured discs like suns, each ringed with thin radiating lines tipped with coloured dots; line length is the level of engagement (short "beautiful!", medium "woah!", long "wooooooah!"), dot colour at the tip is why. Week 45, "I Am Sorry!" (August 2015): a branching tree, each "J" one apology, length the real need to apologise, colour the cause, branch height the person, branch level the medium, a bar above or below the J the language (English above, Italian below). Two further weeks are known only from textual description and are flagged as such: Week 42, laughter as pastel frilled blossoms (colour the person, tendril direction whose laughter, size the intensity), and Week 52, goodbyes as polygons (shape the person, colour the person, sub-glyph the context, side-count the place). [C35948]

The mechanism is a fresh mapping function per dataset, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- no code or process post by Lupi describes a generative procedure; every rule here is reverse-engineered from the viewed cards and their keys, never her documented procedure). For each dataset define encode(event) -> glyph with one visual channel per variable: shape for one categorical variable, colour for a second, size for a magnitude, position for time (day column, or reading order). Parameters: one glyph per event; Dear Data weeks run roughly 10-100 events; three to five visual channels per glyph maximum -- more becomes unreadable, and Lupi's own cards stay at shape plus colour plus size plus position. The key is part of the mechanism, not an appendix: it is drawn on the back in the same hand, with example glyphs, arrows, and marginal notes about how the data was collected and what it leaves out (see M6). What varies between weeks is everything -- the mark, the layout, the channels -- and what stays constant is the discipline: one mark per event, one code per dataset, the code fully keyed. [C35949]

**Takeaway:** the invention of the code is the artwork, not the data. A builder who copies Lupi's colours or her hand-drawn wobble has a style; a builder who invents a fresh glyph grammar per dataset and renders its own key has the mechanism. The six viewed cards are six different pictures from the same discipline, which is why this is the most generative mechanism in the study: it produces a family, not a single image. [C35950]

## M2. Time as a staff: the score (Dear Data Weeks 7 and 32)

Week 7, viewed front and back, is the purest form: ten horizontal pencil staves, one per complaint typology in chronological order (Me as a person, Me at work, Work, Technology, Service/Food, Somebody, Cold, How I feel, Boyfriend, Other), filled with black-ink notes. The notation is complete: each note is one complaint said aloud; note height on the staff is the need to complain (top actual need, middle average, low no real need; an X below the staff is a missed complaint, thought of but not said); filled note to the boyfriend, open note to friend or family, crossed note to a stranger; "99" said in English, the rest in Italian; small dot-plus-circle via text or email; a sharp sign adds emphasis; a slur means close in time, same situation; a red note is to Stefanie, a red dot is about Dear Data itself. The card is sparse and airy and genuinely reads as sheet music. Week 32, a week of sounds heard, is known only from a textual description and is flagged as such: each sound a note bearing a different symbol, drawn on a colour-coded stave corresponding to where it was heard, note length relating to the prevalence of the sound. Posavec's Week 32 used stacked coloured rectangles by time of day -- same data, different grammar -- which is itself evidence that the mechanism is the stave mapping, not the rectangles. [C35951]

The mechanism is a timeline the eye already knows how to read, stated as rules (leg formalisation). x is time of day or sequence order; y-lane is location, one colour-coded stave per place (typically three to six lanes); one note per sound or speech event; note length scales linearly with count or duration; note symbol comes from a small fixed set (eight types or fewer); vertical position within the stave carries a second continuous variable (need, intensity, pitch of feeling). The staff does two jobs at once: it orders the events left to right, and it makes the gaps rhythmic -- a dense passage, a rest, a single note alone on a stave. [C35952]

**Takeaway:** a score is both a chart and a picture, and it generalises further than any other Dear Data grammar: any time-stamped categorical stream -- notifications, interruptions, birdsong, footsteps, door slams -- can be scored, and the "play it back" reading is built in. It is also the most animatable of the Dear Data mechanisms: notes can appear in real time, left to right, and the staff can be read as it is written. [C35953]

## M3. One petal per day: the composite glyph (Bruises)

*Bruises -- The Data We Don't See* (with musician Kaki King; developed 2017, premiered January 2018; shown in the Nature Cooper Hewitt Design Triennial 2019-20) was viewed in full, artwork and legend panel. The subject is 120 days of records on the illness of King's young daughter -- ITP, an autoimmune disease attacking platelets -- kept daily by her mother. The image is a meandering vine of about 120 white petal-like "days" in clusters on a pale warm-grey ground, joined by thin lines, with red and orange dot clusters at the head of each group, pink speckles on petals, purple and green splotches, a few bright yellow spots, and faint handwritten notes filling the background. The legend panel ("Data visualization explanation") was read in full: petechiae are small pink dots on each petal, denser where more present; bruise intensity is purple and yellow splotches, bigger and more colourful where broader and deeper; grey shapes at the right of the day are steroid days; coloured-pencil marks at the bottom of the petal are incidents (fell at the park, bitten by a mosquito); a black dot is Kaki away from home on tour; yellow brush marks are positive moments; purple lines framing the day are Kaki's fear on a 1-10 scale; orange lines are her hope on a 1-10 scale. Pentagram's text adds the group structure: each new group of days starts with a blood test, and red dots at the group head are the platelet count, one dot per platelet, against a normal range of 150-400 -- the opening counts were 1, 7, and 30. King turned the same dataset into music: 120 measures in 3/4 time, one per day, the guitar part "a musical map of the bruises" -- a second medium, not part of this mechanism. [C35954]

The mechanism is a composite glyph carrying hard and soft data in the same mark, fully documented by Pentagram (not a leg formalisation -- the encoding is published). One white petal-like element is one day; days are grouped into sections, a new group starting at each lab test or admission, so medical rhythm, not calendar rhythm, structures the picture; red dot at group start is the platelet count; purple or green splotch on the petal is bruise, size and colour intensity proportional to breadth and depth; tiny pink dots on the petal are petechiae count, density proportional to count; grey brush stroke overlaid is a steroid day; coloured-pencil strokes are a skin-worsening incident; black dot is mother away on tour; bright yellow spot is a positive moment; two floating lines framing the day are hope and fear, each on a 1-10 scale. Parameters: about 120 days; eight simultaneous channels on one petal; hope and fear scale 1-10; platelet values plotted against the 150-400 normal band, so the visual shock of a count of 1 against a band of 150-400 is the point. Handwritten daily notes surround the petals, key words highlighted. [C35955]

**Takeaway:** this is the richest single mechanism in Lupi's work and the strongest single-piece candidate in the study, because it is fully documented, directly executable (a petal is a day-object with eight fields), and emotionally loaded by the encoding rather than by illustration -- a field of pale petals with a few violent splotches. It also breaks the calendar: the timeline re-segments at each hospital visit, which is a rule any long diary can borrow. [C35956]

## M4. A calendar that fills until it chokes (1,374 Days)

*1,374 Days: My Life With Long Covid* (New York Times visual OpEd, online Thursday 14 December 2023; print Sunday Opinion supplement Sunday 24 December 2023, retitled "1,380 Days With Long Covid"; Compasso d'Oro 2025; Fast Company Innovation by Design 2024 for data design) was viewed in six images. The subject is Lupi's own long COVID, March 2020 to late 2023, tracked daily in a spreadsheet: symptoms, severity, medications, food, triggers, movement tolerance. The daily-strip pages show cream ground and serif essay text, symptom words highlighted with textured brushstroke swatches; below each passage a seven-day strip, each day a cluster of two to four vertical textured brushstrokes in different colours, crossed by a single horizontal black line, a handwritten label above each day naming the dominant symptom. 1-7 April 2020: fever, brain fog, flu-like, tachycardia and palpitations, various other symptoms (insomnia, blurred vision), fatigue, burning. 6-12 June 2022: all-blue clusters (pain), the first day annotated with dashed leaders -- back pain, knee pain, hip pain, chest pain -- the blue dripping downward. The full calendar shows rows of months, March 2020 to December 2023, each day a small cluster of coloured brushstrokes crossed by a thin black line; the legend reads "EACH COLOR REPRESENTS A SYMPTOM" (fever purple, fatigue darker purple, headache pale blue, burning pink-red, nausea pink, pain dark purple-blue, brain fog grey-blue, plus chills, tachycardia and palpitations, tinnitus, nose itchiness, flu-like symptoms, yellow for other). Handwritten annotations with dashed leaders mark turning points: "I GOT COVID IN MARCH 2020", "SO I STARTED TRACKING MY DATA AND VISUALIZING IT", "TRACKING MY DATA BECAME MY FORM OF JOURNALING", "MY FIRST VACCINE March 11, 2021", "MY SECOND VACCINE April 7, 2021". The calendar visibly shifts colour: the 2022 rows are dominated by deep blue (the pain period); 2023 returns to mixed purple, pink, and yellow. The facing page quantifies the ordeal in brushstroke icons: 46 doctors, 59 blood draws, 63 medications, 95 supplements, 15 MRIs, 12 X-rays, 4 CT scans, above a grid of small handwritten hope statements from fellow sufferers. [C35957]

The mechanism is accumulation, documented by Communication Arts, Fast Company, and the TED transcript (the exact severity-to-opacity mapping is leg formalisation from description). One brushstroke per symptom per day; hue is the symptom type; stroke opacity and length scale with severity that day; strokes aggregate by day into a calendar grid (four years of days); a second symbol layer sits on top -- dots and lines for doctor's appointments, tests, medications, hospitalisations, vaccinations, reinfections; handwritten annotations mark turning points. Parameters: 1,374 days; about 10-15 symptom hues; severity a continuous variable mapped to stroke weight and opacity; the annotation layer sparse (only a handful of dated notes across four years). In the NYT scroll piece the test-result lists pile up on screen as you scroll and a body figure is progressively overwhelmed by symptom marks -- that pile-up is presentation, not the mechanism; the accumulation rule is the mechanism. [C35958]

**Takeaway:** a calendar that fills until it chokes is a powerful generative image, and the rule generalises to any long daily log -- pain, mood, weather, screen time -- rendered as accumulating strokes, where the density of the field is its meaning. The colour shift across years (the blue pain period) is the proof that the encoding carries narrative without any illustration at all. [C35959]

## M5. One stitch per day, almost invisible (Book of Life)

*Book of Life* (Moleskine Foundation, 2021) was viewed in two images: the title page and the legend spread, photographed in full. Three Moleskine notebooks were disassembled and reassembled as a leporello (accordion book). The title page reads, handwritten: "GIORGIA LUPI / THIS IS AN ACCOUNT / OF MY LIFE SO FAR. / 2021". The legend spread reads: "HOW TO READ IT: ONE WHITE STITCH IS ONE DAY FROM WHEN I WAS BORN. GREEN VERTICAL STITCHES MARK THE FIRST DAY OF THE YEAR. ON TOP OF THEM, I STITCHED THE MOMENTS THAT I REMEMBER THE MOST..." The total is 14,496 white running stitches, one per day of her life from her birth on Friday 24 July 1981 to about age 40, nearly invisible against the white paper; coloured second stitches mark remembered events. The photographed legend is richer than the press summaries: event types are red-pink time passing; yellow life achievements; teal travel (big trips); pale blue moving apartments; pale yellow school, work; peach world events that might have changed how I perceive things; black X losses; red love; red X break-ups; grey health related issues; brown sports, music. And there is a duration code the articles omit: one stitch is a one-day event, two stitches one day to one month, three stitches over one month. A tangle of coloured thread ends is visible on the facing page -- the reverse of the stitching, the work's underside. [C35960]

The mechanism is an inversion, documented by Pentagram (the stitch count itself is flagged, not verified). One running stitch per day of life, in white thread on white paper, deliberately almost imperceptible; a second coloured stitch overlaid on selected days to mark significant events; green vertical stitches marking the first day of each year; a legend at the start of the book mapping thread colour to event type and stitch count to duration. Parameters: 14,496 stitches as reported by Pentagram and the German Design Council -- flagged discrepancy: Friday 24 July 1981 to Saturday 24 July 2021 is 14,610 days by arithmetic (verified), 114 more than the reported stitch count, so "one stitch per day" is carried here as the stated rule, not a verified count; coloured event stitches are a small minority (dozens, not thousands, per the descriptions). The ground is the mundane; the figure is the memorable. [C35961]

**Takeaway:** the inversion is the idea -- draw the unremarkable days as the field and let rare events puncture it. In code, a long uniform sequence (days, keystrokes, steps) rendered as near-identical marks, with a sparse second channel for events, reads as texture first and biography second. It is the quietest mechanism in the study and the only one where the picture is mostly blank, which is exactly why it would stand out in a gallery of dense, saturated pieces. [C35962]

## M6. The key is part of the picture

The decoding apparatus is a mechanism in its own right, and it is the one that separates Lupi from every chart-maker in the sequence. The back of every *Dear Data* postcard carries the key in the same hand as the front: example glyphs, arrows, marginal notes, and asides about how the data was collected and what it leaves out. Pentagram's description is exact: "on the reverse... the designer would squeeze in detailed keys to her drawing, a code to enable the recipient to decipher the picture". *Book of Life* opens with the same apparatus as a legend spread: "HOW TO READ IT", the stitch rule, the colour list, the duration code. *Bruises* ships with its own "Data visualization explanation" panel. *1,374 Days* prints its legend at the top of the calendar: "EACH COLOR REPRESENTS A SYMPTOM". In every case the front is initially unreadable ornament and the key turns it into language; reading is a two-step act, and the second step is designed as carefully as the first. [C35963]

The mechanism is a generated legend, stated as rules (leg formalisation -- no source states the legend as a "generated" object; the two-sided picture-and-key structure is documented, the executable formulation is the leg's). The encoding mapping is itself a data structure: legend = {channel: {value: visual}} plus free-text annotations and asides. The generator must emit both the picture and its key from the same mapping, in the same hand-drawn register -- the key is drawn, not typeset, with example glyphs, arrows, and marginal notes ("sneak context in": how the data was collected, what it leaves out). The key is also the natural interaction surface: tap a glyph and its key entry lights up; tap a key entry and every matching glyph lights up. A piece that ships without a key is decoration; a piece that generates its own legend is legible in Lupi's exact sense. [C35964]

**Takeaway:** most generative pieces ship without a key and are therefore decoration. The generated legend is the cheapest way to make a piece legible, and it is also the most honest interaction in this study: it does not add a control, it completes the picture. Any prompt built from M1, M2, or M3 should carry its key as part of the piece, not as a caption. [C35965]

## M7. A questionnaire that draws your portrait (TED Data Portraits)

TED Data Portraits (TED2017, Vancouver, for Target) was viewed in two images: a grid of about 91 round wearable buttons (7 by 13) on light grey, and a photograph of three attendees wearing them on red TED lanyards. Each button is a unique portrait on the same template: a diagonal split ground (cream or pale yellow above; red, blue, or yellow below), a small black hand-drawn glyph (grids, scribbles, building-like lines), and tiny handwritten text. Same template, visibly different combinations. The system, from Pentagram's text: during the five-day conference a tablet app built by Accurat asked attendees non-invasive multiple-choice questions -- "Which TED letter are you, Technology, Entertainment or Design?", "When do you get your best ideas?", "How messy is your desk?", "Do you follow the rules, or break them?" -- and mapped each answer to shapes, colours, and symbols, replicating Lupi's hand-drawn style and calligraphy. More than 500 portraits were made during the conference. The concept was revived at the Wellesley "Renewing Democracy" summit on Saturday 6 April 2024. [C35966]

The mechanism is a parametric portrait from a questionnaire, stated as rules (leg formalisation from Pentagram's description and the viewed grid). A fixed template with N slots (ground split, glyph, text); each slot's value is a function of one questionnaire answer; the mapping is many-to-one by design, so two people with the same answers get the same portrait, and the portrait is a fingerprint of the answers, not of the person. Parameters: four to six questions, two to four answers each; ground colour from one answer, glyph family from another, glyph rotation or scale from a third; the app renders the result in the hand-drawn register (wobble, uneven stroke) so the output reads as drawn, not computed. The portraits work as conversation starters because they are comparable at a glance: same template, different combinations, and the differences are the conversation. [C35967]

**Takeaway:** this is the only mechanism in the study where the data comes from someone else in real time, which makes it the most directly playable: a piece that asks the viewer four questions and draws their portrait is a Lupi piece that needs no dataset at all. The template discipline -- same frame, different combinations -- is also the most controllable of her systems, and the one most easily verified in a harness (same answers, same portrait). [C35968]

## M8. Wallpaper that is data (The Room of Change)

*The Room of Change* (Accurat, for the XXII Triennale di Milano, "Broken Nature", 2019, curator Paola Antonelli) was viewed in three images: the concept sketch, the printed legend, and the installed wallpaper. The room is the opening gallery of the Triennale: 100-foot-long data murals wrapping three walls, from 1000 BCE to a speculative 2400 CE, plus two screens of NASA "Images of Change" satellite pairs. The concept sketch shows a hand-drawn three-wall room in perspective, horizontal coloured stripe bands (blue, pink-red, orange, yellow) along the walls, annotations for six relations ("Humans and nature / Humans and Humans / Humans and Society / Humans and Technology / Humans and Science / Humans and Animals"), a floor arrow reading "past to present to future", and a "ZOOM" detail pairing each main data story with its data context. The printed legend ("How to interpret it") reads: time flows left to right; each horizontal stripe is one story of change; eight macro topics -- Nature (disappearing Aral Sea, human impact, climate change), Universe (apocalypse predictions, lunar and solar eclipses, space exploration), Animal Kingdom (South American extinctions, bees, cattle and chickens), Society (universal suffrage, world population, terrorism), Hope (Cambodia civil war and life expectancy, death, birth), Happiness (depression in Uganda, cigarettes, alcohol), Science (chemical elements, vaccines and antibiotics, epidemics), Technology (South Korea, fossil fuel, access to technology). Shape is the theme, colour is geographical or cultural affinity, horizontal position is time; rectangle height is the depth of the Aral Sea, pattern area the population of South America. The installed wallpaper reads at a glance as wallpaper -- dense horizontal bands of tiny repeated glyphs (triangles, vertical bars, crosses, dots, dashes) in muted pastel pink, blue, yellow, and grey with thin black rules -- and only on close inspection as data. [C35969]

The mechanism is the same glyph grammar as M1, run at architectural scale (leg formalisation from the viewed legend and installation). One horizontal stripe per story of change; within a stripe, one tiny repeated glyph per data point; shape encodes theme, colour encodes affinity, horizontal position encodes time; a local story frames each global dataset (the Aral Sea for climate, Cambodian life expectancy for hope, Korean exports for technology), so the wall is readable at two distances -- as pattern from the door, as argument from a foot away. Parameters: eight macro topics; stripes in the hundreds; glyph size small enough that the wall reads as texture at distance and as data up close; the two-distance reading is the acceptance test. [C35970]

**Takeaway:** this is M1 at room scale, and it proves the grammar survives being blown up a hundredfold: the same rule (one mark per event, shape and colour as channels) that fits on a postcard also fills a 100-foot wall. For the gallery it offers the one thing no postcard-scale prompt can: a piece that is wallpaper from the door and a document from a foot away. [C35971]

## M9. Observe, Collect, Draw: the loop that makes the data

The last mechanism is not a picture but the loop that produces every picture in this study, and it is the only one that answers where the data comes from. Lupi's process, documented in the PRINT manifesto and in her own accounts: choose one topic on a Monday, observe and jot for a week, then draw. Three states with explicit rules: (1) Observe -- pick one countable, qualitative topic (complaints, doors, smells, envies -- never a metric the machine already tracks); (2) Collect -- log each occurrence with a timestamp and two to three attributes, by hand, for a fixed window (seven days in Dear Data, 120 days in Bruises, 1,374 days in the COVID diary); missing data is recorded as missing, never interpolated; (3) Draw -- invent the grammar after seeing the data's shape ("sketch with data... I draw with data in my mind, but with no data in my pen", PRINT 2017). The manifesto adds the governing constraints, documented in PRINT (2017) and summarised in the 2025 arXiv characterisation "Data Humanism Decoded": embrace complexity (do not simplify to one takeaway), move beyond standards (no default chart types), sneak context in (annotations, collection notes, what was left out), remember data is imperfect (render uncertainty, approximation, gaps). Parameters: collection window seven days (Dear Data) up to 1,374 days; one topic per window; two to four attributes per event. The state-machine phrasing is leg formalisation; the three phases and the four principles are documented. [C35972]

**Takeaway:** M9 is a process rule that should govern how any of the other mechanisms get their data, not a piece on its own. For a generative piece it answers the one question the other mechanisms leave open: the piece can simulate a week of small-data collection -- a synthetic diary with realistic burstiness, missed days, and annotation -- then encode it. The imperfection rules (gaps stay gaps, uncertainty shown as wobble or fading) are what keep the result from looking like a clean dashboard. [C35973]

## The arc: small data, bigger rooms

What stayed constant from 2014 to 2026: one mark per event, one code per dataset, the key drawn beside the picture, and the hand -- ink, pencil, marker, coloured pencil, brushstroke, stitch -- as the rendering engine. What changed is the scale and the subject. 2014-15: the postcard, one week, one person, two friends by mail. 2017: the talk and the manifesto, the same grammar turned into a public argument, and the first live system (TED Data Portraits, 500+ portraits from a questionnaire). 2018-19: other people's data at larger scales -- a child's illness as petals, a census as 10,591 data portraits on a wall over 100 square metres ("...ma poi, che cos'e un nome?", Triennale Milano, 2018), a 100-foot room of global change, a fashion collection from three women's lives (Ada Lovelace's 36-step Note G algorithm as coloured rectangles, Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* as floating bird and butterfly forms, Mae Jemison's 126 orbits as 126 circles on a puffer coat -- the piece count differs across sources, 12, 15, or 16, and is not asserted here). 2020-21: the pandemic diaries -- *My 2020 in Data (So Far)*, NYT "At Home" cover, Sunday 22 November 2020, white dots for events, red dots for "lasts", blue dots for "firsts", wavy lines for the blur of virtual events; the RAND residency (a head in profile bursting with wavy coloured lines, each bundle one mental-health statistic; a loom-like frame of yellow discs as a walk-around dot-plot of income); *Book of Life*. 2023-26: the long-COVID calendar, the subway love letter on 50 screens at Fulton Center in 2025, the Triennale identity in 2025, *Speak Data* in 2025, and the first solo exhibition in Vicenza in 2026. The through-line, stated as interpretation: the code never got bigger, the rooms did -- and the hand never left, even when a tablet app did the drawing. [C35974]

## Aesthetic logic: small marks, invented codes, drawn keys

Three constants. First, the mark is small and the dataset is small: one complaint, one apology, one glance at a clock, one day, one stitch -- never a big dataset, never a default chart. The picture's density comes from a week of attention, not from volume. Second, colour is a channel, never decoration: in every viewed work, colour encodes a variable (person, place, symptom, event type, affinity), and no work in this study uses colour merely to be pretty -- which is why the "hand-drawn watercolour palette" reading of Lupi is wrong, and why her palettes vary completely from one dataset to the next. Third, the key is part of the picture: the front is ornament until the back is read, and the two-sidedness is deliberate -- concealment, then revelation. What is absent throughout: default chart types (no bar charts, no pie charts, no line graphs as the picture), dashboards, real-time feeds, and any claim that the data speaks for itself -- the manifesto's point is that it never does, and that the designer must take responsibility for the code. The work trusts a small, invented, fully-keyed code applied with total consistency to a hand-collected dataset -- and the code, not the data, is the artwork. [C35975]
