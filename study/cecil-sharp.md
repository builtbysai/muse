# Cecil Sharp: Study Notes

**Site:** Cecil James Sharp, b. Tuesday 22 November 1859, Camberwell (Denmark Hill), London, England; d. Monday 23 June 1924, Hampstead, London, England, age 64. ## 1. Camberwell, Uppingham, Cambridge: the collector's unpromising start

Cecil James Sharp was born on Tuesday 22 November 1859 at Camberwell, in south London -- Denmark Hill, in the same parish -- the eldest son of James Sharp, a slate merchant with interests in archaeology, architecture, old furniture and music, and Jane Bloyd, also a music lover. The date is the feast of St Cecilia, patron saint of music, and several sources note he was named after her. His younger sister Evelyn Sharp (1869-1955) became a prominent suffragette, editor of *Votes for Women*, imprisoned in Holloway -- a fact that matters later, because Sharp did not support the suffragettes, and the disagreement ran through his family and through his professional life at once. He started school in Brighton, went to Uppingham School in Rutland at ten, where music-making and theatricals became a major interest, and then to University College School in London. He was admitted to Clare College, Cambridge, on 7 June 1879, and read mathematics -- music was the greater interest, but the degree was the respectable one -- rowed in the Clare boat, and took his B.A. in 1883. One mirror source says 1882; Venn and the ODNB-derived account agree on 1883, and 1883 is used here. At Cambridge he heard William Morris lecture, and the lectures stuck: he later described himself as a "conservative socialist", opposed to capitalism and the Industrial Revolution, believing in rural over urban life, collectivism over private enterprise. He became a vegetarian -- when, exactly, is split: Wikipedia dates it to after his marriage in 1893, for health reasons; the ODNB-derived text puts it at Cambridge, under Morris's influence. The 1893 dating is the more specific claim and is used here, with the split carried.

**Takeaway:** nothing in the first twenty-four years predicts the collector -- a slate merchant's son, a mathematics degree, Morris lectures in the background, and music as the hobby that would not stay a hobby. [C34797]

## 2. Adelaide, 1882-1892: the lost decade that taught him the trade

On his father's suggestion Sharp sailed for Australia to seek his fortune and arrived in Adelaide in November 1882. The first jobs were unglamorous -- washing hansom-cabs, then a clerkship at the Commercial Bank of South Australia in early 1883, then a legal firm, where one source alone says he "practised law, becoming associate to the Chief Justice of South Australia" (Sir Samuel James Way, 1884-1889). That legal detail appears in no other source read and is carried as single-sourced. What the sources agree on is the music: he taught violin, was musical director of the Adelaide String Quartet Club in 1883-84, assistant organist to Arthur Boult at St Peter's Anglican Cathedral, conducted its choral society and the Government House choral society, and composed music for the operetta *Dimple's Lovers* (text by Guy Boothby), performed by the Adelaide Garrick Club at the Albert Hall on 9 September 1890, and a second operetta, *Sylvia* (Boothby text), at the Theatre Royal in December 1890. In 1889 he became co-director, with Immanuel Reimann, of the Adelaide College of Music, resigned after two years, and decided to return to England despite an address with more than three hundred signatures asking him to stay. He arrived home in January 1892. The Adelaide years also gave him his first collaborator: Charles Marson, a friend from those days, who would open the door to field collecting eleven years later.

**Takeaway:** ten years in Australia made him a working musician -- teacher, conductor, administrator, composer of light stage works -- and none of it was folk song. The trade he learned was how to run music as an institution, and that is the skill the revival would actually need. [C34798]

## 3. Return, marriage, Ludgrove and Hampstead: the teacher years

Back in England Sharp married Constance Dorothea Birch, also a music lover, at East Clevedon, Somerset, on 22 August 1893. They had four children: Dorothea, Charles, Joan and Susannah. In 1893 he was taken on as music teacher at Ludgrove School, the prep school for Eton, then in north London, and held the post seventeen years, to 1910. From 1896 he was also principal of the Hampstead Conservatoire of Music, a half-time post with a house, and resigned in July 1905 after a prolonged dispute about payment and his right to take extra pupils; he had to leave the principal's house. Thereafter his income came largely from lecturing and publishing on folk music, plus Ludgrove until 1910, plus teaching at several schools and at the Metropolitan College, Holloway. By 1900 he had written some forty works and few were published; the early attempts at becoming a composer "largely resulted in failure and frustration". In 1902 he compiled *A Book of British Song for Home and School* for John Murray, a mixture of patriotic "National Songs" and folk material, about forty per cent of it drawn from existing folk collections. He joined the Fabian Society in 1900 and later supported Labour. He was, in short, a jobbing music teacher with a failed composing career behind him and no fieldwork ahead of him -- until a Christmas visit to his mother-in-law changed everything.

**Takeaway:** seventeen years at Ludgrove and nine at the Hampstead Conservatoire are the base the revival stands on: Sharp knew schools, knew teachers, knew what a songbook had to do to get used -- and he had learned, by failing as a composer, that his talent was for organising other people's music, not writing his own. [C34799]

## 4. Boxing Day 1899: Headington Quarry and William Kimber

Spending Christmas 1899 with his wife's mother at Sandfield Cottage, Headington, about a mile east of Oxford, Sharp watched from the window on Boxing Day, 26 December 1899, as the Headington Quarry Morris dancers performed in the snow-covered drive: eight men in white with ribbons, bell-pads, sticks and handkerchiefs, a concertina-player of twenty-seven named William Kimber, and a Fool. The dances were "Laudnum Bunches", "Bean Setting", "Constant Billy", "Blue-eyed Stranger" and "Rigs o' Marlow". Sharp was unwell, his eyes giving pain, wearing a shade over them, and "felt that a new world of beauty had been revealed to him". He noted tunes from Kimber the next day and many more later. How many tunes that first time is genuinely disputed -- two that day and two more the next, five in all, or nine, depending on the account -- and no count is asserted here. Two qualifications matter. First, the visit was arranged by his mother-in-law, not pure chance; Percy Manning and Thomas Carter had already got the side dancing again at the Oxford Corn Exchange on 15 March 1899. Second, Sharp at first did little with the encounter: he turned to printed collections for school teaching and only slowly realised the difference between book versions and recent collectors' transcriptions. He had already joined the Folk-Song Society in 1901 (Wikipedia's date; the 6 February 1904 meeting at Lucy Broadwood's, at which Sharp and Vaughan Williams were elected members, is the better-dated event, and both may be true). Kimber, meanwhile, became Sharp's main source for Cotswold morris notation, gave demonstrations at his lectures, and remained a lifelong friend -- and, during the Neal feud a decade later, Sharp's authority witness, got to agree that "the way I have taught you is right".

**Takeaway:** the turning point of his life was a dance seen through a window while he was ill -- and then four slow years before he acted on it. The delay is the point: the encounter planted the interest, but the method, the collecting, and the institution all came later, and separately. [C34800]

## 5. August 1903: "The Seeds of Love" and John England

Visiting his friend the Rev. Charles Marson -- Christian Socialist, met in Adelaide, now vicar of Hambridge, Somerset -- Sharp heard Marson's gardener John England singing "The Seeds of Love" while mowing the vicarage lawn. Sharp took down tune and words, harmonised it at once, and that evening it was sung by Mattie Kay, also staying at Hambridge, at a choir supper, Sharp accompanying; a listener remarked it was the first time the song had been "put into evening dress". This was his first experience of folk song in the field, and it set his new career. The month is split -- August 1903 in Wikipedia and in Schofield (2004), September 1903 in the ODNB-derived account -- and August is the better-attested date, used here. Marson and Sharp then collected together for three years, by bicycle and train, around Somerset: 977 tunes or texts collected in 1903-06, 79 of them published in the first three volumes of *Folk Songs from Somerset*, Marson writing down a third of all the lyrics. The two men fell out in November 1906, for a cause no source read states, and Marson died at Hambridge in March 1914. The friendship that opened the field did not survive the work it started.

**Takeaway:** the founding moment is a gardener singing while he works, a notebook whipped out, and a song harmonised the same evening -- capture, then immediate re-performance. That same-evening rhythm, from field to piano to print, never left Sharp's practice. [C34801]

## 6. The bicycle sweep: slow travel as a listening instrument

Sharp's early Somerset collecting (1903-06) was self-funded -- collecting was his passion, not his paid work -- so he travelled the lanes by bicycle. He could not afford a car, and the Halsway Manor account stresses he would not have wanted one: in a car he might miss a stone-cutter singing by the roadside or drive past a gypsy camp unawares. He went village to village through the Quantocks and into West Somerset and Exmoor -- Nether and Over Stowey, Crowcombe, Combe Florey, Broomfield -- and not just to the village centre, to remote cottages and to people out working. The slowness was functional: the pace of travel set the pace of discovery. Later, in Appalachia, the same logic held on foot -- Karpeles and Cowell's 1950 retracing notes that with Sharp they "travelled predominantly on foot in all manner of weather", whereas the 1950 trip went by car. Between 1904 and 1914 he collected more than 1,600 songs in rural Somerset and over 700 from elsewhere in England, in fifteen other counties, from some 350 Somerset singers alone.

**Takeaway:** the bicycle is not transport; it is the sampling rate. A collector who moves at walking pace and stops at anything singing will find a different repertoire from one who drives to appointments -- and Sharp's archive is, measurably, the repertoire of the lanes. [C34802]

## 7. Oldest first: the age filter

On arriving in a new place, Sharp's first aim was to find the old people. Experience had taught him that asking for singers in general produced people who liked to sing and chose popular tunes; the old songs handed down through families came out only by meeting the oldest people and steering conversation toward singing -- and even then only after patience, listening first to what they thought he wanted to hear. He stated the rule bluntly in *Some Conclusions* (1907:150): "it is, as a rule, only waste of time to call upon singers under the age of sixty. Their songs are nearly all modern." His informants bear this out: the most prolific Somerset singers, sisters Louisa Hooper and Lucy White of Langport, were of the older generation; William Pratley, his Ascot-under-Wychwood morris source, was seventy-two when Sharp collected three dances from him on a single visit in September 1911. The rule is also the core of the Harker critique (see section 31): the archive is the memory of the oldest, not the repertoire of the village as a whole, and Sharp chose it that way, on purpose, and said so in print.

**Takeaway:** a deliberate sampling bias, stated as method -- age equals faintness and slowness, and faintness is what the collector wants. Any piece built on this mechanism must show the filter passing over the loud young marks to settle on the faint old ones. [C34803]

## 8. Notation by ear, on the spot, with a division of labour

Sharp notated songs mostly by ear, in the singer's presence, writing tune and words down as they were sung. The labour was split: Sharp made the musical notations himself; the words were taken down by a collaborator -- Charles Marson in Somerset, Maud Karpeles in the Appalachians. His classical training was the tool: he "was able to write down tunes swiftly, hearing all the note durations and intervals accurately". He was meticulous about metadata -- singers' names, locations, dates, professions -- "enabling subsequent biographical research". The first capture is the model case: in Marson's garden at Hambridge, hearing the gardener John England singing "The Seeds of Love" while mowing the lawn, Sharp "whipped out his notebook and took down the tune; and then persuaded John to give him the words". That same evening the song was sung at a choir supper, Sharp accompanying -- capture, then immediate re-performance. He also photographed many singers: more than three hundred portraits survive online through the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library, the earliest and most famous being John England in the vicarage garden, captioned in the archive as "Singers no. 1" -- the first song Sharp collected.

**Takeaway:** a two-channel capture -- one hand for the tune, one hand for the words -- with a tag attached to every item at the point of capture, not afterwards. The archive visibly grows as a labelled collection, not a pile, and that is why it is still usable a century later. [C34804]

## 9. The return visit: collecting as a relationship, not a raid

Sharp was "great at winning singers' confidence": they opened up to him, and he was then able to return and collect still more songs. The work was spread over years -- he "returned repeatedly to Somerset before heading further afield" (1904-14: 1,600-plus songs in Somerset, 700-plus elsewhere in England) -- and the same pattern held in Appalachia across three seasons (1916-18), and was later repeated by Karpeles herself, who returned in 1950 and 1955 to revisit the same singers and places. The archive is therefore layered: the first visit yields the public repertoire, later visits the private one. He paid singers and gave gifts and outings; Louisa Hooper wrote after his death of his generosity. The relationships were sustained and warm and reciprocal, not extractive one-offs -- and that is exactly what the method required, because the old songs only came out after the singer had decided the collector could be trusted with them.

**Takeaway:** the collection deepens by return, not by widening the net. The same doors open twice, and the second time different songs come out of the same houses -- first-pass songs pale, second-pass songs saturated. [C34805]

## 10. The phonograph tried, then refused

Sharp experimented with the phonograph -- the new recording technology -- and rejected it, on two stated grounds: lack of portability, and its potential to intimidate singers. Gammon puts it precisely: "Sharp tried the phonograph but evidently felt more comfortable with his more impressionistic aural method. He thought that the phonograph made singers self-conscious and sometimes nervous, and that therefore the recording might give a false impression of the usual performance." The irony, noted by the 1909 *Folk-lore* reviewer, is that Sharp himself *recommended* the phonograph in *Some Conclusions* (p. 72) for studying variations and avoiding erroneous notation -- "It is to be sincerely hoped that he may make use of the instrument" -- and did not. His transcriptions, which included melodic variations, were "generally accurate, although some nuances were missed". The contrast is sharp: Percy Grainger was recording on wax cylinder from 1907, and Vaughan Williams and Butterworth also recorded. Sharp's refusal was a choice, not ignorance -- and its consequence is permanent: no Sharp field recordings survive, only notebooks. There is nothing to listen to, which is itself worth stating.

**Takeaway:** a deliberate trade -- fidelity of the machine refused in favour of comfort of the singer. The archive is impressionistic by design: what a trained ear heard and could write down in the moment, with variations noted and nuance lost. [C34806]

## 11. Continuity, variation, selection: the Darwinian engine

The theoretical core of *Some Conclusions* (1907). Sharp argued folk song evolves through oral transmission by three principles -- continuity, variation and selection -- in an explicitly Darwinian model. His own words: "Without the first, *Continuity*, no evolution can take place... The second principle, *Variation*, creates the material which renders development possible... The function of the third principle, *Selection*, is to ensure that variation shall, in certain cases, result in organic growth and development. Of itself, variation merely provides the building material, the bricks and mortar. The moulding of that material, the business of construction, the determination of the form that the building shall take, these are the work of *Selection*." The corollary: a song only becomes a folk song after being submitted to this process, and only where there is no authoritative original to check against -- printed scores and recordings *hamper* variation. The International Folk Music Council adopted the three principles in its 1954 definition of folk music. Sharp's related claim: because folk songs result from "ages of unconscious selection and moulding by English communities", they embody "the musical taste, even the musical soul, of the English nation". He called the theories "advanced... cautiously and tentatively" in his own introduction -- a caveat his later critics mostly ignored and his later defenders mostly cite.

**Takeaway:** every song in the collection is a survivor of a three-stage filter; variation is the raw material, selection the sculptor, continuity the chain that lets the process run long enough to matter. It is the most directly buildable mechanism in this study: a contour passed down a chain, copied with one small change each time, kept only when the group hums it back. [C34807]

## 12. The modal system: naming the scales the singers actually used

Sharp devoted two chapters of *Some Conclusions* to the scales -- Chapter 5, "The Modes", and Chapter 6, "English Folk-Scales" -- plus a "Scales and Modes" section in the introduction to the 1917 Appalachian volume. His system: the four scales commonly found are Ionian (major), Mixolydian, Dorian and Aeolian (natural minor), each occurring in full heptatonic form and in gapped hexatonic and pentatonic forms. In the Appalachian book he labelled them in his own scheme (Ionian as "Mode 1a+b Heptatonic", and so on). Two honest difficulties he recorded: peasants "are often apt to sing neutral thirds which are neither major nor minor", and "there is often difficulty in deciding whether a song belongs to the mixolydian or ionian (major) mode". He also protested "against the indiscriminate setting of folk-tunes to accompaniments in our major or minor scales" and insisted "the accompaniment should be in the same mode as the original melody" -- the modal-harmonisation rule (see section 16). His Appalachian argument went further: he treated the prevalence of the pentatonic (gapped) scale as evidence that the songs were Anglo-Saxon, English in origin -- a claim later scholarship treats as overreach, since pentatonic scales are widespread across many musical cultures and prove nothing about national origin.

**Takeaway:** a classification grid laid over a living repertoire, with a known zone of ambiguity where the system admits it cannot decide -- the neutral third, flickering between two positions, is the honest blind spot, and a piece should show it rather than resolve it. [C34808]

## 13. The Morris notation: a code for feet

Sharp, with Herbert MacIlwaine, invented a written notation for Morris dancing in *The Morris Book* (1907-13, five parts) that reduced a whole dancing body to a compact code. The mechanics, from part 1: steps are classified into two kinds -- the ordinary step (raised foot thrust forward so the heel is about a foot-length ahead of the supporting toe) and the "High" step (toe raised as high as the supporting knee), used chiefly in Capers; the jump "never varies"; every movement begins, unless otherwise stated, by stepping out with the right foot; and "there is always sturdiness in the Morris step; to Morris-men the languorous and the lackadaisical are for ever unknown." Footwork is then encoded as beat-grids: in 4-time, patterns 4/1 (R L R L), 4/2 (R R L L), 4/3 (R L R R L R L L), 4/4 (R R R R L L L L); in 6-time, 6/1, and so on -- each pattern named by its time-signature and index. The books add diagrams of positions and evolutions (Down-and-Back, Up-and-Back, the Chain, Cross-over, Back-to-Back, Go-and-come, the Ring), hand movements, stick-tapping diagrams, and hand-clapping notation. The country-dance books use a parallel diagrammatic code: a plan of the room at the head of each dance (left margin = top of the room, the "Presence"), O = man, Q = woman, with a fixed vocabulary -- pass by the right or left, move up or down, fall back, cast off, cast up or down, clockwise or counter-clockwise, half-turn or whole-turn. The Ascot case shows the code's power and its thinness: Sharp collected three dances from one man, William Pratley, seventy-two, on one occasion (15 September 1911), "in sufficient detail, taken together, to give us a very good idea of 'Ascot' as his source would have danced it" -- the entire modern Ascot tradition rests on that one notation session. The rules are Sharp and MacIlwaine's rules for the notation, not ethnographic facts about how Headington men actually danced: the notation standardises as much as it records.

**Takeaway:** a dance that existed only in bodies is fixed as symbols a stranger can reconstruct -- but only because the notation assumes a shared, unwritten knowledge of sturdiness, vigour and style that the symbols do not carry. The dance is the notation being performed. [C34809]

## 14. The demonstration team: the body as the textbook

Sharp taught by showing, not just by writing. After the Esperance girls began demonstrating Morris and folk dance around Britain (from 1905), Sharp built his own teaching apparatus: a School of Morris Dancing at the South West Polytechnic, Chelsea, founded in September 1909 under Principal Dorette Wilkie, where Maud and Helen Karpeles attended weekly classes; the Karpeles sisters then formed their own club, gave a performance for five hundred friends in 1910 (Sharp accompanying on piano and lecturing on the dances' history, meaning and benefits), and were invited onto Sharp's teaching staff. Sharp's Demonstration Team -- in which George Butterworth danced professionally for a time -- performed the dances as the living reference for the notation. The lecture-demonstration was a fixed format: Sharp at the piano, talking, while trained dancers showed the steps. The team was also the pipeline for teachers: the Karpeles sisters' original motive was to teach the Canning Town children. Knowledge transmitted body-to-body: a trained set dances, an audience watches, the watchers become the next teachers. The book is the backup; the team is the primary text.

**Takeaway:** the mechanism is a wave of imitation moving outward from the centre -- the demonstrators never change, the learners do, and each cycle the copy converges on the original. A drag that disturbs one learner's copy, which stumbles and then re-joins the pattern, is earned here, because the system is about learning by watching. [C34810]

## 15. The graded series and the certificate: difficulty as a ladder, competence as a stamp

Sharp organised the repertoire into graded difficulty and then examined people in it. *The English Country Dance, Graded Series* arranged dances in increasing difficulty -- the second volume notes that *The Country Dance Book* parts 1-6 "contains the notation of 177 dances". From 1912 he offered Elementary and Advanced Folk Dance Certificates of proficiency: candidates had to dance in *any position* in any of a listed set of dances, exhibiting "practical and theoretical" knowledge, with the Advanced certificate demanding more; exams were held in the three weeks of the Summer School at Stratford and thereafter by application. Holding a certificate was explicitly *not* a test of "teaching capacity" -- a separate list of "approved" teachers was kept at the English Folk Dance Society office. The certificates ran until the Second World War, when Douglas Kennedy abolished them as inhibiting participation. The ladder was also the control mechanism: it let Sharp's society define what counted as correct folk dance and who was qualified to teach it.

**Takeaway:** a staircase of dances, each rung a figure of increasing complexity, the candidate rotated to a random position before each attempt, passing a rung lighting it, failing leaving it dim -- and the stamp at the top is a seal that appears once, briefly, while the ledger gains one more name. The mechanism is the ladder, not the prize. [C34811]

## 16. Harmonise in the mode: the editorial rule for making songs singable

Sharp published his collections not as bare transcriptions but as songs for use -- with piano accompaniments, in songbooks for schools and choirs. His stated rule, recorded in the 1909 *Folk-lore* review: the accompaniment must be in the *same mode* as the melody, and he protested against "the indiscriminate setting of folk-tunes to accompaniments in our major or minor scales". The same-evening test case: "The Seeds of Love", taken down from John England in the garden, was harmonised by Sharp immediately, and sung that evening at a choir supper with Sharp accompanying. This was the commercial and pedagogical engine of the whole enterprise: the harmonised songbook -- *Folk Songs from Somerset*, five volumes 1904-09; *Folk-Songs for Schools*, sets I-X, 1908-36 -- is what put the songs into schools and choirs. The rule is also the controversy: harmonisation is an act of composition laid over the transcription, and the line between "setting" and "improving" is where Harker's critique bites (see section 31). Sharp's published songbooks for schools cleaned texts for their audience -- that is not seriously disputed -- but whether he bowdlerised his *field* transcriptions is contested, and the honest version is: the field notebooks are the record; the published songbooks are an edited, harmonised, school-suitable version of it, and the two should not be confused.

**Takeaway:** every published song is a duet between the singer's tune and Sharp's harmony -- the tune in its mode, the piano following it in the same mode, never forcing it into major or minor. Show one version without the harmony (the field recording) and then with it (the published songbook): the second is warmer and fuller, but the tune is unchanged. [C34812]

## 17. The school strategy: put the songs where the children are

Sharp's revival was an education policy, not just a publishing programme. He was a music teacher before he was a collector, and he believed folk songs in schools would preserve the tradition and build a national culture. The sequence: in 1905 the Board of Education's *Suggestions for the Consideration of Teachers* proposed folk songs for elementary schools but with few authentic orally-transmitted songs on its list; Sharp opposed this and developed his own method for adopting folk songs for education. In 1906 the Board officially sanctioned the teaching of folk songs in schools. Sharp then produced the instruments: *Folk-Songs for Schools* (sets I-X, 1908-36), *Folk-Singing in Schools* (1912, published by the English Folk Dance Society), and the teacher-training apparatus (Chelsea school 1909, Summer Schools, certificates 1912, approved-teachers list). The *British Journal of Music Education* verdict: he "introduced the music of an oral tradition into schools. Breaking the domination of the literate tradition was no mean achievement" -- though the movement "developed into a narrow orthodoxy". The fight cost him allies: Broadwood and Kidson sided with the Board's list, and the Society's committee voted to approve it -- a rift. He argued folk song expressed Englishness, should be taught to inculcate national identity, and that its melodies should ground a new English art music against German hegemony -- a belief shared by Vaughan Williams and Holst.

**Takeaway:** the archive leaves the notebook and enters the classroom -- the same tune, reprinted in a school songbook, sung by children who never met the singer, each generation of the copy a little plainer than the last. The mechanism is distribution, not performance. [C34813]

## 18. The Appalachian reversal: when the young know the old songs

In England, Sharp's method depended on the old: singers under sixty were "waste of time". In Appalachia (three collecting seasons, 1916-18, with Maud Karpeles, funded largely by Helen Storrow, introduced by Olive Dame Campbell), the rule collapsed. Karpeles (1967): "There was not as in England the tiresome business of having first to listen to popular music-hall or drawing room songs of fifty years ago before extracting the genuine traditional music, because the people knew little else but folk music; and every one was acquainted with the songs although, as in any other community, not everyone was a singer... There was no such thing as a concert, or even a community singing, but song was just part of [life]." A four-year-old performed a perfect rendition of an English ballad when his neighbour could not remember the words. The logistics were the same -- travelling on foot, visiting families, Karpeles taking words while Sharp took tunes -- but the demography was inverted: the tradition was living, universal and young. The yield, by Sharp's own diary: 400 tunes in 1916, 600 in 1917, 625 in 1918 -- 1,625 tunes in all, across 46 weeks in the mountains. The contrast is also the warning: Sharp went looking for English survivals and found a culturally heterogeneous tradition he had to explain away (see section 32).

**Takeaway:** two villages side by side -- in the English village the collector's cursor passes over many young, silent forms to find one old singing form; in the Appalachian village *every* form sings, old, young, even the smallest, and the collector is overwhelmed. Same collector, same notebook; scarcity on one side, abundance on the other. [C34814]

## 19. The photograph as metadata: the singer's face filed with the song

Sharp made many photographic portraits of singers at their homes or workplaces -- over three hundred images are online through the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library -- "largely portraits of contributors to his music collections from North America and England". The earliest is also the most famous: John England, the Hambridge gardener, photographed in the vicarage garden, probably 1903, captioned in the archive as "Singers no. 1" -- the first song Sharp collected. The photographs were filed with the same archival instinct as the tune-books: a face, a name, a place, a date. But -- and this is the mechanism's second half -- Sharp *omitted* the photographs and the anecdotal information about his informants from *English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians*: the analysis argues his desire to differentiate himself from the antiquarians and present his ideas as "scientific" was the source of that omission. The portrait exists in the archive and is suppressed in the publication.

**Takeaway:** two archives for the same singer -- the full record (face, name, place, song) kept in the library, and the published record (tune and words only) from which the person has been removed. Hold both together for a while, then let the portrait-frame fade out, leaving only the tune-line: the song continues, but its singer is gone from the frame. The portrait-frame should be an abstract placeholder, a still silhouette in a frame, never a likeness of a real singer. [C34815]

## 20. The society as machine: founding, splitting, standardising

Sharp built institutions the way he built collections -- by founding them, and by splitting from them when they would not follow his standard. The sequence: he joined the Folk-Song Society in 1901 (founded 1898 by others). In November 1907 a Goupil Gallery conference aimed to establish a society for folk music in dance and song; a provisional committee formed the Association for the Revival and Practice of Folk-Music in early 1908 -- and Sharp's involvement ended there: "In 1907 Mr Cecil Sharp and I disagreed over the constitution of a committee, and from that date have worked on entirely separate lines" (Mary Neal). The split was substantive: Neal's Esperance approach treated song and dance as amusement and happiness for working girls, taught by direct transmission from traditional dancers like William Kimber; Sharp insisted on correct traditional practice and artistic standards, taught by expert instruction. Sharp then founded his own vehicle: on 6 December 1911, at St Andrew's Hall, Newman Street, he moved the resolution establishing the English Folk Dance Society, "having its headquarters in London, with the object of preserving and promoting the practice of English folk-dances in their traditional forms" -- carried unanimously, seconded by Lady Gomme, with Sharp declaring the movement "primarily an artistic movement". Founding members included Vaughan Williams, Perceval Lucas, George Butterworth and the Karpeles sisters. The Society then became the standard-setting machine: graded books, certificates, approved-teachers list, summer schools. It merged with the Folk-Song Society in 1932 to form the English Folk Dance and Song Society -- eight years after Sharp's death; he did not found the EFDSS, and played no part in the merger.

**Takeaway:** one movement, two philosophies, one winner -- the institution that standardised the tradition outlasted the club that spread it for joy, and the history was, for decades, written by the winner's supporters. Show the process, not a side: the precise set grows, the joyful set shrinks to a corner and then to a single pair, still dancing, still smiling, but no longer the story. [C34816]

## 21. The running set, renamed: Anglicisation as a mechanism

In Appalachia Sharp encountered a living American dance he called the "running set" -- first seen at Pine Mountain Settlement School, Kentucky, on 31 August 1917, and at two other Kentucky locations. Rather than treating it as an American form with its own mixed origins, he theorised it as an older form of English country dance -- an English survival -- "without considering other possible origins or cultural influences". The verdict in the literature: "the Anglicization of an American dance by Sharp and his American followers, and their joint creation of a romanticized, politicized, and inaccurate, persistent legacy." His Anglocentric orientation ran through the whole Appalachian project: he was in search of, and claimed to have found, "an Appalachia that was in, but not of, the American South". The running-set misreading persists in the repertoire a century later.

**Takeaway:** a dance-form with a mixed, unclassifiable character -- several colours, several rhythms at once -- enters the frame; a labelling form approaches, reads it, and attaches a single clean label, "English", in one colour; from that moment the dance is performed in that one colour only, and its other colours drain out of the frame, one by one. The mechanism is visible as a *loss of colour by naming*. [C34817]

## 22. Folk Songs from Somerset: the first field books

*Folk Songs from Somerset*, five series, 1904-09, is where the collecting became print. Series 1-3 (1904-06) were made with Charles Marson, who edited the words while Sharp did the tunes and piano accompaniments; series 4-5 (1908-09) are Sharp alone, the fourth series' twenty-five songs drawn from nineteen singers with an average age just under seventy -- Sharp already doubting in the preface (December 1907) that a fifth volume would fill, then publishing one anyway, largely carols. Series 1 opens with "The Seeds of Love" from John England. In all, 977 tunes or texts were collected in 1903-06 and 79 published in the first three volumes; 48.5 per cent of the songs came from women singers (474 songs). The most prolific were the sisters Louisa Hooper and Lucy White of Langport. The books made Sharp's name and sparked a press campaign for folk song. Two things to get right: the Somerset books are field collections, but they are edited, harmonised, school-suitable field collections -- the tune as Sharp heard it, the words as Marson edited them, the piano as Sharp wrote it the same evening. And the count of what he collected in Somerset is not one number: "more than 1,600 songs in rural Somerset" is the Wikipedia figure for 1904-14, while the 977 figure covers 1903-06 only. The scopes differ; quote the scope with the number.

**Takeaway:** the first field books are the template for everything after -- a singer, a notebook, a harmonisation the same evening, a printed volume within months. The speed from field to print is the system. [C34818]

## 23. English Folk Song: Some Conclusions (1907): the manifesto

*English Folk Song: Some Conclusions* (Simpkin, Novello, Barnicott and Pearce, Taunton, 1907, xvi + 143 pages), dedicated to the Rev. Sabine Baring-Gould, is Sharp's theory in full -- and he called the theories "advanced... cautiously and tentatively" in his own introduction. The argument: folk song is communal, continuous creation; words and melody are inseparable; the modes (Dorian, Mixolydian, Aeolian) are evidence of antiquity; songs evolve by continuity, variation and selection, "as the pebble on the sea shore is rounded and polished by the action of the waves"; songs have no individual composer. The final chapter urges folk song as the staple of state-school music. The book was self-published because he could not find a publisher. It dominated for about fifty years -- the 2nd edition (1936) and 4th edition (1965) were both prepared by Maud Karpeles -- and was then attacked on selectivity, nationalism and bowdlerisation. Two predictions in it were wrong in ways worth recording: that "in less than a decade, English folk singing will be extinct" (1907:130) -- it was not -- and that singers under sixty were "waste of time" (1907:150), a rule Appalachia would break within nine years. The urgency worked, though: the extinction prediction motivated the scale of the collecting.

**Takeaway:** the manifesto is a theory written by a man in a hurry, by his own admission tentative, and its wrong predictions are as instructive as its right ones -- urgency as a collecting method, not a prophecy. [C34819]

## 24. The Morris Book and the Moorish retraction

*The Morris Book*, parts 1-5, 1907-13, with Herbert C. MacIlwaine (parts 1-3), Sharp alone (part 4), and George Butterworth (part 5, 1913; some sources give 1914), is the dance notation and history. Part 1: "A History of Morris Dancing, with a Description of Eleven Dances as Performed by the Morris-Men of England". The division of labour: Sharp notated tunes, MacIlwaine notated dances. The companion *Morris Dance Tunes*, sets 1-10 (1907-13), carries the piano arrangements: sets 1-6 with MacIlwaine, sets 7-8 Sharp alone, sets 9-10 with Butterworth -- though Wikipedia's Butterworth article lists "Books 8 and 9 (1914)", and the two numberings are off by one; neither is asserted here without the footnote. The book contains the clearest documented self-correction in Sharp's oeuvre. The 1st edition of part 1 (1907) claimed Morris was "in all reasonable probability Moorish in origin"; the 2nd edition (1912), "revised and entirely rewritten", retracts it -- the claim "will not bear examination". The 1st edition's notation was partly from the Esperance Club girls; the 2nd edition re-notated directly from William Kimber and other traditional dancers -- an admission the first notation was second-hand. A book that corrects itself in public, five years on, is rarer than a book that is right first time, and more useful to study.

**Takeaway:** the retraction is the mechanism -- a published claim, a better source, a public withdrawal in the next edition. A piece built on it should show a label being written, then visibly crossed out and rewritten, the old wording still legible underneath. [C34820]

## 25. The Country Dance Book: Playford reconstructions

*The Country Dance Book*, parts 1-6, 1909-22 (Novello): parts 1-2 Sharp alone, parts 3-4 with George Butterworth (1912, 1916), parts 5-6 with Maud Karpeles (1918, 1922). 177 dances in all, per the *Graded Series* preface. Part 1 (eighteen traditional dances collected in Warwickshire, Derbyshire, Devon, Somerset and Surrey) is the only part based on living village dance; parts 2-6 are reconstructions from John Playford's *English Dancing Master* (1650-1728), Sharp arguing dance "degenerated very rapidly" after about 1670. The companion *Country Dance Tunes*, sets I-XI (1909-22), carries the tunes. The book made English country dance teachable -- and it is the source of the lasting criticism that Sharp's Playford reconstructions are Sharp's dances, not Playford's: the manuals define almost none of the figures, so the figures are Sharp's invention laid over Playford's tunes. The *Graded Series* (volumes I-V, from about 1919, volume V edited by Karpeles) then froze the repertoire into grades. The companion fact: *The Sword Dances of Northern England*, three parts, 1911-13, is Sharp alone -- not, as the brief had it, with Karpeles, who produced the 2nd edition in 1950-51. It covers the rapper sword dances of Northumbria and the longsword dances of North Yorkshire, including the Abbots Bromley Horn Dance, and Sharp re-checked points in person before publishing part 2 (June 1912), after fieldwork at Flamborough in December 1910 and January 1912.

**Takeaway:** the book is a reconstruction engine -- old tunes in, new figures out, the new figures then taught as if they were the old ones. The honest piece shows the seam: the tune is Playford's, the figure is Sharp's, and the seam is where the history actually lives. [C34821]

## 26. The Appalachian volumes, 1917 and 1932

*English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians* exists in two editions that tell two stories. The first edition (G. P. Putnam's Sons, New York and London, 1917) is by Olive Dame Campbell and Cecil J. Sharp, jointly -- 122 songs and ballads, 323 tunes, from Sharp's 1916 trip plus 32 songs (42 variants) from Campbell's own collection of 1907-10. Karpeles accompanied Sharp, took down the words in shorthand, and is not a 1917 co-author; she edited the enlarged second edition (Oxford University Press, 1932), 273 or 274 songs -- sources differ by one -- with 968 tunes, including 39 tunes contributed by Campbell, who by then had been demoted from joint author to contributor. That demotion is part of the story, not a footnote. The introduction, read in full, claims the mountains as a surviving English folk community. The collection's standing is high -- Bertrand Bronson called it "without question the foremost contribution to the study of British-American folk-song" -- and the criticism is now standard: Sharp framed Appalachian music as an Anglo-Saxon survival, avoided German-American communities, turned back from a Black settlement, and neglected fiddle tunes, hymns, recent compositions and African-American material, though Brian Peters (2018) showed the collection does contain American-made songs, hymns, fiddle tunes and songs Sharp himself described as of "negro" origin. Percy Grainger offered in 1920 to pay for publication of the Appalachian collection; it was not published until 1932.

**Takeaway:** two editions, two title pages, one collection -- and the difference between the title pages is the history of who got credited. A piece built on it should show the same song with two different name-tags, the second tag smaller than the first. [C34822]

## 27. The Dance (1924): the last book

*The Dance: An Historical Survey of Dancing in Europe* (Halton and Truscott Smith, 1924, 78 pages, amply illustrated, many colour plates), with Adolf Paul Oppe, art historian, was finished just before Sharp's death on 23 June 1924. It is Sharp's late turn from fieldwork to synthesis: a history of dancing in Europe, including a full translation of Thoinot Arbeau's *Orchesography* (1589) that fed into it. Gammon quotes its attack on American dances -- the turkey-trot and one-step, the Argentine tango -- displacing European folk dance, the clearest statement of Sharp's anti-cosmopolitanism. Modern scholarship notes it lacks the detailed dance descriptions that only arrived with Mabel Dolmetsch (1949). He completed it in the year of his death, and it was published after it. The arc is complete: from a man who watched a dance through a window in 1899, to a man writing the history of all European dance twenty-five years later, with the same conviction -- that the dance is the tradition, and the tradition is under threat -- carried through both.

**Takeaway:** the last book is synthesis, not collection -- the collector becomes the historian, and the argument hardens into a verdict on the present. The piece it suggests is an archive that stops gathering and starts arranging: the same material, re-shelved as history. [C34823]

## 28. Mary Neal and the Morris wars

The great rivalry of Sharp's career, and the only adjacent figure who *changed* his trajectory. Mary Neal (1860-1944), social worker and suffragette, founder of the Esperance Club for working girls (1895), met Sharp in 1905 -- the meeting arranged through Herbert MacIlwaine, the club's musical director. Sharp gave her the Headington address from his 1899 notebooks; Neal brought William Kimber to London to teach; the Esperance girls performed publicly in February and April 1906 and were teaching the dances in six counties and six London clubs before the year was out. *The Morris Book* part 1 (1907, with MacIlwaine) was dedicated to the Esperance Club. Then the partnership broke down in bitter disagreement over standards, teaching and suffrage: Sharp called Esperance dancing "a graceless, undignified, and uncouth dance quite unfitted for educational uses"; the feud ran in the press in 1909-10 (*Morning Post*, *Vanity Fair*, *Saturday Review*); Neal's American tour engagements were cancelled in December 1910-January 1911 after a friend of Sharp's, "presumably" Elizabeth Burchenal, told New York societies the English education authorities had "thrown [them] over" in favour of Sharp; Neal wrote to Clive Carey on 30 December 1911 that "Cecil Sharp has done his best to poison people's minds over here". Boyes (1993) reads the power struggle as patriarchal; Judge reads it as ideological. Both readings are live in the literature. The feud pushed Sharp to found the English Folk Dance Society (1911) as his own institution and to assert "correct traditional practice" as doctrine. Neal left the field after 1914.

**Takeaway:** the rivalry is the engine of the institution -- without Neal, there is no Esperance launch, and without the split, there is no Folk Dance Society. The two dance-sets side by side, one loose and joyful, one precise and uniform, and the precise one growing while the joyful one shrinks, is the whole story in one image. [C34824]

## 29. Maud Karpeles: amanuensis, executor, continuator

Maud Pauline Karpeles (1885-1976) is the strongest working adjacent to Sharp, and the top pick for #802. She met Sharp at the Stratford Shakespeare Festival in May 1909 with her sister Helen, and was teaching morris, sword and country dancing five hours a day on Sharp's staff. She became his amanuensis in 1913, after neuritis in his right elbow stopped him writing, and lived with the Sharp family from their move to Hampstead in 1915. She accompanied him on three of his four trips to the United States, and in the Appalachians in 1916-18 they visited 281 singers over 46 weeks and collected 1,612 tunes representing some 500 songs -- she took down the words in shorthand while Sharp noted the tunes. She worked closely with him until his death in 1924, became his legal executor, edited the two-volume *English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians* (1932) and the 4th edition of *English Folk Song: Some Conclusions* (1965), co-wrote his biography with A. H. Fox Strangways (1933, revised under her name in 1967), and edited *Cecil Sharp's Collection of English Folk Songs* (1974). She returned to Appalachia in 1950 (with Sidney Robertson Cowell) and 1955 to re-record Sharp's singers -- travelling, the 1950 account notes, by car where she and Sharp had travelled on foot. Her own line, in the last year of her life: "Anything I may have achieved is entirely due to my association with Cecil Sharp" -- and the reverse debt is equally real: without Karpeles, there is no Appalachian volume, no 1932 edition, no biography, and no archive in the state it reached us.

**Takeaway:** the collection was never a one-man achievement, and Karpeles is the proof -- the words in the notebooks are hers, the editions after his death are hers, and the return visits that checked the work are hers. Any prompt that shows Sharp alone in the field is wrong; show two hands, two notebooks, one archive. [C34825]

## 30. Vaughan Williams: the heir

Ralph Vaughan Williams (1872-1958) is the strongest adjacent figure by every measure, and the doorway in the other direction: already studied (#796), so he is a doorway here, not a new subject. The tie is dated and documented: on 6 February 1904, at Lucy Broadwood's house, Sharp, Vaughan Williams and Broadwood "discussed [the] Folk Song Society and made a scheme for reviving its dying embers"; both men were elected to the Society's committee. They collected together in the field, with Butterworth and Jekyll, in 1906-13. Vaughan Williams used Sharp's collections in his compositions and in *The English Hymnal* (1906), and Gammon calls Vaughan Williams "Sharp's Wordsworth" to Sharp's "musical Bishop Percy". He succeeded Sharp as president of the folk-dance-and-song movement -- the date of that succession is not pinned in the sources read and is not dated here -- making him the institutional heir as well as the musical one. The relationship is the proof of Sharp's programme: the collector gathers, the composer carries the material into art music, and the movement outlives them both. It is also the measure of what Sharp was not: Vaughan Williams wrote the music Sharp's tunes pointed toward, and Sharp, who had failed as a composer by 1900, knew it.

**Takeaway:** the heir completes the system -- collection without composition is an archive, composition without collection is invention, and the two men together are the revival. The piece it suggests is a tune that leaves the notebook and comes back as something larger, without losing its shape. [C34826]

## 31. The Harker argument: selection and its limits

Dave Harker, "Cecil Sharp in Somerset: Some Conclusions" (*Folk Music Journal* 2/3, 1972), argued Sharp selected a tiny, unrepresentative fraction of working-class song, edited texts, and imposed the results as a nationalist, bourgeois curriculum -- "folk song as mediated by Cecil Sharp... extracted from a tiny fraction of the rural proletariat". Harker's evidence on selection is real and must be honoured: Sharp asked for old songs by name, and singers obliged by searching memory for the oldest material and setting aside the parlour songs and music-hall numbers that made up much of their active repertoire; the archive therefore overrepresents old ballads and modal tunes and underrepresents what singers actually sang most. But the critique itself has been criticised: David Gregory's "Fakesong in an Imagined Village?" argues Harker (and Boyes) overstate the case and misread the collectors; Leslie Shepard's contemporary rebuttal (*Folk Music Journal* 2/4, 1973) defended Sharp's publication of unaltered texts, "particularly bawdy ones", and noted *Some Conclusions* was self-published because he could not find a publisher. Sharp's field notes record singers mixing Child ballads with recent composed material "without sensing any boundary", and Gammon notes he did note down music-hall and popular songs when they came up, "true to his belief in 'scientific' collecting". Prompt the controversy as a controversy, with both sides named -- never Harker's verdict as settled fact, and never a whitewash either.

**Takeaway:** the filter is the argument -- Sharp's archive is not the village's repertoire, it is the village's repertoire as Sharp asked for it, and the asking is part of the data. Show the filter, not the claim that the filter did not exist. [C34827]

## 32. What the archive is not: Appalachia, race, and the pentatonic proof

Three limits, stated plainly. First, Appalachia was not a time capsule: the popular story -- isolated mountain people preserving Elizabethan England untouched -- does not survive Sharp's own field notes, which record singers mixing Child ballads with recent composed material, nor the scholarship, which stresses the region's cultural heterogeneity. Sharp's framing ("an Appalachia that was in, but not of, the American South") was a choice, and the running-set episode (section 21) shows it in action. Second, race: Sharp's Appalachian diaries contain derogatory references to African-Americans, and he showed no interest in Black music; his collecting in Appalachia was, with two exceptions, from white singers only -- Mrs Sinda Walker at Hyden ("the first coloured person who has sung to me") and Aunt Maria Tomes, eighty-five, a formerly enslaved Black singer, at Buena Vista on 22 May 1918, who "sang very beautifully... with clear and perfect intonation". He also turned back from an African-American settlement at Sylva. The diary language is evidenced; it is not repeated here. The point, in Filene's words, is not that Sharp was strikingly or unusually racist for his day, but that there was a racial undertone beneath the earliest self-conscious efforts to define America's folk song heritage. Prompt the exclusion as a fact about the collection's limits -- the archive is, with two exceptions, a white archive -- and do not present him as uniquely racist for his time. Third, the pentatonic "proof" of Englishness is overreach: Sharp treated the prevalence of gapped (especially pentatonic) scales in Appalachian song as evidence of Anglo-Saxon origin, but pentatonic scales are widespread across many musical cultures, and their presence proves nothing about national origin. Do not prompt the pentatonic scale as a fingerprint of Englishness.

**Takeaway:** the archive's edges are where it is most honest -- what was not collected, and what was renamed on the way in, tells you what the collector was looking for, and a piece that shows only the centre is a flattery, not a study. [C34828]

## 33. Numbers: why no single total

Do not state one unqualified number for how much Sharp collected. The figures in the sources count different things: "over four thousand folk songs" (Wikipedia, both countries); "more than 1,600 songs in rural Somerset and over 700 songs from elsewhere in England" (1904-14); Appalachia: "over 1,500 tunes for over 500 songs" in 46 weeks, against Sharp's own diary total of 400 + 600 + 625 = 1,625 tunes; the 1917 Appalachian volume: 122 songs, 323 tunes; the 1932 edition: 273 or 274 songs, 968 tunes -- a selection, not the total collected; the complete manuscript collection is said to contain 4,977 tunes (single-sourced, treat as approximate). The diary figure of 1,625 Appalachian tunes is the only primary-source number. The totals differ because they count songs versus tunes versus variants, England versus Appalachia, field notebooks versus published volumes. In prompts, give the split figures with their scope -- never a single round number as if settled.

**Takeaway:** the honest number is a range with a scope attached, and the diary's 1,625 is the one figure that comes from Sharp's own hand. Everything else is a later count of a different thing. [C34829]

## 34. Aesthetic logic: the system underneath

Sharp's work, taken whole, is a single system with four moves, and the moves are visible in everything he did. First, *go slowly and listen first* -- the bicycle, the foot, the oldest people, the patience before the true songs come out. Second, *fix it in a code* -- the ear notation, the metadata, the Morris beat-grids, the modal labels, the graded series: everything he touched got a notation, because a notation is what lets a stranger reconstruct it. Third, *make it singable and teachable* -- the same-evening harmonisation, the school songbooks, the demonstration team, the certificates: the archive is not the point, the classroom is. Fourth, *defend the standard by institution* -- the society, the approved-teachers list, the feud with Neal, the retraction in the second edition: when the work was challenged, Sharp answered with a better source and a firmer institution, not with a shrug. The through-line is not nostalgia. It is a man who believed a nation's music could be recovered, written down, taught, and defended -- and who built, piece by piece, the machinery to do all four. The weakness of the system is the same as its strength: the code fixes what it records and loses what it cannot fix -- the nuance the phonograph would have kept, the singers the schoolbooks cleaned up, the colours the label drained out. A generative piece after Sharp should therefore always show both halves: the notation being written *and* what the notation leaves out.

**Takeaway:** the system is go slowly, fix it in a code, make it teachable, defend the standard -- and the honest piece shows the code and its cost in the same frame. [C34830]
