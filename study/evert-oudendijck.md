# Evert Oudendijck: Study Notes

**Site:** Evert Oudendijck (1650-1695; the birth year is not documented - see C479), Dutch Golden Age painter of Haarlem, the pupil of Adriaen van Ostade who took the master's small figures outdoors and painted the hunt and the camp instead of the cottage: stag hunts and soldiers resting outside their encampments in Italianate landscapes, with one late turn back indoors to learned men at their desks. He was registered in the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1663 as the pupil of Adriaen van Ostade, at about fifteen, under the patronymic "Evert Adriaanszen van Oudendijck", which implies a father named Adriaen; Ostade paid a fee to the guild for his pupil that year. He is listed in the same guild register as a member in 1646, which is impossible for a boy born around 1650, and is probably a 1664 misregistration (Hessel Miedema's edition of the guild archives, 1980), the same register error that catches Evert Collier and Egbert van Heemskerck the Elder; he is listed again as a painter in 1670. No baptism record, no birthplace, no burial record, no wife, no patron and no travel is recorded anywhere in the sources read; his death in Haarlem in 1695 is the received date, not a documented one, and Sotheby's says only that he was "active until after 1690". What he painted, according to Arnold Houbraken's De groote schouburgh (1718), the only early biographical source, is stag hunts and other hunting scenes in landscapes, "along with the artist 'Drossaart'", a name no source read identifies. The surviving works that can be firmly attached to his name are seven: a dated stag hunt of 1675; a signed pair of soldiers resting outside their encampment and a cavalry battle in an Italianate landscape, both signed lower right "E. oudendijck", sold at Sotheby's Amsterdam in 2006; a page holding a grey horse in a classical garden, sold at Christie's Amsterdam; riders by a house in a southern landscape, known by title only; a doctor inspecting a flask of urine, oil on panel, signed bottom right, in a Bruges dealer's stock; and a notary's office, signed and dated 1688, known only through the dealer's comparative note - and wrongly given to his son in some sources, which is impossible, since Adriaen was eleven in 1688. His second son Adriaen Oudendijck (1677-1704) was a landscape painter who, according to Houbraken, copied the cattle in his landscapes after Adriaen van de Velde and the figures after Thomas Wyck, earning the nickname "Rapianus" (thief); he worked with Dirk Maas, possibly in the same studio, from 1695 to 1700, and became a member of the Haarlem Guild in 1700. There is also a printmaker of the same name, Adriaen Oudendijck, who is a different person and must not be confused with the son. No museum holds a documented Evert Oudendijck in any source read; there is no monograph, no catalogue raisonne, no retrospective, and no words by Oudendijck himself in any source read - no letters, contracts or statements; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Evert Oudendijck was born and died in Haarlem, and almost everything else about him is a matter of one guild entry, one sentence in Houbraken, and a handful of signatures. The English Wikipedia gives his dates as 1650-1695. The Bruges dealer Jean Moust, who has handled and researched an Oudendijck panel, gives "Birthplace unknown 1648/1653 - 1695 Haarlem". No baptism record, no birthplace and no burial record is cited in any source read. The 1650 date is conventional, not documented, and the pupil registration in 1663 "at about 15" points to about 1648 rather than 1650 - a small disagreement the study does not resolve, because no source settles it.

His teacher is the one solid biographical fact. According to the RKD, he was registered in the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1663 as the pupil of Adriaen van Ostade, and Jean Moust adds the concrete detail that in 1663 van Ostade paid a fee to the Painters' Guild of Saint Luke for his pupil Evert Oudendijck. He is listed in the guild register under the patronymic "Evert Adriaanszen van Oudendijck", which tells us his father was called Adriaen - and nothing more about the father is established anywhere in the sources read.

The guild record is the famous mess, and it is the most interesting document in his life. He is listed as a member of the guild in 1646, which is impossible for a boy born around 1650. The Wikipedia article, following Hessel Miedema's edition of the guild archives (De archiefbescheiden van het St. Lukasgilde te Haarlem 1497-1798, 1980), explains that he probably became a member in 1664, "a year suspiciously lacking member registrations", like other members mistakenly registered in 1646, such as Evert Collier and Egbert van Heemskerck. He is listed two other times in the Haarlem guild: in 1663 as Ostade's pupil and as a painter in 1670. The same 1646 error recurs in the Egbert van Heemskerck article, which names Evert Oudendijck as a fellow victim of the misregistration. Sotheby's, by contrast, still repeats the uncorrected form, heading its lot "Evert Oudendijck Active in Haarlem 1646 - after 1690" and saying he "joined the Haarlem painters' Guild in 1646". That is the old error surviving in the saleroom, and Christie's does the same in its own shorthand, listing him as "Evert Oudendijck (active circa 1646)".

What he painted, according to Houbraken, is stag hunts and other hunting scenes in landscapes, "along with the artist 'Drossaart'". Who Drossaart was is not identified in any source found; the name appears only as Houbraken's passing pairing. Jean Moust broadens the description to "Painter of genre scenes and of landscapes", and the surviving works support that: besides hunts and soldiers there is at least one dated genre interior, a notary's office signed and dated 1688, and a doctor's interior. He must have remained active in Haarlem for the rest of his life, in Jean Moust's words. No travel is recorded: unlike his Haarlem contemporaries Berchem, Both and the Huchtenburg brothers, there is no Italy trip, no Paris sojourn, no court appointment in his record. His Italianate landscapes are therefore second-hand Italy, learned from the Haarlem market, not from the road. No patrons are named anywhere. No wife is named. His death in 1695 in Haarlem is given by Wikipedia and by Jean Moust, without a cited burial record.

His family continues the trade. His second son Adriaen Oudendijck (1677-1704) was a landscape painter who, according to Houbraken, copied the cattle in his landscapes after Adriaen van de Velde and the figures after Thomas Wyck, earning the nickname "Rapianus" (thief). The RKD says Adriaen was the second son of Evert and worked together with the painter Dirk Maas, possibly in the same studio, from 1695 to 1700, and became a member of the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1700. Note the date: the father dies in 1695, the son starts sharing a studio the same year. There is also a printmaker of the same name, Adriaen Oudendijck, who is a different person and must not be confused with the son.

**Takeaway:** a thirty-year Haarlem career with almost no paper trail beyond a guild fee, a signature, and a son who copied better men - and one record-keeping error, the impossible 1646, that is better documented than the painter himself.


## M1. The stag hunt as a chase system

The hunt is not a portrait of a stag; it is a moving system of four agent types - riders, hounds, stag, and landscape - locked in pursuit. Houbraken's whole definition of the painter is that he "painted stag hunts and other hunting scenes in landscapes". The dated Stag-hunt in a landscape of 1675 shows the mechanism in its classic form: the stag flees, the hounds stream after it, the riders follow, and the landscape is the track they run on. Watchable behaviour: the eye is pulled along the chase line from the fleeing animal at one end to the riders entering at the other; the picture reads left-to-right as a sequence, not as a static view. Grounding: Houbraken via Wikipedia and the dated 1675 work (Wikidata).

**Takeaway:** four agents, one chase line - the picture reads as a sequence, not a view.

## M2. The Italianate landscape as imported light

Oudendijck never went to Italy (no travel is recorded), yet his landscapes are sold as "Italianate". The mechanism is second-hand southern light: warm golden tonality, rolling hills, a house or ruin, and a pale expansive sky, assembled in Haarlem from the models of Jan Both, Nicolaes Berchem and Philips Wouwerman. The Rijksmuseum's Berchem note states the rule for the whole Haarlem school: Berchem "probably never crossed the Alps. Nevertheless, he convincingly depicted the Italian landscape and the Mediterranean light. He most likely relied on the work of Italianates such as Jan Asselijn, Pieter van Laer and Jan Both as his models." Oudendijck works the same way one generation later. Watchable behaviour: a Dutch flat landscape re-lit as if by a southern sun, with the warm distance doing the work that a real Italian view would do. Grounding: Sotheby's lot 106 description ("Italianate landscape") and the Berchem rule, read end to end in leg 1's searches.

**Takeaway:** Italy he never saw, lit by a sun he never stood under - the light is imported, the landscape is Haarlem.

## M3. The encampment at rest

Soldiers resting outside their encampment is the pause-state companion to the battle: tents, horses unsaddled or tethered, men sitting, eating, talking, doing nothing urgent. The picture's energy is low and distributed rather than focussed; nothing is about to happen, and that is the point. It is the military equivalent of Ostade's peasants resting outside the inn. Watchable behaviour: the eye wanders instead of jumping; the composition is a scatter of small rests, not a single action. Grounding: the Sotheby's pair title and the Wouwerman tradition of camp scenes ("cavalry battles, hunting scenes, travelers at rest, camp scenes, and horse fairs").

**Takeaway:** the army doing nothing - rest as a subject, not as the absence of one.

## M4. The cavalry battle as the paired opposite

The battle scene is the same cast as the encampment, switched from rest to violence: rearing horses, clashing riders, fallen men, smoke and dust. Sold as a pair with the resting soldiers, it makes the mechanism legible by contrast: identical ingredients, opposite energy state. Watchable behaviour: the eye jumps between collisions instead of wandering; the composition is a set of small impacts rather than one flow. Grounding: the Sotheby's pair.

**Takeaway:** the same soldiers, the same field, the energy switch flipped - rest and battle are one mechanism in two states.

## M5. Pendant pairing as a sales and reading device

The two Sotheby's pictures are a pair, both oil on canvas, both signed lower right "E. oudendijck". The mechanism is commercial as much as pictorial: two pictures, one calm and one violent, hung facing each other, each explaining the other. Pendant pairs were standard Haarlem workshop output. Watchable behaviour: the viewer reads the pair as a before/after or a peace/war couplet, and each picture borrows meaning from its mate. Grounding: the Sotheby's lot description.

**Takeaway:** two pictures that only make sense together - the pair is the picture.

## M6. The signature as a small, low anchor

Both Sotheby's pictures are "signed lower right: E. oudendijck", and the Jean Moust panel is "Signed bottom right 'E. oudendijck'". The signature is small, low, and in lower case, tucked into the foreground rather than displayed. It is a maker's mark for the buyer, not a boast. Watchable behaviour: the eye finds it last, after the landscape; it certifies the picture without interrupting it. Grounding: two independent sources, Sotheby's and Jean Moust, both read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the name is the last thing the eye finds - a signature that certifies without interrupting.

## M7. The white or grey horse as a light-accent - FLAGGED

This is Wouwerman's trademark, and Sotheby's explicitly places Oudendijck beside Wouwerman. In Wouwerman "the white horse typically occupies a prominent position, often in the foreground where its pale coat catches light and draws the viewer's attention", and "in cavalry battles, the white horse often marks the position of a commander or key figure". Whether Oudendijck himself used a pale horse as a compositional accent could NOT be verified from any image of his own work: no reproduction of the Sotheby's pair was seen at usable resolution in this study. FLAGGED: the mechanism is documented for the model (Wouwerman), not evidenced for Oudendijck himself. Do not present it as his without a picture. The Christie's page-holding-a-grey (W3) is the one documented pale horse in his own work, and it is known only through its title.

**Takeaway:** the pale horse is Wouwerman's accent until a picture proves it is Oudendijck's too.

## M8. The Ostade inheritance: small figures, strong light - PARTLY FLAGGED

Oudendijck was Ostade's pupil in 1663, when Ostade was at the height of his powers. Ostade's documented method, per the Dayton Art Institute's Grove entry: "the figures and the space blend in an atmospheric treatment of light", "stronger chiaroscuro light contrasts", and "the element of spatial recession through the diagonal placing of his rafters"; his peasants are small, and "the action is less important than the depiction of a psychological state". A pupil trained to put small figures inside a lit space would carry that habit into landscape: figures as small lit units in a big space. PARTLY FLAGGED: the Ostade side is fully documented; the transfer to Oudendijck's own pictures is a reasonable inference from the pupil relationship, not a documented fact about his brushwork.

**Takeaway:** Ostade's small lit figure, carried outdoors - the training is documented, the transfer is inferred.

## M9. The genre interior as a room full of meaningful objects

The doctor painting shows the second half of Oudendijck's practice: not landscape at all, but a learned interior in which every object is a sign. The doctor sits at a desk "covered with an expensive, magnificently painted Turkish carpet", surrounded by books and letters, an open Herbarium, a large fish (perhaps a sturgeon) hanging from the ceiling, a document "with a red seal attached to it", and a pair of reading glasses in his right hand. Jean Moust's gloss: "In 17th century Dutch painting no element ever comes accidently." The mechanism is accumulation: status and knowledge are built up object by object, and the viewer decodes the room like a text. Watchable behaviour: the eye moves from object to object, each one adding a fact about the sitter. Grounding: the Jean Moust page read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the room is a text and every object is a word - the picture is read, not just seen.

## M10. Light through glass as diagnosis

In the doctor painting, "The light coming through the windows is reflected in it" - in the urine flask the doctor holds up. The flask is both a medical instrument and a small lens: the window light passes through the liquid, and the doctor reads the liquid by the light. Uroscopy, the dealer explains, "regularly returns in 17th century representations of doctors. Painters and patients must have seen it as a positive factor giving faith in the physician's ability to diagnose and prescribe." Watchable behaviour: the brightest small point in the picture is the flask, and the doctor's gaze and the viewer's gaze meet there. Grounding: the Jean Moust page.

**Takeaway:** the brightest point in the room is a glass of urine - light as the diagnostic instrument.

## M11. Tonal recession: dark foreground, light middle, dark background - FLAGGED

This is the Haarlem tonal landscape formula documented for Ostade's Landscape with an old oak: "a dark, raised foreground, followed by a light middle ground and a dark, summarily executed background", with a palette of "golden yellow, brown and ochre". It is the standard depth machine of the Haarlem school in the 1630s-1640s. FLAGGED: documented for Ostade's landscapes, not for any specific Oudendijck picture. It is the formula he was trained inside, and the probable scaffolding of his own landscapes, but no Oudendijck picture is described in these terms in any source read.

**Takeaway:** the depth machine he was trained inside - probable scaffolding, not a documented fact.

## M12. The anecdotal detail - FLAGGED

Wouwerman's pictures are "characterized by subdued colours, a cool atmosphere and a wealth of witty, anecdotal details", and his mid-1640s pictures "often feature a diagonal slope of land, a tree which functions as a repoussoir, and figures accompanied by horses". The anecdote - a man fallen by the roadside, a dog, a gesturing companion - is what keeps a formula landscape from going dead. FLAGGED: documented for Wouwerman; Oudendijck's own anecdotal habits are not described anywhere read, though the doctor painting's fish, seal and spectacles show the same accumulation habit indoors (M9).

**Takeaway:** the anecdote is what keeps a formula landscape alive - documented for the model, not for the man.

## M13. The family workshop and the copy - PARTLY FLAGGED on the Evert half

Copying was the family business: the son Adriaen "copied the cattle in his landscapes after leading landscape artists such as the painter Adriaen van de Velde and the figures after leading portrait painters such as Thomas Wyck". Dusart, the fellow pupil, likewise "painted free variants of his teacher's paintings" and "follows the original painting down to the last detail". Oudendijck himself worked in a genre - the Wouwerman hunt and camp - that was itself a copy-economy: hundreds of followers painting the same subjects for the same buyers. The mechanism is variation inside a template: same hunt, same camp, different arrangement. Watchable behaviour: pictures that feel familiar on first sight and only separate themselves on a second look. Grounded for the son and for Dusart; for Evert himself it is the market context, not a documented act - PARTLY FLAGGED on the Evert half.

**Takeaway:** the same hunt, the same camp, a different arrangement - variation inside a template.

## M14. The hunt as social theatre, not wilderness - FLAGGED

In the Wouwerman model that Sotheby's cites, the hunt is an elegant party: "A party of two ladies and a gentleman, mounted, with five attendants on foot, have halted on the bank, into which a stag and a hind have rushed, pursued by numerous hounds" (Hofstede de Groot on Wouwerman's Stag Hunt). The quarry is less important than the company: costume, horses, hounds, and the halt - the social pause inside the chase. Oudendijck's hunts belong to this theatre, not to the solitary wilderness hunt. FLAGGED: the quoted description is of a Wouwerman, not an Oudendijck; it is included because Sotheby's explicitly frames Oudendijck's subject matter as resembling Wouwerman's, and the social-theatre reading is the best available account of what his hunts depict.

**Takeaway:** the hunt is a party that has halted - the company matters more than the quarry.

No museum holds a catalogued Evert in any source reached, and no catalogue raisonne exists. Entries W1-W7 are the documented paintings. ### Phase 1 - Apprenticeship and formation, 1663 to c. 1670

Evert is registered in the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1663 as the pupil of Adriaen van Ostade, which would make him about 15. He is listed again as Ostade's pupil under the name "Evert Adriaanszen van Oudendijck" and as a painter in 1670. The long-standing "guild member 1646" date is impossible and is now understood as a register error: he probably became a member in 1664, a year suspiciously lacking member registrations, like Evert Collier and Egbert van Heemskerck, who share the same misdated page. Ostade is the key formation fact: Ostade painted peasant and genre scenes, small in scale, warm, brown-toned, full of observed interior detail. The genre-interior strand of Evert's later work (W6, W7) descends directly from this training - the doctor at his desk is an Ostade-type subject carried into a learned, bourgeois register. No surviving work can be securely assigned to this phase; the phase is biographical, not pictorial.

### Phase 2 - Mature Italianate landscapes, c. 1670s to 1680s

The dated anchor is W5, Stag-hunt in a landscape, 1675: Evert about 25, painting the hunting landscape Houbraken names as his speciality. The undated Italianate group belongs here by subject and by the Sotheby's comparison to Wouwerman and Huchtenburg: W1 (soldiers resting), W2 (cavalry battle), W3 (page holding a grey in a classical garden), W4 (riders by a house in a southern landscape). What changes from Phase 1: the subject moves outdoors and south. The landscapes are Italianate - southern houses, classical gardens, open vistas - rather than Dutch, and the figures are mounted: riders, soldiers, pages with horses. Composition, as far as titles and the W1/W2 pairing allow: a wide landscape with small, elegant staffage, horses as the visual engine, and a paired contrast of states (rest in W1, battle in W2; stillness in W3). Handling: the works are signed "E. oudendijck" in the lower right on canvas, a consistent signature habit. Palette cannot be stated from the texts read - the only colour evidence is indirect (the Sotheby's Wouwerman comparison implies warm, sunlit, brown-green landscape tones; the NiceArtGallery marketing copy is generic and was disregarded). This is the phase the study's Italianate-landscape prompts draw on, and its thinness (one dated work) is stated honestly here.

### Phase 3 - Late genre interiors, c. 1688 to 1695

The dated anchor is W7, the notary's office, signed and dated 1688. W6, the doctor inspecting a flask of urine, belongs with it by subject and by the dealer's own comparison (W7 is "the only painting by Oudendijck very similar to ours"). What changes from Phase 2: the subject moves back indoors and the scale shrinks to small panels (W6 is 37.2 x 29.2 cm). The late pictures are object-dense interiors of learned professionals at work: a desk, a Turkish carpet, an open herbarium, books, letters, a sealed document, a hanging fish, spectacles, a urine flask. The mechanism is accumulation - the picture proves the sitter's learning and wealth by inventory, and the single optical event (light through the window caught in the flask) gives the still arrangement its life. The doctor is treated with esteem, not satire, which separates Evert from the quack-doctor tradition of Steen and Ostade even while using its props. Evert dies in Haarlem in 1695; his son Adriaen is already working, and from 1695 to 1700 shares a studio with Dirk Maas (W18). Any painting dated after 1695, or showing the son's copied cattle and figures, is not Evert's.

- W6. A Doctor Inspecting a Flask of Urine. Date: undated. Medium: oil on panel. Dimensions: 37.2 x 29.2 cm (REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED - the figure comes from a dealer page, Jean Moust, Bruges, read end to end; a dealer page is not a museum or auction page, so under the strict rule this counts as reported-but-unverified, not verified. ## Evolution

The datable skeleton is thin - three hard dates (1663, 1675, 1688) plus two probable ones (1664, 1670) - so the phases are built on those anchors and on the two subject groups (Italianate landscape vs. genre interior), not on a year-by-year sequence. Nothing here is claimed from a painting viewed first-hand: no painting image was inspected in this study beyond thumbnail alt-text, so palette and handling are described only as far as the texts support.

Apprentice genre painter under Ostade (1663) -> Italianate hunting and military landscapist in the Wouwerman line, with the single dated proof in 1675 -> late small-panel interiors of learned men (1688, undated doctor). The two subject groups do not obviously succeed one another in a clean sequence - the interiors may run parallel to the landscapes - but the dated anchors (1675 hunt, 1688 notary) show the landscape type established by the mid-1670s and the interior type current in the late 1680s. The corpus is small, undated in the main, entirely in private hands as far as the sources show, and documented mostly through two auction records and one dealer page. That scarcity is itself the finding: Evert is a minor master known by type rather than by individual masterpieces, and any study that claims a fuller chronology is inventing it.

**Takeaway:** three dates hold the whole career together - 1663, 1675, 1688 - and everything else is type, not chronology.


## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

1. BIRTH YEAR. Wikipedia: 1650. Jean Moust: 1648/1653, birthplace unknown. No baptism record is cited anywhere. The 1650 date is conventional. The pupil registration in 1663 "about 15 years old" is the only anchor, and it points to about 1648, not 1650.
2. GUILD MEMBERSHIP 1646 vs 1664. The guild register lists him as a member in 1646; Miedema (1980) argues he probably became a member in 1664, a year "suspiciously lacking member registrations", and Wikipedia follows Miedema. Sotheby's (2006) still prints 1646, and Christie's lists him as "active circa 1646". The 1664 date is probable, not proven.
3. DEATH YEAR. 1695 is given by Wikipedia and Jean Moust, without a cited burial record. Sotheby's only says "active until after 1690". 1695 should be treated as the received date, not a documented one.
4. FATHER vs SON ATTRIBUTION. A signed and dated 1688 notary's office has been attributed by some sources to the son Adriaen, who was 11 in 1688. Jean Moust calls this "absolutely impossible". Any unsigned Oudendijck landscape dated after about 1695, or in the son's manner (copied cattle after van de Velde, figures after Wyck), risks being the son's.
5. JAN VAN HUCHTENBURG'S DATES. Sotheby's gives 1646-1733; Wikipedia gives baptised 20 November 1647, buried 2 July 1733. The 1646 in Sotheby's is likely the same 1646-register confusion or a rounding; use 1647-1733.
6. "DROSSAART". Houbraken's phrase pairs Oudendijck with an artist called "Drossaart" in hunting scenes. No source found identifies this person. It may be a surname, a nickname, or a Houbraken misreading. Unresolved.
7. THE "PAGE HOLDING A GREY" TITLE. The reproduction seller's title "A Page Holding A Grey In Classical Garden, A Landscape Beyond" is garbled English and cannot be trusted as the work's real title. The work itself is verified only through the Christie's sale record (lot 48, realised NLG 10,378).
8. NO TRAVEL, NO PATRONS. The absence of any travel or patron record is a negative finding, not a proven fact: absence of evidence in a thin record. Do not state "he never left Haarlem" as certain; state that no travel is recorded.
9. OSTADE'S DEATH DATE. Sources differ by a year in passing: the Dusart material gives Ostade's death as 1685; one dealer page gives 1610-1687. 1685 is the standard date (Dayton: died Haarlem, 27 April 1685). The 1687 is an error in that dealer page.

## Aesthetic logic

Evert Oudendijck's pictures are built on a single switch: the same cast, the same field, and the energy state flipped. Everything he paints is a negotiation between rest and motion. The hunt is motion: four agent types - riders, hounds, stag, landscape - locked in pursuit along a chase line, the picture reading left to right as a sequence. The encampment is rest: the same soldiers, the same horses, the same tents, with the energy let out, the eye wandering instead of jumping. The battle is motion again, the same cast switched to collision. The pendant pair is the logic made visible: two pictures, one calm and one violent, hung facing each other, each explaining the other, so that the subject is not the soldier or the stag but the state the group is in. Where Dusart paints the household, Oudendijck paints the field; where Ostade paints the peasant at rest in the cottage, Oudendijck paints the soldier at rest outside the camp - the same small figure in a lit space, carried outdoors into a landscape he never saw, lit by a southern sun he never stood under, learned from Wouwerman's pictures the way Berchem learned his Italy from Both's. And late in life the switch flips inward: the field gives way to the desk, the chase gives way to the inventory, and the learned professional sits still while the objects do the moving - the carpet, the herbarium, the sealed document, the hanging fish, the flask held up to the window, the light passing through the glass as the one small event in a still room. Underneath it all is the copy-economy: a genre made of templates, a son who copied van de Velde's cattle and Wyck's figures openly enough to be nicknamed thief, and a guild register that misfiles the father four years before he was born - so that authorship, date and subject are all slightly uncertain, and the pictures are known by type rather than by name.

## Positions

P1. Oudendijck is a minor master whose importance is structural, not individual: he shows how the Haarlem workshop system turned a Wouwerman template into a living.
P2. His real subject is not the stag or the soldier but the energy state of a group: chase, rest, or battle, with the same cast switched between states.
P3. The 1646 guild date is a clerical error, not a fact; any source that still prints it (Sotheby's, Christie's) is repeating a mistake Miedema corrected in 1980.
P4. He is a pupil of Ostade who did not paint like Ostade: he took the master's small figures and lit space outdoors, into the hunt and the camp, while Dusart kept them indoors with the peasants.
P5. His Italianate landscapes are imagined Italy, learned from Haarlem models rather than from travel; there is no evidence he ever left Holland.
P6. The pendant pair (resting soldiers / cavalry battle) is his most legible surviving statement, and it should anchor any study of him.
P7. The doctor interior proves he was not only a landscape-and-hunt man: he could build a learned genre scene object by object, in the Leiden fine manner's orbit.
P8. The family trade ran on copying: the son copied van de Velde's cattle and Wyck's figures openly enough to be nicknamed "Rapianus", and the father's whole genre was a copy-economy.
P9. Attribution between father and son is a live trap: a signed and dated 1688 notary office has been given to the son, who was 11 that year.
P10. "Drossaart", Houbraken's paired hunting painter, is unidentified in the modern literature and should be treated as an open question, not a colleague.
P11. No patron, no price, and no contemporary praise survives for Oudendijck; his market was the anonymous Haarlem open market, not a court.
P12. The thinness of the record is itself the finding: a working Haarlem painter could spend thirty years painting hunts and camps and leave almost no paper trail beyond a guild fee, a signature, and a son who copied better men.
