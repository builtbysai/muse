# Gabriel Belgeonne: Study Notes

**Site:** Gabriel Belgeonne, born 30 May 1935 at Gerpinnes, Belgium -- Belgian master engraver (etching, woodcut, lithography), painter, tapestry-cartoon and stained-glass designer, teacher, editor and institution-builder. He entered engraving in 1964 after meeting Gustave Marchoul, who taught him the rudiments of the craft; he perfected lithography on the advice of Arthur Robbe (C4101, C4104). He taught at the Academie des Beaux-Arts de Mons from the 1960s to 1990 (director 1976-1977), headed the "Gravure et illustration du livre" workshop at La Cambre, Brussels, from 1990 (school director September 1998 to September 2000), and co-founded the Centre de la Gravure et de l'Image imprimee at La Louviere (C4108, C4102, C4101). His student at La Cambre was Fred Penelle (1994-1999), later a woodcut artist and himself a La Cambre teacher, and Penelle's partner in The Two Jimies was Bruno Hellenbosch, the subject of study #609 (C4107). No death date was found in any source read; he is treated as living as of the latest sources (a September 2022 essay discusses him as a living artist with a very recent painting, C4109).

## The life: painter who left painting, then came back through the plate

Belgeonne studied painting at the Academie des Beaux-Arts de Mons and, on graduation, abandoned painting at once -- or so Connaitre la Wallonie says (C4102). The diploma year itself is disputed: 1962 per Connaitre, 1963 per French Wikipedia, and l'Estampe has him a student at Mons in 1963 (C4102, C4103, C4105). After his studies he taught and became an advertising assistant to his uncle Zephir Busine, painter and designer (C4101, C4104). The turn came in 1964, when he met the engraver Gustave Marchoul and entered engraving; Marchoul taught him the rudiments, and he perfected lithography on Arthur Robbe's advice (C4101, C4104). His own summary, quoted in several sources: "it is by making engravings that I rediscovered the function of color, volumes and lines" (C4101, C4104). He did not return to painting until the late 1980s -- l'Estampe dates it to about 1988, driven by interest in the trace, the imprint and the loss of meaning of plastic signs (C4105) -- and when he did, the later painting was fed by his engraving research, his contacts, his taste for silence, slowness, stripping-back, and spirituality and Oriental art (C4109). The arc is a circle with a forty-year detour through the plate: painting abandoned, engraving learned, painting recovered as something engraving had taught him.

The teaching career runs in parallel and is the spine of the chain. At Mons he taught from the 1960s to 1990 -- the start date is disputed (1963 per Connaitre, 1966 per his own CV, "from 1976" as professor of engraving per French Wikipedia and l'Estampe; probably different posts, and this study does not merge them) -- and was director there in the school year 1976-1977 (C4108, C4102, C4103). At La Cambre he was responsible for the "Gravure et illustration du livre" workshop from 1990, then director of the school from September 1998 to September 2000 (C4108, C4102, C4101). The Centre's artist page misprints the workshop start as "A partir de 1999" -- almost certainly a typo for 1990, since the same page correctly gives the directorship as 1999-2000 and every other source says 1990; do not cite the Centre page for the 1990 start (C4206). He also taught as a visiting professor at Krakow (1990), Nantes (1992) and the GRAFF workshop in Montreal (1999), all single-sourced to his CV (C4108). He was elected to the Royal Academy of Belgium -- corresponding member 1989, member 1995, emeritus member 2017 -- again single-sourced to the CV (C4108).

The institution-building is the other spine, and it is what makes him the #610 pick rather than just Penelle's teacher. He was a founding member of Cap d'Encre (1965-1970, with Rene Carcan, Francis De Bolle, Marc Laffineur, Gustave Marchoul, Andre Toussaint and Jules Lismonde; the founding year is 1963 per the Carcan biography against 1964 per the Marchoul sources, unresolved); he founded Editions Tandem, the artist-book house, around 1970-1971 (three dates in the record -- Editions 1970, association 1971, "Gravures Tandem 74" 1974 -- possibly three formalisations, not collapsed here); he founded the International Biennial of Engraving of Conde-Bonsecours with Andre Lamblin in 1976, running to 1984; he was a founding member of the Musee du Petit Format (Couvin per ODRADEK, Nismes per his CV -- Nismes is a village in the commune of Couvin, which likely explains the split, though no source read says so); and he co-founded the Centre de la Gravure et de l'Image imprimee at La Louviere (C4103, C4101, C4104, C4108, C4206). No source read names the Centre's other co-founders as a list; Andre Balthazar was its first director, not a documented co-founder (C4210). The Centre now holds 139 of his works -- French Wikipedia says more than 120, the Centre's own page says 139, and this study does not pick one silently -- among its large monographic ensembles, in a collection of over 17,000 works by about 2,400 artists (C4104, C4103, C4110).

## M1 -- The empty writing: subtraction as the method

The first mechanism is the one ODRADEK names and the whole career demonstrates: engraving as an experience of subtraction, the hollow or recess letting ink find its way; signs forming an unreadable alphabet, a story composed between heavy and light, empty and full, high and low; emptiness celebrated as a founding presence, with affinities to Far Eastern philosophy and the calligrapher's meditative concentration (C4101). Gillemon's 2022 essay says the same thing from the other side: the void is "full", the volume to take into account from the start, the energy pressing on the gesture, the source of line and form; Belgeonne's inks and estampages aggregate and disaggregate signs, traces, stains and slashes; his vocabulary is deliberately volatile, "written on the wind" (C4109). The through-line leg 2 draws from the catalogue is the same sentence in the language of works: from enclosed black circles in 1966 to shells on black grounds in 1977-78 to near-empty sheets in 1989 to monumental calligraphy in 1991 to books where the print answers a poem in 2009-2012 to pale aquatints with a few signs in 2017 -- the constant is subtraction, and the white of the paper does more work every decade (C4152). The mechanism is not "less ink" as a style; it is a procedure: cut the plate, let the hollow decide what survives, and let the unprinted paper carry the meaning the ink declines to state.

**Takeaway:** remove until the paper speaks. A sign that survives subtraction is the only sign the piece needs, and the white around it is not empty -- it is the work.

## M2 -- The ammonite: a fossil spiral as a recurring sign

The second mechanism is the motif that carries the subtraction: the ammonite, a fossil chambered mollusc shell, the recurring figure stamped on his aquatints and lithographs, "mysterious, obstinate", its fossil spiral inscribed in the thickness of the paper (C4102, C4109). It arrives in the catalogue in 1977, in Tandem 77 (W05): an aquatint on a black ground, a pale vertical bar at left, a grid of four ammonite spirals at right, two pale and two dark -- the shell motif arriving and recurring for years (C4157). A year later it is reduced to its minimum in Quatre coquilles (W07): four pale shells floating on a near-square dark brown-black field, with one thin horizontal line -- few signs, much empty dark ground (C4157). Variation sur la plage (W06, 1978) puts the shell among other signs -- a square, a triangle with a shell inset, a small shell tile, a vertical bar -- the beach rendered as arranged signs, not as landscape (C4157). The ammonite does the work a motif is supposed to do in this practice: it is a spiral, so it is a shape that is also a movement inward; it is a fossil, so it is a shape that is also deep time; and it is small, so it can be repeated, gridded, reduced, and still read. Connaitre la Wallonie ties it to his early major series, built around the ammonite and exploring interiority with philosophical preoccupations (C4102).

**Takeaway:** pick one sign that is already a system. A spiral is a shape and a direction and a history at once; repeat it until the repetition, not the shell, is the subject.

## M3 -- The small print in the large dark field

The third mechanism is the format the ammonite taught him: the print area small inside a larger sheet, the image an object set in darkness. Tandem 77 is 39.5 x 32.5 cm in total with a print of only 19 x 24 cm (C4157). Quatre coquilles is 49.5 x 32.4 cm with a print of 20 x 19.7 cm (C4157). Lodz 87.1 (W10, 1986) is smaller still: a total sheet of 24.9 x 25.1 cm holding a print of 11.5 x 14.7 cm, a small sepia-ochre sheet with a dark horizontal cartouche holding shell fragments, a large soft arc at lower right, sparse marks on a pale mottled ground (C4158). La route est solitaire (W14, 1996) is 33 x 50 cm with a print of 16 x 26.5 cm: a sepia field crossed by a horizontal band, a dark cross at left, thin vertical strokes at right (C4159). The margin is not waste in this practice; it is the dark field the sign floats in, and the print is a small lit window inside it. The early 1966 sheets already do this -- Le bonheur git hors du Monde (W02) is a heavy black ring dominating a vertical sheet, dense vertical hatching above and a black mass below (C4156) -- but the 1970s and 1980s sheets make the ratio the point: the smaller the print, the more the sheet around it reads as night, and the sign reads as something found in it.

**Takeaway:** shrink the image and grow the dark around it. A small print on a large dark sheet is not a small work; it is a sign found in the dark, and the dark is part of the sign.

## M4 -- The series as grammar: the same sign dealt again

The fourth mechanism is the series, and it is the printmaker's answer to the question of what a motif is for. The Jeu series (W08) is fifteen numbered sheets plus a title sheet, all dated 1980, illustrating Andre Balthazar's "Jeu. 80" (C4154, C4155). The Pour Arthur series (W12) is twelve sheets, Pour Arthur through Pour Arthur XII, all 1989, lithogravure (C4154). The Lodz sheets come as sisters -- Lodz 87.1, 87.2 and 87.4, same year (C4154). The Corbieres series runs I-V, 1992 (C4154). The Lao Tseu sentences come numbered -- Onzieme sentence de Lao Tseu 3, 5 and 6, all 1989, same medium, same 49 x 58.5 cm, same edition of 3/20 (C4160). A series in Belgeonne's hands is not a set of variations on a theme; it is a grammar being exercised -- the same sign, the same plate logic, dealt again with one variable changed, so that the difference between sheets is the information. Pour Arthur IV (W12) is the extreme case: an almost entirely empty white sheet, ink blots and a brown wash sitting only at the edges and corners, the most extreme "empty writing" in the catalogue (C4158). Twelve sheets, and one of them is nearly blank -- the series contains its own negation, and the negation is one of the twelve.

**Takeaway:** make the series the unit, not the sheet. Twelve prints that differ by one variable teach the eye what one print cannot, and a nearly blank sheet in the middle is the series admitting what it is made of.

## M5 -- The monumental stroke: scale jumps both ways

The fifth mechanism is the scale jump, and it goes in both directions at once. In 1989-1991 the lithogravure moment produces the smallest and the largest works in the catalogue side by side. Pour Arthur IV (W12, 1989) is 32.5 x 50 cm and almost entirely empty white (C4158). Tau (W13, 1991) is a single 122.5 x 79.8 cm sheet, full-bleed, of two huge black gestural masses on cream paper, like enlarged calligraphy strokes, the lower mass reading as a tau or cross shape -- by far the largest work in the catalogue, a monumental lithogravure and the clearest statement of the calligraphic turn (C4158). A related large lithograph, "Grande litho", also 1991, belongs to the same moment (C4154). The jump is the point: the same hand, the same lithographic stone, the same black, at 32.5 cm nearly empty and at 122.5 cm a stroke the size of a person. The calligraphic turn of the late 1980s -- the gesture freeing itself, large white zones appearing, compositions shaken up (C4102) -- is visible here as a scale decision, not a style decision: the stroke got bigger because the gesture got freer, and the white got bigger because the stroke needed room to be a gesture rather than a mark. Colour stays restrained throughout the career -- black, sepia, ochre, one dark orange noted in the Bruges sheet -- and editions stay small (mostly 10-30, a few up to 80-100), so the scale jump is the one variable he lets run wild (C4161).

**Takeaway:** let one variable run wild and hold the rest still. Same black, same stone, same hand -- at 30 cm the sheet is nearly empty, at 120 cm the stroke is a person, and the difference is the gesture, not the material.

## M6 -- The book as a shared spread: the print answers the poem

The sixth mechanism is the artist book, and it is where the subtraction and the series meet a second voice. Quai Rimbaud (W16, 2009) is a book with Denise Desautels: the inspected photograph shows the open book, printed poem text facing a translucent sheet carrying a grey ink blot -- the image as a veiled presence behind the text (C4159). Une nuee d'ombres (W17, 2012) is a book with Yves Namur: a poem on the left page, a bold black ink mass with calligraphic strokes on the right -- print and poem sharing the spread as equals (C4159). Petit silence de nuit (W18, 2012) is a book with Desautels again, published by his own Editions Tandem, in linogravure -- the only linogravure in the catalogue; the inspected photograph shows only the black cloth cover and title page, not an interior print, so the linocut itself was not visually inspected, but the book form, not the single sheet, is the work (C4159). The CV lists illustrated texts by some twenty authors -- Claude Arlan, Andre Balthazar, Denise Desautels, Jean-Luc Fauconnier, Claude Haumont, Gaspard Hons, Francois Jacqmin, Philippe Jones, Emile Lempereur, Lao Tseu (Tao Te King II and XI), Malinconi, Gregory Marszalkowski, Yves Namur, Vincent Paches, Pascale Tison, Guillaume Toebosch, Michel Voiturier and Anne-Marie Wilwerth -- a sustained text-illustration strand, single-sourced to the CV (C4108). The book changes the print's job: it is no longer an object on a wall but a voice in a conversation, answering a poem across a spread, or hiding behind a translucent sheet until the reader turns the page.

**Takeaway:** put the print across from a poem and let it answer. A spread is a dialogue with a gutter in the middle, and a translucent sheet is a print that arrives late on purpose.

## M7 -- The teacher's line and the institution: the workshop as a lineage

The seventh mechanism is not a print at all; it is the line of teaching and the institutions built to hold it. Marchoul taught Belgeonne the rudiments in 1964 (C4201, C4206); Belgeonne replaced Marchoul at the Mons academy when Marchoul moved to Brussels (C4207); Belgeonne directed the La Cambre workshop in which Penelle specialised in wood engraving (C4203); Penelle later taught engraving at La Cambre himself, 2012-2019, so the line runs Marchoul to Belgeonne to Penelle to Penelle's own students (C4203). The Centre's 2025 exhibition "Celles & Ceux de Marchoul" (21 January - 18 May 2025) is explicitly about Marchoul's teaching and the artists he formed, and it presents Belgeonne among his pupils (C4207, C4208). Alongside the line, Belgeonne built the rooms the line could work in: Cap d'Encre to promote engraving, Editions Tandem to publish artist books, the Conde-Bonsecours biennial to show prints, the Musee du Petit Format to keep small prints visible, and the Centre de la Gravure to hold the whole practice -- a collection of more than 16,000 prints, an annual prize since 1989, a documentation centre (C4210). The mechanism is the workshop as a lineage and the institution as its memory: the teaching passes the craft down one pair of hands at a time, and the founding passes the room down so the next pair of hands has somewhere to work. Penelle is the only documented La Cambre pupil found in any source read this run; no other named student of Belgeonne's workshop was found, which is why the doorway runs through him (C4203).

**Takeaway:** teach one student well and build one room that outlasts you. The line is the craft; the institution is the room the line works in; both are the work, and neither is the print.

## M8 -- The late pale aquatint: the path as a few marks

The eighth mechanism is the late manner, and it is the subtraction of M1 carried to its end. Sente parcourue (W19, 2017), aquatint 16 x 26 cm: a pale grey field, a dark ink mass at centre, a brown vertical band, fine etched lines and scratches, small signs scattered at the edges -- the late manner in miniature, the path ("sente") as a few marks crossing emptiness (C4151). Contrepoint subtil (W20, 2017), aquatint, no dimensions found, is the cover image of the ODRADEK booklet "Entre signes et silences" (March-April 2018), which pairs Belgeonne with the Taiwanese artists Mei-Ling Peng and Li-Chiang Chou (C4152). The same booklet's companion late works -- "Arc blanc", drawing, 2017; "Livre de la grande paix", aquatint, 2014; "Sans eclats", aquatint, 2010 -- are all aquatints on pale grounds with a few dark signs (C4152). The early sheets were black fields with a pale void cut in them; the late sheets are pale fields with a dark mass set in them. The polarity has flipped, and the flip is the whole career: the young engraver cut light out of black, the old engraver sets dark down on light, and in both cases the paper does the work. He was 82 when he made Sente parcourue.

**Takeaway:** let the polarity flip. Cut light out of black when you are young and set dark down on light when you are old; the paper does the work either way, and the flip is the career.

## The aesthetic logic: the white does the work

Belgeonne's logic is subtraction carried across six decades, and the career reads as a single sentence with the polarity flipping halfway through. He abandons painting, learns engraving, and discovers through the plate what painting had not taught him -- the function of colour, volumes and lines, recovered by making engravings (C4101). The early work is dense black etching with enclosed circles (1965-66); the 1970s bring aquatint and the ammonite on dark grounds (1977-78); 1980-86 brings series and small formats and lightening; 1989-92 brings lithogravure and text and the scale jump both ways; 1996-2001 brings a return to small intaglio; 2009-2017 brings the artist book and the empty page (C4154, C4155, C4152). The constant is the removal: ink finds its way through the hollow, and the white of the paper does more work every decade. The evolution is not a change of subject -- the ammonite, the shell, the sign, the void are there from the start -- it is a change of ratio: how much ink, how much paper, how much dark, how much light. Colour stays restrained throughout -- black, sepia, ochre, one dark orange -- and editions stay small, so the variables that move are scale, polarity and company: alone on a black ground, then in a series of twelve, then across from a poem, then in a room he built himself. The honest summary is ODRADEK's: signs forming an unreadable alphabet, composed calligraphically between heavy and light, empty and full (C4101, C4152).
