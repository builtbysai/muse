## M1. The hypnotic ostinato

What it is: a pedal tone held under a rising figure for dozens of bars until the listener is pulled under, and in Les sirenes of 1911, "from the introduction through twenty-eight measures, a pedal tone on F-sharp combined with ascending C-sharp octaves evoke their hypnosis", in Wikipedia's words citing Rosenstiel, and the sirens' song is not a melody but a trap built from repetition, one note held, one figure climbing, nothing else changing, and the piece was a Prix de Rome practice piece, premiered at her mother's musical salon and encored, in Auguste Mangeot's report, and the watchable behaviour is a field that narrows to a single held pitch while a bright figure climbs above it, over and over, and the figures in the frame drift toward it without deciding to.

**Takeaway:** the ostinato must be the trap, and a held note that only accompanies has missed the mechanism.

## M2. The unison as love

What it is: two voices singing the same pitch at the same time as the audible sign of a bond, and in Faust et Helene of 1913, "Boulanger highlights the crossover in tenor and mezzo vocal ranges; perfect unisons between Faust and Helene symbolize the pair's eternal love during their duet. There are moments when the tenor voice sings higher than the mezzo, indicating Faust's struggle to ever part with his beloved", in Wikipedia's Faust et Helene article, and the mechanism is interval as drama, unison is union, the tenor breaking above the mezzo is the struggle, and the watchable behaviour is two lines converging to one pitch and holding it, then one line straining above the other and the unison breaking.

**Takeaway:** the unison must mean something, and two voices in unison for blend alone have not used the interval.

## M3. One theme, two days

What it is: a single short theme made into two opposite pieces, and D'un soir triste and D'un matin de printemps of 1917-18 "are based upon the same theme", in the BSO note, "an eight-measure, free-flowing melody that rustles about, first generally upwards, and then back to its starting note", in the Dayton note, and the ASO's dotted-note motive E-G-E-D-E, and in Soir the theme "trudges heavily to convey an inconsolable despair", and in Matin it returns brisk and bright, with light eighth notes in the strings and the solo flute stating the theme, and the same material in two weathers, and the watchable behaviour is one melodic shape shown twice, first dragged and dark, then light and fast, and the viewer recognising it the second time.

**Takeaway:** the theme must be recognisable in both weathers, and two pieces that only share a key have not shared a theme.

## M4. The cyclic recall

What it is: the last song of a cycle remembering the earlier songs, so the whole work closes on its own past, and in Clairieres dans le ciel of 1914-15, the final song Demain fera un an incorporates reminiscences of earlier numbers, borrowing the device from Faure's La Bonne Chanson, in the Nicholas Phan album note, and the cycle ends "with ghostly, bell-like chords and the words 'I have nothing left / nothing left to hold me up'", in the New Yorker via Phan, and the cycle is her longest extant work, about 35 to 40 minutes, thirteen songs on Francis Jammes, dedicated to Faure, and the watchable behaviour is motifs from earlier in the piece returning faintly at the end, like rooms revisited, and the final image a bell tolling over an empty frame.

**Takeaway:** the recall must be the ending, and a cycle that only repeats its opening has not remembered anything.

## M5. Text, form and tonality interlocked

What it is: the psalm's words deciding the form and the key scheme, not decorating them, and in Psaume 130, Boulanger "purposefully interrelates text, form, and tonality in order to convey structural continuity", in the Choral Journal, and Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs describes the unfolding as "organic", and Psaume 24 contrasts brass fanfares with homophonic choral passages, its "variance of sections" against the Faust et Helene style, in Wikipedia's words, and the watchable behaviour is each new block of text bringing a new key and a new texture, and the piece a building whose rooms are the verses.

**Takeaway:** the text must build the form, and a sacred setting that only illustrates its words has not interlocked them.

## M6. The non-liturgical requiem

What it is: a group of separate works that together form a Requiem she never wrote as one piece, and Pour les funerailles d'un soldat, Psaume 24, Psaume 129, Vieille priere bouddhique and Psaume 130, "written between 1912 and 1917, resemble a non-liturgical requiem, likely influenced by Faure's Requiem", in the Choral Journal, and the sketchbook clinches it, a canon sketch in measures 342 to 353 of Psalm 130 suggests the work "was originally going to be a Requiem", and sketches for a Kyrie and for "Dona eis requiem" follow, in Perkins citing Potter, and Pie Jesu, dictated at the end, is the fragment of that unwritten Requiem, and Rosenstiel and Mattis "speculate that Boulanger intended to write a complete Requiem but did not live to complete it", in Wikipedia's words, and the watchable behaviour is separate panels that share one funereal grammar, and a final small panel, Pie Jesu, that reads as the missing movement.

**Takeaway:** the requiem must be assembled, not declared, and a group of sad pieces with no shared grammar is a pile, not a requiem.

## M7. Dictation as composition

What it is: composing by speaking the music to another person's hand when the body can no longer write, and Pie Jesu of 1918 was dictated to Nadia, who kept vigil at her bedside, in the BSO note and Wikipedia, and D'un matin de printemps was "mostly completed by Lili before her death, but final dynamics and performance directions were added by Nadia", in interlude.hk, and the D'un soir triste manuscript was "almost illegible... She was so sick, and her penmanship was really weak", in JoAnn Falletta's words, and the mechanism is authorship split across two people, one mind, two hands, the second hand deciding the dynamics, and the watchable behaviour is a line appearing in a weak, shaking hand, then a second, steadier hand finishing it, and the piece visibly a collaboration with death.

**Takeaway:** the dictation must be audible in the score, and a finished piece that hides whose hand wrote it has hidden its own mechanism.

## M8. The sarrusophone and the low exotic wind

What it is: a military band instrument, the sarrusophone, placed inside sacred and orchestral music as a dark colour, and it appears in Faust et Helene of 1913, in Psaume 130, in Vieille priere bouddhique and in D'un matin de printemps, where the BSO lists "sarrusophone or contrabassoon" in the orchestra, and in Psalm 130 it is part of the low brass-and-organ gloom, and the TON note says it "was used for outdoor military band concerts at the time", and the mechanism is an outsider timbre smuggled into the temple, and the watchable behaviour is a low, reedy voice entering where a bassoon should be, and the whole texture darkening a shade.

**Takeaway:** the outsider must stay an outsider, and a strange instrument blended in until it disappears has not been smuggled anywhere.

## M9. Whole-tone and modal exoticism

What it is: nonfunctional harmony used as atmosphere rather than as argument, and Wikipedia's Music section says she "explored the Impressionists' palette of nonfunctional seventh and ninth chords, parallel chords, and modal progressions", and Vieille priere bouddhique has a "whole-tone-flavored chief motif" that "lends an exotic tinge" to a Buddhist text, in Walter Simmons's words, and the diptych is "heavily modal" with "Debussyan parallelisms", in the ASO note, and the watchable behaviour is chords sliding in parallel instead of resolving, the mode shifting under a held melody, and the scene changing colour without changing place.

**Takeaway:** the mode must change the air, and parallel chords used only as colour have not changed anything.

## M10. The song as grief-work

What it is: setting poems of despondence and melancholy so the voice carries the illness, and Wikipedia says "Within her vocal work, Boulanger often set poetry conveying senses of despondence and melancholy", and her music reflects "solitude and alienation" and "her struggles with depression and loneliness caused by her long-term illness", citing Citron, and the Clairieres finale says "I seem to feel a weeping within me, a heavy, silent sobbing, someone who is not there", in Jammes's words via Wikipedia, and Dans l'immense tristesse of 1916 is the late song of the same grief, and the watchable behaviour is a single voice over a thin accompaniment, the text doing the work, the music refusing to comfort it.

**Takeaway:** the grief must stay uncomforted, and a sad song that resolves into consolation has done the comforting instead of the work.

## M11. The three-version piece

What it is: one piece composed in three scalings at once, and D'un matin de printemps exists for violin and piano, for flute and piano, for piano trio, and for orchestra, and D'un soir triste for cello and piano, piano trio and orchestra, in the BSO and Cincinnati notes, and "The composer conceived each piece in three versions", in the Cincinnati Symphony note, and the mechanism is orchestration as translation, the same theme re-proved at chamber scale and at full-orchestra scale, and the watchable behaviour is the same melody first in a room with two players, then in a hall with eighty, and the theme surviving both.

**Takeaway:** the theme must survive the scaling, and a melody that only works at one size has not been translated.

## M12. Composing without an instrument

What it is: writing a large cantata entirely in the head, and Faust et Helene was composed in a villa over three weeks for the Prix de Rome final, during which "Boulanger had no access to a piano or any musical instrument", in Wikipedia's Faust et Helene article, and the mechanism is audiation under examination conditions, the whole score, voices and orchestra including sarrusophone, heard inwardly and written down, and the watchable behaviour is a figure writing in silence, no keyboard in the room, and a full orchestra emerging from the page.

**Takeaway:** the hearing must be inward, and a score that needed the piano to find its notes has not been composed without one.

## M13. The dead father as address

What it is: a sacred text addressed to a dead parent, and Psaume 130 is dedicated "a la memoire de mon cher Papa", and interlude.hk says "Begun in 1910 and completed in 1917... **Takeaway:** the address must be to the dead, and a psalm that only mourns in general has addressed no one.

## M14. The Gazette as network-keeping

What it is: a newsletter as a lifeline for scattered musicians, and in 1915 Lili and Nadia founded the Franco-American Committee and the Gazette des classes de composition du Conservatoire, "to keep Paris Conservatoire students and alumni serving in World War I in contact through letters and care packages", and the first circular letter drew 51 responses in October 1915, and over its life the Gazette received over 1,600 letters from 316 students and graduates in 12 issues, illustrated by Jacques Debat-Ponsan, and ended in 1918 shortly after her death, and the watchable behaviour is letters arriving from the front, read aloud, answered, and bound into issues, a school held together by post.

**Takeaway:** the letters must be the school, and a newsletter that only reports the news has kept no network.

## M15. The unfinished opera as life's work

What it is: a five-act opera that absorbs the composer's last years and is never finished, and Maeterlinck authorized the setting of La princesse Maleine in February 1916, and Lili "had almost completed the opera before her death, though only the short score of act 1, scene 2, two versions of the libretto, and a sketchbook have survived", in Potter's words via Wikipedia, and the sketchbook references Acts 4 and 5, "IVe Acte - p 30 cahier rouge" and "Ve Acte - 2e cahier rouge", so more existed and was lost or destroyed, in the Cambridge JRMA article, and the 2019 Dell'Arte "Princess Maleine" is a new work by Whitney George and Bea Goodwin from scratch, not a completion of hers, and the watchable behaviour is a vast story told from one surviving scene and a notebook, the rest of the opera a visible absence, see C4.

**Takeaway:** the absence must be visible, and an unfinished opera presented as finished has hidden the work that mattered most.

## M16. The race against the body

What it is: a catalogue shaped by a deadline the composer can feel, and her last years "were musically productive as she laboured to complete as many pieces as she could", in Wikipedia's words, and the BSO note says "The number and variety of pieces she worked on during this time would not seem to suggest that Boulanger was approaching the end of her life; the scores themselves reveal the truth, however, written with faint and unclear handwriting", and the 1917-18 burst, the diptych and Pie Jesu, against the 1911-13 burst, the Prix preparation, shows the same hand working faster as the time shortens, and the watchable behaviour is a handwriting that thins across the page while the music gets denser, and pieces finished in weeks that once took years.

**Takeaway:** the hurry must be in the handwriting, and a late burst described only as prolific has not shown the race.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Lili Boulanger's work is that a composer given twenty-four years and a body that kept failing her made a catalogue that reads as one long act of finishing, and the ostinato is the trap that holds the listener still long enough to hear it, and the unison is the bond made audible, and one theme in two weathers proves a melody can be both a funeral and a morning, and the cycle remembers its own songs because the composer knew she would not write another one, and the psalm's words build the form because the text was the only architecture she could trust, and the separate works assemble into a requiem she never wrote as one piece because the requiem was the work and the pieces were its movements, and the dictation is the authorship split across two sisters because the hand gave out before the mind did, and the sarrusophone is the outsider smuggled into the temple because she was herself an outsider, the first woman through a door that had been closed to women until 1903, and the whole-tone mist is the atmosphere of a music that refuses to argue, and the song is grief-work because the grief was daily and the song was the only place it could be carried, and the three versions prove the theme at every size because there was no time to write three different pieces, and the cantata without an instrument proves the hearing was inward because the examination allowed nothing else, and the dead father is the address because the psalm needed someone to speak to, and the Gazette is the school held together by post because the war had scattered the school, and the unfinished opera is the life's work left as one scene and a notebook because the life ran out first, and the race against the body is the whole catalogue, and the handwriting thins while the music gets denser, and the proof is that twenty-four years were enough, because every piece she finished is still being played, and the sister spent sixty-one years making sure of it.
