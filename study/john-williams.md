## 1. The boy at the piano: origin

John Towner Williams was born 8 February 1932, the son of Esther (nee Towner) Williams and Johnny Williams, a jazz drummer and percussionist who played with the Raymond Scott Quintet; he had an older sister, Joan, and two younger brothers, Jerry and Don, who later played on his film scores, and his father collaborated with Bernard Herrmann, the young John sometimes joining rehearsals. [C32883] The family background, in his own words: "My father was a Maine man -- we were very close. My mother was from Boston. My father's parents ran a department store in Bangor, Maine, and my mother's father was a cabinetmaker." [C32884] The family moved to Los Angeles in 1948; he attended North Hollywood High School, graduating in 1950, then UCLA, studying composition privately with the Italian composer Mario Castelnuovo-Tedesco, and one semester at Los Angeles City College to play in the studio jazz band. [C32885] He joined the U.S. Air Force in 1951 and served until 1955, playing piano and bass and conducting and arranging for the U.S. Air Force Band -- March 1952 assigned to the Northeast Air Command 596th Air Force Band, trained at Lackland AFB, Texas, stationed at Davis-Monthan AFB, Tucson, Arizona (taking music courses at the University of Arizona), then two years at Pepperrell AFB, St. John's, Newfoundland. [C32886] In 1952, while stationed at Pepperrell, he wrote his first film composition, for You Are Welcome, a promotional film for the Province of Newfoundland tourist office. [C32887] In 1955, after service, he moved to New York to study piano with Rosina Lhevinne at Juilliard -- but never enrolled in courses and never graduated, taking private lessons in her practice room (the article cites Tim Greiving's 2025 biography for "never obtained a college degree"); he planned to be a concert pianist but, after hearing John Browning and Van Cliburn, switched to composition: "I could write better than I could play." He worked as a pianist in the city's jazz clubs during his studies. [C32888]

**Takeaway:** The origin is a drummer's son who meant to be a pianist and found he could write better than he could play -- the whole career is a man who learned music from the inside of the orchestra pit, not from a classroom, and never finished a degree.

## 2. The studio years: pianist, orchestrator, television

Back in Los Angeles after Lhevinne, Williams worked as a film-studio orchestrator with Franz Waxman, Bernard Herrmann and Alfred Newman, alongside fellow orchestrators Conrad Salinger and Bob Franklyn, and as a studio pianist and session musician he played scores by Jerry Goldsmith, Elmer Bernstein and Henry Mancini. [C32889] One of his first jobs was an uncredited orchestral role under mentor Alfred Newman on Carousel (1956), starring his soon-to-be wife Barbara Ruick; with Mancini he recorded Peter Gunn (1959), Breakfast at Tiffany's (1961), Days of Wine and Roses (1962) and Charade (1963), playing the piano part of the guitar-piano ostinato in the Peter Gunn title theme; with Bernstein he played Sweet Smell of Success (1957) and To Kill a Mockingbird (1962); he was also pianist on The Apartment (1960), West Side Story (1961) and The Great Race (1966). [C32890] As "Johnny Williams" he released several jazz albums, including Jazz Beginnings, World on a String and The John Towner Touch, and worked as a music arranger and bandleader for popular-music albums with Ray Vasquez and Frankie Laine -- the jazz pianist is not a phase he outgrew but a voice he returns to, most openly in Catch Me If You Can (2002). [C32891] His first feature-film composition was Daddy-O (1958), followed by Because They're Young (1960); his television work ran through Bachelor Father (1957-59), Kraft Suspense Theatre (1963-65), Lost in Space (1965-68), The Time Tunnel (1966-67) and Land of the Giants (1968-70) -- the latter three produced by Irwin Allen -- plus episodes of M Squad and Checkmate and the pilot of Gilligan's Island. [C32892] The American-Japanese anti-war epic None but the Brave (1965) marked the full transition from TV to major Hollywood film composing; a Variety reviewer praised the score's "excellent background." It was also his first collaboration with Frank Sinatra, who directed and starred; he later conducted for Sinatra, including a Sloan-Kettering gala. [C32893] Called William Wyler's How to Steal a Million (1966) "the first film I ever did for a major, super-talent director." His first Academy Award nomination came for Valley of the Dolls (1967); he was nominated again for Goodbye, Mr. Chips (1969); and his first win was Best Scoring: Adaptation and Original Song Score for Fiddler on the Roof (1971). [C32894] He scored Robert Altman's Images (1972) and The Long Goodbye (1973); Pauline Kael on the latter: "Williams' music is a parody of the movies' frequent overuse of a theme, and a demonstration of how adaptable a theme can be." Altman told him to "do whatever you want. Do something you haven't done before." Williams named Images a favorite score; it used "all kinds of effects for piano, percussion, and strings" with "a debt to Varese." [C32895]

**Takeaway:** He learned the trade twice over -- once as the pianist inside other men's scores, once as the orchestrator turning their sketches into parts -- so when his own themes arrived, he already knew exactly which instruments would carry them.

## 3. The Reivers and The Cowboys: the scores that won Spielberg

Early-1970s prominence grew via Irwin Allen's disaster films -- The Poseidon Adventure (1972), The Towering Inferno and Earthquake (both 1974) -- big symphonic writing for big wreckage, the blockbuster prototype: The Towering Inferno's bustling five-minute "Main Title" over a helicopter flight over San Francisco, two love themes introduced as easy-listening source music then transformed into grand orchestral statements, and atonal passages with water chimes and four Peking gongs recalling Images (1972). [C32896] The Reivers (1969), for Mark Rydell's 1905 coming-of-age road story, is Copland-inflected Americana -- folksy, harmonica-driven character writing against orchestral warmth -- a last-minute replacement for a rejected Lalo Schifrin jazz score; it earned an Oscar nomination for Best Original Score, his second nomination that year counting Goodbye, Mr. Chips. [C32897] The Cowboys (1972), also Rydell, is full-blooded traditional western -- a rousing main theme (strings fanfare, accordion, harmonica and tambourine against full orchestra) with separate themes for Training, Paternal, Trail, Mischief and Long Hair (bass harmonica); Wil Andersen's theme opens on a perfect fifth, and the "Overture" became a concert staple. [C32898] His scores for The Reivers and The Cowboys impressed a young Steven Spielberg, preparing his feature debut The Sugarland Express (1974), who requested Williams; Williams recalled meeting "what looked to be this seventeen-year-old kid... who knew more about film music than I did -- every Max Steiner and Dimitri Tiomkin score." [C32899] The Sugarland Express itself is intimate country-blues, not maximalist orchestral -- a Toots Thielemans harmonica-led theme over small strings, guitars and percussion, veering playful, wistful and tragic as the fugitive couple's chase unfolds; Williams talked Spielberg down from a larger score, and the 45-piece Hollywood Studio Symphony plus Wrecking Crew players did not get a commercial release until 2024. [C32900] The partnership that began there is the spine of both careers: Williams has composed music for all but five of Spielberg's feature films, and the October 2025 announcement of Disclosure Day calls it his 30th collaboration with Spielberg -- implying 29 scored features through The Fabelmans (2022). Caveat: the main article does not enumerate all 29 in one list and names only two of the five exceptions (The Color Purple, scored by Quincy Jones, and Bridge of Spies, which went to Thomas Newman); do not present an enumerated 29-title list as article-verified. [C32901]

**Takeaway:** Spielberg did not hire a symphonist; he hired the man who wrote a harmonica theme for a road movie and a perfect fifth for a cattle drive -- the partnership began small, and the bigness came later, on top of the smallness.

## 4. Jaws: two notes and the invention of dread

Jaws (1975) reunited Williams with Spielberg a year after Sugarland Express; Spielberg used the Images score as temp track while editing, and Williams rejected his own temp as too intellectual: "it's a pirate movie! It's a shark and survivors!" [C32902] The shark theme is an ostinato alternating E and F -- an ascending minor second, a semitone -- in the low strings (basses, celli), trombones and tuba; Spielberg, expecting something "weird and melodic, tonal but eerie", laughed when Williams played just the two notes on piano with two fingers ("dun-dun... dun-dun..."), thinking it was a joke, and after hearing it again at different speeds agreed: "sometimes the best ideas are the most simple ones." [C32903] Williams's own account, to Jon Burlingame in 2015: "so simple, insistent and driving, that it seems unstoppable, like the attack of the shark... I just began playing around with simple motifs that could be distributed in the orchestra, and settled on what I thought was the most powerful thing, which is to say the simplest." And: the theme is "grinding away at you, just as a shark would do, instinctual, relentless, unstoppable." [C32904] The mechanism of dread is parametric, not melodic: Williams "carefully slows or accelerates" the ostinato "always in support of the film's dramatic needs" -- tempo, dynamics and register do the work the notes do not. The opening scene enters quietly, almost creepily, with chromatic piano and harp arpeggios punctuated by low celli and basses, slowly builds in tension and size, then launches a violent Stravinsky-like orchestral attack as the shark strikes, then slips away almost silently; every subsequent attack in the first half follows the same pattern -- music becomes the character, cueing worry, fear and relief. [C32905] Tempo is calibrated to a heartbeat: the cello ostinato starts at about 60 bpm -- a resting heartbeat -- then accelerates gradually to a panicked pulse as the shark closes in, and the low register is chosen for its guttural, vibrating, bodily quality, "more akin to the body's rhythm than the grace of a violin" (search-extract only; the 60 bpm figure was not verified in a full page read). [C32906] Orchestration detail: besides the low ostinato, the theme features a highly chromatic tuba solo, "quite hard to play", heard in the main title and many times through the film -- on the original recording performed by Los Angeles studio tubist Tommy Johnson -- so the shark's menace is carried by the bottom of the orchestra, not by a tune; the ostinato is said to derive from Ravel's La valse, and the score also quotes Debussy's La mer and Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring. [C32907] Contrast proves the cell's function: the human hunt is scored in the opposite register -- a jaunty hornpipe-like sea tune for the Orca sailing out (Williams's own tune, after Quint's on-camera shanty "Spanish Ladies"), with intricate contrapuntal writing, virtuoso trumpet (Malcolm McNab) and piccolo in the concert version, and Korngold-esque fanfares in the barrel chase. Primal two-note cell (shark) versus complex, contrapuntal, heroic music (men) -- the opposition is the drama. [C32908] The score won his second Oscar, his first for Best Original Score, plus a BAFTA (shared with The Towering Inferno), a Golden Globe and a Grammy; the Jaws soundtrack album was later expanded and rearranged by Williams from the film cues "to provide a more cohesive listening experience" -- "Out to Sea / The Shark Cage Fugue" as an extended concert-like version with virtuoso trumpet and piccolo parts not heard that way in the film. [C32909]

**Takeaway:** Two notes are enough when the two notes are the shark: the theme is not a melody but a pulse, and the pulse is calibrated to a heartbeat -- the simplest idea in the film is the one that never leaves the audience alone.

## 5. The leitmotif system: themes that travel

Williams revived Wagnerian leitmotivic scoring for Hollywood: main themes for the protagonist or protagonists plus individual character themes, all subject to thematic transformation over the drama. Frank Lehman catalogued some 60-70 themes across the nine Star Wars films -- "one of the largest caches of themes in the history of film music" -- with about 50 recurring themes and about 7 new themes per episode on average, each intended to be distinct, long-lined and memorable. [C32910] Good and Evil are coded thematically: Luke, Obi-Wan, Leia and Yoda are "Good"; the Empire, Darth Vader, Jabba and the Death Star are "Evil". Williams himself said the Luke theme is "larger than he is. His idealism is more the subject than the character himself." [C32911] Transformation works by re-orchestration, mode and stripping: the Main Theme in A New Hope is huge and heroic brass; in The Empire Strikes Back it "hovers in minor mode"; in Return of the Jedi it is applied to the Rebellion as a whole, tracking Luke's growth; in The Rise of Skywalker, at C-3PO's farewell, the same theme returns with no heroic apparatus at all -- a single French horn, somber and sentimental -- while Luke is absent from the film, so the theme carries his heroic history by association. [C32912] The Imperial March is transformed the same way in reverse: in Return of the Jedi, at Vader's death, it is played "very weakly, almost as if it is withering away and dying". Musicologist Irena Paulus describes this as Wagnerian practice -- altering a leitmotif harmonically, rhythmically, tonally, or by instrumentation to match character development. [C32913] Themes migrate in meaning over decades ("semantic migration"): the Rebel Fanfare began as the Blockade Runner and Rebellion fanfare in A New Hope and by The Force Awakens and Solo behaves as an all-purpose heroic motif and specifically the Millennium Falcon's theme; the Force Theme was rebranded from "Old Ben's theme"; Luke's theme was rebranded as the series Main Theme. [C32914] A specific Williams contribution: he assigns a theme to a character when the character is not on screen, so the theme anticipates, recalls, or comments on them -- not only sounding on their appearance -- and themes evolve as characters evolve, so a return is never a literal repeat (search-extract only). [C32915] Transformation is opportunistic, not prophetic: Lehman calls Williams "opportunistic and forgetful" -- themes are transformed by key change and rhythmic alteration (for example Yoda's theme in Return of the Jedi), but the new forms often never return meaningfully ("unrealized potential"); Williams himself revealed gaps in knowledge of future story development, so melodic plot-hints are essentially impossible. [C32916] The system has limits, and Williams scores one episode at a time, basing each score on new material as much as possible: the Imperial March does not appear in the original Star Wars because it was not conceived until Empire; themes are abandoned (the Droid motif after Empire; the original Imperial Motif, which the Imperial March replaced); some uses are non-narrative or mood-only -- Leia's Theme for Obi-Wan's death, Yoda's Theme in Cloud City -- and prequel scoring shifts from long-lined melodies to shorter, more rhythmic ideas. [C32917]

**Takeaway:** The leitmotif is not a label stuck on a character; it is a living thing that grows, weakens, changes key and changes meaning -- the theme remembers what the character has been through, even when the character is not in the room.

## 6. Star Wars: the main title architecture

Spielberg recommended Williams to George Lucas for Star Wars (1977); Lucas wanted "a classical score, with a big sweeping orchestra", and Fox music director Lionel Newman suggested the London Symphony Orchestra for the first score (and five subsequent Star Wars scores). [C32918] The grand symphonic score was influenced by Dvorak, Holst's The Planets and Golden Age Hollywood composers Max Steiner and Erich Wolfgang Korngold; it is often described as Wagnerian for its leitmotifs, which Williams downplays: "People say they hear Wagner in 'Star Wars,' and I can only think, It's not because I put it there... I have been in the big river swimming with all of them." [C32919] Architecture, bars 1-29, Bb major, 4/4: bars 1-3 brass fanfare (logo); bars 4-11 Main Theme A, played twice (crawl); bars 11-20 contrasting B section, gentler and more lyrical (crawl); bars 21-29 Main Theme A twice more, tutti fortissimo, doubled across three octaves with rushing upward woodwind scales and a harp glissando at bar 29. Regular four-bar phrases, AABA shape -- a self-contained, tuneful title, not a through-composed cue. [C32920] The A theme opens with a rising perfect 5th, Bb to F (tonic to dominant) -- heroic, open. Bars 2-3 repeat a leap of a minor 7th up to Bb, then fall a perfect 4th (Bb to F, the opening 5th inverted). Rhythm is triplet quavers then a tied minim across beats 2-3; no dotted rhythms. Four-bar idea, repeated (8 bars). The fanfare intro (bars 1-3) is rapid repeated Bb notes, staccato triplets built on 4ths (F-Bb-Eb), trumpets leaping a minor 7th (F to Eb), quartal harmony F-Bb-Eb-Ab, inverted tonic pedal (high Bb tremolando in violins), 4/4, fortissimo, Bb major. [C32921] The A theme's harmony is not plain I-IV-V: about half the chords in bars 4-7 are quartal (where V might be); an imperfect cadence at bar 7 is approached by parallel chords -- an Ab-major chord (not in Bb major) falling a 3rd to F major (V). The inverted tonic pedal (high Bb tremolando) runs through bars 1-7. This quartal and borrowed-chord colour is what keeps a diatonic fanfare from sounding like a school march. [C32922] B section mechanics: melody in all strings except double basses, across three octaves; glockenspiel picks out important notes; block chords in horns, piano left hand and oboe; triplets in piano right hand, harp, flute and clarinet. It begins with an anacrusis; it is more stepwise than A, but with a rising 6th (bars 12, 14), triplets (bar 13), falling 4th F-C (bar 15); the repeat ends with a descending Bb-minor scale (bars 18-19), contrary motion (bar 18), final dominant F held over two bars. Harmony: dominant pedal in bass (bars 12-15); Db major to F major at bar 15 (chords a 3rd apart, tertiary and chromatic, "rich and Romantic"). Dynamics mezzo-forte, softer than A. [C32923] After the title, the architecture breaks: bars 30-60 are through-composed, picture-led and tonally unstable (Bb major to unclear from bar 30 to C-based from bar 42 to almost atonal and bitonal at 51-60). The title's job is to establish the heroic world in 29 bars; the Rebel Blockade Runner music's job is to dissolve it into threat. The concert Main Title (as on the album) replaces bars 30-60 with a concert ending -- the film cue and the concert piece diverge at bar 30. [C32924] The score won his second Academy Award for Best Original Score (third Oscar overall); it also won the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and Grammys for Best Original Score Written for a Motion Picture or TV Special and Best Instrumental Composition ("Main Title"), was nominated for the Grammy Album of the Year, was named AFI's most memorable score of all time in 2005, and entered the Library of Congress National Recording Registry in 2004. It was recorded 5, 8-12 and 15-16 March 1977 at Anvil Studios, Denham, with the London Symphony Orchestra in 8 sessions; the album peaked at No. 2 on the Billboard 200, the Main Title at No. 10 on the Hot 100, and Meco's disco version reached No. 1. [C32925]

**Takeaway:** The title is a machine for making a galaxy feel familiar in twenty-nine bars: a fanfare, a theme stated twice, a gentler middle, the theme twice more at full force -- and then the music dissolves, because the story is about to.

## 7. The Force Theme and the Imperial March

The Force Theme functions as a separate, more lyrical strand from the Main Theme: it is the most common and flexible leitmotif in the series (played over 100 times), also standing for Obi-Wan, Fate and Destiny, or as an all-purpose leitmotif. Where the Main Theme is Bb-major, brass, fanfare intervals (5th, 7th, 4th), the Force Theme is minor-key, stepwise and striving, horn, strings and winds with high-string tremolo shimmer in gentle statements, and brass and march only when militarised. It first appears in A New Hope in "Binary Sunset" -- the film's contemplative centre -- not in the title. [C32926] The Force Theme divides into four two-bar ideas whose goal notes rise through the first three, climax, then fall -- a "struggle" contour. Idea 1 rises 1-2-3-4 then sinks back to 3 (thwarted); idea 2 rises 1-2-3, touches 5, falls to 4; idea 3 climbs 1-2-3-5 to the octave -- the climax, emphasised in "Binary Sunset" by fuller scoring and strings -- then falls back to the tonic. Rhythm is march-like (4/4, beats 1 and 3 stressed, dotted and triplet rhythms) even in gentle versions, giving Jedi and Rebellion a military undertow. [C32927] Harmony creates hope inside minor: the chord ending idea 2 is a major IV (minor in a minor key normally), and the climax sits over a major VI -- positive within a negative context. Crucially, in A New Hope the theme almost never resolves: only in the "Throne Room" march does it reach a clean final tonic; in "Binary Sunset" it trails off before closure, and its other full statement ends in dissonance. Resolution is withheld until the Death Star mission succeeds -- music mirrors narrative completion. Lehman's catalogue adds an alternative harmonisation (flat-II instead of IV) introduced in Empire and Jedi and developed thereafter (search-extract only). [C32928] The Imperial March (G minor) begins not with the tune but with an accompaniment riff that accelerates through the bar: beat 1 one note, beat 2 note plus fast triplet, beat 3 note plus triplet flowing into beat 4 starting with the triplet -- relentless forward drive. All pitched instruments (strings, horns, timpani) are at or near the bottom of their range, giving a dark colour. The first three beats give only repeated G, so no key is clear; beat 4 gives Eb-F#-Bb (heard as Eb-Gb-Bb), a minor version of a normally major chord, confirming G minor. On repetitions 3-4 Williams adds A, scraping against Bb for gritty dissonance. [C32929] The main melody: opening phrase outlines G-G-G-Eb-Bb-G (repeated tonic, then downward leap to Eb, up to Bb -- triad notes, wide leaps, commanding). A moderately long note sounds on the first seven beats in a row, giving sure-footed, unwavering confidence. Harmony for the first 20 bars is almost all minor chords; the second half uses i-#iv-#v-i in G minor -- IV and V raised by a semitone, a near-normal progression distorted by a semitone, sounding "darkly abnormal". Melodic shape is predominantly descending (five short ideas, each falling), suggesting the Empire bearing down. Trumpets and trombones sit in mid-to-low register, against the Main Title trumpets at the top of their range for heroism. It is partly modelled on Chopin's Marche Funebre dotted rhythms and Holst's The Planets (search-extract only). [C32930] The Empire Strikes Back (1980) introduced the Imperial March, Yoda's Theme and "Han Solo and the Princess"; Return of the Jedi (1983) added the "Emperor's Theme", "Parade of the Ewoks" and "Luke and Leia". Both scores earned Oscar nominations. Empire required 104 players (excluding conductor and synthesiser) because of a fourth flute, third harp, five oboes, added piccolo and eight percussionists, and its score ran about 107 minutes, recorded in 18 three-hour sessions over two weeks just after Christmas 1979 at Anvil/EMI, London, with up to 104 players, at a cost of about $250,000. [C32931] The prequels added new themes: The Phantom Menace (1999) gave "Duel of the Fates" (rapid five-note motif plus repeated nine-note theme, massive choir singing in Sanskrit) and "Anakin's Theme" (childlike strings, woodwinds and harp whose final four notes are the final four notes of the Imperial March); Williams scored all three prequels, then the sequel trilogy -- The Force Awakens (2015) earning his 50th Oscar nomination, The Last Jedi (2017) -- and in March 2018 announced he would retire from Star Wars after The Rise of Skywalker (2019): "It will round out a series of nine, that will be quite enough for me." For Solo: A Star Wars Story (2018) he contributed "The Adventures of Han" and several demos; John Powell wrote the score and adapted his music. [C32932]

**Takeaway:** The Force Theme climbs and never quite arrives, the Imperial March descends and never stops -- one theme is a struggle that withholds its ending, the other is a machine that cannot be argued with, and the saga is the space between them.

## 8. Close Encounters: five notes as language

Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) was a two-year collaboration that built a five-note motif which functions both in the score and in-story as the aliens' communications signal. Williams and Spielberg reportedly went through about 350 five-note phrases before settling on it, the idea being that it should sound like a greeting, not a melody; the motif functions both as score and as the in-story communications signal -- humans offer an oboe variation, the mothership answers in deep tuba tones, building a conversational duet between two civilizations. [C32933] The five notes sit in an Ab major pentatonic frame, with two notes repeated like the double "l" in "hello"; the hand signals in the finale follow the Kodaly method, and the score also quotes the Dies Irae for dread. [C32934] The score moves from atonal, eerie textures (Ligeti-like dissonance, disembodied voices) to a glowing finale that borrows "When You Wish Upon a Star", and the title comes from J. Allen Hynek's UFO classification system. [C32935] The film was shot in 65mm, cast Francois Truffaut as scientist Claude Lacombe, worked with Douglas Trumbull, and was the first of many collaborations with editor Michael Kahn; it won Oscars for cinematography (Vilmos Zsigmond) and sound effects editing, and Pauline Kael called Spielberg "a magician in the age of movies." [C32936] Williams's score was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- and lost to Williams himself, for Star Wars; it won two Grammys (film score and instrumental composition). [C32937] The Special Edition (1980) added the mothership-interior scene Spielberg later regretted, and the Director's Cut (1998) removed it -- the one time he showed the inside of the wonder and took it back. [C32938]

**Takeaway:** First contact is not a battle or a speech but a music lesson: five notes, repeated until two civilizations can say hello -- language is the special effect, and the greeting is the melody.

## 9. Superman and Raiders: building a hero's theme

Superman (1978) is a heroic march and fanfare built on a tonic-to-dominant fifth relationship, with a three-note "SU-per-MAN!" motif, plus the romantic love theme "Can You Read My Mind?" (Leslie Bricusse lyrics); the heroism is theatrical and cartoonish rather than solemn. Director Richard Donner reportedly interrupted the demo premiere, running onto the soundstage exclaiming "The music actually says 'Superman'!"; the three-note motif evokes "SU-per-MAN" ("There's everything to that," Williams replied). [C32939] The score was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score and the Golden Globe, won Grammys for Best Instrumental Composition ("Superman -- Main Title Theme") and Best Album of Original Score, and won the Saturn Award for Best Music; it was recorded July-November 1978 with the London Symphony Orchestra, and Jerry Goldsmith was originally attached. [C32940] Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981) is built set-piece-first, and its score is built the same way: "The Raiders March" (two separate candidate themes Spielberg liked so much he had Williams join them as main theme plus bridge) for Indy, plus the Ark theme (chorus and orchestra, eerie and apocalyptic), Marion's romantic theme (modelled on Now, Voyager, 1942), and low ominous brass for the Nazis. [C32941] The Raiders March is a large three-part form: Part 1 Indy's theme, Part 2 Marion's theme, Part 3 abbreviated return to Indy. Part 1 is itself ABA: Williams wrote the A and B melodies as two rival candidates for Indy's opening; Spielberg asked "can't you use both?", so one became A, the other the bridge. The A-section has four trumpets in unison, every short motive starting with a militaristic dotted rhythm; syncopations after beat 1 and before beat 4 add buoyancy and lightness (Indy's "fun" side). The first six motives all rise (highest notes forming a rising contour C up to F) -- positive, aspiring -- in deliberate contrast to the Imperial March's descending motives. [C32942] Note: a fan analysis claims the march deliberately avoids the "Do-Sol" heroic 5th used for Luke and Superman because Indy is an adventurer, not a justice-hero; this is an interpretive claim, not Williams's stated intent -- flag as unverified, do not present it as his reasoning. [C32943] The Raiders score was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- losing to Vangelis for Chariots of Fire -- and for a Grammy; it is the only Indiana Jones score performed by the London Symphony Orchestra, with orchestrations by Herbert W. Spencer and Al Woodbury, and the original album appeared in June 1981 on Columbia. [C32944] Further themes followed in Temple of Doom (1984), Last Crusade (1989), Kingdom of the Crystal Skull (2008) and Dial of Destiny (2023); Temple of Doom is darker, more violent and occult-inflected than Raiders -- a new driving march for the Temple and the enslaved children (martial snare, clanging metallic percussion, Eastern woodwinds), relentless action, anchored by the Raiders March; Williams resisted recycling. [C32945] Spielberg, on the Raiders score: "Jones did not perish, but listened carefully to the Raiders score... All things considered, Jones listened... and lived." [C32946]

**Takeaway:** A hero's theme is a shape you can hum after one hearing -- a fifth, a dotted rhythm, a rising line -- and the trick is that the shape has to fit the man: Superman gets the fifth, Indy gets the bounce, and neither would work on the other.

## 10. E.T.: the flying theme, and the film recut to the music

E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982) is a web of leitmotifs -- a plaintive 6-note flute opening, E.T.'s theme, and the "Flying Theme" planted as a mere 4-bar fragment early on, growing cue by cue until the bicycle flight -- with the 15-minute finale "Escape/Chase/Saying Goodbye" ("Adventures on Earth") uniting them; Spielberg edited the chase to the music. [C32947] The Flying Theme builds lift by expanding intervals: ascending first by a 5th, then by an octave, then by a minor 6th -- widening leaps meaning physical takeoff (search-extract only; the interval claim rests on a MuseScore feature and was not verified against the Pearson score analysis). The main theme (bars 9-16) is a sweeping unison melody across 3 octaves by all woodwinds and all strings except double basses, over repeated quaver chords (horns and piano), sonorous trombone chords, and a bass and tuba pedal anchoring the harmony; the intro (bars 1-8) is a quaver ostinato in violins, clarinets and flutes (octave-doubled) with tonic-dominant alternation in timpani and basses, in 2/2 metre. [C32948] The E.T. alien theme uses a superball rubbed on a suspended cymbal joined by celesta and shifting string textures for an otherworldly, ambient (non-melodic) sound (search-extract only); the same glittering palette -- celesta, harp, vibraphone, glockenspiel -- carries the Flying Theme, and the film's most quoted image, the bicycle against the moon, works as pure silhouette with the E.T. theme playing in full during the chase. [C32949] The score runs about 80 minutes of music, the full handwritten score over 500 pages, sketches expanded by Herbert W. Spencer from December 1981 to January 1982; it was recorded March-April 1982 at the MGM Scoring Stage. "The Magic of Halloween" quotes "Yoda's Theme" when E.T. sees a child in a Yoda costume. [C32950] The score won his fourth Oscar for Best Original Score (his third for original score), plus the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and the Grammy -- one of only six scores to win all four. [C32951] The adaptation reversed the normal hierarchy: Williams could not sync the 15-minute finale to the film's many precise cuts; after several takes Spielberg turned the film off, let Williams record the music alone at exactly the tempos and phrasing he wanted, "as one would in a concert", and then recut the end of the film to match the musical performance. Williams: "Steven re-edited the last part of the film slightly to configure with the musical performance that I felt was more powerful emotionally." The concert suite is the film's finale, not an arrangement of it. [C32952] The original 1982 E.T. soundtrack album (MCA, about 40 minutes) was not the film score: it was re-recorded, concertised highlights from a 70-minute-plus score -- "less a soundtrack per se than a suite" -- with three film tracks, two expanded cues, and three theme presentations; the film version of "Escape/Chase/Saying Goodbye" differs from the album's "Adventures on Earth" (15:06). A separate 1990 suite was commissioned for the Universal E.T. Adventure ride, with new themes for E.T.'s home planet and mentor Botanicus. [C32953]

**Takeaway:** The flying theme does not accompany the flight; it is the flight -- a fragment planted early, growing cue by cue until the music is strong enough to lift the bicycles, and the film was recut to keep up with it.

## 11. Orchestration: the Williams sound

The sound is late-Romantic and Golden Age Hollywood, in the lineage of Erich Korngold and Max Steiner (via Wagner, Strauss, Puccini, Holst and Stravinsky). Williams revived the grand symphonic score in the 1970s when pop, jazz-combo, rock-band and synthesiser scores dominated; Lucas wanted fantasy, not futuristic sound, to ground the strange setting in accessible music. [C32954] Scale: a full symphony orchestra of 79-113 players overall; The Empire Strikes Back required 104 players (excluding conductor and synthesiser) because of a fourth flute, third harp, five oboes, added piccolo and eight percussionists; Attack of the Clones used 110 players. Chorus ranges from 12 to 120 singers. The score "makes use of a considerable brass section over a comparatively smaller string section, giving the series its heraldic, brassy sound." [C32955] A New Hope Main Title forces: 3 flutes (3rd doubling piccolo), 2 oboes, 2 clarinets, bass clarinet, 2 bassoons; 4 horns, 3 trumpets, 3 trombones, tuba; timpani, triangle, snare, tam-tam, glockenspiel, vibraphone, cymbals; piano, celesta and harp; strings. Herbert Spencer was orchestrator (detailed scoring under Williams's supervision) for Star Wars and E.T.; Empire added Angela Morley, Al Woodbury and Fred Steiner. [C32956] The core technique is Romantic thick scoring with much doubling -- the same idea played by several instruments at once, often an octave or more apart (the Main Theme across three octaves in bars 21-29). Few solo timbres; exceptions are the piccolo star-sky melody (bars 36-38) and the harp glissando (bar 29). Techniques named: violin tremolando, harp glissando, timpani rhythms, huge tam-tam stroke. [C32957] Colour instruments do the pictorial work: celesta, harp, vibraphone and glockenspiel sound "glittering and magical" for space and wonder; in E.T. the same palette (plus piano) carries the Flying Theme. There are no electronics or synthesisers in the 1977 Star Wars score -- a deliberate choice for a futuristic film when synths were everywhere in pop and film scores (later scores do use synthesiser, listed among keyboards in the full nine-film roster). [C32958] The orchestrators are part of the sound, not a footnote: Herbert W. Spencer was the primary orchestrator for years -- Star Wars was "almost entirely orchestrated by Herbert Spencer", plus Jaws, The Eiger Sanction, Black Sunday, Close Encounters, 1941, SpaceCamp, Return of the Jedi and Jaws 2; Alexander Courage orchestrated The Poseidon Adventure, The Cowboys, Jaws 2, Home Alone, Hook, Jurassic Park and the first Harry Potter; Angela Morley orchestrated Star Wars (the Death Star trench "Use the Force, Luke" cue), Empire, Superman (the ice-palace music), E.T., Schindler's List (with Neufeld; the Viennese waltzes are her arrangements), Home Alone and Hook; Conrad Pope worked on Jurassic Park, the prequels and Harry Potter 1-3; John Neufeld on Jurassic Park (most cues), The Phantom Menace, Home Alone, Hook, Schindler's List and Nixon. [C32959] Williams's own style, per the main article, "blends romanticism and impressionism with complex orchestration"; influences named are Holst, Stravinsky, Korngold and others. The Norwegian composer Marcus Paus calls him "one of the great composers of any century" for embodying "dissonance and avant-garde techniques within a larger tonal framework." The Boston Globe named him "the most successful composer of film music in the history of the medium." [C32960]

**Takeaway:** The Williams sound is a choice, not a default: a big brass section over a smaller string section, everything doubled across octaves, colour instruments doing the pictorial work -- and the same hand can take it all away, which is what makes the big moments big.

## 12. Action scoring: mickey-mousing and its refusal

The classical Hollywood idiom Williams revived is defined by tight musico-visual synchronism -- "Mickey-Mousing", named for early Disney cartoons where a mouse tiptoeing got a violin note per step: music imitates visual gestures with corresponding musical gestures. Emilio Audissino calls Williams's idiom "neoclassical" because it restores this grand-symphonic synchronism after it had died out in the 1960s, replaced by jazz combos, rock bands, theme songs and synthesisers (search-extract only). [C32961] Frank Lehman formalises one mechanism as phrasal mickey-mousing: the coincidence of phrase length and visual action is manipulated -- cadential synchronisation, so a musical phrase lands exactly on a visual beat. A second, contrasting mechanism in the same article is the chromatically modulating cadential resolution (CMCR) -- initiating a diatonic cadence in one key whose dominant discharges onto the tonic of a chromatically related key -- strongly associated with cinematic wonder, illustrated through Jurassic Park (search-extract only). [C32962] The Star Wars opening is a textbook of both modes in one cue: bars 1-29 are regular four-bar AABA phrases (music-led), while from bar 30 the cue is through-composed and picture-led -- link (30-32, fanfare material, rising string sequences), transition (33-35, unstable mixed C/Db chords, diminuendo, pulse hard to feel), star-sky (36-38, pianissimo piccolo legato over celesta, harp and vibraphone), planets (39-41, scurrying strings, brass crescendo, strings and brass in different keys), rebel ship (42-43, low C pedal fortissimo, huge tam-tam, hammering timpani), Imperial chase (44-50, dissonant chords in rhythmic unison, clusters C+Db and F+G, tritone G-Db, metre changes to 3/4, ritardando and pause), battle (51-60, very fast, one-bar C ostinato under bitonal Db-then-Fb triads rising and falling by minor 3rds). Pearson's guide compares bars 44-60 to Holst's "Mars". [C32963] The Asteroid Field cue (Empire, 4:12) opens with a portion of the Imperial March, then subtle brass with woodwinds for the melody in two segments (0:50-2:15), big brass (2:17-2:41), a reprise, a crescendo to an altered brass segment, then a segment from "Han Solo and the Princess", ending quiet and eerie with woodwinds; the 2:17-2:41 brass segment is so identified with the Falcon that documentaries and games reuse it as the ship's theme -- a set-piece motif acquiring leitmotif status by reuse (search-extract only, fan wiki). [C32964] Counterpoint by absence is also a Williams decision: spotting sessions decide which scenes get music and which go without. The podrace (Phantom Menace), the Attack of the Clones asteroid chase (music enters only when Jango readies missiles, so Ben Burtt's seismic-charge "audio black hole" dominates), and the Endor speeder-bike chase (Jedi) are largely musicless -- Burtt argued intensity would be "more pronounced" with only point-of-view bike sounds. In Empire, music was cut even after recording (search-extract only). [C32965] Jurassic Park's T. rex sequence: Williams avoids cartoonish mickey-mousing and instead builds tension with low textures and timpani rolls, then delivers a massive brass hit precisely as the T. rex breaks the fence; the music is intentionally stripped back in the attack to focus on the rhythmic thumping of footsteps before full orchestral percussion enters. The raptor kitchen scene uses fragmented motifs, high staccato strings and fluttering flutes mirroring stealth and fear, with music stopping and starting with visual cuts, minor 2nds and tritones for dissonance (search-extract only, secondary commentary, not a score study). Williams himself called the score "a massive job of symphonic cartooning... You have to match the rhythmic gyrations of the dinosaurs and create these kind of funny ballets" -- and singled out the kitchen scene as "one of the most terrifying scenes... scored in a very dramatic way, and the gestures being operatic made it a little larger than life." [C32966] Gap, stated plainly: no source found in this leg giving bar-level sync mechanics for the Raiders truck chase specifically -- do not invent it; the chase is scored by the Raiders March and action ostinato in general terms only in the sources consulted. Duel of the Fates (Phantom Menace lightsaber duel) is mainly polyphonic, E and G minor, about 152 bpm, 4/4, 4:14, built from two minor-mode ostinati plus choral interjections originally heard in Empire; the text comes from the archaic Welsh poem Cad Goddeu ("Battle of the Trees"), translated into Sanskrit (Williams chose Sanskrit "because of the quality of the vowels"; pronunciation is rearranged by ear and not accurate), and Williams said the chorus was added to give a "ritualistic or quasi-religious feeling... we're in a big temple." The three components (theme, chorus, ostinato) are musically detachable and developed independently during the Battle of Naboo; the primary theme is a musical palindrome. [C32967]

**Takeaway:** The sync is the style when it serves the picture and the refusal is the style when it does not: music lands on the visual beat when the beat needs landing, and steps aside when the sound of a bike or a footstep is louder than any orchestra.

## 13. Schindler's List: the inversion

Schindler's List (1993) was a challenge: after the rough cut Williams told Spielberg, "I really think you need a better composer than I am for this film"; Spielberg replied, "I know, but they're all dead." He asked Itzhak Perlman to play the main theme; it earned his fourth Oscar for Best Original Score (fifth Oscar overall). [C32968] The sound is deliberately inverted: Williams abandoned his usual trumpet-heavy, brass bombast for strings, with the main theme as a solo violin (Perlman, who had never before played on a film soundtrack), Jewish folk-song inflection, a children's choir singing Oyfn Pripetshik in the ghetto liquidation, and klezmer clarinet solos by Giora Feidman. Same composer, opposite orchestral hierarchy -- proof the "Williams sound" is a choice, not a default (search-extract only). [C32969] The mechanism is restraint instead of brass -- a heartbreakingly simple solo-violin main theme over strings, drawing on Jewish folk song, with the orchestra stepping back; Williams initially felt the film too challenging. The score won the Oscar, the BAFTA and the Grammy, and was nominated for the Golden Globe. [C32970] It was recorded September-October 1993 at Sony Pictures Studios and Symphony Hall, Boston; album track "Oyf'n Pripetshok and Nacht Aktion" (3:51) and "Theme from Schindler's List" (4:14); the film versions of "Oyf'n Pripetshok" and "Yerushalayim Shel Zahav" are different recordings (from Billy Bathgate and Pour Sacha). [C32971] The concert adaptation works by subtraction: Three Pieces from Schindler's List (concert suite, about 15 minutes, solo violin) -- I. Theme from Schindler's List, II. Jewish Town (Krakow Ghetto -- Winter '41), III. Remembrances. Orchestration is chamber-like for a Williams suite (the Main Theme uses 2 flutes, oboe, clarinets, bassoons, horns, vibraphone, harp, celesta and strings -- no trumpets, trombones or tuba in the main theme). "Remembrances" is in ABA form, a tender epilogue for the survivors visiting Schindler's grave. The adaptation works by removing the brass bombast and letting the solo violin carry the piece as a violin-concerto-like elegy (search-extract only). [C32972] The film itself was shot in black and white, handheld about forty percent of the time, with no storyboards -- a deliberate documentary method -- and the single color intrusion, the little girl in the red coat during the Krakow ghetto liquidation, seen twice, is the film's only chromatic image (per the Spielberg study). Williams's score matches that method: it does not decorate the horror, it stands back from it. [C32973] Perlman's tie to Williams runs on: Perlman also played on Memoirs of a Geisha (2005, with Yo-Yo Ma) and on Cinema Serenade (1997); Williams called him saying "I have a theme, and I have a particular violin sound in my head. I had you especially in mind." [C32974]

**Takeaway:** The loudest composer in Hollywood made his quietest score for the film that needed it most: one violin, one folk song, the orchestra stepping back -- and the restraint is the monument.

## 14. The nineties: awe, scarcity, and Christmas

Jurassic Park (1993) is awe over terror -- two primary themes: a warm, quasi-religious Dinosaur Theme (low woodwinds and strings, horns maestoso, wordless choir) for the herbivores, and a bright trumpet-fanfare Adventure Theme; a menacing four-note Carnivore Motif for raptors and the T. rex; two major T. rex sequences use no music at all, or the adventure theme (heroic T. rex). [C32975] The Jurassic Park main theme (Bb major, broad arching melody, French horns and strings, later full brass and choir, hymn-like) is built on repeating phrases -- "cloned" phrasing, in Williams's own description via the site -- introduced reverently in "Journey to the Island" at the first dinosaur reveal, presented in lullaby form mid-film ("A Tree for My Bed"), otherwise reserved for the finale and end-credits suite. Williams: "There's almost a religious aspect to their music" -- awe, not horror, for the herbivores; a second, bolder, slightly meandering island-adventure motif carries the journey (search-extract only). [C32976] The Jurassic Park score received no Oscar nomination -- the film won 3 Oscars (sound, sound effects editing, visual effects); Williams won Best Original Score that same year for Schindler's List. He began scoring at the end of February 1993, with the recording session on 30 March 1993; he threw his back out conducting and Artie Kane took over some sessions; Spielberg, filming Schindler's List in Poland, heard the score only on piano previews and cassette. [C32977] Saving Private Ryan (1998) is deliberate scarcity -- the least music of any Spielberg-Williams collaboration to that point; long unscored battle sequences for realism, then long elegiac flows (French horns, strings, military drums, Tanglewood Festival Chorus) -- "Hymn to the Fallen" appears only over the end credits. It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Dramatic Score, won the Grammy for Best Instrumental Composition Written for a Motion Picture or TV, and was nominated for the Golden Globe and the BAFTA. [C32978] The Saving Private Ryan score was recorded 21-23 February 1998 in three days at Symphony Hall, Boston, with the Boston Symphony Orchestra, at a reported cost of about $100,000 an hour; brass solos by Tim Morrison; the album runs 64:12 against a film of over 2 hours. Williams's score is withheld entirely during the landing, the sound design (Gary Rydstrom) carrying it, including Miller's temporary deafness after a mortar blast (per the Spielberg study). [C32979] Home Alone (1990) is Christmas innocence as the score's spine -- "Somewhere in My Memory" (Williams and Bricusse) recurring in full, fragmentary, choral and music-box guises as a leitmotif for home, lacing slapstick with lullaby, alongside traditional carols ("O Holy Night", "Carol of the Bells"). It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score and Best Original Song ("Somewhere in My Memory"), and for a Grammy for the song. [C32980] Bruce Broughton was originally hired for Home Alone (early posters list him) but withdrew for The Rescuers Down Under; Williams was brought in via Spielberg; it was recorded in fall 1990 at the 20th Century Fox scoring stage, and the soundtrack was released 4 December 1990 on Sony Classical. [C32981] Empire of the Sun (1987) sits between these modes: a sweeping, elegiac orchestral landscape for a boy's journey from rich expatriate to prisoner of war -- the Welsh lullaby "Suo Gan" (Ambrosian Junior Choir, soloist James Rainbird) as innocence and memory, Chopin's Mazurka Op. 17 No. 4 ("Toy Planes, Home and Hearth"), and the soaring "Cadillac of the Skies". It won the BAFTA for Best Film Music and was nominated for the Oscar, the Golden Globe and the Grammy. [C32982]

**Takeaway:** The nineties prove the range by subtraction: dinosaurs get awe instead of horror, a war film gets almost no music at all, and a Christmas comedy gets a lullaby that keeps coming back in different clothes -- the score is whatever the film needs, not whatever the composer is famous for.

## 15. Harry Potter: Hedwig's Theme

Williams scored the first three Harry Potter adaptations (2001-2004); "Hedwig's Theme" was used in each subsequent film. He was asked to return for Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows -- Part 2, but director David Yates said "their schedules simply did not align", as Williams would have needed a rough cut sooner than was possible. [C32983] The score is a leitmotif atlas -- "Hedwig's Theme" (solo celesta, haunting, bell-like) as the series' main theme, plus two Voldemort themes, two Hogwarts themes, Diagon Alley, Quidditch, flying and two friendship themes. [C32984] Hedwig's Theme: 3/8, Misterioso, E minor, siciliana-like (dotted rhythms, restrained triple metre, cf. Faure's Sicilienne). It opens with a solo celesta (synthesised and real, electronically manipulated -- "literally unreal"), an instrument associated with childhood fantasy via Tchaikovsky's "Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy". The melody uses all 12 chromatic notes within an E-minor frame; diminished intervals and tritones create an "another world" quality (search-extract only). [C32985] Bar 6: B-D#-F-A# -- a distorted dominant 7th (a real B7 would be B-D#-F#-A); substituting F for F# and A# for A makes a chord that "cannot be fully explained, much like the workings of a wizard's magic". Bars 11-12 run through unrelated minor chords (G minor, F minor, A minor, F#7) before resolving to E minor. The concert piece is ternary: Hedwig's Theme (A) plus a faster "Nimbus 2000" theme (B, for the broomstick and fast mischief) plus a restated A, more intense, then a coda (search-extract only). [C32986] The concert version of Hedwig's Theme is itself a composite: it incorporates two film themes -- Hedwig's Theme (A) and the Flying/Nimbus 2000 theme (B) -- in an A-B-A-B-A structure (A at 0:00, B at 0:17, A at 0:45, B at 1:01, A at 1:18 in the cited clip), turning two separate film leitmotifs into one ternary concert piece (search-extract only). [C32987] The first film's score was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score (74th Academy Awards) -- losing to Howard Shore for The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring; Williams conducted the ceremony that night. Hedwig's Theme was first written as promotional material at Warner Bros. and Columbus's request, then became the theme; it was performed by a 102-piece London Session Orchestra at AIR Lyndhurst and Abbey Road, and the soundtrack was released 30 October 2001, entering the Billboard 200 at No. 48. [C32988] William Ross is the stand-in conductor in this story: he conducted Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets (2002) with the London Symphony Orchestra, adapting Williams's material, because Williams was scoring Catch Me If You Can at the same time; Williams composed about 40 minutes of new material for Chamber of Secrets and Ross adapted the rest from Philosopher's Stone. Ross also co-conducted The Force Awakens (2015) and The Last Jedi (2017) with Williams. [C32989]

**Takeaway:** A theme written as a promo became the sound of an entire world: a celesta, twelve chromatic notes inside a minor key, and a chord that cannot be explained -- magic as harmony, not as decoration.

## 16. The 2000s: jazz, noir, and the theme-less score

A.I. Artificial Intelligence (2001), from Kubrick's unfinished project, is atonal and mechanical writing for robots and David's disorientation set against tonal, lyrical music for human bonding (Monica's theme, "Stored Memories and Monica's Theme", about 11 minutes); soprano Barbara Bonney sings vocal solos; a Kubrick-specified Rosenkavalier waltz plays for the Rouge City entry, plus a Swan Lake excerpt. It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- losing to Howard Shore for Fellowship of the Ring -- and for the Golden Globe and the Grammy; Williams competed against himself that year (Harry Potter). [C32990] Catch Me If You Can (2002) is progressive early-1960s jazz -- a return to Williams's jazz-pianist roots and a nod to Henry Mancini (Brubeck, Art Pepper and Lee Konitz as reference points) -- saxophone and vibraphone-led, with period source songs ("Come Fly With Me", "The Look of Love", "The Girl from Ipanema") in the first Spielberg-Williams film to include them. It was nominated for the Oscar, the Grammy and the BAFTA for Best Original Score, and won the Critics' Choice for Best Composer. [C32991] The Catch Me If You Can jazz direction was driven by the animated opening title sequence; saxophone solos by Dan Higgins, vibraphone solos by Alan Estes; it was the 20th Spielberg-Williams collaboration; the concert adaptation Escapades for alto saxophone and orchestra followed in 2002. [C32992] Minority Report (2002) is the noir score: Spielberg asked for a "black and white" score -- dissonant, rhythmic, Herrmann-inflected (lonely brass call, slashing strings, skittery trumpet scales, furious scherzos) with a sparse, redemptive theme for Sean and Lara and a discreet wordless wailing voice for the murder victim; prominent Schubert and Tchaikovsky source pieces appear in the film. It was ruled ineligible for the Oscar for Best Original Score because of the prominent Schubert and Tchaikovsky; it won the BMI Film Music Award and the Critics' Choice for Best Score, and was nominated for the Saturn Award for Best Music. [C32993] War of the Worlds (2005) is deliberately theme-less -- Williams's bleakest, most avant-garde score in about 20 years: textural and motivic rather than melodic, dissonant polytonal writing, propulsive rhythmic action ("The Ferry Scene", "The Attack on the Car"), a female chorus crescendo like a shriek for Tripod attacks, a nearly inaudible male choir, and piano-led respite ("Reaching the Country", "The Separation of the Family"). It received no Oscar score nomination; the film's Oscar nominations were for visual effects, sound mixing and sound editing. [C32994] Williams scored War of the Worlds from only the first six reels (about 60 minutes) of an incomplete film -- a first for him; the album (Decca, 21 June 2005) includes Morgan Freeman's opening and closing narration over the music. [C32995] Memoirs of a Geisha (2005) is poetic, mysterious East-West fusion -- Yo-Yo Ma's cello and Itzhak Perlman's violin as twin solo voices over full orchestra plus traditional Japanese instruments (shakuhachi, koto, shamisen); Sayuri's theme and a Chairman's Waltz. It won the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and the Grammy for Best Score Soundtrack for Visual Media, and was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- losing to Brokeback Mountain; Williams's 44th and 45th nominations that year (with Munich). [C32996] The Memoirs of a Geisha soundtrack was released 22 November 2005 on Sony Classical (61:02); Masakazu Yoshizawa (shakuhachi) and Masayo Ishigure (koto) were among the players; Spielberg was executive producer and Rob Marshall directed. Yo-Yo Ma's tie to Williams runs on: Seven Years in Tibet (1997), Memoirs of a Geisha (2005), and Yo-Yo Ma Plays the Music of John Williams (2002), including the premiere recording of the Cello Concerto composed for Ma. [C32997]

**Takeaway:** The 2000s are the decade he proved he could do without his own trademarks: jazz instead of fanfare, noir instead of wonder, a score with no theme at all -- and the jazz was not a new trick, it was the Johnny Williams piano bench, thirty years later.

## 17. Boston Pops and the concert works

Williams was principal conductor of the Boston Pops from 1980 to 1993, succeeding Arthur Fiedler (whom he never met in person, speaking by telephone); he is now laureate conductor. The route in: in July 1978 he was called by Los Angeles Philharmonic manager Ernest Fleischmann to substitute for the ailing Fiedler at the Hollywood Bowl (with the Los Angeles Philharmonic); he guest-conducted the Pops in May 1979 and was appointed in 1980. ## 18. Late Williams: Lincoln to Dial of Destiny

After a three-year hiatus from film scoring came The Adventures of Tintin and War Horse (both 2011); Tintin was his first animated-film score (1920s-30s European jazz opening, "pirate music" for sea battles) and won an Annie Award. War Horse is concert-like pastoral and emotional writing for a boy and his horse in World War I -- a 90-piece orchestra recorded more like a concert piece, relying on individual musicians' performances, with warm Americana-tinged themes rather than action set-pieces; it was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score (his 47th; also nominated that year for Tintin, his 46th), losing to Ludovic Bource for The Artist. [C33006] Lincoln (2012) is subdued, Americana-bound restraint -- the "Freedom's Call" and "Integrity" theme as the main theme, the "American Process" and "Voice of the People" theme in thin two-part solo-piano texture for Lincoln's isolation, and a "Cost of War" and Remembrance theme, also solo piano; orchestral ominous music is used sparingly. It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score (his 48th per Wikipedia -- against a 43rd figure from Film Music Notes, unresolved), the Golden Globe, the BAFTA and the Grammy, and won the Critics' Choice for Best Score. [C33007] Lincoln was recorded at Symphony Center, Chicago, by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra and Chorus -- Williams persuaded Spielberg to use them, having long wanted a film score with those forces; it was released 2 November 2012 on Sony Classical. [C33008] The Fabelmans (2022) is heart-on-sleeve solo piano throughout -- nostalgia and a tribute to Spielberg's mother Leah Adler, herself a concert pianist -- alongside quoted classical works (Beethoven, Satie, Haydn, Bach); Spielberg called it Williams's most personal gift to him and to his parents' memory. It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- his 53rd nomination, making him the oldest person ever nominated for a competitive Oscar (90 at announcement) and the only person nominated in seven decades. Williams first previewed the score for Spielberg on his Steinway; the film is dedicated to Spielberg's parents, whom Williams knew well. [C33009] Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny (2023) is straight orchestral franchise summation -- reworked Raiders March and older themes plus new themes for Helena Shaw and the Dial itself; no choral element; co-orchestrated and co-conducted with William Ross. It was nominated for the Oscar for Best Original Score -- his 54th, making him again the oldest Oscar nominee ever (91) -- and for the Grammy for Best Score; it won the Grammy for Best Instrumental Composition ("Helena's Theme"). "Helena's Theme (For Violin and Orchestra)" was released as a single on 22 June 2023, soloist Anne-Sophie Mutter. [C33010] In June 2022 Williams announced Dial of Destiny would likely be his last film score as he planned to retire from film and focus on concert music; by January 2023 he reversed this, saying he had at least "10 more years to go. I'll stick around for a while!", comparing himself to Spielberg's father Arnold, who worked until 100. [C33011] The only health item in the main article: in March 2015 it was announced Thomas Newman would score Bridge of Spies instead, "as Williams's schedule was interrupted by a minor health issue." No other health information appears in the article. Possible 30th Spielberg collaboration, flagged as a single report only: Variety via KinoCheck reports Williams, 93, will score Spielberg's untitled 2026 UFO film (script David Koepp), after his 2022 retirement statement was walked back in a late-2023 Times interview; not independently confirmed beyond that report. [C33012]

**Takeaway:** He announced his retirement and then un-announced it, and the late scores are the proof: piano instead of brass for a childhood, a violin theme for a new heroine, ten more years -- the work did not end, it got quieter and more personal.

## 19. Aesthetic logic: the tune that outlives the film

The through-line of seventy years is melody as memory: a theme is written once, in a form simple enough to hum, and then it is allowed to live -- to change key, to lose its brass, to return on a single horn at a farewell, to become a concert piece, to be played by orchestras who never saw the film. The Jaws cell is two notes; the Close Encounters greeting is five; the Star Wars fanfare is a fifth and a seventh; Hedwig's Theme is a celesta and a chord that cannot be explained. None of them needs the film to survive. [C33013] The second through-line is parametric control: tempo, dynamics and register do the work the notes do not. The Jaws ostinato accelerates from a resting heartbeat to a panicked pulse; the Force Theme withholds its resolution until the mission succeeds; the Imperial March descends, five short ideas, each falling; the Flying Theme widens its intervals as the bicycles rise. The mechanism is always the same -- a small musical variable, driven by a dramatic variable, until the audience feels the change in its body before it understands it in its head. [C33014] The third through-line is restraint as a positive choice: Schindler's List takes the brass away; Saving Private Ryan takes the music away; War of the Worlds takes the theme away; Home Alone gives a lullaby instead of a march. The composer famous for the biggest sound in Hollywood is most himself when he refuses it, because the refusal proves the bigness was a decision, not a habit. [C33015] The fourth through-line is the concert life of the film music: the Star Wars Suite reorders themes from across films into a five-movement concert sequence; Adventures on Earth is the film's finale because the film was recut to the music; Three Pieces from Schindler's List works by subtraction; Hedwig's Theme fuses two film themes into one ternary piece. The film cue is fragmented, picture-timed and often incomplete; the concert version completes, reorders, expands and re-orchestrates it into a closed form with a proper ending -- and the concert version is where the themes go to live. [C33016] The fifth through-line is the apprenticeship that never ended: the studio pianist who played Mancini and Bernstein, the orchestrator who learned from Waxman, Herrmann and Newman, the jazz pianist Johnny Williams who came back as the Catch Me If You Can saxophone, the Boston Pops conductor who hissed at his own players and stayed nine more years, the nonagenarian conducting the Vienna Philharmonic. Every stage of the career is still audible in the next one. [C33017] The Oscar record is the frame, not the picture: 54 nominations, 5 wins -- the most-nominated living person, second only to Walt Disney's 59; the only person nominated in seven consecutive decades (1960s-2020s); the oldest nominee in any category at 91; 27 Grammy Awards from 77 nominations, seven BAFTAs, three Emmys from six nominations, four Golden Globes from 25 nominations. The wins, by film year: Fiddler on the Roof (1971, Best Scoring: Adaptation and Original Song Score); Jaws (1975), Star Wars (1977), E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), Schindler's List (1993) -- all four Best Original Score. Flag carried: the awards section states 48 nominations for Best Original Score and five for Best Original Song, which sums to 53, not the stated total of 54; the article does not explain the one-nomination gap. [C33018]

**Takeaway:** The logic is a tune simple enough to remember, a variable small enough to control, a refusal brave enough to make the big moments big, and a concert life long enough to outlive the film -- melody as memory, and memory as the whole career.

## 20. The network: Spielberg, Lucas, and the hands that write the parts

The Spielberg partnership is the centre of the network: 29 features, 1974-2022, from The Sugarland Express through The Fabelmans, with Williams also composing the theme and scoring episodes of Spielberg's television anthology Amazing Stories (1985) and serving as music consultant on West Side Story (2021); the named gaps are The Color Purple (Quincy Jones) and Bridge of Spies (Thomas Newman), and the other three of the "all but five" features are not named in the main article. [C33019] The Lucas partnership is the second centre: all nine Skywalker Saga films, 1977-2019, plus a suite and cues for Solo: A Star Wars Story (2018) and the Galaxy's Edge theme-park theme; about fifty recurring themes, one of the largest theme caches in film music; the first two trilogies performed by the London Symphony Orchestra with the London Voices chorus, the sequel trilogy largely conducted by Williams and William Ross and performed by the Hollywood Freelance Studio Symphony. **Takeaway:** The network is a room full of people who each know one part of the sound -- the director who asks for it, the orchestrator who writes the parts, the soloist who plays the theme, the orchestra that has played it for fifty years -- and Williams is the man in the middle who hears all of it at once.
