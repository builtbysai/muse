# Titian: Study Notes

**Site:** Tiziano Vecellio (also Vecelli), known in English as Titian (c.1488/90, Pieve di Cadore - died Friday 27 August 1576, Venice, of plague, aged 86-88), Italian Renaissance painter, the most important artist of Renaissance Venetian painting and, with Giorgione, a founder of the Venetian school. For sixty years he was the undisputed master of Venetian painting, from the death of Giovanni Bellini in late 1516 to his own death in 1576; official painter to the Republic; the first painter to have a mainly international clientele; painter of the Assumption of the Virgin (1516-18, 690 x 360 cm) for the Frari, the Pesaro Madonna (1519-26), the Ferrara bacchanals for Alfonso d'Este, the equestrian Charles V at Muhlberg (1548) and the poesie for Philip II (six delivered 1553-62, plus the unfinished Death of Actaeon); in his last years he pushed his art to the edge of abstraction, in the Flaying of Marsyas and the Pieta (1575-76, painted for his own tomb in the Frari). Born in Pieve di Cadore in the Dolomites, son of the soldier and timber merchant Gregorio Vecellio; sent to Venice aged about nine or ten, trained in the workshops of Sebastiano Zuccato, Gentile Bellini, Giovanni Bellini and Giorgione (Ludovico Dolce's order; Vasari says Giovanni Bellini first; no documentation survives for any of it); worked beside Giorgione on the Fondaco dei Tedeschi frescoes 1507-09; kept a large workshop at Biri Grande from 1531, with his sons Orazio and Pomponio, his cousins Marco and Cesare Vecellio, and assistants including Girolamo Dente; made Count Palatine and Knight of the Golden Spur by Charles V in 1533; visited Rome 1545-46 and Augsburg 1548 and 1550-51; buried in the Frari beneath the Altar of the Crucifix, his Pieta never installed there. The record is strong: the English Wikipedia Titian article, the Encyclopaedia Britannica entry, the National Gallery London artist page, Peter Humfrey's NGA Online Editions biography, the Smarthistory Assumption essay, and Jill Dunkerton and Marika Spring's National Gallery Technical Bulletin vol. 34 survey essay on his painting technique to c.1540, all read end to end. ## M1. Colorito -- Form Built by Colouring, Not by Line

What it IS: the Venetian doctrine that painting is made by the application of colour, not by a drawn contour filled in afterwards. David Rosand, quoted in EN Wikipedia (Process section): "the semantic distinction between colorito and colore. Colorito is 'the act of colouring, the actual application of paint and manipulation of the brush', while Venetians hardly ever used the uninflectional noun colore. For him, the style of Venetian painting, il colorito alla veneziana, characterized by an open pictorial structure with its parts connected in a fabric of constructive brushstrokes, defines itself by its process." How it works: the composition is assembled from patches and strokes of coloured paint whose edges, overlaps and tonal steps do the work that a Florentine draughtsman would do with line. Edges soften and blend; a form is located by a warm patch against a cool patch, not by an outline. "Titian's technique of open painting with visible traces of brushwork was revolutionary, and gave his portrayals an unprecedented sensuous effect" (EN Wikipedia, citing Sylvia Ferino-Pagden). The watchable behaviour is forms assembling and dissolving as you approach and withdraw: at distance a face or a sleeve reads whole; up close it breaks into separate constructive strokes with no drawn boundary underneath. The picture has no single "drawing" to find -- the drawing IS the paint. **Takeaway:** colour is the structure, not the decoration; build the image from strokes, never from outlines.

## M2. Gesso Ground with a Thin Pale Imprimitura

What it IS: the prepared painting surface. Technical Bulletin 34 (Dunkerton and Spring 2013, survey essay, read lines 0-2826): "Titian actually used a traditional gesso ground, sometimes modified with an imprimitura layer, and... his paint medium was a straightforward drying oil." Gesso is calcium sulphate of mineral origin bound in glue. On canvas it was applied thinly, to fill the interstices of the weave but only to cover the tops of the threads: "It was not Titian's intention to suppress the texture of the woven fabric." In the Portrait of Girolamo Fracastoro, ridges of gesso suggest it was applied with a palette knife. How it works: the slightly absorbent gesso is sealed with an imprimitura, a thin priming of drying oil and pigment, usually including lead white (which speeds drying). In Titian's early National Gallery paintings (c.1510-1514) the priming is about 30 micrometres thick and consists mainly of lead white with a very fine-grained lamp black, making the surface off-white, tending to pale grey. The binder was heat-bodied linseed oil. In three works the priming "was left exposed either at the edge, or in gaps where painted forms do not quite meet", so the pale ground itself becomes part of the picture. The watchable behaviour is a luminous pale field showing through gaps between forms; paint sitting ON the weave rather than sunk into it; the canvas texture still legible through thin passages. Later (late 1520s-1530s) he experiments with darker grounds -- red-brown (Venus Anadyomene, A Boy with a Bird), dark grey-brown with earths and manganese black (Aldobrandini Madonna) -- and by the later career often paints directly on gesso with no priming at all. **Takeaway:** the ground is a working light source; keep it pale and thin so light returns through the paint.

## M3. Sketchy Brush Underdrawing, Freehand, Then Freely Disobeyed

What it IS: contrary to the old myth that Titian "created his works entirely in paint, without first drawing his design" (Technical Bulletin 34), infrared reflectography reveals underdrawing on almost all the works studied. "Titian's initial underdrawing seems usually to have been executed using a brush and a liquid medium containing a carbon black, essentially a black paint" (Technical Bulletin 34). It is broad and fluid: "a shape or a detail is indicated with just a few short strokes or curves", and the same "bubbling curls" of a head recur in a work of c.1510 and in The Triumph of Love more than 30 years later -- the handwriting is consistent across the career. How it works: for new inventions Titian worked freehand, "placing his figures by eye, as demonstrated by the repeated repositioning of Cupid in The Triumph of Love", with no mechanical transfer of cartoons. Only workshop replicas used traced, schematic underdrawings. The underdrawing is a suggestion, not a contract: "For Titian the underdrawing was only a rough guide and he did not hesitate to disregard it in the development of a painting." The watchable behaviour is the first marks being few, fast, heavy black curves, and the finished figure often sitting visibly off those first marks. The picture is found by moving, not by tracing. **Takeaway:** draw fast and loose in paint, then let the painting overrule the drawing.

## M4. Pentimenti -- Perpetual Revision, Even Decades Later

What it IS: Titian "relentlessly revised his works, but the changes he made did not follow a linear progression. He tested the positions of different motifs such as figures and landscape elements numerous times, but new arrangements were not always adopted and those previously rejected might be taken up again" (EN Wikipedia). "When laying-in the background of a composition, the master and his workshop team typically laid in more than one iteration, which might be obliterated by others, as often happened, or revised and reworked later, even 40 or 50 years later" (EN Wikipedia). How it works: changes are made directly over underlying colours, sometimes with the paint underneath not yet dry, producing cracks and drying defects (the faun over the chariot wheel in Bacchus and Ariadne; areas of Christ and the Adulteress). Corrections are drawn in black liquid medium over first paint layers, or indicated with strokes of X-ray-opaque lead white, visible in X-radiographs (the shepherd's head in The Holy Family with a Shepherd; the oculus in The Triumph of Love). NGA Humfrey: "technical examination has revealed that not only works by Titian himself, but also those ascribable to his collaborators or followers, show extensive pentimenti, or departures from the original design." The most significant pentimenti are to heads: "the shepherd's engagement with the Holy Family group, Ariadne's eyes meeting those of Bacchus, the forward movement of the head of the female saint in 'The Aldobrandini Madonna'" (Technical Bulletin 34). Whole landscapes are cancelled: in Noli me Tangere "a broadly applied layer of X-ray-opaque paint... covers much of the upper half of the canvas, which perhaps served to cancel the hill and farm buildings that were initially on the left side of the landscape, before they were moved to the right side." The watchable behaviour is a head turning a fraction and the whole relation between two figures changing; a ghost of an earlier position showing through thin paint; the picture visibly thinking in public. **Takeaway:** revision is the method, not a correction of it; move the head, move the meaning.

## M5. Glazing and Scumbling -- Thin Transparent Colour over Solid Paint

What it IS: the build-up of a picture from many thin layers, each dried before the next. Sir C. J. Holmes, Notes on the Science of Picture Making (1927), Titian section, read end to end: in the early works "the whole subject seems first to have been carried out, with some completeness, in transparent brown upon a luminous ground... the oil colours were applied, sometimes transparent and sometimes opaque... the tints being frequently softened, spread and blended with the fingers... The work was then put aside for some considerable time, all excess oil being bleached out by exposure to sunshine. Then the final painting was begun, with scumbles of opaque colour and glazes of transparent colour, each coat being thoroughly dried before the application of the next." Two points Holmes stresses: "the transparent brown underpainting was never covered over in the shadows, and opaque pigment, when used at all, was used in thin translucent films"; and "the perfect drying of each film before the application of the next removed all superfluous oil." How it works: in the late manner the first painting is a solid monochrome in black, white and red, carried to near-completion, then colour is added by glazing over it, giving "rich, broken colour well adapted to the uncertain vibrant illumination in which Titian's latest subjects are viewed" (Holmes). Technical Bulletin 34 gives the measured case: "the four layers of red lake glaze on Bacchus' cloak, for instance -- each one needing to be dry before the next was applied. This may partly account for the delays and procrastination for which Titian became known." The World History Encyclopedia states the same working rule in short form: "The artist's paintings frequently employed a solid earth colour foundation upon which Titian added successive layers of semi-transparent lighter colours." The watchable behaviour is shadows staying transparent and warm because the brown underpainting is never buried; lights built thick; a red cloak glowing from four stacked glazes rather than from one opaque red. The cost is time: each coat must dry, so a picture takes months and the painter keeps many canvases in progress at once. **Takeaway:** depth is stacked transparency; never bury the shadow layer, and let every coat dry.

## M6. The Finger and Thumb as Finishing Tools

What it IS: in the final stages Titian worked the wet paint with his hands. Holmes (1927, read end to end): "the tints being frequently softened, spread and blended with the fingers." The fuller account comes from Palma il Giovane, via Crowe and Cavalcaselle, Titian: His Life and Times (1877), quoted in search results (Google Books snippet; NOT read end to end -- flagged here): "Now and then he would model the light into half tint with a rub of his finger; or with a touch of his thumb he would dab a spot of dark pigment into some corner to strengthen it... in fact when finishing he painted more with his fingers than with his brush." Maria H. Loh, Titian's Touch (2019), publisher's summary (search snippet): "At the end of his long, prolific life, Titian was rumoured to paint directly on the canvas with his bare hands. He would slide his fingers across bright ridges of oil paint, loosening the colours, blending, blurring, and then bringing them together again. With nothing more than the stroke of a thumb or the flick of a nail, Titian's touch brought the world to life." How it works: a fingertip or thumb drags and smears wet oil paint, softening the transition between light and shadow in a way a brush cannot, because the finger both removes and redistributes paint in one gesture. Philip Sohm, "Venetian Finger Painting after Titian" (Artibus et Historiae, 2020; abstract via search): "Palma Giovane was the first Renaissance painter to openly and proudly declare himself to be a finger painter... Titian's technique made famous by Marco Boschini, Palma's pupil." The watchable behaviour is edges that melt rather than stop; a half-tint modelled by a rub; a dark dab in a corner that steadies the whole picture; flesh that looks touched rather than painted. Flag: the exact Palma Giovane wording is quoted here from search snippets, not from a page read end to end; the Holmes "blended with the fingers" line IS from a page read end to end. **Takeaway:** finish with the hand: a thumb-rub can do what a brush cannot -- soften a transition without losing the form.

## M7. Canvas as an Expressive Instrument -- Weave, Texture, Broken Stroke

What it IS: Titian's pioneering use of the canvas support itself as a pictorial device. NGA Humfrey (read end to end): "Closely linked to this was his pioneering exploitation of the canvas support to endow his paintings with an expressively uneven texture while also using varying brushwork, with the result that some areas are thickly impastoed while others are thin and sketchy." How it works: Technical Bulletin 34 shows he chose his canvas deliberately: "Titian, however, seems to have been more particular in his choice of weave and weight. Only two of the works included in this study are on twill-weave canvases... All the others are tabby (plain) weave." Fine canvases (nearly as high a thread count as the Bacchus and Ariadne canvas sent by Alfonso d'Este) go with refined execution; coarser canvases have "slubs and areas with thicker more raised threads as a result of uneven spinning". The paint answers the weave: "when a brush loaded with stiff lead white paint is quickly dragged over underlying colours that are already dry, a vibrating 'broken' brushstroke results: for example, the long white hairs of the lynx fur in the Portrait of Girolamo Fracastoro" (Technical Bulletin 34). Conversely, adding a little more medium makes paint flow for a translucent, gauzy effect (Christ's loincloth in Noli me Tangere). Early in his career a surprisingly high proportion of his paintings were on panel, not canvas, and the two supports age differently -- a warning that two works close in date "can look markedly different depending on their supports" (Technical Bulletin 34). NG London: "you stop worrying about whether your brushstrokes are showing or not: you actually make that part of the feature of painting... he really exploits the bulk of the paint and he loves the buttery quality of the paint when you mix the pigment with oil. He's putting it on with these long sweeping bold strokes." The watchable behaviour is thick impasto beside thin sketchy passages in the same picture; a broken stroke that skips over the weave; a fur or a feather rendered in four or five wet-in-wet strokes of lead white (the feather in the Presentation of the Virgin, Technical Bulletin 34). **Takeaway:** let the cloth show; the weave is part of the image, and a dragged dry brush over it makes its own mark.

## M8. Oil-Medium Engineering -- Walnut Oil, Linseed Oil, and a Drier

What it IS: Titian did not use one oil for everything. Technical Bulletin 34: "both linseed and walnut oils were used... In every sample there was evidence that the oil had been heat-bodied, at least to some extent: that is, the oil had been heated so that it became thicker and would dry more rapidly." Walnut oil, which yellows less rapidly, was chosen "generally for lighter passages of paint: for example, the shepherd's white breeches in The Holy Family with a Shepherd, and the lighter areas of the architecture in the Portrait of Girolamo Fracastoro"; linseed oil was used where yellowing mattered less, such as brown backgrounds. In The Music Lesson walnut oil appears even in the priming and in a red lake glaze. How it works: in his earliest paintings Titian also used zinc sulphate (white vitriol, white copperas), a colourless paint additive, as a drier: "Zinc sulphate... is colourless and therefore not a pigment but a paint additive... Zinc vitriol is occasionally mentioned as a drier for oil in documentary sources on painting technique." It appears in primings and underpaint, and in orange areas containing realgar "where it might be expected that a drier would be needed". Too much drier causes defects: "in Christ and the Adulteress in particular, the areas in which zinc sulphate has been used show marked paint defects, as can happen if too much drier is incorporated." The Bulletin cautions that "it is not yet possible to draw firm inferences about this material as part of Titian's practice -- but so far it does appear to be present only in his earliest paintings". The watchable behaviour is whites that stay white (walnut oil) against browns that warm with age (linseed oil); early pictures with localised cracking where the drier was over-used. The medium is chosen per passage, not per picture. **Takeaway:** choose the oil by the passage -- slow-yellowing walnut for lights, linseed where warmth is welcome, and dose any drier with care.

## M9. The Workshop as a Production System -- Copies, Variants, Retouching

What it IS: a large, organised shop that multiplied successful compositions. EN Wikipedia: "he began to produce variants and replicas of his works in a systematic almost assembly-line fashion, an unprecedented practice. In his studio Titian used 'specialised collaborators' who made copies of his works, which he then retouched for corrections and to impart his esprit to paintings that might be sold as originals, or to purchasers who were not necessarily averse to settling for copies." How it works: NGA Humfrey: "Sometimes he himself produced repetitions or variants of his most successful compositions. But more often, his extraordinary fame led to a public demand for his works that he could only satisfy by employing a number of assistants to produce replicas and variants after his designs." The master's involvement is typically at the two ends: "Titian's involvement in the initial and final stages of a shop production makes it difficult or impossible to draw a clear line between autograph and nonautograph." It was "sometimes supposed that he made small-scale oil sketches, in the manner of Sir Peter Paul Rubens... which would serve as the basis for repetition by his assistants. But there is no real evidence for this supposition, and it is more likely that his compositions were preserved in his shop in the form of large-scale but long-since-lost drawings, or that a full-size painted version was retained for some years in the workshop." Technical Bulletin 34: workshop products use similar materials "but with perhaps less expensive pigments and fewer of the adjustments that characterise his particular creative method", and "Titian also needed to have assistants in constant attendance to prepare his materials for painting", above all "the grinding of pigments with drying oils to make paints of the consistency and quality that he required." The watchable behaviour is the same Danae, Venus and Adonis or Ecce Homo in several versions, differing in finish; a picture that is Titian's at the start (design) and at the end (retouching) with other hands in the middle; a studio full of unfinished canvases at his death, completed by assistants "in a way that they thought would make them easier to sell" (NGA Humfrey). **Takeaway:** the master's touch is at the beginning and the end; the middle can be delegated, but the retouch is what makes it his.

## M10. Painting for Distance -- Finish Calibrated to Viewing Distance

What it IS: Titian varies his finish according to how far away the picture will be seen, and he knows that detail which will not register is wasted work. Technical Bulletin 34: "His experience in painting murals meant that Titian knew not to waste effort on detail that would not register in the relatively low light levels in which these works were mostly seen." The Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple (1534-38, 335 x 775 cm, set high above panelling and doorways) "is painted in a rapid direct technique, using mainly opaque pigment mixtures. The handling of the heads, including the several portraits, is closer to Titian's Santo frescoes than to his paintings on panel or canvas. The feather in the hat of the figure leaning out of a window in the centre of the Presentation consists of no more than four or five wet-in-wet strokes of lead white paint, and yet it reads perfectly in the context for which it was painted." How it works: at the other extreme, Bacchus and Ariadne, "for an important client and designed for a relatively small and well-illuminated room", shows "his powers of observation and his descriptive ability with the brush, above all in his painting of the plants and animals" -- the satyr's long silky fur, the lion's defensive pose like "a small barking dog... his mouth, with his wide pink tongue and fearsome teeth, indicated with a few quick strokes of lead white". The same economy appears in the Assunta: "The bold execution of figures such as God the Father high up on the Assunta altarpiece, with unblended highlights applied with a stiff impasto... followed only a few years after the delicate refinement of Noli me Tangere." Smarthistory on the Assunta: "Large forms and strong colour make the movement legible from a distance; close looking then reveals the different responses in the group" (thehistoryofart.org). And: "These are paintings that need to be seen in situ... They need to overwhelm you from their scale, from the richness of their color" (Smarthistory, Zucker). The watchable behaviour is a picture that reads as a single surge of movement from the church door, and as a crowd of distinct, differently moved faces from the altar steps; a feather that is five strokes from below and a feather from the floor; a god's face that is rough impasto up close and blazing light from afar. **Takeaway:** finish to the viewing distance: five strokes are enough if the picture will be seen from twenty feet away, and a small bright room earns the fur.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Early / Giorgione circle, c.1503-1515. Titian enters Venice as a boy, works through Sebastiano Zuccato, Gentile Bellini and Giovanni Bellini, then falls under Giorgione. The two paint the Fondaco dei Tedeschi frescoes side by side in 1508-09, and contemporaries already rate the younger man's work the more impressive. When Giorgione dies of plague in 1510, Titian inherits his subjects -- the pastoral, the sleeping nude, the poetic landscape -- and his tonal way of building form out of colour rather than line. The pictures are small to medium canvases (90-150 cm wide), two or three figures in a landscape that does half the emotional work, soft modelling, warm flesh, no hard contour. Attribution is genuinely hard in this phase: the Pastoral Concert, Christ Carrying the Cross and several early Madonnas have all moved between Giorgione and Titian in the 20th century, with traffic almost entirely one way, Giorgione to Titian. What is already there: oil on canvas, colour as the substance of form, the landscape as a mood rather than a backdrop. By 1514 Sacred and Profane Love already shows the break: the composition is entirely clear and certain, the two women are monumental, and the painting is unmistakably Titian's even though its vocabulary is Giorgione's. Scale is intimate; colour warm, luminous, high-key flesh against green landscape; composition frieze-like or landscape-framed; brushwork smooth, blended, invisible.

Phase 2 -- Maturity, 1516-1530s. Everything gets bigger and more violent. The Assunta (1516-18) is 690 cm tall, the largest altarpiece in Venice, with figures over life-size and apostles in poses of unprecedented agitation -- a deliberate break with Bellini's meditative stillness, and a direct answer to Raphael and Michelangelo in Florence and Rome. The Pesaro Madonna (1519-26) pushes the Virgin off-centre to the top of a diagonal stair, with cropped columns slicing the picture vertically; the sacra conversazione will never be symmetrical again. For Alfonso d'Este in Ferrara he paints the first heroic-scale mythologies in oil -- Worship of Venus, Bacchus and Ariadne, Andrians -- crowded, drunken, sunlit, built on Philostratus and Ovid rather than on scripture. The Death of St Peter Martyr (1528-30) fuses extreme violence with a landscape that presses into the scene; Vasari calls it his most complete work, and it looks forward to the Baroque. What stays constant: colour still does the work of form -- in the Assunta the apostles are "created, by strong cross illumination, from patches of light and shade", and Raphael's rule (form first, colour added) is reversed. The reclining nude thread continues from the Dresden Venus through the half-length women (Flora, Woman with a Mirror) to the Venus of Urbino. Scale: monumental altarpieces and room-filling mythologies; the largest works on wood panel until about 1550, canvas for smaller works. Colour: saturated and expensive -- ultramarine skies, vermilion and crimson drapery, gold light. Composition: diagonals, triangles, off-centre weight, figures cut by the frame. Figure handling: heroic, twisting, muscular, Michelangelesque in energy but Venetian in flesh. Brushwork: still controlled and finished, but with bolder modelling than Phase 1.

Phase 3 -- Imperial portraits, 1540s. The patron changes, and with him the subject. After the 1533 Bologna portrait of Charles V, Titian is court painter to the emperor, a Count Palatine and Knight of the Golden Spur. He travels -- Milan 1540-41 for the Allocution, Rome 1545-46 where he meets Michelangelo and sees the antique at first hand, Augsburg 1548 and 1550-51. Portraiture becomes statecraft: the seated Charles V and the equestrian Charles V at Muhlberg are not likenesses but arguments about power, painted from the real armour and harness used at the battle. At the same time the first Danae (1544-46, for Cardinal Farnese) opens the reclining-nude series that will dominate the next twenty years, and the Crowning with Thorns (1542-43) and Ecce Homo (1543) show a new interest in compressed shallow space, classical quotation (Laocoon, Belvedere Torso, a bust of Tiberius) and crowd psychology. What stays constant: flesh, colour, the Venetian refusal to subordinate painting to drawing -- Michelangelo's famous complaint in 1545 is that Titian has colour without disegno. Scale stays large (the equestrian portrait is 335 x 283 cm; Ecce Homo 242 x 361 cm), but the ambition is now psychological and political rather than devotional. Colour: darker grounds, deeper reds (red lake shipped from Venice to Augsburg), armour as a field of small controlled highlights. Composition: a single dominant figure against a low horizon and a vast sky; or a crowd pressed into a shallow stage. Figure handling: likeness held against idealisation -- Charles's weak jaw and gout are present but made to read as resolve. Brushwork: passages of realism (armour, harness) sit next to broad stretches of colour and strong brushstrokes in landscape and sky, the split the National Gallery notes in the Muhlberg portrait.

Phase 4 -- Poesie for Philip II, 1550s-1560s. From 1551 Titian paints mythologies for Philip II that he himself calls poesie -- visual poems, six delivered (Danae, Venus and Adonis, Perseus and Andromeda, Diana and Actaeon, Diana and Callisto, Rape of Europa) plus the undelivered Death of Actaeon. They are all roughly 175-190 x 190-207 cm, all Ovid, all built around the female nude, and all designed as a set with internal rhymes: Danae's front view against Venus and Adonis's back view (painting rivalling sculpture); the two Diana pictures sharing one continuous landscape and stream, with light falling from opposite sides so they hang between two windows. Handling loosens decisively: thin paint in the skies, revisions during painting (infrared shows Actaeon moved; Perseus and Andromeda shows an unusually large number of changes), the workshop producing full-size variants (the Vienna Diana and Callisto, mid-1560s) and endless replicas of Danae and Venus and Adonis traced from studio records. What stays constant: the Ovidian source habit from the Ferrara bacchanals; the reclining nude, now thirty years old as a motif; colour as the carrier of flesh and feeling; the habit of painting in pairs and series. Scale: standardised large canvas, about 2 metres wide -- big enough for life-size nudes, small enough to hang in a prince's rooms. Colour: flesh against deep green landscape and dark sky, coral-red drapery (Perseus), the white bull and red scarf (Europa). Composition: the nude as a diagonal across the lower picture, action entering from the side or above. Figure handling: bodies in mid-motion -- pulling away, twisting, falling -- rather than posed; contours beginning to dissolve in dragged brushstrokes. Brushwork: freer, thinner in places, with pentimenti left visible to technical examination.

Phase 5 -- Late work, 1560s-1576. The famous late style, fully formed from about 1570. The ground goes dark; colour is applied last, in scumbles, often with the fingers, so that greens, blues and reds "only half belong to any of the forms and seem to float on the surface" (Penny on the Marsyas). Forms emerge like wraiths out of circumambient darkness; mass is reduced to a flickering pattern of colour and light (Steer on the Pieta). Vasari, visiting in 1566, records the method: bold strokes and blotches, hardly visible close up, perfect from a distance. Pictures stay in the studio for years and are reworked in campaigns -- the Death of Actaeon was begun in 1559 and still unfinished in 1576; the Pieta was delivered, reclaimed, enlarged with five extra strips of canvas, and still unfinished at his death. Whether these pictures are finished is a live scholarly argument: the Marsyas carries a partial signature, the Death of Actaeon does not. Subjects turn tragic and inward: flaying, a stag torn by hounds, a crucifixion, a Pieta for his own tomb in which he paints himself kneeling as Jerome and puts a little ex voto of himself and his dead son in the corner. The Munich Crowning with Thorns (1570) repaints the clear, coloured 1542 version as darkness lit by a torch. What stays constant: colour, still, is the substance -- even more so, since line has nearly vanished. The scale stays monumental (Pieta 389 x 351 cm; Marsyas 212 x 207 cm). The classical sources are still there (Ovid for Marsyas and Actaeon, Giulio Romano's drawing behind the Marsyas composition). The reclining nude never leaves, but the mood has moved from seduction to mortality. Scale: still monumental for the great late pictures, but the pictures are now studio objects kept and reworked, not delivered commissions. Colour: smoky, dark, greenish-gold overall emanation broken by blood-red splotches and strong white highlights on knives, buckets and flesh. Composition: figures massed in a shallow dark field, V-shapes and triangles (Marsyas's legs, the Pieta's triangle of mourners) holding the picture together where contour no longer does. Figure handling: bodies half-dissolved, faces emerging from shadow, the old man (Midas, Jerome) as the still centre. Brushwork: the loosest of the career -- fingers, scumbles, dragged dry brush, passages of smoky underpainting left showing, detailed finish reserved for a diadem or a blade.

What changed, what stayed constant: what changed most is the surface and the speed of the hand. Titian begins in smooth, blended Giorgionesque tonal painting where the brush disappears, and ends sixty years later in pictures built from scumbles, finger-work and dragged dry brush where the paint itself is the subject and the forms are half-lost in darkness. Scale jumps early -- the Assunta of 1516-18 is already 690 cm -- and never really comes back down; what changes is not size but density, from crowded clear narrative to a few figures in a dark field. Composition moves from centred and landscape-framed, to diagonal and off-centre, to paired pendants with shared landscapes, to late triangles and V-shapes holding a dissolving image together. The patron moves too: Venetian churches, then Ferrarese dukes, then the emperor and the king of Spain, then finally himself -- the Pieta was painted for his own grave. What never changes is the doctrine that colour makes form rather than decorating it; the oil medium worked wet, glazed and reworked over years; the flesh, especially the female nude, as the recurring test of the painter; and the habit of returning to a composition -- Danae six times, Crowning with Thorns twice, Diana twice over -- and remaking it rather than repeating it. The late pictures look like a different painter only until you see that the Assunta's apostles were already "created from patches of light and shade"; the late style is the early principle carried to its limit, with the drawing finally surrendered to the paint.

## Aesthetic Logic

The logic is that the picture is built to be seen from the distance it was made for, and colour is the only structure it needs. In the Assunta the apostles are created from patches of light and shade, not from drawn bodies, and the three zones of the picture are divided by light alone; the figures are over life-size so they read from the far nave, and God the Father high up is unblended highlights in stiff impasto. In the Pesaro Madonna the Virgin is pushed to the top of a diagonal stair and the columns are cropped by the frame, because the picture is designed to be seen at an angle by people walking up the nave -- the composition is a function of the walker's path, not of symmetry. The poesie are the same logic in a prince's room: two metres wide, life-size nudes, light falling from opposite sides so the pair hangs between two windows, Danae's front view answering Venus and Adonis's back view so that painting can rival sculpture. The finish is calibrated to the viewing distance in every case -- five wet-in-wet strokes of lead white for a feather seen from twenty feet below, fur and teeth in a few quick strokes for a bacchanal in a small bright room. The workshop is the other half of the logic: a picture is Titian's at the design and at the retouching, and the middle can be delegated, because the demand was industrial -- six poesie for one king, Danae six times, Ecce Homo and Venus and Adonis in version after version, the same composition remade rather than repeated. The late style is not a break but the doctrine carried to its limit: the ground goes dark, the colour is applied last in scumbles, often with the fingers, and the forms emerge like wraiths out of the dark, because by 1570 there is nothing left to prove about drawing and everything left to prove about paint. And the price of the system is written into the end of the story: the studio closed soon after his death, Orazio dead of plague days after his father, the contents sold off in 1581, the Pieta reclaimed by the friars and finished by another hand -- the enterprise was the man, and it did not survive him. The pictures were made for churches and princes' rooms, not for galleries, and they still want to be seen that way: from the church door first, then from the altar steps, the movement legible from a distance and the faces distinct only up close.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Titian was the greatest painter of the Venetian school. Sources: Britannica, lead ("the greatest Italian Renaissance painter of the Venetian school"); NG London ("Titian was the greatest painter of 16th-century Venice"); EN Wikipedia ("The most important artist of Renaissance Venetian painting"). Evidence: leg 1.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] Titian was the first painter to have a mainly international clientele. Source: NG London, verbatim: "the first painter to have a mainly international clientele." Corroborated by the patron list (Ferrara, Mantua, Urbino, the Farnese, Charles V, Philip II) in NGA Humfrey and the Prado. Evidence: leg 1.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] His reputation never declined after his lifetime. Source: Britannica: "He was recognized early in his own lifetime as a supremely talented painter, and his reputation has in the intervening centuries never suffered a decline." In 1590 Giovanni Paolo Lomazzo declared him "the sun amidst small stars not only among the Italians but all the painters of the world" (Britannica; EN Wikipedia). Evidence: leg 1.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] The birth date is unknowable from documents, and Titian's own statements about his age are unreliable. Sources: EN Wikipedia ("The exact date of Titian's birth is uncertain"); Technical Bulletin 34 ("given Titian's vagueness and tendency to exaggerate his age in later years, neither is necessarily accurate"); Catholic Encyclopedia via search ("there are good reasons for believing that he made himself out to be older than he was"). Modern consensus 1488/90. Evidence: leg 1.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] For sixty years he was the undisputed master of Venetian painting, from Bellini's death in 1516 to his own death in 1576. Sources: EN Wikipedia ("For sixty years he was the undisputed master of Venetian painting"); NGA Humfrey ("Titian dominated painting in Venice from the death in 1516 of his probable master, Giovanni Bellini, until his own death 60 years later"). Evidence: leg 1.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] The poesie for Philip II are among his greatest works, and they were a named, deliberate series: six large mythologies delivered 1553-1562, plus the unfinished Death of Actaeon. Sources: EN Wikipedia ("which scholars regard as among his greatest works"); NG London (six delivered pictures, 1551-1562); thehistoryofart.org (same six). Evidence: leg 1.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] Titian died of plague on Friday 27 August 1576 (date -d) and was buried in the Frari; his last painting, the Pieta, was made for his own tomb there and was finished by Palma il Giovane. Sources: EN Wikipedia (death, burial, Pieta history); NG London (burial after dying of the plague on 27 August 1576). Evidence: leg 1.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] Tintoretto defined his own ambition against Titian: "il disegno di Michelangelo e il colorito di Tiziano" (Michelangelo's design and Titian's colour), written on the walls of his rooms after leaving Titian's studio. Source: EN Wikipedia, citing Ridolfi's Life of Tintoretto (1642). Evidence: leg 1.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] Virtually all the leading painters of the 17th century were deeply indebted to Titian's example, "most especially Rubens, Sir Anthony van Dyck, and Diego Velazquez, but also some of those working in a different tradition, such as Nicolas Poussin and Rembrandt van Rijn". Source: NGA Humfrey, verbatim in substance. Britannica adds that Rubens and Poussin "paid him the compliment of imitation". Evidence: leg 1.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] His surviving oeuvre is about 280-300 works, but workshop output roughly doubles the count, and attribution of the early works remains contested. Sources: EN Wikipedia (Humfrey: 300 catalogued, 20 lost, 280 surviving; "if all the paintings issued from the artist's workshop had been included, that number might have been doubled"); NGA Humfrey (autograph vs nonautograph line often impossible to draw). Evidence: leg 1.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] The Assumption of the Virgin (1516-18, 690 x 360 cm, oil on panel, Frari high altar, still in situ) was the breakthrough: three zones divided by light only, figures over life-size so they read from the far nave, installed Sunday 19 May 1518, creating "a revolutionary watershed in Venetian altarpiece design" (NG London). Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] The Pesaro Madonna (1519-26, 488 x 269 cm per Wikipedia, 478 x 266.5 cm per Save Venice) broke the centred sacra conversazione: the Virgin and Child pushed high and far to the right on a diagonal staircase, two huge cropped columns rising through the centre, donors kneeling motionless at left, designed to be seen at an angle by people walking up the nave. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] The poesie were designed as a set with internal rhymes: Danae's front view against Venus and Adonis's back view, so painting could rival sculpture; the two Diana pictures sharing one continuous landscape and stream, with light falling from the left in Diana and Actaeon and from the right in Diana and Callisto, so the pair was designed to hang between two windows. Evidence: leg 2.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] Charles V at Muhlberg (April-September 1548, 335 x 283 cm, Prado) became the prototype of equestrian portraiture: the emperor rides a black horse out of a dark wood into open landscape, lance diagonal, armour and harness recorded from the real battle equipment, no battle, no enemy -- contained power and a weary, resolute face; Titian refused Aretino's proposed allegorical additions and kept the emperor alone. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] The late style, fully formed from about 1570, works on a dark ground with colour applied last in scumbles, often with the fingers; forms emerge like wraiths out of circumambient darkness, and mass is reduced to a flickering pattern of colour and light. The Munich Crowning with Thorns (1570) repaints the clear, coloured 1542 version as darkness lit by a torch. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P16. [FROM RECORD] The Pieta (1575-76) was painted for Titian's own tomb in the Frari, reclaimed from the friars by papal decree 1 March 1575, enlarged in the studio with five extra canvas strips to fit Pieve di Cadore, and never installed in either church in his lifetime; Palma il Giovane acquired it around 1581 and retouched only parts of it, despite his inscription claiming "What Titian left unfinished, Palma reverently completed". Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P17. [FROM RECORD] The workshop produced variants and replicas systematically, "an unprecedented practice"; Titian's hand is at the design and the retouching, and the middle was delegated. Unlike the workshops of Tintoretto, Veronese and Bassano, Titian's studio closed quite soon after his death: Orazio died of the plague days or weeks after his father, the studio was ransacked, and the building and remaining contents were sold off in 1581 by Pomponio. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
- P18. [FROM RECORD] The Death of St Peter Martyr (1528-29, delivered 27 April 1530, destroyed by fire 16 August 1867 at Santi Giovanni e Paolo) was Vasari's most complete work and a proto-Baroque picture fusing extreme violence with a landscape that presses into the scene; it is known only from copies, the Martino Rota engraving (c.1560) and two blurry photographs of fragments. Evidence: leg 2.
- P19. [FROM RECORD] The Venus of Urbino (begun 1532 or 1534, completed c.1534, sold 1538, 119 x 165 cm, Uffizi) moved Giorgione's sleeping Venus indoors and made it explicitly erotic: a nude woman reclining on a bed, meeting the viewer's gaze, her flesh built from 10-15 translucent glazes over subtle tonal gradation, her pose set against a grid of rectilinear forms. Evidence: leg 2.
- P20. [INFERRED] The late pictures are the early principle carried to its limit, not a break with it: the Assunta's apostles were already "created from patches of light and shade", so the late dissolution of form into scumbled colour is the colour-first doctrine with the drawing finally surrendered. Inferred and labelled as such: no source in the legs states the continuity in exactly these words, though the Assunta description and the late-style accounts are both documented. Evidence: legs 1 and 2.
