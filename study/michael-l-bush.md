## M1. The Fiscal Grievance as the Hidden Cause
Take a motive everyone filed under the wrong heading and re-read the same documents until it becomes the cause. Bush's Albion 1990 article shows the tax complaints were "firmly declared in the rebels' bills, petitions, and songs" yet dismissed since the Dodds sisters (1915) because historians wanted a religious story: the Dodds filed first fruits under religion, fiscal feudalism under law, and the quindime under economics, so no one saw the fiscal case as one case. The tax complaints were "a pervasive force affecting the whole range of society" that "sustained the rebel view that the government had to be checked not only to prevent the subversion of the Church but also the decay of the commonwealth".
Takeaway: the cause was in the documents all along, misfiled.

## M2. The Quindime, or the Word That Unlocks the Case
One misunderstood technical term, correctly glossed, re-opens a whole grievance. The rebels' word "quindime" was misread by the Dodds as a protest about the method of assessment; Bush identifies it as the fifteenth and tenth itself, the direct tax the commons were refusing. The mechanism is philology as forensic tool: get one word right and the rebels' fiscal complaint stops being a vague grumble and becomes a dated, datable demand about a specific levy.
Takeaway: philology as forensic tool.

## M3. Peacetime Tax as Constitutional Breach
Novelty, not weight, makes a tax intolerable. The 1534 subsidy was granted with no emergency cited -- the first peacetime direct tax -- raising "the fearful possibility that... such taxes would... come to be regarded as a source of ordinary revenue". The subsidy raised only 45,000 pounds in two years, against 136,000 pounds in 1523, yet it was the small, novel tax that ignited the rising, because it set the precedent that the king could tax in peacetime. The mechanism is the precedent: people revolt against what a tax makes normal, not against what it costs.
Takeaway: people revolt against precedent, not price.

## M4. Rumour as Accelerant
A posted bill travels faster than a statute and is believed first. Seditious bills "nailed to church doors and trees over much of the north" in late September and early October 1536 threatened taxes on baptisms, food, cattle, ploughs and gold -- none of them real. Collinson's gloss is that "the Government in London was itself a victim of the rumour mill". The commons were, in Collinson's report of Hoyle, "avid devourers of news and gossip", and the rumoured taxes show what they feared the government would do next. The mechanism is the rumour as a political fact: it does the work of the real tax before any tax is levied.
Takeaway: the rumoured tax does the work of the real one.

## M5. The Fourfold Complaint Archive
Sort a chaotic archive into functional genres before judging it. The rebels' documentation falls into four types: (1) circulars to drum up support -- muster proclamations, manifestos, rumour-mongering bills, justificatory prophecies, rousing rhymes; (2) oaths and orders to bind and direct support; (3) petitions to declare grievances and propose remedy; (4) personal accounts by participants and eye-witnesses. Bush's chapter 1 of The Pilgrims' Complaint works through the archive in this order, and the appendix reprints more than two dozen key declarations. The mechanism is the filing system: once the papers are sorted by what they were for, the rebels stop looking like a mob with papers and start looking like a movement with a bureaucracy.
Takeaway: the rebels left a paper trail with a filing system.

## M6. The Petition as Contagion
A text that is read aloud in a market square becomes a muster. The Horncastle articles were "strewed in the market towns" of Yorkshire, read aloud at Beverley on Sunday 8 October 1536 after the common bell was rung, beacons were fired, and musters followed the next day. The petition is not a letter, it is a fuse: the same words that ask the king for remedy also summon the next village. Bush's chapter 1 dates the spread day by day -- 2 October at Louth, 3 October the letter to the king, 4 October the Horncastle articles, 8 October Beverley, 10 October the Lincolnshire petition south -- so the contagion has a timetable, not just a mood.
Takeaway: the petition is not a letter, it is a fuse.

## M7. The Oath as Binding Machine
An oath converts a crowd into an army by making defection perjury. Rebels swore the gentry as well as themselves; the Beverley commons swore to be "true to God and our prince" and "true and faithful to you and other the commons of the realm". Collinson: "They were very busy in riding around the country 'swearing' their social betters." The mechanism is the binding: the oath is the institution the rising builds for itself, the act that turns a crowd into a company and makes the gentlemen's later betrayal a breach of faith rather than a change of mind. Hoyle leaves the mechanism unexplained -- "we need an anthropologist or two and a time machine" -- and the gap itself marks its importance.
Takeaway: the binding is the organisation.

## M8. The Nine Hosts: Rebellion as Army, Not Mob
Count the hosts, map them, and the "mob" becomes a military organisation with captains, musters and wapentake divisions. The Pilgrimage of Grace (1996) studies "nine separate hosts which 'dwarfed' the army royal", "nine regional armies, perhaps fifty thousand armed men" (Collinson), built on pre-existing arms and armour and established organisational structures, with muster rolls as evidence for rebel military assets. Bush's own preface gives "a formidable uprising of 60,000 northerners"; the numbers differ between sources because they count different things, see C10. The mechanism is the count: once the rebels are nine armies with captains, the rising has to be explained as a military campaign, not a riot.
Takeaway: the rebels outnumbered the crown and knew it.

## M9. Captain Poverty and the Alias Captains
A pseudonym lets a movement have a captain without a head to cut off. A shadowy leadership called itself "Captain Poverty"; the Cumberlanders chose "four captains... called charity, faith, poverty and pity"; the Kendal and Dent muster called on men to follow "Master Poverty, the conductor, protector and maintainer of the whole commonalty". Bush's 1992 conclusion is that "the Captain Poverty movement should not be regarded as basically different from the rest of the Pilgrimage". The mechanism is the alias as command structure: the name is a programme -- poverty, charity, faith, pity -- and a captain who is a word cannot be arrested.
Takeaway: anonymity is a command structure.

## M10. Badges, Banners and the Logo of Revolt
A shared emblem makes scattered risings one movement and makes the wearer legible to the next village. Rebels wore the badge of the Five Wounds; Aske "invented the logo 'Pilgrimage of Grace'"; Durham men marched behind the Banner of St Cuthbert. The mechanism is the logo as membership card: the badge says which side you are on before you speak, and the name turns a set of regional risings into a single pilgrimage, giving the commons a banner to march under and the king an enemy with a face.
Takeaway: the badge is the membership card.

## M11. The Song as Propaganda
Rhyme carries a political theology that a petition cannot; a song is memorised, a petition is filed. Pickering's Song, 21 stanzas in the appendix to The Pilgrims' Complaint, calls northerners "faithfull people of the boreal region", brands southerners "southern Turks", and turns Cromwell into Haman from Esther -- "The art of a shearman it was his beginning". The song accuses the south of "spoiling Christ's church" and "perverting our law", and calls the commons to "mell / To make redress" under Aske. The mechanism is the memorable form: the song travels where the petition cannot go, because it does not need to be read.
Takeaway: the movement sang its manifesto.

## M12. The Pardon as Trap
A pardon that depends on the rebels staying quiet is a weapon with a timer. The December 1536 pardon was "issued over the Great Seal and applied to everyone without exception, the only occasion in Henry VIII's reign when traitors were so indulged" (Collinson); then the January-February 1537 revolts "allowed the government to escape the fulfilment of its December pledges" (Bush, preface to The Pilgrims' Complaint). The Defeat of the Pilgrimage of Grace (1999, with Bownes) studies those postpardon revolts, December 1536 to March 1537, and their effect: the settlement was the second act of the defeat, because the pardon bought the government the time to break its promises.
Takeaway: the settlement was the second act of the defeat.

## M13. The Polity Defended: Rights, Not Just Religion
Read a religious rising as a constitutional argument and the rebels become legal thinkers. "A striking feature of the pilgrimage of grace was its concern for lost or threatened rights and liberties" -- constitutional concerns, the law, the manorial system, the society of orders, the body politic, and the pilgrims' own concept of tyranny. The mechanism is the constitutional reading: the commons had a theory of the state, couched in the society of orders, and their programme of reform was "extremely challenging to the king, lord and priest, so much so that the three of them eventually closed rank to bring the uprising to an end" (Bush's final sentence, as Hoyle quotes it). Hoyle's counter is that the petitions are lists produced after six to eight weeks of mobilization, not a spontaneous statement of the commons' mind; the disagreement is the live dispute, see C11.
Takeaway: the commons had a theory of the state.

## M14. North and South as a Moral Geography
Regional grievance stated as drainage: wealth flowing south, leaving the north to ally with the Scots or rebel. "The northern people in time long past Hath little been regarded of the austral nation"; the transfer of clerical wealth "to laymen in the south" (Bowman); Aske's Pontefract account: within years "there should be no money nor treasure in those parts" as abbey profits, tenths and first fruits "went out of those parts". The mechanism is the map as motive: the north is not just far from London, it is being emptied by London, and the rising is the north refusing to be drained.
Takeaway: the map is the motive.

## M15. Agrarian Conflict: Enclosure, Rent, Entry Fines
The same rising carries different material grievances in each valley; the movement is a federation of local angers. "The further to the north-west the disturbances spread, the greater the resentment against landlords and their renting and leasing policies"; Richmondshire and Westmorland grievances "had to do with rents and entry fines". The mechanism is the local anger inside the national cause: the commons rose for faith and commonwealth, but in each valley the commonwealth meant the rent, the fine, and the enclosure, and the rising's strength was that it never asked a valley to forget its own grievance.
Takeaway: one rebellion, many rent disputes.

## M16. The Commons on Their Own Terms
Change the archive and the actors change. Bush reads "popular sources such as prophetic rhymes; often dubious sources, such as eyewitness accounts; and also controversial sources, such as the Pilgrim's Petitions, whose veracity... sometimes has been too quickly dismissed" -- instead of government statements and sermons. The mechanism is the source switch: read the rebels' own texts and the rebels stop being a mob led by gentlemen and become authors, with a programme, a vocabulary, and a theory of the state. Hoyle's review calls the book "indispensable" for exactly this documentary appendix, even as he calls the argument "impossible".
Takeaway: who you read decides who the rebels are.

## M17. The Crusade Frame: Pilgrimage as Holy War
Borrow the oldest licensed form of armed movement -- the pilgrimage -- and a rebellion becomes a crusade. "Assuming the form of a military pilgrimage (that is, a crusade), it planned a march on London to liberate Christ's faith from the coils of heresy." The mechanism is the name as alibi: the pilgrim is allowed to march, to carry arms, to swear an oath, and to claim God's protection, so the rising takes the one form of armed movement the church had already blessed, and the king has to call a crusade a rebellion.
Takeaway: the name is the alibi.

## M18. The Long Tradition, 1381-1549
Treat rebellions as a tradition with a memory, not as isolated explosions. Bush places 1536 in a 168-year sequence from the Peasants' Revolt to Kett, in "The risings of the commons, 1381-1549": the commons remembered how to rise, and each rising quoted the last one -- its bills, its oaths, its captains, its songs. The mechanism is the tradition: a rising that knows it is the latest in a line can borrow the last rising's forms wholesale, and the government facing it is facing 1381 as well as 1536.
Takeaway: the commons remembered how to rise.

## M19. The Casualty Count as Forensic History
Count the bodies name by name and the official story has to answer to the list. The Casualties of Peterloo (2005) is built on eight surviving casualty lists: 654 recorded injured, at least 168 of them women, and 18 dead -- 11 on St Peter's Field, the rest later of wounds, plus 3 "disputable" fatalities and 2 shot on the evening. Bush's 18 are a different 18 from Robert Poole's, both argued from the same records; the book is called "the standard text on the numbers". The mechanism is arithmetic as justice: the list is the argument, and every name on it is a fact the official account cannot smooth away.
Takeaway: arithmetic is a form of justice.

## M20. The Single Principle: Paine and Heredity
Find the one load-bearing idea in a thinker's work and rebuild the whole system from it. Thomas Paine and the Polity of the Blood (2023) reduces Paine to one core principle -- "the rejection of hereditary power" -- and derives the rest of his thought from it, in the manner of Jonathan Israel's two Enlightenments. The mechanism is the reduction: one principle, honestly chosen, carries a whole intellectual biography, and everything else in Paine -- the republicanism, the deism, the attack on monarchy -- follows from the refusal of power by blood.
Takeaway: one principle, honestly chosen, carries a whole intellectual biography.

## M21. Demythologising by Detail
Kill a legend not with a counter-legend but with 180 pages of detail. The Government Policy of Protector Somerset (1975) demolishes the Pollard and Jordan "good duke": Somerset "never countenanced rebellion, nor recommended leniency to rebels, except as a tactic and then only a temporary one"; his policy was driven by war with Scotland and garrisoning, "directed not by ideals, but by idees fixes". The mechanism is accumulation as argument: close reading of elite correspondence, notably Paget's letters, re-characterises a single statesman so thoroughly that the legend has nowhere left to stand.
Takeaway: accumulation is an argument.

## M22. The Bones as Afterlife
Follow a physical object after the ideas have died and the object tells the story of the idea's reception. The appendix to Thomas Paine and the Polity of the Blood traces Paine's exhumed remains from Cobbett's 1819 disinterment through their wanderings, with Bush himself entering the text "for the first time". The mechanism is the object as reception history: the bones were stolen, displayed, lost, and fought over, and each move marks a moment in Paine's reputation -- the afterlife of an idea has a paper trail too, and it can be walked.
Takeaway: the afterlife of an idea has a paper trail too.

## M23. The Society of Orders as Frame
Hold one frame across a whole career and let the subjects change inside it. From the Somerset book to the Pilgrimage trilogy to the Carlile books, Bush's frame is the society of orders and the manorial system as responsive institutions "within the two basic social institutions of the time" in which "commons and lords normally interrelated". The mechanism is the frame that does not move: when Shagan's conclusions about 1549 are answered, they are answered inside the frame -- "a profound misunderstanding of the responsive way commons and lords normally interrelated" -- and when the Pilgrimage is explained, it is explained as the orders defending themselves. The frame is what makes four phases one career.
Takeaway: one frame, honestly held, carries forty years of subjects.

## M24. The Comparative Typology
Widen the scale until the single case becomes a type. The European Nobility (1983, 1988) and The English Aristocracy (1984) are comparative syntheses built on typologies -- privilege, wealth, tenure -- and "An Anatomy of Nobility" (1992) turns the type into a method: define the noble by what privilege he holds, not by what country he lives in. The mechanism is the type: comparison across countries and centuries lets a historian say what is English about the English aristocracy and what is merely noble, and the same habit later lets Bush compare servitude across four continents.
Takeaway: make the type first and the comparison writes itself.

## M25. The Servile Rights Argument
Argue against the "neo-abolitionist" view that the servile were powerless victims by showing they "ingeniously succeeded in acquiring rights and liberties". Servitude in Modern Times (2000) compares slavery, serfdom, debt bondage, indentured service and convict labour since 1500, and finds the same move everywhere: the unfree used the law, the market, and custom to acquire rights the system never meant to give them. The mechanism is the rights claim from below: the servant is not a victim to be pitied but an actor who made property out of servitude, the same move as the tenant who made property out of custom.
Takeaway: even the unfree made rights for themselves.

## M26. The Women Counted Twice
Count the same list twice -- once for the dead, once for the women -- and a second story appears. "The Women at Peterloo" (2004) shows working-class women stepped out "from impatience at the men's slow progress"; women were under 12 percent of the crowd but nearly 3 times the injury risk, at least 168 of the 654 injured, and the yeomanry "went for the women", "not just in the crush". The mechanism is the second count: the casualty list that proves the massacre also proves who the massacre was aimed at, once it is cut by sex.
Takeaway: count the list twice and the target appears.

## M27. The Antiquarian Accident
Let the archive find the historian. At an antiquarian book fair Bush bought a bound run of Carlile's The Republican and found the 'What is Love?' essay; the find became What Is Love? Richard Carlile's Philosophy of Sex (1998), and the Carlile books that followed. He collected first editions -- Paine's The Age of Reason, an early edition of Carlile's Every Woman's Book, a leather-bound first edition of Hobbes's Leviathan -- and the collection did the choosing: the books he owned became the books he wrote. The mechanism is the accident as method: a historian who buys books by hand ends up writing the history of the books he could hold.
Takeaway: the book fair is a research method.

## M28. The Debate Reply
Answer in the journal where you were attacked, in the same form, and make the reply a new argument. "Protector Somerset and the 1549 Rebellions: A Post-Revision Questioned" (English Historical Review, 2000) answers Ethan Shagan's 1999 conclusions alongside G. W. Bernard, with Shagan's rejoinder following in the same issue. The mechanism is the rejoinder as a genre: the debate piece does not defend the old book, it advances a new claim -- that commons and lords interrelated responsively inside the manorial system and the society of orders -- and the exchange itself becomes part of the evidence.
Takeaway: the reply is the next chapter.

## M29. The Trilogy in Three Movements
Tell one rising three times, each time from a different distance. The Pilgrimage of Grace (1996) studies the rebel armies as an organisation; The Defeat (1999, with Bownes) studies the postpardon revolts and their effect; The Pilgrims' Complaint (2009) studies popular thought -- what the rebels meant. The arc runs from counting taxes, to counting armies, to listening to voices, and the Choice review's label, "Bush's Pilgrimage trilogy", names the structure. The mechanism is the three-movement form: each book answers the question the last one raised, and the trilogy is the argument that a rising is an army, a defeat, and a mind, in that order.
Takeaway: one rising, three distances, one argument.

## M30. The Indispensable Disaster
Let the appendix outlive the argument. Hoyle's review of The Pilgrims' Complaint is the sharpest published statement of the Bush-Hoyle disagreement: "As a book though, it is something of a disaster... It is both indispensable and impossible." "Bush does not do shades of certainty." Yet the same review concedes the book's documentary appendix -- more than two dozen key declarations of pilgrim complaint and intent -- is the authoritative collection, "a unique insight into how the people of the north, on the eve of the Reformation, thought about religion, social relations and politics". The mechanism is the apparatus as the legacy: the argument can be called a disaster and the book still be indispensable, because the documents it prints are the field's working set.
Takeaway: print the documents and the book cannot be dismissed.

## Evolution: four phases, and the afterlife so far
Phase 1 -- High politics and the textbook years (1967-1975). First book a broad European survey textbook (Renaissance, Reformation and the Outer World, 1967), written in his late twenties; first article on Tudor dynastic politics (1970); first monograph a revisionist demolition job in high politics: Somerset as soldier, not idealist (1974 article, 1975 book). Method: close reading of elite correspondence, argument by re-characterisation of a single statesman. Audience: academic Tudor specialists.
Phase 2 -- Comparative social structure (1983-1992). A sharp widening of scale: from one English protector to the European nobility as a whole (Noble Privilege 1983; English Aristocracy 1984; Rich Noble, Poor Noble 1988), then to orders and classes across Europe since 1500 (edited volume 1992) and legal bondage worldwide (Serfdom and Slavery, ed. 1996; Servitude in Modern Times, 2000). Method becomes comparative-synthetic: typologies (privilege, wealth, tenure), case comparison across countries and centuries. The common thread with Phase 1 is the society of orders -- hierarchy, privilege, and the legal frame -- now treated as a European system rather than an English episode.
Phase 3 -- The Pilgrimage, in three movements (1990-2009). Returns to Tudor England but from below. First movement is fiscal: five articles 1990-1993 proving tax grievances were central to the 1536 rebellions. Second movement is military-political: The Pilgrimage of Grace (1996) reconstructs the rebel armies, and The Defeat (1999, with Bownes) covers the postpardon revolts. Third movement is mental: "The Tudor polity" (2007) and The Pilgrims' Complaint (2009) read the rebels' own texts to recover how the commons themselves thought about state, society and religion. The arc within the phase: from counting taxes, to counting armies, to listening to voices.
Phase 4 -- The radical underground (1998-2024). Begins by accident: at an antiquarian book fair Bush bought a bound run of Carlile's The Republican and found the 'What is Love?' essay. What Is Love? (1998) recovers Carlile as a philosopher of sex; The Casualties of Peterloo (2005) and "The Women at Peterloo" (2004) apply the same forensic counting to the 1819 massacre; The Friends and Following of Richard Carlile (2016) maps the Paineite network across the 1820s; Thomas Paine and the Polity of the Blood (2023) makes Paine a systematic thinker and ends, unusually for Bush, in the first person, hunting Paine's bones. His last published piece was a review of Desmond's Reign of the Beast, The Freethinker, 25 October 2024.
Afterlife so far: Bush died on Sunday 18 May 2025, aged 87, about eighteen months before this study. The obituary in The Freethinker (27 June 2025, by Bob Forder) frames the career as a single fascination: "He once told me that he was fascinated by 'rebellion'." The Pilgrimage books remain the fiscal and organisational spine of the subject -- Hoyle's 2001 book builds on them and Collinson's verdict stands -- and the Peterloo casualty count remains "the standard text on the numbers". No posthumous publication has appeared; the late books (Carlile, Paine) are still being reviewed as the work of a historian who left the Tudor field to write about the radicals he collected.

## Aesthetic logic
Bush's aesthetic is the list that becomes an argument. Every phase of the career builds the same object: an exhaustive, dated, named list -- the tax complaints in the rebels' bills, the nine hosts with their musters, the four types of complaint documents, the 654 Peterloo casualties, the 18 dead, the Paineite network across the 1820s -- and then reads the list until it yields a thesis. The list is never decoration; it is the evidence in its most honest form, because a list can be checked name by name and a thesis cannot. The second habit is the source switch: read the rebels' own papers instead of the government's, read the casualty lists instead of the official report, read Carlile's own print instead of the history of radicalism, and the actors change. The third habit is the single frame held still: the society of orders and the manorial system, inside which commons and lords interrelate responsively, so that a Tudor rising and a Peterloo massacre and a Paineite print network are the same subject at three removes -- ordinary people making a politics out of what they have. The tone follows: plain, declarative, certain to a fault -- Hoyle's "Bush does not do shades of certainty" is both the criticism and the style. The weakness is the mirror of the strength: certainty without shading leaves the argument exposed where the documents are thin, and the books that are indispensable for their appendices are the ones whose arguments are most attacked. The generative lesson is the list as a visual system: a field of small named units, each one countable, that gathers into a shape -- a host, a crowd, a network -- and the shape is the argument.
