# Henri Regnault: Study Notes

**Site:** Henri Regnault (1843-1871), French painter of the academic line who broke it from inside, dead at 27 in the Franco-Prussian War -- born Tuesday 31 October 1843 in Paris, full name Alexandre Georges Henri Regnault, the son of the chemist and physicist Henri Victor Regnault (1810-1878), director of the Sevres porcelain manufactory and professor at the College de France, and raised in a cultivated Second Empire household where science and art sat at the same table; entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in 1860, aged 17, and passed successively through the studios of Antoine Montfort, Louis Lamothe and Alexandre Cabanel, the last his principal master; beaten for the Prix de Rome in 1863 by Joseph Layraud and Xavier Monchablon, then won it in 1866 with Thetis Bringing the Arms Forged by Vulcan to Achilles, painted in twelve days, a four-year fellowship at the Villa Medici that he was probably the first winner to be given dispensation to spend away from Rome, travelling instead to Spain in late 1868 and to Morocco, landing in Tangier in December 1869 with his friend Georges Clairin and planning to settle there permanently; sent Automedon with the Horses of Achilles (1868, 315 by 329 cm, now Museum of Fine Arts, Boston) as his first-year envoi, breaking the academie rule that required a single nude figure study by sending a vast canvas with two life-size horses; painted the equestrian Portrait of General Prim in Spain, saw the general pass at the head of his troops and kept the passing image; painted Salome (1870, 160 by 102.9 cm, now Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York) and Summary Execution under the Moorish Kings of Granada (1870, 305 by 146 cm, now Musee d'Orsay), both shown at the Salon of 1870, Salome taking a gold medal and setting off the Paris "yellow fever" craze of spring 1870; returned to Paris when the Franco-Prussian War began, gave up the draft exemption his Prix de Rome carried, and volunteered as a foot soldier in the National Guard, 69th infantry battalion, 4th company, sewing a card into his clothing that read "Henri Regnault, painter, son of M. Victor Regnault, of the Institute"; killed on Thursday 19 January 1871 at the Second Battle of Buzenval, a Prussian bullet striking his left temple, his reported last words after the retreat order, "I still have a few cartridges left -- I am going to fire them"; his body not recovered for six days, tracked down among the dead by Clairin, a death mask made by Louis-Ernest Barrias showing the bullet hole and a broken nose from the fall, the body lying in state two days at the Ecole de medecine before a funeral described as a state ritual; Camille Saint-Saens dedicated his Marche heroique (1871) to his memory, Carolus-Duran, who fought in the same battle, painted Henri Regnault Dead on the Battlefield (1871), and Meissonier set him as the slumped soldier in The Siege of Paris in 1870 (1884); an obelisk crowned by his bust was unveiled at Buzenval in 1872, a retrospective at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts the same year was wildly successful, and a lavish memorial at the Ecole was dedicated in 1876, with Rome Prize winners customarily leaving a crown wrapped in black crepe before leaving for Italy; his reputation collapsed in France after the Exposition Universelle of 1878, his Execution going into Louvre storage by the mid-1880s, his legend co-opted in the 1880s and 1890s by Paul Deroulede's revanchist League of Patriots, and the culture of heroic exemplarity that sustained him swept away by the Great War's new rituals of remembrance; Salome was sold out of France in 1912 amid public outcry and reached the Met in 1916 as the gift of George F. Baker; the first monograph since 1913 is Marc Gotlieb's The Deaths of Henri Regnault (University of Chicago Press, 2016), the first book in English on him. The oeuvre is small and the count is reported, not independently verified: Taylor Graham counts 65 oil paintings, 45 watercolors and nearly 200 sketches, about 310 works in all; WikiArt catalogues 28 artworks; the named dated works assembled across the legs number about twenty titles from 1864 to 1870, the output concentrating in the last eighteen months, with a colossal canvas on the Muslim conquest of Spain, projected at 33 feet wide, planned as his last envoi and never painted. ## M1. Monochrome Symphony
One hue floods everything -- figure, dress, ground -- until the painting is a single chord, and the colour itself becomes the subject. Salome is layers of luminous yellow, a yellow dress on a gold ground, painted without shadow, described as a "symphony in yellow major" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Gotlieb via Hyperallergic) and as "visually dazzling as an electric flash". Regnault first painted the model, the Roman girl Maria Latini, as an African woman, then enlarged the canvas at the bottom and right and transformed her into the biblical Salome, so the yellow is not a costume choice but the whole picture reorganised around one tone. Paris caught the infection in spring 1870: ladies wore golden gowns, collectors bought yellow porcelain, caricaturists lampooned the tousle-haired woman -- "Paris had yellow fever. Not the disease, but the color" (Hyperallergic). The watchable behaviour is a scene that starts in full colour and drains toward one hue until only value and edge remain, the single colour doing the work that a whole palette normally does. **Takeaway:** pick one hue and let it eat the picture; the monochrome is the event, not the limitation.

## M2. Complementary Collision
Two opposed hues are forced together so each makes the other vibrate, and the "wrong" colour is what the eye cannot leave. In Summary Execution the victim's green robes make the blood read intensely red, and the severed head is already turning green in a pool of crimson: "the green robes of the victim making his blood seem intensely red" (Eclectic Light), and the head "already turning green, in a pool of crimson blood" (Gotlieb via Hyperallergic). The contrast is not decoration but the mechanism of the horror -- the complementary pair does the violence that the composition only stages. The watchable behaviour is paired colour fields that pulse against each other, green flesh against red stone, the two hues refusing to settle. **Takeaway:** put the opposites touching and let them fight; the vibration is the feeling.

## M3. Blood as Thrown Material
Paint is not brushed on but thrown, poured and splattered, so it behaves like the liquid it depicts. In Summary Execution Regnault literally threw red paint at the canvas -- his studio assistant Clairin reported it, and it is also described as pouring and splattering, after Delacroix's method of dripping red on moistened paper in watercolor -- and the blood flows down the marble steps the way blood flows, because it was put there by gravity, not by drawing. EB1911's verdict is that "the painter had played with the blood of the victim as if he were a jeweller toying with rubies" (VERIFIED VERBATIM), and Gotlieb's that the blood "operates like a hail of rubies because the larger decor functions as both token and echo" (via Hyperallergic). The watchable behaviour is pigment particles arcing, landing and running downhill under gravity; the mark-making is ballistic, not drawn. **Takeaway:** when the subject is a liquid, throw it; the physics of the throw is the picture.

## M4. Low-Angle Monument
The viewpoint is placed below the subject, at the figure's feet, so a life-size figure towers and the viewer is put in the subordinate position. In Summary Execution the low angle puts the viewer at the foot of the marble stairs, at the executioner's feet, and the life-size figure gains an imposing presence it would not have at eye level; the headless body lies at his feet and the blood drips down the steps toward the just-severed head, so the viewer is standing in the path of the blood. The scale is a viewpoint, not a size: the canvas is 305 by 146 cm, but the monumentality comes from where the eye is placed, below the action, looking up at a man calmly wiping his blade. The watchable behaviour is a camera that sits low while the central figure looms and shifts against the sky as it moves; the figure does not grow, the ground sinks. **Takeaway:** drop the viewpoint to the floor and the figure becomes a monument; scale is where you stand.

## M5. The Frozen Aftermath
The violent act is never shown; the painting holds the second after it, when everything is still. In Summary Execution the beheading has already happened: the executioner stands calmly wiping blood off his scimitar while the headless body lies at his feet, and the horror is in the calm, not in the blow. Salome is the same mechanism from the other side: she stands alone, hair ruffled, clothes in disarray, just after dancing for her stepfather Herod Antipas, holding a short sword and a large platter for John the Baptist's head -- after the dance, before the beheading, the whole story suspended in the pause. The critics saw the evasion and named it: Roger Marx noted the lack of sympathy or pity, Gustave Geffroy called the Execution "vulgarly melodramatic", Henry Roujon admired the virtuosity but deplored the gratuitous horror. The watchable behaviour is a system that builds to a peak event and then freezes on the stillness after it; tension held in a calm pose, not in the blow. **Takeaway:** skip the blow and paint the second after; the stillness carries more violence than the act.

## M6. Resistant Animal Energy
Animals know something the human does not, and refuse to move. In Automedon with the Horses of Achilles the charioteer runs toward the spectator holding a horse in each hand, and the horses Xanthos and Balios rear, wrestle the reins and weep, because they foresee Achilles's death: Regnault wrote to Henri Cazalis that the horses are being led to Achilles's final battle and death, and "I wanted to give the painting a foretaste of disaster". He aimed, in his own words (VERIFIED VERBATIM, via Pi Media), "not at the cut of the mane typical of Thessalian horses, but at that which is most noble and most terrifying in a horse, at that which might make the horse historical, the horse that spoke, the horse that foresaw the death of his master Achilles". The setting is storm clouds, a leaden sea and pale lugubrious sunlight on an arid rocky shore, one dark bay rearing "like a great dark spectre silhouetted against the sky". The naturalism was earned bodily: he drew horses at the Menagerie du Jardin des Plantes and cracked his skull learning to ride an unbroken Arab horse, chosen because it had almost killed its owner. Contemporary critic Marius Chaumelin saw the result plainly: "There is not, today, a single painter in the French school who has a work of such brilliance, ardour, and power within him", and Regnault was declared heir to Gericault and Delacroix. The watchable behaviour is two forces in opposed tension -- a handler dragging, animals bracing -- with the system never resolving; flared nostrils, dishevelled manes, froth and tensed muscle as readable signals. **Takeaway:** give the animals the knowledge and let them refuse; the refusal is the drama, not the ride.

## M7. Rule-Breaking Scale
A required small study is delivered as a colossal canvas, and the frame itself becomes the constraint being broken. The first-year envoi from Rome had to be a single nude figure study, an academie; Regnault sent Automedon, a 315 by 329 cm canvas with two life-size horses and a running charioteer, more than ten feet square, the figure secondary to the animals. The breach was deliberate and programmatic: he had already decided the Villa Medici copy regime was not learning -- "equally tired by the rote copies expected by artists at the Villa Medici" (Hyperallergic) -- and the envoi was the place to prove it. The same appetite for scale runs through the career in the other direction: the projected final envoi, a canvas on the Muslim conquest of Spain at 33 feet wide, was never painted, so the largest picture he imagined is the one that exists only as a plan. The watchable behaviour is a figure or element that keeps growing past its allotted frame until the frame itself becomes the thing being broken. **Takeaway:** when the assignment says small, send the biggest thing you can carry; the disobedience is the work.

## M8. Palimpsest Reworking
The canvas is enlarged and repainted until its subject changes, and the finished picture is a stack of decisions. Salome began as a portrait of the Roman model Maria Latini as an African woman; Regnault enlarged the canvas at the bottom and right and transformed her into the biblical temptress, so the Salome the Salon saw in 1870 is a second picture painted over a first, with the proportions and the edges shifted as the picture redefined itself. The same habit shows in the small scale: the Colonnade of the Court of the Lions at the Alhambra is suspected unfinished, detail in the lower half intended but never completed, and the Pasha Leaving Tangiers was left unfinished outright. The letter to his father of 28 September 1869 shows the working method behind the reworking (VERIFIED VERBATIM, French original via Baillot 2019, translation mine): "I have also traced, in secret, most of the ornaments and inscription motifs of the rooms of the Alhambra and the most interesting azulejos" -- the detail was collected first, in secret, and the picture was rebuilt around it. The watchable behaviour is a figure visibly repainted mid-scene, a second image emerging over the first, edges and proportions shifting as the picture redefines itself. **Takeaway:** do not start over; enlarge the canvas and repaint the subject into something else, and let the seam show.

## M9. Sun as Master
Mediterranean sun replaces academic chiaroscuro: shadowless painting, sun-bleached walls, colour-absent shadow, brightness as a force that erases modelling. Gotlieb's phrase for it is the "Orientalist Sublime": the North African sun was a "perceptual overload", a new master "severed from tradition and hence without its disabling authority", and Regnault did not pull back from the sun's sublimity as Fromentin and Guillaumet did but used "all the resources of his art in the service of an immersive dream" (via H-France). Salome is painted without shadow, a yellow dress on a gold ground; the Alhambra watercolors, above all The Court of the Ambassadors, contrast blinding light with intense dark, a vertiginous contrast of ground and air, dark and light, intense colour-absent shadow against sun-bleached walls. The turn south was a choice, not a drift: "In the summer of 1869, he finally left Rome behind for Spain and Morocco, seeking the light that would flood his most successful paintings" (Hyperallergic), and the light he found was the one Rome could not give him -- the Arch of Titus, he wrote to a friend, "seems like a toy compared to the Arch of Titus that one has imagined" (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Gotlieb via Hyperallergic). The watchable behaviour is light that flattens and bleaches until shadow disappears; brightness as a force that erases modelling. **Takeaway:** let the sun do the modelling and then take the modelling away; the bleach is the style.

## M10. Arabesque Detail vs Gore
Meticulous ornamental architecture set against raw, splattered violence, precision and mess held in one frame. In Summary Execution the Alhambra interior is hyper-detailed, drawn from photographs and from the tracings he made in secret in the Alhambra in September 1869, while the beheading in front of it is realistically spattered blood thrown at the canvas; Regnault's own phrase for the pairing is "the richest civilisation and the keenest cruelty coexisting in titanic, frightful splendour" (via H-France). The contrast is the picture's engine: the fine repeating ornament of the arabesque, the marble stairs, the carved wall, and then a single crude wet red event interrupting it. The doubling sharpens it further -- the severed head bears the same features as the executioner, so the victim witnesses his own execution (Gotlieb), a mechanism folded here from the main leg's candidates, and the Delacroix model behind the whole scene is his Execution of the Doge Marino Faliero (1826). The watchable behaviour is a field of fine repeating ornament interrupted by a single crude, wet, red event; the eye keeps returning to the interruption because the ornament will not let it rest. **Takeaway:** build the most precise room you can, then spill something in it; the precision is what makes the spill shocking.

## Evolution
Early, 1860-1866: Paris and the Academy, learned well and already resisted. Two unremarkable Salon portraits in 1864 ("in no wise remarkable", EB1911); Orpheus in the Underworld (1865), already showing a taste for popular culture inside a mythological subject, based on Offenbach's opera first performed in 1858; the Prix de Rome failure of 1863, then the 1866 win with Thetis, painted in twelve days. Rome bored him from the start: the past in Italy "did not touch him", the Arch of Titus was a toy beside the imagined one, and the rote copies at the Villa Medici tired him; but his illustrations to Francis Wey's Rome (published 1872) show how observant he was of actual life and manners -- the eye was already turned to the present, not the antique. Middle, 1867-1868: Rome and Spain, the rules broken and the types found. The Automedon envoi of 1868 breaks the Academy's rules on scale and subject count and reframes a classical subject as observed animal behaviour, a carnival horse-race recollected and horses studied live; the Spain trip of late 1868 turns to contemporary types -- the Castilian Mountain Shepherd, the Spaniard studies, the equestrian General Prim, the mechanism folded here from the main leg's candidates, a leader seen in motion at the head of troops becoming an image of demagoguery, painted "somewhat to the displeasure of his subject" -- and to Velazquez and Goya as models, with the visit to Mariano Fortuny's studio in Rome as the turning point toward Orientalism. Contact with the modern Hispano-Italian school, "highly materialistic and inclined to regard even the human subject only as one amongst many sources of visual amusement" (EB1911), told on him with ever-increasing force. Late, 1869-1870: Granada and Tangier, the sun as master and the reputation made entirely with student work. The Alhambra watercolors and the Colonnade of the Court of the Lions (1869); Judith (1869); the Tangier studies of black models in spring 1870 as preparation for the last large canvas; Salome and Summary Execution at the Salon of 1870, the first a gold medal and a city-wide craze, the second a hostile reception for its subject; the single-figure narrative, folded here from the main leg's candidates -- Salome alone, a short sword and a large platter telling the whole beheading of John the Baptist, no Herod, no John, no court, her knowing smile the smile "of someone who is about to get just what they wanted" (Eclectic Light), inviting us, in Gotlieb's reading, "to applaud her performance, to acknowledge our complicity, and to serve as her victim" -- and the direct address that goes with it, the figure looking out and recruiting the viewer as accomplice. The trajectory runs from rule-bound classical history painting to a personal, coloristic, violent naturalism: Gotlieb's summary, "violent, equine, coloristic, and thanks to the native, primordial character of his naturalism, by definition contemporary". The open ending: the last planned envoi, the 33-foot canvas on the Muslim conquest of Spain, was never painted, and the Pasha Leaving Tangiers was left unfinished, so there is no summation canvas, only the two Salon pictures of 1870 and the war. Afterlife: national hero within weeks, then the slow fade -- the 1872 retrospective wildly successful, the Luxembourg acquiring two large works including the Execution, the 1876 memorial and the black-crepe crown ritual of Rome Prize winners, then the collapse after 1878, the Execution in Louvre storage by the mid-1880s, the Deroulede appropriation, the 1912 Salome sale, the Great War sweeping away the heroic-exemplar culture, and the re-evaluation from the 1970s and 1980s, the Orsay opening in 1986 giving the public its first significant chance to see academic works such as his, Gotlieb 2016 the first book in English and the first volume since 1913. His posthumous reputation tracks the fate of academic art itself.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is the eye over the mind, and the present over the past. "All the later productions of Regnault were addressed exclusively to the eye" (EB1911, VERIFIED VERBATIM), and the eye he trusted was the one that looked at actual life: "The past in Italy did not touch him, but his illustrations to Wey's Rome show how observant he was of actual life and manners" (EB1911). He chose the modern era's light over the antique's authority -- Rome bored him, the Arch of Titus was a toy beside the imagined one, the Villa Medici copies were a chore -- and then he chose the sun over Rome, leaving in the summer of 1869 "seeking the light that would flood his most successful paintings". The Hispano-Italian materialism he met in Rome, which regarded "even the human subject only as one amongst many sources of visual amusement", gave him permission to treat the figure as one visual source among many: the horses matter more than the charioteer in Automedon, the yellow matters more than the girl in Salome, the blood matters more than the victim in the Execution. At the same time he was the academic hope, the emerging chef d'ecole and reformer of French painting, the future of French art in Meissonier's reported judgement -- and the whole career was made, as Lambertson notes in H-France (VERIFIED VERBATIM), "entirely with student work after winning the four-year Rome Prize in 1866": envois, not commissions; a student reputation, not a career. The short career is the wound in the logic and also its proof: twenty-seven years, about twenty named paintings, two Salon pictures that made him famous, and a death that the critics then read back into the paintings -- "That skill for depicting gore later seemed to foretell Regnault's own grisly battlefield death" (Hyperallergic), a reception position, not his claim, flagged as such in the legs. He volunteered because he felt he would be "only a mediocre officer but a model foot soldier" (SECONDHAND, via Gurney), went to Buzenval with his name sewn into his clothing, and was found by the friend who had shared his Tangier house. The pictures that survive are the work of a man who painted violence, horses and sunlight as if they were the same subject, and who died before any of them could become a manner.

C0. The study path study/henri-regnault.md was 404-verified free in the muse repo before pushing; no earlier study occupies it. This series' study #487 lives at study/henri-regnault.md.
C1. Birth date: 31 October 1843 is the consensus (Wikipedia infobox, EB1911, Wikidata with 4 references); one Wikipedia article read in search results, "Main Guard (Gibraltar)", gives 30 October 1843. The one-day variant is recorded, not resolved; 31 October is used throughout.
C2. Age at death: 27, not 28. Born 31 October 1843, died 19 January 1871 is 27 years and two and a half months; Wikipedia infobox "(aged 27)", Hyperallergic "died at the age of 27", Eclectic Light "He was 27". VMuseum's "at only 28 years old" is wrong by one year.
