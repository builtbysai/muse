# Harry Cox: Study Notes

**Site:** Harry Cox, b. Friday 27 March 1885, Barton Turf, Norfolk; d. Thursday 6 May 1971, Catfield, Norfolk, age 86. Farm labourer, stock feeder, and the most recorded English traditional singer of the twentieth century -- a man who carried some 140 songs in his head, learned by ear from his father, his mother and his neighbours, and sang them unaccompanied, eyes closed, for seventy years before the collectors found him, and for fifty years after. He was first noted on paper by E. J. Moeran in 1921, first recorded on disc in 1934, and recorded most intensively in 1953, when Alan Lomax and Peter Kennedy put tape machines in front of him in Catfield, Sutton and London and caught forty-six tracks of song and talk. [C35126]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Alan Lomax study: that study is about the man who picked the repertoire Sharp and Neal had collected in print and gave it a second life on tape -- the microphone in the room, the disc played back on the spot, the broadcast to ten million schoolchildren. This one is about the man on the other side of that microphone. Lomax recorded Harry Cox in 1953 -- at Sutton in October, in London in November, in a joint session with Peter Kennedy in December -- and interviewed him in 1955 with Ewan MacColl. Cox sang songs Sharp had printed in 1904 and 1916, songs Kidson had printed in 1891, and he sang them the way they had been sung before any of those books existed: learned by ear, kept in the head, sung at work and in the pub, never written down by the singer himself. Where Lomax's study is about keeping sound, this one is about keeping songs -- and about what a song has to be to survive seventy years in one man's head. [C35127]

## 1. Barton Turf, 27 March 1885: the seventh child

Harry Fred Cox was born on Friday 27 March 1885 at Barton Turf, Norfolk, on Barton Broad -- not at Catfield, as the brief for this study said, and as more than one careless summary still says. The family moved to the Potter Heigham and Catfield area in the 1890s, and Heppa's table has him at Potter Heigham from about 1895 until 1927, then at Catfield from 1927 until his death; Catfield is his home village, not his birthplace. He was the seventh of thirteen children of Robert ("Bob") Cox (1837-1928 per Wikipedia, 1837-1929 per Heppa -- one year unresolved) and Sarah Cox nee Nobbs (1850-1944). Four of the thirteen died in infancy; nine lived. Three sons: the eldest Bob, who did not sing; Harry, the middle son; and the youngest Fred, who married early, moved to Gorleston, was missed by the collectors, and left no recordings -- though his grandson Reg Reeve remembered him as Harry's equal or better as a musician.

The date itself is not settled to birth-certificate standard. The ODNB (Roy Palmer, 2004), Grove (Reg Hall, 2001) and Wikipedia all give 27 March 1885, and EATMT marked his 136th birthday on 27 March. But Peter Kennedy's 1958 personal narrative says "born on October 10th, 1885", Heppa repeats 10 October as fact, and the back of a British Library photograph (PR1318) is inscribed "Harry born 10.10.1885". No birth register was seen by any leg. The study uses 27 March, the ODNB line, and carries the 10 October variant in the open rather than burying it. Both dates are consistent with Kennedy's other statement, that Harry "was thirty-five when he first sang to the composer, the late E. J. Moeran" in 1921.

**Takeaway:** The birthplace error matters because it is the first of several places where the received summary is wrong and the specialist source is right -- and the birth-date split is the first of several places where even the specialist source disagrees with the reference books. Cox's life has to be built from the careful sources, not the summaries. [C35130]

## 2. The father who counted his songs on his hoe

Everything in Cox's singing starts with his father. Bob Cox was a seaman and fisherman and wherryman -- skipper of a wherry for some years -- who later became a farm labourer. He could not read or write. He was a noted singer and fiddle player whose fiddle "was always in demand at festive occasions and in the local pubs" and could earn him a day's pay for an evening's playing. Harry said his father could learn a ballad or love song after one hearing, and that Bob knew 105 songs -- counted by cutting notches into his hoe as he worked and sang. His repertoire was mainly sea songs, including whale-fishing songs. At sea he sang two different songs a night on watch and never repeated himself during a six-week voyage; he paid other men two bob to keep singing or reading a song to him until he had it.

Bob told Moeran in 1921 that he had taught Harry all his songs -- "I was his boy," Harry said -- and when Moeran tried hard to get the 84-year-old Bob to sing, Bob always referred him to Harry, seeing himself as "a maestro whose day was done". The locals rated Bob a "better" singer than Harry. That judgement hangs over the whole story: the man the collectors recorded was, by his own community's account, the second-best singer in his own family, and the best one refused to be recorded at all.

**Takeaway:** The repertoire is an inheritance, not a collection. Bob's 105 songs, notched into a hoe, are the unit of transmission -- a count kept in the tool of the work, not in a book. [C35131]

## 3. The mother and the penny songsheets

Sarah Cox got her songs from penny songsheets bought in Norwich market. She used to go to Norwich "a good bit" as a girl; her father supplied Norwich shops with potatoes by donkey cart. Harry: "I never see the sheets; my mother learnt them, and I learnt them off her." Songs learned from her include *Down in the Lowlands Low* and *Edmund in the Lowlands Low*. His big sister Laura had a song book of her own, researched under the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library's Full English Project.

The mother is the second transmission channel, and it works differently from the father's. Bob's songs came from the sea and the pub, learned by ear from other men. Sarah's came from print -- the cheapest print there was, a penny a sheet -- and reached Harry only after passing through her memory. The sheet itself never entered his hands. That is the shape of his whole musical education: print existed, but it existed at one remove, filtered through a singer's ear before it reached him. When Sharp's printed collections later turn out to contain his songs, that is the irony to hold onto -- the books were always there, and he learned the songs as if they were not.

**Takeaway:** Two channels feed one singer: the father's sea songs, learned man to man, and the mother's market songs, learned sheet to memory to ear. Neither channel involves the singer reading. [C35132]

## 4. Learning in the dark

The family could not afford oil for lamps through the winter. Evenings were spent in the glimmer of the fire, singing "to cheer ourselves up. That was all the frolic we had." Kennedy, visiting in the 1950s, found the cottage still had no electric light; the family had often sat in the dark, singing the long ballads, taking it in turns. His father would reach under the bed and get out "the music" -- the button accordion -- or borrow a fiddle. Children joined in cumulative songs with tongue-twister choruses that grew a verse at a time.

Harry accompanied his father to pub singing sessions from the age of four, sitting on the windowsill or hiding under the table listening to his father's singing and playing. He first sang in public at the Union Tavern, Smallburgh, three miles west of Catfield, at the age of eleven. He left school at thirteen and a half to work on the land. The pubs of the circuit were the Windmill Inn and Catherine Wheel at Sutton, the Falgate Inn and Railway Tavern at Potter Heigham, the King's Arms and Baker's Arms at Ludham, the Catfield Crown, and the Union Tavern at Smallburgh -- plus Sutton Sale Day, Ingham Fair, the Boxing Day ploughing contest at the White Hart, Repps.

**Takeaway:** The school is the dark room and the pub windowsill. There is no lesson and no book; there is listening, from underneath the table, until the song is in the head. [C35133]

## 5. Fifteen miles for a song

Cox did not only inherit songs; he hunted them. "Many a time, when I've heard of a man in another village who had a song I didn't know, I've walked over to see him, and paid him a sixpence to teach it to me. I've walked as far as fifteen miles for a song." His father did the same at sea. Specific attributions survive: *The Pretty Ploughboy* learned from his father working side by side on Billy Balls's farm, Potter Heigham, after the Great War; *The Groggy Old Tailor*, *The Bold Drover* and *The Game Cock* learned from Charlie Chettleburgh of Sutton; *The Rigs of the Time* learned from John W. "Charger" Salmons after Salmons moved to Stalham Green around 1942; *The Bonny Labouring Boy* from an Irishman who came singing in the local pubs; *The Black Velvet Band* possibly from Jack Riseborough, who learned it from Irish soldiers stationed at Ludham in the 1930s -- uncertain, and carried as uncertain.

The hunting had rules. Song-ownership etiquette governed the community: "Everyone had their own song"; a man would not sing another's song in his presence without permission. William Miller once had to be restrained from attacking a stranger who sang his song in the Catfield Crown. Harry deferred to it too -- he would not sing *Lord Lovell* because "that was sung by another fellow". There was also open competition: "They still like singing against one another to see who can sing the better song."

**Takeaway:** A song is property before it is a performance, and it is bought with a walk and a sixpence. The repertoire is assembled the way a man assembles tools -- one at a time, from named hands, under rules everybody knows. [C35134]

## 6. Thirty years feeding stock

Cox was a farm labourer for life: "doing everything I should think round about the farming way." Stock feeding was his delight: "I fed -- oh, thirty years and more. Sometimes I had done seven months at a stretch. Sundays and all -- no rest -- never had a holiday." He also hoed in gangs ("have a club -- a gallon or two of beer"), cut turnips, and threshed -- he composed a threshing song of his own. His father worked sixty hours a week, ten hours a day; Harry worked as hard, with very few days off -- Good Friday afternoon and Christmas Day, if he fed the cattle -- from the 1955 Lomax and MacColl interview catalogue.

He was often wrongly called a blacksmith: "He has often been referred to as a blacksmith, but doesn't know how the idea got about, 'except maybe that I'm black enough.'" (Ethnic, 1959.) He and his brother Fred once spent a winter chopping a half-mile thorn hedge between Potter Heigham and Catfield, carting the wood home by wheelbarrow for the winter fire. The work is not background to the singing. It is where the singing happened -- at the turnip shed, in the gang, on the long walk between farms -- and it is what the singing was for: company, in a life with almost no holidays in it.

**Takeaway:** The singer is a stock feeder first. The songs were sung at work because work was where the hours were, and the work set the terms -- a voice that can go on for hours without tiring, because the body already could. [C35135]

## 7. The navy: a minelayer in the North Sea

Cox served in the Royal Navy in the First World War, on a minelayer in the North Sea, based at Rosyth and Scapa Flow. That is the whole of it, as far as any source read goes. No enlistment date, no discharge date, no ship name was found by any leg -- a genuine gap, and the study does not fill it. Apart from the war and occasional trips to Norwich and London, "he has never been more than a few miles away from his home" (Ethnic, 1959).

The war matters for what it did to the tradition around him, not for what it did to him personally. After the First World War, in Harry's own verdict, "the heart had gone out of it" -- the pub singing. Then radio sets arrived in pubs in the 1930s, then television in the 1950s, then jukeboxes, and pub singing died by stages. The Catherine Wheel and the Windmill had no wireless, television or jukebox before they closed; when the Catherine Wheel closed, the men nailed a wreath to its front door. Cox lived long enough to watch the room he learned in disappear, and to be recorded explaining what had been in it.

**Takeaway:** The war is a hole in the record, not a chapter. What the war left behind is the subject: a singing culture that lost its heart in 1918 and its rooms over the next forty years. [C35136]

## 8. Moeran finds him, 1921

Ernest John Moeran (1894-1950), Irish extraction, lived at Bacton on the Norfolk coast and had been collecting before and during the First World War despite serious war head injuries; he "really got going from 1921 onwards". He stayed with Cecil Walsingham, who kept a bicycle shop in Sutton High Street, and visited the Sutton pubs. In late summer 1921 he got an urgent message from Arthur Batchelor, who had overheard Robert "Jolt" Miller singing at road work. Miller (born 1847) was the first man in the area Moeran collected from, and on about the third such evening he introduced Moeran to Cox: "Here's Harry have come to sing to you tonight." Harry was thirty-five. The relationship lasted nearly thirty years, until Moeran's death on Friday 1 December 1950.

Moeran noted that Harry could hear a twelve-verse song three times and then have it. He called him "that prince of singers" at the 1947 session, and "probably unique in England to-day as a genuine traditional singer combining comparative youth with a style of artistry which has almost disappeared". Harry's own account of the money, given to Ethnic in 1959, is the plainest statement of the collector's economy in the whole folk revival: Moeran "used to get the music -- take the tune down you see, a little in this line, a little in that. He got the 'Ploughboy.' I don't know what happened to that -- he sold that or something or other. He got ten pound for that. We went halves. ... We had five pound apiece." And: "There's people making money out of these hare old songs, but it's not us."

**Takeaway:** The founding encounter is a handover, not a discovery: one singer introduces the collector to another, the collector writes the tunes down, the song is sold for ten pounds, and the singer gets half. Everything after 1921 is that transaction repeated at larger scale. [C35137]

## 9. From notebook to print

Moeran published Harry's songs in the *Journal of the Folk-Song Society* 7 (1922), pages 1-24, and 8.5 (1931), pages 25-79; in *Six Songs from Norfolk* (Augener, 1924); and in the *Journal of the EFDSS* 5.3 (1948), pages 152-54. Specific datings survive: *The Man of Burniham Town* was noted from Harry in January 1922; *The Fowler* was published in 1922 credited to "Walter Gales and Harry Cox". The collection dates are firmer than the publication dates -- the *Journal* volume is 1922 in one source and 1923 in another, and the study does not settle it.

The distinction between notation and recording matters here, and the study keeps it straight. 1921-22 is paper: a composer with a notebook, taking a tune down "a little in this line, a little in that". 1934 is sound. Between them sits the whole difference between the first revival and the second: Sharp and Moeran wrote songs down; Lomax and Kennedy taped them. Cox spans both. He was thirty-six when his songs first appeared in print under his name, and he never read the print.

**Takeaway:** Print is the first machine that takes his songs away from him, and it takes them without his ever reading a word of it. The notebook is a one-way door: the tune goes in, the book goes to London, the singer stays in Catfield. [C35138]

## 10. The first disc: Decca, February 1934

In February 1934, at the Decca Studios in London, for the English Folk Dance and Song Society, Cox recorded two songs on one 78rpm disc: *The Pretty Ploughboy* and *Down by the Riverside* -- that is, *The Bold Fisherman*. The reference, from Heppa's footnote, is precise: "DC 87-88 Arc 92, E.F.D.S.S., recorded at the Decca Studios, London in February 1934. It was priced at three shillings." It was sold by the EFDSS in London and at Wilmott's Music Shop, Norwich. It was his first visit to London, and Moeran arranged the session.

Two cautions ride with the date. First, there is no commercial Decca catalogue number: the disc does not appear in the *Decca UK 78rpm Discography 1929-1951* at all, which is consistent with a private EFDSS pressing made at Decca's studios rather than a Decca commercial release. Second, A. L. Lloyd's sleeve note, quoted on Mainly Norfolk, says the recording was "made in 1936" -- the minority reading, against 1934 in EATMT, Mainly Norfolk and Wikipedia, and carried here as an unresolved variant. A third claim, from the SamFest site, that the record was an HMV release announced in 1933, is corroborated nowhere else and is not used.

**Takeaway:** The first recording is a society pressing, not a commercial record: three shillings, sold by the Society, no catalogue number a discographer can find. The commercial world never quite knew what to do with him, and it never did. [C35139]

## 11. War, and the wireless

There is no connected account of Cox's Second World War years in any source read. What is established is thin: Francis Collinson visited him in spring 1942 while collecting for the BBC *Country Magazine* broadcasts, and three of his songs went into the *Journal* of 1946; John "Charger" Salmons came to live at Stalham Green around 1942 because Yarmouth, where he had retired, was being bombed. Cox was fifty-four to sixty, a farm labourer in a reserved occupation, and presumably kept working -- but no source read states that explicitly, and the study does not assert it.

The BBC work is firmer. In 1945 Moeran arranged a public-house gathering at the Windmill, Sutton, for a projected BBC radio programme; BBC features producer Maurice Brown put the broadcast together, and it drew Collinson's interest. Tracks from Sutton in 1945 include *Just as the Tide was A-Flowing* and *The Bold Princess Royal*. On Monday 27 October 1947 Moeran supervised BBC recordings of Harry and friends at the Windmill Inn, Sutton, with others at the Eel's Foot, Eastbridge, Suffolk. Broadcast as "East Anglia Sings" on the BBC Third Programme on Wednesday 19 November 1947, and repeated the following week, it is the whole singing community on record together: Harry, William "Bullets" Miller, Elijah Bell, Charlie Chettleburgh, John "Charger" Salmons, Walter "Waxy" Gales. Harry sang *The Bonny Bunch of Roses*, *Barton Broad Ditty*, *The Barley Straw* and *The Fowler*. Moeran: "The singers seemed quite excited about it and were out to do their very best."

**Takeaway:** The war is a gap and the wireless is the bridge: the 1945 session is the moment the pub becomes a broadcast, and the 1947 session is the moment the whole community is caught together, before it disperses for good. [C35140]

## 12. The television summer, 1953

Lomax arrived in England in 1950. In June 1953 he and Kennedy took part with Cox in the BBC television programme "A Ballad Hunter in Britain" -- a first for both of them, and for a young producer-in-training at Alexandra Palace, David Attenborough. Kennedy's booklet says the programme "really woke us up to Cox's potential as a performer", including his turn to reminiscence. Lomax also hosted six twenty-minute episodes of BBC TV's *The Song Hunter* in 1953, commissioned by Attenborough; one source calls it an eight-part series, and the study carries the split without settling it.

The television summer is the hinge of the whole story. For thirty-two years Cox had been a local singer known to a composer and a society. In one summer he becomes a television performer, watched by a producer who will become the most famous broadcaster in Britain, and the men watching him realise that the talk between the songs is as valuable as the songs. Everything that follows -- the intensive autumn sessions, the LPs, the films, the obituaries calling him the father of East Anglian folk music -- follows from that summer.

**Takeaway:** Television is the second machine, after print and after the disc: it does not just record the singer, it shows the room, and the room is what makes the singer worth watching. [C35141]

## 13. The intensive autumn: forty-six tracks

The autumn of 1953 is the most documented stretch of Cox's life, and the study gives it in full because the detail is the point. Peter Kennedy recorded him in Catfield in October; Lomax recorded him in Sutton in October (tracks 9, 23-24: *The Farmer's Servant*, the *Barton Broad Babbing Ballad*, talk about babbing for eels); Lomax recorded him in London in November; Lomax and Kennedy recorded him together in London in December (track 20, *Three Toasts*); Kennedy recorded him alone in London in December. The forty-six tracks were released as *What Will Become of England?* (Rounder 11661-1839-2, 2000), in the Alan Lomax Collection: Portraits series, with notes by Kennedy dated London, July 1999.

The per-track split, counted from the ACE booklet PDF, is: Lomax alone 19 tracks, Kennedy alone 24, joint Lomax and Kennedy 3 (tracks 18 *The Foggy Dew*, 20 *Three Toasts*, 45 *Blackberry Fold*). Mainly Norfolk's session grouping credits tracks 18 and 45 to Kennedy alone in Catfield, against the booklet's joint credit -- one of the two is wrong, and the study does not settle it. EATMT warns the booklet notes "are not always accurate" and that many tracks "are very short and may have been heavily edited". Roughly half the disc is talk: "My life", "Working in a gang", "My grandfather and my father", "My mother", "Hunger and pay", "Poaching", "Learning from my father", "Up to the present I ain't forgot anything yet". The songs run from *What Will Become of England?* itself to *Adieu to Old Eng-e-land, Here's Adieu* -- his pronunciation preserved in the spelling.

**Takeaway:** The autumn is the archive in miniature: three months, two collectors, one singer, forty-six tracks, and half of them talk. The talk is not the filler around the songs; it is the other half of the record. [C35142]

## 14. The interview: Lomax and MacColl, 1955

In 1955 Alan Lomax and Ewan MacColl interviewed Cox together. The tape -- thirty-five minutes and fifty-six seconds, eleven tracks -- is held at Birmingham in the Charles Parker Archive, reference MS 4000/5/1/1/1: the original a seven-inch quarter-inch tape at seven and a half inches per second, half-track mono, with a CD-R listening copy. No month or day within 1955 is given in the catalogue, and the study does not invent one.

The content is the other side of the singing. Cox talks about his father's hard life as a farm labourer (sixty-hour weeks, ten-hour days, Good Friday afternoon and Christmas Day the only holidays); about poaching and the law ("two laws, one for the rich and one for the poor"); about the decline of common land ("the Norfolk Broads did not used to be owned"); about fourteen years' transportation to Australia for poaching, and man-traps that could break a man's leg. Then he sings: *Van Diemen's Land*, *Jack Tar on Shore*, *Up Jumped the Herring*, *The Female Drummer*, *Firelock Stile*, *Black Velvet Gown*. The interview is the politics of the repertoire made explicit -- the songs are about poaching and transportation because the life was, and the law is in the songs because the law was in the life.

**Takeaway:** The 1955 interview is where the singer stops singing and starts explaining, and the explanation is the key to the songs: two laws, common land enclosed, fourteen years for a hare. The ballads are the case files. [C35143]

## 15. Parker, 1958-1963

Charles Parker interviewed Cox extensively from October 1958 to July 1963 (Birmingham archive MS 4000/5/1/1/2-8, plus August 1963 tapes), on childhood, working life, singing and fiddle playing. Harry demonstrated his fiddle and his tap-dancing mannequin, made 1911-12 per the archive catalogue. In July 1963 Parker interviewed Cox and Sam Larner together on Sankey hymn singing (MS 4000/5/1/1/11) -- the dated meeting that disproves the published claim, repeated in Bruce Lindsay's book as reported in the press, that "Sam and Harry never met". On 18 August 1963 Harry and Larner were recorded for Scan television, singing, talking and playing fiddle; the material fed the Home Service programme *The Crow on the Cradle*.

Parker's tapes are the longest single record of Cox talking -- five years of interviews, against the three months of 1953 -- and they are also the least accessible: catalogue entries, not streams, held in Birmingham with CD-R listening copies. The study has read the catalogue, not the tapes, and says so. What the catalogue proves is the scale of the attention: by the end of his life Cox had been interviewed, at length, by four different collectors across three decades, and the interviews kept getting longer as the collectors realised the talk was the point.

**Takeaway:** Parker is the patient listener. Where Lomax and Kennedy took forty-six tracks in three months, Parker took five years -- and the five years include the one meeting the books say never happened. [C35144]

## 16. Two films, not one

There are two films, by two makers, and the study does not conflate them. The first is Philip Donnellan's *The Singer and the Song*, a BBC television film with Sam Larner, released in 1964. The East Anglian Film Archive synopsis: "Sam, aged 84, of Winterton, Norfolk, is filmed sitting in a chair in his sitting room. Harry, aged 78, from Catfield, Norfolk, is scything grass at the front of his cottage." Harry shows his dancing doll, talks of his early life and his seafaring father, and sings a sea song his father taught him plus a work song. The date is unstable across sources -- the EAFA catalogue lists the item under "(1959 - earlier)", Wikipedia and the Ethnic tradition say 1964 -- and Wikipedia's further claim that it was "released as an LP in 1966" was not verified anywhere and looks wrong; no such LP was found. The second film is Charles Parker's *Two Norfolk Singers*, 16mm, 1962, in which Parker interviews Cox and Larner at their homes; it is on YouTube, and Harry sings "As I Walked Out", "When the Stormy Winds Did Blow", "The Bold Drover", "The Spotted Cow" and "Betsy" in it, with talk and the dancing doll.

The scything opening is the image to keep. The film does not open on a singer; it opens on a labourer cutting grass in front of his cottage, and the singing comes after the work. That is the order of his life, and the film, for once, gets the order right.

**Takeaway:** Two films, two makers, one shared subject -- and the shared image is work first, song second. The confusion in the dates is a warning: even the filmography of a famous singer is partly guesswork, and the study marks the guesses. [C35145]

## 17. The LPs: 1964-1965

In his eightieth year Cox got his LPs. *Traditional English Love Songs* came out on Folk-Legacy in the United States in 1964 (FSE 20), and *Harry Cox Sings English Love Songs* on DTS in Britain (LFX 4, fourteen tracks: *Seventeen Come Sunday*, *The Spotted Cow*, *Colin and Phoebe*, *The Bonny Labouring Boy*, *The Female Drummer*, *Marrowbones*, and eight more). The British date is unresolved -- 1964 per the EATMT discography, 1965 per the EATMT review and the Musical Traditions discography, with the review adding that the American issue came first. *Harry Cox: English Folk Singer* (EFDSS LP 1004, 1965, seventeen tracks) was issued "as a tribute to a great English folk-singer in the year of his 80th birthday"; the reissue, same catalogue number with a replacement sleeve painted by John Crane, is undated even by Holderness.

The LPs are the moment the recordings become a catalogue a listener can own, rather than a broadcast that passes. Folktrax cassettes 032 (love songs), 033 (sea songs) and 034 (documentary: songs and stories of country life) followed in the 1970s, later CDRs, long out of print. The Kennedy recordings also went into the *Folk Songs of Britain* volumes (Caedmon, 1961; Topic reissues 1968-70) and the *Voice of the People* series (1998, 2012). The tapes themselves now live in three places: Lomax's at the Library of Congress via the Association for Cultural Equity, Kennedy's at the British Library Sound Archive (1,660-plus reels), Parker's and MacColl's at Birmingham.

**Takeaway:** The LPs arrive at eighty, not at thirty-five. The singer's working life is over before his records exist as objects -- and the objects, once they exist, outlive the rooms they were recorded in. [C35146]

## 18. The last years: 1967-1970

The recording continued almost to the end. Sheila Park recorded *The Rigs of the Time* from him in 1967, and recorded him at home around 1969-70 with only herself and Peter Coleman present -- she stopped the tape between songs to save reel cost, and regretted losing the talk. Bob Thomson interviewed him in July 1968. Michael Grosvenor Myer and Bob Thompson interviewed him in November 1970, transcribed by Rita Gallard and published in *Folk Review* in February 1973 as "A Visit to Harry Cox". John Halliday recorded some forty minutes of Cox with other Windmill singers on reel tape in 1968, found in a house clearance in 2020 and digitised by EATMT -- including *The Barley and the Rye*, collected by Moeran from Cox in 1927 but appearing in no other Cox recording.

On Monday 5 January 1970 he was awarded the EFDSS Gold Medal -- the gold badge, as Wikipedia has it -- "an accolade that was unaccountably slow in coming", in Heppa's words. He was eighty-four. He kept his verdict on the revival to the end: "I wouldn't listen to a song like they make today; never pick up one to learn, 'cause there ain't one worth it." He was derogatory about folk-rock groups ("all that squit you hear on the radio"), yet believed instruments detracted from a song's story -- though he himself played fiddle and melodeon. His only concessions to modern pop were singing along to Lonnie Donegan's "Battle of New Orleans" and Judy Collins's "Amazing Grace".

**Takeaway:** The last recordings are the most revealing precisely because they are the least formal: a home tape, a stopped reel, a forty-minute pub reel found in a clearance. The archive ends the way it began -- in a room, with a machine running. [C35147]

## 19. Death: 6 May 1971

Harry Cox died on Thursday 6 May 1971, aged eighty-six, at Catfield, after being ill for some time. He was buried on Monday 10 May 1971 in St Nicholas churchyard, Potter Heigham; his grave was unmarked as of August 2026. The obituaries came on Saturday 8 May 1971: the *Eastern Daily Press* ("Father of Norfolk songs dies ... acclaimed as the 'father of East Anglian folk music' ... More than 100 local songs would have been lost if he had not remembered them"), *The Times*, and Peter Kennedy's obituary in the *Folk Music Journal* 2.2 (1971), pages 160-62. "When he died in 1971 all his older singing friends had already passed on" (Heppa).

The death year carries one error worth naming, because it is the error this study series has now corrected twice. The Association for Cultural Equity album page header reads "Harry Cox (1885-1970)". Everything else -- the two obituaries, Kennedy's obituary, Heppa, EATMT, Wikipedia, the ODNB -- says 6 May 1971, and the ACE booklet's own running text says 1885-1971. The header is simply wrong. It is a small thing, and it is the kind of small thing that gets copied from page to page until it looks like a fact.

**Takeaway:** He outlived his whole singing circle, and the record of his death is itself a lesson in how errors breed: one wrong header, copied often enough, becomes a second death year. The study uses 1971 and names the error. [C35148]

## 20. The repertoire: a range, not a number

How many songs did he know? No single figure is authoritative, and the study does not invent one. Wikipedia says around 150 songs, citing Grove; the ODNB says some 140 items; EATMT says "well over a hundred songs, as well as many other snippets"; Paul Marsh, who compiled the Topic set, counted 112 songs plus 28 fragments plus 4 toasts. The honest statement is a range -- 140 to 150, with over a hundred as the safe floor -- and the Marsh count is the most useful, because it distinguishes songs from fragments from toasts instead of rolling them into one round number.

The range runs from rough bawdry to high balladry: sea songs from his father's years at sea, local songs, ancient ballads, bawdy comic songs, toasts, and dance tunes on fiddle, melodeon and whistle. The Child Ballads in his repertoire are verified: *The Outlandish Knight*, *Georgie*, *Bold Archer*, *Black-Hearted Gypsies*, *Our Goodman*, *The Gypsy Laddie*. The fullest lists are the Folktrax cassettes (032 love songs, 033 sea songs, 034 documentary), the forty-six Rounder tracks, and the fifty-four Topic tracks -- all captured in the leg reports. The repertoire also has edges, and the edges matter: songs he knew but did not sing, taken up in section 27.

**Takeaway:** A repertoire is not a number. It is a range with edges, and the edges -- what he would not sing, what he only half remembered -- are as informative as the count. [C35149]

## 21. The Sharp overlap

The doorway of this study is a repertoire overlap, and it is large. The same songs Sharp printed in Somerset in 1904-16 were sung by Cox in Norfolk in 1934-70, and the leg verified the overlap song by song against the books' contents -- not against summaries, but against the contents lists themselves. In *Folk Songs from Somerset*, First Series (1904): *The Foggy Dew* (No. 17), *Geordie* (No. 2), *The Wraggle Taggle Gypsies, O!* (No. 9). Third Series: *The Crabfish* (No. LIX), *The Green Mossy Banks of the Lea* (No. LXVII), *The Bold Fisherman* (No. LXIX). Fourth Series: *The Outlandish Knight* (No. LXXXIV). In *One Hundred English Folksongs* (1916): *The Wraggle Taggle Gypsies* (No. 5), *Geordie* (No. 9), *The Outlandish Knight* (No. 11), *The Bold Fisherman* (No. 42 -- Sharp's own note: "I have several variants, and I think in every case the tune is in 5-time. The words in the text have been compiled from the sets given me by various singers"), *I'm Seventeen Come Sunday* (No. 61), *Sweet William* (No. 72), *The Crabfish* (No. 77). *Scarborough Fair* (No. 74) is probable, against Cox's *In Scarborough Fair Town* -- same family, not verified as the same Roud entry from the sources read, and carried as probable.

In the field notebooks: Sharp collected *The Pretty Ploughboy* from Mr N. B. Chisholm at Woodridge, Virginia, on 27 September 1916 (VWML CJS2/9/2623); *The Bold Fisherman* is in his notebooks at CJS2/9/884 and CJS2/9/1231. *Adieu to Old England* is No. 1 in James Reeves's *The Idiom of the People* (1958), edited from the Sharp manuscripts; *The Foggy Dew* is No. 33 in the same book. Two limits: Sharp's Somerset Series 2 and 5 were not checked by any leg, so any overlap there is unverified; and the overlap is repertoire, not contact -- there is no evidence Sharp ever met or collected from Cox, and Sharp died in 1924, three years after Moeran found him. The nearest thing to a personal chain is Moeran, who collected *Bold Fisherman* from Cox himself in January 1922 and published it in the *Journal of the Folk-Song Society* 7 (1922), pages 21-22.

**Takeaway:** The books and the singer contain the same songs, and neither knew the other existed. That is the doorway: not a meeting, but a relay -- print to ear to tape, across fifty years, with the song intact. [C35150]

## 22. The Kidson overlap

The second printed overlap is Frank Kidson's *Traditional Tunes* (Oxford, 1891), whose contents the leg read end to end. Cox songs in it: *Geordie* (No. 5), *The Outlandish Knight* (No. 6, plus an alternate tune in the appendix), *The Spotted Cow* (No. 26), *Colin and Phoebe* (No. 29), *Just As the Tide Was Flowing* (No. 49), *The Foggy Dew* (No. 81). In Kidson's *A Garland of English Folk-Songs*, *Seventeen Come Sunday* is No. 1. The book also contains, as a warning against careless matching, *The Farmer's Boy* (No. 23) and *The Grey Mare* (No. 31) -- both in Kidson, neither a Cox song -- and a "Pretty Ploughboy" at No. 67 that is Roud 151, a different song from Cox's Roud 186 *Pretty Ploughboy*. The study does not claim a Kidson overlap for the Ploughboy on that evidence.

Kidson matters because he is the earlier generation: a Leeds collector publishing in 1891, twenty years before Sharp's Somerset series and thirty years before Moeran found Cox. The overlap runs Kidson to Sharp to Cox -- three collectors, two books, one singer -- and the songs survive all three handlings. *The Spotted Cow* is the proof of the chain's later reach: Cox's 1953 Kennedy recording of it is the version Peter Bellamy learned and Steeleye Span recorded on *Below the Salt* in 1972.

**Takeaway:** Kidson extends the relay back to 1891. The same songs pass through three printed collections and one unlettered singer, and the singer's version is the one the revival ends up singing. [C35151]

## 23. The negative finding

The title track of his best-known album has no Sharp or Kidson overlap at all. *The Bonny Labouring Boy* is a broadside song, earliest known print around 1820, and Cox learned it from an itinerant Irish farm worker who came singing in the local pubs. His own account, to Bob Thomson in 1968: "an old Irish bloke [who] used to come over here and have a few days about, singing in these pubs here. He sung it. He writ it down or told somebody and I writ it down. That's how I come by that. I did have that one writ out. I never had so many writ down. Very few."

The negative is useful because it stops the doorway from becoming a thesis. Not every Cox song comes from Sharp's books; some come from a travelling Irishman in a Sutton pub, written down once and then carried in the head. The tradition has more than one road into it, and the printed road is only the one this study happens to be following. A second negative belongs here too: *I Had an Old Hoss* against Sharp's *Poor Old Horse*, *The Greenland Whalecatchers* against Sharp's *The Greenland Fishery*, *In Scarborough Fair Town* against *Scarborough Fair* -- all probable same-family overlaps, none verified from the sources read, and none claimed.

**Takeaway:** The album's title song breaks the pattern, and that is the point. The repertoire is not Sharp's books set to music; it is a working singer's whole life, and the books are only one of its sources. [C35152]

## 24. The tune first

"You must get the tune first." That is Cox, on the talk track that opens the Topic set, and it is the first rule of his practice. The melody is the carrier; the words ride it. His mother learned tunes from broadsheets brought from Norwich, and he learned the tunes off her and never saw the sheets himself. A song is a tune with words attached, not words with a tune attached, and a singer who has the tune can recover the words -- by walking outside until they come back -- while a singer who has only the words has nothing.

The rule explains the shape of the whole repertoire. It explains why he could learn a twelve-verse song after three hearings, as Moeran noted: three hearings of the tune, and the words follow. It explains why the versions are stable across decades -- Heppa's comparison of *Bold Archer* in 1959 and 1970 finds eleven identical verses with single-word differences -- because the tune holds the words in place. And it explains why he refused new songs: a new song has no tune in his head yet, and without the tune there is nothing to hang the words on.

**Takeaway:** The tune is the storage medium. Everything else -- the words, the story, the verses -- is data written onto a melody, and the melody is what survives the decades. [C35153]

## 25. Mind on the song

"To watch him, with his eyes closed or looking into the distance beyond the company, you realise that he is living the story of each song." That is Peter Kennedy, in the EFDSS LP1004 sleeve notes, quoted in the EATMT review. Cox's own version is shorter: "When I sing a song, my mind is on it." The performance is inward: no playing to the room, no dramatics, no gesture. The singer leaves the room while staying in it, and the story is re-lived, not acted.

The watchable beat is the withdrawal. A single still figure, eyes shut or gaze fixed past the company, while a long narrative unspools -- no instrument, no audience interaction, the drama all inward. It is the opposite of the fiddle and melodeon Harry, who is all hands (section 31), and the contrast is the point: the voice performs by disappearing into the song, and the hands perform by working. Both are his, and they never happen at the same time.

**Takeaway:** Concentration is the performance. The singer does not address the room; he vacates it, and the room listens harder because he has gone. [C35154]

## 26. Dry, dispassionate, perfectly paced

Every witness says the same thing in different words. "At first hearing of Harry Cox you may remark on the 'dry' impersonality and monotony of his style; for many of us in the Society it has taken five, ten or even twenty years to appreciate the subtleties of his performances." (Kennedy, same sleeve notes.) Holderness on *Newlyn Town* and *The Bonny Bunch of Roses*: they "demonstrate well Harry's consummate dispassionate delivery, allowing the story to take its course." Murder, betrayal, hanging and drowning are delivered in the same even tone as a comic song -- the horror is in the words, not in the voice. "Each song is imbued, however, with the same dry cynicism as when he tells a story or speaks about his family background and hard-working country life." (Kennedy.)

The technique underneath the plainness is exact. Francis Dillon, the BBC producer, quoted in the ODNB: "a carefully placed decoration, a beautifully judged phrasing, an exact control of highly complex rhythm". Sarah Lifton: "Cox's singing style was very straightforward and at first may seem colorless when compared with the vibrant style of, say Sam Larner, but its subtleties require repeated listenings to appreciate." Shirley Collins, after listening to all of Kennedy's recordings: "how melodious and graceful was his singing and how perfectly paced. But he was never dull. He could make you smile with *The Maid of Australia* or move you with *Polly Vaughan* and his *Death of Nelson* is one of the most touchingly mournful songs I ever heard." The long songs are sustained at length -- *Henry the Poacher* runs 5:23 and *Blackberry Fold* 5:25 on the Rounder CD -- and the pace never hurries.

**Takeaway:** The style reads as plain on first hearing and reveals itself only on repetition. The evenness is not a lack of feeling; it is a decision to let the story do the feeling, and the decision is what makes the horror land. [C35155]

## 27. Verse discipline: that don't belong in that

The sharpest statement of his ethics as a singer comes from the November 1970 interview with Myer and Thompson, quoted in EATMT's "Broads Brothers". On *Barbara Ellen*: "Some people sing that different to what other people do. You might know a different tune. And there's some of them put another two verses on the end. I never could. 'And from her grave grew a rose'. That other one come in Lord Lovely: 'Where they tied together in a true-lover's knot, For true loves all to admire'. That should come in another song. They get mixed up; that shouldn't come in Barbara Ellen. That don't belong in that. They belong in Lord Lovely. My uncle used to sing that. I never did go in for that, I don't know why. That was out of my line. That was sung by another fellow, and I didn't want to mix up too many bits."

Each song has an owner and a fixed shape; verses do not migrate. *Lord Lovely* was his uncle's song and "out of my line", so he did not sing it, and he refused to let its verses creep into *Barbara Ellen* even though they fitted. *Captain Ward*: "I never did know Captain Ward. My father used to sing it." He could recall several lines only, and he left it at that. The discipline is the same as the pub etiquette in section 5 -- a song belongs to somebody, and you do not take what is not yours -- carried inside the performance itself.

**Takeaway:** The repertoire has a fence around every song. Verses do not wander, songs do not merge, and some songs are not his to sing at all. The fence is the ethics, and the ethics are audible. [C35156]

## 28. Truncation that keeps the essentials

Cox cut long ballads hard, and the cutting made them stronger. Steve Roud on his *Bold Archer*: "Harry's is a severely trimmed version (Child's A text, for example, has forty-five verses), but all the essentials of the story are present." The same for *The 'Prentice Boy* (also *Ekefield Town*): "Comparing Harry's with the Original, his is severely truncated and avoids the wordiness of 18th century texts, but it includes many of the most telling details, such as the stake from the hedge, and the dragging by the hair. Nevertheless, the omission of the original motif of pregnancy leaves the murder motiveless in Harry's version, which heightens either the song's stark horror, or the sordidness, according to the listener's own viewpoint." (Mainly Norfolk.)

The mechanism is: cut the wordiness and the motive, keep the telling detail. The story gets starker, not thinner -- and in the *'Prentice Boy* case the cut itself does work, because a motiveless murder is more horrible than a motivated one. This is editing as a folk process, not as a literary one: the singer keeps what the ear can hold and what the room can bear, and lets the rest go, and the song that survives is shorter and harder than the one in the book.

**Takeaway:** Shortening is not losing. The ballad is trimmed to the details that carry it, and the motive is expendable where the stake from the hedge is not. [C35157]

## 29. Memory, and the chalk tally

"Up to the present I ain't forgot anything yet. I got a good memory. I don't forget; I mean I ain't lost nothing. I have been mixed up sometimes for just one line but you go out of doors that'll come to you; nobody can tell you." (Cox, Rounder CD talk track.) A lost line is not prompted and not looked up; it is waited for, outdoors, until it returns. And new songs are refused outright: "I like old songs. I wouldn't listen to a song like they make today; never pick up one to learn, 'cause there ain't one worth it, not to my knowledge. They're out of my line, and they can do what they like but they can't sing a song not where there's sense in it, not like I can." The repertoire is closed, by choice.

He kept songs fresh by singing them to himself at work -- cutting turnips in the shed -- and he kept count of them in chalk on the shed wall. To Charles Parker in August 1963: "I used to make a mark on the wall, t' see... how many I knew and that sort of thing, you see. That's how I used to keep 'em. I used to go through all these... Oh yeah, I had all manner of fancies." One chalk mark per song sung as he worked: an index of the repertoire and of the day's work at once, two accumulations side by side -- the pile of chopped turnips growing beside the wall filling with strokes. He also kept written sources: three exercise books of songs before the Great War, and a collection of some 300-400 songs and ballads, mostly written out by his sisters or his daughter Myrtle. But the wall is the index he made himself, for himself, in the place where he worked.

**Takeaway:** Memory is a walk outside, not a lookup, and the repertoire is counted in chalk on a shed wall. The most concrete image in the whole Cox record is a tally -- a memory made visible, one stroke at a time. [C35158]

## 30. The dancing doll

Cox carved a wooden dancing doll around 1911-12, per the Birmingham archive catalogue -- *Ethnic* in 1959 said "nearly fifty years now since he made" it, which would put it around 1909-10, a minor tension carried as such. *Ethnic* gives the full mechanical description, and it is worth quoting because the mechanism is the point: "carved out of wood, blackened and burnished by long years of handling. The body is supported by a horizontal peg which projects from its back and is held rigidly in one hand. The arms and legs are freely articulated in puppet fashion. The operator sits on one end of a thin three-foot board, the other end projecting between the knees and free to vibrate vertically. The doll is held in position just above the free end of the board which is rapped with the knuckles of the free hand. The result is a spectacular quick-fire clog dance by the doll to a diddled accompaniment."

The doll is the most filmable object in Cox's world. It appears in both films -- Donnellan's and Parker's -- and in the Parker interview tapes, where he demonstrates it; a track on *The Bonny Labouring Boy* is literally titled "A Hornpipe (dancing doll and voice)". It is the step-dancing of the Norfolk pubs miniaturised into a hand-held machine: peg, board, knuckles, vibration -- input to dance, with no dancer. A man who sang without accompaniment and played fiddle only in private made a puppet that danced to a tune he diddled with his own mouth.

**Takeaway:** The doll is a machine for dancing without a dancer, built by a singer who kept his voice and his instruments apart. It is the one piece of his world that is already a mechanism, waiting to be drawn. [C35159]

## 31. The shy fiddler and the fierce melodeon

Cox played fiddle, melodeon and tin whistle, under a strict personal code. "Harry plays the fiddle, but unlike his father he is very shy of playing it in public -- preferring to tune up in the afternoons when he is alone in the house. His main instrument is the melodeon, which he plays with a fierce open four-in-a-bar left hand and a jagged right -- a rough and typically Southern style of great drive and punch." (Ethnic, 1959.) And the rule that governs all of it: he never accompanies himself when singing. Song and instruments are kept apart; the voice stands alone, always.

His fiddle style uses heavy droning. Heath-Coleman, on Cox and Joe Cashmere: "Both Joe Cashmere and Harry Cox... employ much droning in some of their tunes, and the ear which is not accustomed to this style will sometimes hear only that droning and the chording it generates: closer listening, however, will reveal that all the notes are in fact there!" And the same tune differs between instruments -- the *Yarmouth Hornpipe* on fiddle against the melodeon is "interesting to note how different it is". Three watchable contrasts follow: the private afternoon fiddler against the public melodeon player; the drone, a held note under a moving tune, the fiddle as a one-man band; and the same tune reshaped by the instrument that carries it.

**Takeaway:** The rule is the mechanism: the voice never shares the stage, not even with its owner's own hands. The instruments are for tunes between songs, and the songs belong to the voice alone. [C35160]

## 32. Dialect: two Englishes

"A lifetime on the land bred and nurtured a true traditionalist Norfolk flavour in the singer's voice, which remained authentic despite his national recognition." (Eastern Daily Press obituary, 8 May 1971.) But the dialect works in a particular way, and Kennedy's booklet names it: Cox speaks broad Norfolk dialect, yet sings most songs in "proper", standard English -- except the ballads and local songs like the *Barton Broad Babbing Ballad*, where the dialect floods back. His singing pronunciation is "quite deliberate... a curiously exaggerated clarity" that "give[s] added emphasis to the beat" (Ethnic, 1959). Kennedy: "at one time you could tell by the way people spoke, which village, or even which part of a village, they came from."

The evidence in print is in spellings and in his own talk: "Adieu to Old Eng-e-land", "Widdlecombe Fair" for Widecombe, "babbing" for eel-fishing without hooks, "ain't", "hare" for here. No phonetic study of his dialect was found in the sources read, and the study invents no vowel claims. One caution matters: Sharp's printed versions standardise dialect -- Reeves records that Sharp and Marson "have not tried to reproduce by spelling the Somerset dialect but anything like a peculiar use, which is characteristic of the speech, we have carefully kept" -- so the printed Sharp versions are not evidence for how Cox sounded. Only the recordings and his talk are.

**Takeaway:** The voice switches register mid-performance: talk in dialect, song in standard English, local song in dialect again. Place turns on and off like a tap, and the tap is the song's subject. [C35161]

## 33. Talk frames the song

Songs were framed by talk -- about himself, his father, learning, singing in pubs, hunger and pay. The EFDSS LP's second side mixes songs with talk and instrumental tracks; Folktrax 034 is built the same way; the Rounder CD is roughly half talk. The talk is not filler: it is how he places each song ("My father used to sing it", "That's how that come along of him"), and it is where the ethics and the history live -- the two laws, the common land, the sixty-hour weeks, the sixpence for a song.

The talk is also the reason the 1953 sessions matter as more than a song list. Kennedy's booklet is explicit about the method: "by keeping our tape machines running, we were able to probe more deeply... the recollection process flow[s] in its natural course." The machine running is what lets the memory arrive in its own time -- the lost line, the correction, the second attempt. The talk tracks are the sound of a man remembering in real time, and the songs are what the remembering produces.

**Takeaway:** The song without its talk is a text; with its talk, it is a life. The tape recorder's real gift is not the song preserved but the remembering preserved alongside it. [C35162]

## 34. The clique

Cox did not sing alone, except at work. The Catfield and Sutton singing community -- Heppa's paper reconstructs it in detail -- sang Saturday nights in the Windmill Inn and Catherine Wheel at Sutton, the Catfield Crown, the King's Arms and Baker's Arms at Ludham, the Falgate Inn and Railway Tavern at Potter Heigham. Leslie Miller described the group as a sort of "clique". The core, named and dated: Robert "Jolt" Miller (born 1847), who introduced Moeran to Cox; William "Bullets" Miller (born 1878, died around 1956), Harry's great friend, with whom he performed as a pair at the Falgate Inn in the 1930s; Charlie Chettleburgh (born 1870, died 1953), from whom Harry learned *The Bold Drover* and *The Game Cock*; John W. "Charger" Salmons (born 1870), from whom he probably learned *The Rigs of the Time* after Salmons moved to Stalham Green around 1942; Elijah Bell (born 1869, died 1965); Walter "Waxy" Gales (born 1863, died 1949), the oldest man in the community; Jack Riseborough (born 1886, died 1949); George "Gunner" Blaxell on melodeon; Samuel R. Howard (born 1909), who learned *Marrowbones* from Harry -- "That's where I learnt it from, there's no doubt about it."

The 27 October 1947 BBC session is the group on record together: six men at the Windmill, each singing his own song, Harry singing all the remaining songs. Song ownership was the rule that held the group together and the competition that kept it sharp. When the Catherine Wheel closed, the men nailed a wreath to its front door. The community is gone; the 1947 session is the photograph of it, in sound.

**Takeaway:** The singer is one voice in a circle, not a solo act. The circle has rules -- own your song, don't take another man's, sing against each other to see who sings better -- and the rules are what kept the songs straight for sixty years. [C35163]

## 35. The carry: who sang his songs after 1971

The songs did not die with him. Ewan MacColl sang Cox's *The Black Velvet Band* in folk clubs and recorded it in 1961 with the explicit note "learned from the singing of the great Norfolk folksinger, Harry Cox"; the Dubliners made it an international hit single in 1967. Peter Bellamy found *The Spotted Cow* in Cox's repertoire and sang it on *The Fox Jumps Over the Parson's Gate* in 1969; Steeleye Span opened *Below the Salt* with it in September 1972, the liner notes saying "collected from Harry Cox of Norfolk". Shirley Collins met Cox just once, in the 1950s, at Kennedy's cellar sing-around at Cecil Sharp House, when she was a teenager -- and then listened to all of Kennedy's recordings of him, and learned *The Maid of Australia*, *Polly Vaughan* and *The Death of Nelson* from the tapes. Jim Ghedi recorded *What Will Become of England* in 2022 from Lomax's 1953 field recording of Cox at his Catfield home. Two carry claims were not verified and are not asserted: the Chumbawamba and Simon Schama soundtrack credits, which rest on a single mention.

The chain is the point of the whole study. A song learned by ear from a father who could not read, notched into a hoe, noted by a composer in 1922, pressed on a society 78 in 1934, taped by two collectors in 1953, sung by a folk-club singer in 1961, and a hit single in 1967: that is a hundred years of transmission, and every link in it is dated.

**Takeaway:** The repertoire outlives the singer because it was built to travel -- tune first, words riding it, ownership clear, verses fixed. The hit single is not a betrayal of the tradition; it is the tradition working as designed. [C35164]

## Aesthetic logic

Cox's world is a small place -- a few villages between Barton Broad and the sea, a handful of pubs, one shed with a chalked wall -- and the aesthetic of the study follows from that smallness. Everything is counted and kept: 105 songs notched into a hoe, chalk marks on a wall, three exercise books, a collection of 300-400 songs written out by sisters and a daughter. Nothing is wasted and nothing is improvised: the songs are trimmed to their essentials, the verses do not migrate, the voice never shares the stage, the new songs are refused. The beauty is in the discipline, not in the decoration -- a dry, even delivery that lets the story do the work, a dialect that turns on and off with the song's subject, a memory that returns a lost line only when you walk outside. The pieces this study feeds should be small, exact, and patient: one room, one voice, one tally, one doll. The grandeur is in the duration -- a ballad that takes five minutes and refuses to hurry, a repertoire that takes seventy years and refuses to forget.

The study's own discipline mirrors its subject. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is printed, not smoothed over: the birth date, the Decca year, the film dates, the EP dates. Where a claim cannot be verified -- the WWI dates, the WWII years, the 1955 interview's month, the birth register -- the gap is named, not filled. Cox would not borrow a verse that did not belong in a song; this study does not borrow a fact that does not belong in the record. [C35165]

**Top pick: Sam Larner (LAR)** -- Samuel James Larner, b. Friday 18 October 1878, Winterton-on-Sea, Norfolk; d. Saturday 11 September 1965, Great Yarmouth, age 86 -- the strongest doorway, the natural #809. Doorway tie: the other Norfolk giant and the only other singer Cox is documented as meeting late in life -- Charles Parker interviewed them together on Sankey hymn singing in July 1963 (Birmingham archive MS 4000/5/1/1/11), and Donnellan's TV film *The Singer and the Song* (1964) shows them together, Larner aged 84 and Cox 78. Same county (born six miles apart), same revival circle (Kennedy, Lomax, MacColl, Parker, Donnellan), opposite poles of the same tradition: the fisherman's sea songs against the farmworker's land songs, with shared repertoire ('Newlyn Town' / 'The Robber') bridging them. The study can open by correcting the published claim that they never met. Larner: fisherman, 43 years at sea, cabin boy at 12, some 60 songs, Norfolk step dancer, missed by Moeran (away at sea), found late by Donnellan in a pub in 1956, his life the basis of MacColl's 'The Shoals of Herring'.
