## The life: the engineer of the poem's body

Julio Plaza Gonzalez was born in Madrid on 1 February 1938, the son of Sofia Gonzales and Leocadio Plaza; his aunt Angela encouraged his art, taking him to exhibitions and giving him books and drawing materials, and as an adolescent he worked in a glass factory, an experience that left a scar on a finger and gave him skills with industrial materials he later used in his work. (C13190, leg 1) The birth year is printed as 1938 with a footnote, not as a settled fact: every source read except one gives 1938, and the sole 1937 source is Julia Mitchell's Leubsdorf Gallery student essay, which cites no source for the date. The exact day, 1 February, rests on PT Wikipedia alone. (C13187, leg 1; summary correction 1) He was a Spanish-born artist who became a naturalised Brazilian -- Spanish by birth, Brazilian by choice. (C13189, leg 1) He died in Sao Paulo on 17 June 2003, aged 65, of a stroke. (C13188, leg 1)

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the same as the doorway: the Madrid constructivist who arrived in Brazil in 1967 with programmed modular works, met Augusto de Campos at the IX Bienal, and spent the next thirty-five years giving other people's words new bodies -- paper, post, telephone screen, hologram, pixel -- while building the institutions that let those bodies circulate.

(1) Madrid and Paris, 1950s-1966. He began his artistic training in Madrid in the 1950s; the Razuk timeline dates his formation at the Circulo de Bellas Artes to 1954-1960, the Escritorio de Arte commentary to 1957 -- see Conflicts. (C13191, leg 1) He studied at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts and the Academie Julian in Paris in 1960-1962. (C13192, leg 1) In the early 1960s he travelled in Europe, encountering Russian constructivism (Malevich, Pevsner, Naum Gabo, Moholy-Nagy), Mondrian, Kandinsky, concrete art and the Bauhaus, contacts reflected in his kinetic-marked painting. (C13193, leg 1) In Madrid in 1962 he came close to the Brazilian concrete poetry of the Noigandres group and corresponded with Haroldo de Campos after reading *A Arte no Horizonte Provavel*, an experience described as fundamental to his formation and to his constructivist phase. (C13194, leg 1) In 1963, with other young artists including his first wife Elena Asins and Luis Lugan, he founded the Grupo Castilla 63 in Spain, aiming to break out of anonymity and enter the Madrid art scene. (C13195, leg 1) His first wife was the Spanish concrete artist Elena Asins, with whom he had a daughter. (C13196, leg 1) In 1966 he met the writer Ignacio Gomez de Liano and, with Asins and Lugan, joined the Cooperativa Artistica y Artesana (CPAA), living among musicians, writers and poets. (C13197, leg 1) In 1966 he held his first individual exhibition at the Galeria Nebli, Madrid -- though the Escritorio de Arte list records earlier solos (Melilla 1961, Bilbao 1966), so "first" is contested. (C13198, leg 1)

(2) Arrival in Brazil and the poem-objects, 1967-1976. He met the Brazilian artist Regina Silveira in Madrid in early 1967. (C13199, leg 1) He came to Brazil in 1967 as part of the Spanish delegation to the 9th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo, showing *Elementos Modulares Programados*, *Objeto Cibernetico*, *Cubos Programados de Vertice* and *Movel Programado*, all 1967; on an Itamaraty scholarship he moved to Rio de Janeiro and enrolled at the Escola Superior de Desenho Industrial (ESDI). (C13200, leg 1) At ESDI he was tutored by the art critic and poet Angel Crespo and came into contact with Silvia Steinberg, Antonio Manuel, Raymundo Collares and Cildo Meireles. (C13201, leg 1) In Sao Paulo he made the *Livro-Objeto* with the editor Julio Pacello, in screenprint with cut-outs, dated to the end of the 1960s (1968-69), with a poem by Augusto de Campos. (C13202, leg 1) He married Regina Silveira in February 1969; the couple shared life and work intensely for twenty years, until 1987, in Silveira's own words. (C13203, leg 1) Invited by rector Jose Enrique Arraras, he moved to Puerto Rico with Silveira in 1969 and taught visual language and plastic arts as artist in residence at the University of Puerto Rico (Mayaguez campus), where he lived for four years, until 1973. (C13204, leg 1) There he had advanced technical resources for photography, screenprint, offset and photomechanics, and met Robert Morris, Frank Stella, Roy Lichtenstein and Rafael Ferrer, and the theorists Barbara Rose, Germano Celant and Umbro Apollonio. (C13205, leg 1) In Mayaguez in 1972 he organised *Creacion, Creation...*, an international mail-art exhibition described as probably the first international mail-art exhibition, with about 80 artists from various parts of the world. (C13206, leg 1) He produced *On/Off* (1972-1974) in partnership with Silveira, a mail-art publication in three editions of envelopes containing reproductions of works by different artists, distributed free to art institutions and people in the milieu to question traditional circuits of art circulation. (C13207, leg 1) On returning to Brazil in 1973 and settling in Sao Paulo, he became a professor at FAAP; the Razuk timeline calls him professor titular at FAAP from 1973. (C13208, leg 1) He became a professor in the Department of Plastic Arts of ECA/USP, with the Razuk timeline giving 1974-1998 and PT Wikipedia saying he joined in 1976 -- see Conflicts. (C13209, leg 1) He worked as graphic designer of MAC/USP in 1974-1977, and collaborated with Walter Zanini, then professor at USP and director of MAC/USP, on redefining the museum's programme and its meaning for the public. (C13210, leg 1) With Zanini he organised the emblematic international mail-art exhibitions *Prospectiva* (1974) and *Poeticas Visuais* (1977) at MAC/USP. (C13211, leg 1) With Augusto de Campos he published the manipulable poem-books *Poemobiles* (1974) and *Caixa Preta* (1975). (C13212, leg 1) In 1976 he published *Re-Duchamp* in co-authorship with Augusto de Campos. (C13213, leg 1)

(3) Institution-building and theory, 1978-1990. In 1978, with Donato Ferrari, Walter Zanini and Regina Silveira, he founded the Centro de Artes Visuais ASTER in Sao Paulo, an independent school offering courses, meetings, lectures and exhibitions, which operated until the first semester of 1981. (C13214, leg 1) He was professor of the journalism course at PUC/SP in 1979-1983, and in 1983 defended his master's at PUC/SP, supervised by Lucia Santaella, with the dissertation *Videografia em Videotexto*. (C13215, leg 1) In 1985 he defended his doctorate at PUC/SP, also supervised by Santaella, with the thesis *Traducao Intersemiotica*, on transposition between systems for translating works into varied languages. (C13216, leg 1) He participated in the 16th Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1981 as curator of the mail-art exhibition, while Walter Zanini was curator-general. (C13217, leg 1) He organised *Arte pelo Telefone: Videotexto* (Museu da Imagem e do Som, 1982), the first videotext exhibition in Brazil. (C13218, leg 1) In 1983 he was curator of the 17th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo -- Novos Media, Videotexto, the new-media salon that introduced electronic art to Brazil. (C13219, leg 1) He completed a postdoctorate at the Universidad Complutense de Madrid in 1989-1990, titled *As imagens das Novas Tecnologias da Comunicacao*. (C13220, leg 1)

(4) Late career, 1992-2003. He was professor doutor in the Departamento de Multimeios of the Instituto de Artes da Unicamp in 1992-1997; ICAA instead associates him in the 1990s with the IEL-UNICAMP (Instituto de Estudos da Linguagem) -- see Conflicts. (C13221, leg 1) He was the first artist to become professor titular in Brazil, the highest post in the academic career at public universities. (C13222, leg 1) At ECA/USP he was responsible for creating the Multimidia e Intermidia habilitation in the Department of Plastic Arts, and, with Regina Silveira, Carmela Gross and other professors, created the postgraduate programme in Visual Arts there, including the Multimeios research line on art and technology. (C13223, leg 1) His theoretical publications include *Videografia em Videotexto* (1986), *Traducao Intersemiotica* (1987) and *Os Processos Criativos com os Meios Eletronicos: Poeticas Digitais* (1998), the last in collaboration with Monica Tavares. (C13224, leg 1) In 2001 he taught a course on interactions between image and text at Itau Cultural, Sao Paulo (listed as 2000 in the Razuk timeline -- see Conflicts). (C13225, leg 1) He married a third time, to the Portuguese lawyer Anabela Araujo, with whom he lived until his death in 2003. (C13226, leg 1)

(5) Afterlife. Posthumous exhibitions include *A Subversao dos Meios* at Itau Cultural, Sao Paulo (2003) and the retrospectives *Julio Plaza, o poetico e o politico* at Fundacao Vera Chaves Barcellos, Porto Alegre (2013) and *Julio Plaza, industria poetica* at MAC/USP, Sao Paulo (2013-2014, curated by Cristina Freire). (C13227, leg 1) His works are held by, among others, MAM-SP, MAC/USP, the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo, the Colecao Itau Cultural, MAB/FAAP and the Fundacao Vera Chaves Barcellos. (C13228, leg 1)

The through-line of the life is not a style but a migration of the same question: how does a sign get from one body into another -- from a page into a fold, from a fold into an envelope, from an envelope into a telephone screen, from a screen into a hologram, from a hologram into a pixel? Plaza's answer was always to build the new body himself, and then to build the institution -- the exhibition, the school, the Biennial salon, the postgraduate programme -- that would let other people use it. (C13361)

## M1 -- Fold angle is the reading state

*Objetos* (W01) is an illustrated book with a poem by Augusto de Campos and ten cut-and-folded screen prints, published in Sao Paulo by Julio Pacello in 1968-69, copy VI of 30, now at Princeton (GAX 2021-in process). (C13237, leg 2) The origin story is the mechanism's origin story: de Campos was brought in to write a critical essay as the foreword to Plaza's book project, but instead wrote concrete poetry integrated into Plaza's moving shapes; published as *Objetos*, these were in fact the first poemobiles. (C13238, leg 2) The essay became a poem because the shapes would not sit still for criticism -- the same move Augusto made in the other direction in #664, where the criticism became the poem.

What matters mechanically is that the pages are folded and placed inside a colourful slipcase, allowing exhibition of individual sheets alone or in multi-layer groups; although usually photographed as perfectly symmetrical geometric forms, they can be seen at a variety of stages of opening or closing, from a variety of angles. (C13239, leg 2) The work is therefore a hand-operated state machine: fold angle = reading state. Plaza's own gloss, quoted by Princeton: "There was the idea of breaking with plastic form and advancing into space"; models were developed on stiff white paper, eventually screen-printed in bright primary colours.

**Takeaway:** a fold is a state, not a decoration. If the piece can be read the same way half-open and fully open, the fold is doing nothing; the fold must change what the words say, not just what they look like.


## M2 -- The tent/pulley sheet

*Poemobiles* (W02) is thirteen fold-out poems and a cardboard box, offset printing and perforation on paper, published by the author in Sao Paulo in 1974 (Getty 92-B21581), the poems dating 1969-74. (C13240, leg 2) The Getty checklist corroborates the thirteen-poem count: *Poemobiles*, *Open (Abre)*, *Abre (Open)*, *Rever*, *Viva vaia*, *Luzmente mudacor*, *Reflete*, *Incomunicable*, *Subverter*, *Ovo Voo*, *Um Possivel (A possible) Impossivel (Impossible)*, *Luxo / Lixo*, and *Chance/Words/Change/Worlds*. (C13272, leg 2)

The mechanism, per Leubsdorf's close reading, is this: each page is crafted out of cardboard, folded down the middle, and tents up into an architectural structure supporting a design or series of words; the word "mobiles" invites readers to imagine the poems swinging like a child's mobile; the board works almost as a pulley system pushing text upwards and downwards in space, letters disguised and revealed as the board opens and closes; lighting and shadows cast by the folded sections make focus sway or retract; some triangular constructions animate like a mouth opening and closing. Viewers are invited to manipulate the objects by turning them in their hands to view them from alternate vantage points. Text uses simple, eye-catching primary colours (red, yellow, blue). (C13240, leg 2, continued)

A flat page becomes a small building that breathes. The tent is not a container for the words; it is the machine that moves them. The pulley image is the precise one: the fold is a rope, the text is the load, and opening the sheet hauls the words up into space where they can be seen from a new angle -- and closing it lowers them back down, hiding some letters and showing others.

**Takeaway:** a page that tents up is a machine, not a pop-up. The fold must push text through space and hide and reveal letters as it moves; a shape that only stands up is a card, not a poemobile.


## M3 -- No fixed sequence: the reader composes the book

*Poemobiles* (W02) consists of a grouping of unbound folios with no discernible sequence set by the author or artist, so a single book can be read as an individual object or within various groupings, creating more chances to inspire new meanings. (C13241, leg 2) There is no spine, no page numbers that matter, no first poem and no last poem. The box holds thirteen sheets; the reader takes them out, puts them in an order, changes the order, and the book is different each time.

This is the same move Plaza made with the mail (M8) and with the fold (M1), stated at the level of the whole book: the author surrenders the sequence, and the sequence becomes the reader's work. The poem is not the thirteen sheets; the poem is the thirteen sheets plus the order the reader gives them. A reader who shuffles the folios is not misreading the book; they are reading it the way it was built to be read.

**Takeaway:** an unbound book earns its looseness when the order changes the meaning. If every shuffle reads the same, the folios are loose for convenience; if a new order makes a new poem, the reader is the co-author the binding refused to be.


## M4 -- One letter flips the valence

*Viva Vaia* (W04) is a three-dimensional folding poem from *Poemobiles*, 1974, featuring red geometric shapes spelling the words "Viva" and "Vaia" (hurrah and hissing). (C13243, leg 2) The same word flips valence by one letter -- viva is cheer, vaia is boo and hiss -- as the fold opens. Praise and jeer are the two faces of one sheet.

*Abre* (W03) does the same work with the crease instead of the letter: a colour photolithograph of 1969, a three-dimensional folding poem with lines of text in the shape of a diamond featuring the words "open", "close", "yellow", "reopen", "blue" and "red". (C13242, leg 2) Leubsdorf's close reading: a diamond shape reads "OPEN" on the top and bottom; the two outermost words read "YELL" to the left and "LLOW" on the right, so the eye is invited to connect yellow / yell / low across the centre crease, and the crease makes the eye roam.

The mechanism is a hinge with two meanings. In *Viva Vaia* the hinge is a single letter -- change V to A in the middle of the word and the crowd turns. In *Abre* the hinge is the crease itself -- the word "yellow" is split across it, and the reader has to reassemble it by looking across the fold. Either way the fold is not illustrating the poem; the fold IS the poem's argument, that praise and insult, yellow and yell, are one sheet apart.

**Takeaway:** one letter is enough to turn a crowd. A piece that flips a word's meaning by opening a fold has the mechanism; a piece that merely shows two words side by side has the topic and not the machine.


## M5 -- The word changes colour mid-spelling

In *Poemobiles* (W02) words may spell "yellow" but shift to an alternate colour and word mid-spelling, in simple eye-catching primary colours (red, yellow, blue). (C13240, leg 2) *Luzmente mudacor* (W06) compresses the same move into one portmanteau: the Getty checklist glosses it as "Luz = light, mente = mind, muda = mute and mutate, cor = color." (C13245, leg 2) Four morphemes -- light, mind, mute-mutate, colour -- recombine as the fold is worked. No sheet-level mechanism description for *Luzmente mudacor* was found, hence its B grade; the gloss itself is the mechanism in miniature.

The watchable behaviour is a word betraying its own spelling halfway through. The eye starts reading "yellow" in yellow and finds itself reading something else in red; the colour change is not emphasis, it is a change of word. This is Augusto's colour-as-voice (M1 of #664) put through Plaza's fold: the colour still carries the voice, but now the fold decides when the voice changes, and the change happens mid-word, in the reader's hands.

**Takeaway:** colour is a verb, not an adjective, when it changes the word mid-spelling. If the piece recolours a whole word, it is highlighting; if the colour change interrupts the spelling and hands the reader a different word, it is the mechanism.


## M6 -- The multi-code object: print plus sound plus screen

*Caixa Preta* (W07) is a black box that is print plus vinyl: a lavish portfolio of flat and three-dimensional concrete poems (Getty 92-B22192), published in Sao Paulo by Edicoes Invencao in 1975, with a record containing Caetano Veloso's spoken versions of "dias dias dias" (from *Poetamenos*) and "o pulsar" (from *Stelegramas*). (C13246-C13247, leg 2) The poem arrives in two codes at once -- the printed fold and the spoken voice -- and the box holds both. The dating is adopted as 1975 by majority (Getty, Monoskop, ICAA, Augusto's official biography, Escritorio de Arte); the 1974 date traces to a single UbuWeb interview that contradicts itself, body 1974 against its own footnote 1975. (C13268-C13269, leg 2; summary correction 3)

The same impulse later becomes the videotext terminal. *Arte pelo Telefone: Videotexto* (W14) was the first videotext exhibition in Brazil (MIS, Sao Paulo, 1982). (C13254, leg 2) Brazil had bought the rights to France Telecom's Teletel system and branded it *Videotexto*; the Minitel pixel was divided into six parts and was vertical, a hybrid of ASCII and a proprietary French system; Plaza's exhibitions combined videotex with television and radio. (C13274, leg 2) At the 17th Biennial in 1983, curator Walter Zanini invited Plaza to curate *Arte e Videotexto* (W15); the works were presented on Videotexto monitors, and participating Aster students recall TV sets where "people could go there and press a button and the Videotexto work would begin to play." (C13255, leg 2)

The watchable behaviour is the wait: the poem does not start until the viewer presses a button, and then it plays in chunky six-part pixels on a TV set. From the black box with its record to the telephone screen that waits for a button, the procedure is the same -- the poem is split across two codes, and the reader has to operate the second one.

**Takeaway:** a poem in two codes earns both of them when the second code cannot be skipped. If the record is a bonus and the print stands alone, it is an appendix; if the poem needs the voice, or the button, to begin, the second code is the mechanism.


## M7 -- Intersemiotic translation with a key

*Traducao Intersemiotica* (W18) is Plaza's 1987 book (Editora Perspectiva; reissued 2003), his PUC/SP doctoral thesis of 1985, supervised by Lucia Santaella. (C13258, leg 2; C13216, leg 1) Plaza defines three types of translation: one of iconic root (transcreation), one of index root (transposition) and one of symbolic root (transcoding); translation is "thought by signs", a critical-creative practice that seeks to project the original as difference in a new form. (C13259, leg 2)

The worked example inside the book is the mechanism in drawable form: Plaza did a line-art interpretation of Haroldo de Campos's concrete poem "nascemorre" (1956) as an instance of *traducao simbolica*, visualising the poem's fabric by triangulation, with assigned graphemes for the repeating verbal segments (nasce, morre, renasce, remorre, desnasce, desmorre); the translation would only be comprehensible with a "lexical key as a symbolic code" (Plaza 99-104). (C13260, leg 2) The diagram is a triangle that must be decoded with its key. The "nascemorre" diagram is known only through Perrone's description of Plaza's book, not from Plaza's pages -- no copy of the book was read cover to cover this run, and the Depth line says so.

This is the theory behind every mechanism in the study, stated as a procedure. The fold (M1), the tent (M2), the shuffle (M3), the flip (M4), the colour change (M5), the second code (M6) -- each is a translation of a sign from one body into another, and each needs a key: the fold angle, the order, the crease, the colour, the button. Plaza's triangle makes the key explicit, which is why it is the mechanism and not the theory chapter.

**Takeaway:** a translation is watchable when the viewer can see the original and the new form at once and needs the key to get from one to the other. If the key is hidden, it is a puzzle; if the key is printed beside the diagram, it is a lesson in how signs move.


## M8 -- The mail network is the medium

*Prospectiva '74* (W11) and *Poeticas Visuais* (W12) were the two emblematic international mail-art exhibitions Plaza organised with Walter Zanini at MAC/USP (1974 and 1977); the Tate Archive holds the *Poeticas Visuais* catalogue, 30 pages and an envelope, with an introduction by Zanini, a text by Plaza, a list of participants, and illustrations of some of their work. (C13251-C13252, leg 2) The Mail Art exhibition Plaza created for the XVI Sao Paulo Biennial (W13, 1981) helped legitimise mail art, although it also "diluted" it; Plaza noted the "anarchic, excessive, and contradictory nature of Mail Art when, in its desperate bid to be considered serious art, it enters the enemy terrain of official institutions". Walter Zanini was curator-general of that Biennial. (C13253, leg 2)

The mechanism is that the exhibition is the circulation: works arrive by post and are exhibited as received, and the show grows by post. The envelope is the gallery. Plaza had already practised it in Puerto Rico: *Creacion, Creation...* (1972), probably the first international mail-art exhibition, with about 80 artists; and *On/Off* (1972-1974) with Regina Silveira, three editions of envelopes containing reproductions of works by different artists, distributed free to art institutions and people in the milieu to question traditional circuits of art circulation. (C13206-C13207, leg 1)

The watchable behaviour is the arrival itself: a show that is visibly incomplete on its first day and fuller on its last, because the mail keeps coming. The risk Plaza named is real -- the moment the network enters the institution, the institution tames it -- and the piece has to keep the taming visible, the envelopes still addressed, the stamps still on, the circulation still the work.

**Takeaway:** a network is a medium when the work cannot exist without the arrivals. If the pieces could have been hung without the post, the mail is a theme; if the show is empty until the envelopes come, the post is the mechanism.


## The aesthetic logic: the sign that keeps changing its body

Julio Plaza's aesthetic logic is a single sentence that holds across thirty-five years: a sign is not its body, and its body is the artist's to change. In 1967 the body is a programmed modular object. In 1968 it is a cut-and-folded screen print. In 1974 it is thirteen unbound folios in a cardboard box. In 1975 it is a black box with a record inside. In 1981 it is an envelope arriving by post. In 1982 it is six-part pixels on a television that waits for a button. In 1986 it is a hologram, and a satellite photograph enlarged until its pixels show. At no point does he abandon the sign for the image, and at no point does he keep the sign in the same body for long. That is the difference between him and the orthodox concrete poets who stayed with the page -- and the difference between him and Augusto, too: Augusto widened what the poem could be made of; Plaza engineered the bodies it could be made into, and then built the institutions that would circulate them.

The second half of the logic is the reader's hands. In every mechanism the reader does physical work: opening and closing a fold (M1), turning a tent in the hands (M2), shuffling folios (M3), flipping a sheet to turn praise into jeer (M4), following a colour change mid-word (M5), pressing a button to start a screen (M6), decoding a triangle with a key (M7), posting an envelope and waiting for the reply (M8). The poem is never finished before the reader arrives, and in Plaza's work it is never finished before the reader touches it. That is why the glass-factory scar matters -- not as biography, but as method: he learned materials with his hands, and his poems are made to be handled.

The third half -- and there is a third half, because the logic has three terms -- is translation. Plaza spent his academic life defining it: iconic transcreation, indexical transposition, symbolic transcoding, "thought by signs", projecting the original as difference in a new form. (C13259, leg 2) Translation is the same operation as the fold: take a sign out of one body and put it into another, and see what survives. *Objetos* is a translation of a critical essay into a poem; *Poemobiles* is a translation of a book into a box of loose sheets; *Caixa Preta* is a translation of print into print-plus-voice; *Imagemega* is a translation of a satellite photograph into a megaword. The sign survives each translation by changing, and that is the whole poetics. (C13365)


## The network: the triangle's engineer, and the two adjacents who built the rooms

The core network is the Noigandres triangle, seen now from its engineer. Augusto de Campos is the central collaborator: the *Livro-Objeto* (1968-69) carried a poem by Augusto, and together they made *Poemobiles* (1974), *Caixa Preta* (1975) and *Re-Duchamp* (1976); ICAA describes Plaza producing graphic versions of several of Augusto's concrete poems, transforming printed text into three-dimensional poetic objects. (C13229, leg 1) Haroldo de Campos was the first contact: Plaza corresponded with Haroldo from Madrid in 1962 after reading *A Arte no Horizonte Provavel*; Haroldo, Augusto and Decio Pignatari were the three Noigandres poets who founded Brazilian concrete poetry. (C13230, leg 1) Decio Pignatari was, with the Campos brothers, one of the three founders, and a tireless champion of Charles Sanders Peirce's theoretical works in Brazil; Plaza's creative connection with Pignatari and the Campos brothers nurtured his semiological approach, and Plaza later collaborated with Pignatari, Augusto de Campos and Wagner Garcia on *Triluz* (MIS, Sao Paulo, 1986). (C13231, leg 1) No direct joint work between Plaza and Haroldo or between Plaza and Pignatari was found in the sources read -- the connection is group-level, through the triangle, and is stated as such. (C13366)

Walter Zanini (1925-2013), art historian and director of MAC/USP (1963-1978), was Plaza's great collaborator and institutional partner: Plaza worked with him on redefining the museum's programme, on its graphic design (1974-1977), on *Prospectiva* (1974) and *Poeticas Visuais* (1977), and as mail-art curator under Zanini as curator-general of the 16th Bienal (1981); Zanini also wrote two essays on Plaza's work. (C13232, leg 1) Regina Silveira (b. 1939) is the second adjacent who changed the medium: Plaza met Silveira in Madrid in early 1967, married her in February 1969, and shared life and work with her for twenty years until 1987; they taught together in Puerto Rico (1969-1973) and at FAAP and ECA/USP in Sao Paulo from the mid-1970s, co-founded ASTER (1978), and made *On/Off* (1972-1974) together. (C13233, leg 1)

Further out: Moyses Baumstein, the holography collaborator (*Triluz* 1986, *Idehologia* 1987); Donato Ferrari, ASTER co-founder (1978); Jorge Glusberg and the CAYC in Buenos Aires, where Plaza first showed in July 1975 and then regularly; Monica Tavares, co-author of *Os Processos Criativos* (1998). (C13351, leg 3) Waldemar Cordeiro -- NO direct relationship is established in the sources read: Cordeiro died in 1973, the year Plaza settled permanently in Sao Paulo, and the two are grouped only posthumously, as pioneers of Brazilian digital/computer art, in the Itau Cultural exhibition *Cinetico_Digital* (2005). Do not assert a personal Plaza-Cordeiro relationship without a new source. (C13234, leg 1) Ferreira Gullar, Ronaldo Azeredo, Cid Campos and Vilem Flusser are likewise group-level only; no direct documented link was found for any of them. (C13351, leg 3)
