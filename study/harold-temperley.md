## M1. The documentary anchor

"The drafts of Canning's official despatches in the Record Office... are a full, final and authoritative exposition of policy. They have been freely used and are frequently quoted in the text." (Life of Canning, introduction, pp. 12-13.) The despatch, not the memoir, is the ground truth, and everything else is checked against it, and the checking is the method, because a historian who starts from the despatch has already decided that policy is what was written down in the office, not what was remembered afterwards in the club, and the despatch is the piece, and the piece must show the paper arriving first, the handwriting, the seal, the file mark, and the memoir arriving second and being measured against it, and the measuring being the history.

**Takeaway:** start from the despatch, not the memoir, and the piece should show the paper first and the memory second.

## M2. The balance

"I have attempted, as Canning himself would say, 'by absolute neutrality to hold the balance between the two conflicting principles' of eulogy and depreciation." (Life of Canning, introduction, p. 12.) Neutrality is staged as a Canningite virtue, not a modern one, and the staging matters, because a biographer who borrows his subject's own phrase for his method has already admitted that the method is part of the subject, and the borrowing is the piece, and the piece must show the balance itself, a scale with eulogy on one side and depreciation on the other, and the needle held still in the middle, and the stillness being the argument.

**Takeaway:** hold the balance in the subject's own words, and the piece should show the scale, not the verdict.

## M3. The biographer's disease

"Especially have I endeavoured to avoid the familiar disease of the biographer, which places scales on his eyes and induces him to believe his hero the sole life and source of the age." (Life of Canning, introduction, p. 12.) The cure is stated before the narrative begins, and the stating is the mechanism, because a biographer who names his own disease on page twelve has given the reader a test to apply to every page that follows, and the test is the piece, and the piece must show the scales on the eyes, the hero growing until he fills the whole frame, and then the frame widening again to show the age around him, and the widening being the cure.

**Takeaway:** name the disease before the narrative, and the piece should show the frame widening past the hero.

## M4. Sympathy plus impartiality

"In the study of personal character -- especially a character so complex and subtle as that of Canning -- sympathy is as essential to the understanding as impartiality is necessary to the judgment." (Life of Canning, introduction, p. 13.) Two instruments, used at different moments: sympathy to understand, impartiality to judge, and the separation is the mechanism, because a historian who uses both but never at the same time has solved the problem the biographer's disease poses without pretending it does not exist, and the two instruments are the piece, and the piece must show the same man twice, once in warm light and once in cold, and the reader being asked to hold both pictures at once, and the holding being the biography.

**Takeaway:** use sympathy to understand and impartiality to judge, never both at once, and the piece should show the man twice.

## M5. The permanent-work test

"For Canning alone among English statesmen can we make the double claim that his own work has been permanent and indestructible, whilst his visions of the future have in some measure approached to reality." (Life of Canning, introduction, p. 10.) A statesman is judged by whether the work outlives the man, and the test is the mechanism, because a claim of permanence can be checked against the century that followed, and the checking is the piece, and the piece must show the work standing after the man is gone, the visions arriving one by one in the decades after his death, and the standing being the verdict, and the verdict being that the work, not the life, is the subject.

**Takeaway:** judge the statesman by whether the work outlives him, and the piece should show the work standing after the man.

## M6. The platitude test

Canning's principles "have descended from novelty to platitude"; "great honour should be paid to their earliest upholder, to the man who first foresaw and promoted the growth of national liberty on the Continent of Europe, when a league of despots had a real existence." (Life of Canning, introduction, p. 9.) Success erases the author, and the erasure is the mechanism, because a principle that has become a platitude no longer looks like anyone's achievement, and the historian's job is to restore the novelty, to show the moment when the platitude was a heresy, and the restoration is the piece, and the piece must show the phrase first as a commonplace on everyone's lips, and then winding back to the one man who said it first, when the league of despots was real, and the winding back being the history.

**Takeaway:** restore the novelty inside the platitude, and the piece should show the commonplace winding back to the one man.

## M7. The single thread through the labyrinth

"The only method which offers a real chance of success is to trace the fortunes of a specific race among the other nationalities of that area, and to relate it closely to the history of the Serbians." (History of Serbia, introduction, p. 1.) One nation is the thread, and the Balkans are the labyrinth, and the thread is the mechanism, because a region too tangled to survey can still be walked if one line is followed through it, and the walking is the piece, and the piece must show the labyrinth first, the whole knotted map of the Balkans, and then the single red thread of Serbia running through it, and the thread being followed from room to room, and the following being the book.

**Takeaway:** walk the labyrinth on one thread, and the piece should show the thread running through the knot.

## M8. Geography as destiny

"The principles of strategy are eternal" and geography has affected diplomacy in Serbia "in a strikingly similar way" across the centuries. (History of Serbia, preface; quoted in AHR 23, p. 135.) "Man can take liberties with nature... until he could tunnel mountains, dry up the marshes, and render rivers navigable, the unity of the Jugo-Slav race was a geographical dream." (Introduction, p. 2, OCR garbled but legible in substance.) The mountains and the rivers decide before the ministers do, and the deciding is the mechanism, because a diplomacy that repeats itself across the centuries is not repeating the men, it is repeating the ground, and the ground is the piece, and the piece must show the same pass, the same river, the same marsh, with different centuries marching through it, and the marching being the same every time, and the sameness being the argument.

**Takeaway:** let the ground repeat what the men only imitate, and the piece should show the same pass in different centuries.

## M9. Holding the balance between conflicting opinions

"To hold the balance between their conflicting opinions has been my aim, others will judge of its success." (History of Serbia, preface.) The same balance as M2, now applied to whole national historiographies rather than to one man's reputation, and the widening is the mechanism, because a balance that worked for eulogy and depreciation must now hold Serbian, Austrian, Russian and British accounts at once, and the holding is the piece, and the piece must show four histories of the same war laid side by side, each true in its own telling, and the scale held level between them, and the level being the book.

**Takeaway:** hold the balance between whole historiographies, and the piece should show four histories of one war.

## M10. Travel as method

"Visiting Southeastern Europe almost every year after 1905"; "a special affinity for Serbia, learning the language and publishing a history of that country." (Roberts review.) The preface: "This study is the fruit of some years of travel in the Near East." The historian goes to the place before he writes it, and the going is the mechanism, because a history written from the road knows the passes and the marshes of M8 from the feet, not from the map, and the feet are the piece, and the piece must show the traveller arriving year after year, the same road, the language learned word by word, and the book growing out of the travelling, and the travelling being the research.

**Takeaway:** go to the place before writing it, and the piece should show the road walked year after year.

## M11. The published-document edition as history

British Documents on the Origins of the War: the history IS the documents, selected and arranged with introductions and notes, published as the government's official version. Gooch's appointment "was designed to give the project a credible independence" (Wikipedia). The editor's judgment is exercised in selection, not in narrative, and the selection is the mechanism, because a history made of documents still has an author, and the author is the man who chose which papers to print and which to leave in the file, and the choosing is the piece, and the piece must show the archive first, a mountain of paper, and then the editor's hand taking one sheet and setting it on the pile, and the pile growing into eleven volumes, and the growing being the history.

**Takeaway:** the editor's judgment lives in the selection, and the piece should show the hand choosing the papers.

## M12. The resignation threat as editorial tactic

"To get their own way, they had to employ tactical resignation threats", constrained financially and by the fifty-year rule on document release. (Wikipedia.) Editing as negotiation with the Foreign Office, and the negotiation is the mechanism, because an editor who cannot compel the archive must threaten to walk away from it, and the threat is the piece, and the piece must show the editor at the table with the officials, the letter of resignation drafted and undrafted in the same afternoon, and the documents released one more year early, and the release being the victory.

**Takeaway:** edit by negotiation, and the piece should show the resignation letter drafted and undrafted.

## M13. The companion-study pairing

Serbia was written "as a companion study" to Frederic the Great and Kaiser Joseph, "in a similar way" -- the same method (diplomacy through geography and despatches) applied to a different century. (History of Serbia, preface.) A method is proved by being run twice, and the running twice is the mechanism, because a method that works only on one subject is a coincidence, and a method that works on two is a method, and the proof is the piece, and the piece must show the same instrument applied to two different centuries, the eighteenth and the nineteenth, and the same shape coming out both times, and the sameness being the proof.

**Takeaway:** prove the method by running it twice, and the piece should show the same instrument on two centuries.

## M14. The textbook division of labour

Europe in the Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries, 4th edition note: "Professor Grant assumes all responsibility for any views expressed on the period... between the years 1897-1914. Professor Temperley assumes responsibility for opinions expressed on the period from the outbreak of the War." (Wellcome digitised text.) Authorship is split by period, openly, in print, and the openness is the mechanism, because a textbook that says who is responsible for which years has already refused the fiction of the single author, and the refusal is the piece, and the piece must show the book cut down the middle at 1914, one half signed Grant and the other half signed Temperley, and the cut being visible, and the visibility being the honesty.

**Takeaway:** split the authorship openly by period, and the piece should show the book cut at 1914.

## M15. The journal as gate

Founder and first editor of the Cambridge Historical Journal (1923). He accepted Plumb's article on the 1689 Convention Parliament elections; Butterfield, taking over as editor, replied "I'm personally disappointed" and urged Plumb to "take much more trouble" (Cannadine, PBA 124, p. 275). The journal is where a young historian is made or unmade, and the gate is the mechanism, because the same article can be accepted by one editor and discouraged by the next, and the difference is the man at the desk, and the desk is the piece, and the piece must show the manuscript arriving, the acceptance, and then the second letter, the disappointment, the urging to take more trouble, and the young historian caught between two editors, and the catching being the profession.

**Takeaway:** the journal is the gate where a young historian is made or unmade, and the piece should show the two letters.

## M16. The "Special Periods"

Undergraduate teaching in four-year stages, "being brought closer to the beginnings of the Great War" (CHJ obituary). The syllabus itself walks forward in time toward 1914, and the walking is the mechanism, because a course that advances four years at a time has already decided that the war is the destination, and every period is a step toward it, and the steps are the piece, and the piece must show the students moving through the decades, 1789, then 1815, then 1848, then 1870, and the war getting closer with each step, and the closeness being the curriculum.

**Takeaway:** let the syllabus walk toward 1914, and the piece should show each period as a step closer to the war.

## M17. The research school

"His supervision of research, which made the Cambridge school distinguished in the study of nineteenth-century diplomatic history" (CHJ obituary). The school is a supervision practice, not a doctrine, and the practice is the mechanism, because a school that is made by supervision rather than by a book has no manifesto to quote, only a room where the pupils bring their drafts, and the room is the piece, and the piece must show the drafts arriving, the corrections, the despatches being read aloud, and the pupils leaving with the method in their hands, and the leaving being the school.

**Takeaway:** make the school a supervision room, not a doctrine, and the piece should show the drafts arriving.

## M18. The Junior Historians

"His prominence for a quarter of a century at the curious society of the 'Junior Historians' (historians who indeed had once been juniors, but favoured themselves with a receding age-limit, namely the age of Temperley himself)" (CHJ obituary). The joke is institutional: the society ages with its founder, and the ageing is the mechanism, because a society whose age limit recedes with one man has made the man the constitution, and the constitution is the piece, and the piece must show the juniors growing older year by year, the limit moving with them, and Temperley at the centre getting older too, and the moving being the joke, and the joke being the institution.

**Takeaway:** let the society age with its founder, and the piece should show the age limit receding.

## M19. The congress presidency

President of the International Congress of Historians; "through a continuous outpouring of scholarly works, Temperley was an influential figure in the historical profession in the 1930s" (Fair, via Google Books). Influence exercised through the international meeting, not only the book, and the meeting is the mechanism, because a historian who presides over the congress has already decided that the profession is a place where men meet, not only a shelf where books sit, and the meeting is the piece, and the piece must show the hall, the delegates, the papers read aloud in four languages, and the president keeping order, and the order being the influence.

**Takeaway:** exercise influence through the meeting, not only the book, and the piece should show the hall.

## M20. The diary as non-official record

"As a trained historian, he was aware of the importance of recording events as they unfolded, and regarded his diary as a means of preserving a non-official record." (Readings blurb for Otte's edition.) The diary runs June 1916 to 1939, "a more or less daily record" (Roberts review). The diary is the second archive, kept against the first, and the keeping is the mechanism, because a historian who writes the official documents by day and the unofficial diary by night has already decided that the record needs a shadow, and the shadow is the piece, and the piece must show the two books side by side, the despatch and the diary, the one saying what was decided and the other saying what was thought, and the two together being the history.

**Takeaway:** keep a second, unofficial archive beside the official one, and the piece should show the two books side by side.

## M21. The memorandum that coaches delegates

Of the memoranda prepared under his direction in military intelligence, Temperley noted with pleasure that they "supplied the groundwork of solid information to coach our delegates" (diaries p. 509, via Roberts). Scholarship written to be used at the conference table, and the use is the mechanism, because a memorandum that coaches a delegate has already crossed the line from the study to the negotiation, and the crossing is the piece, and the piece must show the paper leaving the War Office, travelling to Paris, and being opened on the table beside the map, and the delegate reading it before he speaks, and the reading being the scholarship.

**Takeaway:** write scholarship to be used at the table, and the piece should show the paper travelling to Paris.

## M22. The aphoristic aside

By 1917 he believed the League of Nations and Allied victory should be "Siamese twins" (diaries p. 228); he welcomed American intervention, believing "even if France made a separate peace, 'Anglo-Saxondom will war on to victory'" (p. 228). (Roberts review.) The diary entry does the arguing the monograph cannot, and the entry is the mechanism, because an aside in a diary can say in one line what a monograph would need a chapter to prove, and the line is the piece, and the piece must show the diary page, the two words joined at the hip, the twins, and the whole argument about the League and the victory hanging from a single image, and the hanging being the style.

**Takeaway:** argue in one image in the diary, and the piece should show the twins joined at the hip.

## M23. The sardonic institution-sketch

Of the House of Lords, "as an agency for impressing the Lower middle classes, it is really unsurpassed" (diaries p. 509). (Roberts review.) The same pen that writes the despatch history writes the gibe, and the gibe is the mechanism, because a historian who can see the Lords as a stage effect has already refused to take the institution at its own valuation, and the refusal is the piece, and the piece must show the chamber, the ermine, the ceremony, and then the audience below, the lower middle classes, suitably impressed, and the impressing being the whole function, and the function being the joke.

**Takeaway:** sketch the institution by its effect on its audience, and the piece should show the ceremony and the impressed.

## M24. The odyssey framing

Recalling October 1918 to April 1919, "nothing 'could have been better and finer for me than the Jugoslav Odyssey, with seventy beds in six months -- a free wild life and wanderings among simple children of nature'" (diaries p. 399). (Roberts review.) The field trip remembered as a Homeric episode, and the remembering is the mechanism, because a historian who calls six months of Balkan travelling an odyssey has already decided that the journey is the story, not the report that came out of it, and the journey is the piece, and the piece must show the seventy beds, the moving from village to village, the free wild life, and the wanderer coming home with the Balkans in his notebooks, and the wandering being the research.

**Takeaway:** remember the field trip as an odyssey, and the piece should show the seventy beds.

## M25. The Canning system as portable doctrine

Temperley's summary of Canning's policies, "which formed the basis of British foreign policy for decades": "non-intervention; no European police system; every nation for itself, and God for us all; balance of power; respect for facts, not for abstract theories; respect for treaty rights, but caution in extending them ... a republic is as good a member of the comity of nations as a monarch. 'England not Europe'. 'Our foreign policy cannot be conducted against the will of the nation.'" (Wikipedia, "George Canning", citing Temperley, The Foreign Policy of Canning, pp. 342, 381.) The doctrine is a list, and the list is the argument, and the list is the mechanism, because a foreign policy that can be written as a dozen short clauses can be carried in a minister's pocket and applied to any crisis, and the carrying is the piece, and the piece must show the list itself, clause by clause, each one a short sentence, and the clauses being read aloud at the cabinet table, and the reading being the policy.

**Takeaway:** write the doctrine as a portable list, and the piece should show the clauses read aloud at the table.

## M26. The un-Rankean eccentricities

Bentley's charge, via Haughton p. 661: Temperley, "rather than giving Butterfield a healthy helping of Rankean method, blemished the work with his un-Rankean eccentricities"; the teleological passages in Butterfield's The Peace Tactics were "simply the unfortunate hangover of Harold Temperley's influence" (Bentley pp. 61-62). The teacher's flaw survives in the pupil's first book, and the survival is the mechanism, because an influence that the pupil later repudiates is still visible in the pages he wrote before the repudiation, and the visibility is the piece, and the piece must show the first book with the teleology still in it, the ending already written into the beginning, and then the second book with it cut out, and the cutting being the graduation.

**Takeaway:** let the teacher's flaw survive visibly in the pupil's first book, and the piece should show the teleology cut out of the second.

## M27. The mild joke

"Much of the Whig Interpretation can be read as a mild joke against the man across the landing, and it would have been entirely characteristic of Butterfield to have enjoyed the mischief of making it." (Bentley p. 100, via Dahlstrom.) The pupil's demolition of Whig history is also a domestic joke at Peterhouse, and the domesticity is the mechanism, because a book that demolishes a whole school of history can also be a joke played across a landing, and the landing is the piece, and the piece must show the two sets of rooms, the tutor's and the pupil's, the door between them, and the book travelling across the landing like a note slipped under the door, and the slipping being the demolition.

**Takeaway:** let the demolition be a joke across a landing, and the piece should show the book slipped under the door.

## M28. The romantic-liberal confidence

"He also exhibited, from his Victorian upbringing, a great confidence in the rightness of his own country's liberal institutions (in the Gladstone-Acton mode), and constantly sought intervention in the realm of public affairs." (Fair, via Google Books.) The confidence is the engine: it sends him to the War Office, to Paris, to the League, and the sending is the mechanism, because a historian who believes his country's institutions are right will not stay in the library when the war comes, and the leaving is the piece, and the piece must show the library door opening, the historian walking out toward the War Office, the conference, the League, and the walking being the confidence, and the confidence being the life.

**Takeaway:** let confidence send the historian out of the library, and the piece should show the door opening.

## M29. The booming giants

Butterfield, later in life, on the ageing Temperley and Charles Webster dominating the Peterhouse Combination Room, "like booming giants, cumbersome and dangerous to crockery, bulging with warmth and good feeling, yet capable of overbearingness -- terrible lions if you trod on their tales [sic]". (Wikipedia, citing Butterfield's introduction to the 1966 reissue of The Foreign Policy of Canning, pp. xiii-xiv.) Presence as a historical force: the room bends around the man, and the bending is the mechanism, because a historian who dominates a room by presence alone has already shown that history is made by men in rooms, not only by documents in files, and the room is the piece, and the piece must show the Combination Room, the crockery, the two giants booming, and everyone else keeping a careful distance, and the distance being the respect, and the respect being the history.

**Takeaway:** show presence bending the room, and the piece should show the giants and the careful distance.

## M30. The unfinished trilogy

England and the Near East was planned in three volumes; only vol. 1, The Crimea (1936), appeared. (National Library of Ireland catalogue: "Vol. 1 of a planned 3 vol. work. No more published.") The late narrative history stops after one volume; the documents take over instead, and the stopping is the mechanism, because a trilogy that ends at volume one has already confessed that the editor has replaced the narrator, and the confession is the piece, and the piece must show the three empty slots on the shelf, the first filled and the other two bare, and the bare slots being the admission, and the admission being that the archive won.

**Takeaway:** leave the trilogy at one volume, and the piece should show the two bare slots on the shelf.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife

EARLY (1905-1917): the biographer of a statesman. Life of Canning (1905) is a single-man life built on Record Office despatches, with a programmatic introduction about neutrality, sympathy and the biographer's disease. Frederic the Great and Kaiser Joseph (1915) is the same method in the eighteenth century: one episode of war and diplomacy. History of Serbia (1917) widens the frame from the man to the nation: the book starts as nineteenth-century Serbia, then runs back to the medieval kingdoms because "the principles of strategy are eternal" and geography has affected diplomacy "in a strikingly similar way" (preface; AHR review). The AHR reviewer notes the shift and the cost: not a work from primary sources, no Serbian-language material, a popular history from western secondary sources -- but the pre-1800 treatment is the better part of the book.

MIDDLE (1919-1927): the man of affairs. War Office intelligence, Paris 1919, the Albanian boundary commission, Balfour at Geneva 1921; then the editor. A History of the Peace Conference of Paris (6 vols, 1920-24) turns the conference into an official history. The Foreign Policy of Canning (1925) is the mature statement: 636 pages, the statesman separated from the man ("we have for the first time a brilliant analysis, fully and admirably documented, of Canning's system of policy" -- TLS, quoted in the CUP advertisement). Europe in the Nineteenth Century with Grant (1927) is the textbook turn: the scholar becomes the syllabus.

LATE (1927-1939): the editor as historian. British Documents on the Origins of the War (11 vols in 13 parts, 1926/27-1938) dominates the last decade: the history is the documents, selected, arranged and introduced, under a fifty-year rule, with tactical resignation threats to get his way (Wikipedia). The Crimea book (1936) is the return to narrative -- volume 1 of a planned 3-volume England and the Near East, never finished. The Foundation of British Foreign Policy (1938) and A Century of Diplomatic Blue Books (1938), both with Penson, are the same editorial instinct in anthology form: documents old and new, "a striking continuity of ideas in British diplomacy, despite opposed parties and even opposing policies" (Routledge blurb). He dies Master of Peterhouse, a year into the mastership, on 11 July 1939 -- seven weeks before the war whose origins he had spent a decade documenting.

AFTERLIFE: the documents outlived the man, and the joke outlived the documents. The Foreign Policy of Canning was the book Neville Chamberlain read "as he was formulating plans for a settlement of the Czechoslovakian problem in 1938" (Fair); Wikipedia says Temperley "influenced" Chamberlain's European foreign policy including appeasement and Munich, cited to David Reynolds, Summits (2009), which was not read this leg, so the study records the claim as Fair's and Wikipedia's, not as its own. The diaries waited seventy-five years for their editor: An Historian in Peace and War, ed. T. G. Otte (2014), xiii + 592 pp. And the Whig Interpretation, the book that demolished him, is still read as the mild joke Bentley found in it: the tutor across the landing, demolished by his pupil, remembered because the pupil's joke needed a target. The arc: from the life of one minister, to the life of one nation, to the archive itself as the work. The mechanism that stays constant is the document as authority; what changes is the scale of the document -- despatch, then dossier, then the eleven volumes.

## Aesthetic logic

Temperley's aesthetic is the despatch made beautiful: the belief that a well-chosen document, set in the right order, is itself the history, and that the historian's art is the arrangement, not the adornment. The early books wear the art openly -- the balance, the disease named in advance, the platitude restored to novelty, the single thread through the labyrinth -- because the young biographer must show his method before he can use it. The middle books hide the art in the scale: the 636-page Canning, the six-volume Peace Conference history, the textbook, where the method is the size of the undertaking. The late books drop the art altogether and let the documents stand: eleven volumes, two anthologies, one unfinished narrative, because the editor has decided that selection is enough, and that a list of clauses (M25) or a shelf of blue books is a more honest argument than a chapter. The through-line is the confidence of M28, the romantic-liberal belief that his country's institutions were right, which is why the work never doubts its own ground: the despatch is authoritative, the balance can be held, the document can be trusted, and the only question is which documents to print. The joke of M27 is the crack in that confidence, and the crack is the point: the pupil who believed none of it, who thought the teleology was a hangover and the teaching hardly deserved the name, wrote the book that outlived the master's eleven volumes, and did it as a joke across a landing. Temperley's aesthetic logic is the archive as the work; Butterfield's answer is that the archive is only the material, and the joke is the work. The study keeps both, because the tension between them is the history of Cambridge historiography in one landing.
