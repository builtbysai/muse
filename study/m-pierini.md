## The evolution: Genuary grids to bitcrush, with the fringe constant

January 2026 is the Genuary month, and the two verified Genuary pieces set the terms. Day 6, "Lights on/off" (OpenProcessing sketch "On Off", created 5 January): a 7x7 grid of white-outlined squares on dark grey, each holding a white ring, some rings "on" with glowing RGB chromatic-aberration offsets. The prompt was "make something that changes when you switch on or off the 'digital' lights", and the answer is a grid with a binary state and a fringe. Day 30, "Accordion" (posted 30 January, sketch created 27 January): a cream field, an animated GIF morphing between a plain grey 3D cube and a large hexagon of stacked rainbow chevron layers forming a folded accordion ribbon, with a pale grey triangular void at bottom left. The tweet reads "Its not a bug, its a feature" -- the "bug" is a layer misalignment in the animation, kept in as a feature. The glitch-as-feature stance is declared in the first month and never leaves. No other Genuary 2026 days were verified, and no posts at all were found between 30 January and 2 March -- a February gap that may be a real pause or an indexing gap, and the study does not decide (C1623).

Spring (March-May) is the grid's expansion: isometric primary-colour cube tiles (2 March), the Molecules lattice (31 March), the In and Out grid where circles jump in and out of holes (14 April), a neon slot machine (16 April), rows of moon discs in phases (5 May), a black plus-sign grid with rainbow fringes (14 May), and Board Bends (26 May), a black-and-pale-mint checkerboard with a circle of rainbow concentric squares moving across it. The palette settles here: black field, cream field, pale mint, and the rainbow fringe. Summer (June-July) is the illusion turn: the 9 June checkerboard that "changes once you focus on one square" (a replier's words), circles morphing into squares (23 June), black discs with rainbow glitch squares and crescents (25 June, 7 July). Then a summer quiet -- nothing found between 7 July and 15 September -- and autumn (September-November) is the glitch turn: petal/scale flowers with chromatic patches (15 September), moire rectangles that make viewers dizzy (8 October), two rainbow half-moons (12 October), then the bitcrush pair (13 and 15 October), the bubbles video (20 October), the "follow" loop with its invisible loop point (27 October), the diagonal cross-hatch lattice (3 November), the 8-bit fall (12 November), and the split-screen bubbles video (15 November). The arc is real but modest: same grid, same two fields, same fringe -- the variation is in what the grid does (morphs, illusions, crushes, loops) rather than in what it is. The chromatic aberration is the one constant from the Genuary grid to the November split-screen, and it is the signature a replier named: "Such a unique style, instantly recognizable!" (wozo, on Bitcrusher).

**Takeaway:** M:PY's evolution is a deepening, not a widening. One system -- a black-or-cream grid, a binary state, an RGB fringe -- is put through a year's worth of operations, and the operations are the oeuvre.

## From the inside: the code

Nineteen sketches' p5.js source was read (see the Depth line for the method and the gaps). The code confirms the visual reading in some places, corrects it in others, and adds a layer the GIFs cannot show: these are instruments, not just animations.

The loop is a cosine returning to itself. Fourteen of the nineteen read advance one global clock by `t += TAU / (30 * N)` (five run it backwards, `t -=`, which is cosmetic since cos is even), with N in {2, 3, 4, 6} -- loop durations of 2, 3, 4, or 6 whole seconds at 30 fps. Three seconds is the commonest (8 of 14). All motion derives from cos(t) or cos(t - delay), so after one period every cosine is back where it started: the loop is periodic by construction, never a cut. The loop point is hidden by re-randomisation at the extreme: On Off re-rolls tile rotations when the rings re-centre; Accordion rotates 90 degrees and reverses its palette while folded into a plain cube; Push me re-runs its whole setup when the breathing square is at its largest; Round it re-rolls circle directions when the exiting circle is invisible; Moons re-orients each moon when the squash bottoms out; Froot Loops picks a new slide direction when the ring is home. The change always happens when the moving element is at its most invisible or neutral state -- that is the craft the DAEMONS reply noticed, stated in code.

Five of the nineteen have no cosine clock at all. Resolve is an endless random tile-slide machine (a row or column slides out and a new one in over a random 35-80 frames; it never repeats). Clock is a flip scheduler, not a loop: each bar re-rolls its on/off state when int(t - d) hits a multiple of 60, and no cos(t) appears anywhere. Have a seat is one-shot: 625 dots each slide a line home on a staggered timer, and after about 15 seconds every dot has finished and the canvas goes blank. Zen Garden and Repetition are static images (Repetition calls noLoop()). The study's original claim that every verified work moves is wrong (C1639).

The fringe is triple-draw, not a post-process. In On Off, Moons, and Etheral -- the three sketches read that use it -- the same form is drawn three times, once per channel (red, green, blue), under blendMode(ADD), with a per-channel phase offset: a time delay in On Off (dly = [0, 0.15, 0.3]) and Moons (dly = [-0.2, 0, 0.2]), a phase offset in Etheral (TAU/8, TAU/16, TAU/32). The channels are separated in time, and the time offset reads on screen as a spatial RGB fringe; where the three passes coincide the additive blend sums to white. The glow is a canvas-2D shadow (drawingContext.shadowBlur with shadowColor set to the channel colour), not a p5.js effect. But the fringe is a recurring technique, not the universal signature the study first claimed: 3 of the 19 sketches read use it, and the others get their glow and edge effects differently -- shadowBlur in the line's own colour (Toothpaste), background-coloured stroke rings (Round it), shadowColor shading (Grinder, Cheese, Inside us). The more universal signatures are the frame, the clock, and the seam-hiding re-roll (C1636).

The frame is a function, and it signs the work. Twelve of the nineteen have a boards() function drawing a flat frame, 1.5-3% of the width on each edge -- square corners, not rounded (C1630 corrects W02). In six of them (Grinder, Cheese, Etheral, Inside us, Drops, Moons) the frame carries a row of palette swatches in the bottom-right corner: the palette as a visible legend. The shading helper shadowColor() (hue -5, saturation and brightness shifts) appears in six sketches under two names (changeColor() in Toggle), copied verbatim between files.

Nine of the nineteen are mouse-playable, and the GIFs hide it. In Round it, Grinder, Cheese, Drops, Etheral, Academic, Clock, Inside us, and Toggle, the radial wave delay is scaled by mouseX (amm = map(mouseX, 0, width, 0, 0.005-0.5)): mouse at the left edge means all cells move in sync, mouse at the right edge means a full radial ripple. Moons and Froot Loops use a fixed 0.005 delay instead. The posted GIF is one fixed setting of an instrument the viewer can play (C1637). Seven sketches have a saveGif key ('s'), and the export durations match the loop periods (Round it "boob" 6s, Grinder "bez" 3s, Etheral "lati" 3s, Academic "acc" 3s, Clock "clock" 12s, Inside us "cheese" 3s -- misnamed, copied from the Cheese sketch -- Toggle "button" 3s); the two static pieces save PNGs instead. The X GIFs are saveGif exports from the live sketch (C1638).

The colour system is hardcoded arrays, never generated. Palettes are fixed hex lists, often several kept in the file with one active: Toothpaste holds seven named palettes (mypath, menthos, classic, brand, fresh, sundae, aesthetic -- the active one is 'menthos'; classic/brand are red/blue/white toothpaste brands, and the names are the joke); Grinder holds five arrays and picks one at random per load; Cheese, Inside us, and Zen Garden shuffle theirs. No sketch generates a rainbow from HSB math; the rainbow in Accordion is fifteen hardcoded pastel hex values. There is no noise() and no Perlin anywhere in nineteen sketches: randomness is unseeded random() for initial states and per-cycle re-rolls, and the motion itself is fully deterministic. The film grain in Accordion, Cheese, Zen Garden, and Repetition is a static texture -- one full-canvas image of random RGB pixels at alpha 22-25, generated once in setup and drawn over everything every frame -- not animated noise (C1634).

The code is full of small bugs and dead code, and some of it is the work. Accordion's chevron ribbon is built from translated copies of one hexagon face stepped by L/i, so the layers' edges never meet cleanly -- that is the visible misalignment the tweet calls a feature -- and on top of it the fourth chevron vertex uses this.x for its y-coordinate, a genuine x/y mixup that is invisible only because the canvas is square and the cube is centred (C1632). Have a seat calls addColorStop on the same gradient every frame, growing the stop list forever, and initialises two colours to a melting-face emoji as a placeholder sentinel. Moons draws nothing for the first half-cycle because the rotation angle is undefined until the first squash bottom, and calls boards() with a frame colour missing its #. Clock draws every tile twice with identical parameters. Inside us saves its GIFs as "cheese". Round it's inner-dot offset overshoots to seventeen times its range at the cycle peak, invisible because the circle has shrunk to zero at exactly that moment. These are not presented as flaws to fix; they are the texture of a morning practice, written fast and shipped as-is, and several of them are load-bearing accidents -- the overshoot that hides inside a vanishing circle, the bug that only works because the canvas is square.

One biographical note, stated at exactly the strength the evidence supports: the artist writes code comments in Italian. Moons carries "// trigger SOLO quando arriva a -1"; Zen Garden's palette comments are Italian colour names (porcellana, pesca cipriata, verde te matcha, azzurro nuvola, ciclamino polveroso, lilla opaco, grigio marmo); Clock names its off-colours coloriOFF. That suggests an Italian-speaking artist; it does not prove nationality or location, and the study does not upgrade it (C1640).

**Takeaway:** the code is the practice, stated plainly. A cosine clock, a frame, a re-roll at the seam, hardcoded palettes, no noise, and a mouse the GIFs never show. The visuals were the score; the code is the instrument.

## M1 -- The chromatic-aberration fringe

The signature mechanism, present in the posted record's most visible works and in the avatar itself. Forms carry red/cyan/blue (sometimes full-rainbow) offsets at their edges, as if the colour channels have slipped a few pixels apart. In "On Off" the "on" rings glow with RGB offsets; in the plus grid (14 May) the black pluses wear vertical rainbow bars; in the bitcrush pair the white discs fringe at the top; in the bubbles videos the discs carry rainbow edges and striped interiors. The fringe does two jobs at once: it marks the form's edge, and it makes the flat grid feel optical, like light through cheap glass. It is the one element that survives every palette change, and it is how a viewer recognises an M:PY piece before reading the caption. The code, where read, implements it as triple-draw with additive blending and a per-channel phase offset (On Off, Moons, Etheral -- see "From the inside: the code") -- but the code also scopes the claim: only 3 of the 19 sketches read use the fringe at all, and the other sketches get their glow and edge effects differently. The fringe is the most visible signature, not the most universal one (C1636).

**Takeaway:** the fringe is not decoration on the system; it is the system's voice. A grid without it would be anyone's grid. The code adds that the voice is one technique among several -- the frame, the clock, and the seam-hiding re-roll are the deeper constants.

## M2 -- The seamless loop

The works are loops, and the best of them hide the join. The 27 October "follow" piece -- two large overlapping circles with rainbow edges on a striped black field, a small black dot in the lower circle that follows -- drew the telling reply from DAEMONS: "the loop point on this one is invisible, I sat here waiting for it to restart and it just never did." The loop is the unit of the practice: a GIF or short video that returns to itself without a seam, so the watching never has to stop. The Moons video (5 May) cycles phases; the Accordion morphs cube to hexagon and back; the bubbles cross the divide and return. The code shows how the join is hidden: the loop is a cosine returning to itself over 2, 3, 4, or 6 whole seconds, and the re-randomisation that would betray the restart happens at the cycle extreme, when the moving element is at its most invisible (see "From the inside: the code"). But the code also corrects the study's original claim that every verified work moves and loops: Zen Garden and Repetition are static images, Have a seat plays once and ends blank, Resolve never repeats, and Clock runs on a flip scheduler rather than a loop (C1639). The loop is the unit of the posted practice, not of every sketch.

**Takeaway:** the loop point is the craft. A visible seam would break the spell; the invisible one is the point of the piece. The code shows the seam is hidden by timing the change to the moment nothing is visible -- and that some sketches refuse the loop entirely.

## M3 -- Grid morphing

The grid is not a container; it is the material, and it changes shape. Circles morph into squares (23 June: a row of white-outlined circles containing pale mint squares, mid-transformation). A plain grey cube unfolds into a hexagon of stacked rainbow chevrons (Accordion). The plus grid's crosses could be read as squares with corners removed. The petal/scale pattern (15 September) makes diamond flowers out of overlapping black scales. The morph is always geometric and always gradual -- one regular form becoming another regular form, with the grid holding the transformation together so the eye can follow it.

**Takeaway:** morphing works because the grid stays put. The transformation is legible only against a stable field.

## M4 -- Bitcrush and pixelation

The autumn turn makes resolution itself the material. "bitcrush" (13 October): two white discs with rainbow fringes, their edges pixelated and crushed. "Bitcrusher x64" (15 October, objkt): black discs with rainbow fringes falling on a cream field; the objkt version is black-top/cream-bottom with white discs. "8-bit" (12 November): pale cyan discs with rainbow fringes falling over a cream band of pixelated light-blue shapes and small yellow/cyan/red squares. A replier corrected the title -- "6-bit, no transparency on X unfortunately" -- which tells you the audience reads the bit-depth as part of the work. The crush is applied to the same discs and fringes as everything else: the system is not changed, its resolution is lowered, and the lowering is the piece.

**Takeaway:** bitcrush is the fringe's cousin -- both are controlled damage to the signal, and both are applied with restraint.

## M5 -- The optical illusion

Three works turn the viewer's own eye into the mechanism. The 9 June checkerboard (black and pale mint) "changes once you focus on one square" -- the pattern reorganises under attention. The 8 October moire rectangles -- black rectangles filled with rainbow concentric arcs and moire stripes -- made a replier write "this makes me dizzy." Board Bends (26 May) bends the checkerboard under a moving circle of rainbow squares. The illusion pieces are the most-engaged works in the record (100,874 views for the June checkerboard), which suggests the audience comes for the moment the static image refuses to stay static.

**Takeaway:** the illusion is the only mechanism where the viewer does the work. Everything else moves on screen; this one moves in the eye.

## M6 -- Moon phases

The moon is the only recurring subject that is not pure geometry. The 5 May video is rows of pale cream moon discs in phases; 12 October is two small rainbow half-circles on a black field with cream grid lines; the OpenProcessing sketch "Moons" is white discs on black, some half red/yellow. The phase is a natural fit for the practice: a disc that changes by a binary-ish rule (lit/unlit), a cycle that loops, a form that is already a circle in a grid. The moon pieces are the quietest in the record -- no glitch, no crush, just the phase turning.

**Takeaway:** the moon is the fringe's opposite pole -- the same disc, the same loop, with the damage turned off.

## M7 -- The emoji title

The caption is a mechanism, not an afterthought. A moon emoji, a plus sign, a ruler, a slot machine, a bubble, a black disc: the title names the work's one visible thing and nothing else, and #p5js names the tool. The emoji does the work a title usually does -- it tells you what to look at -- without telling you what it means. "Its not a bug, its a feature" (30 January) and "bitcrush" (13 October) are the only captions that say more, and both say it about the process, not the picture. The minimal captioning is part of the minimalism: the sketch speaks, the text gets out of the way.

**Takeaway:** the emoji title is the anti-statement. It refuses interpretation and points at the form.

## M8 -- The two-field palette

Every work sits on one of two fields: black or cream. Black fields carry pale mint, white, lavender/pink, and the rainbow fringe; cream fields carry black discs, pastel squares, and the same fringe. The two fields never mix inside a work except at a deliberate divide -- the 15 November video is split cream-top/black-bottom, with black discs above and rainbow-outlined hollow rings below, bubbles crossing the divide. The binary field is the visual form of the binary state in the Genuary prompt: on/off, black/cream, lit/unlit. The rainbow fringe is the third term that keeps the binary from being monochrome.

**Takeaway:** black and cream are not two palettes; they are one palette with a switch. The switch is the work.

## Aesthetic logic: the glitch is the beauty

M:PY's aesthetic is a wager: that a grid with a defect is more beautiful than a grid without one. The defect takes three forms -- the channel slip (the fringe), the resolution drop (the crush), and the misalignment (the Accordion "bug", kept in as a feature) -- and each is applied to the same spare material: circles, squares, pluses, discs, on black or cream. Nothing is maximal. The palette is two fields plus a rainbow edge; the caption is one emoji; the loop is a few seconds; the sketch is one idea. The restraint is what makes the defect legible: on a busy canvas a fringe would be noise, on this canvas it is the event. The audience's responses track exactly this -- they notice the loop point they cannot find, the square that changes when they focus, the style they recognise at a glance. And the artist's own account of it, the one sentence he gave, is the aesthetic stated plainly: making them is just as attractive as watching them. The work is not about the glitch; the work IS the glitch, made watchable.
