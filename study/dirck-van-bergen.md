# Dirck van Bergen: Study Notes

**Site:** Dirck van Bergen (1645, Haarlem - c. 1700, Haarlem; as commonly cited, not verified from a primary source), Dutch Golden Age painter of Haarlem, the pupil who took the cattle and stayed: Houbraken's pupil of Adriaen van de Velde who "copied his style very well", spent some time in England, then came home to Haarlem and died there; the copy that outlived the master by nearly thirty years, whose works are so close to his master's later period from about 1665 onward as to be very often confused with them, though less neat in drawing, less careful in detail and less skilled in aerial perspective, with gloomier foliage and figures more frequently subordinate to his cattle. He was born in Haarlem and lived mainly in that city; he painted oxen, cows, sheep, figures and landscapes, glowingly and clearly coloured like those of his master but not as detailed, with the trees and landscape more gloomily foliated. He went to England to seek his fortune in about 1675, painted 18 decorative paintings for the Duke and Duchess of Lauderdale in Ham House, c. 1673, and returned to Haarlem a disappointed man, where he was reasonably well paid for his art. He was a well-built man and by nature a second Adonis, eloquent and always lusty and happy, a spendthrift who could consume what he had earned in one journey saying one must not worry about tomorrow, so that when he died his good friends were necessitated to collect coins in all the inns in which he was wont to stay so as to bury him. He died in poverty by 1702 at the latest, when the Haarlem Guild records refer to him as deceased. No words by van Bergen himself - no letters, contracts or statements - were found in any source read; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Dirck van Bergen was born in Haarlem in 1645, as commonly cited. No baptism record was found in any source read, and the date is not settled: Hofstede de Groot gives "about 1640-90", the Art Institute of Chicago gives b. 1640 d. 1695, the National Gallery gives 1645 - about 1690, Gerson gives 1645 - after 1690, and a Christie's header gives "(Haarlem 1645-1690/1702)". His name is spelled Dirck van Bergen, Dirk van Bergen, Dirck van den Bergen, Dirck van Berghen, Dirck van den Berghen, and in one Art Institute of Chicago entry "Theodore Dirck" and "Dirck Berghan". Nothing is known of his parents, his family or his childhood. One commercial site claims an Antwerp teacher named Jacob Savertijns; the claim appears only on AI-generated reproduction-shop sites and is NOT verified - do not cite it as fact.

He was a pupil of Adriaen van de Velde. Houbraken, the only biographical source, does not use the bare word "pupil"; the translated passage (vol. 3, p. 91) reads: "Among the commendable students that he nurtured with his art lessons, appears DIRCK van BERGEN. He was born in Haarlem, and lived mainly in that city. He painted oxen, cows, sheep, figures and landscapes, glowingly and clearly coloured like those of his master but not as detailed, with the trees and landscape more gloomily foliated." Hofstede de Groot (vol. 4, p. 455) confirms: "according to Houbraken, a pupil of A. van de Velde, and his works confirm the statement." Wikipedia paraphrases the relationship as "a pupil of Adriaen van de Velde, who copied his style very well". He remained entirely faithful to van de Velde's example throughout his career - the National Gallery's words - and his works are, in Hofstede de Groot's phrase, "so closely related to the pictures of his master's later period from about 1665 onward as to be very often confused with them. Van Bergen's work is somewhat less neat in drawing, less careful in details, and less skilled in aerial perspective; his figures, too, are more frequently subordinate to his cattle."

He went to England. Houbraken (translated, p. 92): "He did later move to England, but fortune did not entirely favour him there. Thus he returned to his native city." Gerson: he went "to seek his fortune in about 1675" and "returned to Haarlem a disappointed man". The dates do not line up cleanly: the one documented English commission - 18 decorative paintings for the Duke and Duchess of Lauderdale in Ham House, c. 1673 (Gerson note 18, citing Hearn/van Leeuwen 2022) - predates the "about 1675" move, and Wikidata gives work locations "Amsterdam, England (1673), Haarlem". No patron name, no employer, no London address is documented beyond the Lauderdales; no named contact or family record (no wife, no children) was found in any source read. The Ham House pictures include lions, Orientals, Mercury and Battus, and Vertumnus and Pomona - decorative mythologies and exotic animals, not the pastoral cattle pieces his reputation rests on. He came home, and in Haarlem he was "reasonably well paid for his art" (Houbraken).

He was, by Houbraken's account, a popular man who spent money as easily as he earned it: "well-built man and by nature a second Adonis, eloquent and always lusty and happy... if he had received money for a piece of painting, he could consume it with tranquil heart in one journey saying, one must not worry about tomorrow." When he died, "his good friends were necessitated to collect coins in all the inns in which he was wont to stay so as to bury him." The death date is unresolved: Wikipedia gives c. 1700; the Art Institute of Chicago gives 1695; Capitoliumart and Commons give "after 1690"; Christie's gives 1690; NiceArtGallery says he was active to about 1709. The only hard anchors are the latest dated work, 1690, and the Haarlem Guild records referring to him as deceased by 1702 at the latest. He died in poverty by 1702 at the latest. No burial record was found.

His oeuvre has no catalogue raisonne and no authoritative total. The Wikimedia Commons auto-list has 59 rows, of which 18 are the Ham House pictures, 2 are "attributed to" and 1 is a misfiled U-boat picture, leaving roughly 56 plausibly attributed paintings. "Approximately eighty known works" appears only in AI-generated commercial copy (ArtsDot/WahooArt) and is NOT to be cited. He left no pupils or followers that any source names; the fellow van de Velde followers Hofstede de Groot names are Johannes Innevelt (known only from documents, no picture) and Pieter and Gabriel van der Leeuw. The one afterlife his work is given is the RKD's claim, via Wikipedia, that it "later influenced Wilhelm von Kobell" - a single-database claim, weakly sourced (see D4 above).

## M1. The faithful-imitation engine

The mechanism: he learned van de Velde's whole picture by copying it, and kept copying it. The National Gallery: "all the elements... based on pictorial inventions first introduced by van de Velde". The picture is built from the master's kit - the Italianate landscape, the tomb or ruin, the sunlit cattle group, the staffage figures - and the imitation is so close that works are "very often confused" with the master's, including a National Gallery picture (NG984) that was itself misattributed to van de Velde. The imitation is not a phase he grew out of; he remained entirely faithful to it throughout his career.

**Takeaway:** the engine is the copy as a complete system, not a motif borrowed once - build the picture from the master's kit whole, and let the difference live in the handling, not the inventory.

## M2. Gloomier foliage

The mechanism: the trees and the landscape are foliated more darkly than the master's. Houbraken's own words: "with the trees and landscape more gloomily foliated". It is the one difference Houbraken names in the same breath as the likeness, and it is structural: a darker, more unbroken tree curtain against which the glowing cattle stand. Where van de Velde's foliage is open and airy, van Bergen's closes the picture in.

**Takeaway:** darken and thicken the tree mass, not the whole picture - the gloom is a curtain at the edges, and the glow inside it is what makes the cattle read.

## M3. Glowing, clear colour with less detail

The mechanism: the colour is glowing and clear, like the master's, but with less detail. Houbraken: "glowingly and clearly coloured like those of his master but not as detailed". Hofstede de Groot: "somewhat less neat in drawing, less careful in details, and less skilled in aerial perspective". The picture keeps the master's light - sunlit cattle against dark foliage, the golden pastoral glow - and spends less work on the small things: the drawing loosens, the detail thins, the aerial perspective weakens. The glow is the part he kept; the finish is the part he dropped.

**Takeaway:** keep the light and lose the finish - a bright, clear palette over looser drawing, so the picture reads at a glance and falls apart only on close inspection.

## M4. Cattle first, figures subordinate

The mechanism: the figures are subordinate to the cattle. Hofstede de Groot: "his figures, too, are more frequently subordinate to his cattle." The cattle are the subject and the figures are staffage - herdsmen, milkmaids, shepherdesses placed to serve the animals, not to carry the picture. This is the reverse of the #538 mechanism in which van de Velde sold his hand inside other men's landscapes: here the cattle painter keeps his own cattle centre-stage and lets the people shrink to props.

**Takeaway:** put the animals at the centre of the composition and the people at its service - a herdsman who is a scale figure, not a character.

## M5. Weaker aerial perspective and looser drawing

The mechanism: the distance does not recede the way the master's does, and the drawing is less neat. Hofstede de Groot names both in one sentence: "less neat in drawing, less careful in details, and less skilled in aerial perspective". The effect is a flatter picture: the foreground cattle and the background trees sit closer together in space, and the whole composition reads as a frieze rather than a deep landscape. It is a failure as imitation and a mechanism as a picture - the flatness is what makes a van Bergen identifiable once you know to look for it.

**Takeaway:** flatten the depth - let the background press up against the foreground so the picture reads as a band, not a vista.

## M6. The Italianate kit without Italy

The mechanism: he paints Italy without having been there. Christie's: "there is no record of him in Italy... inspired by his teacher, Adriaen van de Velde". The Italianate landscape - ruins, tombs, sunlit southern light, herdsmen in classical dress - is a kit learned from the master's pictures, not from travel. He never went; the kit came second-hand, and the second-handness is the point: an Italy assembled from another man's pictures, repeated until it is his own.

**Takeaway:** build the Italian landscape from borrowed parts - ruin, tomb, sunlit hill - without ever leaving Haarlem, and let the repetition make it a personal language.

## M7. The ruin and tomb anchor

The mechanism: a ruin or a tomb anchors the composition. W1 has the ruin behind the calves and the dun horse; W5 is built around a tomb; W8 has an antique tomb with a ruined urn; W13 is mountainous with a shepherdess and cows. The ruin is the vertical in a horizontal landscape, the dark mass against which the cattle glow, and the memento mori that gives a pastoral picture its weight. It is also the most portable part of the kit - the same ruin returns in picture after picture.

**Takeaway:** anchor every landscape on one dark vertical ruin or tomb, placed to throw the cattle into light.

## M8. The crowded multi-species inventory

The mechanism: the foreground is crowded with several species at once. W1 has two calves, a sheep and a dun horse; W5 has herdsmen and cattle near a tomb; W16 has a herdswoman with a lamb in her arms among her animals; the Ham House series adds lions and leopards. The picture is an inventory - calves, sheep, horse, goat, dog, herdsman - arranged as a frieze across the foreground. One commercial site calls it "more characters... less harmonious" - snippet-only, do not present as verified - but the inventory itself is documented across the works: the picture is a catalogue of animals before it is a scene.

**Takeaway:** fill the foreground with a mixed herd - two calves, a sheep, a horse - arranged side by side like specimens, not like a story.

## M9. Small cabinet format and reused copper

The mechanism: late in his career he works small, on copper, and on copper that has already been used. Christie's lot 3953584: a pair of Italianate landscapes on copper, first signed and dated "D.V Bergen. / 1690", 16.8 x 22 cm each, and "he has re-used incised copper engraving plates as supports". The 1688 Herdswoman with Cattle is also on copper, 37.6 x 48.4 cm. The small format is the one documented change in his career: not a change of subject or style, but of support and scale. A cabinet picture, held in the hand, painted over someone else's engraved plate.

**Takeaway:** shrink the picture to the size of a hand and paint it on a plate that already carries another image - the support is part of the picture.

## M10. The darkening and the nocturne

The mechanism: his pictures darken with age, and at least once he painted the dark itself. The National Gallery on NG984: "sadly darkened through age". The Ham House Night Scene with Figures Grouped around a Fire (W9, 1670s) is the one clear nocturne in the oeuvre - figures grouped around a fire in the dark, the light a single campfire glow. The darkening is partly the varnish and partly the gloomier foliage of M2; the nocturne takes that darkness as the subject. It is also the mechanism that connects the pictures to the life: the man who spent everything in one journey and was buried on coins collected in the inns.

**Takeaway:** let the picture go dark - a single fire against black foliage - and let the glow be the only light left.

### Phase 1 - the early pictures, c. 1665-1668

**W1. Two Calves, a Sheep and a Dun Horse by a Ruin, c. 1665, oil on wood, 23.8 x 30 cm, National Gallery, London (NG984). VERIFIED.** The one picture whose dimensions and description were verified on a museum page read end to end. A ruin behind, two calves, a sheep and a dun horse in front - the inventory of M8 and the ruin anchor of M7 in a single small panel. Previously misattributed to Adriaen van de Velde, and now the textbook case of the confusion: "all the elements... based on pictorial inventions first introduced by van de Velde". The National Gallery notes it has "sadly darkened through age" (M10). Wynn Ellis Bequest 1876. Takeaway: the smallest picture in the list and the most typical - ruin, mixed herd, darkened varnish.

**W14. Paysage et animaux (Landscape and Animals), 1668, 72 x 60 cm, Louvre, Paris (INV 1034). REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED.** The earliest dated picture after W1, and the only Louvre picture with a date attached. Flag: a second 1675 "Landscape and Animals" in the Louvre may be the same picture re-dated, or a second picture - the two are possibly conflated in the listings. Takeaway: the Louvre holds the Italianate kit at its most conventional, and the dating itself is uncertain.

### Phase 2 - England and the Ham House pictures, c. 1673-1677

**W6. Landscape with an Old Herdsman and Young Market Girl fording a Stream followed by Two Horse-and-Carts with Grooms, 1675, 138 x 198 cm, Ham House (National Trust NT 1139667). REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED.** The largest picture attributed to him, and the one most likely to be the centrepiece of the Lauderdale commission. A ford scene - herdsman, market girl, horse-and-carts - at a scale no other van Bergen approaches. Treat the dimensions with caution: they come from a reproduction-shop listing only, and the picture is very large for a painter of cabinet landscapes. This IS van Bergen's ford picture; van de Velde's "Peasants with Cattle fording a Stream" is NOT his. Takeaway: the commission at its grandest - the pastoral scaled up to a wall.

**W7. Landscape with a Milkmaid Milking a Cow, a Herdsman, Sheep and Cattle near a River, 1677, 74 x 57 cm, Ham House (NT 1140027). REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED.** Orientation conflict: some listings give 57 x 74 cm. A milkmaid milking a cow by a river, herdsman, sheep and cattle - the cattle-first hierarchy of M4 with the milkmaid as staffage. Takeaway: the milking scene as a pretext for the animals.

**W8. A Landscape with an Antique Tomb with a Ruined Urn, a Herdsman, Sheep and Cattle, 1677, 92 x 123 cm, Ham House (NT 1140174). REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED.** The tomb anchor of M7 at Ham House scale: an antique tomb with a ruined urn, herdsman, sheep and cattle. Takeaway: the ruin returns, bigger, for a patron's wall.

**W9. Night Scene with Figures Grouped around a Fire, 1670s (WahooArt 1677), 103 x 139 cm, Ham House (NT 1140172). REPORTED-BUT-UNVERIFIED.** Orientation conflict: some listings give 139 x 103 cm. The one clear nocturne (M10) - figures grouped around a fire in the dark, the light a single campfire glow against black foliage. The 1677 date is reproduction-shop data, not museum-verified; Wikidata says "1670s". Takeaway: the only night picture, and the one that makes the darkness the subject.

## Evolution

There is no documented stylistic break in his career. The National Gallery: he "remained entirely faithful to Van de Velde's example throughout his career". ## Aesthetic logic

The aesthetic logic of Dirck van Bergen is the logic of the faithful copy that fails in exactly the ways a copy fails. He takes the master's picture whole - the Italianate landscape, the ruin, the sunlit cattle, the staffage - and reproduces it with a glowing clear colour that is the master's own, and then the copy slips: the foliage goes gloomier, the drawing loosens, the detail thins, the aerial perspective weakens, the figures shrink to props for the cattle. The slip is the style. A van de Velde is a landscape with cattle in it; a van Bergen is cattle with a landscape behind them, the background pressed flat against the foreground, the trees closing in like a curtain. The glow is the inheritance and the gloom is the signature: sunlit animals against dark foliage, the light kept and the finish dropped. Late in life the picture shrinks to the size of a hand and the support changes to copper and vellum, but the picture does not change - the same inventory, the same ruin, the same herd, smaller. The life rhymes with the pictures: a man who copied his master well, spent what he earned in one journey, went to England and came home disappointed, and was buried on coins collected in the inns. The pictures are the same story told in paint: a second-hand Italy, a borrowed light, a herd that outlives the herdsman.

## Positions

**P1. The copy as a complete system.** Van Bergen is not a painter who borrowed a motif; he borrowed a whole picture and kept borrowing it for thirty years. The copy is the oeuvre.

**P2. The gloom is the signature.** Of all the differences from the master, Houbraken names one: the foliage is gloomier. That single darkening is the most reliable way to tell a van Bergen from a van de Velde at a glance.

**P3. The glow is the inheritance.** The colour is glowing and clear like the master's; the light is the part he kept. A van Bergen fails as imitation in the drawing and succeeds as a picture in the light.

**P4. Cattle first.** His figures are subordinate to his cattle, and that subordination is a position: the animal is the subject, the human is the prop. It is the reverse of his master's career as a staffage hand.

**P5. The darkening is the biography.** The pictures darken with age, the nocturne is the one picture that makes the dark the subject, and the man who painted them died so poor his friends collected coins in the inns to bury him. The darkening is not only varnish.

**P6. The Kobell link is thin.** The one afterlife his work is given - "according to the RKD his work later influenced Wilhelm von Kobell" - rests on a single database claim that no leg could read end to end, and Kobell's own biography does not mention him. Treat the link as a stated claim, not as a verified influence.

**P7. The Ham House series is stranger than the pastoral reputation.** The only documented commission of his life is 18 decorative paintings for the Duke and Duchess of Lauderdale, and they include lions, Orientals, Mercury and Battus, and Vertumnus and Pomona - mythologies and exotic animals, not cattle. The pastoral painter was also a decorator of walls.

**P8. The late turn is a change of support, not of subject.** Copper in 1688 and 1690, vellum in 1690, small format throughout: the picture does not change, the surface does. A career that shrinks instead of developing.

**P9. The confusion is the evidence.** That his pictures are "very often confused" with van de Velde's is not a failure of connoisseurship; it is the mechanism working as designed. The confusion file is the oeuvre's own catalogue.

**P10. The commercial biography is fiction.** The ArtsDot/WahooArt biography of van Bergen is AI-generated filler that contradicts Houbraken; the "approximately eighty known works", the Antwerp birth, the Savertijns apprenticeship and the "impasto" are snippet-only claims from the same source family. Cite Houbraken, Hofstede de Groot, Gerson and the museums; do not cite the shops.
