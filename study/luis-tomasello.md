# Luis Tomasello: Study Notes

**Site:** Luis Tomasello, born 29 November 1915 at La Plata, province of Buenos Aires, died 17 January 2014 at Paris, aged 98 -- Argentine kinetic artist, the builder who discovered light: white cubes, cylinders and polyhedrons on a white ground, colour hidden on the faces the viewer cannot see, thrown back onto the surface as reflected glow and shadow, so that nothing in the work moves and the light does everything. He trained as a bricklayer, joiner and painter beside his mason father, studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon in Buenos Aires (1932-1938) and the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Carcova (1940-1944), met Emilio Pettoruti and Carmelo Arden Quin before his first trip to Europe in 1951, co-founded the Salon Arte-Nuevo with Arden Quin in Buenos Aires in 1954, and settled permanently in Paris in 1957, where he said he discovered light and where he lived and worked for 57 years (C5001, C5002, C5003, C5004, C5005, C5006). He joined the Galerie Denise Rene in 1958, made his first kinetic reliefs that year, entitled Reflexion, and had his first solo shows in 1962 at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires and the Galerie Denise Rene in Paris; his Atmospheres chromoplastiques were shown at Bewogen Beweging (Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam, 1961), Nouvelle Tendance II (Zagreb, 1963), The Responsive Eye (Museum of Modern Art, New York, 1965, represented by Atmosphere Chromoplastique Number 109 of 1963) and Lumiere et Mouvement (Musee d'art moderne de la ville de Paris, 1967) (C5007, C5011, C5012, C5018). From 1980 to 1993 he was among those who founded and animated the Espace latino-americain in Paris with Arthur Luiz Piza, Julio Le Parc, Roberto Matta and others (C5107, C5116). ## The life: the builder who discovered light

Tomasello's life divides cleanly in two, and he said so himself: one life in Argentina, another in Paris from the age of 42, and it was in Paris that he discovered light (C5006). He was born in La Plata on 29 November 1915, worked with his father as a bricklayer, joiner and painter, attended evening drawing classes from 1929 to 1931, and in his own later account worked with his mason father, a builder brother and a civil-engineer brother for twenty years and was enchanted by creating construction projects (C5001, C5002). He studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon from 1932 to 1938 and the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Carcova from 1940 to 1944, and before his first trip to Europe in 1951 he met Emilio Pettoruti and Carmelo Arden Quin, two important figures in the Argentine avant-garde; Arden Quin introduced him to abstraction and inspired him to visit Europe (C5003, C5004, C5102). He embarked on abstract art in 1952, adopted geometry following the 1951 trip after appreciating Piet Mondrian -- "With the minimum, the maximum," was the principle Mondrian left him, and he worked with it his whole life -- and co-founded the Salon Arte-Nuevo with Arden Quin in Buenos Aires in 1954 (C5004, C5005, C5106). He spent six months in Paris in 1951 before settling there permanently in 1957, joining a large expatriate community of Latin American kinetic artists; his interest in kineticism was born in 1957, and as of that year, inspired by Mondrian's Boogie-Woogie, he started producing paintings with an optical effect, inquiring into the dynamic properties of colour (C5005, C5006, C5007).

The turn to relief was an accident with a sheet of paper. He had already worked in the abstract, but what interested him most was the Kinetic Art he saw exhibited in the Denise Rene Gallery, and he began to do kinetic work on a plane before losing interest because many artists were working in the same direction, having done seven or eight paintings before working in relief (W01, C5051, C5007). One day he put some little bars on a white sheet of paper and saw how the colour reflected on the surface, and that instant determined his first relief (C5009, C5052). He joined the Galerie Denise Rene in 1958, started using relief that year, and created his first works in kinetic relief, entitled Reflexion (W02, 1958), though Sotheby's dates the start of the reliefs to 1960 and Arte Al Dia says he worked assiduously on the Chromoplastic Atmospheres since 1960 -- the year is left open (C5007, C5009). The poet Carlo Belloli renamed the work: Tomasello had called his early reliefs Reflection 1, 2, 3, and Belloli, asked by Denise Rene to write the catalogue of Tomasello's first exhibition at her gallery, told him he had to change the title because what he had done was to colour the atmosphere, proposing "Chromoplastic Atmosphere", which Tomasello accepted; the renaming is dated by the exhibition texts to the catalogue of the second solo exhibition at Galerie Denise Rene in 1965, where Belloli renamed them Atmospheres mobiles chromoplastiques (C5009). The first solo exhibitions were staged in 1962 at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires and the Galerie Denise Rene in Paris; a solo show followed at Denise Rene in 1965 and at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris in 1976 (Prabook places a 1976 solo show at the Musee National d'Art Moderne instead -- one show or two is open) (C5012). Retrospectives followed at the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Pau, in 1972 (1957-1972); the Museo Espanol de Arte Contemporaneo, Madrid, in 1985 (1957-1984); Sala Parpallo, Valencia, the Palacio Municipal de Exposiciones Kiosco Alfonso, La Coruna, and the Museo de Bellas Artes, Pamplona, in 1986 (1957-1984); Cercle Noroit, Arras, in 1991 (1957-1990); and the Museo de Arte Contemporaneo Latinoamericano (MACLA), La Plata, in 2004 (C5012). Later group showings included Dynamo: A Century of Light and Motion in Art, 1913-2013, at the Grand Palais, Paris, in 2013, and Afterimages: Geometric Abstraction and Perception at the Akron Art Museum in 2021 (C5011). In the early 1980s he pierced lines and small geometric shapes through black monochrome reliefs: the Lumiere noire series (C5010). He and Julio Cortazar were inseparable friends after an architect contracted Tomasello, then painting houses to survive in Europe, to paint Cortazar's house; they did two books together, Un elogio del tres (1980, linocuts, 25 copies) and Negro el 10 (1983, ten silkscreen prints, 60 copies), in a process contrary to tradition, in which Cortazar produced his text taking off from the idea Tomasello developed (C5010, C5114). From 1980 to 1993 he was among those who founded and animated the Espace latino-americain in Paris with Piza, Le Parc, Matta and others, partly funded by Roberto Matta -- the Latin American Paris circle that is also his network, and the same circle as Piza's (C5107, C5116). At 94 he was living and working in Paris since 1957, presenting his first solo exhibition in the Northwest at James Harris Gallery, Seattle, in 2009; his Chromoplastic Mural (2011), 672 twelve-centimetre seven-sided polyhedrons, was installed in the Bloch Building of the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art in Kansas City, and he celebrated his 96th birthday there (C5019, C5020, C5070). Late reliefs continued to 2012, including Atmosphere chromoplastique No. 1019 (C5020). He died in Paris on 17 January 2014 (C5001).

## M1 -- The hidden colour: paint the face nobody sees

The first mechanism is the whole trick, and the sources state it plainly: the colours are usually applied to the back or reverse side of the shapes or on their sides so that they are reflected onto the surface of the work, which itself is usually square (C5009, C5054). The reliefs are built from identical geometric elements -- planes, squares, cubes, cylinders, sometimes rhombohedrons -- jutting out only a short distance from a usually square white support, placed in orthogonal patterns, with colours red, yellow, blue and green, sometimes fluorescent (C5009). From the front the work reads as white on white; the colour is never shown, only its reflection. Atmosphere Chromoplastique No. 121 (W04, 1964), viewed as an image this run, proves it by failing to show it: a dense 15-by-15 grid of small white cube forms photographed frontally, almost no colour visible, which is consistent with the mechanism, since the colour only appears at an angle or as a faint reflection (C5054). Atmosphere Chromoplastique No. 315 (W09, 1973) is the cleanest demonstration in prose: Tomasello painted the pointed tops of pentahedrons a bright kelly green and left the flat bottoms white, attached them from one of their angled sides, and from the front the viewer sees only white squares plus a haze of green light reflected onto the surface between them; only by looking from the side at an acute angle can the green paint itself be seen -- the viewer hardly ever sees the colour, only its reflection (C5059). Atmosphere Chromoplastique No. 863 (W19, shown 2009) is the same lesson late: the underside of each angled cube painted blue and green, giving the white hue a subtle chromatic aura, and it is only when the viewer moves to the side that the saturated colour is revealed (C5069). A piece built on M1 never shows its colour directly -- the colour lives on a hidden face, and the white surface is its screen.

**Takeaway:** paint the face nobody sees. Colour on the back, the underside, the side -- the white ground shows only the reflection, and the reflection is the picture.

## M2 -- The light does it: nothing moves

The second mechanism is the absence at the centre of the work, and Tomasello said it himself: "I don't do anything -- the light does it for me", because if the light is more intense the reflection is stronger, and the same piece can seem to be many different ones (C5009, C5052). Nothing in the work itself moves. The elements are static; their appearance varies owing to the shifting light (C5052). The movement is caused "not merely by the position of the viewer, but above all by the intensity and motion of the illumination. Sunlight -- natural light, in other words -- is ideal" (C5054). The viewer does the other half: the works "change constantly as the viewer moves through their space", by interaction between the movement of the viewer and the movement of light across the surface, and kinetic art "depends upon the active participation of the spectator because as viewers move in relation to the overall work, the light, colors and surfaces appear to change dramatically" (C5054, C5070). Tomasello described his own movement as tranquil, "so calmed that it is almost imperceptible and it generates itself by the repetition of the elements... the movement gets perceived over time" (C5052). Against Piza, whose plate is struck and wiped and re-inked, Tomasello's work is never touched after it is made -- the only touch-responsive element found anywhere in this run is one cylinder in Reflexion No. 31 A (W05, 1969) that swivels slightly when touched, according to the condition report, and nothing else in the work moves (C5055). A piece built on M2 has no motor, no moving part, no animation loop of its own -- the light moves, the viewer moves, and the work is the changing reflection between them.

**Takeaway:** let the light do it. No motor, no moving part -- the sun moves, the viewer walks, and the same white field becomes many different pieces.

## M3 -- One element, repeated: the module is the system

The third mechanism is the discipline that makes the light legible: in any given work all the elements are identical, jutting out only a short distance and placed in orthogonal patterns (C5009). The little squares he painted on the plane in 1957 later turned into cubes in relief, and white was transformed into various tonalities when light encroached on one of the cubes' six sides (C5051). He soon replaced the modules painted on the plane with cylindrical rods, giving rise to his characteristic reliefs, and he utilized cylinders, cubes and polyhedrons fixed by one of their edges to inquire into the instability of colour based on its relative position, creating multiple tonalities depending on the incidence of the light source (C5009). The repetition is not decoration; it is the instrument. Reflexion No. 31 A (W05, 1969), viewed as an image this run, shows about thirty short silver cylinders set at varied angles across a white horizontal panel, each casting a soft shadow, the deliberate placement generating harmonic shadows and bright reflections that playfully interact and move in a continuous dance (C5055). Objet plastique No. 365 (W12, 1975), viewed as an image, is a dense field of small vertical rods in a square field inside a plain frame, with a darker diamond zone at the centre where the rods are angled differently; the pattern emerges from rod orientation and shadow, not from colour, and the whole work reads cream and ivory in the frontal photograph (C5062). Objet Plastique No. 796 (W17, 1997) is a grid of slender dowels projecting perpendicularly like a bed of nails, cut down to varying heights to create the outline of another square within it, so that by alternating their heights the artist creates the appearance of a square sinking into the surface (C5067). A piece built on M3 deals from one element only and repeats it until the repetition itself is the system -- change the angle, the height or the spacing, never the vocabulary.

**Takeaway:** repeat one element until it becomes a system. Same cube, same rod, same dowel -- the variation is in the angle and the light, never in the shape.

## M4 -- Shadow as the second colour

The fourth mechanism is the one the white-on-white surface hides in plain sight: the shadow is a material, not a side effect. He poses white cubes on a white background to engage with the colours of shadows and reflected light (C5009). In No. 246 (W07, 1970), white cubes are placed at different angles, painted with different tones of acrylic, allowing a play of shadow and light, and viewers engage with the work and experience the art (C5057). In No. 796 (W17, 1997), the surface seems to undulate as the overlapping shadows of the taller dowels create a sense of density, and the piece changes depending on where the viewer is standing (C5067). In No. 121 (W04, 1964), the catalogue condition note says the blue and yellow sides of the cubic elements are not fully conveyed by the illustration -- the photograph fails where the shadow succeeds, because the shadow is what the eye actually reads (C5054). The two Tate works viewed this run complicate the picture honestly: Chromoplastic Atmosphere No. 383 (W11, 1975) shows, at 1536 px, an almost entirely blank white square panel with a single tiny dark square right of centre and a faint yellow-green edge along the right side, and No. 710 (W16, 1992) shows a large black circular disc with an inscribed diamond and a tiny square hole at its centre -- neither shows the expected grid of white cubes, and the sources consulted do not resolve whether these are extreme minimal variants, details, or mis-mappings, so the cube mechanism is not claimed for either work from its image (C5061, C5066). A piece built on M4 treats the shadow as a colour with a value -- the dark square, the overlapping dowel shadows, the faint green edge are the drawing, and the white is the paper it is drawn on.

**Takeaway:** draw with shadow. The white field is the paper; the shadow is the ink -- and when the shadow is the only mark, that is enough.

## M5 -- The wall at architectural scale: the relief leaves the room

The fifth mechanism is the scale change: from the Dutch artist Mondrian he also absorbed the need to associate visual arts and architecture, executing murals for public and private buildings in Mexico, France, Morocco, Nicaragua, Norway, Argentina, and the United States (C5013). The Mural Lumino-acoustique in the Salle Bleue (Blue Hall) of the Palais des Congres, Paris (W08, 1973), is the architectural scale of the relief idea: he worked with an acoustical engineer who helped him choose the appropriate materials that would let the sound circulate clearly, in this case made with plaster and gauze, and the lobby was the first he did completely white, with blue chairs, hence the name; the wall carries large white oval and disc reliefs in rows above the raked seating, the audience moves through the hall, daylight and electric light shift across the discs, and the same white surface reads differently from every seat -- and the sound of the auditorium is part of the work, since the material was chosen so sound circulates clearly (C5058, C5013). The Chromoplastic Mural at the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art (W20, 2011) is the same idea at its largest: 672 white seven-sided polyhedrons, each 12 cm, in bass wood and acrylic, 123 by 444 inches on the east wall of the Bloch Building facing a long window looking out on the Kansas City Sculpture Park; the fluorescent orange plane of each angled shape reflects off the surface of the white wall, producing a glowing atmosphere of coloured light, daylight from the facing window changes through the day, and visitors walking the Gallery Walk see the glow shift as they move (C5070). Viewed as images this run, the mural reads as a long white wall with the polyhedron field denser and taller at the left and tapering to the right, a red-orange glow across it, and a single visitor standing at the right for scale; the detail photograph reads pink-red where the museum record says fluorescent orange, a difference recorded rather than resolved (C5070). Other architectural works listed include the Mural cromoplasatico at Ville Nouvelle de Vaudreuil (1979), the Chromoplastic Mural at the Police Headquarters in Grenoble (1981), and the Atmosfera Chromoplastica Mural at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes de Neuquen (2004), plus a metal weaving with polyester at the Pharmacy Faculty in Marseille that filters the light, with a play of light and shadow projected on the floor and walls (C5013). A piece built on M5 takes the relief off the wall and into the room -- the wall is the work, the window is the light source, and the walking visitor is the motor.

**Takeaway:** build the wall, not the picture. White polyhedrons across a whole room, daylight through a long window, the visitor walking the length of it -- the architecture is the relief.

## M6 -- Lumiere noire: the inversion

The sixth mechanism is the system turned inside out. In the early 1980s he pierced lines and small geometric shapes through black monochrome reliefs: the Lumiere noire series, starting from bas-reliefs painted entirely in black, where the thin hollow lines perforate abysses of penetrating darkness (C5010, C5064). Tomasello said the black reliefs that he began to do earlier he also called "Chromoplastic Atmosphere", and Belloli made a new suggestion that he change it to "Black Light", and so he did it (C5010). Lumiere Noire No. 898 (W18, 2008), viewed as an image this run at 471 by 472 px, shows the inversion directly: a near-black panel crossed by thin vertical bars in pale blue-grey and horizontal bars in silver, with a small grid of tiny squares where the bars cross; the bars read as slots cut into the black surface, catching light at their edges while the black field absorbs it, and as the viewer moves, different slot edges catch the light while nothing in the work moves (C5068). The poems by Cortazar that make up the volume Negro el 10 are inspired by the Lumiere noire series (C5010). Against the white reliefs, where colour is hidden and only its reflection is seen, the black reliefs hide the light itself: the white field becomes a black field, the reflection becomes a slot, and the work is read by the edges that catch the light rather than by the glow between them. A piece built on M6 inverts M1 -- same grid, same repetition, but black ground, thin perforations, and light that enters only through the cuts.

**Takeaway:** invert the system. Black ground, thin slots, light entering only through the cuts -- the same grid, read by its edges instead of its glow.

## M7 -- Three lines that live: the book as a kinetic device

The seventh mechanism is the one place the movement is in the viewer's hands. He and Julio Cortazar were inseparable friends after an architect contracted Tomasello, then painting houses to survive in Europe, to paint Cortazar's house, and they did two books together following a process contrary to the traditional one, in which Cortazar produced his text taking off from the idea Tomasello developed (C5010, C5114). Un elogio del tres (W13, 1980) is a rectangular book in which Tomasello painted a yellow line on the first page, a blue one on the second and a red one on the third -- for him they were the father, the mother and the child; on the following pages he moves them, has fun with them, and thus they begin to live (C5063). Negro el 10 (W14, 1983) is a book-object, a poem by Cortazar for ten silkscreen prints by Tomasello inspired by his black paintings; the title came about because Tomasello proposed doing serigraphs of black works, the editor told them to do only ten, and Cortazar wrote about the roulette wheel, which has two colours, black and red -- by chance the number ten on the roulette wheel is black, hence the name (C5064). The kinetic device is the reader turning the pages: the three coloured lines move across the pages, and the reader handles the box and turns the ten black prints -- the work is handled, not wall-hung (C5063, C5064). Against the reliefs, where the viewer walks and the light moves, the books put the movement in the hands: the page turn is the motor, and the three lines are the whole cast. A piece built on M7 gives the viewer a small cast -- three lines, father, mother, child -- and lets the page turn, the drag, the step move them, never more characters than the hand can follow.

**Takeaway:** let three lines live. Yellow, blue, red -- father, mother, child -- and the page turn is the motor that moves them.

## M8 -- The builder's hand: with the minimum, the maximum

The eighth mechanism is the biography inside the work: he was a builder before he was an artist, and the reliefs are built, not painted. He worked with his father as a bricklayer, joiner and painter, attended evening classes in drawing from 1929 to 1931, and worked with his mason father, a builder brother and a civil-engineer brother for twenty years, enchanted by creating construction projects (C5001, C5002). The relief is a construction project at small scale: identical modules, orthogonal patterns, a short projection from the wall, a material chosen for what it does (plaster and gauze so sound circulates, bass wood and acrylic for 672 polyhedrons, metal weaving with polyester to filter light) (C5009, C5058, C5013, C5070). Mondrian's principle, as Tomasello quoted it, is the method in one line: "'With the minimum, the maximum,' was the principle that Mondrian left me with and with that I've worked my whole life" (C5106). He said his interest in architecture much predates his artistic life, and that he is more interested in working in architecture than in making reliefs because there is a space to fill, with walls, roof and floor to be thought out (C5013). Against Piza, whose plate is a block to sculpt by hammering, Tomasello's relief is a wall to build by placing: the same discipline of the hand, but additive and architectural rather than subtractive and sculptural -- the bricklayer's son builds the white field one module at a time and lets the light finish it. A piece built on M8 is assembled, not drawn: place the module, repeat it, choose the material for what it does, and let the light be the last worker on the site.

**Takeaway:** build it, do not paint it. One module, repeated, in the right material -- with the minimum, the maximum, and the light finishes the job.

## The aesthetic logic: the white field that waits

Tomasello's logic is the complement of Piza's and the inversion of it. Piza kept one object, the copper plate, and let the technique drain out of it until the plate was a block to sculpt; Tomasello kept one surface, the white field, and let the colour drain out of it until the picture was a reflection the light makes. The constants are the white ground and the identical module; the variables are the angle of the light and the position of the viewer, and nothing else is allowed to vary. He began on the flat plane with seven or eight optical paintings and gave them up because many artists were working in the same direction, then put little bars on a white sheet and saw the colour reflect, and never left that instant for the remaining fifty-six years of his working life: the same discovery, repeated from Reflexion No. 1 (1958) to the Nelson-Atkins mural (2011), from a 23-centimetre panel to a wall eleven metres long. Against Vasarely, whose line he deliberately left -- "I was feeling that my work was too much like theirs and I wanted to explore other things. I went to relief as an experience and in that process, I discovered the wonderful world of light" -- and against Soto, whose hanging lines vibrate in front of the work, Tomasello's movement is the calmest in the kinetic canon: so calmed that it is almost imperceptible, generating itself by the repetition of the elements, perceived over time (C5052, C5111). His own sentence is the logic in one line: "I don't do anything -- the light does it for me."
