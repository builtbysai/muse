# Fernand Pelez: Study Notes

**Site:** Fernand Pelez (1848-1913), French painter -- born Ferdinand Emmanuel Pelez de Cordoba d'Aguilar on Tuesday 18 January 1848 in Paris, in the former 2nd arrondissement, the son of the illustrator Raymond Pelez (1814-1874), who taught him painting from a young age; his uncle was the painter Fernand Pelez de Cordova, his older brother the painter Raymond Pelez, called Chalumeau. He took about three years of private lessons from Felix-Joseph Barrias to prepare for the Ecole entry competition, was admitted to the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in 1870, and spent three years in the studio of Alexandre Cabanel, with Adolphe Yvon's evening course alongside; he was later named professor of drawing in a school of the City of Paris, the date not stated in the sources read. His Salon debut was 1866, at eighteen, with a work whose title is not recorded in the sources read; his early work was history painting in his teacher's manner, Adam and Eve (1876) and The Death of the Emperor Commodus (1879), and the medals arrived on schedule, third class 1876, second class 1879, first class 1880, the last making him hors concours for life, while the government bought one of his paintings at each Salon from 1875 to 1880 except 1878. In 1880, under the influence of Jules Bastien-Lepage and what the French Wikipedia calls a social awakening, he changed direction completely: Au lavoir (At the Public Washhouse, 1880) replaced myth with washerwomen and won the first-class medal, and from 1881 he became known as the painter of poverty, using the word misere insistently in his titles -- Sans asile (1883), Un martyr - Le Marchand de violettes (1885), Petit Misere (1886), Un nid de misere (1887) -- with one detour into wealth, the 1884 portrait of Ferdinand de Lesseps's ten children, his first and only Salon portrait, which a critic the next year was relieved to see him abandon for the gueux again. Grimaces et misere - Les Saltimbanques (1888), five panels, 222 by 625 cm, a single twenty-foot frieze of sideshow performers, was hung in the last gallery of the Salon of 1888, reproduced as a double spread in L'Illustration on 29 September 1888, and won the silver medal at the Exposition Universelle of 1889; he was made chevalier of the Legion d'honneur in 1891 and officier in 1910, and stayed loyal to the Societe des Artistes Francais after the Salon split of 1890, serving as a Salon juror until 1912. His last exhibited work, L'Humanite ou le Christ dans le square (1896), six feet longer and two feet taller than Grimaces, was widely disliked at the Salon of 1896, and after that reception he withdrew completely from public exhibition and sale, painting on in his studio at 62, boulevard de Clichy -- repeated street boys, the Orphelines (c. 1901), the breadline studies for La Bouchee de pain (1908, now lost), the backstage dancers of 1905-1909 -- and taking part in the Montmartre Vachalcade processions of 1896 and 1897, for which he designed a poster and a float. In 1901 he wrote to the City of Paris asking that his work be preserved as a whole, each painting a page in a book telling the story of the poor of the city. He died in his studio on Thursday 7 August 1913, aged 65; the posthumous exhibition in his studio ran from Saturday 6 December 1913 to Tuesday 6 January 1914, and the City of Paris bought four major works from it, Grimaces among them, while his sisters donated five paintings, ten oil studies and thirteen drawings. Grimaces was exiled in the 1930s to hang in the Maison-Blanche psychiatric hospital at Neuilly-sur-Marne, then stored in municipal depots until the 1970s, when it was installed at the Bibliotheque Trocadero, where Robert Rosenblum found it; his 1981 essay closed with the plea that future historians "may they not forget Fernand Pelez", and the first museum retrospective, "Fernand Pelez (1848-1913): La parade des humbles", curated by Isabelle Collet, ran at the Petit Palais from Thursday 24 September 2009 to Sunday 17 January 2010. His works sit principally in the Petit Palais (Grimaces, Sans asile, Un martyr, Un nid de misere, La Vachalcade, Les Orphelines, Les Danseuses, the La Bouchee de pain studies, the Commodus version on cardboard), with the Musee d'Orsay (Petit Misere, acquired 2023 from Gallery 19C; Misere, c. 1886, is also listed there by French Wikipedia, the two possibly conflated -- see C9), the Musee des Beaux-Arts at Beziers (Commodus), Moulins (Adam and Eve), Le Havre (Au lavoir), Chambery (the last Little Lemon Vendor), Quimper (Le Marchand de citrons), Senlis (La Victime), Laval (a Martyr variant), Ottawa (The Homeless Boy, 1887) and Montreal (Gamin des rues, 1880). ## M1. The Parade Row
A single shallow row of figures stretched across a very wide canvas, no depth, no landscape, the picture as a line of people. Grimaces et misere - Les Saltimbanques (1888, oil on canvas, five panels, 222 by 625 cm, Petit Palais) is the type: a line of sideshow performers and musicians on a shallow makeshift stage, from a small limp child at the left to three aged musicians at the right, with a chained monkey and parrots with clipped wings above them. L'Humanite (1896, lost, known from a Vizzavona photograph) is the same machine doubled: two lines of figures on a raked path in the Parc Monceau, haggard poor to the left, comfortable bourgeois women, nurses and rosy children to the right, a large crucifix suspended above them. The La Bouchee de pain studies (c. 1904-1908) are the machine reduced to its minimum: some twenty men outside a soup kitchen, seen in profile, from the rear, or as silhouettes. The eye scans left to right like reading a line of text; nothing recedes, so each figure gets equal weight and the group reads as a census, not a scene. **Takeaway:** stretch the figures into one row and the picture stops being a scene and starts being a count.

## M2. Ages of Man, Left to Right
The figures in the row rise in age and stature from the left edge to the centre, then fall away to old age at the right, a popular-print "degrees of ages" structure. Grimaces is the type: from a small limp child at the left through adolescents to three middle-aged figures at the centre, then three aged musicians at the right, the progression running left to right as the sources agree. The sting is who occupies the prime of life at the centre: not a hero but a dwarf, a white clown with a scarlet frog on his costume, and Bilboquet the troupe owner in brown knee breeches. The whole life of a troupe, or of a class, passes in one pan, and the centre of a life turns out to be a clown. **Takeaway:** run the ages left to right and put the joke at the peak; the structure promises a hero and delivers a clown.

## M3. The Walk-Past Spectator
The painting only resolves if the viewer walks past it from left to right, as a passerby at a boulevard sideshow would. Grimaces, per Johnson's reading, is deliberately disjointed across its five panels: the striped cloth breaks at the edge of panel two, a partial figure waits behind the stage, the stage planking is clear at both ends and vague and colliding in the middle, and the central figures' right feet have no heels and seem to follow the viewer around. The Salon of 1888 hung it at the right height for exactly this experience, in the last gallery, where the critic of L'Echo de Paris called Pelez a "bad neighbor" for the lesser artists in the final room. Stand still and the picture looks incoherent; walk and the space clicks into place, then the last figure is cut off by the edge, leaving an abbreviated experience, as if the parade went on past the frame. **Takeaway:** make the picture a walk, not a view; the spectator's feet are part of the composition.

## M4. No Audience Inside the Frame
The spectacle is painted with no spectators in it, so the viewer is forced into the spectator's place. Grimaces has no audience, unlike Seurat's Parade de cirque, shown the same year, whose silhouetted heads keep the viewer at a distance; Johnson compares the confrontation to Manet's Olympia and the Bar at the Folies-Bergere, where the viewer is precariously conflated with the consumer. There is nobody in the picture to watch on your behalf; you are the crowd, and the performers stare past you or through you, and the one who pays the thirty centimes is standing where you stand. **Takeaway:** leave the audience out and the viewer has to take its seat; the empty place in front of the stage is the viewer.

## M5. The Frontal Stare
A single isolated figure, full-face, looking straight out of the picture at the viewer, with no anecdote around them. The mother in Sans asile (1883) stares from sunken eyes straight at the viewer while her children huddle in filthy blankets and sacking; Petit Misere (Salon 1886) stands in the doorway of Pelez's studio at 62, boulevard de Clichy, in a grown man's threadbare jacket, holding an oversized bourgeois top hat, with a vacant stare; the Street Child (1880, Montreal) sits on bare ground smoking and looks out; and in the La Bouchee de pain studies, among twenty men seen in profile, from the rear, or as silhouettes, exactly one figure is frontal and stares at the viewer. The stare holds; it does not invite or accuse, it waits, and the viewer has to decide what to do with it. **Takeaway:** point one face at the viewer and stop telling a story; the stare is the whole transaction.

## M6. The Oversized Hand-Me-Down
A child dressed in adult clothes far too big for them, the misfit itself doing the storytelling. Petit Misere's grown-man's threadbare jacket and oversized bourgeois top hat; the central boy of La Vachalcade (c. 1896-1900) in an adult's jacket and a huge hat; the Little Lemon Vendor's oversized derby, repeated across six versions. The costume swallows the child; the gap between the small body and the big clothes is the whole biography, no caption needed -- the clothes say the child inherited them from a grown man who is gone, or bought them from a dead man's stall, and the hat says the child is playing at being the bourgeois who will not feed him. **Takeaway:** dress the child in a dead man's clothes; the misfit is the biography.

## M7. Asleep or Dead
A deliberate ambiguity about whether a reclining child is sleeping or dead. Un Martyr - Le Marchand de violettes (1885, Petit Palais) is the type: a street boy, eyes closed, mouth agape, one small bunch of violets fallen from his tray, impossible to tell whether he is asleep or dead. The Sleeping Laundress (c. 1880) carries a faint smile on a face that could be resting or gone; Un Nid de misere (1887) is a horizontal close-up of two sleeping boys, praised by critics and called by Kazerouni one of the most emblematic examples of la peinture miserabiliste. The viewer keeps checking for breath; the picture refuses to settle the question, and that refusal is the point, because a sleeping child is a rest and a dead child is an indictment, and Pelez will not tell you which one you are looking at. **Takeaway:** do not settle whether the child breathes; the uncertainty is the accusation.

## M8. The Single Touch of Colour in the Grey
An almost monochrome grey-brown world with exactly one saturated accent that the eye cannot escape. Au lavoir (1880) is the type: statuesque washerwomen in a grey field, and a bag of bluing as the only touch of colour. In Grimaces the accent is the heraldic scarlet frog at the epicentre of the canvas, on the white clown's costume, with the flat green front of the stage and the red backdrop behind it; the red, green-eyed toad on the clown's costume was noted by the Sotheby's account of the smaller version. The eye is pulled to the one bright thing, then notices how grey everything else is; the accent measures the drabness around it, the way the bluing measures the washhouse. **Takeaway:** keep the world grey and spend your colour once; one bright thing makes the drabness visible.

## M9. Ironic Advertising Backdrop
Cheerful commercial signage pasted behind misery, the poster contradicting the people in front of it. Sans asile's backdrop is advertisements proclaiming a Grande Fete and dancing, behind a mother and five children with nothing; the price signs in Grimaces have zeros that formally echo the metal loops holding the parrots above the stage; La Vachalcade carries a banner reading "Misere" decorated with a dead rat, a joke on the Le Rat Mort cafe at Place Pigalle. The background sells joy while the foreground cannot afford it; the contradiction is visible in a single glance and needs no narration. **Takeaway:** hang the advertisement behind the beggar; the poster does the preaching for you.

## M10. History-Painting Scale for the Poor
Monumental scale and academic finish, the apparatus of history painting, applied to beggars, child vendors and circus performers. Grimaces at 222 by 625 cm; L'Humanite six feet longer and two feet taller still. The state bought his early history pictures, then stopped buying once the subjects became the poor -- the last government purchase was La Victime in 1886 -- and Montrosier wrote in 1888 that "artists love to paint misery elevated to the rank of history painting". The sheer size forces a grand manner onto small lives; the viewer stands before a beggar the way they would stand before a coronation, and the scale is the argument that these lives are worth a history picture. **Takeaway:** paint the poor at the size of a coronation; the scale is the claim.

## Evolution
1866-1879, the academic phase. Pelez enters the Salon at eighteen with history painting, the approved academic genre, and stays in it for thirteen years: Adam and Eve (1876), The Death of the Emperor Commodus (1879), a lurid set piece in which a hired wrestler bends over the strangled emperor's corpse in his bath while a veiled woman, probably a courtesan, looks on in surprise. Training is the whole story here: three years of private preparation with Barrias, admission to the Ecole in 1870, three years in Cabanel's studio. The medals arrive on schedule, third class 1876, second class 1879, first class 1880, and with the first-class medal he becomes hors concours, free to exhibit without jury approval for life.

1880-1883, the turn. The change is dated by the sources to 1880, "under the influence of the paintings of the naturalist Jules Bastien-Lepage and of a social awakening". The 1880 Salon already shows it: Au lavoir replaces myth with washerwomen, and the single bag of bluing is the only touch of colour in a grey field. The Eclectic Light account says he "changed direction completely, just as Buland was doing" in the early 1880s. The mechanism is not a change of technique -- he keeps the academic finish -- but a change of subject: the same rigorous drawing and life-size figure scale is now applied to laundresses, street children and the homeless. Sotheby's puts it plainly: "the Salon themes of Cabanel had disappeared and in their place, Pelez began to favor scenes depicting the tragic fate of some of the most impoverished members of the French working class." One history subject lingers in the transition year, Les Depouilles opimes (1880), and is kept in the works list where it is dated.

1884-1888, the miserabiliste peak. After 1881 Pelez "became known as the painter of poverty, and he insistently used the word misere in his titles". The system has three moves, all visible in the works list. First, the frontal single figure: isolated street children standing full-face to the viewer, Gamin des rues (1880), Un martyr (1885), the lemon vendor series, repeatedly compared to Murillo's beggar boys, and connected by Segolene Le Men to the traditional cris de Paris and to his father Raymond's illustrations of small trades. Second, the horizontal group close-up: Sans asile (1883) and Un nid de misere (1887) press a family or a pair of sleeping boys into a shallow, frieze-like band, with the mother staring straight out at the viewer. Third, deliberate ambiguity of state: the violet vendor may be asleep or dead; the palette stays narrow and grey, "drawing technique in the service of social subjects, and a fastidious avoidance of anecdote", in Anna Green's phrase quoted by Van Nimmen. Zola labelled him a naturalist in Le Voltaire in June 1880; Josephin Peladan called him "the painter of pity". The political charge was real: Collet observes Pelez "made class antagonism visible in his canvases", and press reactions in the 1880s made his work "a surprising catalyst of the most diametrically opposed political and social energies". At the same time critics accused him of exploiting the poor and of using history-painting scale to aggrandize his subjects. The one detour in this period is the reversal portrait: A Family (1884), ten identically groomed de Lesseps children, reading like a studio photograph, is answered twelve years later by La Vachalcade, thirteen revelers with shorn heads replacing curls, masks replacing groomed faces, and a "Misere" banner replacing the family order -- the same group-portrait composition painted twice, once for the rich and once for the poor, so the second picture indicts the first.

1889-1896, honours then collapse. Silver medal at the 1889 Exposition Universelle; Legion d'honneur 1891. He then shuns the Salon until he has another panoramic entry. Grimaces (1888) scales the miserabiliste subject up to 222 by 625 cm in five panels and changes the viewing contract: Johnson's analysis is the key source, the painting deliberately disjointed across its panels, a break in the striped cloth, a disembodied arm drawing back a red curtain, a dwarf painted across a panel divide, and it only resolves if the viewer walks past it left to right, as a boulevard passerby would pass a sideshow. The figures run left to right as an ages-of-man progression, child to decrepit musicians, with the prime of life represented by a dwarf, a clown and the troupe-master Bilboquet. Each figure is isolated, with no contact even where bodies are close; the chained monkey and parrots with clipped wings above them mark the same captivity. The harsh daylight, ill-fitting costumes, and unfinished passages -- the shoes of the middle girl and the last musician are barely sketched -- break with Salon naturalism toward something Johnson reads as proto-modernist fragmentation. The mask and the ill-fitting costume are part of the same break: the figures no longer embody their roles but indicate the separation between the role and the individual, the white clown's powdered face smiling while the eyes do not, the seven full-face masks in La Vachalcade, the dancers changing from dull street clothes to pearly white tutus backstage in the lost La Misere a l'Opera. L'Humanite (1896) pushes the same device further, a park frieze of rich and poor facing the viewer under a crucifix they ignore, and its hostile reception ends his public career.

1896-1913, the recluse years. After 1896 he never exhibits or sells again, and turns to what the sources call a reclusive, almost mystical life, but he keeps painting in the studio at 62, boulevard de Clichy. The late work changes in three ways. Scale and finish shrink: the lemon vendor is painted six times, never exhibited; the late studies are thinly painted with drawing showing through, in a limited palette of neutral browns, a fresco-like technique that leaves academic rigour behind, and the thin paint admits the picture is a made thing, not a window. Subjects turn inward and ritual: eleven orphan girls kneeling in prayer (Les Orphelines, c. 1901), dancers dressing backstage (Les Danseuses, 1905-1909), echoing the lost La Misere a l'Opera of 1885. The breadline as final statement: La Bouchee de pain (1908) returns to the public subject one last time, twenty men queuing outside a soup kitchen, mostly in profile or from behind, one frontal stare, a government commission that was never delivered or shown. He also joins the Montmartre carnival world directly: organizer of the Vachalcade processions of 1896 and 1897, designer of a poster and a float, contributor to the magazine La Vache enragee. La Vachalcade (c. 1896-1900) is the painted record of that world, and in Johnson's reading its carnival freedom is shown as "a monstrous illusion". Through it all runs the book of the poor: his 1901 plea to the City of Paris, each canvas a page in a book describing the story of the poor of the city, with the same models returning across pictures and the same subjects repeated in versions, the same boy in a new picture the reward for watching the whole run.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic of Pelez is a logic of kept apparatus and changed subject. He never abandons the academic technique he learned from Cabanel; Gallery 19C suggests it was "perhaps... mastery of the Academic technique that has disqualified him as being considered an innovator". The finish stays, the scale stays, the Salon stays -- he remains loyal to the Societe des Artistes Francais after the 1890 split and judges entries until 1912 -- and what changes is who stands in front of the finish. That is the whole move, and it is why the pictures are uncomfortable in a way a looser painting would not be: a beggar painted with the care of a Venus is a claim, not a sketch. The first logic is the census: the parade row, the ages of man, the repeated models, the book of the poor -- Pelez counts the poor the way a naturalist counts specimens, and the count is the accusation. The second logic is the stare: the frontal figure, the asleep-or-dead child, the no-audience stage -- the picture refuses a story and offers a condition instead, and the viewer, given no anecdote and no spectator to hide behind, has to supply the verdict. The third logic is the contradiction in one frame: the advertising behind the beggar, the carnival banner reading Misere, the rich children reversed into masked revelers, the crucifix the crowd ignores with its back turned. Pelez does not preach; he puts the two registers in the same picture and lets them indict each other. The fourth logic is the withdrawal: after 1896 the pictures get thinner, quieter and more ritual, the paint admitting it is paint, the subjects retreating from the street into the chapel and the dressing room, and the painter himself retreating from the public into the studio, still a juror, still painting, with no audience left at all. The recluse is the last mechanism: the man who painted the poor for the Salon stops showing the poor to anyone, and keeps painting them anyway, because the book was the point, not the buyer.

POSITIONS
P1. History first, then the break. His first works were history paintings, "The Death of the Emperor Commodus" (1879) and "Adam and Eve" (1876); from 1880, under the influence of Jules Bastien-Lepage and "a prise de conscience sociale" (a social awakening), he left history subjects for the popular classes.
P2. The book of the poor. In 1901 he wrote a plea to the City of Paris to preserve his work as a whole: each painting was a page in a book in which he had written the story of the Parisian poor. This is his own stated program, the load-bearing self-description of the whole late career.
P3. "Misere" as a program word. After 1881 he became known as the painter of poverty and "insistently used the word misere in his titles": "Misere" (1886), "Un Nid de misere" (1887), "Grimaces et misere" (1888), "La Misere a l'opera" (1885).
P4. Academic finish kept, applied to low subjects. He never abandoned the academic technique he learned from Cabanel; Gallery 19C suggests it was "perhaps... mastery of the Academic technique that has disqualified him as being considered an innovator". The critic Emile Henriot said he painted "almost as if 'there is mud in his brush'". Meticulous detail plus emotional distance, not loose handling.
P5. The frontal stare. His characteristic mature device is the vertical, frontal, isolated figure that looks straight out: the mother in "Homeless" (1883) "stares from sunken eyes straight at the viewer"; the street boys are "vertical frontal figures of isolated street children". The stare is the mechanism, not the narrative.
P6. No anecdote. Anna Green, quoted in Van Nimmen: Pelez's pictures show "a fastidious avoidance of anecdote", "narrow color ranges, a drawing technique in the service of social subjects". The picture refuses a story and offers a condition instead.
P7. The parade as a stage, the viewer as passerby. "Grimaces et misere" (1888) is five panels, 222 by 625 cm, a shallow makeshift stage; Johnson argues the painting only resolves if the viewer walks past it left to right, as a boulevard passerby would pass a sideshow. The ages-of-man progression runs left to right, young to old.
P8. Repetition and versioning as method. He painted "The Little Lemon Vendor" (c. 1895-97) in six different versions, never shown in a Salon. The same boy, the same oversized adult jacket and huge hat, recurs across "La Vachalcade" and other canvases. The motif is reworked, not repeated for sale.
P9. Monumental scale for the lowest subjects. "Grimaces et misere" is over twenty feet wide; "Humanity! Christ in the Square" (1896) "was six feet longer than his sideshow painting and two feet taller". History-painting scale is hijacked for beggars, performers and the homeless.
P10. Withdrawal after 1896. After the failure of "L'Humanite" at the Salon of 1896 he "became something of a recluse, refusing to exhibit or sell his work"; Johnson: "After this reception, Pelez retreated completely from the art world, never publicly showing or selling his work again". He painted on in the studio until 1913 without a public.
P11. Loyalty to the Salon system to the end. He "remained loyal to the Societe des Artistes Francais after the Salon split in 1890 and continued to judge entries until the year before his death". The recluse was still a Salon juror.
P12. Carnival as practice, not just subject. He took part in the Montmartre Vachalcades of March 1896 and June 1897, designed a poster and a float ("Temple of the Golden Calf") for the second, and painted "La Vachalcade" (c. 1896-1900) from the processions. The painter of misery was also a carnival organiser.
P13. Zola's label. Emile Zola labelled him a naturalist (Johnson, citing Zola, Le Voltaire, 18-22 June 1880). The label stuck and misleads: Johnson argues "Grimaces" "does not operate in accordance with positivist, naturalist ideologies".
P14. Peladan's label. Josephin Peladan called him "le peintre de pitie", the painter of pity.
P15. Hamerton's verdict. The British critic Philip Gilbert Hamerton, on the 1889 Exposition Universelle: "of all modern French painters, it is certainly M. Pelez whose sympathy with the suffering classes expresses itself with the most poignant force".
P16. Rosenblum's rescue. Robert Rosenblum's 1981 essay "Fernand Pelez, or The Other Side of the Post-Impressionist Coin" closed: "When future generations of art historians reconstruct these years, may they not forget Fernand Pelez!" The 2009 retrospective was the answer, 28 years later.
P17. The critics split in 1888 and 1896. Hugues Le Roux in Le Temps, 1 May 1888: the sideshow was "very precisely observed". Gustave Geffroy on "L'Humanite" (1896) accused Pelez of believing painting could be revolutionised "simply by choosing a new subject that would attract attention" and called the picture "somewhere between a representation of vaudeville, a desire to make a fierce self-assertion and supernatural evocation". Same painter, opposite verdicts, eight years apart.
P18. Commercial failure in his own lifetime. English Wikipedia: "Pelez was a commercial failure in the 1880s". Gallery 19C: "critics often protested and official patronage dropped off". "Homeless" (1883) "won critical praise yet found no purchaser until the City of Paris acquired it from the artist's estate". Against this, Eclectic Light calls him "in his time very popular with the public and critics, and highly successful": popular and unsold at once, and both are carried here.
P19. Posthumous rescue by the city. "Grimaces et misere was one of four major works astutely purchased by the City of Paris from the exhibition in Pelez's studio after his death in August 1913". The city that had bought one Salon painting a year from him in 1875-1880 bought the rest after he died.
P20. The Seurat double. "Grimaces" has become "the 'norm' from which Parade deviates: in the conversation between the two works, Pelez's naturalism gives way to Seurat's avant-gardism". Rosenblum called it "a virtual double, but in an academic realist vocabulary, of Seurat's circus theme". Pelez is now read through the painting that eclipsed him.

## Load-Bearing Corrections
C0. Birth year: 1848, not 1843. The 2009 Petit Palais curators "established his birth date as 1848. Pelez himself had stated it as five years earlier"; English Wikipedia still gives 18 January 1843, and Peladan's 1913 tribute is titled "Fernand Pelez (1843-1913)". The 1848 date is used throughout; weekday Tuesday, verified with date -d.
C1. Father: the illustrator Raymond Pelez (1814-1874), not "Fernand Pelez de Cordova (1820-1899)". English Wikipedia names the father Fernand Pelez de Cordova; French Wikipedia and the 2009 catalogue say the father was Raymond Pelez and Fernand Pelez de Cordova was the uncle. The Raymond version is used throughout.
C2. Pupil chronology: do not assert a precise start year for the Cabanel studio. Eclectic Light says Pelez was Cabanel's pupil "in the mid to late 1860s", while the Van Nimmen review says he was admitted to the Ecole in 1870; only the three years in Cabanel's studio, per Bonnet, is asserted.
C3. Success vs failure: both are sourced and both are carried. Eclectic Light calls Pelez "in his time very popular with the public and critics, and highly successful", while English Wikipedia calls him "a commercial failure in the 1880s" and the Van Nimmen review shows Homeless unsold until the estate sale. Popular and unsold at once is the record, not a contradiction to resolve by picking one.
C4. Grimaces medium: oil on canvas, five panels (Petit Palais). Johnson's figure captions call the medium "oil on panel" while her text and all other sources say oil on canvas in five panels; the captions are treated as an error.
C5. Grimaces location: Petit Palais, NOT the Musee d'Orsay. MyOpenMuseum search lines place Grimaces at the Musee d'Orsay; the Petit Palais holds it, and the Orsay placement is not repeated anywhere in this study.
C6. L'Humanite was exhibited at the Salon of 1896 and widely disliked, not rejected by the jury. The WahooArt search result saying it was "rejected by the jury" is wrong; Johnson and Sotheby's say it was exhibited and disliked, failing to win the Medaille d'honneur. The painting itself is lost, known only from a Vizzavona glass negative and two surviving studies.
C7. Medal years: third class 1876, second class 1879, first class 1880 (French Wikipedia, Bonjour Paris). MyOpenMuseum's medal dates of 1880 (third class) and 1889 (second class) are wrong and are not used.
C8. La Bouchee de pain dating: the project began in 1881 as "Work", was renamed "Charity", and the finished painting is dated 1908; French Wikipedia gives "1892-1908". It is presented here as a long-gestation work, not a single-year work.
C9. Misere vs Petit Misere: the Salon 1886 "Misere" and the Musee d'Orsay "Misere" (c. 1886) may be two different paintings conflated in the sources, and Petit Misere (Mendiant au chapeau, Salon 1886) is a distinct picture, sold to the Musee d'Orsay at TEFAF Maastricht in March 2023. The three are kept distinct in this study and the Orsay holdings are flagged, not settled.
C10. La Vachalcade dating: 1896 in French Wikipedia and Eclectic Light, c. 1896-1900 in the Van Nimmen review and Johnson. The range is used throughout.
C11. Quimper lemon merchant: Le Marchand de citrons at the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Quimper, may be one of the six Petit marchand de citrons versions rather than a separate work; the sources read do not settle this, and it is counted separately in the bio leg's list with this flag, not as a proven separate work.
C12. Retrospective weekdays: the bio leg's date table lists the retrospective as opening "1909-09-24: Friday" and closing "1910-01-17: Monday", both wrong years and wrong weekdays. The retrospective opened Thursday 24 September 2009 and closed Sunday 17 January 2010, per the parent's own date -d run in America/New_York, and those weekdays are used here.
C13. Network count: the network leg's header says 3 of the 40 network names are studied, counting Eugene Atget, who is not in N1-N40 (he is recorded only as an evidenced extra). The 40 contain 2 studied (Cabanel #492, Regnault #487) and 38 verified not studied, as the leg's own correction note says, and as the parent's script confirms.
