## The life: the researcher who kept the sketchbook

The biography is unusually well documented because she documents it herself: a full CV and project pages on her own site (eyesofpanda.com), two long interviews (Dirt Alley Design, 23 September 2020, 186 lines; Bright Moments, 3 June 2023, 80 lines), and a Goldsmiths residency page. The study does not fill the gaps those sources leave.

Shiqing (Licia) He, born 1992, grew up in China. No city, province, or region is given in any source found (C1807). She came to the United States for college: Union College, Schenectady, NY, 2011-2014, a double B.S. in Computer Science and Studio Art, Phi Beta Kappa and Union Scholar. The two honours theses already contain the whole later practice in miniature: the CS thesis, "Structuring the Unstructured Note: Automatic Organizing and Formatting for lecture notes" (advisor Kristina Striegnitz), and the Studio Art thesis, "Childhood Memory" (advisor Sandy Wimer) -- systems on one side, memory on the other. Her undergraduate focus was traditional printmaking, not digital art.

Between degrees she spent a year as a Thomas J. Watson Fellow (2014-15), researching natural colour production around the globe -- the CV says "Multiple Locations across 10 Countries" (C1808) -- and a summer as a research intern at the IBM Beijing Research Lab (2013). Then the PhD: Information Science at the University of Michigan, 2015-2021, advisor Eytan Adar, committee Sophia Brueckner, Ron Eglash, Matthew Kay. The dissertation, "Finding the Grammar of Generative Craft", introduced Grammar-driven Craft Design Tools (GCDTs): tools that "explicitly embed and utilize craft domain knowledge as their primary mechanisms and interfaces." The PhD was not a detour from the art; it was the art's theory, written first. She was already making generative work during it -- poker-card and Truchet generators in D3.js while teaching D3 -- and already thinking about fabrication: a student competition to build a robot produced a doodling robot for her team, "which made me realize how difficult it is to create a high-precision drawing robot."

The plotter arrived by accident. In the summer of 2019, as a UX-Data Visualization intern at Google in Sunnyvale, she "accidentally had access to an Axidraw during my internship. It was love at first try." Her first AxiDraw arrived 1 October 2019. Inktober became a 30-day challenge, which became four consecutive 100-day challenges -- 300+ days of daily plotting by November 2020, hundreds of works a year by 2023, "three to four years, and I produce hundreds of works a year."

After the PhD she took the tenure-track job the whole education had been preparing her for: assistant professor of Visualization at Texas A&M, 2021-2023, where she founded the Generative Craft Lab. She taught VIZA689 and VIST270, published at VIS'21 and DIS'21, and was still an assistant professor in print in November 2022. Then, in early 2023, she quit it. The base moved with the decision: Texas to London in about May 2023 ("I left the United States about two weeks ago and am now a full-time generative artist based in London"), London to Tokyo in February 2026 (C1800, resolved -- see the base note below). She has been a full-time artist since, founder of The Generative Craft Lab, LTD (2023-present; whether the LTD is the Texas A&M lab carried on independently or a new entity reusing the name is unstated, C1801), and -- almost certainly, though neither source states dates -- a resident at Goldsmiths' EACVA programme (Embodied Agents in Contemporary Visual Art) in 2024-25, one of four artists selected from 60+ applicants, with Gretta Louw, Anna Mirkin, and Patrick Tresset (C1802).

Base note: the "London vs Tokyo" conflict flagged by the #596 run is resolved, not a contradiction. Dated evidence: London from about May 2023 (Bright Moments interview, published 2023-06-03); Tokyo from February 2026 (her own Please Respond project page, documenting work done 10-25 February 2026: "when I started working on this project, I just relocated from the UK to JP", and "on a long commute to the other side of Tokyo"); her own About page, crawled 2026-11-15, says "Licia currently lives and works in Tokyo as a full-time artist." Gazelli, Le Random, Verse, Artsy, and Goldsmiths still say London because they reproduce her 2023-era bio verbatim and were never updated. Her own site is the authoritative, current source.

## The turn: 2023, quitting the tenure track

The turn has a date and a cause, and both are her own words. "In early 2023, I quit a job that I spent years preparing for, left a country after calling it home for more than a decade, and moved to a city I had never visited ... so that I can live my dream." The first major algorithm after the decision is *Sparkling Goodbye* (Bright Moments Tokyo, 2023), built during the move itself: "celebrates all departures... a chaotic blend of happiness, sadness, serenity, and anxiety." The series is the turn made visible -- rotating and expanding geometries, twenty sets of fully-generative colour rules, and a texture that looks like acrylic impasto from a distance and reveals machine marks up close. The work after 2023 is not a different practice; it is the same practice with the safety net removed. The PhD years built the tools, the Texas years built the lab, and the London years spent both: three major systems in 2023 alone (*Drifting Dreams*, *Sparkling Goodbye*, *Fictional Lullaby*), each with a distinct emotional brief and rendering metaphor, each with its own colour-rule engine. The turn is also visible in the framing: before 2023 the plotter is a tool she operates; from 2024 the plotter is a "creative partner" she talks to, and the titles become sentences addressed to it.

## The practice: systems, robots, materials

Her working method is stated plainly and it is three pillars, not a style. "Systems, robots, and materials are the three pillars of my practice." The process, from the Bright Moments interview: "For almost every project I do, I start with some form of physical sketch. And for more elaborate long-form projects, like *Running Moon* and *Drifting Dreams*, I'll do a large group of sketches before I translate it into a generative system." The coding rhythm is a sprint and a rest: "I might code for 12 hours a day for a week and then take a week off for testing and observations before getting back into another round of coding." She codes to fast-paced pop music in foreign languages.

Three standing habits organise the practice. First, the rules are the work: "When I am creating generative art, I focus on the rules that I am making and breaking. I find it exciting to turn simple rules into visual forms and to explore variations of these forms." Second, the emotion is the brief: "My works are emotional: rather than purely focusing on technical capabilities or mathematical precision, I create art to capture emotion and stories." Third, the material gets a vote: "I used to spend a lot of time picking the perfect color scheme. The spontaneity that comes with watercolor helped me to stay away from the perfectionism mindset. Because the digital pattern would usually look completely different from the finished painting, sometimes it becomes hard to predict the output. Therefore, the only option is to plot, observe, and experiment again."

The stack migrated with the practice: JavaScript and D3.js first ("I started in JavaScript (D3.js), gradually moved to python"), Python now for the plotter line, pure JavaScript with no external dependencies for *Sparkling Goodbye*. The plotter line runs AxiDraw pen plotters, by 2024 an AxiDraw A1 (nicknamed 'Elephanty') with customised extra-fine brushes and assorted watermedia. The pipeline is her own, built from scratch: physical sketch, algorithm generating a list of points and SVG path elements, chunking by refill distance, direct export to Python scripts via the AxiDraw API (pyaxidraw), skipping Inkscape because complex files load slowly. The materials tested read like a supply cupboard emptied in order: handmade watercolor (hand-mulled, bigger pigment particles, granulation effects), fountain-pen inks (Diamine, Organic Studio -- valued for colour selection and sheening), liquid watercolor, eyeshadow (low pigment, very time-consuming, worked better by machine than by hand), wall paint, acrylic, ink, yupo, watercolor, and printmaking papers. She calls herself "an art supply enthusiast" and says she is "in love with chaos and bold strokes", "finding balance in chaos."

The inspirations she names are lineage, not decoration: Harold Cohen, the early computer-art pioneer, for the human-machine collaboration ("how machines are not merely enhancing creative expression; they enable creative expression"); Robert and Sonia Delaunay's Orphic Cubism, which Gazelli cites for her "dynamic geometry suggesting musical rhythm and natural light." Her communities are the plotter ones: Generative Artists Club and #plottertwitter, where she posts daily updates. "I am very active on Instagram because of my plotters."

## The evolution: from screen toys to knitting, the material keeps changing

The arc has seven phases, and the constant is the three pillars: a rule system, a physical material, and an emotion. What migrates is the material -- pixels, ink, watercolor, thread, yarn, carpet -- and the plotter's role, from tool to twin to partner to collaborator inside someone else's installation.

Phase 1, screen toys (c. 2018-2019): D3.js browser generators -- a poker-card generator, a Truchet-pattern generator, a hidden-message puzzle -- built while teaching D3 during the PhD. Learning SVG; no physical output. No images of these specific pieces were accessible.

Phase 2, the robot arrives (2019-2020): first AxiDraw, 1 October 2019; Inktober into four 100-day challenges; polygons and lines, then curves, shape filling, noise generators; pens, then eyeshadow, wall paint, acrylic, ink; ThreadPlotter, the open-source toolkit turning an X-Y plotter into a punch-needle embroidery machine. The titles are shouted text fragments ("BIG DEAL. AND GUS -- WHEN YOU SENT THE TELEGRAM?"). The visual language is loose and organic: a 2020 watercolor inspected as an image shows thin green vine-like stems with buds and berries drifting over wet pink and blue washes, wet-on-wet bleed as the whole image -- far freer and more fluid than the later geometric systems. The core belief forms here: chaos and bold strokes over machine precision.

Phase 3, the system becomes the work (2021): *Endless Monologue* (-GRAPH, Feral File, curated by Casey Reas, opened 30 November 2021). The turning point for the digital-physical relationship: before this, digital renderings were demo previews; from *Endless Monologue* on, the digital rendering IS a primary output, and the plotter drawing is its twin. Lines become solid blocks; colour is roughly aligned across media; the plotted results look "delightfully different" while sharing the same spirit. Thirty unique physical plotter drawings, software holding millions of other compositions (C1850 on the count).

Phase 4, the big web system (2022): *Running Moon* (Art Blocks Curated, edition of 500, sold out in under five minutes). About a year to develop, her "most intricate web-based generative art system" to date at the time: 10 expansion-shape types spreading from a focal point and cutting the canvas into shapes, 8 segmenting lines imposed like stained-glass leading, brush-pen-like texture stripes "soldering" the pieces together, 36 palettes in 5 categories, each starting from 6 seed colours expanded by a colour-expanding algorithm. She took her first stained-glass class while building it. In parallel, *Blink of Curves* (June 2022), the Python curve engine that becomes *Drifting Dreams*.

Phase 5, the colour leap and the emotional turn (2023): seed colours give way to generative colour RULES (the Color Rules Generator, open-sourced) -- the single most important technical change in her 2022-2023 work. Three major systems in one year, each with a distinct emotional brief: *Drifting Dreams* (coloured pencil, chaotic repetition under a clock structure, inspired by her experience as a woman in the tech industry; all proceeds donated to organisations supporting female and non-binary technologists), *Sparkling Goodbye* (pure digital, machine marks kept visible, rotating geometries, built during the US-to-UK move), *Fictional Lullaby* (night and insomnia, 16 physical colours including glitter stars and liquid leaf, 15-24 hours per painting, three render modes, digital and physical deliberately non-identical). *Spring in the Winter* brings the expansion algorithm back in Python, with titles from a note written while making it: "When it's sunny, I miss the cloud. When it's dark, I miss the light. This is me in the winter, missing the spring." She quits academia and goes full-time.

Phase 6, the plotter as partner (2024): *A Conversation / Spreading Light* (four unique plotter-rendered paintings, 76 x 57 cm, centrepiece of her first solo exhibition, "I Have To Talk To A Plotter", GAZELL.iO, 7 June-10 July 2024) and the *An Argument* series (two named works, same size, shown at Art Dubai Digital 2025). The titles are sentence fragments mid-dialogue, and a live plotter created five artworks during the solo show. The conversation metaphor replaces the system metaphor in how she frames the work, while the code stays central.

Phase 7, craft systems, collaboration, canonisation (2025-2026): the EACVA residency reimagines robotic painting through challenging materials (natural and mineral pigments, impasto, pen adaptors and paper stretchers built); *Yarntificial City* (2026) turns the grid into entrelac knitting, with a real knittable output; *In Conversation With Jacoba* (2026) puts her generative pattern system inside Claudia Hart's installation, drawn from the stained glass of the largely forgotten Jacoba van Heemskerck; the V&A acquires and displays "I'm Saying I'll Have to Check In" (accession E.314-2026) in *Degrees of Disruption: Vera Molnar and Generative Art* (16 October 2026-30 May 2027); *EMULATION: Selections from the Art Blocks 500* at the Museum of Art and Light. The through-line holds: a rule system, a physical material, and an emotion -- only the material keeps changing.

**Takeaway:** the evolution is a material migration, not a style change. The system stays -- rules, a governing structure, a colour engine -- and the body it is poured into changes: screen, paper, thread, yarn, carpet. A practice that changed its system every year would be restless; a practice that changes its material every few years is learning what the system is for.

## M1 -- The refill station: the machine that must stop to drink

The mechanism is the watercolor plotter's thirst, made into a system. A plotter that paints cannot run continuously: the brush dries, the pigment sinks. So she built a fixed paint and ink refill station at the edge of the plotter area -- an 11 x 2 wooden template with five glued plastic cups -- and a converter that breaks every SVG path into chunks shorter than a "refill distance" (different per brush), approximating Bezier curves with tiny 3-5 px lines, and inserting a refill instruction between chunks: a circle, to stir the watercolor and prevent the pigments from sinking. For ink or liquid watercolor the refill mark simplifies to a dot (pen down, pen up). Two questions govern every painting, in her own words: "first, where do I place my paint/ink refill station? Second, when should I refill my brush?"

The mechanism matters because the painting's rhythm is the machine's thirst. The tiny brush forces constant pen-up and pen-down servo cycles -- "my servo motors run out very quickly... there were weeks when I was replacing motors every other day." The plotter is raised on wooden blocks above a water tray so a spill cannot flood it; the table and wall are covered in paper and board against splashes. The wear is not a flaw in the setup; it is the setup's signature. A 12-20 hour watercolor plot is 12-20 hours of the machine stopping to drink, thousands of times, and every stop is a decision about when the colour is wet enough, mixed enough, alive enough. The refill circle is the smallest unit of the practice: a mark the viewer never sees, made only so the next visible mark can exist.

**Takeaway:** the refill is not overhead; it is the composition's hidden beat. A piece that hides its machine's needs is pretending the machine is a printer; a piece whose rhythm is the machine stopping to drink is honest about what a robot painter is.

## M2 -- Digital and physical as twins, deliberately non-identical

The mechanism is one system with two bodies that refuse to match. In *Endless Monologue* the digital rendering and the physical plotter drawing are the same composition rendered in two media: the digital side crisp vector waves of parallel stripes in vermilion-orange, cream, pale mint green, with navy-blue accents, on white -- clean, flat, precise; the physical side the identical stripe system in bleeding watercolor -- same palette but soft, granular, pooled and feathered, edges dissolving. The pair is the argument: one system, two bodies. She connected them by roughly aligning colour use and transforming lines into solid blocks, and the plotted results look "delightfully different" while sharing the same spirit.

*Fictional Lullaby* makes the refusal explicit. The software has three render modes: Paint Rendering (the default, painterly texture), Structure Rendering (the "R" key -- a clean vector view, close to what the plotter "sees"), and Line Rendering (hatch-line instructions for the plotter). The physical paintings use 16 colours where earlier works used 6, including two shimmering and glitter star colours and liquid leaf, hand-applied, for the eyes; dense watercolor and acrylic medium, the brush refilled after every inch of plotting; 15-24 hours per painting. And the stated goal is that digital and physical outputs should NOT look identical: "My robots are not here to replicate but to create."

The mechanism matters because it is the opposite of the print pipeline. Zumbrunn's code generates and the print fixes; He's code generates twice, once for the screen and once for the robot, and the two are allowed -- required -- to disagree. The disagreement is where the material speaks: watercolor bleeds, granulates, pools; the screen does not. A system that produced identical twins would be a printer driver; a system that produces fraternal twins is a collaboration.

**Takeaway:** the twin is not a copy; it is the same system in a different body, and the difference is the point. A piece whose digital and physical versions match is a file; a piece whose versions argue with each other is a practice.

## M3 -- Expansion from a focal point

The mechanism is a shape that grows until it meets another shape. In *Running Moon* it is the first of three elements: Expansion Shapes, 10 types, from a focal point curves gradually spread across the page and cut the canvas into shapes; Segmenting Lines, 8 types, imposed like stained-glass leading to balance the composition; Texture, brush-pen-like stripes that "solder" the pieces together. Five additional special modes. The rendering is inspired by stained glass (sharpness) and watercolor (softness of light), and the inspected image confirms it: a pale icy-blue field filled edge to edge with a diagonal lattice of stained-glass shards -- soft aqua, white, pale grey-green and sky blue, with small hot-orange accents and thin black outlines, many shapes carrying fine internal hatch lines. Airy, luminous, moonlit; there is no single moon disc -- the "moon" is the light itself. The series is "a search for boundaries between structure and fluidity, precision and errors. A quest for harmony."

The same mechanism returns in *Spring in the Winter* (2023), where the V&A record describes a Python-based expansion algorithm: "a collection of core shapes that fill the space whilst rotating and mutating", plotted with her AxiDraw A1 'Elephanty'. The inspected V&A image of "I'm Saying I'll Have to Check In" shows it: a dense swirling vortex of pink, orange, and red with small rectangular fragments and a few blue and purple touches, the swirl pulling the eye to a brighter circle top-left.

The mechanism matters because expansion is the opposite of tiling. A tile repeats; an expansion grows, and growth has a history: the first shape claims the centre, the later shapes negotiate the edges, and the segmenting lines are the treaty. The 36 palettes (each from 6 seed colours, expanded by a colour-expanding algorithm) are the same logic applied to colour: start small, grow outward, let the edges decide.

**Takeaway:** expansion is not decoration; it is a growth story told in shapes. A piece that tiles a pattern is wallpaper; a piece that grows shapes from a point until they meet is a landscape with a history.

## M4 -- The clock that governs the drift

The mechanism is chaotic repetition under a rigid structure. *Drifting Dreams* (2023) is built from two elements: Curves (5 major types; the mode selects the type; curve thickness varies by distance from the centre, reversible via Reverse Mode) and Lines. The curves are soft and repetitive, gradually filling the space to construct the main view, then expanding, distorting, and mutating to break the controlled composition -- but a clock-like rigid structure always governs it. Special modes name the levers: Grid Mode (a faint background grid), Double Mode and Cross Mode (enhanced section textures), Full Outline Mode (all curves versus a selection). The rendering is inspired by coloured pencils -- not a realistic simulation but the essence, via overlaid texture-treated dots and lines. The inspected image shows it: scalloped, scale-like curved tiles in soft blues, golds, and lavender on white, coloured-pencil grain of dots and fine lines, thin vertical gold lines threading through, the tiles shrinking and fragmenting toward the right as if dissolving. Gentle, airy, repetitive -- the "clock" reads as a slow radial order under the drift.

The mechanism matters because the clock is what makes the chaos legible. Without the governing structure the curves would be drift; without the drift the clock would be a diagram. The subject is the tension itself: "captures bittersweet moments along journeys to dreams", inspired by her experience as a woman in the tech industry. The clock is the industry -- regular, measured, unyielding -- and the drift is the journey through it. The same tension runs through the whole practice: segmenting lines against expansion, a grid against a warp, a refill schedule against wet paint.

**Takeaway:** the clock is not a constraint on the drift; it is what the drift is measured against. A piece with only repetition is a pattern; a piece with only chaos is a spill; a piece where a rigid structure governs soft curves that keep breaking it is a journey.

## M5 -- Colour as rules, not seeds

The mechanism is the single most important technical change in her 2022-2023 work, and she open-sourced it. Before 2023, a palette started from seed colours: pick 6, expand them with a colour-expanding algorithm (*Running Moon*: 36 palettes in 5 categories, each from 6 seeds). From *Drifting Dreams* on, the seed colours are replaced by colour RULES: pick a section of the colour spectrum and a rule for drawing shades from it, then expand. The Color Rules Generator (GitHub, "Color-Hoarding-with-Licia-He", with a tutorial and an online demo) is the tool; the rule sets are the practice. *Drifting Dreams*: 16 sets of colour rules. *Sparkling Goodbye*: 20 sets, 10 rules each. *Fictional Lullaby*: 10 sets, 5 rules each -- strong, contrasting palettes for the "head-spinning" feeling of thoughts at night.

The mechanism matters because rules are more distant than seeds. Outputs within one rule-based palette can be much more distant from each other than seed-based palettes allowed: the same rule set can produce a pale lavender tile and a deep gold one, and both are recognisably the same system. The seed workflow kept the palette close to the artist's taste; the rule workflow lets the palette surprise her, which is the point -- she had already learned from watercolor that the digital pattern would "usually look completely different from the finished painting", and the colour rules extend that unpredictability into the digital side itself. Colour stops being a choice she makes before the system runs and becomes a behaviour the system exhibits while it runs.

**Takeaway:** a palette is not a list of colours; it is a rule about how colours may relate. A piece that picks six colours is a mood board; a piece that picks a spectrum section and a rule for drawing shades from it is a colour system with room to surprise its own author.

## M6 -- The machine marks kept visible

The mechanism is the digital fingerprint, deliberately left in. *Sparkling Goodbye* (2023) is pure JavaScript with no external dependencies, built from groups of rotating and expanding geometries -- 8 major geometry types, 4 centre patterns, 4 background patterns -- and its texture is the signature: at a distance it looks like acrylic or oil impasto; up close it reveals machine marks -- sharp edges, ordered dots, perfect grid. She deliberately kept the digital fingerprint instead of simulating paint. The inspected image, an off-band test output in portrait format, confirms it: dense painterly dabs like thick acrylic -- teal and green ground, coral, salmon, orange and pink shapes, white dabs, dark navy accents, inside a flat terracotta-orange border frame. Chaotic, celebratory, deliberately "maniac" (her word) -- joy and anxiety in one field.

The mechanism matters because it is a refusal, and the refusal is the whole 2023 turn in miniature. The obvious move for a generative painter is to hide the machine -- to simulate impasto, to soften the edges, to make the digital look handmade. She does the opposite: the closer you stand, the more machine you see. The sharp edges, the ordered dots, the perfect grid are not failures of the simulation; they are the proof that no simulation was attempted. The machine is not pretending to be a painter; it is painting as a machine, and the painting is better for it. The same honesty runs through *Fictional Lullaby*'s Structure Rendering, which shows the clean vector view the plotter "sees" -- the machine's own view of the work, offered to the viewer without apology.

**Takeaway:** the machine mark is not a flaw to sand down; it is the signature of the tool, and hiding it is a lie about how the work was made. A piece that simulates paint is a costume; a piece that keeps its sharp edges, ordered dots, and perfect grid is a machine painting honestly.

## M7 -- The conversation with the machine

The mechanism is the plotter as a creative partner, addressed in sentences. *A Conversation / Spreading Light* (2024) is four unique plotter-rendered generative watercolor paintings, 76 x 57 cm, from her original Python algorithm "Spreading Light", rendered on an AxiDraw A1 with a customised extra-fine brush and assorted watermedia. The titles are sentence fragments, as if caught mid-dialogue with the machine: "I think I can make it work ...", "I've got a lot of that too.", "I know what you want to do!", "I'd like to talk to the Press." The inspected image of "I've got a lot of that too." shows the conversation's tone: a radiant sunburst and fan of multicolour rays -- cobalt and teal blue, green, yellow, orange-red -- bursting from a point left of centre, overlaid with translucent diagonal bands; vivid, saturated, kaleidoscopic. The show added two further unique works, and a live plotter machine created five artworks during the exhibition (C1857 -- their titles are unknown). The framing, via Gazelli, is explicit: the plotter as "creative partner" -- a conversation, not a printer.

*An Argument* (2024) is the same mechanism with the tone turned: "Well -- I -- I -- I..." and "No -- no -- no -- not yet." -- stammering sentence fragments, the argument as opposed to the conversation, same size, same Python plotter line, shown at Art Dubai Digital 2025. The mechanism matters because the titles are not labels; they are the dialogue the work is about. A generative system that produces variations is a machine; a generative system the artist talks to, argues with, and lets answer back in the gallery, live, is a partner. The inspiration she names for this is Harold Cohen, whose AARON was the first sustained attempt at a machine that draws as a collaborator rather than a tool -- and the Gazelli comparison to Delaunay's Orphic Cubism, "dynamic geometry suggesting musical rhythm and natural light", names what the conversation sounds like.

**Takeaway:** the partner is not a metaphor; it is a working relationship with a machine that answers back. A piece that uses a plotter as a printer is a print; a piece whose titles are sentences addressed to the machine, and whose gallery contains the machine still answering, is a conversation.

## M8 -- The grid becomes craft

The mechanism is the grid handed to a craft, and the craft handing something back. It appears twice, six years apart, and the second time it is the whole practice. *ThreadPlotter* (2019-2020) is the first instance: an open-source toolkit for designing and fabricating delicate punch-needle embroidery using X-Y plotters. She realised a plotter can perform every motion a punch needle needs, so the plotter drives a punch needle through fabric in programmed paths, and thread becomes the mark-making medium instead of ink or paint. The inspected photograph shows it: a concentric spiral of blue thread stitches, dark navy to pale sky blue, on pale mesh fabric, the needle visible at top right, loose thread ends and shadow beneath -- dense, textile, visibly handmade by machine.

*Yarntificial City* (2026) is the same mechanism at full scale. The grid system comes from entrelac knitting: many mini-grids, some rotated at different angles, pieced into a more complex grid. Every cell is a city block or neighbourhood; the "yarntificial beings" are the stitches living in them; the bird's-eye view is a real-time satellite map. The system is unusually completely documented: each city starts from real yarns she owns, in four groups (cotton fingering, highly saturated; wool worsted, muted greyish; acrylic sock weight, bright base with colourful dots; assorted blend, mixed weights); marls knitting (multi-strand) is visualised but ultra-rare, about 1 in 500 cities; 206 stitch patterns, each block at most two colours (main plus contrast), pattern functions ranging from simple column and row rules to cellular automata (a stitch is contrast if the adjacent three stitches in the previous row are contrast); five animation categories (colour changing, swiping, deformation, grid surfacing, grid glitching), inspired by Factorio and Minecraft -- constantly changing but easy to guess. A city holds more than 100,000 stitches. And the physical counterpart, Yarn City, is a separate Python module that turns a chosen view into knittable entrelac patterns -- a knitting chart with sequence and block info, spread-out chart, per-block charts. Code becomes a craft pattern becomes a garment. The work is dedicated to her grandmother Shubing Cai, who knitted out of necessity and taught her to knit as a child; after Shubing's death in 2025, Licia learned to knit herself and became obsessed with entrelac (Toshiyuki Shimada's "Entrelac Knitting"). The inspected image shows it: a diagonal entrelac grid of rotated square blocks, every block filled with a visible V-stitch knit texture, pastel pink, peach, grey, green and magenta, circular "tree" and "park" motifs in contrasting colours, zigzag stitch texture throughout -- at once a city map and a knitted swatch.

The mechanism matters because it closes the circle the PhD opened. Grammar-driven Craft Design Tools embedded craft domain knowledge as the mechanism; *Yarntificial City* is a craft domain -- knitting -- with its grammar made executable, and then made knittable again. The EACVA residency (2024-25) is the same move in the other direction: "Reimagining Robotic Paintings Through Challenging Materials" -- natural and mineral pigments, impasto, pen adaptors and paper stretchers built so the robot can handle what a brush handles easily. In both directions the material is not a surface the system prints on; it is a grammar the system speaks.

**Takeaway:** the craft is not a theme; it is a grammar the code learns to speak. A piece that pictures knitting is an illustration; a piece whose grid IS entrelac, whose stitches ARE cellular automata, and whose output IS a knitting chart, is a craft and a system in the same body.

## The aesthetic logic

The logic is a rule system, a physical material, and an emotion -- and only the material keeps changing. Every series in the practice is the same three pillars poured into a new body: pixels, ink, watercolor, thread, yarn, carpet. The system stays -- expansion from a point, curves against a governing structure, colour as rules -- and the body it is poured into decides what the system can say. Watercolor says bleed and granulation; thread says stitch and spiral; yarn says entrelac and city; carpet says a room you walk on.

The second logic is the machine as collaborator, not printer. The plotter's thirst sets the rhythm (M1); the digital twin and the physical twin are allowed to disagree (M2); the machine's marks are kept visible (M6); the machine is talked to, argued with, and left running live in the gallery (M7). The collaboration deepens across the phases: in 2019 the machine is a tool she operates; in 2021 it produces a twin; in 2024 it is a partner; in 2026 it is inside someone else's installation, drawing patterns from a forgotten woman's stained glass. Her control retreats one step in each phase while the system stays hers, because the system is the range of variation, not the single output -- and because the emotion is always hers. The machine never chooses the feeling; it chooses the form the feeling takes.

The third logic is emotion over precision, stated as a principle and kept as a practice. Every major system has an emotional brief, not a technical one: joy in *Running Moon* ("every piece conveys joy for me"), bittersweet journeys in *Drifting Dreams*, departures in *Sparkling Goodbye*, insomnia in *Fictional Lullaby*, missing the spring in *Spring in the Winter*, a grandmother's knitting in *Yarntificial City*. The precision is real -- 206 stitch patterns, 36 palettes, 16 rule sets -- but it is in service of the feeling, and the feeling is always specific: a move, a departure, a night awake, a winter missing spring. "I create art to capture emotion and stories."

**Takeaway:** the aesthetic is a system that keeps its body honest. A piece that hides its machine is a print pretending to be a painting; a piece that lets the watercolor bleed, the thread spiral, the yarn knit, and the machine's marks show is a system that knows what it is made of -- and a feeling that knows what it is about.
