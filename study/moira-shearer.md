## 1. Dunfermline, Ndola, and Legat

She was born Moira Shearer King at Morton Lodge, Dunfermline, the only child of civil engineer Harold Charles King and Margaret Crawford Reid, nee Shearer. In 1931 the family moved to Ndola, Northern Rhodesia, where her father worked and she had her first dancing lessons under Ethel Lacey, a former pupil of Enrico Cecchetti, on the Russian dance curriculum. The family returned to Scotland when she was ten; she was educated at Dunfermline High School and Bearsden Academy. In 1936 her mother took her to Nicholas Legat's London studio, where the manager, assuming a beginner, sent her to Flora Fairbairn -- and three months later Legat saw her dance at a private recital and said "This is no beginner", taking her as a pupil. Legat died in 1937 and she continued with his widow Nadine; at fourteen she was accepted into the Sadler's Wells Ballet School. In 1938, still a Legat pupil, she took a part in Mona Inglesby's Endymion at an all-star matinee at the Cambridge Theatre, and around the same time she modelled as Posy Fossil in advertisements for Noel Streatfeild's Ballet Shoes. [C33856]

Her formation was Russian, not English, and it was recognised by gatekeepers at every step before she ever joined a company: a Cecchetti-lineage teacher in Africa, Legat in London, Inglesby giving her a stage part at twelve, de Valois taking her into the school at fourteen. The "This is no beginner" anecdote is the whole of her early career in one sentence -- she was never an apprentice who became exceptional, she was exceptional and the institutions caught up with her.

**Takeaway:** a dancer formed in the Russian school arrives in an English company as an outsider by training -- and the outsider's lightness is exactly what the company cannot teach. [C33857]

## 2. Sadler's Wells, 1942, and the Fonteyn ceiling

She made her professional debut with Mona Inglesby's International Ballet in 1941, on a provincial tour and in a West End season, and joined Sadler's Wells in 1942 -- a soloist that year, a principal by 1944. The promotion year is itself conflicted: Britannica says she was promoted to ballerina in 1944, Encyclopedia.com says she earned the rank in 1946, and the Guardian says only that by 1944 she was a principal of the company; the difference is probably one of terminology, principal against the formal rank, but the years conflict on the face of it and are carried as a conflict. What is not conflicted is the ceiling. The Guardian's obituary states it flatly: she was the ballerina "over whom only Margot Fonteyn took precedence." Fonteyn wrote it herself in her autobiography: "Then came Moira Shearer, with her incredible airy lightness and ease, to be a real threat to my position. Moira was young, fresh, beautiful and different." The two were colleagues and competitors for as long as Shearer stayed -- seven years younger, red-haired against Fonteyn's dark, Russian-trained against Fonteyn's English, and never allowed past the one woman ahead of her.

**Takeaway:** a career with a ceiling built into it -- the threat is the role, and the threat never gets the top room because the top room is already occupied. [C33858]

## 3. Aurora, 1 March 1946

Sadler's Wells reopened at Covent Garden on 20 February 1946 with The Sleeping Beauty; Fonteyn danced Princess Aurora on the gala night, Pamela May the next night, and Shearer a week later, on 1 March 1946. The Manchester Guardian's verdict on her Aurora is the one the whole study keeps coming back to: "extraordinary elegance and grace... these are unteachable things, enchanting beyond technique." Aurora is the classical ballerina's examination piece -- the Rose Adagio, balanced on pointe and handed between four suitors without touching the ground, tests line and balance and nothing else -- and the review names exactly what Shearer had that could not be taught: lightness, ease, the airy quality Fonteyn herself envied. She was nineteen, a week behind the prima ballerina, and the paper was already saying the difference between them was not technique.

**Takeaway:** when a review says "unteachable", it is naming the mechanism -- the lightness is the technique, and the steps are only where it shows. [C33859]

## 4. Ashton and de Valois: creations for a teenager

The choreographers built on her while she was still a teenager. In 1943, at seventeen, she created Pride in Frederick Ashton's The Quest and the pas de trois girl in Ninette de Valois's Promenade -- a personified Deadly Sin and a sparkling trio, both created roles, not inherited repertory. In April 1946 she created one of the three ballerina roles in Ashton's Symphonic Variations, plotless, six dancers in white on a bare stage, with Fonteyn in the lead female role and Pamela May as the third woman; the Washington Post notes the visual contrast the casting made -- blonde May, brunette Fonteyn, red-haired Shearer, set off against each other by hair colour alone. The same year she danced Spring in Ashton's The Fairy Queen. The mechanism is a choreographer's trust exercise repeated three times: Ashton and de Valois each built new material on a seventeen-year-old's lightness, and the lightness was the material.

**Takeaway:** created roles are the test of a young dancer -- repertory can be learned, but a part built on your own lightness cannot be taken from you. [C33860]

## 5. Miracle in the Gorbals: the Lover and the Stranger

Miracle in the Gorbals, premiered 26 October 1944 at the Princes Theatre, London, with music by Arthur Bliss, a scenario by Michael Benthall and designs by Edward Burra, is the wartime ballet that puts a Glasgow slum street on the ballet stage, and Shearer was in its original cast as one of the Lovers. A despairing girl throws herself in the river and is dragged out; a minister fails to revive her; a Christ-like Stranger appears and brings her back to life, and the street rejoices in a collective dance. The jealous Official first tries to compromise the Stranger with the prostitute -- she emerges transfigured, Mary-Magdalene-like -- then incites a razor gang to murder him, and he dies alone with only a beggar for company as dawn breaks. Helpmann choreographed it and danced the Stranger; Shearer's Lovers are the ballet's counter-image, ordinary young love against the Stranger's miracle. [C33861]

The mechanism is the Passion play transplanted to a slum: resurrection as a public event, then murder as the crowd's price for it. It was performed every Sadler's Wells season from 1944 to 1950, revived in 1958, and revived by Birmingham Royal Ballet in 2014 under Gillian Lynne, an original cast member. And it is the hinge of this study's doorway: Helpmann recommended Shearer for The Red Shoes after working with her here, and four years later he choreographed the ballet she danced to Easdale's score. The Lover in the Gorbals becomes the Girl in the red shoes -- the same body, the same choreographer, the miracle now hers to perform and to pay for.

**Takeaway:** resurrection shown as a slow warming into dance, and a crowd that murders the man who performed it -- the community's gratitude curdles, and the curdling is the plot. [C33862]

## 6. Cinderella: stepping into Fonteyn's shoes

On 23 December 1948, the same month The Red Shoes opened, Shearer replaced an injured Fonteyn as Cinderella in Ashton's first full-evening ballet, at Covent Garden, and the Guardian obituary says she played "no small part in ensuring the success of Ashton's first full-evening work". The production mechanism that matters is the comedy around her: Ashton's two grotesque stepsisters are danced by the choreographer himself and by Helpmann, in drag -- the ballet's comedy is built by men in drag, and Shearer's Cinderella had to hold a straight, lyrical centre against their slapstick, stepping into Fonteyn's role at short notice. The Prince was Michael Somes; the Jester was Alexander Grant. In the same season she danced in Ashton's abstract Scenes de ballet, to Stravinsky, and at the Edinburgh Festival she danced the title role in Giselle to great success -- the Romantic ballet's two-act hinge, a living village girl in Act I and a weightless ghost in Act II, the same dancer dying into a different physics.

**Takeaway:** the understudy's triumph is a mechanism, not an accident -- hold the straight centre while two men in drag do the comedy, and the ballet belongs to you. [C33863]

## 7. The Red Shoes: the casting

Powell's casting rule, stated by Powell himself, is the sentence the whole film hangs on: "Before this film could be started it was necessary to find a dancer on the brink of becoming a ballerina; about twenty years of age; beautiful; exquisite figure and legs; strength of character; who could dance all the classical parts; and finally a dancer who could act and not an actress who pretended to dance. If we had not found Moira Shearer we could not have made the film." The production history proves the rule was applied, not just stated: after Shearer first declined, the American ballerinas Nana Gollner and Edwina Seaver tested and were rejected for unsatisfactory acting, and the non-dancers Hazel Court and Ann Todd were briefly considered before Shearer accepted. She had rejected the script as "silly and banal" and held out for a year, fearing film would harm her dance career; she took the role only after Ninette de Valois told her, "Will you please make this movie and get this man Powell off our backs?" Helpmann had recommended her, after Miracle in the Gorbals. [C33864]

She never stopped saying she had been forced into it. To Clive Barnes in 1962: "I have no regrets, except for the film The Red Shoes. That I do regret deeply. I was forced into it and very ill-advised at the time... After The Red Shoes and the later films, whenever I returned to ballet I was met with a solid wall of prejudice -- from the ballet audience, from the ballet critics, eventually even from the dancers. I found it impossible to get people to treat me as a normal member of the ballet company." And in 1994, near the end of her life: "The Red Shoes ruined my career in the ballet. They [her peers] never trusted me again... there was quite a lot of jealousy." Yet she returned to Powell twice -- for The Tales of Hoffmann and, as a favour, for Peeping Tom. The Powell-Pressburger site's own commentator notes the tension and leaves it unresolved, and this study does the same: the woman who said the film ruined her kept going back to the man who made it, and no source read explains why.

**Takeaway:** the casting rule is the mechanism -- a dancer who could act, not an actress who pretended to dance -- and the rule was applied so strictly that the film could not exist without her. [C33865]

## 8. The Red Shoes: the sub-minute fragment

The central ballet of The Red Shoes was not danced through. The score was pre-recorded, and the dancers performed to playback in short fragments, over a shoot Shearer put at about six weeks, with hours of preparation for shots lasting seconds. Shearer recalled that the ballet was "so cinematically worked out that we were lucky if we ever danced for as long as one minute." Her lecture of 17 January 1949 adds the detail: "only half a minute's dancing at a time", fourteen-hour days in make-up under the arc-lamps, fatigue and "limpness after long hours in the heat". The cost was in the body: "After six months of the Red Shoes method of filming, a dancer would not have any technique left. It took me several months after the film to get back to feeling right again." Powell disputed her account of the order -- she said the ballet was completed in the middle of the production, he said it was shot last -- but both agree on the method, and the method is the mechanism: a seventeen-minute ballet that never shows a dancer sustaining a phrase the way a stage performance would, only bursts of dancing cut together with close-ups of face, then feet, then another dancer. The continuity of effort is faked by editing; the dancer's real continuity is broken at every cut. The camera, not the dancer's stamina, supplies the arc.

**Takeaway:** dance in sub-minute fragments to a pre-recorded score, and the edit becomes the stamina -- the body rests while the film keeps dancing. [C33866]

## 9. The Red Shoes: the score written to drawings

The order of operations ran the wrong way round from stage ballet, and it ran that way on purpose. Production designer Hein Heckroth made hundreds of drawings of every stage of the ballet's progress and filmed them as a cartoon; Easdale composed the ballet score to that cartoon, in sequence; only then was the dancing shot, and as finished shots arrived they replaced the drawings. Wikipedia gives the drawing count as 120; Ambler's contemporary account in Ballet Today says four hundred, and the cartoon of drawings ran thirteen minutes -- the thirteen minutes is the storyboard film, not the ballet, and the 120 against 400 is unresolved, possibly different counts (drawings supplied to Easdale against total drawings made). Easdale's stated ambition was a picture "planned in the initial stage and directed throughout in terms of music", because "in a ballet, the music is complementary and not subsidiary to the choreography". The watchable result is a precision no live performance could produce: music, design and dance lock together because the score was fitted to the cinematic design before the dance existed, and the dancer is fitting her body into a pre-drawn, pre-scored slot.

**Takeaway:** music written to drawings, dance written to music -- the storyboard is the score before the score, and the choreographer approves the pictures, not the notes. [C33867]

## 10. The Red Shoes: identity morphing mid-phrase

Inside the ballet, the three characters do not stay fixed. The Girl is Shearer as Victoria Page; the Boy is Helpmann, first her boyfriend, then a sketch on transparent cellophane, then the living counterpart of the Press with "Le Jour" written on his forehead, an alter ego made of folded newspapers, then a prince in a triumphant pas de deux, and finally the village parson who unties the red shoes as she dies in his arms; the Shoemaker is Massine, who tempts her with the shoes, installs them by "movie magic", partners her briefly, leads a mob of monsters who elevate her in a triumphant ballerina pose, and at the end picks up the discarded shoes and offers them to the audience. A floating newspaper alternates between paper and Helpmann's human form, a callback to a windblown newspaper Victoria stepped on the night she got the lead role. Heckroth's own account, from 1947, is the best description of what the sequence is doing: "When the girl is dancing, she feels she is a bird, a flower, a cloud; when the spotlight hits her, she feels she is alone on a small island with waves breaking around; the figure of the conductor melts in turn into the form of the impresario, the magician, the lover, and at last into a figure made of newspapers." The audience starts in the gallery of Covent Garden, comes down to the stage, and is then projected into her thoughts -- the mechanism is identity as a costume change, not a plot event, and the change happens mid-phrase, without announcement.

**Takeaway:** one body becomes another character when the set and the music change around her -- the person changes because the world changed, and the dancer never stops moving while it happens. [C33868]

## 11. The Red Shoes: the camera as dance partner

The ballet is shot as film, not filmed theatre: a moving camera, close-ups that select face or feet, editing that rockets the action across the stage and then reverses it, and variable camera speed used as a choreographic tool. Cinematographer Jack Cardiff changed the speed of shooting mid-pirouette so turning figures appeared to spin like tops and dissolve into another figure. Adrienne McLean's verdict, quoted in Wikipedia, is that the action "rockets from stage right to stage left, a series of swiftly performed vignettes alternating with garishly decorated set pieces", and then, as Helpmann is borne away by a crowd, "the action reverses... into and through the ballerina's subconscious mind" -- and that the sequence is "a greater, or more characteristic, film experience than a dance one". Senses of Cinema calls it the first film to transcend dance's theatricality and move into "pure cinema"; Ian Christie is quoted there: "Before The Red Shoes, there were films with dance numbers. After it, there was a new medium which combined dance, design and music in a dreamlike spectacle." Massine, who danced in it, put the trade-off plainly: "Film ballet lacks the third dimension of stage ballet, but it gains enormously in its greater dramatic effect. It is good ballet yet a medium all its own."

**Takeaway:** vary the camera speed mid-pirouette and the camera becomes a dancer -- the spin is not hers, it is the machine's, and the dissolve is the step. [C33869]

## 12. The Red Shoes: the ballet that is also the film

The ballet's length is disputed in the sources and is stated here as a dispute, not a fact. Wikipedia's main text calls it a seventeen-minute ballet; its casting section calls it a fifteen-minute ballet; the BFI says fifteen minutes; ACMI says fourteen. The conflict carried from #785 runs wider -- 15 vs 16 vs 17 vs 18 vs 20 minutes across sources -- and this study uses seventeen minutes and flags the rest. What is not disputed is the film around it: world premiere in London on 22 July 1948, 107 weeks at the Bijou Theatre in New York, five Academy Award nominations, two wins -- Best Original Score for Easdale, the first British composer so honoured, and Best Art Direction. The filming year is itself split: Wikipedia's lead says filming took place in mid-1946, but its production section says principal photography began in June 1947, ran a planned fifteen weeks and overran to twenty-four, on a budget rising past 500,000 pounds. The 1947 date is the sourced one -- the rights were bought in 1946, the likely source of the lead's error -- and it is used here with the split flagged. [C33870]

The ballet duplicates Victoria's own story and foreshadows her fate: she dies in the film wearing the red shoes, paralleling her character, and the final image of the film is the company performing the ballet with a spotlight on the empty space where Vicky would have been. Powell's stated theme is "dying for art, art is worth dying for" -- and the mechanism is the whole of it in one image: a dance about a dancer who cannot stop dancing, performed by a company that has to go on dancing without her, with the light falling on an absence. The ballet is subjective as well as objective -- what she feels, not only what she does -- and that is why it survives as film rather than as a stage work: no stage could hold the bird, the flower, the cloud and the island in seventeen minutes.

**Takeaway:** the spotlight on an empty space is the ending -- the dance continues and the dancer does not, and the audience watches the gap. [C33871]

## 13. Hoffmann: the automaton dance

In The Tales of Hoffmann (1951) Shearer plays a double role, Stella in the frame and Olympia in the first tale, and Olympia is a clockwork automaton built by Spalanzani and Coppelius. The whole opera was pre-recorded -- Beecham conducting the Royal Philharmonic -- and the film was edited to the rhythms of the music; with the exception of Robert Rounseville and Ann Ayars, none of the actors sang their own roles, so Shearer's Olympia is sung by Dorothy Bond while Shearer dances and lip-syncs. The mechanism is triple: a dancer playing a doll, lip-syncing to a singer's voice, dancing to a pre-recorded orchestra, choreographed by Frederick Ashton, opposite Helpmann as all four villains. The watchable behaviour is stiff, whirling, wound-up dancing -- limbs that move as if on cogs and clockwork -- and then the doll torn apart limb from limb when Coppelius destroys her, her head held up still smiling. The Guardian obituary judges that "the quality of her dancing, as the doll Olympia, is probably best preserved" in this film -- this, more than The Red Shoes, is the cleanest surviving record of how she actually danced. Andre Bazin named the mechanism: "the cinema thus creates here a new artistic monster: the best legs adorned by the best voice... dance itself is renewed by the photography and the editing, which allows a kind of choreography of the second degree."

**Takeaway:** a doll is the honest form of the filmed dancer -- wound up, voiced by someone else, and torn apart when the trick is done. [C33872]

## 14. Hoffmann: the composed film

Powell's term for Hoffmann was a "composed film" -- a marriage of image to operatic music -- and the method is the reverse of The Red Shoes: where Easdale's ballet score was written to drawings, here the entire opera was pre-recorded first, the soundtrack recorded May to September 1950 under Beecham with the Royal Philharmonic, and the film was shot and edited to the rhythms of the music, with the acting fitted to the playback. The film is completely without spoken dialogue, and the acting is "highly stylised and similar to that of the silent-film era": eyebrows, eyes, gliding entrances. Colour is structure, not decoration: each tale has a primary colour -- Olympia in Paris yellow, Giulietta in Venice dark red, Antonia in Greece shades of blue -- and the sets are deliberately artificial, the Venice gondola moving through fake canals. Stella's profession was changed from opera singer to prima ballerina so the film could open with a new ballet, "The Ballet of the Enchanted Dragonfly", danced by Shearer. The film was shot at Shepperton and released in the UK on 26 November 1951 -- Britannica's "1950" is the production year, and 1951 is the release year used here. One production figure is carried as a conflict: Wikipedia gives the shoot as 1-16 July 1950, implausibly short against TCM's nine weeks and the May-September recording dates.

**Takeaway:** record the music first, then cut the film to it -- the edit becomes the choreography, and the mime only has to arrive on the beat. [C33873]

## 15. The Story of Three Loves: the fatal bargain, twice over

In The Story of Three Loves (1953), in the segment "The Jealous Lover", Shearer plays Paula Woodward, a ballerina with a heart condition who will die if she dances again -- and dances anyway. The acting is done through the dancing itself, in two set-pieces choreographed, per contemporary reviews, by Frederick Ashton, to Rachmaninoff's Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini: an audition at which she collapses after "a few exquisite moments", and a solo danced alone on an empty stage after a performance, then again in the choreographer's studio, after which she dies climbing the stairs at home, telling her aunt she will be with Coudray, played by James Mason, always. The watchable behaviour is a slow, lyrical solo danced not in pointe shoes but in an evening gown and high heels -- no pointe work, no developpe, the beauty carried by a pointed foot in a heel, sculptural timing, transitions from waltz-like restraint to sweeping arms and reaching legs. The spoken tag after the studio dance -- "I'm just a little out of breath, I haven't danced for so long" -- lands the lie the body has already told. The mechanism is the same fatal bargain as The Red Shoes, dance equals death, but inverted in tone: here the lover-choreographer does not demand the sacrifice, the dancer's own body and devotion do it, and the death is quiet, off-stage, mid-sentence.

**Takeaway:** dance the bargain a second time and the bargain changes -- the first time the impresario demands it, the second time the body does, and the second death is quieter and worse. [C33874]

## 16. The Man Who Loved Redheads: four redheads, one memory

The Man Who Loved Redheads (1955) is the comedy that proves the acting was real. Shearer plays four roles -- Sylvia, Daphne, Olga and Colette, all redheads -- in Terence Rattigan's Who Is Sylvia?, directed by Harold French. Mark, a young peer, meets sixteen-year-old redhead Sylvia as a boy and vows to love her forever; respectably married as an adult, he spends his life chasing her image through a string of affairs, each affair a different redhead played by Shearer, the same face returning in new guises while the man stays fixed on a memory. The mechanism is serial substitution: one actress, four incarnations of one ideal, against one man's inability to see the woman in front of him. The part let her show comic character acting plus dance -- a Sleeping Beauty extract and a Charleston -- and the contemporary verdict, quoted in Wikipedia, is that "Moira Shearer not only dances delightfully and breaks into a Charleston, but also proves herself to be a brilliant character actress by living the parts of Sylvia, Daphne, Olga and Colette." It was her personal favourite of her films, per the NFT programme notes. ## 17. Peeping Tom: Vivian and the screen test

## 18. Retirement, Titania, and the stage actress

In 1952, aged twenty-six, she became a guest artist with Sadler's Wells -- the Guardian calls it "a prelude to her (almost) complete retirement from dancing" -- and she retired from dancing in 1954 (1953 per Wikipedia; the majority is used here). What followed was a deliberate remake as a straight actress. In 1954 she played Titania in A Midsummer Night's Dream at the Edinburgh Festival, opposite Helpmann's Oberon, then on a North American tour. In 1955 she played Sally Bowles in I Am a Camera on a six-month tour of Britain, and spent the 1955-56 season with the Bristol Old Vic -- Cordelia in King Lear, Lydia Languish in The Rivals, Celia in Volpone, and the title role in Shaw's Major Barbara, which she took to London in 1956. In 1957 she played A Man of Distinction at the Edinburgh Festival opposite Anton Walbrook, remembered for the total lack of sympathy between the two stars. Then the films -- Peeping Tom in 1960, Black Tights in 1961 as Roxane in the Cyrano segment -- and by the early 1960s she had effectively retired from acting to concentrate on marriage and family. [C33877]

The acting career is the same shape as the dance career, compressed: a fast rise inside an institution, a ceiling, and a deliberate exit. The roles are all talk and charm, the opposite of the silent ballerina -- Sally Bowles is nothing but talk, Barbara Undershaft is nothing but argument -- and the choice of them is the point. She was not a dancer who happened to act; she was a dancer who decided to become an actress, took a full repertory season to learn the trade, and then stopped when the trade would not have her on her own terms.

**Takeaway:** retire from one art and take up another at twenty-eight -- the remake is deliberate, the season at Bristol is the apprenticeship, and the exit is as deliberate as the entrance. [C33878]

## 19. The voice that would not carry

The acting career had a ceiling too, and it was the voice. Caryl Brahams reviewing Major Barbara conceded Shearer was a "star" but "roundly criticizes her performance on the basis that she had a weak voice and lacked stage presence. She wondered if it were possible to forge an actress from a ballerina." Of the 1954 Titania, one critic said she danced with "fluent ease" and "spoke in a clear unmoving voice", and the production was generally panned in New York. Theatricalia's summary is blunt: "Her voice seems to have been better adapted to the film medium." Deborah Jowitt, recalling the same Titania years later, called her "a charming, willful, girlish Titania... it was as if she were, touchingly, striving to overcome her strong attack and saucy-soubrette onstage persona to become more thistledown-and-gossamer." The verdict that fits the evidence is Powell's formula, not Brahams's doubt: on film she could act -- Crowther called her "amazingly accomplished and full of a warm and radiant charm", and four roles in Redheads proved the character work -- but on stage her acting was constrained by a weak, unmoving voice and limited presence, and she never fully converted. She was a dancer who could act, and the voice was where the second career stopped.

**Takeaway:** a dancer's body can carry a film performance and fail on a stage -- the voice is the instrument the camera forgives and the theatre does not. [C33879]

## 20. The writer: Balletmaster and Ellen Terry

The final reinvention is as a woman of letters, and it grows out of a single encounter. In the spring of 1950 George Balanchine's Ballet Imperial entered the Sadler's Wells repertory; Fonteyn was cast first, but Shearer, following her, came nearest to capturing Balanchine's virtuoso footwork -- and Balanchine kept pushing her past technical hurdles, "Go on -- go on. I know you can do it", until she was performing everything he wanted, "the dangerous slanting way, even the nearly impossible". Thirty-six years later that one encounter became Balletmaster: A Dancer's View of George Balanchine (1986), built from the single rehearsal-room experience plus research and interviews with colleagues, ex-wives and impresarios -- "informal and distillate rather than compendious", an expert homage from one artist to another, in the dust-jacket's words. The book presents itself as a dancer's view, and it is one by design: one lived rehearsal, extended by reporting. [C33880]

The second book, a biography of Ellen Terry (1998), is a dancer-turned-actress writing the life of a nineteenth-century actress, and it argues against the received romantic legend on the evidence -- calling the Irving-Terry love affair "romantic fiction". In her later years she wrote book reviews for the Daily and Sunday Telegraph, "immensely readable though not celebrated for their generosity towards authors", lectured on ballet history and Diaghilev in the United States in 1973 and regularly in England and Wales, three times aboard the QE2, gave poetry and prose recitals, often with her husband, and in 1972 the BBC chose her to present the Eurovision Song Contest from the Usher Hall, Edinburgh. Asked by Who's Who how she would want to be referred to, she replied "writer" -- not dancer, not actress. The arc closes on her own terms.

**Takeaway:** one rehearsal with Balanchine, thirty-six years later a book -- the writer is the dancer with time to think, and the thinking is the work. [C33881]

## 22. Aesthetic logic: the dancer who would rather be believed than admired

The aesthetic logic of Shearer's work is the logic of a dancer who would rather be believed than admired, and it runs on four rules, each visible in more than one work. First, individuality over uniformity: Russian-trained, she entered a company whose director tried to impose a uniform style, and she drew the contrast herself -- dancers who submerge their personality when dancing, like Fonteyn, against dancers who emphasize their individuality, like Pavlova and herself -- and the critics kept naming the same unteachable lightness. Second, the body as the argument: the dancing does the acting, whether it is Paula's gown solo in high heels, Vicky's pirouette colliding with Julian's downbeat, or the doll Olympia's wound-up limbs. Third, the double and the doll: Odette and Odile in one body, Swanhilda playing the doll who plays the girl, Olympia the doll who is nearly a woman, four redheads as one memory -- the same face returning in a new guise is the shape her career keeps taking. Fourth, the imposed career: the film she did not want made her immortal, and she spent the rest of her life redefining herself away from it -- actress, then writer -- while the film stayed what everyone remembered. [C33883]

What she never does is the thing a classical ballerina is supposed to do: she never builds a career around display technique, never lets a variation exist for its own sake, never trusts the step to carry the evening. The cost of that is visible in the record -- the mixed stage acting, the weak voice, the early retirement from both arts -- and the cost is the same as the achievement: when the dramatic idea is strong the work is unforgettable, and when it is weak there is nothing underneath to save it. She is the dancer of the single strong idea carried by a body, a camera and a change of costume, and the study of her is the study of how far a single idea can be carried when the steps are not allowed to help -- and of what it costs when the idea that carries you is one you never chose.

**Takeaway:** know what the instrument is -- lightness and individuality, not display -- and the failures are as legible as the successes: a voice that will not carry is the same fact as a lightness no one can teach. [C33884]
