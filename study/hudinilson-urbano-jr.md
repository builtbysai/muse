## The life: FAAP, mail art, and the city as a page

Hudinilson Urbano Junior was born in Sao Paulo on 17 October 1957, son of Maria Apparecida Urbano and Hudinilson Urbano, and died in Sao Paulo on 28 August 2013, aged 55. The birth day and month rest on the 2025 Dialogo article in leg 1, but leg 3 confirmed them against two further independent sources (the Pinacoteca 2025 guide and the UNIFESP 2026 thesis, plus Garcia Galeria), so the full date is printed with confidence; the death date is agreed by Garcia Galeria and the Artsy/Posicao Amorosa text. He lived and worked in Sao Paulo for his whole career; no source read claims any other home city. His father was a university professor who introduced him to methodical archiving, an influence the Slash Paris text says shaped the young man strongly, while his mother encouraged his wish to enter art school -- both characterisations rest on that one exhibition text and are plausible colour, not corroborated fact. He studied fine art at FAAP (Fundacao Armando Alvares Penteado) in Sao Paulo, 1975-1977, where he was more interested in intense personal intellectual relationships with his teachers and artists of his generation than in following the classical teaching; in the two years after FAAP he experimented with woodcut and painting, having been deeply impressed in his youth by the painter Lasar Segall (1891-1957). (C17008, leg 1 C16800, C16802, C16805; leg 3 C16909; synthesis C16950)

Before and alongside the collective work he experimented with drawing, painting, graffiti, mail art, performance and urban intervention, with the human body, eroticism and pleasure as recurrent themes (Taipei Biennial). His earliest group exhibitions, as listed in the Itau-style entry, begin in 1976 (IX Exposicao Estadual de Pinturas, Galeria Prestes Maia, Sao Paulo; I Salao de Artes, Itu) and run densely through mail-art shows in Brazil, Mexico, Canada, Italy and elsewhere in 1979-1981. A 2025 article notes his career also spanned research, archiving and teaching, and that his work in the 1970s-1980s covered woodcut, mail art and xerography -- but no teaching post is named in any source read, so do not invent one. (C17009, leg 1 C16800-C16802, C16805; synthesis C16951)

He grew up and worked under the Brazilian military dictatorship (1964-1985) in a climate of extreme homophobia (KOW). Civil government was established in 1985, and the Garcia text links his mature individual work to the political relaxation announcing the arrival of democracy. His collages from the AIDS-crisis period, continued until the early 1990s, refer to a support group and an affective closeness built through desire, and were rarely shown because of their pornographic character. The Slash Paris text adds that by the time of the AIDS epidemic most of his artist friends had already died. (C17010, leg 1 C16800, C16802, C16804; synthesis C16962)


## The night of 9 March 1979: the boot and the mouth

The documented personal encounter with Vallauri is the hinge between Hudinilson's street work and the first generation of Sao Paulo graffiti, and it is the hinge of this study's doorway. The Catalogo das Artes history dates it precisely: 22:27 on 9 March 1979, on Avenida Ipiranga. Hudinilson, thin, long-haired, in a dark overcoat, was spray-painting a big open mouth with the words "AH, BEIJE-ME" (Ah, Kiss Me) on a freshly white-painted wall, using a can of fire-red spray. As he capped the can to leave, a small, extremely fast man had already graffitied beside the mouth a black high-heeled boot with a tall shaft -- Vallauri's Bota -- folding his stencil, a pierced mould through which spray paint was applied. Each noticed the other at the same moment and they said almost simultaneously, "Ah, so it is you?" The Catalogo renders the mouth's words as "AH AH BEIJE-ME"; Hudinilson's own testimony, quoted below, gives "AH, BEIJE-ME". Both wordings are carried; the testimony's is the one quoted. (C17011, leg 1 C16807; leg 3 C16904; synthesis C16953, C16954)

Hudinilson's own words, quoted in the scholarly article "A cidade-corpo" from his 1998 testimony: while doing a large "AH, BEIJE-ME" with only a spray can in his pocket, he finished the mouth and "a short man appears, puts something on the wall, quickly does the thing, does the black thing there [the black boot], wraps up, sees me and gets a fright. At the time he thought I was police, before seeing properly. I wore a hat, I had a spray in my hand, I am not police, we looked at each other and I saw the little boot and he saw the 'ah, beije-me', so I discovered who made the little boot and he discovered who made the 'ah, beije-me'". Note the date: 1998 is the testimony date, not the meeting date. The meeting itself is dated 9 March 1979 by the USP thesis (Caje, 2011) reporting Hudinilson's account, and the Catalogo history agrees on the date and adds the time. The two sources disagree only on the street: Avenida Ipiranga (Catalogo) against a wall in Rua Aurora (USP thesis). (C17012, leg 1 C16807; leg 3 C16904, C16905; synthesis C16953, C16954)

The consequences, per the Catalogo history: a fertile identification of artistic proposals and a solid friendship between two of the most compelling contemporary artists the city produced; with Vallauri's encouragement Hudinilson began printing Greek figures and his own portrait around the city, a project he called Projeto Narciso (Narcissus Project); together with Vallauri, other artists including Carlos Matuck, Waldemar Zeidler, Mauricio Villaca, John Howard, Ozeas Duarte and the group TupiNaoDa began using the city as a support for their works. After the meeting Hudinilson became close friends with Vallauri, received his guidance, and collaborated with him on some projects -- no project is named except a joint wall work, c. 1983, Hudinilson graffiti with Vallauri's acrobat at top left (USP thesis, Figure 160). And after Rafael Franca left 3Nos3, Hudinilson proposed Vallauri join the group; both Ramiro and Vallauri refused. Vallauri was never a member of 3Nos3. (C17013, leg 1 C16807; leg 3 C16906; synthesis C16955)

Two corrections to the #678 framing follow from this run. First, the stencil timing: the Itau-style entry dates Hudinilson's stencil graffiti "from the mid-1980s" and says that in this period he met Vallauri, from whom he received guidance. That dating is incompatible with the precisely dated 9 March 1979 encounter and with Vallauri's 1978-1980 mask period. The 1979 encounter is the better-documented date and is used here; the "mid-1980s" phrasing appears to be an error or to refer to a later phase of stencil work, and must not be quoted as the meeting date. He was the only 3Nos3 member who used pixacao as a means of expression, and his early street work was freehand. Second, the Boot's first appearance: #678 prints 1978 (Medeiros via the Bienal Foundation); the USP thesis says "since 1977" in one wording. Keep 1978; the 1977 wording is single-source and is not followed. (C17014, leg 1 C16802, C16807; leg 3 C16910; synthesis C16954, C16956)


## 3Nos3: the city as a page, 1979-1982

His first big moment of public visibility came in 1979, when he founded the collective 3Nos3 with Mario Ramiro (born 1957) and Rafael Franca (1957-1991). All three were born in 1957. The group treated the city as a page on which to compose graphic design, inserting monumental gestures into Sao Paulo during the military dictatorship, and it operated almost exclusively outside institutional frameworks until May 1982. Its documented actions, in order: Ensacamentos (Baggings, 1979, W01), in which the three covered the heads of classical statues and patriotic public monuments around Sao Paulo with plastic garbage bags, denouncing the regime's use of torture and attacking prominent public symbols of the state -- the KBCB text says 68 public statues were covered, and Momus calls it Hudinilson's "first significant crop", linking the truncated statue heads to the fragmentation of the body in his later work; X-Galeria (night of 2 June 1979, W02's companion action), in which the entrances of twenty-seven commercial art galleries plus MASP on Avenida Paulista were sealed with a large X of masking tape, with mimeographed leaflets reading "O que esta dentro fica / O que esta fora se expande" (What is inside stays / What is outside expands); Interversao (from 1980), the group's own neologism fusing intervention and inversion, defined by the group in 1981 as "the act of inverting; changing the natural or habitual order" -- the first action under that name, Interversao VI, took place on the night of 15 June 1980, and in 1980 the group also laid 100 metres of red plastic across crossings and entrances of the ring road at Avenida Paulista and Rua da Consolacao, in more than one night action, which the traffic department (Detran) dismantled. The photo-novel CASOS (1978, W03) predates the formal founding and is a precursor, not a 3Nos3 work in the strict sense. (C17015, leg 1 C16800, C16804, C16807, C16808; leg 2 C16850, C16854, C16855; synthesis C16952)

Two counts of the collective's output do not agree, and neither is printed as definitive: the Catalogo das Artes history says the group carried out eighteen interventions around the city up to 1982, when it dissolved; the Engramma study counts at least seventeen further interventions between 1979 and 1982 in addition to the museum episodes, with a single exception outside Sao Paulo (the work ARTE in Porto Alegre, Franca's home town). The institutional turn and the end: in May 1982 3Nos3 was invited to exhibit at the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo in the show 3NOS3/3ANOS (19 May-6 June 1982), in which each member showed an individual work -- Hudinilson's Posicao amorosa, Ramiro's Estruturas de Especulacao, Franca's video Carta 23 -- plus a shared red plastic band across the museum facade (27.4/3 - II). The experience in the museum marked the collective and sanctioned its dissolution later the same year, after a final mock-performative farewell titled Acabou (It is Over) at the first 14 Noites de Performance at the newly inaugurated SESC Pompeia. The Garcia text gives the same 1979-1982 span and dissolution in 1982. The Artsy text for the Posicao Amorosa show says the group carried out interventions "until 1981" -- that contradicts the 1982 dissolution and the May-June 1982 Pinacoteca show documented in detail by Rossi, so 1982 is used and the 1981 wording is not. (C17016, leg 1 C16807-C16809; synthesis C16952)


## The Xerox work: Exercicio de me ver

From the early 1980s Hudinilson made Exercicio de Me Ver (Exercise for Seeing Myself), the xerographic reproduction of parts of his own body, by leaning over and lying full-bodied on the Xerox screen: "using the BODY as matrix... The XEROX recreates the BODY in a singular way, erasing details and enhancing others, resulting in images that draw closer to abstraction". The series turned him into a pioneer of Xerox art in Brazil, with access to University of Sao Paulo photocopiers -- machines that were part of a modernisation initiative by the military government, which gave him the tools to manipulate, fragment and abstract bodily images. The performance was sometimes public, photographed by his friend Afonso Roperto. Sources frame it variously: the Garcia text describes the images as documenting a performance of having sex with the photocopying machine, without looking at the camera, to obtain the fragmentation of his own image on a 1:1 scale; the KOW text frames the same act as self-scrutiny and a search for visual structures of skin and flesh, not out of an obsession with sex. No single framing is settled, and the study reports both without choosing. His own words carry the mechanism further: the aim was "to understand the limits imposed by the machine and augment its resources by controlling these limits, inverting the relations, transforming the machine into both vehicle and co-author of this work". (C17017, leg 1 C16800-C16803; leg 2 C16850, C16851; synthesis C16957)

The start date is genuinely unsettled and must be printed as a range, not a year. Garcia Galeria and the Taipei Biennial date the series from 1982; Frieze dates it 1981; Third Text ranges it 1980-1983. The artist himself did not date the series, and the 2025 Dialogo article declines to assign dates to its two sections for that reason. The study gives "from 1981/82" with the caveat. The related title Zona de Tensao is likewise double-dated: 1981 for Pinto nao pode (W10) and 1983 for a later hands revisiting in the Biel text; the two are different uses of one title field, not one work. Early exhibition of the Xerox work: the 1981 exhibition Do Detalhe ao Exercicio (From Detail to Exercise) at the Pinacoteca do Estado, structured in two sections, Detalhe do detalhe (Detail of Detail) and Exercicio de Me Ver; Xerox Action at Galeria Chaves in Porto Alegre (1982) and at MAC/USP (1983). In 1984 he took part in the 1st Havana Biennial and in the exhibition Arte Xerox Brasil at the Pinacoteca do Estado, which he also curated; he exhibited in the 18th Sao Paulo Biennial in 1985. (C17018, leg 1 C16801, C16802, C16805, C16809; leg 2 C16851, C16855, C16856, C16858; synthesis C16958, C16959)


## The Cadernos de referencias: the scrapbook as database

His other life's work is the Cadernos de referencias (Reference Notebooks), dense layered scrapbooks combining mass-media clippings, Greco-Roman mythology, US pornography, French theory and the Brazilian pop-art scene, with the focus on the naked, mostly male body, made by appropriation, photocopying, cut and paste. Mario Ramiro called them "the space of resonance of all his work". The USP article examined 40 of them, about a third of the series. The Cadernos are now scattered across his family home, Galeria Jaqueline Martins, MALBA, MAC, Pinacoteca, CCSP and the Vera Chaves Barcellos Foundation. (C17019, leg 1 C16806; leg 2 C16855, C16859; synthesis C16960)

The total count conflicts and must be printed with both figures. The USP article and the later monograph put the total at about 130 (88 counted in March 2008, 12 then missing, plus the books composed from 2008 to 2013); the Kunsthaus Biel text says "over twenty" are known to exist. The "over twenty" figure is likely a count of located or exhibited books, not a rival total, but the study does not quote a single total without noting the two figures. The Garcia text says he kept over one hundred artist books in his library, covering diary, exercise-book, catechism-notebook or account-book pages filled with photographic clippings, press articles, tobacco wrappings, photocopies and personal documents. The Cadernos scholarship is recent and is one of the reasons the study has solid sources: Rossi and Farias, "Queer Scrapscape", ARS (USP), 2023, and Simone Rossi's monograph Hudinilson Jr.'s Cadernos de referencias (August 2026), which was seen only via book listings, not read. (C17020, leg 1 C16806; leg 2 C16850, C16855, C16859; synthesis C16961)


## Late work, marginality, death

Late work includes the textile sculptures of Posicao Amorosa (c. 1980s, W16: crumpled soiled underpants with a metal plaque; starched socks) and latex-coated clothing items from the 1980s, clothes preserved by immersion in liquid rubber, read as archiving and as a response to the AIDS crisis. The 2011-2012 interviews he gave to Ricardo Resende, then director of the Centro Cultural de Sao Paulo, and researchers Maria Olimpia Vassao and Maria Adelaide Pontes fed the CCSP monographic exhibition Zona de Tensao (2016, 50 works) and the only monograph published to date on Hudinilson, Resende (2016), Posicao Amorosa -- neither read directly this run. (C17021, leg 1 C16806, C16809; leg 2 C16855, C16856; synthesis C16962)

Career assessment is consistent across sources: marginal and underground throughout his life, neglected by the local art market, international attention only toward the end of his life, fame only posthumously. He spent many years as a recluse in his apartment before his death. In 2011 Galeria Jaqueline Martins began representing him; his work was the gallery's highlight at ARCO 2013 in Madrid, sparking interest from international institutions. He died in Sao Paulo on 28 August 2013, aged 55. No cause of death is stated in any source read end to end. One source, the Third Text article (2019), read via search summary only, states he "died in 2013 of complications from long-term alcohol abuse, penniless and notoriously bitter about the lack of recognition". No other source read gives a cause. The claim is single-source and search-summary-only, not independently corroborated, and the study does not print it as fact. Do not write that he died of AIDS, and do not write that he did not die of AIDS: no source states a cause either way. (C17022, leg 1 C16804, C16809; synthesis C16963, C16964)

Posthumous recognition is extensive and recent: the 30th Sao Paulo Biennial (2012, during his lifetime, at the very end of it); the 31st Sao Paulo Biennial (2014); Glasgow International (2014); Garcia Galeria, Madrid (2014); "Historias da sexualidade" at MASP, 2017-2018, with the Cadernos in the "Sexual Games" section; "Let X=X", Kupfer, London (2021), pairing Hudinilson with Alair Gomes; the 16th Lyon Biennale (2022); "Hudinilson Jr.: Explicito", Pinacoteca do Estado, curated by Ana Maria Maia (2020); "Exercicios de me ver", KOW Berlin (2 May-26 July 2025), his first solo there; "HUDINILSON JR.: Echoes in Xerox", Kunsthaus Biel (21 September-30 November 2025), curated by Raphael Gygax, showing 3Nos3 works and works by Franca and Ramiro alongside. Collections now holding his work, as listed in gallery-provided texts and not verified collection by collection: MoMA, Museo Reina Sofia, Migros Museum, MAGA, MALBA, MASP, Pinacoteca, MAM-SP and MAC/USP. The 3rd Mercosul Biennial is dated 2001 by the Taipei Biennial text and 2002 by the Itau-style entry and the Artsy text; the 3rd Mercosul is independently known to have taken place in 2001, so 2001 is likelier, but it was not resolved from a primary biennial source and the conflict is carried. ## The mechanisms

### M1 -- Body as matrix: the body pressed on the copier glass becomes the printing plate

Mechanically: lean over and lie full-bodied on the Xerox screen, composing forms and textures at 1:1 scale, and let the machine do the picturing -- it erases details and enhances others, so the image draws closer to abstraction. The body is the plate, the glass is the press, and the copy is the print. The performance was sometimes public, photographed by Afonso Roperto, and the framing is unsettled: a near-sexual embrace with the machine, or self-scrutiny, or a search for visual structures of skin and flesh. The evidencing works are W06 (Exercicio de Me Ver series, 1981/82-83), W07 (Narcisse - Exercicio de me ver II, 1982), W08 (the five-part photo documentation of that performance, 1982) and W12 (Untitled collage, 1982, 75 x 112 cm). (C17024, leg 2 C16850-C16853, C16858; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: a torso, face or hand appears as a high-contrast black field with the body part emerging as pale, flattened relief, its edges smearing where contact with the glass fails. A piece built on this mechanism should press a form onto a flat field and let the contact, not a drawn line, decide the edge.

**Takeaway:** the body is the plate. Press it on the glass and let the machine draw. (C17025, leg 2 C16851)

### M2 -- Copy as degradation and amplification: each re-copy enlarges, dapples and abstracts

Mechanically: take the first copy, enlarge it, copy it again, and again. Each pass leaves reticular machine marks, dappled texture and lost detail, so the image drifts toward abstraction while getting bigger -- the copy machine imprints its own graphic marks until the organic surface becomes an abstract one. The dappled texture of Zona de Tensao / Pinto nao pode (W10) is described as the result of exactly this repeated enlarging and copying. The evidencing works are W10 (1981), W11 (Area de Tensao III-D, enlarged photocopy panels juxtaposed as billboard-size mosaics) and W12 (Untitled collage, 1982). (C17026, leg 2 C16851, C16858; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: a small body fragment grows into a billboard-size mosaic whose surface is visibly machine-made, not drawn. A piece built on this mechanism should start from one small fragment and grow it by repeated copying, letting the machine's own marks take over as the image scales.

**Takeaway:** copy it bigger and it stops being a body and starts being a machine. (C17027, leg 2 C16851)

### M3 -- Fragmentation and cropping: the body is never shown whole

Mechanically: cut the whole down to a part. Statue heads are bagged off (W01), body parts are isolated on the platen (W06), panels are juxtaposed as fragments (W11, W12). Momus calls Ensacamentos Hudinilson's "first significant crop", linking the truncated statue heads to the later body fragmentation in the collages and the Xerox art. The evidencing works are W01 (Ensacamentos, 1979), W06 (Exercicio de Me Ver series), W11 (Area de Tensao III-D) and W12 (Untitled collage, 1982). (C17028, leg 2 C16851, C16854, C16858; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: a familiar whole -- a statue, a body, a face -- arrives with a piece removed or isolated, and the missing part does the work. A piece built on this mechanism should show the part and withhold the whole, and let the viewer complete what is not there.

**Takeaway:** crop first, and the crop is the picture. (C17029, leg 2 C16854)

### M4 -- The Narcissus loop: self-portrait as a closed system

Mechanically: return to the same face across decades, in different crops and tilings, each pass slightly more abstract. The artist looks at himself looking: from the 1982 Exercicio series through the 1980s Narcisse pair to the 2000 (Des) Construir Narciso. In the 1980s Narcisse pair (W14) a felon-like mugshot is collaged beside a fingerprint (presumably his own), a close-up of his eye, and a column of text with the word "Narcissus" in variable languages and orientations; the second repeats photocopies of the mugshot in a tiled pattern. The evidencing works are W07 (Narcisse - Exercicio de me ver II, 1982), W14 (Narcisse pair, 1980s), W15 ((Des) Construir Narciso - I (a), 2000) and W09 (Exercicio de Me Ver III, undated, tangled hair pressed against the copy machine, enlarged to lewd detail). (C17030, leg 2 C16852-C16854, C16856; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: the same face returns in different crops and tilings, each pass slightly more abstract, until identity itself is the subject. A piece built on this mechanism should repeat one portrait across the frame, each copy cropped or tiled differently, so the viewer watches the face dissolve into pattern.

**Takeaway:** look at yourself looking, and keep looking, until the face becomes a pattern. (C17031, leg 2 C16856)

### M5 -- City as page: the street is the support

Mechanically: take a familiar public object and alter it briefly, at night, with cheap means -- statues hooded with plastic bags (W01), a zebra crossing blocked with cellophane in front of MASP (W02, Interdicao, 1979), a photo novel left behind (W03, CASOS, 1978). The gesture is fast, nocturnal and gone by morning, surviving as press coverage and photographs. The group treated the city as a page on which to compose graphic design. The evidencing works are W01, W02 and W03. (C17032, leg 2 C16850, C16854, C16855; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: a familiar public object is briefly, visibly altered, and the alteration is the artwork. A piece built on this mechanism should change one ordinary thing in the scene overnight, and let the change be the whole event.

**Takeaway:** the street is the page. Hood the statue and the city has to look. (C17033, leg 2 C16854)

### M6 -- Inversion (interversao): a barrier that reroutes instead of blocking

Mechanically: lay a bright, cheap, temporary line across a crossing, an entrance, a facade, and let traffic, visitors and attention flow around it. The group's own neologism, fusing intervention and inversion, defined in 1981 as "the act of inverting; changing the natural or habitual order". Gallery doors are sealed with a masking-tape X so that "what is inside stays, what is outside expands" (X-Galeria, night of 2 June 1979); 100 metres of red plastic cross the ring road at Avenida Paulista and Rua da Consolacao (1980); a red band crosses the Pinacoteca facade (3NOS3/3ANOS, 1982). The evidencing works are W02 (Interdicao, 1979), X-Galeria (1979), Interversao VI (15 June 1980) and the 3NOS3/3ANOS facade band (1982). (C17034, leg 1 C16807, C16808; leg 2 C16855; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: a barrier that does not block so much as reroute -- traffic, visitors and attention are forced around a bright, cheap, temporary line. A piece built on this mechanism should lay one coloured line across the scene and watch everything in it bend around the line.

**Takeaway:** do not block the road. Cross it with a line and watch the city go around. (C17035, leg 1 C16808)

### M7 -- The scrapbook as database: cut-out bodies, accumulated for thirty years

Mechanically: accumulate cut-out bodies from newspapers, fashion magazines, pornographic magazines, art-history publications and anatomy books into dense layered books, by appropriation, photocopying, cut and paste. The same operation as the Xerox work, but with other people's bodies and over thirty years. The pages are visibly over-full, images layered and re-cut until the source is unrecognisable, the book itself becoming the work. The evidencing works are W17 (Caderno de Referencias XX, 1980s), W18 (Caderno de referencia no. 70, c. 2000, MoMA), W19 (Cadernos de Referencias series, c. 1981-2013) and W20 (Untitled notebook, 1979: eight photocopied prints, the first an envelope-like white sheet, followed by over-toned dark pages progressively lightening to reveal a handprint). (C17036, leg 2 C16855-C16857, C16859; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: pages that are visibly over-full, images layered and re-cut until the source is unrecognisable. A piece built on this mechanism should keep adding cut-out fragments to a page until the page is the work, and let the viewer leaf through the accumulation.

**Takeaway:** keep every body you cut out, and the book becomes the body of work. (C17037, leg 2 C16859)

### M8 -- Erotic-political double coding: the explicit body as both desire and protest

Mechanically: let the erotic image carry the political charge, and let the political gesture be carried out in erotic terms. A penis impression stamped "PINTO NAO PODE" (Cocks Not Allowed) confronts the dictatorship's suppression of queerness (W10); a figure suffocating under a black plastic bag alludes to torture (W05, Sufoco, 1979); bagged statues denounce the regime's use of torture (W01); the early paintings stage naked figures as if on a stage, the body as a projection surface for desire, fear and social reality (W04, Amantes e casos, 1977-78); the textile sculptures turn soiled underpants into a manifesto (W16, Posicao Amorosa, c. 1980s). The evidencing works are W10, W05, W01, W04 and W16. (C17038, leg 2 C16854-C16856, C16858; synthesis C16972)

Watchable behaviour: an image that reads first as body, second as accusation -- or the reverse, depending on who is looking. A piece built on this mechanism should open as an erotic image and resolve, on a second look, into a political one, and never settle which reading is first.

**Takeaway:** the body is the accusation. Stamp it plainly and let the regime read it. (C17039, leg 2 C16858)


## The aesthetic logic: the machine as co-author, the city as page

Hudinilson's logic is a copier, not a canvas. The Xerox machine taught him the move -- press the body on the glass, let the machine erase and enhance, and let the copy drift toward abstraction with each enlargement (M1, M2) -- and the street gave him the other machine: a city full of statues, crossings and gallery doors that could be altered overnight with a plastic bag, a sheet of cellophane, a strip of red plastic (M5, M6). The body is never shown whole, so the crop is the picture (M3); the same face returns across decades in different tilings, so identity itself becomes the subject (M4); and the cut-out bodies of other people accumulate for thirty years in the Cadernos, the same operation as the Xerox work with other people's bodies (M7). The erotic image carries the political charge throughout (M8): the penis impression that says cocks are not allowed, the suffocating figure, the hooded statue, the soiled underpants with a metal plaque -- desire and protest in the same gesture, under a dictatorship that suppressed both.

The watchability follows from that copier. A body pressed on the glass (M1) gives an edge decided by contact, not by a drawn line; a fragment copied bigger and bigger (M2) lets the machine's own marks take over until the body becomes a pattern; a familiar whole with a piece removed (M3) makes the viewer complete what is not there; the same face returning in different crops (M4) dissolves identity into tiling; a statue hooded overnight (M5) makes the city look at what it walks past; a bright line across a crossing (M6) reroutes everything in the scene without blocking anything; a scrapbook page kept over-full for thirty years (M7) turns accumulation into the work; and an image that reads first as body and second as accusation (M8) never settles which reading is first.

What he is not: not a painter (the early paintings of 1977-78 are the starting point, abandoned for the copier and the street -- the painting never returns as the practice); not a gallery artist (the market neglected him all his life, the gallery came only in 2011, and fame came only after his death -- the street and the copier came first and the museum came to them); not a stencil pioneer in 1978 (the early street work was freehand pixacao; the stencil is a mid-1980s practice, and he must not be written stencilling beside Vallauri in 1978); not a member of the 1984 Parque Lage show (no source read places him there, and the study makes no claim either way -- do not assert participation); not a Vallauri collaborator inside 3Nos3 (Vallauri was never a member, and the proposal to admit him was refused by both Ramiro and Vallauri); not a late-style artist (there is no late thinning, no late style -- the Cadernos run to 2013, the same operation for thirty years, and the last works are the same gesture as the first: a body, cropped, copied, accumulated). The closest kin in the studied corpus is Vallauri, who also lets a machine do the picturing -- but Vallauri's machine is the stencil, one sharp shape stamped everywhere until the city learns it, while Hudinilson's machine is the copier, one body pressed on the glass until the machine turns it into pattern. His operation is his own: press the body on the glass, copy it until it abstracts, crop it until the missing part does the work, hood the statue and cross the road with a line, and keep every body you cut out for thirty years. (C17040)

Colour: the red spray of the mouth, the black of the bagged statues. Scale: paper, then the city itself. Collective (1979-1982): 3Nos3 treats the city as a page -- hooded statues, sealed gallery doors, red plastic across crossings -- until the Pinacoteca show of May-June 1982 and the farewell Acabou. Xerox (1981/82-1985): the body on the copier glass, the series shown at Galeria Chaves and MAC/USP in 1982-83, Havana 1984, the 18th Biennial 1985; the copy enlarges, dapples and abstracts. Cadernos throughout (c. 1981-2013): the scrapbook as database, the same cut-and-paste operation with other people's bodies, running in parallel with everything else for thirty years. Late (1980s-2013): the textile sculptures and latex clothing of Posicao Amorosa; the AIDS-crisis collages, rarely shown; marginality, reclusion, representation only from 2011; death 28 August 2013. Posthumous (2014-2025): biennials, MASP, the Pinacoteca Explicito show of 2020, KOW 2025, Kunsthaus Biel 2025.
