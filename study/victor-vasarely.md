# Victor Vasarely: Study Notes

**Site:** Victor Vasarely, born 9 April 1906 at Pecs, Hungary, died 15 March 1997 at Paris, aged 90 -- Hungarian-born French artist, the grandfather of Op Art, the man who made movement out of still pattern: two zebras built from stripes with no outline at all, checkerboards that tilt into space, a patented alphabet of coloured units permuted by assistants into endless serial pictures, grids deformed so a sphere bulges out of a flat wall, and a whole building in Aix-en-Provence that is the alphabet at architectural scale. He called the viewer the sole creator, refused the uniqueness of the work of art, and spent forty years arguing that art belonged in the street, on facades, on a car badge and in a polychrome city of happiness -- not only on museum walls. He exhibited in Le Mouvement in 1955, published the Yellow Manifesto the same year, and was made the figurehead of The Responsive Eye at MoMA in 1965, the show his younger colleagues Soto and Cruz-Diez refused and joined respectively, both resisting being presented as his followers (C5801, C5804, C5808, C5903, C5905).

## The life: from the advertising studio to the polychrome city

Victor Vasarely was born on 9 April 1906 in Pecs, then Austria-Hungary, the son of a headwaiter who had moved to Budapest from Timisoara; the birth registration is cited at the Pecs county archives, ref. no. 330/1906 (C5801). His birth name is itself an open question: most sources give Gyozo Vasarhelyi, but the Vasarely Museum in Pecs gives Gyozo Csiszar at birth, his mother's name, with Vasarhelyi taken in 1908 when the family moved to Budapest (C5802). The birth year is disputed the same way -- 1906 with the archive citation against 1908 in Tate, Britannica, Encyclopedia.com and Art UK -- and this study uses 1906 and flags 1908 wherever it appears (C5801, C5805, C5806, C5811, C5812). He grew up in Piestany and Budapest, in a milieu of catering and theatre, took evening drawing classes from 1922, qualified in French and German business correspondence, and in 1925 took up medical studies at Eotvos Lorand University in Budapest while working as an assistant accountant at the Labor Pharmaceutical and Chemical Works, where he received his first graphic design commission (C5801, C5802, C5804).

In 1927 he abandoned medicine for art, studying at the private Podolini-Volkmann Academy, and in 1928/1929 enrolled at Sandor Bortnyik's Muhely -- literally "Workshop" -- "then widely recognized as Budapest's center of Bauhaus studies", a cash-strapped school that concentrated on applied graphic art and typographical design (C5803, C5804). It was there that he first came into contact with Constructivism and abstract art (C5803). In 1929 he painted his Blue Study and Green Study (W01) and published his first graphic designs; in 1930 he exhibited with Bortnyik's school alongside Laszlo Moholy-Nagy and Marcel Breuer, married his fellow student Claire Spinner -- spelled Klara Spinner (1909-1991) by the Pecs museum, against Claire Spinner (1908-1990) elsewhere, another open question -- and moved to Paris (C5802, C5804, C5805). Two sons followed: Andre (born 1931) and Jean-Pierre (born 1934-2002), who worked as Yvaral (C5802, C5804).

Paris meant fifteen years of graphic design and advertising: creative consultant at Havas, Draeger and Devambez (1930-1935), then advertising supplements for medical publications at Roger Bessard's publishing house, where Bessard later took him as a partner, "which brings the artist financial security" (C5802, C5804, C5805). He thought of opening an institution modelled on Bortnyik's Muhely and developed teaching material for it; the plan was never realised (C5802, C5804). The early graphic period, 1929-1944, is where the whole later system is already visible: "Vasarely experimented with textural effects, perspective, shadow, and light", and the period produced Girl-power (W02, 1934), The Chess Board (W03, 1935) and Zebras / Zebra (W04, 1937) -- "two hypnotic, Escher-like black-and-white images that are now considered the progenitors of Op Art" (C5804, C5808). He lived mostly in cheap hotels in the early 1930s and settled in 1942/1944 in Saint-Cere in the Lot (C5804). In 1939, at the Cafe Flore, he met Denise Rene (1913-2012), who ran a fashion house (C5802).

After the war he turned to painting. Galerie Denise Rene opened on 13 February 1944 with his Drawings and Graphic Compositions as its first exhibition -- against 1946 as the first listed show in Wikipedia's period text, a minor open question, the museum's specific date being the stronger claim (C5802, C5804, C5805). The years 1944-1947 he later called Les Fausses Routes, "on the wrong track": cubistic, futuristic, expressionistic, symbolistic and surrealistic paintings "without developing a unique style", with Jacques Prevert writing the catalogue introduction and placing him among the surrealists; Self Portrait (W06, 1944 in the Stadel list, 1941 in Wikipedia) and The Blind Man (1946) belong here (C5802, C5804, C5856). He opened an atelier in Arcueil, about 10 kilometres from Paris, and in 1961 finally settled in Annet-sur-Marne (C5802, C5804). By 1947 he had found his own form of geometrical abstraction, and by 1947 had left his day job behind; "for the next half century he would make his living as an independent artist" (C5805). The overlapping developments are named after their geographical heritage: Denfert, after the white tiled walls of the Paris Denfert-Rochereau metro station; Belle-Isle, after ellipsoid pebbles and shells from a 1947 vacation; Gordes/Cristal, after the cubic houses of the village where he spent his summers from 1948 (C5802, C5803, C5804). The period dates differ across sources and are used here as ranges with their source, never fused into one clean span.

The turn toward kinetics came through Gordes: from those works he developed kinematic images -- superimposed acrylic glass panes that "create dynamic, moving impressions depending on the viewpoint", and black-white photographies transposed into two colours and combined into a single pane (C5804). His first architectural work was Hommage to Malevich (W08): the 1953 painting became, in 1954, a 100-square-metre ceramic wall at the Central University of Venezuela in Caracas, invited by architect Carlos Raul Villanueva (C5802, C5804, C5805, C5858). It is the hinge of the whole career: the work the Stadel report calls the key connecting his early period and his main work (C5858). In 1955 came Le Mouvement at Galerie Denise Rene, 6-30 April, co-curated by Rene and Pontus Hulten, with Agam, Bury, Calder, Duchamp, Jacobsen, Soto, Tinguely and Vasarely -- "the first major international exhibition of kinetic art" -- and in its catalogue Vasarely published his Yellow Manifesto, named for the colour of the sheet, postulating that "visual kinetics (plastique cinetique) relied on the perception of the viewer who is considered the sole creator, playing with optical illusions" (C5802, C5803, C5804, C5905). Le Mouvement presented works from his Black and White series, "complex networks of undulating and angular monochrome forms", together with Duchamp, Calder, Soto and Tinguely (C5808).

On 2 March 1959 he patented his method of unites plastiques: permutations of geometric forms cut out of a coloured square and rearranged (C5804). The museum dates the patent 1959-1961, an open question on the span (C5802). The strictly defined palette and forms were "three reds, three greens, three blues, two violets, two yellows, black, white, gray; three circles, two squares, two rhomboids, two long rectangles, one triangle, two dissected circles, six ellipses", later enlarged and numbered, and "out of this plastic alphabet, he started serial art, an endless permutation of forms and colours worked out by his assistants" (C5804). In 1960 came the first Planetary Folklore series -- "a system of endlessly interchangeable 'units' made from basic shapes inside one another, rendered in a kaleidoscope of colours", organised, in Christie's words, "using algorithms" into "dazzlingly complex grids" -- publicly presented under that name in 1963 (C5804, C5805, C5808). The 1960-versus-1963 dates are compatible (first series versus public presentation) and are kept separate, not fused. He came to consider himself a "plastician" rather than an artist, and his synthesis "came in the phrase 'plastic unity'" (C5805). The Times obituary, quoted in Encyclopedia.com, gives his creed plainly: "To him the artist was simply an artisan who creates his artefacts at will and in volume, so that they can be accessible to the ordinary person" (C5805, C5812). He "never believed in the uniqueness of the work of art" (C5805).

The term "Op Art" was not coined until 1964, when Donald Judd first used it in a review and Time magazine ran "Op Art: Pictures that Attack the Eye" a few weeks later (C5808, C5816). In February 1965 The Responsive Eye opened at MoMA, New York, curated by William C. Seitz, touring to St Louis, Seattle, Pasadena and Baltimore, with about 120 works by one count and around 125 by another -- the count is an open question -- including six paintings by Vasarely (C5804, C5808, C5816). Seitz was quoted as saying the works "exist less as objects to be examined than as generators of perceptual responses", not limited to "delight, anxiety and even vertigo" (C5808). Tate lists Vasarely with Bridget Riley and Jesus Rafael Soto as "three of the most important op artists" (C5807). The show made him the international figurehead of Op Art -- and it is the show that split the network: Soto refused to exhibit, because its design "intended to consecrate Victor Vasarely as the father of kinetic art" while Soto held Vasarely's "technique was not properly kinetic"; Cruz-Diez participated with Physichromie Number 116, with the specific aim of positioning his Physichromies "on a globally recognised platform", while sharing Soto's reservations about being presented as a "follower of Victor Vasarely and Josef Albers" (C5903, C5904). Awards around the peak: Guggenheim Prize 1964, Grand prix at the Sao Paulo Biennial and Grand prix de la gravure in Ljubljana in 1965, Legion d'honneur Chevalier 1970 (C5804, C5813, C5805).

From the late 1960s the Vega series deformed grids into swelling, balloon-like volumes: "His Vega series plays with spherical swelling grids creating an optical illusion of volume" (C5804); Guggenheim dates the Vega period from 1968, "at which time he deformed compositional elements in order to create the optical illusion of a bloated painting surface" (C5803). The Vonal period (1964-1970) went back to drawing, combining the linear and graphic themes of the Black and White period with a new exploration of colour (C5803). The Tribute to the Hexagon series (1964-1976) is "endless transformations of indentations and relief adding color variations, creating a perpetual mobile of optical illusion" (C5804, C5808). In parallel he pursued art in architecture and in everyday life: the Renault diamond logo in 1972, designed with his son Yvaral -- "a logo based on parallel lines", "both simple and intricate, high-tech and visually attractive", the Renault 5 the first model to feature it -- as part of Renault's Art and Industry policy, with some 40 works in dialogue with the manufacturer (C5808, C5809, C5810); the facade of the RTL radio headquarters in Paris, aluminium blinds with graphics and backlight, 1972 (C5804, C5866); a large kinematic object, Georges Pompidou, installed in the Centre Pompidou in 1976 (C5804); 154 serigraphs taken into space by cosmonaut Jean-Loup Chretien aboard Salyut 7 in 1982 (Wikipedia alone, no second source opened) (C5804); the original British cover for David Bowie's second album David Bowie (1969, also called Space Oddity) featuring his work in the background (C5804); a 500-piece run of Suomi tableware by Timo Sarpaneva for Rosenthal's Studio Linie (C5804). The 1972 Olympics logo claim rests on Christie's alone and needs a second source before the study uses it (C5808).

The museum chain is the utopia made concrete. On 5 June 1970 he opened his first dedicated museum, the Musee didactique, with over 500 works in a Renaissance palace in Gordes; it closed in 1996 (C5802, C5804). The Fondation Vasarely in Aix-en-Provence was created in 1966 by Vasarely and his wife Claire, recognised as a public utility in 1971, its building -- designed by Vasarely, architects John Sonnier and Dominique Ronsseray, construction begun 1973 -- inaugurated on 14 February 1976 (C5814, C5908, C5870). The foundation dates must not be collapsed into one year: 1966 creation, 1971 recognition, 1976 building are three different facts, and sources that say "established 1971" or "founded 1976" are using one of the three dates for the whole (C5803, C5805, C5814, C5817, C5908). The building is "a monumental sculpture in glass and aluminium, comprising 16 giant hexagons -- or 'cells', as he called them -- which housed an auditorium, a library, a warehouse and several galleries", holding 42 monumental integrations of 6 by 8 metres (C5808, C5870). Wikipedia's claim that it was "inaugurated in 1976 by French president Georges Pompidou, two years after his death" is impossible as stated -- Pompidou died in 1974 -- and must not be repeated without the flag; one guide records the inauguration "in the presence of Claude Pompidou and Jacques Chirac" (C5804, C5870). The Pecs museum opened 14 July 1976 (World Bank eMuseum says 1978, open question); the Budapest museum opened 8 May 1987 in Zichy Palace with over 400 works (Galerie Wagner says 1986, open question); a Vasarely Center in New York followed in 1978 and a Centre Vasarely in Oslo in 1982 (C5802, C5804, C5805, C5814, C5815). Naturalisation is dated 1959 by Encyclopedia.com and Britannica, 1961 by the Fondation-site Artnet text -- open question, 1959 used with the variant flagged (C5805, C5811, C5817).

In his eighties he battled prostate cancer and died in a Paris clinic on 15 March 1997; his wife of over sixty years had died several years before him, in 1990 (C5805). The age at death is 90 in most sources, 91 in the Pecs museum timeline -- explained by the 1906-versus-1908 birth-year conflict and by counting method -- and the place is Paris in every source except Galerie Wagner, which says Annet-sur-Marne; Paris is used and Annet-sur-Marne flagged (C5801, C5802, C5814). The New York Times obituary ran 18 March 1997 under the headline "Victor Vasarely, Op Art Patriarch, Dies at 90" (C5808, C5819). After his death the Fondation went through legal and financial disputes -- one source records that the man put in charge "was found to have stolen and sold most of the artwork" -- and his reputation, which had faded by the 1980s, was revived a decade later when his grandson Pierre became president in 2009, the building was classified a monument historique and fully restored, and the Centre Pompidou held the first proper French retrospective, "Vasarely -- Sharing Forms", in 2019 (C5805, C5808, C5819). The Yellow Manifesto's utopia is the sentence the whole career answers: a "polychrome city of happiness" where art would no longer be confined to museum walls but integrated into architecture, present in the streets, and accessible to all (C5810).

## M1 -- Stripe-only form: the animal with no outline

Zebra (W04, 1937) and Tigres (W05, 1938) are the mechanism before any system: two animals built only from curving stripes that bend and change width, with no drawn outline at all (C5854, C5855). The viewed image of Zebra shows, on a solid black ground, two zebra bodies interlocked in a tight knot, facing opposite ways: one head points up at top left, the other tucks into the centre, and only thick white stripes, bending and changing width, make the bodies, necks, heads, legs and tails appear (C5854). "It depicts two zebras with bold black stripes that seem to undulate before the eye" (C5854). Tigres is the colour sequel: "the zebras were designed of swirling stripes without outlines, and tigers were made of contrasting colors" (C5855). The viewed image shows two tiger bodies interlocked horizontally, concentric bands of red, yellow, green and black rippling across the backs, legs and tails, on a dark green-black ground with a yellow glow at left; the colour stripes make the bodies vibrate more than the black-and-white Zebra does (C5855). Zebra is "often credited as the earliest Op art painting" and "art historians consider this work to be the first true Op art painting ever created" (C5906). Its dimensions are disputed -- 52 x 60 cm in one source, 73 x 60 cm in another -- and its medium, acrylic on canvas, is reported but not independently verified (C5854). The date is 1937 in the Wikipedia body, "c. 1930s" in the infobox, "1937/38" in Christie's (C5808, C5820).

**Takeaway:** draw nothing but the stripes and the animal assembles itself out of rhythm. The shape is a side effect of the pattern, and it shivers as the eye moves across it.

## M2 -- Checkerboard-as-space: tilted planes on a flat ground

The Chess Board (W03, 1935, 61 x 41 cm) is the checkerboard-as-space device in its earliest form (C5853). The viewed image shows a vertical composition on a pale yellow ground covered edge to edge with a fine black-and-yellow checkerboard; four large tilted chessboard planes float at angles across the field, each carrying larger black and pale squares, with chess pieces standing on and around them -- a pale knight at left, a black knight at right, a crowned king near centre, a smaller knight and a rook lower down, a pale rook at bottom right (C5853). The small background checks and the large tilted boards fight each other, so flat pattern and tilted planes read at once (C5853). It comes from the same early graphic period as Zebra, and Christie's pairs the two exactly: "in 1935 he created L'Echiquier (The Chess Board) and in 1937/38 Zebres (Zebras) -- two hypnotic, Escher-like black-and-white images that are now considered the progenitors of Op Art" (C5808). The medium is not stated in the sources consulted (C5853).

**Takeaway:** cover the ground in fine checks, then tilt a few big boards across it. The ground refuses to stay flat; planes tilt in and out as attention shifts.

## M3 -- The plastic unit: a kit of parts with no single original

The patented system of 2 March 1959 -- variant span 1959-1961 -- is the centre of the career: a strictly defined palette and set of forms, cut from a coloured square and rearranged into endless permutations, worked out by assistants (C5804, C5802). The palette: three reds, three greens, three blues, two violets, two yellows, black, white, gray; the forms: three circles, two squares, two rhomboids, two long rectangles, one triangle, two dissected circles, six ellipses, later enlarged and numbered (C5804). Alphabet VR (W12, 1960) is the early application, and the viewed image shows the system at its clearest: a 3 x 3 grid of nine equal cells, each holding one flat geometric form in saturated colour -- red parallelogram on dark blue, purple circle on orange, dark blue pentagon on red, and so on -- hot orange, red, violet and dark blue, hard edges, no shading, the grid itself the composition (C5862). The alphabet was "designed on 15 roots derived from the square, circle, and triangle" (C5862). Planetary Folklore, from 1960 and publicly presented 1963, is the serial system at full stretch: "a system of endlessly interchangeable 'units' made from basic shapes inside one another, rendered in a kaleidoscope of colours", organised "using algorithms" into "dazzlingly complex grids" (C5804, C5805, C5808). The same system scales without changing its grammar: the Fondation Vasarely building is the alphabet at building scale (W20), its 16 hexagonal cells holding 42 monumental integrations (C5870); the Renault diamond logo (1972, with Yvaral) is the alphabet as a trademark, "a logo based on parallel lines" (C5809, C5810, C5870). Tate's Banya (1964) was made "by first assembling a collage of standardised forms, before painting the structure he'd composed. He called his practice 'Planetary Folklore'" (C5807).

**Takeaway:** fix the palette and the parts, then let the combinations do the work. A small kit recombines into a different picture every time, with no single original -- and the same kit builds a painting, a logo and a building.

## M4 -- Spherical grid distortion: the swelling Vega

Vega III (W10, 1957, oil on canvas, Guggenheim, prose only), Vega 200 (W13, 1968, acrylic on canvas, 200 x 200 cm) and Vega-Nor (W14, 1969, Albright-Knox) are one mechanism: a regular polychromatic grid is deformed so a sphere bulges out of the flat surface; the sphere's edge is never drawn, the distortion of the grid alone makes the ball, and the apparent depth and position of the swelling form change as the viewer moves around the painting (C5860, C5863, C5864). The viewed image of Vega 200 shows a square field of small circles in a regular grid, red and green on a blue ground; at the centre the circles swell into a huge sphere, the cells stretching into wide red ovals ringed in dark blue with a green centre, so a ball seems to bulge out toward the viewer, and toward the edges the circles shrink back to small dots and the sphere flattens into the grid again (C5863). Vega-Nor shows the same device in squares: a near-square field in red, blue, green and yellow, the squares stretching into tall rectangles at the sphere's equator and compressing at its poles, a band of yellow and blue across the middle, green above; "the sphere's edge is not drawn; the distortion of the grid alone makes the ball. The corners stay flat grid, so the ball reads as pressing out of a flat wall" (C5864). Vega III is the first statement: "grid composition that creates three-dimensional sphere illusion through careful value gradation and cell distortion. The viewer's movement around the painting constantly changes the apparent depth and position of the swelling form" (C5860). Its date is 1957 in the main source, with a range 1957-1968 in one survey and a 1997 caption treated as an error (C5860). Vega-Nor's medium is oil on canvas per the Albright-Knox record, acrylic per general sources; the Albright-Knox record is used (C5864). Guggenheim dates the Vega period from 1968, "at which time he deformed compositional elements in order to create the optical illusion of a bloated painting surface" (C5803).

**Takeaway:** deform the grid and the wall breathes. The sphere is never drawn -- only the grid is bent -- and it swells outward and sinks back as you walk past.

## M5 -- Density and size gradation: depth from how many and how big

Supernovae (W11, 1959-61, Tate) makes brightness and depth from nothing but how many marks there are and how big they get (C5861). The viewed image -- the best painting reproduction in this leg, the Tate image at 985 x 1536 -- shows a tall vertical black-and-white grid: the top half is a dense field of black squares on white, with a solid black block at upper left and a white field at upper right where the black squares shrink to nothing, so a bright square seems to glow out of the grid; a horizontal band of tilted black diamonds cuts across the middle; the bottom half keeps the same grid, but black discs of growing size march in diagonal rows from small dots at left to large discs at right, so the flat grid seems to swell and recede (C5861). The Commons photograph confirms the same painting framed, hanging on a plain white gallery wall, in tall portrait format (C5861). The medium is not stated on the Tate page opened, and is left blank rather than guessed (C5861). Wikipedia lists Supernovae in Tate Modern as the illustration of the kinetic-images period, and notes the unites plastiques patent of 2 March 1959 beside it -- the gradation mechanism and the alphabet arrive together (C5861).

**Takeaway:** change only the count and the size of the marks and the flat grid gains depth. A bright square glows out of nothing, and discs marching bigger make the wall swell.

## M6 -- Nested concentric frames: the tunnel that will not let the eye rest

Vonal-Stri (W18, 1975) and Orion Gris (W15, 1969) are the tunnel mechanism: lines and discs of decreasing scale move toward the centre, concentric square frames recede toward a small dark square like looking down a square tunnel, with colour rhythm supplying the kinetic energy (C5865, C5868). The viewed image of Vonal-Stri shows concentric square frames, one inside the next, receding toward a small dark square at the centre; the frames alternate in colour -- green, brown, orange, yellow and blue bands -- with the innermost frames in pale blue and white; the corners are not perfectly centred, the tunnel drifts slightly, so the frames seem to lean (C5868). "In Vonal - Stri (1975), the feeling of depth are created by the use of lines of decreasing scale moving towards the center of the canvas. The use of colors provides the viewer with the feeling of kinetic energy and space" (C5868). Orion Gris breaks the rings: the viewed image shows a dense field of coloured discs on a grid, in grey, black, red, yellow, blue and violet, forming concentric square rings that step inward toward a dark centre, "but the rings are broken and offset, so the eye cannot settle on one centre" (C5865). Grey discs dominate the outer ring, hot red and yellow the middle rings; the effect is a flat pattern that still seems to sink inward (C5865). The Vonal period is when Vasarely "rediscovered his linear work on the theme of the zebras, the grills, and the origins of his black and white period, this time incorporating" colour (C5868).

**Takeaway:** nest the frames and shrink them toward the centre, and the flat pattern sinks inward. Break the rings and the eye cannot settle -- the tunnel keeps pulling.

## M7 -- The ambiguous hexagon-cube: a solid that flips

Ambigu-B (W17, 1970, prose only) is the Kepler's-cube mechanism: "a multicolored hexagon that appears as an unstable set of interlocking cubes and cube-shaped spaces against a blue background", aiming "to create a mobile optical illusion whereby several parts of the picture-plane seems to be advanced towards and moving back from the viewer" (C5867). Vasarely "noted that on the one hand, there is a direction that leads to the world of cells and atoms that are invisible to the eye. On the other direction, there is a huge starry universe" (C5867). No free image was found in this leg, so the entry rests on prose; the same source describes the Kettes sculpture of 1984 as a vertical wooden structure with hexagonal patterns giving "the same 'Kepler's cube' effect as 2D works like Ambigu-B" (C5867). It is the tribute-to-the-hexagon logic in its purest form: "endless transformations of indentations and relief adding color variations, creating a perpetual mobile of optical illusion" (C5804). Kezdi-Ga (W16, 1970) is the same system issued as a print: screenprint in colours, edition of 250, 50.8 x 50.8 cm -- the serial, editioned side of the practice, the same alphabet at print scale (C5866).

**Takeaway:** build one hexagon out of cubes that are also hollows, and the solid never settles. Cube and space trade places as the eye moves -- inside and outside at once.

## M8 -- Viewer-motion kinetics: the pattern that moves although nothing moves

This is the doorway's core mechanism and the contrast with Cruz-Diez: there the colour changes as you move; here the pattern itself moves, although nothing in the work moves (C5901, C5905, C5906). From the Gordes works he developed kinematic images: superimposed acrylic glass panes create dynamic, moving impressions depending on the viewpoint; black-white photographies transposed into two colours combine frames into a single pane (C5804). The Le Mouvement objects of 1955 "gradually reveal themselves in their entirety as the viewer moves around the gallery" (C5905). His own term was "visual kinetics" (Yellow Manifesto, 1955) (C5905). The mechanism scales to the street and the building: the Hommage to Malevich wall in Caracas (W08, 1954, 100 square metres of ceramic tile) and the Sophia mural (W09, 1954) -- "a grid-like pattern against a white background" that "creates several illusions of movement and 3-dimensionality" -- are the kinetic image at architectural scale, walked past by students who never chose to look at art (C5858, C5859). The viewed photographs show both murals in situ, at a distance, the line work unreadable at that range but the scale proved by the buildings themselves (C5858, C5859). At the largest scale the Fondation Vasarely (W20, 1973-1976) is the same mechanism as a building: 16 hexagonal cells holding 42 monumental integrations of 6 by 8 metres, its facade a row of tall panels each carrying one huge black or white disc on a square ground, alternating black disc on white and white disc on black, reflected in the oval pool in front (C5870). And in Sign Sculpture (W19, 1977, Pecs), two stacked cube-like blocks set at an angle, every face covered in small diamond-shaped tiles in pink, blue, yellow, orange, lilac, teal, maroon and black-and-white checks, so "the tile pattern itself makes false cubes and recesses on the flat faces, so the sculpture reads as cubes within cubes" (C5869).

**Takeaway:** make the motion belong to the spectator's body, not to the object. Nothing in the work moves; the viewer walks, and the pattern moves with them -- in a pane of glass, on a university wall, and in a whole building.

## The aesthetic logic: the viewer as sole creator, the alphabet as a city

Vasarely's logic runs in one direction for sixty years, and it is the opposite of the unique masterpiece: from the hand-made picture toward a system anyone can recombine, and from the gallery wall toward the street. It starts with stripes that make an animal without an outline (M1), moves to checkerboards that tilt into space (M2), to a patented alphabet of coloured units permuted by assistants into endless serial pictures (M3), to grids deformed so a sphere bulges out of a flat wall (M4), to depth made from nothing but how many marks there are and how big they get (M5), to nested frames that sink inward and will not let the eye rest (M6), to a hexagon that flips between cube and hollow (M7), to the whole of it set in motion by the viewer's own body walking past (M8). At every step something is taken away -- the outline, the flatness, the single original, the drawn sphere, the artist's hand -- and something is handed to the viewer: first the eye, then the body walking through the gallery, then the citizen in the street. "Visual kinetics relied on the perception of the viewer who is considered the sole creator" is the theory; "the artist was simply an artisan who creates his artefacts at will and in volume, so that they can be accessible to the ordinary person" is the ethic; the "polychrome city of happiness" is the destination -- art integrated into architecture, present in the streets, accessible to all (C5804, C5805, C5810).

Against Cruz-Diez the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Cruz-Diez's motor is the viewer's movement past strips and through chambers of light, ending in colour itself as the space; Vasarely's motor is the viewer's movement past a programmed grid, ending in pattern that moves although nothing moves. Cruz-Diez dematerialised colour into an event you are inside; Vasarely dematerialised the picture into a system you complete. Against Soto the contrast is a generation: Soto's wires and rods make a space you walk through, and he refused The Responsive Eye because it consecrated Vasarely as the father of kinetic art while holding Vasarely's "technique was not properly kinetic" -- the elder's pattern moved only in the eye, and the younger men wanted the work itself to move (C5903). Both objections are honoured in this study: Vasarely's genuinely kinetic works -- the superimposed panes, the Le Mouvement objects -- are recorded beside the op-art side, and the refusal is recorded as a real dispute, not smoothed away. The social programme never left either side of it: the Caracas murals of 1954 were for students, the Fondation was built as "art for all, accessible and social", the Renault diamond put the alphabet on millions of cars, and the serigraphs went into space -- the system was always meant to leave the wall (C5870, C5809, C5804). Tomasello's judgement that Soto was doing kinetic art "before Vasarely" is opinion, never fact -- Tomasello's words in the Literal Magazine interview, "Moreover, Soto was doing Kinetic Art before him", carried verbatim, not presented as settled art history (C5913, carried C5509/C5311).
