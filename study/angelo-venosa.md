## The life: Sao Paulo, Rio, and the sculptor who stayed out of the painting

Angelo Augusto Venosa was born in Sao Paulo on 14 August 1954 -- the year is settled in every source; the day and month rest on Wikipedia alone, and the #675 log's stray "1956" has no source behind it at all, an internal error, not a conflict. He died in Rio de Janeiro on 17 October 2022, aged 68, of problems caused by amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, diagnosed in 2019 -- May 2019 per the Portuguese Wikipedia citing O Globo, 31 July 2019, a citation this run did not open directly. The English Wikipedia infobox shows a parenthetical "(2022-10-18)" beside the death date; the article text, the Portuguese Wikipedia, the Nara Roesler portfolio and the cited G1 obituary all say 17 October, and the study uses 17 October. (C16108, leg 1 C15900, C15904, C15905; synthesis C16051, C16055)

His parents, Giuseppe and Maria Venosa, were Italian immigrants who came to Sao Paulo in the early 1950s. His father had learned carpentry in Italy and worked at the Clube Paulistano designing and producing scenery; his mother did modelling and sewing. The official chronology presents the father's wood constructions and the mother's modelling and sewing as his first references for questions of form and making -- a carpenter father and a seamstress mother, and a sculptor whose lifelong recipe is wood armature wrapped in fabric. The family's region of origin is unresolved: the chronology says Campania, the Portuguese Wikipedia says Basilicata, citing a 2023 Folha de S.Paulo piece; the towns of Venosa and Senise are in Basilicata, and the chronology itself records a joint Venosa-Senise installation built from videos of a trip to those towns, described as the origin of their families. The study prints neither region as settled. (C16109, leg 1 C15900, C15905)

He went to the Ginasio Vocacional, an experimental public school in Sao Paulo state, 1965-1968, taking woodcut classes with the Polish-born artist Maciej Babinsky; then the Escola Brasil in Sao Paulo in 1973, the pioneering art-teaching experiment founded by Jose Resende, Carlos Fajardo, Luiz Paulo Baravelli and Frederico Nasser. In 1974, aged 19, he moved to Rio de Janeiro to study at the Escola Superior de Desenho Industrial (ESDI), the first design course in Latin America, modelled on Ulm, graduating in 1977. Ocula says he "graduated... in 1974"; that is his enrolment year, not his graduation year, and the study does not repeat it. While still a student he did a placement at the Instituto de Desenho Industrial at MAM Rio. In 1977 he headed the installation team for "Projeto construtivo brasileiro na arte" at the Pinacoteca de Sao Paulo. He married Sara Grosseman, a fellow ESDI student, in 1977; their daughter Barbara was born in 1978 and their son Daniel in 1988. (C16110, leg 1 C15900, C15903, C15906)

In the early 1980s -- the chronology says 1982, aged 28 -- he entered the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage and took painting classes with Luiz Aquila, then coordinator of the school. He later said painting was where his work discipline began, but that he was unsure what painting was for him, calling it "a kind of moral obligation". From 1981 he worked as a graphic designer at the Fundacao Casa de Rui Barbosa in Rio. In 2005-2007 he took a master's in Visual Languages at the Escola de Belas Artes, Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ), completing it in 2007 with the thesis "Elogio da opacidade" (In praise of opacity), supervised by Paulo Venancio Filho. (C16111, leg 1 C15900, C15903, C15906)

In 1984 he formed the Atelie da Lapa with Daniel Senise, Luiz Pizarro and Joao Magalhaes. The studio stood at Rua Silvio Romero in the Lapa neighbourhood, 1984-1990, and the Nara Roesler portfolio photographs his Lapa studio in 1985 and 1988, sculptures in progress. The group show "Atelie da Lapa" at the Universidade Federal Fluminense (UFF) in Niteroi in 1985 carried the studio's name, and a 1986 book-length record exists, "No Atelie da Lapa", a conversation between Venosa and critic Marcio Doctors. The Atelier Finep, which operated at the Paco Imperial from 1994 to 2007, was a separate later space for visual-language research; Venosa made an installation there in partnership with Senise, with videos from their trip to the Italian towns of Venosa and Senise. Do not confuse the two spaces. (C16112, leg 1 C15900, C15901, C15903)

His first group appearances were "Pintura! Pintura!" in November 1983 at the Fundacao Casa de Rui Barbosa, organised by Marcio Doctors, and in 1984 the 7th Salao Nacional de Artes Plasticas at MAM Rio and "Arte brasileira atual" at UFF in Niteroi, where he won the Souza Cruz prize. His first solo show opened at the end of 1985 at the Galeria de Arte do Centro Empresarial Rio; his first Sao Paulo solo followed in 1986 at the Subdistrito. In 1987 he showed four monumental works at the 19th Sao Paulo Biennial, curated by Sheila Leirner under the theme "Utopia versus Realidade", and in 1987-88 he was in "Modernidade: Arte brasileira do seculo XX" at the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris and then MAM Sao Paulo. In 1988-1990 he won the competition for a sculpture for Praca Maua in Rio; the untitled corten-steel work, nicknamed "A Baleia" (The Whale), was installed there in 1990 and moved in 1998 to Leme beach, Copacabana, after controversy. (C16113, leg 1 C15900, C15903)

In 1993 he showed at the 45th Venice Biennale, in the Brazilian pavilion shared with Carlos Fajardo and Emmanuel Nassar, selected by Nelson Aguilar and curated by Achille Bonito Oliva. In 1994 he received the Bolsa Vitae de Artes and an APCA prize. In 1998-2001, closing with a book in early 2006, he made "O Aleph" for the Projeto Fronteiras (Itau Cultural): a stone labyrinth based on a deformation of his fingerprint, installed at Santana do Livramento on the Brazil-Uruguay border; it no longer exists. In 2005 he was in the 5th Bienal do Mercosul in Porto Alegre, in the "Da Escultura a Instalacao" segment; a corten-steel work from that biennial was later donated to MAM Rio and installed in its front garden. In 2008 came his first extensive monograph, "Angelo Venosa" with an essay by Luiz Camillo Osorio, published by Cosac Naify. (C16114, leg 1 C15900, C15903)

In 2012 "Angelo Venosa: Panorama" opened at MAM Rio, curated by Ligia Canongia, described as the most comprehensive show of his career: 30 works from 1985-2012, marking 30 years of practice. It travelled to the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo (2013), the Palacio das Artes in Belo Horizonte (2014) and the Museu de Arte Moderna Aloisio Magalhaes (MAMAM) in Recife (2014). In 2016 "Giuse", his first solo show at Galeria Nara Roesler in Sao Paulo, showed about 20 pieces in three new series. In 2018-2019 came "Penumbra", at the Museu Vale in Vila Velha, then the Memorial Vale in Belo Horizonte and Galeria Nara Roesler in Sao Paulo, curated by Vanda Klabin. In July 2019 "Catilina" opened at the Paco Imperial in Rio, curated by Daniela Name; the O Globo piece on it is the source for the May 2019 ALS diagnosis, and the work reflects on memory, the future and his own finitude. In 2021 came "Quasi" at Nara Roesler in Rio and the "Clareira Project" at the Museu de Arte Contemporanea da Universidade de Sao Paulo (MAC USP). (C16115, leg 1 C15900, C15901, C15906, C15907)

He kept working until near the end despite ALS: the 2024 New York preview says the disease "did not keep him from creating until the very end", and the last dated works in the portfolio are 2022 pieces in wood, fabric, resin and fibreglass. Two posthumous shows followed: "Angelo Venosa: Escultor" at the Casa Roberto Marinho in Rio in 2023, and "Angelo Venosa", curated by Vik Muniz, at Nara Roesler New York in March-April 2024, his first solo show in New York City, about twenty works from 2015-2021 plus pieces from the 1980s and 1990s and 3D-printing studies. Muniz writes that Venosa's Rio studio "was a short walk from my parents' home" and names Venosa's "very close friends" as Daniel Senise, Luiz Zerbini and Beatriz Milhazes. He was represented by Galeria Nara Roesler. His collections, on the Nara Roesler list, include the Centro Cultural Sao Paulo, the Instituto Itau Cultural, the Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofia in Madrid, MAC-Niteroi, MAC USP, MAM Sao Paulo, MAM Rio, the Museu Nacional de Belas Artes, the Museu de Arte do Rio, and the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo; the 2024 New York press release adds the Colecao Patricia Phelps de Cisneros. (C16116, leg 1 C15901, C15907; leg 3 C16003, C16009)


## The 1984 show he did not join

Venosa is routinely grouped with Geracao 80 and singled out as one of the few in that generation who chose sculpture over the painting that dominated it. But he did NOT participate in the landmark 1984 exhibition "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" at Parque Lage. The official chronology states it explicitly, and Venosa said so in his own words in the Newcity Brazil interview of 26 February 2019. He was very shy on the occasion, he says, and has "very little to add to the cliches of that period"; he had just moved from Sao Paulo to Rio and had become a father. He WAS included in the 2004 twenty-year revisit "Onde esta voce, Geracao 80?", conceived by Marcos Lontra at the CCBB Rio, alongside works by the Sao Paulo Casa 7 group. (C16117, leg 1 C15900, C15902; synthesis C16056)

The false claim circulates in secondary sources: the Nara Roesler 2024 New York preview says he "emerged... having been part of" the 1984 show, a 2024 Revista Philos piece says the same of all five artists in its group show, the German Wikipedia lists it, and the Nara Roesler 2024 press release repeats it. The study does not repeat it, and treats the Nara Roesler portfolio as unreliable on the 1984 show. The #675 file itself never made the claim; it entered with the #676 brief, and this study corrects it here so it does not travel further down the chain. (C16118, leg 1 C15907, C15908; leg 3 C16003)


## The mechanisms

### M1 -- Skeleton and skin

The mechanism is the recipe that runs through the whole career. A rigid inner armature, usually wood, is wrapped in a soft covering, usually fabric, then sealed in a hard outer coat, usually fiberglass or resin, so the finished surface is a skin over a skeleton. Critic Lorenzo Mammi, quoted in the portfolio and the New York preview, says Venosa "simulates organic procedures and repeats the relationship between skeleton and skin, bone and cartilage, fluid and coagulated materials." The evidencing works are W01, W02, W03, W04, W19 and W20. (C16119, leg 2 C15950, C15951; synthesis C16064)

Watchable behaviour: a soft, lumpy, bodily surface at a distance; hard edges, seams and rigid spans betraying the wooden structure up close. The form looks fleshy from across the room and constructed from two steps away. A piece built on this mechanism should let the viewer read the same object twice -- once as a body, once as a carpentry job.

**Takeaway:** the skin is the lie the skeleton tells. The body is never there; only the layers that pretend it is. (C16120, leg 2 C15950)

### M2 -- Layering as reverse excavation

The work is built up layer upon layer, and the viewer reads it backwards, as if layers were being stripped away to reveal a buried form. Paulo Venancio Filho calls the process akin to reverse excavations. The evidencing works are W01, W02, W05, W06 and W17; the same layer logic becomes literal in the screwed plywood layers of W17 and the superimposed computer layers of M6. (C16121, leg 2 C15950, C15956; synthesis C16065)

Watchable behaviour: overlapping strata, visible joins and partial coverings invite the eye to peel the form mentally, layer by layer, and to treat the finished object as something dug up rather than assembled. A piece built on this mechanism should make the viewer want to strip it, not admire it.

**Takeaway:** build it up so it reads as if it were dug out. The newest layer is the oldest thing in the room. (C16122, leg 2 C15950)

### M3 -- Anatomical fragment

A real bodily fragment, or a material that behaves like one, is set into a small work so the piece reads as a body part rather than a whole body: ox teeth in wax or paraffin, marble against glass and wire, bone against wood and tar. Venosa said the teeth series used oxen teeth with beeswax, paraffin and lead. The evidencing works are W08, W09, W10 and W11; a related 1987 work in bone, wood, fabric, resin, pigment, tar and sand, 153 x 20 x 20 cm, extends the same fragment logic to a long thin format. (C16123, leg 2 C15950, C15951, C15952; synthesis C16066)

Watchable behaviour: a small quiet object first, then teeth, vertebrae or bone-like shapes recognised, and the whole piece re-read as a specimen, a relic or a laboratory sample. The 1990s exhibition space, the portfolio says, resembled a paleontological site or a pathologist laboratory. A piece built on this mechanism should shrink the body to one part and let the part accuse the whole.

**Takeaway:** one tooth is more of a body than a whole statue. The fragment is the evidence; the body is the rumour. (C16124, leg 2 C15950)

### M4 -- Planes that define an imaginary volume

A solid is suggested by flat planes alone, with no filled body between them, so the volume exists only in the viewer's head. Baleia is "made up of planes that evoke an imaginary volume, since it is not a body but a structure that evokes one." The evidencing works are W07, W13, W15 and W16. In W16 the effect is explicit: the bird skull only resolves as a whole from certain positions and varies with viewer movement. (C16125, leg 2 C15950, C15951; synthesis C16067)

Watchable behaviour: walking around changes what is solid and what is empty; from one angle the planes align into a whale or a skull, from another they fall apart into separate sheets. A piece built on this mechanism should make the volume a reward for moving, not a fact of the object.

**Takeaway:** the body is a rumour the planes agree on. Move, and the agreement breaks. (C16126, leg 2 C15951)

### M5 -- Exoskeleton: the shell is the body

A hollow form whose boundary is set only by its outer layer, with no inner mass. The Giuse text defines externally-structured bodies, hollow forms whose boundaries are set exclusively by their outer layer, like exoskeletons. The evidencing works are W17 and W18; the same idea appears in the fiberglass cocoons of W19 and W20, where the shell is wood, fabric and fiberglass rather than plywood or print plastic. (C16127, leg 2 C15954, C15956, C15950; synthesis C16068)

Watchable behaviour: the viewer looks for an interior and finds none, or only air and the thickness of the shell; the form reads as armour, husk or cocoon rather than flesh. A piece built on this mechanism should refuse the inside entirely -- there is nothing to find in there, and that is the point.

**Takeaway:** when the shell is the body, there is nothing inside to disappoint you. The outside is the whole animal. (C16128, leg 2 C15954)

### M6 -- Digital ideal form forced against material limits

An ideal form is made on a computer, then built by superimposing layers, and the material resists the ideal, so the finished work carries the marks of that fight. The Giuse text says the artwork's original conception is forced against its material limits, which can only be truly configured during its making. The evidencing works are W17 and W18. The Giuse text is explicit that 3D-print stringings, "threads jutting out like seam slippages", are kept as part of the artwork and subvert the objectivity of the technological process. (C16129, leg 2 C15954, C15956, C15950; synthesis C16069)

Watchable behaviour: a smooth, computer-like organic curve interrupted by visible layer steps, screw heads, print stringings and handmade irregularities; the work looks both digital and handmade at once. A piece built on this mechanism should keep the mistakes the machine made -- the stringing is the signature, not the flaw.

**Takeaway:** the computer dreams the form; the material argues back. Keep the argument visible. (C16130, leg 2 C15954)

### M7 -- Shadow as a second work (penumbra)

The sculpture is placed so its cast shadow on the wall becomes an integral part of the work, a material body plus an immaterial double. The 2019 interview says the sculpture casts a shadow on the wall to form an integral part of each work, which explains the show title Penumbra. Artsy reports that the Penumbra works explore full and empty areas and that the sculptures combine with the shadows they cast to produce an enigmatic body. The evidencing works are W19 and W20, with the Penumbra 2018 works as the direct examples -- including one wood piece 250 x 157 x 770 cm, the longest single dimension recorded this run. (C16131, leg 2 C15952, C15955, C15950, C15951; synthesis C16070)

Watchable behaviour: two forms at once, the solid and its dark twin, and the twin changes with light, distance and viewing angle while the solid stays fixed; full and empty areas trade places. A piece built on this mechanism should give the shadow a job -- the shadow is not decoration, it is the second half of the sculpture.

**Takeaway:** the sculpture is only half the work. The other half is on the wall, and it moves. (C16132, leg 2 C15952, C15955)

### M8 -- Seed to monument: scale jump

A small work is treated as the seed of a large one, and the same organic logic is run at two wildly different scales. The New York preview says the small 3D prints "look like seeds of the large-scale works for which Venosa is renowned." The evidencing works are W18, W19 and W07. The New York show staged this jump by placing small 3D-printed sculptures and natural found objects -- tree bark, dead flowers -- on a low table in the same room as the large cocoon works, so it was hard to tell which objects were found and which were made. (C16133, leg 2 C15951, C15953, C15950; synthesis C16071)

Watchable behaviour: the same coral, cocoon or bone logic recognised in a 60 cm print and in a 420 cm installation, the recognition jumping scale rather than growing gradually. A piece built on this mechanism should put the seed and the monument in the same room and let the viewer do the jump.

**Takeaway:** scale is not growth, it is a jump. The seed already knows how big it is. (C16134, leg 2 C15951, C15953)


## The aesthetic logic: the body that was never there

Venosa's logic is a refusal of the filled body. His generation went back to painting; he went to sculpture, and then refused what sculpture is supposed to give -- a solid thing. The skeleton-and-skin recipe (M1) makes a body out of carpentry and cloth; the layering (M2) makes it read as something excavated; the fragment (M3) gives one tooth instead of a body; the planes (M4) give a volume that exists only in the viewer's head; the exoskeleton (M5) gives a shell with nothing inside; the digital turn (M6) gives a form the material fought; the shadow (M7) gives the body a twin that is not there either; and the scale jump (M8) gives the same logic at 60 cm and at 420 cm, as if size were irrelevant to the animal. The work is always about a body, and the body is always absent.

The watchability follows from that refusal. A lumpy fiberglass mass reads as flesh until the seams give it away as carpentry. A small wax slab reads as a quiet object until the teeth are recognised and the room becomes a laboratory. A set of steel planes reads as nothing until the viewer walks to the one angle where the whale appears. A hollow plywood form reads as a body until the viewer looks inside and finds air. A shadow on the wall reads as a second sculpture until the light moves. The viewer is always doing the work the artist refused to do: supplying the body.

What he is not: not a carver (he builds up, he does not cut away -- the one exception, the marble of 1997, is a single year, not a practice); not a colourist (colour here is material colour -- cream fiberglass, rust corten, pale wax, blackened wood -- and no source gives a colour system); not a monumental sculptor in the heroic sense (his largest public work is a whale made of empty planes, and his scale swings from 21 cm to 770 cm without ever settling into a steady growth). The closest kin in the studied corpus is Senise, who also lets a place or a material do the picturing -- but Senise's floor prints a picture that was already there, while Venosa builds a body that was never there at all. His operation is his own: build the skeleton, wrap the skin, leave the inside empty, and let the viewer supply the animal.

The portfolio says corten steel gains new meaning in Venosa because the material is imbued with the memory of a figurative image, and its oxidation approaches the concept of skin. (M4, M8)

- W11 Untitled, 1997, marble, glass, wire, pitch, 30 x 45 x 25 cm -- A. Hard marble against glass, wire and pitch; the portfolio reproduces it with a detail view. The only marble work found in the sources read; do not present marble as a continuing late material. (M3)

- W15 Untitled, 2006, corten steel, 238 x 117 x 0.3 cm -- B. Thin corten-steel plane work, 0.3 cm thick; the near-flat plane is the direct descendant of the plane logic of Baleia (W07). (M4)
- W16 Turdus, 2009, acrylic planes, no dimensions sourced -- C. A simulation of a bird skull sliced into acrylic planes and suspended in the air; the identification of the figure, of the whole, depends on the viewer position and varies with viewer movement. (M2, M5, M6)
- W18 Arquipelago, 2016, FDM print, 60 x 60 x 72 cm -- A. 3D-printed group shown in Giuse in 2016; the New York preview says such 3D prints, arranged in groups and reduced in scale, resemble organic structures such as corals and echinoderms and look like seeds of the large-scale works. (M5, M6, M8)
- W19 Catilina, 2019, wood, fabric, fiberglass, 420 x 300 cm -- A. Installation at the Paco Imperial, Rio; the portfolio calls it Venosa's last work in its public-works framing, and the New York preview groups it with Penumbra (2018) and the Clareira Project (2021) as recent practice. The same 420 x 300 cm dimensions are also listed for an Untitled work of 2019 in the portfolio, which may be the same piece under two labels. The portfolio says these recent pieces evoke fossils, fragments, or entire bodies of unknown creatures, prompting contemplation of past, present and future, and of death and survival. (M1, M5, M7, M8)
