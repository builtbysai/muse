# John Baldessari: Study Notes

**Site:** John Anthony Baldessari -- born June 17, 1931, in National City, California; died January 2, 2020, at home in Venice, California, at 88 -- American conceptual artist who spent fifty years turning found photographs, borrowed sentences, and explicit rules into pictures, films, and videos, and who taught a generation of the Pictures Generation at CalArts while doing it. He is the painter who stopped painting, the photographer who never took the photograph, and the teacher whose students -- David Salle, Jack Goldstein, Mike Kelley, Tony Oursler, James Welling, Barbara Bloom, Matt Mullican, Troy Brauntuch, and James Casebere among them -- carried his procedures further than he did. His work is a rule system first and an image second: pick a rule from a how-to book and break it, hire an amateur to paint what you pointed at, throw three balls at the sky thirty-six times and keep the twelve best failures, cover the face with a coloured dot so the posture has to carry the story. ## The life: the teacher who removed his own hand

John Anthony Baldessari was born June 17, 1931, in National City, California, to Hedvig Marie Jensen (1896-1950), a Danish nurse, and Antonio Baldessari (1877-1976), an Italian salvage dealer. He died January 2, 2020, at home in Venice, California, at 88; no cause of death was publicly given. He is survived by daughter Annamarie, son Tony, and sister Betty Sokol. Simple Wikipedia gives January 3 as the death date; that is wrong, and January 2 is confirmed by the UCLA obituary and the Wikipedia article read end to end. (C21908, leg 1 C21700, C21701, C21718; synthesis C21850, C21878)

The education spine is a long apprenticeship in other people's institutions: BA at San Diego State College in 1953, MA in 1957, postgraduate work at UC Berkeley (1954-55), UCLA (1955), Otis Art Institute (1957-59), and Chouinard. He began teaching art in the San Diego school system in 1959, joined the originating faculty of UC San Diego in 1968, and taught at CalArts from 1970 as founding faculty, running the Post-Studio class. The CalArts end year is UNRESOLVED and must be carried, not settled: Tate, Moderna Museet, and UCLA give 1970-1988; EN Wikipedia and the CalArts blog give 1970-1986. Do not print either end year as settled. He later taught at UCLA 1996-2007 by three-source consensus; Wikipedia alone says 1986-2008, and that variant is carried, not resolved. (C21909, leg 1 C21702-C21704, C21707; synthesis C21851)

The CalArts students are the hinge of the whole orbit, and the cohort split matters. The first classes, early 1970s: David Salle (BFA 1973), Jack Goldstein (MFA 1972, Baldessari's teaching assistant), Barbara Bloom (BFA 1972), James Welling (BFA 1972, MFA 1974), Matt Mullican (BFA 1974), Troy Brauntuch (BFA 1975), Eric Fischl (BFA 1972). The LATER cohort, NOT first classes: Mike Kelley (MFA 1978), Tony Oursler (BFA 1979), and James Casebere (MFA 1979), who studied under Baldessari and Doug Huebler after his BFA at Minneapolis in 1976 and the Whitney Independent Study Program in fall 1977. EN Wikipedia's "first classes" list wrongly includes Kelley and Oursler; do not repeat it. (C21910, leg 1 C21705, C21706; leg 3 C21803; synthesis C21852)

The dealers, in order: Ileana Sonnabend worldwide from 1972, for 26 years; Margo Leavin 1984-2013; Spruth Magers from 1998 (the estate since 2021); Marian Goodman, whose start year is unresolved across sources -- 1997 vs 1999 vs early 2002 -- and must not be stated as a single year. The career turning point is dated and literal: a painter who began incorporating text and photography in the mid-1960s, and in 1970 burnt all his 1953-1966 paintings in the Cremation Project -- about 100 works, taken to a crematorium in San Diego with friends and students, some ashes baked into cookies and placed in a book-shaped urn with a bronze plaque giving the paintings birth and death dates, as if they were a person. From 1970 he worked in printmaking, film, video, installation, sculpture, and photography. Over 200 solo shows and 1,000+ group shows; Documenta V (1972) and VII (1982); six Whitney Biennials; Carnegie International 1985-86; Venice Biennales 1997, 2003, 2009. Retrospectives: New Museum 1981 (first US retrospective), MOCA Los Angeles 1990-92, "This Not That" 1995-96, "Pure Beauty" (Tate Modern, opened October 13, 2009, 130+ works, travelled to MACBA, LACMA, and the Met 2010-11). (C21911, leg 1 C21708, C21710-C21713; synthesis C21853, C21854)

Awards, as listed by the sources read: Guggenheim Fellowship 1986; Oscar Kokoschka Prize 1996; CAA Distinguished Artist Award 1999; Americans for the Arts Lifetime Achievement Award 2005; Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement, Venice Biennale 2009; Goslarer Kaiserring 2012; National Medal of Arts 2014, presented at the 2015 ceremony -- the award year vs presentation year variant is carried, not resolved. His personal auction record: "Quality Material" (1966-1968) sold for $4.4 million at Christie's New York in 2007. First solo show: 1960 vs first gallery solo 1968 at Molly Barnes is unresolved across sources; do not state one as settled. (C21912, leg 1 C21716, C21717; synthesis C21855, C21878)



## M1 -- Break a named rule, then label it

Baldessari's first great move was to take a rule out of a how-to photography book and break it on camera. The rule, from a standard manual: never pose a subject in front of a tree, because the tree will look as if it grows from the subject's head. In "Wrong" (W01, 1966-68) he poses himself in front of a palm tree so the palm does exactly that, and captions the photograph with the single word "WRONG" in capitals. The photograph is photoemulsion with acrylic on canvas, 59 by 45 inches. The viewer spots the violation before reading the verdict, and the verdict is the punchline: the work is a rule, a break, and a label, in that order. (C21913, leg 2 W01 and M1; synthesis C21859)

The same mechanism returns a decade later in rectangular form. In "Violent Space Series: Two Stares Making a Point but Blocked by a Plane (for Malevich)" (W12, 1976), a film still of a rooftop shoot-out -- two gangsters staring at each other -- has its exact centre covered by a tilted white square, a quotation of Malevich's "White on White." The narrative climax is replaced by an abstract shape; the stares keep pointing at nothing. Baldessari's own gloss, via the Met: the plaster that fills missing shards in Greek vases raised the question, "How many shards would have to be missing before that vase was no longer a vase?" The question is the mechanism: how much can you remove from a picture before it stops being that picture? (C21914, leg 2 W12 and M1; synthesis C21859)

**Takeaway:** pick a composition rule from a how-to source, violate it precisely once, and caption the violation -- the viewer must spot the break before reading the label. (C21915)


## M2 -- Text is the picture

In 1966-68 Baldessari stopped painting pictures and started painting sentences. "What Is Painting" (W02, 1966-68) is a statement drawn from art theory, lettered on an otherwise empty canvas by a professional sign painter in plain black type, synthetic polymer paint on canvas, 172.1 by 144.1 cm. Somebody else built and primed the canvas, the sign painter painted the text, and Baldessari's own account, via MoMA, is the whole defence: "it is a painting, because it is done with paint on canvas." The tension is between the promise of a picture and the delivery of a sentence, and the sentence wins. (C21916, leg 2 W02 and M2; synthesis C21860)

"Tips for Artists Who Want to Sell" (W03, 1966-68) is the same mechanism with found material instead of theory. While teaching night school he found a sheet of commercial advice to artists -- paintings in light colours sell faster than dark ones, paint popular subjects -- and had the tips lettered large on canvas, without comment, acrylic on canvas, 68 by 56 1/2 inches. The work is ironic because its author was hardly selling, and because the "rules" are cynical ploys dressed as craft wisdom. The viewer reads it as instruction, then re-reads it as satire. No image is needed; the list is the image. The text must be borrowed, found, or instructional -- never invented to sound poetic. Invented lyric text breaks the mechanism. (C21917, leg 2 W03 and M2; synthesis C21860)

**Takeaway:** an empty field plus a borrowed, found, or instructional sentence, lettered plainly in black, no image -- hold the text long enough for the viewer to read it twice and realise the text IS the picture. (C21918)


## M3 -- Delegate the hand

Prompted by Al Held's jibe that "all conceptual art is just pointing at things," Baldessari photographed a friend pointing at ordinary things on a walk, then hired amateur painters found at local craft fairs to copy the slides as faithfully as they could, on canvases he supplied. A professional sign painter then hand-lettered "A Painting by [painter's name]" under each image. That is the "Commissioned Paintings" (W04, 1969), fourteen of them; "A Painting by Anita Storck" is acrylic and oil on canvas, 59 1/4 by 45 1/2 inches. No trace of Baldessari's hand remains; the signature belongs to someone else. The wobble of the execution is part of the piece, and the credit line is the work: the pointing hand drags the viewer's attention to a stain, a crack, a smudge, and then strands it there -- why this, and not anything else? (C21919, leg 2 W04 and M3; synthesis C21861)

The same delegation runs through the videos. Asked to exhibit at the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design in Halifax and unable to travel, he mailed instructions instead: students were to write "I will not make any more boring art" on the gallery walls, floor to ceiling, like a school punishment. They did -- about 4,000 times in April 1971. He filmed himself doing the same in a notebook in Venice until the tape ran out: "I Will Not Make Any More Boring Art" (W06, 1971), black-and-white video with sound, 13:06 minutes. The artist writes only the instruction; an amateur, a student, a sign painter, or the viewer executes it. Do not "improve" the amateur hand -- the wobble is the work. (C21920, leg 2 W06 and M3; synthesis C21861)

**Takeaway:** the artist writes one sentence of instruction and somebody else executes it -- credit the executor by name, and let the wobble of their hand stay visible. (C21921)


## M4 -- Repeat until it becomes texture

One sentence or one action, repeated until the surface is full or the tape runs out, each repetition slightly less careful than the last. In "I Will Not Make Any More Boring Art" (W06) the punishment sentence fills the wall and then the room, the handwriting drifting, the lines crowding, until the sentence becomes texture. The comedy is the contradiction: a vow against boredom performed in the most boring way possible. The boredom is the content; the variation inside the boredom is the pleasure. (C21922, leg 2 W06 and M4; synthesis C21862)

The film version is "Six Colorful Inside Jobs" (W13, 1977): a student filmed from directly overhead as he paints a small cubicle, about 12 by 8 feet, a different colour each day in colour-wheel order -- red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet -- each day three to four hours of painting compressed to about five minutes of film, 16mm colour transferred to video, silent, 32:53 minutes. The title is a pun on a criminal "inside job"; the seventh day, by the Biblical pattern, is rest. A hidden door opens so the painter can escape without walking on wet paint. Baldessari's own account, via MoMA: his father was a landlord, he painted rentals as a boy, and he would occupy his mind by saying "now I am painting a wall, now I am making a painting" -- the activity was identical, only the label changed. The watchable pleasure is total, patient coverage plus the drumbeat of the sequence. (C21923, leg 2 W13 and M4; synthesis C21862)

**Takeaway:** repeat one phrase or one action N times and let drift accumulate -- stop when the wall is full or the timer ends, and let the repetition itself be the content. (C21924)


## M5 -- The impossible throw, keep the best failures

"Throwing Three Balls in the Air to Get a Straight Line (Best of Thirty-Six Attempts)" (W11, 1973) states an impossible physical goal, attempts it a fixed number of times -- one full roll of 35mm film, 36 frames -- while his then-wife Carol Wixom photographed, trying to catch three orange balls in a straight line in mid-air against the bright California sky. He never succeeded. He published the 12 attempts he judged closest, a portfolio of 12 colour offset lithographs, each sheet about 9 1/2 by 12 5/8 inches, edition of 2000. Aperture: "through the magic of photography, gravity was defeated, and the balls never had to come down." Never claim success; the near-miss is the work. The loop is pure chance plus a selection rule, and the score must be shown: three balls arc, scatter, and fall; a frozen frame is judged against an invisible straight line; the best near-miss is kept and the rest discarded. (C21925, leg 2 W11 and M5; synthesis C21863)

**Takeaway:** state an impossible alignment, attempt it a fixed number of times, score each attempt, publish the best few -- and show the score, because the near-miss is the work. (C21926)


## M6 -- Choose, with no criterion given

"Choosing (A Game for Two Players): Carrots" (W09, 1972) is fifteen colour photographs, each 10 by 8 inches. A participant chooses one carrot from a lineup of three; the chosen carrot stays, the rejects are swapped out, and the game continues until the bunch is used up. The finger points; the photograph records the pick. The viewer is left to guess why that carrot won -- which is the point, since aesthetic choice has no stated rule. The sequence is a tournament bracket where the judging standard is invisible. (C21927, leg 2 W09 and M6; synthesis C21864)

The same game returns with faces instead of vegetables. In "Prima Facie (Second State): Hopeless" (W16, 2005), a film-still portrait of a woman with an expression of loss is paired, side by side, with a single adjective in bold capitals -- HOPELESS -- set at the same size as the image. Baldessari's question: "Can one match accurately a facial expression to a word?" His answer is built into the title: our judgement is prima facie, at first sight, and the actors are faking the emotion anyway, so the match may be entirely wrong. Other pairings in the series: Apprehensive, Suspicious, Pleasant, Noble, Kind, Troubled, Scornful, Jolly. Swap the word and the same face reads differently; swap the face and the same word reads differently. The unstated reason is the engine. (C21928, leg 2 W16 and M6; synthesis C21864)

**Takeaway:** present three near-identical objects, or one face and one word, and force a choice without stating the standard -- the reason stays unstated, and that is the engine. (C21929)


## M7 -- Block the point

Find the most informative part of an image -- the object of a gaze, a face, a whole body -- and cover it with a flat shape. In "Violent Space Series" (W12) it is a tilted white square over the point where two stares meet. In "Bloody Sundae" (W15, 1987) it is coloured circles over all five faces: two scenes stacked one above the other like the layers of an ice-cream sundae -- on top, two men attack a third beside a stack of paintings; beneath, a couple lounge decadently on a bed -- black-and-white photographs with vinyl paint on board, 93 by 65.5 inches. The violence above and the languor below are held together only by the title and the stack, and the dots pop onto each face in turn, anonymising everyone at once. The spelling is Sundae, not Sunday; that spelling in some summaries is an error. (C21930, leg 2 W12, W15 and M7; synthesis C21865)

In "Nose & Ears, Etc.: Head (with Nose)" (W17, 2006), three-dimensional digital print with acrylic paint, 54 by 43 1/4 inches, the blocking becomes the portrait: everything except one feature is painted out, and the surviving nose or ear floats, oversized, on a flat field. Noses and ears are chosen because, detached, they look alien; eyes and lips are avoided because they are "freighted with emotion" and "get a lot of attention" in art history. The Met notes the link to his own six-foot-seven frame: "I never thought of the parts of my body as going together." The viewer reconstructs the missing part from the surroundings -- and recognises a person from a fragment that should not be enough. (C21931, leg 2 W17 and M7; synthesis C21865)

**Takeaway:** find the point of maximum information and cover it with a flat shape -- a white square, a coloured dot, a coat of paint -- and leave everything else, so the viewer must rebuild what is missing. (C21932)


## M8 -- Ring the tender with the threatening

"Kiss / Panic" (W14, 1984) is gelatin silver prints with oil tint on board, in eleven parts, 80 by 72 inches overall. Ten black-and-white photographs of male hands holding guns radiate around a centre: a colour close-up of a couple kissing, and below it a chaotic crowd scene, people running in all directions. The two largest categories in Baldessari's film-still archive, he said, were the kiss and the gun. The guns at once threaten the embrace, protect it, and instigate the panic of the crowd. The Met notes the tight rectilinear organisation owes something to Mondrian. Do not explain the relationship; let the viewer invent the plot. The viewer assembles a story -- lovers, threat, stampede -- that no single still contains. (C21933, leg 2 W14 and M8; synthesis C21866)

"Bloody Sundae" (W15) is the same mechanism stacked instead of ringed: the tender scene below, the violent scene above, the title the only glue. In both works the two halves argue and the viewer is the judge. A prompt built from this mechanism must pick two opposed image categories -- tender and threatening -- put one tender image at the centre in colour, ring it with threatening fragments in black and white, and refuse to explain. (C21934, leg 2 W14, W15 and M8; synthesis C21866)

**Takeaway:** one calm, coloured centre inside a ring or stack of threatening fragments -- never explain the relationship, and let the viewer invent the plot. (C21935)


## M9 -- One part, one word, equal weight

Reduce a person to a single feature or a single expression, and pair it with a single word at exactly the same scale. "Prima Facie" (W16) is one face and one word, side by side, equal size. "Nose & Ears, Etc." (W17) is one feature and no word at all, the feature doing the whole job. "Raised Eyebrows / Furrowed Foreheads" (W18, 2008-2009) takes a single expression and features it throughout a whole series: faces and bodies from film stills covered with layers of paint or collage, or altered by paint superimposed over the foreheads and eyebrows, so the expression is both the subject and the thing being hidden. A grid of faces, all wearing the same micro-expression; the paint creeps over each forehead in turn; the expression survives the paint or is cancelled by it. The viewer starts reading eyebrows the way they once read whole faces. Swap either half and the meaning must change: that is the test of whether the mechanism is working. (C21936, leg 2 W16, W17, W18 and M9; synthesis C21867)

**Takeaway:** keep one feature or one expression, erase the rest with flat colour, add one word at exactly the same size -- swap either half and the meaning must change. (C21937)


## M10 -- Repaint the room through the wheel

A fixed top-down room; a figure paints it one flat colour per day, in colour-wheel order; each day time-lapse compressed. "Six Colorful Inside Jobs" (W13, 1977) is the original: six colours, six days, one small cubicle, the painter escaping through a hidden door so as not to walk on wet paint. Forty years later Baldessari returned to the same procedure and extended it to eight colours in print form: "Eight Colorful Inside Jobs" (W20, 2017), shown at Mixografia, Los Angeles, and Librairie Marian Goodman, Paris, 2018, with individual works such as "Eight Colorful Inside Jobs: Magenta" and "Eight Colorful Inside Jobs: Yellow." The mechanism is the same as W13; only the count and the medium have changed, which is itself the point: a good rule outlives its first material. Seen as a sequence, the eight prints replay the film as a flipbook. This is the most directly filmable rule in the set and the default loop when a prompt needs a reliable watchable system. (C21938, leg 2 W13, W20 and M10; synthesis C21868)

**Takeaway:** fix the room, fix the camera, fix the colour order, and let time-lapse do the composing -- one colour per cycle, compressed to seconds, the painter escaping without crossing wet paint. (C21939)


## The aesthetic logic: a procedure, not a look

Baldessari has no look. He has procedures, and the procedures are portable: the same rule that painted a room in 1977 paints it again in 2017, in print, in eight colours instead of six, and nothing is lost, because the rule was the work all along. The career arc is the proof. 1966-1970: language replaces image and the hand is removed -- sign painters, amateurs, cremation. 1971-1977: the rule becomes a performance in time -- repetition, singing, choosing, throwing, repainting. 1976-1987: the found still is the material -- block the centre, ring the kiss with guns, dot the faces. 1997-2009: text returns as caption and as colour name, the body reduced to one part or one expression. 2009-2017: the old rules return at larger scale in new materials -- a brain as a cloud, a room repainted eight times in print, a film script laid over an art image. The through-line is a procedure applied to whatever material is at hand, not a style. (C21940, leg 1, leg 2 evolution; synthesis C21858)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the found thing beats the made thing: a how-to rule, a night-school tip sheet, a film still, a script fragment, a carrot. The artist's job is selection and arrangement, not invention -- and the prompt writer's job is the same: borrow the sentence, do not write a prettier one. Second, erasure is addition: cover the face and the posture starts talking; cover the point of the gaze and the stares start talking; burn the paintings and the urn starts talking. A dot placed at random is not a Baldessari; a dot placed on the most informative part is. Third, the viewer completes the work: the choice with no criterion, the kiss ringed with guns, the face matched to a word that may be entirely wrong. The meaning is not delivered; it is assembled by the person looking, from parts that refuse to add up. That is why the work survives as a prompt system: every mechanism is a rule with a visible input, a visible operation, and a visible output, and the output is always slightly wrong in the interesting way. (C21941, leg 2 mechanisms and "What NOT to do"; synthesis C21869)

The late works do not invent new rules; they run the old rules on bigger stages. "Brain/Cloud (Two Views)" (2009) puts the viewer on a 15-second delay looking at a giant brain that is also a cloud, with a palm tree and a seascape. "Movie Scripts / Art" (W19, 2014) lays a script fragment over an art-historical image and lets the two halves argue: the script tells the viewer what to expect, the image refuses to deliver it. The "Goya Series: There Isn't Time" (1997) and the "Duress Series" (2003) flatten figures in danger into solid-colour silhouettes -- the dot grown to cover the whole person. None of these needs a new mechanism. The rule, repeated, is the career. (C21942, leg 2 W19, W20, evolution; synthesis C21858)
