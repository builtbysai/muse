## 1. Lancaster Gate, Hamilton House, Berlin: the unpromising start

Maud Pauline Karpeles was born on Thursday 12 November 1885 at 33 Lancaster Gate, Bayswater, London, the third of five children. Her father, Joseph Nicolaus Karpeles, was born in Hamburg, a tea merchant and stockbroker who had been naturalised as a British subject in 1881, four years before her birth. Her mother, Emily Karpeles, nee Raphael, was born in London, the daughter of Henry Raphael, a foreign banker. Both parents were of Jewish ancestry but non-practising -- the phrase is "non-practising" in Cecil Sharp's People and "nonreligious" in Wikipedia, and the difference is worth noting because the family is otherwise invisible in the Jewish communal record. The older sisters were Lucy (born 1882) and Florence (born 1884); the younger sister Helen (born 1888, later Mrs Douglas Kennedy) would become the closest collaborator of Maud's life; the younger brother Arthur was born in 1895. When Maud was about ten the family moved from Lancaster Gate to 87 Westbourne Terrace, Paddington, and stayed there until Emily's death in 1914 -- the house that would later host the Folk Dance Club, the girls removing rugs and furniture and ruining the parquet with Vim for grip, "without protest" from the parents. Maud followed her older sisters to boarding school at Hamilton House, Tunbridge Wells, where she learned violin and piano and studied German. In 1906 she spent six months in Berlin with her aunt and uncle, taking private piano lessons at the Hochschule fur Musik and going to concerts. She was twenty, and nothing in this record predicts a collector: a piano student from a comfortable London family, with no folk song in it at all. One material fact matters for everything that follows and is stated directly in Kent Trad: she was of independent means through her father, which is why she could devote her life to folk music unpaid. The Musical Traditions review of Pakenham puts it more bluntly -- she was "independently rich". The unpaid career that follows, sixty years of it, rests on that private income, and no source read quantifies it. She was also diminutive in stature, "much smaller than her younger sister Helen", visible in the Kinora home-movie films -- a single-sourced physical detail, but one that recurs in the field accounts, where she is described as a slight figure walking ahead into the woods.

**Takeaway:** the start is a comfortable London childhood, a Berlin piano term, and a private income -- the exact opposite of Sharp's slate-merchant's son and mathematics degree, and the exact condition that let her work for him, and then for the movement, without ever being paid for it. [C34845]

## 2. Canning Town: settlement work and the Guild of Play

On her return from Berlin she volunteered for the Invalid Children's Aid Association, based within the Mansfield House University Settlement in Canning Town, East London. Three or four days a week she visited disabled children and their families at home in East Ham and Barking, helping with hospital visits and developmental support. Once a week she volunteered at the Mansfield House Guild of Play in Canning Town, teaching music and movement to local girls; her piano skills were the tool. The Guild, started by workers at the Bermondsey Settlement under Grace Kimmins, aimed to teach girls "vigorous happy dances for recreative purposes on educational lines", with songs and dances from "Merrie England" as a counter to urbanisation. Her younger sister Helen soon started helping at the Guild of Play. Around this time Maud joined the Fabian Society. The social-work commitment ran about five years, 1907-12. The Mansfield House Settlement Annual Report for 1911 commended the Guild of Play work; the 1912 Report said the Folk Song and Dance Club had "a quietly successful year under Miss Karpeles with its annual display", but also that the Guild of Play "faces difficulties as (Miss Karpeles) is compelled by pressure of other work to give up the leadership at Xmas" -- she resigned the Guild leadership at Christmas 1912 as Sharp's secretarial work took over. Wachsmann's obituary stresses a point Frank Howes omitted: she discovered folk dance "in a practical way at a time when she was searching for material in her social work in London's East End" -- the dance entered her life as settlement-work material first, aesthetic object second, and "she never changed her opinion" that it was ideal for that work. She later wrote of the material she had been using then that it was "probably somewhat mediocre, but it was the best I knew at that time". The sequence matters: she was already teaching dance to children who had nothing, already using her piano as a teaching instrument, already organising weekly classes, before she ever heard the word "morris". Sharp did not teach her to teach. He hired a teacher who already knew how, and gave her better material to teach with.

**Takeaway:** folk dance reached her as a tool for poor children in Canning Town, not as art -- and the five years of settlement work are the reason she could teach five hours a day on Sharp's staff two years later. [C34846]

## 3. May 1909: Stratford, Sharp, and the instant conversion

In May 1909 Maud and Helen Karpeles went to the Shakespeare Festival in Stratford-upon-Avon, where folk-dancing competitions were being held, and met Cecil Sharp, who was an adjudicator there together with Mary Neal. The sisters were "instantly entranced" by the style of folk dancing they saw. From September 1909 they audited Sharp's classes at the School of Morris Dancing at the Chelsea Polytechnic Institute -- Cecil Sharp's People says the South Western Polytechnic evening classes that autumn -- and their first objective was to be able to teach the dances to the Canning Town settlement children. The meeting is the founding moment of her folk career, and it is worth pausing on who else was in the room: Mary Neal, Sharp's first collaborator, was adjudicating beside him. Neal had worked with Sharp and the Esperance Club teaching morris to London dressmaking girls, and by 1909-10 Sharp had distanced himself from her -- she had moved towards women's suffrage, and the two had split publicly. Karpeles is, in effect, Neal's successor in Sharp's work, and the succession happened in a single afternoon at Stratford: the woman who would replace Neal as Sharp's working partner met him while Neal was standing next to him. On Whit Monday, 16 May 1910, Maud and Helen accompanied Sharp to watch the Bampton morris dancers; afterwards Sharp wrote to Maud that he was keen "to rope you and your sister into my staff of teachers" (letter MK/3/1). They were both good dancers. In 1910 the Karpeles sisters formed an informal Folk Dance Club with a group of girls who had been practising every week at their parents' house; it produced a women's folk-dance team. They tried out new dances Sharp was recording, including sword dances from Grenoside and Flamborough (August and December 1910). The speed of the conversion is the story: in two years she moves from audience to staff, from a settlement volunteer looking for material to a member of Sharp's teaching team.

**Takeaway:** the whole career turns on one afternoon at Stratford -- and the person standing next to Sharp that afternoon, Mary Neal, is the collaborator Karpeles was about to replace. [C34847]

## 4. The Folk Dance Club and five hours a day

On 3 April 1911 the Folk Dance Club held a fundraiser for the Invalid Children's Aid Association at the Portman Rooms, Baker Street. The women's team danced, contralto Mattie Kay sang, Sharp lectured; an audience of 500, male relatives roped in to dance, and 60 pounds raised -- "a huge sum at the time" (Westminster Gazette notice, 29 March 1911). Also in 1911 Maud took some of her Guild of Play children to Stratford to perform, and some older dancers to Retford, Nottinghamshire, in July; Sharp called them her "Canning Town Contingent". The Club's popularity grew fast; Maud and Helen were soon teaching morris, sword and country dancing classes five hours a day as members of Sharp's teaching staff, and giving public demonstrations. At the Shakespeare Festival in summer 1911 the Folk Dance Club gave performances each week in the Memorial Theatre Gardens. The five-hours-a-day figure is the one to hold onto: this was not a hobby taken up in spare time but a full working day of physical teaching, repeated daily, by two sisters in their twenties, in the years before either of them had a salary for it. The demonstration work is also the first appearance of the mechanism that would carry her whole career: the body as the textbook. She did not write about the dances first; she danced them, in front of people, until they could copy her, and the copying was the transmission. The 1912 Kinora home-movie films -- the earliest known film of English folk dance -- show Sharp, Maud and Helen Karpeles and George Butterworth dancing together, and the EFDSS later issued them as a "flicker book" of trading cards. The film is the evidence that the teaching was embodied, public, and recorded on the newest medium of the day, all at once.

**Takeaway:** five hours a day of teaching, plus fundraising, plus demonstration -- the Club was a full-time unpaid job before the Society existed, and the body doing the dance was the notation. [C34848]

## 5. The EFDS founding, December 1911

Following a public meeting in December 1911, the Folk Dance Club dissolved to make way for the English Folk Dance Society (EFDS). The founding resolution was moved by Sharp on Wednesday 6 December 1911 at St Andrew's Hall, Newman Street. Maud joined the EFDS committee at its formation but did not initially take a bigger role, perhaps because of her Mansfield House commitments. Helen became the EFDS Honorary Secretary at the founding; Maud took over the role from Helen -- dated 1922-1930 by Wikipedia and 1921 by Cecil Sharp's People, a conflict carried unresolved, with the 1930 end date Wikipedia-only and the 1928 resignation (see section 22) the better-dated fact. The co-founders alongside Sharp and the Karpeles sisters were Vaughan Williams, Butterworth, Perceval Lucas and G. J. Wilkinson. The founding matters for what it makes visible about Karpeles's position: she is twenty-six, a committee member of the society she helped to fill, but not yet its secretary, not yet its amanuensis, not yet the person Sharp depends on. The household, the shorthand, the fieldwork, the executor's fifty years -- all of that is still ahead. What the founding does establish is the network she will spend her life inside: Sharp at the centre, Vaughan Williams as the senior ally, Butterworth as the exact contemporary (both born 1885), Helen as the sister-colleague, and Douglas Kennedy -- who married Helen on 29 September 1914 -- as the brother-in-law who will become both her boss and her antagonist. The EFDS is also the first institution she helped to build rather than merely join, and the pattern repeats: the 1932 merger into the EFDSS, the 1935 festival, the 1947 council. She builds institutions the way Sharp built books -- by doing the organising work nobody else will do, for years, unpaid.

**Takeaway:** at twenty-six she is a founder but not yet a leader -- the committee seat is the first rung, and the network assembled in that room is the one she will work inside for the next sixty-five years. [C34849]

## 6. The amanuensis: neuritis and the prosthetic hand

In 1913 Maud began working for Sharp as his amanuensis after he developed neuritis in his right elbow and could not write. She first took down his letters in longhand, then quickly learned typewriting and shorthand. The mechanism is the heart of the study, so it is worth stating plainly: she acquired the exact skills his body could no longer perform, and became a living extension of his hand. During 1913, according to her autobiography (MK/7/185, p. 45), Maud travelled daily from her Paddington home out to Uxbridge to act as Sharp's secretary; Sharp had moved his family there in May 1911 because of his daughter Dorothea's ill-health. Sharp's forms of address to her trace the relationship: "Dear Miss Karpeles" in mid-1911, "Dear Maud" later, "My dear little Maudie" by 1913. Her mother Emily had been ill for some time, in a nursing home in Devonshire Terrace, Marylebone (1911 census); she died in February 1914, after which Maud and Helen moved into a flat in Gloucester Road. The amanuensis role widened until she could stand in for him bodily: in May 1924, en route to Newcastle to adjudicate a three-day competition, Sharp became so ill that Karpeles had to take his place. Sharp called her "the faithful Maud" and, on their travels, introduced her as his "adopted daughter". The prosthetic-hand reading is not a metaphor imposed on the sources; it is what the sources describe. A man loses the use of his writing hand; a younger woman learns shorthand and typing to replace it; eleven years later she is adjudicating in his place because his body has failed again. The work continued because the hand changed. And the hand that replaced his was not a machine but a person who had already spent five years teaching dance to children in Canning Town -- someone who understood, before Sharp hired her, that knowledge lives in bodies and is passed on by doing.

**Takeaway:** neuritis took Sharp's right hand in 1913, and Karpeles became it -- first his letters, then his field notes, then, in May 1924, his adjudicating body itself. [C34850]

## 7. The household: Hampstead, Constance's illness, "adopted daughter"

Karpeles lived with the Sharp family when they moved to Hampstead in 1915, not long after Sharp's wife Constance suffered a life-changing illness. No source read names the illness or gives it a date beyond that, and no source records a direct Karpeles-Constance conversation or letter -- the tie is evidenced through co-residence and Sharp's own framing of Karpeles as an "adopted daughter" of the household, and it must not be inflated into a friendship. But the domestic context matters for the work: the amanuensis role became a live-in one because the household had been reshaped by illness, and Karpeles moved into it as a working member, not a guest. Constance herself contributed folk material -- Sharp's *Folk Tunes* 2708 records a tune "Sung to me by CDS [Constance Dorothea Sharp] as sung by Charles Poole of Sinwell, Wotton-under-Edge in 1882" -- so the household was itself a small collecting circle. Sharp's introduction of Karpeles on their travels as his "adopted daughter" is also in Cecil Sharp's People, tied to the 1916 Appalachian trip, when singers asked about their relationship. The phrase did social work: it explained, to mountain singers in 1916 and to everyone else afterwards, what a thirty-year-old unmarried woman was doing travelling with a fifty-seven-year-old married man. It was also, by every account, true in the sense that mattered -- she was faithful, she was present, and she stayed. Maud Karpeles never married. She worked closely with Sharp until his death -- fifteen years from the May 1909 meeting, eleven as his amanuensis and secretary -- and then spent fifty-two years as his executor. The household is the unit of the career: not a job, not a collaboration, but a shared roof, a shared archive, and a shared road.

**Takeaway:** the amanuensis became a housemate in 1915 because Constance fell ill -- and "adopted daughter" was both the explanation offered to strangers and the truth of the working relationship. [C34851]

## 8. Savoy 1914: folk dance on the West End stage

In May 1914 Maud was involved in Harley Granville-Barker's production of *A Midsummer Night's Dream* at the Savoy Theatre: the usual Mendelssohn music in Act 4 was replaced by folk music and dancing arranged by Sharp; Maud trained the dancers. It is the one stage production in her record, and it matters because it shows the revival reaching the West End through her teaching body: Sharp arranged the music and choreography, but Karpeles trained the dancers, which means the folk material reached a commercial theatre audience only because she could get non-folk dancers to move like folk dancers in rehearsal time. When war broke out in August 1914, Sharp -- too old for war service -- accepted Granville-Barker's invitation to take the production to New York. Sharp sailed in December 1914; Maud did not accompany him on this first trip. He returned home in April 1915, having found that American audiences were more interested in folk dance than folk song, and several EFDS branches were formed in the US (New York, Boston, Chicago, Pittsburgh), which became the Country Dance and Song Society of America. The Savoy episode is a hinge between the teaching years and the American years: the dance she had taught five hours a day in London was now a stage commodity in New York, and the American interest in dance over song is the first hint of the Appalachian project, where the dance would turn out to matter as much as the song. What Karpeles did in England between Sharp's sailing in December 1914 and her own sailing in June 1915 is not described in sources read, beyond the flat move after her mother's death in February 1914 -- a six-month gap in the record, carried honestly.

**Takeaway:** the Savoy put her teaching on a West End stage, and the New York tour that followed showed Sharp that America wanted the dance more than the song -- the first hint of the Appalachian turn. [C34852]

## 9. America 1915: the summer school and Olive Campbell

Maud sailed to the USA in June 1915 to join Sharp on his second trip and help him run a summer school at Eliot, Maine (60 students). She was one of three British teachers assisting him. During this trip Sharp met Olive Dame Campbell, who shared her collection of 200 ballads collected in the Appalachian Mountains -- the meeting that inspired Sharp's own Appalachian expedition. The Campbell meeting is usually told as Sharp's story; Karpeles's part in it is narrower and must not be inflated. She was in the same party -- one of the three British teachers at Eliot -- but no source read records a Karpeles-Campbell conversation in 1915, and no source gives the date or place of their first personal meeting. The documented tie is editorial: Campbell and Sharp jointly published *English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians* in 1917, and Karpeles edited the 1932 two-volume edition, which includes thirty-nine tunes contributed by Campbell. The two women are therefore linked through Sharp and through the book, not through a dated friendship. What the 1915 summer school does establish is the pattern of the next three years: Sharp and Karpeles travelling together, teaching together, and collecting together, with Karpeles as the working partner who makes the expedition function. A photograph from Eliot shows Sharp at the piano and Karpeles preparing to dance a Morris jig -- the division of labour in one frame, him at the instrument, her about to move. Sharp sailed again in February 1916 on his third trip; Maud followed in April 1916. Song collecting in North Carolina began at the end of July 1916. They returned to England in December 1916, having collected nearly 400 songs. The expedition that followed is the centre of her life's work, and it is described in the next three sections.

**Takeaway:** the 1915 summer school put her in the room where Olive Campbell's 200 ballads set the Appalachian project in motion -- but her tie to Campbell runs through the book, not through a friendship the sources can date. [C34853]

## 10. The Appalachian method: centre and walking radius

The Appalachian method was a hub-and-spoke walk, not a tour. Sharp and Karpeles made a Presbyterian missionary settlement their centre for a week or ten days, then visited singers who lived "within a walking radius" -- White Rock, Allanstand, Alleghany and Carmen, Big Laurel and Hot Springs in North Carolina, Rocky Fork in Tennessee, Charlottesville in Virginia, in turn. From July to September 1916 they spent nine weeks in Madison County, North Carolina, accompanied at first by John C. Campbell, who introduced them to key contacts and singers including Edith Fish, a teacher at a Presbyterian mission school who was a song collector herself. The journeys were arduous: long hours of walking in extremes of weather, Sharp in his late fifties, Karpeles in her early thirties. Local knowledge was routed through a guide: John C. Campbell introduced them to singers, and afterwards "directed our journeyings". The method has a precise logic that Sharp stated in the 1917 introduction and that Karpeles repeated for the rest of her life: the settlement is a base, the walk is the sampling, and the walking pace is what lets you hear a singer by the roadside instead of driving past. Between 1916 and 1918 they visited 281 singers, often in remote and inaccessible locations, over 46 weeks in the mountains. Cecil Sharp's People gives the total US time as 52 weeks travelling through the Appalachian Mountain states -- against 46 weeks for the collecting proper -- and the Musical Traditions review also says "fifty-two weeks of three years". The discrepancy is unresolved in the sources read; possibly 46 is collecting weeks and 52 is total Appalachian travel including the 1915 summer school, but no source explains it, and both figures are carried. They also "discovered" the country dance now called the "running set" in Kentucky (see section 12). On Armistice Day, 11 November 1918, Karpeles and Sharp were in Cleveland, Ohio. One month later they had cancelled all further speaking engagements and headed home to England.

**Takeaway:** a settlement for a week or ten days, then walking out to the singers -- the hub holds, the spokes are walked, and the slow pace is the sampling instrument. [C34854]

## 11. Two-channel capture: her words, his tunes

In the field, one song was captured by two people at once, each taking a different channel. Karpeles took down the words, "usually in shorthand"; Sharp noted the tune. Sharp's 1917 introduction states it verbatim, and every secondary source repeats it. The archival trace is exact: her typed-out texts survive in the "Folk Words" section of Sharp's archive at the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library (FW2301-3319), cross-referenced against Sharp's tune books -- Sharp's FT3168 against Karpeles's FW2307, both from the singer Granny Banks of White Rock. She carried a portable typewriter on the trips, so shorthand taken live in the field was typed up into a parallel, matching record. The division of labour is the mechanism to hold onto, because it defines her whole career: she was the words, he was the tunes, and the archive is a duet. Two qualifications matter. First, she did not take the tunes in Appalachia -- the division is explicit and repeated, and crediting her with tune-noting in 1916-18 contradicts the sources. She learned tune-noting later, by years of practice, and used it solo in Newfoundland from 1929. Second, there is no evidence Karpeles or Sharp used a phonograph or any recording machine in the mountains. The 1916-18 collecting was by ear and hand only -- shorthand and staff notation. Tape recording enters her method only in 1950, with the Library of Congress Eicor recorder, and in 1955. Do not show a wax cylinder or a microphone in an Appalachian scene; the technology of the expedition was a notebook, a pencil, a shorthand pad, and a portable typewriter. The typed texts, FW2301-3319, are the visible product: two parallel records, cross-referenced, filed together, usable a century later because the two channels were kept in step at the point of capture.

**Takeaway:** one song, two recorders, two channels -- her shorthand words and his notated tunes, typed up on a portable typewriter and cross-referenced number to number. [C34855]

## 12. The running set: Pine Mountain, 31 August 1917

Sharp and Karpeles saw the Kentucky running set at Pine Mountain Settlement School on 31 August 1917 -- one dim lantern, moonlight, whirling dancers, stamping and clapping, the caller's falsetto -- and Sharp theorised it was an older form of English country dance. Maud was co-author with Sharp of *Country Dance Book Part V* (Novello, 1918), which contained the Running Set, with dances of 236 and 468 bars. The seeing and noting are evidenced; the interpretation is not settled. Modern scholarship (Smith, drawing on Phil Jamison) judges Sharp's conclusion incorrect -- an Anglicisation of an American dance, with other possible origins unconsidered. What is evidenced is that they saw, noted and published it. The running set matters for the study because it is the one Appalachian dance in her record, and because it shows the Sharpean method at its most characteristic: see a living practice, note it exactly, and then fit it into a theory about English origins that the noting itself does not prove. Karpeles never wavered from that theory in print, and the 1918 book carries both the notation and the claim together. A piece built on this mechanism should stage the seeing and the noting -- the lantern light, the whirling figures, the long figures counted out in bars -- and carry the "ancient English survival" reading as Sharp's contested interpretation, not as fact. The same caution applies to the wider claim that Appalachian song proved English survival: her own 1950 evidence cuts against the purity doctrine in ways section 29 describes.

**Takeaway:** they saw the running set by lantern light, noted it in figures of 236 and 468 bars, and published it as an ancient English survival -- the noting is fact, the survival is Sharp's contested theory. [C34856]

## 13. The authenticity filter: what she refused to write down

Karpeles did not collect everything she heard. For a song to be noted it had to be old, traditional, and an authentic folksong by Sharpean criteria. She rejected sentimental songs and drawing-room ballads, anything touched by commercial or "polite" taste, locally composed songs of recent origin, and songs previously appearing in print. The same filter ran in Appalachia and in Newfoundland, and it was a doctrine, not a habit: she "never wavered from the original idea of the essential purity of folk song, free from commercialisation or vulgarity". The filter is the most consequential mechanism in her practice, because it determines what the archive contains -- and what it does not. The archive is not everything that arrived; it is everything that passed. In Newfoundland the criteria were age, anonymity, style and oral transmission; ancient songs with British roots were especially valued, and locally composed recent songs and printed songs were "generally not notated at all". The cost of the filter is visible in the Newfoundland record: more than 50 of her notated tunes remain unpublished, the "lost voices", and the published 30 songs of 1934 are a fraction of the nearly 200 she collected. The filter also has a colonial edge that modern scholarship names directly: in Newfoundland she sought only songs of British origin, a colonialist framing, and has been called "a controversial figure" for it. Anna Guigne's reconsideration stresses her stamina and determination as an adventurer in a challenging environment, and the 52 songs from the remote south coast -- but the framing stands. A piece built on the filter must show the gate working, and the rejected shapes piling up outside it before fading, so the cost is on screen, not hidden. And the filter must be staged as her position, not as a verified fact about the songs: her companion on the 1950 return, Sidney Robertson Cowell, held the opposite view -- a dynamic tradition -- and her own 1950 report records the tradition surviving by the very means she distrusted.

**Takeaway:** the gate tests every song -- old, anonymous, orally carried passes; sentimental, printed, newly made is turned away -- and the archive is what passed, not what arrived. [C34857]

## 14. The numbers: 281 singers, 1,612 tunes, 500 songs

The yield of the Appalachian expeditions is given, in Wikipedia and in Wachsmann's obituary, as 1,612 tunes representing some 500 different songs, from 281 singers, over 46 weeks. The numbers are the standard account, and they are the ones in the #801 brief. They are also disputed on their own terms. Sharp's own diary totals differ: 400 tunes in 1916, 600 in 1917, 625 in 1918, which adds to 1,625 -- thirteen more than 1,612. The 1,612 figure is the one in both general sources and the brief; the 1,625 is Sharp's diary arithmetic, as reported in the #801 study. The difference of thirteen is carried unresolved. A third figure circulates in *Folk Song America*: "nearly 1,700 songs and tunes". The 1932 edition is catalogued as 274 songs and 968 tunes, but the Memorial University scan of volume two reads "two hundred and seventy-three songs" -- the one-song discrepancy is carried unresolved in the #801 log and again here, and no reconciliation is invented. The Newfoundland totals are no cleaner: 197 songs from about 115 singers across 46 communities in fourteen weeks (Memorial University Press, *Fourteen Fleeting Weeks*, the most recent scholarly count), against "more than 200 songs from residents of 40 different communities" (Memorial University news release) and 230 songs and variants (one Free Library article). The study gives the range and names its source rather than a single flat number. What the numbers establish, whatever the exact count, is scale: 281 named singers, visited on foot, over three seasons, with every song taken down twice -- once as words, once as tune -- and filed so that a singer's words and a singer's tune can still be matched a century later. The most prolific single singer, Jane Hicks Gentry of Madison County, supplied sixty-four songs -- more than any other single contact -- and the single best-documented day of fieldwork in the sources read is 4 September 1916 at Flag Pond, Tennessee, where Jeff Stockton gave a crop of Child ballads between 10am and 3.30pm after a referral chain through the Hensley, Norton, Rice and Shelton families. The numbers are not the point; the named, dated, timed encounters are.

**Takeaway:** 1,612 tunes or 1,625, 273 songs or 274 -- the totals disagree by one and by thirteen, and the study carries both figures rather than inventing a reconciliation. [C34858]

## 15. Sharp's last tour: Newcastle, May 1924

From 1919 Karpeles and Sharp were busy reviving the EFDS; they travelled extensively, resuming pre-war activities and doing fieldwork whenever they could. In September 1922 Karpeles and Sharp collected morris dances and folk songs across 45 villages in the Midlands. In 1923 the EFDS was reorganised on a county basis, with 43 branches. By 1924 London classes had reached 3,000 attendances weekly, over 3,800 certificates of merit had been awarded, there were 23 provincial EFDS branches to support, and vacation schools at Stratford in summer and the South Western Polytechnic at Christmas, plus Easter schools at York, Manchester and Aldeburgh, all to organise. Sharp continued to suffer from asthma, bronchitis and fevers, and relied increasingly on Karpeles to get through his engagements. In October 1923, when Sharp's health deteriorated, Maud arranged to accompany him to Montreux, Switzerland, where her older sister Lucy lived; he benefited from medical advice in two months there. In May 1924 Karpeles travelled with Sharp through Torquay, Sheffield, Cardiff, Newport, Bath, Birmingham, Lincoln, Norwich and Ilkley. En route to Newcastle to adjudicate a three-day competition, Sharp became so ill that Karpeles had to take his place; on the trip back to London he suffered a seizure; by the third week of June he was only semiconscious, and died peacefully on Monday 23 June 1924. She was thirty-eight and had spent eleven years as his working right hand. In her final year she wrote: "Anything I may have achieved is entirely due to my association with Cecil Sharp. The happiest and most fruitful years of my life were those when I was able to help him in his work." The sentence is quoted in the 1950-trip article and the #801 study; its full text rests on a single chain of sourcing through the autobiography, not independently verified against the typescript, and it is carried as such. It is also, on the evidence of the fifty-two years that followed, an understatement: the happiest years were the ones with Sharp, but the most consequential were the ones after him, when she kept his work alive and built her own institutions on top of it.

**Takeaway:** the last tour ends with her adjudicating in his place at Newcastle and him dying on the journey home -- the prosthetic hand taking over the work completely, eleven years after it was first lent. [C34859]

## 16. Executor: fifty-two years of stewardship

Karpeles became Sharp's literary executor after his death and fought legal battles on behalf of his estate concerning the legacy of his collections. She later came into conflict with the EFDSS over copyright; critics held that her assertion of copyright was ironic, given that the songs had been shared freely by individuals. Wikipedia asserts both the legal battles and the copyright conflict, but no case, date, opponent or outcome is given in any source read, and the substance and dates of the copyright battles are unknown -- a gap carried honestly, not filled. What is documented is the editorial output, and it is the largest single body of work in her life: the enlarged second edition of *English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians* (1932), the biography (1933, second edition 1955, revised alone 1967), material added to the second (1936), third (1954) and fourth (1965) editions of Sharp's *English Folk Song: Some Conclusions*, *Eighty English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians* (1968, with piano accompaniments by Benjamin Britten), *Cecil Sharp's Collection of English Folk Songs* (1974, two volumes), and *The Crystal Spring* (1975). The stewardship also had a humane side she is credited with throughout: helping refugee musicians and the Red Cross in the Second World War, and visiting the Tristan da Cunha evacuees in 1962. The executor's method was administrative and legal as much as scholarly -- she fought for the estate, asserted copyright, edited and re-edited his books through four editions of *Some Conclusions* and three editions of the biography, and organised the institutions that carried the work. The flame is the image the mechanism asks for: a life's work handed from a dying hand to a younger one, and the younger hand carrying it for fifty-two years, feeding it with new editions, shielding it with a legal fence, and carrying it into new rooms. The carrier ages visibly across the piece while the flame stays the same size. Keeping something alive is itself a technique -- tending, not just making.

**Takeaway:** fifty-two years of stewardship -- editing, re-editing, defending the estate in law, and asserting a copyright critics called ironic, since the songs had been shared freely. [C34860]

## 17. The 1932 edition: enlarging a dead man's book

After Sharp's death in 1924, Karpeles rebuilt his Appalachian collection from his manuscripts into a book far larger than the one he had published in his lifetime. The 1917 volume, by Campbell and Sharp jointly, held 122 songs and ballads with 323 tunes; the 1932 edition Karpeles edited (Oxford University Press, two volumes) held 274 songs and ballads with 968 tunes, including 39 tunes contributed by Olive Dame Campbell -- "second and enlarged edition", "carefully edited from Sharp's manuscripts by Maud Karpeles". The enlargement is the mechanism: a small book of 122 marks sits on a table, and over years -- shown as slow seasons passing -- it grows, new pages interleaved, the count visibly rising toward 274 songs and 968 tunes, each addition in the editor's hand rather than the author's. The author's original pages stay untouched at the core; the editor's pages wrap around them. The 1932 title is also the work the brief mislabels: the brief calls it "Cecil Sharp's Collection of English Folk Songs (1932, 2 vols, ed. Karpeles)", but that title belongs to the different, later work of 1974 (see section 18 and leg 2). The 1932 edition was reprinted in 1952, 1960, 1966 and 1973 (two volumes in one). What Karpeles changed in the 1932 edition beyond enlargement -- which songs were added, which tunes corrected, whether any 1917 readings were altered -- was not established by this run: the 1932 volumes were not read cover to cover, only title pages, contents and catalogue records, and the gap is carried. The same is true of what material she added to the second, third and fourth editions of *Some Conclusions*: bibliographic records confirm she added material; the content of the additions was not read. The edition-building is evidenced as a fact of scale; its interior is not.

**Takeaway:** 122 songs and 323 tunes in 1917 became 274 songs and 968 tunes in 1932 -- the dead man's book more than doubled, in the editor's hand, from his manuscripts. [C34861]

## 18. The biography, the Collection, the Crystal Spring: the late editions

With A. H. Fox Strangways she co-wrote the biography *Cecil Sharp* (Oxford University Press, 1933; second edition 1955); Karpeles rewrote it alone for the third edition as *Cecil Sharp: His Life and Work* (Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1967) -- the capstone of her fifty-year stewardship, and the book the brief conflates with the 1933 title. In 1974 she published the two substantial volumes *Cecil Sharp's Collection of English Folk Songs* (Oxford University Press): volume one, 751 pages, 188 songs with tunes; volume two, 661 pages, 225 songs with tunes; 413 songs total, melody only, unaccompanied -- about two-thirds of the songs and tunes Sharp collected in England in the early 1900s, in (mostly) original form, organised by subject (ballads, lovers' laments, false-hearted lovers, love and courtship, sailors' farewell, murderers and robbers and highwaymen, sea songs, country pursuits, carols, humorous, cumulative, nonsense and nursery, various). In 1975 came *The Crystal Spring*, a 143-song selection from the 1974 volumes with guitar chords by Pat Shaw -- not 1926, and with no Douglas Kennedy involvement, despite the brief. The late editions are criticised as well as used: Vic Gammon, reviewing *Still Growing* (2003), called Karpeles's edition "unsatisfactory" and called for a complete edition of the Sharp collection on the Greig and Duncan model, and Folkopedia notes the transcriptions are "not invariably accurately or completely transcribed". At least one scholarly assessment quoted in the Sharp literature calls the 1974 edition "poorly edited, is selective, and... less than totally reliable" -- a judgement this run did not verify independently, so it is carried as a qualification, not a finding. The edition was nevertheless the standard source for Sharp's English songs for thirty years. Her late editing should not be presented as uniformly authoritative; the stewardship was real, and so were its limits.

**Takeaway:** the 1974 *Collection* -- 413 songs in two volumes -- was the standard source for Sharp's English songs for thirty years, and it is also the edition scholars call selective and not invariably accurate. [C34862]

## 19. Newfoundland: prospecting, then solo capture

Sharp had, just before his death, expressed a wish to search for songs in Newfoundland: his theory predicted that emigrants from Scotland and England would have brought folk songs with them that would still be found there. From 1929 to 1930 Karpeles took up the challenge and spent around fourteen weeks collecting songs there. The trip is the hinge of her career, because it is the first time she had to do the half of the job she had never done: noting tunes by ear. Sources say she worried about her ability to note tunes and "with characteristic doggedness she practiced for several years" before, by 1929, she felt expert enough to attempt Newfoundland alone. The trip itself was Sharp's unfulfilled plan -- twice planned with him, twice prevented, first by money, then by his health -- and she went to keep a promise to carry on his work. Her method there was her own: she called the first phase "prospecting" (a vacation in Canada first, testing the ground), then two expeditions in 1929 and 1930, travelling by foot, rowboat, automobile, coastal steamer and train, equipped with a typewriter, notebooks for shorthand, and a camera. Fourteen weeks, 46 communities in eight regions, 197 songs from about 115 singers (some sources say 200+ tunes from 100+ singers, in 40 communities). She kept detailed field diaries and photographed singers and places; she returned to visit several 1929 informants socially in 1930. She travelled alone much of the time and was mistaken for a nurse or a German spy. Frederick Emerson, a lawyer, musician and folklore enthusiast, assisted her as the local fixer -- the parallel to John C. Campbell in Appalachia. In 1929 she and dancer and musician William Kimber laid the foundation stone for Cecil Sharp House (the building was built in 1929 but opened on 7 June 1930). She published 30 of the Newfoundland songs in *Folk Songs from Newfoundland* (1934), a two-volume piano edition with arrangements by Ralph Vaughan Williams, Clive Carey, Hubert J. Foss and Michael Mullinar; the main body of the collection was published only decades later (Faber, 1971, close to 100 songs and ballads, many in multiple variants). More than 50 of her notated tunes remain unpublished -- the "lost voices". Her field diaries, transcriptions and correspondence are the Maud Karpeles Fonds at Memorial University's Folklore and Language Archive, donated by Ursula Vaughan Williams in 1978.

**Takeaway:** she practised tune-noting for years to do alone what she had done as half of a pair -- then prospected, then captured, on foot and by rowboat, with a typewriter, shorthand notebooks and a camera. [C34863]

## 20. "She's Like the Swallow": the adapted text

What Karpeles published was not always what she had noted. The clearest documented case is "She's Like the Swallow", collected on 8 July 1930 by dictation from John Hunt at Dunville, Placentia Bay: three four-line verses, one five-liner, closing with a two-line verse. In the 1934 *Folk Songs from Newfoundland* she printed not that text but a "Text Adapted for Singing": stanza three "slightly amended" for grammar and diction, and the repetition of stanza one given in place of the last "corrupt and incomplete" lines -- the repeated first stanza thereby becoming a chorus. She later said her life would have been worthwhile if collecting that song was all she had done. The same editorial hand is visible across her career: she added material to the second, third and fourth editions of Sharp's *English Folk-Song: Some Conclusions* while holding his doctrine intact. The mechanism is the moment a record becomes a performance text: a raw field text appears -- uneven, with a broken last stanza -- and an editor's hand in a different colour touches it: one line is smoothed, the corrupt ending is lifted away, and the first stanza is copied down to the end and marked as a refrain. The amended lines glow briefly as they are altered, the new chorus locks into place, and the original field version remains faintly visible underneath, like a palimpsest. Two qualifications must travel with the song. First, the version the world knows is her adaptation, not the field text, and the song was collected, not discovered as an ancient Newfoundland song: only five other texts from four other singers have ever been reported, all from Newfoundland oral tradition. Second, the published text must not be presented as the field text, and the song must not be presented as ancient -- its rarity in oral tradition is part of the story.

**Takeaway:** the famous text is her adaptation, not the field text -- a smoothed stanza, a corrupt ending lifted away, and the first stanza copied down to become a chorus. [C34864]

## 21. The dance books: Lancashire, country dances, airs

Her own dance publications run through the 1930s, 1940s and 1950s, and they are the record of the second half of her fieldwork -- the dances, where the songs had been Sharp's. *The Lancashire Morris Dance* (Novello, 1930, tunes arranged for pianoforte by Arnold Foster) describes the Royton Morris Dance, the North-West morris danced in clogs, collected from teams in the Royton and Oldham area, the McDermotts and Colemans pre-eminent among them; a companion volume of tunes followed the same year. "The Abram Morris Dance" appeared in the Journal of the English Folk Dance and Song Society in 1932, with "Some Additional Figures for Set-Running" (1930) and "Square-Dance Figures from Northern New York State" (1931). *Twelve Traditional Country Dances* (Novello for the EFDS, piano arrangements by Vaughan Williams) is dated 1931 in some catalogues and 1940 in others, and reissued in 1956 -- the date is split and not asserted here. *Dances of England & Wales* (Max Parrish, 1950), with Lois Blake and illustrations by Roland A. Beard, is a handbook of English and Welsh dances in the "Handbooks of European National Dances" series. *A Selection of 100 English Folk Dance Airs for Melodic Instruments* (EFDSS, 1951), with Kenworthy Schofield, is the standard tune reference for the revival, still cited tune by tune. The clog-dancing literature judges her "more effective" than Sharp at documenting North-West morris -- a small, pointed verdict, and one of the few places where a source credits her with doing something better than he did. The dance books are also where her teaching mechanism is most visible: the dances were noted on paper, but they were taught by demonstration, five hours a day, and the paper is the residue of the body, not a score written first.

**Takeaway:** the dance books -- Royton and Abram morris, twelve country dances, a hundred airs -- are the paper residue of a teaching body, and the clog-dancing literature judges her more effective than Sharp at documenting the North-West dances. [C34865]

## 22. The Kennedy conflict: resignation 1928

After Sharp's death Karpeles served with Vaughan Williams as adviser to Douglas Kennedy, the newly appointed director of the EFDS -- Kennedy had married her sister Helen on 29 September 1914, soon after he enlisted in the London Scottish Regiment, and succeeded Sharp as director in 1924-25, leading the merged EFDSS until his retirement in 1961. The two were "often at loggerheads": he "violently disagreed" with her democratic and internationalist line, banned women from summer schools unless they brought a male partner, and she resigned as EFDS secretary in 1928, "citing difficulties in distinguishing the functions of the roles of secretary and director". In 1938 he manoeuvred her aside from the EFDSS over women dancing men's morris dances and Sharp copyright issues. The conflict is the central institutional fact of her middle years, and it is what pushed her outward: the national organiser, blocked at home, became an internationalist. Kennedy later credited Karpeles with ensuring the EFDSS would survive in the early 1930s -- the antagonist's own verdict, and the more credible for coming from him. The family dimension must not be separated from the professional one: Helen is both sister and colleague, Douglas is both brother-in-law and boss and antagonist, and their son Peter Kennedy (born 1922) became a folklorist and BBC folk-song recordist -- the network reproducing itself inside the family. The folk revival, for Karpeles, was a family business, and the family business had a quarrel at its centre. The EFDS secretary dates carry the tension: Wikipedia gives Maud's tenure as 1922-1930, but the 1928 resignation is the better-dated fact and the 1930 end date may reflect a formal handover.

**Takeaway:** the brother-in-law who succeeded Sharp blocked her at home -- resignation in 1928, manoeuvred aside in 1938 -- and the block is what pushed her outward to the international work. [C34866]

## 23. The 1932 merger: two societies become one

The EFDS merged with the Folk-Song Society in 1932 to form the English Folk Dance and Song Society (EFDSS). The EFDSS elected Karpeles to its Board of Artistic Control in 1932, together with Douglas Kennedy and Ralph Vaughan Williams. The Dunin ethnochoreology article states the merger came about "through initiatives of Maud Karpeles" -- single-sourced in that article, and carried as such. The merger matters because it reunites the two halves of the movement she had lived in separately: the dance side she entered through Sharp in 1909, and the song side that belonged to Broadwood's generation and the Folk-Song Society founded in 1898. Lucy Broadwood, the Folk-Song Society's founder member, secretary and Journal editor, has no documented tie to Karpeles at all -- no meeting, no correspondence, no joint work in any source read -- and the two societies did not merge until 1932, three years after Broadwood's death. The merger is therefore not a reconciliation of two people but of two institutional halves, and Karpeles is the person who stood in both: Sharp's dance teacher and Sharp's song editor, the amanuensis who took the words and the secretary who ran the society. Kennedy's credit -- that she ensured the EFDSS would survive during this period -- belongs here: the merged society was fragile, and the person who had been manoeuvred aside was also the person who kept it alive. The merger also sets up the international turn: a single national society, with a board of three, is the platform from which a festival of eighteen nations can be organised three years later.

**Takeaway:** the 1932 merger reunited the dance side she entered through Sharp with the song side she inherited through his archive -- and she is the person who stood in both. [C34867]

## 24. The 1935 festival: eighteen nations

In 1935 she organised the International Folk Dance Festival and Conference in London, promoted by the EFDSS. Eighteen nations took part; Wachsmann: "Miss Karpeles was the soul and spirit and skillful organizer that made this international gathering in 1935 such an inspiring success." The experience convinced her a permanent international council was desirable and feasible. In 1936 she travelled to Yugoslavia to watch dances; the team had been unable to attend the 1935 festival due to costs. Returning delegates from 1935 formed an informal body, the International (Advisory) Folk Dance Council; Karpeles was its Honorary Secretary. The festival is the hinge between the national and the international phases, and its mechanism is the one she had practised since Canning Town: get people into a room and let them watch each other do it. The difference is scale -- from a Guild of Play room to a London conference with eighteen national teams -- and the difference is that the watching is now comparative by design. At Bayonne in 1927 she had met the Moravian anthropologist Frantisek Pospisil, who was filming Basque sword dances as part of a comparative study of sword dances -- an encounter the scholarship credits with shaping her next steps, including the 1932 merger. The comparative eye was trained early, and the 1935 festival was its first large-scale test: eighteen nations' dances in one place, watched side by side, by people who had never seen each other's work. The war then interrupted it -- she helped refugee musicians and the Red Cross during the Second World War -- and the council she wanted would not be formed until 1947.

**Takeaway:** eighteen nations in one London room in 1935, watched side by side -- the Guild of Play method scaled up to an international festival, and the proof that a permanent council was feasible. [C34868]

## 25. The IFMC: 22 September 1947

In her capacity as Honorary Secretary of the International (Advisory) Folk Dance Council she organised the International Conference on Folk Song and Folk Dance at the Belgian Institute, London, 22-27 September 1947, with delegates from 28 countries, mostly government-appointed, plus a UNESCO representative. On the afternoon of Monday 22 September 1947, the conference Vice-Chairman Steuart Wilson proposed "that an International Folk Music Council be formed"; the motion was carried by a show of hands. A provisional constitution was adopted and officers appointed for one year: President Ralph Vaughan Williams, Vice-Presidents Poul Lorenzen (Denmark) and Albert Marinus (Belgium), Treasurer W. S. Gwynn Williams, Secretary Maud Karpeles. In the same year the IFMC appointed 140 music and dance experts as correspondents from 35 countries and regions; the first IFMC conference was held in Basel, 13-18 September 1948; the first *Bulletin* appeared in 1948; the first *Journal of the International Folk Music Council* in 1949; and the Council was a founding member of the International Music Council (UNESCO) in 1949. Karpeles was the IFMC's first Secretary General, serving 1947-1963; she was also its first *Bulletin* editor and first *Journal* editor. She was subsequently named Honorary President, serving 1963-1976, to her death. The founding must be stated with its qualifiers, because the shorthand misleads: she organised the conference, she was its secretary, and the ICTMD's own history credits her with organising the conference at which the Council was formed -- but the formation motion was Wilson's, the conference was funded by the EFDSS, Vaughan Williams was the first President, and the Council was built by its officers and 140 correspondents. "Virtually single-handedly" is one reviewer's phrase for 1946-47, not a documented fact. And she was never President: one secondary source, a 2002 seminar notice, says she "was its president for a number of years", and that is wrong -- the presidents were Vaughan Williams (1947-58), then Jaap Kunst (1959-60), then Kodaly. The honest credit is larger than the inflated one: she was the organiser and the secretary, the person who did the work, for sixteen years, and then the honorary president for thirteen more. The Council's name changed to International Council for Traditional Music at the 26th World Conference (Seoul, 27 August 1981) and to International Council for Traditions of Music and Dance on 15 July 2023 (Legon, Ghana) -- both after her death.

**Takeaway:** the Council was formed by a show of hands on 22 September 1947, on Wilson's motion -- and Karpeles, its organiser and first secretary, ran it for sixteen years without ever being its president. [C34869]

## 26. The comparative method: pooling the world's folk music

After the war Karpeles turned the Sharp method outward into an international science. Her own stated purpose was modest -- "to pool the resources and experiences of individual workers and national bodies" -- and the Council's remit was "recognizing the scientific, social and artistic aspects of folk music and their interdependence". The comparative eye was trained early: at Bayonne in 1927 she met Frantisek Pospisil, filming Basque sword dances as part of a comparative study; at Prague the same year the EFDS dance team toured. The mechanism, watched, is a map that keeps gaining threads: many small archives -- one per country -- each glowing in its own colour, each with its own dance-figures and song-lines, and thin lines drawn between archives wherever the same pattern appears in two places (a sword dance here, a sword dance there; a ballad tune in Appalachia matching one in Somerset). The lines accumulate into a web, and patterns that looked local turn out to be shared. She kept the comparative work going into old age: in 1962 she and Peter Kennedy recorded the evacuated Tristan da Cunha islanders at Calshot -- 40 tracks, 13 of them songs -- and published "A Report on Visits to the Tristan Da Cunha Islanders" in the EFDSS journal. A single new node -- a remote island community, evacuated and recorded far from home -- is added late, and its songs light up connections to England that no one had drawn before. The IFMC circle itself was correspondence, not conversation: with the exception of Vaughan Williams, none of its officers has a documented personal exchange with Karpeles in the sources read, and the Jankovic sisters and Gertrude Kurath are the exception that proves the rule -- their letters survive in the archive, and Karpeles is the hub through which Serbian and American dance scholars who never met each other are connected. The study must not present the IFMC officer list as a circle of friends.

**Takeaway:** pool the resources, draw the threads between archives, and watch local patterns turn out to be shared -- the Sharp method scaled from one county to thirty-five countries. [C34870]

## 27. The manuals: collecting as a teachable method

The IFMC's first fieldwork manual was *Manual for Folk Music Collectors* (1951), co-written by Karpeles and Arnold A. Bake, 30 pages, with a supplement on cine-filming by Doris Plaister. Its central ethical rule, quoted in later literature: "It is, of course, essential that the collector should not show, or indeed, feel any superiority over his informant." The revised and enlarged edition, *The Collecting of Folk Music and Other Ethnomusicological Material: A Manual for Field Workers* (1958), edited by Karpeles with a preface by Raymond Firth, extended the scope to extra-European regions and took account of tape recording, cinematography and sound filming. The manuals are the moment her method becomes teachable: what she had learned by doing -- first as Sharp's amanuensis, then alone in Newfoundland -- is written down as rules for strangers. The ethical rule is the one to hold onto, because it is the opposite of the colonial framing her Newfoundland work is criticised for: the collector must not feel superior to the informant, and the manual says so in print. The tension between the rule and the practice is real and is carried, not resolved: she sought only songs of British origin in Newfoundland, and she also wrote the rule that forbids the collector's superiority. *Folk Songs of Europe* (1956), edited by Karpeles for the IFMC under the auspices of the International Music Council with UNESCO assistance, is the companion volume: 183 songs from 30 European countries, texts in the original language with English translation -- the first volume of the IFMC's International Folk Song Anthologies series, and the comparative method as a book. She was General Editor of the *Journal of the International Folk Music Council* from its first volume in 1949, her name on the masthead as General Editor for volumes 4, 5, 10, 11, 12 and 16, and the obituary says she edited the Journal "for many years".

**Takeaway:** the manuals write down what she learned by doing -- and their central rule, that the collector must not feel superior to the informant, sits in tension with the colonial framing of her own Newfoundland collecting. [C34871]

## 28. The return visit: Appalachia 1950, the re-measurement

In 1950, aged sixty-five, she returned to the Appalachians with the American scholar Sidney Robertson Cowell, aged forty-seven, setting out from Washington, DC on Friday 8 September 1950 bound for Afton, Virginia. The plan was to retrace her journeys with Sharp and revisit singers, making tape recordings on an Eicor machine borrowed from the Library of Congress -- the visit was sponsored by the Library of Congress, not the BBC as some secondary summaries say. She now travelled in Cowell's car, where with Sharp she had travelled predominantly on foot. The object was explicit: "to find out to what extent the musical tradition had been maintained and what changes had taken place in the social and cultural life of the people during the intervening period". The expedition lasted 27 days in Virginia, North Carolina and Tennessee and yielded 91 recorded items: of some 50 target singers who had died or could not be traced, she found 31 (former singers or near relatives); 16 no longer remembered the songs; the remaining 15 (nine former singers, six relatives) sang 69 songs; five singers and instrumentalists not previously known gave 22 more pieces. Thirty-four of the recorded songs had already been noted by Sharp and Karpeles and are in the 1932 volumes. She is the only collector in this series who re-visited her own fieldwork thirty years on and reported the losses alongside the survivals -- and the mechanism is a before-and-after on the same ground: the same map as the 1916-18 expeditions, revisited, with half the singer-nodes dark (dead or untraced) and some that light up flickering and going out (forgotten). Cowell, her companion, held the opposite view of the tradition -- dynamic, not purist -- and their diaries record the tension. Cowell later described Karpeles as "a slight figure under a witch's hood" walking ahead into the woods "looking like someone who might have grown up under a mushroom". She revisited the mountains in 1955, aged seventy, with the mountain folksong enthusiast Evelyn Wells, adding to the total of recordings -- but no standalone Karpeles report on the 1955 trip exists, and its dates, places and yield were not established in sources read.

**Takeaway:** thirty-two years later she went back to the same singers by name, with a tape recorder, and counted what had survived -- 31 found, 16 forgotten, 91 items -- the only re-measurement in the whole series. [C34872]

## 29. The print feedback loop: the book re-teaches the singer

The most consequential finding of the 1950 return cuts against her own doctrine. When words had been forgotten, she could produce a printed copy noted over thirty years earlier from the same singer, "and with this before them they were able to complete the song. It was gratifying to be able to repay in kind a debt of longstanding." The printed book fed back into the tradition: singers re-learned from the volumes songs they had sung to Sharp and forgotten, and old songs came back "from the great world beyond the mountains". The loop is the mechanism: field to print, print back to field, the record re-teaching the singer. It is also the evidence that breaks the purity doctrine from inside her own report: the tradition survived, in part, by the very means she distrusted -- print, and the world beyond the mountains. The tunes, meanwhile, had barely changed: in most of the 34 songs already noted by Sharp, "only a slight modification... confined to the auxiliary notes and does not affect the structure of the tune". The structure held; the small notes wobbled. That is a precise, checkable finding, and it is the one that survives her own interpretation of it: continuity is in the structure, variation is in the auxiliaries, and selection -- the community keeping what it wants -- is what the thirty-two years did. Her verdict on the mountains was that roads, electricity, radio, primary education and the money economy had "completely revolutionized" mountain life, and that the mountains were "no longer the folk song collector's paradise" -- yet in the same article she records singers re-learning songs from print, old songs returning from beyond the mountains, and a folk-song wave of popularity in America. The disappearance prediction was not borne out in her own account. The tradition was changed, not dead, and the print feedback loop is the counter-evidence she herself recorded.

**Takeaway:** the book went back to the mountain and re-taught the singer -- field to print, print to field -- and the loop breaks her own purity doctrine from inside her own report. [C34873]

## 30. Radio, the harmonium, and the corrupted tune

Her verdict on what had changed names the agents precisely: roads, electricity, washing machines, primary education, and above all the radio, "the archenemy... of folk song", bringing "cheap 'hill-billies'" in place of the real thing. The radio is the mechanism to stage as an invader: a tower rising at the edge of the map, broadcasting thin, bright, commercial shapes that crowd out the old songs at the margins. The harmonium is the smaller, sharper case: the one exception to the finding that tunes had barely changed was "Black is the Colour", ruined, in her view, because the singer had learned harmonium accompaniment and the sharpened leading note "transformed one of the loveliest of tunes into one that is utterly cheap and commonplace". One note, sharpened by an instrument, corrupts a tune -- and the corruption is audible, visible, and attributable to a specific cause. The two agents are different in kind and should not be conflated: the radio is a system, broadcasting commercial music into every house; the harmonium is a single instrument, in a single singer's hands, changing a single note. Both are, in her account, what "progress" did to the tradition, and both belong in the piece as distinct mechanisms -- the tower at the edge, and the note that sharpens. Her opening address at the 1954 Sao Paulo conference warned against worshipping "the god of progress": "Half a century ago we in Europe were inclined to worship the god of progress... But the events of the last years has brought disillusionment. We now realise that much of what we call progress was in fact merely a turning away from the old established ways of life..." The address is the doctrine in her own words, and the radio and the harmonium are its field evidence.

**Takeaway:** the radio at the edge of the map, and one sharpened leading note from a harmonium -- two different agents of change, one a system and one a single note, both named in her own report. [C34874]

## 31. 1955, Tristan da Cunha, and the late fieldwork

She revisited the mountains in 1955, aged seventy, with Evelyn Wells, adding to the total of recordings. In 1962 refugees from Tristan da Cunha arrived in Britain; Karpeles visited them and recorded their dances, publishing "A Report on Visits to the Tristan Da Cunha Islanders" (*Journal of the English Folk Dance and Song Society*, 1962) -- 40 tracks with Peter Kennedy at Calshot, 13 of them songs. In spring 1952 she made two collecting expeditions to Somerset and Devon -- the first with Marie Slocombe (BBC Sound Recordings Librarian), the second with her nephew Peter Kennedy -- deliberately revisiting descendants of singers Sharp had collected from, to see if they still remembered the old songs. Her autobiography: "Over and over again, those we visited admitted that they had not formerly taken much interest in the 'old' songs, but they now regretted this as they had come to realize that they were better than the new ones." In autumn 1953 she made a Kent collecting expedition, staying with school friend Violet Rumney at Sissinghurst and covering about 400 miles across some 30 villages; she recorded "John Barleycorn" from Dave Wickens at Smarden and five songs from Albert Beale at Kenardington -- "son and brother of singers from whom Cecil Sharp noted songs". She interviewed a gipsy family named Stanley at Bettenham but could not ask them to sing because of a death in the family. Her report (VWML MK/1/2/4907) concludes Romney Marsh was "further away from the tradition than the 'upland' regions" -- "I suspect this is because they are too isolated" -- and notes "a greater understanding of the type of song I required than is usually the case", possibly due to Frank Collinson's *Country Magazine* contributions. She returned to Kent in January 1954 with Peter Kennedy and recorded Albert Beale and the gipsy Charlie Scamp. Kent Trad notes that neither Kennedy nor Karpeles appears to have returned to record Oliver Scamp or Mrs Bird, and that no recordings or transcriptions of the Kentish singers appear to have survived -- it is unclear whether her "recording" meant sound recording or writing down. She did make sound recordings of traditional singers, both in England and in North America. With Pat Shaw she collected in 1952 an alternative tune for "The Holly and the Ivy" from Peter Jones in Herefordshire. She made sure the folk singer Phil Tanner, once discovered in Gower, Wales, was recorded -- no date in any source read. The late fieldwork is the same method at seventy that it was at thirty: go back, by name, to the singers and their descendants, and see what is left.

**Takeaway:** at seventy she was still going back by name -- Somerset and Devon in 1952, Kent in 1953-54, Appalachia in 1955, Tristan da Cunha in 1962 -- the same method, the same question: what is left? [C34875]

## 32. Continuity, variation, selection: the doctrine

At the IFMC conference in Sao Paulo, Brazil, in 1954, the Council set out guidelines for understanding folk music; Karpeles reiterated them as recently as 1972 in *An Introduction to English Folk Song*: three forces at work in folk music -- continuity, variation, and selection by the community. For her these were not academic topics: folk music and dance were living phenomena that had to be sung, played and danced; only in living practice could selection occur, and selection implied the test of time. This was "perhaps the most important article of faith for Maud Karpeles". *An Introduction to English Folk Song* (Oxford University Press, 1973 -- Wachsmann's obituary says 1972, Wikipedia's bibliography says 1973, and the publication year was not verified against the title page) is the only book-length statement of her own views under her own name, as opposed to editions of Sharp: a short introductory course, viii + 120 pages, whose contemporary reviewer calls it "both an introductory course and a refresher course" and notes her concern with "the aesthetic experience of participating in folksinging". The doctrine is the theory underneath the whole career, and it is also the theory her own evidence complicates: continuity is what the 1950 return measured (the structure held, the auxiliaries wobbled); variation is what the harmonium did to "Black is the Colour"; selection is what the community did when it re-learned songs from her printed books. The three forces are not three chapters; they are three things that happen to the same song at the same time, and the 1950 return is the experiment that shows all three at once. She remained active in the folk-music world to the end: in 1975 she formally proposed "that future conferences should include folk music in education, that folk music was distinct from other forms of music such as pop music and art music"; in 1974 she admonished the Executive Board that there was too great a cleavage between the scientific and humanistic aspects of the Council's work and that its raison d'etre was humanistic as well as scientific.

**Takeaway:** continuity, variation, selection -- three forces on the same song at the same time, and the 1950 return is the experiment that shows all three at once. [C34876]

## 33. Honours, late life, death

Honours: OBE in 1961, for services to folk music. Honorary doctorate from Universite Laval, Quebec, 1961. Honorary doctorate from the Memorial University of Newfoundland -- dated 1970 by Wikipedia and 1972 by Wachsmann's obituary, a conflict carried unresolved; Laval 1961 is agreed by both. She is styled "Dr. Maud Karpeles" in the obituary's heading. She attended the Board meeting in Warsaw in 1976 and reported on her return, shortly before her death, "on the whole a good meeting though I did not approve of everything that was decided", and of the newly nominated President, "I think he will be good." She suffered recurrent illness for some years before her death, carried through by "indomitable spirit". The night before she died she had dined with friends. She died in London on Friday 1 October 1976, aged 90, six months after her sister Helen's death in Suffolk (so Helen died around April 1976; the exact date, 24 March 1976, is established in the leg 5 report from Cecil Sharp's People). Her papers: she bequeathed all her manuscript and typescript collection of folk dances and songs, including photostat and typescript copies of Sharp's manuscript collection, to the EFDSS for the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library at Cecil Sharp House; the Newfoundland material instead went to the Memorial University of Newfoundland (MUNFLA, Karpeles Fonds 78-003). The VWML collection (catalogued by David Atkinson, 1997, as GB 1477 Karpeles) runs to seven boxes, reference prefix MK/; her unpublished autobiography is MK/7/185; her Newfoundland diaries are at MUNFLA. Her typed Appalachian texts are FW2301-3319 in Sharp's archive. Posthumous recognition: in 2000 the EFDSS issued a set of 55 trading cards with a "flicker book" celebrating the heroes of the folk-song revival -- a Kinora spool showing a morris dance performed by Cecil Sharp, Maud and Helen Karpeles -- also on the BFI/EFDSS DVD *Here's a Health to the Barley Mow* (2011) and on YouTube. The ICTMD established the Maud Karpeles Fund for members' activities supporting the Council's mission. Pakenham's biography appeared in 2011, the EFDS centenary year. Heaney's review calls her "perhaps the most influential figure in the English folk revival after Cecil Sharp" and "among those few in the pantheon who have shaped the course and nature of the revival", while noting that until Pakenham (2011) there had been no substantial account of her life, that the biography is a chronological narrative rather than a critical assessment ("we still await the critical assessment of her contribution"), and that 90 percent of its 400+ endnotes cite only Karpeles herself and Sharp. The mustrad review adds that it was her IFMC work she most enjoyed and was most lauded for, and judges she "created the IFMC, virtually single-handedly, in 1946-7, at the age of 61" -- a reviewer's judgement, not a source fact, and carried as such.

**Takeaway:** she died at ninety, six months after her sister, still reporting on board meetings -- and the first substantial life of her did not appear until 2011, thirty-five years after her death. [C34877]

## 34. Aesthetic logic: the system underneath

The system underneath Karpeles's career is not a style but a set of transfers, each with a visible direction. First, the hand: knowledge moves from one body to another by substitution -- Sharp's right hand fails, her hand takes over, and the line continues in a different weight. Second, the channel: a song is split into two parallel records, words and tune, taken at the same moment by two people, and the archive is the pair, not either half. Third, the gate: not everything is kept, and the keeping is a doctrine -- old, anonymous, orally carried passes; sentimental, printed, newly made is turned away, and the rejected shapes are the cost of the archive. Fourth, the hub: a centre holds for a week or ten days, spokes are walked, then the centre moves -- the map fills slowly, at walking pace, 281 lights. Fifth, the return: the same ground is visited again thirty years later, and the measurement is the difference -- what died, what was forgotten, what held its structure, what the book taught back. Sixth, the fence: the editor draws a boundary around the growing book, and the boundary is argued over -- the songs were shared freely, and now they are owned. Seventh, the web: the same pattern is found in two countries, a thread is drawn, and the local turns out to be shared. The aesthetic of the pieces this study feeds should come from these transfers, not from the surface of folk art -- not bunting and bell-pads, but a hand taking over mid-stroke, two traces advancing in lockstep, a gate turning shapes away, a hub lighting and fading, a map revisited with half its lights dark, a fence argued over, a web gaining threads. The palette follows the materials: ink and pencil on paper, shorthand ticks, typed carbon copies, the brown of a mission settlement, the green of a mountain ridge, the black of a radio tower at the edge. The motion follows the work: walking pace, never jumped; the second hand picking up the line at the exact point of failure; the printed page floating back to a singer who has forgotten. What must be avoided is the picturesque -- the folk revival as costume -- and the solo genius -- she was never the author of the songs, and her creativity was editorial, organisational and transmissive. She was not a composer. No original compositions are evidenced anywhere in the sources read, and no piece built from this study should present her as writing new melodies.

**Takeaway:** seven transfers -- the hand, the channel, the gate, the hub, the return, the fence, the web -- and the pieces should be built from those motions, not from bunting and bell-pads. [C34878]
