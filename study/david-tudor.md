## M1. Realization-as-composition: the score is not the work, the realization is

Tudor measured Cage's graphs with a decimal ruler or a circular slide rule, computed attack points by formula, position times total duration divided by the graph's area or length, wrote the result out on loose-leaf sheets in a ring binder, and then played that version consistently. Holzaepfel says he pinned them down like butterflies. Iddon says he would create a single realisation and then use that version in all subsequent recordings. The mechanism is exact and small: the open score arrives as a field of marks, and the performer closes it by measuring it, and the measurement is the composition, and the printed score sits untouched while the binder does the work. Brown's December 1952 is the pure form: a page of horizontal and vertical lines of varying thickness, and the performer reads thickness as loudness, length as duration, vertical position as pitch, and Tudor refused to improvise from it and worked out a fixed version instead, and Brown's own liner notes confirm it, quoting the score's own permission, "Further defining of the events is not prohibited... provided that the imposed determinate system is implicit in the score and in these notes", and the result was "a very close and accurate sonic representation of the graphic score implications: relative frequency, duration, loudness and time-space". Four Systems of 1954 got the same treatment, and Eberhard Blum's "Remarks re: Four Systems" answered the piece's parameter questions "by means of various systems of measurement", and Feldman's early graph scores got the same treatment again, and Wolff wrote cue systems, Duo for Pianists and Changing the System of 1972, partly to cope with Tudor's tendency to pre-determine everything in advance, and Tudor "simply wrote out all the possible choices" anyway. **Takeaway:** measure the open score and the measurement is the piece, and leave it open and there is no piece, only a page. Against the chain: Eisler also took other people's material and fixed it, M3 and M4, but his fixing is a quotation planted so the audience recognises it in one bar, and Tudor's fixing is a measurement nobody in the audience ever sees, and the difference is between a flag and a ruler. Cage also left his scores open, M4 in his study, but Cage left them open because he trusted the performer, and Tudor closed them because he trusted the ruler, and the difference is between faith in a person and faith in a measurement.

## M2. Watching time, not experiencing it: space is time, and the ruler is the clock

Music of Changes taught Tudor to put his mind "in a state of non-continuity, not remembering what had passed, so that each moment is alive", and the breakthrough came through the Boulez Second Sonata, which broke his sense of continuity, "I realized that I could play everything, but I had to stop every two measures. I couldn't put it together", and the cure came through Artaud's "affective athleticism", "I put my mind in a state of non-continuity, not remembering what had passed, so that each moment is alive". The formula is space equals time: a quarter note equals 2.5 cm, and barlines mark exact intervals of time-space regardless of content, and Tudor said "I was watching time rather than experiencing it", and "you can't do it unless you're ready for anything at each instant", and "you are now able to telescope some periods and to microscope others at will". The mechanism is a discipline, not a technique: the pianist stares at a ruler's worth of empty space and treats it as a duration, and the empty space is the music, and the notes are only what happens inside it. **Takeaway:** make space into time and the performer stops playing notes and starts watching the clock, and the clock is the instrument. Against the chain: Feldman also stretched time until it became the material, M2 in his study, but Feldman's time is a duration you sit inside, and Tudor's time is a length you measure, and the difference is between a room and a ruler. Cage also made time the subject, M2 in his study, but Cage's time is chance, and Tudor's time is a formula, and the difference is between a throw of the dice and a division.

## M3. Splicing as composition: the cut is the note

In Williams Mix the cut is the note. The piece is four minutes sixteen seconds, eight simultaneous quarter-inch tapes, the first octophonic music, and a 193-page graphic score, "a dressmaker's pattern... you lay the tape on the score itself", and six sound categories, city, country, electronic, manually produced, wind, and "small", and it was realized by a team, Cage, Tudor, Brown, Feldman and Louis and Bebe Barron, not by Tudor alone, see C5, but Wolff's anecdote about Tudor's own splicing work is the mechanism in its purest form, and he "might spend hours fixing the decay of a single note inside a flurry of others, a tape cut of maybe 1/16th of an inch". The mechanism is the hand at the smallest scale: a cut too small to see, a decay that lasts a fraction of a second, and hours spent on it, and the tape is the score, and the razor is the pencil, and the piece is built one sixteenth of an inch at a time. **Takeaway:** cut the tape and the cut is the composition, and the smaller the cut the larger the care, and the care is audible. Against the chain: Brown also built from small units, M4 in his study, but his units are pages you shuffle, and Tudor's units are inches you cut, and the difference is between a deck and a blade. Rzewski also built a large form from small quoted units, M13 in his study, but his units are variations that return to a theme, and Tudor's units are tape fragments that never return, and the difference is between a variation and a splice.

## M4. The graph as a deck of cards: 84 notations, 787 attack points, one binder

Solo for Piano, inside Cage's Concert for Piano and Orchestra, is 84 kinds of notation on 63 pages, lettered A to CF, and there is no full orchestral score, only 13 instrumental parts plus a conductor part, and Tudor treated each instance as a discrete graphic object, selected which to use, transcribed each reading onto its own sheet, and bound them in a ring binder so he could add, remove and rearrange pages. The second realization of 1959 went further: every graph was magnified or shrunk to the same size and laid on top of the others, producing 787 attack points in a Master Table, split into Version 1 with 472 points and Version 2 with 315 points, 90 pages at one page per minute, built only from graphs that produce single attack points so the whole could be stretched or compressed in time, and the quickest written timing is 22 minutes 30 seconds, and a 30-minute version uses 30 pages, and 90 minutes is the Indeterminacy version, not the only one, see C19. Cage's loudness numbers 1 to 64 became Tudor's own dynamic scale, 0.0 to 10.5. Graph BT 54 gave him the amplified Slinky toy, a Slinky hung from a microphone stand with a contact microphone attached and manipulated by hand, and Graph T became clusters read left to right. Winter Music of 1957 is the same mechanism in a smaller form: twenty pages, each holding from 1 to 61 cluster-chords, and Tudor's realization selected and fixed the clusters. **Takeaway:** treat the graph as a deck of cards and the piece becomes a hand you can deal again, and the same binder lasts 22 minutes or 90 minutes, and the length is a decision, not a property. Against the chain: Brown also made the page into a deck, M1 in his study, but Brown's deck is the score itself, dealt by the performer, and Tudor's deck is the realization, dealt in advance and then fixed, and the difference is between a shuffle and a binding. Wolff also wrote scores that are decks of choices, M3 in his study, but Wolff's choices are cues the players exchange, and Tudor's choices are pages he bound, and the difference is between a conversation and a binder.

## M5. The amplified piano as a second instrument: the toy spring, the cartridge, the string from inside

In Variations II of 1961 and Cartridge Music of 1960 the piano is not played, it is listened to through pickups: phonograph cartridges used as pickups on objects, a Slinky hung from a microphone stand with a contact microphone, the strings bowed or struck from inside, and Tudor said "The whole thing taken together is an instrument of its own". The mechanism is the ear moved inside the instrument: the piano stops being a keyboard and becomes a resonant box with things attached to it, and the cartridge is the ear, and the Slinky is the string, and the sound is whatever the box does with the touch. The Slinky is the purest form, because it is a toy, and it sounds like an abstract electronic event, and the joke is that the most serious music in the room comes from the cheapest object in it. **Takeaway:** put a pickup on the piano instead of a finger, and the piano becomes a second instrument, and the second instrument is the one worth listening to. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also turned a body into an instrument, M1 and M2 in his study, but his instrument is a woman blinking on a chair, and Tudor's instrument is a piano with a toy hanging off it, and the difference is between a signal and a spring. Eisler also hid advanced material inside a familiar form, M8, but his disguise is a tune that sounds like a folk song, and Tudor's disguise is a toy that sounds like electronics, and the difference is between a smuggled row and a smuggled Slinky.

## M6. Sound as control signal: the instrument that plays the room

In Bandoneon! of 1966 the instrument does not make the music, it steers it. The bandoneon is a control source, not a sound source, and its sound, picked up by a harmonium-reed "complex microphone", drives programmed audio circuits, and the circuits drive moving loudspeakers, TV images and lights, and Tudor's program note says the piece "uses no composing means, since when activated it composes itself out of its own composite instrumental nature". The hardware is exact: four radio-controlled carts carrying instrumental loudspeakers, aluminium sheets, a steel tray, two wooden planks at 90 degrees, plate glass, and video by Lowell Cross, and the first night failed, because the circuit boards were soldered backwards, back versus front mirror image, and the second night worked perfectly, and Kagel had introduced Tudor to the tango in 1960 and written a bandoneon piece for him, which is how the instrument got into the room at all. In Reunion of 1968, first played Tuesday 5 March 1968 in Toronto, the chess moves are the control signals, and each move triggers sounds through a photoelectric interface built by Lowell Cross, and the game is between Cage and Duchamp, and the piece is sometimes attributed to Cage in error, see C6, and it is Tudor's piece with Cross. **Takeaway:** make the instrument a steering wheel and the room becomes the instrument, and the musician plays the room instead of playing a tune. Against the chain: Curran also made the audience part of the piece, M6 in his study, but his audience brings a sound and adds it to the pool, and Tudor's bandoneon brings a signal and steers the circuits, and the difference is between participation and control. Cardew also made the score into a machine, M5 in his study, but his machine is a school, and Tudor's machine is a room full of carts, and the difference is between a lesson and a steering wheel.

## M7. The transducer, the speaker as instrument: the object becomes the loudspeaker

In Rainforest the loudspeaker has no cone: a transducer, descended from US Navy underwater design, "a speaker without a cone", drives a bedsprings, a wine barrel, a 55-gallon drum, and the object itself becomes the speaker, and Tudor called it an "orchestra of loudspeakers, each speaker being as unique as any musical instrument". The object filters the sound, and the contact microphone listens to what the object does with it. Rainforest I of 1968 was commissioned by the Merce Cunningham Dance Company for $500 plus $25 per performance, with dance by Cunningham, Warhol's Silver Clouds, costumes by Johns, and a dance premiere on Saturday 9 March 1968 at the Buffalo Festival of the Arts Today, and eight tabletop objects, and tone oscillators making bird-like sounds, and sound materials "collected from natural scientific sources". Rainforest II of 1968-69 added vocal input and a sound library gathered in 1969-70, deer, birdsong, whale song, mosquitoes in a jar, satellite data from JPL, neural activity, lab brainwave recordings, and Behrman called the aviary tapes "too literal", and Mumma collaborated, mostly listening and taking notes. Rainforest IV of 1973, at the Chocorua workshop in New Hampshire, "Sliding Pitches in the Rainforest in the Field", about five hours in a barn, ran sixteen to forty suspended objects, a bedspring, a wine barrel, toilet floats, wagon wheel rims, a copper still, a Styrofoam box, a 55-gallon drum, and three-to-six-hour performances, and about 36 locations and 125-plus performances, and the audience was invited to bite the objects and press ears to them, and blind audiences and children were specifically invited. Sea Tails of 1983, Centre Pompidou, Friday 3 June 1983, with Molly Davies and Jackie Monnier, also called Jackie Matisse, see C15, flew kites underwater, and their motion drove the sound, and the kite is a transducer too. **Takeaway:** take the cone out of the speaker and the world becomes the speaker, and every object has a voice, and the voice is what the object does with the sound you give it. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also made the loudspeaker into the instrument, M3 in his study, but his speaker carries a brainwave, and Tudor's speaker is a barrel, and the difference is between a signal and a thing. Rauschenberg also made objects into art, M1 in his study, but his objects are looked at, and Tudor's objects are listened to, and the difference is between a Combine and a loudspeaker.

## M8. Feedback as material: the circuit left alone, singing to itself

At the Pepsi Pavilion at Expo '70 in Osaka, Tudor was one of four core artists, with Fred Waldhauer and Gordon Mumma, and Mumma built an eight-channel sound-modifier console, and Tudor connected all eight modifiers in series and fed the output back into the input, producing Pepscillator, oscillatory rhythms with "an extraordinary degree of variability and unpredictability", and thirty-seven speakers in the dome, and nine pieces, and Anima Pepsi, Pepsibird and Pepscillator recorded by Sony, and Microphone in its first version. The mechanism is the loop closed on itself: the output becomes the input, and the circuit sings to itself, and the performer only nudges the gain, and the unpredictability is the composition, because nobody, not even the composer, knows what the loop will do next. Untitled of 1972, Homage to Toshi Ichiyanagi, is the same mechanism pushed until it breaks: two integrated chains, up to 80 components, phase-shift oscillation plus variable side-band modulation, and Tudor was fond of the 60-degree point, "what attracted me about the phase shift was the difference a degree could make", and the system was too unstable to play live, "not too complicated, simply too difficult... feedback is feedback... it will take off", so Tudor generated recorded sources, then processed them live through a third chain of about 40 components, and his program note says the piece brings together "objects of various natures with different speeds and latencies to form an electronic ecology". Toneburst of 1975, premiere Saturday 8 March 1975 in Detroit, for Cunningham's Sounddance, is one chain plus output processing, fully live, unlike Untitled, and Tudor said in 1994 that Toneburst is "a direct translation of his mind into music", and at Buffalo a 1930s Western Electric speaker caught fire, and Tudor "grinned from ear to ear and cranked up his levels a little bit more". **Takeaway:** feed the output back into the input and the circuit composes itself, and the performer's job is to keep it from taking off, and the grin when it catches fire is the review. Against the chain: Teitelbaum also let a system run itself, M14 in his study, but his system drifts and dissolves, and Tudor's system oscillates and takes off, and the difference is between a fade and a fire. Curran also built systems that play themselves, M1 in his study, but his systems are pools of sound the players wade in, and Tudor's system is a loop that feeds on itself, and the difference is between a pool and a fire.

## M9. Mismatch as method: the components that do not match are the composition

Tudor deliberately used components that did not match: "if the components don't match, then the one component is able to influence the next, so that signals are created at many points within the circuit", and the mismatch is the composition, and matched components would be silent. The mechanism is the refusal of the correct part: the engineer matches components so nothing unexpected happens, and Tudor mismatches them so something unexpected happens at every joint, and the circuit becomes a chain of small accidents, and each accident is a sound, and the label on the box is a lie, because no component does what its label says. **Takeaway:** mismatch the components and the circuit starts talking, and match them and it stays quiet, and the talking is the piece. Against the chain: Eisler also used the wrong tool for the job, M8, but his wrong tool is a twelve-tone row inside a documentary score, and Tudor's wrong tool is a cheap box wired to the wrong box, and the difference is between a smuggled system and a smuggled accident. Cage also let the wrong thing happen, M1 in his study, but Cage's wrong thing is chance, and Tudor's wrong thing is a solder joint, and the difference is between a dice throw and a mismatch.

## M10. The score as circuit diagram: boxes and arrows, read like staff notation

Tudor's working papers are block diagrams, matrix maps and nomographs, not staff notation: the Untitled chains, the Weatherings matrix switchers of 20 by 10 and 10 by 30, the Variations II nomographs, graphical calculating charts that turned the score's measurements into performance decisions, the Master Table of attack points for the second realization of the Solo for Piano. The diagram says what connects to what, and the sound is whatever comes out. Indeterminacy of 1959 is the same mechanism as a switchboard: ninety one-minute stories, and Tudor's 90-minute second realization of the Solo for Piano accompanied them, and worried the accompaniment would be too sparse, Tudor added tracks from Cage's tape piece Fontana Mix, switched on and off at points corresponding to readings of graph BY, "Any noises, their relative pitch given graphically". Soundings: Ocean Diary of 1994, Brussels, Tuesday 17 May 1994, for Cage's Ocean, with Andrew Culver's Ocean 1-95, is the diary as a score, and the last major Cunningham commission, and Cage's Ocean was his last dance work. **Takeaway:** draw the score as a diagram of connections and the music becomes whatever the connections do, and the page is a wiring plan, not a set of notes. Against the chain: Wolff also wrote scores that are instructions, not notes, M2 in his study, but Wolff's instructions are cues between people, and Tudor's instructions are wires between boxes, and the difference is between a conversation and a circuit. Brown also made the score into a picture, M2 in his study, but Brown's picture is a page you look at, and Tudor's picture is a diagram you wire up, and the difference is between a drawing and a plan.

## M11. The performer inside the system, listening as performance: the lid, the hover, the hand on the switch

"I listen to the music. That's how the performance is made. I listen to it and change it as it goes along." "It is important to me that the audience senses the presence of a live musician." The performer does not produce the sound, he tends it. In 4'33" the performer's only act is opening and closing a lid, and the movements are 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, 1 minute 40 seconds per the Woodstock manuscript and program, or 33 seconds, 2 minutes 40 seconds, 1 minute 20 seconds per the First Tacet Edition, and both sets are sourced, see C7, and the original manuscript is lost, and Tudor reconstructed it in 1982, see C23, and Cage's mother, at the Cherry Lane Theatre second performance, said "Oh, David likes this piece so much because he thinks it's like a prayer", and Tudor performed it only about ten times in the early years, plus a later revival. The mechanism is the performer reduced to a gesture: the lid opens, the lid closes, and everything else is the room, and the room is the music, and the man sitting still in front of a table of boxes, hands hovering, doing almost nothing, while the room fills with sound, is the same mechanism forty years later. **Takeaway:** sit still and listen, and the listening is the performance, and the hand that does almost nothing is doing the most important thing in the room. Against the chain: Cage also made silence into a piece, M3 in his study, but Cage's silence is a frame for the room, and Tudor's silence is a lid, and the difference is between a frame and a gesture. Feldman also asked the performer to do less, M5 in his study, but Feldman's less is a quiet note held for a long time, and Tudor's less is a lid opening and closing, and the difference is between a duration and a door.

## M12. The piece that teaches itself, giving the work away: the barn, the bite, the strangers

"Rainforest teaches itself"; "the objects should teach you what it wants to hear." The Chocorua workshop handed the piece to other people: the Composers Inside Electronics group, Paul DeMarinis, John Driscoll, Phil Edelstein, Linda Fisher, Ralph Jones, Martin Kalve, Bill Viola, performed Rainforest IV without Tudor, and Bill Viola, age 22 at Chocorua, credits Tudor with shaping his approach to sound in video, and Tudor only first asked for his music to be performed without him in October 1994. The work is a method for listening to objects, not a fixed set of sounds, and the audience is invited to bite the objects and press ears to them, and blind audiences and children were specifically invited, and the piece happens without its author, and that is the point. Rainforest III of 1972, with Cage's Mureau, Radio Bremen, Friday 5 May 1972, is the portable Rainforest, four objects and two stereo cassette decks, and the late Cunningham commissions, Five Stone Wind of 1988, Webwork of 1987, Virtual Focus of 1990, are the repertory carried from piece to piece, tape sources, matrix switching, and the slow accumulation of a personal library of sounds, and Virtual Focus is the piece Rogalsky's 1999 study takes as its subject. **Takeaway:** build a piece that teaches itself and it outlives you, and build a piece that needs you and it dies with you, and the barn full of strangers is the proof. ## M14. The refused ending: the music does not end, it is switched off

Tudor's music does not end, it is switched off. "There's always a certain point where the work that you do to realize these musical ideas, all of a sudden it has a life of its own, and that's the point where I decide that it's my musical composition. When it's living for itself then I feel, 'Okay, I can sign my name to that.'" At Mills College in the late 1960s he switched off a performance that had been running without end: "I still had lots to do." The music that sprang to life once activated also died every night, and the death is a hand on a switch, and the hand is the only ending the piece gets. Toneburst: Maps and Fragments of 1996, with Sophia Ogielska, Wesleyan, September 1996, was his last work, and the last work is a map and fragments of a piece that never ends, and the map is the piece. **Takeaway:** let the system live until you switch it off, and the switch is the cadence, and the cadence is a hand, not a chord. Against the chain: Eisler also refused to close his work, M2 and M14, but his refusal is a song that ends on a question and an opera that was never written, and Tudor's refusal is a circuit that runs until someone kills it, and the difference is between a question and a switch. ## Evolution

Six phases, and the through-line is constant: the same man who measured a graph with a decimal ruler in 1952 is still measuring a circuit with a block diagram in 1992, and what changes is what he measures, and how loudly it is allowed to answer. Phase 1, organist to virtuoso realizer, 1943 to 1952, the driver is mastery, a technique for playing what no one has played before: organist at Trinity Church, Swarthmore, 1943, Swarthmore College 1945-47, piano with Irma Wolpe, composition and analysis with Stefan Wolpe, organ and theory with H. William Hawke, Black Mountain College summers, the Boulez Second Sonata of 1950 teaching non-continuity, Music of Changes of 1951 teaching watching time. Phase 2, the pianist as co-author in all but name, 1952 to 1961, the driver is exactitude, freedom earned by fixing everything in advance: 4'33", Williams Mix, December 1952, Four Systems, Winter Music, the Concert for Piano and Orchestra, Indeterminacy, Cartridge Music, Variations II, the fixed realization as the method, measure the graph, write it out, play the same version every time, Darmstadt teaching 1956 to 1961. Phase 3, the turn to circuits, 1964 to 1968, the driver is transfer, the same exactitude, now applied to components instead of notes: Fluorescent Sound, Bandoneon!, Reunion, Rainforest I, Video III, the piano bench abandoned, the object, the circuit and the loudspeaker taking the keyboard's place. Phase 4, the feedback years, 1968 to 1976, the driver is listening, "I listen to the music. That's how the performance is made. I listen to it and change it as it goes along": Rainforest II to IV, the Pepsi Pavilion, Untitled, Toneburst, the circuits allowed to misbehave, feedback, phase shift, mismatch, the performer stops playing notes and starts listening to a system and steering it. Phase 5, the modulator years, 1976 to 1990, the driver is accumulation, the system becomes a repertory: Forest Speech, Weatherings, Phonemes, Sea Tails, Webwork, Five Stone Wind, Virtual Focus, tape sources, matrix switchers, a growing repertory of sounds carried from piece to piece, the Cunningham commissions keeping coming. Phase 6, the late return, 1990 to 1996, the driver is handing over, the system finally allowed to outlive its maker: Neural Synthesis, Neural Network Plus, Soundings: Ocean Diary, Toneburst: Maps and Fragments, the computer enters, the climax returns, four to six per performance, by instruction, the 1992 Frankfurt Concert recording closes the circle back to the piano, first permission for others to perform his music only in October 1994. What stayed the same across all six phases: the measurement habit, the decimal ruler becomes the block diagram, the fixed version, the realization becomes the patch, and the refusal to improvise, the performer prepares everything, then listens. What changed: the instrument, piano, then object, then circuit, then computer, and the performer's place in it, in front of the score, then inside the system.

## Load-bearing corrections

C1. "Stopped playing piano" versus the 1992 last piano performance. Mode Records: "This is the last performance of David Tudor at the piano, recorded at 1992's Cage Festival in Frankfurt." Ensemble Modern, Ingo Metzmacher conducting, mode 57. The correct form is "ended his active career as a pianist in the late 1960s", with a final piano performance in 1992.

C2. Toneburst date conflict. The Lovely biography dates Toneburst to 1974; the Cunningham selected-works table says 1975; the Getty finding aid and Ogielska give the premiere as Saturday 8 March 1975, Detroit. Use 1975.

C3. Rainforest version numbering is Tudor's own mess, not a tidy sequence. Tudor himself at times called the 1973 version "RF III"; the title "Rainforest IV" first appears in print in 1980s negotiations; the Edition Block LP mislabels it Rainforest II. Rogalsky: "To be too obsessive about getting the version numbers right is to risk losing sight of the broader picture." Do not state version numbers without this note.

C4. "Ruler and calipers" is half verified, half not. The decimal ruler and the circular slide rule are documented by Holzaepfel, via the New Music USA article, for the Solo for Piano second realization. "Calipers" appears in no source read this leg, and a targeted search returned only tool catalogs. The study says "decimal ruler", not "calipers". See D3.

C5. Williams Mix was a team realization. Cage, Tudor, Brown, Feldman and Louis and Bebe Barron spliced it together over 1951-53. Tudor did not do it alone, though Wolff's anecdote about the 1/16-inch cuts is about Tudor's own splicing work.

C6. Reunion is sometimes attributed to Cage. Pritchett's book erroneously attributes it to Cage; it is Tudor's piece with Lowell Cross, the chess interface. Do not credit Cage.

C7. 4'33" movement timings conflict. The Woodstock manuscript and program give 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, 1 minute 40 seconds; the First Tacet Edition says the premiere timings were 33 seconds, 2 minutes 40 seconds, 1 minute 20 seconds. Both are sourced in the 4'33" Wikipedia article; do not state one set without noting the other.

C8. Boulez Second Sonata US premiere date: Sunday 17 December 1950, per the Getty Scores Project. Wikipedia's Tudor article says only "1950". Use the Getty date.

C9. Merce Cunningham Dance Company music director 1992-1995, Kosugi after 1995. Tudor was a founding musician of the company in 1953; the directorship came only after Cage's death in 1992. The brief's "1953 to ..." conflates the two roles. Use 1953 for founding musician, 1992-1995 for director.

C10. "First composition": Fluorescent Sound of 1964 is identified retrospectively as his first piece; Bandoneon! of 1966 is the first piece he publicly presented as his own, and Tudor himself called it his second piece in the Duffie interview. Both statements are true; do not call either one simply "his first piece" without the qualification.

C11. Rainforest V dates 1973-2015 in MoMA's acquisition record; the installation version dates to 2009. Do not date "Rainforest V" to 1973 alone, and do not call 2009 the composition date.

C12. Neural Synthesis (1992-94, nos. 1-9) and Neural Network Plus (1992, for Cunningham's Enter) are distinct works. Do not merge them; the first is a computer-music series, the second a dance piece with Kosugi.

C13. "Stopped in the early 1960s" is wrong on the decade. Darmstadt teaching ran through 1961, and his active piano career ran into the late 1960s. The turn to composing was gradual, not a clean break in 1961 or 1962.

C14. India studio date: the Getty finding aid and Wikipedia agree on 1969, National Institute of Design, Ahmedabad; the Lovely biography gives no date. Use 1969.

C15. Sea Tails collaborators: the Getty list says Jackie Monnier; Wikipedia says Jackie Matisse. Same person, Monnier, married name Matisse. Note it once and move on.

C16. 9 Evenings ran Thursday 13 to Sunday 23 October 1966; Bandoneon! was performed Friday 14 and Tuesday 18 October, per the Getty list. Wikipedia's table lists the piece without dates. Use the Getty dates.

C17. Music of Changes: complete premiere Tuesday 1 January 1952 versus Book I in public Thursday 5 July 1951. Do not call 5 July 1951 the premiere of the work.

C18. Rainforest naming: MoMA says the 1968 dance premiered at SUNY Buffalo; the Getty list gives the premiere as Saturday 9 March 1968 at the Buffalo Festival of the Arts Today. Same event, two descriptions; use the Getty date.

C19. Second realization of Solo for Piano, the numbers, after Holzaepfel via the New Music USA article: 787 attack points in total, split into Version 1 with 472 and Version 2 with 315; 90 pages at one page per minute; the quickest written timing is 22 minutes 30 seconds, two pages per thirty seconds; a 30-minute version uses 30 pages. These numbers supersede the summary figure of "90-minute" alone: 90 minutes is the Indeterminacy version, not the only one.

C20. The 1992 Concert recording is piano AND live electronics, Mode Records: "Tudor uses both piano and live electronics with the Ensemble Modern"; the 1958 Town Hall recording used no electronics. The "last piano performance" was a hybrid, not a pure piano recital.

C21. Cage died Wednesday 12 August 1992, days before the Frankfurt Cage Festival performances; the festival "sadly became a memorial as Cage passed away shortly before the performances", per Mode Records. Tudor's last piano performance was played after Cage's death.

C22. December 1952 dating oddity: the score is dated December 1952, but Brown said he composed it in a single afternoon on Wednesday 20 January 1954. Do not state a composition year without the note.

C23. 4'33" original manuscript lost: Tudor's account in the Duffie interview is that Cage asked Tudor for the original to make the Kremen score, and it disappeared; Tudor reconstructed it in 1982. Single-source, Tudor's own account; the Kremen score is the surviving authority.

C24. The "co-composer" label is scholarly, not Tudor's. Piekut's "distributed authorship", Holzaepfel's account and Iddon's describe what Tudor did; Tudor himself refused the word, "I still would be unable to call myself a co-composer". See D5.
