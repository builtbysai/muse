# Leslie Mezei: Study Notes

**Site:** Leslie Gabriel Mezei is a Hungarian-Canadian computer scientist and computer-art pioneer, Associate Professor of Computer Science at the University of Toronto (1965-1978), founder of the Computer Graphics Group that became the Dynamic Graphics Project (1967), and author of what is often cited as the field's first article ("Artistic Design by Computer", 1964), of the first recognizable piece of computer-art criticism (with Arnold Rockman, 1964), and of the first yearly bibliography (from 1967). Born Thursday 9 July 1931 in Godollo, Hungary -- the Budapest birthplace given in several secondary sources is an error; his own memoir says Godollo, where the family lived until fleeing to Budapest in spring 1944 -- a Holocaust survivor who arrived in Canada in January 1948, he built the SPARTA and ARTA graphics systems, made the 1967 plotter series (*Girl*, *Babel*, *Bikini Shifted*, *Beaver Scaled*, *Nested Squares*), the films *Art from Computers* (1971) and *Samplart* (1972, ten artists on ARTA), and completed the unpublished book *Computer Art: A Historic International Portfolio of the First Decade* (1972). He left the university and the field in 1977/78, worked as a financial planner and interfaith minister and editor, published the memoir *A Tapestry of Survival* (2019), donated his archive to the Art Gallery of Ontario (2022), and at 95 had his first solo exhibition at RCM Galerie, Paris (October 2026). He is living. [C38480]

**Relevance:** (1) Baecker's open question asks what a picture becomes when the message is made by many hands together, shared and negotiated in real time -- Mezei is the candidate to answer it from the side of early interactive graphics systems built for shared use. (2) The evidence is the doorway itself, the documented hop from #844, and Mezei's Toronto graphics work as the ranked-list continuation -- but the answer his record actually gives is narrower than the question asks, and this study states that honestly: no real-time multi-user system by Mezei exists, and the many-hands answer in his work is sequential and institutional -- one tool passed hand to hand (ARTA to ten artists to the *Samplart* film), one room and one record gathering many hands' pictures (the Dynamic Graphics Project, the annual bibliography, the 1972 book built from some four dozen contributors) -- plus a written vision, in 1964, of many designers each at their own console, stated as a conditional and never built. The study's value is therefore twofold: the strongest early grammar of controlled randomness in the whole chain, and an honest test of how far sequential many-hands can carry a question asked about simultaneity. [C38481]

**Doorway:** The 465th doorway. Takes up the open question from the Ronald Baecker study (#844), quoted verbatim: "If the hand's path is the message, what happens when the message is not drawn by one hand but made by many hands together -- when the gesture is shared, negotiated, and answered in real time, and the picture is what the group agrees it says?" Mezei answers it, but not in the tense the question asks. What he built was never simultaneous: SPARTA was batch (punch cards to plotter, no interaction at all) and ARTA was interactive but single-operator (one light pen, one animator at a time). What he did build, twice, was the sequence: in 1972 ten artists made animation with his interactive ARTA system and the results were sampled into one film, *Samplart* -- many hands in turn through one tool, the film the single picture they agreed it says -- and in the same year his book manuscript gathered pictures, explanations, and manifestos from some four dozen contributors into one account of the field. And in 1964, before he had made a drawing of his own, he wrote the simultaneity down as a conditional: "If a large parallel-processing computer with interrupt features were available, then each designer sitting at his own console could work with a display screen and keys for controlling his designs." The conditional was never built, by him or anywhere in his record; the relay was. So the picture becomes shared, in Mezei's record, not by many hands on one stylus at once but by one tool passed hand to hand, each hand answering the last segment, and by one room and one record that gather many hands' pictures into a single account. The open question carried forward is the one Baecker's critique of ARTA leaves standing: the animator in ARTA could draw images and movements but could not compute them -- the command set was fixed, rigid, not an open-ended programming system -- so what happens when the hand can teach the tool, when a demonstration becomes a program and the user extends the system by showing it what to do, instead of choosing from the commands it was given? [C38482]

## M1 -- The scale of randomness: a Markov chain with a sweet spot

**Subject:** A chessboard field coloured square by square, left to right, where the only parameter is how likely the colour is to change.

**Visual principle:** Order and chaos as two ends of one slider: pure repetition at the left, a perfect checkerboard at the right, and the interesting picture in between -- regular alternation occasionally broken by a continuation. The eye reads the break as the event; without the break there is nothing to look at.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own. A 9x9 board coloured by a first-order Markov chain, left-to-right, each row restarted at the left, the colour of each square decided from the previous square only. Transition probability 0.5 gives the middle board; 0.55, 0.60, 0.65 ... 1.00 step toward certain alternation (regular checkerboard at the right); 0.45, 0.40 ... 0.00 step toward certain continuation (pure repetition at the left). The most interesting zone is around transition probability **0.9** (third from right) -- regular alternation occasionally broken by a continuation. The piece holds a row of boards at stepped probabilities (0.00 to 1.00 in 0.05 steps, as Mezei drew them) and re-rolls each board on its own clock, so the sweet-spot board keeps breaking its own pattern in a new place each time while the end boards stay dead. The row-of-boards layout is Mezei's own figure; the re-rolling clock is this study's formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece whose entire argument is one number: the same rule, twenty-one times, and only one value is alive. The gallery has pieces driven by noise; none driven by the measured distance from noise to order.

**Takeaway:** At 0.9 the pattern breathes: regular enough to read, broken often enough to watch. [C38484]

## M2 -- Two distributions: uniform grass, normal hill

**Subject:** A field of marks whose density is decided by which distribution the random numbers come from.

**Visual principle:** Two kinds of plenty: uniform spread is even all the way across ("blades of grass on a lawn"), normal spread piles up in the middle and thins to the sides. The same count of marks, two different landscapes -- the distribution is visible as a shape before any mark is read singly.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own. Rectangular/uniform distribution (every value in the range equally likely) against normal/Gaussian distribution (values near the average most likely), illustrated by Mezei as spectral lines -- 100 lines over a 10-inch base. Re-running with a new random sequence gives a similar-looking but never identical drawing. The worked example is *O Canada* (1967): four symbols (beaver, maple leaf, centennial symbol, Expo symbol), pictorial elements arranged into clusters; cluster size by uniform distribution (min 4, max 8 -- equal chance of 4, 5, 6, 7 or 8 figures) or normal distribution (average 6, standard deviation 1, min 3, max 12 -- 95% of values fall in 3-9); each figure chosen with equal probability from the four (relative proportions could also be specified); size and orientation drawn from specified probability distributions; each finished cluster **scaled down to about one-eighth size** and drawn by the plotter; clusters -- and figures within them -- may overlap; "a considerable variation in size within the clusters, and between clusters, is necessary to obtain interesting results." The piece runs both distributions side by side on the same symbol set and re-rolls, so the viewer can see the uniform field stay even and the normal field keep piling at the centre. The side-by-side re-roll is this study's formalisation; the distributions, the cluster rules, and the 1/8 scaling are Mezei's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that teaches the eye the difference between two kinds of chance -- not by a chart but by two fields growing differently from the same seed of symbols. The cluster-and-shrink (1/8) is a compositional device the gallery has not used: big decisions made at full size, then shrunk into a crowd.

**Takeaway:** Same symbols, same count -- uniform spreads them like grass, normal piles them like a hill. [C38485]

## M3 -- One source, many distortions: the point list under RDSTP

**Subject:** A single face held in memory as a list of points, shaken, stretched, sliced, and shifted -- and then run backwards out of chaos.

**Visual principle:** Recognition as a threshold: at full distortion the face is gone, a jumble of lines; as the distortion eases, the face returns, and the moment it becomes readable is the piece's event. The drawing is never redrawn -- only the same points, moved.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own. The Girl is stored as the coordinates of **660 points** joined by straight lines and drawn by the plotter (1971 contemporary figure; "about 600" in the 2026 recollection). RDSTP ("random distort points") shakes it: each point moves horizontally by at most **0.3 inch**, with a normal distribution (average 0, standard deviation 0.3). The same program shook the Tower of Babel. SPARTA's command words -- MOVE, SIZE, ROTATE, FRAME, RDSTP -- are "commands, easily learned by a layman". Lines can be broken into (controlled) random sections; each line segment can shift, change size and be rotated. From stills to film: "Since the degree of transformation and distortion can be controlled, we can make a sequence of figures which are continuously transformed from one to the next, and thus produce a film. Running it backwards and starting from the unrecognizable jumbled figure, order and recognizability increase as the girl emerges from chaos." The piece holds one point-list figure and a single distortion parameter d (0 = the plain face, 1 = fully shaken past recognition), and sweeps d down from 1 to 0 and back, so the face emerges from chaos and dissolves into it again on a slow loop. The looping sweep is this study's formalisation; the point list, the RDSTP distribution, and the backwards-film idea are Mezei's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about recognisability itself: the face is never drawn or erased, only shaken, and the viewer watches meaning arrive and leave as a number changes. The gallery has morphs; none where the morph is a single named distortion applied to a fixed point list, with the original recoverable exactly at d = 0.

**Takeaway:** The face is never redrawn -- it is shaken out of the same 660 points, and it comes back when the shaking stops. [C38486]

## M4 -- Break the line, shift the line

**Subject:** One outline, cut into short sections that slide, turn, and change size independently.

**Visual principle:** A clean contour that comes apart in the hand: in one state the outline breaks into short dashes, in another it shivers into zigzags, in another the whole figure leans sideways as its lines slip horizontally. The figure stays the same figure -- only its lines have been allowed to move.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own. Lines can be broken into (controlled) random sections; each line segment can shift, change size and be rotated; *Bikini Shifted* is produced by shifting lines along the horizontal direction. The RCM descriptions of the Girl series give the visible outcomes: in one sheet the outline breaks into short dashes, in another it shivers into zigzags; *Girl Stretched* pulls her into a long tilted ellipse; *Girl Elongated* raises her to the top of a tall triangle with hair streaming sideways "like a pennant". The piece takes one closed outline, breaks it into N sections (N drawn from a controlled distribution, not fixed), and applies one shift rule per section -- horizontal slip by a normal-distributed amount, capped so the figure never leaves the frame -- then re-forms the outline and breaks it again differently. The re-form-and-re-break cycle is this study's formalisation; the break, the horizontal shift, and the named outcomes are Mezei's.

**Artistic opportunity:** The smallest possible transformation with the largest visible effect: the same outline, broken and shifted, reads as a different drawing each time, and the break itself -- dashes, zigzags -- is a texture the gallery has not used as a primary mechanism.

**Takeaway:** Cut the line and let the pieces slip: the figure survives, the contour changes its mind. [C38487]

## M5 -- The interpolation function is the drawing

**Subject:** Two key poses and the curve that connects them -- where the choice of curve, not the poses, decides what the motion feels like.

**Visual principle:** The same two poses, joined four different ways: evenly, easing in, easing out, overshooting and settling. The eye learns to read the in-between as the work -- the poses are the punctuation, the function is the sentence.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's and Baecker's shared lineage, distinguished here. ARTA is an interactive language based on light-pen control, with subroutines for key-frame interpolation -- the animator interactively draws two key frames and **describes the type of interpolation with a function** (Dietrich). Baecker classifies ARTA (with GENESYS and the Burtnyk-Wein system) as a *demonstrative* system: the animator sketches images and movements freehand, with immediate real-time playback -- as against *algorithmic* systems where the movie is described in a written language with no direct visual feedback. The difference from #844 matters and is stated plainly: Baecker's GENESYS records a gesture as a p-curve whose point spacing IS the speed (the hand's timing, replayed); Mezei's ARTA takes two drawn key frames and a *chosen* interpolation function and computes the frames between them; the piece cycles that function through a named set (linear, ease, sine, overshoot) chosen by this study. The piece holds two key frames fixed and cycles the interpolation function through a named set, drawing the in-between path each function produces as a visible trail, so the same two poses make four different motions. The function-cycle and the visible trail are this study's formalisation; the two-key-frames-plus-function structure is Mezei's (via Dietrich).

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about the most ordinary decision in animation -- how to get from A to B -- made visible as four different drawings of the same journey. It is the complement, not the repeat, of #844's p-curve: there the hand's timing is recorded; here the timing is chosen.

**Takeaway:** The poses are fixed; the function does the drawing. Change the function and the same two poses tell a different story. [C38488]

## M6 -- One system, many hands in sequence: the Samplart model

**Subject:** A single shared tool, passed from hand to hand, each hand leaving its segment in one accumulating film.

**Visual principle:** A film that visibly changes authorship: each segment carries a different hand's line quality and choices, yet all segments pass through the same tool's grammar, so the whole holds together as one picture made by many. The seams between hands are visible and are the point.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own, honestly framed. *Samplart* (1972) "samples animation made by ten artists with Leslie's interactive ARTA system" -- ten artists, sequentially, through one system, sampled into one film. That is the closest Mezei comes to multi-author work and the single strongest concrete case of many hands in his record. ARTA itself is interactive but single-operator: one light pen, one artist at a time, key frames drawn by one hand. No source describes simultaneous shared drawing, turn-taking at one display, or negotiated real-time gesture. The 1964 multi-console vision ("each designer sitting at his own console") is a conditional proposal, not a built system. The piece formalises the *Samplart* sequence as a relay: a fixed-length film is divided into segments; each segment is produced by one "hand" -- a distinct transformation hand (one shifts, one stretches, one shakes, one slices, one re-orders) applied through the same command vocabulary (MOVE, SIZE, ROTATE, FRAME, RDSTP); segments accumulate in order and the finished film plays straight through, seams visible. The viewer may take one turn as a hand (choose one transformation for the next segment), then the relay continues without them. The relay formalisation and the viewer-as-one-hand are this study's; the ten-artists-one-system-one-film structure is Mezei's. No simultaneous multi-user ARTA is claimed or built. The ten artists are unnamed in every source read and are not named here.

**Artistic opportunity:** The only honest answer this study can give to #844's doorway: the picture becomes shared not by many hands on one stylus at once, but by one tool passed hand to hand, each hand answering the last segment. The gallery has no piece whose authorship is sequential and visible as a relay.

**Takeaway:** One tool, ten hands in turn: the film is what the group agreed it says, one segment at a time. [C38489]

## M7 -- The measure that picks: Birkhoff's M as a selector

**Subject:** Many variations, one score -- and the picture that survives is the one the measure chose.

**Visual principle:** A field of candidate drawings, most of them faint, one of them bold: the chosen one. The rejected runs stay visible as ghosts, so the viewer sees both the choice and what was refused. Selection itself becomes the composition.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own, with the scoring rule as this study's formalisation. Birkhoff's aesthetic measure: M = order / complexity. For polygons, order elements are vertical symmetry, stability, rotational symmetry, horizontal-vertical network; complexity is the number of distinct lines containing at least one side. Birkhoff rated about 90 polygons; the square scored highest at 1.5, some complex figures scored negative. Mezei proposes extending this with weighted scores for repetition, similarity, contrast, equality, symmetry, balance, sequence, centre of interest, then testing formulas against expert judges. And Mezei's own working rule for variation: the program "embodies the generating rules, or the algorithm, which with random selection is capable of producing an infinite variety of similar pictures. The creativity is in the choice of the algorithm, not in the grinding out of the variations" -- and Birkhoff-style measures are "not yet accurate enough" to automate selection of the most pleasing variation; the maker chooses from many runs. The piece generates N variations of one figure under controlled randomness, scores each by a stated M (order counted as symmetry + repetition + balance terms, complexity counted as distinct line orientations, both computed from the drawing itself and shown on the surface), keeps the highest-M variation bold and the rest as faint ghosts, then re-rolls. The formula's weights and the ghost display are this study's formalisation; M = order/complexity, the about-90-polygon ratings, and the "maker chooses from many runs" rule are Mezei's. The piece admits on its surface that the measure is approximate -- Mezei's own caveat.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that performs its own criticism in public, with the score computed from the drawing rather than asserted. It is distinct from #844's M10 (taxonomy self-scoring: six subjective categories, admittedly subjective): here the score is one formula, computed from line counts and symmetries, and the selection is the visible act.

**Takeaway:** The machine grinds out the variations; the measure picks one -- and shows you the ones it refused. [C38490]

## M8 -- The variation sheet: distance from the plain face

**Subject:** Nine variations on one sheet, arranged by how far each has travelled from the original.

**Visual principle:** A map of distance, not a grid of equals: the plain face sits at the centre, and the further a variation sits from the centre, the more transformed it is -- the eye travels from the centre out, as Mezei's viewer does, and reads transformation as distance.

**Computational mechanism:** Mezei's own. Nine images set against black on a single sheet: the plain face at the centre, its most distant relatives at the edges -- the eye travels from the centre out. The series-as-a-sheet: the variation set displayed as one composition. The piece computes a transformation distance for each variation (a stated sum: point displacement under RDSTP + scale change under SIZE + rotation under ROTATE + section-break count), places the plain figure at the centre, and arranges eight variations around it in rings by that distance -- nearest ring small shakes, outer ring full stretch/shake/slice combinations -- re-rolling the variations while keeping the distance ordering. The distance metric and the ring layout rule are this study's formalisation; the centre-plain / edges-distant sheet is Mezei's (RCM description).

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that is a map of its own possibility space: the sheet shows not nine random faces but the whole journey from plain to distant, ordered so the viewer can read how far each one has gone. The gallery has grids; none ordered by a computed distance from an origin.

**Takeaway:** The centre is the face as drawn; every step outward is a transformation further from home. [C38491]

## Mechanism note -- what is Mezei's and what is not

SPARTA's commands (MOVE, SIZE, ROTATE, FRAME, RDSTP), the 660-point Girl, the RDSTP distribution (normal, mean 0, SD 0.3 inch), the Markov scale of randomness with the about-0.9 sweet spot, the *O Canada* cluster rules (uniform 4-8 / normal mean 6 SD 1, clusters scaled to about 1/8, overlapping), the backwards-film idea, the nine-variations sheet, *Samplart*'s ten-artists-one-system structure, Birkhoff's M = order/complexity and the about-90-polygon ratings, the 1964 multi-console conditional, and the 120x120 circle experiment are Mezei's (or, for ARTA's interpolation, Mezei's via Dietrich/Baecker). The fusions -- the re-rolling board row, the side-by-side distribution re-roll, the looping distortion sweep, the re-form-and-re-break cycle, the function-cycle with visible trail, the relay formalisation with viewer-as-one-hand, the stated M weights with ghost display, the transformation-distance metric and ring layout -- are this study's own formalisations, labelled as such where they appear. The 1964 multi-console vision is a proposal, never a built system, and is never presented as one. The ten *Samplart* artists are unnamed in every source read and are not named here. The Girl is 660 points per the 1971 contemporary text; the 2026 "about 600" is a recollection and is not used as the figure. [C38492]

## The arc: from the chronicler, to the maker, to the turn away, to the archive that returns

Mezei's career reads as three phases, and the arc is unusually clean. First, "the chronicler before the maker" (1964-1966): he writes the first article and the first criticism, gives the first lecture, and proposes the sharing network -- all *before* he has made a drawing of his own. The 1964 essay predates his first plotter work by three years. The through-line is already collective: the work is to gather the field, name it, and circulate it. Second, "the maker" (1967-1972): one year, 1967, holds almost everything visual -- the SPARTA drawings (one source, many transformations), the exhibitions, the bibliography, the *Artscanada* column. Then the systems deepen (ARTA's light pen and keyframes), the films arrive (*Art from Computers* 1971, *Samplart* 1972), and the book manuscript (1972) tries to gather the whole first decade into one volume. The mechanism that recurs is **one source, many transformations**: one 660-point face, one word BABEL, run through shift/scale/shake/slice/stretch. Third, "the turn away and the late return" (1978-2026): he leaves the university and the field in 1977/78, becomes a financial planner and an interfaith minister/editor, and writes the memoir (2019). He does not resume making. What returns instead is the *archive*: renewed interest from the 2009 DGP 40th anniversary, Nake's 2015 edit of the 1972 book, the 2019 foreword, the 2020 survey, the 2022 AGO donation, and finally the first solo show at 95 in 2026. The late phase is other people gathering *him* -- the mirror image of phase 1, where he gathered the field. What changed technically: batch plotter work (SPARTA, IBM 7094 + CalComp, offline) to interactive work (ARTA, light pen, keyframe interpolation, on-screen) to film as the distribution form for interactive work (*Samplart* samples ten artists' ARTA animation because the interactive system itself could not tour). What did not change: the conviction, stated in his own 1976 essay and echoed in the title of the 2023 "Controlled Randomness" essay (not read; paywalled), that randomness must be *controlled* -- chance inside a chosen transformation, never raw noise. This arc paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-2, not Mezei's own words. [C38493]

## The aesthetic logic

Mezei's aesthetic is chance inside a chosen frame, and the frame stated on the surface. The 1964 rule -- "Randomness must be controlled and continuity maintained" -- governs everything after it: the Markov board is interesting only at 0.9 because the rule holds the pattern together while letting it break; the Girl is recognisable only because the shake is capped at 0.3 inch and distributed normally, small distortions more frequent than large ones; the *O Canada* clusters work only because size varies by rule, within the cluster and between clusters. The medium is the black line on white paper because the plotter drew lines and the command vocabulary (MOVE, SIZE, ROTATE, FRAME, RDSTP) is a vocabulary of lines -- the sparseness is the mechanism's own shape, not a style applied afterwards. And the work is shown as a set, not a single: the nine-variations sheet, the twenty-one boards, the ten-artist film, the four-dozen-contributor book -- the picture is the variation set, the comparison across runs, the record of many hands. Selection is part of the work and is admitted as unfinished: Birkhoff's measure is "not yet accurate enough" to pick the best variation, so the maker chooses from many runs, and the choosing is shown rather than hidden. This paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-2, not Mezei's own words. [C38494]
