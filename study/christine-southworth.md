**Site:** Christine Southworth (b. 1978, Boston, Massachusetts; living), American composer and video artist; lecturer in MIT Music and Theater Arts; a post-minimalist composer of what she calls "post-minimalist acoustic electronica" -- a transduction artist who turns the behaviour of natural and mechanical systems into sound and image: a spider's web becomes a harp, a mushroom colony becomes an oscillator, a melting snowflake and a pond's wave pattern become a score, a Van de Graaff generator becomes a percussion instrument, and a robot becomes the player. Christine Southworth was born in 1978 in Boston, Massachusetts, and is based in Lexington, Massachusetts. Her father is Bill Southworth, one of the inventors of MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface), and at age five she joined her father's music technology business, tasked with erasing disks to free up storage space, and got to play with early versions of what became MIDI. Her own account of what MIDI did to her composing is the earliest statement of her method: "With MIDI, you can make music by almost sculpting it visually rather than writing down musical notes... Later on, I could make a piece of music almost like I could make a piece of pottery. It allowed me to write the music I wanted to hear." Composing as shaping, not as notation -- the visual-to-sound working method she has never left. She entered MIT as a physics major, found lab work isolating, and switched to mathematics, taking a B.S. in Mathematics in 2002 with a minor in music, jazz and world music classes alongside, and joining ensembles including Gamelan Galak Tika. She took an M.A. in Computer Music and Multimedia Composition at Brown University (MEME program), 2004-2006, studying with Shep Shapiro and continuing composition study with Evan Ziporyn, and was a fellow of the first annual Bang on a Can Summer Institute at Mass MOCA in 2002. She is a lecturer in MIT Music and Theater Arts, and teaches electronic and Balinese music composition to children and adults in Cambridge and Boston. [C29246]

The turn that made the career came in 1999, on a trip to Bali, Indonesia, where she began studying gender wayang -- the Balinese metallophones that accompany shadow puppet plays -- with I Wayan Loceng in the village of Sukawati. On returning to the United States, Arnold Dreyblatt's Orchestra of Excited Strings premiered her short piece Timor Cotton at MIT Kresge Auditorium, built from the styles she had learned in Bali: non-Western ensemble practice learned by apprenticeship, then recombined with Western ensembles and electronics. She has been a member of MIT-based Gamelan Galak Tika since 1999, was its general manager from 2004 through 2013, performs Balinese gamelan and gender wayang with the group, has composed several pieces for it, and has played with it at Lincoln Center, Carnegie Hall, EMPAC, the Cleveland Museum of Art, Bang on a Can Marathons and the Bali International Arts Festival. About two years before the 2005 MIT News interview she decided she "needed to make robots to play my music, because it was too hard for people to play" -- a notation problem, in her words -- and because she wanted to play electronic music, as a MIDI sequence, on real instruments. Her friend Leila Hasan builds robots; they applied for grants and Ensemble Robot was born, with LEF Foundation support, co-founded in 2003. Nature sonification came later and is now her dominant practice: spiders, mushrooms, snowflakes, water waves, coral reefs, honeybees, volcanoes. The through-line is consistent across all sources: a natural system produces data or a signal, the signal is mapped to sound, and she improvises live with or against it. She calls her music "post-minimalist acoustic electronica", amplified, and names her influences as Vivaldi, Bach, Steve Reich, Terry Riley, Michael Gordon, Louis Andriessen, Arnold Dreyblatt, Meredith Monk and Evan Ziporyn, plus Kraftwerk, the Beatles, A Tribe Called Quest, electronica, jazz, hip-hop, rock and roll, video game music and gamelan. "I would say my music is pretty; it rocks, grooves, and follows no rules really." Her Brown teacher Shep Shapiro's line: "This music doesn't break the rules; it rather renders the rules obsolete." [C29247]

## M1 -- Laser-scan to 3D network to notes: the web as score

The most fully documented mapping in her practice, and the one the whole study turns on. Spider's Canvas / Arachnodrone (2018) starts from the web of a real South American spider, Cyrtophora citricola: the natural web is scanned with a laser to capture 2D cross-sections, computer algorithms reconstruct the web's 3D network from thousands of images, and different frequencies of sound are assigned to strands of the web, creating notes combined in patterns based on the web's 3D structure to generate melodies, which a harp-like instrument then plays live. The physics behind the mapping is the vibrating-string equation: strand tension and length determine frequency, exactly as with a guitar or violin string, in Markus Buehler's words. The mechanism is transduction with a physical law doing the translation: the web's geometry is the score, the string law is the notation, and nothing in the mapping is arbitrary -- a longer or looser strand sounds lower, a shorter or tighter one higher, because that is what the strand would do if you plucked it. The watchable behaviour is a three-dimensional structure becoming audible in the order its strands lie: the audience sits inside a cube of projections and hears the web's shape as a chord field, with Southworth's own photographs of webs layered over the scanned model so the real web and the measured web are visible at once. Seen in W17. [C29249]

**Takeaway:** scan the structure, rebuild it as a graph, and let the string law set the pitches. [C29249]

## M2 -- Hearing distance: only the near fibres sound

Inside the web instrument, audibility is a spatial window that a performer plays. Isabelle Su controls which part of the web the viewer sees, while Ian Hattwick "plays with the hearing distance": all fibres within that distance, shown blue and white, are sonified, while fibres outside it, shown green and brown, stay visible but silent. The mechanism is a moving audibility radius inside a network: the web is always fully present to the eye, but only the part near the viewer is present to the ear, and the boundary between sounding and silent moves as the performance moves. The watchable behaviour is a field of threads lighting blue and white as they enter the radius and falling green and brown as they leave it, the sound following the light, so the audience can see exactly why a note is sounding or not. Seen in W17. [C29250]

**Takeaway:** give the network a hearing distance, and let only the near threads sing. [C29250]

## M3 -- Construction as composition: the music tracks the making

The web team did not only scan a finished web. They scanned a web during construction, transforming each stage of the building into music with different sounds, so the sound tracks the temporal sequence of making: as the spider adds strands, the instrument gains notes, and the piece's form is the building's chronology. The mechanism is process as form: the work does not illustrate a structure, it replays the structure's growth, and the audience hears the web get built strand by strand. The watchable behaviour is a sparse, tentative texture that thickens as the web thickens, new pitches arriving with new threads, the music learning its own shape in real time. Seen in W17. [C29251]

**Takeaway:** scan the thing while it is being made, and let the making be the form. [C29251]

## M4 -- Stretch and snap: force becomes pitch, breakage becomes event

Deforming the web changes what it sounds like, because the mapping is physical. Stretching the web changes string tension and therefore pitch, in real time; strands break with a snapping sound. The mechanism is force-to-pitch mapping with a failure mode built in: pull a strand and its note bends upward, pull too far and the note does not bend further, it breaks, and the break is itself an audible event. The watchable behaviour is a note that rises under tension and then stops with a crack, the web visibly thinner by one strand, the instrument permanently changed by the gesture that played it. Seen in W17. [C29252]

**Takeaway:** let tension bend the pitch, and let the break be a sound. [C29252]

## M5 -- Mycelium biofeedback: the living colony is the oscillator

Mushroom Modulations (2023) replaces the scanned structure with a living one. Electrodes are placed on different parts of live mushroom colonies Southworth has grown; the slight electrical variations given off by the mycelium networks -- thought to be used as communication between different fungal fruiting bodies -- are converted to sound through biofeedback devices hooked up to an analog synthesizer. The mechanism is live biofeedback, not a recording: the electrical signal is the performance input in real time, and the analog synthesizer is the voice, so the organism is the oscillator and the performer is its accompanist. "Each time I play them it sounds different," she says, because the colony is different each time. No source read states the voltage-to-pitch mapping rule, and none is asserted here; what is documented is the loop -- living signal in, sound out, human improvising on top. The watchable behaviour is a performer sitting still among logs and photographs while the sound moves on its own, the mushrooms visibly unchanged and the music visibly theirs. Seen in W18. [C29253]

**Takeaway:** wire the living thing to the synthesizer, and play what it is already saying. [C29253]

## M6 -- The Mush Room: enclosure as instrument

The performance of Mushroom Modulations is staged inside a room Southworth built in 2023, a three-walled room made of shoji screens used as projection screens. Her photographs of mushroom colonies surround her as she sits in the room, with mushrooms she has grown and fallen logs covered in mushrooms from the forest, and using biofeedback devices hooked up to an analog synthesizer, she performs the mushrooms. The mechanism is immersion by enclosure: the performer sits inside her own documentation of the organism, inside the organism's own material, and the room is both the stage and the score's cover -- the photographs show the colonies growing and fading through the seasons while the live colonies on the logs sound in the present. The watchable behaviour is a small lit room inside a dark garden, a figure seated among glowing photographs and living logs, the walls themselves carrying the images of what is being played. Seen in W18. [C29254]

**Takeaway:** build a small room from the subject's own images, and sit inside it to play. [C29254]

## M7 -- Water waves: the pond is the score, the guitar is the response

NAMI (2026) is an ever-evolving, modular audiovisual collaboration with artist and photographer Jun Hoshino. They transform the "gently chaotic wave patterns of moving water into layered sonic and visual environments": Hoshino's unfiltered iPhone framings of the spontaneous luminosity of New England ponds and streams "are literally made into sound by Southworth's data sonifications, then layered into electric guitar textures that produce a distinct soundscape for each video." The mechanism is video-to-sound transduction with modularity: each video yields a distinct soundscape, because each stretch of water has its own pattern, and the performance pairs a different set of water videos with a different soundscape each time -- the water is the score and the guitar is the response. The watchable behaviour is unfiltered phone footage of a pond's surface, light moving on water, with a guitar texture that follows the water's own rhythm, never the same water twice and never the same sound twice. Seen in W20. [C29255]

**Takeaway:** let the water write the piece, and answer it on guitar. [C29255]

## M8 -- Melting snowflakes: the melt is the form

Snowflake Sonifications (2026) transforms microscopic images of melting snowflakes into evolving sound and visual environments that respond in real time. The snowflakes' organic structures -- delicate, unpredictable, and constantly shifting as they melt -- drive the generative sounds and imagery, and Southworth on electric guitar and Evan Ziporyn on electronic wind instrument improvise against that generative layer, "creating a dialogue with the generative sounds and imagery that blurs the line between nature's patterns and human expression." The mechanism is a one-way process made audible: a snowflake only melts, it never un-melts, so the piece has an irreversible direction built into its material -- the structure simplifies and disappears as it sounds, and the improvisers answer a system that is visibly running out. The watchable behaviour is a magnified flake losing its branches on screen while the sound thins with it, two live players holding a conversation with something that is leaving. The image-to-pitch mapping rule is unstated in every source read and is not asserted here. Seen in W19. [C29256]

**Takeaway:** take a process that only runs one way, and let the improvisers talk to it while it goes. [C29256]

## M9 -- High-voltage percussion: lightning as downbeat

Zap! Music for Van de Graaff Generator, Tesla Coils, Instruments and Voices treats the Boston Museum of Science's 40-foot Van de Graaff generator -- the largest of its kind in the world, capable of up to 1.5 million volts, designed and built at MIT in the 1930s by Professor Robert J. Van de Graaff and given to the museum in 1956 -- as "a combination percussive instrument and light show": huge sparks of lightning give deafening cracks that punctuate the music, while at lower settings it makes a glowing corona with a sweet humming buzz. Tesla coils surround the generator; Southworth and Alexandra Andersson trigger the zaps, and Leila Hasan controls variations in voltage using her MIT thesis project, a musical interface and sensing device inspired by the Theremin that she calls a "termenova". Around the generator, human musicians -- flute, keyboards, cello, guitar, bass, drums, voice, Lyricon -- play alongside Ensemble Robot machines, including the double-helix-shaped robotic xylophone, the Heliphon. The mechanism is electricity as percussion: the spark is the downbeat, the corona is the drone, and the voltage is a fader the performers ride. The watchable behaviour is lightning timed against an ensemble, the flash and the crack landing together, the room lit by the instrument itself. The premiere year is carried as a conflict, not resolved: Wikipedia dates the first performance to 4 February 2004, while MIT News implies February 2005, and the MIT News preview of 2 February 2005 calls the premiere "Friday, Feb. 4" -- a Friday that fits 2005, not 2004. Seen in W06. [C29257]

**Takeaway:** make the lightning the drummer, and let the voltage be the fader. [C29257]

## M10 -- Robots play MIDI on real instruments

Ensemble Robot, co-founded by Southworth and Leila Hasan in 2003, is a cooperative of engineers, artists and musicians inventing robotic musical instruments, and its point, per Southworth, is to play MIDI sequences on real instruments: electronic music made physical. The robots produce simple and complex patterns of sound from acoustic sources including strings, pipes, drums and wooden keys. The Heliphon is a MIDI-controlled, double-helix-shaped robotic xylophone that strikes metal keys with solenoids -- a machine percussionist with inhuman speed and precision, which Ziporyn's Belle Labs, premiered on the same 2006 concert as Heavy Metal, treats as a performer with its own timing that never bends tempo. The mechanism is a partner that will not accommodate you: the machine has its own tempo, its own voice, and the humans must play to it. The watchable behaviour is humans chasing a tempo that never wavers, metal keys struck by solenoids in patterns no hand would choose. Seen in W06, W07 and W08. [C29258]

**Takeaway:** give the machine its own tempo, and make the humans keep up. [C29258]

## M11 -- The retunable ensemble: one gesture retunes everybody

Super Collider (2010) builds a whole electronic gamelan so a composer can retune an entire ensemble at once. Gamelan Elektrika, designed and developed by Alex Rigopulos, founder and CEO of Harmonix, is a "virtual gamelan": the players strike sensor-fitted instruments instead of bronze keys, and the sensors trigger electronic gamelan sounds, so the composer can alter the sonic environment globally -- "not just for one instrument at a time but for the complete ensemble", in Southworth's program note. The piece is inspired by the Large Hadron Collider at CERN: two sound worlds collide the way particles collide -- the Kronos Quartet tradition against ancient gamelan performance practice, "unleashed through the unlimited sonic universe of electronics". The mechanism is global retune as a compositional gesture: one control changes every instrument's voice at once, so the ensemble's tuning becomes a parameter the composer plays in real time rather than a fact the instruments arrive with. The watchable behaviour is gamelan players striking familiar gestures while unfamiliar sounds come out, the whole ensemble changing colour together at a single cue, the quartet and the gamelan colliding and separating like particles. Seen in W13. [C29259]

**Takeaway:** let one gesture retune the whole ensemble at once. [C29259]

## M12 -- Two tuning worlds at once: together in rhythm, apart in key

Heavy Metal (2006) puts two harmonic worlds on one stage and refuses to merge them. The Balinese Gamelan Gong Kebyar plays in its own five-note tuning -- a variation of E major: C-sharp, D-sharp, E, G, A -- while the Western instruments and robots work through the same material at the same time but deliberately stay out of the gamelan's key, expanding the sound universe to a full chromatic spectrum. Rhythmically the two sides are together; harmonically they coexist without merging. The robots are the showpiece: the Bot(i)Cello, built by Southworth with Giles Hall and Andy Cavatorta, uses windshield-wiper motors to reel in guitar strings, plucked by computer fans rotating at varying speeds, the strings sitting on bows of tempered spring steel at constant tension, so reeling a string in raises its pitch -- it looks, Southworth says, "like a three-legged spider, or perhaps a strange metal tree" -- and the BlowBot, by Cavatorta, is a dancing tetrahedron of air cylinders expanding from 2 to 4 feet, one of 12 flutes sounding as each of its six cylinders expands and contracts. The piece is based on American hard rock of the late 1970s through the early 1990s, and it was the first time a traditional Balinese gamelan shared a stage with robotic instruments. The mechanism is refusal of the blend: the seam is the material, and a piece that resolved the two tunings into one would have nothing to say. The watchable behaviour is two tuning grids laid over each other, the beats where they disagree shimmering, the piece never settling into one key, while a three-legged spider of a machine plucks its own strings beside them. Seen in W07. [C29260]

**Takeaway:** do not resolve the tuning clash -- the clash is the piece. [C29260]

## M13 -- The field-recording travelogue: place as sample library

In My Mind and In My Car (2013-2014), co-composed with Ziporyn, is an electroacoustic travelogue of "objects to be held onto, material and otherwise": Ziporyn plays clarinets live against samples and electronics drawn from archival recordings made in Bali, Tanzania and the Scottish Highlands, while Southworth's videos show processed footage from beneath mangrove islands in Panama, among stone quarries in the Sierra Nevada, and on Route 225 between Bedford and Carlisle -- the title track shot entirely from the windshield of a yellow 1979 MGB, and the piece test-driven, literally, on that car's sound system en route to Walden Pond. The mechanism is the archive as a living layer: the past is not quoted, it plays alongside the present, and the live performer drives through it. The watchable behaviour is a soloist moving through his own recorded past -- live wind against field recordings and road footage, the car as concert hall. Seen in W15. [C29261]

**Takeaway:** let the recording play beside the performer, not behind it. [C29261]

## M14 -- The multilingual cantata: four languages, one border

Borderland: A Cantata for Ukraine (2014), co-composed with Ziporyn for Roomful of Teeth, is a four-movement cantata in memory of the victims of the Ukrainian conflict, and its mechanism is language as territory. The first two movements view the war from the vantage of Malaysian Airlines Flight MH17, shot down over Ukraine on 17 July 2014 -- first from the air, then from the ground -- and the last two movements turn to Ukrainian poetry by Taras Shevchenko, Ivan Franko and Bekir Coban-Zade, set with overtones of birdsong, chirping and chattering behind longer, keening vocal lines. The piece grew out of news Southworth and Ziporyn followed in polyglot media while in Croatia the previous summer, and "Ukraine" itself means borderland, edge or outskirts. Southworth contributes video; a still from the video accompanying the fourth movement is reproduced on the MIT Arts site. The singers performed from sheet music, conducted by Brad Wells -- unusual for Roomful of Teeth, which normally performs from memory. The mechanism is documentary polyglossia: each movement speaks in a different documentary register -- the air, the ground, the national poets -- and the timbre evokes the linguistic richness at borders. The watchable behaviour is eight voices shifting language and register movement by movement, birdsong behind keening lines, video carrying the images the voices cannot. Seen in W16. [C29262]

**Takeaway:** let each movement speak a different language of the same border. [C29262]

## Aesthetic logic

Southworth's logic is transduction with the human kept in the loop as an improviser, not as an author. Against Ziporyn, whose music knows it is a translation and shows you the translation happening, Southworth's work knows it is a measurement and shows you the measurement happening: the laser slice, the electrode, the microscopic photograph, the unfiltered phone video, the spark gap -- each one is a way of listening to something that was never trying to be heard, and the piece is the listening made audible. What changes across twenty-six years is not the question but the size of the system doing the sounding: first her own hands on gender wayang, then robots she built, then a generator the size of a room, then a spider's web, then a mushroom colony, then a snowflake, then a pond -- each step handing more of the authorship to the system itself, and each step keeping the same rule, that the voice is borrowed and the borrowing is audible. She refuses the blend twice over: the gamelan keeps its tuning against the Western instruments, and the natural signal keeps its own behaviour against the improvisation -- the snowflake melts on its own schedule, the mushroom fires on its own, the web breaks when it breaks. The meaning is the making, and the making is shared: the scanned strand, the wired fungus, the filmed wave, the named spider -- because the work is only honest about what it cost when the cost is on view, and only true when the eye can see the join between the thing and its sound. [C29263]
