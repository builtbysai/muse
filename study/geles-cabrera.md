# Geles Cabrera: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #337.

**Site:** Angeles Maria Cabrera Alvarado, known as Geles Cabrera (Mexico City, 2 August 1926), Mexican sculptor. The first woman to practice sculpture professionally in Mexico and the first Mexican woman sculptor to exhibit individually, in a first solo show at Galeria Mont-Orendain, Colonia Roma, dated 1948 in the El Eco telling and 1949 in the Bellas Artes telling, age 22 in both. Founder in 1966 of the Museo Escultorico Geles Cabrera, a garden museum in her own house at Calle Xicotencatl 181, Del Carmen, Coyoacan, kept free to enter for four decades on her own resources. Member of GUCADIGOSE, the five-hand collective that built walkable public sculpture in Villahermosa, Tabasco, in 1975-1976. A career of seventy years in which the same body is reduced, multiplied, and re-materialized: terracotta, direct-carved stone, bronze, jade, wood, wire, natural fiber, thermoformed plexiglas, papier-mache, newspaper, cut sheet metal. Identity anchors: condensation, not description; the T-incision; the stone stays a stone; the body as a score; recline as the default state; multiplication of the erotic sign; material migration; suspension and precarious balance; the transitable monument; the museum as self-history; the archive as a work; cheap material as access; the synesthetic score; the group as a corridor; the ceremonial group.

**Doorway:** Hop from the angela-gurria study: from Gurria's stone rhythm and the GUCADIGOSE circle to Cabrera, the close friend whose stone carving shaped Gurria's own. #336 closed with a sculptor who listened to matter and let rhythm, not force, decide where the chisel lands, and who built her signal as a pair with exactly one difference. #337 opens with the woman on the other side of that friendship. Teofilo Cohen, Gurria's gallerist at Proyectos Monclova, said it plainly in El Universal's Gurria obituary: Cabrera and Gurria were very good friends, and one can see the mutual influence they had on each other's work, above all in stone carving. Gurria planted a signal on a road; Cabrera planted a museum in a garden and a monument you walk through. Where Gurria's pair reads at car speed, Cabrera's body reads at walking speed, one reduction at a time, in a house where the niches are frames and the paths are galleries. The doorway is the friendship itself: two women carving stone in the same city, teaching each other by working beside each other, and then building, each in her own way, the institution that would not have them.

## M1. Condensation, not description

The governing reduction. Early heads carry eyes and mouths expressing astonishment or contemplation; over time they are reduced to lines and planes that suggest the form without describing it (MPBA text). The 1954 stone viewed first-hand already shows the discipline at work: a seated figure in pale veined stone, head tilted with one circular eye, torso twisting, a horizontal slot cut through the base block, tool marks and veining left visible. It is simplified, not naturalistic, in 1954, and the simplification only deepens. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M7) reduced the figure to silhouette plus one incision; Cabrera reduces it further, to a line and a plane, and keeps reducing across seventy years until the body is a suggestion the viewer completes. Escobedo (#335 M11) assembled a crowd from one prop and one motor; Cabrera assembles a body from one curve and one cut. **Takeaway:** Shed features until one curve or one incision carries the body, then stop. Watch the shedding: when a figure starts nearly legible and loses features one by one until only silhouette and cut remain, and the viewer still reads it as a body, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The T-incision

A T-shaped volume or cut crosses a face or torso and becomes an alphabet. Mascara o Cabeza (1966), bronze with dark patina, defines the face by two volumes in T crossing it. The same T reappears as the chest incision of Sin titulo 1985, viewed first-hand: a reclining bronze figure, head a smooth ball, torso marked by a T, one leg bent up so the knee makes a triangular hole of negative space. It reappears again as the small T head of the ca. 1990 cut-sheet metal figure, viewed first-hand, whose torso is an openwork frame with triangular teeth pointing inward. One mark, three materials, three decades, and a mask, a torso, and a stencil read as one letter. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M7) used one vertical groove per face; Cabrera uses one letter across the whole body, and the letter is the signature that survives even her unsigned late work. Goeritz (#321) built emblems to be read against the sky; Cabrera stamps her emblem onto the body itself. **Takeaway:** Stamp one mark across materials and decades; the repetition is the identity. Watch the T: when the same T turns up on a mask, a torso, and a stencil, and each still reads as the same hand, the mechanism is on.

## M3. The stone stays a stone

Pedro Reyes's coinage is "piedrez": her sculptures are also stones, an organic quality with a vocation for the outdoors. She avoids polished marble, then the prevailing finish, and carves directly in stone and wood and piedra de Xaltocan, keeping the material's own character: porous volcanic surface, veining, rough pocks, tool marks. The two dark volcanic figures viewed first-hand prove it: the pocked surface is unmistakable, and the stone's holes and veins do as much work as the carved contour. The 1954 stone keeps its veining as a visible stripe system running through the figure. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M1) listened to stone for its pitch before fracture; Cabrera listens for its texture and lets the texture stay loud. Escobedo (#335 M7) carried nothing and built with whatever was nearby and cheap; Cabrera carries the stone's own weather with her, and the weather is part of the form. **Takeaway:** Never polish away what the material already says. Watch the surface: when the veining, the pocks, and the tool marks are doing as much work as the contour, the mechanism is on.

## M4. The body as a score

The Partituras corporales title is the mechanism: a movement laid out as a sequence of stills, so time is read across space. The MPBA text calls the works records of gesture, movement, displacement, and memory, a choreographic sculpture; sequences of pieces register a series of movements. The Partituras hang viewed first-hand shows it spatially: a crowd of small dark bronze figures on white plinths at staggered heights, seated, kneeling, reclining, one tall standing figure at the rear. No single figure is the dance; the row is. A leg higher here, a torso turned there, and the eye animates the sequence. Against the chain: Escobedo (#335 M3) repeated one frame and let one parameter drift until a snake emerged; Cabrera repeats one body and lets one parameter drift until a dance emerges, and the drift is choreographic, not serial. Gurria (#336 M2) wound one curve until it was a shell or a river; Cabrera lays one body down in sequence until it is a step. **Takeaway:** Lay the movement out as a row of near-identical bodies with one parameter drifting. Watch the row: when the eye animates a sequence that no single figure contains, the mechanism is on.

## M5. Recline as the default state

The characteristic pose is lying, seated, or at rest, not standing in triumph. Figura recostada, Dama recostada boca arriba, the ca. 1970 volcanic odalisque with voluptuous breasts and a full set of male genitals, Sin titulo 1985 reclining, the V2 cluster of seated and kneeling figures: the horizontal is the heroic axis. A monument that reclines refuses the monument's vertical, and that refusal is the statement. Her public work is transitable (M9); her private work is recumbent. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M5) built towers you walk among; Cabrera builds bodies you lie beside, and the scale relationship reverses, the viewer stands over a reclining figure the way a visitor stands over a grave or a sleeper. Escobedo (#335) built gates you pass through; Cabrera builds bodies you rest beside. **Takeaway:** Make the horizontal the heroic axis; refuse the standing triumph. Watch the rest: when the most monumental figure in the room is lying down, the mechanism is on.

## M6. Multiplication of the erotic sign

Breasts, phalli, and labial forms are doubled or repeated until they stop being anatomy and become pattern. Figura de cuatro pechos (1978), bronze, 22 cm tall, viewed first-hand: a compact upright figure, swollen belly, arms raised framing a rounded head, several rounded breast-like protrusions on the chest. The ca. 1970 volcanic odalisque carries breasts and male genitals on one body. Two small terracottas, ca. 1983, approximate the curves and creases of the labia and are described as talismanic. Avgikos notes figures sprouting erect phalli and portable phallus variations. The count is the point: two becomes four, one body carries both sexes, and desire reads as a system, not a pose. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M9) built one shape with two readings, butterfly or skull, flipped by light; Cabrera builds one body with too many readings, multiplied until anatomy becomes pattern, and the multiplication needs no light to flip. **Takeaway:** Multiply the sign until it stops illustrating and starts patterning. Watch the count: when two becomes four and one body carries both sexes, and the result reads as system rather than scandal, the mechanism is on.

## M7. Material migration

The same body-type migrates across stone, bronze, terracotta, jade, wood, wire, fiber, plexiglas, papier-mache, newspaper, and cut metal, and each migration changes what the body can do. The almost-forty-year object-as-body group in Partituras holds small jade carvings, suspended wire structures, and wood-and-metal fragmented bodies side by side. The 1971 fiber body viewed first-hand is the proof in one object: a standing body of tangled dark rope with a wide bristly brown fiber head, iron feet just visible at the base, no face, no articulated limbs. The wrapping IS the body. The ca. 1990 metal figure is the same body as a stencil: the holes are as structural as the metal. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M10) re-cut one motif in three materials and let each misbehave differently; Cabrera re-cuts one body in eleven materials over seventy years, and the migration is the career. Escobedo (#335 M7) built with whatever was nearby; Cabrera migrates on purpose, and each new material is a new behavior for the same body. **Takeaway:** Cast, carve, wrap, and stencil one pose in turn; let the pose survive and the behavior change. Watch the migration: when the same body is heavy in stone, reflective in bronze, soft in fiber, and hollow in metal, and still the same body, the mechanism is on.

## M8. Suspension and precarious balance

Pieces hang or balance so that air and gravity are part of the form. Formas ambientales (1971), thermoformed plexiglas shown at the Casa de la Cultura de Toluca, are described as suspended or disposed in precarious balances, suggesting a free walk through space. The El Eco install viewed first-hand shows the mode in one room: a suspended dark wire ball hanging from the ceiling, black and white blocks on the floor, photographs of monumental works on the wall, a pale porous reclining stone in the foreground. Three modes at once: suspension, precarious balance, and the lost monument as photograph. The viewer's own walking around the suspended form completes the instability. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M6) froze the toy in stone and let the sun do the spinning; Cabrera suspends the form and lets gravity do the threatening, and the threat never resolves. Escobedo (#335 M8) engineered failure, boats designed to sink; Cabrera engineers near-failure, forms designed almost to fall, and the almost is the work. **Takeaway:** Build the form so it should fall and does not; let the viewer's walk complete the instability. Watch the hang: when a form that should fall stays up, and the room feels unstable because of it, the mechanism is on.

## M9. The transitable monument

Cabrera's Villahermosa piece for GUCADIGOSE (1975-1976) was conceived to be walked through rather than contemplated: transitable, organic forms, wide openings, and inside it the spectator could experience the sculpture as a habitable space. It was later destroyed and survives only in photographs, which is why the El Eco wall of monument photographs, viewed first-hand, matters as much as any surviving work. There is no front view: the work only resolves as a path with light, wind, and earth passing through it. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M5) built the transitable monument as a plaza of towers at Tenayuca; Cabrera built hers as a habitable interior on a Tabasco road, and hers is the one that was lost, so the photograph has to carry the walk. Goeritz (#321) conceived the Ruta as a corridor experienced from a car; Cabrera's corridor is experienced on foot, from inside. **Takeaway:** Build no front view; the work completes only when the viewer is inside it. Watch the path: when the photograph on the wall is the only surviving view and it still shows a route, not a facade, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The museum as self-history

When institutions ignore her, she builds her own. In 1966 she founded the Museo Escultorico in her house and garden at Xicotencatl 181, Coyoacan: founding collection 55 pieces, architecture by Gustavo Lopez, gardens by Guillermina Lugo, free entry, sustained for four decades on her own resources including her salary as a teacher of aesthetic activities at UNAM. It operated simultaneously as workshop, archive, exhibition room, and meeting place. The display devices were her own: works set in wall niches along paths, on plumbing-pipe pedestals, among plants. The New York show in 2022 adopted the installation style, patios, niches, plumbing-part pedestals, plants, because the style was part of the work. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M8) declared the maquette finished because the monument is the maquette grown; Cabrera declares the house finished because the house is the museum grown, and the growth changes nothing but the address. Escobedo (#335 M9) declared the maquette finished because 90 percent of her proposals stayed proposals; Cabrera's proposals became a garden, and the garden outlasted the proposals. **Takeaway:** When the institution will not house the work, house it yourself, and let the housing become the work. Watch the garden: when a domestic garden behaves like a museum, niches as frames, paths as galleries, plants as fellow exhibitors, the mechanism is on.

## M11. The archive as a work

Scrapbooks, slides, letters, maquette photographs, and press clippings are kept, organized, and exhibited as part of the oeuvre, including works that no longer exist. The MPBA text is explicit: many sculptures known only from archive photographs have not been located or identified. The New York show digitized her scrapbook. The Villahermosa monument persists as a photograph on an exhibition wall, captioned by its own absence. The archive is not documentation of the work; for the lost works, it is the work. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M13) dissolved her signature into the material; Cabrera dissolves her lost monuments into the archive, and the archive keeps the signature. Escobedo (#335 M8) made the hoax carry the accusation; Cabrera makes the photograph carry the monument, and the photograph needs no accusation. **Takeaway:** Keep the archive as part of the oeuvre; a lost work that survives as a photograph is still a work. Watch the wall: when a photograph of a destroyed monument is hung with the same care as a sculpture, the mechanism is on.

## M12. Cheap material as access

Papier-mache, newspaper, and plexiglas are chosen because they are cheap, durable, and teachable, not despite it. Her first medium was her father's papier-mache, from a factory making art nouveau residential decor. In the 1980s she introduced newspaper as a sculpture material for low-income secondary students, so all could practice. The plexiglas of Formas ambientales is described as low cost and durable, a popular material. A classroom material is promoted to sculpture without disguise: newsprint still newsprint, plexiglas still plastic. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M10) chose one material per idea; Cabrera chooses one cheap material per student, and the choice is pedagogical before it is aesthetic. Escobedo (#335 M7) built with whatever was nearby and cheap because the work demanded disappearance; Cabrera builds with cheap material because the student demands access, and the access is the work. **Takeaway:** Promote the classroom material to sculpture without disguising it. Watch the newsprint: when newspaper is still newspaper and still sculpture, the mechanism is on.

## M13. The synesthetic score

Sculpture, color, music, and dance are set to a single system so that one art cues the others. With architect Luis Barragan and musician Alfonso Pallares she made Morfocromofonia, an integration of color, music, and movement by means of dance, dated only as around the first solo show and described by Artishock as scarcely documented today. Her own training split the day: Academia de San Carlos in the morning, dance school in the afternoon. Ana Garduno's closing remark, that many of her sculptures dance and have movement, is the late echo of the same system. The form implies a beat: the same body read as a step, a note, and a color at once. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M1) kept time with a hammer; Cabrera keeps time with a dance step, and the beat is choreographic rather than percussive. Goeritz (#321) built emotional architecture as a sequence of spaces; Cabrera builds a score as a sequence of bodies, and the sequence is danced, not walked. **Takeaway:** Set sculpture, color, music, and dance to one system; let one art cue the others. Watch the beat: when a still body implies a step, and the step implies a note, the mechanism is on.

## M14. The group as a corridor

Five artists work as one corridor, not one style: each makes a piece for a shared route, and the route is the work. GUCADIGOSE in Villahermosa, Tabasco, 1975-1976, is the case: Gurria, Cabrera, Juan Luis Diaz, Mathias Goeritz, and Sebastian (Enrique Carbajal Gonzalez), working in five plazas along a road, with Goeritz as the agglutinating figure and friendship, in Sebastian's words, extraordinarily well carried, as the working relation. CORRECTION TO #336: #336 recorded the GUCADIGOSE acronym expansion as unverified. This leg found the gloss in El Eco 2018, verbatim: GUCADIGO, acrostic of its members, Angela GUrria, Geles CAbrera, Juan Luis DIaz, Mathias GOeritz. The four-name expansion IS sourced and may be printed with that citation. The "SE" in the longer form GUCADIGOSE is NOT glossed letter by letter in any source found; it is the natural reading given Sebastian's membership, but it must not be presented as a quoted gloss. Print the member list and the GUCADIGO gloss separately. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M14) built the collective monument and proved the Ruta's thesis by sharing authorship; Cabrera proves the same thesis from inside the corridor, as one of its five hands, and her hand is the one that made the corridor walkable. Goeritz (#321) conceived the corridor; Cabrera walked it. **Takeaway:** Build the ensemble from different hands; the corridor breaks if one is removed. Watch the break: when removing one element collapses the whole, and no element imitates another, the mechanism is on.

## M15. The ceremonial group

Several ambiguous figures are arranged as a chorus or totem cluster, so meaning comes from spacing and number, not from any one figure's expression. The Rosario Castellanos totem project maquette shows four figures in a ceremonial group; two are located, Perfil del viento and an untitled stone restored for Partituras. The Partituras totem hang viewed first-hand shows three tall grey stone totems: left with a long diagonal incision down its flank, center a torso with a small pointed head, right an inverted-feeling figure with a sphere at its base. The clustered small bronzes viewed first-hand work the same way: the gap between figures is part of the composition. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M7) assembled a family from four slabs and four grooves; Cabrera assembles a rite from three totems and their gaps, and the rite needs no faces. Escobedo (#335 M11) assembled a crowd from one prop and one motor; Cabrera assembles a chorus from spacing alone. **Takeaway:** Arrange ambiguous figures as a chorus; let spacing and number carry the meaning. Watch the gap: when the space between figures is doing as much work as the figures, the mechanism is on.

## Aesthetic logic

The career is one long demonstration that a body does not need to be described to be present, and that an artist does not need an institution to have a history. The logic runs: reduce the body until one curve or one cut carries it (M1, M2); let the stone stay a stone (M3); lay the movement out as a row, a score the eye dances (M4, M13); refuse the standing triumph and let the body recline (M5); multiply the erotic sign until anatomy becomes pattern (M6); migrate the same body across eleven materials and let each change its behavior (M7); suspend the form so gravity threatens it (M8); build the monument to be walked through, not contemplated (M9); when the institution will not house the work, house it in a garden (M10); when the work is destroyed, keep it in the archive (M11); promote the cheap material without disguising it, because access is the work (M12); share authorship until the corridor itself is the work (M14); and arrange the figures as a chorus whose gaps are part of the composition (M15). The through-line is her own first-show idea, that the modern artist must possess her own time: a singular way of representing inner and spiritual becoming, carried for seventy years at the same walking speed, in a house museum that charged nothing and in a monument that no longer exists except as a photograph. That is why the stone can be a body and the rope can be a body and the stencil can be a body: nothing is illustrated, everything is reduced to the mechanism, and the mechanism is always something a body can walk through, lie beside, or dance past.

## Evolution

1926 Mexico City, born Angeles Maria Cabrera Alvarado; father a civil engineer owning a papier-mache factory making art nouveau residential decor, her first medium; aunt Rosario Cabrera, artist of the Escuelas de Pintura al Aire Libre -> 1943-1947 Academia de San Carlos in the morning, dance school in the afternoon; teachers Ignacio Asunsolo, Francisco Zuniga (terracotta modeling, at La Esmeralda), Fidias Elizondo, Luis Ortiz Monasterio; among the first women sculpture students at San Carlos -> 1947 family moves to Cuba after the factory closes; Academia de San Alejandro, Havana; second prize 1948, first prize 1949, XXXI Salon de Bellas Artes de La Habana; contact with Wifredo Lam, influence hedged as possible, not proven -> 1949 return to Mexico, La Esmeralda; first solo, Galeria Mont-Orendain, 1948 or 1949, nineteen sculptures, age 22; praised by Paul Westheim and Margarita Nelken; portrait of Dolores del Rio, sitter posed six days, date and material not given in sources read, do not date it -> 1950 solo, Galeria Arte Moderno, catalog text by Westheim; the "poseer tiempo propio" idea -> 1950s Salon de la Plastica Mexicana 1954; "Two Artists from Mexico", Washington, 1956; El Eco show 1957 -> 1960s direct carving in stone and wood, unpolished; Mascara o Cabeza 1966; Perfil del viento 1965, mention honorifica, III Bienal Nacional de Escultura 1967 -> 1966 Museo Escultorico founded, Xicotencatl 181, Coyoacan, 55 pieces, Lopez architecture, Lugo gardens, free entry -> 1971 Formas ambientales, thermoformed plexiglas, Casa de la Cultura de Toluca -> 1975-1976 GUCADIGOSE Villahermosa monument, transitable, later destroyed, surviving in photographs -> 1977 Goeritz's Retrato metafisico de Geles Cabrera, gift;
