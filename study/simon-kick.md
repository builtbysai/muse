## The life

Simon Kick was born in Delft in 1603, the son of Willem Anthonisz Kick, a Delft varnish worker originally from Amsterdam who specialised in lacquer and porcelain painting, and Tanneken de Brey (married 1590). He had a half-brother, Willem Kick, a gold-leather maker -- NL Wikipedia only, not corroborated elsewhere. Nothing is known about Simon Kick's own training. He moved from Delft to Amsterdam c. 1624 and lived and worked until his death in the Nieuwmarktbuurt; he is documented in Amsterdam in 1631, 1633, 1643, 1646, 1647, 1651 and 1652. He was buried on 26 September 1652 in the Zuiderkerk, Amsterdam (leg 1 §1a-1b).

On 5 September 1631 he became engaged to Christina (Stijntje) Cornelisdr. Duyster (1606-1673), sister of the painter Willem Cornelisz. Duyster -- and on the same day Duyster became engaged to Simon's sister Margrieta Kick. A double betrothal binding the two families twice over. The Rijksmuseum's Duyster catalogue entries add, snippet-only, that the two couples lived together in a house called "De Duystere Werelt" (the dim world), the house Duyster was named after. His son Cornelis Kick, baptised in Amsterdam on 12 March 1634, became a still-life painter and was trained under his father from c. 1650. The number of Simon's children is unresolved and is not stated here: EN Wikipedia's infobox says 7, NL Wikipedia says four (Cornelis plus three daughters), and the RKD entry that would settle it was not accessible (leg 1 C5).

He began painting only at 32. Rosen's 2021 catalogue raisonné synopsis says he was "driven to do so only due to the tragic death of his brother-in-law and since the economic situation necessitated it" -- Duyster died of plague in 1635, the year Kick turned 32, and the earliest dated works follow in 1637 and 1639. The claim rests on the publisher's synopsis, not on the book, and the study carries it as Rosen's, not as archival fact. Whether he was self-taught is not stated anywhere read; the synopsis and the silence about a teacher cohere, but the inference is not made here (leg 3 N11).

His career, as the RKD summarises it via the Cornelis Kick article, was as "a painter of genre scenes, portraits including schuttersstukken, landscapes and guardroom scenes (kortegaardjes)". The earliest dated works are portrait studies -- a signed tronie of 1637, a turbaned figure characterised as East Asian, and An Old Man with Folded Arms of 1639 (Rijksmuseum) -- in the chiaroscuro manner of the young Jan Lievens and Rembrandt. The guardroom scenes begin in the same late-1630s period; two are dated 1640. In 1644 he bid at history painting in large format with the signed Elisha Refusing Naaman's Gifts (Berlin). The latest dated guardroom found is 1646. He died in 1652, aged 48 or 49, leaving a known career of about fifteen years and an oeuvre Rosen catalogues at over 125 works (the 2007 appendix numbering runs past no. 125).

One documented pupil: his son Cornelis. No documented teacher, no documented workshop beyond the family. His Amsterdam circle was Dirck Hals, Jacob Duck, Pieter Codde, and above all Willem Duyster -- "deeply bound to Duyster above all" (DE Wikipedia). No still lifes by Simon Kick are known, yet he is presumed to have been his son's teacher. The son took the soldiers and horsemen, then turned to flowers; the father never did.

In 2025, during collection restoration at the musée Bonnat-Helleu in Bayonne, a work long held as an anonymous 17th-century Dutch school painting, Deux femmes occupées de soins domestiques, was attributed to Simon Kick: restoration revealed a date "1643" and a signature, previously invisible, on one of the barrels depicted. The attribution rests on FR Wikipedia's report of Connaissance des Arts, 12 Dec 2025; the article itself was not opened (leg 1 §1c).

## M1. The gentrified guardroom: civic-guard portrait elegance inside soldiers' leisure

The guardroom scene -- kortegaardje, a corruption of corps de garde -- is soldiers off duty: drinking, smoking, playing cards and dice, sleeping, arguing over loot, carousing with camp followers. Rosen's synopsis says Kick's guardrooms "stand out as being more gentrified than others due to the fact that he assimilated in them the elegance of contemporary civic-guard portraits." The mechanism is a transplant: the upright bearing, the fine dress, the composed grouping of the schutterstuk -- the civic-guard group portrait, the most prestigious secular commission in Amsterdam -- carried into a room full of soldiers at leisure. The men keep their posture. The scene stays a guardroom, but it behaves like a portrait.

The genre's own two phases, as the Palamedesz. article states them, sharpen the point. Early phase: resting soldiers seated, in conversation, tending uniforms, arguing over loot, carousing with prostitutes, playing cards, smoking. Second phase from roughly 1645: behaviour more refined, "devoid of booty and other signs of war", middle-class people interacting with soldiers, reflecting growing civility. Kick's gentrification is the Amsterdam version of that second phase, arrived at early -- his guardrooms are dated from 1640 -- and it is what separates him from Duck's dark church halls and Quast's caricature. The Fondation Custodia Genre Scene with Soldiers Playing Dice (W10) shows the mechanism in catalogue prose: "delicate monochrome hues of brown, grey and beige", "subtle textures and materials", and on the right a man turned towards the spectator whose gaze "engages directly with ours, as if to invite us to the gaming table" -- a portrait's address inside a gambling scene. The Christie's Company of Soldiers in a Guardroom (W01), 122 x 120.5 cm, nearly square and unusually large for a guardroom panel, is the same mechanism at scale: a guardroom painted with a portrait's ambition.

**Takeaway:** the guardroom is not a tavern scene with uniforms; in Kick's hands it is a portrait that happens to contain soldiers. The piece must keep the men's bearing upright and the room composed, and let the leisure -- dice, smoke, drink -- happen inside that composure, never dissolve it.

## M2. The contemplative soldier: psychological presence over carousing

Rosen's synopsis again, the second half of the same sentence: Kick's figures are "well-characterized, often depicted in a contemplative mood, and imbued with a strong psychological presence." The mechanism is a choice of moment: not the toast, not the brawl, not the deal of the cards, but the pause between actions -- the soldier looking out, the man with folded arms, the player weighing the dice. The Rijksmuseum catalogue's reading of An Old Man with Folded Arms (W03) gives the same note in portrait form: crossed arms as contrition, Ripa's personification of conversion, "signs of great remorse and regret". Contemplation is Kick's signature note across both halves of his career -- the tronie heads first, then the guardrooms.

It is also what Duyster's guardrooms do not do in the same way. Duyster, per Franits via Wikipedia, avoided prostitutes and put hostages and victims in their place; his women are in danger, his men in command. Kick's men are not in command of anything -- they are thinking. The two mechanisms are complementary, not identical, and the study does not collapse them.

**Takeaway:** give the soldier an inner state before giving him an action. The watchable behaviour is a man pausing -- gaze held, hand still, dice unthrown -- and the room waiting on him.

## M3. The protagonist at the right, in profile

The Rijksmuseum catalogue (Korevaar 2023) names Kick's compositional habit plainly: his "typical practice of placing the protagonist on the right and shown from the side, as he did in several genre pieces". In the Parable of the Unworthy Wedding Guest (W05), the unworthy guest is carried out horizontally, bound hand and foot, at the right in profile; above the crowd a man in a red robe with a stick stands at the far right. In Elisha Refusing Naaman's Gifts (W06), the prophet Elisha -- who resembles the king in the Parable -- stands at the right. In the Custodia dice scene (W10), the right-hand man turns towards the spectator and invites us to the table; the dossier wonders whether he is the artist himself. The right edge is where Kick puts the figure who matters, and he shows that figure in profile, as if the scene were being read left to right and the sentence ended there.

**Takeaway:** compose with a right-hand anchor in profile. The piece should read left to right and resolve at the right edge, where one figure stands apart from the group and carries the meaning.

## M4. The late start: a painter who begins at 32, and a career of fifteen years

Kick's whole known career fits between a signed tronie of 1637 and his burial in 1652 -- about fifteen working years, if Rosen's "began at 32" is right, and the firmly dated works cluster 1637-1646. The mechanism is compression: there is no apprentice decade, no journeyman wandering, no slow mastery curve in the record. The earliest dated works are already assured -- a signed tronie, a 1639 head the Rijksmuseum ties to Lievens -- and within three years he is painting guardrooms, within seven he is painting history in large format. The career reads as a man who arrived late and worked fast, and who died at 48 or 49 before the curve could flatten. Rosen's synopsis puts it as a loss: "the fact that he started his career late and passed away at an early age robbed us of a great painter."

The compression shapes the oeuvre's proportions: tronies and single heads 1637-39, guardrooms and portraits in parallel from 1640, large-format history in 1644, guardrooms continuing to 1646, and then a six-year tail with almost nothing firmly dated. The undated majority -- Rosen catalogues over 125 works -- cannot be sequenced without the catalogue raisonné, and the study does not pretend otherwise.

**Takeaway:** a career as a short fuse. The piece should feel like work made under a deadline the maker did not choose -- fast arrival, fast climb, and an ending that comes before the work is done.

## M5. The recurring old-man model: one face, many heads

The Rijksmuseum catalogue on An Old Man with Folded Arms (W03) records that the same long-haired, bearded figure "features in many of his tronies, including a signed one of 1637, and another of roughly the same date which Sumowski has convincingly attributed to Kick". One model, reused across the early heads: the fleshy hands, the massive drapery folds, the thin pale hair, the white beard over the chest, the simple ribbon knotted at the throat. The 1637 tronie characterises the same figure as East Asian by his turban -- the model in costume, not a different man. The mechanism is a studio economy: a single sitter, probably a man in Kick's circle, standing in for old age itself across a run of study heads, and the heads are close-ups -- tight chest-up framing, dark near-monochrome ground, a single light from the left, deep shadow in the wrinkles.

The affinities the catalogue names are the young Jan Lievens's study heads of the late 1620s and early Salomon Koninck -- the Rembrandt-circle tronie tradition, where a head is a study of character and light, not a portrait of a named person. Kick's version of it is the repeated face: the viewer who has seen one Kick old man has seen the sitter, and the next head is the same man again, older, in different light.

**Takeaway:** one sitter, many heads. The piece should return to the same face -- long hair, beard, folded hands -- and vary only the light, the costume and the mood, so the repetition itself becomes the subject.

## M6. The signature that changes hands: a guardroom sold as Schalcken

A Company of Soldiers in a Guardroom (W01), oil on panel, 122 x 120.5 cm, carries a provenance that is itself a mechanism. Its signature was at some unknown date altered into Godfried Schalcken's; both signatures were removed during cleaning c. 1895; and the picture circulated as a Schalcken through the sales of 1872, 1877 and 1892 before re-emerging as Kick -- Rosen catalogue raisonné no. 29. A painting that spent twenty years under another man's name because someone reworked four letters of paint.

The mechanism is misattribution as a physical act: not a scholar's error but a hand with a brush, changing a signature, and the market following the new name for a generation. It pairs with the Parable's wandering attribution (M7) and with the son's story -- Cornelis Kick's pictures sold as his pupil Walscapelle's -- to make a family theme: in this house, authorship is something that comes off in cleaning. The study notes the parallel without forcing it: father and son both lost their names to other painters, the father to a signature-forger, the son to the market.

**Takeaway:** authorship as a removable layer. The piece should let a name appear, change, and wash away -- a signature that is painted over, a label that peels -- and the picture underneath stays the same.

## M7. The attribution that wanders: the Parable's two centuries of wrong names

The Parable of the Unworthy Wedding Guest (W05), oil on canvas, 130 x 156 cm, Rijksmuseum, was bought in 1802 as Gerbrand van den Eeckhout; was long thought to be Samuel van Hoogstraten; was assigned to Claes Moeyaert by Hofstede de Groot in 1899; was later suggested for the circle of Salomon de Bray, Jacob Backer and Dirck van Santvoort; and is now attributed to Kick on the comparison with the signed Elisha (W06) -- the same right-side profile protagonist, the same rendering of velvet textures in the cloaks. Two centuries, six names, one canvas.

The mechanism is how close Kick's mature history manner sits to the wider Amsterdam mainstream: his large-format biblical painting is good enough, and generic enough in its devices, to pass for five other painters across two hundred years. The attribution rests on two small things -- a compositional habit (M3) and a texture (velvet) -- not on a signature or a document. The study carries the attribution as the Rijksmuseum's, current but not certain: the picture is "attributed to Kick", and the catalogue's reasoning is quoted, not endorsed beyond it.

**Takeaway:** a picture that has been five other pictures. The piece should show a scene whose author keeps changing -- same figures, same velvet, a different name each time -- until the two small tells (the right-hand profile, the velvet) settle it.

## M8. The hidden signature: restoration reveals a date on a barrel

In 2025 the musée Bonnat-Helleu in Bayonne, restoring its collection, attributed Deux femmes occupées de soins domestiques -- two women busy with domestic care -- to Simon Kick: restoration by Giusy Dinardo revealed a date "1643" and a signature, previously invisible, on one of the barrels depicted. The mechanism is the opposite of M6: where the Christie's guardroom had a signature forged onto it, the Bayonne picture had a signature hidden in it, waiting for a restorer to find it on a painted barrel -- a signature placed inside the fiction, on an object in the scene, rather than on the surface of the painting.

The picture itself is a domestic interior, not a guardroom: two women at housework. If the attribution holds, it extends Kick's range beyond soldiers and old men into a subject no other source gives him. The study carries it with its full chain of custody: FR Wikipedia reporting Connaissance des Arts, 12 Dec 2025; the article itself not opened; the attribution new and single-sourced. It is included because the mechanism -- a signature revealed by restoration, on a barrel, in 2025 -- is too good to omit and too thin to build on without the flag.

**Takeaway:** the signature is inside the picture, not on it. The piece should hide its maker's mark on an object in the scene -- a barrel, a bench, a wall -- and let it appear only when the surface is cleaned, lit, or looked at the right way.

## Evolution

Kick's whole career is short: he began painting only at 32 (c. 1635), after his brother-in-law's death forced the change, and died in 1652 aged 48 or 49. Roughly seventeen working years, and the firmly dated works cluster 1637-1646. The evolution below rests only on dated works (W03, W04, W06, W07, W09, W16, W20) plus dated catalogue statements.

Early phase (c. 1635-1640): tronies and single-figure heads. The signed tronie of 1637 (W16), the c. 1637 Sumowski tronie (W17), An Old Man with Folded Arms 1639 (W03) and the Dayton Man with Turban Reading 1639 (W20) form a coherent early group: close-up single heads, dark near-monochrome grounds, strong single-source chiaroscuro from the left, fleshy hands, massive drapery folds. The Rijksmuseum catalogue places W03 in "Kick's early career, before he made his name with genre scenes" and ties the group to the young Jan Lievens's study heads of the late 1620s and to early Salomon Koninck -- the Rembrandt-circle tronie tradition, not yet a guardroom idiom. The 1637 turbaned figure shows the exotic-costume tronie habit of that circle.

Middle phase (c. 1640): the guardroom formula arrives, alongside portraits. Two guardroom scenes are dated 1640 (W07 Schloss; W09 Poznań), the same year as the Hermitage portrait (W04). The turn to genre was not a clean break from portraiture -- both ran in parallel in 1640. W09's subject (peasants begging soldiers for mercy) shows the guardroom frame already being used for civilian-soldier confrontation, not only soldiers at leisure.

Mature phase (1644): large-format history painting. The signed Elisha Refusing Naaman's Gifts 1644 (W06, Berlin) and the attributed Parable of the Unworthy Wedding Guest c. 1644 (W05, Rijksmuseum, 130 x 156 cm) are Kick's bid at history painting "in a large format" (Rijksmuseum catalogue). Scale jumps: from 39 x 31 cm panels and 71 x 54 cm heads to a 130 x 156 cm canvas. Both place the protagonist at the right, shown in profile -- a compositional signature the catalogue says Kick used "in several genre pieces" too. Velvet-cloak rendering is shared between the two.

Late phase (c. 1646-1652): guardroom work continues; little firmly dated. Wachtlokaal met soldaten dated 1646 (W08, RKDimages 39742) is the latest dated guardroom found. The large Christie's Company of Soldiers in a Guardroom (W01, 122 x 120.5 cm -- nearly square, unusually big for a guardroom panel) is undated but catalogued by Rosen as no. 29; its scale and the "gentrified" character Rosen attributes to Kick's guardrooms suggest the mature guardroom manner. The Fondation Custodia Genre Scene with Soldiers Playing Dice (W10, acquired 2021) is undated; its "delicate monochrome hues of brown, grey and beige" and the direct outward gaze of the right-hand figure (possibly a self-portrait) are consistent with the mature group but cannot be placed chronologically from the sources consulted.

What changed: subject, from single heads (1637-39) to multi-figure guardroom interiors (from 1640) to large biblical history (1644); scale, from 39 x 31 cm panels to 71 x 54 cm heads to a 122 x 120.5 cm guardroom panel to a 130 x 156 cm history canvas; support, panel dominating the early work and guardrooms, canvas for the big 1644 histories (and W10); ambition, from Rembrandt-circle study heads to a self-conscious history painter in 1644.

What stayed: the right-side protagonist in profile (W05/W06; genre pieces per the catalogue; the right-hand standing man in W10); chiaroscuro single-source lighting and dark grounds from the tronie years through the guardrooms; a muted, near-monochrome palette -- browns, greys, beige -- noted explicitly for W10 and in the descriptions of W03/W05; small-figure multi-figure grouping in shallow interiors, the guardroom formula once adopted c. 1640 running to the end of the dated record (1646); texture as a selling point -- velvet cloaks (W05/W06), "subtle textures and materials" (W10), fabrics and light effects generally.

What cannot be established from this leg: a year-by-year sequence for the undated guardrooms (W01, W02, W10, W13, W18, W19); whether the guardroom output clusters early-1640s or late-1640s; the date of The Robbery (W02); any landscape work. The full catalogue raisonné (Rosen 2021) was not accessible in full text -- it is the missing key.

## Aesthetic logic

Kick's aesthetic is restraint inside a rowdy genre. The guardroom scene, as Codde and Duyster invented it, is a room of men off duty -- drinking, smoking, gambling, sleeping, carousing -- and the genre's pleasures are abundance, noise, and the textures of loot and drink. Kick keeps the room and quiets the men. His figures are contemplative, well-characterised, psychologically present; his palette is near-monochrome, brown and grey and beige; his light is a single source from the left, the tronie light carried into the interior; his composition puts the figure who matters at the right edge, in profile, where the eye ends up. The elegance he borrows from the civic-guard portrait is not decoration -- it is the mechanism that makes a room full of soldiers into a picture about attention rather than appetite.

The same logic runs through the career in reverse order: the heads first, where attention is the whole subject (a man with folded arms, a man in a turban, a man reading); then the guardrooms, where attention is distributed across a group but still held by one figure at the right; then the histories, where attention is a prophet refusing a gift and a guest being carried out. The late start explains the compression -- a painter who arrives at 32 does not have time for a journeyman decade, and his work reads as if he knew it. The short career explains the coherence: fifteen years, one light, one palette, one compositional habit, one recurring face.

The failures of the record are part of the logic too. Authorship comes off in cleaning (W01), wanders for two centuries (W05), hides on a barrel until 2025 (M8). A painter whose pictures kept losing his name is a painter the market and the museum had to re-find, twice -- and the re-finding always came through the same two tells: the right-hand profile and the velvet.

## Positions

- The dates are 1603-1652, Delft to Amsterdam, burial 26 September 1652. The 1653 of the Rijksmuseum's Ludolf de Jongh entry and the 1651 of the Fondation Custodia dossier are typos; do not cite them.
- "Began painting at 32 after Duyster's death" is Rosen's synopsis claim, not archival fact. The study states it as Rosen's and notes the chronology coheres; it does not state that Kick was self-taught.
- "One of only ten guardroom painters" is Rosen's claim. Nine names are evidenced; the tenth is unknown. Do not guess it.
- "Inventor of the guardroom scene" is not used for Kick. Duyster and Codde developed and popularised the formula; Kick is one of its ten practitioners, and Rosen's "Minor Masters as Inventors" framing is about the group, not Kick individually.
- No life of Simon Kick by Houbraken exists in the sources found. Houbraken's Kick life is of the son Cornelis. Do not write that Houbraken wrote Simon's life.
- The children count is unresolved (7 vs 4). No number is stated in this study.
- "Gerrit van Lievens" is an error in retail copy. The influence is Jan Lievens.
- "Possibly also a sculptor" (NL Wikipedia) is unattributed and is not carried.
- The NGI Interior with Soldiers (1632) is Duyster's, not Kick's. The "three dated works" fact belongs to Duyster.
- The Parable is attributed to Kick, not signed. The study reports the Rijksmuseum's attribution and its reasoning, not a certainty.
- No standalone Kick landscape was found. The RKD's "landscapes" in his genre list is not corroborated by any work in this leg.
- The 2025 Bonnat-Helleu attribution is single-sourced (FR Wikipedia reporting Connaissance des Arts) and is carried as new and provisional.

## Network

The family web first. Simon Kick married Christina (Stijntje) Duyster; her brother Willem Duyster married Simon's sister Margrieta, on the same day, 5 September 1631. The Kick-Duyster double marriage binds the genre's inventor to its gentrifier twice over, and Duyster's death of plague in 1635 is the event Rosen says made Kick a painter at 32. Simon's son Cornelis (#563) was trained under his father from c. 1650 in genre, portraits, schuttersstukken and guardrooms, then turned to flowers -- the guardroom training is the forward line of this study, and the flower turn is its end.

The inventors: Willem Cornelisz Duyster (1599-1635) and Pieter Codde (1599-1678) "developed and popularised the guardroom scene" (NGI). Codde was, per Rosen via Steigrad, "the most dominant and innovative" of the group that formulated it, and "the most prolific and influential genre painter in Holland during the 1630s". Codde's pupil was Duyster, "who was about the same age" -- pupil and exact contemporary at once. Codde's guardroom mechanism, per Rosen via Steigrad: scenes "devoid of violence... focusing on soldiers' off-duty hours spent gambling, drinking, sleeping, and carousing with camp followers"; class distinctions maintained, the commander always recognisable by superior dress, attitude, prominent placement; "an element of theatricality on a stage-like setting... with spot lighting".

The circle, three cities in one sentence (Palamedesz. article): Codde, Duyster and Kick in Amsterdam; Jacob Duck in Utrecht; Palamedesz. and Jacob van Velsen in Delft. Duck (c. 1600-1667), the most successful exponent, painted guardrooms from 1628 to 1655, often wide and tall hallways or church interiors; he was influenced by Codde, not his pupil. Palamedesz. (1602-1673), the Delft branch, painted guardrooms into the 1660s -- a large hall of a ruin or castle, open at the rear, a large fireplace, a chubby officer in a tall hat at the centre -- and his guardrooms are unique in including women as mothers, not courtesans: "masculinity pacified". Van Velsen (1597-1656) is the one documented influence running into Duyster: the RKD says he influenced Duyster. Quast (bapt. 1605-1647) is the caricature and satire branch, the Dutch Callot, who shared the deserted-church guardroom with Duck and Potter. Pieter Symonsz Potter (1597-1652), father of Paulus Potter, painted a Guardroom Interior (Schwerin) and took in Codde's divorced wife as a neighbour, then a household. Gerard ter Borch (1617-1681) is the afterlife: his early-1640s guardrooms, painted in Amsterdam under the influence of Duyster and Codde, carried the genre's lessons -- small groups, full-length figures, upright format, subtle psychological interplay -- into the rarefied domestic interiors of the 1650s and 1660s.

One documented pupil (his son Cornelis), no documented teacher, no documented workshop beyond the family. The early influence is the Rembrandt-Lievens circle, not a master: the tronie heads of 1637-39, the chiaroscuro, the recurring old-man model.
