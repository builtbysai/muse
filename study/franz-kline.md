## M1. The projector enlargement (a small drawing becomes a wall)

Kline's origin story is a piece of equipment, a Bell-Opticon enlarger in his studio, and a small black brush drawing, four by five inches, a rocking chair. Projected onto the studio wall, the drawing grew into gigantic black strokes that, in Kline's own words quoted in Wikipedia and The Art Story, "eradicated any image, the strokes expanding as entities in themselves, unrelated to any entity but that of their own existence". The household object becomes a wall of pure stroke, the image dies, the mark lives, and the source of the story is Elaine de Kooning's ARTnews essay, "Franz Kline: Painter of His Own Life", November 1962, written after Kline's death, and Wikipedia calls her account romanticized, and The Art Story calls the story apocryphal, and the date is 1948 by Wikipedia, "two or three years later" than The Dancer of 1946 by the Smithsonian, "late 1940s" by MoMA, and the study carries 1948 with the apocryphal flag, and the watchable behaviour is a picture that began as a chair and ended as a stroke, the stroke keeping the speed and the pressure of the small drawing and losing everything else, and the mechanism is the opposite of the inspired large gesture, because the gesture was small first and was enlarged by a machine, and the machine is the picture. **Takeaway:** take a small black brush drawing, project it until it fills the wall, and let the image die so the stroke can live as an entity in itself, so the picture is a small gesture made gigantic by a machine. Against the chain: de Kooning (#387 M5) also moved drawings onto canvas by tracing paper, and his is a drawing moved by hand, and Kline's is a drawing moved by a projector, which is why de Kooning's picture is a collage of its own drawings and Kline's picture is one drawing blown up until it forgets what it was, and the forgetting is the enlargement, and the enlargement is the stroke, and the stroke is the wall.

After the color experiments of the late 1950s, Kline reverted in the early 1960s to almost exclusively black and white, per Tate on Meryon, and Merce C of 1961 at the Smithsonian, and Donald Judd's December 1961 review of the Janis show frames it as self-aware repetition that "add[s] complexities which restore naturalistic distinctions originally expunged", and the title Meryon restores a historical reference the abstraction had expunged, the surname of the nineteenth-century French etcher Charles Meryon, and the vertical format of Meryon, black vertical concentric rectangles on a black horizontal base, is portrait-like where the earlier black-and-whites were landscape, and the watchable behaviour is the familiar black-and-white returning, but now carrying a proper noun and a vertical format, the old style coming back changed, and the mechanism is a return that is also a revision, the same vocabulary saying a new thing, and the new thing is the picture. **Takeaway:** return to the old black and white, but bring a proper noun and a vertical format with it, so the familiar style comes back changed and the return is a revision, not a repeat. ## Evolution

Four phases. 1) Figurative training and work, 1931-1946: Boston University 1931-35, Heatherley School London 1937-38, Old Master admiration, Rembrandt, murals for bars, landscapes, cityscapes, portraits, Hot Jazz of 1940 already simplifying forms into quick strokes, Palmerton, Pa. of 1941 filling the canvas edge to edge with broadly painted forms. 2) Transition, 1946-1949: first abstract work 1946, The Dancer of 1946 the first abstraction by Kline's own account, "white paintings" 1947, the projector episode 1948, figurative subjects generalized into lines and planes "much like the works of Cubism", per Wikipedia. 3) Black-and-white maturity, 1950-1956: Egan show October 1950 establishes the reputation, Chief, Wotan, Nijinsky of 1950, Painting No. 7 of 1952, Painting Number 2 of 1954, Mahoning of 1956, house paint, telephone-book studies, the "black and white artist" label, which Kline sometimes felt restricted by. 4) Color and return, 1956-1962: color reasserts itself from about 1956, more consistently after 1959, Requiem of 1958 in chiaroscuro, wall paintings 1959-61, Venice Biennale 1960 with Hans Hofmann, Philip Guston and Theodore Roszak, Italian Ministry of Public Instruction Prize 1960, return to black and white in 1960-61, Meryon and Merce C, rheumatic heart condition discovered 1961, heart attack February 1962, died Sunday 13 May 1962, ten days before his fifty-second birthday, aged fifty-one.

## Aesthetic logic

The aesthetic logic is the enlargement and the girder and the denial and the return. The enlargement is the projector and the study-first procedure and the white painted as actively as black, the small drawing blown up until the image dies and the stroke lives, the wildness composed on telephone book pages and reproduced large, the white pushing forward as a solid and trading places with the black, and the enlargement is the machine, and the machine is the plan, and the plan is the dialogue. The girder is the beam vocabulary and the edge-breaking diagonals and the housepainter's brush and the speed, the black bars reading as structure, the diagonals rushing off the edge and swelling as they go, the cheap industrial enamel carrying broad ragged strokes, the shapes as vectors forever in flux and suggesting impending collapse, and the girder is the bridge, and the bridge is the break, and the break is the house, and the house is the speed. The denial is the table and chair revenant and the titles as place-anchors and the wobbly square and the refusal of meaning and the calligraphy denial, the furniture ghosting inside the abstraction, the name the only link to the coal town, the grid shaken until the certainties blur, the picture offering no story to decode, the stroke reading as girder and as character and settling on neither, and the denial is the ghost, and the ghost is the name, and the name is the wobble, and the wobble is the silence, and the silence is the character. The return is the color re-entry and the chiaroscuro merging and the wall-painting scale and the late return to black and white, the color arriving as a small hot intrusion and growing until it can hold a whole canvas, the binary gaining a middle register, the stroke becoming architecture, the old black and white coming back changed with a proper noun attached, and the return is the accent, and the accent is the grey, and the grey is the wall, and the wall is the name.
