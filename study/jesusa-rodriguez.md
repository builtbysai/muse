# Jesusa Rodriguez: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #349.

**Site:** Laura Maria de Jesus Rodriguez Ramirez (Mexico City, Thursday 3 November 1955 - ), known as Jesusa Rodriguez, Mexican director, actress, playwright, performance artist, scenographer, social activist and senator. She studied theatre at the Centro Universitario de Teatro of the Universidad Nacional Autonoma de Mexico between 1971 and 1973, under the director Julio Castillo, whose work marked her career; with Castillo's group Sombras Blancas she mounted plays and made the scenography for Arde Pinocho and for Ionesco's Que formidable burdel. In 1979 she met her partner, the Argentine singer and actress Liliana Felipe. In 1980 the two opened the cabaret El Cuervo in Coyoacan with the show El Fracaso, and founded the theatre cooperative Grupo Divas A.C.; in 1980 they also rented the old Teatro de la Capilla, founded by the poet Salvador Novo, and opened the theatre-bar El Habito to pay for rebuilding it. El Habito ran from 1990 to 2005, more than three hundred shows, and was then handed to the cabaret group Las Reinas Chulas, who renamed it El Vicio. Her best-known works are the all-female Don Giovanni, Donna Giovanni (1983-1987); El Concilio de Amor (1988), Panizza's blasphemous council, for which she received death threats; La gira mamal de la Coatlicue (1990), in which the stone goddess walks out of the museum and runs for president; La Malinche en Dios T.V. (1991); Cielo de abajo (1992); Sor Juana en Almoloya (1995); Cosi fan tutte (1996-97); Las Horas de Belen (1998-99), made with Ruth Maleczech and Mabou Mines, for which she won an Obie for Best Actor; La serpiente enchilada (2000); La soldadera autogena (2001); the pre-Hispanic cabaret at the Guggenheim (2004); Dialogos entre Darwin y Dios (2009), in which she plays both Darwin and God; and the solo Primero Sueno (2007-2009), Sor Juana's 950-line poem memorized over ten years. She won a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1990 and a Rockefeller Foundation grant in 1994, and has contributed to Debate Feminista since its founding in 1990. After the 2006 presidential election she left the cabaret room for the street, heading Resistencia Creativa and its massive cabaret; in 2018 she took a seat in the Senate as substitute for Olga Sanchez Cordero, serving from Thursday 29 November 2018 to Thursday 26 August 2021, and in 2022 she was proposed as ambassador to Panama, a post she never took. She turned 71 on Tuesday 3 November 2026, two days before this study. Her papers are at the Tamiment Library of New York University, and her Coatlicue costume was transferred there in November 2024. Since 2011 she has lived in San Miguel de Allende with Liliana Felipe.

Lara hung ten collages, Mayer hung eight hundred pink cards, Weiss showed two video-performances, and Rodriguez's answer to the same invitation is the one the record does not keep, because her answer was a body on a stage, and bodies on stages leave no object to hang. Where Mayer's line grows sideways because strangers keep pinning cards to it, and Lara's line grows in a spiral because she keeps returning to the same house, and Weiss's line grows because the camera keeps moving on her shoulder, Rodriguez's line grows because the same woman keeps coming back in a different body: a goddess, a nun, a translator, a soldier, a devil, a president, Darwin, God. Mayer handed the sentence to strangers; Lara kept it for herself; Weiss handed the camera to her own body; Rodriguez hands her own body to the next character, and the character does the talking.

## M1. The mask that is a face

The president's face is worn as makeup, and the crowd stones the image. Jesusa wore the mask and makeup of Carlos Salinas de Gortari through his whole six-year presidency, the president who, in her words, committed the fraud of 1988 and was a thief, a corrupt man, a murderer. The mask was not a joke told once; it was a face she put on for six years, so that the man and the mask became the same thing in the audience's mind. Then Salinas left Mexico, and ten years later, when he tried to come back, she put the face on again and climbed onto a rock concert stage, and the young audience began to stone her, throwing steel cylinders, because they knew it was Jesusa and it did not matter: what the band wanted was to stone the image. She lasted less than three minutes before the rockers pulled her down and told the crowd, wait, this is Jesusa, not Salinas. The mechanism is the mask that stops being a disguise and becomes the person, so completely that the crowd attacks the wearer as if she were the man. A mask usually hides the wearer; this one replaces her, and the replacement is so complete that the violence meant for the president lands on the actress. The Salinas Skits of 1994-1996 in the NYU archive are the same mechanism in its nightly form, a folder of sketches in which the president is played again and again until the playing is the portrait. **Takeaway:** wear the president's face long enough, and the crowd will stone you for him. Watch the mask: when the audience attacks the actress as if she were the man she is playing, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Grobet's mask (#347 M1) also never came off, but her luchadores wore their own masks to be themselves; Rodriguez wears another man's face to be him, which is why her version is an impersonation and Grobet's is an identity.

## M2. The statue that walks out of the museum

The stone goddess stands up, leaves her room, and runs for president. In La gira mamal de la Coatlicue, Jesusa becomes Coatlicue, the Mexica mother of the gods, the statue that stands in the Sala Mexica of the Museo Nacional de Antropologia, and speaks in the first person. The text, published in Debate Feminista, is the mechanism stated as a complaint: she has borne the destruction of her temples, the crushing of her followers, the theft of her gold pectoral and jade mask in the 1985 Christmas Eve robbery of the museum, and the indifference of officials who never bring schoolchildren to sing Las Mananitas to her, and now she demands what the pope gets. "A diferencia de otros idolos, nunca se me han hecho imprimir 500 000 carteles, jamas se me construyo un mamodromo ni se me proporciono un humilde mamamovil, nunca me facilitaron la maquinaria adecuada para poder besar el piso del aeropuerto, nunca he hecho una gira." No posters, no mamadrome, no mama-mobile, no machine to kiss the airport floor, no tour, not even to Chalco, not even to Chapultepec. The mechanism is the museum piece that refuses to be a piece: the statue is the true mother of Mexicans, confined in a room, and the performance lets her walk out and demand a campaign, a tour, a religion that is ecological, and a national crusade to recover the people's origins. The costume survives: it was transferred to New York University in November 2024, and the finding aid forbids researchers to interact with it, which is the mechanism's afterlife, the goddess still too alive to be handled. **Takeaway:** let the statue speak, and she will ask for a tour bus. Watch the demand: when a museum object complains about its treatment and announces its candidacy, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's still life (#345 M11) also let objects stand in for an absent person, but her vase and shoes stay on the table and mourn quietly; Rodriguez's statue gets up and runs for office, which is why her version is a campaign and Lara's is a portrait.

## M3. The old woman who returns

The heroine of the history book comes back, and she comes back wrong on purpose. Rodriguez's whole career is a series of returns: Frida Kahlo in Trece senoritas (1983); La Malinche in La Malinche en Dios T.V. (1991) and in La conquista segun la Malinche, where the translator of Cortes becomes a metro singer telling the true history of the founding of Tecnocratlan, and in later versions the interpreter for President Zedillo and the US Marines; Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz in Las trampas del fatuo (1990) and in Sor Juana en Almoloya (1995), where the seventeenth-century nun is imagined locked in the Almoloya prison and writes a letter answering the one Salinas, played as Zopilotea, sent to the press about the Colosio murder; the Soldadera in La soldadera autogena (2001), who proclaims a sexual and genetic revolution; and Coatlicue herself, the oldest of them. The mechanism is the recasting that restores what official culture cut out: in each case Rodriguez revisits the figure and emphasizes the dissident sexuality that was hidden or strategically forgotten, so that the Malinche who was a traitor becomes the first historical woman and the first actress, a polyglot who only wanted to stop being a slave, and Sor Juana becomes a prisoner of the present writing letters to a president. The Academia thesis on the two plays states the move plainly: the figures are reinvented as defenders of women and of the queer community. The returns are not tributes; they are corrections, and the correction is performed by a body that puts on the habit, the huipil, the uniform, and speaks in the first person. **Takeaway:** bring the heroine back, and let her say what the textbook cut out. Watch the return: when a figure from the history book appears on stage and corrects her own story in the first person, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Weiss's Venusina (#348 M8) was also a woman from somewhere else walking through a grey public space, but her alien came from Venus and had no history to correct; Rodriguez's women come from the history book and correct it, which is why her version is a revision and Weiss's is a landing.

## M4. The genre that eats every genre

The show refuses to be one kind of show, and the refusal is the form. Rodriguez's espectaculos do not adhere to traditional genre classification: Greek tragedy, cabaret, pre-Columbian myth, opera, revue, sketch, carpa, pastorela, political performance art, and they can change shape inside a single evening. Donna Giovanni takes Mozart and Da Ponte's Don Giovanni and recasts it with an all-female cast, cross-dressing, overlapping layers of disguise, music, wordplay and tableaux vivants, and tours it through Europe, the United States and Latin America for four years. Cosi fan tutte o la escuela de los amantes (1996-97) is a video-theatre-performance that parades Mauricio Garces, El Santo and Blue Demon through a lost Acapulco of the 1960s as an anti-tribute to Mexican popular cinema. El Concilio de Amor takes Panizza's 1895 blasphemy, a heavenly council that debates syphilis, and stages it with Jesusa as a naked, androgynous, cabaret devil. The CITRU essay names the move: cabaret is the mother genre that contains all the others, and Rodriguez's show is a journey that starts in traditional theatre, stops at opera and operetta, and ends in cabaret, which can then return to each of them whenever the drama requires. The mechanism is the pastiche that is also a parody: every genre is quoted, and every quotation is bent until it serves the argument. **Takeaway:** take every genre, and let the smallest one eat the rest. Watch the mixture: when an opera, a carpa sketch and a Greek tragedy share one evening and the cabaret holds them together, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's Tendedero (#344 M1) also mixed forms, a clothesline and a questionnaire, but her mixture is two things, a line and a card; Rodriguez's mixture is everything at once, which is why her version is a feast and Mayer's is a census.

## M5. The weekly newspaper made of bodies

The show is written this week, about this week, and next week it will be different. Rodriguez calls her genre journalistic: it gathers public opinion and popular humor, and it has to guess what is coming, because yesterday's newspaper is rubbish and the cabaret, like the press, lives by getting ahead of events. At El Habito the weekend show rewrote the week's news as sketches, songs and impersonations, improvised and updated so that several versions of a piece exist. The pastorela is the annual form of the same mechanism: every year a Nativity play that is really a satirical summary of the year, because the pastorela forces her to think about how the country is and what theme to take, and the theme in 1996 was the nightmare, because Mexico was a nightmare she could not wake from. El ejido en Navidad (c. 1991) is the pastorela as a fairytale gone wrong, with Cachirulo presenting and his television characters Bruja Escaldufa and Fanfarron bewitching it, to attack Salinas's amendment to Article 27 of the Constitution. The mechanism is the deadline made into a dramaturgy: the show must answer the news, and the news keeps coming, so the show never finishes, it only updates. In 2004 she made the mechanism prophetic on purpose, staging Enemigos de Fox: Club de Desobediencia Civil, a civil-disobedience show, two years before the 2006 election fraud and the resistance that followed, because, as she said in 2007, cabaret work consists of looking ahead and guessing what is coming. **Takeaway:** write the show this week, about this week, and let next week rewrite it. Watch the date: when a sketch names a scandal from this week's news and the audience laughs because they read it yesterday, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's archive (#346 M4) also kept the news, but her boxes kept it so it could be re-read forty years later; Rodriguez's show spends the news the same week, which is why her version is a newspaper and Jimenez's is an archive.

## M6. The pun that does the politics

The joke is the argument, and the argument is a word bent until it breaks. Rodriguez's humour is built out of puns, neologisms and wordplay, and the wordplay does the political work that a speech cannot. El ejido en Navidad turns on ejido and elegido, the communal land and the Chosen One, so that a Nativity play about the chosen child becomes a play about the ejido Salinas is selling off. Coatlicue's tour is a gira mamal, and the goddess demands a mamodromo and a mamamovil, a mummy-drome and a mummy-mobile, the pope's popemobile translated into the mother of the gods. La conquista segun la Malinche is built out of the verb decir in all its forms, because in Mexico, the text says, you can tell a whole story with decir, and the telling is the conquest. La serpiente enchilada asks, of the United States, "Pero ademas dicen llamarse America. Cuando se ha visto que un pais se apropie el nombre de un continente entero?" Arquetipas closes with Popocateptl, Moncayo's Huapango parodied into a song about the volcano and the trade agreement. The songs do the same work in music: "Tienes que decidir que prefieres que te mate: la pobreza, la miseria, el Tratado de Libre Comercio o el programa contra el hambre." The mechanism is the word that carries two meanings at once and refuses to choose between them, so that the audience hears the joke and the accusation in the same syllable. **Takeaway:** bend the word, and the word will do the accusing. Watch the pun: when one word means two things and the second meaning is the charge, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Weiss's wordplay (#348 M9) also put her name inside the word, POLArizado, POLAca, arTV, but her puns signed the work; Rodriguez's puns charge the defendant, which is why her version is an indictment and Weiss's is a signature.

## M7. The body where you put it

The question is never what the body does; it is where the body is put. Rodriguez has worked nude on stage in many variables through her whole career, and her own formulation, from the 2007 interview, is the mechanism stated as a question: where do you put your body, and why do you put your body there? A body on a stage is exposed; a body in front of the federal police or the army is exposed differently, and it is the same exposure, doubled. When she was left alone with two companions against a police cordon, afraid because she is asthmatic and tear gas could kill her, she took out a sandwich and ate it calmly, acting unafraid, and the acting was the work: being an actress let her face the moment. In Primero Sueno, performed at FOMMA in San Cristobal de las Casas in July 2009, an 82-minute solo, she strips away the levels of Sor Juana's poem and strips away her clothes at the same time, bringing words and body together. On Tuesday 20 November 2007, before a Lopez Obrador crowd in the Zocalo, she undressed in the square. The mechanism is the placement: the same nude body is art in the theatre, protest in the street, and a scandal in the newspaper, and the difference is the place, not the body. The stills viewed first-hand show the placement as a costume: in La serpiente enchilada she is nearly bare, wrapped in strings of glowing red chili lights, a serpent made of chilies, with a projection of green aliens behind her. **Takeaway:** the body is the same; the place decides what it means. Watch the placement: when the same bare body would be art on a stage and a crime in the street, and she puts it in the street, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Grobet's body (#347 M5) was also the medium, tearing out of a foil box and peeling photographs off itself, but her body performed for a camera in a gallery; Rodriguez's body performs for the police, which is why her version is a risk and Grobet's is a strip-tease.

## M8. The room that is a bar

The theatre is a bar, and the bar pays for the theatre. El Habito was a theatre-bar in Coyoacan, in the house of Salvador Novo, where the audience sat at tables with beer and the stage was close enough to touch, and the money from the bar rebuilt the Teatro de la Capilla next door. The mechanism is the room that dissolves the fourth wall by being a room first: the audience is not seated in the dark in rows, it is sitting at tables, drinking, talking back, and the show talks back to it. Rodriguez calls the move cabuleo, the teasing confrontation of the spectator, questioning the audience about its position on the political question of the moment, and the CITRU essay records that her sketches made the public a character in its own parodies. The same room brought the veterans back: Las Hermanas Aguila, Enrique Alonso Cachirulo, Chavela Vargas returned to the stage at El Habito, because a bar is where old performers belong. The mechanism is also economic and therefore political: a room that pays for itself does not need the state, and a room that does not need the state can say what the state does not want said, which is why the Department of Labor pursued El Habito from 2002 to 2006, in the NYU folder's wording, and why the last day of El Habito has its own folder in the archive. **Takeaway:** put the audience at tables with beer, and the wall comes down by itself. Watch the table: when the spectators are drinking and the show talks to them by name, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Weiss's monitor (#348 M2) also put the audience on the screen, but her audience watched itself on television; Rodriguez's audience is in the room with a beer, which is why her version is a bar and Weiss's is a broadcast.

## M9. The song that carries the argument

The argument is sung, and the singer is the co-author. Liliana Felipe, Rodriguez's partner since 1979, is not the accompaniment; she is the second voice of the work. She composes the music, writes the songs with Rodriguez, sits at the piano, and sings the commentary that the spoken text cannot carry. In Dialogos entre Darwin y Dios (2009), Felipe is at the piano as Darwin's assistant and then, in the same evening, as God's diligent receptionist-secretary, so the music changes jobs when the characters do. In Arquetipas (2004) her songs, "Tienes que decidir", "Mala", "El maiz", provide what the Scalar text calls a powerful antiphonal commentary, the personal and the political answering each other across the stage. In La serpiente enchilada the still viewed first-hand shows the mechanism as a picture: Jesusa ringed in chili lights at the piano, Felipe beside her in a red plaid shawl singing into a microphone, the song and the body in the same frame. The mechanism is the duet as a form of argument: one voice speaks, the other sings, and the song says what the speech leaves out, because a song can be remembered and repeated in the street after the show is over. The songs outlived the room: "Tienes que decidir", once available only at the El Habito box office, is now, in Day's words, available in video and on the internet, serving millions where it once served a few dozen. **Takeaway:** let the song say what the speech cannot, and the song will leave the room. Watch the piano: when the singer at the piano answers the speaker with a song, and the song is the sharper of the two, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's song was a sentence written inside a drawing (#345 M1), but her sentence stayed on the page; Felipe's song leaves the theatre, which is why her version is a broadcast and Lara's is a diary.

## M10. The street as the next stage

The cabaret leaves the bar and takes the avenue. After the presidential election of Sunday 2 July 2006, Rodriguez and Felipe gave El Habito to Las Reinas Chulas and took the work to the street, because, as she said in 2007, the street was asking them to come out and do the work there. The mechanism is the massive cabaret: Resistencia Creativa, born in El Habito in the days after the fraud, ran the cultural stage in the Zocalo through the planton that blocked 18 kilometres of Reforma for 48 days by her count, 50 by the Scalar account, with Rodriguez carrying the stage day and night among people who had never been to a theatre. The street work kept the cabaret's tools and changed their scale: two hundred people walked into a Nike store in a rich suburb's mall, held the door, and did a television programme inside about sneakers that cost five dollars to make and sell for a hundred and fifty; they walked into banks as customers, two hundred at a time, dressed as Pepe el Toro, Pedro Infante's beloved character, and the security guard who did not want to shake the hand of Pepe el Toro shook it anyway, because he could no longer tell where the icon ended and the person began. Sor Juana walked too: in Day's account, Rodriguez crossed Mexico City in a nun's habit toward the Zocalo, carrying a huge placard that rewrote Sor Juana's redondillas as "curas necios que acusais a la resistencia en accion", foolish priests accusing the resistance, among a sea of yellow AMLO t-shirts. The mechanism is the stage that moves: the same performance, the same costume, the same joke, in a place where the audience did not buy a ticket and cannot leave by the exit. **Takeaway:** when the room closes, take the show to the avenue, and let the avenue be the room. Watch the crowd: when a nun walks through a demonstration carrying a placard, and the demonstration is the audience, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's march photographs (#346 M3) also put women in the street, but her camera stood still and the march passed it; Rodriguez walks with the march in costume, which is why her version is a performance and Jimenez's is a vigil.

## M11. The workshop as the work

The theatre goes to the villages, and the villages keep the theatre. Between 2001 and 2004, with Liliana Felipe and Regina Orozco, Rodriguez ran empowerment workshops for indigenous and peasant women, and workshops on rethinking masculinity for indigenous and peasant men, in 18 states: 21 workshops by the NYU count, 17 plus 4 by the Spanish Wikipedia count. The mechanism is theatre in function of health, as she put it in 2007: five years of workshops that sat halfway between theatre and politics, because they were theatre made for the sake of women's health, and health is political. The state health programme made the work possible, because it had spent thirty years finding the women leaders of remote communities, and without it the same work would have taken thirty or forty years. The workshops produced six videos, on violence, alcoholism, drug addiction and three further themes, to be sent around the country, and letters from the participants survive in the NYU archive. The mechanism is also a lesson in listening: her friend Hermelinda Tiburcio Cayetano, an Amusga woman from Guerrero with a doctorate in psychology, told her that for her people a tree does not have a root, a trunk and a crown, it has little feet, little arms, hair and shoulders, and Rodriguez carries the sentence as proof that she understands nothing yet, and that understanding would take ten or twenty years of living in a community. **Takeaway:** take the theatre to the village, and let the village teach the theatre. Watch the tree: when a tree is described as having little feet and shoulders, and the description is the lesson, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Weiss's teaching (#348 M13) also made a movement out of a classroom, but her students picked up garbage in a studio and made video; Rodriguez's students were village women learning about their own health, which is why her version is a clinic and Weiss's is a school.

## M12. The silence that speaks

The loudest woman in Mexico performs without saying a word. In Las Horas de Belen, the piece about the women locked in Belen, the charitable home founded in 1683 that became Mexico City's most terrifying prison, Rodriguez performs a silent movement piece. The Artforum review describes her as a woman in a muslin shift, her hair braided in the Tehuantepec style of Frida Kahlo, appearing like a beseeching figure in an ex-voto painting. She embodies the modern woman, in the Playbill account, imprisoned by her routines of ironing, shopping, cooking, dancing, sleeping and fighting, while around her Catherine Sasanov's twelve poems are sung and spoken and Liliana Felipe's music plays. The mechanism is the silence inside the noise: a performer whose whole art is talk, jokes, songs and impersonation takes the words away, and the body has to do the talking, ironing and shopping and fighting without a sentence to explain it. The piece was devised over two years across the border, premiered in March 1999 at the Festival del Centro Historico in the Claustro de Sor Juana, then opened at P.S. 122 in New York in May 1999, and won the Obie for Best Actor. The silence is also the subject: the women of Belen were locked up precisely so that they could not speak, and the piece answers their silence with a silence that is chosen. **Takeaway:** take the words away from the woman who talks, and her body will say what the words could not. Watch the quiet: when the loudest performer on the bill stands silent in a muslin shift and the room goes quiet with her, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's silence (#345 M3) was also a page that said what the sentence could not, but her silence stayed in a notebook; Rodriguez's silence is performed in a cloister in front of a crowd, which is why her version is a vigil and Lara's is a diary.

## M13. The thousand faces

One woman, a thousand faces, and the faces keep changing. The CITRU essay calls her, after Joseph Campbell, a hero of a thousand faces, and the description fits the catalogue: a devil in El Concilio de Amor, naked and androgynous and cabaret; Darwin and God in Dialogos entre Darwin y Dios, both played by her in one evening, with Felipe at the piano; Freaka Kahlo in Arquetipas, a queer archetype among the Coatlicue and the Serpiente Enchilada; the Soldadera who announces that there is no need to divide humanity into him and her, because now we can be him and her in one body, dos presentaciones en un mismo envase, two presentations in one container. The mechanism is the change itself: the face is not a disguise put on over a fixed self, it is the work, and the work is the proof that a self can be remade as often as the country needs remaking. The paper wedding dresses viewed first-hand are the mechanism as an object: two dresses made of paper, one ruched with paper roses, the other beaded with a huge paper headdress, worn by two women for a wedding the law did not yet recognize, and kept afterwards in a museum on clear mannequins, the bodies gone and the paper remaining. The mechanism is also the joke about it: when she was asked who she was, she answered, in the line quoted by La Jornada de Oriente, "Me defino como mi enemigo: lo peor que conozco soy yo", I define myself as my enemy, the worst thing I know is me. **Takeaway:** change the face, and the face will change the country. Watch the change: when one performer plays both Darwin and God in a single evening, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Grobet's androgyne (#347 M11) was also a figure whose sex could not be settled, carrying fire, but her Prometheus was one figure made once for a video installation; Rodriguez's thousand faces change every night, which is why her version is a wardrobe and Grobet's is a myth.

## M14. The Senate as a stage

The last stage is the chamber, and the cabaret goes in with her. In 2018 Rodriguez took the Senate seat of Olga Sanchez Cordero, who left to become Secretary of the Interior, and served as senator for Morena from Thursday 29 November 2018 to Thursday 26 August 2021. The mechanism is the performance moved into the institution without changing its tools: she defended the native corn in the Senate with a performance, presenting her bill for the protection of native maize before the chamber and relaunching the Sin Maiz No Hay Pais campaign; she wore Olmec heads in defense of the maize; she opposed bullfighting from the tribune by simulating a torture, pretending to hang from a branch and drive a rod into herself; she defended abortion, women, indigenous people and marijuana from the floor. In 2018 she organized the Zocalo ceremony in which Lopez Obrador received the baston de mando, the staff of command, from the indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples. The mechanism is the tribune as a cabaret table: the same body, the same costume changes, the same jokes, now addressed to the chamber instead of the bar, and the chamber has to listen because she holds a seat in it. In 2022 Lopez Obrador proposed her as ambassador to Panama, after Pedro Salmeron was withdrawn; Panama granted its beneplacito, the nomination did not reach the Senate in the February 2022 batch, and she never took the post, which is the mechanism's limit, the one stage that would not take her. **Takeaway:** take the seat, and the chamber becomes the room. Watch the tribune: when a senator performs a bill instead of reading it, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's archive (#344 M5) also moved the work into an institution, but her institution was a library of her own making; Rodriguez moved into the Senate itself, which is why her version is an occupation and Mayer's is a collection.

## Evolution

1955-1979, the child who misheard the word. Born in Mexico City on Thursday 3 November 1955. Her own line, carried by Taylor and Costantino, is the origin story: "When I was a child they told me I was autistic and I understood artistic, and that is why I dedicated myself to this." She studied at the Centro Universitario de Teatro from 1971 to 1973, under Julio Castillo, and worked with his Sombras Blancas, making scenography for Arde Pinocho and for Ionesco's Que formidable burdel. In 1979 she met Liliana Felipe, and the partnership that would make the work began. No work of her own from before 1980 survives in the catalogue, and the gap matters: the first thing we know about her as an author is the cabaret, not a student play.

1980-1989, the room and the tour. El Cuervo and El Fracaso in 1980, Grupo Divas A.C. the same year, Como va la noche, Macbeth? in 1980, Trece senoritas in 1983, Donna Giovanni premiered in 1983 and toured Europe, the United States and Latin America through 1987, Atracciones Fenix in 1986, Ambrosio o La Fabula del Mal Amor from 1986, El Concilio de Amor in 1988, El tour del corazon and Yourcenar and Crimen in 1989. The mechanisms that appear first are the genre that eats every genre, M4, and the old woman who returns, M3, because the first works are already adaptations that change the sex of the cast and the century of the heroine. The death threats after El Concilio de Amor, from Pro-vida Catholics and neo-Nazis, are the first proof that the mask that is a face, M1, was heard as a threat.

1990-1999, El Habito and the masterwork. El Habito opens in 1990, La gira mamal de la Coatlicue in 1990, La Malinche en Dios T.V. in 1991, El ejido en Navidad around 1991, Cielo de abajo in 1992, Sor Juana en Almoloya in 1995, Cosi fan tutte in 1996-97, Las Horas de Belen devised in 1998 and premiered in March 1999, the Obie in 1999 or 2000, El derecho de abortar in 1999, the Salinas house protest in 1999. The mechanisms that appear now are the statue that walks out of the museum, M2, the weekly newspaper made of bodies, M5, and the silence that speaks, M12, because the work has a room, a rhythm and a company, and the room lets her try the quiet piece alongside the loud ones.

2000-2005, the workshops and the handover. La serpiente enchilada in 2000, La soldadera autogena in 2001, the indigenous empowerment workshops from 2001 to 2004, New War, New War in 2002, Enemigos de Fox in 2003-04, El Maiz and the Guggenheim pre-Hispanic cabaret in 2004, Arquetipas in 2004, and the handover of El Habito to Las Reinas Chulas in 2005. The mechanisms that appear now are the workshop as the work, M11, and the thousand faces, M13, at their most deliberate, because the work is no longer only hers: it is taught in villages, sung by Felipe, and handed to a younger company.

2006-2018, the street. The election of Sunday 2 July 2006, the Zocalo rally of Sunday 30 July 2006, the 48-day planton on Reforma, Resistencia Creativa meeting every Sunday at the Hemiciclo a Juarez, the television programme at one in the morning on Tuesdays, the nude in the Zocalo on Tuesday 20 November 2007, Dialogos entre Darwin y Dios in 2009, Primero Sueno at FOMMA in July 2009, Las cartas de Frida in 2011, Apoidea in 2014. The mechanisms that appear now are the street as the next stage, M10, and the body where you put it, M7, at their most exposed, because the stage is the avenue and the audience is the police.

2018-2026, the chamber and the archive. Senator from Thursday 29 November 2018 to Thursday 26 August 2021, the baston de mando ceremony in 2018, the Panama proposal in 2022, the papers at NYU, the Coatlicue costume transferred in November 2024, and the 71st birthday on Tuesday 3 November 2026. The mechanism that appears now is the Senate as a stage, M14, and the archive, because the work has to survive her, and it does, in folders and in a costume no researcher is allowed to touch.

## Aesthetic logic

Rodriguez's aesthetic is the cheapest possible room, the loudest possible joke, and the oldest possible heroine, aimed at the most powerful possible target, performed by one body that keeps changing its face. The materials are a bar, a microphone, a piano, a paper dress, a mask, a chili light, a placard, and a body: the materials of the cabaret, the street and the church, and the cheapness is the argument, because a work made in a bar cannot be stopped by a ministry, and a joke told in a bar can be retold in the street the next morning. The palette is the red of the chili lights and the red of the gallery wall behind the paper wedding dresses, the white of the huipil and the paper, the black of the stage and the Senate chamber, and the gold of the museum's stolen pectoral: red, white and black are the first colours, and the work returns to them every decade. The composition is the duet: Jesusa and Liliana, the speaker and the singer, the body and the piano, the mask and the face, the nun and the president, Darwin and God, and the duet is the argument, because two voices can say what one cannot. The motion is the change: a face put on, a costume changed, a poem peeled, a street crossed, a room left behind, and the change is fast, because the news is fast and the joke has to keep up with it. The scale is the smallest room and the largest square: a bar in Coyoacan and the Zocalo, a table and a tribune, a paper dress and a Senate seat, and the work moves between them without changing its tools. The logic is the newspaper: the work is made week by week, year by year, and the years accumulate into a life, and the life is the work. The viewer is not a spectator but a witness, and sometimes a defendant: the show talks to the audience by name, asks it where it stands, and the answer is part of the show.

## Network

The network that is evidenced, not assumed. Liliana Felipe: the strongest link, partner since 1979, composer, singer and co-founder of everything, El Cuervo, Grupo Divas, El Habito, Teatro de la Capilla; the music is the second voice of the work, and the two married in a public ceremony on Friday 3 August 2007. Julio Castillo: teacher at the Centro Universitario de Teatro, director of Sombras Blancas, the man whose work marked her career. Salvador Novo: founder of Teatro de la Capilla, the room she and Felipe rented and rebuilt. Tito Vasconcelos: fellow cabaret pioneer, founder of CabareTito in the Zona Rosa in 1998, and with Rodriguez the teacher of Las Reinas Chulas. Astrid Hadad: fellow cabaret pioneer, whose La multimamada (1996) the CITRU essay cites as the parallel to the Coatlicue. Regina Orozco: ex-Divas A.C., soprano-arrabalera, collaborator on New War, New War (2002). Las Reinas Chulas: disciples of Tito Vasconcelos and Jesusa Rodriguez, who took El Habito in 2005 and renamed it El Vicio. Ruth Maleczech and Mabou Mines: Las Horas de Belen, the Obie, the two-year collaboration across the border. Diana Taylor and the Hemispheric Institute: Taylor wrote the TDR piece on Cielo de abajo and the Scalar chapter, and Rodriguez is the first artist named Senior Fellow of the Institute. Carmen Boullosa: El tour del corazon (1989). Elena Poniatowska: close friend, defended by Rodriguez in 2006. Hermelinda Tiburcio Cayetano: Amusga friend and teacher of the two-worlds lesson, the tree with little feet. Super Barrio: the 1999 protest before Salinas's house. Chavela Vargas, Las Hermanas Aguila and Enrique Alonso Cachirulo: the veterans who returned to the stage at El Habito. Malu Huacuja: co-writer of El ejido en Navidad. Dario T. Pie, Paloma Woolrich, Horacio Acosta, Juan Ibarra and Ana Ofelia Murguia: collaborators and cast. Sabina Berman: playwright and fellow public intellectual, paired with Rodriguez by Stuart Day as the two women who claimed the national stage after 2006. Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz: artistic kinship, the lifelong interlocutor, the poem memorized over ten years. Olga Sanchez Cordero: the senator whose seat Rodriguez took as substitute in 2018. Marcela Rodriguez Ramirez: sister, composer, author of Las cartas de Frida, staged by Jesusa in 2011. Gabriela Rodriguez Ramirez: sister, sexologist. Andres Manuel Lopez Obrador: the candidate whose campaigns she staged and whose baston de mando ceremony she organized in 2018.

The links that run through the chain, not around it: Mayer, Lara and Weiss are the other women of the 1978 invitation, and the link is the list, not a collaboration; no joint work between Rodriguez and any of the three was found in the sources read. Grobet made Se escoge el tiempo with Lara in 1983, and Rodriguez's link to Grobet runs through Lara and through the room, not through a work of her own. Jimenez's archive holds photographs of the feminist movement Rodriguez performed inside, but no direct contact was evidenced.

## Study-method note

This study was written from texts read end to end and from eight images viewed first-hand; no performance was watched in full. That limit is stated in the Depth line and it shapes the mechanisms: where a mechanism rests on a still image, the study says so, and where it rests on a text, the study quotes the text. The corrections list in the research notes is part of the study, not an appendix to it: the doorway correction (what Rodriguez showed at Salon 77-78 is unverified), the El Fracaso versus El Cuervo naming, the El Habito dates, the Donna Giovanni, Coatlicue, Sor Juana en Almoloya, Las Horas de Belen and Cosi fan tutte dates, the three marriage dates, the workshop counts, the planton length, and the Panama ambassadorship were all carried as conflicts, not resolved by picking the version that told the tidier story. Weekdays were verified with date -d for the birth (Thursday 3 November 1955), the 71st birthday (Tuesday 3 November 2026), the 2006 election (Sunday 2 July 2006), the Zocalo rally (Sunday 30 July 2006), the Senate term (Thursday 29 November 2018 to Thursday 26 August 2021), the wedding ceremony (Friday 3 August 2007) and the Zocalo nude (Tuesday 20 November 2007). Diacritics are stripped throughout, in the house style, so Spanish titles appear without accents. The study does not claim Rodriguez as a feminist without the nuance the sources carry: she contributes to Debate Feminista and is treated as a feminist artist in the literature, and she is also on record, via Kelty, as rejecting the feminist label for the restrictions it imposes.
