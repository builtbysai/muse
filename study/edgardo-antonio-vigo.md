## The life: the clerk who sewed files by day and unmade the object by night

Edgardo Antonio Vigo was born in La Plata on 28 December 1928, the Day of the Innocents, son of a carpenter father and a mother who did housework, and he lived, studied, worked, formed his family and made his art in La Plata all his life (C10102). The birth year is the majority date: Padin's 1997 memorial says 1927, one scholarly article gives 1929, and the 1928 date is carried by Spanish Wikipedia, MoMA, ICAA, the CAEV biography and the UNLP (C10100). He enrolled at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes of La Plata in 1950 and graduated Professor of Drawing in 1953 (C10106). At twenty-two he began work in the Poder Judicial, the provincial judiciary, sewing files together, and stayed in that work more than forty years (C10107). By day he worked in La Plata's Ministry of Justice; in his free time he edited avant-garde magazines, wrote poetry, made prints, built Neo-Dada objects he called cosas, performed, and taught drawing at the Colegio Nacional (C10108). His office in the Tribunals was hexagonal in shape, a detail Carlos Ginzburg later linked to the title of his magazine Hexagono '71 (C10109). His wife Elena Comas -- Elena Ana Francisca Comas in full -- worked with him on his magazines, including WC, and translated books he brought back from Europe (C10103).

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is not a single morning, as with Uriburu, but a slow thinning-out: the made thing gives way to the instruction, the instruction to the journey, the journey to the file.

(1) Concrete beginnings and the useless machine, 1953-1961. In 1953 Vigo travelled to France on a scholarship with his fellow student Miguel Angel Guerena, collecting papers for eight months for an organisation helping foreign students, and there met the Venezuelan artist Jesus Rafael Soto; the encounter with Soto and with kinetic art research shaped his production after his return in 1954 (C10110). No source read for this run links him to Atelier 17 or to Stanley William Hayter: that link is unverified, not established (C10149). On his return he showed polychrome wood, geometric structures and other artefacts at the Asociacion Sarmiento in La Plata, with his future wife Elena Comas; the works were inscribed in concrete art and sought to increase the participation of the spectator -- and the proposal was not understood: the works were attacked by critics in the press and destroyed by the visiting public, and the show closed suddenly after three days (C10111). Vigo treated the episode as a sign that he was not wrong, and kept on with atypical and provocative presentations. In 1955 he made his first mathematical poems; in 1956 he wrote the bases of the Relativuzgir's movement, proposing to escape from the representation of movement toward movement itself -- the word joins relative, electricity as an acting element, and the property of turning (C10112). In 1957 he exhibited eight impossible machines, collages mounted on wood, and one useless machine, at the Asociacion del Poder Judicial, under the name Vigho of the Relativuzgir's Movement (C10113). His useless machines of this period included the Cargador electrico of 1957, the Bi-tricicleta ingenua con ruedas incapaces de rodar of 1960, and the Palanganometro mecedor (que no se mece) para criticos de arte, dated 1963 in the CAEV chronology and 1965 in other accounts (C10115). As a guest of the Grupo Si, a La Plata group made up mostly of informalist painters, he exhibited Maquina inutil 0'0003 in 1961 (C10116). The 1965 presentation of the Palanganometro produced indignation among those present and promoted his decision to avoid institutional spaces (C10119).

(2) Magazines as the work, 1958-1969. Between 1958 and 1960 he published WC with Osvaldo Gigli, Miguel Angel Guerena and Elena Comas: an envelope with loose sheets containing texts on contemporary art and works by the editors (C10114). In 1960 he published the three numbers of DRKW'60, a magazine of his sole authorship, and in 1962, with the Grupo de los Elefantes, BFGV'62, an envelope with poems and wood engravings he called literary object-things (C10114, C10118). In 1962 he began to edit Diagonal Cero, an experimental magazine pioneering in visual poetry, which ran to 28 numbers until 1969 (December 1968 in the CAEV chronology, which excepts number 25, dedicated to nothing) (C10117). Its first stage, to number 19 in September 1966, promoted the poetry and theory of Argentine and international artists; from number 20, dedicated to the Nueva Poesia Platense in December 1966, it became the organ of the Movimiento Diagonal Cero -- Vigo, Jorge de Lujan Gutierrez, Omar Gancedo, Carlos Ginzburg and Luis Pazos, with Ginzburg joining after Gancedo left in 1967 (C10118). In 1967, invited by the French poet Julien Blaine, he exhibited in the Premier inventaire de la poesie elementaire at the Denise Davy gallery in Paris, and the Paris publisher Contexte published his Poemas Matematicos Barrocos, six coloured sheets with holes and folds the reader could manipulate to create new poems (C10120). Also in 1967 he founded the Museo de la Xilografia, a travelling institution made up of boxes of works ready to be mounted and exhibited: about 3,000 prints, built from donations, with no building, its aim the non-traditional dissemination and circulation of wood engraving (C10120, C10121). In 1968 he began the senalamientos: nineteen actions between 1968 and 1992, pointing at ordinary things -- a traffic light, a plaza, a buried block -- and inviting aesthetic attention, often carried out outside artistic institutions, many times without public or publicity, documented precisely through texts, photographs and printed matter that often circulated through the mail (C10122). In 1969 he published the text Un arte a realizar and organised the Expo/Internacional de Novisima Poesia at the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella in Buenos Aires, bringing together visual and sound poets from fifteen countries (C10124); the same year he made his first projects to be realised -- the (in)film Blanco sobre blanco, a homage to Malevich, to be projected on the backs of the public, and Obras (in)completas, a postal sending of four labels to be used freely -- and delivered to Professor Angel Osvaldo Nessi the statement Hacia un arte tocable, summing up the lines of intervention that defined his poetics (C10125).

(3) CAYC, Hexagono '71 and the political turn, 1970-1975. In 1970 Vigo was one of exactly three artists invited, with Luis Fernando Benedit and Nicolas Garcia Uriburu, to De la Figuracion al Arte de Sistemas at the Museo Emilio Caraffa in Cordoba, 14-31 August 1970 -- the show where, for the first time in Argentina, the category of "systems art" was mentioned (C10200). What Vigo showed at Cordoba is NOT established: the digitised catalog was not opened, and no source read this run names his specific works there (C10201). He did NOT join the Grupo de los Trece when it formed in 1971, although he stayed involved with the CAYC in "a relationship based on mutual collaboration: his magazine reported on the CAYC's activities and artists, and the group invited him to their exhibitions and discussion forums"; he also wrote the theoretical text for the group's December 1972 show El grupo de los trece en arte de sistemas, published in the CAYC newsletter with his name omitted, and reprinted in Hexagono '71 (C10203). Between 1971 and 1975 he directed and published Hexagono '71, thirteen numbers, an assembling magazine -- an envelope containing illustrations, visual poems, works to be created, essays, drawings, stories, telegrams and calls for works, with no editorial commentary, page numbers or editorial credits -- which from 1973 took on a more political profile, Vigo adding the slogan Eso si, la mas peligrosa beside his name on the cover (C10128, C10167, C10168). In 1972 he published La calle: escenario del arte actual, proposing the street be recovered as a place of expression; his Senalamiento XI, Souvenir del dolor, in memory of those who fell at Trelew, circulated during 1972, and the Trelew work of 1974 remembered the sixteen prisoners assassinated in the Trelew jail on 22 August 1972, one page for each fallen body (C10129). In 1973 he made with Juan Carlos Romero, Luis Pazos, Perla Benveniste and Eduardo Leonetti the collective work Proceso a nuestra realidad, better known as Ezeiza es Trelew: a brick wall crossing a room of the Museo de Arte Moderno, covered with posters showing the Ezeiza events and the faces of the militants shot at Trelew, with an acrylic drop of blood spectators could hang around their necks (C10130).

(4) The mail circuit and the archive, 1975-1997. Vigo began his mail art activities in the mid-1960s, sending mathematical poems to Julien Blaine and his circle, which widened to the United States, the Eastern Bloc, Japan and all over Latin America (C10131). From the mid-1970s he devoted himself fundamentally to mail art (C10132). In 1975 he organised, with Horacio Zabala, the Ultima Exposicion Internacional de Arte Correo at the Galeria Arte Nuevo in Buenos Aires, 5 December 1975, the first international mail art show in Argentina and the second in Latin America; the participant count conflicts -- 210 artists from 25 countries in the MoMA account, 199 participants from 24 countries in Vigo's own 1984 survey -- and is recorded, not settled (C10132, C10205). Their 1975 text Arte-Correo: Una nueva forma de expresion defined the mechanism (see M4). He did not know, when he began making envelopes, postcards and stamps, that Ray Johnson's New York Correspondence School had been laying the foundations for such practices since 1960, as he wrote to the London artist Julia Tant on 9 August 1995: parallel invention, not contact (C10133, C10211). After the civic-military coup of 1976, his eldest son Abel Luis, Palomo -- born 16 May 1957, a 19-year-old Physical Education student at the UNLP -- was kidnapped at his home, Avenida 7 No. 540, La Plata, in the early hours of 30 July 1976, and remains disappeared (C10104, C10134). Vigo's own unpublished manuscript describes an armed group, which he later learned belonged to the Argentine army, bursting into his house, covering his head with a bag, and taking his son (C10135). From then on he used the mail-art circuit to inform and alert others about living conditions in Argentina under the regime (C10136). Between 1977 and 1983 he worked jointly with Graciela Gutierrez Marx under the signature G. E. MARXVIGO, editing Nuestro Libro Internacional de Estampillas y Matasellos and campaigning with "Set Free Palomo" stamps (C10137, C10140). In November 1983 he carried out, in the courtyard of the Faculty of Fine Arts, the action Siembra de 30.000 semillas de amor, accompanied by relatives of disappeared people (C10140). In the 1980s he edited the 20 numbers of Nuestro Libro Internacional de Estampillas y Matasellos and the boxes of Biopsia, his personal archive organised chronologically from 1953 to 1997 -- 37 boxes in one CAEV-based account, 34 in another scholarly account, conflict recorded (C10139). Late recognition came slowly: the Fundacion San Telmo retrospective in 1991, the XXII Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1994, the 1954-1994 retrospective in La Plata in 1994, the First Bienal del Mercosur in 1996 (C10142, C10143, C10144). He died in La Plata on 4 November 1997, aged 68 (the Castagnino+macro page gives 4 December 1997, probably a transcription error; Padin's memorial says aged 70, which follows from its 1927 birth year) (C10145). Before he died he ceded custody of his work and papers to the Fundacion Centro de Artes Visuales de La Plata; the Centro de Arte Experimental Vigo was founded in 1998 at Calle 15 No. 1187, La Plata, in the house that once belonged to his father (C10145). Posthumous retrospectives: The Unmaker of Objects at MoMA, 2 April-30 June 2014, and Works 1953-1997 at the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires in 2016; in 2017 La Plata declared him a Distinguished Personality post mortem (C10146).

## M1 -- The useless machine: a machine that runs and achieves nothing

The first mechanism is the refusal of function. Around 1955 Vigo worked on "theories and designs for his 'useless and impossible machines'", and built his first objects: the Bi-tricicleta ingenua con ruedas incapaces de rodar -- a bicycle with wheels incapable of rolling -- and the Palanganometro mecedor (que no se mece) para criticos de arte -- a washbasin-measurer that does not rock, made for art critics (W04) (C10115, C10155). In 1957 he had already exhibited eight impossible machines and one useless machine at the Asociacion del Poder Judicial (C10113). The watchable behaviour is the viewer's attempt to read the machine: the form promises a function -- wheels, a rocker, a meter -- and the function cancels itself on inspection. The machine does not break; it was built to not work, and the joke lands on the institution that expects art to work, too: the Palanganometro is addressed to critics, and its 1965 presentation produced indignation among those present and promoted his decision to avoid institutional spaces (C10119). The Relativuzgir's theory behind it, from 1956, proposed escaping from the representation of movement toward movement itself (C10112) -- the machine is the theory made into an object that cannot be used.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M1 needs a machine the viewer tries to operate and that refuses, politely and completely. The refusal is the work; a machine that half-works is a broken machine, not a useless one.

## M2 -- The clock that tells the wrong time

The timekeeping form, filled with the wrong content. W07 Reloj Erotico (1967), published in Diagonal Cero 24 alongside works by Carlos Ginzburg and Jorge de Lujan Gutierrez, is a visual poem in the shape of a clock (C10159). The mechanism is the substitution: the viewer looks for the time and finds a poem, or a body, instead, and must re-read the dial as a sentence (C10179). No separate clock object with dimensions was found, so this mechanism is carried by the Diagonal Cero 24 entry, and by the wider Relojes idea of a clock as a visual poem -- including the Reloj inutil shown with the Bi-tricicleta at the Pequena Galeria de Arte de Radio Universidad (C10116). The clock does not tell the time wrong by accident, the way a broken clock does; it tells a different kind of time on purpose, and the viewer's first act -- checking the hour -- is the misreading the work corrects.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M2 should show a clock face and let the viewer try to read the time, then discover the hands point at words, or at a body, or at nothing the clock should say. Do not make the clock merely broken; the wrongness must be deliberate and readable.

## M3 -- The poem that must be unfolded, rotated, or opened

Reading as a physical act. W06 Poeme Mathematique Baroque (1967), published in Paris by Contexte: six coloured sheets with holes and folds that the reader could manipulate to create new poems (C10120, C10158). W08 Poemas matematicos (in)comestibles (1968): two welded aluminium cans -- sardine cans per Richard Saltoun, tuna cans per Padin's memorial, conflict recorded -- sealing mathematical poems inside, inedible by design (C10160, C10161). W10 Poemas (in)sonoros: un disco para mirar (1969): a disc to look at, a record that is not played but seen (C10163). W14 Hexagono '71 (1971-1975): an assembling magazine distributed as an envelope, not a bound magazine, its contents loose, with no page numbers or editorial credits, systematised by letters (C10167). The mechanism is the same in all four: the poem is not finished until it is handled -- opened, unfolded, turned, or left sealed. The reader's hands do the reading, and the reading changes the poem: the Contexte sheets make new poems when manipulated; the welded cans can only be read by being opened, which destroys the work; the Hexagono envelope has no fixed order, so every reader assembles a different magazine.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M3 must make the viewer do something with their hands before anything is read. A poem on a screen that can be read by looking is not an M3 piece; the handling IS the reading.

## M4 -- The mail circuit: the post is the medium

The central mechanism, and the one the study is built on. Vigo and Zabala's 1975 definition: mail art is not mailed art. A finished sculpture sent by post merely uses transport; in mail art "the fact that the work must travel a set distance is part of its structure, is the work itself", and the recipient stops being a collector and becomes an "incidental custodian" who must circulate the work onward (C10204, C10214). The watchable behaviour is the journey accumulating on the work: stamps, postmarks, envelopes and returns are the content, and the viewer reads the journey, not the object (C10181). W15 Accion de investigar una accion (1973): Vigo selected an envelope he had mailed to various addressees and later returned to the sender's address, withdrew each letterhead from the circuit, made a photographic record with Juan Jose Esteves, and filed the envelope, photos and explanation as an investigated object (C10169). W20 Biopsia (1993-1997): paper envelopes with rubber stamps, handmade postage stamps and hand lettering, filed as a life record (C10176, C10177). The circuit is also the survival channel: after 1976 Vigo used the mail-art circuit to inform and alert others about living conditions under the regime, and the "Set Free Palomo" stamps travelled the network (C10136, C10140). The distance is not a delay between making and showing; it is the making.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M4 needs an envelope that travels, gathers marks, and comes back changed -- and the change is the work. A letter that arrives clean has not travelled; a piece that shows only the envelope and not the journey is a picture of mail art, not mail art.

## M5 -- The stamp and the envelope as artwork: the fake official mark

Handmade stamps, seals and cancellations that mimic official postal authority while having no postal value. W20 Biopsia: envelopes with rubber stamps and handmade postage stamps that look official but lack postal value (C10176, C10177). W18-W19: the G. E. Marx Vigo duo and the Multiples acumulados box, rubber stamps, woodcuts and letterpress -- Set Palomo Free is rubber stamp, woodcut and letterpress on a sheet 26.2 x 27.9 cm (C10173, C10174). W17: the 1975 mail art show itself, whose catalogue sits in the Tate Archive (C10171). The watchable behaviour is the double-take: the viewer first takes the mark as real -- a postmark, a seal, a cancellation -- then notices it is drawn, stamped or invented, and the authority of the mark collapses into play (C10182). The fake mark is also the political instrument: under a regime that controlled the post, inventing your own stamps and postmarks is a small act of counter-authority, and "Set Free Palomo" is a stamp that asks the postal system to carry a demand the state would not.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M5 should show a mark that looks official until the viewer looks twice. Do not make the stamp decorative -- the forgery is the point -- and do not give it real postal value, or it stops being fake.

## M6 -- The viewer-completed object: poetry to be made

An instruction, invitation or blank that only becomes a work when the recipient acts. W11 Actos a Realizar no 0001/69 and Obras (in) Completas (1969): printed instructions and letterheads to be completed by the participant -- the Actos text invites the recipient to approach or move away from a place bearing the invitation and to pronounce, mumble, sing, whistle, shake or wobble the body (C10164). W12 Senalamiento V (1970): take chalk, mark a cross or the limit of one or many paving stones, stand inside the marked area, turn 360 degrees, record within yourself what you see, draw conclusions -- "you have definitively created" (C10165). W19 Multiples acumulados: an unbound box of cards the viewer arranges in any order (C10183). MoMA's summary of the whole practice: Vigo "developed instructions, actions, and visual poems to be carried out or completed by others" (C10214). The mechanism makes the spectator a co-author, in Padin's phrase -- and the work does not exist until the recipient does something. The 1969 text Un arte a realizar says it plainly: the work is a modifiable project, and the spectator's active participation forms the artistic experience (C10125).

**Takeaway:** a piece on M6 must hand the viewer a task and wait. If the piece completes itself, it is not M6; the incompleteness is the work, and the viewer's act is the completion.

## M7 -- The signalling of the ordinary: Senalamiento

Pointing, and nothing else. The senalamientos, nineteen actions between 1968 and 1992, point at an everyday scene or object and invite the viewer to regard it aesthetically; nothing is added except attention and a frame (C10122, C10184). W09 Manojo de Semaforos (1968): an invitation to see a handful of traffic lights -- the traffic light at the crossing of avenues 1 and 60 in La Plata, viewed from an aesthetic point of view (C10122, C10162). W12 Senalamiento V (1970): a walk to the Plaza Ruben Dario, a chalk square, a 360-degree turn (C10165). W19 Senalamiento IX (1990-91): burying a wood block, unearthing it a year later, legally documenting both events, then cutting the block into seventy pieces (C10175). The watchable behaviour is the smallest possible: the ordinary object stays ordinary and becomes a work by being signalled. The mechanism is the inverse of the useless machine: M1 builds a thing that refuses to work; M7 takes a working thing -- a traffic light, a square -- and asks you to look at it as if it were a work, which changes nothing about the thing and everything about the looking.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M7 should frame something ordinary and add nothing to it. Do not decorate the traffic light, do not animate it into a character: the frame is the work, and the viewer supplies the rest.

## M8 -- The archive as biography: Biopsia, the life record as measurement

The artist files his own year into a box and treats staying alive as a record to be beaten. W20 Biopsia, numbers 1 to 6 (1993-1997): paper envelopes with rubber stamps, handmade postage stamps and hand lettering, 13 x 20.1 cm, held by the Blanton Museum; the word biopsy is a label Vigo used as synonymous with biography, and since the 1960s he prepared annual Biopsia boxes archiving information about his own work (C10176, C10177). The boxes themselves, 1953-1997, hold detailed information on his works, exhibitions and actions -- 37 boxes in one CAEV-based account, 34 in another, conflict recorded (C10139). W19 Multiples acumulados (1990-1991) is the same logic as a box: a career filed as cards (C10174). The mechanism reaches its sharpest form in MoMA's Life Records entry Second Attempt to Beat His Own Life Record (1983), "an absurd commentary on the burden of staying alive and a conceptual exercise in measurement" (C10185). The viewer opens a box and reads a life in fragments, dates and cancellations, rather than in a single image. After 1976 the archive is also the memorial: the Biopsia boxes and the Set Palomo Free stamps are the record of a son who has no grave, filed year by year in envelopes that were never meant to be delivered anywhere but the future.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M8 should be a box that opens onto a life in fragments -- dates, stamps, cancellations -- and lets the viewer read the fragments in any order. Do not tell the life as a story; the filing IS the story.

## The aesthetic logic: the object thins out until only the instruction, the journey and the filing remain

Vigo's aesthetic is the logic of the envelope. Everything he makes starts as a thing and ends as a circuit: the magazine becomes an envelope of loose sheets, the poem becomes something to unfold or to seal inside a can, the machine becomes a refusal, the exhibition becomes a postal distance, the life becomes a box of filed envelopes. The object thins out; the instruction, the journey and the filing remain (C10186, C10187). The recipient is never a spectator in the old sense: the recipient is the completer, the custodian, the circulator, the co-author. The work is not finished when Vigo finishes it; it is finished when a stranger opens it, turns it, chalks a square, or mails it on.

Against Uriburu, his predecessor in this chain, the contrast is exact and it is the study's hinge. Uriburu poured a colour into a system that already existed -- a river, a city, a continent -- and let it show itself; he built no system at all. Vigo built the system: the postal circuit, the assembling magazine, the museum without a building, the archive without a shelf. Uriburu's green vanishes with the tide; Vigo's envelope arrives with the postmark on it, and the postmark is the proof it travelled. Both left the canvas and both were architects of a sort -- Uriburu trained as one, Vigo sewed files in a hexagonal office -- but Uriburu took the territory as the canvas and Vigo took the network as the canvas. And where Uriburu's gesture stayed the same while the addressee moved from art world to public to state, Vigo's gesture stayed the same while the support thinned: from the built machine to the printed instruction to the mailed envelope to the filed box. The through line of his own career is the transfer of the work from the maker to the stranger: the 1954 show was destroyed by its public, and the forty years after it are the long answer -- make work the public cannot destroy because the public has to complete it. A study that misses the recipient misses Vigo; a piece prompt that shows only the envelope and not the journey, the instruction and not the completion, the machine and not the refusal, is not a Vigo prompt. The prompt must contain the circuit, the stranger, and the thing that only works when it is used wrong.
