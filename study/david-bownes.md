## M1. The Pardon as a Trap
A pardon that depends on the rebels staying quiet is a weapon with a timer. The Crown issues a general pardon in December 1536, over the Great Seal, applying to everyone without exception -- the only occasion in Henry VIII's reign when traitors were so indulged, in Collinson's words -- to stand the rebel army down; the pardon is honoured just long enough for the army to disperse, while the government keeps its promises unfulfilled; any fresh rising then counts as a breach of the pardon, which retroactively voids the protection and licenses exemplary punishment of the original leaders too. Bush's preface to The Pilgrims' Complaint states the mechanism plainly: the January-February 1537 revolts "allowed the government to escape the fulfilment of its December pledges". Norfolk's reported promise to observe "no part thereof" of the pardon is the mechanism's tell.
Takeaway: the settlement was the second act of the defeat.

## M2. The Postpardon Revolt as the Government's Escape Hatch
A small, badly organised second rising hands the Crown a pretext it did not have while the pardon stood. Francis Bigod launches his revolt on 15 or 16 January 1537, aiming to take Hull and Scarborough Castle; his force is captured at Beverley on 19 January, he escapes, is captured on 10 February 1537 and imprisoned at Carlisle Castle. Collinson's verdict: Bigod's "mad, inept, ill-prepared rising" gave the King the excuse to take revenge. The mechanism is the book's central argument: the defeat of the Pilgrimage was produced not on the battlefield at Doncaster but by the second rising, which converts a negotiated settlement into a treason prosecution.
Takeaway: the defeat was produced by the second rising, not the first.

## M3. The Captain Poverty Pseudonymous-Letter Network
A shared alias signs letters that carry articles of complaint, and the letters are copied and carried from place to place, so a single text appears in many counties at once. The alias -- "Captain Poverty" -- hides the author and lets the letter speak as the commons themselves. The first letter comes from the Richmondshire rebels on 15 October 1536 and is disseminated 16-21 October across Cumberland, Westmorland, north Lancashire, North Riding, Durham and Northumberland. No copy survives; the content is reconstructed from the Townley and Thompson examinations. The letters carry faith and commonwealth, tenant right, and the restoration of holy days "in the old manner" on pain of death. Bush's 1992 article "Captain Poverty and the Pilgrimage of Grace" and his 2009 chapter 1 both work from this network, and the 1999 book's chapter 6 is cited for the Captain Poverty movements.
Takeaway: anonymity is a distribution system.

## M4. Rumour Bills as Mobilisation
A short written bill circulates a frightening rumour -- taxes on burying, christening and marrying, the marking of beasts -- that makes an abstract policy feel like an immediate threat to every household; the rumour, not the policy, does the recruiting. Esch's bill is the named example; the Lancaster Herald told the government the peasants rose because of these feared taxes. The 1999 book studies one such bill for its content, provenance and circulation (pp. 151-154), and notes that both surviving copies were misdated in Letters and Papers (p. 149, n. 16). The mechanism is the rumour as a political fact: it does the work of the real tax before any tax is levied.
Takeaway: the rumoured tax does the work of the real one.

## M5. The Oath as a Binding Technology
Commons "swear" their betters into the cause, and the oath then binds both sides: the sworn gentry cannot withdraw without perjury, and the commons can hold them to it. Aske's oath and the Captain Poverty oath are the examples. The oath converts a crowd into a sworn body with a corporate identity, and it is the institution the rising builds for itself. Hoyle leaves the mechanism unexplained -- "we need an anthropologist or two and a time machine" -- and the gap itself marks its importance.
Takeaway: the binding is the organisation.

## M6. The Five Wounds Banner as Logo
Aske invents a single visual mark, the Five Wounds of Christ, carried on banners and badges; the mark makes a dispersed rising instantly recognisable as one movement, signals Catholic piety without words, and lets strangers identify allies at a muster. Elton and Collinson questioned whether Darcy's badges were what they seemed, so the logo's meaning was itself contested -- the emblem works as a membership card even when its bearer is lying about it.
Takeaway: the badge is the membership card.

## M7. Monastery Restoration as Physical Act of Complaint
Instead of petitioning for the monasteries to be restored, rebels physically restore them. After the December agreement, the Newcastle Friary and Warter are restored (Bush and Bownes, p. 330), along with Watton (pp. 220-221). The act is both the demand and its own fulfilment, and it forces the government to undo something concrete rather than answer a document. A petition can be ignored; a restored friary has to be suppressed again, in public, by soldiers.
Takeaway: do the demand instead of asking for it.

## M8. Prophetic Rhyme Reinterpretation
Old prophecies -- "Christ's Cross Row", the "Prophecies of Rhymer, Bede and Merlin", the "Marvels of Merlin" -- are re-read so that current events fulfil them; the rhyme gives the rising a script written before it happened, which authorises action and predicts success. Bush and Bownes are cited for the wide circulation of "Christ's Cross Row" (p. 158), which "explicitly blamed Cromwell for all things amiss". The mechanism is the old text as a licence: the rebels are not starting something new, they are completing something foretold.
Takeaway: the old rhyme is a licence for the new rising.

## M9. Pickering's Song Typology
A song maps the present onto scripture: Cromwell as Haman, the northerners as the Jews, the Maccabees and Esther story as the template, so that killing Cromwell becomes a biblical duty rather than treason. Pickering's Song, 21 stanzas in the appendix to The Pilgrims' Complaint, calls northerners the "faithfull people of the boreal region", brands southerners "southern Turks", and turns Cromwell into Haman from Esther -- "The art of a shearman it was his beginning". Henry is pointedly NOT cast as Antiochus, which keeps the song loyalist in form while murderous in content. The mechanism is the memorable form: the song travels where the petition cannot go, because it does not need to be read.
Takeaway: the movement sang its manifesto.

## M10. The Two-Stage Petition
The rebels negotiate by document in two rounds: the first petition on 27 October, then the Pontefract Articles of 4-6 December (24 articles), each round raising the stakes and widening the claims. The written articles force the Crown to answer in writing, which creates a record the rebels can later hold the Crown to -- and which the Crown later has to escape. The petition is not a letter, it is a fuse: the same words that ask the king for remedy also summon the next village, and Bush's chapter 1 dates the spread day by day, so the contagion has a timetable, not just a mood.
Takeaway: the petition is not a letter, it is a fuse.

## M11. Martial Law as Exemplary Terror
After the postpardon risings, punishment is staged for maximum visibility: 74 rebels hanged on the Carlisle walls, bodies left hanging, quartering, so that the execution of a few terrifies the many who are pardoned. Henry's January 1537 orders to Norfolk: "cause such dreadful executions... hanging them on trees, quartering them". The terror is the enforcement mechanism of the pardon: the pardoned stay quiet because they have seen what happens to the unpardoned. The numbers themselves are disputed between sources -- 216 executed in the most repeated figure, 178 in another, "144 rebels at least" in a third -- but the staging, not the count, is the mechanism.
Takeaway: the terror enforces the pardon.

## M12. Divide and Rule
After the pardon, Henry and Cromwell split the rebel coalition along its fault line, gentry against commons, by restoring some gentry and executing others on framed-up charges of fresh rebellion, so the commons lose their leaders and the gentry lose their followers. Tudor Times: Henry "embarked on a policy of 'divide and rule'". Ridley's account, via Spartacus: Henry divided the Yorkshire gentry into the forgiven and restored and the executed. The mechanism is the coalition's own seam turned into a weapon: the pardon does not end the rising, it gives the Crown the tool to take it apart person by person.
Takeaway: the pardon takes the coalition apart.

## M13. The "Act of State" Argument
Reframe the religious revolution as the work of "a small number of Protestants" who controlled the government, so that the Pilgrimage appears as a popular response to an imposed, unrepresentative change rather than as resistance to a legitimate national settlement. Quoted second-hand via Loughlin (2016) from Bush and Bownes, p. 406: "Essentially, it was an act of state resulting from the control a small number of Protestants exercised over the government". Loughlin introduces it as Bush and Bownes summarising that the religious revolution "was not a response to the wishes of the people or most of the clergy". The mechanism is the reframing: change who the actor is, and the same events change sides.
Takeaway: change the actor and the events change sides.

## M14. Provenance Discipline
Sort every rebel document by who actually produced it -- commons, gentlemen or clerics -- before using it as evidence of what "the rebels" wanted; read each document by what it meant to its participants at the time, not by a later theological standard. This is how Bush separates commonwealth grievances, treated in their own right, from religious grievances, not treated as a mask for the other. The rebels' documentation falls into four types: circulars to drum up support, oaths and orders, petitions, and personal accounts. The mechanism is the filing system: once the papers are sorted by what they were for, the rebels stop looking like a mob with papers and start looking like a movement with a bureaucracy.
Takeaway: who wrote it decides what it means.

## M15. The County-by-County Narrative
Build the history county by county and let the argument wait. Clark's review of the 1999 book records its shape: "Founded on an intensive trawl of the state papers, it forms a coda to Bush's The pilgrimage of grace... Of the text 70 per cent is a county-by-county narrative of events in the north in the first six weeks of 1537; in the clear argument of chapter vii the book really gets under way. Indeed, chapters vii-ix might have stood alone as an adequate rehearsal and analysis of the aftershocks of revolt." The mechanism is the narrative as evidence: the argument earns its authority by first walking every county, so that when chapters vii-ix argue, they argue from ground already walked.
Takeaway: walk every county before you argue.

## M16. The Coda as Second Act
Write the book as a coda, not a standalone history: it covers only the aftermath. Bush's own preface to The Pilgrims' Complaint gives the trilogy its shape: the 1996 book "studied the formation of a rebel army and its impact upon the government", the 1999 book "examined the disturbances that breached the December pardon and how they affected the original rebellion", and the 2009 book treated rebel grievances more systematically. The mechanism is the second act as the real story: the rising that looked like a success in December is shown, in the coda, to have been defeated by its own aftermath.
Takeaway: the aftermath is the story.

## M17. The Quantitative Finding
Count the bodies by category and the official story has to answer to the number. Sixteen per cent of those executed after the postpardon revolts were members of the clergy, cited by David Callaghan's Warwick MA dissertation (September 2009) from Bush and Bownes, pp. 411-412, second-hand. The figure is why Callaghan treats the book as a key source for clerical involvement: it turns a political purge into a measurable clerical price. The mechanism is arithmetic as argument, the same move Bush makes with the Peterloo casualty lists in The Casualties of Peterloo (2005).
Takeaway: the percentage is the argument.

## M18. The Radical Reinterpretation, Questioned
Be recognised as a radical reinterpretation and have your persuasiveness openly questioned in the same breath. The English Reformation Bibliography annotates the 1999 book, second-hand: "radically reinterprets the traditional one. But convincing?" The sceptical gloss is part of the reception, not a footnote to it: the book is known as the one that re-read the defeat as a second act, and the field has not settled whether the re-reading holds. The mechanism is the open question as reputation: a book can be central and contested at once, and the contest is what keeps it cited.
Takeaway: central and contested at once.

## M19. Anticipation as Vindication
Be vindicated by the book that comes after yours. Collinson, reviewing Hoyle in the London Review of Books on Thursday 1 November 2001: "As for Hoyle's populist and 'religious' account of the Pilgrimage, it has been anticipated, in many of its elements, by Michael Bush's The Pilgrimage of Grace (1996) and The Defeat of the Pilgrimage of Grace (1999), which he wrote with David Bownes." The qualifier "in many of its elements" is kept. The mechanism is anticipation as the verdict: the later, dominant account is measured against the earlier one, and the earlier one is credited with having got there first.
Takeaway: the later book is measured against yours.

## M20. The Thesis in the Reviewer's Words
Let the reviewer state your thesis better than you did. Clark, Journal of Ecclesiastical History 51, no. 2 (2000), p. 413, first page read as an image: "This exhaustive survey of the deliberately lower-case pilgrimage of grace takes as its thesis the failure of the postpardon revolts to secure the pilgrims' aims, turning victory into defeat." "Deliberately lower-case" is Clark's observation about the book's title styling. The mechanism is the review as the record: where the book itself is unreadable -- no preview, no full text, three of four reviews paywalled -- the reviewer's first page becomes the public statement of what the book argues.
Takeaway: the review is the record when the book is closed.

## M21. The Poster as Primary Evidence
Read the poster as evidence of state anxiety and social change, not as decoration. Bownes's readings in Posters of the First World War (2014, with Rob Fleming), as reported by the National Army Museum on Monday 14 July 2014: as interest in recruiting diminished, the government grew increasingly anxious and resorted to a combination of patriotism and guilt to encourage enlistment -- on "Are You In This?", designed by Sir Robert Baden-Powell. And the recruitment advertisements were not aimed at men alone: war posters encouraged women to join the war effort, and women's involvement gave higher status to female workers and helped change attitudes to "women's work" after the war -- on "Every Fit Woman Can Release a Fit Man". The mechanism is the poster as a dated instrument: the design changes when the state's need changes, so the poster is a record of the anxiety, not just of the art.
Takeaway: the poster records the anxiety.

## M22. The Real Thing as Time Travel
Seeing the real thing is time travel. Bownes, in the CATCH-cast 36 interview published Friday 19 December 2025, on why museums matter: "Well, where else can you see these real things from the past and be connected directly with previous generations and see the real thing? I mean, it is such a remarkable experience to have. It's like time travel, really, isn't it?" The mechanism is the object as the connection: the poster, the badge, the bill are not illustrations of the past, they are the past still present, and the curator's job is to keep that connection open. It is the same instinct that makes the 1999 book rest on seditious bills and rhymes rather than on chronicles: the surviving thing is the evidence.
Takeaway: the surviving thing is the evidence.

## M23. One Location as the Type of a Theme
Take one Underground location as the type of a theme, tell its tale, and add details and material from other similar locations. That is the method of Hidden London: Discovering the Forgotten Underground (Yale University Press, 2019), as a reviewer summarises it, second-hand: "The method is to take an Underground location as the type of a theme, tell its tale and add details and material from other similar locations. It is definitely not a narrative history of the London Underground but rather a series of localised narratives that give us a rounded picture of the whole." Ten primary locations, from King William Street (opened 1890, closed 1900) to Down Street, Clapham South, North End, Highgate, the Strand and Euston. The mechanism is the type: one place, fully told, stands for a whole class of places, and the whole is built from localised narratives rather than from a single continuous story -- the same move as the county-by-county narrative of M15, applied to a city instead of a rebellion.
Takeaway: one place, fully told, stands for the whole.

## M24. The Sunday Demonstration
Use the Sunday gathering as the unit of evidence. Bush's footnotes in The Pilgrims' Complaint cite Bush and Bownes for Sunday demonstrations at Kendal (pp. 242-244), Craven (pp. 149-150), the Barony of Westmorland (pp. 264-265) and Richmondshire (pp. 111-112). The mechanism is the calendar as the archive: the commons met on Sunday because Sunday was when they met, so the demonstration is dated by the week, and the rising's spread can be tracked Sunday by Sunday across the counties. The evidence is not a single event but a rhythm.
Takeaway: the rising kept Sunday time.

## M25. The Seditious Bill's Provenance
Study a single seditious bill for its content, its provenance and its circulation, and the bill becomes a map. Bush and Bownes, pp. 151-154: one bill, both surviving copies misdated in Letters and Papers (p. 149, n. 16), traced through who wrote it, who carried it and where it was read. The mechanism is provenance as the argument: a document's journey -- where it was nailed, who copied it, which county it reached -- is itself evidence of the movement's reach, and correcting the archive's date corrects the movement's timetable.
Takeaway: the bill's journey is the movement's map.

## M26. The Tithe-Barn Attack as Local Anger
Attack the tithe barn and the grievance is visible in one building. Bush and Bownes are cited for attacks on tithe barns at Kendal (pp. 246-247), Lancashire (pp. 234-235) and Cumberland (pp. 274-275), and for the attack on Pulleyn (pp. 259-260). The mechanism is the local anger inside the national cause: the commons rose for faith and commonwealth, but in each valley the commonwealth meant the rent, the fine, the tithe and the barn, and the rising's strength was that it never asked a valley to forget its own grievance. Takeaway: one rebellion, many tithe barns.

## M27. The Two David Bownes
Do not merge two men because they share a name. The sources hold two David Bownes: the poster historian and curator, and the Chesterfield Canal boatman who started as a boatman in 1949, led the volunteer build of the Cuckoo boat Dawn Rose (launched April 2015), and wrote Ponds, Tonzs and Boorts (Friday 16 February 2018). A working boatman from 1949 cannot be the man who took a history degree at Sussex (founded 1961) and got his first curatorial job in the mid-1990s. The mechanism is namesake discipline: the name alone proves nothing, and a study that merges the two men imports a canal, a boat and a memoir into a curator's life. The same discipline applies to the identity question in C1: the poster career and the co-authorship are probably one man, but probability is not proof, and the study says so.
Takeaway: the name alone proves nothing.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife so far

Phase one, the Tudor book (1990s). Bownes does a postgraduate qualification in history at Manchester while Bush is Reader there (1988-1994), and the two produce The Defeat of the Pilgrimage of Grace (University of Hull Press, 1999), a coda to Bush's 1996 book that argues the defeat was produced by the postpardon revolts of January-February 1537. It is the only Tudor-history publication on Bownes's record, and no source separates his chapters from Bush's. The phase ends with the book: four journal reviews in 2000, and then silence on the Tudor front.

Phase two, the museum career (mid-1990s to 2012). First curatorial job at the Imperial War Museum in the mid-1990s, cataloguing cat badges in a windowless room; then curator at the Royal Welsh Fusiliers Museum, Caernarvon; back to London in 2002 as curator at London Transport Museum, in charge of the photographic archive, the poster collection and ephemera; Head Curator 2009-2012; National Army Museum Assistant Director (Collections) from Monday 19 March 2012. The phase is a steady climb through the museum world, from the lowest curator grade to the directorship of collections.

Phase three, the poster historian (2008 to now). London Transport Posters (2008, edited with Oliver Green); Underground: How the Tube Shaped London (2012); Posters of the First World War (July 2014, with Rob Fleming); Poster Girls (exhibition October 2017-January 2019, catalogue 2017/2018); Hidden London (Tuesday 15 October 2019); Twentieth Century Posters launched January 2016; television appearances in 2020 and 2021; talks in December 2025 and January 2026; the "Poster Power!" exhibition at the Victoria Art Gallery, Bath, Friday 23 January to Sunday 10 May 2026. The Tudor historian has become a historian of the printed image, and the method is the same: read the surviving thing as the evidence.

Afterlife so far. The 1999 book is heavily cited: Bush cites it 41 times in The Pilgrims' Complaint (2009), and Callaghan's 2009 Warwick dissertation cites it for the clergy execution share. Collinson's 2001 London Review of Books essay credits Bush and Bownes with anticipating Hoyle. The book remains the standard account of the postpardon revolts, and its persuasiveness is still openly questioned. The identity question -- whether the poster historian is the co-author -- is still open, and will stay open until a source names Bownes's Manchester supervisor or thesis, or the book's preface surfaces.

## Aesthetic logic

The book and the poster career share one aesthetic: history written from the surviving thing. The 1999 book is built from seditious bills, rhymes, Sunday demonstrations and tithe-barn attacks, county by county; the poster books are built from the posters themselves, read as records of state anxiety. In both, the object is not an illustration of the argument, it is the argument: the bill's journey is the movement's map, the poster's design change is the government's anxiety. The logic is material before it is narrative. A chronicle tells you what happened; a bill tells you what the commons were carrying in their pockets, and a poster tells you what the state was afraid of. Bownes's credo -- seeing the real thing is time travel -- is the aesthetic stated as a sentence: the past is present in the thing that survived it, and the historian's job is to keep that connection open rather than to summarise it away. The 1999 book's structure follows the same logic: 70 per cent county-by-county narrative before the argument in chapters vii-ix, because the argument has to be earned by walking the ground first. The study's own form follows it too: where the book is closed, the study says so, because a gap honestly stated is worth more than a gap smoothly filled.
