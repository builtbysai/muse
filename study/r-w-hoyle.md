## M1. The Commons-First Reversal
Start from the commons' agency and treat elite involvement as secondary, so the rising is explained from below and the lords arrive late as a problem, not as the cause. The Pilgrimage of Grace and the Politics of the 1530s is built this way: the rising was "best understood as an authentic popular movement, which occurred without the encouragement of the upper classes" (Palmer, reporting Hoyle), and "the engine behind the uprising was the commons, and notably the artisans, of some of the smaller northern towns" (OUP). Chapter 15 is titled "The Rebellions as Commons' Revolts". The gentry are not the authors of the rising but its late, reluctant, and then destructive passengers.
Takeaway: make the commons the engine and the gentry become the plot twist.

## M2. Regional Differentiation
Split a supposedly single event into regionally distinct movements with different drivers, so one label stops doing the work of five explanations. Hoyle's book treats the Pilgrimage as "a series of revolts that differed regionally, socially, and economically" (OUP): Lincolnshire (religious, fear over church goods), the East Riding (Aske's leadership), Richmondshire and Westmorland (rents and entry fines, the "Captain Poverty" local bushfires). Chapters 4-8 treat Lincolnshire, the East Riding and the "Captain Poverty" revolts separately, and the book's argument is that the regional differences are the explanation, not a complication to be smoothed away.
Takeaway: cut the event along its regional seams and each piece explains itself.

## M3. Rumour as Trigger
Treat rumour as a causal mechanism, classified by type and ranked by force, so gossip becomes a dated, datable cause rather than atmosphere. Hoyle's account works with three rumour types: new taxes (least important), confiscation of church goods (most widespread), tearing down of parish churches (most traumatic). The flashpoint was Louth, whose people had recently renovated their parish church at their own expense, so the rumour of confiscation struck a community that had just paid for what it feared losing. The rumours travelled because the commons were, in Collinson's report of Hoyle, "avid devourers of news and gossip".
Takeaway: sort the rumours, rank them, and the spark has a fuse.

## M4. The Coerced Captain
Show leadership imposed from below under threat, then used to capture and contain the movement, so the leader is both the rising's figurehead and its saboteur. The gentry were "threatened to cut off heads and to despoil gentry property" and their "bluff was not called"; once in leadership they "worked to dismantle the movement and help the crown neutralize it by guile" (OUP). Kesselring's summary is "capture, contain, and subvert". The mechanism is the double role: the coerced captain gives the rising discipline and legitimacy, and then spends both on ending it.
Takeaway: a captain drafted at sword-point will steer the ship home, not to battle.

## M5. Rehabilitation by Muster Book
Overturn a conspiracy reading with a single administrative document, so the archive does the acquitting. Elton's chief Pilgrimage conspirator was Thomas, Lord Darcy; Hoyle argues the muster book attributed to Darcy was misread -- it shows Darcy "desperately trying to locate loyal troops after the revolt began", not cataloguing resources beforehand (Kesselring). Chapter 9 of the book is titled "Misunderstanding Darcy". Darcy had only 260 fighting men against thousands at York (Collinson), and his surrender of Pontefract turned on the lack of government "furniture" (supplies). Collinson's summary: "Hoyle believes that Darcy has been misunderstood", and "there is little or no hard evidence of high-level conspiracy in 1536".
Takeaway: one muster book, read the right way round, acquits the conspirator.

## M6. The Logo Invention
A movement is made by naming and branding it, so the invention of the label is the founding act. Robert Aske invented the "Pilgrimage of Grace" name and its five-wounds badge, declared himself "chief captain", and defined the rising as defence of the Church and the removal of Cromwell and Cranmer. Hoyle attributes to Aske charisma and "boundless personal ambition" (via Collinson). The name did the political work: it turned a set of regional risings into a single pilgrimage, gave the commons a banner to march under, and gave the king an enemy with a face.
Takeaway: whoever names the rising owns it.

## M7. Oath as Social Glue
Use swearing as the mechanism that binds commons and gentry into one body, so the oath is the institution the rising builds for itself. Oath-taking runs through Hoyle's Pilgrimage: the commons "swearing" their social betters into the movement, the oath as the act that turns a crowd into a company. Collinson notes that Hoyle leaves the mechanism unexplained -- "we need an anthropologist or two and a time machine" -- and the gap itself marks the mechanism's importance: the oath is the one piece of the rising's machinery Hoyle records without being able to take apart.
Takeaway: the oath is the rising's constitution, even when its workings stay dark.

## M8. Event-Driven History
Make coincidence the motor: three unrelated administrative processes colliding in one place at one time, so the rising needs no master plan. Three sets of commissioners were coincidentally present in north-east Lincolnshire in early October 1536 -- dissolution commissioners, ecclesiastical visitors, tax collectors -- and their coincidental presence turned a local spark at Louth into a regional rising. Collinson's gloss on Hoyle is that "events are the motors of history, not policy decisions", set against Braudel's "slowly shifting tectonic plates". The mechanism is the collision, not the conspiracy: no one planned the three commissions to arrive together, and that is exactly why they ignited.
Takeaway: let three clerks arrive on the same road and the county rises.

## M9. Going Behind the Calendar
Return from printed summaries to the manuscript examinations and testimonies, so the revision is earned at the document level. Collinson's praise of Hoyle is a "meticulous evaluation of the examinations and testimonies... going behind the printed Calendar to... the State Papers themselves", joined to "an unrivalled knowledge of the grass-roots politics of Northern England". The mechanism is the refusal of the digest: the printed Calendar smooths the witnesses into a story, and the State Papers restore the hesitations, contradictions and local knowledge the Calendar edited out. It is the archival version of M1: the commons speak in the examinations, and the Calendar speaks for the crown.
Takeaway: read past the Calendar and the witnesses start talking back.

## M10. Custom as Property Right
Treat custom not as habit but as a right tenants created to buy, sell, mortgage, lease and inherit, so the tenant becomes a proprietor in practice before the law admits it. The argument runs from the 1987 tenant-right article through "The Land-Family Bond" (1995) to the forthcoming Tenure in Tawney's Century: copyhold was protected by the equity courts, especially Chancery, until the late sixteenth century, on a source base of about 400 Chancery decrees; tenant right in the north is the regional anchor. Custom, Improvement and the Landscape (2011) states it as a general claim: custom was "a form of property right created by tenants where no formal rights met their need to buy, sell, mortgage, lease and inherit". The common-law courts offered protection to copyholders only quite late in the sixteenth century; until then tenants relied on equity.
Takeaway: custom is property the tenants made for themselves.

## M11. The Forged Custumal
Use forgery as a weapon in the struggle to control memory, so a fake document is evidence of the fight, not just a fraud to be exposed. "Cromwell v Taverner" (Custom, Improvement, 2011) is the case: James Taverner of Wighton, Norfolk, forged a manorial custumal of North Elmham, was sued and punished in Star Chamber, and the case shows "how custom was contested, and memory annexed by landowners" (TNA STAC 7/30/38, 17 November 1583-16 November 1585). The forged custumal matters because it is a landowner's attempt to rewrite what custom had been; the forgery is the landlord's version of M10, custom as property right, turned against the tenants who made it. Note the names: the Cromwell here is Henry Cromwell, Lord Cromwell, not Thomas Cromwell; see the network note at N27.
Takeaway: a forged custumal is a landlord's memory, written as law.

## M12. The Land-Market Reconstruction
Rebuild a whole market from manorial records by computer, then read structure against agency, so counting becomes the argument. The Earls Colne work with H. R. French (Economic History Review, 2003; book, 2007) is a computer-based reconstruction of the copyhold land market 1546-1750: familial possession of land over long periods was normal alongside an active market in smaller parcels, and there was little consolidation. The Slaidburn figures behind "The Land-Family Bond" are the same method at village scale: 28 of 49 tenements never appeared on the market at all between 1520 and 1592, and the remaining 21 were sold 53 times. The mechanism is the tight definition: separate ownership from occupation, define "family" narrowly, distrust figures built on small parcels, and the market's shape emerges from the counting rather than from the thesis.
Takeaway: reconstruct the market first and the thesis has to answer to the numbers.

## M13. Tithe-as-Harvest-Proxy
Use an ecclesiastical revenue series as a quantitative proxy for harvest failure, so a church receipt becomes a weather station. "Famine as agricultural catastrophe: the crisis of 1622-4 in east Lancashire" (Economic History Review, 2010) argues against a pure entitlements explanation of famine: oat tithe data from the Dunkenhalgh estate, 20 miles east of Preston, show the 1621 harvest short by about a third and 1622 seriously deficient; the crisis hit arable and pastoral alike; weather was an exogenous factor; recovery took several years. The tithe is the instrument: a continuous, dated, local series that records the harvest without anyone intending to record the harvest.
Takeaway: where the harvest was never counted, count the tithe instead.

## M14. The Farmer's Account as National Sample
Treat scattered private accounts, singly and in gross, as a national dataset, so the farmer's day book becomes economic history. The Farmer in England, 1650-1980 (2013) is built on the claim that farmers were normally literate and kept records -- day books, journals, accounts -- "so a history of the farmer can be written from farm records, accounts used singly and in gross as a national sample". The volume's thesis is that "farmers made agriculture happen. They brought together the capital and the technical and management skills which allowed food to be produced... They are the rural equivalent of the small businessman with his own firm." The mechanism is aggregation without apology: one account is anecdote, a hundred accounts are a sample, and the sample is national because the record-keeping habit was.
Takeaway: a hundred day books are a census the state never took.

## M15. The Democratic-Deficit Audit
Show that a decision was taken before consultation began, so that consultation could only adjust boundaries, never desirability. "Opposition to the creation of national parks: the case of the Yorkshire Dales" (Agricultural History Review, 2019) is the audit: the decision that there would be a Yorkshire Dales National Park "had been taken some years before anyone locally was asked their opinion", and "opposition might well be seen to be hopeless". The paper, read end to end this leg at 2,295 lines, shows little public opposition or support -- the opposition was mainly a North Riding County Council turf war -- and the park was hobbled from its origins by a weak Joint Advisory Committee (1956) instead of an executive Joint Board. The audit's finding is procedural: the consultation was real, and it was too late.
Takeaway: date the decision before the consultation and the consultation explains itself.

## M16. The Name-Misunderstanding Trap
A name creates expectations the institution cannot meet, and the mismatch itself drives behaviour. The Dales paper's second mechanism: "national park" suggested nationalisation of the land and open public access; the 1949 Act provided neither. The News Chronicle's "This Land Is Yours" map of 8 December 1953 fed the expectation, and hikers told a landowner "just you wait till we've got our national park here". The trap is the gap between the label and the statute: people acted on the name, the institution acted on the Act, and the friction between the two is the history.
Takeaway: name a thing grandly and the public will hold you to the name, not the Act.

## M17. The Two-Speed Institution
An institution deliberately hobbled at birth by giving it a weaker form than parliament intended, so its weakness is designed, not accidental. The Dales paper's third mechanism: the Joint Advisory Committee (1956) instead of an executive Joint Board; the park "run as two halves" by the parent county councils until 1974; the West Riding's conviction that it could not cooperate with the North Riding in a Joint Board, left mostly unspoken. The designation order was signed Monday 7 December 1953 and confirmed by the Minister on Friday 8 October 1954; the institution that resulted was the weaker form, and its weakness shaped the park for twenty years. The mechanism generalises: look for the form the institution did not take, and the missing form is the policy.
Takeaway: the board that was never created is the decision that matters.

## M18. The Pressure-Group Origin Story
Explain an institution by the lobby that placed its friends in power at the right moment, so the origin is a campaign, not a consensus. The Dales paper's afterword states it directly: "That we have national parks at all is the result of a very successful piece of pressure group politics"; the Ramblers Association's contacts in the wartime coalition put its friends in the right places, and "in a real sense national parks had few friends in their first years, and many enemies: but they were fortunate that their friends had been well placed". The mechanism reverses the usual story: the park was not the expression of a national will but the achievement of a small, well-placed group working a wartime coalition.
Takeaway: find the friends in the right places and the institution explains itself.

## M19. Instance and Big Data Together
Move between the single case and the statistical analysis of big datasets without seeing a contradiction, so the anecdote and the aggregate corroborate each other. Hoyle's own words, from his projects page: "I have never seen any contradiction in moving between instance/counter-instance forms of writing and analyses based on the statistical analysis of big datasets". The career is the demonstration: the single forged custumal of North Elmham beside the 400 Chancery decrees; the single village of Earls Colne beside the computer-reconstructed land market; the single famine of 1622-4 beside the tithe series. The instance tests the aggregate and the aggregate frames the instance, and neither is asked to do the other's job.
Takeaway: let the village and the dataset check each other.

## M20. The Honest Men / Lighter Sort Split
Divide the commons internally between the volatile and the responsible, rather than treating "the people" as one bloc. The Pilgrimage book, via Collinson, splits the commons between the volatile "lighter sort" and the responsible "honest men"; the Darlington crowd of January 1537 demanding "what news?" and correcting an official about the King of Scots being in France is the politically alert commons in action. Towns played a larger part than historians had allowed, and only a small number from each township or parish went forward, a discipline the gentry supplied. The mechanism is the refusal of the singular: "the commons" is a coalition of strata with different tempers, and the rising's behaviour follows from which stratum is in the ascendant on a given day.
Takeaway: split the people and the rising stops behaving like a mob.

## M21. Petitioning as Popular Politics
Treat collective petitioning as a normal, constant form of political communication by the commons, so the petition is a parliament the people built for themselves. "Petitioning as Popular Politics in Early Sixteenth-Century England" (Historical Research, November 2002) argues that collective petitioning -- occupational, regional, "the commons" -- was an important and overlooked form of political communication: "a constant stream of members of the commons petitioning government". The examples include the York weavers and the East Anglia commons of 1549 and 1553. The mechanism connects directly to M1 and M3: a people that petitions constantly is a people that can also rise, and the Pilgrimage's bills and petitions are the same instrument turned from request to demand.
Takeaway: count the petitions and the rising stops looking spontaneous.

## M22. The Manufactured Dissolution
Show a revolution manufactured step by step from above, with no public demand behind it, so the absence of demand is the finding. "The Origins of the Dissolution of the Monasteries" (Historical Journal, 1995) traces the genesis to the suppressions of smaller houses authorised in 1528: a partial dissolution could be construed as a desirable reform without challenging monasticism or the doctrine of purgatory; a previously unnoticed petition casts light on the anti-clerical agitation of 1529; an attempt to secure a dissolution for financial reasons in 1534 failed, forcing the new strategy of collecting damaging evidence of monastic corruption in the 1535 visitations; display of that material to the Commons in 1536 persuaded acceptance of a partial dissolution "in the guise of a reform of monasticism". There was, Hoyle argues, no "public" demand for dissolution in the 1530s except in the artificial circumstances of 1536, and the shape of the dissolution was determined by government's inability to secure parliament's support for a financially justified dissolution. Only 30 percent of small monasteries were actually closed in 1536; the rest continued after a fine for continuance or a successful petition.
Takeaway: a revolution with no demand behind it was built, step by step, in committee.

## M23. The Crown Estate as Enterprise
Treat the Crown lands as a continuing enterprise managed for income, not as a resource plundered by successive monarchs. The Estates of the English Crown, 1558-1640 (ed., 1992) is the demonstration: each essay treats the dialogue between the Exchequer and local administrators and tenants; the estates supplied 19-34 percent of total income 1558-1640, falling to about 14 percent by 1641; selling was not always preferred; the surveyors' work was a serious attempt to gather data to increase income; and "shearing the hog" -- the reform of the estates, c. 1598-1640 -- was a management programme, not a fire sale. Hoyle authored 6 of the 14 chapters, including the introduction, the tenure chapters, the reform chapter, "Disafforestation and drainage: the Crown as entrepreneur?" and the reflections. The mechanism is the accountant's view of monarchy: the Crown as landlord, the estate as a business, the tenant as a counterparty.
Takeaway: read the Crown as a landlord and the sales become a balance sheet.

## M24. The Acquiescent Nobility
Argue that the early Tudor nobility were less rebellious and more willing to accept royal authority than the older historiography assumed, so the absence of a plot is the evidence, not a gap in it. Hoyle's work on the early Tudor nobility, developed with G. W. Bernard, finds a nobility of the 1530s markedly less rebellious than the tradition allowed -- and the finding underpins M5: a nobility that was not plotting rebellion in 1536 is a nobility whose muster book can be read as loyal administration rather than conspiracy. "Faction, Feud and Reconciliation amongst the Northern English Nobility, 1525-1569" (History, 1999) works the same ground: the Crown's shifting of the western march wardenship (Dacres versus Cumberland, then Wharton after 1537) caused noble friction, the earls of Shrewsbury used marriage to integrate Wharton, and only death and illness prevented a coherent Catholic noble block by 1569. The mechanism is the negative finding stated positively: acquiescence is a policy, and it has a paper trail.
Takeaway: prove the lords were not plotting and the conspiracy reading collapses.

## M25. The King's Misreading
Show the king failing to grasp the independent nature of the commons, making tactical mistakes, and then seeking to humiliate the rebels, so royal incomprehension is a cause of what follows. Henry did not understand that the commons were acting for themselves, not as the gentry's instruments; he made tactical mistakes in mustering against them; his demand for unqualified submission was "nonsense" given the military situation (Hoyle via Collinson). His demand that the Lincolnshire rebels hand over their weapons before pardon was a desire to humiliate and emasculate them, depriving rebels of "their civil status as yeomen within a society organized for war" (Hoyle pp. 168-69, via Kesselring). Henry's incomprehension is quoted in the book: a rebellion attempted "for matters of weddings, christenings, churches, eating white bread". The mechanism is the mirror of M1: the commons understood themselves, and the king did not understand the commons.
Takeaway: a king who cannot read his own people will try to disarm them instead.

## M26. The Gentlemen's Agreement
Show the truce as the gentry's instrument for ending the movement they were forced to lead, so the anticlimax is the point. The Doncaster standoff -- nine regional armies, perhaps 50,000 armed men, England north of the Trent in rebel hands, Norfolk's only weapon "smooth talk" -- ended not in battle but in negotiation. "To say that events at Doncaster were anticlimactic is an understatement" (Hoyle via Collinson), and Doncaster was "quite literally a gentlemen's agreement". The Duke of Norfolk exceeded his instructions in negotiating the truce, and Aske was "taken in" as the king's household guest over Christmas 1536. The pardon over the Great Seal was "the only occasion in Henry VIII's reign when traitors were so indulged", and the parliament promised at Doncaster never happened. The mechanism is M4 at the scale of a kingdom: the coerced captains used the truce to dismantle the movement, and the king used the interval to prepare the revenge.
Takeaway: the anticlimax at Doncaster was the gentry's victory, not the rising's.

## M27. Trust the Actor
Take leading actors at their word, and let the deposition speak, so the method is declared in the preface and then tested by the reviewers. Hoyle's preface (p. x): "When Aske says he did not know Darcy, I believe him." Aske's main source is his own deposition of spring 1537. The mechanism is the gamble: trust the actor and the narrative gains a voice it could not otherwise have; the cost is the one Palmer names, that Hoyle's case depends on "a rather uncritical use of sources, particularly taking leading actors at their word", and that Aske's deposition is "riddled with inconsistencies and improbabilities". Kesselring adds the second cost: the commons are depicted as politically conscious yet "void of insight" -- why coerce gentry leaders and then fail to see that all but Aske worked against them? The study records the method and the criticism together, because the mechanism is the wager, not the win.
Takeaway: believe Aske and the rising gets a voice; disbelieve him and it gets a footnote.

## M28. The Famine Escape
Argue England's early escape from famine from the poor law and the price of barley, so the exception is explained by institutions, not by luck. "Why Was There No Crisis in England in the 1690s?" (chapter 3 of The Farmer in England, 2013; authorship attributed to Hoyle on the earlier legs' reading, second-hand here, see C20) argues England made an early escape from famine, "certainly half a century before Scotland, perhaps a whole century before France and most of two centuries before Ireland": demand was maintained by poor-law transfer payments, and those priced off wheat could substitute barley, with barley-eaters protected by stable barley prices or able to turn to oats. The mechanism pairs with M13: the tithe series shows the harvest failing, and the poor law shows the people surviving it. The famine of 1622-4 is the control case -- before the escape, the same region starved.
Takeaway: the poor law, not the harvest, is why England stopped starving first.

## M29. The Editor as Friend
Treat editing as a creative activity and the editor as the writer's friend, so the journal is a workshop, not a gate. Hoyle edited the Agricultural History Review for twenty-one years, first volume 47 (1999) to AgHR 67, II (2019), and his valedictory, read end to end this leg, states the creed: "Editors are a vital part of the publication process, as the writer's friend, as the ultimate quality assurance, as the manager of the process"; "editing is a creative activity despite what authors sometimes feel". The same valedictory records the decline of "cow and plough history" -- the disappearance of Economic History as a discipline, the extinction of extra-mural departments, the decline of specialist agricultural colleges -- and holds the agricultural (economic) versus rural (social) distinction "fundamentally unhelpful": "There are a few cold-war warriors who still fight the war between the two." On open access and Plan S: "I favour open access. It is a great Enlightenment project for the dissemination of knowledge. But eighteenth-century publishers did not print their books and give them away" -- "a populist policy lacking practicality" that "denies that the publication process has any costs attached to it". His departure, he joked, was "equivalent to the flight of the Ravens from the Tower of London or the abandonment of Gibraltar by its Barbary apes"; "Paul Warde has accepted the pilot's seat". And the valedictory's closing hope for the field: "Crop rotations may be the coming thing. Just wait and see!"
Takeaway: edit as a friend and the journal becomes the field's memory.

## M30. The Land-Family Bond Count
Demolish a rival's figures by counting more carefully, so the method is the refutation. "The Land-Family Bond in England" (Past and Present 146, February 1995), read end to end this leg at 1,922 lines, challenges Macfarlane and Sreenivasan on Earls Colne: the right of alienation is compatible with a close family-land bond; kin pre-emption customs operated; Sreenivasan's figures overstate continuity; sales reflect market forces, not the absence of attachment. The empirical anchor is the Slaidburn evidence from Hoyle's own DPhil thesis (ch. 4, pp. 92-187): 34 tenements traced 1520-1592 were "sold" 45 times in 72 years; 15 ex-chantry tenements 1552-1592 sold 18 times; 28 of the 49 tenements never appeared on the market at all, and the remaining 21 were sold 53 times -- Slaidburn had "two quite different tenurial histories". The polemic is exact: postulating a hereditary right to explain the cases is "like claiming that one's missing biros have been misplaced through the intervention of poltergeists"; Sreenivasan's claim of a kin right to land that "cannot be reconciled with Macfarlane's thesis of an individualistic conception of property" "has to be dismissed". The reply by Govind Sreenivasan follows in the same issue (Past and Present 146, 174-87). The mechanism is M12 at its sharpest: the count is the argument, and the rival's count is the target.
Takeaway: count the tenements twice and the rival's thesis falls apart.

## Aesthetic logic
One move holds the work together at every scale: take the people nearest the ground -- the commons of the smaller northern towns, the copyholder of Slaidburn, the farmer keeping his day book, the parishioners of Louth who had just renovated their church -- and read the age from what they did, paid for and believed, rather than from the minister who governed them or the theory that explains them. The mechanism is archival before it is narrative: the State Papers behind the Calendar, the muster book read the right way round, the 400 Chancery decrees, the tithe series, the reconstructed land market. The tone is the patient corrector's -- the count, the qualification, the "little or no hard evidence" -- but the correction serves a historian's purpose, which is to restore agency to the people the court-centred story had made into an audience: the commons who rose, the tenants who made custom into property, the farmers who kept the accounts. Methodologically he is the mirror of Elton in the other direction from Scarisbrick: Elton's archival discipline kept, Elton's agent reversed, the minister replaced by the commons, the conspiracy replaced by the coincidence of three commissions on one road, the court replaced by the parish and the manor. And he is the complement of Scarisbrick: where Scarisbrick read the Pilgrimage as the king's conscience against the minister, Hoyle reads it as the commons' rising against them both -- the same popular reading, demonstrated from the examinations rather than asserted from the biography. The system is Elton's functionalism turned inside out from below: judge what was done, from the record, as Elton taught, but judge it in the market town and the manor court, where Elton did not look.
