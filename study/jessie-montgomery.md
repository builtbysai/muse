# Jessie Montgomery: Study Notes

**Site:** Jessie Montgomery (b. 8 December 1981, New York City; living), American composer and violinist; her Rounds, written for Awadagin Pratt, won the 2024 Grammy for Best Contemporary Classical Composition; 2025 Classical Woman of the Year (Performance Today); 2025-26 Artist-in-Residence at Kaufman Music Center; Curtis Institute Composer in Residence 2025-26; former Mead Composer-in-Residence at the Chicago Symphony Orchestra (2021-2024); the first composer to receive the Sphinx Medal of Excellence (2020). Her practice is the composer as a listener in her own neighbourhood: the Lower East Side walk as a DJ set, the spiritual as source code, the anthem as a question, and the rondo as a groove that keeps coming back -- with the soloist breaking the pattern on purpose, sometimes by singing while playing. [C29431]

Where Pratt brings a poem and lets six composers answer it, Montgomery brings a person and lets the piece become that person: the same move, turned inside out again -- the commissioner as the author of the conditions, the composer as the portraitist of the player. Jessie Montgomery was born on 8 December 1981 in New York City and grew up on the Lower East Side, the daughter of Robbie McCauley (1942-2021), playwright and actress and a leading figure in the Black theatre movement, and of a father whose Context Studios rehearsal space in the East Village hosted rock and jazz bands. She learned as much in that rehearsal studio as at Juilliard, and the Miller Theatre notes trace her Baroque-ensemble phrasing -- "the phrasing and lightness and energy" -- as still filtering into her pieces. She is a violinist first: a two-time Sphinx Competition laureate whose association with the Sphinx Organization runs since 1999, a founding member of PUBLIQuartet (2010), a member of the Catalyst Quartet until January 2021, a founding member of the Blacknificent 7 composer collective, and a doctoral candidate in composition at Princeton. She has taught at Northwestern Bienen, Bard Conservatory and Mannes. The violin is not a credential she left behind; it is the ear the music is written with, and the quartets she played in are the rooms the music was first written for. [C29435]

The career that followed runs through the institutions that now commission her. The Sphinx Organization commissioned Starburst (2012) and Banner (2014) and made her the first composer to receive the Sphinx Medal of Excellence (2020). The Chicago Symphony Orchestra made her Mead Composer-in-Residence (July 2021-June 2024), commissioning Hymn for Everyone (2021) and two further orchestral works; Musical America named her its 2023 Composer of the Year. Rounds won the 2024 Grammy for Best Contemporary Classical Composition. Performance Today named her its 2025 Classical Woman of the Year. In 2025-26 she holds two residencies at once: Composer in Residence at the Curtis Institute of Music and Artist-in-Residence at Kaufman Music Center, where The Everything Band played on 1 May 2026. And in 2026 the new work is a cello concerto, These Righteous Paths, written for Abel Selaocoe and touring the world with him -- Berlin, Brussels, Wroclaw, Toronto, the BBC Proms, Lincoln Center, Aspen, Oregon -- while an opera about her mother waits in development with Lincoln Center Theater and the Metropolitan Opera. [C29436]

The centrepiece of her recent career, and the direct tie to #743 Awadagin Pratt, is Rounds. It was commissioned by the Art of the Piano Foundation for Pratt, with nine co-commissioning orchestras (Hilton Head, Baltimore, Colorado, Indianapolis, IRIS, Kansas City, Milwaukee, Rochester, St. Louis), and Pratt gave the premiere on 27 March 2022 with the Hilton Head Symphony Orchestra under John Morris Russell. Montgomery prepared by listening to Pratt's piano recordings, and her programme note thanks "my friend Awadagin Pratt for his collaborative spirit and ingenuity in helping to usher my first work for solo piano into the world". Pratt recorded it with A Far Cry as track 1 of Stillpoint (New Amsterdam, 25 August 2023), where it won the 2024 Grammy; by August 2023 he had played it with 30 orchestras, and by April 2024 the count was more than 50 performances by 30 ensembles -- by December 2024, 80-90 performances with nearly 50 ensembles, per the Chicago Symphony piece read end to end in #743 and not re-read this run. The piece is a rondo, within a rondo, within a rondo, built on T.S. Eliot's Four Quartets and on fractals, and its cadenza is partially improvised by the soloist -- "probably 10- to 20-percent improvisation each night", in Pratt's words -- so the concerto Pratt tours is never quite the same piece twice. [C29437]

The twenty works inspected run 2006-2026 and trace one practice finding its engine, then widening it. Phase 2, the orchestral arrival, 2014-2019 (W05, W06, W16, W17, W18): commissions from Sphinx, the Albany Symphony and Orpheus turn her into an orchestral composer, and the material is place and protest -- the anthem folded into a melting pot in Banner (2014), King's "soul force" in Soul Force (2015), the vanishing Lower East Side in Records from a Vanishing City (2016), the turning seasons in Shift, Change, Turn, and Variations (2019) -- while she is still, in her own words, learning what her instincts are worth with a large ensemble; working with Orpheus "caused a major shift in my thinking about orchestral writing. ## M1 -- The returning round: rondo-within-rondo and the groove of the return

The mechanism Montgomery names herself, in her own programme note for Rounds (read end to end on her own site): "I set the form of the work as a rondo, within a rondo, within a rondo. The five major sections are a rondo; section 'A' is also a rondo in itself; and the cadenza -- which is partially improvised by the soloist -- breaks the pattern, yet, contains within it, the overall form of the work." The images behind it are circling, not marching: swifts (rondine) flying in circle patterns, fractals self-similar across scales, living organisms whose rhythms interact and produce a multitude of outcomes. The watchable and hearable behaviour is a theme that arrives, leaves, and returns slightly changed, so the audience locks into the return as a groove -- Pratt's own word for it, per the Chicago Symphony article read end to end in #743 and not re-read this run ("groove comes back") -- with a rupture (the cadenza) breaking the pattern on purpose before the groove reasserts itself. Pratt also says he uses an EBow on the piano strings mid-piece, "giving it something of an archaic sound -- it is almost like hearing time being looped upon itself", per that same Chicago Symphony article read in #743; no Montgomery-side source read this run mentions an EBow, including Montgomery's own programme note, so the EBow is recorded here as Pratt's account from #743, not as a Montgomery mechanism verified this run. The same return-engine runs elsewhere in her work without the rondo label: in Shift, Change, Turn, and Variations (W18) the slow chanting drone anchors the beginning, the end and the gaps between episodes, and the last segment "thematically came back to the beginning"; in Records from a Vanishing City (W17) the adapted Angolan lullaby appears in each of the three main sections, a home the piece keeps coming back to. Seen in Rounds (W10), W17, W18. [C29439]

**Takeaway:** let the theme come back, and let the return be the groove. [C29439]

## M2 -- The written break: fixed notation with improvisatory breaks

The seam where the score lets go. Montgomery's own formulation, in the These Righteous Paths interview (read end to end): "Many of my works contain passages that share both fixed and improvisatory elements. When these elements are working well together within a piece, in my opinion, you cannot tell whether something has been notated or whether it's occurring in the moment. And even if you do notice where the 'break' is -- the 'break', meaning the place where the performer breaks away from the score and text -- it's done in such a way that it honours the pacing, mood and overall quality of what has been established in the fixed notation." The watchable behaviour is a written passage dissolving into free playing and re-forming into written music, with no audible join -- or a soloist stepping out of the score in full view, as Pratt does in the Rounds cadenza, which Montgomery's note says is "partially improvised by the soloist" (10-20 percent fresh each night, per Pratt), and as the quartet does in Break Away (W04), where the score asks the players to "break away" from it at various points so "the piece takes on further transformation at each performance". In Five Freedom Songs (W08), "Lay dis Body Down" is set in an improvised style "wherein each part of the ensemble chooses their own pacing of the line to create a swirling meditation" (composer's note, read end to end). The mechanism is the break as a composed event: the score does not merely permit freedom, it schedules it, and the freedom is required to honour what the fixed notation established. Seen in W11, W10, W04, W08. [C29440]

**Takeaway:** write the break into the score, and let the player disappear through it. [C29440]

## M3 -- The concerto as portrait: write for the person, not the instrument

Montgomery does not write generic concertos; she writes for one named musician's body, history and sound, and the piece is unperformable in the same way by anyone else. For These Righteous Paths (W11) she went to Norway to watch Selaocoe perform, heard him sing a piece about mothers, and "decided that writing it around Abel's facility to both sing and play was more compositionally interesting and freed me up more. I abandoned the idea that it's something that would work for any cellist" -- "I had to forget about future cellists! ... writing for a cellist who is essentially a one-man band." For L.E.S. Characters (W14) she wrote for a childhood friend whose instrument she knows from the inside: the viola's voice "is inherently in the middle", so the concerto is built on transparency and balance of registers rather than a battle with the orchestra. For DIVIDED (W15) Mesa has held soloist exclusivity since the premiere, and the soloist "takes on the personae of the victims". For Rounds (W10) she prepared by listening to Pratt's piano recordings and built the piece around his collaborative spirit. The watchable behaviour is a soloist who is unmistakably themselves on stage -- singing while playing, telling a childhood story through five street characters, pleading and weeping as the victims -- and an orchestra that makes room for that person rather than competing with them. Seen in W11, W14, W15, W10. [C29441]

**Takeaway:** write the concerto for one person, and let nobody else play it the same way. [C29441]

## M4 -- The everything band: a collective that plays from charts and free improvisation

The ensemble as a way of working, not just a line-up. Montgomery formed The Everything Band in September 2021, after a decade of musing on the idea, when lockdowns cleared everyone's diaries: "three days, and the plan was to show up and play. I gave them charts -- many things were free." The mechanism is the chart as a half-baked idea handed to people who are also composers: "I can put a chart or a half-baked idea in front of this group of people and watch magic happen"; the band "operates as a collective, with a mix of songs and improvisational concepts contributed by individual band members", the music "created by experimentation/improvisation and open-ended form", flowing "through multiple musical styles freely with dynamic clarity". The watchable behaviour is eight or nine all-star composer-performers on stage together, each bringing their own style and technique, the piece changing shape as members contribute and improvise -- and the permission structure audible in it: "we all felt free to mess up", "taking pressure off perfect execution, keeping it more spontaneous", "keeping the music really fresh and on the surface". The Miller notes call it the destination of her whole practice: "In many ways, all of Montgomery's creative work leads towards the formation of this ensemble." The band's size varies by date and line-up -- eight composer-performers in the Spring 2025 release, nine on the agency biography, Montgomery plus seven named members on stage at Kaufman on 1 May 2026 -- and this study asserts no fixed number. Seen in W12. [C29442]

**Takeaway:** hand a half-baked chart to people you trust, and let them finish it live. [C29442]

## M5 -- The voice inside the instrument: the soloist sings, speaks and narrates

The soloist's voice is a second instrument, not an effect. In These Righteous Paths (W11) Selaocoe is, by turns, "the narrator, the orator", "a percussion instrument -- or several", and "in a duet with himself and with other soloists in the orchestra, singing melodies in counterpoint"; he sings McCauley's words in Xhosa, his voice soaring "into the highest tenor range" and descending "into the basso basement, alternating between an enticing growl and resonant throat singing", accenting his cadenza "with pops, snaps, and clicks that he generated vocally" (Oregon review). The last movement's extended incantation becomes "a kind of prayer meant to uplift and imprint the poems' final meaning in the space". The watchable behaviour is the audience's surprise -- "It's always a surprise to audiences when Abel starts singing in the middle of the first movement" (Montgomery, Playbill) -- followed by recognition: listeners in tears, sharing their own loves and loss, and at the Oregon performance a standing ovation and an encore in which Selaocoe had the whole hall singing. The same voice-inside-the-instrument logic runs in miniature in Loisaida, My Love, which Montgomery thinks of "as a duet for soprano and cello, with these two equal voices in free and open conversation" (Miller notes). Seen in W11; supporting evidence in Loisaida, My Love (unnumbered, Miller notes read end to end). [C29443]

**Takeaway:** let the soloist open their mouth, and let the instrument learn to sing. [C29443]

## M6 -- Memory as material: the archive of a place and a family

Montgomery builds pieces out of inherited material -- records, poems, street characters -- and the archive does the structural work. In Records from a Vanishing City (W17) the inherited vinyl collection and one Angolan lullaby thread all three sections; the lullaby is the piece's home key, returned to rather than developed away from. The lullaby itself arrived on a record -- Sanza and Guitar: Music of the Bena Luluwa of Angola and Zaire (Lyrichord, 1974), from the collection of the late writer and illustrator James Rose, to whose memory the piece is dedicated -- sung in call and response by a women's chorus, and her adaptation of it, with the rhythmic chant that follows, appears in each of the three main sections. In L.E.S. Characters (W14) five real street artists of the 1980s Lower East Side become five movements, each a character piece with the viola as "subject and storyteller, recounting the images and memories of these artist souls": The Can Man (Gene Pool, who rode a unicycle covered in cans), The Poet, Mosaic Man (Jim Power, who filled sidewalk cracks with mosaic tiles), Garbage Art -- for which Montgomery envisioned a percussion instrument "basically a whole bunch of garbage thrown into a spinner", built by the Orlando Philharmonic's percussionist, "like a bingo machine, it's got a hand crank and it makes this big old racket" -- and one further movement whose name does not appear in any source read end to end this run. In These Righteous Paths (W11) the archive is her mother's plays and poems, organised by the Sankofa idea that one must "look back in order to go forward", with the three movements running a chronological storyline -- ancestral incantations, her mother's childhood, a look into the future. In the opera in development (W20) the archive becomes an opera: family histories and her mother's impact on the American historical narrative. The watchable behaviour is recognition rather than invention: a familiar lullaby surfacing inside an orchestral texture, a unicycle covered in cans or a mosaic-filled sidewalk crack becoming a movement, a mother's poem becoming a cello line the soloist sings. Seen in W11, W14, W17, W20. [C29444]

**Takeaway:** build the piece out of what you inherited, and let the archive hold the form. [C29444]

## M7 -- Protest lineage: spirituals, freedom songs, and the voice that struggles to be heard

The Black American freedom tradition is not a topic for Montgomery; it is a source language. Her own account of Source Code (W03): transcribing Civil Rights-era Black artists, she "came to identify the spiritual as the common musical origin. The spiritual is a significant part of the DNA of Black folk music, and subsequently most (arguably all) American pop music forms that have developed to the present day." The mechanism shows up three ways in this study's works. In Soul Force (W16) the programme is King's "soul force" itself -- a voice "that struggles to be heard beyond the shackles of oppression", set as a single-movement orchestral work and recorded by the New York Youth Symphony, the youth orchestra Montgomery once played violin in, on the debut album that won the 2022 Grammy for Best Orchestral Performance. In DIVIDED (W15) the soloist embodies the victims of "the social and political unrest that has plagued our generation", pleading, weeping, then angry, almost violent, over minor-second outcries that never drown out "the lyric beauty of the human condition", and "even to the last note, an unresolved dissonance lingers lyrically poignant in its beauty". In Five Freedom Songs (W08) the five songs come from Slave Songs of the United States (1867), each with its origin and social context intact -- "My Father, How Long?" from a Georgetown, S.C. jail at the break of the Great Rebellion, "accompanied by percussive sounds in the strings evoking the chain gang", and "The Day of Judgment" as "an uneasy celebration over the refrain of a traditional West African drumming pattern" (composer's note, read end to end). The watchable behaviour is the past arriving as a present-tense sound: chain-gang percussion in the strings, a jail song's refrain, a spiritual's seven-note melody set as chant. Seen in W15, W16, W08. [C29445]

**Takeaway:** let the spiritual do the arguing -- set the old song, and let it carry the protest. [C29445]

## M8 -- The violinist's ear: string writing and dance groove from inside the instrument

Montgomery writes strings the way a violinist who grew up in them does -- from the inside, with the bow, not from the score down. The evidence is in how players describe the writing: Rostad on L.E.S. Characters (W14), that she found "the right kind of balance of transparency in the orchestra, and balancing of registers" while keeping the violin section, so the violist never has to battle the orchestra; the National Arts Centre note on Shift, Change, Turn, and Variations (W18), that the work is "orchestrated with a visceral clarity that speaks to Montgomery's depth of experience performing in virtuoso ensembles". The groove is the other half of the same ear: she learned as much in her father's East Village rehearsal studio for rock and jazz bands as at Juilliard, and the Miller notes trace her Baroque-ensemble phrasing as still filtering into her pieces. The hearable behaviour is layered, dancing string texture with a pulse you can feel: the strumming pizzicato that is Strum's (W01) "texture motive and the primary driving rhythmic underpinning"; the "practice licks" flickering through Records from a Vanishing City's (W17) layered textures; the "Bernsteinesque jazzy sounding virtuosic riffs exchanged between the winds and strings" and multi-layered strings of Shift's (W18) "Turn"; the "droning and pulsing harmonies" that anchor W18; and Voodoo Dolls' (W19) origin as literal music for dance. The flagged, search-summary-only line -- a Chicago review of Transfigure to Grace calling hers "some of the most idiomatic and irresistible string writing being penned right now" -- was not read end to end this run and is not asserted as read. Seen in W14, W17, W18, W19. [C29446]

**Takeaway:** write strings from the bow's side, and let the groove do the carrying. [C29446]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Montgomery writes for people she knows, about things she inherited, in forms that return. The rondo returns, the lullaby returns, the spiritual returns, the mother's poem returns -- and each return is slightly changed by whoever is playing it, because the score leaves a break for them to step through. The concerto is a portrait, the band is a family, the archive is the form. The neighbourhood is the first archive: a walk through the Lower East Side is a DJ set of English consort, samba, mbira dance music, swing and techno, and the orchestra's job is to play the walk. The family is the second archive: a mother's poems become a cello line, a mother's life becomes an opera, and the Sankofa turn -- look back in order to go forward -- is the form of the concerto itself. The freedom tradition is the third archive: the spiritual is the DNA, the jail song is the percussion, the anthem is a question asked again in a multicultural room. And the player is the last archive: the score is written for one named body, and the break in the score is where that body answers back, in improvisation or in song. Where Pratt, the study before this one, brings a poem and lets six composers answer it, Montgomery brings a person -- Pratt, Rostad, Mesa, Selaocoe, Bullock -- and lets the piece become that person, singing included. Her music teaches its language as it tells a story, and the story it tells is always the same one underneath: what you inherited is the form, and the form only works if somebody real steps through the break in it. [C29447]
