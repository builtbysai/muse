# Adriaen van de Venne: Study Notes

**Site:** Adriaen van de Venne (born 1589, Delft -- some recent scholarship says 1590 -- died 12 November 1662, The Hague), Dutch Golden Age painter of allegories, genre subjects and portraits, miniaturist, book illustrator, designer of political satires and versifier; the Hague satirist who painted in brown, not grey, and let a proverb on a banderole do the judging.

The documented subject list is "peasants, beggars, thieves and fools" -- D2 dropped peasants and thieves. The study uses the repaired form (leg 3).

## The life

Adriaen Pietersz. van de Venne was born in Delft in 1589 and died in The Hague on 12 November 1662, aged 73 (EN Wikipedia; WGA; Oberlin; the Met). The birth year is hedged in the study because the Zeeuws Museum's 2023-24 exhibition, guest-curated by Edwin Buijsen, gives 1590-1662 (CODART), and Wikimedia Commons hedges "circa 1589". No baptism record was found in any page read; the birth year rests on the secondary literature, not on a cited parish record, and the 2023 Buijsen and Nijboer family article was not read. Culturical's 1580 is wrong and is not used. His parents had fled Protestant persecution in the Southern (Spanish) Netherlands during the 1580s (Oberlin; WGA: "'worthy' parents who had fled to Delft from the southern Netherlands to escape war and religious strife"). The family's town of origin within the south is not established by any page read -- Antwerp is suggested in some weak sources only -- and the study names none.

According to Houbraken he learned Latin in Leiden (EN Wikipedia). WGA: Cornelis de Bie's account (1661) is "the only known source on van de Venne's youth and training"; inspired by his early study of Latin to become an illustrator, he was "partly self-taught but also received instruction in painting and illumination" from Simon de Valck. He learned to paint from the master goldsmith and painter Simon de Valk, and afterwards learned engraving from Jeronimus van Diest, "a good painter of grisailles" (EN Wikipedia). Sotheby's lot 25 agrees: he "trained with the lesser-known Leiden painter Simon de Valck and Hieronymous Diest, the latter of whom taught him to paint *en grisaille*". The spellings are unresolved in the sources (de Valk / de Valck; Jeronimus / Hieronymus / Hieronymous) and both teachers are otherwise obscure. No Leiden University enrolment is evidenced; Oberlin's "part of the vibrant political and intellectual community around the University" is phrasing, not a documented matriculation.

"He then moved to Middelburg in 1614" (EN Wikipedia); "By 1614 he was in Middelburg, where his earliest dated paintings show the influence of... Jan Brueghel the Elder's landscapes and of Jan's father Pieter Bruegel the Elder's satirical, moralizing peasant vignettes" (WGA). Sotheby's lot 25: "based in Middelburg, where he is recorded from 1614 until 1624, after which he moved to The Hague". A CERL/STCN authority record says he settled in Middelburg between 1608 and 1613 and worked there 1614-1624 -- single-source, carried as the alternative. The study uses 1614-1624 as the working period. Wikimedia Commons work-location metadata adds "Delft (1605), Antwerp (1607)"; Antwerp 1607 appears in no biography read and is not stated.

In 1625 he moved to The Hague and joined the Guild of Saint Luke there, remaining until his death (EN Wikipedia; Oberlin). On arrival he was "probably employed at court" (WGA): he produced a series of 105 miniatures celebrating the assumption of power of Frederick Henry and his marriage in 1625 to Amalia von Solms, today mostly in the British Museum (Sotheby's lot 25), and Oberlin records "several commissions for the Dutch stadholder Frederik Hendrik". He was dean of the guild -- in 1637 per EN Wikipedia, in 1640 per WGA, "repeatedly elected dean" per Oberlin. No source read reconciles 1637 against 1640; "repeatedly elected" suggests more than one term, which could reconcile them, but no page read says so explicitly, so the study carries the conflict.

He was a founding member of Confrerie Pictura (EN Wikipedia; Oberlin: "in 1656 was one of the founding members of The Hague painters' confraternity, 'Pictura'"). The Confrerie was founded in 1656 in The Hague by 48 dissatisfied painters, led by first deacon Adriaen Hanneman; the founders named in EN Wikipedia include Adriaen van de Venne among Willem Doudijns, Jacob van der Does, Jan de Bisschop, Theodor van der Schuer, Dirck van der Lisse, Daniel Mijtens the Younger, Johannes Mytens, Willem van Diest, Robbert Duval, Joris van der Haagen and Augustinus Terwesten. He was one of the 48 founders, not one of the founding five (Doudijns, Mytens, Terwesten, Duval, van der Schuer). The Confrerie's goal was to improve the independent status and social position of the artist by encouraging a more academic approach; members had to exhibit permanently in the meeting room, replacing a sold work with a new one; it met upstairs at the Boterwaag, Prinsegracht. The founding date carries its own controversy: Houbraken believed 1662, Van Gool proved 1656, and a 2019 *Early Modern Low Countries* article argues it was founded in 1656 and again in 1662. Van de Venne died 12 November 1662 -- the year of the "second founding" -- but no page read places him in the 1662 events.

His brother Jan (Jan Pietersz. van de Venne) was "a well-known publisher and art dealer" in Middelburg, his collaborator (WGA); CERL: brother Jan ran an art/print dealership and bookshop-publishing house in Middelburg and died there in 1625. The brother must not be confused with the Flemish painter Jan van der Venne (active by 1616, died before 1651), formerly "Pseudo van de Venne", who was "erroneously believed to be the brother, also called Jan, of the better known Dutch painter Adriaen van de Venne. Adriaen's brother Jan, however, died in Middelburg in 1625 and could therefore not have painted the works attributed to this anonymous artist" (EN Wikipedia, Jan van der Venne). Jacques Foucart (Louvre) corrected the conflation in 1978, identifying Pseudo-Van de Venne with a Flemish artist believed born in Mechelen c. 1600, master in the Brussels Guild of Saint Luke in 1616 and court painter to the governors of the Spanish Netherlands. His wife is single-source: CERL/STCN alone records that from 1614 he was married to Elisabeth de Pours, born in 's-Heer-Arendskerke, daughter of a captain at Middelburg -- not corroborated by Wikipedia, WGA or Oberlin. No source read names any son of van de Venne, painter or otherwise; a Doyle auction snippet mentioning "Pieter Adriaens van de Venne, Dutch, 1630-1693" establishes no relationship and must not be used as a son.

He died in The Hague in 1662. His grisaille/proverb work "made him famous during his lifetime, and remained popular throughout the 18th century after his death" (EN Wikipedia). WGA's closing judgement is blunt: "No significant artists followed his lead." CODART (2023): "Although relatively unknown to the general public, Adriaen van de Venne is considered one of the most original and versatile artists of the Dutch Golden Age"; the Zeeuws Museum mounted the first major survey, *The Inverted World of Adriaen van de Venne*, 30 September 2023 - 21 April 2024, guest-curated by Edwin Buijsen. CODART's summary of the work: "Many of his scenes show people mocking social conventions" and he "lived and worked in a period which, like ours, was characterized by major social changes and political unrest".

## M1. The brunaille proverb picture -- brown, not grey

The core mechanism of the late oeuvre, and the study's first correction to the received label. His second teacher van Diest "painted grisailles, a technique that van de Venne later employed extensively" (WGA), and "From 1620 until his death van de Venne made many grisailles and engravings of genre subjects, featuring peasants, beggars, thieves and fools" (EN Wikipedia). But Sotheby's lot 19, footnote 1: "In the literature on Van de Venne, these monochrome oil paintings on panel are universally referred to as grisailles although in this case and the following two lots they are actually in shades of brown." The works are oil on panel, *en brunaille* -- brown monochrome, not grey (Sotheby's lots 19 and 276). Sotheby's lot 25: van Diest "taught him to paint *en grisaille*, a technique he adopted almost exclusively during the latter part of his working life". The mechanism is literal: a two-figure scene in brown, plus an inscribed saying on a curling banderole that names the moral -- in the *Armoe' soeckt list* case the banderole is "lying on the ground" (Sotheby's lot 19). The picture is a joke you read and then get: the image shows the scene, the banderole tells you what it means, and the brown keeps the whole thing low, cheap-looking and unpretentious. Mariet Westermann's thesis, cited in the literature, is that he invented an *ironic* grisaille: "*Fray en Leelijck*. Adriaen van de Venne's Invention of the Ironic Grisaille", *Nederlands Kunsthistorisch Jaarboek* 50 (1999), pp. 250-251. The irony is structural: a deliberately low, monochrome medium carrying a moral picture whose joke depends on the viewer reading the inscribed saying against the image. No page read gives Van de Venne's own stated reason for working monochrome, and no pigment or binder analysis was found. Do not claim grisaille was chosen to imitate sculpture, to save pigment, or for speed -- none of that is in the sources read. *Fray en Leelijck* ("the beautiful and the ugly") is the type specimen: "a blind man playing a flute and a peasant woman playing a Hurdy-Gurdy"; "'beauty' is likely an allusion to the music played by the pair and 'ugly' a reference to their coarse and perhaps less appealing appearance" (Sotheby's lot 276). The same blind flute-player reappears in *Armoe soeckt List*, there with a wooden leg, winding a hurdy-gurdy beside a blind woman playing a rumble pot "while another peasant stands on his head in the background" (Sotheby's lot 276). "Van de Venne's allegories often displayed a degree of spirited amusement in the treatment of their less fortunate subjects and became widely popular" (Sotheby's lot 276) -- the sources' own framing: amusement *at* the less fortunate is the period mechanism, not modern sympathy.

**Takeaway:** build the picture in one brown, not in colour and not in grey, and put the moral on a banderole inside the picture -- the scene shows it, the saying names it, the brown keeps it low. The watchable behaviour is the reading: the figures holding their pose while the banderole's words arrive, the joke landing only when the text is read against the image, and the brown never brightening into colour.

## M2. Fishing for souls -- the river as argument

*Fishing for Souls* (*De zielenvisserij*), 1614, oil on panel, Rijksmuseum SK-A-447, is the early masterpiece and the great political-satire allegory of the Dutch Revolt. "An ironic commentary on the Catholic and Protestant troubles of the Eighty Years War that split the border between the Northern from the Southern Netherlands along the Schelde river, very close to his home in Middleburg. When he painted this picture, the Twelve Years' Truce was in effect since 1609" (EN Wikipedia). The painting "shows an allegory of the jealousy between the various religious denominations during the Twelve Years' Truce" (EN Wikipedia, *Fishing for Souls*). Left bank (Protestant): "the two princes of Orange behind an orange tree branch; Maurice and Frederick Henry... accompanied by Frederick V, Elector Palatine, King James I of England and the young Louis XIII of France with his mother Maria de' Medici". Right bank (Catholic): "the Catholic cardinals carrying their pope accompanied by Albert and Isabella". "The various attributes of the fishermen (nets, boats, etc.) are labelled with Latin text to clarify the meaning." The Rijksmuseum: "The Protestants are on the left side of the river, the Catholics on the right. Who comes out on top?" FitzGerald (*Arcadia* 2018): the orange trees on the left denote the House of Orange-Nassau, while on the opposite side cardinals carry the Pope on his traditional sedia gestatoria; "The river divides the Catholic and Calvinist groups; the sun is shining and the trees are fertile on the Protestant left bank whereas on the Catholic right bank, the land has fallen fallow and ominous clouds gather"; "The Calvinist boats provide a calm refuge while the Catholic boats scramble to hold on to any paraphernalia, statues and sacred vessels along with those drawn up in their fishing nets". The picture is "a piece of religious propaganda coming down in favor of the Calvinist North", depicting Matthew 4:19, where Simon and Andrew are called to become fishers of men. The pentimento is the mechanism's honest edge: "In the original painting the Protestant fishermen had successfully fished up some believers while the Catholic nets were empty. The 'souls' near the Catholic boats were added later" (EN Wikipedia) -- the even-handedness is partly a later hand; the original was a Protestant boast. And the wit is in the corner: a large fly "painted on as a *trompe-l'oeil* wink to Jan Brueghel the Elder" (EN Wikipedia). Provenance: appropriated for the national collection in September 1798 from Prince Willem V's collection during the French occupation; bought 1808; "considered a highlight of the collection since it was bought in 1808". Dimensions 98.5 x 187.8 cm are REPORTED (EN Wikipedia, citing RKD) -- the Rijksmuseum object page was read end to end and gives no dimensions in its extracted text.

**Takeaway:** build a river that divides the picture into two banks, each side fishing for the same thing, with labels on the gear so the argument can be read. The watchable behaviour is the contest: the two banks hauling their nets, the Protestant side calm and fertile while the Catholic side scrambles, and the catch -- the souls -- arriving on one side only, with a fly on the frame to remind the viewer the painter is winking.

## M3. The ascent and decline -- five scenes, made again and again

The most considered serial project of the career is the *Armoe* series, and it shows how van de Venne worked: design once, paint many times. The series is an illustration of the ascent and decline of human life in five scenes: *Armoe' soeckt list* (Poverty leads to Cunning) -- *List soeckt rijckdom* (Cunning leads to Wealth) -- *Rijckdom soeckt weelde* (Wealth leads to Luxury) -- *Weelde soeckt ellende* (Luxury leads to Misery) -- *Ellend' soeckt de doot* (Misery leads to Death) (Sotheby's lot 19). In *Poverty leads to Cunning* the figures are "two old, blind musicians, the man playing a hurdy-gurdy and the woman a *rommelpot*". "The designs for this complex series undoubtedly cost the artist a great deal of time and thought. In order to make the most efficient use of that investment he painted not one but several series of the scenes... at least five versions of the series wholly or in part. The earliest one dates from 1630" (Sotheby's lot 19). "Although details of the different versions can vary, little change was made to the overall design or content. There are, though, variations in format, use of colour (grisaille or polychrome) and execution." The surviving grisaille *Armoe* panel measures "circa 38 by 30 cm, executed between 1630 and 1635 (see Buijsen 2013, C.1)". The working method is documented by infrared reflectography: the Weldon panel "has a loosely sketched underdrawing which shows some minor differences from the final painting. The most striking one is the head of the old man, which in the underdrawing is shown in profile (Buijsen 2013, fig. 11; infrared reflectography by Charlotte Hale, The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York)". And the hinge is datable: Buijsen notes no underdrawings have been detected in van de Venne pictures dated after 1637 -- he later painted directly without drawing. The imagery's sources are print culture, not invention: an anonymous print after a design by Jheronimus Bosch from c. 1570, and 16th-century life's-progress print series by Cornelisz Anthonisz and Jacob de Gheyn II. He "had already toyed with the [life's-progress] subject in a poetry anthology of 1623, and... was to return to it later at greater length in his wide-ranging book *Tafereel van de Belacchende Werelt* of 1635" (Sotheby's lot 19).

**Takeaway:** build a life as five scenes in a fixed order -- poverty, cunning, wealth, luxury, misery, death -- each scene the same design painted again in a slightly different version. The watchable behaviour is the procession: each scene holding still long enough to be read, the figures ageing and the goods accumulating as the scenes advance, the design staying fixed while the versions vary, and the last scene arriving exactly where the first one promised it would.

## M4. The inverted world -- the destitute as mirror

The subject that made him famous and that the Zeeuws Museum exhibition took as its title. Most of his well-known grisaille paintings depict "the destitute and maimed" (WGA), and from 1620 his grisailles and engravings featured "peasants, beggars, thieves and fools as illustrations of current proverbs and sayings, mostly by Jacob Cats" (EN Wikipedia). *Allegory of Poverty* (c. 1630s, Oberlin AMAM 1960.94) is the mechanism in one picture: a blind beggar led by a small dog carries an old woman (leper's clapper and alms bowl) who carries a small child; straw-stuffed wooden shoes, tattered garments, discarded hand-cleats in the foreground; the banderole reads "they are feeble legs which must carry poverty". Its pendant, *An Allegory of Wealth* -- a dandy carrying a fashionable woman who spills wine and scatters gold coins -- is known only through copies, the original lost. *Dancing Beggars* (1635, Mauritshuis inv. 202, 14 x 30.2 cm VERIFIED) dances the same figures with the inscription "all om arm" at lower right. *Al-Arm* (c. 1625-35, Met 2026.17, red and black chalk, 32.7 x 47.7 cm VERIFIED) is beggars fighting, banderole "All-arm" ("All Poor", a pun on "alarm"): a man with a knife about to stab another, a peg-legged man about to strike with his crutch. Buijsen's proposal: the large red-chalk sheets are ricordi, the same size as the grisaille paintings they reproduce; no corresponding painting exists for this composition. The world is inverted because the lowest figures carry the picture's whole moral weight: the blind lead, the lame dance, the poor fight over nothing, and the rich -- in the lost pendant -- spill wine and scatter gold. The exhibition title says it plainly: the inverted world is the world turned upside down so the bottom can judge the top.

**Takeaway:** build the picture from the bottom of the social order -- a blind beggar, a peg-legged fighter, dancing beggars -- and let them carry the moral while the rich are absent or spilling. The watchable behaviour is the inversion holding: the feeble legs carrying poverty step by step, the dancers dancing while the inscription stays readable, the fight never resolving, and the picture's judgement staying with the figures at the bottom.

## M5. The emblem-book engine -- Cats

Van de Venne's satire runs on the emblem book, and Jacob Cats is its engine. "Holland's leading writers employed Van de Venne, whose illustrations contributed greatly to the popularity of Dutch emblem books, which combined pictures and prose to present a moral lesson" (WGA). From 1618 he was "also active as a printmaker and book illustrator, notably for the popular poet Jacob Cats" (Oberlin). The *Houwelick* ("Matrimony") collaboration is the measure of it: first published in 1625, "over a nine-year period, Van de Venne produced no fewer than three entirely different sets of illustrations for this publication" -- the 1625 quarto with 22 prints, the 1628 octavo with an entirely new set of 39 illustrations, reissued 1632 (Sotheby's lots 74/267). The 1625 *Houwelick* was published by his brother Jan in Middelburg (British Museum). He also designed illustrations to Cats's *Proteus* (1618) and *Sinne- en minnebeelden* (1627); the Met holds the 1622 Blaeu edition (*Silenus Alcibiadis*, 21.36.43, 22.2 x 17.6 x 3.5 cm VERIFIED) with 52 engraved emblems by Jan Swelinck after van de Venne in the 1627 *Proteus*, each a circular moral/love emblem with Dutch, Latin and French text. The mechanism is the emblem's three-part machine -- image, motto, riddle -- and the grisaille banderole of M1 is that machine painted: the picture is the emblem without the book. His drawings served as designs for engravings: a Sotheby's drawing is "pen and brown ink and grey wash with touches of white heightening, within brown ink framing lines; indented and blackened on the *verso*" -- indented for transfer to the plate. And the emblem habit made the work travel: "many such works becoming models for prints" (Sotheby's lot 276). The *Spiegel van den ouden en nieuwen tijdt* illustration credit could not be connected to van de Venne in any source read this run -- do not assert it.

**Takeaway:** build each picture as an emblem -- image, motto, riddle -- with the motto inscribed in the picture itself so the viewer reads the moral before they finish looking. The watchable behaviour is the emblem's three parts arriving in order: the image first, the motto second, the riddle dawning third, and the same design returning in a new set of illustrations, the picture remade rather than repeated.

## M6. The Orange court -- propaganda in miniature and on horseback

Van de Venne was an Orange propagandist, and the court work is the other half of the career from the satire -- the same hand, the opposite purpose. *A Winter Landscape with Figures Skating on a Frozen River, Prince Maurits of Orange-Nassau with a Hunting Party in the Foreground* (1620, oil on panel, signed and dated "AV VENNE / 1620", 75 x 114.5 cm REPORTED via Sotheby's) "celebrates the peace and subsequent prosperity established by Prince Maurits of Orange-Nassau" after the 1609 truce, with Maurits hunting in the foreground. A pair of portraits of Maurits and Frederik Hendrik engraved in 1618 by Willem Jacobsz. Delff were purchased by the States General (Sotheby's lot 25). On his 1625 arrival in The Hague he produced the series of 105 miniatures celebrating Frederik Hendrik's assumption of power and his 1625 marriage to Amalia von Solms (Sotheby's lot 25; the miniatures recur in the 1626 album *'tLants Sterckte*, British Museum, c. 105 gouaches -- the skater with a pole, folio 99; the Summer horseman, folio 4). *Prince Maurits accompanied by his two brothers, Frederick V, Elector of the Palatinate, and some counts of Nassau on horseback* (1625, Rijksmuseum SK-A-445) and *Stadtholder Prince Maurice Lying in State* (1625, SK-A-446, impressions ordered by the States-General 21 July 1625) are the state occasions in oil. Late, the loyalty continues: *Allegory of the stadholdership of Prince Frederik Hendrik* (1642, Rijksmuseum SK-C-1343). The mechanism is the same as the satire's -- a picture that argues -- but the argument is praise: the court as the guarantor of peace and prosperity, the prince on horseback as the picture's centre, the miniatures as a portable court that could be carried in an album.

**Takeaway:** build the court as a picture that praises -- the prince on horseback at the centre, the hunting party or the retinue around him, the landscape prosperous behind them. The watchable behaviour is the procession: the retinue moving in order, the prince holding his place at the centre, the miniature scale making the whole court portable, and the praise never wavering into satire.

## M7. The landscape with anecdote -- and the 1625 pivot

The early landscapes are Brueghel-manner pictures with a high bird's-eye vantage point and a crowd of anecdotal figures: *The Summer* and *Winter* pendants (1614, Berlin), *Winter Landscape with Skaters near a Castle* (1615, Worcester, 16.5 x 23.2 cm VERIFIED -- skaters, a horse-drawn sledge and a castle, anecdotal figures from woodchopper to patricians), *A Merry Company in an Arbor* (1615, Getty -- maidens making music under a flower arbor, two women sitting on a finch-catching net, voyeurs in the trees, two jesters tumbling out of the branches), *The Departure of a Dignitary from Middelburg* (1615, SK-A-1775). The Rijksmuseum catalogue biography (Bruijnen 2007) divides the painted oeuvre into a Middelburg period (1614-c.1625) and a Hague period (1625-1662), and the works bear it out: the 1625 Four Seasons series (*Spring* SK-A-1772, *Summer* SK-A-1771, *Winter* SK-A-1774, *Autumn* SK-A-1773) and *Skaters* (1625, SK-A-1768) show the pivot inside single works -- drastically lowered vantage points, figures enlarged relative to the picture surface, numbers reduced, execution freer and more draughtsman-like with dark painted contours. *Skaters* is the catalogue's type-specimen of the Hague shift: elegant skating couples, a gentleman binding skates onto a lady, a single horizontally grained oak plank, dendrochronology youngest heartwood ring 1613. *Winter* (SK-A-1774, support 14.6 x 37.1 cm VERIFIED) keeps the macabre anecdote at the new scale: skaters at right, and at left figures urinating and defecating against trees, a defecating dog in the foreground, a man staring at the bare buttocks of a woman fallen on the ice. The macabre detail is constant across the pivot -- birds frozen to death in *Shrove Tuesday*, a horse's carcase in the Valkenburg horse fair, a drowned lamb in *Autumn*, Death stepping out from behind the curtain in *To Each His Own Pastime* (Met 1994.52, red chalk, 35.2 x 44.9 cm VERIFIED: an elegant couple playing shuttlecock indoors, waited on by servants, while Death emerges from behind the curtain; empty glass, strewn playing cards, lute). The late Hague mode is "almost exclusively grisailles for the open market, most of them genre pieces with a comical, moralistic slant with inscribed banderoles", plus religious and allegorical works and a few large-scale equestrian portraits -- and the technique becomes "freer, sometimes even sketchy", which "enabled him to boost his output to 'hundreds of monochrome pieces'" (Campo Weyerman, quoted in the catalogue). The pivot is not a hard switch: grisaille production is dated "from 1620" while he was still in Middelburg, the 1625 Four Seasons are polychrome, and the Oberlin *Allegory of Poverty* (c. 1630s, 54.5 x 42.2 cm VERIFIED) is itself "a somewhat more finished polychrome painting" -- the Hague period kept a polychrome strand alongside the monochrome one.

**Takeaway:** build a landscape crowded with small anecdotes at a high vantage, then drop the viewpoint to the ground and enlarge the figures until the anecdotes become the picture. The watchable behaviour is the pivot itself: the crowd thinning, the figures growing, the brush loosening, and the macabre detail -- the frozen bird, the carcase, Death behind the curtain -- staying visible at every scale.

## M8. The artist as author -- the laughing world

Van de Venne wrote his own books, and the last mechanism is the painter as author. *Tafereel van de Belacchende Werelt* ("Picture of the Laughing World", The Hague, 1635) is his own humorous depiction of 17th-century life in The Hague: 12 fine half-page engravings after his designs by Daniel van Bremden, Pieter de Jode the Younger, Pieter Serwouters and others (the Met copy, 1993.1126, "Engravings and two woodcuts", 23.3 x 18.2 x 3 cm VERIFIED). Its theme is "a dialogue between the young farmer Tamme Lubbert (Soft Johnny) and his sweetheart... comment on the attractions and visitors at the famous annual fair in The Hague". He had already contributed both verses and illustrations to the *Zeeusche Nachtegael* (Zeeland Nightingale, 1623), with contributions by more than twenty Zeeland poets including Jacob Cats and Johan de Brune (CODART), and published his own *Tafereel van Sinne-Mal* (Middelburg, Jan Pietersz. van de Venne, 1623). His own verse books followed: *Hollandsche Sinne-Droom* (1634) and the *Tafereel* of 1635. The mechanism is the book as a picture that talks: the engravings carry the scenes, the dialogue carries the satire, and the fair -- the annual Hague Fair with its attractions and visitors -- is the subject, the same crowd of fools, peasants and traders as the grisailles, now given lines to speak. His self-portrait was engraved by Wenceslaus Hollar, etching dated 1649, "first state of five" (Met 17.50.84) -- the author presenting himself as the man who laughs at the world and writes it down.

**Takeaway:** build a book, not a single picture -- a dialogue between two plain speakers walking through a fair, the scenes engraved after the painter's own designs, the satire spoken aloud rather than inscribed. The watchable behaviour is the conversation: the two speakers walking from attraction to attraction, each scene arriving as they reach it, the fair's fools performing while the dialogue judges them, and the book turning its own pages.

## Evolution

The arc the dated record supports is the Rijksmuseum catalogue's clean division: a Middelburg period (1614-c.1625) and a Hague period (1625-1662). Early (1614-1625, Middelburg): the most ambitious, meticulously painted, polychrome works -- political allegories (*Fishing for Souls* 1614; Truce allegory 1616; *Satire* c. 1619), Orange propaganda (Valkenburg Horse Fair 1618; Maurits hunting winter landscape 1620), and densely populated Brueghel-manner landscapes with a high bird's-eye vantage point (Berlin Summer/Winter 1614; Worcester winter 1615; *Departure of a Dignitary* 1615). Scale is at its largest here: *Fishing for Souls* is about 1 x 1.9 m, the 1620 winter landscape 75 x 114.5 cm. Transitional (1625-1626): the Four Seasons series and *Skaters* show the pivot inside single works -- drastically lowered vantage points, figures enlarged relative to the picture surface, numbers reduced, execution freer and more draughtsman-like with dark painted contours; still polychrome, still court-referencing (Frederik Hendrik may appear in *Summer*), but made in The Hague and probably for the open market. Late (c. 1625-1662, The Hague): "almost exclusively grisailles for the open market, most of them genre pieces with a comical, moralistic slant with inscribed banderoles", plus religious/allegorical works and a few large-scale equestrian portraits. Scale collapses to cabinet size (*Dancing Beggars* 14 x 30.2 cm; *Armoe' soeckt list* c. 38 x 30 cm; *Allegory of Poverty* 54.5 x 42.2 cm). Technique becomes "freer, sometimes even sketchy", which "enabled him to boost his output to 'hundreds of monochrome pieces'" (Campo Weyerman, quoted in the catalogue). The five-scene ascent-and-decline series (from 1630) was painted in several versions -- serial production, not one-offs.

What stays constant. (1) Satire and moralizing throughout: religious-political allegory early (*Fishing for Souls*) and social-moral satire late (*Fools Have the Most Fun*, 1661, one of the last dated works). (2) The proverb/emblem logic: the grisaille banderole is the painted equivalent of the Cats emblem -- image + motto + riddle, a device already present in the emblem-book designs of 1618-1627 and still structuring the late grisailles. (3) Bruegel-derived peasant types: cripples, beggars, fools, dancing peasants recur from the earliest landscapes to the 1650s (*Fighting Beggars*). (4) Loyalty to the House of Orange: early propaganda for Maurits and Frederick V, late allegory of Frederik Hendrik's stadholdership (1642). (5) The macabre anecdotal detail: birds frozen to death in *Shrove Tuesday*, a horse's carcase in the Valkenburg fair, a drowned lamb in *Autumn*, Death stepping out from behind the curtain in *To Each His Own Pastime*.

Where the record does NOT support a clean arc. (1) Grisaille is not purely late: van de Venne learned grisaille from Jeronimus van Diest before 1614, and Wikipedia dates the grisaille/proverb production "from 1620" -- while he was still in Middelburg. The 1625 move is a softening of the break, not its cause. (2) Late work is not only small and monochrome: *Fools Have the Most Fun* (1661) is oil on canvas, *Where There Are People* (1652) is a large-ish polychrome panel, and the 1642 Frederik Hendrik allegory is a large-scale state portrait. (3) The technical hinge is datable to c. 1637, not 1625: Buijsen's infrared work (via Sotheby's) found underdrawings in works up to 1636 and none detected after 1637 -- he began painting directly without drawing. (4) The late chronology is thin: after c. 1635 there are few securely dated anchors (1642, 1643, 1644, 1647, 1652, 1661), so any claim about a "late style" rests on a handful of dated works plus many undated grisailles. (5) Palette: the early/late colour contrast is real but the 1625 Four Seasons are polychrome and transitional, and the Oberlin *Allegory of Poverty* (c. 1630s) is itself a "somewhat more finished polychrome painting" -- the Hague period kept a polychrome strand alongside the monochrome one.

## Aesthetic logic

Van de Venne's aesthetic is the joke told in brown, at cabinet size, with the moral written on the picture. Everything late is small -- the grisailles 14 to 55 cm, the emblem books the size of a hand -- and everything is brown: the brunaille ground is the world as it is, low and unpretentious, and the banderole is the finger pointing at what is wrong with it. The largeness is in the argument: one river with two banks implies a whole war of religion, one blind beggar carrying an old woman implies a whole social order, five small scenes imply a whole human life. The early work is the exception that proves the rule: the big polychrome allegories are the same argument told large, with labels on the fishing gear instead of a banderole, and the court work is the same hand turned to praise. The Bruegel inheritance is the types -- cripples, beggars, fools, dancers -- and the Cats inheritance is the machine -- image, motto, riddle. The inverted world is the thesis: turn the world upside down and the bottom judges the top, because the blind musicians and the dancing beggars are the only figures honest enough to carry the moral. The honesty limit is the dead end WGA states flatly: "No significant artists followed his lead." His afterlife is in print and emblem culture, not in a school -- the proverb pictures stayed popular through the 18th century, and the 2023-24 Zeeuws Museum survey was the first major one. The dodo is the cautionary emblem of the whole study: a drawing presented as drawn from life, with a Latin caption claiming a living specimen in Amsterdam, that current scholarship assumes he never saw alive -- the caption is the credibility trick, and the study does not repeat it as fact.

## Network

The defining collaboration: Jacob Cats (1577-1660), emblem-book poet -- van de Venne was Cats's principal illustrator; *Houwelick* first published 1625, three entirely different sets of illustrations over nine years (1625 quarto, 22 prints; 1628 octavo, 39 illustrations; reissued 1632); illustrations also to Cats's *Proteus* (1618) and *Sinne- en minnebeelden* (1627); the 1625 *Houwelick* was published by his brother Jan in Middelburg (leg 3).

The brother: Jan Pietersz. van de Venne, publisher and art dealer in Middelburg, died 1625 -- NOT the Flemish painter Jan van der Venne / "Pseudo van de Venne" (Foucart 1978). No painter sons evidenced; WGA closes with "No significant artists followed his lead" (leg 3).

The Orange court: Prince Maurits and Frederik Hendrik -- the 1620 winter landscape celebrates "the peace and prosperity established by Prince Maurits"; a pair of princely portraits engraved 1618 by Willem Jacobsz. Delff were purchased by the States General; on his 1625 arrival in The Hague he was "probably employed at court" and produced 105 miniatures celebrating Frederik Hendrik's assumption of power and 1625 marriage to Amalia von Solms; Oberlin: "several commissions for the Dutch stadholder Frederik Hendrik" (legs 1 and 3).

The Confrerie: founded 1656 in The Hague by 48 dissatisfied painters, led by first deacon Adriaen Hanneman; founders named in EN Wikipedia include Willem Doudijns, Jacob van der Does, Jan de Bisschop, Theodor van der Schuer, Dirck van der Lisse, Daniel Mijtens the Younger, Johannes Mytens, Willem van Diest, Adriaen van de Venne, Robbert Duval, Joris van der Haagen and Augustinus Terwesten (leg 3).

The guilds: Hague Guild of Saint Luke 1625, dean 1637 (Wikipedia) vs 1640 (WGA) vs "repeatedly elected" (Oberlin). Middelburg guild membership not established in any source read -- do not assert it (leg 3).

The influences on him: Jan Brueghel the Elder and Pieter Bruegel the Elder (EN Wikipedia; WGA: earliest dated paintings show Jan Brueghel's landscapes and Pieter Bruegel's "satirical, moralizing peasant vignettes"). Teachers Simon de Valck and Hieronymus van Diest (the grisaille technique came from van Diest). Emblem tradition via Cats. Callot: NO source found tying Callot to van de Venne -- Callot's influence is documented for Quast, not for van de Venne; do not carry it (leg 3).

The doorway link, both directions: Grove/Leistra via EN Wikipedia Quast (see D1) plus Onno van Seggelen's "main sources of inspiration" list (Callot, Brouwer, van de Venne). The asymmetry Grove itself states: Quast's *looser style and individual types* are closer to Brouwer than to van de Venne -- van de Venne is the caricatural-quality model, Brouwer the type-and-handling model (leg 3).

The Brouwer line: do NOT claim van de Venne influenced Brouwer. No source found claims it. Brouwer (Oudenaarde c. 1605/06 - Antwerp, January 1638) was the tavern/brawl innovator whose work "had an important influence on the next generation of Flemish and Dutch genre painters"; his models were Dirck Hals and the Haarlem circle, and the Hals-pupil story is one of Houbraken's "most glaring errors". The shared root of van de Venne and Brouwer is Bruegel, not each other (leg 3).

The afterlife: no painterly school after him. His grisaille proverb work "made him famous during his lifetime, and remained popular throughout the 18th century after his death" (EN Wikipedia). His self-portrait was engraved by Wenceslas Hollar. The study's influence section is honest about this dead end rather than inventing a lineage (leg 3).
