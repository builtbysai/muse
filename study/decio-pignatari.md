## The life: the Brazilian who met Gomringer and then widened the poem

Decio Pignatari was born on 20 August 1927 in Jundiai, Sao Paulo state, Brazil, the son of Italian immigrants Antonio Pignatari and Italia Maria Micheli, and he spent his childhood and adolescence in Osasco, in the west of Greater Sao Paulo. (C12862, leg 1) The birth date and place agree across Wikipedia EN, Wikipedia PT, the Delfos chronology and the IASS-AIS obituary's lifespan; no source read gives a conflicting birth date. (C12862, leg 1) His later family life is thinly sourced: two low-authority web biographies say he married Lilla Pignatari and the couple had three children; no source of higher standing read this run names his wife or children, so the marriage and children are recorded as weakly sourced, not confirmed. (C12884, leg 1)

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the same as the doorway: the 1955 meeting turns a local experiment into an international movement, and then Pignatari keeps widening what a poem can be made of, long after the orthodox concrete phase ends.

(1) Lyric beginnings and the break, 1948-1952. He enrolled in law at the University of Sao Paulo and graduated from its Faculty of Law; the graduation year conflicts, 1954 in Wikipedia PT and the German Wikipedia against a 1950-1953 course in the Delfos chronology. (C12863, leg 1) He debuted as a poet in February 1949 in the Revista de Novissimos, alongside Haroldo and Augusto de Campos, and published in the Revista Brasileira de Poesia, mouthpiece of the Geracao de 45. (C12864, leg 1) His first book of poems, O Carrossel, appeared in 1950, sponsored by the Clube de Poesia de Sao Paulo; in 1951 he broke with the Clube de Poesia group. (C12864, leg 1) The Campos brothers met Pignatari in 1948, and by the following year the three were already thinking about poetry together. (C12865, leg 1)

(2) Noigandres and Ulm, 1952-1958. In 1952 Pignatari founded the Noigandres group with the brothers Haroldo de Campos (1929-2003) and Augusto de Campos (born 1931), and edited the magazine-book of the same name; the name "Noigandres" was taken from Ezra Pound's Canto XX, quoting the Occitan troubadour Arnaut Daniel, and was understood as a synonym of poetry in progress. (C12866, leg 1) In the same year the group made contact with the visual concrete artists of the Ruptura group, led by Waldemar Cordeiro. (C12866, leg 1) The movement was officially launched at the First National Exhibition of Concrete Art at the Museum of Modern Art of Sao Paulo (MAM-SP) in December 1956, with poster poems exhibited alongside paintings and sculpture; five issues of Noigandres were published over the following ten years. (C12867, C12889, leg 1) In January 1954 he taught the course "Raizes da Poesia Moderna" in Teresopolis, then left for Europe, where he lived until 1956, meeting Pierre Boulez, Tomas Maldonado and Eugen Gomringer. (C12868, leg 1) In 1955, in Ulm, Germany, Pignatari met Gomringer at the Hochschule fur Gestaltung, where Gomringer was then secretary to Max Bill; Solt calls this meeting "the beginning of the international movement of concrete poetry". (C12869, leg 1) Who introduced them is unresolved: Sarduy says Tomas Maldonado; Augusto de Campos's 2008 interview says Alexandre Wollner; the Bauhaus Imaginista article names no introducer. (C12869, leg 1) What the meeting led to is consistent: when Pignatari returned to Brazil in 1956, he and Gomringer were planning an international anthology that was never published, and Gomringer agreed in a letter dated 30 August that the whole anthology could well be named "poesie concrete". (C12870, leg 1) After the meeting the Brazilians and Gomringer converged toward a simplification of language, and for the orthodox phase from 1956 to the end of the decade they approached each other closely, arriving at a kind of least common multiple of poetical language and even at the one-word poem. (C12870, leg 1) The group's synthesis followed in Noigandres 4, March 1958: the "Plano-Piloto para Poesia Concreta" (Pilot Plan for Concrete Poetry), signed by all three. (C12871, leg 1)

(3) Theory and the semiotic turn, 1958-1968. The group's theoretical writings were collected as Teoria da Poesia Concreta: Textos Criticos e Manifestos 1950-1960, first published in 1965 by Edicoes Invencao, with later editions in 1975, 1987 and 2006. (C12872, leg 1) The shared poetics was "verbivocovisual" poetry, a term from Joyce's Finnegans Wake, denoting the interweaving of the verbal, vocal and visual dimensions of language, with the word treated as a material thing-word in space-time. (C12871, leg 1) Two later theoretical turns are Pignatari's own: in 1961 he announced the Noigandres group's "salto participante" (participant leap), and in 1964 he introduced the "poema-codigo" or "semiotic" poem, publishing Nova Linguagem, Nova Poesia. (C12881, leg 1) The 1964 military dictatorship, which lasted twenty years, upset the group's poetics: they could not just make structural poems any more, concrete rationalism was run over as their utopias shattered. (C12881, leg 1)

(4) Semiotician and teacher, 1968-1994. As a theorist of communication and semiotics, Pignatari translated works of Marshall McLuhan and published Informacao, Linguagem, Comunicacao in 1968; he contributed to disseminating Peirce's semiotics in Brazil. (C12873, leg 1) He was one of the first professors of the post-graduate program in Communication and Semiotics at PUC-SP, helped found the Association Internationale de Semiotique in 1969 (left 1984), and created the Brazilian Association for Semiotics Studies in 1974 according to the IASS-AIS obituary, 1975 according to Delfos -- unresolved. (C12874, leg 1) He began a doctorate on semiotics and literature in 1970, supervised by Antonio Candido, and completed it in 1973; his livre-docencia thesis, Por um Pensamento Iconico, was presented at FAU-USP in 1979. (C12875, leg 1) His teaching posts ran from ESDI Rio in 1961, through UnB (where he organised its Advertising School), Marilia and PUC-SP in 1967, FAU-USP in 1974, retirement from USP in 1994, PUC-SP again in 1997, and the Universidade Tuiuti in Curitiba in 1999. (C12876, leg 1) Alongside all of it he worked as an advertising copywriter and layout planner from 1957, wrote the football column "Terceiro Tempo" for Folha de S. Paulo from 1965, was a television critic for O Estado de S. Paulo from 1978, composed film songs, and acted in films. (C12877, C12878, leg 1)

(5) Late life, 1994-2012. In 1996 the Casa das Rosas in Sao Paulo mounted Desexpl(l)os(ign)cao to mark forty years of the concretist movement; in 2006 MAM-SP reconstructed the first concrete-art exhibition as Concreta '56: A Raiz da Forma. (C12885, leg 1) Two books were published posthumously in 2014, Terceiro Tempo and Viagem Magnetica, and in 2015 the Centro Cultural Sao Paulo mounted the exhibition "Arquivo Decio Pignatari: Um Lance de Dados". (C12885, leg 1) He died in Sao Paulo on Sunday 2 December 2012, aged 85, at 9h in the morning, of respiratory insufficiency and aspiration pneumonia; he had been admitted to the Hospital Universitario da USP on 30 November 2012, and he also suffered from Alzheimer's disease. (C12883, leg 1) No source read gives a burial or funeral detail. (C12883, leg 1)

Honours (confirmed): one only -- Premio Jabuti 1997, Best Translation, for 31 Poetas, 214 Poemas: Do Rigveda e Safo a Apollinaire. (C12882, leg 1) No other prize, honour, medal or academy membership for Pignatari was found in any source read this run; Wikipedia EN lists no awards at all, Wikipedia PT lists only the Jabuti. (C12882, leg 1) This thin honours record is itself a finding: unlike Gomringer (#662, six confirmed honours), Pignatari's recognition in the sources read is essentially the single Jabuti for translation. (C12882, leg 1) Any further honours would need a fresh search; none are claimed here. (C12882, leg 1)

The Avenidas controversy has no Pignatari equivalent in the sources read: his most famous poem was read as an attack on advertising and on American cultural export, not as an attack on a protected group, and it was anthologised rather than painted over. The plainness of his poems never broke into public argument the way Gomringer's did; the argument his work provoked was with the advertising industry, which is to say with the people who paid him. (C13021)

## Evolution: from the word as material to the sign as material to the archive as material

Phase 1, 1950-1952: pre-concrete lyric books. O Carrossel (1950) and Rumo a Nausicaa (1952) precede the concrete turn; no source read describes their contents, and they sit in the table as the baseline the concrete work breaks from. (C12912, C12913, leg 2) The break is dated precisely: the three poets met in 1948, were thinking poetry together by 1949, and paid for Noigandres in 1952, taking the name from Pound's Cantos. (C12941, leg 2)

Phase 2, 1956-1958: the concrete peak, and it is all in the table as A-grade work. In three years come terra, um movimento, beba coca cola, LIFE and the Pilot Plan, plus the First National Exhibition of Concrete Art at the Museum of Modern Art of Sao Paulo in December 1956, which launched the movement publicly, and the 1955 Ulm meeting with Gomringer, which Solt calls the beginning of the international movement. (C12939, leg 2) The poems of this phase are poster poems and kinetic sequences, not books: the unit is the page, the plate, the fold-out. The theory is written at the same time as the poems, not after them.

Phase 3, 1960-1968: regrouping and going beyond the word. Organismo (1960) is a book-poem; Invencao replaces Noigandres; and in 1964 the Semiotic Poem manifesto proposes poems written without words at all, with designed sign sets instead. (C12922, C12928, leg 2) Teoria da Poesia Concreta (1965) collects the 1950-1960 manifestos, which reads as a closing of the first account even as the practice moves on. (C12924, leg 2) Exercicio Findo (1968) is the last poetry book of this phase attested in the bibliography, and Informacao, Linguagem, Comunicacao (1968) opens the theory turn. (C12913, C12914, leg 2)

Phase 4, 1971-1985: the semiotician. Contracomunicacao (1971), Comunicacao Poetica (1977), Semiotica and Literatura (1979), Semiotica da Arte e da Arquitetura (1980), Signagem da Televisao (1984) and Vocogramas (1985) shift the work from making poems to theorizing signs, media and voice; Vocogramas brings the voice back as diagram, the sound of syllables drawn rather than written. (C12914, C12932, leg 2) He taught information theory at the Superior School of Industrial Design in Rio from 1965 through 1976. (C12928, leg 2)

Phase 5, 1986-2000: the biographical turn. O Rosto da Memoria (1986), Panteros (1992) and Errancias (2000) carry the concrete poetics into prose: fragmented short narratives, non-linear structure, photographs interleaved with text, the writer's own body as a photographic sign lent to his characters. The Errancias study argues explicitly that this updates the concrete principle of isomorphism for narrative: as the poem's structure had to correspond to its message in the 1950s and 1960s, so the non-linear, heterogeneous form of these texts corresponds to the non-linear inscription of memory's traces. (C12935, C12936, leg 2) The through-line across all five phases is the same: the material of the poem is widened step by step -- first the word, then the sign, then the voice, then the photograph and the archive -- while the rule stays fixed, that form must correspond to content and the reader must do the work of realizing the piece. (C12924, C12936, leg 2)

## Mechanism M1: phonetic permutation down to the sewer

Work: beba coca cola (W06, 1957). (C12916, leg 2) The viewer sees the Portuguese advertising slogan "beba coca cola" in Coca-Cola red and white, printed in uniform bold letters, and watches it permute line by line: "beba coca cola" becomes "babe cola", "beba coca", "babe cola caco", "caco", "cola", and finally simply "cloaca". (C12918, leg 2) The device is mechanical and minimal: by simply exchanging the position of the vowels in "coca" the poet gets "caco" (shard), and the final damning word "cloaca" (cesspool, filthy place) takes its letters from "coca cola" itself -- the slogan contains its own insult as an anagram. (C12920, leg 2) Solt reads it as an anti-advertisement condemning both the culture that makes and exports Coca-Cola and the culture that drinks it; Tolman calls it a protest poem in which the reader is invited to participate in an experience that begins with a slogan and culminates in an explosion of rage, nausea and expulsion. (C12920, C12926, leg 2) The English translation reverses the colour scheme to red ink on white, and loses the extra senses -- cola as tail as well as glue, caco as thief as well as shard, cloaca as digestive tract as well as sewer pipe -- which is why the poem must be built in the language of the slogan it attacks. (C12918, C12949, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** take a slogan the viewer already knows by heart and permute only its own letters until it insults itself -- never import an outside insult; the filth must already be inside the brand name.

## Mechanism M2: stroke-by-stroke letter architecture across plates

Work: LIFE (W07, 1958). (C12918, leg 2) The viewer turns pages and on each plate finds one large letter -- I, then L, then F, then E -- each simplified to an accumulation of intersecting lines. A new stroke is added at each plate, so the letters grow by construction: the "I" gains a thinner line at its base to become "L", another line is added at the centre and the whole is rotated 180 degrees, a third line makes "E", and a further line encloses the figure in what looks like an eight but is the ancient Chinese calligraphic symbol for the sun. (C12919, leg 2) On the final plate the four letters are rearranged into the word "LIFE", printed as it would appear on the cover of LIFE magazine. (C12919, leg 2) The mechanism is cinematic montage in print: like Eisenstein's cuts and superimpositions, the letters transform by the addition of one line per page, and the reader's page-turning is the motion -- Solt calls it a kinetic or cinepoem, a visual succession based on the architectural structure of the letters. (C12921, leg 2) The found typeface does half the work: Pignatari departs from typical Brazilian bold type to use the familiar commercial lettering of the magazine, and by making semantic use of its constructive principles transforms a word tarnished by mass consumption into an object of radiant beauty. (C12923, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** build one word across pages by adding a single stroke per page, so the reader's turning is the animation -- and end on a found commercial typeface, so the last plate lands as recognition, not invention.

## Mechanism M3: the spiral axis that will not let the words fly off

Work: um movimento (W05, 1956). (C12929, leg 2) The viewer sees words arranged to form a spiral rotating about a vertical axis consisting of a series of "m"s: the word "movimento" is broken across the spiral -- "um", "movi", "mento" -- and as the spiral turns it becomes "momento", "num mo / mento / vivo". (C12929, leg 2) The vertical axis anchors the revolving words and prevents them from flying off the page; the vocabulary is ordinary and there is almost no rhyme, so all the energy is in the rotation. (C12930, leg 2) The poem enacts its own content: it is a kinetic description of its own content, a movement composing a battlefield beyond a cloud, a mirage, the fury of a pure horizon at a vivid moment -- and the second half resolves the scene as a landscape, a vivid sunset, so the movement evoked is that of the artist's hand painting the scene. (C12930, leg 2) Bohn notes the poem is both dynamic and isomorphic: it enacts what it depicts and depicts what it enacts. (C12930, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** spin the words around one fixed vertical spine, and let one word turn into another by rotation alone -- the axis is the discipline; without it the spiral is just scatter.

## Mechanism M4: the diagonal cut as terrain

Work: terra (W04, 1956). (C12931, leg 2) The viewer sees a pattern of words about the earth, spatially organized in neutral type, in which a sinuosity -- a cut running downhill from northwest to southeast -- can be picked out across the page. (C12931, leg 2) The cut is the meaning: it expresses the earth being overturned for planting, its entrails exposed, so that plants will sprout and become food for society. (C12931, leg 2) Solt's reading adds the semantic layer: all the words in the section of the pattern relating to the earth are suggestive of the terror and brevity of life, and the spatially organized typographical word play yields truths about the earth. (C12921, C12923, leg 2) The device is a map that is also a wound: a diagonal line drawn through the word-field turns the page into ploughed ground, and the words sit in the furrow. The full word list of terra was not recovered in this leg, so the mechanism is stated at the level of the cut and the spatial organization, not at the level of individual words. (C12931, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** draw one diagonal cut through the word-field and let it be the plough -- the words must sit in the furrow it makes, so the page reads as ground that has been turned over.

## Mechanism M5: the verbivocovisual triple channel

Works: Plano-Piloto (W08, 1958); LIFE (W07); beba coca cola (W06). (C12924, leg 2) The Pilot Plan defines the concrete poem as creating a specific linguistic area, "verbivocovisual", by using the phonetical system and analogical syntax: the word is treated as three-dimensional -- verbal (its meaning), vocal (its sound), visual (its shape) -- all at once, so the poem shares the advantages of nonverbal communication without giving up the word's virtualities. (C12924, leg 2) In practice this means every element must work on all three channels simultaneously: in LIFE the letters are sound-shapes that build an ideogram; in beba coca cola the permutation is at once a sound change (coca to caco), a visual change (the letters rearrange) and a semantic change (drink to shard to cesspool). (C12918, C12921, leg 2) The Pilot Plan calls the result metacommunication: the coincidence and simultaneity of verbal and nonverbal communication, dealing with a communication of forms, of a structure-content, not with the usual message communication. (C12924, leg 2) The term comes from Joyce's Finnegans Wake, and the Noigandres poets understood it through modern semiotics: the voco and the visual live in equal condition with the verbi, the signified. (C12940, C12941, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** make every element do three jobs at once -- mean, sound and look -- and if a change works on only one channel, it is decoration, not structure.

## Mechanism M6: isomorphism in two stages, with chance on a leash

Works: Plano-Piloto (W08, 1958); terra (W04); um movimento (W05); LIFE (W07). (C12924, leg 2) The Pilot Plan names the central conflict "isomorphism": form and subject looking for identification, with a parallel space-time isomorphism that creates movement. (C12924, leg 2) In a first moment, isomorphism tends to physiognomy, a movement imitating natural appearance: organic form prevails, and the poem looks like what it is about -- terra's cut like ploughed earth, um movimento's spiral like turning motion. (C12924, C12929, C12931, leg 2) In a more advanced stage, isomorphism resolves itself into pure structural movement: geometric form and the mathematics of composition prevail, and the poem moves by rule rather than by resemblance. (C12924, leg 2) Chance is not excluded but controlled: Pignatari speaks of the "chronomicrometering of chance" and "the most lucid intellectual work for the clearest intuition", redefining intuition as a physiological, psycho-cultural perception mechanism at the root of elementary feedback circuits, and the Pilot Plan describes the poem as a mechanism regulating itself, a feed-back loop. (C12925, C12924, leg 2) The composition is built from factors of proximity and likeness: juxtaposed words resonate together and set off refractions of meaning around the columns, a Gestalt the reader must realize. (C12927, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** first make the form imitate the subject, then let the form move by rule alone -- and meter your chance like a chronometer, so every accident is planned before the word is placed.

## Mechanism M7: the wordless poem, or the poet as language designer

Works: Nova linguagem, nova poesia (W11, 1964), with Luiz Angelo Pinto; Semiotic Poems. (C12922, C12928, leg 2) The viewer sees no words at all. Instead the poem is a set of designed visual signs -- traffic signs, diagram languages, computer-programming diagrams, Venn diagrams, mathematical and symbolic-logical languages are all named as legitimate material -- arranged so that their syntax comes from, or is related to, the very form of the signs. (C12922, leg 2) The manifesto defines language as any set of signs and the way of using them, relating them among themselves (syntax) and with referents (semantics) by some interpreting (pragmatics), and proposes the creation of designed and constructed languages according to each situation or necessity. (C12922, leg 2) The poet's job becomes that of a language designer: to design and construct new sets of signs, visual and audible, plus new syntactical and semantical rules for them, because in traditional writing syntax is the same as in spoken language, so we cannot talk about anything except what spoken language allows. (C12922, leg 2) The Semiotic Poems themselves still carry a word definition of the signs, a code key, so the viewer can learn the code and then perceive the meaning as quickly as from a road sign. (C12922, leg 2) The move is dated by Small to Pignatari's turn to non-verbal communication and the nature of signs, in the same years he began teaching information theory. (C12928, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** delete the words entirely and design a sign set whose syntax comes from the shape of the signs themselves -- but always print the code key once, so the viewer can learn the language and then read at road-sign speed.

## Mechanism M8: the commercial letters that eat the ailment

Works: beba coca cola (W06, 1957) as anti-advertisement; the "Disenformio" advertisement, discussed by Tolman. (C12920, C12926, leg 2) The device is the found commercial message turned against itself. In beba coca cola the slogan's own letters produce the insult (M1); in the Disenformio ad -- for an anti-diarrheal medicine -- the letters of the product invade and consume the "intestinal disturbance", so the ad is at once a highly effective commercial message, part of whose effectiveness is subliminal, and a demonstration that what works has artistic value. (C12926, leg 2) Pignatari ran a successful advertising agency in Sao Paulo for a number of years, and one of his ads is usually included in concrete anthologies; the concrete poets treated the use of concrete graphic techniques by ad agencies and television as proof of the validity of their theoretical position, and the concrete poem as analogous in economy to a billboard, a poster or an advertising slogan. (C12926, C12927, leg 2) The mechanism is invasion by the product's own letterforms: the letters do not illustrate the ailment, they occupy it and eat it, and the viewer reads the cure as a typographic event. Tolman notes the same logic in reverse for beba: the reader is invited to participate in an experience that begins with a slogan and culminates in expulsion. (C12926, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** let the product's own letters do the work -- invade the problem with the brand's letterforms until they consume it -- and never illustrate the ailment when the type can eat it instead.

## The aesthetic logic: the poem as a designed thing that the reader must finish

Pignatari's own programmatic claim, recorded by Bauhaus Imaginista, is the key to the whole career: "Our century is the century of planning, of design and designers... writers, poets, journalists, advertisers, musicians, photographers, filmmakers, radio and television producers, painters and sculptors are beginning to become aware of designers, forgers of new languages." (C12887, leg 1) Sarduy's definition of the movement Pignatari co-founded says the same thing from the other side: a concrete poem is akin to a diagram or drawing, a pattern on the printed page representing not only objects or concepts but the relationships between them, and the poet becomes a "designer" of meaning, dismantling words so we can see what they are made of. (C12886, leg 1)

Three rules follow from that claim, and every mechanism above is an instance of one of them. First, the word is a material, not a messenger: it has a sound, a shape and a meaning, and a poem that uses only one of the three is unfinished work (M5). Second, form must correspond to content, and the correspondence must be earned twice -- first by resemblance, then by rule (M6): terra looks like ploughed ground and then moves like a rule; um movimento looks like turning and then turns by rotation. Third, the reader is not an audience but a co-operator: the constellation is "an invitation" and "a play-area of fixed dimensions" in Gomringer's words, and in Pignatari's practice the reader permutes the slogan, turns the plates, learns the code key, and only then does the poem happen. The Gestalt the reader must realize is not a bonus; it is the poem.

That is also why the career widens the way it does. Once the word is a material, the next material is the sign (M7: traffic signs, Venn diagrams, code), then the voice (Vocogramas: the sound of syllables drawn rather than written), then the photograph and the archive (Panteros, Errancias: the writer's own body as a photographic sign, memory as non-linear inscription). The rule never changes -- form corresponds to content, the reader does the work -- only the material changes. And it is why the advertising career is not a side job but the proof: the concrete poem is analogous in economy to a billboard, a poster or an advertising slogan, and an ad that works by its own letterforms is, in Pignatari's account, art that works. (C12926, C12927, leg 2) The insult in beba coca cola lands because the slogan is real, the red is real, and the letters are the brand's own. A made-up brand could never be insulted; it has nothing to lose.

The contrast with Gomringer (#662) is the contrast between a poet who stayed inside the constellation and a poet who kept leaving it. Gomringer's poems stay almost absurdly small -- a few words pinned to a page -- and his career widened sideways into design, industry, teaching and the museum while the poems stayed put. Pignatari's poems also start small -- terra, um movimento, beba coca cola, LIFE are all single-page or few-plate works -- but his practice kept widening the material itself, until the poem is a sign system, a voice diagram, a novel with photographs, a memoir in album format. Gomringer named the form; Pignatari tested how far the form's rule could travel before it stopped being a poem. The answer, on the evidence of Errancias, is that it travels all the way into prose and photography, as long as the form still corresponds to its content and the reader still has to do the work. (C13022)

## The network: the Ulm meeting and what it did not produce

The network runs through one meeting and three co-founders, and the honest account has to say what is not evidenced as clearly as what is.

Eugen Gomringer. The meeting is verified item by item (leg 3, G1-G7). In 1955 Pignatari visited the Hochschule fur Gestaltung at Ulm and met Gomringer, then secretary to Max Bill (Solt; Augusto de Campos 2008; Hilder 2010 treats 1955 as consensus). (C12963, leg 3) Solt's sentence is verbatim: "This meeting of mutual interest and surprise can be taken as the beginning of the international movement of concrete poetry." (C12965, leg 3) When Pignatari returned to Brazil in 1956, he and Gomringer were planning an international anthology; it was never published, though Solt believed its introduction was Gomringer's essay "Concrete Poetry". (C12966, leg 3) Gomringer agreed in a letter to Pignatari dated 30 August -- the year is Solt's summary's, 1955, with Noel-Tod reading the agreement as settled in 1956, reported as compatible rather than contradictory -- that the whole anthology could well be named "poesie concrete", having himself thought of calling his poems "concrete" before settling on "constellations". (C12967, leg 3) Who introduced them is unresolved: Sarduy says Tomas Maldonado; Augusto says Alexandre Wollner; the Bauhaus Imaginista article names no introducer. (C12968, leg 3) What followed is documented: an intense exchange of letters, a convergence toward simplification in the orthodox phase 1956 to the end of the decade, shared publication in Spirale 6/7 (1958) and the 1959 Stuttgart "Konkrete Texte" catalogue, and Pignatari stopping in Portugal on his return to speak about the concrete poem. (C12969, leg 3) No source read gives the length of the Ulm stay, the exact day of the meeting, or any second meeting between the two men after 1955-1956. (C12970, leg 3)

Augusto de Campos (born 1931). Met Pignatari in 1948 with his brother Haroldo; "the following year the three of us were already thinking of poetry together"; in 1952 they paid for the publishing of Noigandres. (C12971, leg 3) The three signed the Pilot Plan in 1958 and collected their writings as Teoria da Poesia Concreta in 1965. (C12971, leg 3) Augusto is the primary witness for the Ulm meeting and for the group's later divergence, and describes the founding group as a "team spirit, a communion of ideas and an individual detachment". (C12971, leg 3) No source read shows any break or quarrel between Augusto and Pignatari; the divergence Augusto describes is aesthetic, not personal. (C12971, leg 3) Augusto is the top pick for study #664.

Haroldo de Campos (1929-2003). The third Noigandres founder, described by Sarduy as "the Pound-like patriarch of the movement and its chief theorist"; co-signed the Pilot Plan and co-authored Teoria, and co-edited all five numbers of Noigandres. (C12972, leg 3) His distinct contribution was geographic: in 1957 he introduced concrete poetry to Kitasono Katue in Japan, and the poets of the magazine Vou followed. (C12972, leg 3) No source read shows Haroldo meeting Gomringer himself; his contact with the German side ran through Pignatari's Ulm meeting and, later, through his own long correspondence with Max Bense. (C12972, leg 3) Haroldo is held for a later study, not #664, so the two Campos brothers are not studied back to back.

Three further adjacents with documented Pignatari-specific interaction. Max Bense (1910-1990), German philosopher of information aesthetics, staged the exhibition "konkrete texte" at the Studiengalerie Stuttgart in winter 1959/60, whose catalogue lists the Noigandres group programme; in 1962 Bense and Elisabeth Walther published the anthology "Noigandres / Konkrete Texte"; Bense visited Brazil five times from 1961 and taught courses at ESDI in the early 1960s while Pignatari worked there as professor of information theory -- the same school, the same field, the same years, though no source states the two men met in person at ESDI, and the long Bense correspondence documented in the sources is Haroldo's, not Decio's. (C12973, leg 3) Tomas Maldonado (1922-2018), Argentine theorist and Ulm professor, is named by Sarduy as the man who introduced Gomringer to Pignatari in 1955; independently, he wrote the curriculum proposal for a design school at the Museum of Modern Art in Rio and co-taught a visual communication course there, and the early-1960s ESDI curriculum referred to his plan -- but no source read places Maldonado and Pignatari in the same room, and the introduction claim rests on Sarduy alone. (C12974, leg 3) Alexandre Wollner (1928-2018), Brazilian graphic designer, is named by Augusto as the channel for the Ulm meeting; he studied at Ulm 1954-1958, invited by Max Bill himself, and later co-founded ESDI -- but no source read quotes Wollner himself on the introduction, and Sarduy credits Maldonado instead. (C12975, leg 3)

Shared publications only, no meeting evidenced. Max Bill: his 1951 first-place award at the first Sao Paulo Biennial had a significant impact on the formation of the Brazilian concrete movement, and Bill invited Wollner to Ulm; Pignatari met Gomringer while Gomringer was Bill's secretary -- but no source read states Pignatari ever met Bill, spoke to him, or corresponded with him. (C12976, leg 3) Dieter Roth: no documented direct interaction with Pignatari was found this run; the two appear together only in shared listings, Emmett Williams's An Anthology of Concrete Poetry (1967) and the 1961 Wuppertal exhibition; Roth co-founded Spirale with Gomringer in 1953, but no source links Roth to Pignatari beyond co-publication, and a meeting must not be claimed.
