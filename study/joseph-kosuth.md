Joseph Kosuth was born on 31 January 1945 in Toledo, Ohio, the son of an American mother and a Hungarian father from an old noble family; a relative, Lajos Kossuth, led Hungary's struggle for independence from Austria during the Revolution of 1848, and the family name is spelled Kossuth in Hungarian, Kosuth with one s in his own surname. He began art studies at ten, attending the Toledo Museum School of Design from 1955 to 1962 and studying privately under the Belgian painter Line Bloom Draper; in 1963 he enrolled at the Cleveland Institute of Art on a scholarship for a year, then spent a year in Paris and travelled through Europe and North Africa. He moved to New York in 1965 and attended the School of Visual Arts until 1967, studying painting; while still technically a student he made such an impact that in 1967 Silas Rhodes, the school's founder and president, removed him from the student body and gave him a teaching position, a move that reportedly caused a near-revolt among faculty who found him disruptive. He remained on the School of Visual Arts fine-art faculty until 1985. [C28307]

In 1965, at twenty, Kosuth created One and Three Chairs -- an actual chair, a photograph of that chair, and an enlarged dictionary definition of the word "chair" -- and in 1966 he embarked on First Investigations, subtitled Art as Idea as Idea, enlarged photostats of dictionary definitions of words such as "water", "meaning" and "idea", mounted like paintings, which eliminated objects and images entirely; the series title was inspired by Ad Reinhardt's 1958 remark that "art is art as art and everything else is everything else". In 1967, while still a student, he co-founded the Museum of Normal Art in New York with Christine Kozlov, an exhibition space that gave first exposure to artists including Robert Ryman, On Kawara and Hanne Darboven, and he curated Fifteen People Present Their Favorite Book at the Lannis Gallery, assembling Robert Morris, Ad Reinhardt, Sol LeWitt, Robert Mangold, Dan Graham, Robert Smithson, Carl Andre and Robert Ryman, among others. His seminal text "Art after Philosophy", written in 1968-69 and published in three parts in Studio International in 1969, argued that art is the continuation of philosophy, that the change from appearance to conception beginning with Duchamp's first unassisted readymade was the beginning of both modern art and conceptual art, and that conceptual works are analytic propositions, linguistic in character and therefore tautological; it has been described as a manifesto of Conceptual art, translated into 14 languages, and was in part an attack on Greenbergian formalism. [C28308]

In 1969 Kosuth became the American editor of the journal Art-Language, joining the British-based Conceptual group Art and Language (founded 1968 by Terry Atkinson, Michael Baldwin, David Bainbridge and Harold Hurrell; Kosuth joined with Ian Burn and Mel Ramsden in 1969); he stayed with the group until 1976, leaving after differences over what was to be published and over some artists, including himself, becoming well known independently of the group. In 1975, with the New York Art and Language circle frustrated by Michael Baldwin's direction of Art-Language from the UK, he co-founded the journal The Fox with Sarah Charlesworth, a politically oriented New York publishing vehicle that ran for three issues in 1975-76 amid constant squabbling and divided loyalties between New York and the UK. From 1971 he studied anthropology and philosophy with Stanley Diamond and Bob Scholte at the New School for Social Research (1971-72), concerned, as he put it, with his "ethnocentricity as a white, male artist"; he did fieldwork in the Peruvian Amazon with the Yagua (Huallaga) Indians, in the Trobriand Islands in the South Pacific, and in Australia, some hundreds of kilometres north of Alice Springs, with an Aboriginal tribe that had not previously known of the existence of white people, saying he knew he could never enter their cultural reality but wanted "to experience the edge of my own". The experience led to his text "The Artist as Anthropologist" (1975). [C28309]

Kosuth taught widely: School of Visual Arts, New York (1967-85); Hochschule fuer Bildende Kuenste, Hamburg (1988-90); State Academy of Fine Arts Stuttgart (1991-97); Academy of Fine Arts, Munich (2001-06); professor at the Istituto Universitario di Architettura (IUAV), Venice, from 2007. His first solo exhibition was at the Leo Castelli Gallery, New York, in 1969; the same year he organised Fifteen Locations, an exhibition of his work shown simultaneously at fifteen museums and galleries worldwide; a major retrospective was presented by the Kunstmuseum Luzern in 1973 and travelled in Europe, and another by the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart and the Kunsthalle Bielefeld in 1981. By the time he was twenty-five he had already had twenty-two solo exhibitions, and his career total is given as over 170 one-person exhibitions in museums and galleries worldwide. He exhibited at documenta VI (1977), VII (1982) and IX (1992) in Kassel, on the rosters read end to end; documenta V (1972) is claimed by his bio sources (Sprueth Magers; Wikipedia) but he does NOT appear in the Documenta 5 participant roster as read, so no documenta 5 bill is asserted for him anywhere in this study. His Venice Biennale appearances are enumerated as 1976, 1993, 1999 and 2007, with the prose counts in his bios running higher and flagged as differently counted (see "What is NOT settled"). [C28310]

Since 1990 Kosuth has worked on permanent public commissions, including a government-sponsored monument to the Egyptologist Jean-Francois Champollion, decipherer of the Rosetta Stone, at the Place des Ecritures in Figeac, France (1991); neon monuments to Walter Benjamin in Frankfurt and Columbus, Ohio; Words of a Spell, a 136-foot mural for Tachikawa, Japan (1994); and works at the Deutsche Bundesbank (1997), the Parliament House in Stockholm (1998), the Parliament of the Brussels-Capital Region (1999) and the Paul Loebe Haus of the Bundestag in Berlin (2001). In 2003 he created three installations at the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston using text, archival material and objects from the collection; in 2007 he exhibited The Language of Equilibrium on the island of San Lazzaro degli Armeni for the Venice Biennale; and in 2009 he installed Neither Appearance Nor Illusion (ni apparence ni illusion) in the medieval walls of the Louvre Palace, Paris, which became a permanent work (the year is disputed: 2012, 2014 and 2016 are all given). He has also worked as a curator and organiser: in 1989 he and Peter Pakesch founded The Foundation for the Arts as part of the Sigmund Freud Museum, Vienna, of which he is president; in 1989 he curated the exhibition Ludwig Wittgenstein: Das Spiel des Unsagbaren (The Play of the Unsayable) for Wittgenstein's centenary, shown at the Wiener Secession and the Palais des Beaux-Arts, Brussels; and in 1990, responding to conservative attacks on the National Endowment for the Arts, he organised A Play of the Unmentionable at the Brooklyn Museum, juxtaposing works from the permanent collection with texts by writers, philosophers and critics. He designed the album cover for John Cale's Fragments of a Rainy Season (1992) and collaborated with Ilya Kabakov on The Corridor of Two Banalities, shown at the Centre for Contemporary Art in Warsaw in 1994. His awards include the Cassandra Foundation Grant (1968), awarded at 23 as the choice of Marcel Duchamp one week before Duchamp died; the Brandeis Award (1990); the Frederick Weisman Award (1991); the Menzione d'Onore at the Venice Biennale and Chevalier de l'ordre des Arts et des Lettres from the French government (both 1993); a 3-franc French postage stamp issued in his honour in Figeac (1999); the Laurea Honoris Causa from the University of Bologna (2001); the Austrian Republic's Decoration of Honour in Gold (2003); election to la classe des Arts of the Academie Royale of Belgium (2012); an Honoris Causa doctorate from the Instituto Superior de Arte, University of Havana (2015); the European Cultural Centre Art Award (2017); the National Arts Club Medal of Honor (2022); and an honorary degree from the Accademia Albertina di Belle Arti, Turin (2025). [C28311]

Recent activity confirms a working artist in his eighties: solo shows in 2025 included The Question at Almine Rech Paris, Lia Rumma Naples and Sprueth Magers London, Nothing but memory at Kunstmuseum Stuttgart, and Future Memory at Sean Kelly New York; in 2026 he showed I Shall Offer It To You As A Ready Made Product at Castelli Gallery, New York, and The-exchange-value-of-language-has-fallen-to-zero at Casa dei Tre Oci, Venice (28 March-22 November 2026), a survey with a newly commissioned neon installation, A Chain of Resemblance. In September 2026 he was interviewed for BOMB Magazine by his daughters Klio and Noema Kosuth, co-directors of the Joseph Kosuth Foundation. He is living, aged 81. [C28312]

The twenty works inspected run 1965-2022 and trace one practice shedding its objects until only language is left. Early, 1965-1970: the work is a small, wall-sized assembly of a found object, a photograph and a printed definition, or a neon sign spelling its own title, and the audience is a reader standing still and thinking through a logical proposition -- Leaning Glass (W01, 1965), One and Three Chairs (W02, 1965), Clock (One and Five) (W03, 1965), One and Three Photographs [Ety.] (W04, conceived 1965), One and Three Shovels (W05, 1965), Five Words in Orange Neon (W06, 1965), Five Words in Green Neon (W07, 1965), Five Words and Five Colors (W08, 1965), Neon (Self-Defined) (W09, 1965) and Glass (Words) (W10, c. 1965-70). Middle, 1966-1993: the object drops away and language stands alone, first as dictionary definitions on photostats, then as appropriated quotations set against museum collections and public squares, and the audience becomes a walker moving through arguments and across a text laid into paving -- Titled (Art as Idea as Idea) [Water] (W11, 1966), [Meaning] (W12, 1967), [Nothing] (W13, 1967), The Play of the Unmentionable (W14, 1990), Ex Libris J.-F. Champollion (W15, 1990-91) and Zeno at the Edge of the Known World (W16, 1993). Recent, 2004-2022: the text becomes architecture itself, 86 glass panels of philosophy to walk through, 15 neon sentences on medieval stone, 136 glowing mine names in a civic atrium, clocks that quote philosophers while they tick, and the audience is a citizen or passer-by who encounters the work in the course of ordinary public life -- A Propos (Reflecteur de Reflecteur) (W17, 2004), ni apparence ni illusion (W18, 2009), A Monument of Mines (W19, 2015) and Quoted Clocks (W20, 2022). Grades: A 15, B 5, C 0 -- the fifteen A grades are W01, W02, W04, W06, W07, W08, W09, W11, W12, W14, W15, W17, W18, W19 and W20; the five B grades are W03, W05, W10, W13 and W16. ## M1 -- The triad: object, image, word

One object is shown three ways at once, as the thing itself, as a photograph of it, and as its dictionary definition, and the three representations disagree about what the thing is. Kosuth did not make the chair, take the photograph, or write the definition; he selected and assembled the three, so the viewer is asked which of the three is most "accurate" and whether all three are chairs, or codes for one: a visual code, a verbal code, and a code in the language of objects. The watchable behaviour is a visitor's eyes shuttling between the three, checking each against the others and finding no single one that is the chair, the clock, or the photograph. Seen in W02, W03, W04 and W05, and in the Leaning Glass variant (W01), where the object is reduced to any sheet of glass leaned against any wall and the instruction is the work.

**Takeaway:** show the thing three ways at once and let the three disagree; the disagreement is the work. [C28314]

## M2 -- The self-describing neon tautology

A neon sign spells out exactly what it is, in the material it names, so the description and the object are the same thing and there is nothing left to interpret. "FIVE WORDS IN ORANGE NEON" is five words in orange neon; "neon" in white tubing is the word neon in neon. The transformer and wiring are left visible and the artist's hand is deliberately diminished, because neon was neutral and legible for Kosuth, a material borrowed from commercial advertising and stripped of its promotional function, so the glow reads as public writing rather than decoration. The watchable behaviour is instant verification: the viewer reads the sign, looks at the sign, and the claim checks out, which is the whole event. Seen in W06, W07, W08 and W09.

**Takeaway:** make the sign describe itself in its own material; the verification is the experience. [C28315]

## M3 -- The definition as the work, the presentation as disposable

The dictionary definition is the artwork and the photostat that carries it is only a form of presentation, explicitly throwable away and remade. Kosuth insisted the photostats could be thrown away and remade, since the art is in the idea, not in the printed sheet. The viewer reads a definition of something that has no shape or colour -- water, formless and colourless -- and the words must do all the work the object cannot; with [Meaning], the dictionary is asked to define the very thing a definition is supposed to deliver, and the definition refers the question back to language itself, with no object left to point at. The watchable behaviour is reading as viewing: the eye moves across dense small print on a wall the way it would across a painting, and the work survives its own destruction because the idea can be reprinted. Seen in W11, W12 and W13.

**Takeaway:** print the definition, then throw the print away; the idea is the work and the paper is not. [C28316]

## M4 -- Quoted text as architecture

Appropriated sentences are set into walls, floors, glass panels and stone, at a scale the visitor walks through rather than reads at a desk. In A Propos, 86 quotations from dozens of philosophers are fabricated as vinyl letters on 289 glass panels and backlit in neon, arranged with little rhyme or reason to their placement, so philosophy appears as a dialogue the visitor moves inside; in the Louvre, 15 neon texts sit directly on twelfth-century stonework; in Figeac, the Rosetta Stone's face is laid flat as paving underfoot; in Kongsberg, 136 mine names glow in the silver they once produced. The watchable behaviour is reading in motion: the visitor turns, descends, or crosses a square to follow a sentence, and the building's history and the text's argument annotate each other. Seen in W15, W17, W18 and W19.

**Takeaway:** set the sentence into the building at walking scale; the visitor reads by moving. [C28317]

## M5 -- The dictionary as found material: authorities that disagree

The dictionary is treated as a found object, and found objects disagree with each other. [Nothing] took the word "nothing" from a dozen different dictionaries, so twelve definitions of nothing hang as twelve slightly different answers to the same question, and the viewer compares them and finds that even nothing is defined differently depending on who is defining it. The series moves from abstractions of particulars, like "Water", to abstractions of abstractions, like "Meaning", so the authority the work leans on is shown to be a set of competing voices, not a single verdict. The watchable behaviour is comparison: the eye jumps from one definition to the next, hunting for the difference, and the difference is the point. Seen in W11, W12 and W13.

**Takeaway:** quote the authority twelve times and let it contradict itself; the disagreement is the definition. [C28318]

## M6 -- The clock that quotes: empirical time against quoted time

A working clock is shown with photographs and printed definitions, or with a quotation in vinyl lettering, so the clock keeps real time while the words fix it in language and the two disagree about what a clock is: a machine that measures, an image of a machine, or a word. In Quoted Clocks, each standard analog clock carries a quotation from a familiar or obscure writer or philosopher, so the viewer reads about duration while watching duration happen, and time is contained in a clock no more than meaning is contained in a single object. The watchable behaviour is double attention: the eye checks the hands against the sentence, and the sentence changes what the ticking means. Seen in W03 and W20.

**Takeaway:** put a quotation on the clock face and let the ticking argue with it. [C28319]

## M7 -- The museum collection as material: the show that curates itself

The museum's own holdings become the work's objects, and quotations are set against them. In The Play of the Unmentionable, the Brooklyn Museum's Grand Lobby filled with objects drawn from its permanent collection -- ancient Egyptian artifacts, Renaissance religious paintings, Japanese prints, Bauhaus furniture, contemporary photographs by Larry Clark and Robert Mapplethorpe, each chosen because it had at some point been deemed politically, religiously, or sexually objectionable -- alongside quotations emblazoned across the walls in various sizes of type, from Rousseau and Oscar Wilde to Hitler and Goebbels on Nazi cultural policy. The show was seen by over 90,000 people, and its aim, in the catalogue essayist's words, was to engender self-reflexiveness in each viewer's judgment about the relations between art, morality, and censorship. The watchable behaviour is judgment in public: the visitor stands between an object the museum owns and a sentence the museum painted, and has to decide for themselves. Seen in W14, and in the Gardner Museum installations of 2003, where text, archival material and objects from the collection were combined the same way.

**Takeaway:** curate the museum's own objects against sentences it painted on its own walls; the visitor does the judging. [C28320]

## M8 -- The monument as text: a square you walk across, names that glow

A public square becomes a text the visitor walks across, and dead names glow in the metal they once produced. In Figeac, the ground of the Place des Ecritures is covered by an immense reproduction of the face of the Rosetta Stone, laid flat as paving at the foot of the Musee Champollion, with a terraced garden of papyrus, tamaris and aromatic plants beside it; the same statement the stone carries three times, in hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek, is here carried once more, at architectural scale, underfoot. In Kongsberg, 136 neon elements set into the walls of a civic atrium each carry the name of one of the silver mines that once operated in the area together with the year that mine closed, stencil-like glass rectangles covered in silver leaf and illuminated from behind by white neon, so a passer-by reads an industrial obituary as architecture. The watchable behaviour is reading underfoot and overhead in the course of ordinary public life: the monument is not visited, it is crossed. Seen in W15 and W19.

**Takeaway:** lay the text in the paving and the names in the wall; the monument is read by walking, not by visiting. [C28321]

## M9 -- The labyrinth of arguments: philosophy you walk through

Eighty-six quotations from dozens of philosophers, fabricated as vinyl letters on 289 glass panels and backlit in neon, arranged in horizontal and vertical patterns with little rhyme or reason to their placement, form a monumental labyrinth the visitor walks through rather than reads in sequence. One text by Claude Levi-Strauss reads "Marx's and Freud's combined lesson: they have taught us that man has meaning only on the condition that he view himself as meaningful." The visitor turns corners into arguments from different eras that were never meant to meet, so philosophy stops being a text read in order and becomes a space navigated by the body. The watchable behaviour is getting lost in an argument: the eye catches a sentence, the feet follow the panels, and the next turn contradicts the last. Seen in W17.

**Takeaway:** build the argument as a maze and let the visitor get lost in it. [C28322]

## M10 -- The first-person turn: the artist quotes himself

For the first time since 1979, Kosuth appropriated his own writing for a work, adopting the first-person pronoun and speaking to the viewer in his own voice, in French, in a text contemplating Nietzsche's reversal of the metaphysical paradigm, which repositioned appearance, rather than truth or being, as the fundamental condition of existential experience. Fifteen neon texts mounted directly on the stone walls of the medieval Louvre Palace, first opened as a temporary installation in the ancient moats of the Sully Wing in October 2009 and later made permanent in the Charles V Hall, put the artist's own sentences in glowing first person against twelfth-century stonework, with a millennium of art and history sitting directly above. The watchable behaviour is being addressed: the visitor descends into old stone and is spoken to, in the first person, by the work itself. Seen in W18.

**Takeaway:** after sixty years of quoting others, quote yourself, in the first person, on the oldest wall you can find. [C28323]

## Aesthetic logic

Kosuth's logic is language as the only material that cannot be separated from meaning: an object can be photographed, a photograph can be defined, a definition can be printed, a print can be thrown away, and at every step the work survives because the idea was never in the thing. The materials are deliberately borrowed and everyday -- the dictionary, the neon sign, the photostat, the museum's own collection, the paving of a public square -- and the forms are deliberately simple: a chair, a clock, a sheet of glass, a definition, a sentence on a wall. What changes across sixty years is not the question but the scale at which it is asked: in the 1960s the work asks it of a reader alone with a definition on a wall; in the 1990s of a walker crossing a square that is a text, or standing in a museum lobby between objects and quotations; from the 2000s on of a citizen passing through a building whose walls are sentences. Against Graham's word become a room that watches you back -- a mirror, a camera, a hedge doing the describing -- Kosuth's answer is the word that refuses to become anything else: no object to read, only the definition, the tautology, the quotation, set where it cannot be ignored, in neon, in stone, underfoot. The work is analytic in his own sense, a proposition true by its own form, and the viewer verifies it the way one verifies a sentence: by reading it, checking it against what is there, and finding that it holds. [C28324]
