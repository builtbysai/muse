# Enzo G. Castellari: Study Notes

**Site:** Enzo G. Castellari (b. Friday 29 July 1938, Rome, Italy; living), Italian film director, screenwriter and actor, born Enzo Girolami -- Castellari is his mother's surname, the G. stands for Girolami -- the kinetic-action stylist of Italian genre cinema across five decades: the spaghetti westerns, the poliziotteschi, the macaroni war film, the post-apocalyptic trilogy, and the shark film that a United States federal judge pulled from theatres. His practice is the remix with a signature: one visual grammar -- slow motion, the opening montage, the specialist team, the song-score -- run through whatever hit somebody else had last year, until the remix becomes the style and the style gets remixed back by Tarantino. [C30045]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Guido & Maurizio De Angelis study: the brothers end their study with a song that is a durable object -- a title song that is the film, a chorus that comments on the silent hero, a theme driven through a whole crime score -- and the man who gave those songs their pictures is the director who hired them nine times in nine years. Where the brothers bring the song that is sung, Castellari brings the image that is slowed: the car chase through Genoa that is the plot's argument, the gunfighter walking through his own past, the shark that is a pretext for a month in the sun. The study starts where theirs leaves off -- with the other half of the Italian genre shelf, the director whose films are the reason the brothers' themes have pictures attached to them, and who is still being honoured for them, in person, in 2025. [C30046]

## Corrections to the brief

Three errors in the material handed to this study, all corrected before any claim was built on them. First, *The Seven Vipers* (1964) is NOT a Castellari film: *Le sette vipere* was directed by Renato Polselli, a comedy about Argentine and Italian marriage law, and appears in no Castellari filmography; his credited directorial debut is *Renegade Riders* (1967), substituted as W01. Second, *The Two Missionaries* is NOT a Castellari film: *Porgi l'altra guancia* (1974) was directed by Franco Rossi and stars Terence Hill and Bud Spencer; the Castellari film meant is *Te Deum* (1972), released as *Father Jackleg*, *Sting of the West* and *Con Men*, used as W04. Third, *Road to Hell* / *Tu crois?* (2016) could NOT be verified as a Castellari film: a single blu-ray.com filmography listing is the only source, with no review, plot, cast or production record, and the other *Road to Hell* films found are Albert Pyun's (2008) and an unrelated Italian 2018 film; W20 is graded B and no study claim is built on it. A fourth correction reaches back to #755: the #755 study and its leg 3 claimed Franco Nero starred in Martino's *Mannaja* -- he did not; *Mannaja* (13 August 1977) stars Maurizio Merli, and the Keoma-derivative reading stands on the shared composer and the shared actor Donald O'Brien, not on Nero. And a fifth, from leg 3: the Tarantino-*Keoma* influence must not be asserted -- The Skinny calls Tarantino "a very big fan" of *Keoma* with no Tarantino quote attached, and Tarantino's own three favourite westerns (Rio Bravo, The Good the Bad and the Ugly, One-Eyed Jacks) exclude *Keoma*; the evidenced Tarantino links are the title nod, the cameos, the Death Proof music reuse and the 2008 conversation. [C30049]

## Works

**W01 -- Renegade Riders (*Sette winchester per un massacro* / *Payment in Blood*), 1967.** Spaghetti western. Grade A. The debut as a deliberate style exercise: shot in Spain, Castellari set out to duplicate the look of Sidney J. Furie's *The Appaloosa* (1966) -- in his own words at USC, he was struck by Furie's use of the Techniscope format, "its big, wide rectangular shape". The mechanism is already visible: a widescreen frame used as an action stage, not a landscape postcard. Roberto Curti calls it the official debut, the first film Castellari was credited as director on after uncredited work on *Few Dollars for Django* (1966); credited as E.G. Rowland. Evidence: Wikipedia "Renegade Riders"; FDTimes USC interview; Wikipedia "Enzo G. Castellari".

**W02 -- Any Gun Can Play (*Vado... l'ammazzo e torno*), 1967.** Spaghetti western, comic. Grade A. The three-man treasure-hunt engine, played with a wink: a bounty hunter (George Hilton), a Mexican bandit (Gilbert Roland) and a crooked banker (Edd Byrnes) double-cross each other almost constantly over a hidden gold stash, openly borrowing the *Good, the Bad and the Ugly* premise. The opening is the mechanism in miniature: three Leone-lookalike gunmen ride into town, doors and windows slam shut, and a gravedigger carts three coffins out of town -- an in-joke that tells the audience the film knows its genre. Castellari claimed in interview that this film and its loose sequel mark the beginning of the comedy spaghetti western, before Barboni's Trinity formula. The action is over-elaborated on purpose -- a marketplace chase ends with Byrnes escaping on trampolines -- pace over plausibility. Evidence: Wikipedia "Any Gun Can Play"; Spaghetti Western Database review.

**W03 -- Kill Them All and Come Back Alone (*Ammazzali tutti e torna solo*), 1968.** Spaghetti western / macaroni combat. Grade A. The specialist-team action machine, perfected: a Confederate agent (Chuck Connors) assembles a Dirty Dozen-style team -- each member an expert in a different kind of violence (knife, explosives, acrobatics) -- to raid a Union hilltop fortress for a million dollars in gold. The cast is almost entirely stuntmen, and the film is "fighting, shooting and explosions all the way". The signature move: Castellari pauses the frantic action to lift the camera high, letting the viewer read the fortress space and the team's progress through it -- geography as a scoreboard. Notably the fortress is taken early -- the gold is secured well before the finale, and the film is about *keeping* it, a structural twist on the heist. Evidence: Spaghetti Western Database review; DVD Talk Savant review.

**W04 -- Sting of the West (*Te Deum*, aka *Father Jackleg* / *Con Men*), 1972.** Spaghetti western comedy. Grade A. The con-man ensemble, built on character names as jokes: Jack Palance plays Buck Santini, a con man who inherits a supposedly worthless gold mine and teams with Giancarlo Prete's Tedeum to sell it to unsuspecting marks -- only for the mine to turn out real, forcing the swindlers to recover the deed. The supporting cast carries gag names (Stinky Manure, Ma Manure, Rags Manure). Mechanically this is the *Any Gun Can Play* comic-western line carried forward: the double-cross is now a sales pitch, and the action set-pieces are slapstick routines rather than gunfights. Score by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis -- the first De Angelis collaboration in this list, a partnership that will define the next decade. Evidence: Wikipedia "Sting of the West"; blu-ray.com "Father Jackleg (1972)".

**W05 -- High Crime (*La polizia incrimina, la legge assolve*), 1973.** Poliziottesco. Grade A. The opening chase as thesis statement, then the genre template: the film opens with a bone-crunching, roughly ten-minute car chase through Genoa's cobbled streets, then settles into a *French Connection* echo -- Commissioner Belli (Franco Nero) against the Lebanon-Marseilles-Genoa heroin route, with Fernando Rey imported from Friedkin's film. Castellari avoids Friedkin's jittery verite and instead stages action with the deliberation of *Bullitt* and Peckinpah's *The Getaway*. The mechanism that matters: the chase is not a set-piece inserted into a plot, it *is* the plot's argument -- the law can only move at speed, and speed is where the film lives. A commercial success that "changed the trajectory of its genre, influencing countless imitators". A factory-union strike backdrop runs alongside the mob war, giving the action a political hum Castellari otherwise avoids. First of seven Castellari-Nero features. Score by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis. Evidence: Genre Grinder 4K review; Furious Cinema; Wikipedia "Enzo G. Castellari". [C30050]

**W06 -- Street Law (*Il cittadino si ribella*), 1974.** Poliziottesco / vigilante film. Grade A. The ten-minute opening montage as a civic indictment, then the incompetent vigilante: like *The Big Racket* after it, the film opens with a blistering montage of gang violence across Genoa -- robberies and shootings in the streets. Then the turn: ordinary engineer Carlo Antonelli (Nero), robbed, kidnapped and beaten during a bank robbery, decides to take justice into his own hands -- and is *bad at it*. He is vulnerable, scared, repeatedly beaten and dragged through the mud; Nero did many of his own stunts, which sells the realism. The mechanism is the anti-*Death Wish*: made the same year, but the citizen-hero is no Bronson -- he needs a blackmailed young criminal (Giancarlo Prete as Tommy) to function at all, and a genuine affection develops between them. Shot on location in Genoa; Castellari shot the opening sequence a little each day without pay or permit, by agreement with the stuntmen, after the producer refused the budget. Grossed 1.723 billion lire in Italy and "paved the way for the most critically panned subgenre of poliziotteschi, the vigilante film". Score by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis. Evidence: Wikipedia "Street Law (film)"; Mondo Digital; City on Fire.

**W07 -- Cry, Onion! (*Cipolla Colt*), 1975.** Spaghetti western comedy / live-action cartoon. Grade A. The same grammar, pushed until it breaks into slapstick: one year before *Keoma*, Castellari and Nero killed the western a different way -- Nero plays Onion Stark, a man obsessed with onions (he eats them raw like apples, drinks them, sleeps under them, weaponises them), who wants to start an onion farm on land coveted by oil baron Petrus Lamb (Martin Balsam), whose prosthetic hand is made of gold and serviced by a henchman who may or may not be Hitler. His sidekick is a talking horse in a cowboy hat. The set-pieces are sped-up Keystone Cops routines and silent-film tributes; the film "plays out like a live-action cartoon". The mechanism is saturation: every gag in the comic-western line (W02, W04) is turned up until the genre itself is the joke. Music again by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis -- this credit rests on the duo's filmography table alone this run (flagged). Released 25 August 1975. Evidence: Wikipedia "Cry, Onion!"; Letterboxd reviews.

**W08 -- Keoma, 1976.** Spaghetti western, twilight / revisionist. Grade A. Slow motion plus song-score plus the walk-through flashback -- the three devices that make this the late masterpiece. (1) Slow motion as elegy: Peckinpah-derived slow motion punctuates the violence -- bodies flip and tumble in the air -- but here it is mournful rather than ecstatic, lending "an undeniably epic sweep" to a downbeat story. (2) The vocal score: Guido & Maurizio De Angelis wrote a melancholy folk score, inspired by Leonard Cohen (Nero's account), whose lyrics narrate the action on screen -- a Greek chorus in song form, modelled on Dylan in *Pat Garrett & Billy the Kid* and Cohen in *McCabe & Mrs. Miller*. (3) The walk-through flashback: adult characters literally step into their own past as captive observers of their younger selves -- "completely breaking with the Leone flashback style". The opening shot quotes *The Searchers* (a rider seen through a doorway) but the doorway is a broken screen door in an abandoned shack. Half-Native American ex-Union gunslinger Keoma (Nero) returns to a plague-ridden hometown run by ex-Confederate Caldwell (Donald O'Brien) and his three racist half-brothers; the town, torch-lit at night, "looks more like a medieval town than the traditional western town". Shot in eight weeks, mostly at Elios Studios, Rome, where Corbucci shot *Django*. Only a mild success in Italy on release; later praised as one of Castellari's best films and one of the last great spaghetti westerns. Cinematography by Aiace Parolin. Evidence: Wikipedia "Keoma (film)"; Spectrum Culture; Spaghetti Western Database.

**W09 -- The Big Racket (*Il grande racket*), 1976.** Poliziottesco. Grade A. The slow-motion crime montage as cold open, then the vigilante squad as ensemble: the film opens, as the credits roll, with a gang in catcher's masks arriving on motorcycles and smashing a jewellery shop to pieces in slow motion, scored to heavy rock riffs by the De Angelis brothers -- the racket literalised as noise. Inspector Nico Palmieri (Fabio Testi) surveys the wreckage the next morning while the music is still playing: crime is a daytime affair. When the law proves useless -- citizens too scared to talk, lawyers in the way -- Palmieri is fired and assembles a team of vigilantes, each wronged by the racket: the *Kill Them All* specialist-team formula (W03) transplanted into the city. The climax is a warehouse shootout choreographed around slow-motion bullet wounds, Testi with a big shotgun. The film ends without a coda -- no peaceful shopkeepers, no welcome home. Fondazione Prada, programming it in 2024, calls it "one of the must-see films in Castellari's filmography in which the distinctive traits of his highly personal style can be recognised". Same year as *Keoma* -- Castellari made his best western and his most brutal crime film in the same twelve months. Evidence: Letterboxd reviews; City on Fire; Genre Grinder; Fondazione Prada.

**W10 -- The Heroin Busters (*La via della droga*), 1977.** Poliziottesco, international. Grade A. The globe-trotting montage as the film's argument, then a stunt-reel finale: the film opens with Castellari's most ambitious montage yet -- a drug cartel mapped across New York, Hong Kong, Colombia, Amsterdam and Italy. Undercover narc Fabio (Fabio Testi -- a licensed pilot who did his own aerial stunts) infiltrates the ring while Interpol's Mike Hamilton (David Hemmings) runs the operation from outside; only Hamilton knows Fabio's true position, so Fabio must twice punch his own handler to keep cover. The converging plots stop making sense early and Castellari "loses interest in favour of increasingly elaborate technically flashy set-pieces, montages, foot chases, shoot-outs, and motorcycle crashes, ending in a light aircraft dogfight". The standout image: Testi jumping between escalators, flying off at the bottom and shooting a punk mid-air. Score by Goblin, fresh from *Suspiria* -- funk, psychedelia and rock rather than the De Angelis sound. Year flagged: Wikipedia's filmography table dates it 1978; Arrow, Genre Grinder and most sources say 1977 -- 1977 used here. Evidence: Genre Grinder; Son of Celluloid; DVD Talk; Letterboxd. [C30051]

**W11 -- The House by the Edge of the Lake (*Sensitivita*), 1979.** Erotic horror / supernatural. Grade A. Atmosphere and the erotic-psychic circuit, shot on holiday: Lilian (Leonora Fani), a leather-clad motorcycle daredevil, returns to her family's villa by a cursed lake to research local superstition for a college paper; her mother drowned there years earlier, dragged under by a hand from the water. The mechanism is a psychic link between two sisters: every man who has sex with Lilian dies soon after, while her sister Lilith watches and is possessed by the witch Kyra -- sex and death wired together as a circuit. Castellari himself disowned it in interview: made during his holidays in Spain with friends, "some sort of joke", with questionable money left over from another production; the producer ran out of money, used Castellari's name to raise more, failed, and finished the film himself -- "I've never seen it but I'm sure it's completely unwatchable" (interviewers later note he had since seen it and was "pleasantly surprised"). The Italian title *Sensitivita* is a word that does not exist in Italian, invented by the distributor. Released 28 September 1979. The one horror film he actually made, after turning down *Zombi 2* because he "did not feel he would be the right director for a horror film" -- the film went to Lucio Fulci. The De Angelis credit rests on the duo's filmography table alone this run (flagged). Evidence: Wikipedia "The House by the Edge of the Lake"; Quiet Cool blog (interview quote); Delirium guide via archive.org.

**W12 -- The Shark Hunter (*Il cacciatore di squali*), 1979.** Adventure / sharksploitation -- a crime thriller in a shark skin. Grade A. The shark as pretext; the location as the film: Mike Di Donato (Franco Nero, in a long blond wig with a braided headband, "looking a bit like a weathered Jesus") is a shark-hunting recluse on a Caribbean island who discovers a crashed plane carrying $50 million and plans to vacuum it out with a giant suction device. For a good half hour the film "functions as an aimless if picturesque travelogue" -- brawling, boozing, sun, sea -- before the treasure plot and rival mafias kick in. The mechanism is the hang-out film wearing an exploitation title: it is "more of a sunny hang out movie than a Jaws rip off", a crime thriller posing as a *Jaws* cash-in, like Margheriti's *Killer Fish*. Real shark-wrestling footage -- a stunt double actually grappling sharks in the water -- is the one genuinely startling device. Castellari himself appears as a mob hitman, staging a Django-esque scene where Nero gets roughed up by a mob led by the director. Shot in Mexico; score by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis. Evidence: The Spinning Image; Misantropey; Letterboxd; Delirium guide.

**W13 -- The Last Shark (*L'ultimo squalo*, aka *Great White*), 1981.** Horror / sharksploitation / mockbuster. Grade A. The *Jaws* transcription, pushed until the law intervened -- the rip-off as a production method: a great white terrorises the seaside community of Port Harbor during a windsurfing regatta; a gruff shark hunter (Vic Morrow), a writer (James Franciscus) and a tourism-obsessed governor (Joshua Sinclair) replay *Jaws* beat for beat, down to the shark snagging a buoy cable and dragging it through the water like the yellow barrels. The mechanism is escalation by imitation: more gore than *Jaws* (a girl loses a leg, the mayor is bitten in half), miniatures, and stock footage of real sharks cut against an immobile mock-up that "occasionally resembled a Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade balloon". It grossed over $18 million in its first month in the US -- then Universal sued for plagiarism; on 5 April 1982 federal judge David V. Kenyon ruled it too similar to *Jaws* and it was pulled from American theatres after about a month, and distributor Film Ventures later went bankrupt. Castellari considered a sequel but the mechanical shark was damaged after shooting. Score by Guido & Maurizio De Angelis. Originally assigned to Ruggero Deodato. Evidence: Wikipedia "Great White (1981 film)"; JoBlo; Mana Pop; Moria.

**W14 -- 1990: The Bronx Warriors (*1990: I guerrieri del Bronx*), 1982.** Post-apocalyptic action. Grade A. The gang-parade as world-building: the city is a costume catalogue. In 1990 the US government has cordoned off the Bronx as too crime-ridden to save; the heiress Ann (Stefania Girolami, the director's daughter) flees into it, and the Riders gang must escort her across a borough ruled by themed gangs -- roller-skaters, Broadway dancers, zombies, hockey players. The mechanism is lifted wholesale from *The Warriors* (gang traversal) and *Escape from New York* (walled-off city, a motorcade villain in Fred Williamson's Ogre, echoing the Duke) -- Kim Newman in the Monthly Film Bulletin noted Castellari "nimbly steals back all the stylistic elements that Carpenter and Hill poached from Leone's Westerns in the first place". Castellari added weirder gangs during filming, including the roller-skaters. The low budget shows in the world-building: exteriors shot in the real Bronx, interiors in Rome, and "in many, many shots we can see cars going over bridges and people going about their daily lives" -- the apocalypse with commuters in the background. Castellari plays the Vice President (cameo). A surprise hit that "created a small wave of films from Italy inspired by *Escape from New York*". Music by Walter Rizzati (rock-oriented). The three films -- this, *The New Barbarians*, *Escape from the Bronx* -- were written, prepared and filmed in six months. Evidence: Wikipedia "1990: The Bronx Warriors"; City on Fire; DVD Sleuth; DVD Talk.

**W15 -- The New Barbarians (*I nuovi barbari*, aka *Warriors of the Wasteland*), 1983.** Post-apocalyptic action. Grade A. The *Road Warrior* transplant, played as a Saturday-morning cartoon: filmed between the two Bronx films, it opens with a nuclear missile obliterating New York, then jumps to 2019 -- Scorpion (Giancarlo Prete) roams the wasteland in a skull-motif car, joined by Nadir (Fred Williamson) with his explosive arrows, against the Templars, an all-male, white-uniformed nihilist motorcycle cult led by George Eastman, determined to purify the world of everyone but themselves. The mechanism is the same as W14's gang parade, but the gangs are now a death cult with a theology, and the vehicles are tricked-out old cars with laser guns. Castellari's budget trick: "the same fifteen white uniforms reused in every shot". Score by Claudio Simonetti (Goblin) -- synth, catchy, borderline repetitive. The only one of the three that is genuinely post-apocalyptic rather than near-future urban. Year flagged: Wikipedia's filmography table dates it 1982; most sources say 1983 -- 1983 used here. Evidence: Offscreen; Dread Central; Letterboxd.

**W16 -- Escape from the Bronx (*Fuga dal Bronx*, aka *Escape 2000*), 1983.** Post-apocalyptic action. Grade A. The sequel as escalation: same hero, bigger firepower, united gangs. Trash (Mark Gregory) returns; this time the gangs of the Bronx have united against a common foe -- a corporation planning to clear the borough, with an extermination squad -- which "bares some striking similarities to *RoboCop*, a film which would be released just a few years later". The mechanism is wall-to-wall action with almost no plot room left: "even more wall-to-wall action than *Bronx Warriors*... Castellari seems quite okay with this". The title is the most direct lift from Carpenter of the three, and the film is both a standalone and a quasi-sequel. Famously riffed by Mystery Science Theater 3000 under the title *Escape 2000*. Castellari cameos as a radio operator. Score by Francesco De Masi. Evidence: Dread Central; Wikipedia "1990: The Bronx Warriors" (trilogy context); Wikipedia "Enzo G. Castellari". [C30052]

**W17 -- Sinbad of the Seven Seas, 1989.** Sword-and-sorcery fantasy. Grade A. The salvage job: a film assembled from a wrecked production, held together by a story-within-a-story. Written by Luigi Cozzi, who was to direct; Cozzi was replaced by Castellari, who rewrote the script toward a three-hour epic, went over budget, and the production was cancelled by the international producers; three years later Cozzi was brought back to salvage the footage, wrapping it in a framing device -- Daria Nicolodi reading the tale to her daughter (played by Cozzi's real daughter Giada) -- with a narrator describing on screen what the shredded edit no longer shows. Lou Ferrigno plays Sinbad as pure beefcake -- "a Sinbad that does not leap, bound and twirl with a scimitar but wades through melee combat with a broadsword" -- hunting four gems, against John Steiner's hammy Jaffar. The mechanism is the opposite of Castellari's usual control: the action is "a headlong rush through a series of episodes, almost wilfully bereft of any logic", and the slow-motion fight sequences are "lively enough" -- the Castellari grammar surviving inside someone else's edit. Cannon Films production. Purports to adapt Poe's "The Thousand-and-Second Tale of Scheherazade"; it does not, in any meaningful way. Evidence: Moria; InsidePulse; Letterboxd. (Note: the production history is told consistently across three secondary sources, but all three retell the same anecdote -- well-attested, not independently verified from production documents.)

**W18 -- Jonathan of the Bears (*Jonathan degli orsi*), 1994.** Western, revisionist / ecological. Grade A. The *Keoma* return, eighteen years on -- same devices, colder reception: Nero (who co-wrote the story and co-produced) plays Jonathan Kowalski, orphaned by bandits as a boy, befriended by a bear cub, raised by an Indian tribe; as an adult he renounces revenge, then takes up arms again when a businessman (John Saxon) wants the oil -- "black gold" -- under the tribe's burial grounds. The mechanism is *Keoma*'s toolkit recycled: the walk-through flashback (adult Jonathan literally walking through his own childhood), the folk-rock song-score as Greek chorus, the crucifixion image, the mystical witch figure. But the balance shifts: the first half is a spiritual quest told in flashbacks, the second half drops the flashbacks for "a lot of *slomo* violence and truly awful horse-tripping". Filmed in Russia -- the western town built inside a military artillery base near Moscow -- as an Italian-Russian co-production financed by Silvio Berlusconi; Castellari was brought in very late but "still managed to put his imprint on the production". Native actors cast in the leads (Floyd "Red Crow" Westerman, who was in *Dances with Wolves*), Mongolian extras as warriors -- "works distracting". Dedicated to Sergio Corbucci, whose *Django* made Nero a star. Castellari's last theatrical feature. ## Evolution: early vs late

**1966-1968 -- Apprenticeship and the western.** Uncredited co-direction (*Few Dollars for Django*, 1966), then the credited debut *Renegade Riders* (1967) as a conscious style exercise after *The Appaloosa*. Three westerns in two years (W01-W03) establish the two lines that never leave him: the comic double-cross western (W02) and the specialist-team action machine (W03). The grammar is already there -- widescreen staging, over-elaborated action, pace over plausibility.

**1971-1976 -- Genre-hopping, then the poliziottesco peak.** Thriller (*Cold Eyes of Fear*, 1971), comedy (*Hector the Mighty*, 1972), comic western (*Te Deum*, 1972) -- then *High Crime* (1973) finds the niche. Four crime films in five years (W05, W06, W09, W10) plus *Keoma* (1976) in the same year as *The Big Racket*: the western and the crime film are the same machine with different costumes -- the specialist team, the opening montage, the slow-motion set-piece, the De Angelis score. Nero is the constant: seven features together.

**1978-1983 -- The filone years.** *The Inglorious Bastards* (1978) replays *The Dirty Dozen*; *The Shark Hunter* (1979) and *The Last Shark* (1981) replay *Jaws* -- the second one so closely that a US federal judge pulled it from theatres. Then the Bronx trilogy (1982-83) replays *Escape from New York*, *The Warriors* and *The Road Warrior* in six months of production. The mechanism does not change; the source text does. This is the period where Castellari's reputation as a "rip-off" director hardens -- but the films are not copies so much as remixes: the same action grammar applied to someone else's premise, with the seams (real Bronx streets, commuters in the background, a mechanical shark that looks like a parade balloon) left visible.

**1984-1994 -- Foreign-market work and the last western.** *Light Blast* (1985), *Striker*, *Sinbad* (1989) -- work "made for foreign markets" -- culminating in *Jonathan of the Bears* (1994), the *Keoma* devices replayed with less conviction and a Russian-built western town. Last theatrical feature.

**1994-2010 -- Television, teaching, silence.** Made-for-TV productions in the 1990s, then film teaching in Italy and Spain. Sixteen years without a feature.

**2010-2025 -- The comeback and the honour.** *Caribbean Basterds* (2010) returns by stealing back Tarantino's stolen title. No further features found; the 2025 Sitges Time Machine Award is the capstone -- the festival circuit now honours the man whose films were once pulled from theatres by court order. [C30054]

## M1 -- The slow-motion shootout as elegy

Slow motion is Castellari's signature, and its function changes across the career. In *Keoma* it is mournful -- bodies tumbling through the air in a dying genre, "an undeniably epic sweep". In *The Big Racket* it is the opening crime itself, stretched out for the credits, the racket literalised as noise. In *The Last Shark* it becomes, per one reviewer, "shameless runtime padding, elevated to the point where it has become Castellari's signature style". Same device, three registers: elegy, spectacle, padding. The Italian Wikipedia "Stile" section, read end to end, names slow motion in every film, borrowed from Peckinpah's *The Wild Bunch* -- and Tarantino, in the 2008 conversation, singles out exactly this device for praise. The slow motion is never decoration: it is how Castellari tells the audience what kind of film this is -- a dying western, a daytime crime, a film that knows it is padding and does it anyway. Evidence: W08, W09, W13; Italian Wikipedia "Stile"; the 2008 Tarantino conversation.

**Takeaway:** one device, held for sixty years, changes its meaning every decade -- slow motion is not a trick, it is the film's tone of voice, and the tone is what survives when the plot does not.

## M2 -- The opening montage as thesis

*Street Law*, *The Big Racket* and *The Heroin Busters* all open with a montage that states the film's argument before the plot starts: crime is everywhere and the law is absent (Genoa street violence); the racket is noise (a motorcycle gang smashing a shop to a rock score); the drug trade is global (five cities in one montage). The montage is not exposition -- it is the film's politics, delivered as action. *High Crime* does the same work with a chase instead of a montage: a ten-minute pursuit through Genoa that argues the law can only move at speed. The Italian Wikipedia "Stile" section adds the companion device: a dry incipit straight into action, no establishing shot, no preamble. The film begins by showing you the world it believes in, and only then bothers with a story. Evidence: W05, W06, W09, W10; Italian Wikipedia "Stile".

**Takeaway:** a film that opens with its argument does not need to earn it later -- the first two minutes are the thesis, and everything after is the evidence.

## M3 -- The specialist-team machine

From *Kill Them All* (1968) through *The Big Racket* (1976) to the Bronx films, Castellari's plots are built the same way: assemble a team, each member an expert in one kind of violence, and point them at a fortress -- a Union stronghold, a warehouse full of racketeers, a walled-off Bronx. The cast is often stuntmen first, actors second. The pleasure is the team's competence, not the plot -- and the camera proves it by lifting high to show the fortress space and the team's progress through it, geography as a scoreboard. The device survives every genre change because it is not a genre device at all: it is a way of organising action so the audience can read it. Evidence: W03, W09, W14, W15, W16.

**Takeaway:** a team of specialists is a readable machine -- give each member one skill and the audience one fortress, and the action explains itself.

## M4 -- The Franco Nero star-vehicle system

Seven features together (English Wikipedia): *High Crime* (1973), *Street Law* (1974), *Cry, Onion!* (1975), *Keoma* (1976), *The Shark Hunter* (1979), *Day of the Cobra* (1980), *Jonathan of the Bears* (1993/94). Nero is the constant across the western, the crime film, the shark film and the late western. Castellari: "I think that to have an actor like Franco Nero is one of the best things that can happen to a director... if it had been possible, I would have made all my films with him" (Wikipedia, quoting Castellari). The mechanism: a star whose physicality -- he does his own stunts, is repeatedly beaten on camera in *Street Law* -- carries the realism the budgets cannot buy. The hinge runs deeper: Barboni shot *Django* (1966) as cinematographer, the film that made Nero, and Castellari then directed him seven times; Margheriti gave Nero his first sci-fi leads in 1965/66. Nero is the thread that stitches the whole Italian genre shelf together. Evidence: W05, W06, W08, W12, W18; Wikipedia "Enzo G. Castellari"; leg 3 section 1.

**Takeaway:** a star who will take the beating on camera is worth more than a budget -- his body is the special effect, and the film's realism is his bruises.

## M5 -- The song-score: De Angelis as narrator

Guido & Maurizio De Angelis score *Te Deum*, *Cry, Onion!*, *Keoma*, *Street Law*, *The Big Racket*, *The Shark Hunter* and *The Last Shark* -- the brothers are as much a Castellari collaborator as Nero, and the mechanism is the same one their own study names: the song that comments. In *Keoma* the score is the film's most argued-about device: folk songs in the Leonard Cohen mode whose lyrics narrate the action on screen, a Greek chorus (SWDB review; Wikipedia). In *The Big Racket* the same brothers supply heavy rock riffs that keep playing into the morning after the crime. In *Street Law*, "Goodbye My Friend" narrates the ending over a freeze-frame. The score is never wallpaper; it comments, narrates, or contradicts. The counterpoint proves the rule: *The Heroin Busters* uses Goblin instead -- funk and psychedelia -- and *The New Barbarians* uses Claudio Simonetti's synth, and both films feel different in kind, not just in sound. Evidence: W04, W06, W07, W08, W09, W12, W13; the De Angelis study M2 and M3.

**Takeaway:** a score that narrates is a second script -- the song says what the silent hero cannot, and the film is the argument between the two.

## M6 -- The city (or the wasteland) as a set: location as character

Genoa in *High Crime* and *Street Law* (cobbled streets that make every stunt more dangerous); the real Bronx in *1990* (exteriors shot on location, interiors in Rome); the Moscow artillery base dressed as a western town in *Jonathan of the Bears*; the Caribbean island in *The Shark Hunter*, where the location is the film for half an hour. The mechanism: shoot the real place, let its texture do the world-building the budget cannot, and accept the seams -- commuters visible behind the apocalypse, a shallow pond misidentified as a lake. The Italian Wikipedia "Stile" section names the companion habit: shootouts in enclosed spaces (garages, abandoned factories), where the location constrains the action into something readable. The place is never decoration: Genoa's cobbles are the chase, the Bronx's streets are the gang parade, the island's sun is the first half of the film. Evidence: W05, W06, W12, W14, W18; Italian Wikipedia "Stile".

**Takeaway:** a real place, shot honestly, out-builds any set -- and the seams where the budget shows are not flaws, they are the film's signature.

## M7 -- The filone remix: one visual grammar, any source text

The career's organising principle. Castellari takes a hit -- *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* (W02), *The Dirty Dozen* (W03, the Appendix), *Death Wish* (W06, and pointedly in the same year), *Jaws* (W12, W13), *Escape from New York* / *The Warriors* / *The Road Warrior* (W14-W16), *Conan* (W17) -- and runs his own grammar through it: the team, the montage, the slow motion, the De Angelis score. Kim Newman's line on *Bronx Warriors* is the key: Castellari "nimbly steals back all the stylistic elements that Carpenter and Hill poached from Leone's Westerns in the first place". The remix is the style. The proof is that the films survive their sources: nobody watches *The Last Shark* to see *Jaws* again, they watch it to see Castellari do *Jaws* -- the buoy dragged like the yellow barrels, the mayor bitten in half, the parade-balloon shark. The filone is not theft; it is a commission system in which the hit is the brief and the director's grammar is the answer. Evidence: W02, W03, W06, W13, W14, W15, W16, W17.

**Takeaway:** a strong enough grammar can take any brief -- the source text is the assignment, the director's devices are the style, and the remix outlives the original when the grammar is stronger than the premise.

## M8 -- The echo: his films echoed by Tarantino and others

The feedback loop that defines his late reputation. Tarantino's *Inglourious Basterds* (2009) takes the title (respelt) of Castellari's *The Inglorious Bastards* (1978) -- "deliberately misspelled as 'a Basquiat-esque touch'" -- casts Castellari himself as a Wehrmacht general in the cameo role Castellari played in his own film, and gives Bo Svenson a part. *Death Proof* (2007) reuses the De Angelis cue "Gangster Story" from *High Crime* (1973), verified by three sources (the Death Proof soundtrack page, a2zsoundtrack.com, the Finders Keepers reissue copy). The 2008 Severin conversation has Tarantino praising Castellari's slow motion to his face. The 2009 publicity then enables Castellari's own comeback, *Caribbean Basterds* (2010), which steals the misspelling back. Earlier echoes: *Keoma* is repeatedly cited as the last great spaghetti western and a Peckinpah heir; *The Big Racket* is described by one reviewer as "proto-John Woo" -- "iron-jawed tough guys with limitless bullets, shooting it out in slow-motion for a cause that feels all but lost". The study treats Castellari as a director whose influence runs through the filmmakers who remixed him -- the filone, returned. What is NOT asserted: that Tarantino cited *Keoma* as an influence -- no Tarantino quote to that effect was found, and his own three favourite westerns exclude it. Evidence: Appendix, W19, W08, W09; leg 3 section 3.

**Takeaway:** a remix that is good enough gets remixed back -- the echo is the career's second act, and the title stolen twice is the proof the style outlived the film.

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Castellari is not a director who has a genre; he is a director who has a grammar, and the grammar goes looking for genres. Early he is the western man, learning the widescreen stage from Furie and the double-cross from Leone, building the specialist team out of stuntmen; then the crime man, who finds in Genoa a city whose cobbles make every chase dangerous and in Franco Nero a body that will take the beating the budget cannot buy; then the remix man, who runs the same machine -- team, montage, slow motion, song-score -- through *Jaws*, through *Escape from New York*, through *Conan*, and leaves the seams visible on purpose, because the seams are the signature; then the salvage man, holding a wrecked Sinbad together with a story inside a story; late he is the echoed man, the director whose title Tarantino steals and whose misspelling he steals back, whose shark film a judge pulled from theatres and whose career a festival honours in 2025. The place is never decoration: the Techniscope frame, the Genoa cobbles, the real Bronx with commuters behind the apocalypse, the Moscow artillery base dressed as a western town, the Caribbean sun that is half the film. His own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one the work states: take the hit of the year, run your grammar through it, let the seams show -- and when someone remixes you, remix them back.
