# Ronald Baecker: Study Notes

**Site:** Ronald Michael Baecker is a Canadian computer scientist and human-computer interaction (HCI) pioneer, Emeritus Professor of Computer Science and Emeritus Bell Chair in Human-Computer Interaction at the University of Toronto, and Adjunct Professor of Computer Science at Columbia University, where he teaches Computers and Society each fall. Born 7 October 1942 in Kenosha, Wisconsin, he took a B.Sc. in physics (MIT, 1963), an M.Sc. in electrical engineering (MIT, 1964), and a Ph.D. in computer science (MIT, June 1969) for the dissertation *Interactive Computer-Mediated Animation* (Project MAC TR-61), built on the TX-2 at MIT Lincoln Laboratory in 1966-1969. He joined the University of Toronto in 1972 and co-founded the Dynamic Graphics Project (DGP), later founding the Knowledge Media Design Institute (1996) and the Technologies for Aging Gracefully Lab (TAGlab, director from 2009). His work runs from GENESYS, the first interactive computer animation system, through *Sorting Out Sorting* (1978-1981), a founding film of software visualization, through the *Readings in Human-Computer Interaction* volumes (1987, with Bill Buxton; 1995, with Jonathan Grudin, Buxton, and Saul Greenberg), the ePresence webcasting and archiving system (2003-2007), and the later books *Computers and Society* (Oxford University Press, 2019) and *Digital Dreams Have Become Nightmares* (2021, 2nd ed. 2024, with Jonathan Grudin). He was named one of ACM SIGGRAPH's 60 Pioneers of Computer Graphics (1998), elected to the ACM SIGCHI Academy (2005) and as an ACM Fellow (2011), and received the SIGCHI Social Impact Award (2020). He is living and active into 2026 (a 2025 book, *Reinvention: Meaningful Ventures in Later Life*, and a scheduled February 2026 ACM Distinguished Speaker talk at UBC). [C38260]

**Relevance:** (1) Baecker helps investigate what happens when a movement is not a reach toward a target but a gesture that means something -- when the hand's path is itself the message, and the question is not how fast it arrives but what it says on the way. (2) The evidence is GENESYS (built 1966-1969), the first significant interactive computer animation system, in which the animator sketches pictures and motions -- a p-curve whose point spacing records the speed of the hand -- and the pictures drive algorithms that generate the dynamic display. This is a correction to the brief for this study, which first described GENESYS as key-frame interpolation ("draw key frames, computer generates the in-betweens"): the dissertation treats interpolation between pairs of critical frames as one of three older approaches, distinct from picture-driven animation, which replaces it. The worked case of what the system computes between two poses is therefore not an in-between at all -- it is a recorded gesture, replayed and reused -- together with his later work showing programs as races with clocks (*Sorting Out Sorting*), video as a navigable record of a human event (ePresence), and computers as a question of what should be done (*Computers and Society*, *Digital Dreams*). [C38261]

**Doorway:** The 464th doorway. Takes up the open question from the I. Scott MacKenzie study (#843), quoted verbatim: "What happens when the movement being measured is not a reach toward a target but a gesture that means something -- when the hand's path is itself the message, and the question is not how fast it arrives but what it says on the way?" Baecker answers it from the animation side, and his answer is that the hand's path is the message because in GENESYS the path is recorded as a p-curve whose point spacing IS the speed -- slow where the symbols cluster, fast where they are widely spaced -- so one sketch carries both the route and its timing, and the gesture is then stored, replayed, and reused under different pictures. The question is no longer how fast the hand arrives but what the hand said while travelling, and the answer is readable in the dots it left behind. Norman McLaren's line, quoted approvingly by Baecker, states the same answer as a principle: animation is not the art of DRAWINGS-that-move but the art of MOVEMENTS-that-are-drawn. The joining is this study's construction, not Baecker's: he never measures a gesture against Fitts's law, and the law is MacKenzie's, not his -- what he supplies is the recorded gesture itself, a path with its tempo inside it, where MacKenzie supplied only the time the reach took. Staged in RBA1, the viewer draws one path and watches their own hesitation and hurry played back as motion, the dots of the path visibly denser where the hand lingered. The open question carried forward is the one a single hand cannot answer: if the hand's path is the message, what happens when the message is not drawn by one hand but made by many hands together -- when the gesture is shared, negotiated, and answered in real time, and the picture is what the group agrees it says? [C38262]

## M1 -- The p-curve: spacing is speed

**Subject:** A single hand-drawn path across the canvas, with a small form riding it.

**Visual principle:** The path is a dotted line whose dot spacing is the whole story: clustered dots where the hand lingered, wide gaps where it hurried. Speed made visible as density along one line; the path is both route and rhythm. The eye reads tempo before it reads shape.

**Computational mechanism:** The p-curve is GENESYS's core mechanism. The animator sketches a path with the stylus; the symbols making up the sketch are not evenly spaced, because their spacing records the dynamics of the hand: where she moves slowly the symbols cluster, where she moves quickly they are widely spaced, so one sketch carries both the path and its timing. Playback works by selecting a cel and a position from the p-curve once per frame, each frame shown for 1/24 sec; the animator can slow playback (the frog hop is slowed to 1/8 sec per frame, almost 3 sec for the whole sequence), and filming paints each frame three times on the film so the hop runs at the desired speed. The piece records a drawn stroke as a p-curve (sampled points with their real inter-point timing) and replays a form along it at the recorded pace; a second pass can re-time the same path by re-spacing the samples, so the same route can be hurried or lingered over without redrawing it. The p-curve as the piece's only clock is this study's own formalisation; the curve itself is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A drawing whose timing is literally the handwriting of its maker -- the same path drawn hesitantly and drawn boldly are two different pieces. The gallery has pieces driven by clocks and by physics; none driven by the recorded hesitation of a hand.

**Takeaway:** The dots are the tempo: where they crowd, the hand lingered; where they stretch, it hurried. [C38264]

## M2 -- Picture and motion are separable

**Subject:** One motion -- a hop, a swing, a fall -- worn by a changing cast of pictures.

**Visual principle:** Same arc, different body: the eye learns to see the motion as an object apart from the thing moving. Contrast between the fixed, repeated trajectory and the parade of forms that ride it. The motion becomes the constant; the picture becomes the variable.

**Computational mechanism:** In GENESYS, pictures and motions have truly separate identities: any movement can be freely applied to any picture, so the hop and its contact change can be reused on new objects -- the frog scenario's hop was applied to new objects in a variant built in under 6 minutes, against about 5 minutes for the original. The piece holds one p-curve fixed and cycles the form riding it -- drawn forms, generated forms, a dot, a letterform, a shape -- each completing the same motion and differing only by its own silhouette and squash at the contact point. Cel 0 is part of the cycle: selecting cel 0 in a selection graph means the object is invisible in those frames, so the motion continues with nothing riding it, and absence becomes one beat of the rhythm. The cast-cycle formalisation is this study's own; the separation is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about the motion itself, proved by changing everything except the motion. The disappearance beat (cel 0) makes absence part of the rhythm rather than a gap in it.

**Takeaway:** Change the rider, keep the ride -- the motion is the piece, the picture only visits. [C38265]

## M3 -- The selection graph: the timeline as a drawable object

**Subject:** A score -- a graph of positions against time -- with pictures pinned to it.

**Visual principle:** Time laid flat and drawn on: a horizontal score where each column is a frame and each row is a choice of cel. The picture is read left to right like music; a substitution (one cel for another at the bottom of a hop) is a visible notch in the score.

**Computational mechanism:** GENESYS has three modes: DRAWINGS (sketch and file cels), DYNAMICS (p-curves, waveforms, selection graphs), and EDITFRAME (reposition a cel in one frame by grabbing it with the stylus and dragging it, with adjacent frames superimposable). The selection graph picks which cel appears in each frame. The piece renders its own selection graph as a visible strip: the viewer sees which form is live in each frame, and dragging a form in the main view edits exactly one frame's entry in the graph. The visible-score formalisation is this study's own; the graph is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** The control surface is the artwork: the timeline is not hidden in a panel but drawn as part of the picture, so the viewer watches the piece conduct itself.

**Takeaway:** The score is drawn on the canvas: every frame's choice is a mark you can read. [C38266]

## M4 -- The race: efficiency made visible

**Subject:** Several processes racing to put the same disorder in order, each with its own clock.

**Visual principle:** Order emerging at different speeds: bars or dots that start as a cloud and end as a diagonal, with one racer visibly pulling away while another stalls. Colour marks state -- unconsidered, sorted, being compared -- and the clocks are part of the picture, not a caption.

**Computational mechanism:** *Sorting Out Sorting* encodes each datum as a vertical bar whose height is proportional to its value; one colour marks items yet to be considered, a second marks items already sorted, and a third forms borders around items currently being compared, while dimmed items are not currently being considered. After each group of three, a race sorts 250 items with a digital clock per algorithm; in the exchange race quicksort finishes in about 7 seconds, shakersort in about 2 minutes 21 seconds, and bubblesort in just over 2 minutes 45 seconds. The closing grand race runs all nine algorithms in parallel on 2,500 items each, represented as coloured dots whose vertical position is value and horizontal position is array position, so unsorted data appears as a cloud and sorted data as a diagonal line; tree selection and quicksort finish in about 20 seconds each, and the narrator notes it would take another 54 minutes for bubblesort to complete. As an epilogue the entire film is replayed at 12 times normal speed, for review and to show visual patterns unique to each method that are not obvious at normal speed. The piece runs several ordering processes on the same shuffled field, each with its own clock, and ends with a 12x-speed replay epilogue. The racer set is this study's own formalisation; the race, the clocks, and the epilogue are Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece whose argument is a finish line: you do not need to understand the algorithms to feel the difference between 7 seconds and 54 minutes. The cloud-to-diagonal is one of the strongest images in the study.

**Takeaway:** The clock is the criticism: the same disorder, ordered at wildly different speeds. [C38267]

## M5 -- The title that sorts itself

**Subject:** A jumble of letters that arranges itself into the piece's own name.

**Visual principle:** Scramble resolving into sense: letters wander, compare, swap, and settle -- the title is not printed but earned, in front of the viewer. The arc from disorder to order is legible as motion even before any word is read.

**Computational mechanism:** Each of the nine parts of *Sorting Out Sorting* opens with a title animation of 10 to 20 seconds in which the letters of the algorithm's name appear scrambled and are rearranged by the algorithm itself until they spell the title correctly. The piece treats its own title as the data: each letter is a datum with a target position, and a comparison-exchange process moves letters by comparing and swapping until the sequence is sorted and the title is complete. The self-titling formalisation is this study's own; the letter-scramble device is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that introduces itself by demonstrating itself -- the first thing you see is the system proving what it does, on its own name. The motion must read without reading the words: the scramble-to-order arc carries the piece even for a viewer who never parses the letters.

**Takeaway:** The piece spells its own name by sorting itself -- the demonstration is the title. [C38268]

## M6 -- Flash frames: the accident the system could not play

**Subject:** A moving field of dots that sometimes floods -- frames drenched or spattered with light the system never intended.

**Visual principle:** Order interrupted by bloom: clean dot drawings give way to dense, light-textured fields, then order returns. The flood frames are the most beautiful thing in the piece precisely because the system cannot play them.

**Computational mechanism:** Lynn Smith's discovery, made while filming from the scope: with the camera disconnected and the shutter left open, frames were drenched or spattered with dots, producing light-textured "flash frames"; these could not be played back in real time and had to be recorded on film first, then ordered and combined in a Moviola, a frame-by-frame process at odds with the system's design goals. The piece accumulates dot trails until a density threshold tips it into an open-shutter flood: the frame saturates, the flood frames are set aside, and a second, slower pass replays them in the order they occurred. The threshold-and-replay formalisation is this study's own; the flash frames are Smith's, inside Baecker's system.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that honours the accident: the system's failure mode becomes its climax, and the slow replay of the floods is a second film inside the first.

**Takeaway:** The most beautiful frames are the ones the machine could not play -- so they get their own slower film. [C38269]

## M7 -- Probes that sing: hearing what the program does

**Subject:** A running process whose state you hear as well as see -- pitch and volume standing in for values the eye cannot hold.

**Visual principle:** Sound as a second picture: the image stays spare (a few moving marks, a quiet field) while the audio carries the density -- a low hum for a slow variable, a rising pitch for a climbing one, a struck note when a threshold is crossed.

**Computational mechanism:** LogoMedia auralization probes let programmers attach audio "probes" to code or data without modifying the program: control probes monitor execution flow and data probes track variable values, turning on synthesized instruments, playing sound samples, or adjusting pitch or volume. The piece binds each visible process to a probe: flow events trigger short tones, a tracked value bends a continuous pitch. Sound is a data channel with stated mappings, not decoration; the piece stays legible silent, and the sound carries the temporal pattern (the trend over time) that a single glance cannot hold. The probe-binding formalisation is this study's own; the probes are DiGiano's implementation within Baecker's programme.

**Artistic opportunity:** The gallery's first piece where the sound is the measurement: mute it and you lose the trend, not the mood. It extends Baecker's own move from showing a program to hearing one.

**Takeaway:** The program sings its own state: flow as rhythm, value as pitch. [C38270]

## M8 -- The archive that stays navigable

**Subject:** A recorded event -- a talk, a performance, a making -- that you can walk through afterwards by chapter, slide, timeline, or word.

**Visual principle:** The record as a landscape with paths through it: a timeline with chapter marks, a strip of stills, a search that lights up the moments a word was spoken. The past event is not replayed flat but navigated, like a place.

**Computational mechanism:** ePresence is an open-source webcasting and archiving system; archive viewers navigate by a two-level structure of chapters and slides, by a timeline, or by keyword search over words appearing in the slides; slide titles are picked up automatically, chapter titles are entered by the moderator during the talk. Live interaction delay was cut from 25-30 seconds to 5-15 seconds, with a stated goal of no more than 5 seconds. The piece records its own making as it runs -- every state change logged as a chapter -- and offers the archive as the piece: scrub the timeline to revisit any earlier state, jump by chapter, search by the labels the piece gave its own phases. The self-archiving formalisation is this study's own; the navigation structure is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that remembers itself in public: the viewer does not just watch a system, they browse its history, and the history is the work.

**Takeaway:** The piece keeps its own archive, and the archive is the piece. [C38271]

## M9 -- Phases intermingled: the film made while it is planned

**Subject:** A small film being made -- planned, shot, and cut in the same gesture, not in separate phases.

**Visual principle:** Making visible as making: sketches, shots, and edits share one surface; a plan becomes a shot becomes a cut without changing rooms. The roughness is the point: a first movie in five hours.

**Computational mechanism:** The MAD system supports structuring, modifying, and visualizing motion pictures across pre-production, production, and post-production, intermingling phases that are traditionally separate; in the 1996-97 summer-camp studies, eight groups of three seventh graders made two movies each in a week, and students could make a first movie in as little as 5 hours. The piece collapses the pipeline into one canvas: draw a shot (pre-production), set it in motion (production), and cut it against the last shot (post-production) in a single continuous interaction, with no mode switch. The single-canvas pipeline is this study's own formalisation; the intermingling is Baecker's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about amateur fearlessness: the speed and roughness of a first movie as a virtue, not a limitation -- the gallery's first piece whose subject is how quickly a complete thing can exist.

**Takeaway:** Plan, shoot, cut on one surface: a whole film in five hours, roughness included. [C38272]

## M10 -- The taxonomy as a mirror: six questions for any system

**Subject:** Any generative system, held up to six questions: scope, content, form, method, interaction, effectiveness.

**Visual principle:** A piece that grades itself in public: six small meters or marks, one per category, that move as the piece runs -- the form meter rises when the picture clarifies, the effectiveness meter when the system does what it claims.

**Computational mechanism:** The taxonomy (Price, Baecker, Small 1993) has six categories A-F -- Scope, Content, Form, Method, Interaction, Effectiveness -- applied to twelve systems; only *Sorting Out Sorting*, BALSA, Zeus, and ANIM provide program synchronization, the centralized control needed to run an algorithm race; the rankings are subjective by the authors' own admission, which they call the taxonomy's weakest point. The piece runs its own system and scores itself live against the six categories, displaying the scores as part of the picture; the scores are computed from stated rules (for example, interaction scored by how many distinct states viewer input can reach), and the subjectivity is admitted on the surface rather than hidden. The self-scoring formalisation is this study's own; the six categories are the taxonomy's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that performs its own criticism -- the taxonomy turned from a paper about systems into a system about itself. It is the most Baecker-like of the mechanisms: the crafts of typography, graphic design, animation, and cinematography turned back on the work itself.

**Takeaway:** Six questions, answered live by the piece about itself -- criticism as a visible meter. [C38273]

## Mechanism note -- what is Baecker's and what is not

GENESYS, the dissertation, *Sorting Out Sorting*, and *Computers and Society* are Baecker's lead work; the GENESYS film's images are largely Lynn Smith's (she used the system extensively in 1969 and 1970), with camera by Eric Martin and sound by Reg Oliver. The taxonomy is first-authored by Blaine Price, with Baecker and Ian Small; its "thirty features" phrasing comes from the 1992 conference version. ePresence is Baecker with Peter Wolf and Kelly Rankin. LogoMedia's probes are DiGiano's implementation within Baecker's programme. APPL is a design outline in the dissertation only, never a running system. BALSA, Zeus, and TANGO are Brown's and Stasko's systems, discussed in the taxonomy as successors and comparators, not Baecker's. Knowlton's 1966 L6 films, Burtnyk and Wein's 1970 key-frame film, and McLaren's direct-animation work are cited influences and contemporaries, not his. *Sorting Out Sorting* was designed by Baecker with assistance from David Sherman and programmed by Sherman with assistance. The "Bell" in his chair title is Bell University Laboratories (Bell Canada), not Bell Telephone Laboratories; he never worked at Bell Labs. The fusions -- the p-curve as a piece's only clock, the cast cycle with cel 0, the visible score, the racer set, self-titling, threshold-and-replay floods, probe binding, self-archiving, the single-canvas pipeline, self-scoring -- are this study's own formalisations, and are labelled as such where they appear. No prompt in this study is built from a mechanism whose attribution is misstated. [C38274]

## The arc: from the hand that draws, to the program that shows, to the people that are recorded, to the question of what should be

Baecker's career reads as one long move across four questions, and the move is the through-line. First, "the computer draws what the hand does" (GENESYS, 1966-1969): the hand sketches pictures and motions, and the machine plays them back. Then, "the computer shows what a program does" (*Sorting Out Sorting*, 1978-1981, and the taxonomy, 1993): value as bar height, state as colour, efficiency as a race with clocks, and a six-category frame for judging any such system. Then, "the computer records what people do" (MAD, 1996; ePresence, 2004): a child's film, a lecture, kept as a structured, searchable archive that stays navigable. Then, "what should the computer do" (*Computers and Society*, 2019, in three parts -- Opportunities, Risks, Choices -- across 12 chapters; and *Digital Dreams*, 2021/2024, in three parts -- the dreams that came true, their unintended side effects, and what must be done). The constants across all four phases: the picture is never the point; the change in the picture is. And the system is always shown working in public -- the race with its clocks, the archive with its chapters, the taxonomy with its admitted subjectivity. This arc paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-2, not Baecker's own words. [C38275]

## The aesthetic logic

Baecker's aesthetic is the sparse mark on a plain ground, and the mark that changes. The GENESYS cels are sparse dot drawings because the TX-2's memory organization and display speed limit dot density; the *Sorting Out Sorting* bars are plain verticals in three colours; the ePresence archive is a timeline with chapter marks. Nothing is decorated to hide the mechanism -- the mechanism is the decoration. Slowness here is not a mood but a price that is stated on the surface: precise diagrammatic sequences took 2 to 4 hours of console work to produce 10 seconds of film, and the film's own narrator admits it would take another 54 minutes for bubblesort to finish. The honest piece shows the price rather than hiding it, and lets the viewer watch change itself: a p-curve's dots crowding and stretching, a cloud becoming a diagonal, a flood frame blooming out of order. This paragraph is an interpretation built from legs 1-2, not Baecker's own words. [C38276]
