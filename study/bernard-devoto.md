# Bernard DeVoto: Study Notes

**Site:** Bernard Augustine DeVoto (1897-1955), American writer and historian of the American West -- born Monday 11 January 1897 in Ogden, Utah, only son of Florian Bernard DeVoto (1856-1935), a Catholic of Italian descent from Indiana, and Rhoda Ann Dye DeVoto (1861-1919), daughter of a Mormon farmer of Uintah; Sacred Heart Academy and Ogden High School (graduated 1914); University of Utah 1914-1915; Harvard College, entered 1915 as a sophomore, B.A. cum laude in philosophy 1920, Phi Beta Kappa; Army 1917-1919, second lieutenant, instructor of musketry and riflery at Camp Perry, Ohio; instructor in English at Northwestern University, 1922-1927, where he met Garrett Mattingly in 1926 and married Helen Avis MacVicar in June 1923; lecturer in English at Harvard, 1929-1936; editor of the Saturday Review of Literature, 1936-January 1938; "The Easy Chair" columnist at Harper's, November 1935-November 1955, 241 columns; unpaid literary curator of the Mark Twain Estate, 1938-1946; author of Mark Twain's America (1932), The Year of Decision: 1846 (1943), Across the Wide Missouri (1947, Pulitzer Prize for History 1948 and inaugural Bancroft Prize 1948), The Course of Empire (1952, National Book Award for Nonfiction 1953), The Literary Fallacy (1944), The World of Fiction (1950) and The Hour: A Cocktail Manifesto (1951); editor of The Journals of Lewis and Clark (1953); conservation polemicist ("The West: A Plundered Province", Harper's, August 1934; "The West Against Itself", Harper's, January 1947); speechwriter and adviser on public lands to Adlai Stevenson in 1952; died Sunday 13 November 1955 in New York City of a ruptured aortic aneurysm, aged 58, hours after appearing on the CBS television program Adventure; ashes scattered over the Clearwater National Forest along the Lochsa River, Idaho, near the Bernard DeVoto Memorial Cedar Grove dedicated in 1962. ## M1. History as Synecdoche
Illuminate a whole period through intense concentration on one brief time and a few representative historical characters. Stegner named the device; Croke defines it: "illuminating whole periods through intense concentration on one brief time and a few representative historical characters." The Year of Decision takes one calendar year, 1846, for the whole of Manifest Destiny; Across the Wide Missouri takes Sir William Drummond Stewart and the painter Alfred Jacob Miller on their 1837 tour for the whole fur trade; James Clyman stands for the mountain-man era. What it IS: the part chosen so exactly that it carries the whole. **Takeaway:** pick one year, one tour, one man, and make the whole movement visible through them.

## M2. Chronological Symphony / Simultaneity
Run several storylines in parallel, darting back and forth among the scenes, while the narrative itself stays rigorously chronological. DeVoto's own Preface states the method: "the actual narrative is always rigorously chronological, and the parts of the book are kept as close to a chronological order as the multiplicity of stories will permit. In passages designed to illustrate or interpret the narrative, however, I range forward and backward in time as far as the end in view requires." Frederick Paxson called it "chronological symphony." Chapter 1 briefly introduces the 1846 storylines, then the book darts among them for the rest of its length. This is the device Mattingly borrowed, per EBSCO, and the doorway of this study: the pupil's "history-in-the-round" is the friend's method, carried into sixteenth-century Europe. What it IS: parallel scenes held in strict calendar order, with interpretation free to range. **Takeaway:** cut between storylines on the same dates and the reader holds the year whole.

## M3. The Heavy First Chapter
Open with a first chapter "of grievous weight" that states the book's purpose and method outright, so that by its end the reader knows how the whole book will work. The Year of Decision opens with an omen -- John T. Hughes's story of traders seeing an eagle spread across the setting sun after a prairie thunderstorm ("in less than twelve months the eagle of liberty would spread his broad pinions over the plains of the west") -- immediately undercut by a sterner omen: Biela's comet, due 11 February 1846, was found on 13 January 1846 by Matthew Maury to have split in two. Then a direct address to the reader stating the book's purpose ("to realize the far western frontier experience... as personal experience"), then the January 1, 1846 map. The Indiana Magazine of History review: "The artistry of these first fifty pages will command respect from all who read them." What it IS: the argument and the method declared in the opening, not earned gradually. **Takeaway:** make the first chapter carry the whole book's weight, omen first, purpose stated plainly.

## M4. The Map as Argument
Make the reader look at a dated map and feel its blankness, so geography does the arguing. The January 1, 1846 map: Texas annexed three days, Oregon jointly occupied, California and New Mexico Mexican, and the blank paper between known marks lettered "Great American Desert." In The Course of Empire the spine is the 278-year search for the Northwest Passage, "how the reality of exploration measured up to the imagined lands," with hydrology -- above all the Missouri River -- channeling empire: "If the river had not been there, the white man would doubtless have gone west..." (Bakeless, Minnesota History). The maps by Erwin Raisz were called "the best maps I have ever seen in a history book." What it IS: the map as the thesis, the blank as the provocation. **Takeaway:** show the dated map with its blanks, and let the geography argue before the prose does.

## M5. The Trade as a Business and a Way of Life
Render an industry as an economic system, with the romantic figure inside it as an economic actor. Across the Wide Missouri: the Rocky Mountain fur trade of the 1830s was "a business and a way of life" (DeVoto's preface) -- the Rocky Mountain Fur Company against John Jacob Astor's American Fur Company and the Hudson's Bay Company; the annual summer rendezvous, a different mountain valley each year, supplied by overland wagon train, as the recurring set-piece; free trappers unable to get out of debt to the companies that both bought their furs and sold them their supplies; beaver over-hunted and fashion changed, and the trade collapsed. The mountain men, by exploring the plateau and setting up trade networks, "shaped the materialism that typifies modern American society." The book itself grew out of a commission: Houghton Mifflin hired DeVoto to write 20,000 words of scholarly captions for a book of reproductions of 100-plus Alfred Jacob Miller watercolors. What it IS: the ledger under the legend, the market under the myth. **Takeaway:** show the money moving -- who buys, who owes, who supplies -- and the romance becomes a system.

## M6. The Representative-Man Portrait
Fix a frontiersman with a biographical set-piece that treats him as a classical figure, and tell a whole life as an outline of American history in a few pages. Chapter 2 of The Year of Decision, "The Mountain Man," gives James Clyman's life-outline, ending "Odysseus was back in Ithaca." Croke: Ashley's men are "Caesar and Alexander in buckskins, with himself as their Plutarch." Topping: DeVoto wrote "prose that sings," presenting "characters that are among the most vividly portrayed in all historical literature." The risk is on the record too: Topping warns the "hyperventilating enthusiasm often dragged DeVoto over the line between vividness and distortion," his figures appearing "as mere caricatures." What it IS: one life, told classically, standing for an era -- with the distortion risk named, not hidden. **Takeaway:** give one man the classical analogy and the whole era rides on his back.

## M7. The Three-Layer Stack
Build the book as three workers standing on one another's shoulders: scrupulous gatherer of facts at the bottom, impressionistic-fiction storyteller in the middle, Easy Chair controversialist on top. Stegner's image: "When he applied the methods of impressionistic fiction to history, mounting upon the scrupulous gatherer of facts the storyteller skilled in mass entertainment; and when he mounted upon those, like an acrobat on the shoulders of two fellow acrobats, the social analyst and controversialist and editorial thinker of The Easy Chair, he had a combination that might look unstable and even self-contradictory, but that worked." EBSCO: "He used his novelistic techniques to bring history to life and give it the plot of an intense novel." The polemic voice delivers the punchline -- "get out and give us more money" -- that the facts alone could not. What it IS: fact, fiction-method and polemic in one unstable stack that holds. **Takeaway:** let the facts carry the storyteller, and let the controversialist land the punchline the facts earned.

## M8. Writing History Backward
Write the trilogy in reverse chronological order of subject, digging backward toward the origin. The Year of Decision (1846) came first, 1943; then the 1830s fur trade, Across the Wide Missouri, 1947; then 278 years of exploration ending at Lewis and Clark, The Course of Empire, 1952. Mattingly joked DeVoto suffered "a severe case of regressus historicus -- the irrepressible urge to search [for the] end." Croke notes the cost: in The Course of Empire "Gone is 'History as Synecdoche'... DeVoto drops his favorite device in favor of the full view," and the book reads "like a highbrow textbook," "lacking the literary sparkle of Across the Wide Missouri." What it IS: each book answering the question the previous one left behind it. **Takeaway:** write the end first, then dig backward for the cause -- and know the device may not survive the widening.

## M9. The Primary-Document Voice
Quote the participants verbatim, misspellings and all, and let the document carry the scene. Clyman's diary, quoted with its spelling kept: "This stone shews us that all ages and all sects are found to undertake this long tedious and even dangerous Journy..." Hughes's eagle story is flagged as "doubtless more beautiful than true" -- the doubt stated, the story kept. The Lewis and Clark condensation is the same craft at editorial scale: eight volumes cut to one without losing the journals' voice, Clark's spelling preserved ("one of the worst spellers in American history"). What it IS: the archive speaking in its own accent, with the historian's doubt in the margin. **Takeaway:** quote the document as written, doubt and all, and the past sounds like itself.

## M10. The Plundered-Province Argument
Tell the West as a colony: a sequence of resource raids, wealth siphoned east, the West left impoverished -- and then turn the argument on the West itself. "The West: A Plundered Province" (1934) won him the Easy Chair; "The West Against Itself" (1947) is the double-length sequel, read in full in this study. The sequence: furs (beaver cleaned out by 1840, "a century later, beaver has not yet come back"), mining ("Mining is liquidation. You clean out the deposit, exhaust the lode, and move on"), oil and gas, cattle, lumber. "The East has always held a mortgage on the permanent West, channeling its wealth eastward, maintaining it in a debtor status, and confining its economic function to that of a mercantilist province." Then the turn: "The West does not want to be liberated from the system of exploitation that it has always violently resented. It only wants to buy into it." The punchline: "It shakes down to a platform: get out and give us more money." "The West as its own worst enemy. The West committing suicide." What it IS: victimhood argued, then reversed into complicity. **Takeaway:** name the sequence of raids, then show the victim buying into the system that raids it.

## M11. The Demythologizing Rebuttal
Take a received explanation and dismantle it piece by piece before replacing it with a messier, multi-cause account. On Texas in chapter 1 of The Year of Decision: the received story is "the naive mythology called economic determinism" -- "It makes a pretty picture and most separate parts of it are in some degree true, but the picture is false." In "Fossil Remnants of the Frontier" (Harper's, April 1935) he announces himself as "the dry-eyed demolitions expert armed with the facts and determined to blow sky-high every fable, figment, or fabrication of the Western past." His quarrel was with frontier fable, not with Frederick Jackson Turner, whose thesis he defended (see C2). What it IS: demolition as a first act, replacement as the second. **Takeaway:** take the pretty picture apart piece by piece, then build the messier true one.

## M12. The Literary-Fallacy Polemic
Name a fallacy, list its victims, and attack the critical establishment from the position of the Westerner. The Literary Fallacy (1944): the mistaken notion that a country's character can be determined by analyses of its literature -- hence the claim that America had failed to produce great literature was an indictment of American culture as a whole. The named victims: Anderson, Brooks, Fitzgerald, Mencken, Lewis, Hemingway, Eliot. Eli Siegel's 1932 review of Mark Twain's America: "He is the West intellectually rising at the East... His book should start a much-needed and extensive row." The same move in "Genius Is Not Enough" (1936): Wolfe's novels "hacked and shaped and compressed into something resembling a novel by Mr. Perkins and the assembly-line at Scribners" -- an attack on Perkins's editorial authorship by proxy, which helped break Wolfe from Scribners months before his death. Reception was hostile: Partisan Review called The Literary Fallacy "as vicious and mindless a tract as any so far produced." What it IS: the essay as a demolition, the Westerner as the prosecutor. **Takeaway:** name the fallacy, name the names, and let the row be the point.

## M13. Negative Space as a Character
Let the blank do dramatic work: the unknown sketched as a long litany of names on empty paper. Chapter 2, "The Mountain Man": "Substance begins when we mention some names" -- the long litany of rivers (Pecos, Gila, Rio Grande, Colorado of the West) and ranges (Flatheads, Uintas) sketched onto blank paper from a station above Long's Peak. The known marks are few; the paper between them is the subject. "They argued it, were willing to send men to fight for it... And knew nothing about it." What it IS: absence staged as a presence, the map's emptiness as the drama. **Takeaway:** list the few known names on a blank field and let the blank carry the tension.

## M14. Precipitated Inevitability
Treat the year as a chemical crucible: a brief period in which contingency converges into the inevitable. Chapter 1: "Sometimes there are exceedingly brief periods which determine a long future. A moment of time holds in solution ingredients which might combine in any of several or many ways, and then another moment precipitates out of the possible the at last determined thing." 1846 is that moment: in one calendar year the United States became a continental nation, expansion forced the slavery question into the open and made the Civil War inevitable, and the empire created in 1846 "doomed the idea of a loose affiliation of states." What it IS: chance held in solution, then precipitated -- the year as the hinge of the century. **Takeaway:** hold the ingredients in solution through the year, then precipitate them in one scene.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife so far
Phase one, the novelist and magazine writer, 1922-1932. English instructor at Northwestern, then Harvard; five serious novels under his own name (The Crooked Mile 1924, The Chariot of Fire 1926, The House of Sun-Goes-Down 1928, We Accept With Pleasure 1934, Mountain Time 1947) and potboilers as John August to pay the bills; the Pareto articles in Harper's (1933) that made his name there. Croke's verdict: the six own-name novels are "all competent, bland, and forgettable," and "DeVoto was such a great historical writer in part because he was such a mediocre novelist." Phase two, the critic and editor, 1932-1938. Mark Twain's America (1932), the breakthrough; the Easy Chair from November 1935; editor of the Saturday Review of Literature 1936-1938; "Genius Is Not Enough" (1936) and the Wolfe feud. Phase three, the historian and conservation polemicist, 1938-1955. Twain Estate curator 1938-1946 and Mark Twain at Work (1942); the 1940 western auto trip with Arthur Schlesinger Jr. to research the trilogy; The Year of Decision (1943); The Literary Fallacy (1944) as the last big critical statement; Across the Wide Missouri (1947, Pulitzer), The Course of Empire (1952, National Book Award), the Lewis and Clark condensation (1953); the conservation campaign from the 1946 road trip onward, some twenty articles; speechwriter for Adlai Stevenson in 1952; Western Paradox unfinished at death, two-thirds drafted. The afterlife so far: the Memorial Cedar Grove dedicated 1962; Stegner's The Uneasy Chair (1974) and The Letters of Bernard DeVoto (1975); John L. Thomas, A Country in the Mind (2000); Gary Topping, Utah Historians and the Reconstruction of Western History (2003); The Western Paradox published 2001, edited by Douglas Brinkley and Patricia Nelson Limerick; DeVoto's West (2002/2005, see C6); Nate Schweber, This America of Ours (2022), the dual biography that revived DeVoto as a conservationist and reframed McCarthyism as McCarranism. The arc is a slow turn from fiction to fact: the novelist who could not make novels live became the historian who made history read like a novel, and the polemicist who made conservation a national argument.

## Aesthetic logic
The book and the life share one aesthetic: the outsider tells the story straight, and the truth is the constraint on the story, not its enemy. DeVoto grew up accepted by neither of his parents' faiths -- an apostate Catholic father, a lapsed Mormon mother -- in a town that came to hate him for lampooning it, and the exile never left him: "it troubled him all his life that he was hated in his home state" (Stegner). The outsider's position is the method: he writes from the West against the East, from experience against theory -- "I distrust absolutes. Rather, I long ago passed from distrust of them to opposition. And with them let me include prophecy, simplification, generalization, abstract logic, and especially the habit of mind which consult theory first and experience only afterward" (Saturday Review editorial, 13 February 1937). "You see, it's the concrete thing, the living thing, that I go for, and the abstraction from it repels me." Everything else follows: the synecdoche (the concrete thing that carries the whole), the map (the concrete ground), the trade (the concrete money), the document quoted verbatim (the concrete voice), the polemic (the concrete fight). The three-layer stack is the same logic from the other side: the facts are real (the archives, the diaries, the 241 columns) precisely so that the story can be told like a novel and the punchline can land like a blow. The pupil inverts the master without breaking from him: Mattingly took DeVoto's simultaneity and his curtain-raiser and played every scene; DeVoto took Turner's frontier frame and stripped out the fable while keeping the frame. The aesthetic is a refusal: no theory in place of the story, no abstraction in place of the concrete thing, no footnote in place of the scene -- and no mercy for the critic who reads a country out of its books without knowing the country.

## Load-bearing corrections
C0. The doorway HOLDS with one second-hand caveat. The sentence "When you get a scene, play it" is reported via Prabook and the Mattingly biographical tradition, not from a DeVoto text. Do not quote it as a verified DeVoto utterance; quote it as reported speech, with the source named. Everything else in the doorway -- the friendship, the coaching, the simultaneity loan, the Renaissance Diplomacy dedication -- is verified, the dedication from the primary text.
C1. The brief was wrong about The Hour. It is The Hour: A Cocktail Manifesto (1951), a nonfiction book about drinking -- NOT a novel, NOT 1950. The late novel is Mountain Time. The martini line is real: "You can no more keep a martini in the refrigerator than you can keep a kiss there. The proper union of gin and vermouth is a great and sudden glory; it is one of the happiest marriages on earth and one of the shortest-lived."
C2. DeVoto defended Turner; he did not challenge him. The brief asked for a challenge to the frontier thesis; the evidence points the other way (Gastronomica, "The Map and the Menu," 2023: DeVoto's "defense of Frederick Jackson Turner's influential essay"). His quarrel was with frontier fable, not with Turner: in "Fossil Remnants of the Frontier" he is "the dry-eyed demolitions expert armed with the facts and determined to blow sky-high every fable, figment, or fabrication of the Western past," while keeping Turner's westward-expansion frame. The revision of Turner belongs to his successors, above all Patricia Nelson Limerick.
C3. The Literary Fallacy is not an attack on the New Criticism by name. No source found ties it to the New Criticism; it is an attack on the 1920s literary intelligentsia and on reading national character out of literature. Do not attribute a New Criticism attack without a further source.
C4. The Year of Decision: 1846 -- 1942 vs 1943. The Preface is dated 12 February 1942 and the copyright is 1942; the first edition is Little, Brown 1943 (National Library of Ireland MARC, bernarddevoto.org, Wikipedia prose, Book-of-the-Month Club selection 1943). The widespread 1942 dating (Wikipedia's own works list, Mapping Literary Utah, Utah Historical Society, the Stanford chronology) is a copyright-vs-publication artifact. This study gives 1943 as publication year with the 1942 copyright noted, and does not assert either unqualified.
C5. Mountain Time -- 1946 vs 1947. 1947 per bernarddevoto.org, Britannica, Prabook, the Utah Historical Society novels list and the Stanford chronology; 1946 per Wikipedia's works list and Mapping Literary Utah. This study gives 1947, noting the 1946 variant.
C6. DeVoto's West -- 2002 vs 2005. 2002 per Wikipedia and Mapping Literary Utah; 2005 per the Penn Libraries catalogue (Swallow Press / Ohio University Press, c2005, 1st ed.), the Utah Historical Society bibliography and Rewilding.org. Possibly 2002 was an announcement or an earlier edition and 2005 the Swallow/Ohio UP edition. This study leans 2005 for the Ohio UP edition and flags the dispute; verify against WorldCat before stating either unqualified.
C7. Prabook's header gives the death date as "January 13, 1955." Wrong; its own body text says November 13, 1955, and every other source agrees. Do not cite Prabook's header.
C8. Land-grab acreage: Taking Bearings says McCarran's plan covered about 146 million BLM acres; the Yellowstonian says "as many as 230 million acres of public lands." Different scopes (BLM-only vs the whole scheme); do not merge into one number.
C9. The Across the Wide Missouri origin: the Miller-captions commission is attested by Croke, but the patron's name was truncated in the source read. Do not name her without a second source.
C10. The AZ Quotes attributions without a book cited -- the wilderness line ("It is imperative to maintain portions of the wilderness untouched..."), the 100th-meridian line ("The West begins where the average annual rainfall drops below twenty inches..."), "History abhors determinism but cannot tolerate chance," and "The best reason for putting anything down on paper is that one may then change it" -- are aggregator attributions. Verify against a DeVoto text before using any of them as a headline quote. The Hour martini line and the violet-hour line are better attested; the violet-hour line has an "affections/affectations" variant -- the book text per libquotes is "affections."
C11. The "Fossil Remnants of the Frontier" passage on the American Indian of legend ("I have not found him a beauty lover...") is the ugliest material found; DeVoto himself anticipates the charge ("Call it racism and philistinism, if you must"). Quote it only with that context; do not present it as a neutral aphorism.
C12. No book by DeVoto was read cover to cover in any leg. Read in full text: The Year of Decision Preface and chapter 1 (pp. 3-14) and chapter 2 in part (pp. 49-55), and "The West Against Itself" (509 of 563 lines). Everything else is via reviews, encyclopedias and critical essays. The World of Fiction and Mountain Time are credit-level only (no contents or plot established). The exact birth day 11 January 1897 and death day 13 November 1955 rest on Wikipedia, Britannica, bernarddevoto.org and the Utah Historical Society; no birth or death certificate was seen in any leg. Do not state either date as primary-source verified.
C13. Ogden teaching duration: "two years" (bernarddevoto.org) vs "a year" (Utah Historical Society) vs November 1921-June 1922 (Prabook). Treat as: he taught at North Junior High in 1921-1922 within a two-year Ogden sojourn (1920-1922). Move to Massachusetts: "Cambridge" in most sources vs "Lincoln, Mass." in the Stanford chronology; the family home was in Lincoln, Cambridge is shorthand. This study says Lincoln/Cambridge, Massachusetts.
C14. Mark Twain, Letters from the Earth: Wikipedia's works list dates it "(1938), editor" -- that is when DeVoto edited the manuscript, not publication (1962). Do not list it as a 1938 publication.
C15. The John August potboilers: bernarddevoto.org says DeVoto contracted in 1938 to write "the second of five" potboiler novels for Collier's under the pseudonym John August, but only four are universally listed (Troubled Star 1939, Rain Before Seven 1940, Advance Agent 1942, The Woman in the Picture 1944). The fifth is unidentified; Life Begins So Soon (1936) is a candidate but predates the 1938 contract and its pseudonym attribution is not established. Do not state "five John August novels" with titles unless the fifth is verified. Likewise Writer's Handbook (1927) and "Americana Deserta" (1930) rest on the Stanford chronology alone; verify before stating. "Strange Fruit" under 1944 in the Stanford chronology is almost certainly DeVoto's review of Lillian Smith's novel, not a book of his.
C16. This study confirms #475's M2 from the other side. #475 recorded Mattingly's simultaneity as borrowed from DeVoto on EBSCO's word; this study reads DeVoto's own Preface stating the method in his own words ("the actual narrative is always rigorously chronological... I range forward and backward in time as far as the end in view requires"), and the Renaissance Diplomacy dedication and foreword confirming the DeVoto-Mattingly coaching from the primary text. The doorway of #476 is closed on primary evidence, not only on secondary report.
