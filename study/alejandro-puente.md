# Alejandro Puente: Study Notes

**Site:** Alejandro Puente, born La Plata, Argentina, 1933 (no day or month found anywhere), died Buenos Aires, 2013 (no exact date found), Argentine painter, the self-taught platense who co-created "geometria sensible" with Cesar Paternosto (study #638) and then spent forty years proving that a geometry could be human: first the colour carried to the very edge of the stretcher so the painting becomes an object the viewer must walk around; then, in New York, a system in which one repeated module and one rule about colour generate unlimited objects; then, back in Argentina, the recognition that the system he had invented was already an Andean one -- the quipu, the stepped greca, the textile grid -- and the long late work of building paintings the way temples are built, from an initial geometric structure outward, with a soft brushstroke laid over the rigid matrix like a weave (C9761, C9766).

## The life: self-taught, the group of two, New York, the Americanist turn

Alejandro Puente was born in La Plata in 1933. The day and month are not found anywhere, and the birthplace itself carries a small wobble: the consensus is La Plata, a 2008 municipal decree says Ensenada, the adjacent port city, which is reconcilable, and one Arte al Dia profile says Buenos Aires, treated here as a single-source error. He was self-taught -- "autodidacta" -- and the one correction this study must carry in its opening paragraph is that three biographical facts that look like his are not his at all: the law degree of 1958, the painting lessons with Jorge R. Mieri from 1956, and Hector Cartier's "mini-Bauhaus" of 1957-1959 belong to Cesar Paternosto, study #638, verbatim from that study's leg 1. No Puente source mentions a law degree or Mieri. What Puente did do was attend Cartier's Teoria de la Vision course at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes in La Plata as a free student, "alumno libre", with no dated attendance span attested, and learn canvas preparation from Salvador Stringa, the Italian painter-restorer at the Museo Provincial de Bellas Artes de La Plata. Cartier is the shared teacher -- both men called him theirs -- but the dated items must not enter Puente's timeline (C9766).

La Plata matters to the work the way it mattered to Paternosto's: a city founded in 1882, the first modern city designed on a drawing board, its plan a square grid of blocks criss-crossed by diagonals at 45 degrees. Puente co-founded Grupo Si there, constituted in 1960, first museum show 1961 at the Museo Provincial de Bellas Artes de La Plata and then at MAMBA; the group's phase was gestural informalism, and almost all of the early Grupo Si work was lost or destroyed by the artist himself. Puente invited Paternosto into the group around 1960. The two scholarly rosters of Grupo Si disagree in detail -- Gustavino 2026 lists 17 names, Monkes and Pino 2017 list 16, with "Cesar Blanco" against "Nelson Blanco", "Painceira" against "Panceira", "Ambrossini" against "Ambrosini", Gancedo only in Monkes, Vaca, Rivas and Larralde only in Gustavino -- and the disagreement is carried, not smoothed.

In October 1964 the pair showed together at Galeria Lirolay, Buenos Aires, as "Nueva geometria: Paternosto y Puente", with a catalogue prologue by Aldo Pellegrini coining "geometria sensible", and the two were nicknamed "el grupo de dos". In 1965 came the second joint Lirolay series, in which Puente extended form and colour to the stretcher edges, the cantos, and the paintings became objects the viewer must move around; in 1966 the joint show at Galeria Bonino, with a Jorge Romero Brest essay -- "parece un cuadro y no es un cuadro, parece un objeto y no es un objeto", a portable mural. The Premio Nacional Di Tella 1966 was a participation, not a win, the same caution as #638. In 1967 the pair were among the seven signatories of the "La vision elemental" manifesto at the MNBA, and Puente won a Guggenheim Fellowship on letters from Sam Hunter and Clement Greenberg. He went to New York in 1967 and stayed until 1971 -- Revista Magenta and a MoMA record give 1968-1971, and the study presents 1967-1971 with the 1968-start variant noted; PROA's "more than three decades" in New York is an error, a confusion with Paternosto, who stayed until 2004. In New York he showed Todo vale in Information at MoMA in 1970, curated by Kynaston McShine; met Sol LeWitt, whom he had first met in Buenos Aires; worked with Lucy Lippard; studied Albers.

He returned to Argentina in 1971, settled in Buenos Aires, and the Americanist turn began: the quipu, the stepped greca, the textile grid, the temple facade. He represented Argentina at the XVIII Bienal de Sao Paulo in 1985 and was named Academico de Numero of the Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes the same year, the latter single-source. He taught painting at the Universidad Nacional de La Plata and at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes "Ernesto de la Carcova" in the 1980s, exact years not found; the posthumous homage Huellas sensibles at the Museo de Calcos y Escultura Comparada in 2014 was explicitly "en homenaje a su labor docente". His dealer was Galeria Ruth Benzacar from 1980; the estate gallery is Henrique Faria / Herlitzka and Faria. He co-curated "Ojo al Pais" with Luis Felipe Noe in 2001-04, sat on the MNBA advisory council in 2006, painted the mural Homenaje al artista artesano at the Museo de Arte Popular "Jose Hernandez" in 2007, and was named Ciudadano Ilustre de la Provincia de Buenos Aires in 2008. He died in Buenos Aires in 2013; no exact date, cause or obituary was located. His wife was Hilda Abelleira de Puente, thanked in the TAREA article acknowledgements; no further family detail was found.

The doctrine, in one line from leg 1: a humanised geometry -- elementary geometric forms as a neutral stage on which colour, treated as a grammatical system, and the texture of the support act, with the maker's touch preserved -- first against concrete art's industrial finish, then re-rooted in Andean textile and architectural systems.

## M1 -- Colour carried to the frame edge (1965)

In 1965 Puente extended shape and colour to the frame. The colour does not stop at an internal border and does not respect the window of the picture; it runs out to the physical edge and onto the cantos, the sides of the stretcher, so the painting stops being a window and becomes an object, and the viewer has to walk around it to see it whole. W02 Pintura 1965, acrylic on canvas, 96 x 128 cm, is the hinge: Rossi's account is that he "extendio la forma y el color hasta los cantos del bastidor". W04 Untitled 1967, acrylic on canvas, 150 x 176 cm, acquired by the Met in 2016 through Henrique Faria, does the same at large scale -- the Arte al Dia notice says Puente "experiments with the organization of shapes and colors on the surface, extending the color to the edges of the canvas" -- and Rossi notes that "those modules with color accents on the edges invaded the space, breaking bidimensionality". Watchable behaviour: colour visible from oblique angles; the sides of the support are painted, not bare. The mechanism is the bridge between the two studies in this pair: Paternosto moved the picture to the side of the stretcher and left the front blank; Puente carried the colour to the edge and kept the front full. Same year, same city, same problem -- where does a painting end -- opposite answers.

**Takeaway:** the edge is not a frame, it is the end of the picture. A piece that draws a border inside the canvas has missed the mechanism; the colour must run out to the physical edge and onto the sides, and the viewer must have to move to see it.

## M2 -- The sensible brushstroke (geometria sensible)

Pellegrini's 1964 prologue is the doctrine in one sentence: "la figura geometrica deja de ser un elemento activo para convertirse solo en el soporte pasivo... sobre el cual actuan los verdaderos personajes del cuadro: el color y la textura" -- the geometric figure stops being an active element and becomes only the passive support on which the real characters of the picture act: colour and texture. The elementary geometric form is a neutral stage; colour and the texture of the support are the actors; the maker's trace is preserved against concrete art's industrial finish. Rossi's verdict: "a humanized geometry that aimed to overcome the rigidity of the neo-plasticist approach". Puente himself: "I did not endorse... the precepts or the productions of Argentinean concrete art; I thought they were too closely related to technology." The mechanism shows in the whole 1964-65 Lirolay turn, from the gestural informalism of W01 Pintura 1961, oil on jute, 195 x 172 cm, MAMBA, to the simple shapes of W02, where the brushstroke modulates the colour instead of disappearing into a flat plane. The geometry is simple on purpose, because the simplicity is what lets the colour and the hand be seen.

**Takeaway:** the shape is the stage, not the actor. A piece that makes the geometry clever and the colour flat has the mechanism backwards; the shape must be elementary and the colour and texture must do the work, with the hand visible in the paint.

## M3 -- Module, system, object

Puente's New York sentence, the one the whole 1966-71 phase hangs on: "I began to base my work not on the object itself but on a system which, through the repetition of modular elements, would create the object." One repeated modular element generates the object; the system is a generator of rules, not a totality. W03 Estructura 1966, acrylic on canvas, 190 x 160 x 200 cm, Blanton, is three panels become one floor object -- the painting leaves the wall. W06 Cubo 1967 is a walk-in modular structure, listed in Elementos Modulares. W07 Sistema (Ele), also called Sistema Modular, 1968, is six L-shaped metal elements with a preparatory drawing: "an L-shaped module spread over a large white table, generating a formal sequence. Each shape is painted according to a system, a value scale that tends towards white". The corollary, also his: "The artist becomes a 'designer of systems that make objects' instead of a 'designer of objects'." And the further corollary, the one that makes the system a piece rather than a picture: "What is important about it is the idea: others can take care of the execution. Once established a combination, I could as well give the problem for a computer to determinate the amount of possible relations." Note the caution carried in the catalogue: Sistema (Ele) 1968 and the Sistema Modular L-module on a white table may be two presentations of one system or two related works; the sources do not settle it, so they are not merged here.

**Takeaway:** the module is the work and the object is its output. A piece that draws one finished shape has missed the mechanism; the piece must define one module and one rule, and then let the repetition build the object, so the viewer can see the rule working.

## M4 -- Colour as language: the Todo vale imperative

Colour, for Puente, has grammatical and syntactical properties in and of itself -- the words are written inside Todo vale. The work states one imperative and generates unlimited objects from it: "To bring primary and secondary colors to a whiter value." W09 Todo vale (Everything Goes), 1968-70, shown in Information at MoMA in 1970, is an installation of pigments, liquid colours, shaped canvases and modular sculptures, with the materials themselves -- jars, pigments, notes, maps -- exhibited as part of the work. MoMA's label: "limited combinatory rules capable of producing an unlimited number of artistic objects". The paper laboratory sheets are the grammar tables: W05 The Primarys 1967, gouache and pencil on paper, 43.8 x 55.9 cm, MoMA object 1679.2009; W08 Sistemas Cromaticos 1968, watercolour and pencil on paper, MoMA object 1876.2008, listing-only; W10 The Birren's Triangles 1970, pencil and gouache on paper, MoMA object 1879.2008, listing-only. The palette of the New York years is unmixed primaries and secondaries, black as anchor, white as the direction of the system, flat, pure, hard-edged; Burucua's phrase for the colour is that it suggests "colours of strong pulses and heartbeats, never languors". The borrowed systems behind the grammar are reported, not his words: Scriabin's synesthetic colour-sound correspondences, Munsell colour studies and Weber-Fechner laws for the grid modulations.

**Takeaway:** one sentence is enough, if it is a rule. A piece that shows many colours has missed the mechanism; the piece must state one imperative about colour and then generate visibly different objects from it, so the rule, not the palette, is what the viewer watches.

## M5 -- The textile grid: warp and weft as code

After the 1971 return the homogeneous grid becomes the layout of textiles, with symbolic borders, and chromatic sequences take garment form. The stepped greca, the fret, becomes the central motif. W12 Uncu, also spelled Unku, 1973, is a chromatic sequence in the format of an Inca tunic; the 2018 Henrique Faria show took its name from it. W13 Yayahuala 1978, acrylic on canvas, 150 x 150 cm, Burucua figure 1. Chicama 1978, Atacama 1979 and Cuismancu 1980 carry the luminous greca to what Burucua calls its apotheosis, "a celestial staircase... without visible beginning or end, extended diagonally". W15 Cuadriculado (Squared) 1981, watercolour on cardboard, 41 x 31 cm, listing-only, is the grid as a named subject. Watchable behaviour: grid cells as woven units; a stepped diagonal that never terminates, entering at one edge and leaving at another; borders that are part of the code, not decoration. The grid here is not a ruler, it is a loom.

**Takeaway:** the grid is woven, not drawn. A piece that fills a grid with colour has missed the mechanism; the cells must behave like woven units and the stepped diagonal must run without beginning or end, so the picture reads as a textile that happens to be a painting.

## M6 -- Diluted colour soaking into lean canvas: matte ethics

The handling changes decisively in the late 1970s, and the change is a moral choice as much as a technical one. The colour is applied very diluted; part of it penetrates, dyeing the canvas; the composition starts from a grid executed in graphite, which in some planes appears above the final colour; the surface is matte. W14 Mancapa 1979, acrylic on canvas, 85 x 125 cm, MAMBA, listing-only, is the locus classicus -- Monkes's description is quoted in the previous sentence. W18 Paicha 1988, acrylic on canvas, 140 x 115 cm, is the same handling a decade on. Puente's own words: "Yo siempre tuve rechazo a los artificios... si hay artificios, deja de ser verdadero" -- I always rejected artifice; if there is artifice, it stops being true. Fevre's phrase for the result is "una metafisica de la materia", a metaphysics of matter. The undyed canvas shows through; the graphite grid shows through; the matte surface refuses shine. Against concrete art's industrial finish, the finish here is the absence of finish.

**Takeaway:** the paint must soak, not sit. A piece with glossy opaque colour on a sealed ground has missed the mechanism; the colour must dye the cloth, the under-drawing must show through in places, and the surface must stay matte, because the honesty is in the material, not in the image.

## M7 -- The quipu: colour, knot and sequence as information

The 1971 pivot, and the moment the New York system recognises itself as an Andean one. W11 Sistemas Cromaticos Primarios y Secundarios 1971 is coloured knotted threads in quipu format: "echoes the colored and knotted string quipus of the Inca, which were used to record and transmit information" (Henrique Faria). Colour plus knot plus sequence equals meaning; the system leaves paper and canvas and becomes a recording device, not a picture. Watchable behaviour: a sequence of coloured knots that reads like a sentence, the length and position of each knot carrying the information, the whole thing hangable and legible in order. This is the mechanism that turns M4's grammar into a technology: colour had grammar in New York; in Buenos Aires it turns out the grammar already existed, tied in string, centuries before.

**Takeaway:** the sequence is the sentence. A piece that hangs coloured threads as decoration has missed the mechanism; the knots must be in an order, the order must be readable, and the reading must be the point -- the system records something, it does not merely show something.

## M8 -- Architecture built from an initial geometric structure

The late work is built the way a temple is built: from an initial geometric structure outward. W19 Uculla 2000, acrylic on canvas, 163 x 249 cm, Burucua figure 4, is the type -- "temple facades... proceeded from some initial geometrical structure as the basis for composing the rest of the work" (Jacome-Moreno). W20 Ruyala 2001, acrylic on canvas, 45 x 59.9 cm, is the same architecture at small scale, though Rossi calls its group large format, an unresolved tension carried in the corrections. Kathino 1989, Luan 1994, Huaraz 1997, Huacalera and Uquia 1998, Tanka 2000 carry Mapuche, Inca and Maya titles over the same construction. The rigid constructive matrix is filtered, in Rossi's phrase, "by a weave of color created by the soft brushstroke in overlapping layers" -- the brushstroke is now itself a weave, M2 returning inside M8. The late variant, the interior-spaces series of 2004-06, puts two perspectives at once on cut-out frames and simulates a third dimension: the 1965 problem, the viewer must move, returns as an interior. The 2007 mural, Homenaje al artista artesano, is the public coda: a tribute to the artisan, that is, to making itself.

**Takeaway:** the painting is a building, not a picture. A piece that arranges temple-like shapes decoratively has missed the mechanism; the work must start from one geometric structure and grow outward from it, the soft overlapping brushstroke veiling the rigid matrix, so the facade is the result of a construction, not a motif.

## Evolution: informalism, the edge, the system, the textile, the temple

1961-65: gestural informalism on jute, a handmade wooden knife, strong contrast (W01) gives way at Lirolay to simple shapes and colour modulated by the sensible brushstroke; by 1965 colour runs to the stretcher edge and the painting becomes an object (W02). The Renaissance window is broken from inside. 1966-71: system, module, colour as language, in New York. The painting leaves the wall (W03, a floor object); 1967 is the densest year in the catalogue (W04, W05, W06); oil and enamel give way to acrylic; the brushstroke is minimised "to the point of its virtual disappearance"; the system is the work (W07-W10); Todo vale states the rule in one sentence and generates unlimited objects. 1971 is the pivot (W11): the system is recognised as an Andean information system, quipu, colour plus knot plus sequence. 1973-88: grid, textile, material. The homogeneous grid as the layout of textiles with symbolic borders; chromatic sequences in uncu format (W12); the stepped greca as central motif; the handling changes to diluted acrylic soaking into leanly primed absorbent canvas, graphite grid showing through, matte surface (W14); materials diversify -- feathers (an untitled 1985 work, feathers on jute, 40 x 30 cm), cork on chipboard (W17 Nazca 1987, 32 x 40.9 cm), incised wood, knotted threads. The module stays strict and the hand gets loose (W16 Ancasti 1982, W18 Paicha 1988). 1989-2006: architecture, then interior space. Titles in Mapuche, Inca and Maya words; paintings built like temple facades from an initial geometric structure outward (W19); the brushstroke now a weave; the 2004-06 interior spaces hold two perspectives at once on cut-out frames; the 2007 mural is the coda. The square format is not the constant -- the module is, and the module survives every change of material, scale and continent.

## Aesthetic logic

Puente's aesthetic is the logic of the system that admits the hand. Pick one simple geometric form, so simple it cannot be the subject, and let colour and texture be the subject instead; then let one module and one rule generate the object; then notice that the rule you invented is older than you are, tied in string and woven in wool and built in stone centuries before modernism, and follow it home. The geometry is sensible because it is made by a hand that leaves its trace in the paint, dilutes the colour until it dyes the cloth, lets the graphite grid show through, and refuses the industrial finish; the colour is grammatical because a small set of relations -- primaries and secondaries brought up to white, tone and value varied step by step -- can generate unlimited objects; the system is Andean because the quipu already recorded information in colour and knot and sequence, and the greca already ran without beginning or end, and the temple already grew from one geometric structure. That is why the work keeps returning to the same move in new materials, oil on jute, acrylic on canvas, gouache on paper, L-shaped metal, knotted thread, feathers, cork on chipboard: the support changes, the system does not. A Puente that paints a clever geometric pattern has not simplified him; it has kept the stage and sacked the actors. The colour must reach the edge, the module must repeat, the rule must be statable in one sentence, and the hand must show.

The contrast with Paternosto is the study's spine, and it is a contrast inside one friendship, between two men of the same city who shared a show, a group and a critic. Paternosto's generator is a band pushed to the edge, then a blank front, then a perimeter; Puente's is a module repeated until it becomes an object, then a colour rule that generates unlimited objects, then a textile system. Paternosto's depth comes from displacement, the picture moved to where the eye does not expect it; Puente's presence comes from generation, the object built by a rule the viewer can follow. Paternosto painted on the side of the stretcher and removed frontality; Puente carried the colour to the edge and kept the front full, and then built the front like a temple. Both asked how a flat painting becomes an object, and Paternosto answered by moving the picture, Puente by making a system that makes the object. That is why the two studies belong together, and why Puente is the complement, not the follower: same generation, same city, same rooms, the same 1964 show -- and from that shared room, two opposite mechanisms.
