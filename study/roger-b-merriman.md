**Site:** Roger Bigelow Merriman (1876-1945), Harvard historian of the Spanish Empire -- born Wednesday 24 May 1876 in Boston, Massachusetts, the only child of the Rev. Daniel Merriman, minister of the Central Congregational Church, Worcester, and the artist and collector Helen Bigelow Merriman, granddaughter of the weaving-machinery inventor Erastus Brigham Bigelow; Harvard College, A.B. ## M1. The Long Fuse
The empire as the continuation of the Middle Ages, not the accident of conquest. Merriman's thesis, in Mattingly's formulation, is that "the Spanish Empire, unlike its British rival, was the natural continuation of Spain's medieval history"; in Merriman's own Introduction, "The Spanish Empire of Ferdinand and Isabella and their successors has its origins in the earliest periods of antiquity. Far more than the British Empire, to which it has often been compared, it is linked with the history and traditions of the past." The whole first volume is backstory before the empire exists: the medieval kingdoms, the Reconquista, the union of the crowns, and only then the crossing to America, which Keen's 1962 summary calls "a sequel to Spain's empire-building in the Mediterranean and Atlantic". What it IS: the thesis that makes eight centuries of prologue into the argument itself. Watchable behaviour: many small streams -- Castile, Aragon, Catalonia, Granada, the Canaries -- flowing separately across a map for a long time, then converging into one river; the empire appears only when the streams meet, and the meeting is the point, not the conquest that follows.

## M2. The Cross-Section
A single reign cut open to show the whole subject at once. Trenholme's praise of Vol. II, in the AHR review read in the positions leg: the reign of the Catholic Kings treated "as a cross-section of the history of Spain", "a profoundly interesting disquisition on the reign of the Catholic kings, treating it as a cross-section of the history of Spain". Moses agrees the volume is vigorous where Vol. I is a chronicle: the union, Granada, the Canaries, America, the marriage alliances, all in one slice. What it IS: the vertical cut through a horizontal subject, the method by which a thousand-year arc is made visible in a single reign. Watchable behaviour: a long horizontal timeline; a vertical blade drops and a single span of years is sliced open to show all its layers at once in one frozen frame, then the blade lifts and the timeline runs on.

## M3. The Incubus of Chronology
The chronicle's style transferred unconsciously to the page. Moses, AHR 24 (1919), pp. 83-85, read end to end: Vol. I is largely "a dull chronicle", "many sections have evidently been written with the open chronicle by the side of the author's manuscript", and the volume "may be considered as an introduction to the 350 pages of text, in the second volume". Merriman only frees himself in Vol. II, where the narrative turns vigorous. What it IS: the source's form becoming the book's form, the medieval chronicle as an incubus the historian has to shake off before he can write. Watchable behaviour: a procession of named kings and dates marching in lockstep until the march breaks and one figure steps out of the line and the scene comes alive.

## M4. The Quarry of Printed Documents
The building assembled from other people's quarried stone. For Vols. I-II Merriman worked "almost exclusively based on printed sources and standard secondary works", reserving unpublished manuscript material for Vols. III-IV (Charles V and Philip II); manuscript material is used only in chapters IV, V, XV and XVI of the early volumes. His stated reason, in the Vol. I Preface read end to end: "an unusually large number of documents, edited by Spanish scholars, have remained almost unutilized", and the field was so vast and so little known outside Spain that the printed editions were the quarry to work first. What it IS: the deliberate choice of the edited document over the fresh manuscript, because the unworked material was already in print. Watchable behaviour: a wall of printed volumes; hands pull one down, open it, and a fact is lifted out and set in place, and the building rises from stone already cut by other hands.

## M5. The Footnote as Rescue
The apparatus as an act of courtesy to the reader. The Rise carries dense footnotes throughout, "in the hope of sparing my readers such labor" -- the labour of verifying statements for which no specific reference was given -- and a bibliographical note at the end of each chapter plus a "Note on the General Authorities" after the Introduction, instead of a general bibliography, which he calls "a rather specious credential of erudition". What it IS: the footnote as a rescue line, thrown to the reader before the reader drowns in unverifiable claims. Watchable behaviour: a claim floats unanchored on the page; a footnote drops like an anchor line and pins it to a source below; unanchored claims drift and fade.

## M6. The Apprentice's Letters
The method in miniature, before there was a subject. The Life and Letters of Thomas Cromwell (2 vols., Clarendon Press, 1902), his Oxford B.Litt. thesis, is built on full, accurate transcription of every extant letter Cromwell wrote, with 21 letters never before printed or calendared, transcription rules stated in a prefatory note, and letters received summarised from the Calendars of State Papers; the archives worked were the Public Record Office, the British Museum, the Heralds' College and the Bodleian, plus private collections opened by the Duke of Rutland, the Marquess of Salisbury, Earl Spencer, Lord Calthorpe, William Berington and Alfred Henry Huth, with help from PRO staff in deciphering difficult manuscripts. He called it "apprentice work" (per Mattingly, quoted in Wikipedia). His verdict on Cromwell, quoted in Collinson: "I cannot agree... with those who have represented Cromwell as a purely selfish political adventurer", and he believed Cromwell aspired to "an 'all-powerful kingship under the forms of ostensible constitutionalism'". What it IS: the craft demonstrated before the thesis, the transcription as the training. Watchable behaviour: a stack of letters, each one copied out in full by hand, and a life assembled from the copies; the apprentice's bench, before the masterwork.

## M7. The British Comparison
The comparison that does half the thesis's work. Merriman's Introduction, read in the positions leg: England's insular position "ultimately forced her into a maritime career", and "it is possible to make an intelligent study of the British Empire without going back of the sixteenth century. But the story of the empire of Spain is at once more complicated and more continuous." The Iberian Peninsula "tempted its inhabitants to expand both by land and sea", and Spain's refusal to choose between irreconcilable opportunities explains "some of her most disastrous defeats". Moses disputes the British half of the comparison: "the spirit of liberty had its origin surely prior to the sixteenth century". What it IS: the rival empire as the measuring stick, the argument made by contrast. Watchable behaviour: two empires drawn side by side, one a single line from the sixteenth century, the other a braid running back to antiquity; the braid keeps tangling because it will not choose one direction.

## M8. The Alien Sovereign
The turning point is a change of hands, not a change of policy. Vol. III, The Emperor (1925): "The six years which followed the death of Ferdinand the Catholic were in some respects the most critical in the history of the Spanish Empire. They saw the control of its destinies pass from native into alien hands." Under Charles V the empire's responsibilities and burdens increased "all over the earth, and at a pace hitherto undreamed of". What it IS: the succession as the hinge, the native king replaced by the foreign emperor and the empire's scale changing with the change of hands. Watchable behaviour: a crown passing from one head to another across a border; the map underneath it doubles in size in the same moment, and the new king's hands are suddenly full of continents.

## M9. Ferdinand, Not Isabella
The hero corrected by subtraction. Moses, read end to end: Merriman presents Ferdinand as "an heroic figure", "a timely and well-administered antidote to the sentimental exaltation of the Isabella of tradition". Ferdinand, not Isabella, is the maker of the empire in Merriman's telling -- the union, Granada, the Italian wars, the marriage alliances, all read as his work. What it IS: the revision performed by rebalancing a pair, the queen's halo moved to the king's head. Watchable behaviour: two portraits hanging side by side, one lit warmly by tradition; the light is moved, and the other face comes forward while the first recedes into shadow.

## M10. The Prudent Householder
The empire explained through one man's character. Max Handman, Annals, July 1936, on Vol. IV, Philip the Prudent (second-hand, via search snippet, see C12): Merriman "calls [him] the Prudent, but prudent in the sense of a narrow, honest, limited, and suspicious householder", and "absolves [him] from the traditional sins of capricious and cruel bigotry to show him as a person whose suspicious honesty made it impossible for him to delegate powers and responsibilities in an empire altogether too big for his narrow shoulders, and limited capacities". Handman's criticism, same source: the economic structure of Philip's empire "is treated much more meagerly than the political, and the intellectual", because of the lack of available secondary sources and documentary material. What it IS: character as the explanation of empire, the householder's virtues becoming the empire's limits. Watchable behaviour: an empire drawn as a household ledger -- every door locked, every account checked, the householder counting coins at his desk while the house quietly contracts around him.

## M11. The Refusal of Decline
The book that stops at the summit on principle. Rise Vol. I Preface, read end to end: "It is my purpose to carry the story, in four volumes, down to the death of Philip II, under whose rule the Spanish Empire attained its greatest territorial extent, through the annexation of Portugal and of her dominions. The long period of 'decline and fall' I am content to leave to others, the more so because the tendency to regard Spain and the Spanish administration as synonymous with inefficiency and decadence is so common that it is a pleasure to emphasize the other side." What it IS: the deliberate refusal, the rise written and the fall declined, because the fall had already been written too often and too easily. Watchable behaviour: a mountain climbed to its peak and the narrative stopping there, the descent left blank on the map, with the blank itself as the argument.

## M12. The Moorish Gap
The promise the book does not keep. Moses, read end to end: Merriman promises Moorish civilization as the basis of Christian society in Spain and does not deliver it -- "One is disappointed in not finding this expectation met; for dynastic changes and lines of Saracenic rulers, how ever fully set forth, do not adequately reveal this basis." No counter-statement by Merriman was found in any leg. What it IS: the gap as a reception fact, the foundation named and then not built on. Watchable behaviour: a building raised with its foundation drawn in dotted lines underneath, the dotted lines never filled in, and the building standing anyway.

## M13. The Debt to Prescott
The reverent successor, not the iconoclast. Rise Vol. I Preface, read end to end: "Eighty-one years have now elapsed since William Hickling Prescott published the first edition of his 'History of the Reign of Ferdinand and Isabella.'... Prescott's work still remains the standard authority on the reign of the Catholic Kings." Merriman used Prescott's own books and manuscripts in Harvard College Library for fifteen years, "scrutinized the passages he underscored and read his penciled notes in the margins", and testifies to "the profound learning, deep insight, and above all to the unfailing honesty with which his work was done. Such errors as he made were due to lack of material, and to a really noble inability to comprehend a policy of treachery or deceit." "My debt of gratitude to him is the deepest of all." Vols. I-II are dedicated "To the honored memory of William Hickling Prescott." What it IS: the succession performed as gratitude, the new book built on the old one's margins. Watchable behaviour: a scholar reading another scholar's pencil marks in the margins for fifteen years, and then writing the book the margins point to.

## M14. The Lecture Course as Engine
The book that grew out of the classroom. The Rise's plan was drawn up "in general accordance with the scheme of a course of lectures at Harvard University which I had been giving intermittently since 1903" -- about 1909, nine years before Vols. I-II appeared. "When one's knowledge of a subject is largely derived from the teaching of it, one must not forget to render thanks to one's pupils... the knowledge that his pupils have gone beyond him" is "the highest satisfaction that a teacher can enjoy." The course he taught on the Spanish Empire "gave the impetus for his research and voluminous publication" (via Mattingly). What it IS: teaching as the research programme, the lecture course as the book's first draft. Watchable behaviour: a lecture hall where the same course is given year after year, and each year the notes grow thicker, until the notes stand up and walk out as four volumes.

## M15. Spain and North Africa Are One
The geographical premise that carries the whole Rise. Rise Vol. I Introduction, read in the positions leg: "The well known phrase 'Africa begins with the Pyrenees' should always be interpreted to mean rather that Spain and North Africa are one, than that Spain and France are divided." The Straits of Gibraltar "has never offered serious hindrance to peoples who have been desirous of travelling north and south." What it IS: the strait as a bridge, not a border, the premise that makes the Mediterranean and Atlantic conquests one story. Watchable behaviour: a map where the Pyrenees are drawn as a wall and the Straits as a narrow door, and peoples keep walking through the door while the wall holds.

## M16. The Inherited Manuscript
The book that arrives on a flyleaf. Suleiman the Magnificent (1944) is built on an unfinished life of Suleiman written by Merriman's friend Archibald Cary Coolidge in 1901-1902, discussed constantly between them for five years; Coolidge died in 1928 and the manuscript lay in the Harvard Archives until Merriman found it about 1942-1943, its flyleaf inscribed "For R. B. Merriman" in the secretary's hand. He first meant to finish and edit it under Coolidge's name, found that impracticable, and rewrote it "ab initio": much of chapter I and scattered paragraphs in chapters II-VI and IX-XI are Coolidge's, revised; chapters VII, VIII and XII are wholly Merriman's. The 1966 reissue credits Coolidge as co-author. What it IS: the debt repaid eighteen years after the death, the friend's unfinished book taken up as a summons. Watchable behaviour: a closed manuscript on a shelf for twenty-six years; a hand opens it, the flyleaf inscription glows, and a second hand takes up the pen where the first stopped, two handwritings merging on one page.

## M17. Drum and Trumpet, Unapologetic
The defence of narrative against the new schools, stated as a creed. Suleiman Preface, September 1944, read end to end in all three legs: "No apology is offered for the fact that the following pages are chiefly a story of diplomacy and campaigns. Military considerations invariably came first in the Ottoman mind, and Suleiman was primarily a conqueror. Professor Coolidge's unfinished manuscript is even more of what has been called a 'drum and a trumpet history' than is this book, but I am glad to take this opportunity to testify to my unshaken belief in the doctrine which he constantly preached -- namely that a knowledge of the narrative is the indispensable foundation for everything else." Constitutional, economic, social, psychological and other "aspects" of history "are perfectly meaningless without it" and "none of them really new... they have all been studied -- without their modern titles -- ever since the time of Herodotus." What it IS: the old method defended in the open, the drum and trumpet owned rather than apologised for. Watchable behaviour: a drumbeat and a trumpet call set the pace; maps, laws and economies try to speak and fall silent until the march of events gives them something to stand on.

## M18. Primarily a Conqueror
The subject defined by one adjective. Suleiman, Preface and chapter I, read in the legs: Suleiman is "one of the greatest yet least known sovereigns of the sixteenth century"; "Military considerations invariably came first in the Ottoman mind, and Suleiman was primarily a conqueror." "He was equally noteworthy as a warrior and a statesman, and his private character, though stained by a few acts that even his inherited surroundings and traditions cannot excuse, was yet such as to command the admiration of his enemies." He was "the last and perhaps the greatest" of the Ottoman line that built the state "from a little vassal principality into a mighty empire, and made it the terror of Christendom." What it IS: the conqueror as the definition, the statesman admitted only after the warrior. Watchable behaviour: a sultan drawn first with a sword and only then with a pen, the campaigns marching across the map while the laws wait in the tent behind them.

## M19. The Four Sovereigns
Four thrones, four crowns, one moment in Europe. Suleiman, chapter I, read in the legs: Charles V, Francis I, Henry VIII and Suleiman -- "the thrones of Europe were now filled by the strongest men who ever contemporaneously occupied them" (a sentence Merriman quotes from "a great English historian", unnamed in the passage read, and not his own words, see C18); Suleiman "played a more decisive part in the struggle between Charles V and Francis I than did Henry VIII"; "there were never so many great ones together, of so well-consolidated dominion, so great ability, or so long tenure of power". What it IS: the quartet as the frame, the sixteenth century understood as four sovereigns moving in counterpoint. Watchable behaviour: four thrones in a row, four crowns; the camera holds all four at once and the four figures move in counterpoint, each move by one answered by another.

## M20. The Genealogical Thread
The review's most distinctive passage, and the one that survives into the Dodds' own Conclusion. Merriman, AHR 21(4), July 1916, p. 788, read in full: "Many of the men who opposed Charles I were lineal descendants of the Pilgrims. Philip and Brian Stapleton, the great-great-grandsons of Christopher Stapleton, both distinguished themselves in the cause of the Parliament. Richard Aske, the great-great-grandson of young Robert Aske... was one of the lawyers who drew up the indictment of Charles I. Watchable behaviour: a family tree drawn in 1536; its branches grow forward a century and bloom again in 1642, the same names, the same defiance, one century later.

## M21. The Cross-Class Rising
The review's emphasis, and the Dodds' positive thesis. Merriman, AHR 21(4), July 1916, p. 787, read in full: "It emphasizes the great importance of the fact that the ranks of the Pilgrims contained both gentlemen and commons, that the movement, like the Great Rebellion of the seventeenth century, was the monopoly of no one class of society." The Dodds' Conclusion makes the same point the book's significance: "the union between the gentlemen and the commons", the first breaking of the king-gentlemen alliance that had been the throne's chief support. What it IS: the rising defined by who was in it, not by what it wanted. Watchable behaviour: two columns -- gentlemen in one colour, commons in another -- marching side by side under one banner; the banner holds only while both columns keep pace.

## M22. The Vital Clause
The reviewer's technique: quote the document past where the authors stopped, to the sentence that decides the matter. Merriman, AHR 21(4), July 1916, pp. 788-789, read in full: the Dodds "have failed adequately to emphasize the importance of the dispute between the Duke of Norfolk on the one hand and the king and Cromwell on the other about the class of men who were to compose it [the Council of the North]". He then quotes the king's letter closing the February-May 1537 correspondence, including the "vital clause": "For surely we woll not be bounde of a necessitie to be served there with lordes, but we wol be served with such men what degre soever they be of as we shall appointe to the same." -- "much more than a triumph of the king's plebeian minister over the head of one of the most ancient families in the land... perhaps the most striking exemplification that has come down to us of the way in which the principle of Parcere subjectis et debellare superbos was used to support the edifice of Tudor absolutism." What it IS: the document read one clause further than the book reads it, and the extra clause reframing everything above it. Watchable behaviour: a letter unrolling line by line; the authors stop reading halfway; the reviewer's finger keeps going to the final clause, which lights up and reframes the whole page.

## M23. The Standard Authority
The review's verdict, praise and blame held together. Merriman, AHR 21(4), July 1916, pp. 787-789, read in full: "This is an admirable piece of work, which bids fair to remain the standard authority on the events with which it deals." They have made full use of the documents in the Record Office and in the British Museum, and of the invaluable collection of Letters and Papers. ## M25. The Teacher's Letter-a-Year
The pupil who became a president, and the correspondence that outlived the classroom. Merriman taught the young Franklin D. Roosevelt European history at Harvard (Roosevelt, Harvard Class of 1904); the two exchanged a letter each year until 1941. In 1939 Merriman forwarded Roosevelt a George M. Trevelyan assessment of the German threat, and on Wednesday 15 February 1939 Roosevelt wrote back about American neutrality: "What the British need today is a good stiff grog, inducing not only the desire to save civilization but the continued belief that they can do it." What it IS: the teacher-pupil relation carried into statecraft, the annual letter as the thread between the lecture hall and the White House. Watchable behaviour: one envelope a year crossing the Atlantic for decades; the 1939 envelope arrives heavier, and the reply is written in a different temper.

## M26. The House Master's Cellar
The historian as host, and the college as a household. Merriman was the founding Master of Eliot House, opened 1931, still Master in December 1936 when the Iacovleff portrait was presented with about 150 present and President Conant accepting it; John Finley was named Master in 1942, so Merriman's mastership ended by then. He was toastmaster at the first Eliot House Feast (1935), and the Harvard Crimson records "immense quantities of beer brewed in the Merriman cellar". What it IS: the house run from below, the feast upstairs supplied by what was made downstairs. Watchable behaviour: a formal college hall above, a cellar below where the master brews; the feast upstairs runs on what was made downstairs.

## M27. The Historian's Historian
The reputation that never found the public and never needed to. ## M28. The Succession
The three successors who rewrote the Rise without naming it. No direct verdict on Merriman by J. H. Elliott, John Lynch or Henry Kamen was retrieved in any leg; what follows is successor inference, explicitly marked as such, not quotation. Elliott's Imperial Spain (1963) became the English textbook that displaced Merriman, and his Empires of the Atlantic World (2006) revisits Merriman's British-Spanish comparison with a comparative method driven by institutions and economies, not by kings' characters -- and keeps Merriman's Reconquista-continuity point for the Spanish conquests without crediting it to him. Lynch's Spain Under the Habsburgs (1964-1969) replaces Merriman's personal-political explanation of empire with an economic and social one, and his centre-of-gravity thesis (a shift from metropolis to colony rather than absolute decline) directly answers the decline story Merriman refused to tell. Kamen's Empire: How Spain Became a World Power (2003) reverses Merriman's Spain-centred rise: "Castile had little to offer. By contrast, the creation of the world monarchy would not have been possible without the bullion of America, and the manpower, expertise, and finance of other Europeans" (via review summary). What it IS: the succession performed by displacement rather than by refutation, three books that take the Rise apart without ever arguing with it by name. Watchable behaviour: a four-volume work on a shelf, and three later books placed beside it, each one quietly taking a piece of its argument and rebuilding it in a different shape, while the original stays on the shelf, still consulted.

## Evolution: three phases, and the afterlife so far

Phase one, the Tudor apprentice, 1897-1908. Training, not yet a subject: Balliol under F. York Powell and A. L. Smith, then Berlin under Max Lenz and Adolf von Harnack, gives him the German scientific method -- work from original documents, transcribe fully and accurately, judge without confessional bias. The Life and Letters of Thomas Cromwell (1902) is the method in miniature: a statesman's life built on his letters, 21 of them never printed or calendared before, with full transcription rules stated in a prefatory note. He calls it "apprentice work", a telling self-judgment: the book is a demonstration of craft, not a thesis. His one substantial article of the phase, "Some Notes on the Treatment of the English Catholics in the Reign of Elizabeth I" (American Historical Review 13(3), April 1908), stays in the same Tudor world. What changes at the end of the phase: the lecture course on the Spanish Empire, its plan drawn up about 1909, pulls him out of England into the Iberian world his Berlin and Oxford training had not covered. The 1916 review of the Dodds book belongs to the seam between the phases: a Tudor historian reviewing a Tudor book, two years before the Spanish volumes begin to appear.

Phase two, the Spanish Empire, 1909-1934. One book, twenty-five years: four volumes, 1918-1934, with a seven-year gap between Vols. II and III (1918-1925, which the Vol. III preface apologises for -- "I DEEPLY regret that more than seven years have elapsed since the publication of the preceding volumes of this work") and a nine-year gap between Vols. III and IV (1925-1934). The slowness is structural: the medieval Vols. I-II are synthesis from chronicles and secondary work, while Vols. III-IV rest on the dense diplomatic archive of Charles V and Philip II. The scale shifts from one man's letters to a thousand-year arc, medieval Iberia to Philip II, and two continents. The thesis hardens into a single claim: the Spanish Empire was the natural continuation of medieval Spain, not an accident of conquest -- the opposite of the British Empire, which he treats as a break. The method shifts visibly in the reviews: Moses (1919) finds Vol. I chronicle-bound, "written with the open Cronica by the side of the author's manuscript", while Vol. II is freer, a cross-section of a reign; by Vol. IV the analysis is characterological, Philip II explained through personal traits rather than institutions alone. The institutional payoff: the work makes him the American authority on Spain, earns the Real Academia de la Historia corresponding membership (certificate Saturday 11 November 1922), the Gurney chair (Monday 22 April 1929), and founds a United States field -- Wikipedia and Mattingly call it a pioneering work in Latin American history. His Harvard course (History 16) and the book feed each other for two decades. Side work stays in the same century: Bodleian research on Philip II (May 1926), notes on Philip II's finances (1929), a transcript of a Conde-Duque de Olivares paper (Egerton MS 338).

Phase three, widening and coda, 1935-1945. The empire book done, he widens geographically and turns public: lectures on viceroys, Ximenes in North Africa, the Spanish embassy in Tudor England (returning to his phase-one ground with phase-two knowledge); three lectures at the University of London in May 1939 ("Spain in North Africa: Cardinal Ximenes", "The Spanish Viceroy in the New World: Francisco Alvarez de Toledo", "The Palace of Spain in the Spanish Empire"); radio broadcasts in 1941; and an Anglophile diplomatic role in 1939-1941, channelling Trevelyan to Roosevelt against American isolationism. Suleiman (1944) is the coda, and its origin explains why it came last. It is not a new research programme but a debt repaid: his friend Archibald Cary Coolidge wrote an unfinished life of Suleiman in 1901-1902, they discussed it for five years, Coolidge died in 1928, the manuscript lay in the Harvard Archives for twenty-six years, and about 1942-1943 Merriman found it, flyleaf inscribed "For R. B. Merriman", and took it as a summons. He first meant to finish and edit it under Coolidge's name, found that impracticable, and rewrote it "ab initio". Why Suleiman, why then: friendship and Harvard duty -- the book is also a memorial to Coolidge, who built Harvard's modern-history horizon; the subject completes the quartet his whole career circled -- Charles V, Francis I, Henry VIII, and now Suleiman, "the fourth great sovereign of the time", whom modern historians had neglected; and wartime urgency -- the preface says the "tragic events of the past five years" made Coolidge's wide horizons "seem prophetic", and his wife Dorothea's encouragement carried the book to completion. His late creed, stated in the Suleiman preface, is a defence of the old method against the new schools: the book is "chiefly a story of diplomacy and campaigns" -- "drum and trumpet history" -- because "a knowledge of the narrative is the indispensable foundation for everything else". The apprentice of 1902 ends as the deliberate defender of narrative against the interwar turn to impersonal forces -- the same position his pupil Mattingly would carry into Renaissance Diplomacy (1955) and The Defeat of the Spanish Armada (1959). His final publication, "An Historian Looks at the Young Entry", appeared in The Atlantic 176(4), October 1945, a month after his death.

Afterlife so far. The Rise was reprinted by Cooper Square Publishers in 1962; Suleiman was reissued by Cooper Square in 1966 with Coolidge credited as co-author, and again in modern reprints. Mattingly's AHR tribute of October 1948 fixed the verdict the field has kept: a historian's historian, never a public success, secure in the hands of his fellow historians. Elliott's Imperial Spain (1963) displaced the Rise as the English textbook; Lynch's Spain Under the Habsburgs (1964-1969) replaced its personal-political account with a structural one; Kamen's Empire (2003) reversed its Spain-centred thesis. None of the three is shown in any source read to name Merriman in the act of displacing him, which is itself the afterlife's shape: the Rise was not refuted, it was walked around. Merriman is still cited as a source authority -- Richard Bonney's Encyclopedia of War entry cites "Merriman 1944: 213, 285" for Barbarossa dates -- and his books remain in print in reprint editions. No modern scholarly reassessment essay devoted to Merriman was found in any leg. The MassHist papers (Merriman Family Papers, 1837-1976) hold the archive: correspondence, book-publication folders, speeches and writings, Harvard, Oxford and Berlin records, First World War service papers, honours, obituaries and funeral folders, and the undated "Bibliography of Roger Bigelow Merriman" in Carton 4, Folder 12, which would settle the complete bibliography and was not read.

## Aesthetic logic

The book and the life share one aesthetic: the narrative first, and everything else built on it, or not built at all. Merriman's creed is stated once, in the Suleiman preface, and it governs the whole career: "a knowledge of the narrative is the indispensable foundation for everything else". The Rise is the creed applied at the largest scale -- four volumes, a thousand years, two continents -- and Suleiman is the creed applied at the smallest, a single life taken up as a debt. In both, the story comes first and the "aspects" -- constitutional, economic, social, psychological -- are "perfectly meaningless without it", and the refusal is the point: he writes the rise and leaves the decline to others, he writes the conqueror and leaves the institutions to others, he writes the four sovereigns and leaves the forces to others. The logic is chronological before it is analytical. A chronicle tells you what happened; a cross-section tells you what one reign contained; a character tells you why an empire rose and why it could not be delegated. The book's structure follows the same logic: two volumes of medieval prologue before the empire exists, because the empire is the continuation and the continuation has to be earned by walking the ground first, and the fourth volume ends at the summit, Philip II's death, because the summit is where the story stops. The apprentice's letters are the aesthetic's origin: a life assembled from full transcriptions, the document before the judgment, and the footnote as the rescue line thrown to the reader. The teacher's letter-a-year is the aesthetic's shadow: the same patience applied to people, one envelope a year for decades, the correspondence as the slow accumulation that becomes, in 1939, a piece of statecraft. The study's own form follows it too: where the book is closed, the study says so, because a gap honestly stated is worth more than a gap smoothly filled, and because Merriman himself refused the general bibliography as "a rather specious credential of erudition" and printed his chapter-end notes instead.
