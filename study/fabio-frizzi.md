Fabio Frizzi was born in Bologna on Monday 2 July 1951 and raised in Rome, the older brother of the television presenter Fabrizio Frizzi, and the music in his life began before any instrument did: the family singing together on the way to the sea, his father Fulvio's choir voice, his aunt Adriana -- Bobo -- singing beside him, a memory he still carries as the thing you put in your heart and keep there. [C29770]

He took up the guitar at fourteen and founded a rock group, and the path into music ran through the classical first -- the Baroque period, a much-loved era -- before widening into pop and rock, a widening that never closed: the classical groups and the pop rock band of his pre-film years, two acting roles in 1970, and then the trio. [C29771]

The trio is the first career. With Franco Bixio and Vince Tempera he scored a spaghetti western in 1968, Ed ora... raccomanda l'anima a Dio!, and by the mid-1970s the three were scoring several pictures a year -- comedies, westerns, crime dramas -- a working band for hire, with Frizzi on guitar and synthesizer, and it was inside that band that he first met Lucio Fulci. [C29772]

The breakthrough was a clerk. Fantozzi, Luciano Salce's 1975 satire of the unlucky accountant Ugo Fantozzi, gave Frizzi the score that made him known to the general public, and its ballad -- "La Ballata Di Fantozzi" -- is the first Frizzi melody Italy hummed. [C29773]

The trio years run 1975 to 1978 under one shared byline, Bixio-Frizzi-Tempera: Fantozzi and I quattro dell'apocalisse and Vai gorilla in 1975, Febbre da cavallo and Il secondo tragico Fantozzi in 1976, Sette note in nero in 1977, Silver Saddle in 1978 -- six films the pick names, and more besides, across comedy, western, poliziottesco and giallo. The same three men also stepped out from behind the films as Magnetic System, one 7-inch in 1977, "Godzilla" / "Escape", and under further masks, the Day-Glo Fruit Of The Gum and the Benjamin Franklyn Band. [C29774]

Sette note in nero is the trio's purest device and its longest afterlife: a seven-note music-box theme for Fulci's 1977 giallo, written by three men, which sat for twenty-six years and then came back without anyone writing a new note -- Tarantino included an excerpt in Kill Bill: Volume 1 in 2003, and the theme is still the set piece of Frizzi's live show in 2026. [C29775]

The solo career is the horror career. From Zombi 2 in 1979 through Contraband, City of the Living Dead, The Beyond, Manhattan Baby and Cat in the Brain in 1990, Frizzi scored Fulci's goriest films alone, and the sound is the man: electronic, classical and rock drawn together, mellotron and Moog from a love of progressive rock, analog synthesizers whose fuzzy sound adds a layer of creepiness to downbeat melodies, minimal and atmospheric, slow repetitive rhythms under a tune that will not leave. [C29776]

He was never only the horror man. The same decades hold Salce's comedies and Corbucci's crime films and Bava's action, and then the long television turn -- Il Capitano and the Rai fiction series of the 2000s -- and in 2002 the trio came back together once, for Febbre da cavallo - La mandrakata, the sequel directed by Steno's son Carlo Vanzina. [C29777]

The revival came from the internet and the reissue labels. Death Waltz began issuing his scores in high-quality editions, Febbre da Cavallo became a stage musical with a cast recording in 2017, and the new work kept coming -- House of Forbidden Secrets in 2013, Puppet Master: The Littlest Reich in 2018, a limited Cadabra 7-inch of The Beyond composer's-cut mixes in 2019 -- until the catalogue stood at more than one hundred film and television scores. [C29778]

The tribute is the third career, and it is the one that never stopped. Frizzi 2 Fulci was prepared in 2012 and premiered on Halloween night 2013 at London's Union Chapel, an eight-piece rock orchestra replaying his Fulci suites live while the film excerpts played behind them; it sold out London in 2013 and 2014, reached North America for the first time in October 2015 with five concerts, toured the Composer's Cut of The Beyond in 2017 and of Zombie in 2024, and by 2026 it was on its eleventh tour of the United States. [C29779]

The last two months are the proof of life. Thirty dates across the United States and Canada, 23 September to 1 November 2026, opening at TV Eye in Ridgewood, Queens -- eleven pieces, "Silver Saddle" to "Sette Note in Nero" to the full weight of Zombi 2, Frizzi telling stories before nearly every piece, the films running behind the band -- and then home to Rome, the Forum Theatre on Saturday 14 November 2026, the living-dead trilogy and Four of the Apocalypse and an unreleased suite from Black Eyed Susan, the maestro staying on to sign autographs. [C29780]

The arc is a single line with one hinge and no reversal: the trio's hired hand in the seventies, the solo horror voice of the eighties, the television composer of the 2000s, and finally the conductor of his own tribute -- the same melodies, played louder, in front of the films, by the man who wrote them, telling the stories between the pieces. [C29781]

## M1 -- The music-box theme that turns threatening by repetition [C29787]

The seven-note tune of *Sette note in nero* is a carillon melody -- a child's music box -- played on carillon with strings, synthesisers and piano, described as "simple, elegant and gravely beautiful", steering clear of the atonality and shrieking strings of typical giallo scores [C29788]. The tune is not laid over the film; it is inside it. It is the chime of a wristwatch Gloria gives Virginia, heard first in a vision, then on the hour, then -- in the climax -- playing from inside the wall where her husband has entombed her, betraying him. The mechanism is repetition as corruption: the same seven notes, unchanged, heard often enough that a pretty tune becomes a threat. Nothing about the notes changes; the context does, and the repetition does the work. The theme then repeats across decades the same way: Tarantino needle-drops it into *Kill Bill: Vol. 1* (2003), RZA samples it, drum-and-bass producer High Contrast samples it again -- each time the same seven notes, each time more menacing for having survived. [C29789]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one small, pretty tune -- a music box, seven notes, nothing more -- repeated until the repetition itself turns it threatening: first a gift, then a clock, then a sound coming from inside a wall.

## M2 -- The synth-orchestra hybrid: band and orchestra, seriously this time [C29790]

Frizzi's stated aim for *The Beyond* was "to combine two different instrumental forms I had always loved: the band and the orchestra. When I started writing music some years before, I had learned to combine these two sounds, but for many reasons, the roles of strings and wind instruments were mainly created by keyboards. This time I decided to get serious." [C29791] The score is the hybrid made literal: Mellotron and bass and rock-band instruments alongside orchestral and choral arrangements -- and as the film progresses toward its conclusion, "Frizzi's score also darkens, growing heavy, underlining the inescapable fate of the characters" [C29792]. The gear is the signature: Mellotron (tape-loop choir), Yamaha CS80, Prophet 5, Jupiter 8, plus real strings, real flutes, real voices [C29793]. The Goblin adjacency is real and sourced: Maurizio Guarini came to *Zombi 2* straight from Goblin, and critics hear Goblin's *Dawn of the Dead* pulse in Frizzi's electronic scores -- but Frizzi adds what Goblin doesn't: the orchestra, the choir, the folk sheen. The hybrid is the sound: analogue synths that waver and detune, held inside an orchestral frame that keeps them from collapsing. [C29794]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be built from two bands at once -- a rock band of buzzing, detuning synths and a small orchestra with a choir -- and the two should trade the same melody back and forth until you can't tell which one is the horror and which one is the beauty.

## M3 -- Leitmotif economy: few themes, many returns [C29795]

Frizzi writes a handful of themes per film and then returns them, varied slightly, until they are the film. *Zombi 2* is eight numbered cues, "Sequence 1" through "Sequence 8", all by Frizzi, built on one main theme: "a click-track pulse and droning keys before erupting into an evolving loop of percussion, guitar and keyboard" [C29796]. Film Score Monthly's description is the mechanism stated plainly: the memorable theme "is used numerous times with various little changes to keep it interesting", over "a remorselessly consistent drum machine rhythm as a backbone for coldly repeated synthesizer dronings and a Mellotron-esque choir" [C29797]. The returns are the economy: the same theme, a little faster, a little lower, a little more broken. And the economy extends across films -- *Manhattan Baby*'s score, by one reviewer's account, is largely older Frizzi cues from *City of the Living Dead* and *The Beyond*, with a new main theme on top [C29798]. The composer reuses his own themes the way the films reuse their own images. [C29799]

**Takeaway:** the piece should have exactly one theme and one pulse, and it should return that theme again and again with only tiny changes -- a note lower, a beat later, a voice added -- until the audience knows it by heart and dreads hearing it again.

## M4 -- Beautiful melody over dread: the Frizzi contrast [C29800]

The governing contrast of Frizzi's music is a downbeat, singable melody set against images of gore -- AllMusic's formula is "analog synthesizers, with their fuzzy sound adding a layer of creepiness to his downbeat melodies" [C29801]. It runs through the whole catalogue: *Four of the Apocalypse* sets "gentle majesty" against on-screen brutality; the *Zombi 2* theme is "laced with dread and yet... overwhelmingly melancholy"; *Manhattan Baby* "favors romance and majesty over misery"; the *Puppet Master* score is driven by "a deceptively sweet, waltz-like melody for Toulon" over fascist-puppet slaughter [C29802]. The sharpest instance is a trick: in *Zombi 2*, Frizzi writes Caribbean musical cues -- sunny, touristic, pleasant -- "intended to 'pleasantly deceive' the audience" before the eye-gouging [C29803]. The melody is not the opposite of the horror; it is the lure. The audience leans in because the tune is beautiful, and the tune is what holds them still for the dread. [C29804]

**Takeaway:** the piece should open with the prettiest melody it has -- a waltz, a lullaby, a holiday tune -- and keep it playing, unchanged and lovely, while everything around it gets worse; the beauty is the trap, not the relief.

## M5 -- The trio credit vs the solo voice: one byline, then one name [C29805]

From their first score in 1968 to 1979 Frizzi wrote as one third of Bixio-Frizzi-Tempera: the trio's objective was film and television music, and they scored "countless low-budget Italian movies" together -- westerns, comedies, poliziotteschi, the Fantozzi films, and the first Fulci films [C29806]. The trio's last Fulci score together was *Sette note in nero* (1977); ComingSoon attributes the split to "creative differences" while noting "they remain friends today" [C29807]. Frizzi's solo career begins in 1978, and *Zombi 2* (1979) is the hinge: the first time he and Fulci worked together on a straight horror film, and the first time the score carries his name alone [C29808]. The solo voice is immediately different -- the Mellotron choir, the numbered sequences, the loop -- and it is the voice the next forty years belong to. But the trio never quite ends: the three reunited once, for *Febbre da cavallo - La mandrakata* in 2002 [C29809]. The byline system is the career in miniature: three hands learning to write as one name, then one hand learning to write alone, then -- once, twenty-three years later -- the three hands again. [C29810]

**Takeaway:** the piece should start with three figures writing one melody together, then let two of them step back until one figure is left playing it alone -- and the melody should sound thinner and stranger alone, and more his own.

## M6 -- The theme that outlives its film [C29811]

The seven-note theme of *Sette note in nero* was written for a giallo that grossed modestly and was called "nondescript" at home -- and then it outlived the film by decades [C29812]. In 2003 Tarantino put it in *Kill Bill: Vol. 1* and RZA sampled it; Tarantino had already considered remaking the film in the 1990s with Bridget Fonda; drum-and-bass producer High Contrast sampled the theme again; hip-hop producers made it "a veritable grab bag" through the 2010s; Boards of Canada's *Tomorrow's Harvest* carries its hallmarks [C29813]. The same afterlife runs through the rest of the catalogue: Death Waltz reissued *Zombi 2* and *The Beyond* on vinyl in 2015, Rolling Stone ranked *The Beyond* score the 11th-best horror score of all time in 2016, and the 2025 Grindhouse UHD box set carries a newly recorded Composer's Cut [C29814]. The mechanism is reuse without revision: nobody rewrites the theme, nobody improves it -- a director hears it, a producer cuts it, a label represses it, and the same notes acquire a new audience each time. The theme is now more famous than the film it was written for. [C29815]

**Takeaway:** the piece should let one small theme sleep for most of its runtime, then wake it unchanged in a completely new room -- sampled, re-lit, surrounded by strangers -- and the theme should be the loudest thing in the piece precisely because it never changed.

## M7 -- Live re-performance: the horror score as a rock concert [C29816]

In 2013, seventeen years after Fulci's death, Frizzi put his horror scores on stage as Frizzi 2 Fulci: an eight-piece "orchestra rock" playing rearranged suites live while clips from the films project behind the band [C29817]. The world premiere was Halloween night 2013 at London's Union Chapel, sold out; the first US shows followed in October 2015; the project has now run eleven US tours, the latest a 30-date Fall 2026 run from TV Eye in Ridgewood, Queens, to Atlanta, followed by a Rome homecoming on 14 November 2026 [C29818]. The mechanism is re-performance as re-reading: the scores are rearranged for a live band -- bass and drums, two guitarists, keyboards, a female voice, Frizzi himself on keyboard and classical guitar -- and played in suite form, "I tell the film through his musical themes, and the progressive rock flavor comes out from start to finish" [C29819]. The *Beyond* Composer's Cut is the same mechanism pushed further: Frizzi recovered his original notes from the 1981 sessions, restored cues that had been shortened or cut from the film's final mix, and added a new theme to tie them together -- "not an alternative, but an affectionate musical review of a movie I loved very much. Even with the experience of 35 years more." [C29820] The Ghost Cult review of the 2026 opener names the effect: synchronized projections, every cue matched to its scene, "somewhere between a concert and a seance" [C29821].

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a small band on a dark stage playing old horror themes live, with the films projected behind them in sync -- and the music should be the same notes as forty years ago, but louder, heavier, and played by a band that knows them by heart.

## M8 -- The composer as bandleader: Bach, the Beatles, and the man at the front [C29822]

Frizzi does not sit behind the score; on stage he plays it, conducts it, and narrates it. He plays keyboard and classical guitar in the F2F Band, conducts and leads the ensemble, and -- per the Ghost Cult review -- talks before nearly every piece, "sharing stories of working directly with Lucio Fulci, the history behind a score, the memory tied to a specific film, turning the concert into something closer to an oral history delivered in music", trading banter with his bandmates in Italian between songs [C29823]. The spine under the showmanship is classical and stated by Frizzi himself: "my atavistic references are Johann Sebastian Bach and The Beatles" -- and among Italian cinema composers, Rota, Morricone, Trovaioli [C29824]. The classical work is real, not a pose: six guitar preludes, a G major concert for clarinet and strings, ballets, and a television theme sung by Edda Dell'Orso, Morricone's vocalist, which "makes me shiver whenever I listen to it" [C29825]. The mechanism is the composer stepping out of the studio -- "human warmth is perhaps the most lacking in a composer who lives his life in a recording studio" -- and becoming the frontman of his own music, forty years after writing it [C29826].

**Takeaway:** the piece should put the composer himself on stage at the front -- playing, conducting, and stopping between pieces to tell a short story about where the next theme came from -- and the story should change how the next piece sounds.

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Frizzi does not write horror music; he writes beautiful music and lets the horror happen next to it -- a music-box tune that turns by repetition, a waltz over fascist puppets, a Caribbean holiday cue before an eye-gouging, a folk sheen over a gate of hell. The melody is never the opposite of the dread; it is the lure, and the lure is what holds the audience still. Early he is the third of three hands writing as one name, the most melodic hand of a working band for hire; then he is the solo voice, the Mellotron choir and the numbered sequences, the man whose name alone is on the horror scores; late he is the bandleader at the front of his own tribute, playing the same notes louder, telling the stories between them, and the story changes how the next piece sounds. The place is never decoration: the Rome studios, the Cinevox offices, the Union Chapel on Halloween night, the small rooms of the 2026 American tour with the films projected behind the band. His own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one he states in the Dread Central interview: the relationship between the composer and the movie is very important, it is very personal, and music is like a magic potion -- if you truly believe in a project, you can, within certain limits, change the destiny of that film. The work is the tune, the repetition, and the return -- and the tune is the same at fourteen and at seventy-five, only the room it plays in keeps changing. [C29827]

Top pick for #752: **Franco Bixio (b. 29 January 1950, Rome, Italy; living)** -- Italian film composer and publisher, son of Cesare Andrea Bixio, founder of Cinevox.
