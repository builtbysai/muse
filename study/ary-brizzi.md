# Ary Brizzi: Study Notes

**Site:** Ary Brizzi, born Buenos Aires 1930, died 2014 -- Argentine painter, sculptor and designer, a major figure of the second generation of Argentine abstract-geometric artists, whose work developed from the theories of Ignacio Pirovano and the Grupo Arte Generativo (C9202, C9301). He was a member of the generativist circle, not a founder: the movement was founded in 1959-1960 by Eduardo MacEntyre and Miguel Angel Vidal, and Brizzi exhibited alongside them from 1963 onward, in the same rooms, the same school, and the same five-name abstraction circle (MacEntyre, Vidal, Silva, Paternosto, Alvarez) (C9201, C9302, C9304). He died aged 84; the year 2014 is carried by six sources, and this study prints it with the single dissenting source -- a Castagnino+macro museum page giving 29 December 2015, which also misspells his name "Brizzy" on the same page -- carried as a flagged variant, not a rival date (C9201). The birthplace is also in conflict: Buenos Aires by the majority of sources, Avellaneda by several others, and this study prints Buenos Aires with the conflict carried (C9201, C9213, C9214). No birth day or month was found anywhere. He graduated in 1951 from the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Carcova, specialised in drawing and mural decoration, and ran a parallel design career from 1951 to 1973 -- posters, fair stands, advertising graphics, interiors -- before the painting took over entirely (C9201, C9202, C9206). His base was Buenos Aires for his whole life, with shows in Paris, New York, Sao Paulo, Quito, Lima, Mexico City and Bergamo (C9201, C9203). The work that matters is the beam of light made out of paint: one bright line cutting a dark field, a fan of graded planes rotating around a pivot, the same proposition repeated in numbered series until the light itself seems to move -- and then, in the last painting found, the beam become a still white slit down the middle of an architectural blue block (C9251, C9252, C9259).

## The life: the designer who painted light

Ary Brizzi was born in 1930, in Buenos Aires by most sources, in Avellaneda by several others (C9201, C9213, C9214). Avellaneda is the industrial city immediately south of the capital, and his first solo show was at Galeria Gente de Arte in Avellaneda, so the two may be conflated in some listings; no source explains the difference, and no birth day or month was found anywhere. At fifteen he took his first drawing and perspective lessons from his father, an Italian architect, drawing and colouring his father's architectural projects; his own words, "Supe que queria pintar desde que era muy joven. Mi padre era arquitecto..." (C9205, C9213). He studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Manuel Belgrano, then at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes Ernesto de la Carcova, working already at fifteen with his father and brother in architecture and interior design while attending art school (C9201, C9202, C9204). He graduated in 1951, specialised in drawing and mural decoration (C9201, C9202, C9208). With his family he set up a design office; his early painting was figurative, centred on the human figure, and he made study trips through northern Argentina, Bolivia, Peru, Chile and Brazil (C9202, C9206, C9208).

From 1952 to 1957 he carried out daily studies in tempera on cardboard, putting into practice the geometry and constructivism he had analysed; shocked by Max Bill's visual developments, he moved toward an art "governed by mathematics" (C9260, C9207). Many of these temperas were never exhibited, most unpublished, each dated with day, month and year, kept by the artist as a "visual thinking" repertoire and a reserve of forms (C9260). In 1954 a stand he designed for the Feria de America in Mendoza won a prize, single-source (C9207). In 1958 he held his first solo exhibition at Galeria Gente de Arte, Avellaneda, by which time his abstract style of rigorous geometry was formed, and he made posters for the Ministerio de Economia, a key moment in his absorption of Bill via Tomas Maldonado (C9201, C9202, C9207). In 1959 he took part in the first Paris Biennial and designed and directed construction of the Argentine pavilion at the III World Trade Fair in New York (C9201, C9202, C9203).

The 1960s carried him into the generativist circle without ever making him a founder. In 1960 he was included in the "Exposicion internacional de arte moderno" at the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires; the first Generative Art exhibition that year at Galeria Peuser showed MacEntyre and Vidal, and Brizzi is not listed among its exhibitors (C9201, C9203, C9209). In the early 1960s he turned to sculpture: aluminium, bronze, coloured wood, Plexiglas, sometimes with enamel, motors or neon lights, his interest in luminosity and transparencies driving the move (C9201, C9202). In July 1963 he showed in "Ocho artistas constructivos" at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, convened by director Jorge Romero Brest, with Espinosa, Lozza, Sabelli, Vidal, Silva, Martorell and MacEntyre -- the first Brizzi-Vidal joint exhibition verified this run, and the first time Brizzi and Martorell exhibited together (C9204, C9205, C9209). The same year he showed in "Del arte concreto a la nueva tendencia 1944-1963" at the Museo Moderno (C9204, C9209). In 1964 came "Brizzi, Mac Entyre, Silva, Vidal. Pinturas" at Galeria Bonino, and "Buenos Aires 64" at the Museo de Arte Moderno, New York showing, where Hugo Parpagnoli's catalogue text singled out Brizzi as giving "a palpable body to austere geometric formulas" (C9209, C9211). In 1965 he represented Argentina at the 8th Sao Paulo Biennial (C9201, C9203). In 1966 he joined Grupo 13 (G13), presented in Buenos Aires with Durante, Espinosa, Heras Velasco, Lezama, MacEntyre, Paternosto, Puente, Sabelli, Silva, Simon and Vidal, praised by Cayetano Cordova Iturburu in El Mundo on 31 July 1966 (C9204). In 1967 he showed in "Mas alla de la geometria" (Beyond Geometry) at the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, and held a solo show at Galeria Bonino whose catalogue-objet he designed with five pop-ups, facsimiled in the 2025 Herrera book (C9203, C9204, C9207). In 1968 he showed in "Four New Argentinian Artists: Brizzi, Mac Entyre, Polesello, Vidal" at Galeria Bonino, New York, and in "Beyond Geometry" at the Center for Inter-American Relations, New York, and won the First Prize at the Quito Biennial (C9201, C9203, C9209).

The late 1960s state the turn. JCMC: Brizzi "made a series of works using the multiplication of lines as the generating source of the image and its forms. The lines, cleverly organized with gradations of color, produced false revolving or displacing movements that gave the works a characteristic dynamism" (C9252). In 1969 he painted Interaccion No. 10, acrylic on canvas, 100 x 100 cm, now at the Art Museum of the Americas (C9253). In 1970 he painted Tension Multiple No. 2, 148 x 148 cm, now at the Museo Municipal de Bellas Artes, La Plata, by national donation (C9215, leg 1). In 1972 he joined the Centro de Investigaciones en Comunicacion Masiva, Arte y Tecnologia (CICMAT), Buenos Aires, for 1972-1973, and began teaching at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredon, where he taught until 1995 by the JCMC biography, until 1996 by the Caiana review of the Herrera book -- a one-year conflict this study carries unresolved (C9202, C9207, C9208). In 1973 he showed in "Arte Generativo Argentino. Espacio y vibracion" at Galeria Arvil, Mexico City, 21 works by Brizzi, Lezama, MacEntyre, Martorell, Polesello, Silva and Vidal (C9218, search-only).

In 1976 he won the Gran Premio de Honor (painting) at the Salon Nacional de Artes Plasticas and was named academico de numero of the Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes (C9201, C9202, C9203). In 1978 he served on the jury for the official poster competition of the 1978 World Cup (search-only). In 1980 he won the Premio Rosario at the Museo Castagnino for Sthenos VI, acquired into the collection, and became director of the Fondo Nacional de las Artes, 1980-1983, single-source (C9201, C9208). In 1982 he won the Premio Konex, Diploma al Merito, Pintura Geometrica, and served as secretary of the Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes, 1982-1991, single-source (C9201, C9208). The 1980s, per the Herrera book review, found him disoriented before a local scene that privileged a kind of painting far from the interests he had cultivated; he continued the late work in the series Krypton, Argon, Sincronico and Pleno, dated 1977-1997 (C9203, C9207). In 1992 he was invited guest of honour at the II Salon Nacional Arte Joven (painting) at the Museo Castagnino, Rosario (C9201). In 1993 the Fundacion Batuz in Dresden opened two permanent rooms with twenty works by Brizzi, single-source (C9216). In 1997 the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires held "Retrospectiva 1939-1997", single gallery-listing source, the 1939 start date implausible for a 1930-born artist and carried as suspect (C9216). In 2003 he was included in "Arte Abstracto Argentino" in Bergamo and at Fundacion Proa, Buenos Aires (C9216). In 2012 he showed in "Real / Virtual. Argentine kinetic art in the 60s" at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes (C9203).

He died in Buenos Aires in 2014, aged 84 (C9205, C9206, C9207, C9208, C9213). Posthumous shows include "Legado", Fundacion Naum Knop, Buenos Aires, 23 September - 15 December 2023, Brizzi shown with Vidal, MacEntyre and Blaszko, his daughter Barbara Brizzi representing the estate (C9208); and "Ary Brizzi. Arte, Diseno y Modernidad", Museo Nacional de Arte Decorativo, Buenos Aires, 17 October 2024 - March 2025, curated by Maria Jose Herrera, 300+ pieces, the first showing of the design facet (C9207, C9212). The first full monograph, Maria Jose Herrera's "Ary Brizzi: la armonia de la modernidad" (376 pp.), was published in 2025 with ISLAA support (C9207). His work is held by the Museo Castagnino (Sthenos VI), the Art Museum of the Americas (Interaccion No. 10, 1969), the Banco de la Republica, Bogota (Particion 4, 1971), the Museo Municipal de Bellas Artes, La Plata (Tension Multiple No. 2, 1970), and the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection (untitled 1964, painted metal) (C9201, C9214, C9215, C9252, C9253). No verified Brizzi holding was found at MNBA, MALBA or Museo Moderno; his presence there is exhibition only (C9208, leg 1).

**Takeaway:** the biography is the argument: a designer by trade who painted for sixty years, and whose design eye -- flat, hard-edged, serial, commercial-scale -- is visible in every painting. He never stopped being the man who laid out a pavilion; he just laid it out in light.

## M1 -- Seriation: one proposition, many numbered variations

A single formal proposition -- a fan, a beam, a column -- is repeated across a numbered series with small changes in colour or position of its elements, until the series itself shows the range of the proposition. Attested twice: "each exercise that belongs to a thematic series has color or position variations of its elements" (C9260); "chose to work in series, introducing small variations on a given initial proposition" (C9259). Visible in the catalogue: Kripton 3 (1977) and Krypton 16 (1979) are the same series two years apart; Sincronico 10 and Desplazamiento 2 are numbered members of their series; the daily tempera studies of 1952-1957 are the habit's origin, small daily exercises, each a colour or position variation on a proposition, most never exhibited (C9260). The series titles are the proposition's name: Kripton, Argon, Sincronico, Desplazamiento, Interaccion, Particion, Sthenos. A Brizzi painting is never a one-off; it is one numbered member, and the number matters because it says this is the tenth try at the same question.

**Takeaway:** the series is the work, not the single canvas. A piece that paints one fan once has illustrated Brizzi; a piece that cycles numbered variations of one fan has done what he did.

## M2 -- The pivot fan: a plane rotated in equal steps around a fixed point

A triangle or plane is copied at successive small rotations around a pivot point, each copy painted flat. Seen in Kripton 3 (W11, 1977): a bright white diagonal beam runs from a pivot at lower centre-left up to a second pivot at upper right, and around the beam a fan of thin grey-to-white triangular wedges spreads, densest near the beam and fading into the blue at the edges, with a second, darker fan opening below the lower pivot (C9251). The study author viewed W11 directly: a deep flat blue field, the white beam the brightest thing in the picture, the grey wedges reading as the beam's after-images, or as a plane rotating around the two pivots -- a frozen swing, a searchlight caught mid-arc (C9351). The same device returns in Argon 6 (W12, 1978), chevrons opening around the beam as a hinge; in Sincronico 10 (W15, 1991), two fans mirrored around a vertical bar; in Desplazamiento 2 (W14, 1986), a fan opening from a pivot at the top edge like a hand of cards fanned open (C9251). Speed and step-size are the controls: few large steps read as jerky, many small steps read as smooth rotation.

**Takeaway:** the fan is a rotation frozen by counting. A piece that draws a fan shape without the pivot -- wedges fanning from nowhere -- has the look and none of the mechanism; the pivot is the point the whole thing turns on, and it must be visible.

## M3 -- The degrade ramp: a stepwise colour scale along the fan

Colour changes in small equal steps from light to dark -- or white to near-black -- along the sequence of copies. Attested: "The scales of values, also called degrade, burst into the work of Brizzi and Martorell to blur planes, turn color into atmosphere" (C9259); "lines, cleverly organized with gradations of color" (C9252). Seen in every viewed painting: the grey ramp in Kripton 3, the green-grey-brown ramp in Argon 6, the blue ramp in Desplazamiento 2 and Sincronico 10 (C9251). The ramp is what makes the fan read as light rather than as geometry: the bright end advances, the dark end recedes, and the eye reads a gradient of value as a gradient of illumination. Reversing the ramp reverses the apparent direction of light; animating the ramp -- shifting which copy is brightest -- makes the beam pulse without moving at all.

**Takeaway:** the ramp is the light. A fan painted in one flat colour is a diagram; the same fan painted in graded steps is a searchlight. The number of steps is the resolution of the light.

## M4 -- Line multiplication: false rotation from dense lines

Many parallel or near-parallel lines, organised with colour gradation, generate an illusory revolving or displacing movement that is not in the paint. Attested verbatim in JCMC: "Brizzi made a series of works using the multiplication of lines as the generating source of the image and its forms. The lines, cleverly organized with gradations of color, produced false revolving or displacing movements that gave the works a characteristic dynamism" (C9252). The device sits inside Interaccion No. 10 (W07, 1969): concentric rounded-square frames in olive green, ochre and gold stepping inward, and at the centre a disc filled with fine vertical lines and diagonal hatching in gold-green, denser at the edges, with a pale vertical seam down the middle -- the line-multiplication motif in miniature, held inside the nested-frame motif (C9253). The movement belongs to the eye, not the object: the pattern shimmers and seems to turn as the viewer moves their head or as the lines shift by a pixel or two. This is the most directly generative mechanism in Brizzi: a simple rule -- copy a line, shift it, recolour it -- yields a movement no single line contains.

**Takeaway:** the motion is in the spacing, not in any playback. A piece that animates Brizzi's lines sliding around has mistaken the effect for the mechanism; the lines must stay put and the eye must do the moving.

## M5 -- Beam and slit: a single bright line cutting the field

One high-contrast linear element -- a diagonal white beam (Kripton 3, Argon 6), a vertical dark bar with a fan (Sincronico 10), a full-height white slit (Pleno) -- divides the field and organises everything around it. Attested in prose: "A beam of light breaks a plane, shatters into the colors that form it" (C9259). In Argon 6 (W12, 1978) the brilliant white diagonal beam runs from lower left to upper right with a thin black line down its centre, and flanking it on both sides a stack of nested chevrons in graded greys, greens and browns, each a slightly rotated, slightly darker copy of the last, like a folded sheet opening around the beam as a hinge; the fan is asymmetric, opening wider above the beam than below (C9251). In Pleno (W16, 1997) the beam has become a thin vertical white slit running the full height of the blue block, bisecting both the nested blue rectangles above and the orange semicircle below -- the single unifying element of the most architectural work viewed (C9251). The study author viewed W16 directly: a maroon ground, nested U-shaped bands in graded blues, largest outermost, smallest innermost, all cut by the white slit, and below the blue block a large semicircle in graded orange-yellow vertical stripes, also cut by the same slit; the nested bands read as a tunnel receding, the striped semicircle as a sun cut in half (C9352). The beam or slit is the hinge and the clock of the composition: everything rotates, nests or steps around it, and moving it a few degrees re-aims the whole picture.

**Takeaway:** count the beams. There is one, and it is the brightest thing in the picture. A piece with two competing beams, or a beam that is only decoration on a busy field, has lost the hinge the whole system hangs on.

## M6 -- Nested scaling and stepped translation

One shape is repeated at decreasing scale (nested) or at regular spatial offsets (stepped), each copy flat and hard-edged. Seen in Pleno (nested U-bands, largest to smallest, plus a striped semicircle), in Interaccion No. 10 (concentric rounded-square frames holding a line-filled disc), in Desplazamiento 2 (W14, 1986: thick diagonal bars in stepped blues descending in a staircase, each bar offset from the last, so the block reads as one slab sliding in steps), and in the chevron stack of Argon 6 (C9251, C9253). The early form is the 1964 Untitled (W05): a white ground, a central vertical column of green rectangles striped with blue and black vertical bands, the column broken into stacked blocks with white gaps, small red, black and blue trapezoids floating off-axis left and right like fragments thrown off the column -- a cut-up striped column, a pattern interrupted and scattered (C9252). The title of W14 is literal: things are displaced -- the fan is a rotation displaced in steps, the bars are a translation displaced in steps. Nesting reads as a tunnel or zoom, the eye pulled inward step by step; stepping reads as a slide, a slab moving in discrete jumps. Animating the step size makes the tunnel breathe or the slab march.

**Takeaway:** nesting is depth without perspective, stepping is motion without animation. A piece that scales shapes smoothly has made a zoom; Brizzi's nesting is stepped, countable, and the count is the depth.

## M7 -- Advance and recession of colour

Warm, light or saturated colours advance toward the viewer; dark, cool or desaturated colours recede. Named in the sources: "Attractions and repulsions, colors that advance or recede" (C9260); "sensual curves navigate through spaces of clear colors; they attract, repel, change course" (C9259). Seen in Pleno, the orange semicircle advancing against the maroon ground while the blue block recedes; in Kripton 3, the white beam leaping off deep blue; in Argon 6, the white beam on near-black (C9251). In Sincronico 10 (W15, 1991) the mechanism is at its most literal: a pale blue-grey ground, a dark navy vertical bar slightly right of centre running top to bottom, and fanning out from the bar to left and right two symmetric fans of translucent blue triangles in graded blues, left fan opening downward-left, right fan opening upward-right, like a propeller or a book opening both ways at once; the triangles overlap the bar and each other, their overlaps making slightly darker blues -- the painted equivalent of the real Plexiglas layering of the early sculptures (C9251). The picture breathes in depth without any perspective: planes swell forward and sink back as their colours shift.

**Takeaway:** depth in Brizzi is a colour decision, not a drawing decision. A piece that adds perspective lines or shadows to make depth has left his system; change which colour is warmest and the depth changes by itself.

## M8 -- Material light: transparency, reflection, motor and neon

Real light behaviour as a medium: Plexiglas and laminated acrylic transmitting and layering light, aluminium reflecting it, enamel giving a hard glossy surface, and -- in the early-1960s sculptures -- motors and neon making light and form actually move. Attested: JCMC on aluminium, Plexiglas, enamel, motors and neon (C9252); Google Arts and Culture on plexiglass and laminated acrylic sculpture (C9258); the critic Basilio Uribe, quoted in GAC: "access to technology... translates to new materials" (C9258). This is the only literally kinetic mechanism in Brizzi, and it is prose-only: no individual sculpture title, date, dimensions or image was retrieved, and no motor or neon work was seen in motion (C9252, C9258). The early-1960s sculptures are the material original of everything painted later: the translucency of overlapping triangles in Sincronico 10 is painted Plexiglas; the amber strip at the foot of Focus (W09, c.1970) -- a tall dark vertical slab, 190 x 50 x 60 cm, standing on a black ground against white, with a short horizontal amber-yellow translucent strip of laminated acrylic catching light at its base -- is the bridge between the sculpture period and the painted beam (C9251). In a screen piece this becomes layered translucency plus a slow real-time rotation or glow cycle: the one mechanism in Brizzi that was literally kinetic rather than illusory, and the one that must be handled most carefully, because no moving original survives to check against.

**Takeaway:** Brizzi's light started as real light and became painted light. A piece that treats the beam as only a painted shape has the late work without its origin; the beam is a neon tube remembered in paint.

## Evolution: striped column, real light, painted beam, white slit

Early (1952-1964): small, hand-scale, on cardboard or wood -- tempera studies 22 x 15 cm and 24 x 24 cm, enamel on wood 199 x 33 cm (the exception, tall and narrow), painted metal 18 x 60 cm (C9254, C9256, C9257, C9252). Material: tempera, synthetic enamel, painted metal; oil is present early and then abandoned (C9252). Motif: the striped column -- vertical bands of colour, cut, stacked and scattered (W05). Geometry is rigorous and flat; the "movement" is implied by fragmentation, not by illusion. Context: daily private study (1952-1957), first solo show 1958, pavilion design 1959, Paris Biennial 1959. The work is a laboratory: small formats, many variations, mostly unpublished. No early image was viewable this run: the Artsy images for W02 and W04 exist on their pages but were not extracted, and the Christie's image for W03 was not retrieved.

Middle (early 1960s-1970s): scale jumps -- sculpture, then the 100 x 100 cm canvas as standard (W07, W11, W13). Material: aluminium, Plexiglas, laminated acrylic, motors, neon; then acrylic on canvas replaces oil entirely (C9252). Motif: real light and real movement give way to painted light and painted movement. The line-multiplication series (W10, late 1960s) states the turn; the Kripton/Argon series (W11-W13, 1977-1979) is the payoff: dark ground, one bright beam, a fan of graded copies around a pivot. Series naming turns scientific: Kripton, Argon (lighting gases), Interaccion, Activated Surface, Focus -- the titles name light, gas, interaction, activation. The viewer changes role: the work no longer asks to be looked at, it asks to be moved past -- the false movement only happens in the eye, over time.

Late (1986-1997): format splits -- small 50 x 50 cm (W14, W15) and back to large 100 x 100 cm (W16). Palette brightens and flattens: saturated blues in Desplazamiento 2, pale blue-grey in Sincronico 10, maroon, blue and orange in Pleno, against the near-black grounds of Argon 6. Motif: the same kit, recombined. The fan is now double and synchronous (W15); displacement is stepped and literal (W14); nesting is architectural and the beam has become a static white slit (W16). The movement is calmer: less searchlight, more structure. Pleno reads as a summary -- nested frames, striped semicircle, one slit unifying both halves.

What does NOT change across 45 years: the square format, the series method (one proposition, many numbered variations), the flat hard edge, and the refusal of figuration. What changes is the light source: from real neon and motor (1960s) to a painted beam (1970s) to a painted slit (1997).

## The network: the circle, the school, the most frequent co-exhibitor

The anchor is Vidal. Four shared rooms, all evidenced, plus a shared school: 1963, Eight Constructive Artists, Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, invited by Jorge Romero Brest, Brizzi and Maria Martorell "together with Manuel Espinosa, Raul Lozza, Eduardo Sabelli, Miguel Angel Vidal and Carlos Silva" (C9303); 1963, Del Arte Concreto a la Nueva Tendencia, Museo de Arte Moderno, Buenos Aires, included both Brizzi and Martorell (C9303); 1968, Bonino Gallery, New York, MacEntyre "showed with Brizzi, Polesello and Vidal" (C9312); 1972, "Projection and Dynamics", Museo de Arte Moderno, Buenos Aires, then Musee d'art moderne de la Ville de Paris, "the two members of Generative Art, together with Ary Brizzi and Carlos Silva, exhibited" in Buenos Aires, with Manuel Espinosa's and Miguel Ocampo's works added in Paris (C9312, carried from #635 leg 3, C9103; not re-opened this run). Doctrine: Brizzi's work "has been developed from the theories of Ignacio Pirovano and the Grupo Arte Generativo" (C9301). Shared school: Brizzi taught at the Pueyrredon from 1972 until 1995 (C9301); Vidal was its principal 1989-1991 (C9312, carried from #635); the overlap is about two years, not twenty-three -- see D2.

MacEntyre: the same five-name circle -- Brizzi turned to abstraction, "an interest that he shared with his colleagues Eduardo Mac Entyre, Manuel Alvarez, Miguel Angel Vidal, Carlos Silva and Cesar Paternosto" (C9302). Shared rooms 1968 Bonino New York and 1972 Projection and Dynamics (C9312). No teacher-pupil link: MacEntyre was self-taught and Brizzi was formally trained; the link is collegial and doctrinal, Brizzi developing the generativists' theories a generation later (C9301).

Carlos Silva (1930-1987): the closest peer, and the #637 pick. Same birth year as Brizzi, same city, same generation, same circle, four shared rooms across 1961-2021: 1961 Brizzi-Santiso-Silva exhibition, 8 May 1961 (C9309); 1963 Eight Constructive Artists (C9303); 1972 Projection and Dynamics (C9312); 2021 Brizzi-Lozza-Silva, Alejandro Faggioni Estudio de Arte, Buenos Aires, "Tres grandes maestros de la abstraccion constructiva" (C9307). Silva won the Premio Nacional of the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella in 1965 with "Thalassios" (C9308, C9309); judge Giulio Carlo Argan is quoted as saying Silva "se adelanto un anio a Vasarely" with his series of displaced points (C9309). Born Buenos Aires 13 November 1930; died Buenos Aires 12 June 1987 (C9309, C9308).

Cesar Paternosto (b. La Plata 1931): same five-name circle (C9302, C9304); two shared rooms -- 1967 Beyond Geometry, Instituto Torcuato Di Tella (C9302, C9312), and 1964 Magnet: New York, Galeria Bonino, New York, carried from #635 (C9312, C9110, not re-opened this run). Maria Martorell (b. Salta 1909, d. 2010): the most frequent co-exhibitor. "Few artists shared as many spaces and a common vision as Ary Brizzi and Maria Martorell" (C9303); they "participaron conjuntamente en mas de 14 muestras" -- more than 14 joint shows (C9306), the first July 1963, Eight Constructive Artists, at Romero Brest's invitation (C9306, C9303). The 1940s roots: no direct contact between Brizzi and Carmelo Arden Quin was found; the link is doctrinal and institutional -- Brizzi's early abstract geometry "was compatible with the Madi movement and the Asociacion Arte Concreto-Invencion" (C9301); Madi was founded among others by Arden Quin, manifesto 1946 (C9301). Max Bill: "Influenced by the Swiss artist Max Bill, he turned to abstraction" (C9302); Castagnino+macro names Vasarely, Albers "and especially, Max Bill" as his rational-concrete models (C9304). Jorge Romero Brest invited Brizzi and Martorell to the 1963 show as MNBA director: promoter, not collaborator (C9303, C9306). No Brizzi correspondence, joint statement, manifesto signature or studio link with any of Vidal, MacEntyre, Silva or Paternosto was found; the links are shared rooms, a shared circle, a shared school and a shared doctrine. ## Aesthetic logic

Brizzi's aesthetic is the logic of a designer executing a rule without deviation, and the rule is about light, not about shape. Pick one proposition -- a fan, a beam, a column -- repeat it in numbered variations, grade the colours in equal steps, and let the accumulation do the work. The beauty is not in any single wedge or bar; no wedge in a Brizzi is interesting on its own. It is in the gradient the wedges produce together, the way a field of identical flat shapes becomes a searchlight, a swing, a tunnel. That is why the early sculptures matter even though none survives to be seen: the light in the paintings is remembered light, real neon and real Plexiglas translated into flat acrylic, and the degrade ramp is the translation. It is also why the late work, which calms the fan into a static slit, is still Brizzi: the rule was never the fan, it was the generation, and when the element changed from real light to painted beam to painted slit, the generation stayed. The square format never changes, the series method never changes, the flat hard edge never changes, the palette never exceeds one ground, one ramp and one beam in the mature work. Restraint is not a limitation here; it is the proof. A Brizzi that is busy, curved, asymmetrical or multicoloured has not added to the system; it has left it.

The contrast with Vidal is the study's spine, and it is a contrast inside one movement. Vidal's generator is a polygon displaced step by step, his lines joining successive positions until a luminous solid hovers; Brizzi's generator is a plane rotated around a pivot, his wedges fanning until a beam of light seems to sweep. Vidal's light comes from foci, points where lines converge; Brizzi's light comes from a ramp, a scale of values along the fan. Vidal drew straight lines with a ruler; Brizzi laid out planes like a designer laying out a stand. The movement held both, because the manifesto defined generation, not geometry: "engenders a series of optical sequences through a development generated by a form". The form was Vidal's polygon and Brizzi's fan; the development was the same. That is why the two studies belong together, and why Brizzi is the next generation rather than the second voice: younger by two years, trained where Vidal taught, carrying the same rule into a second set of hands.
