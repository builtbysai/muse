# Marcus Gheeraerts the Younger: Study Notes

**Site:** Marcus Gheeraerts the Younger (c.1561/62 - 19 January 1636), Flemish portrait painter at the Tudor and Jacobean courts, described by Roy Strong as "the most important artist of quality to work in England in large-scale between Eworth and van Dyck". Born in Bruges, son of the painter and printmaker Marcus Gheeraerts the Elder by his first wife Johanna; brought to England as a child when his father fled Protestant persecution under the Duke of Alba, father and son recorded living with a Dutch servant in the London parish of St Mary Abchurch in 1568, the boy aged about seven. His training is undocumented: probably his father's pupil, possibly also taught by Lucas de Heere during de Heere's residence in England, though the old Dictionary of National Biography notes the Antwerp guild entry usually cited for him refers to his father, not to him. On 19 May 1590 he married Magdalena de Critz at the Dutch Church, Austin Friars, sister of his stepmother Susanna and of the painter John de Critz; the couple had six children, only two of whom survived, Marcus III (c.1602 - c.1654), also a painter, and Henry (1604-1650). His half-sister Sara, the Elder's daughter by Susanna de Critz, married the miniaturist Isaac Oliver in 1602, making Gheeraerts Oliver's brother-in-law (subject of #524). From around 1590, under the patronage of Sir Henry Lee, Queen's Champion and pageant-master, he led what Strong calls a "revolution" in English portraiture: sitters rendered in three dimensions through tonality and shadow, sombre colour and greyed flesh, against the flat modelling and pure brilliant colour of Hilliard's generation, painted in oil on canvas at life-size full length, one of the first English artists to work on canvas rather than panel. The Ditchley Portrait of Elizabeth I (c.1592, NPG 2561) and the 1596 full-length of the Earl of Essex at Woburn Abbey are his supreme Elizabethan works; his 1591 portrait of Drake shows Drake wearing Hilliard's Drake Jewel. After Elizabeth's death in 1603 he remained at the forefront of fashion: Anne of Denmark employed him for large paintings and his brother-in-law Oliver for miniatures, and in 1611 he was paid for portraits of the King, Queen and Princess Elizabeth (the treasurer's accounts record four portraits, the fourth of Prince Charles; see C315). His 1611 portrait of Frances Howard, Countess of Hertford, is the first known draped-curtain portrait setting, later used by William Larkin. Isaac Oliver died in 1617, and around the same time Gheeraerts' position at court began to decline before a new generation of Netherlandish immigrants; Paul van Somer had likely displaced him as Anne's chief portraitist some time before her death in 1619, though Gheeraerts walked in her funeral procession as "Queen's Painter". For the last twenty years of his life he was employed chiefly by the country gentry and by academic sitters. He was a member of the Court of the Painter-Stainers' Company in the 1620s, had an apprentice, Ferdinando Clifton, freeman of the Company in 1627, painted a self-portrait in 1627, now lost and known only through Wenceslas Hollar's engraving of 1644, and died in London on 19 January 1636 (the old DNB gives 19 January 1635, Old Style; see C310). The record is strong on the technique, thanks to Tate's published technical studies of Captain Thomas Lee and the Woman in Red, and thin on his own words: no treatise or letter of his survives, and the only verbatim words of Gheeraerts found in this run are in the 1618 return of foreigners. ## M1. Three-dimensional tonal modelling against Hilliard's flat modelling

The central mechanism of the "revolution" Strong dates to around 1590. Wikipedia, summarising Strong (1993) and Hearn (1995): "For the first time in English art sitters were rendered in three dimensions, achieving a lifelike impression through tonality and shadow." Against this stands the flat modelling of features and pure, brilliant colours associated with Elizabethan artists such as Nicolas Hilliard. The mechanism is modelling by graded tone and cast shadow rather than by outline and flat local colour: a face is built as a volume lit from one side, with the shadowed side sunk, so the head reads as a solid turning in space. Strong's gloss, quoted in Wikipedia: "The implications suggest that Oliver and Gheeraerts singly or together visited Antwerp in the late eighties and were influenced by the portrait style of Frans Pourbus." That Antwerp visit is a suggestion, not a documented fact. Leg 2's tonal-modelling mechanism folds in here: the face is painted thinly, wet-in-wet, in flesh tones mixed on the palette, over a warm-toned priming that is allowed to glow through, while the costume and background are built up in orderly layers (translucent underpainting, bright opaque paint, decorative detail, rich translucent glaze). The result is two paint systems in one picture: a lit face against flat, patterned, opaque costume.

**Takeaway:** the head stops being a flat badge and becomes a volume; the revolution is a lighting change before it is a style change.

## M2. Sombre colour and greyed flesh tones

"New too were capturing the character of individual sitters through close observation and the use of sombre colour and greyed flesh tones." The flesh is not the porcelain white of Hilliard's miniatures but a muted, greyed range: lead white cut with black, azurite and yellow ochre (see M7). Tate's reading of Portrait of an Unknown Lady (c.1595) makes the mechanism explicit: "Largely limited to a discreet range of black, white and the palest of greys, it benefits from the discipline of an artist who knows when to stop." Only the sitter's brazenly painted lips and a few crimson jewels in her hair break free from the pared-down palette. The Ditchley portrait's NPG analysis adds the period logic: high-born women were depicted "as though drained of blood: their blanched skin proved their pedigree."

**Takeaway:** restraint is the palette: the greyed flesh is a social code, pedigree made visible, and the one red accent is the exception that proves the rule.

## M3. Oil on canvas instead of panel: the scale enabler

"Gheeraerts was one of the first English artists to paint on canvas rather than wood panel, allowing much larger pictures to be produced." Tate's technical research on Captain Thomas Lee (1594) confirms the support: "This painting is in oil paint on canvas measuring 2150 x 1330 mm. The canvas is a single piece of plain, very finely woven linen. To date, it is the earliest painting on canvas in Tate's collection." Tate adds the European context: by the mid-sixteenth century in Venice stretched linen had all but replaced panel, "but elsewhere the change was more gradual," and "in this, as in so many other matters artistic, England appears to have lagged behind. Marcus Gheeraerts was among the first artists to popularise canvas for formal English portraits." Christie's makes the same point: he "was one of the first artists in Britain to paint in oil on canvas rather than on panel, an innovation which was particularly suitable for full-length portraits, and his work seems to have done much to make such full-length portraits fashionable." Note the split practice: he still painted on panel for smaller works (William Camden 1609, Ellen Maurice 1597, Mary Throckmorton 1615, Tom Durie 1614, Margaret Laton c.1620 are all oil on panel), so canvas is a format choice tied to scale, not a total conversion.

**Takeaway:** the support is the enabler: the Ditchley Portrait at 2413 x 1524 mm exists only because the picture stopped being a board.

## M4. Wet-in-wet on canvas vs sequential layering on panel

Tate's technical study of Captain Thomas Lee gives the working method in unusual detail: "In most areas of the painting the colours were pre-mixed on the palette and worked into one another wet-in-wet on the prepared canvas support. Most colours are opaque. The only discernible preparatory underpainting is in the shield on his shoulder, where opaque paint was glazed with a red lake." And: "The extensive use of wet-in-wet work in this painting on canvas is quite different from Gheeraerts's technique for panel paintings; there he painted the face wet-in-wet but used a sequential, layered technique for the costumes and backgrounds." So the support dictates the handling: canvas invites direct, blended, single-session paint; panel invites built-up layers, with the face kept wet-in-wet as the one direct passage. The Woman in Red (1620, oil on oak panel) shows the panel system at its most finished: the face painted wet-in-wet in thin flesh tones letting the salmon-pink priming glow through, while curtains, chair, background and costume were built up in orderly layers, thin translucent underpainting, then bright opaque paint, decorative detail and rich translucent glazes.

**Takeaway:** two supports, two speeds: canvas is painted in one wet breath, panel is built in layers, and the face is always the fast passage.

## M5. Ground and priming build

From the Tate study of Captain Thomas Lee, the preparation of the canvas: "The ground is off-white in colour and is composed mainly of chalk with gypsum, lead white, some ochres and lamp black. It was applied after the canvas had been given a thick coat of animal glue size. The ground is covered by white priming, which is thicker than the ground and is made principally of lead white with small amounts of the other ingredients. Both layers appear to be bound in oil." A cross-section through a vein in the sitter's right leg shows the stack from the bottom: white ground; white priming; black pigment for the vein; flesh paint; traces of varnish. The bright white ground-plus-priming is the reflective bed that lets thin flesh paint glow and lets dark underdrawing read through as tone (see M6). On panel the recipe changes: Mary Rogers (1592) has a white chalk ground about 200 microns thick and a pale bluish-grey priming of lead white and charcoal black, left visible as the grey of bodice and sleeves; the Woman in Red has an off-white chalk ground about 80 microns thick and an opaque salmon-pink priming about 25 microns thick (lead white, chalk, red ochre, yellow ochre, red lake, black) which gives the overall warm tone, the rosy glow in the face, and the illusion of skin beneath the thin paint of the linen kerchief and cuffs.

**Takeaway:** the ground is the light source: white under canvas flesh, salmon-pink under panel flesh, and the priming colour is the portrait's undertone.

## M6. The turbid medium effect: underdrawing as visible anatomy

The most distinctive single technical device in the evidence. In Captain Thomas Lee, Gheeraerts drew the figure outline "fairly simply in black crayon," and the veins in the bare legs are black crayon lines left under thin flesh paint: "This usage relies on the knowledge that a dark line or mark beneath a thin layer of lighter opaque paint will read as a darker, pearly tone of the overlying colour; it is known as the 'turbid medium effect'. Many a sixteenth-century portrait painter utilised it in the flesh tones... Here, however, Gheeraerts has not used it in the densely painted face and hands, in which all the tones were mixed up on the palette and applied wet-in-wet; he has kept it only for the striking presence of the veins in the legs." A cross-section through the vein on the front of the left leg "shows the black crayon-line beneath the flesh tone, in which colour there is no modulation at this point. Given the tendency of oil paint to become more translucent with time, these lines are probably more visible now than in 1594; nevertheless they would always have been a noticeable and quite intentional feature of this portrait." So a drawing line is deliberately left to function as anatomy: the vein is not painted on top, it is the underdrawing showing through.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is not scaffolding to be hidden; in the legs it IS the painting, a black line doing the work of a vein.

## M7. Pigment system

Tate's analysis of Captain Thomas Lee lists the palette concretely: "The sky was made from mixtures of lead white and smalt, in which pigment there has been some fading. The principal pigment in all the greens is azurite mixed with varying amounts of yellow and red earth colours, lead tin yellow or massicot, Cologne earth, black, green earth and pale smalt or ground glass. For the distant, bluish green hills Gheeraerts mixed the azurite with lead white and smalt... The flesh tones contain lead white, chalk, vermilion and small amounts of black, azurite and yellow ochre. His shirt is lead white mixed with bone or ivory black, and the blackwork embroidery was applied on top. His jerkin appears to be lamp black, the blackest of black pigments but a very slow drier in oil. This probably accounts for the presence of ground glass or pale smalt in the mixture to act as a siccative. Ground glass occurs also and for the same reason with the yellow ochre describing the braid on his jerkin. The highlights here are lead tin yellow or massicot. The shield was painted first in an opaque red mixture before being given a thick glaze of red lake with a bit of black mixed in to enrich its tone." Two working facts fall out: he used ground glass / pale smalt as a drier for slow-drying lamp black, and smalt fading has shifted his greens and skies (a wood that now looks yellowish brown "was probably a dark bluish green").

**Takeaway:** the palette is a chemistry set with a clock in it: smalt fades, lamp black will not dry, and the picture you see now is not the colour he laid down.

## M8. Costume, jewellery and material simulation

The DNB's 1890 judgement: "The rich costumes and accessories are always carefully executed." Bonhams, on Ellen Maurice (1597), describes "beautiful drawing, soft modelling and a skilful manipulation of paint to mimic the texture and properties of various surfaces and materials." The mechanism is discriminating touch per material: pearls painted as plump white fruit with a hard highlight; lace as a radiating sun-burst ruff; embroidery as silver-gilt thread with floral motifs; spangles that "would have shimmered in the light when she moved" (Christie's on Frances Howard); blackwork embroidery laid on top of a lead-white-and-black shirt (Tate technical). Tate on the Unknown Lady: pearls "dance around her dark hair, dangle from her blanched forehead, curl round her neck and hang like plump white fruits from her ear-lobes... cascade from both shoulders before curving upwards to be held, in an almost nonchalant loop, between her breasts." The NPG's Ditchley analysis inventories the same system at full scale: hundreds of pearls in hair, neck and collar; a dress covered in gold stitching and precious stones; a gold necklace; gloves and a fan; a fine lace ruff. Costume is not decoration added to a face; it is half the portrait's information, and it is painted with a different, more linear and descriptive touch than the soft-modelled flesh. The Tate technical reports make the touch concrete: in Mary Rogers, pearls are applied with quick precise swirls of pale grey paint (lead white, smalt, black) with a pure lead-white catchlight and a pale yellow reflected light; in Lady Scudamore, three different floral designs on doublet, coat and skirt are painted with "incredible confidence", translucency of skin and floating lace against the weight of brocaded fabrics.

**Takeaway:** the face is observed and the dress is inventoried; the portrait's texture is the tension between the two touches.

## M9. Face-pattern, workshop copying and the "softened" versions

Most Elizabethan portraits were not painted from life: "Only a few portraits of Elizabeth were painted from life (while she stood in front of the artist). Most of the artists who painted her portrait never even met her. They used a 'pattern' of her face as a guide." (NPG schools hub.) The Ditchley Portrait is the case study. The queen likely sat for the original, but "the new portrait aesthetic did not please the aging queen, and in the many versions of this painting made with the allegorical items removed, likely in Gheeraerts' workshop, Elizabeth's features are 'softened' from the stark realism of her face in the original. One of these was sent as a diplomatic gift to Ferdinando I de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany and is now in the Palazzo Pitti." The workshop therefore operated a two-track system: a hard, observed prime version from the sitting, then softened, de-allegorised replicas for circulation. The same pattern logic governed Anne of Denmark: John de Critz "established a facial pattern for Anne, which was used in subsequent depictions by other artists until a work by Marcus Gheeraerts the younger (1611-14; Woburn Abbey) superseded the de Critz pattern" (Government Art Collection). And the Rainbow Portrait's 2024 restoration found "underdrawings suggesting the face was created using a pre-existing pattern, so the queen need not have sat for this portrait" (artnet). The face-pattern is a reusable template, traced or pounced onto new supports, that fixes the royal face while costume, setting and emblems change around it.

**Takeaway:** the royal face is state property, reproducible and softenable on demand; the workshop's softened Ditchleys are the first English instance of image management.

## M10. The draped-curtain portrait setting

"His 1611 portrait of Frances Howard, Countess of Hertford in rich attire framed by a draped silk curtain, with a fringed pelmet across the top of the canvas, is the first known instance of a portrait setting that would be used by Hilliard's former apprentice William Larkin in a series of full-length portraits between 1613 and 1618." (Wikipedia, citing Strong 1993 and Hearn 2003.) Christie's describes the evolved form in the same picture: the sitter "full-length, strongly lit, beside an opulently upholstered x-framed chair, between looped shiny satin curtains, standing on a luxurious imported carpet." The curtain does three mechanical jobs at once: it frames the figure and crops the world to a stage; it supplies a dark, shiny vertical against which pale flesh and white linen read; and it signals interior luxury without painting a room. The setting was copied by Larkin and became the standard Jacobean full-length formula. Leg 2's inventory adds the mechanics: the Ushak ("Lotto") carpet from Anatolia underfoot, the looped shiny satin, the fringed pelmet across the top, the X-framed chair; the same furniture grammar recurs in the late gentry portraits (Magdalen Poultney: red velvet and gold-trimmed curtains and chair, Turkish carpet).

**Takeaway:** the curtain is a stage, not a room: it converts the full-length portrait into theatre, and Larkin's adoption proves it was a transferable device, not a one-off.

## M11. Full-length figure outdoors in a naturalistic landscape

"He also introduced the full-length figure set out-out-of-doors in a naturalistic landscape for full-scale portraiture, a feature seen in portrait miniatures of the same era." The Ditchley Portrait puts Elizabeth on a map of England with storms behind her and sun before her; the 1596 Essex at Woburn stands in a landscape with burning Cadiz behind him; Captain Thomas Lee (1594) stands in an open Irish landscape on the left of the canvas, with a wood and pond and armed soldiers on the right referring to his victory at Belleek. The landscape is not backdrop but biography: place, campaign and weather carry the sitter's story. Christie's notes the Captain Lee portrait as "one of the earliest full-length English portraits to be set in a landscape background." Leg 2's contribution is the per-picture landscape content: the map under Elizabeth's feet, Cadiz burning behind Essex, the Irish bog behind Lee, each a different argument carried by the ground itself.

**Takeaway:** the ground the sitter stands on is the argument of the portrait; change the landscape and you change what the picture claims.

## M12. Emblematic and iconographic portraiture

The portrait as a coded device. In the Ditchley Portrait: the queen stands on a globe bearing a map of England, her feet on Oxfordshire; storms rage behind her while the sun shines before her; she wears a jewel in the form of a celestial or armillary sphere close to her left ear; three Latin inscriptions read (in the NPG's interpretation) "She gives and does not expect", "She can but does not take revenge", and "In giving back she increases". In the Rainbow Portrait (c.1600-02, attributed to Gheeraerts): the queen grasps a rainbow with the motto "non sine sole iris" ("No rainbow without the sun"), wears a cloak with eyes and ears, the serpent of wisdom, and the celestial armillary sphere. In Captain Thomas Lee: the sitter wears Irish kerne dress (bare legs, half-open shirt), a Latin motto from Livy hangs from a tree, "Facere et pati Fortia" ("To act and suffer bravely"), and the landscape splits between open country and a battle scene. Leg 2's object inventory extends the system across the oeuvre: Drake's jewel, globe, coat of arms and sword; Frances Howard's pansy wreath and sprig (thought), her mantle embroidered with her initials "F" and "S", spangled lace that would shimmer when she moved; Lady Scudamore's floral wreath and motto as a statement about family regeneration; Magdalen Poultney's ringless left hand as a marriage advertisement. The mechanism is emblem-book literacy: each object is a legible symbol, and the portrait is assembled like a pageant device rather than a likeness alone.

**Takeaway:** the sitter is still, frontal and hieratic; the objects do the talking, and the portrait reads like a rebus.

## M13. Preparation: reuse, false starts and the unfinished sketch

Tate's technical study shows Gheeraerts reusing supports and changing his mind in paint. Before drawing the Captain Lee figure, "he had started another portrait, whose painted head and collar appear in the X-radiograph. This head is off-set from centre and no other trace of the figure is visible with any form of technical examination. The absence of a beard makes it unlikely that this was a false start for Captain Lee." A cross-section from the right side of Lee's hair shows "the pink flesh paint of the underlying head" and "the coat of off-white paint used locally to obliterate it." Separately, the unfinished portrait sketch of Sir Henry Lee (c.1600, oil on canvas) is described by Wikipedia as "likely to have been from life", a rare surviving record of a life sitting, where the drawing is left visible and the paint uncompleted. Together these show a practice of drawing first in black crayon on the prepared support, painting the head from the sitting, and reworking or abandoning whole figures on the same canvas.

**Takeaway:** the canvas keeps its history: a painted-out head under the finished portrait, and an unfinished sketch that shows the sitting itself.

## M14. The pregnancy portrait and the restrained palette

Tate's exhibition text identifies a sub-genre Gheeraerts helped define: "Paradoxically, it was under this celibate monarch that a surprising English sub-genre, the 'pregnancy portrait' developed. These portraits depict women who are clearly - even exaggeratedly - pregnant... Gheeraerts painted a number of these." The Unknown Lady (c.1595) is the type example: the swell of the belly "is shown with relish, and more pearls pepper the dress enclosing its ripeness." Tate's reading ties the mechanism to mortality: "Pregnancy portraits were commissioned not only as proud demonstrations of dynastic prowess, but as records of deeply cherished wives who might not survive their confinement... one in a hundred women died as a direct result of the stress endured in labour." The portrait's power comes from the collision of celebration and warning: "Almost as white as a corpse, she can easily be imagined lying horizontally on a tomb. By portraying her magnificence even as he warns of the hazards to come, Gheeraerts produces a poignant image where joy and anxiety compete for the viewer's attention." Leg 2's catalogue extends the type: Anne, Lady Pope, with her children (1596), pregnant at 35 with her first child by her second husband, surrounded by her three children from her first marriage; the Woman in Red (1620), expansive belly in a red dress before a red curtain. Karen Hearn's term is "pregnancy portraits"; cultural historian Marieke de Winkel notes it was a relatively unique proclivity in early modern European portrait painting.

**Takeaway:** the belly is the subject: a silhouette that carries both dynastic hope and the one-in-a-hundred chance of death, painted with restraint rather than symbol overload.

## M15. Inscription as image: age, date and motto painted into the picture

Text painted into the portrait itself, the sitter's age and the year, a motto, a Latin tag, functioning as part of the composition, not as a later label. Lady Jermyn (1614): "Aetatis Suae 35 Anno: 1614" painted by Gheeraerts himself in the upper right corner. Lady Scudamore (1615): the wedding date of 12 March 1615 inscribed at top right, surrounded by a floral wreath, with the motto "No Spring Till now". Captain Thomas Lee (1594): the Livy motto "Facere et pati Fortia" hanging from a tree. The Ditchley portrait (1592): three Latin inscriptions and a sonnet in a cartouche. The inscription fixes the portrait to a dated occasion, a 35th birthday, a son's wedding, a military reputation, and turns the picture into a dated document. Strong's Burlington Magazine work on inscriptions (1963) made this the scholarly key to Gheeraerts' dating; Millar's sequel the same year followed it.

**Takeaway:** the lettering is part of the picture plane: text that can be read, that frames the figure, that dates the image from inside.

## Evolution

Early phase, c.1586-1592: panel, attribution, apprenticeship to the court. The earliest signed works date from c.1592, but Roy Strong identified Burghley portraits of around 1586 as likely based on a Gheeraerts original. The early work is small-scale and on panel, in the established Elizabethan idiom: bust and half-length formats, flat modelling, brilliant colour. What is new even here is the continental, Flemish training showing through, close observation of the individual face rather than a repeated pattern. The 1592 pictures are the hinge: Mary Rogers is still oil on oak panel, but the Ditchley portrait is oil on canvas at 2413 x 1524 mm, the largest surviving image of Elizabeth I. The move to canvas is the technical enabler of everything that follows.

Middle phase, 1592-1603: the revolution. Three changes arrive together and are visible across W1, W2, W4, W6, W7: scale and support (canvas replaces panel for formal portraits, allowing life-size full-length figures); setting (the full-length figure set out-of-doors in a naturalistic landscape for the first time in English full-scale portraiture); modelling (sitters rendered in three dimensions through tonality and shadow, sombre colour and greyed flesh tones). Patronage in this phase is Lee and Essex: Lee, the Queen's Champion and pageant-master, became Gheeraerts's patron around 1590; Essex employed him from 1596. The sitters are heroes and the Queen herself, and the pictures are emblematic, allegory carried by objects, inscriptions and costume. The same years produce the workshop system: assistants completing backgrounds and details on the big canvases, and many surviving copies and variants (the "softened" Ditchley patterns). The 1596 Anne, Lady Pope group portrait shows the other middle-phase invention, the pregnancy portrait. The phase ends with the deaths of its patrons: Essex and Thomas Lee were both executed for treason in 1601, yet Gheeraerts's popularity "does not seem to have been tainted" by the association.

Jacobean phase, 1603-1619: the Queen's painter, quieter mood, curtain interiors. After Elizabeth's death Gheeraerts remained at the forefront of fashion. Anne of Denmark employed him for large-scale paintings while Oliver did her miniatures; in 1611 he was paid for portraits of the king, queen, Princess Elizabeth (and Prince Charles, see C315), and in 1617 he was referred to as "her Majesties painter". The Jacobean portraits are characterised, in Strong's phrase, by "quietness, pensiveness, and gentle charm of mood", seen in Lady Jermyn (1614) and Lady Scudamore (1615). What changed mechanically: half-length and three-quarter-length on panel return for private sitters alongside full-length canvas for state portraits; the outdoor landscape gives way to the draped interior, the 1611 Frances Howard being the first known instance of the curtain setting Larkin then used 1613-1618; the virtuoso rendering of fabric texture becomes the main display, with the face handled more softly and naturalistically; inscriptions become part of the design, age and date painted by the artist's own hand, mottoes and wreaths. Competition arrives between 1616 and 1618: a new wave of Netherlandish portraitists (Paul van Somer, Daniel Mytens) with a new aesthetic displaces him; Isaac Oliver died in 1617; Anne died in 1619. Gheeraerts walked in her funeral procession as "Queen's Painter", but van Somer had likely displaced him as her chief portraitist some time before.

Late phase, 1619-1636: country gentry, old-fashioned formality. For the last twenty years of his life Gheeraerts was employed chiefly by the country gentry and by academic sitters. The late pictures keep the Jacobean machinery, panel support, layered costume painting over a warm priming, inscribed dates, pregnancy as subject, but the manner is described as stiff and old-fashioned: by the time of Magdalen Poultney (c.1620) his "stiff, formal manner was regarded as old-fashioned" (AGSA). The compositions are more frontal and symmetrical, the sitters are gentry women rather than queens, and the portraits function as social documents, marriage advertisements, records of a wife before childbirth, remarriage portraits. The one late flash of the old vitality is the c.1621 Frances Howard, which Graham-Dixon calls "disconcertingly vivacious" and "a vibrant, flesh-and-blood presence", though still with "a trace of playing-card stiffness". He was a member of the Court of the Painter-Stainers' Company in the 1620s, had an apprentice Ferdinando Clifton (freeman 1627), painted a self-portrait in 1627 (lost; known from Hollar's 1644 engraving), and died 19 January 1636.

## Aesthetic logic

Gheeraerts' portraits work by a deliberate split. The face is the new thing: modelled in tone, lit from one side, observed from life where a sitting could be had, softened by the workshop where it could not. Everything else is the old thing, carried further: the dress inventoried thread by thread, the jewel counted, the motto painted in, the emblem assembled like a pageant device. The split is the point. A court portrait in 1592 had two jobs that pulled in opposite directions: it had to be a true likeness of a person, and it had to be a legible statement of rank, favour, dynasty and theology. Hilliard's generation solved the tension by flattening the person into the statement. Gheeraerts solved it by letting the two systems run side by side in the same picture: a breathing head inside an emblem machine, a wet-in-wet face against a layered, linear, descriptive costume, a warm priming glowing through thin flesh while the brocade stays flat and opaque. The canvas made the split possible at scale, and the curtain made it possible indoors; the pregnancy portraits show the split at its most extreme, a restrained palette holding fertility and mortality in one image. When the fashion changed in 1616-1618, what went out of date was not the face but the machine around it: van Somer and Mytens offered grandeur and fluency without the emblems, and the emblems, once the court's common language, read as stiffness. The last twenty years among the gentry are the machine running on after the court stopped listening, and the pictures are no worse for it: they are social documents now, marriage advertisements and records of wives before childbirth, and their formality is their honesty. His afterlife is the same split in another form: the self-portrait survives only as Hollar's engraving, the queens survive only as workshop patterns, and the painter himself survives as a copy of a lost original.

## Positions

P1. Gheeraerts is the hinge between two Englands of portraiture: the flat, jewelled, emblematic court of Hilliard and the volumetric, observed, continental court of Van Dyck. He does not abandon emblem for likeness; he puts a modelled, breathing head inside the emblem machine.

P2. His "revolution" is technical before it is stylistic: canvas, white ground, wet-in-wet paint and tonal shadow are the tools, and the new lifelikeness is their by-product. The scale of the Ditchley Portrait (2413 x 1524 mm) is only possible because of the support change.

P3. The sombre palette is a social code as much as an aesthetic: greyed flesh and black-and-white dress mark rank, breeding and Protestant restraint, and the blanched skin of the Ditchley queen is pedigree made visible.

P4. The face-pattern system makes the royal face a piece of state property, reproducible and softenable on demand. The workshop's "softened" Ditchley versions are the first English instance of image management: the same queen, retouched for diplomacy.

P5. The curtain setting (1611) is a stage, not a room. It converts the full-length portrait into theatre: the sitter is presented, lit, and framed, and the curtain's shine does the work of architecture. Larkin's adoption of it proves it was a transferable device, not a one-off.

P6. Landscape in his full-lengths is biography, not scenery. Cadiz burning behind Essex, the Irish bog behind Captain Lee, the map of England under Elizabeth's feet: the ground the sitter stands on is the argument of the portrait.

P7. The pregnancy portraits are his most modern works: they hold two incompatible facts, fertility and mortality, in one image, and they do it by restraint rather than by symbol overload. The pared-down palette is the mechanism of the poignancy.

P8. His handling splits by material on purpose: soft, wet-in-wet flesh against hard, linear, descriptive costume. The face is observed; the dress is inventoried. The tension between the two touches is the portrait's texture.

P9. The turbid medium effect in Captain Thomas Lee is drawing made permanent: a black crayon line under thin paint becomes a vein. It shows a painter who thinks of the underdrawing as part of the finished surface, not as scaffolding to be hidden.

P10. His decline after 1617 is a fashion story, not a quality story. Van Somer and Mytens brought a new grandeur and fluency; Gheeraerts' sombre, precise, emblematic mode read as old-fashioned within two years, and his last twenty years among the gentry are the long tail of a superseded aesthetic.

P11. The exile biography matters to the work: a Bruges boy raised in the London Dutch church community, married into the de Critz family, painting the English court in a Flemish idiom he then subdued to fit it. He is an immigrant who defined the English public image.

P12. The self-portrait of 1627, known only through Hollar's 1644 engraving, is the emblem of his afterlife: the painter survives as a print, a copy of a lost original, in the same way his queens survive as workshop patterns. His career is a study in originals that live on as copies.

11. Robert Peake the Elder (c.1551-1619), fellow workshop head. "Peake was the only English-born painter of a group of four artists whose workshops were closely connected. Gheeraerts' tonal modelling was "very different from the flat modeling of features and pure, brilliant colours associated with Elizabethan artists such as Nicolas Hilliard". 13. George Gower (c.1540-1596), Serjeant Painter predecessor. Serjeant Painter to Elizabeth I before the Jacobean group; Custodis "also seems to owe something to the English portrait painter George Gower". Status: CARRIED in #522, not studied.
14. Hans Eworth (c.1520-1574), the earlier Flemish court painter. The anchor's opening line places Gheeraerts as "the most important artist of quality to work in England in large-scale between Eworth and van Dyck"; Eworth is the benchmark Gheeraerts is measured against. Status: CARRIED in #522, not studied.
15. Cornelis Ketel (1548-1616), the earlier Dutch exile in London. Dutch Mannerist "active in Elizabethan London from 1573 to 1581"; Elizabeth I sat to him in 1578; inventor of the full-length Dutch civic group portrait. The previous generation of the same emigre line Gheeraerts belongs to. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, reserve.

"One of many Flemish artists of the Tudor court who had fled to England to avoid the persecution of Protestants in the Spanish Netherlands", resident in London by 1589, three signed portraits dated 1589. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.

Portrait painter and officer of arms to Elizabeth I, Garter King of Arms under James I; painted Essex in his "Sable sad" armour for the 1590 Accession Day tilt; sometimes credited with the Ermine Portrait of Elizabeth; wrote Honor Military and Civill (1602), the literature of Lee's pageantry. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.

"Like Isaac Oliver and Rowland Lockey, Bettes the Younger is believed to have studied under Nicholas Hilliard"; his studio "are thought to have made a number of portraits of Elizabeth I". Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.

### Patrons and sitters

21. Elizabeth I (1533-1603), the queen who made him. Gheeraerts became fashionable "in the last decade of the reign of Elizabeth I under the patronage of her champion and pageant-master Sir Henry Lee"; the Ditchley Portrait (c.1592) is his supreme work. She disliked the new realism: in workshop versions her features are "softened". Status: royal sitter/patron, not a study subject.
22. Sir Henry Lee (1533-1611), patron, Queen's Champion, pageant-master. "Lee became Gheeraerts' patron around 1590, and Gheeraerts quickly became fashionable in court circles"; Lee devised the Accession Day tilts and commissioned the Ditchley Portrait. Status: patron, not a study subject.
23. Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex (1565-1601), sitter and employer. "Her favourite Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex employed Gheeraerts from 1596" and "seems to have used Gheeraerts exclusively for large-scale portraits from the mid-1590s". Status: sitter, not a study subject.
24. Captain Thomas Lee (died 1601), sitter, the Irish portrait. "Around 1594, Gheeraerts painted a portrait of Lee's cousin Captain Thomas Lee standing in a landscape wearing Irish dress." Both Essex and Thomas Lee were executed for treason in 1601, yet Gheeraerts' popularity was not tainted. Status: sitter, not a study subject.
25. Anne of Denmark (1574-1619), the Jacobean patron. "James I's queen, Anne of Denmark, employed Gheeraerts for large scale paintings and his brother-in-law Isaac Oliver for miniatures"; in 1611 he was paid for portraits of the King, Queen, and Princess Elizabeth. She died 1619; Gheeraerts walked in her funeral procession as "Queen's Painter". Status: royal patron, not a study subject.
26. James I (1566-1625), the king. Gheeraerts was paid for a portrait of the King in 1611; James was "markedly averse to sitting for his portrait", which is why the Serjeant Painters' copies mattered. Status: royal sitter, not a study subject.
27. Frances Howard, Countess of Hertford (1590-1632), sitter, the curtain. His 1611 portrait of her, "framed by a draped silk curtain, with a fringed pelmet across the top of the canvas, is the first known instance of a portrait setting that would be used by Hilliard's former apprentice William Larkin in a series of full-length portraits between 1613 and 1618". Status: sitter, not a study subject.
28. Princess Elizabeth (1596-1662), sitter, the 1611 payment. One of the royal portraits Gheeraerts was paid for in 1611, with the King and Queen. Status: royal sitter, not a study subject.

### Successors and rivals who displaced him

29. Paul van Somer (c.1577-1621), the man who displaced him. Van Somer "had by then become Anne's favourite painter, supplanting John de Critz and Marcus Gheeraerts the Younger"; Anne sat to him in 1617 for the hunting portrait. At her death in 1619 she owed him 170 pounds, and he joined her funeral procession as her "picture maker" with Gheeraerts and Peter Oliver. Status: CARRIED in #523, not studied.
30. Daniel Mytens (c.1590-1647/48), the successor. Mytens "must have arrived in England as early as 1613, lured there by the success of Paulus van Somer, to whom he was related" and "became the official successor to van Somer after the latter's death". He "introduced a new naturalism into the English court portrait" and was court painter to Charles I until van Dyck arrived in 1632. Status: CARRIED in #524, not studied.
31. William Larkin (early 1580s-1619), the curtain heir. Larkin took up Gheeraerts' 1611 curtain setting and made it his signature as the "Curtain Master"; his training is thought to point to "one of the London studios - of Isaac Oliver or Marcus Gheeraerts". He painted the same Jacobean court circle but never the royal family. Status: CARRIED in #524, not studied.
32. Anthony van Dyck (1599-1641), the terminus. The anchor's opening line frames Gheeraerts as the great large-scale painter "between Eworth and van Dyck"; van Dyck's arrival in 1632 ended Mytens' primacy and closed the Elizabethan/Jacobean portrait world Gheeraerts built. Status: CARRIED in #524, not studied.
33. Michiel Jansz van Mierevelt (1566-1641), the Dutch portraitist who refused England. "Both Prince Henry and Charles I attempted in vain to attract Michiel van Mierevelt (1566-1641) to their courts. The plague was alleged to have prevented him from coming." Mytens was "possibly" trained in his studio. He is the road not taken: the Dutch portrait factory England wanted but never got. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, main 3.
34. Aert Mytens (c.1541-1601), uncle of Daniel Mytens. First known member of the Mytens painter family; worked in Naples 1574-1592 for the Viceroy's court. Status: SPARE, held back; his career is Italian, one step removed from the English court line.
35. Cornelius Johnson (1593-1661), workshop assistant to Mytens. Mytens' workshop assistance around 1630 included "Cornelius Johnson (amongst others)". Johnson carried the Gheeraerts/Mytens full-length portrait practice into the Caroline court. Status: CARRIED in #524, not studied.
36. David des Granges (active 1620s-1640s), miniaturist after Oliver. Carried in #524 as part of the Oliver miniature succession. Status: CARRIED in #524, not studied.
37. Peter Oliver (1594-1647), Isaac Oliver's son, miniature heir. Walked in Anne of Denmark's funeral procession in 1619 alongside van Somer and Gheeraerts; son and pupil of Isaac Oliver (#524). Status: CARRIED in #523, not studied.

### The print line: how Gheeraerts' face survives

42. Wenceslas Hollar (1607-1677), the engraver of Gheeraerts' face. The only surviving image of Gheeraerts himself is Hollar's 1644 engraving after Gheeraerts' lost 1627 self-portrait. Hollar, Bohemian etcher, came to England with the Earl of Arundel in 1637 and produced some 2,740 plates. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, main 2.
43. Simon de Passe (c.1595-1647), the London portrait engraver. Son of Crispijn the Elder; settled in London in 1616, "established for himself a successful portrait engraving practice", contributed to Holland's Baziliologia (1618), engraved the royal family, and made James I's silver portrait medallions. He engraved the same sitters Gheeraerts painted, in the print medium. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.
44. Crispijn van de Passe the Elder (c.1564-1637), founder of the dynasty. Dutch publisher and engraver; his works include the famous 1605 Gunpowder Plotters print and a 1598 engraved James VI portrait. Father of Simon; his prints carried the Jacobean court's faces across Europe. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.
45. Hendrick Goltzius (1558-1617), the great Northern engraver. "The leading Dutch engraver of the early Baroque period, or Northern Mannerism", 388 plates credited; in 1577 he was employed in Haarlem by the publisher Philip Galle. Goltzius is the print standard against which the English engravers (de Passe, Hollar) measure themselves, and the Elder Gheeraerts' print world runs through Galle. Status: NEW CANDIDATE, alternate.
46. Philip Galle (1537-1612), the Antwerp publisher of the Elder's prints. "Some of his numerous prints made in Antwerp were after Anthonie van Blocklandt, Hans Bol, Marcus Gheeraerts, Gerard Groening, and Hans Vredeman de Vries"; his pupils included Hendrick Goltzius. He published the Elder Gheeraerts' designs, so the family's print reputation runs through his press. Status: SPARE, held back; his link is to the Elder, not the Younger.

## Future candidates
Top pick for #526 is Frans Pourbus the Younger (1569-1622; as commonly cited, not verified from a primary source) - Flemish portrait painter, third generation of the Pourbus dynasty of Antwerp. Trained in his grandfather Pieter Pourbus' workshop, admitted master in the Antwerp Guild of St Luke in 1591-92 at 22. Court painter in succession to the Archdukes Albert and Isabella in Brussels, to Vincenzo Gonzaga in Mantua (1600-1609), and to Marie de' Medici in Paris until his death. His royal portraits - mostly full-length, static, and exact in costume, jewellery, and drapery - set the canon of European court portraiture that Rubens and van Dyck then overturned. His 1600 portrait of the Infanta Isabella was presented to Anne of Denmark and still sits in the Royal Collection. - C310. The death-date Old Style explanation. 19 January 1636: Wikipedia, NPG, National Galleries of Scotland, Tate, Christie's, Proantic, Darnley Fine Art. 19 January 1635: old Dictionary of National Biography ("He died in London on 19 Jan. 1635, in his seventy-fourth year"), Government Art Collection (header "1561 - 1635"), Bonhams and Sotheby's lot headers, V and A, Weiss Gallery. Before 1752 the English legal year began on 25 March, so a 19 January death falls in 1635 Old Style and 1636 in modern reckoning; the one-year gap is the year-boundary, not a disagreement about the day. The study uses 19 January 1636 (modern style) and records the 1635 form. Leg 1 flagged the Old Style reading as a hypothesis only; leg 2 states it as the assessment; the parent adopts it, because it is the standard reconciliation and the "in his seventy-fourth year" fits a birth in 1561/62.
