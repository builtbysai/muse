# Alfred Sisley: Study Notes

**Site:** Alfred Sisley (1839-1899), French-born British Impressionist landscape painter -- born Wednesday 30 October 1839 in Paris, at 19 rue des Trois-Bornes, the youngest of four children of William Sisley, an affluent merchant of imported luxury goods, and Felicia Sell, a music connoisseur; British citizen all his life, twice refused French citizenship; sent to London at 18 in 1857 to learn the family business, where, per his biographer Gustave Geffroy, he learned painting from Turner and Constable in the National Gallery; returned to Paris in 1861 (one source says 1860) and entered the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in the atelier of Marc-Charles-Gabriel Gleyre late in 1861 (some sources say 1862), where he met Frederic Bazille, Claude Monet and Pierre-Auguste Renoir; Salon debut 1866 with two paintings; met his future wife Eugenie Lescouezec in 1866, children Pierre (June 1867), Jeanne-Adele (January 1869) and Jacques (November 1871, died in infancy), married only on Thursday 5 August 1897 at Cardiff Register Office; the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71 ended his family's support, his studio at Bougival was destroyed and only twelve pre-1870 paintings survive in Daulte's catalogue raisonne; based at Louveciennes and Argenteuil 1870-74, Marly-le-Roi 1875-77, Sevres 1877-80, Veneux-Nadon and Moret-sur-Loing from 1880 to his death; founding member of the Societe anonyme in December 1873 and exhibitor of five landscapes at the First Impressionist Exhibition, April 1874, then again in 1876, 1877 and 1882; first one-man show, Paris, 1883; elected to the Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in 1890; twenty Thames paintings from the 1874 trip with his patron Jean-Baptiste Faure, described by Kenneth Clark as "a perfect moment of Impressionism"; a final trip to Wales in the summer of 1897, painting the cliffs of Penarth and Langland Bay; Eugenie died of throat cancer on Saturday 8 October 1898; Sisley died of throat cancer on Sunday 29 January 1899 at Moret-sur-Loing, aged 59, his funeral on Wednesday 1 February 1899 attended by Monet, Renoir, Cazin, Lebourg and Tavernier; a benefit auction at Galerie Georges Petit on Monday 1 May 1899, arranged by Monet, raised 145,000 francs for his children; over 900 oil paintings and about 100 pastels; the most consistent plein-air landscapist of the Impressionist group, and the one Pissarro called, a week before his death, "a master equal to the greatest". ## M1. Sky First, Sky as Subject
Begin every picture with the sky, and treat the sky as a solid with planes, not a backdrop. The letter to Adolphe Tavernier, 1892 (VERIFIED VERBATIM, Bonhams citing Shone 1992, p. 218): "The sky is not simply a background; its planes give depth (for the sky has planes, as well as solid ground), and the shapes of clouds give movement to a picture. What is more beautiful indeed than the summer sky, with its wispy clouds idly floating across the blue? What movement and grace! Don't you agree? They are like waves on the sea; one is uplifted and carried away." And the same letter: "I always begin a picture with the sky." The Bell Tower at Noisy-le-Roi, Autumn (1874) is half sky, its clouds shadowed underneath with light orange; The Bridge at Moret, Morning Effect (1891) gives the sky most of the canvas, and the sky sets the time and the mood; The Barge Port and Saint-Mammes (1884) lets the sky claim the upper half. What it IS: the picture's clock is read from the clouds, not from the land; the sky breathes and drifts while the ground stays still. **Takeaway:** paint the sky first and let it carry half the picture; the clouds are the moving element, and the land is what they move over.

## M2. Horizontal Banding: Sky, Shore, Water
Stack the picture in three horizontal bands -- sky, shore, water -- and let reflection and cloud-shadow carry the drama. The Seine river scenes generally; Flood at Port-Marly (1876) sets the wine merchant's house at an angle and gives most of the canvas to sky and water; The Meadow (1875) and the Loing river scenes do the same with less event. What it IS: bands that slide against each other -- cloud shadows cross the water band, reflections rise into the sky band's colours -- while the shore band, a thin dark line, holds the picture together. **Takeaway:** build the picture as three bands and let the top and bottom bands do the work; the middle band is only the hinge.

## M3. The Sky-Water Mirror Echo
The water repeats the sky's colours and cloud shapes in broken ribbons, so the two halves of the picture rhyme. Sotheby's on the 1872 flood paintings: "the sky and water echo each other and provide the dramatic force behind the canvas"; the flood water is painted in light colours with broad side-by-side strokes, and the rising water carries the sky down into the street. The Road from Versailles to Saint-Germain (1875) does it on dry land by other means: the road's pale surface takes the sky's light. What it IS: a rhyme between above and below, so a cloud passing overhead is felt twice -- once in the sky, once as a ripple on the water. **Takeaway:** let the water repeat the sky in broken ribbons; the picture's drama is the echo, not the thing reflected.

## M4. Solid Against Fluid Brushwork
Two brush languages in one canvas: precise, tight strokes for solid things -- house walls, bridge stone, a church tower -- and broad, loose, side-by-side strokes for water and sky. Boat in the Flood at Port-Marly (1876): "the stability and permanence of the solid elements contrast with the fleeting movements of the water"; the wine merchant's house in Flood at Port-Marly is pink and yellow walls in precise brushwork, the floodwater in light colours and broad strokes, and the serenity of the result is "more characteristic of a tranquil lagoon than the violent waters of the Seine". The Bell Tower at Noisy-le-Roi: near buildings in wide intense strokes, distant ones in lighter feathery strokes. What it IS: solidity and fluidity side by side in the same frame, so the eye trusts the house and doubts the water -- the house holds still while everything around it shimmers and slips. **Takeaway:** paint the solid things tight and the fluid things loose, in the same canvas, and let the contrast be the picture's argument.

## M5. The Same Spot, Again
Return to one fixed viewpoint and repaint it under different light, weather, season and water level. The Port-Marly floods of December 1872 (four paintings) and spring 1876 (six or seven, sources differ); the poplar avenues at Moret, 1888-92, the same avenue with younger trees; the Moret bridge, 1891-93; the Church at Moret, 1893-94, about fourteen paintings from the same south-west vantage point, morning, afternoon, evening sun, morning rain, winter. The historians agree Sisley could not have known about Monet's Rouen Cathedral series when he began the church series -- the parallel was independent, not imitation. What it IS: the motif is a constant and light, weather and water are the variables; the series is a time-lapse of one place, held in separate frames. **Takeaway:** hold one view absolutely still and repaint it as the light changes; the picture is the change, and the place is only the control.

## M6. Snow as Coloured Light
Snow is never white; it is built from warm shadows of light blue and pink, with the sun's warmth in the shade. Road to Louveciennes, Snow Effect (1874): "worked out the forms of the snow-covered landscape with warm shadows of light blue and pink" (Google Arts and Culture); the Snow at Louveciennes pictures of 1874 (Courtauld, Phillips Collection, Barberini, and the version that made 7,358,750 pounds at Sotheby's in March 2017, a record for the artist) work the same way, and the snow scenes across the list -- 26 of them, from Early Snow at Louveciennes (1871) to Rue Eugene Moussoir at Moret: Winter (1891) -- repeat the lesson. He and Pissarro, the legs report, discovered the wintry Louveciennes scenery together. What it IS: a white field that blushes pink and blue as the light moves; footprints and ruts are the only dark notes, and the cold is painted warm. **Takeaway:** never paint snow white; paint it blue and pink, and let the shadows carry the sun.

## M7. Fog as a Veil
Fog desaturates and dissolves distance: near things keep colour and impasto, far things go grey-blue and vague, the light is diffuse and there are no cast shadows. Fog, Voisins (1874), Musee d'Orsay, 50.5 x 65 cm, sent to the First Impressionist Exhibition: a woman crouched tending plants in a foggy garden; the foreground split between bare earth and bright impasto flowers (yellow, white, carnation pink); a paling fence crossing the middle distance; trees and foliage dissolving into grey, pale blue and yellow fog. Eclectic Light calls it "perhaps his equivalent of Pissarro's Hoar Frost at Ennery (1873)": the fog, not the woman, is the subject. His general rule, as reported by Talbot Spy: distant objects "appear fainter and bluer". What it IS: a scene that materialises and dissolves as the fog thickens and thins; a fence line that appears and vanishes. **Takeaway:** put the colour in the foreground and let the fog take the distance; the picture is the veil, and what shows through it is the reward.

## M8. The Bridge as Armature
A bridge -- suspension cables, arches, span -- gives the landscape a diagonal or horizontal skeleton that the sky and water play against, and the bridge is repainted obsessively. The Bridge at Villeneuve-la-Garenne (1872), Met, 49.5 x 65.4 cm, bought from the artist by Durand-Ruel on 24 August 1872; Under the Bridge at Hampton Court (1874), looking up from beneath the span so the bridge frames and shadows the world; Le Pont de Moret, effet d'orage (1887), the Moret bridge under an imminent storm; The Bridge at Moret (1893), Musee d'Orsay, 116 x 97 cm, a large-format return to the bridge from the east bank, with traffic crossing. The Sevres bridges of 1877 come in at least five versions. What it IS: the bridge holds its geometry while light, storm and water move around and through it; from under a bridge, the world is framed. **Takeaway:** give the landscape a bridge and let it hold still; everything else -- sky, storm, water, traffic -- moves against the armature.

## M9. Pale High-Key Tranquillity
A restrained palette -- pale green, pink, purple, dusty blue, cream -- and a calm, unpeopled or thinly peopled composition; no drama in the subject, all the drama in the light. Wikipedia's characterisation of the Seine and bridge pictures; the Bruce Museum: "modest in scale and tonally restrained... the effects of the light dancing on water, the brilliance of winter sun on snow and hoar frost, the movement of the wind in trees". Windy Day at Veneux (1882) is cloudy sky dominating, loose expressive strokes, muted greens and browns with splashes of blue, wind as the subject; The Barge Port and Saint-Mammes (1884) is a tranquil harbour, short broken strokes, wet-on-wet merging of sky and water. What it IS: a scene that stays still and lets the light do all the moving; a slow breathing of colour. **Takeaway:** keep the palette pale and the subject quiet; the light is the event, and the picture must not compete with it.

## M10. Distance by Touch
Aerial perspective by stroke weight, not by outline: near is wide, intense strokes; far is lighter, feathery strokes; and horizontal dark blue and purple dashes laid across the middle tie the composition together. Talbot Spy on The Bell Tower at Noisy-le-Roi: "The nearer buildings are painted with wide brush strokes and more intense colors... as the buildings recede... lighter, feathery brush strokes", and "several horizontal dark blue and purple dashes across the center". The same rule governs the river scenes: the near bank is worked, the far bank is breathed. What it IS: depth built by the hand's pressure, not by geometry; the picture gains air as the eye travels back. **Takeaway:** make the near strokes wide and intense and the far strokes light and feathery; depth is a difference of touch, not of line.

## Evolution
Early, to about 1870: sombre, Barbizon-inflected; dark browns, greens, pale blues; Marly and Saint-Cloud; Turner and Constable (seen in London 1857-61) and Corot and Courbet the cited influences; Pissarro and Manet the modern models. The student works are lost and only twelve pre-1870 paintings survive, so the record effectively starts with Avenue of Chestnut Trees at La Celle-Saint-Cloud (1865) and the 1866 Salon debut. Middle, 1871-1877: the classic Impressionist decade. High-key broken colour; Louveciennes, Argenteuil, Marly; the two flood series (December 1872, spring 1876) -- the flood as geography-transformer, the mechanism folded here from the main leg's candidates: rising water rewrites a familiar street, roads become canals, houses become islands, reflections replace pavements, and the same view is painted at successive stages of the flood, with figures in boats on streets to mark an ordinary day transformed. The Thames campaign of 1874 (about twenty paintings, Kenneth Clark's "perfect moment of Impressionism"); snow, fog and frost as light laboratories (Fog, Voisins; the Snow at Louveciennes pictures). Moret, 1880-1899: settling at Veneux-Nadon and Moret-sur-Loing; the work becomes more formal and serial -- the Loing becomes the dominant motif (34 Loing-titled works in the list), poplar avenues 1888-92, Moret bridge views 1891-93, the tight Church at Moret series 1893-94, about fourteen paintings against nine in the Wikipedia list page (the list page is incomplete here, with zero rows for 1886, 1887 and 1889). By 1890 he was, per Eclectic Light, "the only [inner-circle Impressionist] still painting in the manner which had originally defined Impressionism, outdoors in front of the motif". Poverty persisted despite election to the Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in 1890. Late, 1897: the Welsh seascapes -- his only seascapes, painted at 57-58, dying of throat cancer, fighting wind on the cliffs of Penarth and Langland Bay, freer and more dramatic. The tidal rock as timepiece, the second mechanism folded here from the main leg's candidates: Storr's Rock, Lady's Cove, in five views, morning and evening, as the tide covers and uncovers it, the sea transforming the rock's appearance -- a fixed form that grows and shrinks with the tide, painted within about a hundred yards of the Osborne Hotel. Afterlife so far: the benefit auction of 1 May 1899 raised 145,000 francs; Flood at Port-Marly sold for 43,000 francs in March 1900, nearly half what all 27 of his paintings had brought the previous year; posthumous critical and market reappraisal was slow, and "only after his death did the critical and public attitude change, sending the prices of Sisley's canvases skyrocketing". Retrospectives: Royal Academy, Orsay and Walters 1992-93; Japan 2000; Ferrara, Thyssen-Bornemisza and Lyon 2002-03; National Gallery and Cardiff 2008-09 (Sisley in England and Wales); Wuppertal 2011-12; Bruce Museum and Caumont 2017. The auction record is 9,064,733 dollars for Effet de neige a Louveciennes (1874) at Sotheby's London, March 2017; a Durand-Ruel family sale in October 2026 set a French auction record for the artist twice in one evening (2.79 million euros, then 3.7 million euros). Looted and stolen works run through the legacy: Soleil de printemps, le Loing (1892) restored to the Hirsch family in 2004; Bateaux en Reparation a Saint Mammes (1885) recognised as Nazi-looted in 2008; Allee des peupliers de Moret stolen three times from the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Nice (1978, 1998, 2007). The largest single holder in the list is the Musee d'Orsay, with about twenty works.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is a refusal to leave the motif. While Monet, Renoir and Pissarro questioned the fundamental principles of Impressionism in the 1880s, Sisley "appears to have remained convinced of the validity of the aims of Impressionism, remaining to the end, as contemporary critics rightly observed, a true Impressionist" (Sylvie Patin, RA 1992, p. 183, via Sotheby's). He was the most consistent plein-air landscapist of the group, and the consistency was a position, not a failure of imagination: the sky first, because the sky has planes and the clouds give movement; the motif set down simply, the superfluous detail eliminated, the spectator led along the road the artist indicates (P3, widely circulated, no dated source found); every picture showing a spot the artist has fallen in love with -- "there is always a favorite corner in every painting" (Getty essay). The hard problem, as he put it to Tavernier (SECONDHAND, Goldwater and Treves 1947, pp. 308-310): "To give life to the work of art is certainly one of the most necessary tasks of the true artist... The animation of the canvas is one of the hardest problems of painting." And the surface must transmit the sensation: "the artist must remain the master of his craft, the surface, at times raised to the highest pitch of liveliness, should transmit to the beholder the sensation which possessed the artist." The effects of light that have "an almost material expression in nature must be rendered in material fashion on the canvas" -- paint as stuff, not as window. The quietness is the point and also the wound: Robert Rosenblum's verdict, that his work has "almost a generic character, an impersonal textbook idea of a perfect Impressionist painting", sits beside Pissarro's "the typical Impressionist" and Monet's deathbed letter to Geffroy (VERIFIED VERBATIM, January 1899): "He is a truly great artist, and I believe he is as great a master as any who has ever lived." The two judgments are both true and neither cancels the other: the pictures are textbook Impressionism because he never left the textbook, and they are great because he stayed inside it and found everything there -- sky, water, snow, fog, flood, tide -- while his friends went looking elsewhere. His poverty is the condition, not the subject: pictures sold for 25-30 francs, the 24 March 1875 Drouot auction a jeering failure (20 of his 21 canvases for 2,445 francs by Getty's count, 21 for 2,455 francs by The Arts -- the totals conflict and are not settled here), and he kept painting the same quiet motifs at Louveciennes, Marly and Moret for thirty years. The foundation, as Renoir put it in The Dial in 1920 (SECONDHAND): "It was our mutual poverty which created a union... The school had as its foundation our friendships, discussions, and poverty."
