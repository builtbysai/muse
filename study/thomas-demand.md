Thomas Cyrill Demand was born in Munich in 1964. He studied sculpture under Fritz Schwegler at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf, alongside Katharina Fritsch and Thomas Schutte; the education line runs through the Akademie der Bildenden Kunste Munich (1987), Dusseldorf (1989), the Cite des Arts in Paris (1992) and Goldsmiths' College, London (1993-94). His first solo show was at Tanit Galerie, Munich, in 1992. He began as a sculptor and took up photography to record his ephemeral paper constructions; in 1993 he turned the tables and henceforth made constructions for the sole purpose of photographing them. He lives and works in Berlin and Los Angeles, and has been Professor of Sculpture at the University of Fine Arts Hamburg since 2011; honorary doctorates followed from the Royal College of Art, London (2012) and the Accademia di Brera, Milan (2019), and he was made Chevalier de l'ordre des Arts et des Lettres in 2024. [C29074]

He emerged in the mid-1990s through the German art schools and the New Photography circuit -- MoMA's New Photography 12 in 1996, a first film at Tate Britain in 1999 -- and the mid-career survey at MoMA in 2005, organised by Roxana Marcoci, fixed the method in the museum record: 25 large-scale photographs, 1993-2005, plus the United States premiere of Trick. The Neue Nationalgalerie retrospective in 2009-10, staged with the architects Caruso St John in Mies van der Rohe's glass hall, made the installation itself part of the work. Two commissions from The New York Times Magazine -- Parlor in 1997, a brothel room near Times Square, and Presidency I-V in 2008, the Oval Office photographed in the last weeks of the George W. Bush presidency and published the week after Barack Obama's election -- put the paper rooms in front of a newspaper audience. The touring retrospective "The Stutter of History", curated by Douglas Fogle, ran from UCCA Edge Shanghai in 2022 through the Jeu de Paume in Paris in 2023, the Israel Museum in 2023-24, the Museum of Fine Arts Houston in 2024 and the Taipei Fine Arts Museum in 2024-25 to Fundacion Proa in Buenos Aires in 2026. [C29075]

His practice is one operation repeated for thirty years, and the operation is a chain. Start with a pre-existing image, usually culled from the media, of a place where something happened -- a bunker, a corridor, a recount office, a control room. Translate it into a life-size model in coloured paper and cardboard, built in the studio, usually alone, with the large-format camera fixed in position first and the model built in front of it, checked constantly through the lens. Light it theatrically -- a 10,000-watt film-industry lamp for Clearing -- and photograph it once, in an edition of six, displayed unframed behind Plexiglas. Then destroy the model. The photograph is the work; the model is the means; the event is off-frame; the viewer supplies the history. He has said the core of the work is "a very simple operation -- looking at the picture, diving in, and trying to see what's in the picture. In the end it is all about translation; in this case, it is about translating the flat image into a three-dimensional setting, back to its two-dimensional representation, and then relating it to a bigger context." [C29076]

The career has one constant and one change. The constant is the chain: image, model, photograph, destruction, and the photograph as the only survivor. "I don't know more than the viewer; I just look more carefully," he says. "And I don't want to be a pamphlet or a teacher of history." What changed is where the first image comes from, and what the model is. Early, the image comes from the newspaper and the history book -- a bunker, a corridor, a barn from a famous photograph -- and the model is his own, built to be destroyed. Middle, the image comes from live television, surveillance footage and YouTube -- a tunnel, a recount, a tsunami, a cruise-ship CCTV clip -- and the model starts to move, in stop-motion film, and once, in Grotto, to survive. Late, the image comes from his own phone and from other people's models -- a cigarette butt, a washing machine, a theatre maquette from 1781 -- and the model itself becomes the subject, photographed with a periscope, printed on copper and wallpaper, carried through a museum on floating pavilions. [C29077]

## M1 -- The chain: found image, life-size paper model, photograph, destruction

Demand's method is a chain of four links, and every link matters. He starts with a pre-existing image, usually culled from the media, of a place where something consequential happened -- the hallway to Jeffrey Dahmer's apartment, the Palm Beach recount office, the Fukushima control room, the Tikrit compound where Saddam Hussein hid. He translates it into a life-size model in coloured paper and cardboard, built in the studio, usually alone. He photographs it once with a large-format camera, in an edition of six, displayed unframed behind Plexiglas. Then he destroys the model. The photograph is the work; the model is the means; nothing else survives. The chain is the point: the photograph is triply removed from the scene it purports to depict -- a photograph of a paper sculpture of a photograph -- and each removal is visible if you look long enough. He came to it gradually: he started making paper models because he was a painter who wanted a new challenge, the materials were cheap, there was nowhere to store the models, and they could be rebuilt quickly if damaged; only after systematically making and destroying work for a couple of years, and finding he had nothing to show for it, did he photograph them before throwing them away. The mechanism is the chain itself: the image is built, the building is photographed, the building is destroyed, and the photograph is all that remains of a room that never existed as anything but paper. The watchable behaviour is the double take: first the room looks real, then the paper shows -- a flat texture, a crease, a blank sheet -- and the viewer watches their own recognition fall apart. Seen in W01-W20 passim. [C29079]

**Takeaway:** build the room in paper at life size, photograph it once, destroy it, and let the photograph be the only survivor. [C29079]

## M2 -- The camera first: build the model in front of a fixed lens

Demand does not build a model and then photograph it. He sets up the camera in a fixed position first, and then builds the model in front of it, constantly checking through the lens as he works, adjusting the details and the perspective of the actual model to meet his compositional needs -- a process the Tate text calls directly inverse to the digital manipulation now common in photography. The camera is the first tool, not the last: the model is built to satisfy the lens, not the eye in the room. That is why the models look right in the photograph and would look wrong, or at least strange, standing in the studio -- they are sculptures subordinated to photography, as he puts it: "If you are not a good photographer you need to modify the sculpture in order to look right on a photograph." The mechanism is the fixed viewpoint as a building constraint: the model exists only from one angle, at one distance, under one light, and everything outside the frame is not built at all. The watchable behaviour is the view that cannot be walked around -- a corridor that leads nowhere you can enter, a kitchen you can only see from above, a forest that is a flat wall of leaves -- and the suspicion, growing as you look, that the room was never a room. Seen in W04, W09, W11 and W12, and stated in the Tate text on Tunnel. [C29080]

**Takeaway:** fix the camera first, build the room to satisfy the lens, and never let the viewer walk around it. [C29080]

## M3 -- Deliberate imperfection: the visible seam, the blank paper, the missing text

The reconstructions are meant to be close to, but never perfectly, realistic, so that the gap between truth and fiction always subtly shows. The models are highly detailed, yet they retain subtle but deliberate flaws: an unnaturally uniform texture, pencil marks, remnants of adhesive tape, creases in the paper. Every trace of language has vanished: the papers strewn across the work table in Office (1995) have no text on them; the labels next to the doorbells in Hinterhaus (2005) are variously coloured but bear no names; the telephones in Poll have no numbers and the memo pads are blank. Demand is explicit about the ethic: "I don't take the mistakes out, I have a certain amount of details missing: the traces of use, any writing, other details -- it always looks like somebody just left it. It looks more like an idea, a utopian composition of the thing rather than the real thing. It is a proposition of a thing rather than the real thing." And: "I don't want to trick you, I don't want to fool you, I want you to recognize the process." The hand-cutting is the same ethic at the level of the hand: every clover in Klee (2026) is cut by hand "so that nothing is the same twice", against industrial replication. The mechanism is imperfection left in as a signature: the seam is the proof that the image was made, and the missing text is the proof that the image is not the document it pretends to be. The watchable behaviour is the slow approach: from across the room the Oval Office looks real, and at arm's length it is paper, tape and blank sheets -- the deception is, as Frieze says of Poll, "cleverly transparent, obvious to the viewer". Seen in W09, W11, W12 and W15. [C29081]

**Takeaway:** leave the tape, the crease and the blank paper in, and let the viewer catch the lie. [C29081]

## M4 -- The empty room: no people, traces only

No human figure ever appears in a Demand photograph. The closest he has come is the cut-out silhouettes of politicians and heads of state in the picture frames on Sir Edward Heath's grand piano in 1993 -- paper people, literally flat. Everything else is traces: "Cups, windows, doors. They're very anthropomorphic, traces of someone having been there." The photograph becomes a record of "the moment everybody left already, or the moment before everybody stands in the picture. You see this little thing at the side and that tells me the whole story I need to know." The logic is borrowed from criminology: "if they show the scene of the crime without people, without witnesses, they find it much easier to identify that it happened there, or there, or there. We tend to look at people when we look at pictures." Remove the people and the viewer is forced to read the room instead -- the empty chairs in Poll, the blank monitors in Control Room, the dangling ceiling panels that are the only sign of catastrophe, the room-service tray in Junior Suite that is all that remains of Whitney Houston's last meal. The mechanism is absence as a reading instruction: the room is empty so the viewer has to do the work the people would have done for them. The watchable behaviour is the inventory: the eye goes from object to object -- pitcher, egg carton, bowl of soup, 215 identical grey boxes -- and the history assembles itself from the things, because there is nobody in the frame to look at instead. Seen in W04, W09, W12, W18 and W01. [C29082]

**Takeaway:** take the people out, leave the cups and the chairs, and let the empty room be read like a crime scene. [C29082]

## M5 -- Memory as filtration; the stutter

Demand's subject is not the event but the memory of the event, and memory, he says, is a construction. Historians "have to connect single incidents to write a chronology. They have to fill gaps in which nothing happened, where images are lingering and memories are fading." The retrospective title, proposed by Douglas Fogle, names the mechanism: a stutter is "a hiccup or blank spots in historical narratives", "an involuntary rupture, a feedback loop gone awry, a glitch in speech". The work is the translation chain -- flat image into three-dimensional setting, back to two-dimensional representation, related to a bigger context -- and the chain is leaky at every step: "Photography constructs reality, not the other way around." The pictures do not carry the meaning; the viewer does: "the pictures themselves don't have that [hidden violence], it's what you bring to them. there's no intentionality to the pictures, you just bring your knowledge to the picture." The charged-site catalogue is the evidence: Room, Corridor, Poll, Kitchen, Yellowcake, Presidency, Control Room, Backyard -- each a room the viewer already knows from the news, rebuilt in paper so that the knowing becomes visible as a gap. The mechanism is filtration: memory reduces the world to the amount of data you can process, the model reduces the photograph to the same amount, and the photograph reduces the model to one flat image -- and what survives the three filtrations is the picture that can stand next to the picture we know from the news. The watchable behaviour is the trigger: "It triggers your picture library to spill something out... And once the viewer makes the connection, and realises they are seeing a reproduction, not an original, it falls apart, but in really nice ways." Seen in W02, W04, W09, W12, W14, W15 and W18. [C29083]

**Takeaway:** filter the event three times -- memory, model, photograph -- and let the viewer's own picture library do the rest. [C29083]

## M6 -- Two model systems, opposite poles

Demand photographs two kinds of model, and they are opposites. His own models are, in his words, "non-spatial -- where either something just happened, or something is about to happen" -- super calculated, very crisp, built for one photograph and then destroyed. The other kind he found during a Getty Research Institute residency in Los Angeles: the archive of architect John Lautner, 12 models, 32 close-ups of cardboard, tar paper and foam-core panels -- models that were never meant to be photographed as finished work, working tools for unbuilt buildings. For the first time he photographed models that were not his own. He extended the system to SANAA, spending two years in their Tokyo offices, and to Hans Hollein and to Azzedine Alaia's dress patterns, and in 2026 to historical theatre maquettes -- Theatermuseum Vienna, the Nouveau Musee National de Monaco, the German Theater Museum Munich -- photographed with a periscope into about 25 images for the MAK show. "What I like about architectural models is their potential of suggesting forms without being too explicit and literal, which is the opposite of my own models, which are super calculated and very crisp." His own models are a room at the moment after the event; other people's models are a building at the moment before it exists. The mechanism is the scale flip: a 30-centimetre maquette photographed close up reads as a tower, the tape and glue marks becoming the architecture, and the unbuilt stays permanently possible -- "stage sets reflecting an unrealized legacy of postmodern architecture". The watchable behaviour is the double scale: the viewer sees a monumental building and then, at the taped seam, sees a model on a table, and the building does not collapse -- it stays both. Seen in W17 and in the MAK 2026 works. [C29084]

**Takeaway:** photograph the model that was never meant to be photographed, close up, and let the tape be the tower. [C29084]

## M7 -- The Dailies: the everyday as haiku

Since 2008 Demand has kept a second practice beside the history rooms: snapshots taken with his mobile phone camera while going about his day -- two takeaway coffee cups jammed in a chain-link fence, a pile of mail on a doorstep, a stubbed-out cigarette butt, a washing machine full of coloured clothes, a plant seen through frosted window panes, a discarded straw and cup lid on the pavement -- rebuilt in paper and printed by dye transfer, a long-outmoded process of fixing dyes with gelatine to ordinary paper, chosen for "saturated, but not garish colours, the spatial depth, the intense darkness, the durability". He calls them "visual haikus": "two cups in a fence and it's a small love story... It's not worth telling anyone but if you do it right, suddenly there's a whole tiny little story like a poem. Like a Japanese haiku." The mechanism is scale inversion: the smallest subjects get the slowest, most painstaking treatment in photography -- a discontinued process, one printer left on an island in Washington state, weeks of hand work per print, compared by Demand to Japanese woodcutting -- for a moment nobody would keep. The title refers to film dailies, the leftover images from the cutting-room floor. The shift matters: the source is no longer the media but his own photographs, and the subject is no longer history but the overlooked arrangement, the thing at the side that tells the whole story. The watchable behaviour is lingering: a cigarette butt held the way a history painting is held, the viewer giving a deleted snapshot the attention of a monument. Seen in W16. [C29085]

**Takeaway:** take the snapshot you would delete, rebuild it in paper, print it by the slowest process left, and let it be a haiku. [C29085]

## M8 -- Film as animated still

Demand's films are still photographs that have learned to move, and they move by millimetres. Tunnel (1999) is his first film: a 32-metre cardboard tunnel, a driver's-eye tracking shot through an empty concrete underpass modelled on the Pont de l'Alma where Princess Diana died, endlessly repeated drive-throughs of a place that is "strangely familiar yet unidentified", the soundtrack entirely artificial, computer-generated to suggest movement -- "more an animated still image than a cinematographic undertaking". Escalator (2000) is an animation of 24 still images in continual loop -- two empty escalators ascending forever, from surveillance footage of a robbery near Charing Cross Bridge -- one second of film, looped without end. Recorder (2002) is a paper reel-to-reel tape machine apparently playing the Beach Boys' Smile, an album that at the time could not be heard, over a piano variation on "Bicycle Rider". Pacific Sun (2012) is the mechanism at full scale: a life-size paper cruise-ship dining room, 55 chairs, plates, lemons, pendant lamps, an upright piano and a refrigerator, shifted by millimetres per frame over three and a half months of twelve-hour days by a twelve-person team, nearly 3,000 frames, about a hundred seconds -- a room destroying itself in slow, precise motion, based on a YouTube clip of CCTV footage from a storm in the Tasman Sea. The work began as slapstick; then the 2011 Japan tsunami and the Costa Concordia grounding happened mid-production and "it was no longer slapstick". The mechanism is stop-motion as a way of filming a photograph: the model does not move, the camera does not move, only the paper objects move, a millimetre at a time, and the disaster happens without anyone in it. The watchable behaviour is the furniture performing the disaster -- chairs sliding, plates tumbling, the piano crossing the room -- while the viewer stands inside the listing room at full scale, watching physics that is real in the footage and fake in the model. Seen in W06, W07, W10 and W19. [C29086]

**Takeaway:** move the paper furniture a millimetre at a time, film every frame, and let the room destroy itself without anyone in it. [C29086]

## M9 -- The exception that proves the rule: Grotto, the model that survived

One model was not destroyed, and it is the one that could not be rebuilt. Grotto (2006) starts from a postcard of a Mallorcan grotto Demand had never visited -- a place he knew only as an image, like all his subjects, but this time the image was a souvenir, not a news photograph. For the first time he used computer technology: a 3D model drove the cutting of grey cardboard into about 900,000 layers, stratified like geological sediment, built in his Berlin studio over more than two years into a cave weighing, by different accounts, 30 to 52 tons. Fondazione Prada persuaded him to keep it, and it was shown as Processo grottesco at the Fondazione Giorgio Cini in Venice in 2007, alongside the source postcards, books and guides and the final photograph, and now stands as a permanent installation at Fondazione Prada in Milan. The mechanism is the exception: the model survived because the labour was too great to repeat and the material too heavy to throw away -- "It is a relief. It is also quite humbling because you need to get rid of things: it is a simple rule of life" is the rule, and Grotto is the one time the rule bent. The watchable behaviour is the only direct comparison the public ever gets: visitors to Processo grottesco could walk around the actual cardboard cave and then see the photograph, and the photograph flattens 900,000 layers into one dark image -- the model is the work's own evidence, and the photograph is still the work. Seen in W13. [C29087]

**Takeaway:** keep the one model that cannot be rebuilt, show it beside its photograph, and let the comparison be the exception. [C29087]

## M10 -- Exhibition as model: pavilions, curtains, copper, and the building that looks like paper

Demand designs his own display architecture, and the display is a model too. With Caruso St John he hung lengths of weighty grey domestic-textile curtains in Mies van der Rohe's glass Neue Nationalgalerie in 2009, breaking the colossal cold space down to a human size, the fabric "better used for a sofa" answering the marble, wood, glass and steel of the building -- the installation itself appearing "like a model, in one-to-one scale". For the Stutter of History tour he designed demountable, portable pavilions and a bold red drum-shaped cinema for the film works. At Next Door in 2026 he floats pavilions across the Hamburger Bahnhof space, carrying large-scale photographs -- Clearing as a UV print on non-woven wallpaper -- through the hall where his studio used to stand. And with Caruso St John again, the Triple Folly at Kvadrat headquarters in Ebeltoft, Denmark (completed 2022), turns the logic inside out: from making paper and card look like buildings, he has made a building resemble paper and card -- a meeting space roofed like a sheet of paper from a yellow legal pad, folded in the middle, made from fibreglass, paper-thin, with ring-binder holes; a kitchen roofed white and crimped like a paper plate. The newest material turn, 2025-26, is smaller images printed on copper -- a support with a lineage through engraving, painting and the daguerreotype. The mechanism is the exhibition as a model at 1:1: the pavilion, the curtain, the copper plate and the paper-roofed building are all the same operation as the photograph -- a flat image translated into a built thing, built to be looked at from one side, and then, in the case of the building, left standing. The watchable behaviour is the room that is a model of itself: the viewer stands inside the pavilion looking at a photograph of a paper room, in a museum next to the warehouse where the paper room was built. Seen outside the W01-W20 set, in the Caruso St John collaborations, the Stutter scenography, Next Door 2026 and the Triple Folly. [C29088]

**Takeaway:** build the exhibition like the model -- a pavilion, a curtain, a paper roof -- and let the viewer stand inside it. [C29088]

## Aesthetic logic

Demand's logic is the photograph that knows it is lying and tells you so, politely, at arm's length. Against Dean, whose film contradicts the eye that tries to see it, Demand's photograph contradicts the memory that tries to recognise it: you know the room at once -- the Oval Office, the recount office, the control room -- and then the room falls apart into paper, and the falling apart is the work. What changes across thirty-three years is not the question but where the first image comes from and what the model is: first the history book and the newspaper, then the live news feed and the surveillance camera, then his own phone and other people's archives; first a room built to be destroyed, then a film set moved millimetre by millimetre, then a found model photographed as a monument. The work is always the interval -- between the press image and the paper room, between the click and the destruction, between the photograph and the thing -- and the interval is where the viewer has to stand, looking at a room that is not a room. He refuses the digital fix, and the refusal is the point: he could ask AI to make these pictures, or make them more perfect, but that would not challenge him, he would be bored, and "these days there are more AI generated pictures of Venice than there are actual pictures of Venice". The meaning is the making -- the hand-cut leaf, the taped seam, the blank sheet, the model destroyed -- because the photograph is only the record of a thing that no longer exists, and the record is honest about what it cost. [C29089]
