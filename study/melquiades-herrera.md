# Melquiades Herrera: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #343.

**Site:** Melquiades Herrera Becerril (Mexico City, 24 May 1949 - Mexico City, 18 Oct 2003), Mexican visual artist, geometry teacher at the Escuela Nacional de Artes Plasticas (Academia de San Carlos), co-founder of the No-grupo (1977-1983), "winner of the Nobel prize in art" (self-awarded, December 1978), collector of some 750 mass-produced objects, performance pioneer, television humorist, "el peaton profesional" (the professional pedestrian), author of the four-color map theorem as art.

**Doorway:** From Aquino, the printmaker who built a group to carry the poster into the street, to Herrera, Bustamante's closest No-grupo partner, the geometry teacher turned performance pioneer, whose work turns the group's street logic into pure conceptual play. Aquino's mechanism was the poster as a portable street object, printed cheap and pasted where people pass; Herrera's is the object itself as a portable museum, carried in a Samsonite case and opened where people pass. Aquino's group, Grupo Mira, was a vehicle for distribution; Herrera's group, No-grupo, was a frame inside which each member made his own action under a shared title. Where Aquino asked what a poster can do on a wall, Herrera asks what a comb can do in a hand. The bridge between the two studies is literal: Herrera and Aquino sat together on a roundtable at UAM-Xochimilco on 18 October 1990, "Teoria de la chambrita", and Aquino is the one who kept the group's poster logic alive while Herrera took it into the object, the street vendor's cry, and the television capsule.

## M1. The self-awarded prize

Herrera writes an acceptance speech for a Nobel Prize in Art that he awards to himself, in December 1978, and the fiction is the work. There is no trophy, no committee, no ceremony photographed: there is a text, "Discurso de aceptacion del Premio Nobel", produced in December 1978 and published decades later in Sol Henaro's Alias compilation, in which he announces "Mi tradicion cantinflesca explicara a vuestro internacionalismo mis raices lumpenproletarias". Roselin Rodriguez is explicit that it was a "galardon que el artista se otorgo a si mismo en un acto de arte ficcion", and the Sul Ponticello essay on the No-grupo's sound works treats it as the group's purest example of enunciation changing meaning: the award exists because it is said, and the saying is the artwork. The mechanism is the reverse of a forgery. A forgery imitates a real document to borrow its authority; Herrera's prize imitates nothing, it invents the authority and then performs the acceptance, so the authority is real in the only place it needs to be real, which is in the room where the speech is read. The joke is also a theory of the art world: if a prize is a speech act, then a speech act is a prize, and the whole apparatus of juries, museums, and prizes is shown to run on sentences. That is why the Nobel speech belongs with the No-grupo's other 1978-1979 gestures, the radionovela in which museum directors incinerate mediocre works on fictional radio, and the Esculturas Sonoras cards that make a sheet of paper into a sculpture by tearing it: all three are fictions that produce real effects by being stated. **Takeaway:** the prize is a sentence that does the work of a prize. Watch the acceptance speech: when the text is read aloud and the room treats it as an award, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's Patente del taco (#341 M6) also uses a legal fiction, a patent, to make an object into art; Herrera goes one step further and patents himself, making the artist into the fiction. Where Bustamante's patent needed the bureaucratic form, Herrera's prize needs only the speech.

## M2. The returned object

In La lonchera (1979), at the Salon Nacional de Artes Plasticas, Herrera places a Coca-Cola bottle with an inscription that reads: "Esta Coca Cola, recien descubierta por el Pop-Art, la rescato del arte para proponer reintegrarla a la realidad, solo que hay un detalle: entre conservarla o bebersela, que sea o no sea arte, la decision es de usted." The bottle is rescued from art and returned to reality, and the decision whether it is art is handed to the person standing in front of it. The mechanism is the readymade run backwards. Duchamp took an object out of the world and put it in the museum; Warhol took a bottle out of the supermarket and put it on a canvas; Herrera takes the bottle out of Pop Art and puts it back in the world, with the condition that the viewer must choose between keeping it and drinking it. Keeping it makes it art; drinking it makes it a Coke. The choice cannot be deferred, because the bottle is right there and the inscription is a question, not a statement. That is the whole of Herrera's anti-Duchamp position in one sentence: the object is not ennobled by being exhibited, it is ennobled by being used, and the museum's job is to return things to the street. The Coca-Cola vitrine at the 2018 MUAC retrospective makes the same point from the other side: a tall glass case full of Coke bottles and cans, a Coke tote bag, a red Coke tray, and a black-stockinged leg with a Coca-Cola sock sticking out of the top, as if the collection itself were wearing the brand. **Takeaway:** the object goes back to the world and the viewer decides what it is. Watch the inscription: when the text forces a choice between keeping and drinking, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's poster (#342 M4) also hands the meaning to the street, but the poster is a message that stays a message; Herrera's bottle is an object that stops being art the moment you drink it, which is a harder test.

## M3. The suitcase museum

The Samsonite portfolio is Herrera's studio, museum, fridge, and prop box, and the case opens and the exhibition is already installed. The yellow Samsonite, ca. 1994, 41 x 32 x 10 cm, holds a folder with 42 illustrations of the four-color map, a pearl bow, a sequin tie, toy traffic signs, bells, an oven-sharpener, a heart comb, and a Mickey Mouse watch; viewed open, it also holds a red glitter ball, blue and yellow balls, a white beaded bow, a green ruler with circles, a pink toy camera, an orange plastic ladder, a small Mexican flag, a "100" tag, a blue Samsonite tag, a red plastic boomerang shape, and a black turtle. The black Samsonite holds 63 plastic combs; the pink one holds 15 objects; the rose transparent one holds a black box; the navy one holds a green pencil case and a black comb; the black maletin holds a comb in a box and a comb in a tube. Herrera calls himself a "maletero", a suitcase man, and Frieze's comparison is to Duchamp's Boite-en-valise, but the difference is the point: Duchamp's box was a portable retrospective of a career, Herrera's case is a portable studio for a practice that has no studio, because the studio is the street and the case is what you carry into it. The case is also the argument about the museum: if a museum is a place where objects are kept and shown, then a suitcase that keeps and shows objects is a museum, and the museum's building is the one unnecessary part. The vitrine photograph of the pink and yellow Samsonites, one open and full of straw and dried fibers, the other covered in travel stickers for Paris, London, Italy, the USA, and Mexico, shows the case as both archive and luggage. **Takeaway:** the museum is the case, not the building. Watch the open Samsonite: when the contents are arranged as a display and the case is the wall, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's group actions (#341 M12) needed a room to happen in; Herrera's case needs only a floor, which is why his work travels and hers stays.

## M4. The double-edged object

"El cuchillo de Fantomas" (1999) is Herrera's definition of the object as a weapon with two edges: an object with a double edge, aesthetic on one side and artistic on the other, with two or more functions, symbolic and practical, and with a perverse poetic ambiguity. The examples are the whole vocabulary of the collection: a key-ring that is a crucifix that is a pocket knife; a nose that is a bottle opener; a spoon that is an airplane; a virgin that is a bottle; glasses that are combs; a lipstick that is a projectile; a hamburger that is a transformer; a lighter that is a truck; a coin purse that is a jacket. The comb is the master example, and the mechanism is visible in the Venta de peines action: a comb plus an elastic band is a muzzle; a colander is glasses; a coat rack is a moustache; a comb is a pistol. One object, two uses, both live at once, and the second use is discovered by handling the first. The name matters: Fantomas was the masked criminal of the French serial novels and the Mexican comic, a figure who is always two things at once, a gentleman and a thief, and Herrera's objects are the same, respectable on one edge and criminal on the other. The MUAC Fantomas wall makes the definition physical: a framed text panel reading "EL CUCHILLO DE FANTOMAS", shadow boxes, mannequin heads along the top, and a long pink-topped table with vitrines, as if the definition had been built as a room. **Takeaway:** one object, two edges, both live. Watch the comb: when the second function appears by handling, not by looking, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's poster (#342 M2) also had two faces, the printed image and the pasted street, but the poster's two faces are sequential; Herrera's object's two edges are simultaneous, which is why the object is a weapon and the poster is a message.

## M5. The vendor as the artist

Herrera sells combs in a wig shop and sells objects on a blanket as the MoMA Store, and the transaction is the performance. In Venta de peines (1993), a three-minute action for Television Metropolitana directed by Jorge Prior, Herrera is a comb-seller in a wig shop who reads the combs by color and shape: a wooden comb with thin sharp bristles is "muy estresado", another "tiene miedo", black comb shapes are "los pasos del hombre", a green brush is grass that grows and dies, car-shaped combs are red for youth, white for marriage, white for maturity, black for a funeral. He closes by aiming a pistol-lighter at the camera and announcing "En Mexico solo hay tres grandes: 'el Panzon' Soto, el panzon Diego Rivera y el panzon Melquiades Herrera." His own line, quoted by Rodriguez, is the theory: "Cual es la diferencia entre un artista visual y un vendedor de peines? Pues, seguramente, la diferencia es la corbata." In The Museum of Modern Art. Store Stand (5 September 1997, EDINBA), he spreads a plastic sheet on the ground, sells dissimilar objects at a minimal price, and puts up a vertical sign reading "Melquiades Herrera. The Museum of Modern Art"; the invitation text says "Melquiades no es caro". Viewed in the photograph, he stands in a striped shirt, white cap, and red polka-dot scarf behind a blanket of toys and comics, and a second man crouches examining a paper, which is exactly what a market looks like. The price tag is the label, the sale is the exhibition, and the customer is the collector. **Takeaway:** the sale is the show and the price is the label. Watch the blanket: when a buyer picks up an object and the object changes hands, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's street actions (#341 M9) also used the market, but she used the market as a stage; Herrera uses the market as the work, and the stage is the only thing he removes.

## M6. The geometry lesson that walks out of the room

As a geometry teacher at San Carlos, Herrera gives the first drawing class of the semester with the announcement "El tema de hoy es: la linea", draws a straight horizontal line in black marker across the blackboard, continues over the brick wall, the door, out of the room, down the halls, past the offices, the plants, the yellow gate, out the school door, along the outside walls, to the bus stop, and the line apparently continues onto the moving wall of public transport. The second class begins with a line parallel to the floor, reaches the blackboard, connects to the line's origin, and ends with "Ahora veremos: el punto." The mechanism is that the lesson is the line and the line is the lesson: geometry is not a subject taught in a room, it is a route through a building, and the point is not a dot on a board but the place where two lines meet after leaving the room. The same logic runs through his whole teaching: the foam chicken sandwich pulled out of his bag in a "Principios de orden geometrico" class, with the warning that he was hungry and would go eat at any moment; the sign "No entre quien no sepa geometria" (1978), an 8 x 10 cm plastic and cardboard object that bars the door to anyone without geometry; the "Laboratorio de experimentacion plastica" program co-authored with Carlos Blas Galindo, which declares "La conciencia de grupo es el primer paso para hacer arte". Geometry is the most radical practice because it is the one that cannot be faked: a line is straight or it is not, and a line that leaves the room is straight in the world. **Takeaway:** the lesson leaves the room and the room is the lesson. Watch the line: when it crosses the door and keeps going, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's teaching (#342 M9) was the poster workshop, a room where students learned to print; Herrera's teaching is the opposite, a room that the lesson leaves, which is why his students remember the line and not the lecture.

## M7. The optical device as a change of perspective

Kaleidoscopes, joke glasses, anamorphosis, and the "estrabismo critico" (critical strabismus) required at the door: Herrera's optical work is a device that does the seeing, not the eye. The MUAC folio's first section, "Prodigios opticos", opens with the condition "es recomendable padecer de estrabismo critico", and the optical vitrine at the 2018 show makes it physical: mannequin heads wearing joke glasses, pink glasses trees, packaged joke glasses, a yellow Samsonite at the base, as if the whole room were a joke shop. Herrera narrates the videoperformance Uno x 5, 3 x diez (1993) from inside a kaleidoscope, and the Hinojosa portraits put heart-shaped white sunglasses on him while he holds a heart-tipped wand, and the close-up portrait puts a black plastic comb across the top of round wire glasses. The mechanism is that the device changes what is seen without changing what is there: the kaleidoscope does not add color, it rearranges it; the joke glasses do not add a face, they rearrange it; the comb on the glasses does not add an eyebrow, it rearranges the face into a moustache. That is why Herrera says "ver, mirar, es un fenomeno fisiologico terrible": seeing is not passive, it is a mechanism, and the mechanism can be swapped. The four-color map work is the same mechanism at the scale of a theorem: a device for seeing a map differently. **Takeaway:** the device does the seeing, not the eye. Watch the kaleidoscope: when the view rearranges without the object changing, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's mirrors and masks (#341 M4) also rearranged the face, but her rearrangement was the body's; Herrera's is the device's, which is why his version travels in a pocket.

## M8. The instant camera as automatic writing

Noticias del Mexico surrealista (1979-1989) is a series of Polaroid and Instant Kodak photographs that replaces the surrealists' psychic automatism with a cheap camera. Herrera's answer to Breton, whom he told that the Mexican surrealism of 1938 was no longer valid, is not an argument but a method: walk the city with the cheapest camera there is and let the city produce the paradoxes itself. Two frames are described in the sources: a loncheria sign reading "2 de octubre" with tortas and tacos, where the date of a massacre sits on a lunch menu; and a girl in yellow shorts walking past a grey anthropomorphic stain on a cement wall, where the wall has a figure and the figure has a wall. The mechanism is that the Polaroid is automatic in the same sense the surrealists meant: the shutter is pressed without a plan, the image develops without an editor, and the result is a document of a coincidence that no one arranged. The difference is that the coincidence is urban and cheap, not psychic and expensive: the camera costs almost nothing, the film costs almost nothing, and the surrealism is in the street, not in the mind. The exhibition folleto at the Galeria Frida Kahlo in 1990 is the only time the series was shown as a show, which is fitting, because the series is not a show but a walk. **Takeaway:** the cheap camera finds the surrealism the city already made. Watch the Polaroid: when the frame holds a coincidence nobody arranged, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's photographs (#342 M7) documented the street to make a poster; Herrera's Polaroids document the street to prove the street is already a poster, which is why his series needs no caption.

## M9. The disguise as the argument

Herrera appears as merolico, magician, Pita Amor, Cantinflas, Superman, Blue Demon, El Mago Loco, a sailor, a comb-seller, and on Sundays at the Casa de los Azulejos he dresses in objects: a kitchen shovel as a tie, springs or combs as a hat, a colander, false moustaches shaped like a spoon, and on special occasions the presidential sash. Each object brings a character mutation, and the body is the presenter of the objects: "mi accion corporal simplemente es una conductora o presentadora de los objetos" and "yo sin los objetos no soy nada". The mechanism is that the disguise is not a costume but an argument: to wear a comb as a moustache is to say that a comb is a moustache; to wear a shovel as a tie is to say that a tie is a shovel; to wear the presidential sash with a colander is to say that the sash is a colander. The "three panzones" joke is the same mechanism as a sentence: "en Mexico solo hay tres panzones: el Panzon Soto, el panzon Diego Rivera y el panzon Melquiades Herrera", which places Herrera in a lineage of fat men who are also national monuments, and makes the body into a monument by naming it. The collage portrait viewed first-hand, a grey face with orange striped hair, a spiky black crown, big red square glasses with cyan lenses, a green textured torso, holding a red comb up, is the disguise as a portrait: the face is assembled from the same objects the body wears. **Takeaway:** the disguise is the argument, not the costume. Watch the comb: when it becomes a moustache by being worn, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's masks (#341 M4) also changed the face, but her masks were the work's subject; Herrera's disguises are the work's method, which is why he has a dozen of them and she has one.

## M10. The four-color map as a lifelong theorem

From "Existe el virus de los cuatro colores. Demostracion sencilla sobre 4 colores es suficiente" (1991) through the presentation at the Instituto de Investigaciones de Matematicas at UNAM on 4 February 1992 to "Expedicion en el infierno. Demostracion sencilla sobre el Mapa de los cuatro colores" (ca. 1994-2002), Herrera works the four-color theorem by hand, in public, as art. Appel and Haken solved it by computer in 1976; Herrera solves it by drawing, and the drawing is the solution. His axiom, recorded by Los Yacuzis, is "Para cambiar o modificar un mapa hay que modificar cinco mapas", and his claim is that the map is a "mecanismo neuronal del cerebro": the map is a brain, and the theorem is a way of thinking. The MUAC corner viewed first-hand makes the project physical: grids of diagrams pinned on two walls, long vitrines, headphones hanging, as if the theorem were a room you could walk into. The mechanism is that the theorem is not a result but a practice: Herrera does not prove the four-color theorem, he performs it, over and over, in notebooks, on barajas, on rings of 12 and 13 countries, on a "Ciclo diedrico", on a "Estrella Universal de los mapas normales", and each performance is a new map. The work is endless because the theorem is endless: there is always another map to color, and the coloring is the art. **Takeaway:** the theorem is performed, not proved. Watch the map: when a new coloring appears and the hand is still drawing, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's grids (#342 M5) were also mathematical, but his grid was a printing matrix; Herrera's map is a thinking machine, which is why his version has no edition and no final state.

## M11. The anti-readymade

The Circulo de Conocedores de Marcel Duchamp meets only once, publicly, on 28 July 1987 at the Museo de Arte Moderno, to cut a Sanborns cake in "crema chantilly y turron almendrado" in the shape of Duchamp's Molino de Chocolate no. 1 (1913), and then it never meets again. The members are Olivier Debroise, Adolfo Patino, Alberto Gutierrez Chong, Herrera, and Ruben Valencia, and the cake is the whole of the circle's work. Herrera's axioms, printed in "Ajuste de cuentas con Marcel Duchamp" (30 August 1987), are the theory: "Duchamp es dadaista, yo tampoco"; "?Que le debo a Marcel Duchamp? -Yo, nada"; "me considero aduchampiano sin fisuras", quoting Bunuel on atheism. He draws Duchamp as Groucho Marx in 1977, ink on paper, and again as "Marcel Duchamp visto por Melquiades", and the joke is precise: Duchamp with a false moustache is the readymade with a false moustache, which is what the readymade always was. The mechanism is to out-Duchamp Duchamp by refusing him: the readymade says an object becomes art by being chosen; Herrera says an object becomes art by being used, and a comb is the proof, because a comb is an object nobody ever thought to call art, and therefore the only one left. The cake is the same mechanism as food: the Chocolate Grinder is eaten, not exhibited, and the eating is the criticism. **Takeaway:** refuse Duchamp by out-Duchamping him, and eat the grinder. Watch the cake: when the homage is cut and served, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's Duchamp (#341 M7) was a target she argued with; Herrera's Duchamp is a target he eats, which is why his version ends the argument and hers keeps it going.

## M12. The television capsule as the gallery

Fifteen humorous capsules for La Caravana (1988-89, directed by Alejandro Gamboa, Imevision Canal 7), the Moneros y monitos bumpers for TV UNAM (1996, directed by Leopoldo Best), Venta de peines and Vendedores ambulantes on television (1993): Herrera's gallery is the mass medium, and the joke is broadcast, not hung. The mechanism is that the capsule is a gallery with no walls and no opening: it appears between programs, it lasts three minutes or ten minutes and twenty seconds, and then it is gone, and the audience is everyone who happened to have the television on. That is why the television work is the purest form of Herrera's practice: the object is cheap, the venue is free, the audience is accidental, and the work is a joke that arrives unannounced. The La Caravana capsules are described as a "Galeria del Profesor Melquiades Herrera", which is the joke in the title: the gallery is a professor, and the professor is on television. The Moneros y monitos bumpers are the same mechanism at the scale of a station ident: the entry and exit of a program are the work, and the program is the frame. **Takeaway:** the broadcast is the gallery and the joke is the show. Watch the capsule: when it appears between programs and disappears, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's poster (#342 M4) also used a mass medium, but the poster stays on the wall after the passer-by leaves; Herrera's capsule leaves when the passer-by does, which is why his version is the more radical use of the medium.

## M13. The collection as the archive of a transition

The some 750 objects are "documentos historicos", not artworks, and they witness Mexico's passage from the GATT era to the NAFTA era. Roselin Rodriguez's three material typologies are the mechanism: Chinese, Hong Kong, and Taiwanese imports; US fayuca contraband; Mexican bootleg and knock-offs. The Gonzalez family workshop, run by Mario Gonzalez Marquez, a San Carlos-trained sculptor, made the prototypes for Cantinflas, Pedro Infante, Tongolele, and El Santo, and the workshop's output is the Mexican version of the same global flow: a figure is made in Mexico, copied in Taiwan, smuggled from the United States, and sold in a tianguis, and each copy is a little worse and a little cheaper than the last. Plastic is the material of neoliberalism, and the fake dollar viewed first-hand is the theory in one image: a red "ENJOY Coca-Cola" placemat with a fake one-dollar bill reading "BANCARROTA", a skull for Washington, "UN DOLOR" for "UN DOLAR", and "LA INFLACION ES LA MADRE DE TODOS LOS MALES". The Coca-Cola bottle-cap yoyo on a 20-dollar bill is the same theory as a toy: the currency is a toy and the toy is a currency. The collection is not a set of objects but a set of witnesses, and the witnesses are arranged by material, not by subject, which is why the MUAC show's six boards are not themes but frames: the culture's frame, the installation's frame, the artist's frame. **Takeaway:** the objects are witnesses, not artworks, and plastic is the evidence. Watch the fake dollar: when the currency is a toy and the toy is a currency, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's archive (#341 M13) was also a collection of the everyday, but hers was a personal archive of a life; Herrera's is a public archive of an economy, which is why his version reads as history and hers as biography.

## M14. The word as the object

Typewritten cards, axioms, and captions carry the work, and the sentence is the work while the object is its illustration. "Las miniaturas me hacen llorar", typewritten on a card with a miniature glitter heart in a paper envelope, 8 x 4.5 cm, viewed first-hand in a torn-edge envelope with the red glitter heart pinned at the corner of a white diamond card, is the whole mechanism in one sentence: the sentence is sentimental, the heart is tiny, the envelope is torn, and the work is the gap between the sentence and the object. "Yo no lo vi primero, solo lo pense antes que todos" is the same mechanism as a boast: the thought is the work, the seeing is secondary. "Fuera de Mexico todo es Taiwan" is the same mechanism as a geography: the sentence redraws the map, and the map is the sentence. "Melquiades viene del futuro o apenas esta por ocurrir" is the same mechanism as a tense: the artist is not in the present, he is in the future, and the present is the work's frame. The notebook viewed first-hand, salmon-pink, spiral-bound, with a black comb silhouette and the text "La inteligencia como valor identitario. El arte de Melquiades Herrera Becerril, un performer de trescientos sesenta y cinco dias al ano. Alvaro Villalobos", is the mechanism as a book: the notebook is the work, and the comb on its cover is the illustration. **Takeaway:** the sentence is the work and the object is its illustration. Watch the typewritten card: when the sentence does the work and the object only illustrates it, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's posters (#342 M3) also used words, but his words were slogans for a crowd; Herrera's words are axioms for one reader, which is why his version is quieter and harder to forget.

## M15. The classroom as the laboratory

The "Laboratorio de experimentacion plastica" program, co-authored with Carlos Blas Galindo, and the 1985 text "El cafe teatro: Arte popular para licuar el arte posmoderno" treat teaching as the most radical practice, and the laboratory is the classroom. The program declares "La conciencia de grupo es el primer paso para hacer arte", and the declaration is the method: the group is not a theme, it is a step, and the step is taken in a room where people are learning. Herrera taught geometry, visual education, and basic design at San Carlos for decades, and the teaching is the work in the same sense the collection is the work: it is a practice that produces objects, texts, and actions, and the objects, texts, and actions are the evidence of the teaching. The line class (M6) is the laboratory's purest experiment: a line is drawn, the line leaves the room, and the room is the laboratory. The foam chicken sandwich (M6) is the laboratory's second experiment: a sandwich is pulled out of a bag, the sandwich is foam, the chicken is cloth, and the class is the laboratory where the sandwich is tested. The four-color map (M10) is the laboratory's third experiment: a theorem is worked by hand, the hand is the laboratory, and the map is the result. **Takeaway:** the classroom is the laboratory and the lesson is the experiment. Watch the line: when the lesson leaves the room and the room is still the laboratory, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's workshop (#342 M9) was also a classroom, but his classroom taught a trade; Herrera's classroom teaches a way of seeing, which is why his version has no syllabus and no final exam.

## M16. The individual inside the group

No-grupo's rule is that each member makes his own action under a shared title, and Herrera's documented individual contributions to the Montajes are the 1979 Coca-Cola bottle (M2) and the 1979 nopal curtain for "Atentado al hijo prodigo (Jose Luis Cuevas)", while the Nobel speech (M1) precedes the Montajes and belongs to him alone. The group is the frame and the object is the signature: the Montaje is a title, the action is a person, and the person is identified by the object he brings. The nopal curtain is the mechanism in its purest form: a curtain made of nopal, a cactus that is also a food and a flag, hung in front of a Montaje about Jose Luis Cuevas, the prodigal son, and the curtain is both a barrier and a meal. The Agenda Colombia 83 (1982), a group day-planner in photocopy, is the same mechanism as a book: the group is a calendar, and each member has a page. The Espacio Escultorico intervention (ca. 1982), a hand intervention on a photographic print, is the same mechanism as a photograph: the group is a picture, and the hand is the signature. The eleven Montajes themselves are still not individually enumerated in any source read, and Herrera's contributions to most of them are unattributed, which is itself the mechanism: the group is designed to make attribution hard, and the hard attribution is the point. **Takeaway:** the group is the frame and the object is the signature. Watch the curtain: when the object identifies the member and the title identifies the group, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's group logic (#341 M12) was the same rule, but her version was the group's center; Herrera's version is the group's edge, the member who brings the smallest object and the biggest joke, which is why his signature is the hardest to see and the easiest to remember.

## Aesthetic logic

Herrera's aesthetic is the cheap joke as theory. The objects are mass-produced, plastic, and funny, and the funniness is not a decoration but the argument: a joke is a sentence that changes meaning by being said, and Herrera's work is a set of sentences that change meaning by being handled. The palette is the palette of the tianguis: Coca-Cola red, plastic yellow, comb black, glitter pink, Samsonite yellow, Bumble Ball red and green, the fake dollar's green. The composition is the vitrine and the blanket: objects arranged in a case or on a sheet, with a typewritten card that tells you what they are. The motion is the handling: the comb becomes a muzzle, the case opens, the line leaves the room, the bottle is drunk or kept. The work is never finished because the objects are never finished: there is always another comb, another map, another capsule, another disguise. The logic is geometric in the literal sense: a line, a point, a map, a theorem, a case, a vitrine, all of them are shapes, and the shapes are the work. The logic is also pedestrian in the literal sense: the work is done by walking, by collecting, by selling, by teaching, by broadcasting, and the walking is the work.

## Study-method note

This study was built from the MUAC folio 063 (2018) as the spine, read end to end including the 253-entry catalog, with the Frieze review, the Artishock report, Rodriguez's two essays, the local.mx profile, the Correo Mayor profile, the Sul Ponticello sound-works essay, and the Wikipedia articles as the corroborating reads. Images were downloaded sequentially in one foreground loop, verified as real JPEGs, and viewed first-hand as images, not described from captions; three local.mx files failed as HTML hotlink blocks and were replaced by Sala de Prensa images. The eleven Montajes were not individually enumerated in any source read, and the study says so rather than inventing attributions; Herrera's two documented 1979 contributions are named, and the rest are carried as group work. Two image attributions are probable, not certain: the collage portrait with the red comb and the masked-in-traffic still. The Nobel prize is carried as self-awarded fiction throughout, never as a real prize.
