## The life: the body is the cast, the joke is the system

Martin Kippenberger was born on February 25, 1953, in Dortmund, West Germany, the only boy among five children, with two elder and two younger sisters. His father was director of the Katharina-Elisabeth colliery and his mother a dermatologist; after his mother was killed by a pallet falling off a truck he inherited enough money to live on. He grew up in Essen, where the family were regular visitors to the local Folkwang Museum, and his schooling in 1962-1968 is placed in the Black Forest and the Westerwald. (C25277, leg 1 C25204, C25205, C25208; synthesis C25264)

He studied at the Hochschule fur bildende Kunste Hamburg in 1972-1976. Who taught him there splits three ways and is not settled: the LBBW biography names Rudolf Hausner and Franz Erhard Walther, the Stadel Museum biography names Franz-Erhard Walther and Arnold Hauser, and a third source names Hausner, Claus Bohmler and Walther. What is settled is the negative that matters to this lane: Sigmar Polke influenced him in Hamburg although Polke did not teach him directly -- Kippenberger's Hamburg years end the year before Polke's Hamburg professorship begins in 1977. (C25278, leg 1 C25206, C25207; synthesis C25264)

After a sojourn in Florence, where he had his first solo show in 1977 by the Wikipedia and Sotheby's dating, he settled in Berlin in 1978. The first-solo-show claim carries a variant: the Tate press release says his first solo exhibition was in Hamburg in 1979, and the LBBW biography lists a first solo exhibition at the Kunstforum Rottweil in 1982; no single show is printed here as settled. In Berlin he founded Kippenberger's Office (Kippenbergers Buro) with Gisela Capitain, mounting exhibitions of his own art and that of his friends, became business director of SO36, a performance, film and music space, and started a punk rock band called Grugas, which recorded a single called Luxus with Christine Hahn and Eric Mitchell. (C25279, leg 1 C25209, C25210, C25211; synthesis C25264)

Leaving Berlin, originally for a long visit to Paris, he spent the early 1980s as an active member of the Cologne art scene, working as much on how the art was presented -- the framework and the sideshows -- as on producing works of art. In Cologne, Max Hetzler said Kippenberger was tremendously committed to the gallery's artists, and the Vienna gallerist Peter Pakesch thought Kippenberger did an enormous amount for the Hetzler Gallery's success in his double role as clown and strategist, adding that Max without Martin's strategy would have been unimaginable in the early years. He stayed in Sankt Georgen im Schwarzwald as a guest of the Grasslin family of art collectors from 1980 to 1981, and later on and off from 1991 to 1994, in order to work and to recover from his excessive life. (C25280, leg 1 C25212, C25213, C25214; synthesis C25264)

His closest peer was Albert Oehlen: Hamburg student peers, friends, drinking partners, and band-mates in a jazz group called The Alma Band, with musical approaches oriented toward the New Wave movement and characterised by improvisation, lo-fi and irony. Works titled The Alma Band are listed by Tate in 1984 and again in 1988. In 1984, by the Wikipedia dating, he became a founding member of the artists association Lord Jim Lodge, motto "No-one helps anyone" (German "Keiner hilft keinem"), signet Sun Breasts Hammer. The founding date splits three ways and is not settled: Wikipedia and two secondary biographies give 1984, the Lord Jim Lodge article, the Stadel Museum and a Christie's catalogue note give 1985 with the founding in Graz (founders Jorg Schlick, Kippenberger, Oehlen and Wolfgang Bauer), and one Sotheby's lot note gives 1991. (C25281, leg 1 C25215, C25216, C25217; synthesis C25264)

Exhibitions carried him outward: "Buying America and Selling El Salvador" at Metro Pictures, New York, in 1985; first museum show at Hessisches Landesmuseum Darmstadt in 1986; Centre Pompidou in 1993; Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen in 1994. The first Laterne (Lamp) sculptures came in 1988, a year spent largely in Seville and Madrid, and Laterne an Betrunkene (Street Lamp for Drunks) became well known through the 1988 Venice Biennale. He took part in documenta 10 in 1997, dying shortly before it opened, and represented Germany at the Venice Biennale posthumously in 2003 with Candida Hofer. The posthumous Venice years carry a variant: the Stadel Museum biography says he was represented posthumously at the 48th and 50th Venice Biennales in 1999 and 2003, while Wikipedia dates a Metro-Net showing posthumously without giving a year in that passage. (C25282, leg 1 C25218, C25219, C25220, C25221; synthesis C25264)

After moving to Los Angeles in late 1989, he bought a 35 percent share in ownership of the Italian restaurant Capri in Venice, Los Angeles, and together with the Museum of Modern Art he opened his own museum, MOMAS (Museum of Modern Art Syros), on the Greek island of Syros in 1993. In his last years he taught at the Stadelschule and the Art Academy in Kassel; the LBBW biography gives a visiting professorship at the Stadelschule in Frankfurt in 1990 and a professorship at the Gesamthochschule Kassel in 1991-1992, and whether those are the same posts or separate appointments is not settled. In 1996 he made the Medusa lithograph series, using photography as his starting point and posing himself for his wife, the photographer Elfie Semotan, mimicking the postures of the characters in Theodore Gericault's The Raft of the Medusa (1819), and a 1996 work in the hotel-drawings series shows him standing side by side with his daughter Helena as a little girl. (C25283, leg 1 C25223; synthesis C25264)

Martin Kippenberger died at age 44 from liver cancer at the Vienna General Hospital in Vienna on March 7, 1997. The age and cause wording carries a variant: the infobox and main text give age 44 and liver cancer, while the New York Times obituary cited by Wikipedia is titled with age 43, and a secondary biography says he died due to chronic alcoholism; liver cancer at the Vienna General Hospital is printed as the stated cause and the 43-versus-44 wording is not settled here. His grave is in Jennersdorf, Austria, according to the captioned photograph in the Wikipedia article, recorded here as Wikipedia-only. Major retrospectives followed at Tate Modern in 2006, at the Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles, in 2008, with the exhibition travelling to the Museum of Modern Art, New York, in 2009, and his reputation and influence have grown since his death. (C25284, leg 1 C25204, C25222; synthesis C25264)

## M1 -- Self-portrait as alter-ego system: one body, a new role each time

The artist's own body is the reusable cast, recast as a different persona in each cycle: the beaten club manager in Dialogue with the Youth of Today (W04), Picasso in white underpants in the 1988 self-portraits (W10), the hand-painted contortionist of the Hand Painted Pictures (W16), Jacqueline, Picasso's widow, in the 1996 Jacqueline series (W18), the Gericault shipwreck victim of the Raft of Medusa (W19), and Fred the Frog on a cross (W13). The mechanism is not vanity and not confession: the body is a prop that stays constant while the role, the costume and the source photograph change, so the viewer tracks a character across cycles rather than a likeness in one picture. Works: W04, W10, W13, W16, W18, W19. (C25285, leg 2 M1, W04, W10, W13, W16, W18, W19; synthesis M1)

The watchable behaviour is recognition without resemblance: the same belly, face and posture return, but the picture they arrive in is different every time, so the eye does the work of casting -- is that him again, in whose clothes, in whose painting? In W10 the restaging is exact enough to be funny and wrong enough to be a portrait: a David Douglas Duncan photograph of Picasso in white underpants on the steps of Chateau Vauvenargues in 1962, re-performed with Kippenberger's own belly pulled high under the same white underpants, turning to examine himself in a mirror. In W19 the same body is bloated by liver cancer and posed for his wife Elfie Semotan after Gericault, so the joke and the illness share one frame and neither cancels the other. (C25286, leg 2 M1, W10, W19; synthesis M1)

**Takeaway:** the self-portrait works when the body is the constant and the role is the variable; a new face each time is a gallery of strangers, not a system. (C25287)

## M2 -- Bad painting: deliberately "wrong" painting, mistakes left visible

Crude, impudent canvases that reject good taste and skilful finish, in the lineage of the 1978 "Bad Painting" show at the New Museum, which Kippenberger did not feature in. He claimed he did not have a style, and Tate notes his trademarks include deliberately leaving mistakes visible. The mechanism is a refusal performed as technique: inconsistent style inside one work, kitsch subjects, visible errors kept in rather than corrected. The proof that the badness is a system and not an accident is W12: for Heavy Burschi he asked assistant Merlin Carpenter to make paintings from catalogue images, judged them "just too good, too skilful, and therefore too much kitsch", had them destroyed and exhibited in a skip, keeping a photograph of each. Works: W03, W12, W07. (C25288, leg 2 M2, W03, W12, W07; synthesis M2)

The watchable behaviour is a painting that will not settle into one register: 21 canvases in Blass vor Neid (W03), each separately titled and in no consistent style, shown together as one work, so the viewer keeps re-deciding whether the clumsiness is failure or choice -- and the work's answer is that the decision is the point. The danger for any imitator is the obvious one: bad painting done without a system is just bad painting, and Kippenberger's own test for it was destruction. (C25289, leg 2 M2, W03, W12; synthesis M2)

**Takeaway:** the mistake must be visible on purpose and the system must be able to reject its own product; a painting that is merely clumsy has no mechanism. (C25290)

## M3 -- Text and slogan painting: the viewer reads before looking

Words painted into the image as the picture's subject, not as caption: titles that are sentences, slogans in Greek letters, word-lists on the canvas. The mechanism puts language in the way of looking, so the first act of seeing the picture is reading it. A phrase such as "My Lies Are Honest" (Meine Lugen sind Ehrlich, W16) or the words Witz, Effekt, Gefuhle, Bild, Anschauung in a Fred the Frog painting (W13) sets the joke and the argument at once; the sentence-titles do the same work from outside the frame, as in Martin, ab in die Ecke und scham Dich (W11) and Ich kann beim besten Willen kein Hakenkreuz entdecken (W07). Works: W13, W16, W11, W07. (C25291, leg 2 M3, W13, W16, W11, W07; synthesis M3)

The watchable behaviour is a two-beat reading: the words land first and frame everything after, so the image is never seen innocently -- the frog is already a defendant, the corner already a punishment, the swastika already a protest against the ban on depicting it. In the Hand Painted Pictures (W16), painted in 1992 on Syros and in Frankfurt from photographs by Jory Felice taken in Venice Beach in 1989-1990, many portraits include German phrases in Greek letters, so the text is doubly foreign: a German sentence in a Greek alphabet on a self-portrait, read slowly or not at all. (C25292, leg 2 M3, W16; synthesis M3)

**Takeaway:** the text must be the picture, not a label on it; a caption that explains the image is illustration, not this mechanism. (C25293)

## M4 -- Hotel drawing as diary: small sheets, edited, not raw

Drawings made on collected hotel stationery, in pen, coloured pencil, collage and watercolour, in thematic series, over the last ten years of the life. The Hotel Drawings run 1987-1997: first ad hoc preparatory diagrams for the three-dimensional Peter sculptures, later hotel letterheads used for portraits of scientists, Frank Sinatra, war depictions and other subjects. The mechanism is portability as a working method: a small, quick, portable sheet that records a place, a joke or a plan the same day, on paper that already says where it came from. Works: W14, W15, W06. (C25294, leg 2 M4, W14, W15, W06; synthesis M4)

The watchable behaviour is the diary that admits it was edited: many of the drawings were composed elsewhere, not sketched on the spot, so the sheet that looks like a same-day note is a reconstruction, and the hotel letterhead -- the Anchorage Hotel Alaska in W14, coloured pencil on hotel stationery, 27.9 x 21.6 cm, grade A -- is both the ground and the alibi. Invention of a Joke (W15) shows the method at its most deliberate: one macabre joke image, from a 1950 book on practical jokes found on a postcard, divided across nine equal-sized canvases that cannot be pieced together, the source image showing a man disfigured by an exploding cigar in Cuba in 1889. (C25295, leg 2 M4, W14, W15; synthesis M4)

**Takeaway:** the diary works when the paper remembers the place and the drawing admits the delay; a sketchbook page with no elsewhere in it is a doodle, not a diary. (C25296)

## M5 -- Installation as total environment: the Kafka job fair

A whole room built as a fictional institution that the viewer enters and watches from bleachers, rather than a single object on a wall. The Happy End of Franz Kafka's "Amerika" (W17, 1994) reimagines the end of Kafka's unfinished 1927 novel Amerika, where Karl Rossman arrives at a recruitment centre for the Theatre of Oklahoma: Kippenberger sets scores of simultaneous job interviews on a green soccer-field floor, with bleachers, using design chairs by Arne Jacobsen and Charles Eames, flea-market tables, remnants of earlier shows and works by other artists. The table count splits between sources -- over 40 tables and twice as many chairs in Wikipedia as opened, 50 table-and-chair ensembles in Museum Folkwang and Sotheby's summaries -- and no single count is printed here as settled. METRO-Net (W20, 1993-1997) is the same mechanism at a smaller scale: false subway entrances as site-specific sculpture, a globe-spanning subway system with entrances on Syros and in Dawson City, Canada. Works: W17, W20. (C25297, leg 2 M5, W17, W20; synthesis M5)

The watchable behaviour is the viewer kept off the field: the bleachers make a spectator of everyone who enters, and the interviews that never happen are watched the way a match is watched, from the stands. The furniture does the character work -- design classics next to flea-market chairs, so the institution is assembled from status objects and junk in the same room, and the viewer reads the hierarchy of the chairs before any person sits in one. (C25298, leg 2 M5, W17; synthesis M5)

**Takeaway:** the environment works when the viewer is given a place to stand that is not inside the fiction; a room full of props with no bleachers is a set, not an institution. (C25299)

## M6 -- The multiple: one idea, many copies, no original

One idea issued as many near-identical or deliberately varied copies: 21 canvases as one work (W03), five Peter panels (W06), several life-size corner mannequins (W11), an edition of 5 Zuerst die Fusse variants plus 2 artist's proofs (W13). The mechanism is repetition with small differences, so no single copy is the original and the viewer compares versions instead of contemplating one image. The Peter denominator is the purest form of it: first used as an open-ended generic reference to imaginary figures, the Peter works run through painting, sculpture, installation and daily life, and a five-part Peter-Series example includes a still life, a macaroni portrait, a figure showering upside-down, a cartoon policeman and an angry executive. Works: W03, W06, W11, W13. (C25300, leg 2 M6, W03, W06, W11, W13; synthesis M6)

The watchable behaviour is comparison as the experience: the eye moves sideways between versions, hunting the difference, and the difference -- a panel's subject, a frog's variant, a mannequin's repeat -- is the only event the work offers. A single Peter panel is a cartoon; five of them, each a different genre, is a system for generating Peters. (C25301, leg 2 M6, W06; synthesis M6)

**Takeaway:** the copies must differ enough to compare and agree enough to be the same idea; identical prints are an edition, and unrelated pictures are a group show. (C25302)

## M7 -- The joke that stays a joke and the joke that turns serious

A split, not a single rule: some jokes stay jokes -- Fred the Frog as a cartoon crucifixion, a Santa Claus on a warship -- while others harden into a serious charge. The corner mannequin (W11) answers a real accusation: after critic Wolfgang Max Faust accused Kippenberger of neo-Nazi attitudes, he made a life-size dressed mannequin self-portrait, cast resin with real clothing, placed facing the wall -- Martin, ab in die Ecke und scham Dich (Martin, into the corner, you should be ashamed of yourself), 1989, 177.8 x 67 x 43.8 cm, grade A. The Kafka installation (W17) starts as a comic job fair and becomes a picture of integration, repression and power. The Hakenkreuz painting (W07) sits on the seam: a deliberate jumble of quasi-deconstructed swastikas, protesting the ban on depicting them, and Krieg Bose, initiated in 1984, puts a Santa Claus figure on a warship, turning a battleship motif into a joke about commercial Christmas and German aggression. Works: W07 and W13 (joke stays joke); W11 and W17 (joke turns serious). (C25303, leg 2 M7, W07, W11, W13, W17; synthesis M7)

The watchable behaviour is the laugh that arrives first and the target that has not moved: the viewer laughs, then notices the serious works keep the joke's form and change its weight. The corner mannequin is funny for exactly as long as it takes to read the title -- then the punishment is real, the artist is the punished child, and the accuser is nowhere in the room. Fred the Frog, crucified on stretcher-bar-like wood holding a beer mug in one hand and an egg in the other, stays a joke even when Pope Benedict XVI condemned it as blasphemous in 2008; the condemnation is absorbed into the joke rather than answering it. (C25304, leg 2 M7, W11, W13; synthesis M7)

**Takeaway:** a joke that turns serious must keep the joke's form and change only its weight; a joke that drops the form to get serious is a lecture. (C25305)

## M8 -- Collaboration as mechanism: handing the making to someone else

Handing the making to someone else, so the signature is shared or faked, the hand changes, and the idea, not the execution, is what Kippenberger claims. The twelve Lieber Maler works (W02, 1981) were made by a professional poster artist named Werner, from Kippenberger's snapshots, and were first credited to Werner Kippenberger; MoMA states that collaboration of this sort prevailed throughout the short career and that Kippenberger rarely worked alone. Capri by Night (W05, 1982) parks a real Ford Capri inside a gallery, covered in orange and brown paint mixed with oat flakes, made with Albert Oehlen, the paint-and-oat texture parodying Beuys and Kiefer. The assistants Carpenter and Strothjohann made the Heavy Burschi paintings (W12); Jeff Koons worked with him on a Parkett issue in 1989, and other artists' works sit inside the Kafka installation (W17). Works: W02, W05, W12, W17. (C25306, leg 2 M8, W02, W05, W12, W17; synthesis M8)

The watchable behaviour is the hand that is visibly not his: a poster painter's smooth commercial finish on a Kippenberger snapshot, an Oehlen brush on a car Kippenberger parked, an assistant's paintings destroyed for being too good. The collaboration is legible in the surface, and the viewer is left to decide what the artist actually did -- which is the work. (C25307, leg 2 M8, W02, W05, W12; synthesis M8)

**Takeaway:** the collaborator must leave a visible hand in the work; a credit line with no change in the surface is administration, not collaboration. (C25308)

## M9 -- Object-painting hybrid: paint leaves the canvas

Works that are both painting and object: a Richter grey painting built into a coffee table in 1987, Peter sculptures made from found furniture with mistakes left visible, drunken lamps that are sculpture and motif at once. The correction carried from leg 2 matters here: Martin, ab in die Ecke (W11) is NOT itself a painting hybrid -- it is a cast-resin sculpture with real clothing. The hybrid is shown by the Laterne sculptures (W09, first made 1988, bent as if leaning, Laterne an Betrunkene well known through the 1988 Venice Biennale), by Capri by Night (W05), and by the Tate Untitled 1989 that combines a Peter sculpture and a drunken lamp. ## M10 -- Appropriation: restaging the canonical image with his own body in it

Taking a famous image and re-performing it with his own body in it: Picasso in underpants after Duncan's photograph (W10), Jacqueline after Duncan's photographs of the widow (W18), Gericault's Raft after the 1819 painting (W19), and a Richter grey abstract bought and set into a coffee table in 1987 (Modell Interconti). The mechanism is the old masterpiece as a stage: the viewer recognises the source first, then sees Kippenberger inside it. In the Jacqueline series (W18, 1996, 8 paintings, oil on canvas, 180 x 149.9 cm, grade A), based on photographs taken in Picasso's studio after his death, Kippenberger said he was taking over Picasso's job and painting Picassos. Works: W10, W18, W19. (C25312, leg 2 M10, W10, W18, W19; synthesis M10)

The watchable behaviour is double recognition: the source is famous enough to arrive before the picture does, and then the body inside it rewrites the fame -- Picasso's underpants on a belly that is not Picasso's, Jacqueline's widowhood painted by a man who is not her husband, Gericault's shipwreck crewed by one bloated German in 1996. The Richter table is the same mechanism without the body: the masterpiece is not quoted, it is bought, turned on its back, and made to hold coffee. (C25313, leg 2 M10, W10, W18, W19; synthesis M10)

**Takeaway:** the source must be recognisable before the restaging begins; an obscure source restaged is a private joke, not an appropriation. (C25314)

## The aesthetic logic: the joke is the system, the self is the material

Kippenberger's whole career is one refusal performed fifty ways: refuse to have a style, refuse to make the work alone, refuse to keep painting on the canvas, refuse to let a joke stay only a joke or a serious subject stay only serious -- and let the refusal itself do the work. The self-portrait refuses likeness by casting the same body in a new role each time. The bad painting refuses taste by leaving the mistake in. The hotel drawing refuses the studio by working on borrowed paper in borrowed rooms. The Kafka installation refuses the single object by building the institution instead. The Richter table refuses reverence by buying the masterpiece and using it as furniture. In each case the artist sets up a system -- a cast, a diary, a room, a collaboration, a restaging -- and then lets the system carry what his hand alone could not. (C25315, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25264, C25265)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the persona is the medium: the belly, the face, the posture are the one constant across thirty years of work in every medium, and the work is a casting session that never ends -- which is why Kippenberger's work is the opposite of Richter's procedure even though the two men are now tied by a coffee table: Richter sets up a system and obeys what it returns, Kippenberger sets up a persona and performs what it demands. Second, the joke is a dial, not a tone: every major mechanism only works when the viewer laughs first and then notices the target has not moved, so the picture is never finished in one reaction. Third, the institution is the material: the gallery, the museum, the hotel, the subway, the job fair are not the settings for the work, they are the work -- the Office, the MOMAS museum on Syros, the Metro-Net entrances are all Kippenberger building the frame and calling it the picture. (C25316, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25264)

The evolution is the proof. 1976-77: the Florence grey canvases (W01, Uno di voi), small 50 x 60 cm paintings from souvenir postcards and street photography -- milkman, wanted criminal, stuffed pig, a copy of a Botticelli, a dead pigeon -- already comparing themselves to Richter's 48 Portraits. 1981-85: the Berlin and Cologne years, the work made by other hands (W02), the beaten self-portrait (W04), the car as painting (W05), the Peter system (W06), the war jokes (W07, W08). 1988-89: the lamps (W09), the Picasso self-portraits (W10), the corner mannequin (W11) -- the body and the object meeting at full size. 1990-92: the frog (W13), the hotel drawings (W14), the joke divided across nine canvases (W15), the hand-painted portraits (W16). 1993-97: the institutions -- METRO-Net (W20), the Kafka job fair (W17) -- and the final restagings, Jacqueline (W18) and Medusa (W19), the body posed after Gericault while it was dying. The late work pushes both poles further apart and closer together at once: the jokes get bigger and more architectural, the self-portraits get more unflinching, and the answer in both cases is the same -- put your own body in the masterpiece, and build the room around it. (C25317, leg 2 evolution; leg 1 C25209-C25223; synthesis C25264)
