## The life: the Mexican who turned the post into a way of life

Felipe Ehrenberg Enriquez was born 27 June 1943 in Tlacopac, Mexico City, and died 15 May 2017 in Cuernavaca, Morelos, aged 73; the cause of death is unresolved (heart attack per ArtReview, cancer per Harper's Bazaar Arabia -- do not state either as fact without qualification) and the place is carried both ways (Cuernavaca vs Ahuatepec, a locality in the Cuernavaca municipality, possibly compatible). (C11500, C11510, C11516, C11523)

The career runs in six phases, and the hinge is Tlatelolco: the work leaves the studio, enters the postal and printing systems, and then leaves the systems to become the teaching, the walking and the archive that carry it.

(1) Mexico painter/printmaker, 1960-68. He grew up in a carpentry workshop and apprenticed, aged nine to eleven, with Catalan printers, anarchists exiled in Mexico under Franco; he later said he was formed under the apprentice system, not art school. First show 1960; editor of the Mexico City Times arts section 1964-67, writing a film column as "Montenegro"; first solos 1965; Salon Codex prize, Buenos Aires, 1968; involved with the Independent Salon from 1968 as a protest against state control of the art market. Mentors: Jose Chavez Morado, Feliciano Bejar, Mathias Goeritz. (C11500, C11506, C11507, C11512)

(2) English exile, systems and networks, 1968-74. After the Tlatelolco massacre (2 October 1968 -- official toll around forty, Comite 68 estimate more than three hundred; report the range), in danger of imprisonment, he went into exile in England with his family. In London and Devon he made telegram, mail, performance and film work and co-founded Beau Geste Press at Langford Court, Cullompton, Devon -- founded 1971 in most sources, 1970 in Conwell/Wikipedia, 1968 in ISLAA (treated as an error); carry 1971 with the conflict noted, never one year silently. The press (active to 1976; founders parted 1974 per Conwell) printed some 75-80 books by hand -- mimeograph, offset, letterpress -- outside the commercial market; slogan "Our Press is not a business, it's a way of life". Founders: Ehrenberg, Martha Hellion and David Mayor as the core trio; Conwell and Wikipedia add Chris Welch and Madeleine Gallard. In 1973 Carrion produced Arguments and Tras la Poesia / Looking for Poetry with the press. (C11500-C11515)

(3) Return and the grupos, 1974-79. He returned to Mexico late 1973 / early 1974 (sources split across the year boundary) and settled in Xico, Veracruz; co-founded Grupo Proceso Pentagono (1976 per ISLAA/Monoskop; ICAA ties it to the return -- carry 1976 with the conflict noted); Guggenheim Fellowship 1975 (1976 per one outlier). (C11500, C11501, C11506, C11509, C11520, C11521)

(4) Pedagogy, manuals, community publishing, 1979-1990s. Founded H2O Talleres de Comunicacion (1979), 25 instructors (26 per his own account), 500+ community presses / 1,100 murals per Wikipedia/Stanford vs "over 800 presses" per his interview -- report both figures with their sources. The wooden mimeograph he called "Pinocchio", built where no mimeograph existed (sixty built at the rural teachers' college in Mactumactza, Chiapas), with ink from soot mixed with cold cream and screens from synthetic silk nightgowns. Manual del editor con huaraches (Manual for the Editor in Sandals). Ran for PSUM in 1982; Tepito reconstruction after the 1985 earthquake; Barrio a Barrio in San Salvador after the 1986 earthquake. (C11500, C11507, C11512, C11513)

(5) Installations, border works, US, 1990-2000. Nexus Press residency, Atlanta, 1990; Codex Aeroscriptus Ehrenbergensis (1990); Light Up Our Border I-II (1990); Preterito Imperfecto (first shown November 1992, also dated 1993); inSITE 94; Tzompantli (1995). (C11500, C11506, C11555)

(6) Diplomat, retrospective, late work, 2001-17. Cultural attache in Brazil (Sao Paulo) 2001-2006. First retrospective Manchuria -- Vision periferica, Museo de Arte Moderno, 2008, travelling to MOLAA and Pinacoteca Sao Paulo 2010 (around 200-250 works). On 21 September 2016 at Museo Reina Sofia he gave "Digressions: Memories of Carrion" in the seminar "The New Art of Making Books. Ulises Carrion and expanded publishing". Testamento, 34 collages compiled in his final months, first shown at ISLAA, New York, 2021. (C11500, C11503, C11505, C11521)

In his own words. On the telegram: "CONVIRTAMOS / TODOS / LOS SISTEMAS / EN / POESIA / Y / VISIONES ..." -- "Let's convert all the systems into poetry and visions." On founding the press: "The main reason we set up our press was to cut out the grievous bullshit about submitting work 'for consideration'; and the ensuing stress." The press catalogue: "Beau Geste Press is not a business. It's a way of life. We exist because you exist." On books: "I've always insisted that I don't 'make' books ... books happen to me!" On the economics of conceptual art: "I think the financial realities of artists are the real origin of conceptual art" -- on sending 200 postcards because stamps were cheaper than packing, insuring and shipping a painting. On art and people: "Art is about linking people up, no two ways about it." On Carrion: "Not exactly. We became accomplices." The mail-art quote "Who sends a letter is always waiting for an answer" is exhibition-reported only, NOT primary-verified -- do not present it as his words. (C11502, C11507, C11508, C11511, C11512, C11516, C11517)

## Mechanism M1: the telegram as a poem, the system as the medium

An official communication system (telegram, post) is hijacked so that a poetic or political message must travel through it, and the system itself -- its delays, losses, censorship -- becomes part of the work. Telegraphic Work (1970) sends "LET'S CONVERT ALL THE SYSTEMS INTO POETRY AND VISIONS" as a telegram, a form built for brevity and urgency, so the message arrives as chopped capitals with the system's own voice ("FIN DE MENSAJE / FELIPE"). Arriba y Adelante (1970) is the same mechanism at postcard scale: 200 hand-painted fragments, each illegible alone, sent from three London post offices to the Mexican Independent Salon, and assembled in order into a topless woman holding a Mexico 70 soccer ball. Any postcard the state post lost shows as a crimson gap on the red backing: the failure of the system is visible inside the finished image. (C11550, C11502)

**Takeaway:** the system is not the delivery channel, it is the material. A piece built on this mechanism must let the channel fail visibly: fragments arrive out of order or not at all, and the gaps are part of the picture, not a bug to hide.

## Mechanism M2: the body at the door of the institution

The artist's own body presents itself as the work at the threshold of a museum or public system, forcing the gatekeepers to decide what counts as art in real time. A Date with Fate at the Tate (1970): Ehrenberg enters the Tate with his head covered, claiming admission not as a visitor but as a work of art; the guards' discussion is recorded on audio and later acquired by the Tate -- the refusal and the negotiation become the work's documentation. The Tube-O-Nauts (1970): collaborators in handmade cardboard astronaut helmets ride the London Underground, turning a commute into a social space with an audience that did not buy a ticket. Kinekaligrafias (1967) is the early form: a body on a ladder, posters, written instructions for viewers to take part. (C11554, C11559, C11570, C11551)

**Takeaway:** the threshold is the stage. The piece needs a gatekeeper -- a guard, a barrier, a door -- whose real-time decision is the first audience reaction, and the work must record that decision rather than stage it.

## Mechanism M3: rubbish as material, the street as studio

The city's waste stream -- strike rubbish, dustbin piles -- is documented and marked in place, turning municipal failure into a visible, growing drawing. La Poubelle (1970) is a 16mm film of the mounting rubbish during the London dustbin workers' strike; Ehrenberg sprayed the peripheries of rubbish piles in white spray paint as they grew, so the outline creeps outward with the pile. Garbage Walk (1972, or the 1970 strike documentation under a later name -- date unresolved) repeats the gesture as photographs tracing the footprint of massive rubbish piles. His own line, from the film: art is "anything that breaks your programmations, that jolts you, pushes you, catalyses you, outside a mode of behaviour". (C11551, C11564, C11570, C11571, C11507)

**Takeaway:** the city draws itself if you mark it. The piece must let a real, growing quantity (a pile, a count, a backlog) drive the mark-making, so the drawing is a record of accumulation, not an illustration of it.

## Mechanism M4: the handmade book as a portable exhibition

A book made by hand from waste paper and corrugated cardboard, printed on a mimeograph in a farmhouse, carries an exhibition that can be posted anywhere -- the book IS the gallery. Sunday / Cantata Dominical (1972) and Pussywillow: A Journal of Conditions (1973) are Beau Geste Press books built this way: Pussywillow is printed on waste wrapping paper, bound in used corrugated cardboard, 22 x 15.5 x 0.5 cm, holding letters, clippings, journal entries, family photos, poems and drawings in the aftermath of the Pinochet coup. The FLUXshoe catalogue (1972), the press's first publication, is the same mechanism as a travelling show in paper form. His own account: "the author himself gets his hands dirty with ink and glue in order to reach the very guts of that piece of furniture, that receptacle, that display case, that platform we call a book. You eliminate outsourcing. You eliminate intermediaries." (C11556, C11557, C11574, C11512)

**Takeaway:** a book that is an exhibition, not a catalogue of one. The piece must make turning the page feel like entering the next room, with cheap, precarious material as a deliberate choice, not a budget constraint.

## Mechanism M5: the press as a way of life, the commune as the machine

Publishing is not a business but a household -- a farmhouse community that prints, binds and distributes its own books, with letter-writing as the distribution infrastructure. Beau Geste Press itself is the work: Langford Court, a deserted farmhouse in Devon, humming with duplicators; envelopes leaving daily for Britain, Latin America and Eastern Europe; the catalogue declaring "We exist because you exist" and "Our Press is not a business, it's a way of life". David Mayor's obsessive letter-writing made the press internationally recognised; Martha Hellion's logistical organisation and hospitality enabled the community to function. Carrion's two 1973 bookworks are the press's most famous output, and Carrion's own account says he was inspired by its artisanal, communal, self-directed approach. (C11508, C11606-C11609, C11619, C11600)

**Takeaway:** the machine is the household. A piece on this mechanism must show the work of keeping the press alive -- the printing, the binding, the envelopes -- as the artwork, with the books as its by-product, not its point.

## Mechanism M6: the collective poem, the mail network scaled up

An open invitation with no selection criteria turns the postal network into a collective author -- hundreds of strangers fill identical blank squares on one theme and the replies become the work. Poema Colectivo: Revolucion (1981-1983), by Colectivo 3 (not solo Ehrenberg -- never present it as his): 700 invitations, each with a letter-size sheet and a blank square to fill on the theme of revolution; 350 submissions from 45 countries over two years. MoMA presents it as the zenith of the mail-art network Ehrenberg helped develop. The small-scale prototype is Arriba y Adelante (1970): 200 postcards, one image, assembled by strangers at the Salon. (C11562, C11563, C11550)

**Takeaway:** the invitation is the artwork and the replies are the exhibition. The piece must set one blank, identical frame, invite the crowd to fill it with no selection, and let the pile of answers -- its size, its spread, its gaps -- be the visible result.

## Mechanism M7: walking as sculpture, art defined in public

A walk, or a public gesture documented only in pinhole photographs, answers a theoretical question ("what is art?") with an ephemeral act instead of an object. El arte segun yo (1979): a performance in Chapultepec Park, Mexico City, documented in pinhole photographs, 11 selected by MoMA -- an ephemeral public gesture answering the definition of art outside the museum. Caminata escultorica, escultura caminada (1982): urban walking as aesthetic practice, the walk itself the sculpture. The stance behind both: "Creation and art are two completely different concepts for me. Creation is organic, it is internal matter. Art is a historic definition -- a solidifying element." (C11551, C11554, C11507)

**Takeaway:** the act is the answer and the trace is the only object. The piece must let a walk or a gesture carry the whole argument, and let the documentation be thin and incidental -- a pinhole photo, a route line -- never a monument to the walk.

## Mechanism M8: the archive recycled -- giant book-objects and the life as collage

Decades of stencils, ephemera, fragments and files -- the "Visual Information Bank" -- are condensed into codex form, blown up into six-foot book-objects and outdoor installations, and finally recycled into 34 collages that retell the whole career. Codex Aeroscriptus Ehrenbergensis (1990): a casebound book, 20 pages, edition of 500, images drawn from the Visual Information Bank of stencil and rubber-stamp ephemera. Preterito Imperfecto (1992): a semicircle of six-foot-tall two-panelled "book objects" with painting, cut-outs, ropes, toys and mechanics. Light Up Our Border I-II (1990): large outdoor installations on the US-Mexico relationship. Testamento (1968-2017): 34 collages on paper from documents, photos, writings, drawings, sketches, ephemera, handwritten texts, snapshots and portraits amassed over nearly fifty years, compiled in the final months of his life, referencing La caida, A Date with Fate, Garbage Walk and Beau Geste Press correspondence. His own frame: "FileWorks: My Archive as Artwork" -- the archive itself is claimed as a work of art. (C11566, C11567, C11555, C11578, C11553, C11551)

**Takeaway:** the file is the work, and the same motifs return in new forms. A piece on this mechanism must recycle its own earlier material -- the same stencil, the same fragment, the same photograph -- at a new scale, so the archive is visibly being re-read rather than displayed.

## Aesthetic logic

Ehrenberg has no recognisable style, and that is the point: he called himself a "neologist" and refused the modernist demand to "find your voice" and keep to it for the market. The continuity is not a look but a method: cheap and found material (waste wrapping paper, corrugated cardboard, soot ink, nightgown silk screens, white spray paint on rubbish); handmade production even inside conceptualism ("we cannot abandon handmade work: drawing, printing, making things with our hands"); process and circulation as part of the finished product; art and life as a single indivisible unit; collective authorship over the single signature. The political address is constant -- to Mexico, from a distance, under regimes that made the post dangerous -- but the tone is joyful rather than grim: "Caramba, dear!! Is joie-de-vivre good enough for you? I can't conceive myself making art without enjoying doing so." The scale moves over fifty years from postcard to six-foot book-object to outdoor installation, and the durability moves from ephemeral telegram and rubbish film to acquired installation and bound codex, but the voice moves the other way: from single provocateur at the Tate door to teacher of 25 instructors to witness-narrator of Moctezuma. A piece built from this study should feel handmade, cheap, postal and collective -- ink on the hands, envelopes in the post, a crowd filling the frame -- and should never look like a gallery object pretending to be poor. (C11507, C11512, C11513, C11551, C11552, C11554)
