## M1. The artisan who cannot sew

Farani's own line: "non sono un sarto, io non so cucire... ho una base perche mia madre aveva fatto scuola di taglio a Parma intorno al 1903-1904." The founder of a tailoring house disclaims the trade. The knowledge is watched, not learned: his mother chalking and cutting while the dressmaker sewed, and he learned these things there. The mechanism is the outsider's eye: he runs the house by judgement, not by hand. The paradox is not a conflict to resolve, see C15; it is the point. A man who cannot sew can still decide what sewing is for, and the house he built proves it, because the decisions were his and the stitches were other people's, and the costumes are famous for the decisions, not for the stitches.

**Takeaway:** let the eye lead and the hand follow, and the piece should be judged by a decision, not by a technique.

## M2. The officina, not the sartoria

"Farani aveva ragione a dire di non essere un sarto, perche non era solo un sarto e la sua piu che una sartoria era un'officina, una bottega, un luogo di sperimentazione", per Basile. The atelier holds archaic looms, a pleating press, blacksmiths melting lead, carpenters making busts two centimetres thick, dyers and painters. The costume is a manufactured object; the sewing room is the smallest part of the factory. Tosi Pamphili: the costumier "seemed more like a Renaissance bottega, where they experimented out of instinct and not out of duty". The word matters: a sartoria makes clothes, an officina makes things, and the Farani house made costumes the way a workshop makes instruments, by inventing the tool and then the object.

**Takeaway:** build the workshop before the garment, and the piece should show the tools that made it, not hide them.

## M3. The invented material, "le materie"

Donati and Farani "rivoluzionato i canoni classici del far costume sperimentando nuove tecniche, usando materiali inconsueti, creando le 'materie'". The material is invented per film: riscaldo, wool wadding put inside jackets, as protagonist in the Gospel; hat felt as fresco in the Decameron; mirror velvet in the Canterbury Tales; laminates, upholstery tassels and sponge fringe in Arabian Nights; plastic, vinyl and fur in Barbarella. The period is secondary; the matter is primary. No film starts from a period fabric catalogue; each film starts from a question about what this world is made of, and the atelier answers by making the matter first and the costume second.

**Takeaway:** invent the material first and let the period follow it, and the piece should be made of one invented matter, not of a period look.

## M4. The machine built for one film

The "macchina infernale": an enormous iron press fed by four improbable gas cylinders, built to pleat kilometres of cotton and silk for Satyricon, a real forge of other times, "una vera fucina d'altri tempi", per Piccolo. The archaic loom was built to weave "tribal" weaves for Oedipus Rex. The pleating machine was invented with Donati for La ricotta. The tool is invented for the production and then kept, and exhibited: the press used to shape the Oedipus Rex costumes was shown in I vestiti dei sogni in 2015. The atelier accumulates machines the way a painter accumulates brushes, and each machine is a mechanism made permanent, a one-film invention that stays in the room for the next film.

**Takeaway:** invent one tool for the piece and keep it visible, and the piece should show the machine that made it as part of itself.

## M5. Costume as painting and sculpture

"I costumi perdono la loro natura sartoriale e diventano pittura, scultura: i tessuti si trasformano o vengono accostati a materie altre che a loro volta dimenticano di essere nate plastiche, carta, paglia, metallo o conchiglia", per Basile. In La ricotta the dyed taffeta is treated until it takes the folds of Rosso Fiorentino's Deposition of 1521 and Pontormo's of 1525-28. Saillard on the Gospel: "It's a beautiful exercise in painting, certainly more painting than fashion." The costume is judged as a picture, not as a garment. The drape in Donati's work is the translation of an untranslatable character into cloth, see #431 M13, and Farani is the hand that makes the translation hold, because a painted fold that will not stand up on a body is only a sketch, and the atelier makes it stand.

**Takeaway:** judge the costume as a picture that has to stand up, and the piece should be composed like a painting and built like a sculpture.

## M6. One colour code per film

"According to Donati, every film had to have its own specific colour code and a fabric", per Saillard. Gospel: wool and boiled wool, black and white, against the colourful Hollywood colossal. Decameron: felt. Canterbury Tales: mirror velvet, one material, brazen mixed colours. Oedipus Rex: salmon nuances, Oedipus always in black, Jocasta always in white. Casanova: three worlds, pastel taffetas for the marquise d'Urfe, showy coloured velvets and satins for the sex contest, black with enormous sequin ruffles for the Parma dinner. Salo: period prints, rigorous lines, the discipline at the end of the line. Non ci resta che piangere: the actors' own colours, white and sky blue, like the Napoli shirt, for Troisi, and red and white, the colours of Castiglion Fiorentino, Benigni's birthplace, for Benigni. The code is the film's identity, worn.

**Takeaway:** give the piece one colour code and one fabric and let nothing outside it in, and the piece should be identifiable by its palette alone.

## M7. Weight as acting

The Canterbury Tales costumes weigh "like 20 kilos... it might have been nearer 30 kilos", per Saillard. The huge boiled-wool dresses "were really designed to give a 'backbone' to the actor, and to deny his or her belonging to the 20th century. Those clothes really weren't the actor's friends!" The costume constrains the body into the period; the actor must earn the movement. Saillard's parallel is Comme des Garcons, Kawakubo, Yohji, clothes that hinder on purpose. The mechanism is the opposite of the comfortable costume: the weight is the performance, because a body that moves easily in a medieval dress is still a twentieth-century body, and a body that must fight the dress has already left its own century.

**Takeaway:** make the costume heavy enough to change how the body moves, and the piece should show the effort, not hide it.

## M8. Ageing as a second making

The dyers and painters "trasformano i colori industriali dei tessuti nelle piu raffinate palette desunte dai capolavori dell'arte italiana, ma che anche stingono, invecchiano, sporcano, patinano regalando a quei costumi una storia, una vita precedente", per Basile. The new is made to look lived-in: faded, aged, dirtied, patinated. The costume arrives on set already carrying a past it never had. This is the atelier's second making, after the construction: the first making builds the garment, the second making gives it a life, and the life is painted on, stain by stain, so that the actor does not have to pretend the clothes are old, because the clothes already are.

**Takeaway:** age everything before it is seen, and the piece should arrive with a past already painted into it.

## M9. No needle

Oedipus Rex: "senza usare l'ago!", without using a needle. La ricotta: draped cloths held underneath with iron wire, "i costumi erano panni drappeggiati retti sotto con fil di ferro". The Gospel's big felt outfits are "held together with staples... made like costumes for the theatre", per Saillard. Construction without sewing is the atelier's signature refusal: the garment is draped, wired, stapled, pleated, anything but sewn. The refusal is the mechanism: sewing makes a garment, and Farani's house makes objects that only look like garments, and the look is held together by whatever the material demands, wire for a painting, staples for a felt, a press for a pleat.

**Takeaway:** hold the piece together without its obvious fastener, and let the holding be visible.

## M10. The crowd as one organism

"For Arabian Nights alone, there are literally hundreds of costumes for all the extras", per Saillard. The atelier's scale is the crowd's scale: the warehouse holds hundreds per film, arranged by film. The star's costume and the fiftieth extra's are made in the same room, by the same hands, to the same standard, and the exhibition hangs them the same way, on hangers, by film. Donati dresses the mass, not the extra, see #431 M14, and Farani is the reason the mass can be dressed at all, because a crowd of hundreds cannot be bought, it can only be manufactured, and the manufacture is the atelier's real product.

**Takeaway:** design the crowd as one garment, and the piece should read as a single field before it reads as many bodies.

## M11. Paper and glue

Canzonissima: "costumes out of a little glue and a lot of imagination", per Tosi Pamphili. Paper outfits for a television variety show are the purest Donati-Farani object: the cheapest possible material, made permanent for the night by craft. The mechanism is the opposite of the archive: the costume that is designed to die after one broadcast. Where M12 keeps everything, M11 keeps nothing, and both are the same house, because the atelier's craft is not in the material, it is in the making, and paper made with imagination holds for a night as well as wool holds for fifty years.

**Takeaway:** make the cheapest material hold for exactly one night, and the piece should be built to vanish after it is seen.

## M12. The archive as a second life

The costumes are kept at the atelier, arranged by film, on hangers, and exhibited the same way, in Tutto e santo, "just as they are in the archives of Sartoria Farani". They are not rented, not reused, not given away: "They do lend out costumes, just not those from Pasolini's films", per Saillard. Then the second life arrives: the same costumes become the performance in Embodying Pasolini, worn again by Swinton fifty years on, forty costumes by the widely cited count, see C7. Saillard: "these Pasolini costumes are monuments because we have decided that they are." The atelier is a museum that never decided to be one, and the decision came later, from the people who looked at the hangers and saw monuments.

**Takeaway:** keep everything and let the keeping become the work, and the piece should be an archive that wakes up and is worn again.

## M13. Succession by choice, not blood

Farani, "lungimirante", chose Luigi Piccolo in the early 1980s "dopo tanti tentativi andati male", after many failed attempts, per Basile. The succession is a willed act, not an inheritance: he picked the continuator, trained him beside him from 1980, and the house changed direction under him, toward philological reconstruction, without breaking. The atelier survives its founder because the founder planned the survival. Piccolo then met Lina Cardone, "young, tireless and enthusiastic continuator of everything told so far", and the second succession is already in place. The mechanism is the opposite of the family business: the house is passed to the person who can hold it, not to the person who was born into it.

**Takeaway:** choose the successor and train them beside you, and the piece should hand itself on, not end with its maker.

## M14. The authentic collection beside the invented

Piccolo built a collection of about 700 authentic garments, mostly early 1800s to post-war, beside Dior and Balmain anonymous pieces chosen for an original cut or embroidery, plus old books, fashion magazines from the early 1800s, ethnic costumes, per Basile. Invention is fed by reference: the house that invents materials also collects the real thing, to measure against. Romaison's "widespread museum" is this logic at city scale: authentic pieces from the late 1700s to today, set beside the ateliers' inventions. The collection is not a museum of the past; it is the atelier's measuring instrument, and every invented material is checked against a real one.

**Takeaway:** keep the real thing beside the invention and measure one against the other, and the piece should show both.

## M15. The school inside the shop

"Accanto ai grandi costumisti si proteggono e si aiutano i giovani", per Basile. Alessandra Torella, grown in the sartoria and Donati's assistant, now signs opera and cinema and curated Costumi a Corte. Alessandro Ciammarughi, Samaritani's assistant, now a sought-after theatre designer. Maria Filippi, working with Carla Fracci and Beppe Menegatti. Antonella Cappuccio worked there as a young woman and photographs the costumes as art. The atelier trains by trust: "si decide spesso di lavorare comunque in piena liberta anche se i budget non lo permetterebbero", they often decide to work in full freedom even when the budget would not allow it. The school has no curriculum; it has a room, and the young are protected inside it until they can sign their own work.

**Takeaway:** protect the young inside the shop and let them sign their own work, and the piece should make room for a second hand.

## M16. The shadow ban

The Pasolini costumes are preserved but unused because of the cultural shadow over Pasolini: "There is a sort of shadow ban against Pasolini, and people are scared of delving into that. Italy isn't like France. There, he is still seen as a 'communist pederast'", per Saillard. "It remains a very fragile legacy." Preservation by neglect: the costumes survive in good condition precisely because no one dares to touch them. The mechanism is political, not technical. The atelier kept the costumes safe by keeping them still, and the stillness is what made the second life possible, because a costume that had been rented out a hundred times would not have survived to be worn by Swinton in 2021.

**Takeaway:** let the ban be the reason the thing survives, and the piece should show what neglect preserved.

## M17. The sideways career

Radio actor, then film actor, then prop man, then assistant in a sartoria, then director of it, then founder of his own. No school, no apprenticeship in the trade, no sewing. The career path is the mechanism: the man who arrives from the side sees the house differently from the man trained inside it. Compare M1: the eye over the hand. The steps of Trinita dei Monti were the real school, because the network was formed before the trade, and the trade was learned inside the network, from Donati and the Allegri sisters and the cutter Trochei, not from a classroom.

**Takeaway:** arrive from the side and let the outside eye run the house, and the piece should be made by someone who never trained for it.

## M18. The bottega chain

"Ogni abito sara un'opera, ogni costume diviene la traduzione della visione di un regista fatta da un artista e poi da un artigiano. Una catena creativa che tocca livelli altissimi sempre grazie a quelle intese profonde fra persone", per Tosi Pamphili. Director to designer to artisan to actor: four links, and the chain only holds if the understanding between the people is profound and personal. "Without a profound and very personal understanding between all those involved, this creative chain could never have achieved such brilliance", per Grey Magazine. The mechanism is relational, not technical, and it is why the partnership lasted forty years, and why the atelier outlived the partnership, because the chain was rebuilt with Piccolo and then with Cardone, link by link.

**Takeaway:** make the chain of four hands visible, and the piece should show each hand passing the work to the next.

## Evolution

Seven phases. Phase 1, the child of the cutting table, 1922 to the early 1950s: Cadeo, the mother's Parma cutting course of 1903-04, the Prati marionettes in Fidenza, radio acting at RAI Turin; the knowledge is domestic and watched, not taught in a school, and he will later say he never learned to sew. Phase 2, Rome, the steps, early 1950s: actor and props man, the Spanish Steps afternoons with the generation that will make post-war Italian cinema; the network is formed before the trade. Phase 3, Annamode, mid-1950s to 1962: assistant, then director of the Allegri sisters' sartoria, variety sketches with Donati, the cutter Benito Trochei; the apprenticeship is in someone else's house, on television and variety, not on film. Phase 4, the officina, 1962 to the late 1970s: Viale Mazzini, the Donati partnership becomes a factory of invention, looms, the pleating press, blacksmiths, carpenters, dyers, painters; the Pasolini cycle 1963-1975, the Fellini cycle 1969-1987, the Zeffirelli cycle 1962-1968, Barbarella 1968; two Oscars for the designer, none for the maker; television runs alongside, Canzonissima, Studio Uno, La vita di Leonardo. Phase 5, the turn to the stage, late 1970s to 1980s: cinema declines, the sartoria turns to prose and opera with a new generation of designers and directors, Luzzati, Cali, Squarciapino, Scaparro, Lavia, Pasqual, Konchalovsky; The Magic Flute 1980, La Traviata 1984, Armida 1985; the invention continues, but the stage replaces the screen. Phase 6, succession and the gift, early 1980s to 1997: Farani chooses Piccolo after failed attempts with others, Piccolo at his side from 1980, the CSAC donation, date disputed, see C2; Farani dies in November 1997, Piccolo moves the atelier to Via Dandolo and steers it toward philological reconstruction. Phase 7, the afterlife, 1997 to 2026: the atelier outlives its founder by nearly thirty years, the archive becomes the work, the 1996 Parma show, Costumi a Corte 2007, Trame di Cinema 2014, Romaison 2020, Embodying Pasolini 2021-2024, Tutto e santo 2022-23, Cinecitta si Mostra 2024 and 2026; the Pasolini costumes, never lent, are now the most exhibited Italian film costumes in the world, and the maker is finally the subject.

## Aesthetic logic

The Farani logic is that the costume is not a garment but a manufactured object, and the object is not designed but realized, and the realization is the art. Donati imagined, Farani made, and the making is where the invention lives, because Donati never drew and the paper never held the costume, and the costume only exists because a workshop held looms and presses and dyers and blacksmiths and carpenters and painters in one room. The logic runs against the fashion logic: the atelier does not follow a period, it invents a material per film, and it does not follow a silhouette, it follows a colour code, and it does not make the actor comfortable, it gives the actor a backbone of boiled wool and thirty kilos of mirror velvet, and it does not preserve by exhibiting, it preserves by not lending, and the not lending is what keeps the costumes alive. The second logic is that the house is a chain, not a person: director to designer to artisan to actor, and the chain holds only on profound personal understanding, and when the understanding breaks the house finds a new link, Piccolo for Farani, Cardone for Piccolo, and the house goes on. The third logic is that the archive is the work: the costumes were made for films that ended, and the films ended, and the costumes stayed on their hangers, arranged by film, and fifty years later a woman put them on again in a slaughterhouse in Rome and they were monuments, because someone decided they were. The maker's revenge is quiet: the designer got the Oscars, the maker got the afterlife, and the afterlife is longer.

## Network

N1. DANILO DONATI (1926-2001). The designer, studied as #431. The partnership runs from the Annamode years, mid-1950s, through Farani's death in 1997, more than forty years, television, cinema and theatre. Tosi Pamphili: "His relationship with Piero Farani is proof of the benefit derived from the synergy of conception and realization, design and production, artist and artisan." Donati never drew; Farani was the hand that turned notes into matter. The two Oscars, 1969 and 1977, are Donati's; the work is the partnership's.

N2. PIER PAOLO PASOLINI (1922-1975). The director whose films are the atelier's core. Donati met Pasolini on Mamma Roma in 1962 and made the costumes for practically all his films except Medea, nine titles in the Trame selection, from La ricotta in 1963 to Salo in 1975, and Pasolini died on Sunday 2 November 1975. Saillard: "Did Donati make the costumes for Pasolini's period films at Farani? Yes, always at Farani. All the costumes in the exhibition are by Sartoria Farani." Treccani's Costumi entry says Pasolini could accentuate the "archaic" character of his works precisely thanks to the Farani costumes.

N3. FEDERICO FELLINI (1920-1993). Satyricon, I Clowns, Amarcord, Casanova, Intervista, and Roma and Ginger and Fred in Donati's filmography. Tosi Pamphili notes the partnership's "creative antipathy for Federico Fellini", a phrase that is ambiguous, whose antipathy and for what, and it is carried as a quote, not a paraphrase. Fellini's Casanova won Donati his second Oscar.

N4. FRANCO ZEFFIRELLI (1923-2019). Met on the Spanish Steps in the early 1950s. Directed the atelier's first production, Hamlet, 1962, and its two Oscar films' first, Romeo and Juliet, 1968, and The Taming of the Shrew, 1967. Also La Traviata, 1984, at Farani. Zeffirelli was studied as #425, do NOT re-pick.

N5. ANNA AND TERESA ALLEGRI. Sisters, owners and soul of the Annamode sartoria, where Farani served five years and Donati brought him. A photograph of the two sisters appears in Romaison 2020. The house that trained both men. Annamode is itself a candidate subject.

N6. BENITO TROCHEI. The cutter Farani met at Annamode, "giovanissimo", who decided to follow him into the new adventure in 1962. The one named pair of hands who moved with the founder. No further biographical detail found this leg.

N7. LUIGI (LUIGINO) PICCOLO, called Giuti. From Friuli. At Farani's side from 1980, chosen in the early 1980s as the ideal continuator after failed attempts with others, succeeded on Farani's death in 1997, moved the atelier to Via Dandolo. Curator of the Trame di Cinema installation in 2014 and of the Neuberger loan in 2020, ideator of Costumi a Corte in 2007, credited on Embodying Pasolini, "Ateliers Farani, directed by Luigi Piccolo". Under him the direction shifts toward the study and philological reconstruction of costume. He built a collection of about 700 authentic garments, mostly early 1800s to post-war, including Dior and Balmain.

N8. LINA CARDONE. Head of the costume shop in the USITT visit of 2013: the tour group "spoke with Lina Cardone, head of the costume shop, and saw two original Watteaus". N10. OLIVIER SAILLARD. French fashion historian, former director of the Palais Galliera, Paris. Met Tosi Pamphili at a party in Florence, via Silvia Fendi, asked to see the Pasolini costumes, was taken to Sartoria Farani. With Tilda Swinton he created Embodying Pasolini, 2021-2024, and co-curated the costume section of Tutto e santo. His System Magazine interview is the single richest source this leg on how the costumes are made and kept. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N11. TILDA SWINTON. Wore the Pasolini costumes in Embodying Pasolini, Rome 2021, Paris 2022, Athens 2023, Taipei 2024. Saillard: "the base is the shoulders and the moulding is the flesh". The body that re-inhabited the costumes. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N12. UMBERTO TIRELLI (1928-1990). Founder of Tirelli Costumi in 1964, the rival Roman house. Photographed "e Dindo in Sartoria 1983", in Romaison 2020. Tirelli's house built pieces for Taming of the Shrew, see C9, and rents out its archive, Visconti's Leopard costumes still circulate, per Saillard, where Farani's Pasolini costumes do not. The two houses are the twin poles of Roman costume: Tirelli the collector-renter, Farani the maker-keeper. Already stands as a carried candidate from #429 and #430, NOT re-proposed as new.

N13. JACQUES FONTERAY. French costume designer of Barbarella, 1968. All eight of Fonda's outfits are his, manufactured by Farani. The proof that the atelier worked for designers beyond Donati, and that its name travels without Donati's. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N14. PACO RABANNE (1934-2023). Fashion designer, inspired one Barbarella costume, the green final dress, and is wrongly credited with the whole film, see C11. The false credit is itself evidence: the world remembers the famous name, not the maker. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N15. PIERO TOSI (1927-2019). Studied as #428. The other great Italian costume designer, did Medea in 1969 where Donati did not, and did make-up for Satyricon. The philological pole against Donati's inventive pole, see #431. Do NOT re-pick.

N16. EZIO FRIGERIO (1930-2022). Set and costume designer, La vita di Leonardo, 1971, for Farani, Strehler's masterpieces at the Piccolo in Milan. One of the great names who "frequented" the sartoria, per Basile. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N17. SANTUZZA CALI. Costume designer, Armida, 1985, at Farani, subject of a 2024 Silvana monograph. Named in the late-1970s new generation. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N18. EMANUELE (LELE) LUZZATI (1921-2007). Set and costume designer, named among the late-1970s generation and among Farani's collaborators. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N19. FRANCA SQUARCIAPINO (born 1940). Costume designer, Oscar winner for Cyrano de Bergerac, 1990, named in the late-1970s generation and among Farani's collaborators. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N20. MARIA DE MATTEIS (1898-1988). Costume designer, named among Farani's collaborators. One of the earliest Italian film costume designers. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N21. EZIO ALTIERI. Costume designer of Non ci resta che piangere, 1984, made at Farani. The colour code turned personal. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N22. ALESSANDRA TORELLA. Grew up in the sartoria, Donati's assistant, now signs for opera and cinema. Curator of Costumi a Corte, 2007, costumes for Tosca at Sannio 2005. The atelier's own graduate. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N23. ROBERTO CHIESI. Film critic, head of the Centro Studi - Archivio Pasolini, Fondazione Cineteca di Bologna. Author of the Trame di Cinema catalogue essay on Pasolini and Donati, Il Piccolo, 22 March 2014, the source for the no-drawing fact, the iron-wire drape, the pleating machine, the archaic loom, and the boiling dye pots.

N24. ARTURO CARLO QUINTAVALLE and BONIZZA GIORDANI ARAGNO. CSAC scholars, authors of the two key essays in the 1996 Skira catalogue, "Piero Farani: il mestiere del costumista" and "Officina Farani". The catalogue itself was not read this leg, see Depth; the essays are known by title, page and citation only.

N25. MUSTAFA SABBAGH (born 1964). Photographer of the Trame di Cinema catalogue; his images of the costumes on models with covered faces are the visual record of the 2014 show. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N26. ANTONELLA CAPPUCCIO. Artist, worked in the Farani atelier as a young woman; her Theatrum Mundi photographs Farani costumes as plastic and poetic objects. The atelier as a school that keeps producing artists. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED, see Future candidates.

N27. CARLO RAMBALDI (1925-2012). Special effects designer, realized the Black Queen's "leathermen" guards in Barbarella. The boundary between costume and effect, inside one film.

N28. LOREDANA BUSCEMI. Costume designer, the Monica Bellucci dress in Le meraviglie, 2014, made by Sartoria Farani. The most recent Farani film work evidenced this leg.

N29. SARA MARTIN and MATILDE ALGHISI. University of Parma, authors of the 2026 ZoneModa Journal paper on the Farani opera costumes, Martin is deputy director of the CSAC. The current academic custodians of the archive.

N30. SILVIA FENDI and VIRGINIA RAGGI. Fendi: at whose Florence party Saillard met Tosi Pamphili, originator of the Secrets of Couture project, 2016, which gave a virtual-reality visit to the Valentino and Sartoria Farani ateliers. Raggi: Mayor of Rome 2016-2021, supported Romaison and Embodying Pasolini, "Rome starts anew from its excellences, and Italy starts anew with Rome".

## Load-bearing corrections

C0. BIRTH NAME: Piero Faverzani, it.wikipedia Piero Farani and ask-oracle, against Pietro Faverzani, Treccani, Enciclopedia del Cinema, Mario Verdone, "Nome d'arte di Pietro Faverzani". Treccani is the stronger source. This file uses Pietro Faverzani, stage name Piero Farani, and notes the variant. The Wikipedia "Piero" looks like a back-formation from the stage name. Unresolved in the strict sense: no birth record was consulted this leg.

C1. FOUNDING DATE: September 1962, farani.it via it.wikipedia, Basile, Treccani, Italian Journal, "established in 1962", against "fondata da Piero Farani a Roma alla fine degli anni cinquanta", undo.net Trame di Cinema notice. 1962 stands; the undo.net line is an error, probably confusing the late 1950s Annamode years with the founding.

C2. CSAC DONATION, DATE AND COUNT: three dates, 1980, "un gruppo di oltre duecento costumi", Basile 2007; 1993, by public deed, it.wikipedia Sartoria Farani; "poco prima di morire", it.wikipedia Piero Farani, that is 1997. Two counts: 250 costumes donated, Piero Farani page, against a fondo today of 221 costumes and 225 photographic prints, Sartoria Farani page. UNRESOLVED. The most specific claim is the 1993 public deed; 1980 may be a confusion with an earlier partial gift or simply wrong; 250 against 221 may be donation against catalogued count. This file says "donated by public deed in 1993" and notes the dissent.

C3. OSCAR YEARS, FILM AGAINST CEREMONY: sources mix freely. Grey Magazine says Donati won "with Romeo and Juliet in 1969 and Fellini's Casanova in 1976", one ceremony year and one film year in a single sentence. it.wikipedia Piero Farani says "nel 1968 arriva anche l'Oscar per i costumi di Romeo e Giulietta" and "altro Oscar nel 1976 per Casanova", both film years. undo.net Trame says "nel 1969 ... e nel 1977", both ceremony years. Resolution: film 1968, 41st Academy Awards Monday 14 April 1969; film 1976, 49th Academy Awards Monday 28 March 1977, ceremony dates verified in #431. Always give both years.

C4. INTERVISTA YEAR: 1986, it.wikipedia Sartoria Farani, against 1987, undo.net Trame, Italian Journal, Frock Flicks' Donati list. 1987 stands, the film premiered in 1987; 1986 is the production year. Flagged, not silently picked.

C5. DECAMERON YEAR: 1970, undo.net Trame, against 1971, it.wikipedia Sartoria Farani, Frock Flicks. 1971 stands, Berlinale premiere June 1971; 1970 is the production year. Flagged.

C6. CANTERBURY TALES YEAR: 1971, undo.net Trame, against 1972, it.wikipedia Sartoria Farani, Frock Flicks. 1972 stands, release year; 1971 is production. Note the pattern: the undo.net Trame text dates the Trilogia della vita a year early throughout, Decameron 1970, Canterbury 1971, Arabian Nights given as 1973 in its list against 1974 elsewhere. The catalogue text's years are unreliable; use release years.

C7. EMBODYING PASOLINI COSTUME COUNT: 40, A Shaded View on Fashion and System Magazine introduction, "wear a selection of 40 costumes", against "approximately 80 costumes for the performance", Saillard, same System interview, against "more than thirty costumes", Onassis Foundation. UNRESOLVED. 40 is the widely cited figure for the Rome premiere; 80 may count the full selection including the four-hour loop, or may be Saillard speaking loosely. This file says "forty costumes by the widely cited count" with the 80 noted.

C8. COSTUMI A CORTE CLOSING DATE: 15 September 2007, undo.net catalogue text, against 11 November 2007, it.wikipedia Sartoria Farani bibliography. UNRESOLVED. The catalogue text, 15 September, is preferred; the show may have been extended. Inauguration Wednesday 13 June 2007 is agreed and weekday-verified.

C9. TAMING OF THE SHREW MAKER: Farani, undo.net Trame and the Frock Flicks photograph credited "Photo by Sartoria Farani", against Tirelli, Frock Flicks, "Donati had the edge of working with Tirelli Costumes, which built the pieces". UNRESOLVED. Both houses may have built pieces; the film's costume work was split with Irene Sharaff in any case. This file does not assert a single maker.

C10. "COSTUMES DESIGNED BY FARANI": the Neuberger Museum text says the show displays "the costumes designed by Farani that were used in his [Pasolini's] films". Wrong: Donati designed, Farani realized. The same conflation appears in looser writing elsewhere. Attribution error in the museum text; this file keeps the split clean, because the split IS the study.

C11. BARBARELLA CREDIT: Paco Rabanne as designer, en.wikipedia Paco Rabanne, "known for designing the iconic green dress, as well as the other costumes", Vintage News Daily, Escama Studio, against Jacques Fonteray designed all, Rabanne inspired one costume, Costume Design Archive, Lunden, "Barbarella's wardrobe", Film, Fashion and Consumption 2016, en.wikipedia Barbarella, "All costumes in the film... were designed by French costume designer Jacques Fonteray and manufactured by Sartoria Farani, with Barbarella's costume in the final scenes being, as the credits put it, 'inspired by ideas of' Paco Rabanne". Fonteray designed; Rabanne one costume. The Rabanne credit is the famous-name error, corrected in the scholarship.

C12. DONATI-PASOLINI FIRST FILM: La ricotta, 1963, as first collaboration, Chiesi, against the Gospel, 1964, as "the first time Danilo Donati worked with director Pier Paolo Pasolini", Frock Flicks. La ricotta was the first collaboration; the Gospel was the first full costume film. Both statements are true of different things.

C13. "FOR ABOUT 20 YEARS, DONATI WAS PRETTY MUCH THE ONLY ONE USING THE ATELIER", Saillard, System, against the forty-year partnership and the many other designers from the late 1970s, Luzzati, Cali, Squarciapino and others. Saillard is speaking loosely: roughly 1962 to the early 1980s the atelier was effectively Donati's, then it opened to others. Treat "20 years" as approximate, not a date.

C14. LUIGI AGAINST LUIGINO PICCOLO: sources use Luigi, it.wikipedia, Luigino, Mocenigo, Neuberger, Trame, and Giuti as nickname, Basile, JustPaste. Onassis writes "Luigi Picolo", one c. Taipei writes "Luigi Picolo". Luigi "Luigino" Piccolo, called Giuti; "Picolo" is a typo. This file uses Luigi Piccolo with Luigino noted.

C15. "I AM NOT A TAILOR" AGAINST FOUNDER OF A TAILORING HOUSE: not a factual conflict, a paradox to carry, not resolve. Farani's self-description is the point: the house is run by judgement, not by the needle.

C16. MEDEA ANECDOTE: Saillard, Callas was scared of Donati's costumes, so Tosi did Medea. Single source, Saillard, System interview. The credit itself, Tosi, not Donati, is multiply attested, see #431 C0; Callas's motive is anecdote. Do not state the motive as fact.

C17. NAMES OF THE HOUSE: "Sartoria Farani", "Sartoria Teatrale Farani", "Casa di Alta Moda Farani", Mocenigo, "Sartoria Teatrale Farani di Piccolo Luigino", Mocenigo, "Ateliers Farani", Onassis. Variants, not conflicts. The house is one house; the names track its phases.

C18. "DONATI NEVER THOUGHT VERY HIGHLY OF THE OSCARS", Tosi Pamphili, via Grey Magazine. Secondhand, and #431 C11 already flags it: the claim rests on Tosi Pamphili's reading of the 1967 Sharaff loss. Carried as attributed opinion, not fact.

C19. 1996 EXHIBITION VENUE: "sede dello CSAC" and Abbazia di Valserena, ZMJ references, against "Padiglione Nervi", Sartoria Farani bibliography, "Padiglione Nervi, 10 Febbraio-31 Marzo 1996". UNRESOLVED. Both may be true of different parts of the show; do not assert a single venue.

C20. TRAME DI CINEMA DATES: "9 marzo-22 giugno 2014", undo.net, the catalogue, Chiesi, against arte.it's calendar listing "Dal 8 Marzo 2013 al 22 Giugno 2014". 2014 stands; arte.it's 2013 is a typo; the show opened Saturday 8 March 2014, weekday-verified.
