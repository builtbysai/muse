# I. Scott MacKenzie: Study Notes

**Site:** I. Scott MacKenzie is a Canadian computer scientist and human-computer interaction (HCI) researcher, Associate Professor in the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science (EECS), Lassonde School of Engineering, York University, Toronto, Ontario, Canada M3J 1P3 (email mack@yorku.ca). His research centres on human performance measurement and modelling -- above all Fitts's law -- together with interaction devices and techniques, text entry, mobile computing, accessible computing, touch-based interaction, eye tracking, gaming, and experimental methodology. He is the author of *Human-Computer Interaction: An Empirical Research Perspective* (2013, 2nd ed. 2024), sole-authored -- not, as the brief for this study first had it, co-authored with R. William Soukoreff, who is his most frequent paper collaborator but not the book's co-author. He was elected to the ACM SIGCHI Academy in 2015 and received the Canadian Human-Computer Communication Society (CHCCS) Achievement Award in the same year. His birth year is 1951 (Library of Congress cataloguing, "MacKenzie, I. Scott, 1951-"); no birth date or birthplace was established in any source read; do not assert them. The brief also described him as "professor emeritus"; that title was not verified and is contradicted by the live sources -- Lassonde currently lists him as Associate Professor, his home page shows no emeritus designation, and he has new peer-reviewed work in 2025 and 2026, with items in press dated 2027. [C38060]

**Relevance:** (1) MacKenzie helps investigate whether a quantitative law of aimed movement can become a generative rule: his Shannon formulation of Fitts's law turns every reach into a number of bits of difficulty, and his throughput and error taxonomy turn performance into measurable quantities that could parameterise a drawing rather than decorate it. (2) The evidence is the 1992 paper's Shannon formulation ID = log2(A/W + 1) with its worked stylus data (MT = 53 + 148 x ID ms, r = .992), the effective-width adjustment We = 4.133 x SDx that corrects a target's size by what the hand actually did, and the CHI 2003 unified error metric that splits every keystroke into correct, incorrect-not-fixed, incorrect-but-fixed, and fixes -- a complete apparatus for turning human movement into numbers a piece can be governed by. [C38061]

**Doorway:** The 463rd doorway. Takes up the open question from the Mark Tapia study: the grammar that generates ornament and the menu that hides itself never meet in the sources read -- so what happens when the rule being hidden IS the generative rule, and can a shape grammar be taught the way a marking menu is taught, by press-and-wait and by a traceable demonstration, rather than by being written down? MacKenzie answers from the measurement side, and his answer is that the teaching can be made measurable: the novice-to-expert transition has numbers -- movement time, throughput, learning curves -- so a grammar taught by waiting can be watched becoming fluent, sector by sector, as the marks get faster and the menu is needed less. The joining itself is this study's construction, not MacKenzie's: he never joins a grammar to a menu, and the menu is Kurtenbach and Buxton's, not his -- what he supplies is the frame that lets the joining be measured rather than asserted. Staged in MCK1, the menu's eight sectors are bound to eight generative drawing rules, so the rule the viewer learns by press-and-wait is exactly the rule that generates the picture, and each sector's label fades permanently once the viewer can draw its mark without waiting. The open question carried forward is the one MacKenzie's law cannot answer: the law measures how long a reach takes and how often it misses, but it treats every target as worth the same -- distance and width only, no account of why the hand goes, or what it does when it arrives. What happens when the movement being measured is not a reach toward a target but a gesture that means something -- when the hand's path is itself the message, and the question is not how fast it arrives but what it says on the way? [C38062]

## M1 -- The Fitts field: index of difficulty as a growth law

**Subject:** A field of targets -- discs, rings, or nodes -- scattered across the canvas, each one a place a moving point must reach.

**Visual principle:** Rhythm and density governed by a single invisible quantity. Where targets are far apart and small, the field feels tense, sparse, hard-won; where they are near and large, it feels loose and easy. The eye reads difficulty as spacing: difficulty IS the composition. Contrast between the fast, open reaches and the slow, cramped clusters.

**Computational mechanism:** The Shannon formulation, MacKenzie (1992), read end to end in its GI'92 companion: ID = log2(D/W + 1) bits, and MT = a + b x ID. A walker/pen moves from target to target; the time it spends travelling each leg is set by that leg's ID, with fitted constants a and b -- MacKenzie's 1992 stylus point-select data give MT = 53 + 148 x ID (ms), r = .992, over 16 A-W conditions, ID 1-7 bits, each row the mean of about 470 trials, grand mean MT 645 ms, error rate 3.6%. The walker's trail thickness, opacity, or growth rate is a function of ID: high-ID legs draw thin, hesitant, slow lines; low-ID legs draw bold, fast strokes. Target placement itself is generated under an ID budget: each new target is placed so its ID from the current position falls in a chosen band, so the whole field is generated by the law rather than decorated with it. The ID-budget placement rule and the ID-to-stroke mapping are this study's own formalisation, labelled as such; the law itself is MacKenzie's.

**Artistic opportunity:** A drawing whose line quality is literally a record of how hard each move was -- the picture is a performance trace, not an illustration of one. Two runs with different ID bands look and feel like different temperaments (cautious vs reckless), which is a genuinely new visual variable for the gallery: difficulty as texture.

**Takeaway:** The line is thin where the move was hard, bold where it was easy -- difficulty as texture. [C38064]

## M2 -- Throughput as density: one number that is speed and accuracy

**Subject:** A surface being filled -- stipple, hatching, or a growing colony -- whose fill rate is a measured throughput.

**Visual principle:** Density as performance. A high-throughput region fills quickly and cleanly; a low-throughput region fills slowly and raggedly. The boundary between a dense, confident patch and a sparse, faltering one is the visible trace of a speed-accuracy trade-off. Layering: newer, faster fill over older, slower fill.

**Computational mechanism:** Throughput TP = IDe/MT (bits/s), the ISO 9241-9 standardised measure MacKenzie's 2004 paper defends with seven recommendations (use the Shannon formulation; use a wide ID range of 2-8 bits; measure endpoint scatter; adjust for accuracy; regress MT on effective ID and check the intercept is small; predict only within the tested ID range; compare conditions with throughput calculated as the mean of per-sequence means). The standardisation is what makes the number usable: among ISO studies, mouse throughput falls in a narrow 3.7-4.9 bits/s across five models from four research groups, against 2.6-12.5 bits/s in the unstandardised literature; Fitts's own 1954 data, re-tabulated in the 2018 chapter, give mean throughput 10.10 bits/s with SD only 1.33 bits/s -- the empirical case that throughput is roughly independent of task difficulty. The piece runs an internal Fitts task: a simulated pointer repeatedly acquires targets; each trial yields an IDe and an MT, hence a TP. The fill process consumes TP as its resource: marks are deposited at a rate proportional to running TP, and mark quality (size regularity, alignment) degrades as the simulated pointer's error rate rises. A speed-accuracy bias parameter shifts the trade-off -- push speed and TP rises while scatter widens; push accuracy and TP falls while marks tighten. The piece can also measure the viewer: if the viewer taps targets, their real TP drives the fill. TP-as-fill-rate and the bias parameter's mapping to mark regularity are this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A picture that is a live speedometer of its own making -- the viewer watches a single number become a texture. It makes the abstract idea "throughput" visible as the difference between a confident wash and a nervous speckle, and it gives the gallery its first piece whose density is a measurement, not a style choice.

**Takeaway:** One number, speed and accuracy together, becomes the density of the fill. [C38065]

## M3 -- Effective width: the target you actually hit

**Subject:** A ring of targets surrounded by clouds of landing points -- the scatter of where the hand actually arrived, not where it aimed.

**Visual principle:** Occlusion and halo. Each nominal target (a crisp ring) is surrounded by a soft cloud of endpoints; the cloud, not the ring, is the real shape. Tension between the intended geometry (exact, circular, small) and the performed geometry (diffuse, lopsided, larger). Where scatter is tight the image is hard-edged; where it is wide the image dissolves into mist.

**Computational mechanism:** Effective width We = 4.133 x SDx, where SDx is the standard deviation of selection endpoints projected on the task axis (MacKenzie; ISO 9241-9). The adjustment normalises every condition to a nominal 4% error rate -- 96% of hits inside the target, bounded by z = +/-2.066. The worked example, repeated identically in 1992 and 2018: 2% errors on a 5 cm target give We = 2.066/2.326 x 5 = 4.45 cm; We is smaller than W when errors are below 4%, larger when they exceed 4%. Effective ID follows: IDe = log2(D/We + 1). The piece simulates (or records, from viewer taps) a set of endpoint samples per target, computes SDx, and draws the effective target -- a disc of diameter We -- instead of (or over) the nominal one. Over trials the cloud grows, the effective disc breathes, and targets whose endpoints are wild swell until they overlap and merge. The 4.133 factor (the width covering about 96% of a normal distribution) is fixed by the theory, not chosen. Rendering the effective disc as the primary form and letting overlapping effective discs merge is this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A portrait of human fallibility as a visual system: every target carries its own aura of error, and the aura is computed, not painted. The image is at once rigorous (the cloud IS the data) and painterly (scatter as stipple, mist, bloom). Nothing else in the gallery makes error the subject of the drawing itself.

**Takeaway:** The ring is the intention; the cloud is the truth -- and the cloud is the picture. [C38066]

## M4 -- Ballistic then corrective: the two-phase stroke

**Subject:** A single aimed stroke, repeated -- a pen racing toward a mark and then correcting onto it.

**Visual principle:** Tension between a fast, overshooting gesture and a small, careful repair. The primary stroke is long, sweeping, slightly wrong; the correction is short, tight, exact. Rhythm comes from the alternation: fling, settle, fling, settle. The overshoot leaves a visible hook or tail past each target.

**Computational mechanism:** The stochastic optimized-submovement model (Meyer et al. 1988; Woodworth's two-phase observation), NOT MacKenzie's own work -- included because it is the movement model inside the law MacKenzie codified. An aimed movement is a primary (ballistic, open-loop) submovement plus an optional secondary (corrective, closed-loop) submovement, programmed to minimise total time at a high hit rate; primary-submovement endpoint SD grows linearly with primary velocity (signal-dependent noise). The piece draws each move as two segments: a fast primary stroke aimed at the target with Gaussian endpoint error proportional to its speed, then -- only if the primary missed -- a slower corrective stroke to the target centre. The proportion of moves needing correction, and the size of the hooks, follow from the speed the piece chooses. The two-segment stroke rendering and the speed-to-miss-probability mapping are this study's own formalisation; the submovement model itself is Meyer et al.

**Artistic opportunity:** Handwriting-like energy with a law behind it -- every stroke visibly contains its own mistake and its own repair. It would read as calligraphy with a conscience. Held as a candidate because the mechanism is real and the visual (hook plus correction) is distinctive, but rejected as a standalone prompt: as a whole piece it is thin, and its best material is absorbed into MCK2 and MCK3.

**Takeaway:** Every stroke carries its own mistake and its own repair -- fling, then settle. [C38067]

## M5 -- The steering tunnel: speed is proportional to width

**Subject:** A winding channel, river, or corridor of varying width, with a traveller moving through it.

**Visual principle:** Flow governed by constriction. Wide reaches feel slow, calm, expansive; narrow necks feel fast, pressured, urgent -- the opposite of the naive reading (narrow = slow), which is exactly what makes it interesting. Layering of the channel walls against the traveller's trail; the trail's spacing (dots per unit length) records local speed.

**Computational mechanism:** The Accot-Zhai steering law: traversal time T = a + b times the integral of ds/W(s) along the path, with instantaneous form ds/dT = W(s)/b -- speed through a tunnel is proportional to local width (Accot and Zhai 1997, derived from Fitts's law; MacKenzie's lineage, not his own result -- flagged). The piece generates a curvilinear path with a width profile W(s) (wide pools, narrow gates), and a traveller moves along it at speed W(s)/b, leaving a trail whose dot spacing directly encodes W(s). Where the tunnel narrows the dots crowd; where it widens they stretch out. Total traversal time is the integral, so the piece's duration is computed from its own geometry. The trail-dot encoding and the width-profile generator are this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A river that obeys a law of the hand rather than a law of water -- the counter-intuitive speed rule (wider = faster) gives the piece a perceptual puzzle the eye keeps re-solving. The trail becomes a readable graph of the channel: width made visible as rhythm. No gallery piece is currently a tunnel whose geometry IS its clock.

**Takeaway:** Wider means faster -- the river runs on a law of the hand, and its trail is its own graph. [C38068]

## M6 -- Corrected / left behind: the two error rates

**Subject:** A line of text being typed, corrected, and left imperfect -- the visible residue of fixing and not fixing.

**Visual principle:** Two inks, two fates. Corrected errors leave a scar: a struck-through or overpainted mark that is still legible as having-been-wrong. Uncorrected errors stand clean and wrong, indistinguishable from intention until compared against the intended text. Contrast between the palimpsest (dense, worked, honest) and the clean error (smooth, confident, false). Density of scars records effort; density of clean errors records inattention.

**Computational mechanism:** Soukoreff and MacKenzie (CHI 2003), read end to end: the unified error metric. The pre-2003 problem was that a character-wise comparison of presented vs transcribed text can report six errors where two seem right, and the field's workarounds -- forcing error-free entry, or forcing synchrony with the presented text -- are unnatural and compromise external validity. The replacement is a four-class keystroke taxonomy -- Correct (C), Incorrect-Not-Fixed (INF), Incorrect-but-Fixed (IF), Fixes (F) -- from which total error rate = (INF + IF)/(C + INF + IF), splitting cleanly into not-corrected rate and corrected rate; in their worked example C=14, INF=2, IF=1, F=1 gives total 3/17 = 17.6%, not-corrected 11.8%, corrected 5.9%. The same taxonomy yields error-correction efficiency IF/F, participant conscientiousness IF/(IF+INF), utilised bandwidth, and wasted bandwidth -- statistics the authors explicitly frame as information transfer, with C as useful information and the rest as wasted bandwidth. Their demonstration experiment (12 volunteers, mean age 32.3, Sharp EL-6053 miniature QWERTY, 8 mm x 5 mm keys, 30 minutes per condition) found hiding the presented text raised speed from 25.3 to 27.1 wpm but raised the not-corrected error rate, while total error rate did not differ significantly -- the speed-accuracy trade-off made visible. The piece generates a target phrase (from the MacKenzie-Soukoreff 500-phrase set tradition: 500 phrases, 16-43 characters, mean 28.61, letter-frequency correlation 0.9541 with Mayzner and Tresselt), simulates a typist with a per-character error probability and a per-error correction probability, and renders three layers: intended characters (faint), keystroke stream (with backspaces shown as erasures that leave a mark), final transcribed text (with uncorrected errors highlighted). The three-layer rendering and the typist simulation parameters are this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece about the ethics of the draft -- what we fix vs what we let stand -- rendered as a measurable, generative texture. The scarred palimpsest is visually rich (overstrike, ghost letters, erasure bloom) and conceptually exact: every mark is accounted for by the error taxonomy. It is the gallery's first piece where proofreading is the medium.

**Takeaway:** The scar is honest and the clean error lies -- the draft wears what it cost. [C38069]

## M7 -- KSPC: the economy of keystrokes

**Subject:** A keyboard that rearranges itself -- characters ordered by frequency so the commonest letters cost the fewest moves.

**Visual principle:** Hierarchy by cost. Frequent characters sit large, near, cheap; rare characters sit small, far, expensive. The layout is a map of English itself. Symmetry is broken by frequency: the alphabet is no longer A-Z but a ranked terrain.

**Computational mechanism:** Keystrokes per character, KSPC (MacKenzie 2002): the average keystrokes to produce one character in a language with a given technique. QWERTY has KSPC = 1; multitap about 2.03. The CHI 2003 critique of KSPC, read end to end, is threefold: it conflates how many errors were made with how hard they were to fix; it is device-dependent (error-free QWERTY about 1.0 KSPC vs multitap 2.03 KSPC, so cross-method comparison is meaningless); and it trades off inversely with MSD -- not correcting an error drops KSPC from 1.13 to 1.0 while MSD rises from 0% to 6.25%. MacKenzie's three-key work reorders characters by frequency (FOCL: frequency-ordered character layout, snap-to-home cursor) and codes by Huffman base-4 (H4-Writer): the figures quoted for those layouts (three-key methods ranging 10.66 down to 4.23 keystrokes per character as the layout is linguistically reordered; H4-Writer KSPC = 2.321) come from secondary summaries consulted by leg 3, not from a paper read end to end in this study, and must be verified against the source paper before a build fixes them. The piece would generate a layout by sorting characters by digram frequency after each entry, and draw the text-entry path as a walk across the ranked terrain, with path length per character equal to its current KSPC cost. Rendering KSPC cost as terrain height is this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A keyboard as a landscape shaped by the language it types -- the most-used letters become valleys, the rare ones peaks. It makes the invisible economics of typing topographic. Rejected as a prompt: as a piece it risks reading as a diagram of a keyboard, and its visual payoff depends on the viewer reading text, which the gallery's wordless pieces avoid.

**Takeaway:** The alphabet as a terrain, priced by frequency -- but a diagram, not a piece. [C38070]

## M8 -- Press-and-wait: the menu that teaches its own grammar

**Subject:** A radial menu -- eight sectors around a point -- that appears only when you press and wait, and disappears the moment you know the gesture.

**Visual principle:** Self-revelation, guidance, rehearsal (Kurtenbach's three principles). The menu is a flower that blooms on waiting and folds away on knowing: novice mode shows the full pie, expert mode shows nothing but the ink trail of the mark itself. The visual arc of the piece is the fading of the menu -- from labelled sectors to bare strokes -- so learning is visible as disappearance. Symmetry of the pie against the asymmetry of the marks actually used: the sectors you never choose stay pale and unvisited.

**Computational mechanism:** Marking menus (Kurtenbach, Sellen and Buxton 1993; Kurtenbach thesis 1993) -- NOT MacKenzie's own work, and the study must not attribute them to him. Press-and-wait about 1/3 s pops up a radial (pie) menu; a stroke toward a sector selects it; an expert draws the same mark without waiting and the menu never appears -- the same physical action serves novice and expert, so guidance IS rehearsal of the expert gesture. Hierarchical menus use zig-zag marks. MacKenzie's contribution here is the measurement frame, not the menu: movement time, throughput, and learning curves quantify the novice-to-expert transition (marks about 3.5 times faster than menu selection in Kurtenbach and Buxton's case study, as reported in the Tapia study; MacKenzie's own longitudinal numbers give the same shape of curve -- OPTI rising from 17.0 wpm to 44.3 wpm over 20 sessions, passing QWERTY after session 10). The piece's generative rule, this study's own formalisation: each of the 8 directions is bound to a drawing rule (N branch, NE split, E lengthen, SE thicken, S seed, SW curl, W fade, NW mirror). A mark executes its rule on the growing form; a press-and-wait reveals which rule each direction holds. The form is therefore generated by the menu, and the menu is learned by using it -- the hidden rule IS the generative rule. The piece tracks each direction's usage and fades its label as its mark becomes fluent (measured: mark time approaching the expert asymptote, or simply repeated successful use without waiting).

**Artistic opportunity:** This is the doorway piece, and the only candidate that answers the #842 question literally: the grammar is not written down anywhere in the piece -- it is demonstrated, by waiting, and then it vanishes into the hand. The resulting image is a record of which gestures the viewer learned, a drawing that could not exist without the teaching. It is also the gallery's first piece where interaction is pedagogy rather than control: you do not press a button to change the picture, you learn a gesture and the picture learns you back.

**Takeaway:** The lesson is the control, and the rule you learn is the rule that draws. [C38071]

## M9 -- The learning curve: practice as a sharpening

**Subject:** The same task, repeated over sessions -- a form drawn again and again, getting faster and cleaner each time.

**Visual principle:** Convergence. Early repetitions are loose, slow, error-prone -- wide scatter, heavy correction, hesitant lines. Late repetitions are tight, fast, economical -- the same form drawn in fewer, surer strokes. Superimposed repetitions produce a gradient from fog (early) to edge (late): the history of learning as a visible depth of field.

**Computational mechanism:** The power law of practice (learning curve): movement time and error rate fall as a power function of trial/session number. MacKenzie's longitudinal studies supply the numbers that were read end to end or in part in this study: OPTI rising from 17.0 wpm to 44.3 wpm over 20 sessions (with a power-law extrapolation of 60.7 wpm vs 44.8 wpm for QWERTY), and LetterWise ending 36% faster than Multi-tap over 20 sessions with keystrokes cut by close to 50%. The Unipad and scanning-keyboard figures quoted by leg 3 (Unipad 11.6 wpm after 2 hours, experts 17-35 wpm; scanning keyboard 3.01 to 4.51 wpm over 5 sessions as the scan interval tightened 900 to 500 ms) come from secondary summaries, not from papers read end to end here, and must be verified before a build fixes them. The piece simulates a learner: each session's MT and error rate are drawn from a fitted power law, and the drawn form for that session is parameterised by them (scatter proportional to error rate, stroke count proportional to MT). Sessions accumulate as translucent layers. Mapping session number to stroke parameters via a fitted power law is this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** A time-lapse of skill -- the rare piece whose subject is improvement itself, rendered as fog resolving into line. Rejected as a prompt: it needs longitudinal time to be experienced honestly, and a single sitting can only fake it with a simulation, which weakens the claim. The honest version of this idea is MCK1, where learning happens in real time inside one session (fluency fade), not as a compressed simulation of weeks.

**Takeaway:** Fog resolving into line -- but a sitting cannot honestly hold weeks of practice. [C38072]

## M10 -- MSD alignment: the distance between intention and result

**Subject:** Two strings -- what was meant, what was typed -- and the minimal edits that turn one into the other.

**Visual principle:** Alignment as braiding. Presented and transcribed texts run as two parallel lines; insertions, deletions, and substitutions are drawn as stitches crossing between them -- the optimal alignment (the minimal set of edit primitives) is the pattern of the braid. Where the strings agree the lines run straight and parallel; where they differ the braid knots.

**Computational mechanism:** Minimum string distance (Levenshtein), as applied by Soukoreff and MacKenzie (CHI 2001): MSD is the minimum number of insertions, deletions, substitutions transforming presented text into transcribed text; MSD error rate = MSD / max(|P|, |T|), so the rate is capped at 100% and over-typing is penalised. The 2001 solution exists because the pre-2003 problem was that a character-wise comparison can report six errors where two seem right. The piece takes a presented phrase and a (simulated or viewer-typed) transcription, computes MSD and one optimal alignment, and renders the alignment as the braid: matches as straight connectors, substitutions as crossed stitches, insertions/deletions as loops out and back. MSD itself sets the knot count -- a single number, visible as tangling. The braid rendering of an optimal alignment is this study's own formalisation.

**Artistic opportunity:** Edit distance made tactile -- the abstract notion "these two texts are 2 edits apart" becomes a visible knot count. Rejected as a prompt: it is text-bound and diagram-like; its beauty depends on the viewer reading both strings, and the braid, once understood, has one visual idea that does not develop. The error taxonomy survives in its stronger form as MCK5, where the fates of errors (corrected vs left) rather than their count carry the visual and conceptual weight.

**Takeaway:** The knot count is the distance between what was meant and what was typed. [C38073]

## Mechanism note -- what is MacKenzie's and what is not

Three of the ten mechanisms are not MacKenzie's own work, and the study must keep the attribution clean. The marking menu (M8) is Kurtenbach, Sellen and Buxton (1993); MacKenzie's role is the measurement frame -- movement time, throughput, learning curves -- that quantifies the novice-to-expert transition the menu produces. The steering law (M5) is Accot and Zhai (1997), derived from Fitts's law; it belongs here because it is the trajectory form of the law MacKenzie codified, and because the doorway question's menu is itself a steering task (navigating a pie). The submovement model (M4) is Meyer et al. (1988), building on Woodworth; it belongs because it is the movement model inside the law. What is MacKenzie's, verified from papers read end to end in this study: the Shannon formulation of the index of difficulty and its defence against the Fitts and Welford forms; the effective-width adjustment and its worked example; the throughput definition and the ISO 9241-9 standardisation programme with its seven recommendations; the text-entry prediction model (Fitts plus Hick-Hyman plus digram frequencies plus repeat-key time); the MSD/KSPC critique and the unified total error rate with its four-class taxonomy; the 500-phrase evaluation set. The fusions -- ID-budget placement, TP-as-fill-rate, the effective disc as primary form, menu sectors bound to generative rules, the fluency fade, the three-layer error rendering, the braid -- are this study's own formalisations, and are labelled as such where they appear. No prompt in this study is built from a mechanism whose attribution is misstated. [C38074]

## The arc: from fixing the formula, to fixing the measurement, to fixing the field

MacKenzie's arc is a single question asked at widening scales, and the question is always about measurement. In the first phase (1989-1992) the question is the formula: Fitts (1954) deviated unnecessarily from Shannon's Theorem 17, so the index of difficulty should be ID = log2(A/W + 1), with W replaced by W-prime or the smaller of W and H in two dimensions, and with the effective width correcting for what the hand actually did -- and the worked example (deleting a Macintosh file icon 14 cm from a 2 cm trashcan: 882 ms by drag-select, 728 ms by point-select, 528 ms by stroke-through, 40% faster) turns the model into a design verdict. In the second phase (1995-2003) the question is the measurement of text: a prediction model built from Fitts plus Hick-Hyman plus digram frequencies (space the most frequent character at .187, e-space the most frequent digram at .0457), then MSD and KSPC, then the unified total error rate and the 500-phrase standard -- each step replacing a workaround (forcing error-free entry, forcing synchrony) with a measurement that lets the error stand. In the third phase (2004) the question is the field's comparability: seven recommendations plus ISO 9241-9 collapse mouse throughput from a wild 2.6-12.5 bits/s band to 3.7-4.9 bits/s, and the correct unit of analysis (one uninterrupted sequence of trials for one participant, not the ratio of grand means -- 8.97 vs 10.10 bits/s on Fitts's own data) is stated as a rule, not a preference. In the late phase (2015-2026) the same apparatus is applied to new bodies and displays: touch at 6.85 bits/s on a Nexus 4 (1D 7.43 vs 2D 6.27), gaze and head pointing at about 2 bits/s in a head-mounted display, one-key text entry at about 5 wpm, and the recent turn to mixed reality and adaptation models (QMAP, 2025). Three constants run through all four phases. First, the law is the object and the image is its residue: the field is what the ID budget leaves behind, the fill is what throughput deposits, the cloud is what the endpoints scatter. Second, error is always data, never noise to be hidden: the effective width corrects by it, the error taxonomy counts it, the standardisation normalises by it. Third, the system corrects itself in public: the OPTI prediction is downgraded from 58.2 to 42.16 wpm when the denominator error is found (and the observed 44 wpm then exceeds the corrected prediction), and the Shannon formulation is defended in 2013 against the charge of invalidity on the grounds that validity is the wrong test for a model by analogy and only utility counts. ## The aesthetic logic

MacKenzie's aesthetic is the measured mark on a plain ground. The beauty is in a number you can watch becoming a picture: a line thinning as its leg's ID rises, a disc swelling as its scatter widens, a label fading as its gesture becomes fluent, a scar marking where a fix was made. Nothing is hidden in a preference panel or a confidence score; the system is on the surface, where the viewer can see it and act on it, or it is deliberately absent, and the absence is the system -- the menu that never appears because the hand already knows. The palette is restraint itself -- ink on paper white, one accent for difficulty, one wash for error -- because the mark must be readable as a measurement's state, and decoration would break the reading. Slowness is not a mood here; it is the price of the law, paid in the milliseconds of a hard leg, the sessions before a layout passes its rival, the third of a second before a menu appears -- and the honest piece shows the price rather than hiding it.
