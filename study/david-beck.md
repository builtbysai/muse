# David Beck: Study Notes

**Site:** David Beck (25 May 1621, Delft -- 20 December 1656, The Hague), Dutch Golden Age portrait painter; son of a Delft schoolmaster, pupil of Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt, then in Van Dyck's London studio in the last year of Van Dyck's life, drawing teacher to the young Stuart princes, and from 1647 portrait painter and valet de chambre to Queen Christina of Sweden in Stockholm -- her travelling portraitist, sent from court to court to paint the sovereigns of Europe for her gallery while his own queen's likeness travelled out as a present in return. He rejoined her in Rome in 1655, followed her to France in 1656, and died at The Hague at 35. His visible oeuvre is seven Stockholm years: every securely located painting still sits in a Swedish collection, and the travelling output survives only as engravings and copies.

## The life

David Beck was born in Delft on 25 May 1621, the son of a schoolmaster, and was named after his paternal uncle, a poet who died in Arnhem in Gelderland. The uncle's name is not given in any source read, and neither is his father's. He learned painting in Delft from Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt (1566/67-1641), the prominent portrait painter of the Netherlands, court portraitist of the Princes of Orange, whose workshop turned out portraits by the hundred. No source read gives a date for the start or end of the apprenticeship, and no Delft guild registration for Beck was found. Mierevelt himself is already studied (#527); the doorway back to the teacher is closed.

Late in 1640, aged nineteen, Beck moved to London to join Anthony van Dyck's studio as pupil and assistant. He had little time to learn from Van Dyck himself: Van Dyck fell ill and died on 9 December 1641, so the overlap was at most about twelve months, much of it with a dying master. The study phrases it as "joined Van Dyck's London studio late in 1640, months before Van Dyck's death", not as a formed pupilage (see C1205). What London gave him instead was the Stuart court. Charles I, observing the expeditious manner of Beck's painting, exclaimed, "Faith! Beck, I believe you could paint riding post" -- Houbraken's Dutch version has it as "paint on horseback" -- and asked him to teach drawing to his sons: Charles II, James Duke of York, and Henry Stuart Duke of Gloucester, with Prince Rupert of the Palatinate added to the list by Houbraken alone. The speed in the remark is the whole of Beck's technical reputation: Britannica 1911 calls it "freedom of hand and readiness, or rather rapidity of execution, for which Van Dyck was so remarkable", and Houbraken calls him "so exceptionally facile in painting". No source read discusses palette, pigments, ground, underdrawing, or studio practice in technical terms, and there is effectively no modern technical literature on Beck findable by web search; the study does not invent one. He left England after the outbreak of the English Civil War that led to Charles I's execution. No departure date is given anywhere.

Houbraken then says he "entered the service of the King of France, also of Denmark, and finally of Queen Christina of Sweden". No dates, no king named, no works are given for the French or Danish service in any source read, and Wikipedia omits the episode entirely. It is Houbraken-only, and the study does not date it or assert it as fact beyond Houbraken's claim (see C1202). The 1641-47 gap -- leaving England to the 1647 Stockholm appointment -- has no dated itinerary in any source read.

In 1647 he was appointed portrait painter and valet de chambre to Queen Christina of Sweden in Stockholm. Houbraken's title differs: Christina "loved him above all others, gave him various presents and made him her First Chamberlain" (see C1201). The appointment is the hinge of the career, and the commission that came with it is the mechanism that matters: from 1647 Christina sent him from court to court to paint notabilities, "apparently also for political purposes", and Houbraken gives the exchange logic in full -- Beck visited "Italy, Spain, France, England, Denmark and all the courts of Germany to portray all the potentates and famous persons for her, whom, wanting the glory of being famous, she then presented with her portrait as painted by our DAVID BECK". Gerson Digital section 1.5 independently confirms the German leg in the same words. Joost van den Vondel's verse on the exchange is quoted by Houbraken: "DAVID BECK recreates the rulers of Europe, / With his art at the charge of the Queen, / And honours Christina for them with her immortal portrait. / Thus Gustav's blood is borne from court to court." His friends witnessed that through this profitable opportunity he had received nine golden chains and medallions as gifts from kings and rulers, "amongst these one from his queen". The chains are the material trace of the tours, and no named German-court sitter from them was located in sixty-three search passes (see C1207).

In Rome in 1653 he was initiated into the Bentvueghels, the Dutch painters' confraternity, on 7 December 1653, taking the bent-name "Golden Sceptre" -- signed by sixty hands, "Long live the Golden Sceptre", the initiation verse naming him as the man "Who often portrayed the Queen of Sweden, / With your famous brush, so valued by the crown". Houbraken adds, in his own voice, that he would rather call the Bentvueghels "snatchers because he himself was the Bentvueghel that they fleeced" -- Beck paid handsomely for the initiation meal. Houbraken's sole physical description: "He was a handsome and well-built person but not stout."

He rejoined Christina's court in Rome in 1655, after her abdication of 6 June 1654, and followed her to France in 1656. The two Roman stays must not be collapsed -- 1653 on the travelling circuit, 1655 with the queen after her abdication -- and no source read spells out the 1653-55 movements (see C1200). He asked leave to visit friends and family in the Netherlands while Christina planned a pleasure journey to France; Houbraken says she suspected he meant never to return, and "This also happened, for on the 20th of December 1656 he came to die in The Hague (not without suspicion of having imbibed poison)". The study says "died at The Hague aged 35, cause unrecorded": the poisoning is Houbraken's parenthetical suspicion, repeated by Britannica 1911 and Wikipedia and amplified by low-authority sites into mystery, with no evidence, no suspect, and no modern reassessment anywhere (see C1198). The other late anecdote is the "died twice" story: travelling through Germany he fell suddenly and violently ill, was laid out as dead, and was revived when a servant poured wine into his mouth. Houbraken himself flags doubt -- "I would not so easily accept this rare incident for truth, had not something of the kind happened in my family line" -- and the study presents it as his anecdote, not as fact (see C1206).

His successor, in Houbraken's telling, was the London miniaturist Alexander Cooper, "taken for the best portrait painter in water colours" of his time, who "came to the court of Sweden and then into the service of Queen Christina" after Beck's death. The succession is chronologically tangled -- Cooper is also said to have travelled to Sweden in 1646, before Beck's 1647 appointment -- and the two men most likely overlapped with different functions, miniature against oil portraiture, rather than succeeding each other cleanly (see C1204). No named pupil of Beck's, no workshop, no letters, no birth or baptism record, no Delft guild record, no Stockholm appointment document was found. All biographical detail traces to Houbraken (1718), sixty-two years after Beck's death, plus nineteenth-century encyclopaedias and Steneberg's single portrait reading.

## The evolution: seven Stockholm years, and silence on either side

No painting from Beck's Delft years or his London years was identified in any pass. The London period survives only as a role: drawing teacher to the Stuart princes, favourite of Charles I. No Beck portrait of Charles II as a young prince was found -- the 1641 child portraits of the Stuart princes are Van Dyck's, not Beck's. This is a genuine gap, not a search failure: Beck was nineteen to twenty-six in London, Van Dyck died in 1641, and the Civil War scattered the court. The catalogue therefore opens in Stockholm, and the study does not invent an early work.

The Stockholm years (1647-53, W01-W12) are the whole surviving oeuvre, and every securely located Beck painting still sits in a Swedish collection: Nationalmuseum (W01 the copy, W02, W07, W08, W09), Livrustkammaren (W03, W04, W10), Skokloster (W05, W06), Lacko Castle (W11), the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (W12). The sitters are the queen herself in five versions (W01-W05), her heir (W08), her statesmen (W06, W07, W10), her commanders (W09, W17), her family (W11), and her famous guest (W12). Format: the evidence points to bust and half-length court portraits as the working norm, with the 1650 Christina (W01, 114 x 94 cm, three-quarter length with allegorical apparatus -- veil, fountain, globe) as the large-format state portrait, and one variant in oil on copper (W03) -- small, durable, portable, the right support for a likeness meant to travel. No palette claim can be evidenced from the sources read: no colour description, no technical examination, and no painting was inspected in person. The study does not invent one.

The travelling years (1647-53, concurrent with Stockholm) change the function, not the pictorial style. The court tours and the Stockholm sittings overlap -- Beck was sent out from Stockholm and returned. The product was a gallery: portraits of sovereigns and notabilities "to adorn her gallery", painted at foreign courts and shipped back, with Christina's portrait travelling out as the return gift. What survives of this output is second-hand: Jeremias Falck's engravings after Beck (W13-W18, Stockholm 1649-54), copies after Beck (W01), and Houbraken's account. The evolution here is in function: from single court portraits to a diplomatic portrait economy in which the picture is a token of exchange.

Rome and France (1653-56) leave nothing. Beck was in Rome in 1653, rejoined Christina in Rome in 1655, followed her to France in 1656, and died at The Hague on 20 December 1656 -- and no painting dated to Rome or France was identified in any pass: no Roman portrait, no French court portrait, no late self-portrait. The late work is a blank in the record, and the study does not fill it with an invented late style.

The summary arc: pupil and teacher in London (1640-47, no surviving picture) -- court portraitist in Stockholm (1647-53, the whole visible oeuvre, all still in Sweden) -- travelling portraitist whose pictures became diplomatic objects (the output surviving only as engravings and copies) -- silence in Rome and France (1653-56, no work). The arc is not a stylistic development toward looseness or darkness (no evidence for either); it is a change in commission and function -- from likeness, to gallery, to exchange.

## M1 -- The travelling portrait: paint the ruler, leave the queen

Beck's first mechanism is the exchange Houbraken spells out and Gerson confirms: Christina sent him to the courts of Europe to paint the potentates, and to each potentate she presented her own portrait as painted by Beck. The portrait is the currency in both directions -- his likeness of the ruler travels back to Stockholm to fill her gallery, her likeness by him travels out as the present that buys the sitting. The nine golden chains and medallions are the receipts. The mechanism generalises beyond Stockholm: the same court-to-court system runs in parallel for Toussaint Gelton under Christian V of Denmark, for Anselm van Hulle at the Peace of Munster, for the Vaillant brothers at the Frankfurt coronation -- but Beck's is the purest case, because the exchange is stated as the system, not inferred from it. The study carries the gap openly: no individual German-court sitter portrait by Beck was located, so the mechanism is documented as a system, not illustrated by a named sitting (see C1207). No documented personal interaction between Beck and Danckerts was found either -- the two Dutchmen overlapped in Stockholm 1648-53, one painting the gallery by travel, the other by residence, and the link is institutional, not personal.

**Takeaway:** build the exchange, not the portrait -- one painter arriving at a court, painting the ruler, and leaving the queen's likeness behind in return, the two portraits crossing in the doorway, and the gallery at home filling one sovereign at a time.

## M2 -- Speed as a court skill: paint riding post

Beck's reputation, in every source from Houbraken to Britannica 1911, rests on one technical characterisation: "freedom of hand and readiness, or rather rapidity of execution". Charles I's remark -- "Faith! Beck, I believe you could paint riding post" -- makes speed itself the court skill: the painter who can keep up with a king on horseback, the portrait finished before the sitter's patience runs out. Houbraken calls him "so exceptionally facile in painting", Chalmers "extremely free and quick". The mechanism is speed as social currency: in a court where the sitter is a sovereign, the painter's value is partly that he does not waste the sovereign's time, and the speed is what the king notices and names. The trap travels with it: the famous speed feats in Houbraken pp. 80-83 -- three life-sized half-figure portraits in one summery day, six-to-seven-foot canvases, thumb-and-finger painting -- belong to Jan Weenix, whose life precedes Beck's on the same pages, and search snippets misattribute them to Beck. The study credits Beck only with the rapidity the sources actually give him (see C1197).

**Takeaway:** paint fast, and let the speed show -- one portrait made at the pace of a king on horseback, the brush keeping up with the sitter, and the quickness itself as the thing the court admires.

## M3 -- The gallery as a system: portraits to adorn her gallery

Beck "executed portraits of most of the sovereigns of Europe to adorn her gallery" -- the gallery is the work, not any single portrait. The mechanism is the collection as a system: a roomful of rulers, painted to one commission, to one purpose, each face a sample from a diplomatic programme rather than a standalone likeness. The Stockholm sittings feed it from one side (the queen, her heir, her statesmen, her commanders, her family, her guest -- W01-W12), the court tours from the other (the potentates, unlocated, known only through Falck's engravings W13-W18). The gallery is also what makes the losses legible: the travelling portraits are lost or unidentified, but the system that produced them is fully documented, so the absence of individual pictures does not dissolve the mechanism. Compare Danckerts's twenty-two octagonal plates (#583, M3): the same logic -- the set is the history -- executed in oil on tin for a dynasty instead of in travelling portraits for a gallery of sovereigns.

**Takeaway:** build the gallery, not the portrait -- a wall filling one sovereign at a time, each face painted to the same commission, and the room itself as the picture, the single portrait only a sample.

## M4 -- The copy is the portrait: the famous Christina survives as a copy

The most famous Beck image is not a Beck. The 1650 Queen Christina -- the coronation portrait, the one Steneberg built his four-elements iconology on (the breeze catching the veil as air, the barely visible fountain as water, the globe under her right hand as earth, the queen herself as fire) -- survives in the Nationalmuseum impression NMGrh 406 at Gripsholm, oil on canvas, 114 x 94 cm, catalogued "After David Beck" / "Kopia". The original Beck painting of 1650 is lost or unlocated. The mechanism is the copy as the portrait's afterlife: the iconological reading, the reproductions, the fame, all rest on a copy, and the copy is what Europe actually saw. A second Christina in the Nationalmuseum (17258) is attributed to Beck himself, and a third on copper (W03) is listed by Meisterdrucke and Bridgeman -- but the famous one, the one the scholarship is about, is the copy. The study does not call NMGrh 406 an autograph Beck, and notes that Steneberg's reading rests on it (see C1203).

**Takeaway:** show the copy where the original should be -- the famous portrait that is itself a copy, the scholarship built on the second hand, and let the copy be the picture rather than apologising for it.

## M5 -- The portrait that travels out as a present: copper, mourning, and Wrangel

The return leg of the exchange has its clearest documented case at Skokloster. The earliest of Skokloster's three Kristina portraits is by Beck, early 1650s, showing her in mourning clothes -- the museum dates it to soon after her mother Maria Eleonora died in 1655, a dating that sits in tension with Beck leaving Stockholm in 1653, and the study carries the tension rather than resolving it -- and it was given by Kristina to Carl Gustaf Wrangel. The mechanism is the present-portrait: the queen's likeness as a gift object, travelling out to a magnate while other sovereigns' likenesses travel in. The oil-on-copper Christina (W03) is the same mechanism in its material form: copper is small, durable, portable, the right support for a likeness meant to be carried from court to court and presented, where a large canvas stays home. The portrait that travels is a different object from the portrait that hangs: smaller, harder, made to survive the road.

**Takeaway:** let the portrait travel as a gift -- one small copper likeness, carried from court to court and presented to a magnate, the queen's face as the present, and the road as part of the picture.

## M6 -- The engraving is the record: Falck after Beck

The travelling output survives only in print. Jeremias Falck, working in Stockholm 1649-54, engraved after Beck's paintings -- Carl Gustav as Pfalzgraf (1649, Blocks 216/217), Magnus Gabriel De la Gardie (1649, Block 240, verified at the Fitzwilliam), two Christina sheets (1649, Blocks 222/223), the 1650-51 officer series (Torstensson, Oxenstierna, Douglas, Hammerstein, Horn, Konigsmarck, Lillie, Wittenberg), Oxenstierna (1652, Block 272), Karl X Gustav as king (1654, Block 218, "large-format splendid portrait... wearing a breastplate, with an ermine cloak in oval laurel wreaths wound around with oak branches", signed with the single word "Falck" because his post as court engraver had not been renewed by Karl). The paintings themselves are unlocated -- presumably dispersed with Christina's gallery, lost in the Tre Kronor fire of 1697, or still unidentified in a European collection -- and the engravings are the evidence that the travelling-portrait system existed and what it looked like. The 1654 sheet marks the end of the sequence: Christina abdicated in June 1654, Falck lost his post and returned to Danzig. Compare Danckerts's Hondius engraving (#583, M5): the same mechanism -- the painting is the matrix, the copper is the record -- but for Beck the engravings are not the afterlife of a few lost pictures, they are the only record of the whole travelling half of the oeuvre.

**Takeaway:** show the engraving where the painting should be -- the court portrait that survives only in copper lines, the gallery of sovereigns as a set of prints, and the print as the record of a system whose paintings are gone.

## M7 -- The seven-year oeuvre: no early work, no late work

Beck's visible career is seven Stockholm years, 1647-53, and the study states the boundary as a fact rather than filling it. No Delft painting, no London painting, no Rome painting, no France painting was identified in any pass. The oeuvre begins when the appointment begins and ends when he leaves Stockholm, and the silence on either side is part of the shape: a painter whose whole surviving body of work is one commission, in one place, for one queen. The mechanism is the commission as the oeuvre: the appointment is not a phase of the career, it is the career, and everything before it is biography without pictures, everything after it is biography without pictures. The contrast with Danckerts is the point of the doorway: Danckerts's career is a long arc from burgher pendants to dynastic series to an eleven-year gap, while Beck's is a single bright run and then nothing -- the travelling portraitist whose travelling pictures are the ones that did not survive.

**Takeaway:** build the seven years, and leave the rest blank -- one commission, one court, one queen, the pictures filling a single room, and the years before and after left empty rather than invented.

## M8 -- The golden chains: payment in honour

Nine golden chains and medallions from kings and rulers, "amongst these one from his queen" -- Beck's payment for the tours was partly in honour, not in money, and the chains are the mechanism's material form. A chain is a portrait's receipt: each sovereign who sat for him paid with a piece of gold worn around the painter's neck, so the painter carried his gallery with him in metal, sovereign by sovereign, the way the gallery at home carried it in paint. The self-portrait engraving (W20) shows the same logic in image: Beck at work with a Christina portrait on the easel, a rolled paper in one hand and a medal in the other -- the travelling portraitist as a man wearing his commissions. The mechanism is honour as currency: in a diplomatic portrait economy the painter is paid in the same coin as the portraits themselves, recognition, and the recognition is wearable, countable, nine chains deep.

**Takeaway:** count the chains -- one golden chain for each sovereign painted, worn around the painter's neck, the gallery carried in metal as well as in paint, and the medal in the sitter's hand as the price of the sitting.

## The aesthetic logic

Beck's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the logic of a man who painted a gallery rather than a person. Paint the exchange, not the face: the portrait is a token that travels in both directions -- the ruler's likeness home to Stockholm, the queen's likeness out as a present -- and the sitting is a diplomatic act before it is a pictorial one. Paint fast: rapidity of execution is the court skill, the thing the king names, because a sovereign's time is the scarcest material in the room. Paint the set, not the picture: the sovereigns of Europe to adorn her gallery, each face a sample from a programme, the room as the work. And let the second medium carry the first: the copper engraving is the record of the travelling half of the oeuvre, the copy is the famous portrait, and the original is lost without the system collapsing -- the mechanism survives its pictures.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. Beck was a schoolmaster's son from Delft who became the favourite of a queen by being quick, charming, and willing to travel -- Houbraken stresses his "excellent brush and lifestyle" making him "admired by most of the great of Europe", and the nine golden chains are the receipts of a man who was paid in honour. He never becomes a Van Dyck, his nominal master, and the contact was twelve months with a dying man; he never becomes a Rembrandt, his exact contemporary, and no source connects them. He remains a court portraitist working in a Flemish-inflected representative mode inside a queen's travelling gallery, and the thinness of his surviving oeuvre -- one verified dimension, one famous copy, a set of engravings after lost paintings -- is the price of that position: the pictures were made to travel, and travelling pictures get lost. The speed that made him famous is also what the record keeps: a remark from a king, a verse from a poet, nine chains, and a gallery that survives mostly in print.

## The network

Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt (1566/67-1641): teacher, Delft; court portraitist of the Princes of Orange; already studied #527 -- do not propose. Sebastien Bourdon (1616-1671): French, first court painter from 1652, the title neither Dutchman held; top pick for #585. Alexander Cooper (bapt. 1609-early 1660): miniaturist, Houbraken's successor after Beck's death; the succession is tangled (see C1204). Joachim von Sandrart (1606-1688): the historiographer on the next page of Houbraken, author of the Teutsche Academie; held for a future top-pick slot. David Klocker Ehrenstrahl (1628-1698): the next Swedish court painter, arrived the year Bourdon left. Bartholomeus van der Helst: painted Beck's portrait (Houbraken Plate C.1). Jeremias Falck (c. 1610-1677): the engraver who carried Beck's travelling portraits into print, Stockholm 1649-54. Karl Erik Steneberg (1903-1960): Kristinatidens maleri (1955), the four-elements reading of the 1650 Christina -- built on a copy. Arnold Houbraken (1660-1719): the biographer, sole origin of most facts. Modern scholarship: none found beyond Steneberg; the whole modern narrative rests on Houbraken plus nineteenth-century encyclopaedias.

The three life-sized half-figure portraits in one summery day, the six-to-seven-foot canvases, the thumb-and-finger painting after Cornelis Ketel, the miniature painting "which he always cursed", and the "pissing boy" merry company all belong to Weenix. Beck's life begins mid-p. 83 ("DAVID BECK, bent-named the Golden Sceptre"). Search snippets that quote those lines under a "David Beck" heading are misattributed by the search engine, not by Houbraken. Do not credit Beck with Weenix's feats. (Legs 1 and 2 both caught this independently; leg 3's flag numbering collided -- see the Depth note.)
C1198 -- Death: died at The Hague, 20 December 1656, aged 35, cause unrecorded. The poisoning is Houbraken's parenthetical only -- "(not without suspicion of having imbibed poison)" -- repeated by Britannica 1911, Wikipedia and Chalmers, and amplified by ArtsDot/WahooArt into mystery. No evidence, no suspect, no modern reassessment. Say "died at The Hague aged 35, cause unrecorded"; mention the suspicion only as Houbraken's parenthetical, attributed, never asserted. The #583 flag is confirmed.
C1199 -- Dates: use 25 May 1621 -- 20 December 1656 (Houbraken, Wikipedia, Wikidata, Britannica). Prabook gives "March 25, 1621 -- December 6, 1656" on both dates and is wrong on both; do not cite it. The Jan Sysmus register (Oud Holland 1890) gives "obiit 1657, Rotterdami (de oudste bronnen zeggen den Haag)" -- death 1657 in Rotterdam, with a parenthetical noting the oldest sources say The Hague; treat as an erroneous outlier register entry.
C1200 -- Two Roman stays: Rome 1653 (Bentvueghels initiation, 7 December 1653, bent-name "Golden Sceptre") and Rome 1655 (rejoining Christina after her abdication). Both can be true, but no source read spells out the 1653-55 movements. Say "in Rome in 1653; rejoined Christina in Rome in 1655" and do not imply a continuous Roman residence. No work is dated to either Roman stay.
C1201 -- Title: "First Chamberlain" (Houbraken: Christina "made him her First Chamberlain") vs "portrait painter and valet de chambre" (Wikipedia, Britannica-derived texts). These may be the same office rendered differently -- Chalmers has "first gentleman of her bedchamber" -- but the study notes the two wordings and does not present either as a quotation of the appointment document, which was not seen.
C1202 -- France and Denmark service: Houbraken (p. 83) says Beck "entered the service of the King of France, also of Denmark, and finally of Queen Christina of Sweden". No dates, no king named, no works, in any source read; Wikipedia omits the episode. Houbraken-only; do not date it or assert it beyond Houbraken's claim.
C1203 -- The famous 1650 Christina is a copy: Nationalmuseum NMGrh 406 (Gripsholm, oil on canvas, 114 x 94 cm, 1650) is catalogued "After David Beck" / "Kopia". Steneberg's four-elements iconology was built on this image. Do not call NMGrh 406 an autograph Beck. Whether an autograph 1650 original survives, and where, was not established in this study. The second Nationalmuseum Christina (17258) is attributed to Beck himself; its object page was not read end to end, and Wikidata entity Q20355592's relationship to the two pictures is unresolved -- do not merge them.
C1204 -- Cooper succession: Houbraken says Cooper came to Christina's court "after the death of David Beck"; Wikipedia says he "succeeded David Beck as official court painter when Beck returned to The Hague" but also that he travelled to Sweden in 1646, before Beck's 1647 appointment, and served as her miniature painter until her abdication in 1654. Both cannot be literally true as a clean succession. Likely Cooper (miniature/watercolour) and Beck (oil portraiture) overlapped with different functions. Do not write a simple "Cooper succeeded Beck" sentence without the overlap caveat.
C1205 -- "Van Dyck's pupil" overstates the contact: Beck arrived late 1640; Van Dyck was ill and died 9 December 1641. At most about twelve months' overlap, much of it with a dying master. Wikipedia itself stresses he "had little time to learn from Van Dyck himself". Phrase it as "joined Van Dyck's London studio late in 1640, months before Van Dyck's death".
C1206 -- "Died twice" anecdote: travelling through Germany Beck fell suddenly ill, was laid out as dead, and was revived when a servant poured wine into his mouth. Houbraken introduces it as "it was told that he died twice" and says "I would not so easily accept this rare incident for truth" but for a family parallel. Chalmers and Wikipedia repeat it straight. Present as anecdote with Houbraken's reservation, not as fact. The German town and date are not given in any source read.
C1207 -- Court-tour sovereigns: the central gap. Gerson section 1.5 and the Christina article both state Christina sent Beck to the courts of Germany and beyond to paint potentates, sending her own portrait as a present. No individual German-court sitter portrait by Beck was located in sixty-three search passes -- no named German prince, no surviving canvas in a German collection. The travelling output survives only as the Falck engravings of Stockholm sitters (W13-W18) and as copies. Treat the tour portraits as lost or unidentified, not as a list of works.
C1208 -- No early work, no late work: no Delft painting and no London painting identified; no painting dated to Rome (1653/1655) or France (1656) identified. The visible oeuvre is seven Stockholm years, all still in Swedish collections. Do not invent an early style, a late style, or a palette: no colour description, no technical examination, and no painting inspected in person.
