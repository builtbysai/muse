# Louise Breslau: Study Notes

**Site:** Louise Catherine Breslau (1856-1927), German-born Swiss painter, pastellist and portraitist -- born Maria Luise Katharina Breslau on Saturday 6 December 1856 at Munich, in the Kingdom of Bavaria, the daughter of a professor and head physician of obstetrics and gynaecology; in 1858, when she was two, her father accepted the chair at the University of Zurich and the family moved to Switzerland, so Zurich is her childhood home, not her birthplace (the doorway brief's "Swiss-born" is wrong; see C0). Her father died suddenly in December 1866, of a staph infection contracted at a post-mortem, when she was nine, and she helped her mother bring up three younger sisters. Chronic asthma kept her bedridden as a child; drawing passed the time, and a stay at a convent near Lake Constance for her health awakened her art. She took drawing lessons from the Zurich portraitist Eduard Pfyffer, and by 1874 knew she must leave Switzerland to study professionally. She went to Paris in 1876, and, barred from the Ecole des Beaux-Arts as a woman, enrolled at the Academie Julian in 1878, in the women's atelier that allowed drawing from nude models, under Rodolphe Julian and Tony Robert-Fleury. Her Salon debut came in 1879 with Tout passe, a self-portrait including her two friends, the only debut from the Julian women's atelier that year. Les amies followed at the Salon of 1881, winning an honourable mention; Tea at Five and the Isabelle de Rodays commission in 1883; Chez soi in 1885; Contre-jour, the backlit portrait of herself and Madeleine Zillhardt, in 1887-88 (the year is unresolved; see C2). She joined the Salon de la Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in 1890 and served on its jury, won a medaille d'or in 1889 (single-source; see C13), and in 1901 was awarded the Legion of Honour, the first foreign woman to receive it. She and Zillhardt, her fellow Julian pupil, muse and model since 1884, moved in together in 1886 and to a studio in Neuilly-sur-Seine in 1902; they lived together for 41 years, until Breslau's death. La Vie pensive (1908) revisits the couple portrait twenty years after Contre-jour. During the First World War she and Zillhardt drew portraits of French soldiers and nurses on their way to the Front, to give to their families; after the war she retired from public life and painted flowers from her garden. She died at Neuilly-sur-Seine (given as Paris in Wikipedia; see C4) on Thursday 12 May 1927, aged 70 years and 5 months, and is buried at Baden, Switzerland, next to her mother. Her output is counted at almost 800 works (Hauptman), of which the firmly dated, individually documented core in the sources read is 25 works; the rest are individually undated in those sources. Her works sit principally in the Kunstmuseum Bern (Contre-jour, Five O'clock Tea), the Musee Cantonal des Beaux-Arts, Lausanne (La Vie pensive), the Musee d'Art et d'Histoire, Geneva (Les amies), the Musee d'Orsay, Paris (Chez soi, with Rouen claimed by one catalogue note), the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Dijon (sixty pastels and drawings donated by Zillhardt), the Musee Comtadin-Duplessis, Carpentras (Gamines), and the Museum of Art Lucerne (Crying Woman). After her death her name fell into oblivion until the 2001-02 Lausanne retrospective, Louise Breslau: de l'impressionnisme aux annees folles, gathered more than a hundred works and began her rehabilitation. ## M1. Backlight as the Portrait (Contre-jour)
The light source sits behind the sitter, between the sitter and the viewer, so the face falls into shadow and the figure reads as a dark shape edged with light. The portrait is built from the rim, not from the face. The window or the fading day does the modelling; the painter does not. Contre-jour (1887-88) is the master statement -- Breslau and Zillhardt together, backlit, in their intimacy; Hauptman calls it "the most distinguished interior portrait... a remarkable play on forms, light, color, and emotional presence". Tea at Five (1883) is the early version: "A woman, perhaps herself, is backlit by the fading light, her face shadowed over". The same device returns in Sous la lampe, where lamplight replaces daylight (title only, unverified in detail). The general rule of her light is stated by Aucties: "Light is dappled, diffused and often sideways, as if filtered through a curtain or window, accentuating the melancholy mood". **Takeaway:** put the light behind the subject and let the face go dark; the portrait becomes a mood before it becomes a likeness.

## M2. The Direct Gaze (The Sitter Looks Out and Holds It)
The sitter meets the viewer's eyes straight on, without flinching and without performing. The gaze is the whole event of the picture; everything else -- dress, furniture, setting -- is reduced to a sober stage. Bergliot Ibsen (1889) "gazes straight at the viewer, a thinking woman rather than a showpiece". The Minneapolis Institute of Art: "Her pictures have a directness that's both disarming and intimidating, the subjects often gazing out at the viewer, whether lounging in bed or in the woods. As if they have nothing to hide. As if to say, this too is real -- as real as a cow in a field". La Toilette (1898) turns the gaze into a trap: Zillhardt "seems to watch us looking at her, well aware of our curiosity and choosing to indulge it". **Takeaway:** a portrait earns its charge when the sitter looks back; the viewer becomes the one being examined.

## M3. The Withheld Face (Turned Away, Shadowed, Covered)
The deliberate opposite of M2: the face is refused to the viewer -- turned away, sunk in shadow, or covered by the sitter's own arm. The portrait works without a face, and the refusal is the point. "In an 1886 self-portrait, muted and warm, she turns her face away. Others' faces, windows opening onto private battles of the heart, mattered most to her". Tea at Five: "her face shadowed over". Paresse matinale (c. 1900): "The arm flung across the young woman's eyes speaks of trust and ease". Aucties states the rule generally: she favours "absent glances, suspended gestures and natural poses". **Takeaway:** withholding the face is not a failure of portraiture; it is a portrait of privacy itself.

## M4. The Current of Sympathy (Pose Chosen by Emotional Connection)
Breslau's own stated method. The pose is not chosen for elegance or for the sitter's status; it is chosen when the artist feels an emotional connection -- "a current of sympathy" -- with the model, and that connection is what produces the likeness. The likeness is a double existence, not a copy. "Louise Breslau said the choice of pose was all-important. So, too, was finding an emotional connection between artist and model, 'a current of sympathy'". Her words, quoted in Arsene Alexandre (1928, p. 50): "A current of sympathy with [the model] will help [the artist] to create this supreme resemblance, which will be like a double existence. The work of art, as Goethe says, will be closer to the truth the further it is from reality". "To that end, Breslau avoided a seamless illusion of reality. She strove to show a deeper truth". **Takeaway:** choose the pose by sympathy, not by composition; the truth of a person arrives when the pose stops performing.

## M5. Muted Palette with One Saturated Accent
The picture is built from muffled, near-monochrome tones, and a single saturated object or surface is allowed to burn through it. The accent is never decoration; it is the emotional centre, and it is usually small. Aucties lists her palette exactly: "She is fond of muted, muffled tones (bluish gray, golden brown, old pink and moss green)", and for After Work: "The palette is sober and muted (browns, grays, ivory, verdigris, creating an almost silent atmosphere". The accent in Les amies is the scarlet tablecloth with the white dog sitting on it. The accent in the Minneapolis portrait of Yves Osterlund is the boy's blue shirt and the half-eaten orange on his desk. **Takeaway:** muffle everything, then let one colour speak; the accent is the feeling, the grey is the silence around it.

## M6. The Intimate Interior as a System (Domestic Stillness)
The picture is a small room -- a studio, a living room, a bed -- with two or three people doing nothing dramatic. The absence of action is the subject. The room is the system: bodies, furniture, and a few objects (table, canvases, curtains, books, scores, an easel) hold a stable, horizontal composition, and the light grazes across it from the side. After Work: "The absence of dramatic action gives the scene a contemplative value, reinforced by the stable, horizontal composition. The background is almost neutral... simply a few studio objects (table, canvases, curtains). The composition is highly constructed, diagonal, with a skilful interplay of lines between bodies and furniture". Chez soi: "this genre piece exudes an air of silent contemplation". Aucties: "The background is often a modest bourgeois interior (living room, study, bed or studio, which reinforces the effect of intimacy). She regularly introduces elements of intellectual or artistic life (books, scores, easels)". "She depicts everyday attitudes, namely boredom, reverie or work, where the unspoken occupies a fairly central place". **Takeaway:** build the room first and let the people be quiet in it; stillness is a subject, not an absence of one.

## M7. One Model Across a Lifetime (Zillhardt as a Lifelong Instrument)
A single model -- Madeleine Zillhardt, met at the Academie Julian, asked for her portrait in 1884, lived with from 1886 -- is painted again and again for over forty years, so the portraits become a record of a life shared, not a series of commissions. The model is also the muse, the confidant, and the supporter, and the pictures track the relationship itself. "Madeleine, a fellow student at the Academie Julian, became Breslau's muse, model, confidant, and supporter". "Zillhardt, with her full lips and tender air, was a frequent model of Breslau's". The dated sequence: the portrait Zillhardt asked for (1884), Contre-jour (1887-88, the couple), Portrait of Madeleine Zillhardt (c. 1895), La Toilette (1898, the mirror), Paresse matinale (c. 1900, the covered eyes), La Vie pensive (1908, the couple again), The Artist and Her Model (1921, the two roles). "They lived together for more than forty years". **Takeaway:** paint one person for forty years and the portraits become a biography; the model is the medium.

## M8. Pastel and Drawing as a Second Medium (The Paper Practice)
Alongside the oils runs a paper practice -- charcoal, sanguine, pastel -- used for preparatory studies and for finished portraits in its own right. The pastel is not a sketch for the oil; it is a parallel language, softer and faster, and it is the medium in which the most intimate portraits live. "The artist uses charcoal, sanguine or pastel with great ease in her preparatory studies". Zillhardt "donated sixty of the artist's pastels and drawings to the Musee des Beaux-Arts in Dijon" -- sixty works, a whole second oeuvre given away as a group. Dated pastels: Le chapeau rose (1890, pastel on paper, private collection) and Gabriel Yturride (pastel, 1904). The 1928 retrospective was of "her paintings, pastels, and drawings". AWARE: "L. Breslau was highly sought after for her ability to render the physical and psychological traits of her models, and made several portraits of children, most often in pastels". **Takeaway:** keep a paper practice beside the oil practice; the soft medium carries the intimacy the oil cannot.

## M9. The Unflattering Portrait (Refusal to Flatter)
She paints the sitter as she finds them -- bored, unfocused, ironic, tired -- and refuses the flattering image a paying client would expect. The unflattering truth is the portrait's value, and it is also a professional stance: she could afford to refuse because the commissions kept coming. The Minneapolis portrait of Yves Osterlund, age 9: "a bored-looking boy in a blue shirt... 'if not quite lazy, perhaps unfocused.' He sharpens his chalk distractedly, his desk in disarray. A half-eaten orange sits among his papers. Many portrait artists would have conjured a more flattering image for their client. But not Louise Catherine Breslau... Indeed, Breslau was not one to compromise, in art or in life". Henry Davison (c. 1903): "His elongated figure, casual pose, and ironic expression suggest he is cocksure about his place in the established canon" -- she paints the arrogance, not around it. Aucties: "In her portraits, she seeks to capture a state of mind rather than a social status". **Takeaway:** do not flatter the sitter; the flaw is the likeness.

## M10. The Double Portrait (Two People as One Unit)
The picture is not one person but a pair -- or a trio -- held together as a single unit. The relationship between the figures is the subject, not either figure alone. The pair is posed so that the space between them, the shared light, or the shared table does the joining. Les amies (1881): three friends at one table, one scarlet cloth, one white dog, "three females in a reflective mood". Tout passe (1879): a self-portrait "that included her two friends" -- she paints herself only as part of a group. Contre-jour (1887-88): "representing the couple she formed with Zillhardt in their intimacy". La Vie pensive (1908): "another work of the couple". The Artist and Her Model (1921): painter and model in one frame. Aucties on After Work: "The composition is highly constructed, diagonal, with a skilful interplay of lines between bodies and furniture" -- the bodies are joined by the furniture. **Takeaway:** paint the relationship, not the people; the pair is the portrait.

## Evolution
1856-1878, formation. Born Munich 6 December 1856, raised in Zurich from age two; her father, head of obstetrics and gynaecology at the University of Zurich, dies of a staph infection in December 1866, when she is nine; she helps raise three younger sisters. Chronic asthma keeps her bedridden as a child; drawing passes the time. A convent stay near Lake Constance is meant to help her health and awakens her art. Drawing lessons with Eduard Pfyffer in Zurich; by 1874 she knows she must leave Switzerland. She goes to Paris in 1876 -- Aucties says 1874, which is wrong on both counts (see C5) -- and, barred from the Ecole des Beaux-Arts as a woman, enrols at the Academie Julian, in the women's atelier that allows drawing from nude models. Enrolment at the Julian is dated 1878 by the Clark. Bashkirtseff's diary, 15-16 November 1877, already names her the rival to fear: "I fear Breslau most.... She will attract attention.... I can't forgive myself for not knowing as much as Breslau."

1879-1885, Salon breakthrough. Tout passe at the Salon in 1879, the only debut from the Julian women's atelier; she changes her name to Louise Catherine and opens her own studio. Les amies in 1881 -- honourable mention, three-woman studio scene, the muted palette and the scarlet accent. Tea at Five and the Isabelle de Rodays commission in 1883 -- backlight and melancholy enter. Chez soi in 1885 -- mother and sister, silent contemplation, the intimate interior as a system. By now she is a regular Salon contributor and medal winner, receiving commissions from wealthy Parisians. In 1879 she, Sophie Schaeppi and the singer Maria Fuller move into a large apartment in the Avenue des Thermes (the singer's surname against the Italian fellow student Maria Feller is unresolved; see C18).

1886-1901, partnership and maturity. Zillhardt asks for her portrait in 1884; they move in together in 1886 (she is 30, Zillhardt 23); a brief affair with the sculptor Jean Carries in 1886 ends, and she chooses Zillhardt for life. Contre-jour (1887-88) is the couple portrait, bought by the Swiss government in 1896. She joins the Salon de la Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in 1890 and serves on its jury; Gamines is bought by the French government in 1890. The 1890s are the Zillhardt portraits -- Bergliot Ibsen (1889), Le chapeau rose (1890, pastel), the 1891 self-portrait in a fur coat, La Toilette (1898), Paresse matinale (c. 1900) -- and the Henry Davison portrait (c. 1903). The Legion of Honour comes in 1901: the first foreign woman to receive it, and, as Wikipedia reports, the third woman artist.

1902-1914, Neuilly and the Impressionist turn. In 1902 she and Zillhardt move to a studio in Neuilly-sur-Seine and set up home there. The work loosens toward Impressionism -- the catalogue title of the 2001 Lausanne retrospective is "de l'impressionnisme aux annees folles" (from Impressionism to the Roaring Twenties) -- but no dated turning-point work was found in the sources read, and Hauptman qualifies the relation: Impressionist lessons "adapted rather than flaunted". La Vie pensive (1908) revisits the couple portrait twenty years on. Pastels continue (Gabriel Yturride, 1904). She is now a respected colleague of Degas and Anatole France.

1914-1927, war and retreat. During World War I she and Zillhardt stay at Neuilly and draw portraits of French soldiers and nurses on their way to the Front, to give to their families -- she was 61 at the Armistice, not 62 as one source says (see C14). After the war she withdraws from public view, paints flowers from her garden, and entertains friends. The Artist and Her Model (1921) is the late self-statement; Famille Baumann (1921) is the latest dated work in the list. She dies after a long illness on 12 May 1927, aged 70, and is buried next to her mother in Baden, Switzerland.

Afterlife: a retrospective at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts follows in 1928 -- the school that had barred women from study until 1897 -- and a second at Galerie Charpentier in 1932, dedicated to women who trained at the Academie Julian. Zillhardt publishes Louise Catherine Breslau et ses amis in 1932, donates sixty pastels and drawings to Dijon, and spends the rest of her life perpetuating Breslau's work. Then oblivion: the 1988 High Museum survey of Swiss art includes no Breslau at all, and the Lausanne museum's own 1989 masterpieces volume omits her. The rehabilitation is the 2001-02 Lausanne exhibition, 13 October 2001 to 20 January 2002, more than a hundred paintings, pastels and drawings, "the most extensive presentation of Breslau's artistry since the 1928 Paris show". The barge Zillhardt bought in 1928 and had Le Corbusier refit as a homeless shelter takes the name Louise-Catherine; it sinks in the Seine flood on Saturday 10 February 2018. A Paris street is named Place Louise Catherine Breslau and Madeleine Zillhardt, in the 6th arrondissement.

## Aesthetic Logic
Breslau's logic is the logic of intimacy as a method. She paints the people in her life -- women, mostly, herself and Zillhardt together -- and she paints them the way a person who lives with them sees them: at rest, in thought, in a room, in fading light. The system has four rules, all sourced. First, the pose is chosen by sympathy, not by composition: "the choice of pose was all-important" and the "current of sympathy" with the model produces "this supreme resemblance, which will be like a double existence". Second, the likeness is a state of mind, not a social status: she seeks "to capture a state of mind rather than a social status, favoring absent glances, suspended gestures and natural poses", and she "avoids ostentatious accessories in favor of sober staging that leaves all room for the body and the gaze". Third, the light is the mood: dappled, diffused, sideways, "as if filtered through a curtain or window", and often behind the sitter, so the face goes dark and the feeling comes from the silhouette. Fourth, the truth is kept even when it is unflattering: "Breslau was not one to compromise, in art or in life", and her subjects gaze out "as if they have nothing to hide. As if to say, this too is real -- as real as a cow in a field". The palette follows the logic: muted, muffled tones (bluish gray, golden brown, old pink, moss green) with one saturated accent, because a quiet room needs one thing to burn.

The opposite ending to Bashkirtseff is the point of the doorway: Bashkirtseff painted the street and died at 25 with the Journal as her monument; Breslau painted the room and lived to 70, and the room -- the studio, the bed, the table at five o'clock -- became a fifty-year practice. The long life is the mechanism the study turns on: 48 years of exhibiting (1879-1927) against Bashkirtseff's five (1879-1884). The late turn is a withdrawal, not a collapse: after the war she paints flowers from her garden, the same intimacy with the subject moved from people to plants. What she never does: she never paints history, never paints the crowd, never paints a sitter she does not know. Her whole oeuvre is a circle of people she lived with, and the circle is the work.

The through-line for prompts: a system of attention, not a style. Backlight that darkens the face and lights the rim; a gaze that holds the viewer and a face that is withheld; one saturated note in a muffled room; a room that stays still while the people inside it think; one model painted for forty years until the portraits become a biography; a pair held together as a single unit. All of it is watchable because it is all about looking -- who looks at whom, who looks away, and who is allowed to look at all.

## POSITIONS
Status key: OWN = her own stated view or practice, evidenced from a source read this leg; SECONDHAND = her view as reported/quoted by a contemporary or biographer; RECEPTION = what others said or did about her. All her own quoted sentences are translations from French or German.

P1. OWN (practice): art was to be a profession, not a pastime. After Pfyffer she believed she had learnt all she could from him and "wanted her art to be more than just a pleasing hobby" and "wanted to become a professional artist" -- so she left Switzerland for Paris. The career decision is the founding position of her life.
P2. OWN (VERIFIED VERBATIM, TRANSLATION, AWARE): the thirst for knowledge was the motive -- "My thirst for knowledge was unquenchable, and I knew that I would find ways to learn in Paris", written June 1926 in the magazine Am Hauslichen Herd (p. 270). A backward look at the 1870s decision, in her own words, a year before her death.
P3. OWN (via Arsene Alexandre 1928, quoted in the Medium article): the portrait is made by pose and by sympathy, not by copying. "Breslau said the choice of pose was all-important. So, too, was finding an emotional connection between artist and model, 'a current of sympathy.'" Her stated theory: "A current of sympathy with [the model] will help [the artist] to create this supreme resemblance, which will be like a double existence. The work of art, as Goethe says, will be closer to the truth the further it is from reality." Status: SECONDHAND quotation of her theory, via Alexandre as translated by the article's author.
P4. OWN (via the Medium article, quoting Women Artists in Paris: 1850-1900, 2017, p. 16, c. 1925): France was the motherland of the woman artist -- "Lucky Frenchwomen....one has to love this country! It has proven that the woman artist has a motherland on this earth." She credited "the open-mindedness of Gilded Age Paris for fostering her success". Status: SECONDHAND quotation.
P5. OWN (practice, AWARE): after the 1881 Salon she painted portraits exclusively, mostly of her relatives -- Chez Soi (1885) is her mother and sister. The portrait, and the family interior, became her whole field; she did not diversify into history or landscape.
P6. OWN (practice): the interior as a psychological stage, lit from behind. Tea at Five (1883): a woman backlit by fading light, face shadowed over, home and hearth meaning melancholy. Chez Soi (1885): mother and sister in silent contemplation, the dog at her mother's feet. Contre-jour (1887-88): the couple against the light, "a remarkable play on forms, light, color, and emotional presence" (Hauptman). The mechanism is consistent across forty years: put the sitter between the viewer and the window, and let the shadow do the psychology.
P7. OWN (practice): one model for forty years. Zillhardt -- asked Breslau for her portrait in 1884, moved in together 1886, lived together until Breslau's death in 1927 -- is the standing subject: Contre-jour, La Toilette (1898), Paresse matinale (c. 1900), La Vie pensive (1908), Sous la lampe. The couple itself, not the commission, is the work that recurs.
P8. OWN (practice): pastel as a primary medium. Sixty of her pastels and drawings went to the Musee des Beaux-Arts in Dijon from Zillhardt's bequest; Le chapeau rose (1890) and the Gabriel Yturri pastel (1904) are pastels, not oil sketches; the 1892 portrait of Adeline Poznanska was preceded by "a number of studies in various poses and attitudes" (AWARE); children were "most often" painted in pastels (AWARE). Pastel is where she worked out poses and where she painted children.
P9. OWN (practice): self-support through commissions, and commissions as the engine of invention. She left the Academie shortly after her 1879 Salon debut, opened her own studio, and "supported herself through portrait commissions" (Clark); the commissions "financed her own inventive compositions that showed how ordinary women lived and loved" (Medium). Economic independence is the condition of the intimate pictures, not their opposite.
P10. OWN (practice): no compromise on likeness. "Breslau was not one to compromise, in art or in life" (Minneapolis Institute of Art). The proof is the portrait of Yves Osterlund, age 9: the boy appears "if not quite lazy, perhaps unfocused", sharpening his chalk distractedly, desk in disarray, a half-eaten orange among his papers. A paying client's child, painted unflattered.
P11. OWN (practice): her own face withheld. In the 1886 self-portrait, "muted and warm, she turns her face away"; the 1891 self-portrait holds a dog and looks out past the viewer. Others' faces -- "windows opening onto private battles of the heart" -- mattered most to her. The artist's face is the one face she refuses to give.
P12. OWN (practice): absorbed figures, not performing ones. Les amies (1881): three women "in a reflective mood" around a scarlet tablecloth, a white dog on top of it. Chez Soi: "an air of silent contemplation". La Toilette: Zillhardt fixes her hair in the mirror but "instead of gazing at herself, she seems to watch us looking at her". Paresse matinale: "an almost aching intimacy... no self-conscious attitude here. The arm flung across the young woman's eyes speaks of trust and ease". The sitter is caught inside her own moment, not arranged for the viewer.
P13. OWN (institutional choice): she chose the Julian atelier that let women draw from the nude model. "Breslau went to the only one that allowed her to draw from nude models" (Minneapolis Institute of Art). The price was unequal: "Julian charged women more than men, reasoning that some well-to-do relative was probably paying their way, but this was not the case for Breslau -- she had to work for a living and she knew it". The training condition and the economic condition are one fact.
P14. SECONDHAND: the two masters' verdicts. Degas approved the 1880 portrait of the poet Henry Davison (Hauptman: "the poet Henry Davison of 1880, which won the approval of Degas"). Julian, the founder, "is rumored to have been so impressed by her talent that he predicted her work would be widely known" (Hauptman). Both verdicts are reported, not quoted verbatim; no direct Degas sentence about Breslau was found this leg.
P15. RECEPTION: the Salon record of a woman who kept winning. Only student from the Julian women's atelier to debut at the 1879 Salon, with Tout passe; honourable mention at the 1881 Salon for Les amies, "one of the victors of the 1881 Salon" (Ernest Hoschede in Henri IV, 14 May 1881, via AWARE); gold medal at the 1889 Exposition Universelle (Hauptman, single-source; AWARE says a gold medal at the 1887 World's Fair -- see C13); the French state bought Gamines in 1890 and other works during her lifetime (AWARE); the Swiss government bought Contre-jour in 1896; she represented Switzerland at the 1887 World's Fair and won a gold medal (AWARE).
P16. RECEPTION: the Legion of Honour and the jury. In 1901 she was awarded the Legion of Honour, "the first foreign woman to receive that distinction" and the third woman artist overall (Clark; Wikipedia). She joined the Salon de la Societe Nationale des Beaux-Arts in 1890 and served on its jury. A woman, a foreigner, judging the Salon that had once been the only door open to her.
P17. RECEPTION: the rival's verdict, in the rival's own diary. Bashkirtseff, 15-16 November 1877: "I fear Breslau most....She will attract attention....I can't forgive myself for not knowing as much as Breslau" (Kernberger translation, quoted in the Medium article). Bashkirtseff "obsessed over her less endowed, more talented peer" and "envied her and vowed to surpass her" (Medium). The jealousy is the contemporary critical judgment that matters most: the best student in the room, named by the other best student.
P18. RECEPTION: the 1928 retrospective at the school that had barred her. "In 1928, the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in Paris honored her with a retrospective" -- the same institution that had barred women from study until 1897, "hence forcing her instruction in Julian's private studio" (Hauptman). A second retrospective followed in 1932 at Galerie Charpentier, "dedicated to women who trained at the Academie Julian".
P19. RECEPTION: oblivion, then rehabilitation. "After her death in 1927, however, her name fell into oblivion, and today few historians of the nineteenth century would count her among the elevated ranks" (Hauptman). Her corpus of almost 800 works "has remained hidden from view" (Hauptman); the 1988 Swiss survey included no Breslau at all, and the Lausanne museum's own 1989 masterpieces volume omitted her. The rehabilitation: Lausanne 2001, "the most extensive presentation of Breslau's artistry since the 1928 Paris show" with more than a hundred works; Dijon 2005-2006, "Louise Breslau, dans l'intimite du portrait" (AWARE); Women Artists in Paris, 1850-1900, Clark 2018. Hauptman's verdict: "beyond Bonheur and Cassatt there are indeed overlooked figures who are worthy of attention and study".
P20. RECEPTION / OWN: patriotic duty in the war, then flowers. At the onset of World War I, having lived in France for forty years, she "took the side of her adopted country" and "created a series of portraits of soldiers called to the front and donated them to their families", and from 1915 to 1917 drew Red Cross nurses (AWARE; Wikipedia). After the war she "retired from the public and spent much of her time painting flowers from her garden and entertaining friends"; "at the end of her life [she] chose to work predominantly on still lifes" (AWARE). The career ends where it began -- in a room, looking closely at something small.

## Load-Bearing Corrections
C0. The doorway brief's "Swiss-born" is wrong. She was born in Munich on 6 December 1856 and moved to Zurich aged two in 1858, when her father took the University of Zurich chair. Every source read says Munich (Wikipedia, My Daily Art Display, Hauptman, Smithsonian). Zurich is her childhood home, not her birthplace. The same error appears in Aucties ("Born in Zurich") and is rejected with it.
C1. Leg 1's date table gives Zillhardt's birth as Tuesday 10 June 1884. That is her birthday in 1884, the year she asked Breslau for her portrait, not her birth date. Her birth is Wednesday 10 June 1863, verified by the parent with date -d and by legs 2 and 3.
C2. Contre-jour date: 1887 (Zillhardt page) vs 1888 (Hauptman). A third figure, 1883, comes from a tea page that may confuse the couple portrait with Le the a cinq heures (1883). Carry 1887-88 unresolved; do not state a single year as settled.
C3. Gamines date: bought by the French government in 1890 (Wikipedia, purchase record) vs Hauptman "Gamines of 1893". A painting bought by the state in 1890 cannot have been painted in 1893; unresolved, possibly two works or a misdate.
C4. Place of death: Paris (Wikipedia infobox, Zillhardt page) vs Neuilly-sur-Seine (FreeDictionary, Smithsonian). Neuilly-sur-Seine is her home suburb; "Paris" is the loose form. Carry Neuilly-sur-Seine with Paris flagged, not the reverse.
C5. Paris arrival: 1876 (My Daily Art Display, Hauptman) vs summer 1875 (Invaluable, a sales listing) vs 1874 (Aucties, which also wrongly joins her to the Julian that year). Use 1876; flag 1875 as the outlier.
C6. Julian enrolment 1878 (Clark) vs arrival 1876; the gap is unexplained, and Bashkirtseff's diary entries of November 1877 predate the 1878 enrolment date. Do not merge arrival and enrolment into one year, and do not overstate the sequence.
C7. Les amies vs Apres le travail: legs 1 and 2 disagree. Leg 1 lists two 1881 three-woman studio scenes; leg 2 treats them as one canvas under two titles, on the shared subject, date and Salon. The study carries leg 2's majority reading (Clark, My Daily Art Display, Hauptman against Aucties alone). Dimensions conflict too: 85 x 160 cm (Clark) vs 125 x 160 cm (Aucties). If they were two pictures the dated total would rise by one, from 25 to 26.
C8. Chez soi location: Musee d'Orsay (My Daily Art Display, allpaintingsstore) vs Musee des Beaux-Arts, Rouen (National Library of Ireland catalogue note on the 1885 letter). Orsay has two sources; carry Orsay with Rouen flagged.
C9. Self-portrait 1891 location: Musee d'Art Moderne et Contemporain, Strasbourg (FeelTheArt, wahooart) vs Musee des Beaux-Arts Jules Cheret, Nice (allpaintingsstore, artsdot). Possibly two different self-portraits; unresolved for the 1891 picture.
C10. Fillette a l'Orange date: 1897 (painting, My Daily Art Display) vs 1897-1899 (lithograph, Dallas Museum of Art via Google Arts and Culture) vs 1899 (artsdot FAQ). Carry 1897-1899 for the image across media, with the three figures flagged.
C11. Henry Davison portraits: 1880 (Hauptman, Wikipedia selected works) vs c. 1903 (Medium). Two sittings or one misdated portrait; unresolved. Dated total 25 as listed, 24 if one canvas.
C12. Legion of Honour ordinal: "first foreign woman" is firm (Clark, Hauptman); "third woman artist" is Wikipedia-only, and no source read names the first two women artists. Carry "first foreign woman" as the firm claim and "third woman artist" as reported-by-Wikipedia only.
C13. Medaille d'or: 1889 (Hauptman only) vs AWARE's gold medal at the 1887 World's Fair. The 1889 Exposition Universelle is the documented Paris fair; AWARE's 1887 may be an error or a different fair. Year and occasion are single-source either way; do not state the medal year without the flag.
C14. "Sixty-two years of age when the war ended" (My Daily Art Display) is off by one: born 6 December 1856, she was 61 at the Armistice of 11 November 1918 and turned 62 a month later. Use 61.
C15. Christie's lot heading "(1856-1928)" is wrong; she died in 1927.
C16. Artsdot FAQ "lived about 71 years" is wrong; 70 years and 5 months.
C17. Rodolphe Julian's birth year: 1839 (general sources, the #496 leg) vs 1830 (AWARE). Use 1839 with the flag.
C18. Maria Fuller (singer, Avenue des Thermes flat, 1879) vs Maria Feller (Italian fellow student, left figure of Les amies): two people or one misreported name; do not merge.
C19. Convent vs women's school near Lake Constance (Wikipedia vs biographs): wording only; place and reason (asthma) agree.
C20. Wikipedia's infobox "Spouse Madeleine Zillhardt" is anachronistic framing. They were lifelong companions who lived together from 1886; they were never married. The study says companion/partner, not spouse.
C21. Wikipedia's movement field "Impressionnism" is a typo for Impressionism. Her relation to Impressionism is in any case qualified by Hauptman: Impressionist lessons "adapted rather than flaunted". No dated turning point was found.
C22. Naturalisation: Wikipedia says she naturalised Swiss "many years earlier" than the war, but no source read gives a date. Do not state a naturalisation year.
C23. Name-change timing: "shortly afterwards [after the 1879 Salon] Breslau had changed her name to Louise Catherine" (Wikipedia). No source read gives an exact date or a document for the change; carry it as "after 1879", not as a dated event.
C24. Family background "German Jewish family of Polish Jewish descent" (Wikipedia, citing Ancestry.com): single-source; no second source read confirms it. Carry with the attribution, not as settled fact.
C25. First exhibition year: 1879 Salon debut (Wikipedia, My Daily Art Display, Clark) vs Hauptman "began to exhibit four years later" than 1876, i.e. 1880. Hauptman's arithmetic is loose (1876 to 1879 is three years). Use 1879.
C26. Sisters' names (Marie-Henrietta, Emma, Bernadette): single-source (My Daily Art Display only); no second source read names them.
C27. Her father's first name and her mother's name were not given in any source read this leg.
C28. Portrait of Henry Rochefort: named in the doorway brief only; no source read in any leg confirms the work, its date, its medium, or its location. It is dropped from the works list and is not carried as a work.
C29. "Joined the Societe Nationale in 1890 after it had finally opened its doors to women" is loose phrasing; the Societe Nationale was founded in 1890 after the split from the Societe des Artistes Francais. Use the plain fact, not the "opened its doors" explanation.
C30. The 1886 self-portrait (face turned away) and the 1891 self-portrait (fur coat, hood, scarf) are two different works; do not merge them.
C31. Partnership dating: AWARE says "partner since 1885"; Wikipedia, Zillhardt's page and My Daily Art Display say they moved permanently together in 1886. Use 1886 for living together, 1885 as AWARE's dating of the partnership.
C32. Winnareta Singer spelling: "Winnareta" on the Breslau page, "Winnaretta" on the Zillhardt page. Use "Winnareta" and note the variant.
C33. "Moved in together in 1886" vs "moved to a studio in Neuilly-sur-Seine in 1902": two different moves, sixteen years apart. Do not conflate them.
C34. Gabriel Faure as a friend rests on a single commercial-site line (Aucties). List him as a frequented circle name, not a documented friendship.
