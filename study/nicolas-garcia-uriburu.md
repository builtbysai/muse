## The life: the painter who left the canvas for the river

Nicolas Garcia Uriburu was born in Buenos Aires on 24 December 1937, the eldest child of the lawyer Eduardo Garcia Uriburu Castilla and Maria Susana Massini Ezcurra Jolly, named for his grandfather, in a family that had produced two presidents of Argentina. The date is agreed by every source read -- English and Spanish Wikipedia, the estate's Obras page ("nacio en Buenos Aires en visperas de Navidad, el 24 de diciembre de 1937"), and the genealogical record (C9952). He was a timid, introverted boy in a strict household who did not see the night sky until age eight; his mother drew well, his uncle Pablo Cardenas, a writer, encouraged his drawings, organised his first exhibition at the Galeria Muller in 1954 when he was sixteen, exposed him to Picasso and Modigliani, and introduced him to the critic Rafael Squirru, then director of the Museo de Arte Moderno, who later exhibited his early work (C9956, C9957). The sibling count conflicts and is recorded, not resolved: nine children (Spanish Wikipedia) against eldest of ten (estate, drawing on Restany) (C9955).

He enrolled in architecture at UBA in 1958, "to calm his father's fears", and there met Luis Fernando Benedit; the two "continued a parallel artistic career until the end of their studies", both self-taught as artists (C9958). He took his architecture degree just before Christmas 1965 -- single-source, estate/Restany only -- and no source records any architecture practice after graduation, except that he later designed the structure housing his own studio (C9958, C9996). Around 1961 he and Benedit travelled together to Peru, a trip MC Galeria calls decisive for a thinking committed to the cultural history of the Latin American continent; the year is single-source (C9959).

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is a single morning in Venice. (1) Matter and the pampa, 1954-1965: informalist matter-painting -- thick oil relieved by transparencies, sand and water rubbed into the paint -- resolving from dark earth tones into geological cross-sections (W01 El espectro, 1960); the 1962 Pampas series; the return to figuration through ombues in 1963-64, green asserting itself over a red ground, the foliage a microcosm (W02); then Pop colectivos, the vernacular Buenos Aires buses, shown at Galeria Guernica in 1965 (W03). He met Blanca Isabel Alvarez de Toledo at the 1963 Rubbers opening and married her in 1964; by his own account meeting her changed everything overnight (C9960-C9962). (2) Paris and the turn outward, 1965-1968: the Braque Prize, 7 April 1965 (the 1965 date is best supported; both Wikipedia prize lists give wrong years, 1985 and 1993 -- C9963); Cite des Arts 1966 (single-source); Expo 67; the Guggenheim's The Emergent Decade 1965; Arcadian Pop -- Marie-Antoinette obsessively, Las tres gracias 1967 (W04, medium conflict: oil on canvas per the estate and MALBA, Pop sculpture per English Wikipedia alone -- C9990, C10008); Prototipos para un jardin artificial at Iris Clert, February 1968, a plastic garden of Plexiglas animals interrupted by May 1968 (W05). (3) Green Power, 1968-1982: the Venice colouring (W06); Hidrocromia Intercontinental 1970, four rivers on two continents (W09); body colourings 1971-74 (W10); the manifesto Yo denuncio con mi arte and the Portfolio (W12, 1973); the border-erasing maps (W13); the Green Series paintings of nature against New York modernity (W14); CAYC association from 1970, peripheral -- Benedit sat on the board and joined the Thirteen, Uriburu "installed abroad during those years, had a more peripheral participation" (C9974); Beuys and the Rhine 1981 and the first of the 7,000 Oaks, Documenta 7, June 1982 (W15, W16). (4) Return, planting, Latin Americanism, 1982-2016: 50,000 trees in Buenos Aires in 1982 (the "returned in 1982" framing is single-source and sits uneasily with a 1980 Buenos Aires show -- C9978); democracy fountains 1983; Grupo Bosque founded December 1984, president, 600,000+ pines; 9 de Julio plantings from 1987; the myths series (two different Evas, 1972 and 1980, do not merge -- C9982); endangered species; Utopia del Sur 1993 (estate list says 1990, conflict recorded -- C9984); the MNBA retrospective 1998; the censored Empresas contaminantes 2000 (W19); Greenpeace actions; the Riachuelo colouring with Greenpeace, 22 March 2010 (W20). The late maps turn red where the early work was green. He presided over the Fundacion Nicolas Garcia Uriburu (founding year not established: it did not yet exist in 2001 and existed before his death -- C9992) and was lifetime curator of the Museo Coleccion Garcia Uriburu in Maldonado, Uruguay, donated to the Uruguayan state in 2000 (single-source year); member of number of the Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes (year unknown -- C9991). He died in Buenos Aires on 19 June 2016, aged 78 -- the same calendar day as the 1968 Venice colouring, a coincidence, not a claim (C9953). Cause of death conflicts: acute myocardial infarction (Spanish Wikipedia) against cause not disclosed (contemporary report of the Rivadavia Hospital confirmation) (C9954). Posthumous: six works in VIVA ARTE VIVA, Venice 2017 -- he had been dead a year, so "again invited" is wrong (C9995); MNBA Venecia en clave verde, 2018, fiftieth anniversary (C9995).

## M1 -- The coloration: a three-kilometre painting whose paint disappears

On Tuesday 19 June 1968, at 8 in the morning (the estate; MALBA says c. 6:30 at high tide -- conflict, C9968, C10034), during the 34th Venice Biennale, Uriburu boarded a gondola with the gondolier Memo and poured thirty kilos of fluorescein -- bought in Milan days before with Blanca, Isabel Alvarez de Toledo and Raquel Forner -- into the Grand Canal. The water turned bright green; the green lasted about eight hours, until the low tide carried the dye away. He was detained with the gondolier and taken to Milan, held twelve hours until a scientific committee certified the fluorescein harmless (the "three days" figure is single-source Maleva; twelve hours is best supported -- C9968). The action was unauthorised and outside the Biennale programme -- the Met is explicit, "not as part of its official program" -- so English Wikipedia's "invited to the Venice Biennale" is wrong as phrasing (C9969). His own gloss: "pinte un cuadro de tres kilometros cuyo lienzo era el canal" -- I painted a three-kilometre picture whose canvas was the canal. And the theory, from La movilidad del color (1968): "The new notion of a work of art is no longer an object in itself -- it is an idea -- neither canvas nor gallery are now sufficient for it -- it is a public act in which the public fully participates because it surrounds them -- it is penetrable and it can be moved through, it has no limits and can be repeated. It is against the notion of eternity -- it is momentary -- the coloration lasted from dawn until nightfall. It is a pure idea. It cannot be sold." The watchable behaviour is a city changing colour and then changing back: the work moves because the water moves, and the painter has relinquished the control painters usually enjoy -- the tide, the current, the gondolas and vaporettos finish the painting (C10000). The gesture then repeats more than thirty times: Hidrocromia Intercontinental 1970 (East River 25 May, Seine 15 June, Grand Canal 27 June, Rio de la Plata 15 July -- the Riachuelo mouth was the first intention, the Rio de la Plata the actual site -- C9970); fountains and ports to 1974, then sporadically; the Rhine with Beuys 1981 (W15); the Riachuelo with Greenpeace 2010 (W20), the first colouring repeated forty-two years later as a bicentennial counter-celebration under a flag reading "Riachuelo: 200 years of contamination".

**Takeaway:** the coloration is the whole practice in one gesture: pour green into a system, let the system's own motion spread it, watch it vanish. A piece on M1 needs a waterway, a pour, a green that spreads by current and tide, and an ending -- the green must go away, or it is not a coloration.

## M2 -- The double intervention: intervene, then intervene on the photograph

The action is momentary, so the work survives as a document -- and Uriburu re-works the document. W07 Coloracion en Venecia (1968, intervened photograph + typed text, 82 x 50 cm, MNBA): the photograph of the green canal carries the typed manifesto text beside it. W09 Hidrocromia Intercontinental (1970): pastel on four photographs, each 88.5 x 30 cm, MALBA -- he "volvio a colorear con pastel tiza las fotografias color que registran la transformacion temporal que produjo su mano sobre el entorno natural", re-colouring in pastel the photographs that record the transformation his hand produced, "in a tautological game akin to historical conceptualism". The mechanism is the second intervention: the landscape is intervened in water, then the photograph of the landscape is intervened in pastel. The document is not filed; it is re-painted. The same logic governs the Portfolio (W12, 1973): six screenprints that review and order five years of actions, with cartographic prints locating each action, maps of Latin American unity, and the closing plate a project never executed -- the colouring of the Iguazu Falls.

**Takeaway:** a piece on M2 should show the same green twice -- once in the water, once drawn over the photograph of the water -- and the second pass is the work, not the record of it. Do not treat the photograph as evidence; treat it as a second canvas.

## M3 -- The body as smallest waterway: wear the protest

The colouring shrinks to its minimum support: skin, hair, a garment. W10 Coloraciones del cuerpo (1971-74): his sex and face in 1971 (the sex painted green in New York in October 1971, photographed, the image mailed to acquaintances), his daughter Azul's shoes and nappies in 1971, framed as a box "as if to induct her, from birth, into the environmental movement", his hair in 1973 (dyed green for the CAYC silkscreen opening), the clothes of the dancer Iris Scaccheri in 1974. The mechanism makes the body the proof that the dye is harmless -- he wears it -- and that the protest is personal -- he is the site. There is no museum holding for any individual body colouring; they survive as photographs and as the Portfolio plate.

**Takeaway:** M3 is the coloration at human scale: the river becomes a face, a head of hair, a pair of baby shoes. A piece on M3 should dye a small thing the viewer cares about and let the dye be the whole statement -- no river needed.

## M4 -- The border-erasing map: the map is the argument

Towards the end of 1970 he began a map of Latin America, left unfinished, and completed it in 1981 by adding a line uniting the continent through its river basins -- a yellow stroke on dominant turquoise marking the Belem-Buenos Aires route through the Amazon, Orinoco and Rio de la Plata (W13). Between 1973 and 1974 he composed a series of maps erasing Latin America's political borders to highlight natural limits: "Los rios son brazos de union continental que sobrepasan esos limites". The inverted-map idea he took from a mid-nineteenth-century inverted map of Buenos Aires province made for the caudillo Rosas, and later from Joaquin Torres Garcia's inverted Southern Cone (C9976). The MNBA holds his 1972 print Una sola latinoamerica (signed "una sola latinoamerica / nicolas garcia uriburu / cayc 5-1972"). In the 1990s the maps turn red and burnt-orange -- Red South (1997), South in Flames (1993), Granary of the World (1993, the continent formed from thousands of glued corn kernels) -- the last a joke from his hiking childhood, "Don't lose your north". The map-painting reaches its finished, exhibitable form in W18 Utopia del Sur (1993, oil on canvas, 180 x 190 cm, MNBA): "Todo un continente unido por la naturaleza. El agua, la tierra y el aire son las reservas del futuro en el continente latinoamericano."

**Takeaway:** the map is not a picture of a place; it is a political sentence drawn in rivers. A piece on M4 should let the viewer erase the borders and watch the rivers join -- south on top, borders gone, the continent re-learned by its water.

## M5 -- The bottled river: pollution you can hold and refuse to drink

The colouring, decanted. The 1968 precedent: the Met holds Coloration Uriburu (1968), glass, water, cork and fluorescein, 33 x 8 cm -- "the artist kept a group of bottles of the colored water as a testament to his artistic intervention". The full statement is W17 Coloracion Dock 3 / 500 anos de polucion (1992): fluorescein poured into the Rio de la Plata at Dock 3, Puerto Madero, to denounce 500 years of exploitation of natural resources; the coloured water then bottled -- a series of thirty, three held by the MNBA, "etiquetadas, numeradas y firmadas con la huella digital del artista, y dispuestas en una estructura de madera, similar al modo en que se presentan los vinos" -- labelled, numbered, signed with the artist's fingerprint, displayed in a wooden structure like wine. The MNBA teaching material asks schoolchildren exactly the question the work asks: would you drink it? The mechanism turns the river into a commodity you can hold: the label replaces the landscape, the fingerprint replaces the barcode, and the refusal to drink is the protest.

**Takeaway:** M5 is the coloration made shelf-sized. A piece on M5 should show the bottle, the label, the fingerprint -- and let the viewer decide whether to drink. The wine-rack presentation is the joke and the accusation at once.

## M6 -- The planting as slow coloration: green that grows instead of vanishing

The colouring inverted. In 1981 Joseph Beuys asked Uriburu to take part in the colouring against Rhine pollution; they coloured the Rhine at Dusseldorf, bottled polluted water labelled "H2O + 10,000 toxins" and signed it as a limited edition (W15). The following year, at Documenta 7 in June 1982, Beuys invited Uriburu to plant the first of 7,000 oaks in Kassel (W16) -- the first tree, not 7,000; the interview phrasing "mainly Beuys and I planted seven thousand young oak trees" overstates his role (C10057). The Rhine (1981) and Documenta 7 (1982) must be kept apart; the official site's own caption dates the planting 1981, and Arte al Dia compresses both into 1981 (C9977, C10075). A tree planted now is green that grows for decades, the inverse of a dye that vanishes in hours: instead of making pollution visible, making the remedy visible. He carried the inversion home -- 50,000 trees in Buenos Aires neighbourhoods in 1982; Grupo Bosque founded December 1984 in Punta del Este, president, more than 600,000 pines; 1,000 trees on Avenida 9 de Julio on 9 July 1987, repeated on the same national date over the years (C9980). The two men shared views on humanism, social philosophy, ecology and libertarianism, and remained personal and working friends; the contact was at Beuys's initiative (Cytlak), though who invited whom differs by source -- Wikipedia has the Green Party inviting Uriburu and Beuys approaching him, the official site has Beuys asking Uriburu, Uriburu himself says Beuys "asked to collaborate in my project" (C10057).

**Takeaway:** M6 is the only mechanism in this study where time works for the artist instead of against him. A piece on M6 should plant one thing and let it grow while the dye fades -- the two greens on opposite clocks.

## M7 -- The named carpet: walk on the polluters' names

W19 Empresas contaminantes auspician (2000, Primera Bienal de Buenos Aires, MNBA): intervened photographs of industrial effluents, and on a carpet the names of some of the companies responsible -- nine companies pouring toxic waste into the Riachuelo, contaminating an area inhabited by 5 million people. The organiser, Jorge Glusberg, removed some company names for fear of reprisals, censoring the work: Uriburu's own account says Glusberg cut the packing tape fixing the plastics to the floor with a cutter, moved the names to disguise the removal of Frigorificos Coto, and then denied everything (C9986). A La Nacion readers' letter of 14 December 2000 compared the censorship to the 1967 banning of the opera Bomarzo. The mechanism is the inverse of the coloration: instead of dyeing the river to show the pollution, he dyes the photographs and writes the polluters' names where the audience must step. The viewer walks on the names; the censorship became part of the work's history.

**Takeaway:** M7 is naming as a material. A piece on M7 should put the names on the floor and make the viewer walk across them -- and if a name gets removed, the removal is part of the piece, not a repair.

## M8 -- The nature-vs-city juxtaposition: the building washed green, the animal in front

The weakest mechanism in this study, and flagged as such: it rests on W14 and the Christie's Green Series description, and has no separate mechanism section in leg 2 (C10095). W14 Cascada y Museo Guggenheim (1975, oil on canvas; acquired by the Guggenheim into its permanent collection in 2023): a waterfall and a museum in the same frame. The Green Series, early 1970s, "staged encounters between nature -- through the metaphor of chlorophyllic color -- and the built landscape of North American modernity, seen in juxtaposed images of the Guggenheim Museum and a cow, for example, and the Chrysler Building and a giraffe". The estate illustrates Vaca y Museo Guggenheim (1975) and Iguazu y Museo Guggenheim (1975) alongside the cascade version. The watchable behaviour: a waterfall and a museum in the same frame, neither winning -- the building washed green, the animal standing in front of it, and the juxtaposition is the argument. The same logic runs through the 1969 New York paintings -- Blanca kneeling naked in place of the Atlas statue at Rockefeller Center, a pigeon perched on her; Los delfines (1969); La rebelion (W11, 1973), a green fist clenched in front of green skyscrapers.

**Takeaway:** M8 is the coloration as a picture again -- the green applied to a building instead of a river. A piece on M8 should keep the juxtaposition flat and unreconciled: the animal in front, the building behind, the green washing over both, no winner declared.

## The aesthetic logic: make the invisible visible with green, then let time do the rest

Uriburu's aesthetic is the logic of the dye. Everything he makes introduces a visible green into a system and lets the system's own motion complete the work: the tide spreads it, the current carries it, the tree grows it, the reader reads it, the walker steps on it. The green is never decoration; it is a reagent. The river is already polluted -- the dye does not pollute it, it reveals it. The map is already divided -- the erasure does not divide it, it reveals the rivers underneath. The bottle is already wine-shaped -- the fingerprint does not decorate it, it replaces the barcode with a body.

Against Bedel, his predecessor in this chain, the contrast is exact and it is the study's hinge. Bedel builds a container and lets the viewer complete it: the hand opens the book, the feet find the silver, the drag unrolls the data. Uriburu takes the territory itself as the canvas and lets the public walk through the work while it lasts hours: the river is the painting, the tide is the duration, the city is the gallery, and nobody owns the result. Both are architects by training who left the building behind -- Bedel for the container, Uriburu for the territory. Both worked through Glusberg's CAYC, but from opposite positions: Bedel inside the Grupo de los Trece, Uriburu outside it, abroad, peripheral, showing beside them in Chicago in 1974. Benedit, the third man in that Chicago roster, built a living system and let a living agent complete it -- the bees choose, the mice solve, the plant dies from kindness. Uriburu built no system at all: he poured a colour into a system that already existed -- a river, a city, a continent -- and let it show itself. The through line of his own career is the addressee moving while the gesture stays the same: from the art world (Venice 1968, uninvited) to the public (rivers, fountains, thirty-plus cities) to the state and the corporation (Dock 3, the carpet of names, the Riachuelo with Greenpeace). The gesture never changes; the support gets larger and slower -- canvas, then canal, then river system, then forest, then the named corporation. A study that misses the dye misses Uriburu; a piece prompt that shows only the green and not the system it reveals -- the river, the map, the bottle, the name -- is not an Uriburu prompt. The prompt must contain the system, the green, and the vanishing.
