# Gregorio Vardanega: Study Notes

**Site:** Gregorio Vardanega, 21 March 1923 - 7 October 2007 -- Argentine kinetic artist, born in Possagno, Italy, raised in Buenos Aires from the age of three, living in Paris from 1959 until his death, 48 years; Sicardi Gallery calls him one of the central figures of Latin American kinetic art, above all for light boxes and programmed luminous sculptures built around colour, movement and electronics (C7801). He began with wire and Plexiglas constructions in Buenos Aires, put an electric motor into a work in 1957, let the viewer change the colours in 1958, and then, in Paris in the 1960s, built the programmed light boxes, the chromatic spaces and the towers that are his mature work -- the light box as a machine, the tower as a small city. With his wife and collaborator Martha Boto he coined the word chromocinetisme for their joint 1964 show at the Maison des Beaux-Arts. He was never a member of GRAV: he worked in the same Paris orbit, exhibited at Denise Rene, and took part in Nouvelle Tendance collective exhibitions alongside the GRAV men, but he is on no GRAV roster.

## The life: the husband who shared the box and the word

Gregorio Vardanega was born on 21 March 1923 in Possagno, Italy, and died in Paris on 7 October 2007, aged 84 (C7802). His family moved to Buenos Aires when he was three years old, and he studied at the Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes in Buenos Aires from 1939 to 1946, graduating as a professor of drawing (C7801). The French Wikipedia gives the emigration year as 1926, consistent with the move at age three, and adds that he was introduced early to engraving and drawing in the workshop of one of his brothers (C7803). He worked in Argentina and then in France, and he is remembered as one of the central figures of Latin American kinetic art, above all for light boxes and programmed luminous sculptures built around colour, movement and electronics (C7801).

Vardanega entered the Buenos Aires avant-garde through Tomas Maldonado, who introduced him to the European avant-garde and to the circles of concrete art then revolutionizing Argentine art (C7803). In 1946 he took part in the exhibitions organized by the Asociacion Arte Concreto-Invencion (AACI), and he joined that association from 1946, attending its meetings and discussions at Maldonado's home (C7801, C7803). In 1947 he took part with Juan Del Prete, Maldonado, Juan Mele and Lidy Prati, among others, in the first "Arte Nuevo" exhibition organized by Carmelo Arden Quin (C7803). His earliest three-dimensional works of this period were half-spheres dressed with a network of taut wires and "paintings in space" made by juxtaposing several Plexiglas plates (C7803).

In 1948 he traveled to Europe with Arden Quin, according to the Sicardi biography and the LIFA curriculum vitae (C7801, C7804). The French Wikipedia gives a slightly different account, saying he went to Paris in September 1948 on a study trip with Mele and at the same time as Arden Quin (C7803). The trip mattered: a year later he showed work at the Salon d'Amerique Latine in Paris, and the exhibition put him in contact with Denise Rene, Georges Vantongerloo, Nicolas Pevsner, Sonia Delaunay, Max Bill and Constantin Brancusi (C7801). The French account adds Michel Seuphor, Bruno Munari and Picabia to the list of artists he visited, and notes that the catalogue of the 1948 Salon des Realites Nouvelles reproduced one of his works, that he exhibited at the Salon d'Amerique latine in 1949, and that he showed with the Madistes at the Galerie Colette Allendy in 1950 (C7803). Back in Buenos Aires he began making his earliest kinetic works, using metal bands and celluloid (C7801).

Vardanega was a founding member of the Asociacion Arte Nuevo in 1955 and, the following year, of the Agrupacion de Artistas No Figurativos Argentinos, known as ANFA (C7801, C7804). The French Wikipedia agrees on 1956 for the creation of ANFA with Martha Boto (C7803). Two Boto sources give 1957 instead: the English Wikipedia entry for Boto says she started ANFA in 1957 alongside Vardanega, and the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection says she joined ANFA in 1957 (C7806, C7807). The disagreement is flagged below; 1956 is the date given by the Vardanega biographies, while 1957 is the date given by the Boto biographies. In 1957 he was also included in the group exhibition "14 Pintores abstractos" at the Galeria Galatea in Buenos Aires (C7801). His first kinetic piece using an electric motor was exhibited at the Estimulo de Bellas Artes gallery in October 1957 (C7802, C7803). He was included in the IV Bienal de Sao Paulo, and he won the gold medal at the Exposition Universelle et Internationale de Bruxelles (C7801). The Brussels medal is dated 1958 by the current Sicardi biography and by both Wikipedias, but the older LIFA biography dates it 1957 (C7801, C7802, C7803, C7804).

Martha Boto was born on 27 December 1925 in Buenos Aires and died in Paris on 13 October 2004 (C7806). She studied drawing and painting at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes, graduating in 1950, and she was a co-founder of the Group of Non-Figurative Artists of Argentina and a pioneer of kinetic and programmed art (C7806). The two artists' lives were intertwined for half a century, as partners in life and in work, and the sources describe the relationship in overlapping but not identical terms: the English Wikipedia for Vardanega calls Boto "his companion" (C7802); the English Wikipedia for Boto lists Vardanega as her spouse and says she moved to Paris in 1959 "with her husband and collaborator Gregorio Vardanega" (C7806); the French Wikipedia for Vardanega lists Boto as his spouse ("conjoint") (C7803); LACMA calls Vardanega her "soon-to-be husband" at the time of the 1956 ANFA founding (C7808); and the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection says she joined the Grupo Arte Nuevo in 1956 along with Vardanega, "whom she married the following year," which implies a marriage in 1957 (C7807). No source consulted states an exact marriage date, so 1957 is an implied year only, and 1959 is the year by which the sources first plainly call him her husband. The two worked side by side from the mid-1950s. Together, Vardanega and Boto created the term "chromocinetisme" to describe their artistic research into colour, light and movement (C7802, C7803). The Peggy Guggenheim Collection page for Boto calls them the first artists in Argentina to create works of art that involved movement (C7805) -- attributed, not asserted as settled fact.

In 1959 Vardanega moved to Paris with Martha Boto, and there he began experimenting with Plexiglas spheres illuminated with moving projections of coloured lights (C7801, C7804). All his subsequent work explored light, movement, colour and electronic programs, and he was especially drawn to the cultural phenomenon of machines that "think" (C7801). He thought of his work as being in dialogue with architecture and urban planning, hoped his towers and light works would be accompanied by music and other modes of performative work, and considered many of the sculptures to be prototypes for large-scale public projects (C7801).

One sentence in the English Wikipedia must be corrected. It says: "In 1959 Vardanega moved to Paris, where he met the Argentinian artist Martha Boto" (C7802). That is wrong. They were already partners and collaborators in Buenos Aires before 1959: they co-founded ANFA together in 1956, Boto's biography says she moved to Paris in 1959 with her husband and collaborator (C7806), the Sicardi biography says he moved to Paris "with Martha Boto" (C7801), LACMA says "three years later the couple relocated to Paris" after the 1956 ANFA founding (C7808), and the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection says "in 1959, she moved to Paris with Vardanega" (C7807). The Peggy Guggenheim Collection likewise says "Vardanega and Boto chose to move to France in 1959 to further their artistic pursuits" (C7805). The "where he met" wording appears to be a mistranslation or careless edit that has been copied into derivative pages, and it should not be repeated.

In Paris he quickly entered the circle of the Galerie Denise Rene, which represented him and showed his work regularly (C7802, C7803). His first major exhibition in Paris, "Chromocinetisme" (1964), was a two-person show with Boto at the Maison des Beaux-Arts in Paris (C7801, C7804). Both Wikipedias instead call the 1964 Paris show his "first solo exhibition," saying it established him as an important practitioner of kinetic art (C7802, C7803). The two accounts cannot both be literally true of the same show, and the Juan Carlos Maldonado Collection adds a third version, calling the 1964 Maison des Beaux-Arts show Boto's first solo exhibition in Paris (C7807). The most likely reading is that a single joint 1964 show at the Maison des Beaux-Arts has been misdescribed as a solo show in each artist's separate biography, but the sources do not settle whether there were two distinct 1964 shows at the same venue. What is secure is that 1964 was his breakthrough year in Paris, that the show carried the joint term chromocinetisme, and that Denise Rene gave him his largest solo exhibition in 1969 (C7803).

His Paris work of the 1960s was built on programmed light. "Electronic Universe" of 1958 already allowed the viewer to interact directly and modify the work through the evolution of colours (C7802). "Couleurs Sonores" (Sound Colours), dated 1963-1979, was his design for a futuristic city of skyscrapers: a work 4.6 metres high that was permanently installed in his studio, was too tall for the intended gallery, could not be included in the 2015 Cosmic Dialogues exhibition, and was donated to the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston by his estate (C7802, C7809). Its mechanism used plastic sleeves over clear bulbs to create coloured lights, sequenced by a music-box-like drum hitting a row of switches (C7809). Another major work, "Espaces chromatiques carrees en spirale" (Chromatic Spaces Turning in a Spiral) of 1968, used offset white square panels receding in size, with coloured lights at each corner producing a spiral effect, driven by a motor-driven analog sequencing system with a cylinder of hand-carved white plastic disks turning switches on and off (C7809). Boto's work shared materials with his: when her "Deplacements optiques" (1967-69) needed bulbs, they were the same type he had used, and were on hand in the conservation lab (C7809).

From the mid-1970s he devoted himself essentially to a geometric and purified painting, while also making many abstract sculptures intended mainly for public spaces; his last creations were paintings with reliefs in vivid colours and studied arrangements (C7803). He lived in France from 1959 until his death in Paris in 2007 (C7802). Boto died in Paris on 13 October 2004, three years before him (C7805). In 2006 the Sicardi Gallery in Houston dedicated an exhibition to both of them, "Contact: Le cyber-cosmos de Boto et Vardanega" (C7805), and a joint show, "Gregorio Vardanega Martha Boto," followed at Bartha Contemporary in London in 2008 (C7801).

The Sicardi exhibition list records, among others: Galeria H, Buenos Aires, 1959; "Trente Argentins de la Nouvelle Generation," Galerie Creuze, Paris, 1962; "L'Art Latino-americain," Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1963; "Lumiere, Mouvement et Optique," Palais des Beaux-Arts, Brussels, 1964; "Mouvement 2," Galerie Denise Rene, Paris, 1964; "Lumiere et Mouvement," Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1967; "Light Motion Space," Walker Art Center, Minneapolis, and Albright-Knox Art Gallery, Buffalo, 1967; Galerie Denise Rene, Paris, 1969; "L'Amerique Latine a Paris," Grand Palais, Paris, 1982; "Electra," Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1983; "L'Art en Mouvement," Fondation Maeght, Saint-Paul de Vence, 1992; "Real/Virtual, Arte Cinetico Argentino de los Anos Sesenta," Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires, 2012; "Constructed Dialogues," Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, 2012; "Dynamo: A Century of Light and Motion in Art, 1913-2013," Grand Palais, Paris, 2013; "Lo[s] Cinetico[s]," Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofia, Madrid, 2007; "Kinesthesia: Latin American Kinetic Art, 1954-1969," Palm Springs Art Museum, 2017; and "Line into Space," Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, 2020 (C7801). The French Wikipedia adds group shows including "Realites Nouvelles" 1961, "Nouvelles Tendances, Zagreb and Venice" 1963, "Art Argentin Actuel" 1964, "Kinetik II" Dusseldorf 1964, the Paris Biennale 1967, "Latin American Artists of the Twentieth Century," Museum of Modern Art, New York, 1990, and "L'oeil moteur," Strasbourg, 2005 (C7803).

His works are represented in collections including the Heide Museum of Modern Art, Melbourne; the Kemper Museum of Contemporary Art, Kansas City; the Milwaukee Art Museum; the Musee d'Art Moderne de Paris; the Musee National d'Art Moderne - Centre Pompidou, Paris; the Museo de Arte Latinoamericano de Buenos Aires (MALBA); the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires; the Museu de Arte Moderna do Rio de Janeiro; the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston; and Muzeon Tel Aviv Leomanut, Tel-Aviv (C7801). The Sicardi curriculum vitae adds the Cisneros Fontanals Art Foundation (CIFO), Miami; the Fonds National d'Art Contemporain, Paris; the Museo de Arte Contemporaneo Latinoamericano (MACLA), La Plata; the Museum of Geometric and MADI Art, Dallas; the Recklinghausen Museum; the Rembrandt van Rijn Foundation, South Africa; and the Tel-Aviv Museum of Art (C7801). On the market, a Christie's Latin American Art sale listed his "Pintura," oil on canvas, at lot 59, with an estimate of $50,000-$70,000 and a price realized of $68,750 (C7812). A Sotheby's lot from the Denise Rene sale, "Sans titre," executed in 1957-1958, is a painted wood box with metal, tinted Plexiglas and light bulbs, 90 x 100 x 20 cm (C7811). That object matters for the chronology: it shows him building light boxes with tinted Plexiglas and bulbs before the Paris move, not only after it.

## M1 -- The wire spiral: a spiral built in real space, not drawn

The earliest works in this study are discs with wires, not pictures of spirals. Spirales Spatiales (bleue) of 1947 (W01, C7851) is a deep-blue circular disc on a small black stand, 71.8 x 26 cm, thin wires strung across its face with small pale-blue beads at intervals; the spiral is implied by the wire, not drawn. Spirales Spatiales blanche of 1954-60 (W03, C7853), also called Developpement d'un carre dans le nombre d'or, is a white circular disc, 70.2 x 40 cm, dense with fine black and coloured wires -- gold, blue, red -- strung in straight lines and arcs across the face, forming triangular and curved intersections, with two small black triangles. The French Wikipedia describes the same early vocabulary: half-spheres dressed with a network of taut wires and "paintings in space" made by juxtaposing several Plexiglas plates (C7803). The mechanism is string geometry on a disc: straight taut lines, crossing at angles, read by the eye as a spiral and as depth, because the wires sit in front of the disc and cast their own small shadows. Nothing moves yet. The movement is optical and bodily: the viewer walks, the wires shift against the disc, the spiral re-forms. Constelacion Fantasia Cromatica of 1954 (W02, C7852), a flat painting on a black ground, shows the same idea before it left the canvas: a large thin circle made of small blue rectangles, open at the top, with scattered small coloured squares and rectangles floating inside and around it -- colour points in dark space, a constellation.

**Takeaway:** the spiral is the first constant. It begins as wire on a disc, becomes a spiral of offset squares lit from the corners (M5), and ends as a tower of stacked plates (M6). A piece that uses Vardanega should build its spiral out of real lines in space -- wires, rods, edges -- and let the eye do the turning.

## M2 -- The motor arrives: movement becomes a component

His first kinetic piece using an electric motor was exhibited at the Estimulo de Bellas Artes gallery in October 1957 (C7854). No image of it survives in the sources consulted, no title is attested, no medium beyond the motor itself -- but the date is the hinge of the whole career. Sicardi's biography adds that on returning to Buenos Aires he began his earliest kinetic works using metal bands and celluloid (C7801). Before 1957 the movement in his work was implied: wire geometry, colour points, the viewer's own walking. After 1957 the movement is in the object: a motor turns something, and the work changes while the viewer stands still. That is the whole difference between a kinetic painting and a kinetic work, and Vardanega crosses it in Buenos Aires, two years before Paris, not in Paris. The Sotheby's Sans titre of 1957-58 (C7811) shows the same crossing from the other side: a painted wood box with metal, tinted Plexiglas and light bulbs, 90 x 100 x 20 cm -- a light box already, before the move.

**Takeaway:** the motor is not decoration on a sculpture; it is the moment the work stops being an object to look at and becomes a system that changes itself. A piece should mark that crossing plainly: first the still spiral, then the motor switches on and the same spiral starts to turn.

## M3 -- The viewer changes the colour: Electronic Universe, 1958

Electronic Universe of 1958 (W05, C7855) allowed the viewer "to interact directly to modify the work through the evolution of colors" (C7802). No image was found, no medium or dimensions are stated, and the gold medal at the Brussels International Exposition came the same year -- but the mechanism as described is the first interactive work in this study, and the first in which the viewer, not a fixed program, drives the colour change. Read it against what came before and after: in 1957 the motor moved the work; in 1958 the viewer moves the colours; in the 1960s the program moves the lights on a fixed sequence. Vardanega tried all three drivers in three years -- motor, viewer, program -- and kept all three. The Plexiglas work of 1960 (W06, C7856) sits between them: small spheres inside Plexiglas, illuminated by projections of lights, the spheres themselves still, the light moving across them. Studio photographs from 2006 show clear Plexiglas spheres among other works in the Paris studio, but they do not identify the 1960 work, so it stays prose-only.

**Takeaway:** interaction, for Vardanega, is a hand on the colour evolution, not a button that restarts the piece. A piece should let the viewer retune the colour sequence itself -- drag to shift the palette, and the program re-composes around the new colours -- never click-to-reload, never a reset switch.

## M4 -- The programmed light box: a grid of cells, each lit in turn

The Paris boxes of 1963-69 share one kit of parts: a wood box, Plexiglas, bulbs, a motor, a programmed sequence. The forms narrow to three -- the grid of squares, the circle, the tower -- and the colour is produced by light, not pigment, so the work is finished only when switched on. Diffraction spectrale IV "Diamants" of 1965/94 (W10, C7860) is a black box, 80.3 x 80.5 x 32.7 cm, its face set with 25 round glass discs in a 5 x 5 grid, each disc catching light like a cut diamond, motor and bulbs behind them driving the diffraction and colour change. Polychromie Electronique 1 of 1965-70 (W11, C7861) is a white square panel, 100.6 x 100.2 x 19.5 cm, carrying a 6 x 6 grid of small coloured cubes -- blue, green, orange, pink, yellow, red, violet, black -- each cube with a small lit point at its centre, some lit and some dark in the still image: 36 separate colour cells on one panel, each independently lit. Multiplication Electronique II and III of 1966 (W12, C7862; W13, C7863) are shallow white wooden boxes, 4 x 4 compartments, each holding a smaller nested square box with a lit bulb, some bulbs on in warm yellow, some off, in an irregular pattern; the Houston Press description of the 2006 Sicardi show reads exactly as the mechanism: "a shallow wooden box is set on end and divided into squares by a wooden grid, which is inset with square boxes within square boxes. Lights in and behind them turn off and on to create repeating patterns." The two Multiplications look near-identical in still photographs and are distinguished only by caption and dimensions -- a warning that the still is not the work. Echecs electroniques chromatiques of 1965 (W09, C7859), wood, Plexiglas and electrical system, 60 x 60 x 17.5 cm, signed and inscribed "220 V" on the reverse, is the same grid idea in chessboard format: the title says electronic chromatic chess, but no source describes the board layout, so none is claimed here. Electronic Relief of 1964 (W08, C7858) is white wood with 10 cylindrical lamps, the support using square and circle repeated for optical effect.

The sequencing mechanism itself is documented by the Getty conservation chapter: Couleurs Sonores used plastic sleeves over clear bulbs to create coloured lights, sequenced by a music-box-like drum hitting a row of switches (C7809); Espaces chromatiques carrees en spirale used a motor-driven analog sequencing system with a cylinder of hand-carved white plastic disks turning switches on and off (C7809). A drum, a cylinder, a row of switches: the program is a physical object, hand-carved, turning at motor speed. The sequence is slow enough to follow -- one cell, then the next, then two together -- and the still photograph, with some cells lit and some dark, is one frame of that slow rhythm.

**Takeaway:** the light box is a grid of cells and a drum that switches them. A piece should build the grid first, then the drum: a visible sequence, slow enough to follow, each cell lit in turn, the pattern repeating but never quite the same because the drum's timing is mechanical. What to avoid: a flashing sign, a light show, a steady glow. The rhythm is the picture.

## M5 -- Chromatic space: colour made by light, not by paint

Espaces chromatiques of 1967 (W15, C7865), painted wood and electrical system, 62 x 62 x 36 cm, is a black square field carrying a 3 x 3 grid of large magenta and pink discs, each disc holding overlapping pale lens shapes that shift from cell to cell, like nine circular windows with moving coloured glass behind them. The related Museum of Fine Arts, Houston work, Espaces Chromatiques Carrees en Spirale (Chromatic Square Spaces Turning in a Spiral) of 1968, uses offset white square panels receding in size, with coloured lights at each corner producing a spiral effect (C7809). Cercles Chromatiques en Progression of 1967 (W14, C7864), wood, Plexiglas, light bulbs and electric motor, 71 x 71 x 36 cm, Princeton University Art Museum, is described in the museum record as a "cone shaped object with descending circle cuts down to center; behind plexi glass and mounted in wooden box. Concentric circular bands light up in programmed changes of color." Metamorphose Chromatique I of 1968 (W16, C7866), a wooden case inset with layers of wooden panels, concentric circles cut into them off centre, forming a vortex ending in black, multicoloured lights illuminating each level, was called by the Houston Press "Vardanega's most elegant and hypnotic work" -- "a mesmerizing psychedelic effect." The word he and Boto coined for all of this, chromocinetisme, names the mechanism exactly: colour (chromo) in motion (cinetisme). The colour is not applied to a surface; it is produced by light passing through sleeves, discs and Plexiglas, and it changes because the light changes. That is why the same spiral that began as wire on a disc in 1947 can reappear as a spiral of lit squares in 1968 without changing its identity: the form is constant, the means of colouring it is what moves.

**Takeaway:** make the colour out of light, not out of paint. A piece should put its colour behind glass or Plexiglas, light it from inside, and change the light -- the surface stays white or black, and the colour arrives and leaves. The spiral of offset squares, lit from the corners, is the signature form.

## M6 -- The tower: the light box stands up and becomes a city

From the start Vardanega thought of his work in dialogue with architecture and urban planning, hoped his towers and light works would be accompanied by music and other modes of performative work, and considered many of the sculptures to be prototypes for large-scale public projects (C7801). Couleurs Sonores (W07, C7857), dated 1963-1979, is that ambition in its rawest form: a luminous sculpture 4.6 metres high, a design for a futuristic city of skyscrapers, permanently installed in his studio, too tall for the intended gallery, unable to be included in the 2015 Cosmic Dialogues exhibition, donated to the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston by his estate (C7802, C7809). No image of it was found. What was found, and viewed, is the ambition finally built at standing height: Tour Orthogonale of 1987 (W20, C7870), aluminum, Plexiglas, wood, light bulbs and motor, 189.2 x 81.9 x 81.9 cm -- a tall open tower of thin vertical metal rods on a black plinth, carrying stacked horizontal plates in pink, orange, teal and white, like the floors of a skyscraper seen from below. The Houston Press called it "a skyscraper-like aluminum structure with illuminated 'floors' made from white opaque Plexiglas." Between the two stands Sans Titre of 1969 (W18, C7868), aluminum, glass, metal, wood, light bulbs and motor, 137.5 x 35.9 x 35.9 cm: three tall perforated aluminum tubes of different heights on thin metal legs over a black plinth, the tubes pierced with rows of small holes and studded with small lights -- the tallest work in this list before the tower, the light box become a standing column. The tower is the box turned on end and grown to the height of a room: same kit of parts, same programmed light, but now the viewer looks up at it, and the floors light in sequence like windows in a building at dusk.

**Takeaway:** build the tower as a small city, not as a tall box. Floors of opaque Plexiglas, lit in sequence, the tower open so the light escapes between the plates, the viewer below looking up. The scale shift is the point: the same mechanism that fit in a 33 cm cube now stands 189 cm tall, and the piece should let the eye climb it floor by floor.

## M7 -- The multiple: the edition is the same principle twice over

Denise Rene published Vardanega's smaller light works as editions, and the edition is the same principle as the grid: one module repeated until it becomes a system. Carres Lumineux of 1969 (W17, C7867), electric lighting system with white painted panels in a black metal case, 34 x 33 x 24 cm, edition of 200, is a black metal cube, open at the front, holding three nested white square frames receding to a small black square at the centre, like a tunnel of light: no colour, only nested squares and light, the white panels the light source and the subject at once. Metamorphose Chromatique I (W16, C7866) was issued as Denise Rene edition 33/200, 33 x 33 x 33 cm. Scintillement electronique of 1969, edition 15 of 200, metal, motor and glass, 33 x 33 cm, is a black-sided metal box with a silver face set with a grid of small lit points. Couleurs en diffusion of 1969, metal sculpture and PVC, luminous electrical system, 34 x 33 x 17.5 cm, Editions Denise Rene, work no. 30/200, is the same scale again. The cube of about 33 cm, the edition of 200, the black case, the white or silver face: a format as strict as a record sleeve, and inside it the same mechanisms as the one-off boxes -- grids, circles, nested squares, sequenced light. The multiple is not a lesser version of the unique work; it is the mechanism made repeatable, the way the grid makes one square repeatable.

**Takeaway:** repetition is the method twice over -- the module repeats inside the box, and the box repeats in the edition. A piece should hold one strict format (a 33 cm cube, a black case, a white face) and let the mechanism vary inside it; or show the edition as a wall of near-identical cubes, each one running its own sequence.

## M8 -- Flat paint keeps the system: the Chromographies

From the mid-1970s Vardanega devoted himself essentially to a geometric and purified painting (C7803), and the Chromographies are what that looks like when the motor is taken away and the vocabulary stays. Chromographie x4: Graphisme chromatique sur carres of 1974 (W19, C7869) is dense vertical stripes in blue, red, green, pink and orange over a field of nested squares in pale blue, green and pink, the stripes running uninterrupted across the squares so the squares seem to sit behind a coloured grille. After a decade of motorised light boxes, the work is flat paint again, but it keeps the kinetic vocabulary: stripes, nested squares, receding centre, colour as the moving element. The viewer supplies the movement now, by eye movement across the stripes. The same system continues in Chromography x3 of 1973-74 (vertical multicolour stripes on white, square format), Chromographie x6 of 1975 (vertical multicolour stripes with a dark horizontal band across the middle), Chromographie Paris of 1986 (vertical coloured line segments on a white ground), and Cites Metaphoriques of 1980 (floating coloured hollow cubes, wireframe and solid, on a blue-green ground). His last creations were paintings with reliefs in vivid colours and studied arrangements (C7803). The arc of the career is therefore a loop, not a line: flat geometric painting in the 1950s, wire constructions, motor, interactivity, programmed light, towers -- and then flat painting again, but a flat painting that has learned everything the machines taught it. The stripes are the light sequence, painted; the nested squares are the light box, painted; the movement is the eye's, not the motor's.

**Takeaway:** the system survives the removal of the machine. A piece should be able to turn its motor off and still move: stripes across nested squares, the eye doing the work the drum used to do. If a painted panel does not shimmer when the eye crosses it, the system has not survived.

## The aesthetic logic: light is the material, the viewer is part of the circuit

Vardanega's work has one material and it is not Plexiglas or wood or aluminum. It is light, and specifically light that changes on a schedule the viewer can follow. The Plexiglas, the bulbs, the motors, the drums and the hand-carved plastic disks are all means of producing, filtering and timing light; the box, the grid and the tower are all housings for light to happen in. That is why his forms are so few -- the spiral, the square, the circle, the tower -- and why they repeat for forty years: the form is a housing, and the housing does not need to change when the light inside it changes. The spiral of 1947 is the spiral of 1968 is the tower of 1987, because the spiral is a shape that reads as movement even when it is still, and the tower is a spiral stood on end. Colour, for him, is not a property of a surface but an event in time: a sleeve over a bulb, a disc turning in front of a lamp, a cell switching on. The viewer is part of the circuit in three different ways across the career, and a piece that uses him should choose one and honour it: the viewer who walks around the wire disc and re-forms the spiral by moving; the viewer who changes the colour evolution of Electronic Universe by hand; the viewer who stands still and watches the drum's sequence, the eye following the rhythm. What he never does is decorate. There is no ornament in the boxes, no pattern for its own sake, no colour that is not produced by the mechanism. The black case exists so the light inside it reads; the white panel exists so the coloured cubes read against it; the tower is open so the light escapes between the floors. Subtraction is the design: one box, one grid, one drum, one sequence, and the patience to let the sequence play.
