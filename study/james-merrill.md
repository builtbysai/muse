## The life: twenty years of code before the plotter

The biography is unusually well documented for a plotter artist, because he tells it himself at length: the Art Blocks interview (November 2022, read end to end), his own BUSY essay (April 2025), two OpenSea interviews (April and December 2025), and a 411-line portfolio PDF hosted at thecurrentnow.org (the host's identity was not established, C2305). The study does not fill the gaps those sources leave. The name itself is a trap: search is dominated by the American poet James Ingram Merrill (1926-1995, Pulitzer 1977), and every fact here is about the living Vermont artist unless stated otherwise (C2310).

James Merrill was born in 1987 -- single source, his own portfolio PDF (C2300). He is a third-generation artist: "Both she [his mother] and my grandfather were art teachers. They both had studios in their homes and plenty of things to play around with." He lives in a 150-year-old farmhouse in rural Vermont with his spouse, daughter, cat, and dog -- that family detail is 2022-vintage, from the Art Blocks interview, and may be out of date (C2316). His studio is in the historic Batell Block building in downtown Middlebury, Vermont. His site's live counter, read 2026-11-15, says his drawing robots have spent 2,352 hours drawing 137,746.262 metres of lines -- a live counter, so the numbers will have moved since (C2370).

He has, in his own words, "a partial high school education" and no formal education in mathematics (C2301). Do not expand the schooling phrase into "high-school dropout" or any other gloss; no source says more. He is a self-taught coder throughout, and a professional programmer for years alongside the art: data-visualisation work in Flash (GPS bike data, music wave spectrums), then web development. Paragraph reports he "gave up his programming day job to become a full-time generative artist" after the -GRAPH invite -- a narrative summary, not his quote, and no date is pinned (C2303).

The start is dated and specific. Around 2002/03 a family friend introduced him to Newgrounds.com; soon after he got his own copy of Macromedia Flash. "I immediately began creating abstract designs and animations. Flash came bundled with Actionscript, and I started learning how to program to make my own websites." He made daily artwork in Flash from about 2003, and went on to collaborate with digital artists worldwide in the collectives EvokeOne and Depthcore, which "pushed the envelope in digital art by doing curated group releases together." The EvokeONE Digital Art exhibition opened in Baltimore City in 2011; the portfolio CV lists Depthcore "Chapter XLIV: Echos" (2012), "Chapter XLV: Primal" (2013), EvokeONE "Forever" (2016). The earliest surviving dated work in the catalogue is an untitled 2003 Flash drawing: a burst of sharp red shards radiating from a dense knot -- the spiky, shard-like geometry that recurs in his later folded work.

The 2010s run through digital painting -- "I developed a style mimicking mixed media brushwork" -- until painting felt stale. "I returned to my 3D roots and began deeply exploring the nature of simulating physics with tools and making artistic outputs. My design goal was to start with realistic fluid dynamics simulations, but combine unusual forces and emphasize color to create psychedelic visuals." The short films OCHRE and Waveforms 1 and 2 came out of this phase (both undated in every source read, C2352, C2353). He names the Navier-Stokes equations as the theoretical horizon: "Could we use math to forecast any turn of events? From a drip of water to a waterfall? The Navier-Stokes equations are theoretically able to do this with fluids."

## The turn: the plotter, 2018

The turn has a date, a website, and a machine. "My sentiment changed in 2018 when I randomly caught a post on HackerNews about #plottertwitter. My mind was blown by these strange drawing robots that artists would use to plot generative art onto paper. Soon after, I bought my Axidraw from EvilMadScientist and downloaded Processing... I began investing hundreds of hours into learning everything I could about generative programming." A later interview says he "ended up buying a pen plotter in 2019" -- the two years differ between interviews, likely the same purchase remembered differently, or two purchases (C2317). Do not state a single year as certain. The Processing syntax, he says, "reminded me of the drawing APIs in Actionscript" -- the Flash years were the training, twenty years before anyone called it that.

The plotter imposed the discipline that defines everything after it. His own formulation, from the OpenSea BUSY interview: "Plotters only accept lines, and they do not care about layering." A plotter has no depth: overlapping shapes show both, front and back at once. Everything he built after 2018 is, in one way or another, an answer to that sentence. The occlusion solver (M4), the layering (M1), the wandering line (M5), the hand-drawn sprites (M8) -- all of them are ways of getting depth, order, and warmth out of a machine that draws lines and does not care.

He entered NFTs in early 2021, when his friend Justin Maller's Nifty Gateway drop "rekindled the Depthcore community" -- Maller gave him his first NFT and crypto tutorial and helped set up his MetaMask (Maller's drop and the revival were not independently verified, C2312) -- and Merrill took part in two Depthcore Nifty Gateway releases, listed in the CV as "Frontiers" (his individual contributions are undescribed, C2354). Casey Reas invited him to -GRAPH, which opened November 30, 2021. His submission was Chaos Blocks. He entered Art Blocks Curated in November 2022 with ORI. He has, by his own count, aimed to "develop 1-2 new algorithmic systems per year," each tuned to a set number of drawings, "ranging from as few as 5 to as many as 50," and artworks "may take days to complete."

## The practice: the geometry and the rendering engine

Three load-bearing ideas organise everything he makes, all in his own words or in the words of the people who write about him.

First, emotion inside logic. His artist statement (portfolio PDF): "My work serves as a continuous exploration into ways in which emotion can be embedded within logic systems. I utilize technology as a device to create feelings -- to impart them onto paper and canvas." And: "I believe that digital art can move beyond screens. It should be held, touched, and hold space within a room." The three pillars in the same document: Computation ("There is a very real sense of infiniteness when exploring algorithmic spaces that utilize randomness as a key variable... each drawing being a sample of a larger universe"); Physicality ("pioneers in digital art have utilized pen plotters to realize their explorations into mathematics, abstraction, and artificial intelligence. My practice focuses on this approach to unleash the possibilities of digital art in the physical world"); and "A Fully Incorporated Process" ("With a fully automated, bespoke art studio that utilizes computation and drawing machines, new opportunities arise beyond the workflow of design and production").

Second, mathematics as the enemy and the tool. "Maths is my greatest enemy, and it's also my most used tool. I do not have any formal education in mathematics, so each problem I solve is a significant milestone in my understanding of it. I have engineered a way of implementing maths into code that works for me. It involves creating basic visuals of my problem and slowly using deductive reasoning to solve it." The method is visual debugging: "I don't have a background in math or computer science, but I could basically create scenarios that I could visually debug." On tedium, which the plotter years made central: "I weirdly enjoy tedium. Some of my 3D projects took hundreds of hours to simulate and render. I've spent countless nights running my computer at max speed to wake up to a new JPG sequence. It reminds me of baking cookies."

Third, imperfection as the artist's job. "In computational artwork, perfection is easy. Computers are great at calculations and can do them thousands or millions of times quickly. So, perfection on screen is simple. The role of a generative artist is often to add imperfection digitally. But when you bring art into the physical world, imperfection is everywhere. The flow of ink, the texture of the paper, even the humidity in the room." Chaos Blocks is the programmatic version of the same sentence: he is "reacting against the inhuman perfection that often characterizes visual art created through machines," and reaching back to 1960s plotter drawings that used randomness to circumvent precision.

The tools, in his own account: JavaScript is primary -- "Artwork for me starts as an idea that quickly takes rudimentary form in JavaScript... In the web browser, I iteratively refine my ideas by implementing algorithms, translating mathematical formulas into code, and experimenting with parameters." Processing from 2018. The machines: AxiDraw (later an AxiDraw A1 for BUSY, 5-8 inks, plots up to 16 hours), Nextdraw 2234 large-format units, a Bantam Tools Art Frame Panorama with variable pressure control (60-by-24-inch drawings, sub-millimetre precision), an Egg Bot for spherical plotting on golf balls and baseballs (acquired 2025), and a one-of-a-kind 72-by-24-inch prototype plotter he planned to acquire -- a plan, not a completed fact, and the "one of a kind in the world" claim is his own (C2313). The materials: Strathmore Bristol 400 (Chaos Blocks), Legion Stonehenge 250 gsm white (Breakpoint), Staedtler Triplus Fineliner pens, Pilot fountain pen with extra-fine nib and Pilot Iroshizuku ink; archival inks and papers systematically tested for machine-drawing compatibility.

## The evolution: from shards to cities, via the tube

The arc has four phases, and the constants are the three pillars of the practice: a simulation, a deconstructed geometry, and a plotter that draws lines. What migrates is the scale of the system, and then the amount of hand inside it.

Phase 1, the screen years (2003-2018): Flash shards, digital painting, then fluid simulation. The work is a static image or a film; the machine is a renderer. The Blueprint (2015) -- glossy 3D ribbons over a technical-drawing ground -- and STREET SERIES: RUINTOWN (2016) -- folded neon ribbons pasted over a graffiti wall -- are the two inspected images from this phase. RUINTOWN matters because Merrill says its graffiti-like elements "would later appear again in ORI": the spray-paint simulation in ORI is the graffiti of 2016, rebuilt as an algorithm.

Phase 2, the plotter decade's first systems (2018-2021): A Series of Tubes (system 2019, pictured drawing 2020, C2351) and Subdivider (2020). Tubes is the seed of everything: a dense maze of meandering double-line tubes in pastel pink, mint, orange and blue, threaded with tiny squares, circles, dots and dashes -- "like a circuit board drawn as plumbing" -- interlocking paths that never collide. Merrill: "The system is something I developed in 2019 with a previous artwork called A Series of Tubes. BUSY is a maximal version of that." Subdivider is the hybrid made visible: a column of subdivided rectangles, each cell filled with a different micro-pattern, plotted in black ink and then washed by hand with teal, green and blue watercolour -- the inspected photograph shows the sheet on the plotter bed with fineliners and a watercolour palette beside it. The hand enters the machine work here, and never leaves.

Phase 3, the chain years (2021-2023): the same systems go on-chain, and the edition becomes the work's distribution. Chaos Blocks (30, Feral File, November 2021) turns layering into geology. ORI (450, Art Blocks Curated, November 2022) turns origami into a folding engine with a spray-paint simulation and a destroy key. Breakpoint. (30, +GRAPH, 2023) turns one wandering line into a disruption. RUNAWAY (open edition, Highlight/Base, August 2023) turns randomisation itself into the rarity. Every Moment (2023) turns a random camera position into depth. The Algorithmic Art Studio (SIGGRAPH 2023, Los Angeles) turns the visitor into the operator: twelve unlabeled MIDI knobs, no instructions, 230-plus drawings in five days, each visitor taking theirs home.

Phase 4, the world-generator (2024-2025): BUSY is Tubes grown into a city. Eighty-five hand-drawn illustrations piped into the algorithm, a custom occlusion solver, a proprietary compression that cut the source about 90 percent so it fits on-chain, 50 drawings auctioned April 2025, each plotting 5-8 inks over up to 16 hours. Then BUSIEST: the same system at 60 by 24 inches on the Bantam panorama plotter, five unique works. Then the framework travels: BUSY in Marfa (2024 -- year from the CV only, C2357), where he drove around Google Street View for months documenting water towers, barns and low desert architecture because Marfa has no skyscrapers; BUSY in Istanbul (2025), the same framework re-illustrated for a second city. The system is no longer a picture. It is a framework with a place plugged in.

## M1: The layer that draws on top of the next

Chaos Blocks (2021, -GRAPH, 30 mints; first collector of each receives the corresponding plotter drawing; software and drawing are not linked, so the NFT can trade while the drawing stays with its first collector). The mechanism is the whole work, and toshareproject states it in one sentence: "we see them created on the screen as each layer draws on top of the next. After the layers are created, each is filled with an unexpected, vibrant pattern." The algorithm builds the image layer by layer, each layer drawn on top of the next, then fills each layer with a pattern it did not expect. The result reads like a geological drawing: diagonal strata of hatching, crosshatch, dots, zigzags and wavy lines, mostly black fineliner with pastel pink, blue and yellow accents. The inspected photograph shows one drawing clipped to a dark teal wall -- a vertical rectangle of strata on a large white sheet, signed bottom right. On screen, pressing enter or clicking generates a new image from a different seed; each software NFT embeds "millions of other images," the generative space. The tension the work is named for is order against chaos: the strata are ordered, the fills are not, and the order is what makes the chaos legible. The dimensions conflict between sources (Art Blocks caption 18 by 12 in; Le Random 45.7 by 61 cm, 18 by 24 in, on Strathmore Bristol 400 with Staedtler Triplus Fineliner) and are unresolved (C2350); the Le Random spec is more detailed and probably right.

**Takeaway:** order is the frame that lets chaos be seen. A layer system where each layer is drawn in full before the next arrives, and where the fill of each layer is chosen independently, produces geology out of pure procedure -- the strata are the visible record of the order the layers were laid down.

## M2: Deconstructing geometry through simulation

Reas's sentence is the mechanism stated as a characterisation: "James is deep into simulation and exploring that through deconstructing geometry." The deconstruction attaches to geometry; the simulation is the medium it happens in. Two works show the two halves. ORI (2022) is the simulation half made visible: paper treated as geometry, folds as manipulations of its coordinates, a folding engine generating faceted shards, a spray-paint simulation applying colour with a wandering nozzle that sometimes overflows boundaries and drips downwards. "I created a system that stacks up mutations and randomness. By design, this gives ORI a wide variety of output possibilities. It was my intention to create a system that is geometrically emergent with a style that provides coherence." The inspected ORI #0 shows large dark faceted shards (navy, black, burgundy, with orange spray speckle) on a bright blue field, threaded with dashed fold lines. Fold lines evoke Yoshizawa-Randlett origami diagrams; kirigami -- folding plus cutting -- appears as highly destructured forms. Every Moment (2023) is the geometry half: polygons sliced, diced and bent by curves (solved with basic calculus -- curve shape and point positions); a 3D scene rendered into 2D with matrix transformations, so each generation picks a random camera position "almost as if the photographer was taking a candid shot," adding subtle perspective; element coordinates determining colour from a curated gradient band, pieces closer to the "camera" moving toward the warm end against cooler, further elements. No image of Every Moment was obtained (C2355); the mechanism rests on the Unit London interview prose only, and the link to the "Building Blocks" group show is plausible but unstated anywhere. The fluid films are the origin of the simulation half: realistic fluid dynamics bent by unusual forces, the Navier-Stokes equations as the theoretical horizon, OCHRE and Waveforms 1 and 2 as the films (both undated, C2352, C2353; the Waveforms installation photograph shows luminous blue-white filaments on a dark-blue wall-sized screen in Utrecht).

**Takeaway:** take a geometry apart by simulating the forces that could have made it -- folding, slicing, bending, flowing -- and let the simulation decide the result. The coherence comes from the system, not from any single output; the variety comes from stacking mutations and randomness on top of it.

## M3: The tube that never collides

A Series of Tubes (2019/2020) is the foundational system, and BUSY (2025) is its maximal version. The rule is simple and total: lay out interlocking paths that never collide. Tubes is a dense maze of meandering double-line tubes threaded with tiny squares, circles, dots and dashes; the inspected photograph shows the finished sheet on the plotter bed with the AxiDraw head visible at top left. BUSY takes the same rule and adds a world: interlocking systems generating a god's-eye city -- buildings, roads, parks, cars, trees and abstract elements that react to one another, coexist and build on a shared foundation "without treading on each other." Under the surface: random walkers, Poisson disk sampling, Gaussian distributions. The non-collision rule is what makes the city readable: nothing treads on anything else, so the density stays legible at any scale, from a 30-by-22-inch BUSY sheet to a 60-by-24-inch BUSIEST panorama. The system was sold early on Etsy at about $135 a piece; the inspected BUSY pen-plot close-up shows the plotter head mid-draw over a map of tiny buildings, roads, a Ferris wheel and a cylindrical tower.

**Takeaway:** a non-collision rule is a world-builder. Give every element a path and forbid overlap, and density becomes a city instead of a tangle; the same rule scales from a maze to a metropolis without changing.

## M4: Geometric occlusion -- depth for a machine that has no depth

The plotter's constraint is the mechanism's origin: "Plotters only accept lines, and they do not care about layering." Overlapping shapes show both, front and back at once, so a plotted city has no depth unless the artist builds it. Merrill's answer is geometric occlusion, his own solution, built in JavaScript with visual debugging over about a month, after finding AI assistants and libraries unhelpful for on-chain use (OpenSea BUSY interview). It is what lets things sit in front of other things in BUSY: buildings occlude roads, cars occlude streets, trees occlude buildings, and the city reads as a place with a front and a back instead of a flat tangle of lines. The sprite sheet inspected by leg 2 shows the machinery: about 85 hand-drawn building, vehicle and tree sprites in red bounding boxes, joined by red arcs -- the relationship graph, the occlusion order made visible as a diagram. The mechanism is the reason BUSY can be a Wimmelbilder -- a Where's Waldo logic of one-of-a-kind hidden elements -- instead of a wallpaper: depth is what lets the eye search a scene rather than scan a pattern.

**Takeaway:** when the machine cannot do depth, build the depth yourself as a rule, and make the rule visible. Occlusion is not a rendering trick here; it is the difference between a pattern and a place.

## M5: The wandering line that disrupts

Breakpoint. (2023, +GRAPH, 30 drawings; first collector of each receives the corresponding plotter drawing). The mechanism is a two-step: first the ordered system, then the disruption. It starts with Merrill's signature dense, highly structured geometry, then one single meandering line cuts through the composition and displaces it -- the ordered system is generated, then obliterated to create new forms. The line pierces and punctures the original form. Merrill insists the work is not violent: order and messiness coexist and complement each other. The portfolio PDF states the program: "Breakpoint is a series of 30 drawings that explore the relationship between the stalwart nature of algorithms and the childlike playfulness of a single wandering line, acting as a disruptive force." Le Random: "Each composition in the series uses a single meandering line to create this change." Physical drawings: 24 by 18 in, Legion Stonehenge white 250 gsm paper, Pilot fountain pen (extra-fine nib), Pilot Iroshizuku ink, AxiDraw. On screen, pressing spacebar generates a new image. No image of a Breakpoint drawing was obtained (C2356); the mechanism rests on prose only, and the Feral File artwork page itself was not read. The wandering line is the smallest possible disruption -- one line, not a system -- and that is the point: a single childlike gesture against a stalwart algorithm, and the algorithm has to accommodate it.

**Takeaway:** build the order first, then let exactly one thing break it. A single wandering line, given the power to displace what it crosses, turns a finished geometry into a negotiation between the system and the gesture.

## M6: Randomness as rarity, not consistency

RUNAWAY (2023, Highlight, Base, Onchain Summer, in partnership with Fingerprints DAO; released August 22, 2023; uncapped open edition, seven-day mint window; final mint count unknown, Merrill speculated 5,000-10,000-plus). The mechanism is a deliberate break with long-form generative conventions. Instead of balanced randomisation for consistency -- ten palettes at ten percent each, every output recognisably the same project -- systems upon systems of randomisation stack so outputs diverge wildly: "very, very, very random," with rare traits producing extremely different results, while a shared colour and shape language and subtle pattern use keep outputs relatable to one another. "RUNAWAY is about taking the concept of long-form generative art and the supposed rules around it and breaking them. With this project, I created an algorithm that would run away from a typical [generative art] collection, which looks very familiar from one piece to the other." "I wanted something with the most diversity in any long-form generative art project." The three inspected test outputs prove the divergence: one is a light field of diagonal streaks, navy blobs and an orange hatched zone; the second is a dark purple-navy field of stacked rounded tablets in hot pink, orange and blue; the third is pale grey and white with huge red slabs and diagonal bands -- three outputs that barely look like the same project. The governing rule, in his own words: "As a general rule for generative artists, the worst thing you could do is make everything completely random. A lot of the time it won't look good. The goal is to have a mastery of when to use randomness and how to use it." And: "The key thing is the balance of randomization -- around creating rarity instead of achieving consistency." Mobile-first aspect ratio, designed to be phone wallpapers.

**Takeaway:** randomness is a dial, not a switch. Stack randomisation systems until the outputs diverge wildly, but keep a shared language underneath so they stay one project; the rarity is the point, and the mastery is knowing when to stop adding randomness.

## M7: The destroyable render

ORI's second mechanism, after the folding engine, is the one that makes it a performance rather than a picture. The spray-paint simulation may take many seconds to fully render, and after the render completes, pressing any key destroys the artwork. The destruction is part of the work's contract: the image is built slowly, in front of the viewer, and then the viewer is given the power -- and the responsibility -- of ending it. The graffiti root is explicit: the spray-paint simulation, the wandering nozzle, the overflow and the drips, all descend from the graffiti writers Merrill met under bridges in Winooski and the graffiti-like elements of RUINTOWN (2016). The fold lines evoke Yoshizawa-Randlett origami diagrams, so the work holds two diagram traditions at once: the origami diagram that tells you how to fold, and the graffiti tag that tells you someone was here. The edition is 450 (the Art Blocks page calls it "an Art Blocks 500 release," which is the release-series label, not the edition; 450 is the edition, C2360).

**Takeaway:** a render that can be destroyed is a different object from a render that cannot. Give the viewer a slow build and then a single key that ends it, and the image becomes an event with a lifetime instead of a file with a hash.

## M8: The hand inside the machine

BUSY's most load-bearing mechanism is not algorithmic at all: 85 hand-drawn illustrations, drawn in Adobe Illustrator and saved as SVG files, piped into the algorithm alongside the algorithmic parts that react to them. The buildings, cars and trees are drawn by hand; the system places them, occludes them, and lets them react to one another. The sprite sheet inspected by leg 2 is the evidence: 85 sprites in red bounding boxes joined by red arcs, the relationship graph. A proprietary compression technique cut the roughly 1.5 MB source about 90 percent so the 85 illustrations fit on-chain. The hand was already in the machine in 2020: Subdivider's watercolour washes were applied by hand after plotting, the palette sitting beside the plotter in the photograph. OpenSea's framing of BUSY names the result: "Wimmelbilder-style landscapes that blend the mechanical precision of code with the hand-drawn warmth of illustration." The hand is also the reason the work survives: "It's really important to me because I've been doing this for a number of decades at this point and so many times in the past, the artwork and the artists get lost in time. The people that I grew up making art with when I was a teenager -- I can hardly find their work online anymore." The physical plot is the permanence; the hand is what makes it worth keeping.

**Takeaway:** the algorithm places; the hand draws. Piping hand-drawn assets into a generative system -- and letting the algorithmic parts react to them, not just sit beside them -- is how a machine drawing gets warmth without losing precision.

## M9: The city as a layered system, god's-eye

BUSY (2024-2025; the 2024 start is approximate, C2368; auctioned as 50 individual drawings with tokenised assets, April 2025, Art Blocks Curated; each output plots in 5-8 inks over up to 16 hours on an AxiDraw A1) is a world-generator, and its mechanism is the layering of systems. "I often observe cities as layered systems that we've normalized through familiarity, and I wonder how to capture that totality. I'm interested in offering viewers a god's eye perspective -- not just of urban environments, but of the underlying coexisting elements that form them." The city is not drawn; it is grown from interlocking systems -- buildings, roads, parks, cars, trees, abstract elements -- that react to one another and build on a shared foundation. The finished sheet inspected by leg 2 is an edge-to-edge pale lavender, pink and blue city map on white paper, signed bottom right. The concept, in his own account, is post-COVID urban overwhelm resolving into order: chaos at first glance, systems on closer looking. Videos of BUSY being drawn went viral, over 10 million social views by his portfolio PDF's self-reported claim (C2308). BUSIEST (2025, Verse / Art Blocks Curated, 5 unique works, 60 by 24 in) is the same system at cinematic scale; the inspected wall mockup shows one framed panoramic drawing above a dining table, an extremely dense pastel cityscape reading as a landscape. The framework then travels: BUSY in Marfa re-illustrates the city for a small high-desert town (water towers, barns, low architecture, no skyscrapers); BUSY in Istanbul does the same for a second city (a 5-by-2-foot "BUSIEST" drawing is listed for Istanbul, but whether it is a BUSIEST edition piece or a separate drawing is unclear, C2359).

**Takeaway:** a city is not a picture of buildings; it is layered systems that coexist without treading on each other, seen from above. Build the layers as systems that react, give the viewer the god's-eye view, and let the chaos resolve into order the longer they look.

## M10: The visitor as operator

The Algorithmic Art Studio (2023, SIGGRAPH 2023 Labs, Los Angeles) is the one work in the catalogue where the audience is the mechanism. Visitors use an app and physical MIDI hardware to tune a generative algorithm, then send the result to a plotter. Twelve unlabeled MIDI inputs -- knobs, sliders, dials -- map to algorithm parameters: one slider controls scale, another rotation. There are no instructions, so participants learn by watching the on-screen visuals on accompanying tablets. Five applications capture inputs, save them, and handle a work queue remotely across multiple workstations and plotters. Algorithms were optimised from typical 8-plus-hour plot times down to 5-15-minute full drawing cycles, enabling 230-plus drawings over the five days (the numbers are Merrill's own, from the SIGGRAPH interview, C2362). Some participants stayed 20-30 minutes fine-tuning before sending to the plotter, and took the drawing home as a memento. No image of the installation was obtained; the SIGGRAPH blog thumbnail is a portrait of the artist, and the Adafruit build post was bot-blocked (C2363). The mechanism is the unlabeled knob: the visitor does not know what a control does until they move it and watch the picture change, so the learning is the interaction, and the drawing is the record of what they learned.

**Takeaway:** hide the labels and let the visitor learn the system by moving it. An unlabeled control turns tuning into play, and the plotted drawing becomes a memento of a system the visitor briefly understood.

## The aesthetic logic: warmth against the machine

Merrill's aesthetic is a deliberate argument with his own medium, and the argument has three terms. First, the machine is precise and the artist adds imperfection: "perfection on screen is simple. The role of a generative artist is often to add imperfection digitally." The randomness in Chaos Blocks, the wandering line in Breakpoint, the stacked mutations in ORI, the wild divergence in RUNAWAY -- all of them are imperfection added on purpose, and all of them cite the 1960s plotter drawings that used randomness to circumvent precision. Second, the physical is the point: "digital art can move beyond screens. It should be held, touched, and hold space within a room." The plot is not a print of a digital work; it is the work, and the NFT is the distribution. The studio counter -- 2,352 hours, 137,746 metres of line -- is the aesthetic stated as a number: the drawing is labour, measured in metres. Third, the hand is the warmth: 85 hand-drawn illustrations in BUSY, watercolour on Subdivider, graffiti under ORI, a farmhouse in Vermont, a studio in a historic block. The work is at its best when the three terms are all present at once -- the simulation precise, the imperfection deliberate, the hand visible -- and at its thinnest when one of them drops out, as in the early screen work, which is precise and cold, or the film phase, which is simulated and unheld.

The evolution, stated as one sentence: he spent twenty years learning to make the machine draw, then spent the plotter years teaching the machine to draw like a place -- first a tube that never collides, then a layer that reads like geology, then a city that reads like a city, then a framework that reads like wherever you plug it in.
