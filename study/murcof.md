# Murcof: Study Notes

**Site:** Murcof is the performing and recording name of Mexican electronic musician Fernando Corona (b. Tijuana, 1970; lives in Celra, near Girona, Spain -- not Barcelona, as older bios say; see C3512). The exact birth date, 26 July 1970, and the surname Murillo rest on the Wikipedia infobox alone (C3501). His music is sparse, minimalist electronics built from modern classical material -- first sampled from records, later recorded live, lately synthesised -- set against glitchy percussion, drones and silence. Labels: Static Discos (which he co-founded), The Leaf Label, Context, InFine. The visual half of his practice is not his own: since 2009 it has belonged to Simon Geilfus of AntiVJ, whose custom software draws Murcof's music as galaxies and atoms, and since 2022 to a whole rendered landscape built in video-game software.

## The life: from the border to the farmhouse

Corona was born in Tijuana in 1970 and grew up in Ensenada, a small port city about a hundred miles south, after a few years across the border in Chula Vista, where he went to school in San Diego. The border is the first mechanism in his life: the United States was his "window to the world" -- he had to cross it to obtain records and magazines. His father sang and played bass and accordion and kept Bach, The Beatles and The Carpenters in the house; at eleven a friend of his father gave him a tape of Jean-Michel Jarre's "Oxygene", his introduction to electronic music, and soon after his father gave him Jon Santos playing Bach on a Moog. He bought a Casio sample keyboard in 1985, excited to record and transform snippets of everyday sounds, then a Kawai K1 in 1988, programming sounds on a Commodore 64, writing techno pop, acid house and industrial. He studied cello in Ensenada in 1993-1994 and audio engineering in 1995-1998, played keys in the rock band Sonios from 1991 (their 1998 album "200 Fonios" won Baja California cultural prizes and reached cult status in Mexico, Wikipedia-only, C3518), DJed in Ensenada, and worked from 1992 to 2000 in a nursing agency in San Diego dealing primarily with the elderly and the terminally ill (Wikipedia-only, C3504). In the mid-1990s Xenakis and Ligeti's "Lux Aeterna" altered his perception of music "immediately and drastically".

The turn to Murcof came through Terrestre and the Nortec Collective. In early 1999 he left Sonios to focus on Terrestre, sampling vinyl tambora, danzon and salsa; later that year three Tijuana electronic musicians invited him into what became the Nortec Collective, fusing norteno and tambora with electronic dance music. Terrestre appeared on "The Tijuana Sessions, Vol. 1" (2001); John Peel played "El Maquilazo" that June. He left Nortec in 2002 (Wikipedia-only, C3506), co-founded Static Discos with Ruben Tamayo (Fax) and Ejival, and in 2001 -- during the Terrestre years -- the Murcof project came alive out of the need to explore modern classical territories: the holy minimalism of Arvo Part and Henryk Gorecki, the post-modern atmosphere of Valentyn Sylvestrov. "Murcof was born out of the necessity to do something more private, more personal, more introspective. And then it took off." No source read end to end explains what the word "Murcof" means (C3502). At Sonar in 2001 a friend pushed his Murcof demo CD on Tony Marley of The Leaf Label in a club; that handoff launched him internationally. "Martes" (2002) coincided, in his own account, with the birth of his son; "Remembranza" (2005) was produced during his mother's illness until her death and became her requiem. He moved to Barcelona in 2006, the move "Cosmos" (2007) expresses; around 2015 he moved to Girona and then to Celra, to a three-hundred-year-old farmhouse by a forest, where his Plasma Studio now sits. His daughter Alina sings on Twin Color and, per the album credits, "accidentally" named the project -- how is not explained (C3517).

The career runs in four phases. Phase 1, the sample years (1999-2004): Nortec border samples as Terrestre, then holy-minimalist record samples plus glitchy minimal-techno beats as Murcof. Phase 2, record the players (2005-2008): "Remembranza" replaces record samples with friends playing piano, harp and strings; "Cosmos" drops the beat toward dark ambient; Versailles removes techno entirely. Phase 3, the commissioned years (2006-2021): live dialogue with real players (Singh, Truffaz, Tristano, BCN 216), dance scores for Alias, film scores, piano-plus-electronics duos with Vanessa Wagner -- sound built for a room, a stage, a screen or dancers, not for a club. Phase 4, field recording and synthesis (2015-2025): Etna built from field recordings on the volcano with no electronic beats; Twin Color turns away from sampling altogether toward analog synthesisers, motorik pulse, and a full audio-visual show built with Geilfus at IRCAM. The visual side, marginal in the early records, becomes a co-author of the sound structure.

## M1 -- The cut-up: a record sliced into a beat

The founding mechanism, and the one everything else is a variation on. Take a recording of modern classical music -- Part, Gorecki, Feldman -- slice it into fragments, and set the fragments against sparse, precise, glitchy electronic percussion, often around a 4/4 pulse. The first Murcof track, "MF Relay" (2001-2002), is the recipe in miniature: glitchy minimal-techno percussion, a synth drone, and "sliced and diced" samples from orchestral percussion, choir, piano, strings and woodwinds. On "Martes" (2002) the strings and piano are cut into fragments and repeated like incantation, and long silences are used as structure, in the manner of Part: "Memoria" opens with sombre strings rising against a deep dub-like bass pulse. The classical fragment is the melody; the beat is the frame; the silence is the room the two stand in. The method is the same one Terrestre used on Mexican dance records -- local acoustic source, electronic cut-up -- with classical records replacing tambora and danzon. What makes it Murcof and not a remix is the proportion: the fragments are short, the beat is sparse, and the silence does more work than either.

**Takeaway:** a cut-up is not a collage; it is a melody made of other people's endings, and the silence between the cuts is the composition.

## M2 -- Record the players, not the records

The decisive break, on "Remembranza" (2005) and the "Ulysses" 12-inch (2003) before it. Instead of sampling records, Corona recorded musicians -- friends playing piano, harp and strings in the studio; violin and double bass recorded from Tijuana musicians -- and moulded those recordings into progressive arrangements with more complex, melancholic harmonic progressions. He said he could hardly play the instruments himself, but that did not bother him because he was mainly interested in texture and vibe. The mechanism is the same cut-up as M1, but the source is now a performance made for the piece, so the fragments carry room tone, hesitation and touch instead of another record's mastering. "Recuerdos" drapes string shudders and brooding piano chords over softly clanking rhythms; "Razon" is atonal pianos over percolating rustlings and static. The same rule governs the duo work a decade later: on "Statea" (2016) Vanessa Wagner plays the piano and Corona adds electronics, under Wagner's rule that the electronic effects must not swallow the acoustic piano sound and must stay true to the composers' scores -- Cage's "In a Landscape" echoing and stretching until acoustic and electric are hard to distinguish, Glass's "Metamorphosis" frantic synth loops racing with the piano. The balance between the two sound worlds is the piece, and the rule that protects it is stated out loud.

**Takeaway:** when the source is a performance instead of a record, the cut-up keeps the player's hands in it; and when electronics meet a piano, the rule that the electronics must not swallow the piano is itself a mechanism.

## M3 -- The site as score: composing for a space

The Versailles commission (summer 2007; album "The Versailles Sessions", 2008) is the mechanism in its purest form. Corona was invited to compose a six-speaker piece for the grand evening fountain display in the Jardin du Roi. He and a Parisian baroque ensemble spent two days recording interpretations of Lully, Purcell and Couperin -- played traditionally and with experimentation: modifying score, tempo and pitch, inserting random pauses, throwing material inside the harpsichord, pounding on the viola da gamba's body. Then, two weeks before opening, a site visit in pouring rain prevented sound tests, and he realised he was going in the wrong musical direction: the big space demanded more openness, more time for sounds to develop and resonate. He reworked the whole commission in the remaining two weeks. The result is spacious, empty sound -- slowly unfolding arias and harpsichord solos, cello lines distorted at the edges with otherworldly static -- and the fountains and light are the visual system while the score works "like the sound of thunder, as flashes reflected from shiny cannon balls". The mechanism is a feedback loop between a place and a piece: the space rejects the first version, and the rejection is the composition. The same loop, smaller, runs through the dance scores: Botelho gives Corona carte blanche, they talk about the general intention, then Corona builds the music by following the dancers' progress -- the Alias Sessions (2021) are music made by watching bodies move.

**Takeaway:** a commission is not a brief with a venue attached; the venue edits the music, and the rework the space forces is the work.

## M4 -- The dome as screen

"Cosmos" (2007) is a record about looking up -- "we must see beyond the skyscrapers around us, look above, look [at] the star and the sky" -- and its live form puts the audience inside the image. At the Peter Harrison Planetarium, Greenwich, on 4 October 2007, the dome itself was the visual system: stars appearing one by one, random constellations identified, the nearest planets taking centre stage with orbits traced against the dark backdrop. During "Cosmos I" the display turned psychedelic -- vector renderings of constellations materialising and shooting to the centre of the dome, gaining 3D depth, a circular set of stars twisting into a tube while the camera floated over the roller-coaster formation at increasing speed. During "Cosmos II" planets appeared as pin points against a far sun, grew progressively bigger, then flew left or right and disappeared; Saturn, Jupiter, Pluto and the sun last, then Earth with the Moon in tow. The reviewer describes an intricate correlation between the cosmic stretches on display and the music as Corona layers strings, chimes and bells. The visuals were the planetarium's own show, driven by its own system -- no source states Corona or a collaborator generated them live, and this study claims no live-generation mechanism -- but the mechanism that matters is the choice of venue as instrument: a dome that already knows how to show the sky, borrowed whole, instead of a screen built for the night. The record's own sound follows the same logic: very low bass drones sustaining intervals between lusher orchestral motifs, micro-electronic effects -- a soft high cicada sound, the pinging of a modem -- altering and deepening the themes, and "Oort" perverting Beethoven's Fifth, a massive orchestral hit followed by a flutter of string harmonics.

**Takeaway:** when the subject is the sky, do not build a screen; borrow a building that already shows it, and let the music be the narration over an image the room already knows how to make.

## M5 -- The live-sampling dialogue

"Oceano" (commissioned early 2008, UK tour November 2008) is the mechanism as a conversation. The Spanish classical ensemble BCN 216 -- viola, cello, trombone -- plays notes from a score; Corona samples them live and manipulates them, playing them back alongside the live musicians' sound, adding his own pre-made and collected noises. The musicians have monitors displaying Corona's arrange window, so they can see what he is doing at his laptop. Corona called it new territory and "primarily a technical dialogue between the electronica and the acoustic instruments". The visual half, by Flicker (Roderick Maclachlan), is a second dialogue of the same shape: camera feeds from micro-installations -- a flat surface of water oscillating under a bright light, projected as criss-crossing psychedelic ripples; a plant rotating on a turntable in a light box, seen as interlocking leaves and kaleidoscope; powder falling into bright light from a powderpuff connected to a subwoofer; dust, smoke, dark water and live scratch animations -- manipulated with feedback effects and projected onto a huge white circle suspended halo-like behind the artists. Corona on the sync: "The synchronising with Flicker for Oceano is more thematic than technical... Mostly, though, it is about having fun." Two rules fall out of it. First, the sampler is visible: the players watch the arrange window, so the manipulation is part of the performance, not a secret behind the laptop. Second, the visual source is microscopic and physical -- water, dust, a plant -- blown up to fill a hall, the same scale jump Geilfus later makes digital. The mechanism is dialogue, not accompaniment: both sides play, both sides listen, and the audience watches the listening happen.

**Takeaway:** a live sampler is not a playback device; it is a third player, and the piece is the conversation between the players and the machine that eats their notes.

## M6 -- One scene per track: the sound-bound scene

The Geilfus live show (first gig Futuresonic, Manchester, 2009; a couple of dozen shows by 2012) is the mechanism the whole study turns on. Geilfus sketches a scene -- objects, lights, animations -- then adds layers of sound-reactive animation, selecting specific sounds to affect specific parts of the visuals, "the way one would use effects and keyframes in animation software". Corona's part is fluid and spontaneous: reacting to the imagery, exploring what sounds can control which visual elements, so that over time more sounds gain a direct influence on how certain visual elements behave. The inspected screenshot of Geilfus's software interface is the single most informative image in this study: black control panels surround a render pane showing a dense white tangled line network on black, with panels reading "AUDIOANALYSIS", "RENDERING", "TESTING", and a scene library -- "OORT THING", "COSMOS", "BACTERIAS", "UNISSON", "COSMOS II", "CIELO" -- with sliders for scale, friction, sound reactivity, glow, rotation. The track names from "Martes" and "Cosmos" are the panel titles: one visual scene per track, each scene a different mapping from the same audio analysis. The software began as a small buggy prototype in Processing for the first gig and evolved into several C++ applications, patched constantly -- Corona: Geilfus "works with his own customized software and he's constantly changing and patching it". The staging completes the mechanism: at the Alpha-ville LIVE show (Hackney Empire, London, 6 October 2012) a giant transparent fabric screen stood between the artists and the audience, with the sound-reactive visuals projected onto it, so the performers are inside their own image. Producer Nicolas Boritch on the aim: "create an experience where you can't necessarily separate the sound and visual elements. You might forget about one of the two, but hopefully it is because it is a whole, and you are lost in it."

**Takeaway:** bind sounds to pictures the way you bind effects to keyframes -- one scene per track, each sound owning one visual behaviour -- and put the binding on a screen the audience can see through.

## M7 -- Scale play: galaxies and atoms in one frame

The visual language Geilfus built for Murcof, and the most portable mechanism in the study. Geilfus: "For this particular project I've been focusing on more dreamy visuals, visuals about the scale of galaxies and the scale of atoms." Corona, describing the imagery: "a giant spider-like creature that stretches its arms each time the music explodes, there's bacteria holding hands and working in groups whilst maintaining their individuality, there are tons of stars that swirl around and become comets, rays of light that become a network of neurons -- it's perfect." And the musical claim behind it: "It has taken the music closer to where the inspiration to make it came from, evoking macro and micro universes, which I also try to do musically." The mechanism is a single rule applied at every scale: a field of points with rules -- stars swirl, bacteria flock, neurons branch -- and the scale is the only depth. The galaxy and the bacterium are the same system at different zooms, so the jump between scales is the picture, not a transition between pictures. The music earns the jump: the spider stretches its arms when the music explodes, so the visual event and the sonic event are one event, and the audience reads scale as intensity. The same rule runs backwards through the catalogue: "Cosmos" is the record of it in sound alone, and the planetarium dome (M4) is its architectural form.

**Takeaway:** pick one system that works at every scale, and let the zoom be the drama -- the galaxy and the atom are the same picture, and the music decides when to jump.

## M8 -- The rendered landscape: a world built to be filmed

Twin Color (EP No. 1, 2022; Vol. I, 15 November 2024; EP No. 2, 29 August 2025) is the late turn, and the furthest point from the early sample years. Corona announced it as the synthesiser side of electronic music, "as opposed to sample- or audio-based stuff like I have done so far under the Murcof name": analog synthesisers -- Dave Smith, Yamaha and Kodamo instruments recorded to tape on a Tascam Portastudio -- motorik and post-punk pulse, a cinematic, dystopian narrative influenced by 1980s science fiction, "Blade Runner" named. Geilfus's visual half is a full audio-visual performance conceived in the IRCAM studios in Paris in 2022: he built a landscape using video-game rendering software, creating scenes and stages to shoot footage within, then used post-processing to make the world resemble a grainy movie instead of a video game. The performance unfolds the album's tracks against moving natural landscapes, "as mysterious as they are fascinating" -- the Twin Color cover itself is a rendered fleshy red canopy over sunlit grass, not a photograph of a real place. The crucial fact, from Neural's review, is that Geilfus's algorithmic elaborations of natural landscapes helped define the album's sound structure: a symbiotic dialogue between acoustic dimension and visual representation, not a visual afterthought. The visuals are no longer bound to the sound after the fact (M6); they are part of how the sound was shaped. The premiere history is unsettled -- an InFine video description says a live show premiered at IRCAM in 2023, while Bandcamp and InFine say the A/V performance premiered at MUTEK Montreal at the end of August 2024 (C3510, C3555, C3603) -- most likely a 2023 IRCAM work-in-progress showing and a full 2024 MUTEK premiere, but no source read states that distinction.

**Takeaway:** build the world first, film it, and let the film change the music -- when the landscape is rendered before the record is finished, the visual stops illustrating the sound and starts composing it.

## The practice now: fragment, room, world

Put the three turns together and the career reads as a single widening. 1999-2004: the work is a fragment -- a record sliced, a border sample cut up, a beat and a silence. The image is absent; the cover is a landscape photograph, not a system. 2005-2008: the fragment becomes a performance -- friends recorded in a studio, a trio on stage, a six-speaker fountain garden, a planetarium dome -- and the room becomes the instrument. 2008-2012: the room gains a second author -- Geilfus's software binds sound to image, one scene per track, and the audience watches through a transparent screen. 2015-2025: the performance becomes a world -- field recordings from a volcano, a landscape rendered in a game engine, the visuals feeding back into the sound structure. The through-line never changes: classical material, electronic processing, and a refusal to repeat himself -- "I didn't want to repeat myself and I was also curious to explore other ways of combining electronic music with classical music elements." The proportion changes, not the ingredients: the beat thins until it disappears (the recent works, per the programme notes, no longer include electronic beats at all), the silence grows, the scale widens from a sliced string sample to a rendered landscape. And the collaboration is the career's second spine: the fifteen years with Geilfus run from a buggy prototype to a game-engine world, and the music is what the tools draw.

## Aesthetic logic: the fragment, the room, the world

Three aesthetics, one career, in sequence -- and the sequence is the logic. The first aesthetic is the fragment: a sliced sample, a glitch beat, a long silence, the holy-minimalist chord held until it hurts. The image is restrained because there is no image yet; the record is the whole work, and the silence is its frame. The second aesthetic is the room: the fountain garden, the dome, the stage with a transparent screen, the micro-installation projected halo-like behind the players. The image is architectural because the subject is now a place, and a place needs a room. The third aesthetic is the world: a rendered landscape, a fleshy red canopy over sunlit grass, a grainy movie made from a game engine. The image is a world because the subject is now a film -- Twin Color's narrative is film-like, each track a scene -- and a film needs a world to shoot in. What holds the three together is the refusal underneath them all: never repeat the last record's method, never let the electronics swallow the acoustic source, never make an image that could have been shown anywhere else. Every piece is source-first -- a record, a player, a volcano, a landscape -- and the processing is the work, not the decoration.

## Network

The collaboration at the centre is Geilfus (W11, W19): the only collaborator Corona has worked with for fifteen years, from a buggy Processing prototype at Futuresonic 2009 to a video-game-rendered world for Twin Color. The contact did not come through Geilfus -- Boritch and Lemercier knew Murcof first, did a one-off gig with him in Bristol in 2008, then asked Geilfus to collaborate (C3305, carried from #606).
