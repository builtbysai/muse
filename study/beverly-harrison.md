# Beverly Harrison: Study Notes

**Site:** Beverly L. Harrison is a Canadian-trained human-computer interaction researcher whose career ran through industrial research labs: B. Mathematics (Computer Science) at the University of Waterloo, then M.A.Sc. (1991, thesis "The Annotation and Analysis of Video Documents") and PhD in Human Factors Engineering at the University of Toronto, where she was a member of the Dynamic Graphics Project (DGP); then Alias Research and Nortel, Xerox PARC (1998-2000), IBM Research Almaden (by 2002-2005), Intel Research Seattle (senior scientist from 2005), Amazon Lab126 (about 2011-12), Yahoo Labs (Senior Director of Mobile Research by October 2014), and Microsoft (by March 2016), with affiliate associate-professor appointments in Computer Science and Engineering and the Information School at the University of Washington. Her work moved in three phases -- experimental studies of transparent interfaces and video annotation at Toronto (1991-1996), embodied and tangible interfaces at PARC (1998-2005), and mobile sensing and ubiquitous computing at Intel, Lab126, Yahoo, and Microsoft (2005-2016) -- and the method never changed: build a novel sensing or interface system, then evaluate it empirically, in situ where possible. [C37221]

**Relevance:** Harrison helps investigate what happens when one mark must be read by a person and a machine at the same time, and who gets to decide what it means when the two readings disagree: her transparent interfaces put a Stroop word and its ink colour in the same glyph, and her later sensing systems let a machine infer activities the wearer can then correct. The evidence is the CHI '95 depth-multiplexing experiment, where transparency thresholds were measured to the percent; the CHI '96 transparent-menu study, which specified the anti-interference outline as an algorithm; and UbiFit Garden, where the human journal overrides the sensor's flower and both readings stay on the record -- three systems in which the disagreement between readings is measured, not asserted. [C37223]

**Doorway:** The 459th doorway. Hardock asked: when one mark must speak to both a person and a machine, who decides what it means when the two disagree -- the writer, the reader, or the recogniser? Harrison answers from the lab bench, and her answer is that nobody decides once: who decides is itself a design variable. In the Stroop experiment the recogniser's rule (name the ink) and the reader's habit (read the word) fight inside one glyph, and the winner depends on transparency -- neither side wins by right. In UbiFit Garden the writer wins by design: the human journal overrides the sensor, and both readings stay on the record. In LEGO OASIS the recogniser wins by default until the viewer moves the object and forces a re-reading. The open question carried forward is: when the recogniser is wrong and nobody corrects it, does the error become the record -- and who pays for the correction, the writer who must keep watch, or the reader who must keep doubting? [C37224]

## M1 -- Depth multiplexing: two layers, one surface, one transparency variable (1994-1995)

**Subject:** Two superimposed images sharing one screen -- a foreground tool or menu and a background work surface -- instead of tiling them side by side (space multiplexing) or swapping them in time (time multiplexing). Harrison, Ishii, Vicente and Buxton name the third strategy depth multiplexing: through transparency in the background of windows, the contents of windows underneath are visible, or at least partially visible.

**Visual principle:** Occlusion resolved by translucency rather than by rearrangement. The two layers compete for the same pixels; legibility of each is a function of the other's density and of the transparency level. The paper frames the whole problem as an attentional trade-off: opaque overlap facilitates focused attention (the hidden background cannot create visual interference) but blocks divided attention, while transparency reverses both.

**Computational mechanism:** A single continuous control variable, transparency from 0% (opaque) to 100% (clear), applied to the foreground layer. The CHI '95 paper hypothesises two opposing curves (Figure 4, explicitly labelled hypothetical): as transparency rises, divided attention gets easier and focused attention gets harder, and their crossing defines an "optimal transparency." The measured effects are non-linear and asymmetric: in the Stroop colour-naming task interference dilutes in a seemingly logarithmic fashion, levelling off at 50% transparency, and at 5% minimal or no interference takes place; in the word-naming task substantial performance gains occur within the first 20-25% transparency, but levels of 5% or less do not seem usable. So the usable band is roughly 10-50%, with different optima for focusing versus dividing attention. A second variable: visual distinctiveness between layers (colour, analog versus verbal content, font size and style) changes interference independently of transparency. A third, behavioural finding from the application study: as users gained familiarity with a layout they preferred more transparency -- the dialog items were needed only as outlines to target selections, and legibility mattered substantially less -- pointing to interfaces that evolve with expertise. [C37226]

**Artistic opportunity:** Transparency is a genuinely computational dial with a measured sweet spot and a measured failure zone, and the failure is visible: at the wrong level the two layers fuse into illegible noise, at the right level they separate into two readable worlds on one surface. A piece can let the viewer live inside that trade-off instead of illustrating it. This is also the doorway question in its purest form: one mark, two readers, and transparency decides which reader wins. [C37227]

**Takeaway:** One dial, two readers, and a measured band where both can see -- outside it, one of them goes blind. [C37228]

## M2 -- Stroop interference: the mark that contradicts itself (1995)

**Subject:** A colour word printed in a conflicting ink colour ("red" in green ink), seen through a colour patch -- the reader must name the ink while ignoring the word, or read the word while ignoring the patch.

**Visual principle:** Semantic conflict staged as colour conflict. The word and the ink occupy the same glyph; the contradiction is not beside the mark, it is the mark. Interference is worst for incongruent pairs, absent for neutral words, and errors occur almost exclusively on the colour-incongruent trials.

**Computational mechanism:** A finite vocabulary (4 colours, 4 colour words, 4 neutral words -- UNCLE, NAIL, CUTE, FOOD -- in 78-point Helvetica uppercase) combined with 6 transparency levels (0/5/10/20/50/100%) gives 180 unique word-colour-transparency combinations, each run 3 times per subject at 5-second intervals, about 45 minutes per experiment, 16 University of Toronto students pre-screened for colour-blindness. Two opposed tasks run on the same stimuli: colour-naming (ignore the word) and word-naming (ignore the patch). Colour-naming showed significant main effects for transparency (F(5,719)=11.12, p<.0001), word type, and colour, with no significant interactions; transparency means fell into four groups (100%+50%+20%, 10%, 5%, 0%), and interference peaked at 50% -- more transparency did not degrade performance further. Word-naming showed transparency groups of 5%, 10%, and 100%+50%+20%+0% -- the 0% word-only baseline was not statistically different from 100%; at 5% transparency subjects reported great difficulty and about 15% of trials were errors, above 10% there were virtually no errors; yellow was easiest, then green, blue and red hardest and not different from each other; performance levelled off at 20%, so the hypothesised divided-attention curve underestimated the effect. [C37229]

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece can be a Stroop machine rather than depict one: the viewer is put in the subject's seat, and the piece records which reading wins, when, and how often -- the disagreement itself becomes the visible trace. No other gallery piece stages a controlled, measurable conflict between what a mark says and what a mark is. [C37230]

**Takeaway:** The word and the ink share one glyph, and the experiment measures exactly when the reader stops believing either of them. [C37231]

## M3 -- Anti-interference outlining: legibility by boundary, not by fill (1995-1996)

**Subject:** Text and object borders in a transparent foreground, given an outline in the opposing contrast level (white objects get black outlines) so they survive over any background.

**Visual principle:** A glyph's survival depends on its edge, not its interior. The outline is a thin, high-contrast contour that separates foreground from background regardless of what lies beneath. Harrison's CHI '96 follow-up generalised the technique into "Anti-Interference" outlining for menus over text pages, wire-frame images, and solid images.

**Computational mechanism:** The CHI '96 paper specifies the outline as an algorithm: for a text colour vector [R,G,B], compute luminance Y under the YIQ colour model; the outline colour is the maximal-luminance-contrast candidate -- [0,0,0] (black) when Y is greater than half the maximum luminance, otherwise [Rmax,Gmax,Bmax] -- so the outline is always visible against any background. The rule is binary -- present or absent -- while the fill carries the graded transparency of M1. Background density modulates how much outline is needed: in the 3-D modelling prototype, users found transparent windows easier to use over solid models and images than over wire-frame drawings. The payoff is quantified: anti-interference fonts at 75% and 90% transparency perform like regular fonts at 50%. [C37232]

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece where marks are drawn only as outlines over a dense, moving ground, and the outlines thicken, thin, or fail as the ground's density changes -- legibility as a live, visible negotiation rather than a typesetting choice. The visual payoff is calligraphic: bright thread-like contours holding their shape over chaos. [C37233]

**Takeaway:** When the fill can no longer be trusted, the edge is the whole mark. [C37234]

## M4 -- Transparent menus: the labels stay opaque (1996)

**Subject:** A variably transparent linear text menu superimposed over realistic backgrounds -- the applied sequel to CHI '95, testing whether the Stroop thresholds survive when the foreground object is a real menu.

**Visual principle:** The text labels stay opaque and only the menu surface around them is made transparent (Figure 3b, called "the more realistic design choice"), on the reasoning that text occupies a small fraction of the selectable area. The menu is therefore two objects at once: readable labels and a see-through body.

**Computational mechanism:** Fully randomised within-subject repeated measures: 10 University of Toronto students, 12-item menu (Revolve X/Y/Z, Dup/Comb/Del Curve plus 6 distractors), items randomly repositioned every trial so the test measures legibility rather than positional learning, 540 trials per subject, transparency at 0/50/75/90/100% by alpha blending, three background classes (text pages, wire-frame images, solid images, 3 samples of each). For regular fonts the transparency groups were 100% poorest, then 90%, 75%, and 50%+0% equivalently good; for anti-interference fonts 100% poorest, 50%+75%+90% not different, 0% best -- a substantially flatter curve. Background effects were driven by individual image properties, not by type: the text page whose font matched the menu font was slowest, dense dark wireframes were slowest, solid images with black components were slowest because the menu font was black. Surprisingly, text backgrounds overall performed better than expected -- the critical dimension was colour conflict (background shade or hue close to text colour), not background type. Errors: targeting errors under 1% of trials (45% adjacent-item slips, 55% substitutions of a similarly named item); only about 1% of trials were marked illegible, 90% of those at 100% transparency and 10% at 90%, all in the regular-font condition -- no anti-interference trial was ever marked illegible. Mean response time for illegible trials was 6.84 seconds, almost three times the normal trial time -- the "threshold of frustration," the point at which subjects give up. Subjects disliked the outline font aesthetically and, given a choice, preferred 50% transparency; several noted that even the 100%-to-90% step made black-on-black items selectable. [C37235]

**Artistic opportunity:** The experiment's most human number is not a threshold but a surrender time: 6.84 seconds of staring at an unreadable menu before giving up. A piece can stage that exact moment -- the mark that stays legible one step longer because its edge was drawn at the opposite pole -- and let the viewer feel the difference between a menu that fails at 100% and one that survives at 90%. [C37236]

**Takeaway:** The labels stay solid while the body goes clear -- and the experiment measures the exact second at which a reader gives up. [C37237]

## M5 -- UbiFit Garden: behaviour translated into a garden, corrected by hand (2008)

**Subject:** A phone-wallpaper display in which a garden blooms across the week as the wearer exercises. Each flower is one activity event -- a 40-minute run and a 3-hour bike ride are each one pink cardio flower; a 22-minute walk is one sunflower; yoga is one white flexibility flower; weightlifting one blue strength flower. Flower species encodes activity type; flower height has no relation to duration; a small white butterfly marks a recent goal attainment and a large yellow butterfly marks the week's goal met; the week starts as an empty lawn. Positive reinforcement only: no wilting flowers, weeds, or stormy sky for inactivity -- the garden is simply sparse under a blue sky.

**Visual principle:** Categorical encoding by species and count, not by magnitude. Density is the number of events; variety is the number of species present; a full garden is a week with variety, not a week with volume. The display is ambient and persistent -- a wallpaper, glanced at, not opened.

**Computational mechanism:** A sensing pipeline (the Mobile Sensing Platform: a pager-sized device with a 3-axis accelerometer and barometer, sending activity labels with predicted likelihoods to the phone 4 times per second via Bluetooth) infers five activities -- walking, running, cycling, elliptical trainer, stair machine -- and the phone aggregates and smooths the fine-grained noisy margins into human-scale chunks (a 22-minute walk, a 35-minute run); smoothing is defined at the application level, not on the device. Walking and cardio must last at least 10 minutes to earn a flower. Crucially, the pipeline is correctable: users can add, edit, or delete activities manually when the sensor made a mistake, forgot, or missed an untrained activity -- the human journal overrides the machine inference, and both readings are kept. The CHI '08 field trial ran 3 weeks with 12 participants (6 women, 6 men, 25-35, Seattle area, study phone used as their personal phone): 532 activities recorded, 325 manually journaled (61%) versus 207 inferred (39%); of inferred activities 77% were left unchanged (71% of those were walks); 23 inferred activities were deleted by 6 participants, 70% of them false cycling; of activities edited for type, 45% were incorrectly detected as bicycling and 26% were walks confused as stair-machine sessions. [C37238]

**Artistic opportunity:** A garden whose blooms are claims about what happened, claims the viewer can dispute and reassign -- the garden keeps both the machine's flower and the human's correction visible, so disagreement is part of the picture, not an error to hide. The weekly reset gives the piece a natural arc: lawn, bloom, reset. [C37239]

The paper's central argument is that traditional true-positive / false-positive / false-negative / true-negative metrics do not capture how users experience inference errors. Participants perceived seven error types: start-time error, duration error, confusing two trained activities, confusing an untrained activity with a trained one, missing a trained activity, missing an untrained activity, and detecting an activity that never happened. The same false positive -- a "bike ride" -- was intolerable when the user had driven a car, appreciated when she had done housework ("boom, boom, boom, I'm getting all these little pink flowers... very satisfying"), and a stair-machine label for a walk was tolerable. The two credibility-killers were failing to detect a trained activity ("Why am I wearing you if you don't pick it up?") and phantom detection (cycling while driving, typing email, or reclining). One participant's accelerometer broke shortly after session 1 and went unnoticed for almost a week ("I wanted the flower, you know?"). Allowing users to add, edit, and delete inferred data is presented as what rescues an imperfect system's credibility; most participants called all three components essential and preferred inference over manual entry, but did not want manual-only either. A separate follow-up experiment on the same system, "Flowers or a Robot Army?", UbiComp 2008, ran 3 months with 28 participants over the winter holidays: participants with an awareness display maintained their activity level while those without one dropped significantly, and 25 of 28 would want a glanceable display, though not necessarily with a garden metaphor. Harrison is not an author on that follow-up paper; the garden display rules and the 3-week trial above are from the CHI '08 paper she co-authored, and the 3-month result is the same system's later evaluation. [C37241]

**Takeaway:** The sensor plants a flower; the wearer can replant it -- and the garden remembers both plantings. [C37240]

## M6 -- MyExperience: Sensor-Trigger-Action (2007)

**Subject:** A phone system combining passive logging of device usage, user context, and environmental sensor readings -- more than 140 event types, including calls, SMS, app usage, key presses, calendar, GPS, Bluetooth -- with active, context-triggered experience sampling: self-report surveys fired when sensor conditions are met (device idle more than 15 minutes, an incoming SMS from a named contact, stationary for 10 minutes).

**Visual principle:** Two parallel traces of one life -- the machine's continuous log and the person's sampled answers -- laid over the same timeline. Where they agree, the trace is single; where they disagree, it forks.

**Computational mechanism:** A three-tiered, object-oriented, event-driven architecture. Sensors are state-based: entering a new state exits the previous one and generates an event, and state history is auto-logged; hardware and software sensors alike (DeviceIdleSensor, SmsSensor, PhoneCallSensor, RawGpsSensor, PlaceSensor). Triggers subscribe to sensor state changes and evaluate conditional logic only on change; they can be written in native C# or as Simkin scripts that auto-subscribe to the sensors they mention, and can use state-history metadata (time-in-state, time-since-transition, ever-reached) to express rules like "user has been at a location at least 10 minutes" or to detect sensor failure. Actions all derive from a base class and implement Run; 11 were built, including Survey, DatabaseSync, Screenshot, Notification, SendSms, Player, and CreateProcess, executed by a scheduler thread over a thread pool and priority queue, only when the device is in an interruptible state. Configuration is a lightweight XML file in four sections (sensors, triggers, actions, UI) whose type attributes map directly to C# namespaces and classes via reflection, so custom sensors load dynamically without recompilation; a worked example survey times out after 30 seconds and samples the user only 20% of the time after a completed call. The event model is explicitly asynchronous and unidirectional (sensors to triggers to actions); a trigger cannot conditionally run a second action based on a first action's result -- a stated limitation. [C37242]

**Artistic opportunity:** A piece that watches the viewer's pointer the way MyExperience watches a phone -- logging continuously -- and at moments its own rules choose, interrupts with a single question; the piece's later behaviour depends on the answers, and the log and the answers are both drawn, so the viewer can see the machine's version of their visit next to their own. The interaction is earned: the questions are the piece's content, not a survey bolted on. [C37243]

**Takeaway:** The phone keeps a log; the person keeps a story -- and the system is built to ask when the two stop matching. [C37244]

## M7 -- The Mobile Sensing Platform: inference as a measured craft (2008)

**Subject:** A single small wearable multimodal sensor board for embedded, real-time activity recognition -- not the then-standard 2 to 12 accelerometers strapped around the body. Version 1.0: 46 g, about 43 mW with all sensors running, more than 12 hours on a 200 mAh battery, seven sensors (microphone at about 16 kHz, visible-light phototransistor 550 Hz, 3-axis accelerometer 550 Hz, barometer/temperature 30 Hz, IR and visible/IR light 5 Hz, humidity/temperature 2 Hz, compass 30 Hz) on an Intel iMote (32-bit ARM7). Version 2.0: sensor board on an iMote2 (416 MHz XScale), 115 g, 1,800 mAh battery, 10-20 hours, removable miniSD, Bluetooth plus ZigBee, optional location daughterboard. Design drivers stated explicitly: no physiological or skin-contact sensors; lightweight and unobtrusive; trainable without extensive human supervision; privacy-protecting, with audio processed to features on the fly -- enough to infer that a conversation happened, not enough to reconstruct the words.

**Visual principle:** The sensor board is a black box whose output is a label with a probability, not a picture. The visual work of the system is all downstream: margins smoothed into activities, activities drawn as flowers, errors drawn as corrections. The mechanism matters here because it sets the exact rate at which the machine is wrong -- and therefore how much correcting the human must do.

**Computational mechanism:** Three components: sensing, feature processing and selection, classification. Version 1.0 uses boosting-based automatic feature selection to pick a small useful subset from a 600-feature superset (linear and log-scale frequency coefficients, cepstral coefficients, spectral entropy, band-pass filter coefficients, correlations, integrals, means, variances); features are computed at 0.1-10 Hz depending on activity; the classifier outputs probability scores every 0.25 seconds and picks the maximum. The two most discriminative modalities of the seven were accelerometer and microphone; the barometer alone could detect elevator and stair use. Version 2.0 extends boosting to do feature selection and structured (temporal) prediction jointly, outperforming standard boosting, hidden Markov models, and conditional random fields, improving accuracy and smoothness by about 10%. Version 3.0 is semi-supervised: training time down 20 times and memory down 13 times versus related semi-supervised methods. Accuracy on eight activities (walking, sitting, standing, stairs up and down, elevator up and down, brushing teeth), classified every 0.25 seconds: version 1.0 supervised without temporal information, 83.6%; version 2.0 with temporal information, 93.8%; version 3.0 semi-supervised, 79.7% with 5% of training data labelled, 83.1% at 20%, 87.4% at 40%. Models trained on 6-8 people generalised to new individuals at comparable rates. [C37245]

**Artistic opportunity:** The accuracy table is a contract: the machine is right about 94 times in 100 and wrong about 6, and the piece can make those 6 visible instead of averaging them away. The UbiFit error taxonomy (M5) is the human side of this table -- the same 6% experienced as a flower for a drive that never happened. [C37246]

**Takeaway:** Inference is a percentage, not a verdict -- and the percentage is what the human has to live with. [C37247]

## M8 -- In-situ evaluation: the lab is not where the technology lives (2007)

**Subject:** A methods paper, not a system paper: adapting psychology and HCI techniques -- Wizard of Oz and Experience Sampling -- for in-situ evaluation of ubiquitous computing, on the argument that lab evaluation is insufficient because ubicomp interactions can be implicit or sensed, mobile, and affect indirect stakeholders. Three data-collection strategies are distinguished: evaluator-initiated (Wizard of Oz), context-triggered self-report, and participant-initiated self-report.

**Visual principle:** Not a visual mechanism in itself -- it is a procedural mechanism (trigger, sample, log). It earns a place here only as the operating logic behind M6; on its own it is a research method, not a piece.

**Computational mechanism:** Three field studies, each a different strategy. CareNet Display (Wizard of Oz): a touch-screen tablet PC in a custom wooden picture frame augmenting an elder's photo with icons for medications, meals, activities, outings, mood, falls, and a shared calendar (red medication icon means a problem, black means as expected); deployed in the homes of 2-3 family members per elder, 4 elders, 13 participants, 3 weeks each; sensors were simulated by a researcher phoning the elder or caregiver up to 6 times per day, every day including weekends, nights, and holidays, then updating displays manually over a GPRS web connection. Key finding: pre-study interviews ranked "falls" the number-one information need and "household needs" number eight; after living with the display, participants wanted household needs instead -- a requirement the lab would never have surfaced. Voting with your feet (context-triggered experience sampling): 16 participants, 4 weeks, questionnaire triggered after about 10 minutes at a place, 3,458 in-situ questionnaires, web diaries averaging 5.8 sessions per week per participant against 4 requested. UbiFit version 1 (participant-initiated): 3 groups of 4-5 women from pre-existing social networks, 13 participants, 3 weeks, Nokia Series 6600 plus Omron HJ-112 pedometer, step counts manually entered and shared with "fitness buddies"; pedometers misrepresent activity (cycling invisible to them) which mattered because counts were shared, and seeing goal progress through the day prompted unplanned walks. Practical lessons recorded: pedometers dislodged and fell into toilets (fixed with a clip and safety strap); study phones need nightly charging, which non-habitual chargers do inconsistently; the personal-SIM versus study-phone dilemma is left explicitly unresolved. [C37248]

**Artistic opportunity:** None standalone. The method's one transferable image is the inversion -- what people say they want before living with a system is not what they want after -- and that inversion is a rule a piece can run: ask first, then let the viewer live with the answer, then ask again. [C37249]

**Takeaway:** Ask before, ask after, and believe the second answer -- the first one was a guess about a life not yet lived. [C37250]

## M9 -- Manipulative interfaces: the device itself is the input (1998-2000, listing level)

**Subject:** At Xerox PARC, the physical device stops being a container for buttons and becomes the input device. "Squeeze Me, Hold Me, Tilt Me!" (CHI '98, with Fishkin, Gujar, Mochon and Want) builds three prototypes with squeeze, tilt, and pressure sensing built into handheld devices. "Bridging Physical and Virtual Worlds with Electronic Tags" (CHI '99, with Want, Fishkin and Gujar) links RFID-tagged everyday objects to electronic services matched to the object's form (a dictionary to translation). "Design and Technology for Collaborage" (UIST '99, with Moran, Saund, van Melle, Gujar and Fishkin) tracks physical wall collages -- In/Out boards -- by camera and computer vision, triggering electronic services. The CACM 2000 synthesis paper, "Embodied User Interfaces for Really Direct Manipulation" (with Fishkin, Gujar, Moran and Want), states the programme: paper-document handling tasks as embodied interfaces.

**Visual principle:** The hand's ordinary handling of a thing -- squeezing it, tilting it, tagging it, pinning it to a wall -- is the command. No menu, no cursor, no screen between the gesture and its meaning.

**Computational mechanism:** Stated here only at listing level, because no leg read these papers end to end: the mechanisms are named in their titles and abstracts (squeeze/tilt/pressure sensing in the device; RFID tag to service mapping by object form; camera-tracked physical collage triggering services), but thresholds, sensor values, and recognition accuracies are not established this run and are not claimed. The doorway bearing is direct all the same: a squeeze is a mark the machine must read, and the machine's reading of a squeeze can disagree with the hand's intent -- the same question as M2, asked of the body instead of the eye. [C37251]

**Artistic opportunity:** Held for a future read. The squeeze-and-tilt vocabulary is a real mechanism family, but without the papers read end to end there is no honest prompt to build from; a piece from titles alone would be invention. [C37252]

**Takeaway:** The hand already knows the command -- the question is whether the machine does. [C37253]

## M10 -- The recogniser names the thing, and the house keeps score (2011-2012, abstract and excerpt level)

**Subject:** Two late works, both read at abstract or excerpt level only. LEGO OASIS (VL/HCC 2011, with Ziola, Grampurohit, Landes and Fogarty; Harrison's affiliation Lab126) is a camera-and-projector system that recognises uninstrumented LEGO toys and augments them with projected digital content; the paper's stated focus is the challenges in establishing and manipulating relationships between physical objects and their digital capabilities. "Living in a Glass House" (UbiComp 2011, with Choe, Consolvo, Jung and Kientz) is an anonymous survey (475 respondents) of the activities people do at home that they would not want recorded, and where in the home they happen; its follow-up (UbiComp 2012) used sensor proxies -- stand-in devices that simulate a sensing system -- to probe people's receptiveness in situ, and was nominated for best paper.

**Visual principle:** Projection as a second skin: digital content cast onto and around a physical object, so the object carries two identities at once -- what it is and what the system thinks it is. And a floor plan in which rooms light up not by occupancy but by inferability -- what a sensor could deduce from that room -- against what the inhabitant wants seen.

**Computational mechanism:** Stated at the level the sources were read: OASIS runs a general-purpose object-recognition pipeline over uninstrumented objects (no tags, no markers), with confidence scores; the system must establish a relationship (this projection belongs to this object), maintain it as the object moves, and repair it when the object changes or recognition flips; the paper's focus is the failure modes of that relationship, not the success case. The Glass House work is a per-room, per-activity receptiveness score, probed with proxy devices that pretend to sense and record the inhabitant's response (accept, object, negotiate), with progressive exposure -- the system reveals more of what it infers, step by step, and comfort is re-measured at each step. No thresholds, scores, or recognition accuracies from either paper are claimed this run, because neither paper was read end to end. [C37254]

**Artistic opportunity:** Misrecognition as a visible event: when recognition is wrong, the projection visibly mismatches the object, and the viewer can move the shape to test whether the machine changes its mind. And the negotiation: a house that gradually infers more about its viewer and shows its inferences as light in rooms, while the viewer can shutter rooms -- and the shuttering itself is drawn. Both are direct doorway readings, and both carry a read-before-build flag in the prompts: the OASIS piece must keep its similarity margin a stated rule rather than a faked confidence, and the Glass House piece must stay on the negotiation, never drift into surveillance as spectacle. [C37255]

**Takeaway:** The machine names the toy, and the house keeps score -- and in both cases the person can see the naming happen. [C37256]

## The arc: from milliseconds to weeks, from the eye to the house

Harrison's arc is a single question asked at three scales. In Toronto (1994-1996) the question is perceptual and lab-bound: how much transparency can a display tolerate before focused or divided attention breaks, measured in milliseconds and response times, with 10 to 16 students and an ANOVA. At PARC and IBM (1998-2005) the question moves into the hand and the room: a squeeze, a tagged object, a wall collage -- the mark is now a gesture on a physical thing, and the machine must read the body. At Intel, Lab126, Yahoo, and Microsoft (2005-2016) the question moves out of the lab entirely: how do you study and build technology that lives in people's pockets and homes, measured in weeks of behaviour -- charging habits, a pedometer in a toilet, a flower for a walk, a grandmother's willingness to share medication data. [C37257]

Three constants run through all three eras. First, design guidelines derived from experiment, never asserted: every number in this study is a measured threshold, an accuracy, or a count. Second, a tolerance for reporting what failed: the 5% transparency floor, the cycling confusions, the broken accelerometer, the unresolved SIM dilemma, the falls ranking that inverted after three weeks. Third, the user as editor and corrector of the system -- from choosing a personal transparency level with a slider (CHI '95) to editing inferred activities (UbiFit) -- the system is treated as fallible and the human as the repair mechanism. The arc's end is the doorway's answer: the recogniser is wrong a measurable fraction of the time, and the work is about who gets to say so. [C37258]

## The aesthetic logic

Harrison's aesthetic is the readable surface. The beauty is in a display that admits what it hides: a menu whose body is clear but whose labels stay solid, an outline drawn at the opposite pole so the letter survives any ground, a garden that shows the week's effort as flowers and the week's disputes as rings. Nothing is hidden in a preference panel or a confidence score; the disagreement is on the surface, where the viewer can see it and act on it. The palette is restraint itself -- four ink colours on black, five flower species on dark green, white walls on a dark plan -- because the mark must be readable by a person and a machine at the same time, and decoration would break the reading for one of them. The visual language that follows is the ledger: a row of ticks recording which reading won each round, a garden keeping the machine's flower beside the human's correction, a floor plan keeping the shuttered rooms dark. Slowness is not a mood here; it is the price of attention, paid in the 6.84 seconds before a reader gives up on an unreadable menu, and the honest piece shows the price rather than hiding it. [C37259]
