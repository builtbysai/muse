## The life: Rio, Parque Lage, and the transfer

Beatriz Ferreira Milhazes was born in Rio de Janeiro in 1960 -- the year only; no day or month appears in any source read this run. She lives and works in Rio. She studied Social Communication at FACHA in Rio from 1978 to 1981, then painting at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage from 1980 to 1983. The Parque Lage dates carry a conflict: a White Cube CV snippet gives 1981-82. The study prints 1980-1983, the dates given by the Portuguese Wikipedia and by Ocula, and records the variant in Corrections 1. What matters is not the year boundary but the place: Parque Lage was the school of the Geracao 80 moment, and Milhazes was in it at exactly the moment it happened. (C15508, leg 1 C15300)

She showed in the 6th National Visual Arts Salon at MAM Rio in 1983-84 and the 7th Salon in 1984-85, winning the Ibere Camargo Prize -- an acquisition prize -- at the 7th Salon. In the summer of 1984 she was one of 123 artists in "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" at Parque Lage, the exhibition that named the generation. Her first solo, "Beatriz Milhazes: Pinturas", opened at Galeria Cesar Ache in Rio on 24 September 1985 and ran to 11 October. From 1986 to 1996 she taught at Parque Lage and coordinated its Painting Department -- ten years of teaching inside the school that had formed her, while her own painting was finding its method. (C15509, leg 1 C15301, C15302)

The method arrived in 1989: the monotransfer, a dry monotype. She paints a motif in layers of acrylic on a transparent plastic sheet, lets it dry, presses the sheet onto the canvas with acrylic medium, and peels the plastic away, leaving the dried film on the canvas like a decal. The sheet can be reused; a motif she likes can be repainted and transferred into many compositions over as much as ten years. The technique is the hinge of the whole career: everything before it is preparation, everything after it is variation. The first work the legs grade as the breakthrough is "Me perdoa, te perdoo!" of 1989, now in the Museu Nacional de Belas Artes in Rio. (C15510, leg 1 C15303; leg 2 C15351)

Parallel to the painting, from 1987 to 2008, she made scenography for the dance company of her sister Marcia Milhazes -- "Dancando Villa-Lobos" (1987), "Santa Cruz" (1995-96), "Tempo de Verao" (2004), "Meu Prazer" (2008). The stage work matters because it is where the motifs learned to move: a painted flower that has to read from the back row, a colour that has to hold against a moving body. The "Tempo de Verao" scenography later seeded "Gamboa", her mobile. In 1995-96 she studied engraving at Atelier 78, and in 1995 she showed at the Carnegie International in Pittsburgh. Visiting-artist posts followed -- Tyler, Wichita, Yale in 1997-98 -- and both the Sydney and Sao Paulo Biennials in 1998. (C15511, leg 1 C15304)

The 2000s made her international. "Mares do Sul", her first large solo in Brazil, opened at CCBB Rio in 2002. In 2003 she represented Brazil at the 50th Venice Biennale, and the same year she returned to found-material collage -- sweet wrappers, branded packaging, tissues, hologrammed papers -- a journal-like practice running alongside the big acrylics. In 2004 the public-art turn began with "Gavea", a glass facade for Selfridges in Manchester; "Peace and Love", a vinyl panel for Gloucester Road tube station, and "Guanabara", a vinyl panel for the Tate Modern Level 7 restaurant, followed in 2005-07. France made her Chevalier des Arts et des Lettres in 2007. (C15512, leg 1 C15305)

The survey decade followed: Pinacoteca Sao Paulo (2008), Fondation Cartier Paris (2009), Fondation Beyeler Basel (2011), Gulbenkian Lisbon and MALBA Buenos Aires (2012), Paco Imperial Rio (2013). Her first US career survey, "Beatriz Milhazes: Jardim Botanico", opened at Perez Art Museum Miami in 2014-15. In 2016 the Jewish Museum in New York showed "Using Walls, Floors, and Ceilings", including the mobile "Gamboa II", and Brazil awarded her the Order of Cultural Merit, Commander Class. Around 2017 she began thinking of circles as the core of spirituality, and figuration -- flowers, trees, totems, suns, stars -- began returning to the work after years of pure motif. "Rio Azul" showed at White Cube Bermondsey in 2018. (C15513, leg 1 C15306, C15307)

The largest exhibition of her work, "Avenida Paulista" at MASP and Itau Cultural in 2020-21, held more than 170 works made since 1989. Its closing date is conflicted -- 30 May 2021 in exhibition listings, 6 June 2021 in the MASP page header -- and the study prints 30 May with the variant in Corrections 2. "Ballet em Diagonais" showed at the Long Museum, Shanghai, in 2021; "Mistura Sagrada" at Pace Gallery, New York, in 2022; the pandemic prompted a new preparatory-drawings method in the same period. "Maresias" travelled from Turner Contemporary Margate to Tate St Ives in 2023-24, with a presentation at the 60th Venice Biennale. In 2025 the Guggenheim in New York showed "Rigor and Beauty", Casa Roberto Marinho showed "Pinturas Nomades", and "Alem do Horizonte" opened at White Cube Mason's Yard in London on 19 November 2025, running to 17 January 2026 -- Hokusai kimono research, black-white contrast, silhouettes surfacing through overlapping geometries. (C15514, leg 1 C15308, C15309)

Her collections include MoMA, the Guggenheim, the Met, Tate Modern, the Reina Sofia, the Pompidou, the Beyeler, SFMoMA, the Museu Nacional de Belas Artes in Rio, the Pinacoteca Sao Paulo, MCA Tokyo, the 21st Century Museum of Contemporary Art in Kanazawa, the Carnegie Museum of Art, and Perez Art Museum Miami. She is represented by Pace, Fortes D'Aloia & Gabriel, Max Hetzler, and White Cube. Her own descriptions of the work are the best short statements of it: "colour is what makes everything happen"; "chromatic free geometry"; "Every work I create is a mathematical dream". (C15515, leg 1 C15310)

## The mechanisms

### M1 -- The monotransfer: a motif painted, dried, peeled, placed

The mechanism is literal and physical. Milhazes paints a motif -- a flower, a rosette, an arabesque, a circle -- in layers of acrylic on an individual transparent plastic sheet. The paint dries into a film. She lays the sheet face-down on the canvas, bonds it with acrylic medium, and peels the plastic away. The motif stays on the canvas as a flat skin with no visible brushstroke. A sheet can be reused, and a motif she likes can be repainted and transferred into many compositions over years -- up to ten years, per the sources read. The work "Avenida Paulista" (2020) makes the mechanism visible as an object: one of the source plastic sheets was displayed in the MASP Cabinet vitrine beside the painting. The breakthrough work is "Me perdoa, te perdoo!" (1989, W03). The technique is still her core method after some thirty years -- "Banho de Rio" (2017, W16) is monotransfer, and so are the 2025 works.

Watchable behaviour: forms arrive as finished skins rather than being painted in place. The same flower or rosette recurs in different colours and scales across different paintings. The surface stays smooth; nothing is brushed on in front of the viewer. A piece built on this mechanism should show motifs that look placed, not painted -- a decal logic, with the same unit reappearing changed.

**Takeaway:** a motif that can be peeled off and placed again is a motif that can live in many paintings at once. Reuse is not repetition; it is a library with a long memory. (C15516, leg 2 C15353)

### M2 -- The motif library: repeat until it comes different

Milhazes keeps a personal icon library -- rosettes, flowers, arabesques, circles, lace, peace symbols -- indexed, the legs report, like embroidery pouncing patterns. The rosette motif is established by 1992 ("Untitled", 1993, W04). The rule of the library is repetition with mutation: a motif is repeated until it comes different. Colour changes, scale changes, rotation changes; the unit stays recognisable while the field changes around it. "Assim na Terra como no ceu" (1988, W02) runs the rule on a grid of repeated peace-symbol circles.

Watchable behaviour: the eye tracks a family of forms rather than a single image. The same unit cycles across the field with small mutations, so the viewer compares versions instead of reading a picture once. A piece built on this mechanism should give the viewer a small set of units and let the differences between their repetitions do the work.

**Takeaway:** a library is not a stockpile; it is a set of forms the artist already knows, so the painting can spend its energy on what changes between one use and the next. (C15517, leg 2 C15354)

### M3 -- Chromatic free geometry: colour does the structuring

Her own term, "chromatic free geometry", names a system that balances planned structure with spontaneity, movement and chance. The geometry is free -- no drawn grid holds the composition -- and the colour does the structuring instead: warm and cool areas push against each other and hold the picture together. Her rule, as reported in the sources read: "if the combination of colours is not right, the work is not finished". "O Diamante" (2002, W10) is the legs' example: motifs dividing like cells against square and rectangle grounds, the colour doing the work a drawn structure would do.

Watchable behaviour: colour zones hold the composition together without a visible grid. The picture feels mathematically ordered yet never rigid. A piece built on this mechanism should let colour relationships -- not lines, not a frame -- decide where the eye goes and where it rests.

**Takeaway:** when colour is the structure, a wrong colour is not a decoration error; it is a structural failure. The palette is the plan. (C15518, leg 2 C15355)

### M4 -- Circle, square, stripe: a grammar with three words

From 2001-02 the work settles into a three-word grammar. Circles run from ruffles and gilded rosettes to beadwork circles, daisies and compass-drawn flowers. Solid-colour squares arrive as a pause -- a flat, still block that stops the eye and continues circulatory sight, in the words of the sources read. Then stripes set the eye moving again. "Meu Limao" (2000, W09) is where the grammar begins: solid-colour squares as pause, then stripes; circle-plus-square-plus-stripe from there on.

Watchable behaviour: the eye circulates around rings, is halted by a flat square, then is set moving again by a stripe. At shape boundaries the colour pulses optically. A piece built on this mechanism should stage that three-step rhythm -- circulate, halt, release -- and let the viewer feel the grammar rather than read it.

**Takeaway:** three shapes are enough for a whole language when each one has a job: the circle moves, the square stops, the stripe carries. (C15519, leg 2 C15356)

### M5 -- Baroque, lace, arabesque: ornament transferred at large scale

The ornament comes from 18th-century Brazilian Baroque churches, colonial ironwork, crochet, lace, floral fabrics and Carnival costume -- and from the fabrics of Carnival costumes painted over in her earliest collages ("Untitled", 1981, W01). The mechanism is transfer: a piece of church carving or a lace pattern becomes a discrete motif on a plastic sheet, then lands on canvas at large scale. "Santa Cruz" (1995, W05) and "In albis" (1995-96, W06) are the baroque group -- colonial-church ornament, lace, arabesques, ruffles, pearls -- with "In albis" the dark, near-gothic end of the early palette.

Watchable behaviour: dense ornamental fields that read as lace or church carving from a distance and as flat painted skins up close. Arabesque lines curl, loop and connect zones. A piece built on this mechanism should let the viewer cross that distance -- ornament as texture far away, ornament as flat skin near.

**Takeaway:** ornament is not decoration added to a picture; it is the picture's material, carried in from churches, lace and Carnival and laid down flat. (C15520, leg 2 C15357)

### M6 -- Visible underdrawing and construction seams

The finished surface shows its making. Preliminary line-drawn arabesques and abandoned elements are left visible; transfer seams and sketchy ochre lines reveal the work's stages. "Succulent Eggplants" (1996, W07, MoMA) is the legs' evidence: visible underdrawing and abandoned arabesque lines, with the artist described in the sources as a "constructor" of signs rather than a painter of illusions.

Watchable behaviour: ghost lines and half-erased shapes flicker under the finished surface. The painting shows its own making, so the viewer watches two pictures at once -- the finished motif field and the sketch underneath it. A piece built on this mechanism should keep a visible trace of an earlier state: a line that was abandoned, a seam where two skins meet.

**Takeaway:** a painting that shows its construction gives the viewer a second picture for free -- the picture of how it was made. (C15521, leg 2 C15358)

### M7 -- Diagonal fragmentation and the return to figuration

From 2017 figuration returns -- flowers, trees, totems, suns, stars -- and diagonals fragment the circles, creating three-dimensional receding space. The diagonal is a device she had feared, per the sources read, for the unbalancing it creates. "Mistura Sagrada" (2022, W18) is the example: diagonals fragment circles and create receding space while figures emerge from the pattern. The shift begins with the circles-as-spirituality thinking of 2017 and runs through "Banho de Rio" (2017, W16), where nature -- Copacabana, Tijuca, the botanical gardens -- becomes the primary source.

Watchable behaviour: a diagonal cut slices across the circular field and the flat pattern suddenly recedes. Figures emerge from the pattern and dissolve back into it. A piece built on this mechanism should let a diagonal do the work of depth -- one cut that turns a flat field into a space, and figures that are never quite separate from the pattern that made them.

**Takeaway:** flatness is a choice, not a limit. One diagonal is enough to break it -- which is why she feared the diagonal, and why it works. (C15522, leg 2 C15359)

### M8 -- Pattern-figure and the stripe-veil: the newest inflection

The newest inflection is 2025. The source is Hokusai's Edo-period kimono portraits: patterned volumes conceal and articulate the human form, so pattern evokes presence without direct figuration. "Pictures of the Floating World" (2025, W20) is the example -- violet-blue and white grounds, mono-transfer in reverse, a silhouette that surfaces only where overlapping geometries align. Alongside it, veils of bicoloured stripes run across the canvas, transparency set against solid-colour opacity, as in "The Botanical Mind" (2025, W19) -- a dense aggregated field around a central presence, with Op Art rhythm and intervals of black-white respite.

Watchable behaviour: a silhouette surfaces only where overlapping geometries align, then vanishes into pattern. Translucent stripe veils drift over opaque grounds. A piece built on this mechanism should hide its figure in the pattern and let the viewer find it -- presence without a portrait.

**Takeaway:** a figure does not need to be drawn to be present. Pattern, overlapped until it aligns, can stand in for a body. (C15523, leg 2 C15360)


## The aesthetic logic: the decal baroque

Milhazes's logic is a baroque made of decals. The baroque part is the excess: too many flowers, too much lace, too much colour, too many motifs in one field -- the colonial church and the Carnival costume carried into a flat painting. The decal part is the discipline: every excess is a discrete skin, painted elsewhere, dried, transferred, placed. Nothing is improvised on the canvas in the romantic sense; everything arrives finished and is then arranged. That is why the work can be both maximal and controlled at once: the exuberance is in the library, the control is in the placement.

The watchability follows from the transfer, not from the subject. A motif that appears as a finished skin keeps the eye comparing versions. A colour that has to hold the structure keeps the viewer testing the balance. A circle that halts at a square keeps the eye moving in a rhythm. An underdrawing that shows through keeps the viewer seeing double. A diagonal that cuts the field keeps the flatness from settling. A figure that hides in pattern keeps the viewer searching. In every case the work gives the viewer something to do, and the doing is slow enough to watch -- the picture does not deliver itself in one look, because the motifs have to be compared, the colours weighed, the ghosts noticed, the figure found.

What she is not: not a pop artist in the American sense (the found packaging in the collages is a journal, not a quotation); not an abstract painter in the geometric sense (the geometry is free and the colour does the structuring, not a drawn grid); not a decorative painter in the dismissive sense (the ornament is the argument, carried from churches and lace, not applied to a picture that already exists). The closest kin in the studied corpus are Catunda, who also sews printed ready-made images into new wholes, and Zerbini, whose grid also holds a jungle -- but Milhazes's operation is her own: paint the motif on plastic, peel it, place it, and let colour decide whether the picture is finished.
