# Umberto Lenzi: Study Notes

**Site:** Umberto Lenzi (b. Thursday 6 August 1931, Massa Marittima, Grosseto, Tuscany, Italy; d. Thursday 19 October 2017, Rome, Italy, age 86), Italian film director, screenwriter and novelist -- the journeyman who made over sixty films across every Italian genre system of four decades, peplum to Eurospy to western to giallo to poliziottesco to cannibal horror to television, and then, retired from film, wrote ten noir novels set on the film sets of Fascist Italy. He is the director Milian went to when the westerns ran out: the man who built the poliziottesco core of Milian's career in six films, and who is remembered, against his own judgment, for the three jungle films he made almost by accident. [C30630] [C30631]

## 1. The law graduate who learned to sell a genre

Umberto Lenzi was born 6 August 1931 in Massa Marittima, a town in the province of Grosseto, Tuscany, and he was a film enthusiast from grade school. In the 1950s the young Lenzi founded and ran his town's film club, bringing in authors such as Vasco Pratolini, Pietro Germi and Rossellini to present premieres -- the Italian Wikipedia writes "Federico Rossellini," which is almost certainly an article error (the director was Roberto; possibly a confusion with Federico Fellini), so the first name is not asserted here -- and he met the writers Carlo Cassola and Luciano Bianciardi, collaborated with them in founding further film clubs, wrote essays, and took part in the protest after the Ribolla mining disaster. He then left for Rome and the Centro Sperimentale. [C30645] [C30667]

He did not abandon law halfway: he graduated in law (*Giurisprudenza*) at the University of Pisa in 1954, passed the Centro Sperimentale entrance exam, won a scholarship, and graduated from the CSC in directing in 1956. His diploma film was the short *I ragazzi di Trastevere*, a Pasolini-inflected story about boys of the popular Roman quarter, based on Pasolini's novel *Ragazzi di vita*. At the CSC he was a film critic for the Centro's journal *Bianco e Nero* and an avid follower of European and American film, favouring John Ford, Raoul Walsh and Michael Curtiz -- the last two he would name, for the rest of his life, as his masters ahead of all others. [C30644] [C30646] [C30650]

After graduation Lenzi kept writing: journalist and film critic for various newspapers and magazines including *Bianco e Nero*, and between 1957 and 1960 he penned a number of detective novels and adventure stories under a pseudonym -- the titles were not identified by any source read this run, and none is asserted here. [C30647] His production career began at the bottom of the call sheet: location scout for the 1958 film *Raw Wind in Eden* (dir. Richard Wilson), a bit part as an actor ("Soldier on Train") in Alessandro Blasetti's *Amore e chiacchiere* (1958), and assistant director on Renzo Merusi's *Apocalisse sul fiume giallo* (1960) and Domenico Paolella's *Il terrore dei mari* (1961), for which he is also credited as writer only. [C30648] His first film as director was shot in Greece in 1958 -- *Mia Italida stin Ellada*, also listed as *Vacanze ad Atene* (*An Italian in Greece*) -- and was never released. His official, first credited feature as director was the 1961 pirate film *Queen of the Seas* (*Le avventure di Mary Read*). [C30649]

Lenzi was married to Olga Pehar, who co-wrote some of his films -- her dates (1938-2015) and a daughter, Alessandra Lenzi, come from a single fan blog and are not asserted here -- and he always declared himself an anarchist, the Italian Wikipedia lead stating it flatly ("Sempre dichiaratosi anarchico"). He sometimes signed with Anglophone pseudonyms: Hank Milestone, Bob Collins, Humphrey Humbert and Harry Kirkpatrick -- and two of those four would resurface, decades later, as the credits under which he hid his late horror films, which is the first hint that the pseudonym was not a joke but a tool: a name you could put on a film you did not want to own. [C30650] [C30666]

The constants of the whole career are already visible in the formation, named on the filmography page: prominent close-ups of his actors, zoom-lens effects, and a self-image as a storyteller "not heavy-handed with cinematic effects." The law degree matters more than it looks: Lenzi's films are built like briefs -- a situation stated, a mechanism applied, an outcome forced -- and the CSC years gave him the Ford/Walsh/Curtiz diet, the classical American grammar of the hero, the exotic location and the set-piece, which he would spend forty years re-skinning for whatever genre was selling that year. [C30660]

**Takeaway:** A career can be built out of finishing the degree and then ignoring it -- the law graduate who learned the classical grammar at film school and spent forty years applying it to whatever the market was buying that year, one genre at a time.

## 2. The Salgari machine

The first phase is swashbucklers, historical adventure and peplum, eleven features in four years, nearly all of them literary or legendary recycling: *Queen of the Seas* (1961), the pirate Mary Read film and official debut; *Duel of Fire* (1962); *The Triumph of Robin Hood* (1962) and *The Invincible Masked Rider* (1963), two Robin Hood features; *Catherine of Russia* (1963); *Samson and the Slave Queen* (*Zorro contro Maciste*, 1963), the Zorro/Maciste hybrid that one western blog counts as a western; *Sandokan the Great* (1963) and *Pirates of Malaysia* (1964), the two Emilio Salgari adaptations, the first starring Steve Reeves; *Temple of the White Elephant* (1964); *Adventures of the Bengal Lancers* (1964); *Messalina vs. the Son of Hercules* (1964). [C30652]

The mechanism that matters here is adaptation-as-system: Salgari's novels gave Lenzi pre-sold stories, exotic locations and a star (Reeves) in one package, and the films were built to be sold territory by territory. The Italian Wikipedia records one production fact that shows the machine colliding with the world: during the shooting of *Pirates of Malaysia* the crew was caught by the outbreak of the civil war that led to Singapore's separation from Malaysia. [C30653] What stayed constant through the phase is the adventure grammar -- the hero, the exotic location, the set-piece -- learned from the Walsh/Curtiz/Ford diet of his CSC years. What changed is the scale: the hero gets bigger and the location gets further away, from Sherwood to Malaya, and the pre-sold property does the marketing before a frame is shot.

**Takeaway:** Start with a story the audience already owns -- Salgari, Robin Hood, Maciste -- and the film only has to deliver the location and the star; the plot arrives pre-sold.

## 3. The numbers game

On the wave of the James Bond films, Lenzi made four spy films in two years for producer Fortunato Misiano. The numbering is the joke and the system: he could not have 007, so he had 008. *008: Operation Exterminate* (*A 008, operazione Sterminio*, released 3 September 1965) is an Italian/Egyptian co-production filmed in Egypt and Switzerland, directed and written by Lenzi: British agent 006 (Alberto Lupo, as Frank Smith) and American agent 008 (Ingrid Schoeller, as MacDonald, masquerading as lounge singer Piper Astor) hunt a stolen anti-radar device from Cairo to Switzerland, and the twist ending, set in a bowling alley, reveals 006 is a Russian spy -- then that he is a British spy after all. The Eurospy film article notes this film "features the first female Bond-type hero." The MacGuffin (the anti-radar), the exotic location, the double-cross: the whole Bond formula reduced to its load-bearing parts and shot cheap. Then *Super Seven Calling Cairo* (1965), *Last Man to Kill* (1966) and *The Spy Who Loved Flowers* (1966): three films starring the American actor Roger Browne as the series hero, Lenzi writing two of the three. And *Kriminal* (1966): the adaptation of the *fumetti neri* comic character, credited by the filmography page as the first film adaptation of an Italian *fumetti neri* comic. [C30654] There is no 077 film anywhere in Lenzi's filmography, and no Secret Agent 077 series; the numbers are 008 (Schoeller) and Super Seven (Browne). [C30655]

The war films follow, and war was, the Italian Wikipedia says, one of Lenzi's favourite genres: *Desert Commandos* (1967) and *Battle of the Commandos* (*La legione dei dannati*, 1969), the second the more interesting object -- a screenplay by the young Dario Argento, a rich Titanus production, and, per the Italian Wikipedia, "a sort of re-reading of *The Guns of Navarone* (1961)." He would return to war in 1978-79 with *The Greatest Battle* and *From Hell to Victory*. [C30657] Then the two unambiguous spaghetti westerns, both 1968: *Tutto per tutto* (*Go for Broke*, also *All Out*) and *Una pistola per cento bare* (*A Pistol for a Hundred Coffins*). Both are director-only credits -- he did not write them -- which is itself a signal: the westerns were assignments taken as the genre peaked, not authored statements. The "three westerns" count depends on the 1963 Zorro/Maciste hybrid; the safe wording is two in 1968, a third if the hybrid is counted. [C30656]

**Takeaway:** When you cannot have the number, take the next one down and make the number the joke -- 008 is not a copy of 007, it is a confession that the copy is the product, and the product is the formula with its load-bearing parts intact.

## 4. The high-neighbourhood thriller

The pivot that matters most for the study's mechanism set. After the westerns, Lenzi found his first durable formula: the erotic thriller starring the ex-Hollywood star Carroll Baker. The Italian Wikipedia says he invented a subgenre with it, the "giallo erotico italiano," which he himself later called the "thriller dei quartieri alti" (the high-neighbourhood thriller): a trilogy of *Orgasmo* (1969), *Così dolce... così perversa* (*So Sweet... So Perverse*, 1969) and *Paranoia* (*A Quiet Place to Kill*, 1970), all starring Baker, combining eroticism, psychology and the intrigues of the aristocratic world. The same page says *Orgasmo* was one of the best-selling films in the United States in that period. English Wikipedia adds the influence line: the trilogy drew on French film noir, from Jacques Deray and René Clément. [C30658] The Baker films had box-office success in Italy and were "influenced by French 'film noir' movies drawing from the works of Jacques Deray and René Clement." [C30681]

The mechanism, stated plainly: take a faded Hollywood name (Baker), put her in a beautiful house among rich people with secrets, and let sex, money and murder rotate through the same rooms. The close-ups and zooms from the formation years are the camera side of the same trick: the face tells you what the plot will not. Lenzi directed eight giallo films in all: *Orgasmo* (1969), *So Sweet... So Perverse* (1969), *A Quiet Place to Kill* (1970), *Oasis of Fear* (1971), *Seven Bloodstained Orchids* (1972), *Knife of Ice* (1972), *Spasmo* (1974), *Eyeball* (1975). VHS Revival: "That's three more than Mario Bava, and Bava invented the genre. That's two more than Lucio Fulci... Only Dario Argento, the undisputed king of giallo, matched Lenzi's output." Four of the eight starred Carroll Baker. [C30682]

The first cycle (1969-1971) is Hitchcockian and pre-Argento, and its engine is deception in a closed luxurious location. *Orgasmo* (1969, aka *Paranoia*): wealthy American widow Kathryn West (Baker) flees the US to live in seclusion at her late husband's Italian villa; two counterculture hippies (Lou Castel, Colette Descombes) move in and lure her into a debauched lifestyle, to an end she does not foresee. The mechanism is "a helpless character is under threat from some sort of nefarious manipulator," modelled on Hitchcock's *Strangers on a Train* (1951) and *Dial M for Murder* (1954), "stir in nudity and graphic violence, and a pinch of cynicism." VHS Revival's key craft claim: "it's easier to string together set-piece kills than it is to assemble a character-driven slow burn" -- Lenzi's first-cycle skill is sustained tension, not murder set-pieces. [C30683] *So Sweet... So Perverse* (1969): married businessman Jean Reynaud (Jean-Louis Trintignant) becomes obsessed with his new upstairs neighbour Nicole (Baker), who claims to be terrorised by an abusive boyfriend (Horst Frank). The mechanism is "deception is laid upon deception"; what begins as relationship melodrama "slowly morphs into a thriller... a carefully crafted, character-driven mystery that grows darker with each chapter." Structural devices: the *Vertigo* parallel (Baker as Lenzi's Kim Novak figure) and the *Psycho* parallel (the protagonist changes midway through the film). The screenplay is by Ernesto Gastaldi, who also wrote *The Sweet Body of Deborah* (1968), the template film. Nerdist: three of the four Baker films "hew very closely to the formula of *The Sweet Body of Deborah*. Baker plays a wealthy socialite who falls in with dangerous, handsome men. Eventually a third party enters frame; a parade of sex, drink, gaslighting, and murder ensues until the guilty parties pay. The innocent often do as well." [C30684] *A Quiet Place to Kill* (1970, *Paranoia*): race-car driver Helen (Baker), injured in a crash, is invited by her cad ex-husband (Jean Sorel) to recuperate at his Mallorcan villa, where she finds him remarried with an adult stepdaughter; she becomes entangled in a murder plot and it is unclear whether she is meant to be victim or killer. The mechanism: "Lenzi's aim is tension and suspense... turning the screws when the evidence piles up against the murderer. We want the killer to be caught, right? Or maybe we don't. A very Hitchcockian manipulation." Spectacle: it "plays like a murder mystery featuring beautiful locations, double-crossing socialites, love triangles, and exciting plot twists," lacking "excessive gore or a black-gloved killer." [C30685] *Oasis of Fear* (1971, *Un posto ideale per uccidere*): two hippies fleeing the Italian police (Ray Lovelock, Ornella Muti) take refuge at the remote estate of a lonely older woman (Irene Papas), who has her own manipulative agenda. The mechanism is *Orgasmo* with the poles reversed -- this time the hippies are the protagonists and the older woman the manipulator. VHS Revival: "Umberto Lenzi has a low opinion of hippies... these 'heroes' are hard to like. As Hitchcock did with the murderous Norman Bates, Lenzi still manages to make you care about what happens to them." It is "the last of the director's giallo films done in his homegrown style." [C30686]

**Takeaway:** The first-cycle giallo is not a murder film but a deception film -- a beautiful house, a lonely woman, a stranger with a story, and the slow rotation of sex, money and murder through the same rooms until someone pays.

## 5. The killer's itinerary

The second cycle (1972-1975) follows Argento's *The Bird with the Crystal Plumage* (1970), and the trap moves outdoors: from the victim's address to the killer's itinerary. *Seven Bloodstained Orchids* (1972, *Sette orchidee macchiate di rosso*, released 24 February 1972 -- not 1971, as Spectacle Theater's series page dates it): a slasher murders women and leaves a silver half-moon pendant at each scene; a would-be victim, Giulia (Uschi Glas), fakes her own death after a train attack, and she and her husband Mario (Antonio Sabato) investigate. The mechanism shift is complete: "murderer POV shots, a high body count, and knifings and power-tool killings"; "instead of being portrayed as clueless side characters, the police take center stage"; "fast-paced, humourless, and plot driven and... absent the colourful melodrama that featured so prominently in the Carroll Baker entries." Sources: loosely based on an Edgar Wallace novel and Cornell Woolrich's *Rendezvous in Black* (Lenzi's stated main inspiration); score by Riz Ortolani; Lenzi said in interviews it was his favourite giallo after *Orgasmo*. [C30687] [C30708] *Knife of Ice* (1972, *Il coltello di ghiaccio*): a mute woman (Baker, reunited with Lenzi) in a small town grows paranoid as a black-gloved slasher picks off people around her. VHS Revival: "the director had moved on entirely from his Hitchcock-influenced approach and accepted, though perhaps not embraced, the proto-slasher style that largely defined the genre by 1972... a well-made and efficient giallo, but it does not bear as much of a personal stamp as Lenzi's and Baker's earlier collaborations." [C30688] *Spasmo* (1974): a man (Robert Hoffman) meets a woman (Suzy Kendall) on a beach who at first looks like a corpse washed ashore; then everyone starts acting weird, people die, and he cannot tell dream from reality. The mechanism is surrealism and deliberate incoherence -- "Characters die, and later they're fine. People disappear and later reappear. Mannequins pop up in strange places." VHS Revival: "one of Lenzi's most stylishly shot, visually interesting films." The score is by Ennio Morricone -- his first of two Lenzi collaborations, the second being *Almost Human* a few months later. [C30689] *Eyeball* (1975, *Gatti rossi in un labirinto di vetro*): tourists on a Barcelona bus tour are picked off by a slasher in a rain slicker who removes each victim's left eye; Inspector Tudela investigates. The mechanism is satire. VHS Revival: "it is a comedy. Or a satire at least... he is mocking the genre for how lowbrow it had become by 1975"; the tourists are unconcerned, red herrings are ridiculous, dialogue is "comically over the top." Spectacle agrees: "an intentionally low-brow film that never takes itself too seriously," Lenzi's reflection on a market "oversaturated with subpar Gialli." It was Lenzi's last giallo. [C30690]

What distinguishes Lenzi's gialli from Argento's and Bava's is chronology and definition both. Chronology: Lenzi's first giallo, *Orgasmo*, came "a year before Dario Argento re-invented the genre as we understand it today" with *The Bird with the Crystal Plumage* (1970). His model was Hitchcock and French film noir, not Bava: "Lenzi's major influence for *Orgasmo* was not Mario Bava but Alfred Hitchcock." The definitional difference: for Lenzi's first cycle, "there must be an element of mystery. There must be an element of deception. And some combination of sex and violence. Black-gloved killers and body counts became major tropes of giallo cinema, but not consistently so until the 1970s." Argento's giallo is the body-count set-piece film; Lenzi's early giallo is the character-driven deception film. Cagey Films: Lenzi's Baker films "hearken back to the women-in-peril melodramas Hollywood was so fond of in the 1940s and '50s, updated with explicit sexuality and a surprising amount of nudity... each story centres on a neurotic woman who gradually seems to lose her grip on reality," and "Baker's characters are a long way from being innocent victims." Nerdist contrasts them directly with Argento: the Baker films are "female-centric gialli that stood in contrast to Argento's body count horrors." The location engine moves with the cycles: the first cycle is built on the closed luxurious location -- the Italian villa (*Orgasmo*), the Paris apartment building (*So Sweet*), the Mallorcan villa (*A Quiet Place to Kill*), the remote estate (*Oasis of Fear*) -- a trap the protagonist walks into voluntarily (loneliness, lust, injury, flight from police) and cannot leave once the deception closes; the second cycle moves the trap outdoors (Rome streets, trains, the beach, the Barcelona tour bus) and the trap becomes the killer's itinerary instead of the victim's address. The verdict arc is a journeyman's arc, and VHS Revival makes the journeyman point explicitly: "all the popular Italian genre directors, aside from Dario Argento, were journeymen... Lenzi demonstrated great adaptability, as a top journeyman director must." Lenzi held out against the black-glove style for a year after Argento's success, then adopted it wholesale, then abandoned character for surrealism, then mocked the whole genre -- and then left it. [C30691] The scale of the apprenticeship is worth stating plainly: VHS Revival says Lenzi "had directed 25 films before Argento directed one," but counting the filmography gives about 23 features between *Queen of the Seas* (1961) and *A Quiet Place to Kill* (1970), Argento's debut year -- so the "25" is the author's rounded figure, close but not exact, and the study says "more than twenty features." [C30707]

**Takeaway:** Lenzi's giallo splits in two -- a Hitchcockian first cycle about deception in closed luxurious rooms, and an Argento-imitating second cycle about a killer's itinerary through open streets -- and he ended the second cycle by satirising it, which is the journeyman's honest exit from a genre he had outlived.

## 6. The accidental cannibal

*Man from the Deep River* (Italian: *Il paese del sesso selvaggio*, *The Country of Savage Sex*) was released in Italy 8 August 1972 and in the US 23 May 1973 as *Sacrifice!*. Directed by Lenzi, written by Francesco Barilli and Massimo D'Avack, produced by Ovidio G. Assonitis, Stenio Fiorentini, Giorgio Carlo Rossi and Marcello Soffiantini. It stars Ivan Rassimov as British photographer John Bradley, Me Me Lai as Maraya, Pratitsak Singhara as Taima. Cinematography Riccardo Pallottini, music Daniele Patucchi, 93 minutes. [C30670] The "first Italian cannibal film" claim needs its two qualifications in the same sentence: the film is "perhaps best known for starting the 'cannibal boom' of Italian exploitation cinema during the late 1970s and early 1980s" and "is seen as either the inspiration or the beginning of the cannibal genre, as the combination of the rain forest setting and onscreen cannibalism was not seen until its release" -- BUT the boom itself "did not start until Ruggero Deodato released his film *Ultimo mondo cannibale* in 1977," and "director Umberto Lenzi said that cannibalism was not intended to be the film's central theme." The honest wording is: Lenzi made the first film of the type, five years before the cycle existed; he did not set out to found a genre. [C30671]

The mechanism is borrowed, not invented. The film and its title were "mainly inspired by *A Man Called Horse*" (1970), which also featured a white man incorporated into a tribe that originally held him captive; and Lenzi was "trying to imitate the content of notorious Mondo cinema," popular since Gualtiero Jacopetti and Paolo Cavara's *Mondo Cane* (1962). Mondo-Digital calls it "a bridge between the popular, morally questionable mondo movies... and the much more ferocious, extreme cannibal films." [C30672] The engine, from the full plot summary, runs in five beats: (1) Outsider enters: photographer John Bradley travels upriver in Thailand, his guide Tuan is killed, and a native tribe captures him in a net, hanging him as a "fish-man." (2) Incorporation by ordeal: the chief's daughter Maraya argues he is a man, not a fish; he is kept as her slave, then put through rituals and torture until accepted as a warrior; he repays the tribe with modern technology and medicine. (3) Rival tribe: the tribe is at war with the more primitive Kuru cannibals, who attack and eat a young girl. (4) The turn: after six months John joins the warriors in fighting the Kuru and "participates in activities he had once condemned." (5) The exit that fails: Maraya falls ill and blind from pregnancy; John tries to take her to civilization for treatment, is forced back; she dies in childbirth under a black-butterfly death omen; a rescue helicopter flies over and John takes cover with the tribe instead of signalling it. The engine is capture, incorporation, moral absorption, failed return. The cannibalism itself is confined to the rival Kuru; the film is, as Moria notes, "not even a horror film, more a cod-anthropological drama about native life" with "little actual cannibalism... just a brief encounter with an enemy tribe." [C30673] Released in America it "would prove successful on Times Square's 42nd Street under the title of *Sacrifice!*," opening the way for similar films. Lenzi was even offered *Ultimo mondo cannibale*, but the producers chose Ruggero Deodato "when they refused to match Lenzi's price." [C30674] The film was a UK video nasty (BBFC rejected it for cinema in 1975; on the DPP list in 1983; banned under the Video Recordings Act 1984; passed 18 only in 2003 after nearly four minutes of cuts, and again cut in 2016). [C30675]

*Eaten Alive!* (Italian: *Mangiati vivi!*, released in Italy 20 March 1980) is the dry run. Written and directed by Lenzi, starring Robert Kerman (Mark Butler), Janet Agren (Sheila Morris), Ivan Rassimov (Jonas), Paola Senatore (Diana), Me Me Lai (Mowara), Mel Ferrer. The plot engine is now standardised: a New York trigger (a blow-dart killer murdering people in New York City; a canister of 8mm film found in his pocket) sends a white search party into the jungle (New Guinea) to find a missing white woman (Diana). The jungle now contains TWO horrors stacked: a Jim Jones-style cult run by Jonas, who physically and sexually abuses followers and locals, and a cannibal tribe. The searchers find Diana brainwashed; Diana and Mowara are caught by cannibals, raped, cut up and eaten while Mark and Sheila watch helplessly; the cult then commits ritual suicide, leaving one young female survivor. Genre Grinder states the shared template explicitly: "following some kind of frightening/murderous event in America (almost always New York City), a group of white folks venture into a rainforest in search of a different group of white folks that went missing." The Jonestown mechanism: "The plot of the film was heavily inspired by the 1978 Jonestown massacre." Jonas is a Reverend Jones stand-in, and the cult, not the tribe, is the film's main engine of atrocity; DVDBeaver argues "it can be argued rather convincingly that the cannibals are the good guys." The production mechanism is cheapness as method. Mondo-Digital: Lenzi "pulled the same trick" as a clip-compilation film, "cobbling together clips from the likes of *Jungle Holocaust*, *The Man from Deep River*, and *Mountain of the Cannibal God* by way of a new storyline inspired by the Jim Jones tragedy." Genre Grinder calls it "a less notorious, but similarly revolting copy/paste dry-run" made "just before *Ferox*." Lenzi directed it back-to-back with *Cannibal Ferox*; both feature Robert Kerman. [C30676]

*Cannibal Ferox* (released in Italy 24 April 1981) turns the mechanism inside out. Written and directed by Lenzi, produced by Mino Loy and Luciano Martino, starring Giovanni Lombardo Radice (as John Morghen) as Mike Logan, Lorraine De Selle as Gloria Davis, Danilo Mattei as Rudy, Zora Kerova as Pat, Robert Kerman as Lt. Rizzo. 93 minutes. Filmed on location in Leticia, Amazonas, Colombia and New York City, interiors at Elios Studios, Rome. [C30677] [C30678] Three New Yorkers (siblings Gloria and Rudy, friend Pat) go to the Colombian jungle to prove Gloria's thesis that cannibalism is a myth. They meet drug dealer Mike and his wounded partner Joe, who claim cannibals attacked them. The reveal inverts the 1972 engine: Joe, dying, confesses that he and Mike came to exploit the natives for emeralds and cocaine; high on cocaine, Mike tortured and castrated the tribe's guide in full view of the village. The tribe's violence is revenge, not nature. The set-pieces escalate as punishment mirroring the original crime: Mike is castrated with a machete and his penis eaten; Rudy is caught in a booby trap and finished with a poisoned dart; Pat is hung by hooks through her breasts; Mike's skull is cut open and his brain eaten. Gloria alone survives and then LIES: she publishes a book, *Cannibalism: End of a Myth*, repeating the natives' cover story that the others were eaten by crocodiles. The film ends on the thesis it set out to disprove being fraudulently confirmed in print. [C30677] In production, actor Giovanni Lombardo Radice was "highly-critical of the film, considering his character one-dimensional and objecting to the unsimulated animal violence"; during filming he refused a scene where his character slaughtered a pig, Lenzi replied "[Robert De Niro] would do it," Radice answered "De Niro would kick your ass all the way back to Rome," and the scene was done by a body double. Radice used the stage name "John Morghen" to disassociate himself, though he later admitted the film brought him fans worldwide. The score is by Fiamma Maglione (who also appears as Myrna) and Roberto Donati. [C30678] In reception, the US distributor claimed it was "the most violent film ever made," and it was "dubiously claimed to be 'banned in 31 countries'" -- the word "dubiously" is Wikipedia's, so the 31-countries figure is presented here as a marketing claim, not a fact. It opened in New York in September 1983 as *Make Them Die Slowly*; Aquarius Releasing's marketing was a sensational trailer, no print ads, and the Liberty Theatre marquee on 42nd Street covered in banners; it was "hugely successful at venues that would play it" but most cinemas shunned it, with perhaps only 100 theatres nationwide willing to show it. In the UK it was banned under the Obscene Publications Act and sat on the video nasties list; the full version was only submitted to the BBFC in 2018 and still received 2 minutes of cuts. Rotten Tomatoes: 40% from five reviews. [C30679] The Lenzi-Deodato rivalry runs both ways: in a 2015 interview Deodato "identified the film as a knockoff of his own *Cannibal Holocaust* (1980) -- the two films share a similar plot and many cast members and locations"; Lenzi "claimed that Deodato's film had copied his own, earlier film *Man from the Deep River* (1972)." Genre Grinder's verdict: Deodato made "the best overall cannibal film" with *Cannibal Holocaust*, "Lenzi making the most cannibal films with a total of three," and *Ferox* was "the last 'pure' Italian cannibal film to make any kind of international box-office impact." [C30680]

The mechanism changed across the three films, and the change is the story: 1972, cannibalism is incidental (one rival-tribe scene); the film is an incorporation drama; the white man is absorbed and stays -- the tribe is a world to enter. 1980, cannibalism is one of two stacked atrocity engines (cult plus tribe); the white search party is the victim pool; the film is assembled from stock footage plus a ripped-from-the-headlines cult plot -- the jungle is a clip library. 1981, cannibalism is revenge for white crimes; the white man is the monster and the tribe the executioner; the survivor then lies about it in a book -- the thesis (cannibalism is a myth) is both the premise and the final fraud. The through-line for prompts: the outsider who enters a closed world and is processed by it, with the processing getting more literal each time (ritual acceptance, dismemberment, castration and brain-eating).

**Takeaway:** Lenzi did not invent the cannibal film on purpose; he made a Mondo-flavoured *A Man Called Horse* copy in which the cannibalism was a two-minute incident, and the genre grew out of the incident, not the film -- and across his three films the tribe stops being a world to enter and becomes an executioner.

## 7. The escalation ladder

The late 1960s began "a period of political violence and social unrest in Italy known as the Years of Lead. Italian filmmakers processed the movement by creating a wave of ultra-violent crime films known as Poliziotteschi. These films not only showcase the criminals' ferocity but also expose parallel brutality and corruption from within the police force." Spectacle: "For four years after *Eyeball*, Lenzi turned his attention exclusively to Poliziotteschi films and directed ten movies in the genre between 1974 and 1979" -- but counting the filmography gives nine in that window (*Almost Human*, *Manhunt in the City*, *Syndicate Sadists*, *The Tough Ones*, *Free Hand for a Tough Cop*, *Violent Naples*, *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist*, *Brothers Till We Die*, *From Corleone to Brooklyn*), ten only if *Gang War in Milan* (1973) is counted, which falls outside the stated window. The study says nine, ten counting *Gang War in Milan*. [C30703] [C30704]

*Gang War in Milan* (Italian: *Milano rovente*, released 23 February 1973) is the first crime film. Directed by Lenzi, screenplay by Franco Enna and Lenzi, starring Antonio Sabato (Salvatore Cangemi), Philippe Leroy (Roger Daverty), Antonio Casagrande, Carla Romanelli. It grossed 631.702 million lire domestically. The plot engine: Salvatore "Toto" Cangemi is a Milan produce vendor who moonlights as the city's biggest pimp; French drug kingpin Daverty ("Le Capitaine") wants Toto's girls to sell his drugs and unite organised crime under himself; Toto refuses; a turf war escalates on the "they put one of ours in the hospital, we put one of theirs in the morgue" credo. It was "Lenzi's first entry in the crime genre after making several spy and action films in the 1960s"; he rewrote the script because the original was "closer to a 1930s crime film than a film noir." Lenzi's own verdict on the mechanism is the first statement of his poliziottesco problem: he later felt the film had "a basic error on the behalf of the producers... We shouldn't have made the protagonist a pimp. You can have a pickpocket, a drug dealer, or a killer, but not a pimp, because the viewer doesn't sympathize with him. He's a dirty scoundrel with whom you can't identify." The criminal-protagonist needs the audience's identification, and the film's job is to engineer it. He solved it in the next film by making the criminal a psychopath the audience watches rather than roots for, and later by making him a clown. [C30692]

*Almost Human* (Italian: *Milano odia: la polizia non può sparare*, *Milan Hates: The Police Can't Shoot*, released 8 August 1974) is the criminal as protagonist. Directed by Lenzi, screenplay by Ernesto Gastaldi, produced by Luciano Martino (Dania Film), starring Tomas Milian (Giulio Sacchi), Henry Silva (Inspector Walter Grandi), Laura Belli (Marilu Porrino), Ray Lovelock (Carmine), Gino Santercole (Vittorio), Anita Strindberg. Music by Ennio Morricone. 100 minutes. It grossed ITL 1,168,745,000 domestically. The plot engine is the escalation ladder, stated as a sequence: Sacchi, a small-time criminal, waits in a getaway car during a bank robbery and shoots a policeman who merely asks him to move the car; he kidnaps a child, leads police on a high-speed chase, stabs an officer who catches him stealing coins from a cigarette machine; he then decides petty crime does not pay and kidnaps heiress Marilu Porrino, murdering her boyfriend, her family protectors, his own girlfriend (drowned), and his gun supplier "Papa" to avoid paying a deposit; he strings three naked bourgeois victims to a chandelier and spins them like a roulette wheel before machine-gunning them; he finally murders the hostage himself, survives the shoot-out, and is shot dead by Grandi the next day. The film "ends with its criminal protagonist gunned down in a pile of trash." What Milian does mechanically: Sacchi kills "even when it clearly goes against his own self-interest" (Slant). Nocturno's Manlio Gomarasca: "Sacchi is probably the most chilling villain figure brought to the screen in an Italian poliziesco/noir precisely because he is fundamentally weak, insecure and at the same time crazy, greedy and ruthless. Milian is superb as he descends into this whirlwind of depravation and doubts, building up on his face an imperceptible array of nervous tics, sweating profusely for the whole film and whimpering like a child when his character ends up in the clutches of the police." Dave Kehr (*New York Times*, 2005 DVD pick): Milian's portrayal is "close to Al Pacino's *Scarface*, but without the camp exaggeration" and the film "anticipates John McNaughton's notorious *Henry: Portrait of a Serial Killer* in its up-close sadism but displays even less moral distance from its repellent protagonist." [C30693] Lenzi's own framing: he "did not consider it to be a poliziesco because it centers on the criminals, and as such more closely resembles French film noirs, such as those of Jacques Becker." He did no rewrites on Gastaldi's script, "marking the first time he did not contribute to the narrative of one of his films." The original title is a callback to *Milano trema: la polizia vuole giustizia* (*The Violent Professionals*, 1973), a Gastaldi/Martino hit; Sergio Martino was first asked to direct and passed. The production was a tightly run shoot: Lenzi's contract allowed seven weeks, a limit of 15,000 feet of film, and the obligation to build a car chase around stock footage from *Milano trema*. Milian was dubbed for the first time by Ferruccio Amendola, who became a fixture of his crime cycle. Morricone reviewed the assembly cut with Lenzi and instructed where to put music and where to keep silence; his wife was so upset by the film's violence she dissuaded him from further Lenzi collaborations. The film received an under-18 ban in Italy. [C30694] Contemporary Italian reviews were hostile (Paese Sera: "Tomas Milian at his worst, almost a caricature of himself"; L'Unità: "this worthless mess" pushing "the reactionary thesis according to which the police should be given free rein"). It is "now regarded as one of the best Italian crime films of the 1970s, and marked the first of several collaborations between Lenzi and Milian." In the US it flopped twice over (Chicago 1975 as *The Kidnap of Mary Lou*: 3,200 patrons, $11,200; New York 1980 as *Almost Human*: $120,000 across 43 screens), partly because distributor Joseph Brenner mis-marketed it as a horror film with key art stolen from the 1920 film *The Golem*. It was shown at the 2024 San Sebastián Festival retrospective "Violent Italy" and in 2026 at the French Cinémathèque's "Italie, années de plomb." [C30693] [C30694]

*Manhunt in the City* (Italian: *L'uomo della strada fa giustizia*, *The Average Man Gets Justice*, released 8 May 1975) is the vigilante subversion. Directed by Lenzi, starring Henry Silva (David Vannucchi), Luciana Paluzzi, Silvano Tranquilli, Raymond Pellegrin. During a jewel robbery, a gang shoots a construction surveyor's daughter, who dies whispering "a scorpion" (the killer's bracelet). Her father Vannucchi, frustrated by police impotence, investigates himself, is courted by a secret vigilante organisation, and is "subtly encouraged by authority figures to take justice into his own hands." The mechanism inverts *Death Wish*: Vannucchi is incompetent, follows wrong leads, gets people killed, and in the climax massacres the WRONG gang with a shotgun -- the real murderer was killed by police the night before. His actions were manipulated by a fascist lawyer heading the vigilante army, "resulting in tragic consequences and the punishment of an innocent man." The film ends with Vannucchi reaching for his gun at a police chase while his wife begs him to stop. The casting note matters: the Sacchi role in *Almost Human* was originally meant for Henry Silva; Milian insisted on taking it. Lenzi "rectified this by giving Silva the lead here." Silva's "restrained and detached performance aligned with the film's themes"; Lenzi later cited the casting as "central to the film's unsettling tone." [C30695]

*Syndicate Sadists* (Italian: *Il giustiziere sfida la città*, *The Executioner Challenges the City*, released 16 August 1975) is Rambo before Rambo. Directed by Lenzi, story and screenplay by Vincenzo Mannino, produced by Luciano Martino (Dania Film/Medusa), starring Tomas Milian (Rambo), Joseph Cotten (Paterno), Maria Fiore. 93 minutes. Rambo, a biker and ex-cop, visits a friend who works for a private anti-mob police force; the friend is murdered while investigating the kidnapping of a doctor's young son, a by-product of a war between two crime families (Paterno's and Conti's). Rambo's mechanism is to play the two families against each other -- he feeds each side information, lets them slaughter each other, and rescues the boy in the chaos. The name: the film "predates Ted Kotcheff's *First Blood*... by seven years. Tomas Milian happened to read David Morrell's novel while flying from the U.S. to Rome. Loving the story he tried to talk some Italian producers into making a film out of it, with him starring as John Rambo. Nothing came of this, but he was allowed to use the Rambo moniker in the next poliziottesco he starred in." The film borrows nothing else from the novel; Lenzi said he was "more influenced by Don Siegel's crime films." [C30696]

*The Tough Ones* (Italian: *Roma a mano armata*, *Rome Armed to the Teeth*, released 25 February 1976) is the system film. Directed by Lenzi, screenplay by Dardano Sacchetti, story by Lenzi, produced by Mino Loy and Luciano Martino, starring Maurizio Merli (Inspector Leonardo Tanzi), Tomas Milian (Vincenzo Moretto, "The Hunchback"), Arthur Kennedy, Giampiero Albertini, Ivan Rassimov, Biagio Pelligra. Music by Franco Micalizzi. 95 minutes. It grossed ITL 1.617 billion domestically. The plot engine is multi-threaded and episodic: Tanzi, head of Rome's anti-gang squad, hunts Emmanuel Dominique Ferrender, a French crime boss with a monopoly on Rome's underworld. Threads: bank robber Savelli (one-armed); Tony the pimp (Rassimov), who overdoses a teenage girl with heroin; Moretto, a hunchbacked slaughterhouse worker and Savelli's brother-in-law, whom Tanzi arrests by planting drugs in his Porsche and humiliates by forcing him to swallow a bullet. Moretto's friends avenge him by kidnapping Tanzi's girlfriend Anna (a juvenile court magistrate), taking her to a junkyard and pretending to crush her in the machinery. The reveal: Ferrender is already dead -- Moretto killed him weeks earlier; the whole hunt was chasing a ghost. Moretto kills Tanzi's partner Caputo with Tanzi's own sidearm; Tanzi shoots Moretto dead with Caputo's gun. What Milian does mechanically: Moretto is the engine of the film's second half. He converts humiliation into status: arrested, planted with drugs, forced to swallow a bullet, he rises through the criminal ranks by murdering the boss above him and taking his place. His signature object is the bullet Tanzi made him swallow -- he produces it later ("I shat this out just for you") as a promise of revenge. Daily Grindhouse: Moretto is "very Scorpio-ish... somehow manages to out-ooze Dirty Harry's Andrew Robinson." The Hunchback was modelled on a butcher Lenzi knew in childhood; Ferrender on Jacques Berenguer, a real French mobster operating in Rome at the time. The production facts are the method: Lenzi was first offered a spy story set in Trastevere, *Roma ha un segreto*, rejected it as nonsense, and asked to make a film about contemporary urban violence; "within a week, a new script was improvised." Sacchetti's script was written quickly to replace one Lenzi disliked; Lenzi found it their best collaboration though Sacchetti's output "often lacked structure and required rewrites." Filmed end of 1975 under the working title *Roma vuole giustizia*; the bulk of the motor chases were filmed without a permit, in real traffic -- at one point two real police cars joined the chase and Lenzi had to sort it out at a police station. The film "emulates the formula of *Violent Rome*," Merli's hit for rival producer Edmondo Amati, "although it is widely considered to be the superior film." Milian was booked for fewer days and billed as special guest star because he demanded too much money; his name was later put in larger font on the billboards. *Variety* (April 1976): "Milian stands out with his flamboyant portrayal of the Hunchback... Lenzi keeps his action, cast and camera moving at a fast clip." *Il Messaggero*: it "stands out both for narrative content and for a remarkable degree of showmanship." L'Unità (far left): it presents "a blatantly fascistic ideology" and is "worse than its predecessor." Retrospective: AllMovie calls it "a memorably outrageous example of the Italian crime film... a string of breathless comic-book displays of crime and action." It was selected for the BFI's 2011 book *100 Cult Films*. The US release as *Brutal Justice* (1978-79) was re-credited to a fake director, Simon Nuchtern, who had only shot a few New York establishing shots. [C30697]

*Violent Naples* (Italian: *Napoli violenta*, released 7 August 1976) is the franchise entry. Directed by Lenzi, starring Maurizio Merli as Inspector Betti. Domestic gross 2,046,936,220 lire -- the biggest Lenzi poliziottesco gross in this leg's set. Betti, the unorthodox cop from *Violent Rome* (1975, Marino Girolami), is sent to Naples to battle a crime wave as two syndicates wage war -- home invasions, muggings, a protection racket. The Spinning Image: Lenzi takes his cue from *Bullitt*, *Dirty Harry* and *The French Connection*, plus Castellari's vigilante thrillers and French policiers (Lautner, Verneuil, Deray), and "envisions Naples as a lawless cesspool inhabited by thieves, rapists and slavering scumbags." It was the second in a trilogy of Betti films (*Violent Rome* 1975, *Violent Naples* 1976, *A Special Cop in Action* 1976, both others by Girolami) and "kick-started a string of poliziotteschi collaborations between Lenzi and star Maurizio Merli including *Rome Armed to the Teeth* (1976), *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* (1977) and *From Corleone to Brooklyn* (1979)." [C30698]

*Free Hand for a Tough Cop* (Italian: *Il trucido e lo sbirro*, *The Numbskull and the Cop*, released 28 August 1976) is the birth of Er Monnezza. Directed by Lenzi, written by Lenzi, Dardano Sacchetti and Elisa Briganti, produced by Claudio Mancini and Ugo Tucci, starring Tomas Milian (Sergio Marazzi, "Er Monnezza"), Claudio Cassinelli (Commissioner Antonio Sarti), Henry Silva (Brescianelli). 90 minutes. The plot engine: Camilla, a little girl with a kidney disorder, is kidnapped before her next treatment so the gang can blackmail her rich father. Commissioner Sarti knows time is running out and takes desperate measures: he secretly organises a prison escape for petty crook Sergio Marazzi, "Er Monnezza" (Roman dialect: "Trash"), and uses Marazzi's insider knowledge of the criminal milieu to find the hideout. The mechanism is the criminal as bloodhound: the cop lends the crook his freedom and the crook lends the cop his map of the underworld. This is Milian's first appearance as Er Monnezza, "a role that he later played several more times, in Lenzi's *Brothers Till We Die* (1977, a sort of sequel of this movie), in *Destruction Force* by Stelvio Massi (1977) and, with slight differences, in *Uno contro l'altro, praticamente amici* by Bruno Corbucci (1980) and in Francesco Massaro's *Il lupo e l'agnello* (1980)." The Wikipedia *Almost Human* Legacy section states the arc plainly: "Milian's antiheroes became increasingly sympathetic, with the last two starring lovable rogue Er Monnezza ('Trash')." After Milian scored an all-audience hit with *The Cop in Blue Jeans*, whose protagonist retained many of Monnezza's traits, he moved to comedy specialist Bruno Corbucci. The box-office flag: the *Free Hand* infobox prints "Box office: 509,747 million" lire, which as written means 509 billion lire -- roughly 250 times the gross of *Violent Naples* and impossible. The figure is presumably 509.747 million lire with a misplaced separator, but no source confirms the corrected number, so no *Free Hand* gross is cited in this study. [C30699] [C30702]

*The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* (Italian: *Il cinico, l'infame, il violento*, released 3 February 1977) is the sequel engine. Directed by Lenzi, screenplay by Ernesto Gastaldi, Dardano Sacchetti and Lenzi, story by Sauro Scavolini, produced by Luciano Martino, starring Maurizio Merli (Leonardo Tanzi), Tomas Milian (Luigi "The Chinaman" Maietto), John Saxon (Frank Di Maggio). 100 minutes. It grossed 1,818,523,920 lire domestically. The plot engine: Maietto ("The Chinaman") escapes from prison and sends two hitmen after Tanzi, the inspector whose testimony jailed him. Tanzi survives; the newspapers pretend the assassination succeeded; his superiors want to hide him in Switzerland; Tanzi refuses, now a civilian ex-cop (he has resigned and writes detective novels), and goes vigilante. His mechanism is to set Maietto against his own mafia business partner, Frank Di Maggio (Saxon) -- the two-criminals-against-each-other device, reused from *Syndicate Sadists* but now with the cop as the instigator rather than the biker. It is the "third entry into the Tanzi/Moretto/Monnezza shared universe" and "a sequel of sorts" to *The Tough Ones*, per Roberto Curti, with Merli reprising Tanzi. Gastaldi later spoke negatively about his own work: "the story wasn't mine, and I just did supervising work on the finished script. Actually, I didn't even like the movie." The title inspired the 2005 book *Cinici infami e violenti*, a guide to the genre. [C30700]

*Brothers Till We Die* (Italian: *La banda del gobbo*, *The Hunchback's Gang*, released 18 August 1977 -- not 1978, as the *Free Hand* page and Genre Grinder's box-set listing print) is the crossover finale. Directed by Lenzi, written by Lenzi, produced by Luciano Martino, starring Tomas Milian in a double role. 98 minutes. It grossed 1,523,844,720 lire domestically. The plot engine: Vincenzo Marazzi, "Il Gobbo" (the Hunchback from *The Tough Ones*), returns to Rome from Corsica after imprisonment and plans an armoured-truck robbery with his gang; the gang betrays him and takes the loot; he hides in the sewers, then at his girlfriend's house, and kills his betrayers one by one -- while the police, unable to find him, interrogate his twin brother Sergio Marazzi, "Er Monnezza" (from *Free Hand for a Tough Cop*), as a decoy. During a car chase the Hunchback's car plunges into the Tiber and his corpse is never found. The double-role mechanism: Milian plays both twins -- "one brother is the ferocious 'hunchback' and the other is a likeable swindler called 'monnezza' who lives in the suburbs of Rome. The first is a ruthless criminal, the second a soft-hearted thief." The film is the "fifth and final entry into the Tanzi/Moretto/Monnezza shared universe" and "the last collaboration among Lenzi and Tomas Milian." Digitmovies: "Umberto Lenzi, a Tuscan director with an incredible activity of making movies of various genres... expertly directs this milestone of the Italian Police Movies genre. The action scenes in *La banda del gobbo* show the proof of his excellence in this genre." The score is by Franco Micalizzi, with a recurring Moog-synthesiser main theme. [C30701]

Lenzi's poliziottesco system, stated as five devices. THE CITY AS SET: each film is titled by its city and shot in it -- Milan (*Gang War*, *Almost Human*), Rome (*The Tough Ones*, *Cynic*, *Brothers*), Naples (*Violent Naples*). The city is not background; it is the trap -- the junkyard at the edge of town, the slaughterhouse, the sewers, the Tiber, the rooftops of Ponte and Parione. Chases were shot without permits in real traffic. THE ESCALATION LADDER: every film opens with a small crime and climbs -- a coin-machine theft becomes a kidnapping becomes a massacre (*Almost Human*); a friend's murder becomes a gang war (*Syndicate Sadists*); a planted-drugs arrest becomes a kidnapping becomes a murder of the boss (*The Tough Ones*); a prison break becomes a kidnapping becomes a gang betrayal becomes a revenge killing spree (*Brothers*). The ladder is the plot; there is no investigation, only ascent. THE CRIMINAL AS PROTAGONIST (the inversion): Lenzi said *Almost Human* was not a poliziesco because it centres on the criminals, closer to French film noir. The cop is the straight man (Silva's weary Grandi, Merli's shouting Tanzi) and the criminal carries the film's energy, its comedy and its camera time. Slant's framing: Lenzi's films show "a more reactionary brand of poliziottesco" than Fernando Di Leo's -- "Where Di Leo acknowledges elements of social inequity that have contributed to this social unrest (poverty in Southern Italy, for one, plays a key role in *Caliber 9*), Lenzi remains more deliberately apolitical, content to revel in the filth that surfaced as increasingly weaponized criminals and widespread mob violence were met with vigilante justice and the fascist tactics of a police state." THE COP WHO BREAKS THE LAW: Tanzi plants drugs, forces a man to swallow a bullet, beats suspects, infiltrates a bank through air-conditioning ducts; Sarti springs a convict from prison; Vannucchi joins a fascist vigilante army. The system's moral engine is that the law cannot work ("If we stick to the book, we've had it!"), so the hero works outside it -- and the films repeatedly show that outside-it work producing the wrong corpse (*Manhunt*), a dead partner (*The Tough Ones*), or a scandal. The left press read this as fascist apologetics (L'Unità on both *Almost Human* and *The Tough Ones*); the genre press read it as showmanship (*Il Messaggero*). Both readings are sourced; this study carries both, not one. THE DOUBLE / THE COUNTERPART: *Syndicate Sadists* pits two crime families; *Cynic* pits two crime bosses; *Brothers* pits twin brothers; *The Tough Ones* pits Tanzi against Moretto as mirror enemies (both violent, both obsessed, both willing to break the law). Lenzi's recurring move is to split one force into two and let them destroy each other while a third party watches. The box-office shape (domestic lire, all from Wikipedia infoboxes and release sections): *Gang War in Milan* 631.7m (1973); *Almost Human* 1,168.7m (1974); *The Tough Ones* 1,617m (1976); *Violent Naples* 2,046.9m (1976); *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* 1,818.5m (1977); *Brothers Till We Die* 1,523.8m (1977). The peak is 1976 (*Violent Naples*), the year Lenzi released three poliziotteschi. *Syndicate Sadists* and *Manhunt in the City* grosses were not found this run. [C30703]

**Takeaway:** The poliziottesco engine is an escalation ladder with no investigation -- a small crime climbs rung by rung until someone is dead in a pile of trash, and the audience watches the criminal climb, not the cop solve.

## 8. The six skins of Milian

Six Lenzi-Milian collaborations in four years: *Almost Human* (1974), *Syndicate Sadists* (1975), *The Tough Ones* (1976), *Free Hand for a Tough Cop* (1976), *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* (1977), *Brothers Till We Die* (1977). Severin's *Violent Streets* box set collects five of the six (all but *The Tough Ones*, which is a Grindhouse title). The Wikipedia *Almost Human* Legacy section lists the same five follow-ups. [C30702] [C30633] The arc of the character runs psychopath to avenger to hunchback to clown to twins: Giulio Sacchi (psychopath who kills against his own interest), Rambo (righteous avenger who turns gangs on each other), Vincenzo Moretto (humiliated hunchback who murders his way up), Luigi "The Chinaman" Maietto (vengeful gangster, a straight villain again), Sergio "Er Monnezza" Marazzi (lovable petty rogue), then both Marazzi twins at once (the villain and the clown sharing one body). The through-line is Milian's body as the instrument: nervous tics and sweating (Sacchi), the biker's chrome machine (Rambo), the hunchback's deformity and the swallowed bullet (Moretto), the afro wig and Roman dialect (Monnezza). The audience relationship moves from horror to admiration to affection, which is exactly the identification problem Lenzi named after *Gang War in Milan*, solved in stages. [C30702]

Er Monnezza is the character that outlived the partnership. Created by screenwriter Dardano Sacchetti and director Umberto Lenzi with Milian (per the #762 draft), Italian Wikipedia confirms Lenzi and Milian together "invent" the character ("il regista inventa anche il personaggio di Er Monnezza, simpatico e furbo ladruncolo borgataro"), a petty thief from the Roman *borgate*. He appears in exactly three films: *Il trucido e lo sbirro* (Lenzi, 1976), *La banda del trucido* (Stelvio Massi, 1977) and *La banda del gobbo* (Lenzi, 1977) -- the middle one is NOT a Lenzi film, and Italian Wikipedia says Milian playing Er Monnezza for Massi was "il piccolo tradimento" (the little betrayal) that cracked the Lenzi-Milian partnership ("il rapporto tra i due artisti si incrina, producendo di fatto la scissione del loro sodalizio cinematografico"). [C30640] The Severin set holds five of the six collaborations; the omitted one is *The Tough Ones* (1976), so the set must not be described as the complete collaboration. [C30669] The Stanford catalogue lists *Brothers Till We Die* as 1978 (its export/release year in some territories) against the Italian release of 18 August 1977; the study uses 1977, the Italian release, as the film year. [C30668] One quote is carried from #762, not re-verified this run: Lenzi is quoted saying that to call the Milian-Merli rivalry a rivalry "is still a euphemism" -- it was verified in the #762 draft from its sources, and it is carried here as carried, not as freshly verified. [C30642]

**Takeaway:** Milian is Lenzi's real auteur signature in the 1970s -- one actor's body, re-skinned six times from psychopath to clown, doing the genre-hopping for him.

## 9. The company he kept

Lenzi's network is a writers' and musicians' network as much as a directors' one, and the rule applied here is that a tie counts only with a named shared film or credit and a date. Milian is the doorway, carried from the last study: six poliziotteschi, 1974-1977, and the five-film Severin set *Violent Streets: The Umberto Lenzi/Tomas Milian Collection* (Severin, 2023) containing *Almost Human*, *Syndicate Sadists*, *Free Hand for a Tough Cop*, *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* and *Brothers Till We Die*. Milian was already studied as #762 and is excluded as a #764 pick. [C30725]

The strongest writing tie is Ernesto Gastaldi. *Almost Human* (1974) carries a sole screenplay credit by Gastaldi; when Lenzi was hired he did no rewrites, "marking the first time he did not contribute to the narrative of one of his films." Gastaldi self-published the original screenplay in book form in 2013; in 2018 Milieu Edizioni published Paolo Spagnuolo's book on the making of the film, reprinting the full screenplay, with a foreword by Lenzi and a postface by Enzo G. Castellari. Gastaldi co-wrote *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* (1977) as well. Wider Gastaldi: 18 spaghetti westerns including *My Name Is Nobody* (1973), *Day of Anger* (1967), *The Grand Duel* (1972); gialli including *The Whip and the Body* (1963, Bava), *The Strange Vice of Mrs. Wardh* (1971), *Torso* (1973, Martino); nearly 120 films, often as "Julian Berry." [C30726] Dardano Sacchetti is the second screenwriter: *Rome Armed to the Teeth* (1976), scriptwriters Sacchetti and Lenzi, and *The Cynic, the Rat and the Fist* (1977), screenplay by Gastaldi, Sacchetti and Lenzi. Sacchetti is otherwise best known for Fulci (*Zombi 2*, 1979, co-written with his wife Elisa Briganti; *The Beyond*) and the Bavas (Mario: *A Bay of Blood* 1971, *Shock* 1977; Lamberto: *Demons* 1985, *Demons 2* 1986), and for Argento's *The Cat o' Nine Tails* (1971) as first credit. The claim that Sacchetti "created the 'Monezza' character with Tomas Milian" appears only in one weak biography summary and is usable only as a flagged aside, not as a load-bearing tie. [C30727]

Henry Silva acted for Lenzi three times, all poliziotteschi: *Almost Human* (1974) as Commissario Walter Grandi, the commissioner hunting Milian's Giulio Sacchi; *Manhunt in the City* (1975) as David Vannucchi; *Free Hand for a Tough Cop* (1976) as Brescianelli. Silva's Italian period ran 1966-1977, "at least 25 movies, the majority of which were Italian poliziotteschi." [C30728] Ray Lovelock co-starred in *Almost Human* (1974) alongside Milian, Silva, Laura Belli, Gino Santercole and Anita Strindberg, and appears in the Severin set's interview material alongside Lenzi and Gastaldi. [C30729]

The composers run parallel. Riz Ortolani scored three Lenzi films: *So Sweet... So Perverse* (1969), *Seven Blood-Stained Orchids* (1972), *From Hell to Victory* (1979). His career context: over 200 films/TV 1955-2014; "More" from *Mondo Cane* (1962) won the 1964 Grammy for Best Instrumental Theme and was Oscar-nominated for Best Original Song; four David di Donatello, three Nastro d'Argento, a Golden Globe for Best Original Song, and a 2013 World Soundtrack Academy Lifetime Achievement award. [C30730] Stelvio Cipriani composed and conducted the score for *Nightmare City* (1980); no second Lenzi-Cipriani score was verified this run, so none is asserted. Cipriani otherwise scored 200+ films including Bava's *A Bay of Blood* (1971) and *Baron Blood* (1972), *Tentacles* (1977) and *Piranha II* (1981). [C30731] Ennio Morricone scored *Almost Human* (1974) -- the Lenzi tie is verified, but his birth and death dates were NOT re-verified this run, and the prefix MOR is TAKEN, so Morricone is not ranked for #764 without a fresh date verification and a new prefix search. [C30732]

Two fellow directors carry direct, dated, credited ties in the opposite direction from the usual assumption. Lenzi wrote *Striker* (1988), directed by Enzo G. Castellari -- listed in the "Writer only" section of the Lenzi filmography table -- and Lenzi wrote the foreword and Castellari the postface to Spagnuolo's 2018 book on *Almost Human*. Castellari was already studied as #756. Lenzi wrote *Craving Desire* (*Graffiante desiderio*, 1993), directed by Sergio Martino -- his latest dated film credit of any kind, and the reason his years active run to 1993 although his last directing credit is 1991. Martino's brother Luciano Martino produced *Almost Human* (1974, Dania Film) and set up the *Le case maledette* TV series. Martino was already studied as #757. [C30733] [C30734] Fernando Di Leo is INDIRECT ONLY: no direct shared film, writing or production credit between Lenzi and Di Leo was found in any source read this run. The tie runs through Henry Silva: Di Leo directed Silva as hitman Dave Catania in *The Italian Connection* (*La mala ordina*, 1972) and as Nick Lanzetta in *Il Boss* (1973), and Silva then played for Lenzi in 1974-76. Di Leo is not ranked for #764; he is the natural #765 doorway out of a Silva study. [C30735] Explicitly not found in any source read this run: no shared film or credit between Lenzi and Sergio Corbucci or Bruno Corbucci; no Lenzi score by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis; no Lenzi-Fulci shared credit (Sacchetti is the Fulci bridge, not Lenzi himself); and no sourced Lenzi-vs-Fulci critical comparison. [C30723]

**Takeaway:** The company is the mechanism -- Gastaldi's screenplay that Lenzi did not touch, Sacchetti's quick rewrites, Silva's three films, Ortolani's three scores: a journeyman's career is a writers' room and a band, and the director is the man who keeps the room working.

## 10. The long tail

The cannibal turn has a coda that belongs here, not in the 1970s: *Nightmare City* (Italian: *Incubo sulla città contaminata*) is a 1980 film, placed by the Lenzi filmography table in 1980 alongside *Eaten Alive!*, a year before *Cannibal Ferox*. It belongs to the cannibal/zombie turn of 1980-81, not to the late-80s horror phase. Lenzi himself appears in it in a small acting role, credited as "Reporter." Its fast, armed, radioactive mutants were Lenzi's deliberate rejection of slow Romero/Fulci zombies. [C30710]

1988 is the American turn, and the pseudonyms come back. *Ghosthouse* (Italian: *La casa 3*, 1988): Lenzi directed and wrote it (story author credit in the filmography table) under the pseudonym Humphrey Humbert, for Joe D'Amato's Filmirage operation, which specialised in cheap English-language genre films for international markets. Shooting in the United States gave it an outwardly American identity despite Italian financing and creative leadership. The filmography page lists it among the late-career "foreign productions in the United States." [C30711] *Nightmare Beach* (1988; Italian: *Nightmare Beach -- La spiaggia del terrore*; released in the US as *Welcome to Spring Break*): a slasher Lenzi directed and wrote but refused to sign -- it was credited to Harry Kirkpatrick. The refusal to sign is a usable character detail: the director disowning his own late slasher. [C30712]

1989 is television horror that television would not show. Lenzi directed two made-for-TV horror films, *La casa del sortilegio* (*The House of Witchcraft* -- the English Wikipedia article text once misspells it "Witchraft"; the filmography table gives "House of Witchcraft") and *La casa delle anime erranti* (*The House of Lost Souls*). Both were part of *Le case maledette* ("Houses of Doom"), a series set up by producer Luciano Martino and linked only by the haunted-house theme. The films were shot in 1989 but not broadcast immediately: Lenzi said he made them as if for theatrical release and that neither the producers, his colleagues nor he had considered that television sponsors would not accept horror films. They were eventually released on VHS in Italy in 2000 and broadcast on Italian satellite TV in 2006. The filmography table lists both under year 2000 with the note "Television film" -- that 2000 is the release year, NOT the shoot year; the shoot year is 1989. [C30713] The same year: *Cop Target* (Italian: *Obiettivo poliziotto*, 1989), a police action film shot in Miami and Santo Domingo, starring Robert Ginty and Charles Napier, in which Lenzi also appears as an actor, credited as "Casino Patron"; [C30714] *Hitcher in the Dark* (Italian: *Paura nel buio*, 1989), directed and story-authored by Lenzi, again under the Humphrey Humbert credit, again for Filmirage -- a return from supernatural horror to a human serial predator, reconnecting with the psychologically disturbed killers of his early gialli; and *Hell's Gate* (Italian: *Le porte dell'inferno*, 1989), directed and written by Lenzi, on which no further detail was verified this run. [C30715]

In 1990, using his own company and a low amount of funds, Lenzi shot two films in Brazil in three months: the horror film *Black Demons* (Italian: *Demoni 3*, released 1991; Lenzi credited for story) and the adventure film *Hunt for the Golden Scorpion* (Italian: *Caccia allo scorpione d'oro*, 1990). In 1996 Lenzi considered *Black Demons* to be his masterpiece. That 1996 self-assessment -- the cheap late Brazilian horror as the masterpiece, not the poliziotteschi -- is the single most quotable late-career fact in the study. [C30716] *Mean Tricks* (Italian: *Hornsby e Rodriguez*, starring Charles Napier, David Warbeck and David Brandon) carries a year conflict: the English Wikipedia filmography table dates it 1990, while the main "Umberto Lenzi" article text says "In 1992, he shot the adventure film *Mean Tricks*." Neither year is asserted without the flag. [C30717] *Black Cobra 4 -- Detective Malone* (1991) is Lenzi's last dated directing credit in the filmography table. The mixi/IMDb-derived list dates it 1990 and credits Lenzi as "Bob Collins" -- the filmography table (1991, director "Yes") is the better source; the study uses 1991 and does not assert the Bob Collins alias. Lenzi's years active are given as 1956-1993; the only 1993 credit in the filmography is a writing-only credit (see below). [C30718]

*Horror Baby* (2006) is the unmade slasher. *Variety* reported on 30 July 2006 ("Buzz is back on Italian B-pix," Nick Vivarelli) that Lenzi was shooting a slasher film in Italy titled *Horror Baby*, about a 15-year-old paraplegic girl who becomes a serial killer after viewing a neighbour having sex from her window. No completion, release, or later mention of *Horror Baby* was found in any source read this run. It is treated here as reported-in-production in 2006 and almost certainly unmade/unreleased, not as a completed film. [C30719]

Then the second career. Lenzi debuted as a noir novelist in 2008 and published ten novels in all. He created the literary figure of Bruno Astolfi, an antifascist private detective who investigates in the Italy of the 1940s, moving through the world of Italian cinema under Fascism; the novels are set on real film sets and involve real-life figures of the Italian film industry. The early pseudonymous detective novels and adventure stories of 1957-1960 are a separate, earlier phase. [C30720] [C30664] The verified titles and years (Treccani; the Fondazione Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia; Italian Wikipedia; the Museo Nazionale del Cinema bulletin, July 2011 -- all four agree on the core list): *Delitti a Cinecittà* (2008, Coniglio Editore; reissued in *Il Giallo Mondadori* no. 3090, 22 September 2013); *Terrore ad Harlem* (2009; set on the set of Carmine Gallone's *Harlem*, 1943); *Morte al Cinevillaggio* (2010; set in the Venice film city of the Repubblica di Salò); *Scalera di sangue* (2011; previewed at the Cinema Massimo, Turin, 5 July 2011); *Roma assassina* (2012), first of a four-book *Roma assassina* quadrilogy set 1942-1945, published experimentally in digital format, comprising *Roma assassina*, *Carte in regola*, *La guerra non è finita* and *Spiaggia a mano armata*; *Il clan dei miserabili* (2014; set on the set of Riccardo Freda's *I miserabili*, 1948); *Cuore criminale* (2015, Golem Edizioni; set during filming of Duilio Coletti's *Cuore*, 1948, after the novel by Edmondo De Amicis). Treccani's shorter list matches the fuller list; the count of ten includes the four quadrilogy volumes. [C30721] The first biography, *Una vita per il cinema. L'avventurosa storia di Umberto Lenzi regista*, by Silvia Trovato and Tiziano Arrigoni, appeared in 2016 -- cited, not read. [C30665]

He died on 19 October 2017, age 86, hospitalised in Ostia, buried in Follonica, cause unknown -- no cause is asserted. The *la Repubblica* obituary of 19 October 2017 by Alessandra Vitali is titled "È morto Umberto Lenzi, papà del Monnezza e dei film più amati da Tarantino" -- the Italian press framed him at death as the father of Er Monnezza and the maker of the films Tarantino loves best. That Tarantino admiration is the strongest available counterweight to the critical dismissal below. [C30722] [C30651]

Three sourced verdicts, all from the English Wikipedia Legacy section; the study presents all three and picks none. Roberto Curti called Lenzi "one of the undisputed leading figures in Italian genre cinema" and "a sort of institution in Italian genre cinema." [C30662] Louis Paul judged that although Lenzi released "quite enjoyable action films in the 1960s and some good thrillers in the '70s, he never consistently excelled at any one genre" and would "probably be remembered most for his cannibal-themed horror films." Kim Newman, writing in 2021, said Lenzi "has been rated towards the bottom of the ranks of Italian genre craftsmen by many -- me included -- because of the greater availability of his pulpier, more gruesome 1980s work," naming *Cannibal Ferox* and *Nightmare City*; "though a trailblazer for the little-loved jungle cannibal cycle, contributing its earliest and most gruesome entries, in general Lenzi seemed one of the coat-tail riders, turning to whatever subgenre of exploitation was selling that year... and even in that class, he's less consistently interesting and exciting than Sergio Martino." Newman added that the Carroll Baker films of the late 1960s "force a reassessment" of Lenzi's work. [C30663] [C30704] Two notes for the reader: the Newman comparison is to Sergio Martino, NOT to Fulci -- no sourced Lenzi-vs-Fulci comparison was found in any source read this run, so none is invented here; and the "journeyman vs auteur" frame is defensible only in these terms (Curti's "institution" against Paul/Newman on inconsistency and coat-tail-riding), with the reassessment hinge the Baker erotic-giallo trilogy. The cannibal reputation rests on availability bias, Newman's own point: the 1980-81 films circulated most, so they defined him. [C30723]

Posthumous: in May 2018, twenty-nine previously unpublished writings and a series of photographs belonging to Lenzi were donated to the Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia; the bequest of a novel, scriptments, treatments and screenplays supplemented the collection already held by legal deposit. The same CSC page puts his filmography at sixty-five cinema and TV titles. Troy Howarth's *Make Them Die Slowly: The Kinetic Cinema of Umberto Lenzi*, the first book-length study of Lenzi in English (c. 500 pages, interviews with collaborators), was published in 2022. One item stays unresolved: "Sarayevo -- Inferno di fuoco" (1996), which the Museo Nazionale del Cinema bulletin calls Lenzi's last film -- it does NOT appear in the English Wikipedia Lenzi filmography table, which ends at 1991 (directing) plus the 2000 TV-film releases, and gives years active 1956-1993. No 1996 Lenzi film is asserted in this study until a leg verifies it against a filmographic source. [C30724] Over sixty films across four decades, years active 1956-1993 by the infobox (the FreeDictionary mirror says 1958-1993; the difference is whether the 1956 diploma short counts) -- the study says "over sixty films" without pinning the start year. [C30661]

**Takeaway:** The long tail is the honest part of the career -- the director who refused to sign his own slasher, made his TV horror as if for the cinema and waited eleven years for anyone to show it, shot two films in Brazil in three months on his own money, called the cheap one his masterpiece, and then wrote ten novels about a detective walking the film sets of Fascist Italy, as if the only genre left was the one he had lived in all along.

## Aesthetic logic

Lenzi's aesthetic is a set of plot machines, not a look. He has no Argento-style visual system -- Cagey Films calls him "not a real stylist" whose work is "merely functional" at its weakest -- but the machines are exact, and they survive every genre change because they are about what happens, not what it looks like. Four of them recur across at least two genres each: (a) THE OUTSIDER ENTERS A CLOSED WORLD -- the photographer enters the Thai tribe (*Deep River*); the widow lets hippies into her villa (*Orgasmo*); the businessman enters the neighbour's life (*So Sweet*); the search party enters the jungle cult (*Eaten Alive!*); the anthropologists enter the Colombian village (*Ferox*); the biker Rambo enters a city run by two families (*Syndicate Sadists*); the cop enters the criminal milieu by borrowing a crook (*Free Hand*). In every case the outsider is processed by the world -- absorbed, deceived, eaten, or converted -- and the film's suspense is the processing, not the entry. (b) THE ESCALATION LADDER -- small crime to atrocity (*Almost Human*); small deception to murder (*So Sweet*); small trespass to gang war (*Gang War in Milan*); one murder to seven (*Seven Bloodstained Orchids*); one humiliation to a killing spree (*The Tough Ones*, *Brothers*). Lenzi rarely writes a mystery to be solved; he writes a situation to be worsened, one rung at a time, until it breaks. (c) THE LOCATION AS TRAP -- the villa, the Paris apartment, the Mallorcan villa, the remote estate, the Thai village, the Colombian village, the Roman junkyard, the Milan bank, the Naples streets. The location is introduced as shelter or workplace and revealed as a cage: the helicopter flies over and John hides (*Deep River*); the tour bus keeps touring while tourists are murdered (*Eyeball*); the junkyard machinery nearly crushes Anna (*The Tough Ones*); the sewers hide the Hunchback (*Brothers*). The trap is always entered voluntarily and exited only by violence or death. (d) THE PROTAGONIST SWITCH / THE DOUBLE -- *So Sweet* changes protagonist midway (the *Psycho* parallel); *Seven Bloodstained Orchids* fakes its heroine's death; *Cynic* fakes Tanzi's death; *Brothers* literally doubles Milian. Lenzi's favourite trick is to kill, fake-kill, or duplicate the person the audience is following, then continue with whoever is left. [C30705] The constants never change -- the commercial instinct (pre-sold material: Salgari, *fumetti neri*, a Hollywood name), the speed (eleven features in 1961-64 alone), the camera habits (close-up, zoom), the self-description as a storyteller rather than a stylist, and the habit of building a film around a repeatable mechanism (a number, a hero, a house full of rich suspects) rather than around a single image -- and the variables are everything else: the genre, the year, the country, the decade, the size of the budget, and whether the name on the film is his, Humphrey Humbert's, or Harry Kirkpatrick's. [C30660] That is why the same machines show up in a villa giallo, a Milan crime film and a Colombian cannibal film, and why his best films in each genre are the ones where the machine fits the material (*Orgasmo*, *Almost Human*, *The Tough Ones*) and his worst are the ones where the machine is just pasted on (the clip-compilation *Eaten Alive!*). Over sixty films across four decades, and the signature is the machine set. [C30661] [C30705]
