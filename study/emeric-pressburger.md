# Emeric Pressburger: Study Notes

**Site:** Imre Jozsef Pressburger (b. Friday 5 December 1902, Miskolc, Kingdom of Hungary, Austria-Hungary; d. Friday 5 February 1988, Saxtead, Suffolk, England, age 85, of bronchial pneumonia), Hungarian-British screenwriter, film director and producer; and with Michael Powell -- as The Archers -- the co-author of the nineteen Powell-Pressburger features, the most visually ambitious films in British cinema: 49th Parallel (1941), The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp (1943), Black Narcissus (1947), The Red Shoes (1948), The Tales of Hoffmann (1951). Oscar winner, Best Writing Original Story, 49th Parallel (1943); BAFTA Fellow 1981, BFI Fellow 1983 (both joint with Powell); British citizen 1946. He began as a penniless short-story writer in Berlin, learned English from a dictionary after arriving stateless in London, and ended as the man whose scripts Powell shot sometimes blindly -- "but they were all right most of the time" -- and whose novels, written alone in Suffolk after the partnership ended, were the work he had to prove he could do on his own. [C33521]

## 1. Miskolc and Temesvar: the outsider's schooling

Imre Jozsef Pressburger was born in Miskolc, in a family of Jewish ancestry, the only son of Kalman Pressburger, an estate manager -- a land agent for a local baron, in another source's wording -- and Katalin Wichs, a local girl; his birth there was, in the Hungarian National Film Institute's phrase, "almost accidental", his father travelling the lands serving a number of landowners and spending only a few years around Miskolc. At eight he moved to Subotica, a year later to Rudna, studying with the Franciscans in Timisoara and lodging weekdays at the local police chief's house, travelling home to his parents only at weekends; then boarding school in Temesvar (today Timisoara, Romania), where he was a good pupil, excelling at mathematics, literature and music, and a gifted violinist. The school-inspector anecdote he told his grandson Kevin Macdonald is the first Pressburger story in miniature: a visiting inspector from Budapest set the class a short story, praised one the next morning for "genuine literary talent" without naming the author, and Imre stood up and declared "It is my story you are referring to" -- the inspector smiled, but refused to deny or confirm the assertion. [C33531]

Two losses from those years haunted him for life, in Ian Christie's account: an unrequited love of his youth, Magda Rona of Timisoara, who rejected him because she considered him too short -- he exchanged letters with his "Dear Magda" for years -- and a dearly loved mother who disappeared into Auschwitz, with many close relatives who remained in Hungary. He tried after the war to contact his mother in Miskolc. The survivor's guilt Christie and Lucy Scholes both trace to that loss runs through the whole career: the sympathetic Germans, the outsiders learning a new language, the Nazi war criminal of The Glass Pearls who steals another man's memories of fleeing Germany. [C33532]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's material was fixed before he was twenty -- the outsider, the unrequited love, the mother lost to history -- and he spent fifty years reworking it, "sometimes aware of the autobiographical component; sometimes it crept in involuntarily", in his own words.

## 2. Prague, Stuttgart, radio sets: the unfinished engineer

He studied mathematics and engineering at the Universities of Prague and Stuttgart, and finished neither. Why he left is itself a small conflict the legs record rather than resolve: Wikipedia, citing Macdonald, says his father's death forced him to abandon his studies; the NFI gives a more detailed account -- the Treaty of Trianon fell during his last school year in Timisoara and, as a Hungarian national, he was refused a visa and could not study in Budapest either, so he went to "neutral" Prague, finished his engineering studies there "before earning a degree" (the most memorable part a guest lecture by Albert Einstein), then tried again in Stuttgart at the Technische Hochschule with a wealthy relative's support, "but he failed to graduate once again and he didn't much care for the whole vibe and latent antisemitism" of the city. What he did next is agreed: he went into manufacturing radio sets, was soon out of business, and left for Berlin. [C33533]

**Takeaway:** The engineer who never qualified is the writer who builds plots like machines -- routes, timetables, procedures, devices with parts that fit -- and the mathematics never left him: his scripts are structures first and dialogue second.

## 3. Berlin: journalism, short stories, the road to UFA

Berlin, 1926 -- four sources say 1926, one (JewishGen KehilaLinks) says 1925, and the majority reading is used here. He arrived in abject poverty, staying in a synagogue library among other places, surviving on odd jobs; for a single morning he even tried his hand as a violinist in a top silent-film orchestra, "but the talent that made him a shining star in Timisoara was not enough for Berlin." He wrote dozens of short stories and submitted them to newspapers until one, Auf Reisen, was published by BZ am Mittag, earning him just short of eighty German marks; Faber dates his first short story to 1928. He wrote to Germany's leading film-industry paper, Film-Kurier, proposing a series of articles on every aspect of film-making -- the first on writing a film script, the others on acting and directing. They replied within a week asking for the remaining articles by return post; only two were ever published, "but this was still quite an achievement considering how he had absolutely no connection with the film industry except for being an enthusiastic moviegoer." [C33534]

Citing the article series, he submitted story ideas to UFA; Fritz Podehl in the screenplay department put one of his ideas forward -- "this only happened with one or two of the thousands of unlicensed submissions arriving every year." Nothing came of that idea, but UFA hired him to write screenplays from novels the studio had already acquired. The route matters: he did not enter film through a film school or a set, but through print -- a short story sold for eighty marks, two articles in a trade paper, one idea forwarded out of thousands. He was a writer before he was a film man, and he stayed one. [C33535]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's career begins with a submission system -- stories sent out, most refused, one forwarded -- which is the same system his later characters live inside: papers, passes, applications, a world run on documents.

## 4. UFA: the Dramaturgie department

At Universum Film AG, then the largest film company in Europe, Pressburger worked in the Dramaturgie department -- script selection, approval and editing -- and as a scriptwriter in his own right, starting to write scripts for UFA in 1930. UFA's structure was "extremely hierarchical, even when compared with Hollywood", with the fate of all films decided by department leaders, "many of whom had no cinematic experience". He first worked under the "conservative cavalry officer Bruno Duday", then was handed to the "much more liberal Gunther von Stapenhorst's department where he had fewer creative clashes"; Christie confirms he "worked closely with Gunther Stapenhorst, his producer from UFA", who later followed him, in a sense, to London. The Dramaturgie job is the hidden school of the Archers scripts: a man paid to read other people's scripts, approve or reject them, and fix them -- learning structure by judging it, before he ever had to invent it. [C33536]

In the 1930s many European films were produced in multiple-language versions, some films made in Germany surviving with French intertitles and vice versa; that multi-language system shaped his Paris work, where the same story had to be told in two tongues. The habit of reworking -- the same material in another language, another version, another cut -- never left him: The Red Shoes was written in 1937 and remade into something else in 1946; Miracle in Soho was written in Paris in 1934 and filmed in London in 1957; Gone to Earth was re-cut by Selznick into The Wild Heart. [C33537]

**Takeaway:** The script department taught him that a story is not a fixed object but a draft that can be re-versioned -- in another language, another country, another decade -- and the Archers films are full of stories that arrive in their second or third version.

## 5. The UFA films: what survives, what does not

His UFA-period credits, as Wikipedia's filmography lists them, run to more than twenty titles in three years: Die Grosse Sehnsucht and Farewell (1930); Ronny, Das Ekel, Dann schon lieber Lebertran, Emil und die Detektive, Der kleine Seitensprung (1931); Une jeune fille et un million, ...und es leuchtet die Puszta, Sehnsucht 202, Petit ecart, Lumpenkavaliere, Held wider Willen, Eine von uns, La belle aventure, Wer zahlt heute noch?, Das schone Abenteuer, A ven gazember (Old Villain) (1932). Only a few survive -- Farewell, Old Villain and ...und es leuchtet die Puszta are named as survivors -- and the specifics the legs could establish are these: Die Grosse Sehnsucht (1930) was his invitation from UFA, to co-script compatriot Steve Sekely's directorial debut; Farewell (Abschied, 1930), directed by Robert Siodmak, was Siodmak's first sound film and UFA's first sound film, a boarding-house story charting the break-up of a young couple in real time -- and Christie notes the boarding house with "lodgers meeting and gossiping in the hall" recurs in his novel The Glass Pearls thirty-six years later. [C33538]

Emil und die Detektive (1931), from Erich Kastner's novel, is the famous credit conflict of the period: Wikipedia's infobox credits Billy Wilder, Erich Kastner and Emeric Pressburger, the article text says "The film script was written by Billy Wilder", and the on-screen credit went to Wilder alone; the safest statement is co-writer with Wilder (and Kastner), on-screen credit Wilder's. It "launched the career of Billy Wilder as a screenwriter" -- Pressburger's first brush with a collaborator who would outshine him in the credits. Old Villain (1932) was his only film shot in his native Hungary, based on a story by Kalman Mikszath, shot at the Hunnia studios with director Heinz Hille; it was during Old Villain that he met his first wife, Agi Donath. One correction, recorded here because the brief floated it: The Secret of the Blue Room (1932) is NOT his -- screenplay by Arnold Lippschitz, directed by Erich Engels; Pressburger appears nowhere in its credits. [C33539]

**Takeaway:** The UFA years are mostly lost film, and that is the point: Pressburger learned to write for a medium that eats its own work, and the habit of the surviving draft -- the story kept, reworked, filmed again -- starts here.

## 6. Flight: Paris 1933-1935

In January 1933 Hitler came to power, and UFA's head "sacked the company's remaining Jewish employees", Pressburger being told his contract would not be renewed; the NFI puts it more bluntly, that the "dejewification program of UFA had completely obliterated the entire dramaturgy department." He left his Berlin apartment, in the famous account from Macdonald via Wikipedia, "leaving the key in the door so that the Stormtroopers wouldn't have to break the door down", and left for Paris. In Paris he worked in the closed-off French community -- Monsieur Sans-Gene (1935, dir. Karl Anton), written in exile, was so successful it was remade in Hollywood by Rowland V. Lee as One Rainy Afternoon (1936), though Graham Greene, writing in the Spectator in July 1936, "strongly disliked it"; Abdul the Damned (1935, dir. Karl Grune), co-written by Pressburger, was made for British International Pictures, "which resulted in Pressburger crossing the Channel". [C33540]

The Paris years also produced the story he would carry longest: in 1934 he wrote The Miracle in St Anthony's Lane, about German exiles in Paris, for director Kurt Gerron; optioned several times in France in the 1930s, never made; it reached the screen only in 1957 as Miracle in Soho, converted from German exiles in Paris to Italian immigrants in London -- twenty-three years in the drawer. And one error to record, not repeat: an IMDb synopsis implies he fled France for England only after the Nazi invasion of France in 1940; he was in London by late 1935, years before. [C33541]

**Takeaway:** Exile is Pressburger's founding condition, not a biographical footnote: a man who left a key in a door spends the rest of his career writing about people who arrive somewhere with the wrong papers, the wrong language, and a story that has to be told twice.

## 7. London and Korda: English learned from a dictionary

Late in 1935 he arrived in Britain as a stateless person, entering on a stateless passport, speaking no English. He "methodically taught himself English with a course book and a dictionary, searching for an intimate relationship with the language, trying to avoid cliche and the simple phrases of tourism, teaching himself puns and inversions" -- Christie's phrasing, and the contrast Christie draws is with Curt Siodmak, who "sat in the cinema watching the same film all day to learn useful phrases and idioms." Pressburger learned the language as a writer learns it: not phrases, but the varying ways in which the same word can be used, "finding inflections and changes in pitch that effect meaning. It was a habit he did not abandon. During the late 50s he took to linguistic philosophy and the study of Ludwig Wittgenstein." He changed his name to Emeric in 1938, once he decided to settle, and became a British citizen in 1946. [C33542]

In England he found a small community of Hungarian film-makers who had fled the Nazis, including Alexander Korda, owner of London Films, who employed him as a screenwriter from 1937; Korda "made a habit of hiring fellow Hungarians". His British screenwriting debut was The Challenge (1938), an adventure film about the climbing of the Matterhorn in 1865, directed by Milton Rosmer and Luis Trenker -- and the uneasiness of that credit is itself Pressburger: Trenker "didn't distance himself enough from Nazism", and "some of Pressburger's own German colleagues labelled himself as a traitor." The pre-Powell British films that followed -- The Great Barrier (1937), The Silent Battle (1939), Spy for a Day (1939/1940), Atlantic Ferry (1941), Breach of Promise (1942), Squadron Leader X (1943), Wanted for Murder (1946) -- were, in Christie's blunt word, "hacking": scripts written to order, "the unmade Double Crime on the Maginot Line for producer Marcel Hellman", and none of the seven films he worked on without Powell over the next five years is available on disc. One flag: do NOT credit him with The Private Life of Henry VIII -- an ambiguous AFI person-page listing against credited writers Lajos Biro and Arthur Wimperis. [C33543]

**Takeaway:** He learned English the way he later wrote scripts -- by studying how one word changes meaning with pitch -- and the foreigner's ear for a language is the whole of his dialogue: precise, slightly formal, never colloquial for its own sake.

## 8. The meeting: the rolled-up paper

Korda asked Pressburger to improve the script for The Spy in Black (1939), and he met the film's director, Michael Powell. The film was a vehicle for Conrad Veidt and Valerie Hobson, based on a novel by J. Storer Clouston; Powell had been "brought in to save a film" whose original script "followed the book quite closely, but was too verbose and did not have a good role for either Veidt or Hobson." The meeting year is 1939 -- both Wikipedia articles say so, and the Voyager biography's "1938" is corrected by its own editor to 1939. Powell's account, from A Life in Movies, is the founding scene of the partnership: "Emeric produced a very small piece of rolled-up paper, and addressed the meeting. I listened spellbound. Since talkies took over the movies, I had worked with some good writers, but I had never met anything like this." [C33544]

What the rolled-up paper contained was a restructure, not a polish: Pressburger "had stood Storer Clouston's plot on its head and completely restructured the film", in Powell's words -- "He'd stood the story on its head, he'd turned a man into a woman and a woman into a man, he'd altered the suspense, he'd rewritten the end." Concretely, the novel's Rev. Alexander Burnett became the schoolteacher Ann Burnett (Valerie Hobson), a gender swap that creates the female lead and the love triangle, and the plot was re-centred on Captain Ernst Hardt (Conrad Veidt), a U-boat captain landed on Hoy, Orkney, to spy on the Grand Fleet at Scapa Flow, who falls for his contact. Powell recognised immediately "a marvellous continuity" -- "he had at last found a soulmate", in Christie's words -- and "they both soon recognised that although they were total opposites in background and personality, they had a common attitude to film-making and that they could work very well together." After The Spy in Black they made Contraband (1940) and 49th Parallel (1941) together, then left London Films and formed The Archers. [C33545]

**Takeaway:** The partnership is founded on a rewrite, not a pitch: Pressburger's signature move arrives fully formed at the first meeting -- take a flat literal adaptation, invert its structure, swap a gender to create a woman with agency, and re-centre the suspense on a sympathetic antagonist.

## 9. The joint-credit mechanism: who did what

The credit reads "Written, Produced and Directed by Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger", and the sources agree on what it meant in practice. Generally, Pressburger created the original story (for all their films from 1940-1946) and wrote the first draft of the script; they then passed the script back and forth a few times -- "they could never work on it together in the same room." For the dialogue, Pressburger knew what he wanted the characters to say but Powell would often supply some of the actual words. They both acted as producers, "perhaps Pressburger slightly more than Powell, since he could soothe the feathers ruffled by Powell's forthright manner", and they became their own producers "mainly to stop anyone else from interfering". The direction was nearly all done by Powell, but Pressburger was "always on hand, usually on the studio floor, to make sure that these late changes fit seamlessly into the story" -- Christie puts it more sharply, that he "collaborated (but never on the floor) in the directing, and worked patiently in the editing room." Once filming finished, Powell usually went off for a walk in the hills of Scotland to clear his head, but Pressburger was often closely involved in the editing, "especially in the way the music was used. Pressburger was a musician himself and had played the violin in an orchestra in Hungary." [C33546]

The cleanest contemporary shorthand is from Picture Show, 8 December 1956, on Ill Met by Moonlight: "Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger share joint responsibility for the triple function of writing, producing and directing all their productions. On a working basis Emeric Pressburger writes and Michael Powell directs, and they produce together." Powell consistently, emphatically rejected the characterisation of Pressburger as merely "Michael Powell's scriptwriter", and "was the first to say, in many interviews, that he couldn't have done most of what he did without Pressburger working as a full and equal film-making partner." Pressburger on Powell: "He knows what I am going to say even before I say it -- maybe even before I have thought it -- and that is very rare." And the division the people around them saw, per Bruce Eder: "Powell was the partly out-of-control genius, while Pressburger was the force that focused the team onto their most viable projects." [C33547]

The joint credit mattered fiercely to Pressburger, and the 1970-71 NFT retrospective proved it: when Christie proposed calling it "Written, Produced and Directed by Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger", Powell objected (it would include pre-Emeric quota films) and suggested "Michael Powell in Collaboration with Emeric Pressburger". Pressburger "didn't take well to this shift in emphasis. The films were, after all, 'Written, Produced and Directed' by them both." A patronising NFT letter ("We understand that you would like to appear on stage with Michael Powell...") led him to refuse to appear at all. Christie, who first met him in November 1970, found in him "a feeling of intimacy completely absent in Powell. If Powell was something of a boyscout, clipping his account of events, signalling but rarely detailing essential information and never questioning his own judgment, Pressburger had a reflective presence, as if he was considering an issue for the first time. The films that he made with Powell are like diaries." [C33548]

**Takeaway:** The credit is not a courtesy -- it is the mechanism. One man invents the story and holds the structure; the other turns it into images; neither can be removed without the films collapsing into something else, as Twice Upon a Time proves.

## 10. The Archers manifesto: five points, one unit

In 1942, in a letter to Wendy Hiller asking her to appear in Colonel Blimp, Pressburger set out "The Archers' Manifesto" in five points: (1) "We owe allegiance to nobody except the financial interests which provide our money; and, to them, the sole responsibility of ensuring them a profit, not a loss." (2) "Every single foot in our films is our own responsibility and nobody else's. We refuse to be guided or coerced by any influence but our own judgement." (3) "When we start work on a new idea, we must be a year ahead, not only of our competitors, but also of the times. A real film, from idea to universal release, takes a year. Or more." (4) "No artist believes in escapism. And we secretly believe that no audience does. We have proved, at any rate, that they will pay to see the truth, for other reasons than her nakedness." (5) "At any time, and particularly at the present, the self-respect of all collaborators, from star to propman, is sustained, or diminished, by the theme and purpose of the film they are working on." The joint credit was adopted for One of Our Aircraft Is Missing (1942); in 1943 they incorporated Archers Film Productions and adopted the archery-target logo -- the arrow thudding into a bull's-eye -- which opens the films. The name was taken from a poem by critic James Agate ("The arrow was pure gold / But somehow missed the target..."). The Rank offer after One of Our Aircraft Is Missing let them operate within Independent Producers, Rank's 1942 vehicle for ambitious independent companies, with finance through General Film Distributors -- "a rare freedom for filmmakers in the 1940s". [C33549]

**Takeaway:** Point 4 is the whole aesthetic in one sentence: the fantasy films are not escapism, they are the truth told in a form the audience will pay to see -- and point 2 is why Selznick, in Gone to Earth, is the villain of the story.

## 11. 49th Parallel: the journey as a series of arguments

49th Parallel (1941) is Pressburger's first great structural invention, and it won him the Oscar for Best Story. Conceived by the Ministry of Information as propaganda to sway American involvement in the war, it works, in DVD Savant's phrase, by having "the audacity to make its main character a Nazi fugitive" -- and, in Dennis Schwartz's, by being "fair-minded, richly imaginative, and suspenseful", the debate, not the lecture, carrying the propaganda. The mechanism is the one Pressburger took from Agatha Christie's Ten Little Indians: six U-boat men are stranded in Hudson Bay when RCAF bombers sink U-37 while their raiding party is ashore, and they must cross Canada to neutral America. Each episode removes one or more of them and stages a debate about freedom against totalitarianism with a different Canadian community: the French-Canadian trapper Johnnie (Laurence Olivier), who dies rather than submit; the Hutterite farming community near Winnipeg, where leader Peter (Anton Walbrook) refutes Lieutenant Hirth's (Eric Portman) fanatical speech and sailor Vogel (Niall MacGinnis) is tried and executed by Hirth for wanting to stay as the community's baker; the English academic Philip Armstrong Scott (Leslie Howard), whom Hirth calls a decadent coward; finally the freight-car confrontation with AWOL Canadian soldier Andy Brock (Raymond Massey) at Niagara Falls, where Brock gets Hirth sent back to Canada because he is not on the manifest. [C33550]

The film was nominated for Best Picture and Best Original Screenplay (shared with Rodney Ackland) as well as winning Best Story. After returning from the Canadian research trip, Pressburger -- still an enemy alien -- was jailed; "Powell insisted on joining his friend in custody. The writer could only survive the ordeal with help from his powerful friends in high places." The Oscar is now on display at the Savile Club, London. The structure is the thesis: the propaganda is not a speech but a route, each stop a community with a different answer to the same question, the group whittled down one by one until only the fanatic is left, defeated by a manifest -- a document. [C33551]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's first master mechanism -- the journey as a series of staged arguments, each community a thesis, each German a counter-thesis -- and the document (the manifest) is what finally decides it.

## 12. One of Our Aircraft Is Missing: the reversal, the joint credit is born

One of Our Aircraft Is Missing (1942) is the first film to carry the joint credit and the first made under the banner of The Archers, and it is a deliberate reversal of 49th Parallel: "A reversal of the plot of Powell and Pressburger's previous film... In the 49th Parallel, the Germans stranded in Canada argued and fought amongst themselves, while the British fliers in this film work well together as a team." The crew of Wellington bomber "B for Bertie" bail out over the Netherlands near the Zuiderzee after a raid on Stuttgart; the abandoned bomber flies on to England and crashes into a pylon, so no crash is reported in Holland and the first Dutch helpers are suspicious. The crew are passed hand to hand: bicycling in disguise to a football match where the missing sixth man is found playing for one of the teams; hidden in a truck to Jo de Vries (Googie Withers), who pretends to be pro-German while the Germans are garrisoned in her mansion; rowing to the North Sea under cover of an air raid; sheltering in a German rescue buoy, taking two shot-down enemy airmen prisoner; the buoy towed back to England because the wounded Sir George Corbett cannot be moved. Powell "encouraged Pressburger to create some significant female characters, such as Pamela Brown and Googie Withers as female Dutch Resistance leaders." Oscar-nominated for Best Writing, Original Screenplay and Best Effects. [C33552]

**Takeaway:** The joint credit begins with a mirror image: same stranded-crew engine, polarity reversed, and the suspense now carried by a chain of civilian helpers -- the "passed from hand to hand" structure that is pure Pressburger logistics.

## 13. The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp: forty years in one pan

Colonel Blimp (1943) is structured as a 1942 prologue and epilogue framing three flashback sections (1902, 1918, 1940), and it compresses forty years by omission: it leaves out entire world wars, marriages, deaths and a duel, and marks the gaps with visual tallies -- the wall of the hunter's room filling with animal-head trophies, the turning pages of a scrapbook, the Turkish bath -- rather than exposition. The frame is 1942: an elderly, bald General Clive Wynn-Candy (Roger Livesey) is "captured" in a Turkish bath by a young lieutenant during a war game. The transition to 1902 is a single continuous camera move, not a cut: the camera pans beyond the scuffle to the other end of the pool, where a younger and fully-haired Candy emerges from the bath, and forty years pass in one move. Churchill tried to prevent the film being made, and an export ban remained until August 1943 at his insistence; the War Office refused Laurence Olivier's release and military equipment -- "so we stole them", in Powell's phrase. The US version of 1945 lost about fifty minutes and replaced the flashback structure with a chronological storyline; the original 163-minute version was restored in the 1980s. [C33553]

Pressburger's devices in the film are three, and they are all his. One actress, three women: Deborah Kerr as Edith Hunter (1902), Barbara Wynne (the WWI nurse) and Angela "Johnny" Cannon (1942) -- the "eternal feminine", the same device of doubling that returns in Hoffmann; Pressburger said of it, "I think that I myself used to be like that, and I think that many people have a certain type that they are always chasing through their lives, trying to find their lives again and again and again." A cartoon turned into an epic: Colonel Blimp was David Low's satirical cartoon of a "puffy, pompous... near-Fascist" reactionary, and Pressburger "spun a tale that spans forty years" from non-linear cartoons. And the German as the moral centre: Theo Kretschmar-Schuldorff (Anton Walbrook), the anti-Nazi Prussian, is more clear-sighted than the hero -- the Spy in Black sympathetic-German device at full scale -- and his speech explaining why Britain must abandon gentlemanly warfare, filmed in a single three-and-a-half-minute take, is what one Criterion essay calls "the crowning masterpiece of screenwriter Emeric Pressburger". [C33554]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's flashback is not exposition but the argument itself -- the pool-dive makes forty years a single gesture, and the film's thesis (the old were young once; the gentleman's code is dead) is proved by structure, not stated.

## 14. A Canterbury Tale: the Glue Man, the falcon and the Spitfire

A Canterbury Tale (1944) opens with Esmond Knight narrating Chaucer's Prologue in Middle English as medieval pilgrims climb to the Kentish track, and then the match cut: "a soaring falcon released by Chaucer's Squire... into a swooping Spitfire. The Squire in his medieval garb is transformed into a British infantryman" -- a cut The Arts Desk notes came twenty-four years before Kubrick telescoped millennia with 2001's match cut. The plot is Pressburger's oddest crime: on Friday night, 27 August 1943, three strangers arrive at Chillingbourne -- British Sergeant Peter Gibbs (Dennis Price), US Sergeant Bob Johnson (real-life Sgt. John Sweet, who got off the train by mistake), and Land Girl Alison Smith (Sheila Sim). Alison is attacked by a uniformed man who pours glue on her hair -- the "glue man", who has attacked other women. The three spend the weekend detecting: Alison interviews the victims for dates and times; Gibbs borrows the fire-watch roster from magistrate Thomas Colpeper (Eric Portman); Johnson's boy commandos, on a paper salvage drive, find receipts for gum sold to Colpeper. The dates match Colpeper's night watches. [C33555]

On the Monday train to Canterbury they confront him; he does not deny it. Colpeper poured glue so the girls "became too scared to date the sex-hungry soldiers... and were less likely to cheat on their husbands and boyfriends overseas" -- and then, in Graham Fuller's reading, "has emerged as a beneficent magus, an agent of divine blessings." The ending delivers three blessings, set to Allan Gray's medley of Bach's Toccata: Alison learns her RAF lover is alive in Gibraltar; Peter, a cinema organist in peacetime, gets to play Canterbury Cathedral's organ; Bob receives the packet of letters from the girlfriend he suspected of forsaking him. "Their sleuthing is an excuse for a series of rural epiphanies", in Fuller's phrase; the film "eases... from perverse comedy-thriller into perverse Romantic pastoral." The thesis, per Wikipedia: "the past always haunts the present" in the English landscape. [C33556]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger invents a crime so odd (glue in the hair) that solving it cannot be the point -- the investigation is a pilgrimage machine that delivers three strangers to Canterbury and three blessings. The falcon-to-Spitfire cut is the whole film in one edit: 600 years collapsed, the past alive in the present.

## 15. I Know Where I'm Going!: the journey that never arrives

I Know Where I'm Going! (1945) is Pressburger's most elegant obstruction plot. Joan Webster (Wendy Hiller), 25, "upper middle class... ambitious, independent", has "always known where she is going" -- the prologue tells her whole life at speed from infancy ("When Joan was only one, she already knew where she was going. Going right? Left? No -- straight on") to her engagement to industrialist Sir Robert Bellinger, one of the wealthiest men in England, nearly her father's age. She travels Manchester to Mull to marry him on Kiloran, an island he leases. Bad weather strands her on Mull; she meets Torquil MacNeil (Roger Livesey), a Royal Navy officer on eight days' leave trying to get home to Kiloran. The film's wager, in one critic's phrase: "a will that knows what it wants can be a way of not knowing what one values, that money buys the use of a place but not its life." [C33557]

Pressburger's devices are three. Weather as the antagonist and the plot: gale, fog, the Corryvreckan whirlpool -- Joan bribes Kenny (20 pounds) to attempt the crossing; Torquil boards to stop her; the boat is caught in the whirlpool and Torquil restarts the flooded engine just in time. The curse: Moy Castle, home of the MacNeils of Kiloran -- no laird may cross its threshold. Torquil refuses to enter; Joan learns he IS the laird (Bellinger only rents the island). At the end he enters the castle and finds the curse's inscription -- and it turns out to be a blessing. And the song as structure: the folk song "I Know Where I'm Going" recurs with the ironic last line -- "I know who I love / But the dear knows who I'll marry" -- and the film's joke that she is marrying "Consolidated Chemical Industries" (Bellinger's company), staged as a mock wedding. The whirlpool itself, as the Powell study records, is the most constructed passage in the film -- real footage through telescopic lenses, a studio tank with gelatin-thickened water, a replica boat on gimbals, model boats, the final shot filmed with a high-speed camera running in reverse -- and as the boat nears it the music drops out entirely. [C33558]

**Takeaway:** The heroine's plan is perfect and the island is one short boat-ride away, but weather, a curse and a ceilidh dismantle the plan day by day until the plan itself is the thing she abandons. The whirlpool is the climax because the obstacle IS the story.

## 16. A Matter of Life and Death: heaven in dye-monochrome

A Matter of Life and Death (1946) -- Stairway to Heaven in America -- is Pressburger turning a diplomatic commission into a metaphysical love story whose central machine is a courtroom. 2 May 1945: Squadron Leader Peter Carter (David Niven), in a burning Lancaster over the Channel, radios the American WAC June (Kim Hunter) -- and falls in love with the voice before he jumps without a parachute. He should have died; Conductor 71 (Marius Goring) fails to collect him because of fog. Peter survives with a brain injury; the Other World claims him; Dr. Reeves (Roger Livesey) defends him in a heavenly court. The defence's proof: June will take Peter's place on the stairway without hesitation -- she steps onto it, it halts, she rushes back to his arms. The opening title sustains both readings at once: "This is a story of two Worlds -- the one we know and another which exists only in the mind of a young airman whose life and imagination have been violently shaped by war" -- so heaven is real, or it is a concussed brain's fantasy. [C33559]

The colour inversion does the philosophy: Earth in three-strip Technicolor, the Other World in black-and-white -- filmed in Technicolor but not fully developed, "giving a pearly hue". The escalator -- "Operation Ethel", built by London Passenger Transport Board engineers -- is where the sources conflict and the study records the conflict: Wikipedia gives 106 steps, each 20 feet wide, 12 hp, 3,000 pounds, three months to make; TCM gives 266 twenty-foot steps and 85 tons; and on the sound, Wikipedia notes the claim that the machinery noise prevented live recording and all escalator scenes were dubbed, against cinematographer Jack Cardiff's recollection that it operated completely silently. Neither figure is safe to cite without naming the source. The trial itself, with an American prosecutor and a jury of the dead from many nations, is Pressburger's answer to the commission to mend US-UK relations -- "the closest the Archers came to a standard message picture, with an overt intent to propagate postwar harmony between England and the United States." BFI 100 list #20; Total Film's 2004 poll of 25 critics named it the second greatest British film ever. [C33560]

**Takeaway:** Love is proved not by a speech but by a woman stepping onto an escalator. The colour inversion does the philosophy -- the afterlife is the drab, bureaucratic world; living Earth is the coloured one.

## 17. Black Narcissus: the studio Himalayas

Black Narcissus (1947), from Rumer Godden's 1939 novel, is the film where Pressburger's script makes the landscape the antagonist and Powell's decision to fake the Himalayas entirely is what lets colour and sound behave like music. Powell decided NOT to shoot in India and to combine no real location photography with studio scenes, so that the soundtrack could be "an organic whole of dialogue, sound effects, and music, very much in the way that an opera is composed". The Palace of Mopu (a former harem turned convent) was built at Pinewood by Alfred Junge; the Himalayas are huge canvas backdrops, glass shots and matte paintings by W. Percy Day, and scale models. The backdrops "were tilted back at a 35 degree angle to eliminate shadows and soak up as much of the sun as possible" (TCM) -- the 35-degree figure is the only sourced angle; the 45-degree figure in the brief was NOT found in any source read and is not used here. BAFTA's production-design piece confirms the glass painting: "hanging a sheet of glass between the camera and the scene, and painting in the view you needed"; the bell-drop shot -- Clodagh appearing hundreds of feet high -- was filmed with her "only about ten feet from the ground". [C33561]

The five-nuns structure is Pressburger's: each nun is given a failure mode by the place -- Clodagh's remembered failed romance in Ireland; Ruth's jealousy and obsession with Mr. Dean (the lipstick scene, the red dress ordered to charm him, the rejected declaration, the attempt to push Clodagh off the cliff at the morning bell); Philippa planting flowers instead of vegetables and asking for a transfer; Honey's fatal dosing of a baby. The place dismantles the mission one nun at a time. The ending was rewritten in the cutting: the film was to end with Clodagh sobbing and blaming herself to Mother Dorothea; when Powell saw the rainfall-on-leaves shot he made THAT the last scene and scrapped the Dorothea scene. Godden "allowed Pressburger to write his own screenplay adaptation with Powell" -- adaptation by permission to reinvent. Oscars for Best Cinematography, Color (Cardiff) and Best Art Direction-Set Decoration, Color (Junge); the US Legion of Decency condemned it, and ten cuts (about 900 feet), including Clodagh's flashbacks, were supervised by Pressburger himself, who called them "reasonable, fair and just". [C33562]

**Takeaway:** The convent fails because the place is designed, by the script, to leak -- a palace that was a harem, a wind that never stops, a cliff at the end of every corridor -- and refusing the location is what makes the film true.

## 18. The Red Shoes: the ballet that exists only on film

The Red Shoes (1948) is the film with the longest shelf-life in the partnership: Pressburger had written the first version in 1937 for Korda as a vehicle for Merle Oberon (who could not dance; a double would have been used); Korda abandoned it for The Thief of Bagdad. In 1946 Powell and Pressburger bought the rights back for 9,000 pounds. Powell: the original contained "significantly more dialogue and less story". When Pressburger returned to the script he rewrote the light vehicle into the macabre parable -- the IADT dissertation's phrase is that the 1937 version "was originally much lighter". The ballet itself is the mechanism: The Ballet of the Red Shoes duplicates Vicky's story, foreshadows her love for Julian (composer/conductor in the ballet's orchestra) and Lermontov's jealousy, and collapses the two worlds -- Vicky wears the red shoes in her dressing room before the performance although her character does not put them on until part-way through the ballet; Powell decided it was artistically "right" because otherwise "it takes away the ambiguity over why she died". The ballet's length is conflicted in the sources -- Wikipedia's Themes section says 17 minutes, its Casting section says fifteen, BFI Player says 15, others say 16 or 20 -- plausibly a matter of where the sequence is taken to start and end; no single figure is safe without naming the source. [C33563]

The score was composed to pictures: Easdale wrote the ballet music to 120 cartoon drawings and storyboards approved by Robert Helpmann, replaced by completed shots as filming progressed; Beecham conducted with the Royal Philharmonic. Powell and Pressburger "decided early on that they had to use dancers who could act rather than actors who could dance"; Moira Shearer declined for a year ("The Red Shoes was the last thing I wanted to do. I fought for a year to get away from that film"), then accepted with Ninette de Valois's blessing; the dancers "were lucky if we ever danced for as long as one minute" at a stretch. The ultimatum structure drives the plot: Lermontov's rule (no marriage in the company), his ultimatum to Julian (break up with Vicky or leave), Vicky's final choice -- and the last image, a ballet performed around an absence, a spotlight on the empty space where Vicky would have danced, after she jumps in front of a train wearing the red shoes. Five Oscar nominations including Best Picture and Best Original Story (Pressburger); wins for Art Direction and Music Scoring (Easdale). The first British film to gross over $5 million in US theatrical rentals; the Rank relationship broke over the film's cost -- planned 15 weeks and 300,000 pounds, actual 24 weeks and over 500,000 -- and the Archers moved to Korda. [C33564]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's mechanism is the mise en abyme with teeth -- a fairy tale staged inside a backstage drama so that the heroine lives the tale twice, once on stage and once in life, and the film's final image (a ballet performed around an absence) is the thesis: art goes on, the artist does not.

## 19. The Small Back Room: the bottle and the bomb

The Small Back Room (1949), from Nigel Balchin's 1943 novel, is Pressburger's smallest machine -- one man, one bottle, one bomb -- and the deliberate contrast with the Technicolor melodramas before it: black and white, sealed-off interior spaces (offices, Tube carriages, pubs, dancehalls), "the everyday functions of the men and women who worked far from the front in support of the British war effort". Sammy Rice (David Farrar), a back-room scientist in wartime London, has a prosthetic foot ("tin foot") in constant pain, a whisky habit, and a belief that Susan's love is pity. The film's engine is a bottle of whisky kept in his flat "for celebrating when the Allies win the war" -- the temptation object; the subjective sequence, alone in the flat awaiting Susan, has "the bottle of whiskey loom ever larger, as does the ticking of the clock". The booby-trap is the character test: a new Nazi anti-personnel device, flask-shaped, trembler-detonated, with two booby traps. Rice's predecessor Stuart dies defusing the first; Rice, still drunk after a rampage that smashed his flat and drove Susan away, listens to a transcript of Stuart's comments during the disarming and discovers the second trap, then defuses it on Chesil Beach, shot in close detail -- "faces, hands, tools such as wrenches and the bomb itself". The theme, per Wikipedia: "the unsung heroes of the Second World War" -- back-room boffins, not front-line soldiers. Powell called it "my best film". [C33565]

**Takeaway:** The bomb can only be defused by a man who has just failed at everything else, and the transcript of a dead man's voice is the script's most Pressburger device: the dead colleague talks the hero through the trap.

## 20. Gone to Earth: Selznick and the limit of independence

Gone to Earth (1950), from Mary Webb's 1917 novel, is the film whose real subject, for a study of Pressburger, is what happened after the script. Hazel Woodus (Jennifer Jones), "the bare-footed, superstitious, and backwoods daughter" of a coffin-maker, harp-repairer and beekeeper in late-1890s Shropshire, "refuses to bow down to her father, the spiritual clergyman to whom she is betrothed, or the reckless squire who desires her" -- married to the clergyman Marston (Cyril Cusack), desired by squire Reddin (David Farrar), loyal to her tame fox, the third term in the triangle, the wildness she will not surrender. The film was co-produced with David O. Selznick, who held the American rights; he "had a penchant for dictating long and rambling notes... while under the influence of amphetamines and flooded the production with memos, most of which were ignored". He disliked the finished film, sued the Archers, lost the court case, but discovered he had the right to change the film for its American release -- and had it re-edited with extra scenes shot in Hollywood under Rouben Mamoulian (uncredited) as The Wild Heart (1952, 82 minutes): a prologue (Joseph Cotten narration), explanatory scenes "often literally, with labels or inscriptions on items", more close-ups of his wife Jennifer Jones, and a stuffed-toy fox in the added final scenes. [C33566]

Powell claimed Selznick left only about 35 minutes of the original; in fact about two-thirds remains intact -- the cut removed 28 minutes (110 to 82). The original was fully restored by the BFI National Archive in 1985; a further restoration (Disney/Film Foundation/BFI, supervised by Thelma Schoonmaker) was announced for 2026. The film's real lesson is the Manifesto's point 2 meeting its limit: "Every single foot in our films is our own responsibility and nobody else's" -- except when a co-producer owns the American rights. Selznick's literalising additions (labels on objects, a spoken prologue, a stuffed fox) are the photographic negative of Pressburger's method: explanation pasted onto a film built from atmosphere. [C33567]

**Takeaway:** The Archers' independence was a contract, not a fact -- and the man who owned the rights could paste labels onto the mystery. Pressburger's method is the opposite of Selznick's: never explain what the atmosphere already says.

## 21. The Tales of Hoffmann: the composed film

The Tales of Hoffmann (1951) is the logical endpoint of the Archers' method -- the screenplay disappears into a score, the actors disappear into singers and dancers, and the film is edited to music rather than dialogue. "In the later years of his partnership with Pressburger, Powell became interested in what he termed 'a composed film', a marriage of image to operatic music. The finale of Black Narcissus and the ballet sequence of The Red Shoes were earlier steps toward his goal." The entire opera was prerecorded to create the soundtrack and the film was edited to the rhythms of the music; the production is "completely without dialogue and, with the exception of Robert Rounseville and Ann Ayars, none of the actors did their own singing." Powell, quoted by TCM: "We were virtually making a silent film." The continuity girl worked not from a normal typed script but from "a fat musical score, weighing about two and a half pounds, embroidered round the edges with a mixture of film and musical terms" -- the score WAS the shooting script, the one case where the brief's "screenplay writes the camera move" idea is literally true, and it is a score, not a screenplay. [C33568]

Pressburger's contribution is structural adaptation: nearly every character is played by two people, a singer and a dancer -- "The cinema thus creates here a new artistic monster: the best legs adorned by the best voice" (Bazin); Lindorf's music omitted, making him a silent character; Stella changed from opera singer to ballerina; Olympia's seduction done by dancing as well as singing; "Giulietta" moved before "Antonia"; the Antonia sequence shortened to end with the trio rather than her death scene. Colour marks the acts: Olympia/Paris yellow (farcical), Giulietta/Venice dark red (hellish), Antonia/Greece shades of blue (sad); sets "deliberately made to look artificial", the Giulietta gondola moving through "deliberately artificial Venetian canals". Production dates are conflicted and recorded as such: Wikipedia states "in production from 1 to 16 July 1950 at Shepperton Studios" -- sixteen days for a 128-minute Technicolor opera film is implausible as a total production span -- against TCM's "completed in nine weeks, with two weeks of extensive rehearsals", and Wikipedia's own Soundtrack section says the soundtrack was recorded at Shepperton between May and September 1950. Bosley Crowther in the New York Times found it "vastly wearying... sates the senses without striking any real dramatic fire"; Cecil B. DeMille wrote to Powell and Pressburger: "For the first time in my life, I was treated to Grand Opera where the beauty, power and scope of the music was equally matched by the visual presentation." Silver Bear for Best Musical at the 1st Berlin International Film Festival. [C33569]

**Takeaway:** Hoffmann is the composed film with the dialogue removed entirely -- the score is the script, the continuity girl reads music, and the voice does not have to belong to the body that moves.

## 22. The Battle of the River Plate: a memoir found on a working holiday

The Battle of the River Plate (1956) is the late Archers method stripped of fantasy: no studio Himalayas, no ballet -- real ships, real harbours, and a memoir found on a working holiday supplies the human angle the script could not invent. The film "had its genesis in an invitation to Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger to attend a film festival in Argentina in 1954. They decided they could not afford to take the time from their schedules unless it was a working holiday, and used the trip to research the defeat of Admiral Graf Spee." Early in planning, "the team was hard-pressed to find a human angle... They didn't want to do just a pseudo-documentary about ships at sea. Then, while interviewing one of the surviving British naval officers, Pressburger was given a copy of I Was Graf Spee's Prisoner (1940), a memoir by Captain Patrick Dove, a merchant seaman whose ship was sunk by the Germans -- which became the basis of the human story of the film." Dove (Bernard Lee) is the civilian through-line: the captured merchant seamen locked below decks on the Graf Spee are the film's human scale against the naval battle. And the sympathetic German, again: Langsdorff (Peter Finch), who scuttles his ship rather than hand it over, is played as an honourable antagonist -- the Spy in Black/Blimp device in its last Archers form. The battle itself is a negotiation: three smaller cruisers "outmaneuvered, outclassed and ultimately out-negotiated" the pocket battleship, and the climax is diplomatic (Montevideo, the 72-hour rule, the scuttling) as much as ballistic. Real ships -- USS Salem as Graf Spee (the US Navy refused to let them paint over her bow number 139 or put Nazi insignia on her, so the script explains "139" as German camouflage), HMS Ajax played by HMS Sheffield, Exeter by HMS Jamaica, Achilles playing herself -- and the scuttling shot with a 23-foot model in a six-foot-deep tank at Pinewood. "A return to box-office glory" after the mid-1950s failures; selected for the Royal Film Performance. [C33570]

**Takeaway:** Pressburger's last great structural find is a book handed to him during research -- the human angle cannot be invented, only found, and the writer's job is to be in the room when it is handed over.

## 23. The sympathetic enemy

Hardt (The Spy in Black), Theo (Blimp), Langsdorff (River Plate), and by inversion Hirth (49th Parallel, the enemy as protagonist): Pressburger humanises the antagonist first and lets the thesis follow. The device is there from the first meeting -- the first third of The Spy in Black "completely downplays any menace", Hardt is "articulate, refined, and patient", a German officer "whose morals and code of conduct is vastly superior to the Nazi equivalents of 1939" -- and it is there because Pressburger knew, from the inside, what it was to be the German-speaking outsider in a country at war with Germany: he had been one himself, interned as an enemy alien after the 49th Parallel trip, jailed while Powell insisted on joining him in custody. The sympathetic enemy is not a trick of even-handedness; it is autobiography with the serial numbers filed off. In Blimp the device reaches full scale, because Theo, the anti-Nazi Prussian, is more clear-sighted than the hero -- the film's thesis (the gentleman's code is dead) can only be spoken by the man who has already lost everything to it. [C33571]

**Takeaway:** Humanise the antagonist first; the thesis follows. A villain you understand is worth ten villains you merely defeat.

## 24. The document as engine

Shore passes, a cigarette-paper message, a train timetable (Contraband); the fire-watch roster and gum receipts (Canterbury); a dead man's transcript (Small Back Room); a prisoner's memoir (River Plate); a musical score as shooting script (Hoffmann); 120 storyboard drawings as a score (Red Shoes); the manifest that defeats Hirth (49th Parallel). Paper carries the plot. This is the Dramaturgie department made into dramaturgy: a man who spent years selecting, approving and editing other people's scripts writes films in which the plot turns on a piece of paper being stolen, read, matched, or misfiled. The document is never decoration -- it is the MacGuffin, the clue, the proof, and the verdict, and it is also the most honest image of how Pressburger himself lived: a stateless man on a Nansen passport, a writer whose whole career was papers submitted, papers refused, papers forwarded. [C33572]

**Takeaway:** When in doubt, give the plot a piece of paper: something that can be stolen, forged, matched against a roster, or read aloud by a dead man.

## 25. Doubling: one person, two or three incarnations

Deborah Kerr three times in Blimp (Edith, Barbara, Angela) -- the "eternal feminine", the type a man chases through his life trying to find again and again; Stella/Olympia/Giulietta/Antonia as aspects of one woman in Hoffmann, each played by two people, a singer and a dancer; Vicky living the ballet she dances in The Red Shoes, wearing the red shoes in her dressing room before her character puts them on; the Boy in the ballet turning into a sketch on cellophane, then into a living newspaper, then a prince, then the parson who unties the shoes as the girl dies. One person, two or three incarnations, is Pressburger's casting device, and it is the same device as the rest of his life: Imre becomes Emmerich becomes Emeric becomes Richard Imrie; the same story is written in 1937 and rewritten in 1946; the same man is a mathematician, a violinist, a journalist, a script-reader, a screenwriter, a novelist. Doubling is not a trick in his films; it is the condition of the exile, who is always at least two people -- the one he was, and the one he has become. [C33573]

**Takeaway:** Cast one person twice and the audience does the work: the second incarnation is the first one's future, or its past, or its fate.

## 26. Twice Upon a Time: the solo test

Twice Upon a Time (1953) is Pressburger's only solo directing credit, and it is the test case for everything the partnership's division of labour claims. A twin-swap family comedy from Erich Kastner's Das doppelte Lottchen (1949), co-written with his then-wife Wendy Orme, starring Hugh Williams, Elizabeth Allan and Jack Hawkins; produced by Pressburger; cinematography Christopher Challis; released 6 July 1953, 75 minutes, budget 102,000 pounds (some filmographies give 1952, the production year). Twin sisters separated when their parents divorce meet again by accident at the same summer camp and hatch a plan to reunite their parents; it was the first English-language adaptation of Kastner's story, later twice adapted as The Parent Trap (1961 and 1998). The contemporary reception was poor: the Monthly Film Bulletin found "generally lifeless direction, a pace unremittingly slow, and, apart from the performances of Jack Hawkins and Elizabeth Allan... very indifferent acting"; the Daily Film Renter called it "a woman's picture if ever there was one." Korda's verdict, as the NFI records it: "Boys, I could eat a tin of trims and shit a better picture." [C33574]

The BFI's judgement is the one that matters, because it is the partnership's judgement: "Pressburger's one attempt at directing, Twice Upon a Time (1953), showed how greatly he depended on Powell's sprightly visual cockiness. On the other hand, Powell's films without Pressburger lack ambiguity and are directed with little sensitivity towards the actors." The NFI makes the same point more bluntly: "the fact that Pressburger was ill-fit for actual on-set directing is exemplified by his single solo directing credit." No box-office figure was found; no evidence was found of any later critical rehabilitation comparable to the Archers films; the film remains a footnote, notable mainly for its singularity -- though the NFI's claim that "no copies of the film survived for posterity" is wrong, contradicted by the BFI National Archive's holdings and a BFI screening. The solo film proves the division of labour by failing at exactly the point where Powell was needed: the floor. [C33575]

**Takeaway:** The solo directing test is the control experiment for the partnership: without Powell on the floor, the script is lifeless; without Pressburger at the desk, Powell's films lack ambiguity. Neither half works alone.

## 27. The novels: Killing a Mouse on Sunday and The Glass Pearls

After the partnership ended, Pressburger did what he had told the Daily Mail he wanted to do after years of "communal" creativity in film: "prove I could do something on my own." The first novel, Killing a Mouse on Sunday (1961), is set in the aftermath of the Spanish Civil War and tells of a once-notorious bandit, now a tired old man living in exile in France, who resolves to cross the border back into Spain, despite the danger to his life, to visit his dying mother; the anarchist Quico Sabate's death inspired the book, and the NFI notes it is told from four different perspectives. It "received favourable reviews and was soon translated into a dozen languages", and was filmed as Behold a Pale Horse (1964, dir. Fred Zinnemann, screenplay by JP Miller) -- Pressburger was not trusted with the screenplay. The publishing detail is conflicted (Collins, London, 1961 in the novel article; Harcourt 1961 with a Collins reprint as Behold a Pale Horse in 1964 in Contemporary Authors); the title form "on Sunday" (not "on a Sunday") is used here. [C33576]

The second novel, The Glass Pearls (1966, Heinemann), is the darker book and the one that matters: set in the summer of 1965, it opens on the first Saturday in June when Karl Braun, a piano tuner, moves into new lodgings in southwest London. Braun is really the infamous Dr. Otto Reitmuller, a brain surgeon turned Nazi war criminal hiding for twenty years, "one of 'the biggest fish' sought by those in pursuit of justice, wanted for crimes against humanity in the form of the inhumane surgical experiments he carried out on concentration camp inmates." It garnered one lone review, a damning write-up in the Times Literary Supplement (28 April 1966), and "barely sold its initial print run of four thousand copies, immediately sinking without a trace"; reissued by Faber Finds in 2015 (with a preface by Macdonald and an introduction by Caitlin McDonald) and again in 2022, it has since been highly praised -- Lucy Scholes in the Paris Review (2019) calls it "a truly remarkable work... an important addition to the genre of Holocaust literature" and "a master class in rendering the banality of evil". The TLS complained the "twist" is revealed too soon; Scholes argues this misunderstands the book -- the horror is in forcing the reader to sympathise with Braun, "a cultured man, a fine musician, an accomplished violinist", who never expresses regret. And the book is autobiographical in the most unsettling way: Braun passes off as his own the memories of one of his patients -- fleeing Germany on a night train, hands trembling as the Gestapo examined his passport; queuing at the Paris Prefecture; a murder in a house on the rue Quentin Bauchart -- Pressburger's own memories, stolen by his Nazi. McDonald reads the novel as "the culmination of Pressburger's attempt to understand the Nazi mentality", continuous with the "good German" figures in his films, and compares its delayed reception to Powell's Peeping Tom -- "simply too shocking for the audiences of the time". The unpublished third novel is The Unholy Lie; the BFI notes a third novel was turned down and relegated to the desk drawer, "where like wine, he would wait for them to mature" -- the identification of the two is probable, not proven. [C33577]

**Takeaway:** The novels are the solo work the films could not be: the first proves he can write alone, the second proves what he was writing about all along -- the Nazi mind, understood from the inside, by a man who fled it and whose mother it killed.

## 28. Richard Imrie: the pseudonymous years

The post-Archers screen career is thin and largely pseudonymous. Miracle in Soho (1957, dir. Julian Amyes) was his first solo production and it flopped: the script dated to Paris 1934 as The Miracle in St Anthony's Lane, about German exiles in Paris, relocated to Italian immigrants in London; Powell called it "a tender trap, a good fairy, a marshmallow, the sort of film that attracts and sucks in top talent like William Wyler, Margaret Sullavan and then leaves them floundering in the gooey mess." Pressburger "sometimes saw his pension in the continual optioning of stories that went unmade... Sometimes he misjudged it and the ideas went flat, as with Miracle in St. Anthony's Lane." The unmade Gandhi film with David Lean (India trip 1958, script discarded) rests on the NFI profile -- the trip is corroborated, the "too didactic" judgement is NFI-only. [C33578]

Then the pseudonym: "Richard Imrie". Operation Crossbow (1965, dir. Michael Anderson) -- the screenplay "was written by Emeric Pressburger (under the pseudonym Richard Imrie), in collaboration with Derry Quinn and Ray Rigby". TCM's Rob Nixon gives the backstory: he "had fallen on hard times by the 1960s. His financial situation, aggravated by extravagant spending, was rocky, and he found himself in the position of having to take on any film work he could get. He was brought in to doctor Derry Quinn and Ray Rigby's script for Operation Crossbow only a few weeks before shooting began in June 1964. He frantically re-wrote scenes and dictated new ones and dialogue to his assistant, who then rushed them to the studio, often only a few hours ahead of the camera. He received screen credit as Imrie, a name he chose not so much to protect his reputation but to re-launch his career when he was no longer much in demand." They're a Weird Mob (1966), directed by Powell from the novel by John O'Grady, was written by "Richard Imrie" -- a Powell-directed film with a Pressburger script, a collaboration, not a solo credit, even though the script is pseudonymous. The Boy Who Turned Yellow's writing credit is conflicted between "Emeric Pressburger" (Wikipedia infobox; Senses of Cinema) and "Richard Imrie" (Radio Times guide; implied by NFI); the on-screen credit was not established from the sources read. [C33579]

**Takeaway:** The pseudonym is the exile's last device: when the name no longer opens doors, take another one -- and the work dictated hours ahead of the camera is the same man who once sent short stories to newspapers for eighty marks.

## 29. The Boy Who Turned Yellow: the last film

The Boy Who Turned Yellow (1972, Children's Film Foundation, 55 minutes, slightly over 40,000 pounds, dir. Powell) is the last film collaboration of Powell and Pressburger and the last theatrical feature written by Pressburger or directed by Powell. John (Mark Dightam) loses a pet mouse, Alice, on a school trip to the Tower of London; on the Underground the train and everyone in it suddenly turn bright yellow; that evening he meets the eccentric yellow Nick (short for "Electronic", played by Robert Eddison), who has come out of the television; they return to the Tower to find Alice, are menaced by Yeoman Warders, John is threatened with execution; reunited with his pet, he wakes in class -- was it all a dream? Old colleagues return: cinematographer Christopher Challis and actor Esmond Knight. It won a "Chiffy" award from the Children's Film Foundation, voted for by CFF audiences (the NFI's "Best Children's Film of the Year in two consecutive years" may be the same award described differently; the two are not merged here without a source). "According to director Powell, it was a dream of Pressburger to make a film solely intended for children, and he both devised the story and wrote the screenplay." After 1972 there is no further screen credit in any filmography read. [C33580]

**Takeaway:** The last film is a children's dream about a boy, a mouse and a man who came out of the television -- the fantasy apparatus of the Archers reduced to its smallest, gentlest form, and the partnership ends not with a statement but with a question: was it all a dream?

## 30. The break-up and the Suffolk years

Powell and Pressburger "began to go their separate ways after the mid-1950s. They remained close friends but wanted to explore different things, having done about as much as they could together. Their last film as a partnership was Ill Met by Moonlight [1957]." The NFI adds the mechanics: the Archers as a company name had effectively ceased earlier -- The Small Back Room (1949) was "actually their last collaboration to use that company name" -- and "the real breaking point was Ill Met by Moonlight (1957) starring Dirk Bogarde as Powell kept disappearing for days, leaving his colleague in awkward situations." The BFI dates the working partnership's end to 1956 (the production year of Ill Met by Moonlight); the partnership's formal span is usually given as 1939-1957, with the two late reunions outside it. The Archers count is definitional and must always be stated with its definition: 14 joint-credit features (Faber, Archers-banner only); 15 sharing the triple credit (TCM, presumably the 14 plus the short The Volunteer, 1944); 19 (Wikipedia's filmography arithmetic -- 17 titles 1939-1957 plus the two reunions); 24 (Wikipedia's lead, all collaborations 1939-1972, including producer-only films and variant cuts). This study uses "nineteen" in that Wikipedia-filmography sense. [C33581]

From 1970 he lived in Aspall, Suffolk (Shoemaker's Cottage); he loved to cook and introduce English guests to Hungarian cuisine, "in his autumn years he organised a real Hungarian pig slaughter in his Suffolk home", and followed Arsenal to away games "as much as he could". In the late 1950s he "took to linguistic philosophy and the study of Ludwig Wittgenstein" -- the only intellectual pursuit of the later years named in the sources read. His personal life: married Agi Donath 24 June 1938, divorced 1941, childless; married Wendy Orme 29 March 1947; daughter Angela and a second child who died as a baby in 1948 (the NFI names her Sally-Sue, and says Wendy felt his "carelessness and the insistence on a business trip to the US contributed to the tragedy"); divorced in Reno, Nevada in 1953 and in Britain in 1971 -- both dates real, not a conflict. Angela's sons both became film-makers: Andrew Macdonald (producer, Trainspotting, Ex Machina) and Kevin Macdonald (Oscar-winning director, One Day in September; author of the biography Emeric Pressburger: The Life and Death of a Screenwriter, Faber, 1994). He died 5 February 1988 in a nursing home in nearby Saxtead, of bronchial pneumonia, and is buried in Aspall -- the only grave in that Church of England graveyard with a Star of David, the stone carrying a Walter Scott quotation also used in A Matter of Life and Death: "Love rules the court, the camp, the grove, / This world below and heaven above, / For love is heaven, and heaven is love." His own summary of his life: "[the] worst things that happened to me were the political consequences of events beyond my control ... the best things were exactly the same." [C33582]

**Takeaway:** The partnership ended because Powell kept disappearing and Pressburger was left holding the administration -- the same division of labour that made the films, carried past the point where it could hold them together.

## 31. Reputation: wilderness, rediscovery, restoration

Contemporary standing was hostile: the colour Archers films -- Colonel Blimp, A Matter of Life and Death, Black Narcissus, The Red Shoes, The Tales of Hoffmann -- "were discounted as the product of filmmakers of aberrant taste, outside the national concerns" of British realist cinema; Richard Winnington's November 1946 review of A Matter of Life and Death found it "even further away from the essential realism and the true business of the British movie than their two previous films". In the 1960s and 1970s, "the reviewers obsessed with auteur theory had no way to handle The Archers phenomenon as a collaboration of two equal individuals seemed to contradict the very essence of their concept of auteur." Even mounting the first NFT retrospective (c.1971) was difficult: "Powell was still unpopular... No one, it was feared, would come to watch their sentimental drivel, and critical interest was elsewhere." [C33583]

The rediscovery came in the 1970s, from critics like Ian Christie and filmmakers like Martin Scorsese, who "began to champion the work"; Scorsese first saw the films as a boy in 1950s New York on a small black-and-white TV, tracked Powell down in England in the 1970s, befriended him, and brought him to the US as senior director in residence at Coppola's American Zoetrope. The honours followed within Pressburger's lifetime, all joint: BFI Special Award 1978, BAFTA Fellowship 1981, BFI Fellowship 1983 -- but the 1982 Venice Career Golden Lion was Powell's alone (the NFI's joint attribution is not followed; Wikipedia's honours table and Encyclopedia.com both give it to Powell only). Posthumously: the Red Shoes restoration (UCLA Film and Television Archive with the BFI, funded by the Hollywood Foreign Press Association, the Film Foundation and the Louis B. Mayer Foundation; 579,000 individual frames scanned from the three-strip camera negatives; first screened at Cannes 2009), eight Archers films restored by Scorsese and Schoonmaker by 2024, the English Heritage blue plaque unveiled 17 February 2014 by Scorsese and Schoonmaker at Dorset House, Gloucester Place (the Archers' offices 1942-1947), the Faber reissues of The Glass Pearls (2015, 2022), and Made in England: The Films of Powell and Pressburger (dir. David Hinton, narrated by Scorsese; Berlinale 21 February 2024, UK release 10 May 2024). The BFI's verdict on the wilderness years now reads as the epitaph: "Such views, by no means uncommon at the time, look quaint today, when these films have established themselves at the very centre of British cinema history." Pressburger's specific share is the point the BFI insists on: "Pressburger was more than the scenarist whose work Powell rewrote" -- "the concentration on relationships, the dispirited desolation that the strongest characters often feel (in short the human face of their joint films) are entirely Pressburger's." [C33584]

**Takeaway:** Auteur theory could not process two authors, so it erased one; the restoration era put him back -- not as Powell's scriptwriter, but as the man whose stories, structures and human face the films are made of.

## Aesthetic logic: the writer as the other half, and fantasy as truth

Pressburger's aesthetic is the anti-rationalist one Christie names: "a deeply religious appreciation of existence that transcended immediate political events", "the primacy of the perceiver and the role of tradition in constructing a morally integrated world" -- and, in his own conduct, the belief that "the minutiae of living [are] an expression of moral integrity". The films are, in his phrase to Christie, "like diaries": not autobiography in the literal sense -- no film of his is about a Hungarian screenwriter -- but reworkings of personal experience, "sometimes aware... sometimes... involuntarily", in which "everybody has something in his own make-up which recurs without him being conscious of it." What recurs is the outsider learning a language, the love that is the wrong one or the lost one, the mother who cannot be saved, the enemy who turns out to be human, the plan that is dismantled by weather or a curse or a piece of paper. [C33585]

The logic of the work is the logic of the Manifesto's fourth point: "No artist believes in escapism. And we secretly believe that no audience does. We have proved, at any rate, that they will pay to see the truth, for other reasons than her nakedness." The fantasy apparatus -- the studio Himalayas, the heaven in dye-monochrome, the ballet that exists only on film, the opera without dialogue -- is not decoration on a realistic story; it is the truth told in the only form that can hold it, because the realistic form (a real mountain, a real heaven, a real ballet) would be a lie. That is why the films refuse location, refuse explanation, refuse the cut that would make the forty years literal: the artifice is the honesty. And the mechanism that delivers it is always a structure, never a speech: a journey with five stops, a document that changes hands, a double cast, an obstruction that is the plot, a courtroom where love is proved by stepping onto a staircase. The writer's job, as Pressburger understood it, was to build the machine so precisely that the director could shoot it and the audience could feel it without being told what it meant -- and then, when the partnership ended, to discover that the machine without the director was lifeless, and the director without the machine was without ambiguity. The films are the proof that neither half was enough, and that together they were. [C33586]
