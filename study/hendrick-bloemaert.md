# Hendrick Bloemaert: Study Notes

**Site:** Hendrick Bloemaert (1601 or 1602 - 30 December 1672), Dutch Golden Age painter and poet of Utrecht, eldest son of Abraham Bloemaert, the one pupil who stayed in his father's style while every other pupil left it for Rome and came home changed. He was born in Utrecht, the oldest of Abraham's four artist sons - Cornelis II (c. 1603-1692), Adriaen (after 1609 - buried 8 January 1666) and Frederick (c. 1614/17-1690) were his younger brothers - and he grew up inside his father's Utrecht workshop, receiving his primary training there from Abraham himself; no other teacher is named for him anywhere in the sources read. His earliest recorded work is a St John the Baptist of 1624, a family production in the literal sense: Hendrick copied the figure from an engraving his brother Cornelis had made after a painting by their father Abraham, and Glasgow Museums calls it his earliest recorded work. He is recorded as registered in Rome in 1626 (RKD, via Wikipedia) and was back in Utrecht by 1631, where in that year he registered in the Utrecht Guild of St. Luke and married Margaretha van der Eem, the daughter of a lawyer; Christie's gives his Italy sojourn as 1627-30, a one-year spread on the start date that is not resolved here, and one modern survey (NiceArtGallery, 2025) questions whether he travelled to Italy himself at all, saying the evidence is scarce and inconclusive - the trip is therefore presented in this study as recorded-but-disputed, not as settled fact (see C439). He then worked in Utrecht for the remaining four decades of his life, with no other residence documented and no move away recorded. His career runs through the three styles of his time - the lingering Mannerism of his father's studio, the Caravaggism he absorbed in Rome or from the returned Utrecht painters around him, and a late Classicist softening toward clearer light, balanced composition and cooler colour - and the dated work falls into four phases: family workshop to c. 1630, the dense Caravaggesque years 1631-38 (eleven of the eighteen catalogued works), mature portraits and civic religion c. 1638-57, and a late phase of small landscapes and last portraits 1657-72. He is considered an important member of the Utrecht school of Caravaggisti, known for his portraits and historical allegories, and he was also known for his poetry - though no poem, title or text of his poetry was found in any source read, and no verse style is described here. His posthumous reputation was fixed by Arnold Houbraken, who wrote in De groote schouburgh (1718) that he never became as good as his father; Wikipedia warns it is unclear whether Houbraken saw his work or copied the comment from Joachim von Sandrart, so the verdict is treated here as second-hand biographical tradition, not as a first-hand critical judgement (see C440). He died in Utrecht on 30 December 1672, aged 71 if born in 1601, about 70 if born in 1602, outliving his father by nearly 22 years. ## The life

Hendrick Bloemaert was born in Utrecht, the eldest son of the man who ran the most important teaching workshop in the city, and he never really left either the city or the workshop's orbit. His father Abraham had moved the family to Utrecht in 1576, when Cornelis Bloemaert I became city architect there; by Hendrick's youth Abraham's studio was the machine that made the Utrecht school, with thirty-three named pupils passing through it between 1590 and 1650. Hendrick grew up inside it, trained by his father alongside his three brothers, and his earliest work is a copy of a family image - the 1624 St John the Baptist, taken from an engraving his brother Cornelis had made after their father's painting. That is the pattern of the whole career: the same subjects, the same figures, the same workshop, reworked by the son's hand.

Rome is the one move, and even it is uncertain. The archival record says he was registered in Rome in 1626; by 1631 he was back in Utrecht, a guild member and a married man. No detail of what he did in Rome survives - no itinerary, no patron, no Bentvueghels membership (his brother Cornelis is the one documented as a Bentvueghels member, bent-name Winter), no Roman contact named. What is certain is what he came home to: a Utrecht full of painters who had been to Rome and brought Caravaggio home - Honthorst back in 1620, ter Brugghen and Bijlert around the same years - and a Catholic city that still wanted saints and altarpieces. Whether he learned Caravaggism in Rome or from his father's returning pupils, the 1630s work shows it: hard single light, half-length figures pushed close to the picture plane, wrinkled skin and coarse fabric rendered with convincing detail.

The 1630s are the dense decade - Winter in 1631, Old Woman Selling Eggs in 1632, Granida and Daifilo and Paul before Agrippa in 1634, Vertumnus and Pomona in 1635 and again in 1638 - and then the datable record thins. After a small number of genre subjects in the early 1630s he specialised, in Christie's summary, in portraits and religious subjects, for which he is now best known: a male portrait in Brunswick dated 1641, a Maria van Pallaes charity picture in 1657, and, if the single aggregator report holds, a very large full-length portrait of Hendrik Jacob van Tuyl van Serooskerken in 1670, two years before his death. From the 1640s his style softened toward Classicism - clearer, more diffused light, more balanced compositions, cooler and brighter colour, smoother paint - under French Classicism and the Bolognese school, though he never completely abandoned the lessons of Caravaggism. The latest securely dated work is a small one: a landscape with a farmhouse and animals, 27 x 30 cm, inscribed on the reverse "HB fecit / ANO 1663", a quiet, observational panel with no history subject at all, painted when he was about sixty-one. He died in Utrecht on 30 December 1672. No named patron, no child and no pupil of his own is documented in the sources consulted; the poetry he was known for survives as a reputation, not as a text.

**Takeaway:** a career with one move and one inheritance - the son who went to Rome and came home to paint his father's subjects in the new light, and the only pupil who never left his father's style.

## M1. Moderated chiaroscuro - directed light that models, not light that slashes

He adopted the strong contrasts of light and shadow of Caravaggism, using directed light to model forms, create volume and enhance drama, but his lighting is repeatedly described as softer than the starker tenebrism of Honthorst or ter Brugghen, with smoother transitions. Watchable behaviour: a face or a hand emerges out of a dark ground, fully modelled and readable, while the surrounding dark stays warm and continuous instead of going dead black; the brightest passage sits on the narrative centre and the light falls off gradually rather than cutting the picture in two.

**Takeaway:** the light is a modeller before it is a dramatist; Hendrick's dark is never empty.

## M2. Figures emerging from a dark ground

Figures often emerge from dark backgrounds, their features and textures highlighted by a focused illumination. Watchable behaviour: the edge of a shoulder, a cheek, a cuff dissolves into the dark at the picture's edge while the lit side holds all the information; the viewer reads the figure first and the setting second, or not at all. This is the Utrecht Caravaggisti half-length formula (see M6) used as a figure-ground device.

**Takeaway:** the ground is not a background, it is a curtain the figure steps out of.

## M3. A richer, more varied palette than the earthy Caravaggisti

His colour palette often remained richer and more varied than the predominantly earthy tones favoured by some Caravaggisti, and he retains something of his father's Mannerist palette, described as vibrant and sometimes acidic. Watchable behaviour: inside a dark, Caravaggesque picture, separate hues stay separately legible - a red, a blue, a warm flesh, a green - instead of collapsing into brown monochrome; the picture reads as coloured, not just lit. (Parent correction, see C438: leg 1's evidence sentence for this mechanism cited a Moses Striking the Rock as Hendrick's; the Met Moses is Abraham's of 1596, so the Moses clause is removed here and the mechanism rests on the general palette claim and the father's inheritance.)

**Takeaway:** colour survives the dark; the picture is painted, not just lit.

## M4. Mannerist inheritance - figura serpentinata and intricate composition

From his father's studio he kept a taste for sophisticated, often intricate compositions and figures in elegant, sometimes contorted poses (the figura serpentinata), with a bright, decorative colour palette. Watchable behaviour: a body twists so that head, torso and limbs face in different directions, making a single figure feel like a spiral; a composition packs several turning bodies into a shallow space and still stays legible because each twist is a separate accent.

**Takeaway:** the twist is the family grammar; Hendrick keeps it and calms it.

## M5. Naturalistic texture - wrinkled skin, coarse fabric, expressive physiognomy

He "depicted wrinkled skin, coarse fabrics, and expressive physiognomies with convincing detail, bringing sacred and historical figures to life in a relatable, human manner". Watchable behaviour: the eye is invited to linger on surface - the crease of an old face, the weave of a sleeve, the roughness of a hand - and that surface detail is what makes a biblical or mythological figure feel like a real Utrecht person rather than an ideal type.

**Takeaway:** texture is the humanising device; the saint is a neighbour before he is a saint.

## M6. The close-cropped half-length single figure

The Utrecht Caravaggisti formula he used: "half-length figures of musicians, drinkers, or fortune tellers pushed close to the picture plane". His genre work is single figures of this kind - musicians playing lutes or violins, drinkers, character types - often combined with allegorical intent. Watchable behaviour: a single figure fills the frame to the shoulders or waist, close enough that the viewer is in the same room; there is no distance to retreat into, so expression, instrument and costume do all the work.

**Takeaway:** closeness is the composition; the frame is a room, not a window.

## M7. Restrained multi-figure composition - still, frontal, classically stable

Where Honthorst and ter Brugghen make Winter busy and exaggerated, Hendrick makes it still, frontal and sombre: An Allegory of Winter (1631) seats a finely robed elderly gentleman at a carpet-draped table, served with wine and fat bread, attended by a female servant, holding the viewer's gaze frontally, arrested mid-movement, while strong horizontal and vertical accents (chair arm, table edge, the upright figure) make the composition almost classical in stability. Watchable behaviour: a multi-figure scene that could be chaos resolves into a still arrangement around one central action; the viewer reads the picture figure by figure, each face giving a different emotional caption to the same event, and the stillness itself is the drama. (Parent correction, see C438: leg 1 grounded this mechanism on a Moses Striking the Rock described as Hendrick's; the Met Moses is Abraham's of 1596, so the mechanism is re-grounded here on Christie's account of Winter, the verified Hendrick picture.)

**Takeaway:** restraint is the signature; where his contemporaries hurry, Hendrick stops the picture.

## M8. Light as narrative focus

In his religious narratives generally, "the lighting in such works typically serves to focus attention on the main protagonists and the miraculous event". Watchable behaviour: the light behaves like a pointer - it lands on the central figure and the action and lets everything else sink, so the viewer looks where the light looks. (Parent correction, see C438: leg 1's evidence sentence for this mechanism described the lighting of a Moses Striking the Rock as Hendrick's; the Met Moses is Abraham's of 1596, so the Moses sentence is removed here and the mechanism rests on the general claim about his religious narratives.)

**Takeaway:** the light is a finger pointing at the story.

## M9. Emotion made legible through gesture and expression

His single figures and small groups carry their feeling in faces and hands: Democritus laughs, the Winter gentleman holds the viewer's gaze arrested mid-movement, the servant stands obediently poised. More generally, his religious scenes capture "the emotional distress of the figures through gesture and expression". Watchable behaviour: two or three faces in one picture tell different parts of the story - sternness, grief, confusion, laughter - and the hands (a gesture, a hand to a face, a grip on a staff) do as much narrative work as the faces. (Parent correction, see C438: leg 1 grounded this mechanism on an Expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael described as Hendrick's, with Abraham's stern gesture, Hagar's tearful face and Ishmael's bewildered look; the Getty Expulsion is Abraham's of 1638, so the Hagar example is removed here and the mechanism is re-grounded on Democritus and Winter, verified Hendrick pictures.)

**Takeaway:** the face is the caption and the hand is the verb.

## M10. Text-faithful detail accumulation - the Ovidian checklist

In Vertumnus and Pomona he "honoured the Ovidian text with lively representation of details mentioned in the story": the abundance of fruit at Pomona's feet, her pruning-hook balanced between the fingers of her left hand, the perforated jug used to water her plants, the staff and veiled head of the approaching old woman (Vertumnus in disguise), and behind Pomona the fence with which, as Ovid wrote, she "fenced her orchard and stayed inside it to shun and forestall all contact with men". Watchable behaviour: the picture rewards slow looking - each small object is a footnote to the text, and the fence itself tells the plot: it is "now lopsided and split, leaving an opening for the impending arrival of Vertumnus and foreshadowing Pomona's eventual surrender". A single prop, the broken fence, carries the ending.

**Takeaway:** the detail is the plot; the broken fence is the whole story in one object.

## M11. Allegory as single-figure character study

His allegorical figures, "representing concepts like the Five Senses or philosophical ideas (e.g., his depictions of Heraclitus and Democritus), were also part of his repertoire, often presented as compelling character studies". Watchable behaviour: an abstraction (a sense, a philosophy, a season) is not painted as a symbol with a label but as one person with a face, a posture and a mood - Heraclitus weeping, Democritus laughing - so the viewer meets the idea as a personality. Note: the Heraclitus / Democritus pairing is a standard philosophical contrast; the mechanism is the reduction of allegory to portrait-like character. Only the Democritus is verified as a Hendrick picture in this study (W11); no separate Hendrick Heraclitus was verified.

**Takeaway:** an idea becomes a person; the allegory has a face.

## M12. Portraiture - three-quarter view, dark ground, material realism

His portraits "typically present the sitter in a three-quarter view, often against a neutral dark background, focusing attention on the face and costume. The lighting models the features clearly, revealing character through subtle expression and gaze. Attention is paid to rendering the textures of clothing, lace collars, and hair". Watchable behaviour: the sitter turns slightly out of full frontal address, the dark ground removes all context, and the picture becomes a study of two things - a face thinking, and cloth rendered so exactly you can tell lace from linen from skin. The large late portrait of Hendrik Jacob van Tuyl van Serooskerken (1670, reported 200 x 125 cm, see W18) shows the formula scaled up to full length, if the single-source report holds.

**Takeaway:** the portrait is two studies at once - a mind and a material.

## M13. The Classicist softening - late clarity, balance and cool colour

From the 1640s onward his style "evolved towards greater elegance and calmness. The dramatic chiaroscuro often softened into a clearer, more diffused light. Compositions became more balanced and stable, sometimes incorporating classical architectural elements. Figures might appear more idealized, their gestures and expressions more restrained. His colour palette could become brighter and cooler, with smoother paint application, moving away from the vigorous brushwork sometimes seen in earlier Dutch painting." Watchable behaviour: compare an early and a late picture and the change is visible as a change in weather - hard spotlight becomes even daylight, a crowded diagonal becomes a stable, centred arrangement, hot colour cools, and the paint surface calms.

**Takeaway:** the late style is a change of weather, not a decline.

## M14. Family-workshop reuse - copy, rework, reprise

His practice reused family material openly: the 1624 St John the Baptist copies a figure from an engraving by his brother Cornelis after a painting by his father Abraham; his Vertumnus and Pomona pictures rework his father's famous composition (popularised by Jan Saenredam's engraving of 1605) and then reprise his own 1635 and 1638 designs, with the later versions moving Vertumnus to the middle distance, dropping the wide orchard, and recasting Pomona as an Arcadian shepherdess rather than a Mannerist nymph. Watchable behaviour: the same subject returns across decades with the same bones and different flesh - the viewer who knows the earlier version sees what was kept, what was cut, and what was moved, and the change of Pomona's type (nymph to shepherdess) is itself the stylistic argument.

**Takeaway:** the same picture, painted again, is the autobiography; what changes between versions is the career.

## M15. The quiet late landscape - measured, observational, small

The 1663 landscape (27 x 30 cm, oil on panel) is described as "quiet atmosphere, measured execution": a modest rural setting, farm buildings at the centre partly veiled by mature trees, a horse and two sheep grazing in a shaded foreground, a woman pausing beside a well as a discreet narrative moment; palette of "soft greens, warm earth tones and a pale, gently modulated sky"; architecture "with crisp, economical precision" and foliage "with steady, even brushwork"; the scene "carefully structured, guiding the viewer through the clearing toward the farmhouse beyond". Watchable behaviour: nothing dramatic happens; the picture works by slow guidance - eye from shaded foreground animals, through the clearing, to the farmhouse - and by evenness of handling, where no passage shouts. This is the late, Classicist calm of M13 applied to landscape, and a lesser-known facet of his work. Note: this is a dealer's description of a single attributed panel; treat the mechanism as evidenced for this work, not as a claim about a large landscape oeuvre.

**Takeaway:** the last Hendrick is the quietest one; the picture guides, it does not shout.

This is the dense phase: eleven of the eighteen catalogue entries fall here, and it is where Hendrick's identity forms. He is back from Rome (registered there 1626, back in Utrecht by 1631, Guild of St Luke 1631) and the work shows it: half-length single figures lit hard from one side (Winter, Democritus, Saint Paul, St Jerome), genre subjects with a moral edge (Old Woman Selling Eggs), and mythological-literary set pieces taken from the Utrecht repertory (Granida and Daifilo, Vertumnus and Pomona, Diana and Callisto). The change from Phase 1 is one of authorship and temperature: the same family subjects, but now signed, dated, and restrained. Christie's contrast is the key critical observation - where Honthorst and ter Brugghen make Winter busy and exaggerated, Hendrick makes it still, frontal, and sombre, with a classical horizontal-vertical armature. He is also already repeating himself productively: Vertumnus and Pomona in 1635, again in 1638, and again in a later reprise - a motif he will return to across his career rather than a one-off.

The 1663 landscape is the clearest evidence that late Hendrick could work small, quiet, and observational, a long way from the hard-lit saints of the 1630s.

## Evolution

What he kept from Abraham: the subject repertory is his father's almost entire - Vertumnus and Pomona, Diana and Callisto, Granida and Daifilo, St Jerome, St John the Baptist, Moses Striking the Rock (as a family theme), the Seasons, the single-figure saint, the pastoral. He kept the workshop method too: copying a family engraving for his earliest work (W1), drawing on his father's head-study sheets for faces (Christie's on Winter), and reworking his father's compositions across decades (Vertumnus in 1635, 1638, and later). He also kept Utrecht's Catholic legibility - saints holding readable attributes, subjects a clandestine Catholic audience would understand at a glance.

What Rome and Caravaggism changed: the light and the figure. The early-1630s works are built on a single hard light source, half-length framing, and a frontal address to the viewer that Abraham's Mannerist multi-figure scenes do not have. The figure types change with it: wrinkled skin, coarse fabric, expressive physiognomy rendered with convincing detail, and a move from Abraham's elegant, elongated bodies toward stockier, more present bodies. Rome also gave him the confidence to sign and date, which is why Phase 2 is datable and Phase 1 is not.

What stayed constant: restraint. Across all four phases, the critical consensus in the sources is that Hendrick is the quiet Bloemaert. Christie's says it outright for Winter: restrained and sombre where his contemporaries are urgent and momentary, classical in stability, static figures arrested mid-movement. Houbraken's old verdict - that he never became as good as his father - is repeated by Wikipedia with the caveat that it is unclear whether Houbraken saw his work or copied Sandrart; the works themselves suggest a different story, of a painter who chose a narrower, quieter register and stayed in it for forty years. The through-line is a frontal, half-length, single-light figure, whether saint, philosopher, shepherdess, or sitter, with the story told by attributes and expression rather than by crowd and action.

## Aesthetic logic

Hendrick's pictures are built on a single tension: the father's Mannerist language against the Roman light. Everything he paints is a negotiation between the two. The early work takes the father's subjects, figures and compositions and lights them the Roman way; the mature work keeps the Roman light and calms it into Classicist balance; the late work drops the subjects altogether and keeps only the light, the guidance and the quiet. The through-line is restraint as a positive value, not as a failure of ambition: where Honthorst and ter Brugghen make Winter busy and exaggerated, Hendrick makes it still; where Abraham crowds a frieze with twisting bodies, Hendrick puts one figure close to the picture plane and lets the face and the hands do the work. The palette follows the same negotiation - richer and more varied than the earthy Caravaggisti, retaining something of the father's bright, decorative colour, but never returning to the father's acidic Mannerist temperature. The workshop is the other logic: pictures are made by copying, reworking and reprising family material, so a subject is never finished, only revisited - Vertumnus in 1635, in 1638, and again later, each time with the same bones and different flesh. The late landscape is the endpoint of that logic: no history subject at all, just a clearing, a farmhouse, animals and a woman at a well, the eye guided slowly from shade to house. The reputation that dogged him - Houbraken's "never as good as his father" - mistakes the narrowness for a shortcoming; the work argues the opposite, that he chose the narrow register and stayed in it for forty years, and the staying is the achievement. He is the son who stayed: in Utrecht, in his father's style, in the quiet.

## Positions

P1. Hendrick's career is a passage through the three styles of his time: "moving from the lingering elegance of Mannerism through the dramatic intensity of Caravaggism to the balanced harmony of Classicism." - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P2. He forged his own path inside a formidable inheritance: as the eldest son of Abraham Bloemaert he "inherited a formidable artistic legacy, yet he forged his own path". - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P3. His Caravaggism was moderated, not imitative: his lighting "could be softer, the transitions smoother, and his colour palette often remained richer and more varied than the predominantly earthy tones favoured by some Caravaggisti". - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P4. The blend has a name in the literature surveyed: a characteristic "Bloemaert effect" - "a blend of Caravaggesque drama with a certain refinement and attention to colouristic harmony". - NiceArtGallery (read end to end), presented there as what "some have described", not as a settled term.

P5. His naturalism is a humanising device: he brought "sacred and historical figures to life in a relatable, human manner" by depicting "wrinkled skin, coarse fabrics, and expressive physiognomies with convincing detail". - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P6. His late work is a deliberate softening, not a decline: "While he never completely abandoned the lessons learned from Caravaggism, his style evolved towards greater elegance and calmness." - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P7. His standing is that of a respected Utrecht master rather than an international star: "Hendrick Bloemaert was a respected and successful master in his own right", even though Rembrandt, Hals and Vermeer "might command greater international fame today". - NiceArtGallery (read end to end).

P8. The father's shadow distorted his reception: "Although later biographers - Houbraken among them - claimed he never equalled his father, Hendrick's surviving work reveals an artist of distinct temperament, attentive to structure, atmosphere and the quiet drama of daily life." - Mayfair Gallery via InCollect (read end to end).

P9. He is "considered an important member of the Utrecht school of Caravaggisti, and was known for his portraits and historical allegories. He was also known for his poetry." - Wikipedia (read end to end). This is the standard one-sentence summary of his reputation; the study file quotes it as the consensus line.

P10. Utrecht's Catholic character explains why his work looks Italian: "Utrecht was also unusual in the 17th century Netherlands in remaining predominantly Catholic. These facts explain why this painting looks more like a work painted in Italy than something produced by a Dutch artist." - Glasgow Museums (read end to end), on the St John the Baptist.

P11. His allegories are moral and temporal in theme: "His allegorical paintings often explored themes of morality, virtue, and the passage of time." - Wikioo summary (SNIPPET ONLY - not read end to end; treat as a secondary summary, not a verified scholarly claim).

P12. His portraiture is psychological, not merely likeness: "He was particularly skilled at portraiture, capturing the likenesses of prominent citizens with a keen eye for detail and psychological insight." - Wikioo summary (SNIPPET ONLY - not read end to end).

## Corrections

- C437. The Lot and His Daughters error in the #531 doorway (D7). The #531 top pick named "(Lot and His Daughters, rediscovered 2006)" as a Hendrick work. The parent verified first-hand, from the National Gallery press release, that the picture was sold in 2004 attributed to Abraham Bloemaert's son Hendrick, and that Abraham Bloemaert's signature and date were discovered during the painting's restoration in 2004, identifying it as Abraham's work (NG6701, 165.5 x 228.5 cm, bought 2023). The study does not list it under Hendrick; it appears only in the separate list and the disputes section as the cautionary tale of the father-son attribution tangle. The NiceArtGallery survey used by leg 1 illustrates its Hendrick article with a detail of the same Abraham picture, which is how the error propagates; the survey is not relied on for any catalogue entry.
- C438. The Moses and Hagar misattributions in leg 1's mechanisms. Leg 1 grounded M7 (crowded but legible multi-figure composition) and M8 (light as narrative focus) on a Moses Striking the Rock described as Hendrick's, M9 (emotion through gesture and expression) on an Expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael described as Hendrick's, and M3's palette claim on the same Moses - all from the NiceArtGallery survey. Leg 2 proved both pictures are Abraham's: the Met Moses Striking the Rock (1972.171) is Abraham's of 1596, and the Getty Expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael (69.PA.16) is Abraham's of 1638. The parent re-grounded the mechanisms on verified Hendrick works: M7 on Christie's account of An Allegory of Winter (1631), M8 on the general claim about his religious narratives with the Moses sentence removed, M9 on Democritus (W11) and Winter (W2), and M3 with the Moses clause removed. The mechanisms' headings survive; their evidence does not repeat the error.
- C439. The Rome trip, stated as recorded-but-disputed. The archival record (RKD, via Wikipedia) says Hendrick was registered in Rome in 1626 and back in Utrecht by 1631; Christie's gives the Italy sojourn as 1627-30. The NiceArtGallery survey (2025) says "While it's debated whether Hendrick travelled to Italy himself (evidence is scarce and inconclusive), he was certainly exposed to the style directly through the works of the returned Utrecht painters and through his father's interpretations." Glasgow Museums sides with the trip. The study presents the registration as documented and the trip itself as disputed, and invents no Roman itinerary, Bentvueghels membership, or Roman patron. The doorway's D4 is recorded as a HOLD on the registration claim, with this dispute carried inside it.
- C440. The Houbraken verdict, "via Sandrart". The #531 top pick said Houbraken (via Sandrart) says Hendrick never became as good as his father. Wikipedia's caveat is that "it is unclear whether Houbraken saw his work or copied this comment from Joachim von Sandrart". The "via Sandrart" origin is probable, not proven; the doorway's D10 is recorded as a HOLD in substance with the caveat carried in the letter.
- C441. Birth year carried as a range. Sources give 1601 (Wikipedia infobox; Wikioo text), 1602 (Wikioo headings and fact boxes; Wikipedia Bloemaert disambiguation page), and 1601 or 1602 / c. 1601/1602 (Wikipedia lead; NiceArtGallery; InCollect; Getty ULAN "ca. 1601-1672"). The study uses "1601 or 1602" throughout, and the age at death as 71 or about 70. Abraham's birth year is given as 1566, the majority and museum figure (Leiden Collection endnote 1: long assumed 1564, Ruurs argues convincingly for 1566); the 1564 in one Sotheby's lot note and in the Getty ULAN's IMJ record is not used.
- C442. The doorway tally and the D7 rewording. This is the one-hundred-and-fifty-third doorway check; the check is recorded as a HOLD on the substance (the one-hundred-and-sixth hold) and a FAIL on the letter (the twenty-ninth non-hold), and the streak that began at #420 now stands at one hundred and six holds and twenty-nine non-holds. The hold tally counts only since #420, which is why 106 + 29 does not sum to 153. The letter fails on D7 alone: the Lot and His Daughters parenthetical attributes Abraham's picture to Hendrick.
- C443. Jan Both promoted from a carried spare in #529. Leg 3 proposed Jan Both as top pick for #533 and also as main 1 of "New from #532". Jan Both was already carried as a spare in the #529 block ("Italianate landscapist, pupil per Sandrart. Held back (carried from #528)"). He is therefore promoted, not freshly proposed, following the Isaac Oliver precedent (#524, promoted from a carried alternate in #522). The "New from #532" line follows the #531 convention: main 1 is the top pick above, with mains 2, 3 and 4 as the new mains. The #529 carried block is spliced verbatim and still lists Jan Both as held back; the promotion is recorded here, not by editing the carried block.
- C444. Guild offices. Leg 3 reported, from the Cyclopedia of Painters and Paintings, that Hendrick was Guild of St. Luke master about 1630-32 and repeatedly its dean, last mentioned in 1664. That is a 19th-century secondary compilation; only the 1631 registration is firm (Wikipedia/RKD). The study records the dean claim as flagged, not as fact (see D14).
- C445. The Serooskerken portrait (W18). Leg 2 catalogued the Portrait of Hendrik Jacob van Tuyl van Serooskerken (1670, reported 200 x 125 cm, Zuylen Castle) on the sole authority of artsdot. The study keeps the entry with every fact flagged as needing museum confirmation, and the dimensions tally counts it as reported-but-unverified, not verified.
- C446. Leg 1's twelfth quote excluded. Leg 1 supplied a Q12, a National Gallery curator's remark that "Bloemaert was among the most influential artists of the Dutch Golden Age, but his work was not represented at the National Gallery" - it concerns Abraham Bloemaert and the Lot and His Daughters acquisition, not Hendrick. The study's Quotes section holds eleven quotes; Q12 is recorded here, not reproduced as a Hendrick quote.
