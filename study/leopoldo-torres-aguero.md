# Leopoldo Torres Aguero: Study Notes

**Site:** Leopoldo Torres Aguero, 1924 - 31 December 1995 -- Argentine geometric painter, born in Argentina (La Rioja or Buenos Aires, the sources disagree; see Corrections), died in Paris as Argentina's ambassador to UNESCO; a painter first, not a machine builder, whose mature work is "sensitive geometry" -- concentric configurations, the circle inscribed within a square, axial symmetry, and colour vibration through systematic colour scales (C8201). He co-founded Groupe Position in Paris in April 1971 with Hugo Demarco, Armando Durante, Horacio Garcia Rossi and Antonio Asis, and five years earlier he had curated Julio Le Parc's one-man show at the 33rd Venice Biennale of 1966, the show at which Le Parc won the Grand Prize for Painting (C8211). His kinetic work is a single late episode: four silkscreen prints of 1979, static-optical, with no motor and no moving part attested (C8252, C8253).

## The life: the painter who gathered five Argentines

Leopoldo Torres Aguero was born in 1924 and died in Paris on 31 December 1995 (C8202, C8205). Where he was born, and on which day, the sources cannot agree, and this study does not pick one: Spanish Wikipedia says La Rioja, 12 February 1924 (C8202); SEPA says La Rioja, 14 February 1924, descendant of a family from La Rioja (C8205); Sigvaldason, Artsper and the Ville de Braine all say Buenos Aires, 1924 (C8203, C8208, C8209). Arte al Dia supports a La Rioja upbringing -- an initial formative stage in the province, then settlement in Buenos Aires in 1941 -- without settling the birthplace (C8201). He was known as "Leo", the name used by the trumpet player Gustavo Bergalli and by Julio Cortazar, who called him "Leo Aguero" (C8201, C8205). He won a prize at the National Salon, and after that, in 1949, decided to travel to Paris (C8201).

In 1949 he travelled to Paris and lived at the Maison Argentine de la Cite Universitaire, where he befriended Candido Portinari (C8201). Spanish Wikipedia calls him a disciple of Portinari (C8202). The contact reinforced his interest in monumental art, which he put into practice in shopping malls designed by the Aslan and Ezcurra Studio in Buenos Aires, and later in buildings housing banks and theatres, and in the parish church in Olivos (C8201). He later experimented in monumental works with marble, stainless steel, cloth and jute (C8201). He stayed in Paris about two years and returned to Buenos Aires in 1952, becoming a professor at the Academy of Fine Arts and joining the group formed by Raul Soldi, Batlle Planas and Osvaldo Svanascini (C8202, C8205). In 1953 he painted the mural "Cristo en el Monte de los Olivos" in the Catholic church of Olivos (C8202, C8205).

Towards the mid-1950s the students at the Buenos Aires fine-arts schools demanded updated study programmes, and on 3 October 1955 a Students' Movement occupied the three art schools; Torres Aguero was among the young artists the students asked to teach, and was one of their favourite teachers (C8201). In 1955, with Raul Soldi, Batlle Planas and others, he drew up a project to reform the Visual Arts study plan, incorporating the innovations of the Bauhaus (of Ulm, Germany) -- the Bauhaus-Ulm framing is SEPA's alone (C8205). Arte al Dia describes him as an exemplary draftsman whose teaching aimed to make each student sensitive to the different qualities of line and to liberate line (C8201). He kept an atelier in the "Ghosts' House", an old mansion in the Belgrano neighbourhood, where musicians and painters took part in jam sessions he organised weekly, and Bergalli remembers him as "Leo" (C8201). He was one of Georges Mathieu's hosts when, in November 1959, the French artist stretched out his canvas on the floor of the courtyard of the Manuel Belgrano School of Fine Arts during his visit to Buenos Aires (C8201). A former pupil, Gabriela Aberastury, met him in 1958, aged fifteen, and remembers his love of colour and form and sensual pleasure in line (C8206).

Japan is the hinge of his career, and its date is in conflict. Spanish Wikipedia says he lived in Japan between 1959 and 1962, a stay that influenced his art towards abstraction and then Op Art (C8202). SEPA says 1959-61, advancing his studies of Oriental philosophy, calligraphy and ceramics (C8205). Arte al Dia is the most specific: in April 1960 he travelled to Kyoto, where he perfected his natural inclination towards the spontaneous handling of line, of colour gradations and the highlighting of light and shadows, and before his return he presented an exhibition in Tokyo, where Japanese critics acknowledged that he had succeeded in making the imprint of Sumi-e visible in the colour subtleties of his style (C8201). The New Straits Times says he studied sumi-e in Kyoto (C8212). Against all of these, the Sigvaldason / Artsper / Braine dealer text says "long stays in Paris (1950) and Japan (1952)" (C8203, C8208, C8209) -- a date contradicted by four more detailed sources and incompatible with the 1953 mural in Buenos Aires, so this study does not use 1952 for Japan (C8354). What the influence was is agreed: it would be impossible to understand his mature painting without the influence of the mandala in Oriental culture, the instinctive strength of its calligraphic stroke, and the notion of diversity in unity (C8201); the vertical and horizontal forces of yin-yang symbolism, Heaven and Earth, were potentiated by the Eastern experience (C8201); the Braine text puts it as horizontal forms, the earth, against vertical forms, the sky, with coloured lines, through which one accesses light as a path in the manner of the Tao (C8209).

After Japan he returned to Paris and gradually synthesised these experiences (C8201). After a stage of symbolism he began abstract works in which he capitalised on the spontaneous handling of liquid matter: sometimes threads of dripping colour wove thin meshes whose effect lies in their transparency; in other cases monochrome or multicoloured lines generated dense wefts which appeal to the tactile through thickness and texture (C8201). Towards the end of the 1960s, mastery of dripping gave way to compositions structured in geometric spaces in which straight lines prevailed (C8201). But his straight lines were never hard-edged: rather than drawing straight lines in the strict sense, he proposed straight lines that admitted different thicknesses of soft edges, obtained through the dripping of a liquid paint that glided on the canvas following the law of gravity (C8201). His concentric configurations, the recurrence of the circle inscribed within a square, and the balance and power of axial symmetry began to appear in Paris (C8201). SEPA summarises the arc in three stages -- figurative representations first, then lyrical abstractions, and as a synthesis, sensitive geometric forms (C8205); Arte-Online calls the third and last stage "geometria sensible", sensitive geometry (C8207). In the production of his last two decades his paintings were based on a space built through successive layers that thrust the composition forward or make it recede, screens through which the viewer's gaze penetrates in depth (C8201). The optical play in his painting rests on colour vibrations; the palette is often handled through subtle but systematic colour scales, calibrated by a connoisseur of the methodical practice that any improvisation paradoxically requires, and occasionally a strong colour emphasis disrupts the serenity of the surface (C8201). Even before his death in 1995 he was experimenting with textures for the backgrounds of large-scale compositions (C8201).

The strongest evidenced one-to-one interaction in his whole network is a curatorial one, and it was not in the #630 brief. Julian Gallego, writing in 1977 in the Julio Le Parc Critical Anthology, says the Republic of Argentina organised a one-man show honouring Julio Le Parc at the 33rd Venice Biennale in 1966, "the curator of which was my great friend Leopoldo Torres Aguero", and that Le Parc was awarded the Grand Prize for Painting at that Biennale (C8211). The curatorship is single-source (Gallego, 1977) and must not be expanded into a general Venice career, but on the evidence read it is the one dated event in which Torres Aguero stands behind the single most visible Argentine kinetic success of the decade, five years before Groupe Position (C8351).

Groupe Position was created in April 1971 and dissolved at the end of 1972 (C8201). Arte al Dia gives the fullest account: his interest in the vibration of colour and the clash of lights and shadows promoted the foundation of the Groupe Position which, in the framework of the developments of Kinetic Art and the experiences involving Op Art, connected him to Hugo Demarco, Armando Durante, Horacio Garcia Rossi and Antonio Asis; the artists managed to show their works in Brussels, Paris, Brescia, Madrid, Seville, Bilbao, Bergamo and Zurich (C8201). The creation month, the dissolution date and the eight-city list are single-source (Arte al Dia only); Sicardi confirms only the year 1971, the co-founding and part of the membership (C8210). The group was short-lived, about twenty months, but genuinely exhibition-active, not a paper grouping, and study #630 adds a show at the Gallery Les Ambassadeurs in Zurich in 1972 (C8309). The roster naming is handled in the Corrections section: this study prints Leopoldo Torres Aguero, not "Carlos Aguero" (C8351). Torres Aguero was not in GRAV -- GRAV's six-name core was Garcia Rossi, Le Parc, Morellet, Sobrino, Stein and Yvaral, and the group agreed to dissolve in November 1968, more than two years before Position was created (C8312, C8307). His GRAV connection is indirect twice over: through Garcia Rossi, a GRAV member and Position co-founder, and through Le Parc, a GRAV founder whose 1966 Venice show he curated (C8312, C8307).

In 1983 he returned to Buenos Aires (C8205, single-source for the date). He received the Premio Palanza in 1989 and the Order of Arts and Letters of the French government (C8202, C8206, C8205). In 1989 President Carlos Saul Menem named him Minister Plenipotentiary in charge of cultural affairs at the Embassy of France, and then Argentine ambassador to UNESCO, based in Paris (C8205, single-source for the Minister Plenipotentiary step). Spanish Wikipedia gives the UNESCO term as Permanent Delegate of Argentina to UNESCO, 1994 - 31 December 1995, under President Menem, predecessor Jorge Asis, successor Carlos Alberto Floria (C8202, single-source for the predecessor and successor). Arte-Online agrees he died in Paris in December 1995 while serving as Argentine ambassador to UNESCO (C8206, C8207). What is not verified: the exact start month in 1994, the formal title (Permanent Delegate versus Ambassador -- both wordings appear), and any UNESCO activity, speech or programme during his term (C8202, C8205). He was married to the French sculptor Monique Rozanes, with whom he shared his life and workshops between France and Argentina (C8202, C8203). Spanish Wikipedia alone adds earlier marriages, to Odile Begue Baron Supervielle and to Katia Schekhter, with two children each (C8202).

His works are listed as held in museums in Argentina, Bolivia, Cuba, the United States (including Dallas), the Georges Pompidou Museum, Dunkirk, Saint Omer, Roquebrune and Soissons (C8203, C8208); SEPA adds the Museo de Potosi, the Museo de La Habana, the Museo Nacional de Arte Moderno and the Museo de la Ville in Paris, and a list of Argentine museums including the Museo Genaro Perez of Cordoba, the Museo Inca Huasi of La Rioja and the Museo Eduardo Sivori (C8205). No source read gives a work-by-work museum list, and no Pompidou collection page for a named Torres Aguero work was found, so the Pompidou holding is dealer-list only (C8213). The one verified museum holding in this study is the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, which holds the two 1955 lithographs W02 and W03 in storage, inventory GE-0405 and GE-0406 (C8255, C8256); SURDOC is the Chilean heritage register, so the record names the institution but not the city branch, and this study does not guess which one.

No award is recorded for Torres Aguero in the sources read except the Premio Palanza 1989 and the French Chevalier des Arts et des Lettres, both corroborated (C8202, C8206, C8205). SEPA alone adds a 1980 Grand Prize for Sculpture at the Alexandria International Biennial and a 1988 First Prize at the Salon Nacional de Artes Plasticas, Buenos Aires (C8205); carry them as single-source, not as settled fact.

## M1 -- The circle in the square: concentric configurations, axial symmetry

The core visual motif, sourced to Arte al Dia: concentric configurations, the recurrence of the circle inscribed within a square, and the balance and power of axial symmetry (C8201). The circle sits inside the square and the square holds the circle; the symmetry is axial, a vertical and a horizontal axis crossing at the centre, and the rings step inward or outward in a measured sequence. The late paintings are built from successive layers that thrust the composition forward or make it recede, screens through which the viewer's gaze penetrates in depth (C8201) -- a text claim only, since no late large painting image was inspected in this study, but it is the same mechanism as the circle-in-square at a larger scale: the disc is not a flat mark but a screen, and the eye passes through it. The numbered oils show the motif in scale: the 1966 no. 119 is 130 x 97 cm, the 1972 square is 150 x 150 cm, the 1973 no. 354 is 100 x 100 cm, the 1981 no. 566 is 151 x 150 cm (C8254). The titles are numbers or "Untitled" -- the subject has left the picture, and what remains is the configuration.

**Takeaway:** the square is the frame and the circle is the event. A piece that uses Torres Aguero should put one disc inside one square, hold the axial symmetry, and let the rings step inward or outward while the colour shifts ring by ring; a disc without its square is a spot, and a square without its disc is a box.

## M2 -- The soft-edged straight line: a line poured, not drawn

His straight lines were never hard-edged but had a sensible quality: rather than drawing straight lines in the strict sense, he proposed straight lines that admitted different thicknesses of soft edges, obtained through the dripping of a liquid paint that glided on the canvas following the law of gravity (C8201). The line is straight in direction and soft in edge; it thickens and thins as the paint glides, and its thickness is a record of how the liquid moved, not of a hand pressing harder. This is the mechanism of the late-1960s turn, when dripping gave way to geometric composition but the drip stayed inside the geometry (C8201). Threads of dripping colour wove thin meshes whose effect lies in their transparency, and monochrome or multicoloured lines generated dense wefts which appeal to the tactile through thickness and texture (C8201). The line is therefore both a geometric element and a poured one: the geometry says where it goes, the gravity says how thick it is.

**Takeaway:** a straight line that wavers is not a mistake, it is the mechanism. A piece should draw its lines by pouring -- straight in direction, soft in edge, varying in thickness as if gravity decided -- and never use a hard ruler edge; a hard-edged grid is Demarco or Asis, not Torres Aguero.

## M3 -- Colour vibration: the systematic scale

The optical play in his painting rests on colour vibrations; the palette is often handled through subtle but systematic colour scales, calibrated by a connoisseur of the methodical practice that any improvisation paradoxically requires (C8201). The vibration does not come from complementary clash, as in Demarco, but from adjacency along a scale: neighbouring hues step through a calibrated sequence, and the eye feels the step. Occasionally a strong colour emphasis disrupts the serenity of the surface (C8201) -- the scale is the rule and the strong colour is the single exception that proves it. The four viewed 1979 prints show the scale inside the stripes: fine parallel stripes shading from white to blue, from red-orange to dark green, from pale cream to dark blue-grey, so the flat print reads as curved or receding (C8252, C8253). The gradient is the scale made visible in one shape.

**Takeaway:** the scale is the mechanism, not the hue. A piece should build its field from a calibrated sequence of adjacent colours and let the steps do the shimmering; a random scatter of bright colours is a mosaic, and one complementary clash is Demarco's trick, not his.

## M4 -- The stripe that changes direction at every boundary

The mechanism observed in all four viewed 1979 prints (C8252, C8253): fine parallel stripes fill each major shape, and the stripe direction changes at every shape boundary -- diagonal in one shape, horizontal in the next -- so the eye cannot settle on a single figure-and-ground reading. In W16 the two halves of one disc carry different stripe systems; in W17 the semicircle, triangle and pentagons each carry a different stripe direction and colour gradient (C8253). The boundary is where the work happens: crossing from one shape to the next, the eye must re-tune its reading, and the re-tuning is the flicker. Nothing moves on the sheet; the movement is the viewer's eye moving across stripe boundaries and colour vibrations, in the framework Arte al Dia gives for the paintings generally (C8251). The 1979 prints are silkscreens, 15.5 x 21.5 in, all the same size (C8252, C8253), and the stripe systems in them are mechanical and exact -- a change from the poured lines of the 1960s, and the one place in his work where the geometry is hard rather than soft.

**Takeaway:** the boundary is the work, not the shape. A piece should fill every shape with fine parallel stripes and change the stripe direction at every boundary, so the eye re-tunes at each crossing; if all the stripes run the same way, the field goes flat and the shapes stop arguing.

## M5 -- The split disc: one circle, two incompatible halves

Observed in W16, Kinetic Composition III, 1979, viewed as the work itself (C8253): a deep blue ground, at left a large half-disc filled with fine diagonal white-to-dark-blue stripes, at right a large blue disc filled with fine horizontal lines, pale irregular cloud-like shapes scattered across the field. The two halves of the disc do not match: the left half is striped diagonally in white-to-blue, the right half is a flat blue disc with horizontal lines. The mismatch is the optical device: the eye reads one circle split into two incompatible halves, and cannot decide whether it is looking at one object or two. The circle-in-square of M1 is split open here: the symmetry holds in outline and breaks in fill. Signed lower right in the image (C8253).

**Takeaway:** one circle is enough if its halves disagree. A piece should draw a single disc and fill its two halves with two incompatible systems -- diagonal against horizontal, gradient against flat -- and let the eye argue about whether it is one object; two discs side by side is a pair, not a split.

## M6 -- Overlapping translucent geometry: shapes that pass through each other

Observed in W17, Kinetic Composition V, 1979, viewed as the work itself (C8253): a large semicircle at right in fine diagonal red-orange stripes shading to dark green at the bottom, a large green triangle at centre in fine horizontal green lines, a blue-grey disc behind it at upper left, three orange pentagons at left and centre, one partly overlapping the triangle, on an olive-green ground. The shapes interlock -- semicircle, triangle, pentagons and disc -- with stripe direction and colour gradient changing per shape, so that the eye cannot settle on a single depth order: the triangle seems in front of the disc and behind the pentagon at once. The overlap reads as translucent because the stripe systems pass through each other without a hard occlusion edge. This is the most colour-saturated and most frankly geometric of the four viewed prints (C8253).

**Takeaway:** overlap without occlusion is the mechanism. A piece should interlock its shapes so no settled depth order exists, with each shape carrying its own stripe direction and gradient; if one shape cleanly covers another, the depth is decided and the argument stops.

## M7 -- Floating bars over a striped field

Observed in W18, Cinetic Composition II, 1979, viewed as the work itself, spelling "Cinetic" as printed (C8252, C8253): a large semicircle at right in fine diagonal stripes shading from pale cream to dark blue-grey, a pale blue rectangle at left in fine horizontal lines, a dark mauve-grey disc behind the centre, and short thick yellow bars scattered across the middle, apparently floating over the striped shapes, on a pale blue-grey ground. The yellow bars are the only non-striped, non-circular element in the four viewed prints, and they read as separate floating marks rather than as part of the ground geometry (C8253). The medium of W18 is unattested -- the Artwork Archive collection page gives title, date and dimensions but no medium for this entry, unlike the other three -- so do not assume silkscreen for it from its neighbours (C8252, C8253). The bars float because they refuse the stripe system: everything else in the print is striped and bounded, and the bars are solid, short and thick, sitting across the stripes without belonging to any shape.

**Takeaway:** a mark that refuses the system floats. A piece should lay a striped geometric field and then scatter a few short solid bars across it, bars that belong to no shape and follow no stripe direction, so they read as hovering in front of the ground; if the bars are striped too, they sink into the field and stop floating.

## M8 -- The mandala: vertical against horizontal, diversity in unity

The mandala in Oriental culture, the calligraphic stroke, diversity in unity: Arte al Dia says it would be impossible to understand his mature painting without taking into account the influence of the mandala, the instinctive strength of its calligraphic stroke, and the notion of diversity in unity (C8201). The mandalic form is regular -- Sigvaldason and Braine both name it -- and through horizontal forms, the earth, and vertical forms, the sky, with coloured lines, in his paintings one accesses light as a path in the manner of the Tao (C8209, C8208; the "Ying/Yang" spelling is as printed in those sources). The vertical and horizontal forces of yin-yang symbolism, Heaven and Earth, passiveness and action, were potentiated by the Eastern experience (C8201). The mandala is therefore not a decorative motif but a structure: one calligraphic gesture repeated and varied, vertical lift against horizontal rest, many marks held in one unity. The Sumi-e imprint is visible in the colour subtleties of his style, acknowledged by Japanese critics at his Tokyo exhibition before his return from Japan (C8201).

**Takeaway:** one gesture, repeated until it becomes a field. A piece should build its mandala from a single calligraphic stroke repeated and varied, holding vertical against horizontal, and let the field breathe between lift and rest; a drawn mandala outline with decoration inside it is a symbol, not a system.

## The aesthetic logic: the line that softens, the colour that steps, the circle that will not settle

Torres Aguero's work has one subject and it is not the circle or the square. It is the interval between a hard idea and a soft hand. The geometry is strict -- the circle inscribed in the square, the axial symmetry, the numbered oils, the exact stripe systems of 1979 -- and the hand is soft -- the dripped line that thickens as it glides, the colour scale that steps rather than clashes, the Sumi-e subtlety that Japanese critics saw in his Tokyo show. Everything he builds is a way of holding those two things together: a straight line that is poured, a circle that is split, a square that is a screen the gaze penetrates in depth, a mandala that is one gesture repeated until it becomes a field. That is why his forms are so few -- the circle, the square, the disc, the triangle, the pentagon, the bar -- and why they repeat for thirty years: the form is a housing for the tension between the rule and the hand, and the housing does not need to change when the tension does the work. Colour, for him, is not a property of a surface but a relation between neighbours on a scale: a hue seen next to its calibrated neighbour is a different hue from the same hue seen alone, and the strong colour that occasionally disrupts the serenity is the exception that proves the scale. The viewer is part of the circuit in exactly one way, and a piece that uses him should honour it: the viewer who stands still and lets the eye do the moving, crossing stripe boundaries, re-tuning at each shape, arguing about whether a split disc is one object or two, penetrating layer after layer of screens. What he never does is build a machine. There is no motor in the 1979 prints, no moving part, no viewer-activated movement attested anywhere in the sources read; the word "kinetic" in his work means optical and perceptual movement only, and a piece that gives him a motor has invented a different artist. Subtraction is the design: one circle, one square, one scale, one gesture, and the patience to let the eye cross them.
