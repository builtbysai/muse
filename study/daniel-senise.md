## The life: Rio, engineering, and the return of painting

Daniel Senise was born in Rio de Janeiro in 1955 -- the year only; no day or month appears in any source read this run. He lives and works in Rio. He is the grandson of Italian immigrants from Basilicata, in southern Italy. He took a civil engineering degree at the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ) in 1980, then studied at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage in Rio with John Nicholson and Luiz Aquila, 1981-1983 in his own words. The engineering training is not a biographical footnote: the building skeleton, the crisscross perspective, the museum interior rebuilt from its own floor -- the work keeps thinking like an engineer about how a space is put together. He later taught painting at Parque Lage; the dates are disputed, 1986-1991 in the Itau-derived biography against 1985-1996 in the Filomena Soares, Artsper and Catalogo das Artes biographies, and the study prints neither span as fact (Corrections 2). (C15808, leg 1 C15600; synthesis C15750)

He was one of 123 artists in "Como vai voce, Geracao 80?" at Parque Lage in July 1984, the exhibition that named the generation, showing "Sansao", a site-specific work in acrylic in three colours over orange plastic, since destroyed. His first solo show opened the same year, at the Galeria do Centro Empresarial Rio -- though the Catalogo das Artes lists the venue instead as the Galeria do Centro Cultural Candido Mendes, and the study records the variant rather than choosing (Corrections 19). In 1985 he showed "Grande Tela" at the 18th Bienal Internacional de Sao Paulo, his international breakthrough. In 1986 he won the gold medal at the 1st Latin American Biennial of Art on Paper in Buenos Aires, and showed at the 2nd Havana Biennial and the 6th Triennale India in New Delhi. (C15809, leg 1 C15601; leg 2 C15650)

The decisive turn came in 1989: after the Sao Paulo Biennial that year he decided to leave brush painting behind and combine print procedures -- the monotype, the imprint, the transfer -- with painting. In 1990 he showed at the 44th Venice Biennale. In 1991 he had his first solo museum show abroad, at the Museum of Contemporary Art in Chicago, curated by Bruce Guenther, and in 1994 at the Museo de Arte Contemporaneo in Monterrey, Mexico. In 1992-93 he was in "Latin American Artists of the Twentieth Century", which travelled to the Centre Georges Pompidou in Paris, Seville, the Kunsthalle Cologne and MoMA New York. The MoMA catalogue for that show prints his birth year as 1953; every other source says 1955, and the study treats 1953 as a catalogue typo (Corrections 14). (C15810, leg 1 C15602)

His father was a pilot who photographed on his travels -- the Amazon, Europe -- in the 1950s and 1960s. After his father's death at age 85, Senise inherited a large trove of those photographs, and they are the genesis of "Biografo": 85 planned works, one per year of his father's life, each a uniform 125 x 200 cm panel with an inner rectangle of fixed size and proportion, sometimes empty, sometimes occupied by an image that carries variations in rhythm and direction. He married the American artist Courtney Smith, moved with her to New York, kept a studio there, divorced, and moved back to Rio; no dates were sourced for the marriage or the divorce. The New York years are themselves unsettled: four continuous years inside a roughly fifteen-year period of shuttling between Rio and New York, with the return year given as 2004 in his own words to Littman and 2005 in the Itau-derived biography and the Portuguese Wikipedia. The study prints 2004 as his account and records the variant (Corrections 3). (C15811, leg 1 C15603, C15604)

The 1998 book "Daniel Senise: Ela que nao esta" (Cosac and Naify) is a different object from the 1994 five-canvas series of the same title, and the two must not be conflated. In 2002 came the book "The Piano Factory" and a show at the Instituto Tomie Ohtake in Sao Paulo. In 2003 he showed "Quase infinito" at the Museu de Arte Contemporanea de Niteroi (MAC-Niteroi). In 2006 he showed 23 paintings at the Museu Oscar Niemeyer in Curitiba, and in 2010 he was in the 29th Sao Paulo Biennial. In 2011 "2892" opened at the Casa Franca-Brasil in Rio. In 2015 came "Quase aqui" at Oi Futuro Flamengo in Rio and "Le Salon" at the Galeria Silvia Cintra in Rio. He joined the Galeria Nara Roesler in 2016; "Printed Matter" opened at Nara Roesler in New York in 2017, curated by Brett Littman. In 2019 he showed "Antes da palavra" at the Fundacao Ibere Camargo in Porto Alegre and "Todos os santos" at the Instituto Tomie Ohtake in Sao Paulo. In 2022 "Veronica" opened at Nara Roesler in Sao Paulo, curated by Luis Perez-Oramas. In 2023-24 "Biografo: Daniel Senise" showed at MAC-USP in Sao Paulo, and in 2025 "I Live Comfortably in the Museum" opened at Nara Roesler in Rio. (C15812, leg 1 C15605)

His collections, on the Nara Roesler list, are six: the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam; the Cisneros Fontanals Art Foundation in Miami; the Ludwig Museum in Cologne; MAM Rio; MAC-Niteroi; and MASP in Sao Paulo. MoMA New York is a 1993 exhibition venue for Senise, not a listed collection holding, and the study does not upgrade it. He was represented by the Galeria Silvia Cintra in Rio in 2015 and by the Galeria Nara Roesler from 2016, his current gallery. His own descriptions of the work are the best short statements of it: "Instead of paint, I started using materials that had already a presence in the world, like leftovers, dust from my studio, rust, metal fillings, sawdust"; "I made the decision to create the minimum possible and instead use images appropriated from the history of art or from other systems of representation. My action was mostly related to composition or disposition of these images on the surface"; "You are American. I'm Brazilian. I'm much more baroque... I don't respect boundaries very well." (C15813, leg 1 C15606, C15607)


## The mechanisms

### M1 -- Floor and wall monotype: the capture

The mechanism is literal and physical. Lay thin cotton fabric -- cretonne -- on a wooden or cement floor or a wall, spread white glue and water over it, let the mixture pass through the fabric and collect residue from the surface, then pull the fabric off. The print carries the floor's grain, its marks and indentations, its dust, dirt and debris. Senise's own account of the discovery: working on thin fabric directly on the floor, he turned it over and found it had picked up the image of the floorboard and other studio debris; he worked on both sides for five years before knowing what to do with the prints, and in 1992/1993 realised the imprints were "very profound for me personally". The floor is the plate, not the canvas: the fabric is laid on the floor, the floor's marks transfer to the fabric, and the fabric becomes the work. A second, earlier variant runs the other way: pigment applied to canvas, the canvas pressed against the still-wet studio floor, then peeled off. From 2019 the capture moved from floors to walls -- "excavation monotypes" -- revealing the marks and physical traces of time left on architecture. Senise has since abandoned the word "monotype" in favour of "captures": "a transfer of matter. It happens only once". The study uses "capture" for the recent wall work and "monotype" only for the period when Senise used the word. The evidencing works are W11, W12, W14, W17, W18 and W20. Two corrections belong here: there is no "bed series" and no bedsheet floor-imprint technique -- bedsheets occur in exactly one work, 2.892 (W15) -- and the first floor impression has no single "invented in" year (1987 in an FMU thesis summary, the second half of the 1980s with systematic use later in the Itau-derived biography, the realisation in 1992/1993 in the Littman interview; different moments, not one date). (C15814, leg 2 C15651; synthesis C15754, C15758)

Watchable behaviour: a surface arrives carrying the marks of a place it has never been shown in -- floorboards, scuffs, dust -- as if the room had printed itself. The picture is a record of a floor, not a picture of one. A piece built on this mechanism should let a real surface do the drawing, with the artist's hand visible only in the choice of where to lay the cloth and when to pull it up.

**Takeaway:** when the floor is the plate, the painting is evidence before it is an image. The artist does not draw the room; the room draws itself, once. (C15815, leg 2 C15652)

### M2 -- Iron-oxide drawing: nails, water and salt, oxidize, fix, repeat

Position small nails on the canvas, pour water and salt over them, let the surface oxidize, fix the rust image with a fixative, repeat until the desired result. Variants run through the work: iron dust, iron powder, iron filings, iron oxide detached from nails in a water and salt solution, synthetic enamel as ground. From 1989 onward Senise "uses iron nails, whose rust marks are recorded in the painting". The image is grown by corrosion, not drawn by hand; the artist only decides when to stop it. The evidencing works are W04, W05, W07, W08 and W09. (C15816, leg 2 C15653; synthesis C15754)

Watchable behaviour: brown blooms spread slowly across the surface like a stain finding its own shape, then freeze. The drawing has a clock in it -- the rust keeps working until the fixative stops it. A piece built on this mechanism should show time as a material: marks that grow, spread and are arrested, never placed.

**Takeaway:** corrosion is a drawing tool with its own will. The artist's job is not to make the mark but to know when to stop it. (C15817, leg 2 C15654)

### M3 -- Absence as the subject: draw the outline of what is missing

Repeat one image until only its absence remains; remove the figure and keep the support, the frame, the cloth or the outline. W10 reproduces "the shape of this absence, the outline of the tomb" in Giotto's Santa Croce fresco. W20 removes the face of Christ and keeps only the veil, so "the focus is on the brackets that support the image". The museum interiors (W11, W12) remove the paintings and keep the empty frames and the architecture. W17 leaves the advertising square empty. A 2023 review states it plainly: "there is a certain absence felt in the 'paintings' of museums... because there are no people, no true details". The evidencing works are W10, W20, W11, W12 and W17. (C15818, leg 2 C15655; synthesis C15755)

Watchable behaviour: the eye goes looking for the thing that should be there and finds only its frame, its outline, its empty room. The missing thing is more present than anything drawn. A piece built on this mechanism should remove the subject and keep everything that held it -- the viewer does the rest.

**Takeaway:** an absence drawn carefully is heavier than a presence painted carelessly. The frame remembers what it used to hold. (C15819, leg 2 C15656)

### M4 -- Appropriation of art-history images as systems of representation

Take a complete image from art history -- Giotto, Whistler, Hobbema, de Hooch, Velazquez, Hammershoi, Friedrich, Heim, El Greco, Zurbaran, Memling -- and rebuild it from Senise's own materials, usually emptied, doubled, mirrored or covered, so the old image becomes a structure to test rather than a picture to copy. "I made the decision to create the minimum possible and instead use images appropriated from the history of art or from other systems of representation. My action was mostly related to composition or disposition of these images on the surface." The 2015 show "Le Salon" duplicated the spatial perspective and dimensions of Heim's 1827 "Le Salon" and "annihilated" it "with a precise collage of fabric and torn-out pages of art history books". The evidencing works are W06 and W08 (Whistler's mother), W10 (Giotto), W11 (de Hooch, Velazquez, Hammershoi) and W20 (Zurbaran, El Greco, Memling). One guard: Whistler's "Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1" -- Whistler's Mother -- is 1871, not 1883 (Newcity Brazil) and not 1887 (Nara Roesler portfolio); both source dates are wrong. (C15820, leg 2 C15657; synthesis C15755, C15763 item 6)

Watchable behaviour: a famous picture arrives recognisable and wrong at the same time -- the mother covered, the tomb empty, the salon annihilated by its own catalogue pages. The viewer knows the image and has to re-read it. A piece built on this mechanism should take a picture everyone already knows and change exactly one load-bearing thing in it.

**Takeaway:** an old master is not a quotation; it is a system of representation you can take apart and rebuild with different materials, to see what it was holding up. (C15821, leg 2 C15658)

### M5 -- Archive, cut and recombine: the cloth-imprint library as paint

Build an archive of cloth imprints of real surfaces, then cut, juxtapose and mount fragments from different places and years into a new image; unfinished or rejected panels are re-combined the same way. "Later, I decided to start an archive of these cloth imprints of surfaces and conditions in the real world and then used them to create illusions or represent images." "Each swatch of cloth had come from a different place. For me it was like a crowd of different people in the works." The same archival logic extends beyond cloth: 2,000 Paris silhouette scraps bought from Seine portrait cutters in 2000, kept for years until a use is found; old art invitations and catalogues shredded to paper pulp for installations. The evidencing works are W12, W14, W18 (Biografo panels as revisions of earlier production) and W17 (Billboard built from old panels with floor samples glued on). (C15822, leg 2 C15659; synthesis C15756)

Watchable behaviour: fragments from different rooms, different years, different floors sit side by side in one picture, and the seams between them are the composition. A piece built on this mechanism should show its archive: pieces of different places held together by the decision to put them in one frame.

**Takeaway:** a library of surfaces is a palette. Cutting and recombining is painting with places instead of colours. (C15823, leg 2 C15660)

### M6 -- Rectangle in rectangle, and rotation as a solving game

Impose one fixed rectangle inside another of the same proportion, then solve the picture inside that constraint, rotating the panel during construction to work from several orientations at once. Biografo: uniform 125 x 200 cm panels, inner rectangle always the same size and proportion, "sometimes... empty. Other times... occupied by an image that carries variations in rhythm and direction compared to the rest of the artwork". Senise: "I only rotate these works depending on what I am trying to see and represent. If I am making a grid then I can turn the panel so I can create a lot of simultaneous orientations." "I really think these works are a kind of visual problem solving for me, a kind of game, where basically at the end of it I will have found 85 solutions." The same rectangle returns in Quase aqui as a sanded, white-painted central void in a used worktable (W16). The evidencing works are W18 and W16. (C15824, leg 2 C15661; synthesis C15756)

Watchable behaviour: a fixed inner frame that sometimes holds an image and sometimes holds nothing, while the panel turns under the maker's hands and the picture is solved from several directions at once. The constraint never changes; the solution does, eighty-five times. A piece built on this mechanism should fix its frame and vary everything inside it -- including the emptiness.

**Takeaway:** a constraint that never moves is not a limitation; it is the game board. Eighty-five solutions to the same rectangle is the work. (C15825, leg 2 C15662)

### M7 -- Reductive emptying: making and unmaking

Start from a complex image and remove pieces of cloth, paintings, figures or information one by one until the work is nearly empty, and stop at the point where it still holds. Littman on the museum interiors: "you've kind of stripped it down by removing pieces of cloth one by one. There is a clear kind of interplay between making and unmaking, a search for that right balance of what's the maximum and what's the minimum amount of information needed to make an image." Senise on the Hermitage: "I have decided to remove the paintings from the walls of the Hermitage and have just left the hint of their presence." The sequence runs 2.892 (white on white, almost no visual information) to Quase aqui (white void in a marked table) to Billboard (empty ad square) to museum rooms "devoid of paintings". The evidencing works are W15, W16, W17, W11 and W12. (C15826; synthesis C15757)

Watchable behaviour: the picture thins out in front of the viewer until almost nothing is left -- and the almost-nothing is the point. Each removal makes the remaining marks work harder. A piece built on this mechanism should let the viewer feel the subtraction: the image getting quieter, step by step, until one more removal would kill it.

**Takeaway:** emptying is not erasing. It is finding the exact point where the least information still makes an image -- and stopping there, one removal short of nothing. (C15827)

### M8 -- Found debris and photo-plus-object: the site supplies both the image and the matter

Use objects and residues found in a specific place -- broken ceramics, pieces of wood, dust, wall chippings -- as the material of the work, and since 2005 glue fragments of those objects onto photographs of the same place, so the work gives "the double indication of a space: its photographic image and the material remnants glued to it". Examples: "Sorocabana II", 2017, objects (tiles) glued on photograph on aluminum, 150 x 250 x 2 cm; "Sorocabana V", 2017, wooden objects rivetted on photograph glued on aluminum, 150 x 270 x 2 cm; "S.R. 34 III", 2005, fabric on inkjet print, 66 x 66 cm. The burnt carpet of W19 is the extreme case: the debris is the paint. "I don't use paint or for that matter any material bought in art supplies or hardware stores. The materials I use are presented to me in my life experience." The evidencing works are W19, W14, W13 (the museum's own carpet) and W15 (used sheets). (C15828; synthesis C15757)

Watchable behaviour: a photograph of a room with pieces of the room itself stuck to it -- the picture and its own rubble, together. The viewer reads the place twice at once, as image and as matter. A piece built on this mechanism should let the site supply both halves: the picture of the place and the pieces of the place.

**Takeaway:** when the material comes from the place, the work cannot be about anywhere else. The debris is the proof the photograph is telling the truth. (C15829)


## The aesthetic logic: the picture that was already there

Senise's logic is a refusal to invent. He decided, in his own words, "to create the minimum possible" and to use images and materials that already had a presence in the world: floors, walls, dust, rust, old masters, his father's photographs, a hospital's sheets, a theatre's burnt carpet. The work is not painting in the classic sense -- "I'm no longer a painter" -- but representation carried out by other means: capture, corrosion, cutting, removal. The artist's action is mostly composition and disposition: where to lay the cloth, when to stop the rust, which fragment goes next to which, how much to take away.

The watchability follows from that refusal. A floor that prints itself gives the viewer a surface that was made by a room, not by a hand. Rust that grows and is arrested gives the viewer a process with a clock in it. An absence -- a tomb outline, a veil without a face, a museum room without paintings -- gives the viewer a search. An old master rebuilt from floor cloth gives the viewer a picture they know, made strange. An archive cut and recombined gives the viewer seams to read. A fixed rectangle solved eighty-five ways gives the viewer a game with visible rules. A picture emptied one piece at a time gives the viewer the exact moment where least becomes enough. A photograph with its own debris glued to it gives the viewer a place twice over.

What he is not: not a process artist in the decorative sense (the process is always in service of a representation -- a floor, a room, a mother, a tomb); not a conceptual artist who has abandoned the picture (the picture is the point, and the materials are chosen because they carry it); not a nostalgic painter of old masters (the old image is a system to test, not a picture to copy). The closest kin in the studied corpus are Milhazes, who also builds pictures from pre-made skins -- but hers are painted motifs placed by hand, while his are surfaces captured by the place itself -- and Jac Leirner, who also lets accumulation and counting do the picturing. Senise's operation is his own: lay the cloth down, let the place print itself, cut what you need, remove what you do not, and stop when the least that remains still holds the image.

## Corrections -- contradictions and single-source items (do NOT smooth over)

1. Parque Lage entry year: 1980 (Itau-derived biography, "entered in 1980") vs 1981-1983 (Senise himself, Filomena Soares, Catalogo das Artes, Artsper). Prefer 1981-1983. (C15831, synthesis C15763 item 1)
