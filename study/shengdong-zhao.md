## M1 -- Phosphor afterglow: the transition explained after it happens (UIST 2006)

**Subject:** A user-interface change (a slider dragged, a checkbox toggled, an icon moved, a collaborator's edit) shown instantly, with the change itself explained afterwards by a diagram left behind on the screen -- an afterglow that fades over a few seconds. Baudisch, Tan, Collomb, Robbins, Hinckley, Agrawala, Zhao, Ramos, UIST 2006, pp. 169-178 (ACM record; the HAL record says pp. 169-174 -- discrepancy not settled). DOI 10.1145/1166253.1166280. Zhao is 7th author; Agrawala is a co-author, a direct bridge from #834. [C37026]

**Visual principle:** Time is projected into space. Instead of an animation that forces the eye to follow a moving object through time, Phosphor draws the object's whole path at once as a static, translucent trail, so the eye can read the change the way it reads a diagram. Three path styles, all borrowed from comics: strobe (finite partially overlapping frames), motion blur (the object compressed to single-pixel width, then stretched along the path), and speed lines (a pair of edge lines plus a center line on a background surface). Speed lines were used in most applications and both studies. The visual language is coding, not decoration: path opacity, path shape, and temporal order each carry one fact. Good results at 50 percent opacity for the densest part of a path; prototype defaults 12.5-50 percent fill, 25-87.5 percent edges. Colors are sampled and averaged from the moving object itself, so the trail belongs to the thing that moved. Temporal order is coded three ways at once: fading old path segments, fading initial-state-first; stacking newer frames on top of older ones, final state on top of everything; distinctive initial states -- an outline for a move, a solid for a copy. Path shape codes the operation: a narrow midriff means a transient or temporary move or copy (an elastic rubber band); a tapered path means child-of, extract, or drop-in. In-place transitions (rotation, opacity, bring-to-front) get motion added before projection so the path does not collapse onto one spot. Clutter is managed by rule: paths are transparent to mouse input; a repeated transition extends the path while the initial state stays; up to 10 simultaneous translations were visually separable in pilot testing; paths that exactly overlap are routed to avoid each other. Fig. 1, viewed as pixels, shows the principle in one image: green afterglow trails on a slider dragged all the way left, two checkboxes toggled, a combo box changed from 1 to 2 -- the whole recent history of a panel readable at a glance. [C37027]

**Computational mechanism:** A phosphor transition is defined by two rules: (1) it shows the outcome of the change instantly, and (2) it explains the change in retrospect using a diagrammatic depiction. The afterglow typically fades over a few seconds. Study 1 (following a collaborator's changes): 12 university students, ages 24-30, 1 female; a 5 by 8 grid of 40 widgets; 6 adjustments per trial (2 sliders, 2 checkboxes, 2 combo boxes); 500 ms pause, 200 ms hover, traversal medium 600/800/1000 ms (total 4,800 ms), slow x1.3, fast x0.7; 4,000 ms pause; afterglow faded independently after 4,000 ms; 8 repetitions per cell, 2 by 3 within-subjects design. Median completion time phosphor 7.17 s vs control 9.35 s, F(1,11)=20.07, p=.001. Playback-speed main effect F(2,22)=4.06, p=.031. Errors about 3.1 on average (about half of 6 undone), no significant error difference. All 12 participants preferred phosphor; "helped remember more changes" mean 5.9/7. Study 2 (phosphor vs animation): 12 volunteers, ages 22-35, 4 female; 6 interfaces (phosphor plus animations at 125, 250, 500, 1000, 2000 ms, 25 fps slow-in/slow-out); 3 tasks (singleIcon, tripleIcon, distractor), 4 outcomes, 60 trials per interface per task; screen of 11 windows plus 25 icons; highlight 1,200 ms, 500 ms pause. Task times: tripleIcon 1,010.72 ms vs singleIcon 645.54 ms (+56 percent), distractor 804.25 ms (+25 percent over single). Phosphor was significantly faster than all animations except 125 ms (no significant difference there). Error-rate main effect of interface F(5,55)=4.704, p<.001, driven by the 2000 ms animation vs phosphor/125/250/500 ms. Preference: fast animation favorite for singleIcon and distractor (7 of 11); tripleIcon split 3 each fast/slow/phosphor; phosphor called more distracting in the distractor task. The paper's own stated limit: space-for-time breaks down when paths span the whole screen (the eye cannot foveate the whole path); the greatest benefit is likely in localized effects (Proximity Compatibility Principle). [C37028]

**Takeaway:** Show the outcome at once; leave the explanation behind as a fading diagram the eye can read. [C37029]

## M2 -- Kinetic textures: a patch of objects, set in motion at two scales (CHI 2014)

**Subject:** A still illustration brought to life by continuous, looping motion of collections of small objects -- snowfall, falling leaves, a school of fish, water dripping from a fountain, a breathing dragon -- while the drawing keeps the timeless quality of a still picture. Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Zhao, Fitzmaurice, CHI 2014, pp. 351-360. DOI 10.1145/2556288.2556987. CHI 2014 Honorable Mention and Best Talk; the shipped app, SketchBook Motion, was Apple iPad App of the Year 2016. [C37030]

**Visual principle:** Motion at two scales, both sketched rather than programmed. A kinetic texture is a patch (a small number of representative objects drawn as a source example) plus motion properties. Global motion is the shape of the whole collection's movement; granular motion is the fine movement of each individual object. Two types: emitting textures, in which objects continuously emanate from an emitter and follow a global motion field drawn as sketched motion paths, decay as they cross the texture outline, and can hide in masks (a point emitter emits in all directions); and oscillating textures, in which a finite collection is replicated along a brush skeleton and oscillates between that skeleton and a target oscillating skeleton by simple harmonic (sinusoidal) interpolation of position and orientation. The contrast between the two types is the visual argument: emitting is open, continuous, and dissipative (rain, waterfalls, flocks); oscillating is closed, bounded, and periodic (swaying grass, breathing, hair, cloth). Fig. 1, viewed as pixels, shows a single scene holding both at once: waterfall and rippling water (emitting), flying birds and falling leaves (emitting along paths), a breathing dragon and swarming flies (oscillating and emitting side by side). Fig. 2 shows five small examples -- snowfall, tree leaves blowing, fountain water, school of fish, air blowing off a fan -- each a different pairing of the same two motions. Fig. 5 shows cohesion as a visible variable: low cohesion spreads objects evenly between motion paths, high cohesion pulls them adhesive to the paths and produces obstacle avoidance. Fig. 6 (motion factorization, a fish) shows global trajectory plus granular motion combining into the trajectory of an individual object. [C37031]

**Computational mechanism:** Emitting dynamics are controlled by 3 parameters: emission velocity (initial velocity of objects), emission frequency (how often objects are emitted), and cohesion (alpha in the weighted-sum motion field; higher alpha means objects are more adhesive to the motion paths; low cohesion means a uniform distribution between paths, obstacle avoidance at high cohesion). The motion field is a weighted sum of unit vectors at discrete points Pm on the motion paths, the weight falling with distance d(P, Pm). Granular motion is translation (a 2D path) or pivot (rotation about a pivot point); it controls velocity (frequency along the global path) and phase (synchronization; minimum phase means all objects synchronized). A motion profile is a user-sketched curve whose height defines scale or velocity along the trajectory (for example bubbles that grow and decelerate). A perspective tilt tool gives a 3D effect. Design study: analysis of YouTube tutorials for Flash, Maya, 3DS Max, and After Effects (rain/snow, falling leaves, swaying grass, flocks/swarms, crowds, water ripples) plus interviews with 2 professional animators (both Maya users), identifying three animation types -- particle systems, flocking, stochastic motion. User evaluation: 8 participants, ages 24-43 (average 32), 7 male; 4 amateur illustrators, 4 professional animators; Wacom Cintiq 21ux; 60-80 min each; training 20-25 min (6 tasks); exercise task of 5 kinetic textures (3 emitting, 2 oscillating) reproduced from a model video -- all finished without assistance, average 7:40 min (min 4:30, max 13:40), 7 workflow errors (5 tool mix-ups, 2 conceptual -- emitting chosen where oscillating was better); overall ease of use 4.63/5 (min 4). Freeform uses: oscillating for trees, weeds, hair, clothes, fire, and a scuba diver's legs; emitting for rain, waterfalls, flocking, water ripples, and camera movement with moving backgrounds. Stated limitations: no single-object animation, no object interaction (collisions/attraction), only two skeletons for oscillating textures. [C37032]

**Takeaway:** Draw a few objects and a path; the multitude and its motion follow. [C37033]

## M3 -- Example-patch texture synthesis: the artist draws a little, the system draws the rest (CHI 2012)

**Subject:** Pen-and-ink illustration in which the artist draws a small example patch of a texture and gestures to fill a region with it -- brushing along a line, flood-filling an area, or hatching continuously across a shape -- preserving the artist's own stroke character. Kazi, Igarashi, Zhao, Davis, CHI 2012, pp. 1727-1736. DOI 10.1145/2207676.2208302. [C37034]

**Visual principle:** Tone, stroke, and texture as three separable variables. Tone (also called "value" or "density") is the ratio of black ink to white paper over a region. Strokes are the building blocks; a texture is a collection of strokes that gives an object or scene the illusion of shape, surface, and lighting. Fig. 4, viewed as pixels, shows the taxonomy in one plate: (a)-(f) six different kinds of texture, (g)-(l) the same texture at varying tone by changing stroke density, (j)-(l) subsequent cross-hatching. Fig. 5 shows the traditional five-step workflow as a tree drawn in five stages: light pencil outlines, pencil details and shadows, small example texture patches, repeatedly applying patches to fill the outlines (the most tedious, least unique step -- hence the one automated), touch-up details. Fig. 6 shows the three automation techniques in use: brushing (hatched lines, landscape, trees and grasses), flood filling (stippling, clothes, walls, wood), continuous hatching (architectural drawings, cross-hatching, depth by different darkness on different planes). The visual claim is that a texture's identity lives in the example patch -- the irregularity of the hand -- and that synthesis must preserve that irregularity rather than regularize it. The paper is explicit that subtle variation in the reproduced pattern is desirable for changing density and avoiding regularity, and that pixel-based synthesis cannot do it because it cannot represent individual or discrete elements with semantic meaning. [C37035]

**Computational mechanism:** Analysis of 56 pen-and-ink illustrations by 32 artists (mostly from The Technical Pen and Rendering with Pen and Ink). Three automation techniques: brushing (37/56 illustrations), flood filling (25/56), continuous hatching (count not stated as a fraction in the text read). Implementation: a patch is the strokes near the gesture, clustered into elements by merging strokes with overlapping bounds; each element is represented by its centroid point sample. Brush: 1D synthesis along the gesture, spacing from the example patch, orientation from the gesture tangent. Continuous hatching: 1D synthesis plus duplicate/paste to fill either side plus simplified stitching to merge into long seamless lines. Flood fill: EM texture-optimization iterating search (find the input sample with the most similar neighborhood, minimizing energy E) and assignment (move the output element to the energy-minimizing position). Refinement: a tone/density slider (scales element-position density), stroke width, grayscale, sweep/curvature editing, one-point perspective tilt (angle is the foreshortening direction, length is the amount), and a flow field (a gesture sets vector-field direction within a set distance; elements orient along it). Evaluation: 4 professional artists (P1-P4, 3 male 1 female, ages 23-55; P1/P2 15+ years pen-and-ink illustration, animation, and direction; P3/P4 4+ years, design/illustration trained); Cintiq 12wx; training 15-20 min (7 practice drawings); illustration 40-65 min; average 11 min per drawing; example artworks took 16, 13, 19 min from scratch; a crowd/fireworks illustration took under 5 min from 3 example strokes plus a few gestures. Learnability 4/5 (all four), satisfaction 4.25/5. Free-task logging: 215 feature uses in 66 min (3.16/min); brush 123 uses (55.4 percent). Limits: performance degrades above about 1,000 strokes; leftward brush gestures vertically flip the texture (happened 9 times to P1 and P3); long overlapping example strokes can look repetitive. [C37036]

**Takeaway:** One patch of real strokes, grown by rule, carries the whole drawing. [C37037]

## M4 -- Elastic hierarchies: one picture, two ways of drawing a tree, changed node by node (InfoVis 2005)

**Subject:** A hierarchy drawn as a single picture that interleaves node-link diagrams and Treemaps at different nodes -- dense deep subtrees as Treemaps, higher levels as node-link -- with the representation at each point chosen independently. Zhao, McGuffin, Chignell, IEEE InfoVis 2005, pp. 57-64. DOI 10.1109/INFVIS.2005.1532129. [C37038]

**Visual principle:** Two opposite pictorial conventions held in one image, and the seam between them made visible. Node-link diagrams show nesting structure clearly and are more familiar to users, but use screen space inefficiently and do not scale to large datasets. Treemaps are space-efficient and make relative node sizes easy to compare, but show depth and levels poorly. "Elastic" means the representation at each point is chosen independently, so a single picture can be node-link at the top and Treemap at the bottom, or Treemap outside and node-link inside, or any mixture. Fig. 2, viewed as pixels, shows the same tree four ways: (A) node-link, (B) nested containment, (C) alignment and adjacency, (D) indented outline. Fig. 3 is a taxonomy (also viewed as pixels) of the six transitions between the two forms, Fig. 4 (A)-(F): A pure node-link, F pure Treemap, B-E hybrids. Four transitional forms: B, Treemap outside node-link (dense deep subtrees as Treemaps; small Treemaps act as previews or overviews that are also fully operable); C/D, Treemap or node-link pulled out of a Treemap (drilling down; edges overlaid on the Treemap cause mild occlusion); E, node-link inside a Treemap (Treemap as overview and context, node-link as local detail; enlarging a Treemap portion distorts substructures and can hinder perceptual landmarks -- the tradeoff is stated in the paper). Fig. 4, viewed as pixels, is the strongest single image in the paper: the same tree six times, red node-link branches against grey Treemap blocks, the hybrid panels visibly neither one form nor the other. Fig. 5 (screenshot, viewed as pixels) shows Treemap-outside-node-link on a real directory tree; Fig. 6 shows drilling down. Overview plus detail: a Treemap overview on the left, the elastic hierarchy detail on the right, a draggable vertical divider; the Treemap aspect ratio matched between views; rubber bands linking selected nodes across views; a search box highlighting matches in orange in both views; tabs above each Treemap node selecting the level to roll over or highlight (descendants partially faded); double-click expanding a subtree into node-link. [C37039]

**Computational mechanism:** Prototype in Java 1.4.2 with the Piccolo toolkit; Strip Treemap variant (Bederson/Wattenberg) preserving node order; node-link layout similar to Walker's algorithm / SpaceTree; demo data a Postgres directory tree of almost 1,000 files and folders. Layout algorithm: greedy breadth-first traversal, set nodes to node-link, stop and backtrack when there is no room (goal 1: maximize higher-level node-link nodes); then size Treemaps by a subtree size/depth heuristic and rescale to fit the screen (goal 2: maximize remaining Treemap area). The generalization is stated explicitly: any connected subgraph S can be collapsed into a Treemap with the nodes under S pulled out. No user study -- an exploratory prototype; empirical research is explicitly called for in the paper. That absence must be stated, not papered over: there are no measured outcomes for this mechanism, only the design space, the taxonomy, and the working prototype. Note for the builder: the paper's elasticity is per-node and per-subtree choice among discrete transitional forms, not a single continuous blend parameter; a prompt built on a continuous morph must be corrected to the paper's mechanism, not built on the summary. [C37040]

**Takeaway:** Let each part of the tree choose its own way of being drawn. [C37041]

## M5 -- Zone and polygon menus: the start point of the stroke is a choice (CHI 2006)

**Subject:** Marking menus whose breadth is increased not by adding stroke directions but by using where the stroke starts. Zhao, Agrawala, Hinckley, CHI 2006, pp. 1077-1086. Built during Zhao's summer 2005 Microsoft Research internship under Agrawala and Hinckley. [C37042]

**Visual principle:** A straight stroke is defined by orientation, length, and the position of its start point -- line segments in the plane are four-dimensional (orientation 1D, length 1D, position 2D). Orientation-only marking menus cap at breadth 8, because accuracy falls substantially beyond 8 directions; breadth-8 depth-2 gives 64 items at over 90 percent accuracy (Kurtenbach and Buxton), while real menus average breadth 13.7 (Word menus: minimum 4 Window, maximum 17 Tools). Length is rejected as a selection channel: it is not scale-independent and not attention-free. Position is the new channel, and it changes the geometry of the whole interaction. Zone menus: an initial tap sets the origin; each later stroke is classified by the radial zone its start point falls in plus its orientation -- example 4 zones by 4 orientations for breadth 16, upper bound 8 zones by 8 orientations for breadth 64. Polygon menus: strokes correspond to edges of an N-sided polygon; each edge selects one of 2 items by stroke direction, so breadth is 2N (an 8-sided polygon gives 16). Orientation eliminates all but 2 opposite edges; the interaction surface is split into two half-plane zones parallel to the edge through the origin, and the stroke and the edge must lie in the same half-plane. Zones adapt their orientation per stroke, so polygon menus need less positioning precision than zone menus. Fig. 3, viewed as pixels, shows the 8-sided polygon menu with color labels (Purple, Pink, Red, Orange, Brown, Gold, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Gray, Silver, Black, White, Cyan, Magenta) and the half-plane split. Fig. 4 shows the experimental stimuli as small grids of colored strokes (blue first, red second, green third). The visual idea is a compass that has learned to care about where your hand lands, not only which way it moves. [C37043]

**Computational mechanism:** Novice mode: the visualization fades in if the user pauses more than 300 ms at any point. Abort: a spring-loaded quasimode -- hold Ctrl for the whole interaction, release to abort (a timeout-based abort was rejected after novice testing: no single timeout fits both novices and experts). Hybrids: first-stroke length decides -- a short tap invokes the Zone or Polygon menu, a longer stroke invokes an orientation menu (up to 8 frequent items, no tap needed). Hybrid Zone-Orientation breadth 40; Hybrid Polygon-Orientation breadth 24; the increase applies only at the top level; not fully scale-independent (it uses first-stroke length). Experiment 1: 9 right-handed volunteers, ages 26-60, 5 women 4 men, little or no TabletPC experience; Toshiba Portege 3500 TabletPC, Windows XP TabletPC; input area 6 in. by 4.5 in.; 5,670 selections. Breadth-12: accuracy orientation 87.1 percent vs zone 97.7 percent vs polygon 98.2 percent (F2,16=15.71, p<.01); execution time orientation 675 ms vs zone 869 ms vs polygon 887 ms (the tap costs time); total time not significantly different by technique. Breadth-16 (orientation-4x4 vs zone-16 vs polygon-16): accuracy 97.4-99.8 percent all, not significant; total time orientation-4x4 1,489 ms vs zone-16 1,208 ms vs polygon-16 1,081 ms (F2,16=12.72, p<.01) -- shallower is faster. Same pattern at 256 items. Zone-32 (extra condition, 648 selections): accuracy 96 percent, reaction 626 ms, execution 553 ms, total 1,179 ms -- viable; zone-64 was eliminated in pilots (unacceptably low accuracy). Experiment 2: same design, small input area -- a 1-inch square hole in a cardboard cutout, origin fixed at the hole center, no initial tap; 9 new volunteers, ages 28-54. Breadth-12 execution zone 505 ms, polygon 517 ms vs orientation 673 ms (F2,16=3.62, p=.051) -- tapless position menus are faster. Eyes-free pilot (4 subjects, no visual feedback or ink trails): polygon menus gained even more, attributed to lower stroke precision. Design guidance in the paper: use Polygon up to breadth 16, Zone up to breadth 32. [C37044]

**Takeaway:** Where a stroke starts can say as much as which way it goes. [C37045]

## M6 -- Simple-mark menus: lift the pen, start again, lose nothing (UIST 2004)

**Subject:** Hierarchical marking menus made from a series of short, inflection-free marks with the pen lifted between each, instead of one continuous compound mark. Zhao and Balakrishnan, UIST 2004, pp. 33-42. DOI 10.1145/1029632.1029639. The paper received 5,5,5,5 from all reviewers, the highest review score in UIST 2004 (per the authors' CV). [C37046]

**Visual principle:** The mark is freed from the path. A compound mark is one continuous stroke with inflections; its shape must encode the whole hierarchy at once, so it grows quadratically with depth -- a 4-level compound mark needs about 16 times the space of a 1-level mark -- and it is ambiguous, because scale invariance makes a straight mark unreadable as S vs S-S, or S-S-N vs S-N-N. A simple mark is one straight flick; the pen lifts; the next flick is independent. Because each mark is independent, there is no ambiguity, the depth cost is only linear and additive, and marks can overlap in space -- the user can draw each flick in the same small patch, or offset them slightly. Fig. 4, viewed as pixels, shows the experimental setup: a compass layout on screen, a red instruction ("Select NW - SW - NW"), and below it the participant's ink trail as feedback -- three short strokes, each its own gesture, the trail persisting just long enough to confirm. The visual principle is accumulation by discrete, repeatable units rather than by one continuous line: a dashed rhythm instead of a drawn curve. [C37047]

**Computational mechanism:** Three compound-mark limits are quantified. (1) Breadth/depth/speed/accuracy tradeoff: breadth-4 maximum depth 4, breadth-8 maximum depth 2 at under 10 percent error. (2) Ambiguity from scale invariance: leaves must be left unassigned at depth 3 and beyond; unambiguous leaves equal B times (B-1) to the power (D-1), plus B -- for example compass4-4 gives 4 x 3^3 + 4 = 112 of 256 leaves; compass8-3 gives 8 x 7^2 + 8 = 400 of 512. (3) Space grows quadratically with depth. The simple-mark fix: back up by tapping the menu center; abort by a special gesture (circle or pigtail) or by exceeding the inter-mark timeout. Experiment: 12 right-handed participants, ages 20-35, 4 women 8 men, no marking-menu experience; Pentium 4 2 GHz, Windows XP, 19 in. display, Wacom Intuos2 12 in. by 18 in. pen in absolute mode; input sizes large 7.8 in. by 8.8 in., medium 3.5 in. by 4.25 in., small 1.25 in. by 1.25 in.; layouts compass4-2, compass4-3, compass8-2, compass8-3; 9,216 selections; 80 warm-up trials per technique. Accuracy simple 93 percent vs compound 80 percent (F1,11=131.38, p<.0001); total time 2.92 s vs 3.09 s; drawing time 1.79 s vs 1.97 s; reaction time not significant. Accuracy stayed about 93 percent across all four layouts for simple marks; compound degraded sharply at compass8-3. On-axis vs off-axis: compound on-axis 86.05 percent vs off-axis 75.87 percent vs mixed 77.20 percent (F2,22=4.57, p<.05); simple showed no significant on/off difference. Size had no significant effect on simple-mark accuracy or time; compound accuracy and time degraded significantly at the small size. Space: simple significantly more space-efficient (F1,11=14.90, p<.001); observed usage fell roughly halfway between the fully-overlapping lower bound and the compound upper bound -- users offset marks slightly and did not make a "dashed" compound mark. Inter-mark gap: mean 0.481 s, SD 0.377 s, median 0.375 s; 99 percent under 1.875 s, 95 percent under 1.156 s; recommended timeout threshold 1-2 s. Preference: 9 of 12 preferred simple, 3 compound. Viable menu size rises from 112 items (compound compass4-4) to 512 items (simple compass8-3) -- a factor of about 5. [C37048]

**Takeaway:** Break the gesture into flicks and the hierarchy stops fighting you. [C37049]

## M7 -- The interruptible voice: reactive eyes-free interaction (CHI 2007; CHI 2018; ToCHI 2020)

**Subject:** Interaction without vision, in three generations: a menu you explore by touch while a voice names each item (earPod, 2007); a word processor you edit by interrupting its own reading aloud (EDITalk, 2018); and a correction method that lets you simply re-speak the sentence the way it should have been (Commanding and Re-Dictation, 2020). Zhao is an author on all three; the thread is one mechanism with three embodiments. earPod: Zhao, Dragicevic, Chignell, Balakrishnan, Baudisch, CHI 2007, pp. 1395-1404, DOI 10.1145/1240624.1240836. EDITalk: Ghosh, Foong, Zhao, Chen, Fjeld, CHI 2018, Paper 403, DOI 10.1145/3173574.3173977. Commanding and Re-Dictation: Ghosh, Liu, Zhao, Hara, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 27(4), Article 28, August 2020, 31 pp. [C37050]

**Visual principle:** This mechanism has no picture; its principle is temporal and spatial, and it is stated here as the perceptual structure instead of a visual one. The core idea is synchrony: the system only reacts to the user, never makes the user wait for it. Traditional IVR is an asynchronous sequential dialog -- the system speaks, the user waits, the user answers -- which the earPod paper diagrams as two timelines out of step (Fig. 4, viewed as pixels: left, the asynchronous model with "Waiting" gaps; right, earPod's synchronous model where audio outputs track touchpad input with no waiting). earPod replaces that with a reactive model: touch the dial and the item name is spoken at once; slide and the previous playback is aborted mid-word with a click, the new item spoken instead; lift the finger to select, confirmed by a "camera-shutter" sound. A word like "banana" can be rejected after the syllable "ba" -- partial playback is enough to decide. EDITalk adds the barge-in: the user interrupts real-time text-to-speech playback with an utterance; the system pauses real time, executes the operation, and resumes from the saved location. The spatial part is the dial: a circular touchpad divided into sectors like a pie menu (Fig. 3, viewed as pixels: 8 numbered sectors around an inner disc, the inner disc meaning cancel), up to 12 items per track, 8 per level recommended from pilots. Spatialized audio -- simple left-right interaural time and intensity manipulation -- was enough in pilots to convey the illusion of items laid out on a circle around the head. The user scans and compares by moving the finger back and forth without committing anything to memory; the layout, not the user's recall, holds the list. In Commanding and Re-Dictation the spatial anchor becomes the sentence itself: the correction is located by when it is spoken, and by the context words around it. [C37051]

**Computational mechanism:** earPod hardware: a Cirque EasyCat USB touchpad with a thin plastic overlay and circular cutout, touch-sensitive radius 19 mm, overlay 2 mm thick, 3 mm notch on top as a tactile orientation cue; spatial hysteresis on boundaries (a boundary moves slightly opposite after crossing, so returning needs a slightly larger movement); involuntary lift movements filtered out. Speech: human voices recorded CD quality (16-bit, 44 kHz), leading silences removed, levels normalized, a real-time audio library (Java native sound had perceptible lag). No timeout, unlike marking menus' roughly 300 ms: the transition is self-discoverable through interruptible audio plus spatial layout. Expert path: long exploratory glides, then brief corrective gestures, then direct taps on remembered locations. Experiment vs an iPod-like visual linear menu: 12 right-handed participants, ages 17-30 (mean 24), 3 female, none had used an iPod; 2-level hierarchy, 8 items per level (64 items, 16 stimuli presented, 2 per top-level category: Clothing, Fish, Instrument, Job, Animal, Color, Country, Fruit; words 1-3 syllables, about 1 s audio); 20 blocks; 11,520 selections total; 5 min training per interface. Accuracy audio 92.1 percent vs visual 93.9 percent, not significant; 1-level 94.2 percent vs 2-level 91.8 percent (F1,11=21.16, p<.001). Speed: no overall difference; 2-level 3.4 s vs 1-level 1.9 s (F1,10=82.84, p<.001); learning-by-technique interaction significant -- audio initially slower, then faster; final time period audio 2.1 s vs visual 2.5 s (F1,10=6.03, p<.05), so audio beat visual within 30 minutes of practice. Expert tapping was discovered independently by 9 of 12 participants (1-level) and 8 of 12 (2-level), though only gliding was taught. Preference: 4 audio, 4 visual, 4 no preference; all but one agreed or strongly agreed a combined audio-plus-visual technique would be desirable. Confound stated in the paper: earPod differs from the iPod menu in two dimensions at once (audio vs visual feedback, and direct vs linear access). EDITalk design: a closed loop of TTS engine plus asynchronous ASR engine plus word processor plus controller. Information flow: ASR hears an utterance, the controller sends pause to TTS, TTS returns the pause location and saves it, the controller parses voice context plus location to determine the operation and target range, the word processor performs it, the controller sends resume, TTS resumes from the saved location. Sentence-level operations use the TTS's location awareness to compute sentence boundaries, removing the recall burden from the user. Command structure: all operations (including text entry) go through command syntax; fail-safes: for an unrecognized command, pause TTS 0.5 s and resume from the beginning of the interrupted sentence; for an unmatched or missing target phrase, pause 2 s, voice an error prompt, and resume from sentence start. 19 user intents supported (including Restart, Repeat Para, Pause/Resume, Note, Insert Before, Undo/Redo). Elicitation study (Wizard-of-Oz): 12 participants, 5 female 7 male, mean age 26.75; each revised their own academic text (200-250 words) via Apple Notes plus VoiceOver plus a human facilitator. Operations performed: Markup 18.2 percent, Comment 31.8 percent, Core (insert/delete/replace) 24.5 percent, Navigation 14.5 percent, Others (pause/resume) 11 percent. Meta-level operations (markup plus comment) 50 percent vs core 24.5 percent -- twice as frequent. 9 of 12 (75 percent) were willing to use a device hands- and eyes-free in the elicited scenarios (commuting, walking, tired eyes). Observational study of Dragon Professional Individual for Mac 6 plus Microsoft Word: 8 participants, 4 female 4 male, mean age 28.25; 8 operations, paragraphs mean 46.1 words. Accuracy mean 20.57 percent, SD 15.3, min 0, max 50; sentence-level operations lowest (repeat sentence 8.33 percent, repeat last sentence 0 percent, highlight sentence 12.5 percent). Usability study: 12 participants, 6 female 6 male, mean age 27.67 (SD 6.27); 10 tasks, paragraphs mean 48 words (SD 8.63), children's-story excerpts; blindfolded, walking in a low-noise lab, Bose QC35 headphones with microphone -- fully eyes- and hands-free. Results, read from the chart in Fig. 3 (accuracy / utterance precision, percent, averaged across 12 participants): Repeat Current 97/100, Repeat Prev. 100/100, Restart 100/92, Highlight Sentence 100/100, Highlight Phrase 92/94, Comment on Sentence 75/100, Comment on Phrase 64/94, Insert 75/80, Delete 92/84, Replace 92/85. Fig. 4 (read from chart) compares accuracy, Dragon vs EDITalk: Repeat Current 8.33 vs 97, Repeat Prev. 0 vs 100, Restart 50 vs 100, Highlight Sentence 12.5 vs 100, Highlight Phrase 25 vs 92, Comment on Sentence n/a vs 75, Comment on Phrase n/a vs 64, Insert 18.75 vs 75, Delete 31.25 vs 92, Replace 18.75 vs 92. Likert: useful in achieving eyes-free word processing mean 5.92 (SD 0.67); sure that what was instructed was what was performed mean 5.58 (SD 0.67); amount of confirmation/feedback apt mean 5.17 (SD 1.03). Error analysis: almost 50 percent of participants had ASR trouble with the insert and comment keywords -- insert misrecognized as "inside", comment as "commence". Commanding and Re-Dictation: the Wizard-of-Oz study (10 participants, 5 female 5 male, mean age 25.2) found six categories of instruction along a spectrum of specificity -- think aloud, questions, high-level commands, low-level commands, re-dictation, re-dictation with commands -- and two dominant strategies: editing by commands that do not adhere to strict syntax ("please change this to that") and editing by re-speaking over the erroneous part ("he go there" corrected by speaking "he goes there"). The re-dictation alignment mechanism: two strategies -- limit the search space, then use language analysis. The timing heuristic: from pilot studies, nearly 98 percent of the time a correction was spoken within an average of 1.4 seconds of the target text being read out (the barge-in phenomenon, attributed to short-term memory limits); the search space is therefore limited to the sentence being interrupted, plus the preceding sentence if the interruption falls within the first four words (experimentally determined). Alignment itself uses left and right context words when both are given (trivial case: replace the text between the contexts with the utterance between the contexts); otherwise a three-pass similarity match -- stem matching, then lemma matching, then semantic matching -- using tokens from the Google Cloud Natural Language API, terminating at the first pass that finds a match. Controlled experiment: 16 participants, 6 female 10 male, mean age 24 (SD 4.38), 10 native English speakers. TwoTriggers: technique main effect F1,15=36.56, p<.001, eta2=0.709 -- re-dictation faster than commanding for complex edits. Attempts: OneTrigger re-dictation 1.63 +/- 1.2 vs commanding 2.15 +/- 2.04 (F1,15=7.15, p=.017); TwoTriggers 2.32 +/- 2.1 vs 4.73 +/- 3.39 (F1,15=27.74, p<.001). Replays of the correct sentence, TwoTriggers: re-dictation 0.45 +/- 0.7 vs commanding 1.23 +/- 1.13 (F1,15=32.37, p<.001). Subjective: SUS re-dictation 75.94 +/- 14.3 vs commanding 55.47 +/- 21.43 (W=5.5, p=.006); weighted NASA-TLX 45.17 +/- 15.8 vs 60.37 +/- 24.05 (W=115, p=.016). 11 of 16 participants said re-dictation felt more "natural", aligning with how they "would correct a human-being". VoiceRev realistic study: 8 participants; 266 successful revision trials -- 165 re-dictation (62 percent), 77 command-based deletions (about 29 percent), 18 command-based replacements, 6 command-based insertions. Mean words corrected per utterance: re-dictation 6.01 +/- 3.88 vs command delete 1.82 +/- 1.74, replace 1.39 +/- 0.5, add 1.17 +/- 0.41. False positives: commanding utterances misclassified as re-dictation about 13.3 percent (22 of 165); re-dictation misclassified as commanding about 7.35 percent. [C37052]

**Takeaway:** Never make the user wait for the system to finish speaking. [C37053]

## M8 -- Breadcrumbs: a query that stays where you left it (CHI 2007)

**Subject:** Search that lives inside the note-taking itself: a query made by lassoing your own ink, left behind as a persistent mark on the page, opened later with a stroke, gathering documents and clippings back into the notes. Hinckley, Zhao, Sarin, Baudisch, Cutrell, Shilman, Tan, CHI 2007, pp. 251-260. DOI 10.1145/1240624.1240666. Built during Zhao's summer 2006 Microsoft Research internship under Hinckley. [C37054]

**Visual principle:** The query is a first-class object, commingled with the ink. Four concepts: leverage preexisting ink to start a search; tight coupling of queries with application content; queries persist as first-class objects commingled with ink; free interleaving of inking, searching, and gathering. The breadcrumb: an incomplete (non-self-intersecting) lasso around ink creates a persistent breadcrumb in about 1 s; stroke down opens personal-search results, stroke right does a web search; hover shows details (unexamined vs opened, with result count; web breadcrumbs look different); breadcrumbs can be cut, copied, pasted, and moved, and serve as reminders and history in context. A complete lasso plus a pigtail brings up a command menu, with Search as one command (opens results in one stroke). Fig. 3, viewed as pixels, shows the breadcrumb itself: the word "breadcrumb" in blue ink with small document icons, and on the right a web breadcrumb with a globe icon and a red "Remove" label -- a query drawn the way a note is drawn. Fig. 2 shows a finished note: a query on "Line Rider" with documents, web pages, and screen clippings gathered and annotated around it. Fig. 4 is an 8-step walkthrough (viewed as pixels): write ink notes, lasso to create and open a breadcrumb, open the search panel, drag a file icon into the notes, open the document's tracking menu, capture a rectangle, and see the new content in the notes. The search panel is compact, appears just below the lasso when possible, with a minimum of about 1 cm white margin on all sides so icons can be dragged out in any direction; results sorted by date by default (time as a memory landmark); a focus result shows a 2-line snippet, contextual hyperlinks, and a thumbnail. The tracking menu (semi-transparent, pinned upper-right by default): pen-down in the outer ring scrolls the host document; Capture applies a gray mask and the user sweeps a rectangle to clip content; Close returns to InkSeine with the clipping auto-selected. Multiple search panels can be open simultaneously, each with persistent state. Gestures: the scroll ring (a circling motion with no fixed center, eyes-free; large/fast circles mean fast scroll, small/controlled mean slow scroll; made the "dominant" gesture that locks in until pen-up after early tests found the linear-drag combination too sensitive); vertical drag scrubs focus; horizontal stroke left opens a result; horizontal stroke right launches a sideways search from title, date, or author. The time-cost ladder is stated as a design goal: record the thought to search about 1 s; trigger a search and see a list about 5 s; open a familiar document, drag out its icon, or capture a snapshot 10-20 s; scroll a larger set, filter, or revise a query 20 s to 2 min; inspect unfamiliar documents or sideways searches several minutes. [C37055]

**Computational mechanism:** Evaluation was qualitative and formative, not a controlled experiment with timings -- that must be stated, not presented as measured performance. 12 computer-science graduate students, ages 25-35, 9 male 3 female, all Microsoft interns; 5 in early-stage evaluations (paper prototyping with cut-outs; the Phlat desktop search tool was found tedious -- an "extra step" with context switching), 4 in usability round 1, 3 in round 2. Round 1: none of the 4 discovered the scroll ring, stroking across filters, or horizontal dragging on their own. The highlighter hints were added after round 1, when hover tooltips failed completely (one user thought they were pop-up menus to tap): gestural hints drawn as if with a thick orange chisel marker, hand-printed labels with a bright yellow glow, shown by default when the panel first opens; tapping Close Tips animates them into the question-mark icon so they can be recalled. Round 2 (with highlighter hints): all 3 discovered the scroll ring, stroking across filters, excluding items, and dragging left to open a document, with no experimenter help; all 3 named the scroll ring a favorite feature. Two of 3 still did not discover dragging out document icons or initially notice the tracking menu (those functions had no hints yet). Users valued breaking searches into episodes ("During brainstorming I don't have time to perform searches right away...", round-1 user) and gathering content into notes. Fig. 5, viewed as pixels, is a photograph of the paper-prototyping setup: a Tablet PC on a table covered with cut-out interface elements. [C37056]

**Takeaway:** A question left on the page is worth more than a search box opened and closed. [C37057]

## The arc: the system must not make the human wait

Twenty-two years of work, one sustained argument: the system should never make the human wait for it to finish. The early work (2004-2007, Toronto PhD, then Microsoft Research) works on the hand: the pen, the stroke, the menu. Simple vs. Compound Mark Menus (2004) and Zone and Polygon Menus (2006) ask how much choice a single gesture can carry -- breadth 8 by orientation alone, breadth 16 or 32 once the start position of the stroke is allowed to speak, 512 items once the pen is allowed to lift. Elastic Hierarchies (2005) asks the same question of a picture: how much of a tree can one view hold before it must change its form, and can the form change locally, node by node, instead of all at once? Phosphor (2006) is the pivot of the early period: it stops asking the user to watch a transition happen and instead shows the outcome at once and leaves the explanation behind as a fading diagram -- time projected into space. earPod (2007) and InkSeine (2007) move the same instinct off the screen edge: the ear replaces the eye, the ink itself becomes the query, and the system must react to the user rather than making the user wait for it. The middle period (2011-2014, NUS, with Rubaiat Habib Kazi) turns from menus to making. Vignette (2012) and Draco (2014) are both built from a close reading of how artists actually work -- 56 pen-and-ink illustrations by 32 artists for Vignette, YouTube tutorials for Flash, Maya, 3DS Max and After Effects plus two professional animators for Draco -- and both hand the artist a small example (a patch of strokes, a patch of objects) and let the system do the tedious repetition: fill the region, keep the motion going. The unit of authorship shrinks to the example; the system supplies the multitude. The late period (2018-2026) removes the eye altogether. EDITalk (2018) and Commanding and Re-Dictation (2020) build voice editing around a single temporal fact -- 98 percent of corrections arrive within an average of 1.4 seconds of the fault being read aloud -- and around the user's own phrasing rather than a command syntax. Heads-Up Computing (2023) generalizes the whole trajectory into a paradigm: sense what the body has free, estimate what each channel can carry, and deliver only through the channels that are idle. The recent listings (ViFeed, DIMIRA-HUC, PersonaMail, CRAFT, ColoGene, 2025-2026) extend the same move into eating, email, fiction writing on smart glasses, and color -- none read beyond listings in this leg. The through-line: Zhao never asks the user to adapt to the system's clock. He moves the explanation after the event (Phosphor), the choice into the stroke's start point (Zone/Polygon), the repetition into the example (Vignette, Draco), and the correction into the 1.4 seconds after the fault is heard (EDITalk, Commanding). Each paper finds one moment where the system was making the human wait, and removes the wait. [C37058]

## The aesthetic logic

Zhao's systems share a visual grammar that is directly portable: the correction is always a visible thing, and the system always shows its work. Phosphor's afterglow is the correction made visible as a fading diagram; the simple-mark menu's lifted pen is a second chance built into the timing; the breadcrumb is a question left on the page where it can be answered later; the kinetic texture's patch is the example the system is not allowed to forget. Three intervals recur across the whole corpus and give a piece its tempo: the 300 ms novice fade-in (Zone/Polygon), the 1-2 s inter-mark timeout (Simple vs. Compound), and the 1.4 s barge-in window (Commanding) -- roughly a second to a second and a half in which a human decides, corrects, or moves on. Nothing is hidden behind a black box; the computation shows its work as a trace, a second chance, or a deferred question. That is why his mechanisms transfer to generative pieces so cleanly: each one is already a small machine for letting a human interrupt, correct, or complete a system -- and the piece is that machine running on new material. [C37059]
