Luepertz is the painter who was thrown out of the academy and then ran it for twenty-one years. Born in Reichenberg in 1941, in the Sudetenland then annexed by Germany, he moved with his family to Rheydt in the Rhineland in 1948, was dismissed from an apprenticeship painting wine-bottle labels for an alleged lack of talent, studied at the Werkkunstschule Krefeld from 1956 to 1961, and spent one semester at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf before a physical conflict ended in his exmatriculation -- he remembered himself as "an unloved, as an outcast" expelled from the house. He joined the French Foreign Legion and deserted before he could be sent to Algeria. The expulsion is not biography decoration: it is the reason the whole career reads as a man who learned the academy's language from outside it and then, once inside as rector, used the institution to protect the freedom he had been denied. [C26615]

The painting began in Berlin in 1962, when he moved there to avoid military service. In 1964 he co-founded the self-help gallery Grossgoerschen 35, and the same year held his exhibition of "Dithyrambic Paintings", a term taken from Friedrich Nietzsche, combining the opposites of objectivity and abstraction into a synthesis; the "Dithyrambic Manifesto" followed in 1966 and a second manifesto, "The Grace of the Twentieth Century", in 1968. The early series ran on cartoon figures -- Mickey Mouse from 1962, Donald Duck from 1963 -- abstracted almost to non-recognizability, so that little remains of the duck except two wide-open eyes and the contours of what might be the bill. From 1969 to 1977 he painted predominantly German motifs: steel helmets, shovels, flags and monumental antlers in large formats, in earthy colours, thematising the unmanaged German national pathos and evoking memories of the Third Reich era. A helmet fascinated him as a person, he said, and he is not responsible for the history behind the helmet; the helmet tells its own story. [C26616]

The middle years turned away from the motif and then back to it through other painters. From 1977 to 1984 came the style pictures, almost completely free of motifs, playing with surface and volume-forming forms; from 1985 to 1990 he reinterpreted Corot and Poussin. He has cultivated sculpture and designed stage sets since 1980, took part in documenta 7 in 1982, held a professorship at the Summer Academy in Salzburg from 1983, and taught painting at the Academy of Fine Arts in Karlsruhe from 1974 (some sources say 1976) until 1986, when he took a professorship at the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf and was appointed rector in 1988. The 1990s belong to the human figure: the picture series "Men without women - Parsifal" (1993-1997), an extensive series adhering to a single theme, the frontal male face, often depicted as crying, referring to Wagner's Parsifal and to the loneliness of the painter in the studio; landscape painting from 1997; the cycles "Vanitas" (1999) and "Vesper" (2000). Public commissions anchor the later career: the mural "The Six Virtues" for the foyer of the new Federal Chancellery in Berlin (2001), on which nothing is visible except colours, after Cesare Ripa's "Iconologia"; "The Fallen Warrior", a three-metre bronze in the Berlin Kantstrasse (1994); and the monumental Hercules, 18 metres high and 23 tons, placed on the tower of the Zeche Nordstern in Gelsenkirchen at the close of RUHR.2010. [C26617]

The rectorship is the long second half of the story, and it is also the doorway. Luepertz led the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf from 1988 to 2009, more than twenty years, and was succeeded by Tony Cragg in June 2009. He filled vacancies with internationally known artists -- A. R. Penck, Jannis Kounellis, Rosemarie Trockel, Jorg Immendorff, Albert Oehlen, Peter Doig and Tony Cragg among them -- and he is credited with upholding the academy's autonomy against trends seeking to standardise higher education into less independent, school-like instruction, in effect re-establishing the academy for the new millennium. The late work stays classical but dialogic: the Arcadia paintings of 2013-2017, the Achilles sculpture of 2014, and the Pierre Puvis de Chavannes responses of 2013-2023, in which he cites, subtracts from and interrupts 19th-century classicism with flat modernist rectangles, erased faces and painted frames. The 2017 twin United States survey -- "Threads of History" at the Hirshhorn (25 May to 10 September 2017, more than 30 large-scale paintings from 1962-1975, including the 40-foot "Westwall" on view for the first time in the US) and the full-career survey at the Phillips Collection (27 May to 3 September 2017, nearly 50 paintings) -- was his first major US museum retrospective. With solo exhibitions in 2024 and into early 2025 and a group show "Konferenz" at Schloss Derneburg from May 2026, and no obituary or death notice found, he is living as of November 2026. [C26618]

## M1 -- Dithyrambic painting: one motif, isolated and magnified

The mechanism is stated in the name. Dithyrambic painting, developed in Berlin from 1962 and exhibited in 1964, takes one simple representational motif -- a log, a railroad track, a moth, a waterlily, a helmet, a cap -- and isolates it, enlarges it to monumental scale, and lets it hold the whole picture. "Baumstamm Dithyrambisch" (1966, oil on canvas, 305 x 114 cm) is a tall narrow log filling a canvas more than three metres high; "Eisenbahnschienen-Motte" (1969, oil on canvas, 192 x 250 cm) joins railroad tracks and a moth in one title, the tracks not a realistic representation but a formal cipher, a rhythmically arranged structure. The dithyramb, after Nietzsche, is the synthesis of objectivity and abstraction: the motif stays legible, but the painting is not about the motif, it is about what the motif can carry when it is the only thing in the room. The same isolation runs through the whole career -- the duck, the helmet, the face, the hero -- and the dithyramb is the first statement of it.

**Takeaway:** pick one motif, take everything else away, and make it big enough to carry the picture alone. [C26619]

## M2 -- The German motif: helmet, flag, wall

From 1969 to 1977 Luepertz painted predominantly German motifs -- symbolic objects such as steel helmets, shovels, flags and monumental antlers in large formats, executed in earthy colours -- and the mechanism is confrontation without commentary. "Helme sinkend" (1970) is a helmet painting from the Stoffel Collection; "Westwall" (1968), 40 feet long, treats the supposedly impenetrable line of bunkers along Germany's western border more like an earthwork than a wartime embankment; "Schwarz Rot Gold I" (1974) names the German national colours in its title and assembles helmets, arrows, snails and wheat fields as military emblems. He does not paint the war and he does not paint the guilt; he paints the object the war left behind, at a scale that will not let you walk past it, and lets the object accuse itself. The helmet tells its own story, and the painter is not responsible for the history behind it.

**Takeaway:** never paint the event; paint the object the event left behind, at a scale that will not let the viewer walk past it. [C26620]

## M3 -- Serial repetition: the same motif, again, until it changes

Luepertz works in series and versions on the same theme-problem, isolating a motif in order to resolve it. The Donald Duck series runs from 1963, the dithyrambic titles repeat the same epithet for a decade, the Parsifal series runs 1993-1997 across paintings, prints, works on paper and bronze reliefs, and the Puvis responses run 2013-2023. The repetition is not a habit, it is the method: the Museo Reina Sofia describes the 1976-1984 style-painting phase as series and versions on the same theme-problem, and the Parsifal bronzes make the point literally -- 24 reliefs numbered in Arabic numerals and 24 in Roman numerals, each unique through its individual paintwork. The motif does not change; the handling does, and the series is the record of what the handling found.

**Takeaway:** repeat one motif until the repetition itself becomes the picture; vary the handling, not the subject. [C26621]

## M4 -- The crying face: Parsifal

The 1990s mechanism is the frontal male face, often depicted as crying. "Men without women - Parsifal" (1993-1997) adheres to a single theme: the face front-on, filling the frame, the tears doing the work that a body would do in a history painting. The series refers to Wagner's Parsifal -- female temptation and salvation in a man's world -- and to the loneliness of the painter in the studio. The dated works show the range of media the face survives: a painting of 1993, the painted bronze relief of 1994 (173 x 173 x 6 cm, one of 48, each unique through its paintwork), a four-colour etching of 1995 (support 80 x 60 cm), a gouache, ink, oil stick and watercolour work on paper of 1997 (44.5 x 31.8 cm). The face is never given a setting, a narrative or a companion; it is alone, facing out, and crying. The loneliness is the subject, and the face is the only evidence of it.

**Takeaway:** put one face front-on and let it cry; the face is the whole picture, and the tears are the only story it needs. [C26622]

## M5 -- Monumental sculpture: the hero in public

Sculpture, cultivated since 1980, is the same isolation mechanism at civic scale. "The Fallen Warrior" (1994) is a three-metre-long bronze of a fallen warrior with helmet and shield, inspired by an ancient Greek sculpture, lying in the Berlin Kantstrasse; the Apollo for the Alte Oper in Frankfurt (1989) stands in a niche with his right arm bent, grasping his left shoulder as he reaches for an arrow, six casts made; "The Ugly Scares the Beauty" (1990) is a bridge sculpture in Karlsruhe; "Herkules" (2010) is 18 metres high and 23 tons, cast aluminium in 244 parts, standing on the tower of the former Nordstern coal mine in Gelsenkirchen so that statue and tower together reach about 100 metres. The Kewenig account of the Hercules bozzetti says the hero is triumphant but also broken and scarred, a sensitive, vulnerable human god, holding a club recalling the Farnese Hercules in one hand and an apple of the Hesperides in the other. The classical hero arrives in public space already damaged, and the damage is the point: monumentality without triumph.

**Takeaway:** put the hero in public at a scale nobody can ignore, then break him; a wounded monument is truer than a triumphant one. [C26623]

## M6 -- Colour as meaning: the Six Virtues

The mural "The Six Virtues" (2001) for the foyer of the new Federal Chancellery in Berlin is the purest statement of Luepertz's colour thinking: on the monochrome paintings nothing is visible except colours. The main source was Cesare Ripa's "Iconologia", drawing on ancient iconological concepts that assigned the colours of the ruler's virtues. The mechanism is colour as a code with a public meaning: the virtues are not depicted, they are not personified, they are not even named in the picture -- they are present as colours, and the colour is the virtue. The same Chancellery foyer holds his bronze "The Philosopher", a monumental female nude from 1998 placed there in 2001, so the colour-code mural and the body share one room: the body is the figure, the colour is the idea, and neither illustrates the other.

**Takeaway:** let colour carry the meaning alone; a virtue painted as a colour is more public than a virtue painted as a person. [C26624]

## M7 -- Vanitas: death in the picture

Death enters Luepertz's painting through the vanitas, and it enters twice under the same title. The print suite "Vanitas" (1992) is six sheets in etching, aquatint, spit-bite, drypoint and plate-tone in brown and black ink, each plate about 41.3 x 59.5 cm; the painting cycle "Vanitas" follows in 1999. The Museo Reina Sofia text cites "Schwarz Rot Gold I" as the example of the issue of death becoming established in his painting, as in vanitas -- the military emblems, helmets and arrows, are already a vanitas, a display of the things that outlive the men who wore them. The mechanism is the same as M2 with the emphasis shifted: the object is not only the history, it is the mortality. The helmet, the flag, the antler and the skull are all the same kind of thing -- a hard object that survives the soft body.

**Takeaway:** paint the thing that outlives the man; the helmet is a skull that has not admitted it yet. [C26625]

## M8 -- The painted frame and the interrupted classic

The late work answers 19th-century classicism by interrupting it. "Student" (2020, mixed media on canvas in the artist's frame, two parts, 100 x 163 cm) directly cites two figures from Pierre Puvis de Chavannes's "Saint Genevieve as a Child in Prayer" (1879), and the title signals how Luepertz views his relationship with Puvis: he is the student, and the student answers back. In several of these late works faces are erased, figures are pushed to the edges, and his self-made, splodgily daubed, camouflage-like frames disrupt the boundary of the image. The frame is not a border, it is an intervention: the picture quotes the classic, and the frame argues with the quotation. The same interruption runs back through the Corot and Poussin reinterpretations of 1985-1990 -- the master is not copied, he is answered, and the answer is allowed to damage the original.

**Takeaway:** quote the classic, then interrupt it; erase a face, push a figure to the edge, and let the frame argue with the picture. [C26626]

## M9 -- The rector who rebuilt the academy by bringing people in

The institutional mechanism is the mirror of the painting mechanism: isolate, enlarge, repeat -- but with people. As rector of the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf from 1988 to 2009, Luepertz brought internationally renowned artists to the academy: A. R. Penck (professor of painting 1988-2003), Jorg Immendorff (professor 1996-2007), Albert Oehlen (professor of painting 2000-2009), alongside Jannis Kounellis, Rosemarie Trockel, Peter Doig and Tony Cragg. The sources say he "brought" or "attracted" them; no source shows him personally signing their appointments, and German state academy professorships are not a rector's unilateral appointment, so the claim is "brought during his rectorship", not "appointed by Luepertz". His educational ideal was allowing artistic personalities to develop in complete freedom, and he defended the academy's autonomy against standardisation into less independent, school-like instruction. The expelled student rebuilt the house he was thrown out of by filling it with people who would not have fitted the old one.

**Takeaway:** build the institution the way you build the picture: bring in the people who do not fit, and protect their freedom to stay that way. [C26627]

## M10 -- Painting as an answer to painting

The through-line of the whole career is dialogue with earlier painting. The dithyrambs answer Nietzsche; the German motifs answer the history Germany would not discuss; the style pictures answer the abstract painting of the 1950s; the Corot and Poussin reinterpretations of 1985-1990 answer the masters directly; the Puvis responses of 2013-2023 answer a 19th-century classicist with flat modernist rectangles and erased faces; the church windows -- Nevers (1989-90), St. Andreas in Cologne (2007, with five more in 2010), Lippstadt (2017), Bamberg (2019-2022), Hanover (2023), Regensburg (since 2023) -- answer stained glass with a painter's colour. Even the magazine "Frau und Hund", published since 2003 with his own poetry and prose, is an answer in another medium. Luepertz does not invent subjects; he takes a subject that already exists -- a duck, a helmet, a hero, a master -- and answers it until the answer becomes his own picture.

**Takeaway:** never invent a subject; take one that already exists and answer it until the answer is yours. [C26628]

## Aesthetic logic

Luepertz's logic is isolation at scale. One motif -- a duck, a log, a helmet, a face, a hero -- is taken out of its context, enlarged until it fills the picture, and repeated in series until the repetition finds what the single image could not. The early work does it with cartoon figures and German objects in earthy colours; the middle work does it with style pictures and masters; the late work does it with classical heroes in public space and with Puvis figures interrupted by painted frames. The colour is never decoration: in the German motifs it is the earth of the history, in the Six Virtues it is the virtue itself, in the Parsifal bronzes it is what makes each of the 48 reliefs unique. The monumentality is not triumphal -- the fallen warrior lies down, the Hercules is broken and scarred, the crying face is alone -- and that is the point: Luepertz's monument is a monument to the thing that survives, not to the thing that won. The rector mirrors the painter: the academy, like the picture, is a room with one thing in it, and the thing is protected until it can stand on its own. [C26629]
