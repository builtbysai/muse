## The life: the anthologist who became the historian of how pictures make history

Stephen Bann was born on 1 August 1942 in Manchester, England. (C12100, leg 1 Sec. 2) He was educated at Winchester College, where he met Philip Steadman as a schoolboy, and at King's College, Cambridge, where he was a student from 1960 to 1966 and gained his PhD in 1967, as a student of the historian Herbert Butterfield. (C12100, C12104, C12105, leg 1 Sec. 2; C12211, leg 3) He turned 84 on 1 August 2026 and is alive as of November 2026: he gave a public talk in London in December 2023 and was listed as a speaker at a Cambridge symposium on 30 November 2024; no obituary was found. (C12115, leg 1 Sec. 2) No source found in this run gives his parents, spouse or children, and none is stated here; the Debrett's entry returned HTTP 403. (C12100, C12133, leg 1 Sec. 2) His PhD thesis title and subject were not established. (C12105, leg 1 Sec. 2)

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is the move from editing the avant-garde to reading the nineteenth century: the work starts as concrete poetry and kinetic art, becomes the editing of theory and constructivism, turns to the museum and the representation of history, and ends as curation and a return to English subject matter.

(1) Cambridge and the avant-garde, 1964-67. In the summer of 1964 Bann met the concrete poet Ian Hamilton Finlay in Edinburgh, together with Weaver. (C12105, C12114, leg 1 Sec. 3) In November 1964 he co-organised, with Weaver and Reg Gadney, the first international exhibition of concrete, kinetic and phonic poetry in Britain, in the Rushmore Rooms, St Catharine's College, Cambridge, 28 November - 5 December 1964. (C12105, leg 1 Sec. 3; = #658) He lived in Paris from 1964 to mid-1965. (C12122, leg 1 Sec. 3) In 1966 he co-authored Four Essays on Kinetic Art with Gadney, Frank Popper and Steadman, and co-founded Form magazine with Steadman and Weaver. (C12100, C12104, leg 1 Sec. 3) Form ran quarterly from summer 1966 until October 1969, when it ceased after ten issues (eleven per Greg Thomas), financial trouble and the withdrawal of an Arts Council grant being the cause; Bann was a co-editor throughout, Steadman the primary editor, Weaver the American coeditor. (C12104, leg 1 Sec. 4; C12151, leg 2 W01) In 1967 he edited Concrete Poetry: An International Anthology (London Magazine Editions), one of the three major concrete-poetry anthologies of the late 1960s, alongside those of Emmett Williams and Mary Ellen Solt. (C12153, leg 2 W03; C12207, leg 3)

(2) Kent, 1967-2000. Bann joined the University of Kent in 1967 as Lecturer in History, then Senior Lecturer (1975-1980), Reader in Modern Cultural Studies (1980-1988), and Professor of Modern Cultural Studies (1988-2000): thirty-three years at one university. (C12201, leg 3) The Kent years are the editorial and theoretical middle: Russian Formalism, edited with J. E. Bowlt (1973); The Tradition of Constructivism, edited (1974); the exhibition Constructive Context, selected from the Arts Council Collection (1978); and the turn to historical representation with The Clothing of Clio (1984), The True Vine (1989) and The Inventions of History (1990). (C12153, C12166, leg 2) He also translated in this period: Roland Barthes's essays for Form, including "The Activity of Structuralism", and later Barthes's The Discourse of History; Julia Kristeva's Proust and the Sense of Time (1993); and Frank Popper's Origins and Development of Kinetic Art (1968). (C12204, C12215, leg 3; C12208, C12209, leg 3)

(3) Bristol, 2000-2008, and emeritus. In 2000 Bann was appointed Professor of History of Art at the University of Bristol, where he remained until retirement in 2008, then Emeritus Professor of History of Art and Senior Research Fellow, a post he still holds. (C12201, leg 3) The Bristol years are the French print-culture project and the public exhibitions: Parallel Lines (2001), Ways Around Modernism (2006), The Coral Mind, edited (2007), the National Gallery exhibition Painting History: Delaroche and Lady Jane Grey, co-curated with Linda Whiteley (24 February - 23 May 2010), Distinguished Images (2013), and L'Invention du passe, directed at Lyon (19 April - 21 July 2014). (C12108, C12109, leg 1 Sec. 6; C12171, leg 2 W18) Since 2008 he has served as a guest curator for major exhibitions at the National Gallery, London, and the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Lyon. (C12154, leg 2)

(4) Honours and the Finlay thread. Bann was elected a Fellow of the British Academy in 1998 and made a Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 2004; he was a Fellow of the Society of Antiquaries, held a Leverhulme Emeritus Fellowship in 2009, joined the Academia Europaea in 2014, and was President of the Comite international d'histoire de l'art from 2000 to 2004. (C12100, C12102, C12202, C12203, leg 3) Visiting posts include the Canadian Centre for Architecture (2003), Safra Professor at CASVA, Washington (2005), Dumbarton Oaks (2009), and the Yale Center for British Art (2011-2014). (C12102, leg 1 Sec. 6) The Finlay thread runs the whole length of the career: from the 1964 meeting, through a private collection of Finlay's work shown at Kettle's Yard as Beauty and Revolution (6 December 2014 - 1 March 2015), to editing Midway (2014) and Stonypath Days (2016), the volumes of Finlay's letters to Bann, and making a 16mm film, Stonypath Days, about Little Sparta; he is a former trustee of the Little Sparta Trust. (C12109, C12110, C12123, C12128, C12129, leg 1 Sec. 6) A Festschrift, About Stephen Bann, edited by Deborah Cherry, appeared in 2006, from Art History vol. 28 no. 5 (November 2005), and "The Stephen Bann Effect" conference was held at Bristol on 20-21 June 2009, with fourteen former research students. (C12116, C12118, leg 1 Sec. 6) He is also, unusually for a historian, a maker of a small body of visual work: Tate holds eight of his concrete-poem works, dated 1964-1982, including Orange (1964) and Fleece (1967). (C12106, leg 1 Sec. 6)

## Mechanism M1: concrete poetry as a visual system -- the poem is seen before it is read

Concrete poetry arranges words as shapes and letters as building blocks, so the poem is seen before it is read. Bann's route into it was practical, not theoretical: he co-organised the 1964 Cambridge exhibition of concrete, kinetic and phonic poetry, co-edited Form, and edited Concrete Poetry: An International Anthology (1967), one of the three major anthologies of the moment. (C12105, leg 1 Sec. 3; C12151, C12153, leg 2) He also made the things himself: Tate holds eight of his concrete-poem works, 1964-1982, including Orange (1964) and Fleece (1967). (C12106, leg 1 Sec. 6) The mechanism is the word treated as a visual unit: spacing, line-break and typeface carry the meaning before the sentence does, and the anthology is the system that lets one poet's arrangement sit beside another's, so the reader compares shapes, not just texts.

**Takeaway:** the word is a shape first and a sentence second. A piece built on this mechanism must arrange words as visible units -- letters as blocks, spacing as structure -- and let the reader see the poem before reading it, with the arrangement itself doing the work.

## Mechanism M2: kinetic art as moving structure -- the viewer completes the work

Kinetic art changes in time, and the viewer's movement completes it. Four Essays on Kinetic Art (1966), co-authored with Gadney, Popper and Steadman, grew directly out of the same Cambridge circle as the 1964 exhibition, which was explicitly an exhibition of concrete, kinetic and phonic poetry together. (C12153, leg 2 W02; C12151, leg 2) The mechanism is structure in motion: a work whose form is not fixed but produced by movement, whether the movement of the object, the light, or the viewer walking past it. Bann's contribution was to write about it and to edit the magazine that carried it, so the mechanism arrives here as an editorial act: the kinetic work needs a text that tells the reader the movement is the work, not a defect in it.

**Takeaway:** movement is the structure, not the decoration. A piece on this mechanism must change over time in a way the viewer can drive -- by moving, by waiting, by returning -- and must never present a still frame as the work.

## Mechanism M3: historical-mindedness -- pictures make the past, they do not record it

The long middle of Bann's career argues that images, museums, collections and monuments shape a collective understanding of history rather than documenting it. The Clothing of Clio (1984) applies structuralist and rhetorical analysis to pictures, houses, museums and history books; The True Vine (1989) studies visual representation and the Western tradition; The Inventions of History (1990) collects essays on the structures that made an integrated regime of historical representation possible; Romanticism and the Rise of History (1995) closes by saying that writing the history of the past is the most productive, and perhaps the only, way of writing the history of the present. (C12156, leg 2 W08; C12153, leg 2 W09; C12157, leg 2 W10; C12179, leg 2 W13) The mechanism is the picture as an argument about the past: a history painting, a museum hang, a monument does not illustrate a history that already exists somewhere else, it makes the history its viewers carry away.

**Takeaway:** the picture is the history, not the illustration of it. A piece on this mechanism must show an image that argues a version of the past -- and must let the viewer see that the argument changes when the image changes, not when the facts do.

## Mechanism M4: intermediality -- the image in its unlike double

A painting reappears as an engraving, a lithograph or a photograph, and each version changes the image. Parallel Lines (2001) is the most extended exposition of this: Delaroche's Hemicycle mural of 1841 and its celebrated three-part engraving by Louis Henriquel-Dupont, exhibited in 1853, are the same image in two unlike media, and the engraving is not a copy but a second version with its own argument. (C12158, leg 2 W15; C12179, leg 2 W15) Distinguished Images (2013) carries the same mechanism across the whole French nineteenth century, with Mona Lisa reproductions and their part in popularising the painting. (C12159, leg 2 W19) The mechanism is the image surviving translation: what carries across media is not the likeness but the structure, and what changes is the meaning -- the engraving is slower, more public, more monumental than the painting it doubles.

**Takeaway:** every copy is a second version. A piece on this mechanism must show one image in two or more media at once -- painting and print, print and photograph -- and let the viewer see what each medium adds and what it takes away.

## Mechanism M5: the cabinet of curiosity -- a collector's objects as self-definition

Under the Sign (1994) reads the seventeenth-century collector John Bargrave through his cabinet of curiosity: the objects he travelled to acquire, and the order he put them in, are an aid in the self-definition of the collector, bringing together travel and acquisition to detect meaning. (C12150, leg 2; C12178, leg 2 W12) The mechanism is the collection as a portrait: the collector is not described by a biography but by what he kept, where he got it, and what he put next to what. Bann's own life rhymes with it: his private collection of Finlay's work, shown at Kettle's Yard in 2014, is a collector's portrait of a sixty-year friendship. (C12110, leg 1 Sec. 6) The cabinet is a small system with a large claim -- that a life can be read from its objects, in the order they were gathered.

**Takeaway:** the collection is the portrait. A piece on this mechanism must let a set of gathered objects stand for a person -- travel, acquisition and arrangement as biography -- and must let the order of the objects be the argument about who gathered them.

## Mechanism M6: the visual economy of print -- media feeding each other

Distinguished Images (2013) surveys lithography, etching and engraving as a whole rather than as separate histories of individual processes, and asks how the media relate inside one overall visual economy; Parallel Lines (2001) argues that rising reproductive media did not supplant traditional modes, and that printmakers, photographers and painters influenced and inspired each other's work. (C12159, leg 2 W19; C12158, leg 2 W15) The mechanism is an economy, not a hierarchy: the print is not a lesser painting, the photograph is not a better print; each medium feeds the others, and the system is the traffic between them -- a painting becomes an engraving, the engraving becomes a photograph, the photograph becomes a painting again. The book's question, "Is lithography an art?", is the mechanism stated as a provocation: the answer depends on where the work sits in the economy, not on the medium alone. (C12159, leg 2 W19)

**Takeaway:** the traffic between media is the system. A piece on this mechanism must show images moving between media -- drawn, printed, photographed -- and must make the movement visible as the work, not as a sequence of separate objects.

## Mechanism M7: the garden as text -- a landscape read by walking

The Finlay thread gives Bann a mechanism no book of his states directly: the garden as a language of words, stones and paths, read by walking. Little Sparta, the garden Finlay created with his wife Sue in the Pentland Hills from 1966, is a landscape in which inscriptions, stones and planting carry meaning the way sentences do; Bann cited Finlay in his own writing on the history of gardens, edited the two volumes of Finlay's letters to him (Midway, 2014; Stonypath Days, 2016), made a 16mm film, Stonypath Days, about Little Sparta, and is a former trustee of the Little Sparta Trust. (C12235, leg 3; C12123, C12128, C12129, leg 1 Sec. 6) The mechanism is the landscape as a text that can only be read on foot: the path is the sentence, the stone is the word, and the reader's position in the garden is the reader's position in the argument. It is the only mechanism in this study that Bann practised as a filmmaker and trustee rather than as a writer.

**Takeaway:** the path is the sentence. A piece on this mechanism must make a landscape that reads -- words set in stone, a route that is an argument -- and must make the viewer walk it, because the meaning is in the walking, not in the view.

## Mechanism M8: exhibition as argument -- the show is the thesis

A show makes a historical claim by hanging, sequence and catalogue, not just by display. Constructive Context (1978), selected by Bann from the Arts Council Collection and toured to ten venues, is the early form: the selection is the argument. (C12166, leg 2 W07) Painting History: Delaroche and Lady Jane Grey (National Gallery, 24 February - 23 May 2010), co-curated with Linda Whiteley, is the late form: a single painting, The Execution of Lady Jane Grey (1833), is put at the centre of a historical claim about Delaroche, and the catalogue is the claim in book form. (C12171, leg 2 W18) L'Invention du passe at Lyon (2014) extends the same method to a whole museum. (C12176, leg 2) Bann's own phrase for the method is the "poetics of the museum": the hang is a sentence, the sequence is a paragraph, and the visitor reads the exhibition the way a reader reads a book -- except that the visitor can also walk out of order, and the argument has to survive that. (C12154, leg 2)

**Takeaway:** the hang is the thesis. A piece on this mechanism must make its argument by hanging and sequencing -- a show whose order the viewer can walk through or rearrange -- and whose claim changes when the order does.

## Aesthetic logic

Bann has no recognisable style, and that is the point three times over: he is not primarily an author of objects at all, he is the man who arranges other people's work and then reads what the arrangement says. The continuity is not a look but a method, and the method has three moves that repeat across the whole career. First, gather: found the magazine, edit the anthology, translate the theory, collect the friend's work -- bring the material together. Second, arrange: put the poems in an order, put the essays in a volume, put the prints in a sequence, hang the paintings in a room -- the order is the argument. Third, read: write the book that says what the arrangement means, and let the reading change when the arrangement changes. The materials are the cheapest communication systems that travel: a quarterly magazine, an anthology, a translated essay, a 304-page monograph, a museum hang, a garden path. The tone is scholarly and patient where Weaver's is stubborn and Mayor's is funny: Bann's books take fifty years to say that pictures make history, and they say it by re-reading the same pictures until they mean something else. The scale moves from a single concrete poem to a ten-issue magazine to a three-venue international survey to a garden in the Pentland Hills, and the durability moves from a stapled magazine that can be lost to books that are still cited twenty years after the last one -- but the hand stays the same: the hand that edits the anthology, arranges the exhibition, and writes the reading. A piece built from this study should feel gathered, arranged and read -- words set as shapes, images shown in their unlike doubles, a collection hung as a portrait -- and should never look like a solo artwork pretending to be a system, or a system pretending to have a single author. (C12258)

## The network

The origin node is the magazine, and Bann is the co-editor who stayed with the network for sixty years while the others moved on. The strongest links are the co-editors, the poet, and the co-authors.
