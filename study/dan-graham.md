Dan Graham was born on 31 March 1942 in Urbana, Illinois, the son of a chemist and an educational psychologist. When he was 3 the family moved to Winfield Township, New Jersey, and when he was 14 to nearby Westfield. He had no formal education after high school and was self-educated, reading widely as a teenager in Margaret Mead, Claude Levi-Strauss, the critic Leslie Fiedler and the French Nouveau Roman writers; his mother worked with children, often in playgrounds, and Graham later said that background fed his interest in how people respond to spatial environments. He moved to New York City in 1963 and began a career as a writer, publishing criticism on art, architecture, television, music and self-awareness. In 1964, at 22, he founded the John Daniels Gallery in New York with two friends, acting as its director and curator; the gallery showed Carl Andre, Dan Flavin, Donald Judd, Sol LeWitt, Robert Morris and Robert Smithson, gave LeWitt his first solo gallery show, and closed after less than a year, in 1965, without having made a single sale. [C28162]

After the gallery closed, Graham began making his own work, and his first art was made for the magazine page rather than the gallery wall. Early pieces included Figurative (1965), Schema (1966), Side Effects/Common Drugs (1966) and Detumescence (1966), all built from photographs, numbers, words and typographic systems printed in magazines. His breakthrough was Homes for America (1966-67), a sequence of photographs of suburban single-family housing in New Jersey with a text on land use, economics and the obsolescence of architecture and craftsmanship, published as a two-page spread in Arts Magazine; Graham said the magazine format was deliberate, the work disposable and without monetary value, a hybrid of art criticism and essay. His 1968 work appeared in 0 to 9 magazine, the avant-garde journal of language and meaning-making. In the late 1960s he turned to performance, film and video, a shift tied to teaching posts that gave him access to equipment he could not afford himself: he taught at the University of California, San Diego in 1969 and then at the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design in Halifax until 1971, where he used local community television and staged pieces through TV, newspapers and astrology. His first film, Sunset to Sunrise (1969), moved the camera against the course of the sun; other films of the period were Two Correlated Rotations (1969), Roll (1970) and Body Press (1970-72), all using two cameras or two films and the body as a perceptual instrument. [C28163]

Through the 1970s Graham made performance and video installations that put the audience inside a feedback loop of watching and being watched, drawing on structural linguistics and Jacques Lacan. Two Consciousness Projection(s) (1972) and Past Future Split Attention (1972) staged live narration against live video images of the speaker; Time Delay Room (1974), Opposing Mirrors and Video Monitors on Time Delay (1974) and Present Continuous Past(s) (1974) used closed-circuit television with a tape delay, the last on an 8-second delay with the regress continuing in 8-second steps. His best-known performance, Performer/Audience/Mirror (1975), had Graham standing between a large mirror and a seated audience, describing himself, the audience and their reflections in continuous commentary; Public Space/Two Audiences (1976), made for the Venice Biennale, and Yesterday/Today (1975) extended the same inquiry into built installations. The date of Performer/Audience/Mirror is carried as 1975, per the joint gallery statement, UbuWeb and the Whitney work list, against 1977 in Wikipedia's performance section and Britannica. [C28164]

In the 1980s Graham turned to popular culture and music, becoming an active observer of New York punk and experimental music and befriending Glenn Branca and Sonic Youth. His video essay Rock My Religion (1982-84) parallels 18th-century Shaker circle dancing and ecstatic religion with hard-core punk and rock, and it gave him a cult following among younger artists. In 2004 he collaborated with the rock group Japanther and with Tony Oursler, Rodney Graham, Bruce Odland and Laurent P. Berger on the rock puppet opera Don't Trust Anyone Over Thirty, and he designed the stage set for Japanther at Performa in 2007. From the late 1970s onward his best-known work was the pavilion: freestanding, walk-in structures of plate glass, two-way mirror and steel armatures, derived from geometric forms, which he described as geometric forms inhabited and activated by the viewer's presence, producing uneasiness and psychological alienation through a constant play between inclusion and exclusion, intended as punctuation marks in physical space, places for romance, play, reading or watching videos. Early examples include Pavilion/Sculpture for Argonne (1978-81), Two Adjacent Pavilions (1981), Pavilion Sculpture II (1984), Octagon for Muenster (1987) and Two-Way Mirror with Hedge Labyrinth (1989); major commissions followed, Two-Way Mirror Cylinder Inside Cube and Video Salon (1981-91) with architects Mojdeh Baratloo and Clifton Balch on the Dia rooftop at 548 West 22nd Street, opened 12 September 1991 and publicly accessible until 2004, the Yin/Yang Pavilion at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, completed 2002 in Steven Holl's Simmons Hall, Hedge Two-Way Mirror Walkabout (2014) with landscape architect Gunther Vogt on the Metropolitan Museum of Art roof garden, and Child's Play in the Museum of Modern Art sculpture garden in 2017. He participated in documenta 5 (1972), 6 (1977), 7 (1982), 9 (1992) and 10 (1997), showed at the Venice Biennale in 1976, 2003 and 2005, and at Skulptur Projekte Muenster in 1987 and 1997. Two retrospectives framed his reputation: Dan Graham, Works 1965-2000, opened 2001 at the Museu Serralves in Porto and travelled to the Musee d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, the Kroller-Muller Museum in Otterlo and Kiasma in Helsinki; Dan Graham: Beyond, his first significant American retrospective, showed at MOCA Los Angeles, the Whitney Museum and the Walker Art Center in 2009-10. His books include Rock My Religion: Writings and Projects 1965-1990 (MIT Press, 1993/1994), Two-Way Mirror Power: Selected Writings by Dan Graham on His Art (MIT Press, 1999) and Video, Architecture, Television: Writings on Video and Video Works 1970-1978 (Press of the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design, 1979). He received the Coutts Contemporary Art Foundation Award in 1992 and the Skowhegan Medal for Mixed Media in 1992, the French Vermeil Medal awarded by the City of Paris in 2001, and election to the American Academy of Arts and Letters in 2010. He was married to the artist Mieko Meguro, who survived him. He died in New York City, in Manhattan, on 19 February 2022 at the age of 79; his four galleries (Lisson Gallery, Marian Goodman Gallery, 303 Gallery and Regen Projects) confirmed the death in a joint statement and no cause of death was announced. [C28165]

The twenty works inspected run 1965-2020 and trace one practice moving its audience from the page into the room and then into the park. Early, 1965-1967: the work is flat, printed and portable, and the audience is a reader turning pages -- Figurative (W01, 1965, printed 1968), Schema (W02, 1966-70), Homes for America (W03, 1966-67) and Detumescence (W04, 1966), the last the one C grade, thin and conflicting evidence on the advertisement's venue. Middle, 1970-1984: the work becomes a live apparatus in a room, built from cameras, monitors, mirrors and a performing body, and the audience is drafted as material -- Body Press (W05, 1970-72), Two Consciousness Projection(s) (W06, 1972), Past Future Split Attention (W07, 1972), Present Continuous Past(s) (W08, 1974), Opposing Mirrors and Video Monitors on Time Delay (W09, 1974, reinstalled 1993), Performer/Audience/Mirror (W10, 1975), Public Space/Two Audiences (W11, 1976), Rock My Religion (W12, 1982-84). Recent, 1981-2020: the apparatus leaves the gallery and becomes walk-in architecture in parks, on rooftops and on campuses, built from two-way mirror glass, steel, hedges, gravel and water -- Two-Way Mirror Cylinder Inside Cube and a Video Salon (W13, 1981-91), Octagon for Muenster (W14, 1987), Children's Pavilion with Jeff Wall (W15, 1989), Two-Way Mirror Punched Steel Hedge Labyrinth (W16, 1994-96), Yin/Yang Pavilion (W17, 1997-2002), Bisected Triangle, Interior Curve (W18, 2002), Hedge Two-Way Mirror Walkabout (W19, 2014), Neo-Baroque Walkway (W20, 2019-20). ## M1 -- The two-way mirror: seeing and being seen at once

A two-way mirror panel is transparent from the darker side and reflective from the brighter side, so the same sheet of glass shows a viewer their own face and, a step later, the person on the far side. In the pavilions the balance flips as the viewer walks, as clouds pass, and as bodies gather on one side, so a wall that was a window becomes a mirror and back again without any switch being thrown. The watchable behaviour is a slow oscillation: you watch others, then catch yourself watching, then realise others are watching you watch. Seen in W13, W16, W17, W18 and W19, and in the pavilion programme as a whole.

**Takeaway:** build the wall out of two-way mirror and let the light decide who sees whom; the flip is the work. [C28167]

## M2 -- The time-delay video loop: the just-past self

A camera feeds a monitor through a tape delay of 5 or 8 seconds, and mirrors fold the delayed image back into the camera, so each return is older by one delay step. The viewer sees their present body in the mirror and their 8-seconds-ago body on the monitor and a 16-seconds-ago body reflected from the monitor, and the three versions disagree about where the arms and head were. The watchable behaviour is a stutter of self-recognition: you wave, wait, and watch your own wave come back late, then watch it come back again. Seen in W08 (8-second delay, regress in 8-second steps) and W09 (delay reported as five seconds).

**Takeaway:** delay the image by seconds and mirror it back; the viewer meets their own just-past self. [C28168]

## M3 -- The performer/audience/mirror triangle: language as feedback

One person describes what they and others are doing, in the present tense, while everyone looks at the speaker and at their shared reflection. Turning to face the mirror reverses the triangle, so the describer joins the described group and the audience watches itself being described. The watchable behaviour is a group becoming self-conscious in stages: first they listen, then they notice they are being watched, then they watch themselves being watched. Seen in W10, where Graham faces the audience with his back to the mirror and describes his own movements and then individual audience members, and halfway through turns his back on the audience to face the mirror with them; and in W06, where the split runs three ways between inner thought, spoken description and the live image.

**Takeaway:** put one describer between the group and the mirror and let present-tense language be the feedback loop. [C28169]

## M4 -- The pavilion as optical instrument: a room that looks back

A pavilion is not a shelter with pictures on it but a device made of curved and flat two-way mirror panels, sometimes with a video salon or a pool or gravel floor, that takes the sky, the skyline and the visitors as its image. Walking through it changes the image continuously, since each step changes the angle of reflection and the balance of transparency, and the building behaves like a camera obscura the visitor stands inside. The watchable behaviour is a landscape that bends and doubles as you move, with strangers' reflections sliding across your own. Seen in W13 (the cylinder bends the city into a curved image while the cube holds the grid), W17 (one half in a shallow pool, the other in white raked gravel) and W18 (glass walls turning transparent or opaque as the light shifts).

**Takeaway:** build the pavilion as a camera, not a shelter; the skyline and the strangers are the picture. [C28170]

## M5 -- The hedge/labyrinth as social condenser: getting lost together

Hedges borrowed from Baroque mazes and from suburban garden fences are set against punched steel and two-way mirror glass, so the path forces strangers into the same narrow turns and dead ends. You hear voices you cannot see, glimpse a body through a hedge gap, then meet the same person face to face around a mirrored corner, and the delay between hearing and seeing stretches the encounter. The watchable behaviour is a small crowd repeatedly losing and finding itself, with the maze turning casual park strolling into a social game. Seen in W16, where the punched steel panels are semi-transparent at close range, adding a third state between seeing through and seeing oneself, and the hedges grow and change the work over seasons.

**Takeaway:** maze the hedge against the mirror; strangers lose each other, then find each other, then lose each other again. [C28171]

## M6 -- The magazine page as exhibition site: art that arrives by post

A work printed in a mass magazine reaches its audience without a gallery, a ticket or a wall, and it sits in sequence with advertisements, reviews and reproductions of other art. The reader meets it while looking for something else, and the work borrows meaning from whatever ad or article faces it on the opposite page. The watchable behaviour is reading as viewing: the eye moves across text and image in the order the magazine dictates, and the work changes with every reprinting because the surrounding pages change. Seen in W01, printed in Harper's Bazaar between advertisements, one side facing a bra advertisement and the other a tampon advertisement by Graham's own account; W02, a self-referential poem whose values an editor fills in differently at every printing; and W03, published as a two-page spread in Arts Magazine.

**Takeaway:** print the work where nobody expects art and let the facing page finish it; the magazine is the gallery. [C28172]

## M7 -- The cylinder and the curve: bending the reflection

A flat mirror returns a faithful image, but a cylinder or a sine-wave wall stretches, compresses and doubles it, and a concave panel pulls the sky and the skyline into an anamorphic smear. In the cylinder pavilion the curved inner wall turns the city into a panorama that wraps around the visitor, and in the walkway the wave walls turn a walking body into a rippling double. The watchable behaviour is a body and a skyline that refuse to hold still: lean left and your reflection thins, step forward and it splits. Seen in W13 (the cylinder inside the cube), W17 (concave and convex two-way mirrored glass giving anamorphic reflections of sky, landscape and other spectators) and W20 (convoluted sine-wave walls of two-way mirror glass doubling, stretching and dissolving the walker).

**Takeaway:** curve the mirror and the reflection stops behaving; the bend is the portrait. [C28173]

## M8 -- The public/private threshold: a wall you can see through

Graham's rooms and pavilions are built from the materials of corporate facades and suburban fences, and they stage the line between being inside and being outside as something you can walk through and test. A soundproof glass wall divides two audiences who can see but not hear each other, a hedge marks where a garden stops being public, and a sliding door lets a park visitor step from a crowded pavement into a quiet mirrored room. The watchable behaviour is threshold-testing: visitors pause at the edge, wave through the glass, enter, and then look back out at the street they just left. Seen in W11 (the two audiences trying to communicate by gesture and waving, the audience itself the exhibited commodity), W16 (the suburban hedge as the boundary between private property and public street) and W18 (a sliding door into a two-room pavilion on Broadway).

**Takeaway:** build the threshold out of glass and let visitors test it; the wall you can see through is the work. [C28174]

## M9 -- The child-scale and body-scale pavilion: the funhouse as instrument

Several pavilions are sized and themed for play: a funhouse with anamorphic mirrors, a children's pavilion built into a hill, a skateboard pavilion proposed but not built, and a rooftop walkabout described by the artist as a funhouse for children and a photo opportunity for parents. The mechanisms stay the same -- mirror, curve and delay -- but the register changes, since a child ducks, runs and presses a face to the glass where an adult stands and watches. The watchable behaviour is play as measurement: running the same corridor twice gives a different doubled self each time, and the work is learned by the body rather than by looking. Seen in W14 and its paired Fun House of 1997, W15 (a circular interior with a domed roof set into a hill, portraits as backlit lightboxes high around the walls, a hemispherical two-way mirror oculus at the apex) and W19 (an S-shaped curve of two-way mirror glass between two ivy hedgerows, two chambers, one open to the north and one to the south).

**Takeaway:** size the pavilion for a child and the optics become a game; run it twice and it is a different work. [C28175]

## M10 -- The video essay as montage argument: the collision argues

Found footage, scrolling text, voice-over and original music are cut together so the thesis is carried by collision rather than by a single narrator. Shaker circle dancing is cut against hard-core punk, Ann Lee against Patti Smith, Puritanism and the Ghost Dance against Elvis Presley and Jerry Lee Lewis, and the viewer watches montage do the arguing. The watchable behaviour is a thesis arriving in flashes: each cut reframes the last image, and the argument is the sequence, not any one shot. Seen in W12, Rock My Religion, 55 minutes 27 seconds, with original music by Glenn Branca and Sonic Youth.

**Takeaway:** cut the doctrine against the gig; let the collision argue, not the narrator. [C28176]

## Aesthetic logic

Graham's logic is the viewer made into the work's material: a reader turning a magazine page becomes a performer watched by a camera, becomes a body standing inside a mirror, becomes one stranger among others in a hedge maze. The materials are deliberately borrowed and everyday -- the magazine page, the closed-circuit camera, the corporate facade's two-way glass, the suburban hedge -- and the forms are deliberately simple: a page, a room, a cylinder, a triangle, an S-curve. What changes across sixty years is not the question but the scale at which it is asked: in the 1960s the work asks it of a reader alone with a magazine; in the 1970s of a small group in a room with a mirror and a delay; from the 1980s on of a crowd in a park, on a roof, on a campus, in daylight that keeps changing the answer. Against Klingelhoeller's solid word -- language cut from cardboard and piled on the floor, a sentence turned into furniture -- Graham's answer is the word dissolved into a space that watches you back: no object to read, only a room that returns your image late, bent, doubled, and mixed with strangers. The pavilions are punctuation marks in physical space, as he called them, but the sentence they punctuate is the visitor's own movement -- inclusion and exclusion, inside and outside, seeing and being seen, held in one glass wall that cannot decide which it is. [C28177]
