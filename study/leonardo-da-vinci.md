# Leonardo da Vinci: Study Notes

**Site:** Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci (15 April 1452, Vinci - 2 May 1519, Clos Luce, Amboise), painter, sculptor, engineer, anatomist and writer of notebooks, the most famous painter in the world on the strength of fewer than 25 attributed major works, most of them unfinished. Born out of wedlock to the notary Ser Piero da Vinci and Caterina di Meo Lippi, a lower-class woman, he received only a basic informal education in vernacular reading, writing and mathematics, taught himself Latin, and learned mathematics in the 1490s under Luca Pacioli. At about 14 he became a garzone in the Florence workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, where he trained for seven years alongside Ghirlandaio, Perugino, Botticelli and Lorenzo di Credi, learning drafting, chemistry, metallurgy, metalworking, plaster casting, leather working, mechanics and woodwork as well as drawing, painting, sculpting and modelling. By 1472, aged 20, he qualified as a master in the Guild of Saint Luke, but stayed with Verrocchio, painting the young angel in the Baptism of Christ (c.1472-1475) so well that Vasari says the master put down his brush and never painted again -- a flourish Wikipedia itself flags as probably apocryphal, though the oil passages in the mostly tempera panel are verifiably Leonardo's hand. His earliest dated drawing is the 1473 pen-and-ink Landscape of the Arno Valley. In January 1478 he received an independent commission for an altarpiece for the Chapel of Saint Bernard in the Palazzo della Signoria, and in March 1481 the monks of San Donato a Scopeto commissioned the Adoration of the Magi; neither was completed, both abandoned when he left for Milan. In 1482 Lorenzo de' Medici sent him as ambassador to Ludovico Sforza of Milan, carrying a letter that listed his engineering and weapon-design skills first and mentioned painting almost as an afterthought, and a silver stringed instrument in the form of a horse's head. In Milan (c.1482-1499) he painted the Virgin of the Rocks for the Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception and the Last Supper for Santa Maria delle Grazie, designed floats and pageants and a wooden model for the Milan Cathedral cupola competition, and planned the Gran Cavallo, a huge equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza that would have surpassed Donatello's Gattamelata and Verrocchio's Colleoni; a model was completed and casting plans made, but in November 1494 Ludovico gave the metal to his brother-in-law for a cannon against Charles VIII of France. In c.1498 he and assistants painted the Sala delle Asse in the Sforza Castle as a trompe-l'oeil pergola of sixteen interwoven mulberry trees. This is also the period of the Lady with an Ermine (presumed Cecilia Gallerani, c.1489-1491), the Vitruvian Man (c.1490; see C150 and C174 for the dating conflict), and the start of serious anatomy. When Ludovico was overthrown by France in 1500, Leonardo fled Milan for Venice with Salai and Luca Pacioli, working there as a military architect devising defences against naval attack. In his second Florentine period (1500-1508) he was guest of the Servite monks at Santissima Annunziata, where Vasari says his cartoon of the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist drew crowds "as if they were going to a solemn festival"; in 1502 he entered the service of Cesare Borgia as military architect and engineer, making the town plan of Imola and a map of the Chiana Valley and a scheme to divert the Arno; back in Florence by early 1503 he rejoined the Guild of Saint Luke on 18 October 1503 and by that month had begun the portrait of Lisa del Giocondo, the Mona Lisa, which he would keep and rework until his later years; in January 1504 he sat on the committee to place Michelangelo's David; and he then spent two years on the Battle of Anghiari mural (see the Doorway). In 1506 he was summoned to Milan by Charles II d'Amboise, French governor, and took on Francesco Melzi, considered his favourite pupil. In 1507 he was in Florence settling a dispute with his brothers over his father's estate (Ser Piero died 1504). Back in Milan by 1508, living in his own house in Porta Orientale, he worked in 1512 on plans for an equestrian monument for Gian Giacomo Trivulzio, prevented by the Swiss, Spanish and Venetian invasion that drove the French from Milan. From September 1513 to 1516 he was in Rome in the Belvedere Courtyard of the Apostolic Palace, where Michelangelo and Raphael were also active, on 33 ducats a month from Leo X; the pope gave him a painting commission of unknown subject and cancelled it when Leonardo set about developing a new kind of varnish; he practised botany in the Vatican Gardens, made plans to drain the Pontine Marshes, dissected cadavers and made notes for a treatise on vocal cords, and became ill, in what may have been the first of multiple strokes. In October 1515 Francis I recaptured Milan; in 1516 Leonardo entered Francis's service, given the manor of Clos Luce near Amboise, a pension totalling 10,000 scudi, and the title of first painter, engineer and architect to the king. He drew plans for a castle town at Romorantin and made a mechanical lion which walked towards the king and, struck by a wand, opened its chest to reveal lilies. An October 1517 visit record (Louis d'Aragon's secretary) and a drawing by Giovanni Ambrogio Figino confirm his right hand was paralytic at 65, which may explain why works such as the Mona Lisa were left unfinished. He died at Clos Luce on 2 May 1519, aged 67, possibly of a stroke. Melzi was principal heir and executor, receiving his paintings, tools, library and personal effects; Salai and his servant Baptista de Vilanis each received half his vineyards. Vasari's deathbed scene -- Francis I holding Leonardo's head in his arms -- is flagged as possibly legend; a royal edict was issued at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, a two-day journey away, on the day of his death, though it was not signed by the king (see C183 and C190). ## M1. Sfumato Glaze Stack
Sfumato, from Italian sfumare, "to evaporate / to tone down like smoke," is not a soft brushstroke. It is a slow, layered building process. Leonardo applied multiple ultra-thin, translucent oil glazes, each allowed to dry before the next, each shifting the tone or colour beneath it only fractionally. Scientific analysis published in Angewandte Chemie (2010) on seven Leonardo paintings at the Louvre found 20-40 separate glaze layers in some passages, individual layers 1-2 micrometres thick, total thickness under about 40 micrometres -- roughly half a human hair. Up to about 30 layers are reported on the Mona Lisa. The reported layer sequence on flesh: lead-white priming, pink base (lead white, vermilion, earth pigments), translucent dark shadow glaze, final varnish. A 2023 Journal of the American Chemical Society study (Gonzalez et al.) found plumbonacrite in the Mona Lisa's ground, evidence Leonardo treated his oil and ground with lead oxide to thicken it and sustain those micro-glazes. Why oil, not tempera: oil dries slowly, stays workable across sessions, and becomes transparent in thin applications -- exactly what glazing requires. Tempera sets almost as soon as it touches the panel and cannot be reworked this way. Leonardo's own maxim, in the Treatise on Painting: "Your shadows and lights should be blended without lines or borders in the manner of smoke losing itself in the air" (Q1). Vasari reported he sometimes blended with his fingers directly on wet paint, and the Vatican Museums' technical study of St Jerome confirms finger-painting in his practice. The effect is not indiscriminate blur: sfumato preserves form with controlled micro-gradients. Neuroscientist Margaret Livingstone (2000) offered a testable account of the Mona Lisa's shifting smile: the soft, low-spatial-frequency shading around the mouth is read as a smile by peripheral vision but flattens when looked at directly. A 2013 University of Salzburg study argued Livingstone's account does not explain the whole effect -- flagged, not settled. Watchable behaviour: no visible edge anywhere on the Mona Lisa's mouth or eyes; the smile strengthens in peripheral vision and fades on direct gaze; flesh has no outline, only a smoke gradient. Source: Angewandte Chemie (2010) via secondary summaries; JACS (2023) via secondary summaries; Treatise on Painting; Vatican Museums St Jerome technique study; Livingstone (2000); Salzburg (2013).

**Takeaway:** the edge is never drawn, it is accumulated -- thirty glazes of nothing, each one a fraction of a tone, until the line disappears and only the smoke remains.

## M2. Chiaroscuro Under-Modelling
A monochrome grey and brown under-painting (lead-white primer over underdrawing, brown and green shadow) that sculpts form before colour. Leonardo's paintings are built, not drawn-then-coloured. The unfinished Adoration of the Magi (begun 1481, Uffizi) and St Jerome in the Wilderness (c.1480-1490, Vatican) expose the system: a detailed underdrawing (X-rays reveal highly detailed preparatory layers in the Adoration), then an under-modelling in watercolour, then a semi-transparent primer with a lead-white base that gives the underlying drawing a grey appearance -- the grey acting as a half-tone for modelling. Most shadow was painted with brown pigments, often mixed with green; sky and distant mountains in azurite. Only the saint's face, part of his right leg and the dark rocks in St Jerome show a finished underlying chiaroscuro. A thin brown wash lets the underdrawing show through. Watchable behaviour: the picture is legible as light-and-dark sculpture with almost no colour, and the finished passages (Jerome's face, leg) emerge from that grey ground; the unfinished works are more instructive than most finished pictures because the system is exposed. Source: Vatican Museums St Jerome technique study; Adoration diagnostics (Seracini 2002, via leg 2); Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci".

**Takeaway:** colour is the last thing to arrive; the picture is finished in grey first, and the grey is the picture.

## M3. Aerial Perspective (Blue-Shift With Distance)
Farther means bluer, paler, lower contrast, softer edge, in proportion to distance. Leonardo distinguished three ways distance changes what the eye sees: objects get smaller (linear perspective), blurrier ("perspective of disappearance" -- detail is the first thing distance takes), and bluer (aerial or atmospheric perspective). He is credited with first using the term "aerial perspective" in what became the Treatise on Painting, and with the rule "if one is to be five times as distant, make it five times bluer" (Q11) and "colours become weaker in proportion to their distance from the person who is looking at them" (Q12). His explanation -- air is not itself blue; the colour comes from moisture and fine particles lit by the sun against dark distant hills -- anticipates Rayleigh scattering (1871) in outline. He studied light from the summit of Monte Rosa, and in Paris MS D (c.1508-1509) made his most refined study of the eye, experimenting with eyeglasses, lenses, coloured filters and mirrors. Watchable behaviour: in the Mona Lisa and Virgin of the Rocks backgrounds, near rocks are warm and detailed, far mountains dissolve into blue-grey haze with no hard contour; the horizon sits at eye level and the air itself does the modelling. Source: Treatise on Painting via leg 1; Beyondeveryart, "Atmospheric Perspective"; Russell Collection, "What Is Aerial Perspective in Painting?"; Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci".

**Takeaway:** distance is not drawn smaller, it is painted bluer -- the air between the eye and the mountain is a material, and it thickens with every mile.

## M4. Linear Perspective and Perspective of Disappearance
A constructed vanishing-point architecture combined with deliberate loss of detail at distance. The Adoration's ruined classical background is drawn in detailed linear perspective; the Last Supper's coffered ceiling converges on Christ's head, the vanishing point sitting at his face, with the apostles in four groups of three around him; the Annunciation sets Gabriel and Mary across a central vanishing point. At the same time, detail is withheld rather than drawn small: the perspective of disappearance means far things lose their edges and their particulars before they lose their size. He is reported to have identified at least 15 basic types of perspective. Watchable behaviour: the room reads as a measurable box -- you could walk it -- while distant detail is refused, not miniaturised; the architecture is exact and the distance is smoke. Source: Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci" and "The Last Supper (Leonardo)"; leg 1 optics section.

**Takeaway:** the near is measured and the far is surrendered; one-point perspective builds the room, and the refusal to draw the distance finishes it.

## M5. Moti Mentali: Gesture as Inner State
Figures are posed by anatomy and emotion together -- a head turned at a different angle to the torso (Lady with an Ermine), hands that continue a thought, apostles recoiling in differentiated shock at "one of you will betray me." Leonardo's term, carried through the notebooks and the Treatise, is moti mentali -- movements of the mind made visible in the body. The Ermine sitter is caught mid-turn, listening to someone off-frame; her body faces right, her face faces left, and the ermine twists the same way, so the whole portrait is one interrupted gesture. In the Last Supper each apostle's twist is a different verb -- recoil, protest, question, reach -- and the composition is a grammar of reactions, not a row of poses. The payoff of the anatomy is here: the hands and faces are modelled as flesh over known bone and muscle rather than as outline filled with colour, so a turn of the head carries the weight of a skull turning on a spine. Watchable behaviour: no two figures in a group do the same thing; the twist is never decoration, it is the thought happening. Source: Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci" and "Lady with an Ermine"; leg 1 anatomy and moti mentali sections.

**Takeaway:** the pose is a sentence; every figure is caught mid-thought, and the group is a conversation, not a lineup.


## M6. Vortex and Turbulence as a Visible System
Water and air drawn as interlocking linear and circular motions -- eddies, whirlpools, welling currents, concentric bubble-rings that cross without cancelling. Water obsessed Leonardo from his earliest dated drawing (the 1473 Arno landscape) to his final sheets, forty-five years later, haunted by deluges. He surveyed the Arno for Florence and planned a canal to make Florence navigable from the sea; around 1508-1511 he studied hydraulics in great detail with the unrealised intention of compiling a treatise. Royal Collection Trust: he made hundreds of observations on water movement; his analyses of complex motions in terms of linear and circular components recur, but "the superabundance of particular cases prevented him from ever realising a set of generally applicable laws." His water sheets show flow past a planar obstruction (stable, repeatable patterns -- evidence flow was subject to physical laws, framed in terms of incidence, percussion and reflection), and a stream falling from a sluice into a pool with multiple layered vortices extending far below the surface, each welling current giving rise to concentric circles of bubbles that expand across each other without interference. The late Deluge drawings (black chalk, c.1515, Windsor, a series of 10 paired with written description) and the Description of the Deluge (Richter sections 608-609, MS G c.1515) describe mountains sliding, swollen waters striking city walls, eddying whirlpools, water revolving more swiftly nearer the centre of an eddy, waves grinding falling water into mist "in the manner of curling smoke" (Q20), and people driven to mountain peaks. He watched a whirlwind on a sandbank in the Arno hollow sand "to a depth more than the height of a man." Watchable behaviour: flow has a stable, repeatable pattern that can be read, not just a splash; the same spiral recurs in hair, in drapery, in the lion's tail in St Jerome, and in the Deluge's curling smoke-mist -- one motion, many materials. Source: Royal Collection Trust, "Studies of water"; Richter, Notebooks sections 608-609; leg 1 water section.

**Takeaway:** turbulence is not chaos, it is a system with a grammar -- circles inside lines, rings crossing rings -- and once you can draw the eddy you can draw anything that moves.

## M7. Air as a Fluid; Wing as a Machine
Birds and flying machines drawn as instruments working by mathematical law -- wing curvature, air "condensed" under the wing on the downstroke, balance by imperceptible adjustment. Leonardo studied birds for decades, dissecting them, noting wing shape, air pressure and weight distribution, treating air as a fluid that resists flight. The Codex on the Flight of Birds (c.1505) is an early theory of aerodynamics. Designs: the flapping ornithopter (operator lying face down, arms and legs driving wings via pulleys and levers), a glider, and the aerial screw (Manuscript B, folio 83v, c.1489) -- a helical rotor about 4 metres in diameter, frame of reeds with starch-sealed linen covering supported by wires, to be rotated by four men pushing levers, on the principle of an Archimedes screw in air: spun fast enough it would "bite" the air and generate lift. He never built a working aircraft; human muscle cannot supply the strength-to-weight ratio for sustained flapping flight. Modern small-scale models of the aerial screw generate some lift but not true flight. His own words: "A bird is an instrument working according to mathematical law, which instrument it is within the capacity of man to reproduce with all its movements, but not with a corresponding degree of strength" (Q7). Research by Marc van den Broek found older prototypes for more than 100 inventions ascribed to Leonardo -- his innovation was often combining existing functions into new scenes, not inventing from nothing. In his notebooks he first stated the laws of sliding friction in 1493; they were not rediscovered until Guillaume Amontons in 1699. He correctly concluded perpetual motion was impossible. Watchable behaviour: the aerial screw's helix, the ornithopter's pulley-and-lever wing, the Flight of Birds notes on wind curving over crags and birds holding still with wings open in the up-draught -- the machine is drawn the way the bird is drawn, as a working system. Source: Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci" sections on engineering and inventions; Codex on the Flight of Birds; leg 1 engineering and flight sections.

**Takeaway:** the bird and the machine are the same drawing -- once air is a fluid, the wing is an instrument, and the only thing missing is the strength.

## M8. Drawing as Thinking, in Layered Media
Silverpoint on bone-ash and indigo-blue prepared paper (fine, unerasable, hatched tone), then white-lead heightening, then black and red chalk, charcoal for lay-in, iron-gall pen for the committed line. A single sheet can hold a rough charcoal sketch under a precise silverpoint study under ink; left-handed hatching slants down left-to-right; red chalk dominates the late figure studies. The Turin presumed self-portrait (c.1510-1512, red chalk on paper, 33.3 x 21.6 cm) is drawn left-handed in fine hatching -- and its identification as a self-portrait, made only in the 19th century, is now contested (see C159). The Vitruvian Man (c.1490, pen, brown ink and watercolour over metalpoint on paper, 34.4 x 24.5 cm) is compass-and-caliper construction with no sketchy lines, mirror-written proportion text around a man in two superimposed poses. Most writing is left-handed mirror-image cursive, right to left -- probably easier for a left-hander, avoiding smearing wet ink; he also sometimes wrote conventionally with his right hand, and used shorthand and symbols. A single topic is often covered in words and pictures on a single sheet, so information would not be lost if pages were published out of order. Watchable behaviour: the medium changes with the stage of the thought -- charcoal to find it, silverpoint to fix it, chalk to feel it, ink to commit it -- and the sheet keeps all four stages visible at once. Source: Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci" and "Vitruvian Man"; leg 1 drawing section.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is not the record of the thought, it is the thought itself, in four media at once -- and the sheet is allowed to show its own history.

## M9. Slow Oil, Spolvero Transfer, Revision in Paint
Cartoon transferred by spolvero (charcoal dusted through pricked holes in a paper cartoon), then years of returning to the same panel, moving a hand, a hairline, a horizon. For the Mona Lisa, Pascal Cotte's multispectral imaging (2020, reported in secondary sources) found spolvero marks and evidence Leonardo shifted the fingers, hairline, nose bridge and horizon line in paint. The panel was carried with him for about 16 years and reworked in France. The Adoration shows the same process frozen mid-way, with pentimenti visible in the underdrawing. The Salvator Mundi carries pentimenti in the blessing thumb. The portrait of Lisa del Giocondo was begun in Florence c.1503 (confirmed by Agostino Vespucci's 1503 note, discovered 2005), never delivered, carried to France in 1516, and reworked until near his death. The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne was kept by Leonardo until his death, unfinished, with sketches (a horse's head, half a skull, a child with a lamb) found on the panel's reverse in 2008. Watchable behaviour: the Mona Lisa is a palimpsest -- underdrawing, shifted fingers, 20-30 glazes -- while the Adoration shows the same process frozen mid-way; nothing is painted once, everything is painted over, and the painting is never declared done, only carried. Source: Cotte multispectral imaging via secondary summaries; Wikipedia "Mona Lisa" and "Leonardo da Vinci"; leg 1 underdrawing section; leg 2 works table.

**Takeaway:** the painting is a place you keep returning to, not a thing you finish -- the panel travels with the painter, and the work is the revisions.

## M10. The Unfinished as a Working State (and Its Cost)
Experimental media chosen for control over durability -- oil and tempera on dry plaster in the Last Supper, thick oil and wax ground on the Anghiari wall -- that then run, drip, flake or refuse to dry, and are abandoned. For the Last Supper (c.1495-1498, refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan, 460 x 880 cm) he used tempera mixed with emulsifying oils on a dry wall -- plaster, then a thin layer of calcium carbonate, magnesium and animal glue, then a lead-white primer coat -- instead of true buon fresco. It let him work slowly, return to a face days later, and build sfumato gradations. It also failed: the paint sat on top of the wall rather than fusing into it, and deterioration was visible within decades -- flaking by 1517, "a muddle of blots" by 1556; humidity from the adjacent kitchens, an exterior wall, steam, and later bombing (August 1943) and misguided restorations compounded it. The 1978-1999 restoration under Pinin Brambilla Barcilon stripped overpainting and stabilised what remained. The same experimental impulse destroyed the Battle of Anghiari: he wanted oil colours on the wall after his bad experience with fresco at the Last Supper; he applied so thick an undercoat (possibly mingled with wax) that the paint began to drip and run; he hung large charcoal braziers to dry it in a hurry; only the lower part could be saved, the upper colours intermingled, and he abandoned the project. Paolo Giovio (c.1527-28) gives the same account: defective plaster "persistently rejected the colours ground in walnut oil" (Q16). Fewer than 25 attributed major works survive, including numerous unfinished ones. Watchable behaviour: the two great murals exist only as copies and as deterioration; the Adoration and St Jerome are more instructive unfinished than most finished pictures, because the system is exposed -- and the failure is always the same failure, the medium chosen for slowness refusing to stay put. Source: Wikipedia "The Battle of Anghiari (Leonardo)" and "Conservation-restoration of Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper"; Giovio via leg 1; leg 2 works table.

**Takeaway:** he chose the medium that let him think slowly, and the medium punished him for it -- the unfinished state is not a habit, it is the price of the method.


## Evolution
Four phases, from legs 1 and 2. Phase 1, Florence apprenticeship and first independence, c.1472-1482 (ages 20-30): the workshop prodigy. Palette: clear, relatively high-key colour on white gesso; tempera-dominant, oil used experimentally (Baptism). Composition: formal, symmetrical, Fra Angelico-derived (Annunciation: angel left, Mary right); figures placed parallel to the picture plane. By the Adoration (1481) this breaks open: a pyramidal foreground group, a semicircle of about 50 agitated figures, a high horizon and a deep, split background (ruins left, battle right). Finish: the Annunciation is complete and polished; the Adoration and St Jerome are abandoned mid-process -- the lifelong pattern starts here, commissions taken, then left unfinished when a bigger problem (Milan, 1482) intervenes. Signature moves already present: sfumato and atmospheric perspective in the Annunciation; anatomy-driven drapery studies; the humanist Mary who greets rather than submits; optics treated as a research problem (the long arm as a lateral-viewpoint correction). Phase 2, first Milan, c.1482-1499: the system matures. Palette: darker, warmer, smokier. Walnut panels replace poplar for portraits; black backgrounds (Musician, Ermine, Ferronniere). The Louvre Virgin of the Rocks is described by Clark as buried under yellow varnish and bitumen -- the "dark painter" reputation is partly a conservation artefact. Composition: the pyramid becomes the governing machine -- Virgin of the Rocks (Mary apex, two children below), Ermine (pyramidal spiral), and then the Last Supper: a one-point perspective grid with Christ's face at the vanishing point, apostles in four groups of three, all reacting to a single spoken sentence. Movement is now the subject: the Ermine sitter is caught mid-turn, listening to someone off-frame. Finish: ambition outruns technique. The Last Supper is painted in tempera on a sealed dry wall precisely so it can be reworked slowly -- and it starts flaking within years. The London Virgin of the Rocks takes 25 years of legal wrangling to deliver (1508). The Gran Cavallo never gets cast. New scale: the two largest works of his life are Milanese -- Last Supper (460 x 880 cm) and the Sala delle Asse ceiling -- plus the first true court portraits (Ermine, Musician, Ferronniere), where the sitter's psychology replaces the profile-medal format. Phase 3, second Florence, c.1500-1508: portraiture carried for 16 years and the public mural duel. The Mona Lisa begun October 1503 and never delivered; the Burlington House Cartoon drawing crowds "as if they were going to a solemn festival"; the Battle of Anghiari (1503-1505) the counter-pole to everything else -- a knot of four rearing horses and men fighting for a standard, pure violent motion, no architecture at all. Phase 4, late Milan, Rome and France, c.1508-1519: slowness, smoke, and the unfinished as a state. Palette: near-monochrome darkness. St John is a face and a hand emerging from black; Mona Lisa is built from dozens of micrometre-thin glazes with no visible brushwork; Saint Anne is a landscape of mountains, chasms and waterfalls in which the figures are almost geological. Composition: the pyramid collapses into interlocking diagonals -- Mary seated on Anne's knee, two bodies on one axis; the Burlington House Cartoon reworks the same group with St John instead of the lamb. Finish: almost nothing is finished after 1500. Mona Lisa, Saint Anne and St John were all still in his possession at his death; the Battle of Anghiari was abandoned when the oil-on-wall paint ran; Leda was painted (1508) and then lost, probably deliberately destroyed. The right-hand paralysis (by October 1517) is cited as a reason the Mona Lisa stayed unfinished.

Early vs recent, stated plainly. Early Leonardo (1472-1499) still finishes things: the Annunciation is complete and polished, the Virgin of the Rocks is delivered, the Last Supper is painted to a public unveiling even if the medium fails. Late Leonardo (1500-1519) does not finish things: the Mona Lisa, the Saint Anne and the St John are all still in his possession at his death, the Anghiari is abandoned, the Leda is lost. Early colour is clear and high-key on white gesso; late colour is near-monochrome darkness, faces emerging from black. Early composition is formal and symmetrical; late composition is interlocking diagonals and pyramids collapsing into one another. What stayed constant across all four phases: (1) the pyramidal and triangular figure group; (2) sfumato -- softening contour into atmosphere -- from the Annunciation to St John; (3) the figure caught mid-movement or mid-thought rather than posed; (4) landscape as active geology and weather, not backdrop; (5) drawing as the primary act -- the paintings are outnumbered by studies, and the late works are effectively drawings in oil; (6) chronic non-completion, driven by technical experiment (oil on wall, walnut oil, thick gesso grounds) and by treating each painting as an open research problem. The through-line is not a style change but a method change: from outline-and-fill to light-and-air as the modelling system, and from finished commission to open, revisable, research-driven work.

## Aesthetic Logic
The logic is one operation repeated at every scale: make the edge disappear and let light and air do the modelling. In paint the operation is literal -- thirty glazes of nothing until the outline is gone (M1) -- and the same operation governs the under-painting, where the picture is finished in grey before colour arrives (M2); the distance, where the far mountain is surrendered to blue (M3); the room, where the near is measured and the far is refused (M4); and the body, where a turn of the head is a thought (M5). The second constant is the system that can be read: water and air have a grammar of circles inside lines (M6), and the bird and the machine are the same drawing (M7). The third constant is the open work: the drawing keeps all four stages visible at once (M8), the painting is returned to for sixteen years (M9), and the unfinished state is the price of the method, not a failure of it (M10). Against Michelangelo the contrast is exact and is the point of the doorway: Michelangelo's logic is taking away until only what cannot be removed remains, and the figure is freed from the block; Leonardo's logic is adding nothing until only what cannot be seen remains, and the figure is freed from the line. One carves the excess off the stone, the other glazes the edge off the paint; one finishes by destruction, the other by slow accumulation; one leaves the back of the block raw, the other leaves the panel wet for sixteen years. Both leave their great public murals unfinished in the same hall, and both are copied for a generation from the cartoons -- the school is the drawing either way. The essay voice of the study is this: Leonardo is not a painter who also did science, and not a scientist who also painted; he is a man for whom observation is the single act, and the painting, the anatomy, the water study and the flying machine are four media for the same observation, each one unfinished because the observation never ends.

## POSITIONS
- P1. Leonardo's fame rested first on painting, and only later on the notebooks. Source: Wikipedia, "Leonardo da Vinci," lead and Paintings section: "for the better part of four hundred years his fame rested on his achievements as a painter."
- P2. He produced fewer than 25 attributed major works, including numerous unfinished works, yet made some of the most influential paintings in the Western canon. Source: Wikipedia, lead.
- P3. Sfumato is built from 20-40 glaze layers of 1-2 micrometres each (total under 40 micrometres) on the Louvre Leonardos. Source: Angewandte Chemie (2010) study as reported in the Russell Collection, "What Is Sfumato in Painting?" and Beyondeveryart, "Sfumato: Leonardo's Technique With No Edges."
- P4. The Last Supper is not a true fresco; it is oil and tempera on dry plaster over a gesso and lead-white ground, and it deteriorated within Leonardo's lifetime because the paint never bonded to the wall. Source: Wikipedia, "Conservation-restoration of Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper" and "Leonardo da Vinci," Paintings of the 1490s; The Collector, "Who Is Who in Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper?"
- P5. By his own count Leonardo dissected 30 corpses, producing 240-plus anatomical drawings and about 13,000 words toward a treatise, in collaboration 1510-1511 with Marcantonio della Torre. Source: Britannica, "Leonardo da Vinci" (Anatomical studies; Vitruvian Man); Wikipedia, Anatomy and physiology.
- P6. The notebooks total about 13,000 pages made, about 7,000-7,200 surviving, written mostly in left-handed mirror cursive, and were largely unpublished for centuries, so his scientific findings had little direct influence on subsequent science. Source: Wikipedia, Journals and notes, Science and inventions; HistoryPrime and Royal Collection summaries.
- P7. Leonardo coined and systematised "aerial perspective" in the Treatise on Painting (Melzi's compilation, published 1651), with the proportional rule that colours weaken -- and blue -- with distance. Source: Beyondeveryart, "Atmospheric Perspective"; Russell Collection, "What Is Aerial Perspective in Painting?"; Wikipedia on the Treatise.
- P8. The aerial screw (Manuscript B, folio 83v, c.1489) and the ornithopter were designs only -- never built or flown by Leonardo -- and human muscle power is the binding constraint. Source: Wikipedia, Engineering and inventions; Sovereign Magazine and The Conversation, "Leonardo da Vinci's Helicopter"; leonardodavincisinventions.com, "Da Vinci Flying Machine."
- P9. The Mona Lisa was begun in Florence c.1503 (Lisa del Giocondo, confirmed by Agostino Vespucci's 1503 note, discovered 2005), never delivered, carried to France in 1516, and reworked until near his death. Source: Wikipedia, Paintings of the 1500s; paintingrecognition.com and academiaaesthetics.com summaries of the Vespucci note.
- P10. Leonardo's right hand was paralytic at 65 (October 1517 visit record; Figino drawing), which may explain unfinished late works including the Mona Lisa. Source: Wikipedia, Rome and France (1513-1519), citing the Louis d'Aragon visit record and Figino drawing.
- P11. The Adoration of the Magi exposes the system: only the underdrawing is Leonardo's; all visible paint is by later hands. Source: Seracini's diagnostics (2002), via leg 2; the 2012-2017 restoration treated it as Leonardo's underdrawing with later overpaint.
- P12. The Last Supper's deterioration was visible within decades -- flaking by 1517, "a muddle of blots" by 1556 -- because the paint sat on a sealed dry wall rather than bonding into wet plaster. Source: Wikipedia, "The Last Supper (Leonardo)" and "Conservation-restoration of Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper"; leg 2.
- P13. The Mona Lisa was never delivered, carried to France in 1516, and reworked for about 16 years; the panel is a palimpsest of underdrawing, shifted fingers, hairline, nose bridge and horizon line, under 20-30 glazes. Source: Cotte multispectral imaging (2020) via secondary summaries; Wikipedia "Mona Lisa"; legs 1 and 2.
- P14. The Battle of Anghiari was painted in oil on a thick gesso and wax ground that ran; Leonardo abandoned it in 1505, and whether it was ever executed at all is now contested. Source: Wikipedia "The Battle of Anghiari (Leonardo)"; Giovio (c.1527-28); 2020 art-historians' paper via Wikipedia; legs 1 and 2; see C160.
- P15. The Leda and the Swan (standing version, 1508) is lost, probably deliberately destroyed; nine copies survive, earliest the Spiridon Leda, probably by Francesco Melzi (c.1515, Uffizi). Source: Wikipedia "Leda and the Swan (Leonardo)"; List of works; leg 2.
- P16. The Salvator Mundi's attribution is disputed: the full-Leonardo case (National Gallery 2011 exhibition, Martin Kemp, Louvre C2RMF 2018 study) against the partial case (Carmen Bambach, Matthew Landrus, Frank Zollner, Prado 2021 catalogue downgrade to workshop). Source: Wikipedia "Salvator Mundi (painting)"; leg 2; see C154.
- P17. The Turin red-chalk "self-portrait" identification dates only from the 19th century and is now contested; the chief objection is that the sitter looks older than 67, Leonardo's age at death. Source: Wikipedia "Portrait of a Man in Red Chalk"; leg 2; see C159.
- P18. The Virgin of the Rocks exists in two versions; the majority view (Davies, Clark, National Gallery, Louvre) says the Louvre version is earlier, but Tamsyn Taylor argues the London version is earlier and the Louvre version is 1490s. Source: Wikipedia "Virgin of the Rocks"; leg 2; see C151.
- P19. Leonardo's late work is near-monochrome darkness: Saint John emerges from black by chiaroscuro, and La Scapigliata reduces the late style to a single head in pure sfumato. Source: Wikipedia "Saint John the Baptist (Leonardo)"; leg 2.
- P20. The Gran Cavallo bronze was diverted by Ludovico in November 1494 for a cannon against Charles VIII of France -- the monument that would have surpassed Donatello's Gattamelata and Verrocchio's Colleoni was lost to a war, not to a technical failure. Source: Wikipedia "Leonardo da Vinci"; leg 1; leg 2 works table entry 30.

## QUOTES
- Q1. "Your shadows and lights should be blended without lines or borders in the manner of smoke losing itself in the air." -- Leonardo, maxims for painters, in the Treatise on Painting (Trattato della Pittura); via Walter Isaacson, Leonardo da Vinci (2017), quoted in multiple summaries; also ResearchGate, "Another Ambiguous Expression" (citing Trattato, par. 67: "your shadows and lights must be jointed without any sign, as smoke"). Genuine, via Melzi's compilation -- FLAG: not a verbatim autograph manuscript; it reaches us through Melzi's posthumous compilation.
- Q2. "He who loves practice without theory is like the sailor who boards ship without a rudder and compass and never knows where he may cast." -- Leonardo, Treatise on Painting, Richter (trans.), G.8a, The Literary Works of Leonardo da Vinci (1883), Vol. 1, p.18; also in Today in Sci with Italian original. Genuine, notebook-derived.
- Q3. "Poor is the pupil who does not surpass his master." / Italian: "Tristo e quel discepolo che non avanza il suo maestro." -- Leonardo, Libro di Pittura, c.1493, section 498 ("He is a poor disciple who does not excel his master"); also MacCurdy, Notebooks (1938), Vol. 1, p.98, 'Aphorisms'. Genuine.
- Q4. "Just as iron rusts unless it is used, and water putrefies or, in cold, turns to ice, so our intellect spoils unless it is kept in use." (Variant: "Iron rusts from disuse; stagnant water loses its purity and in cold weather becomes frozen; even so does inaction sap the vigour of the mind.") -- Codex Atlanticus 289 v.; Richter, Notebooks (1888), Note 1177; McCurdy (1939), Vol. 1, p.89. Genuine, two translations differ in wording -- use either, not both as separate quotes.
- Q5. "The water you touch in a river is the last of that which has passed, and the first of that which is coming. Thus it is with time present." -- Notebooks, Richter (1883/1888); Wikiquote, "Leonardo da Vinci," Sourced. Genuine.
- Q6. "Now do you not see that the eye embraces the beauty of the whole world? It counsels and corrects all the arts of mankind... it is the prince of mathematics, and the sciences founded on it are absolutely certain. It has measured the distances and sizes of the stars... it has given birth to architecture and to perspective and to the divine art of painting." -- Treatise on Painting (1651), via Today in Sci. Genuine, via Melzi compilation -- FLAG: ellipses are the source's, not mine.
- Q7. "A bird is an instrument working according to mathematical law, which instrument it is within the capacity of man to reproduce with all its movements, but not with a corresponding degree of strength... such an instrument constructed by man is lacking in nothing except the life of the bird." -- Codex Atlanticus 161 r.a., 'Of the Bird's Movement,' in MacCurdy, Notebooks (1906), Vol. 1, p.153; via Today in Sci. Genuine.
- Q8. "Mechanics is the paradise of the mathematical sciences, because by means of it one comes to the fruits of mathematics." / Italian: "La meccanica e il paradiso delle scienze matematiche, perche con quella si viene al frutto matematico." -- Notebook E (1513), folio 8 back; Richter, Literary Works (1883), Vol. 1, Part 2, p.289, Aphorism 1155; McCurdy Vol. 1, p.613. Genuine.
- Q9. "Truth was the only daughter of Time." / Italian: "La verita fu sola figliola del tempo." -- Manuscript "Moto, colpo," 58b; Richter, Literary Works (1883), Vol. 2, p.288, Maxim No. 1152; via Today in Sci. Genuine.
- Q10. "Learning never exhausts the mind." -- Widely attributed to Leonardo's Notebooks; repeated in many quote collections (for example Kabushiki Hakushi, UpJourney, Science and Nonduality). FLAG -- WEAKLY SOURCED: leg 1 found no folio, codex, Richter number or McCurdy page for this line in any source read. It is probably genuine in sentiment, but do not cite it with a manuscript reference. Do not use it as a verified notebook quotation.
- Q11. "If one is to be five times as distant, make it five times bluer." -- Leonardo, rule for aerial perspective, in the Treatise on Painting; via leg 1, which reports it as Leonardo's proportional rule. FLAG: reaches us through Melzi's posthumous compilation, not as a verbatim autograph manuscript.
- Q12. "Colours become weaker in proportion to their distance from the person who is looking at them." -- Leonardo, rule for aerial perspective, in the Treatise on Painting; via leg 1. FLAG: same as Q11, via Melzi's compilation.
- Q13. "Beautiful spectacles of rippling water." -- Leonardo, on water movement; via leg 1, which quotes the phrase from the water studies. FLAG: quoted via secondary summary, no folio cited in the leg; use as a reported phrase, not a cited manuscript line.
- Q14. "The beautiful movements which result from one element [air] penetrating another [water]." -- Leonardo, on water movement; via leg 1. FLAG: same as Q13, reported phrase, no folio cited.
- Q15. "As if they were going to a solemn festival." -- Giorgio Vasari, Lives, on the crowds that flocked to see Leonardo's cartoon of the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist at Santissima Annunziata; via leg 2. FLAG: Vasari's words about Leonardo, not Leonardo's words.
- Q16. Defective plaster "persistently rejected the colours ground in walnut oil." -- Paolo Giovio (c.1527-28), on the failure of the Battle of Anghiari; via Wikipedia "The Battle of Anghiari (Leonardo)" and leg 1. FLAG: a contemporary's account of the failure, not Leonardo's words.
- Q17. Vasari reports that on his deathbed Leonardo said he had offended God and mankind because his work did not reach the quality it should have. FLAG: a modern first-person rewrite of Vasari's third-person account, not Leonardo's words; and Vasari's deathbed scene itself (Francis I holding Leonardo's head) is flagged as possibly legend (see C183 and C190). Do not quote it as Leonardo speaking.
- Q18. Matteo Bandello observed Leonardo painting the Last Supper dawn to dusk some days, then not painting for three or four days. FLAG: paraphrase reported in leg 1, not a verbatim quotation; Bandello's observation, not Leonardo's words.
- Q19. "CERCA TROVA" ("he who seeks, finds"). -- Motto painted by Vasari in the Salone dei Cinquecento; sometimes read as Vasari's hint that Leonardo's Anghiari survives behind his fresco. FLAG: disputed; Musci and Savorelli (2012) argue it alludes to Dante and the 1554 Battle of Scannagallo, not to Leonardo. Vasari's words, not Leonardo's.
- Q20. Waves grinding falling water into mist "in the manner of curling smoke." -- Leonardo, Description of the Deluge (Richter sections 608-609, MS G c.1515); via leg 1. FLAG: quoted via Richter's edition and secondary summary, not read in the manuscript.

Deliberately excluded (misattributed -- do NOT use as Leonardo): "Art is never finished, only abandoned" -- Paul Valery, "Au sujet du Cimetiere marin" (1933): "un ouvrage n'est jamais acheve... mais abandonne." No source in Leonardo's notebooks or Vasari; Wikiquote Talk and Quote Investigator both reject it. "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication" -- no source in Leonardo; credited to him only from around 2000; Today in Sci lists it with no citation. "Once you have flown, you will walk the earth with your eyes turned skyward..." -- Today in Sci: "[No known source]... almost certainly not authentic." "Study the science of art. Study the art of science..." -- Today in Sci lists it with no citation.
