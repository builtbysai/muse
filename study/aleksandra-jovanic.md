## The life: the starving-artist narrative, answered with a degree

The biography is unusually well documented because she tells it herself, at length, in two interviews: Gorilla Sun (30 April 2024, 476 lines) and objkt (4 September 2025, 116 lines), plus a CV dated to about 2016 and a 2024 Open University transcript. The study does not fill the gaps those interviews leave.

Aleksandra Jovanic, artist and programmer from Belgrade, Serbia. As a child she was curious about "drawing, sculpting, making, and deconstructing how things are built"; she was better at subjects that used logic rather than repetition and memorisation, mathematics above all. She chose computer science over art because of "the always present narrative about struggling artists" and wanting a more future-proof field -- "a programmer's future appeared to be a more realistic option with a foreseeable future, compared to arts" -- and chose the Faculty of Mathematics among the CS options. She kept exploring art media on her own until the Digital Arts programme opened. The choice was a dilemma about the educational path, not about the practice: "otherwise, I continued to explore various art mediums on my own until I had the chance to combine them academically."

The degrees stack in order: BSc in Computer Science, 2001, major in Computer and Information Technology, Faculty of Mathematics, University of Belgrade; MA in Digital Art, 2005; PhD in Digital Art, 2011, both at the University of Arts in Belgrade. The Digital Arts programme was brand new when she entered it -- established in 2000 as an interdisciplinary programme, open to applicants from any faculty with a portfolio. Her master's final project (2005) was Digital Dreams, an installation of animations imagining what machines would dream if they could dream, shown on computer monitors. Her doctoral work used Flash and game mechanics to build environments where players explore short stories and poems, solving puzzles to unlock new chunks of story; the puzzles were always different, already generative in that sense.

She has taught since her doctorate, and now teaches at all three levels: Associate Professor at the Faculty of Fine Arts in Belgrade, New Media department, where her subject set is called Technology of New Media -- Creative Coding, Physical Computing, 3D modelling and 3D animation, Web Design, Makers Technologies -- plus master studies at the Faculty of Applied Arts and doctoral studies at the University of Arts. The rank is associate professor, current per her own bio, the Open University transcript (2024), and Please Respond (2026); older pages saying assistant professor lag a promotion that falls somewhere between 2023 and 2024 (L1-F1). No birth date is evidenced anywhere; a birth year of 1976 appears in exactly one source (Spalter Digital) and is not stated as fact here (C1768).

Before the master's she spent half a year at a web-design agency, where she first noticed her CS and mathematics background gave her an edge -- boolean algebra, regular expressions. Web design during her studies was the first bridge between coding and design. She has also worked as a designer and programmer since 2003, with clients including the National Museum in Belgrade, and designed film title sequences. She has exhibited since 2003.

## The turn: January 2021, and a mentor twelve hours before the first mint

She began focusing seriously on generative art and creative coding in January 2021, as a result of active participation in an interdisciplinary educational project on algorithmic art, and began posting experiments regularly on social media. She first got into NFTs via hicetnunc. Raphael (de Courville -- the identification is inferential, role-matched, not printed in one source; see L3-F10) mentored her, including on pricing, and she minted her first piece twelve hours after his pointers. Her first mint on HeN was code, and she has minted almost exclusively code ever since: "I can't recall any other piece that I've minted that isn't code. So even if the pieces are minted as editions, when you view them they always look different, generating a different variation."

An IRL friend, lomz, was one of the first to mint on fxhash and encouraged her. At one of Raphael's monthly creative-code meetups (online, during the pandemic) they worked out together how the fxhash generator produces random hashes; days later she released her first fxhash series -- a three-part series on 22 November (2021, year inferred from context, not stated; C1776): Reflection of Stripes, Diffraction of Waves, Refraction of Stones -- interactive pieces, minted one per hour in a single morning. The early pieces carry a known bug she owns openly: a window resize resets the artwork, so it is not always the same -- "it was so early no one really complained about it." Her first year on fxhash was prolific to a degree that surprises even her: "When I look at the calendar of how many things I minted in the first year of fxhash, I have to ask myself 'am I normal', how did I manage to develop so many pieces?"

## The practice: concept first, palette small, series as a drawer

Her working method is stated plainly and it inverts the usual generative-art story in the opposite direction from Ridgley's. Ridgley is photographic, not mathematical; Jovanic is conceptual, not algorithmic. "For me its important to have a strong initial concept -- if you start with a strong concept it doesn't need to be as algorithmically complex." And: "I think that my stronger pieces are those that have an underlying concept, and I generally value them more. Although I do have pieces that emerge from exploration and process." Creativity, for her, is the balance between knowing and not knowing the solution: "If you already know the solution for something that you want to create, you're not very different from a computer executing instructions; you're not forced to be creative and think outside of the box... our creativity emerges from balancing between learned concepts and adding something new."

Three standing habits organise the practice. First, series, because she collects: "I think what makes me inclined towards series is my tendency to collect things. I was always fascinated with apothecary drawers -- you know, big apothecary closets with multiple drawers each of which contains different things." Chromatlas is ten volumes because the idea kept growing; she did not know at the start it would be ten. Second, composition before detail: "Maybe the composition is the first part of it for me, because it shows me the whole piece and if it works, followed by the details." Third, palette discipline: "I usually limit myself to a simple palette with 2 or 3 colors and do everything with them." Chromatlas is the exception that proves it -- eight colours, two of them similar reds, so seven in effect, with gradients between four chosen colours overlapping and multiplying into new hues.

The tools move with the work: Processing from around 2011 (her own hedge, consistent with the CV), migration to p5.js to publish interactive work on the web, and increasingly vanilla JavaScript. The favoured algorithm, named repeatedly across interviews, is the random walk -- bands that walk pseudorandomly, bounce off screen edges, decay and are reborn (Returns); paths connecting 3D-model vertices (Please Respond). The daily posting practice is deliberate exercise: "posting regularly on social media gives me a sense that I'm working on something... this morning I did my daily, posted it on my socials -- done! Sort of like a conclusion for the daily exercise/workout." And the market does not set the pace: "We are creating no matter what."

## The evolution: from the screen, out of the screen, and back as a body

The arc has five phases, and the constant is the walk.

Phase 1, the game-poems (2005-2011): Digital Dreams (machines dreaming, collage animation) and the Flash doctoral pieces where play unlocks story. Interactivity is a narrative gate, not decoration; the puzzle is already generative.

Phase 2, the quiet accumulation (2011-2021): teaching with Processing, practice-research scores (Converging Horizontals -- an algorithmic score for four voices tuned to 434 Hz), web design, title sequences. Ten years of systems thinking before the chain.

Phase 3, the blockchain turn (2021-2022): first HeN mint (code only, a rule she keeps), the fxhash debut trilogy (22 November 2021, interactive, with the resize bug she owns), then Chromatlas -- a year-long, ten-volume series built from the visual language of data science and vintage colour-classification books, exploring apophenia, the viewer's tendency to see patterns in random elements. Posting Chromatlas work-in-progress is what made her Instagram following explode. Herbarium and Balancium spin out of it for the Art Basel Hong Kong live-mint (May 2022), the two "bonus pieces" that let the ten-volume grid complete as twelve.

Phase 4, concept and data (2022-2023): Code Weaveresses (December 2022) -- Hofmann exercises turning into weaving, Bauhaus women weavers, real Serbian gender statistics embedded in the distribution of shapes; A leap, a splash, a walk (March 2023) -- interactive minting where the collector's choices shape the token, time as the subject, the Pitch Drop Experiment and Muybridge behind it; Latitudes (October 2023) -- travelling agents on parallel infinite paths, the sad realisation that parallels rarely intersect.

Phase 5, the physical turn and the anatomies (2023-2026): line(); (December 2023) -- LeWitt's written instructions as code constraints, plus an infinite interactive book projected onto a blank oversized book; The Space in Between (November 2023) -- plotter line plus hand-applied pastel gradient, software and physical drawing deliberately unlinked; the phenakistoscopes (2024-ongoing) -- code discs on a record player, animated only through a phone camera; the red/blue linocuts, where the code is split by colour so two printed colours overprint into a third; the "anatomies" -- Fracture (October 2024), line density and proximity faking three-dimensional volume, miscommunication as a bug in the code; Sensitivity (December 2024-January 2025), eighty-five years of open-meteo sun data driving bands, live code on a Raspberry Pi beside cyanotypes; Returns (2025), the random walk returning as bands that decay and are reborn, paper planes circling home; and Please Respond Day 9 (May 2026), her signature walk run over another artist's 3D-model vertices, the source figure deliberately half-hidden.

**Takeaway:** the evolution is an escape from the screen that keeps the screen's logic. She leaves the monitor for plotter, pastel, cyanotype, plexiglass, and spinning discs -- and every physical object is still a system with parameters, a walk with rules, a dataset with a mapping. The medium changes; the algorithm walks on.

## M1 -- The random walk as signature engine

The mechanism is her stated favoured algorithm, and it is the through-line from the doctoral game-poems to 2026. A random walk is a path that chooses its next step from its neighbours, with no destination and no memory beyond the last position. In Returns (2025) a sequence of bands emerges, "walking" pseudorandomly, bouncing off the screen edges, decaying, renewed with each new birth; the background graphics symbolise periodic trends -- life cycles, decades, years, seasons, months, days -- and in the foreground paper planes fly in circles, always returning to their starting position. In Please Respond Day 9 (2026) the same walk runs over another artist's data: she deconstructed Juan's code, extracted the vertex coordinates of his 3D human-figure model in Maya, and connected them into continuous paths -- testing the model's original vertex order against nearest-neighbour paths and convex-hull grouping, and settling on the inherited topology combined with nearest-neighbour. The walk is what makes the figure dissolve without disappearing: randomly framing only a portion of the vertex dataset keeps the full human figure unrecognisable while letting observant viewers recognise fragments of the body.

The mechanism matters because it is the opposite of a drawn line. A drawn line knows where it is going; a walk does not, and the watching is the work -- the line hesitates, doubles back, returns home. Ridgley's brush also walks (nearest-neighbour through pixels, #594 M2), but hers walks to paint; Jovanic's walks to travel. Same family, different organ: hers is a journey, not a stroke.

**Takeaway:** the walk is not a way of drawing a line; it is a way of giving a line a life. A piece whose lines are plotted is a diagram; a piece whose lines walk, bounce, decay, and are reborn is a creature with a route.

## M2 -- Data-viz language and apophenia

The mechanism is a scientific figure that encodes nothing the viewer can read -- so the viewer reads themselves into it. Chromatlas (10 volumes, c. 2021-22) draws on the visual language of data science and vintage colour-classification books: charts, bands, grids, gradients, the apparatus of measurement, arranged so the eye treats them as evidence. The concept is apophenia -- the tendency to see patterns in random elements, viewers attributing their own meaning to the work. She exhibited the series as a big book showing two cherry-picked iterations of each volume with a scientific-style poster, the display format doing half the work: a poster says "this means something," and the viewer's brain supplies the something.

The same mechanism returns with real data behind it, which is what makes the pair worth naming together. In Code Weaveresses the shapes carry actual Serbian gender statistics -- female-vs-male student numbers sampled at the Faculty of Mathematics and the University of Arts, and graduate distribution -- implemented as percentages in the distribution of shapes. In The Anatomy of Sensitivity the bands are driven field by field by open-meteo sun data for Belgrade, 1940 to end of 2024. The mechanism is identical in both cases (a figure that looks like data); what changes is whether the data is real. Chromatlas asks what the viewer projects; Sensitivity asks what the data projects. A piece that looks like a chart and means nothing is a joke; a piece that looks like a chart, means nothing, and makes the viewer supply the meaning is a mirror; a piece that looks like a chart and means eighty-five years of weather is a record.

**Takeaway:** the chart is not a picture of data; it is a frame the viewer fills. A piece that shows its data is an infographic; a piece that shows the shape of data and lets the viewer do the reading is apophenia made visible.

## M3 -- Gradient overlap and multiply

The mechanism is colour that appears from layering, not from the palette. Her discipline is two or three colours and everything done with them; Chromatlas stretches the discipline to eight (two similar reds, so seven in effect) and then, in the later volumes, introduces gradients: values chosen between four colours, the gradients overlapping and multiplying each other. Where two translucent gradients cross, a third colour appears that was never in the palette -- the multiply of the overlap. The palette is small; the colour space is not.

The mechanism matters because it is the visual form of her concept-first rule. The strong concept (a colour atlas, a classification book) does not need algorithmic complexity; it needs a rule about how colours meet. The viewer sees a rich, varied field and assumes a rich palette; the system knows it used four colours and a multiply. The same economy runs through Returns (a short palette, gradients between those colours giving surprising variation) and the red/blue linocuts, where the code is split by colour so two printed colours overprint into a third. In every case the third colour is the work: it exists only where layers meet.

**Takeaway:** the colour is not chosen; it is produced. A piece with a big palette is a paintbox; a piece with four colours and a multiply rule is a chemistry set -- the colours the viewer sees are the ones the layers made.

## M4 -- Instruction as constraint

The mechanism is a written instruction, taken literally, and exhausted. For line(); (December 2023) she took one of Sol LeWitt's etchings as a starting point -- not the artwork but the instructions, the words describing how to make it: straight, wavy, broken lines -- and explored what she could make strictly inside those limits, saving "what if..." ideas for later projects instead of adding them. The Anatomy of Fracture (October 2024) continues the research: the algorithm plays with the density and proximity of lines to produce dimensional visual forms, "as if they were physically present," from a Sol LeWitt work's title and instructions. The concept is communication and miscommunication -- language imperfections, disruptions of verbal precision, unpredictable visual incidents from loose interpretations of spoken language, embedded ambiguity in the code.

The mechanism matters because the constraint is the generator. LeWitt's instruction is a sentence; the system is what happens when a sentence is followed exactly, thousands of times, with one parameter (density, proximity) varied. "Hidden inside every edition... is a unique sequence of fractures." The fracture is the instruction misheard -- and the mishearing is the art. Compare Ridgley's kept miscalculation (#594 M3): hers is a bug kept because the texture is delicious; Jovanic's is an instruction followed until it breaks, and the break is the work. Same family -- the system doing something the artist did not quite tell it to -- different organ: hers is texture, Jovanic's is meaning.

**Takeaway:** the instruction is not a recipe; it is a boundary to walk to the edge of. A piece that follows instructions is a print; a piece that follows them until they fracture is a conversation with the person who wrote them.

## M5 -- Travelling agents on parallel paths

The mechanism is agents that move in the same direction along an infinite path and almost never meet. Latitudes (October 2023, solo at MONO Tunis Gallery, released on highlight.xyz) is travelling agents, all travelling in the same direction along an infinite path. The persistent question while making it was "where are they going?" -- plus the poetic, sad realisation that parallel movements diminish the chance of intersections on those paths. The agents are parallel lines with a life: same direction, same speed class, separate lanes, and the near-miss is the event. Nothing collides; nothing arrives; the work is the loneliness of parallel motion.

The mechanism is the inversion of M1. The random walk wanders and returns; the travelling agent proceeds and never meets. Both are lines with a life, but the walk is a life that circles home and the agent is a life that goes on. Returns (2025) is the synthesis: bands that walk and decay, and paper planes that fly in circles and always return to their starting position -- the walk and the agent in one system, the return and the departure held together. The viewer watches for the intersection that the geometry forbids, and the watching is the feeling.

**Takeaway:** the path is not a route to somewhere; it is a direction held forever. A piece whose agents meet is a story; a piece whose agents travel in parallel and never meet is a latitude -- a line on a map that never touches another line.

## M6 -- Phenakistoscope sync

The mechanism is a loop that exists only in the viewer's hand. The phenakistoscope discs (2024-ongoing) are code-generated, printed, and placed on a record player; viewed through a camera with a fixed frame rate they appear as continuous looping animations. Three parameters govern the sync: disc rotation (RPM), camera frame rate (fps), and the number of slices on the disc -- the loop animation frames. The record player fixes the first (speeds are already defined), the camera fixes the second, so the slice count is the computed variable: "When you know those two pieces of information, you can calculate how many frames you can put on the circle to obtain a looping animation. It's not that difficult, simple math."

The material research is the work, not the decoration. She tests prints on matte paper versus plexiglass (perfect colour but grainy relief, needs bright light or the recording blurs), buys record-player parts and motors from AliExpress after a curator asked for "like 10 of them rotating on the wall," and notes that each reload generates a different disc and animation -- randomness inside the system. The intent is the mechanism's point: "Ultimately it could be an animation on the screen but then you lose the physicality of it. I want it to be a piece that works in a gallery, physical space, your home, where you can play the record and see it through your own phone and record it. The key thing is that we curse time spent on our phones, but in this case you'll need your phone to fully experience the work." The phone is not the screen the work is shown on; it is the shutter the work is seen through. A still disc on a shelf is a print; the same disc spinning under a phone is an animation; the animation exists only in the interval between the disc, the motor, and the camera.

**Takeaway:** the loop is not in the disc; it is in the sync. A piece that animates on its own is a video; a piece that animates only when you spin it and look through your phone is a machine the viewer completes.

## M7 -- Plotter line plus hand gradient

The mechanism is the machine drawing the line and the hand painting the colour, in the same drawing, with the two halves deliberately unlinked. The Space in Between (November 2023, Feral File +GRAPH, curated by Casey Reas) is a generative software NFT plus thirty physical plotter drawings, each 1/1. The concept comes from John Baldessari: "when you're looking at two things, don't look at them, look between them." The space between is vibrant and full of life; the space outside frames the central area of activation. The active area is a block of hand-painted gradient colour plus an undulating cluster of plant-like organisms; the reference is life emerging through cracks in concrete, and her lived experience of moving through Belgrade.

The process is the mechanism: she draws the black line with a plotter first, then cuts a custom adhesive mask to keep the rest of the paper clean while applying dry pastels for a custom gradient per drawing. The software NFT contains millions of other images beyond the primary one per piece -- and the NFT and the plotter drawing are NOT linked: the NFT can trade on secondary while the drawing stays with the first collector. The machine's precision and the hand's softness are in the same frame and belong to different owners. The paper itself is part of the record (Fedrigoni Woodstock Betulla 260gr per the Feral File listing; her own site lists Fedrigoni Old Mill 300gr -- the conflict is unresolved, C1771, and both are recorded). The controls are the artist's own: spacebar generates a new image, "s" saves a PNG, "p" pauses or plays the animation.

**Takeaway:** the line and the colour are not two halves of one drawing; they are two drawings that happen to share a sheet. A piece where the machine paints is a print; a piece where the machine draws the line and the hand paints the gradient -- and the two can be sold separately -- is a negotiation made visible.

## M8 -- Real data driving bands

The mechanism is eighty-five years of weather, mapped field by field onto geometry. The Anatomy of Sensitivity (25 December 2024-20 January 2025, Gallery of Contemporary Art Pancevo) is part two of the ongoing "anatomies" series; all parts are loosely based on the same algorithm, gradually diverging from the initial set of instructions, each part revealing analogies between human conditions and code development. The theme is personal sensitivity and insensitivity: the ability to perceive and process information, surprising loss of reaction, overreaction. The medium is live code plus four polyptych cyanotypes, 70 by 70 cm.

The mapping is the mechanism, and it is the most mechanically detailed work in the catalogue. The code is driven by environmental and meteorological data -- mostly sun-radiation data, to which humans are objectively sensitive -- from open-meteo.com, 1940 to end of 2024, for her home city (44.82N 20.45E). Where sunshine duration is greater than zero, random three-to-six rows in sequence are chosen to generate bands. Cloud coverage decides whether bands are striped or chaotic; wind speed sets the number of stripes in the bends; shortwave solar radiation (GHI) sets band size; direct solar radiation sets vertical position; diffuse solar radiation (DHI) sets the band start angle; band length is direct normal irradiance (DNI); remaining angles along the bands come from GTI, terrestrial solar radiation, and instant values of GHI, direct, DHI, DNI, GTI, and terrestrial. All values are normalised before use. In the exhibition, live code executed for a random date and time every few minutes, on a Raspberry Pi with a 4-inch LCD screen inside a custom frame. The physical part is cyanotype -- an iron-compound process sensitive to UVA sunlight, a second level of randomness via formula mixes, exposure times, layer distances and angles, mask placements. The code is split into layers (bands, numbers, grids) to allow random errors and blurs with offset placements. The prints were made first on rice paper (35 by 35 cm), then mounted on watercolour paper in groups of four from the same year (1943, 1984, 2009, 2018).

The mechanism matters because the data is not decoration and not a seed. Every field of the weather record becomes a visible property of a band, and the band is the weather, drawn. The sun that exposed the cyanotype is the same sun the data describes: the process and the subject are the same light. A piece that uses random numbers is a generator; a piece that uses eighty-five years of sunshine as its random numbers is a diary the sky kept.

**Takeaway:** the data is not an input; it is the material. A piece that randomises is a system; a piece whose every band is a year of sun, a cloud, a wind -- and whose print was itself exposed by the sun -- is a weather report that learned to feel.

## Aesthetic logic: the concept is the system, the walk is the hand

The logic is one sentence, hers: "if you start with a strong concept it doesn't need to be as algorithmically complex." Everything in the practice is a way of letting a concept do the work a complicated algorithm would otherwise do: a colour atlas that needs only four colours and a multiply; a LeWitt instruction that needs only density and proximity; a phenakistoscope that needs only RPM, fps, and a slice count; eighty-five years of sun that need only a field-by-field mapping onto bands. The algorithm is deliberately simple; the concept is deliberately strong; the watching is where the complexity lives.

Two strands braid through the whole catalogue and never separate: data and the body. Data -- colour atlases, gender statistics, sun radiation, vertex coordinates -- is the material the systems are built from, and the systems are honest about it: the figure looks like a chart because it is a chart, and the chart is allowed to mean nothing (Chromatlas) or everything (Sensitivity) or exactly what the numbers say (Code Weaveresses). The body -- the walk, the travelling agent, the paper plane circling home, the human figure dissolved into vertex paths -- is what the data is for: a line with a life, a path that hesitates, returns, or never meets. The random walk is the meeting point: a mathematical procedure that behaves like a living thing, wandering without a destination and arriving, every time, somewhere worth watching.

The palette follows the same logic. Colour comes from classification books and from two or three chosen hues, not from a formula -- and the third colour, the one the viewer actually notices, is produced by the overlap, never selected. The picture is built the way a collector builds a drawer: one concept per drawer, one system per concept, the drawers accumulating into a series. That is the difference between her and the formula-first generative artist: the system is not there to find a beautiful equation; it is there to hold a concept the way a hand would hold a specimen -- carefully, in a labelled drawer, with room for it to move.

## Works catalogue

**W01 -- Digital Dreams (2005, solo exhibition, SKC gallery, Belgrade).** Master's final project; installation of a series of animations on computer monitors. Premise: if machines became intelligent enough to dream, what would they dream? The animations were collages of materials she imagined computer owners would expose their computers to. The first machine-dream in the catalogue, and the title the whole practice keeps answering.

**W02 -- Doctoral interactive game-poems (PhD era, pre-2011; Flash).** Interactive pieces built in Flash during doctoral studies. Game mechanics and game algorithms build environments where players explore short stories and poems; solving puzzles and playing the game unlocks new chunks of story. The puzzles were always different -- already generative in that sense. No individual titles evidenced; the entry exists to keep the practice honest.

**W03 -- Converging Horizontals (undated on artist site; pre-2021 practice).** "Algorithmic score for 4 live and 12 pre-recorded parts" -- Buchla (4 pre-recorded tracks), flute (2), cello (2), 4 processed voices (pre-recorded). Practice-research in musical structuring and notation with a generative system programmed in Processing. The algorithm generates four random wavy horizontal lines under a set of rules; each line sits in the comfortable range of a singing voice (soprano, alto, tenor, bass) tuned to 434 Hz. Each line is performed live or played as one of three recording types. Seed for the first composition, 2512, was randomly chosen by the program. The graphic score is used by singers and visible to the audience during performance. Inspired by accidental vertical cross-sections between four voices in continuous linear motion. Concept and realisation: Svetlana Maras; score and animation: Jovanic. Date not evidenced (L2-F10).

**W04 -- First hicetnunc mint (2021, code; title not evidenced).** Her first mint on hicetnunc was code, and she then maintained a practice of minting exclusively code. Even editioned pieces generate a different variation on every view. The rule, stated once, kept ever since.

**W09 -- Chromatlas (10 volumes, developed over about a year, c. 2021-22).** Her breakout series and the work that grew her Instagram following. Ten volumes, an evolving idea -- she did not know at the start it would be ten. Concept: the visual language of data science plus vintage colour-classification books (hence the name: chromatlas, a colour atlas). Explores apophenia -- viewers attributing their own meaning to the work, the tendency to see patterns in random elements. Palette: limited to eight colours, two of them similar shades of red, so seven in effect. She exhibited the series with a big book showing two cherry-picked iterations of each volume plus a scientific-style poster. **W14 -- Code Weaveresses (December 2022, fxhash #21986; edition of 64).** Part of FEMGEN, curated by Micol Ap (@verticalcrypto) with @RtClick_Save and @artblocks_io. Spark: Armin Hofmann's exercises in style and composition (Graphic Design Manual) -- dots, circles, lines, opposed components in confrontation (thin-thick, solid-transparent, softened-hardened line), extended from 2D into 3D. The opposed components began to look like weaving -- visually similar to Bauhaus women weavers (Gunta Stolzl, Anni Albers, Gertrud Preiswerk). The piece folds in real gender data: she sampled female-vs-male student numbers at the Faculty of Mathematics and the University of Arts (where she studied and works) and graduate gender distribution, and implemented some of those percentages in the distribution of shapes in the final compositions -- then realised she is the only female professor (of 5) teaching main subjects in the doctoral digital-arts programme. Title uses the dated feminine "weaveress" deliberately; in Serbian the dominant noun for a weaver is the feminine "tkalja", the male form so rare it is missing from some dictionaries. Her own words: "When a group is underrepresented, people come to take that underrepresentation as the norm."

**W15 -- A leap, a splash, a walk (20 March [2023], fxhash #26062; edition 200).** Part of THE PASSAGE: Pitch Drop -- an interactive minting experience where the minter's choices determine the token's design. Inspired by the Pitch Drop Experiment (running since 1927; only nine drops fallen, never witnessed live) and by Eadweard Muybridge's 1878 horse sequence. Concept: time and change, capturing a moment, how we manipulate time itself. Algorithmically generated shapes recorded through subtle changes in time and space, in the style of vintage scientific illustrations, film strips, long-exposure photos, comic-book panels. Each frame is one fraction of a second later than the previous one, and the whole frame view is slightly shifted. The minter's actions inside the Passage influence everything: number of rows and columns in the grid, colour palettes, shapes, sizes of objects. References Henri Bergson ("the essence of time is that it goes by"), Futurists and Giacomo Balla and Marcel Duchamp, and a Radiolab episode on Muybridge. Year 2023 inferred from the page's 2023/04 upload path, not stated (C1772).

**W16 -- Latitudes (October 2023; solo exhibition, MONO Tunis Gallery; released on highlight.xyz).** Travelling agents, all travelling in the same direction along an infinite path. The persistent question while making it: "where are they going?" -- plus the poetic, sad realisation that parallel movements diminish the chance of intersections on those paths. Curated by Kenza Zouari. Visual specifics, edition, and the highlight.xyz project page not read.

**W17 -- line(); (solo exhibition, December 2023; Centre for Graphic Arts and Visual Researches, Belgrade).** Took one of Sol LeWitt's etchings as a starting point -- not the artwork but the instructions, the words describing the piece and how to make it, about drawing different types of lines: straight, wavy, broken. She explored what she could make strictly inside those limits, saving "what if..." ideas for later projects. The installation solved her Chromatlas problem (indecision choosing a finite number of outputs for print): an "infinite interactive book" -- code outputs projected onto a blank oversized book, each turn of the page generating a new output.

**W18 -- The Space in Between (November 2023; Feral File +GRAPH, curated by Casey Reas).** Generative software NFT plus 30 physical plotter drawings (each 1/1). Concept from John Baldessari: "when you're looking at two things, don't look at them, look between them." The space between is vibrant and full of life; the space outside frames the central area of activation. Positive and negative space, a reference to life emerging through cracks in concrete; the artist reacting to her lived experience of moving through Belgrade. The active area: a block of hand-painted gradient colour plus an undulating cluster of plant-like organisms. Process: she draws the black line with a plotter first, then cuts a custom adhesive mask to keep the rest of the paper clean while applying dry pastels for a custom gradient per drawing. The software NFT contains millions of other images beyond the primary one per piece; the NFT and the plotter drawing are NOT linked -- the NFT can trade on secondary while the drawing stays with the first collector. Materials: Fedrigoni Woodstock Betulla 260gr (Feral File listing) -- her own site lists Fedrigoni Old Mill 300gr (see C1771); Fine Liner 0.1; KOH-I-NOOR dry pastels over an adhesive stencil cut on a cutter. Controls: spacebar generates a new image, "s" saves PNG, "p" pause/play animation. Context: +GRAPH, two years after Feral File's -GRAPH (2021); five returning artists (Iskra Velitchkova, Licia He, Jovanic, Julien Gachadoat, James Merrill) plus Joanie Lemercier.

**W19 -- Phenakistoscopes (2024-ongoing; "should be a big project").** Code-generated colourful phenakistoscope discs, animated when put in motion on a record player and viewed through a camera with a fixed frame rate -- seen as continuous looping animations. She generates many variations fast with code and randomness, within tight constraints. Material research is central: testing prints on different materials under different lighting; latest test is direct print on plexiglass (perfect colour but grainy; relief visible at angles; needs bright light to record without blur) versus matte paper (preferred). Her curator asked for about ten rotating on a wall, so she is buying record-player parts and motors from AliExpress. Intent: a piece that works in a gallery, physical space, or home -- you play the record and see it through your own phone and record it. No finished exhibition, edition, or disc titles evidenced; the project was still in development at interview time (April 2024).

**W20 -- The Anatomy of Fracture (8 October 2024; fxhash [Base]; 200 editions, 0.01 ETH, public mint 11am ET).** Part of DISRUPT, Galerie Data, Paris (3-26 October 2024, opening 4 October), with Florian Zumbrunn; fx(hash) x Galerie Data collaboration -- a drop on fx(hash) in parallel with the gallery show. Continuation of the research begun with line();: drawing infinite combinations of lines prompted by a Sol LeWitt work's title and instructions. The algorithm plays with the density and proximity of lines to produce dimensional visual forms, "as if they were physically present". Concept: communication-miscommunication, language imperfections, disruptions of verbal precision, unpredictable visual incidents from loose interpretations of spoken language, embedded ambiguity in the code. "Hidden inside every edition... is a unique sequence of fractures" (artist tweet, 9 October 2024). Physical counterparts: paper objects #1-4, archival inkjet on Fedrigoni Woodstock Betulla 285gr, hand cut and glued; plus paper prints. Curators: Gabrielle Debeuret and Olha Pylypenko.

**W21 -- The Anatomy of Sensitivity (25 December 2024-20 January 2025; Gallery of Contemporary Art Pancevo).** Part 2 of the ongoing "anatomies" series; all parts loosely based on the same algorithm that gradually diverges from the initial set of instructions. Each part reveals analogies between human conditions and code development. Theme: personal sensitivity and insensitivity levels, ability to perceive and process information, surprising loss of reaction and overreaction. Medium: live code plus 4 polyptych cyanotypes 70 by 70 cm. Code driven by environmental and meteorological data -- mostly sun-radiation data, to which humans are objectively sensitive -- from open-meteo.com, 1940 to end of 2024, for her home city (44.82N 20.45E). Where sunshine duration is greater than zero, random 3-6 rows in sequence are chosen to generate bands. Cloud coverage decides whether bands are striped or chaotic; wind speed sets the number of stripes in the bends; shortwave solar radiation (GHI) sets band size; direct solar radiation sets vertical position; diffuse solar radiation (DHI) sets the band start angle; band length is direct normal irradiance (DNI); remaining angles along the bands come from GTI, terrestrial solar radiation, and instant values of GHI, direct, DHI, DNI, GTI, and terrestrial. All values normalised before use. In the exhibition, live code executed for a random date and time every few minutes; custom frame hosts a Raspberry Pi with a 4-inch LCD screen. Physical part: cyanotype (iron-compound process sensitive to UVA sunlight) -- a second level of randomness via formula mixes, exposure times, layer distances and angles, mask placements. Code split into layers (bands, numbers, grids) to allow random errors and blurs with offset placements. Prints first on rice paper (35 by 35 cm), then mounted on watercolour paper in groups of four from the same year (1943, 1984, 2009, 2018). Group show: PLACE OF RETURN / XVIII NEW TECHNOLOGIES, curated by Milica Lapcevic.

**W22 -- Returns (2025; objkt.one; Museum of the Moving Image, "Compositions in Code").** Long-form generative release on objkt.one, paired with Processing pioneer Marius Watz in the first diptych of MoMI's "Compositions in Code" (curator Regina Harsanyi: "a diptych that reflects a spectrum of transience and stillness"). Her side: real-time looping lines, "enduring, ever-evolving patterns"; his: fleeting digital projections captured as singular artefacts. Originally planned as one older Processing piece next to a newer p5.js piece; for the objkt.one release both were reworked into animated p5.js. Concept: a comeback to earlier topics and algorithms. A sequence of bands emerges, "walking" pseudorandomly in homage to her favoured random-walk algorithm; they bounce off screen edges and eventually decay, yet the cycle renews with each new birth. Background graphics symbolise periodic trends and the cyclic nature of life and time (seasonal, annual, monthly, daily); foreground "paper planes" fly in circles, always returning to their starting position. Outputs generated from five different sorts of bands; short palette, but gradients between those colours give surprising variation. For the MoMI installation she prepared an infinite generative animation that changes variables after each path is drawn and erased. Conceptually a return to Latitudes (W16): parallel travelling agents versus the searching, returning random walk. Both artists work with lines: his rendered in colour tape on the wall, hers as animated curvy lines. MoMI exhibition dates and edition size not evidenced (L2-F6).

**W23 -- Echoes of Forgotten Tales (EmProps collab with Danielle King; c. 2024).** Generative-AI project collaboration with Danielle King (King introduced her to AI; Jovanic now trains King in coding; weekly meetings continue). Listed as an EmProps collaboration. No dedicated page, date, platform, or visual description found (L2-F9).

**W24 -- Please Respond, Day 9 (released 14 May 2026; edition 50; 0.07 ETH; sold out).** Sequential chain piece, 10 artists over 10 days, each responding to the previous artist's code -- likened by the artist to Chinese whispers and the Surrealists' exquisite corpse. She was 9th of 10; the wait caused anxiety about what the previous artist (Juan) would hand over. Juan uses essentially the same programming techniques, except he uses shaders (GLSL) for postproduction; removing that layer made his code legible. Process: deconstructed Juan's code, ran a week of quick experiments; inspected the 3D models embedded in his pieces in Maya to see what data could be extracted; tested radial and cylindrical structural patterns. The vertex positions of one 3D model gave more than enough data. Her core algorithm: random walk connecting the model's vertex coordinates into one or more continuous paths. Three strategies tested: (1) original vertex order from the model's topology (interesting but mixed very long and very short steps); (2) nearest-neighbour continuous paths; (3) grouping vertices with convex hull. Final piece combines the inherited vertex arrangement from the original topology with the nearest-neighbour method. She randomly frames only a portion of the vertex dataset, avoiding showing the full human figure (the dominant element in Juan's practice) while letting observant viewers recognise fragments of the body; an earlier version showed the whole figure in the background with a square overlay marking selected vertices, later replaced by her own stick figure, also readable as a simplified rigging skeleton. She kept Juan's recognisable 3D human figure plus its extractable data, treating them as a flexible dataset like any set of random values. Juan's surname not given on the page (C1774).
