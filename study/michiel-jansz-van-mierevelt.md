# Michiel Jansz van Mierevelt: Study Notes

**Site:** Michiel Jansz van Mierevelt (1566/67-1641), Dutch Golden Age portrait painter of Delft, son of a goldsmith, and the most successful portraitist of the Northern Netherlands at the start of the Golden Age. Born in Delft on 1 May (the year is disputed, 1566 or 1567 - see C346), son of the goldsmith Jan Michielsz van Mierevelt (1528-92), himself said to have been a Mennonite. Apprenticed first to the copperplate engraver Hieronymus Wierix in Antwerp, then pupil in Delft of Willem Willemsz and of Augustijn, a pupil of Anthonie van Montfoort (Blocklandt), and then pupil of Blocklandt himself in Utrecht, presumably in 1581 at the age of 14 for two years and three months, where he learned to handle paint and became accomplished in history painting (Bikker). He returned to Delft after Blocklandt's death in 1583 (Artsy and the National Gallery of Scotland give 1583; Sotheby's says c. 1589 - see C357). He joined the Delft painters' guild in 1587 and served as warden in 1589-90 and again in 1611-12 (Bikker); he registered as a member of the Guild of St Luke in The Hague in 1625 (Britannica; National Portrait Gallery; Government Art Collection) - the two dates belong to two different guilds, not to one career (see C333). Much to the regret of his father he abandoned history painting for the more lucrative portraiture, first adhering to the style of his fellow townsman Jacob Willemsz Delff, and from 1590 devoted himself almost entirely to portraiture (Bikker; Artsy; National Portrait Gallery). Van Mander's Schilder-boeck (1604) already praised him specifically as a portraitist. In 1607 the Delft authorities commissioned him to portray the stadholder, Prince Maurits (panel, 110 x 98 cm, Prinsenhof, Delft), and in the same year he became official painter to the Stadholder's Court in The Hague, a position he held for about a quarter of a century until Honthorst usurped it (Bikker); he went on working for Frederik Hendrik too. Over 500 paintings are or have been attributed to him (Wikipedia, citing the RKD entry); Houbraken says he painted 5,000 portraits and Sandrart 10,000 (Bikker) - three different figures, keep them distinct (see C347). Comparatively few of the two thousand or more portraits that bear his name are wholly his own handiwork (1911 Britannica). His sons Pieter (1596-1623) and Jan (1604-33) worked as assistants, his son-in-law Willem Jacobsz Delff (1580-1638) engraved the portraits, and his grandson Jacob Willemsz Delff (1619-61) took over the workshop on his death. The king of Sweden and the Count Palatine of Neuburg presented him with golden chains; Archduke Albert gave him a pension; Charles I vainly endeavoured to induce him to visit the English court (Britannica; Wikipedia). Prince Henry and Charles I both tried, in separate attempts - 1611 negotiations via Sir Edward Conway for Henry, Prince of Wales, and Charles I's attempt in 1625, with the plague alleged as the blocker (Gerson Digital, Hearn/van Leeuwen 2022). In his autobiography of 1631 Constantijn Huygens described Mierevelt as "the leader in this field [portraiture]" (Artsy/The Weiss Gallery - single-sourced, see C360). Van Dyck painted his portrait; Delff engraved it. He married twice, in 1589 and 1633 (Bikker); two children died at Oude Delft in 1623 and his wife died in 1628 (Kalden). He died a wealthy man in Delft on 27 June 1641 (Bikker; Britannica); his posthumous inventory included bonds valued at 14,500 guilders and paintings by Cornelis van Haarlem and Carel van Mander (Kalden). A Mennonite, part of a minority officially frowned upon by the Dutch Reformed majority, yet given important commissions; Duke Albertus of Austria called him to Brussels and granted him the very unusual privilege of practicing his Mennonite religion undisturbed, as Sandrart reports (GAMEO). ## M1. The engraver's line: dry, linear style, severe drawing

Trained first as a copperplate engraver under Hieronymus Wierix, and a calligrapher by age eight - he could write in calligraphy as fine as the best Delft schoolmaster (Kalden, citing Oosterloo). His portraits are built on exact contour and linear definition rather than painterly blending. Britannica: "sincerity, severe drawing and harmonious colour". Sotheby's: "a rather dry, linear style". The watchable behaviour is faces that read as drawn outlines filled with flat, even modelling; edges stay crisp, hair and lace are line-work. The engraver's training is the reason the factory could work at all: a line can be copied by an assistant, a painterly touch cannot.

**Takeaway:** the portrait is an engraving that happens to be painted: line first, modelling second, and the brush never shows.

## M2. Likeness without flattery

"Painting his customers very true to nature" (CODART); works "characterized by a rather dry, linear style with very little flattery" (Sotheby's). The watchable behaviour is unidealised faces - age, asymmetry and blemish retained; the sitter looks like a specific person, not a type. This is the direct opposite of Pourbus's court switch (M8 in #526), where the face is idealised and the dress tells the truth. In Mierevelt both tell the truth, and the truth is the product: a Protestant court that distrusted flattery bought likeness the way a Catholic court bought splendour.

**Takeaway:** flattery is the one luxury the Delft portrait does not stock; the likeness is the goods.

## M3. Detail-first, composition-second

"In general, Van Mierevelt's portraits show great attention to detail and little compositional adventure" (Bikker). The watchable behaviour is that the face and costume carry the picture while pose and background stay conventional - a bust or half-length against a plain ground, three-quarter profile, repeated for decades. From 1607 to 1640 the face stays tightly modelled, the ground dark, the composition frontal and frontal-ish. The evolution is in scale, market and looseness of handling, not in the visual language (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** the composition is a shelf; the detail is the stock. Nothing moves because nothing needs to move.

## M4. Costume, lace and jewellery as status ledger

Rank is legible from the objects painted: armour, lace collars, gold chains, orders, heraldry. The golden chains he himself received from the king of Sweden and the Count Palatine of Neuburg belong to the same economy of visible honour his portraits record. The watchable behaviour is that the eye is led from the face down to the collar, chain and coat of arms; the inventory of rank is painted as carefully as the face. The Prado lady (W9) carries a heraldic shield upper left and her age and the year inscribed; the Van Dalen pair (W18-W19) carry the family arms upper left and right.

**Takeaway:** a Mierevelt portrait is a ledger with a face at the top; read the objects and you have read the sitter's rank.

## M5. The fixed formats: bust, half-length, three-quarter, full-length

A small set of framing templates reused across the whole career: bust; half-length in three-quarter profile (the 1640 Hooft pair); three-quarter-length versions made from half-length prototypes (the 1640 Van Dalen pair versus the Met half-lengths); life-size full-length for the stadholder (Maurice, 220.3 x 143.5 cm, W15); and the small portable copper oval, 28 x 23 cm (W5), for the collectible dynastic series. The watchable behaviour is that the same sitter reappears at different scales in the same pose; scale, not composition, is the variable. Four near-identical knee-length Maurits portraits were painted in and shortly after 1607 with no identifiable prototype (Jansen/Ekkart/Verhave 2011) - the template is the product.

**Takeaway:** the format is the mould and the sitter is the casting; change the scale and you change the price, not the picture.

## M6. The inscribed face: age, year and signature painted into the picture

"AEtatis. 23. / A.o 1640 / M. Miereveld" (Hooft, centre left); "AEtatis. 70. / A.o 1640. / M. Miereveld" (Jacob van Dalen); "Ao 1632 / M. Miereveld" (Sotheby's lot 146); "AEtatis 32 / A.o 1620" (Prado lady, W9); "AEtatis. 56 / A.o 1639 / M. Miereveld" (Margaretha van Clootwijk, W18). The watchable behaviour is text sitting on the panel as part of the image - a data label inside the portrait, giving the sitter's age at sitting and the year, in the same paint as the face. The inscription is what makes the portrait a document rather than a picture: it dates the likeness, and it dates the stock. The three-quarter-length Van Dalen of 1640 is inscribed "AEtatis. 70", which pins its completion to the last six weeks of 1640 (Van Dalen was born 21 November 1570) - the workshop painting the same sitters twice in one year, at two scales, and the inscription is the receipt.

**Takeaway:** the portrait carries its own catalogue entry on its face; the paint is the paperwork.

## M7. The prototype-and-replica factory: the winckel stock

"The inventory of his shop reveals that he kept a supply of replicas of his most famous sitters on hand" (Bikker). His portraits "are copied dozens of times - in several sizes - and sold in his 'winckel' (workshop)" (CODART). Even decades later, Nicolaes Maes copied the 1640 Hooft prototypes (Aegje shown in a gown of c. 1675, after her death in 1664). The watchable behaviour is that the same face recurs at different scales and dates across collections; buying a Mierevelt could mean buying off the shelf. The factory's inventory practice is visible in the paint itself: the 1639 Jacob Cats (W17) shows the round collar and tabbaard of the 1634 portrait (W16) as a pentimento beneath updated clothing - a stock replica kept and updated by overpainting, then reframed in a carved gilt frame when Cats furnished his Sorghvliet estate around 1652. The portrait is a maintained object, not a frozen likeness.

**Takeaway:** the shop is the artwork: the prototype is the mould, the stock is the catalogue, and the overpainting is the restocking.

## M8. The signed copy

"Distinguishing Mierevelt's hand is complicated by his habit of signing copies by his studio assistants" (Government Art Collection). "Many of the pictures painted by his pupils in his studio were signed by him; this practice had a depreciating effect on his reputation as well as on the value of his paintings" (GAMEO). The watchable behaviour is an identical "M. Miereveld" signature on works of visibly different quality; the signature is a brand mark, not a certificate of autograph work. Six of the twenty catalogue works are workshop or studio, and the lost 1610 Frederik Hendrik prototype (W4) survives only as studio replicas. The signature is the warranty on the brand, and the brand is what the customer bought.

**Takeaway:** the signature does not say who painted it; it says whose shop sold it.

## M9. The family workshop

Sons Pieter (1596-1623) and Jan (1604-33) worked as assistants; their work "could easily pass for his father's" (Houbraken, via the Pieter article); son-in-law Willem Jacobsz Delff (1580-1638) engraved the portraits; grandson Jacob Willemsz Delff (1619-61) inherited the workshop. (Wikipedia's "son-in-law Jacob Delff" conflates Willem Jacobsz Delff with his son Jacob Willemsz Delff - see C355.) The watchable behaviour is several family hands inside one brand; a son's picture is indistinguishable from the father's except by signature or archive. The attribution problem is the family story: the Anatomy Lesson (W6) is attributed by the RKD to Pieter, not Michiel, and the only person looking out at the viewer in it is Pieter himself.

**Takeaway:** the workshop is a family, and the family is the brand; the hand is not the signature.

## M10. The reproductive engraving as distribution

"Many of his portraits have been reproduced in line by the leading Dutch engravers of his time" (Britannica). Delff's engravings disseminated the inventions; the National Portrait Gallery alone holds dozens of Delff prints after Mierevelt (Carleton, Princess Elizabeth, Grotius, Buckingham, Gustavus Adolphus, Spinola, Cats). The watchable behaviour is that the same portrait exists as a painting in one city and as a print in many; the engraved face becomes the public image of the sitter. The engraving is the other half of the factory: the painting is the prototype, the print is the distribution, and the sitter's fame travels in paper. This closes the circle with M1: the boy who learned the burin first ended by having his paintings turned back into burin-work.

**Takeaway:** the portrait's real edition is the print run; the painting is the plate.

## M11. The diplomatic portrait: the travelling likeness

"Thereafter, he was regularly employed to paint official portraits destined as diplomatic gifts" (Artsy). Portraits were sent to London (Carleton to Arundel, 1621); English sitters sat at The Hague; for English clients he "chose to use thicker panels for their portraits" - the only technical change he made for them (Gerson Digital, note 14, citing Jansen/Verhave 2015). The watchable behaviour is the portrait as an export object - the same image in London and The Hague; a thicker, sturdier panel for the sea crossing. In 1613, when Arundel accompanied Princess Elizabeth and the Count Palatine Friedrich to the Palatinate, "Mierevelt was obliged to follow the procession to the borders of the States General" and produced portraits of Arundel and others in his entourage (Gerson). The portrait goes where the painter cannot: England wanted the man and got the panels instead.

**Takeaway:** the portrait is the ambassador; the painter stays home and the likeness does the travelling.

## M12. The group portrait and the anatomy lesson

The Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Willem van der Meer (1617), oil on canvas, 144 x 198 cm, Prinsenhof, Delft - Mierevelt's only large-scale group portrait and his only anatomy lesson, a civic commission of a completely different order from the single-figure court portraits. It predates Rembrandt's Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp (1632) by fifteen years and is the model for the genre in the north. The watchable behaviour is a room of men arranged around a body, all looking at the lesson except one face that looks out at the viewer - the painter smuggled into his own picture. "The only person looking at the doctor is Pieter Mierevelt himself" (Wikipedia, Pieter article). Attribution is disputed and must be presented as such: the RKD attributes the painting to his son Pieter van Mierevelt (1596-1623); Sotheby's 2010 note calls it a group portrait by van Mierevelt and his son Pieter. Jacob van Dalen, depicted in it, was painted again as a single portrait 23 years later (1640, W19) - Mierevelt painted him across 23 years.

**Takeaway:** the one picture where the workshop looks back at you is the one picture whose author is in doubt.

## M13. The landscape-background collaboration

"He also collaborated with Jan Martsen, who painted landscapes backgrounds for some of his portraits" (Government Art Collection). The watchable behaviour is a portrait whose background is a different hand's landscape - the sitter in one style, the world behind him in another. This is the one mechanism in the study that rests on a single source (the Government Art Collection page; no second source found in 16 searches), so it is carried as thin and not built on further. If it holds, it is the factory's one division of labour by subject rather than by sitter: the face is the brand, the background is subcontracted.

**Takeaway:** even the background could be bought in; only the face had to be Mierevelt's.

## M14. The late loosening against the conservative drift

"His later paintings, however, are more animated, loosely painted productions" (Bikker); the 1640 Hooft pair, "painted toward the end of Van Mierevelt's life, [is] astonishingly vivacious in comparison to the artist's earlier output" (Rijksmuseum) - the unbuttoned doublet, the slit sleeve revealing lace jabot and shirt, the loose thin background with visible brushmarking and the light ground showing through. Kalden reads the same career the other way: "As the years moved on he slowly became more and more conservative if not old-fashioned", his works "always remained distant, aloof and of a subdued high-class distinction". The watchable behaviour is that late brushwork loosens and the sitters liven while the format stays frozen - two readings of one late style, both citable. He was still painting major pairs in 1640-41 (Hooft, Van Dalen; the Huygens portrait 1641) and died wealthy. The factory ends not with exhaustion but with a small, unexpected loosening.

**Takeaway:** the last works are the freest because the machine was already built; the painter could finally paint.

## M15. The court-face canon: the face we remember

"As no other did the portrait painter from Delft Michiel Jansz van Mierevelt (1566-1641) paint the portraits of well-known 17th-century Dutchmen, giving them the face with which we associate them to this day" - Maurits, Hugo de Groot, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, Pieter Cornelisz Hooft, Jacob Cats, Constantijn Huygens (CODART/Prinsenhof). "At the start of the Golden Age, van Mierevelt is the most successful portrait painter of the Northern Netherlands." The watchable behaviour is that one painter's version of a face becomes the historical face, repeated in textbooks and prints for 400 years. Oldenbarnevelt (W7) is the hard case: the portrait of the Republic's fallen statesman, painted at the exact moment of his destruction by Maurits, the same patron who made Mierevelt. Mierevelt painted both the victor and the victim, and both faces stuck.

**Takeaway:** the factory did not just supply portraits; it supplied history with its faces.

## Works

### Early phase (to 1607): Delft, panel, burgher emblem

**W1 -- Double Portrait of a Husband and Wife with Tulip, Bulb, and Shells, 1609.** Oil on panel. Dimensions **[UNVERIFIED]** - not found in any source read this session. Private collection (sold Sotheby's New York, 2011, lot 282). Half-length husband and wife; he holds a tulip and a bulb, shells arranged on a shelf below. Signed lower left (partly strengthened) "M.J. Miere...lt", inscribed and dated upper left "AE...tis 3(9?)/1609". The earliest securely dated surviving painting in this catalogue and the key early-Delft work. Not a court portrait but a burgher marriage portrait, and already a vanitas: the tulip (wealth, prized in 1609 Holland) blooms briefly from the bulb and dies; the empty shells are departed life. Sotheby's notes it may be commemorative, commissioned by one sitter after the other's death. It shows the young Mierevelt already working in the emblematic, object-loaded mode that the court portraits would later strip away. Attribution: autograph (signed and dated).

### Court phase I (1607-1613): the Orange template

**W2 -- Portrait of Prince Maurits (Maurice of Orange), 1607.** Oil on panel. **[VERIFIED]** 110 x 98 cm (Rijksmuseum catalogue entry for the Lyon copy, citing "Panel, 110 x 98 cm; Delft, Stedelijk Museum Het Prinsenhof"). Museum Prinsenhof, Delft. Maurits in armour, bust/three-quarter length - the portrait commissioned by the Delft authorities in 1607 that launched Mierevelt's career (Bikker: his enormous output "began in earnest with the 1607 commission from the Delft authorities to portray the stadholder, Prince Maurits"). The founding commission. Four near-identical knee-length Maurits portraits were painted in and shortly after 1607; no single prototype can be identified (Jansen/Ekkart/Verhave 2011). It is the template from which dozens of Maurits portraits descend - the factory's first product line. Attribution: autograph prototype (with many studio replicas and copies; the Rijksmuseum's SK-A-2100 is a small copper copy by Jacob Lyon after c. 1612).

**W3 -- Portrait of Philip William, Prince of Orange, 1608.** Oil on canvas. **[VERIFIED]** 122.3 x 108.3 cm (Wikimedia Commons file data, citing RKDimages 195068 and Rijksmuseum). Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, SK-A-256. Philip William (1554-1618), eldest son of William the Silent, three-quarter length in armour. Painted the year after the Maurits commission, it extends the Orange series to the whole dynasty - Mierevelt was already building the visual archive of the House of Orange, not painting one-off likenesses. The canvas support (rather than panel) at this scale is notable for the early period. Attribution: autograph.

**W4 -- Portrait of Frederik Hendrik, Prince of Orange, c. 1610 (studio replica after a lost prototype).** Oil on panel. **[VERIFIED]** 110 x 84 cm (Rijksmuseum catalogue entry, citing "studio replica in Delft, Stedelijk Museum Het Prinsenhof; panel, 110 x 84 cm", Tiethoff-Spliethoff 1978). Museum Prinsenhof, Delft (studio replica; the c. 1610 prototype is lost, known only through studio replicas and copies). The young Frederik Hendrik, bust length. A Sotheby's workshop fragment (34.6 x 22.9 cm) is described as a variant of the Prinsenhof portrait (inv. PDS 68c) and as relating to "another prototype, now lost, by Mierevelt of about 1610". The clearest early evidence of the workshop system - the prototype is gone, the replicas survive, and the composition was still being copied in fragments in the 1630s. It also shows the second Orange brother entering the portrait series before he became stadholder (1625). Attribution: studio replica after lost autograph prototype - **not autograph**.

**W5 -- Portrait of Frederick V, Elector Palatine, as King of Bohemia, c. 1619-1620.** Oil on copper, oval. **[VERIFIED]** 28 x 23 cm (Mauritshuis Abridged Catalogue 1910, no. 101; same dimensions as nos. 96-100, the six-piece Orange series, purchased 1828). ### Court phase III (1625-1641): the factory at full scale, then a loosening

## Evolution

### Phase 1 - Delft, before 1607: the burgher portraitist

The earliest dated painting (W1, 1609) is a burgher double portrait loaded with vanitas emblems - tulip, bulb, shells. Before that, the record is engravings (item 14 above) and still lifes (per Britannica: "devoting himself first to still lifes"). The early work is small, panel-based, object-rich, and local: Delft burghers, not princes. The goldsmith's son's training shows in the finish - precise, enamel-smooth, every jewel and lace point accounted for. Few early portraits survive, even fewer history paintings, and none at all of the kitchen pieces reported by Van Mander (Bikker).

### Phase 2 - 1607-1613: the Orange commission and the template

The 1607 Maurits commission (W2) is the hinge. Within a year Mierevelt is painting the whole Orange dynasty (W3, Philip William, 1608; W4, Frederik Hendrik, c. 1610) and foreign Protestant royalty (W5's type begins with Frederick V, 1613). The format settles: bust or three-quarter length, dark ground, armour or black with a white ruff, the sitter's face modelled tightly, the rest of the picture increasingly formulaic. Four near-identical Maurits portraits in 1607 with no identifiable prototype already show the workshop logic: paint the type, repeat it.

### Phase 3 - 1613-1625: court painter, two markets at once

Appointed official painter to the Prince of Orange in 1607 (NPG; Britannica says he was "painter to the court of the Princes of Orange" and lived at The Hague for a time; he joined the Hague Guild of St Luke in 1625). This phase runs two product lines in parallel: the monumental state portrait (W15, the 2.2 m full-length Maurits, c. 1613-1620) and the small portable copper oval (W5, 28 x 23 cm). Alongside them: the civic group portrait (W6, Anatomy Lesson, 1617 - probably his son Pieter's hand), the political portrait (W7, Oldenbarnevelt, at his execution), the burgher portrait (W9, Prado lady, 1620), and the foreign-diplomat portrait (W11's milieu). The style does not change much across these markets - that is the point: one severe, sincere, tightly drawn manner, applied to everyone from a prince to a surgeon's wife.

### Phase 4 - 1625-1632: the factory at full scale

After 1625 the workshop is the story. The Grotius portrait (W12) survives only as studio replicas of a lost original; the William the Silent (W13) and Frederik Hendrik (W14) of 1632 are workshop productions for a civic institution (Admiralty of the Maas), painted nearly fifty years after William's death from inherited likenesses. Houbraken's figure is 5,000 portraits, Von Sandrart's 10,000; the Rijksmuseum's own text calls the 10,000 figure "somewhat exaggerated". Over 500 paintings are or have been attributed to him (Wikipedia/RKD); "comparatively few of the two thousand or more portraits that bear his name are wholly his own handiwork" (Britannica 1911). The sons Pieter (1596-1623) and Jan (d. 1633), son-in-law Willem Jacobsz Delff, and pupils Paulus Moreelse, Jan van Ravesteyn, Daniel Mijtens, Anthonie Palamedesz. and Hendrick van Vliet did much of the painting; many pictures were signed by Michiel himself. GAMEO's summary is blunt: "Many of the pictures painted by his pupils in his studio were signed by him; this practice had a depreciating effect on his reputation."

### Phase 5 - 1632-1641: late work - repetition, then a loosening

The last decade is dominated by repetition of established types (Cats 1634 and 1639, W16-W17, with stock replicas updated by overpainting) - but the final dated works break the pattern. The Van Dalen pair (W18-W19, 1639-1640) is intimate, signed, and inscribed with ages and arms; the Hooft wedding pair (W20, 1640) is, in the Rijksmuseum's words, "astonishingly vivacious" - loose thin paint, light ground showing through, visible brushmarking, an unbuttoned doublet. The 74-year-old painter, a year before his death, paints freer than he did at 40. The factory ends not with exhaustion but with a small, unexpected loosening.

## Aesthetic logic

The Mierevelt portrait is a manufactured likeness, and its honesty is its sales pitch. Pourbus sold splendour: the face idealised, the dress exact, the portrait a display case for jewels. Mierevelt sold sincerity: the face unflattered, the dress exact, the portrait a document with its own catalogue entry painted on it. The difference is confessional and commercial at once. A Catholic court buys the portrait as ceremony; a Protestant republic buys it as evidence. The inscription - age, year, signature - is the mechanism that turns a picture into a record, and the record is what a burgher, a magistrate and a stadholder all needed: proof that this person, at this age, in this year, held this rank.

That is why the manner never changed. A factory cannot afford a new style every decade; it can afford a new scale. The bust becomes the three-quarter, the three-quarter becomes the full-length, the panel becomes the copper oval, the painting becomes the engraving - but the face stays drawn in the same severe line, because the line is the brand. The assistants could copy a line. They could not copy a touch, which is why there is no touch to copy: the dry, linear style is not a limitation the factory worked around, it is the technology that made the factory possible.

And that is why the late loosening matters. The Hooft pair of 1640 is the one moment the painter stops being the factory and paints like a man with a brush in his hand - loose thin paint, light ground showing through, an unbuttoned doublet. It is the only Mierevelt that looks as if it were painted rather than produced, and it comes a year before his death, when the machine was already built and the orders were already filled. The aesthetic logic of the whole career is in that contrast: forty years of perfect, repeatable sincerity, and one pair of portraits at the end that are sincere in a different way - not accurate, but alive.

## Positions

**P1.** Delft, born 1 May 1566/67, died 27 June 1641; lived and worked in Delft apart from Utrecht 1581-83 and Hague periods (Jeffares: Utrecht 1581-83, The Hague 1625-34).

**P2.** Son of goldsmith Jan Michielsz van Mierevelt (1528-92).

**P3.** Apprentice: Hieronymus Wierix (engraver); Willem Willemsz and Augustijn in Delft; pupil of Anthonie van Montfoort (Blocklandt) in Utrecht 1581-83.

**P4.** Delft guild 1587, warden 1589-90 and 1611-12; Hague guild member 1625.

**P5.** Official painter to the Stadholder's Court from 1607 (Maurice), held about 25 years; also painted for Frederik Hendrik.

**P6.** Married twice (1589, 1633); two children died 1623; wife died 1628.

**P7.** Died wealthy 1641 (bonds 14,500 guilders); workshop passed to grandson Jacob Willemsz Delff.

**P8.** Mennonite; epitaph by Joachim Oudaen; privilege from Archduke Albert to practise his religion undisturbed.

## Network

## Corrections
- C333. The 1625 Guild date: both dates are true, different guilds. Mierevelt was an officer of the Delft Guild of St Luke by 1589 (Sotheby's, Artsy: "became an officer of the Guild of St. Luke as early as 1589") and registered as a member of the Guild of St Luke in The Hague in 1625 (Wikipedia/RKD). The #525 carried block's "registered with the Guild of St Luke in 1625" is the Hague registration, not a Delft one; the #527 study gives both, and the C327 note in #526 stands confirmed.
- C334. The son-in-law's name: the EN Wikipedia Mierevelt article's Legacy section calls the son-in-law "Jacob Delff" (and says his portrait was "engraved by Jacob Delff"). That is wrong. The son-in-law and engraver is Willem Jacobsz Delff (c.1580-1638), married to Geertruid van Mierevelt on 14 January 1618 (1911 Britannica, RKD via Rijksmuseum, Met catalogue, Kalden). "Jacob Delff" confuses him with his father Jacob Willemsz Delff the Elder (c.1550-1601) or his son Jacob Willemsz Delff the Younger (1619-1661). The article's own infobox caption gets it right ("by Willem Jacobsz. Delff"). The study uses Willem Jacobsz Delff and notes the Wikipedia error.
- C335. Hanneman's teacher: disputed between the Ravesteyn brothers. The Leiden Collection (2024, citing guild records dated 1619) says Hanneman "studied with the portrait painter Anthony van Ravesteyn II (ca. 1580-1669), younger brother of the better-known artist Jan van Ravesteyn". Wikipedia's Jan van Ravesteyn article lists Adriaen Hanneman among Jan's pupils, and dealers repeat Jan. The guild-record evidence favours Anthony II; the #527 study records the dispute, not pick a brother silently. (Anthony II was primarily a glass painter, fl. 1614-1669.)
- C336. Ravesteyn's pupillage under Mierevelt is unproven. Wikipedia: "It is unknown who taught him how to paint, but he was a follower of the Delft portrait painter Michiel Jansz van Mierevelt." Auction houses and dealers say "probably a pupil"; the niceartgallery-style sources now question even that, noting stylistic differences in his earliest works.
