Lawrence Charles Weiner was born on 10 February 1942, premature, in Manhattan, and grew up in the South Bronx, where his parents Harold Weiner and Toba (Horowitz) Weiner ran a small candy store at 149th Street and Southern Boulevard; he was an only child, his parents Jewish but not making that a significant part of his upbringing, and he described his working-class childhood as basically happy, working part time on the docks by twelve and helping at the store from nine. [C28550] As a teenager he worked a run of manual jobs -- on an oil tanker, on docks, unloading railroad cars -- was arrested several times for anti-nuclear protests, went to Harlem to eat and listen to jazz, and learned to hotwire cars. [C28550] He attended Stuyvesant High School, cutting class frequently and crediting his early education more to the public library than to the school system, graduated early at sixteen in 1958, and studied philosophy and literature at Hunter College for less than a year before dropping out; he received no formal art education, and his first encounters with art were visits to the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Museum of Modern Art in the 1950s. [C28550] When at sixteen he told his mother he planned to become an artist because he wanted to use art to dialogue with his fellow citizens, her reply was: "Lawrence, you'll break your heart. Art is for rich people [...] and it's never going to change." [C28550]

After leaving Hunter he hitchhiked across North America, Mexico and Canada, as far as the Arctic, working on tuna and albacore boats and sleeping under bridges and in doorways, marking his progress by leaving small sculptures on the roadside, and landed in San Francisco, where he hung out with Beat poets at the City Lights Bookstore. [C28551] Back in New York he showed Abstract Expressionist paintings, all now destroyed, in coffee houses, seeing the movement as "a sign of freedom for working-class people" because its practitioners "accepted anybody's work that they thought was interesting". [C28551] His debut public artwork was the undocumented action Cratering Piece (1960), in which he set off explosives simultaneously at the four corners of a field in a park in Mill Valley, California, and declared the resulting craters sculptures; he continued crater-like explosive works in California for six years. [C28551] In 1963 he took his first trip to Europe and met curator Seth Siegelaub, who had by chance attended the same high school; his first solo show was at the Seth Siegelaub Contemporary Art gallery in 1964. [C28551] Before language he made two painting series with Siegelaub -- Propeller Paintings (1964-1966) and Removal Paintings (1966-1968) -- concerned with "the idea of painting rather than a painting", in readily available materials, all sizes and shapes at the same price, leading to the cut-out sculptures of 1966 and the notched paintings. [C28551] In 1967 sculptor John Chamberlain invited him on a night out that ended at Max's Kansas City, where he met Alice Zimmerman, a waitress; they married in 2003, had a daughter Kirsten, and soon after meeting went to Amsterdam, where Siegelaub offered them an apartment and they bought a houseboat, the Joma, living on it for years without heat, electricity or running water -- "That was not easy and not fun." [C28551] For the rest of his life he divided his time between New York City and the boat in Amsterdam. [C28551]

The turning point came in 1968, at the outdoor group exhibition "Carl Andre, Robert Barry, Lawrence Weiner" at Windham College in Putney, Vermont, organised by Siegelaub: Weiner's sculpture, a grid of 34 wooden stakes linked with twine across a grassy field, was destroyed by the students it was addressed to, who cut the twine to walk across the lawn, taking the arrangement for a surveying exercise. [C28552] Alice Zimmerman Weiner recounts that the work had "trespassed a boundary that Weiner had not been aware of"; when he saw the dismantled piece he said, "It didn't seem as if the philistines had done the work any particular harm." [C28552] The incident prompted him to realise that an artwork need not impose on physical space at all, and he began to use language as a primary material: the work "had become a manifestation of 'language + the materials referred to'". [C28552] He asserted that by using language, "there is no edge that the picture drops over or drops off. You are dealing with something completely infinite." [C28552] Joseph Kosuth stated that Weiner "changed his notion of 'place'... from the context of the canvas (which could only be specific) to a context which was 'general', yet all the while continuing his concern with specific materials and processes." [C28552] TheArtStory puts the lesson the same way: the work would have functioned better as a simple verbal description than as a physical manifestation, and he later called the shift to words "a political decision. If you wanted to make something that everybody could get, the way to do that was to make something with genuine sculptural values and to portray it in a language so that people could be able to do it themselves. Not instructions. But something that sat by itself." [C28552]

From 1968 onwards his work operated under the conditions of the Declaration of Intent, first presented in Art News in the fall of 1968 and in the catalogue of the exhibition "January 5-31, 1969": 1. THE ARTIST MAY CONSTRUCT THE PIECE; 2. THE PIECE MAY BE FABRICATED; 3. THE PIECE NEED NOT BE BUILT -- EACH BEING EQUAL AND CONSISTENT WITH THE INTENT OF THE ARTIST, THE DECISION AS TO CONDITION RESTS WITH THE RECEIVER UPON THE OCCASION OF RECEIVERSHIP. [C28553] The first condition recalls the traditional relationship between artist and artwork; the second permits the work to be fabricated by someone else; the third means the work need not be acted upon at all, it can exist as language or an idea to function as art. [C28553] His first body of work in language was the solo-exhibition artist's book Statements, published in December 1968 by The Louis Kellner Foundation and Seth Siegelaub: twenty-four works in a small 64-page paperback with no illustrations, some describing works never produced. [C28553] He also contributed text works to Siegelaub's Xerox Book, a compendium of photocopies by seven conceptual artists, and from then on most of his work took the form of text-based wall installations. [C28553] Using a contract formulated by Siegelaub and lawyer Robert Projansky, he sold pieces as documents giving owners legal possession of the concept and the freedom to realise it as they saw fit, and designated some works "public freeholds", which could never be bought or sold. [C28553] He used the artist's book format extensively, calling books in 1976 "perhaps the least impositional means of transferring information from one to another (source)", and the Vancouver Art Gallery has maintained The Lawrence Weiner Poster Archive since 1986. [C28553]

Weiner regarded his practice as sculpture, not Conceptual art, a label he spurned: "The Conceptual artist moniker makes absolutely no sense to me. I don't like the term... Why not just say sculptor?" [C28554] His abiding formulation was that art is not a metaphor upon the relationship of human beings to objects and objects to objects in relation to human beings, but a representation of an empirical existing fact, and that "the subject matter of my art is - art." [C28554] He wanted the work usable by its receiver and left open for translation, transference and transformation, so each time the work is made it is made anew. [C28554] He worked in film and video throughout his life, in parallel with the text works -- A First Quarter (1973, 85 minutes), which he called his "Godard movie", Do You Believe in Water? (1976), A Bit of Matter and a Little Bit More (1976), a work on pornography as a political statement -- and collaborated on films with director Kathryn Bigelow. [C28554] In typography he experimented at length with sans-serif faces: he began with Helvetica, which he called "nice enough" although "sort of dumpy", turned away from it as it accrued an air of "intellectual power", then used Franklin Gothic until the work "entered the culture so much that if anybody saw something in Franklin Gothic, they thought it was me and it wasn't", and in the end developed his own typeface, Margaret Seaworthy Gothic. [C28554] In later work he added simple graphic marks and lines when he felt language could not fully speak for itself, and revised his early Chomskyan assumption that all language was universal and innate: "I am not so sure any longer, but I don't know the answer to that. I find that I have to look at something and determine what it's called, in order to be able to communicate it to somebody." [C28554] He showed at Live in Your Head: When Attitudes Become Form, Kunsthalle Bern, 1969; Information, MoMA, 1970; documenta 5 (1972), 6 (1977), 7 (1982) and 13 (2012); the Venice Biennale in 1972, 1984, 2003, 2007 and 2013; Displacement at Dia, New York, 1991-92; and the retrospective AS FAR AS THE EYE CAN SEE at the Whitney Museum in 2007-08, travelling to MOCA Los Angeles and K21 Duesseldorf. [C28554] He joined the Marian Goodman Gallery in 1986 and was represented by it for more than three decades. [C28554] His honours include National Endowment for the Arts Fellowships in 1976 and 1983, a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1994, the Wolfgang Hahn Prize in 1995, the Skowhegan Medal in 1999, an honorary doctorate from the Graduate Center of the City University of New York in 2013, the Roswitha Haftmann Prize in 2015, the Wolf Prize in 2017, and the Oskar Kokoschka Prize in 2022, posthumously. [C28554] No named teaching post was found in any source read end to end; TheArtStory states he rarely accepted teaching positions, "as he was more interesting in questioning authority than assuming it", and generally avoided them. [C28554] He died on 2 December 2021 at his home and studio in Manhattan, aged 79; the gallery cited no cause, and the Times says only that he had cancer for several years -- no specific type is named in any source read. [C28554] He is survived by his wife Alice, their daughter Kirsten Vibeke Thueson Weiner, a sister Eileen Judith Weiner, and a grandson. [C28554] He worked until just before his death, travelling for projects around the world; posthumous shows include APROPOS LAWRENCE WEINER at Marian Goodman in 2022 and a Dia:Beacon reinstallation in 2025, the first presentation of his work at Dia since his passing. [C28554]

The twenty works inspected run 1963-2019 and trace one practice shedding its objects until only the sentence is left, and then letting the sentence grow back into the world. Early, 1963-1968: the work is still a thing -- a shaped painting, a notched canvas, a trench cut in a driveway, a grid of stakes in turf -- and the audience is a walker who trips over it or cuts through it: Yellow Wheel with Orange Border (W01, 1963), Propeller Paintings (W02, 1960-1965), Removal Paintings (W03, 1966-1968), A 2" WIDE 1" DEEP TRENCH CUT ACROSS A STANDARD ONE-CAR DRIVEWAY (W04, 1968) and STAPLES, STAKES, TWINE, TURF (W05, 1968). Middle, 1968-1976: the sentence replaces the object, first as a book, then as spray, removal, throw, pour and flare, and the audience becomes a reader and a receiver who may or may not build it -- STATEMENTS and the Declaration of Intent (W06, 1968), TWO MINUTES OF SPRAY PAINT DIRECTLY UPON THE FLOOR FROM A STANDARD AEROSOL SPRAY CAN (W07, 1968), A 36" X 36" REMOVAL TO THE LATHING OR SUPPORT WALL OF PLASTER OR WALLBOARD FROM A WALL (W08, 1968), ONE QUART EXTERIOR GREEN ENAMEL THROWN ON A BRICK WALL (W09, 1968), A SQUARE REMOVAL FROM A RUG IN USE (W10, 1969), THE RESIDUE OF A FLARE IGNITED UPON A BOUNDARY (W11, 1969), A TRANSLATION FROM ONE LANGUAGE TO ANOTHER (W12, 1969), A CUP OF SEA WATER POURED UPON THE FLOOR (W13, 1969), GREEN AS WELL AS BLUE AS WELL AS RED (W14, 1975-1976), A BIT OF MATTER AND A LITTLE BIT MORE (W15, 1976) and DO YOU BELIEVE IN WATER? (W16, 1976). Recent, 1981-2019: the sentences grow longer, more lyrical and more public, the wall text becomes a visual system with specified font, size and placement, and the same sentence appears in several languages on museum walls, facades and a tunnel vent building, the audience now a passer-by who reads in their own language -- AN ACCUMULATION OF SUFFICIENT ABRASION TO REMOVE ENOUGH OF AN OPAQUE SURFACE TO LET LIGHT THROUGH WITH MORE INTENSITY (W17, 1981), WE ARE SHIPS AT SEA NOT DUCKS ON A POND (W18, 1986), THE MIDDLE OF THE MIDDLE OF THE MIDDLE OF / IN THE MIDDLE OF (W19, 2012) and ATTACHED BY EBB & FLOW (W20, 2019). Grades: A 13, B 7, C 0 -- the thirteen A grades are W02, W03, W05, W06, W07, W08, W10, W11, W12, W14, W15, W19 and W20; the seven B grades are W01, W04, W09, W13, W16, W17 and W18. Only one work has stated dimensions in the sources read: W18, the lithograph, 16 3/8 x 23 3/8 inches; the museum records otherwise give "dimensions variable". ## M1 -- The removal: the work is what is taken away

A notch is cut from a canvas, a trench from a driveway, a 36-inch square of plaster from a wall, a square from a rug in use -- and the absence is the sculpture. The Removal Paintings (W03) take a small rectangular notch from the bottom frame of the canvas, growing from a dialogue with Frank Stella's notched rectangles and pushing further: the absent rectangle is the sculptural event, and the same logic later leaves the canvas entirely and becomes a sentence. The trench (W04) interrupts an ordinary piece of infrastructure by a precise, measured cut, two inches wide and one inch deep, with the dimensions in the title, so the work is fully specified by its wording and the trench can be cut in any driveway, or only imagined. The 36-inch removal (W08) exposes the lathing behind the gallery wall, showing what the white cube hides; reconstructed in 2013 as the starting point for "For the Time Being: Hidden Behind Plaster", removing the plaster uncovered the material under the surface and exposed the logic of the exhibition space itself. The rug (W10) only makes sense where a rug is actually walked on: the collector asked Weiner to realise the piece, and he cut a square from a rug inside the collector's home; if the collector moves, a different rug in use gets the square instead. The watchable behaviour is noticing what is missing and then noticing that the missing part is the whole point: the eye goes to the hole first, and the hole stays the work. Seen in W03, W04, W08 and W10.

**Takeaway:** cut the piece out of the world and let the hole be the work. [C28556]

## M2 -- The Declaration of Intent: three equal conditions, the receiver decides

The work is offered under three conditions, each equal and consistent with the intent of the artist: the artist may construct the piece, the piece may be fabricated, the piece need not be built, and the decision as to condition rests with the receiver upon the occasion of receivership. The declaration first appeared in Art News in the fall of 1968 and in the catalogue of the January 5-31, 1969 exhibition, and it accompanied presentations of his work for the rest of his life. The first condition recalls the traditional relationship between artist and artwork; the second permits fabrication by someone else; the third means the work need not be acted upon at all -- it can exist as language or an idea and still function as art. Weiner used the general term "receiver" for any individual, collector, gallery or museum who accepted the responsibility of ownership under these conditions. The watchable behaviour is a decision, not a viewing: the receiver reads the statement and decides whether anything happens, and either answer is the work. Seen in W06.

**Takeaway:** hand the decision to the receiver; the piece is equal built, fabricated, or unbuilt. [C28557]

## M3 -- The statement as specification: the measure is in the title, the world finishes the shape

The quantities are fixed in the words -- one quart, two minutes, 36 inches, a cup -- and the throw, the pour, the spray and gravity decide the rest, so no two realisations can look the same while the statement stays identical. TWO MINUTES OF SPRAY PAINT (W07) is applied to the floor for exactly two minutes from a standard can; Weiner insisted the paint goes on the floor, never on a wall, because spraying downward is how the material is actually used in the world, and the time limit, not the artist's hand, decides the shape and density of the patch. ONE QUART EXTERIOR GREEN ENAMEL THROWN ON A BRICK WALL (W09) is a throw, not a painting: the quart measure and the material are fixed, the pattern is decided by the throw, the wall and gravity. A CUP OF SEA WATER POURED UPON THE FLOOR (W13) is specific in material (sea water, not tap water) and domestic in quantity (a cup), but uncontrolled in outcome: the water spreads according to the floor, then evaporates and leaves a faint salt trace. The trench (W04) carries its dimensions in its title. The watchable behaviour is watching a fixed instruction meet an uncontrolled world: the same sentence, a different splash every time. Seen in W04, W07, W09 and W13.

**Takeaway:** fix the measure in words, let the world finish the shape. [C28558]

## M4 -- The book as the exhibition: the page is the gallery

Statements (W06) is a small 64-page paperback, edition of 1,000 copies, priced at $1.95, published by The Louis Kellner Foundation and Seth Siegelaub in December 1968: twenty-four short descriptions of actions involving everyday materials, printed in uniform typography on single-sided pages with no illustrations, some describing works that had never been produced. The book is the exhibition -- there is no object to display, because the works consist only of words. Line breaks inside individual words are wrapped directly from line to line without a hyphen, so the text reads as a continuous block of specification. Weiner used the artist's book format extensively to disseminate his work, calling books "perhaps the least impositional means of transferring information from one to another (source)", and the Vancouver Art Gallery has maintained The Lawrence Weiner Poster Archive since 1986. The watchable behaviour is reading a show: the eye moves across dense small print the way it would across a painting, and the show fits in a pocket. Seen in W06.

**Takeaway:** print the show; the page is the gallery. [C28559]

## M5 -- Translation as the work: the same sentence in another language is the same work, and the gap is material

A TRANSLATION FROM ONE LANGUAGE TO ANOTHER (W12) first appeared as Art & Project Bulletin 10 in September 1969, the first bulletin issued without a physical exhibition: three lines on an otherwise empty page, the sentence in English, then in Dutch ("een vertaling van de ene taal in de andere") and in French ("une traduction d'une langue en une autre"). Weiner asked that the bulletin contain nothing but the piece, no announcements, no biography, no text, with a layout as minimal as possible. The sentence describes its own operation, and he later widened translation to cover all forms of communication, including the basic mechanics of objects. The same words in a new language are the same work, not a copy: in Boston in 2015 the seven words appeared in large orange block letters against an aqua blue background on a tunnel vent building wall, 70 by 76 feet. At documenta 13 (W19) the fragments DIE MITTE VON / THE MIDDLE OF are arranged in a circle in German and English, so a German reader and an English reader each meet the same structure in their own language, on the same wall. In ATTACHED BY EBB & FLOW (W20) the first word already exposes the gap: ATTACHED / ATTACCATO / ATTACCAU, where English discriminates between attached and attacked but Italian and Sardu do not, exposing the ambiguity and potential danger of physical closeness; the museum notes that something is lost in translation but much more is to be found. The watchable behaviour is reading twice: the eye crosses from one language to the other and finds the difference, and the difference is the point. Seen in W12, W19 and W20.

**Takeaway:** translate the sentence and let the gap between languages do the sculpting. [C28560]

## M6 -- The threshold text: the sentence where the body passes

A BIT OF MATTER AND A LITTLE BIT MORE (W15) was written on the inside of the front entrance and on the outside of the back door of MoMA PS1 for the exhibition "Rooms" in 1976, so entering or leaving the building, the visitor reads the phrase on the doors; when the original wood and metal doors were removed in the 1997 renovation, Weiner was invited back to recreate the piece on the new glass doors, where it remains on view. The text is the threshold: the building itself carries the work forward through a renovation, and the same title names both a public inscription and a private film, a 23-minute video on repression and materiality that Weiner described as a reaction to censorship in the United States in the 1970s. The watchable behaviour is reading while moving: the sentence is read on the way in and again on the way out, and it means something different the second time. Seen in W15, and in W12's Boston mural, which a passer-by meets on a tunnel vent building in the course of ordinary public life.

**Takeaway:** put the sentence where the body passes; the doorway reads it twice. [C28561]

## M7 -- The remote action: do it somewhere else, show only what is left

THE RESIDUE OF A FLARE IGNITED UPON A BOUNDARY (W11) was first constructed in early 1969 for "Op Losse Schroeven" at the Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam: Weiner lit a flare on the outer edge of Amsterdam, at the city border, and in the museum there was only a statement on a small card on the wall. The remote action was documented in photographs taken by a journalist for the local newspaper Het Parool, handed out at the exhibition's press opening. The work was translated into German, so the same statement circulates in another language with the same structure, and Count Giuseppe Panza bought it from Galerie Konrad Fischer in early 1970, receiving a sheet of graph paper with the statement handwritten by Weiner in all capitals; for a 1994 Guggenheim exhibition it was realised in one of the bays of the Frank Lloyd Wright rotunda. The watchable behaviour is reading about an event that happened somewhere else: the action is far from the viewer and leaves only residue, and the residue is the work. Seen in W11.

**Takeaway:** do the action somewhere else; show only what is left. [C28562]

## M8 -- The video dialectic: stage the argument as a game, let the title tell the truth

GREEN AS WELL AS BLUE AS WELL AS RED (W14, 1975-1976) is a single-channel video, colour, with sound, 18 minutes: two women sit opposite each other at a table, using and handling objects and two small red books, while off-screen voices (Kathryn Bigelow and Weiner) discuss methodology, logic, dialectics, syllogisms and forms of domination, over a march recorded by a brigade during the Spanish Civil War. In another description the performers manipulate books and poker chips in a game or competition as the off-camera discourse runs. The staged actions are structured around dialectical relationships among gestures and words, colours of objects and clothing, and the topics discussed, and Weiner's own comment on the palette was plain: "it is green, as well as blue, as well as red. It really is." DO YOU BELIEVE IN WATER? (W16, 1976), 39 minutes, comes from the same group as A Bit of Matter and a Little Bit More and For Example Decorated (1977), in which staged actions and spoken statements test what moving images can claim; the title is a question addressed to the viewer, and the work treats belief itself as a material to be tested. The watchable behaviour is watching a game whose rules are being argued about while it is played: the eye follows the hands, the ear follows the argument, and the two do not resolve. Seen in W14 and W16.

**Takeaway:** stage the argument as a game and let the title tell the truth about the colours. [C28563]

## M9 -- The sentence that performs its own process: reading time matches process time

AN ACCUMULATION OF SUFFICIENT ABRASION TO REMOVE ENOUGH OF AN OPAQUE SURFACE TO LET LIGHT THROUGH WITH MORE INTENSITY (W17, 1981) describes a slow physical process -- abrasion removing an opaque surface until light passes through -- that the text itself performs on the wall: reading the long sentence takes time, and the words accumulate like the abrasion they describe. The title is the whole work; nothing is depicted. First shown at Leo Castelli Gallery in 1981 and held by MOCA Los Angeles, it was shown in the Whitney retrospective of 2007-08 with overlapping typographic definition, and the review noted Weiner's painterly sensibility in it. The watchable behaviour is slow reading: the eye works through the sentence the way a hand would work through a surface, and the light at the end of the sentence is the light the sentence describes. Seen in W17.

**Takeaway:** make the reading time match the process time. [C28564]

## M10 -- The circle and the empty middle: name the middle and leave it empty

THE MIDDLE OF THE MIDDLE OF THE MIDDLE OF / IN THE MIDDLE OF (W19, 2012) was created for documenta 13 at the Hugenottenhaus in Kassel: text fragments arranged in a circle on the house wall, reading "DIE MITTE VON / THE MIDDLE OF" in German and English, so the fragments can be understood both spatially and temporally, and a second inscription with the same wording was placed on a glass wall in the Museum Fridericianum. The circle makes the reader walk around the phrase to read it, and the centre of the circle is empty: the middle is named but never shown. The related IN THE MIDDLE OF (catalogue number 1055, 2012) sits on the outside wall of the Kunsthalle Bielefeld and greets visitors approaching from afar. WE ARE SHIPS AT SEA NOT DUCKS ON A POND (W18, 1986), a lithograph, does the same work in one line: ships at sea and ducks on a pond are both floating bodies, but at utterly different scales and in different conditions, and the sentence asks the reader to hold both scales at once. The watchable behaviour is walking around a phrase, or holding two scales in the head at once, and finding the empty centre where the meaning sits. Seen in W19 and W18.

**Takeaway:** name the middle and leave it empty; the reader walks around the hole. [C28565]

## Aesthetic logic

Weiner's logic is language plus the materials referred to, and nothing else: the sentence names a material and an action, and the material and the action are the sculpture, whether or not anyone performs them. The materials are deliberately ordinary and named in full -- a quart of exterior green enamel, a cup of sea water, two minutes of spray paint, a flare, a rug in use -- and the forms are deliberately simple: a trench, a notch, a patch of paint, a sentence on a wall. What changes across sixty years is not the question but the scale at which it is asked and the language it is asked in: in the 1960s the work asks it of a walker tripping over stakes in a college lawn; in the 1970s of a receiver deciding whether to cut a rug or light a flare; from the 1980s on of a citizen passing a sentence in three languages on a museum wall, a facade, or a tunnel vent building. Against Kosuth's word that refuses to become anything else -- the definition is the work, the paper is disposable -- Weiner's answer is the word that invites the world back in: the sentence is a specification, and the world is the fabricator. Kosuth asks what a thing is; Weiner asks what would happen if you did this to it, and leaves the answer to the receiver, the throw, the tide, and the translation. The work is a representation of an empirical existing fact, in his own phrase, and the viewer verifies it the way one verifies a sentence: by reading it, checking it against what is there, and finding that it holds -- or by building it, and finding that it holds in the world too. [C28566]
