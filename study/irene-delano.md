# Irene Delano: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-03. ig-artist-study #302.

**Site:** Irene Delano (born Irene Esser, 1919, Detroit, Michigan; grew up in Toronto; died 1982, Puerto Rico) has no personal Instagram presence: verified absence, not a gap. She was the painter who met Jack Delano at the Pennsylvania Academy of Fine Arts, held his extension flash and logged his exposures through the FSA years, ran the publications desk at Standard Oil (the Photo Memo), and then went to Puerto Rico and built the island's screen-print poster workshop from 1946 to 1952, because the silkscreen poster was, in her own doctrine, the most economical means of communication. Identity anchors: Ben Shahn's second cousin (stated by Jack in the 1965 oral history, corroborated by the Howard Greenberg bio); the age-16 visit to Shahn's studio and his PAFA summer-school recommendation; the two handmade coal-country photo books she assembled with Jack that won him the Stryker job; the 1941 blacked-out freighter to San Juan, ten days zigzagging through submarine waters with a 16mm camera in case of torpedoes; the Georgia church aisle where she held the flash while shaking; the per-shot exposure and caption logs ("I would write down after practically every shot what was taken"); the 1943 Chicago hotel-room mirror self-portrait, cable release in her hand, the one photograph verifiably hers; the Taller de Grafica she founded and directed until Lorenzo Homar succeeded her in 1952; the book design of Homar's "Plenas" (1955); the twenty serigraph illustrations for Tomas Blanco's "Los Aguinaldos del Infante"; the Three Kings Christmas card line that replaced mainland Santas and reindeer with medieval Spanish and Moorish imagery; the June 1981 retrospective with Jack at the Museo de la Universidad, Rio Piedras; the Puerto Rico Mio re-photography trip that same year, their last project together; and the 2016 Irene typeface by Laura Rossi Garcia, tribute to a woman "too often been left out of the limelight." She was never FSA staff, no FSA/OWI negative is credited to her, and her attributable visual corpus is small and mostly graphic; the study is honest about that throughout.

**Doorway:** Hop from the Louise Rosskam study (#301): Louise was the hand the signature never named, finishing the exposure in the darkroom while the file stamped "Rosskam." Irene was the hand on the next shutter over: she held Jack's extension flash in the Georgia church aisle and logged his exposures shot by shot, then left the shutter behind entirely and spent the Puerto Rico years building the cheapest press on the island, so that a public-health message would never need a photographer's byline or a patron's budget to travel. Where Louise corrected the archive fifty years late, Irene never waited for the archive: she printed the image as a poster, a card, a book, and the village finished the poster by hand. ## M1. The second pair of hands is the authorship

What it is: Irene's FSA labor was never the shutter but everything around it: the extension flash held in the Georgia church aisle ("Take that flash up to the front and put it where I tell you... I walked right up to the front of that church and shot the flash off... I came out of that place just shaking, and so was Jack"), the per-shot exposure and caption log ("I would write down after practically every shot what was taken so that we'd be able to check afterwards and make sure"), and the editing and criticism Jack called the best of it ("best of all, editing and criticizing my work"). She assembled the two handmade coal-country books with Jack that were "actually that material that he showed Roy." The 1943 Chicago mirror self-portrait (f03) is the mechanism made visible and reversed: she holds the cable release and meets the lens while Jack, in suspenders, tends the press camera behind her. The byline problem from #300 and #301 repeats here one generation later: her hands are in Jack's frames, her name is on none of the FSA negatives.

What it IS, watchably: f03 stages the reversal directly, her hand on the release, his on the camera; in Jack's flash-lit FSA interiors her labor is the invisible condition of the image, the held light the frame exists in. "I did an awful lot of criticizing" is the mechanism said plainly: the second authorship was editorial before it was technical.

Against the chain: Louise (#301) ran the two-handed unit with a worked-out signal system and held the light for Edwin's frames; Irene held Jack's flash and logged his exposures, and her mirror portrait reverses the roles once. Wolcott (#294) drove the payroll's miles alone; Irene was never on the payroll at all, which is why her hands are in Jack's frames and her name is on none of his negatives. Rosskam (#300) said the byline dissolved into the partnership and signed the team; Irene's partnership had no byline to dissolve into. Nobody else in the chain held the light for years and then put the camera down for good.

**Takeaway:** credit the hands that hold the light: the flash-holder, the logger, the critic are the authorship the byline cannot see, and the mirror portrait is the receipt.

## M2. The poster as the cheapest possible government

What it is: Irene's stated doctrine as head of the Taller, carried by the New Paltz history: the silkscreen poster was "the most economical means of communication since lithography and photomechanical reproduction required costly equipment." Hand-printed, often by the designing artist, flat color, large simple images: the medium chosen because the barrio had no press and the government had no money. Every DIVEDCO poster is a budget made visible. She organized and directed the first screen-printing and graphic arts workshop on the island; the golden age of the Puerto Rican poster starts the day she leaves, with Homar succeeding her in 1952.

What it IS, watchably: f11 and f14 carry a whole public-health argument in two or three flat colors; f13 spends its one luxury, the giant red BCG, on the single word that matters. These are not designed down from lithography; they are designed up from nothing.

Against the chain: Rosskam (#300) edited at building size with the 11,000-square-foot Grand Central mural; Irene printed at hand size because the barrio had no press. Evans (#284) deleted the photographer; Irene deleted the budget. Man Ray (#288) made the picture after the shutter, on paper; Irene made the picture without the shutter, on the screen-printing frame. Collins (#298) filed the merchandise; Irene's merchandise was free. Nobody else in the chain stated the economics of the medium as doctrine and then ran the workshop that proved it.

**Takeaway:** print at the price the audience can afford: the cheapest workable press is the medium, the budget is the design, and the poster's flatness is the proof.

## M3. The footprint trail: make the invisible vector visible

What it is: the public-health posters do not show sickness; they show transmission. In f14 the giant black housefly drags a trail of black footprints arcing up across the sky: the microbe's path drawn as a dotted line so a non-reading viewer can follow it. In f11 the blue water and the bar of "JABON" are the counter-vector, staged as a handoff between the woman and the reaching child. The mechanism is diagram, not illustration: draw the route the danger travels.

What it IS, watchably: f14's footprint arc is the whole argument; remove it and the poster is just a big bug. f11's handoff is the answer to f14's trail: wash here, where the feet land. The LOC caption calls f14's fly a "giant mosquito" where the image plainly shows a housefly ("LA MOSCA"): the image wins, and the diagram needs no caption at all.

Against the chain: Louise (#301) baked the caption into the negative so no editor could overwrite it; Irene drew the caption as a diagram so the non-reader could follow it. Parr (#282) made the number the work; Irene made the route the work. Lange (#293) kept the seeing for herself, field notes private; Irene gave the seeing to the village as a dotted line. Nobody else in the chain diagrammed transmission as the whole argument.

**Takeaway:** draw the route, not the disease: the vector's path is the message, the counter-vector the answer, and the diagram is the caption the illiterate can read.

## M4. The before/after in one frame

What it is: the development posters compress policy into a single comparative image. f01 puts the painted yellow caserio (diagonal, close, being worked on right now, a man on a bosun's chair painting it) against the sketched arrabales (small, receding, stippled) with the statistics as caption: 31 caserios, 32,000 people since 1941. The future is rendered solid; the past is rendered as outline. f10 does the civic version: the leader's portrait as the screen, the people's crowd as the audience.

What it IS, watchably: f01's split rendering is the mechanism: solid versus sketch, color versus line, now versus then, all in one frame, the numbers printed underneath as the receipt.

Against the chain: Delano (#299) posed the worker to direct the essence; Irene posed the policy, solid against sketch. Rosskam (#300) demanded the good so the bad would count; Irene rendered the good solid and the bad as outline, in the same frame. Lartigue (#289) wrote the day in the album; Irene wrote the five-year plan in the poster. Nobody else in the chain compressed a policy argument into a single comparative rendering.

**Takeaway:** render the future solid and the past in outline: one frame can hold two dates if the rendering does the dating, and the statistics are the caption.

## M5. Text baked into the image

What it is: Irene's posters never separate word from picture; the lettering is drawn, not set. f04's villancico stanzas use archaic orthography (PAXARILLOS, QVISO, MELODIAS) as visual texture, four bands, four ground colors, the text bands ARE the image bands. f12's SABADO and DOMINGO blocks are part of the composition, and the printer's registration crosshairs are left visible in the frame: the print shop inside the poster. f14's PELIGRO is hand-lettered to shout. The Sacred Art Pilgrim note names it: "Her command of the medium and attention to typography are evident." And the afterlife: Laura Rossi Garcia's whole Irene typeface began as an attempt to copy this lettering, until she learned Jack drew some of it. The hands blur again, fifty years later.

What it IS, watchably: f04 reads top to bottom like a manuscript page; f12's crosshairs are the workshop refusing to hide its work. The poster cannot be re-typeset because there is no type: only drawing.

Against the chain: Louise (#301) captioned the negative at the moment of exposure to disarm the editor; Irene lettered the poster by hand at printing, making word and picture one drawn object. Weegee (#281) typed the headline in the car, after; Irene drew the headline first, as the image. Maier (#290) let the world develop her frames fifty years later; Irene's lettering developed into someone else's typeface, and the attribution blurred on contact. Nobody else in the chain left handwriting that became a typeface.

**Takeaway:** draw the words: lettering as image cannot be re-set, re-captioned, or separated from the picture, and the visible registration mark is the honest press.

## M6. The medieval substitution: replace the imported icon

What it is: Irene's signature graphic move, stated plainly by the Sacred Art Pilgrim: "medieval Spanish and Moorish imagery of the Three Kings replaced mainland Santas and reindeer." The Christmas card line (f04 through f08) builds a Puerto Rican sacred image-world out of flat medievalism: arched panels, heraldic banners (the lamb, the palm, the tower), kraft-paper grounds, woodcut-black outlines. It is a deliberate cultural substitution performed in the medium of the greeting card. f08's "Tres reyes en arcos" (1957) borrows the retablo triptych for the Magi: town, sea with fish and ship, fortress under blue starry skies.

What it IS, watchably: f05's three banners read as a coat of arms for the island; f07's three bands (king on horseback with angels, king on caparisoned elephant with doves, musicians with king on camel) are a procession in card form. The style is not nostalgic decoration; it is the argument that the island's Christmas needs no imported Santa.

Against the chain: Parr (#282) fired the flash at noon on the imported icon; Irene replaced the imported icon. Maier (#290) never put the practice down; Irene's card line put a whole island's Christmas down on kraft paper. Brassaï (#285) walked the night patrol and was admitted to the closed room; Irene built the room's own iconography out of arches and heraldry. Nobody else in the chain performed cultural substitution as a greeting-card industry.

**Takeaway:** substitute the icon, not the holiday: draw the local sacred in the local hand, and the imported image starves for lack of a frame.

## M7. The template poster: leave blanks for the village

What it is: f02 ("Los peloteros") and f10 ("Jesus T. Pinero") are not finished announcements but fill-in forms: "Veala en: ___ dia: ___ hora: ___" and "SE EXHIBIRA PRONTO." The DIVEDCO film unit's posters were designed to be completed by hand in each barrio: the center prints the image once, the local organizer writes the where and when. Distribution is part of the design. The "GRATIS!" is the policy: the screening is a public utility.

What it IS, watchably: f02's blank lines after "dia:" and "hora:" are the mechanism, the poster's unfinished edge; the film's title is set, the village's evening is not.

Against the chain: Rosskam (#300) built the coverage as the object, the thousand frames; Irene built the distribution as the object, the poster unfinished until the village writes in it. Stryker's shooting scripts directed the photographer; Irene's blanks directed the local organizer. Lange (#293) kept the seeing for herself; Irene handed the pen to the barrio. Nobody else in the chain designed the image to be completed by its audience.

**Takeaway:** leave the poster unfinished: print the image once, let the village write the where and when, and the blank is the distribution system.

## Evolution

Painter (PAFA, a New York studio, World's Fair murals in 1939) -> the FSA second hand (1940-43: the flash, the logs, the criticism, the coal books; never on the payroll) -> the freighter crossing (1941, the 16mm camera held against torpedoes, "I am at the Palace Hotel. What shall I do now?") -> war graphics (Wright Field technical orders and posters, her first real job) -> the editor as author (Standard Oil's Photo Memo: publications, exhibitions, "the use of the pictures") -> the workshop director (1946-52: founds the screen-print Taller, runs DIVEDCO's graphics, trains the generation Homar inherits) -> the book designer and illustrator ("Plenas," 1955; "Los Aguinaldos," 1954 and 1962; "The Emperor's New Clothes," 1971; "Boriquen," 1975) -> the card line (the medieval Three Kings as a Puerto Rican Christmas industry) -> the 1981 retrospective and the last project with Jack (the Puerto Rico Mio re-photography trip) -> death in 1982. The through-line: she moves from holding Jack's light to running the island's cheapest press, and the work gets more public at every step.

## The aesthetic logic: the image as public utility

Every move Irene made took the image further from the byline and closer to the village. The flash-holder's labor lived inside Jack's frames, uncredited. The logger's information lived inside the file, unsigned. The workshop director's posters lived on the barrio wall, unsigned by design, printed cheap enough that no patron was needed. The card-maker's Three Kings lived in mailboxes across the island, a sacred image that belonged to whoever bought the card. The typeface-maker's letters, fifty years later, live in other designers' hands. Her aesthetic is not a style; it is a direction of travel: from the private negative to the public wall, from the credited author to the usable image. The poster's flatness is not a limitation of the medium; it is the proof that the medium can be afforded. Where the chain's photographers fought over who owns the frame, Irene's whole practice asks what the frame is for once nobody owns it: it is for the village to finish, the child to wash, the island to keep its own Christmas. "No picture is the same when it's put together with another one. As soon as you put two together you tell one thing." She spent forty years putting the picture together with the public.

## Study method

The brief's assumptions were challenged by the research and corrected where they broke. (1) She was born in 1919 in Detroit, not 1916 as briefed (Met, AAA tags, el-status, VCFA). (2) She died in 1982 in Puerto Rico, not 2017 (Met, Sacred Art Pilgrim, Taylor's "died the following year" after the 1981 trip); no obituary was located. (3) She was never FSA staff and no FSA/OWI negative is credited to her; the study was reframed as a designer and printmaker who photographed secondarily, not a photographer with a hidden archive. (4) Her attributable visual corpus is 14 frames, below the 15-20 target; the shortfall is carried honestly and no frame was invented. (5) The Tourist Guide's actual title, date, and publisher were never established; Louise Rosskam's 2000 testimony stands alone. (6) The Standard Oil Photo Memo rests on Irene's 1965 account alone; no issue was located. (7) The #303 candidate list corrects the research leg: Marion Post Wolcott was already studied (#294), so the leg's top pick is invalid; Ben Shahn moves to the top.
