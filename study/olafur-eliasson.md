# Olafur Eliasson: Study Notes

**Site:** Olafur Eliasson (b. 5 February 1967, Copenhagen, Denmark; living), Icelandic-Danish artist who builds large-scale installations from elemental materials -- light, water, air, fog, ice, stone -- so that the viewer's own body, movement and perception complete the work; founder of Studio Olafur Eliasson, Berlin, in 1995; lives and works in Copenhagen and Berlin. [C29105]

Olafur Eliasson was born in Copenhagen on 5 February 1967, to Icelandic parents, Elias Hjorleifsson, a cook, and Ingibjorg Olafsdottir, a seamstress, who had emigrated from Iceland to Copenhagen in 1966; his parents separated when he was about eight, his father, then an artist, moved back to Iceland, and the family spent summers and holidays in Iceland. He grew up in Iceland and Denmark, had his first solo show at fifteen -- landscape drawings and gouaches at a small alternative gallery in Denmark -- and in the mid-1980s break-danced with two school friends as the Harlem Gun Crew, Scandinavian championship winners; he himself considered the dancing his first artworks. He studied at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, Copenhagen, 1989-1995, receiving his degree in 1995; in 1990 he took an Academy travel budget to New York, worked as studio assistant to the artist Christian Eckart in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, and read phenomenology and Gestalt psychology. He showed first in Cologne in 1993, with Nicolaus Schafhausen, and moved to Berlin -- 1994 by one part of Wikipedia, 1995 by the studio's own biography and EBSCO, and the study uses 1995 with the alternative footnoted -- founding Studio Olafur Eliasson there in 1995, first in a three-storey former train depot next to Hamburger Bahnhof, moving to a former brewery in Prenzlauer Berg in 2008. [C29109]

The studio was founded as a laboratory for spatial research and grew from about 30 members in 2008 to roughly 100 names on the current studio roster, across crafts, design, production, research, archive, communications, cooking and administration; Studio Other Spaces, the architecture office with Sebastian Behmann, followed in 2014. The key early collaborator was Einar Thorsteinn (1942-2015), Icelandic architect, mathematician and geometry expert, 25 years his senior, a former friend of Buckminster Fuller; the collaboration began in 1996 and ran to 2014. Eliasson taught as professor at Berlin University of the Arts 2009-2014, founded the Institute for Spatial Experiments in his studio building in 2009, and has been adjunct professor at the Alle School of Fine Arts and Design, Addis Ababa, since 2014. He represented Denmark at the 50th Venice Biennale in 2003; the first major US survey, "Take Your Time: Olafur Eliasson", opened at SFMOMA in September 2007; he was appointed UNDP Goodwill Ambassador for climate action on 22 September 2019. His influences, as named by Wikipedia, are Bruce Nauman, James Turrell and Robert Irwin. He lives and works in Copenhagen and Berlin, per the studio biography. [C29110]

The twenty-two works inspected run 1993-2026 and trace one practice learning to hand the work over -- first to the viewer's eye, then to the viewer's body, then to the weather, then to the public. ## M1 -- The visible mechanism: hose, fan, lamp, scaffold

Nothing is hidden. Beauty is a perforated hose, a pump and a spotlight in a dark room; Ventilator is a fan on a cord; Room for one colour is sodium lamps in aluminium channels; The weather project is two hundred lamps, scaffolding and mirror foil, and the visitor is invited to walk behind the sun and see the wiring. Eliasson learned this from the early rooms and never gave it up: the hose, the spotlight, the fan and the cable are all on view, because the mechanism is the work and the illusion is the demonstration. The viewer is not tricked; the viewer is shown the trick and then watches it work anyway. That is why the works survive being explained -- the explanation is part of the experience, and the rainbow still appears when you know where the hose is. The mechanism is transparency as a method: build the phenomenon from ordinary parts, leave the parts visible, and let the wonder come from watching ordinary parts do an extraordinary thing. The watchable behaviour is the walk behind: the visitor who goes around the back of the sun, sees the lamps, and comes back to the front to lie down under it again, now knowing and still moved. Seen in W01, W05, W06 and W09. [C29112]

**Takeaway:** show the hose, the fan and the wiring, and let the wonder survive the explanation. [C29112]

## M2 -- The viewer as co-producer: the work does not exist without a body

From Beauty onward the work does not exist without a body moving through it. The rainbow in Beauty appears only at the exact angle between the spotlight, the falling droplets and one viewer's eyes; no two viewers see the same rainbow, and from some positions there is no rainbow at all. In Room for one colour the yellow is in the lamps, but the monochrome world is in the viewer's retina, and the purple afterimage on leaving is made by the eye, not the room. In 360-degree room for all colours the slowly cycling red, green and blue tubes are only the start: each colour leaves its complement burned on the retina, and the next colour is seen through the last one's ghost, so the eye itself becomes the projector. In The weather project the crowd is the picture: two million visitors lying on the Turbine Hall floor, watching themselves upside down in the mirror ceiling, waving arms and legs at their own reflections. The studio's own line, on Beauty, is that the viewer is a necessary coproducer of the artwork. The mechanism is the body as the last component: light, water and fog are only the raw material, and the work is completed by a retina, a position, a step. The watchable behaviour is the negotiation: two people standing a metre apart compare rainbows that are not the same rainbow, and both are right. Seen in W01, W06, W08 and W09. [C29113]

**Takeaway:** build the phenomenon so it only appears at the angle of one viewer's eyes, and let the viewer finish it. [C29113]

## M3 -- One wavelength: colour as a material you can pour into a room

Eliasson's colour research is a pair of opposites. Room for one colour (1997) floods a white room with monofrequency light -- low-pressure sodium lamps emitting at a single wavelength, about 589 nanometres, in the yellow region -- so every other colour is rendered as shades of yellow, grey and black; skin, clothes and companions all go monochrome, and on leaving, the next room appears washed in purple, the complementary afterimage. 360-degree room for all colours (2002) is the colour-positive twin: a circular enclosure ringed by 525 fluorescent tubes, 175 each of red, green and blue, cycling slowly through ice blues, yellows, pale lavenders and lime at roughly 30-second intervals, each colour leaving an afterimage that is superimposed on the next, until viewers lose their sense of space and feel subsumed by intense light. The same monofrequency lamps make the sun of The weather project, and the same yellow floods Presence (2025), where printed textile wedges, perforated aluminium and mirror foil make a moire effect activated only by the visitor's own motion. The mechanism is colour as a physical material with a dose: one wavelength deletes the spectrum, all wavelengths plus the retina synthesise colours that are not on the screen, and the room becomes a test of the eye rather than a picture of anything. The watchable behaviour is the leaving: the purple room after the yellow room, the green ghost after the red light, the proof that what you saw was made inside your own head. Seen in W06, W08, W09 and W20. [C29114]

**Takeaway:** pour one wavelength into a room, and let the viewer's retina do the painting. [C29114]

## M4 -- Fog and the senses that remain: perception without sight

Din blinde passager (2010) is the early programme turned inward. A 90-metre plywood tunnel at ARKEN, dense fog closing around you until you can see barely a metre and a half ahead; other people appear and vanish as shapes in the mist; you grope along the wall, listen for footsteps, feel your own heartbeat. As you walk the fog changes colour -- white, then pitch black, then yellow monofrequency light, then back to white in the ARKEN staging; the Tate 2019 version, 39 metres, ran its own sequence -- and the fog vapour is sugar-based, so the air tastes faintly sweet. The Danish title means "stowaway": you travel blind as a passenger in your own body. Eliasson's line to the Guardian is the mechanism stated plainly: "very quickly you realise... that you are not completely blind after all, you have a lot of other senses which start to kick in." The mechanism is deprivation as an instrument: take sight away and hearing, touch, balance and taste report for duty, proving that perception continues without the sense the gallery normally serves. The watchable behaviour is the hand on the wall: the visitor who came to look, and ends up listening, and hears their own heartbeat in their ears. Seen in W12, and in the fog room of The mediated motion (W07) as its first sketch. [C29115]

**Takeaway:** take the sight away, and let the other senses report for duty. [C29115]

## M5 -- The building as the work: route, gradient, dead end

The mediated motion (2001) is the hinge of the whole practice: the unit of work stops being a single room and becomes a whole building and a choreographed route through it. At Kunsthaus Bregenz, with landscape architect Gunther Vogt, Eliasson staged four disconnected landscapes across four floors -- logs sprouting shiitake mushrooms on the ground floor, a shallow duckweed pond crossed on pontoons, a sloping floor of compressed soil, a top-floor suspension bridge across a room full of fog that ends abruptly at a blank wall, forcing visitors to turn back -- linked by a staircase of roughly hewn wood built over the existing concrete stairs. His own line: "The work was primarily a staging of movement and of people... This stressed the mediated, staged feeling of the interior landscapes." Riverbed (2014) is the same operation with real weight: 180 tons of Icelandic rock, up to 2 metres thick, filling Louisiana's whole South Wing, a pumped stream meandering through it, no marked route, the visitor scrambling uphill to find the water's source while the white walls and rectangular rooms stay visible. Your rainbow panorama (2011) is the same operation made permanent: a 150-metre ring of spectrum glass on the ARoS roof, the city of Aarhus re-tinted every few metres as you walk the circle. The mechanism is choreography as medium: the floor, the gradient, the dead end and the unmarked route are the work, and the visitor's whole body, route and negotiation with other visitors become the material. The watchable behaviour is the turn-back: the bridge that ends at a blank wall, the riverbed with no path, the ring that brings you back to where you started having seen the same city in different colours. Seen in W07, W13 and W15. [C29116]

**Takeaway:** stage the route, not the object -- a dead end, a slope, a ring -- and let the visitor's body be the medium. [C29116]

## M6 -- The city as material: scaffolding, river, street

The New York City Waterfalls (2008) leaves the museum entirely and uses the city's own building materials: four waterfalls, 90 to 120 feet tall, built from ordinary construction scaffolding, pumps cycling East River water to the top and letting it fall back into the river, intake pools filtering the water to protect fish, run on renewable electricity and lit with LEDs after sunset, at a cost of $15.5 million -- at the time the most expensive public art project since Christo and Jeanne-Claude's The Gates. You do not enter the work; you meet it from the shore, a bridge or a boat, and from several vantage points you can see more than one fall at once. The friction is part of the record: the light flow meant wind picked up salt spray over shore paths and trees, and operating hours were cut. Ice Watch (2014-2018) is the same operation at the scale of a summit: blocks of 15,000-year-old Greenland ice, fished from the Nuup Kangerlua fjord where they had already broken off and were melting, placed on city streets in a clock-shaped circle outside Copenhagen City Hall for the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report, at Place du Pantheon for COP21, and in a grove of 30 blocks on Bankside for COP24 -- timed to the politics that might save them, touched, climbed on, hugged, watched shrinking over days. The cubic structural evolution project (2004) is the same operation turned over to the public: a tonne of white Lego bricks, no instructions, no branding, visitors building a city no single person designed, demolished and rebuilt by many hands. The mechanism is the city as both material and audience: scaffolding, river water, a street, a summit, a table of bricks -- the work is made of what is already there, and the public is whoever is already there. The watchable behaviour is the passer-by who did not set out to see art, standing on a pavement watching a glacier die. Seen in W10, W11 and W16. [C29117]

**Takeaway:** build the waterfall from scaffolding, put the glacier on the street, and let the city be the audience. [C29117]

## M7 -- Geometry with Thorsteinn: the dome, the star, the sphere

The second track of the practice enters in 1996, with Einar Thorsteinn, and it is geometry. 8900054 (1996) is a fragment of a pentagonal and hexagonal sphere -- exactly twelve pentagons form a full sphere, six a dome -- sunk into the earth outside Arken so only its crown shows above the lawn, a low steel dome rising like something growing from beneath the ground, usable as a climbing frame. Its companion the same year, By means of a sudden intuitive realisation, is a ready-made dome of white pentagonal and hexagonal fibreglass panels, originally designed by Thorsteinn for geothermal energy use in Iceland, turned into a dark chamber with a fountain ejecting a two-metre jet of water in bursts. The two must not be merged: one is an outdoor steel dome, the other an inverted dome with water inside. The geometry track runs through the kaleidoscopes and pavilions to Shadows travelling on the sea of the day (2022), twenty circular shelters and free-standing rings across the desert north of Doha, the ten central shelters forming a five-pointed star after medieval Islamic patterns, each mirrored underside doubling its curved support pipe into a full ring that seems to pass through the mirror. The mechanism is the Platonic solid as a found form: hexagons and pentagons, fivefold symmetry, the sphere as a fragment, so that geometry feels like archaeology -- something dug up rather than drawn. The watchable behaviour is the doubling: the half-circle the mirror completes, the dome crown that implies a buried sphere, the visitor seeing themself small and upside down inside a geometry the desert completes. Seen in W04 and W18. [C29118]

**Takeaway:** sink the sphere so only its crown shows, and let the mirror finish the circle. [C29118]

## M8 -- The product that works in life: Little Sun

Little Sun (2012-) is the decisive turn from phenomenon to product and from audience to user. A hand-held solar LED lamp, 12 centimetres across, 120 grams, yellow polycarbonate moulded in the shape of the Ethiopian meskal flower, a small mono-crystalline panel on the back, a rechargeable battery and an LED at the centre; about four to five hours of solar charging gives about five hours of bright light or up to fifty hours of soft light. Founded with engineer Frederik Ottesen and launched at the World Economic Forum in Addis Ababa in May 2012, it is a social business: lamps sold at a higher price to wealthier buyers subsidise low prices for local entrepreneurs in off-grid communities, who sell them on at locally affordable prices while making a profit, aimed at the roughly 1.3 to 1.6 billion people then living without electricity. One lamp delivers about ten times more light at about a tenth of the cost of a kerosene lantern over three years, with no fire risk, no fumes and no fuel spending. The evidence of effect, from the artist in the New Scientist interview: a study found the lamp increased a boy's homework efficiency by 20 percent but a girl's by 80 percent, because girls did chores first and ran out of kerosene before homework. Eliasson's line: "a work of art that works in life." The mechanism is no longer optical but economic and energetic: sunlight is harvested, stored, sold and shared, and the work is judged by homework done, not by reviews. The watchable behaviour is the lamp in a hand after dark, in a place the grid never reached -- the smallest Eliasson, and the one that travels furthest. Seen in W14. [C29119]

**Takeaway:** put the sun in a flower-shaped lamp, sell it so the poorest buyer profits, and measure the work in homework. [C29119]

## M9 -- Nature imported, not simulated: moss, rock, ice, pond

The early works reproduce weather indoors with lamps and mist. The later works stop simulating and start importing. Moss wall (1994) transplants a living piece of Scandinavian landscape into the white cube: reindeer lichen on a wood-and-wire structure, soft and spongy to touch, giving off a forest scent, shrinking and drying when left alone and reviving when watered, its colour changing as the exhibition ages. Riverbed (2014) pours 180 tons of Icelandic rock into a museum wing, a real stream pumped through it, no plants or animals except the tap-water brook -- a piece of Iceland inside a modernist museum, sterile and rough at once. Ice Watch (2014-2018) does not manufacture the phenomenon at all: it harvests blocks of ancient ice and displays them as they die, on the street, timed to the summits. Life (2021) goes further and gives up the building itself: Eliasson removed a section of the Fondation Beyeler's glass facade and let the outdoor lily pond flood the galleries up to 80 centimetres deep, dyed bright green with uranine, visitors crossing on dark wooden walkways among dwarf water lilies, shellflowers and water ferns, the plants selected by landscape architect Gunther Vogt, the building open to the elements day and night, UV light making the green water fluoresce after dark. The mechanism is import as honesty: the simulation gives way to the thing itself, and the thing itself is allowed to change, dry out, melt, grow and attract insects, bats and birds. The watchable behaviour is the smell and the wet feet: the moss you can touch, the rock you must climb, the ice you can press your cheek to, the pond that floods the gallery whether or not anyone is watching. Seen in W02, W15, W16 and W17. [C29120]

**Takeaway:** stop simulating the weather and import it -- moss, rock, ice, pond -- and let it change while you watch. [C29120]

## M10 -- Giving up control: the work that only exists sometimes

The late works are defined by what the artist no longer controls. In Life (2021) he says it outright: "Together with the museum, I am giving up control over the artwork, so to speak, handing it over to human and non-human visitors, to plants, microorganisms, the weather, the climate" -- the piece is never the same twice, because the pond, the weather and the insects decide. Your daylight destination (2023), selected for the Cumbrian coast with writer Robert Macfarlane, is an elliptical steel pool about 30 metres long set into the mud flats near Silecroft: twice a day, at high tide, the basin is submerged entirely, and the water left behind acts as a mirror reflecting sun, moon and sky, "a 'borrowed' view of the sky in the sand" -- the artwork only exists as a mirror at low tide and disappears under the sea at high tide. It is carried here as a selected proposal, not a built work: no completion or opening date was found in any source read this run. Ice Watch (2014-2018) gives up control to the melt itself: the blocks shrink, slump and split on their own schedule, and the artist's accounting is public -- the London project cost the equivalent of 52 return flights from London to Ilulissat, a figure he states rather than hiding. Deep seeing currents (2026), a permanent installation in the restored ARTIS-Aquarium rotunda with no animals in it, hands the room to darkness, vibration and falling light standing in for pressure and marine snow, sound realised with 4DSOUND. The mechanism is surrender as a system: the tide, the melt, the pond and the dark are co-authors with schedules of their own, and the work is a window onto their timetable rather than an object on a plinth. The watchable behaviour is the return visit that finds a different work: the pond higher, the ice smaller, the basin gone under the sea. Seen in W16, W17, W19 and W21. [C29121]

**Takeaway:** hand the work to the tide, the melt and the weather, and let the return visit find a different piece. [C29121]

## Aesthetic logic

Eliasson's logic is the phenomenon with the instructions left on. Against Demand, whose photograph knows it is lying and tells you so at arm's length, Eliasson's installation knows it is a machine and shows you the machine: the hose, the fan, the sodium lamp, the scaffolding behind the sun. The work is always the interval -- between the light and the retina, between the fog and the hand on the wall, between the tide and the mirror -- and the interval is where the viewer has to stand, with their own body as the last component. What changes across thirty-three years is not the question but the scale of the surrender: first the eye completes the rainbow, then the body completes the route, then the public completes the city, then the weather completes the work and the artist steps back. He refuses the digital fix, and the refusal is the point: the rainbow is made with a punctured hosepipe and a spotlight, the sun with two hundred lamps and a mirror, the glacier with a fjord and a street, and none of it needs a screen. The meaning is the making -- the visible hose, the taped seam of the dome, the moss that dries when nobody waters it, the ice that melts whether or not anyone watches -- because the work is only honest about what it cost when the cost is on view. [C29122]
