# Clemente Padin: Study Notes

**Site:** Clemente Padin, born in Lascano, Rocha, Uruguay 8 October 1939, died in Montevideo 3 October 2025 -- Uruguayan visual poet, mail artist, performer and networker, who printed apocryphal postage stamps denouncing his own dictatorship and served two years and three months in prison for them, then kept the mail moving for another forty years (C11000).

**Doorway:** Hop from the Gutierrez Marx study: from the mail artist who with Vigo stamped 'Set Free Palomo' onto envelopes that kept travelling despite the junta's censorship to the Uruguayan who printed apocryphal stamps denouncing his own dictatorship -- Clemente Padin, whose stamps were answered with two years and three months in prison.

Two-hundred-and-seventy-fourth doorway check in a row. The draft doorway FAILS on D0, D1 and D4 as written (C11001) and is carried only in corrected form below. The hold count stays at one hundred and ninety-two and the non-hold count moves from eighty-eight to eighty-nine: this check is a non-hold on the draft as written, because the draft overstates sole agency, stitches two adjacent sentences, and rounds two years and three months down to two years. (The tally arithmetic is flagged, not fixed: 192 + 89 = 281 against 274 checks run -- see C9557, carried; the gap is 7, unchanged from #652.)

D0 "from the mail artist who stamped 'Set Free Palomo' onto envelopes" -- PARTLY CONFIRMED. The stamping is real and the wording is exact, but it was joint, not solo: MoMA's Vigo exhibition text states that Vigo and Gutierrez Marx "often stamped the envelopes they sent out through the mail art network with the English phrase 'Set Free Palomo'", campaigning for the return of Vigo's disappeared son Palomo, and the Blanton label attributes the campaign itself to Vigo. The #652 study's own doorway already says this correctly ("who with Vigo stamped"). The corrected doorway carries the joint form. (C11002)

D1 "that reached artists the world over" -- PARTLY CONFIRMED. The worldwide reach is attested, but the attestation attaches to Vigo's letters and graphic works, not specifically to the Palomo-stamped envelopes: MoMA says "Despite government censorship, Vigo's moving letters and graphic works reached artists the world over". The stamped envelopes did go "through the mail art network" per the same MoMA sentence, and the network's worldwide reach is independently attested for Padin's own case ("mail artists from around the world secured Padin's release within two years"). The draft stitches two adjacent sentences; the Palomo envelopes' own worldwide delivery is not separately documented. The corrected doorway softens to "envelopes that kept travelling despite the junta's censorship". (C11003)

D2 "to the Uruguayan who printed apocryphal stamps denouncing his own dictatorship" -- CONFIRMED. Padin's own words: "My edition of apocryphal mail art stamps denounced the dictatorial regime for its brutal suppression of human rights". Corroborated by the Yugoblok account: "In 1977, Padin published a series of mail art stamps denouncing the military dictatorship." The word "apocryphal" is Padin's own. (C11004)

D3 "Clemente Padin" -- CONFIRMED. Born 1939 in Lascano, Rocha, Uruguay; a Uruguayan poet, performer and promoter of mail art; his prison, his exhibitions and his magazines are all Montevideo-based unless stated otherwise. (C11005)

D4 "whose stamps were answered with two years in prison" -- PARTLY CONFIRMED, with a numbers conflict to fix, not hide. Padin's own account gives the dates August 1977 to November 1979 and says the stamps "eventually led to my incarceration". August 1977 to November 1979 is two years and three months by arithmetic, which matches his curriculum's figure ("two years and three months"); the sentence was four years, paroled after half; five years' house arrest followed. The doorway's "two years" is the common rounding, but it understates the served time by three months and drops the house arrest entirely. The corrected doorway uses the exact figure. One nuance on cause: Cook records that Padin was specifically cited in his conviction for writing an anti-American tract, so the stamps were the trigger Padin names but not the only item cited against him. (C11006)

D5 Background: the 274th doorway check in a row. The draft holds in substance on D2 and D3 and fails only where it compresses -- sole agency where the work was joint, worldwide delivery where the attestation is adjacent, two years where the record says two years and three months. The failure is the point of the doorway: the mail got through, and the price of sending it was counted in months, not rounded away. The corrected doorway above is what the study carries. The tally gap of 7 (C9557) is carried, not repaired, as in #652. (C11001)

## The life: the poet who mailed the accusation

Clemente Padin Portugal was born 8 October 1939 in Lascano, Rocha, Uruguay, and died in Montevideo on 3 October 2025, aged 85, five days before his 86th birthday (C10850, C10851). The death year is carried as resolved, not open: the Spanish Wikipedia entry gives 8 October 1939 - 3 October 2025 and cites Brecha, "Clemente Padin (1939-2025) La poesia como insubordinacion", 10 October 2025, and La Diaria, "Concentrado vanguardista: Clemente Padin (1939-2025)", 10 October 2025; the UDELAR General Archive actor record also gives 1939-2025 and states he died in Montevideo on 3 October 2025; ICAA author headings and Artprice both give 1939-2025; the MIDECIANT farewell page confirms his passing without giving a date in the text as read. The 2015 date that the #652 study carried as an open three-way conflict is DISPROVED, not merely outvoted: it appears only in the ICAA record for "Inobjetal 4" (doc. no. 1478739), which calls him "Clemente A. Padin (1939-2015)", while Padin was demonstrably active afterwards, including email correspondence with Michaela de Lacaze in March-April-June 2019, the Centre Pompidou Bernard Heidsieck award in 2019, the 2021 exhibition catalogue "Poesia visual y experimental - Arte correo - Accion (1965-2021)", and a video work shown in BIENALSUR 2025. Use 1939-2025 in the study, and treat any 1939-2015 heading as an ICAA metadata error (C10852). He was a Uruguayan poet, experimental and visual poet, graphic designer, performer, curator, video artist, multimedia artist, networker, art critic and theorist, and teacher (C10854). He graduated in Hispanic Literature (Licenciatura en Letras Hispanicas) from the Facultad de Humanidades y Ciencias de la Educacion, Universidad de la Republica, Uruguay, and he also trained as a physical education teacher (C10855). He taught postgraduate work at IUNA in Buenos Aires, Argentina, in the programme "Lenguajes Artisticos Combinados", and Spanish Wikipedia lists the pseudonym Antonio Maltez for him, which is single-source and should be treated as unverified (C10856).

The career runs in five phases, and the hinge is the stamp: the work leaves the page, enters the post, and the post answers with a cell.

(1) Formation, 1939-1965. Padin said his artistic life began around 1960, when, in his early 20s, he experienced the first frustrations of love and their subsequent sublimation into poetry, while studying literature at the University of Uruguay (C10857). He formed the Uruguayan group Los Hachepientos and published a manifesto under the same name, and in 1965 he began publishing and editing the magazine Los Huevos del Plata with Hector Paz, Juan Jose Linares and Julio Moses, publishing sixteen issues over three years as a counterweight to the "editorial monopoly" of the Generation of '45, including Mario Benedetti, Idea Vilarino and Angel Rama (C10858). The start year is carried with a caveat: most sources date Los Huevos del Plata to 1965, but two ICAA synopses give 1966 (C10895).

(2) Visual poetry, mail art, inobjetal, 1965-1977. His mail-art exchange began in 1967, when he, Edgardo Antonio Vigo, Guillermo Deisler and Damaso Ogaz exchanged their respective publications: Diagonal Cero, Ediciones Mimbre, La Pata de Palo and Los Huevos del Plata (C10859). Correction, carried from leg 3: "Clemente Ogaz" does not exist in this circle -- the fourth partner is Damaso Ogaz (1924-1990, Caracas); do not use "Clemente Ogaz" in the study (C10952). He officially began creating mail art in 1969, when the Uruguayan magazine OVUM 10 published his postcards and visual poems, the same year Los Huevos del Plata ended and OVUM 10 began (C10860). His visual-poetry work included the cycles "Signografias y textos" (1967-1972), "Signo" (1974), "Homenaje al cuadrado" (1999) and "Sonetario" (2000), the Inobjetal experiences of 1971, and the book De la Representation a l'Action, published by Doc(k)s in Marseille in 1975 (C10861). His landmark performances included "La Poesia Debe Ser Hecha por Todos" (Poetry Should Be Made For All), performed on 30 September 1970 in the hall of the Universidad de la Republica in Montevideo, and "El Artista debe estar al Servicio de la Comunidad" (The Artist Is at the Service of the Community), performed in Sao Paulo in 1975 and again in 1981 (C10862). In 1974, during the Uruguayan military dictatorship, he organised the First Latin American Mail Art Exposition, the Festival de la Postal Creativa, at Galeria U in Montevideo, dated 11-24 October 1974 in the Tate Archive catalogue record, and described in his own curriculum as the first documented mail art show in Latin America (C10863). In 1976 he made Happy Bicentennial, a booklet giving step-by-step instructions for building a bomb to be delivered to Wall Street offices, which he asked his friend Ulises Carrion in Amsterdam to publish through Daylight Press and mail to the United States as a sardonic gift for the US bicentennial (C10864).

(3) Prison and enforced silence, 1977-1983. CARRY BOTH FRAMINGS, DO NOT COLLAPSE THEM: Padin's own account says his apocryphal mail-art stamps denouncing the regime "eventually led to my incarceration from August, 1977 to November, 1979", while his curriculum says he was jailed by the dictatorship for "to hurt the morale and reputation of the Army" for two years and three months, having been condemned to four years but freed earlier through international artistic solidarity; August 1977 to November 1979 is exactly two years and three months, so the two framings agree on duration and differ only in form (dates vs. duration plus sentence) (C10865). The surrounding record adds: he went missing, was arrested with Jorge Caraballo in August 1977, was tried by a military court under Article 58, section II of the Uruguayan military code for attacking the morale and reputation of the army, was condemned to four years, was released on parole after serving half his sentence, and for the remainder of the dictatorship could not leave Uruguay, create art or use the postal system; the Whitener interview introduction instead dates the jailing to 1979-1981 for two years and three months, which conflicts with Padin's own August 1977-November 1979 dates and should be treated as an error in that introduction, not as a third imprisonment (C10866). Whether the curriculum itself carries the 1979-1981 dates is disputed between the legs (leg 2 attributes them to the curriculum as well as the introduction; leg 1 attributes them to the introduction only) -- carried unresolved, and the study does not attribute 1979-1981 to the curriculum. After prison came five years' house arrest (C10973). The dictatorship's own span differs by source -- 1973-1984, 1973-1985, 1973-1986 -- and the study does not pick one without noting the spread (C10894).

(4) Resumption and organisation, 1983-1992. He resumed his artistic activities in October 1983 with the "1st. of May" Exhibition at the Association of Banking Employees of Uruguay, and on 31 August 1984, in Rosario, Argentina, he proposed and organised the Latin American and Caribbean Association of Mail Artists, co-founded with Graciela Gutierrez Marx, N. N. Arganaraz and others, in the framework of the international Arte Postal exhibition organised by Jorge Orta (C10867). As an editor he directed Los Huevos del Plata (1965-1969), OVUM 10 and OVUM (1969-1975 in most sources, 1969-1977 in the 2021 book), Participacion (1984-1986) and Correo del Sur (2000-2003) (C10868). After 1984 he distributed the first number of Participacion in June 1984, a monthly four-page mail-art bulletin that ran to ten numbers until October 1986, and he worked actively in internet projects from 1992, including the interactive CD-ROMs PAZ/PAN (2001) and Spams Trashes (2002) (C10869). He was a scholar of the Academy of Arts and Letters (DAAD) in West Berlin in 1984, taught seminars on experimental poetry, performance and mail art around the world, and was a special guest at the XVI Sao Paulo Biennial in 1981 (C10876).

(5) Network elder, digital work, archive, 1992-2025. He made video art, took part in the First Video Show in Montevideo in 1986, worked in internet projects from 1992, published interactive CD-ROM poems, and continued performing into the 2000s, including the 2009 performance cycle "Ciclos" at La Diaria in Montevideo (C10874). He lost his personal archive twice during the dictatorship, reconstructed it, placed it in custody with the Archivo General de la Universidad de la Republica (AGU) under an agreement signed in 2010, saw part of it digitised in 2014 with Reina Sofia / Red de Conceptualismos del Sur support, and completed its donation to the AGU in 2020, fifty years after his first performance (C10870). Books: the count varies by source and date, with his own curriculum listing 20 books and later sources listing 25 books, published in France, Germany, Holland, Italy, Spain, Venezuela, the United States and Uruguay (C10875). Awards included the Pedro Figari Award for artistic excellence / career achievement, Uruguay, 2005 (XI edition, Espacio Pedro Figari, Montevideo, October 2006); the 400 Years of the National University of Cordoba award, Argentina, 2015; and the Bernard Heidsieck Literary Award from the Centre Pompidou, France, 2019 (spelling varies as Heidseik / Heidsiek in secondary sources) (C10892). He exhibited collectively in more than 200 exhibitions and in more than 1,200 mail art shows from 1969 to 1999, with later versions of his curriculum raising the mail-art count to more than 1,500 shows to 2008 and more than 2,000 to 2010, so the count grows with the curriculum date and should be quoted with its date (C10890).

In his own words. On the start of mail art: "My experiences in Mail Art date from 1967 when Edgardo Antonio Vigo, Guillermo Deisler, Damaso Ogaz and me exchanged our respective publications: Diagonal Cero (Diagonal Zero), Ediciones Mimbre (Osier Editions), La Pata de Palo (leg of Wood) and Los Huevos del Plata (The Eggs of Silver)." (C10877) On the stamps and prison: "My edition of apocryphal mail art stamps denounced the dictatorial regime for its brutal suppression of human rights and this eventually led to my incarceration from August, 1977 to November, 1979." (C10878) On beginnings: "My artistic life began around 1960, when, in my early 20s, I experienced the first frustrations of love and their subsequent sublimation into poetry." (C10879) On what mail art is: "My attraction to Mail Art was the responsive, genuine nature of communication exchanges. Mail Art is an artistic school without any 'isms'." and "Mail artworks are not made for the art market to be consumed. Rather, they are products of communication." (C10880) On money: "Mail art attempts to keep art in the area of use, in its unrestricted social function, freeing it from the area of exchange." and its "trademark anti-consumption and anti-commercial stances ('Money and mail art don't mix')." (C10881) On Happy Bicentennial and ambiguity: "It's a work born of anger and resentment . . . born in [an] environment of rejection and impotence" (email to Michaela de Lacaze, 31 March 2019, as translated in MoMA post), and "Ambiguity and self-reflexivity are without a doubt the marks of the poetic" (from "Estrictamente Autorreflexivos", in Poesias completas, p. 317, as quoted in MoMA post) (C10882).

Evolution. Early (1965-1976): from the word to the sign, then to the act. Padin's move is described as from Noigandres-style concrete poetry to visual poetry, or as he put it, from the word to the sign, from semantics to semiotics (C10927). His No-Object poems of the early 1970s were distributed in English and Spanish not in journals but through the mail art network, building on the Poem/Process poets of Rio de Janeiro and Edgardo Antonio Vigo, and treating the reader as co-creator in a "language of action" (C10928). The early work is dense, material and confrontational: distorted letters (W02), self-published small-format prints (W05, W08), and instruction-manual booklets that weaponise the diagram (W06, W11, W15). Performance enters in 1970-1971 (W03, W07) and is theorised in 1975 (W14): the object is eliminated and replaced by action in direct relation to the environment. Middle (1977-1989): prison, silence, return. The apocryphal stamps (W16) led to arrest in August 1977; for the rest of the dictatorship he could not leave Uruguay, create art or use the postal system, and mail artists worldwide campaigned for his release (C10932). After resuming activity in October 1983, his visual poetry turns sparser and self-referential: Oximoron (W17) is a single word made into a falling tower, not a dense field of letters. Recent (1990-2021): network elder, minimalist, digital. From the mid-1980s, performance in public spaces became his main axis of work, while he stayed active in visual poetry and mail art; his recent minimalist visual poems respond to the 2003 invasion of Iraq by the United States (C10929). He worked actively in internet projects from 1992 onward, including the interactive poem PAZ/PAN on CD-ROM (Montevideo, 2001) and SpamsTrashes on CD-ROM (Montevideo, 2002) (C10930). The late visual-poem cycles are geometric and restrained: Sonetario (2000) is the fourth cycle in the 2021 peer-reviewed survey, after Signografias y textos, Signo (1974) and Homenaje al cuadrado; late books include La poesia es la poesia (2003), Poemas para (h)ojear (2004) and Poesias completas (2014) (C10931). Arc in one line: dense illegible letter-fields and bomb-manual booklets under dictatorship, then a single unstable word, then square geometry and screen-based work -- the politics stays, the means get lighter and the reader is given more of the meaning to finish. (C11010)

## Mechanisms

M1 -- The apocryphal stamp. A fake postage stamp, official in format and seditious in content, printed as an edition and mailed into the system it denounces. Works: W16. Watchable behaviour: a sheet of perforated stamps fills the frame; each stamp at first looks state-issued (portrait, denomination, border), then its image or legend flips to an accusation; the sheet is torn along the perforations and the stamps are posted one by one. Takeaway: the stamp borrows the state's own visual authority and spends it on the accusation -- the format is the forgery, and the forgery is the work. (C10972)

M2 -- The instruction manual as a weapon. A booklet that reads as a how-to guide and is in fact a political detournement. Happy Bicentennial (1976) is a manual with step-by-step diagrams for building a bomb to be delivered to a Wall Street office, wrapped as a birthday gift with a ludicrously large bow and ending "Happy Bicentenary of the Explotation!"; an earlier version, a 1971 OVUM 10 how-to for a Molotov cocktail, put revolutionary mantras in place of captions. Works: W15, W06, W11. Watchable behaviour: diagrams assemble themselves step by step on screen, each step adding one part; the "delivery address" is stamped on at the end; the tone stays deadpan while the content turns sinister, and the final pun lands as a title card. Takeaway: the manual is deniable -- it only describes -- and that deniability is what lets it travel; do not present Happy Bicentennial as a sincere bomb manual, Padin himself later wished the work erased, and the MoMA essay stresses its ambiguity, flowers-for-shrapnel and sarcastic final pun. (C10982, C10936)

M3 -- The decaying letter (signografia). Visual poetry in which letters of the alphabet are repeated and distorted until they become illegible abstract forms, appreciated as pattern rather than read as text (Padin's signografias, 1967-1970); Oximoron (1984) stacks the letters of "stability" into a precarious tower from which a letter falls, jeopardizing the column structurally and semantically. Works: W02, W04, W17. Watchable behaviour: a word is built letter by letter into a tower, then letters warp, slide, and drop out; meaning drains away as the shape holds, and the fallen letter is the only legible thing left. Takeaway: the letter is a material before it is a meaning -- repeat it until the meaning falls off, and what is left is the shape of the word's collapse. (C10983, C10933)

M4 -- The little magazine as exchange node. Four editors swapping publications across borders instead of exhibiting in galleries (the 1967 exchange of Diagonal Cero, Ediciones Mimbre, La Pata de Palo, and Los Huevos del Plata); Padin's Los Huevos del Plata ran 16 issues over three years, followed by OVUM 10 and OVUM. Works: W01 (and OVUM 10/OVUM). Watchable behaviour: four magazine covers sit at four corners of a map; pages fly between them along postal routes; each arrival adds a page to the receiving magazine, and the magazines thicken over time. Takeaway: the magazine is not a container for the work, it is the network's address book -- each issue is a handshake between editors. (C10984)

M5 -- The open-call wall. An exhibition with no gatekeeper, where anyone who mails a postcard is in the show (First Latinoamerican Mail Art Exposition / Festival de la Postal Creativa, Galeria U, Montevideo, 1974, the first documented mail-art exhibition in Latin America -- Padin's characterisation, repeated by ICAA; attribute, do not state as uncontested fact). Works: W12. Watchable behaviour: postcards arrive from off-screen in a steady stream and pin themselves to a gallery wall; the wall fills from sparse to dense; each card is small, cheap, and different, and no card is ever rejected. Takeaway: the wall is the opposite of a jury -- the work is the accumulation, and the accumulation is the show. (C10962)

M6 -- The third-party relay. Under censorship, mail that cannot go directly is passed hand to hand through intermediaries (in the mid-1970s Padin received and sent mail only through third parties rather than through the post, and asked Carrion in Amsterdam to publish and distribute Happy Bicentennial for him). Works: W15 via Carrion/Amsterdam; Padin's mid-1970s third-party mailing. Watchable behaviour: an envelope never travels in a straight line; it hops from node to node across a map, each hop redrawing the route; when one node goes dark, the route reroutes around it and the envelope still arrives, later and more creased. Takeaway: the network is the route -- cut the direct line and the mail finds a longer one; the delay and the creases are part of the message. (C10985)

M7 -- The rescue campaign. The network answering an arrest with mail, turning stamps and letters into diplomatic pressure (Cook's project and Blaine's French actions; rubber stamps reading "Uruguay is a Prison" and "Free Padin & Caraballo"; Carrion's Ephemera cover; US and French diplomatic intervention; release in November 1979). Grounded in the W16 arrest and its campaign, not a single object. Watchable behaviour: letters from many countries converge on one cell; each arrival adds a stamp to a growing pile outside the door; the pile grows until the door opens. The same mechanism in reverse is the archive's destruction: the authorities confiscated and destroyed Padin's archive. Takeaway: mail as a crowd -- one letter is a message, a thousand letters is a siege; the campaign is the work, and the release is its opening night. (C10961, C10973)

M8 -- Survival by copy. When the originals are seized, the work lives on in other people's archives and reprints (Padin's archive was confiscated and destroyed, yet his works survive in the MoMA Library, the Franklin Furnace Archive, the Smithsonian's Held papers, and the Archivo Lafuente's 1,000-plus Padin documents; Instrumentos/74 was reissued in Montevideo in 2019). Works: W11 reissued 2019; archive survivals. Watchable behaviour: an original document burns or is seized and its image fades; then copies bloom elsewhere on the map, each slightly degraded, none identical, and the work persists as a swarm of imperfect copies rather than a single original. Takeaway: the copy is not a lesser original -- under a regime that destroys originals, the copy is the only original that survives. (C10986)

## Aesthetic logic

His logic is the logic of the post office and the printing press, not the studio. The materials are the cheapest that travel: printed magazine, distorted letters, photocopy, postcard, stamp, booklet, a cart, a CD-ROM. The typography is the typography of bureaucracy turned back on itself -- perforated sheets, denominations, "PRIMER DIA" logic without the seal, the word "revolution" blighted away page by page -- because the stamp is a small piece of state authority, and he learned to print his own, and the manual is a small piece of state instruction, and he learned to write his own. The palette, as reported in sources, is the palette of the mail: black ink on cheap paper, the blue and red of stamps and postmarks, the gift-wrap brightness of a bomb manual dressed as a birthday present. Nothing is precious; the work is designed to be mailed, returned, pinned to a wall, seized, copied, and re-issued as a call years later. The through line is the network as the work: early the network carries a magazine (W01), then a letter that decays into pattern (W02), then a manual that should not exist (W15), then a stamp that gets its maker jailed (W16), then a campaign that gets him out (M7), then copies that outlive the archive (M8). The viewer is never a spectator: they read the letter until it stops being readable, they follow the manual until it stops being innocent, they load themselves into the cart, they answer the call. And the politics is never illustrated: it is built into the procedure -- a stamp that should not exist, a word tied to a tower that falls, a wall that takes every card, a route that reroutes around the censor. The work that began as a magazine against an editorial monopoly in Montevideo in 1965 ends as an archive of 1,500 postcards in a university vault, and the distance between those two gestures is the whole career. (C11011)

## The network

The origin node is the 1967 four-way exchange: Padin's own account dates his mail art experience to 1967, when he and three others exchanged their publications: Edgardo Antonio Vigo (Diagonal Cero), Guillermo Deisler (Ediciones Mimbre), Damaso Ogaz (La Pata de Palo), and Padin himself (Los Huevos del Plata) (C10951). The Argentine pole: Edgardo Antonio Vigo (1928-1997) of La Plata, Argentina, founded Diagonal Cero (1962-1969, 28 issues) and Hexagono '71 (1971-1975); he introduced Graciela Gutierrez Marx to mail art in 1968, and the two worked as the duo G.E. MarxVigo from 1977 to 1983 (C10953). Graciela Gutierrez Marx, Argentine mail artist of La Plata, co-founded the Asociacion Latinoamericana y del Caribe de Artistascorreo with Padin and others at Rosario, Argentina, on 31 August 1984 (C10954). She is study #652, the hop source for this study's doorway. Horacio Zabala (b. 1943), Argentine artist, invited Gutierrez Marx to the 1975 Ultima exposicion internacional de arte correo in Argentina and co-authored Vigo's 1975 text "Arte-correo, una nueva forma de expresion" (C10955). Zabala already has a local study file, so he is a network node here, not a future pick. The Chilean pole: Guillermo Deisler (1940-1995), Chilean visual poet born in Santiago, ran Ediciones Mimbre (1963-1973), was arrested after the 1973 coup, and lived in exile in France, Bulgaria, and the German Democratic Republic, settling in Halle (Saale) in 1986; his international mail-art portfolio UNI/vers(;) ran 1987-1995 in 35 issues, and his collection of more than 5,000 mail-art works is held by the Academy of the Arts, Berlin (C10956). The fellow prisoner: Jorge Caraballo (1941-2014), Uruguayan artist, was arrested together with Padin in August 1977 and disappeared into Montevideo's political prison system; the two were the joint subjects of the International Campaign to Free Padin and Caraballo, and Caraballo's own 1975 work was a rubber-stamp imprint with writing on a postcard (C10957). Padin and Caraballo viewed art as a way to expose the underlying causes of the conflicts, using photography, signography, and visual poetry (C10958). The Amsterdam relay: Ulises Carrion (1941-1989), Mexican artist in Amsterdam, was Padin's friend who published and distributed Happy Bicentennial (1976) through his Daylight Press when Padin could not safely mail it himself; Carrion made the imprisonment of Caraballo and Padin the cover of the fifth issue of his assembling magazine Ephemera, and in spring 1978, after hearing of the imprisonment, he presented his text "Mail Art and the Big Monster" in Warsaw and launched his own artist-led postal system E.A.M.I.S. (C10959). The Brazilian pole: Paulo Bruscky (b. 1949) of Recife, Brazil, names Clemente Padin among the mail artists he was in touch with, alongside Edgardo Vigo, Horacio Zabala, and Jorge Caraballo; Bruscky's 1975 Recife mail-art exhibition with Daniel Santiago was closed immediately after opening and both artists were imprisoned for three days (C10960). The rescue network: Geoffrey Cook (USA) was one of two prime organizers who channelled the mail-art network's protest over the Padin/Caraballo arrests into action, initiating a mail-art project to help them, while Julien Blaine in France instituted similar actions; rubber stamps circulated with the legends "Uruguay is a Prison" and "Free Padin & Caraballo", and both the American and French governments intervened diplomatically, the artists being convicted by a military court under Article 58, section II, of the Uruguayan military code for attacking the morale and reputation of the army (C10961). The network reached beyond Latin America and outlived the dictatorship: in February 2015 Padin sent the young Uruguayan artist Francisco Tomsich a list of six Yugoslav mail artists with whom he had corresponded in the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s, including the Novi Sad mail artist and critic Andrej Tisma (C10963). In 1990 Padin sent mail art to John Held Jr. in Dallas, Texas, a postcard with artistamps and stamps about the imprisonment of Nelson Mandela and apartheid in South Africa, now in the Smithsonian's Archives of American Art (C10964). Adjacent artists for the chain: Ulises Carrion, the Amsterdam switchboard (see Future candidates); Jorge Caraballo, the cellmate; Guillermo Deisler, the exile. (C11012)
