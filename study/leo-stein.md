# Leo Stein: Study Notes

**Site:** Leo Stein (1872-1947), American art collector and critic -- born Saturday 11 May 1872 in Allegheny City, Pennsylvania (now part of Pittsburgh), second son and last surviving child of Daniel Stein (1832-1891) and Amelia Keyser Stein (1842-1888), German-Jewish parents in the wholesale clothing business; the family lived in Vienna and Paris in the 1870s, then settled in Oakland, California, in 1880; both parents dead before he was twenty (mother 1888, father 1891), eldest brother Michael taking over the family business and the purse; Harvard 1892-1894 (two undergraduate years, then one semester of law school, withdrawn), a world trip 1895-96 with his cousin Fred, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, BA 1898, then one year in a doctoral program in zoology; Fiesole, near Florence, autumn 1900 to 1902, studying Mantegna and the Quattrocento and befriending Bernard Berenson, who drew his attention to Cezanne, and seeing the Cezanne collections of Egisto Fabbri and Charles Loeser; London 1902; Paris from late December 1902, renting the atelier and two-bedroom apartment at 27 rue de Fleurus in summer 1903, his youngest sister Gertrude joining him that fall, Michael and Sarah Stein following in January 1904 to 58 rue Madame; the collecting decade 1903-1913, Saturday-evening salons at which he lectured on modern art from 1906 to about 1912; the split 1913-14, Leo leaving rue de Fleurus in spring/April 1914 for Settignano, near Florence, the joint collection divided, Gertrude keeping the Picasso paintings and most of the Matisse, Leo taking sixteen Renoirs, two Cezannes (one of them Five Apples, 1877-78), a few Matisses, one Delacroix, one Daumier, a portrait sketch Picasso had done of him, and a few drawings; World War I in New York, writing regular art reviews and criticism for the New Republic; married Nina Auzias in March 1921 and settled in Settignano for the rest of his life; lecture series at the New School, New York, 1929; author of The A-B-C of Aesthetics (Boni and Liveright, 1927) and Appreciation: Painting, Poetry, and Prose (Crown, 1947, published in the year of his death), and of the New Republic essay "Pablo Picasso" (Wednesday 23 April 1924); died Tuesday 29 July 1947 in Florence, of stomach cancer, aged 75, buried in Settignano; his letters, papers and journals published posthumously as Journey into the Self (1950), edited by Edmund Fuller, introduction by Van Wyck Brooks; his papers at the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University (Leo Stein Collection, 1892-1950). ## M1. The Pooled Windfall
Two siblings, 8,000 francs each, pooled to buy art instead of bonds. The joint collection began in late 1904 when Michael Stein, the family banker, announced that their trust account had accumulated a balance of 8,000 francs each (about $1,600 each), and Leo and Gertrude "decided to pool their resources and buy art rather than bonds" (Met, Gallery Two). On Friday 28 October 1904 they spent it at Ambroise Vollard's shop on the rue Laffitte: seven works in one day, including Gauguin's Sunflowers and Three Tahitians, Cezanne's Bathers, and two Renoirs. Leo had assumed buying art was a hobby for the rich until he found he could afford work by his contemporaries: "a bit like a desperado... One could actually own paintings even if one were not a millionaire." Vollard told him he was one of the few who bought pictures "not because he was rich but despite the fact that he wasn't." What it IS: two separate purses merging into one buying eye; every acquisition is a joint decision made with joint money, and the wall that results belongs to neither purse alone. **Takeaway:** give two agents one shared purse and make every acquisition require both to say yes; the collection is the record of their agreements.

## M2. Buying in Twos
"They frequently bought in twos because one of them usually liked one more than the other one did." The sentence is Gertrude's, from The Autobiography of Alice B. Toklas, and it is the collection's operating rule: when taste diverged, they did not argue the single picture down to one choice, they doubled the purchase. On Gauguin the split was visible in the same canvas: "Gertrude liked his sun-flowers but not his figures and her brother preferred the figures." What it IS: paired acquisition as a hedge against divergent taste; the pair is the unit, not the picture, and the collection is a record of disagreements resolved by doubling rather than by deciding. **Takeaway:** when two tastes disagree, buy the pair; let the disagreement stand as two objects instead of forcing one winner.

## M3. The Divided Taste
One image, two readings, each viewer extracting a different region. The Gauguin is the cleanest case: the same Sunflowers canvas holds flowers for Gertrude and figures for Leo, and neither reading cancels the other. The mechanism generalizes across the whole collection: Leo was "most attracted to colorful figurative work -- traditional subject matter rendered in innovative ways" (Met, Gallery Two), Gertrude to the strangeness of the paint itself, and the wall held both readings at once. What it IS: a painting that re-crops itself depending on who is looking; the sunflowers brighten for one viewer, the figures for the other, and the picture is the sum of the two incompatible attentions. **Takeaway:** build one image that holds two readings at once and let the viewer choose which region lights up; never resolve the two readings into one.

## M4. The Nasty Smear
First reaction to Matisse's Woman with a Hat at the Salon d'Automne of 1905: "It was a tremendous effort on his part, a thing brilliant and powerful, but the nastiest smear of paint I had ever seen. It was what I was unknowingly waiting for, and I would have snatched it at once if I had not needed a few days to get over the unpleasantness of the putting on of the paint." He bought it days later, for 500 francs (NPR/SFMOMA reporting) or 300 francs (Gertrude's own account; see C9), straight out of the Salle des Fauves, where it made everybody laugh except a few who got mad. The same delayed conversion happened with Matisse's Luxe, Calme et Volupte, seen at the Salon d'Automne of 1904: it made on Leo "the strongest impression, though not the most agreeable." What it IS: revulsion converting into recognition after a delay; the smear is unreadable on first sight and the picture arrives only after the viewer has sat with the unpleasantness for a few days. **Takeaway:** make the first sight ugly on purpose and reward staying; a viewer who leaves early must never see the portrait, and a viewer who stays must watch the smear resolve.

## M5. The Plate Experiment
Leo put an ordinary Italian earthenware plate on the table and looked at it every day "for minutes or for hours... and waited for it to become one [a picture]. In time it did. The change came suddenly when the plate as an inventorial object, one made up of parts that could be separately listed, a certain shape, certain colors applied to it, and so on, went over into a composition to which all these elements were merely contributory. The painted composition on the plate ceased to be on it but became a part of a larger composition which was the plate as a whole. I had made a beginning to seeing pictorially." (Appreciation, "On Reading Poetry and Seeing Pictures", verified verbatim from the book.) The doctrine behind it, from the same chapter: "People commonly assume that they can see, and need only learn what they should look at. My experience contradicts this, and insists on the all-importance of learning to see. The what will take care of itself if the how has been acquired." What it IS: sustained attention flipping an inventory of parts into a composition; the snap from list to picture is sudden, it cannot be forced, and it only happens after enough looking time has accumulated. **Takeaway:** show a still life of labeled parts and let the labels dissolve one by one until the parts snap into a single picture; the snap is the event, and it must arrive suddenly after a wait, never on a timer the viewer can see.

## M6. The Division
One shared wall split by declared indifference. Leo's division letter to Gertrude, 1913-14: "The Cezanne apples have a unique importance to me that nothing can replace. The Picasso landscape is not important in any such sense. We are, as it seems to me on the whole, both so well off now that we needn't repine. The Cezanne's had to be divided. I am willing to leave you the Picasso oeuvre, as you left me the Renoir, and you can have everything except that. I want to keep the few drawings that I have. This leaves no string for me, it is financially equable either way for estimates are only rough & ready methods, & I'm afraid you'll have to look upon the loss of the apples as an act of God. I have been anxious above all things that each should have in reason all that he wanted, and just as I was glad that Renoir was sufficiently indifferent to you so that you were ready to give them up, so I am glad that Pablo is sufficiently indifferent to me that I am willing to let you have all you want of it." The division itself: Gertrude kept the Picasso paintings and most of the Matisse; Leo took sixteen Renoirs, two Cezannes (Five Apples, 1877-78, plus one other, unidentified), a few Matisses, one Delacroix, one Daumier, the Picasso portrait sketch of himself, and a few drawings. What it IS: division by what each party does NOT want; the apples are the exception -- the one object with "a unique importance to me that nothing can replace", indivisible, kept at the cost of everything else, its loss to the other side an act of God. **Takeaway:** sort a shared wall into two rooms by indifference, not by desire; let one small cluster of five apples refuse to split and travel together, and make the refusal the piece.

## M7. The Explainer's Salon
Saturday evenings, 1906 to about 1912: Leo lectured while the paintings hung around him. "People came, and so I explained, because it was my nature to explain." And: "I can't give a lecture, but I can explain. So on Saturday evenings I explained." (Appreciation, verified.) The salon was the collection as a lecture hall: the pictures were prompts and the talk was the work, and visitors came to hear Leo explain what they were looking at -- Matisse once asked Leo to give a speech about his work to a visiting group, which Leo declined, citing it as proof from Matisse himself that he "really understood him". The mechanism has a built-in ending: Leo's increasing deafness led him to distance himself from the Saturday evenings, and by 1913 he recognized it was time to leave rue de Fleurus altogether. What it IS: a room where each painting, when approached, starts talking -- a voice that explains it -- and the room goes quiet when the explainer's hearing fails. **Takeaway:** make the pictures talk when approached, one explanation per picture, in a single steady voice; then let the voice fade as the explainer withdraws, and leave the pictures hanging in the silence.

## M8. The Apostasy
Last Matisse purchased 1908, last Picasso 1910; the champion sells his discoveries to buy Renoir and walks out. Around 1910 Leo "turned from champion of the avant-garde to critic, especially with regard to Picasso and the artist's shift toward Cubism" (Met), and in 1913-14 he "began to sell off his Cezannes, Matisses, and Picassos in order to purchase paintings by Pierre-Auguste Renoir", many from Bernheim-Jeune. His own account, in Appreciation: "When my interest in Cezanne declined, when Matisse was temporarily in eclipse, when Picasso turned to foolishness, I began to withdraw from the Saturday evenings." The turn was not a conversion to conservatism -- in the same book he still defends Matisse's genuineness, still calls early Picasso a great talent, still uses Cezanne as the standard of interlocking planes, and still prefers "a good Laurens or a good Meissonier than half a ton of the empty canvases covered moderately well with paint that of late years one has seen everywhere". What he rejected was Cubism as a system and "modern art" as a label and a program: "In 'modern art' I was never interested. I don't even know what it is, and I dislike the term." What it IS: a collector's taste with a half-life; the eye that bet on the future gets tired of the future and retreats to the past, selling the bright shapes to buy soft ones. **Takeaway:** build a wall where the bright Fauve and Cubist shapes slowly desaturate and are replaced, one by one, by soft Renoir pinks; let the collector's own figure walk out of the frame while the replacements are still arriving.

## M9. The Mirror Decision
Late December 1902: after dining with the cellist Pablo Casals, Leo sketched his own reflection in a wardrobe mirror in his Paris hotel room, and "later recalled it as the moment he decided to become an artist" (Met, Gallery Two). Within a year he had concluded he was "too neurotic to make it as a painter" and became a collector instead -- a collector by nature, as the Met puts it, who had already been buying inexpensive first editions, small panel paintings, antique weapons and Renaissance furniture in Fiesole. The sketch itself does not survive in any source read; do not illustrate it. What it IS: a self-portrait that fails and converts into a collection -- the eye turns outward, from making pictures to choosing them, and the choosing becomes the life's work. **Takeaway:** show a mirror that reflects the viewer, then let the reflection step aside and the mirror become a window full of other people's paintings; the failure of the self-portrait is the beginning of the collection.

## M10. The Quarrel Purchase
Picasso's Young Girl with a Flower Basket (Fillette a la corbeille fleurie), 1905, Rose Period: Leo wanted it, Gertrude did not; bought anyway from Clovis Sagot's gallery in fall 1905 for 150 francs (about $30), after a quarrel in which Sagot, when Gertrude disliked the legs, offered to "guillotine the canvas in half" and Leo insisted on the whole picture. Gertrude later acquired Leo's share, then kept it through the split. It last sold at Christie's on 8 May 2018 for $115 million, to the Nahmad family. The contested purchase outlived the partnership and ended up the most valuable thing either of them owned. What it IS: two hands pulling at one small canvas; the canvas stays, the hands leave, and the price tag keeps climbing in the corner. **Takeaway:** stage one small canvas pulled between two hands; let the hands leave and the canvas remain, and let a small price tag in the corner climb from 150 francs to $115 million while nothing else in the room moves.

## M11. Pictorial vs Sculptural Seeing
Things can be seen in two ways, and Leo's distinction is explicit: "I found that things could be seen in two ways, pictorially and sculpturally, as though one saw with reference to a plane in front of the object, or with reference to a plane behind the object. In one case the thing was seen as flat, but in deep space, which is the paradox of pictorial composition. In the other, it was seen as round, but in shallow space." (Appreciation, verified.) The plate experiment is the pictorial way: the plate stops being an inventory of parts and becomes a composition referred to a plane in front of it. The sculptural way is the inventory itself: round things in shallow space, each part separately listable. What it IS: one object with two seeings, toggled by where the viewer puts the reference plane -- in front of the object the parts flatten into deep space and compose; behind it the parts round out into shallow space and list themselves. **Takeaway:** give the viewer a plane they can slide from the front of the object to behind it; as it crosses the object, the same still life must flip from a flat composition in deep space to a round inventory in shallow space, with no third state in between.

## M12. Interlock, Not Intersect
Cezanne is Leo's standard, and the standard is a technical one: "In Cezanne the planes do not intersect, but interlock." Against it, Cubism fails: "Intersecting planes seem to me to be entirely without justification. Long before cubism, it had seemed to me reasonable that if the subject did not count, one might make pictures entirely abstract, and I made a number of drawings on that assumption in 1906. But I found that when lines and planes intersected, they became confused, and the composition lost determinate character, and could be read in different ways." (Appreciation, verified.) He tried the abstract drawings himself in 1906, before Cubism, and abandoned them on the evidence of his own hand. The companion observation, from hanging the collection: Cezannes "maintained their full affirmations" after a summer away while other pictures "were somewhat dimmed". What it IS: two ways for planes to meet -- interlocking, where each plane holds the next in place and the composition has one determinate reading, and intersecting, where lines cross, the composition confuses itself, and it can be read in different ways. **Takeaway:** build a field of planes that meet in two modes and let the viewer toggle them; in interlock mode the picture holds still and reads one way, in intersect mode the same planes cross, confuse, and offer several readings at once.

## Evolution

EARLY (1872-1903, Allegheny, Oakland, Harvard, Baltimore, Fiesole): born into a German-Jewish wholesale clothing family, orphaned young, raised on the family trust; Harvard 1892-1894, law for one semester, then the 1895-96 world trip with cousin Fred (no Japan leg verified); Johns Hopkins BA 1898, a year of zoology; Fiesole 1900-02, Mantegna and the Quattrocento under Berenson's influence, Cezanne first seen on the walls of Fabbri and Loeser; the wardrobe-mirror sketch of December 1902 and the decision to become an artist, abandoned within a year for collecting. The early Leo is a student of looking: he collects inexpensive first editions, small panels, antique weapons, Renaissance furniture, and he learns from Berenson that a picture can be judged by the relations of its parts.

MIDDLE (1903-1914, the rue de Fleurus decade): the collecting years. First purchase Cezanne's The Spring House, ca. 1879, in 1903 (Met; see C8 for the dispute); the pooled 8,000 francs and the seven-work Vollard purchase of Friday 28 October 1904; Woman with a Hat and the Picasso Girl with a Basket of Flowers in fall 1905; the Saturday salons and the lectures, 1906 to about 1912; Alfred Barr's verdict that between 1905 and 1907 Leo "was possibly the most discerning connoisseur and collector of 20th century painting in the world". The middle period is the discovery and the explanation: he buys what nobody else wants, he explains it on Saturday evenings, and the wall fills three to five deep, unframed. Then the turn: last Matisse 1908, last Picasso 1910, deafness, the selling of the avant-garde for Renoir, the division letter, the move to Settignano in spring 1914.

LATE (1914-1947, New York, Settignano, the writing years): World War I in New York, New Republic reviews, close with Mabel Dodge and Dr. Albert C. Barnes; marriage to Nina Auzias, March 1921; Settignano for the rest of his life; the 1924 New Republic essay restating the refusal of Cubism ("As for his intellectuality, that is rubbish... Picasso is thoroughly intelligent in the ordinary human way, and is ingenious to the last turn, but he is in no serious sense a thinker"); the New School lectures 1929; The A-B-C of Aesthetics (1927), a primer that opens by denying the subject exists ("For aesthetics there are no such books. There are no authorities and it is somewhat doubtful whether the subject exists"); Appreciation (1947), written about a year before his death, the farewell meditation on learning to see, the plate experiment, the interlocking planes, the Delacroix verdict, the Picasso exchanges. He wrote to Gertrude in 1916 and 1919; she did not respond. He read of her death in a newspaper. They died 12 months and two days apart, still not speaking.

AFTERLIFE SO FAR: Brenda Wineapple, Sister Brother: Gertrude and Leo Stein (Putnam, 1996), "the first book ever written about Leo", six-plus years of research, the standard dual biography and the source behind most modern accounts of the split; James R. Mellow, Charmed Circle (1974, reissued), the standard narrative of the salon years; Linda Wagner-Martin, Favored Strangers (1995); Diana Souhami, No Modernism Without Lesbians (2020). The Museum of Modern Art mounted "Four Americans in Paris: The Collections of Gertrude Stein and Her Family" (December 1970 - March 1971), reuniting more than 200 works; "The Steins Collect: Matisse, Picasso, and the Parisian Avant-Garde" reunited about 200 works in 2011-12 (SFMOMA, 21 May - 6 September 2011, more than 350,000 visitors; Grand Palais, Paris, 3 October 2011 - 16 January 2012; Metropolitan Museum of Art, February 2012 - 3 June 2012). The New Criterion on the 2011-12 show: it "finally gives Leo, Sarah, and Michael proper recognition for their discernment and important support of French modernism... something conspicuously absent from Gertrude's recollections." His papers are at Yale: the Leo Stein Collection, 1892-1950, Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library. The pictures dispersed: Leo's Renoirs and Cezannes through Barnes, Durand-Ruel and private hands; Five Apples sold by Leo to Barnes in 1921, its present location not established in sources read; Gertrude's Picassos largely bequeathed to the Met, her Portrait by Picasso there. The ironic fate, per the reprint blurb: to be referred to as "Gertrude's brother", "ironic as for many years he was her mentor and counsellor."

## Aesthetic Logic

Leo's aesthetic is an aesthetic of learning to see, and it is the opposite of his sister's. Where Gertrude repeats a word until it becomes a thing, Leo looks at a plate until it becomes a picture; where she refuses the timeline, he refuses the label -- "In 'modern art' I was never interested. I don't even know what it is, and I dislike the term." The unit of his system is not the sentence but the relation: "A work of art is a composition, and its persistent reality as a work of art depends on relations between the elements of this composition. 'Right' relations make the composition real, and when the relations are not 'right' the composition lacks reality and disintegrates." (Appreciation, foreword.) Seeing is a skill, not a gift: most people assume they can see and need only be told what to look at; Leo insists the how matters and the what takes care of itself. The plate experiment is the proof: an inventory of parts (a certain shape, certain colors applied to it) flips, suddenly, into a composition, and the flip cannot be scheduled or argued into happening. The same discipline governs his judgments: Cezanne's planes interlock, so the composition holds one determinate reading; Cubism's planes intersect, so it confuses itself and can be read in different ways; Delacroix is "by all odds the greatest French painter of his century" because he can get away with his subjects; Picasso is a great talent and a great illustrator but "in no serious sense a thinker". And the whole system is held together by a democratic demand, stated in the foreword: "Art is no place for snobs." Great things are exceptional "not in their kind but in their degree"; every personal letter "may be creative writing if one's interest is to make it such"; "Wisdom that is worth having must be brought down to earth," even if "on solid earth a snail's pace is the measure of its progress." A piece built on Leo fails when it decorates (adds a salon, adds a French accent, adds a Picasso) and succeeds when it makes the viewer do the work of seeing: one thing, looked at long enough, until it flips.

## Network

Gertrude Stein (1874-1946) -- the unnumbered doorway, the subject of #480. Younger sister, co-collector at 27 rue de Fleurus 1903-14, then estranged; Leo introduced her to modern art and was her mentor for years; she became his rival in judgment (Cubism, her prose); split spring 1914; never spoke again after one brief post-WWI street greeting. Full detail in study #480 and in the split section of the corrections below.

N1. Alice B. Toklas (1877-1967) -- Gertrude's partner. DIRECT. Met Gertrude Sunday 8 September 1907; moved into 27 rue de Fleurus in 1910, making the flat a three-person household; Leo reportedly called her "a kind of abnormal vampire" (secondhand, see C24). Her arrival is one of the three co-causes of the split. No evidenced direct Leo-Toklas friendship; the relationship is evidenced antagonism, which still counts as direct contact (housemates 1910-14).

N2. Michael Stein (1865-1938) -- Elder brother. DIRECT. Family banker: he managed the trust and announced the 8,000-franc balance that started the joint buying. Followed Leo and Gertrude to Paris January 1904. Fellow collector with his wife Sarah, emphasizing Matisse; lent two pieces to the 1913 Armory Show (Leo lent three).

N3. Sarah Stein (1870-1953) -- Sister-in-law, Michael's wife. DIRECT. Co-collector of Matisse with Michael; helped Matisse establish an art school in Paris; ran the second Stein salon at 58 rue Madame (afternoon salon, complementing the rue de Fleurus Saturdays). Met Gertrude and Alice on Toklas's first day in Paris (Sunday 8 September 1907, at Sarah and Michael's apartment). She played a major role in convincing Leo to buy Woman with a Hat, and later bought it from him.

N4. Nina Auzias (d. 1949; dates NOT verified this leg, commonly cited) -- Wife. DIRECT. Leo's long-time love interest; they settled near Florence and married in March 1921 (Wikipedia; Prabook). Kirkus/Wineapple: "the woman who would become Leo's wife" was one of the two women who drew the siblings apart. She died by suicide two years after Leo (Alchetron only; treat the suicide detail as secondhand, see C15).

N5. Bernard Berenson (1865-1959) -- Art historian and connoisseur. DIRECT. Befriended Leo in Florence 1900-02 while Leo studied Mantegna and the Quattrocento; "drew Stein's attention to Paul Cezanne, whose work was virtually unknown at the time" (Met). Berenson's views "had a lasting impression on Stein". Also hosted Gertrude and Leo at his English country house in 1902. The single most important intellectual influence on Leo's eye. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #479's Future candidates section ("New from #459" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N6. Pablo Picasso (1881-1973) -- DIRECT. Leo bought his first Picassos in 1905 (two large pictures after seeing drawings at a furniture store); bought Girl with a Basket of Flowers from Sagot that fall; bought his last Picasso in 1910; refused Cubism from 1911-12 on; met Picasso again in Paris in 1920 (Rue de la Boetie) and on the street some years after 1929. Picasso painted portraits of both Leo and Gertrude (1905-06). Their exchanges are quoted verbatim in Appreciation: "You have no right to judge. I'm an artist, and you are not."; "If Leo sells pictures of mine to get a Renoir, I hope he'll get a good one."; "Even if this new way is a mistake, it has profited me."

N7. Henri Matisse (1869-1954) -- DIRECT. Leo bought Woman with a Hat at the Salon d'Automne 1905 and Le bonheur de vivre in 1906; called the latter "the most important work of our time" (secondhand via Richardson). Matisse came to the salon once only; his wife never dared come at all (Leo, Appreciation). Leo abandoned Matisse after 1908 but defended his genuineness to the end: "Matisse's work, unlike Picasso's, was one whose genuineness one could never question."

N8. Paul Cezanne (1839-1906) -- INFLUENCE, not a contact (no meeting evidenced; Cezanne died 1906, reclusive in Aix; Leo knew the work through Berenson, Fabbri and Loeser in Florence 1900-02 and through Vollard in Paris). Leo's first purchase (1903) was Cezanne's The Spring House; the Cezanne apples (Five Apples) were the one picture he fought Gertrude over at the split. His technical standard: interlocking, not intersecting, planes.

N9. Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1841-1919) -- INFLUENCE (no meeting evidenced; Leo bought Renoirs from Vollard and Bernheim-Jeune, never records meeting him). The late turn: from 1913-14 Leo sold Cezannes, Matisses and Picassos to buy Renoirs; took sixteen Renoirs in the division. Hanging observation: Renoirs hung together "enhanced each other" while grouping the Cezannes "had no such result".

N10. Paul Gauguin (1848-1903) -- INFLUENCE (died 1903, the year Leo's collecting began in earnest; no meeting possible). Leo bought two Gauguins in the first Vollard spend (Sunflowers and Three Tahitians) and "long before" the split disposed of them; late verdict "only secondrate".

N11. Ambroise Vollard (1866-1939) -- Dealer. DIRECT. Sold the Steins their first pictures from his rue Laffitte shop (1903-04): Gauguins, Cezannes, Renoirs, and later Matisses. Leo records Vollard's remark that the Steins "were the only customers who bought pictures, not because they were rich, but despite the fact that they weren't", and that Vollard never had to bargain with them. Leo also records talking Vollard into taking Matisse seriously ("And you really think that Matisse is as important as that?").

N12. Clovis Sagot (1854-1913) -- Dealer, ex-clown. DIRECT. Showed Leo and Gertrude the Picasso Girl with a Basket of Flowers (1905); when Gertrude disliked the legs he offered to "guillotine the canvas in half"; Leo insisted on the whole picture and bought it. Sagot was the conduit for the early Picassos.

N13. Juan Gris (1887-1927) -- THIN. Gris is Gertrude's post-split cubist (her collection at her death emphasized Picasso and Juan Gris), and he was a salon presence, but no direct Leo-Gris contact is evidenced in this leg and Leo's Appreciation never mentions him. Do not assert a Leo-Gris relationship; the link is collection orbit only.

N14. Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler (1884-1979) -- Dealer. THIN. Kahnweiler bought Picasso from 1907 and signed him exclusively in 1912 -- he took over Picasso's market just as Leo was leaving it. No direct Leo-Kahnweiler meeting is evidenced in this leg. The link is structural (dealer succession), not personal. Flag as thin.

N15. Claribel Cone (1864-1929) -- Collector. DIRECT. Baltimore friend from the Steins' Baltimore years; visited the Steins in Paris 1903; traveled in Italy with Leo as guide to museums; attended the 1905 Salon d'Automne with Gertrude and Leo. Leo loaned Matisse's Blue Nude to the 1913 Armory Show and sold it to Claribel in 1926. She is the clearest "both sides" figure after the split. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section ("New from #480" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N16. Etta Cone (1870-1949) -- Collector. DIRECT. Same Baltimore/Paris history as Claribel; "Etta credited Leo Stein with helping her develop an appreciation of modern art" (Wikipedia, Cone sisters); typed the manuscript of Gertrude's Three Lives. With Claribel built the largest private Matisse holding; their collection now forms a wing of the Baltimore Museum of Art. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section ("New from #480" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N17. Mabel Dodge Luhan (1879-1962) -- Salonniere. DIRECT. Met Leo in Paris; "developed close relationships with Mabel Dodge... both of whom he had met in Paris" during his WWI years in New York (Met). Leo was active in her New York circles while writing for the New Republic. She is one of the few documented post-split Leo intimates. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section ("New from #480" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N18. Carl Van Vechten (1880-1964) -- Writer and photographer. DIRECT. Took the well-known portrait photograph of Leo Stein, Tuesday 9 November 1937. Salon attendee at 27 rue de Fleurus. Gertrude's close friend and promoter; the 1937 portrait proves he also kept contact with Leo late -- a both-sides figure. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section ("New from #480" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N19. Sylvia Beach (1887-1962) -- Bookseller, Shakespeare and Company. THIN. Beach introduced Sherwood Anderson to Gertrude in 1921 and was central to the post-Leo salon generation. No Leo-Beach contact is evidenced in this leg (Leo had left Paris in 1914; Beach opened her shop in 1919). Link is via Gertrude/Anderson, not direct.

N20. James Joyce (1882-1941) -- THIN. Joyce is a Gertrude salon figure of the 1920s and met Gertrude in Paris; no Leo-Joyce contact is evidenced. Leo was in Italy/New York during Joyce's Paris years. Do not assert.

N21. F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896-1940) -- THIN. Fitzgerald met Gertrude in Paris in the 1920s (salon regular); no Leo-Fitzgerald contact is evidenced. Leo was in Settignano. Do not assert.

N22. Ernest Hemingway (1899-1961) -- STUDIED as #479 (2026-11-10; log header "## Study #479: Ernest Hemingway"; local file ernest-hemingway.md exists). DIRECT (claimed by Hemingway himself). Hemingway arrived Paris 1921, after Leo left, and was Gertrude's protege -- but in a 1952 letter to Charles Fenton he wrote: "Stein and Pound I knew very well and Leo Stein was a friend of mine as were Picasso, Braque, Masson, and Miro" (Sotheby's catalogue, Selected Letters, pp. 774-78). So a Leo-Hemingway friendship is evidenced by Hemingway's own words; where and when they met is NOT evidenced in this leg (Leo visited Paris in the 1920s and the US in 1929; Hemingway visited Italy). Treat as direct-but-undated.

N23. Sherwood Anderson (1876-1941) -- THIN. Anderson visited Paris 1921, was introduced to Gertrude by Sylvia Beach, and gave Hemingway his introduction to Gertrude. No Leo-Anderson contact is evidenced; Leo had left Paris seven years earlier. Link is via Gertrude only.

N24. Alfred Stieglitz (1864-1946) -- THIN / PARALLEL. No personal meeting is evidenced. The link is institutional: Leo lent three pieces to the 1913 Armory Show while Stieglitz's 291 lent six Matisse drawings, a Picasso drawing and the bronze Head of a Woman; the Steins' Paris studio and 291 were the two places in 1905-13 where an American could see the new European painting. Do not claim they met.

N25. Egisto Fabbri (1866-1933) -- Collector. DIRECT. Italian-American painter and banking heir; in Fiesole in autumn 1900 he showed Leo his recently formed collection of Cezanne's work (Met, Gallery One), with Charles Loeser. With Loeser he owned the two most important early Cezanne collections in Florence. Leo's Cezanne education happened on Fabbri's walls.

N26. Charles Loeser (1864-1928) -- Collector. DIRECT. Heir to the Brooklyn department store of the same name; showed Leo his Cezannes in Florence 1900-02 alongside Fabbri (Met, Gallery One). Same role as Fabbri: Leo's Florence Cezanne school.

N27. Brenda Wineapple (born 1950) -- Biographer. SCHOLARLY. Author of Sister Brother: Gertrude and Leo Stein (Putnam, 1996), the standard dual biography and the source behind most modern accounts of the split, the "abnormal vampire" line, and the street greeting. Also wrote the introduction to the 1996 Nebraska reprint of Appreciation. No personal contact (posthumous scholarship).

N28. Daniel Stein (1832-1891) -- Father. DIRECT (family). German-born, married Amelia Keyser in Baltimore 1864; with his brother Solomon ran the Pittsburgh branch of the family wholesale clothing firm; moved the family to Vienna 1875, then Paris, then Oakland. Died 1891, leaving the children orphaned before either Leo or Gertrude was 20.

N29. Amelia Keyser Stein (1842-1888) -- Mother. DIRECT (family). Died 1888, when Leo was 16. The children were orphaned young, which gave Leo and Gertrude financial independence via the family trust.

N30. Simon Stein (1868-1913) and Bertha Stein Raffel (1870-1924) -- Siblings. DIRECT (family). Simon died 1913, the year of the split; Bertha died 1924. Neither figures in the Paris story; they stayed in America. Listed for completeness of the sibling set (five surviving children: Michael, Simon, Bertha, Leo, Gertrude).

N31. Allan Stein (1895-1951) -- Nephew, Michael and Sarah's son. DIRECT (family). Present in Fiesole summer 1908 (villa with Leo, Gertrude, Michael, Sarah, Toklas). Later co-recipient, with Alice B. Toklas, of the gift of Gertrude's papers to Yale (Beinecke).

N32. Fred Stein (cousin) and Beatrice Stein -- DIRECT. Leo traveled the world with his cousin Fred in 1895-96 after Harvard (Wikipedia). Appreciation is dedicated "to beatrice and fred stein". Fred is the dedicatee-relative; do not confuse with Fred Stein the photographer (different person, no relation evidenced).

N33. Wilhelm Uhde (1874-1947) -- Collector and critic. SALON. Hosted his own Sunday gatherings in Paris; a rue de Fleurus regular. Collector of Rousseau and Picasso. Contact with Leo is via the salon, not documented one-to-one; treat as salon contact.

N34. Guillaume Apollinaire (1880-1918) -- Poet and critic. SALON. Picasso's friend, "a sort of master of ceremonies" at the Steins'; invited by Fernande Olivier to the Banquet Rousseau (Leo, Appreciation). Direct salon contact with Leo in 1908; later the great theorist of Cubism, the movement Leo rejected. Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section (carried from #434); NOT the subject of a study.

N35. Max Jacob (1876-1944) -- Poet and painter. SALON (thin-direct). Picasso's closest friend, present at the salon and at the Banquet Rousseau orbit; his recollection of Matisse showing Picasso the African statuette is a key Cubism origin anecdote. No one-to-one Leo-Jacob record found in this leg; salon contact only.

N36. Georges Braque (1882-1963) -- DIRECT. "For some time one of the intimates at the house" (Leo, Appreciation), yet Leo owned nothing of his and judged the early cubist work "like Picasso's, but weaker". Invited by Fernande to the Banquet Rousseau. With Picasso, inventor of Cubism -- the movement Leo refused.

N37. Fernande Olivier (1881-1966) -- Picasso's lover. DIRECT. Leo's account of the Banquet Rousseau turns on her: visiting Picasso (who was out), talking with Fernande, asking Rousseau to play, Fernande fixing the dinner date and inviting "the Steins, and Braque and Apollinaire and Marie Laurencin" (Appreciation). Direct personal contact, 1908.

N38. Henri Rousseau (1844-1910) -- "Le Douanier". DIRECT. Leo met him at Picasso's flat in 1908 (he had been giving a violin lesson in the Rue Lepic); Leo asked him to play, Fernande invited him to dinner -- the accident that produced the Banquet Rousseau. Leo's judgment: "I recognized a talent, a gift for composition and color, but I don't want that sort of thing hanging on my wall" (Appreciation). Already carried as a standalone candidate in #480's Future candidates section ("New from #480" block); NOT the subject of a study.

N39. Marie Laurencin (1883-1956) -- Painter. SALON. Apollinaire's lover; invited to the Banquet Rousseau by Fernande; a salon regular at 27 rue de Fleurus. No one-to-one Leo-Laurencin record found; salon contact only.

N40. Marcel Duchamp (1887-1968) -- STUDIED as #397 (2026-11-07; log header "## #397 Marcel Duchamp"; local file marcel-duchamp.md exists). DIRECT (via guest list). In 1917 Duchamp invited Leo Stein to his 30th birthday party, as recorded by Florine Stettheimer in her canvas La fete a Duchamp (Thyssen). Leo was in the New York wartime avant-garde circle.

Others evidenced (not numbered): Albert C. C7. Harvard dates: "Beginning in 1892, he studied at Harvard... for two years" (Wikipedia) vs 1893 (LitHub: "When Leo headed to Harvard in 1893, Gertrude followed"); Met, Gallery One, says he enrolled at Harvard Law School before finishing his undergraduate degree and withdrew after one semester, and that he transferred from Berkeley to Harvard in 1891. C10. Degree: BA 1898, Johns Hopkins (Wikipedia; Alchetron) vs Met, Gallery One: "Neither sibling successfully completed the requirements necessary to graduate." Likely the Met sentence refers to the doctoral (Leo, zoology) and medical (Gertrude) programmes, but as written it contradicts the BA. Flag as a tension, not a settled fact.

C11. Split year: 1913 (Apollo: "Leo left the household in 1913"; Met press release and Gallery Seven: "by/by early 1913 he recognized it was time to leave") vs 1914 (Wikipedia; Liquisearch: April 1914 relocation; Met: "in the spring of 1914 Leo left rue de Fleurus"). Resolution: a 1913-14 process; decision in 1913, departure spring 1914. The #480 leg recorded "Leo departure 1913 vs April 1914" as an open conflict; this study confirms both dates are attested and the process reading reconciles them.

C12. Renoir count: 16 Renoirs taken in the division (Met x3, Liquisearch -- unanimous) vs "Leo sold 24 Renoirs to Dr. Barnes" (artnet, Cone review). He bought Renoirs in 1913-14 before the split, so the 24 likely counts later sales of a larger holding. Flag both numbers; do not smooth over.

C13. The Steins Collect dates: Met opening February 28, 2012 (Met press release; artnet) vs February 21, 2012 (SFMOMA press release; SFMOMA Open Space). Grand Palais closing January 16, 2012 (most sources) vs January 20, 2012 (museumpublicity). SFMOMA run 21 May - 6 September 2011 is consistent across sources.

C15. Nina Auzias suicide (1949): attested only by Prabook and Alchetron (the latter largely mirrors Wikipedia's old text). No newspaper, biography or scholarly source found in any leg. Thin sourcing -- flag, do not state as settled fact.

C16. Japan trip: NO source found in any leg confirms Japan on Leo's 1895-96 world trip with cousin Fred. The world trip itself is confirmed (Met, Gallery One; Wikipedia). Do not assert a Japan trip; flag as unverified.

C17. Meetings never evidenced: Leo never met Cezanne (no source in any leg records it; treat as not met); whether Leo met Renoir in person is unverified (the Dial anecdote implies contact at least by post). Do not assert either meeting.

C18. "Gertrude said he taught her to look": NOT FOUND as a Gertrude quotation in any source in any leg. Do not assert it. What IS evidenced: Gertrude lists Leo among her influences; her own words: "When I arrived in Paris, I began going around with Leo, looking for interesting works of art"; LitHub: early on Gertrude deferred "to her brother's discerning eye and greater knowledge"; Payot: "for many years he was her mentor and counsellor." C19. Apollo's "taking with him all the Matisses" (June 2025) conflicts with the Met's itemized division (a few Matisses stayed with Leo; most went to Gertrude). The Met's account is the reliable one; do not repeat the Apollo line.

C20. The street greeting: "one brief greeting on a Paris street sometime after the war; they never spoke again" -- HOLDS as phrased, date unverified. C21. Young Girl with a Flower Basket date: 1905 (Wikipedia, sale literature) vs "1904" in one Unger/artnet passage. Use 1905.

"Cezanne is the greatest of us all" is Monet's words to Clemenceau; "the father of us all" about Cezanne is Picasso's (and Matisse's) phrase. Do not attribute either to Leo.
