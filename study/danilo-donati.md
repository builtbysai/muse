## M1. Faithful to an idea, not to an era

The core mechanism, and the D1 thesis. Donati "was not restricted by classical scholarship nor by the need to be faithful to an era; on the contrary, he was faithful to an idea", per Clara Tosi Pamphili, and he "uses inventive shapes, sometimes modified from tradition, sometimes entirely fanciful", per Encyclopedia.com, and Paulicelli writes that he "could never be called a philologist of costume adaptation". The evidence is the invented past: Satyricon, where specially created textiles were made so the clothes would resemble nothing contemporary, and Casanova, which starts with the 18th century and then goes wild, and Flash Gordon, where Asian and medieval European and art-deco vocabularies sit in a single robe. The study's contrast is Tosi, who reconstructed 1860 from conversation pieces and genre paintings, and whose Leopard ball put hundreds on screen in the full dress of 1860, as individual in the far background as in foreground closeup, per Hartley. Donati's period is a starting point the film is allowed to leave, and the leaving is the design.

**Takeaway:** pick the idea first and let the era follow it, and the piece should name its idea before it names its century.

## M2. The specially-made textile

Donati commissions cloth that does not exist yet. "Specially created textiles were made for Satyricon so that they would in no way resemble anything contemporary", per Encyclopedia.com, and the 2021 MDPI analysis proves the habit at the fibre level: the Casanova golden lady dress mixes silk, viscose, polyester, polyethylene, plastic inserts and metal-foil lame, with synthetic dyes, Direct Yellow 28, crystal violet, dioxazines, chosen for camera effect, not for period truth. The textile is designed for the shot, not for the century, and the weave is the first design decision, not the last. Against this, the failure mode is the film where the cloth is bought off the rail and the period is read off a label, and the audience reads the label too.

**Takeaway:** make the cloth before making the costume, and the piece should invent at least one textile no shop sells.

## M3. The costume as a visual unity, or a deliberate contrast

"Donati costumes interact with each other; as lovely as they may be individually, they work together to form visual unities (or even contrasts when necessary, as seen in the dinner party of the Spanish and French in Casanova) and themes", per Encyclopedia.com. The mechanism is relational: no costume is designed alone, the scene is dressed as a composition. The evidence is the dinner party in Casanova, Spanish against French at one table, and Romeo and Juliet, where Juliet is understated in red and gold against an overdressed, pompous high society, and Flash Gordon, where Mongo's tribes are colour-coded against each other, red and gold for Ming's court, woven green and brown for the Arborians, leather and gold for the Hawkmen. The single costume is the wrong unit of design, and the scene is the right one.

**Takeaway:** dress the scene, not the actor, and the piece should compose its figures against each other, never alone.

## M4. The reflective surface

"Donati often uses light reflective surfaces such as sequins or materials such as feathers or furs that create eye-catching movement on the screen. His approach to costume decoration rivals the great Hollywood costume designers of the 1930s", per Encyclopedia.com. The MDPI paper found the mechanism in hardware: aluminium foil sandwiched between polyester layers, copper and zinc and silver and copper foils varnished with acrylic resin, gold and silver lame on synthetic supports, in the Casanova golden lady dress. In Flash Gordon the same device is millions of bugle beads forming the starburst on Ming's chest emblem and the trim on his triangular-cuffed robe sleeves. The shine is functional: it makes the costume move when the actor barely does, and the camera reads the movement before it reads the cut.

**Takeaway:** put the movement in the material, and the piece should shine in a way that survives a still frame.

## M5. Over-dyeing to the shade

Donati re-dyes finished cloth until the colour is the one in his head. "Danilo Donati often performed further dyeing processes on already-colored costumes and fabrics to obtain the desired shade", per the collector Giuseppe Bruno Bossio, interviewed in the MDPI paper. The analysis confirms it: the Roma chasuble was first dyed Direct Yellow 28, then over-dyed with Pigment Yellow 1 and Disperse Yellow 3 in consecutive baths. The colour is built in layers, like a painting, and the dye bath is a brush. Compare Canonero's patina, see #430 M4: dye and boil to break the newness down, four operations in order, and the same Italian school, the same rule that colour is a process, not a swatch. A swatch is chosen once and looks the same everywhere; a layered dye leaves a history, and the history is different where the light hits.

**Takeaway:** dye in successive passes, each pass visible, and the piece should let the colour carry its own making.

## M6. The metamaterial costume

The golden lady dress in Casanova is "a metamaterial artwork composed of natural (silk), artificial (viscose), and synthetic (polyester and polyethylene) fibers, plastic inserts, and lame made of metal foils (aluminum or alloys, brass or gold and silver) applied on synthetic supports. The different materials were used in mixture or composites for manufacturing each detail of the dress", per the MDPI paper, 2021, conclusions. Cheap and precious, natural and synthetic, are not alternatives in Donati; they are ingredients in one object. The same mixed construction is implied by the Roma bishop costume, viscose underneath and Bemberg on the show surface, see M7. The costume is a composite the way a modern building is a composite, and the period is only the shape it is poured into.

**Takeaway:** mix the materials in one object, and the piece should never be all one fibre, all one century, all one price.

## M7. Cheap underneath, precious on top

The hierarchy of fabrics follows the hierarchy of the shot. "The tunic is made of viscose, a cheap fabric for the underneath part of the costume, while the chasuble is composed of Bemberg, a more precious fabric, which looks like silk (hence the common name 'Bemberg silk') and is, thus, ideal for a golden surface enriched with decorations and jewelries made of metallized plastic", per the MDPI paper, 2021. The camera never sees the cheap layer, and the audience never suspects it. The economy is a design decision, not a budget accident: the money goes where the light goes, and the under-layer is allowed to be honest about what it is. The failure mode is the film where the cheap layer shows, and the audience reads the budget instead of the bishop.

**Takeaway:** spend the precious fabric where the camera looks, and the piece should hide its economy in plain sight.

## M8. The pictorial citation

Donati dresses from paintings, not from pattern books. The Gospel cites Piero della Francesca's Arezzo frescoes, and filmreference lists Botticelli, Rouault, Masaccio and Piero della Francesca behind the same film. Satyricon's costumes "were inspired by Fortuny gowns, among other things", per Frock Flicks. Casanova's dinner party stages a parody of Durer's rhinoceros behind a pile of pasta. Medea's Corinth is red "inspired by Pontormo's painting", but that is Tosi's citation, not Donati's, and the contrast is the point: Tosi cites a painting to reconstruct it, and Donati cites one to depart from it. The painting gives the colour and the carriage, and the departure gives the film. Canonero's version is the same rule with the refusal made explicit, see #430 M2, and the two heirs share the method and split on what the painting is for.

**Takeaway:** cite a painting, then leave it, and the piece should show where the reference ends and the invention starts.

## M9. Rags with the same care as robes

"Donati pays as great an attention to details in the rags of the poor and the mundane attire of the middle class, as he would for robes of a Renaissance princess. Often Donati will contrast the ultraglamorous or unusual with the more humble in the same movie", per Encyclopedia.com. The evidence is the Decameron, where Giotto's pupil and his companion are given tattered outfits by a toothless farmer and arrive at the church dressed in them, and Amarcord's small-town poor against the Fascist uniforms, and Life Is Beautiful, where the pink gown of the early scenes gives way to the grim camp uniforms, and the Gospel, where the peasant apostles in wool and cotton stand against the Pharisees' outrageous hats. The rag is not the absence of design, it is design spent where the audience is not looking, and the contrast is what makes the robe read as a robe.

**Takeaway:** design the poorest figure as carefully as the richest, and the piece should let the humble half carry the glamorous half.

## M10. The hat as character

Donati thinks from the head outward, the same lesson Kubrick gave Canonero, but Donati's version is sculptural. The Pharisees' "outrageous hats" in the Gospel, per Encyclopedia.com; Tybalt's "ridiculous red hat" in Romeo and Juliet, per Tuscan Trends, which "entirely engulfs the head of its dummy"; the "particularly ornate women's hats" the fascists wear in drag in Salo; the wooden hat blocks from the Laboratorio Pieroni displayed as silent presences in Embodying Pasolini; Ming's black plastic skullcap and the domed helmets of his soldiers in Flash Gordon. The hat is the fastest way to change a body's meaning, and the cheapest: one object, one silhouette, and the century changes with it.

**Takeaway:** change the hat and the character changes, and the piece should design the head first and let the body follow it.

## M11. Colour as character code

Donati assigns a colour to a fate and then never breaks the rule. Juliet is red and gold, natural cycles, fertility, womanhood, against an overdressed court, and Romeo is lilac-and-silver silk, the passive recipient of her desire, per Tuscan Trends. Dale Arden wears white the night before her wedding and black on the day, in Flash Gordon. Ming is red and gold, Klytus is black, the Arborians are woven green and brown, the Hawkmen are leather and gold. The code is legible at a glance, which is why it survives bad transfers and small screens. Canonero's version is the closed system, see #430 M6, thirteen colours for a whole film, and Donati's version is the same discipline worn looser: the colour tells you who the character is, which tribe they belong to, and what is about to happen to them, and a colour that appears outside its system is a plot event.

**Takeaway:** give each fate a colour and never lend it out, and the piece should be readable with the sound off.

## M12. The paper costume: glue and imagination

"This extraordinary artist was capable of generating costumes out of a little glue and a lot of imagination thanks to the skill and flair of Piero Farani", per Clara Tosi Pamphili, via Grey Magazine. The Canzonissima paper outfits are the proof that Donati's luxury never depended on luxury materials: the design is in the cut and the idea, and the material is whatever is at hand. The same economy is visible in M5 and M7, the over-dye and the cheap under-layer, and the lesson is one mechanism in three costumes: the material is never the point, the idea is, and the idea survives the cheapest material because the idea is the design. The failure mode is the film where the budget is the design, and the audience can price every garment.

**Takeaway:** design as if the material were paper, and the piece should prove its idea in the cheapest cloth it can find.

## M13. The costume-sculpture and the draping

The Mocenigo exhibition text: the Theatre Biennale chose to feature "the 'costume-sculptures', the creative fabrics, the period tailoring and the mysterious and so precisely developed draping which Danilo Donati used to translate into 'dressed matter' the impenetrable matter of the mythical, visionary, symbolic or damned characters". The drape is not decoration; it is the translation of an untranslatable character into cloth. The Saillard line from Embodying Pasolini sharpens it: "the base is the shoulders and the moulding is the flesh". The evidence is the Decameron's "costume-sculptures" with their precisely developed draping, and the loom weaving created for Oedipus Rex, where the costume is made on a loom, not bought as cloth, and the weave is the design, and the cartridge-belt shapes of the soldiers' costumes in Pigsty, where ammunition belts are worn as costume architecture. The character is mythical, visionary, symbolic or damned, and the cloth must say which, because the dialogue cannot.

**Takeaway:** let the drape do the talking, and the piece should translate its character into cloth before it gives it a line.

## M14. The crowd as one organism

Donati dresses the mass, not the extra. The Venetian carnival opening of Casanova, hundreds of masked revellers dragging the bust of Venus from the water; the ecclesiastical fashion show in Roma, priests to pope in a nine-minute catwalk; the palace crowds of Satyricon; the tribes of Mongo in Flash Gordon. Each crowd is a single visual field with internal contrast, see M3, not a background of identical bodies. The crowd is designed the way a single costume is designed, with a palette, a hierarchy, and a focal point, and the individual extra is a stitch in it, not a body in it. Canonero's version is the crowd as a costume, see #430 M20, and the two heirs agree: the mass is a design problem, and a crowd left undressed is a crowd left unthought.

**Takeaway:** design the crowd as one garment, and the piece should read as a single field before it reads as many bodies.

## M15. The fashion show as satire

Twice Donati builds a runway inside a film and lets the clothes accuse their wearers: the ecclesiastical fashion show in Roma, priests, nuns, bishops, archbishops, cardinals and finally the pope parading in ever more outlandish vestments, to Nino Rota's "sinister jigs", nine minutes of catwalk inside a fifteen-minute sequence, and the clown fashion show in The Clowns, "eye-popping fashion shows for clowns in The Clowns and for the well-dressed pope in Roma", per Encyclopedia.com. The mechanism is the same in both: take a real social ritual, the catwalk, the liturgy, dress it literally, and let the literalness become the joke. The vestments are not exaggerated, they are accurate, and the accuracy is the satire, because the institution already dresses like a fashion show and only needed a runway to admit it.

**Takeaway:** put a real ritual on a runway and let it convict itself, and the piece should satirize by dressing the truth, not by inventing a lie.

## M16. Opulence and realism in one film

"Donati's oeuvre conjures up monuments to opulence in the minds of many; even lesser films, such as Caligula and the entertaining Dino De Laurentiis production of Flash Gordon were memorable due to their outlandish Donati designs. Nevertheless, Donati equally excels in depicting realism", per Encyclopedia.com. The two registers are not two careers; they are two stops on the same dial, and the dial is turned inside a single film. The evidence is Romeo and Juliet, the overdressed court against the understated Juliet, and the Decameron, the Renaissance princess against the peasant in rags, and Casanova, the golden lady against the hollow man, and Life Is Beautiful, the pink gown against the camp, at its most extreme. The glamour needs the humility to read as glamour, and the humility needs the glamour to read as humility, and a film with only one stop is a film with one note.

**Takeaway:** turn the dial inside one film, and the piece should hold its richest and its poorest figure in the same frame.

## M17. The shoulder as the base

"The base is the shoulders and the moulding is the flesh", per Olivier Saillard, quoted in the Embodying Pasolini text. Donati builds the costume from the shoulder line down: the shoulder treatment Frock Flicks admires in Vanina Vanini, "I like the shoulder treatment here"; Ming's pointed shoulder pads and massive crested collars in Flash Gordon; Klytus's triangular shoulder pieces; Dale's spiked shoulder pads for the marriage itself; the slashed sleeves of Romeo. Change the shoulder and the whole body changes its century. The shoulder is the joint where the costume meets the body, and where the century meets the actor, and Donati designs the joint first because the joint decides everything below it.

**Takeaway:** build from the shoulder down, and the piece should let one shoulder line carry the whole century.

## M18. The bottega chain: artist, artisan, director

Donati's work was made in a Renaissance-style workshop, not a studio system: "The materialization of ideas took place in words, in drawings, and in a costumier that seemed more like a Renaissance bottega, where they experimented out of instinct and not out of duty", per Clara Tosi Pamphili, via Grey Magazine. "Each garment was a work of art, each costume a translation of the director's vision as realized first by an artist and then by an artisan." The chain is Donati drawing, to Farani or Tirelli or Cerratelli making, to the director's vision. The study names the chain because it is the reason the costumes survive as objects: they were made by named hands in named shops, and the paper outfits of Canzonissima exist because Farani's flair met Donati's glue, see M12. The workshop is the medium, and the film is only one of its products.

**Takeaway:** name the hands that make the work, and the piece should be built by a chain, not by a single hand.

## M19. Anachronism as method

Donati starts from the period and then deliberately leaves it. Casanova starts "with 18th century and then goes wild", per Frock Flicks. Satyricon is a "bizarre interpretation of history", per Encyclopedia.com. Flash Gordon fuses Asian, medieval European and art-deco vocabularies in a single robe. The anachronism is not a mistake; it is the signal that the film is an invention, not a reconstruction, and the M1 mechanism applied to the timeline. Canonero's version is the deliberate anachronism, see #430 M9, the Converse in the Marie Antoinette montage, and the licence is the same, but Donati's anachronism is the whole film, not one frame: the entire Casanova is an 18th century that never was, and the film says so in every costume. The failure mode is the anachronism that is not deliberate, which is an error, and the test is whether the break says what the period cannot.

**Takeaway:** leave the period on purpose and say so in the cloth, and the piece should announce its invention, not smuggle it in.

## M20. The costume outlives the film

Donati's costumes were built to last one shoot and then refused to die: re-used for carnival parties and modified, per the MDPI paper, on the Casanova and Roma costumes; bought by the collector Giuseppe Bruno Bossio; acquired by the Cineteca Comunale di Rimini; restored by the Opificio delle Pietre Dure; exhibited at Pienza, the Romeo and Juliet costumes for the 50th anniversary in 2019, and Florence, the Brother Sun costumes in 2024, and Venice, the Mocenigo Pasolini-Donati exhibition, and Villa Manin, the Trame di Cinema show, and Mantta, the Glamour exhibition of Casanova costumes; worn again by Tilda Swinton in Embodying Pasolini in 2021. The afterlife is part of the work, not an accident: a costume that can be re-worn, re-dyed and re-exhibited was designed as an object, not a prop. Canonero's version is the archive that eats itself, see #430 M8, the reuse as restyling, and Donati's version is the same habit worn harder: the costume's second life is also design, and the third life too.

**Takeaway:** design the costume as an object that will outlive its film, and the piece should be worth re-wearing, re-dyeing and re-exhibiting.

## Evolution

P1. Apprenticeship and first films, 1955 to 1963. Stage designs for Visconti in Rome, The Crucible in 1955, Contessa Giulia and Impresario delle Smirne in 1957, and supervising art director at RAI, and first feature credit The Great War with Piero Gherardi in 1959, then Rossellini, Pietrangeli and Lattuada, Adua and Her Friends in 1960, Vanina Vanini in 1961, The Steppe in 1962. The mechanism set is already visible: painting training under Ottone Rosai, theatre discipline under Visconti, and the Ro.Go.Pa.G. anthology in 1963 that opens the Pasolini door. The film career is dated from 1959 with the Gherardi qualification, see C3, and the stage career from 1955.

P2. The Pasolini years and the first Oscar, 1964 to 1969. The Gospel in 1964 establishes the Pasolini grammar: natural fibres, fresco citations, non-actors, black and white, and the Pharisees' outrageous hats. The Zeffirelli Shakespeare films establish the other pole: The Taming of the Shrew in 1967, richly coloured gowns shared with Irene Sharaff, and Romeo and Juliet in 1968, rich colour, Renaissance splendour, the Oscar. Satyricon in 1969 is the hinge, the first Fellini film and the first full unleashing of invention, with specially made textiles. The phase ends with the two 1967 Oscar nominations lost to Irene Sharaff and the 1969 win, and the method is now fully formed: faithful to an idea, specially made textile, rags with the same care as robes.

P3. The Fellini decade and the second Oscar, 1970 to 1976. The Clowns in 1970, Roma in 1972, his first production-design credit, Amarcord in 1973, Casanova in 1976, the second Oscar. In parallel, the Pasolini Trilogy of Life, The Decameron in 1971, The Canterbury Tales in 1972, Arabian Nights in 1974, and Salo in 1975, and the last Zeffirelli feature, Brother Sun, Sister Moon in 1972. This is the peak: Donati is now designing sets and costumes together, and the costume is a total environment, not a garment, and the ecclesiastical fashion show in Roma and the carnival opening of Casanova are the set-pieces where the whole system is visible at once, crowd, colour code, reflective surface and satire in a single shot.

P4. The international decade, 1977 to 1986. Fewer films, bigger budgets, English-language productions: Hurricane in 1979, Caligula, shot 1976 and released 1979, the one film where he took his name off, Flash Gordon in 1980, Red Sonja in 1985, Ginger and Fred in 1986, Momo in 1986, Intervista in 1987. The mechanism does not change, colour-coding, reflective surfaces, invented textiles, but the ateliers change: in England Donati works without Tirelli, Farani or Costumi d'Arte, and had the Flash Gordon costumes fabricated at The Theatre of The Primitive Future, a British costumier. Frock Flicks: "After all that sexytimes in the '70s, Danilo Donati only did a handful of movies in the '80s, most notably Flash Gordon (1980)." The $1 million costume figure and the complete creative freedom claim for Flash Gordon are unverified, see C14, and the study does not use them.

P5. The late return to Italy, 1987 to 2001. Francesco in 1989, The Monster in 1994, Nostromo in 1997, We Free Kings in 1996, Marianna Ucria in 1997, Life Is Beautiful in 1997, the late masterpiece, which won the David di Donatello for costumes and production design in 1998, and Pinocchio in 2002, unfinished at his death on Saturday 1 December 2001. The late mechanism is restraint: the same hand, now dressing a waiter, a bookshop and a concentration camp, and proving M9 and M16 one last time, that the rags get the same care as the robes, and that opulence and realism are two stops on the same dial. The arc of the whole career: from the Visconti stage apprentice who learned painting from Rosai, to the inventor who dressed two of cinema's great visionaries and won two Oscars for it, to the elder who dressed a clown's funeral and a camp and a wooden boy, and died at the workbench.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is a sentence: the costume is an invention before it is a reconstruction, and an object before it is a garment, and the idea is the end and the period is the means. Everything in the system serves the idea first. The idea gives the licence, M1, and the textile gives the invention its body, M2, and the scene gives the composition, M3, and the shine gives the movement, M4, and the dye gives the shade, M5, and the mixture gives the object, M6, and the cheap layer gives the economy, M7, and the painting gives the citation, M8, and the rag gives the contrast, M9, and the hat gives the character, M10, and the colour gives the code, M11, and the paper gives the proof, M12, and the drape gives the translation, M13, and the crowd gives the field, M14, and the runway gives the satire, M15, and the dial gives the range, M16, and the shoulder gives the base, M17, and the bottega gives the hands, M18, and the anachronism gives the signal, M19, and the afterlife gives the proof, M20. The failure mode is the film where the idea was never named and the period is all there is, or where the cloth was bought and not made, or where the crowd was left undressed, or where the anachronism was an error and not a method, or where the costume was a prop and died with the shoot. The success mode is the film where every layer is present at once, and Casanova is the proof: the 18th century departed from on purpose, the golden lady dress analysed thread by thread and found to be silk and viscose and polyester and polyethylene and plastic and foil, the dinner party of Spanish against French, the carnival opening with hundreds dragging Venus from the water, the mechanical doll dancing twice, the pink suit and the black suit with the gold waistcoat, and the Oscar won at the 49th ceremony on Monday 28 March 1977, accepted by Tamara Dobson because Donati was not there. The clothes are not what the character wears, they are what the character is invented from, and the audience watches the invention happen, and the costume is the film's first fact and its last surviving object.

## Network

N1. PIERO TOSI, 1927 to 2019. The parallel. Same age, same Porta Romana school, Donati painting under Ottone Rosai, Tosi set design under Gianni Vagnetti, same Visconti apprenticeship via Zeffirelli, opposite methods: imaginative versus philological, per the Zeffirelli Foundation. Tosi did the make-up on Donati's Satyricon and the costumes on Pasolini's Medea, the two films where their territories touch. Tosi's five Oscar nominations, The Leopard, Death in Venice, Ludwig, La Cage aux Folles, La Traviata, against Donati's five nominations and two wins. Studied as #428.

N2. FRANCO ZEFFIRELLI. The introducer and the first great collaborator. Knew Donati and Tosi in Florence, moved to Rome first, introduced both to Visconti, per the Zeffirelli Foundation. Three features with Donati, The Taming of the Shrew in 1967, Romeo and Juliet in 1968, Brother Sun, Sister Moon in 1972, plus two operas and three plays, 1957 to 1972. The Foundation's 2024 exhibition of the Brother Sun costumes is the main posthumous Donati show. Studied as #425, per the #430 carried list.

N3. PIER PAOLO PASOLINI. The long partnership: 1962 or 1963 to 1975, nine films, Ro.Go.Pa.G., The Gospel According to St. Matthew, The Hawks and the Sparrows, Oedipus Rex, Pigsty, The Decameron, The Canterbury Tales, Arabian Nights, Salo. "An extreme and refined experimentation, carried out in Donati's partnership with Pasolini, beginning in 1962 and continued until the director's death, through the use of materials such as wool, cotton, natural fibres", per the Embodying Pasolini text. The one Pasolini film Donati did NOT dress is Medea, Tosi's, see C0, and Teorema appears in no Donati filmography read this leg.

N4. FEDERICO FELLINI, 1920 to 1993. The second long partnership: seven films, Satyricon in 1969 to Intervista in 1987. Fellini "longed to work with" Tosi but "could not abide Fellini's chaos", per Hartley, and Donati could, and became Fellini's set and costume designer. Fellini designed many of the Roma vestments himself and planned them closely with Donati. The Casanova Oscar is Donati's second. Tosi Pamphili's catalogue text speaks of Donati and Farani's "creative antipathy for Federico Fellini", an ambiguous phrase against an eighteen-year collaboration, see C12.

N5. LUCHINO VISCONTI. The master both men apprenticed under. Donati's first stage designs were for Visconti's Rome productions, The Crucible in 1955, Contessa Giulia in 1957, Impresario delle Smirne in 1957. Tosi worked for Visconti on twelve films; Donati never made a Visconti feature, and the apprenticeship is the whole of the Visconti-Donati relationship. Studied as #427.

N6. OTTONE ROSAI, 1895 to 1957. Donati's painting teacher at the Porta Romana, per the Zeffirelli Foundation. Florentine painter, Futurist in youth, then a solemn figurative style of Tuscan country folk and landscapes, and chair in painting at the Florence Academy of Fine Arts from 1942. The painter's eye is the root of Donati's method: he designed from painting, not from pattern books, see M8. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED: no study header in the log, no local file.

N7. GIANNI VAGNETTI. Tosi's set-design teacher at the Porta Romana, per the Zeffirelli Foundation. The other half of the school: Donati took painting, Tosi took set design, and the two trainings explain the two careers.

N8. UMBERTO TIRELLI. Founder of Sartoria Tirelli, the Roman costume house that built Donati's pieces, and Frock Flicks says Donati "had the edge of working with Tirelli Costumes, which built the pieces". Tirelli collected antique garments to study and established the company to replicate them, per Hartley, on Tosi. The Roma vestments were made by Farani and Tirelli together. Already stands as a carried candidate from #429 and #430; NOT re-proposed as new.

N9. PIERO FARANI. Owner of Sartoria Teatrale Farani, the other great Roman atelier and Donati's closest artisan partner. "Donati's relationship with Piero Farani started in the post-war period", per Grey Magazine, and "His relationship with Piero Farani is proof of the benefit derived from the synergy of conception and realization, design and production, artist and artisan." The Farani shop preserved the Pasolini costumes used in Embodying Pasolini and curated the Mocenigo exhibition. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED: no study header in the log, no local file.

N10. CASA D'ARTE CERRATELLI, Fondazione Cerratelli, Pisa. The third atelier. Made the Brother Sun, Sister Moon costumes, more than seventy exhibited in 2024, and loaned the Romeo and Juliet costumes for the Pienza 50th-anniversary exhibition in 2019. The three houses, Tirelli, Farani, Cerratelli, are Donati's factory floor.

N11. IRENE SHARAFF. The American rival. Won the 1967 Black-and-White Costume Oscar for Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf, beating Donati's Gospel nomination, per Grey Magazine. Elizabeth Taylor wanted Sharaff for The Taming of the Shrew; Zeffirelli insisted on Donati, and the work was split, see W2 and C19. Tosi Pamphili suggests Donati's low opinion of the Oscars dates from the Sharaff loss, see C11. Sharaff was the Hollywood standard Donati was measured against.

N12. GABRIELLA PESCUCCI. Tosi's assistant on Medea, per the Fondazione CSC film record, which lists her as assistant costume designer with Piero Cicoletti, and one of the generation Tosi "influenced and trained", per Hartley. The Medea credit chain runs Tosi to Pescucci, not Donati, and the rival credit made flesh. Studied as #429.

N13. DANTE FERRETTI. Art director on the Pasolini films where Donati did costumes: The Decameron and The Canterbury Tales, per Criterion, and set designer on Medea, per the Fondazione CSC. The Donati-Ferretti pairing is the Pasolini visual system: Ferretti builds the world, Donati dresses it. Already stands as a carried candidate from #429 and #430; NOT re-proposed as new.

N14. GIUSEPPE ROTUNNO. Cinematographer on Satyricon, his first feature with Fellini, nine collaborations in total, and on Casanova, per Criterion. The camera that had to photograph Donati's reflective surfaces and invented textiles. Already stands as a carried candidate from #428 and #430; NOT re-proposed as new.

N15. NINO ROTA. Composer for Romeo and Juliet, Satyricon and Casanova, and for the Roma fashion show, the "sinister jigs". The third member of the Fellini-Donati-Rota visual system. Already stands as a candidate from #417 and #427; NOT re-proposed as new.

N16. RENZO MONGIARDINO. Production designer on Romeo and Juliet, per Criterion. The architect of Zeffirelli's Verona against Donati's costumes. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED: no study header in the log, no local file.

N17. ALBERTO GRIMALDI. Producer of Satyricon, The Decameron and The Canterbury Tales, per Criterion and Wikipedia. The producer who bankrolled both the Fellini and Pasolini Donati films. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED: no study header in the log, no local file.

N18. DINO DE LAURENTIIS. Producer of Flash Gordon and Caligula, per Encyclopedia.com, "the entertaining Dino De Laurentiis production of Flash Gordon". The bridge to the English-language decade.

N19. ROBERTO BENIGNI. The late collaborator: The Monster in 1994, Life Is Beautiful in 1997, Pinocchio in 2002. Donati died during the Pinocchio production; the film is dedicated to him. Benigni said the Pinocchio project began with Fellini.

N20. CLARA TOSI PAMPHILI. Curator and fashion historian: author of the Trame di Cinema catalogue, Villa Manin, 2014, curator of Embodying Pasolini in 2021 with Olivier Saillard, co-author of the Rizzoli book Embodying Pasolini in 2022 with Swinton and Saillard. The main living interpreter of Donati's work, and the source of the "faithful to an idea" line and the Oscars anecdote. Her surname joins Tosi and Pamphili, and no source read this leg states a family relation to Piero Tosi, so do not assert one. VERIFIED NOT STUDIED: no study header in the log, no local file.

Also noted, not proposed as new: Piero Gherardi, Donati's co-designer on The Great War and the Fellini designer, La Dolce Vita, 8 1/2, whose Oscar candidacies in the 1960s preceded Donati's; Tonino Delli Colli, cinematographer on the Gospel, Decameron, Canterbury Tales and Life Is Beautiful; Ennio Morricone, music collaborator on the Decameron and Canterbury Tales, per Criterion; Sergio Citti, Pasolini's assistant director and brother, later director of We Free Kings with Donati as designer, VERIFIED NOT STUDIED; Liliana Cavani, director of Francesco, VERIFIED NOT STUDIED; Mike Hodges, director of Flash Gordon; Olivier Saillard, fashion historian and co-creator of Embodying Pasolini, VERIFIED NOT STUDIED; Tilda Swinton, who wore Donati's Pasolini costumes in Embodying Pasolini, VERIFIED NOT STUDIED; Giuseppe Bruno Bossio, the Roman collector who bought the Casanova and Roma costumes after shooting and preserved them; Massimo and Sonia Cirulli, whose archive holds Donati's drawings for Casanova and Amarcord; Laboratorio Pieroni, makers of the wooden hat blocks used in Embodying Pasolini; Luigi Scaccianoce, art director on the Gospel, Oscar-nominated with Donati; Ennio Guarnieri, cinematographer on Medea; Maria Callas, Medea herself, dressed by Tosi in metal plaques; Donald Sutherland, Casanova, shaved head, false nose and chin; Olivia Hussey and Leonard Whiting, the teenage Romeo and Juliet; Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton, the Taming of the Shrew principals.

C6. OSCAR DATING CONVENTION: film year versus ceremony year. The two wins are dated 1968 and 1976 by film year, per Encyclopedia.com, filmreference, the Wikipedia lead and Frock Flicks, and 1969 and 1977 by ceremony year, per the Wikipedia awards table note, "Indicates the year of ceremony", and A Shaded View, "respectively in 1969 and 1977", and Live Design, "won Donati a second Oscar for Best Costume Design, in 1977". C7. MDPI PAPER TYPO: "Roma (1927)".
