# Sergio Corbucci: Study Notes

**Site:** Sergio Corbucci (b. Monday 6 December 1926, Rome, Italy; d. Saturday 1 December 1990, Rome, Italy, age 63), Italian film director, screenwriter and producer, sometimes called "the other Sergio" after Sergio Leone -- the older brother of Bruno Corbucci, the man who dragged a coffin full of machine gun through the mud in *Django* (1966), froze a western solid in *The Great Silence* (1968), taught peasants to buy their revolutions in *The Mercenary* (1968) and *Companeros* (1970), and then spent sixteen years making domestic comedies for an audience that had stopped going to westerns at all. He directed more than sixty films in forty years, from tearjerkers in 1951 to a television film broadcast a month after his death, and he is remembered for the violent pole of the Italian western -- the one Leone's shadow never quite covered, and the one Tarantino spent a whole book trying to understand. [C30361]

## Corrections to the brief

Three corrections to the #759 pick's wording, verified this run and carried into the study. (1) The *Rimini Rimini* / *Roba da ricchi* writing credits are one-of-many, not two-handed: Bruno is one of eight writers on *Rimini Rimini* (Mario Amendola, Bruno Corbucci, Sergio Corbucci, Massimo Franciosa, Maurizio Micheli, Marco Risi, Gianni Romoli, Bernardino Zapponi) and one of six on *Roba da ricchi* (Amendola, Bruno, Sergio, Franciosa, Romoli, Zapponi). The brothers wrote together again late, but inside a writing room, not as a pair. (2) The *Two Marshals* / *Gli onorevoli* writing credits rest on Bruno's Italian-Wikipedia screenwriter filmography alone: the two films' own pages contain no mention of Bruno anywhere in their wikitext, and the English *Two Marshals* page says only "written and directed by Sergio Corbucci". (3) *I'm for the Hippopotamus* (1979), often shelved between Sergio's Hill and Spencer films in duo filmographies, was directed by Italo Zingarelli, not Sergio Corbucci -- it appears in the Hill/Spencer navbox on the *Odds and Evens* page but is absent from the Sergio Corbucci directed-films navbox, and its own page credits Zingarelli. Do not attribute it to Sergio. [C30306] [C30365]

## Biography

Sergio Corbucci was born in Rome on 6 December 1926, five years before his younger brother Bruno (b. 23 October 1931, Rome; d. 7 September 1996). English Wikipedia: "He had a younger brother Bruno (1931-1996), also a filmmaker"; Italian Wikipedia opens its biography "Fratello maggiore di Bruno Corbucci" (older brother of Bruno Corbucci). He studied economics at university before working as a film critic. The two sources differ on where he criticised: Senses of Cinema says he "began working as a film critic for the journal Schermi del Mondo" and "after the Second World War, he contributed to the U.S. army magazine Stars and Stripes", while English Wikipedia says only "For a period after World War II, he wrote for Stars and Stripes". Both can be true in sequence; the study names both outlets and does not pick one. [C30227]

He entered cinema through writing. Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography lists him as screenwriter on *Gli ultimi giorni di Pompei* (1959, directed by Mario Bonnard and Sergio Leone), and Senses of Cinema says the idea for a western in Spain came to him "while doing second unit work for Leone on Gli ultimi giorni di Pompei (The Last Days of Pompeii, 1959)" -- so his western apprenticeship was literally on Leone's set, five years before either man directed a western. Bruno's Italian-Wikipedia screenwriter filmography shows the brothers writing together for Sergio's early films from the start: *Chi si ferma e perduto* (1960), *Toto, Peppino e... la dolce vita* (1961), *I due marescialli* (1961), *Il giorno piu corto* (1962 in that listing), *Il figlio di Spartacus* (1962), *Lo smemorato di Collegno* (1962), *Il monaco di Monza* (1963), *Gli onorevoli* (1963), *I figli del leopardo* (1965). The screenwriter years and the directing years overlap: he was still writing for others (including Margheriti's *Danza macabra*, 1964, which he also uncreditedly co-directed) while directing his own films. [C30228]

His directorial debut was *Salvate mia figlia* in 1951 (English Wikipedia: "Corbucci made his directorial debut in 1951 with Salvate mia figlia"). Italian Wikipedia: he directs his first film in 1951, tries "lungometraggi strappalacrime e canzonettistici" (tearjerker and song films) at first, and in 1960 "stringe un sodalizio artistico con Toto" (forms an artistic partnership with Toto), the famous works of the pair being *I due marescialli*, *Lo smemorato di Collegno* and *Il monaco di Monza*. English Wikipedia: his early works "were mainly melodramas and crime films", and "beginning with 1961's Goliath and the Vampires, he directed sword and sandal movies", plus "several popular comedies, featuring the likes of Toto and Franco and Ciccio". He was married twice; his nephew is filmmaker Leonardo Corbucci (English Wikipedia, Personal life). His infobox lists pseudonyms Stanley Corbett, Gordon Wilson Jr. and Enzo Corbucci -- note: Bruno also used "Gordon Wilson Jr." as a pseudonym (per #759), so the brothers shared at least one working alias. [C30229]

Year wobbles found this run (all in the early filmography; use the form given with each work entry below): *Il giorno piu corto* is 1963 in English Wikipedia's Sergio page and in its own Italian page, but 1962 in Bruno's Italian filmography; *Il figlio di Spartacus* is 1963 in English Wikipedia's Sergio filmography, but 1962 in its own Italian page, in Bruno's filmography, and in Senses of Cinema (use 1962); *Minnesota Clay* is 1964 in English Wikipedia and in its own film page (shot September-October 1964), but 1965 in Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography (use 1964). [C30230]

## Works

**W01 -- *Salvate mia figlia* (Save My Daughter), 1951.** Directorial debut. English Wikipedia's film page is a stub (cast only: Bianca Doria, Lamberto Maggiorani, Franca Marzi, Ermanno Randi) with no plot summary; Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography lists it as his first directed film, 1951. No plot or production detail located this run beyond title, year and debut status. Grade B. [C30231]

**W02 -- *La peccatrice dell'isola* (The Sinner of the Island), 1952.** Melodrama. Italian Wikipedia: a 1952 film directed by Sergio Corbucci, ascribable to the "melodrammi sentimentali, comunemente detto strappalacrime" (sentimental melodramas, commonly called tearjerkers), very popular with Italian audiences in the post-war years 1945-1955, later renamed by critics "neorealismo d'appendice" (appendix neorealism). No plot summary exists on the page. The mechanical fact is the genre label itself: his first decade is the tearjerker, not the western. Grade B. [C30232]

**W03 -- *Suonno d'ammore* (Dream of Love), 1955.** Musical melodrama. Italian Wikipedia: a 1955 film directed by Sergio Corbucci, also known as *Sogno d'amore*; Alberto loves Maria, daughter of Arturo, a rich boat owner, the love reciprocated and approved by her father, but someone is determined to prevent the marriage by any means. A musical film, again in the strappalacrime line; released in Italian cinemas 24 June 1955. The device is the blocked bourgeois marriage -- the obstacle, not the lovers, drives the plot. Grade A. [C30233]

**W04 -- *Suprema confessione* (Supreme Confession), 1956.** Melodrama. Italian Wikipedia: the wife of an orchestra conductor with a stormy past agrees to meet a blackmailer in a hotel; she refuses him, he dies fleeing the police, her husband loses trust in her, and a child's illness reunites the couple. A West German co-production, and "il primo film a colori realizzato dal regista, con la tecnica del Ferraniacolor" (the director's first colour film, in Ferraniacolor). The mechanism is the blackmailer's accidental death -- the crisis resolves itself, and the marriage is repaired by a sick child, not by anyone's choice. Grade A. [C30234]

**W05 -- *I ragazzi dei Parioli* (The Boys of Parioli), 1959.** Youth melodrama. Italian Wikipedia plot, read end to end: two country girls, Grazia and Nuccia, dream of film stardom and go to a Parioli flat for an audition; the boys Bob and Fabrizio stage a fake director and a fake film to amuse themselves; the girls get drunk, Nuccia arms herself with a statuette, Bob slaps her, she falls unconscious and he believes he has killed her; she has only fainted and is revived with vinegar; the girls leave disillusioned while Giuseppe, who waited outside all evening for Grazia, takes the last tram with them. The engine is the fake audition -- cinema itself as the con. Grade A. [C30235]

**W06 -- *Chi si ferma e perduto* (Who Hesitates Is Lost), 1960.** Office farce with Toto and Peppino De Filippo. Italian Wikipedia, read end to end: two Naples transport-company clerks, Guardalavecchia and Colabona, compete for their dead boss's post; the mechanism is a double mistaken identity -- two men named M. Rossi arrive on the same train, the transport inspector Matteo Rossi and a school inspector Mario Rossi, and Guardalavecchia stages a fake mugging to impress the wrong one, then courts the company president's sister in a parody of the Romeo and Juliet balcony scene (in a 17th-century cloak, in the garden, promising "e cosi alfin potrai sapere com'e buono il formaggio con le pere!"). The title comes from a Mussolini speech in Genoa, 14 May 1938; the film carries a sponsor, the Maico hearing-aid firm (an acoustic device built into a pair of glasses, used as a plot prop); Corbucci cameos as the man playing billiards; shot entirely in Rome (the "Formia" station is Roma Ostiense). Bruno is listed as a screenwriter on it in his Italian filmography. Grade A. [C30236]

**W07 -- *Romolo e Remo* (Duel of the Titans), 1961.** Peplum. Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography lists it 1961; Senses of Cinema: Steve Reeves as Romulus, Gordon Scott as Remus, the twins set afloat by their mother Rea Silvia, raised by a wolf until the shepherd Faustulus kills the wolf and adopts them, then a rebel war against the tyrant Amulias, Romulus killing Remus. "Co-written with Sergio Leone and another spaghetti master, Duccio Tessari, the film makes breathtaking use of widescreen with no wasted shots, along with a riveting choreography to the brawling scenes." The brothers-killing-brothers plot, told in widescreen brawl choreography, is the peplum seed of the later westerns' fratricidal violence. Note: the Italian Wikipedia title "Romolo e Remo" returns the mythology article, not the film; the film facts here come from Senses of Cinema and the Sergio filmography. Grade A. [C30237]

**W08 -- *Goliath and the Vampires* (Maciste contro il vampiro), 1961.** Peplum. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: directed by Sergio Corbucci and Giacomo Gentilomo; Gordon Scott as Maciste, Gianna Maria Canale as Astra, a vampire raiding villages for slaves and women. Production: "Both Giacomo Gentilomo and Sergio Corbucci are credited as directors of the film. Barry Atkinson stated Corbucci's input to the film was minimal." Released in Italy 21 August 1961; in the US in April 1964, the hero's name redubbed to Goliath because American International Pictures felt "Maciste" meant nothing to American audiences. Monthly Film Bulletin called it "a mixture of handsomely decorated spectacle and the supernatural" and attributed "the film's intermittent drive and suspense... more safely... to Corbucci than to his co-director". The mechanical fact is the renaming: the hero's name is a marketing variable, the muscles are the constant. Grade A. [C30238]

**W09 -- *I due marescialli* (The Two Marshals), 1961.** Toto comedy, the peak of the Toto partnership. English Wikipedia film page: "a 1961 Italian comedy film written and directed by Sergio Corbucci", a hit with 2,765,531 spectators and 536,513,000 lire. Plot: in 1943, thief Antonio Capurro disguises himself as a priest and marshal Vittorio Cotone (Vittorio De Sica) chases him; a bombing swaps their clothes, so the thief becomes the marshal serving the Nazis and the marshal becomes the priest hiding a Jewish girl, a partisan and an American soldier. Italian Wikipedia production notes, read end to end: shot mostly in Castel San Pietro Romano, about 40 km from Rome; censorship forced a total story change (the original was a rivalry between two marshals); Corbucci shot it in the afternoons while finishing *Romolo e Remo* in the mornings, and De Sica did the same split with *Boccaccio '70*; it was shot on a special film stock needing little light "in modo da evitare affaticamenti alla gia precaria vista di Toto" (to avoid straining Toto's already precarious eyesight), in 2.35:1 CinemaScope; released in Italy 21 December 1961. The device is the uniform swap: put the thief in the uniform and the uniform redeems the thief -- the same identity-swap engine as *Chi si ferma e perduto*, now with the war as the stake. Grade A. [C30239]

**W10 -- *Il giorno piu corto* (The Shortest Day), 1963.** War parody. English Wikipedia: "In 1963, he directed the ensemble war comedy The Shortest Day, which was produced as a benefit film for the studio Titanus. A parody of the Hollywood epic The Longest Day, the film featured an all-star cast of dozens of well-known performers, many of them in brief cameo appearances." Italian Wikipedia, read end to end: a parody of *Il giorno piu lungo* (1962), but set in the First World War; a Sicilian pedlar and his hapless nephew enlist by mistake and accidentally fire a 420 mm Austrian cannon, are court-martialled (their lawyer demands they be shot, to consecrate them as heroes) and acquitted. Production: 88 actors were hired (44+44, against 42 in *The Longest Day*), all working for free to save Titanus from bankruptcy; Toto's part came about because he was filming *Il monaco di Monza* in a nearby studio, already dressed as a friar -- they put a feathered hat on him and turned him into a friar bersagliere; exteriors at Manziana; 40th among the top 100 Italian grossers of the 1962-63 season. Year flag: Bruno's filmography lists it as 1962; use 1963. Grade A. [C30240]

**W11 -- *Il monaco di Monza* (The Monk of Monza), 1963.** Toto costume farce. Italian Wikipedia, read end to end: a joking reworking of Manzoni's *I promessi sposi* and the Monaca di Monza; Pasquale Cicciacalda (Toto), a widowed cobbler from Casoria who cannot feed his twelve children (six pairs of twins), disguises himself and the children as monks and wanders as a begging friar; they reach the castle of the perfidious Marquis Egidio, who holds his sister-in-law Fiorenza prisoner to force her to marry him, while she loves the Spanish captain Don Manuel (Pasquale's double) and is pregnant by him; the sisters of the convent, all seduced by the Marquis in the past, intervene to save them. Shot at the Castello di Torcrescenza in Rome (the Marquis's castle) and the Casal de' Pazzi (the convent). The device is the disguise as a religious order -- the cassock as a begging licence -- and the double: Toto plays both the cobbler and the captain. Grade A. [C30241]

**W12 -- *Gli onorevoli* (The Honourables), 1963.** Anthology election comedy. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: a 1963 Italian anthology comedy directed by Sergio Corbucci, five small stories about politicians' votes in 1960s Italy. Italian Wikipedia: the stories are a DC feminist (Franca Valeri) who has her posters put up by prostitutes and falls for the man paid to photograph her; a PLI senator whose rally is sabotaged by children; a communist writer who denounces the US while cashing in on an American lecture tour; an MSI candidate (Peppino De Filippo) grotesquely made up for TV, his speech suspended after seconds; and Toto's episode, the most important: Antonio La Trippa, a retired bersagliere, runs for parliament on list number 47 -- in the tombola (bingo) game, 47 is the dead man talking -- trumpeting his programme from his window every morning, and on election day commits electoral suicide, denouncing his corrupt fellow candidates to the voters and insulting everyone, then angrily tears down his own posters. The mechanism is the anthology as a parliament: five parties, five frauds, one honest man who wins by losing. Grade A. [C30242]

**W13 -- *Il figlio di Spartacus* (The Son of Spartacus / The Slave), 1962.** Peplum. Italian Wikipedia film page, read end to end: a 1962 film directed by Sergio Corbucci, with Steve Reeves and Gianna Maria Canale, an unofficial sequel to *Spartacus* (1960) that mentions Varinia, a character invented for the novel the Kubrick film is based on; the centurion Rando discovers he is Spartacus's son and leads the slave revolt against Crassus, his father's killer. Senses of Cinema: Randus is assigned by Caesar to spy on Crassus; thrown overboard saving the slave girl Saida, he is told by his amulet that he is Spartacus's son; in the ruins of a sand-coloured city he finds a Corinthian helmet, shoulder guard and sword matching his amulet -- his father's grave -- and the helmet that hid the faces of evil men in *Goliath* here masks the face of a hero. Year: use 1962 (Italian film page, Bruno's filmography, Senses of Cinema) against 1963 (English Wikipedia's Sergio filmography). Grade A. [C30243]

**W14 -- *Massacro al Grande Canyon* (Grand Canyon Massacre), 1964.** First western. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: a 1964 Italian spaghetti western filmed in Croatia, starring James Mitchum, Milla Sannoner and George Ardisson, directed by Sergio Corbucci and produced by Albert Band; theme song performed by Rod Dana; "The film has nothing to do with the Grand Canyon." Plot: Wes Evans returns home after killing the Slade brothers who murdered his father the sheriff, and is drawn into a range war between the Dancers and Whitmore, ending as sheriff with Nancy at his side. English Wikipedia's Sergio page: his first westerns were this film, "which he co-directed (under the pseudonym Stanley Corbett) with Albert Band", and *Minnesota Clay*. Senses of Cinema: released in May 1964, four months before Leone's *A Fistful of Dollars*, "perhaps... the first modern spaghetti western, although it's hardly Corbucci's finest work"; the white Croatian rock "doesn't look so American" but is ruggedly appealing, and Corbucci's uncertainty with the genre "was perhaps the reason he released it under the pseudonym of Stanley Corbett". The device is the reluctant sheriff: the avenger who only wants to settle down is forced to take the badge. Grade A. [C30244]

**W15 -- *Minnesota Clay*, 1964.** First solo-directed spaghetti western. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: Cameron Mitchell as Clay, a gunfighter with health problems interned in a labour camp, framed by Fox, the sheriff of Mesa Encantada, who now runs a protection racket; Clay escapes, meets the bandit Ortiz, and in the final showdown, having lost his sight, kills Fox's gang one by one while Fox tries to lure him into shooting his own daughter Nancy; in the European epilogue Clay shoots a single bullet through his thrown glasses; in the English version he dies. Production: listed as shot in Spain, but film historian Tim Lucas found very little was actually shot there -- the bulk was shot at the Elios Film studios near Rome, September-October 1964; Cameron Mitchell recalled Mario Bava was on set to assist Corbucci, and Lucas suggests Bava assisted with the cinematography and the final shootout; Mitchell dubbed his own performance and also provided the voices of Georges Riviere and Antonio Casas. Release: one of the first spaghetti westerns released in Rome after *A Fistful of Dollars*; earned 400 million lire against *Fistful*'s 3.182 billion. The device is the disabled gunman: blindness as the price of the revenge, and the gunfight fought by hearing. Year flag: Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography says 1965; use 1964 (film page, shooting dates, English Wikipedia). Grade A. [C30245]

**W16 -- *Django*, 1966.** The breakthrough. English Wikipedia's Sergio page: "His biggest commercial success was with the cult spaghetti Western Django, starring Franco Nero, the leading man in many of his movies." The *Django* film page (read end to end in #759, Bruno-credit passages re-checked this run): Django trudges through mud dragging a coffin that holds a machine gun; the name is "a sick joke on the part of Corbucci and his screenwriter-brother Bruno" (Alex Cox), referencing jazz guitarist Django Reinhardt, who played with two paralysed fingers -- and Django's own hands are crushed with a rifle butt before the final showdown; Major Jackson's men wear red hoods and are modelled on the Ku Klux Klan; the violence (whipping, an ear sliced off, mass shootouts in the mud) got it banned in England and several other countries. Senses of Cinema: the mud is "a suitable pathetic fallacy" for Django's depressive state and "the slippery and amorphous morality" of the characters; the brothel is "a Switzerland of neutrality" between Jackson's men and the revolutionaries; the machine-gun massacre lays waste to "the expressed or unexpressed racism inherent in many early westerns". The device is the coffin: a gun you have to drag behind you, in the open, through the mud. Grade A. [C30246]

**W17 -- *Navajo Joe*, 1966.** The star vehicle that misfired. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: directed by Sergio Corbucci, starring Burt Reynolds as the Navajo who opposes the bandits who killed his tribe. Production: producer Dino De Laurentiis approached Corbucci with a script titled *Un dollaro a testa* (A Dollar a Head); Corbucci claimed Marlon Brando was promised for the lead; De Laurentiis cast Reynolds because he resembled Brando; Reynolds was a friend of Clint Eastwood, who introduced him to De Laurentiis; Reynolds "was surprised that the cast was predominantly non-English-speaking and that the director was not Sergio Leone as he had thought, but Sergio Corbucci", and later said the film failed because he had worked with "the wrong Sergio". The device is the price list: Joe demands "a dollar a head from every man in this town for every bandit I kill", the town refuses because they "don't make bargains with Indians", and the whole film is the town learning the price was fair. Reynolds: "so awful it was only shown in prisons and airplanes because nobody could leave"; Corbucci: "I made European Westerns the way they like them over here. Plenty of action. Little talk." Released in Italy November 1966, in the US 1967. Grade A. [C30247]

**W18 -- *Johnny Oro* (Ringo and His Golden Pistol), 1966.** The small western made in Django's shadow. Italian Wikipedia, read end to end: Johnny Oro is a bounty killer so rich he can afford a pistol made entirely of gold; he spares the youngest Perez brother, Juanito, because there is no bounty on him -- Juanito is the most dangerous precisely because he leaves no witnesses, so no bounty exists; fake mariachi hide pistols in guitar cases in a town where carrying arms is banned; Johnny's canteen holds nitroglycerin, which he throws at them; the hidden gold is inside the barrel of the cannon-monument in the town square, and Johnny blows up nearly the whole town with a dynamite-laden pulley above it, then gives the treasure to the sheriff as compensation for the damage. Production: shot in 1965, released only from 15 July 1966, after Django's enormous success -- "che in realta fu realizzato successivamente" (which in reality was made afterwards); Marco Giusti does not rate it among Corbucci's best westerns; Mark Damon's first western; Damon is dressed as Paladin from the TV serial *Have Gun - Will Travel*, "di cui Sergio Corbucci era un fan" (of which Corbucci was a fan); abroad it was retitled Ringo to ride on Duccio Tessari's Ringo films. The device is the golden pistol: wealth worn as a weapon. Grade A. [C30248]

**W19 -- *L'uomo che ride* (The Man Who Laughs), 1966.** The literary detour. English Wikipedia film page, read end to end: a 1966 Italian historical drama based on Victor Hugo's 1869 novel; Gwynplaine is renamed Angelo (Jean Sorel), his disfigurement a single broad slash across his mouth, "crude yet convincing"; the story -- attributed in the credits to the director, producer and others, "but not to Victor Hugo" -- makes him a henchman for the Borgias, seduced by a noblewoman, while Dea miraculously regains her sight and falls for a young nobleman; Angelo's assassination attempt fails, he is mortally wounded, and staggers into a leper colony and falls dead. The device is the erased author: Hugo's name is missing from the credits of a film based on Hugo's novel. Grade A. [C30249]

**W20 -- *The Great Silence* (Il grande silenzio), 1968.** The masterpiece. An Italian-French co-production starring Jean-Louis Trintignant, Klaus Kinski, Vonetta McGee in her film debut, and Frank Wolff, with music by Ennio Morricone; released in Italy on 22 November 1968 and in France on 27 January 1969, running 105 minutes. Corbucci conceived the film as a politically charged allegory inspired by the deaths of Che Guevara and Malcolm X; set in Utah just before the Great Blizzard of 1899, it pits a mute gunslinger (Trintignant) defending a group of starving outlaws and a vengeful young widow, Pauline (McGee), against a gang of ruthless bounty killers led by "Loco" (Kinski) and the corrupt banker Henry Pollicut. [C30261] [C30262]

Unlike most spaghetti westerns, which were shot in the Almeria province of Spain, *The Great Silence* was filmed primarily on location in the Italian Dolomites (Cortina d'Ampezzo and San Cassiano in Badia); Snow Hill scenes were shot on a purpose-built set and on the Elios Film town set in Rome previously used for *Django*, where 26 tons of shaving cream stood in for snow, night shooting and fog disguised the snowless Roman countryside, and the final duel was staged. [C30263]

The plot runs on a rule: Silence provokes his enemies into drawing first so he can kill them in self-defence with his semi-automatic Mauser C96; as a boy he was rendered permanently mute when a bounty killer sliced his throat after murdering his parents, and Pollicut puts prices on the heads of townsfolk forced to steal in a blizzard so he can profit from their property once they are killed. [C30264]

The theatrical ending is the famous bleak one: Silence's left hand is shot before the duel, Loco kills him, Pauline dies trying to shoot Loco, and the bounty killers massacre the entire outlaw group before riding out of Snow Hill; a closing title card says Loco's actions brought widespread public condemnation of bounty killing and a memorial was erected to the dead. [C30265]

Corbucci was obliged to shoot an alternate "happy" ending to appease producers who wanted "seasonal" appeal for the intended Christmas release; production histories claimed it was shot for North African and Asian markets, but the article states there is no evidence it was ever seen in either region, though footage from it appeared in the trailers; in it Burnett survives the frozen lake, shoots Loco, Silence kills the remaining killers, and Burnett asks Silence to become his deputy, which he accepts with a smile. [C30266]

Corbucci also delivered a lesser-known "ambiguous" ending, a recut of the intended ending in which Silence is shot in both hands before he can draw, collapses as Pauline watches, and Loco gestures his men to leave, leaving the fates of Silence, Pauline and the outlaws unknown; it was never publicly released until it appeared as a special feature on Film Movement's Blu-ray. [C30267]

The muteness was a casting solution, not a first principle: Franco Nero turned the lead down, Trintignant was hired by co-producer Les Films Corona and did not speak English, so Corbucci made the character mute (the mute-gunfighter idea itself came from Marcello Mastroianni); Kinski's Loco was partly modelled on Boris Karloff's vampire Gorca in Mario Bava's *Black Sabbath*, and Frank Wolff, usually a villain, was cast against type as the semi-comical Sheriff Gideon Burnett. [C30268]

Morricone's score, conducted by Bruno Nicolai, is melancholic and emotive, and Morricone himself viewed it as his best spaghetti western soundtrack apart from his Leone scores; the celeste and choir of Silence's theme ("Restless") mark him as an eternal outsider, cackling trumpets mark Loco, soaring strings appear only in the Pauline-Silence love scene ("Invito all'Amore"), and the rest is dirgelike and funereal. [C30269]

The film did mediocre business in Italy (309 million lire, held back by an 18 rating) but fared better in France and West Germany; 20th Century Fox's Darryl F. Zanuck was so shocked by the ending he refused US distribution, the film reached the US only on DVD in 2001 and theatrically in 2012, and it is now widely regarded as Corbucci's masterpiece and one of the greatest spaghetti westerns -- the second film of his "Mud and Blood" trilogy with *Django* (1966) and *The Specialists* (1969); at one Sicily screening an audience member fired a gun at the screen in anger over the ending. [C30270]

**W21 -- *The Mercenary* (Il mercenario), 1968.** Released in Italy on 20 December 1968, directed by Corbucci from a screenplay by Luciano Vincenzoni, Sergio Spina, Adriano Bolzoni and Corbucci, from a story by Franco Solinas and Giorgio Arlorio, produced by Alberto Grimaldi; it stars Franco Nero as the Polish mercenary Sergei "Polack" Kowalski, Tony Musante as the peon-revolutionary Paco Roman, Jack Palance as the rival Curly, and Giovanna Ralli as Columba, with a score by Morricone and Bruno Nicolai, and it grossed 1.101 billion lire in Italy. [C30271]

The Zapata western is defined on the genre page as the hero-pair variant of a revolutionary Mexican bandit and a mostly money-oriented American; Corbucci's *The Mercenary* and *Companeros* are named as Zapata westerns alongside Damiano Damiani's *A Bullet for the General* and Sergio Sollima's trilogy, and the films are often read as a leftist critique of Hollywood's handling of the Mexican Revolution and of imperialism in general. [C30272]

*The Mercenary*'s mechanism is a paid pedagogy: Paco hires Kowalski to teach him how to lead a revolution, Kowalski's Hotchkiss machine gun wins the battles, Kowalski doubles his fee, Paco imprisons him and confiscates his money, then has to free him when the army attacks -- every lesson has a price, and the pupil eventually jails the teacher. [C30273]

The film is a frame story -- Kowalski reminisces at a circus where Paco is now a rodeo clown -- and it ends with Paco killing Curly in a fair duel, Kowalski taking Paco prisoner to collect his reward, both men sentenced to death by firing squad, Columba's circus rescue, Kowalski handing Paco a share of his own reward money, and Kowalski shooting Garcia's ambush from a hillside as they part, shouting "Good luck, Paco! Keep dreaming, but with your eyes open!" [C30274]

*The Mercenary* was originally going to be directed by Gillo Pontecorvo, from the Solinas and Arlorio story; Corbucci took it over, keeping the political spine and turning it into the template *Companeros* then repeated two years later with the mercenary recast as an arms dealer. [C30278]

**W22 -- *Companeros* (Vamos a matar companeros), 1970.** Released in Italy on 18 December 1970, directed by Corbucci and starring Franco Nero as Yodlaf "Penguin" Peterson, a Swedish arms dealer, Tomas Milian as the peasant El Vasco, Jack Palance as John (a wooden-armed American avenging himself on Peterson), Fernando Rey as Professor Xantos and Iris Berben as Lola, with a Morricone score; it grossed 1,451,782,000 lire in Italy. [C30275]

*Companeros* repeats the pedagogy with the price made literal: Peterson once left John crucified and John's pet hawk had to peck his right hand off to save him; El Vasco and Peterson double-cross the self-enriching General Mongo to free Xantos; and when the safe is finally opened it holds no gold, silver or cash, only the community's "true wealth" -- a few ears of corn -- while Peterson rides away from the final battle and then rides back at the last minute rather than let the Xantistas be slaughtered. [C30276]

Against Leone, Corbucci's bounty killers are drawn far more negatively: the *Great Silence* article says his presentation of them is "far more negative than such figures in Sergio Leone's films", with the closing title card condemning bounty killing set against the opening title card of Leone's *For a Few Dollars More*, and where Leone's pairs are bound by unstable, money-driven alliances, Corbucci's Zapata pairs are bound by a lesson the peasant must buy and then outgrow. [C30277]

**W23 -- *The Specialists* (Gli specialisti), 1969.** Premiered in Italy on 26 November 1969, co-written and directed by Corbucci, starring French rock singer Johnny Hallyday as Hud Dixon; retrospective critics deem it the final film of the "Mud and Blood" trilogy with *Django* and *The Great Silence*, and it began as a Lee Van Cleef vehicle (draft title *Il ritorno del mercenario*, "The return of the mercenary") before Corbucci and Van Cleef fell out and it was retooled for Hallyday, keeping the hero's bulletproof chainmail vest from the drafts. [C30279]

Its mechanism is the town that disarms the hero and then arms itself against him: Hud returns to Blackstone, where no guns are allowed, to avenge his lynched brother; the secretly armed townsfolk try to kill him and fail; he recovers the stolen money and burns it in front of the whole town; and rebellious teenagers seize the town and shoot at the wounded, bulletless Hud, who survives on his vest and bluffs them into flight by raising his empty gun -- Corbucci called it "a film about how the wealthy are oppressors", and paired that leftism with an open anti-hippie stance in the teenagers. [C30280]

**W24 -- *Sonny and Jed* (La banda J. & S. -- Cronaca criminale del Far-West), 1972.** Released 11 August 1972, directed by Corbucci and starring Tomas Milian as the bandit Jed Trigado, Susan George as Sonny, and Telly Savalas as Sheriff Franciscus, with a Morricone score; Jed is a solitary bandit who steals from the rich to give to the poor in the Mexican region of Bandera, while the sheriff relentlessly pursues him and his girlfriend. [C30281]

The film was "almost unanimously labelled as a failure upon its initial release", according to the Spaghetti Western Database quoted on the page: "as a comedy it's often too crude to be funny, and as a western it's a bit short on action", though the same review praises Morricone's "unobtrusive, wistful score" and visuals of "Fellinesque beauty". [C30282]

**W25 -- *The White, the Yellow, and the Black* (Il bianco, il giallo, il nero), 1975.** Released 17 January 1975, directed by Corbucci and starring Giuliano Gemma as the outlaw Blanc de Blanc, Tomas Milian as the inept Japanese servant Sakura, and Eli Wallach as Sheriff Gideon, with music by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis; it is the last spaghetti western Corbucci directed, openly parodic as a spoof of *Red Sun*, and was generally poorly received by critics. [C30283]

Its plot is a retrieval caper -- a horse from Japan, meant as a gift for the United States government, has been stolen, and the motley trio of sheriff, outlaw and servant must recover it from ex-Confederate desperadoes -- and Milian reprised his Sakura role, with slight changes, in the comedy *Crime at the Chinese Restaurant*, the bridge from Sergio's late westerns into the comedy world his brother Bruno was then running. [C30284]

**W26 -- *The Con Artists* (Bluff -- storia di truffe e di imbroglioni), 1976.** Directed by Sergio Corbucci, written by Corbucci, Dino Maiuri and Massimo De Rita, produced by Mario Cecchi Gori, starring Adriano Celentano as Felix, Anthony Quinn as the master con man Philip Bang, Corinne Clery and Capucine; set in southern France in the Roaring Twenties, it won Celentano the David di Donatello for Best Actor. Grade B (search-extract only). [C30290]

Its mechanism is nested cons with a physical punchline: Belle Duke arranges the jailbreak of her ex-lover Philip Bang for revenge, but Felix escapes instead and must break Philip out; the two con men join forces to defraud Belle while each tries to con the other; and as a final point Felix spills the fleeced 100 million francs out of a plane's open door, then reels every bill back in, each tied to a long line. [C30291]

The Celentano run is the spine of Corbucci's comedy decade -- his own page says that in the 1970s and 1980s he "mostly directed comedies, often starring Adriano Celentano": *What's Your Sign?* (1975, Celentano as Alfredo Astarita), *The Con Artists* (1976), *Ecco noi per esempio* (1977, the first film starring the Celentano-Renato Pozzetto duo, mostly set in Milan), and *Sing Sing* (1983, Celentano with Enrico Montesano and Vanessa Redgrave). [C30292]

Not every Corbucci comedy of the period stars Celentano: *Mr. Robinson* (1976) stars Paolo Villaggio (with Zeudi Araya), written by Villaggio, Castellano e Pipolo and Corbucci, as a parody of Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* -- the comic engine here is a star vehicle for Villaggio, not the Celentano persona. [C30293]

**W27 -- *Odds and Evens* (Pari e dispari), 1978.** Released 28 October 1978, directed by Sergio Corbucci and written by Mario Amendola and Bruno Corbucci; it stars Terence Hill as US Marine Johnny Firpo and Bud Spencer as gambler-turned-trucker Charlie Firpo, his unknowing half-brother, with music by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis, set in Florida against a Mafia illegal-betting ring; it won the Golden Screen Award in 1979 and was the second most popular Italian film of the 1978-79 season. [C30285]

Its engine is a lie that recruits the reluctant brother: Johnny invents a blind father who needs an eye operation to get Charlie's help, then teaches him nothing -- Charlie teaches Johnny poker while Johnny rigs a horse race (riding as jockey Sam Stallion on Lightning Rod) and a pelota match (Charlie impersonating a former star); when the ruse is exposed on Paragoulis the Greek's cruise-ship casino, the brothers turn on the Mafia together and beat them up, rolling up the ring. [C30286]

**W28 -- *Super Fuzz* (Poliziotto superpiu, also Super Snooper), 1980.** Co-written and directed by Sergio Corbucci, starring Terence Hill as Miami police officer Dave Speed and Ernest Borgnine as Sergeant Willy Dunlop, with music by La Bionda; shot in Miami and designed for an American audience, it belongs to the phase in which, since the mid-1970s, Corbucci was known as one of Italy's most successful filmmakers in comedy. [C30294]

Its mechanism is a conditional superpower: a secret nuclear missile loaded with red plutonium gives Dave super reflexes, speed, strength, telekinesis, precognition, hypnotism and invulnerability -- but the powers fail whenever he sees the colour red, the colour of the explosion -- so the film's set-pieces are built around keeping red out of frame, and it ends with Dave and Dunlop floating home on a bubblegum balloon while Dave's fiancee Evelyn, reluctant to marry a super-powered husband, has her hair dyed red. [C30295]

**W29 -- *Who Finds a Friend Finds a Treasure* (Chi trova un amico trova un tesoro), 1981.** Released 10 December 1981, directed by Sergio Corbucci from a screenplay by Amendola and Corbucci, starring Hill as the runaway gambler Alan Lloyd and Spencer as the actor Charlie O'Brien, with music by La Bionda; filmed at Key Biscayne, Florida, it strands the pair on a Pacific island where they protect the natives from pirates and steal money from a Japanese holdout general who still believes World War II is not over. [C30287]

Its engine is fake money with a double twist: the general confides to Alan that the stolen US Navy money is fake, Charlie steals it anyway, and when the pair are rescued by the crew of the USS *Forrestal* and hand it over, they are told it is real -- the government spread the fake story to stop the Japanese spending it -- so the heroes end broke, working as museum janitors, and are last seen planning a museum heist. [C30288] [C30311]

The contrast with the other two duo-handlers is structural: Bruno Corbucci directed Hill and Spencer in *Miami Supercops* (released 11 December 1985, Milan, 96 minutes, their final non-Western film together, per the verified #759 study), so Sergio's duo films (1978, 1981) sit just before Bruno's, both Florida-set, but Sergio's are gambling and island capers rather than a cop film; and against Enzo Barboni, whose *They Call Me Trinity* (1970) and *Trinity Is Still My Name* (1971) turned the duo into burlesque-comedy western heroes, Sergio's duo films are contemporary -- no western setting at all -- with the comedy carried by contests and cons, not gunplay. [C30289]

**W30 -- *My Darling, My Dearest* (Bello mio, bellezza mia), 1981, and *Count Tacchia* (Il conte Tacchia), 1982.** Two star-vehicle comedies in consecutive years. *My Darling, My Dearest* is an Adriano Celentano comedy (filmography listing only; no plot verified this run). *Count Tacchia* stars Enrico Montesano as Francesco Puricelli, a man who impersonates the aristocrat Count Tacchia, with Vittorio Gassman, Paolo Panelli and Ninetto Davoli; released 1982. The pattern is set: the 1980s are built on Italy's big comic stars, one per film. Grade B. [C30312]

**W31 -- *Sing Sing*, 1983, and *Questo e quello* (This and That), 1983.** *Sing Sing* stars Adriano Celentano with Desiree Nosbusch; Eduardo accidentally discovers he is adopted and that his real father is dying. Conflict: release day is 15 October 1983 per one listing and 16 December 1983 per another -- use 1983 only. *Questo e quello* is a filmography listing only; no director detail beyond Corbucci and no plot verified this run. Two features in one year, both comedies, both star-led. Grade B. [C30313]

**W32 -- *A tu per tu* (Tit for Tat), 1984, and *I Am an ESP* (Sono un fenomeno paranormale), 1985.** *A tu per tu* (1984) stars Paolo Villaggio as Gino Sciaccaluga and Johnny Dorelli as Emanuele/Enrichetta Sansoni, with Marisa Laurito. *I Am an ESP* (1985) stars Alberto Sordi as Roberto Razzi, a sceptical atheist TV host; written by Bernardino Zapponi, Giovanni Romoli, Sergio Corbucci and Sordi himself. The comic-star rotation continues -- Villaggio, Dorelli, Sordi -- and the Sordi film is the last of the run: no theatrical feature followed in 1986. [C30314]

**W33 -- *Rimini Rimini*, 1987.** Anthology comedy in five segments, all set in Rimini; released 3 March 1987. Directed by Sergio Corbucci; written by Mario Amendola, Bruno Corbucci, Sergio Corbucci, Massimo Franciosa, Maurizio Micheli, Marco Risi, Gianni Romoli and Bernardino Zapponi -- eight writers, the brothers writing together again. Cast includes Paolo Villaggio as moralistic magistrate Gildo, blackmailed with photos of him with sex goddess Lola (Serena Grandi), Jerry Cala, Andrea Roncato, Paolo Bonacelli, Laura Antonelli. Bruno directed the sequel, *Rimini Rimini - Un anno dopo* (1988). [C30315]

**W34 -- *Roba da ricchi* (Stuff for Rich People), 1987.** Anthology comedy in three segments, all set in Monte Carlo; released 1987. Directed by Sergio Corbucci; written by Mario Amendola, Bruno Corbucci, Sergio Corbucci, Massimo Franciosa, Gianni Romoli and Bernardino Zapponi. Cast: Lino Banfi, Laura Antonelli, Renato Pozzetto, Francesca Dellera, Serena Grandi, Paolo Villaggio. Two anthology comedies in one year, both co-written with Bruno -- the late-career pattern is the brothers as a writing pair with Sergio directing. [C30316]

**W35 -- *Days of Inspector Ambrosio* (I giorni del commissario Ambrosio), 1988.** Italian crime film directed by Sergio Corbucci; released 21 October 1988. Starring Ugo Tognazzi as Inspector Ambrosio, with Carlo Delle Piane, Carla Gravina, Claudio Amendola and Pupella Maggio. The only non-comedy of the late run after 1981 and the only late film built on a dramatic star (Tognazzi) rather than a comic one -- a return to the crime register of *The Payoff* (1978), thirteen years into the comedy decade. [C30317]

**W36 -- *Night Club*, 1989.** Italian comedy-drama co-written and directed by Sergio Corbucci; screenplay by Corbucci and Massimo Franciosa; released 1989. Starring Christian De Sica, Mara Venier, Massimo Wertmuller, Sergio Vastano, Sabina Guzzanti, Sabrina Ferilli and Claudia Gerini. Plot: set on the night singer Fred Buscaglione died in a car accident, two bank clerks try to convince a rich Calabrian businessman to finance their enterprise by giving him an unforgettable night of alcohol and women in a Via Veneto night club. A period elegy disguised as a farce -- the only late film anchored to a real death and a real date. [C30318]

**W37 -- *Women in Arms* (Donne armate), 1991.** Italian television action-drama directed by Sergio Corbucci; his final work. Starring Lina Sastri as Nadia Cossa, a political prisoner who escapes during a transfer to a new prison, with Cristina Marsillach and Donald Pleasence as Dreyfuss. Co-produced by Fulvio Lucisano, Rai 2 and Odeon TV -- the first co-production between RAI and a private television channel -- on a budget of about 6 billion lire. Verified posthumous: broadcast on Rai 2 in two parts on 9-10 January 1991, about a month after Corbucci's death on 1 December 1990. [C30319]

The brothers co-wrote across the whole arc: Bruno Corbucci is credited on the *Great Silence* screenplay (with Vittoriano Petrilli, Mario Amendola and Sergio) and on *Odds and Evens* (written by Mario Amendola and Bruno Corbucci), so the same sibling partnership spans Sergio's bleakest western and his biggest duo comedy. [C30302]

The composer handover marks the turn: Morricone scores the western peak -- *The Great Silence*, *The Mercenary* (with Bruno Nicolai), *Companeros*, *Sonny and Jed* -- and then the comedy era switches to Guido and Maurizio De Angelis (*The White, the Yellow, and the Black*; *Odds and Evens*) and La Bionda (*Super Fuzz*; *Who Finds a Friend Finds a Treasure*). [C30303]

The box-office arc runs the other way from the reputation arc: the political westerns were big Italian hits (*The Mercenary* 1.101 billion lire, *Companeros* 1.451 billion), while the bleak masterpiece was not -- *The Great Silence* took 309 million lire in Italy and *The Specialists* 309.9 million, barely more, despite the latter becoming Corbucci's most successful western in France (1,252,173 admissions). [C30304]

Tomas Milian is the human bridge between Sergio's westerns and Bruno's comedies: in Sergio's films he is El Vasco in *Companeros* (1970), Jed in *Sonny and Jed* (1972) and Sakura in *The White, the Yellow, and the Black* (1975), and the *White/Yellow/Black* article notes he reprised Sakura in *Crime at the Chinese Restaurant*, a Nico Giraldi film -- Giraldi being the role Milian played in all eleven Bruno-directed films of 1976-84, per the verified #759 study. [C30301]

Corbucci dedicated *The Great Silence* to the memories of Che Guevara, Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy, telling the German magazine *Film* that their assassinations, all happening while he was making the film, served to further their causes; his widow Nori told producer Katsumi Ishikuma that he had the deaths of Guevara and Malcolm X in mind when he conceived it. [C30307]

In Japan in 1995 *The Great Silence* received a theatrical re-release alongside *Sonny and Jed* through Cable Hogue and PSC as part of a "Spaghetti Western Revival" event -- the bleak masterpiece paired with the film first labelled a failure. [C30308]

Early titles listed in Italian Wikipedia's Sergio filmography but not inspected beyond title, year and director: *Terra straniera* (1952), *Carovana di canzoni* (1954), *Baracca e burattini* (1954), *Acque amare* (1954), *Il ragazzo dal cuore di fango* (1957, co-directed with Carlos Arevalo; Italian page has no plot), *I figli del leopardo* (1965), *Danza macabra* (1964, co-directed with Antonio Margheriti, uncredited; Sergio also has a screenwriter credit on it), *Bersaglio mobile* (1967), *I crudeli* (1967). No claims are made about them beyond this listing. [C30251]

Not researched beyond director credit, flagged for a later study: *What Am I Doing in the Middle of a Revolution?* (1972) sits in Sergio's filmography between *Sonny and Jed* and *The Beast* (1974) and was not covered here; *The Beast* (1974), *What's Your Sign?* (1975) beyond its Celentano credit, *Three Tigers Against Three Tigers* (1977), *The Payoff* (1978), *Neapolitan Mystery* (1979) and *Count Tacchia* (1982, just past this leg's end date) were checked only for director credit in the filmography navboxes, not read. [C30309]

## Evolution: early vs late

The early work, 1951-1966, is a genre education taken in public: tearjerker melodrama (1951-1956), youth and office farce with Toto (1960-1963), peplum (1961-1962), then the western (1964-1966). Three continuities cross the seam. First, the disguise/identity swap: the priest's cassock in *I due marescialli*, the friar's habit in *Il monaco di Monza*, the fake inspector in *Chi si ferma e perduto* -- the same trick returns as the coffin in *Django* (a gun disguised as a burial) and the golden pistol in *Johnny Oro* (wealth disguised as a weapon). Second, the disabled hero: *Minnesota Clay*'s blind gunfighter is the dry run for Django's crushed hands and *The Great Silence*'s mute hero. Third, the ensemble that does the work for free: 88 unpaid actors saving Titanus in *Il giorno piu corto* is the same production logic as the peplum and western factories -- speed, reuse, and a cast that costs less than the set. What changes at the seam is the moral temperature: the early comedies resolve by forgiveness and return (the thief redeemed by the uniform, the couple reunited by a sick child), while *Django* resolves by massacre in the mud. [C30250]

The late career, 1981-1991, is the second seam, and it runs the other way. 1981-85: the comedy decade, star by star -- Hill/Spencer (1981), Celentano (1981, 1983), Montesano/Gassman (1982), Villaggio/Dorelli (1984), Sordi (1985). No western after *The White, the Yellow, and the Black* (1975): the last sixteen years of the career contain no western at all, and the international scope of the 1960s westerns is gone -- these are domestic Italian comedies for the domestic market. [C30320]

1986 is the only blank year in the 1980s filmography: *I Am an ESP* (1985) is followed by *Rimini Rimini* (1987), and the 1980s total 12 directed features per an aggregator count. IMDbPro also lists a 1985 TV mini-series credit, *Cinecitta*, one episode -- role not verified this run, do not assert more. The gap reads as a pause, not a retirement: he returns with two features in 1987. [C30321]

1987-91: the brothers close ranks and the medium shrinks. *Django Strikes Again* (1987), the only official sequel to *Django*, was co-written by Sergio Corbucci, who had initially agreed to direct it and then refused to take part after Duccio Tessari's *Tex* failed commercially; Nello Rossati directed it (as Ted Archer), Franco Nero reprised Django, and it was released in West Germany on 6 November 1987. Then the two anthologies (1987), one crime film (1988), one elegy (1989), and a final television film released after his death (1991). What changed: genre (western to star comedy to TV drama), scale (international to domestic), medium (cinema to television). What stayed: the anthology instinct (already there in *Di che segno sei?*, 1975), the Bruno partnership, the Roman comic-star system, and the political undertow -- even *Night Club* is built on a real death. [C30322]

The arc, whole: a tearjerker apprentice becomes a peplum journeyman becomes the violent pole of the spaghetti western, and then, when the western dies, becomes a domestic comedy professional who never stops working -- forty years, no gap longer than a year except the last one, and the last film is a television drama about a woman escaping prison, broadcast after he is dead. [C30366]

## Network

The network is a small room with the same people in it for thirty years. **Franco Nero** is the face of Corbucci: Django (1966, title role, his breakthrough), The Mercenary (1968, Kowalski), Companeros (1970, Peterson), Django Strikes Again (1987, reprised Django), Django & Django (2021, on-screen witness), and the Django Unchained cameo (2012, Amerigo Vessepi) -- over 200 leading and supporting roles across six decades, the actor is the through-line from Corbucci's westerns to Tarantino's. [C30341] **Tomas Milian** is the other Corbucci leading man: El Vasco in Companeros (1970), Sonny in Sonny and Jed (1972), and a role in The White, the Yellow, and the Black (1975); also the bridge to Bruno -- Nico Giraldi in all 11 films (1976-84), all directed by Bruno and scripted by Amendola. In Companeros he plays opposite Nero: the two Corbucci stars in one film. ## M1 -- The coffin: a gun you have to drag

The disguise engine from the early comedies hardens into a single object in *Django*: a coffin that holds a machine gun, dragged through the mud in the open, in front of everyone. The priest's cassock in *I due marescialli* hides a thief; the friar's habit in *Il monaco di Monza* hides a beggar; the coffin hides a gun -- and the difference is that the coffin cannot be taken off. Django carries his disguise on a rope behind him for the whole film, and the film's first image is the drag itself: a man walking through mud pulling his own burial. The golden pistol in *Johnny Oro* is the same trick turned inside out -- wealth worn as a weapon, a gun you cannot fire without spending your fortune. The mechanism is a weapon that costs the bearer something visible every time he uses it: the coffin costs him speed and secrecy, the golden pistol costs him money, the Mauser in *The Great Silence* costs him his hands. [C30246] [C30248] [C30250]

**Takeaway:** hide the weapon in something the hero has to carry in public -- the drag is the character, and the moment he sets it down is the moment the film ends.

## M2 -- Landscape as moral condition: mud, then snow

The *Great Silence* article states the snowbound Utah setting "complements the dark and pessimistic tone", makes survival more dire, isolates the story by giving "very little visible geographical detail", and creates "fair metaphors for the enclosed, cruel world herein"; Corbucci had already considered snowed-in valleys for *Django* but shot it in mud for time and budget reasons, so the snow is the mud idea finished -- watchable when characters wade, freeze and die in a white expanse that swallows geography. [C30296]

The mud in *Django* is the same device in its cheap form: Senses of Cinema calls it "a suitable pathetic fallacy" for Django's depressive state and "the slippery and amorphous morality" of the characters. The landscape is never a backdrop in Corbucci; it is the condition that makes the morality legible. Mud means nobody can move fast and nobody can hide. Snow means nobody can move at all and nobody can be found. The Dolomites shoot -- 26 tons of shaving cream on a Roman backlot, night shooting and fog to disguise the snowless countryside -- is the production fact that proves the point: the snow had to be manufactured because the moral condition it expressed was not available on location. [C30263] [C30246]

**Takeaway:** pick the weather that makes your moral claim physical -- mud for a morality that slips, snow for a world that has already decided -- and if the location will not give it to you, build it out of shaving cream.

## M3 -- The disabled hero

Silence is completely mute because bounty killers cut his throat as a boy, which makes him unlike Django or Leone's Joe, who are merely "strong and silent" -- the article stresses the muteness gives him "a sense of vulnerability and sensitivity"; his advantage is technological (the Mauser C96's rapid fire) and procedural (provoke the first draw), and like Django and Joe his hands are injured before the climax -- but unlike them, neither will nor weaponry saves him, and Loco takes the Mauser from Pauline's hands as a "symbolic castration". [C30297]

The device runs the whole career backwards: Clay in *Minnesota Clay* loses his sight and kills Fox's gang one by one by hearing, in the European epilogue shooting a single bullet through his thrown glasses; Django's hands are crushed with a rifle butt before the final showdown; Silence is mute from the first frame and shot in both hands before he can draw. Each time the disability is the price of the revenge, and each time the film asks whether the price was worth it -- Clay's answer is yes, Django's is a massacre, Silence's is no. The casting accident is part of the mechanism: Nero turned the lead down, Trintignant did not speak English, so Corbucci made the character mute -- a limitation of the production became the moral centre of the film. [C30245] [C30246] [C30268]

**Takeaway:** wound the hero where the genre expects strength -- hands, eyes, voice -- and make the wound the price of the revenge, not a decoration on it; a casting problem solved by subtraction is a better character than one invented by addition.

## M4 -- The mercenary-peasant pedagogy

In *The Mercenary* Paco hires Kowalski to teach him revolution, in *Companeros* El Vasco learns from the arms dealer Peterson, and the lesson is always priced -- Kowalski doubles his fee, Peterson's safe holds corn instead of gold -- until the pupil imprisons or outgrows the teacher and the teacher returns at the last minute (Peterson riding back into the final battle; Kowalski shooting Garcia's ambush from the hillside). [C30298]

The structure is a transaction with a moral remainder. Paco pays for lessons and then jails the teacher and confiscates his money; El Vasco double-crosses the self-enriching General Mongo and finds the community's "true wealth" is a few ears of corn. The teacher is never the hero and never the villain: Kowalski ends the film handing Paco a share of his own reward money and shouting "Keep dreaming, but with your eyes open!" -- the lesson is the dream, priced, and the price is what makes it stick. Against Leone, whose pairs are bound by unstable, money-driven alliances, Corbucci's Zapata pairs are bound by a lesson the peasant must buy and then outgrow. [C30273] [C30274] [C30276] [C30277]

**Takeaway:** make the lesson a purchase -- fee, doubling fee, confiscation, corn instead of gold -- and let the pupil outgrow the teacher in public, because a lesson nobody paid for is a lecture, and a lecture is not a scene.

## M5 -- The bleak ending as signature

*The Great Silence* kills its hero, its heroine and the outlaws it spent two hours defending, and the film's history is the history of people trying to undo that ending -- producers forcing a happy ending and an ambiguous ending (both judged "emotionally dissatisfying" by critic Simon Abrams), Zanuck refusing US distribution over it, a Sicilian viewer firing a gun at the screen -- while Alex Cox's verdict is that by facing an unbeatable foe and dying, Silence "becomes the noblest hero of any Western film since *Shane*". [C30299]

The ending is the film's argument, not its accident. The producers' happy ending -- Burnett survives the frozen lake, shoots Loco, Silence kills the remaining killers, Burnett asks Silence to become his deputy, which he accepts with a smile -- is a different film with the same footage, and the article's verdict is that there is no evidence it was ever shown anywhere. The ambiguous ending, unreleased until a Blu-ray special feature, leaves Silence shot in both hands as Pauline watches and Loco gestures his men to leave. Three endings exist because the one that shipped was unbearable to the people who paid for it, and the one that shipped is the one that made the film a masterpiece. [C30266] [C30267]

**Takeaway:** an ending nobody can bear is a signature, not a mistake -- if the producers demand a happy one, shoot it, let it vanish, and keep the one that cost you the American release.

## M6 -- The comedy engine is a rule with a visible failure mode

*Odds and Evens* runs on a lie (the fake blind father) that must not be exposed; *Who Finds a Friend* runs on money that may or may not be fake; *Bluff* runs on nested cons where each man is conning the other; *Super Fuzz* runs on a superpower that switches off at the sight of red -- in each case the audience can watch for the moment the rule breaks, and the set-piece is built around that break. [C30300]

The rule is always stated early and plainly, and the comedy is the maintenance of it. Dave Speed's powers fail whenever he sees red, so the set-pieces are built around keeping red out of frame; the fake blind father must not be exposed, so the film is a series of near-exposures; the money may be fake, so every scene is a test of whether anyone will spend it. The failure mode is visible from the start, which is what makes the comedy watchable rather than merely funny: the audience is not waiting for a joke, it is waiting for the red dress, the wrong question, the bill that turns out to be real. [C30286] [C30288] [C30291] [C30295]

**Takeaway:** give the comedy one rule with one visible off-switch, state it early, and build every set-piece around the moment the switch flips -- the audience should be watching for the break, not waiting for the gag.

## M7 -- The star-vehicle rotation and the domestic turn

The last sixteen years contain no western at all, and the mechanism that replaces it is the star, one per film: Hill and Spencer (1981), Celentano (1981, 1983), Montesano and Gassman (1982), Villaggio and Dorelli (1984), Sordi (1985). The comic-star system is the same factory logic as the peplum and the western -- speed, reuse, a cast that costs less than the set -- with the star as the set. The anthology instinct survives the turn: *Gli onorevoli* (1963) is five stories about politicians' votes, *Rimini Rimini* (1987) is five segments set in Rimini, *Roba da ricchi* (1987) is three segments set in Monte Carlo -- the same parliament of frauds, now in beach resorts. [C30320] [C30242] [C30315] [C30316]

What changes is the audience. The westerns were international -- shot in Spain and Croatia and the Dolomites, scored by Morricone, sold everywhere. The comedies are domestic: Italian stars, Italian jokes, Italian box office, "little seen in English-speaking territories". The composer handover marks the turn as cleanly as the genre does: Morricone scores the western peak, and then the comedy era switches to Guido and Maurizio De Angelis and La Bionda. The director did not change his method; the market changed what his method was for. [C30224] [C30303]

**Takeaway:** when the genre dies, keep the factory and change the star -- one famous face per film, the anthology as the container, and let the composer tell the audience which decade they are in.

## M8 -- The afterlife: the book that became a film

Tarantino was writing a book on Sergio Corbucci when he came up with *Django Unchained* -- English Wikipedia's *Django Unchained* development section and ScreenRant agree on this, so the Corbucci book is the documented seed of the film, not a later homage bolted on. [C30323]

*Django Unchained* (2012) is a revisionist tribute whose title refers particularly to Corbucci's 1966 *Django*; the title alludes to *Django*, to *Hercules Unchained* (the American title of the 1959 *Ercole e la regina di Lidia*), and to *Angel Unchained* (1970). Jamie Foxx plays Django Freeman -- the name Django is taken, the character is new: a freed slave turned bounty hunter rescuing his wife, not Corbucci's drifter avenging a murdered lover. [C30324]

Franco Nero, the original Django, cameos as Amerigo Vessepi, a slave owner who loses a Mandingo fight, goes to the bar, meets Foxx's Django, asks his name and how it is spelt, and is told "the D is silent" -- a direct callback to the spelling exchange in the 1966 film. Nero had suggested playing a mysterious horseman haunting Django's visions, revealed at the end as Django's father; Tarantino vetoed the idea. [C30325]

*Django Unchained* uses the Rocky Roberts-Luis Bacalov title song from the 1966 film along with several score pieces, and Dr. King Schultz is heard whistling Bacalov's theme while rigging dynamite before the proto-Klan raid. The end-credits theme, RZA's "Ode to Django (The D Is Silent)", samples dialogue from Django's English dub. [C30326]

ScreenRant reports Tarantino "recycled a bunch of tracks from Ennio Morricone's *Mercenary* score" in *Django Unchained*, and that the blood-from-the-flower shot in Calvin Candie's suit nods to a similar shot of Jack Palance's villain in *The Mercenary* -- no second source found this run, so treat the cue-recycling as reported, not established. What is established: Morricone publicly criticised Tarantino's use of his music in *Django Unchained* and said he would "never work" with him -- then agreed to score *The Hateful Eight*. [C30327]

Tarantino calls *The Great Silence* his favourite "snow Western" and has paid homage to it in both *Django Unchained* (the snowy-plains section is a homage to it, per its Wikipedia page) and *The Hateful Eight*. Robert Richardson, the cinematographer of *The Hateful Eight*, said he and Tarantino studied *The Great Silence*'s photography to understand the intimacy Tarantino wanted, and Silence's ride through a valley is referenced in the opening of *The Hateful Eight*. [C30328]

*The Hateful Eight* began as a *Django Unchained* sequel novel titled *Django in White Hell* -- Tarantino first attempted the story as a novel sequel, realised the heroic Django did not fit a story in which everybody snowed into the haberdashery had to be a bad guy, and retooled it as a standalone film; Entertainment Weekly (Keith Staskiewicz, 11 December 2015) is the cited source for the account. [C30329]

The soundtrack album contains the only complete original score for a Tarantino film, composed, orchestrated and conducted by Morricone, and it won the Golden Globe and the Academy Award for Best Original Score on 28 February 2016. But the "original" label needs the footnote: the score repurposes Morricone's unused score from John Carpenter's *The Thing* (1982) -- Morricone wrote a full orchestral score and a full synthesiser score for *The Thing*, Carpenter used almost only the synthesiser main title -- plus "Regan's Theme" from *Exorcist II: The Heretic* (1977). Conflict on the split: Tarantino's own Q and A account (via Fact Magazine) has Morricone writing about 25 minutes of new music (a theme plus *Thing* pieces) while the soundtrack page credits Morricone with 50 minutes of original music -- do not assert an exact new-vs-reused split. [C30330]

The ear-severing scene in *Reservoir Dogs* -- Vic Vega cutting off Nash's ear -- was inspired by the scene in *Django* in which Brother Jonathan's ear is cut off by General Hugo's men as punishment; the *Django* film page states this directly. [C30331]

CBR reports a poster of Corbucci's *The Mercenary* (1968) on the outside of the theatre Sharon Tate visits in *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood*, calling Corbucci "the greatest Spaghetti Western director, second only to Sergio Leone" -- no second source found this run, so treat it as reported, not established. [C30332]

*Django & Django* (2021): Italian documentary film co-written and directed by Luca Rea (screenplay with Steve Della Casa), an homage to Corbucci's career with particular attention to his spaghetti westerns. World premiere out of competition at the 78th Venice Film Festival on 7 September 2021; limited Italian theatrical release 15 November 2021 (Lucky Red); 80 minutes; narrated by Quentin Tarantino, with Franco Nero and Ruggero Deodato (Corbucci's assistant director on *Django*) as the other on-screen voices; producers Nico Marzano, Nicoletta Ercole and Gianni Romoli. Reception: 75% of 8 critics' reviews positive on Rotten Tomatoes, average rating 7.30/10. ## Aesthetic logic

Corbucci's aesthetic is the professional's aesthetic with a wound in it: the genre is the product, the star is the delivery system, and the body pays the price. He spent a decade learning popular cinema from inside the typewriter -- the tearjerker, Toto's farce, the peplum's widescreen brawl -- and then he built a western in which the landscape is the moral condition, the hero is disabled where the genre expects strength, the lesson is priced, and the ending kills everybody the film defended. The logic runs: the disguise enters the world, the wound pays for the revenge, the weather judges it, and the ending refuses to forgive it. Everything in the films is built to be returned to rather than followed: the coffin is an image you remember before you remember the plot, the snow is a condition you feel before you understand the story, and the bleak ending is the reason the film is still being screened in a university cinema thirty-five years after the director died. That is also why the comedy decade reads as a different career with the same hands: the factory never stopped, the star changed, and the wound went quiet -- until Tarantino opened the book again and the coffin started moving. The memorial fact is the aesthetic fact: the most durable image of the Italian western is a man dragging his own burial through the mud, and the man who made it spent the last sixteen years of his life making comedies about men who impersonate counts. [C30367]
