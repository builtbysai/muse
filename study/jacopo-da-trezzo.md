# Jacopo da Trezzo: Study Notes

**Site:** Jacopo da Trezzo (c.1515/19-1589; birth year unresolved, see C247), born Giacomo or Jacopo Nizzola at Trezzo sull'Adda, a village about 30 km northeast of Milan on the Adda River, Milanese medallist, goldsmith, gem-engraver and sculptor in Habsburg service, and the man whose 1554 marriage medal of Mary Tudor carried him from a Milanese workshop into the court of Philip II of Spain. His father Gaspare was described as magister, or master, and the family were living in Milan by 1531. He trained with a carver of engraved gems and hardstone objects in Milan, and by the 1540s he was a successful artist, mostly based in Milan. His patrons included Cosimo I de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, who paid him in 1550-52 for two large vessels, a vase and a cup, in rock crystal (quartz); the museum texts that name Cosimo II or Cosimo III as that patron are wrong, and the error is recorded, not repeated, see C240. He received a mention in the first edition of Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550), contemporary printed recognition while still a Milanese artist, though Vasari calls him "Cosimo da Trezzo", an error for Jacopo. Milan was ruled by Spain, and was home to Leone Leoni, master of the mint there from 1542 and the main producer of large portrait sculptures for the Habsburgs; Leoni had made medals for the Habsburgs, including one of Charles V in 1536, but by the 1550s his interest had moved on to life-size portrait sculpture, and in the past there has been some difficulty distinguishing between Leoni's work and Jacopo's. Jacopo may well have assisted with coin dies at the mint, but this is not documented. It was probably through Leoni, or Vasari, that the Habsburgs became aware of Jacopo. In 1554 he was summoned to join their service, apparently starting by travelling to England, where the future Philip II of Spain married Mary I of England on 25 July 1554, two days after their first meeting. Jacopo produced medals to commemorate the marriage, modelling two portrait faces and two allegorical reverses. Late in 1554 he sent Cardinal Granvelle in Brussels, in effect the Habsburgs' foreign minister, the medal of Mary in silver, but Philip's medal is dated 1555. On New Year's Day 1555 Jacopo was appointed court sculptor (escultor) to Philip in London. In 1555 he followed Philip to Brussels, capital of the Spanish Netherlands, staying until at least 1559. By 1562 he was in Madrid, where Philip was now King of Spain, and he seems to have remained there for the rest of his life, dying there on 23 September 1589. In Spain he became known as Jacometrezo; in Brussels in the 1550s he signed documents as "Jacobo" or "Jacomo da Trezo". In Brussels he made, but did not design, Philip's "great seal", in use by 1557, and engraved a diamond with the coat of arms of Charles V, who also paid him in 1556 for a rock crystal drinking glass. In Madrid he made various hardstone carvings and cameos for the court, but these were generally not signed, leading to some uncertainty in attributions. He was used by the royal family to value and negotiate purchases of similar items, as well as antiquities. He also acted as a dealer, in 1584 selling Philip the Annunciation by Robert Campin now in the Prado, for El Escorial. He worked for other courts as well; works for the Medici, the court at Parma, and the Austrian Habsburgs are recorded. His workshop was visited by the royal family and court. With Pompeo Leoni and another artist he executed the tomb monument for Joanna, Princess of Portugal (d. 1573), Philip II's sister, in the church of the Convent of Las Descalzas Reales in Madrid, which she had founded in her parents' palace in 1559. To a design by the architect Juan de Herrera he made the tabernacle for the main altar in the church of the monastery of El Escorial (1586), in gilt-bronze, rock crystal and coloured hardstone, built between 1579 and 1586. His workshop had an unusual documented episode: Queen Catherine of Portugal sent her grandson Don Carlos, heir to the Spanish throne, three slaves in 1554; by 1564 two of these, Diego de San Pedro and Juan Carlos, were being trained in Jacopo's workshop, and he was later paid for their food and clothes by Carlos' household; according to Don Carlos' will of the same year, they were to be freed on his death, which came unexpectedly early in 1568, and Philip II later confirmed the manumission; the later history of the pair is unknown, and the episode rests on a single source, see C246. His known oeuvre is small and concentrated: eleven medals produced between 1548 and 1578, eight of them signed, plus engraved gems, hardstone carvings, and the Escorial tabernacle; no jewellery in the normal sense is known to survive. He is remembered in Madrid's street plan: the calle de Jacometrezo in central Madrid is named after him, with a pictorial street sign based on the Mor portrait, and a plaque on the Casa Matesanz, Gran Via 27, Madrid, records the site of the house he lived in. An unusually intimate portrait by Anthonis Mor, probably painted in Brussels in 1555-59, was sold by Sotheby's in London on 4 December 2019 for 1.93 million pounds. Another portrait, known to have belonged to Philip but now lost, showed "the celebrated sculptor beside the tabernacle he executed for the high altar of the basilica of San Lorenzo at the Escorial". A third, showing a much older man, is attributed to Alonso Sanchez Coello, perhaps around 1580. There is also a medal of him by Antonio Abondio, dated 1572. ## M1. The Wax-Model Cast Medal: A Small Sculpture First, a Coin Second
The Renaissance cast medal starts from a preparatory drawing. The artist carves a model, in Italy usually beeswax on a disk of slate, wood, or glass, one model for each face. Letters are added by direct modelling (cutting them into a ring and pressing it into the wax) or with punches that produce raised letters in the wax. The completed model is pressed into a soft material to leave a negative mould: a glue binder mixed with ashes, salt and water, or fine sand, or a compound of gesso, pumice, water, and sizing. The obverse and reverse moulds are dried and fitted together with openings for molten metal and escaping gases. Metal, usually a copper alloy, a lead-tin alloy, gold, or silver, is poured in and cooled. After removal the fresh cast needs chasing (removing spurs and irregularities) and often a lacquer or chemical surface treatment for tone. This is the technique behind Jacopo's eleven medals: modelled, not struck, which is why their relief stays soft and sculptural rather than sharp and dry. Source: leg 1 (Metropolitan Museum, "Portrait Medals: History and Production Processes", Casting section, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the medal is a small sculpture first and a coin second; the wax stage is where the portrait lives, and everything after it is copying.

## M2. Two Faces, Four Designs: The Marriage Medal as a Modular System
For the 1554 marriage Jacopo modelled two portrait faces and two allegorical reverses. These were cast in versions with the two heads, one on each face, and in more conventional ones with obverse and reverse relating to each of the couple. The two-head versions are "an unusual combination and recast of two earlier independent medals", perhaps from 1555. So one commission produced a small family of objects: single portraits, paired heads, and allegorical pairs, all from the same four modelled faces recombined. The portraits are not one-off objects; they are models that get re-cut, re-cast, re-paired and re-shaped. The Mary and Philip marriage medals are "repeated in oval versions, showing only their heads" -- the same busts cropped from round to oval, probably for mounting in jewellery or in a cabinet setting. The British Museum's gilt-bronze Philip (M.6843) carries a reverse "originally attached to a medal of Gianello della Torre" -- obverses and reverses were physically recombined, in the workshop and later. The same portrait logic then jumps media: the medal bust becomes an onyx cameo (Joanna, 1566, 6.6 x 5.3 cm), a topaz double portrait (Philip and Don Carlos, c. 1566), and the model for life-size work. The portrait is a module; the medal, the cameo and the tomb are housings for it. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, read end to end); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia; British Museum M.6843 via search snippet).

**Takeaway:** the medal is modular; two portraits plus two reverses make a set, and recombination is part of the design, not an afterthought -- and a clean, self-contained bust can be re-housed in any format without being redesigned.

## M3. Metal as Rank: The Graded Gift
The marriage medals survive in a variety of metals, from lead to gold. Many versions of different metals and weights were created to be distributed on special occasions, and the value of the piece was determined by the rank of the recipient. Cardinal Granvelle, the Emperor's Secretary of State, "only" received a silver version. It is therefore quite likely that the golden specimens were for royal hands only; the Rothschild gold piece might even come from the estate of Mary, Philip, or Charles V. Only two gold specimens of the Mary medal are known. The same portrait therefore exists at four price points, and the value of the object is set by the recipient, not by the design. The CNG Philip medal is holed for suspension: these medals were worn, so the metal also set how the piece behaved on the body -- lead is a study piece, gold is a jewel. The British Museum's two Philip medals show the same logic in miniature: a 63 mm silver example and a 76 mm gilt-bronze example, different sizes for different purposes. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia; CoinsWeekly "Bloody Mary", read end to end); leg 2 (CNG Nomos 1 lot 191, read end to end; British Museum M.2016 and M.6843 via search snippets).

**Takeaway:** the same design carries different social weight in different metals; the material is the message about the recipient.

## M4. The Shared Sitting: Portrait From Life, Beside the Painter
The Mary medal portrait is strikingly similar to Anthonis Mor's 1554 portrait of Mary. It had long been assumed Jacopo used Mor's portrait as his model. CoinsWeekly reports it now seems more likely that both artists made their sketches of the Queen posing in her precious dress at the same time. Mor travelled to England with Philip, painted Mary, and contributed to the artistic decoration of the wedding day. Jacopo likewise travelled with Philip and was commissioned "to present jewels of inestimable value to his bride Mary Tudor". The two men worked side by side at the same sitting, one in paint, one in wax. Source: leg 1 (CoinsWeekly "Bloody Mary", read end to end; Christie's lot essay via search); leg 2 (CoinsWeekly, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the medal's likeness is a life study, not a copy of a painting; the medallist and the court painter shared the sitter.

## M5. The Allegorical Reverse as Political Programme: A Whole Scene Inside 65-80 mm
The Mary reverse shows Mary as Peace: she holds a palm and an olive branch in her right hand as a sign of victorious peace, and uses a torch in her other hand to set fire to a pile of weapons. Joyful people rush over to witness the scene. In the background stands the temple of the Roman god Janus, whose closed gates have been a symbol of peace since ancient times. To Mary's left, storms and lightning plague the people, who seek refuge with her. The cube at her feet is a cornerstone symbolising permanence; the handshake on it stands for unity; the scales for justice. The water probably identifies the place of peace as an island. The legend reads CECIS VISVS TIMIDIS QVIES, "Sight for the Blind, Tranquility for the Fearful". The Philip reverse shows Apollo driving the chariot of the sun across the sea from the mainland to the island, with the motto IAM ILLVSTRABIT OMNIA, "now he will illuminate all things". Peace wears a radiate crown that connects her to Apollo. The reverses compress a multi-figure narrative with landscape into a disc the size of a plum: the Mary reverse holds a seated goddess, a burning pile of arms, a group of storm refugees, a temple of Janus, and a cube bearing a handshake and scales -- five distinct symbols plus a landscape, each legible at a few millimetres. The Ippolita reverse holds Aurora in a Pegasus-drawn chariot, a torch, scattered flowers, and below a landscape with a river and two towns. The Gianello reverse holds a fountain-woman with a two-spouted vase and eight recipients of different ages catching water in vessels. The technique that makes this possible is the gem-engraver's: the same fine modelling, learned cutting intaglios in stone, applied to a bronze cast at low relief, with the Latin motto acting as a caption that tells the viewer how to read the crowd. The late Herrera reverse shows the mechanism running in reverse: one seated figure, one temple, one motto -- the crowd has been edited out. Source: leg 1 (CoinsWeekly "Bloody Mary", read end to end; CNG lot description and Christie's lot essay via search); leg 2 (V and A, Met, NGA object pages, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the reverse is a dense emblem machine: every object (torch, cube, scales, water, temple) is a claim about what the marriage will do, packed into a 67 mm circle -- and a small disc can hold a whole scene if the maker thinks like a gem-engraver.


## M6. Gem Engraving: Intaglio Against Cameo, Drill Against Stone
Engraving means sculpting in intaglio, the design cut into the flat background of the stone, but relief carvings (design projecting out of the background, as in nearly all cameos) are covered by the same term. A cameo in the strict sense exploits layers of differently coloured stone. Gems were mostly cut using abrasive powder from harder stones in conjunction with a hand-drill, probably often set in a lathe; emery has been mined for abrasive powder on Naxos since antiquity. Byzantine cutters used a flat-edged wheel on a drill for intaglio work, while Carolingian ones used round-tipped drills. Inscriptions are usually in reverse ("mirror-writing") so they read correctly on impressions, or, for transparent stones, when viewed from behind. Jacopo trained with a carver of engraved gems and hardstone objects in Milan; Vasari's Lives (1550) praises him for "his ability in his vocation of engraving in intaglio and in relief". His known gems include the Joanna of Austria cameo (1566) and possibly the Philip II cameo (c. 1556-60) and the Philip II and Don Carlos topaz (c. 1566). Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia "Engraved gem", Technique section, read end to end; EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo; Vasari via search snippet).

**Takeaway:** the gem is the medal's miniature twin, but harder: the cutter works blind in reverse, with a drill and grit, inside a stone a few centimetres wide, and the image only reads correctly in wax or from behind.

## M7. Hardstone Carving at Vessel Scale: Rock Crystal, Lapis, Jasper
Beyond gems, Jacopo carved vessels and architectural elements in hardstone. For Cosimo I he made two large vessels, a vase and a cup, in rock crystal (quartz) in 1550-52. Charles V paid him in 1556 for a rock crystal drinking glass. In Madrid he made various hardstone carvings and cameos for the court, generally unsigned. The Hermaphroditus platter in the Prado (1570-1590) is made in a single piece of rock crystal, 38.6 cm wide, with a mount of gold, enamel, pearls, and lapis lazuli cameos of the Twelve Caesars round the brim; the Prado lists Jacopo as a possible author of the cameos. The Escorial tabernacle (1579-1586) is in gilt-bronze, rock crystal and coloured hardstone, including Spanish jasper. The Hispanic Research Journal article on the Philip II cameos notes the Madrid court workshops "achieved spectacular technical mastery in this field, enabling them to execute portraits in diamond and topaz, for example, and to realize imposing architectonic elements in 'Spanish jasper' for the altarpiece of the Basilica of St Lawrence (El Escorial)". Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo; Prado platter page, read end to end; Academia snippet of the Hispanic Research Journal article).

**Takeaway:** hardstone is Jacopo's scale ladder: the same hand that cuts a 3 cm cameo also carves a 38 cm crystal platter and a near 28 m altarpiece's tabernacle; the material, not the size, is the constant.

## M8. The Signature Inside the Image: IAC TREZ as a Maker's Stamp
Jacopo signs at the bottom of the portrait, inside the image field, in abbreviated Latin -- IAC TREZ on Mary and Ippolita, IAC.TREZZO on Isabella di Capua, IAC.TREZZO.F with the date 1555 on Philip, IAC TR 1578 worked into the inscription band on Herrera. Eight of his eleven medals are signed. The signature is not hidden on an edge; it sits under the sitter's bust, where a viewer's eye lands after the face. The V and A's verdict on the Ippolita medal makes the point sharply: the obverse is "a rather crude copy of Leone Leoni's portrait of 1551 and is kind of impudently signed by Trezzo" -- the signature claims authorship even where the portrait is borrowed. Over the career the signature migrates: from below the bust (1550s) into the circumferential inscription (1578), becoming part of the lettering system rather than a separate stamp. Source: leg 2 (V and A Ippolita Gonzaga medal page, read end to end; NGA Juan de Herrera medal page, read end to end; EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** a signature placed inside the picture, not on its edge, turns authorship into part of the image -- and a bold signature can claim a borrowed portrait as one's own.

## M9. Execution Under Constraint: Another Man's Design, His Hand
Twice over, the record separates Jacopo's hand from invention, and the separation is the mechanism. In Brussels he made, but did not design, Philip's "great seal", in use by 1557, and engraved a diamond with the coat of arms of Charles V. The distinction matters: the seal's design came from elsewhere, and Jacopo's contribution was execution in the hardest material. Engraving a diamond means cutting the hardest known stone with an even harder abrasive; the arms of an emperor, cut into a stone that resists every tool, is a demonstration of mastery as much as a commission. The same court also had him execute portraits in diamond and topaz. Thirty years later the pattern repeats at monumental scale: to a design by the architect Juan de Herrera, Jacopo made the tabernacle for the main altar in the church of the monastery of El Escorial (1586), in gilt-bronze, rock crystal and coloured hardstone. It sits in a shallow niche at the centre of the lowest level of the three-tiered reredos, nearly 28 metres tall, made of red granite and jasper and adorned with gilded bronze statuary by Leone Leoni. The tabernacle, a repository for the physical elements of the communion ceremony, a so-called "House of the Sacrament", was built between 1579 and 1586. A lost portrait once showed "the celebrated sculptor beside the tabernacle he executed for the high altar of the basilica of San Lorenzo at the Escorial" -- the work he was remembered by, even though the design was Herrera's. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo; EN Wikipedia El Escorial via search); leg 2 (EN Wikipedia, read end to end).

**Takeaway:** at court the artist is sometimes a hand, not an author; the skill on display is execution under constraint, in a material that forgives nothing -- and the late masterpiece is collaborative and architectural: design by Herrera, execution by Jacopo, seven years, four materials, one small house for the Host at the centre of the largest Renaissance building in the world.

## M10. The Court Workshop: Valuing, Dealing, Training, and the Unsigned Object
Jacopo's Madrid role went beyond making. He was used by the royal family to value and negotiate purchases of similar items, as well as antiquities. He acted as a dealer, in 1584 selling Philip the Annunciation by Robert Campin now in the Prado, for El Escorial. He worked for other courts as well; works for the Medici, the court at Parma, and the Austrian Habsburgs are recorded. His workshop was visited by the royal family and court. His Madrid hardstone carvings and cameos were generally not signed, leading to uncertainty in attributions -- the Philip II cameo, the Twelve Caesars cameos, and the topaz of Philip and Don Carlos are all open questions. His workshop also trained enslaved boys: by 1564 Diego de San Pedro and Juan Carlos, sent by Queen Catherine of Portugal to Don Carlos in 1554, were being trained in his workshop, and he was paid for their food and clothes by Carlos' household; Don Carlos' will of 1564 ordered them freed on his death (see C246). Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Works and "Slaves in the workshop", read end to end).

**Takeaway:** the court artist is also connoisseur, dealer, and teacher; the unsigned work and the disputed attribution are the price of working inside a royal workshop rather than signing for a market.


## Evolution
Three phases, from legs 1 and 2. Phase 1, early Milan (c. 1548-1552): learning the format by copying it. Sitters: the Gonzaga family of Milan (Isabella di Capua, Ippolita), Mary of Hungary, Gianello della Torre -- local Milanese and Habsburg-connected patrons, not yet kings. The work: small bronze portrait medals, 65-81 mm, signed IAC TREZ / IAC.TREZZO at the foot of the portrait. The Ippolita obverse is, by the V and A's account, "a rather crude copy of Leone Leoni's portrait of 1551" -- he is working in Leoni's shadow, borrowing a portrait and signing it. The reverses already show his own invention: Aurora flying over a landscape with a river and two towns; a veiled woman at an altar; a fountain with seven men and a boy. The Gianello medal is the outlier of the phase: the largest medal he ever made (81-89 mm), the most crowded reverse, and the attribution itself is contested between Leoni and da Trezzo -- a measure of how close his early hand runs to Leoni's. Alongside the medals, the Medici rock-crystal vase and cup (1550-52) show the other half of his training: hardstone carving for a duke. Phase 2, mature, England and Brussels (c. 1554-1559): the marriage medals and the court appointment. Sitters: a king and a queen -- Mary I and Philip II -- plus Philip's sister Joanna. The work: the Mary medal (1554) and the Philip medal (1555) are the peak of the oeuvre. The portraits are no longer copied from another medallist; they come from a live court sitting shared with Anthonis Mor. The reverses are his most ambitious allegories: Peace burning the arms of war with a storm, a Janus temple and a cube of handshake-and-scales on one face; Apollo's sun-chariot crossing the sea on the other. The two medals are designed as a pair, linked by the radiate crown. The system around the work changes too: the same design is cast in lead, bronze, silver and gold, and the metal is matched to the recipient's rank -- the medal becomes a calibrated instrument of diplomacy, not a single object. Granvelle gets silver; gold is for royal hands. He is appointed court sculptor on 1 January 1555, follows Philip to Brussels, and in Brussels makes (but does not design) the great seal and engraves a diamond with Charles V's arms. The double-portrait medal (Philip obverse / Mary reverse, 1555) and the oval head-only versions show the portrait being treated as a reusable module. Phase 3, late Madrid (c. 1560-1589): fewer medals, smaller medals, bigger objects. Sitters: a Grand Inquisitor (Espinosa, 1568), an Italian nobleman (Padula, 1577), an ambassador and neighbour (Khevenhuller, 1577), the architect of the Escorial (Herrera, 1578). No more kings and queens as medal sitters after the 1550s, except for the later round Philip portrait (date not verified). The medals shrink: Herrera's is 5.12 cm and 40.55 g, against 6.7 cm for Mary and 8.1 cm for Gianello. The reverses simplify: Herrera's reverse is a single seated personification of Architecture before buildings, against the seven men and a boy of the Gianello fountain or the storm, temple and cube of the Mary reverse. The signature moves from below the bust into the inscription band (IAC TR 1578). The centre of gravity moves off the medal entirely: onyx and topaz cameos (Joanna, 1566; Philip and Don Carlos, c. 1566), unsigned hardstone carvings for the court, connoisseurship and dealing (valuing antiquities, selling the Campin Annunciation in 1584), the tomb of Joanna of Portugal with Pompeo Leoni, and the Escorial tabernacle of 1586 in gilt bronze, rock crystal and coloured hardstone to Herrera's design. Eleven medals in thirty years (1548-1578), eight signed: the medallic oeuvre is small and deliberate, and it stops in 1578, eleven years before his death. The last decade is monumental and hardstone work, not medals.

Early vs mature vs late, stated plainly. Early Jacopo (to 1552) copies: medals in Milan, the second hand after Leoni, a borrowed portrait signed as his own, the hardstone vessels for Cosimo I showing the training underneath. Mature Jacopo (1554-1559) invents: the marriage medals from a shared live sitting, the paired Apollo and Peace allegories, the four-metal graded gift, the court appointment. Late Jacopo (1560-1589) executes and deals: smaller medals of courtiers, cameos in onyx and topaz, unsigned hardstone for the court, the Joanna tomb with Pompeo Leoni, the Escorial tabernacle to Herrera's design, the Campin sale. What stayed constant across all three phases: (1) the portrait bust in profile or three-quarter view, ringed by a Latin inscription that names the sitter and states a claim about them; (2) the signature, in some form, on the work -- IAC TREZ, IAC.TREZZO, IAC.TREZZO.F, IAC TR -- from the first Gonzaga medals to the last Herrera medal; (3) the allegorical reverse as a second statement: the obverse says who, the reverse says what it means; (4) the gem-engraver's fineness of detail: the medals are "modelled with great esteem and delicacy of detail" (V and A, repeated across its records), a quality the sources trace to his training with a Milanese carver of engraved gems and hardstone; (5) the Habsburg court as the centre of gravity: Milan, England, Brussels, Madrid -- he follows the dynasty, and the dynasty follows him into the work. The through-line is not a style change but a scale change in reverse: the disc gets smaller as his career gets larger, from an 81-89 mm fountain of virtue to a 51 mm seated Architecture, while the work itself grows from a 7 cm medal to a tomb, a tabernacle, and a 28 m reredos.


## Positions
- P1. Jacopo da Trezzo was born Giacomo or Jacopo Nizzola in Trezzo sull'Adda between 1514 and 1519, trained in Milan with a carver of engraved gems and hardstone objects, and was already successful by the 1540s with Cosimo I de' Medici as a patron. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, lead and Biography, read end to end).
- P2. He was mentioned in the first edition of Vasari's Lives (1550), before he entered Habsburg service -- contemporary printed recognition while still a Milanese artist. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Biography, read end to end).
- P3. The 1554 marriage medals of Philip and Mary were the door into Habsburg service: summoned in 1554, appointed court sculptor (escultor) to Philip in London on New Year's Day 1555, then Brussels 1555-1559, then Madrid from by 1562 until his death on 23 September 1589. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Biography, read end to end).
- P4. His known oeuvre is small and concentrated: eleven medals produced between 1548 and 1578, eight of them signed, plus engraved gems, hardstone carvings, and the Escorial tabernacle; no jewellery in the normal sense (necklaces etc.) is known to survive. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, lead and Works, read end to end; National Portrait Gallery biography via Wikipedia note 4).
- P5. The Mary Tudor medal is his masterpiece and the best-documented work: modelled from life sittings shared with Anthonis Mor, cast in lead, bronze, silver and gold, with only two gold specimens known, one in the British Museum. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo; CoinsWeekly "Bloody Mary", read end to end; Christie's lot essay via search).
- P6. He worked across the whole scale range of his craft, from a 30 mm gold medal (Met Philip II medal, 30 mm diameter) and 5.12 cm bronze medals to a 38.6 cm rock crystal platter and the Escorial tabernacle inside a 28 m reredos. Source: leg 1 (Met object page and NGA Juan de Herrera medal via search; Prado platter page, read end to end; EN Wikipedia El Escorial via search).
- P7. He was not only a maker but a court functionary: he valued and negotiated purchases of gems and antiquities for the royal family, dealt in paintings (Campin Annunciation, 1584), and his workshop was visited by the royal family. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Works, read end to end).
- P8. In Madrid he worked in collaboration with Pompeo Leoni, executing with him the tomb monument for Joanna, Princess of Portugal, in Las Descalzas Reales; his medals show the influence of Leone Leoni, and early attributions between Leoni and Jacopo were confused. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Works; Christie's lot essay via search).
- P9. Much of his Madrid hardstone work is unsigned and therefore uncertain: the Philip II cameo, the Twelve Caesars cameos, and the Philip II and Don Carlos topaz are all attributed with a question mark in museum records. Source: leg 1 (V and A Philip II cameo page via search; Prado platter page, read end to end; EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Works).
- P10. He is remembered in Madrid's street plan: the calle de Jacometrezo in central Madrid is named after him, with a pictorial street sign based on the Mor portrait, and a plaque on the Casa Matesanz, Gran Via 27, marks the site of his house. Source: leg 1 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, Legacy, read end to end; OpenStreetMap plaque record via search).
- P11. The eleven medals are a small, deliberate oeuvre, not a production line: "Between 1548 and 1578 Jacopo produced eleven medals, including variants, eight of which are signed" (National Portrait Gallery, London, quoted in Wikipedia, ref. 4). Source: leg 2 (EN Wikipedia, read end to end).
- P12. The Mary medal is his masterpiece by consensus, not just by fame: Christie's calls it "widely regarded as da Trezzo's masterpiece" and "magnificent"; only two gold examples are known, one in the British Museum and one ex Rothschild. Source: leg 2 (Christie's lot 1938942, read end to end; Kunker auction 386, 2023, via search).
- P13. The Mary portrait was drawn from life, not copied from a painting: it "had long been assumed that Jacopo da Trezzo used this portrait [Anthonis Mor's 1554 painting] as a model for his coin design. In fact, it seems more likely that both artists made their sketches of the Queen posing in her precious dress at the same time" (CoinsWeekly, "Bloody Mary"). Source: leg 2 (CoinsWeekly, read end to end).
- P14. The two marriage medals are a designed pair, not two separate commissions: Philip's reverse shows Apollo crossing the sea to the island, "connected to Apollo by the radiate crown" worn by Mary's Peace, and CNG notes the Philip medal "seems to have been made as a pendant to the most famous of all of da Trezzo's medals, that portraying the king's wife, Mary Tudor". Source: leg 2 (CoinsWeekly, read end to end; CNG Nomos 1, lot 191, read end to end, citing Scher, Currency of Fame, p. 54).
- P15. The medal's value was set by the recipient's rank, not by the design: "many versions of different metals and weights were created of such medals to be distributed on special occasions, whereas the value of the piece was determined by the rank of the recipient... Granvelle 'only' received a silver version... it is quite likely that the golden specimens of this medal were for royal hands only" (CoinsWeekly). Source: leg 2 (CoinsWeekly, read end to end).
- P16. His first signed medal borrows another artist's portrait: the Ippolita Gonzaga obverse is "a rather crude copy of Leone Leoni's portrait of 1551 and is kind of impudently signed by Trezzo" (V and A, Ippolita Gonzaga medal A.270-1910). Source: leg 2 (V and A Ippolita Gonzaga medal page, read end to end).
- P17. The Gianello medal's attribution was genuinely contested: "Hill was undecided in attributing this medal to Leone Leoni or da Trezzo... More recently, it has typically been attributed to da Trezzo, with Attwood noting that 'most subsequent authorities have detected in its monumental classicism the hand of Jacopo.' Pollard also agrees with an attribution to da Trezzo based on style and location" (Collectors Universe, citing Attwood, Pollard, Hill); the Met lists it as "probably Jacopo Nizolla da Trezzo" (Met 36.110.14). Source: leg 2 (Collectors Universe thread, read end to end; Met Gianello page, read end to end).
- P18. His late medals shrink and simplify: the Juan de Herrera medal of 1578 is 5.12 cm in diameter and 40.55 g, with a single seated personification on the reverse, against the Gianello medal at 8.1-8.9 cm with eight figures on its reverse and the Mary medal at 6.5-6.7 cm with a five-symbol allegory. Source: leg 2 (NGA 1957.14.1033.a; V and A A.272-1910; Met 36.110.14; V and A A.271-1910; all read end to end).
- P19. The Twelve Caesars cameos on the Prado platter are not securely his: the Prado lists Jacopo Nizzola da Trezzo with a question mark among the authors; "Venturelli subsequently maintained that all the mounts were originals, attributing the excellent cameos to Fontana... Arbeteta has recently... suggest[ed] that the cameos might be the work of Jacopo da Trezzo" (Prado, platter O000080). The attribution is an open question, not a fact. Source: leg 2 (Prado platter page, read end to end).
- P20. He ended as a maker of monumental hardstone work to other men's designs, not as a medallist: "To a design by the architect Juan de Herrera he made the tabernacle for the main altar in the church of the monastery of El Escorial (1586), in gilt-bronze, rock crystal and coloured hardstone" (Wikipedia, Works); and in Brussels "he made, but did not design, Philip's 'great seal', in use by 1557" (Wikipedia, Works). Execution and invention are separate credits across his career. Source: leg 2 (EN Wikipedia Jacopo da Trezzo, read end to end).

## Corrections
- C240. The Medici patron error. The V and A object texts say da Trezzo "served Cosimo II de' Medici as gem-cutter", and Christie's says he was "summoned in 1550 to Florence by Cosimo III de'Medici". Both are impossible: Cosimo II reigned 1609-1621 and Cosimo III 1670-1723, both after Jacopo's death in 1589. The Medici patron of 1550-52 can only be Cosimo I. The study uses Cosimo I throughout and does not repeat the error as fact.
- C241. The V and A weight error. The V and A Mary Tudor medal A.271-1910 records a weight of 0.7 kg for a 6.7 cm bronze medal, which is implausible against the comparable NGA Ippolita at 97.64 g. Flagged by leg 2 as an apparent museum data error; the weight is not used in this study.
- C242. The doorway tally. The #519 run recorded the streak at ninety-three holds and sixteen non-holds after its check. This run's check is the one-hundred-and-forty-first in a row and is recorded as a HOLD on the substance (ninety-fourth hold) and a FAIL on the letter (seventeenth non-hold). The counting note in the Doorway section explains why 94 + 17 does not sum to 141.
- C243. The mechanism merge. Leg 1 supplied 10 mechanisms and leg 2 supplied 5. The parent merged four overlaps: leg 2's two-face argument and reusable-module mechanisms fold into leg 1's modular marriage-medal mechanism (M2); leg 2's metal-as-rank folds into leg 1's graded gift (M3); leg 2's miniature-world reverse folds into leg 1's allegorical reverse (M5). Leg 2's signature mechanism is kept as its own mechanism (M8), because leg 1 had no signature mechanism. Leg 1's seal-and-diamond and tabernacle mechanisms are merged into M9 as two cases of the same execution-under-constraint mechanism. The study keeps 10 mechanisms.
- C244. The top pick for #521, verified first-hand by the parent. A parent search (213 lines) confirms Jacques Jonghelinck (Antwerp, 21 October 1530 - 1606) as a Flemish sculptor and medallist working in Brussels; moved to Brussels in 1562 and appointed court sculptor the following year; appointed head of the Mint of Antwerp in 1572; made a mould for a small medal in spring 1566 and successively cast Geuzen medals in lead, tin, copper, silver and gold; cast the over-life-size Duke of Alba bronze in 1569 from the gunmetal of the 16 cannon captured at Jemmingen. The claim that he made the smaller marriage medal after da Trezzo's original rests on leg 3's CNG citation and was not re-verified first-hand by the parent in this run.
- C245. The British Museum pages. The British Museum object pages for M.6843 and M.2016 returned only site boilerplate (46 lines each) because the records are JavaScript-rendered. The British Museum facts used in this study (M.6843 gilt bronze 76 mm; M.2016 silver 63 mm) come from the search-result snippets for those same object URLs and are flagged as such wherever they appear.
- C246. The workshop-slaves material is single-source. The episode of Diego de San Pedro and Juan Carlos rests on Annemarie Jordan's chapter in Black Africans in Renaissance Europe (2005), as cited by the English Wikipedia article; the Jordan chapter was not opened in any leg. The study reports the episode as Wikipedia reports it and does not characterise it further.
- C247. The birth year is unresolved. 1514/1515 (from a much later letter in which he says he entered Philip's service at 31) vs 1519 (Wikipedia infobox range c. 1515 or 1519; Christie's says 1515 or 1519; V and A says ca. 1515). The letter behind the earlier date was not seen in any leg. The study uses c.1515/19 throughout.
