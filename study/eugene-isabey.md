# Eugene Isabey: Study Notes

**Site:** Eugene Louis Gabriel Isabey (Friday 22 July 1803 - Sunday 25 April 1886), French painter, lithographer and watercolourist -- born in Paris in the Palais du Louvre, where his family was lodged, the son of the miniaturist Jean-Baptiste Isabey (1767-1855); died at Montevrain, east of Paris, aged 82, and buried in Pere-Lachaise cemetery, 20th division. He wanted to be a sailor; his father insisted on painting, and he trained by copying the Old Masters at the Louvre. Salon debut 1824 with three Normandy views and a first-class medal at 20; first-class medals again in 1827 and at the Exposition Universelle of 1855; Peintre officiel de la Marine royale 1830, official draughtsman on the Algiers campaign; court painter to Louis-Philippe; knight of the Legion of Honour 1832, officer Thursday 22 January 1852. He found Etretat at 17, in 1820, and painted the Normandy coast for sixty years; he introduced Lepoittevin to Etretat. He refused the 1831 Morocco mission and Delacroix took his place; he met Jongkind at The Hague in 1845 and brought him to Paris; Boudin enrolled in his Paris studio in 1850; Monet met Jongkind at his Honfleur house in 1862. He was called the best marine painter of his time and the "Horace Vernet of marine painting"; his late watercolours, made after he gave up oil, are said to announce Impressionism. The signature works are La Plage a maree basse (1833, Louvre), the Battle of Texel (1839), the Return of Napoleon aboard the Belle Poule (1841-42, Versailles), the Town and Port of Dieppe (1842), the Fish Market, Dieppe (1845, National Gallery), the Wreck (1854, Detroit), the Shipwreck of the Emily (1865, Nantes), and the late watercolours of the Normandy shore. His atelier was sold at the Galerie Georges Petit, Wednesday 30 to Thursday 31 March 1887; the Louvre gave him a solo show, "Eugene Isabey (1803-1886), par les ruelles et par les greves", Thursday 5 July to Monday 17 September 2012, curator Christophe Leribault. ## M1. The Sea-Less Marine (the Low-Tide Shore as the Subject)
He is famous, in fr.wikipedia's words, for painting marines "sans mer" -- without sea. He prefers to paint the misery of sailors at low tide than the wave breaking against the cliffs. The signature picture is La Plage a maree basse (1833, Louvre, 124 x 168 cm), bought by the State the year it was painted; the habit runs from Small Boat, Beach at Low Tide (1829) through the Six Marines lithograph "Refitting of a Boat at Low Tide" (1833), Boats on the Shore at Calais (1851) and Debarquement de la peche sur la plage (signed and dated 1882). Roger de Beauvoir's 1836 Salon review says it in period language: leave him before the rusted anchors, the old boats, the mussel fishermen, the sun slashing the pebbles -- he is the poet of the sailors' misery. The sea is absent; the drama is stranded boats, wet sand, anchors, nets, figures working on a tilted ground plane. **Takeaway:** a seascape with the sea removed -- animate the tide line, not the waves. The tide itself becomes the clock: the picture is a shore waiting for water that never arrives in frame.

## M2. Grey Tonalism / Grisaille (Tone over Outline)
En.wikipedia: he was "especially skillful at rendering subtleties in darker colors; which might be called a form of grisaille." Fr.wikipedia: the interiors are "d'une coloration grise tout en nuances" -- a grey colouring all in nuances. Apres l'orage (1869, Hermitage) is described as muted indigo, slate grey, ochre. Barnsley: he "developed a dark palette with light touches as highlights for his more dramatic scenes." Forms emerge from a narrow tonal band; edges dissolve; one grey field carries the whole picture and colour is a small accent inside it. **Takeaway:** build a scene from one grey ramp with a single warm intrusion; let edges be tonal, never drawn. The picture is a weather system first and a subject second.

## M3. Loose Wash vs Precise Detail (Two Speeds in One Picture)
Fr.wikipedia's style section: his style is "fluide" and "lache" -- fluid and loose -- "fait d'effets taches et d'execution rapide, qu'il oppose a des details precis", made of stained effects and rapid execution, set against precise details. Gautier (quoted in fr.wikipedia): "chaque coup de pinceau les signe... un alambic, une pierre, un canot, ont l'air spirituel" -- each brushstroke signs it; an alembic, a stone, a canoe, painted by Isabey, look witty. Barnsley: "a swift and chaotic manner of painting, a method of execution which produced works full of movement, vigour and expression." Broad stained washes lay the weather; then a few hard, small, exact details (a rope, a mast, a face) snap the image into focus. **Takeaway:** two-layer rendering -- fast loose field first, then a sparse set of razor-sharp details dropped in last. The watchable behaviour is the snap: the picture goes from stain to thing in one stroke.

## M4. The Reworked Sheet (Scrape, Incise, Gum Arabic, Gouache over Watercolour)
The medium lists give the technique away. A work of c.1850 (Art Gallery of Ontario) is "red chalk, watercolour, gum arabic, gouache and scraping on laid paper"; another is "watercolor with touches of gouache and pen and brown ink with gum arabic, over black chalk, with scraping and incising into wet paint on wove paper". Highlights are not painted on, they are cut back to the paper; the surface shows its own history of erasure and re-attack. The Getty's watercolour-technique notes describe the same scraping for white highlights on waves -- the Turner technique Isabey learned in England. **Takeaway:** light as subtraction -- erase and scrape the bright marks out of a dark wash instead of adding white. The sheet keeps a memory of every correction.

## M5. Light-Ground Watercolour (Abandoning the Dark Ground)
After the 1825 England trip he "abandoned the traditional dark ground technique favored by many French artists and embraced a lighter palette, mirroring Turner's own style" (WahooArt/artsdot summary, used here only for the claim the other sources corroborate). Ongpin: "From Bonington, Isabey adopted the practice of drawing his watercolours on a light or white ground, instead of the dark ground commonly used in France at the time." Bonington's fluid watercolours and luminous oils "profoundly influenced" him (NiceArtGallery). The white of the paper does the work of sky and spray; washes stay transparent; the picture glows from behind. **Takeaway:** reserve the white ground as the light source; never paint the sky, only stain around it. The ground is the lamp.

## M6. The Storm/Wreck Machine (One Composition, Forty Years of Variations)
Sotheby's: "Isabey explored the idea of shipwrecks and storms throughout his career." The run: Dutch Ships in a Storm (1839), the Wreck (1854, Detroit, 96 x 76 cm, lighter palette, more finished), the Wreck (c.1860, Barnsley, nine passengers in a boat at night, two birds above), Hurricane Before Saint Malo (1860, Cooper Gallery), Shipwreck of the Emily (1865, Nantes, 200 x 345 cm -- a wreck of 1823 painted 42 years later), After the Storm (1869, Hermitage), Caravan Caught in a Storm Along the Shore (undated, Sotheby's: more intense and dynamic than the 1854 Detroit Wreck). Same grammar every time: a small human group against huge weather; the boat tilts, the sky darkens, the sea rises; only the palette and the finish change. **Takeaway:** a parametric storm -- one compositional engine, with weather intensity and finish as the two dials. The wreck is a machine with settings, not a scene painted once.

## M7. The Busy Shore (Crowd Choreography of Work)
The Fish Market, Dieppe (1845, National Gallery, NG2715): figures selling and buying fish, "a sense of movement and energy... arranged in a way that creates a sense of depth". Fishing Village (1855). Beach at Low Tide (Caen). Roger de Beauvoir: Isabey is "le poete de votre misere, o marins" -- the poet of sailors' misery: rusted anchors, old boats, mussel fishermen, sun slashing the pebbles. Dozens of small figures each doing a different small job (hauling, mending nets, gutting fish); the crowd is a system of parallel tasks, not a mass. **Takeaway:** a shore of many small simultaneous labours -- each figure a loop, the crowd the composition. The watchable behaviour is the parallelism: nothing waits for anything else.

## M8. History as Theatre (Reconstruction with Period Costume)
The Return of Napoleon (1841-42, Versailles, 237 x 369 cm): the night transfer of Napoleon's body to the Belle Poule by torchlight, gun salutes in driving rain, 15 October 1840. The Battle of Texel (1839, 250 x 358 cm). The Embarkment of De Ruyter at the Battle of Texel (1850, 226 x 328 cm). Arrivee du duc d'Albe a Rotterdam (1844, Orsay). The Departure of Elisabeth of France for Spain (Walters). Leribault (quoted in fr.wikipedia): his "evocations chatoyantes de la cour d'Elisabeth ou des derniers Valois" -- shimmering evocations of the court of Elizabeth or the last Valois -- won a Second Empire clientele. A dark stage, a single pool of light on the ceremony, massed ships or crowd as backdrop; history painted as a night scene. **Takeaway:** stage history at night -- one lit action, everything else in tonal darkness. The ceremony is the only thing the light is allowed to touch.

## M9. Sketching from the Boat (Plein Air by Oar)
Soireeaulouvre: "Both Isabey and Morel-Fatio practised sketching from rowing boats around Paris, making frequent stops to draw en plein air, a marked departure from atelier-only production. Isabey became an accomplished oarsman and was locally nicknamed 'admiral of the lake' at Enghien." His cottage on the lake adjoined that of Frederic de Reiset, Louvre conservator and mayor of Enghien-les-Bains; Princess Mathilde and de Nieuwerkerke lived a short distance away at Saint-Gratien; Isabey and de Nieuwerkerke regularly sailed or rowed together. Horace Vernet, whose studio was nearby, caricatured Isabey as an owl perched on a branch after noting the cottage stood on four large poplar beams at the lake's edge. Vernet, Isabey and Morel-Fatio were among the founders of the Le Havre Regatta Society in 1839. **Takeaway:** a drifting viewpoint -- the camera is a boat, the scene slides past, nothing is framed from a fixed point. The viewpoint itself is the motion.

## M10. Late Blue vs Brown-Red (The Final Palette)
Lespinasse (quoted in fr.wikipedia): the late watercolours mix "de larges empatements de gouache... ou les bleus s'opposent aux tonalites brun-rouge mises en valeur par des taches de blanc" -- broad gouache impasto, where blues oppose brown-red tones set off by touches of white. Fr.wikipedia: the late watercolours "annoncent l'impressionnisme tant par le style que par les lieux" -- announce Impressionism as much by style as by the places he paints. Two colour families fight -- cold blue sea and sky against warm brown-red shore and roofs -- with white gouache spots as the referee. **Takeaway:** a two-colour war -- blue field vs brown-red mass, white sparks marking the boundary. The palette is a contest with a score.

## Evolution
Phase 1 -- Apprenticeship and the Normandy discovery, 1820-1827. 1820: six months at Etretat with a coastguard captain (fr.wikipedia, citing Joanne 1872 and Cochet 1853); the Morgan Library says he first ventured to Normandy with his father in 1820, Christie's that he went with Nodier, Taylor and Cailleux -- the company differs by source, the year does not. Winter 1820-21: a two-month tour of England and Scotland with Nodier. 1821: first marine watercolours, stylistically closer to Gericault and Eugene Lami than to British models. 1824: shares a studio with Xavier Leprince at Honfleur; Salon debut with Vue de la plage d'Honfleur, Interieur du port de Trouville and Ouragan devant Dieppe; first-class medal. 1826: Leprince dies Tuesday 26 December 1826; Isabey settles at Saint-Simeon. 1827: Salon, four marine oils, first-class medal; meets Paul Huet. Work: small coastal sketches and watercolours, topographical accuracy, English watercolour influence arriving.

Phase 2 -- England, Turner and Bonington, and the marine reputation, 1825-1833. 1825: trip to England with Delacroix and Bonington to study Turner and the English watercolourists; Delacroix takes the grand manner, Isabey takes fine detail and topographical accuracy. From Bonington he adopts the light ground. 1830: official artist on the Algiers expedition; panorama of the Toulon roads and the French landing camp at Sidi Ferruch. 1831: refuses the Morocco mission; Delacroix goes instead and makes the 100+ works that become classics of Orientalism. 1832: court painter, Legion of Honour. 1833: Six Marines lithographs; Harbour at Low Tide bought by the State. The low-tide shore becomes his signature subject.

Phase 3 -- Court painter and history painting, 1839-1855. Large-scale historical marines and royal commissions: Battle of Texel (1839), Return of Napoleon (1841-42, Versailles), Queen Victoria at Treport (1843-44), Duke of Alba at Rotterdam (1844), Fish Market, Dieppe (1845). First-class medal at the Exposition Universelle 1855 for Le Depart a la chasse de Louis XIII. Paintings occasionally monumental (up to about 3 m); the 1840 Lake Erie naval combat was so large he co-signed it with his pupil Morel-Fatio, and its present location is unknown. 1845: meets Jongkind at The Hague, brings him to Paris (1846); joint Normandy trips 1850 (Fecamp, Yport, Saint-Valery-en-Caux, Etretat with Jehannot and Eugene Ciceri). 1850: Boudin enrols in his Paris studio.

Phase 4 -- Storms, wrecks, and the grey interior, 1851-1869. Repeated storm and wreck machines: Storm on the Coast at Etretat (1851), the Wreck (1854 Detroit; c.1860 Barnsley), Hurricane Before Saint Malo (1860), Shipwreck of the Emily (1865), After the Storm (1869 Hermitage). Parallel track of grey-toned interiors and history: Le Cabinet d'un alchimiste (1841), church interiors, the Duel pictures (1863, 1867), the Massacre of Saint-Bartholomew (c.1866). 1858: the burning of the steamer Austria (242 x 430 cm, Bordeaux) -- a contemporary disaster, painted the year it happened. 1863: joins about 180 painters (with Delacroix and Corot) petitioning for Salon reform -- the episode leading to the Salon des Refuses.

Phase 5 -- Late watercolour, the turn from oil, 1870-1886. After about 1878 he stops exhibiting at the Salon; he gives up oil for watercolour and gouache worked on the motif (fr.wikipedia; Lespinasse). Rapid notations mixing broad gouache impasto; blues opposed to brown-red tones, lifted by white spots. These late watercolours are described as announcing Impressionism in style and in the sites he paints. He is a founder member of the Societe des Aquarellistes Francais (1879). Still painting at 82: Debarquement de la peche signed and dated 1882; A Church Interior in the Met is dated 1866 in the inventory, not 1886 as one low-authority source claims (see C8).

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is a machine for catching the moment from the waterline, and the moment is always somebody else's weather. He starts from the opposite problem to Lepoittevin's: not a painter who found one cliff and rotated genres across it, but a painter who found one viewpoint -- low, on the shore, at the tide's edge -- and let the subject change around it: fishermen, wrecks, queens, Napoleon's ashes, church interiors, duels. The low-tide shore is the thesis: the sea is most interesting when it is not there, because then the work of the shore becomes visible -- boats being refitted, nets mended, fish sold, anchors rusting. The white ground is the second thesis: the paper is the light, and everything painted is a stain that lets the light through. The sketch is the third: a rapid notation is a finished work, not a preparation for one, and the critics who called him an improviser were describing the system accurately and disapproving of it at the same time. What makes him usable for generative work is not the marine subject -- that is the biography -- but the staging system underneath it: one low drifting viewpoint, one grey tonal field with a single warm intrusion, a loose wash that a few precise details snap into focus, and a tide that acts as a clock. And the transmission is the point: the white ground, the sketch as finished work, the low-tide subject and the Normandy coast pass through Boudin and Jongkind to Monet, so Isabey's pictures are the relay station between British Romantic watercolour and Impressionism, not a destination in themselves.

## POSITIONS
Tags: [FROM RECORD] = directly evidenced in a source read; [INFERRED] = read from the work and the record; [ABSENCE] = stated plainly. P1-P16 are his own practice and life; P17 is inferred; P18 is an absence stated plainly; P19-P20 are reception.

P1 [FROM RECORD]: Salon debut at 20 with a first-class medal, and a sixty-year Salon career. He exhibited at the Paris Salon from 1824, sending Vue de la plage d'Honfleur, Interieur du port de Trouville and Ouragan devant Dieppe, which won him a gold/first-class medal (fr.wikipedia; Ongpin says first-class medal in genre and marine painting; soiree says first-class medal in marine painting). He went on exhibiting into the late 1870s: fr.wikipedia says he stopped exhibiting after 1878; Barnsley says he last exhibited at the Salon in 1877. The one-year discrepancy is unresolved in the sources read. He also won a first-class medal at the Exposition universelle of 1855 for Le Depart a la chasse de Louis XIII (fr.wikipedia) and first-class medals in 1824, 1827 and 1855 per the old Met catalogue (search snippet).

P2 [FROM RECORD]: He wanted to be a sailor and his father overruled him. En.wikipedia: "Originally, he wanted to be a sailor, but his father insisted that he study painting; a turnabout from the usual situation where the family opposes an artistic career in favor of something more practical." Soiree agrees: he enjoyed rowing, learned to sail, "at one point aspired to become a sailor or other naval profession"; a visit with his father to Le Havre exposed him to light on sea and shore, and coastal sketches reconciled his maritime interests with his father's wishes.

P3 [FROM RECORD]: He found Etretat at 17 and stayed half a year. Fr.wikipedia: "On lui attribue la decouverte du site d'Etretat, ou il s'installe durant six mois en 1820 pour peindre chez un capitaine des garde-cotes et ou il revient les annees suivantes avec Bonington et Camille Roqueplan", citing Adolphe Joanne's 1872 guide (p. 102) and Abbe Cochet's Etretat, son passe, son present, son avenir (1853, p. 7). The Morgan Library note agrees: "Isabey first ventured west of Paris to Normandy with his father in 1820." Normandy Tourism: "It began with Isabey in the 1820s." Note the two framings: the French article says he settled there for six months in 1820; the Morgan note says he first ventured there with his father in 1820. Both agree on 1820 as the first year.

P4 [FROM RECORD]: The Leprince studio at Honfleur, then Saint-Simeon. En.wikipedia: "After studying with his father and copying the Old Masters at the Louvre, he began sharing a studio with the landscape painter Xavier Leprince at Honfleur in 1824, then moved to Saint-Simeon after Leprince's untimely death." Fr.wikipedia: from 1824 he stayed at Honfleur with Leprince; he settled at Saint-Simeon in 1826. Leprince died 26 December 1826, so the move to Saint-Simeon dates to 1826, not immediately after the 1824 sharing. The 1827 Salon views of the region won him a first-class medal, and there he met Paul Huet (fr.wikipedia).

P5 [FROM RECORD]: England 1825 with Delacroix and Bonington, and the white ground. Ongpin: "The three artists traveled together to England in 1825, where Isabey was able to study the work of Turner and the English watercolourists. From Bonington, Isabey adopted the practice of drawing his watercolours on a light or white ground, instead of the dark ground commonly used in France at the time." Soiree: he "travelled with them to England in 1825 to study William Turner and other English watercolourists. Whereas Delacroix assimilated the English painters' grand manner, Isabey focused on their fine detail and topographical accuracy." Christie's lot essay adds that Bonington was "instrumental in persuading Eugene to become a professional artist despite his father's objections" (citing Pierre Miquel), and that his initial marine watercolours of 1821 are stylistically closer to Gericault and Eugene Lami than to British models, "although by mid-decade his work is almost indistinguishable from that of Bonington."

P6 [FROM RECORD]: Algiers 1830, and the Morocco refusal that handed Delacroix his subject. En.wikipedia: in 1831 he was chosen to accompany the Comte de Mornay's diplomatic mission to Morocco but refused, having just returned from Algiers, where he had painted scenes of the Royal Navy's campaign; "He was replaced by his friend, Eugene Delacroix, who created over 100 works that are now considered classics of Orientalism." Fr.wikipedia: in 1830 he did the Africa campaign, painting a panorama of the Toulon roadstead and the French landing camp at Sidi Ferruch; in 1831 he was chosen for the Mornay mission and refused. The old Met catalogue calls him royal marine painter on the 1830 Algiers expedition. One source conflict: ru.wikipedia (via search) says he visited Algeria in 1821; the French and English articles and the Met catalogue all say 1830, and 1830 is used here.

P7 [FROM RECORD]: Court painter to Louis-Philippe; Legion of Honour knight 1832, officer 1852. En.wikipedia: "Shortly after, Isabey became a court painter for King Louis-Philippe and was named a Knight in the Legion d'Honneur in 1832." Fr.wikipedia: knight 1832, promoted officer 22 January 1852. Soiree: in 1852 he was made Officer of the Legion of Honour and donated a family portrait by Gerard and David's 1794 autoportrait to the Louvre. Viel-Castel's memoirs complain about large commissions awarded to Isabey by de Nieuwerkerke, one reported at 6,000 francs (soiree).

P8 [FROM RECORD]: The "marine without sea" -- low tide, not breaking waves. Fr.wikipedia, style section: "Eugene Isabey est celebre pour avoir peint des scenes de marines 'sans mer'. Il prefere peindre la misere des marins a maree basse, que la vague bondissante de la mer contre les falaises." Roger de Beauvoir's 1836 Salon review (quoted in full in fr.wikipedia, see Q5) makes the same point in period language: if he leaves the port it is rarely for the high sea; leave him before the rusted anchors, the old boats, the mussel fishermen. His best-known early state purchase fits the habit: Harbour at Low Tide (1833), bought by the State (Ongpin); Low Tide Harbor (1833) in the NiceArtGallery summary.

P9 [FROM RECORD]: A fluid, loose, stained style set against precise detail. Fr.wikipedia: his "fluide" and "lache" style "est fait d'effets taches et d'execution rapide, qu'il oppose a des details precis." Barnsley: "a swift and chaotic manner of painting, a method of execution which produced works full of movement, vigour and expression suited to the waves and storms of his seascapes. He also developed a dark palette with light touches as highlights for his more dramatic scenes." En.wikipedia: "He was especially skillful at rendering subtleties in darker colors; which might be called a form of grisaille."

P10 [FROM RECORD]: The sketch as an autonomous work, against the critics. Ongpin: "the artist seems to have regarded [his oil and watercolour sketches] as autonomous works; a view at odds with that of some contemporary writers. In his review of the Salon of 1844, for example, the reactionary critic Thore-Burger noted that 'M. Eugene Isabey is another of these improvisers who have no time to work at a picture... A sketch may often have more style and vigour than a finished painting. But these spontaneous studies should only be motifs destined to undergo a stricter and more thorough elaboration... M. Isabey is more successful in his small unpretentious marine pictures.'" His sketches stayed in demand anyway: one sold in 1882 for 4,400 francs (Ongpin).

P11 [FROM RECORD]: The late renunciation of oil for watercolour and gouache on the motif. Fr.wikipedia: "A la fin de sa vie, Eugene Isabey renonce a la peinture a l'huile pour se consacrer a l'aquarelle et a la gouache, travaillant sur le motif. Ses aquarelles annoncent l'impressionnisme tant par le style que par les lieux qu'il decouvre." Lespinasse (quoted in fr.wikipedia, see Q8) adds that he mixed broad gouache impasto, blues against brown-red tones set off by white touches. He was a founder member of the Societe des Aquarellistes Francais, established 1879 (Ongpin; en.wikipedia).

P12 [FROM RECORD]: The teacher of the pre-Impressionists. Fr.wikipedia's Eleves list names 15 pupils: Louis Bentabole, Eugene Boudin, Jean-Baptiste Henri Durand-Brager, Stanislas Gorin, Adolphe Hervier, Fritz Hildebrandt, Charles Hoguet, Johan Barthold Jongkind, Charles Euphrasie Kuwasseg, Jean-Pierre-Theodore Lebeuffe, George Loring Brown, Jean-Baptiste Messager, Clara Montalba, Charles-Theodore Sauvageot, Theodore Alexander Weber. En.wikipedia names Boudin, Jongkind and Durand-Brager. Ongpin names Ciceri, Hervier and Ziem as further pupils, and says he may have met Boudin as early as 1844 and met Jongkind on a visit to Holland in 1846; Jongkind then became his student in Paris. Fr.wikipedia: on the 1845 Hague trip Isabey met Jongkind and urged him to come to Paris; Jongkind settled in 1846 place Pigalle, "dans la maison ou Isabey a son atelier, lieu de rencontres des peintres et des ecrivains." The meeting of Claude Monet and Jongkind took place at Isabey's house in Honfleur in 1862 (fr.wikipedia).

P13 [FROM RECORD]: The history painter in parallel, including the return of Napoleon's ashes. En.wikipedia: one of his best-known paintings, done in 1840, depicts the return of Napoleon's remains from Saint Helena aboard the Belle Poule; in later years he turned from marine painting to historical scenes, "usually of a violent nature, such as massacres, duels and robberies." Ongpin: in later years he received official commissions and produced grandiose history pictures, "typified by the Return of the Ashes of Napoleon at Versailles." Fr.wikipedia lists L'Embarquement de Ruyter, William de Witt and Tentation de saint Antoine among the works, and Arrivee du duc d'Albe a Rotterdam (1844, Musee d'Orsay).

P14 [FROM RECORD]: The oarsman: sketching from rowing boats, "admiral of the lake". Soiree: "Both Isabey and Morel-Fatio practised sketching from rowing boats around Paris, making frequent stops to draw en plein air, a marked departure from atelier-only production. Isabey became an accomplished oarsman and was locally nicknamed 'admiral of the lake' at Enghien. His cottage on the lake adjoined that of Frederic de Reiset, Louvre conservator and mayor of Enghien-les-Bains; Princess Mathilde and de Nieuwerkerke resided a short distance away at Saint-Gratien. Isabey and de Nieuwerkerke regularly sailed or rowed together." Horace Vernet, whose studio was nearby, caricatured Isabey as an owl perched on a branch after noting the cottage stood on four large poplar beams at the lake's edge. "Vernet, Isabey, and Morel-Fatio were among the founders of the Le Havre Regatta Society in 1839."

P15 [FROM RECORD]: The Lake Erie collaboration, signed by two men. Soiree: "His depiction of the naval combat on Lake Erie (1840) was so large that he collaborated with his pupil and friend Morel-Fatio; the work bears both signatures. Unfortunately, its present location is unknown."

P16 [FROM RECORD]: Varengeville, widowhood, remarriage, gout, Montevrain. Barnsley: "In 1857 Isabey settled permanently at Varengeville, just to the west of Dieppe on the Normandy coast. His wife was in ill-health and the couple hoped the sea air would be of benefit to her, but she passed away in 1862. Isabey married Marie-Emma Marizot two years later... By 1874, however, it was necessary for him to spend more time on the southern coast of France, where the climate was kinder to his gout. He last exhibited at the Salon in 1877 and died at his house in Montevrain, east of Paris, in 1886." CONFLICT: the Morgan note says he moved to Varengeville, near Dieppe, in 1856; Barnsley says 1857. Fr.wikipedia says he married Laure Lebreton in June 1832 (died 1862), daughter Marie born 1833, and married Emma Morizot in second nuptials in June 1864. De.wikipedia (search snippet) says he retired in 1881 to his country estate near Lagny, where he died in 1886.

P17 [INFERRED]: The bridge figure, not the destination. The through-line of the reception is that Isabey connects British Romantic watercolour to French pre-Impressionism without himself becoming an Impressionist: Ongpin ("Isabey's work also provides a link between the Romantic tradition and that of the pre-Impressionists"); Christie's ("directly linked the British Romantics to the proto-Impressionists Eugene Boudin and Johan Barthold Jongkind"); soiree ("his work also forms a link between neoclassical academicism (for example Ingres) and the approaches that anticipated impressionism"); fr.wikipedia (his watercolours "annoncent l'impressionnisme"). The inference: his historical importance is transmissive -- the white ground, the sketch as finished work, the low-tide subject, the Normandy coast -- carried by Boudin and Jongkind to Monet, rather than a style of his own that others copied.

P18 [ABSENCE, stated plainly]: No published words of his own were found. No letters, diary, interview, manifesto or statement of intent by Eugene Isabey surfaced in any source read or searched. Barnsley states it outright: "There are few personal documents which give us an insight into Isabey's character, but what we can glean from the writings of others is that he was highly sociable with many friends amongst artists and the wealthy of society." The INHA (Institut national d'histoire de l'art) holds a "fonds d'archives Eugene Isabey: dessins et manuscrits" (fr.wikipedia), which was not consulted. Delacroix's correspondence files include letters TO Isabey (ArchiveGrid: "Other correspondents include Eugene Isabey (undated)"), but none was read and none is quoted here. Everything about his aims must be inferred from the pictures, the pupils and the critics.

P19 [RECEPTION]: The best marine painter of his time, and the "Horace Vernet of marine painting". Barnsley: "he was widely regarded as the best marine painter of his time, excelling in his speciality of coastal views." Soiree: "Often described as the 'Horace Vernet of marine painting,' Isabey's work also forms a link between neoclassical academicism (for example Ingres) and the approaches that anticipated impressionism." Gerald Schurr (fr.wikipedia, see Q9) calls him "in his time, and without doubt still today, considered the best of the Romantic marine painters."

P20 [RECEPTION]: The 2012 Louvre revival and the civic afterlife. Fr.wikipedia: solo exhibition "Eugene Isabey (1803-1886), par les ruelles et par les greves", Musee du Louvre, 5 July-17 September 2012, curator Christophe Leribault; earlier solo shows at the Pavillon des Arts, Paris (May-June 1966), Chateau de Dieppe (June-September 1966), Fogg Art Museum, Cambridge MA (November-December 1967). Hommages: a school group at Montevrain bears his name; streets bear his name in Etretat, Le Havre, Trouville-sur-Mer and Paris; an annual race at Dieppe racecourse is the Prix Eugene-Isabey. He is buried in Pere-Lachaise cemetery, 20th division, Paris (fr.wikipedia; en.wikipedia category "Burials at Pere Lachaise Cemetery").
