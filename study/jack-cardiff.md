**Site:** Jack Cardiff OBE (b. Friday 18 September 1914, Great Yarmouth, Norfolk, England; d. Wednesday 22 April 2009, Ely, Cambridgeshire, England, age 94, of age-related causes -- the cause rests on IMDb alone), British cinematographer, film and television director and photographer; the painter who learned to shoot. ## 1. Great Yarmouth and the travelling stage

Jack Cardiff was born 18 September 1914 in Great Yarmouth, Norfolk, the son of Florence and John Joseph Cardiff, music-hall entertainers; his father had played centre-half for Watford football club. He had no stable childhood home: the family travelled town to town on the music-hall circuit, he went to a different school every week until about age eleven or twelve, and between shows stayed with his grandmother in Watford, at 7 Sutton Road. His education, in the Powell and Pressburger pages' phrase, came to him ad hoc -- unable to attend a single school for more than a few weeks at a time. [C33613]

That is the first fact of the life, and it explains the second: a boy raised in digs, watching his parents work a room every night, learned early that a performance is a place you build and then strike. He described his childhood as very happy and his parents as quite loving; they performed as comedians, and his father once worked with Charles Chaplin. The travelling life also gave him the one thing a cameraman needs most and cannot be taught in a studio: he had seen, before he was twelve, what a crowd looks like from the stage and what a stage looks like from the crowd, night after night, in a different hall every week. [C33614]

**Takeaway:** Cardiff's eye was formed before he ever touched a camera -- by a childhood with no fixed room, in which every evening's world was assembled, lit, played and taken down again.

## 2. Child actor, 1918-1927

He started acting at age four, in the music hall and in silent films: My Son, My Son (1918), Billy's Rose (1922) -- in which his parents played his character's parents -- The Loves of Mary, Queen of Scots (1923) and Tip Toes (1927). Encyclopedia.com puts it plainly: "Child actor from age four to fourteen." [C33615]

The child actor matters because he spent his first decade on the other side of the lens, learning what it feels like to be lit, to be framed, to be told where to stand -- and then, when the stage career stalled, he walked around to the camera's side and never went back. Most cinematographers arrive at the camera from photography; Cardiff arrived from being photographed. He knew what a face looks like when the light is wrong because he had stood in it. [C33616]

**Takeaway:** The cameraman who had been a child actor knew the subject's side of the frame first -- and spent the rest of his career lighting people the way he would have wanted to be lit.

## 3. The Informer: a runner at thirteen going on fourteen

His first film job was on the silent version of The Informer (1928), as an office boy and runner -- fetching drinks and running messages, including giving director Arthur Robison Vichy water all day. He was, in his own words, "two weeks short of 14". Wikipedia says "at 15, he began working as a camera assistant, clapper boy and production runner for British International Pictures, including Alfred Hitchcock's The Skin Game (1931)"; Cardiff's own account and Encyclopedia.com both say 1928, and this study prefers his dating. The Hitchcock edge is real either way: at fifteen he was a camera assistant on The Skin Game, eighteen years before he shot Under Capricorn for the same director. [C33617]

He had no interest in photography at all when he joined the camera department. He became a "number boy" because he noticed the camera lads travelled abroad a lot -- France, Italy, Germany, Egypt -- and "That's the job for me." The joke, in his own telling, was that in two years the nearest he got to going abroad was the Isle of Wight one afternoon. The motive is worth recording because it is the opposite of the romantic version: he did not join the camera to make pictures, he joined it to travel, and the pictures came later, as a by-product of looking. [C33618]

**Takeaway:** The career begins with a practical motive -- travel, not art -- and the art arrives because the man who wanted to see the world had to learn to look at it.

## 4. The ladder: number boy, clapper boy, focus puller

The ladder was number boy, then clapper boy when sound arrived, then focus puller, then camera operator, eased along by lucky breaks when operators were unavailable: "Usually something happens where the director wants so many cameras and there aren't that many operators so they give you a camera to operate a bit." An early break as a lighting cameraman came at Elstree, when he was asked to light a test of child actor Freddie Bartholomew (for David Copperfield) when the cameraman fell ill; the test was liked, but the returning chief cameraman was furious. Cardiff dates his confidence to that moment: "that was the moment I felt I could do things here." [C33619]

When the British International Pictures studios at Elstree caught fire, Cardiff, with colleagues Ted Moore and Skeets Kelly, rescued cameras from the camera room. One uninsured French camera earned him three or four days in Paris -- his first trip abroad, paid for by a fire. By the mid-1930s he was a camera operator, working mostly for London Films (Alexander Korda) at Denham Studios, and had become camera operator for director Rene Clair on The Ghost Goes West (1935, photographed by Harold Rosson). He deliberately stayed as an operator for a time rather than taking an early break as a cameraman, working with the Hollywood cameramen Korda brought over, to gain experience: "I could have got an early break as a cameraman but I wasn't sort of confident that I would be ready to photograph anything. I thought I would stay as an operator working with good cameramen." [C33620]

**Takeaway:** Cardiff climbed slowly on purpose -- operator beside the best, rather than cameraman before he was ready -- and the patience is visible in everything he shot afterwards: nothing in his work looks hurried, because the man had already waited.

## 5. The Technicolor interview: the dunce who knew Rembrandt

In 1936, aged 22 and a camera operator at Denham, Cardiff was interviewed by the American Technicolor company, which was setting up in England after the Hollywood success of Becky Sharp (1935). Older, more experienced operators emerged from the interviews "dazed and disoriented" by technical questions -- inverse-square law, lux, lumens, lamberts. Cardiff told them outright he was not their man: "I was a mathematical dunce." What he knew of light came from studying painting -- the Old Masters and J. M. W. Turner. [C33621]

In the interview he was asked which side of the face Rembrandt used as key light (he answered the left, frame left -- by his own later admission partly a guess, a bluff), which painters did beautiful interiors (Vermeer and Pieter de Hooch, who he said used a camera obscura), and enthused about the candlelight of Georges de La Tour. He was chosen. Asked later what made him a good cameraman, he replied: "My love of painting and the old masters." [C33622]

**Takeaway:** The hiring decision is the whole method in miniature: a technical process hired an artistic eye over technical hands. Cardiff never became a mathematician of light; he remained a painter of it, and Technicolor was better for it.

## 6. The 600-pound camera: Rennahan, Kalmus, and the first English master

The plan was to send him to Hollywood to train on the new three-strip Technicolor camera, but the trip never happened: Technicolor instead opened a laboratory in England, taking over a converted stable on the Denham Studios lot, and Cardiff was trained there under American cinematographer Ray Rennahan, supervised by colour consultant Natalie Kalmus, who "had final word on virtually all-creative decisions concerning the new process." The Powell and Pressburger biography calls him Technicolor's "first technician" in England; the ASC calls him "the first English cameraman to master this complex new camera". [C33623]

The machine itself is the constraint that shapes everything in this study. The three-strip Technicolor camera weighed about 600 pounds, and three-strip Technicolor required sets to be virtually flooded with light. Technicolor cameras were only rented out with Technicolor-trained crew, because the process was so complex. Every Cardiff colour decision -- the underlighting, the pastel backings, the fog filter, the Brute -- is a negotiation with a camera the size of a piano that needed a flood of light and could not be moved quickly. [C33624]

**Takeaway:** The tool was a piano that needed a flood. Cardiff's whole style is the art of making a piano dance: light, fast, and apparently effortless, against a machine built for none of those things.

## 7. Wings of the Morning and the coronation

His first assignment as a Technicolor camera operator was filming the coronation of King George VI in 1937. Wings of the Morning (1937, dir. Harold D. Schuster, starring Henry Fonda and Annabella) was the first film in Britain shot in Technicolor. The credit is disputed and this study states the ambiguity: Wikipedia and the BSC say Cardiff was camera operator and Ray Rennahan the credited cinematographer; Film Reference and altfg list Cardiff as cinematographer alongside Rennahan and Henry Imus. The weight of evidence (BSC plus Wikipedia) is that Rennahan was the credited cinematographer and Cardiff the operator -- but the shared-credit version is widespread, so do not present Cardiff as sole DP. [C33625]

The Four Feathers (1939) is a cleaner correction: Cardiff was camera operator, NOT cinematographer. Georges Perinal was director of photography. Any list of Cardiff's "early features as DP" that includes it is wrong, and this study does not carry it. [C33626]

**Takeaway:** In the apprenticeship years the credits blur -- operator, technician, shared credit -- and the study records the blur instead of sharpening it into a false first.

## 8. World Window: ten-minute films around the world

The World Window travelogue series (1937-40) was commissioned by wealthy amateur 16mm filmmakers Count von Keller and his wife, who arrived at Technicolor in a Cadillac coupe in autumn 1936 wanting a three-strip cameraman for ambitious travel films. Cardiff went with them, crew and fully loaded van, on a worldwide series of ten-minute films released theatrically by United Artists. Locations ranged from Rome, Florence and Naples through Africa to the Far East, with several films on India. His assistant was Chris Challis, later a distinguished cinematographer himself -- the man who would shoot Gone to Earth for the Archers in Cardiff's idiom. [C33627]

Back in Britain between travelogues he could only get work as a Technicolor technician or as an operator, apart from a couple of weeks instructing principal cameramen how to light for colour. The travelogues were the school: a cameraman alone with a 600-pound camera, no studio, no second unit, and a world that would not wait for the light to be right. [C33628]

**Takeaway:** World Window is the hidden apprenticeship -- ten-minute films, one cameraman, the whole world as a location -- and it is where Cardiff learned to solve a lighting problem with whatever was at hand.

## 9. St Peter's by hand, the Taj Mahal by day

Two World Window feats, from Magic Hour as quoted by the ASC, show the method at its most literal. Inside St Peter's Basilica (700 feet square, 400 feet high), where slow Technicolor stock needed 600 foot-candles at f/1.5 and he had fewer than a dozen small lamps, he cranked the film through by hand with the loading handle, twenty feet at a time, reversed and repeated -- a twenty-minute exposure per pass. For the Taj Mahal in Temples of India, he faked a starry night in daylight: pinhead-sized pieces of Plasticine with silver paper stuck on wires between two poles, reflectors behind the camera catching the sun, plus a pale-blue filter and underexposure for day-for-night. [C33629]

Both feats are the same trick seen from two sides: when the light is not enough, add time; when the night is not there, build it out of wire, silver paper and a filter. The camera is not a recorder here; it is a workshop. The St Peter's exposure is twenty minutes of hand-cranking for a single shot -- the opposite of the fast, nimble camera of the later ballet films, and the proof that Cardiff had already learned to make the heavy machine do what the scene needed, however long it took. [C33630]

**Takeaway:** Constraint is the material. Too little light becomes a twenty-minute exposure; no night becomes silver paper on wires. The mechanism is always the same: build the missing thing out of what is in the room.

## 10. War: Western Approaches and the lifeboat

When the Second World War began, Cardiff worked as a cinematographer on public information films, including a number of films on India, and for the Crown Film Unit. Western Approaches (1944), directed by Pat Jackson for the Crown Film Unit, was his first assignment as cinematographer on a feature-length wartime documentary -- a film about the Merchant Navy and the struggle to protect Allied shipping in the seas west of Britain. Altfg calls it "Cardiff's first solo effort as a feature-film cinematographer". [C33631]

It was shot almost entirely at sea in a lifeboat, with the mammoth near-200-pound three-strip Technicolor camera in a custom lighter sound blimp. The lifeboat held the director, Cardiff, his assistant, the soundman, script girl, camera and sound gear, a portable radio transmitter, a water barrel, sandwiches -- and twenty-two merchant seamen -- every day for six months. The film was scheduled for six weeks and took over a year; Cardiff called it "the most despairingly difficult ordeal I have ever known". Once, on the only scene shot on a stage, the camera booth in a tank overturned and camera and cameraman went into eight feet of water; the camera was rescued, bathed in oil, stripped at Technicolor, and the soaked exposed film developed unharmed. [C33632]

Two earlier cinematographer credits exist and are recorded here so the "first feature" claim stays honest: Delhi (1939, Hans Nieter), a World Window documentary short, and The Great Mr. Handel (1942, dir. Norman Walker), which altfg says Cardiff shot "paired with Claude Friese-Greene". Cardiff himself called A Matter of Life and Death his first feature film, and the Powell and Pressburger biography calls it "his first feature film as principal lighting cameraman". This study says first major feature, not first film as DP. [C33633]

**Takeaway:** The war film is a stress test: a 200-pound camera in a lifeboat for six months, and the film survives a dunking in eight feet of water. After that, a studio set is easy -- and the cameraman who has shot at sea trusts the machine because he has already nearly drowned with it.

## 11. The animal heads: Colonel Blimp and the audition

The turning point was second-unit Technicolor work on Powell and Pressburger's The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp (1943). The director of photography was Georges Perinal, not Cardiff; Cardiff is credited as one of the "Cameramen", alongside Geoffrey Unsworth and Harold Haysom, with W. Percy Day on process shots, and IMDb lists his credit as "Technicolor cameraman for the Technicolor Company". At least one secondary source credits the film's Technicolor cinematography to Cardiff outright; that is wrong, and this study does not repeat it. [C33634]

As a Technicolor employee, Cardiff was routinely asked to shoot inserts or additional photography on Technicolor features. On Blimp he found himself lighting a wall of stuffed animal heads. He said: "I spent more time than usual setting the lights without casting multiple shadows, then while looking through the camera I heard a voice say, 'Interesting,' and there was Michael Powell. He turned slowly toward me and said, 'Would you like to photograph my next picture?'" His memoir gives the same story with more detail: "I was doing a rather difficult insert for a change. It was a wall with a lot of animal heads with horns. Every light I would put on would make multiple shadows. Luckily, second unit can take more time, and I did a pretty good job on this." Documentary director Craig McCall adds that Cardiff "was brought in at the end of The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp only to do pickup shots, like pipes and ashtrays. But Jack also got to shoot a whole interesting sequence of heads of animals on walls -- and Michael Powell was behind him when he was lighting them." [C33635]

The mechanical core of the anecdote is a lighting problem, not a camera move: a wall of horned animal heads, where every added light throws a second set of shadows. The fix was time -- second unit could afford to place and re-place lights until the shadows merged into one set. No source describes the exact lamp arrangement, so it is not reconstructed here. [C33636]

**Takeaway:** A career is won on an insert. The mechanism is mastery of a small, hard lighting problem, watched by the right person, with the time second unit allows -- and the hire that follows is the whole Archers run.

## 12. A Matter of Life and Death: heaven drained, earth saturated

That next picture was A Matter of Life and Death (1946) -- his first feature as principal lighting cameraman, and by his own account his first feature film. Powell first wanted Erwin Hillier (cinematographer of A Canterbury Tale, 1944) to share the photography with Cardiff; Hillier declined, and Powell writes, "I gave the whole job to Jack." McCall's version differs in detail -- at first Powell offered to hire Cardiff alongside a much more senior cameraman, but the other cameraman refused to share a credit -- and both agree the job ended up Cardiff's alone. Between the offer and the shoot, Cardiff went to Egypt for second-unit work on Caesar and Cleopatra; a telegram from Powell recalled him with three weeks to go. His own summary of his position: "I had worked for Technicolor for ten years and had more experience of the problems posed with Technicolor than anyone else in Europe, yet this was my first major feature film." He engaged Geoffrey Unsworth as camera operator and Chris Challis -- who had previously been his operator -- as focus puller. [C33637]

Cardiff had imagined heaven would be photographed in colour. Powell replied: "Because everyone will expect heaven to be in color, I'm doing it in black and white." The scenes on earth were to be in colour. This reverses the effect in The Wizard of Oz (sepia Kansas, colour Oz): here earth is the saturated world and the afterlife is the drained one. [C33638]

How the monochrome heaven was executed is itself disputed, and the dispute is recorded rather than resolved. Wikipedia: the Other World scenes were filmed in three-strip Technicolor, but colour was not added during printing, giving a pearly hue to the black-and-white shots -- a process cited in the screen credits as "Colour and Dye-Monochrome Processed in Technicolor". Powell-Pressburger.org: it was Cardiff who suggested using Technicolor rather than standard black-and-white stock for the Heaven scenes, to give them a pearly look, and also to make the gradual transitions between colour and black-and-white possible. Cardiff himself told Netribution the heaven sequences were shot with "black and white cameras and black and white film", with only the penultimate shot done on a Technicolor camera merged into colour -- and added that he had never shot black-and-white before: "I didn't tell anybody that I hadn't photographed anything in black and white." Both accounts agree on the pearly result and on the photographic dissolves between "Technicolor Dye-Monochrome" (the Other World) and Three-Strip Technicolor (Earth), used several times -- on a close-up of a flower, on Niven's tormented face, on the beaches. The black-and-white heaven is also formally rigid where earth is fluid: "the black and white formality and compositional rigidity of heaven offer a marked contrast" to the earth scenes. [C33639]

The opening sequence sets the method: a desperate radio transmission between Niven in the burning plane and Kim Hunter in the tower -- "the play of shadows and the tracking camera on Hunter in the tower, and the mix of color on Niven in the fiery plane, propel you into the tense drama from the first frame." Production ran 2 September to 2 December 1945, used 29 sets, and cost an estimated 320,000 pounds. There was a nine-month wait for film stock and Technicolor cameras because they were being used by the US Army to make training films. The film was chosen for the first ever Royal Film Performance, 1 November 1946 at the Empire Theatre, London, in the presence of George VI. [C33640]

**Takeaway:** The two-world colour rule: one world saturated, the other drained -- and the drained world is still shot on the colour process, with the colour withheld at print, so the monochrome has a pearly body no black-and-white stock gives. Transitions are photographic dissolves between the two states, not cuts. Colour is not decoration here; it is the metaphysics.

## 13. Ethel: the escalator and its three numbers

The escalator linking Earth to the Other World was called "Operation Ethel" by the firm of engineers who built it under the aegis of the London Passenger Transport Board; it took three months to make and cost 3,000 pounds. The stairway was lined with giant statues of famous people (Solomon, Voltaire, Plato, Abraham Lincoln). The full shot of the stairway was completed by hanging miniatures -- the camera sees the built escalator in the foreground and a miniature extension above it. [C33641]

The number of steps is a three-way conflict, recorded, not resolved. Wikipedia: "Ethel" had 106 steps, each 20 feet (6.1 m) wide, driven by a 12 horsepower engine. TCM, citing Scott Salwolke's The Films of Michael Powell and the Archers: the escalator "weighed eighty-five tons and had 266 twenty-foot steps". Powell-Pressburger.org: "It had 100 20 foot-wide steps (the crew nicknamed it 'Ethel')". Three figures -- 106, 266, 100 -- from three sources; the 20-foot width is agreed everywhere. This study uses none of the three as settled. [C33642]

Whether "Ethel" was noisy is a second conflict. It has been claimed that the noise of the machinery prevented recording the soundtrack live and all scenes with the escalator were dubbed in post-production; however, in an interview incorporated into the Carlton DVD release, Cardiff recalls his surprise that it operated completely silently. Both versions are on record; Cardiff's is first-hand. [C33643]

**Takeaway:** Even the machine's measurements disagree -- 106, 100, 266 -- and the study keeps the disagreement. A generative piece does not need the true number; it needs the escalator as a device: a stairway between two worlds, built by engineers, named by a crew, and finished with a miniature.

## 14. Black Narcissus: a Himalaya built in Surrey

Filming began 16 May 1946 and was completed 22 August. The film was shot primarily at Pinewood Studios, with some scenes in Leonardslee Gardens, West Sussex -- the home of an Indian army retiree, with appropriate trees and plants. Powell, known for location shooting, became fascinated with filming in the studio as much as possible on this film. His stated reason, at the first production meeting: "The atmosphere in this film is everything, and we must create and control it from the start. Wind, the altitude, the beauty of the setting -- it must all be under our control. If we went to India and shot a lot of exteriors, according to the usual plan, and then came back to Pinewood and then tried to match them here, you would have two different kinds of color and two kinds of style." The logistics of transporting Technicolor equipment and stock to and from India were a further reason. [C33644]

Production designer Alfred Junge built the Palace of Mopu -- the convent, a former harem -- as a life-size set of part of the palace on the Pinewood backlot, surrounded by huge cycloramas of painted mountains. It was Junge's eighth collaboration with Powell and Pressburger, and it won him the Oscar for art direction. The film makes extensive use of matte paintings and large-scale landscape paintings, credited to W. Percy Day, to suggest the mountainous Himalayan environment, plus scale models for motion shots of the convent. Cardiff gives great credit to Junge's sets and backings and to Percy "Poppa" Day's mattes and effects work. [C33645]

**Takeaway:** The world is a set, and the set is a painting. The decision to build the Himalaya in Surrey is not a compromise; it is the method -- control the atmosphere, and the mountain will follow.

## 15. Glass paintings and tilted cycloramas

A sheet of glass was hung between the camera and the scene, and the needed view was painted on it in situ -- often over several days, without moving the glass or the camera. The famous example is the bell at the edge of the cliff and the drop into the forest below: it appears hundreds of feet high, but Sister Clodagh (Deborah Kerr) is in fact only about ten feet from the ground. Several such glass paintings are in the film. Wikipedia's before-and-after stills show the same shot as filmed (bare set edge) and with W. Percy Day's matte painting incorporated, creating the illusion of a deep cliff. The film's first view of the palace uses a model to establish the location of the convent. [C33646]

The painted panoramas of the mountains were built at an angle so they could be lit by the sun -- the backdrop itself is a tilted plane, catching real sunlight, which is what sells it as exterior. The Powell and Pressburger biography gives the angle as 45 degrees, to keep the sun on the backdrop all day; the same figure appears in the #782 study's flags as "Black Narcissus cyclorama tilt 35 vs 45 degrees", with 35 degrees sourced (TCM) and 45 degrees not found in any source read this run -- so this study does not state an angle. What is certain is the mechanism: a painted mountain, tilted into real sunlight, photographed as if it were a place. [C33647]

**Takeaway:** The glass painting is the purest Cardiff device: a painting hung between the camera and the world, painted in place over days while the camera does not move. The camera waits for the painting; the painting does the travelling.

## 16. Pastel over photograph

The ever-present painted backdrops, some quite close to the set walls, worried Cardiff. He suggested photographic rather than painted backings: an 8x10 still camera would give prints of high enough resolution for the large backings -- but only in black and white. Junge wanted to paint over them lightly with colour paints. Cardiff disagreed and convinced him that pastel chalk, rubbed in small quantities over the black-and-white print, would hint at colour rather than intrude: "we rubbed blue pastel in the sky and various ochres in the mountain shadows, letting the body of the print make the illusion. It really did look like a real exterior." [C33648]

This pastel-over-photograph technique was used again on the next film, The Red Shoes. The principle is worth naming because it recurs across Cardiff's whole career: photograph first, tint second. The print's body -- its photographic truth -- does the illusion; the colour is only a hint laid over it, never the thing itself. It is the same principle as the drained heaven in A Matter of Life and Death: the colour process underneath, the colour withheld or hinted at the surface. [C33649]

**Takeaway:** Photograph first, tint second. A black-and-white print rubbed with blue pastel is more convincing as a mountain than a painted mountain, because the photograph supplies the truth and the pastel only supplies the weather.

## 17. Painted light: blue shadows, green dawn, one red

In 1946 most of the lamps used were large carbon arcs, not incandescent. The emitted smoke was a constant challenge, and the noise of the arcs and of the rotating feed mechanism that burned the carbon rods evenly forced nearly all the sound interiors to be dubbed. The paradox of the lighting is that three-strip Technicolor required sets to be virtually flooded with light, yet Cardiff paints St. Faith as a place of real as well as moral shadows -- "a gloomy cloister of lust and envy". [C33650]

Cardiff's stated programme for the convent's look: "In order to enhance the dreamlike strangeness and sensuous beauty of the nuns' environment I exaggerated my effects, sometimes using more blue than usual in the shadows; in the dawn sequence where Sister Ruth goes mad, I used soft green in the shadows, not only because this coolness is always evident at dawn, but because the juxtaposition of green and red is uncomfortable and suggestive of tragedy -- like Van Gogh's billiard room at Arles." The palette starts restrained -- the nuns' white robes shining against blue walls and skies -- and changes as Sister Ruth goes off the rails: a copper-pink light is thrown onto the walls through grills and windows, and the scene of Sister Ruth applying red lipstick while holding a vivid red compact is the payoff. The red is the film's single most loaded colour: the lipstick, the red dress, the red compact, and the moment the screen goes red as Sister Ruth passes out -- which Kent Jones notes may represent a genuine neurological phenomenon, a "redout". [C33651]

One hedge is preserved: Jones suggests Cardiff "may have patterned his lighting after Vermeer" -- the phrasing is speculative, and no source in this leg confirms a Vermeer reference from Cardiff himself. It is not stated as fact here. The wind is a lighting-adjacent effect: the sisters' white habits constantly move, "folds and ripples of fabric buffeted by the wind, even inside the palace rooms", giving the robes a fluidity against the static painted world. [C33652]

**Takeaway:** Unnatural colour as psychology. More blue than usual in the shadows, soft green against red at dawn after Van Gogh's Night Cafe, one red object carrying the whole erotic charge. The light is not illumination; it is diagnosis.

## 18. The fog filter at dawn

The climactic dawn exterior -- the struggle between Sister Ruth and Sister Clodagh ending in Ruth's fall -- was shot at real dawn, against Pinewood Works Committee policy, with no latitude for weather contingencies, because it was Deborah Kerr's last contractual day of filming. Just before shooting, Cardiff suggested a fog filter to help the dawn effect. Fog filters were never used in Technicolor in those days; Cardiff used "all the effects, filters and lights, which were taboo". Technicolor telephoned the next day saying everything shot at dawn was no good and should be retaken without the fog filter, and came over with the rushes. The rushes were good -- Powell's verdict in the dark: "Marvelous, Jack, well done." [C33653]

The climax was staged to a pre-existing musical track, as visual choreography. The sequence is the whole Cardiff method in one morning: a real dawn, a forbidden filter, no retake possible, a studio committee overruled, and a telephone call from the colour company the next day saying it must be done again -- answered by the rushes themselves. [C33654]

**Takeaway:** Taboo filters, used once, at dawn. The risk is part of the method: the shot that cannot be retaken is the shot that has to be right, and the only authority that matters is the rushes in the dark.

## 19. The rhubarb leaf and the lost rain scene

One of the last scenes shot was the rains starting, as the nuns leave on mules. Cardiff's idea: a close-up of a huge rhubarb leaf on which just one drop of rain fell, then more and more, until the rains pelted down, as the camera tilted up to the stream of nuns going sadly away. Powell loved it and made it the ending, cutting a filmed Calcutta scene in which Clodagh sobs to Mother Dorothea. [C33655]

Cardiff believed his best photography in the film was a scene that was cut entirely and is now lost: the Indian rains running in rivulets down the window panes, his lighting "close to black and white", the shadows of the rain on the windows running "all over the walls and on the taut faces of the women; the shadows were hard and black". It exists only in his memoir. [C33656]

**Takeaway:** Cheap materials, expensive illusion. A rhubarb leaf and one raindrop stand in for the monsoon; the best shot in the film is the one nobody can see. The mechanism is synecdoche -- one drop, then more, then the rains -- and the loss is part of the record: the cameraman's favourite work is the work that was cut.

## 20. The Red Shoes: soaking up ballet

Powell sent Cardiff to Covent Garden practically every night to watch the Sadler's Wells Ballet -- "I want you to soak up ballet as if your life depended on it" -- after Cardiff confessed he had not been to the ballet since childhood and considered it "so precious -- all those sissies prancing about". He became, in his words, "a balletomane". [C33657]

The centrepiece was an original ballet choreographed by Robert Helpmann, who dances with Moira Shearer; Leonide Massine choreographed his own role as the shoemaker; Brian Easdale composed the score -- the man who is the top pick for #785, and whose music is the clock the whole sequence runs on. Production designer Alfred Junge told Powell "You go too far" about the ballet designs; Powell replaced him with the painter Hein Heckroth. [C33658]

**Takeaway:** Before the camera moves, the cameraman watches. The assignment was not "shoot a ballet" but "soak up ballet as if your life depended on it" -- and the soaking is the technique: the camera learns the dance by watching it every night until the dance is in the body.

## 21. One hundred and twenty paintings first

Heckroth began work six months before production, drafting some 2,000 sketches; for the ballet he painted 120 scenes which were then filmed in sequence. Easdale's score was adapted to match the paintings, each held for whatever length was needed. Cardiff and Challis then adapted the camera angles and lighting indicated in the paintings as the dancers performed to musical playback, and each completed shot replaced the corresponding painting. The score was written to "fit the cinematic design": Easdale composed from 120 cartoon drawings and storyboards approved by Helpmann, assembled in sequence; as filming progressed the drawings were replaced by the completed shots. [C33659]

Principal photography began in June 1947 -- the Nitrate page dates it precisely to 5 June 1947 at the Gare de Lyon in Paris -- and filming also took place in London, Monte Carlo and the Cote d'Azur, with stage and orchestra-pit sequences on sets built at Pinewood. The shoot was planned for fifteen weeks and ran to twenty-four; the budget ballooned from 300,000 pounds to over 505,600 pounds, and Rank forced a 10,000-pound cut to Powell and Pressburger's salaries. The ballet sequence took approximately six weeks to shoot; dancers "were lucky if we ever danced for as long as one minute" at a stretch, so cinematically worked out was the sequence. Christopher Challis -- a distinguished lighting cameraman in his own right -- temporarily demoted himself to operate the camera for Cardiff on The Red Shoes. [C33660]

How long is the ballet? Four figures circulate, and this study records them rather than choosing one: 15 minutes (the ASC account calls it "an original fifteen-minute dance piece"; Wikipedia's casting section says the producers created their own ballet company "to be able to include a fifteen-minute ballet as the high point of the film"); 17 minutes (Wikipedia's main text, DVD Savant, theartsdesk, Film Obsessive, and the Powell-Pressburger.org Nitrate page); 18 minutes (the BSC Heritage piece says the film's "18-minute dance sequence was once described by Martin Scorsese as 'a moving painting'"). The #783 study recorded 15 vs 16 vs 17 vs 20 minutes; this run adds the BSC's 18 as a fifth figure. Seventeen minutes is the majority figure; fifteen appears in the production-history sources (the planned or announced length); eighteen appears only in the BSC piece. The discrepancy is plausibly planned length versus finished length versus rounding, but no source says so -- it is not asserted here. [C33661]

**Takeaway:** The film is painted before it is shot, and the score is composed to the paintings. The mechanism is a storyboard that is also the set: 120 paintings, each replaced by its shot, one by one, until the ballet exists only on film.

## 22. The speed-ramping gadget

The problem, as Cardiff framed it: the behemoth three-strip Technicolor camera would never be as nimble as the high-jumping dancers, so he ran tests to make a fluid, sensuous film rather than a proscenium record of a ballet. The ballet opens on a fully framed theatrical proscenium, including the curtain draw, recorded conventionally; then Cardiff "slowly develops it into a dance with the camera and the expressionistic lighting, both of which move inside the action, into the psychological recesses of the young dancer's mind". [C33662]

In tests, Cardiff had a male dancer jump from a six-foot platform while he over-cranked the camera at five times normal speed. What proved most successful was a speed-ramping device he had made that allowed him to change speed within the shot. His own description of the gadget: "I had a gadget made to change the camera speeds during a scene so I could go from normal speed to double speed [48 fps]. This was used to great effect when a dancer leapt in the air; just before the apex of flight, I slowed the action for a fraction of a second, so that they appeared to hover in the air. I changed speed with pirouettes so that a dancer would start off at normal speed and then, as I changed the speed to only four frames a second, whirl faster and faster until she was only a spinning blur." The 4-fps whirl became "The Paper Dance" with Moira Shearer. Under-cranking at four frames per second provided "a mad whirl to the paper dance that unfolds midway through the ballet". But the music was pre-recorded and the choreography already fixed before shooting, so all of Cardiff's camera-motion tricks had to fit the playback. [C33663]

During a Swan Lake scene in the film, the camera actually spins to show Vicky's point of view as she executes a pirouette -- the camera takes the dancer's rotating viewpoint rather than watching the rotation. Outside the ballet, Cardiff's camera also fragments stylistically once the company reaches the South of France, "often shoot[ing] the dancers at the slower silent-era rate of 18 or 19 frames per second just to add a layer of Expressionism and mystery" -- for example Vicky in a blue dress climbing the overgrown staircase of Lermontov's villa. [C33664]

**Takeaway:** Speed as choreography. A device that ramps camera speed inside a shot: 24 fps to 48 fps at the apex of a leap (the dancer hovers), 24 fps to 4 fps in a pirouette (the dancer becomes a spinning blur). The camera also spins to take the dancer's point of view. All of it fitted to pre-recorded music and fixed choreography -- the freedom is inside the constraint.

## 23. The Brute and the 300-amp spot

Three-strip Technicolor still required very high levels of light, and Cardiff also wanted a heavier stop for deep focus in the ballet. Many of the lighting units available in England were not up to the task, so a research visit to Hollywood was arranged. At Mole-Richardson, Peter Mole had just made a powerful lamp he named a "brute": 225 amps with a wide beam, much more power than the 150-amp arcs Cardiff had been using in England. Cardiff persuaded Mole to let him have the two prototypes: "And so The Red Shoes used the first two brutes ever made." [C33665]

The abundance of general light created a new problem: no spotlight then existed powerful enough to register as a spot through it. Conferences with Mole and lens manufacturer Taylor Hobson Cooke led to a specially constructed, water-cooled 300-amp arc lamp that could place 1,200 footcandles on a subject some 100 feet away. That spotlight became an actor: it stands in for the ballerina at the film's end -- the company performs the ballet with a spotlight on the empty space where Vicky would have been -- and it picks out Lermontov as he pulls open the curtain to announce her death. [C33666]

**Takeaway:** Light as a character. A 225-amp "Brute" and a water-cooled 300-amp spot exist so that a single hard beam can still read through a flooded set -- and then the spot plays the dead ballerina's role, lighting empty space. The tool becomes the elegy.

## 24. Underlighting as palette

Cardiff on underlit Technicolor: wartime coal shortage forced rationing of electrical power; since Technicolor requires more intense illumination than black-and-white, many studios dropped colour altogether, but a few struggled on under rationing. "To the surprise of everyone, Technicolor was improved by the reduced lighting. Instead of blatant, glaring colours, the underlit film produced soft, pleasing pastel tints. I used the same technique in shooting The Red Shoes, and to good advantage, particularly in recording the beauty of lovely Moira Shearer ... and the gorgeous settings by Hein Heckroth." [C33667]

This is the same lesson as the pastel backings and the drained heaven, stated as a general rule: less light, better colour. The constraint -- rationed power -- becomes the look, and the look is then kept deliberately after the constraint is gone. The sequence uses superimpositions, travelling mattes and altered frame rates and shutter speeds; one of Shearer's dance partners is transformed into a man made of newspapers, and at one point coloured transparent gels rain down from above. A shift from day to night in the ballet was achieved with multiple dissolves between five background paintings. The gigantic falling leaves in one sequence were actually bits of cellophane dropped from wires into a set fashioned from transparent sheets. The auditorium engulfed by huge waves was done by doubling a shot of the raging sea at Cornwall over the studio set, augmented with a glass painting. [C33668]

The "dancing through space" shot: towering mountains in the background and several ranges in the distance, with multi-coloured wisps twisting past in the foreground. Each mountain range and the clouds were painted on separate glass sheets, in front of which was a flat glass water tank; chemicals that created streaks and trails without clouding the water were dropped from various heights into the tank. A shot of Shearer suspended on wires was photographed separately and matted into the scenes in post-production by George Gunn, using the Gunnshot travelling-matte process he had developed. Heckroth's own summary of the ballet's design intent: "We have tried to make our [ballet] sequence subjective as well as objective. When the girl is dancing, she feels she is a bird, a flower, a cloud; when the spotlight hits her, she feels she is alone on a small island with waves breaking around; the figure of the conductor melts in turn into the form of the impresario, the magician, the lover, and at last into a figure made of newspapers." [C33669]

**Takeaway:** Underlighting as palette. Wartime power rationing forced reduced lighting, which softened Technicolor's "blatant, glaring colours" into pastels; Cardiff kept the technique deliberately afterwards. Constraint becomes the look -- and the look is then a choice.

## 25. The Oscar that didn't happen

Rank executives hated the film; there was no premiere, no publicity to speak of, and it opened unheralded at the Gaumont Haymarket. Eagle-Lion's William J. Heineman opened it in New York at the 500-seat Bijou Cinema, where it played to packed houses for over two years -- 107 weeks in total -- and by the end of 1949 it was one of Variety's top ten pictures. The film received five Academy Award nominations including Best Picture, and won for Best Original Score and Best Art Direction. Cardiff was not nominated for cinematography. [C33670]

Cardiff's account of the missing nomination: a phone call from Lee Garmes told him the American Society of Cinematographers had agreed The Red Shoes was a certainty for the award, but as Cardiff, "an alien", had won the Oscar the previous year, a foreign cameraman winning two years running "would put the American cameramen in an inferior light. Bad for American prestige, they said. So the only way to prevent me getting the award was not to nominate me." This is Cardiff's second-hand account of a meeting; the ASC alone did not have authority to decide Academy nominations (branch members make nominations). It is treated here as Cardiff's story, not established fact. Technicolor's founders Natalie and Herbert T. Kalmus considered The Red Shoes the single finest example of three-strip Technicolor. [C33671]

Because of the lengthy reload time of the three-strip camera, Cardiff kept a second Technicolor camera on hand. One of its prisms was misaligned, which compromised the registration of one image record in odd shots; the problem was resolved in the 4K digital restoration. The film was shot in three-strip Technicolor on black-and-white nitrate stock. Restoration began in fall 2006 at UCLA under Robert Gitt: 192,960 frames in the print, 578,880 in the tripartite negative, with shrinkage and mould damage. The 4K restoration premiered at Cannes 2009. [C33672]

**Takeaway:** The snub is part of the story, and it is told as Cardiff told it -- a phone call, a meeting, "an alien" -- with the caveat attached. The film that the Kalmuses called the finest example of three-strip Technicolor ever made was not nominated for its photography, and the cameraman carried the story for the rest of his life.

## 26. The African Queen: the black river, two lights

The African Queen was directed by John Huston and photographed in Technicolor by Jack Cardiff. It starred Humphrey Bogart -- who won the Academy Award for Best Actor, his only Oscar -- and Katharine Hepburn. The cast and crew assembled in Africa in December 1950, and much of the film was shot in Lake Albert, Uganda, and in Ubundu, in the Belgian Congo. This was novel for the time, especially for a Technicolor picture using large, cumbersome three-strip cameras. [C33673]

The cast and crew endured sickness and spartan living conditions on location. In the early church scene in which Rose (Hepburn) plays the organ, a bucket was placed off-camera in which she could vomit between takes because she was sick. Bogart later bragged that he and Huston were the only members of the cast and crew who escaped illness, which he credited to having drunk whiskey on location rather than the local water. About half of the film was shot in the UK. The scenes in which Bogart and Hepburn are seen in the water were all shot in studio tanks at Worton Hall Studios in Isleworth, near London, because those scenes were considered too dangerous to shoot in Africa, and all of the foreground plates for the process shots were also filmed in studio. The shots of the German-occupied Fort Shona were all filmed at Worton Hall, where a fortress set was constructed from tubular scaffolding and covered with plaster. Scenes on the boat were filmed using a large raft with a mockup of the boat on top, sections of which could be removed to make room for the large Technicolor camera. [C33674]

Cardiff's own technical account, as reported by the Berlinale: he said of The African Queen that "the river itself was incredibly black". He managed to light the jungle scenes with just two lights, while the rapids' vortices, with their quick-change reflections, took him by surprise. He also faced the challenge of coordinating the colours of those location shots with the scenes shot in a studio water tank. The Berlinale notes that Cardiff had already proven with the war film Western Approaches (1944) that the heavy Technicolor cameras could handle the high seas. Kim Newman's assessment is a useful corrective to the romance of the shoot: The African Queen, "made in color under grueling conditions, is unfussy about its exotic backgrounds, spirited in its action, and modestly competent when it comes to keeping its unglamorized stars center screen and registering the nuances of their performances". Cardiff's contribution here was restraint and legibility under hostile conditions, not pictorial excess. [C33675]

**Takeaway:** The black river, two lights. After the painted Himalaya and the 120-painting ballet, the same cameraman lights a jungle with two lamps and matches a real river to a studio tank -- and the achievement is that nothing looks achieved. Restraint is the same skill as excess, used the other way.

## 27. The prestige international: Contessa, War and Peace, Vikings, Fanny, Nile

After the Archers period he became, in Newman's words, "a prestige international director of photography, especially adept at American productions with a European or period flavor". He was the camera operator and then cinematographer for 73 films, documentaries and TV series between 1935 and 2007 (Wikipedia's count, with its stated scope; IMDb counts 87 cinematographer credits on a different basis, including shorts and television). [C33676]

The Barefoot Contessa (1954), written and directed by Joseph L. Mankiewicz, starred Humphrey Bogart, Ava Gardner and Edmond O'Brien, and was photographed by Cardiff in Technicolor; shot at Rome's Cinecitta Studios, with exteriors in Sanremo, Portofino and Tivoli. The Chicago Tribune's contemporary verdict: "The camera work is exceptionally fine, the color exquisite. Ava Gardner was never lovelier to look at." The film's own mechanism: Cardiff lighting Gardner -- the first of his run of star-portrait assignments -- in sunlit Italian exteriors, with the flashback structure (three men remembering Maria Vargas at her funeral) letting the same woman be re-lit in three different registers. [C33677]

War and Peace (1956), directed by King Vidor and produced by Dino De Laurentiis, was photographed by Cardiff, with Aldo Tonti as second-unit director of photography; shot 5 July to mid-December 1955 at Cinecitta and Ponti-De Laurentiis Studios, Rome, in Technicolor and VistaVision, with a 208-minute running time. Cardiff was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Cinematography for it, and won the British Society of Cinematographers award for Best Cinematography for it in 1956. Two concrete devices from the DVD Savant review: for Pierre's duel, "the bleak snowscape is a soundstage set, and in the wide shots, Cardiff did a quickly handpainted glass matte, with a light bulb for the weak sun, to fill in the top half of the image"; and the film's look is characterised by "a frequent dash of Cardiff's expressive reds and oranges" in big empty rooms with shiny floors. [C33678]

The Vikings (1958), directed by Richard Fleischer, filmed in Technirama and printed in Technicolor, was shot mostly in Maurangerfjorden and Maurangsnes in Norway, while Aella's castle was the real Fort-la-Latte in north-east Brittany, France. The visual facts that recur across reviews: "Stately Viking ships glide between towering rock walls, the camera often floating just above the water's surface to catch the marble reflections. Blue skies, green coastal meadows, and earthy wardrobes radiate from the Technicolor image." Fleischer held his master shots "a little longer than logically necessary just so we can properly savor all these details". The Eastman Museum calls it "[o]ne of the most beautiful films ever shot by famed cinematographer Jack Cardiff, who used the widescreen Technirama frame and stunning locations in Norway and Brittany to their full advantage". Newman singles out the set pieces Cardiff captured: "Kirk Douglas walking on the oars, Ernest Borgnine jumping into a pit of starving wolves, Tony Curtis ripping Janet Leigh's dress so she can row better, and plenty of Boys' Own boat and swordplay". [C33679]

Fanny (1961), directed by Joshua Logan, was set in early-1920s Marseille and filmed on location there, including Notre-Dame de la Garde and the Vieux Port. Cardiff was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Cinematography -- Color for it; the film received five Oscar nominations in all, including Best Picture. The DVD Savant review gives the concrete visual facts: "Jack Cardiff gets maximum effect from the exotic location. He bathes the show in warm, bright colors that make the Marseilles fish markets look like they don't smell. Most of the shots in Cesar's saloon have the real Marseilles harbor peeking through his bead curtains; any other movie would just use rear-projection. When Cardiff does resort to an artificial background, as in a midnight rendezvous at the end of the pier, the blend between location and stage work is almost imperceptible. Leslie Caron is only 29 but dozens of soft-focus close-ups turn her into a dream vision." [C33680]

Death on the Nile (1978), directed by John Guillermin from Agatha Christie's 1937 novel, was photographed in Technicolor by Cardiff, with Hercule Poirot played by Peter Ustinov for the first time. The production spent seven weeks on location in Egypt in late 1977: four weeks filming on the steamer Karnak (the historic ship SS Memnon) and the rest at Aswan, Abu Simbel, Luxor and Cairo. Desert filming required a makeup call at 4 a.m. and shooting at 6 a.m., with a two-hour halt around noon when temperatures hovered near 54 C (130 F). Cardiff's cinematography was described as "stunning, giving the film 'an old-fashioned Thirties look'"; the colour logic: sandy yellow-brown sands and stones against the blue of the Nile and the sky, with a slightly hazy softness "as if linen on soft sand". [C33681]

One correction, recorded here so it is never carried forward: The Lion in Winter (1968) is NOT a Cardiff film. It was photographed by Douglas Slocombe, who was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Cinematography for it and won the BSC award for it in 1968. The confusion is understandable (Hepburn, 1968, British prestige picture, BSC-winning colour cinematography) but the credit belongs to Slocombe. [C33682]

**Takeaway:** The later career is the same hand in different weather: a glass matte and a light-bulb sun for a duel in the snow, a real harbour through bead curtains instead of rear projection, a camera floating just above Norwegian water. The mechanism never changes -- build the missing thing, match the real to the built, and let the star be lit.

## 28. The star portraits: Gardner, Monroe

Cardiff was a still photographer as well as a cinematographer, and his photographs were often taken as preliminaries to lighting the films. His still portraits included Ava Gardner, Sophia Loren, Gina Lollobrigida, Ingrid Bergman, Deborah Kerr, Marlene Dietrich, Marilyn Monroe, Julie Christie and Audrey Hepburn. [C33683]

The Prince and the Showgirl (1957) starred Marilyn Monroe and Laurence Olivier, who also directed and produced; the screenplay by Terence Rattigan was based on his 1953 stage play The Sleeping Prince, and the film was shot in Technicolor at Pinewood Studios. Production was marred by difficulties: Monroe was regularly late, never said a line the same way twice, seemed unable to hit her marks, and would do nothing without consulting her acting coach Paula Strasberg. Cardiff himself thought Monroe "as near perfect as any cameraman could wish for". Monroe called Cardiff's skills "the best in the world" -- the BBC's obituary repeats the same quote. [C33684]

The famous Monroe sitting: in 1956, during the filming of The Prince and the Showgirl, Cardiff created a series of intimate photographs of Marilyn Monroe. They were taken at Parkside House in Surrey, where Marilyn and her husband Arthur Miller were staying during production; Cardiff arrived in the morning and discovered Marilyn was still asleep. One of his offscreen portraits of Monroe became a favourite of Arthur Miller, her third husband. A vintage oversized photo of Marilyn as a "Renoir Girl", taken by Jack Cardiff in London in 1956, sold at Julien's Auctions for $1,040. The National Portrait Gallery's Marilyn Monroe: A British Love Affair display included Cardiff's 1956 portraits and noted that Monroe "instantly warmed to her cameraman, Jack Cardiff". IMDb adds that Monroe requested Cardiff photograph The Prince and the Showgirl and that he "was one of the only photographers Marilyn Monroe trusted to make her look beautiful" -- the "requested" claim is IMDb-only; the NPG and BBC accounts support the trust and warmth but not a formal request, so this study states the trust, not the request. [C33685]

**Takeaway:** The stills are the lighting rehearsal. A cameraman who photographs his star before he lights her already knows what the light has to do -- and the star who trusts him is the star who will stand still for it.

## 29. Director: Sons and Lovers in black and white

Cardiff began directing in the late 1950s. His version of D. H. Lawrence's novel Sons and Lovers (1960), starring Trevor Howard, Wendy Hiller and Dean Stockwell, was a hit critically and at the box office. It received seven Oscar nominations (including a Best Director nomination for Cardiff) and Freddie Francis won for Best Black-and-White Cinematography. Cardiff won the Golden Globe Award for Best Director and the National Board of Review Award for Best Director for the film. It premiered at the 1960 Cannes Film Festival, where it competed for the Palme d'Or. [C33686]

The cinematographer's-eye point matters here: Cardiff, the great colourist, chose to make his masterpiece in black and white and in CinemaScope, and handed the camera to another great cinematographer, Freddie Francis -- who was hired, Francis said, on the strength of Room at the Top (1959). The AFI Catalog notes that although production charts indicate the film was to be shot in Technicolor, it was released in black and white. Bosley Crowther's New York Times review said: "Jack Cardiff, cameraman turned director, has filled it with picture poetry... The drabness of a north-of-England coal town, the warmth of a poor coal miner's home, the bleakness of the wintry English country near Eastwood, where Lawrence was born -- all are caught and concentrated in this film, appropriately black-and-white." Cardiff himself considered Sons and Lovers his best effort and the most difficult to shoot, and said: "I wanted to make it into a good film, because the book is marvelous and I didn't want to let the author down." [C33687]

Location shooting took place near Nottingham, very close to where Lawrence himself grew up; interiors were filmed at Pinewood Studios. The film centres on a young man (Stockwell) with artistic talent living in a close-knit coal-mining town in the early 20th century, inhibited by his emotionally manipulative, domineering mother (Hiller). The choice to shoot it black-and-white is the same decision as the drained heaven, made the other way round: the colourist who knew what colour could do chose to withhold it, because the coal town needed drabness, not Technicolor. [C33688]

**Takeaway:** The great colourist's masterpiece is black and white. Direction, for Cardiff, was the control of the image rather than the operation of the camera -- he hired a fellow master rather than shooting it himself, and the film is remembered above all for its pictorial concentration on place.

## 30. The rest of the directing: smell, ships, the motorcycle

The critical consensus, stated most sharply by Kim Newman, is that Cardiff's directing career was "interesting but not especially successful": "Like Freddie Francis, Jack Cardiff made the transition from director of photography to director in the 1960s and gradually drifted back to his old job in the 1970s and 1980s, leaving behind him an interesting but not especially successful body of directorial work, from the prestige of Sons and Lovers (for which, ironically, Francis won an Oscar for best cinematography) through the trendy idiocy of The Girl on a Motorcycle... to the grotesque mad-scientist melodrama of The Mutations". He directed 15 films in total (per IMDb's director count) between 1953 and 1974. [C33689]

Scent of Mystery (1960), starring Denholm Elliott and Peter Lorre, was the first film to use the Smell-O-Vision system to release odours at points in the film's plot, and the first film in which aromas were integral to the story, providing important details to the audience. The system, invented by Swiss professor Hans Laube, piped scents -- including pipe tobacco (the villain's marker), coffee, roses, wine, gunpowder, peppermint, shoe polish, gasoline, perfume (the method to identify the girl whose life is in danger), flowers, brandy, fresh sea air, peaches, bananas and wood shavings -- to seats in the theatre. Cardiff later called Smell-O-Vision "a complete disaster" and the film the "one film I want to erase". [C33690]

The Long Ships (1964) was an Anglo-Yugoslav adventure film shot in Technirama, directed by Cardiff and starring Richard Widmark, Sidney Poitier, Russ Tamblyn and Rosanna Schiaffino, very loosely based on Frans G. Bengtsson's novel Rode Orm. The story centres on an immense golden bell named the Mother of Voices, which may or may not exist. Jose Ferrer was originally meant to direct but dropped out and was replaced by Cardiff, who had been cinematographer on The Vikings (1958). The film was a $3.5 million spectacle shot in Yugoslavia; the cinematographer was Christopher Challis (not Cardiff). Cardiff said of it: "When it came out it was really quite successful as a light, slightly comedic kind of thriller. But I was never really happy with it." Poitier went further: "To say it was disastrous is a compliment." (The film is listed as 1963 in Cardiff's director filmography but released March/June 1964; this study uses 1964, the release year.) [C33691]

The Girl on a Motorcycle (1968), starring Alain Delon and Marianne Faithfull, was an erotic romantic drama in which Faithfull plays Rebecca, a bored housewife who leaves her husband in the French countryside and rides her Harley-Davidson Electra Glide from France to Germany to meet her lover Daniel (Delon), a philosophy professor, in Heidelberg. The film redefined the leather jacket for motorcyclists into a catsuit, created by John Sutcliffe, that Faithfull wore in the film. Cardiff also served as director of photography and co-wrote the screenplay. It was reportedly the first film given an 'X' rating in the US by the newly established MPAA ratings system -- a single-source claim (a Rotten Tomatoes user review), treated here as unverified and not stated as fact. The Slant review makes the mechanism explicit: "Cardiff's genius as a camera artist was the translation of psychological states into cinematic terms" -- and in that film the subject (arousal, escape, speed) is rendered as soaked, psychedelic colour rather than through dialogue or plot. [C33692]

What his cinematographer's eye did to his directing, on the evidence: (a) he directed through the image -- Sons and Lovers is remembered above all for its pictorial concentration on place, and he hired a fellow master rather than shooting it himself; (b) he chased visual novelty as a directing idea in itself -- Smell-O-Vision in Scent of Mystery, psychedelic colour and daydream sequences (including a circus sequence with Rebecca standing on a moving horse) in Girl on a Motorcycle, the widescreen Yugoslav coast in The Long Ships; (c) where the script was weak, the image carried the film and not much else. Newman adds that Cardiff the director "seemed mainly there to collect scraps from the table: allowed to finish Young Cassidy when John Ford fell ill... permitted to make in The Long Ships a sequel to The Vikings -- a film he had photographed -- and failing to match Richard Fleischer's bold swashbuckling approach to the original". [C33693]

**Takeaway:** As a director he chased visual novelty as an idea in itself -- smell, psychedelic colour, widescreen coasts -- and where the script was weak the image carried the film and not much else. The directing career is the cameraman trying to direct with his eyes; it works once, brilliantly, in black and white.

## 31. Return to the camera: Conan, Rambo, Lights 2

After concentrating on direction in the 1960s, Cardiff returned to cinematography in the 1970s and 1980s, working on mainstream commercial films in the United States. Conan the Destroyer (1984), directed by Richard Fleischer and photographed by Cardiff, was the fourth film on which he worked with Fleischer, after The Vikings (1958), Crossed Swords (1977) and Amityville 3-D (1983); they worked together twice more, on Million Dollar Mystery (1987) and Fleischer's last film, the short Call from Space (1989), shot in the 65-mm Showscan process. Shooting took place in Mexico City from 1 November 1983 to 10 February 1984. [C33694]

Rambo: First Blood Part II (1985), starring Sylvester Stallone and directed by George P. Cosmatos, was photographed by Cardiff; shot between June and August 1984 on location in the state of Guerrero, Mexico, and Thailand; released 22 May 1985 and became a major global blockbuster, with an estimated $150 million box-office gross in the United States. Newman's verdict on this late period: returning in the 1970s and 1980s as a photographer, Cardiff found himself "on familiar ground -- swashbuckling and faraway places -- with Scalawag, The Prince and the Pauper, Death on the Nile, and Conan the Destroyer, then perhaps recaptured some of the discomfort of The African Queen and sadomasochistic action man feel of The Vikings in Rambo: First Blood, Part II, in which he followed Stallone's glistening torso through mud, blood, leeches, machine gun bandoliers, electric torture, and nonstop jungle engagements. It was as if he had never been away". [C33695]

One of the last films he photographed was at Pinewood Studios in 2004, when he lit 96-year-old Sir John Mills in the short Lights 2 (dir. Marcus Dillistone); their combined age was a record 186 years. His last credited cinematography work was the documentary miniseries The Other Side of the Screen (2007). Other late DP credits worth noting from the filmography: Scalawag (1973, Kirk Douglas), The Prince and the Pauper (1977, Fleischer), The Fifth Musketeer (1979, Ken Annakin), The Awakening (1980, Mike Newell), Ghost Story and The Dogs of War (both 1981, John Irvin), Cat's Eye (1985, Lewis Teague), Tai-Pan (1986, Daryl Duke) and Million Dollar Mystery (1987, Fleischer); the TV mini-series The Far Pavilions and The Last Days of Pompeii, both 1984. [C33696]

**Takeaway:** He came back to the camera at sixty and shot blockbusters as if he had never been away -- because he hadn't. The swashbuckling and the faraway places were the same ground as the travelogues, forty years on, and the same hand lit them.

## 32. Magic Hour and the honours

Cardiff published his autobiography Magic Hour in 1996 (Faber and Faber, foreword by Martin Scorsese). The book gives his accounts of the off-screen lives of the stars he worked with, including his friendship with the "childlike, frightened Marilyn", his unconsummated romance with Sophia Loren, and his conversations with Errol Flynn. He said that had he not worked in films he would have been a painter, and that much of his work was inspired by Impressionist painters -- he said the lighting and colour palette of Black Narcissus drew on them. [C33697]

Honours: appointed Officer of the Order of the British Empire (OBE) in 2000; Academy Honorary Award (lifetime achievement) in 2001 at the 73rd Academy Awards -- the Powell and Pressburger pages' 2001 profile reports he "became the first cameraman in Academy history to win an Honorary Oscar"; BAFTA Fellowship presented 31 July 2001 at BAFTA, 195 Piccadilly, after an on-stage interview with Ian Christie at a BFI/BAFTA celebration evening; BFI Fellowship in 2002. The British Society of Cinematographers' lifetime achievement award is dated 1994 by the BSC's own publication and by Wikipedia's awards table, and 1995 by Wikipedia's prose -- prefer 1994. He also received the American Society of Cinematographers' International Award in 1994 and the London Film Critics' Circle lifetime achievement award in 1996 (altfg dates it 1997; the year is uncertain). He was a founding member of the British Society of Cinematographers. [C33698]

**Takeaway:** The honours arrive late and all at once -- OBE, Honorary Oscar, BAFTA Fellowship, BFI Fellowship in three years -- because the industry took half a century to name what Powell had named on the Blimp set in 1943: the greatest colour cameraman in the world.

## 33. Death, the documentary, and the long afterlife

Cardiff died on 22 April 2009, aged 94, at Ely, Cambridgeshire -- the same day as Ken Annakin, with whom he had worked on The Fifth Musketeer (1979). He was survived by his wife and his four sons. His wife's name is given variously as Nicki/Niki/Nikki across sources, and the marriage date is unresolved (Film Reference's "c. 1970" versus IMDb's "19 March 1997", possibly different marriages; Cardiff was married three times per Film Reference); no marriage date is stated in this study. [C33699]

His life and work are reviewed in the documentary Cameraman: The Life and Work of Jack Cardiff (2010, dir. Craig McCall), which took 17 years to make and was completed after his death, and in Terry Johnson's stage play Prism (2017). His lighting and techniques influenced later filmmakers, notably Martin Scorsese, who used the extreme close-ups of the sisters in Black Narcissus as inspiration for the treatment of Tom Cruise's character around the pool table in The Color of Money. Frozen art director Michael Giaimo was greatly influenced by Cardiff's work in Black Narcissus while designing the look of Disney's Frozen (2013). [C33700]

**Takeaway:** The afterlife is the proof: Scorsese borrows the close-ups, Disney borrows the palette, and a documentary seventeen years in the making finishes after its subject is gone. The light outlives the cameraman because the light was the work.

## 34. Aesthetic logic: the painter's eye

Cardiff's aesthetic logic can be stated as a set of rules, each one earned by a film. First: colour is a language, not a decoration. Heaven is drained and earth is saturated, not because heaven is dull but because the audience expects the opposite; the reversal is the meaning. Second: the world is built, not found. A Himalaya in Surrey, a ballet in 120 paintings, a sea doubled over a studio set -- the location is the set, and the set is a painting, and the painting is lit by real sun. Third: photograph first, tint second. The black-and-white print does the illusion; the pastel only hints the weather. Fourth: unnatural colour is psychology. Blue shadows, green dawn against red, one red object carrying the charge -- the light diagnoses the character. Fifth: constraint is the material. Rationed power makes pastels; a 600-pound camera makes a speed-ramping gadget; two lights in a jungle make restraint. Sixth: cheap materials, expensive illusion. Cellophane leaves, chemicals in a water tank, a rhubarb leaf for the monsoon, a spotlight for a dead ballerina. Seventh: the risk is part of the method. A fog filter never used in Technicolor, at real dawn, with no retake possible, against studio policy. [C33701]

The through-line is the Technicolor interview of 1936: a man who knew nothing about the mathematics of light and everything about Rembrandt, Vermeer, Turner and de La Tour, hired because he could talk about painting. He never stopped being that man. He said he would have been a painter if he had not worked in films, and his work is the proof that he was one anyway -- a painter whose canvas was a 600-pound camera, whose brush was a carbon arc, and whose palette was a process that needed to be flooded with light and could be made, by underlighting it, to whisper. [C33702]

**Takeaway:** Cardiff's rule is the painter's rule: the picture comes first, the machine serves it, and the light is not what you see by but what you think with.
