# James Casebere: Study Notes

**Site:** James Casebere -- born 1953, in Lansing or East Lansing, Michigan (the variant is open; see below) -- American artist and photographer who builds table-sized architectural models from plaster, Styrofoam, and cardboard in his studio and photographs them, presenting the photographs -- not the models -- as the work. He is the builder, the lighter, and the flooder, and the work is the system that takes a room type -- a library, a cell, a hallway, a suburb -- strips it to its essential forms, empties it of people, lights it with a single staged source, and sometimes drowns it, so the photograph reads as a real place and then, on the second look, gives itself away as a model on a table. Sean Kelly Gallery calls his work pioneering in constructed photography; the Minneapolis College of Art and Design calls him "a celebrated member of the Pictures Generation." ## The life: the builder who photographs what he builds

James Casebere was born in 1953, in Lansing or East Lansing, Michigan -- the variant is open and must be carried, not resolved. Lansing: Wikipedia, the Templon biography table, artnet, the Sean Kelly CV, the Nasher Museum bio. East Lansing: the Sean Kelly artist-page prose, the University of Pennsylvania lecture bio, the Parsons lecture notice, the Williamsburg Biannual publication. Sean Kelly contradicts itself -- its artist page says East Lansing while its own CV document says Lansing -- and no source read states an explanation, though the obvious candidate is confusion with Michigan State University, which is in East Lansing and which Casebere attended in 1971-73; that is a hypothesis, not a fact, and no birth record was consulted. He grew up in Livonia, Michigan, a suburb of Detroit, drew architectural plans, and took industrial drafting classes; his father was an idealistic high-school principal who worked with an architect twice to design and build open-plan high schools and died in 1976; his mother taught elementary school; the family library held "the radical literature of the time," which he read. (C21608, leg 1 C21400, C21401, C21403, C21407, C21409, C21412, C21415-C21418; synthesis C21553)

The education spine is consistent across sources; only the birthplace varies. Michigan State University, 1971-73, then the Minneapolis College of Art and Design, BFA 1976, where he studied with the architectural sculptor Siah Armajani and the sculpture faculty member Kinji Akagawa, with visiting artists Dennis Oppenheim and Vito Acconci as influences; he had expected to study graphic arts before "falling under the spell of" the sculpture faculty. The Whitney Independent Study Program in New York, fall 1977, where he encountered conceptual artists and French theory -- structuralism, post-structuralism, deconstruction, the Frankfurt School, Walter Benjamin -- then the California Institute of the Arts, MFA 1979, under John Baldessari and Doug Huebler, with classmates Mike Kelley and Tony Oursler; he was Baldessari's teaching assistant and rented an apartment from the musician Laurie Anderson while in Los Angeles. The first constructed photograph came in his senior year at MCAD, after a day reading Harold Bloom's "The Anxiety of Influence" on the beach at Lake Harriet: "Fan as Eudemonist: Relaxing After an Exhausting Day at the Beach," a black-and-white image of an electric fan on its side in the glow of a cardboard television. He told artnet Magazine that his experience of art was "primarily through photographs," so photographing what he made made sense, and he cited Andre Malraux's "The Museum Without Walls" on photography's effect on perception. (C21609, leg 1 C21400-C21405, C21407, C21412, C21419; synthesis C21553)

After CalArts he returned to New York. His early New York exhibitions were at Artists Space (a solo in 1979), Franklin Furnace (1981), Sonnabend Gallery (1982 and 1984), and Diane Brown Gallery (1984); by 2000 he showed with Sean Kelly in SoHo, who remains his principal dealer -- "Seeds of Time" in 2024 was his ninth solo show there -- with Templon in Paris and Brussels and Helga de Alvear in Madrid in Europe, and Lisson in London earlier (shows in 2000, 2003, 2011). No evidence was found in the sources read of representation by Pace Gallery; do not claim a Pace tie. Early bodies of work: images of the American suburban home, followed by photographs and sculptural installations addressing "a mythical American West," sometimes poking fun at it; in 1984 he taught at CalArts, and return trips driving across the country fed a series of frontier pictures of ranch buildings, covered wagons, and split-rail fences, mostly black and white and "self-consciously artificial, more like stage sets than special effects," presented as lightboxes. From Sonsbeek '86 in Arnhem, Holland, until around 1991, he also made large-scale sculptural installations. (C21610, leg 1 C21400, C21401, C21403, C21407, C21413; synthesis C21553)

The subject-matter turns are the career in miniature. Early 1990s: attention turned to institutional buildings of the Enlightenment, especially prisons, as architectural types -- Sean Kelly dates the institutional turn to 1992, focused on "social control, societal structure" and the mythologies around institutions. Late 1990s onward: large photographs of flooded spaces -- a bunker under the Reichstag ("Flooded Hallway"), Berlin sewers ("Two Tunnels"), and works tied to the Atlantic slave trade ("Four Flooded Arches," "Nevisian Underground," "Monticello") -- and references to modern architects Victor Horta ("Spiral Staircase," "Turning Hallway") and Richard Neutra ("Garage," "Dorm Room"). After 9/11: Spain and the Eastern Mediterranean -- 10th-century Andalusia ("La Alberca," "Abadia," "Spanish Bath," "Mahgreb"), then Tripoli, Nineveh, Samarra, and Luxor, including mosques after the 16th-century Ottoman architect Mimar Sinan. 2009 onward: "Landscape with Houses (Dutchess County, NY)," an invented community seen from the air, two photographs from it in the 2010 Whitney Biennial. 2017: "Emotional Architecture" debuted at Sean Kelly. 2019-20: "On the Water's Edge" at Sean Kelly (December 13, 2019-January 25, 2020), flood and climate-change imagery. 2024: "Seeds of Time" at Sean Kelly -- flood water engulfing schools, homes, and beach huts, after architects Balkrishna Doshi, Yasmeen Lari, and Francis Kere. (C21611, leg 1 C21400, C21401, C21404, C21413; synthesis C21553)

The exhibition spine: 1985 Whitney Biennial; 1996 Venice Biennale "Campo," curated by Francesco Bonami, travelling to Turin and Malmo; 1999 "Asylum," Museum of Modern Art, Oxford, travelling to Santiago de Compostela and the Sainsbury Centre, Norwich; 2000-2001 "The Architectural Unconscious: James Casebere and Glen Seator," Addison Gallery of American Art, travelling to the Institute of Contemporary Art, Philadelphia; a 2002-2003 solo travelling from the Southeastern Center for Contemporary Art, Winston-Salem, to Cleveland, Montreal, and Indianapolis; 2010 Whitney Biennial; 2016 "Fugitive," a large-scale survey at the Haus der Kunst, Munich, February-June 2016, curated by Okwui Enwezor, plus "After Scale Model: Dwelling in the Work of James Casebere" at BOZAR, Brussels, and "Immersion" at Espace Images Vevey; 2025 "The Spatial Unconscious" at the Williamsburg Biannual, Brooklyn, September 25, 2025-January 17, 2026. Teaching is thin and must not be inflated: CalArts in 1984 and Master Class Instructor at the ICP-Bard Program (undated) are the only documented posts; no full-time academic post was found, and Anthony Vidler's being quoted on Casebere's work as Dean of the Cooper Union School of Architecture is not a Casebere appointment. Awards, CV-corroborated: three fellowships each from the National Endowment for the Arts (1982, 1986, 1990) and the New York Foundation for the Arts (1985, 1989, 1994), and one from the John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation (1995); NYFA Hall of Fame Honoree 2016; the Abigail Cohen Rome Prize, American Academy in Rome, awarded 2019, fellowship 2019-20 (MCAD says "won the Rome Prize in 2020" -- award-year vs fellowship-year wording, minor); MacDowell residencies 1994 and 2026, plus a 2026 Pollock-Krasner award (MacDowell spells it "Pollack-Krasner," a typo in the source) and a 2026 Casa Wabi residency; elected one of 12 artists and architects to the National Academicians Class of 2026, National Academy of Design. Collections, as listed by the dealer: Whitney, Guggenheim, Met, Walker, LACMA, Los Angeles Museum of Contemporary Art, Victoria and Albert Museum, plus MoMA, Tate Modern, the National Gallery of Art, Washington, and the National Gallery of Canada per Templon's longer list. He was married to the artist Lorna Simpson from 2007 until 2018; they have a daughter, Zora Casebere, and shared a four-story studio at 208 Vanderbilt Avenue in Brooklyn's Fort Greene neighbourhood from 2009 until 2018 -- the building was David Adjaye's first completed project in the US. The artnet biography still says in the present tense "He is married to Lorna Simpson"; that wording is outdated against the 2007-2018 dates and must not be repeated as current. He lives and works in New York and Canaan, New York. (C21612, leg 1 C21400-C21409, C21411-C21413, C21415; synthesis C21553)


## M1 -- The model is the artwork; the photograph is the record

Casebere builds a physical tabletop model -- plaster, Styrofoam, and cardboard, per the Met's wording; foam core and plaster and mat board for the W02 model, per the artist's own MoMA audio note; paint and poured resin where the work needs them -- lights it in his studio, and photographs it with a view camera on a tripod. The model is usually temporary; the editioned photograph is what circulates and what is collected. He has said the set or model was temporary and the image was what most viewers experienced, and that working the model became more and more the pursuit. The inversion of scale is the first thing to understand about the work: the model is a few feet across and the print is blown up to near-architectural scale, up to 96 inches wide, so the viewer is looking at a sculpture through a photograph, and the photograph is the record of a sculpture that may no longer exist. In "Subdivision with Spotlight" (W02, 1982) the artist describes the model himself: about 36 inches wide and 24 inches deep, houses at most 8 inches tall, built of foam core and plaster and mat board, all white, photographed in black and white, with little lights inside the houses and one house spotlit from above to read as a searchlight. In "Landscape with Houses (Dutchess County, NY)" (W16/W17, 2009-2010) the suburb model took about 18 months to build, house by house, and was reconfigured between shots, with model-train foliage, plaster, chicken wire, and cheese cloth. In "Flooded Street" (W19, 2019) and "Patio with Blue Sky" (W20, 2024) the model is a designed object first -- a waterproofed street, a patio -- and a picture second. The models are "table-sized constructions... made of simple materials, pared down to essential forms," based on architectural, art-historical, and cinematic sources, and the constructions are not exhibited as the work. (C21613, leg 1 C21401, C21407; leg 2 W02, W16/W17, W19/W20 and M1; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** build the room, photograph the room, and let the photograph be the work -- the model is temporary, the print is the record, and the scale inversion is the first thing the viewer feels. (C21614)


## M2 -- Unpeopled architecture: absence as the occupant

No people, ever, in any inspected work. Rooms, cells, streets, and suburbs are shown empty, so the viewer must supply the missing event. In "Library I" (W01, 1980) an empty library is lit like a pharaoh's tomb; in "Two Bunk Cell" (W06, 1998) a cell holds two bunks and no prisoner; in "Landscape with Houses" (W16/W17) a suburb has bicycles and school buses but no people; in "Flooded Street" (W19, 2019) a flooded street has no residents and no rescue. The Sean Kelly text calls the abandoned spaces "suggestive of prior events, encouraging the viewer to reconstitute a narrative or symbolic reading." The watchable behavior is exact: the eye searches for a figure, finds none, and reads the room itself as evidence of something that just happened or is about to happen. This is the direct complement to Simmons's miniature-domestic system, where the dollhouse is peopled by a solitary housewife in the wrong pose: Casebere keeps the miniature stage and removes the figure entirely, so the architecture has to carry the whole narrative load. The absence is not an omission; it is the occupant. A prompt built from this mechanism must never smuggle a figure back in to "add life" -- the life is the viewer's, projected into the empty room. (C21615, leg 1 C21401; leg 2 W01, W06, W16/W17, W19 and M2; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** empty the room completely and the viewer becomes the missing person, reading the architecture as evidence of an event. (C21616)


## M3 -- Light as the only occupant

A single, staged light source does the narrative work that a figure would do: a spotlight, interior bulbs, a raking beam from an unseen source, morning light against moonlight, day against night versions of one courtyard. In "Subdivision with Spotlight" (W02, 1982) one house is spotlit from above to read as a searchlight, and the artist calls the feeling surveillance, bleakness, and confinement; in "Library I" (W01, 1980) a raking light from an unseen source makes the room feel like a pharaoh's tomb; in "Landscape with Houses" the same suburb is shot in early morning light (#1) and in moonlight (#2), and the Whitney Biennial note reads the morning version as an ironic "Morning in America" after the foreclosure crisis; in the late work the 2021 "Courtyard with Foliage (Day)" and "Courtyard with Towers (Night)" pair runs the same courtyard under two lights. The watchable behavior is that the light moves across the sequence even when the architecture does not: changing the light changes the meaning -- morning is promise, moonlight is elegy, the spotlight is surveillance. Early on the light is theatrical and single-source by design: a spotlight, interior bulbs, a raking beam. The mechanism is cheap to state and hard to fake: one light, one direction, one temperature, and the whole room reorganizes itself around it. (C21617, leg 2 W01, W02, W16 and M3; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** give the room one light and no people, and the light tells the story the figure would have told. (C21618)


## M4 -- Scale deception: the tabletop reads as full size

A model a few feet across is photographed at eye level or from above with shallow depth cues removed, so it reads as a real building, and then the print is blown up to near-architectural scale -- up to 92 by 116 inches in the multi-panel works -- so the miniature reads as the monumental. In "Subdivision with Spotlight" (W02) houses eight inches tall read as a real subdivision under a searchlight; in "Flooded Hallway" (W07, 1998) a resin puddle in a model reads as a flooded corridor; in "Landscape with Houses #8" (W17, 2010) a tabletop suburb at 69 by 86 inches reads as an aerial survey photograph. The watchable behavior is a two-step read: the first read is documentary ("a photograph of a place"), the second read is the giveaway (too clean, too empty, too perfect), and the tension between the two is the work. The Nelson-Atkins text names this directly, on "Tenement" (W03, 1992): "an ambiguity of scale" and a blur between reality and artifice. Early on he insisted the seams had to show, so the viewer kept critical distance; in the recent work the seams no longer have to show, and the illusion is meant to hold. Either way the mechanism is the same: the photograph lies about size, and the lie is the point, because a model that reads as a building lets one small object stand for a whole world. (C21619, leg 2 W02, W03, W07, W17 and M4; synthesis C21555-C21556)

**Takeaway:** photograph the tabletop so it reads as a building, then print it big enough to make the viewer believe it -- the giveaway on the second look is the work. (C21620)


## M5 -- Flooded / submerged space: resin water as time and emotion

From 1998, Casebere pours a model resin ("E-2 Water," in the Harvard account) into the model so the floor becomes a still, reflective surface; later he shapes melted resin with a heat gun on a painted wooden base. The water is not illustration. The artist calls it time, temporality, emotion, fullness, overflow, and fear of drowning. In "Flooded Hallway" (W07, 1998) a hallway holds water at ankle height and reflections double the arches; the work is after photographs of flooded bunkers under the Reichstag. In "Four Flooded Arches from Left" (W08, 1999) arches stand in water with fog softening the far end, the subject a flooded slave factory in West Africa. In "Monticello #3" (W10, 2001) a flooded Colonial interior puts Thomas Jefferson's house under water. In "Flooded Street" (W19, 2019) an entire street is flooded, water at the windows, reflections doubling the houses -- and the sun is out, which is the late twist: the survivalist rather than apocalyptic version of the flood. The watchable behavior: reflections double the architecture and bend straight lines (stilts become squiggles in roiled water, per Collector Daily); the waterline cuts the room in half and makes the space feel both still and drowned; the same device moves from claustrophobic interiors in 1998 to open-air survivalist streets in 2019, and the meaning moves with it, from the past drowning the present to the present learning to live with the water. (C21621, leg 2 W07, W08, W10, W19 and M5; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** pour water into the model and the room drowns twice -- once in the flood, once in its own reflection -- and the waterline is where the meaning sits. (C21622)


## M6 -- Serial variation on one room type

One architectural type is built once and then re-shot as a numbered series: Hallway #1/#2/#3, Monticello #1/#2/#3, Nevisian Underground #2/#3, Landscape with Houses #1 through #12, Green Staircase #1/#4, Cavern with Skylights V. Each version changes one variable: color, viewpoint, light, water level, panel count, print size. "Nevisian Underground #3" (W09, 2001, printed 2009) and "Four Flooded Arches from Left" (W08, 1999) share the same flooded-arches DNA at different sites; "Yellow Hallway #2" (W11, 2001) is the saturated-yellow corridor with a single vanishing point, its sibling #1 known only from summary sources; "Landscape with Houses #1" (W16, 2009) and "#8" (W17, 2010) are the same suburb model at a small print and a monumental print, in morning light and in the wider view. The watchable behavior is that the viewer learns the type by comparison: the differences -- yellow against pink hallway, day against night courtyard, small print against monumental print -- carry the meaning, not any single image. This is the same serial logic Charlesworth runs in Modern History and Stills, but the variable here is architectural rather than editorial: the rule is the room type, and the series is the rule run to its end. A prompt built from this mechanism must build one room and then change one thing at a time; a set of unrelated rooms is not a series. (C21623, leg 2 W08, W09, W11, W16, W17 and M6; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** build one room type and shoot it again and again, changing one variable at a time -- the differences between versions are the work. (C21624)


## M7 -- The institutional type as an argument

Casebere picks building types that embody a social system -- prison, asylum, school, library, courtroom, tenement, slave factory, plantation house -- and strips them to essential forms so the type, not the individual building, is on trial. In "Tenement" (W03, 1992) a generic tenement is stripped to its simplest form, a generalized symbol of poverty and home. In "Arcade" (W04, 1995), "Tunnels" (W05, 1995), and "Two Bunk Cell" (W06, 1998) the prison is the Enlightenment institution, pared down and muted in tone. In "Four Flooded Arches from Left" (W08, 1999), "Nevisian Underground #3" (W09, 2001), and "Monticello #3" (W10, 2001) the same argument is applied to the Atlantic slave trade: a flooded slave factory in West Africa, Nevis plantation architecture, Jefferson's house under water -- and the Sean Kelly text reads Monticello as a statement on the gap between the founding principles of American democracy and their selective, imperfect realization. In "Garage" (W13, 2003), after Richard Neutra, the mid-century American domestic and commercial type is reduced to planes and a dark car-shaped void. The watchable behavior: the building looks generic on purpose; the viewer recognizes the type before the place, and the political reading arrives through recognition, not through caption. There is no text in the work, no label on the wall of the picture; the argument is the choice of type and the decision to strip it bare. (C21625, leg 2 W03-W06, W08-W10, W13 and M7; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** pick a building type that embodies a system, strip it to its essentials, and let recognition -- not a caption -- deliver the argument. (C21626)


## M8 -- Borrowed architecture: citation as construction

The model is built after a named source -- an architect, a place, a historical building -- and the citation is part of the title or the checklist: Victor Horta, Richard Neutra, Mimar Sinan, Luis Barragan, Thomas Jefferson's Monticello, Nevis, the Reichstag bunker, Berlin sewers, 10th-century Andalusia. "Spiral Staircase" (W12, 2002-2003) is after Horta, a modernist spiral stair as an austere, near-abstract interior, part of the group the gallery reads as a critique of the homogenizing effects of globalization. "Garage" (W13, 2003) is after Neutra. "Mosque (After Sinan) #2" (W15, 2006) is after the 16th-century Ottoman architect Mimar Sinan, an elaborate mosque interior, domes and arches, unpeopled, lit as if by a single high source. "Monticello #3" (W10, 2001) is Jefferson's house; "Nevisian Underground #3" (W09, 2001) is Nevis; "Abadia (from Lower Left)" (W14, 2005) is a 10th-century Andalusian interior, low viewpoint, warm stone and shadow, inspired by a trip to southern Spain; "Flooded Hallway" (W07, 1998) is after the Reichstag bunker. The watchable behavior: the work rewards knowing the source -- a Horta stair, a Sinan dome -- but does not require it; the citation lets one small model stand for a whole architectural history, and the deviations from the source are where the comment lives: flooding Monticello, emptying a mosque, turning a Horta stair into an abstraction. The citation is construction, not decoration, because the model could not be built without choosing a source, and the choice of source is the first editorial decision in the work. (C21627, leg 2 W07, W09, W10, W12-W15 and M8; synthesis C21556)

**Takeaway:** build after a named source and let the deviations from it -- the flood, the emptiness, the abstraction -- carry the comment. (C21628)


## The evolution: the camera moves out of the room and into the world

Early (1975-1992): black-and-white gelatin silver prints, small to medium scale, roughly 15 by 19 inches to 40 by 50 inches. Subjects are American mythologies: the suburban home, the West, then institutional rooms (courtroom, library, tenement). Models are all-white foamcore, plaster, and mat board, pared to essential forms, with empty black rectangles standing in for windows and doors. Light is theatrical and single-source: a spotlight, interior bulbs, a raking beam. The artist himself said black and white was about memory and the past, and that color felt too much like the present (color TV). Early on he insisted the seams had to show: the model had to read as a model, so the viewer kept critical distance. Middle (1992-2005): the turn inside and into color. From about 1992 the subject is institutional space and social control: prisons, cells, tunnels, arcades, hallways. He moves the camera inside the model instead of looking at its exterior, and starts shooting in color even though the models are still built in monochrome materials. Print scale jumps: 46 by 58 inches paper becomes standard, with large versions at 71 by 90 inches and multi-panel works at 92 by 116 inches and 96 by 77 inches. Flooding arrives in 1998 (resin poured into the model, called "E-2 Water" in the Harvard account). Subject matter goes international and historical: the Reichstag bunker, Berlin sewers, the Atlantic slave trade (West African slave factory, Nevis plantations, Monticello), then Spain and the eastern Mediterranean after 9/11. Recent (2006-2024): large color works, mostly archival pigment or digital chromogenic prints mounted to Dibond or Plexiglas, typically 46 by 66 inches to 70 by 90 inches paper, editions of 5 with 2 APs. The camera moves outside: landscapes, exteriors, courtyards, beach huts, pavilions, flooded streets. Color becomes the subject: bright yellow, orange, pink, and blue houses on water; day and night pairs of the same courtyard; blue sky over a patio. The model is no longer only a room; in "Landscape with Houses (Dutchess County, NY)" it is a whole suburb, built house by house over about 18 months and reconfigured between shots, with model-train foliage, plaster, chicken wire, and cheese cloth. Backgrounds in the latest work are compiled in Photoshop from the artist's own images. The stance also flips: from documenting a haunted past (prisons, slave trade, bunkers) to designing a speculative future ("On the Water's Edge," 2017-2019: flood-proof refuges, lifesaving stations as social infrastructure for climate migrants). The seams no longer have to show; the illusion is now meant to hold, and the model is also a proposal. Two cross-cutting devices run the whole career and are not a ninth mechanism: the palette shift from black-and-white memory to color present (early work is black and white, small, and shadowy; the mid-1990s move into muted color; by 2017-2024 color is saturated and declarative, and the late bright colors create a push-pull the Collector Daily review names -- eye-popping yellows against a drowned street -- so the picture feels optimistic and catastrophic at once), and the slow camera move from the cell inward to the landscape outward (a single room, then a hallway at the vanishing point, then a suburb from above, then a frozen sea with no building at all, then streets and patios under open sky; the threat moves from confinement to exposure, and the viewer's position moves from intruder in a room to surveyor of a world). The mechanism never changes: build the type, empty it, light it, photograph it. (C21629, leg 2 Evolution and M9-M10; synthesis C21555)

## Aesthetic logic: the model is a proposal about the world, not a picture of it

Casebere's aesthetic logic is that a small built thing, photographed as if it were a real place, can carry an argument about the real place it stands for. Every series removes something -- people (always), context (the model is pared to essential forms), the certainty of scale (the tabletop reads as full size), the dryness of the floor (the flood) -- and the removal is where the meaning is made. The viewer is left to complete the work: who lived in the cell, who fled the flooded street, whether the suburb in morning light is a promise or an irony after the foreclosure crisis. Scale is a mechanism, not a format choice: the model is a few feet across and the print is up to 96 inches wide, so the miniature becomes monumental and the monumental is revealed, on the second look, to be a miniature; the inversion is the experience, and the early insistence that the seams show and the late decision to let the illusion hold are two positions on the same mechanism, not two different practices. Light is a mechanism, not a mood: one staged source does the work of a figure, and changing the light changes the story. Water is a mechanism, not a subject: resin poured into the model makes time and emotion visible as a waterline, and the same device moves from the claustrophobic interior to the open-air street as the career moves from documenting a haunted past to designing a speculative future. Color is a code before it is decoration: black and white is memory and the past, muted color is the institutional turn, saturated color is the late push-pull in which a drowned street looks optimistic and catastrophic at once. The building type is the argument: the work picks types that embody systems -- prison, tenement, slave factory, plantation house, mosque, suburb -- and the citation of a named source lets one small model stand for a whole architectural history, with the deviations from the source doing the commenting. A prompt that takes the model and leaves the emptiness will make a diorama; a prompt that takes the emptiness and leaves the light will make a photograph of a room; a prompt that takes the model, the emptiness, the light, and the type, and lets the viewer finish it, will make a Casebere. (C21631, synthesis C21555-C21556)
