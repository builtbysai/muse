# William Bouguereau: Study Notes

**Site:** William-Adolphe Bouguereau (1825-1905), French academic painter -- born William Adolphe Bouguereau in La Rochelle on Wednesday 30 November 1825, son of Theodore Bouguereau (born 1800) and Marie Bonnin, known as Adeline (1804), wine and olive oil merchants; died in La Rochelle on Saturday 19 August 1905, aged 79, of heart disease, and is buried in the family vault at Montparnasse Cemetery, Paris, with his first wife Nelly and his children. He trained at the Municipal School of Drawing and Painting in Bordeaux from November 1841 under Charles Marionneau (his first teacher, Louis Sage at the Catholic college in Pons, had studied under Ingres), then at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in Paris from March 1846 in the studio of Francois-Edouard Picot; Second Prix de Rome 1848, failed 1849, joint Premier Prix de Rome 1850 with Paul Baudry for Shepherds Find Zenobia on the Banks of the Araxes, and resident at the Villa Medici January 1851 to April 1854. He exhibited at the Paris Salon for his entire working life (debut 1849 per the Musee d'Orsay, 1847 per the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica -- see C3); first prize medal at the Salon 1857; Knight of the Legion of Honour Tuesday 12 July 1859, Officer Wednesday 26 July 1876, Commander Sunday 12 July 1885, Grand Officier 1905; elected to the Academie des beaux-arts Saturday 8 January 1876, painting seat XIV, succeeding Isidore Pils; Grand Medal of Honour at the Salon 1885; vice-president of the Societe des Artistes Francais. From the 1860s he was closely associated with the Academie Julian, teaching drawing there from 1875 (variants 1872 and 1882 for different roles -- see C5), where Amelie Beaury-Saurel (#498) was his pupil and where he taught hundreds of students from around the world, among them Elizabeth Jane Gardner, who became his second wife on Monday 22 June 1896 after a nineteen-year engagement. He married his first wife, the model Marie-Nelly Monchablon, quietly on Thursday 24 May 1866; she died Tuesday 3 April 1877, aged 40, probably of tuberculosis; of their five children only Henriette (born April 1857) outlived him -- Georges died Saturday 19 June 1875 aged 16, Jeanne died Friday 1 June 1866, Paul died April 1900 aged 32, Maurice died June 1877. His output is 822 known finished paintings per the biography article, 828 per the Bartoli-Ross catalogue raisonne (2010), while the Wikipedia list table actually contains 737 rows (733 named, 4 untitled) -- see C0. The signature works are Equality Before Death (1848), Dante and Virgil in Hell (1850), The Bather (1864), Nymphs and Satyr (1873), The Birth of Venus (1879, 300 x 218 cm, Musee d'Orsay), The Nut Gatherers (1882), The First Mourning (1888), The Shepherdess (1889), The Bohemian (1890), The Broken Pitcher (1891), The Abduction of Psyche (1895) and The Oreads (1902, 237.5 x 181.5 cm, Musee d'Orsay). In his lifetime he was considered one of the greatest painters in the world by the academic community and simultaneously reviled by the avant-garde; after his death he fell into disrepute after 1920 and his name became shorthand for everything anti-modern, until the 1974 New York Cultural Center show, the 1984 Montreal / Petit-Palais / Wadsworth exhibition, the promotion by collector Fred Ross and the Art Renewal Center, and the 2019 Milwaukee retrospective "Bouguereau & America" restored him as an artist who must be understood if nineteenth-century art is to be understood honestly. His works sit principally in the Musee d'Orsay, Paris (Equality Before Death, The Birth of Venus, The Oreads), the Clark Art Institute, Williamstown (Nymphs and Satyr), the Detroit Institute of Arts (The Nut Gatherers), the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, Buenos Aires (The First Mourning), the Joslyn Art Museum, Omaha (The Return of Spring), the Minneapolis Institute of Arts (The Bohemian), the Philbrook Museum, Tulsa (The Shepherdess), the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Bordeaux (The Day of the Dead, A Bacchante Teasing a Goat), the Musee des Beaux-Arts, Dijon (The Return of Tobit, the Galatea copy) and the Musee des Augustins, Toulouse. ## M1. The Academic Pipeline: Draw First, Freeze the Canvas (Nymphs and Satyr, 1873; The First Mourning, 1888)
The painting is fully solved before paint touches the final canvas. The sequence, per Elliott (Art Renewal Center letter) and the Getty/Bordeaux accounts as cited second-hand by thehistoryofart.org: inked charcoal line drawing, then frottis (thin wash), then ebauche (dead-colouring underpainting), then fini (finish). Detailed pencil studies and oil sketches for every figure, drapery studies, composition studies; "few changes once he began the final canvas" (Getty). Technical study of Virgin of the Angels found few major corrections. In Nymphs and Satyr the figure studies survive; for The First Mourning an 1885 sketch in the Institut de France already has the bodies "almost identical to the final result"; for The Birth of Venus a study drawing for Venus survives; for the Pieta a Louvre graphite composition study survives from c. 1863-1865. **Takeaway:** pre-compute the whole arrangement -- a solved layout rendered without mid-flight changes; motion, if any, is the slow reveal of a solved composition, never a search.

## M2. The Leche (Licked) Finish: The Invisible Hand (The Birth of Venus, 1879; The Oreads, 1902)
Wet-on-wet blending until no brushstroke shows. Badger blenders (soft fan brushes) whip the edges of wet paint until the transition between light and shadow is perfectly smooth. The physical hand of the artist is suppressed in favour of a photographic, idealised realism. Degas and his circle coined "Bouguereaut" for any style reliant on "slick and artificial surfaces", also called a licked finish; Kenneth Clark faulted the "convention of smoothed-out form and waxen surface". Most visible in The Birth of Venus and The Oreads, where vast pale flesh areas carry no visible touch at all. **Takeaway:** render with zero visible stroke or noise texture; gradients must be perfectly continuous; the craft is in the modelling, not in the mark.

## M3. Translucent Flesh over a Hot Underlayer (The Broken Pitcher, 1891; The Oreads, 1902)
From about the early 1870s (late 1860s at the earliest), Bouguereau stopped painting flesh opaquely -- this dating is Elliott's claim, carried as his, not as consensus (see C2). A higher-chroma opaque underlayer is laid first; over it goes a thin, semi-opaque scumble of greyed fleshtone -- more grey than colour -- so the colour shows THROUGH the pale top layer, exactly as real light-complected skin works (the top layer of skin is nearly neutral; colour comes from below). Only the lightest lights are fully opaque. Evidence: in The Broken Pitcher he scratched through the top scumble with a brush handle to mark the elbow crease, revealing the higher-chroma underlayer. Elliott confirmed the same handling in the Forest Lawn chapel painting at close range. It does NOT come through in reproductions. Earlier and middle works were more opaque. **Takeaway:** build skin as TWO layers -- a saturated under-colour and a desaturated translucent veil over it -- and let light seem to pass through the veil; never paint flesh as a single flat tone.

## M4. The Chain of Pressure: Bodies as Linked Force (Nymphs and Satyr, 1873; Dante and Virgil in Hell, 1850)
A group is built as a chain of touch -- one figure pulls, another braces, another turns, the resister pushes back. Limbs overlap and redirect the eye; the viewer moves across the surface by following contact. Diagonal pressure runs through the canvas; the action has a destination (water, sky, ground) and a resistance that gives it purpose. In Nymphs and Satyr four nymphs pull the satyr by arms, ears and horns toward water while he leans back; in Dante and Virgil in Hell Capocchio and Gianni Schicchi are locked in a wrestling knot ("swastika posture"); in The First Mourning Adam holds Eve and his own chest while Abel's body arches away. **Takeaway:** figures must be connected by visible force lines (grips, leans, counter-leans); a drag interaction should transfer force along the chain -- pull one figure and the others shift, brace, or resist.

## M5. The Pyramidal Grande Machine: Salon Legibility (The First Mourning, 1888; The Birth of Venus, 1879)
The big public picture is organised as a Renaissance pyramid -- broad base, most important head at the top -- with life-size figures, so it reads from across a crowded Salon (over 300,000 visitors a year) and rewards close inspection with detail. The 1888 First Mourning commentary explicitly recalls "the pyramidal composition utilized during the Renaissance by both Michelangelo and Raphael"; Bouguereau was called the "French Raphael". Scale is part of the mechanism: The Birth of Venus 300 x 218 cm, The First Mourning 203 x 250 cm, The Oreads 237.5 x 181.5 cm. **Takeaway:** one dominant triangular mass, figures at near-life scale relative to the frame, and a two-distance design -- silhouette first, detail second.

## M6. The Reversal: The Pursuer Pursued (Nymphs and Satyr, 1873; The Oreads, 1902)
Bouguereau takes a stock power relation and flips it. The satyr -- emblem of male lust and pursuit -- is himself dragged, teased and controlled by the nymphs. The fauns who expect to watch become the watched, earthbound while the nymphs escape upward. The comedy and the erotic charge both come from the flip, licensed by a classical text (Statius's Silvae: the creature "dares not trust himself to deep waters"). The same flip in miniature in Girl Defending herself against Cupid (1880). **Takeaway:** stage an encounter where the apparent aggressor is visibly losing; let the reversal be the motion -- the dragged figure resists, slips, is pulled a little further with each beat.

## M7. The Cleaned Peasant: Idealised Genre with One Accent (The Nut Gatherers, 1882; The Shepherdess, 1889)
Peasant identity is signalled by costume only -- creamy blouses, solid bodices, bare feet, a crook -- while dirt, labour and hardship are removed. The figures are "unusually clean and content" (WikiArt on Nut Gatherers). The picture's one vivid note is a single small accent in an otherwise subdued palette: the red hair ribbon in The Nut Gatherers. The DIA notes the "mostly subdued color palette is enlivened by the red hair ribbon of the girl to the left". The same device in The Shepherdess (lone girl, crook across shoulders, oxen behind), The Bohemian (1890), The Goose Girl (1891), Breton Brother and Sister (1871) and The Elder Sister (1869). **Takeaway:** a near-monochrome pastoral scene with exactly ONE saturated accent that slowly travels or pulses; the rest of the image stays still and clean.

## M8. The Object as Symbol: One Prop Carries the Meaning (The Broken Pitcher, 1891; The First Mourning, 1888)
Bouguereau borrows Old Master symbolism and plants it as a single legible object. The broken pitcher connotes lost innocence (The Broken Pitcher, 1891 -- a girl sits beside the shattered vessel). In The First Mourning the ONLY contrasting colour in an earth-tone picture is the small blood stain on the ground -- the whole violence of Cain reduced to one spot -- plus, in the background, the altar with Abel's smoking offering, whose smoke mixes with storm clouds. The symbol does the storytelling so the bodies can stay beautiful. In Innocence (1893) the lamb is the second sign of innocence beside the child. **Takeaway:** a serene tableau with one small charged object (a crack spreading, a stain blooming, smoke rising and merging with clouds) that slowly changes state and changes the meaning of the scene.

## M9. Myth Aged Down: Gods as Children, Wings from Real Butterflies (L'Amour et Psyche, enfants, 1890; Flora and Zephyr, 1875)
Bouguereau breaks convention by casting adult myths with children -- Cupid and Psyche as near-infants sharing a kiss, nine cupids crowding Spring -- so the subject reads as innocence before corruption. And he paints nature exactly: a 2026 ISTE OpenScience paper identifies the butterfly wings in his mythological paintings as real European species with vivid eyespots -- Psyche's wings in L'Amour et Psyche, enfants are Hipparchia fagi, Zephyr's wings in Flora and Zephyr are Hipparchia fidia. The fantasy is built from observed specimens. **Takeaway:** a mythic couple rendered as small children, with wings that slowly open and close, each wing patterned from a real butterfly species with visible eyespots; the "magic" is entomological accuracy.

## M10. The Threshold: Dawn, Ascent, and the Watchers Below (The Oreads, 1902; Dawn, 1881; Dusk, 1882)
The scene is set at a transition -- night ending, dawn arriving -- and the group moves collectively upward, away from the earthbound watchers below. The 1902 Salon catalogue: "The shadows are dissipating; dawn appears, radiant, and colors the mountain tops pink. Then a long procession soars up into the sky." The composition is a current, not a centre: bodies overlap, separate and rejoin; some face out, some turn away, some lift arms, some float almost horizontally. The fauns below are darker, lower, fixed -- the counterweight that stops the picture becoming pure drift. Orsay notes a Symbolist tone and a twilight landscape connected to Corot. Dawn (1881) and Dusk (1882) are the threshold as a pair; The Return of Spring (1886) is the seasonal threshold. **Takeaway:** a slow, endless rising current of pale figures against a dawn gradient, with a few dark grounded watchers at the base who never rise; the loop is the departure itself.

## Evolution
Bouguereau's career is famous for NOT changing. Frederick Lincoln Stoddard, writing in Brush and Pencil just after the death in 1905, says the last picture from his brush "was as smooth in color, as painfully accurate in modeling and drawing, as was the canvas which gave him the four years' study at Rome". The evolution is therefore a refinement of one language, not a break. Four phases, all grounded in sources read.

Phase 1 -- Proof (1846-1854, ages 20-29). Ecole des Beaux-Arts under Francois-Edouard Picot; anatomical dissections; historical costumes and archaeology. Equality Before Death (1848), his first major painting, age 23, 141 x 269 cm, Musee d'Orsay. Dante and Virgil in Hell (1850), a violent literary subject, contorted biting bodies, dramatic shadow -- the young painter proving himself with anatomy and drama. Prix de Rome 1850 (joint first) for Shepherds Find Zenobia on the Banks of the Araxes; Villa Medici 1851-1854; old-master copy of Raphael's Triumph of Galatea as a Prix requirement. Dark, muscular, theatrical.

Phase 2 -- Turn to the Personal (1859-1870s). Legion of Honour 12 July 1859. Wikipedia: "By this time, Bouguereau was turning away from history painting and lengthy commissions to work on more personal paintings, with realistic and rustic themes." The Bather (1864) a spectacular success at Ghent. The Holy Family (1863) sold to Napoleon III. Genre and peasant children enter: The Elder Sister (1869), The Knitting Girl (1869), Breton Brother and Sister (1871). Dealers (Paul Durand-Ruel) and American buyers become central.

Phase 3 -- Mature Grande Machine (1873-1890). The big Salon mythologies and the sentimental genre run side by side: Nymphs and Satyr (1873), Flora and Zephyr (1875), Pieta (1876, tribute to his dead son Georges), The Birth of Venus (1879, 300 x 218 cm, Musee d'Orsay), The Nut Gatherers (1882), The Return of Spring (1886), The First Mourning (1888), The Shepherdess (1889), The Bohemian (1890), L'Amour et Psyche, enfants (1890). Life Member of the Academie 1876; Grand Medal of Honour and Commander of the Legion of Honour 1885. He admitted in 1891 that his mature direction followed the market: "What do you expect, you have to follow public taste, and the public only buys what it likes. That's why, with time, I changed my way of painting."

Phase 4 -- Late Persistence (1891-1905). Same language, more atmosphere. The Broken Pitcher (1891), Innocence (1893), The Abduction of Psyche (1895), The Wave (1896), Compassion (1897), The Onslaught (1898), The Virgin of the Lilies (1899), Queen of the Angels (1900), Reve de printemps (1901), The Oreads (1902, 237.5 x 181.5 cm, Musee d'Orsay). The Oreads is the key late work: Orsay notes a Symbolist tone and a poetic twilight landscape connected to Corot -- the only late drift toward atmosphere in the whole career. He painted 20+ easel paintings a year even in old age, rising at dawn six days a week. Died 19 August 1905, La Rochelle, of heart disease, aged 79. Total output: 822 known finished paintings per Wikipedia (828 in the Bartoli-Ross catalogue raisonne, 2010); many whereabouts unknown.

One technical evolution IS documented: Virgil Elliott's close-range visual analysis (Art Renewal Center letter) says the translucent-flesh method (M3) "starts showing up in Bouguereau's work beginning around the early 1870s, and perhaps one or two from the late 1860s", and that from his late forties onward "his work made a noticeable improvement over the early and middle periods, during which I do not doubt that he worked more opaquely." That dating is Elliott's, carried as his claim (see C2).

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is the defence of a single sentence: "In painting, I am an idealist. I see only the beautiful in art, and for me, art is the beautiful." Everything else is machinery in service of that sentence. The academic pipeline (M1) exists so that nothing unconsidered reaches the canvas; the licked finish (M2) exists so that nothing of the maker's hand interrupts the beautiful surface; the translucent flesh (M3) exists so that skin glows from within rather than sitting on the surface; the pyramid (M5) exists so that the beautiful reads at Salon distance, across a room of 300,000 visitors a year. The peasant is cleaned (M7) because dirt is not beautiful; the myth is aged down (M9) because a child is more beautiful than an adult god; the single red ribbon, the single blood stain, the single broken pitcher (M7, M8) are the only permitted interruptions, and each one is a symbol, not a fact -- the idea superior to the fact, as he habitually said (P4). The reversal (M6) is the one place where wit enters: the satyr dragged by the nymphs is the beautiful surface telling a joke about power, and the joke is licensed by a classical text, so it stays inside the doctrine. The threshold (M10) is the late form of the same logic: dawn is the most beautiful hour, ascent the most beautiful motion, and the watchers below are there to keep the beautiful from floating away entirely. The market confession (P10) is the crack in the doctrine and also its proof: he changed his way of painting to follow public taste because the public only buys what it likes -- the beautiful, as the market understood it, was what sold, and he supplied it at a rate of twenty pictures a year, three or four canvases in progress at once, rising at seven without fail. The hostility of Degas, Gauguin, Huysmans, Muther, Mather and Clark (P14-P19) is the same sentence read from the other side: what they call slick, mawkish, lubricious and prearranged is exactly what he called perfection -- "the kind of beautiful and impeccable enamel you find in Veronese and Titian" (P3). The revival (P20) does not resolve the argument; it restores it. He remains divisive -- extraordinary technique and grace to some, excessive polish and social conservatism to others -- and that division is the work's afterlife: the doctrine outlived the man because the argument about beauty never ended.

## POSITIONS
P1-P13: his own practice or stated views.
- P1. Idealism, not realism, is the program. "In painting, I am an idealist. I see only the beautiful in art, and for me, art is the beautiful. Why reproduce what is ugly in nature? I do not see why it should be necessary." 1895 interview in L'Echo de Paris, via Eugene Tardieu. Source: Christie's lot essay (lot 2432942); PRLog quotes page.
- P2. Beauty and Truth are the twin quarry. "One has to seek Beauty and Truth, Sir!" 1895. Source: WebMuseum (ibiblio), quoting Bouguereau 1895.
- P3. There is only one kind of painting: the painting that presents the eye with perfection, "the kind of beautiful and impeccable enamel you find in Veronese and Titian." 1895. Source: WebMuseum (ibiblio).
- P4. The idea is superior to the fact. "I think the idea superior to the fact," his habitual justification for suppressing the facts of form and color in the interest of ideal elegance and grace. Reported by Frederick Lincoln Stoddard, Brush and Pencil, 1906. Source: archive.org JSTOR text of Stoddard.
- P5. Theory has no place in basic education. "Theory has no place in an artist's basic education. It is the eye and the hand that should be exercised during the impressionable years of youth... never can the will, perseverance, and tenacity of a mature man make up for insufficient practice." Discours, Seance publique annuelle des cinq Academies, Institut de France, 24 October 1885. Source: Mark Walker, "Bouguereau at Work", Art Renewal Center.
- P6. Paint as you see; accuracy of drawing is the whole secret. "Paint as you see and be accurate in your drawing: the whole secret of your art is there." Allocution, Distribution des Prix de l'Ecole de Dessin au Grand Theatre, Bordeaux, 1899. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".
- P7. Work to the finish. "As I always say to my pupils, you have to work to the finish." 1895, quoted with P2-P3. Source: WebMuseum (ibiblio).
- P8. The Parthenon pediment lesson: subtlety of accents against large planes is the secret of art. Strolling the Louvre in 1846, discouraged, he saw the casts of the Parthenon pediment: "I understood that the subtlety of accents, in contrast with large planes, is what makes a drawing great. This truth, which I have yearned all my life to express and which still drives me on, is the secret of art." Paris Journal. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".
- P9. One is born an artist; the instinct is innate, the craft is acquired. "One is born an artist. The artist is a man endowed with a special nature, with a particular feeling for seeing form and color spontaneously, as a whole, in perfect harmony. If one lacks that feeling, one is not an artist and will never become an artist... This craft is acquired through study, observation, and practice... First, one has to love nature with all one's heart and soul." Via Marius Vachon, p. 114. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".
- P10. The market confession: he changed his way of painting to follow public taste. "What do you expect, you have to follow public taste, and the public only buys what it likes. That's why, with time, I changed my way of painting." 1891. Source: Wikipedia, citing Robert Jensen, Marketing Modernism in Fin-de-siecle Europe (1996).
- P11. Production discipline: work all day without breaks, several pictures at once. "I produce a lot because I work all day long, without any breaks... I never work on one picture only but have three or four in progress in my studio; that way, if a model doesn't turn up one day, I don't have to sit around with my hands in my pockets, I can work on the others." L'Eclair, 9 May 1891. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work". Corroborated: he rose at dawn and painted six days a week until nightfall. Source: Wikipedia (Bartoli and Ross, 2010).
- P12. Photography: collect it, study its light, almost never paint from it. His photograph collection was extensive, yet he "almost never worked from photographs" (the rare exceptions, usually posthumous portraits, show an uncharacteristic flatness). Hence Michael Gibson's label "Photo-Idealist". Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work"; Gibson, International Herald Tribune, 1984.
- P13. Open the institutions to women. He used his Academy influence to open French art institutions to women for the first time, including the Academie francaise (as the claim stands in the source). Source: bouguereau.org biography. Corroboration of the surrounding fact: his pupil Elizabeth Jane Gardner was the first female student at the Academie Julian (1875) and the first American woman to receive a Salon medal (1887). Source: Dahesh Museum.

P14-P20: reception and critical verdicts.
- P14. Degas's coinage: "Bouguereaut" (spelled with a final e-acute in French), the derogatory term Degas and his associates used for any style reliant on "slick and artificial surfaces", the licked finish. Source: Wikipedia.
- P15. Gauguin rated him a round zero in Racontars de rapin, and in Avant et apres described the single occasion Bouguereau made him smile: coming across a couple of his paintings in an Arles brothel, "where they belonged". Source: Wikipedia.
- P16. Huysmans on The Birth of Venus (1879): "so bad that there isn't even a word for it." He also panned the official Salon of 1879 as "a mass of sad works". Source: daisysainsbury.com, on the Musee d'Orsay Huysmans exhibition (2019-2020).
- P17. Richard Muther, 1894: Bouguereau was a man "destitute of artistic feeling but possessing a cultured taste [who] reveals... in his feeble mawkishness, the fatal decline of the old schools of convention". Source: Wikipedia.
- P18. Frank Jewett Mather, 1926: the artist "multiplied vague, pink effigies of nymphs... I am convinced that the nude of Bouguereau was prearranged to meet the ideals of a New York stockbroker of the black walnut generation." Source: Wikipedia.
- P19. Kenneth Clark faulted the painting for "lubricity" and characterized such Salon art as superficial, employing the "convention of smoothed-out form and waxen surface". Source: Wikipedia, citing Clark, The Nude (1956), pp. 163-164.
- P20. The revival: after near-erasure in the 20th century, the 1974 New York Cultural Center show (curator Robert Isaacson), the 1984 Montreal / Petit-Palais / Wadsworth exhibition, and the promotion by collector Fred Ross and the Art Renewal Center (founded 2000) restored him; the 2019 Milwaukee retrospective asked readers, per the Wall Street Journal, to "see Bouguereau through the eyes of an age when he was lionized". Prices climbed steadily from 1975 (The Heart's Awakening $1.5m in 1998; Charity $3.5m in 2000). Source: Wikipedia.

- Q8. [OWN WORDS (INTERVIEW)] "Starting a picture is very pleasant, for you always believe that this time you're going to create a masterpiece; you take pains, and little by little the painting takes shape, the effect comes through. You feel marvelous sensations. When it's done however things are different. You want to touch up the arm, the movement of the body doesn't seem graceful... and you end up doing nothing for fear of having to redo the whole thing completely." L'Eclair, 9 May 1891. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work"; PRLog.

But I have to paint all the time as I see feel, and know. That's all there is to it. People pay a lot for my paintings and I'm not complaining; it proves my work is still appreciated. But if they didn't sell as well as they do it wouldn't stop me from making them." L'Eclair, 1891. Source: PRLog.
- Q10. [OWN WORDS (INTERVIEW)] "That depends. I produce a lot because I work all day long, without any breaks. It is the only way in fact of achieving good work. Often I find the desired gestures for my figures immediately; in that case, my painting is quickly completed. If, on the other hand, things don't go the way I want, I put the canvas aside for a day or two and wait till I feel better disposed. I never work on one picture only but have three or four in progress in my studio." L'Eclair, 9 May 1891. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".

- Q13. [OWN WORDS (INTERVIEW)] "Every morning I get up at seven without fail and have breakfast, then I go up to my studio which I don't leave all day. Around three o'clock, a light meal is brought in; I don't have to leave my work. I rarely have visitors, since I hate to be disturbed. My friends, though, are always welcome. They don't bother me, I can work even when it's noisy or while they're chatting. When I'm painting, I don't pay attention to anything else." L'Eclair, 1891. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".
- Q14. [REPORTED REMARK] "They make sidewalks with it." On bitumen, which he defended as safe for superficial retouching. Reported by Charles Moreau-Vauthier, who heard it from Bouguereau. Source: Walker, "Bouguereau at Work".

Here's a person with a turned up nose and a receding chin: if I did a profile, do you think she would be flattered? No, right? You have to take another approach. Source: Seattle Art Museum blog, quoting Louise d'Argencourt, William Bouguereau 1825-1905 (Montreal, 1984), cat. no. 130.
- Q18. [ABOUT HIM] "M. Bouguereau has a natural instinct and knowledge of contour. The eurythmie of the human body preoccupies him, and in recalling the happy results which, in this genre, the ancients and the artists of the sixteenth century arrived at, one can only congratulate M. Early reviewer, quoted in Wikipedia (Wissman 1996, p. 24); Bouguereau took it as a high compliment.
