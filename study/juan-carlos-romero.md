## The life: the telephone technician who printed the word until it broke

Juan Carlos Romero was born in Avellaneda on 28 October 1931, in the working-class immigrant neighbourhood of Crucecita, a district of factories; his father worked in a soda factory and his mother was a homemaker (C10452). At seventeen he was a metalworkers' union delegate in an Avellaneda factory; later a telephone workers' delegate, and still later a delegate of the teachers' association at the art school (C10455). He worked as a telephone technician from 1954, did military service in Neuquen, and worked in the YPF oil wells of Caleta Olivia and Comodoro Rivadavia in the south, saving enough to begin formal art studies in 1956 (C10454). The technician's life is not background colour: industrial-school training and years at ENTEL fed his interest in telecommunications processes and in mail art, of which he became a leading Argentine promoter in the 1970s (C10459b).

He began drawing classes at nine at the Asociacion Gente de Arte de Avellaneda, attended an industrial secondary school, and in 1956 entered the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes of the Universidad Nacional de La Plata, studying there 1956-1963 and graduating Profesor Superior de Grabado (C10453, C10456, C10457). His first exhibition was in 1956 (C10458). In 1964 he showed works at Galeria Lirolay based on Victor Vasarely's kinetic ideas, seeking active audience participation -- a central objective throughout his career (C10459). In 1966 he took part in Homenaje al Viet-Nam at Galeria Van Riel with American Way of Life, combining geometric die-cutting with a press photograph from Vietnam -- the first time he incorporated a press clipping (C10460, C10502).

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is repetition: the same device, turned three times.

(1) Formation and Op art, 1956-1969. From 1968 he worked with stencil lettering, making bold graphic compositions reminiscent of Op art's illusory movement; works such as R (Romero) and M (Mundo) (both 1968) and the Graficos series (1969) repeat single letters systematically until they fragment and become almost illegible (C10461). In 1969 his colour stencil O won the Gran Premio de Honor at the Salon Nacional; it used punched-metal typography of the kind used to label packing crates, treating letters as geometric forms to fragment, superimpose and repeat (C10462). The Salon numbering conflicts -- LVIII (Argentina.gob.ar) vs LXIII (Castagnino+macro, Wikipedia) -- and is carried unresolved (C10610).

(2) Systems art and ideological conceptualism, 1970-1975. In 1970 he co-founded Arte Grafico-Grupo Buenos Aires, which combined the rescue of graphic traditions with experimental production and diffusion, taking art into the street; its end date conflicts, 1975 vs 1978 (C10463). The same year he presented Swift en Swift at the third Salon Swift de Grabado (Hugo Parpagnoli prize): four 4-metre sheets carrying fragments of Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels in unspaced stencil letters -- his first "definitively political work", a hidden denunciation of labour conditions at the Swift meatpacking plant that funded the show (C10464). In November 1970 he showed 4,000,000 m2 of the City of Buenos Aires at the first Certamen Nacional de Investigaciones Visuales, a plan, a map, eight photographs and a text treating the city as a system of semi-fixed and movable elements; it travelled to the Camden Arts Centre, London, in February 1971 in From Figuration Art to Systems Art in Argentina (C10465). In November 1971 he joined the Grupo de los Trece, created by Jorge Glusberg at the CAYC after the group's encounter with theatre director Jerzy Grotowski; the thirteen named members were Jacques Bedel, Luis Fernando Benedit, Gregorio Dujovny, Carlos Ginzburg, Victor Grippo, Jorge Gonzalez Mir, Vicente Marotta, Luis Pazos, Alberto Pellegrino, Alfredo Portillos, Juan Carlos Romero, Julio Teich and Jorge Glusberg (C10468). The group's method combined corporate "brainstorming", psychoanalytic group-therapy procedures and political-cell tactics (C10468).

In April 1973 he opened the solo show Violencia at the CAYC: a graphic installation occupying all three floors -- texts on violence from various sources and periods, newspaper images and headlines from the sensationalist magazine Asi, posters on the walls and floor, and the word "VIOLENCIA" printed in large letters (C10470). It opened a few weeks before Hector J. Campora took office as president, closing seven years of military dictatorship (Ongania-Levingston-Lanusse) and eighteen years of Peronist proscription; the work treated violence as a tool of domination and of social transformation (C10470). Violencia was re-created at the Museo Moderno in March 2000 and shown in the 31a Bienal de Sao Paulo in 2014; a 1977 ink-on-paper version is held by ISLAA, New York (C10491, C10498). The Reina Sofia holds a museum edition (2011, reg. AD06406): 24 colour typographic posters plus 48 black-and-white digital prints, 1.5 x 14 m overall (C10512).

(3) Dictatorship, exile, return, 1976-c. 1989. He taught at UNLP -- Teoria del Arte from 1970, Grabado from 1971 -- and in 1974 was elected union delegate of ATUDI (C10475). He was removed from his teaching posts in 1975 for political and union militancy, and went into exile in Honduras in 1977; the exact year of his return was not verified (C10474). On his return he placed his street-graphic work at the disposal of the human-rights organisations (C10474). In 1980, at the height of the "Dirty War", he began the series La vida de la muerte, serialising and fragmenting his own body in photographic collages (C10476). In 1986-1988 he formed Grafica Experimental with Rodolfo Aguero, Hilda Paz, Susana Rodriguez and Mabel Rubli (C10478). In 1988 he joined Horacio D'Alessandro, David Edward, Hector Puppo and Luis Pazos to form, in La Plata, Grupo Escombros ("Artistas de lo que queda"), opening with a street performance; the group aimed to take art to the street as an amplifier of collective consciousness (C10477). Aletheia dates a membership list to 1989 and adds Teresa Volco; the founding year is carried as 1988 (C10611).

(4) Late work, 1990-2017. In the 1990s he was on the editorial staff of Observador Daltonico, described as the first CD-ROM magazine published in Argentina, and of the visual-poetry magazine Dos de Oro; later he co-directed the visual-poetry magazine La Tzara with Hilda Paz and, from 1997, the experimental-graphics magazine Vortex with Fernando Garcia Delgado (C10484). In 1995 he published the artist's book 5 poesias pobres and began La desaparicion: stencilled cut-off words ("margin", "extinc", "exclusi", "carenc", "desocu") pasted through an abandoned lime kiln in Ringuelet as part of Grupo Escombros' Todos o ninguno (1995); in 2000 the work was wheat-pasted on a 14-metre wall at the 7th Bienal de La Habana (C10479, C10480). In 2010 he showed selknam (yo/el otro) at Galeria Carla Rey -- large-format digital prints in which he appears semi-nude with his body fully painted, recreating Selk'nam (Ona) Hain ritual body painting (C10486). In 2011 he published the book Juan Carlos Romero. Tipo Grafico, and the Facultad de Artes (UNLP) named him Profesor Consulto as a reparative gesture, restoring the post taken from him in the 1970s (C10487, C10474). He won the Premio Konex de Platino, Arte Conceptual, in November 2012, and the Premio Trayectoria Artistica of the Fondo Nacional de las Artes in 2010 (C10482, C10483). The retrospective Cartografias del cuerpo, asperezas de la palabra, curated by Fernando Davis, was shown at Fundacion OSDE in 2009 (C10485).

He died in Buenos Aires on 22 April 2017 (C10451). The Union Nacional de Artistas Visuales said on his death: "No lloramos la irremediable muerte; nos duele el vacio que deja como uno de los mejores artistas argentinos, cuyo quehacer creador articulaba el siglo XX con el presente" (C10451b). In January 2019 his archive was sold by his heirs, through gallerist Ricardo Ocampo, to a private Latin American art collection in New York; the Red Conceptualismos del Sur protested that the archive would be privatised, closed to public access and removed from Argentina, against his stated wish that it remain open and in Argentina (C10494). Argentine Senate bills S-0293/2020 and S-0423/2022 sought to declare the archive of public utility and subject to expropriation; the outcome of the bills is not verified (C10495). He was a passionate archivist from his student years: CAYC publications, clandestine left-wing press of the 1970s, some 1,300-2,500 political posters, trade-union leaflets, catalogues, magazines, videocassettes, and collections on popular culture (mate, tango, death) (C10493).

Footnote on the birth year. The study gives 1931 (28 October 1931, Avellaneda -- 22 April 2017, Buenos Aires). The 1931 side is the only one giving an exact day, stated in an obituary and confirmed in Buenos Aires Province Law 15158, and is the majority institutional position (Castagnino+macro, Argentina.gob.ar/Palais de Glace, Artprice, Red Conceptualismos del Sur) (C10450, C10451). The 1930 side is real but weaker: the ICAA record 1476425 body text (EN + ES), the Spanish Wikipedia lead, a 2024 UNC thesis, a 2022 journal article, and the Senate bills (C10468, C10495). Critically, the ICAA record is internally inconsistent -- body text 1930, Topics metadata 1931 on the same record, verified by direct read (C10468). No civil-registry document was consulted, so 1931 is strongly favoured, not proven beyond doubt. (C10612)

Evolution. Early (1966-73): the letter as machine, the press as material. Romero starts inside Op/kinetic geometry: single stencilled letters repeated until they fragment into near-illegibility (C10503, C10504). The device is already repetition, but at this stage it is formal -- Vasarely-like optical vibration, the letter as a module. The turn comes in 1966, when American Way of Life first splices a press photograph of war violence into the geometric field (C10502); from then on appropriation is the engine. By 1970-71 the work leaves the sheet: Swift en Swift turns a novel into 16 metres of unreadable text the viewer must walk and decode (C10506); 4,000,000 m2 treats the city itself as a system of fixed and movable elements (C10507); Unidad serial uses offset printing to dissolve original and copy -- the flyer fragment is the work, and "the work keeps growing" (C10508). The 1972-73 CAYC years radicalise this into systems art and heliography and then into the total installation Violencia (1973), where the word becomes architecture -- posters on walls and floor, the spectator walking inside the word (C10512). Middle (1976-80): the body under dictatorship -- concealment as survival. After the 1976 coup and exile in Honduras, the letter gives way to the body: La muerte (1976) uses a dead flower as a printing matrix, an index of an absent body, with the "M" hiding "Montoneros" (C10515). El placer y la nada (1978) appropriates a press photo of charred corpses but blows out the grain until the referent dissolves -- a way of speaking about Argentine state terror through a Spanish accident, under censorship (C10517). La vida de la muerte (1980) makes Romero's own photographed body the subject, X-marked, camouflaged, fragmented so it cannot be filed by the state's identification photography (C10518). Repetition is now defensive: multiplicity as a way not to be pinned down, legible only to those who decode it. Recent (1995-2017): the truncated word in the street. The late work returns to the street poster but strips it to near-nothing. 5 poesias pobres (1995) and La desaparicion (1995-2000) reduce words to their severed stems, printed cheaply (Xerox), pasted on a derelict lime kiln, a 14-metre Havana wall, even adult diapers (C10519, C10520). Where the 1973 Violencia shouted the whole word in giant type, the late work whispers a fragment and lets the viewer complete it -- the same repetition device, inverted: early repetition eroded meaning by excess; late repetition erodes the word itself, leaving unemployment, exclusion and disappearance as unfinished business. The arc closes in 2017 with La desaparicion re-installed at the Parque de la Memoria as his last, posthumous show (C10520). (C10613)

## Mechanisms

M1 -- Repetition until the letter breaks. A single stencilled letter is systematically reprinted until it fragments and becomes almost illegible (C10503). Works: R (Romero), M (Mundo) (1968, W02/W03); Graficos series (1969, W05); O (1969, W04). Early repetition is formal (optical vibration); later it becomes accusatory (M4) and then subtractive (M8). Watchable behaviour: a letter that starts crisp and, copy by copy, vibrates, doubles and falls apart -- the viewer watches legibility die by multiplication. Takeaway: repetition as erosion, not emphasis; a letter repeated until it can no longer be read, not a logo repeated until it sticks. (C10614)

M2 -- The viewer as decoder and completer. Works are built to be pieced together by the spectator, not passively received: repetition is "a means of corporeally engaging the audience, often leaving them to piece together the content" (C10555). Works: Swift en Swift (1970, W06 -- 16 m of unspaced, unpunctuated text the viewer must walk and decode); Unidad serial (1971, W08 -- flyer fragments, "the work keeps growing" (C10508)); 5 poesias pobres (1995, W19 -- unbound, reorderable sheets). Watchable behaviour: the viewer walks, sorts, reorders and reassembles; the work is unfinished until somebody does the reading with their body. Takeaway: the spectator is the second half of the machine; a work that can be taken in at a glance is not M2. (C10615)

M3 -- Press appropriation: re-cutting the news. Press photographs and headlines are spliced into the geometric field, then re-framed so the news accuses itself. Works: American Way of Life (1966, W01 -- first press clipping in his work (C10502)); En homenaje a los caidos... (1973, W14 -- Bellocq woodcut progressively displaced by a press photo of mounted police (C10514)); El placer y la nada (1978, W17 -- a Spanish accident photo blown out until the referent dissolves, speaking about Argentine state terror through a foreign image (C10517)); Violencia (1973, W12 -- Asi headlines (C10512)). Watchable behaviour: a familiar news image is cut, repeated or blown out until it stops reporting and starts accusing; the viewer recognises the source and then loses it. Takeaway: the news is the material, not the subject; an invented image is not M3, a re-cut real one is. (C10616)

M4 -- The word as architecture: the room you walk inside. Typography leaves the sheet and becomes a space: "VIOLENCIA" in thick black stencilled letters "reminiscent of the rugged typeface seen in Western 'Wanted' posters" across dozens of flyers "plastered on the walls and floor of the main gallery", creating "a claustrophobic effect in its obsessive repetition" (C10554); the installation "occupied all three floors at the CAYC" (C10550). Works: Violencia (1973, W12); echoed in La desaparicion (1995-2000, W20 -- the 14-m Havana wall). Watchable behaviour: the viewer enters a word, walks on it, is surrounded by it; the room closes in as the same word repeats on every surface. Takeaway: one word, room-sized, repeated until the space itself is the statement; a big poster on a wall is not M4, a word you stand inside is. (C10617)

M5 -- Camouflage as political message. A denunciation hidden inside a respectable commission or a decorative surface, legible only to those who decode it. Works: Swift en Swift (1970, W06 -- a hidden denunciation of labour conditions at the Swift meatpacking plant that funded the show (C10464)); La muerte (1976, W15 -- the "M" of the estrella federal flower hiding "Montoneros" (C10515)); Camuflaje I-IV (1980, W18 -- body-paint camouflage on self-portraits (C10518)). Watchable behaviour: the work passes as one thing (a literary tribute, a flower print, a portrait) and, on a second look, turns out to be another; the decoding is the political act. Takeaway: the message must be deniable on the surface and unmistakable once seen; an open slogan is not M5. (C10618)

M6 -- The body as the surface the work is printed on. Under dictatorship the body replaces the letter: serialised, fragmented, X-marked, camouflaged so it cannot be filed by state identification photography. Works: Destruccion y exaltacion del cuerpo humano (1973, W13 -- reclining male figure repeated in six frames (C10513)); La vida de la muerte (1980, W18 -- grainy self-portraits, front vs back, X marks that "miss" the front and "strike" the back (C10518)); selknam (yo/el otro) (2010 -- body painted after Selk'nam Hain ritual (C10486)). Watchable behaviour: the same body repeated, flipped front-to-back, crossed out, painted over -- the viewer watches identification fail, frame by frame. Takeaway: the body is the paper; a portrait that merely shows a body is not M6, a body processed until it cannot be filed is. (C10619)

M7 -- Cheap, reproducible media over precious technique. Stencil, offset, photocopy, Xerox, rubber stamp -- the work is designed to multiply and travel, not to be a unique object. Works: Unidad serial (1971, W08 -- offset, editioned flyers (C10508)); Violencia artist's book (1976/77, W16 -- photocopy + rubber stamp (C10516)); La desaparicion (1995-2000, W20 -- Xerox posters wheat-pasted (C10520)). "From the mid-1960s until his death in 2017, Romero used various methods of printmaking to make posters, flyers and photographic collages, using efficient, unadorned typographies" (C10553). Watchable behaviour: the work arrives as a stack, a pile, a wall of copies; taking one away does not diminish it, because the copy is the work. Takeaway: reproducibility is the medium, not the distribution; a unique print that happens to be cheap is not M7, a work built to be copied is. (C10620)

M8 -- The truncated word: meaning by subtraction. Late work reduces words to severed stems and lets the viewer complete them -- early repetition eroded meaning by excess; late repetition erodes the word itself. Works: 5 poesias pobres (1995, W19 -- "desocu", "exclusi", "carenc", "margin", "extinc" (C10519)); La desaparicion (1995-2000, W20 -- "DESOCU", "EXCLUS", "MARGIN", "EXTINC" from "desocupacion", "exclusion", "marginalizacion", "extincion" (C10520)). Watchable behaviour: a word appears cut off mid-syllable and the viewer's mind finishes it -- desocupacion, exclusion, disappearance -- and the finishing is the accusation. Takeaway: the missing letters do the work; a complete word is not M8, a stem that forces the viewer to supply the rest is. (C10621)

## Aesthetic logic

Romero's logic is the logic of the print shop and the union hall, not the studio. The materials are the cheapest that multiply: stencil, offset, photocopy, Xerox, rubber stamp, newsprint, wheat paste. The typography is unadorned and industrial -- punched-metal crate lettering, cowboy-film wanted-poster type, stencil capitals -- because the letter is a tool before it is an image. The palette, as reported in sources, is the palette of the street poster and the newspaper: black ink on paper, one red for the policeman's hand and the stamp, the faded colour of a withered flower, the grey of blown-out newsprint. Nothing is precious; the work is designed to be pasted up, torn down, mailed, copied, and re-installed forty years later without losing its charge. The through line is repetition with a changing charge: first formal (the letter vibrates until it breaks), then spatial (the word fills the room until it closes in), then defensive (the body multiplies so it cannot be filed), then subtractive (the word is cut until the viewer must finish it). The viewer is never a spectator: they walk the text, decode the camouflage, reorder the sheets, complete the stem. And the politics is never illustrated: it is built into the procedure -- a denunciation hidden in a sponsored show, a flower that is also an initial, a foreign accident photo that stands in for domestic terror. The work that began as a poster in a union hall in 1964 ends as a memorial inscription at the Parque de la Memoria in 2017, and the distance between those two walls is the whole career. (C10622)
