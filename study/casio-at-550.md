# Casio AT-550: Study Notes

**Site:** Casio AT-550, module 320, the "Janus" analog-digital watch with the Janus Read Sensor -- a wristwatch you calculate on by writing numbers and signs with your fingertip on the glass. Born in Japan in 1983, the same year as the G-SHOCK; on sale in America in 1984 for $99.95; made for one short run and never again. Analog hands for the time, an eight-digit LCD window at the top for the answers, and between them a sheet of glass that is itself the input: transparent electrodes on the crystal read the path of your finger and turn it into arithmetic. It is the first watch you could write on, thirty-two years before the Apple Watch, and the reason a museum in Tokyo keeps one on a shelf next to the first compact electric calculator. [C35227]

## 1. The brief: 1983, and why the year is the fight

Hans said "1983 Casio AT-550", and the first thing the research had to settle was whether he was right, because the internet disagrees with him. Open any English-language collector page and it says 1984: gafortiby ("introduced in 1984"), liquidcrystal ("this was 1984"), technostalgism ("Casio's 1984 AT-550"), Buxton ("Casio released the AT-550 in 1984"), the seller listings ("1984 Japan"), digital-watch.com ("RELEASED 1984"). Against that stands Casio itself, twice over: the company's 50th anniversary heritage page lists the AT-550 under 1983, and its "Interesting Watch" retrospective says it was "Born in 1983, the same year as the G-SHOCK". And behind Casio stands a museum: the National Museum of Nature and Science in Tokyo holds an AT-550 in its industrial technology collection, catalogued as manufactured in 1983, a mass-produced item, kept at the Kashio Toshio Invention Memorial Hall. The split is not a mistake on either side; it is two markets. The watch was made and sold in Japan in 1983 and reached America in 1984, where the February 1984 Popular Science advert sold it for $99.95 and the 1984 Casio catalog showed it. The collector literature is written in English, from the American side, so it dates the watch by its American arrival. Hans's 1983 is the birth; 1984 is the export. The brief holds, and the correction runs the other way: the sources that say 1984 are not wrong about the watch, they are wrong about which event they are dating.

**Takeaway:** A date is not a fact until you know which event it dates. 1983 is when the watch was born; 1984 is when America met it. [C35231]

## 2. What it is: one paragraph, the whole watch

Model AT-550, module 320, code name "Janus Finger Trace Touch Sensor Calc". An analog-digital combination watch: hour, minute and second hands on a dial, and above them a small eight-digit LCD window. The case is chromium-plated base metal with a stainless steel snapback, on a stainless steel band with a locking clasp (a leather band was also available), about 31.9mm across excluding the crown, about 61.3g. A crown on the right sets the analog movement; two buttons on the left run the digital movement. The digital side does daily alarm, hourly time signal, stopwatch, and day/date/month display -- all by buttons, none of it touching the sensor. The sensor is the calculator: press the lower-left button for calculator mode, then write numbers and signs with your fingertip on the glass, and the answer appears in the LCD window. That is the whole watch: a normal-looking ana-digi that hides a handwriting reader in its crystal, sold for one short run in 1983-84 and never made again.

**Takeaway:** It looks boring on purpose. The experiment is invisible until you write on it. [C35232]

## 3. Janus: the name, and the line it belongs to

Janus was not invented for the AT-550. For years in the 1980s Casio used "Janus" for its analog-digital combination watches, after the Roman god with two faces -- one face looking forward, one back. The line began with the AQ-500 in 1982, Casio's first analog-digital watch with three hands: hour, minute and second hands for reading the time at a glance, plus a digital display for exact confirmation, with stopwatch and dual time. The AT-550 is the Janus line at its most ambitious: the same two-faced idea, but the digital face has learned to read handwriting. The name is doing real work here. A Janus watch is a watch that looks two ways at once -- analog and digital, glance and precision, hands and numbers -- and the AT-550 adds a third look: the glass looking back at your finger. Note the correction that rides with the name: one collector page calls Janus "the greek god Janus". He is Roman. Casio's own heritage page says so, in so many words: the name comes from the two-headed god of time of ancient Roman mythology.

**Takeaway:** The name is the design brief: two faces, two ways of telling time, and then a third -- the face that reads. [C35233]

## 4. The problem: adding functions to an analog watch

Casio's own retrospective states the problem plainly, and it is the problem the whole watch exists to solve. In the early 1980s the combination watch -- analog hands plus digital display -- was very popular, because it took the advantages of both: the hands for a quick glance, the digits for exactness. But keeping the analog hands made it hard to add functions. A digital watch can sprout buttons across its face; an analog watch has a dial full of hands and markers, and there is nowhere to put a keypad. A calculator needs input, and the input needs somewhere to live. Casio's answer was to stop looking for somewhere on the watch to put the input and to use the one surface every watch already has: the glass. "That is why Casio found the possibility of operation on the glass surface." The AT-550 is not a watch with a calculator bolted on; it is a watch whose front window became the keypad, the keys, and the handwriting pad all at once.

**Takeaway:** The constraint made the invention. No room for keys meant the glass had to become the keys. [C35234]

## 5. The glass is the sensor: transparent electrodes

The museum record describes the mechanism in one sentence, and it is the most precise sentence in the whole study: the watch's glass surface carries transparent electrodes, and their detection is what recognises the numbers and the arithmetic signs, with the results shown on the LCD. Casio's heritage page says the same thing in plainer language: the touch sensor has a transparent terminal switch built into the cover glass of the watch face. The glass is not a window over a sensor. The glass IS the sensor. A thin "zebra" strip on the right top edge of the glass, camouflaged in the inner black trim, carries the signals from the glass to the electronics -- visible only if you have seen the watch taken apart, which no leg of this study has. That invisibility is the point and also the weakness: the whole input system lives in a surface the wearer also looks through, knocks against doorframes, and scratches. A scratch is not cosmetic damage on this watch. It is damage to the input device.

**Takeaway:** The cleverest part of the watch is the part you cannot see, and the most fragile part is the part you look through. [C35235]

## 6. FTRS: recognition that does not care where or how big

Casio's name for the system is the Finger Trace Recognition System -- FTRS -- developed in-house. Its claim, repeated in Casio's own words in both the retrospective and the heritage page, is that it recognises characters "regardless of the size or position of the handwritten characters" -- accurate results no matter how big the letter or where on the screen it was traced. That is the whole trick, and it is what separates the AT-550 from every keypad watch before it. A keypad needs you to hit a small target in a fixed place. FTRS needs only the shape of the stroke, anywhere on the glass, at any size. The owner who has actually used one reports that the recognition takes the common variations: an 8 as two circles stacked, or as one continuous line crossing itself in the middle. Each recognised character earns a single beep, which can be switched off; a mistake is undone with the upper-left button. What no source read explains is HOW the matching works inside the chip -- template matching, stroke-direction sampling, or something else. The behaviour is documented; the algorithm is not. That gap is carried open, not filled.

**Takeaway:** The input is the gesture, not the target. Size and place do not matter; the shape does. [C35236]

## 7. How you use it: drawing a sum

Buxton, who owns one and has written about it more carefully than anyone, gives the clearest description of use. Press the lower-left button for calculator mode. To enter 1, draw a downward stroke from 12 o'clock to 6 o'clock. To enter +, draw that same stroke and follow it with a horizontal stroke from 9 o'clock to 3 o'clock. The numbers appear in the main part of the LCD window, and the current operator as a kind of superscript above them. The full character set is the digits 1 to 9 and 0, plus, minus, multiply, divide, equals, and decimal point -- sixteen characters, drawn one at a time across the whole crystal. Draw the first number, draw the operator, draw the second number, draw equals, read the answer. The owner at gafortiby, who was "very very skeptical at first", reports that it works "incredibly well" with "almost no learning curve" -- you just draw, and the watch beeps its acknowledgement each time. The sum is written the way sums have always been written: left to right, in the air, with a finger. The watch just happens to be the paper.

**Takeaway:** The interface is the oldest one there is: write the sum down. The novelty is what you write it on. [C35237]

## 8. The calculator: eight digits, four operations, and its limits

The calculator is deliberately small. Eight digits, four operations, a decimal point, and an equals sign: that is the whole arithmetic. No memory, no percent, no square root, no scientific functions -- the CFX-200, from the same year 1983, did the scientific work with 25 functions on the C-80's fingertip keypad, and the AT-550 does not try to be it. What it does, it does with the whole screen per character: each digit or sign is drawn across the full crystal, so the gesture is large and the recognition has room to work. The owner at gafortiby notes the limit honestly -- "limited to simple 8-digit arithmetics" -- and then says he never fails to get a "wow" showing it to people. The limit is the point, in a way: the watch is not trying to replace a calculator. It is proving that a calculator can live on a wrist at all, without keys, and that a finger is a good enough input device for sums. The eight-digit ceiling is also a period detail: in 1983, eight digits on a wrist was the state of the art for a calculator watch, and the C-80 had set that bar in 1980.

**Takeaway:** Small arithmetic, large gestures. The watch proves input, not mathematics. [C35238]

## 9. The rest of the watch: what the touch never touches

If the touch sensor dies, the watch does not die with it. The daily alarm, the hourly time signal, the stopwatch and the day/date/month display all run on the two left-side buttons and never use the sensor -- a fact the gafortiby owner states with a touch of regret ("which is quite a shame"). That separation matters, because it is what makes the watch collectable in used condition at all: a dead sensor leaves a working ana-digi, and the touch is a bonus rather than the whole watch. It also tells you how Casio thought about the sensor -- as an addition to a complete watch, not as the watch itself. The analog hands keep time whether or not the glass can read; the digital window keeps showing the date whether or not it can calculate. The experiment sits on top of a finished product, which is why the experiment's failure mode is graceful instead of fatal.

**Takeaway:** The touch is a layer, not the foundation. The watch underneath it is complete. [C35239]

## 10. Two movements in one case

An ana-digi is two watches sharing a case, and the AT-550 makes the split visible in its controls. The crown on the right sets the analog movement -- the hands. The two buttons on the left run the digital movement -- the LCD, the alarm, the stopwatch, the calculator mode. Liquidcrystal's repair notes make the split concrete: usually it is the analog portion that stops working, the two halves are "a nightmare to disassemble", and the author has transplanted pieces between like AT models with success -- even speculating, untested, that a generic analog/digital Casio unit could stand in for the analog half. That fragility is the price of the two-faced design: two movements, two power draws, two things to break, in a case about 32mm across. The Janus idea is elegant on the wrist and fiddly on the bench.

**Takeaway:** Two faces means two movements, two controls, and twice as much to break. [C35240]

## 11. The case, the band, the numbers

The construction is ordinary for a 1983 Casio and that ordinariness is part of the story. Chromium-plated base metal case, stainless steel snapback, stainless steel band with the common locking clasp; a leather band was also available. About 31.9mm across excluding the crown, 20mm lug width, about 61.3g on the bracelet -- those numbers come from a seller's listing for an AT-552, not from Casio, and are carried as listing figures. The caseback of an AT-551, photographed by a seller, reads "CASIO, 320 AT-551, STAINLESS STEEL BACK, WATER RESISTANT, JAPAN YK" -- "WATER RESISTANT" with no depth rating, which is all the water claim this study can verify. The dial is ivory-white on the AT-552 in that listing, with stick indices and "CASIO QUARTZ" under the digital window. None of this is remarkable, and that is the remark: the most futuristic watch Casio made that year is dressed as the plainest one, a steel rectangle with hands. The future arrived in a boring case, which is why gafortiby's first impression of his own example is "boring!" and "YAWN" -- and why the reveal lands so hard.

**Takeaway:** The case lies about the contents. Boring outside, handwriting reader inside. [C35241]

## 12. The variants: 550, 551, 552

The family is the AT-550, the AT-551 and the AT-552, all on module 320, all with the same sensor. What exactly separates them is not established in the sources read, and the study does not invent a split. What is known: the AT-552G in gold is the most commonly seen of the series on the second-hand market, for reasons liquidcrystal admits he does not understand; the AT-550-7 is the silver or white-dial variant in his photograph and his favourite of the lot; the AT-551's caseback carries the "320 AT-551" engraving. Finish and band are the likely difference -- gold against steel, bracelet against leather -- but no source read states the mapping, and Casio's own pages do not break the family down. The variants matter for collectors (a working AT-552G is the affordable way in; a mint AT-550 is the expensive one) and barely at all for the study: the sensor is the same in every one, and the idea does not change with the plating.

**Takeaway:** One sensor, three badges. The variant split is a collector's question, carried open. [C35242]

## 13. The price: $99.95

In February 1984 the AT-550 was advertised in Popular Science at $99.95 -- Buxton rounds it to "under $100", gafortiby calls it "a lot of money!" and both are right. Ninety-nine dollars in 1984 is roughly two hundred and ninety in 2026 money, for a watch that looks, at a glance, like any other Casio ana-digi. Gafortiby's explanation for the short production run leans on exactly that: "I can't imagine a lot of people buying this watch, as it looks so simple and cost so much." The price is the second half of the disguise. The first half is the case, which hides the sensor; the second half is the price, which asks the buyer to pay a premium for a feature nobody can see until it is demonstrated. A watch that needs a demonstration to justify its price is a hard sell in a shop, and the AT-550 was a hard sell. That is not a footnote to the story. It is the reason there are so few of them.

**Takeaway:** The future cost $99.95 and looked like $29.95. That gap is why it sold once. [C35243]

## 14. The year, settled

Put the evidence in one place. For 1983: Casio's 50th anniversary heritage page lists the AT-550 under 1983; Casio's "Interesting Watch" Vol. 6 (2024) says it was "Born in 1983, the same year as the G-SHOCK"; the National Museum of Nature and Science catalogues its AT-550 as manufactured in 1983; the Pocket Calculator Show dates it "c. 1983"; the Korean hada.io piece puts the AT-550's launch in 1983. For 1984: the February 1984 Popular Science advert; the 1984 Casio catalog; gafortiby, liquidcrystal, technostalgism, Buxton, digital-watch.com, and the seller listings, all saying 1984. The two piles are not in conflict once you see that they date different events: 1983 is manufacture and Japanese sale, 1984 is the American launch. Casio's own telling is consistent on this -- the heritage page is a Japanese company writing its own history, and it puts the watch in 1983 beside the G-SHOCK, which launched in Japan in 1983. The study therefore uses 1983 as the birth year, notes 1984 as the export year, and treats any source that gives only 1984 as dating the American arrival. The remaining uncertainty is narrow: the exact Japanese launch month and the exact end of the production run are not established.

**Takeaway:** Both years are true, about different things. 1983 is the birth, 1984 the arrival. [C35244]

## 15. The siblings: C-80, TC-500, CFX-200, DB-1000

The AT-550 does not stand alone; it is the middle child of a five-year argument Casio was having with itself about input. The C-80 of 1980 was the first calculator watch: a physical fingertip keypad, eight-digit arithmetic, and a circuit that guessed which key the wearer meant when two were pressed at once. The TC-500, announced in 1983, was the first watch with a touch panel: the calculator keys appeared on an LCD and you tapped them -- a keypad made of light, but still a keypad, with small targets for fat fingers. The CFX-200, also 1983, took the C-80's keypad and added 25 scientific functions. The AT-550, also 1983, threw the keypad away and let the whole crystal be the input. And the DB-1000, later, took the AT-550's handwriting idea and extended it to alphanumeric database entry -- but it had a full LCD screen to work with, where the AT watches had only eight digits at the top. Read in order, the line is a clean progression: keys you press, keys you tap, no keys at all, then letters as well as numbers. The AT-550 is the step where the keys disappear.

**Takeaway:** Five years, four input ideas. The AT-550 is the one where the keys vanish. [C35245]

## 16. Buxton: the long nose of innovation

The reason this watch matters beyond watch collecting is a three-page BusinessWeek column by Bill Buxton, "The Mad Dash Toward Touch Technology", 21 October 2009. Buxton owns an AT-550 -- it is watch (a) in a photograph of four watches from his collection -- and he uses it to make an argument about innovation itself. Touch and multitouch, he says, are "decidedly not new": multitouch was first discovered by researchers in the very early 1980s, before the first generally available PC with a mouse was commercially released, and "has been gradually mined and refined ever since". He calls this the "Long Nose of Innovation": the long, mostly invisible period in which an idea is discovered, refined, and laid down as foundation, before some company recognises its latent value and takes on the risk of exploiting it. The companies that start the buzz "neither invented the underlying technology, nor were they the first companies to exploit it commercially" -- and that, he insists, is "not a criticism, but rather a respectful commentary on the nature of design and innovation". The AT-550 is his exhibit A: a handwriting recogniser on a wrist in 1983, built with one 131,072th of the computer power of an equivalently sized chip in 2009, doing in 1984 what the industry would call new twenty-five years later. Buxton is a Canadian computer scientist and designer -- born Thursday 10 March 1949 in Edmonton, Alberta; music degree from Queen's in 1973, computer science master's from Toronto in 1978; Xerox PARC, Toronto professor, chief scientist at Alias and SGI, principal researcher at Microsoft Research from 2005 to 2022; SIGCHI Lifetime Achievement Award 2008; author of Sketching User Experiences (2007) -- and the AT-550 sits in his collection because it is, for him, a physical argument: the future is usually old before it is new.

**Takeaway:** The watch is evidence for a theory: innovation has a long nose, and the AT-550 is the nose. [C35246]

## 17. Heads up: the whole screen is the key

Buxton's sharpest observation about the AT-550 is not that it has a touchscreen. It is that the whole screen is used for entering each character, "thereby bringing the scale of the action well within the bounds of normal human finger motor control". Compare his watch (b), the TC-50 -- the TC-500 in every other source -- where a keypad appears on the face and you must touch small virtual keys: "a victim of what happens when fat fingers meet small targets -- even when accompanied by high concentration. As for touch typing, forget it." The AT-550's trick is the opposite: because the gesture is the whole crystal, you do not have to look at what you are doing. Buxton calls this "heads up" data entry, "the equivalent of touch typing": "I can input numbers without diverting my gaze from you or from the document from which I am copying a number." That is the design lesson the watch teaches, and it is the reason Buxton keeps it: touch is not one thing. The AT-550 and the TC-500 are both "touchscreen watches" and knowing how to use one buys you "pretty much nothing in terms of knowing how to use the other two" -- his watch (c), the Tissot Touch, is a third grammar again. "Anyone who describes a product as having a 'touch interface' is likely unqualified to comment on the topic. The granularity of the description is just too coarse."

**Takeaway:** "Touchscreen" says almost nothing. The AT-550's touch is a whole-screen gesture you can do without looking. [C35247]

## 18. Why it failed: price, scratches, one short run

The watch failed commercially, and the reasons are all in the record. It cost $99.95 while looking like an ordinary Casio. It was sold for a single short run -- Japan 1983, America 1984 -- and then discontinued and never made again. And its input system is fragile in exactly the way a watch is vulnerable: the entire glass plate is the sensor, so any scratch on the surface, or even a dirty finger, hinders recognition, as gafortiby warns buyers of used examples. The analog half breaks too -- usually the analog portion stops first, and the watch is "a nightmare to disassemble". None of this is a design flaw in the narrow sense; it is the cost of the idea. A sensor made of glass, worn on a wrist, sold at a premium, in a case that hides what it does: every one of those choices is correct for the demonstration and wrong for the shop. The watch did not fail because the recognition was bad -- every witness says the recognition is good. It failed because the product around the recognition could not carry the price, the fragility, and the invisibility all at once.

**Takeaway:** The idea worked; the product could not. Good recognition, wrong price, glass for a sensor. [C35248]

## 19. The afterlife: collectors and transplants

What survives is a collector's object with an odd supply-and-demand curve, in gafortiby's phrase: "very very few people want it, but even fewer people have it." A fully working example changes hands for around US$200 or more, in liquidcrystal's and gafortiby's matching estimates; a mint, new-in-box example with tag and manual is far rarer, and one such example, bought in 1984 by a single careful owner, was listed for sale at 2,500 GBP -- an asking price, not a sale, and noted as such. The AT-552G in gold is the most commonly seen of the family on the second-hand market; the AT-550-7 in silver is the one its most enthusiastic owner reaches for. And the afterlife has its own craft: because the analog half usually fails first, owners transplant parts between like AT models, and liquidcrystal speculates -- untested, and flagged as a guess -- that a generic analog/digital Casio unit could stand in for the analog half. The watch that was too fragile to sell has become, forty years on, a watch people keep alive by surgery. It also became a museum piece: the National Museum of Nature and Science keeps one at the Kashio Toshio Invention Memorial Hall, filed not as a watch but as an invention -- a wristwatch with handwritten-digit recognition and calculation, mass-produced, 1983.

**Takeaway:** Too rare to sell, too interesting to die. Collectors keep it alive by transplant. [C35249]

## 20. The lineage: from the wrist to the pocket

The AT-550's idea took thirteen years to reach everyone's pocket. In 1996 the PalmPilot shipped with Graffiti, Jeff Hawkins's handwriting recognition -- a simplified alphabet you learned to write so the machine could read it, running on a device that fit in a shirt pocket and sold over a million units in eighteen months. Hawkins, born Saturday 1 June 1957 in Huntington, New York, had been working on handwriting recognition since the mid-1980s at GRiD, where his PalmPrint algorithm and the GRiDPad pen computer came first; his first Palm product, the Zoomer of 1993 -- built with Casio hardware, of all things -- failed, partly on recognition, and Graffiti was the answer. Graffiti is not FTRS: it asks the writer to learn a simplified alphabet, where FTRS claims to read natural handwriting at any size and position. But the lineage is direct -- the wrist in 1983, the pocket in 1996, the phone in your hand today, where gesture recognition is so ordinary nobody notices it. Technostalgism puts it as "the first touchscreen watch ever made... some 32 years before the Apple Watch", and the arithmetic holds (2015 minus 1983 is 32) if you take the Apple Watch as the endpoint. The cleaner statement is Buxton's: the AT-550 is the long nose, and the devices that followed are the face. The watch did not become the smartwatch. It became the proof that the smartwatch's input could exist.

**Takeaway:** The wrist in 1983, the pocket in 1996, the phone today. The idea travelled; the watch stayed behind. [C35250]

## Aesthetic logic

The AT-550's world is a small rectangle of glass, and the aesthetic of the study follows from that smallness. Everything is hidden and then revealed: a boring case, a premium price, a sensor you cannot see, a calculation that appears in a window eight digits wide. The beauty is in the disproportion -- a whole crystal for one character at a time, a gesture as big as the watch for a number as small as a 1 -- and in the discipline of the input: sixteen characters, no more, each drawn the same way every time, acknowledged by a single beep. The pieces this study feeds should be small, exact, and deceptive: an ordinary object that turns out to read your hand; a surface that is secretly an input; a sum written in the air. The grandeur is in the delay -- an idea from 1983 that took thirteen years to reach a pocket and thirty-two to reach a wrist again, and that was right all along.
