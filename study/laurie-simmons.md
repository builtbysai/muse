## The life: the dollhouse as a way of seeing

Laurie Simmons was born October 3, 1949, in Far Rockaway, Queens, the second of three daughters of Dorothy "Dot" Simmons, a homemaker, and Samuel "Sam" Simmons, a dentist; the family raised her in Great Neck, Long Island. Her father gave her a Kodak Brownie at six. She took a BFA from the Tyler School of Art in Philadelphia in 1971, junior year in Rome in 1969, studying printmaking, painting, and sculpture; photography she took only briefly and largely dismissed at the time. Pratt Institute was an accepted application, never attended -- do not list it as education. After a commune in upstate New York and travel in Europe in 1971-73, she moved to New York in 1973 into a SoHo loft shared with Jimmy DeSana, who helped set up a darkroom; she is now executor of the DeSana Estate/Trust. Her first mature dollhouse photographs came in 1976; "Sink / Ivy Wallpaper" (1976) is her first mature work. (C20608, leg 1 C20400-C20404; synthesis C20550-C20551)

The series spine runs in CV order: Black and White Interiors 1976-78; Early Color Interiors 1978-79; Cowboys 1979; Water Ballet 1980-81; Family Collision 1981; Ballet and Color-Coordinated Interiors 1982-83; Tourism 1983-84; Fake Fashion 1984-85; Ventriloquism 1987-90, Talking Objects 1987-89, Walking and Lying Objects 1987-91; Clothes Make the Man 1990-92; Cafe of the Inner Mind and The Music of Regret (series) 1994; The Instant Decorator 2001-04; The Love Doll 2009-11; Kigurumi, Dollers 2014; How We See 2014-15; Color Pictures / Deep Photos 2007-22; Some New 2018; Autofiction (AI) from 2022. First solos came at Artists Space, MoMA PS1, and the University of Rhode Island, all 1979; Metro Pictures and the Diane Brown Gallery in Washington, D.C., both 1981. Retrospectives: San Jose Museum of Art 1990; Baltimore Museum of Art 1997 ("The Music of Regret," 150 photographs); Gothenburg Museum of Art 2012; Neues Museum Nurnberg 2014; "Big Camera/Little Camera," Modern Art Museum of Fort Worth, October 14, 2018-January 27, 2019, and MCA Chicago, February 23-May 5, 2019. She showed in the Whitney Biennials of 1985 and 1991, the Bienal de Sao Paulo 1985, the Biennale of Sydney 1986, and the Met's "The Pictures Generation, 1974-1984" in 2009. She made two films: "The Music of Regret" (2006, MoMA premiere, Meryl Streep) and "My Art" (Venice, September 2016; North American premiere at Tribeca 2017 -- do not date Venice to 2017). She married Carroll Dunham in 1983; their children are Lena Dunham, born May 13, 1986 (not TheArtStory's implied 1985), and Cyrus Grace Dunham, born 1992, one child, called "Grace" by TheArtStory. (C20609, leg 1 C20405-C20411, C20414; synthesis C20552-C20553)

## M1 -- The miniature stage shot as a real room: the eye corrects itself

Photograph a dollhouse interior through its cutaway back wall, at eye level, in real sunlight, as if it were full-size, and the eye reads a real room first and corrects itself second; the correction is the experience. "Sink / Ivy Wallpaper" (W02, 1976) is the first mature proof: a kitchen sink against ivy wallpaper, a doll's world lit like a real kitchen, and the viewer believes the room before noticing the scale. "Untitled (Woman Standing on Head)" (W03, 1976) and "First Bathroom/Woman Kneeling" (W04, 1978) run the same camera at the same height in a bathroom and a bedroom. The mechanism is a camera position, not a prop: shoot the miniature from inside its own scale, at the height a person standing in the room would hold the camera, and the photograph stops being a photograph of a dollhouse and becomes a photograph of a room that happens to be small. Raise the camera to a god's-eye view and the trick collapses into a toy catalogue; keep it at eye level and the room is real until the viewer catches it. (C20612, leg 2 C20470; synthesis C20557)

**Takeaway:** shoot the miniature at the height of a person standing inside it, and the eye reads a real room first and corrects itself second. (C20613)


## M2 -- The solitary housewife in the wrong pose: the pose is the only narrative

Put one doll alone in a domestic room, doing the chore wrong, and the pose is the only narrative and it is unexplained; the solitude is structural. In W03 the doll does a handstand on the kitchen tiles; in W04 she kneels at the bath as if at prayer. Nobody else is in the room, nobody is coming, and nothing explains why the housewife is upside down. The wrongness is the whole story: a correct pose would be a dollhouse advertisement, a second doll would be a scene, and either would let the viewer off the hook. The same grammar returns at human scale forty years later: the love doll's poses (W17/W18), the dollers' arms reaching for the walls (W19). What changes is the scale of the body; what does not change is that one figure, alone, is doing one thing wrong in a room built for doing it right. (C20614, leg 2 C20471; synthesis C20558)

**Takeaway:** one figure alone, one chore done wrong, no explanation -- the pose is the only narrative. (C20615)


## M3 -- The scale break: one human-sized object in a miniature world

Introduce a single full-size object into a miniature scene and the scale contract breaks: the father's real camera beside its toy replica in "Big Camera/Little Camera" (W01, 1976); the life-size red lipstick the doll must push in "Pushing Lipstick I" (W05, 1979). Simmons treated this as a rule she was breaking ("absolutely verboten," kept secret); it happens only twice early and both times becomes the picture's subject. The lipstick is the stronger proof: a doll pushing a lipstick taller than she is turns a cosmetic into a boulder, and the labour of the pose -- the whole body braced against it -- is the picture. The mechanism returns inverted later: in "Walking House" (W09) the object is the body, and in "The Love Doll/Day 1 (New in Box)" (W17) the doll itself is the human-sized object. The rule is the same in both directions: one thing at the wrong scale, everything else at the right one, and the wrong thing is the subject. Break the scale of two things and the scene is a mess; break it of one and it is a picture. (C20616, leg 2 C20472; synthesis C20559)

**Takeaway:** one thing at the wrong scale, everything else at the right one -- the wrong thing is the subject. (C20617)


## M4 -- Rear-projection absorption: the doll dissolved into its setting

Photograph a monochrome doll in front of a rear-projected image -- a decorated room, a postcard view -- in the same colour as the doll, so the figure sinks into its background. In "Color Coordinated Interiors" (W08, 1982-83) and "Tourism" (1983-84) the room is a photograph of a room and the doll is posed against images of places rather than places; the doll and the backdrop share a palette and the edge between them goes soft. The mechanism predicts "How We See/Tatiana (Pink)" (W20, 2015), where the backdrop is a flat colour curtain and the woman is the image: thirty years later the projection has flattened into a colour field and the figure has dissolved into it completely. The point is never the place pictured; it is that the figure belongs to the picture of the place and to no place at all. (C20618, leg 2 C20473; synthesis C20560)

**Takeaway:** match the figure to its projected backdrop and the figure dissolves into the setting it was posed against. (C20619)


## M5 -- The object on legs: possessions take agency

Fit a domestic or consumer object -- house, cake, gun, purse, hourglass -- with a pair of slender female legs, photograph it at life size against a plain backdrop, and the object walks. The legs are the only human part and always the same legs; the object does the identifying while the legs do the animating. "Walking House" (W09, 1989), 64 by 46 inches at MoMA, is the proof at full scale: a house on legs is funny for a second and then it is not, because a house that can walk can leave. "Walking Cake II (Color)" (W10, 1989) and "Walking Gun" (W11, 1991) run the same legs under a cake and a gun, and the gun is the one that stops being funny fastest. MoMA's gloss is the mechanism in one line: "People's possessions take on agency." The lineup returns as the finale of "Magnum Opus II" and as Act III of the film: the objects walk in procession, and the procession is the argument that the things in a house outlive the people who bought them. (C20620, leg 2 C20474; synthesis C20561)

**Takeaway:** give the object legs and keep everything else plain, and the possession walks away from its owner. (C20621)


## M6 -- The identical dummy and the borrowed voice: thought bubbles instead of speech

Make ventriloquist dummies identical, differentiate them only by clothing, then give them thought bubbles instead of speech. In "Clothes Make the Man" (W12, 1990-92) the dummies are the same face in different outfits, and the clothes are doing the work of identity; the dummy count is seven in some sources and eight in Simmons's own Art21 account, so report both and state no firm number. In "Cafe of the Inner Mind" (W13, 1994) the dummy cannot speak, so Simmons speaks for it, and what she gives it to think is cliched male fantasy clipped from pornographic magazines -- the thought bubble is the only voice the dummy gets, and it is borrowed from somebody else's magazine. It escalates in "The Music of Regret IV" (W14, 1994), where the female dummy is made in Simmons's own likeness and courted by six male dummies. The mechanism is a ventriloquism with the direction reversed: the dummy is supposed to speak for the ventriloquist, but here the artist speaks for the dummy, and the dummy's thoughts are not its own. (C20622, leg 2 C20475; synthesis C20562)

**Takeaway:** the dummy cannot speak, so give it a thought bubble in somebody else's words -- the borrowed voice is the portrait. (C20623)


## M7 -- The body as a house: the room nested inside the woman

Reframe a woman's body so a domestic interior sits where her body should be -- a room between her legs, a house under her lifted dress -- and crop out the face, so the woman is present as a body and absent as a person, and the interior at the centre of her body becomes the centre of the photograph. "Room Underneath (Red)" (W15, 1998) and its siblings literalise the career's founding equation (woman = house) by putting the house inside the woman instead of the woman inside the house. For twenty years Simmons photographed a woman inside a house; here she photographs a house inside a woman, and the swap is the whole argument: the domestic interior was never the setting, it was the subject, and the woman was its container all along. The face is cropped out because a face would give the viewer a person to look at instead of a room. (C20624, leg 2 C20476; synthesis C20563)

**Takeaway:** put the house inside the woman and crop out the face, and the interior becomes the person. (C20625)


## M8 -- The surrogate that becomes the sitter: the doll/human exchange, run both ways

Run one exchange in both directions across forty years. First the doll is made to act human: a life-size doll is bought, named, and documented day by day as if it had a life -- "The Love Doll/Day 1 (New in Box)" (W17, 2009), still in its box, and "The Love Doll/Day 30 (Meeting)" (W18, 2011), meeting its maker, one work under two titling conventions (TheArtStory's "Day 30 (Meeting)" against CAM St. Louis's "Day 30/Day 2 (Meeting)"). Then the human is made to act doll: a real person wears a doll's head and latex costume in "Brunette/Red Dress/Standing Corner" (W19, Kigurumi, 2014), and real models have doll eyes painted onto closed lids in "How We See/Tatiana (Pink)" (W20, 2015). Simmons's own summary via Phaidon is the mechanism stated plainly: she began by investing inanimate objects with an interior life and ended by transforming real people into subjects "as listlessly vacant as any doll." The exchange is the career: doll toward human, then human toward doll, meeting in the middle where neither is quite a person. (C20626, leg 2 C20477; synthesis C20564)

**Takeaway:** run the exchange both ways -- doll made human, human made doll -- and the middle, where neither is quite a person, is the work. (C20627)


## The evolution: the doll leaves the house, then the house leaves the doll

Early (1976-79): miniature room, solitary doll, small prints, large psychology. The doll is inside the house and the camera is at her height. Middle (1980-94): the doll leaves the house -- into water (W07, "Family Collision: Boy Walking Bottom of Pool," c. 1981, the thinnest-documented work in the set, search-summary-only, kept because it marks the underwater turn and the first real people, friends including Cindy Sherman, in the work), into rear projection (W08), into objects that grow legs and walk at life size (W09-W11), into men who arrive as identical ventriloquist dummies (W12-W14); print scale grows to 64 by 46 inches and seven feet tall. Late (1998-2015): the body becomes the house (W15), the dollhouse becomes flat collage (W16, "The Instant Decorator," 2004), the doll becomes life-size and the artist's own home becomes the dollhouse (W17/W18), the doll's head goes on a real person (W19), and the exchange reduces to paint on closed eyelids (W20). After 2015 the mechanism leaves photography: body paint ("Some New," 2018), grisaille wall reliefs ("Deep Photos," 2020-25), text-to-image with kept AI glitches ("Autofiction," 2022-25). What does not change across fifty years: one figure, one room, one wrong scale, a surrogate standing in for a person. (C20628, leg 2 C20478-C20479; synthesis C20565)

## Aesthetic logic: the domestic as a stage, the surrogate as the self

Simmons's aesthetic logic is that the home is a stage and the person in it is a stand-in. Everything in the work serves that one equation. The dollhouse is not a toy and not a model; it is a house with the people removed and one doll put back in, so the viewer sees the house as the person sees it -- too small, too neat, too permanent. The miniature is photographed as if it were real because the domestic interior is experienced as if it were real: the kitchen and the bathroom are the rooms where a life is spent, and a doll standing on her head in one of them is a truer picture of that life than a documentary photograph of a real kitchen would be. The scale breaks, the walking objects, the dummies, and the doll/human exchange are all the same move repeated at larger and larger scales: take the thing that is supposed to stay put -- the doll in the house, the house on its lot, the dummy on the knee, the woman in the room -- and let it move, or swap it for its opposite, and watch what the room does without it. The palette follows the same logic: early work is the muted colour of 1970s interiors, wallpaper ivy and sink white; the middle work goes monochrome and then flat colour; the late work is pink, red, and latex. The colour is never decoration; it is the room's own colour, and the figure is matched to it until the figure disappears into it. That is why the work survives being copied image by image and fails when it is shown as one picture without its series: the system is the exchange, and the exchange needs the run. (C20630)

The gender of the work is structural, not topical. The solitary figure is a housewife because the dollhouse is a housewife's world; the legs under the house are female legs because the house is a woman's object; the dummies think in male fantasy because the dummy is a woman's body with a man's words in it. Simmons does not illustrate a position about domestic life; she builds a machine -- one figure, one room, one wrong scale -- and lets the machine say it. A prompt that takes the dollhouse and leaves the machine will make a cute miniature; a prompt that takes the machine will make a Simmons. (C20631)
