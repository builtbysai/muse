# Cornelis Dusart: Study Notes

**Site:** Cornelis Dusart (24 April 1660 - 1 October 1704), Dutch Golden Age genre painter of Haarlem, the pupil who inherited his master's whole world - the studio, the drawings, the unfinished pictures, the peasants themselves - and carried the Haarlem peasant genre one generation forward, louder, more burlesque, and in coloured chalk. He was born in Haarlem on 24 April 1660, the son of Johannes Dusart (1626-1691), Baroque composer, organist and bell-ringer of the Bavokerk, and Catharina Brouwers, married 14 May 1656; the family name is also spelled "du Sart" in one secondary source, and the father's birth date (16 July 1626) rests on the inscription on the portrait drawing (see C473). He became a pupil of Adriaen van Ostade in about 1675 and remained until about 1679 - the modern consensus (Robinson 2002, via Wikipedia and Harvard), though Hofstede de Groot (1910) says he "must have entered Adriaen's studio shortly before the year 1680", an older inference that conflicts with 1675 and is carried here as a disagreement, not resolved (see C473). Harvard calls Ostade his "probable master", a shade more cautious than the other sources, which state the pupilage as fact. He entered the Haarlem Guild of St Luke in January 1680 - NOT 1679, and NOT on 10 January 1679: the 1679 date is a consistent misreading of the guild ledgers, corrected by Hessel Miedema in his 1980 publication of the guild archives (vol. 2, p. 685), cited by Harvard's Susan Anderson, and independently confirmed by Alexis Bordes and the artlogic Charlatan catalogue, which both give January 1680 (see C473). He was dean of the Haarlem guild in 1692 - the Haarlem guild, not Amsterdam; reports of an Amsterdam deanship are confused and are not repeated here. When Ostade died in 1685 Dusart took over the contents of his master's studio, including the drawings of Ostade and of his short-lived brother Isack, and finished some of his unfinished paintings - and Binyon adds the detail the others omit, that he kept the finished pictures in his own possession until his own death, which the 1708 sale catalogue of his goods supports (works on which master and pupil both worked). Dated pictures by Dusart survive from almost every year between 1679 and 1702 (Onno van Seggelen); a dated picture from 1679 does not prove a 1679 guild entry, since he painted before joining the guild. His own contribution, distinct from Ostade's, is the highly finished single-peasant drawing in coloured chalks and watercolour, made as an independent saleable work: Sotheby's calls them "undoubtedly intended for sale as independent works of art", Bordes says the wash drawings were "perfectly independent of his painting production and intended for sale", and Harvard calls the single figures "unprecedented objects that could be viewed as both practical studies and collectible pieces". In his later years he devoted himself to mezzotint, a tonal process he was one of the first to practise, producing the Five Senses, the Months, and the Communia Gaudia series on the rejoicing at the Siege of Namur (c. 1695). Late in life he made a series of portrait drawings of his forebears and family members, each in a drawn oval frame with an inscription giving the sitter's identity, age, date of birth and parents' names, copied after lost paintings, the whole series done in 1694 or later (van Regteren Altena 1946, via Onno van Seggelen). He never married and lived the last eleven years of his life in the company of his two aunts - a single-source detail (arthistoryreference.com, the only source read that gives it), reported but treated as unverified until a scholarly source confirms it; his Reformed Church membership from 1682 rests on a single commercial gallery page and is flagged the same way. By his death he had acquired a remarkable collection of paintings by Italian and Dutch artists, and his probate inventory of 1704 lists hundreds of figural drawings by himself and by Adriaen and Isaac van Ostade, 125 figure studies by Cornelis Bega in two portfolios, and figure studies by Salomon de Bray and Leendert van der Cooghen - the collector's inventory is itself a map of his network. He died in Haarlem on 1 October 1704, aged 44. No words by Dusart himself were found in any source read - no letters, contracts or statements; everything quoted in this study is about him, not by him. ## The life

Cornelis Dusart was born in Haarlem on 24 April 1660, the son of a composer, and he never really left Haarlem - not for Rome, not for Amsterdam, not for anywhere a painter of his generation was supposed to go. His father Johannes Dusart (1626-1691) was organist of the Bavokerk from 1655, a Baroque composer who published the madrigal book Zang-wortel in 1653; the boy grew up above the organ loft, in a house where music was a trade. At about fifteen he entered the studio of Adriaen van Ostade, the peasant painter of Haarlem, and stayed until about 1679 - or shortly before 1680, if Hofstede de Groot's inference is right; the sources disagree and the study does not pick one silently. What he learned there was not Hals's loose brush - Hals had been dead nine years when the boy arrived - but Ostade's late manner: the cottage interior, the short ill-favoured figure, the single light, the joke told in posture. He learned it so exactly that Hofstede de Groot says it is "often very difficult to distinguish the works of the two painters", and he learned it well enough to finish the master's own pictures when the master died.

The career that follows is twenty-four years in one town, and its shape is the shape of the inheritance. He entered the guild in January 1680 - the 1679 date in most sources is a misreading of the ledgers, corrected by Miedema in 1980 and still widely repeated - and was its dean in 1692. In 1685 Ostade died and Dusart took over the studio contents, including the drawings of Ostade and of his brother Isack, and completed some unfinished paintings, which he then kept in his own possession until his death: the pupil became the custodian of the master's unfinished business, and the boundary between the two hands is still a detective problem, not a label. He painted dated pictures in almost every year from 1679 to 1702, and the arc runs from the quiet early panels - The Doll of 1680, a family at the hearth - through the big market canvas of 1683 and the etched crowd of the Village Fair of 1685, the year of the inheritance, into the mature comic theatre of the 1690s - the charlatan on his boat-stage, the fiddler of 1693, the gamblers about to play - and finally into series and mezzotint: the Five Senses, the Months, the rejoicing at the Siege of Namur, the family portraits of 1694 and later, each ancestor in a drawn oval frame with name, age and birth date inscribed. He never married, and lived his last eleven years with his two aunts - single-source, and flagged as such. He collected Italian and Dutch paintings, and his probate inventory of 1704 lists hundreds of figural drawings, his own and Ostade's and Bega's, and the figure studies of Salomon de Bray and Leendert van der Cooghen: the draughtsman as collector of other draughtsmen. He died in Haarlem on 1 October 1704, aged 44, leaving no named pupil on record - a search run expressly to find one found nobody - and no words of his own.

**Takeaway:** a twenty-four-year stay-at-home career with one move that mattered - the inheritance of 1685 - and one subject that never changed: the Haarlem peasant, inside and out, painted from the quiet panel to the etched crowd to the mezzotint night, while the joke got louder and the chalk got more colourful.

## M1. The single-figure chalk study as a finished, saleable object

A drawing of one peasant, alone, drawn large on the sheet in black and red chalk (sometimes with blue, brown and white chalk and watercolour), finished to the standard of a painting and sold as an independent work, not as a preparatory sketch. Seated Man with Large Hat, Harvard, black, red, blue, brown and white chalk on cream antique laid paper, 30.8 x 22 cm; Study of a Seated Peasant, Met, black and red chalk on beige paper; A Seated Peasant Holding Jug and Pipe, Walker Art Gallery, Liverpool. Watchable behaviour: one body fills the sheet; nothing else competes; the figure's pose, hat, and jug do all the storytelling. Sotheby's: these watercolours were "undoubtedly intended for sale as independent works of art". Grounding: Harvard, Met, Liverpool, Sotheby's, all read end to end.

**Takeaway:** one peasant, one sheet, finished like a painting - the study as the picture.

## M2. Modular reuse of model, costume and prop across many sheets

Dusart kept a stock of costume pieces and reused the same model in different poses and sheets, so a hat, a slashed doublet, or a face recurs as a module. Seated Man with Large Hat (Harvard) and Seated Man Sticking Out His Tongue (Teylers Museum, Haarlem): same costume and model, one seated leaning askew, one crouching, apparently drawn in the same session. The model appears in three further studies, the doublet in three more, the hat in three more still. Watchable behaviour: the same floppy hat and slashed doublet turn up on different bodies in different poses, like a repertory company changing roles. Grounding: Harvard catalogue entry no. 28, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** a repertory company of one model, one doublet, one hat - cast, not copied.

## M3. Multi-colour chalk layering on tinted paper

Black chalk for structure, red chalk for flesh and warmth, blue and brown chalk for clothing and shadow, white chalk for highlights, laid on cream antique laid paper, sometimes on coloured paper or parchment. Seated Man with Large Hat: black, red, blue, brown and white chalk on cream antique laid paper. The Met's Study of a Seated Peasant: black and red chalk on beige paper with framing lines in pen and brown ink. Watchable behaviour: the red chalk glows in the face and hands against the black chalk of the clothes; the white chalk lifts the hat brim and the knuckles out of the paper. Grounding: Harvard and Met object records, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the red chalk is the flesh and the white chalk is the light - colour as the signature, not the decoration.

## M4. Brown-wash chiaroscuro drawing: pen, then wash, then pen again

A composition first traced with a fine feather pen, then built up with a brush loaded with brown wash, leaving large white areas of the paper in reserve as light, then re-entered with the pen to accent details. The Charlatan, c. 1690, pen and brown ink, brown wash, 51.9 x 35.5 cm (Alexis Bordes). "Large white areas left in reserve form light which falls on the platform, ground, and figures gathered around the charlatan and illuminate the facade of the house in the background." Watchable behaviour: the light is paper-white and hard-edged against the wash; the pen returns at the end for small jokes, like the ghostly face in the window, the skirt stripes of the little girl, the dead rats on the drum rattle. Grounding: Alexis Bordes, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the light is the paper left alone, and the pen comes back last for the jokes.

## M5. The dark brown wash background in pen-and-ink drawings

In many pen-and-ink drawings Dusart lays a distinctive, boldly applied dark brown wash as a background field, against which the pen work stands out. The family portrait series, including the Portrait of the Artist's Father, is described as pen-and-ink drawings "with a distinctive and boldly applied dark brown wash background". Watchable behaviour: the figure is lit as if against a dark curtain; the wash is brushed on broadly, not hatched, so the edge of the figure is soft where the wash meets the paper. Grounding: Onno van Seggelen, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** a dark curtain brushed on, not hatched - the background as a wash, not a line.

## M6. Finished and unfinished in the same sheet

Dusart sometimes polishes one part of a figure to full finish and leaves the rest as a delicate outline, so the drawing shows its own making. Christie's Study of a seated man looking to the left, black and red chalk on Japanese paper: "While the upper part of the figure appears carefully polished with red and black chalks, the lower part was just delicately outlined." Watchable behaviour: the eye moves from a fully modelled head and torso down to legs that are only a few chalk lines, and the drawing feels caught mid-thought. Grounding: Christie's lot essay, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the top of the figure finished, the legs only lines - the drawing caught thinking.

## M7. Burlesque exaggeration: comedy, not moralising

Exaggerated expressions, gestures and accoutrements pushed past likeness into caricature, but presented as comedy rather than as a moral lesson on vice. Drunken Lovers (Harvard, c. 1670-1700): a lecherous man caressing the chin of a reticent woman, his angled standing posture and tipped hat against her downward glance and nearly spilled pitcher. The Charlatan (c. 1690): a patched huckster on a board stage selling rat poison to credulous children. Watchable behaviour: the joke is in the body: a hat tipped too far, a chin grabbed too fast, a pitcher about to tip. The viewer laughs at the gesture, not at a sermon. Grounding: arthistoryreference.com, Alexis Bordes, Harvard Drunken Lovers entry, all read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the laugh is in the gesture, not the sermon - burlesque, not moralising.

## M8. Violent action as the compositional engine

Dusart's own vein, distinct from Ostade, is "a gross, riotous, extravagant vein, with a great fondness for violent action" (Binyon): figures crash, dance, shout and collide, and the energy of the action organises the picture. Le Violon Assis (The Seated Fiddler, Bartsch 15): "The fiddler plays with a wild fantastic energy; one peasant accompanies him with crashing tankard and roaring chorus; another sits bent and sullen with his head on his hands. The landlord, with huge frame and round paunch, looks on with twinkling eyes." Village Fair (1685): "A quartet of frenzied and teetering dancers is the focus of this lively composition." Watchable behaviour: the dancers teeter; the fiddler saws; the tankard crashes; the picture is loud before it is legible. Grounding: Binyon (section read end to end) and Princeton, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the noise organises the picture - the action is the composition.

## M9. The operatic crowd: dozens of vignettes inside a classicising frame

A large fair or festival plate packs dozens of small incidents - acrobats, quacks, bullying children, marble players, a blind hurdy-gurdy player coaxed to dance - into one wide composition whose overall organisation stays orderly and stage-like. The Large Village Fair / Village Fair (1685), etching and engraving, about 25 x 33 cm, signed "Corn. duSart fe. / 1685". The inn on the left carries a banner reading "Gulde Schenk Kan" (The Golden Tankard). Nelson-Atkins: "a blind, itinerant hurdy-gurdy player has left his instrument on a bench to accommodate a couple coaxing him to dance." Watchable behaviour: the eye roams from vignette to vignette - the dancers, the acrobats on their podium, the children bullying a smaller child, the pigs and chickens scavenging - and each small scene is complete in itself. Grounding: Princeton and Nelson-Atkins, read end to end. The phrase "grand operatic stage" for this plate appears in a dealer listing found only as a search snippet and is NOT used as evidence here; the multi-vignette claim itself is grounded in Princeton and Nelson-Atkins.

**Takeaway:** a dozen small scenes sharing one square - the crowd as a stage, not a scramble.


## M10. Fine, tight, precise etched line - PARTLY FLAGGED

Dusart's etchings use a finer, tighter, more precise line than Ostade's, more concerned with anecdotal detail and less with texture. The Large Village Fair (1685): "strong contrasts and inky plate edges" (Swann, second state). The sky is "described by finely etched horizontal and diagonal lines with areas of white corresponding to unworked areas of the plate" (Nelson-Atkins). Watchable behaviour: the linework stays crisp and legible even in a crowd of dozens; the sky is built from parallel hatching, not from tone. Grounding: PARTLY FLAGGED. The sky-hatching detail is from Nelson-Atkins (read end to end). The "finer, tighter, more precise line than Van Ostade's" comparison and the "strong contrasts and inky plate edges" come from search snippets only (a dealer listing and a Swann lot note) and are flagged as such. Binyon (read) says only that "The Village Fete" (B. 16) has "a pleasing effect, with well-managed light and shade".

**Takeaway:** crisp line in a crowd of dozens - the hatching is the sky, the white is the cloud. FLAGGED in part: the comparison with Ostade rests on snippets, not on a page read end to end.

## M11. Mezzotint as a tonal, candlelit medium - PARTLY FLAGGED

In his later years Dusart devoted himself to mezzotint, the tonal process in which a roughened plate holds ink for velvety blacks and the lights are scraped and burnished out, which suits nocturnal and candlelit scenes. The series of the Five Senses in mezzotint (Bordes: "a process which he was one of the first to practice and in which the technique resembles wash"); the Months series, e.g. "April" (British Museum, Sheepshanks.3763, inscribed "C. Dusart inv: J. Gole exc. Amstelodami cum Privil. / Aprillis"); the "Communia Gaudia" series on the rejoicing at the Siege of Namur, e.g. "The Patriotic Peasant", c. 1695 (British Museum, Sheepshanks.3783); "Bonus Odor" from the Five Senses (Vassar, mezzotint). Watchable behaviour: a single candle or a dark room, figures emerging from black; the tonal gradation is smooth where etching would be linear. Grounding: Binyon (read) for the late turn to mezzotint and the Namur series; Bordes (read) for the Five Senses and the wash resemblance. The BM object page itself failed to load content (see "What was NOT accessible" in the Site line).

**Takeaway:** mezzotint is wash in print - black scraped back to light, not line built up to tone. FLAGGED in part: the BM and Vassar object details rest on snippets.

## M12. Single-source candlelight in prints and drawings

Men smoking or drinking by candlelight, a man lighting a candle: one small flame organises a dark interior, faces and hands lit, the rest lost. The "Man Lighting a Candle" series and the Five Senses prints (niceartgallery.com, read end to end), said to rival "the chiaroscuro effects found in the works of Rembrandt van Rijn, albeit on a different scale and subject matter". Watchable behaviour: the flame is the only bright point; everything else is graded darkness; the game pieces and the players' faces catch the light in turn. Grounding: niceartgallery.com, read end to end. The Rembrandt comparison is that site's claim, not a scholarly one; flagged as such.

**Takeaway:** one flame, one room, everything else graded darkness.

## M13. Finishing the master's pictures, and signing the master's name

Dusart completed Ostade's unfinished paintings and drawings, and the boundary between the two hands is often invisible; he also sometimes added Ostade's monogram "AO" to his own drawings. Liverpool: "Dusart even added Ostade's monogram 'AO' to some of his own drawings, as is the case with both this drawing and another in the Walker's Weld-Blundell collection." Hofstede de Groot: "there must be a certain number of Ostades in the style of Dusart and Dusarts in the style of Ostade, on which master and pupil both worked, for in the sale catalogue of Dusart's goods (1708)..." (page ends mid-sentence). Watchable behaviour: a drawing signed "AO" that is in fact Dusart's; a painting where the underpainting is Ostade and the finish is Dusart. Attribution becomes a detective problem, not a label. Grounding: Liverpool and Hofstede de Groot, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** authorship itself is a Dusart subject - the signature lies, the hand tells the truth.

## M14. Copying after the master: exact repetition and free variation - FLAGGED

Besides finishing Ostade's work, Dusart painted free variants of his teacher's paintings from the rural milieu, and occasionally faithful copies - for example a "Village Festival" that repeats an Ostade composition down to the last detail, and a "Farmhouse with a Well" drawn after a drawing by Isaac van Ostade. "Village Festival", c. 1690, oil on wood, 49.5 x 64.7 cm (RKD ill. no. 7581), a repetition of an Ostade painting; "Farmhouse with a Well", after Isaac van Ostade. Watchable behaviour: the same village square, the same musicians, the same pig snuffling behind the seated men - but Dusart's palette is "somewhat more radiant and details also appear a little more graphic". Grounding: FLAGGED. Both works and the palette comparison come from search snippets only (Van Ham lot note; Google Arts and Culture). No page read end to end confirms them. The mechanism is plausible and consistent with M13, but the study must not present the Van Ham palette claim as verified.

**Takeaway:** copy it exactly first, then vary it freely - repetition as apprenticeship, variation as authorship. FLAGGED: rests on snippets only.

## M15. The preparatory drawing that becomes a painting

Dusart made wash-and-chalk studies that directly prepare signed oil paintings, so a drawing and a painting can be a matched pair. "Peasant Family at the Hearth" (watercolour, brown ink and black chalk, Paris, Ecole nationale superieure des Beaux-Arts) prepares "The Doll", 1680, oil on panel, Lord Northbrook Collection. "Fish Seller at Market" (brown ink and brown and gray wash over black chalk, Brussels) prepares "Fish Seller at Market" / "Fish market", 1683, oil on canvas, 68.5 x 90.5 cm, Rijksmuseum, SK-A-98. Watchable behaviour: the same fish stall, the same seller, first in wash on paper, then in oil on canvas; the drawing is the rehearsal, the painting the performance. Grounding: Harvard Drunken Lovers entry, notes, read end to end. TITLE NOTE: the 1683 painting is called "Fish market" in the Wikipedia caption and "Fish Seller at Market" in the Harvard note. Both refer to Rijksmuseum SK-A-98.

**Takeaway:** the drawing is the rehearsal, the painting the performance - same stall, same seller, twice.

## M16. The charlatan's stage as a boat

A huckster's board stage is drawn as a ship: a sail made of a large engraving explaining the potion, a mast made of the charlatan's drum rattle, a cabin boy who is a tame monkey on a chain. The Charlatan, c. 1690 (Alexis Bordes): "Made of boards, his stage resembles a boat which floats over the crowd with a sail in the form of a large engraving which explains in great detail the effects of the potion, and for a mast, the charlatan's large drum rattle, and for a cabin boy, a tame monkey attached to a chain." Watchable behaviour: the stage floats above the crowd like a ship; children leave their games to watch; the monkey, the drum, and the poster-sail are three jokes stacked vertically. Grounding: Alexis Bordes, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the pitch is a ship - sail, mast and cabin boy, three jokes stacked vertically.

## M17. The refused caress: an amorous grammar of two bodies

Amorous couples are built from a small set of readable moves: the man's angled standing posture and tipped hat, the woman's downward glance, a nearly spilled pitcher and a roemer (drinking glass) as evidence of a rapid, ill-received advance. Drunken Lovers (Harvard, c. 1670-1700): "the man's angled standing posture and tipped hat, together with the seated woman's downward glance and nearly spilled pitcher and roemer, indicate that his rapid advances were startling and ill-received." Related sheets: "Peasant Couple Kissing" (Fondation Custodia), "Drunken Couple", 1686 (watercolour over black and red chalk on parchment, Konstanz), "Amorous Man and a Bashful Woman" (Boijmans van Beuningen). Watchable behaviour: the waffles on the table signal a festive occasion; the pitcher tilts; the woman looks down, not at him. Grounding: Harvard Drunken Lovers entry, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the advance is a posture, the refusal is a glance - the couple as a readable sentence.

## M18. The sky as a hatched field with white reserve

In the etchings, the sky is not empty paper and not solid tone: it is built from finely etched horizontal and diagonal lines, with areas of white left where the plate was unworked. The Large Village Fair (1685), Nelson-Atkins impression: "The lively scene occurs beneath a sky described by finely etched horizontal and diagonal lines with areas of white corresponding to unworked areas of the plate." Watchable behaviour: the sky has a direction - the hatching leans - and the white gaps are clouds made by not working the plate at all. Grounding: Nelson-Atkins, read end to end.

**Takeaway:** the clouds are made by not working the plate - absence as the brightest thing in the sky.


## Works

### Phase 1 - Apprentice echo, c.1679-1682: small panels, quiet rooms, Ostade's hand

The starting point is Ostade's late manner, not Hals: small panels, domestic interiors and courtyards, muted earthy tones, thin brown-ink line under gray wash in the drawings, figures observed rather than exaggerated. The 1682 Courtyard is already Dusart's own composition - frame-within-frame arch, secular-nativity calm - not a copy of an Ostade layout, which suggests the break from imitation was underway before Ostade's death.

- W1. The Doll, 1680. Oil on panel. Dimensions: 45.7 x 36.1 cm (REPORTED - Harvard catalogue note, in the Drunken Lovers entry; no museum object page for this painting was read). Location: Lord Northbrook Collection (private). A peasant family at the hearth, with a mother and small daughter playing with a doll while the father looks on. The earliest firmly dated painting in this catalogue and the key early work: small in scale, domestic rather than rowdy, and directly tied to a surviving preparatory drawing (Peasant Family at the Hearth, watercolor and black chalk, 310 x 210 mm, Ecole nationale superieure des Beaux-Arts, Paris). The quiet pole of his range - tenderness and close looking, not caricature.

- W2. Drunken Lovers, early 1680s (Harvard date c.1670-1700 - UNCERTAIN). Brown ink and gray wash over traces of black chalk on off-white antique laid paper, laid down on card. Dimensions: 10 x 8.5 cm (VERIFIED - Harvard Art Museums object page, read end to end). Location: Harvard Art Museums, Fogg Museum, object 1999.138, Maida and George Abrams Collection, Cambridge, MA. A lecherous standing man in a tipped hat caresses the chin of a seated, reticent woman; her pitcher and roemer (drinking glass) are nearly spilled, and waffles on the table signal a festive, indulgent occasion. The clearest small-scale example of the amorous-couple motif (refused caress vs eager embrace), and it carries a false signature - an unknown hand inscribed "JSteen" - which documents how often Dusart's early drawings were passed off as Jan Steen.

- W3. Figures in the Courtyard of an Old Building, 1682. Oil on oak panel. Dimensions: 49 x 37.2 cm (VERIFIED - Dulwich Picture Gallery object page, read end to end). Location: Dulwich Picture Gallery, London, DPG039, Bourgeois Bequest 1811. Signed and dated indistinctly on the side of a kennel, lower right: "C. Dusart 1682". A family group under a gently curving archway - a frame within a frame - in a quiet courtyard: a man gazes at a mother and child, a donkey rests in shadow, a dog and a cat lie calmly, clothes hang out to dry, broken window panes and creeper leaves are picked out in precise detail. Dusart's most un-Ostade-like early painting: muted earthy tones, a single flash of russet in the woman's skirt, moralising calm instead of tavern noise. Dulwich reads it as a secular nativity. FLAG: Google Arts and Culture gives the dimensions as "w372 x h490 cm", a 3.7 by 4.9 metre painting - a unit/decimal error; Dulwich's 49 x 37.2 cm is used here (see Disputes item 8).

### Phase 2 - Scale and print ambition, 1683-1685: the market canvas and the etched crowd

Public space takes over - market, village square, fair. Music becomes the organising force: fiddlers, hurdy-gurdy players, singers, dancers. The Fish Seller canvas (68.5 x 90.5 cm) is nearly twice the area of the early panels; the 1685 etchings are the most densely figured work of his life, with skies built from fine horizontal and diagonal hatching and white paper left as light. Caricature hardens - teetering dancers, bulging cheeks, splayed legs. The widest spread of his career in a single span, from the 12.7 x 10.5 cm Drunken Couple etching to the 90.5 cm-wide market canvas. Ostade died in 1685 and Dusart inherited the studio contents; the 1685 print burst reads as a declaration of succession: the pupil now owns the subject and can out-crowd the master.

- W4. Fish Seller at Market (Fish Market), 1683. Oil on canvas. Location: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, SK-A-98. A fish seller at her market stall, the largest canvas in this catalogue and Dusart's early bid for market-scene scale. NOTE: not Adriaen van Ostade's The Fish Market (1659, Louvre) - same generic title family, different artists (see Disputes item 14).

### Phase 3 - Mature comic theatre, c.1686-1693: finished drawings, bigger inns, signed late oils

The showman, the quack, the gambler-about-to-play, the fiddler as host. Jan Steen's influence is now explicit in the dealer and Harvard literature: exaggerated expressions, theatrical gestures, moralising comedy rather than plain observation. The Van Ham essay notes Dusart's palette in this phase as "somewhat more radiant" than Ostade's, with details "a little more graphic" - a snippet-only claim, flagged in M14. The finished watercolours of this decade - single figures and couples on parchment and paper - were made as independent saleable works, not studies. Large canvas returns: Country Inn at 68.3 x 57 cm. The 1693 signed fiddler is the last firmly dated painting in this catalogue and the most confident - signature and date displayed, English country-house provenance from 1815, public exhibition in 1966.

- W10. The Charlatan, c.1690 - UNCERTAIN. Pen and brown ink, brown wash, on a rounded sheet of paper. Dimensions: 51.9 x 35.5 cm (REPORTED - dealer catalogue PDF snippet; the PDF was not opened end to end). Location: private collection, France. On a summer day in the village square, a huckster in patched, once-elegant clothing vaunts a miraculous product - perhaps rat poison - from a stage built like a boat, with a sail made of a large explanatory engraving, a drum-rattle for a mast, and a tame monkey on a chain as cabin boy; children abandon their games to watch, alongside glum peasants, a woman with a basket, and a turbaned traveller. The clearest "showman as stage" composition: the seller's platform is literally a boat floating over the crowd.

- W11. Country Inn, c.1690 - UNCERTAIN. Oil on canvas. Dimensions: 68.3 x 57 cm (VERIFIED - Museum of Fine Arts, Budapest object page, read end to end; inventory 283). Location: Museum of Fine Arts, Budapest, Old Master Paintings collection, not on display. A country inn scene, signed lower left (signature partly abraded, reading "[...]art. fec."). At 68.3 x 57 cm it is the second-largest canvas in this catalogue, nearly matching the 1683 Fish Seller: after the small-panel years of the mid-1680s, Dusart returned to large canvas around 1690 for inn scenes. The "big inn" counterpart to the small tavern interiors - same crowd energy, but with room for architecture, depth, and a full cast instead of a cropped table group.

- W12. Village Festival, c.1690 - UNCERTAIN. Oil on wood. Dimensions: 49.5 x 64.7 cm (REPORTED - Van Ham auction listing snippet; RKD ill. no. 7581 cited there). Location: private collection, Germany (Van Ham, Cologne, 14 November 2024; earlier Christie's New York 16 January 1992 lot 28; David Koetser, New York/Zurich, 1975). Cheerful hustle on a village square with market stalls and playing children; in the left foreground four men smoke and drink on benches and stools on a day of rest, while a woman and a little girl step out of a house and a man peers from a doorway, all turned toward two musicians - a hurdy-gurdy player looking out at the viewer and a little boy playing the violin. Documented proof of Dusart's copy practice: he follows Ostade's original "down to the last detail" but with a somewhat more radiant palette and more graphic details - the palette claim is snippet-only (see M14). The cleanest evidence of the Ostade-to-Dusart handover - same composition, Dusart's colour and line doing the talking. FLAG: the work is Dusart's variant/copy after an Adriaen van Ostade original of shortly after 1670.

The dated 1691 anchor between the c.1690 inn pictures and the signed 1693 fiddler piece, and the clearest "anticipation" subject in the catalogue: the drama is in the waiting, the raised glasses, the not-yet-started game. ### Phase 4 - Late series and mezzotint, c.1694-1702: tone, sets, and the family record

Series thinking replaces single scenes - months of the year, five senses, seven prints of public rejoicing after the Siege of Namur, occupational types (shoemaker, surgeon, watchman, letter-reader). The single figure returns as a type, not a portrait. Mezzotint dominates - soft tonal modelling, velvety blacks, candlelight and night effects that etching cannot reach. Prints settle to a standard plate around 25 x 18 cm - a repeatable, collectible format published by Jacob Gole in Amsterdam. Dated pictures survive from almost every year between 1679 and 1702, so the late phase is not a decline but a systematisation - same peasants, now issued as sets. The 1704 probate inventory confirms the scale of the drawing practice: hundreds of figural drawings by Dusart and the Ostades, plus 125 Bega figure studies in two portfolios.

- W16. The Village Surgeon (Heelmeester; The Operation), c.1695 - UNCERTAIN. Etching. Dimensions: 25.2 x 17.7 cm (REPORTED - auction listing, Hollstein VI.55.13). A second source, the Art Gallery of Greater Victoria, records its impression "The Operation", 1695, at plate 25.9 x 18 cm - a small discrepancy, see Disputes item 6. Location: impressions in several collections, including the Art Gallery of Greater Victoria (The Operation, 1695) and the Petit Palais, Paris (Le Barbier ou chirurgien de village, Dutuit 18, GDUT4277). An operation on a man's arm performed by an archaic village doctor, the patient seated in a chair while a woman behind him clasps her hands together. Dusart's medical subject - the barber-surgeon at work - which sits beside the shoemaker, the fish seller, and the quack as one of his occupational portraits: a job shown as a small piece of theatre, with the onlooker's clasped hands doing the emotional work. The "wince" mechanism: pain, attention, and a witness, all in one tight group.

- W17. The Patriotic Peasant, 1695. Mezzotint on laid paper. Dimensions: sheet 32.9 x 23 cm, plate 25.9 x 17.9 cm, image 23.7 x 17.9 cm (VERIFIED - Yale Center for British Art collections page, read end to end). Location: Yale Center for British Art, Paul Mellon Fund (also British Museum, Sheepshanks.3783, which holds a complete set of the series). A male figure dancing to the left in a landscape, holding his hat in his right hand and a glass in his left, from the series Communia Gaudia (The widespread rejoicing at the Siege of Namur), published by Jacob Gole in Amsterdam, c.1695. It ties Dusart's peasant to a dated public event - the 1695 rejoicing after the Siege of Namur - and it is mezzotint, his tonal medium: the dancing figure is modelled in soft black-to-white gradation rather than etched line. The "one dancer, hat in hand, glass raised" motif, and proof that his peasants could carry topical, celebratory meaning, not only timeless vice.

- W18. Seated Man with Large Hat, 17th century (undated). Black, red, blue, brown, and white chalk on cream antique laid paper. Dimensions: 30.8 x 22 cm (12 1/8 x 8 11/16 in.) (VERIFIED - Harvard Art Museums object page, read end to end). Location: Harvard Art Museums, object 2009.208, Maida and George Abrams Collection. A mature man with thinning hair and a goatee, in a slashed doublet and floppy hat, leaning askew in a chair. The Harvard entry is the single most informative text read in this leg: the same model, doublet, and hat recur across many sheets (same costume in a crouching pose at Teylers Museum; the model again in Vienna, Rotterdam, and Amsterdam sheets), implying Dusart kept a stock of costume pieces and used one model across a session. The purest example of his single-figure chalk studies - the work his contemporaries valued most - and evidence for his method: repeat the model, vary the pose, change the chalks. The "one sitter, many sheets" system: a reusable cast, not a one-off portrait.

- W19. Study of a Seated Peasant, undated. Black and red chalk on beige paper, framing lines in pen and brown ink. Dimensions: 26.7 x 19.5 cm (10 1/2 x 7 11/16 in.) (VERIFIED - Metropolitan Museum of Art object page, read end to end; object 40.91.13). Location: Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Gift of Harold K. Hochschild, 1940. Its authorship was forgotten along the way - a later owner wrote "Sachteleven" in the lower left corner, assuming it was by the Utrecht artist Cornelis Saftleven - which documents the same attribution drift that haunts the whole oeuvre. The companion to W18: same system, different sitter, same collectible finish. The "single figure, two chalks, no background" unit - the smallest complete Dusart.

- W20. The Famous Shoemaker (De Vermaarde Schoenmaaker), undated. Etching. Dimensions: sheet 25 x 17.8 cm (9 13/16 x 7 in.) (VERIFIED - Metropolitan Museum of Art object page, read end to end; object 56.597.55). A Petit Palais impression measures 25.3 x 18 cm, corroborating. Location: Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, Elisha Whittelsey Collection (also Te Papa, Wellington; Petit Palais, Paris, GDUT4271). Published by Jacob Gole, Amsterdam. A busy shoemaker's shop turned into popular theatre - the shoemaker seated at left with his awl, a heavy-set woman dominating the centre with splayed legs and a cap folded like ass's ears, pointing at an upside-down funnel hanging above the crouching assistant who fits her shoe, while two workers labour quietly in the background and tools litter the floor. The Te Papa essay (after Linda Stone-Ferrier) reads it as a comic exploration of fooling and being fooled: the funnel is foolishness, the shoe-fitting is a sexual metaphor, the signature itself is wittily inscribed on a piece of shoe leather, "Corn. Dusart fecit et invent". Dusart's most analysed print and the best-documented case of his humour being deliberate, theatrical, and learned - stock types from rederijker plays and commedia dell'arte, not random coarseness. The "workshop as stage" mechanism with the richest symbolic props.

## Evolution

What he kept from Ostade: the Netherlandish peasant tradition itself - the cottage interior, the tavern, the short figure type, the single light, the drawing habit underneath everything, the signature-and-date habit that makes the chronology traceable at all (dated pictures survive from almost every year between 1679 and 1702). The subject never changed; the handling did.

What the 1685 inheritance did: it turned the pupil into the custodian. The 1685 print burst - the Village Fair, the Drunken Couple etching, the hurdy-gurdy and singing plates - is the declaration of succession, and the finishing of Ostade's unfinished pictures, kept in his own possession until his death, made authorship itself a Dusart subject: the signature lies, the hand tells the truth.

What changed, in one paragraph: from quiet small panels and domestic courtyards in muted earthy tones (c.1679-1682), to the big market canvas and the etched crowd, music as the organising force and caricature hardening (1683-1685), to mature comic theatre - the charlatan on his boat-stage, the bigger inns, the finished watercolours made as independent saleable works, the signed 1693 fiddler (c.1686-1693), to late series and mezzotint - months, senses, rejoicing at Namur, occupational types, the family portraits of 1694 and later (c.1694-1702) - the same peasants, now issued as sets, in tone instead of line.

What stayed constant: the Haarlem peasant as the one true subject, inside and out, for twenty-four years; the small format (almost everything is under 70 cm on the long side, and the drawings are jewel-like, 10-30 cm); the single-figure chalk study as the work his contemporaries valued most; the drawing that prepares the painting; and the joke told in the body - a hat tipped too far, a pitcher about to tip, a dancer about to fall.


## Disputes and unverified items (flagged)

1. Guild entry date - 1679 vs 1680. Most sources, including Wikipedia and Dulwich, say Dusart entered the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1679, often precisely 10 January 1679. The Harvard catalogue entries (Susan Anderson, on both W2 and W18) state this arises from a consistent misreading of the guild ledgers, corrected by Hessel Miedema in De Archiefbescheiden van het St. Lukasgilde te Haarlem (1980, vol. 2, p. 685): the correct year is 1680. The 1679 date "widely persists" anyway. Do not state 10 January 1679 as fact. See C473.
2. Pupilage dates - c. 1675-1679 vs shortly before 1680. The modern consensus (Robinson 2002, via Wikipedia and Harvard) is c. 1675-1679. Hofstede de Groot (1910, p. 141) says Dusart "must have entered Adriaen's studio shortly before the year 1680". The earlier date rests on modern scholarship, the later on Hofstede de Groot's inference. Carried as a disagreement, not resolved. See C473.
3. Ostade vs Dusart - the inherited studio. When Ostade died in 1685 Dusart took over the studio contents and finished unfinished paintings and drawings, including work by Ostade's brother Isack (d. 1649). The NiceArtGallery survey and the Te Papa essay both warn that distinguishing Ostade's late work, work finished by Dusart, and Dusart's own early work "can sometimes be challenging". Specific cases in this study: (a) the V and A Rustic Courtyard is inscribed "ostade", was sold at Christie's in 1840 as "Adrian Ostade. Two children in a courtyard of a cottage", and is now only "attributed to" Dusart; (b) the Met Study of a Seated Peasant (W19) was later inscribed "Sachteleven"; (c) Drunken Lovers (W2) was falsely signed "JSteen"; (d) Christie's "Peasants in a tavern" drawing was, per Susan Anderson and Bernhard Schnackenburg, possibly reworked by Dusart on a sketch first laid out by Ostade; (e) Dusart even added Ostade's "AO" monogram to some of his own drawings (National Museums Liverpool, on WAG 1995.85). ## Aesthetic logic

Cornelis Dusart's pictures are built on a single tension: the peasant against the stage the peasant is put on. Everything he paints is a negotiation between the two. The peasant is Dutch - short, ill-favoured, thickset, in a slashed doublet and a floppy hat, with a jug, a pipe, a fiddle, a tankard. The stage is whatever Dusart builds around him: a cottage interior with one sunbeam, a tavern table with a tilting pitcher, a village square with a boat for a stage, an etched fair with a dozen small scenes, a black mezzotint plate with one candle in it. The joke is what joins them at first: one tight knot of bodies, one bodily act, one laugh at the peasants' expense, staged in a small panel or on a small sheet a collector could hold at arm's length. Then the joke gets louder, and the same mechanism carries a different meaning: the same peasant, the same short figure, the same single light - but now the act is a fiddler sawing while the whole street stops, a charlatan selling rat poison from a ship, a shoemaker's shop turned into a play about fooling and being fooled, and the light says the peasant is not a butt but a performer. Where Ostade paints the household, Dusart paints the theatre; where Steen paints the moral, Dusart paints the gesture. The single-figure chalk study is the hinge of the whole logic: one body, drawn large, finished like a painting, sold like a painting - the peasant taken out of the crowd and given the whole sheet, so the viewer has to look at the face, the hat, the hands, and nothing else. And underneath it all is the inheritance: the pupil who finished the master's pictures and signed the master's monogram, so that authorship itself becomes part of the picture - a drawing signed "AO" that is in fact Dusart's is the most Dusart-like object there is, a joke about who made it, told by the maker himself.

## Positions

P1. Dusart is the hinge of the Haarlem peasant genre: the last pupil who carried Ostade's world past 1685 into the 18th century.
P2. His originality is not in inventing subjects but in pushing Ostade's subjects toward burlesque, noise, and violent action.
P3. The single-figure chalk study is his real invention: a drawing that is both a working study and a finished, saleable picture.
P4. Colour is his signature move: red, blue and white chalk turn a black-chalk study into a small painted object.
P5. He is a comic artist, not a moralist: drunkenness and lechery are staged as theatre, not condemned as vice.
P6. His crowd scenes work because the vignettes are modular: each small incident is complete, and the whole is a stage, not a scramble.
P7. The mezzotints show a different Dusart: tonal, nocturnal, and interested in a single light source rather than a crowd.
P8. Attribution is part of the work: finishing Ostade's pictures and signing Ostade's monogram make authorship itself a Dusart subject.
P9. He learned by copying and then by varying: exact repetition first, free variation second, personal vivacity last.
P10. His peasants are observed, not idealised: the same model, hat and doublet recur because he drew from life in his own studio.
P11. The wash drawings are his most personal work, freer than the oils and independent of them, made for collectors who kept them carefully.
P12. He is a bridge figure, not a peak: below Ostade in delicacy and technique (Hofstede de Groot's verdict), but the last great master of the Haarlem peasant genre.
