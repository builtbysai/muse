# Federico Silva: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #339.

**Site:** Federico Hector Gutierrez Silva (Mexico City, 16 September 1923 - 30 November 2022, Tlaxcala studio, aged 99), Mexican painter, muralist, sculptor, kinetic artist, writer, UNAM researcher, member of number of the Academia de Artes. Siqueiros's assistant from 1944; destroyer of his own figurative past on television in 1967; pioneer of kinetic art in Mexico; proposer of the Espacio Escultorico at UNAM in 1977; author of the 5,000-square-metre cave painting El Principio at Huites; founder, by donation, of the first museum in Latin America devoted exclusively to sculpture, the Museo Federico Silva in San Luis Potosi, opened 2003. Identity anchors: the destruction that clears the ground; the solar object; the suspended structure; sound as a building material; the viewer who completes the geometry; straight lines only; the figure geometrized, not erased; the incised sign; polychrome stone; the stacked glyph; the serpent as a line in the lava field; the altar and the gate; the cave as canvas; the museum as a work; the gift as civic duty.

**Doorway:** Hop from the sebastian study: from the youngest hand Silva invited into the Espacio Escultorico to Silva himself, the kinetic sculptor who detonated Sebastian's trajectory and built Ocho Conejo beside his Colotl. #338 closed with a sculptor who folds one plane until it becomes a city symbol, and who learned the fold by walking the Espacio Escultorico corridor as its youngest hand. #339 opens with the man who proposed that corridor, who invited him into it, and who spent the previous thirty years learning how to make geometry move: first by destroying his own paintings on television with a bow and arrow, then by hanging prisms in the sun, then by laying serpents low across the same lava field where Sebastian's scorpion stands. Where Sebastian reduces the city until one fold carries it, Silva reduces the whole of art until one straight line carries it, and then he lets the sun, the sound, and the walking spectator finish the work. The doorway is the invitation itself: Silva's hand on Sebastian's shoulder, in Sainz's word a detonation, and the corridor that detonation opened.

## M1. Destruction as the turn

The governing act, and it comes before the work, not after it. In 1967 Silva destroyed his figurative work on television with a bow and arrow, to make way for abstraction. Manuel Felguerez remembered it: Silva, heir of the Escuela Mexicana de Pintura, "grabbed his old pictures and smashed them, as if to free himself from a past and start a new path." The destruction is not a tantrum and not a performance for its own sake; it is a method. The figurative past is a material, and the method is to break it in public so there is no going back. The television matters: the break is witnessed, which makes it irreversible in a way a private bonfire is not. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M2) rehearsed the monument as a cube the hand could refold, and the refolding was the work; Silva rehearses the new language by breaking the old language where everyone can see it, and the breaking is the work. Escobedo (#335 M8) engineered failure, boats designed to sink; Silva engineers a beginning, pictures designed to be shot through, and the beginning is the work. **Takeaway:** Break the old work in public before building the new one; the break is the first act, not the backstory. Watch the break: when a piece begins with a shattering, a burning, or a shot-through image, and only then reassembles as geometry, the mechanism is on.

## M2. The solar object

The sculpture whose material is light, not mass. The solar objects of the 1960s and 1970s are prisms, Fresnel lenses, mirrors, magnets, laser, and bodies suspended in space. The work does nothing in the dark; the sun, or a lamp, switches it on. The laser method of the 1980 mural is the same operation made into a working procedure: a laser projected through prisms to compose across three walls. The object is not the prism; the object is the beam the prism makes, the bright spot that crawls across a wall as the sun moves, the suspended body that turns in the light. No photograph of a solar object itself was found this run, and the study says so plainly: the mechanism is text-grounded, from the obituary essay's inventory and the Gaceta triptych article, and from the mural photograph where the geometry visibly continues around corners as if a beam had drawn it. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M13) built the armature and let the sea finish it; Silva builds the prism and lets the sun finish it, and the finishing is the work in both cases. Gurria (#336 M6) let sunlight be the only motor of her frozen stone pinwheels; Silva lets sunlight be the only material of his suspended prisms, and the motor becomes the medium. **Takeaway:** Build the prism, not the picture; let the light do the drawing. Watch the light: when a beam splits through glass, a bright spot crawls across a wall, and the work goes dark when the sun goes down, the mechanism is on.

## M3. Suspension: weight denied

Metal geometric structures designed to hang, recalling the flight of birds or spaceships. That is the kinetic doctrine stated plainly in the FES Zaragoza restoration note for Nave de Carnegiae: the sculpture refuses the ground. A heavy-looking frame floats, turns slowly on its suspension, casts a moving shadow below itself, and the eye keeps checking what holds it up. The unidentified steel frame photographed in front of the Museo Federico Silva, black open frame on a rust-red base, thin vertical rods rising high, small red weights hanging, is consistent with the doctrine, and it is cited here only as suggestive, never as a titled work. Against the chain: Cabrera (#337 M8) suspended the form so gravity threatened it; Silva suspends the form so gravity is denied it, and the denial is the work. Sebastian (#338 M9) made the scale jump from a 20 cm cube to a 64 m letter; Silva makes the weight jump from a steel frame to a bird, and the frame stays a frame while the eye supplies the flight. **Takeaway:** Hang the structure and let the air hold it; the suspension is the sculpture. Watch the float: when a steel frame hangs in the air, turns slowly, and casts a shadow that moves faster than the frame, the mechanism is on.

## M4. Sound as a building material

Kinetic work with a composed soundtrack as a structural element, not decoration. The 1969 Museo de Arte Moderno show Arte cinetico presented 40 pieces with music by the composer Manuel Enriquez, and the workshop made kinetic works with sound and light as a matter of course. Motion and tone are locked together: a turning prism changes pitch, a swinging body strikes or passes a tone, and there is silence when the mechanism stops. No recording or program survives in the sources read, and no list of the 40 pieces exists there either; the study carries that absence as a gap, not as a license to invent what the music sounded like. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M1) kept time with a hammer and listened to stone; Silva keeps time with a composer and lets the turning prism keep it, and the beat is the structure in both cases. Sebastian (#338 M14) taught the fold at hand scale so the city could learn it; Silva lets the sound teach the motion, and the lesson is the work. **Takeaway:** Compose the sound with the mechanism, not over it; when the motion stops, the music must stop too. Watch the lock: when a turning form and a tone change together, and the silence lands exactly when the movement lands, the mechanism is on.

## M5. The viewer completes the geometry

The work is composed for a moving spectator, not a fixed viewpoint. Historia de un espacio matematico was composed with a laser projected through prisms, taking into account the spectator in movement and his route, and Silva says it himself: "the spectator imposes a geometry on it to the extent that he moves and observes it. It is an art that changes, it transforms." The photograph viewed first-hand proves the point: three walls of straight-line geometry that only compose into one image from the stairwell's moving viewpoint, lines that align and break apart as you walk, thin red and green horizontals that run across all three walls like a beam. Against the chain: Goeritz (#321) built emotional architecture as a sequence of spaces the body walks; Silva builds the sequence into a single stairwell, and the walk is the composition. Sebastian (#338 M5) built a tunnel of arches the driver completes by driving; Silva builds a triptych the walker completes by walking, and the completion is the work in both cases. **Takeaway:** Compose for the moving spectator; the geometry is finished by the walk, not by the wall. Watch the walk: when three walls compose into one image only from the moving viewpoint, and the lines align and break apart as you move, the mechanism is on.

## M6. Straight lines only

A self-imposed rule, stated in his own words: curves soften, straight lines integrate precise formal structures. The mural uses no curves at all. The field is built entirely from triangles, prisms, and hard diagonals; every edge is a cut; the image feels engineered rather than drawn. The photograph viewed first-hand confirms it: across all three walls of Historia de un espacio matematico, not one curve is visible. The rule is what makes the geometry legible at a glance and what makes the moving spectator's completion possible: a curve would blur the alignment, a straight line either aligns or it does not. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M1) let one fold do the drawing; Silva lets one straight line do the drawing, and the discipline is the same operation with a different edge. Cabrera (#337 M1) reduced the body until one curve carried it; Silva refuses the curve entirely, and the refusal is the signature. **Takeaway:** Ban the curve and let the straight line carry the whole image; the rule is the style. Watch the edge: when every line is a cut, every shape a triangle or a prism, and the image reads like engineering, the mechanism is on.

## M7. The figure geometrized, not erased

Pre-Hispanic gods and animals reduced to slabs, triangles, and incisions, but the figure is still there, and it is what holds the sculpture up physically. Silva's own rule: "abstraction and figuration march together... sculpture demands very precise, very concrete forms. Figuration is what sustains it physically." The grasshopper you can only read once you have walked around it: Ocho Conejo, viewed first-hand from two angles, a giant folded grasshopper in volcanic stone, tall vertical spike, long horizontal beam with chevron reliefs, triangular pierced base. The eagle-knight that is three slabs and still a bird: Caballero Aguila, vertical head block, two swept-down wing slabs, glowing pale under spotlights. The rain god that is a zigzag: Chac, a stone ribbon ending in a tall triangular blade with a small incised triangle. The bat that is a V and a cube: Murcielago, two huge triangular wings with stepped inner notches, cube head, blocky body with a cross incision at the base. Against the chain: Gurria (#336 M7) reduced the figure to silhouette plus one incision; Silva reduces the god to slabs plus one incision, and the incision is the god. Sebastian (#338 M6) reduced the animal until the fold was the animal; Silva reduces the animal until the slab is the animal, and the species is the angle of the cut. **Takeaway:** Geometrise the figure, never erase it; the figure is what holds the stone up. Watch the reading: when a grasshopper is a spike and a beam, an eagle is three slabs, and a bat is a V and a cube, and each still reads as the animal, the mechanism is on.

## M8. The incised sign

The stone surface is carved with a small vocabulary of marks, chevron and herringbone rows, X-crosses, drilled dot-eyes, triangular notches, so the surface reads like a text. A plain grey mass that, up close, is covered in regular incised patterns; the pattern, not the silhouette, tells you what the thing is. The photographs prove it: the chevron reliefs on Ocho Conejo's long beam; the dense herringbone panel and the X-incised tower faces on Altar I; the X-crossed faces and paired dot eyes on Interseccion; the paired drilled dot holes on Ofrenda; the incised glyphs on the gridded base slab of Principio at Matamoros. The incision is the writing system of the whole career: the same marks appear on the 1980 grasshopper, the 1988 altar, and the 2000 polyptychs, which is how you know they are a vocabulary and not a decoration. Against the chain: Cabrera (#337 M2) stamped one letter, the T, across masks and torsos and stencils; Silva stamps one sign, the X or the dot pair, across grasshoppers and altars and totems, and the stamping is the authorship. Sebastian (#338 M4) stamped one letter at 64 m; Silva stamps one sign at the scale of a fingertip, and the sign does the same work at either size. **Takeaway:** Carve the sign into the stone, not onto it; the surface is the text. Watch the close-up: when a plain grey mass resolves, up close, into regular chevrons, X-crosses, and dot pairs, and the pattern tells you what the thing is, the mechanism is on.

## M9. Polychrome stone

Tlalmimilolpa stone painted in flat colors, pink, yellow, blue, teal, black, white, so color becomes a second structure laid over the stone one. A totem whose blocks change color as well as shape; the same form in two colors reads as two different objects; wings that shade from green to blue like a spectrum. The labels read in the photographs say it plainly: En Espera, 1995, "Piedra Tlalmimilolpa y fierro policromado", white stone block figure with two huge gradient wings spread upward in a V; Interseccion, 2000, "Piedra Tlalmimilolpa polychromada", tall totem of interlocked T- and cross-shaped polychrome blocks; Murcielago II, 2000, "fierro policromado"; El Jugador, 2001, flat polychrome steel figure mid-kick, red and black body, blue striped belt. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M8) painted the monument one color and let the color be the reading; Silva paints the stone many colors and lets the color be the second structure, and the structure is the difference. Gurria (#336 M3) used the split pair with exactly one differing parameter, color; Silva uses the stacked totem with color as the differing parameter on every block, and the totem is a split pair repeated until it is a column. **Takeaway:** Paint the stone in flat colors and let color be a second structure; the block is one thing, its color is another. Watch the second structure: when a totem's blocks change color as well as shape, and the same form in two colors reads as two different objects, the mechanism is on.

## M10. The stacked glyph (polyptych / totem)

The work is built by stacking one interlocked unit, a T-block, a cross-block, a slotted rectangle, until the stack itself is the sculpture. A tower that reads as writing, each block a letter; the eye climbs it the way it reads a column of glyphs; removing one block would collapse the meaning, not just the pile. Interseccion is the proof, a tall totem of interlocked T- and cross-shaped polychrome blocks with X-crossed faces and paired dot eyes, its shadow on the wall a second, flat version of itself. Ofrenda is the proof in pale stone, a symmetric stepped-diamond polyptych of layered horizontal bars and diagonal struts with drilled dot holes in pairs and a small cross at the center. Principio at Matamoros is the proof at civic scale, a tall stacked totem of dark stone glyph-blocks: interlocked crosses, slotted rectangles, gridded base slab with incised signs, triangular inlays. The polyptych is Silva's own word for the 2000 works, and the totem is the same word grown large. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M2) laid one cube down in the hand until it was a monument; Silva lays one glyph-block down in the stack until it is a totem, and the stacking is the reading. Cabrera (#337 M4) laid one body down in sequence until it was a step; Silva lays one block down in sequence until it is a sentence, and the sentence is the work. **Takeaway:** Stack one interlocked unit until the stack is the sculpture; the tower reads as writing. Watch the climb: when the eye climbs a totem the way it reads a column of glyphs, and removing one block would collapse the meaning, the mechanism is on.

## M11. The serpent as a line in the lava field

Sculpture that follows the ground instead of rising from it: a line laid across volcanic rock. Serpientes del Pedregal, 1986, volcanic stone, in the Paseo de las Esculturas, is the work, and Silva's account of it is the turn from geometric painting to monumental sculpture integrated with nature. The watchable behavior is a low, long form that the walker follows like a path; the lava field, not a plinth, is the base; the work is found by walking, not by looking up. The study must say plainly what it cannot show: no dedicated photograph of Serpientes del Pedregal was found anywhere searched, and it is not identifiable in the Paseo panorama photograph. The mechanism is text-grounded, from the 2022 interview and the Gaceta triptych article, and the absence of a photograph is itself part of the record. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M11) exhibited in the median and timed the work to the speed of the road; Silva exhibits in the lava field and times the work to the speed of the walk, and the slowness is the point. Escobedo (#335) built Coatl, a serpent, in the same Paseo, and Silva's serpents lie in the same lava a few steps away; the two serpents are the two readings of one field, hers a gate, his a line. **Takeaway:** Lay the sculpture low and let the ground be the plinth; the work is found by walking. Watch the path: when a low, long form leads the walker across a lava field, and the field, not a plinth, is the base, the mechanism is on.

## M12. The altar and the gate

Sculpture as a threshold you could walk through: splayed legs forming a triangular arch, a tower with a doorway in it. The work frames the space behind it; the viewer passes under or between its legs; the empty triangle in the middle is the point of the piece. Altar I, viewed first-hand, is the proof: two splayed trapezoidal legs forming a triangular arch, two blocky towers with X-incised faces on top, a big tilted trapezoidal back panel covered in dense herringbone incisions, and the triangular void between the legs is the center of the composition. Murcielago II carries the same void at a smaller scale, a triangular cutout at the base; Ocho Conejo carries it as a pierced triangular base. The Espacio Escultorico itself is the monumental version: a ring of 64 prisms you enter, proposed by Silva in 1977 with Goeritz, Felguerez, Escobedo, Hersua, and Sebastian, inaugurated 23 April 1979. Against the chain: Escobedo (#335) built gates you pass through as a refusal of the monument; Silva builds altars you pass through as the monument itself, and the passage is the work. Sebastian (#338 M3) built gates you drive through as the monument; Silva builds altars you walk through as the monument, and the walking is the difference between their two thresholds. **Takeaway:** Build the threshold and leave the middle empty; the void is the point. Watch the passage: when a work frames the space behind it, and the empty triangle in the middle is what the eye keeps returning to, the mechanism is on.

## M13. The cave as canvas

Painting returned to the cave: the rock's own texture and shape become the composition. El Principio at Huites, 1993-1996, is the proof: invited in January 1992 by the Comision Federal de Electricidad to make a sculpture for the dam at the presa Luis Donaldo Colosio, Choix, Sinaloa, Silva instead painted a cave, 5,000 square metres, 225 to 235 m long, 9 m wide and 9 m high, drawings projected onto the rock, made with more than 50 local artists, artisans, masons, and dam workers over four years. The work imitates rupestrian painting, referring to the origins of pre-Columbian art and to the theme of life and death, and the 1996 thesis stresses the influence of the cave surface on the composition and the gradation of gloom and light as a tonal message. The watchable behavior is figures that follow the rock's bulges and cracks; a painting you walk through in darkness with electric light; the oldest and the newest art in the same gesture. No photograph of the cave interior was found this run, and the study says so. Against the chain: Cabrera (#337 M10) built a museum in a garden because the institution would not house her; Silva painted a cave because the dam commission would not hold him, and the cave is the bigger museum. Gurria (#336 M11) built the hydraulic monument, a pyramid and towers for water; Silva painted the cave beside the water, and the painting is the dam's other face. **Takeaway:** Paint the cave, not the canvas; let the rock's bulges and cracks compose the picture. Watch the dark: when figures follow the rock's own shape, and the painting is walked through in darkness with electric light, the mechanism is on.

## M14. The museum as a work

The artist builds his own museum and the collection becomes a self-history. The Museo Federico Silva Escultura Contemporanea opened 18 September 2003 (a 19 September variant exists in one walkthrough), in San Luis Potosi, in the former Escuela Modelo of 1905-1907, a neoclassical school with a vanguardist tie-rod roof structure so the roof could become exhibition space; the first museum in Latin America devoted exclusively to sculpture. Silva donated the work; governor Fernando Silva Nieto backed it, the two men having met by chance on a plane, same first surname; replicas were made in Tlaxcala to test how each sculpture would sit in the building, then brought one by one. The museum holds 68, 80, or 84 of his sculptures depending on the source, carried here as a range. The watchable behavior is a permanent exhibition that is also an autobiography in stone: the visitor walks the artist's life room by room, down to Scriptum in the basement, "writing", his homage to humanity. The corridor photograph viewed first-hand shows it: framed geometric works on both walls, a stairwell descending in the foreground, a black panel at the far end. Against the chain: Cabrera (#337 M10) built a museum in a garden because the institution would not house her, and her museum closed in 2006; Silva built a museum in a school because the institution would not hold him, and his museum is still open, which is the difference between a house and a donation. Sebastian (#338) founded the Museo Sebastian in Chihuahua in 1995; Silva founded his museum in 2003, and the two museums are the two answers to the same question, what a sculptor does with a life's work. **Takeaway:** Build the museum and let the collection be the autobiography; the visitor walks the life room by room. Watch the walk: when a permanent exhibition is also a life in stone, and the basement holds a work called "writing", the mechanism is on.

## M15. The gift: public art as civic duty

The work is given, not sold, to a place that needs it, and the gift is the political act. Principio, donated to Matamoros, Tamaulipas, unveiled in September 2011 by the Puente Internacional Puerta Mexico, in a year of violence in Tamaulipas, is the proof: a tall stacked totem of dark stone glyph-blocks, interlocked crosses, slotted rectangles, gridded base slab with incised signs, triangular inlays, photographed and viewed first-hand, standing in a border plaza as if it grew there. Silva's words carry the mechanism: "my preoccupation, the neuralgic center of my work, has always been Mexico... the artist has to be inside reality, and if not, he stops being an artist and becomes only a merchant"; and his standing line, "art will be alive while it dialogues permanently with society"; and the gift's own logic, "it no longer belongs to me... it will defend itself." The work belongs to the city from the moment it is handed over. Against the chain: Sebastian (#338 M12) let the city argue about the price, and the argument kept the work visible; Silva gives the work away, and the gift keeps the work visible, and the two are the same operation from opposite ends. Gurria (#336 M14) built the collective monument, five hands, and the corridor breaks if one is removed; Silva gives the single monument to a border city, and the city holds it if he is removed. **Takeaway:** Give the work away and let the city defend it; the gift is the political act. Watch the handover: when a totem appears in a border plaza as if it grew there, and the work belongs to the city from the moment it is handed over, the mechanism is on.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic is: geometry is not a style, it is the structure of everything, mural, sculpture, engineering, the cave, the city, and the artist's job is to make that structure move, sound, shine, or stand in the street. He keeps the figure, pre-Hispanic gods, animals, the human body, but only as a scaffold for geometry: the figure is what holds the stone up physically. He destroys in order to begin (M1), he lets the sun and the walking spectator finish the work (M2, M5), he lets sound build with the mechanism (M4), he refuses the curve (M6), he carves the sign into the surface (M8), he paints the stone a second structure (M9), he stacks the glyph until it is a sentence (M10), he lays the serpent low in the lava (M11), he leaves the middle of the altar empty (M12), he paints the cave (M13), he builds his own museum (M14), and he gives the work away (M15). His own summary, from the 1993 Academia admission speech: art must be prospective, must invent, "but without confusing itself with others... invention does not mean abjuring nationality." And from the 2022 interview, at 99: "all arts complement each other, they are twins... in the end, they are the same thing." That is why the same straight line can be a mural, a prism, a totem, and a cave: nothing is decorated, everything is reduced to the operation, and the operation is always something the sun can switch on, the walker can complete, or the city can defend.

## Evolution

1923 Mexico City, born 16 September, Federico Hector Gutierrez Silva; childhood drawing, carving animal heads into the soles of his shoes; sees Rivera's La creacion (1922) at San Ildefonso while at the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria -> before art, studies medicine, veterinary medicine, law, and anthropology (medicine reported by INBAL and the obituary essay, absent from Spanish Wikipedia); self-taught in art from books: encaustic, fresco, tempera -> 1944 becomes Siqueiros's assistant, collaborates on Nueva democracia, Palacio de Bellas Artes; through Siqueiros meets Rivera, Mendez, O'Higgins, Revueltas; later dines with Pablo Neruda at Bellas Artes; Siqueiros's lesson: integrate the mural space into a whole instead of dividing it into little squares -> 1945 first solo show, Galeria de Arte Mexicano of Ines Amor -> late 1940s travels Europe after the war, lives mainly in Austria and Italy; on return organizes the Primer Salon de la Pintura, shows Aregumentum Baculinum, honorable mention from INBA (single-sourced) -> 1949 mural La lucha del pueblo mexicano a traves de su historia, old seat of the Conservatorio Nacional de Musica -> 1950 begins painting his own murals -> 1951-53 La tecnica al servicio de la paz, ESIA-IPN, commissioned 1951, executed 1952, dated 1953 in the INBAL release, first outdoor mural painting; founds and directs the magazine 1935 with Rivera, Mendez, Revueltas, and others -> 1960s turns to sculpture, works alongside Pedro Coronel (single-sourced); first kinetic works appear after his Europe trip -> 1967 destroys his figurative work on television with bow and arrow, to make way for abstraction; Felguerez: he became "more radical than us" -> 1969 travels to Paris to perfect his kinetics, works in the studio of Carlos Cruz-Diez (single-sourced); on return, two major kinetic exhibitions, one at the Museo de Arte Moderno: Arte cinetico, 40 pieces with music by Manuel Enriquez; workshop becomes an experimental laboratory, kinetic works with sound and light, laser and natural or artificial sunlight -> 1970s sculpture with kinetic principles, metal geometric structures designed to hang, recalling birds or spaceships; solar objects, prisms, Fresnel lenses, mirrors, magnets, laser, bodies suspended in space -> 1977 proposes the Espacio Escultorico at UNAM with Goeritz, Felguerez, Escobedo, Hersua, and Sebastian; inaugurated 23 April 1979 -> 1980 Historia de un espacio matematico, Facultad de Ingenieria UNAM, begun 1 May 1980, inaugurated 16 September 1980, finished 12 February 1981, laser through prisms, acrylic by airbrush, straight lines only; same year Ocho Conejo, Paseo de las Esculturas, volcanic stone -> 1982 Mural escultorico, south wall of the same auditorium, quadrangular, rectangular, and triangular reliefs, geometry as the fusion of architecture and sculpture -> 1985 moves to Amaxac de Guerrero, Tlaxcala, the former thread factory La Estrella becomes his multidisciplinary workshop; works with regional materials -> 1986 Serpientes del Pedregal, Paseo de las Esculturas, volcanic stone; museum works Chac and Caballero Aguila dated 1986 -> 1988 Altar I; La Muerte Presente, reinforced concrete, MUAC -> 1991 or 1992 member of number, Academia de Artes, Sculpture section, admission speech 1993 -> 1993 FONCA Creador Emerito -> 1993-96 El Principio, Huites cave, presa Luis Donaldo Colosio, Choix, Sinaloa, invited January 1992 by CFE to make a sculpture for the dam, paints a 5,000-square-metre cave instead, 225-235 m long, with more than 50 local workers -> 1994 Nave de Carnegiae, FES Zaragoza, iron clad in cactus form, interposed triangles on a river-stone base, green matte enamel, some triangles pink, nine wire arcs -> 1995 Premio Nacional de Ciencias y Artes, Bellas Artes; En Espera dated 1995 -> 1996 Guerra florida, FES Acatlan; El Senor de los Rayos dated 1996; El Principio completed 1996 -> 2000 Murcielago II, Interseccion, Ofrenda, all piedra Tlalmimilolpa -> 2001 El Jugador, UDLAP Puebla campus -> 2003 Museo Federico Silva Escultura Contemporanea opens 18 September 2003, San Luis Potosi, first museum in Latin America devoted exclusively to sculpture; Silva donates the work -> 2010 doctorado honoris causa, UASLP 23 August and UNAM 23 September -> 2011 Principio donated to Matamoros, Tamaulipas, unveiled September 2011 by the Puente Internacional Puerta Mexico -> 2016 Medalla Bellas Artes; speech: push projects imagined by young artists, "where the changes, the rebellion and the innovation, the future of the arts, are" -> 2021 exhibition "Federico Silva: Al Alba de 100 Anos" -> 2022 retrospective "Federico Silva, lucha y fraternidad. El triunfo de la rebeldia", Museo del Palacio de Bellas Artes, 150-plus pieces, opened 30 November 2022; Silva dies in the early hours of 30 November, hours before the opening; his coffin is received in the palace vestibule at 19:00, the scheduled inauguration hour; the show runs to March 2023 -> 2023 Museo Federico Silva retrospective "Federico Silva: un creador combativo"; centenary tributes. Through-line: NOT a Ruta de la Amistad author, whatever one 2024 article claims; NOT a Ruptura roster member, though Felguerez called him more radical than the Ruptura; the geometry is the politics, and the politics is the gift. The 1969 MAM show's 40 pieces are undocumented beyond their count and their composer, and no solar object was photographed this run.
