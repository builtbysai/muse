# Lourdes Grobet: Study Notes

Study date: 2026-11-05. ig-artist-study #347.

**Site:** Maria de Lourdes Grobet Arguelles (Mexico City, Thursday 25 July 1940 - Mexico City, Friday 15 July 2022, aged 81), Mexican photographer. Daughter of Ernesto Grobet Palacio, Olympic cyclist at Los Angeles 1932 and later a plumbing businessman, and Maria Luisa Arguelles de Grobet, a homemaker. Gymnast and dancer as a girl, five years of classical dance ended by hepatitis. BA in visual arts 1960; trained as a painter at the Universidad Iberoamericana under Mathias Goeritz, Gilberto Aceves Navarro and Kati Horna; assistant to Aceves Navarro and to Goeritz on stained glass for Mexico City Cathedral. Married Xavier Perez Barba 1962-1974; four children, Alejandra, Xavier, Ximena and Juan Cristobal Perez Grobet. Went to Paris (1965 by her own account, 1968 in most secondary sources), made kinetic art and light-projection actions at jazz concerts with Juan Jose Calatayud, came home, burned all her paintings and drawings, and turned to photography and multimedia because it was the time of mass media and that had to be the language she used. Co-founder of the Consejo Mexicano de Fotografia (1977); member of Proceso Pentagon; theatre work with Susana Alexander 1978-1992; twenty-three years photographing lucha libre (1980-2003) and three decades documenting the Laboratorio de Teatro Campesino e Indigena (more than 25,000 images); more than 11,000 lucha libre photographs published. First US solo show at Bruce Silverstein, New York, 2005. Work in Radical Women (Hammer Museum 2017, Brooklyn Museum 2018) and Out of Place: A Feminist Look at the Collection (Brooklyn Museum 2020). Posthumous retrospective Lourdes Grobet: Crear sin miedo, Casa del Lago UNAM, 29 September 2023 - 10 March 2024. Her book on the Teatro Campesino won the Best Historical Book Award at Les Rencontres de la Photographie d'Arles in 2023, a year after her death. She died of pancreatic cancer.

**Doorway:** From Jimenez's boxes, which kept the movement's pictures so the movement could be re-read forty years later, to Grobet, who was photographing the movement's masked body in the same years and made the book that ties the chain together. The bridge is literal and dated. In 1983, the year Grobet published Se escoge el tiempo with Magali Lara and staged De mugir a mujer at Casa del Lago, Jimenez was editing at the Instituto de Investigaciones Economicas at UNAM and keeping the archive that would one day hold photographs of both women. Lara's mechanism was the sentence written inside the drawing; Jimenez's was the photograph that keeps the evidence; Grobet's is the mask that never comes off. Lara drew the body in private; Jimenez photographed other women's hands at work; Grobet put the mask on the body and followed it from the ring to the kitchen to the dentist's chair. Where Lara's line grows in a spiral because she keeps returning to the same house, and Jimenez's line grows by accumulation because one more march and one more poster go into the next numbered box, Grobet's line grows by following one object through every room of Mexican life: the mask, worn in the ring, worn at the stove, worn at work, worn in front of the mirror, until the mask is not a disguise but the face Mexico wears to itself.

## M1. The mask that never comes off

The mask is worn through every ordinary act, not just in the ring. La Venus, in La doble lucha (1981-1982), viewed first-hand, is a tight black-and-white close-up: a masked woman applying eyeliner with a pencil, holding a small rectangular hand mirror in her other hand, dark nail polish, the mask embroidered with a V and wing motifs around the eyes, a bottle on a table behind her. The mask stays on for the most intimate act of beautification, and the eyeliner goes on over the mask, a second face drawn onto the first. La Briosa, also La doble lucha (1981), viewed first-hand, is a masked woman in a patterned blouse and white apron bottle-feeding a swaddled baby, tiled wall behind, her dark nails and a ring and a watch visible; the mask is on and the tenderness is ordinary. The luchador-dentist, viewed first-hand in colour, wears a shiny purple mask with red lightning-bolt applique under a white coat and latex gloves while treating a patient under a dental lamp. The masked luchador in his mother's kitchen, viewed first-hand, is shirtless, dark mask with white applique, tasting soup from a spoon at the stove while his mother in a floral apron stirs a pot. The sequined-mask wrestler waiting on a bench, viewed first-hand, sits cross-legged in a black satin outfit and knee-high boots, hands folded on his knee, wrestling posters on the wall behind, a suitcase edge at right; the costume is his everyday dress. The mechanism is the mask as identity, not disguise. A disguise is taken off when the performance ends; Grobet's mask is never taken off, because the performance never ends, and the photograph's job is to catch the moment when the extraordinary object is doing the most ordinary thing. El Santo and Blue Demon were the only luchadores whose faces she never saw, and did not want to see, which is the rule stated as a refusal: the mask is the face, and wanting the face underneath is the wrong kind of looking. **Takeaway:** the mask is the face, and the ordinary act is the proof. Watch the hands: when a masked figure feeds, heals, cooks or waits and the mask stays on, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's hands (#346 M1) were also a refusal of the face, but her refusal was a crop that cut the face out of the frame; Grobet's refusal is a mask that covers the face and stays in the frame, which is why her version is a costume and Jimenez's is an amputation.

## M2. The double struggle

The woman wrestler fights twice: once in the ring, once for a living. La doble lucha, the series of portraits of female wrestlers from 1981-1982 (the La Venus caption) or 1981-2005 (the La Briosa caption in the same article, a conflict carried unresolved), and Lucha por la vida, from 1980 onward, name the feminist engine directly. The women could not live on wrestling alone, so Grobet photographed them at their second jobs and at home, and the resulting photographs, in AWARE's words, are "of the lives these women were leading to sustain their life in the wrestling ring". The mechanism is the same body in two uniforms, with the mask carried across both. In the ring the body wears the costume and the mask; at the second job the body wears work clothes and an apron, and the mask is still there, because the second struggle is also fought in costume. The history behind it is a ban: women's wrestling was vetoed in Mexico City in 1954 and the veto was only lifted in 1986, so the whole first decade of Grobet's lucha libre work was made while the women she photographed were officially not allowed to wrestle in the capital. Tania la Guerrillera and Reina Gallegos, viewed first-hand in black and white, walk toward the ring through a seated arena crowd in shiny short outfits and knee boots, a man in a light shirt bending nearby; the photograph is the walk to a fight that the city had forbidden for thirty years. La Venus wrestled in Japan, where the ban did not reach. The mechanism is the double shift made visible: the photograph shows the same woman twice, once as a fighter and once as a worker, and the two photographs together are the argument that the ring alone does not pay. **Takeaway:** the second job is the real fight, and the mask is carried into it. Watch the uniform change: when the same masked body appears in ring costume and then in work clothes, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's Cuaderno de tareas (#346 M1-M2) was also a portrait of women's double work, but her women work at home and are never seen as fighters; Grobet's women are fighters first and workers second, which is why her version is a double struggle and Jimenez's is a single shift that never ends.

## M3. The family portrait in costume

Wrestlers are posed as if for a generic family portrait, with mothers, wives and children, except the costumes are fantastic. Lucha Libre: The Family Portraits (Editorial RM, 2009, text by Rafael Tonatiuh) is the book form of the mechanism: the family portrait is the most conventional photograph there is, a group arranged by height, everyone facing the camera, everyone holding still, and Grobet keeps every convention except one, which is that the people in the portrait are wearing masks, capes and boots. Hijo del Santo with Santo, viewed first-hand in colour, is the mechanism at its most monumental: Hijo del Santo in a grey suit and silver mask, arms crossed, standing before a bronze statue of El Santo on a plinth with a large floral wreath, sun flaring behind the statue's raised hand, two onlookers at right. The son honours the father as a monument, and the portrait is a family photograph in which one member of the family is a statue. The luchador in the kitchen with his mother, viewed first-hand, is the same mechanism at domestic scale: the mother stirs a pot, the son tastes the soup, and the only fantastic thing in the frame is the mask. The luchador with his daughter in the living room, described in the Mexico Desconocido caption but not viewed first-hand because the download failed, belongs to the same strand. The mechanism is the formal group pose that holds still until the eye finds the mask. A family portrait asks the viewer to believe the group is ordinary; Grobet's family portraits ask the viewer to believe the group is ordinary and then to notice that it is not, and the noticing is the joke and the tenderness at once. The wrestler is not performing for the camera; he is posing for the family album, and the album is where the mask lives. **Takeaway:** the family album is where the fantastic becomes ordinary. Watch the pose: when a group holds a formal family pose and the eye has to find the mask, the cape or the boots, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's archive (#344 M5) was also a family of sorts, a chosen family of texts kept in boxes; Grobet's family is literal, posed in living rooms and kitchens, which is why her version is a portrait and Mayer's is a filing system.

## M4. The photograph that destroys itself

A photograph developed without fixer and shown in the light, so it vanishes in front of the audience. Hora y media (Hour and a half), 1975, at Casa del Lago, with Marcos Kurtycz photographing, is the mechanism in its purest form. Grobet came out of each of three boxes, walked across a wooden frame and tore stretched aluminium foil; the gallery was turned into a darkroom; three photographs were enlarged and developed without fixer and hung on three walls; when the lights came on the images disappeared before the audience. The three black-and-white photographs, 160 by 105 cm each, are in the Hammer collection, but what the Hammer holds is the record of a photograph that no longer exists, because the work was the disappearance. The large version viewed first-hand shows Grobet herself emerging through a torn foil-covered frame: bare torso, jeans, one bare foot, arms raised tearing the foil overhead, long dark hair, black background, the foil hanging in bright torn strips around her. The three small frames viewed first-hand show the sequence: the tall foil-covered box standing alone with a small tear and a hand just emerging; the figure half out, torso and leg visible, foil tearing around her; the figure fully emerged, standing in front of the torn box, arms raised to the foil above her head. Grobet's own line, carried in the research notes, is "with light, you can create or destroy", and Hora y media is the proof: the same light that makes the photograph also unmakes it, and the audience watches a photograph die. The mechanism is the image as a timed event, not an object. A normal photograph promises to last; this one promises to vanish, and the promise is kept in front of witnesses. **Takeaway:** light makes the photograph and light kills it. Watch the fade: when an image disappears as you watch it, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's notebooks (#345 M3) were also fields where evidence is laid out, but her pages are kept and returned to for forty years; Grobet's photograph is kept for ninety minutes and then destroyed, which is why her version is a performance and Lara's is a record.

## M5. The body as the image

The artist's own body is the medium and the content. Grobet said it herself in the 1999 Debate Feminista roundtable: the body in her work is handled formally, in images of the nude, and conceptually, "where my own body has been the medium and the content". Hora y media is the first case: she is the figure tearing out of the foil box, bare torso and bare feet, and the photograph is a record of her own body doing the work. De mugir a mujer (1983-1984), at Casa del Lago, is the second and bolder case: seven women artists reflecting on what it means to be female, and Grobet's contribution was a strip-tease in which she photographed herself doing it, printed twelve life-size photographs, hung them on herself, and removed them one by one before the public until nude. The image is worn and then peeled off, and the peeling is the work: the body sheds its own photographs the way a wrestler sheds a robe, except what is shed is the image of the body itself. The Walking Exhibition, in England in the late 1970s, is the third case: Grobet put photographs on her own body and walked through the gallery, so the body is the wall the photographs hang on and also the visitor walking past them. The self-portrait viewed first-hand, young Grobet holding a Pentax camera up to her face with both hands, lens centred, her eyes hidden behind the camera body, long dark hair, white blouse, a watch and a ring, is the mechanism in miniature: the camera replaces the face, and the photographer is photographed by her own instrument. The mechanism is the body that refuses to be only the thing behind the camera. A photographer is supposed to disappear behind the lens; Grobet steps in front of it, wears it, tears out of it, and the photograph becomes a record of the body that made it. **Takeaway:** the body is the wall, the image and the visitor at once. Watch the peeling: when a body emerges from, wears, and sheds its own image, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Bustamante's performances (#341) also used her own body as the medium, but her body was a political instrument aimed at the state; Grobet's body is an image aimed at itself, which is why her version is a strip-tease and Bustamante's is a protest.

## M6. Painting the landscape

The landscape is altered with house paint, then photographed as if it were always that way. Paisajes pintados (Painted landscapes), 1977-1991, began in England in the late 1970s and continued in Mexico, in Morelos, Michoacan and Oaxaca. Grobet painted rocks and other natural features with house paint, then photographed them. In Britain her teachers failed her for altering the landscape, and neighbours called the police; the failure and the police are part of the work, because the work is a transgression against the idea that a landscape is something you look at and leave alone. In Mexico she painted cactuses. The Untitled (Cactus Painted Red/Yellow), c. 1986, in the Brooklyn Museum, viewed first-hand, is a tall columnar cactus painted flat bright red with a yellow trunk and base, standing in scrub desert under a blue cloudy sky with pale mountains, unpainted green cacti surrounding it. The painted plant reads as a sculpture planted in a real landscape, and the photograph's job is to insist that the sculpture was always there. The mechanism is the intervention that the photograph naturalises. A painted rock is a prank; a photograph of a painted rock is a landscape, because the photograph does not show the painting happening, it shows the painting finished, and the finished painting looks like nature. The colour is the giveaway and also the point: flat saturated house paint, red and yellow, against blue sky and scrub, the cheapest paint on the most permanent object, and the photograph is the only place the two meet without argument. MacDowell reworked the series in 2002 as a three-dimensional walk-through space, which is the mechanism extended: the painted landscape becomes a room you walk through instead of a picture you look at. **Takeaway:** paint the rock, photograph it, and the photograph makes it nature. Watch the flat colour: when a natural form wears one impossible flat colour in an otherwise untouched landscape, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Sebastian's monumental sculpture (#338) also puts a made object into a landscape, but his object is steel and concrete and declares itself; Grobet's object is a cactus that pretends it grew that way, which is why her version is a prank and his is a monument.

## M7. The mask as pre-Columbian continuity

The mask links the pre-Columbian past to the present, Aztec and Maya mask traditions to the masked Zapatistas of Chiapas. Grobet said it in the AWARE interview and in the Hyperallergic obituary: the masks link the pre-Columbian past to the Zapatistas, and the luchador's mask is the modern form of an old ritual object. Gabriel Rodriguez Alvarez, writing in the 2005 book, called her wrestlers "images of urban Indians with their new masks, carrying out modern rituals", which is the mechanism stated as a sentence: the ritual did not die, it changed costume, and the new costume is a wrestling mask. Her 1999 remark that the Zapatista women are where 1990s feminism lives is the same mechanism applied to politics: the masked woman is not a new figure, she is the oldest figure in Mexico, and the photograph catches her in her latest form. The mechanism is the collapse of centuries into one face. A mask with no date on it could be from any century, and Grobet's photographs use that timelessness deliberately: the arena is modern, the boots are modern, the audience is modern, but the mask could be a thousand years old, and the photograph holds both times at once. This is not a claim that the wrestling mask descends directly from Aztec masks; it is a claim about how the mask works as an image, that it carries the past into the present without needing a pedigree. The study carries it as Grobet's own framing, not as an art-historical fact, and the distinction matters, because the mechanism is her way of seeing, not a provenance. **Takeaway:** the mask is old, and the arena is new, and the photograph holds both. Watch the timelessness: when a mask appears and the centuries collapse into one face, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's street posters (#342 M4) also carried Mexican history into the present, but his carrier was the printed image pasted on a wall; Grobet's carrier is a face, which is why her version is a ritual and his is a wall.

## M8. Two theatres, one Mexico

Urban lucha libre and rural Teatro Campesino are parallel popular stages, and Grobet photographed both for twenty years side by side. The lucha libre series ran 1980-2003; the Laboratorio de Teatro Campesino e Indigena ran from the mid-1980s onward, more than 25,000 images, and Grobet said that seeing the Teatro Campesino was "the same feeling I experienced when I first saw the lucha libre". The troupe was founded and directed for fifty years by the playwright Maria Alicia Martinez Medrano, working with chontal, chol, zoque, maya, mayo and nahuatl communities in the southeast; Grobet's first contact came through the playwright Tomas Espinosa, and a playwright friend took her to Tabasco in 1986 after she had grown tired of lucha libre. La Tragedia del jaguar, viewed first-hand, is a night outdoor staging: a row of figures in white masks and pale costumes standing on a tall thatch platform, a seated masked figure on a low bench at left, a tall standing figure behind, ritual, frontal, still. The Musicos, viewed first-hand, are three musicians in white hats and pale clothes seated on a thatch platform at night, one holding a large drum, dark trees behind. Set those beside Pista Arena Revolucion (1983), viewed first-hand: El Santo in a silver mask, bare chest, grey knit tights, silver lace-up boots, standing in the ring reading a small paper, a man in a suit smoking below the ropes, audience faces in shadow. The mechanism is the same frontal, ritual staging in two places: a platform, a mask, a crowd, in a city arena and in a jungle clearing. The stagings Grobet photographed include Romeo y Julieta and Bodas de sangre, Lorca staged in Tabasco in 1983 with about two hundred indigenous and campesino actors, dancers, three regional music groups, horses, badgers and other animals. The mechanism is the popular theatre as the real Mexico: not the official culture of museums and ministries, but the masked performance that ordinary people make for themselves, in the city and in the countryside, and the camera's job is to stand in front of it and record the ritual as it is. **Takeaway:** the arena and the clearing are the same stage. Watch the platform: when a masked figure stands frontal on a raised platform before a crowd, in the city or in the jungle, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Hersua's Espacio Escultorico (#340) was also a public stage for a Mexican ritual of sorts, but his stage is concrete and permanent; Grobet's stages are a ring that is struck after the match and a thatch platform in a clearing, which is why her version is a popular theatre and his is a monument.

## M9. Photography as mass media, never edited

The photograph is a cheap, reproducible object, and editing would betray it. Grobet said it in the AWARE interview, in her own words: "I never edited my photographs. Because for me photography is a mass media. The more I can print and produce, the happier I am." The mechanism is quantity as the point. More than 11,000 lucha libre photographs published; more than 25,000 Teatro Campesino images; the books, the posters, the subway-station shows in Mexico City in 2006, the photographs printed and reprinted until they are everywhere. A photograph that is never edited is a photograph that refuses the hierarchy of the single perfect image; every frame counts, because the mass is the work. This is the opposite of the fine-art photograph, which is rare, large, signed, and hung alone on a white wall. Grobet's photographs are many, small, repeated, and pasted in stations, and the repetition is the argument: lucha libre is a mass spectacle, the photograph should be a mass object, and the artist's job is to produce, not to select. The mechanism is also a refusal of preciousness, and it connects to her teacher Goeritz's advice, "If you don't enjoy your life with art, then forget about it. And don't take yourself too seriously." Not editing is not laziness; it is a position about what a photograph is for, which is to circulate. **Takeaway:** print them all, because the mass is the work. Watch the pile: when images multiply, repeat and pile up instead of being selected down to one, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's Pinto mi Raya (#344 M5) was also a mass-media practice, a newspaper and a radio show and ten thousand texts; Grobet's mass media is the photograph itself, printed eleven thousand times, which is why her version is a pile and Mayer's is a library.

## M10. The group that rejects the protagonist

Collective work as anonymity. Grobet joined Proceso Pentagon on her return from England, through her partner Felipe Ehrenberg, a founding member, and the group is the mechanism's home. Her own words in the AWARE interview: "many heads think better than one... A group rejects the figure of the protagonist." The work was "shown and created on the streets and in unofficial artistic spaces", not in galleries, and the point of the group was that no single author could be credited, which is also the point of the work: the street does not have a protagonist, it has a crowd. The dates of her membership cannot be stated cleanly, because the sources disagree three ways (1969-1976, 1973-1990, 1978-1993), and the disagreement is itself evidence of how loosely the group held its own history; a group that rejects the protagonist also rejects the archive that would fix its dates. The mechanism is many hands and no single author. A photograph credited to one person is a portrait of the photographer; a photograph made by a group is a document of a moment, and the moment belongs to everyone who was there. Cuarto de tortura (c. 1975), a Proceso Pentagon installation photographed by Grobet and viewed first-hand, is the mechanism in a single frame: a dark room lit by one bare ceiling bulb, a barrel, clothes hanging on the wall, a chair with a cloth, a blood-stained floor, theatrical, minimal, menacing, and nobody's name is on it. **Takeaway:** many heads think better than one, and the group has no protagonist. Watch the credit line: when many hands make the work and no single author is named, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: No-grupo (#341) was also a group that worked collectively, but Bustamante's group staged its actions as authored interventions with named artists; Grobet's group refused the name itself, which is why her version is an anonymity and Bustamante's is a signature.

## M11. The androgynous figure

A single figure that is neither man nor woman. Prometeo Unisex (Prometheus Unisex), 2000, a video installation shown at Ex-Teresa Arte Actual and at the Musee du Quai Branly in Paris, is the mechanism's home. Grobet said in 1999 that she debated whether to make a Prometea or a Prometeo and settled on an androgynous figure "as the representation of the being of the twentieth century". The mechanism is the figure whose sex cannot be settled, carrying fire. Prometheus steals fire for humanity; Grobet's Prometheus is neither male nor female, because the being of the twentieth century is neither, and the fire is carried by a body that refuses the choice. Her 1999 observation of "an impressive number of proposals of androgynes, of homosexualisms" in the art around her is the context: the androgyne was in the air, and she gave it the oldest story she could find. No image of Prometeo Unisex was viewed first-hand, and the study carries the mechanism on Grobet's own words alone, which is an honest limit: the work is described, not seen. The mechanism matters because it is the only place in Grobet's work where the body is neither masked nor naked but remade, a third term between the wrestler's costume and the strip-tease's nudity. The mask hides the face; the strip-tease removes the clothes; the androgyne removes the sex, and what is left is a figure that can carry fire without belonging to either side of the division. **Takeaway:** the being of the twentieth century has no settled sex. Watch the undecided body: when a figure carrying fire cannot be settled as man or woman, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Lara's confessional drawings (#345) were also about a body that refuses to be one thing, but her body is a woman's body drawn in private; Grobet's androgyne is a public figure on a screen, which is why her version is a myth and Lara's is a diary.

## M12. The walk (deriva) as method

Seeing by drifting on foot. Caminanta, at the Museo Universitario del Chopo (year unverified, the page gives no year), takes its theme from Grobet's "deriva", her frequent drift or wandering: her physical displacement to exercise the photographic gaze, the public's walking in relation to reading images, and displacement in visual language. The show is explicitly not a retrospective and not about lucha libre, which is the point: the drift is the subject, not the wrestlers. Pasos, or Estas fotografias fueron hechas con las patas (These photographs were made with the feet), 1992, states the mechanism as a title: the photographs were made by walking, and the feet are the instrument. The painted-landscape walks through Morelos, Michoacan and Oaxaca are the same mechanism in practice: she walked the scrub, found the cactus, painted it, photographed it, and the photograph is a record of a walk, not of a view. The mechanism is the viewpoint that moves step by step. A photograph taken from a fixed point is a claim about a place; a photograph taken while walking is a claim about a route, and the route is the work. The audience of Caminanta walks too, and the walking is part of reading the images, because Grobet's images were made by a body in motion and can only be read by a body in motion. **Takeaway:** the image is found by walking, not by framing from a fixed point. Watch the footsteps: when the viewpoint moves step by step and the photograph is a record of a route, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's march photographs (#346 M3-M4) also show bodies in motion, but her camera stands still and the march passes it; Grobet's camera walks with the walker, which is why her version is a drift and Jimenez's is a vigil.

## M13. The border that splits a family

A line drawn through a people. Bering. Equilibrio y resistencia (Equilibrium and Resistance), 2013, an 80-minute documentary film, is about the political separation of Big Diomede in Russia and Little Diomede in the United States in the Bering Strait, and the divided Inupiat families who live on either side. Writing began at MacDowell in 2002 and continued in 2007; the film was supported by the Secretaria de Cultura del Distrito Federal and the Musee du Quai Branly, and was nominated for an Ariel. Bering. Reunion familiar (Family reunion), 2022, 93 minutes, is the second film, screened at Casa del Lago in March 2024. The mechanism is two shores, one family, and a strait between them that cannot be crossed. The mask is replaced by the strait as the dividing line: where the lucha libre photographs show a mask that divides the face from the world, the Bering films show a border that divides a family from itself, and the division is political, drawn by states, not worn by people. The Hyperallergic obituary dates the first film 2021, which is carried here as an error; Casa del Lago, the English Wikipedia and the Chopo page all say 2013. The portrait of Grobet viewed first-hand, in a bright yellow raincoat with red life-vest straps standing beside a man by a lake under a cloudy sky, a boat behind, is almost certainly a still from the Bering work, and it shows the artist dressed for the border itself, raincoat and life vest, ready for the strait. The mechanism is the line that cannot be crossed by the people it divides, and the camera's job is to stand on one shore and look at the other. **Takeaway:** the strait is the mask, worn by a whole people. Watch the two shores: when one family lives on either side of a line that cannot be crossed, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Aquino's street posters (#342) were also about a divided Mexico, but his division was class and the street; Grobet's division is a border drawn on water, which is why her version is a strait and his is a wall.

## M14. The mirror ritual

Preparing the face that the mask will cover. La Venus applying eyeliner in the mirror while masked, viewed first-hand, is the mechanism in one frame: the mask is already on, and she is drawing a second face onto it, pencil to the eyelid, hand mirror in the other hand, dark nails, the embroidered V and wing motifs framing the eyes she is lining. The mirror shows her a face that is already a mask, and she decorates the mask as if it were skin. La Venus in the dressing room (1981) is the same ritual from a step back: the dressing room is where the mask goes on and the face is prepared, and the preparation is part of the performance, not the preparation for it. The self-portrait with the Pentax, viewed first-hand, is the mechanism turned on the photographer: Grobet holds the camera up to her face with both hands, lens centred, her eyes hidden behind the camera body, and the camera is a mirror that hides the face instead of showing it. The mechanism is a figure looking at itself and applying a second face over the first. A mirror is supposed to show the truth of a face; Grobet's mirror shows a face that is already false, and the act of beautifying it is the work. The eyeliner is not decoration, it is a ritual, the daily preparation of the mask for the ring, and the photograph catches the moment when the private act and the public performance are the same act. **Takeaway:** the mirror shows a mask, and the eyeliner is drawn on the mask. Watch the hand mirror: when a masked figure looks at itself and applies a second face over the first, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's sewing-machine hands (#346 M1) were also a private act made public by the camera, but her act is work and the camera watches the hands; Grobet's act is preparation and the camera watches the face that is not a face, which is why her version is a ritual and Jimenez's is a timesheet.

## M15. The audience as subject

The crowd is part of the work. Grobet photographed the fights, then the spectators, then the fighters, in that order, which is the mechanism stated as a sequence: the spectacle is not the fight, it is the watching. Pista Arena Revolucion (1983), viewed first-hand, shows El Santo in a silver mask reading a small paper in the ring, and below the ropes a man in a suit smokes and audience faces sit in shadow; the hero is doing paperwork and the crowd is watching him do it, and the photograph is about the watching, not the hero. Tania la Guerrillera and Reina Gallegos, viewed first-hand, walk toward the ring through a seated arena crowd, and the crowd is the frame the wrestlers walk through. The Google Arts and Culture story lists a photograph titled simply Audience, undated, which is the mechanism given a title: the audience is a subject, not a background. The mechanism is the camera turning from the ring to the faces watching it. A fight photograph shows the fight; a Grobet photograph shows the fight and the people watching the fight, and the watching is the real event, because lucha libre is a popular spectacle and the people are the spectacle. The crowd in the arena is the same crowd as the crowd in the Teatro Campesino clearing, and the camera's job in both places is to show the watchers as well as the watched. **Takeaway:** the spectacle is the watching, not the fight. Watch the turn: when the camera leaves the ring and shows the faces watching it, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Mayer's clothesline (#344 M1) was also a work whose subject was the crowd, the strangers who pinned cards to it; Grobet's crowd watches instead of pinning, which is why her version is an audience and Mayer's is a census.

## M16. The archive as the life's work

Decades of looking, edited late into a book. More than 25,000 Teatro Campesino images over three decades, edited into the 2022 book Laboratorio de Teatro Campesino e Indigena. Medio siglo de historia, which won the Best Historical Book Award at Les Rencontres de la Photographie d'Arles in 2023, a year after her death. More than 11,000 lucha libre photographs published. The retrospective Crear sin miedo (Casa del Lago, 2023-24) was organised around three axes, and the first of them is "archivo", the archive, which is the mechanism stated as a curatorial decision: the life's work is the archive, and the archive is the work. The mechanism is a mountain of images, slowly sorted, until a few dozen carry the whole story. A photograph taken in 1980 and filed for forty years is not an old photograph; it is a photograph waiting for its book, and the book is the work that the forty years were for. Grobet never edited her photographs, which is the paradox of the mechanism: she refused to select, and then, at the end, she selected, and the selection is the archive edited into a book by her daughter Ximena, who designed and produced it, and by Beatriz Mackenzie, who edited the pictures. The archive is also the reason the work survives her: the 2025 Madrid show selected 73 photographs from the 25,000, and the selection is still going on after her death. **Takeaway:** the mountain is the work, and the book is the sorting. Watch the shelf: when decades of images are edited late into a few dozen that carry the story, the mechanism is on. Against the chain: Jimenez's archive (#346 M6) was also a life's work of filing, but her archive was built alone in her house for forty years before any institution asked to see it; Grobet's archive was built in public, in arenas and clearings, and edited by her daughter after her death, which is why her version is a mountain and Jimenez's is a habit.

## Evolution

1960s, painting and the burning. Trained as a painter under Goeritz, Aceves Navarro and Horna; assistant to Aceves Navarro and to Goeritz on stained glass for Mexico City Cathedral. Paris (1965 by her account, 1968 in the secondary sources), kinetic art, jazz-concert light actions with Calatayud. On return she burned all her paintings and drawings, a deliberate renovation of herself, and turned to photography and multimedia because it was the time of mass media.

1970-1977, installation, happening, failure as method. Serendipiti (1970), a walk-through maze of raised floors, lights and mirrors at Galeria Misrachi; A la mesa (1973/74), photomurals of household appliances; Hora y media (1975), the photograph that destroys itself, made with the Proceso Pentagon circle. Then Britain (Cardiff and Derby, 1977): the painted landscapes, failed by her teachers for altering the landscape, neighbours calling the police; Travelling (1977), photography on an escalator. The pattern is set: the work is an action in a space, the photograph is a record that may not survive, and the audience must walk through it.

1978-1985, groups and the body as medium. Co-founder of the Consejo Mexicano de Fotografia (1977); Proceso Pentagon through Ehrenberg; theatre work with Susana Alexander; De mugir a mujer (1983-84), her own body as the image, twelve life-size photographs peeled off in public; Walking Exhibition in England, photographs worn on the body; 53 cuadras (1985), fifty-three photographs as an installation. Lucha libre begins in 1980: first the ring, then the spectators, then the fighters, above all the women.

1980-2003, the long documentary decades. Lucha libre and the Laboratorio de Teatro Campesino e Indigena run in parallel for two decades: the urban masked ritual and the rural masked ritual as two faces of the same Mexico. The camera moves from spectacle to domesticity: dressing rooms, kitchens, dentists' chairs, bedrooms, family portraits. Books: Se escoge el tiempo (1983, with Lara), Luciernagas (1984), Bodas de sangre (1987), the Turner monograph (2004), Espectacular de Lucha Libre (2005), Family Portraits (2009). La doble lucha and Lucha por la vida name the feminist engine: the mask as empowerment, the second job as the real fight.

1990s-2000s, video, androgyny, the painted landscape returns. La Filomena (1996), Tres Caidas (1997), Prometeo Unisex (2000), the androgynous figure as the being of the twentieth century; Paisajes pintados reworked as a three-dimensional walk-through at MacDowell (2002); first US retrospective at Bruce Silverstein (2005); Pasos (1992) and Flujos (2010), walking and flow as subjects.

2010s-2022, the border and the archive. Bering. Equilibrio y resistencia (2013): the divided Diomede islands, the border that splits families, the mask replaced by the strait as the dividing line. Bering. Reunion familiar (2022). Late shows reframe the whole career as drift and archive: Caminanta (Chopo, year unverified), Crear sin miedo (Casa del Lago, 2023-24, posthumous), Libertad y Rebeldia (Leon, 2024), the Teatro Campesino show (Madrid, 2025). The late work is archival: 25,000 Teatro Campesino images edited into a book that won at Arles in 2023, a year after her death.

## Aesthetic logic

The mask that never comes off, worn through the most ordinary acts. The photograph as a mass-produced, unedited, disposable object that can also vanish in light. The body, hers, the wrestlers', the actors', as the stage. Humour and provocation instead of solemnity; the walk and the drift as the method of seeing; the archive as the life's work. Palette: black-and-white grain for the lucha libre and theatre work; flat saturated house paint, red and yellow, against blue sky and scrub for the painted landscapes; silver, purple, red and gold masks against dark arenas and pale domestic tile. The work refuses exquisiteness, in the CCCB's words she is an "enemy of exquisiteness", and it refuses the single perfect image, because for Grobet photography is a mass media and the more she can print and produce, the happier she is. She does not identify as a feminist artist, in the AWARE framing, though her work gave agency to working-class female subjects; the feminism is in the looking, not in the label. Her line, "Yo no necesito entender por que hago fotos. Siempre he tenido claro por que hago un proyecto", is the aesthetic stated as a working rule: the project is the reason, and the photographs are its byproduct.

## Network

Magali Lara (study #345) is the strongest chain link: the artist book Se escoge el tiempo (1983, Los Talleres), made together; both in De mugir a mujer (1983-84); Lara's 2025 MUAC retrospective text names Grobet among the collaborators of her feminist book projects; Lara spoke at the opening of Crear sin miedo on 30 September 2023. Monica Mayer (study #344) is the feminist network link: both sat in the 1999 Debate Feminista roundtable with Lara and Bustamante; Mayer and Bustamante's ten-year group Polvo de Gallina Negra (1983-1993) ran in parallel with Grobet's feminist theatre work; Kati Horna taught Mayer and introduced Grobet to photography. Kati Horna (1912-2000) is the teacher link: Hungarian-born surrealist photographer, named by Grobet as one of her three great mentors with Goeritz and Aceves Navarro, the link that binds #344, #345 and #347. Mathias Goeritz (1915-1990) is the mentor link: her teacher at the Universidad Iberoamericana, building an anti-academy; she was his assistant on stained glass for Mexico City Cathedral; his advice, "If you don't enjoy your life with art, then forget about it. And don't take yourself too seriously", guided her; she photographed his portrait, viewed first-hand. Gilberto Aceves Navarro (1931-2019) is the second mentor link: Ruptura painter, her early employer as an assistant. El Santo (1917-1984) and Blue Demon (1922-2000) are the subject link: Grobet names El Santo as a principal influence alongside her teachers, photographed both for two decades, and never saw either man's face. Felipe Ehrenberg (1943-2017) is the partner and grupo link: founding member of Proceso Pentagon, her partner, the person through whom she joined the group. Marcos Kurtycz (1934-1996) is the collaborator link: he photographed her for Hora y media. Susana Alexander is the theatre link: collaborations 1978-1992. Maria Alicia Martinez Medrano is the theatre link: playwright who founded and directed the Laboratorio de Teatro Campesino e Indigena for fifty years, which Grobet documented for three decades. Tomas Espinosa is the theatre link: playwright, her first contact with the troupe. Victor Munoz (b. 1948) is the curator and grupo link: founding member of Proceso Pentagon, curator of Caminanta and of Crear sin miedo with Beruz Herrero. Ximena Perez Grobet is the archive link: her daughter, designer and editorial producer of the 2022 Teatro Campesino book, co-curator of the 2025 Madrid show, who announced her mother's death to the New York Times. Carlos Monsivais and Alfonso Morales are the book link: Monsivais wrote the text of Lucha Libre: Masked Superstars (2005), Morales edited it.
