## M1. The French line

What it is: melody first, lucid textures, concision of expression, and the Boulanger training carried for life, and it is learned in Paris, 1927 to 1932, as her first English student, in rigorous strict counterpoint, and it is the ground of everything from the Serenade to the late songs, and Wikipedia's style section says Berkeley's contact and friendship with Ravel and Poulenc and his studies in Paris with Boulanger lend his music a "French" quality, demonstrated by its "emphasis on melody, the lucid textures and a conciseness of expression", and Britannica adds "light textures and piquant harmonies", and Dickinson says he seems closer to Faure, or to Ravel and Poulenc, who were both personal friends, than to Elgar or Vaughan Williams, and the watchable behaviour is a tune you can follow, nothing wasted, the texture always transparent enough to hear through, and the piece never shouts when it can sing.

**Takeaway:** the French line is the discipline, not the decoration, and a piece built on it must stay transparent enough to hear through, or it is only a French accent.

## M2. Neoclassical compression

What it is: large forms shrunk without losing their argument, and compressed sonata form, and the symphony's job done in eighteen minutes, and it shows up in the Divertimento, Op. 18, whose Prelude is in extremely compressed sonata form and whose Scherzo is larger than either outside movement, and in the Symphony No. 3, Op. 74, a whole symphony in one movement, under fourteen minutes, and in the Nocturne of the Divertimento, which does the emotional work a whole slow movement would do, and the watchable behaviour is a movement that starts, argues and ends before you expect it to, and density, not length, and the piece feels complete at half the size you expected.

**Takeaway:** compression must keep the argument, and a short piece that only leaves things out is a sketch, not a compression.

## M3. The moto perpetuo

What it is: a fast, light, endlessly running figure that carries a whole movement on rhythmic energy, and it shows up in the Serenade for Strings, Op. 12, first movement Vivace, "an exhilarating moto perpetuo recalling the rhythmic energy of Bach's Brandenburg Concertos", in Dickinson's words, and in the finale of the Piano Concerto, Op. 29, which "bubbles over with an exuberant gaiety", and the watchable behaviour is the strings running without stopping, and the energy itself becoming the subject, and the listener carried along by the run rather than by the tune.

**Takeaway:** the run must never stop to catch its breath, and a fast passage that pauses for effect has broken the mechanism.

## M4. Night music

What it is: the slow, nocturnal movement as the emotional centre of a work, and the Nocturne not as decoration but as the place where the piece says what it means, and it shows up in the Divertimento, second movement Nocturne, "leading to the emotional climax of the whole work", in Dickinson's words, and in the Nocturne for orchestra, Op. 25, premiered Wednesday 28 August 1946 at the Proms, and in the Nocturne for solo harp of 1967, and in Voices of the Night, Op. 86, of 1973, and Rob Barnett calls it "another isolated night piece" in a lifelong series, and the watchable behaviour is the lights going down mid-piece, and one long singing line taking over, and everything else listening.

**Takeaway:** the night movement must be the emotional climax, and a nocturne that only rests the ear is a lullaby, not a mechanism.

## M5. The sacred voice

What it is: Catholic faith turned into the most passionate music in the output, and Latin texts, and Teresa of Avila, and the Stabat Mater, and the Mass, written for the concert hall as much as for the liturgy, and it shows up in the Four Poems of St Teresa of Avila, Op. 27, "strangely passionate", in Wright's words, and "a truly magnificent song cycle", and in the Stabat Mater, Op. 28, of 1947, written for Britten's English Opera Group, and in the Missa Brevis, Op. 57, of 1960, and in the Mass for Five Voices, Op. 64, of 1964, and Berkeley said, "Being a Catholic has meant that I feel at home with the Roman liturgy... I felt attracted to these texts because I believed what they were about", and the watchable behaviour is a voice singing a Latin text as if it were a love song, and the sacred treated as the most intimate subject, and the faith producing the heat that romance never does in his work.

**Takeaway:** the sacred text must burn hotter than any love song in the piece, and a devout setting that stays decorous has missed the mechanism.

## M6. The concerto as dialogue

What it is: soloist and orchestra as equal partners sharing material, not as hero and antagonist, and the Mozart model against the Romantic trial of strength, and it shows up in the Piano Concerto, Op. 29, in Berkeley's own words, "more affinity with Mozart than with the concerto writers of the nineteenth century... the manner is rather that of a dialogue between the two, the one commenting or enlarging upon what has been proposed by the other", and "the percussive quality of the piano has on the whole been avoided in favour of the melodic side", and in the Concerto for Two Pianos, Op. 30, of 1948, and the watchable behaviour is the soloist handing a tune to the orchestra and getting it back changed, instead of fighting it, and the two voices finishing each other's sentences.

**Takeaway:** the dialogue must be equal, and a concerto where the soloist wins the argument is a different piece.

## M7. Writing for a named player

What it is: music composed for one person's sound, so the piece is inseparable from the player, and it shows up in the Piano Concerto and the third Piano Concerto, Op. 46, written for Colin Horsley, who commissioned the Horn Trio and gave first performances of several works, and in the Sonatina, Op. 52 No. 1, the Songs of the Half-Light and the Guitar Concerto, Op. 88, written for Julian Bream, and in the Sonatina, Op. 61, the Oboe Quartet, Op. 70, and the Sinfonia Concertante, Op. 84, written for Janet Craxton, and in the Four Poems of St Teresa, written for Kathleen Ferrier, who premiered them Sunday 4 April 1948, and in the Horn Trio, Op. 44, written for Dennis Brain, and in the Violin Concerto, Op. 59, written for Yehudi Menuhin, and Britannica singles out Bream and Craxton, and the watchable behaviour is a part that fits one pair of hands exactly, and the performer is the subject, not the vehicle.

**Takeaway:** the part must fit one player and no other, and a piece that any virtuoso could play has lost the name in it.

## M8. The twelve-note ground bass

What it is: serial elements used inside a tonal frame, and a twelve-note row as a bass, and a hexachord as scaffolding, never as a system, and it shows up in Aria 1 of the Concertino, Op. 49, a twelve-note ground bass, and in Boaz's recognition aria near the end of Ruth, and in the Lento of the Violin Concerto, Op. 59, a twelve-note theme stated by the violas, "but it is not serial", and in the Oboe Sonatina, Op. 61, where a row at the start "soon gets lost", and in the Symphony No. 3, Op. 74, built from a twelve-note set divided into two hexachords, and Berkeley said in 1959, "I'm not opposed to serial music; I've benefited from studying it, and I have sometimes found myself writing serial themes, although I don't elaborate on them according to strict serial principles, because I'm quite definitely a tonal composer", and Dickinson calls it a productive crisis, "At a time when tonality was regarded as exhausted, this was a productive crisis for Berkeley", and the watchable behaviour is a theme that uses all twelve notes and still sounds like a tune, and the row smuggled into a tonal room.

**Takeaway:** the row must stay a guest in a tonal house, and a row that takes over the house is a different composer's piece.

## M9. The borrowed theme and the shared authorship

What it is: another composer's material, or another composer, taken as a partner, and the homage that becomes an original work, and it shows up in Mont Juic, two composers and one suite, movements 1 and 2 Berkeley and movements 3 and 4 Britten, the division of labour kept secret for over forty years, until Berkeley told Peter Dickinson in 1980, and in the Variations on an Elizabethan Theme, Variation 3, Sellinger's Round, harmonised by Byrd, orchestrated by Holst, varied by Berkeley, and in the Flute Sonata, Op. 93 part 2, Poulenc's sonata orchestrated by Berkeley, and in the Introduction and Allegro for solo violin of 1949, edited by Ivry Gitlis, and the watchable behaviour is a familiar tune arriving in someone else's hands, and the hands changing it, and the collaboration visible in the seams.

**Takeaway:** the borrowed thing must return changed by its new home, and a quotation that only decorates is a programme note, not a mechanism.

## M10. Opera as reserve

What it is: the small, witty, reserved opera succeeding where the grand opera fails, and the composer's temperament deciding the form, and it shows up in A Dinner Engagement, one act, comic, "his most 'Mozartian'", in MusicWeb's words, and "the only one of Berkeley's operas to have held the stage", in Wikipedia's, and "staged regularly and its sophisticated wit invariably comes across", in Dickinson's, against Nelson, three acts, grand, nine performances in the 1954-55 season, not revived for 34 years, and in Ruth, the sacred tableau, the St Teresa language stretched to operatic length, and in Castaway, one act, Odysseus and Nausicaa, the economy that reshaped the late style, and Dickinson says Berkeley "seems to have been more at home in something less ambitious than grand opera, something more in keeping with his personal reserve", and the watchable behaviour is a small stage, and a few singers, and the wit doing the work that spectacle could not.

**Takeaway:** the small opera must win by wit and reserve, and a grand opera that only shrinks is a cut-down, not a reserve.

## M11. The single-movement concentration

What it is: a whole symphonic argument in one unbroken span, with no time to repeat itself, and it shows up in the Symphony No. 3, Op. 74, one movement, under fourteen minutes, "taut cogency", in Dickinson's words, and "concentration of material and the subtle balance between aggression and introspection", in John France's, and the one-movement tendency is already visible in the Divertimento's compressed Prelude, and the watchable behaviour is a piece that never stops to rest, and one arc, and aggression and introspection trading places inside it.

**Takeaway:** the single span must trade its two poles without a break, and a piece that pauses between them is two movements wearing one coat.

## M12. The guitar as late voice

What it is: the guitar taken up late and made into the composer's most personal late instrument, and austere timbres, and atonality on his own terms, and it shows up in the Sonatina, Op. 52 No. 1, of 1957, and the Theme and Variations, Op. 77, of 1970, and the Guitar Concerto, Op. 88, of 1974, "Berkeley's last concerto", and the Quatre pieces pour la guitare of 1928 were written for Segovia, who never played them, and the real guitar music starts thirty years later, with Bream, and MusicWeb says of the concerto, "with this work he veers towards atonality but on his own terms", and Dickinson regards it "as one of the most successful works in Berkeley's late style; a style that incorporates aspects of atonality within Berkeley's traditional idiom", and the watchable behaviour is a quiet plucked sound against the orchestra, holding its own without getting louder, and intimacy as a late style.

**Takeaway:** the quiet instrument must hold its own without getting louder, and a guitar that has to shout has lost the late style.

## M13. The blues tint

What it is: a blues colouring inside the French line, traced to the Parisian 1920s, surfacing at intervals for the whole career, and it shows up in the second subject of the Piano Concerto, Op. 29, "a blues influence which can be traced back to the Parisian 1920s", in Dickinson's words, and in the slow movement's "disarmingly pastoral" tune, and in the finale, which "combines the pawkiness of Prokofiev with the high spirits of Les Six", and the watchable behaviour is a melody that bends blue for a bar or two and then straightens out, and the Paris jazz age remembered, not imitated.

**Takeaway:** the blues must tint, not take over, and a blues that stays blue for a whole movement is a different piece.

## M14. The variation as portrait

What it is: theme-and-variations used as a way of looking at one idea from many sides, and each variation a different character, and it shows up in the Theme and Variations for guitar, Op. 77, of 1970, and the Theme and Variations for four hands, Op. 73, of 1968, and the Theme and Variations for solo violin of 1950, whose opus number is unconfirmed, see C14, and in the second movement of the Symphony No. 4, an andante with five variations, and in Variation 3 of the Elizabethan Variations itself, Andante, and the watchable behaviour is the same tune returning in a new disguise, and each time telling you something the last version did not.

**Takeaway:** each variation must be a different character, and variations that only decorate the tune are a set of ornaments, not portraits.

## Aesthetic logic

The logic of Berkeley's work is that a quiet Englishman learns to speak French and then spends sixty years deciding how much English to let back in, and the French line is the discipline, melody first and nothing wasted, and the Englishness is the reserve, the refusal of drama and the preference for the small form, and the career is a long education in compression, from the spacious Symphony No. 1 of 1940 to the thirteen-minute Symphony No. 3 of 1969, and the compression is not a shrinking but a concentration, and the slow movement does the emotional work, and the night music is the confession, and the sacred music is the passion, because faith is the one subject he never treats with reserve, and the concerto is a dialogue because he does not believe in heroes, and the opera fails when it is grand because grandeur needs a hero and he has none to offer, and the small comic opera succeeds because wit is reserve made audible, and the twelve-note row is admitted late because a tonal composer can borrow a row the way he borrows a theme, as a guest, and the guitar is the late voice because it is quiet and plucked and intimate, and it fits the reserve, and the blues tint is the Paris of the 1920s remembered in a bar or two, and the whole oeuvre is one long proof that a composer can be minor in scale and major in craft, and can stay tonal when tonality is declared exhausted, and can end with a plucked guitar against an orchestra, and the proof is the transparency, and the tune is always audible, and the piece is whole.
