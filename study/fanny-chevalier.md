## 1. The brief: the eye that follows the moving picture

Chevalier is the perception-and-animation researcher of the Toronto HCI lineage. Where Grossman (#816) built instruments for the signals people already send and Kazi (#817) made the sketch the whole work, Chevalier spent her career asking what the eye actually follows once the picture starts moving, and building the transitions, experiments, and frameworks that make the answer legible. Her mechanisms are about reading motion, not making it: how a static sketch is given motion (Draco, Kitty), how a moving picture stays readable (Diffamation, staggered-animation experiments, staging), how a number is made graspable (concrete scales), and how a story is told in panels that can branch (interactive data comics). The through-line is legibility under motion. A picture that moves is a picture that can lose its reader, and her whole career is the study of where that loss happens and what prevents it.

**Takeaway:** The subject is not animation. It is the eye trying to keep up with animation, and the rules that decide whether it can. [C35541]

## 3. Diffamation: the text that rewrites itself (CHI 2010)

Chevalier, Dragicevic, Bezerianos, Fekete, "Using Text Animated Transitions to Support Navigation in Document Histories", CHI 2010, pp. 683-692. DOI 10.1145/1753326.1753427. Chevalier is FIRST author, and the full PDF was read by this study. This is the most piece-ready Chevalier mechanism the pipeline has not touched yet: it is about text itself moving, not about pictures. Revision control systems let you retrieve a revision and diff two revisions, but not browse a history, so the paper derives six requirements for an animation language between two text revisions: non-ambiguity (a sentence that shrinks is read as deleted; one that flies off-screen could be misread as moved); minimalism (few basic animations, used consistently); coherence (paragraphs, sentences and words are visual landmarks; the animation must preserve document structure); simple motions (minimise total travel distance); smoothness (no sudden changes); adaptive speed (duration depends on the amount of change). The two revisions are stored in one merged structure, the superdocument, built by sorting the diff operations (Heckel's diff algorithm, at character level; moves are NOT a primitive -- a move is a delete plus an insert). It is hierarchical: paragraphs split into stable, deleted, or inserted blocks, cut at every carriage return; blocks contain words, which do not always match actual words (the paper's example: "poule" replaced by "poussin" yields the "words" pou, stable; le, deleted; ssin, inserted). Every entity stores its geometry as a pair of points (p, q): top-left corner of the first word and bottom-right corner of the last word, relative to the parent. Rendering a revision is computing its geometry from the superdocument with parameter t: t = 0 gives the older revision, t = 1 the newer one. Positions are interpolated top-down, hierarchically, for t in (0, 1), in two modes: direct interpolation (linear, a simple translation) and modulo interpolation, where the target position is replaced by an equivalent position p1* in the same equivalence class -- a word can travel horizontally off the right edge and reappear on the next line, as if the document were one long line wrapped around a cylinder. Paragraph position is always directly interpolated (paragraphs only shift vertically). Block position is directly interpolated if p0 is closer to p1 than to p1*, otherwise modulo. Word position is ALWAYS modulo-interpolated, so words never shift vertically inside stable regions of text. Deletion and insertion are conveyed by blocks shrinking or growing: for an insertion, q0 = p0 (zero-size block) and q is linearly interpolated toward q1, with font size, line width and line height set to zero when q0 = p0 and interpolated linearly. Inserted and deleted blocks are coloured with the standard diff scheme (insertions green, deletions red in the paper's rendering), shown only for t in (0, 1), with colour opacity animated; deletion highlights appear slightly in ADVANCE (anticipation) and insertion highlights persist for some time AFTER the animation, both reversing when the animation plays backwards. Vertical stabilization: the system averages the vertical motion dY of the visible words into dYavg and translates the whole document vertically by interpolating from DocY to DocY - dYavg, so a document that does not change inside the viewport does not move at all. Timing: all edit operations within one transition play IN PARALLEL (sequential animation would take too long, the true edit order is unknown, and parallel is more consistent with minimalism); transitions across several revisions play SEQUENTIALLY. The dynamics are slow-out -- the transition starts fast and ends slow. Duration is adaptive, from the largest relative motion: duration T = a * dDmax + b, with a = 0.37 ms per pixel and b = 100 ms, fitted by linear regression, capped at 500 ms (above that complexity the animation is hard to follow at any speed). The Diffamation system adds a timeline widget where revisions are vertical marks spaced by edit distance d = k * log(1 + E), E the total number of characters edited, and edit summaries between successive revisions rendered with a slit-tear approach with alpha compositing. The user study reports that smooth text animation lets users track changes more effectively than flipping pages. Figure 1 in the paper is a detail of an animated transition between two revisions of the Wikipedia article "User interfaces".

**Takeaway:** The modulo wrap is the whole piece in one rule: a word slides off the right edge and reappears on the next line, and the reader never loses the thread. [C35543]

## 4. Concrete scales: the number as a pile of things (TVCG 2013)

Chevalier, Vuillemot, Gali, "Using Concrete Scales: A Practical Framework for Effective Visual Depiction of Complex Measures", IEEE TVCG 19(12):2426-2435, 2013. DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2013.210. Chevalier is FIRST author, and the full PDF was read by this study. A design framework, not a tool, for "concrete scales": graphic compositions that make an extreme or unfamiliar measure graspable by relating, re-expressing, and comparing it through familiar magnitudes and units. The corpus is 300 graphic compositions collected from infographics, news sites and image search, deconstructed with Engelhardt's recursive model; 293 of 300 use pictorial depictions of real-life objects. Three cognitive mechanisms from psychology: unitization (an improvised, subjective systematisation of units -- the observer redefines a familiar object as a new unit, "1 Earth of mass", "1 sugar cube of sugar", and breaks a scale into countable chunks of it); anchor and adjustment (a tangible reference serves as an anchor from which the observer estimates by adjustment, "about the size of..."); analogies (pairwise analogies comparing the difference in magnitude between one pair of objects with that of another, more familiar pair). The taxonomy of measure relations: comparison (83% of the corpus has at least one; exact when magnitudes can be accurately compared, approximate when vaguely defined; juxtaposition 82% of relations, or superimposition); containment (8% of corpus; 26 examples, 15 of them loose matter filling physical objects; the container's capacity can be graduated for partial filling or translucent to reveal content); unitization (16% of corpus, all re-expressing non-visible units such as weight, calories or time with familiar objects; the relation "A accounts for n instances of B", where B is the new unit; the remainder rule -- when the ratio is not an integer, the remainder is expressed as a subportion of the unit, a decimal ratio, or recursively unitized with a finer-grained unit; the paper's example: an orange re-expressed as two and a half apples by nutrition, or two apples and one apricot); analogy (4% of corpus). Strategies built from these relations: anchor and adjustment (83%), unitization (16%), analogy (4%), zoom (2%: progressive adjustment and containment, nested views where a scaled version is embedded in the larger picture, with a magnification factor), lock (4%: lock the relation type, or lock a dimension and magnitude, e.g. "is equal to, on a caloric scale", to explore by facets), small multiples (11 of 28 small-multiple cases are unitization, e.g. the sugar cubes in Figure 1b). The paper also introduces CSML (Concrete Scale Markup Language), a UML-like visual notation for objects and relations, and a design protocol with a worked scenario. The headline examples in Figure 1: the US debt as progressively stacked 100-dollar bills next to a human, sports fields, and New York buildings (115 trillion dollars); sugar stacks comparing calories in foods and drinks with sugar cubes; the volume of Earth's water as spheres sized against the Earth.

**Takeaway:** The unit is the picture. One familiar object, repeated n times at true relative scale, IS the number -- and the honest half-object remainder is the visual event. [C35544]

## 5. Kinetic textures: the picture that breathes (Draco, CHI 2014)

Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Zhao, Fitzmaurice, "Draco: Bringing Life to Illustrations with Kinetic Textures", CHI 2014, pp. 351-360. DOI 10.1145/2556288.2556987. Honorable Mention Award (top 5 percent) and Best Talk Award; the system shipped as Autodesk SketchBook Motion, Apple iPad App of the Year 2016. Chevalier is second author. The mechanism detail here is taken from the #817 mechanisms leg's verified full-paper read, credited as such; this study verified the abstract and framing but did not re-read the full Draco PDF. A kinetic texture is one animation component that encodes a whole collection of similar objects AND their motion together. It has exactly two parts: a patch -- a small number of representative objects drawn by the user, the source example from which the full collection is generated -- and a set of motion properties, defined at two scales: global motion (the overall shape and direction of the whole collection) and granular motion (variation of the individual elements). The animation is continuous and looping, with no start or end event: it sits between a static illustration and a video. Two families. Emitting textures: the user draws a few representative objects with the patch tool, sketches the emitter as a stroke, and the system immediately starts emitting elements perpendicular to the emitter (a point emitter emits in all directions); the user then sketches motion paths, and the motion field is recomputed on completion of each new path; three scalar parameters control the dynamics -- emission velocity (initial velocity of objects), emission frequency (how often objects are emitted), and cohesion (magnetism of objects toward the motion paths; low cohesion gives a uniform spread between paths, high cohesion makes objects cling to the paths, which also yields obstacle-avoidance-like effects). Each motion path is assigned discrete points at fixed intervals, each with a unit motion vector; the global motion vector at an object position is a weighted sum of those vectors, weighted by distance, with cohesion as the coefficient (the exact weighting kernel formula was not legible in the extracted text). Additional components: a texture outline (objects decay and are removed when they cross it) and masks (regions inside which objects temporarily hide). A motion profile widget lets the user sketch a curve for scale or velocity along the trajectory -- bubbles that grow and decelerate as they move away from the emitter. Canonical examples: rain, snow, falling leaves, water dripping from a fountain, a school of fish, waterfall, water ripples, flocking. Oscillating textures: a FINITE collection, built by replicating the source patch along a brush skeleton; no emitter and no continuous birth/death. The user draws the patch, sketches the brush skeleton, then sketches a second, target oscillating skeleton; the texture oscillates between the two skeletons, position and orientation interpolating along the skeleton between the two keyframe shapes, implemented as a simple harmonic oscillation using a sinusoidal curve. A speed slider controls the oscillation speed. Granular motion can be added without any oscillating skeleton at all. Canonical examples: tree leaves blowing in the wind, swaying grass, seaweed underwater, hair, clothes, fire, a scuba diver's legs. Granular motion, applying to both types, has two kinds only: translation (each object moves along a small 2D path) and pivot / rotation (each object rotates about a pivot point), authored by direct manipulation in an interactive patch widget; two controls, velocity (frequency of the granular motion) and phase (level of synchronisation among individual objects; at minimum phase all granular motions are synchronised, at higher phase they desynchronise). The final trajectory and orientation of each object is the combination of global motion and granular motion. This section is substrate, not a fresh proposal: GRO1 and KAZ1/KAZ2 already cover the emitting and oscillating types with exact parameters, and this study does not re-propose Draco. Its value here is as the ground the later mechanisms build on.

**Takeaway:** One drawn example stands for the whole crowd, and the crowd's motion reads as one phenomenon while each member differs a little. [C35545]

## 6. The graph that makes things cause things (Kitty, UIST 2014)

Kazi, Chevalier, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, "Kitty: Sketching Dynamic and Interactive Illustrations", UIST 2014, pp. 395-405. DOI 10.1145/2642918.2647375. Chevalier is second author. Mechanism detail is taken from the #817 mechanisms leg's verified full-paper read; this study verified the abstract and framing but did not re-read the full Kitty PDF. A dynamic illustration in Kitty consists of visual entities (objects and kinetic textures), parameters (the visual, spatial, temporal, or quantitative attributes of those entities), a relational graph defining functional relationships between entities, and interactivity. The motivating example is a cook holding an egg over a pot: dragging the egg down into the pot triggers soup splashes and the cat's eyelids closing; turning the stove knob increases the fire and the steam. Nodes are visual entities -- objects (green nodes), emitting textures (blue nodes), oscillating textures (orange nodes) -- each carrying a set of parameters: individual objects have translation, rotation, scale, opacity, ambient speed, ambient rotation and ambient translation (continuous motion defined by direct manipulation, with a draggable pivot point for rotation) and a mask region; emitting textures have opacity, emission frequency, emission speed; oscillating textures have opacity and skeleton length; granular velocity is the single local parameter shared by both texture types. Edges are directed and encode a functional relationship between ONE parameter of a source node and ONE parameter of a target node; multiple edges between the same pair define mappings for additional parameter pairs, and bi-directional relations are two separate edges. Each edge carries a one-dimensional function tp = f(sp), where sp is the source parameter value and tp the target parameter value; the default is identity (tp = sp), and the author overwrites it by directly sketching a new curve in a mapping widget (source on the x-axis, target on the y-axis), with an exploration cursor that can be dragged in the mapping space to preview the corresponding values live. A mapping can also be loaded from an external CSV file. Chain effects: edges combine in sequence -- the verified example is knob global rotation to fire scale (oscillating texture) to steam emission frequency (emitting texture), so turning the knob makes the fire bigger, which makes the steam more intense; a second verified example is egg translation to soup-splash emission frequency, where f is shaped so emission frequency is highest exactly when the egg hits the soup surface (a peaked, not monotonic, curve). Interactivity is defined at node level: each node can have ONE of its parameters assigned for input control; in presentation mode the viewer directly manipulates it -- spatial parameters by direct manipulation (drag the egg, rotate the windmill), non-spatial parameters by a swipe gesture toward or away from the entity. Hovering or tapping shows red interaction guides. Update rule: when a parameter changes, all dependent parameters update in real time; chain effects are computed by traversing the relational graph starting at the node of interaction, and any edge that would induce a cycle is NOT traversed (prevents infinite loops while still allowing bi-directional relationships). Authoring the graph: the author enters graph mode, the system animates to an abstract graph view laid out by a force-directed layout, colour-coded by type; creating an edge is a three-step process (sketch a stroke from source node to target node; pick the parameter pair from radial menus; sketch the mapping curve f in the widget). Authored examples in the paper: a weather infographic, a technical illustration of car steering, a comic-style illustration of passing time, a children's illustration, plus the cook/egg/knob scene. User study: 8 participants; the exercise task required authoring 5 visual entities and 4 functional relationships. Note on vocabulary: the paper does NOT use the words "trigger" or "response" as named grammar terms; what it defines is a relational graph with directed edges, sketched 1D correspondence functions, and chain effects. "Trigger/response" is a fair paraphrase, not the paper's vocabulary. This section is substrate: KAZ6 ("Turn the Knob") already covers this mechanism with exact parameters, and this study does not re-propose Kitty. Its value here is as the interaction grammar that the Diffamation and trajectory candidates below deliberately do NOT use -- they are watch-and-scrub, not cause-and-effect.

**Takeaway:** Causality made visible and touchable: one drag propagates through a visible chain, and the shape of each link is itself the behaviour. [C35546]

## 7. The animated figure inside the paper (Your Paper Is Dead, CHI EA 2015)

Grossman, Chevalier, Kazi, "Your Paper Is Dead! Bringing Life to Research Articles with Animated Figures", CHI Extended Abstracts (alt.chi), 2015. DOI 10.1145/2702613.2732501. Page range unresolved: her own publications page and the INRIA activity report say pp. 461-470; the ACM DL page, the Interactions adaptation's citation, and researchr say pp. 461-475. Carry both, or verify against the PDF, before citing a page range. Adapted as "Bringing Research Articles to Life with Animated Figures", ACM Interactions 23(4):52-57, 2016. Chevalier is second author. The paper proposes and demonstrates "animated figures": short, autonomous, continuously looping videos embedded inside the PDF of a research article itself, in place of a static figure. The Draco CHI 2014 paper was, per the Interactions article, the first paper in the ACM Digital Library to embed such a figure. The key properties, as stated in the Interactions adaptation: autonomous (it plays by itself, no interaction required), continuously looping (no start, no end, no timeline to scrub), and minimising disruption to the typical reading experience. It is a figure, not a video: it lives inside the document layout, at figure size, and the reader's eye treats it as a picture that happens to move. The verified use-case families, from the abstract and the Interactions summary: showing a process or mechanism that a static figure can only imply (the Draco figure itself, which demonstrates kinetic textures by being one); showing change over time inside a figure that must stay in place in the text; and demonstrating an interactive technique by a looped recording of it. The paper is a reflection on the authors' experience of authoring the Draco paper with an embedded animated figure, and a discussion of the opportunities and caveats of transitioning to the practice more widely. The full text was not read by this study, so the detailed caveats list is not reproduced here. The transferable rule is the framing rule: loop, no controls, figure-sized, embedded in static context -- 95 percent static diagram and 5 percent looping motion, where the stillness is the point. That framing alone is not a new mechanism, and the pipeline already has the looping-figure family in GRO1, so no piece is proposed on it alone.

**Takeaway:** A figure that moves inside a frozen document commands the eye precisely because everything else refuses to move. [C35547]

## 8. Staggering fails: the negative result (TVCG 2014)

Chevalier, Dragicevic, Franconeri, "The Not-so-Staggering Effect of Staggered Animated Transitions on Visual Tracking", IEEE TVCG 20(12):2241-2250, 2014 (Proc. InfoVis 2014). DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346424. Chevalier is FIRST author. Abstract read; full text not read; the Chalbi thesis discusses the same study. This is a NEGATIVE result, and it matters: the pipeline's GRO2/KAZ3 use staggering-adjacent ideas (slow-in/slow-out, trails) without ever testing whether staggering helps the eye. Staggering is an incremental delay in start times across the moving elements of an animated transition. It is widely believed to help: it should reduce occlusion and display complexity and produce less overwhelming animations. No empirical evidence had demonstrated this. Perceptual psychology does show that reducing occlusion and reducing inter-object proximity (crowding) improves multiple-object tracking. The authors ran simulations confirming that staggering CAN in some cases reduce crowding in animated transitions involving dot clouds (as in animated 2D scatterplots). They then empirically evaluated two staggering techniques on tracking tasks, in conditions chosen to most favour staggering. Finding: introducing staggering has a negligible, or even negative, impact on multiple-object tracking performance. The costs outweigh the benefits: first, a loss of common-motion grouping information about which objects travel in similar paths -- when objects start at different times, the eye can no longer group them by shared motion (Gestalt common fate); second, less predictability about when any specific object would begin to move. Staggering may be beneficial in some conditions, but those have yet to be demonstrated. The authors frame the result as a step toward understanding animation pacing. The study used no p-values and separated planned from exploratory analyses; Monte-Carlo simulation was used to find the best stagger configuration for a field of points before testing it. The two staggering techniques evaluated are not named in detail here, because the full text was not read.

**Takeaway:** The fashionable technique does not help, and the reason is exact: staggering breaks the shared motion that lets the eye group things at all. [C35548]

## 9. Common fate: what the eye groups (TVCG 2020)

Chalbi, Ritchie, Park, Choi, Roussel, Elmqvist, Chevalier, "Common Fate for Animated Transitions in Visualization", IEEE TVCG 26(1):386-396, 2020. DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2019.2934288. A crowdsourced graphical-perception study of the Gestalt Law of Common Fate, extended beyond velocity: does coordinated change in luminance or size also group objects? One hundred crowdworkers judged groupings of four graphical objects under conflicting static and dynamic factors (position, size, luminance). Reported ranking: motion beats dynamic luminance, size, and luminance; dynamic size beats dynamic luminance and position; dynamic luminance beats size. A follow-up experiment used a Gapminder-like animated scatterplot and a thematic election map. The paper's conclusion is conditional: in practice the relative grouping strengths depend on the visualization and the data. The picture is deliberately minimal -- four dots or shapes changing size, brightness, and position in conflict with each other -- because the experiment is about what the eye groups, so the picture is reduced to the grouping cues. This is the positive half of the staggering finding: the same grouping law, measured rather than assumed, with a ranking the piece can show.

**Takeaway:** Motion is the strongest grouping cue the eye has; change the motion and you change what counts as one thing. [C35549]

## 10. The 23 roles of animation (AVI 2016)

Chevalier, Riche, Plaisant, Chalbi, Hurter, "Animations 25 Years Later: New Roles and Opportunities", AVI 2016, pp. 280-287. DOI 10.1145/2909132.2909255. Chevalier is lead author. The paper revisits Baecker and Small's 1990 taxonomy of animation at the interface, 25 years on, from a review of publications and commercial systems plus interviews with 20 professionals of various backgrounds. The result is a taxonomy of 23 roles in 5 categories, verified from Chalbi thesis Table 2.1, which reproduces it: KEEPING IN CONTEXT (roles 1-4): 1. Staying oriented during navigation. 2. Supporting tracking during layout changes. 3. Maintaining up to date. 4. Replaying history, summarizing. TEACHING AID (roles 5-9): 5. Affordance and preview. 6. Training, demonstration by example. 7. Explaining how a system works. 8. Illustrating an algorithm. 9. Teaching a new representation of information. USER EXPERIENCE (roles 10-16): 10. Hooking the user. 11. Keeping the user engaged. 12. Providing visual comfort and aesthetics. 13. Making activity and progress visible. 14. Revealing/Hiding Content. 15. Providing a virtual tour. 16. Providing feedback of input mechanism. DATA ENCODING (roles 17-20): 17. Revealing data relationships. 18. Conveying uncertainty or randomness. 19. Encoding emotion. 20. Encoding object attribute. VISUAL DISCOURSE (roles 21-23): 21. Supporting a narrative. 22. Highlight content. 23. Persuading and convincing. The full AVI paper was not read; the 23-role table is verified from the Chalbi thesis, which reproduces the taxonomy.

**Takeaway:** Animation has 23 jobs, and the two most overlooked -- data encoding and visual discourse -- are the ones a generative piece is built to do. [C35550]

## 11. Staging: three beats to a new tree (Chalbi thesis, 2018)

Two sources: Chevalier et al., "Animations 25 Years Later" (AVI 2016, section 10 above), and Chalbi's thesis, "Understanding and designing animations in the user interfaces", PhD thesis, Universite Lille 1, defended 17 April 2018 (HAL tel-01881889; Chevalier co-supervisor with Nicolas Roussel), chapter 6. Chapter 6 was read at contents level only; the role definitions in chapter 2 were read in full. Staging, in the thesis's design space for staged animation, is the decomposition of one animated transition into ordered steps. The design space has fixed dimensions -- data attributes, change characteristics, observer task -- and variable dimensions -- staging strategy and staging design, the latter split into decomposition (how the transition is broken into steps) and timing (how the steps are paced relative to each other). The thesis's running example is a staged navigation between directories in a 3D tree-map (SpaceTree-style): (1) shrink the branches that get in the way, (2) re-centre the trimmed tree so the new branch fits, (3) grow the branch out of the new focus point. Note the difference from staggering (section 8): staggering delays the START of each element's motion within one transition; staging breaks the transition into SEQUENTIAL STEPS, each a different kind of change. Also note the difference from SKUID's "staging" (KAZ3/GRO2): in SKUID, staging is implemented narrowly as dynamic motion trails; here it is the decomposition of a layout change into steps. Different mechanisms, same word -- this study does not conflate them. The fixed/variable dimensions above are taken from the chapter-6 contents listing, and the SpaceTree three-step example is from chapter 2's role-1 discussion, read in full; the full chapter-6 staging design space was not read end to end.

**Takeaway:** One change at a time. Each beat does exactly one kind of thing, so the eye never has to track two kinds of change at once. [C35551]

## 12. The comic that branches (Interactive Data Comics, TVCG 2021)

Wang, Romat, Chevalier, Riche, Murray-Rust, Bach, "Interactive Data Comics", IEEE TVCG 28(1):944-954, 2022 (VIS 2021). DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2021.3114849. Chevalier is third author. Abstract and introduction read; full text not read. Data comics use panels in expressive layouts to give an overview of a storyline while enforcing the linearity of narration. The paper asks how to make them interactive, so the story is no longer a single linear, immutable storyline curated by the author. It establishes six goals of interactivity for data-driven storytelling; the abstract lists five phrasings for six goals (non-linear storytelling, personalization, levels of detail, explanations, and enriched user experiences), and the exact sixth goal's wording was not verified this run, so this study quotes the paper, not the mechanisms report, for the goal list. The operations introduced, verified from the abstract: add and remove panels into pre-defined layouts, to support branching, change of perspective, or access to detail-on-demand; provide and modify data, and interact with the data representation, to support personalization and reader-defined data focus. The paper also proposes COMICSCRIPT, a lightweight declarative scripting language (specification language) for designers to add such interactivity to static comics, and validates the authoring process with six professional illustrators, designers and data-comics enthusiasts who crafted an interactive comic each. Examples are shown in a gallery. A sibling paper, "Data Comics for Reporting Controlled User Studies in HCI" (Wang, Ritchie, Zhou, Chevalier, Bach, TVCG 27(2):967-977, 2021), applies the comics form to study reports, with a 10-stage storyboarding framework and a gallery at statscomics.github.io. The companion site interactivedatacomics.github.io was not opened. The visual principle is that the panel grid is a state machine: adding a panel reflows the layout, removing one closes the gap, and the reader's choice is visible as a change in the grid itself, not just in the content.

**Takeaway:** The panel grid is a state machine. Choosing a branch adds panels and reflows the grid, so the choice is visible as a change in the layout itself. [C35552]

## 13. Trajectory as time (DirectPaint, CHI 2013)

Santosa, Chevalier, Balakrishnan, Singh, "Direct Space-Time Trajectory Control for Visual Media Editing", CHI 2013, pp. 1149-1158. DOI 10.1145/2470654.2466148. Abstract and figure captions read; full text not read. Pen-based techniques for painting and annotating over video, slides, and 2D animation, in space and in time. The user draws a stroke and the system propagates the visual element along the motion trajectory of the object underneath, using feature-based optical flow, so paint sticks to a moving thing instead of sitting on one frame. Pressure and multi-touch input control the tools. The verified mechanism, from the paper's Figure 2 caption: motion trajectories can be invoked for any animated object; the user can DRAG THE OBJECT ALONG ITS OWN TRAJECTORY to navigate through the animation; and the user can apply and propagate attributes by directly specifying a PROPAGATION WINDOW along the object trajectory. In plain terms: the path is a timeline you can grab, and a window drawn on the path says "this colour / this size applies only here". Contributions from the abstract: the trajectory-based design space; the pen techniques for controlling propagation of visual elements; the DirectPaint implementation; a usability evaluation. The supplemental video (27.59 MB) was not played. The visual principle is time made touchable: dragging the thing backwards along its own path rewinds it; the path shows where it has been and where it is going; a window on the path shows where a property applies.

**Takeaway:** The path is the timeline. Drag the creature backwards along its own path and it rewinds; the window on the path is where the colour lives. [C35553]

## 14. The layer stack and slack time (LACES, CHI 2014)

Freeman, Santosa, Chevalier, Balakrishnan, Singh, "LACES: Live Authoring through Compositing and Editing of Streaming Video", CHI 2014, pp. 1207-1216. DOI 10.1145/2556288.2557304. Abstract and system description read; the 35.19 MB supplemental video was not played. A tablet system that collapses video production into one live phase. Recording starts automatically when the app launches, and the operator edits and composites while filming instead of in post-production. The interface has three parts: a main viewer showing the current frame, a stack of timeline layers (the live stream plus compositing layers), and a workspace library where clips are saved and arranged. The paper's argument is about "slack time": the camera operator watches the scene unfold during capture and the editor re-watches footage in post; LACES puts the editing work into those idle moments. The computable rule is the layer stack: the live stream is always the bottom layer, additional layers composite over it, and the viewer always shows the composite of the stack at the current time. This is the least piece-ready mechanism in this study: it is a video workflow, not a visual rule. It is recorded here for completeness and because the layer-stack rule is real; no candidate is built on it alone.

**Takeaway:** The live stream is the bottom layer and everything else composites over it -- a rule about time, not a picture, and the study records it without forcing it into a piece. [C35554]

## 15. Eight little worlds (Parallel universes, alt.chi 2014)

Dragicevic, Chevalier, Huot, "Running an HCI Experiment in Multiple Parallel Universes", alt.chi 2014, pp. 607-618. DOI 10.1145/2559206.2578881. Chevalier is second author. Abstract and HAL full-text opening read. The authors evaluated a haptic touch slider in 8 parallel universes: the same experiment, conducted and analysed separately in each universe, by the "same" investigators, using the same methods. The results were overall similar but exhibited surprisingly high variability in terms of statistical significance patterns -- the same data, the same test, and whether the result was "significant" flipped between universes. The paper's point is about the ritual of null-hypothesis significance testing (NHST): with small samples, chance alone decides the verdict more often than researchers admit. The authors' stated position (on the aviz.fr bad-stats page, which collects this paper): HCI should drop NHST and p-values and report effect sizes with interval estimates instead. The paper does not elaborate the many problems of NHST (covered elsewhere); it demonstrates one, by running the experiment eight times. The executable rule: N parallel simulations of the same system (same rules, same initial conditions up to a random seed), each running independently, each producing a scalar outcome and a verdict (above or below a threshold), displayed side by side with the verdict marked per panel. The simulations share everything except the seed, so any difference in outcome is attributable to chance alone.

**Takeaway:** Sameness that disagrees. Eight panels, same setup, same rule, different outcome -- and the variation is the picture. [C35555]

## 16. Uncertainty as jitter (role 18)

Role 18 of the AVI 2016 taxonomy (section 10 above), "Conveying uncertainty or randomness", is the closest Chevalier-linked mechanism for uncertainty depiction, and it is a TAXONOMY role, not a paper about uncertainty. No dedicated Chevalier paper on visualising uncertainty was found, and this study does not invent one. What the role gives is the rule: motion itself can encode uncertainty -- jitter, wobble, or flicker whose amplitude is the uncertainty. That rule is executable: each flock member has a true position from the flock rule and a displayed position equal to the true position plus a random offset drawn each frame from a distribution whose width w is the uncertainty value; trail opacity also scales with w, so uncertain members leave smeared trails and certain members leave clean ones; when w reaches zero the flock is perfectly sharp. The rule is credited to the taxonomy, not to a nonexistent uncertainty paper. It is the only candidate mechanism in this study that encodes a number in motion quality rather than in position or count.

**Takeaway:** A sharp flock reads as certain and a trembling one as unsure -- the width of the tremble is the number, and the picture's own blur is the data. [C35556]

## 17. Capacity limits: how much the eye can hold (2026)

Jiang et al. (including Chevalier, Isenberg, Franconeri), "Design guidelines for animated data visualization based on perceptual capacity limits", Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications, 2026, 11(1):31. DOI 10.1186/s41235-026-00724-y. Abstracts read (the paper and the companion JOV article, "How to ensure that animated data visualizations respect visual capacity limits"); full text not read. Animated data visualizations can easily overwhelm visual processing capacity, and designers rarely have clear rules for when and why. The paper reviews 40 real-world animated visualizations and builds a taxonomy of the perceptual tasks those animations demand: holistic judgments (extracting optic flow, extracting ensemble statistics, evaluating changes to the shape envelope surrounding a set of points) and tracking tasks across individual objects (tracking objects with a common feature, noticing the removal or addition of objects, tracking objects without remembering their features -- easier -- or with remembering their features -- tougher). For each task the paper describes the known processing limits, so designers can create animations that respect those limits -- for example, people generally track only three to four objects at once. It catalogues design techniques for preventing information overload, such as making a subset of objects more salient, or animating only a subset of objects at once, and explains why they work. The two catalogued techniques are directly executable rules: a salience function over the object set, and a per-frame cap on the number of simultaneously moving objects. The exact numeric limits per task were not extracted this run; the study quotes the paper for numbers, not the mechanisms report. This is the most recent mechanism in this study, and it generalises section 8: the staggering experiment asked whether one pacing trick helps; the capacity-limits paper asks how much the eye can hold at all, and answers with a budget.

**Takeaway:** Salience is a budget. When only a few things move at once, the eye can follow them; when everything moves, nothing can be followed. [C35557]

## 18. The drawn line does double duty (DataInk, Squidgets, DataGarden)

Three systems, one motif: a sketch stroke that is both picture and control. DataInk (Xia, Riche, Chevalier, De Araujo, Wigdor, CHI 2018, Paper 223, DOI 10.1145/3173574.3173797, Honorable Mention Award): a pen-and-touch system for making expressive, personal data visualizations by drawing. The author sketches a visual design in digital ink; selecting a drawn shape and specifying a data-visual mapping automatically populates the canvas with all relevant data points; compound glyphs represent additional dimensions; drawing a layout along a data dimension structures the data spatially, and redrawing the layout remaps it. Illustration, data binding, and layout configuration are interleaved rather than staged. A study with eight designers and non-experts is reported. Abstract read, plus Figure 1 caption, Figure 2 storyboard description (six steps, a-f) and Figure 3 design decisions in fetched text; the project site datainkresearch.github.io was not opened. Squidgets (Kim, Chevalier, Singh, UIST 2025, Honorable Mention, DOI 10.1145/3746059.3747690): "sketched widgets" -- curves that visually abstract a scene (a silhouette, a rig curve, a line of action) act as handles; the user draws a stroke, it is partially matched against scene curves to select a squidget, and the stroke then drives the associated scene parameter in place. Implemented in Maya; evaluated with casual users and professional artists. Abstract read, plus Figure 1 caption and introduction. DataGarden (Offenwanger, Tsandilas, Chevalier, IEEE TVCG 31(1):1268-1278, 2025, DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2024.3456336): the author sketches one representative visualization, and the system turns it into an expressive template for an envisioned or partial dataset, capturing implicit style as well as explicit data mappings, so the sketch survives instead of being thrown away and redrawn from scratch. Evaluated with a reproduction study and a freeform study. Abstract read (PubMed). Across all three, the same rule holds: one mark is both picture and control -- a sketch stroke in Draco is the motion path, a sketched curve in Kitty is the functional wiring, a stroke in Squidgets is the widget, a drawn layout in DataInk is the chart structure.

**Takeaway:** One mark, two jobs. The line you see is the line that does the work, and that is why her interfaces need no menus. [C35558]

## 19. Text as material (Textoshop, Texterial, HaLLMark)

The writing thread of the last three years, converging with the sketch thread through LLMs. Textoshop (Masson, Kim, Chevalier, CHI 2025, DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713862): treats words as pixels, sentences as regions, tones as colours -- direct manipulation moves, shortens, expands, and reorders text; boolean operations (union, intersection, subtraction) combine text fragments; a tone picker explores tone along three dimensions; layers organize and version text; tools include an eraser, a tone eyedropper, and a smudge tool that paraphrases and smooths transitions. Built with an LLM plus traditional NLP. Abstract read, plus introduction in the arXiv HTML fetch. Texterial (Shen, Marquardt, Romat, Hinckley, Riche, Chevalier, CHI 2026, Barcelona, 13-17 April 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790330, Honorable Mention Award): reimagines text as a material to be sculpted like clay or cultivated like a plant; two probes, Text as Clay (gestural sculpting of text) and Text as Plants (ideas growing serendipitously over time). Framework layers: LLM operations on semantics, structure, and style (compose, abstract, ideate, condense, transform), mapped to material-like interactions. Abstract read, plus introduction in the arXiv fetch. HaLLMark (Hoque, Mashiat, Ghai, Shelton, Chevalier, Kraus, Elmqvist, CHI 2024, Honolulu, DOI 10.1145/3613904.3641895; arXiv:2311.13057, v4, 24 March 2024): a web-based technology probe that records and visualizes a writer's interaction with an LLM as the document evolves, so provenance becomes visible. The text editor highlights AI-written text in orange (the two accounts agree on this) and AI-influenced text in green per the paper's Figure 1 caption, while a co-author's summary says human-generated text is green and AI-generated text is orange -- the two accounts agree that orange marks AI-generated text but disagree about what green marks, and this study did not resolve it from the full text, so it does not state the green mapping without checking the paper's Figure 1. A timeline of prompts and responses sits beside the editor, and hovering a timeline glyph shows the prompt and the linked text. Summary statistics report the share of text written by the AI and the number of prompts used. Evaluated with 13 creative writers; the paper reports it helped them retain a sense of control and ownership. Abstract read (arXiv v2 and v4 agree); introduction and system description read in the arXiv HTML fetch. The visual language across all three is the same as the rest of her work: colour as code (two highlight colours doing all the work in HaLLMark), and the document itself as the medium.

**Takeaway:** Words are pixels, sentences are regions, tone is a colour. Text stops being something you type and becomes something you handle. [C35559]

## 21. The aesthetic logic

Chevalier's visual language, as observed across the works, has four constants. First, the drawn line does double duty: a sketch stroke in Draco is the motion path; a sketched curve in Kitty is the functional wiring; a stroke in Squidgets is the widget; a drawn layout in DataInk is the chart structure. One mark is both picture and control, which is why her interfaces need no menus. Second, colour is code, not decoration: orange/green provenance in HaLLMark, subway-line identity in EgoLines, sugar-cube units in Concrete Scales, highlight colour as tone in Textoshop. Palettes are restrained and functional; there is no signature decorative palette. Third, motion is the material. In the perception studies the picture is reduced to dots so that timing is the only variable; in the authoring tools the motion is the content the user creates. Nothing in this body of work treats animation as a flourish. Fourth, scale is made visible by familiar things: a human, a sports field, a sugar cube, a pile of bills. The concrete scale is her most portable visual idea: when a number is too big to feel, show it as a quantity of things the viewer already knows. Her interfaces are diagrammatic: panels, lenses, timelines, layers, timelines of glyphs. The system is shown as a visible structure the user can read, not hidden behind chrome. And the through-line that separates her from her collaborators: every system is paired with an evaluation that can say no. The staggering study is the purest example, a paper whose finding is that the fashionable technique does not help. The aesthetic is an honest one: the picture is a test, and the test is allowed to fail.

**Takeaway:** Motion is information, never ornament; colour is code, never decoration; and the system is always allowed to say no. [C35561]

## 22. Evolution: four eras

2010-2013 (Inria Aviz / OCAD period): instruments for experts. GraphDice, Diffamation, ChronoLenses, KronoMiner, Gliimpse, Histomages, Concrete Scales, DirectPaint. The question is how a person explores or edits a rich representation: networks, revision histories, time series, code, photographs, video. The characteristic move is to make the representation itself directly manipulable -- a histogram you can paint, a lens that recomputes what it covers, a diff you can scrub through. Technique: novel interaction models and systems, evaluated with user studies. Subject: data and documents for analysts and editors. 2014-2016 (Toronto / Autodesk period): the living picture, and the science of animation. Draco, Kitty, LACES, the staggered-animation study, Your Paper Is Dead, Animation 25 Years Later, EgoLines. The sketch becomes the input language, and animation becomes both the medium and the object of study: first build tools that let drawings move (Draco, Kitty), then measure rigorously what motion does to perception (staggering, common fate's beginnings) and taxonomize what animation is for. Ambition widens from "a better tool" to "a new kind of document" (the animated figure inside the paper). 2017-2021 (U of T assistant professor): literacy, communication, narrative. C'est la Vis, DataInk, Interactive Data Comics, Common Fate, the data-comics study reports. The audience shifts from experts to children, non-expert authors, and general readers. The work asks how people learn to read visualizations at all, and how a story can branch, be drawn by hand, or be re-read as a comic. Technique shifts toward co-design with teachers, design frameworks, and declarative specifications (COMICSCRIPT) rather than single systems. 2024-2026 (U of T associate professor, Vector affiliate): text as material, and media that stays alive. HaLLMark, DataGarden, Textoshop, Squidgets, Texterial, Live Artifacts, the 2026 capacity-limits guidelines. Two threads converge: the sketch/direct-manipulation thread (Squidgets, DataGarden) and the writing thread (HaLLMark, Textoshop, Texterial), both now running through LLMs. The newest move, Live Artifacts, generalizes the whole career: a picture that keeps its generative logic instead of collapsing into a static file is Draco's living illustration, grown up into a media theory. The 2026 guidelines paper closes a loop opened in 2014: the perception science now yields quantitative design rules (track at most three to four objects). What did NOT change: animation is treated as information, never as ornament; the user acts directly on the thing itself (a histogram, a sketch, a sentence, a layer) rather than through menus and dialogs; and every system is paired with an evaluation that can say no.

**Takeaway:** Four eras, one question. The instruments change, the audience widens, the medium goes from paper to LLM -- and the eye is still the thing being tested. [C35562]
