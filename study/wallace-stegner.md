# Wallace Stegner: Study Notes

**Site:** Wallace Earle Stegner (1909-1993), American novelist, historian and conservationist, often called "The Dean of Western Writers" -- born Thursday 18 February 1909 on a farm outside Lake Mills, Iowa, second son of George Henry Stegner, a serial schemer (gold seeker in Alaska, farmer in Saskatchewan, bootlegger in Utah), and Hilda Emelia Paulson Stegner, who died of breast cancer in 1933 aged 50; raised, by his own count, in twenty places in eight states and Canada, above all Eastend, Saskatchewan (about ages 5 to 12), Great Falls, Montana, and Salt Lake City, Utah from 1921; B.A. English, University of Utah, 1930, Phi Beta Kappa; M.A. 1932 and Ph.D. 1935, State University of Iowa (the dissertation on the geologist Clarence Edward Dutton), with additional study at the University of California in 1932-33; married Mary Stuart Page on Saturday 1 September 1934, one son, Page (born 1937); taught at Augustana College 1933-34, the University of Utah 1934-37, the University of Wisconsin 1937-39, Harvard 1939-45, and Stanford 1945-71, where he founded the Creative Writing Program in 1946 and directed it into the early 1970s; author of thirteen novels from Remembering Laughter (1937) to Crossing to Safety (1987), including The Big Rock Candy Mountain (1943), Angle of Repose (1971, Pulitzer Prize for Fiction 1972) and The Spectator Bird (1976, National Book Award 1977); historian of the arid West in Beyond the Hundredth Meridian (1954) and Wolf Willow (1962); biographer of his friend and mentor Bernard DeVoto in The Uneasy Chair (1974) and editor of The Letters of Bernard DeVoto (1975); editor of This Is Dinosaur (1955) in the Echo Park fight; author of the Wilderness Letter of Saturday 3 December 1960, whose closing words "the geography of hope" helped carry the Wilderness Act of Thursday 3 September 1964; Sierra Club board 1964-66; National Parks Advisory Board 1962-66, chairman in the final year; special assistant to Interior Secretary Stewart Udall in 1961; co-founder of the Committee for Green Foothills in 1962; refused the National Endowment for the Arts medal in 1992; died Tuesday 13 April 1993 in Santa Fe, New Mexico, aged 84, of injuries from a car accident on 28 March 1993; ashes scattered on a hill near the family summer cottage in Greensboro, Vermont. ## M1. The Document Reconstruction
Rebuild a dead person's life entirely from surviving paper -- letters, diaries, clippings -- and make the novel BE the act of rebuilding, gaps and guesses included. Angle of Repose: Lyman Ward, 58, retired history professor, divorced amputee in a wheelchair, dictates a biography of his grandmother Susan Burling Ward from her letters to her friend Augusta, from 12 April 1970 through the summer, in nine parts, at Zodiac Cottage, Grass Valley. "Lyman interprets the story at times and leaves gaps that he points out at other times." His own divorce colors his reading; the text is presented as transcribed tapes; the narrative runs in three registers at once -- first-person Lyman, first-person letters, third-person reconstruction from Susan's point of view. What it IS: the archive as the plot, the historian's uncertainty as the drama. **Takeaway:** give the narrator only paper, let him say where the paper runs out, and the gaps become the story.

## M2. The Angle of Repose
Take a physics term -- the steepest angle at which loose material stays stable without sliding -- and make it the novel's governing image: a marriage, a life, a family that tumbles until it finds the slope where it can rest, and the rest may be mere cessation, not contentment. The title is explained on page 13 of the novel; Lyman and Susan agree it is "too good for mere dirt" and "descriptive of human as well as detrital rest." Lyman: "Did you ever come down out of that into some restful 30 degree angle and live happily ever after? ... Was the quiet I always felt in you really repose?" Wikipedia: "the angle at which soil finally settles after, for example, being dumped from a mine as tailings." The canal-bank reading is literal too: the Wards' daughter drowns in a canal whose dirt banks sit at the angle of repose and slide under her. Oliver Ward is a mining engineer who spends his life trying to find that physical angle in the landscape, and never finds the human one. What it IS: one technical image, carried from geology into marriage, that names the whole book. **Takeaway:** find the one term that is true in the trade and true in the marriage, and let it do the titling.

## M3. The Two-Timeline Braid
Alternate a thin present (days, a summer, a weekend) with a deep past (decades), so the past is always being judged by an old person who knows how it ends -- and put a spectator at the present end of the braid, a man whose job was watching other people's lives. Angle of Repose (1970 present against the 1870s-1900s past). The Spectator Bird: present-day Joe Allston, almost 70, rereads journals he kept on a Denmark trip twenty years earlier, journals his wife never knew about; "the memories ... move through layers of time and meaning." Recapitulation: two days in May 1977 against Salt Lake City in the 1920s-30s, "the narrative floats back and forth between the present and the past." Crossing to Safety: a Vermont farewell weekend in the 1970s against Madison 1937 onward. The spectator is the mechanism's hinge: Joe Allston, retired literary agent, "just killing time till time gets around to killing me," who "sees himself as being on the perimeter of the lives of those writers that he represents and also reads," parallels himself with "the bird that watches and observes the living of other, more active and involved birds" -- and is then forced into the action, the Danish countess Astrid, the journal his wife never saw, the unresolved romance. In All the Little Live Things the same watcher is dragged into his neighbors' lives (Marian's cancer, Peck's squat) on his own hilltop "Eden." What it IS: the past braid, the present spectator, and the moment the spectator stops spectating. **Takeaway:** braid the decades against a weekend, and make the present-tense man a watcher until the braid forces him to act.

## M4. The Return
Send an older man back, physically, to the ground of his childhood or youth, and let the return release memory as sensory flood rather than as chronology. The material is the migrant childhood itself: "lived in twenty places in eight states and Canada," and the same few years keep being retold -- in fiction, in memoir, in history -- until they yield meaning. Wolf Willow opens with Stegner as a "middle-aged pilgrim" walking Eastend/Whitemud, trying to make memory real; it happens only when he smells the wolf willow -- "the tantalizing and ambiguous and wholly native smell." Recapitulation: Bruce Mason, 70, drives into Salt Lake City past Black Rock and the Saltair Pavilion, "smelling the foul, exciting salt-flat odor," and feels "like the newsreel diver whom the reversed projector sucks feet first out of his splash." Crossing to Safety: the Morgans drive back to the Lang compound at Battel Pond, Vermont, for a last farewell. The Big Rock Candy Mountain (1943) is the Mason family saga told from inside the moves; Recapitulation (1979) returns to Bruce Mason thirty-six years later; Wolf Willow (1962) returns to Eastend; "Finding the Place: A Migrant Childhood" returns again. What it IS: the same ground, revisited at three ages, each time in a different genre. **Takeaway:** do not invent new material; walk the same ground again, older, and let the smell do what chronology cannot.

## M5. The Deep Map
Take one small, precisely bounded tract of country and stack its histories -- geology, native peoples, settlement, personal memory, fiction -- until the place itself becomes the argument. History, story, and memory are braided, not separated. Wolf Willow's subtitle: "A History, a Story, and a Memory of the Last Plains Frontier." Its territory: "that block of country between the Milk River and the main line of the Canadian Pacific, and between approximately the Saskatchewan-Alberta line and Wood Mountain." Maher: "the headwaters of deep mapping ... the multiple histories of place, the intercalated stories of natural and human history." Method: "the warp of memory and the woof of history." The town dump as archive: "If the history of Whitemud was not exactly written, it was at least hinted, in the dump" -- "our poetry and our history." The book turns to fiction halfway through ("Carrion Spring") to tell why Whitemud died in the winter of 1906-07. The sensory register is the proof of the method: scale as a bodily sense, "the smell of distance." "Whenever I return to the Rocky Mountain states where I am most at home... the smell of distance excites me, the largeness and the clarity take the scales from eyes, and I respond as unthinkingly as a salmon that swims past a river mouth and tastes the waters of its birth." On the Saskatchewan prairie: "the sky in that country came clear down to the ground on every side... a prairie like that, one big enough to carry the eye clear to the sinking, rounding horizon, can be as lonely and grand and simple in its forms as the sea." Homesickness in Iowa: "I was used to seeing a long way. I was used to earth colors -- tan, rusty, red, toned white -- and the endless green of Iowa offended me." What it IS: one bounded place, all its layers at once, proved by the nose. **Takeaway:** bound the place tight, stack every layer on it, and let a smell prove the map is real.

## M6. The Career Biography
Refuse the personality biography on principle; write the history of a career and of the ideas it carried, wrenching chronology as needed and ignoring private life. Beyond the Hundredth Meridian, Author's Note: "THIS BOOK is an attempt to write a biography that is the history not of a personality but of a career. I am not interested in Major Powell's personality... I am interested in him ... As the personification of an ideal of public service... As the source and mouthpiece of ideas three quarters of a century ahead of their possible fulfillment." Hanson, quoting Benson: "I will wrench chronology as much as seems necessary, and I will continue to ignore the home life, bathroom habits, and other minutiae of Major Powell." Powell's importance: "seventy-five years ago he pierced through those misconceptions to the realities" and "tried to shape legal and political and social institutions so that they would accord with the necessities of the West." The same discipline, inverted, governs The Uneasy Chair: Stegner writes DeVoto's career -- the novels, the Easy Chair, the trilogy, the conservation campaign -- and lets the friendship show only where it bears on the work. What it IS: the life told as the ideas it carried, with the bedroom door shut. **Takeaway:** biography the career, not the man, and the ideas get their own plot.

## M7. Aridity as the Defining Limit
Define the West by inadequate rainfall, and make water, not land, the measure of wealth. Where the Bluebird Sings: "The West is defined, that is, by inadequate rainfall, which means a general deficiency of water." West of the 100th meridian, "the amount of land a man has no longer is the defining characteristic of his success -- it is instead the amount of water that he controls." Powell's Report on the Arid Region (1878/1879): "there is enough water for only a fifth of the land." Powell's reforms produced a two-year suspension of homestead and land laws, 1888-90. Stegner: "in a dry country such as the American West the wounds men make in the earth do not quickly heal." The choice the fact forces is the argument of the book: "You may deny [the aridity] for a while. Then you must try to engineer it out of existence or adapt to it." Both DeVoto and Stegner held adaptation preferable to engineering, and Stegner's last essays argue that engineering aridity out of existence produced dammed, diverted, drought-vulnerable rivers and cities. What it IS: one physical limit, stated as a number, that rebuilds every institution. **Takeaway:** name the limit in numbers, then show every institution bending to it or breaking on it.

## M8. The Culture of Motion
Treat most Western literature as a literature of travel, not of place: the land is a barrier to cross on the way to somewhere else; the story is about desire, not fulfillment. Where the Bluebird Sings: "Look at any book that is western in its feel... and you will find that it is a book not about place but about motion, not about fulfillment but about desire. There is always a seeking, generally unsatisfied." Hanson: "most people (other than the Mormons) viewed the West as something to get through, not to." The Big Rock Candy Mountain is this mechanism as a family tragedy: Bo Mason drags Elsa, Chet and Bruce from North Dakota to Washington to Saskatchewan to Montana to Utah, always chasing the next plenty, and the quest "can drive wedges between a man and his wife, between a father and his children." The song's title is the mirage: a land where "hand-outs grow on bushes" and the bluebird sings to a lemonade spring. The Mormons are the great exception -- a culture of place, not of motion -- which is why The Gathering of Zion (1964) sits beside the novel as its counter-argument. What it IS: restlessness as the Western disease, diagnosed in one family. **Takeaway:** show the moves, not the arrival, and the seeking itself becomes the tragedy.

## M9. The Unromantic West
Strip the romance off the Western image -- the cowboy, the rugged individualist, the garden West -- and replace it with cooperation, wage labor, and aridity. "The cowboy in practice was and is an overworked, underpaid hireling, almost as dispossessed as a modern crop worker, and his fabled independence was and is chiefly the privilege of quitting his job in order to go looking for another just as bad." DeVoto, quoted by Stegner: "the only real individualists in the West had wound up on one end of a rope whose other end was in the hands of a bunch of cooperators." "The West never got over its heightened and romantic notion of itself." "I don't purvey horse opera," Stegner said. "I don't think that six-gun West amounts to much ... it never did." His novella Genesis has no six-guns, fights, gamblers or desperados -- only riders and cattle in a storm, a story of work and trust. The positive form is cooperation, not the loner: Hanson: "these factors created individuals who were part of a group rather than the mythical loner. Instead of rugged individualists, the West was an area where people depended on one another rather than on themselves." Stegner: "there is something about living in big empty space... that not only tells an individual how small he is, but steadily tells him who he is." He lived the point in the 1940s, co-founding the Peninsula Housing Association, a co-operative housing project for Stanford faculty, and abandoning it rather than accept a whites-only financing condition. What it IS: the myth demolished from inside, and the group put in its place. **Takeaway:** take the cowboy's independence away and show the crew that actually did the work.

## M10. The Conservation Letter as Public Act
Argue for wilderness not by its uses (hunting, hiking, scenery) but as an idea -- a spiritual resource, a genetic reserve, a yardstick, a bank against which character was formed -- and make the argument in a letter that becomes law. The Wilderness Letter, 3 December 1960, to David E. Pesonen of the Outdoor Recreation Resources Review Commission, read in full in this study, written in a single afternoon, per his son Page: "What I want to speak for is not so much the wilderness uses, valuable as those are, but the wilderness idea, which is a resource in itself." "Something will have gone out of us as a people if we ever let the remaining wilderness be destroyed; if we permit the last virgin forests to be turned into comic books and plastic cigarette cases..." "One means of sanity is to retain a hold on the natural world, to remain, insofar as we can, good animals." "every such loss is a little death in me. In us." "Better a wounded wilderness than none at all." "We need wilderness preserved -- as much of it as is still left, and as many kinds -- because it was the challenge against which our character as a people was formed." The closing: "We simply need that wild country available to us, even if we never do more than drive to its edge and look in. For it can be a means of reassuring ourselves of our sanity as creatures, a part of the geography of hope." The letter was used to introduce the Wilderness Act (1964); the Sierra Club borrowed its last four words for Eliot Porter's Baja book. Stegner, in his 1980 introduction, repeats that preservation is "not simply as a scientific reserve, or a land-bank, or a playground, but as a spiritual resource," and late in life told Brian Meehan he had been describing "a psychological condition, not necessarily an economic probability." The method extends to editing: in This Is Dinosaur (1955) he deleted, altered and rewrote Alfred Knopf's angrier passages and personal attacks -- "We are trying to sound objective, good-tempered, and without personal rancor (though we burn inside)" -- and the book helped kill the Echo Park and Split Mountain dam proposals in 1956, at the price of Glen Canyon, a bargain Stegner and Brower came to regret ("Having saved Dinosaur, we accepted the ruin of Glen Canyon, which was not very smart of us"). The positions around the letter: the reluctant environmentalist who "always talked about himself as a reluctant environmentalist" and did not call himself an environmentalist; the middle ground "between the toadstool worshippers and land-grabbing sagebrush rebels"; the Sagebrush Rebellion as "the worst enemy not only of long-range management of the public lands, but of wilderness," the 1980s rerun of the 1940s "Landgrab" DeVoto fought -- the land "belongs as much to me, or to a grocer in Des Moines, or a taxi driver in Newark, as to anyone else." What it IS: the idea argued as a resource, in prose that stays good-tempered while burning inside. **Takeaway:** argue the idea, not the use, and let the letter do what the picket line cannot.

## M11. The Skeptical Biographer
Treat biography as disciplined skepticism toward its own materials, and say so inside the book. The Uneasy Chair opens with DeVoto's own credo, "The Skeptical Biographer": "Biography is the wrong field for the mystical... It enforces an unremitting skepticism -- toward its material, toward the subject, most of all toward the biographer... His job is not dramatic; it is only to discover evidence and to analyze it. And all the evidence he can find is the least satisfactory kind, documentary evidence, which is among the most treacherous phenomena in a malevolent world." Stegner on the difficulty: writing about DeVoto was "complicated by the fact that I had known Benny DeVoto very well. It's always more difficult to write about someone you know well." His verdict on DeVoto: "flawed, brilliant, provocative, outrageous, ... often wrong, often spectacularly right, always stimulating, sometimes infuriating, and never, never dull." The same skepticism governs his own fiction's documents: the letters in Angle of Repose are borrowed, warped, and partly invented, and the book owns the warping in its acknowledgment. What it IS: the biographer doubting his documents in public. **Takeaway:** put the doubt in the book, not in the footnote, and the reader trusts the rest.

## M12. The Teaching Method
Found a program with no grand theory: give young writers a stipend, a room, and each other; teach by cutting prose clean, by insisting on concrete things over concepts, and by endless revision. Founded the Stanford Creative Writing Program in 1946 (the fellowship created for returning World War II soldiers; at the time Iowa was the only other degree-granting creative writing program in the country); directed it into the early 1970s; students included Wendell Berry, Edward Abbey, Ken Kesey, Larry McMurtry, Robert Stone, Ernest Gaines, Sandra Day O'Connor, N. Scott Momaday, Robert Hass, Gordon Lish, Thomas McGuane, Simin Daneshvar, George V. Higgins, Andrew Glaze, Nancy Packer, Scott Turow and Tillie Olsen. "There were no grand theories. We just tried to help writers do what they were trying to do." (Nancy Packer.) "He was critical in a pure sense. He said things to teach you, not to please you." (Merrill Joan Gerber.) "The creative writer is compulsively concrete... he is concerned with people, places, actions, feelings, sensations... One Macbeth on stage is worth a thousand essays on ambition." Frost, quoted by Stegner: a fiction writer should "tell what happened to himself as if it had happened to someone else, and what happened to someone else as if it had happened to himself." On revision: "practice and rewriting, cutting the prose clean, honing the exigent art of seeing straight." The Kesey tension is the method's limit case: Stegner taught "sustained work, constant revision and carefully honed perspective"; Kesey (1959-61) was the raw-material rival, his Stegner Fellowship applications repeatedly rejected, and Bob Stone told Benson that Stegner "saw Kesey and what he represented as a threat to civilization and intellectualism and sobriety." Lynn Stegner's foreword: of hundreds of thousands of pages Stegner produced, "from first, to fifteenth, to final drafts (because he was, above all else, a believer in revision), fewer than two hundred pages concern the art of fiction." What it IS: the greenhouse, not the lecture hall -- a disciplined place to write, with criticism and support. **Takeaway:** teach revision and concreteness, and let the writers do the writing.

## M13. The Borrowed Archive
Take a real, unpublished archive -- a woman's letters and reminiscences -- and build a novel on it so closely that whole passages are quoted word for word, changing only names and a few facts; the novel's vividness IS the archive's vividness. Angle of Repose is "directly based on the letters of Mary Hallock Foote, later published as A Victorian Gentlewoman in the Far West." An estimated ~10% of the novel (about 66 pages of 555) is verbatim or near-verbatim from Foote's letters and reminiscences; indented, smaller-font passages are largely Foote's. Stegner had permission from Foote's granddaughters -- per Wikipedia, secured only after claiming the novel would not use direct quotations, a charge Sands Hall makes and Stegner's defenders contest. His acknowledgment: "My thanks to JM and her sister for the loan of their ancestors. Though I have used many details of their lives and characters, I have not hesitated to warp both personalities and events to fictional needs." He also invented an affair and a drowning that never happened in Foote's life. Stegner: "the ways of fiction are devious indeed"; "I quite honestly don't quite know where fiction ends and nonfiction begins." The plagiarism charge is live and contested; this study presents both the permission and the uncredited-quotation facts and does not pick a side. What it IS: a novel whose best sentences are someone else's letters, owned in the acknowledgment and contested ever since. **Takeaway:** borrow the archive, warp it openly, and accept that the argument about where fiction ends will outlive the book.

## M14. Friendship as the Load-Bearing Structure
Build a whole novel out of a friendship between two couples over decades, with no crisis except time, illness, and the difference between people who impose order and people who endure chaos. Crossing to Safety: Larry and Sally Morgan (poor, western, writer) and Sid and Charity Lang (rich, eastern, academic), meeting in Madison, Wisconsin, 1937, reuniting in Vermont as Charity dies of cancer. Stegner: "[Friendship] is a relationship that has no formal shape, there are no rules or obligations or bonds as in marriage or the family, it is held together by neither law nor property nor blood, there is no glue in it but mutual liking. It is therefore rare." The Henry Adams epigraph: "Chaos is the law of nature; order is the dream of man." Charity is the order-imposer (the tea-bag camping scene); Larry and Sally are the chaos-endurers (Sally's polio). "Drama, Stegner writes, demands the reversal of expectation, but in such a way that the first surprise is followed by an immediate recognition of inevitability." (Terry Tempest Williams introduction.) Larry's stated intention: to write only about his friends, exposing the divide between his life and his work. The same structure, in life, is the DeVoto friendship: the mentor whose biography Stegner wrote as an act of homage, and the Abbey eulogy line -- Abbey as "a red-hot moment in the conscience of the country," Berry's return letter calling Stegner "not a red-hot moment, but one that was luminous, clarifying, and steady." What it IS: friendship as the only structure that holds, because nothing holds it. **Takeaway:** give two couples thirty years and one dying friend, and let order and chaos take turns.

## Evolution: four phases, and the afterlife so far
Phase one, apprenticeship and the autobiographical West, 1937-1947. Short novels (Remembering Laughter, The Potter's House, On a Darkling Plain, Fire and Ice) -- well-crafted but flawed, poor sellers. First nonfiction (Mormon Country, 1942) and the breakthrough semi-autobiographical epic The Big Rock Candy Mountain (1943). Driver: his own migrant childhood and his father Bo Mason/George Stegner; restless individualism as a destructive expression of outworn frontier mythology. Teaching moves east (Wisconsin, Harvard) while the material stays western. Phase two, history as argument, the West in fact not myth, 1947-1962. Fewer novels, longer gaps (Second Growth 1947, The Preacher and the Slave 1950, then no novel until A Shooting Star 1961). The turn is to nonfiction history with a thesis: One Nation (1945, race and religion in wartime America), Beyond the Hundredth Meridian (1954, Powell and aridity -- institutions must fit the land, not the myth), This Is Dinosaur (1955, activism), The Gathering of Zion (1964, the Mormon Trail), Wolf Willow (1962, history plus story plus memory fused in one book about one place). Stegner becomes a public historian and conservation advocate; the Stanford program (1946 onward) becomes his second lifework. Phase three, late mastery: memory, marriage, friendship, 1961-1979. A Shooting Star (1961), All the Little Live Things (1967), then the two prize novels built on documents and retrospection: Angle of Repose (1971, letters and diaries, past/present braid, East against West marriage) and The Spectator Bird (1976, old age, return to Denmark, the same Joe Allston). The Uneasy Chair (1974) -- biography as homage to a friend and mentor. The voice becomes more reflective, elegiac, concerned with how the past is remembered and misremembered. Phase four, valediction, 1979-1992. Recapitulation (1979) -- direct return to Bruce Mason and Salt Lake City, reconciliation with the father. Crossing to Safety (1987) -- friendship and marriage over thirty-four years, Vermont as the "safety" after western turbulence, his most commercially popular late book. Final essay collection Where the Bluebird Sings to the Lemonade Springs (1992) -- debunking western illusions and mirages, arguing that engineering aridity out of existence produced dammed, diverted, drought-vulnerable rivers and cities. Ends with the refusal of the NEA medal and the car accident in Santa Fe. The afterlife so far: The Geography of Hope: A Tribute to Wallace Stegner (1996, edited by Page and Mary Stegner); Jackson J. Benson, Wallace Stegner: His Life and Work (1996, Viking), written with interviews and unrestricted access to his papers; Philip L. Fradkin, Wallace Stegner and the American West (2008); Alex Beam, Wallace Stegner: Dean of Western Writers (2025); the papers at the Marriott Library, University of Utah (Ms0676, 1935-2004, 29 boxes, 139 linear feet, including an unpublished autobiography); the Stegner Prize in Environmental or American Western History established 2010 at University of Utah Press; the Wallace Stegner Academy charter school opened in Salt Lake City in August 2016. The arc is a slow turn from the self to the place: the boy who moved twenty times became the man who stayed put in one program and one region, and wrote the West as something to live in rather than to cross.

## Aesthetic logic
The book and the life share one aesthetic: the Westerner tells the story straight, from inside the place, and the truth is the constraint on the story, not its enemy. Stegner grew up a migrant in his own country -- twenty places, no hometown, a father who kept chasing the next plenty -- and the homelessness never left him: "I may not know who I am, but I know where I am from. I've got an exaggerated sense of place ... my personal experiences are all I surely know, and those experiences are very likely to be rooted in places." The outsider's position is the method, but it is the opposite of DeVoto's exile: DeVoto wrote from the West against the East; Stegner wrote from inside the West against the West's own romance about itself. "No place is a place until things that have happened in it are remembered in history, ballads, yarns, legends, or monuments. Fictions serve as well as facts." Everything else follows: the document reconstruction (the paper that remembers), the deep map (the place that holds the layers), aridity (the fact that will not be wished away), the unromantic West (the myth stripped off), the conservation letter (the idea defended as a resource), the teaching method (the concrete thing over the concept). The pupil completes the master without breaking from him: DeVoto gave Stegner the plundered-province frame and the simultaneity; Stegner gave the frame a second generation, a program, and a law. The limit is on the record too: Stanford Magazine notes he "never fully grappled with the murder and dislocation of Indigenous people by the on-rushing settlers," and the deep map's layers stop short of the first one. The aesthetic is a refusal: no horse opera, no garden West, no theory in place of the place, no abstraction in place of the concrete thing -- and no wilderness defended for its uses when it can be defended as an idea. "The world is very large, the sky even larger, and you are very small."
