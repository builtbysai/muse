# Romain Tardy: Study Notes

**Site:** Romain Tardy (b. Paris, 23 September 1984), French visual artist and "digital sculptor" (his own term, in use since 2008), based in Brussels; co-founder of the visual label AntiVJ (2008 to the end of 2013); since then an independent artist making tangible, offline light-and-sound installations that materialise the virtual in architecture and public space. Trained in art and design (ecole d'arts appliques, then Beaux-Arts du Mans), digital skills self-taught on computer forums; VJ as Aalto from 2004 to June 2011. His stated program, assembled from his own words: installations that "transcend the limitations of the screen"; mapping as "materialising the virtual rather than virtualising the material... a concrete, shared, and re-politicised experience"; "Storytelling is key. Everything starts with a story"; the building and its surroundings are always the starting point, with no pre-produced content before the first site visit. The arc runs 2008-2026: the collective AntiVJ years of white-line geometry on buildings; the narrative turn toward drawing and the internet as subject; the object turn, where projection recedes and LED line sculptures become the work; and the public turn, where the subject is data, migration, rest, and the tech giants themselves, sculpted in light and air.

## The life: a VJ who wanted the image off the screen

Tardy was born in Paris on 23 September 1984 and studied art and design for four years -- ecole d'arts appliques, then Beaux-Arts du Mans -- before deciding it was time for something different. The digital half of his training was self-taught, learned on computer forums in parallel with art school (C2704). He started VJing in 2004, amateur at first and then professionally, performing under the pseudonym Aalto until June 2011 (the dates rest on the French mapping-history document alone; the alias itself is corroborated by a 2010 interview that addresses him as "Romain Tardy a.k.a. Aalto from AntiVJ", C2705). At the same time he worked for animation and post-production studios in Paris, and the VJ years taught him the thing that organises everything after: the complex links between the audio and the visual, observed night after night from inside a live set.

AntiVJ is the hinge, and its founding year is the record's first fault line. The label's own page says it "was originally created by Yannick Jacquet, Joanie Lemercier, Olivier Ratsi and Romain Tardy around 2006". The French history document says 2007-2008; Mapping Festival says he "forms in 2008 ANTIVJ, a European visual label, with three other artists"; Lemercier's own notice says 2008. This study uses 2008 as the founding year -- his own words, Mapping Festival, and the Lemercier notice -- and treats "around 2006" as the informal pre-history, the four working together before the label formalised (C2703). What the sources agree on: the four came out of VJing and set out to move the image off screens and onto architecture; the label worked as a label, not a collective -- solo projects, collective projects and external commissions, like a music label. Tardy was a founder-artist, not a co-director: the label was co-directed by Lemercier and producer Nicolas Boritch until 2010, then managed by Boritch alone (C2715). Composer Thomas Vaquie scored most works from 2008; coder Simon Geilfus joined in 2009.

His departure is the flagged question from #602, and it is resolved this run by his own words: "I am the co-founder of the European visual label ANTIVJ and worked as an artist in this group from 2008 until the end of 2013" (GLOW Eindhoven, first person). His own About page agrees ("From 2008 to 2013"), Mapping Festival agrees ("a leading player in the field until 2013"). The single outlier is the French history document's "active until 2014", which most likely reflects lingering credits: MGNTRN premiered in June 2014, and his #DEFILE page still credits "Coordination & project management: ANTIVJ" for a work premiered in 2013 and re-mounted in 2017 (C2702). The study says 2008 to the end of 2013 and carries 2014 as a footnote only. After several years in Paris he moved to Brussels, where he currently lives and works -- no move date is given in any source read, so none is assigned here (C2717). He leads a team that adjusts its size to the project, does creative and art direction and consulting, teaches graphic design, and gives workshops and lectures at art schools and universities.

The solo practice after AntiVJ keeps the mapping but broadens it: The Ark (Proyecta Oaxaca, 2013/2014; Ars Electronica official selection 2014; winner of the 4th Bienal Iberoamericana de Diseno, Madrid, 2014), MGNTRN (Proyecta Puebla, 2014), "Je rate mon cerveau pre-internet" (Musee d'Art et d'Histoire de Geneve, Mapping Festival 2016), The Great Indecision Council (commissioned by Into The Great Wide Open; GLOW 2018; Prix Ars Electronica 2019 honorary mention), "Borders at sea level" (the Tunisian pavilion, Venice Biennale 2017). After 2019 the record in sources read is festival commissions and light installations rather than gallery shows: Sharjah Light Festival 2024 (two works, via LDA Productions), Noor Riyadh 2024, Fete des Lumieres 2025 as residency mentor for "Nouvelle vague", Bright Brussels February 2026 ("Emergences", vertical beams bursting from manhole covers through mist), and the thin Vimeo record of The Drift in Rome (October 2026). His installations have been presented in more than 15 countries by Mapping Festival's 2019 count -- a floor, not a current figure, since the 2024-2026 commissions postdate it (C2708). Residencies run from CDA Enghien-les-Bains (2009) and Nabi Art Center, Seoul (2009) through Meetfactory Prague (2010), Institut Francais Havana (2011) and Tokyo (2012), Centro de Diseno Oaxaca (2013), Musee de l'Elysee Lausanne (2015), Perte de signal Montreal (2016), Base sous-marine Bordeaux (2017), to the AI art-and-science seminaries at Quai des Savoirs, Toulouse (2022) and L'Hexagone, Grenoble (2023) -- the last two listed with no detail about what he made there (C2719).

The commercial strand is attested and undetailed, and the study does not invent it. Fete des Lumieres: "As a co-founder of the AntiVJ label, Tardy has forged collaborations with notable hip-hop artists including Jay-Z, Flying Lotus and Murcof." AKQA: "His collaborations include digital scenography for Jay Z and Stromae." His own About page repeats the Jay Z / Flying Lotus / Murcof line. What the Jay-Z work actually was -- tour visuals, a specific show -- is not detailed in any source read, and no project title or venue is stated for it (C2706). Twenty-five smaller project pages on his own sitemap were not opened this leg, most of them commercial or scenography commissions (Jay-Z x Barneys NYC 2013, Flying Lotus 2011, Stromae 2011, Nosaj Thing 2010, Boss Black 2012 among them); the scenography strand is therefore narrowed in this study, and the works catalogue leans on the festival and installation works.

## The turn: from the white line to the browser, 2008-2013

The early AntiVJ manner is monochrome and collective: white lines, red planes, black voids, a built structure or a facade as the screen. Light Sculpture (2008) is the thesis in miniature -- angular planes and blocks built as a projection surface, glowing white slabs and deep red planes cutting across black voids, no architecture behind the light. The same formal language scales up to a stage (Nuits Sonores, 2009: a pristine white faceted structure inside a derelict wholesale market, pulsing to the DJ's music), to a heritage monument (Grote Kerk, 2009: mapping on a 25-metre baroque organ, played live as an instrument at the same time), to a new city (Songdo, 2009: blocky white geometry on a curved facade in a city built from scratch on reclaimed land, sold as a "u-City" of ubiquitous computing, with "an intriguing aftertaste of an impending apocalypse"). The subject in these years is the building and the city, and the sound is live -- a DJ, an organist -- or by Squeaky Lobster (Laurent Delforge).

The turn has a date, a collaborator, and a quote. Desherence (2009, Enghien-les-Bains) is the first fully narrative facade: sepia-and-gold clockwork drawn in fine line over the city hall, gears and wheels over stone, "LIBERTE" and "FRATERNITE" still legible on the balcony, the piece rooted in the swamp the city was built on and in the word itself -- desherence, the French legal term for abandoned property -- with a "Kafkaesque aesthetic" of neglect and transience. Le Bestiaire (2011, Havana) makes the turn explicit: a 12-minute "dynamic painting" of bone creatures and skeletons moving in the windows of a Havana building, tangled vines drawn across the stone, built by digital collage after Hieronymus Bosch and the Cuban artist Belkis Ayon, deliberately departing from typical 3D mapping aesthetics toward a hand-made animation style, each frame "meticulously crafted". The image is now figurative and drawn; the references are painters, not buildings.

The same years produce the two anchor works of the period. O (Omicron) (2012) is the peak of the early manner and the first permanent work: a deliberately minimal projection inside Max Berg's 1913 concrete dome at the Hala Stulecia in Wroclaw -- 65 metres across, UNESCO World Heritage since 2006 -- built from references spanning 1910s construction, Fritz Lang's Metropolis and Archigram into "a vision of the future that transcends any specific time frame -- a timeless future", with Vaquie's orchestral-electronic score aiming to simulate the sonic aging of the dome's materials. Whether it still runs in 2026 was not verified from any recent source (C2761). The Ark (2013, Oaxaca) is the break with the facade altogether: a three-part installation built around the cacti lining the Aljibe, the garden's water cistern, opened at night exceptionally, where the viewer walks a trail and becomes the camera -- the piece "unfolds like a movie set in space" -- and the plants are given a voice and become "an unpredictable choir", loosely inspired by the myth of the Great Flood. Its white parallelepiped, reflected in the pond with a tiny infinity symbol at its centre, is the first of his pure geometric intruders.

And then the subject becomes the internet. #DEFILE (2013, Prague) hangs a giant browser against the Hybernia Opera House facade: overlapping pastel panels holding black sculptural forms -- a faucet, a wireframe chair, a dark organic blob -- a digital-voice preface, then a visual essay in which pop-up windows materialise and fade, a Google image search for "sculpture" returning the Venus de Milo, the bust of Julius Caesar, and a series of faucets side by side. The text cites Camille Henrot and Bertrand Lavier (explicitly Lavier's Disney Sculptures) and Richard Dawkins's "meme", and argues three things: the browser is a private, intimate object transplanted into public physical space; images, emojis and memes are becoming a post-verbal language; and the apparent logic of feeds and recommendations gives us "the illusion of understanding our surroundings". The action unfolds before the audience without their interaction, "as if watching a film where popup windows materialize and fade". Tardy left AntiVJ at the end of that same year.

## M1 -- The building as screen, the screen replaced by a built volume

The founding gesture, and the one that never leaves the practice: never project on a flat screen when a volume, a facade, an organ, a cube or a dome will do. Light Sculpture (2008) builds the volume itself -- angular planes and blocks whose only purpose is to catch light. Square (2009) is the same move as a freestanding object in a public park: a large glass-and-metal cube, glowing saturated orange-red, smoke rising from its roof, turning organic, "reminiscent of a cocoon", through light, shadow and colour -- the first break from the monochrome palette, orange-red throughout. Grote Kerk (2009) takes an existing volume and refuses to overwrite it: the projection on the baroque organ is restrained, working with the ornament, while the organ itself is played live -- the screen is also the instrument. The mechanism is a refusal with a method: the surface is chosen first, and the image is fitted to what the surface already is -- its geometry, its history, its function. The 2010 process, described first-hand in the Swedish VJ Union interview, is the method stated plainly: "the building and its surroundings are always the starting point... We didn't have any pre-produced content before we saw the site for the first time. We created everything from scratch, after we saw the place." For the Norrkoping Visualiseringscenter C project that meant three months from first discussion to result: a site visit in March, two days on site for mapping at the end of April, three weeks of content work at home, four days back on site for final tests; a custom audio-reactive vector-graphics tool built by Simon Geilfus, plus After Effects, Illustrator and Cinema 4D; two Barco XLM HD30 projectors at 30K lumens, 1080p, Dataton Watchout for sync; a storyboard as the shared document and Skype check-ins every two days while working remotely. Those are 2010 specifics and are not presented as his current toolchain (C2714).

**Takeaway:** the surface is the first decision and the image is the second; a piece that starts from content and then looks for a wall has the order backwards.

## M2 -- Site-first: the scenario is written from the place, not laid over it

M1 is the refusal; M2 is the positive method. "I started to write a 'scenario', using the notion of labour as the central point of the story" -- Tardy on the Norrkoping project, where the scenario came from the site, a visualisation centre, not from a library of effects. Desherence writes its scenario from the swamp under Enghien-les-Bains and from the legal word for abandoned property. Pagan (2012, Toulouse) writes its scenario from the museum's own holding: a 3rd-century bacchanalian head, a pagan idol found in the late 17th century, abstracted into a white wireframe polyhedron on the facade, integrated into a line network until it is almost imperceptible -- "polymorphism", patterns from abstract constellations to enigmatic symbols emerging through light. The Drift (2018, Rome) writes its scenario from the Temple of Hadrian and from Europe's migration crisis: projections on the facade plus 28 light modules adorned with flags making black-and-white patterns from graphic elements of European countries' flags, a near-future world of sudden continental drift as a metaphor for social and hospitality crises. The mechanism is always the same: visit first, read the place, write a story the place could have told, and only then make images. The story is the constraint that keeps the mapping from becoming decoration.

**Takeaway:** a mapping without a scenario is wallpaper; the scenario is what the site gives you, and it has to be written after the visit, never before.

## M3 -- The white geometric intruder: a foreign body, left in on purpose

From The Ark (2013) onward, Tardy keeps placing one immaculate geometric object inside a living or historical setting and refusing to blend it in. The Ark's white parallelepiped floats in the pond among the cacti, reflected, with a tiny infinity symbol at its centre -- "an immaculate white parallelepiped structure" in a garden at night. Future Ruins (2015, Lausanne) scatters roof elements, portals and bay windows "reminiscent of life-sized architectural fragments" across the museum's garden as glowing white-violet LED line sculptures -- "these neo-classical and newly created elements, though in ruins, illuminated the darkness of the night, symbolizing the enduring remnants of our world regardless of time". Future Ruins Act II (2016 and on) subtracts the projection entirely and leaves only the line: LED frames scattered on the floor of a raw concrete submarine base, cables and a power box visible, "evoking the essence of tangible line drawings". The point, stated in The Ark's own text, is explicitly not symbiosis but "a peculiar encounter" between nature and technology, where the technical means are forgotten and only the confrontation shows. The intruder is the argument made visible: the digital does not belong here, and that is exactly why it is here.

**Takeaway:** one clean foreign object, honestly foreign, says more about technology in a place than a whole facade of blended imagery.

## M4 -- The scale abolition: one pattern at two sizes

Paleodictyon (2012, Centre Pompidou-Metz) is the clearest statement of the AntiVJ habit of letting the building's own geometry choose the image, and it is the one work in the catalogue where Tardy is a contributor rather than a lead -- artistic direction by Simon Geilfus, Yannick Jacquet and Thomas Vaquie; visual content by Geilfus, Jacquet and Tardy; music by Vaquie (C2757, C2820). The museum's roof is a sweeping white woven shell by Shigeru Ban; the projection covers it in a dense black-and-white hexagonal cellular pattern -- the markings of paleodictyon nodosum, a deep-sea creature known only from the hexagonal structures it leaves behind. The concept, loosely informed by deep-sea expert Peter A. Rona, "abolishes notions of scale by contrasting micro-architecture with human construction": the bacteria-scale hexagon rhymes with the museum's hexagonal frame. A digital counterpart, the Paleodictyon App, was released alongside the physical installation -- the app, not the building, is the interactive element. The mechanism is a rhyme: find the pattern the building already carries, find the same pattern at a wildly different scale in nature, and let the two sizes collapse into one image. It is the most economical idea in the catalogue -- one shape, twice -- and the one that most directly shows what mapping can do that a screen cannot: make a building recognise itself in a microbe.

**Takeaway:** the cheapest strong image in mapping is a pattern the building already has, found again at another scale.

## M5 -- The private browser made public: our searches, at building scale

#DEFILE (2013) hangs the browser on the facade; The Great Indecision Council (2018) removes the facade and keeps the browser's logic. The Council is a 25-metre circle of 18 modules emitting light and sound, displaying in real time the most frequently searched words from Google Search and Google News -- in the language of the country where the piece stands. The words appear as light pulses, accompanied by computer-generated mystical chants, visible and audible both inside and outside the circle. The framing, in the work's own text: "an unsolicited self-portrait of our current network society", "an image that shows humanity as it really is, not how it wants to be seen". At Lyon the ring encircled the statue of Sergent Blandan in Place Sathonay, "perplexed by the Sibylline conversation of this odd grouping". The piece is never the same twice because the data is live, not a loop; it toured from Vlieland (September 2018) to GLOW Eindhoven (2018), STRP (March-April 2019), Lyon (year unpinned on the Lyon page -- C2760), and Noor Riyadh 2024. The mechanism is the transplant: take a private, intimate act -- typing a search alone -- and stage it in public, at a scale where a crowd has to stand inside it to read it. The critique is not in the words themselves, which are whatever we searched today; it is in the staging, which makes the private act unavoidably shared.

**Takeaway:** the strongest data work does not visualise the data; it stages the act of producing it, in public, at a scale you cannot ignore.

## M6 -- The ruin as image: what remains of the digital era

Future Ruins asks the question -- what will remain of the digital era? -- and In Vivam Memoriam (2019, Metz) answers it as a liturgy. Staged in the Temple Neuf over a summer, it is a ceremony for the end of the digital era: four custom-made programmable LED structures, lasers, eight analog lightboxes holding still-life photographs made for the project, a 12-channel soundtrack by Laurent Delforge accompanying a poem by Maud Marique, "both audible and visually represented". The inspected image looks down the dark nave: two huge glowing white LED starburst emblems hang high on either side; black banners below carry internet icons -- a Wi-Fi symbol, a globe, a link chain, a map pin, a cursor arrow; small still-life lightboxes line the pews; thin vertical light bars stand at the altar end. The tone is "contemplation, bewilderment, and a hint of feverish anticipation". The LED structures from Future Ruins are now religious emblems; the internet's icons are hung like heraldic banners. The mechanism is the funeral as a form: take the objects of the present, stage their ending with the full apparatus of ceremony, and let the audience feel the loss of something that has not ended yet. It is the most theatrical late work, and the clearest bridge to Brain Dance (2024), where the same satirical instinct turns to puppetry.

**Takeaway:** to criticise a present, stage its funeral; ceremony makes the audience feel what an essay only argues.

## M7 -- The audience as camera: walking is the editing

The Ark's text says it plainly: the piece "unfolds like a movie set in space" and the viewer walks a trail and becomes the camera. Three parts along a walking trail in the Oaxaca ethnobotanical garden; the cacti are at times independent characters and at times "weaving together the whole visual mass by interlacing their own strands of light"; the 8-channel soundscape by Squeaky Lobster moves with you. There are no buttons and no sensors -- just walking. The Great Indecision Council uses the same mechanism in a purer form: the audience stands inside the ring and is watched by spectators outside it; the interaction is positional, inside versus outside, and the piece reads differently from each side. Future Ruins Act II makes the audience's flow through the room part of the composition: "the spotlight shifts to the surrounding space -- from the materiality of the exhibition room to the flow of the audience within the installation". Captive (2022, Marseille) is the intimate extreme of the same device: sleeping pods -- the chambers used in capsule hotels -- into which the audience is invited to enter, one person at a time, for a face-to-face encounter with an audiovisual setup "both close and eerily absent", framed against nightlife as high-energy collective activity, with rest as "a form of quiet defiance" against the pressure for productive use of time. The mechanism is a single rule: the spectator's body is the only input device, and the piece is edited by where that body goes.

**Takeaway:** if the audience has to move to see the work, the movement is the work; design the path before the image.

## M8 -- The satire of air: the machine as puppet, the wind as animator

The late turn is the furthest point from the early minimalism, and it is a return to the smart-city critique of Songdo with faces attached. MGNTRN (2014, Puebla) is the maximalist hinge: a Ferris wheel wrapped in projection, laser-cut structures as projection canvases, LEDs, and a small robot operating the LEDs from the wheel, the references 1970s psychedelic sci-fi (Sun Ra's "Space is the Place") and a 1974 Gottlieb space-themed pinball machine -- starfields, black holes, kawaii characters, retrofuturistic spaceships. Galaxy Express (2021/2022, Brussels) dresses a genuine M4 locomotive in the visual excess of Japanese dekotora -- extravagantly customised cargo trucks -- white LED strips tracing every edge, windows glowing with dot patterns, "an expansive playground of sensory exploration", the "inner child" as a new register. Brain Dance (2024, Lyon) drops projection, building and screen entirely: eight customised "air dancer" puppets, inflatable figures 6 to 8 metres tall undulating in the wind, each stylising one of the emblematic leaders of the world's largest tech companies, including the GAFAM giants, in two rows on platforms, framed by a lighting and sound system that evolves with an energetic score by Ofer Smilansky. The tone is humorous and surreal, but the critique is sharp: "by reducing them to animated puppets, Brain Dance suggests that, just like their inflatable effigies, these powerful figures are subject to the unpredictable forces of history and society". The puppets move by wind and air, not by motors or sensors -- the weather is the animator, and the individual puppet identities are not named in the text (C2766). The "digital sculptor" ends up sculpting air.

**Takeaway:** when the subject is power, do not project it -- inflate it, and let the wind do the mocking.

## The practice now: light, data, and air

Put the four turns together and the career reads as a single subtraction. 2008-2009: the work is collective, live or nightly, monochrome, and the screen is the problem the building solves. 2011-2013: the image becomes figurative and drawn, the references become art-historical, the subject becomes the internet, and the viewer becomes a camera. 2014-2016: projection recedes, built objects take over, the ruin becomes the central image, and Hand Coded becomes the standing technical partner. 2018-2024: the facade returns but the subject is political and the medium is data, light or air rather than video -- migration flags in Rome, live search terms in a ring you stand inside, a funeral for the digital era in a church, a train dressed as a toy, a capsule pod for one resting body, inflatable tech CEOs dancing in the wind. The through-line never changes: the work is always in situ, always at night, always bigger than a screen, and the audience always has to be there in the dark to get it. The one flat work in the catalogue, [AURA] (2013, ongoing), names the tension the whole practice lives in: prints of a noise terrain -- a black jagged mountain range floating on pale grey-white -- quoting Walter Benjamin at length on the aura as "the unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be" and its decay under reproduction. "[AURA] is organic. [AURA] is mineral. [AURA] is synthetic. [AURA] is made of pigments. [AURA] is made of noise. [AURA] is unique. [AURA] is multiple." The mountain-as-noise image is also the most Lemercier-like object Tardy ever made -- a plotted terrain, but printed, not projected.

The sound half of the practice is a duet. Thomas Vaquie, the AntiVJ composer since 2008, scores the monumental works -- Omicron above all, where orchestral scale meets electronic texture to simulate the aging of concrete. Laurent Delforge, as Squeaky Lobster, scores the narrative and late works -- Desherence, The Ark's 8-channel soundscape, MGNTRN, The Great Indecision Council, The Drift, In Vivam Memoriam -- spatial sound for site-specific installation, also recording as Before Tigers. The two composers are the two registers of the practice: Vaquie for the building as instrument, Delforge for the story as sound. The coder in the network, Simon Geilfus (joined AntiVJ 2009), built the tools -- the custom audio-reactive vector-graphics tool for the 2010 Norrkoping show, the audio visualisation tool that turned Vaquie's Ecume waveforms into topographical artwork -- and co-directed Paleodictyon. The producer, Nicolas Boritch, managed the label from 2010 and carries the production credits on Songdo, Omicron, Le Bestiaire, The Ark, Onion Skin and Ecume; he is a manager, not a maker, and is not queued as a study subject.

## Aesthetic logic: the minimal line, the drawn image, the inflated joke

Three aesthetics, one career, in sequence -- and the sequence is the logic. The first aesthetic is the minimal line: white geometry on black, red planes, sepia clockwork, the Sol LeWitt and technical-drawing lineage Tardy names himself for Omicron -- "What's projected onto the dome could also be seen as some kind of technical drawing in process, maybe drawn by the building itself" -- with sci-fi (Metropolis, Archigram) as the other parent. The image is restrained because the building is doing the work; the projection's job is to reveal the architecture, not to cover it. The second aesthetic is the drawn image: Bosch and Belkis Ayon in Le Bestiaire, Lavier and Henrot in #DEFILE, Benjamin in [AURA] -- collage, silhouette, pastel browser panels, the hand returning to a practice that had been pure geometry. The image is figurative because the subject is now cultural (the internet, the archive, the meme) rather than architectural, and a figurative subject needs a drawn image. The third aesthetic is the inflated joke: dekotora excess on a locomotive, air dancers as tech CEOs -- colour, caricature, wind. The image is ridiculous because the subject is power, and power mocked politely is not mocked at all. What holds the three together is the refusal underneath them all: no flat screen, no pre-made content, no image that could have been shown anywhere else. Every piece is site-first, night-first, and bigger than the rectangle it refuses.
