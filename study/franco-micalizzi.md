# Franco Micalizzi: Study Notes

**Site:** Franco Micalizzi (b. Thursday 21 December 1939, Rome, Italy; living), Italian composer, arranger and conductor, best known for his poliziotteschi scores -- the Roman session keyboardist who whistled his way into the spaghetti western, wrote the tune that made Trinity a hit, then spent the seventies giving the Italian crime film its sound: one gripping theme, a big brass section, a funk pulse and a folk graft, varied fifteen ways across a single film. His practice is the theme, the variation, and the afterlife: the whistle that identifies a hero in two bars, the one melody that carries a whole score, and the cue that outlives its film by fifty years to close a Tarantino picture, to run a television stare, and to be played back live by his own eighteen-piece band. [C29944]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Claudio Simonetti study: Simonetti ends his study on a stage in Nashville, playing Tenebre live to the film it came from, the catalogue become repertoire -- and the man who shares his publisher's office, his label's shelf, and his afterlife as a needle-drop is the Roman on the other side of the Cinevox room, the composer who co-wrote two songs with Franco Bixio himself. Where Simonetti brings the deadline, the theme and the replay, Micalizzi brings the thing that had to be replayed: a whistle that still means Trinity, a brass crescendo that still means a Roman car chase, a theme so portable that Tarantino lifted it whole twice and a sitcom slowed it down to mean a stare. The study starts where his leaves off -- with the other half of the Italian genre shelf, the poliziottesco composer, the man who wrote eighty-odd scores and then spent forty years conducting them back to the audiences that bought them. Franco Micalizzi was born in Rome on Thursday 21 December 1939, where he did his musical studies, learning piano and guitar young. He began playing piano in an amateur group and was discovered by Robby Poitevin, who took him as keyboardist into his band I Robby's -- eight singles in 1959-1961, backing other artists (Emilio Pericoli, Ornella Vanoni) -- and he left the band after some years to compose film music. The guitar is his native instrument; the whistle and the big band come later, and the keyboardist's ear for what a band can do in a room never leaves the scores. [C29948]

His first film score was Il ragazzo che sapeva amare in 1967 (Enzo Dell'Aquila), followed by Addio mamma the same year -- and the study has to say so plainly, because two of its own sources get this wrong: both the Italian Wikipedia biography and the Tacco di Bacco bio call They Call Me Trinity (1970) his first soundtrack, contradicting their own filmographies, which list the 1967-1969 credits. The correct statement is "first success", not "first soundtrack". The English Wikipedia selected filmography starts in 1968 and omits the 1967 titles altogether; the study uses the Italian filmography for the early years. [C29949]

The apprenticeship is audible in The Forgotten Pistolero (Il pistolero dell'Ave Maria, 1969, Ferdinando Baldi), scored with Roberto Pregadio -- Pregadio credited first by the specialist press, Micalizzi's share real but secondary. The score is a dark-toned spaghetti-western built on a whistled main theme in two parts: a bell-clanging whistled intro, then a slow, sentimental, almost Germanic lounge melody, an orchestral reworking of the cowboy song "Home on the Range" with whistling and a horn section, plus fuzz guitar, rippling timpani, bell hits, solo whistle recaps, female chorals, a gentle variation ("Ritorno a casa") with soft strings and solo electric guitar, solo Spanish guitar with a galloping rhythm, and one overtly dramatic cue ("Fiato sospeso", muted brass, timid oboe, harpsichord). The spaghetti-western database calls it one of the best, if not the best, non-Morricone score for any spaghetti western, and notes the whistling theme was often used in commercials. This is the workshop where Micalizzi learned the whistle. [C29950]

Then the breakthrough. They Call Me Trinity (Lo chiamavano Trinita, 1970, Enzo Barboni as E. B. Clucher, producer Italo Zingarelli, a friend of Micalizzi) -- the main title song "Trinity: Titoli", a brisk, whistled, guitar-driven march, sung by the Italian-Australian Annibale Giannarelli with I Cantori Moderni di Alessandroni, Alessandro Alessandroni himself whistling, English lyrics by Lally Stott, orchestra directed by Gianfranco Plenizio. The composition story, per Spencer Hill Festival, is a tempo fight: Micalizzi wrote the lively theme straight after reading the script; at the audition Zingarelli rejected it and wanted a slower, broader lullaby; Micalizzi wrote the lullaby; it proved too slow once the film was cut -- the editor complained the two heroes would almost have to move in slow motion to fit the music; at a new meeting Barboni backed the original lively piece and it was accepted. The film, released 22 December 1970 in Italy, was one of the biggest box-office hits in Italian cinema history and fixed the Spencer/Hill buddy-comedy formula; Italian Wikipedia says its success decreed Micalizzi's entry among the most important Italian soundtrack composers. The album, first released January 1971 on Carosello, also carries one track co-composed with Guido and Maurizio De Angelis, "In Due Contro Tutti" -- a single weak source (a2zsoundtrack.com), uncorroborated by the label listings, and the study flags it as such. And the filmography error travels with the film: English Wikipedia's Micalizzi table names Gianfranco Plenizio as director of They Call Me Trinity. Plenizio conducted; Barboni directed. [C29951]

The seventies then run in two opposite registers at once. The Last Snows of Spring (L'ultima neve di primavera, 1973, Raimondo Del Balzo) is straight orchestral sentiment -- a tearjerker about a boy who loses his mother, romantic, melancholic and dramatic themes alternating with happy, danceable, lounge and Latino cues, soloists including Oscar Valdambrini on flugelhorn, vocals by Irio De Paula, Giovanna Marinuzzi and Edda Dell'Orso -- and its main theme reached the top of the Italian hit parade, the film dated 1973 and the chart success 1974 (the Italian biography says 1974 for the film; both filmographies say 1973; the study dates the film 1973 and the hit 1974). The same years give the opposite: Beyond the Door (Chi sei?, 1974, Ovidio Assonitis), a funk-and-disco horror score recorded in the United States with American session musicians, "quite unheard of in horror" -- soulful lead vocals, prominent sax and synthesiser, superb percussion, a sound reminiscent of a blaxploitation film, the film opening on a recording-studio montage of a jazz-funk combo laying down "Bargain with the Devil" while the producer-character yells "cut" and the montage cuts too, songs that taunt the characters directly ("No one will help you"), a nose-flute among the flourishes. Same two years, opposite registers -- and Istantanea per un delitto (1975, Mario Imperoli as Arthur Saxon) shows a third: recycling, Bixio's 1971 Motowns songs re-cut as a thriller score with new psychedelic and obsessive cues filling the gaps. [C29952]

The poliziottesco peak is 1975-1977, and 1976 alone holds four scores. Syndicate Sadists (Il giustiziere sfida la citta, 1975, Umberto Lenzi) states the toolkit in words: tight hard-funk rhythms, wah-wah guitars, woodwinds in great relief, piano in the medium-low register, clavinets, music that adheres to the narrative while adding dynamic and lyrical value. Then Roma a mano armata (1976, Lenzi), a series of variations on a main theme mixing surfy, Bond-inspired guitar, funky clav and big rich brass horns, a fat trumpet line branching off into fragments with funky breaks, Herrmannesque tense strings, infectious basslines -- Death Waltz hears David Shire's The Taking of Pelham One Two Three filtered through the crazed Italian house style. Then Napoli violenta (1976, Lenzi), the most documented score of his career: one theme, fifteen cues. Then Italia a mano armata (1976, Marino Girolami as Franco Martinelli), wind sections driven into an irresistible seventies funk crescendo, spacey grooves, jazz atmospheres, timeless brass crescendos. Genova a mano armata (1976, Mario Lanfranchi) is the one he calls his favourite, a theme he still performs live with his orchestra. The cycle closes with Il cinico, l'infame, il violento (1977, Lenzi) and La banda del gobbo (1977, Lenzi -- released 18 August 1977; several listings, including Wikipedia's Micalizzi filmography, say 1978), where a Moog synthesiser states the recurring main theme first and an energetic woodwind section develops it, reprised in slow and fast versions. The dates wobble in the sources and the study trusts the filmographies: Roma a mano armata is 1976, not 1975 as Spencer Hill's prose says; La banda del gobbo is 1977, not 1978. [C29953]

The network around the peak is directors, and the study keeps the names straight because the brief got three of them wrong. The deepest tie is Umberto Lenzi -- eleven films, 1975-1992, from Syndicate Sadists to Mean Tricks, including The Greatest Battle (1978), Scusi lei e normale? (1979), From Corleone to Brooklyn (1979), Black Demons (1991) -- and two Lenzi films that are NOT Micalizzi's, verified against: Free Hand for a Tough Cop (1976) is Bruno Canfora's, Almost Human (1974) is Ennio Morricone's. The Trinity films were directed by Enzo Barboni, not by any Corbucci -- They Call Me Trinity (1970), Odd Squad (1982), Go for It (1983), Double Trouble (1984) -- and the Corbucci Micalizzi scored for was Bruno Corbucci, Sergio's younger brother: Crime at Porta Romana (1980), Il ficcanaso (1981), Crime on the Highway (1982), Rimini Rimini - A Year Later (1988), the Nico Giraldi series, whose musical voice Micalizzi was from the start. No Micalizzi score for Sergio Corbucci was found, and no Micalizzi score for Enzo G. Castellari either -- Castellari's poliziottesco scores were by the De Angelis brothers (High Crime, Street Law, The Big Racket) -- nor for Bruno Mattei, whose The Other Hell (1981) carries unreleased Goblin stock music obtained via the publisher Carlo Bixio. The study does not assert any of the three. [C29954]

Two other brief anchors are not his either, and the study drops them rather than repeating them. Street Law (Il cittadino si ribella, 1974, Castellari) is scored by Guido and Maurizio De Angelis -- three themes, the title track, "Goodbye My Friend" in nine versions, "Drivin' All Around". Trinity Is Still My Name (1971, Barboni) is scored by the De Angelis brothers too; Micalizzi scored only the first Trinity, and the sequel -- the highest-grossing Italian film to that point -- handed the music chair to the brothers, who continued the whistling-comedy template he set. And My Name Is Thomas (2018, Terence Hill) is scored by Pino Donaggio; Micalizzi's role, on the Spencer Hill Festival page alone, is the title song "The World Under My Shoes", a variation on the Trinity whistle with the original whistler, Alessandro Alessandroni, then 91, on the recording. The song credit is single-source and the study says so. [C29955]

The late seventies export the toolkit out of the city. Stridulum, released as The Visitor (1979, Giulio Paradisi), was recorded in New York in September 1978: a disco-orchestral spacemusic score, the big-band crime blasts replaced by a full orchestra and a small rock combo, an opening brass fanfare, a "Sadness Theme" in multiple variations, the sci-fi disco-funk of "Jerzy Again", and -- omitted from the 2023 expanded CD, to reviewers' regret -- nearly three minutes of electronic and Mellotron voices for the intergalactic confrontations, nerve-jangling tonalities against the dance funk. The Last Hunter (L'ultimo cacciatore, 1980, Antonio Margheriti) drops the same funk apparatus into a Vietnam jungle film made to capitalise on Apocalypse Now and The Deer Hunter. From Corleone to Brooklyn (1979, Lenzi) is called the swan song of the Italian cop movie, a nostalgic main theme plus a second pop-flavoured motif, each reprised in fast and slow orchestral versions. Then the Barboni reunion softens everything back into title songs and Latin breeziness -- Go for It (1983), Double Trouble (1984), "What's Going On in Brazil?" -- the composer returns to where he started, the sung theme, but now as light comedy. [C29956]

The eighties also hold the television work and the family turn. He was musical director of the first thirteen episodes of the third edition of Domenica in (RAI variety, hosted by Corrado, 1978-79) and returned to the show in 1986 under Elisabetta Gardini. In 1982 he wrote the Lupin III theme, accordion, sung by Irene Vioni with Orchestra Castellina-Pasi. In 1984 he founded the orchestra The Micalizzi Family with his sons Cristiano and Alessandro. RAI fiction followed -- Albert e l'uomo nero, the hundred episodes of Passioni (Rai 1), the two-part Un figlio a meta (Rai 2, Giorgio Capitani, with Gigi Proietti). Late horror came too, thinly sourced: The Curse (1987, David Keith) and Black Demons (1991, Lenzi), no score detail found this run, and the study invents none. [C29957]

The nineties and two-thousands are institutions and the orchestra. In October 1991, with Franco Migliacci and Domenico Modugno, he founded the Sindacato Nazionale Autori e Compositori (SNAC); he is now its president -- and the study repeats the warning: Migliacci the lyricist died 15 September 2023 and must not be confused with Micalizzi. In 2003 he was elected to the board of SIAE and later became advisor to the SIAE president. In 2005 he formed the eighteen-piece Big Bubbling Band to play his soundtracks in concert, above all the 1970s-80s poliziotteschi themes -- first concerts at Casa del Jazz in Rome on 24 October 2005, then Florence, Rolling Stone in Milan on 30 May 2006, Teatro Palladium in Rome on 20 December 2006. No roster of the band's players was found anywhere this run; the study names no lineup. The 2006 album Cinema a mano armata re-records the crime themes with the band, and the project Gli Originali the same year puts his themes beside Italian hip-hop artists. In 2012 he conducted the orchestra for the Canale 5 show Panariello non esiste, and the album Veleno came out with the Big Bubbling Band, widely covered in the Italian press. [C29958]

The twenty-tens are late albums, books, and the Hill reunion. November 2013 brought the album Miele with the Big Bubbling Band and the autobiography C'est la vie d'artiste. October 2014 brought Ondanuova 1, with international jazz guests Jeff Lorber, Eric Marienthal, Jimmy Haslip and Fabrizio Bosso, his son Cristiano on drums and as producer. 2015 brought Celebrities, seventeen musical portraits -- sixteen figures plus a self-portrait, from Picasso and Stevie Wonder to Marilyn Monroe, Quentin Tarantino, Federico Fellini, Toto and Ray Charles. 2017 brought the second book, Le chiamavano colonne sonore. 2018 brought the Terence Hill reunion, the title song for My Name Is Thomas (single-source, see above). 2019 brought the album Calibro 70's, released 21 June 2019, and the concert at Parco Schuster in Rome on 17 July 2019 with the Big Bubbling Band, Umberto Lenzi's daughter Alessandra in the front row -- the last fully dated concert found -- plus a Spencer Hill Festival appearance in Lommatzsch, Germany, the same year. The present tense is release activity, and it is current. The reissue wave runs through 2024-2026: Beat Records remastered Italia a mano armata (15 March 2024) and Napoli violenta (29 March 2024); the Trinity score was remastered from six rediscovered 24-track master tapes for a 2021 deluxe edition, repressed in 2025 with unreleased material, including a deguello version with trumpet instead of flugelhorn and unreleased takes with Franco De Gemini's harmonica prominent; Four Flies reissued Chi sei? in 2018 on a 500-copy LP with two tracks never before on vinyl. Holy Basil Records issued Non c'e due senza quattro as an expanded LP (HBR 032LP) in May 2026 -- Forced Exposure gives 22 May, deejay.de an expected 29 May -- with previously unreleased material, an eight-page booklet with liner notes by Fabio Babini and a personal contribution from Micalizzi himself. Then two newer releases, found fresh this run: the 15-track album Io, proprio io... (New Team Music, published 22 October 2026), new original songs with his son Cristiano on percussion and programming, featured vocalists Cristiana Polegri and Cristiano Turrini, lyricist Carla Vistarini -- whether the tracks were newly recorded in 2026 or assembled from earlier sessions is not established by the Qobuz metadata alone -- and Violence!, a clear-vinyl reworking of the Il cinico score (Cometa Edizioni, 30 October 2026). No dated 2025-2026 performance, interview or public appearance was found; the most recent video interview is "Jungle Boogie" (2023). He is 86, turning 87 on Monday 21 December 2026. [C29960]

The afterlife is the longest phase of the career, and it is the one the study is really about. Tarantino used the Italia a mano armata main theme over the climactic car chase in Death Proof (2007) -- the duelling Dodge Charger and Dodge Challenger jumping a dirt ramp -- and the Trinity theme as the closing theme of Django Unchained (2012). The library cue "The Puzzle" is used in Curb Your Enthusiasm, and a slow-tempo excerpt rearranged for clarinet and bassoon is often used in the series as the "stare" theme, at varying speeds, revived as late as season 12, episode 4, "Disgruntled" (2024). The Big Bubbling Band re-performs the crime themes live; the Genova a mano armata main theme is a live staple. The work's second life is longer than its first: the themes now circulate as samples, needle-drops, live-band standards and collector vinyl, detached from the films that commissioned them. No new film score of note after 1984 appears in the sources found, and the study does not invent one. [C29961]

The arc is a single line with two hinges and no reversal: the session keyboardist of the late fifties, the western whistler of 1969-70, the melodrama and horror-funk man of 1973-75, the poliziottesco composer of 1975-77 who wrote four scores in one year, the genre exporter of 1979-84, and then the conductor of his own past -- the family band, the eighteen-piece orchestra, the reissues from rediscovered tapes, the themes lifted by other directors into other decades. The place is never decoration: Rome, the recording studio, the Cinevox offices where the young Frizzi met the young Simonetti and where Bixio's songs waited four years to become a thriller, the Parco Schuster stage, and now the record shop, where a 1970 whistle still sells. ## M1 -- The signature whistle as a portable identity [C29963]

A short, whistled melodic line -- usually over a marching or galloping guitar figure -- that identifies a character or a film in a couple of bars, and that survives being lifted out of its film entirely. The Forgotten Pistolero's whistled theme was often used in commercials; the Trinity title song is whistled by Alessandroni and sung by Giannarelli over I Cantori Moderni; Tarantino lifts the Trinity whistle whole as the Django Unchained closing theme; and in 2018 Micalizzi writes a variation of the same whistle for the Thomas title song and re-hires the 91-year-old Alessandroni to whistle it. The mechanism is portability: the line is thin enough to travel -- one voice, one guitar, one march -- and strong enough that wherever it lands, the film it came from lands with it. [C29964]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one thin line over a busy field -- a whistle, a march, a guitar -- and the line should be quotable, so that when it is dropped into a new scene it still reads as the same identity. [C29965]

## M2 -- One theme, fifteen variations: the variation engine [C29966]

A whole score built from a single main theme, restated in fast, slow, loud, soft, solo and tutti versions across the film, so the audience learns one melody and hears it change clothes. Napoli violenta is the proof, fifteen named cues carrying the "Folk and Violence" melody or its rhythmic intro -- "Welcome to Napoli", "The Violence Face", "Naples' Alley", "The Garage of the Giant", "Rush for a Sign", "Betti's Investigation", "The Chase Over the Roofs", "Ten Minutes to One", "Get Down the Train", "Targeting a Killer", "The Price for a Fight", "Looking for Capuano", "Thus Ended the General", "The Price Is Too High" -- and the same construction runs through Roma a mano armata ("a series of variations on a main theme"), La banda del gobbo (the Moog theme reprised in slow and fast versions), Genova a mano armata (the main theme reprised with different arrangements throughout), and Da Corleone a Brooklyn (two themes, each reprised in fast and slow orchestral versions). The mechanism is sameness-through-change: one shape, new tempo, new instrument, new mood, and the viewer tracks the shape, not the arrangement. [C29967]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be one shape that recurs and mutates -- same contour, new tempo, new instrument, new mood -- so the viewer watches sameness change clothes and learns to recognise the theme by its outline alone. [C29968]

## M3 -- The brass-crescendo funk attack [C29969]

A wind and brass section driven into a seventies funk crescendo -- a fat trumpet line that fragments into breaks over a hard funk pulse, surfy guitar and clavinet underneath, electric bass pushing forward. Italia a mano armata taps "the wind sections into an irresistible seventies funk crescendo"; Roma a mano armata runs "a fat trumpet line that branches off into fragments with some incredibly funky breaks" over "the most infectious basslines"; Syndicate Sadists runs "tight hard-funk rhythms, guitars effected with wha-wha, woodwinds in great relief". The mechanism is invasion: a calm field is entered by a brass line that gets louder, faster and more fragmented until it breaks apart into rhythmic pieces, and then the pulse takes over. The cue is built to be listened to apart from the film -- and it was: 45s, LPs, and now a fifty-year reissue tail. [C29970]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a calm field invaded by brass -- one trumpet line that grows louder and more fragmented until it breaks into pieces, and the pulse underneath taking over when the melody lets go. [C29971]

## M4 -- The folk graft: tarantella inside the funk [C29972]

A local folk idiom welded onto the funk apparatus so the score sounds like a specific place, not generic crime music. Napoli violenta's "Folk & Violence" is "a superb blend of Funk, tarantella and Neapolitan folklore", the main theme carrying "some touches of traditional Italian folk music", and the film also carries a Neapolitan lounge song, "Tira a rezza o piscatore", beside the pop-rock ballad "A Man Before Your Time". The mechanism is the graft: a folk pattern -- a dance rhythm, a street-song contour -- appears inside a modern groove, neither swallowing the other, and the two idioms trade the lead. The place is in the rhythm, not in the scenery. [C29973]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be two idioms in one groove -- a folk dance inside a funk pulse, trading the lead -- so the place is audible before anything is shown. [C29974]

## M5 -- The diegetic song that runs the scene [C29975]

A song that is not background but an actor in the scene -- performed on screen, or written so its lyrics comment on and taunt the characters, so the music drives the action instead of decorating it. Beyond the Door opens inside a recording studio while the combo lays down "Bargain with the Devil", and the cut lands when the producer-character yells "cut"; its songs taunt the characters directly rather than sitting under the action. The Trinity title song IS the film's identity and its marketing. In Napoli violenta the lounge song and the ballad are set-pieces inside the score, not underscore. The mechanism is the performance as the scene's engine: the song begins, the scene reorganises itself around the performers, and the song's words or rhythm dictate what happens next. [C29976]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a performance that runs the scene -- the song starts, the room reorganises around it, and the words or the rhythm decide what happens next. [C29977]

## M6 -- Register switching: the genre-swap [C29978]

The score changes idiom abruptly -- jazz to funk to disco to dread -- inside one film, so the audience is kept off balance and the film's tone flips with the music. Beyond the Door is "apt to switch up from sizzling jazz to heavy funk to bizarre flourishes like the aforementioned nose-flute at the drop of a hat"; Stridulum sets disco-funk ("Jerzy Again") against electronic and Mellotron voices and nerve-jangling tonalities for the intergalactic confrontations; the lacrima scores alternate romantic, melancholic, dramatic, lounge and Latino cues in one sitting. The mechanism is the jump: the visual field holds steady while the sound -- and with it the mood -- changes register without warning, and the same image reads as comedy, then menace. [C29979]

**Takeaway:** the piece should hold one image steady while the music jumps register without warning -- jazz, then funk, then dread -- so the same scene reads as comedy and then as menace. [C29980]

## M7 -- Tempo as drama: the too-slow theme [C29981]

The tempo of the theme literally sets the speed of the bodies on screen -- too slow and the heroes look as if they are moving in slow motion; right and the comedy lands. The Trinity audition story states it by the participants themselves: Zingarelli's lullaby was too slow once the film was cut, and the editor complained the two heroes would almost have to move in slow motion to make it fit the music. The mechanism is tempo as a physical constraint on movement: the music does not accompany the bodies, it paces them. [C29982]

**Takeaway:** the piece should have one tempo control that visibly speeds or slows the figures -- at the wrong tempo the action drags and stretches and goes comic, at the right tempo it lands. [C29983]

## M8 -- The slow re-read: the afterlife reuse [C29984]

An old cue, slowed down and re-instrumented, acquires a completely new function decades later -- menace becomes comedy, or a theme becomes a running gag. "The Puzzle", a library cue, is slowed to a clarinet-and-bassoon excerpt and used as Curb Your Enthusiasm's "stare" theme, at varying speeds, for over twenty years, revived in season 12 (2024); Tarantino's needle-drops lift 1970s cues into 2007 and 2012 films where they read as fresh; the Big Bubbling Band re-performs the crime themes live, and the 2025-2026 reissues remaster them from rediscovered tapes. The mechanism is the re-read: a familiar motif returns at half speed in a new colour, and the whole scene re-reads itself around it -- the same shape, a different meaning. [C29985]

**Takeaway:** the piece should be a familiar motif returning at half speed in a new colour -- the same shape, a different meaning -- and the scene re-reading itself around it. [C29986]

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. Micalizzi is not a composer who writes to a genre; he is a bandleader who writes one theme and then plays it fifteen ways -- the whistle that identifies, the variation that carries, the brass that attacks, the folk graft that places, the song that runs the scene, the register jump that flips the tone, the tempo that paces the bodies, and the slow re-read that gives the old cue a new life. Early he is the session keyboardist, the guitar his native instrument; then the western whistler, the tune that made Trinity; then the melodrama man and the horror-funk man in the same two years, opposite registers; then the poliziottesco composer, four scores in one year, the toolkit hardening into a sound that could be listened to apart from the films; then the genre exporter, the same funk apparatus in a jungle and in space; late he is the conductor of his own past, the eighteen-piece band, the reissues from rediscovered tapes, the themes lifted by other directors into other decades. The place is never decoration: Rome, the studio, the Cinevox offices, the Parco Schuster stage, the record shop. His own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one the work states: one theme, varied until it is a world -- and then, fifty years later, played back, slowed down, and heard again as something new. [C29987]
