Bruce Nauman was born on 6 December 1941 in Fort Wayne, Indiana. [C28999] He studied mathematics and physics at the University of Wisconsin-Madison (B.A. 1964) and art with William T. Wiley and Robert Arneson at the University of California, Davis (M.F.A. 1966), taught at the San Francisco Art Institute 1966-68 and at UC Irvine in 1970, worked as an assistant to Wayne Thiebaud, and in 1968 met Meredith Monk and signed with dealer Leo Castelli. He moved from Northern California to Pasadena in 1969, to Pecos, New Mexico in 1979, and in 1989 established a home and studio in Galisteo, New Mexico, where he lived and worked with his second wife, the painter Susan Rothenberg, until her death in 2020; he has two children, Erik and Zoe, with his first wife, Judy Govan. He is a rancher who breeds and sells Quarter Horses, and has never lived in New York. [C28999]

Soon after graduating, confronted with "What to do?" in his studio, Nauman reached the realisation that governs everything after: "If I was an artist and I was in the studio, then whatever I was doing in the studio must be art. At this point art became more of an activity and less of a product." There was nothing in the studio because he did not have much money for materials, so he was forced to examine himself. Between 1966 and 1970 he made videos in which he used his body to explore the potentials of art, the role of the artist, and psychological states and behavioural codes; his early performed actions were captured in real time on a fixed camera over the 10-minute duration of a 16mm film reel, in studios in a former grocery shop in San Francisco's Mission district and in a sublet in Mill Valley. He made his first neon works in 1965 and refers to them as "signs"; they are often text-based, containing wordplay such as puns, anagrams or palindromes, and when he began studying art at Wisconsin the only course initially available to him was in "lettering". Much of his work turns on language, often as visual puns, setting the metaphoric and descriptive functions of language against each other -- a debt to Ludwig Wittgenstein's "language game". [C29000]

The career that followed has no signature style, and Nauman says so himself: his "signature style ... is his lack of signature style", and looking back on art school he said, "Basically, I would have to start over every day and figure out what art was going to be." His first solo exhibition was at Nicholas Wilder Gallery, Los Angeles, in 1966, followed the same year by inclusion in "Eccentric Abstraction" at New York's Fischbach Gallery; his first retrospective came in 1972, aged thirty-one, at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art and the Whitney Museum of American Art. He participated in five editions of Documenta (1968, 1972, 1977, 1982 and 1992) and was awarded the Golden Lion at the Venice Biennale twice, in 1999 and 2009, plus the Praemium Imperiale in 2004, the Wolf Prize in 1993, the Wexner Prize in 1994 and the Max Beckmann Prize in 1990. The MoMA retrospective of 1995 comprised more than sixty works and was his first museum retrospective in the United States in more than two decades; "Disappearing Acts" was presented at Schaulager, Basel, and MoMA and MoMA PS1, New York, in 2018-19; the Tate Modern survey ran October 2020-February 2021. In 2006 Artfacts.net ranked him the number one artist in the world, ahead of Robert Rauschenberg and Gerhard Richter. Asked to define the purpose of his art he replied, "To keep me busy." [C29001]

After a break of nearly twenty years he returned to drawing, because he felt he had forgotten how to draw. He cleaned up a corner of the studio and started drawing what was on the wall -- two foam foxes left from 1989, the year he made "Animal Pyramid", set up as a still life -- and made two drawings of Susan Rothenberg from a photograph he took of her just after she died at the house, one upside down because he thought it would be easier emotionally and one right side up, plus drawings of just her hair where her face would be; one of them, he said, is "the best drawing I have ever done". The drawings are in silverpoint -- silver wire in a pencil holder, on paper prepared with a ground including ground-up bone meal, with highlights scraped out with a razor blade -- a medium he took up after seeing Joseph Stella's silverpoint portrait of Marcel Duchamp, "very precise but also very strong as an image". He began filming himself drawing using a 3D iPhone rig of his own invention, often drawing blind with his mind's eye and memory, and observed, "You can't go wrong when you are drawing with your eyes closed." [C29002]

The drawing return is the whole of his 2026 output. "No Mistakes" at Konrad Fischer Galerie, New York, 9 May-20 June 2026, his twenty-first exhibition with the gallery and the first in New York, grew from a question by his friend the painter Eric Fischl -- "How do you hold your pencil?" -- and presents over sixty slow-motion 3D videos of the artist drawing still lifes, self-portraits and thumb-wrestling scenes, made with his assistant Ellen Babcock, with the blind drawings exhibited for the first time. "Identical" at the Nasher Sculpture Center, Dallas, 19 September 2026-21 March 2027, his first exhibition organised by a Texas museum, presents polyurethane, silicone rubber and plaster-cast animal forms alongside a rotating selection of drawings and a 3D video, all produced in his New Mexico studio from 2023 to 2026, the taxidermy forms displayed as sculptures themselves and the casting process rendered visible by showing forms and molds alongside their resulting plaster casts. "Tacita Dean and Bruce Nauman" at Marian Goodman Gallery, Paris, 19 October-19 December 2026, presents "Drawing Blind Drawing" (2026), a collaborative 35mm film diptych made after Dean and Nauman met in February 2025 and filmed at Nauman's home in November 2025 and January 2026. [C29003]

The twenty works inspected run 1965-2026 and trace one practice moving its own body through three positions: first the body performs, then the body is delegated, then the body returns as a hand. Early, 1965-1972: authorship is the artist alone, performing his own body in his own studio before a fixed camera, on cheap non-art material, and the audience is a witness to a set task, then is squeezed into the corridor itself -- "A Cast of the Space Under My Chair" (W01, 1965-68), "Self Portrait as a Fountain" (W02, 1966-67), "From Hand to Mouth" (W03, 1967), "Henry Moore Bound to Fail" (W04, 1967/1970), "The True Artist Helps the World by Revealing Mystic Truths" (W05, 1967), "Bouncing Two Balls Between the Floor and Ceiling with Changing Rhythms" (W06, 1967-68), "Walking in an Exaggerated Manner Around the Perimeter of a Square" (W07, 1967-68), "Walk with Contrapposto" (W08, 1968), "Stamping in the Studio" (W09, 1968), "Green Light Corridor" (W10, 1970), "Run from Fear, Fun from Rear" (W11, 1972). Middle, 1984-2001: authorship withdraws -- the artist's body is delegated to actors, a spinning head, five speakers, or absent altogether -- and the audience is surrounded, addressed and made to endure -- "One Hundred Live and Die" (W12, 1984), "Clown Torture" (W13, 1987), "Anthro/Socio" (W14, 1991/1992), "World Peace" (W15, 1996), "Mapping the Studio II" (W16, 2001). Recent, 2015-2026: authorship returns to the artist's own body, through digital re-processing and then through drawing itself as the action, filmed in slow motion or on 35mm -- "Contrapposto Studies, i through vii" (W17, 2015/16), "Begin Again" and "Identical" (W18, 2024/2026), "No Mistakes" (W19, 2026), "Drawing Blind Drawing" with Tacita Dean (W20, 2026). Grades: A 17, B 3, C 0 -- the seventeen A grades are W01, W02, W03, W04, W05, W06, W08, W10, W11, W12, W13, W15, W16, W17, W18, W19 and W20; the three B grades are W07, W09 and W14. What changed, in one line each: authorship moved from the artist performing alone, to the artist delegating his body to actors and machines, to the artist returning as a hand drawing blind; the support moved from concrete, wax and neon shop-sign tubing, to multi-channel video and walk-in architecture, to HD/3D video, silverpoint and found taxidermy molds; the audience role moved from witnessing a set task, to being surrounded and made to endure, to being invited to watch making at a crawl and share studio time. ## M1 -- The task that sets the time: a fixed job, control decaying in real time

A single task is set, and the task sets the duration: throw two balls as hard as possible at floor and ceiling and scramble to catch them; walk heel-to-toe around a taped square, hips swinging; stamp changing rhythms with your feet for an hour; walk up and down a corridor striking the contrapposto pose at every step. Nauman's own rule for the films is that a specific job structures the length: "You began when the job started; and when the job was over, the film was over." The mechanism is a fixed camera recording a single rule-bound action in real time, with no edit and no escape: the performer's movements grow jumpier as control fails, the bounce rhythm the ear expects comes apart from the one the eye sees because the sound is desynchronised, the inverted camera disorients up and down, and the artist is noticeably fatigued toward the end. In "Bouncing Two Balls" he said, "I was picking things that were hard to do, so that would be part of the tension of watching." The watchable behaviour is ten minutes or an hour of accelerating disorder -- pattern attempted, pattern lost, body chasing balls -- and the tension is that the task cannot be completed cleanly, only endured until the tape runs out. Seen in W06, W07, W08 and W09. [C29005]

**Takeaway:** set one hard job, run the camera until it is over, and let control decay on screen. [C29005]

## M2 -- The idiom made literal: a saying staged as a physical fact

A common expression is taken at its word and built: the artist's body becomes a fountain, spouting an arc of water from pursed lips, imitating the nude statues of decorative fountains; "from hand to mouth" becomes a fragmentary wax cast of lips, neck, shoulder and extended arm, the rest of the body absent; "bound to fail" becomes a cast-iron back bound with rope and titled as a verdict on Henry Moore; "run from fear" becomes neon, and its letter-swap, "fun from rear", sits beside it, fright, flight and erotic pleasure in six words. The mechanism is a visual pun in the Wittgenstein sense -- the metaphoric and descriptive functions of language set against each other -- so a saying becomes a sculptural object and the joke lands in one look, then a second turn follows: the Duchamp "Fountain" echo behind the spouting mouth, the heroic modernist back rendered as a captive, the graffiti on Pasadena's Suicide Bridge turned into a shop sign. The watchable behaviour is the double-take: first the phrase is recognised, then the object insists on it literally, and the eye travels the missing body -- hand to mouth -- and supplies what is not there. Seen in W02, W03, W04 and W11. [C29006]

**Takeaway:** take the phrase at its word and build it, so the saying becomes the object. [C29006]

## M3 -- Negative space cast solid: absence given weight

The empty space beneath a chair is given physical existence: Nauman tied cardboard to the legs of a steel chair, made a trial cast in plaster, then had a factory produce the concrete version, a plain notched rectangular block, after de Kooning's remark (as Nauman reported it) to paint the spaces between a chair's rungs, not the chair. The same move runs through the whole career: a body fragment cast in wax from a forensic mould, a back cast in iron and bound, and sixty years later taxidermy molds -- commercially manufactured interior bodies for skins, without ears, eyes, teeth, tails or paws -- stacked, bound with wire and anchored with hammers, with plaster casts displayed beside their foam and silicone molds, appearing at first glance identical. The mechanism is casting what is normally unnoticed -- the space under a chair, the interior of a skin, the gap a body leaves -- so absence becomes a heavy, Minimal-looking solid, and the casting process itself is displayed rather than hidden. The watchable behaviour is the viewer's re-reading: the block is nothing, and then it is the chair's missing volume; the fox is a factory form, and then it is a memento mori; the cast is a copy, and then the copy is the work. Seen in W01, W03, W04 and W18. [C29007]

**Takeaway:** cast the space nobody looks at, and show the mold beside the cast. [C29007]

## M4 -- The neon sign that flips its meaning: advertising's medium, a claim that inverts

Neon is borrowed from the shop window -- his first neon was made for the window of his storefront studio, a disused grocery store in San Francisco where a neon beer sign had hung -- and the medium carries a claim that inverts on second reading. "The True Artist Helps the World by Revealing Mystic Truths" winds in cursive light-blue neon into a spiral, reinforced by a red line, and at a distance the spiral reads as the number 6; the claim, in Nauman's own account, is "true and not true at the same time". "Run from Fear" becomes "Fun from Rear" by transposing two letters. One hundred three-word sentences -- "Smile and Live" / "Smile and Die", "Kiss and Live" / "Kiss and Die" -- light one after another, then each column, then the entire piece, with the insistent rhythm of a nursery rhyme. The mechanism is a sequencing system plus the medium itself: the live current in neon creates a hum, especially apparent in larger works, evoking danger behind the candy colours, and the sequencing builds the wall to full blaze and drops it back to single sentences. The watchable behaviour is reading inward along the spiral, shuttling between two lines until the meaning flips, and watching a life inventory -- eat, sleep, fuck, cry, touch, play -- blink on and off in changing combinations. Seen in W05, W11 and W12. [C29008]

**Takeaway:** borrow the shop sign, write the claim in light, and let it flip when read twice. [C29008]

## M5 -- The corridor that puts your body in the work: passable, but not comfortably

A narrow free-standing hallway, about 30 cm wide and 12 m long, flooded with green fluorescent light: the visitor must decide whether to squeeze through -- passable, but not comfortably -- so the viewer's own body, and a creeping claustrophobia, complete the work. The corridor is the set of the 1968 video first: a purpose-built corridor in the studio, camera fixed at the end, Nauman walking up and down for an hour striking the contrapposto pose, coming close to the lens as a blur of white T-shirt, then receding -- and the set outlived the performance, becoming the standalone "Performance Corridor" (1969). The same squeeze appears as two cages, one inside the other, dimensioned to Nauman's own body with a narrow gap barely sufficient to enter. The mechanism is architecture as the work: light and wallboard, not an image, do the manipulating, and the classical pose is turned into an endurance task inside the prop. The watchable behaviour is the decision at the entrance -- enter, hesitate, compress, pass through bathed in lime-green glow, or refuse and watch others do it -- and the hour of the same walk, banal at first, then rigorous, as the pose holds and the body tires. Seen in W08 and W10. [C29009]

**Takeaway:** build the passage too narrow to walk through comfortably, and let the visitor's body finish the piece. [C29009]

## M6 -- Surveillance and the delayed self-image: your back, after you turn the corner

Closed-circuit television records the visitor, and the monitor shows their back only after they have turned the corner. In "Going Around the Corner Piece with Live and Taped Monitors" (1970), walking towards a monitor, one's passage is recorded from behind by a CCTV camera, but the monitor is blank, and only on turning the corner does one see a fleeting image of one's own back on a second monitor; in the "Nick Wilder Corridor" (1970), cameras track visitors entering the narrow walls and what appears on the monitors differs conspicuously from what the visitors experience. The mechanism is a delay and a displacement: the camera sees you from behind, the screen withholds the image until you have passed it, and the viewer is both audience and subject at once -- watched, recorded, and shown themselves a moment too late. Tate notes CCTV was quite uncommon in the United States during the 1970s, so the apparatus itself was strange. The watchable behaviour is the turn: walking toward a blank screen, rounding the corner, and catching your own back as it disappears -- the work is the interval between being filmed and seeing it. Seen outside the W01-W20 set, in the Tate exhibition guide and the Dia handout, both read end to end this run. [C29010]

**Takeaway:** film the visitor from behind and show them only after they have passed. [C29010]

## M7 -- The unsynchronised multi-channel assault: endurance passes to the audience

Four named misadventures of a clown loop simultaneously in an enclosed room -- screaming "No, No, No, No" while jumping, kicking or lying down; balancing a goldfish bowl on the ceiling with a broom handle; opening a door booby-trapped with a bucket of water; succumbing to "Pete and Repeat" -- on two projections and four stacked monitors, sound from all six displays, playing over each other, unsynchronised, as a visual and auditory assault. A rotating disembodied head shouts contradictory imperatives -- "Feed Me, Eat Me, Anthropology", "Help Me, Hurt Me, Sociology" -- from three walls at once, with no quiet corner. Five speakers around a stool talk and sign variations on a script about talking and listening, and the grammatical shifters -- "I'll talk to you. You'll listen to me. You'll talk to me. I'll listen to you." -- force the seated viewer to re-locate themselves between speaker and listener while the overlapping voices turn a plea for peace into hectoring. The mechanism is unsynchronised loops in an enclosed room: the artist's body is delegated to an actor, a spinning head, five speakers, and the endurance task is no longer the artist's but the audience's -- viewers typically last minutes in the clown room before leaving. Nauman treats the clown as emblematic of the artist's role: make-up and costume licence behaviour that would otherwise not be tolerated. The watchable behaviour is cacophony and repetition until leaving is the only move left. Seen in W13, W14 and W15. [C29011]

**Takeaway:** loop the loops out of sync in a closed room, and make leaving the audience's job. [C29011]

## M8 -- The sequenced chant: phrases that light and shout until the room blazes

Phrases are run through a system until the room blazes: one hundred three-word sentences light one after another, then each column, then the entire piece, in changing combinations, with the neon hum underneath; a rotating head chants contradictory imperatives in different tonalities until the performer becomes a rotating alarm siren. The mechanism is a sequencing system applied to language -- a nursery-rhyme rhythm for "Smile and Live" / "Smile and Die", directional sound isolating a voice into a dense acoustic architecture for "Feed Me, Help Me, Eat Me, Hurt Me" -- so the same words return in different orders and different voices, and the system, not the speaker, decides what is said next. The watchable behaviour is the build: single sentences, then columns, then the full blaze, then a drop back to single sentences -- a visual symphony of verbs as a life inventory -- and the head spinning endlessly while the chant loops, the room shouting at the visitor from three walls at once. Seen in W12 and W14. [C29012]

**Takeaway:** run the phrases through a sequencer until the wall blazes, then drop back to one sentence. [C29012]

## M9 -- The empty-studio vigil: long near-empty looking, and the mouse is the event

Seven large projections show different views inside Nauman's New Mexico studio, filmed at night over several weeks in summer 2000 with an infrared camera at seven positions, while the artist is absent. The footage is digitally manipulated -- colour shifts gradually red to green to blue and back over a 28-minute cycle, and images flip left-right, flop upside-down or flip/flop roughly every 15 minutes -- and the subject is mundane stillness, so whenever a mouse, moth or the cat enters the frame it becomes an event. The title comes from Nauman's faxed reply to an invitation to a Cage-scores show, read as a refusal and intended as the work. Nauman advised viewers, "You have to kind of not watch anything, so you can be aware of everything." The mechanism is duration plus a slow, indifferent system: five hours forty-five minutes per projection, the colour cycle and the flips running on their own schedule regardless of what crosses the frame, so attention drifts across seven screens until a small animal crossing turns the room into a vigil. The watchable behaviour is long, near-empty looking -- the opposite of the clown room's assault -- where doing nothing is the discipline and a moth is a plot point. Seen in W16. [C29013]

**Takeaway:** point seven cameras at an empty room all night, shift the colour on a slow cycle, and let the mouse be the event. [C29013]

## M10 -- Blind drawing, no mistakes: eyes closed, the line accrues at a crawl

The artist looks at an object, then closes his eyes to draw blind in silverpoint or goldpoint -- after Kasper Konig's exercise of drawing a pig with eyes closed without lifting the pencil -- and, as Nauman observed, when you draw with your eyes closed there are no mistakes to be made. The work began when Eric Fischl asked "How do you hold your pencil?"; Nauman's daughter filmed over his shoulder while he made a silverpoint drawing, prompting "How do I draw?"; with his assistant Ellen Babcock he then made over sixty slow-motion 3D videos of himself drawing still lifes, self-portraits and thumb-wrestling scenes, sometimes with both hands at once, a doubling echoed by the 3D glasses. The mechanism is slowness as the medium: all video and audio are slowed, producing an ambient sound, and the image accrues line by line, out of focus without the glasses, doubled with them; in the middle gallery a group of 27 videos changes daily, so repeat visits offer a fresh experience, and in the furthest room a single work of fifteen videos loops for over five hours. The collaboration with Tacita Dean extends the same mechanism to two pairs of hands: only the hands are visible as they draw, one artist blind and assisted by the other who can see, the roles reversing between halves, the drawings emerging over around four minutes. The watchable behaviour is sitting in mismatched chairs and watching drawing happen at a crawl, the line arriving without error because no error is possible. Seen in W19 and W20. [C29014]

**Takeaway:** close your eyes, never lift the pencil, and film the drawing so slowly the line is the event. [C29014]

## Aesthetic logic

Nauman's logic is the body given a job it cannot do cleanly, in a room that will not let it off the hook. Against Holzer, whose sentence arrives in a crowd and contradicts itself, Nauman's task arrives in an empty studio and contradicts the body that tries to perform it: the balls will not stay caught, the walk will not stay straight, the corridor will not let you through without compressing, the clown will not stop screaming, the five speakers will not stop talking at you. What changes across sixty years is not the question but who is doing the enduring: first the artist alone, performing his own body before a fixed camera, with language as a pun made literal; then actors, a spinning head and five speakers, with the audience made to endure in the artist's place; then the artist returning as a hand, drawing blind, with language giving way to the line, because when you draw with your eyes closed there are no mistakes to be made. The work is always the interval -- between the task set and the body failing it, between the phrase and its letter-swap, between the cast and the mold it came from, between being filmed and seeing your own back -- and the interval is where the viewer has to stand, usually squeezed into a corridor 30 cm wide. He refuses to assign specific meanings to his artworks, and the refusal is the point: the meaning is the duration, the squeeze, the hum, the chant, the crawl. [C29015]
