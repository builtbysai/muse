## 1. Down Ampney and Leith Hill: birth and family

He was born on Saturday 12 October 1872 at the vicarage, Down Ampney, Gloucestershire, the third child and younger son of the Rev. Arthur Charles Vaughan Williams, vicar of All Saints, and Margaret Susan Vaughan Williams, nee Wedgwood. The mother's line is the famous one: Margaret was a great-granddaughter of Josiah Wedgwood, founder of the Wedgwood pottery, and a niece of Charles Darwin -- her father Josiah Wedgwood III married his cousin Caroline Darwin, Charles Darwin's sister, so Darwin was Ralph's great-uncle, and his great-great-grandfathers on the two sides were Josiah Wedgwood the potter and Erasmus Darwin. The father's line was law and Church: his grandfather Sir Edward Vaughan Williams was a judge and author of the Treatise on the Law of Executors and Administrators, and the unhyphenated double-barrelled surname was, by one account, invented and bequeathed by Sir Edward. His father died suddenly on Tuesday 9 February 1875, when Ralph was two, and his widow took the children to her family home, Leith Hill Place, Wotton, Surrey, where they were cared for by a nurse, Sara Wager, who instilled liberal social and philosophical opinions as well as manners. When the young Ralph asked his mother about Darwin's On the Origin of Species, she answered: "The Bible says that God made the world in six days. Great Uncle Charles thinks it took longer: but we need not worry about it, for it is equally wonderful either way." He grew up, in Wikipedia's phrase, "born to a well-to-do family with strong moral views and a progressive social outlook", with a modest private income in early career. His elder brother Hervey Wedgwood Vaughan Williams lived 1869-1944; his elder sister Margaret Jane, "Meggie", co-founded the Leith Hill Musical Festival with him. Two small splits ride on the family dates and are carried, not settled: Margaret Wedgwood's birth year is 1842 in the Trinity College Cambridge archives and the Mahler Foundation, 1843 in the Wedgwood genealogy site and Familypedia; Margaret Jane's birth year is 1870 in a Wikipedia note, 1871 in the Trinity archives. [C34532]

**Takeaway:** the family gave him two inheritances that never left the music -- the Wedgwood/Darwin side, which made evolution and the English countryside a birthright rather than a subject, and the clerical side, which made the Church and its music a daily habit rather than a belief. [C34533]

## 2. Charterhouse, the RCM, and Cambridge

He took piano lessons from his aunt Sophy Wedgwood from 1878, aged five, wrote his first composition the same year, a four-bar piano piece called "The Robin's Nest", disliked the piano, and was pleased to start violin lessons the following year; in 1880, aged eight, he took a correspondence course in music from Edinburgh University and passed its examinations. In September 1883 he went as a boarder to Field House preparatory school, Rottingdean, Sussex, and in January 1887 to Charterhouse, Godalming, where he stayed until July 1890, organising a concert in 1888 that included his own G major Piano Trio, now lost, with himself playing violin. At Charterhouse religion "meant less and less to him, and for a while he was an atheist. This softened into 'a cheerful agnosticism', and he continued to attend church regularly to avoid upsetting the family" -- while his love of the Authorised Version of the Bible remained, in Ursula Vaughan Williams's words, "one of his essential companions through life". In September 1890, a month before his eighteenth birthday, he enrolled at the Royal College of Music, intending to study composition under Hubert Parry, whom he idolised; Parry's reported advice, "Write choral music as befits an Englishman and a democrat", is the first of the mechanisms that runs through his whole career. In October 1892 he left the RCM temporarily for Trinity College, Cambridge, where he spent three years studying music and history, befriended G. E. Moore, Bertrand Russell, G. M. Trevelyan and Hugh Allen, continued weekly lessons with Parry, and graduated Bachelor of Music in 1894 and Bachelor of Arts in History in 1895. Back at the RCM in 1895-96, his new composition professor was Charles Villiers Stanford, and relations were "stormy but affectionate": Stanford, once adventurous, had grown deeply conservative and clashed with his modern-minded pupil, who refused the Brahms and Wagner traditions and stood up to his teacher as few students dared to do, while Stanford recognised the talent and wanted to correct his "opaque orchestration and extreme predilection for modal music". It was in this second RCM spell that he met fellow student Gustav Holst, who became a lifelong friend and his most valued mutual critic. He passed the Fellow of the Royal College of Organists examination in 1898, passed the Doctor of Music examination at Cambridge in 1899, and had the title formally conferred in 1901 -- hence "Dr Vaughan Williams", the style he preferred for life. [C34534]

**Takeaway:** the education gave him two teachers who pulled in opposite directions -- Parry, who told him to be English and democratic, and Stanford, who told him to be Germanic and correct -- and a fellow student, Holst, who mattered more than either, because what one really learns at a college is "not so much from one's official teachers as from one's fellow-students". [C34535]

## 3. Adeline, Bruch, and the slow apprenticeship

His only salaried post was church organist and choirmaster at St Barnabas, South Lambeth, inner London, 1895-1899, at fifty pounds a year -- "the only post he ever held for an annual salary" -- which he disliked, though the choir work was valuable experience. He had known Adeline Fisher since childhood; they were engaged in June 1897 and married on Saturday 9 October 1897 at All Saints Church, Hove. Adeline Maria Fisher, born 16 July 1870 -- a date that rests on a single genealogical source and is carried here as provisional -- was an accomplished cellist and pianist, daughter of Herbert William Fisher, and first cousin to Virginia Woolf; there were no children of the marriage. The honeymoon was several months in Berlin, where he studied with Max Bruch and heard an uncut performance of Wagner's Ring; Britannica dates the Berlin study 1897-98. On return they settled in London, first in Westminster, then from November 1905 at 13 Cheyne Walk, Chelsea, where he lived for the next twenty-four years. The first of his works to appear in print was the song "Linden Lea", in The Vocalist magazine in April 1902, then as separate sheet music -- the RVW Society timeline lists it under 1901, which is treated here as a year bucket against Wikipedia's precise April 1902. Through the first decade of the century he wrote for musical journals and for the second edition of Grove's Dictionary, edited the first volume of Purcell's Welcome Songs for the Purcell Society, and lectured in University Extension. He was a late developer by his own account and the sources': first publication at twenty-nine, no personal voice yet, Germanic in training and technique, opaque in orchestration by Stanford's complaint. What stayed constant even here was choral writing, a service ethic of adult education and editing, and the English choral lineage Parry had taught him. [C34536]

**Takeaway:** the apprenticeship was long because nothing in it was his yet -- the organist's post, the German training, the journal work -- and the voice arrived only when he stopped trying to be a German composer and started collecting the songs of his own countryside. [C34537]

## 4. The folk-song turn: 4 December 1903

On Friday 4 December 1903, at Ingrave, Essex, Vaughan Williams collected his first folk song, "Bushes and Briars", from Charles Pottipher -- the surname is spelled Pottipher, Potiphar and Pottiphar in different sources, an unresolved variant -- a seventy-year-old labourer (seventy-four in one account). He had lectured on folk song at Brentwood in the spring of 1903, had been invited to tea at Ingrave rectory by Georgiana Heatley, and visited Pottipher's cottage the next day; he borrowed a bicycle and rode the three miles from Brentwood to Ingrave. He wrote afterwards that hearing the song felt like "something he had known all his life". Over the following ten years he collected more than 800 songs -- "some 810" in one review, both figures approximations of the same total -- plus singing games and dance tunes, devoting up to thirty days a year, in twenty-one English counties, chiefly Essex, Norfolk, Herefordshire and Sussex; a small number were recorded on phonograph, the vast majority notated by hand, by ear, preserving modal structures and rhythmic irregularities. He was following Cecil Sharp and Lucy Broadwood -- Sharp had collected his first song in September 1903, three months before Vaughan Williams -- and he knew them all, serving on the Folk Song Society's committee from 1904 to 1946 and, when it amalgamated with the English Folk Dance Society in 1946, becoming president of the merged English Folk Dance and Song Society, a post he still held at his death; the EFDSS library was named the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library in his honour. The mechanism is assimilation, not quotation: he absorbed the melodic habits -- stepwise motion, narrow range, pentatonic and modal outlines, irregular phrase lengths, tunes that end without a dominant clinch -- until his own invented melodies were, in Hyperion's words on The Lark Ascending, "indistinguishable" from folk song, and the piece contains "no actual folk-song borrowing". The tune is then used as a theme, a bass, a countersubject or a whole movement's material, and developed by repetition, re-harmonisation and re-voicing rather than by Germanic motivic fragmentation. The first work to use collected material was the Fantasia on Christmas Carols (1912); the Norfolk Rhapsody No. 1 (1906) was the first work to show the assimilated folk contour in melody and harmony. Before 1903 he believed folk song belonged only to the past; after 4 December 1903 it became, in his words, a liberating force showing him a path to develop his own musical personality. [C34538]

**Takeaway:** one song, heard in a labourer's cottage on a borrowed bicycle, did what seven years of German training could not -- it gave him a grammar. The collecting was not research for the music; it was the music learning how to sound English. [C34539]

## 5. The English Hymnal: the tune laboratory

In 1904 Percy Dearmer and the committee preparing a new Anglican hymnal asked Vaughan Williams to serve as music editor; he accepted on condition of absolute veto over the music, worked on it from 1904 to 1906, and The English Hymnal appeared in 1906, with a revised edition in 1933. He later said: "I now know that two years of close association with some of the best (as well as some of the worst) tunes in the world was a better musical education than any amount of sonatas and fugues." The job gave him a laboratory for tune-making under strict constraints: a tune must be singable by an untrained congregation, memorable after one hearing, modal or diatonic enough to sit in the voice without chromatic traps, and dignified enough to carry a sacred text. He worked in three ways. First, he wrote new tunes that obey folk-song habits so closely they sound traditional: Sine Nomine, for "For All the Saints", in F major and modal, metre 10.10.10 with alleluias; Down Ampney, for "Come Down, O Love Divine", in D major, metre 6.6.11 D, named after the Gloucestershire village of his birth; King's Weston, for "At the Name of Jesus". Second, he adapted collected folk melodies as hymn tunes and renamed them after the place they were found, to obscure secular origins: Kingsfold, from a folk melody, for "I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say"; Forest Green, for "O Little Town of Bethlehem"; Monks Gate, for Bunyan's "He Who Would Valiant Be". Third, he revived old material -- plainsong, Genevan psalm tunes, Tallis and Gibbons -- and reprinted it in usable form, including Tallis's Third Mode Melody in The English Hymnal in 1906 as the tune for "When Rising from the Bed of Death", the same tune he later used for the Tallis Fantasia. He discarded numerous sugary Victorian tunes and replaced them with plainsong, German chorales, old psalm tunes, more than forty of his own folk-tune arrangements, and Welsh hymn tunes little known outside Wales; he arranged upwards of two hundred tunes for the Hymnal and Songs of Praise (1925, enlarged 1931), and later co-edited The Oxford Book of Carols (1928) with Martin Shaw and Dearmer. Sine Nomine appears in 271 hymnals in Hymnary's count, Down Ampney in 101, and Down Ampney was sung at his funeral in Westminster Abbey in 1958 as his ashes were interred. The Tallis tune he found while editing the Hymnal became the Tallis Fantasia; the Kingsfold tune became the Five Variants; the "York" tune frames The Pilgrim's Progress. A "cheerful agnostic" -- Ursula Vaughan Williams's phrase -- reshaped Anglican worship more than any other modern figure, and the hymnody is arguably his most widely sung legacy. [C34540]

**Takeaway:** the hymnal was a constraint machine -- singable, modal, memorable -- and the constraint produced the anonymity: a tune written in 1906 that congregations now treat as traditional is a tune that has stopped belonging to its composer. [C34541]

## 6. Ravel in Paris: points of colour

After unsuccessfully seeking lessons from Elgar, and considering Vincent d'Indy in Paris, he was introduced by the critic M. D. Calvocoressi to Maurice Ravel. He spent three months in Paris in the winter of 1907-1908, working with Ravel four or five times a week, chiefly on orchestration. His own account is that Ravel showed him how to orchestrate "in points of colour rather than in lines" -- to build a sound from short, distinct instrumental touches, a harp arpeggio, a muted string chord, a solo wind phrase, rather than from long continuous contrapuntal lines in the German manner. He described his pre-Ravel scoring as "lumpy and stodgy" and said he wanted "a little French polish". The mechanical result after Paris, noted by Kennedy, Adams, Ottaway and Frogley, is lighter, sharper instrumental texture: thinner doublings, more solo wind writing, more use of mutes, harp and celesta as colour, and chords moved as blocks. Ravel's own verdict, often quoted, was that Vaughan Williams was "my only pupil who does not write my music": the French clarity was absorbed, the modal and folk harmonic language was kept. He said Ravel helped him escape "the heavy contrapuntal Teutonic manner". The music written after his return -- the String Quartet in G minor, On Wenlock Edge, the overture to The Wasps, A Sea Symphony -- shows lighter, sharper textures. Two cautions ride on this episode and are carried, not settled: Britannica gives the Ravel year as 1909, against Wikipedia, the RVW Society timeline and the Polish Music Library, which all place it in winter 1907-08, and the 1909 date is treated here as an error, one visible consequence of Britannica's largely AI-generated current page; and the degree of Ravel's influence is debated, with Byron Adams advising caution about Vaughan Williams's own recollections in the Musical Autobiography, written forty-three years after the event. [C34542]

**Takeaway:** three months in Paris did not make him French; it gave him the orchestral manners to let an English tune be heard -- lighter texture, colour in points, chords as blocks -- and the teacher's verdict is the proof: the pupil who does not write his teacher's music is the pupil who learned. [C34543]

## 7. Breakthrough 1910: Tallis and the Sea

The breakthrough is public in 1910, in two premieres a month apart. The Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis was first performed on Tuesday 6 September 1910 at Gloucester Cathedral, at the Three Choirs Festival, Vaughan Williams conducting the London Symphony Orchestra strings; the same concert had Elgar conducting his own The Dream of Gerontius in the second half, before an audience of about two thousand, mostly there for Elgar. A Sea Symphony was first performed on Wednesday 12 October 1910 at the Leeds Festival, on his thirty-eighth birthday, the composer conducting; the same evening Rachmaninov played his Second Piano Concerto. J. A. Fuller Maitland of The Times praised the Fantasia; Samuel Langford of the Manchester Guardian said the symphony "definitely places a new figure in the first rank of our English composers". The Tallis Fantasia takes one of nine tunes Tallis wrote for Archbishop Matthew Parker's Psalter of 1567, a setting of Parker's metrical Psalm 2, "Why fumeth in sight", in the Phrygian mode and in Double Common Metre. Vaughan Williams had found it while editing The English Hymnal; he had already used it in 1906 incidental music for The Pilgrim's Progress and adapted it for the hymn "When rising from the bed of death". The Fantasia is built as introduction, opening statement of the theme broken into its four phrases interspersed with a "swaying chord" motif, four episodes exploring variations and different voicings across three string bodies, then restatement and coda. The mechanism is antiphony, not fugue: the three string bodies answer each other, often like an echo, with spread chords, majestic cadences and extreme dynamic range. It was revised in January 1913 and again in April 1919, made more concise each time, about two minutes cut in total. Biographer James Day called it "unquestionably the first work by Vaughan Williams that is recognizably and unmistakably his and no one else's". The cool first reception -- the organist Brewer called it "a queer, mad work by an odd fellow from Chelsea" -- gave way to canonical status. Herbert Howells and Ivor Gurney were in the audience and walked Gloucester all night discussing it. A Sea Symphony, begun in 1903 as "Songs of the Sea" and completed in 1909, sets Walt Whitman from Leaves of Grass across four movements -- "A Song for All Seas, All Ships", "On the Beach at Night, Alone", the Scherzo "The Waves", "The Explorers" -- for soprano, baritone, chorus and large orchestra, with the chorus singing in all four movements, voices as integral texture throughout rather than a finale-only addition. Two unifying motives run through it: a harmonic motive of two chords a third apart, the opening brass fanfare in B-flat minor resolving to D major on the word "sea", and a melodic figure juxtaposing duplets and triplets. [C34544]

**Takeaway:** the hinge work is not the big choral symphony but the string fantasia -- a 1567 psalm tune, given whole in Tallis's own harmonisation, then varied by three string bodies, so the listener hears Tudor music made present rather than a modern composer quoting it. [C34545]

## 8. A London Symphony: the city, the loss, the cutting

Symphony No. 2, A London Symphony, was first mentioned in a letter to Cecil Sharp in July 1911 and finished by the end of 1913. George Butterworth urged him to write a symphony -- "You know, you ought to write a symphony" -- and after Butterworth's death Vaughan Williams dedicated the piece to his memory. He never numbered it; "Symphony No. 2" is a later convenience. It was first performed on Friday 27 March 1914 at Queen's Hall, London, Geoffrey Toye conducting, and the first movement was "tremendously applauded". The forces are a large orchestra with harp and a percussion section that includes sleigh bells and glockenspiel; the four movements run Lento - Allegro risoluto, Lento, Scherzo (Nocturne), and a Finale closing with a quiet Epilogue. Vaughan Williams's own 1925 programme note insists it is "absolute" music, not descriptive, though it carries "local colour": Westminster chimes as a harp motive, a "Lavender Cry" reminiscence in the slow movement, mouth-organ hints in the Scherzo; and a finale closing with the chimes again and an Epilogue inspired by the last chapter of H. G. Wells's Tono-Bungay. The defining fact of the work's later history is loss and cutting. The manuscript full score was sent to Fritz Busch in Germany and lost in the outbreak of the First World War; Butterworth, Toye and E. J. Dent reconstructed it from orchestral parts, and the reconstruction was performed on Thursday 11 February 1915 by the Bournemouth Municipal Orchestra under Dan Godfrey, while Vaughan Williams was on army duty. He then revised it three times: in 1918, premiered by Boult; in 1919-20, premiered by Albert Coates at Queen's Hall in May 1920 and first published; and finally in 1933, first given by Beecham in February 1934 and published in 1936. The bar counts fall from 1,322 in 1914 to 1,177 in 1936; the slow movement alone lost fifty-two bars in 1933-36, and the original Epilogue of 109 bars became 60. The 1914 score was recorded in 2001, and critics noted the original is more meditative, almost Mahlerian, while the revision is Sibelian in logic and severity. Kennedy's judgement is that the cuts were Vaughan Williams's own decisions, not forced on him. [C34546]

The London Symphony is the first purely orchestral symphony, the city symphony, and the work that taught him revision as a compositional act. It sits between the choral Sea Symphony and the war-incubated Pastoral, and its cutting anticipates the concision of the Pastoral and the Fifth. The definitive version, at about forty to forty-three minutes against the original's sixty-one, is a choice, and the earlier version remains musically valuable -- which is the point: for Vaughan Williams, revision is not correction, it is composition continued by other means. [C34547]

**Takeaway:** a symphony can be lost in a war, rebuilt from its parts by friends, and then cut by a fifth by its own composer -- and the cutting is the composition, not the damage. The city stays; the music gets shorter. [C34548]

## 9. The Lark Ascending: the bird that never lands

The Lark Ascending was completed in 1914 in its original form for violin and piano, set aside when he volunteered for the war, and reworked for solo violin and orchestra after it. The violin-and-piano version was first performed on Wednesday 15 December 1920 at Shirehampton Public Hall, Marie Hall, the dedicatee, playing with Geoffrey Mendham at the piano; the orchestral version was first performed on Tuesday 14 June 1921 at Queen's Hall, London, Marie Hall with the British Symphony Orchestra under Adrian Boult. The orchestral forces are small -- solo violin, two flutes, one oboe, two clarinets, two bassoons, two horns, triangle as the only percussion, and strings -- and the piece runs typically thirteen to sixteen minutes, subtitled "A Romance". It was inspired by George Meredith's 1881 poem "The Lark Ascending", 122 lines, of which Vaughan Williams printed twelve at the head of the score. Ursula Vaughan Williams said he "had made the violin become both the bird's song and its flight, being, rather than illustrating the poem". Mechanically it is a two-bar woodwind and muted-string introduction in 6/8, then an unaccompanied solo cadenza marked pianissimo and sur la touche, written senza misura, without bar-lines. A folk-like melody enters when the orchestra re-enters in 2/4; a second, shorter unaccompanied cadenza leads to a contrasting Allegretto tranquillo quasi andante episode with a new flute melody; an Allegro tranquillo section with solo-violin trills punctuated by off-beat triangle moves from ambiguous G major to F major in 6/8, with a scherzando oboe melody; the flute melody returns on the solo violin, then a reprise, ending with an unaccompanied closing cadenza rising to a high D and falling a minor third to B. Analysis by Christopher Mark finds Dorian and Aeolian modes with extensive pentatonic writing. Crucially, there is no actual folk-song borrowing in the piece; the folk-like quality is Vaughan Williams's own melodic invention, assimilated from folk song. It is the purest statement of his pastoral-Romance voice, and the work that, with the Tallis Fantasia, made his reputation as a voice unlike anyone else's. Its simplicity is deceptive: rhythmic freedom, avoidance of tonic-dominant cadencing, modal ambiguity. First recording 1928, Isolde Menges with Malcolm Sargent. It has repeatedly topped Classic FM Hall of Fame-type polls in Britain, Australia, New Zealand and the United States. One honest gap: it is not known when and where exactly he composed it; the Margate story is reported, not established, and the original manuscript is lost. Marie Hall may have influenced details of the score; the extent is unknown. [C34549]

**Takeaway:** the lark is a solo violin that keeps leaving the orchestra and coming back to it, and the piece ends with the bird alone in the air -- the orchestra is the field, the violin is the flight, and the flight is what the piece is about, not the field. [C34550]

## 10. War: the ambulance, the guns, the hearing

Despite his age -- approaching forty-two in October 1914 -- he volunteered on the outbreak of war in August 1914, Britain having declared war on Tuesday 4 August 1914. Joining the Royal Army Medical Corps as a private, he served as a stretcher-bearer and ambulance crew in France and later in Greece, at Salonika; his unit was billeted in Dorking for three months in 1915, and he was posted to France in June 1916, just before the Battle of the Somme, then to Salonika. In 1917 he was commissioned as a lieutenant in the Royal Artillery -- Dorking Museum specifies December 1917, second lieutenant in the Royal Garrison Artillery -- seeing action in France from March 1918. "The continual noise of the guns damaged his hearing, and led to deafness in his later years." After the armistice of 11 November 1918 he served as director of music for the British First Army until demobilised in February 1919. He stopped composing during the war and took time before writing new music, revising earlier pieces instead. The war "left its emotional mark"; he lost many comrades and friends, including the young composer George Butterworth, killed on Saturday 5 August 1916 at the Somme, aged thirty-one. Wikipedia's lead calls the war one of "two episodes [that] made notably deep impressions in Vaughan Williams's personal life". The other episode, by the same account, was the folk-song discovery -- the two hinges of the life, one that gave him a language and one that took his hearing and his friends. [C34551]

**Takeaway:** he went to war at forty-one as a private stretcher-bearer, not as a famous composer, and came back deaf in one ear and with the material for the Pastoral Symphony -- the war did not interrupt the career, it became the career's second subject. [C34552]

## 11. A Pastoral Symphony: elegy, not idyll

A Pastoral Symphony, his third, was incubated in France in 1916-18 while he served as ambulance orderly and stretcher bearer, and completed in 1921. It was first performed on Thursday 26 January 1922 at Queen's Hall, London, at a Royal Philharmonic Society concert, Adrian Boult conducting the RPS Orchestra, with soprano Flora Mann in the finale; the American premiere followed on Wednesday 7 June 1922 at the Litchfield County Music Festival, Norfolk, Connecticut, the composer conducting. The premiere date carries the study's most persistent split: the dedicated article, the British Library manuscript catalogue, Hyperion and IMSLP all give Thursday 26 January 1922, while the main biography article says "first performance in London in May", and a FreeDictionary mirror gives 16 January 1922. This study uses 26 January 1922; the May wording may conflate a later performance. The forces include a natural trumpet in E-flat in the second movement and a wordless solo voice -- soprano, with a tenor or solo clarinet alternative -- in the fourth. Four movements, all slow in character: Molto moderato, Lento moderato, Moderato pesante, a slow dance, and Lento; about thirty-five minutes. It is not programmatic, but it is war-time music. The genesis Vaughan Williams gave long afterwards: going up night after night with the ambulance wagon at Ecoivres, a steep hill, "a wonderful Corot-like landscape in the sunset"; an army bugler practising became part of that landscape and is the genesis of the long trumpet cadenza in the second movement. The cadenza is played on a natural trumpet in E-flat, valves not used, so the seventh harmonic sounds slightly out of tune against equal temperament, over a string pedal. The first movement shifts between 4/4 and 3/4; the second opens with an F major natural-horn solo above an F minor chord, developed by solo cello; the third is a slow dance with a folk-contoured brass trio and a fugal coda, the only fast music in the work, all pianissimo; the finale opens with a pentatonic wordless vocal solo, senza misura, over a soft drumroll, then an elegiac rhapsody closing pianissimo. Vaughan Williams's own premiere note: "almost entirely quiet and contemplative -- there are few fortissimos and few allegros". Hardly three fortissimos in the whole work -- Tovey's "massive quietness". He said himself that the symphony is "really war-time music", incubated on ambulance runs in France, "not really lambkins frisking at all, as most people take for granted". Constant Lambert called it "one of the landmarks in modern music"; Holst preferred it to any of Vaughan Williams's other orchestral works. It is the least-often played of the early symphonies, but after a century it is ranked among his strongest and most personal utterances. The "cow looking over a gate" line, by Peter Warlock, was about Vaughan Williams's style in general, not this symphony specifically, which Warlock called "a truly splendid work". [C34553]

**Takeaway:** the pastoral is a war landscape with the battle removed -- a bugler practising on a hill at sunset, a wordless voice over a drumroll, and almost no loud music at all. The idyll is the misreading; the elegy is the piece. [C34554]

## 12. Teacher, conductor, and the 1920s

In 1919 he accepted Hugh Allen's invitation to teach composition at the RCM and remained on the faculty for the next twenty years, to about 1939 -- Hymnary gives 1920-1940, a split carried here, with 1919-c.1939 the better-supported span. His students included Ivor Gurney, Constant Lambert, Elizabeth Maconchy, Grace Williams and Gordon Jacob. In 1921 he succeeded Allen as conductor of The Bach Choir, London, and resigned in 1928 -- Hymnary gives 1920-1927, the same kind of one-year split, with 1921-1928 preferred. He took an honorary Doctor of Music at Oxford in 1919. During the 1920s Adeline became increasingly immobilised by arthritis, and the stairs of their London house forced a move in 1929 to "The White Gates", Dorking, where they lived until Adeline's death in 1951; the onset is 1920s in the better sources, against a single-source claim of 1909. The 1920s were his most productive decade by count -- forty works premiered, by Kennedy's count -- and the decade's centre of gravity shifts from pastoral landscape to drama, ritual and the Bible: the Mass in G minor (1922), Old King Cole (1923), Hugh the Drover (1924) and Sir John in Love (1928), Flos Campi (1925), Sancta Civitas (1925), Riders to the Sea (1925-31). In September-December 1932 he was visiting lecturer at Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania, giving the Mary Flexner Lectures on National Music, published as National Music in 1934; he was elected president of the English Folk Dance and Song Society in 1932. He conducted the Leith Hill Musical Festival, which he had helped found in 1905 -- first festival Wednesday 10 May 1905 -- as principal conductor until 1953. In 1938 he met Ursula Wood; the Shakespeare Prize of the University of Hamburg was awarded in 1937 and received in Hamburg in June 1938, though currency restrictions prevented taking the 10,000-mark prize money out of Germany. After the deaths of Elgar, Delius and Holst in 1934 he came to be regarded as the leading figure in British music; Holst's death on Friday 25 May 1934 was "a severe personal and professional blow". [C34555]

**Takeaway:** the twenties are the decade he becomes an institution himself -- teacher, conductor, festival conductor, lecturer, society president -- and the institution does not eat him the way it ate Lambert, because the composing never stops: the teaching is the day job, the symphony is the life. [C34556]

## 13. Job: the masque and Lambert

Job: A Masque for Dancing was composed 1927-30, on a scenario by Geoffrey Keynes after William Blake's Illustrations of the Book of Job, published 1826; Keynes selected eight of Blake's twenty-one illustrations. The designs were by Gwendolen Raverat, Vaughan Williams's cousin; the choreography by Ninette de Valois. The impulse was the 1927 centenary of Blake's death. Vaughan Williams pressed ahead with composition before Keynes and Raverat had finished their work. The concert premiere of the full orchestral score was on Thursday 23 October 1930 at the Norwich Festival, conducted by the composer; a second concert performance followed in London in February 1931, its exact date not established. The danced premiere was on Sunday 5 July 1931 at the Cambridge Theatre, London, for the Camargo Society, conducted by Constant Lambert in his reduced version, choreography by de Valois, Satan danced by Anton Dolin; it was repeated on 6 July and on 24 July at Oxford, and the first public performance at the Old Vic followed on 22 September 1931. The full orchestral score was too large for a West End theatre pit, so Lambert -- "much to the composer's admiration" -- reduced it for a small theatre orchestra; the reduction is what is normally heard in the theatre, the full score in the concert hall. The work runs about forty-five minutes in nine scenes, with eighteen section headings in the score -- the scene count carries a split, the Job article giving nine scenes and the de Valois article eight, and this study follows the fuller nine-scene account. The nine scenes follow Blake's sequence, each headed by a Bible quotation: the Saraband of the Sons of God, Satan's Dance of Triumph, the Minuet of the Sons of Job, Job's Dream, the Dance of the Three Messengers, the Dance of Job's Comforters, Elihu's Dance of Youth and Beauty, the Pavane of the Sons of the Morning, and the Epilogue. Mechanically it is built from old dance forms -- saraband, minuet, pavane, galliard -- made modern by modal harmony, bitonal clashes and the saxophone colour; the famous "Satan's Dance" and the pastoral dance are the two poles. Vaughan Williams refused to call it a ballet -- he detested dance en pointe -- hence "a Masque for Dancing". Diaghilev turned the scenario down as "too English and too old-fashioned"; Vaughan Williams was not displeased. It was the first ballet produced by an entirely British creative team, and the first of the great 1930s scores in which Tudor and archaic dance forms carry a modern harmonic language, the direct preparation for the Fourth Symphony. It stayed in the Vic-Wells and Sadler's Wells repertoire, reached the Royal Opera House in May 1948 with Boult conducting the full score, and was revived by Birmingham Royal Ballet in the 1990s. The Times called it "a completely satisfying synthesis of the arts". The score was dedicated to Boult in 1934, after he got the Bach Choir to fund engraving the full score. [C34557]

Lambert's part in Job is the doorway of this study in miniature. He entered the RCM in September 1922, aged seventeen, and studied composition with Vaughan Williams, with R. O. Morris and George Dyson, conducting with Malcolm Sargent and piano with Herbert Fryer, from 1922 to 1926. Hyperion's booklet note says of Lambert that "for Vaughan Williams Lambert formed considerable friendship and admiration -- without [imitating him]". The London Review of Books review of Stephen Lloyd's biography puts it the same way: Lambert "had no interest in folksong (though he studied with Vaughan Williams and liked and respected him)". The teacher he admired without imitating is the exact phrase of the #795 study, and the Job rescoring is the exact act: a student who would not write his teacher's music took his teacher's largest score and made it fit a theatre pit, and conducted it himself. In 1942 Lambert wrote Aubade heroique, a short orchestral "morning song" inspired by the Sadler's Wells troupe's narrow escape from Holland in 1940, and dedicated it to Vaughan Williams on his seventieth birthday. One correction from this run: a leg report repeated the claim that de Valois engaged Lambert as musical director in 1935. That is the split resolved in #795 and it is not repeated here: the appointment is 1931, and 1935 is the year the company consolidated at Sadler's Wells. [C34558]

**Takeaway:** Job is the whole English project in one work -- Blake's pictures, Tudor dance forms, modal harmony, a student doing the practical work -- and the teacher's music needed the student's hands to reach the stage at all. [C34559]

## 14. The Fourth Symphony: violence at sixty

Symphony No. 4 in F minor was begun late in 1931 and completed in 1934 -- the article lead's "between 1931 and 1935" is treated here as conflating completion with the premiere year -- and first performed on Wednesday 10 April 1935 at Queen's Hall, London, Adrian Boult conducting the BBC Symphony Orchestra, broadcast live by the BBC. It has no title, by design: "to be understood as pure music, without any incidental or external inspiration". It is dedicated to Arnold Bax. It opens with a grinding semitone clash, then two germinal motives that dominate the whole symphony: a four-note figure E - E-flat - F - E natural, the BACH shape compressed, beginning and ending on the same note, and a rising-fourths figure in the brass a few bars later. The severity is obsessive and concentrated, not episodic, built in the manner of Sibelius's Fourth from those two cells. The slow movement is "a lovely texture of wandering counterpoints"; the Scherzo has humour that broadens when the tuba capers in the Trio; the Finale is a fugato epilogue ending on an open fifth. Vaughan Williams's own words, in rehearsal: "I don't know whether I like it, but it's what I meant." He refused programmatic readings -- Nazi Europe, a prophet seeing naked violence triumphant: "I wrote it not as a definite picture of anything external... but simply because it occurred to me like this -- I can't explain why." Boult nevertheless heard it as prophecy. The impulse, per Ursula Vaughan Williams, was a Times article by H. C. Colles in August 1931 on abstract modern symphonies: Vaughan Williams read it at breakfast and said "il faut que je compose cela" -- I need to compose this. Holst died on Friday 25 May 1934 while Vaughan Williams was finishing the work; he felt the loss of "that loving and critical mind" acutely, and Holst never heard the finished result. Walton and Lambert reportedly called it "the greatest symphony since Beethoven". Cardus: no great tune, but the composer's most concentrated work. It proved Vaughan Williams could be dissonant, terse and violent at sixty, and it set up the Fifth as its opposite pole. First recording 1937, the composer conducting the BBC Symphony Orchestra -- his only commercial recording of any of his symphonies. American premiere 19 December 1935, Cleveland Orchestra, Artur Rodzinski; German premiere 8 January 1936, Munich, Boult. [C34560]

**Takeaway:** the Fourth is the proof that the pastoral was a choice, not a limitation -- two cells, a semitone clash, and a composer who refuses to explain it, at sixty, after a lifetime of being called gentle. [C34561]

## 15. The Fifth Symphony: Pilgrim salvaged

Symphony No. 5 in D major was composed 1938-43. After the Fourth he had temporary writer's block, and much of the material comes from the then-unfinished opera The Pilgrim's Progress, which he believed might never be completed; he salvaged its themes into the symphony. It is dedicated "without permission to Jean Sibelius", and Sibelius, hearing a broadcast, wrote that it was "a marvellous work" and the dedication made him "proud and grateful". It was first performed on Thursday 24 June 1943 at the Royal Albert Hall, London, at a Henry Wood Promenade Concert, the London Philharmonic Orchestra conducted by the composer; the American premiere followed on 30 November 1944 at Carnegie Hall, New York Philharmonic, Artur Rodzinski. The 24 June 1943 date rests on a single secondary source and is carried as such. The forces are the smallest orchestra of any of his symphonies: two flutes, oboe, cor anglais, two clarinets, two bassoons, two horns, two trumpets, three trombones, timpani and strings -- no tuba, no harps, no percussion except timpani. Four movements with titles: Preludio, Scherzo (Presto misterioso), Romanza (Lento), Passacaglia (Moderato); about thirty-five to thirty-seven minutes. The Preludio opens on a low C pedal, answered by a horn call outlining a D major chord, implying Mixolydian D; the violins use the pentatonic scale, so the key stays ambiguous. The Scherzo is a counterpoint of rhythms rather than pitches: hemiolas, five-bar phrases, Dorian violins against Aeolian woodwind. The Romanza is the heart: in the manuscript it carried Bunyan's words "Upon that place there stood a cross / And a little below a sepulchre", omitted from the published score so the symphony reads as absolute music; its cor anglais solo is taken from Pilgrim. The Passacaglia begins on a repeated bass, then abandons it; its triumphant primary melody is Pilgrim's dialogue with Interpreter, and the horn calls of the opening return, now firmly in D major, for a quiet valediction. Echoes of the hymn tune Sine Nomine appear in the second subject of the first movement and at the end of the fourth. The Times called it "transcendental... music not only of contemplation but of benediction"; Cardus called it "the most benedictory and consoling music of our time". He lightly revised it in 1951, not published in his lifetime. In the premiere week he testified for Michael Tippett, on trial as a pacifist refusing war work, calling Tippett a "national asset" though no pacifist himself. [C34562]

**Takeaway:** the Fifth is the opera that could not be finished, salvaged as a symphony -- Pilgrim's themes, a hymn tune, a passacaglia that abandons its own bass -- and the salvage is the masterpiece, not the consolation prize. [C34563]

## 16. The Sixth Symphony: the drifting epilogue

Symphony No. 6 in E minor was composed 1944-47 -- the RVW Society list of works gives 1946-47, against the British Library catalogue and the symphony list, which give 1944-47, and this study uses 1944-47 -- and dedicated to his musical assistant, the pianist Michael Mullinar, who gave the first piano run-through at Vaughan Williams's home in Dorking in 1946. A starting point was a theme composed for, but not used in, the wartime film Flemish Farm (1943). It was first performed on Wednesday 21 April 1948 at the Royal Albert Hall, London, at a Royal Philharmonic Society concert, Adrian Boult conducting the BBC Symphony Orchestra, broadcast live on the BBC Home Service. It was an immediate sensation: over a hundred performances within two years, and first recordings in February 1949 by Stokowski and by Boult days later. The forces include a tenor saxophone doubling bass clarinet. Four movements, linked without pause: Allegro, Moderato, Scherzo (Allegro vivace), Epilogue (Moderato); about thirty-five minutes. The opening is a fortissimo clash of F minor and E minor played simultaneously by the full orchestra. The symphony is built from opposition of major and minor thirds, the augmented fourth, and conflict of tonalities, plus recurring ideas -- simultaneous chords a semitone apart, a short-short-long rhythmic figure. The second movement, in B-flat minor a tritone away, is dominated by a "rat-a-tat" three-note ostinato that grows to the single loudest point in the symphony, then winds down to a long cor anglais solo. The third is a heavily fugal, raucous, sardonic Scherzo; its Trio gives the tenor saxophone its only true solo, on a theme Vaughan Williams glossed with Constant Lambert's joke about playing a folk tune soft and repeating it loud. The fourth movement, the Epilogue, is marked pianissimo throughout with repeated senza crescendo: "drifting" and "whiffs of theme", a pianissimo farewell that ends in nothingness. Vaughan Williams insisted it was absolute music and disliked The Times calling it "the war symphony" -- "It never seems to occur to people that a man might just want to write a piece of music" -- though he later hinted the nearest words for the last movement were Prospero's "We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep." Listeners have heard in it the Cafe de Paris bombing, a world laid waste by atomic war, and a "Waste Land"; Ursula Vaughan Williams wrote that he never allowed the idea behind the last movement to be known. Deryck Cooke, at the premiere, felt unable to applaud: "the blood had frozen in my veins". The Scherzo was partly re-orchestrated in 1949-50; that revised version is now most commonly played. [C34564]

**Takeaway:** the Sixth does not end, it drifts -- one motif repeated pianissimo until it stops resolving, an epilogue as a whole movement, and a composer who will not tell you what it means. The bleakness is in the repetition, not the volume. [C34565]

## 17. Film music: the sketchbook years

His first film score was 49th Parallel, in 1940-41, at sixty-eight or sixty-nine. He was talked into it by Muir Mathieson, to assuage guilt at doing nothing for the war effort. The film -- Powell and Pressburger, released 1941 -- follows five members of a German U-boat crew stranded in Canada trying to escape across the 49th parallel into the then-neutral United States. Vaughan Williams used French-Canadian melodies in the Hudson's Bay scenes and German traditional melodies in the Hutterite scenes; the Prelude was adapted as the song "The New Commonwealth" (1943). The main-title music is his most frequently re-recorded film theme. Coastal Command followed in 1942, music for the wartime RAF documentary about flying boats patrolling the North Sea; a suite of seven movements was arranged by Muir Mathieson. Scott of the Antarctic (1947-48) was the longest and most musically inventive of his film scores, for Ealing Studios, John Mills as Captain Scott. Twenty-eight items were composed, six not used in the film; the autograph full score runs 996 bars, about half used. It was first shown in London on Monday 29 November 1948, with the Philharmonia Orchestra conducted by Ernest Irving. The wider film list runs to eleven scores in all: The People's Land (1941-43); The Story of a Flemish Farm (1943, a theme from it seeded the Sixth Symphony); Stricken Peninsula (1944); The Loves of Joanna Godden (1946); Dim Little Island (1949, re-using Five Variants material); Bitter Springs (1950); The England of Elizabeth (1955); The Vision of William Blake (1957). He told his OUP editor Alan Frank that "almost always when I write film music I have an ulterior idea of a concert version in my own mind", because in the concert room "we are pretty sure we shall not have half the music cut out and the rest inaudible". Film music therefore functions in his output as a sketchbook for concert works: Flemish Farm into the Sixth, Scott into the Seventh, Five Variants into Dim Little Island, the 49th Parallel Prelude into a song and a piano piece. The film scores are sectional, mood-painting and economical -- they had to be written fast, often before seeing the film -- and they loosened his late style towards exotic percussion, wind machine and pictorial directness. He greatly enjoyed the genre; his 1944 essay "Film Music" says so. [C34566]

**Takeaway:** the film studio was a sketchbook with a deadline -- write fast, before seeing the film, with an ulterior concert version already in mind -- and the sketchbook fed two of his last three symphonies. The pastoral reputation was already out of date by 1940. [C34567]

## 18. Sinfonia antartica: the film becomes a symphony

Symphony No. 7, Sinfonia antartica, was composed 1949-52, reworking music from the 1947-48 film score for Scott of the Antarctic, and dedicated to Ernest Irving, who commissioned the film score. The title was changed at the last minute from Sinfonia Antarctica to Sinfonia antartica, for consistently Italian spelling. It was first performed on Wednesday 14 January 1953 at the Free Trade Hall, Manchester, Sir John Barbirolli conducting the Halle Orchestra and Choir, with Margaret Ritchie as wordless soprano solo; the London premiere followed on Wednesday 21 January 1953. The 14 January date is used here; a classical-music.com guide gives "14 July 1953", treated as an error. The forces are a large orchestra with wind machine, bells, xylophone, glockenspiel, vibraphone, celesta, piano, organ in the third movement, harp and strings, plus soprano solo and three-part women's chorus, wordless, in the first and fifth movements, placed off-stage at the premiere. Five movements, about forty-five minutes: Prelude (Andante maestoso), Scherzo (Moderato), Landscape (Lento, leading directly into the fourth), Intermezzo (Andante sostenuto), Epilogue (Alla marcia, moderato). Each movement carries a literary quotation at its head -- Shelley from Prometheus Unbound, Psalm 104:26, Coleridge's Hymn before Sunrise, Donne's The Sun Rising, Captain Scott's Last Journal: "I do not regret this journey..." -- the quotations for reading, not performance, though some recordings declaim them. It is not a patchwork of film cues but a re-ordering and in many cases re-composition of the film material into a five-movement symphonic architecture. The film score itself was mostly composed before Vaughan Williams had seen the script; music "suggested by ice and wind, penguins and whales came into his head". The symphony keeps the pictorial means -- wind machine, wordless women's voices as ice-voices, vast percussion -- but imposes symphonic recurrence and a tragic arc from Shelley's defiance to Scott's stoic last-journal words. Vaughan Williams said in his premiere note only that it was "suggested by the film Scott of the Antarctic". It is the return, with old-age mastery, to the free-form method of A Sea Symphony four decades earlier, and the proof that film music could be a legitimate quarry for a symphony -- a point critics in 1953 held against it. It divides opinion still: some hear it as not truly symphonic; others, Kennedy among them, call it "enduring music, brilliantly imaginative, genuinely tragic and heroic, wonderfully scored". It is also the bridge work between the Sixth's violence and the Eighth's playfulness, and the first of the final triptych of symphonies shaped by his film work. [C34568]

**Takeaway:** the film score was written before he saw the film, from ice and wind and penguins in his head -- and the symphony is what happens when that imagined Antarctica is given five movements, a wind machine, and Scott's last words. The programme is the architecture. [C34569]

## 19. The Eighth Symphony: playfulness at eighty-three

Symphony No. 8 in D minor was composed 1953-55, substantially complete by January 1955, and dedicated to Sir John Barbirolli -- "For Glorious John, with love and admiration from Ralph". It is the first of his symphonies he actually allowed to be numbered; he had said "I have never put numbers to my symphonies and don't want to start now", and Oxford University Press published it as "Symphony No 8 in D minor". A late addition: after hearing Turandot at Covent Garden with his wife, he added tuned gongs, fascinated by Puccini's use of them. It was first performed on Wednesday 2 May 1956 at Kings Hall, Manchester -- not the Free Trade Hall -- the Halle Orchestra conducted by Barbirolli, and recorded by the BBC; the London premiere followed on Monday 14 May 1956 at the Royal Festival Hall, and the United States premiere on 5 October 1956 with the Philadelphia Orchestra under Eugene Ormandy. The 2 May 1956 day rests on a single secondary source and is carried as such. The Halle took it around the country, about twenty performances in twelve months. The forces are conventional woodwind, brass and strings with two harps, but an augmented percussion section -- timpani, vibraphone, triangle, glockenspiel, side drum, cymbals, tubular bells, tuned gongs, bass drum, xylophone, celesta -- "all the 'phones and 'spiels known to the composer". Four movements, the shortest of the nine symphonies, just under thirty minutes: Fantasia (Variazioni senza tema, variations without a theme); Scherzo alla marcia, for wind instruments only; Cavatina, for strings only; Toccata. Each movement isolates a choir, a late-life experiment in instrumental colour. The Fantasia is "seven variations in search of a theme", three closely related motifs worked through seven variations, ending with the trumpet motif returning over a vibraphone-and-strings shimmer. The Scherzo alla marcia, in C minor, winds and brass only, runs 181 bars but under four minutes. The Cavatina, in E minor, strings only, is a five-part rondo, meditative; its main theme resembles the chorale "O sacred head sore wounded" -- Vaughan Williams called it "a mix-up in my mind" -- and Kennedy called it "a beautiful old-age reverie of farewell to Tallis and larks ascending"; it closes quietly in E major. The Toccata, in D major and minor, is virtuoso and exuberant, with a percussion-dominated climax. It is the only symphony besides the Fourth to end loudly; the others end quietly, often niente. Grove (2001) finds lightness and humour "shot through with sadness and anxiety". The Manchester Guardian after the premiere: "It is not often that the entire audience in an English concert hall gets on its feet to cheer, particularly after a new work." It shows the eighty-three-year-old composer enjoying exotic combinations he had eschewed earlier, and it sets up the Ninth's darker return to E minor. [C34570]

**Takeaway:** at eighty-three he writes a symphony in four movements that isolate the choirs -- winds alone, strings alone, variations without a theme -- and adds tuned gongs because he heard Turandot the night before. The playfulness is the experiment, not the relief from it. [C34571]

## 20. The Ninth Symphony: saxophones and the deleted Tess

Symphony No. 9 in E minor was composed 1956-57, in London, Majorca and Ashmansworth, the home of Gerald Finzi and his wife Joy -- Finzi died in September 1956. It was commissioned by and dedicated to the Royal Philharmonic Society, complete by November 1957, with further revisions in spring 1958. A fortnight before the premiere Vaughan Williams paid for a three-hour rehearsal, played the work through twice, and made minor adjustments. It was first performed on Wednesday 2 April 1958 at the Royal Festival Hall, London, at a Royal Philharmonic Society concert, the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra conducted by Sir Malcolm Sargent. Sargent conducted it again at the Proms on 5 August 1958, three weeks before Vaughan Williams's death. The forces are a normal orchestra plus two alto saxophones, a tenor saxophone and a flugelhorn. Four movements: Moderato maestoso, Andante sostenuto, Scherzo (Allegro pesante), Andante tranquillo; about thirty to forty minutes. It is the most enigmatic of the nine. Early sketches carried an explicit programme -- first a musical depiction of Salisbury, the Plain and Stonehenge, then an evocation of Thomas Hardy's Tess of the d'Urbervilles set in the same Wessex landscape, the first movement headed "Wessex Prelude" and "Tess" above sketches for the second. By completion he had deleted the programmatic details and insisted it was absolute music: "it started off with a programme, but it got lost on the journey -- so now, oh no, we never mention it -- and the music must be left to speak for itself". Analysts, Frogley among them, find the programme essentially unaltered in the score: the first movement opens with a held unison E in four octaves and the three saxophones in a solemn triadic theme; the second opens with a solo flugelhorn theme -- Vaughan Williams said it was "borrowed from an early work of the composer's, luckily long since scrapped" -- then a barbaric march and a gong stroke recalling the theme, heard as wind through Stonehenge, Tess, the pursuing constabulary, her arrest, the bell striking eight before her hanging; the third is a heavy Scherzo, 6/8 against 2/4, ending quietly on solo B-flat saxophone and side drum; the fourth ends on an E major triad, fortissimo but fading to silence. His programme note joked about the flugelhorn -- "banished to the brass band, where it is allowed to indulge in the bad habit of vibrato... While in the orchestra it will be obliged to sit up and play straight" -- and the saxophones -- "not expected, except possibly in one place in the scherzo, to behave like demented cats, but are allowed to be their own romantic selves". The flugelhorn theme is drawn from an unpublished tone poem, The Solent (1903), also used in A Sea Symphony. [C34572]

First reception was respectful but cool; critics fastened on the flugelhorn instead of the work. Its reputation grew from the mid-1990s, once the deleted programme became widely known. The Times in 2008 called it "the synthesis and summation of all that had gone before", "visionary, violent, elusive and ambiguous". Boult, preparing the first commercial recording in August 1958, thought the ending too abrupt; Vaughan Williams said he could play it "a good deal slower" while considering adding twenty or thirty bars -- he died before deciding. It closes the symphonic arc that began in 1903: E minor, the key of the Sixth, returns as the final key. He died on Tuesday 26 August 1958, a few months after the Ninth's premiere, still composing, with an unfinished cello concerto and a new opera, Thomas the Rhymer, on his desk; he was working on the piano score of Thomas the Rhymer the day before he died. [C34573]

**Takeaway:** the last symphony offers a programme and then deletes it -- Tess, Stonehenge, the bell striking eight -- and insists the music speak for itself, while the saxophones and the flugelhorn say it anyway. The refusal is part of the work. [C34574]

## 21. The Pilgrim's Progress: forty-five years of Bunyan

The Pilgrim's Progress is the work of a lifetime, in stages over forty-five years. In 1906 he wrote incidental music for an amateur dramatisation at Reigate Priory, in December 1906, repeated in London in March 1907 -- his first Bunyan music, using the Scottish hymn tune "York" to open and close. In 1921-22 came the one-act "Pastoral Episode", The Shepherds of the Delectable Mountains, later Act 4, Scene 2. From 1925 he worked intermittently on the full-scale opera, Acts 1 and 2 finished over the next decade. In 1940 he wrote a motet on Mr. Valiant-for-Truth's speech; in 1942 BBC incidental music for a radio dramatisation; in 1943 he resumed work in earnest and finished in 1948. Themes from the opera were salvaged into the Fifth Symphony (1938-43) while the opera was believed unfinishable. The libretto is by Vaughan Williams himself, with interpolations from the Bible and text from his second wife Ursula Wood; he renamed the hero from Bunyan's "Christian" to "Pilgrim" to universalise the message -- "I want the work to be universal and apply to anybody who aims at the spiritual life whether he is Xtian Jew, Buddhist, Shintoist or 5th day Adventist", in a letter to Rutland Boughton in May 1951. He called it a "Morality", not an opera, but insisted it was a stage piece, not a church piece. It was first performed on Thursday 26 April 1951 at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, as part of the Festival of Britain, Leonard Hancock conducting -- Vaughan Williams's personal choice -- Nevill Coghill directing; Pilgrim, Arnold Matters; John Bunyan, Inia Te Wiata; Evangelist, Norman Walker; Apollyon, Michael Langdon. It runs about two and a half hours, with forty-one individual singing roles, a Prologue, four acts and an Epilogue, and opens and closes on the "York" psalm tune in sonorous G major brass, Bunyan in Bedford Gaol reading his book to the audience. The action follows Bunyan's allegory as a spiritual quest: the Prologue, Bunyan in prison, Pilgrim appearing with his burden; Act 1, the Wicket Gate, the House Beautiful, the burden lifted at the Cross; Act 2, the Arming of the Pilgrim and single combat with Apollyon; Act 3, Vanity Fair -- "I buy the truth!" -- trial, imprisonment, escape by the Key of Promise; Act 4, the Shepherds of the Delectable Mountains, the River of Death, the Celestial City; the Epilogue, Bunyan again, holding out his book. Musically it summarises "in three hours virtually the whole creative output of a great composer", in Herbert Murrill's phrase: folk-song melody, hymn tunes, Tudor polyphony, the modal language of the symphonies, and the Pilgrim themes already heard in the Fifth. The criticism from the start was preponderance of slow music and stretches lacking dramatic action; the defence is that slowness is the point -- contemplation as drama. The Covent Garden run was not a success; the company dropped it in 1952, revived it the following year, still without great success; Vaughan Williams called it "a flop" and "The Pilgrim is dead and that's that." A Cambridge student production in 1954, with John Noble as Pilgrim, pleased him more. The North American premiere was on 28 April 1969 at Brigham Young University -- an Alchetron mirror gives 1968, a split carried here, with 1969 used. Revivals followed: Charterhouse 1972 for the centenary, the Royal Northern College of Music 1992, Hickox semi-stagings 1997-2008, English National Opera in November 2012. It has never entered the permanent repertory, but it is increasingly seen as one of his crowning achievements. Adeline Vaughan Williams died in 1951, the same year as the premiere. [C34575]

**Takeaway:** forty-five years on one book, read to him as a child in the late 1870s, and the opera is a flop on its own stage -- and still the crowning achievement, because the slowness is the drama and the burden is lifted at the Cross, not by a plot twist. [C34576]

## 22. The choral voice: Sea, Sancta Civitas, Serenade, Flos Campi

Vaughan Williams treats the chorus as an orchestral section and the orchestra as a vocal partner, and he uses three distinct choral dispositions. First, full chorus plus soloists as the symphonic body: in A Sea Symphony the chorus sings throughout all four movements, not only in the finale, and the word-setting is mostly syllabic and conjunct, with large leaps reserved for emphasis. Second, wordless chorus as pure timbre: in Flos Campi (1925) a small chorus sings without words behind the solo viola; in the Pastoral Symphony finale a wordless soprano sings a pentatonic lament over a soft drumroll; in Sinfonia antartica wordless female voices with bells evoke Antarctic emptiness. Third, stratified choruses at different distances: in Sancta Civitas (1923-25, first performed Friday 7 May 1926 at the Sheldonian Theatre, Oxford, Sir Hugh Allen conducting, the General Strike in progress on its fourth day) there are three strata -- full chorus and orchestra, a semi-chorus of twenty voices, and a distant chorus, preferably boys' voices, preceded by a trumpet motif -- each placed to create a spatial, visionary perspective on the Revelation text. Sancta Civitas runs about thirty to thirty-five minutes as a single continuous piece, with tenor and baritone soloists, text entirely in English, mainly from the Book of Revelation, and headed by a quotation in Greek from Plato's Phaedo: "It is worthwhile to stake everything on this belief." The baritone acts as narrator; there are wordless choral interjections and stark silences. It is far removed from the nineteenth-century oratorio: no narrative-driven drama, but a continuous thirty-minute meditation, with dissonant climaxes for the destruction of Babylon -- graphic enough, Faber notes, to have given a hint to Walton for Belshazzar's Feast. Michael Kennedy called it "in the form of a homage to Bach from the twentieth century". Late in life Vaughan Williams called it the favourite of his choral works. Its composition dating carries a split -- the article text says 1923-25, its infobox 1925-1926, Faber "probably 1924-5" -- and this study uses 1923-25. [C34577]

Serenade to Music (1938) is the fourth disposition, solo voices as named individuals: completed in 1938 for and dedicated to Sir Henry Wood on his jubilee, fifty years as a professional conductor, with text adapted from Act V, Scene I of Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice, the discussion of music and the music of the spheres -- "How sweet the moonlight sleeps upon this bank!" It was first performed on Wednesday 5 October 1938 at the Royal Albert Hall, London, at the Henry Wood Jubilee Concert, Wood conducting, with sixteen soloists; Rachmaninov played his Second Piano Concerto in the first half and, hearing the Serenade from the audience, wept. The sixteen solo parts were written for specific voices, and the published score prints each singer's initials beside their lines. The voices sometimes sing together as a choir in up to twelve parts, sometimes as individual solos; it is a single continuous lyrical span, not a set of numbers: the orchestra provides a moonlit cushion, the voices enter one by one as if the stars were speaking in turn, then join. He later made versions for four soloists plus choir and orchestra, for choir and orchestra, for choir and piano, and for solo violin and orchestra. Flos Campi, completed 1925 and first performed on Saturday 10 October 1925 at Queen's Hall, London, Sir Henry Wood's Saturday afternoon symphony concert, with Lionel Tertis, the dedicatee, on viola, is a suite for solo viola, small chorus and small orchestra. Its title is Latin for "flower of the field", the Vulgate equivalent of "Rose of Sharon" from Song of Solomon 2:1, not "buttercups and daisies", as Vaughan Williams irritably pointed out in a 1927 programme note. Six movements played without pause, each headed by a Latin verse from the Song of Solomon, to be read, not sung; the chorus sings vowels only -- the voice as another instrument. The opening bars, a duet between solo viola and oboe, are a classic example of bitonality. The viola is the voice of the lover, languishing; the final movement is a D-major benediction, which Howells called "a diatonic fulfillment of longing". Holst, a close friend, said he "couldn't get hold of it" -- and was disappointed more with himself than with the work. The personal paradox runs through all of it: Vaughan Williams was a "cheerful agnostic", in Ursula's phrase, yet his largest sacred and visionary choral works are among his most personal. [C34578]

**Takeaway:** the voice is never decoration -- it is either the argument, as in the Sea Symphony, a colour with no words, as in Flos Campi and the Pastoral finale, or a distant vision, as in Sancta Civitas. Choosing which of the three is a structural decision made before a note is written. [C34579]

## 23. Five Variants: memory, not development

Five Variants of Dives and Lazarus was composed in 1939, commissioned by the British Council for the 1939 New York World's Fair, and based on the English folk song "Dives and Lazarus", from Luke 16:19-31, the rich man and the beggar. Vaughan Williams first heard the song in 1893 or 1898 -- his own accounts differ, and the split is his, not the sources': his Musical Autobiography says 1893, his 1940 essay "Let us Remember... Early Days" says 1898. He felt "that sense of recognition -- here's something which I have known all my life, only I didn't know it!" He transcribed several versions during his collecting; the Kingsfold, near Horsham, version heard in 1904 became the hymn tune Kingsfold in The English Hymnal (1906, No. 574, "I Heard the Voice of Jesus Say"), and he quoted the melody in the first movement of the English Folk Song Suite (1923). It was first performed on Saturday 10 June 1939 at Carnegie Hall, New York, the New York Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra conducted by Sir Adrian Boult -- originally intended for the Fair's Hall of Music, moved to Carnegie Hall by a budget deficit and lack of public interest, during a heat wave, with no air conditioning; the programme claimed a dedication "To the People of the United States of America", which does not appear in the printed score. The first British performance was on Wednesday 1 November 1939 at Colston Hall, Bristol, Boult and the BBC Symphony Orchestra, evacuated there for the war, broadcast. The forces are string orchestra and harp, about thirteen minutes: Introduction and Theme, then five variants moving between B modal minor and D modal minor. The variants are, in Vaughan Williams's words, "not exact replicas of traditional tunes, but rather reminiscences of various versions in my own collection and those of others". The tune is stated in rich harmonies, then varied by mode, metre and texture: Variant I in triple time with harps providing a running commentary; Variant II quicker, in three-bar phrases; Variant III a new modal version in D minor, solo violin and harp, followed by a slower F minor variation; Variant IV stately in 2/4; Variant V the version collected in 1905 to words about Maria Martin, the Red Barn victim. James Day called it "a cross between a folk song arrangement and original composition" and compared it favourably to the Tallis Fantasia; he suggested it may have influenced the opening of the Sixth Symphony. It was played at his funeral in Westminster Abbey in 1958, Boult conducting; Kennedy wrote, "It was as if Vaughan Williams himself had spoken." In 1949 he adapted it, with Doreen Carwithen, for Humphrey Jennings's documentary Dim Little Island. [C34580]

**Takeaway:** a variant is not a variation -- it is a tune as remembered, misremembered and reshaped by transmission, with small melodic differences no single author owns. Compose as if remembering, and the tune stops being yours. [C34581]

## 24. Modal harmony: the engine

Vaughan Williams builds harmony from the church modes -- Dorian, Mixolydian, Aeolian and Phrygian -- rather than from major-minor key function. A mode is a diatonic scale rotated so a different degree is the centre: Dorian is a minor scale with a raised sixth; Mixolydian is a major scale with a flattened seventh; Aeolian is the natural minor; Phrygian is a minor scale with a flattened second, third, sixth and seventh. Because the mode has no raised leading note, there is no strong dominant-to-tonic pull. Cadences are therefore weak, sideways or plagal in feeling: chords move by step or by third rather than by the V-I snap of common-practice tonality. Flattened sevenths are the chief audible marker -- Howes notes they "became positively fashionable" in this music -- and the Phrygian flat second gives an exotic, archaic colour. Triads are often juxtaposed whole, unrelated by function, rather than resolved. In the Tallis Fantasia, Tallis's tune is in the Phrygian mode, white notes starting on E, and the Fantasia keeps that flat second, third, sixth and seventh throughout, so the harmony never behaves like E minor in the textbook sense. In The Lark Ascending the opening orchestral re-entry sits over an E pedal and uses the pitch collection of D major re-centred on E, giving E Dorian: the raised sixth, C sharp, is the Dorian marker, and the solo violin deliberately omits C sharp, using a hexatonic and pentatonic set, so the music does not tip over into D major. In On Wenlock Edge the songs move between Dorian -- D Dorian at the opening of "Is my team ploughing" -- Mixolydian in "Bredon Hill", and pentatonic collections, with ambiguous tonality throughout. David Manning's thesis (Cardiff, 2003) explicitly notes that his music is "not tonal or atonal, nor does it stand on any historical path between these two thoroughly theorised principles", and that no single theoretical model fits it. There is no codified system, no serialism, no twelve-tone writing, no atonal system: he absorbed dissonance in the Fourth and Sixth Symphonies by germ-motive concentration and modal alteration, not by a row or a system, and no source claims otherwise. [C34582]

**Takeaway:** mode is not decoration here. It is the engine that removes forward harmonic drive and replaces it with hovering, circling, landscape-like stasis. Pick a mode, delete the leading note, and let chords move by step and by third. [C34583]

## 25. Parallel triads, pedals, and withheld notes

Vaughan Williams moves whole triads in parallel -- all voices stepping in the same direction by the same interval, real parallel harmony -- or staying inside the mode while stepping together, tonal parallel harmony. In tonal parallel harmony the chord qualities change as the line stays diatonic, so some voices move by semitone and others by tone; the result is an undulating mass rather than a functional progression. He also uses consecutive, parallel, perfect fifths openly -- the interval classical part-writing forbids because two voices fuse and lose independence. Here the fusion is the point: the bass gains a strong, archaic line, and the harmony becomes a block that slides. Open voicings emphasise the parallel fifth between the outer or inner voices; closed voicings, harmonic planing, compress the same triad into a dense block, often in oboes and horns. Howes calls the parallel consecutive fifths in the Tallis Fantasia "a sign manual of the composer's style". The Ravel study of winter 1907-08 is one source of the licence: Ravel taught him to move chords as block units and to orchestrate "in points of colour rather than in lines". In the Tallis Fantasia the opening statement harmonises Tallis's tune in block triads with parallel fifths in the bass, and the "swaying chord" motif that interrupts the theme is a parallel-chord refrain. In The Lark Ascending, after the opening cadenza, the strings move in first-inversion parallel triads over an E pedal; at bars 51-55 a root-position parallel passage shifts from C Mixolydian to C major by raising B flat to B natural, turning tonal parallelism into real parallelism for one pivot. A pedal is a single sustained bass note held while the harmony above it changes, and Vaughan Williams uses it to anchor a tonal centre that the upper parts refuse to confirm: without the pedal, a passage whose pitch content is D major could be heard as D major; with an E pedal held underneath, the ear is forced to hear the same notes as E Dorian. The pedal therefore both anchors and imposes the centre. A second, related device is withholding: leaving out the note that would settle the mode -- typically the leading note or Dorian sixth, C sharp in E Dorian -- so the solo line stays pentatonic or hexatonic and cannot pull toward the major tonic. A third device is modal interchange and modal modulation: keeping the same tonic but changing the mode, C Mixolydian to C Ionian by raising the seventh, or keeping the mode but moving the tonic, C Mixolydian to D Mixolydian. Climaxes are often built by one of these switches rather than by a dominant cadence. In the Pastoral Symphony's second movement, the natural-trumpet cadenza is played over a pedal note in the strings, so the out-of-tune seventh harmonic floats without harmonic resolution. All harmonic claims in this section rest on secondary analysis, not on a score reading by this study, and no source read gives a bar-by-bar account of the Tallis Fantasia's four episodes that agrees in every detail. [C34584]

**Takeaway:** take a triad, keep its shape, walk it up or down by step; hold one bass note, withhold one tendency note, and the music hovers instead of arriving. Planing is a way to make harmony move without arguing. [C34585]

## 26. Antiphony: three string bodies and the cathedral

Vaughan Williams divides the string orchestra into three spatially separated bodies that answer each other, imitating Tudor antiphonal church practice and the acoustic of a large stone building. In the Tallis Fantasia the three bodies are: Orchestra I, the main body, size not stipulated in the score; Orchestra II, a smaller orchestra of nine players -- two first violins, two seconds, two violas, two cellos, one double bass; and a solo string quartet drawn from the leaders of Orchestra I. The bodies are set apart in the hall or church. The music is built as dialogue, not as tutti-plus-solo: the large orchestra states, the small orchestra echoes or answers with the "swaying chord" motif, the quartet steps out as a single chanting voice and is then received back into the mass. Within each body the strings are further divided, divisi, into many parts, so a chord can be spread across eight or more real voices. Harp is used as a separate colour -- arpeggiated commentary, a rising figure at the close -- rather than as mere accompaniment. The premiere acoustic mattered mechanically: Gloucester Cathedral's long reverberation lets a pianissimo chord hang while the next body enters, so antiphony becomes overlapping resonance rather than clean call-and-response. The introduction is low pizzicato hints of the Tallis phrases under a sustained high violin note; the full theme is then given in Tallis's own nine-part harmonisation, with rich arpeggiation; the three bodies trade variants through four episodes and build to a single fortissimo climax before the tune returns quietly. Five Variants of Dives and Lazarus (1939) reuses the string-plus-harp scoring in a smaller form: seven-part strings and harp or harps, the harp initiating the theme and providing a "running commentary". The Lark Ascending uses a lighter version of the same thinking: solo violin against muted divided strings, with the soloist emerging from and dissolving back into the texture. [C34586]

**Takeaway:** space is an instrument here. Separating the bodies and letting the building blur them is a compositional decision, not a staging nicety -- the cathedral is in the score, not around it. [C34587]

## 27. The pastoral kit and the anonymous voice

"Pastoral" in Vaughan Williams is not a mood label; it is a small kit, and the parts can be listed: open fifths and bare triads with the third omitted or delayed; pedal points; high, muted, divided strings in long sustained lines, often pianissimo; a solo wind or solo string voice -- solo violin, solo viola, oboe, cor anglais, flute -- placed high above the texture, moving in free, bird-like arabesques, often in cadenza-like passages marked senza misura; pentatonic and modal melody with narrow range and stepwise motion; quiet dynamics, slow harmonic rhythm, and long silences between phrases, so the acoustic of the room becomes part of the sound. The Lark is the textbook assembly: woodwind and muted strings introduce, the solo violin enters pianissimo and sur la touche, for a veiled tone, with flurries that imitate skylark song, and the piece ends with the unaccompanied violin alone, "lost on his aerial rings". A Pastoral Symphony is the same kit turned elegiac: high muted violins, solo horn and cor anglais, a wordless soprano, and a final fading into silence. The pastoral kit is therefore also a war-landscape kit; the mechanism does not change, the referent does. The kit, the modes, the folk DNA and the hymn tunes add up to the effect listeners keep naming: the "anonymous" or impersonal voice, music that sounds older than its composer. The ingredients are modal harmony with no leading-note drive; melodies that obey folk-song habits; tunes presented as found material -- Tallis, Dives and Lazarus, a hymn tune -- rather than as personal invention; orchestration that favours blended string and choral mass over solo display; and forms -- fantasia, variants, hymn, pastoral -- that predate the composer by centuries. The result is music in which the composer's ego is deliberately hard to locate: the tune could be four hundred years old, the harmony could be Tudor, the voice could be a congregation. The impersonality is also a social choice -- music for a community, not for a virtuoso -- and not only a stylistic one: he was a late developer who did not find his true voice until his late thirties, and he sought, in his own phrase, to be of service and to make music available to everybody. One caution is carried with the term: "anonymous" is a critical description, not a claim Vaughan Williams made about himself in those words; no source read attributes the term to him. The symphony, meanwhile, is treated as a container for a place -- sea, city, countryside, Antarctica, each rendered by a concrete sonic emblem -- and closed not with a triumphant peroration but with an epilogue, a quiet return of opening material that reframes everything that came before. [C34588]

The aesthetic logic, stated plainly, is this: take a tune that sounds found, put it in a mode that will not resolve, spread it across bodies of strings that answer each other in a stone building, and end quietly, as if the music had been there before you arrived and will still be there after you leave. The refusal of programme is part of the same logic -- the Fourth, the Sixth and the Ninth are insisted on as absolute music even when the sketches carry Tess and Stonehenge, even when the critics hear fascism and atomic war -- because a programme would give the music an author and a date, and the whole project is to make music that has neither. That is why the late style can be violent and still sound like Vaughan Williams: the Fourth's germ motives, the Sixth's drifting epilogue and the Ninth's saxophones are new engines on the same chassis, and the chassis is the mode, the tune, the antiphony and the quiet ending. He was a "cheerful agnostic" who wrote the century's most personal sacred music, who conducted an amateur festival at Leith Hill from 1905 to 1953 and wrote film music at sixty-eight because the war effort needed it. The service ethic and the anonymity are the same decision made twice: music for everybody, by nobody in particular. [C34589]

**Takeaway:** pastoral is reproducible because it is a parts list, not a feeling -- mute the strings, hold a pedal, put one high solo voice on top, withhold the leading note -- and the anonymity is the point: a tune that sounds four hundred years old does not need its composer, and that is why it survives him. [C34590]

## 28. Death, ashes, and the institutions

He was working on the piano score of Thomas the Rhymer the day before he died, and he had gone to bed on the evening of 25 August contemplating a recording session for the Ninth Symphony the next day, with Ursula at his side. He died suddenly in the early hours of Tuesday 26 August 1958 at 10 Hanover Terrace, aged eighty-five -- he would have turned eighty-six that October -- "having been in excellent health", and the Cambridge obituary in the Musical Times says "The end came peacefully and suddenly. He spent his last day working in his study." Two days later, after a private funeral at Golders Green, he was cremated. On Friday 19 September 1958, at a crowded memorial service, his ashes were interred near the burial plots of Purcell and Stanford in the north choir aisle of Westminster Abbey; the Abbey's own site confirms the ashes of Ralph and his second wife Ursula are buried there, and the slate gravestone was originally cut by Reynolds Stone and re-cut in 1965. Two splits ride on the Abbey service and are carried, not settled: some popular pieces describe the 19 September service as his actual funeral and claim he was "the first commoner to be buried there for almost 300 years", against Wikipedia and the RVW Society timeline, which call it a memorial service following a private funeral and cremation; and Britain Unlimited places the interment in "The Nave", against Wikipedia, the Abbey itself and the Mahler Foundation, which all say the north choir aisle. Five Variants of Dives and Lazarus was the first work performed after his ashes were placed, Boult conducting. He accepted the Order of Merit in 1935, "the one state honour he accepted"; he refused a knighthood at least once and declined the post of Master of the King's Music after Elgar's death, preferring to remain "Dr Vaughan Williams". His other honours included an honorary Doctor of Music at Oxford in 1919, the Royal Philharmonic Society's Gold Medal in 1930, and the Albert Medal of the Royal Society of Arts in 1955. The Times' leading article after his death summed up his achievement as cutting "the bonds that from the times of Handel and Mendelssohn had bound England hand and foot to the Continent". [C34591]

The institutions he left behind are still the working shape of his legacy. In 1956 he set up and endowed the RVW Trust to support young composers and promote new or neglected music. After Ursula's death in 2007, the Vaughan Williams Charitable Trust was established under her will and became active in 2008. On Wednesday 12 October 2022 -- exactly one hundred and fifty years after his birth -- the two trusts were launched as the merged Vaughan Williams Foundation, becoming active on 1 January 2023; the Foundation represents the estates of Ralph and Ursula, and notes copyright in his music expires in 2028. The Ralph Vaughan Williams Society was founded in 1994, with Ursula as president until her death in October 2007; the Society sponsors performances including complete symphony cycles, publishes the RVW Journal three times a year, and established its own recording label, Albion Records, in 2007. He was president of the English Folk Dance and Song Society from 1932, in post at his death, and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library is named for him; his manuscripts were bequeathed by Ursula to the British Library; a collected edition of 757 of his letters was published by Oxford University Press in 2008. Posthumous reputation followed the usual arc: neglected in the 1960s as diatonic and melodic music fell from fashion, then reappraised in the twenty-first century; the 150th anniversary in 2022, RVW150, was celebrated nationally and internationally. There is a statue in Dorking and a bust by Marcus Cornish in Chelsea Embankment Gardens near Cheyne Walk. [C34592]

**Takeaway:** he died working, the day before a recording session, and left the money to other composers -- a trust, a society, a library, a foundation -- because the music was never really his: it belonged to the singers, the amateurs, the countryside, and the next generation. [C34593]
