# Reinhard Mucha: Study Notes

**Site:** Reinhard Mucha (b. 19 February 1950, Duesseldorf, Germany; living), German installation and sculpture artist working with found objects, industrial materials and photography around memory, nationhood and institutional power; studied at the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf under Klaus Rinke 1975-1982 (after a brief 1972 stint); lives and works in Duesseldorf and Saint-Haon-le-Vieux, France. Mucha was born in Duesseldorf in 1950; the year and place are agreed by every institutional source read, and the day and month, 19 February, are given by German Wikipedia and Lempertz while the museums and galleries give the year only, so the date is carried, not overstated. He studied at the Kunstakademie Duesseldorf in the class of Klaus Rinke in 1972 and then from 1975 to 1982, a split dating every source gives in the same form, "1972 / 1975-1982", with nothing in the sources explaining the brief 1972 entry or the three empty years that follow it. What he did before art school is a three-way split no source resolves: MoMA's label for Kalkar says he was trained as a mechanic, the WahooArt text says metalsmithing, and the e-flux review, describing documents Mucha himself filed in Schnee von gestern, reports notes and diagrams from an apprenticeship as a blacksmith -- the only one of the three grounded in documents the artist put on display. His master-class standing is evidenced by his own work rather than a CV line: Der kluge Knecht (2002, part of Frankfurter Block) is a wall-mounted display case holding his master class sculpture certificate from the Staatliche Kunstakademie Duesseldorf, notarized by the Duesseldorf Chamber of Craft and Trade and dated 1981. [C27733]

Since the early 1980s Mucha has had a studio in a 1908 former railway administration building in Duesseldorf, and the railway runs through the work from the start: while still a student, in 1979, he began the large archive of names that became Wartesaal, collecting the names of 242 German towns with railway stations, each name no more than six letters long, taken from a German railway booklet of 1943 revised in 1948. His practice from the late 1970s on is wall-mounted sculpture and room installation built from found materials and objects -- chairs, signs, windows, floorboards, salvaged doors -- encased in glass or set in felt-lined display cases, reworked and re-exhibited in new arrangements over decades rather than finished once. His first works of note come from the student years: for Kopfdiktate (1980) he combined old school exercise books with black-and-white photographs of himself as a young man, and Lempertz calls Kopf im Sand, small magazine coupons he filled in by hand, his first major work, made for the Ars Viva prizewinners show at Kunsthalle Bielefeld in 1981. Mucha works his objects rather than presenting them as found: unlike Duchamp, he does not rely on readymades but works each object until it matches his idea, and he keeps returning to the same works, adding elements and reinterpreting them as a puzzle to which new pieces are constantly added. Wartesaal itself carries a dating split: the core work is 1979-1982 everywhere and first shown in 1982, but Sprueth Magers brackets reworkings in 1986 and 1997 while Castello di Rivoli and e-flux record only the 1997 showing at documenta X, so the 1986 bracket is carried as Sprueth Magers' dating only. [C27734]

The breakthrough runs 1983 to 1987, all of it while he was still a student or just out of the academy. The Venice Biennale in 1990 is the international arrival: Das Deutschlandgeraet in the German Pavilion at the 44th Venice Biennale, exhibited together with the Duesseldorf photographer couple Hilla and Bernd Becher. The work takes its title from a patented mechanism for hoisting derailed trains back onto the tracks, and it was first shown a few months before German reunification. As a partial reconstruction it was adapted to the former plenary hall of the Staendehaus in 2002, expanded with a sound and video installation, and it has been on permanent display at Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen, K21, Duesseldorf, since 2002; the 2022 survey gave it a second technical and content-related adaptation. He took part in documenta in Kassel twice: at documenta IX in 1992 (13 June to 20 September 1992, artistic director Jan Hoet) he showed Dokumente I-IV, and at documenta X in 1997 he showed Wartesaal in its modified form. The twenty works inspected run 1978-2016 and trace one practice at growing scale, then turning back on itself. Early, 1978-1982: small and personal -- self-photographs with school punishments, a rejected library model built from found bicycles (Untitled (MILCH), 1979, 273.0 x 800.5 x 174.0 cm), and the waiting-room archive -- already using the vitrine, the found object and the photograph as documents. ## M1 -- Found-object assemblage

The work is built from used, worn things taken out of their ordinary jobs -- footstools, folding rulers, floorboards, doors, sinks, pool tables, bicycles, chairs, flooring felt -- and put together with wood, aluminium, float glass and felt into a new construction that keeps the wear visible. The watchable behaviour is recognition followed by estrangement: the viewer knows each thing at once, then cannot use it, because its function has been taken away and only its history is left on show. Seen in Baden-Baden (sink, piano model, congress-hall chairs), Flinger Broich (pool table parts), Untitled (MILCH) (found bicycles and cable reels) and Before the Wall came down (126 folding rulers, 2 footstools).

**Takeaway:** take used things out of service and show the wear; the object keeps its past and loses its job. [C27740]

## M2 -- The vitrine / cabinet

A glass case -- wall-mounted, floor-standing, hanging or low on the wall -- holds the found material behind float glass, usually with felt lining, aluminium profiles and enamel painted on the reverse of the glass. The watchable behaviour is looking through a window into a kept thing: the glass puts the object at museum distance, the felt and the frame make it precious, and the viewer reads the contents as evidence rather than as stuff. Seen in Mutterseelenallein (sixteen heavy cases hung low, lit by vertical neon), Das Deutschlandgeraet (27 showcases inside, 38 wall showcases outside), Der kluge Knecht (wall-mounted display case holding the academy certificate) and the recent Hidden Tracks vitrine works.

**Takeaway:** put it behind glass on felt; the case turns a used thing into evidence. [C27741]

## M3 -- Photography as document

Photographs are used as records, not as pictures: black-and-white chair photographs at the centre of the Mutterseelenallein cases, 30 dated self-photographs in Kopfdiktate, laser copies of a little boy on construction sites in Der Bau, video-animated photographs on monitors in Die Verwandlung, and still photographs of the original Venice installation in the 2002 version of Das Deutschlandgeraet. The watchable behaviour is the photograph testifying to an absence -- the empty chair, the boy on the building site, the earlier installation -- so the viewer looks at a document of something that is not in the room.

**Takeaway:** let the photograph stand in for what is missing; the image is a witness, not a decoration. [C27742]

## M4 -- The waiting room

A transit space is rebuilt as a room the viewer must wait in: Wartesaal holds 11 large filing cabinets with 22 individually labelled shelves each, containing 242 handmade replicas of train-station signs from German cities with six-letter names, while the visitor mostly cannot reach the signs and must make do with grimy station debris enshrined in surrounding vitrines. The watchable behaviour is blocked access producing inertia: the cabinets promise order and filing, the signs promise departure, and neither is delivered, so the room feels like navigating German bureaucracy. Eller Bahnhof works the same way at smaller scale -- a closed grey box with a footstool slid under a raised corner, a station name with no train.

**Takeaway:** build the waiting room and withhold the departure; the blocked archive is the experience. [C27743]

## M5 -- National-symbol reworking

A named piece of German industrial or national history is taken as the title and the mechanism: Das Deutschlandgeraet names the hydraulic device for righting derailed locomotives, built by the Maschinenfabrik Deutschland in Dortmund, and the installation answers reunification with a studio rebuilt in marble inside the German Pavilion; The Wirtschaftswunder, To the People of Pittsburgh III names the postwar economic miracle against deindustrialisation; Krupp names the steel firm as a stool-and-base sculpture. The watchable behaviour is the familiar national word turning strange: the device that puts trains back on track becomes a room that puts the artist's studio back inside the nation, and the viewer has to decide whether the nation is being repaired or exhibited.

**Takeaway:** name the national device and rebuild it as a room; the symbol does the work. [C27744]

## M6 -- The footstool as repeated unit

The small wooden footstool recurs as a standard unit, counted and repeated: 38 wooden footstools with 38 bronze casts and 38 steel measuring tapes in Das Deutschlandgeraet, a footstool slid under the raised corner of Eller Bahnhof, 2 footstools with graphite facing in Before the Wall came down, and footstools standing under the vitrine train in Frankfurter Block. The watchable behaviour is the humble object gaining weight by repetition: one footstool is furniture, thirty-eight in a row is a system, and the bronze cast next to the wooden original makes the copy and the thing sit side by side.

**Takeaway:** repeat the smallest piece until it becomes the system; the footstool is the unit of measure. [C27745]

## M7 -- The carousel and the futile circle

Office furniture is arranged in a circle that promises motion and delivers none: in Kasse beim Fahrer the chairs are set in a circular, dynamically staggered arrangement so the carousel seems to turn while standing still, and in Das Figur-Grund Problem the chairs and ladders are trussed into a Ferris wheel and a wall of death that never move. The watchable behaviour is the eye going round and arriving nowhere: the fairground attraction has solidified into sculpture, and the futile movement that only circles in on itself becomes the image of progress at a standstill.

**Takeaway:** arrange the furniture in a circle and let it not move; the stalled ride is the argument. [C27746]

## M8 -- Self-quotation and reworking

Older works are revisited, rebuilt and re-dated rather than left finished: Wartesaal carries the dates 1979-1982 with later versions in 1986 and 1997, Das Deutschlandgeraet carries 1990 with adaptations in 2002 and 2021, Kasse beim Fahrer returns as version 0.2 in 2022, Frankfurter Block gathers works from 1981 to 2014 into one ensemble, and Die Verwandlung rebuilds Mutterseelenallein as a miniature in 2016. The watchable behaviour is the work visibly carrying its own history: the bracketed dates in the title are part of the piece, and each new showing adds a layer instead of replacing the last one.

**Takeaway:** rebuild the old work and date the rebuild; the bracketed dates are the work. [C27747]

## M9 -- The museum as material

The museum itself -- its walls, its display furniture, its storage -- is cut into and rebuilt: in Stockholmer Raum rectangular apertures are cut into three temporary museum walls and the cut-outs are reassembled into the white box of Eller Bahnhof, and in Frankfurter Block a demountable museum space (Gallery 4.1) is installed as part of the work, while Der kluge Knecht puts the artist's own academy certificate and a Grimm fairy tale into a wall case. The watchable behaviour is the display apparatus becoming visible: the viewer sees the wall that was cut, the case that holds the certificate, and the museum's own furniture doing the exhibiting.

**Takeaway:** cut the museum wall and show the cut; the display system is the material. [C27748]

## M10 -- Time as transformation

Works are built to change between showings rather than to stay fixed: Mutterseelenallein grows and is transformed over time, gaining wooden walls in Frankfurt and a heaped pile of leftover parquet at Castello di Rivoli, and Wartesaal and Das Deutschlandgeraet are re-made for each new room, so the work marks the rhythm of its own exhibition history. The watchable behaviour is the piece refusing to be the same object twice: the viewer who saw it in Naples meets a different arrangement in Frankfurt and again in Turin, and the changes themselves are the record.

**Takeaway:** let the work change at every showing; the transformation is the record. [C27749]

## Aesthetic logic

Mucha's logic is the archive made sculptural: found objects, photographs and institutional furniture are taken out of use, put behind glass or into cabinets, counted and repeated, and set in rooms that promise transit, display or progress and then withhold it. The materials are those of a bygone everyday -- bitumen-coated felt flooring, aluminium profiles, fluorescent tubes, enamel on glass -- and the scale runs from a single vitrine to a room-filling installation, while the titles do the naming work: a waiting room, a Germany device, an economic miracle, a hashtag with a bright spot in pain. The practice, as the galleries and museums describe it in agreement, uses the ground zero of sculpture after minimalism as a conceptual springboard; strips found objects of their practical purpose to expose the sediments of time and lived history; and returns across decades to the same works, reworking and re-presenting them so that each new showing absorbs more time and history. Against Rueckriem's stone, which is divided and put back exactly, Mucha's answer is a room that is taken apart and filed: the block is replaced by the cabinet, the seam by the glass, and the quarry by the station, the museum and the studio floor. [C27750]
