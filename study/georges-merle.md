# Georges Merle: Study Notes

**Site:** Jean Adrien Georges Merle (1851-1886), French academic painter -- born in Paris on Friday 18 April 1851 at 9 rue de la Grange-aux-Belles, the son of the painter Francois Hippolyte Hugues Merle (1822 or 1823-1881) and Victorine Adelaide Delplanque, legitimised at his parents' marriage on Saturday 7 July 1866 at Saint-Maur-des-Fosses; died at his home in the rue de Lisbonne on Thursday 6 May 1886, aged 35 years and 18 days, and buried on Saturday 8 May at Pere-Lachaise, in his father's grave. Pupil of his father and of William-Adolphe Bouguereau (#499); assistant in his father's studio, which specialised in genre paintings and depictions of female beauties; studio at 63 rue de Lisbonne, next to his father's at no. 55 of the same street. He exhibited at the Paris Salon in 8 of the 11 years from 1876 to 1886, ten pictures in all: debut at 25 with Le Pas d'Armes de l'Arbre-d'Or, a Burgundian tournament of 1463, and last entry in the year he died, Porte du Grand-Valene, a Etretat. No medals, no honours, no teaching, no dealer relationship is recorded for him in any source read. His output has no catalogue raisonne and no counted total: this study tallies 17 individually named dated works, of which exactly one sits in a public collection -- Une sorciere au XVe siecle (L'Envouteuse / The Sorceress, 1883, Birmingham Museum of Art, Alabama). The signature works are Le Pas d'Armes de l'Arbre-d'Or (1876), Faust et les trois vaillants (1877), Mort de Philippe Arteveld (1878), Contraste (1880), La fille du pecheur, Etretat (1880), Venus (1882), L'Envouteuse (1883), Femme a l'oiseau bleu (1884), Apres la surprise, XVIe siecle (1885) and Porte du Grand-Valene, a Etretat (1886). The contemporary verdict on him, such as it is, is a single French Wikipedia sentence: a painter of remarkable skill, particularly appreciated for history subjects of Pre-Raphaelite inspiration, sublimating women above all in his neo-Gothic and neo-Renaissance compositions -- and that sentence is itself single-sourced and flagged as insufficiently sourced since January 2019. After his death he fell further than his father: Hugues at least has Rehs, Bearded Roman, the Clark catalogue and Vottero's 2011 article, while Georges has no monograph, no exhibition, no catalogue raisonne and no modern scholarly article found in 65 searches. The fullest treatments are a 172-line French Wikipedia article and a 16-line English stub. The Italian Wikipedia, in its Hugues Merle article, gives the whole afterlife in one clause: he "ebbe assai meno successo di suo padre" -- had much less success than his father. This file is plain ASCII by house rule, so the French words in it are written without their accents here.

## M1. The Crouched Sorceress: The Ritual Still Life as the Picture (L'Envouteuse, 1883)
The figure is painted crouched low, at the centre of a ring of charged objects, so that the picture reads as an inventory of a spell in progress rather than as a portrait. The woman is one object among objects. She holds a forked staff in her right hand and a sculpted hairpin in the left; another pin is stuck through the chest of a doll or effigy lying at her feet; a magical symbol is suggested painted on the floor; she is flanked by a lit pillar candle on her right and a table with a skull sitting in a metal basin on the left; the hilt of a long sword rests against the table (Bhamwiki description). The picture is "likely to have been inspired by an as-yet unidentified work of literature" (Bhamwiki) -- the spell has a source the viewer cannot name. **Takeaway:** build the scene as a ring of props around a still, crouched figure, and light the props one by one -- candle, symbol, skull, sword -- while the woman never moves; the watchable behaviour is the spell assembling itself around her.

## M2. The Eyes That Follow (L'Envouteuse, 1883)
A frontal female gaze painted with centred pupils and a level head, so that the stare appears to track the viewer across the room. This is the only first-hand viewing testimony found for any Georges Merle picture: Birmingham Museum of Art staff (Kate Tully Delgreco) on the Sorceress's "captivating eyes, which follow you as you pass the painting." The head does not turn; the gaze does. **Takeaway:** hold one pair of eyes fixed on the viewer while everything else in the frame stays still, and let the viewer's own movement do the work; the watchable behaviour is the stare that stays locked as the viewer passes, the watched becoming the watcher.

## M3. Light Against Shadow: The Two Couples (Contraste, 1880)
A single canvas split into a lit half and a shadowed half, each holding a couple from the same period, so that the lighting itself states the moral contrast. The title names the device. On the left, in the light, a couple amused, almost improper; on the right, in shadow, a couple holding each other by the arm, a child holding its mother's right hand (fr.wikipedia note; the picture sold at Christie's London in 1998 under the title "The troubadour"). **Takeaway:** split one frame into a bright half and a dark half with a couple in each, and let the light/shadow boundary drift across the canvas, so first one couple and then the other is the bright one and the "proper" and "improper" readings swap places.

## M4. The Quoted Text as Caption (Le Pas d'Armes de l'Arbre-d'Or, 1876; Faust et les trois vaillants, 1877; Mort de Philippe Arteveld, 1878)
Each history picture is exhibited with a printed literary or chronicle quotation in the Salon livret, so the picture is presented as the illustration of a named passage, not as a free invention. The debut quotes Olivier de La Marche, Memoires, II, ch. IV (the pas d'armes given at Bruges in May 1463 for the marriage of Charles le Temeraire to Marguerite d'York); Faust quotes Goethe, Faust, Part 2, Act 4 -- Mephistopheles presenting three warriors of different ages, dress and armour to Faust; Arteveld quotes Juvenal des Ursins, Histoire de Charles VI (the young King Charles VI asking whether Arteveld is dead, a wounded Flemish captain leading them to the body). Three of his first four Salon pictures carry such a quotation; his father's Salon pictures are not recorded as doing this. **Takeaway:** let the quotation appear first as text, then let the scene assemble itself out of it -- tournament, three warriors, the body on the battlefield -- as if the words were the instruction for the picture.

## M5. The Etretat Double Register: Men Talk, Women Watch (Etretat, conversation de pecheurs, 1878; La fille du pecheur, Etretat, 1880; La femme du pecheur et son fils a la longue vue, 1883; Porte du Grand-Valene, a Etretat, 1886)
The same Normandy fishing village painted in two alternating casts -- working men in conversation, and women with children looking out to sea. The fishermen talk in a small signed cabinet picture (54 x 45 cm, 1878); the fisherman's daughter stands life-size (115 x 80 cm, Salon 1880, no. 2788); the fisherman's wife and her son watch with a telescope (56 x 46 cm, signed and dated 1883); the last Salon picture, in the year he died, is the named coastal gate at Etretat (Salon 1886, no. 3129). The telescope in the 1883 picture is the hinge of the mechanism. Guy de Maupassant, in his Chroniques, speaks of Georges as a habitue of Etretat -- a claim resting on fr.wikipedia's paraphrase, the Maupassant text itself not located (see C9). **Takeaway:** run two scenes side by side -- the men's talk going on in the foreground while, beside it, a woman raises a telescope and the view cuts to the empty sea she is scanning; waiting is the action.

## M6. The Wide Canvas of Surprise (Apres la surprise, XVIe siecle, 1885)
A panorama format -- nearly two metres wide against less than one metre high (94 x 190 cm, Salon 1885, no. 1736) -- used for a single moment of shock, so the surprise has to travel sideways across the picture. It is the widest picture in his list by a large margin; the next widest is Contraste at 91 x 72 cm. **Takeaway:** stretch one startled reaction across a long horizontal field and let it ripple from one end to the other, figures turning in sequence like a wave, so the surprise itself is the motion.

## M7. The Single Life-Size Woman (Venus, 1882; Femme a l'oiseau bleu, 1884)
A single female figure, painted at life size on a tall canvas (roughly 112-116 cm high by 81 cm wide), filling the frame alone, with no narrative, no companion and no setting to dilute her. Venus (116 x 81 cm) and Femme a l'oiseau bleu (112 x 81 cm, signed and dated) are the two largest female-figure formats in the list. This is the "outstanding command of the female form" of the English Wikipedia lead, and the one place where the son most directly continues the father's studio manner -- Hugues's studio "specialized in genre paintings and depictions of female beauties" (Bhamwiki) -- while dropping the father's sentiment and moral. Neither picture was a Salon entry; both were painted for the market, not the Salon (see C2). **Takeaway:** put one woman alone in a tall frame, slowly turning or shifting her weight, the blue bird as the single moving accent; the watchable behaviour is a solitary figure holding a tall canvas by herself.

## M8. The Pre-Raphaelite History Woman: Neo-Gothic and Neo-Renaissance Dress (Le Pas d'Armes de l'Arbre-d'Or, 1876; Une ligueuse, 1879; Au XVe siecle, courtisan et sa belle dans un paysage boise, 1886)
History painting in which the vehicle is not the battle or the king but the woman, costumed in researched late-medieval or Renaissance dress, idealised in the Pre-Raphaelite manner -- the fr.wikipedia characterisation, the only critical sentence found about him: history subjects "d'inspiration pre-raphaelite, sublimant notamment les femmes dans ses compositions neo-gothiques et neo-Renaissance". The Burgundian court of 1463, a woman of the Catholic League, a fifteenth-century courtier and his beauty in a wooded landscape: the father painted literary couples in contemporary dress and moralised the present; the son puts the woman in period dress and lets the costume carry the history. **Takeaway:** move a woman in heavy period fabric -- brocade, jewel, veil -- slowly through a wooded or court setting, the dress doing the period work while her face stays modern and direct.

## M9. Two Scales: Cabinet Panel Versus Salon Machine (Nicolas le Flamand, 1883; Venus, 1882; Apres la surprise, 1885)
The same hand working at two radically different scales -- small signed cabinet pictures (oil on panel or small canvas, 35 x 26 to 56 x 46 cm) against large Salon statements (91 x 72 to 116 x 81 cm, and the 94 x 190 cm panorama). The small ones are signed and dated: Nicolas le Flamand (oil on panel, 35 x 26 cm, the smallest picture in the list), Etretat, conversation de pecheurs (54 x 45 cm), La femme du pecheur et son fils a la longue vue (56 x 46 cm). The large ones mostly are not recorded as signed -- the signature lives on the cabinet pictures. **Takeaway:** show the same subject first as a small panel and then growing to Salon scale, the composition stretching and the figures gaining room to move; the watchable behaviour is the slow zoom from the miniature to the monumental.

## M10. The Unsigned Hand: The Son in the Father's Studio (the early pictures generally)
Apprenticeship inside the family workshop, painting in the father's manner without signing, so that authorship itself is the unstable element. "As Georges rarely signed his early pieces, several of his early works were at first mistakenly attributed to his father" (en.wikipedia, citing the Birmingham Museum of Art guide, 2010, p. 225). Bhamwiki: he "assisted in his father's studio ... before painting under his own name"; his studio at 63 rue de Lisbonne stood next to his father's at no. 55. The mechanism is evidenced by the market, not by a single canvas: no signed early work is identified in any source, and no source names a single reattributed picture. The confusion persists in the trade today -- Christie's catalogued L'Envouteuse as "Georges Hugues Merle" and Invaluable files it under Hugues Merle's artist page, so the son's one famous picture is still filed under his father (see C5 and C6). **Takeaway:** let a picture slowly resolve from "father" to "son" -- the signature "GEORGES. MERLE" appearing in the corner, the handling shifting from the father's sentimental softness to the son's harder period surface -- while the viewer is asked which Merle painted it.

## Evolution
The sources do NOT support a phased division of Georges's career. No source read divides his work into periods, and the career is short -- Salon debut at 25, dead at 35, ten exhibiting years, seventeen named works. What can be said, from the dated list alone, is a drift rather than phases. The drift below is the bio leg's, built from the dated list; the main leg's four movements are the same drift with different joints, and the two agree on the shape.

Apprentice and studio-assistant years, before 1876, undocumented. He worked in his father's studio; the unsigned early pictures of the misattribution story belong here, but NONE is named in any source read, so nothing concrete can be said about them. His father is building his Salon career through these years; the family addresses move (rue de Lancry 53 in the early 1850s, rue Racine 3 from spring 1857, rue de Fleurus 26 in 1866, rue de Lisbonne from 1874 -- study #500).

Public debut, literary-historical set-pieces, 1876-1879. The first four Salon entries are all large literary or historical subjects with quoted texts in the livret -- a Burgundian tournament (1463), Goethe's Faust, the death of Arteveld (1382), Shakespeare's Timon of Athens -- plus a League woman. This is the academic history-painting programme, learned from his father and Bouguereau, but with medieval and Renaissance subjects rather than his father's sentimental genre. He is 25-28.

Etretat and the female figure, 1878-1886, interleaved. From 1878 on, fishermen and fishermen's families at Etretat run alongside the history pictures (Etretat, conversation de pecheurs, 1878; La fille du pecheur, 1880; La femme du pecheur, 1883; Porte du Grand-Valene, 1886). The two strands alternate rather than succeed each other; there is no point at which the history pictures stop and the Etretat pictures start.

Neo-Gothic and neo-Renaissance women, 1880-1886. Contraste (1880), Venus (1882), Une sorciere au XVe siecle (1883), Femme a l'oiseau bleu (1884), Apres la surprise, XVIe siecle (1885), Courtisan et sa belle (1886). This is the mature manner fr.wikipedia describes: history subjects of Pre-Raphaelite inspiration, women idealised inside medieval and Renaissance settings. The Sorceress (1883) is the fullest expression. No late turn is visible: he dies at 35 in the middle of this manner, with his last dated works (1886) still in it. There is no equivalent of his father's late religious experiments. The Salon record goes quiet in 1881, 1882 and 1884 -- whether he submitted and was refused, or did not submit, is NOT established in any source read -- and the big female figures of those years (Venus, Femme a l'oiseau bleu) were painted for the market, not the Salon.

## Aesthetic Logic
The whole system is a machine for making one woman legible at a glance, and for making the past legible through her. He starts from the father's academic correctness (the studio manner, the female beauties, the thin blended paint he was trained in) and then strips the picture down to one figure and one signal: a sorceress crouched in a ring of props, a single woman alone in a tall frame, two couples split by a line of light, a telescope pointed at an empty sea. Where the father moralised the present, the son costumed the past: the history is carried by the dress, the quotation and the prop, not by the event. The quotation in the livret is the warrant -- the picture is an illustration of a named passage, and the passage does the explaining so the picture does not have to. The signature is the unstable element: the early work is unsigned and filed under his father, the cabinet pictures are signed and dated, the big Salon pictures mostly are not recorded as signed at all, and the trade still files his one famous picture under his father's name. The career is a ten-year compression of his father's thirty-four: history set-pieces first, then the woman takes over, then the sorceress and the panorama -- and then it stops, at 35, with the last Salon picture an Etretat gate and the last dated picture a fifteenth-century courtier and his beauty in a wooded landscape, the two registers side by side at the end. What makes him usable for generative work is not the sentimentality -- he has none of his father's -- but the reduction: every picture is a small system with one moving part (the eyes that follow, the light/shadow boundary drifting, the telescope scanning, the surprise rippling sideways, the signature appearing in the corner) and everything else held still. And the authorship question is itself a system: a picture that asks, while you watch it, which Merle painted it.

## POSITIONS
P1-P13 are his own practice. He left no writings (see caveat 1 in the network leg), so each is marked [INFERRED] (read from the work and the record) or [FROM RECORD] (directly evidenced). P14-P20 are reception. Parent corrections to leg 3 are marked [CORRECTED].

P1 [INFERRED]: History painting in a Pre-Raphaelite-inflected, neo-Gothic / neo-Renaissance idiom was his chosen field. Evidence: fr.wikipedia's critical line (see Q4) and the Salon subjects -- Faust (1877), Timon of Athens (1879), Philip van Artevelde (1878), Charles the Bold (1876). Source: fr.wikipedia Georges Merle.
P2 [INFERRED]: The female figure is the centre of the picture. Evidence: "known for his outstanding command of the female form" (en.wikipedia); Venus (1882), Femme a l'oiseau bleu (1884), L'Envouteuse (1883), La fille du pecheur (1880). Source: en.wikipedia; fr.wikipedia Salon list.
P3 [INFERRED]: Literary and historical texts drove subject choice. Evidence: Goethe's Faust (1877 Salon picture), Shakespeare's Timon of Athens (1879), Juvenal des Ursins's chronicle (Artevelde, 1878), Olivier de La Marche's chronicle (Le Pas d'Armes, 1876). Source: fr.wikipedia Salon list.
P4 [FROM RECORD] [CORRECTED]: The Salon was his single public arena -- but not every year. He exhibited ten pictures in eight Salons between 1876 and 1886 (1876 no. 1444; 1877 no. 1481; 1878 no. 1565; 1879 nos. 2106-2107; 1880 nos. 2587, 2788; 1883 no. 1672; 1885 no. 1736; 1886 no. 3129), with no entries listed for 1881, 1882 or 1884. Leg 3's "eleven consecutive Salons" is wrong; see C2. Source: fr.wikipedia Salon list; the 1883 entry independently confirmed in the 1883 illustrated Salon catalogue (Dumas), line 3588.
P5 [FROM RECORD]: A workshop practice learned in his father's studio. He "assisted in his father's studio, which specialized in genre paintings and depictions of female beauties, before painting under his own name" (Bhamwiki). His atelier was at 63 rue de Lisbonne, next to his father's at no. 55. Source: Bhamwiki L'Envouteuse; fr.wikipedia.
P6 [FROM RECORD] [CORRECTED]: Etretat was a second home and a recurring subject. Etretat pictures across 1878-1886: Etretat, conversation de pecheurs (1878), La fille du pecheur, Etretat (Salon 1880, no. 2788), La femme du pecheur et son fils a la longue vue (1883), Porte du Grand-Valene, a Etretat (Salon 1886, no. 3129). Only the 1880 and 1886 Etretat pictures were Salon entries; leg 3's listing of the 1878 conversation as a Salon picture is wrong (see C3). Maupassant places him among the Etretat habitues. Source: fr.wikipedia.
P7 [INFERRED] [CORRECTED]: He worked at two scales: small-to-mid pictures for the bourgeois market alongside a few large machines. Evidence: Nicolas le Flamand 35 x 26 cm against Apres la surprise 94 x 190 cm (1885). Leg 3 adds "the Esther cycle was made for the American market", but that rests on Dorotheum text cataloguing the cycle as Hugues's; the attribution is disputed (see C5 and C7). Source: fr.wikipedia Salon list; Dorotheum Esther lot text.
P8 [FROM RECORD]: His signature practice changed over time. "As Georges rarely signed his early pieces, several of his early works were at first mistakenly attributed to his father" (en.wikipedia). By 1883 he signed plainly: L'Envouteuse is signed "GEORGES. MERLE" lower right. Source: en.wikipedia; Bhamwiki.
P9 [FROM RECORD] [CORRECTED]: The American market mattered to the Merle name. He painted a Portrait de George A. Lucas in 1884, and the Esther canvas was shown at William Schaus Art Gallery, New York, in 1877. Leg 3's claim that "the Esther cycle was painted for the American market from the 1870s onwards, with dealer P. Avery and George Lucas regularly commissioning preparatory studies" is Dorotheum's text about a cycle it catalogues as Hugues Merle's; whether any of it is Georges's is the disputed attribution (see C5 and C7). Source: Dorotheum; fr.wikipedia Salon list.
P10 [INFERRED]: Occult and legendary female subjects were a signature. Evidence: L'Envouteuse (a sorceress working effigy magic, 1883), Venus (1882), Femme a l'oiseau bleu (1884). The sorceress picture is the one work that survives in a public collection and still anchors museum programming (May 2026). Source: fr.wikipedia Salon list; Bhamwiki.
P11 [ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE, stated plainly]: No record of teaching, manifestos, memberships, or public statements survives. No writings of his were found in any source consulted this leg. Any account of his views must be read from the pictures and the Salon record, not from his words. Source: negative result across 33 searches and 4 pages read end to end.
P12 [FROM RECORD] [CORRECTED]: A short, compressed career. Born 18 April 1851, died 6 May 1886, age 35; ten Salon pictures in eight Salons; the 1886 Salon was his last. Leg 3's "eleven Salon years" is wrong; see C2. Source: fr.wikipedia.
P13 [FROM RECORD]: Family continuity as a working method. He was born in the house where Leon Cogniet had an atelier (9 rue de la Grange-aux-Belles), worked beside his father on rue de Lisbonne, married his cousin Marie Celine Merle in 1882, and was buried on 8 May 1886 in his father's grave at Pere-Lachaise. Source: fr.wikipedia.
P14 [RECEPTION]: Contemporary Salon criticism noticed him. Josephin Peladan discussed L'Envouteuse in L'Art ochlocratique: Salons de 1882 & de 1883 (Paris, 1888, p. 95) -- cited second-hand by the Archaeology Art video; the Peladan text itself was not read this leg. Source: YouTube video (via search result).
P15 [RECEPTION]: Confusion with his father dogged him in life and after. Unsigned early works were misattributed to Hugues (en.wikipedia), and the large Esther is still catalogued under both names and both dates (Christie's 1885/Georges vs Dorotheum 1875/Hugues). Source: en.wikipedia; Christie's lot listing; Dorotheum lot text.
P16 [RECEPTION]: Modern reference works give him a single line. en.wikipedia's whole article is 16 lines resting on one source (Birmingham Museum of Art guide 2010, p. 225); fr.wikipedia carries an insufficient-sourcing flag since January 2019. Source: en.wikipedia; fr.wikipedia.
P17 [RECEPTION]: The market remembers him in the low five figures. L'Envouteuse was estimated at 20,000-30,000 pounds at Christie's London, 2 July 2008, and went unsold; the Esther canvas realised EUR 71,500 at Dorotheum, 24 Oct 2023 (catalogued as Hugues). Source: Bhamwiki; Dorotheum.
P18 [RECEPTION]: Museum presence is thin but real. L'Envouteuse is in the Birmingham Museum of Art, Alabama (accession AFI.2.2009), bought by the Art Fund of Birmingham in 2009, and was still the prompt for museum programming in May 2026 (Soothing Saturday yoga). Source: Bhamwiki.
P19 [RECEPTION]: The Italian verdict is blunt. it.wikipedia, in its Hugues Merle article, says of Georges that he "ebbe assai meno successo di suo padre" -- had much less success than his father. Source: it.wikipedia Hugues Merle (via search result).
P20 [RECEPTION, INFERRED]: The name itself is unstable in the record. Christie's and artnet list "Georges Hugues Merle (French, 1851-1886)"; fr.wikipedia gives his full name as Jean Adrien Georges Merle. The "Hugues" in the double name is most plausibly a cataloguing conflation with his father's middle name. Source: Christie's lot listing; artnet listing; fr.wikipedia.

## Load-Bearing Corrections
C0. Doorway scoring (see the Doorway line): D1 HOLDS WITH CAVEAT, D2 HOLDS. The caveat is in the clauses: sonship is solid; "same women, same manner" is supported in substance but not in the premise's exact words, and his mature subjects diverge from his father's; "rarely signed" is weaker than "did not sign"; the misattribution rests on one museum guide with no named example. Both claims count as holds in the streak, the first with a caveat, as in #500's D2.
C1. Leg 1's Salon count is wrong arithmetic. The leg says "thirteen Salon pictures in nine years, 1876-1886" and, in the works-count check, "13 of them are Salon pictures" and "4 dated pictures ... actually 5" have no Salon number. The fr.wikipedia Salon list gives 1876 (1), 1877 (1), 1878 (1), 1879 (2), 1880 (2), 1883 (1), 1885 (1), 1886 (1) -- ten pictures in eight Salons. The study carries ten in eight throughout. The correct split of the 17 named dated works, agreeing with leg 2, is 10 Salon pictures (Pas d'Armes 1876, Faust 1877, Arteveld 1878, Timon 1879, Ligueuse 1879, Contraste 1880, Fille du pecheur 1880, Sorciere 1883, Apres la surprise 1885, Porte du Grand-Valene 1886) and 7 non-Salon pictures (Etretat, conversation de pecheurs 1878, Venus 1882, Femme du pecheur 1883, Nicolas le Flamand 1883, Portrait de Lucas 1884, Femme a l'oiseau bleu 1884, Courtisan et sa belle 1886). Leg 1's parenthetical counted only five of the seven non-Salon pictures, missing the 1878 Etretat conversation and the 1884 Lucas portrait.
C2. Leg 3's P4 and P12 say he "exhibited every year from 1876 to 1886 -- eleven consecutive Salons" and "eleven Salon years". Wrong: fr.wikipedia lists no entries for 1881, 1882 or 1884, and its own phrasing is only "Il expose au Salon entre 1876 et 1886" (a span, not a yearly claim). Corrected in P4 and P12 to ten pictures in eight Salons.
C3. Leg 3's P6 lists Etretat, conversation de pecheurs (1878) among the "Etretat pictures at the Salon". It has no Salon number in the fr.wikipedia list; only La fille du pecheur (1880, no. 2788) and Porte du Grand-Valene (1886, no. 3129) were Salon entries. Corrected in P6.
C4. Marriage conflict INSIDE fr.wikipedia: body text says "Il epouse Marie Celine Merle en mai 1882 a Paris"; its own reference 3 (Archives de l'Isere, Saint-Marcellin, view 160/327, act no. 11 of 10 June 1882) says the civil marriage took place at Saint-Marcellin on 10 June 1882. The archival act wins: Saturday 10 June 1882, Saint-Marcellin. The "May / Paris" wording is the article's own error.
C5. The Esther attribution is UNRESOLVED, and it resolves study #500's correction C7 (which had said an 1885 Esther under Hugues was impossible). Christie's lists the large Esther as "GEORGES HUGUES MERLE (French, 1851-1886), signed and dated 'Hugues.Merle.1885'". Dorotheum (24 Oct 2023, realised EUR 71,500) catalogues the same canvas (same dimensions 145 x 95.5 cm, same provenance: William Schaus Art Gallery New York 1877; Percy Avery Rockefeller; Parke-Bernet 24 March 1938 lot 25) as Hugues Merle, "signed, dated Hugues Merle.1875". An 1885 date makes it Georges's (Hugues died March 1881); an 1875 date makes it Hugues's. The houses disagree on both date and attribution. The Esther is therefore NOT counted among Georges's 17 named dated works; it is carried here as a disputed canvas. The Dorotheum lot text (Q9-Q13) is quoted as the house's wording about a cycle it attributes to Hugues.
C6. "Georges Hugues Merle" is a trade conflation, not his civil name. Christie's (2008) and the Musee d'Orsay artist-page title use it; his full civil name is Jean Adrien Georges Merle (fr.wikipedia, citing the Paris reconstituted archives, birth act, folio 23/51). The study uses "Georges Merle" and notes the double form where it appears in catalogue wording.
C7. Leg 3's P7 and P9 present the Esther cycle as Georges's American-market work ("painted for the American market from the 1870s onwards", "P. Avery and George Lucas regularly commissioned preparatory studies"). Those sentences are Dorotheum's text about a cycle it catalogues as Hugues Merle's. Whether any of the cycle is Georges's is exactly the disputed attribution in C5. The positions are corrected to state only what is secure for Georges himself: the 1884 Portrait de George A. Lucas, and the Schaus 1877 showing of the Esther canvas (which belongs to Georges only if the Christie's attribution is right).
C8. Mother's name order differs between sources: "Adelaide Victorine Delplanque" (study #500 record) vs "Victorine Adelaide Delplanque" (fr.wikipedia). The study uses #500's order in the Site line and flags the variant in N2.
C9. Etretat circle chronology problem: fr.wikipedia, reporting Maupassant's Chroniques, has Georges mixing at Etretat with Jules Larcher and Eugene Lepoittevin. Lepoittevin died 6 August 1870, when Georges was 19 and six years before his Salon debut -- so the Etretat circle claim, if it concerns Lepoittevin, must refer to the very young Georges or is garbled. The Maupassant passage itself was not located in any leg. Flagged, not resolved. Jules Larcher is unverifiable: no painter of that name was found in any search, and he is not a candidate.
C10. L'Envouteuse dimensions: Bhamwiki and fr.wikipedia give 57.5 x 45 cm; the Christie's/Invaluable lot line gives 57.5 x 45 in. The cm figures are consistent with each other and with the Birmingham record; the inches are almost certainly a lot-listing error, but the discrepancy is recorded, not silently corrected.
C11. Leg 3's N1 gives Hugues Merle as "(1823-1881)". Study #500 established the birth year as 1822 or 1823 (civil register 28 April 1822 vs 28 February 1822 vs English-language 1823). The study carries "(1822 or 1823-1881)" here.

C14. Birth place: fr.wikipedia says "Ancien 5e arrondissement de Paris" in the infobox and "9 rue de la Grange-aux-Belles" in the text; the street address is in the 10th arrondissement in modern Paris.
