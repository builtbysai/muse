## The life: the organiser who never finished his degree

David F. Mayor was born in 1948. (C11701) His mother Beate was an Austrian-born amateur painter who taught art history, daughter of the actor Anny Schey von Koromla, who emigrated to the UK with her family in the early 1930s; his father worked at the British Museum and later in the manuscript department at Sotheby's, and translated Proust's Time Regained. (C11702-C11704) He was educated at Bryanston, specialising in mathematics and physics. (C11705)

The career runs in four phases, and the hinge is the show that became a press: the work starts as research, becomes a travelling exhibition, becomes a publishing house, and ends as an archive.

(1) Exeter, 1970-71: the postgraduate who never finished. Mayor went to Exeter University in 1970 for a post-graduate degree under Mike Weaver at the American Arts Documentation Centre. (C11706) At Exeter he organised the Polygonal Workshop exhibition, created his own events, and constructed assemblages. (C11708) He first became aware of Fluxus when Weaver gave him the task of organising an exhibition of Fluxus, which meant interviewing many Fluxus personalities, including George Brecht, Robert Filliou, Tomas Schmit, Wolf Vostell and Arthur Koepcke. (C11709) His academic research fell by the wayside while he initiated and administered Fluxshoe, and he never completed his post-graduate studies. (C11710) He first met Felipe Ehrenberg and Martha Hellion at the 7th-Day Chicken show, Sigi Krauss Gallery, Covent Garden, in 1970. (C11711)

(2) Fluxshoe, 1970-73. Fluxshoe was conceived by Mike Weaver in a letter to Ken Friedman on 9 June 1970; from October 1970 material was shipped to Mayor, who was given the task of organising it, with the blessing and support of Maciunas and Friedman. (C11712, C11713, C11716) It was the first major Fluxus show in Britain while George Maciunas was still alive, and it toured nine months in 1972-73 to provincial locations -- Falmouth (opened 23 October 1972), Exeter, Croydon (15-26 January 1973), Oxford, Nottingham (June 1973), Blackburn (July 1973), and Hastings (17-24 August 1973) -- giving a platform to then emerging British artists such as Ian Breakwell, Helen Chadwick and COUM Transmissions. (C11715, C11716, C11717, C11752) The show arrived in boxes and parcels, much of it postable table-top work sent through the mail, and was installed on white plinths, some pieces in perspex boxes. (C11718) The FLUXshoe catalogue was Beau Geste Press's first publication. (C11719)

(3) Beau Geste Press, 1971-76. In the winter of 1971 Ehrenberg invited Mayor to help establish Beau Geste Press; Mayor joined the nucleus late in 1971 at Langford Court, the deserted thatched farmhouse overlooking Clyst Hydon near Cullompton, Devon. (C11722, C11723) Ehrenberg's own account says the little group's dynamics took over: Dave's project to gather his fluxdocuments into a travelling show proved to be the mechanism that triggered the press into being, the idea being to help "all-thumbs Dave" get the thing going. (C11724) Mayor's role was public relations and record-keeping: Wikipedia calls him the press's public-relations manager; Frieze calls him head of public relations and says he kept meticulous records; Conwell, quoting Ehrenberg, says Hellion's logistical organisation and hospitality enabled the community to function, "while Mayor's obsessive letter writing ensured that Beau Geste Press became internationally recognised." (C11726) The press printed some 75 books over five years, in mimeograph, offset lithography and letterpress, outside the commercial market, under the slogan "Our Press is not a business, it's a way of life." (C11727) Mayor drove Schmuck, the press's periodical of ideas and anti-authoritarian art practice organised around a specific culture or region: launched March 1972, eight issues, covering Iceland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, France, Germany and Japan, each edited by an artist from the location. (C11731) He issued the calls for proposals, gathered the works, and Ehrenberg printed, duplicated and rubber-stamped them before the group collated and hand-bound each edition. (C11732) He edited General Schmuck No. 5 (1975) and co-edited the final issue, Japanese Schmuck No. 8 (1976), with Taii Ashizawa and Takehisa Kosugi. (C11733) His own publications with the press included Framed Pieces (1972), C(l)ues (1973), Door to Door with Helen Chadwick (1973, edition of 250), and the broadsheet Humphrey is here! (1973), marking the christening of the press's M.A.N. two rev. letterpress cylinder. (C11729) In 1974 the founders parted company; Mayor continued publishing under the BGP name and Schmuck until 1976, and the issue 4 introduction refers to a plan to reconceptualise Schmuck as the decentralised publishing project Beau Geste Press / Libro Accion Libre, produced jointly by Mayor in the UK and Ehrenberg in Mexico. (C11734, C11735, C11736)

(4) After the press: away from the art world, then back through publishing. In the mid-1970s Mayor moved away from the art world. (C11739) In 1976, together with Mike Weaver, he began publishing Revealer Cassettes, an audio magazine; the first two issues were devoted to the dowsing discoveries of Weaver's father, Herbert L. Weaver, and later issues covered radionics, acupuncture and the biodynamic massage methods of Gerda Boyesen. (C11740, C11768) Conwell's note 17 is the only other published account of this turn found: after the members went their separate ways, "Mayor continued to publish artists' books under the Beau Geste Press moniker until 1976, when he began to pursue other interests such as acupuncture." (C11742) The Tate administrative history adds that Mayor "went on to train as a practitioner of all these therapies, and in 2007 re-entered the world of publishing as an editor of books on acupuncture and related subjects." (C11743) No teaching post, no further exhibition, and no later artwork by Mayor was found in any source read this run. (C11745)

Archival afterlife. The main collection is TGA 815, the Mayor / Fluxshoe / Beau Geste Press (BGP) Archive, dated 1951-1989, in three parts: David Mayor 1960-1989; The Fluxshoe 1972-1973; Beau Geste Press correspondence and papers. (C11747) A second Tate collection, TGA 200730, Additional papers of David Mayor, 1863-2011, 79 objects, concerns his Austrian ancestry; it was presented to the Tate Archive by David Mayor himself in December 2007, 2015 and 2016. (C11748) His last evidenced public activity is a performance specially devised by David Mayor for the Beau Geste Press Study Day at the CAPC, Bordeaux, on 31 March 2017. (C11749) No death date was found in any source read this run; do not state one. He is evidenced alive in March 2017; anything after that is unknown from sources read this run. (C11700, C11749)

In his own words. Only two quotations from Mayor himself were found, both from Schmuck no. 1 (March 1972): "Schmuck wants the real meat and no fancy dressing. I have no specific ideology to push, and would like to see Schmuck develop, if not as an open forum for discussion of some of the problems relating to art, at least as a vehicle for artists to present their ideas as well as their art (when the two don't overlap!)" -- and, as reported by Frieze, that the publication would include "feedback from the humming wires of global activity". (C11733) From C(l)ues (1973), his portrait of the Langford Court household: "martha is looking out the window / felipe reads aloud from the newspaper / takako is making paper boxes / much of our time we spend thinking about each other / pat is typing". (C11729)

Evolution. Early work (1970-72) is intake and organisation: Mayor researches Fluxus, interviews artists, sends questionnaires, coordinates the Polygonal Workshop, and gathers his "fluxdocuments" into a travelling show. (C11750, C11751) The middle BGP years (1972-76) are editorial and distributive: he edits the Fluxshoe catalogue and Schmuck, prints other artists' books, runs the mail-order catalogue, and keeps the records that make the press internationally known. (C11753, C11774, C11770) His own authored pieces in this period are small, funny and domestic -- C(l)ues watches the household, Door to Door walks a Croydon block, Humphrey is here! christens a press -- while the big works are systems other people fill: a tour, a magazine series, an envelope of loose sheets. (C11760, C11761, C11762) After BGP closes in 1976 he leaves the art world, and the work changes medium rather than method: Revealer Cassettes carries the same postal, serial, fringe-subject logic onto audio tape, and the later acupuncture editing (from 2007) carries the editorial role into a new field. (C11768, C11772) The arc is therefore from gathering (letters, interviews, documents) to distributing (catalogues, magazines, tours) to archiving (the TGA 815 record itself), with the organiser's hand visible throughout and the solo authored object always the smallest part. (C11769)

## Mechanism M1: the questionnaire as intake -- research as curation

The show is built from the answers that come back. When Weaver gave Mayor the task of organising a Fluxus exhibition, Mayor interviewed many Fluxus personalities -- Brecht, Filliou, Schmit, Vostell, Koepcke and others -- and sent out questionnaires to potential participants "in a characteristically methodical way", visiting Hanns Sohm, Vostell, Schmit and Koepcke in Europe over the summer. (C11751) The interviews and questionnaires are the intake system that decides who is in the show; the viewer never sees this work directly, they see its result in W03-W05. The mechanism is curation by correspondence: the list of participants is not chosen from a canon, it is assembled from replies, and a reply that never comes is a gap in the show.

**Takeaway:** the invitation is the first edit. A piece built on this mechanism must let the audience's answers decide what appears, and must show the unanswered invitations as visibly as the answered ones.

## Mechanism M2: the travelling show as a postal kit

A show that arrives in boxes, gets unpacked by strangers, and is different in every town. Fluxshoe travelled nine months to seven provincial venues -- Falmouth, Exeter, Croydon, Oxford, Nottingham, Blackburn, Hastings -- in three parts: slides and tapes of events; live performances; and a catalogue that recorded events as they occurred. (C11752) At Falmouth the exhibition arrived in boxes or parcels which the local committee helped unpack; white plinths were made, some works were in perspex boxes, and much of the work travelled through the post in a postable, table-top format, some of it opened by customs or lost in transit. (C11752) Over 100 Fluxus and non-Fluxus artists had agreed to appear in person, in the exhibition, or in the catalogue, and events were created specifically for each venue. (C11752) The mechanism is a touring kit: small, postable multiples plus live scores, unpacked and re-installed in each town, with a different live programme each time. The audience experiences a show that is part exhibition, part concert, part postal accident -- and the postal accident is not a failure of the show, it is one of its parts.

**Takeaway:** the kit is the show, and the town is the co-curator. A piece on this mechanism must arrive in pieces, be unpacked on screen by the viewer, and change its live programme with each unpacking, with some pieces lost in transit and the loss visible.

## Mechanism M3: the envelope as the publication

Nothing is bound, so the order is the reader's choice. Fluxshoe add end a 72-73 (1973) is an envelope containing varied printed ephemera -- 26 loose sheets and miscellaneous items by Fluxus artists, press reviews, photo collages and first-hand photographs -- documenting the nine-month tour. (C11754) Sheets can be pinned up, passed on or lost, which repeats the postal logic of the tour itself. The same logic is in the BGP 1973-74 mail-order catalogue: wrappers with provision to keep adding sheets as you receive them, so the catalogue grows like a filing system rather than going out of date. (C11765) The catalogue's own manifesto is the sales pitch: "Beau Geste Press is not a business. It's a way of life. We exist because you exist." (C11765)

**Takeaway:** binding is a decision, and unbinding is one too. A piece on this mechanism must hand the viewer a loose pile, not a page order, and let the pile be re-sorted, pinned, passed on or lost -- the reader's arrangement is the edition.

## Mechanism M4: the regional anthology by post

One region per issue, contributions arriving by post, the editor selecting and sequencing rather than authoring. Schmuck ran to eight issues (1972-76), each devoted to artists of one country or region -- Iceland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, France, Germany, Japan -- each edited by an artist from the location. (C11774) Mayor issued the calls for proposals, gathered the works, and Ehrenberg printed, duplicated and rubber-stamped them before the group collated and hand-bound each edition. (C11732) The best-evidenced issue is Teutonic Schmuck No. 7 (1975): a 28-page monograph in a pocket envelope glued to the back of the cover, red medium-gauge cover printed with black ink, white pages with text and illustration in sepia-toned ink, the envelope white paper printed with blue ink in a denim pattern, plus one inset matchstick taped to a page. (C11758) General Schmuck No. 5 (1975) breaks the regional rule: instead of one country it gathers the network at large, a roll-call of the whole BGP/Fluxus correspondence circle. (C11757) The mechanism is material as much as editorial: the reader opens a pocket envelope, pulls out a small red-covered monograph, and finds a real matchstick taped to a page -- the object behaves like a posted parcel that happens to be a magazine.

**Takeaway:** the magazine is a parcel, and the region is the brief. A piece on this mechanism must arrive as a thing to open, not a page to scroll -- an envelope, a pocket, a taped-in object -- and the contents must come from one place, gathered by post, with the editor's hand visible only in the sequence.

## Mechanism M5: the household as a poem

The communal house, set down as lines, lower-case and unpunctuated, so reading the poem is like watching the house work. C(l)ues (1973) is a short play-cum-concrete poem by Mayor, an affectionate portrait of the cohabiting friends at Langford Court: "martha is looking out the window / felipe reads aloud from the newspaper / takako is making paper boxes / much of our time we spend thinking about each other / pat is typing". (C11760) The title's parenthesis makes the reader do the work of choosing between "cues" (theatrical prompts) and "clues" (evidence), which is the whole joke. The mechanism is documentary poetry: ordinary actions, recorded without comment, become the work, and the household's division of labour -- looking, reading, making boxes, typing, thinking about each other -- is the entire content.

**Takeaway:** the house is the cast and the day is the script. A piece on this mechanism must watch a small group doing ordinary things and set the actions down as lines, with no narrator and no climax -- the accumulation of small acts is the poem.

## Mechanism M6: the walk through the block

A book that makes the reader perform a walk. Door to Door (1973), by Helen Chadwick and David Mayor, is a three-dimensional concept booklet built from photographs of a concrete housing block in Croydon, edition of 250, wrappers, signed in black pen. (C11761) The title is the mechanism: door to door suggests a walk from flat to flat, a delivery round, or a survey, and the book makes the reader perform that walk by turning pages that move through the block's doors and concrete surfaces. It is one of the multidisciplinary concept booklets produced at BGP in 1973 alongside works by Chadwick, Claudio Bertoni and Ulises Carrion. (C11761)

**Takeaway:** the page turn is a footstep. A piece on this mechanism must make the viewer walk a real route -- flat to flat, door to door -- with each turn of the page a step along the block, and the concrete itself as the only scenery.

## Mechanism M7: the machine christened, the press on a train

Equipment and journeys become characters by being named and celebrated in print. Humphrey is here! (1973) is a broadsheet of thoughts gathered when Beau Geste Press christened its M.A.N. two rev. letterpress cylinder -- a machine given the name Humphrey, so the press celebrates a piece of equipment by publishing a broadsheet around it, and the reader is invited to the christening after the fact. (C11762) The same logic is in the Thomas Alva Edison Centenary Issue (August 1972): the community took the BGP duplicator on the ICES '72 train from London to Edinburgh, producing an 81-page loose-leaf publication derived from contributions by their fellow travellers. (C11764) The mechanism is a moving press: the train is the studio, fellow passengers are the contributors, and the publication is assembled in transit and left loose-leaf so pages can be added, reordered or lost. The reader holds a journey rather than a book.

**Takeaway:** name the machine and the journey becomes the work. A piece on this mechanism must give its equipment a name and a ceremony, or put its press on a moving vehicle, so the publication is a record of a trip, not a product of a studio.

## Mechanism M8: the archive as the work -- record-keeping, and the magazine you listen to

A mail artist's output is input: what you get back through the post -- and the point of mail art was not to keep it but to send it. The archive reverses that rule by keeping everything. (C11769) The Mayor / Fluxshoe / Beau Geste Press Archive (TGA 815) holds mail art sent to Mayor (1971-1979), artists' books, catalogues, periodicals, publicity, audio-visual material, photographs and press cuttings; the David Mayor sub-fonds alone is 51 boxes, and the Fluxshoe material fills 152 boxes, "ranging from hand-drawn spreadsheets to a Daily Telegraph tourists' map with the venues marked by big black crosses". (C11769) Mayor was BGP's head of public relations and kept meticulous records of the press's numerous activities. (C11770) The mechanism is record-keeping as the artwork: the filing, stamping ("Beau Geste Press ARCHIVE COPY"), annotating and cross-referencing turn correspondence into a system the next reader can navigate. The same method changes medium after the press closes: Revealer Cassettes (from 1976, with Mike Weaver) is an audio magazine on tape, the first two issues devoted to the dowsing discoveries of Weaver's father, later issues covering radionics, acupuncture and biodynamic massage -- a magazine you listen to rather than read, posted like the BGP books. (C11768)

**Takeaway:** the file is the work, and the same method survives a change of medium. A piece on this mechanism must keep everything -- every letter, every dead letter, every map with crosses on it -- and let the next reader navigate the pile; or it must carry a serial, postal logic onto a medium that was never meant for it, like a magazine on tape.

## Aesthetic logic

Mayor has no recognisable style, and that is the point twice over: he is not primarily an author of objects at all, he is the organiser the objects needed. The continuity is not a look but a method, and the method has three moves that repeat across the whole career. First, intake: send the questionnaire, make the call, gather the documents, and let the replies decide what exists. Second, distribution: pack it small enough to post, print it cheap enough to give away, and let the network carry it -- a tour in boxes, a magazine by post, a catalogue that grows by added sheets, a tape in an envelope. Third, filing: stamp it, annotate it, cross-reference it, and keep it, so the traffic itself becomes a readable object. The materials are the cheapest communication systems that travel: mimeograph, offset, letterpress, loose sheets, envelopes, pocket envelopes, matchsticks taped to pages, white plinths and perspex boxes, a second-hand van, a train duplicator, a cassette tape. The tone is funny and domestic where Ehrenberg's is political and Carrion's is systematic: a photograph of a man with four sauces in his mouth, a press christening a machine called Humphrey, a household poem in lower case, a man not bathing for six months on tour. The scale moves from a questionnaire to a seven-town tour to an eight-issue magazine to a 51-box archive, and the durability moves from a loose sheet that can be lost to an archive the Tate keeps -- but the hand stays the same: the hand that writes the letter, packs the box, edits the issue, and files the reply. A piece built from this study should feel posted, unpacked, filed and re-read -- ink on the hands, envelopes in the post, a pile of loose sheets to sort -- and should never look like a gallery object pretending to be poor, or a system pretending to be a solo artwork. (C11858)
