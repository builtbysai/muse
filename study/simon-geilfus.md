# Simon Geilfus: Study Notes

**Site:** Simon Geilfus (b. Brussels, 1985), Belgian interaction designer and software engineer, lives and works in Brussels; the coder of the AntiVJ visual label, which he joined in 2009, and the builder of the tools behind its mappings -- the Murcof live software, the Paleodictyon particle system, the Ecume waveform-to-topography tool, the Elytre generative software. He enjoys, in the label bio's own words, "as much creating graphics and visual abstractions as creating the softwares and systems to generate them" -- and the study that follows takes that equality literally: in Geilfus the tool is not behind the work, it is half the work, and the image is what the tool does when it is played.

## The life: the coder who joined the label

Geilfus was born in Brussels in 1985 and lives there still -- on the evidence of a single bio text carried identically on the AntiVJ label page and the Montalvo Arts Center page, one source chain, not two independent attestations (C3302). No birth date, no second source. Nothing in any source read names a school, a degree, or a teacher. What exists instead is a positive statement of how he learned, from Lemercier in 2011, speaking for the label's two programmers: "Simon Geilfus and myself are the two programmers on the label, and we've learned pretty much everything we know online, especially from the open source community (Processing, openFrameworks, Cinder, vvvv)." It is Lemercier speaking about him, not Geilfus about himself (C3301) -- but it is evidenced, not inferred, and it fixes his formation: self-taught, online, open-source, in the first generation of creative coders.

The label bio gives the only origin story, and it dates his formation to a software release: "Starting a web design career at the time when Processing was released and the Flash Hackers Community got big, Simon quickly got sucked in creative coding, computational design and generative graphics." Processing was released in 2001, when Geilfus was about sixteen; the bio does not date his start, and no source gives a first job, first client, or first computer. The earliest dated activity found is 2008: visuals for Federico Monti at the Storung Festival in Barcelona, and sound-reactive videos sent speculatively to Murcof, made to music Murcof had not commissioned. He arrives at AntiVJ not as a VJ with clips but as a coder making sound-reactive systems -- and Murcof hires him on the strength of videos made on his own initiative.

He is not a founder of AntiVJ. The label was created by Yannick Jacquet, Joanie Lemercier, Olivier Ratsi and Romain Tardy around 2006; Geilfus joined in 2009, three years after the founding and a year after Vaquie became the label's composer. His function inside the label is specific: he is one of its two programmers, and the tool-builder. Lemercier's 2011 account of why the label builds its own software is the context for his whole career: "we use more and more code / custom software in the production of our work. Firstly because we're doing more technically challenging projects, and no software can really handle our needs, so we have to build our own tools... We sometimes have ideas that cannot be achieved with existing tools, so we need to create new ones. And on the other hand, the technical possibilities offered by the tools we developed in house sometimes will guide the way we approach a project or the type of content that will be used." Geilfus is the person that "we have to build our own tools" refers to, in practice.

The teaching runs alongside the shows, and it is the other half of the same program. "Three years lecturer at Brussels' Superior National School of Visual Arts, La Cambre and occasional lecturer at Graphic Research School of Brussels, ERG. He regularly teaches the art of creative coding in cultural events and festivals around Europe." The "three years" has no anchor date in the bio, and the identical text appears in sources from 2014 and 2015, so the bio was likely written circa 2014 and never updated (C3303). Two dated instances pin the practice down: Coded Matter(s) in Amsterdam, 14 February 2014 -- an artist talk plus the workshop "Creative Coding: Live Visuals with Cinder", built on Lua so students could "code in a live environment, and directly see the result of your work" -- and Gray Area in San Francisco, 3 August 2014, a six-hour "Cinder & Scripts" workshop. No source read gives an end date, a current post, or any teaching after 2014; do not present him as currently lecturing at La Cambre. The tools were not trade secrets: the Murcof software, the mapping software, the visualisation tool were all things he also taught, in public.

The career runs in three phases. Phase 1, web design into creative coding (c. 2001-2009, dates soft): the Processing-era start, the Storung visuals, the speculative Murcof videos. Phase 2, AntiVJ, the tool-building decade (2009-2018): the Murcof live software from 2009; mapping software for the Stromae Tour (2011); the Flying Lotus Roundhouse work and the Hugo Boss Black stage design (both 2012, both single-sourced to Coded Matter(s)); Paleodictyon at Centre Pompidou-Metz, 6 October 2012; the Coded Matter(s) talk and workshop, February 2014; Grove, an Android experiment, 2015; the Ecume tool, 2016; software for Elytre, Jacquet's permanent generative work at the Pont Alexandre III, Paris, 2016-2017; the ETHER video tool reuse, 2018. Phase 3, the Murcof renewal: Twin Color (2022-2025) -- conceived in the IRCAM studios in Paris in 2022, live show premiered at IRCAM in 2023, album show premiered at MUTEK Montreal at the end of August 2024, album Twin Color Vol. 1 on InFine, 15 November 2024, Twin Color (Extended Play No. 2) 29 August 2025. Most recent firmly dated item found: August 2025. No works dated 2026 found (C3319). After Elytre in 2017 the public record of dated works goes quiet -- a Belgian company register entry, "Simon Geilfus", private limited company, registered 13 August 2019, computer programming and consultancy, is the only post-2017 fact found, and it is administrative, not artistic. Do not present 2017 as an end date (C3358).

## The program, in his own words

Three texts carry the program, and they are unusually rich for a man with almost no press footprint -- one interview, one blog post, one bio. The interview is the only one found (200percent, 26 September 2012, with Murcof); the blog post is the fullest method statement found anywhere in the study series this year (AntiVJ blog, "Bacteria farming and Software design", 4 March 2013). Everything else is label bios, festival listings, or passing mentions in pieces about other people. The study leans on the blog post and the 2012 interview and does not pretend a wider critical literature exists (C3301-C3320, leg 1).

First, the identity, from the label bio: "Designing tools and new approaches for graphic and motion design has become one of his major interest. He enjoys as much creating graphics and visual abstractions as creating the softwares and systems to generate them." The equality in the sentence matters: the tool is not a means to the image, it is half the work. A CDA Enghien note puts him "in the line of John Maeda or Joshua Davis" -- between graphic design and computer programming, developing his own tools for a generative and interactive art. This is the finding the prompts must be built on: a Geilfus piece is a system that makes images, and the system itself is the subject.

Second, the working method, from the 2012 interview, on the Murcof show: "I usually start by sketching a scene, creating some objects, lights and animations; then when I'm happy with that I can start adding layers of sound reactive animations. It's similar to the way in which you would use effects on your timeline and keyframes in an animation software to enhance your visuals, I use his sounds to add some complexity and reactivity to my animations. Together, we selected sounds that affect those parts of my visuals." Sketch the scene first, then bind sounds to it -- the image leads, the sound complicates it. The music leads "most of the time" because they started from existing material rather than composing an hour of new music -- a practical constraint stated honestly. And the software: "It started as a small bugy prototype (not fully functional software) in Processing... for our first gig and evolved over the years into several c++ applications... that I'm still using today." ("bugy" is the source's spelling.) Murcof on the division of labour: "It's Simon who does most of the hard work, before a dot or a line moves or reacts. There has to be quite a bit of programming as he works with his own customized software and he's constantly changing and patching it."

Third, the method stated as a system, from the 2013 blog post on Paleodictyon -- research, then software: deep-sea organisms, Paleodictyon nodosum and its "supposedly faculties for bacteria farming", and the coincidence between those organisms and Shigeru Ban's architecture; a storyboard of "multiple individuals creating, on a higher scale, a big complex organism" and "skin being a structure constantly rearranging itself in reaction to different stimuli", which pointed to "agents, particles and group behaviour" -- with an explicit refusal to repeat himself: "I really wanted to try to push this further... not just to create an nth Craig Reynolds's Steering behaviors implementation." Modularity over hard-coding: a scene graph, "a number of objects and re-arrange at will how those objects could influence each other", with a small number of object types per scene: "Particle groups, particle behaviour and effectors." Complexification by layers: "When it comes to programming physical processes, I've always been fascinated by how combining different layers of complexity can be so powerful. Each layer bringing its new set of rules and surprises." First layer, per-particle properties -- "Sometimes it does really feel like putting your finger into a Petri dish just to see what might happen." Second layer, groups with their own behaviours -- "Make a small group act like a flock of fish and another one more as a fluid." Timeline animation of behaviour parameters: "changing those behaviours over time would create such unexpected reactions... skin contraction and dilatation, structure's construction, re-organisation and deconstruction." Music as a literal input: "we wanted to give the music a real literal role in the piece, making it one of the actual inputs in our (eco)system... The music became quickly the main antagonist in our story, attracting/repelling those organisms, controlling their every move", routed through a node graph: "allowing me to visually route any part of the audio to any part of the visual/physical system." Computer vision as a bridge to the motion designer: an OpenCV module analysing Jacquet's videos, "extracting interesting data that I could use for my animations", including polygons for collisions and grayscale gradients for effect strength, so that "graphical animation [could] physically collide with particles, scaring them off or attracting them... voila, please meet interactive physical compositing!" And the stopping rule, the sentence every tool-builder should be made to memorise: "Because time is often the main constraint for that kind of project, especially when you are the lead and only developer, there's always a moment when you have to stop building new toys and start playing with the one you already have!"

## The turn: from the stage to the building to the object, 2008-2025

The early manner is the reactive video. Before 2009 there is no single titled work, only the mechanism: take existing music, analyse it, drive visuals from the analysis (W01). The Storung Festival, Barcelona, 2008 -- visuals for Federico Monti, and Murcof working with XX+XY on visuals at the same festival -- is the dated anchor, and the crossing of paths there is the pre-history of everything after. The mechanism is already the whole later practice in miniature.

The years 2009-2012 are the instrument and the building, in two strands that peak on the same night. The Murcof software (W02) is the instrument: a Processing prototype for the first gig at Futuresonic, Manchester, May 2009, grown over three years into several C++ applications, played live through a couple of dozen shows -- Sonderborg, Arnolfini Bristol, LEV Gijon, Mutek Montreal, TodaysArt, Scopitone, Beijing, Lisbon -- to the Alpha-ville LIVE show at the Hackney Empire, London, 6 October 2012, where a giant transparent fabric screen stood between the artists and the audience and the visuals were projected onto it, the performers visible through their own image. Boritch's aim for that staging: "to try and create an experience where you can't necessarily separate the sound and visual elements. You might forget about one of the two, but hopefully it is because it is a whole, and you are lost in it." The imagery is geometric abstraction at two scales at once -- "visuals about the scale of galaxies and the scale of atoms" -- and Murcof's description of it is the best catalogue of the scenes: "a giant spider like creature that stretches its arms each time the music explodes, there's bacteria holding hands and working in groups whilst maintaining their individuality, there are tons of stars that swirl around and become comets, rays of light that become a network of neurons." The inspected software screenshot is the instrument itself: an AUDIOANALYSIS panel (FFT sample and scale, FFT average damping, FFT peaks fade, waveform readouts), a RENDERING preview of a dense white line-network, and scene panels named OORT THING, CIELO, COSMOS, BACTERIAS, UNISSON and COSMOS II, each with sliders for sound scale, scale, travel speed, rotation, glow strength, line width and colour, plus SOFTEDGE BLENDING and UTILS panels. The scene names match Murcof's description exactly -- the software is the piece, and the screenshot is its score.

The building strand runs alongside it. Principles of Geometry (W03, 2009-2010) takes the line-network language stereoscopic -- "thin white lines into mountains skeletons and infinite perspectives leading to void space" -- with Geilfus as a secondary credit ("visuals also produced by Simon Geilfus & Desaxismundi"). Chateau des Ducs de Bretagne (W04, Nantes, 2010) is his first large-scale architectural mapping as a credited visual artist, one hand among four (Geilfus, Jacquet, Lemercier, Tardy), with Vaquie's score -- "an organic and mineral chaos for an intense audiovisual experience" on a newly restored castle. Clair Obscur (W05, Collegiale de Neuchatel, 2011) turns a church vault into a starfield: the inspected image looks along the nave into receding Gothic arches, the stone vault filled with a teal and white nebula, dense with stars -- the Murcof "Cosmos" scene fitted to stone, the organ confronted by electronics inside the church's own acoustics. The commercial strand sits in the same years and rests on a single source: mapping software for the Stromae Tour (W06, 2011) -- the credit is software, not visuals, and the surviving stage photograph comes from Tardy's page; the Flying Lotus Roundhouse work (W07, 2011 per DJ Mag, 2012 per Coded Matter(s), date unresolved) -- the credit is single-sourced and the later Layer3/Hypercube staging is Strangeloop and Timeboy's, not his; stage design for the Hugo Boss Black Fashion Show (W09, Beijing, 18 May 2012) -- the runway photograph shows the show, and the chevron light tunnel in it is NOT verified as his design. These three are the thinnest credits in the catalogue and the study says so plainly.

L'Orage (W08, Institut Francais, Tokyo, 2012) is the quietest mapping in the catalogue, and the proof that his system can whisper: the garden's real tree shadows plus a layer of computer-generated shadows, fireflies, birds, anthropomorphic insects -- the inspected image is a night photograph of the institute's white cylindrical tower with projected tree-branch shadows across it, real trees in the foreground, the projection almost indistinguishable from the real shadows. The same year as the maximalist Paleodictyon, he also makes a piece whose whole point is that you cannot see the trick.

Paleodictyon (W10, Centre Pompidou-Metz, 6 October 2012) is the masterpiece and the hinge -- and the date is a correction: #605 leg 2 dated it 2013, but the primary sources (AntiVJ page, Vimeo, designboom) all give 6 October 2012 (C3353, C3368, C3430). Co-direction, not just visuals or software, for the first time: "A project by Simon Geilfus, Yannick Jacquet and Thomas Vaquie", artistic direction by all three, visual content by Geilfus, Jacquet and Tardy, music by Vaquie in two mirrored parts, Plancton (the abyss) and Otarie (just below the surface). The Pompidou-Metz's undulating white roof -- Shigeru Ban's hexagonal woven-timber frame -- becomes the screen for hexagonal patterns after the deep-sea trace fossil Paleodictyon nodosum: the inspected images show the roof from above covered in a dense black-and-white cracked network, the whole building alive with a pale hexagonal mesh like a sea creature's skin, a fine white triangular wireframe sweeping across it. The concept "abolishes notions of scale by contrasting micro-architecture with human construction" -- the hexagonal burrows of an invisible creature, magnified to the size of a museum, on a building whose own frame is hexagonal. The layer-and-rules system is stated explicitly as a method, and the scale trick -- micro made monumental -- is the concept. The same night, 6 October 2012, Alpha-ville opened in London: the two strands of his practice, the live system and the architectural mapping, peak on the same evening, in two countries. Paleodictyon -- The App (W11, 2012) lifts the mapping system out of the physical site and re-implements it as a simulation: virtual projectors giving light to a 3D model of the museum, "a digital replica" -- the first time the tool becomes the work in its own right.

The years 2014-2017 give the tool a permanent form. The Coded Matter(s) talk and workshop (W13, Amsterdam, 14 February 2014) make the teaching public: the toolmaker explaining the tool rather than hiding it behind the spectacle. Grove (2015) is the only standalone consumer product found -- one of the 20 launch titles on Google's Android Experiments site, "A virtual playground", version 1.2, 2,326 downloads -- and what it shows or does is undescribed anywhere read (C3312). Ecume (W14, June 2016) is the tool leaving the stage and becoming an object: Geilfus's visualisation tool analyses the album's waveforms into topographical imagery; Jacquet 3D-prints those sonic landscapes, makes a silicone mould, casts them in concrete, photographs them under light -- "a photography of Ecume". The inspected images show a flat grey concrete surface covered in fine vertical ridged lines, dense at the edges, sparse in the middle, and the finished objects on a white shelf: dark ridged slabs, one sliding open to reveal a black disc printed with a white radiating sunburst. The attribution is split and the study keeps it split: tool by Geilfus, artwork by Jacquet (C3307). The same tool, two years later, drives the ETHER video (2018): a concrete mountain built from the spectrogram, eruptions of metal powder caused by rhythmic impulses. Elytre (W15, also called Flow, Le Flow, Paris, 2016-2017) is the permanent turn: a forty-metre generative light work wrapping a floating building on the Seine, its own dedicated custom software -- software by Eric Renaud-Houde and Simon Geilfus, to Jacquet's design, music by Vaquie -- "inspired by the cycle of the seasons and explores our relationship with time. It is designed less as an invitation to a journey as an order to slow down." The inspected images show the barge's facade banded in warm orange-red at dusk, the Roue de Paris behind it, and at night a long grid of small coloured rectangles doubled in the water below. The software's internals are unrecorded, and Geilfus's share versus Renaud-Houde's is unrecorded (C3375). After a decade of one-night mappings and tours, the system is installed to run indefinitely on a building -- the opposite of the Murcof show, which existed only while it was played.

The renewal, Twin Color (W18, 2022-2025), is not a return to the label but a resumption of the one collaboration that never needed it. Conceived in the IRCAM studios in Paris in 2022; the live show premiered with Geilfus at IRCAM in 2023; the album show premiered at MUTEK Montreal at the end of August 2024; Twin Color Vol. 1 on InFine, 15 November 2024, Murcof's first full-length album since Cosmos (2007); Twin Color (Extended Play No. 2), 29 August 2025, crediting "Visual art by Simon Geilfus, additional technical art by Robert Hodgin". His method has changed tools but not logic: "Using video game rendering software, Geilfus attempted to play Twin Color's cinematic, neon-drenched energy. 'From the first listen, I was picturing something around a late '80s or early '90s science fiction movie,' he says... Geilfus built a landscape, creating scenes and stages to shoot footage within. He then used post-processing to help his world resemble a grainy movie instead of a video game." And the direction of influence has reversed: Neural notes that "his algorithmic elaborations of natural landscapes have helped define the album's sound structure, establishing a symbiotic dialogue between acoustic dimension and visual representation" -- the visual work feeding back into the music, where in 2012 the music led. The premiere dates carry a spread -- IRCAM 2023 versus MUTEK August 2024 -- and the study keeps both, as live-show premiere and album-show premiere (C3405). A 2014 CDM note also promised "a cosmic installation by Simon Geilfus and mexican composer Murcof" among AntiVJ's 2014 plans; no such installation under that description was found, and it may be the seed of Twin Color or a separate unmade project (C3311).

## M1 -- Sound-bound scenes: sketch the scene, then bind the sounds to it

The mechanism that names the whole practice, and the best documented in the catalogue. The Murcof software (W02) is built in two passes: first sketch a scene -- objects, lights, animations -- and when the scene is good, add layers of sound-reactive animation on top of it, the way an animator adds effects to a timeline. Then, with Murcof, select which sounds affect which parts of the visuals, and bind them: an FFT analysis panel (sample and scale, average damping, peaks fade, waveform readouts) feeds per-scene sliders -- sound scale, scale, travel speed, rotation, glow strength, line width, colour. The scenes are a library, each with its own behaviour: OORT THING, CIELO, COSMOS, BACTERIAS, UNISSON, COSMOS II. The binding is the piece: a giant spider-like creature that stretches its arms each time the music explodes; bacteria holding hands and working in groups whilst maintaining their individuality; stars that swirl around and become comets; rays of light that become a network of neurons. The software is patched live, show after show -- "constantly changing and patching it", Murcof says -- and the show evolves across residencies from existing material agreed in advance to tracks developed together from scratch, "quite a few sounds that have direct influence on how certain visual elements behave". The instrument is visible in the inspected screenshot: the control interface is the work's score, and the scenes are its movements.

**Takeaway:** a scene with its sounds bound to it cannot drift out of sync with the music, because the music is one of its parameters; build the scene first, then choose which sounds touch which parts, and let the binding do the performing.

## M2 -- Layered rule systems: each layer brings new rules and surprises

The Paleodictyon system (W10), stated in his own blog post as a method. Instead of hard-coding a particle system, build a modular one around a scene graph -- particle groups, particle behaviours, effectors -- so objects can be re-arranged at will and influence each other in new hierarchies. Then complexify by layers, each layer bringing its new set of rules and surprises: first layer, per-particle properties (sizes, masses, shapes) -- "putting your finger into a Petri dish just to see what might happen"; second layer, groups with their own behaviours -- "Make a small group act like a flock of fish and another one more as a fluid". Then animate the behaviour parameters themselves over a timeline, so the system produces skin contraction and dilatation, structure's construction, re-organisation and deconstruction -- reactions no single layer could produce. Then give the music a literal role: Vaquie's score becomes "the main antagonist in our story, attracting/repelling those organisms, controlling their every move", routed through a node graph that lets any part of the audio reach any part of the visual and physical system. The division of labour inside the piece is explicit: Jacquet takes the graphical and geometric parts, Geilfus the procedural and organic parts. The organism that results is in "constant symbiosis with the sound" -- its behaviour driven by the system, not by a fixed timeline.

**Takeaway:** complexity is not drawn, it is layered -- one rule set per layer, each layer allowed to surprise the ones below it, and the music admitted as a layer with the same standing as any other.

## M3 -- Waveform to topography: the song cast in concrete

The Ecume mechanism (W14), the most literal statement of his practice. The tool analyses an album's waveforms into topographical imagery; the sonic landscapes are 3D-printed, silicone-moulded, cast in concrete, lit and photographed for the artwork. The pipeline is: audio waveform in, analysis by Geilfus's tool, topographical height-field out, 3D print, silicone mould, concrete cast, photography. The same analysis-to-form translation as the Murcof software (M1), with the time axis frozen: a song's waveform becomes a landscape you can hold. The tool outlived the album: the 2018 ETHER video reuses it, a concrete mountain built from the spectrogram, metal powder animated by sonic vibrations -- "The sounds really do generate the visuals here, from generating terrain from an analysis from the waveform." The attribution is split and must stay split: Geilfus developed the tool, Jacquet used it to make the artwork (C3307). The limited edition carries a discrepancy the study does not resolve: the press kit promises 25 black resin editions moulded from the same 3D print as the cover; CDM describes a later "ultra-limited handmade physical release" of 33cm x 33cm plates (C3308).

**Takeaway:** a live reactive system turned ninety degrees becomes an object -- freeze the time axis, let the waveform become a height-field, and the sound is no longer something you hear but something you stand on.

## M4 -- Interactive physical compositing: the video that pushes back

The OpenCV module from the Paleodictyon build, and the mechanism L'Orage (W08) performs in reverse. In Paleodictyon the module analyses Jacquet's videos, extracting polygons for collisions and grayscale gradients for effect strength, so that "graphical animation [could] physically collide with particles, scaring them off or attracting them". The projected image is not laid over the physical system; it enters it, as a force. In L'Orage the direction is reversed: a layer of generated shadows is added to the real shadows of the garden -- real tree shadows plus computer-generated shadows, fireflies, birds, anthropomorphic insects -- so the viewer cannot tell which shadows are real and which are drawn. The projection almost disappears into the world it augments. Both directions are the same mechanism: the projected layer and the physical world share one physics, and the boundary between them is the work. The division with Tardy on L'Orage is unrecorded; both are credited with visuals, and the study does not split the credit.

**Takeaway:** compositing is not overlaying -- let the projected layer collide with the physical one, or let it hide inside the physical one, and the seam between them becomes the piece.

## M5 -- The virtual-projector app: the mapping as a simulation

Paleodictyon -- The App (W11, 2012), the digital counterpart of the installation. Developed by Geilfus, who co-directed the original piece, the application "makes use of the same video mapping technique, however here it is instead transposed to a digital space, where virtual projectors give light to a 3D model of the museum. A digital replica." The mapping system is lifted out of the physical site and re-implemented as a simulation: the same content, the same technique, but the projectors are virtual and the museum is a model. The app was not run this leg and its current availability was not verified (C3362). It matters because it is the first time the tool becomes the work in its own right -- not a show, not a stage, but a piece of software the audience runs -- and it anticipates the Ecume tool (M3), where the software's output becomes a physical object. The tool has three lives in this practice: played live (M1), installed permanently (M6), and handed to the audience as a simulation (M5).

**Takeaway:** a mapping system does not need a building -- transpose the projectors into software and the museum into a model, and the technique itself is the piece the audience can run.

## M6 -- Generative software for permanent light: the system that outlives the show

Elytre, also called Flow (W15, Le Flow, Paris, 2016-2017), a forty-metre generative light work wrapping a floating building on the Seine at the foot of the Pont Alexandre III. "As a generative work, it has its own dedicated, custom made software programme" -- built by Eric Renaud-Houde and Simon Geilfus to Jacquet's design, music by Vaquie. The work "is inspired by the cycle of the seasons and explores our relationship with time. It is designed less as an invitation to a journey as an order to slow down. To take the time for contemplation." No source read describes the software's internals -- what drives the colour fields, what time scale the seasons run on, whether it responds to the river, the weather or the building -- and Geilfus's share versus Renaud-Houde's is unrecorded (C3375, C3320). The naming is unresolved: Jacquet's own press PDF calls the 2016 work "Flow" and the AntiVJ and Vimeo pages call it "Elytre"; the study treats them as the same work (C3363). What the mechanism means is clear even without the internals: after a decade of one-night mappings and tours, the system is installed to run indefinitely on a building. The toolmaker's job is now to write software that outlives the performance -- the opposite of the Murcof show, which existed only while it was played.

**Takeaway:** a live system made permanent changes its tense -- the software is no longer performed, it is left running, and the piece becomes the building's slow behaviour rather than the night's event.

## M7 -- The tool-first program: the system is the subject

The mechanism behind all the others, and the one the study's prompts must be built on. "He enjoys as much creating graphics and visual abstractions as creating the softwares and systems to generate them." The tool is not a means to the image; it is half the work, and in the strongest cases it is the work: the Murcof software is the piece the audience watches being played; the Ecume tool's output is cast in concrete; the Paleodictyon app is handed to the audience to run; the Elytre software is installed to run on a building indefinitely. The stopping rule from the blog post is the discipline that makes the program sustainable: "there's always a moment when you have to stop building new toys and start playing with the one you already have!" -- the lead and only developer, under time pressure, must choose playing over building. The teaching is the same program turned outward: the tools are taught in public, at festivals and schools, because the system is the subject and the subject can be shared. A Geilfus piece, built from this study, must show its system working -- the panels, the layers, the bindings -- not hide it behind a finished image.

**Takeaway:** when the tool is half the work, the piece must let the audience see the tool working; a finished image with the system hidden has missed the point.

## M8 -- Scale play: galaxies and atoms in the same frame

The imagery, stated in his own words: "visuals about the scale of galaxies and the scale of atoms." Geometric abstraction, never figurative -- "I'm usually working with some sort of geometric abstractions. I'm not that much into figurative things." The Murcof scenes run the whole scale ladder in one show: tons of stars that swirl around and become comets; a giant spider-like creature stretching its arms; bacteria holding hands and working in groups whilst maintaining their individuality; rays of light that become a network of neurons. Paleodictyon is the same jump made architectural: the hexagonal burrows of an invisible deep-sea creature, magnified to the size of a museum, on a building whose own frame is hexagonal -- "abolishes notions of scale by contrasting micro-architecture with human construction." Clair Obscur is the same jump fitted to stone: a church vault becomes a starfield, the Gothic arches framing a nebula like the ribs of a sky. The scale jump is not decoration; it is the content. The viewer is asked to hold two scales at once -- the cosmic and the microbial -- and the geometric abstraction is what lets them coexist in one frame, because a starfield and a bacterial colony are both, at the level of the code, a field of points with rules.

**Takeaway:** scale is a material, not a setting -- put the galaxy and the bacterium in the same system, at the same level of abstraction, and the jump between them is the picture.

## The aesthetic logic: the tool is the picture

The whole practice is one question, asked in eight different materials: what does this sound, this building, this organism look like when the code that draws it is allowed to be the subject? The answer is always a system, never a picture. In the early work the system is reactive: analyse the sound, drive the image. In the mapping years the system is layered: rules on rules, each layer surprising the ones below it, the music admitted as a layer. In the object years the system is frozen: the waveform becomes a height-field, the song becomes concrete. In the permanent years the system is left running: the building's slow behaviour is the piece. In the renewal the system feeds back: the visual elaborations define the album's sound structure, and the music answers the images it once led.

Two palettes carry the arc, and they are both black. The Murcof show is blackness with light in it: a dense field of tiny white star-points, huge flat white and grey geometric shards like broken planes of glass, a luminous blue wireframe cube over a starfield -- the dark theatre, the transparent screen, the performers visible through their own image. The Ecume objects are the same palette turned solid: grey concrete, fine vertical ridged lines, a black disc printed with a white radiating sunburst. The Elytre facade is the palette warmed: orange-red bands at dusk, small coloured rectangles at night, doubled in the river. The break is not decorative; it is the moment the system stops being played in the dark and starts being left running in daylight.

Three rules govern the work, and they are the rules a piece built from this study must obey. First, the system must be visible: the panels, the layers, the bindings are the subject, and a finished image with the system hidden has missed the point. Second, the sound must be a parameter, not a soundtrack: bound to the scene, routed through the node graph, admitted as an antagonist that attracts and repels -- a score that could belong to any image belongs to none. Third, scale is a material: the galaxy and the bacterium belong in the same frame, at the same level of abstraction, and the jump between them is the picture. A piece that draws a pretty starfield has missed the point; a piece that lets the audience watch the system draw the starfield, and change it by changing the sound, has found it.

## The network: six joint works, one fifteen-year collaboration

Geilfus's network is the AntiVJ label, and inside it the closest and most-evidenced collaborator is Vaquie -- six joint works, not the two #605 listed (C3420). Chateau des Ducs de Bretagne (2010, Nantes): visuals Geilfus, Jacquet, Lemercier, Tardy; music Vaquie -- the earliest evidenced joint work. Clair Obscur (2011, Neuchatel): visuals Jacquet and Geilfus; music Vaquie; live organ Simon Peguiron -- the three-way credit that rested on the Jacquet study alone in #605 is now confirmed by Vaquie's own site (C3419). Paleodictyon (2012): co-direction by Geilfus, Jacquet and Vaquie, the co-direction credit now independently confirmed from two sources (C3401). L'Orage (2012, Tokyo): visuals Tardy and Geilfus; music Vaquie. Ecume (2016): tool by Geilfus, artwork by Jacquet, music by Vaquie. Elytre / Flow (2016, Paris): software by Renaud-Houde and Geilfus, music Vaquie, concept Jacquet -- a joint work #605 did not list (C3402).

Jacquet is the co-director: Paleodictyon, Clair Obscur, Elytre, the Chateau mapping, the Scopitone 2010 mapping -- the Jacquet-Geilfus line runs through architecture, not through Murcof, and Murcof is NOT a direct Jacquet collaborator. The division inside Paleodictyon is explicit: Jacquet the graphical and geometric parts, Geilfus the procedural and organic parts. Tardy is a three-way credit, never a duo: Paleodictyon visual content, L'Orage visuals, the Chateau mapping; his closest musical collaborator is Vaquie (Omicron), not Geilfus. Lemercier is the thinnest direct link: the Chateau mapping and the Scopitone 2010 mapping, both collective; no Lemercier-Geilfus duo work found -- and the Murcof pre-history belongs to her, not to Geilfus: Boritch and Lemercier did the one-off Bristol 2008 gig with Murcof, its visuals improvised by Lemercier, a year before Geilfus was asked in (C3404). Ratsi has no direct joint work with Geilfus at all (C3403): the link is label membership and shared festival bills, and the triangle is Ratsi-Vaquie-Geilfus, with Vaquie as the hinge.

Murcof is the deepest collaboration, and the one that outlasts the label: fifteen years, from a buggy Processing prototype at Futuresonic, Manchester, May 2009, through a couple of dozen shows and the Alpha-ville transparent screen, to Twin Color, conceived at IRCAM in 2022 and premiered at IRCAM in 2023 and MUTEK Montreal in August 2024. Murcof's AntiVJ music came through Geilfus, not Vaquie (Ecume press kit; C3208) -- and the Geilfus link is the reason Murcof belongs in this network as a collaborator, not a label-mate. The commercial strand -- Flying Lotus at the Roundhouse, Stromae tour mapping software, Hugo Boss Black stage design, all 2011-2012, all single-sourced to Coded Matter(s) -- is the thinnest part of the record, and the study does not expand it beyond that source (C3407, C3408). The label itself is dormant, not formally closed; its event listings stop in October 2014, and Geilfus's post-label work runs through Murcof and direct commissions, not through a live label frame (C3414).
