## 1. Horsley and the first scholarship boy

Born 28 September 1917 in Horsley, Gloucestershire, the son of Edwin Joseph Somes (1882-1973), a professional musician, and Ethel M. M. Pridham (1889-1972), a schoolmistress; an elder brother, Laurence Joseph Somes (1913-1987). The parents' dates rest on private genealogical research cited by Wikipedia, not on an independently verified source. In 1933 or 1934 -- the sources split, Voices of British Ballet giving 1933 and Wikipedia, Britannica, the danze appreciation and the Royal Albert Hall catalogue giving 1934 -- he became the first boy ever to win a scholarship to the Sadler's Wells, then Vic-Wells, Ballet School. The likely reconciliation is that the scholarship was awarded in 1933 and he started at the school in 1934, aged seventeen, but no source says this explicitly, and 1934 is the majority reading. He joined the company in 1935 by the majority account, 1936 by Voices alone. The fact that matters is the first: a working boy from a Gloucestershire village, the son of a musician and a schoolmistress, entering a school that had never before given a boy a scholarship -- the first male dancer the English school made for itself, rather than imported. [C34229]

**Takeaway:** the English prince is not born, he is admitted -- the first boy on a scholarship, and the whole career that follows is the school proving its own judgement. [C34230]

## 2. Les Patineurs and Horoscope: the discovery

He was first noticed in the premiere of Ashton's Les Patineurs in 1937, "jumping right out of the corps de ballet" in the danze appreciation's phrase; Robert Helpmann reportedly decided then that Somes would one day replace him. No named solo role is recorded -- he was a corps dancer who stood out by elevation alone. A year later came the first leading role: Horoscope, Ashton to a score by Constant Lambert, first performed 27 January 1938 at the Sadler's Wells Theatre, with Somes and Fonteyn creating the principal roles, the Leo man and the Virgo woman, born under "disjoint" sun signs. One critic called him "potentially the finest British male dancer of the half century", and the ballet made stars of both principal dancers. It was lost when the company fled Holland at the start of the war, and the score was later read as a symbolic representation of Lambert's affair with Fonteyn -- the ballet that made him was gone within two years of its making, and the career it launched had to be rebuilt from nothing. [C34231]

**Takeaway:** the discovery is elevation, not acting -- a corps dancer jumps out of the line, and within a year he is the leading man; the jump is the first mechanism, and everything later is built on what the jump promised. [C34232]

## 3. War: four years and a fall from a lorry

Four years' armed service in the Second World War, culminating in a serious injury that threatened his career -- the sources agree on the shape and disagree on nothing else, because no source read names his regiment or unit, and the injury itself is recalled only as a bad fall from a lorry. He returned and rebuilt himself as the noble prince rather than the virtuoso, and the injury is the plausible pivot, though no source states it as cause. The war also took Horoscope itself, lost in the flight from Holland, so the ballet that had made him at twenty-one no longer existed when he came back at twenty-eight. The post-war career is therefore a second beginning, not a resumption: the same dancer, the same jump, but the roles now built on line, stamina and partnering rather than on the brilliance the pre-war critics had praised. [C34233]

**Takeaway:** the war is the hinge inside the hinge -- four years out, a fall from a lorry, a lost ballet, and the dancer who comes back is the prince, not the virtuoso. [C34234]

## 4. Symphonic Variations: the most exhausting role in ballet

Symphonic Variations, Ashton to Cesar Franck, premiered 24 April 1946 at the Royal Opera House, with Somes in the lead middle male role opposite Fonteyn, with Pamela May, Moira Shearer, Brian Shaw and Henry Danton. Six dancers who never leave the stage for about twenty minutes; the lead male role is "widely known as the most exhausting in all ballet", in Alastair Macaulay's phrase, "the more so as its style should remain limpid and Apollonian throughout". The premiere was delayed a month by an injury to Somes, so Helpmann's Adam Zero opened the Covent Garden season instead, on 10 April. It was a success from the outset, and Somes later staged it himself -- a 1992 production under his direction -- and Ashton left the rights to Symphonic Variations to him in his will, with Cinderella, because those were the ballets he thought would bring money and he wanted the right people to have them. The mechanism is already visible here: exhaustion hidden inside calm, the hardest role in the repertory danced as if it cost nothing. [C34235]

**Takeaway:** the signature is set at twenty-eight -- the most exhausting male role in ballet, danced limpid and Apollonian, the effort invisible and the calm the whole point. [C34236]

## 5. Scenes de ballet: the geometric prince

Scenes de ballet, Ashton to Stravinsky, premiered 11 February 1948 at the Royal Opera House, created for Fonteyn and Somes, who was her main partner in the first performances. Ashton's Wikipedia biography dates it 1947, but the dedicated article, the Ashton Foundation and the Stravinsky catalogue all give 11 February 1948, and 1948 is used. It is plotless, neo-classical, geometrically devised -- Ashton bought a second-hand Euclid and built figures "that could be seen from any angle" -- and the five supporting male roles demanded "a polished dash and graphic dynamism that had not been seen before in British male dancing", in the Arts Desk's phrase. The ballet is the English answer to Balanchine: classical line turned into geometry, the male dancer as a moving angle rather than a leaping hero, and Somes at its centre as the partner who holds the geometry together. [C34237]

**Takeaway:** the prince becomes a figure in a diagram -- geometry you can see from any angle, and the partner is the one who keeps the angles true. [C34238]

## 6. Cinderella: the original Prince

Cinderella, Ashton to Prokofiev, premiered 23 December 1948 at the Royal Opera House. Somes was the original Prince opposite Moira Shearer as Cinderella -- Fonteyn took the role for the 1949 American debut -- with Alexander Grant as the Jester and Ashton and Helpmann en travesti as the stepsisters. It was Ashton's first major three-act ballet for a British company; some critics found the choreography intermittently weak, but the stepsisters' comedy was a lasting favourite, and a 2003 retrospective recalled "the gallantry of her Prince, Michael Somes". Ashton left Cinderella to Somes in his will, and his widow Wendy Ellis Somes now stages it worldwide. The role is the archetype of the whole first career: the prince as gallantry, not display -- the man whose job is to make the ballerina the whole picture, and whose own dancing is the frame around her. [C34239]

**Takeaway:** the Prince is a frame, not a portrait -- gallantry as a technique, the man's dancing built to show the woman, and Ashton leaves him the ballet because he trusted the frame to hold. [C34240]

## 7. Ballet Imperial and the walk offstage

Ballet Imperial, Balanchine to Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 2, was staged by Balanchine himself in London in 1950, with Somes as one of the three leads, with Fonteyn and Beryl Grey, in the company's first staging of the plotless showcase. No exact premiere date for the Sadler's Wells staging was found in the sources read; 1950 only is used. His most-quoted single moment in the whole career came in it, and it was not a jump: "his walk off the stage at the end of the second movement of Ballet Imperial is always quoted as the supreme example of simplicity and dignity", in the ballet.co appreciation's words. A plain walk -- upright, unhurried, no flourish -- that the audience remembers as the emotional peak of the ballet. The mechanism is subtraction: the dancer refuses the exit flourish, no bow, no final pose, and lets the walk itself carry the meaning. It is the stage equivalent of his partnering doctrine: the ego removed, the image left clean. [C34241]

**Takeaway:** the most famous moment of his career is a walk -- subtraction as the climax, the dancer doing nothing extra at the exact moment when display is expected. [C34242]

## 8. Succeeding Helpmann: the official partner

In 1950 Somes succeeded Robert Helpmann as Fonteyn's official, regular partner, and from 1951 to 1962 he was the company's lead male dancer. The partnership ran from Horoscope in 1938 through Symphonic Variations, Cinderella, Daphnis and Chloe, Sylvia and Ondine, and through the classics -- Swan Lake, Giselle, The Sleeping Beauty -- coached in the classics by Tamara Karsavina, who told Fonteyn: "You have the finest partner in the world. I only had Nijinsky." The line is reported speech from a BalletcoForum discussion of Dynamo, not a verified verbatim quote, and it is carried as such. De Valois could announce Antoinette Sibley's Swan Lake debut at days' notice with the assurance "there was no need to worry as Somes would be her partner", and he "danced with every ballerina in the company, often being happy to give a young debutante the immense benefit of his calm and unobtrusive help". The partnership was the institution: for twelve years, the English style had one male dancer at its centre, and his job was to be the certainty the ballerina could commit to. [C34243]

**Takeaway:** the partner is the institution -- for twelve years the company's leading man is the man whose catch is certain, and every debutante's first night rests on him. [C34244]

## 9. Daphnis, Tiresias, Sylvia: the Ashton creations of 1951-52

Three creations in eighteen months, all Ashton, all opposite Fonteyn. Daphnis and Chloe, to Ravel, premiered in 1951 at the Royal Opera House, Somes creating Daphnis opposite Fonteyn's Chloe, with Violetta Elvin as Lykanion, Philip Chatfield as Dorkon, Alexander Grant as Bryaxis and Alfred Rodrigues as Pan. Critics initially misunderstood it; "continued acquaintance soon recognized that it is in fact one of Ashton's most moving and rewarding short ballets". The premiere date is split: Wikipedia and dancersforum give 5 April 1951, the Royal Ballet and Opera production page gives 3 April 1951, and the split could not be resolved from the sources read -- 5 April is used, with the RBO variant noted. Tiresias, to Lambert's original score, premiered 9 July 1951, Somes creating Tiresias the male and Fonteyn Tiresias the female, a double-gendered title role in a prelude and three acts, designs by Isabel Lambert; twelve London performances in 1951, then a short run dwindling to two in 1955, a creation that never took lasting hold. Sylvia, to Delibes, premiered 3 September 1952, Somes as Aminta, the shepherd lover, opposite Fonteyn's Sylvia, with John Hart as Orion and Alexander Grant as Eros; Ashton re-choreographed the 1876 Merante ballet as "a garland presented to the ballerina", in Clive Barnes's phrase, a tribute to Fonteyn, with Somes as the devoted pastoral partner, and the production popularised the work after seven earlier commercial failures. [C34245]

**Takeaway:** the creations come in a run, and the run is the point -- Ashton builds his classicism around one man's particular gifts, and the gifts are line, stamina and partnering, not virtuoso brilliance. [C34246]

## 10. The classics: Florimund, Siegfried, Albrecht, Ivan

In the classics he was the archetypal danseur noble, and the record is thinner than the Ashton creations because the roles were repertory, not premieres. He danced Florimund in The Sleeping Beauty opposite Fonteyn's Aurora through the 1950s, named in the La Scala cast of 13 October 1954 and in the NBC television Producer's Showcase telecast of 1955, with Ashton himself as Carabosse; a 1952 note records his Florimund being taken over by John Hart after Somes went to Paris to study with Volinine, evidence he danced the role into at least 1952, and the Act 3 pas de deux with Fonteyn was described as the "high point of the ballet", Somes "in total sync with her". He danced Siegfried in Swan Lake opposite Fonteyn's Odette-Odile, named in the La Scala cast of 17 October 1954, the 1954 television version and Act II in The Film of the Royal Ballet of 1959; the ballet.co appreciation says "there is a film of Act 2 of Swan Lake which wonderfully shows the sympathy between them and the strength of his support", and programme notes describe "Fonteyn's Odette... all fluttering vulnerability with Somes as an ardent, caring protector". Britannica lists Giselle among the classical ballets he performed, and the Dynamo review notes photographs of Somes "partnering Alicia Markova as Giselle", with "just one supporting hand visible... which makes [her] look as if he has arrested [her] in mid flight" -- but no specific date, venue or cast list naming him as Albrecht was found in the sources read, and the Markova partnership is the concrete evidence, the rest repertoire-list assertion. In the Royal Ballet's 1954 revival of Fokine's The Firebird he made "an unforgettable impression... as the slightly simple Prince Ivan", repeated opposite Nadia Nerina's Firebird and in The Film of the Royal Ballet, where the Firebird episode is the second of three tragic tales. No first-Siegfried, first-Florimund or first-Albrecht date was found in the sources read. [C34247]

**Takeaway:** the classics are the repertory, not the premieres -- the prince as a job done every night, and the evidence is a film of Act II and a photograph of one hand, not a first night. [C34248]

## 11. Ondine: Palemon, the last great Ashton creation

Ondine, Ashton to Henze, premiered 27 October 1958 at the Royal Opera House. Somes created Palemon, the mortal prince torn between the water-nymph Ondine -- Fonteyn, for whom the title role was made -- and his betrothed Berta, danced by Julia Farron, with Alexander Grant as Tirrenio, Lord of the Mediterranean. First-night reviews were unanimous about Fonteyn's triumph but "mixed, half-hearted" about the rest, including Henze's "difficult" score; the ballet left the repertoire in 1966. Somes held the role until the first cast change in March 1961, when Beriosova and MacLeary took it, and an Ondine excerpt closed The Film of the Royal Ballet in 1959. Palemon is the last of the great Ashton creations on him, and the role is defined by divided loyalty rather than display -- a prince who cannot choose, danced by a man of forty-one whose own leading-man years were nearly over. In 1988 Anthony Dowell danced Palemon in the Ondine revival, explicitly "the role originated by Michael Somes" -- a named role inheritance, thirty years on. [C34249]

**Takeaway:** the last creation is a prince who cannot choose -- divided loyalty instead of display, and the role outlives him into another dancer's body. [C34250]

## 12. The hinge: 1961-62, the arrival

Britannica says he retired as premier danseur in 1961; Wikipedia and Voices of British Ballet say he was lead male dancer until Nureyev's arrival in 1962. The two are compatible if 1961 is the last leading roles and 1962 the formal handover, but no source spells this out. What the sources agree on is the shape: his retirement from leading roles "more or less coincided with the arrival of Nureyev", in the ballet.co appreciation's words, and the Fonteyn-Nureyev partnership eclipsed him just as he was relinquishing performance. He was forty-four. He did not leave -- he changed register. The hinge is the whole study in one season: the prince steps aside, the new partnership takes the stage, and the man who was the certainty of the company for sixteen years becomes, within two years, the father in the ballets that define the new partnership. [C34251]

**Takeaway:** the hinge is a single season -- forty-four, the new partnership arrives, and the prince does not leave, he changes register. [C34252]

## 13. Marguerite and Armand: the father who breaks the lovers

Marguerite and Armand, Ashton to Liszt, premiered 12 March 1963 at the Royal Opera House. Somes created Armand's father -- the role that breaks the lovers apart by demanding Marguerite renounce Armand. The ballet was made for Fonteyn and Nureyev, the new partnership that had displaced Somes as lead male; Somes, now assistant director, played the stern patriarch in a work that is effectively one extended pas de deux between the two principals, with the father scene as the exception. Britannica calls it a "pantomime role". The New York Public Library holds a 1963-64 filmed rehearsal naming "Michael Somes (his father)". The casting is the mechanism made visible: the displaced partner returns to the stage as the authority that forbids the love, the man who held Fonteyn for sixteen years now telling her, in character, to give the new lover up. [C34253]

**Takeaway:** the displaced partner comes back as the father -- the authority that breaks the lovers, played by the man who held her for sixteen years. [C34254]

## 14. Lord Capulet: the father a generation remembers

Lord Capulet in MacMillan's Romeo and Juliet, premiered 9 February 1965 at the Royal Opera House, in the original cast with Fonteyn and Nureyev as the lovers, David Blair as Mercutio, Desmond Doyle as Tybalt, Anthony Dowell as Benvolio and Julia Farron as Lady Capulet. MacMillan's first full-length ballet; the premiere took 43 curtain calls. Somes was still playing Capulet in 1973, in a 14 May 1973 cast, and in Paul Czinner's 1966 film of the production. The ballet.co appreciation: "a whole generation will remember him principally as Juliet's father". The role is the same mechanism as Armand's father, held for eight years on stage and fixed on film: authority without display, the same upright carriage that made his Ballet Imperial exit famous, now doing dramatic work in mime and presence, the prince grown into the patriarch. [C34255]

**Takeaway:** a whole generation remembers him as Juliet's father -- the prince's carriage, kept, and turned into a father's authority. [C34256]

## 15. Mayerling: the emperor at sixty

Mayerling, MacMillan to Liszt, premiered 14 February 1978 at the Royal Opera House. Somes created Emperor Franz Josef, father of Crown Prince Rudolf, danced by David Wall, opposite Georgina Parkinson as Empress Elisabeth -- and his own wife Wendy Ellis as Princess Stephanie, Rudolf's wife. The first-night cast was "a Who's Who of The Royal Ballet". A late-career patriarch role at age sixty, fourteen years after his last leading-role season, and the last of the three father roles: Armand's father, Lord Capulet, the Emperor. MacMillan died backstage at a Mayerling revival in 1992, fourteen years after Somes created the Emperor in it -- the ballet that holds the two men's ends together, the choreographer's death in the wings of the work in which the dancer played the father. [C34257]

**Takeaway:** the third father is an emperor -- sixty years old, still creating roles, the authority now absolute, and the ballet outlives them both. [C34258]

## 16. Summer Interlude: the one choreography

Somes "tried his hand at choreography only once, with a slight but pleasing piece called Summer Interlude for the smaller Sadler's Wells Theatre Ballet", in the ballet.co appreciation's words, premiered 28 March 1950 to Respighi's Ancient Airs and Dances, 71 performances over four seasons, 1950-53. "Many people have built successful careers on much less promising beginnings, but for some reason Somes never made another ballet." The fact is the point: a dancer who could remember a ballet after one viewing, who staged Ashton's works for forty years, who ran the company classes and coached every ballerina in it, made exactly one ballet of his own and stopped. He was, in the book's phrase, a "sponge" and a "telephone wire" -- the conduit, not the inventor. The one choreography is the negative space of the career: everything he knew went into keeping other people's ballets alive, not into making his own. [C34259]

**Takeaway:** one ballet, then never again -- the memory, the staging, the coaching, all of it spent keeping other people's work alive, and the single choreography is the proof he chose the conduit over the invention. [C34260]

## 17. Three marriages

In 1954 he married Deirdre Annette Dixon (1934-1959), a junior soloist of the Royal Ballet. She died in a London hospital on 28 May 1959, aged twenty-five -- the death certificate gives cerebral abscess and meningitis, her Stage obituary of 4 June 1959 attributed the death to the effects of a car accident two years earlier, and the two accounts are unresolved in the sources read. In 1964 he married Antoinette Sibley; they divorced in 1973. Later he married Wendy Ellis, a former principal ballerina; no marriage year is given in any source read, and she survives him, now producing Ashton's Cinderella and Symphonic Variations worldwide -- the two ballets Ashton left to Somes, willed by him to Ellis on his death in 1994. No children are documented in any source read; that is not stated as childlessness, only as undocumented. The marriages trace the same arc as the career: the young soloist, the prima ballerina, the staging partner -- and the last of them is the one who carries the ballets on after him. [C34261]

**Takeaway:** the marriages follow the career -- the soloist, the prima, the staging partner -- and the last one inherits the ballets, not the house. [C34262]

## 18. The brake-hand pirouette

In supported pirouettes Somes used minimal contact -- one hand, placed to stop the turn rather than to drive it. His hands acted "as a brake rather than an accelerator", in the Seeing Dance review of Dynamo's words, "utterly different to that taught today". The period Western convention corroborates it: the man supports the ballerina on one side with one hand, she turns, and he arrests her -- the same one-handed stop Balanchine expected. Photographs of him partnering Alicia Markova in Giselle and Nadia Nerina in The Firebird show "just one supporting hand visible to the audience which makes both look as if he has arrested them in mid flight". The ballerina appears to turn on her own momentum and be caught -- the man's effort is invisible, so the turn reads as her flight, not his push. The mechanism only works if the placement is exact every night, which is why it belongs to the reliability system, not to strength: a brake hand placed an inch wrong is a dropped partner. [C34263]

**Takeaway:** the hand is a brake, not an accelerator -- one hand, placed to stop the turn, and the ballerina's flight is hers because his effort is invisible. [C34264]

## 19. The partner who disappears

A partnering doctrine, not just modesty: "his partnering technique required that he not hide the faults of his partner but use his own self-effacing support to show her off to best advantage", in Woodcock's phrase via Seeing Dance. The man's job is to make the woman the whole picture -- lifts and supports engineered so the audience sees her line, not his strength. The ballet.co appreciation puts it as "calm and unobtrusive help", and notes a film of Act II Swan Lake "which wonderfully shows the sympathy between them and the strength of his support". Lifts look weightless because the man's hands are hidden or single; the eye never leaves the ballerina; the partnership reads as one body with the woman in front of it. It is the opposite of the modern showy partner who displays his own power -- and it is the same mechanism as the walk offstage in Ballet Imperial: the ego removed, the image left clean. [C34265]

**Takeaway:** the partner disappears so the ballerina appears -- the lift is engineered so the audience sees her line, never his strength. [C34266]

## 20. Reliability earned, not born

Somes was not a natural safe pair of hands. "Given his later reputation, it is surprising to know that as a young man, Somes was not the most reliable of partners. Hard work changed that", in the ballet.co appreciation's words. Reliability was a trained capacity -- repetition until the support was automatic. It is the mechanism behind the brake hand and the disappearing partner: you can only afford a one-handed brake if the placement is exact every night, and you can only disappear if the catch is certain. The famous dependability itself is the evidence: de Valois could announce Sibley's Swan Lake debut at days' notice with the assurance "there was no need to worry as Somes would be her partner", and he "danced with every ballerina in the company, often being happy to give a young debutante the immense benefit of his calm and unobtrusive help". Watchable as total absence of visible anxiety in the ballerina -- she commits fully because the catch is certain. Sibley danced her first Swan Lake partnered by Somes on 24 October 1959. [C34267]

**Takeaway:** reliability is trained, not born -- the young man was not the most reliable of partners, and the hard work that changed it is the whole of the later reputation. [C34268]

## 21. The danseur noble and the 90-degree arabesque

The classical prince built on carriage, proportion and authority rather than virtuoso tricks. "As a dancer he was the archetypal Royal Ballet prince, noble and mature, and although never the most brilliant of technicians, his stage presence and authority gave a weight to the classical roles rarely seen nowadays", in the ballet.co appreciation's words; the National Portrait Gallery: "Noted as a danseur noble". On the company's early-1960s Russia tour he "was recognised there as a danseur noble, a type of dancer who had all but disappeared from the Soviet stage". Early on he had "matinee idol looks and exceptional jump", but the mature style subordinated the jump to line. The English style as he enforced it in the studio: extensions held exactly at 90 degrees -- hip height, no higher -- with the arm kept down in the line, against the "Russian" look of higher legs and higher arms. Bryony Brind, coached by Somes for her 1981 Swan Lake debut: "I would take my arm a little higher and my leg would go up... as Michael said, 'Told you so, it's far too high. Can't you be more "English"?'" -- and by the end of her career, she notes, "everybody was going, four [turns], and then going into an arabesque up there". The arabesque reads as contained and classical -- the leg stops dead at horizontal, the shape a clean right angle, the line legible rather than spectacular. A modern eye reads it as held back; in Somes's grammar it was correctness. [C34269]

**Takeaway:** nobility is weight, not display -- the leg stops dead at ninety degrees, the shape a clean right angle, and restraint is the style, not the failure of it. [C34270]

## 22. Slowness as technique

Tempo control in the supported adagio: after the turns in the Swan Lake Act II pas de deux, Somes demanded "two really slow ones", in Brind's words -- turns taken at half the speed a virtuoso would use, arriving in the pose rather than snapping into it, ending in a still, exact 90-degree arabesque. A turn that takes its time is harder, not easier -- balance must be held through the whole rotation with no momentum to hide behind. Watchable as a long, suspended rotation ending in stillness. It belongs to the pre-Vaganova performance style of the 1950s West, which was faster and less deliberately finished than today's slow, sculpted Vaganova-influenced performance -- yet within that, Somes's supported turns were deliberately, conspicuously slow, the slowness itself the signature. The mechanism is the same as the brake hand and the walk: time spent, not time saved, and the difficulty hidden inside the calm. [C34271]

**Takeaway:** slowness is the technique -- two really slow turns, balance held through the whole rotation with no momentum to hide behind, and the stillness at the end is the point. [C34272]

## 23. The one-view memory: the living archive

Somes could "remember accurately the steps upon seeing a ballet performed", in the New York Public Library catalog summary of the 1972 John Gruen interview -- a catalog summary, not the interview audio, so the memory claim is second-hand from NYPL's own summary, not a quote from Somes, and it is carried as such. This is the cognitive mechanism behind his entire second career: he carried the Ashton repertory in his head, which is why he could stage and revive ballets without notation and why Ashton left him Cinderella and Symphonic Variations in his will. In rehearsal, a ballet reproduced from memory after a single viewing; in performance history, his revivals -- Enigma Variations for Birmingham Royal Ballet, his last work, "immensely successful"; Cinderella in Sweden; Symphonic Variations at American Ballet Theatre; Job at Coventry Cathedral -- all staged with his widow Wendy Ellis Somes assisting. The archive is a person, not a shelf, and when the person dies the archive has to be carried by the widow. [C34273]

**Takeaway:** the archive is a person -- a ballet remembered after one viewing, staged without notation, and the repertory survives because one man's memory held it. [C34274]

## 24. The martinet rehearsal

Coaching by relentless repetition under pressure, with no praise until the detail is exact. Rupert Christiansen in the Literary Review: "a ruthless taskmaster and martinet in the rehearsal room. Nothing and nobody would be spared in the cause of the art for which he lived. Chairs were thrown, insults screamed, reputations trashed and 99 per cent of what he saw was branded as miserable." The engine inside the brutality was faith: "he knew you could do it", in Antoinette Sibley's words as quoted by Christiansen. Lynn Seymour, taken through Swan Lake by him: "he'll kill me and it'll be tears and everything but that's the only way I'll get through it." Brind: "he was the taskmaster. He really took me through it... he didn't let up on anything." The ballet.co appreciation: "a hard taskmaster, insisting that the dancers went on working until every detail was right, and the works gained immeasurably." The method's object was "purity of style" and "the value of the smallest detail". The temper was real and consequential: he left the company for a period in the 1980s "after an argument in which he knocked Kenneth MacMillan down" -- the ballet.co appreciation is the only source for this, and it is flagged as a single-source anecdote. A small, human counter-detail from Brind: before her Swan Lake coaching sessions he "insisted I had half a glass of Guinness and a Mars Bar before I started" -- the taskmaster's odd, practical superstition about energy. [C34275]

**Takeaway:** the rehearsal is a forge -- chairs thrown, insults screamed, nothing spared, and the brutality works because he knew you could do it. [C34276]

## 25. The whole-theatre eye

Somes staged ballets by walking the house, not from the stalls. Wendy Ellis Somes, in the Ballet Association interview of 2004: "She learnt a great deal from Michael about production, theatre, sightlines. He would take her to every part of the theatre to see how a production looked, even into the dome of the Opera House." The mechanism: verify the image from every seat, including the worst ones, and adjust spacing, sightlines and the proscenium -- she later had a proscenium arch raised so all parts of the house could see a set, his lesson applied. The same interview records the teaching content he passed on: "the style, port de bras, clean lines, neat footwork rather than the more acrobatic style of many newer works." His stagings read clearly from the back of the house; the Ashton ballets as he left them are spatially legible, with clean lines and neat footwork rather than acrobatic display. A 1963 newspaper interview says he was "largely responsible for the company classes which the dancers take daily" -- but no source describes the content of his classes, and no account of it is invented here. [C34277]

**Takeaway:** the staging is checked from the worst seat -- walk the whole house, even into the dome, and the ballet is legible from the back row or it is not finished. [C34278]

## 26. The guardian: assistant director, teacher, repetiteur

Assistant director of the Royal Ballet under Ashton, 1963-1970, unanimous across Wikipedia, the National Portrait Gallery, Britannica and Voices of British Ballet. After 1970 he was principal teacher and repetiteur, with special responsibility for Ashton's ballets; the National Portrait Gallery gives principal repetiteur 1970-85, Britannica says "principal teacher until 1984", Voices of British Ballet says he "retired from The Royal Ballet in 1984" -- a one-year discrepancy, unresolved. He "forged the Sibley-Dowell partnership", in the National Portrait Gallery's words, "raised standards within the Company", and was the keeper of the Ashton repertoire. His last work was the revival of Ashton's Enigma Variations for Birmingham Royal Ballet. Post-retirement he produced, with Wendy Ellis, Ashton's Cinderella for the Royal Swedish Ballet and Symphonic Variations for Dutch National Ballet and then American Ballet Theatre. The Seeing Dance review says he was treated shabbily at his exit, with no proper farewell gala -- "shunted out of the back door", fifty years of service ending without ceremony. Royal Ballet performances were dedicated to his memory within days of his death: La Valse on 21 and 30 November 1994, and Homage to The Queen on 15 December 1994. [C34279]

**Takeaway:** the power moves from the stage to the rehearsal room -- the guardian keeps the ballets alive for twenty years, and the company says goodbye with a dedication, not a gala. [C34280]

## 27. Aesthetic logic: the prince, the father, the memory

The career has three acts and one mechanism running through all three: subtraction. As a prince he subtracts the display -- the jump subordinated to line, the pirouette stopped by one hand, the lift engineered so the eye never leaves the woman, the arabesque held at ninety degrees, the turns taken slowly, the exit a plain walk. As a father he subtracts the dancing itself -- Armand's father, Lord Capulet, the Emperor are authority carried in carriage and mime, the same upright bearing doing dramatic work now that elevation is gone. As a guardian he subtracts himself altogether -- the ballets staged from memory, the classes run, the partnerships forged, the repertory kept alive by a man who made exactly one ballet of his own and spent the rest of his working life on other people's. The through-line is the English style as he understood it: clean lines, neat footwork, the value of the smallest detail, the image legible from the back of the house. "If Fonteyn was the heart of the Royal Ballet, Somes was its soul and moral centre", in an observer's line quoted by Christiansen -- useful as the closing beat, not as a mechanism. The soul and moral centre is the man who disappears: from the lift, from the exit, from the stage, from the company, until only the ballets are left, held in his memory and then in his widow's. [C34281]

**Takeaway:** subtraction is the style -- the display removed, the dancing removed, the self removed, until only the ballet is left, held in one man's memory. [C34282]
