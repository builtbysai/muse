# Carlos Cruz-Diez: Study Notes

**Site:** Carlos Cruz-Diez, born 17 August 1923 at Caracas, Venezuela, died 27 July 2019 at Paris, aged 95 -- Franco-Venezuelan kinetic artist, the man who took colour off the canvas and set it loose in space: green and red lines that make a yellow that is not there, strips of cardboard and aluminium in two levels that trap light and multiply tones as you walk past, afterimages held still, moire from superimposed frequencies, and from 1965 walk-in chambers of pure red, green and blue light in which the visitor's own body becomes the site of the work. He proposed colour as an autonomous reality that develops in time and space, without the aid of form or the need for support, in eight named investigations from 1959 to 1995, projected in parallel from 1967 at monumental scale into airports, dams, stations and plazas. The Caracas school friend of Soto and Otero, the last survivor of the Venezuelan kinetic triad, he settled in Paris in 1960 and stayed there for the rest of his life, exhibiting in The Responsive Eye at MoMA in 1965 -- the show Soto refused -- and representing Venezuela at the Venice Biennale in 1970 (C5601, C5602, C5606, C5610, C5613, C5701).

## The life: the kite, the cola bottle, and the colour that left the canvas

Carlos Eduardo Cruz-Diez was born on 17 August 1923 in La Pastora, Caracas, the son of Carlos Eduardo Cruz Lander, a chemist and poet of Canarian descent, and Mariana Diez Feo; through his mother he was the great-grandson of General Mariano Diez, who fought in the Venezuelan independence struggle (C5601). As a child he was fascinated by the red projections cast on his white shirt by sunlight passing through cola bottles in his father's plant -- the anecdote he himself returned to as the seed of his lifelong attention to colour as light rather than pigment (C5601). He studied at the Colegio Atenas and the Liceo Andres Bello, and in 1940, aged 17, entered the Escuela de Artes Plasticas y Aplicadas in Caracas, graduating in 1945 as a Professor of Applied Arts and Manual Arts (C5603). His teachers included Marcos Castillo and Luis Alfredo Lopez Mendez; from Rafael Ramon Gonzalez he learned not to paint "the flat and evident color of objects" but to interpret the nuances composing it -- a lesson he later cited as a step toward his discovery that colour lives in the space around us (C5603). At the school he became friends with Alejandro Otero and Jesus Rafael Soto, who would later become his peers in geometric abstraction and the kinetic movement (C5603). While studying he drew cartoons for La Esfera and Tricolor, and in 1944 worked as an illustrator and graphic designer for El Farol, the magazine of the Creole Petroleum Corporation (C5604).

His painting in the 1940s was social realism: the marginalised and precarious sectors of Caracas, marked by social inequality (C5604). Works named from this period include Paisaje de los Andes (1941), Los tres comisarios (1943), Adan el chichero, Las aguadoras and Techo; his first solo exhibition was at the Venezuelan-American Institute in Caracas in 1947 (C5604). In 1946 he was appointed creative director of the advertising agency McCann-Erickson Venezuela, rising to art director by 25; he left in 1951 and worked as an illustrator for El Nacional from 1953 (C5604). A key influence was the 1947 Caracas exhibition of Hector Poleo on his return from Mexico: Poleo's paintings of modern social outcasts showed Cruz-Diez that social reality could be painted differently, "without the Cezannian brushstroke" (C5604). El Papagayo Verde (W01, 1949) places the green kite of the title in the slum areas on the edges of Caracas to narrate extreme urban inequality (C5604). But in the BOMB interview he is blunt about the limits he later saw in this phase: his figurative paintings denounced shantytowns and poverty, but "rich people were the ones buying those little paintings of shantytowns in which I denounced poverty. I was fooling myself" (C5604). Representing the problems offered no solution to the communities depicted, and his social calling had to manifest through another language (C5605).

The turn came in 1954, influenced by his study of the Bauhaus and the European avant-gardes: projects for outdoor murals using geometric elements, shown at the XV Official Salon in 1954, "sparked surprise due to his shift toward abstraction" (C5605). In his own dating, 1954 is when he began "very focused investigations on how discourse could change in order for art to become more participatory" (C5605). Between 1954 and 1959 his work passed through study stages he named Signos Vegetales (1955-1956), Objetos Ritmos Moviles (1956-1957) and Estudio de la percepcion Optica y la inestabilidad del plano (1956-1959), alongside intense reading and research on the chromatic phenomenon (C5605). The mural projects of 1954 could be manipulated: they changed with the movement of the sun, creating shadows and animating the surface with radiant reflections of colour (C5605). In 1955 he travelled to Europe for the first time, living for a year and a half in El Masnou, near Barcelona, intending to go on to Paris but staying to learn French; he travelled to Paris three or four times in that period (C5605). In Paris in 1955 he visited the exhibition Le Mouvement at Galerie Denise Rene -- where his friend Soto was participating -- arriving just after it closed and meeting Denise Rene as she took the works down; he later said he discovered he was "already in sync with the new discourse of creating work with which the viewer could directly interact" although he had seen no photographs of Soto's new work and knew nothing of Agam, Tinguely or Pol Bury (C5605). He must not be listed as a Le Mouvement exhibitor: he visited as a spectator; his first Denise Rene group show was Mouvement 2 in 1964-65 (C5710). He returned to Caracas in late 1956 (other sources say 1957), founded the Estudio de Artes Visuales, a graphic and industrial design studio, in 1957, and served as Assistant Director and Professor at the Caracas School of Fine Arts 1958-1960 (C5605, C5607).

Two things he did NOT do in this period, in his own words: he did not take part in the Ciudad Universitaria project of architect Carlos Raul Villanueva ("No, I didn't participate in that"), because his figurative, socially denunciatory work had no room in it; and he did not join Los Disidentes -- "I was in Caracas when they formed; I never agreed with them", because they attacked the post-Impressionist landscape painters who had been his professors (C5617). He must not be called a member or co-founder of Los Disidentes either.

The breakthrough was double, and both halves landed in 1959: his first Additive Colour (Couleur Additive) and his first Fisicromia (C5606). In Additive Yellow (W05, 1959) two painted lines, green and red, make a yellow line appear between them although no yellow paint is present -- a phenomenon Cruz-Diez derived from Edwin Land's investigation, itself derived from an experiment with screens by Isaac Newton (C5606). The yellow is not on the canvas but on the observer's retina; Cruz-Diez called this a "colour-event" (C5606). Fisicromia 1 (W06, 1959) takes the same phenomenon off the flat plane: colour appears as painted edges on a series of cardboard strips, so the strips in relief let colour addition combine with reflection, and the viewer's movement parallel and perpendicular to the work multiplies the effects (C5606). In his own words, "The Physichromie are like light traps, a space where a series of color strips interact and transform one another. They generate new ranges of color and invade the space that surrounds the vertical bands that cover the entire work" (C5606). He displayed the first Fisicromias at the Museo de Bellas Artes in Caracas in 1960; the critical reaction was hostile -- he was accused of having lost his creativity to graphic design -- but when he showed the same series in Caracas in 1966, half the works sold within two hours of opening (C5616). In a 1960 letter to Soto he wrote of the Fisicromia: "I believe this is the greatest step of my life, my most solid and definitive step yet" (C5616). The same letter asked Soto to discuss the first Physichromias with Denise Rene and Victor Vasarely (C5706). From 1959 on he refused the labels "Latin American" or "American" art for his work and promoted a "universal" art (C5606).

In the fall of 1960 he settled permanently in Paris with his family (C5607). Soto "helped me a lot by introducing me to all his friends"; Denise Rene and Iris Clert visited, and Denise Rene invited him into group shows (C5607). In 1961 he took part in Bewogen Beweging (Moving Movement) at the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam -- invited by Pontus Hulten with Daniel Spoerri -- showing Fisicromia No. 13 among other works; he calls this "That started everything", although he sold nothing yet (C5607). He supported himself as a graphic designer in Paris until 1968, designing catalogues for Denise Rene, for Sonnabend, for Rauschenberg and Jim Dine, and posters for Lichtenstein; Denise Rene gave him a contract in 1966 after his 1965 show at Galerie Jacques Kerchache, and only then did he abandon graphic design and devote himself completely to art (C5607). He also showed at Signals in London in 1965, where the Signals journal devoted a special issue to him although he was barely known in Europe; Paul Keeler and David Medalla had visited the Paris workshops in 1964 and invited the Parisians to exhibit, and the Victoria and Albert Museum and the Montreal Museum of Contemporary Art each bought a Fisicromia from the Signals show that year (C5707, C5709).

The eight investigations, with the dates given in the Wikipedia "Investigaciones" list, are: Couleur Additive, 1959; Fisicromia, 1959; Induction Chromatique, 1963; Cromo-interferencia, 1964; Transchromie, 1965; Cromosaturacion, 1965; Cromoscope, 1967; Couleur a l'espace, 1993 (C5615). Disputes inside this list are recorded under Q1, Q3 and Q5 below. In 1965 he conceived his first chromatic environment, Laberinto de Descondicionamiento, which led to Cromosaturacion -- three chambers of red, green and blue light in which the spectator is immersed in a completely monochrome reality and becomes aware of colour as a material, physical phenomenon (C5608). In 1969 he installed 22 electrically lit cabins of red, blue and green Plexiglas, grouped in four maze-like structures, at the subway entrance of the Place de l'Odeon in Paris, as a commission that began as a proposal for the Theatre du Chatelet (60 booths, refused as too costly) (C5608). His first public artwork was a different 1969 intervention: 20 coloured Plexiglas cubicles installed along the pedestrian Boulevard Saint-Germain for a street festival -- the two counts, 20 and 22, describe two installations, not one disputed count (C5751).

The Responsive Eye, MoMA, New York, 1965: Cruz-Diez DID participate, with Fisicromia Number 116 (1964, Hans Neumann collection) -- MoMA's own April 1965 press release states it, and Tate Papers states the contrast exactly: "While fellow Venezuelan Jesus Soto refused to exhibit his works at MoMA, Cruz-Diez ... participated with the specific aim of positioning his Physichromies on a globally recognised platform" (C5609, C5705). His own verdict on the show, in BOMB, is that it "distorted kinetic art", was "not well received", "wasn't structured properly, so it damaged the movement and William Seitz, the poor MoMA curator who had organized it", and that kinetic art was "erased from history" for years afterwards (C5609). Tate Papers adds that both Cruz-Diez and Soto shared reservations about being presented as followers of Victor Vasarely and Josef Albers (C5609). The #617 trap is not repeated here, and the reverse error is not made either. Prizes of the decade: Grand Prize at the III American Art Biennial, Cordoba, 1966; International Painting Prize at the IX Sao Paulo Biennial, 1967; Second Prize at the International Painting Festival, Cagnes-sur-Mer, 1969 (C5610). He represented Venezuela at the 35th Venice Biennale in 1970, presenting a Chromatic Induction, a Fisicromia and a chromatic environment, Cromosaturacion (1965/1970) -- the exhibition that solidified his international recognition as a leading figure of kinetic art (C5610).

Starting in 1967 he executed numerous monumental works in architecture, public spaces and habitable environments worldwide, with Venezuela holding the largest number (C5611). Named 1970s works: Ambientacion Cromatica, Jose Antonio Paez Hydroelectric Plant, Santo Domingo (1973); Ambientacion Color Aditivo, floor of the Simon Bolivar International Airport, Maiquetia (1974) -- a 270 x 9 metre mosaic of sialex tiles, his best-known work in Venezuela, the floor arriving travellers photograph as a rite of passage and departing migrants remember as a last image of home; Environnement Chromo-cinetique, Union Bank of Switzerland headquarters, Zurich (1975); Cilindros de Induccion Cromatica, Port of La Guaira (1975); Fisicromia Doble Faz, Plaza Venezuela, Paris (1976); Ambientacion Cromatica, Simon Bolivar Hydroelectric Plant (Guri Dam) (1977); Ambientacion Cromatica, Venezuelan Navigation Company, Caracas (1979) (C5611). Later public works include a sculpture for the plaza in front of the Venezuelan embassy in Paris; a sculpture marking the border between Andorra and Spain (1991); Plafond Fisicromia (1980) on a platform at the Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines railway station; Fisicromia Boricua, Botanical Garden, University of Puerto Rico (1992); Induction Chromatique par changement de frequence, Ravensburger headquarters (1994); Chromatic Induction in a Double Frequency (2011), walkways at Marlins Ballpark in Miami, 5,485 square feet of one-inch square tile mosaics; and the crosswalks on the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston campus transformed into grids of colour (C5611). His stated position on this whole strand: "The works I create in the urban environment and habitat are conceived as a visual discourse generated in time and space, creating chromatic situations and events that change the dialectic between the viewer and the work... They constitute the support for an event that evolves in real time and space" (Paris, 1996) (C5611).

From 1972 to 1973 he served as professor at the Ecole Superieure des Beaux-Arts et des Techniques Cinetiques in Paris, teaching Kinetic Techniques; he was a member of the diploma jury of the Ecole Nationale Superieure des Beaux-Arts from 1973 to 1980; and from 1986 to 1993 he was Titular Professor and Director of the Art Unit of the Institute of Advanced Studies (IDEA), Caracas (C5607). In 1971 he established his workshop at 23 rue Pierre Semard in Paris, in a former Belle Epoque-era butcher's shop, where he pursued his colour research for the rest of his life (C5607). During the 1980s he crystallised the reflections developed since the 1950s and wrote methodically, drafting Reflexion sobre el color, first published in Spanish in 1989 (C5613). In 1995 his experiments with irradiated colour culminated in Couleur a l'espace, and he began exploring computing with Experiencia Cromatica Aleatoria Interactiva (W19, 1995), software letting users create colour compositions "much like a musician performs a composer's work using a written score" (C5613). In 1996 construction began on the Carlos Cruz-Diez Museum of Print and Design in Caracas, inaugurated in 1997, with Cruz-Diez appointed its President for life (C5613). In 2005 his family established the Atelier Cruz-Diez and the Cruz-Diez Foundation to preserve, develop and diffuse his legacy; in 2009 the family established the Articruz workshop in Panama City (C5613).

Retrospectives and major surveys, in sequence: Die Autonomie der Farbe, Josef Albers Museum Quadrat Bottrop, 1988; Retrospectiva 1954-1998, Museo de Arte Moderno del Zulia, Maracaibo, 1999; (In)formed by Color, Americas Society, New York, 2008 -- his first major exhibition in a United States institution; Carlos Cruz-Diez: Color in Space and Time, Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, 6 February-4 July 2011, more than 150 works, curated by Mari Carmen Ramirez, touring to the Pinacoteca do Estado de Sao Paulo (2011), MUAC Mexico City (2012) and MALBA Buenos Aires (2012) -- described by the artist's own chronology as his "most important retrospective exhibition" (C5612). Later shows include Dynamo: A Century of Light and Movement in Art, 1913-2013, Grand Palais, Paris, 2013; Cruz-Diez: Spatial Color, Niemeyer Center, Aviles, 2014; Chroma, SCAD Museum of Art, Savannah, 2017; Cistern: Spatial Chromointerference, Buffalo Bayou Park Cistern, Houston, 2018; and posthumously El Peso de la Forma, Museo Reina Sofia, Madrid, 2021, and a Cromosaturacion for the underground tunnel to the Nancy and Rich Kinder Building at MFAH (C5612). He obtained French citizenship in 2008 (C5613). He died of natural causes on 27 July 2019 in Paris, aged 95; the foundation's statement was quoted in the press: "He managed to demonstrate that colour, in interaction with the observer, becomes an autonomous reality that exists without the help of form or need for support" (C5602). He is buried at Pere-Lachaise Cemetery, Paris -- single-source (Wikipedia) within this run (C5602). He was the last surviving member of the trio Gabriel Perez-Barreiro once called the "Holy Trinity" of Venezuelan art -- Alejandro Otero (1921-1990), Jesus Rafael Soto (1923-2005) and Carlos Cruz-Diez (C5613). In 2023, for the centenary of his birth, the travelling didactic exhibition RGB: The Colors of the Century, conceived and curated by the artist in 2014, was shown at the Fondation Vasarely, France; Shanghai Rainbow Space, China; Hangaram Art Museum, Seoul; and the Carlos Cruz-Diez Museum of Print and Design, Caracas; and the retrospective El color en dos tiempos was presented at the National Art Gallery in Caracas (C5620).

## M1 -- Social figuration: the kite in the slum

El Papagayo Verde (W01, 1949, Collection Familia Savino) is the mechanism before any mechanism: a figurative painting that narrates a local social reality -- extreme urban inequality on the edges of Caracas -- with the green kite of the title as the object that carries the scene (C5652). His geometry and colour experiments were already starting in this work, but his commitment was visibly directed towards portraying a local context, in line with his idea of the "American problem" (C5652). The viewed image shows a hillside landscape of shacks and houses on steep slopes under a blue sky, with men in the foreground flying kites on the hilltops overlooking the slum areas. Nothing moves; the viewer reads a scene. The work matters to the study because it fixes the starting point -- the social calling -- against which the whole later removal of figuration, pigment and support is measured, and because Cruz-Diez himself later judged this phase a failure of method, not of intent: the rich bought the little paintings of the shantytowns he denounced (C5604).

**Takeaway:** paint the scene first, so the later abstraction has something to leave behind. The kite is the last representational object before colour becomes the subject.

## M2 -- Manipulable mural relief: colour launched by sun and hand

Proyecto para un muro exterior manipulable (W02, 1954) is the first of the Proyectos Murales of Caracas, with geometric and linear forms, an attempt to set up an autonomous object without reference to nature (C5653). On the project there are structures that already prefigure what would later become Cruz-Diez's biggest concern: to launch colour into space by means of reflected light (C5653). The mural projects of 1954 could be manipulated; they changed with the movement of the sun, creating shadows and animating the surface plane with radiant reflections of colour (C5656). The viewed image shows a white board carrying coloured vertical bars (red, teal, yellow, green) crossed at angles by black horizontal bars, some bars projecting forward from the surface -- a relief of movable-looking coloured elements on a pale ground. The importance of the external walls lay in their social aspect rather than the aesthetic: the participatory works were designed so the artist could share the pleasure of his studies with the most dispossessed people, who otherwise had no access to art (C5653). Muro Rojo (W03, 1956) is the climax of the Parenchymas or Signos Vegetales series, Barcelona 1955-1956: works with organic forms in which there is already evidence of interest in the problem of overlapping colour and coloured frames, while the design still reflects some attachment to the organic; it is the last work of the series, and despite the organic structures, spots of colour can already be seen in it (C5653). The viewed image shows a deep red-magenta ground crossed by an organic network of branching blue and orange-yellow lines that widen into star-like nodes, like a cellular or vegetal web. Cruz-Diez later said his intention in the Parenchymas and El Muro Rojo, and all his later work, was to include people in his proposal (C5653).

**Takeaway:** mount colour on bars the sun and the hand can move, and put the work outside where the people who never enter galleries can reach it. Participation is the social programme, not a feature.

## M3 -- Plane instability: forms that invert by optical fatigue

Doble animacion de un plano (W04, 1959) is the most important work of the El problema de la percepcion optica y la inestabilidad del plano series, Caracas 1956-1959 (C5653). From the earliest works in the series -- Modulacion optica de la superficie, Construccion en el espacio and Construccion en cadena in 1957, then Estructura optica in 1958 -- the artist proposes a succession of forms that are inversed as a consequence of optical fatigue (C5653). The aim was to impart movement to what never had it: the surface (C5653). The viewed image, the largest in the set at 3072x2048, shows a white field of bold black-and-white chevron zigzags crossed by vertical stripes in green, blue, red and yellow. What moves is the viewer's visual system, tired by the pattern. He concluded the evidence lacked sufficient strength and continued searching for a more effective means to release colour into space (C5653) -- which is why this mechanism is a bridge, not a destination: it proves the eye can be made to move a surface, but it does not yet free colour from the surface.

**Takeaway:** tire the eye with a succession of forms until the surface inverts. The movement is in the viewer, and the surface only borrows it -- a proof that is not yet enough.

## M4 -- Additive colour: the colour-event (a colour that is not there)

Amarillo aditivo (W05, 1959, Coleccion Patricia Phelps de Cisneros) is the first step towards the Fisicromia series and a programmatic work in his research on colour (C5652). Cruz-Diez had studied an investigation by US scientist Edwin Land, derived from an experiment with screens by Isaac Newton, in which Land used projection to combine two colours, making a third colour appear between them without it being physically there (C5652). In Additive Yellow the yellow formed by two lines of green and red does not appear to expand laterally out towards the black background, but frontally, towards the spectator (C5652). The yellow line seems to come towards the eyes and change intensity according to distance from the work (C5652). The viewed image shows a black canvas with a single thin diagonal line, red and yellow-green, running down the centre -- the near-empty black field with one fine line matches the Tate caption exactly. The experience is surprising when the spectator views it more closely and realises the yellow colour is not really there: this optical illusion is what Cruz-Diez called a colour-event (C5652). The official site names the first Amarillo aditivo and Fisicromia 1 together as the culmination of the 1954-1959 investigations in Caracas (C5653). The mechanism scales: at civic scale it becomes the Maiquetia airport floor (W17, 1974-1978), a 270 x 9 metre sialex tile mosaic in yellow, black, red and blue, walked over by every traveller, where the colour event happens underfoot for people who never intended to look at art and changes with the shifting light and the distance of the spectator (C5663, C5651); and late it becomes Double Physichromie (W20, 2009), a freestanding serpentine wall of nearly 2,000 painted aluminium and steel elements outside the University of Houston, exploring "additive colour", virtual colours that the eye perceives but that do not actually exist in the work (C5665).

**Takeaway:** put two colours side by side and let the retina invent the third. The colour that is not there is the first colour that is free -- it exists only in the viewer, and it comes toward them.

## M5 -- Fisicromia: the light trap (addition + reflection + subtraction)

Fisicromia 1 (W06, 1959, Cruz-Diez Foundation at the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston) is the programmatic work of the new view of colour, a direct consequence of Additive Yellow (C5652). While in Additive Yellow colour was simply painted onto the canvas, in Fisicromia 1 colour is featured as painted edges on a series of cardboard strips (C5652). The strips are not painted on a flat surface but appear in relief, so the combination of colour addition and reflection depends on how light impacts the work, and viewers experience colour as an evolving situation when moving both parallel and perpendicular to the work (C5652). The Fisicromias are made of coloured strips of cardboard, aluminium or Plexiglas assembled in two interspersed levels, one flat, one raised; the colour schemes produce a sensation of vibratory movement that causes the colour tones to multiply according to the position and distance of the spectator and the angle of reflected light (C5656). The viewed image shows two black-framed panels side by side like the pages of a book, each framing a square of dense thin vertical stripes in red, green and white. Fisicromia No. 123 (W07, 1964, Tate T03715) is the mature form, the synthesis of three colour phenomena: colour addition, reflection and subtraction (C5652). Subtraction takes place when the original additive strips start to be alternated with transparent monochrome strips which, instead of being laid flat on the support, are adhered at a ninety-degree angle from it along their long edge, projecting out towards the viewer, so the strips play the role of filters (C5652). The viewed image shows a black vertical rectangle, its upper half a square frame of fine vertical gold-brown strips forming a circular shape. All works are produced according to a plan and then coded, eliminating romantic traces of the artist's hand, so they can be reconstructed should they deteriorate (C5660). Fisicromia No. 500 (W15, 1970, Coleccion Patricia Phelps de Cisneros, 183 x 484 x 8 cm) is the wall-size Fisicromia at monumental scale, first exhibited at the 35th Venice Biennale in 1970: a large number of semi-transparent strips attached perpendicularly onto a support, the artist having chosen the specific colours and angles; as the viewer moves from side to side in front of it, the work seems to shift in shape and colour, producing the sensation of an artwork never quite finished but completed each time a viewer engages with it (C5658). The viewed image shows a very wide wall panel in a white gallery, dense vertical strips grading from blue-green on the left through purple and orange to deep red on the right. The artist: "The Physichromies force us to deal with an event of colour in the process of occurring in the moment, without past or future" (C5658). The date of Fisicromia No. 123 is disputed -- produced 1963 in the artist's own Tate interview, dated 1964 in the Tate catalogue (C5660, C5652).

**Takeaway:** build the work in two levels of strips and let light do the painting. Addition plus reflection plus subtraction, and the tones multiply with every step the viewer takes -- a light trap, never the same twice.

## M6 -- Chromatic induction: the afterimage made stable

Induccion Cromatica (W08, 1963) works on the afterimage: confronted with two complementary colours on a plane, the viewer's retina produces a third, virtual colour (retinal persistence); stripes of blue and yellow might optically blend to create green but generate a red "afterimage" in the viewer's retina (C5655). The artist studies the phenomenon of the "after image", also known as retinal persistence: when staring at a colour for a certain time, the retina preserves an image of the surface even after looking away, but in its complementary colour (C5662). In the Induction Chromatique the artist manages to stabilise and make visible a fleeting phenomenon that can only be observed under particular circumstances (C5662). The artist's own words: "the Chromatic Inductions offer a solution in order to assimilate real space and time to a static plan. A chromatic event occurs in these works and continuously evolves depending on the spectator's position but also the changes of light" (C5662). No image of a 1963 Induction was obtained; the entry rests on prose. The mechanism is the temporal one in the catalogue: where M4 invents a colour in space and M5 multiplies tones with position, M6 makes the viewer's own retina, in time, the instrument -- stare, look away, and the work continues in the complementary colour after the gaze has moved on. Duchas de induccion cromatica (1968), unnumbered, is the same mechanism as a walk-in shower of transparent coloured plastic strips (C5657).

**Takeaway:** hold two complementary colours still until the retina answers back. The afterimage is a colour the work never painted, stabilised long enough to be seen.

## M7 -- Chromo-interference: moire from superimposed frequencies

Cromointerferencia (W09, 1964) superimposes patterned lines of identical or varied frequencies to generate a range of colours (C5661). Transparent layers, colour overlays and grids allow light to pass through, mixing, interacting and transforming in a way Cruz-Diez called "interference" (C5661). As soon as the viewer starts moving, colours start to move, patterns begin to shimmer and seem to be moving, creating an optical illusion (C5661). Cruz-Diez uses the moire effect to produce the sensation of motion by his particular composition of lines (C5661). The mechanical form is Cromointerferencia Mecanica (W14, 1970): silkscreen on paper and plastic, motor and wood -- a circular piece of Plexiglas printed with a pattern of lines rotates above the multi-coloured background of the Couleur Additive modules (C5661). The motor pushes the central tenets of the Chromointerference series further: the interference of patterned lines generates a range of colours, and the rotation makes the patterns move for real, not only in the viewer's eye (C5661). The work debuted at the Venice Biennale in 1970, one of twelve mechanical Chromointerferences, each producing varying patterns of movement (C5661). It is the only mechanism in the catalogue with a motor. In the environmental form, Ambiente Cromointerferente (W16, 1974), the interference device leaves the wall and becomes a room: projections on four walls walk straight, while on the floor they walk to meet each other, as if they added up, forming random figures at their junctions, and the viewer walks inside the moire, the patterns projected rather than built (C5669). No image of any Chromointerference work was obtained; all three entries rest on prose.

**Takeaway:** overlay two frequencies and let them beat against each other. The moire shimmers as the viewer moves -- and once, with a motor, the work moves for real.

## M8 -- Environments: colour without support (walk-in, worn, and digital)

The investigation leaves the wall entirely. Labyrinthe de Transchromie (W10, 1965, first presented 1968) hangs acrylic laths, one next to the other at different angles, in the basic colours green, red, yellow and blue; Transchromie means "through colour": the viewer walks among transparent coloured planes and discovers combinations of chromatic subtraction orchestrated by the artist (C5669). The viewer moves from spectator to participant of a universe of sensorial knowledge and experiences his own reactions in the installation, which floats in space hung from the ceiling (C5669). Cromosaturacion (W11, 1965, first presented 1968) is the turning point of the career: the moment the artist fully transposed his pictorial investigations into three-dimensional space (C5654). Three connected chambers infused with intensely saturated red, green and blue, the primary colours of light; the viewer is immersed in a single colour, which overloads the retina, normally accustomed to perceiving a wide range of colours simultaneously (C5654). As the viewer moves through the installation, the colour dominating each chamber blends seamlessly with the next, producing a gradient at every threshold; suspended cubes bathed in reflected colour heighten the experience (C5654). It is an open artwork, in Ariel Jimenez's words a "penetrable painting", completed only through the viewer's engagement (C5654). Early versions are considered a precursor to the experimental light installations of Robert Irwin and James Turrell (C5654). No image of a Cromosaturacion was obtained; the two PAMM downloads attempted returned HTML error pages twice each and were deleted. The Cromoscope (W12, 1967) is the smallest-scale of the colour investigations: instead of building a room or a panel, Cruz-Diez puts the filter on the viewer's own eyes -- a pair of glasses with a filter that altered the perception of colours, optimal for seeing city lights at night (C5668). The Labyrinth for a Public Place (W13, 1969) takes the Cromosaturacion out to the street: 22 electrically lit cabins of red, blue and green Plexiglas at the subway entrance of the Place de l'Odeon (C5656). The public Ambientaciones put the colour event underfoot and inside industry: the Maiquetia airport floor (W17) and the Guri dam turbine halls (W18, 1977-1986), where the massive engine rooms -- Engine Room No. 2: 28 m high x 26 m wide x 300 m deep -- carry murals of colour additive with conical and circular Cromoestructuras in metal and industrial paints, almost three acres of surface in the pair (C5664). As the turbines extracted electric power from the waters of the Caroni, Cruz-Diez's colourful, vibrating murals performed a conversion of their own: raw materials of metal and industrial paints into ethereal hues that appeared to come to life (C5664). The digital turn is Experiencia Cromatica Aleatoria Interactiva (W19, 1995): software on CD-ROM letting users create compositions of forms and colour harmonies, entering the artist's creative spirit through his own tools, much like a musician performs a composer's work using a written score; the disc is inscribed "Just like a musical score, the interpreter experiences the language and spirit of the artist" (C5667, C5651). The mechanism hands the colour-event over to the user entirely: the viewer becomes the interpreter, and the work is a score, not an object.

**Takeaway:** take the colour off the wall, out of the frame, and off the support altogether. Walk through it, wear it on your eyes, walk on it in an airport, stand inside it in a dam -- and finally, perform it like a score.

## The aesthetic logic: colour as a situation, not a property

Cruz-Diez's logic runs in one direction for sixty years, and it is the opposite of the picture: from colour as a property of things, toward colour as a situation happening in space. It starts with colour painted on a scene to denounce inequality (M1), moves to colour mounted on bars the sun and the hand can move (M2), to a surface the tired eye inverts (M3), to a colour the retina invents that is not on the support at all (M4), to strips in relief that trap light and multiply tones with every step (M5), to an afterimage held still (M6), to frequencies beating against each other until the pattern shimmers (M7), to chambers of pure light, glasses on the viewer's eyes, floors in airports, halls in dams, and software the user performs (M8). At every step something is taken away -- the scene, the pigment, the flatness, the support, the object -- and something is handed to the viewer: first the eye, then the body walking through chambers, then the whole person underfoot in an airport, then the interpreter at a keyboard. "Colour, in interaction with the observer, becomes an autonomous reality that exists without the help of form or need for support" is the late form of the rule; the cola bottle's red projection on a white shirt is the early form, and they are the same rule. Against Soto the contrast is exact and is the doorway of this study: Soto's motor is the viewer's movement past wires, rods and planes, ending in a space the viewer walks through; Cruz-Diez's motor is the viewer's movement past strips and through chambers of light, ending in colour itself as the space. Soto dematerialised the object into a space you enter; Cruz-Diez dematerialised colour itself into an event you are inside. The social programme never left either: the mural projects of 1954 were for people with no access to art, the Boulevard Saint-Germain cubicles and the Odeon cabins were free in the street, the airport floor and the dam halls were for travellers and workers who never chose to look -- colour as a public good, not a private possession. He refused the labels "Latin American" and "American" and promoted a universal art, and the refusal is consistent with the system: a colour-event has no nationality, no past and no future -- it occurs in the moment, in the viewer, and then it is gone.
