## The life

Jacob Duck was born in Utrecht around 1600 -- no birth record survives, and the date is inferred from a 1660 document in which Duck states he was about 60 years of age (White Rose; Wuestman 2007; Sotheby's lot 41, 2011). His family was Roman Catholic in a city where about a third of the population professed the faith, and the faith "appears to have played no overt role in his choice of imagery" (HNA review). His full name is given as Jacob Cornelisz Duck, though NL Wikipedia's sources cite an Ecartico entry under "Jacob Jansz. Duck" -- the patronymic is uncertain, and the study uses "Jacob Duck" (leg 1 C11).

He was a goldsmith before he was a painter, and he stayed one. At eleven, in 1611, his parents placed him as an apprentice in the Utrecht goldsmiths' guild; eight years later, in 1619, he became a master in the guild; and he was still listed as a member of the goldsmiths' guild in 1642 (HNA review; White Rose; Wuestman 2007). The goldsmith's eye for metal, jewel and fabric is the standard explanation for his surface rendering -- the light on armour, gold goblets and shimmering silks (M4).

He turned to painting at what Sotheby's calls "the advanced age of 21". From 1621 he took drawing lessons from Joost Cornelisz Droochsloot (1586-1666), and in the same year is recorded as a *conterfeyt jongen* (apprentice portraitist) in the archives of the Utrecht painters' guild (EN/NL Wikipedia; White Rose; Wuestman 2007). Koslow argues Duck likely acquired his figural skills there, "and his drawings may well have been from live models", accounting for "Duck's portrait-like types in some pictures and skillfully foreshortened figures in others" (HNA review). Droochsloot is the only documented teacher Duck ever had.

He married Rijckgen Croock in 1620, per White Rose and Wuestman 2007; NL Wikipedia gives 29 April 1632 in Utrecht instead -- the two cannot both be right, and the study carries 1620 with the 1632 variant flagged (leg 1 C2). In 1629 he gave a painting of a musical company to the St Jobsgasthuis in Utrecht; in 1630-33 he was listed as a master in the city's Guild of St Luke (White Rose; Wuestman 2007). Sotheby's lot 41 (2011) loosely says he "joined the Guild of St. Luke that same year [1621]" -- that is the apprentice record, not mastership (leg 1 C8).

Where he lived is less settled than the catalogues pretend. EN/NL Wikipedia and the RKD-derived lists give Utrecht (1619-1646), Haarlem (1636-1646), The Hague (1656-1660), Utrecht (1661-1667). Wuestman 2007 corrects the Haarlem story: because some of Duck's paintings were offered in a lottery sponsored by the Haarlem guild in 1636, "it was thought that he lived in Haarlem for a while. However, he is documented in Utrecht in 1636 and 1637, and appears to have been in his native city throughout the 1630s and 1640s." The Hague is weaker still: "He may have been in The Hague around 1660, for there is a record of a painter there with the same name." The study presents Haarlem and The Hague as traditional attributions now doubted, not as fact (leg 1 C3, C4).

He died in Utrecht on or just before 21 January 1667, and was buried on 28 January in the Convent of St Mary Magdalen (White Rose; Wuestman 2007). EN Wikipedia gives "(1600 - buried 22/28 January 1667)"; Wikimedia Commons gives "28 November 1667 (buried)", which is an error -- November, not January (leg 1 C1). Christie's lot header "Jacob Duck (1600-1660)" is also wrong (leg 1 C5).

The oeuvre in numbers: Rosen authenticates only 157 pictures across a career of almost four decades; Koslow calls the low count "puzzling" given the career length, while allowing for loss. Only a few genre scenes are dated; the earliest dated painting is from 1628, the latest dated guardroom 1655 (Sotheby's lot 41, 2011). Rosen organizes the oeuvre into three categories: guardroom, brothel and prostitutes, and "other" -- "scenes of tranquil domesticity, portraits, saints, and fanciful etchings" (HNA review). Panel is the preferred support; copper and canvas are exceptional (HNA review). He also made several etchings, none examined in this study (leg 1 F23).

His younger brother was a painter too, but Rosen does not identify work by him; Koslow asks "Might he have assisted Jacob?" The brother is unnamed in the sources read (HNA review). In the 18th and 19th centuries Duck was confused with the Hague cattle painter Johan le Ducq: Rijksmuseum collection catalogues listed SK-A-93 as Johan le Ducq from 1809 through 1876 (Wuestman 2007). Little is known about his clientele, but documents show some pictures fetched high prices and entered elite collections: a *Guardroom* (Rosen no. 6) was acquired by the Archduke Leopold Wilhelm (HNA review); artnet adds, stated-by, that his work was collected by "many important patrons, including the Stadholder Frederik Hendrik of Orange" (leg 1 F17; leg 3 K7).

## M1. The Utrecht branch: the room gets big -- wide halls and deserted churches

The guardroom was invented in Amsterdam, in small rooms. Duck's move was architectural: he made the room big. "These *cortegaerdjes* were characterized by their somewhat dark, and in the case of Duck, often wide and tall hallways or even church interiors, in which soldiers and officers were at their leisure" (Sotheby's lot 41, 2011). The soldiers are not cleaning weapons or preparing for battle but "sitting around a table smoking, in the presence of several elegantly dressed women". The Louvre church guardroom (W12) is the extreme form: "interior of a decrepit church with soldiers relaxing amongst their booty scattered over the floor, some playing cards, others smoking, three soldiers standing at the side of a rich woman seated in right foreground and being robbed of her jewellery" (British Museum record of the c. 1776 Masquelier engraving after it).

The mechanism is the inversion of the merry company: in the merry company the figures fill the frame; in Duck's guardroom the staffage is small against the architecture, and "no narrative unifies the figures' diverse actions" (HNA review). The room is the subject; the people are small incidents inside it. The church pictures -- W03 (Rosen no. 4, c. 1635-40, NCMA) and W12 (Louvre) -- put several unconnected actions at different depths with no single narrative: a woman pleading with her captors in the foreground, another displaying a strand of pearls behind a broken column, a couple strolling in the background, cards at the far left (leg 2 W03). The desecrated church does the moral work the figures refuse to do: the sacred space profaned by idleness and theft, the architecture itself the accusation (leg 2 W12).

Against Codde, the contrast is exact. Codde has only one known church guardroom, and he painted it once, in brown, sketchier than anything else, and did not go back (#566 M5). Duck made the church interior his signature expansion, and shared it with Quast and Potter -- Steigrad: "Deserted churches as backdrops for the Guardroom Scenes of Jacob Duck, Pieter Quast and Pieter Potter were commonly employed." What Codde tried once, Duck built a career on (leg 1 F12; leg 3 K4, K5).

**Takeaway:** build the room first and the people small inside it -- a wide tall hall or a deserted church, several unconnected actions at different depths, no single narrative, the architecture doing the moral work. The watchable behaviour is the eye travelling the room's depth, not following a story.

## M2. Two perspectives: the geometry and the air

Duck "became masterful in linear perspective, as depictions of manorial barns, inn stables, and monumental church interiors attest" (HNA review). Koslow asks -- as a question, not a fact -- "Might Hans Vredeman de Vries's perspective handbook have taught Duck how to organize interiors with mathematical exactitude? (Vermeer used this publication as an assist, too.)" The study poses the Vredeman de Vries hypothesis only as Koslow poses it, as a question (leg 1 T1).

The paired mechanism is aerial perspective: "Accompanying the mathematical perspective system is aerial perspective modulating spatial transitions with spectacular tonal mastery. Light diffuses within penumbral interiors to enhance effects of distance and quietude" (HNA review). Geometry for the architecture, tone for the depth. The WGA description of the Budapest guardroom (W04) names the toolkit: "curtains or large piles of weaponry in the foreground to set the front plane, an emphasis on the orthogonal lines made by the tiles or boards on the floor, vistas into rooms beyond the one in which the main action takes place, and aerial perspective" (leg 2 W04).

The two perspectives are what let the room get big in M1. Without the linear system the wide hall would collapse into a backdrop; without the aerial modulation the far figures would read as flat cut-outs. Together they produce the quietude Koslow names: distance you can feel, not just measure (leg 1 T2).

**Takeaway:** give the room two depth systems at once -- hard orthogonal lines and floor tiles for the geometry, diffusing light and tonal fall-off for the air -- and let the far figures soften into the distance. The watchable behaviour is depth itself: the eye measuring the room, then losing the measurement in haze.

## M3. Spotlighting: the eye's itinerary

"In such interiors, staffage is small in relationship to architecture, and no narrative unifies the figures' diverse actions. Spotlighting particular groups guides the eye through space, intensifies hues, and brings about both movement and focus" (HNA review). The mechanism is the itinerary: a pool of light picks out one cluster, the eye travels to the next pool, the room reads as a sequence rather than a scene (leg 1 T3).

The NGV's description of W05 (dated 1635) is the textbook case: soldiers playing cards in a stable, "spotlit by strong light from an open door at the left -- a classic example of Duck's darkish interiors against which figures stand out as if spotlit", reflecting the influence of Caravaggio (leg 2 W05). The card game is played on an upturned drum; a central soldier rests his booted, spurred left foot on a stool with raw-hewn legs. The light does not illuminate the room; it selects the incidents (leg 2 W05).

This is the guardroom's answer to Codde's stage (M1 of #566). Codde stages several small scenes at once, each under its own spot, with the commander always legible. Duck keeps the spots but removes the legibility: no commander is the centre, no narrative unifies the clusters, and the eye's path through the pools of light is the only plot the picture has (leg 1 T3; leg 3).

**Takeaway:** light the room as a sequence of pools, not as a scene -- one cluster lit, then the next, the eye travelling from pool to pool with no centre to settle on. The watchable behaviour is the itinerary itself: the gaze moving through the dark from one lit group to the next.

## M4. The goldsmith's light: metal, gold, silk -- and the silver tonality

"Light fashions texture as well, be it metallic armor, gold goblets, or shimmering silks. All these qualities are found in Duck's guardroom scenes and appear too, in his representations of brothels" (HNA review). The goldsmith training is the biographical root: a maker who knew how metal catches light before he painted it. "His controlled brush strokes and the muted palette mirror the restrained nature of this portrayal" (Sotheby's lot 41, 2008). The house vocabulary for Duck is consistent across the Sotheby's lots: lucid, controlled, restrained, muted -- never bravura (leg 1 T4, T10).

The palette has a dating signal built into it. "The corresponding limited palette and silver tonality of these works would suggest a dating for both to the mid-1630s" (Sotheby's lot 258, 2005). "Duck rarely dated his pictures, but the costumes and silvery tonality of this work suggest a dating to the mid 1630s" (artnet). The silvery, restrained palette is a mid-1630s marker, not a lifelong constant -- the same silver Codde developed in the early 1630s, now shared across the Utrecht branch (leg 1 T5; #566 M2).

The technical notes for SK-A-93 (W13) give the material facts: plain-weave canvas, lined; cusping visible along the top, bottom and left edges; the ground appears to be dark red; no underdrawing could be detected with infrared reflectography; the paint was applied thinly and smoothly (Rijksmuseum). Panel is the preferred support overall; copper and canvas are exceptional (HNA review). The thin smooth paint on a dark red ground is what lets the light do the texture work -- the ground stays dark, the thin paint lets the metal gleam against it (leg 1 T7).

The sleeping-courtesan picture (W09, NGI.335) is the mechanism in miniature: a courtesan wearing pearl earrings and a precious necklace has fallen asleep leaning on a large drum covered with jewellery, coins and a gold goblet -- military booty piled behind her. Small panel, 27.1 x 23.8 cm, jewel-like, the goldsmith's eye on metal and pearl (leg 1 T14; leg 2 W09).

**Takeaway:** let the light do the texture -- armour, gold goblets, silks, pearls -- against a dark ground and a restrained silver palette, the paint thin and smooth. One bright thing (a sash, a goblet, a pearl) should carry the picture; if the room gets bright or colourful, the tonality is gone.

## M5. The stock company: transfer drawings and literal repetition

"Although Duck could occasionally come up with some surprisingly original subjects, he could milk other ones for all they were worth, and had no scruples about repeating figures and motifs literally" (Wuestman 2007, SK-A-93 entry). The proof is a tracing comparison: "A comparison of tracings of the main contour lines in the Amsterdam painting and the related one in Louisville shows that Duck must have used drawings to transfer his figures... The contours of several figures, such as the horse and the man seen from behind in the background, match almost exactly in both paintings. The differences... were caused by shifting the drawing, deliberately or otherwise, during the transfer process" (Wuestman 2007). The dog "occurs in all three works in the same pose, although not always in the same position" -- Amsterdam (W13), Louisville (W14) and St Petersburg (W16) (leg 1 T6; leg 2 W13, W14).

The mechanism is a workshop system, not a flaw: a stock company of figures recombined across pictures. The stable, the man bending forwards by the horse, the seated man pulling on his boot, and the standing officer are found in larger horizontal scenes in St Petersburg and Louisville; the same boot-pulling man, the same standing officer with tilted hat, the same dog, shifted and restaged. A signed *A Game of Cards* (Rosen no. 29) was repeated by Duck "on two other occasions", indicating collector appeal (HNA review) -- repetition as a commercial system as well as a workshop one (leg 1 F17; leg 2 W01).

This is the Utrecht version of Codde's figure stock (#566 M4: the chin-on-hand figure reappearing across works, the Schwerin composition in four versions, the Karlsruhe guardroom in five). Codde re-issued compositions; Duck transferred figures -- the same cast, moved between rooms, the drawing shifted a little each time so the contour matches but the position changes (leg 1 T6).

**Takeaway:** build one cast of figures and recombine them -- the same boot-pulling man, the same standing officer, the same dog in the same pose -- shifted slightly between rooms, never redrawn from scratch. The piece should feel like one of several versions, the cast familiar, the room new.

## M6. The game as moral shorthand: cards, tric-trac, the lute, the mirror

"The figures are set in a spacious but rather bare interior, at the center of which is a table with a large board for tric-trac, a game associated with idleness and deceit" (Sotheby's lot 41, 2008). "While music can indicate love and harmony, the lute is also an erotic symbol" (same). *A Game of Cards* (Rosen no. 29) is an early guardroom in a stable (HNA review). The props are a legible moral code: cards, tric-trac, lute, flute glass, booty, sleeping woman (leg 1 T13).

The mirror-cheat is Duck's sharpest version of the code, and he repeats it. In W05 (NGV, dated 1635), a moustachioed man half hidden behind the door holds a mirror up so the seated woman can "read" the cards of the gullible soldier being duped. In W08 (Worcester, c. 1640), the female card player cheats by means of a small hand mirror held up by her female accomplice -- the same mirror-cheat, restaged. The Worcester picture reads the whole table as a moral machine: prostitution at one end, gambling at the other, the well-dressed man at left facing the viewer with a gold coin in his right hand read as the Prodigal Son about to "squander his property in loose living"; broken bass-viol strings stand for vanitas; the Five Senses are distributed across the table (sight: the mirror; touch: coins; hearing: the lute; smell: the dog; taste: wine and pipe) (leg 2 W08).

The game is the engine of the scene in the same way it is in Codde -- the shared action that lets the figures be together without speaking to one another -- but Duck's game is played for stakes the viewer can read: the cheat is visible, the mirror is in the picture, and the moral is in the prop, not in a sermon (leg 1 T13; leg 2 W05, W08).

**Takeaway:** put a game at the centre of the room -- cards or tric-trac -- and let the props carry the moral: the mirror held up behind the door, the lute, the flute glass, the gold coin. The watchable behaviour is the cheat itself, visible to the viewer and invisible to the dupe.

## M7. The brothel disguised as a merry company: gentrification

Duck's *bordeeltjes* "usually consisted of small companies, mostly three figures in an interior, usually richly dressed and with strong erotic connotations. The cast usually included a gypsy fortune teller or a procuress, or... two prospective suitors" (White Rose). "Many of Duck's brothel scenes seem at first glance to represent respectable people in upper middle class interiors, but they are in fact brothel scenes. However, for centuries they have generally been prudently described as '*vrolijke gezelschappen*', 'merry companies', out of false modesty" (White Rose, citing Kolfin 2005). In his merry companies Duck "deliberately eschewed both the lower orders and aristocracy to portray the middle classes" (Sotheby's lot 41, 2008, citing Salomon 1998 p. 108) (leg 1 F13).

The mechanism is the double reading, and it is the subject of the other monograph: Salomon 1998, *Jacob Duck and the Gentrification of Dutch Genre Painting*, reads the oeuvre from the perspective of gender, linguistic theoretical systems and iconographical traditions. Rosen does not engage Salomon's ideas; his catalogue only gives page citations (HNA review). The final signature form's calligraphic aspect "concurs with the trend Salomon associated with 'gentrification' in Duck's later works" (HNA review). The brothel is gentrified by stages: the early *bordeeltje* (W06, 1632-33, oval, 25.1 x 33 cm; W07, c. 1630-32) is a transaction in miniature -- a prostitute holding hands with one seated suitor, "a highly unusual motif in Dutch Golden Age painting" (Rosen via White Rose), while a second suitor tilts a flute glass towards her; the later merry companies (after 1650) are lucid, controlled, bare interiors with a tric-trac board at the centre, a lute-playing woman, a maid pulling sheets over a large bed at right (Sotheby's 2008 lot 41) -- the transaction dissolved into respectability, the bed still in the picture (leg 2 W06, W07, B24).

The sleeping woman and the booty (M4's miniature) is the still centre of the same mechanism: motion suspended, loot gleaming, everyone else circling. A courtesan asleep on a drum of jewellery, a soldier selecting more jewellery for her, a procuress observing from the corner while another soldier approaches her from behind (NGI.335). The picture looks like a still life with people in it, and the people are the still life (leg 1 T14).

**Takeaway:** stage the brothel as a respectable interior -- three richly dressed figures, a fortune-teller or procuress, a tilted flute glass -- and let the transaction be legible only to a viewer who knows the code. The watchable behaviour is the double reading: respectable at first glance, a brothel on the second.

## M8. The late emptying: women out, the stable, the upright format, the refined idiom

"As pointed out by Salomon, *op. cit.*, p.57, Duck introduced a different type of guardroom interior from the early 1640's, eliminating female presence and focusing on the almost domestic atmosphere in the guardroom. The present lot, Duck's last dated picture, is a typical example of his late oeuvre, where the master has reached his refined idiom and where the moral implications are less pointed" (Christie's ensign lot, snippet). Wuestman 2007 adds: "Using a stable as the setting for his guardroom scenes is also a feature of his late work. According to Rosen (2003, pp. 154-60), Duck started using this setting in the mid-1640s" and "it is assumed that Duck's paintings with an upright format are from late in his career" (leg 1 F14).

The evolution runs: early 1630s crowded erotic guardrooms with women, to early 1640s women eliminated and domestic quiet, to mid-1640s onward stable settings and upright formats. W19, the last dated picture (1655), is its pure form: an ensign sits by a barrel in the foreground; beyond, officers play cards by a chimney. The same room, the same game, the transaction gone, the light and the chimney doing the work (leg 2 W19). The stable group (W13 c. 1655, W14, W16) reuses the same transferred figures across three pictures -- the workshop system of M5 made visible in the late manner. The tail runs past 1655: *The Tric-Trac Players* dated 1660 and *Woman Playing a Theorbo* in the 1660s show the genre dissolving into music and domestic quiet (leg 2).

The one counter-current is scale: W16, the Hermitage *Soldier's Rest* on canvas, is the big late statement of a motif otherwise painted small -- Duck never repeated the experiment at that size as far as the authenticated 157 allow us to see. (The 150 x 204 cm figure circulating for it comes only from WahooArt, an AI-content site, and is not relied on here; dimensions NOT FOUND -- leg 2 C3.) The late idiom is otherwise a shrinking: fewer women, fewer morals, taller rooms, quieter light (leg 2).

**Takeaway:** empty the room by stages -- the women out first, then the transaction, then the moral -- until only the game, the chimney and the light are left. The piece should end where Duck ends: an ensign by a barrel, officers at cards by a chimney, the idiom refined and the implications less pointed.

## The pupillage question

The open thread of this study, hedged three ways, and no document settles it:

1. **Flat assertion (dealer copy):** artnet: "was a pupil of Pieter Codde" and "The latter's studio produced a number of very accomplished practitioners in the genre, Jacob Duck amongst them."
2. **Probability (Sotheby's):** lot 258 (2005): "probably learnt his trade in the studio of Pieter Codde in Amsterdam." Note "probably" -- and the same house's other lots do NOT repeat it.
3. **Influence only (scholarly sources):** EN Wikipedia's Codde article: "Codde's pupil was Willem Duyster... and Jacob Duck was influenced by him." Sotheby's lots 41 (2008, 2011) and 22 (2015): Duck "modelling his style on the works of the Amsterdam artists Pieter Codde and Willem Duyster." Wuestman 2007: "His early paintings are stylistically related to the work of the Amsterdam painter Pieter Codde."
4. **Documented fact (all sources agree):** Duck's documented teacher is Joost Cornelisz Droochsloot (1621, drawing lessons; listed among his pupils; *conterfeyt jongen*). There is no document placing Duck in Codde's Amsterdam studio.

The hard evidence for the connection is the *Dancing Lesson* quotation inside Duck's own picture, not a pupillage document: "His early paintings are stylistically related to the work of the Amsterdam painter Pieter Codde, whose *Dancing Lesson* of 1627 appears in the background of one of Duck's merry companies" (White Rose; Wuestman 2007, citing Béguin 1952 p. 114 fig. 2). A picture-within-a-picture citation. The study presents the Codde pupillage as UNPROVEN -- "probably learnt his trade in Codde's studio" is a Sotheby's catalogue-note probability, artnet states it flatly, and the scholarly sources say influence/modelled-on. Do not write "pupil of Codde" as fact. Note the parallel from #566: Duyster-as-Codde's-pupil is itself stated-by with modern doubt -- so both halves of the "Codde taught them" story are soft (leg 1; leg 3 K2, K3).

## Works

W01 -- A Game of Cards (Rosen no. 29). One of the few dated paintings by Duck (date not stated in the sources consulted; Rosen's catalogue gives the date, the HNA review does not repeat it). Early work; a signed, early form of the guardroom scene set in a stable. Location and dimensions NOT FOUND. Its appeal to collectors is shown by Duck repeating the composition on two other occasions. The anchor for the whole stable-guardroom line that returns in the 1650s (W13, W14, W16). Text only; no image viewed.

## Evolution

Early (c. 1628-1635): the Amsterdam inheritance, small and silver. The earliest dated picture is 1628; the guardroom scenes run 1628-1655 (Sotheby's). Early Duck is Codde and Duyster filtered through Utrecht: small panels (25-45 cm), a limited palette and silvery tonality (Sotheby's lot 258, mid-1630s), figures large against the room, the transaction in the foreground -- cards, mirror-cheat, procuress, fortune-teller. Anchors: W06 (1632-33, oval, 25 cm), W07 (c. 1630-32), W05 (dated 1635, the mirror-cheat in a stable), W01 (Rosen no. 29, the early stable guardroom). The erotic-moral charge is at its sharpest here, and so is the Caravaggesque spotlight: darkish interiors, figures standing out as if spotlit (NGV).

Middle (c. 1635-1645): the room gets big. Duck's signature move is architectural: the guardroom becomes a wide, tall hallway or a deserted church interior (Sotheby's; Koslow). The church pictures -- W03 (Rosen no. 4, c. 1635-40) and W12 (Louvre) -- put small staffage against monumental architecture, with "aerial perspective modulating spatial transitions" and "spotlighting particular groups" guiding the eye through space (Koslow). Linear perspective becomes masterful: orthogonal floor lines, foreground piles of weaponry setting the front plane, vistas into rooms beyond (WGA on W04). The booty pictures (W11, c. 1635/38; W03) add the pleading woman and the hostage economy. The brothel scenes peak (W08, c. 1640; W17, c. 1640-42). The 1644 portrait collaboration (W20) is the dated mid-career anchor, and the signature forms -- five distinct variants spanning 1627-1667 -- give the chronology its spine.

Late (c. 1645-1655 and after): the stable, the upright, the emptying room. Rosen: Duck starts using the stable setting in the mid-1640s, and upright formats belong late (Rijksmuseum on W13). The stable group (W13 c. 1655, W14, W16) reuses the same transferred figures across three pictures -- the workshop system made visible. From the early 1640s Duck introduces a guardroom type that eliminates female presence and turns toward an almost domestic atmosphere (Salomon via Christie's); W19, the last dated picture (1655), is its pure form: ensign by a barrel, officers at cards by a chimney, moral implications "less pointed," the idiom "refined." The late merry companies (after 1650; Sotheby's 2008 lot 41) are lucid, controlled, bare interiors with a tric-trac board at the centre. The tail runs past 1655: The Tric-Trac Players dated 1660 (B19) and Woman Playing a Theorbo in the 1660s (B02) show the genre dissolving into music and domestic quiet. The one counter-current is scale: W16, the Hermitage Soldier's Rest on canvas, is the big late statement of a motif otherwise painted small -- Duck never repeated the experiment at that size as far as the authenticated 157 allow us to see.

What does not change: the dark tonality (Sotheby's: the kortegaardjes are "somewhat dark"), the small figure against the big room, the game as the engine of the scene, and the double reading -- amusement on the surface, a warning underneath. What changes is the room (tavern interior to stable to church to bare hall), the cast (courtesans out, ensigns in), and the light (Caravaggesque spotlight early, diffused aerial distance late).

## Aesthetic logic

Duck's aesthetic is a big dark room, small people in it, and a game on the table. The guardroom as Codde and Duyster invented it is a room of men off duty, and the genre's pleasures are abundance, noise, and the textures of loot and drink. Duck keeps the room and makes it bigger: the wide hall, the deserted church, the stable -- architecture as the subject, staffage as small incidents inside it, no narrative unifying the figures' diverse actions. His light is two systems at once: linear perspective for the geometry, aerial perspective for the air, and spotlights to guide the eye from one small group to the next through the dark. His palette is the goldsmith's -- silver tonality, thin smooth paint on a dark red ground, metal and silk and pearl doing the talking -- and his signature is five forms across forty years, a calligraphic chronology in itself.

The same logic runs through the career in one direction: small silver guardrooms inherited from Amsterdam, the room growing through the 1630s into churches and wide halls, the brothel gentrified into the respectable merry company, then the late emptying -- women out, the stable, the upright format, the refined idiom, the moral less pointed. The repertoire never quite changes -- cards, tric-trac, the lute, the mirror, the booty, the sleeping woman -- and the refinement is all in the room, the light, and the repetition: the same figures transferred by drawing from one picture to the next, the same dog in the same pose, the same cast restaged in a taller room.

The failures of the record are part of the logic too. His birth is inferred from a document in which he says he is about sixty; his training is a goldsmith's apprenticeship and drawing lessons from Droochsloot, with the Codde pupillage asserted flatly by dealers, hedged by Sotheby's, and denied by implication in the scholarly sources; his most-cited book, Rosen 2017, was not read by any leg, only reviewed; his pictures were confused for a century with a cattle painter's. A painter who spent forty years making the same room bigger and quieter, and whose own biography is mostly a set of dates other people wrote down -- the guild entry, the marriage, the burial -- is a painter whose career is a long study of one interior, emptied by stages until only the game and the light are left.

## Positions

- The dates are c. 1600 (inferred from a 1660 document stating he was about 60) to 1667 (died on or just before 21 January, buried 28 January, Convent of St Mary Magdalen). The "c. 1600-1667" form is used; the birth year is an inference, not a record.
- The marriage date is 1620 (White Rose; Wuestman 2007), with NL Wikipedia's 29 April 1632 carried as a single-source variant.
- Haarlem 1636-1646 and The Hague 1656-1660 are traditional attributions now doubted (Wuestman 2007: documented in Utrecht in 1636 and 1637; The Hague rests on a same-name record). Utrecht is the home base.
- "Most successful exponent of the genre of guardroom interiors" is artnet's wording; "finest exponent of the kortegaard genre" is Sotheby's lot 258. Both are used as stated-by.
- The Codde pupillage is UNPROVEN: artnet flat, Sotheby's "probably learnt his trade in the studio of Pieter Codde", scholarly sources "influenced by" / "modelled his style on". The documented teacher is Droochsloot only. The hard evidence for the Codde connection is the Dancing Lesson quotation inside Duck's own picture (Béguin 1952).
- The 1644 Wittenborst portrait: three painters per the family inventory (Koslow: "Duyck, Poelenburch, and Bartholomeus van der Helst"), four per Wikipedia's van der Helst article (adding Jan Both, landscape). Both forms are carried, attributed. Spelling: Wyttenhorst (Wuestman) vs Wittenborst (Koslow).
- Rosen no. 6 (Guardroom, acquired by Archduke Leopold Wilhelm) is NOT identified with KHM GG_610 (W11, also Leopold Wilhelm provenance). The identity is unverified; do not assert it.
- Potter is the father, not the grandfather, of Paulus Potter (RKD over Houbraken). His guardroom standing rests on the Schwerin picture plus Steigrad, not on his biography, which never mentions guardrooms.
- Quast "died in poverty" is contested ("It is questionable he died in poverty"). Do not carry it flat.
- Koslow's Droochsloot dates (1618-1665) are wrong: Droochsloot lived 1586-1666. Do not carry the 1618-1665 form. Sotheby's "Cornelis Droochsloot" is the patronymic mistaken for a first name; the teacher was Joost Cornelisz Droochsloot.
- Benjamins calls Rosen 2017 "the first ever monograph study", but Wuestman 2007 cites a Rosen 2003 catalogue raisonné. Do not call 2017 the first catalogue without qualification.

## Network

The city map in one sentence (Palamedesz. article): "The principal exponents in this genre were Pieter Codde, Willem Duyster and Simon Kick in Amsterdam, Jacob Duck in Utrecht and Anthonie Palamedesz. and Jacob van Velsen in Delft." The chain has been walking the guardroom from city to city -- Amsterdam (#563-#566), now Utrecht (#567), next Delft (#568).

The two inventors: Pieter Codde (baptised 11 December 1599, buried 12 October 1678) and Willem Cornelisz. Duyster (1599-1635) "developed and popularised the guardroom scene" (NGI). Duck modelled his style on both -- Sotheby's verbatim -- and the only documented teacher he ever had was Joost Cornelisz. Droochsloot in Utrecht, from 1621. The pupillage under Codde is hedged three ways and unsettled; the hard evidence is a picture inside a picture, Codde's Dancing Lesson of 1627 hanging in the background of one of Duck's merry companies (Béguin 1952).

The church-interior sharers: Pieter Jansz. Quast (bapt. 17 April 1605 - buried 29 May 1647) competed with Codde and Duyster, was influenced by Adriaen Brouwer, and was called the Dutch Callot after Jacques Callot; the deserted-church link rests on Steigrad alone, his own article never mentions church interiors. Pieter Symonsz Potter (1597-1652), father of Paulus Potter (RKD, not grandfather as Houbraken says), painted a Guardroom Interior held at Schwerin; his biography never mentions guardrooms at all -- his standing rests on the Schwerin picture plus Steigrad's grouping.

The 1644 collaboration: the Portrait of Baron Willem Vincent van Wyttenhorst, dated 1644, identified in a family inventory as produced by three painters -- "Duyck, Poelenburch, and Bartholomeus van der Helst" (Koslow); Wikipedia's van der Helst article says four had a hand, adding Jan Both (landscape), Duck possibly the harness "as he was a specialist in guardroom scenes", Poelenburch's role unclear. The portrait is 46 x 35 cm, oil on panel, Westphalian State Museum of Art and Cultural History. Van der Helst is also the man who won the schutterstuk market Codde lost: "Van der Helst was probably a pupil of either Codde or Hals" and "went on to paint several more militia group portrait commissions" where "Codde was never given another" (per #566 leg 3).

The collector: a Guardroom (Rosen no. 6) was acquired by the Archduke Leopold Wilhelm (Koslow). Cross-link: carried main Jan Anton van der Baren (1615-1686) was the Archduke's court chaplain and director of his Vienna gallery and compiler of the 1659 inventory (per the #562 entry) -- the same collection that collected a Duck guardroom also employed the carried candidate. A second elite patron, stated-by artnet: the Stadholder Frederik Hendrik of Orange.

The upstream influence: Jacob Jansz. van Velsen (1597-1656) -- "According to the RKD Jacob influenced Willem Cornelisz Duyster and is known for genre works and military pieces, most notably conversation pieces in the manner of Pieter Codde and Dirck Hals." The influence runs van Velsen to Duyster, and van Velsen himself worked in the manner of Codde -- upstream of one inventor and downstream of the other. The WahooArt "possible apprenticeship under Palamedesz." is AI copy, excluded.

The afterlife: Gerard ter Borch (1617-1681) -- "The inscription on a study of a head proves that ter Borch was at Amsterdam in 1632, where he studied possibly under Willem Cornelisz Duyster or Pieter Codde. Duyster's influence can be traced in a picture bearing the date 1638." He then studied under Pieter de Molijn in Haarlem 1634, travelled London, Germany, France, Spain and Italy, was in Rome 1641, painted the Münster treaty picture 1648, was knighted by Philip IV in Madrid, settled in Deventer and sat on the town council. About eighty works catalogued. He influenced Metsu, Dou, Eglon van der Neer and Vermeer. The "studied possibly under Duyster or Codde" is the same hedged form as Duck's pupillage -- the two guardroom afterlives mirror each other.

The Delft reserve in full: Anthonie Palamedesz. (bapt. 9 November 1602 - 27 November 1673), born Leith, Scotland; taught by Michiel Jansz. van Mierevelt and Hans Jordaens; Delft Guild 1621 or 1636 (deacon 1635 -- the wobble is carried, not resolved); married Anna Joosten van Hoorndijck 1630, six children; died Amsterdam 27 November 1673, buried Oude Kerk 1 December. The second phase: "starting roughly around 1645, the behaviour of the soldiers became more refined... devoid of booty and other signs of war and showed middle-class people interacting with the soldiers"; his setting is "a large hall of a ruin or a castle, which is open at the rear and usually has a large fireplace", centre "almost always taken up by an officer, who is chubby and wearing a tall hat and a small moustache"; "he includes women in their role as mothers and not their more conventional role of courtesans... masculinity pacified". Spine: Jochai Rosen, "Masculinity Pacified: Women as Mothers in the Guardroom Scenes of Anthonie Palamedes (1602-1673)", De Zeventiende Eeuw 24 (2008), pp. 181-195. Pupils: his brother Palamedes (d. 1638, battle painter) and Ludolf de Jongh. He also painted staffage in church interiors by Dirck van Delen and Anthonie de Lorme -- a second church link.

The spares: Adriaen Brouwer (1605/06-1638) -- Quast's acknowledged influence, the brawl-and-tavern cousin of the guardroom. Hendrik Pot (c. 1580-1657) -- the Palamedesz. article gives him a better anchor than "a single comparative sentence": Palamedesz.'s merry companies "are reminiscent of works of contemporary artists in Northern Holland such as Dirck Hals, Willem Duyster, Pieter Codde and Hendrik Pot whose works Palamedesz. must have known".
