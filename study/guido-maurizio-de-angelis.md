# Guido & Maurizio De Angelis: Study Notes

**Site:** Guido De Angelis (b. Friday 22 December 1944, Rocca di Papa, Italy; living) and Maurizio De Angelis (b. Saturday 22 February 1947, Rocca di Papa, Italy; living), Italian musicians, multi-instrumentalists, composers and singers, as well as television and film producers, also known as Oliver Onions -- the brothers who sang the Spencer/Hill sound themselves: not a whistled march but a pop song, in English, with their own voices on it, a chorus built around a brand noun that detaches from its film and charts on its own. Their practice is the song-score, the variation, and the replay: the title song that is the film's identity, the one theme driven through a whole crime score in a dozen guises, the TV earworm that schoolchildren hum the next morning, and the catalogue re-sung forty years later with younger stars as guest witnesses. [C30007]

**Doorway:** Hop from the Franco Micalizzi study: Micalizzi ends his study with a whistle that still means Trinity, a brass crescendo that still means a Roman car chase, and a theme so portable Tarantino lifted it whole twice -- and the men who own the other half of that shelf are the brothers who shared his Trinity album by one co-written track and then took the sequel, the franchise, and the whole Spencer/Hill sound for themselves. Where Micalizzi brings the whistle that identifies, the De Angelis brothers bring the song that is sung: the title song as the film, the chorus as the hook, the same two hands writing the hardest poliziottesco scores of the decade in parallel with the lightest comedies. The study starts where his leaves off -- with the other half of the Italian genre shelf, the duo who scored a hundred films and sang their own hits, and who are still singing them, live, in 2025. Guido and Maurizio De Angelis were born in Rocca di Papa, near Rome -- Guido on Friday 22 December 1944, Maurizio on Saturday 22 February 1947 -- and began playing guitar young: Maurizio graduated in composition and harmony, Guido took a flute diploma. They formed the band Black Stones, later The G & M, toured Italy with it, and from 1967 played in orchestras performing Morricone, Ortolani and Trovaioli. Their first record came out on 27 March 1963 -- the sources split on whether it was an LP or the CBS single "Un vecchio macinino / La goccia d'acqua", and the study says "first record" and notes the split. After it they became arrangers for RCA Italiana, staff arrangers and session players (turnisti), together and singly: arrangements for Gabriella Ferri (1970-74), for Lucio Dalla's Terra di Gaibola (1970) and Storie di casa mia (1971), for Nicola Di Bari (1975). The younger sister Emma De Angelis became a composer and singer herself (pseudonym Juniper) and contributed to the brothers' scores. [C30011]

The first film score was Per grazia ricevuta (1971, Nino Manfredi), Manfredi giving them the chance; the same year came Trastevere (Fausto Tozzi) and the sequel that made them, Trinity Is Still My Name (1971, Enzo Barboni). The famous whistled march of the first Trinity is NOT theirs -- it is Micalizzi's, whistled by Alessandro Alessandroni, and the study says so plainly, because the confusion is the whole point: the brothers' sound is the sung song, not the whistle. From the sequel on, the score IS a set of songs: the title song carries the film, the instrumental score is variations of it, and the song detaches and charts. "Flying Through the Air" (All the Way Boys, 1972, Colizzi) was the first Oliver Onions single, January 1973 -- Italy #8, Germany #4, 17 weeks in the Italian Top Ten -- and the film's score won a Nastro d'argento. "Dune Buggy" (Watch Out, We're Mad!, 1974, Fondato) reached Italy #2 and Germany #8. The 1973 breakout year held twelve scores; 1974 held ten; 1976, the peak year, held sixteen. [C30012]

In parallel with the comedies, the same two hands wrote the hardest poliziottesco scores of the decade -- the dark twin. For Castellari: High Crime (1973), Street Law (1974), Cry, Onion! (1975), The Big Racket (1976), Keoma (1976), The Shark Hunter (1979), The Last Shark (1981) -- nine verified films, 1972-1981, with a tenth claimed by Chris' Soundtrack Corner and unidentified. For Martino: Torso (1973), The Violent Professionals (1973), Mannaja (1977), Slave of the Cannibal God (1978), 2019: After the Fall of New York (1983) -- nine films, 1973-1984. For Bruno Corbucci the Nico Giraldi comedies; for Salce Il sindacalista (1972); for Margheriti Death Rage (1976), Killer Fish (1979), Yor (1983); for Lenzi exactly one film, Ironmaster (1983) -- the Lenzi tie is real but thin, one late sword-and-sorcery film against Micalizzi's eleven-film Lenzi cycle. The same Rome, the same years, the same session world: Micalizzi is Lenzi's crime composer, the brothers are Castellari's. [C30013]

Then television took over, and the TV theme became their biggest commercial form. Sandokan (1976, Sollima, six-part RAI series starring Kabir Bedi): the brothers wrote the full score and performed the opening-credits theme, #1 in Italy (see the Depth flag), #1 in Spain, Germany #13. Orzowei (1977, Yves Allegret): Germany #1, Austria #3, Italy #9. "Santa Maria" (1980), a standalone single with no film attached, was Germany #1 for six consecutive weeks and Austria #1 -- and then Roland Kaiser's German-language version was #1 for another five weeks, also #1 in the Netherlands and Belgium: the same chorus, translated, re-sung, topping the charts twice. From 1979 they wrote Italian anime themes -- Doraemon ("Il Gatto Doraemon", sung by Oliver Onions, used 1982-2003), Ashita no Joe, Galaxy Express 999 -- and European cartoon series (Around the World with Willy Fog, Dogtanian, Bobobobs, Ruy). Two anime themes the brief gave them are NOT theirs and the study drops them: Daitarn 3's Italian theme is Luigi Albertelli and Vince Tempera (I Micronauti), and Grendizer's "UFO Robot" is Albertelli and Tempera (Actarus); no evidence of De Angelis involvement in UFO Diapolon was found anywhere. [C30014]

The slide is dated and named. Yor, the Hunter from the Future (1983, Margheriti) is the camp endpoint: the theme song "Yor's World" was nominated at the 4th Golden Raspberry Awards (1984) for Worst Original Song AND Worst Musical Score (the score shared with John Scott, who wrote the orchestral score for the US version; the brothers are credited as "additional music"). The same singalong-title-song machine, pushed past the point of taste -- and therefore cult-immortal. In 1983 they also wrote "Stand By My Side" for Agnetha Faltzskog, on Wrap Your Arms Around Me. Then the withdrawal: disillusioned by lack of recognition in Italy, they moved into TV and film production through their own company, with long-running hits Elisa di Rivombrosa and Incantesimo; music reduced to a minimum, mostly Maurizio alone with Guido contributing basic melodic ideas. The last scores of the run are Delitti (1987) and L'uomo delle grandi pianure (1987). [C30015]

The replay is the second career, and it is still running. November 2007: first concert in 25 years, Lucca Comics Festival. July 2016: Budapest reunion, organised by promoter Gabor Koves as a tribute to the recently deceased Bud Spencer (d. 27 June 2016) -- rock band, full orchestra, string quartet, six female backing vocalists, the Hungarian orchestra Magyar Studio Zenekar and choir Cinema Studio Korus; a double CD/DVD box set and book in 2017, liner notes by Maurizio, who records Guido "utterly terrified" at singing lead live after decades away and the "Bicycle Rule" ("once you have learned how to ride a bicycle, you never forget") getting them through a week of rehearsals. Tours billed "Oliver Onions Is Still Our Name" -- a joke on Trinity Is Still My Name -- ran to 30 October 2019 at Lucca Comics and Games, resumed late summer 2020 after the COVID-19 interruption. October 2021: Future Memorabilia (BMG), re-recorded "2.0" versions of the greatest hits as duets -- Tommaso Paradiso (Orzowei), Claudio Baglioni (Sandokan), Elio (the firefighters' choir from Watch Out, We're Mad!), Roland Kaiser, David Hasselhoff, Elhaida Dani -- and "Banana Joe" reusing Bud Spencer's own vocal snippets from beyond the grave. 2023: Guido's memoir Il Sognatore. Io, gli Oliver Onions e tutta una vita (Mondadori), presented 15 November 2023 in Pisa, and the 60th-anniversary concert at Lucca Comics and Games 2023. 2024: both brothers as themselves in the documentary Pretendo l'inferno (Eugenio Ercolani). 2025: both as themselves in Il bar del cult (Mirko Zullo); the Roma violenta reissue (11 July 2025); the Shark Hunter vinyl signed by both at Music Day Roma in December 2025. The song is the durable object; the performance is replaceable. [C30016]

## M1 -- The singalong title song as franchise hook [C30017]

The film's identity is a pop song, not an instrumental motif. A short, major-key, English-lyric chorus built around a repeatable brand noun -- "Dune Buggy", "Sandokan", "Orzowei", "Santa Maria", "Zorro Is Back", "Flying Through the Air" -- sung by the composers themselves, released as a single in parallel with the film. The song detaches from the film and charts on its own: "Dune Buggy" Italy #2, Germany #8; "Sandokan" 7 weeks at #1 in Italy; "Orzowei" #1 Germany; "Santa Maria" #1 Germany for 6 weeks, then #1 again in a German-language cover by another singer. The title is the hook; the hook is the title. The lyrics are an invitation -- "Come with me for fun in my buggy... I feel like a king in my buggy" -- and the chorus is a repeated brand name. Evidence: W03, W08, W11, W12, W15, W18; the singles chart table.

**Takeaway:** when the title is the chorus, the film markets itself every time the song plays -- the hook is not attached to the film, it IS the film.

## M2 -- The Greek-chorus song-score [C30018]

Instead of underscoring a scene, a song comments on it: a small set of vocal chapters of one song, returning across the film to voice what the (often silent) hero cannot say. In Keoma, Castellari edited to Leonard Cohen and Bob Dylan temp tracks, then instructed the brothers to write a song-score with songs in the style of Cohen and Dylan; the result is six chapters of the "Keoma" song punctuating the gunfighter's actions like a chorus, "lending words to the gunfighter's silent actions" and "letting loose the raging voices of cynicism roiling about in his head". In Street Law, the closing song "Goodbye My Friend" narrates the ending over a freeze-frame of Franco Nero under the words "The End". The song is the film's interior monologue. Evidence: W09, W14; the Cohen/Dylan temp-track instruction.

**Takeaway:** a song that returns in chapters does the work of dialogue -- it tells the audience what the silent hero is thinking, and it does it better than a line would.

## M3 -- The monothematic poliziottesco drive [C30019]

One melancholic or aggressive theme, stated once, then varied relentlessly -- tempo, instrument, mood -- across the whole score, over a funk-bass, fuzz-guitar and Moog bed. The Violent Professionals: a Moog-led melancholic theme reprised as flute, piano, dancefloor and finale versions, tracking the detective's moods, per the Digitmovies liner notes -- slower with flute, nostalgic piano, faster with flute and electric piano, a dancefloor version, and a finale. The Big Racket: the same bold theme "dispersed throughout the film in varied tempos and plentiful variations", over fuzz distortion, psychedelic flutes, obsessive rhythms, sessionists Dino Kappa, Franco Alissimi, Marianne Eckestine answering Guido's "gritty flute", Gianni Oddi on sax and Moog -- "abundant reverb/phasing and tape manipulations". High Crime: "an acid rock piece with funk episodes, heavy guitar, funky undercurrents, a sense of suspension" -- the brothers "drew from the Lalo Schifrin tradition but added their own darkness, the one they learned scoring Italian westerns". The score never leaves its one idea -- it just drives it harder. Evidence: W05, W06, W13; also Squadra antifurto (a prog-funk joyride on a tarantella).

**Takeaway:** one theme, driven hard enough and varied long enough, is a whole score -- the variation is the composition.

## M4 -- The comedy score that follows the gag [C30020]

The music is written into the joke, not under it. The gag needs the music to work, and the music is part of the plot. In Watch Out, We're Mad!, Ben's membership in a firefighters' choir is a plot thread -- he studies his part, gets kicked out, and the climactic fight erupts mid-rehearsal with the assassin forced to play violin at gunpoint. The choir then becomes a permanent live-show set piece, re-sung with Elio in 2021. In Torso, the opposite move: serene acoustic-guitar music plays against slasher violence -- Mondo Digital calls the score a mix of "sultry jazz, chilling percussive suspense music, and funky folk rock without faltering once" -- and the lightness is the joke's cold edge. The music refuses to underline the gore, which makes the suspense colder. Evidence: W04, W08, W20.

**Takeaway:** when the music is inside the gag, the joke survives the film -- the choir outlives the fight scene by fifty years.

## M5 -- The TV/cartoon earworm [C30021]

A theme written to be hummed by children the next morning: simple diatonic melody, big singable chorus, the show's name in the lyric, recorded by a full orchestra with the composers singing. Sandokan and Orzowei both reached #1 in Germany as singles; the Italian Doraemon theme "Il Gatto Doraemon" ran unchanged from 1982 to 2003 -- twenty-one years of children learning the same tune. The Springer chapter on Italian anime songs notes the period's themes were "treated with extreme care: highly competent composers were asked to write the songs, whole orchestras were hired to record them" -- and these themes are "still object of references, quotations, and remakes among fans". The earworm is engineered, not accidental: the name is the chorus, the chorus is the name. Evidence: W12, W15, W17.

**Takeaway:** a theme that a child can hum the next morning is a theme that survives its show by decades -- simplicity is the durability.

## M6 -- The two-brother division of labour [C30022]

A fixed split inside the duo, stable for sixty years. Guido: lead and backing vocals, guitars, percussion, the basic melodic ideas. Maurizio: lead and backing vocals, guitars, piano, keyboards, synths -- the arrangement engine. Around them a small fixed family: Cesare De Natale (backing vocals, lyricist, co-composer), Susan Duncan-Smith (English-language lyricist, backing vocals) -- the British-born journalist in RCA's foreign-relations department who named them Oliver Onions and wrote the English lyrics. The split shows under stress: in the 1990s-2000s production years, some scores "were mainly composed and arranged by Maurizio (the duo's most musically active member) on his own, with his older brother Guido only providing basic melodic ideas". The 2016 comeback staging makes the split visible: Maurizio sings and plays guitar, mandolin and keyboards; Guido sings; a twelve-piece band plus trumpet, sax/flute/clarinet, two keyboardists and extra vocalists fill the roles the two brothers used to play alone in the studio. Evidence: personnel lists (1971-1990 and 2007-present); the "Later years" section.

**Takeaway:** a duo that lasts sixty years is not two people doing the same job -- it is two fixed jobs, one melodic and one architectural, with a small family around them.

## M7 -- The pseudonym / saturation system [C30023]

Because they were too prolific, they fragmented their own name. Oliver Onions is the famous one, but the same two men also released as G&M Orchestra, Barqueros, Charango, Kathy and Gulliver, Hombres del Mar and Dilly Dilly -- "forced to use different names for many of their projects to avoid over-saturating the market". The name is chosen per market, not per person. Susan Duncan-Smith advised that their credibility as singers of English theme songs would suffer under Italian names, so the performing duo got an English name -- Oliver Onions, after the British writer George Oliver Onions (1873-1961), picked because "the two words are pronounced the same as they are written" in any language. The film scores stay "Guido & Maurizio De Angelis"; the hit singles are "Oliver Onions". One pair of hands, two storefronts. Evidence: the Oliver Onions section; singles vs. score credits throughout the filmography.

**Takeaway:** a pseudonym is not a disguise, it is a shelf label -- the same hands, a different storefront for each market, so the market never tires of one name.

## M8 -- The comeback / replay [C30024]

Old themes re-performed live, re-recorded, and re-issued as events: the catalogue as a repertoire, not a back catalogue. 2007 Lucca Comics: first concert as Oliver Onions in 25 years. July 2016 Budapest: one-off reunion, rock band plus full orchestra plus string quartet plus six female backing vocalists plus Hungarian orchestra and session choir, released as a double CD/DVD box set (2017). Tours billed "Oliver Onions Is Still Our Name". October 2021: Future Memorabilia re-records the themes as duets with Paradiso, Kaiser, Baglioni, Elio and Elhaida Dani; "Banana Joe" reuses Bud Spencer's own voice from beyond the grave. 2025/26: the new Sandokan series re-records the 1976 theme with Calibro 35, keeping the melody intact. The mechanism: the song is the durable object; the performance is replaceable. Evidence: W20; the "Later years" section; a2zsoundtrack.com (2026 Sandokan).

**Takeaway:** a song that survives its film, its singers and its century is not a recording -- it is a durable object that any new performance can pick up and carry.

## Aesthetic logic

Put the eight mechanisms together and one logic holds them. The De Angelis brothers are not composers who write to a genre; they are two brothers who write one song and then sing it everywhere -- the title song that is the film, the chorus that comments on the silent hero, the one theme driven through a whole crime score, the gag that needs the music, the earworm the children hum, the fixed split of melody and architecture, the name changed per market, and the old song re-sung forty years later with younger stars as witnesses. Early they are RCA arrangers, the session players who can do everything themselves; then the song-score inventors, the Spencer/Hill sound's other half, the pop songs you sing against Micalizzi's march you whistle; then the dark twin, the same hands writing the hardest poliziottesco scores of the decade; then the television men, the themes that top the German charts; then the cartoon men, the themes that run unchanged for twenty-one years; late they are the producers, the music thinned to Maurizio alone; and now the replay men, the catalogue as repertoire, the song as the durable object. The place is never decoration: Rocca di Papa, the RCA studios, the Lucca Comics stage, the Budapest hall, the Music Day Roma signing table. Their own programme, insofar as any source gives one, is the one the work states: write a song simple enough to sing, hook it to a name, vary it until it is a world -- and then, fifty years later, sing it again. [C30025]
