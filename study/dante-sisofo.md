## M1. The same street, every day (the 75%-slower walk)

"I pretty much walk the same routine walk every single day, and I've been doing it for nearly four years straight. I've been purposely walking the very mundane street that I walk every day." The pace is part of the instrument: "I'm walking at an extremely slow pace, around 75% slower than everybody around me." The street is held constant and the photographer becomes the variable: "Every single day I return to the streets as a new person." The watchable behavior is repetition as method: frames f02 (a ghost figure dissolving across the same zebra crossing), f06 (a hydrant spraying beside an ice-cream truck), f13 (an overexposed pedestrian with sunglasses) all read as products of the routine walk, not of subject hunting. Time is the subject; the street is the fixed plate. This is a morning-lane variant of the route-methods in #267 (Wayman's timestamp route) and #266 (Agbay's pocket camera), but it is the most extreme: the walk itself is the artwork's clock, and the mundane street is chosen BECAUSE it is mundane, which is what lets him see it as new every day.

**Takeaway:** fix the route and walk it at a fraction of crowd speed (his number is 75% slower); let the mundane street be the subject by holding it constant; photograph it as if returning to it new each day.

## M2. The stream, not the image (Philadelphia in Flux / the Flux Archive)

The numbers are the mechanism: nearly 400,000 frames in four years, about 250,000 in two, roughly 10,000 pulled into the Flux Archive. He is archiving Philadelphia street by street, modeled on Atget: "I'm very much inspired by Eugene Atget's systematic approach to documenting the streets of Paris... my direct source of inspiration photographically," via Berenice Abbott's "The World of Atget," found in an Old City Philadelphia bookshop. The output is anti-curation: "It's less about creating the perfect curated body of work, but more about the endless stream of imagery." The zines are the publication unit, and they are deliberately cheap: 36-picture zines printed on a monochrome laserjet on cheap copy paper, stapled, stored in manila folders, viewed chronologically. The philosophy is stated as an equation: "I simply treat photography as a stream of becoming where each next photo I create is my next best photo that I've made." The quality filter is kept almost absurdly wide: "Out of those 250,000 photos, there might only be 10 that meet my standards of what I consider good." The watchable behavior is volume as devotion: the archive keeps everything, the folder is the final form, and the timestamped caption (time, date, street) is the only metadata that matters.

**Takeaway:** publish the stream, not the selects (36-picture cheap zines, stapled, in manila folders, chronological); keep the archive rate absurdly high and the quality bar honest (10 good out of 250,000); the timestamp is the metadata.

## M3. Shoot first, no viewfinder (somatic trigger)

The camera is aimed by the body, not the eye: "I pretty much just shoot first and ask questions later. A lot of the time it's a physical act, where I just move the camera in the direction." The method has a shutdown sequence: "When I shut off my mind and I enter my body on the streets, my body intuitively tells me to photograph things." The payoff is surprise as the product: "I see the movement, I see the shape, I see the gesture, I click the button, and the surprise is what I'm interested in." / "I throw the camera around, open to whatever the result might be." (Snapshot essay, Feb 2024). The watchable behavior is frames that look thrown: f10 (a snake silhouette against City Hall, pure gesture-as-shape), f11 (a figure flipping over a bike rack, mid-motion freeze), f15 (a girl reaching into the lens, contact without framing). He names the no-viewfinder peer, Shin Noguchi of Osaka, whose reasoning (not hiding that he is a photographer) converges on the same practice from the opposite direction. One focal length, and he knows where to stand without looking. The viewfinder is off; the gesture is the composition.

**Takeaway:** let the body aim the camera (shoot first, look later, one focal length); make the surprise the product, not the mistake; judge the frame by gesture and shape, not by alignment.

## M4. High-contrast B&W as limitation engine

The contrast is a creative technology, not a look: "By using high-contrast black-and-white, I'm setting a limitation that actually enhances my creativity." The payoff is the unseen: "What I get back in the photograph, a lot of the time, is what I didn't see." The unlock: "Pushing the contrast to the extremes unlocks this ability for me to photograph endlessly." The technique is a single control: the exposure-compensation dial only, "crush the background and expose for the highlights" (the FRAMES example is the Penn's Landing birds, underexposed). Frames f03 (smoke over the Hotel Palomar sign at night), f13 (blown highlights), f14 (a half face swallowed by black), f17 (night construction) all show the engine working: the black crushes, the highlight holds, and the unseen returns. The history is honest: it started as a technical decision (the processing backlog, the backlog of frames), and became the visual language. The 2022 pivot marks it: after Hanoi he "felt as though I accomplished my goals within the realm of color photography and was looking for a new challenge," sold all his Fujifilm at B&H in New York, and switched to the Ricoh GRIII plus GR IIIx in high-contrast black and white. Limitation first, language second.

**Takeaway:** reduce the image to one control (exposure compensation) and push contrast to the extreme; use the limitation to photograph endlessly; let the photograph show what you did not see.

## M5. Simplification (remove from the frame)

The selection rule is subtraction: "I'm looking to remove more from the frame and then reveal the essence of it, whether it's the formalities of the composition, the shape, the gesture, or the emotion on the face." The selection interface is the thumbnail: he edits on an iPad Pro in a 3x3 grid, and pins small prints to the wall. "The images that I typically select stand out to me as a small thumbnail that I can easily read." If it does not read at thumbnail scale, it is not the image. The watchable behavior is the payoff frames: f10 (two shapes: hands, a snake, a tower), f14 (one face), f18 (one wall, one blur). Each is the remainder after everything removable was removed. This pairs with M3: the thrown frame is messy, and the edit is ruthless; the two operations together produce the legibility. The selection is a reading test, not a taste test.

**Takeaway:** edit by removing until only the essence reads at thumbnail scale; select with the small view (3x3 grid, small wall prints), never the large one; make removal the whole edit.

## M6. Deleting the feedback loop (the completed break)

The asceticism of #267 carried to its conclusion. Instagram: deleted around 2024, never rebuilt ("Yeah, deleted it a couple of years ago."). YouTube: comments disabled AND likes disabled. The philosophy is stated as a refusal: "I purposely decided to stop consuming contemporary photography, and to simply stop engaging with the feedback loop." The consequence is named plainly: "That act alone has completely transformed my relationship to the medium and to life itself." The audience is replaced, not mourned: "I'm more interested in engaging with one individual who understands what I'm doing than a million people who are just liking the work." Publishing continues on his own site and on YouTube, which he reframes as "a medium of note-taking." The watchable behavior is absence as practice: there is no metric to optimize, so there is no optimization. The practice is the reward, and the reward is private.

**Takeaway:** remove the feedback loop entirely (delete the account, disable the comments AND the likes); treat publishing as note-taking, not performance; optimize for one individual who understands, not a million who like.

## M7. Amateur forever (beginner's mind as doctrine)

The doctrine is childlike on purpose: "I want to be like a child forever, just a kid with a camera, treating the world as a playground or my canvas." Mastery is refused as a goal: "The goal for me is to return to day one and never master photography. I want to be an amateur forever." Quality judgment is dissolved into volume: "Perhaps there really is no such thing as good or bad photographs, but only new photographs to make." The commandment is blunt: "When it's all set and done, did you make the photo or did you not make the photo? I say, just make the damn photo!" (Snapshot essay). The supporting rule: "In order to become more creative, you must make more than not. It's better to not be so decisive, to not be so serious about the practice, and to just liberate yourself through snapshotting through life." The watchable behavior is the engine underneath M2 and M3: the volume is not ambition, it is play; "I don't like to think of what I do as work, but play." The daily sunrise walk on the Schuylkill River Trail ("Every morning... for two years straight now I haven't missed a single sunrise") is the ritual form of the same doctrine: keep beginning.

**Takeaway:** refuse mastery as a goal (return to day one on purpose); replace quality judgment with volume ("just make the damn photo"); treat the practice as play, kept daily, not as work.

## M8. Human first, photographer second (two modes)

The stance is explicit: "My approach is more about being a human first and a photographer second. So I'm able to go from making candid, sneaky pictures that are off the cuff, to then engaging with people, embedding myself in scenes, and photographing." Two modes, one photographer. The candid mode is the off-the-cuff snatch (frames f02 through f09). The engaged mode has a method going back to Baltimore (2015-17): bus stops, asking permission, returning repeatedly. "I learned to photograph within a community as an outsider on the inside." (PetaPixel, 2021). The access tool is a gift: "I carried around an instax camera and gift prints to strangers as a way to interact with people and ask for permission. The joy of photography is the gift of photography." (MY STORY). In Jericho he lived with a Palestinian family for two months; in Zambia he embedded with his host father Bwalya. The watchable behavior is the mode shift: f01 (an intimate close portrait with gear visible in frame), f09 (a layered group portrait, Big Gulp and cigarette, everyone held), f12 (a smoker portrait, direct, relaxed) are the engaged mode; the street frames around them are the candid mode. Engagement is rare ("maybe once in a blue moon") but when a subject has character he provokes them into comfort. The gift comes before the frame.

**Takeaway:** hold two modes (candid off-the-cuff, then engaged embedding); earn the engagement by giving first (the instax print is the access tool); photograph within the community as an outsider on the inside.

## Aesthetic logic

The whole practice reduces to one sentence from the Snapshot essay: "In order to become more creative, you must make more than not." Everything downstream is a machine for making more: the limitation (M4, one dial, crushed contrast) makes each frame cheap; the somatic trigger (M3, no viewfinder, throw the camera) makes each frame fast; the walk (M1, same street, 75% slower) makes the subject infinite; the amateur doctrine (M7, play, never master) makes repetition joyful instead of grinding. The feedback loop (M6) is deleted so the volume can never be corrupted by the audience; the stream (M2, the Flux Archive, the stapled zines in manila folders) replaces curation as the final form; simplification (M5, the thumbnail test) is the only edit allowed; and the human-first stance (M8, the gift before the frame) keeps the subjects from becoming material. The lineage he names is the proof: Larry Towell's "The Mennonites," Cartier-Bresson, Susan Meiselas's "Nicaragua" for the engaged mode; Atget's systematic Paris for the archive mode; Moriyama's dog for the instinctual mode. "As a street photographer, the real goal isn't just to capture the decisive moment, it's to feel it." / "If you walk with love, curiosity, and playfulness, your photos will radiate those same qualities." / "falling in love with life, again and again, one frame at a time." And the question he leaves for the photograph itself: "What if the photograph becomes a self-portrait of the photographer?" Four hundred thousand frames on one city, one camera, one dial, and the answer is yes.
