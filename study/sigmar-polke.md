## The life: the dot is the picture, the poison is the paint

Sigmar Polke was born February 13, 1941, in Oels in Lower Silesia, the seventh in a family of eight children. In 1945 the family fled to Thuringia; in 1953 they fled East Germany for the West. The biography matters here because Polke's whole career is a series of escapes from a fixed position -- from East to West, from painting to photography and back, from figuration to abstraction and back -- and the work never settles in the place it just arrived. (C25033, leg 1 C24960, C24961; synthesis C25033)

After an apprenticeship in a stained-glass factory in Dusseldorf (1959-1960) -- a first trade in coloured light passing through a material, which returns forty-five years later in the Grossmunster windows -- he entered the Kunstakademie Dusseldorf, studying 1961-1967 under Karl Otto Gotz and Gerhard Hoehme and deeply influenced by Joseph Beuys. The stained glass matters: Polke learned early that a picture can be a filter light passes through, not a surface paint sits on, and the transparent supports of the 1980s and the lens paintings of the 2000s are that lesson grown up. (C25034, leg 1 C24962; synthesis C25034)

In 1963 Polke founded "Kapitalistischer Realismus" (Capitalist Realism) with Gerhard Richter and Konrad Fischer (alias Konrad Lueg), and took part in the "Demonstrative Ausstellung", a store-front exhibition in Dusseldorf with Manfred Kuttner, Lueg and Richter, May 11-26, 1963. The venue is disputed in the sources -- store-front, empty butcher's shop, furniture shop (U7) -- but the gesture is not: four young painters put unbranded commodity images in a shop window and called it a realism, a mocking mirror of Soviet Socialist Realism and a comment on the commodification of art in capitalist culture. His first one-person show was at Galerie Rene Block, West Berlin, in 1966; his first US retrospective ran 1990-1992. (C25035, leg 1 C24963, C24964; synthesis C25035)

In the 1970s Polke concentrated on photography, film and video, travelling to Afghanistan, Brazil, France, Pakistan and the United States, including photographic suites based on Paris (1971), Afghanistan and Pakistan (1974) and Sao Paulo (1975). In the early 1970s he lived at the Gaspelhof artists' commune -- the spelling splits between Gaspelhof (Wikipedia) and Gaspelshof (Anna Polke Stiftung, TheArtStory), carried unresolved (U2) -- then moved to a farm in 1973; Tate links the period to experiment with hallucinogenic substances and many works featuring mushrooms. The decade is often told as a detour from painting; it is better read as the laboratory where the darkroom mechanisms (M9) and the photocopy mechanisms (M8) were worked out before they were applied to paint. (C25036, leg 1 C24965, C24966; synthesis C25036)

From 1977 to 1991 Polke was a professor at the Academy of Fine Arts, Hamburg -- the Hamburg teaching dates carry a variant, with the Stiftung suggesting a guest-teacher role as early as 1970-1971 against the 1977-1991 full-professor dating (U3) -- and he settled in Cologne in 1978, where he lived and worked until his death. Albert Oehlen studied under Polke at the Hochschule fur Bildende Kunste in Hamburg from 1978 to 1981, and the teacher claim is the doorway of this study: it holds on the Bilbao bio, the Hamburger Kunsthalle press release and the Christie's essay, and Oehlen's own recollection of destroying a chair in Polke's class is the human detail that makes the institutional record believable. (C25037, leg 1 C24967, C24968; synthesis C25037)

Returning to painting in the 1980s, Polke worked with arsenic, meteor dust, smoke, uranium rays, lavender, cinnabar and a purple pigment from snail mucus, making large gestural paintings combining figurative and abstract imagery. This is the decade of the pours and the poisons (M5, M6): the materials are not decoration on the painting, they are the painting's engine, and several of them go on changing after the picture leaves the studio. He took part in Documenta 5 (1972), 6 (1977) and 7 (1982), was in "A New Spirit in Painting" at the Royal Academy in 1981, and won the Golden Lion at the XLII Biennale di Venezia in 1986 for a mural of hygroscopic pigments -- whether the Lion was shared with Frank Auerbach (Wikipedia) or a solo presentation (Zwirner) is carried unresolved (U6). (C25038, leg 1 C24969, C24970; synthesis C25038)

In the mid-1990s he began the "Druckfehler" (Printing Mistakes) series; in 2002 he developed "machine painting", his first completely mechanically-produced paintings; from 2007 he developed the "Lens Paintings" series. Late public work included five lenticular lightboxes for the reopening of the Reichstag in Berlin (1999) and stained-glass windows for the Grossmunster cathedral in Zurich (2006-2009) -- the stained-glass apprentice, closing the circle, now making windows that a whole city looks through. (C25039, leg 1 C24971, C24972; synthesis C25039)

Polke died in Cologne at age 69 after a long battle with cancer. The date is June 10, 2010, on five sources -- Wikipedia, Britannica, artnet, the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao and the Anna Polke Stiftung -- while the Praemium Imperiale site alone gives June 11, 2010. This study prints June 10 and carries June 11 as unresolved (U1), not resolved: June 11 may be the announcement date or a Praemium error, and no primary death certificate was read. (C25040, leg 1 C24973; synthesis C25020, C25040)

Selected exhibitions trace the institutional arc: Kunsthalle Tubingen 1976 (travelling); the Harald Szeemann retrospective 1984 (Kunsthaus Zurich, Cologne); "Die drei Lugen der Malerei", Bonn 1997; the MoMA works-on-paper retrospective 1999; "History of Everything", Dallas 2002-2003 and Tate Modern 2003-2004; "Alibis: Sigmar Polke, 1963-2010", MoMA, Tate Modern and Museum Ludwig, 2014-2015. Awards: the Sao Paulo painting prize 1975, Erasmus Prize 1994, Carnegie Prize 1995, Praemium Imperiale 2002, Rubens Prize 2007, Roswitha Haftmann Prize 2010; he refused the Wolf Prize in 1999. (C25041, leg 1 C24974-C24977; synthesis C25041)

The market followed late and then fast. "Strand (Beach)" (1966) sold at Christie's London in 2007 for 2.7 million pounds (then $5.3 million); "Dschungel (Jungle)" (1967) sold for $9.2 million in 2011 and $27,130,000 on May 13, 2015, the current record as reported by Wikipedia -- the $27.13m figure splits against a $24m figure elsewhere, probably hammer versus premium, carried unresolved (U5). The 1960s Rasterbilder command the highest prices, which is the market confirming what the work always said: the dot is the picture. (C25042, leg 1 C24978; synthesis C25042)

The critical verdict is settled and is not smoothed over here: the Praemium Imperiale site and Britannica call him one of the most important figures in post-war German art, David Zwirner calls him one of the most innovative painters of the postwar era, and John Baldessari called him an "artist's artist". He influenced Martin Kippenberger and Albert Oehlen, Lara Schnitger, Richard Prince, Julian Schnabel and David Salle, and Fischli and Weiss. His work is held by MoMA, Tate, the Centre Pompidou, the Art Institute of Chicago and the J. Paul Getty Museum. The Estate of Sigmar Polke was established by his heirs (widow Augustina Baroness von Nagel, daughter Anna Polke, son Georg Polke); the Anna Polke Foundation was established by his daughter in 2018. (C25043, leg 1 C24979; synthesis C25043)

## M1 -- Raster-dot painting: the dot is the picture

Polke enlarges a newspaper or magazine photograph until its halftone screen is visible, then paints the dots by hand -- through a stencil, with a spray gun, with a pencil eraser dipped in ink, or with a brush -- in the four process colours cyan, magenta, yellow and black. The dots are deliberately irregular in size, shape, spacing and colour, unlike Lichtenstein's uniform Ben-Day dots inside heavy dark outlines: Lichtenstein industrialises the dot and frames it, Polke hand-paints it and lets it wobble. The raster is not a style applied to an image; it is the image's own material, made visible by enlargement and then painted rather than printed. Works: W01 Raster Drawing (Portrait of Lee Harvey Oswald) 1963, poster paint and pencil on paper; W04 Bunnies 1966; W06 Girlfriends I 1967; W07 Weekend House 1967; W19 Police Pig 1986. (C25044, leg 2 M1, W01, W04, W06, W07, W19; synthesis M1)

The watchable behaviour is a picture that refuses to settle at any single distance. Stand close and the image dissolves into dots and smudges; step back and the figure barely coheres; step back further and it coheres, then dissolves again as the irregular dots refuse to settle. In W04 the Playboy Bunnies frustrate the desire the source ad promised, because the material of the image is put ahead of the clarity of the women -- the erotic photograph is still there and still legible, and it is also, insistently, a field of hand-painted spots. (C25045, leg 2 M1; synthesis M1)

**Takeaway:** the dot is the picture, not decoration on it; irregularity is the hand asserting itself inside a supposedly mechanical image. (C25046)

## M2 -- Capitalist Realism: the unbranded commodity

Take a mass-produced commodity or advertisement image -- sausage, chocolate, biscuit, socks, shirts -- and paint it with deadpan banality, stripping away the brand label and the advertising promise. The label was coined in 1963 by Polke with Gerhard Richter and Konrad Lueg, as a mocking mirror of Soviet Socialist Realism and a comment on the commodification of art in capitalist culture. The joke is structural, not verbal: Socialist Realism showed the heroic worker, Capitalist Realism shows the sausage, and the sausage is shown exactly as the advertisement showed it minus the reason to buy it. Works: W02 The Sausage Eater (Der Wurstesser) 1963; W03 Chocolate Painting 1964, enamel paint on canvas; W07 Weekend House 1967; W09 Kathreiners Morgenlatte 1969-79. (C25047, leg 2 M2, W02, W03, W07, W09; synthesis M2)

The watchable behaviour is an object sitting alone in vacant space, too perfect and too null at once; the viewer expects a sales pitch and gets a shrug. The satire lands because nothing is exaggerated -- the chill mundanity IS the exaggeration. A sausage painted with the care a history painter would give a saint, and with the same deadpan as the photograph it came from, is funnier and colder than any caricature of a sausage could be. (C25048, leg 2 M2; synthesis M2)

**Takeaway:** refusal to sell is the critique; glamour or a witty slogan would kill it. (C25049)

## M3 -- Dot-screen enlargement: projection, stencil, spray, hand

Superimpose a photograph onto a large canvas with a slide projector or episcope, enlarging it until the minute raster dots become visible, then follow that projected blueprint by hand. A mesh laid in front of the projector can give a dot grid to follow; stencils and a spray gun can lay the dots down before the hand finishes them. The mechanism is a chain of transfers -- photograph to projection to stencil to spray to hand -- and each transfer leaves its own mark, so the finished picture carries the history of its making in its surface. Works: W04 Bunnies 1966, acrylic on canvas, 150 x 100 cm; W06 Girlfriends I 1967; W07 Weekend House 1967, screenprint, composition 52 x 83.9 cm. (C25050, leg 2 M3, W04, W06, W07; synthesis M3)

The watchable behaviour is a picture carrying two scales at once -- the far view (a face, a house, a body) and the near view (a field of spots, a sprayed edge, a hand-painted wobble). The viewer is forced to move, because no single distance gives the whole picture: the far view gives the subject and hides the making, the near view gives the making and loses the subject, and the picture is the argument between the two. (C25051, leg 2 M3; synthesis M3)

**Takeaway:** the projection step must stay visible; a clean digital enlargement with no hand in it is not this mechanism. (C25052)

## M4 -- Found patterned fabric as support, and the destroyed stretcher

Paint on cheap, store-bought printed fabric -- flannel, patterned wool, mattress ticking, bubble wrap, Lurex -- instead of primed canvas, so the fabric's own pattern becomes the ground and the painting has to fight it or ride it. In the extreme case the wooden stretcher itself is cut up and collaged onto the surface, as in W09 Kathreiners Morgenlatte (1969-79), acrylic, wood, mixed media, framed collage on canvas and fabric, 235 x 305 cm. The support is not a neutral carrier; it is a co-author with its own opinion, and the painting is the negotiation. Works: W09; W12 Alice in Wonderland 1971, mixed media on patterned fabric, 300 x 290 cm; W15 Paganini 1981-83, dispersion paint, aluminium paint and graphite on printed fabric; W17 Watchtower (Hochsitz) 1984, synthetic polymer paints and dry pigment on patterned fabric, 300 x 224.8 cm. (C25053, leg 2 M4, W09, W12, W15, W17; synthesis M4)

The watchable behaviour is two patterns competing -- the printed flowers or checks underneath and the painted image on top -- and the eye cannot decide which is figure and which is ground. In W09 the stretcher bars, normally hidden, become visible content; in W17 the tower is stencilled over flowers that refuse to stay in the background. The painting never resolves into a clean figure on a clean ground, and that unresolved argument is the whole point. (C25054, leg 2 M4; synthesis M4)

**Takeaway:** the ground is a co-author; priming over the pattern to make a clean white ground destroys the mechanism. (C25055)

## M5 -- Resin and lacquer pours, burnished metallic pigment

Pour, scatter, blow and spread lacquers, pigments and resins onto canvas or fabric, then burnish the surface. In the Negative Value triptych (W16 Negative Value II (Mizar) 1982, dispersion paint, resin and pigment on canvas) white primer is followed by violet pigment, covered with a dissolving medium and brushed with dispersion paint, turning much of the violet to bronze. Gravity, accident and the chemistry of the materials do the composing; the hand sets the conditions and then watches. Works: W16; W18 The Spirits That Lend Strength Are Invisible V (Otter Creek) 1988, silver leaf, Neolithic tools, artificial resin on canvas, 300.0 x 400.1 cm; W20 Axial Age 2005-2007, violet pigment and mixed media on very fine synthetic fabric. (C25056, leg 2 M5, W16, W18, W20; synthesis M5)

The watchable behaviour is a surface that changes as the viewer moves -- bronze to gold to purple in W16 -- because the burnished pigment catches light differently from each angle. No single photograph captures it; the painting has to be walked past. The pour is a system with conditions set, not a one-off accident: the same conditions produce a family of results, and the family resemblance is the proof that a system is running. (C25057, leg 2 M5; synthesis M5)

**Takeaway:** the pour is a system with conditions set, not a one-off accident; a fixed composition that looks the same from every angle is not this mechanism. (C25058)

## M6 -- Chemical-reactive, toxic and alchemical pigments

Use pigments that keep reacting after the painting is finished -- silver oxide and silver bromide that darken with light, cobalt chloride that shifts blue to pink with humidity, cinnabar (mercuric sulphide), arsenic, meteor dust, tellurium, nickel and silver nitrate. The Athanor installation at the Venice Biennale in 1986, which won the Golden Lion, included a salt-based mural that shifted from delicate blue to bright pink with the humidity of the Venetian lagoon, plus six large lacquer paintings and a cinnabar stone, a quartz crystal and a meteorite placed in the space. The picture is a slow chemical reaction that happens to have a frame around it. Works: W17 Watchtower 1984; W18 Spirits V 1988; W19 Police Pig 1986. (C25059, leg 2 M6, W17, W18, W19; synthesis M6)

The watchable behaviour is a slow-motion film, not a static object. A Watchtower II image darkens over years until it disappears into a black haze; an Athanor mural is blue in the morning and pink by the end of the day; a Spirits panel only gradually displays its image. The viewer who returns sees a different painting, and the viewer who stays sees it change -- which means the painting has a duration, like music, and a single viewing is an incomplete viewing. (C25060, leg 2 M6; synthesis M6)

**Takeaway:** the change is the work; sealing the pigment so it can never change, or treating toxicity as a label rather than a working system, breaks it. (C25061)

## M7 -- Transparent and double-sided layered supports, including lens paintings

Paint on sheer synthetic fabric, silk or polyester coated with resin, on both sides of the support, or behind a raked transparent resin screen. In the Transparent series (from 1988) the support is diaphanous; in the Lens Paintings (2006-2009, e.g. the Strahlen Sehen / Seeing Rays series of 2007) a thick transparent acrylic gel is raked into corrugated ridges like a lenticular postcard, with paint on the fabric below and paint on the gel above. The source for Strahlen Sehen is Johann Zahn's 1685 book on the telescope -- a 17th-century optics manual as the blueprint for a 21st-century painting. Works: W18 Spirits V 1988; W20 Axial Age 2005-2007. (C25062, leg 2 M7, W18, W20; synthesis M7)

The watchable behaviour is an image that appears and disappears with the viewing angle. From a sharp angle a Strahlen Sehen painting is a milky fog; head-on, two engraved gentlemen and a jumble of ghosts emerge from behind a white pool. In W20 the paintings are transparencies: daylight passes through, the backs are worth walking around to see, and the colour floats rather than sitting on a surface. Polke's transparencies stay suspended between states, never snapping cleanly between two images -- unlike a lenticular postcard, which flips, Polke's lens painting hovers. (C25063, leg 2 M7; synthesis M7)

**Takeaway:** the image must hover between states, never snap cleanly between two images; a flat backlit transparency is not this mechanism. (C25064)

## M8 -- Photocopy / Xerox misuse

From the late 1980s on, Polke abused the office photocopier -- moving the image across the scanning bed while copying, copying copies on top of copies, enlarging and reducing, dragging and compressing -- then turned the results into screenprints. The First Cut, The Second Fall and The Third Estate (1995, screenprints) are the first deliberate Xerox-misuse works; the later Photocopierarbeiten (1995-2005) push the same method further. The copier is treated the way the darkroom was treated in the 1970s: a machine with a manual, and the art is in disobeying the manual in a repeatable way. The print lineage runs back through W06 Girlfriends I 1967 and W07 Weekend House 1967, the early photolithographs and screenprints that already treated the printed image as raw material. (C25065, leg 2 M8, W06, W07; synthesis M8)

The watchable behaviour is dots stretching into lines and waves, figures elongating and smearing, images doubling and rippling with a machined precision that no hand-drawn blur has. The error is the image: each copy is a riff on the original, contorting the ink until the representational source becomes a hallucination -- and because the movement on the bed is physical and repeatable, the hallucination has a grammar, not just a mood. (C25066, leg 2 M8; synthesis M8)

**Takeaway:** the physical movement on the bed is the method; a clean scan or a random glitch filter is not. (C25067)

## M9 -- Darkroom photography as painting: manipulated and overpainted prints

Treat every stage of photography as an arena for experiment -- manipulating the negative, multiple exposure, solarisation, sandwiching negatives, brushing developer directly onto the paper, using outdated chemistry, printing under the influence of LSD or opium, then overpainting the print with egg tempera inks and gold and silver paint. Polke's line was "a negative is never finished." The photograph is not a record of something seen; it is a painting that happens to start with light, and every step between the light and the finished print is a place where the artist can intervene. Works: W14 Untitled (Quetta, Pakistan) 1974/1978, gelatin silver print with applied colour; W13 Hallo Shiva... 1974, photolithograph. (C25068, leg 2 M9, W13, W14; synthesis M9)

The watchable behaviour is a photograph that shimmers, doubles and fractures; colour sits on the print like a tint and also wanders off the subject like ornament; scratches in the negative are gilded rather than retouched away. In W14 the air between the smokers seems to shimmer and the wall arabesque seems to dance, because the printing method is matched to the perceptual effect of the drug -- the technique and the subject are the same thing, seen from two sides. (C25069, leg 2 M9; synthesis M9)

**Takeaway:** the method is the work and the intoxication only its occasion; a straight documentary print presented as a Polke photograph is not this mechanism. (C25070)

## M10 -- Command, instruction and delegated authorship: the Higher Beings

Let the composition be dictated by an outside authority -- higher beings, a typed order, a telepathic sender -- and paint the obedience. In W10 Higher Beings Commanded: Paint the Upper Right Corner Black! (1969, lacquer on canvas, 1.5 x 1.25 m per one search source, unconfirmed) the typewritten line on the canvas IS the work's rationale: the instruction is the picture and the painted corner is only the receipt. In Telepathic Session II (1968, lacquer on two canvases with cords, Sender and Receiver panels marked Ja/Nein) two canvases are set up as a device for telepathic communication with William Blake. The artist presents himself as the receiver, not the author. (C25071, leg 2 M10, W10; synthesis M10)

The watchable behaviour is that the viewer reads the instruction first and the picture second, then realises the picture is only the residue of obeying the instruction. The joke deepens on a second look: Polke is himself the ultimate higher being, since he chose to paint and exhibit the obedience -- the delegation is a fiction the artist wrote, and the fiction is the work. (C25072, leg 2 M10; synthesis M10)

**Takeaway:** the power is in the deadpan, not in the mystery; pretending the delegation is real, or attaching a clever caption to an unrelated picture, breaks it. (C25073)

## The aesthetic logic: the picture is what the material does when you let it

Polke's career is one act performed fifty ways: take a system that is supposed to reproduce the world faithfully -- the halftone screen, the advertisement, the photocopier, the camera, the chemical pigment, the telescope -- and misuse it until it produces something the system was never meant to produce. The misuse is not vandalism; it is research. Each mechanism is a different way of asking the same question: what does this machine do when you stop asking it to behave? The raster dot, enlarged and hand-painted, stops being invisible and becomes the picture. The copier, dragged across its bed, stops copying and starts hallucinating. The pigment, left to react, stops being a colour and becomes a clock. The lens, raked into ridges, stops being a window and becomes a fog that occasionally clears. (C25074, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25074)

Three habits hold the whole career together. First, the material is the author: the dot, the fabric, the poison, the lens and the instruction each do work the artist could not do alone, and the artist's job is to set the conditions and then get out of the way -- which is why Polke's work is the opposite of Oehlen's rule-breaking even though the two men are teacher and student: Oehlen sets a rule and fights it, Polke sets a material loose and watches it. Second, distance is a dial: every major mechanism only works when the viewer moves -- close for the dots, back for the figure, sideways for the lens, tomorrow for the pigment -- so the picture is never finished in one viewing position. Third, the joke is always deadpan: the sausage, the instruction from higher beings, the telepathic session with William Blake are all presented without a wink, and the absence of the wink is what makes them funny and what makes them serious at the same time. (C25075, leg 2 aesthetic logic; synthesis C25075)

The evolution is the proof. 1963-69: the commodity and the dot, Capitalist Realism and the Rasterbilder, the image enlarged until its machinery shows. 1967-79: the machine as a toy -- the Potato House, the Potato Machine, the patterned fabric, the stretcher cut up and collaged -- and the darkroom as a painting studio. 1981-88: the return to painting as chemistry, pours and poisons, the Golden Lion mural that changes colour with the lagoon. 1988-2007: transparency and lenses, the picture as a filter light passes through, the stained-glass apprentice grown up. What stays constant is the question the painting asks without answering: what happens when you trust the material more than your own hand, and let the machine, the chemical and the accident finish the picture? (C25076, leg 2 evolution; leg 1 C24969-C24972; synthesis C25076)
