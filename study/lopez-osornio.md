## The life: La Plata, Japan, the chair, exile, Barcelona, the museum

Cesar Eduardo Lopez Osornio was born in La Plata, Buenos Aires Province, in 1930. Almost nothing is recorded about his childhood or family. The municipal decrees give his full name and DNI 5.130.703, "Clase 1930" -- the class year in Argentine identity documents is the birth year, and it is the strongest evidence for 1930 against the single dissenting source, the heading of the Gonzalez de Nava memorial, "(1931-2015)", most likely an error in the heading, recorded here rather than silently corrected. One personal detail survives, from the memorial: in his "primera juventud" he had been a good football player, and Gonzalez de Nava compares the way he dodged death through years of illness to the skill with which he once dodged opponents on the field.

He trained at the Escuela Superior de Bellas Artes, UNLP, as a disciple of Hector Cartier (1907-1997), the teacher who built his teaching on Gestalt psychology and the phenomenology of perception -- "a scientific and poetic language of the image, centred on how the eye actually perceives" -- and who transmitted Bauhaus knowledge to Argentine students. Cartier's pupils included Julio Le Parc, Paternosto, Polesello, Aizenberg, Roux, Grippo, Vigo, MacEntyre, Asis, Demarco, Garcia Rossi, Puente and Lopez Osornio. The Vision chair was essentially Cartier's subject carried forward: lectures on point, line, plane, colour and light, with Kandinsky as the main conceptual reference, and the insistence that geometry is a matter of perception, not of rigid rule.

His early work was informalist. The first recorded prizes come from the Salon Estimulo in La Plata: Third Prize, IV Salon Estimulo (1958), Second Prize, V Salon Estimulo (1959). In 1960 he won a scholarship from the Comision de Investigaciones Cientificas de la Provincia de Buenos Aires and moved to Japan, where he lived until 1963 (the 2012 MACLA catalogue says 1960-1962; two sources against one, and askART's "three years", favour 1960-1963). He studied Oriental Art at the University of Osaka and Landscape Architecture at Kyoto University, studied Zen, and spent 45 days in a Buddhist temple. He also worked as a muralist, designer and landscape architect in Japan, as he later did in Argentina, Venezuela and Spain.

The Japan years left a permanent mark, but not in the form of imitation. In his own words, quoted in the Arte-Online account of the Sivori exhibition: "Creo que todo eso se convierte en una forma de vivir. Siempre pense que, de aparecer algo de Oriente en mi trabajo, iba a aparecer naturalmente. Una de las ideas favorables que me dijeron alla es que no habia cultivado falsos orientalismos. Siempre me segui a mi mismo." Gonzalez de Nava, reading the unpublished "El punto" text, judges that the mystical root in his thought, first visible in the Japan stay, had "mas caracter chino que japones" -- more Chinese than Japanese in character -- and notes possible echoes of Rudolf Steiner and of the Platonic current in the West.

On his return from Japan he took up the Vision chair at the Faculty of Fine Arts, UNLP, as its titular professor, and also taught at the Faculty of Architecture of the Universidad Catolica de La Plata. The chair was "muy estructurada": all visual-arts students passed through it, served by a team of assistants in fairly strict shifts, with bulky hand-ins of varied exercises. Lopez Osornio did not sit at a fixed table for assessments; he roamed around, organising the general plan and giving, from time to time, passionate theoretical talks full of references to the point, the line, the plane, colour, light, and the artists who were his conceptual sources, principally Kandinsky. When Gonzalez de Nava asked him whether the students actually followed these dense talks, he answered: "No importa, lo principal es comunicar entusiasmo." In the early 1970s he tried, with Gonzalez de Nava's help, to translate Sibyl Moholy-Nagy's English version of Paul Klee's Pedagogical Sketchbook for use in the chair; the plan was never implemented -- the years 1974 and 1975, and the coming of the military dictatorship, intervened. Prizes followed: the "George Braque" prize, Museo de Arte Moderno, Buenos Aires (1965); the "Premio Nacional de Pintura Martin" and the First Prize "Martini" (both 1966 -- the two names may be one prize reported two ways); First Prize, "Salon de Mayo", La Plata (1969).

In 1975 he was dismissed from his university post -- "dejado cesante" -- and went into exile suddenly, with his wife Mary and their young children, after a denunciation that turned out to be baseless, from someone in his closest circle, under threat from the AAA (Alianza Anticomunista Argentina). His farewell to Gonzalez de Nava: "A partir de ahora, no podemos vernos mas; nos vamos en una o dos semanas; ahora no te puedo contar nada por tu seguridad; cuando vuelva, te cuento." He travelled through Colombia, Costa Rica, Puerto Rico and Venezuela, reflecting, per askART, "on the possibility of bringing together all Latin American artists" -- the exile journey itself, during which the museum idea first took shape. He settled in Caracas (1975-1980), Professor of Percepcion Visual at the national university of Venezuela -- the sources name it "la Universidad Nacional de Venezuela", an institution that does not exist under that exact name; presumably the Universidad Central de Venezuela, not asserted. In 1978 the First Encuentro de Artistas Plasticos y Criticos Iberoamericanos was held in Caracas, where artists first recognised that Latin American art lacked a museum of its own; Lopez Osornio later traced the MACLA directly to that meeting.

From 1980 to 1998 he lived in Barcelona, teaching at various universities (none named in any source found) and working as a muralist, designer and landscape architect. When they met again, probably in Buenos Aires in 1985, his painting had developed notably: a series of works in which color-luz and the primary forms -- circle, square, triangle -- combined in images worked with roller or brush. Between 1992 and 1994 he organised "Confluencias" in Spain, the first exhibition to gather Latin American artists resident in Europe, whether in voluntary or forced exile. In his own words (Arte al Dia, September 2009): "El interes y exito suscitado por esta exposicion y, dado que el arte latinoamericano carecia de un museo que nucleara y mostrara con idoneidad su produccion, todos los artistas de la muestra aceptaron donar sus obras para la concrecion de un museo."

The MACLA was inaugurated on 10 September 1999 in the Pasaje Dardo Rocha, an 1883 former railway station at Calle 50 between 6 and 7, La Plata, with an initial holding of about 100 donated works. (Gonzalez de Nava says the return to La Plata with a contract to found the MACLA was in 2000 -- the 1999 inauguration date is the dated fact; the return year carries the tension.) Lopez Osornio was its founder and director general, and also its curator. He described it as a "casa para los artistas" and as the "Museo de todos", refusing the idea of naming it after individuals, including himself. Its holdings were organised in three tendencies -- figurative, abstract and non-figurative -- with a preference for contemporary, non-figurative work. By the 10th anniversary in 2009 the collection had grown to about 1,000 works, all donated, including 85 works by Luis Tomasello, 27 by Caceres, 30 by Jonquieres, 84 drawings by Julio Silva and 110 works of MADI Internacional. In 2005 the museum had been named cultural institution / Museum of the Year by the Asociacion Argentina de Criticos de Arte. The directorship was formalised by Municipal Decree No. 1995/07 (from 10 December 2007), renewed December 2011 for a further four years. He also founded the MACLA's annual benefit event, "La Noche de los Artistas", which grew out of drawings he and Pedro Roth made on paper napkins and small scalloped-edge folders over coffee at the Cafe de las Artes, later replaced (2009) by small canvases on board.

On balancing the museum job with his own painting, he told Arte al Dia in 2009: "Trabajando mucho. Yo creo que en los museos, en el conjunto academico, tendria que haber mas artistas, porque en la practica generalmente los dirigen historiadores y economistas... Y en lo personal, no dejo de trabajar nunca, aunque solo le saque punta al lapiz. Y estar en el museo me produce alegria. Porque sigo? Porque amo esto y el museo es una casa para el arte, mi casa."

By 2011 he had been through "incontables percances corporales y fisiologicos", many of them grave, and those close to him -- above all Mary -- had grown used to seeing him in a wheelchair. Gonzalez de Nava stresses: "Sentado, si, pero no postrado." Seated, yes, but not laid low. He set out to develop, and achieved, a new kind of relief image in his work, which let him go on exploring color-luz with a horizontal reading radius of almost 180 degrees. His last exhibitions: Museo Sivori, Buenos Aires, c. 2013-2014, and Galeria Hoy en el Arte (Teresa Nachman), Buenos Aires, "a fines de 2014" -- his last. Gonzalez de Nava's last memory is from leaving that 2014 exhibition: he told Cesar "al fin, vos sos el mas guapo entre todos nosotros", and Cesar, in his wheelchair, raised both arms high like a boxer who has just won a fight. "Esa es la ultima imagen que tengo de el, la de un campeon." He died in early 2015, before March 2015, when the memorial was written in Temperley. No newspaper obituary and no exact date of death were found.

## M1 -- The brush-drawn line, not the ruled line

The Sivori text states the technique plainly: "la linea es trazada con pincel" -- the line is drawn with a brush. The Japan legacy made visible: a hand-drawn line with wobble and pressure, holding geometry that a ruler would make dead. The line is the meeting point of the two halves of his career -- the informalist gesture of the 1958-59 salon paintings survives inside the rigour of the mature geometry, not as a mistake but as the drawing instrument itself. A ruled line would be exact and lifeless; the brush line is exact enough to hold a circle, a square, a triangle, and alive enough that the eye reads the hand in it.

**Takeaway:** geometry drawn by hand, one continuous brush line, the wobble and pressure variation visible, the shape still exact. A piece that rules its lines straight has missed the mechanism; the line must be drawn, not constructed.

## M2 -- Slow chromatic gradation to reverberation

"Una gradacion cromatica sutil hasta llegar a la reverberacion" -- a subtle chromatic gradation until it reaches reverberation. Colour is built in small steps, layer on transparent layer, until the field vibrates at its edge: a glowing, humming boundary where two close colours meet and will not settle. The Sivori text gives the "Soles" series as the case: the sun's edge is where the gradation does its work. This is not a gradient for its own sake; it is a slow climb, step by step, and the vibration is the arrival, not the decoration. The mature description -- "transparencias, severidad, concentracion" -- is a restrained, layered palette, not a high-key one.

**Takeaway:** colour that climbs in small transparent steps until the edge between two near colours shivers. A piece that jumps from one flat colour to the next has missed the mechanism; the reverberation must be earned by the climb.

## M3 -- The point as the origin of all form

The doctrine of the unpublished text "El punto como centro de la nada" (The point as the centre of nothing), written in the 1970s or 1980s, lost for decades among boxes of books brought from Barcelona, resurfaced during a 2011 exhibition at the MACLA, and published in full only in the Gonzalez de Nava memorial. In Cesar's own words, as quoted there: "The point is 'something' that arises from NOTHING... Philosophically the POINT links NOTHING with EVERYTHING... The point determines and qualifies, organises and synthesises. Nothing can exist geometrically or mathematically without the point." And: "The point, whatever its location in Space, is always an expression of the Will or Design of the Divinity. The point has no centre, but it is the centre of all things. God, the ineffable GEOMETER, extends the immense area of the universe by making the POINT arise." From the point arise line, plane, circle, square, triangle. The text runs to sacred geometry, the golden (solar) measures, and the claim that "the artist must be as good a mathematician as a good geometer" -- and that modern art lost its way when the golden measures were profaned.

**Takeaway:** one point on an empty field, from which a whole composition grows -- line, then plane, then circle, square, triangle -- the point never disappearing, always the centre that has no centre. A piece that starts with a finished shape has missed the mechanism; the point must come first and stay visible.

## M4 -- Color-luz: light as the material, geometry as the vessel

The constant of his mature work was "color-luz" (color-light): paintings built from the primary forms -- circle, square, triangle -- in function of color-light, not of outline. The early-1970s "Soles de la Pampa" are the named case. Light is the material; the geometry is only the vessel that holds it. The Arte-Online Sivori account: "Cesar Lopez Osornio califica su obra como geometria libre. No se cine a una linea rigida", which in a way relates him to the Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel in Paris -- Argentine artists among them -- who sought "la profundidad de la expresion, la permanencia de la imagen para que el observador no se detuviera en lo ilusorio sino en la emocion". Taverna Irigoyen: the work is "una formula racional de fuerzas que se entroncan y que, impulsadas por la energia cromatica, adquieren una visualidad convocante" -- a rational formula of forces that join together and, driven by chromatic energy, acquire a compelling visuality. Forms "se interpenetran... sin disociarse" -- they interpenetrate without coming apart.

**Takeaway:** primary forms -- circle, square, triangle -- whose only job is to hold light; the light does the work, the outline does nothing. A piece that outlines its shapes in dark lines has missed the mechanism; the forms must interpenetrate and glow from inside.

## M5 -- The formal/informal tension: gesture surviving inside rigour

Graciela Limardo, head of Museography at Museo Sivori, states the governing tension of the whole oeuvre: "En la pintura de Lopez Osornio hay una tension entre lo formal y lo informal, la huella del gesto en la materia convive con la severidad de la geometria como si un logos retuviera la impronta azarosa o como si la materia huyera del rigor de las formas. Un binomio que al convivir se refuerza mutuamente." The trace of gesture in the matter coexists with the severity of geometry, as if a logos held back the chance imprint, or as if the matter fled the rigour of the forms -- a pair that, living together, strengthens each other. The Arte-Online account notes "tal vez resabios de su inicial epoca informalista" -- perhaps remnants of his early informalist period -- in the gestural touch that breaks the rigour without breaking its poetry. The informalism never left; it went inside the geometry and stayed there, breaking it just enough.

**Takeaway:** a severe geometric composition with one gestural break in it -- a brushstroke, a smear, a wobble -- that the geometry does not absorb and does not expel. A piece that is pure geometry has missed the mechanism; a piece that is pure gesture has too. The break must be inside the rigour.

## M6 -- Serial variation: one system, many states

"La otra geometria" 1, 2, 5, 7, 12 (1994): lithographs on Guarro paper, sheet 77 x 57 cm or 57 x 77 cm, image 67 x 46 cm, edition 75 + HC, edited and printed by Poligrafa, Barcelona, sold through Galerie Hus, Paris. Same sheet, same edition, same printer -- varied compositions. The numbering runs to at least 12, and nos. 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11 were not found -- do not assume the series ends at 12. The series title is the Barcelona-period restatement of "geometria libre": where the 1960s geometry was defined against informalism, the 1994 geometry is defined against geometry itself -- an "other" geometry, freer, printed in series. Seriality is new in the documented record; nothing in the early record shows a numbered series. The series is the work: one system, many states, and the differences between the states are what the viewer is meant to read.

**Takeaway:** one fixed system -- same sheet, same three forms, same palette -- run through many states, each print a different arrangement of the same elements. A piece that shows one composition has missed the mechanism; the series, and the variation between its states, is the work.

## M7 -- The scale split: murals against napkin drawings

The largest and the smallest scales of his career sit side by side, and the record proves both while itemising neither. On one side: mural, design and landscape-architecture work in Japan (W08), Venezuela (W09) and Spain (W10) -- the largest scale of his career, no site, size or image known. On the other: the late small object -- the napkin drawings he and Pedro Roth made over coffee at the Cafe de las Artes, the small scalloped-edge folders, the small canvases on board that replaced them from 2009, the 34 x 34 cm paintings, the two small 2012 auction lots (W19-W20). The post-1999 return shrinks the object to something you can hold, while the largest late "work" becomes the museum itself. The split is the mechanism: the same hand, the same geometry, at wall scale and at pocket scale, and the geometry must survive both.

**Takeaway:** the same geometric system at two scales at once -- a wall-sized field and a pocket-sized object -- the small one dense and held, the large one open and walked past. A piece that works at one scale only has missed the mechanism; the geometry must hold at both.

## M8 -- The museum as the work: gathering as making

"Confluencias" (1992-1994, Spain) turns the exile network into the object: the first exhibition to gather Latin American artists resident in Europe, whether in voluntary or forced exile, and every exhibiting artist agreed to donate their works toward a museum. The donated works -- about 100 at the inauguration, about 1,000 by 2009, all donated by the artists or their families -- became the MACLA. The artist as organiser and gatherer, not only maker: the 1978 Caracas recognition that Latin American art lacked a museum of its own, the 1992-94 gathering in Spain, the 1999 house in La Plata. "Una casa para los artistas" -- a house for the artists. The museum is not the frame for the work; it is the work, the largest object he ever made, built out of other people's paintings and his own persistence. He refused to name it after individuals, including himself: the "Museo de todos", the museum of everyone.

**Takeaway:** a picture that gathers -- many small donated works, many hands, arriving from many places and assembling themselves into one house. A piece that shows one artist's one picture has missed the mechanism; the gathering, and the house it builds, is the work.

## Evolution: informalism, Japan, the chair, exile, print, the museum

1958-59 La Plata: informalist salon painter, Third then Second Prize at the Salon Estimulo -- the only measurable progress the early record offers. 1960-63 Japan: Oriental art in Osaka, landscape architecture in Kyoto, Zen, 45 days in a Buddhist temple -- the brush line, the concentration, the refusal of false orientalism. On return: the Vision chair c. 1963-75, the prizes (Braque 1965, Martin/Martini 1966, Salon de Mayo 1969), the early-1970s "Soles de la Pampa" and the "color-luz" doctrine -- severe, transparent geometry with the informal gesture surviving inside the rigour. 1975: dismissal and exile -- Caracas 1975-80, Barcelona 1980-98 -- the work disperses into undocumented murals, design and landscape architecture, while the only fully documented objects appear: the serial 1994 Barcelona lithographs, "La otra geometria". 1999: the return, the MACLA, the object shrinking to napkin drawings and 34 x 34 cm paintings while the largest late "work" becomes the museum itself. 2011: wheelchair, the new relief-image technique, a horizontal reading radius of almost 180 degrees. Late 2014: the last show, Galeria Hoy en el Arte. Early 2015: death. The constants: the point, the primary forms, color-luz, the brush line, the "Soles" motif running through all periods -- and the organising instinct, the born organiser of groups, present from the Vision chair to "Confluencias" to the MACLA. What cannot be claimed: any dated stylistic sequence for the easel paintings between the 1960s and the 1980s (no dated paintings found); any development within the "Soles" (no individual canvas dated); any visual description of a single painting beyond the Sivori texts (no image viewed).

## The network: the stayer outside the manifesto, the quartet, the chair

Dalmiro Sirabo: the strongest link. Boletin de Arte 12 (2010), section III, re-read fresh by leg 3, states it verbatim: "Cesar Lopez Osornio y Dalmiro Sirabo han mantenido el lenguaje geometrico hasta el presente." Both Cartier students; both stayed geometric while Paternosto and Puente turned Andean. Leg 1 could not find the phrase; leg 3 found it in A1 section III, and leg 3 supersedes leg 1. They appear together as exhibitors and lot-holders in the MACLA's 2012 benefit catalogue (lots 42-43 for Lopez Osornio, lot 66 for Sirabo), and both are listed in a 2011 text on geometry in art among Argentine geometric artists. What is NOT established: that Sirabo was Lopez Osornio's disciple. The catalogue line "Su maestro orientador es el Prof. Cesar Lopez Osornio" belongs to Mario Feller (La Plata, 1941), not to Sirabo; do not transfer it. Sirabo's own catalogue entry names no teacher.

Horacio Elena (#641): same teacher, same city, opposite answers; both displaced in 1975 -- Elena to Sitges, Lopez Osornio to Caracas. MACLA gave Elena a 2008 solo show (the dated fact); the MACLA holding of Elena's work is Elena-bio-only (C9789 carried).

Cesar Paternosto (#638) and Alejandro Puente (#639): fellow Cartier students (Paternosto; no Cartier link found for Puente), fellow members of the platense quartet, and both signed La vision elemental (1967). Lopez Osornio did not sign it. He was NOT a Grupo Si member -- A1: "Si bien este ultimo artista no participo de dicho grupo" -- the quartet member from outside the group, linked by the Vision chair, the stayer outside the manifesto. That is the position this study names: the geometric stayer who never needed the manifesto because he already held the chair.

Hector Cartier: the common teacher, Vision 1956-65, Gestalt and phenomenology of perception, the Bauhaus line. The chair also produced disciples: Mario Feller ("Su maestro orientador es el Prof. Cesar Lopez Osornio", 2012 catalogue) and a woman artist recorded as "Discipula de Lopez Osornio, en La Plata desde 1968". Rodolfo Castagna: the succession problem, not a collaborator -- Vision from Cartier's 1965 retirement (Design careers), overlapping Lopez Osornio's 1963-75 tenure; no clean handover in either direction.

Institutions: MACLA (founded 1999, Pasaje Dardo Rocha), the UNLP/ESBA Vision chair, Caracas 1975-80 (Percepcion Visual), Barcelona 1980-98 (universities unnamed in all sources). One refusal worth recording: Gonzalez de Nava notes Lopez Osornio pointedly did NOT accept the presence of his works in MADI exhibitions at the MACLA -- MADI, for him, achieves a purely phenomenological presence, "lo que hay", with the drama of life absent, while his works carry "una fuerte presencia simbolica" that makes them "verdaderos intrusos dentro del clima casto que instala una muestra MADI". And his own summary of his attitude to criticism, quoted in the Sivori account: "Yo soy egoista: yo trabajo para mi. Me importa un cuerno la opinion de los criticos, de mi mujer, de mis hijos. Si vos trabajas solo para los demas, te mentis a vos y les mentis a los demas."

## Aesthetic logic

Lopez Osornio's aesthetic is the logic of the point that refuses to stay small. Pick one simple thing -- a point, then the three primary forms it generates -- and let everything else change around it: the scale it is drawn at (a napkin, a lithograph sheet, a mural wall, a museum), the city it is drawn in (La Plata, Osaka, Kyoto, Caracas, Barcelona, La Plata again), and the job it is asked to do (a salon prize, a chair exercise, a sun, a donation, a house for artists). The Vision teaching is the through-line: point, line, plane, colour, light, and the eye that perceives them -- but where Sirabo took it into the sign and Paternosto into the edge of the stretcher and Elena into the children's book, Lopez Osornio took it into the institution: the classroom first, then the museum. The geometry is free exactly because it is severe: the rigour is the condition of the freedom, not its opposite, and the informal gesture survives inside the rigour because the line is drawn with a brush, not ruled. That is why the "Soles" can return in different periods and places and still be the same work -- the sun is the point grown large, the light made visible -- and why the museum can be the largest work -- the point grown into a house, the gathering made visible. A Lopez Osornio that rules its lines, flattens its colour, or forgets the point has kept the shapes and sacked the mechanism; the line must be brushed, the colour must climb to reverberation, the point must be there at the start, and the gathering must be visible at the end.

The contrast with Elena is the study's spine, and it is a contrast inside one classroom, between two men who sat in Cartier's room and took the same teaching to opposite rooms. Elena's generator is a figure repeated until it becomes a life: the rounded volume in texture, the pure-colour wood figure, the cropped fragment, the woman looking at the sea. Lopez Osornio's generator is a point repeated until it becomes a house: the brush line, the gradation to reverberation, the primary forms in function of color-light, the series, the museum. Elena left the gallery trajectory for the children's book and kept the figure; Lopez Osornio kept the classroom and built the museum and kept the geometry. Both were displaced in 1975; one went to the bookshop and the beach, the other to Caracas and Barcelona and back to La Plata with a museum in his luggage. Both asked what Vision teaching was for, and Elena answered with accompaniment, Lopez Osornio with institution. That is why the two studies belong together, and why Lopez Osornio is the complement, not the follower: same generation, same city, same teacher, the same 1960 founding night in the background -- and from that shared room, two opposite mechanisms.
