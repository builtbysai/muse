# Salomon de Bray: Study Notes

**Site:** Salomon de Bray (1597, Amsterdam -- 11 May 1664, Haarlem), Dutch Golden Age painter, architect, town planner, poet and guild reformer; with Pieter de Grebber the forerunner and first peak of Haarlem classicism, the Catholic painter-architect who wrote the movement's book about building, Architectura Moderna (1631), designed the Zijlpoort (1627) and planned Haarlem's northward expansion (1661), and painted three large Oranjezaal canvases (1649-51) for Amalia van Solms. He dated most of his drawings to the day and numbered his pictures in his own inventory; his sons Jan, Dirck and Joseph all became painters, and four of his children died with him in the Haarlem plague of 1664.

## The life

Salomon de Bray was born in Amsterdam in 1597, the son of Simon de Bray and Marie Jansdr; his father came from Lier in the Southern Netherlands, likely emigrating after the Siege of Antwerp (1585). He settled in Haarlem before 1617, when he is recorded as a member of the St Adrian's civic guard (cloveniers) and as a "friend" of the rhetoric chamber De Wijngaardranken -- the only year he is known as a member; his brother Simon, an attorney, was a member 1616-22.

His training is disputed. He is registered as a pupil of Hendrick Goltzius and Cornelis Cornelisz van Haarlem, and a 1621 memorandum by Theodoor Rodenburg for King Christian IV of Denmark names the same two teachers; the memorandum also says de Bray was willing to leave Haarlem for Copenhagen and took a painting of a woman as a sample -- it is not known whether he actually went or what the king thought. Wikipedia and RKD add that he "probably started his training in Amsterdam with Jan Pynas, Nicolaes Moeyaert and Pieter Lastman"; the National Galleries of Scotland says only "possibly trained with Pieter Lastman". Treat the Amsterdam start as probable, not proven, and do not assert Lastman as his teacher.

He published banns on 4 May 1625 and married Anna Westerbaen, daughter of a Hague rope-maker and sister of the portraitist Jan Westerbaen and the poet-physician Jacob Westerbaen, both also Wijngaardranken members. At least ten children followed; three became notable painters -- Jan (c. 1627-1697), Dirck (c. 1635-1694) and Joseph (c. 1632/34-1664). He was a devout Catholic in Protestant Haarlem; he signed a letter to a priest together with Pieter Soutman and Claas van Bouckhorst, assuring the clergyman he could reply frankly as all three were "Catolycke persoonen", and he probably painted altarpieces for Haarlem's underground Catholic churches. He was also a poet and rhetorician: he published a poetry anthology in 1627 (Amsterdam publisher Jacob Aertsz Colom, dedicated to his niece Margarita Kick), and the composer Cornelis Padbrue set one of his poems to music in Kusjes (1631).

He joined the Haarlem Guild of St Luke in 1630 and became its headman. In 1631 he prepared a new guild charter, signed by Pieter de Molijn, Outgert Ariss Akersloot, Willem Claesz Heda, Cornelis Cornelisz, Cornelis Claesz van Wieringen, Floris van Dyck and Isaak Halinck, with the stated aim of "the renewal of the ancient luster of the art of painting"; it was never ratified, meeting opposition from the Haarlem council, reportedly because it promoted painting above engraving, woodcarving, tapestry, smithwork and pottery. (Taverne's Simiolus article suggests a charter may have succeeded in November 1634 -- see C1143.) In 1632 he tried unsuccessfully to retrieve the guild's St Lucas relic from Catholic safekeeping in the St Bavochurch; it was never found.

As an architect he was paid in 1627 for his design for the Zijlpoort, a Haarlem city gate; he was involved in the construction and expansion of Haarlem City Hall in 1630 (door and balcony design), the new consistory of the Bavokerk, and St Annakerk; outside Haarlem he designed a new entrance (1629) for Huis te Warmond and a design for the Nijmegen city orphanage. His town-planning treatise Bedenckingen over het uytleggen en vergrooten der stadt Haarlem (1661) proposed expanding the city northward with three canals -- Brouwers gracht, Staten gracht, Linde gracht -- partially implemented only in the decades after his death as the Nieuwe Gracht and today's Parklaan. In 1658 he produced the Dutch translation of Italian terms for the publication of Vincenzo Scamozzi's L'Idea della Architettura Universale; the publisher described him as "conversant in Greek, Latin, French and Italian". He also designed silverwork -- two design drawings survive, a silver drinking horn (the horn itself survives) and a dish -- and his son Jan's 1664 will refers to "drawings of chalices and monstrances made by Mr Salomon de Bray".

He died on 11 May 1664, probably of the plague ravaging Haarlem in 1663-64 -- every source hedges with "probably", and the evidence is circumstantial, the family deaths of April-May 1664 -- and was buried three days later in the church of St Bavo. His wife Anna had been buried on 3 March 1663; children Jacob, Josef, Juliana and Margaretha also died in April-May 1664. His son Jan painted the double profile Portrait of the Artist's Parents, Salomon de Bray and Anna Westerbaen (1664, NGA Washington) between 11 May and 17 June 1664, bequeathing it in his will to burgomaster Gaeff Meynertsz Fabritius on condition it pass to the city of Haarlem.

## The evolution: from small panels to the palace, and back

The 1620s (W01-W04) are small portraits and pastoral panels: Portrait of a Young Woman (1622, Berlin, the earliest dated painting in the list), Portrait of Melchior Moretus (1625, Antwerp), A Shepherdess (1625, Bristol) and The Finding of Moses (1626, Stockholm, attribution shared with Jacob Pynas). The work is Mannerist in training -- Goltzius and Cornelis van Haarlem -- and small in scale.

The 1630s (W05-W13) are the mature core, the signature mode: half-length biblical hero and heroine pendants on plain dark grounds, clear even light, Utrecht Caravaggist influence -- Jael, Deborah and Barak (1630, with an identical second copy 1635, Utrecht), Judith offering the Head of Holofernes (1636, Prado), Samson with the Jawbone and David with His Sword (both 1636, Getty, pendants) -- alongside tronies and fantasy-costume studies (Young Woman Combing Her Hair, 1635, Louvre; Study of a Young Woman in Profile, 1636, Boston; Young Shepherdess with a Straw Hat, 1635, Montreal; Jeune paysanne, c. 1635, Orleans) and The Agony in the Garden (1639, Hatchlands Park). A secondary note says Rembrandt's chiaroscuro enters his work around 1640; treat that as unverified blog opinion, not scholarship.

The 1640s (W14-W15) stay small and intimate: Abrahams offer (The Sacrifice of Isaac, 1647, Stockholm) and The Twins Clara and Aelbert de Bray (c. 1646, National Galleries of Scotland, 82.60 x 64.80 cm, VERIFIED) -- his own twin children, swathed in luminous white against deep shadow, in a baroque shell cradle.

The years 1649-51 (W16-W18) are the monumental turn: the Oranjezaal commission for Amalia van Solms, decorating the room in memory of her late husband Stadtholder Frederik Hendrik (d. 1647) -- Triumphal Procession with Musicians (1649, 385 x 205 cm, REPORTED), Triumphal Procession with Captured Weapons (c. 1650) and a grisaille of Putti Bearing a Cartouche with Stadholder Frederik Hendrik's Date of Birth (1651, Mauritshuis). A roughly tenfold scale jump from the 60-90 cm panels to 385 cm canvases, and the only time he works at palace scale. The Barak figure from 1630 reappears as the triumphal knight on a white horse.

The 1650s and early 1660s (W19-W28) return to two registers at once: larger multi-figure history scenes set against classical architecture used as scholarly argument -- The Queen of Sheba before the Temple of Solomon (dated 5 June 1657, Frans Hals Museum), Eliezer and Rebecca (1660, Douai, 90 x 156 cm, REPORTED), The Daughters of Cecrops (1663, last recorded with a dealer) -- alongside a return to small intimate panels (Hagar Brought to Abraham by Sarah, 1650, Agnes Etherington, 31.2 x 23.5 cm, VERIFIED; The Expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael, 1662, Norton Simon, 54.3 x 47.3 cm, VERIFIED). Subjects stay overwhelmingly Old Testament, and he revisits the same stories repeatedly: Hagar at least three times (1650, 1659, 1662), Jael twice, Eliezer in drawing and painting.

## M1 -- The peintre-philosophe: the picture as a scholarly argument

De Bray is the erudite painter of the Haarlem classicists, and his best-documented mechanism is treating a painting as a piece of scholarship. His surviving drawings show numerous subtly differing versions of the same subject -- laborious planning and revision, not improvisation. Two cases carry the point. Odysseus and Circe (c. 1650) paints an unspectacular, rarely painted moment -- Odysseus mourning companions turned to swine -- with a cloth draped over Odysseus's chair, as in Homer, not over a bed as in Coornhert's Latin-Dutch translation; the detail suggests he worked directly from Homer, perhaps in the Greek. Joseph Receives His Father and Brothers in Egypt (1655) prominently places a Corinthian capital and column fragments in the foreground -- an apparent anachronism he used deliberately to restate his Architectura Moderna thesis refuting Vitruvius: biblical evidence, he argued, showed the Corinthian capital existing as early as Moses's lifetime. The mechanism is the telling detail that is only "wrong" until you know the source: the picture argues, and the argument is visible in one small thing placed where a lazy painter would put decoration.

**Takeaway:** paint the argument, not the illustration -- one small detail that only makes sense if you have read the source, placed in the foreground where it cannot be missed, and the whole picture becomes a thesis with a picture attached.

## M2 -- The absent victim: three figures who never meet

Jael, Deborah and Barak (1630, with an identical second copy 1635) is de Bray's signature subversion, and its mechanism is omission. Three life-size half-length figures are crowded into an indeterminate space under Caravaggesque lighting -- the influence of the Dutch Caravaggists Hendrik ter Brugghen, Gerrit van Honthorst and Dirck van Baburen, whose 1621 Procuress is a likely model -- but the narrative core is missing: Sisera, the victim, is entirely absent, practically unique in the tradition. The three figures never interact and seem unaware of each other: Jael, lusty and furious, glaring directly out at the viewer, hammer and tent peg held as attributes, not used; Deborah, anomalously aged and wrinkled, hands clasped in prayer, without precedent -- Deborah is normally a vigorous young woman; Barak, armoured, whiskered, red-orange sash with golden clasp, dull, passive, eclipsed by shadow. They form a "seesaw" with Deborah as fulcrum. Jael wears white trimmed with red and blue and a tricolour sash recalling the Dutch flag. The scholarly reading is a political allegory of the Dutch Republic around 1630 -- Barak as the shrinking prince, Jael as the virago-Republic -- but the mechanism does not need the allegory: put three people who belong to one story in one frame, let none of them look at the others, and leave the event itself out. The viewer supplies the missing murder.

**Takeaway:** the strongest scene is the one you refuse to paint -- three figures, no victim, no eye contact, and the story happens in the viewer's head, which is the only place it cannot be gotten wrong.

## M3 -- The half-length pendant system: one hero, one heroine, one dark ground

The 1630s core is a system, not a set of pictures: half-length biblical hero and heroine pendants on plain dark grounds, clear even light, the same size, the same composition, made to hang as a pair. Samson with the Jawbone and David with His Sword (both 1636, Getty, 64 x 52 cm, REPORTED) share size, medium and composition and were probably intended as pendants; Jael and Judith (1630/1636) were claimed as pendants by Dirkse (1988) and Van Suchtelen (1989), though the Prado itself disputes it -- the compositions do not coincide, curtain against neutral ground. The jawbone in the Samson drips water, a mistranslation of the Dutch Bible (water from the rock, Judges 15) rendered literally as water from the bone. The mechanism is the pair as a unit: two pictures that mean more together than apart, the hero and the heroine answering each other across a wall, the dark ground doing the work of a frame. Do not over-claim pairings: where the museum disputes the pendant claim, say so.

**Takeaway:** build pictures in pairs -- same size, same dark ground, same clear light, hero against heroine -- and let the wall between them do the talking; the pair is the work, and each half is only half of it.

## M4 -- The inventory code: an oeuvre you can count

De Bray dated most of his drawings to the day, and he numbered his pictures in his own inventory system. The Boston Study of a Young Woman in Profile (1636) is inscribed on the reverse of its panel with the code "46 / 1636 / 4 / 14"; The Queen of Sheba before the Temple of Solomon is dated via the code "57 6/5", the 5th of June 1657, with the signature and date on a column base. The mechanism is a working method made visible: a countable, datable oeuvre, where a code on the back of a panel is as much a part of the picture as the paint on the front. A painter who numbers his work can be studied the way an architect can be studied -- by the sequence, not just by the single masterpiece. It is also why the study can date the late work so precisely, and why the gaps (the lost Hagar versions, the dealer-only Cecrops) show up as holes in a list rather than as vague absences.

**Takeaway:** number the work and date it to the day -- the code on the back turns a pile of pictures into a sequence, and a sequence can be read, argued with, and continued.

## M5 -- Architecture as argument: columns that prove a thesis

De Bray was an architect who painted, and in the late work the architecture stops being a setting and starts being the argument. The Queen of Sheba (1657) inscribes its columns IACHIN and BOOZ -- the pillars of Solomon's Temple -- and dates itself on a column base; Joseph Receives His Father and Brothers in Egypt (1655) puts a Corinthian capital and column fragments prominently in the foreground to restate the Architectura Moderna thesis that the Corinthian order predates the fifth century BCE. The mechanism is the building as evidence: a column is not a backdrop, it is a citation, placed where the eye must trip over it. The same hand that wrote a treatise arguing ancient forms could be used "as adornment and ennoblement" paints the adornment into the history and lets it carry the proof. The early work has no architecture worth naming; the late work cannot be described without it.

**Takeaway:** let the building argue -- a named column in the foreground is a footnote the viewer cannot skip, and a picture that cites its sources in stone is harder to dismiss than one that only shows a story.

## M6 -- The tenfold scale jump: the same figure, ten times larger

The Oranjezaal commission (1649-51) is a scale jump of roughly ten times: from the 60-90 cm panels of the 1630s to 385 cm canvases, painted under Jacob van Campen's programme alongside about twelve artists, for Amalia van Solms in memory of Frederik Hendrik. Triumphal Procession with Musicians (1649, 385 x 205 cm, REPORTED) was the first canvas de Bray completed; Triumphal Procession with Captured Weapons followed about a year later; the 1651 grisaille of putti with the stadholder's birth date closed the commission. And the jump is not just size: the Barak figure from 1630 -- the armoured, whiskered, passive knight in shadow -- reappears eighteen years later as the triumphal knight on a white horse at the head of the procession. The mechanism is reuse at a new scale: the same figure type, lifted out of a dark half-length panel and set on a horse in a palace hall, the small picture's cast promoted to the big picture's lead. The count is disputed -- three canvases per NGS, two per CODART -- and the study reconciles it as two procession canvases plus one grisaille, three works (see C1138).

**Takeaway:** when the commission grows tenfold, do not invent a new figure -- promote the old one: take the passive knight out of the shadows, put him on a white horse at the head of the procession, and let the small picture's cast carry the palace wall.

## M7 -- The book as building: a treatise that builds

Architectura Moderna ofte Bouwinge van onsen tyt (1631) is de Bray's other rulebook, and its mechanism is the reverse of de Grebber's Regulen: where the Regulen tells painters how to paint, Architectura Moderna tells builders how to build, and does it by showing contemporary buildings, not antiquity. Text completed 14 December 1630; first edition 1631, 44 plates (mostly engravings after Hendrick de Keyser) and 25 pages of text plus a dedication to Amsterdam's burgomasters; the first original architecture treatise in Dutch since Hans Vredeman de Vries. Its thesis: de Keyser was for the Netherlands what Alberti was for Italy, Lescot for France, Inigo Jones for England -- initiator of an improved architecture "in accordance with the proportions of the ancients". And its notable move: the plates show contemporary buildings, because de Bray argued ancient forms could be used "as adornment and ennoblement" but imitating every ancient building form was "unfeasible and impractical". The mechanism is the treatise as a building site: rules demonstrated on buildings that exist, in the city the reader lives in, rather than on ruins in Rome.

**Takeaway:** write the rules about the buildings in front of you -- a treatise that shows a living city, not a dead antiquity, is a rulebook the reader can walk out and check.

## M8 -- The family workshop and the plague: the system outlives its founder

De Bray's network is a family workshop that becomes a school and then a memorial. His sons Jan, Dirck and Joseph copied and worked from his paintings in an organized workshop but "drew different lessons from him"; Jan, the closest in classical subjects and large scale, was Houbraken's "pearl in Haarlem's crown". The 2008 Frans Hals Museum / Dulwich exhibition Painting Family: The De Brays covered all four. The mechanism is transmission with divergence: the same models, the same workshop, three different painters -- Jan the classicist history painter, Dirck the flower painter turned monk at Gaesdonck, Joseph the still-life painter of whom only one work (In Praise of Pekelharing, 1656) was long known. And the workshop ends the way the life ends: the plague of 1663-64 took Anna in March 1663, then Salomon on 11 May 1664 with four of his children, and Jan painted the posthumous double portrait of his parents between 11 May and 17 June 1664 -- the son finishing the father's portrait after the father's death, the workshop's last commission a memorial. The Banquet of Anthony and Cleopatra is Jan's, not Salomon's -- signed and dated 1652, with Salomon and Anna as the sitters -- and the confusion is itself evidence of how completely the son absorbed the father's system (see C1140).

**Takeaway:** a workshop is not a copy machine, it is a divergence machine -- the same models, the same light, three different painters -- and when the founder dies the workshop's last work is the portrait of the founder himself, painted by the son.

## The aesthetic logic

De Bray's logic can be stated as four rules, and they are the classicism's, stated by a man who also wrote them down for buildings. Know the source first: the picture is made for a tutored audience, and the detail that proves you read Homer in Greek or measured the Corinthian capital against the Bible is the picture's credential. Leave the event out: the victim absent, the three figures not looking at each other, the story completed by the viewer, because a scene the viewer finishes cannot be gotten wrong by the painter. Pair the pictures and number them: the pendant is the unit, the inventory code is the sequence, and a countable oeuvre is an oeuvre that can be argued with. And let the building argue: a named column in the foreground is a citation, and the treatise and the painting are the same argument in two media -- one in plates, one in paint.

There is a second logic underneath, and the study does not hide it. De Bray was a devout Catholic painting altarpieces for hidden churches in a Calvinist republic, and palace decorations for a Protestant stadholder's widow, at the same time -- the same clarity that let de Grebber serve three masters. But de Bray's version of the bridge is different: his classicism is not only a shared visual language, it is a learned one. The order in his pictures is the order of a man who translated Scamozzi, measured de Keyser's buildings, drafted a guild charter and numbered his own pictures -- order as a habit of mind, applied to paint, to stone and to paper alike. The clarity is the man's character, not just his style.

## The network

Hendrick Goltzius (1558-1617) and Cornelis Cornelisz van Haarlem (1562-1638): registered teachers, per the 1621 Rodenburg memorandum; with Karel van Mander they ran the small Haarlem academy de Bray probably followed. Pieter Lastman (1583-1633), Jan Pynas (c. 1582/83-1631) and Nicolaes Moeyaert (c. 1591-1655): probable Amsterdam starting point, unproven. Pieter de Grebber (c. 1600-1652/53, #580): fellow forerunner of Haarlem classicism, fellow Catholic, fellow Goltzius pupil, fellow Oranjezaal painter. Jacob van Campen (1596/1595-1657, #578): coordinator of the Oranjezaal programme, fellow guild member and Catholic architect-artist; de Bray "cooperated with" him on the Huis ten Bosch decoration. Pieter Post (1608-1669, #579): architect of Huis ten Bosch with Van Campen, builder of the rooms de Bray decorated. Caesar van Everdingen (1616/17-1678, #577): fellow Oranjezaal painter. Oranjezaal collaborators: Jacob Jordaens, Theodoor van Thulden, Thomas Willeboirts Bosschaert, Jan Lievens, Christiaen van Couwenbergh, Pieter Soutman, Gonzales Coques, Adriaen Hanneman, Gerard van Honthorst (#528). Constantijn Huygens (1596-1687): adviser who secured the court commissions. Amalia van Solms (1602-1675) and Frederik Hendrik (1584-1647): court patrons. Pieter Soutman (c. 1593/1601-1657): fellow Haarlem Catholic, co-signatory of the letter to a priest, Oranjezaal painter. Willem Claesz Heda (1593/94-c. 1680/82): co-signatory of the 1631 guild charter. Pieter de Molijn (1595-1661): co-signatory of the 1631 charter. Dirck van Baburen (c. 1595-1624): his 1621 Procuress is the likely model for the Jael composition. Frans Hals (c. 1582/83-1666): fellow Haarlem rederijker; de Bray painted an addition to a Hals family portrait, left fragment, 1624. Cornelis Padbrue (c. 1592-1670): composer, close acquaintance, set de Bray's poem to music. Christian IV of Denmark (1577-1648): requested his services in 1621, outcome unknown. Family: Anna Westerbaen (wife, d. 1663); Jan de Bray (son, c. 1627-1697), Dirck de Bray (son, c. 1635-1694), Joseph de Bray (son, c. 1632/34-1664); Jan Westerbaen and Jacob Westerbaen (brothers-in-law); Margarita Kick (niece). Modern scholarship: J. W. von Moltke, Salomon de Bray, Marburger Jahrbuch 11/12 (1938-39); Pieter Biesboer et al., Painting Family: The De Brays (2008); Friso Lammertse's forthcoming catalogue raisonne. Do not call Leendert van der Cooghen a pupil -- he drew after de Bray but was trained by Jordaens. Do not repeat the Design Encyclopedia claims that de Bray taught Pieter de Hooch and Jan Steen or designed the Grote Kerk.
