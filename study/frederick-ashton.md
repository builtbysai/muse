## 1. Guayaquil, Lima, and the Pavlova poison

He was born Frederick William Mallandaine Ashton on 17 September 1904 in Guayaquil, Ecuador, the fourth of the five children of George Ashton (1864-1924), manager of the Central and South American Cable Company and British vice-consul, and his second wife Georgiana (1869-1939), nee Fulcher. The family moved to Lima, Peru, in 1907, where he attended a Dominican school, and returned to Guayaquil in 1914, where he attended a school for the children of the English colony. He grew up speaking mostly Spanish, and at English school his classmates teased him for his Spanish accent. Two formative influences are recorded from childhood: serving as an altar boy, which inspired a love of ritual that shows later in The Wise Virgins, and -- the one that decided everything -- being taken to see Anna Pavlova dance in Lima in 1917. He was thirteen. His own reported words, quoted in several sources with slightly varying wording and so treated here as paraphrase rather than verbatim: "from the end of that evening I wanted to dance", and "She injected me with her poison, and from the end of that evening I wanted to dance." The family was horrified. Dance was not considered a proper occupation for a young man in a conventional middle-class British family, and Ashton recalled: "My father was horrified. You can imagine the middle-class attitude. My mother would say, 'He wants to go on the stage.' She could not bring herself to say 'into the ballet.'" The step that would become his signature, the Fred Step, was itself based on a step Pavlova used in a gavotte she frequently performed -- the poison went in as a step, and fifty years later it was still coming out as steps. [C33913]

**Takeaway:** the vocation arrives whole at thirteen, from a single evening, and everything after it is the working-out of what that evening put in him. [C33914]

## 2. Dover College, the City, and the late start

In 1919 his father sent him to England, to Dover College, where he was miserable. He left in 1921, and his father decided he should join a commercial company; he worked for an import-export firm in the City of London, where speaking Spanish and French as well as English was an advantage. In January 1924 his father committed suicide. His widow was left financially dependent on her elder sons, who ran a successful business in Guayaquil, and she moved to London to be with Ashton and his younger sister Edith. One dating trap is flagged and not used: the Frederick Ashton Foundation timeline groups the father's suicide into its 1921 entry, as if the City job followed from it, while Wikipedia and the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography date the suicide to January 1924, after Ashton had already left Dover College and started clerking -- the 1924 date is the sourced one. In secret, and against his family's wishes, Ashton pursued dance. He auditioned for Leonide Massine, the former Ballets Russes dancer and choreographer under Diaghilev, and was accepted as a pupil at the unusually late age of twenty. When the training began is itself split three ways and is carried as a conflict: the Foundation timeline says 1921, Encyclopedia.com says 1922 (his brother Charlie paying for a few lessons, and Ashton using money saved from his job as a translator to begin studies with Massine), and Wikipedia and the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography imply 1924-25 from the "age twenty" figure. After Massine left London, Ashton was taken on as a student by Marie Rambert, becoming a regular pupil in 1925; Rambert convinced him of his potential as a choreographer and encouraged him to start creating ballets. His debut as a dancer came on Good Friday, 10 April 1925, performing a Fandango with the Duenna Dancers on the Palace Pier in Brighton. Britannica also names Nicholas Legat as a London teacher alongside Massine and Rambert; no other source consulted mentions Legat for Ashton, and it is noted, not corroborated. [C33915]

**Takeaway:** a dancer who starts at twenty has no time to waste on display -- he has to learn what a step means before he has fully learned how to do it. [C33916]

## 3. Rambert, Nijinska, and the first ballet

In 1928 Ashton joined Ida Rubinstein's company in Paris as a dancer and apprenticed himself to Bronislava Nijinska. He danced in most of the company's performances, including Rubinstein's famous Bolero; Nijinska was a hard taskmaster, and Ashton learnt a lot from her -- above all, as the technique sections below show, the upper body. In 1929 Nijinska resigned from Rubinstein's company; Ashton did not renew his contract and returned to London at Rambert's persuasion. Rambert took her students to Diaghilev Ballet performances in London, and Nijinska's Les biches particularly influenced him. His first choreography came in 1926, before Paris: A Tragedy of Fashion, or The Scarlet Scissors, for the revue Riverside Nights staged by Nigel Playfair and Rambert's husband Ashley Dukes, after Mme de Sevigne's letters about the chef Vatel, who killed himself when the fish arrived late for Louis XIV's banquet. Ashton himself danced in it, as a distracted man-modist, alongside Rambert as a mannequin, and The Observer called it "an engaging little ballet". It ran about fifty performances in a six-week run -- the only full performance of the work in Ashton's lifetime -- and Rambert calls it the company's founding work; it is generally considered to be the birth of British ballet. The mechanism is already there in miniature: a tragic servant transposed into a couturier, despair at a failed autumn collection ending in suicide by scissors, and the fashion show itself -- mannequin and model dances -- as the ballet's material. In 1930 Rambert founded the Ballet Club, forerunner of Ballet Rambert, with Alicia Markova as prima ballerina and Ashton as main choreographer and one of the leading dancers, and Ashton was also a founder member of the Camargo Society, established to foster British dancers and choreographers. His early-1930s Rambert works included Capriol Suite (1930), La peri (1931), The Lady of Shalott (1931), Facade (1931), Foyer de danse (1932) and Les Masques (1933). [C33917]

**Takeaway:** the first ballet is already the whole method -- a literary pretext, a joke about fashion, and character transposed into movement. [C33918]

## 4. De Valois and the triumvirate

Ashton's association with Ninette de Valois, founder of the Vic-Wells Ballet, began in 1931, when he created the comic ballet Regatta for her company, to mixed reviews. In 1933 he made Les Rendezvous for the company, an immediate success, still in the Royal Ballet repertoire decades later. In 1935 de Valois appointed him resident choreographer -- Britannica says he joined the Vic-Wells Ballet in 1933 and was principal choreographer from 1933, but 1935 is the appointment used here, the year he first worked for the company being 1933 -- and Wikipedia calls him chief choreographer to de Valois from 1935 until her retirement in 1963, through the company's renamings from Vic-Wells to Sadler's Wells Ballet in 1939 and to the Royal Ballet when the charter took effect on 15 January 1957. Alongside him stood the musical director Constant Lambert, in post from 1931 to 1947, and the three of them -- de Valois, Ashton and Lambert -- were the triumvirate that guided and nurtured British ballet through the 1930s and 1940s, in a company that included Markova, Anton Dolin and Robert Helpmann. 1935 also began his association with Margot Fonteyn, then Peggy Hookham, when he created the Bride in Le Baiser de la fee for her. In 1939 came his first ballet for a foreign company, Devil's Holiday for the Ballet Russe de Monte-Carlo, and shortly before the Second World War he was offered a position in New York with what became American Ballet Theatre; he declined and returned to de Valois's company. In 1941 he was called up and commissioned as an officer in the Royal Air Force, at first analysing aerial photographs, later as an intelligence officer, granted occasional leave to continue working with the ballet; his wartime works included The Quest (1943), with Walton. [C33919]

**Takeaway:** the institution is the instrument -- Ashton never built a company of his own, he built a style inside someone else's, and the style outlasted every one of the institution's name changes. [C33920]

## 5. Les Rendezvous: the rhythmic argument

Les Rendezvous, premiered 5 December 1933 by the Vic-Wells Ballet at Sadler's Wells, to Daniel Auber arranged by Constant Lambert, with designs by William Chappell and leads Alicia Markova and Stanislas Idzikowski, is a plotless one-act divertissement: a group of friends meet in a park, and dances follow in sequence. It is the clearest early statement of Ashton's rhythmic system, and Stephanie Jordan's close analysis is the most detailed mechanism source found for any early Ashton work. The three opening grand jete sequences each time the music differently -- "two, three or four changes of weight across 2-bar musical units. The accent is sometimes up, at the top of the leap, coinciding with the musical downbeat, sometimes down with the musical downbeat. The effect varies: quick, light and soaring, or slow and boldly deliberate, each entry followed by a fleet departure." Two men and one woman process in petits jetes devants against a square 2/4, alternating arms up and down on weak counts, in changing patterns across four phrases. In Markova's waltz solo the music moves into duple time against triple, creating 3 by 2 then 2 by 3 patterns, and her turning steps on pointe highlight the change to more rapid figuration. Ashton "also thwarts rhythmic expectations by shifting the musical accent from coinciding with movement up on pointe or in the air to down in plie, or vice versa." The Royal Ballet and Opera notes that the geometric floor patterns and angled entrances recall the formations of Petipa's ballet classics, but the work also featured what would become Ashtonian signatures: fleet footwork and an expressive use of the upper body known as epaulement. It is called Ashton's first substantial classical composition, "the first full-fledged statement of his own personal form of classicism -- and thus the basis of the British style: lyrical, precise, yet robust". [C33921]

**Takeaway:** the dance does not illustrate the music, it argues with it -- the same steps, a different accent, and a different meaning. [C33922]

## 6. Les Patineurs and A Wedding Bouquet: the divertissement as a place

Les Patineurs, premiered 16 February 1937 by the Vic-Wells Ballet at Sadler's Wells, to Meyerbeer arranged by Lambert, is a divertissement as a place, not a plot: a Victorian skating party on a frozen pond, about twenty-five minutes, no narrative development. The device is the translation of skating into ballet -- gliding, spinning, the "crocodile" chain in which the whole company holds waists and skates off as snow falls -- and character by skill level: graceful experts, a romantic couple, confident show-offs, and comically clumsy beginners, the Red Girls "whose unfortunate difficulties in maintaining themselves in an upright position afford a humorous interlude". Colour-coding does the dramaturgy: brown couples, blue girls, red girls, the white couple of Fonteyn and Helpmann, and the lone Blue Boy, Harold Turner. The structure is a strict divertissement sequence, and the ending is a pure virtuoso mechanism: the Blue Boy alone, "whirling like a top", an astonishing series of pirouettes even as the curtain falls. A Wedding Bouquet, premiered 27 April 1937 -- the day is provisional, from a Dancers Forum summary only, and is carried as such -- to Lord Berners, with a text by Gertrude Stein from her play They Must Be Wedded to Their Wife, works the opposite trick: spoken text as a second, disobedient score. The opera chorus sang or chanted the words at the early performances, and the words "explained the action, introduced the characters, made utterly irrelevant comments, worked themselves into a frenzied rhythmical accompaniment or injected on occasional apt phrase that devastated dancers and audience alike". A provincial French wedding goes wrong -- one guest gets drunk and is removed, the groom is a lecher, the demented Julia clings to his leg, the dog gets a tutu number -- and the bride and groom perform a pas de deux that "parodies the climactic duet of any classic ballet, turning upside down the conventions of the genre (and the ballerina herself)". The ballet ends, like Enigma Variations thirty-one years later, with everybody assembled for a photograph. [C33923]

**Takeaway:** give the steps a place to be in -- a rink, a wedding -- and the place does the dramaturgy the plot would have done. [C33924]

## 7. The Fred Step: a signature hidden in plain sight

David Vaughan defines the Fred Step, Ashton's signature kernel, as: pose en arabesque, coupe dessous, small developpe a la seconde, pas de bourree dessous, pas de chat. In plain terms, as far as the sources allow: a travelling phrase that steps or poses into arabesque, cuts the working foot underneath, opens a small developpe to second position, runs a pas de bourree dessous, and finishes with a pas de chat. It is based on a step Anna Pavlova used in a gavotte she frequently performed -- the Lima evening of 1917, returning as a phrase. Alicia Markova recalled in 1994 that Ashton first used it in a short ballet closing Nigel Playfair's 1930 production of Marriage a la Mode; it is not in Facade (1931), but after that it became a feature of his choreography. The critic Alastair Macaulay, quoted in Wikipedia, describes how he used it: "the Fred step is often tucked away. He may give it to the ballerina (Antoinette Sibley as La Capricciosa in Varii Capricci, 1983) or to supporting dancers (Symphonic Variations, 1946). He may give it to a corps de ballet of peasants (Sylvia, 1952), to junior dancers (a pair of dancing artichokes in the vegetable ballet he made for the 1979 film of The Tales of Beatrix Potter). Often the eye is distracted from it by action elsewhere onstage. In each instance, it is changed in some aspect (particularly its conclusion), so that the entire step seems metamorphosed." A Royal Opera House slow-motion demonstration, danced by Francesca Hayward and reported by Pointe, gives a slightly different variant -- arabesque, fondu, coupe, petit developpe, pas de bourree, pas de chat, leaving out the pas de bourree in that take -- which confirms the step is a flexible motif, not a fixed counts-and-positions formula. Adrian Grater enlarged the definition to include the transitional movements, and the step is transcribed in Benesh notation in the Wikipedia article: the transitions matter, not just the five named poses. Ashton himself danced it as the timorous stepsister in Cinderella, and he and Fonteyn danced a gentle version together in Salut d'amour, made for her sixtieth-birthday gala. What is not verified, and so not asserted: no source found gives counts, beats, or a bar-by-bar rhythmic placement for the Fred Step, nor a single correct arm line for it. [C33925]

**Takeaway:** a signature is not a trick you show off, it is a phrase you hide -- varied at the ending, given to anyone, and recognised only by those looking for it. [C33926]

## 8. Epaulement and the Ashton bend

Epaulement is "the angling of the head and shoulders that stems from a twist at the waist", in the Royal Ballet and Opera's definition, and "the nuanced angling of the head and shoulders, beginning at the waist", in Boston Ballet's -- a signature element detectable throughout his work. The twist originates at the waist, not the neck, so the shoulders and head sit in opposition to the hips and legs, and the torso is never simply square to the audience. Nijinska, his Paris mentor, "helped heighten his fondness for epaulement" and taught him "the transformative effect that a subtly inflected limb could have on a dancer's line", and the Australian Ballet notes that under her mentorship Ashton "focused on articulating upper body movements, making his technique distinct from the Russian style that concentrated on lower limbs". The practitioner version, from Leigh Witchel watching the Sarasota Ballet Ashton stagings, is that "Ashton epaulement is classical-plus. A cambre back goes back, and then more back for a greater, exaggerated sweep. A bend to the side is all the way to the side." A good place to see it, he says, is the opening entrance of the couples in Birthday Offering, and during rehearsal the coach Margaret Barbieri's note to a dancer was reportedly: "DANIEL PRATT! More body!" -- the correction is always more upper-body involvement, not less. Antoinette Sibley, quoted by the Ashton Foundation: "From the moment we went into the Company... we were trained to use our whole bodies, this was the way we danced... even up to my last Scenes de ballet, or The Dream, or A Month in the Country, he would still prod me and say bend more, bend more!" The contrast the Royal Ballet and Opera draws is with Balanchine: "where Balanchine fetishizes the leg, Ashton places a special emphasis on epaulement, constantly surprising changes of direction and uniquely intricate footwork." Balanchine's neoclassicism is grand and lofty, favouring tall, long-limbed dancers; Ashton's requires below-the-knee dexterity and is traditionally best suited to less lofty dancers -- Fonteyn, "the perfectly proportioned 5ft 4in" dancer, is the type-case. On joining the Royal Ballet in 1961, even Nureyev, "used to the broader brushstrokes of Soviet choreography, was astounded at how much there was to think about when realizing Ashton's work." De Valois, visiting the USSR in 1957, was more impressed by the milder-mannered young pupils at the Kirov's academy than by their more physically precocious counterparts at the Bolshoi's -- the spirit she wanted in the English school, grace before display, which she had taken from Enrico Cecchetti: as she said in her memoirs, he "put 'grace' before anything in a woman dancer". No source consulted makes a direct, sourced French-school comparison, and none is invented here. [C33927]

**Takeaway:** the line is made in the torso, not the leg -- and the correction is always more body, never less. [C33928]

## 9. Footwork: speed under the body

Ashton's footwork is fast, intricate, and under the body. Petit allegro and bourree are detail and emotion, not travel. Witchel: "Ashtonian footwork is fast and under the body. The point of pointework isn't as much to travel... as to show detail work -- and also states of emotion. When an Ashton ballerina gets lifted doing a battement serre [a small, tight, beaten step]; it's the fluttering of her heart." Speed is not Balanchine's monopoly, he adds: "We love to think of this as the province of Balanchine. That's parochialism. Ashton is blisteringly quick." Johan Kobborg, quoted in Dance Magazine, gives the specific difficulty: "It is very challenging for dancers to perform Ashton today because so much emphasis in their training is on adage movements, big jumps, and high legs. Ashton demands speed, and he uses the upper body a lot... in Ashton, you have the identical [fast] footwork, but doing a lot of port de bras at the same time." The Royal Ballet and Opera's summary is that Ashton is "the master of seraphic grace who, thanks to the combination of serene poise and formidable attention to detail required by his steps, is devilishly difficult to perform... There is, as everyone who performs him soon learns, nowhere to hide." Geraldine Morris's conference paper is titled "The Bourree: a Myriad of Uses", which is evidence that the bourree is a distinct, much-studied Ashton device -- but its argument was not accessed for this study, and only the title is claimed. The claim that Ashton phrases over the bar line rather than punctuating it appears only in unverified aggregator pages and is treated here as unconfirmed. [C33929]

**Takeaway:** speed is not the trick -- speed with the upper body moving at the same time is, and the footwork is where the feeling lives. [C33930]

## 10. Musicality without a score

The headline facts are unanimous: the Royal Ballet and Opera calls Ashton's neoclassicism "a match for Balanchine's in its extraordinary musicality", and Fonteyn's summary, quoted by Boston Ballet and the Ashton Foundation, is that "Frederick Ashton's ear for music, his eye for movement and his perception of human nature make him to ballet as William Shakespeare is to drama." The counter-intuitive detail, sourced and central, is how he actually worked: Ashton, unlike Balanchine, did not read a score and did not play an instrument. He familiarised himself thoroughly with the music and then relied on the collaboration of the arranger or rehearsal pianist for such technical analysis as might be necessary, such as breaking the music down into counts for the dancers. The rehearsal pianist Philip Gammon: "He never actually wanted to work out counts as such, as so many choreographers like to do. Kenneth MacMillan, for instance, would always want me to work out the dancers' counts before he even started to create the choreography." And: "Very often, the fitting of the dance to the musical phrase is done afterwards, by further adjustment, and for this reason the relation between the two is something that has to be felt by the dancers rather than analysed." A dancer asked how Ashton fits movement to music reportedly answered: "If I knew that, we could all be choreographers." He also let a dancer's different hearing stand: Lesley Collier, on creating Rhapsody, records that Baryshnikov "heard the music quite differently from either her or Ashton, and Ashton let him do it his way." His musicality is melodic, not metronomic, in Witchel's practitioner observation: in one of the first entrances of a quartet of chic ladies in Scenes de ballet, "they hop forward and in exactly on the melodic line. You can practically hear Ashton singing it as he demonstrated." The composers he worked with show the method. Stravinsky: Le Baiser de la fee (1935) and Scenes de ballet (1948), the latter after Ashton heard the music on the radio in the bath in 1947, was fascinated by its rhythm, rang the BBC, and had the recording sent from America. Walton, a lifelong friend: Facade (1931), The Wise Virgins (1940, Bach orchestrated by Walton), The Quest (1943, Walton's only original ballet score, written in great haste to a detailed timed synopsis Ashton supplied during six weeks' RAF leave), and Varii Capricci (1983). Delius: Nocturne (1936, to Delius's Paris) -- a posthumous borrowing, not a collaboration, Delius having died in 1934 -- and The Walk to the Paradise Garden (1972). Lambert, the institutional musical partner: musical director 1931-47, arranger of Les Rendezvous, Les Patineurs and Dante Sonata, composer of Horoscope (1938) and Tiresias (1951), conductor of the Symphonic Variations premiere -- and the man whose objection to Franck's music made Ashton drop his scenario and make that ballet abstract. Lanchbery, the later arranger-collaborator and principal conductor 1959-72: he rebuilt Herold for La Fille mal gardee, and arranged The Two Pigeons, The Dream and A Month in the Country. [C33931]

**Takeaway:** musicality is not counting -- it is living inside the music until the steps come out of it, and letting the dancers feel the fit instead of analysing it. [C33932]

## 11. Symphonic Variations: subtraction

Symphonic Variations, premiered 24 April 1946 by the Sadler's Wells Ballet at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, in a triple bill with Les Patineurs and Helpmann's Adam Zero, to Cesar Franck's Variations symphoniques, with designs by Sophie Fedorovitch and a cast of Margot Fonteyn, Moira Shearer, Pamela May, Michael Somes, Brian Shaw and Henry Danton, conducted by Constant Lambert, is Ashton's first ballet for the company in its new Covent Garden home, and it is a ballet made by taking things away. During the war Ashton listened to Franck's score constantly and first wrote an elaborate scenario -- readings of St Teresa of Avila and St John of the Cross, an allegory of the awakening of spring. Lambert objected to the music for ballet, and Ashton dropped his original scenario and created an abstract ballet. During the war the repertory had become increasingly literary, and Ashton's purpose was to counteract this. It was not his intention to display ingenuity of invention but to construct a more abstract piece, setting three men and three women dancing on the vast expanse of the Opera House stage uncluttered with scenery and effect. The Royal Ballet and Opera: "Stripping away narrative or ornamentation, Ashton emerged with a work of wondrous sparseness, featuring six dancers who never leave the stage and who end the ballet in the same position as they began it -- a masterful lesson in the art of simplicity." Six dancers in white on a vast empty stage, merging and parting in solos, duets, quartets and sextets; at one point they join hands and circle the stage; the opening image -- six immobile, minute dancers against a sprayed white-and-green backcloth -- proposes, in the critic A. V. Coton's phrase, "an imagery of infinities". The stamina mechanism matters: it was "an 'absolute marathon'" for the dancers, who took no part in any other ballet on the same bill in early seasons. The Royal Ballet and Opera calls it "Ashton's radiant celebration of 'pure dance'" and "a summation of his art hitherto", and Jennifer Homans calls it "practically a social democratic ballet" -- a community of individuals bound together by discipline and a common pursuit. [C33933]

**Takeaway:** subtraction is the bravest move -- take away the story, the scenery and the exits, and see whether the steps can hold a stage on their own. [C33934]

## 12. Scenes de ballet: Euclid on the floor

Scenes de ballet, premiered 11 February 1948 by the Sadler's Wells Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Stravinsky's Scenes de ballet of 1944, written for a Broadway revue and first choreographed by Anton Dolin for himself and Alicia Markova, with designs by the Picasso-influenced young Frenchman Andre Beaurepaire, a principal couple and a corps of sixteen, about twenty-four minutes, is the cold twin of the warm Cinderella of the same year. Ashton's choreography is along classical lines, in the tradition of Petipa's nineteenth-century works, but instead of Petipa's symmetry he used a system of Euclidean geometry, with geometric theorems adapted to serve as floor patterns for the dancers. As a result the ballet is unusual in that it makes sense from all angles, as Ashton himself explained: "I wanted to do a ballet that could be seen from any angle -- anywhere could be front, so to speak. So I did these geometric figures that are not always facing front -- if you saw Scenes de ballet from the wings, you'd get a very different but equally good picture." The watchable behaviour is daring asymmetry that resolves just as you notice it: at one point the entire female ensemble, twelve strong, "turns its back to us and does a stately combination while facing upstage left. We can't see their faces, their 'story.' We only see their lovely backs and thighs, moving away from us", in Acocella's description. Hands, heads and feet slot smartly into Stravinsky's mercurial time signatures. Henze, hearing it in Hamburg, said it showed him "one could achieve classicism in music and music theatre without having to be exclusively retrospective" and that Ashton "gave meaning to a pirouette, to a grand jete. These classical steps suddenly became something new." Ashton said he was drawn to the music's "cold, distant, uncompromising beauty" and "made a ballet to match, all steps and architectonics". With a rhythmically complex Stravinsky score, it is at once purely classic and uncompromisingly modernist, and it distilled the essence of Petipa's ballets down to just one act. One dating note: some catalogues list the work as 1947, the year of creation; the premiere is 11 February 1948, and 1948 is used here. [C33935]

**Takeaway:** a ballet that makes sense from the wings is a ballet built on geometry, not on facing -- and the backs of twelve dancers can be a picture. [C33936]

## 13. Cinderella: Petipa, pantomime, and the stepsister's dream

Cinderella, premiered 23 December 1948 by the Sadler's Wells Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Prokofiev, with a libretto by Ashton after Charles Perrault, and a cast of Moira Shearer as Cinderella, Michael Somes as the Prince, Alexander Grant as the Jester, and Ashton and Robert Helpmann en travesti as the Ugly Stepsisters, is Ashton's first major three-act ballet for a British company -- he had considered a full-evening ballet since 1939, and de Valois urged it. The choreography is Ashton's homage to the classical tradition of Petipa, as Symphonic Variations had been two years earlier, albeit on a smaller scale, and the ballet itself enacts the realisation of dreams, notably Cinderella's own. Ashton cut Prokofiev's third-act Prince's journey, a divertissement of national dances. The watchable behaviour runs in two registers at once: the yearning classical heroine, wistful, with lyrical pas de deux and the ballroom as a dream; and the comic preening of the stepsisters in drag, drawn on the traditions of the English Christmas pantomime. The stepsister mechanism is specific: the shy Ugly Sister -- Ashton's own role -- "performs a Petipa figure that amounts to her dream of being Odile at Siegfried's ball or the Sugar Plum Fairy". The comedy is a failed classical variation, danced by a man in drag who dreams of being the ballerina. "The charm of his Cinderella owes as much to the comic preening of his stepsisters-in-drag as it does to the yearning of his wistful heroine." Critics noted Ashton was not yet fully in control of a full-length ballet, with intermittent weaknesses in the choreography, but the comedy of the stepsisters was, and has remained, a favourite with audiences. Ashton danced the Fred Step himself as the timorous sister, and he played the role for years after the premiere. [C33937]

**Takeaway:** put the comedy inside the grandest work, and make the joke a dream -- the stepsister is funny because she wants, badly, to be the ballerina. [C33938]

## 14. Daphnis and Chloe, Romeo and Juliet: the music-led detours

Daphnis and Chloe, to Ravel, with designs by John Craxton -- chosen because he knew Greece, Ashton rejecting an antique look in favour of contemporary Greek costume -- and leads Margot Fonteyn as Chloe and Michael Somes as Daphnis, with Violetta Elvin as Lykanion, John Field as Dorkon and Alexander Grant as Bryaxis, the pirate chief, is a music-led pastoral narrative in one act and three scenes, after Longus. Its premiere day is split and is carried as a conflict: the Royal Ballet and Opera's production page says 3 April 1951, while Wikipedia and Dancers Forum say 5 April 1951; both agree on company, venue, leads and year. The Royal Ballet and Opera: "Ashton was driven to create new work after being inspired by music... Ravel's [score] so moved Ashton that he 'sometimes... felt that it was like waves that were going to submerge me'." The choreographic system is contrast of dance-characters, not plot machinery: "The contrasted dances for Daphnis and Dorkon, and the fierce passion of Lykanion, are all dramatically exciting, and the pirates' dances in the second scene are excellently crafted." The dance contest in Scene 1 is the mechanism made literal: Dorkon's rough and lusty coarse dancing is easily outshone by the grace of Daphnis. Ashton avoided any archaisms in the general dances of the first act, which recapture the feeling of ritual and homage to the god in simple and beautiful terms -- the ritual is classical, not archaeological. Critics misunderstood it at the premiere; continued acquaintance soon recognised that it is one of Ashton's most moving and rewarding short ballets. Romeo and Juliet, premiered in May 1955 by the Royal Danish Ballet at the Royal Theatre, Copenhagen, for the Spring Festival -- the exact day was not found in any source read, and no day is stated here -- to Prokofiev, with designs by Peter Rice, and a cast of Mona Vangsaae as Juliet, Henning Kronstam as Romeo and Frank Schaufuss as Mercutio, took seven weeks to choreograph. Erik Bruhn was Ashton's expected Romeo but was in the United States; he danced it months later at the Edinburgh Festival. Ashton had asked de Valois to do Romeo and Juliet for his home company; she refused -- Cinderella was already one Prokofiev in the repertoire. Vera Volkova invited him to Copenhagen, and he proposed Romeo or Sylvia. He had not seen the Lavrovsky version, and made the first version outside of the Soviet sphere of influence using Prokofiev's score without that model: the checkpoints were Prokofiev and Petipa, but also Shakespeare, and Kronstam recalled that Ashton would recite lines from the play to explain what was happening in the duets. It was also the first full-length Romeo and Juliet produced outside the Soviet Union. The watchable behaviour is intimate scale: "an intimate, lyrical imagining of Shakespeare's tragedy: a watercolor in a gallery of oil paintings". For the precise and modest Danish dancers, Ashton kept an intimate scale, though he allowed Romeo to handle Juliet's apparent corpse with what was considered at first distasteful excess. After the Bolshoi's 1956 London triumph, de Valois asked Ashton to bring his Romeo to the Royal Ballet for the 1964 Shakespeare quatercentenary; Ashton declined, judging a more vigorous approach was needed for a post-Bolshoi audience, and commissioned the young Kenneth MacMillan's 1965 version instead. Ashton's version was dropped in Denmark in 1965, after a rights withdrawal following a La Sylphide exchange dispute, and was not seen in London until 1985, when London Festival Ballet revived it. [C33939]

**Takeaway:** when the score is the source, let the score do the character work -- grace against coarseness, lyricism against scale, and the dance contest decides. [C33940]

## 15. Ondine: water without set pieces

Ondine, premiered 27 October 1958 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to a commissioned score by Hans Werner Henze, titled Undine, with designs by Lila de Nobili, was created for Dame Margot Fonteyn, with Michael Somes as Palemon, Julia Farron as Berta and Alexander Grant as Tirrenio, and Henze guest-conducted the premiere. Ashton first approached Walton for the score; Walton suggested Henze, and the two met on Ischia and moved Fouque's northern novella Undine to the Mediterranean. It is the only full-length ballet that Ashton choreographed to original music. One critic called it "a concerto for Fonteyn": nearly every performance from 1958 until the work left the repertoire in 1966 was danced by Fonteyn, the exceptions being Nadia Nerina and Svetlana Beriosova. "Through the ballet's three acts the watery imagery pervades the choreography, and Hans Werner Henze's specially commissioned score complements it perfectly with its rippling, flowing textures and translucent orchestrations." And: "Like the water, this ballet flows along with none of the set pieces that one might expect" -- the mechanism is continuity rather than divertissement numbers. Specific devices: Ondine dances with her own shadow in the moonlight, the pas de l'ombre, Ashton's tribute to Perrot's 1843 Ondine, ou La naiade; and a long final-act divertissement was originally intended to end with Tirrenio's creatures throwing off their disguises and driving the guests into the sea, cut as unstageable. Ashton devised his own libretto and worked for the first time with a commissioned score, rather than with one already written, and was completely in control of all its elements. The first major revival came in 1988, and the work has been regularly performed since. Henze's music, in the Arts Desk's phrase, "was written to summon pictures of the sea in all its moods, rather than spin melodies". The specific step-vocabulary for Ondine's water quality -- for instance any undulating port de bras -- was not verified from a source in this study and is not asserted. [C33941]

**Takeaway:** continuity is a mechanism -- when the water never stops, there is nowhere for a set piece to hide, and the star has to carry three acts on flow alone. [C33942]

## 16. La Fille mal gardee: the prop as plot

La Fille mal gardee, premiered 28 January 1960 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Ferdinand Herold of 1828, arranged and rebuilt by John Lanchbery with additions from the 1789 pastiche and one Hertel number for the Clog Dance, with designs by Osbert Lancaster and a cast of Nadia Nerina as Lise, David Blair as Colas, Stanley Holden as Widow Simone and Alexander Grant as Alain, is Ashton's sunniest masterpiece and his clearest demonstration that a prop can be the plot. Ashton did his customary careful research: he rejected the standard Hertel 1864 score on Ivor Guest's advice, took Herold's lighter 1828 score, and had Lanchbery rebuild it into effectively a new score. The ballet is built as a run of named set pieces, each driven by a physical object or an inherited dance. The Pas de ruban: Lise and Colas perform "a charming pas with intricate tricks using a pink satin ribbon", resurrected from Petipa's 1885 revival for Virginia Zucchi. The Fanny Elssler pas de deux takes the ribbon idea to an entirely new level: "a spectacular Grand adage for Lise, Colas and eight women with eight ribbons", the music for which was recovered from a violin reduction found in an old box at the Paris Opera. Lise gathers the ribbon ends while eight women run circles around her as she turns on pointe -- Alastair Macaulay likened it to "the music of the spheres" -- and the ribbons are tied and untied on hooks and wrists, "neat symbolism for making and breaking engagements, signing then tearing up contracts". The Clog Dance: Widow Simone, traditionally a male dancer en travesti, and four dancers in wooden clogs, "whirling through steps that are executed sur la pointe while wearing the clogs, directly modelled on a Lancashire clog dancing performance" Ashton took Lanchbery to see -- a deliberate collision of vernacular percussive dance and pointe technique. The Maypole Dance: ribbons braid as dancers weave figures around the pole. The "When I'm Married" mime: Petipa's original mimed sequence, taught to Ashton by Tamara Karsavina, in which Lise pantomimes married domestic life. And around these: four chickens and a rooster in a vaudeville routine, a Mack Sennett-style storm scene, butter churns, wine bottles, carts and live ponies. Mark Morris, asked to name a work by another choreographer he loved, named this one: "It's got everything, including a Maypole." It is "perhaps his most beloved creation" and "widely regarded as Ashton's sunniest masterpiece". [C33943]

**Takeaway:** give the lovers a ribbon and the ribbon does the plotting -- it tangles, untangles, binds and releases them exactly as the story does. [C33944]

## 17. Marguerite and Armand: one extended pas de deux

Marguerite and Armand, premiered 12 March 1963 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, at a Royal Gala in the presence of Queen Elizabeth II, to Franz Liszt's Piano Sonata in B minor orchestrated by Humphrey Searle, with designs by Cecil Beaton, was created for Margot Fonteyn as Marguerite and Rudolf Nureyev as Armand, with Michael Somes as Armand's father. Ashton heard the Liszt sonata on the radio and only afterwards discovered that Liszt had been a lover of Marie Duplessis, the real Marguerite. The mechanism is the flashback as a single extended pas de deux: Ashton set the ballet as a fevered dream in which Marguerite, from her sickbed, looks back on the great romance of her short life. The entire work contains no ensemble numbers and is effectively, with the exception of the scene between Marguerite and Armand's father, one extended pas de deux between the two principals. Six short scenes: first meeting, escape to the country, the father's confrontation, renunciation, return to Paris, deathbed reunion; she dies in his arms. "Less a ballet than a series of psychological closeups", in Richard Buckle's phrase. It was one of the first works of modern ballet in which the male lead was of equal dramatic weight to the female lead -- the mechanism is the equality of the partnership: Fonteyn, forty-three and planning retirement, and Nureyev, newly defected, made the vehicle. The Royal Gala audience gave it twenty-one curtain calls. Ashton restricted it to them for decades; Sylvie Guillem was the first other Marguerite, in 2000. Running time is about thirty to thirty-five minutes. [C33945]

**Takeaway:** a whole novel in one duet -- take away the ensemble and the two bodies have to carry the story, the past and the dying, between them. [C33946]

## 18. The Dream: compression by pas de deux

The Dream, premiered 2 April 1964 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, in a triple bill with MacMillan's Images of Love and Helpmann's Hamlet to mark the 400th anniversary of Shakespeare's birth, to Felix Mendelssohn's incidental music to A Midsummer Night's Dream, Opp. 21 and 61, arranged by John Lanchbery, with designs by Henry Bardon and David Walker, is set in or about the 1840s -- Mendelssohn's time, not Athens. The cast was Antoinette Sibley as Titania, Anthony Dowell as Oberon, Keith Martin as Puck, Alexander Grant as Bottom and Alan Bauch as the Changeling Indian Boy; the Sibley-Dowell partnership began here. Ashton drastically trimmed Shakespeare's plot, discarding Theseus and Hippolyta and the play-within-a-play, Pyramus and Thisbe. The focus is the fairies and the four lovers, and the argument of the ballet is carried by two partnered dances: Titania and Bottom in the first half, Titania and Oberon in the second. Oberon expands one line -- "Sound, music! Come my queen, take hands with me" -- "into one of the loveliest pas de deux ever created". The watchable behaviour: Oberon's "quicksilver choreography... full of fast, intricate footwork and jumps"; the fleet-footed fairy corps darting and skittering; Puck "flitting in and out of the action... smoothly buzzing to and fro"; Bottom -- "one of the few male roles danced en pointe" -- scratching his hooves on the stage, intertwining legs with the seducing Titania, his ass's head and en pointe dressages as the comic mechanism; and the four lovers' choreography in confusion, fleetingly teaming up with the wrong partner in the finale. The fairies pay homage to Romantic ballet, Taglioni-era wreaths and longish tutus, while the eroticism is Ashton's own: the ballet underscores the eroticism that has been eclipsed by the emphasis placed on dance in the play's reception history. It was not an initial success; it is now an Ashtonian classic. [C33947]

**Takeaway:** compression is not cutting -- it is finding the two duets the whole play turns on, and letting everything else fall away. [C33948]

## 19. Monotones: the frieze in process

Monotones II came first: 24 March 1965, at a Royal Ballet Benevolent Fund Gala at the Royal Opera House, to Erik Satie's Trois Gymnopedies of 1888, orchestrated by Claude Debussy and Roland-Manuel, with a cast of Vyvyan Lorrayne, Anthony Dowell and Robert Mead -- two men and one woman -- in costumes designed by Ashton himself, white unitards with glittering belts and skullcaps. Monotones I was added on 25 April 1966, to Satie's Trois Gnossiennes of 1890 and Prelude d'Eginhard of 1893, orchestrated by John Lanchbery, with a cast of Antoinette Sibley, Georgina Parkinson and Brian Shaw -- two women and one man -- in green unitards. The performance order is I then II, although II was composed first. Two dating cautions: some sources give 26 April 1966 for Monotones I, a one-day discrepancy against the Royal Ballet and Opera Collections record, which is preferred here; and Britannica's Ashton entry reverses the numbering, saying Part I, 1965, and Part II, 1966, which is wrong against the Royal Ballet and Opera Collections, the New York Public Library and Vaughan -- II, the Gymnopedies, is 1965, and I, the Gnossiennes, is 1966. The mechanism is arabesque and attitude as sculpture. "Choreographically, the dance was built on a basic vocabulary of arabesques and attitudes. Some of the motifs repeated those in Symphonic Variations." "With their sculptural groupings of precisely calibrated arabesques, these dances distilled Ashton's personal classicism to pure essence." The Royal Ballet and Opera: each of these is for just three performers who appear, especially in Monotones II, "both to come from and to be performing on some distant, weightless planet". The two trios are gently contrasting: II is the more hieratic and earthbound one, two men slowly manipulating the woman through beautiful contortions, while I is the more human and relational, the two women and one man travelling together as equals. "Set to music by Erik Satie, Monotones keeps its dancers clustered... the dancing figures inhabit a frieze in process, dreamily making and unmaking it in a timeless golden void." Arlene Croce: "The continuity of Ashton's line is like that of a master draftsman whose pen never leaves the paper." Ashton, in the Sarasota programme note's summary, "fully grasps Satie's intention to discard grandiose, orchestral sonority... in search of a lighter, understated, melodic clarity, giving the ballet limpid, flowing lines, and precise, calm, metronomic movements". [C33949]

**Takeaway:** three bodies, one vocabulary, no story -- the arabesque is the whole ballet, made and unmade in a golden void. [C33950]

## 20. Enigma Variations: the portrait gallery

Enigma Variations, premiered 25 October 1968 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Edward Elgar's Variations on an Original Theme ("Enigma"), Op. 36 of 1898-99, with Ashton's restoration of Elgar's original shorter finale -- the published 96-bar extension added at the publisher Jaeger's urging was dropped, with the Elgar estate's permission -- and designs by Julia Trevelyan Oman, whose idea originated in the early 1950s when Oman, then a student, submitted designs, runs about thirty minutes. The cast included Derek Rencher as Elgar, Svetlana Beriosova as Mrs Elgar, Desmond Doyle as Jaeger, or Nimrod, Ann Jenner as Dorabella, Anthony Dowell as Troyte and Wayne Sleep as Sinclair, in the bicycle solo. Elgar's variations portray "My friends pictured within", and Ashton stages an imaginary gathering at the Elgars' Worcestershire house in 1898: the struggling, little-known composer awaits a message from London, and while they wait each friend is portrayed in dances representing their personalities. Each variation is a prop-and-gait portrait: Steuart-Powell enters on his bicycle, knocks out his pipe on his heel, rushes off; Townshend arrives on his tricycle with children and his ear trumpet; Meath Baker bounds downstairs brandishing a list; Ysobel rises from a hammock; Troyte bursts in with a dazzling classical variation; Sinclair arrives with children and a dog that dashes into the River Wye; Dorabella, with a speech impediment told by the music, dashes in and hugs the composer; Lady Mary Lygon drifts across the stage "like some unattainable ideal" in curls of mist. The centre is the Nimrod variation, in Acocella's description: Elgar and Jaeger "mostly walk, interspersing their paces with slow, plangent gestures. They turn outward from each other, then back inward. They do ronds de jambe par terre, in which the foot traces a circle on the floor. This, I suppose, is their ratiocination, the discussion of Beethoven that... both disguises and contains their feelings for each other. Soon they are joined by Elgar's wife, and the men pass her back and forth between them, in low lifts. She becomes the stand-in for Beethoven, the medium through which the two friends speak to each other." "The dance is very austere, particularly compared with the welling music... and it ends as modestly as it began, with the three dancers simply lifting their arms to Heaven, as if asking a question. The subject is sympathy." Ashton's own enigma: a telegram arrives -- the characters know, the audience does not, that Hans Richter has agreed to conduct Elgar's new work -- and an exuberant finale follows. It is "a gallery of portraits of the composer Edward Elgar and his wife and friends, [which] became the clearest example of the way Ashton extended the vocabulary of ballet as a poetic language." Acocella notes the autobiographical charge: Ashton made it in 1968 knowing he would be replaced as director in 1970; Elgar gets the telegram, and Ashton got no such reprieve. [C33951]

**Takeaway:** a friend is a walk, a prop and a gait -- and the deepest feeling in the ballet is two men walking together, tracing circles on the floor. [C33952]

## 21. A Month in the Country: chamber drama by duet

A Month in the Country, premiered 12 February 1976 by the Royal Ballet at the Royal Opera House, to Frederic Chopin -- three works for piano and orchestra arranged by John Lanchbery: the Variations on "La ci darem la mano" Op. 2, the Fantasia on Polish Airs Op. 13, and the Andante Spianato and Grande Polonaise Op. 22 -- with designs by Julia Trevelyan Oman, after Ivan Turgenev's play in its 1850 setting, and a cast of Lynn Seymour as Natalia Petrovna, Anthony Dowell as Beliaev the tutor, Alexander Grant as Yslaev, Wayne Sleep as Kolia, Denise Nunn as Vera and Derek Rencher as Rakitin, dedicated to Sophie Fedorovitch and Bronislava Nijinska, runs about forty minutes. Ashton had wanted to adapt the play since seeing it at the Westminster Theatre in the 1930s; a meeting with Isaiah Berlin in the late 1960s settled it, and Berlin suggested Chopin. Ashton stripped Turgenev's tale of its extraneous details, boiling it down to its essential relationships, which manifest in the ballet as various solos and pas de deux. The story is a sequence of duets that carry the action forward: Natalia and her admirer Rakitin, then Beliaev and Vera, then Beliaev and the maid Katia, and finally Beliaev and Natalia. The Don Giovanni theme in Chopin's Op. 2 is the joke and the warning at once: Beliaev "echoes Don Giovanni in his opportunities for conquest" -- easy with the maid, illicit with the ward, then knocked sideways by the mother's passionate advance. Jordan's analysis: in the Polonaise, Rakitin's declamatory piano gestures accompany pointing, and the scene where Natalia discovers Beliaev and Vera embracing, listens, and strikes Vera is set to "firm gestures" in the music, with Natalia opening and closing double doors on piano arpeggios "to histrionic effect". The watchable behaviour is restraint as the mechanism: longing shown through formality, not abandon. The critic Alastair Macaulay, via Jordan, proposes that Ashton used the music "to look back to a world where 'romantic flirtation might combine with social formality more comfortably than in a twentieth-century setting'." "A miraculous balletic rendering... Fascinating, in psychology, in storytelling, in dance", in the Arts Desk's verdict. It is Ashton's only longer work after retiring as director in 1970, the other being the Tales of Beatrix Potter film, and it has been revived in every decade since. One correction, carried from the technique leg: do not credit Sibley as the original Natalia -- that was Seymour, on 12 February 1976, with Dowell as Beliaev. [C33953]

**Takeaway:** the late proof that the system scales down -- a sequence of duets, no corps spectacle, and longing shown through formality is stronger than longing shown through abandon. [C33954]

## 22. Mime reformed: same signs, smaller space

Ashton wrote in 1951, quoted by Giannandrea Poesio: "In my balletic ideology it is the dancing which must be the foremost factor, for ballet is an expression of emotions and ideas through dancing." His criticism, Poesio argues, was directed at the insertion of overwhelming and often gratuitous gestural recitatives in contemporary ballets. Although he acknowledged the importance of expressive gestures and their contribution to the narrative of the work, he regarded long, conventional mime sequences as superfluous additions, if not as detrimental to the dance's purity. He planned mime scenes for Ondine in 1958 that were eventually omitted. How he reformed mime mechanically, in Poesio's account, is the most precise source found for any Ashton technique. He drew the fundamentals of his gesture language from pre-existing formulae -- above all the nineteenth-century Italian mime tradition, "carefully preserved and perpetuated in twentieth-century England" -- but "never complied with the grammar of that language, and constantly filtered the rules of its syntax through a personal approach". Even the most canonical Italian gestures -- "to marry", "to love", "to hear", the fairies' listening gesture in The Dream being Poesio's example -- are rendered by Ashton "smoother, less rigid than the original model". In his ballets the use of space, an important component of Italian ballet mime, is reduced considerably, and gestures lack that flashy, flowery complexity that characterised the Italian style. The constant opposition between one gesture and another, one of the fundamentals of the Italian language of gesture -- not to be confused with the balletic law of epaulement -- is generally disregarded in Ashton's ballets, "to provide the gesture with more spontaneity". To the originally unrhythmical nature of the movements, Ashton juxtaposes an accurate phrasing which confers on the gesture an incredible musical fluidity. The result, Poesio says, was "significantly tempered by Ashton's refined taste, by what is generally indicated by non-English people as the restrained and innately elegant" English manner. In short: same signs, smaller space, smoother transitions, no gesture-versus-gesture opposition, and the gesture phrased to the music like a step. That is the mechanism that makes his mime legible without embarrassment -- it is danced, not recited. The famous mime set-piece is "When I'm Married" in La Fille mal gardee: Ashton resurrected Petipa's original mimed sequence, taught to him by Tamara Karsavina. Lise, locked in by her mother, dreams of married life with Colas and of having his children -- then discovers Colas has been hiding behind the hay bales and watching. The comedy is in the situation and the dawning embarrassment, played through the smoothed mime above, not in mugging. [C33955]

**Takeaway:** mime works when it is danced -- the same signs, smaller, smoother, and phrased to the music like steps. [C33956]

## 23. Comedy: character and timing, not slapstick

Ashton and Robert Helpmann originated the two stepsisters in Cinderella, "drawing on the traditions of the English Christmas pantomime" and "the English vaudeville tradition". Ashton himself played one sister, the timorous one -- he also danced the Fred Step in the role -- and the other was Helpmann. The Australian Ballet's summary of Ashton's solution to "how do you make dancers ugly and ungainly?" is "having his Stepsisters played by men in drag and clownish prosthetics". But a BalletcoForum corrective, citing the filmed Ashton-MacMillan and Ashton-Helpmann versions, argues the original sisters "are not pantomime dames": in Macles' original designs their costumes were "positively ordinary" compared with today's over-the-top versions, and the sisters "actually belong to the same balletic world that Cinderella and her prince inhabited"; the fault in modern productions is "the failure to rein in the antics of the Ugly Sisters... through a want of good taste". The same forum notes Ashton "disliked drag", in Macaulay's phrase, despite famously playing women's roles himself -- Mrs Tiggy-Winkle, Carabosse, Queen Victoria. The comedy, then, was in character, timing, and the fact that two famous men of the theatre were playing the sisters, with reported on-stage improvisation and rivalry between Ashton and Helpmann -- not in grotesque slapstick. Modern broad playing is a later distortion, not the design. La Fille mal gardee works as a comedy system on the same principle. Widow Simone is "essentially based on a Pantomime dame"; Lise is "a truly comic role"; Alain, her reluctant, simple but rich groom, is "extremely funny, and would often have the cast in fits of laughter". The Clog Dance is a mechanical joke -- vernacular percussive dance colliding with pointe technique -- and Simone "shows off her over zealous skills and nearly comes unstuck". The Royal Ballet and Opera's framing of his comedy generally is that he is "probably best known to many as the wittiest and most passionate of all romantic comedians", and that Cinderella, La Fille mal gardee, The Two Pigeons and The Dream are "largely comic tales of true love conquering all, as witty, playful and tasteful as they are profoundly and poetically heartfelt". [C33957]

**Takeaway:** the joke is in the character and the timing -- two famous men playing sisters, a widow in clogs nearly coming unstuck -- and the moment it becomes grotesque, it stops being Ashton's. [C33958]

## 24. The muse system: roles built on named dancers

Roles were built on specific dancers, and the mechanism changes when the instrument changes: Alicia Markova in the 1930s, then Margot Fonteyn for more than thirty years, then Antoinette Sibley and Anthony Dowell, then Fonteyn and Nureyev. Fonteyn's association with Ashton began in 1935, when he created the Bride in Le Baiser de la fee for her, and he went on to create roles for her across his career: Nocturne in 1936, in which she played the wistful flower seller, Horoscope in 1938, Apparitions in 1936, Dante Sonata in 1940, Symphonic Variations in 1946, Daphnis and Chloe in 1951, Sylvia in 1952, Ondine in 1958 -- "a concerto for Fonteyn", the title role choreographed specially for her -- and Marguerite and Armand in 1963, with Nureyev. Fonteyn succeeded Markova as prima ballerina in 1935 and was Ashton's leading ballerina for the whole of his Royal Ballet career. Anthony Dowell, quoted by the Ashton Foundation, describes the creative process: it "was always a collaborative one... he was interested, intrigued and inspired not only by the dancers' talent but also by their personality and the role, once completed, was expertly tailor made for them." Michael Somes was the leading man -- Symphonic Variations, the Prince in Cinderella, Palemon in Ondine -- and later assistant director of the Royal Ballet from 1963 to 1970 and principal repetiteur from 1970 to 1985, staging many Ashton ballets after retirement; he also played the Father in Marguerite and Armand and organised Ashton's 1970 farewell gala. Robert Helpmann, besides the stepsister double-act that ran from 1948 to 1975, took over as the company's choreographer while continuing as principal dancer when Ashton was called up for RAF service, and choreographed his own ballets for the company -- Comus in 1942, Hamlet in 1942, Miracle in the Gorbals in 1944. Nadia Nerina was the technical star for whom Ashton created Lise in La Fille mal gardee, and whose first Ashton role was the Fairy Spring in Cinderella; the Telegraph obituary said it was for Nerina that he created what is often thought of as his masterpiece. Svetlana Beriosova was one of the seven ballerinas of Birthday Offering in 1956, each given a solo tailored to her qualities, and took over the title role of Ondine from Fonteyn on 30 March 1961. Sibley and Dowell were the next generation's muse pair: Titania and Oberon in The Dream were created on them in 1964, and Dowell's created roles ran on through Troyte in Enigma Variations, Beliaev in A Month in the Country and Lo Straniero in Varii Capricci; he later directed the Royal Ballet from 1986 to 2001. The will is the proof of the system: Ashton bequeathed the rights to Daphnis and Chloe and Ondine to Fonteyn, to Cinderella and Symphonic Variations to Somes, and to The Dream and A Month in the Country to Dowell. [C33959]

**Takeaway:** the role is the dancer -- change the instrument and the mechanism changes, and the will itself is the proof, bequeathing ballets to the bodies they were built on. [C33960]

## 25. Director, 1963-1970: the golden age and the exit

In 1963, on de Valois's retirement, Ashton succeeded her as Director of The Royal Ballet. His directorship is described as "something of a golden age": the corps de ballet was recognised as rivalling the best anywhere. He continued choreographing -- The Dream in 1964, Monotones II in 1965, Jazz Calendar in 1968, Enigma Variations in 1968 -- revived Nijinska's Les biches and Les noces and Massine's Mam'zelle Angot, brought in Antony Tudor, and commissioned Kenneth MacMillan's Romeo and Juliet. 1963 also saw Marguerite and Armand, created especially for the new Fonteyn-Nureyev partnership. The critic John Percival, quoted in Wikipedia, considered Ashton unsuited to and uninterested in management and lacking de Valois's gift for strategic planning -- a judgement offered as contemporary criticism, not fact. In July 1970 Ashton stood down as director after a farewell gala organised by Michael Somes, John Hart and Leslie Edwards. The timing was driven by the general administrator David Webster's own retirement in 1970; Ashton had often said he looked forward to retirement but was hurt by the abruptness with which his departure was arranged and announced. He was succeeded as director by Kenneth MacMillan. One dating caveat belongs here: the formal appointment as Associate Director is 1952, per the Foundation timeline, the Royal Ballet School and Britannica, but American Ballet Theatre says his position as Associate Director was publicly recognised in 1948, and Vienna State Opera says he was de Valois's deputy from 1942. The 1952 appointment is the majority position, and the earlier dates likely reflect an informal deputyship; no single start year is stated here without that caveat. [C33961]

**Takeaway:** the director who would rather choreograph -- a golden age built by a man who did not want the job, and an exit that hurt because it was arranged, not chosen. [C33962]

## 26. Late work and the long goodbye

After retirement he choreographed freelance, resolving to create only works that particularly interested him or presented a challenge. The cinema film The Tales of Beatrix Potter was made in 1970 and released in 1971, and he danced Mrs Tiggy-Winkle in it himself. A Month in the Country followed in 1976. Rhapsody in 1980 was his last major work -- Britannica lists it as 1981, and the 1980 date is the majority position -- and Varii Capricci in 1983 was his final ballet, premiered at the Royal Ballet. In 1985 his partner Martyn Thomas died in a car crash, "a blow from which Ashton never fully recovered". He retired to a country home near Eye, Suffolk, Chandos Lodge, returning to London to supervise revivals. His honours, confirmed across Wikipedia, the Foundation, the Royal Ballet School and American Ballet Theatre: CBE in the 1950 Birthday Honours, Knight Bachelor in the 1962 Birthday Honours, Companion of Honour in the 1970 Birthday Honours, and the Order of Merit in October 1977 -- confirmed, a select honour in the personal gift of the Queen, who invited him to become a member -- plus the Queen Elizabeth II Coronation Award in 1959, the Freedom of the City of London in 1981, and honorary doctorates from Durham in 1962, East Anglia in 1967, London in 1970, Hull in 1971 and Oxford in 1976. The foreign honours' years conflict between sources: the Legion of Honour is 1960 per Wikipedia and 1962 per the Foundation, American Ballet Theatre and the Bavarian State Opera, and the Order of the Dannebrog is 1964 per Wikipedia and 1963 per American Ballet Theatre and the Bavarian State Opera; neither is settled here. Vienna State Opera's claim that the 1962 knighthood was a K.B.E. is treated as an error: every other source says Knight Bachelor, and the post-nominals are consistently OM CH CBE, not KBE. He left his legacy of over a hundred ballets to family, friends and those who inspired him. The Frederick Ashton Foundation was established in 2011; a memorial stone to the founders of the Royal Ballet -- de Valois, Ashton, Constant Lambert and Margot Fonteyn -- was dedicated at Westminster Abbey on 17 November 2009; and an English Heritage Blue Plaque was mounted at his former home in Marlborough Street, Chelsea, in 2016. [C33963]

**Takeaway:** the late work is the proof of the system -- one chamber piece, one last major work, one final ballet, each made only because it interested him, and nothing made for the sake of making. [C33964]

## 27. Aesthetic logic: the small kernel, the music, the torso, the joke

The aesthetic logic of Ashton's work runs on four rules, each visible in more than one work. First, the small kernel: a five-step phrase, a ribbon, a walk, a bicycle -- varied rather than multiplied, hidden in plain sight, given to any rank of dancer, and changed at the ending so the whole seems metamorphosed. Second, music as structure: the dance argues with the score, visualises it, or compresses it, and never merely decorates it -- and the man who made the most musical ballets in England could not read a note, working instead by immersion and feel, fitted afterwards, with the dancers feeling the fit rather than analysing it. Third, the upper body over the leg: the line is made by epaulement and the curve of the back, not by height; speed with port de bras at the same time is the specific difficulty; and understatement is the English manner -- restraint, simplicity and poise, with technical innovations that are almost invariably means of heightened expression rather than ends in themselves. Fourth, comedy and lyricism in the same work: stepsisters inside Cinderella, Bottom inside The Dream, the Clog Dance inside La Fille mal gardee, the dog in A Wedding Bouquet -- wit is not relief from the lyricism, it is part of it. [C33965]

What he never does is the thing a choreographer of his standing is supposed to do: he never lets a variation exist for its own sake, never trusts the step to carry the evening, never multiplies when variation will do, and never explains what the dance can show. The cost of that is visible in the record -- full-lengths where critics found intermittent weaknesses, works misunderstood at their premieres, a directorship he did not want and an exit that hurt -- and the cost is the same as the achievement: when the kernel is strong the work is unforgettable, and when it is weak there is nothing underneath to save it. He is the choreographer of the single strong idea carried by a body, a score and a change of direction, and the study of him is the study of how far a small phrase can be carried when the steps are not allowed to help -- and of what it costs when the idea that carries you is one you built yourself, over fifty years, one variation at a time.

**Takeaway:** know what the instrument is -- a kernel, a score, a torso and a joke -- and the failures are as legible as the successes: a full-length that sags is the same fact as a trio that floats, a phrase varied too far. [C33966]
