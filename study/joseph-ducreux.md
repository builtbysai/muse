# Joseph Ducreux: Study Notes

**Site:** Joseph Ducreux (Sunday 26 June 1735 - Saturday 24 July 1802), French portrait painter in pastel and oil -- born at Nancy, in Lorraine, the son of Charles Ducreux, premier peintre to Stanislaw Leszczynski, former king of Poland and duke of Lorraine; came to Paris about 1760; married Philippine-Rose Cosse at Saint-Sulpice on Wednesday 12 November 1760; was received by the Academie de Saint-Luc in late October 1764 and was rejected three times by the Academie royale; was sent to Vienna in 1769 by the duc de Choiseul to paint the Archduchess Marie-Antoinette before her marriage to the Dauphin, arriving Tuesday 14 February 1769 and staying ten months; was made premier peintre de la reine by Marie-Antoinette although never an Academician; painted the court in pastel and, increasingly from about 1800, in oil; exhibited at Pahin de La Blancherie's Salon de la Correspondance from 1781, showing his Self-Portrait, Yawning there on Wednesday 10 December 1783; fled to London in 1791, exhibited five portraits at the Royal Academy and etched self-portraits there, and was back in Paris by September 1791 for the first open Salon; painted the revolutionaries after painting the king, and drew the black-and-white chalk portrait inscribed as made at the Temple on Wednesday 2 January 1793, traditionally called the last portrait of Louis XVI; was granted lodgings in the Louvre by the Convention in April 1793 which he could not take up, served on the Gobelins jury in 1794, received a national reward of 3,000 livres in 1795; showed for the last time at the Salon of the year IX in 1801, where the critics told him to return to pastel; and died of apoplexy on the road from Paris to Saint-Denis on Saturday 24 July 1802, aged 67, his eldest daughter Rose-Adelaide dying of yellow fever in Saint-Domingue two days later. His eldest daughter Rose-Adelaide Ducreux (1761-1802) was a painter in her own right. The signature works are the Marie-Antoinette of 1769 (pastel on parchment, 64.8 x 49.5 cm, Versailles), the Turgot pastel of 1774-77 on five joined sheets, the Franklin pastel of 1777, the Self-Portrait, Yawning of by 1783 (Getty), Le Discret of c. 1791 (Spencer Museum), the Mocker of c. 1793 (Louvre, on deposit at Vizille), the Louis XVI at the Temple of 2 January 1793 (Musee Carnavalet, attribution contested), the Bougainville of 1793 (Versailles), and the Lalande of 1802 (Versailles). The modern record is Georgette Lyon's monograph Joseph Ducreux, Premier peintre de Marie-Antoinette (1958), cited everywhere and read nowhere in this study, and Neil Jeffares's Dictionary of Pastellists Before 1800 entry, the authority used throughout. ## M1. The Scumbled Flesh -- White Chalk over Ochre, Smooth against Broken

Ducreux's pastel flesh is built the same way from the 1767 self-portrait to the late pastels, and the unfinished works let us watch it happen. He lays down alternating layers of white chalk, scumbled on -- dragged dry and thin across the paper tooth -- over browns and ochres, and the result is a distinctive, slightly muddy warmth, not the porcelain finish of La Tour. Then he works the surface in two registers at once: some passages are stumped, rubbed smooth with a paper stump or fingertip until the powder melts into a continuous skin, while other passages keep bold, exposed strokes that show the hand and the stick. The contrast is the mechanism: the smooth passages read as flesh, the broken strokes read as energy, and the viewer gets both a likeness and a performance of likeness in the same square inch. Because pastel cannot be erased, he also built in a correction system -- piecing. The Turgot portrait (1774-77) is pastel on five joined sheets of paper laid on canvas; a head could be drawn separately and pasted in, a mistake papered over, a sheet swapped. Some of his supports are paper lined with paper cut from his own discarded academic studies, so the ground itself is a reused drawing. The watchable behaviour is the surface breathing between smooth and broken: a face that is calm in the stumped cheek and agitated in the hatched hair and collar, with the joins of the construction hiding just under the pigment. **Takeaway:** a portrait whose skin is two textures at once -- stumped smooth where the likeness lives, bold exposed strokes where the hand shows -- and whose seams are part of the making, not hidden by it.

## M2. The Involuntary Portrait -- Five Named Expressions, One Face, No Sitter

The yawning self-portrait is a machine for breaking the rules of portraiture one at a time, and it is one of five. The formal portrait promises a composed sitter at their best, meeting the eye. Ducreux gives the opposite: a mouth wide open mid-yawn, the face contorted by the effort, the back swaying, the stomach pushing forward, the right arm stretched out toward the viewer, the body pressed up close to the picture surface, dressed in a turban and a bright red jacket -- undress, not court dress. It is a whole-body event, not a face study, and the stretch runs from the raised arm through the arched back to the thrust belly. He first showed it outside the Academy system entirely, at the Salon de la Correspondance on Wednesday 10 December 1783, a free populist exhibition; when he showed it again inside the Louvre in 1791, the critics jeered that he made the audience yawn. The five types, as Prytz lists them, are Le Rieur (laughing), Le Bailleur (yawning), Le Moqueur (mocking), Silence or Le Discret (finger pressed to pursed lips), and La Surprise or La Surprise en Terreur -- and each type names its emotion in its title, so the work arrives already labelled and the viewer is told what to read before they read it. All five are frontal, all five are oil, and all five keep the immediacy of his pastel work: the paint is handled as if it were chalk, laid down fast and left visible, because the expression being caught is itself fast. The repetitions make them hard to date; the same grimace reappears year after year, so much so that a critic of the 1796 Salon said the Rieur's grimace was indistinguishable from the previous year's Bailleur. He defended the series as "tableaux de caractere", character paintings that proved a portrait painter could paint history, and rooted them in Le Brun's expression theory, Caylus's prize heads, Lavater's physiognomy, Messerschmidt's character heads, and the Dutch tronies. The watchable behaviour is a portrait that refuses to hold still for its own dignity: a body caught mid-reflex, a gesture aimed at the viewer, a likeness that performs losing control -- and a face that cycles through a small fixed set of named states, so the viewer learns the set and starts anticipating the change. **Takeaway:** one face, five states, and the change of state is the picture -- a self-portrait that breaks the formal contract by showing the body mid-reflex, with each state held just long enough to read before the next takes over.

## M3. The Diplomatic Likeness -- The Head Is the Treaty, the Body Is Hired Out

The Vienna mission of 1769 shows how a court portrait actually worked as a political object. After the Seven Years' War, France had lost its North American territories to Britain; the duc de Choiseul's plan to rebuild France ran through an alliance with Austria, sealed by marrying the Dauphin to the Archduchess Marie-Antoinette. The Dauphin wanted to see his bride "at least in painting". The Empress first sent a pocket miniature; Louis wanted her life-size and offered to send a French painter. The fashionable choice, Francois-Hubert Drouais, asked 80,000 livres to cover the commissions he would have to drop -- too expensive even for the king. Choiseul chose Ducreux: discreet, affordable, already known to the Austrian court from his Lorraine years, and, by one account, good-mannered and entertaining, a charming entertainer "whose gaiety and renowned good grace of his remarks could facilitate the patience of the child", which mattered because sitting was long and boring for a thirteen-year-old archduchess. Ducreux left Paris in February 1769 with the hairdresser Larseneur, whose job was to correct Marie-Antoinette's high hairline so "the forehead appears better furnished". He arrived in Vienna on Tuesday 14 February 1769, began the Marie-Antoinette portrait on 18 February -- but was immediately diverted by the Empress's orders to paint the archduchesses Christine, Therese, Elisabeth, and Prince Albert of Saxony, and had to start Marie's portrait over after secondary sessions. The French court grew impatient; the portrait took three months. The verdict, Thursday 18 May 1769: the king expressed great satisfaction; courtiers seeing the portrait said that if public rumor was to be believed, reality must far surpass the portrait in beauty -- and the court's judgement praised exactly one thing, the heads, "of perfect likeness and very finished execution", while faulting the bodies and accessories, blaming haste. That split is the system: the head is painted from life, with the sitter's patience bought by charm and by the hairdresser fixing the hairline; the body, dress, and background are secondary work, done fast, sometimes by others, sometimes copied. Then the portrait is multiplied: the Viennese copyist Kranzinger duplicated the Marie-Antoinette portraits for the Austrian court, modifying them to be "a bit more Austrian", and Ducreux's Joseph II pastel was copied outright from Batoni's double portrait shipped from Florence. The same operating system runs through his whole career: the Mesdames portraits of 1769 were drawn partly from life and partly copied from Drouais, and his early connoisseur portraits may be copies after La Tour. The watchable behaviour is a portrait that is really two objects fused -- a fiercely observed, flattered head floating over a hasty, interchangeable body -- and a likeness that travels by being copied, each copy slightly re-nationalized for its new court. (The portrait was a life-size pastel, 64.8 x 49.5 cm, not a miniature; see C2. The mechanism is the diplomatic likeness, and the word "miniature" in the earlier record describes the Empress's gift, not his work.) **Takeaway:** a portrait in which the head is the whole negotiation -- painted from life, flattered, finished -- while the body is fast, borrowed, or copied, and the likeness then multiplies through copyists who adjust it for each new court.

## M4. The Finger That Breaks the Frame -- Pointing, Shushing, and the Viewer as Sitter

In the Mocker, Ducreux points straight out of the canvas at whoever is looking; in Le Discret he raises a finger to his own lips and tells the viewer to be quiet; in La Surprise en terreur the outstretched hand pushes the viewer back. Prytz puts it precisely: the frontal self-portrait "creates an almost unavoidable interaction between the portrait and the viewer", and the viewer starts to wonder whether they are the cause of the surprise or the fear -- "is this what we ourselves look like when we feel those feelings?" The gesture turns the portrait around: the sitter is looking at you, and you become the sitter. The mechanism is address, not expression alone. A yawn is private; a pointed finger is public. The Mocker's laugh is aimed, the Discret's silence is a command, the Surprise's hand is a ward. All three gestures cross the picture plane and assign the viewer a role -- accused, silenced, or feared. The Mocker's point is the key gesture: La Tour's self-portrait points to one side, an elegant aside; Ducreux points straight out of the picture at us, an accusation. That is why the Mocker survived as a meme two centuries later: the pointing finger is already an image macro, a caption waiting for text, and the faux-archaic captions of the late 2000s only filled a slot the painting had left open in 1793. **Takeaway:** a face that points, shushes, or recoils at the viewer, making the viewer the second sitter in the picture -- watchable because the gesture never resolves; every look is a fresh accusation.

## M5. The Refusal and the Revenge Venue -- Three Rejections, Then a Portrait a Year

Ducreux was rejected three times by the Academie royale de peinture et de sculpture, and the sources agree his irascible character was at least part of the reason -- the dealer Wille wrote that Ducreux "said and wrote so badly of the Academy that it would have been very singular or very magnanimous on his part to admit him". He never joined. Instead he built a parallel career: the Academie de Saint-Luc (1764, suppressed 1776), the Salon de la Correspondance (from 1781), the Royal Academy in London (1791), and, when the Revolution opened the Louvre Salon to non-academicians in 1791, the open Salon itself -- where he exhibited his own portrait every year. By 1799 a critic joked that Ducreux had undertaken to offer the public his own portrait annually, that from long habit he now painted himself without a mirror and purely from memory, and that the run formed "a complete and graduated gallery" of one man. The mechanism is repetition as defiance: denied the Academy's validation, he made his own face the exhibition, year after year, in a new grimace each time, until the self-portrait series became a serial work -- the same head, varied by expression, like a printer's proof run. The watchable behaviour is a gallery wall of one face repeated with variations: the same man laughing, yawning, shushing, pointing, aging across the series, each version a little more exaggerated than the last. **Takeaway:** a serial self-portrait wall -- the same face returned to every year in a new exaggerated expression, turning exclusion from the Academy into a one-man exhibition that never ends.

## M6. The Outside Salon -- Two Rooms, Two Painters

Shut out of the Academie royale three times, Ducreux exhibited where the Academy could not reach him: Pahin de La Blancherie's Salon de la Correspondance, a free newspaper and a free room, where a portrait of the founder came first (1781), then Franklin (1782), then the Yawning Man on Wednesday 10 December 1783. The mechanism is the parallel institution: when the official room refuses you, you build a second room with different rules -- no jury, no fee, no rank, and women artists hung as pendants to each other so the public could compare them. The Salon de la Correspondance is where Ducreux stopped painting queens and started painting himself, and where Labille-Guiard and Vigee Le Brun were first set against each other as rivals. The same painter behaves differently in each room: in the official room he paints the court in pastel, heads finished and bodies hired out; in the free room he paints himself mid-yawn in oil at nearly life size, in a turban and a red jacket. The venue is part of the work -- the yawn needed a room without a jury, because no jury would have hung it. The watchable behaviour is the formal head loosening into a yawn the moment it crosses into the free room: the same hand, the same face, a different set of rules, and the picture changes with the room. **Takeaway:** the same painter in two rooms paints two different painters; the watchable behaviour is the portrait changing register as it moves from the official wall to the free wall.

## M7. Copying as the Operating System -- The Likeness Is Made by Handing It On

Copying was not a sideline in Ducreux's practice but the operating system of the court portrait, and it ran in both directions. His early pastels may have been copies after La Tour and after Drouais rather than original work, and the similarity of his work to La Tour's derives from this early copying practice; the 1769 Mesdames portraits were drawn partly from life and partly copied from Drouais paintings; the Joseph II pastel was copied from Batoni's double portrait shipped from Florence to Vienna; and the Vienna Marie-Antoinette portraits were then duplicated by the copyist Kranzinger for the Austrian court, who modified them to be "a bit more Austrian". Jeffares adds that the sheer numbers in his accounts explain the uneven quality of the known oeuvre -- hundreds of portraits, many of them variants of a single sitting. The mechanism is the portrait as a relay: the likeness is established once, from life, and then handed on through copyists, each of whom adjusts it for a new court, a new size, a new medium, while the head -- the part that was observed -- stays constant and the rest drifts. Because he rarely signed, many of his works remain misattributed to La Tour or Greuze, which is the same mechanism seen from the other side: a copy so good it changes author. The watchable behaviour is the handover itself: one head, many hands, and the likeness surviving each transfer with a little local accent added. **Takeaway:** a likeness that is made by being handed on -- painted once from life, then copied, re-sized, and re-nationalized by other hands, with the head held constant and everything else allowed to drift.

## M8. The Low Viewpoint -- The Sitter Seen from Below

The di sotto in su composition -- the sitter seen from below, looming over the viewer -- is prevalent throughout Ducreux's oeuvre, and it is a mechanism, not a habit. A portrait seen from below does two things at once: it makes the sitter larger than the viewer, which is the court portrait's job, and it puts the viewer in the position of the person being looked down on, which is the expressive portrait's job. The Getty's description of the yawning self-portrait shows the device at full stretch: the raised arm, the arched back, the thrust stomach, the whole body pressing up close to the surface of the picture -- the low viewpoint is what makes the yawn loom, because a yawn seen from below is a mouth opening over you. The same device serves the diplomatic portrait: the thirteen-year-old archduchess, painted from below, is not a child being inspected but a presence being presented, and the court's verdict on the heads -- "of perfect likeness and very finished execution" -- was a verdict on faces that looked down at the judges. The watchable behaviour is the tilt: the picture plane tips, the sitter rises over the viewer, and the viewer is put underneath the face they came to judge. **Takeaway:** a portrait shot from below so the sitter looms -- the viewer is placed underneath the face, and the likeness does its judging from above.

## M9. The Unsigned Catalogue -- The Work-List Is the Signature

Ducreux catalogued his works in list form from 1760 onward -- his own chronological records, kept from 1762 and surviving in transcription through Lyon 1958 -- but he rarely signed his paintings, so many of his works remain erroneously attributed to other artists, above all to La Tour. The mechanism is the list as the oeuvre: where other painters sign the picture, Ducreux signed the ledger, and the record of the work is more reliable than the work itself. The list is also the evidence for the scale of the practice -- hundreds of portraits, with the numbers explaining the uneven quality Jeffares notes -- and for the sitter census: the great Paris families, the connoisseurs, the court, the revolutionaries, all entered in order, year by year. The attribution hazard runs through the whole corpus the way the shared surname runs through the Dumonts in #505: a pastel that looks like La Tour may be Ducreux copying La Tour, or Ducreux being Ducreux, and the list is the only thing that can tell the two apart. The watchable behaviour is the ledger filling up: the same name entered again and again, the sitters changing around it -- connoisseurs, then kings, then revolutionaries -- while the hand that made them stays unsigned. **Takeaway:** an oeuvre held together by a list instead of a signature; the watchable behaviour is the catalogue growing, sitter by sitter, while the pictures themselves refuse to say who made them.

## M10. The About-Face -- The Same Hand, the New Sitters

At the outbreak of the Revolution Ducreux went to London (1791), where he showed five portraits at the Royal Academy and traded hard on his titles, styling himself "Painter to the King, and first Painter to the Queen of France". He etched self-portraits there to sell alongside the show, including Le Rieur, Le Joueur eplore (the weeping gambler), and an engraved Le Discret. He was back in Paris by September 1791 for the first open Salon, and then he made a political about-face: he painted the revolutionaries Couthon, Manuel, Petion, and Robespierre, and exhibited at the open Salon where his work was praised for truth and fresh, pure tones. David became an associate and helped him continue an official career; his house became an informal salon for artists and musicians; he was granted lodgings in the Louvre in April 1793 but could not take them up; he served on a Gobelins jury in 1794; he received a 3,000-livres national reward in 1795; he signed the petition for Vigee Le Brun in 1799. The mechanism is the transfer of flattery: the same hand that had flattered a thirteen-year-old archduchess into a marriage now flattered the men who had killed her husband, and the portrait system did not change -- the head observed, the body hired out, the likeness travelling by copy -- only the sitters did. The same year he painted Louis XVI at the Temple (2 January 1793, if the attribution holds) he showed a portrait of Robespierre at the Salon of 1793: the king's last portrait and the revolutionary's portrait in the same twelve months, from the same brush. The watchable behaviour is the sitter list turning over: the queens leave the ledger and the revolutionaries enter it, and the painter keeps painting, at the same scale, in the same medium, as if nothing had happened -- because for the portrait, nothing had. **Takeaway:** a portrait practice that survives a revolution by changing sitters, not methods; the watchable behaviour is the ledger turning over, queens out and revolutionaries in, while the hand stays the same.

## Evolution

Phase 1 -- Early Nancy and Paris, 1760-1768. Born Sunday 26 June 1735 at Nancy, son of Charles Ducreux, premier peintre to Stanislaw Leszczynski; raised largely in Luneville, where his father worked for Leszczynski; probably trained first under his father. The La Tour connection is the first open question of the career: he is "widely referred to as La Tour's only pupil", but Jeffares shows there is little evidence of a formal apprenticeship, and the likelier story is that he visited La Tour's workshop and learned by copying (see C9). What is certain is the work: he arrives in Paris in 1760, aged 25, marries Philippine-Rose Cosse on Wednesday 12 November 1760, and from 1762 paints pastels of the Parisian Enlightenment elite -- La Live de Jully (1762), Caylus (1763) -- and by 1767 has a settled technique of his own, flesh built by "alternating layers of white chalk, scumbled on, with browns and ochres, giving a distinctive, slightly muddy effect", combining "stumped, smooth areas with others where bold strokes are left exposed". He is recu by the Academie de Saint-Luc in late October 1764 and rejected three times by the Academie royale, reportedly for his irascible character -- the guild door stays open while the Academy door stays shut, and that exclusion shapes everything after. The first self-portrait, as a young man, dates to 1767. Work: small pastels, the chalk idiom of La Tour, men of letters rather than kings.

Phase 2 -- Vienna and the court, 1769-1789. In 1769 Choiseul selects Ducreux to travel to Vienna because Drouais cannot be afforded: he must paint Marie-Antoinette and the other Austrian archduchesses before the marriage, and first produce portraits of the French royal Mesdames, drawn partly from life and partly copied from Drouais. He arrives in Vienna Tuesday 14 February 1769, begins the Marie-Antoinette portrait four days later, and stays until Thursday 16 November 1769, painting the empress, the emperor and the archdukes as well. The reward is the post of premier peintre de la reine -- granted by Marie-Antoinette herself even though he is not an Academician, a break with the rule that court posts go to Academy members -- and, it is said but without corroboration, the title of baron (see C3). Back in Paris he paints the court in pastel (Madame Victoire 1776, Turgot 1774-77) and begins to work in oil under Greuze's influence. The Franklin pastel of 1777 is the phase's best non-royal work: a celebrity sitter, glasses on, painted the year Franklin became the fashion of Paris. From 1781 he exhibits at Pahin de La Blancherie's Salon de la Correspondance, the free alternative salon for non-Academicians -- first a portrait of its founder (1781), then Franklin, then, on Wednesday 10 December 1783, the yawning self-portrait. The yawn is the hinge of the whole career: the same hand that made the diplomatic likeness now paints itself mid-yawn, red jacket, turban, arm stretched at the viewer, in oil at nearly life size. Work: scale grows (pastel heads become full oil half-lengths), the sitter list widens from connoisseurs to kings and celebrities, and the expressive self-portrait appears as a second career inside the first.

Phase 3 -- Revolution, 1789-1799. At the outbreak of the Revolution Ducreux goes to London. In 1791 he exhibits five works at the Royal Academy, trading on his titles ("Painter to the King and first Painter to the Queen of France"), and on Monday 21 February 1791 he etches and publishes a facetious profile self-portrait. The London exhibits include Surprise and Surprise Mixte with Terror -- the expression pieces are now his public identity. Back in Paris by September 1791, he shows at the first open Salon, where a critic praises the pastels: "it would be impossible to paint with more veracity, purer and fresher tonalities. The head turns well, with fine modeling. Continuez, M. Ducreux." Then the about-face: as the monarchy collapses he embraces the revolutionary cause and paints its leaders -- Robespierre, Couthon, Louis Pierre Manuel, Jerome Petion de Villeneuve -- and on Wednesday 2 January 1793 the chalk portrait inscribed "fait au Temple" is made, the portrait traditionally called the last of Louis XVI before his execution on Monday 21 January 1793 (attribution contested, see C1). In the Salon of 1793 he shows the Mocker, painted in London, guffawing and pointing at the viewer; David becomes his associate and helps him continue an official career, and his house becomes an informal salon for artists and musicians. Prytz codifies the period's output as five basic self-portrait types: Le Rieur (laughing), Le Bailleur (yawning), Le Moqueur (mocking), Silence or Le Discret, and La Surprise or La Surprise en Terreur -- all oil, all frontal, all retaining the immediacy of pastel. Work: pastel gives way to oil for the self-portraits; the subject becomes his own face in named emotional states; the audience shifts from patrons to the Salon public and the jury of the Revolution; and the portrait stops flattering and starts accusing, shushing, or startling.

Phase 4 -- Late Saint-Denis, 1800-1802. Around 1800 Ducreux uses oil more and more, and it does not meet with universal praise: the critic of the Salon of 1801 wished "that Ducreux return to pastel, which he handles much better", calling the brush an enemy and the General Clarke portrait raw and red. He shows at the Salon regularly until the year before his death; his last Salon is that of the year IX, 1801. The late self-portrait with natural long hair (c. 1798, Louvre) drops the wig and the grimace alike -- the same face, uncostumed. The last dated work is the Lalande portrait of 1802, an academician in academician's dress, painted for Versailles. On Saturday 24 July 1802 he dies of apoplexy while walking from Paris to Saint-Denis, aged 67, dying in the arms of a passing soldier within minutes. His daughter Rose-Adelaide dies in Saint-Domingue two days later, Monday 26 July 1802, of yellow fever. Work: almost nothing changes in handling -- the late works are a settling, not a new departure; the experiment was finished by 1793 and the last years repeat its types at lower volume.

## Aesthetic Logic

The whole system is a machine for making a face do work that a letter cannot. He starts from the opposite problem to his predecessor's: not a miniaturist who found one unit and let the sitters change around it, but a portraitist who found one transaction -- a head, observed from life, flattered just enough to be trusted, and delivered on time -- and let the transaction change hands: connoisseurs, then kings, then revolutionaries, with the same head, the same flattery, the same haste in the body. The pastel is the thesis: a face built from scumbled white chalk over ochre, smooth where the likeness lives and broken where the hand shows, on a support that cannot be erased and must be pieced when it goes wrong. The expression is the second thesis: a face is not a still but a state, and five named states -- laugh, yawn, silence, mockery, surprise -- are enough to run a whole career on, because the face is the one model that never objects and never charges. The venue is the third: shut out of the Academy three times, he built his career in the rooms the Academy did not control, and the yawn needed a free room because no jury would have hung it. And the copy is the fourth: the likeness is made by being handed on, from life to copyist to print, each hand adding a little local accent, so that the portrait is a message edited in transit. What makes him usable for generative work is not the royal subject -- that is the biography -- but the system underneath it: a surface that is two textures at once, a portrait that is a document with a job, a face that cycles through named states, a gesture that points straight out of the frame at the viewer, a serial self-portrait wall that turns rejection into a one-man exhibition, and a ledger that holds the oeuvre together because the pictures themselves refuse to say who made them. And the refusal is the point twice over: the Academy refused him, and he refused the portrait's contract in return -- and the portrait that breaks the contract, the man yawning in a turban and a red jacket, is the one the world still knows, two centuries later, first as a meme and only then as a painting.

## POSITIONS

- P1. [FROM RECORD] Pastel first, oil late. His early portraits are in pastel, including the connoisseurs Mariette, Caylus and La Live de Jully; by around 1800 he was using oil more and more, and the 1801 Salon critic wished him back in pastel, "which he handles much better". Evidence: en.wikipedia; Jeffares.
- P2. [FROM RECORD] A catalogue kept, a signature withheld. He catalogued his works in list form from 1760 onward (his own records, kept from 1762, survive in transcription through Lyon 1958 and Bellier de La Chavignerie 1865) but rarely signed his paintings, so many of his works remain erroneously attributed to other artists -- above all to La Tour. Evidence: en.wikipedia; Jeffares.
- P3. [FROM RECORD] Copying as apprenticeship. His early pastels may have been copies after La Tour and after Drouais rather than original work, and the similarity of his work to La Tour's derives from this early copying practice; Jeffares adds that the sheer numbers in his accounts explain the uneven quality of the known oeuvre. Evidence: en.wikipedia; Jeffares.
- P4. [FROM RECORD] The low viewpoint. The di sotto in su composition -- the sitter seen from below, looming over the viewer -- is prevalent throughout Ducreux's oeuvre. Evidence: Jeffares.
- P5. [FROM RECORD] A built-up pastel surface. His technique was established as early as the 1767 self-portrait: alternating layers of white chalk, scumbled on, with browns and ochres, giving a distinctive, slightly muddy effect; stumped, smooth areas combined with others where bold strokes are left exposed; a significant number of unfinished pastels let the build be seen. Evidence: Jeffares.
- P6. [FROM RECORD] Heads first, bodies second. The French court's reaction to the first Vienna portraits was that the heads were "d'une parfaite ressemblance et d'une execution bien finie", while the bodies and accessories were not without faults, attributed to the haste with which Ducreux had had to work. Evidence: Jeffares.
- P7. [FROM RECORD] Four faces, one system. There are four basic types of self-portrait: Le Rieur (laughing), Le Bailleur (yawning), Le Silence or Le Discret (finger to the lips), and Le Moqueur (smiling directly at the viewer, pointing at us); they appeared in pastel, oil and in the three etchings he made of himself in London, and the repetitions make them particularly difficult to date. Evidence: Jeffares; en.wikipedia.
- P8. [FROM RECORD] Character heads claimed as history painting. In his letter to the state he argued that he was criticised for being a portrait painter, but that his "tableaux de caractere" such as Le Railleur, Le Silence and Le Moqueur, exhibited at the Salon, were in effect history paintings rather than portraits. Evidence: Jeffares; arthistoryproject.com.
- P9. [FROM RECORD] Physiognomy as the working theory. His interest in physiognomy -- the belief that a person's outer appearance, especially the shape and lines of the face, could reveal their inner character -- influenced his warm and individualistic works; his self-portraits take the tradition established by Charles Le Brun and enshrined by the comte de Caylus's prize into the age of Lavater. Evidence: en.wikipedia; Jeffares; Google Arts and Culture.
- P10. [FROM RECORD] The irascible outsider. Recu by the Academie de Saint-Luc in 1764, he was rejected three times by the Academie royale de peinture et de sculpture because of his irascible character; Johann Georg Wille wrote that Ducreux "said and wrote so badly of the Academy" that admitting him would have been singular or magnanimous; he never joined the Academie royale, yet Marie-Antoinette gave him the premier peintre de la reine appointment that was customarily reserved for its members. Evidence: en.wikipedia; arthistoryproject.com.
- P11. [FROM RECORD] The parallel salon. Shut out of the Academy, he exhibited at Pahin de La Blancherie's Salon de la Correspondance -- his first contribution in 1781 a portrait of La Blancherie, in 1782 the Franklin, on 10 December 1783 the Yawning Man -- and at the open Salons after 1791, where his self-portraits were met with criticism and mockery for their extravagant poses. Evidence: arthistoryproject.com; dailyartmagazine.com; en.wikipedia.
- P12. [FROM RECORD] The portrait as an instrument of state. The Vienna mission was diplomacy conducted in pastel: a portrait had to settle a marriage alliance between France and Austria, satisfy a king who wanted to see his bride life-sized, survive the Empress's orders, a restart, and a copyist who made the results "a bit more Austrian" -- and it succeeded, the marriage following on 16 May 1770. Evidence: arthistoryproject.com; Jeffares.
- P13. [FROM RECORD] Flattery with a limit. The advice he took from Greuze, and the rule he is recorded as living by: be sharp if you cannot be true; undertake only what you can do in your essence and hasten slowly; never flatter his sitters, even the old ladies he painted, and never make a head bigger than life or smaller than it should be. Evidence: arthistoryproject.com; Jeffares.
- P14. [FROM RECORD] Finish by returning. Greuze's manuscript note to him: finish your works as much as you can, come back to them thirty times if need be, your grounds well impasted (see Q6); his own matching rule was to try to do it at the first stroke and never fear coming back afterwards, provided it is in glaze (see Q3). Evidence: Jeffares.
- P15. [FROM RECORD] Study before painting, and slowly. His own written advice to pupils (see Q2-Q3): make studies before painting, by drawing above all; establish your comparisons of strong against weak slowly; furnish your memory, above all with landscape, to become harmonious; never impaste your laces or your gauzes. Evidence: Jeffares.
- P16. [FROM RECORD] The republican second career. At the outbreak of the Revolution he fled to London (1791), showed five portraits at the Royal Academy from 232 Piccadilly, engraved three of his expressive self-portraits to sell with the show, and drew the portrait traditionally called the last of Louis XVI; he was back in Paris by September 1791 for the first open Salon (see C11), became David's associate, served as commissaire de police du camp sous Paris, was granted lodgings in the Louvre by the Convention in April 1793 (which he could not take up), sat on the 1794 jury for the Gobelins and Savonnerie, received a recompense nationale of 3,000 livres in July 1795, signed the petition for Vigee Le Brun in 1799, and showed a portrait of Robespierre at the Salon of 1793 in the same year the Temple portrait of Louis XVI was made. Evidence: Jeffares; en.wikipedia; arthistoryproject.com.
- P17. [FROM RECORD] The house as a salon. His residence became an informal salon for artists and musicians who commissioned portraits from him; Jeffares names La Harpe, Piccini, Sophie Arnould, Mme Recamier and the young Josephine Beauharnais among the circle, and says the attractions of his wife and daughters "compensated for the artist's difficult personality". Evidence: en.wikipedia; Jeffares.
- P18. [INFERRED -- labelled as such] The self-portrait as a laboratory. No source states why he returned to his own face dozens of times; the inference from the record is that his own face was the one model always available, always patient, and always free -- the only sitter on whom he could test an extreme expression without a patron objecting, a court complaining, or a fee being at stake. The inference is supported by the facts that the four types repeat across pastel, oil and etching, that he painted himself "purely from memory" by 1799 (see Q17), and that the work was done outside commissions, in the alternative salon and in London exile. It is an inference, not a quotation.
- P19. [ABSENCE -- stated plainly] No published statement of his about his art exists in the record found. There is no treatise, no Salon pamphlet under his name, no aesthetic letter: what survives in his own words is his work-list (from 1760/1762), a set of short written counsels to pupils transcribed by Jeffares (see Q2-Q3), and two begging letters to the state (1794 and 30 April 1799; see Q4-Q5). His views have to be read off the pictures and off Greuze's note to him, not off any text of his own. The absence is the finding.
- P20. [RECEPTION] Mocked in his lifetime, admired for warmth, memed after death. Salon critics called him "one of those ordinary talents which tends more towards mediocrity than towards perfection", his heads "strong wooden heads, a yawner to make the lackeys laugh"; a contemporary critic admired the Yawning Man for its warmth, color and expression, while later critics complained about the repetition; the Journal des Debats obituary (1802) called him a painter who "had achieved success in the genre of painting he had adopted"; in 2009 the Moqueur became an internet meme (archaic-rap captions), and the Getty and the Belvedere now hang him beside Messerschmidt as a fellow student of physiognomy. Evidence: Jeffares; Google Arts and Culture; arthistoryproject.com; Louvre-Lens.

## Load-bearing corrections

- C0. #505'S EXCLUSION NOTE IS WRONG ABOUT LOUIS XVIII AND CHARLES X. The #505 file's exclusion note claims Marie-Antoinette, Louis XVI, Louis XVIII, Charles X and the Comtesse d'Artois are "not carried anywhere in the chain". That is wrong for two of the five: the "New from #504" block in the same file lists Louis XVIII (1755-1824) and Charles X (1757-1836) as genuine entries. The carried status recorded in this study follows the block, not the note. Marie-Antoinette, Louis XVI and the Comtesse d'Artois are indeed not carried anywhere, on the evidence of the entry-start grep. Found by the network leg and confirmed by the parent's own check of the block text.
- C1. THE "LAST PORTRAIT OF LOUIS XVI" CLAIM -- TRUST JEFFARES, NOT WIKIPEDIA. Wikipedia says: "At the outbreak of the French Revolution, Ducreux traveled to London. There he drew the last portrait ever made of Louis XVI before the king's execution." This is wrong in two ways. First, geography: Ducreux was in London in 1791; Louis XVI was in Paris, and was executed there on Monday 21 January 1793. No portrait could have been drawn "there" (London) from life. Second, the object behind the claim: Jeffares catalogues it as J.285.492, LOUIS XVI, black and white chalk, 28.2 x 21 cm, inscribed "fait au Temple, 2.I.1793" (made at the Temple prison, 2 January 1793), now Musee Carnavalet, Paris, inv. D.131/D7108. Jeffares marks the attribution with question marks ("??; new attr., ?; cf. Lenoir") and notes that Versailles 2019 reproduced the same sheet as anonymous, c. 1788, of Charles Edward Stuart. Lyon 1958 (p. 171) is the source of the Temple dating. So: the drawing exists, it is dated by inscription to Wednesday 2 January 1793 (19 days before the execution), and it is plausibly Ducreux's -- but the attribution is contested, there is no record of Ducreux being admitted to the Temple to draw the imprisoned king, and the Wikipedia sentence tying it to London is simply wrong. VERDICT: use "a black-and-white chalk portrait inscribed as made at the Temple on 2 January 1793, traditionally called the last portrait of Louis XVI, attribution contested" -- never "drawn in London", never unqualified "the last portrait".
- C2. "MINIATURE" OF MARIE-ANTOINETTE, 1769 -- WIKIPEDIA'S WORD, NOT JEFFARES'. Wikipedia says Ducreux was sent to Vienna "to paint a miniature of Marie Antoinette". The sources agree the Empress first sent a pocket miniature to Paris; Ducreux was sent precisely because Louis wanted her life-size. Jeffares' catalogue gives the 1769 Marie-Antoinette as pastel on parchment, 64.8 x 49.5 cm (Versailles MV 8973), and another version 65 x 52 cm -- full-scale pastels, not miniatures. VERDICT: Ducreux painted life-size pastels; "miniature" describes the Empress's earlier gift, not his work. This corrects the wording of #505's top-pick line, which called the 1769 betrothal portrait "the diplomatic-miniature case".
- C3. BARON TITLE -- UNCORROBORATED. Wikipedia and Wikiart state flatly that Ducreux "was made a baron". Jeffares: he was sent home from Vienna "with a present of 1000 ducats, a diamond jewel, and, it is said (but without corroboration) the title of baron conferred on him by Marie-Antoinette." VERDICT: report the barony as tradition, not fact.
- C4. NUMBER OF CHILDREN -- 5, 6, OR 7. Wildenstein says Ducreux and Philippine-Rose Cosse "would have five children". NMWA says Rose-Adelaide was "the eldest of six children". Wikipedia lists seven children by name (Rose-Adelaide, Emmanuel, Adrien-Charles, Auguste-Joseph, Alexandre-Jules, Antoinette-Clemence, Antoine-Leon) and Jeffares' genealogy supports seven births. The lower counts probably exclude the two who died young (Auguste-Joseph, 1770-1771; Adrien-Charles, d. 1776 aged 10). VERDICT: seven children born, five surviving to adulthood; say so explicitly.
- C5. YAWNING PORTRAIT DIMENSIONS -- JEFFARES VS GETTY. Jeffares J.285.112 gives 114 x 89 cm; Getty-derived listings give 117.8 x 90.8 cm (46 3/8 x 35 3/4 in.). Unresolved; the Getty's own object page was not opened end to end in any leg. This study uses the Getty's 117.8 x 90.8 cm with Jeffares's figure noted.
- C6. THE MOCKER DATE AND LOCATION. Wikipedia infobox: c. 1793, Louvre. Arthistoryproject: shown at the 1793 Salon, livret entry "A mockingbird pointing, 3 feet 2 inches high by 2 and a half feet wide". Consistent. No conflict on the exhibition; the tension is the engraving dated 1791 and the Commons file record saying "painted in London" -- a 1791 London painting exhibited in Paris in 1793 reconciles both, and one print vendor dates a "mocking face" version to 1802. Location: one Commons file record puts it in the Louvre, Room 933; another puts it at the Musee de la Revolution francaise, Vizille, since 1997, under Louvre inventory RF 2261. Read as a Louvre-owned work on deposit at Vizille; not fully resolved. VERDICT: c. 1793, Louvre RF 2261, on deposit at Vizille since 1997, with the 1791 engraving noted.
- C7. MIGNARD AND THE ACADEMIE DE SAINT-LUC, 1776 -- ARTHISTORYPROJECT ERROR. Arthistoryproject says Pierre Mignard, first painter to the king, "manipulated financial reforms" in 1776 to snuff out the Academie de Saint-Luc. Pierre Mignard died in 1695; he cannot have acted in 1776. The Academie de Saint-Luc was indeed suppressed in 1776, but by royal edict, not by Mignard. Do not repeat the Mignard version. (The error is in the popular article only; Jeffares and Wildenstein do not name Mignard.)
- C8. HOW JOSEPH DUCREUX DIED -- APOPLEXY IN FRANCE, NOT YELLOW FEVER. Jeffares, Wikipedia, Wildenstein, and the Journal des Debats obituary agree: apoplexy (stroke) on the road from Paris to Saint-Denis, Saturday 24 July 1802, dying in the arms of a passing soldier. One secondary source (thefashionhistorian.com, via search snippet) says Rose's father died of yellow fever two days after her. That reverses the dates and the cause: Rose died of yellow fever in Saint-Domingue on Monday 26 July 1802, two days AFTER her father died in France. VERDICT: father apoplexy, 24 July 1802, France; daughter yellow fever, 26 July 1802, Saint-Domingue. The obituary's "died in his sixty-fourth year" is also wrong against the dates: born 26 June 1735, died 24 July 1802, he was 67.
- C9. LA TOUR PUPILAGE -- TRADITION, NOT DOCUMENTED FACT. See the network leg's section 3. "Only pupil of La Tour" is repeated by Wikipedia, Wikiart, artincontext, and the Wildenstein note, but Jeffares shows there is no documentary evidence of an apprenticeship; the claim rests on an 1822 secondary source and a 1796 Salon livret. The Greuze connection, by contrast, is documented (manuscript note, close friendship). VERDICT: say "said to have studied under La Tour; the evidence is thin" and give Greuze the weight.
- C10. ALEXANDRE-JULES DID NOT DIE AT JEMMAPES. Wikipedia's child list says Alexandre-Jules "died shortly after the Battle of Jemmapes" (6 November 1792). - C11. RETURN TO PARIS -- BY SEPTEMBER 1791, NOT 1792 OR 1793. The legs disagree: leg 1 says he returned to Paris in 1792 ("returns to Paris for good"); leg 3 and Wikipedia say he returned in 1793; leg 2 says he was back by August 1791. The anchor is the first open Salon of September 1791, at which he exhibited -- he was in Paris for it -- and the Garde nationale brevet of 12 January 1792 confirms his presence in Paris by that date. VERDICT: back in Paris by September 1791; neither leg 1's 1792 nor Wikipedia's 1793 is followed. (Parent correction.)
- C12. THE 1771-72 MARIE-ANTOINETTE IS DUPLESSIS'S, NOT DUCREUX'S. The brief's premise that Ducreux painted a Marie-Antoinette in 1771-72 (Versailles/Louvre versions) is wrong. Versailles acquired Joseph-Siffred Duplessis's oil (1772-1773, 44.4 x 33.4 cm) in December 2021; it is the first portrait of Marie-Antoinette made on French soil. Ducreux's Marie-Antoinette is the 1769 Vienna pastel. (Parent correction, from the works leg.)
- C13. NO DUCREUX PORTRAIT OF THE COMTE D'ARTOIS WAS FOUND. The brief lists the comte d'Artois among Ducreux's sitters, but no Ducreux portrait of him was found in any source read. The known pastel of the Comtesse d'Artois is by Joseph Boze, signed and dated 1783; the known Drouais portrait of the comte as a child with Clotilde (1767) was engraved by Beauvarlet. The Jeffares catalogue portion viewed lists no Artois sitter. The listing is recorded here as unverified, not as fact. (Parent correction, from the network leg.)
- C14. FRANKLIN -- PAINTED 1777, EXHIBITED 1782. Christie's dates the Franklin pastel to 1777; the Daily Art Magazine account says Ducreux "presented" the Franklin portrait in 1782, and the Salon de la Correspondance 1782 catalogue lists it. Read as painted 1777, exhibited 1782 -- no real conflict once creation and exhibition are separated.
- C15. TEMPLE DRAWING DIMENSIONS -- JEFFARES VS MEISTERDRUCKE. Jeffares J.285.492 gives black and white chalk, 28.2 x 21 cm, Musee Carnavalet, inv. D.131/D7108. A Meisterdrucke listing gives a chalk-and-charcoal "Portrait of Louis XVI at the Temple" of height 0.49 m, with no location established. Unresolved whether these are the same sheet; this study uses Jeffares's figures and location, and notes the variant.
- C16. LE DISCRET DATE -- C. 1791 VS C. 1790. The Spencer Museum dates Le Discret "circa 1791"; Wikipedia says "c. 1790". The Nationalmuseum's Silence is dated "1790s" with no finer date. The 1791 Salon exhibition is the anchor; the "c. 1790" variant is not resolved.
- C17. LA VAUGUYON DATE -- 1773 WITH A QUERY. Jeffares's catalogue gives the Duchesse de La Vauguyon as 1773 with a "[??1775]" query. The 1773 date is used with the query noted.
- C18. ROSE-ADELAIDE'S BIRTH DAY -- UNVERIFIED. Wikipedia gives 1761 with no day; thefashionhistorian gives 19 August 1761; unverified -- this study uses 1761 only and does not use the day.
